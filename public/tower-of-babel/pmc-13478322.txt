Multi-scale computational analysis of monoacylglycerol lipase inhibition as a new therapeutic strategy for hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Mubarak A. Alamri, Abdullah S. Alawam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains one of the most critical global health challenges, particularly in connection to metabolically linked diseases like metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Current studies established a relationship between monoacylglycerol lipase (MAGL), a critical enzyme in lipid signal transduction pathways, and HCC development through control of pro-tumorigenic lipid signal modulators. The research presented here utilized an integrative computational pipeline to identify new sources of MAGL inhibitors with potential therapeutic value and the preliminary high-throughput screening yielded three lead candidates. Among these lead candidates, compound 17475070 exhibited optimal digital features in DFT-based quantum mechanics assessments, predicting high stability and low reactivity. Re-docking experiments were in support of strong binding interactions, which in turn were further supported through an array of critical hydrogen bonds as well as hydrophobic contacts in the MAGL binding site. Extensive molecular dynamics simulations (500 ns) confirmed that 17475070 remained stably bound, with minimal con-formational drift and persistent active site engagement. Principal component and free energy landscape analysis demonstrated that the compound almost entirely populated one low-energy conformational basin indicative of strong and stable interaction. Additional confirmation in terms of QM/MM simulations established that it continues to maintain strong electronic interactions in the catalytic pocket of MAGL. Machine learning based ADMET profiling predicted that the compound 17475070 would exhibit good oral bioavailability, low toxicity risks, and a long biological half-life. These findings, in aggregate, designate 17475070 as an interesting candidate to further develop as a potential MAGL-targeted HCC therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55557-y.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) continues to constitute a leading global cancer burden and is among the top causes of cancer mortality worldwide. In recent years, the rise of metabolic risk factors, particularly non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), now often described as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), together with obesity, diabetes, and changing lifestyles, has further escalated incidence in many countries1. Globally, estimates suggest over one million new cases of primary liver cancer annually, the majority being HCC and projections warn of increasing mortality unless detection, prevention, and treatment strategies are rein-forced2. Chronic infections with hepatitis B and C remain major etiologic agents, especially in Asia and Africa; however, in many regions, MASLD/NASH and alcohol related liver disease are rising rapidly and may overtake viral causes in some cohorts.

At the molecular level, HCC is heterogeneous. Commonly dysregulated signalling pathways include Wnt/β catenin, TGF-β, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, Hippo/YAP, and MAPK3. There is also accumulating evidence that lipid metabolism reprogramming plays a crucial role in HCC pathogenesis. Features such as upregulation of fatty acid synthesis, altered lipid oxidation, elevated free fatty acids, and dysregulated bioactive lipids con-tribute to disease progression and metastasis4. In this context, enzyme regulators of lipid signalling are under investigation as potential therapeutic targets. One such enzyme is monoacylglycerol lipase (MAGL, also MGLL), which hydrolyzes monoacylglycerols to free fatty acids and glycerol. MAGL also plays a key role in regulating endocannabinoid signaling by degrading 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG), thereby modulating cannabinoid receptor activity and generating arachidonic acid for downstream inflammatory pathways. In addition, MAGL is frequently overexpressed in cancer and contributes to tumour progression by regulating pro-tumorigenic lipid signalling. Catalytically, MAGL functions as a serine hydrolase with a conserved Ser122–Asp239–His269 catalytic triad, operating through a nucleophilic attack mechanism5. Structurally, MAGL contains a deep hydrophobic tunnel that accommodates lipid substrates, along with a flexible lid domain (residues 151–225) that regulates access to the active site through conformational changes. In its open state, the lid exposes a hydrophobic surface that facilitates interaction with lipophilic ligands, while the enclosed active site ensures proper ligand positioning. These structural features are critical for ligand recognition and binding stability6.

Prior studies have shown that MAGL expression is elevated in HCC tumour tissues relative to adjacent normal liver and that higher MAGL is associated with worse tumor grade, greater invasion, poorer differentiation, and reduced survival. Knockdown or pharmacological inhibition of MAGL in HCC cell lines and animal models suppressed proliferation, invasiveness, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), implicating MAGL as both a prognostic marker and therapeutic vulnerability7. In HCC, MAGL functions as a metabolic hub linking lipid metabolism to oncogenic signalling, where its overexpression promotes tumor growth, migration, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition through lipid-mediated signaling pathways. Inhibition of MAGL has been shown to disrupt pro-tumorigenic lipid networks and reduce cancer cell aggressiveness, further supporting its potential as a therapeutic target8.

Despite these advances, there remain gaps: many studies to date have not focused on populations or regional molecular features; the transition in dominant aetiologies sug-gests that MAGL’s role may be particularly relevant in MASLD/NASH-associated HCC; and clinical translation of MAGL inhibitors in liver cancers remains underdeveloped. Over the past decade, several MAGL inhibitors have been described. Early irreversible inhibitors such as JZL184 have been used widely as tool compounds, showing suppression of tumour growth in non-HCC cancer models via accumulation of the endocannabinoid 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG) and reduction in prostaglandin E₂. Reversible inhibitors also exist. for example, o-hydroxyanilide scaffolds have yielded compounds with low micromolar potency and improved selectivity over related serine hydrolases. Natural products and derivatives have also contributed leads. More recently, a set of covalent inhibitors bearing nitrile warheads against MAGL was re-ported in 2025 via high-throughput screening, broadening the chemical space of irreversible MAGL targeting9. Additionally, work on MAGL23, a potent reversible MAGL inhibitor, demonstrated promising anticancer effects in vitro, with improved delivery via carrier-free systems10. Additionally, innovative strategies, such as PROTACs targeting MAGL, have been proposed to enhance tumor selectivity and reduce off-target toxicity in non-liver settings5.

In our work, we aimed to address these gaps by discovering new MAGL inhibitors with strong potential for treating HCC. We sought to identify compounds with high affinity and selectivity for MAGL, favourable pharmacokinetic/toxicity predictions, and stable binding under dynamic conditions. Our objective is to propose novel lead molecules from a chemically diverse library that not only engage MAGL potently but also show promise for translation toward preclinical development in HCC.

Methodology

Virtual screening of diverse-lib database

High-throughput virtual screening was performed using the MTiOpenScreen platform. The crystal structure of human monoacylglycerol lipase (MAGL) (PDB ID: 7L4W) was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank and pre-processed to remove artifacts, add hydrogens, and optimize the active site 11,12,13, 14. A total of 99,288 compounds from the Diverse-lib database were curated with Open Babel for format conversion and drug-likeness compliance. Screening yielded ranked docking poses, from which the top 100 ligands were initially shortlisted, and finally, the three most promising candidates were selected for detailed downstream analyses.

Density functional theory (DFT) optimization

To efficaciously adapt the electronic and geometric characteristics of these optimized ligands, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted within the quantum mechanics-grounded optimization framework15,16. Three potential Diverse-lib molecule candidates and one control molecule of an inhibitor were taken directly from their.sdf files by means of employing the RDKit in conjunction with inclusion of hydrogen atoms in specific instances to guarantee stereochemical accuracy and proper bond directionality17. The atomic coordinates were thus converted to quantum input files and run in the PySCF package18. The B3LYP functional, combining Becke’s three-parameter exchange functional and the Lee–Yang–Parr correlation functional, was adopted in conjunction with the cc-pVDZ basis set to have precise modeling of interactions of electrons19.

All the above systems were treated within the Restricted Kohn–Sham formalism of closed-shell electronic configurations, and stringent control over self-consistent field (SCF) cycle convergence was imposed in the interest of wavefunction stability20. The structures, after successful optimization, were put through analysis of frontier molecular orbits (FMOs). Energy computations of HOMO and LUMO were carried out under standard three-dimensional grid of 80 × 80 × 80, saved in cube file format, and visualized to monitor spatial localization of electron density, thus revealing sites of utmost susceptibility to electrophilic and/or nucleophilic attack. HOMO–LUMO energy gap (ΔE) in terms of eV by enforcing the conversion factor of the Hartree to eV (27.2114) was realized to be the master descriptor of reactivity. Gaps (ΔE) negligible implicated higher chemical reactivity and lower kinetic stability, whereas gaps larger indicated structurally stable scaffolding of lower polarizability and higher stability.

Re-docking studies using AutoDock Vina

The DFT-optimized conformers of one control inhibitor and three candidate ligands were re-docked in AutoDock Vina into the human monoacylglycerol lipase (MAGL) active site in an attempt to recover superior binding poses consistent with quantum-level optimization21,22. The docking grid was laid out by placing it at X = − 30.84, Y = − 10.32, and Z = − 49.9, corresponding to the binding site of the co-crystallized native ligand within the catalytic pocket of MAGL. The grid was sized to fully encompass this region, including key residues involved in substrate recognition and enzymatic activity, thereby ensuring biologically relevant docking and adequate conformational sampling of ligands. An appropriate number of independent docking runs was conducted for each ligand to minimize stochastic bias, and the resulting poses were ranked by binding affinity. Top-ranked conformations by affinity were then analyzed for pocket orientation stability, hydrogen-bonding properties, and hydrophobic complementarity. Promising poses were selected as initial complexes for downstream molecular dynamics simulations to ensure sampling of trajectories was initiated from geometries that were optimized at the quantum and docking levels.

MD-based conformational sampling

To evaluate the dynamic stability of the ligand–MAGL complexes, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted using the free academic Desmond program integrated within the Maestro software, applying the OPLS-2005 force field to ensure accurate modeling of biomolecular interactions23,24. Each complex structure was prepared by running the Protein Preparation Wizard to correct bond orders and hydrogen bonding networks and minimize the structure. . The complexes were solvated in an orthorhombic TIP3P water box with 10 Å buffer and by adding counterions (Na⁺/Cl⁻) to neutralize the system and to mirror physiological ionic conditions25.

System relaxation was achieved by using Desmond’s standard relaxation protocol and subsequent heating up to 300 K at a constant-volume (NVT) state. The system was equilibrated at an isothermal–isobaric (NPT) ensemble at 1 bar for 1 ns to achieve stability for density and pressure. Production MD runs were subsequently performed for 500 ns per complex and snapshots were stored at 10 ps intervals. The trajectory data were analyzed with the Simulation Event Analysis module of Desmond to get structural measures such as root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), and hydrogen-bond occupancy.

Principal component analysis and free energy landscape

Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted for the motion paths of the Cα atoms during the final 500 ns of all molecular dynamics runs to reduce dimensional complexity and to identify dominating modes of motion in the MAGL–ligand complexes26. By using Python-based libraries through a workflow by Jupyter Notebook, we calculated and diagonalized the covariance matrix of positional fluctuations at the atom level to identify eigenvectors and eigenvalues describing collective protein motions. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) were selected because they indicated the most dominating conformational variations. To further characterize the conformation space, free energy landscapes (FELs) were generated by projecting the MD trajectories onto PC1 and PC2 followed by Boltzmann inversion of the distributions of probabilities to acquire the corresponding free energy profiles27. The plots of FEL thus obtained defined sharp low-energy basins corresponding to metastable states and hence unveiled structural details about ligand-stabilized conformations and dynamical MAGL active site rearrangements28.

Minima structure superimposition

To interrogate large-scale conformational motions, the final 500 ns of each molecular dynamics trajectory were subjected to PCA. The PCA condensed Cα atom fluctuations to eigenvectors and permitted identification of dominating collective motions and PC1 and PC2 as descriptors of key dynamics. Free energy landscapes (FELs) were therefore constructed by projecting trajectory data onto the plane defined by PC1–PC2 and subsequent application of Boltzmann inversion of state probabilities by employing Python-based scripts from Jupyter27. The resultant energy profiles revealed sharp-cut low-energy conformational basins corresponding to metastable states and thus revealed information regarding stable binding geometries and ligand-driven shifts upon dynamics within MAGL–ligand complexes.

Quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) analysis

All of the QM/MM calculations were then carried out using the PySCF 2.0 package and the qm.mm_charge module for embedding the electrostatic influence of the environment through fixed point charges29. It partitioned the system into a QM region (active site and ligand atoms) and an MM region (surrounding residues and solvent, in x, y, z, and q format) of the protein structure of interest. The QM region was treated using DFT at the B3LYP/def2-SVP level of theory, taking up restricted Kohn–Sham (RKS) formalism for closed-shell singlets and unrestricted Kohn–Sham (UKS) for open-shell species. DIIS extrapolation and level shifting were employed in the self-consistent field approach to achieve robust convergence. Upon electronic ground-state identification, analytic gradients of the QM atoms were calculated. At the same time, MM charges were frozen, and electron density and molecular orbital distributions were displayed through cube file creation. It provided a balanced description of active site electronic structure and enabled preservation of the electrostatic environment of the macromolecular framework.

QM/MM calculations were performed on representative structures extracted from the equilibrated phase of the MD trajectories, ensuring that the analysis reflects dynamically stable binding conformations. The selection of MD-equilibrated structures for QM/MM calculations was based on their ability to capture protein flexibility, solvent effects, and ligand-induced conformational changes, thereby providing a more realistic representation of the binding environment compared to static docking poses. Unlike re-docked poses, MD-equilibrated structures incorporate protein flexibility, solvent effects, and ligand-induced conformational adjustments within the binding pocket. This provides a more biologically relevant structural context for evaluating electronic interactions at the active site. Therefore, the use of MD-derived conformations allows for a more accurate and reliable assessment of ligand stability and interaction patterns at the quantum mechanical level.

Machine-learning-based ADMET analysis

These three compounds, along with reference, were additionally filtered by in silico ADMET (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, and Toxicity) profiling with the ADMET.ai platform (https://admet.ai.greenstonebio.com/), a predictive machine-learning platform developed by Greenstone Bio30. It employs machine learning models trained with large, annotated data to make predictions about key pharmacokinetic and toxicity parameters of interest to early-stage drug development. These parameters were predicted to be human intestinal absorption (HIA), blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability, CYP450 enzyme inhibition, hERG liability, and hepatotoxicity. Molecules predicted to bear unfavorable ADMET properties or to be a high toxicity risk were deprioritized for future consideration.

Result and discussion

Virtual screening

In this study, we utilized a high-throughput virtual screening protocol to sift through the Diverse-lib set of compounds with the MTiOpenScreen web-based docking server, with support for ligand docking within pre-designed binding pockets or throughout the whole protein surface when needed11,12,14. The X-ray structure of the human monoacylglycerol lipase (MAGL) (PDB code: 7L4W) was downloaded from the Protein Data Bank and prepared with a preprocessing workflow involving removal of the crystallographic artefacts, adding hydrogens, and optimisation of active site conformation to render it molecular docking compatible. The Diverse-lib set of compounds was cleaned and normalised using Open Babel to confirm correct formatting for files and application of drug-likeness filters by physicochemical criteria31. Structure validation ensured all molecules met the docking platform input criteria and contained conformation flexibility. The MTiOpenScreen workflow was executed and resulted in binding energy scores as shown in the Supplementary Table S1. The three highest-ranking compounds from this list were investigated further with intensive computational studies.

Density functional theory (DFT)

The DFT calculations provided insight into the electronic properties of the candidate ligands in comparison with the reference inhibitor (Fig. 1). Compound 17475070 exhibited a HOMO energy of − 0.254792 Hartree and a LUMO energy of − 0.054915 Hartree, corresponding to a HOMO–LUMO energy gap of 5.439 eV (Fig. 1 (a–b)). The large energy gap indicates high electronic stability and a reduced tendency toward chemical reactivity, suggesting a stable molecular framework. Compound 24326684 showed a HOMO energy of − 0.155300 Hartree and a LUMO energy of − 0.045419 Hartree, resulting in the smallest HOMO–LUMO gap of 2.990 eV (Fig. 1 (c–d)). This lower energy gap reflects increased electronic reactivity and potential adaptability within the binding environment. Compound 17387410 displayed a HOMO energy of − 0.212953 Hartree and a LUMO energy of − 0.083616 Hartree, with a corresponding energy gap of 3.519 eV (Fig. 1 (e–f)). This intermediate gap suggests moderate stability and reactivity compared to the other compounds. The reference inhibitor exhibited a HOMO energy of − 0.235774 Hartree and a LUMO energy of − 0.037251 Hartree, corresponding to a HOMO–LUMO gap of 5.402 eV (Fig. 1 (g–h)). The relatively large energy gap indicates a stable electronic configuration, comparable to that of compound 17475070. Overall, the variation in HOMO–LUMO energy gaps among the compounds reflects differences in electronic stability and reactivity, which may influence their interaction potential within the MAGL active site.

Redocking and intermolecular analysis

Redocking was done through application of the exact grid coordinates utilized in the initial virtual screening to retain methodological consistency, as shown in Fig. 2, generated (3D and 2D) by the Desmond academic software. Binding energy analysis revealed that there was a tendency for each of the three candidate ligands to exhibit stronger binding than the control inhibitor. Compound 17475070 had a docking of − 11.3 kcal/mol, 24326684 showed the highest binding affinity of − 12.2 kcal/mol, and 17387410 showed a constant value of − 11.2 kcal/mol. On the other hand, the control inhibitor showed a relatively weaker affinity of − 9.5 kcal/mol, verifying the superior binding ability of screened ligands.

The reference inhibitor formed hydrogen bonds with Ala51, Arg57, His212, and Met123, complemented by hydrophobic interactions with Leu148, Ala151, Ile179, Tyr194, Leu205, Leu213, Leu214, Val217, Leu241, Cys242, and Val270, which explained its moderate binding affinity (Fig. 2 (g-h)).

Structural analysis of the identified compounds revealed distinct heteroaromatic frameworks with varying degrees of rigidity and functional group distribution. Compound 17475070 possesses a fused heteroaromatic scaffold with extended π-conjugation, enabling stable π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding interactions, which is consistent with its superior stability observed during molecular dynamics simulations. Compound 24326684 exhibits a flexible diaryl architecture connected via an amine linker, allowing adaptive binding but resulting in moderate conformational fluctuations. In contrast, compound 17387410 is predominantly hydrophobic with limited polar functionality, leading to reduced interaction specificity and increased structural instability.

MD simulation

A 500 ns molecular dynamics simulation was carried out with an academic license version of Schrödinger Desmond module with the OPLS-2005 force field and TIP3P water model. Analyses after simulation with root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), and hydrogen-bond occupancy were performed to characterize conformation stability and dynamism of ligand–protein interactions.

RMSD analysis

The conformational stability of the ligand–MAGL complexes was analyzed using the protein and ligand RMSD throughout a 500 ns simulation (Fig. 3). In the case of the protein backbone RMSD (panels a, c, e, g), all of the complexes demonstrated fluctuations within a stable window of ~ 1.2–2.5 Å. The complexes with 17475070 (Fig. 3 (a-b)) and with the control inhibitor (g) proved most stable since there was minimal deviance in the RMSD values between ~ 1.2–2.0 Å throughout the entire trajectory, indicating there was minimal deviance in the protein backbone. The complex with 24326684 (Fig. 3 (c-d)) demonstrated slow drift with increasing RMSD from ~ 1.2 Å at the beginning until ~ 1.8 Å after 400 ns, and the complex with 17387410 (Fig. 3(e-f)) oscillated substantially with values near ~ 2.4 Å, indicating moderate deviance of the protein backbone upon binding of the ligand. For the ligand RMSD (panels b, d, f, h), evident patterns ensued. Ligands 17475070 (Fig. 3 (b)) and the reference inhibitor (Fig. 3 (h)) remained stably stabilized, with RMSDs between ~ 1.0–2.0 Å, indicating robust maintenance of the original binding pose. Ligand 24326684 (Fig. 3 (d)) was stabilized after ~ 100 ns, oscillating between ~ 4.0–5.5 Å, indicating some flexibility but stable binding in general. In contrast, the ligand 17387410 (Fig. 3 (f)) registered the highest oscillations, continuously increasing from ~ 2.5 Å until surpassing peaks of over 12 Å, indicating conformational reorganization/partial displacement out of the active site. In general, these data reassert that 17475070 and the reference inhibitor retained the most stable protein–ligand complexes. At the same time, 24326684 registered moderate but controlled oscillations, and 17387410 registered the lowest binding stability due to extensive ligand drift.

Protein RMSF analysis

The flexibility of individual residues in the MAGL–ligand complexes was examined using protein root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis (Fig. 4). For the 17475070 complex (Fig. 4 (a)), most residues exhibited fluctuations below 1.5 Å, with moderate peaks around loop regions near residue indices ~ 150 and ~ 290, suggesting localized flexibility while the core remained stable. The 24326684 complex (Fig. 4 (b)) showed a similar pattern, with average fluctuations under 1.5 Å, but displayed a more pronounced peak exceeding 3.0 Å near residue ~ 150, reflecting higher mobility in this loop region. In the case of the 17387410 complex (Fig. 4 (c)), the RMSF profile revealed slightly elevated fluctuations, with several peaks between 2.0 and 3.5 Å, again most prominent around the ~ 150 residue index, indicating increased flexibility relative to the other complexes. Conversely, the reference inhibitor complex (Fig. 4(d)) demonstrated a highly stable profile, with most residues fluctuating below 1.2 Å, apart from expected loop flexibility near ~ 150 and ~ 290, confirming rigid stabilization of the protein during ligand binding.

Ligand RMSF

The atomical mobility of the ligands of the bound forms was assessed with ligand RMSF profiles (Fig. 5). For 17475070 (Fig. 5 (a)), the majority of the ligand atoms oscillated below 1.0 Å, apart from terminal atoms that showed relatively elevated deviations of ~ 2.0–2.5 Å, indicating an essentially rigid and stable binding orientation. The complex of 24326684 (Fig. 5 (b)) exhibited moderate motion with oscillations between 1.0 and 2.2 Å in multiple atoms, corresponding to elevated internal motion while showing an overall stable binding orientation. The molecule 17387410 (Fig. 5(c)) exhibited, in simulation, the strongest atomic motion. RMSF values were highly variable, ranging from an average of 1.5 Å to nearly 6.0 Å. This degree of motion indicates significant conformational shifts and reduced stabilization of the MAGL binding pocket. The control inhibitor (Fig. 5(d)) exhibited the most restrained atomic fluctuations, and RMSF values were restrained between 0.5 and 1.2 Å. This limited range of motion indicates strong retention at the active site and suggests it to be the most stable reference among the studied ligands.

Intermolecular interaction analysis after MD simulation

As a supplement to interaction fraction information in Fig. 6, individual post-simulation interaction maps were built for each ligand–MAGL complex (Fig. 7). These graphs provide details on the most abundant types and frequency of intermolecular contacts holding each ligand in an active site conformation.

In compound 17475070 (Fig. 6(a)), an interaction network of high density and diversity was observed. It consisted of several hydrogen bonds through water bridges involving critical residues, including Ala51, Asn152, Arg202, Tyr194, and Cys201. It also included hydrophobic interactions involving residues Ile179 and Ala203. The richness of this contact profile in this example aligns well with increased conformational stability observed in RMSD and RMSF outcomes.

On the other hand, compound 24326684 (Fig. 6(b)) formed fewer stabilizing interactions. Since it did have hydrogen bonding to Asn152 and backbone interactions to Leu205 and Ile179, but interaction numbers and, more significantly, strengths were relatively low, this smaller interaction footprint would dictate a moderate binding strength and elevated conformational motion, as can be witnessed in its dynamic simulations’ behavior. The 17387410 complex (Fig. 6 (c)) was stabilized with one stable hydrogen bond with Ser176, supported by hydrophobic interactions with Leu205, Leu213, and Ala164. The low hydrogen-bonding profile is in agreement with its large ligand RMSD and RMSF values, indicative of less stable binding. In contrast, the reference inhibitor (Fig. 6 (d)) exhibited an even and compact network of interactions, with hydrogen bonds with Ala51, Met123, Arg57, and His272, as well as hydrophobic interactions in the neighborhood of Leu241. The numerous water bridges served to stabilize binding as well. Overall, Figs. 6 and 7 indicate that 17475070 provided the largest and most stable network of interactions, the reference inhibitor disclosed a stable but compact set of contacts, 24326684 maintained a reduced number of but functional support interactions, and 17387410 demonstrated sparse stabilizing interactions in agreement with its less stable stability scores.

SASA analysis

Solvent accessibility surface area (SASA) was also assessed to estimate the extent of solvent exposure and structural plasticity of the complexes33. As shown in Fig. 9, the 17475070 complex showed relatively stable values of SASA in ~ 70–110 Å², revealing compactness and efficient shielding of the solvent (Fig. 9 (a)). The 24326684 complex showed moderate fluctuation, exhibiting values between ~ 60–140 Å², and nonetheless exhibited general stability (Fig. 9 (b)). Conversely, the 17387410 complex showed the most considerable fluctuation, varying between ~ 60–180 Å², which reflected larger exposure of the solvent and thus correspondingly weaker conformational stability (Fig. 9(c)). The control inhibitor maintained values around ~ 70–120 Å², and closely reflected the steady profile of 17475070. Together, these data suggest that 17475070 and the control inhibitor privileged the best solvent-protected conformations, and conform with their steady binding behaviors (Fig. 9 (d)).

FEL analysis

Their conformational stability was further interrogated by 3D free energy landscape (FEL) analysis, projected onto the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) (Fig. 11). The 17475070 complex exhibited a compact and deep energy basin featuring thin ridges, characteristic of a single dominant metastable state and few conformation transitions, consonant with its highly stable RMSD and low RMSF values (Fig. 11 (a)). With the 24326684 complex, numerous shallow minima linked by low barriers became apparent, forming a corridor-like surface, and this hints at controlled motion and adaptive sampling of highly interconnected substates (Fig. 11 (b)). The 17387410 complex exhibited the most distributed energy distribution, featuring numerous isolated basins and larger barriers, characteristic of frequent conformation transitions and sampling of dispersed conditions, consonant with its elevated ligand RMSD, RMSF, and SASA profiles (Fig. 11 (c)). By contrast, the reference inhibitor occupied a moderately deep and centrally located basin and few attendant minima, consonant with a dominant stable form supported by occasional excursions (Fig. 11 (d)). Together, the FEL analysis offered additional validation of the deduced stability hierarchy from other structural measures, pointing toward 17475070 as the most conformationally stable binder, followed by the reference inhibitor and 24326684. At the same time, 17387410 demonstrated relatively low stability due to marked conformational plasticity.

QM/MM analysis

The QM/MM setup for all ligand–protein systems was carefully examined to ensure accurate representation of electronic interactions at the binding interface. This was performed to refine the understanding of protein-ligand complex. As illustrated in Fig. 13, the ligands were consistently assigned to the QM region (red spheres), while the surrounding protein residues formed the MM region (blue cloud). This clear and well-defined partitioning demonstrates the suitability of the computational framework, enabling quantum-level precision for ligand interactions while preserving efficiency in describing the protein environment. Notably, across all four systems—17475070 (Fig. 13 (a)), 24326684 (Fig. 13 (b)), 17387410 (Fig. 13 (c)), and the reference inhibitor (Fig. 13 (d))—the QM region remained clearly localized within the protein environment, indicating consistent QM/MM Model construction for subsequent hybrid calculations. The total QM/MM energies obtained for the systems are presented as a measure electronic characteristics of each optimized complex. Compound 17475070 exhibited a QM/MM total energy of − 2216.68770930 Hartree, indicating a highly stabilized complex within the MAGL binding pocket. In comparison, compound 17387410 showed a total energy of − 1332.07475081 Hartree, suggesting moderate stabilization, while compound 24326684 displayed a QM/MM energy of − 1167.14949795 Hartree, reflecting relatively lower stabilization among the candidate ligands. The reference inhibitor demonstrated a QM/MM total energy of − 1705.7822144 Hartree, which lies between the values observed for compound 17475070 and the other candidates. Although absolute QM/MM energies are influenced by system size and composition, the observed values suggests that compound 17475070 forms an energetically stable complex within the active site. These results provide additional support for its potential as a promising MAGL inhibitor. Altogether, the QM/MM results further complement the trends observed in docking and molecular dynamics analyses by providing an additional electronic level perspective.

ADMET analysis

The machine learning–driven ADMET predictions provided a comparative view of the three candidate ligands alongside the control inhibitor (Supplementary Table S2)15. All compounds satisfied Lipinski’s rule of five, with molecular weights between 363 and 406 Da and logP values ranging from 3.7 to 5.9—features that are generally favorable for oral absorption.

Compound 17475070 emerged with the most balanced pharmacokinetic profile. It combined moderate lipophilicity (logP 3.79) with a relatively high polar surface area (80.5 Å²), translating into excellent predicted intestinal absorption (HIA 0.99). Importantly, it showed a good likelihood of penetrating the blood–brain barrier (0.74) and was predicted to have the longest systemic half-life (~ 91 h). The main concern flagged was its strong interaction with CYP isoforms, which may raise metabolic stability and drug–drug interaction issues.

Compound 24326684 was the most hydrophobic of the set (logP 5.92) and had the smallest topological polar surface area (51.8 Å²). This combination points to strong passive permeability but, at the same time, raises red flags: higher estimated carcinogenicity (0.47), weaker clearance, and overall safety concerns.

Compound 17387410 showed a more moderate profile, with logP of 4.08 and predicted oral absorption comparable to 17475070 (HIA 0.99). It also carried the lowest mutagenicity risk (0.13), making it the safest from that perspective. However, its limited BBB penetration score (0.31) and relatively short predicted half-life suggest poor systemic persistence, which could limit therapeutic effectiveness unless formulated for targeted delivery.

By comparison, the control inhibitor presented a consistently favorable ADMET signature. It combined good oral absorption with the strongest BBB penetration potential (0.94), while also displaying relatively low carcinogenicity and hepatotoxicity risks. This makes it a suitable reference standard for MAGL-targeted studies.

Taken together, these results highlight distinct strengths and weaknesses among the candidate molecules: compound 17475070 stands out for stability and overall drug-likeness; compound 24326684 demonstrates high permeability but requires optimization to address safety liabilities; and compound 17387410, while safest with respect to mutagenicity, appears less viable due to weaker systemic stability.

Discussion

This study employed a multi-scale computational approach to explore potential inhibitors of monoacylglycerol lipase (MAGL), an enzyme with relevance in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), particularly within the metabolic reprogramming landscape seen in MASLD-associated HCC, where dysregulated lipid metabolism contributes significantly to tumor development and progression 34,35,36.

Initial virtual screening of over 99,000 compounds through MTiOpenScreen enabled high-throughput filtration and prioritization based on docking affinity and pose geometry. From this, three ligands—17475070, 24326684, and 17387410—were shortlisted. These candidates were further investigated through a combination of quantum mechanical and dynamic simulations. Among them, compound 24326684 exhibited the strongest binding energy (− 12.2 kcal/mol), although all three showed favorable interaction patterns, indicating potential as pharmacophores. Computationally identified MAGL inhibitors, particularly benzylpiperidine derivatives such as compound 13, have been reported to exhibit binding free energies in the range of approximately − 6 to − 9 kcal/mol, as estimated using MM-PBSA approaches. Other related derivatives, including compounds 7 and 8, showed weaker binding energies (~ − 6.2 kcal/mol), correlating with other findings37. In comparison, the compounds identified in the present study demonstrate stronger docking affinities (− 11.2 to − 12.2 kcal/mol), suggesting enhanced binding potential within the MAGL active site. This comparison provides additional context for the observed potency of the proposed ligands.

Electronic structure analysis via density functional theory (DFT) revealed differences in kinetic stability. Compound 17475070 showed the widest HOMO–LUMO gap (~ 5.44 eV), suggesting high electronic stability and low reactivity, which is advantageous for drug-like properties38. Conversely, compound 24326684 had a narrower gap (~ 2.99 eV), indicating higher reactivity and possible bioactivation—potentially useful in selective targeting but also raising stability concerns. Visual inspection of frontier molecular orbitals allowed identification of chemically sensitive regions that can be targeted in future optimization.

Re-docking experiments using AutoDock Vina on DFT-optimized conformers confirmed improved binding of all three compounds compared to the control inhibitor20. Compound 17475070 formed a robust interaction network, notably engaging Tyr194 and Asn152—key residues within the MAGL active site—suggesting its capacity for competitive inhibition. Several MAGL inhibitors have been previously reported, including carbamate-based irreversible inhibitors such as JZL184, KML29, and MJN110, which act by covalently modifying the catalytic serine residue within the active site. While these compounds exhibit high potency, their clinical application is often limited by issues related to selectivity, off-target effects, and irreversible enzyme inhibition39,40. In addition, urea-based inhibitors have been developed as reversible alternatives, although they generally display moderate potency and distinct pharmacokinetic limitations39. In contrast, the compounds identified in the present study do not belong to these classical scaffold classes. Instead, they exhibit heteroaromatic frameworks and appear to engage MAGL through non-covalent interactions. This distinction suggests that the identified molecules may represent novel chemotypes with alternative binding mechanisms, potentially offering advantages in terms of selectivity and reduced toxicity associated with irreversible inhibition.

Molecular dynamics simulations (500 ns) provided insights into the long-term stability of the ligand–protein complexes. RMSD analysis confirmed that 17475070 and the control inhibitor adopted stable backbone and ligand conformations and revealed minimum structure drift. Minimum fluctuation in RMSD and radius of gyration (Rg) also offered added support for the hypothesis of compact, energetically favorable binding conformations. Throughout the molecular dynamic simulations, both compound 17475070 and the control inhibitor maintained relatively stable positioning within the MAGL active site, as indicated by consistently low RMSD values. These results imply that neither compound experienced significant structural drift during the trajectory. Complementary analysis of the radius of gyration (Rg) supported this observation, with both ligands displaying compact configurations typically associated with energetically stable complexes. In contrast, compound 17387410 exhibited wider RMSD fluctuations, hinting at less consistent binding behavior and a higher probability of conformational shifts or partial unbinding.

Further insights were drawn from the RMSF analysis, which measures residue-level flexibility. For 17475070, fluctuations were minimal and mostly confined to flexible loop regions areas of the protein that generally tolerate movement without affecting structural integrity. This suggests that the ligand was stably embedded within the binding pocket. On the other hand, 17387410 showed elevated RMSF values across multiple regions, implying weaker anchoring and potential solvent exposure, which may correlate with reduced binding efficiency. A comparative analysis across docking and molecular dynamics simulations shows that compound 17475070 exhibits the most stable interaction profile, with consistent hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts involving key residues such as Asn152, Tyr194, and Ile179. In contrast, compound 24326684 relies more on hydrophobic interactions with fewer persistent hydrogen bonds, resulting in greater flexibility. Compound 17387410 displays the weakest interaction network, consistent with its lower stability. The reference inhibitor shows a stable but less extensive interaction profile. These findings indicate that binding stability is governed by interaction persistence rather than docking affinity alone.

At the global motion level, PCA and FEL mapping provided a broader view of system behavior during the simulation26. Compound 17475070 largely occupied a single, deep energy minimum, consistent with a stable and energetically favorable conformation. In contrast, both 24326684 and 17387410 sampled broader regions of the energy landscape, moving among multiple shallow minima—indicative of greater conformational variability and potentially less stable interactions with the target. When simulation-derived low-energy conformations were superimposed onto the original docking poses, all compounds retained the core geometry, with RMSD values under 1.3 Å. Notably, 17475070 showed the least deviation (~ 1.07 Å), reinforcing its structural consistency during the simulation. Further, QM/MM analysis indicated differences in electronic stabilization among the complexes, with compound 17475070 showing the most favorable energy profile within the catalytic pocket. However, only compound 17475070 demonstrated optimal charge polarization within the catalytic pocket, aligning with the electrostatic requirements of MAGL’s enzymatic function. This attribute may be key to its effectiveness as a pharmacological inhibitor.

Pharmacokinetic profiles were evaluated using machine learning-based ADMET predictions. Compound 17475070 demonstrated a well-balanced profile with moderate lipophilicity, favorable intestinal absorption, acceptable BBB permeability, and an extended predicted half-life (~ 91 h), supporting its suitability for systemic administration. However, the predicted interaction with CYP450 enzymes suggests possible metabolic liabilities, including altered drug metabolism and potential drug–drug interactions. These aspects warrant further investigation through dedicated metabolic and CYP inhibition studies. Despite these concerns, the compound remains promising and could be further optimized through rational structural modifications or formulation-based strategies such as prodrug design or nano formulation. Compound 24326684 also showed high permeability but raised carcinogenicity concerns, warranting further toxicogenomic validation. Compound 17387410, while least mutagenic, demonstrated poor systemic exposure and limited BBB penetration, restricting its standalone therapeutic potential unless delivered through targeted hepatic systems.

Recent studies indicate that many currently available MAGL inhibitors, including widely studied compounds such as JZL184 and ABX-1431, are predominantly irreversible and may present limitations related to safety, off-target effects, and pharmacokinetic control. Irreversible inhibition can decouple pharmacodynamics from pharmacokinetics and has been associated with adverse effects such as cannabinoid receptor desensitization and potential toxicity41,42,45. The experimental studies further support the role of MAGL as a metabolic driver in HCC, where its inhibition has been shown to reduce tumor cell migration and invasion by disrupting NF-κB-mediated epithelial–mesenchymal transition and pro-tumorigenic lipid signaling pathways involving LPA and prostaglandins7,43. Additionally, clinically evaluated MAGL inhibitors such as ABX-1431 (elcubragistat) have demonstrated favorable safety profiles in Phase I trials, highlighting the translational potential of targeting MAGL, although challenges associated with irreversible inhibition have shifted current efforts toward reversible inhibitor development44.

In contrast, newer-generation MAGL inhibitors are being developed with improved pharmacokinetic and safety profiles, including reversible inhibitors and optimized scaffolds showing better ADME characteristics and reduced toxicity. In this context, the balanced ADMET profile observed for compound 17475070, particularly its moderate lipophilicity and predicted systemic stability, suggests potential advantages over earlier MAGL inhibitors, although further experimental validation is required. Although machine learning–based ADMET predictions provide valuable early insights, they are inherently limited in their ability to fully capture complex pharmacokinetic and toxicological behaviours. In particular, the accuracy of such models may vary across different endpoints, especially for toxicity-related predictions that depend on multifactorial biological processes. Therefore, the ADMET results presented in this study should be interpreted as preliminary indicators rather than definitive assessments. Experimental validation will be essential to confirm these predictions and to establish the safety profile of the identified compounds.

Mechanistically, inhibition of MAGL disrupts lipid signaling pathways essential for tumor progression by reducing the availability of free fatty acids and arachidonic acid, which serve as precursors for pro-tumorigenic mediators such as prostaglandins and lysophosphatidic acid. This disruption can impair cancer cell proliferation, migration, and survival, particularly in lipid-driven cancers such as HCC. While the computational results indicate promising inhibitory potential, it is important to recognize that these findings are based on in silico predictions. Therefore, experimental validation will be essential to confirm the biological relevance of compound 17475070. In particular, enzymatic assays targeting MAGL activity would help quantify its inhibitory potency, while cell-based studies in hepatocellular carcinoma models could provide insights into its antiproliferative effects and impact on lipid signaling pathways. Furthermore, such studies would enable evaluation of cytotoxicity, selectivity, and potential off-target effects, thereby supporting the translational applicability of the proposed compound. Overall, these combined computational analyses make a strong case for compound 17475070 as a leading candidate for MAGL inhibition. Its consistently favourable performance in electronic structure, dynamic behaviour, and pharmacokinetic predictions suggests significant therapeutic potential—especially in lipid-driven liver cancers such as MASLD-associated HCC.

Conclusion

HCC, particularly when associated with MASLD-related metabolic alterations, remains a pressing clinical concern with limited targeted options. This study explored the therapeutic potential of MAGL inhibition through a fully integrated computational strategy.

Three candidate inhibitors were identified, with compound 17475070 showing the most promise. Docking studies demonstrated strong and specific binding to the active site, and DFT analysis confirmed its electronic stability. Long-term molecular dynamics simulations supported its structural stability, while PCA and FEL analysis confirmed the persistence of a low-energy, dominant binding conformation. QM/MM simulations reinforced its compatibility with the enzyme’s active site electrostatics. Finally, machine learning-based ADMET predictions indicated favorable pharmacokinetics, including good oral absorption and extended half-life.

Taken together, the results support compound 17475070 as a high-potential lead molecule for MAGL-targeted treatment in HCC; however, further experimental studies will be required to validate its efficacy, safety, and therapeutic potential in hepatocellular carcinoma. The study also highlights the power of integrated computational workflows in accelerating early-stage drug discovery for metabolically complex cancers.
