Serum magnesium and risk of hyperglycemia, type 2 diabetes, and prediabetes: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of observational studies
Authors: Alireza Rashki, Zahra Moradmand, Parvane Saneei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeveral studies have examined the association between serum magnesium and hyperglycemia, but findings have been inconsistent. Some prospective cohort studies report inverse associations, while others show no significant relationship, and cross-sectional studies have yielded mixed effect sizes. This systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis examines the association between serum magnesium and hyperglycemia.MethodsA comprehensive and systematic search was undertaken utilizing PubMed, Scopus, ISI Web of Science, and Google Scholar, extending up to January 2026. Epidemiologic studies presenting relative risks or odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals regarding serum magnesium and hyperglycemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and prediabetes were included. The GRADE approach was utilized to evaluate the body of evidence.ResultsA total of 24 studies (7 cohorts, 16 cross-sectional, 2 cross-sectional analyses of cohort baseline data, and 1 case-control) were included. In cohort studies including 46,190 participants and 4,330 cases, negative associations were observed in the highest versus lowest category of serum magnesium with hyperglycemia (RR = 0.67; 95%CI: 0.51, 0.78) and T2DM (RR = 0.67; 95%CI: 0.56, 0.82); this relation was not significant in the case of prediabetes (RR = 0.69; 95%CI: 0.42, 1.02). In non-cohort studies with 40,834 participants and 8,161 cases, reductions in odds were observed for hyperglycemia (OR = 0.43, 95%CI: 0.32, 0.57), T2DM (OR = 0.34, 95%CI: 0.24, 0.46), and prediabetes (OR = 0.53, 95%CI: 0.36, 0.76). In addition, each 0.20 mmol/L increase in serum magnesium level was associated with lower risk of T2DM (RR = 0.92; 95%CI: 0.87, 0.97) in cohort studies and lower odds of hyperglycemia (OR = 0.79; 95%CI: 0.72, 0.86) and T2DM (OR = 0.77; 95%CI: 0.69, 0.87) in cross-sectional studies. In non-linear dose-response analysis, increasing serum magnesium concentration from deficient to sufficient values (> 0.85 mmol/L) was associated with a decreasing trend in hyperglycemia and T2DM risk.ConclusionThere were significant inverse dose-response associations between serum magnesium and hyperglycemia and T2DM. The association with prediabetes was weak and uncertain.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41043-026-01415-6.Keywords: Serum magnesium, Hyperglycemia, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Prediabetes, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Hyperglycemia, an abnormal increase in blood glucose, is a key indicator of metabolic disorders, including prediabetes and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [1]. These diseases can lead to serious macro- and micro-complications such as nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy, and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) [2] and impose high costs on patients and healthcare systems [3]. Prevalence of hyperglycemia is increasing worldwide, and it is predicted that the mortality rate related to hyperglycemia and its complications will reach 204 per 100,000 people by 2050 [4].

Risk factors for hyperglycemia and T2DM include obesity, inactivity, family history, aging, and poor diet [5–7]. Also, the direct and indirect roles of micronutrients in preventing oxidative stress and diabetes have been discussed [8]. In this context, some previous studies showed that magnesium (Mg) is considered a key mineral for regulating glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity [9, 10].

Although several previous observational or epidemiologic investigations have examined the association between serum magnesium and hyperglycemia or T2DM, there were inconsistencies between reported findings. Some cohort studies observed a risk reduction between higher levels of serum magnesium and risk of hyperglycemia and T2DM [11, 12]. However, some others did not affirm such a relation with T2DM [13, 14]. Many cross-sectional studies have observed a lower odds between the highest versus lowest serum magnesium levels and hyperglycemia, T2DM, and prediabetes [15–20], but this association has not been confirmed in others [21–23]. To the best of our knowledge, no previous dose-response meta-analysis has quantified the association between serum magnesium and hyperglycemia or prediabetes across observational studies. Moreover, a simple comparison of categories cannot reveal the shape of the relationship, identify potential thresholds of benefit, or determine whether the risk reduction follows a linear or non-linear pattern. A dose-response meta-analysis addresses these gaps by modelling the continuous gradient of risk across magnesium concentrations, thereby providing clinically beneficial information on optimal ranges. Therefore, we aimed to (1) summarize the association between serum magnesium and hyperglycemia, T2DM, and prediabetes, and (2) evaluate the dose-response relationship.

Methods and materials

Search strategy

A comprehensive and systematic review of all scientific articles published up to January 2026 was conducted in the electronic databases (Scopus, PubMed, ISI Web of Science, and a hand search in Google Scholar), without restrictions in publication date or language. The comprehensive search methodology is elaborated in Supplementary Table 1. To mitigate the risk of overlooking any pertinent articles, we executed a manual review of the reference lists from the relevant studies. The systematic search was independently undertaken by two researchers (A.R and Z.M). The main reviewer (P.S) was consulted in order to resolve any discrepancies. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) standard was followed to report this meta-analysis and systematic review (Supplementary Table 2). The study protocol was registered at Prospero (http://www.crd.york.ac.uk/Prospero).

Inclusion criteria

As shown in Table 1, articles were considered eligible, if they met the subsequent criteria: (1) were population-based observational investigations including prospective cohort, case-cohort, or cross-sectional studies; (2) reported serum magnesium as the exposure and hyperglycemia or T2DM or prediabetes as the outcome of interest; (3) provided relative risks (RRs), hazard ratios (HRs), or odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), or represented adequate data to compute these values.

Exclusion criteria

Details of more relevant investigations that were excluded are provided in Supplementary Table 3. Studies were excluded if they: (1) did not provide HR, RR, OR for the outcomes; (2) only provided correlation coefficient (r) between the exposure and outcomes; (3) considered Mg deficiency or low serum magnesium as the outcome of interest; (4) only studied populations with diabetes or prediabetes.

Data extraction: The following information was taken from all eligible articles: first author’s last name, publication date, investigation design, country or location of the study, health status of individuals, age range or mean age of participants at the start of the study, gender, sample size and number of cases, method of measuring serum magnesium, outcome definition, comparison exposure categories, fully-adjusted ORs, HRs or RRs with 95% CIs, and adjusted variables. Two reviewers (A.R and Z.M) were independently engaged in obtaining the essential data. The extraction process was overseen by the principal investigator (P.S).

Quality assessment of studies

As described in Supplementary Table 4, quality assessment of each included paper and the degree of certainty of the obtained evidence were respectively done based on the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) and the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) [24]. Details of these assessments are displayed in Supplementary Tables 5 and 6.

Statistical analysis

Log RRs/ORs and their standard errors (SEs) were calculated by using reported ORs/RRs and their 95% CIs from publications that reported the lowest vs. the highest serum level. When effect sizes were reported per one SD increase of circulating magnesium, the log OR/RR and its SE were multiplied by 2.18 to rescale estimates for the highest vs. the lowest tertile, based on previously published methods [25, 26]. To determine the overall effect size, a random effects model that accounted for heterogeneity between studies was used. Cochrane’s Q test and I-squared were used to assess heterogeneity between studies, and heterogeneity was considered high when I² exceeded 75%. To determine the causes of heterogeneity, subgroup analysis and meta-regression based on different variables were performed. The fixed effects model was utilized to assess heterogeneity among subgroups. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to determine whether the overall estimate was affected by a specific study. To conduct a comprehensive evaluation of potential publication bias, a multifaceted approach was used, which included the examination of the asymmetry in Begg’s funnel plot, coupled with the application of the statistical methodologies known as Begg’s test and Egger’s test, both of which are specifically designed to detect biases that may affect the validity of the findings. As described in Supplementary Tables 4, to perform a dose-response analysis, the previous approach proposed by Greenland and Longnecker [27] and Orsini et al. [28] was used. Dose-response estimates for circulating magnesium were standardized to a 0.20 mmol/L increment (0.486 mg/dL), corresponding to the unweighted median difference between the highest and lowest categories of the included investigations [29]. Limited cubic splines (with three points at fixed percentiles of 10%, 50%, and 90% of the distribution) were used to examine the potential nonlinear dose-response relationship between blood magnesium and hyperglycemia, type 2 diabetes, and prediabetes. STATA version 14.0 (STATA Crop, College Station, TX, USA) was used to perform the statistical analyses. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Literature search

As part of the preliminary investigation, a total of 11,888 scientific articles were identified for screening purposes (Fig. 1). After excluding those articles that did not meet our predetermined inclusion criteria and removing duplicates, 54 articles were found to be potentially relevant. A thorough review of the full-text articles led to the exclusion of an additional 30 studies based on the specified inclusion and exclusion criteria. Ultimately, 24 studies (7 prospective cohort studies, 16 cross-sectional studies, 2 other cross-sectionals on baseline data of cohorts, and one case-control study) were eligible for inclusion in this systematic review and meta-analysis.

Characteristics of cohort studies

Table 2 contains data from 7 eligible cohort studies published between 1999 and 2024. A total of 46,190 participants took part in these studies, with a minimum average age of 20 years. A total of 4,330 cases of hyperglycemia, diabetes, and prediabetes were reported in these studies. However, 2 studies did not report the number of cases [14, 30]. Follow-up duration varied across studies and ranged from 5.6 to 22 years. Geographically, one study was conducted in Asia, 3 in Europe, and 3 others in the America. The studies included both men and women in their participant populations [30]. Two methods have been used to define the assessment of serum magnesium. Most studies (n = 5) used the calorimetric method; only one study used the metallochromic method [13], and another one used the atomic absorption spectrophotometry method [31]. The definition of hyperglycemia in studies was a fasting blood sugar (FBS) greater than 100 mg/dL. T2DM was diagnosed with blood glucose values [FBS ≥ 126 mg/dL, non-FBS ≥ 200 mg/dL, or use of blood glucose-lowering medication] or International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision codes representing diabetes mellitus (250.0 through 250.9) [31]. Prediabetes was defined as FBS ≥ 110 and < 126 mg/dL or 2-h post-glucose > 140 mg/dL and < 200 mg/dL or non-FBS between 138 and 200 mg/dL. The reported RR or HRs associated with hyperglycemia and diabetes and prediabetes ranged from 0.39 to 1.11. All relevant studies (n = 7) adjusted for age and sex. In addition, four studies considered the use of diuretics. Likewise, Body mass index (BMI), smoking, physical activity (PA), alcohol consumption, and family history of diabetes were identified as potential confounding variables in three studies. All studies used serum samples for magnesium levels. Also, all studies were conducted on general populations. Out of 7 studies, 6 studies addressed T2DM, 2 studies focused on prediabetes, and 3 studies on hyperglycemia. For these cohort studies, the ROBINS-I tool identified low risk of bias in many domains, especially in participant selection, exposure assessment, misclassification during follow-up, selective reporting of the results, and outcome measurement (Supplementary Table 5). Nevertheless, the overall risk of bias tended to be classified as moderate, driven largely by potential biases in confounding adjustments and missing data. These findings could reflect the strict requirements of ROBINS-I for minimizing bias in observational study designs.

Characteristics of non-cohort studies: Table 3 shows the information from 16 cross-sectional studies, 2 other cross-sectional studies on baseline data of cohorts, and 1 case-control study published between 2005 and 2024 with 40,834 participants and 8,161 cases (four studies did not report the number of cases) [19, 22, 23, 32]. More Details of these investigations are provided in Supplementary Table 7. Out of all non-cohort studies, 9 were addressing T2DM, 2 studies focused on prediabetes, and 11 studies on hyperglycemia. Using the ROBINS-I tool, most of these studies demonstrated a low risk of bias across several domains, such as outcome measurement, exposure assessment, selection of participants, and results reporting (Supplementary Table 5). However, the overall risk of bias was often rated as moderate or serious, primarily due to challenges in considering confounding factors and the lack of information in case of missing data. These findings could highlight the inherent limitations of cross-sectional studies when they have been evaluated under the rigorous criteria of ROBINS-I.

Discussion

Our main finding was that higher serum magnesium was consistently associated with lower risk of hyperglycemia and T2DM, with a clear dose-response pattern. In cohort studies, this association was significant in hyperglycemia and T2DM. In non-cohort studies, the association was significant in the case of hyperglycemia, T2DM, and prediabetes. Linear dose-response analysis in cohort studies has shown that each 0.20 mmol/L increase in serum magnesium was associated with an 8% reduction in T2DM risk. Also, non-cohort studies have shown that each 0.20 mmol/L increase in serum magnesium was associated with a 23% reduction in odds of T2DM and a 21% reduction in odds of hyperglycemia. A nonlinear dose-response analysis in cohort studies revealed a sharply decreasing slope of the curve for T2DM when serum magnesium was between 0.85 and 1.15 mmol/L. In non-cohort studies, the lowest odds of T2DM were observed in a serum magnesium level of 0.95 mmol/L. The lowest odds of hyperglycemia have also been seen in a magnesium level of 1.30 mmol/L, which is in the normal range of serum magnesium concentrations. The certainty of the obtained evidence was high for hyperglycemia and T2DM in both cohort and cross-sectional studies.

Hyperglycemia and T2DM can threaten an individual’s health. A wide spectrum of chronic and acute complications of hyperglycemia and T2DM, such as CVDs, neuropathy [2], and ketoacidosis [33], is well known. We have discovered that elevated serum magnesium levels can lower the risk of hyperglycemia and may prevent related complications. Several vegetables like green leafy vegetables, spinach, and cabbage, as well as nuts, legumes, whole grains, and some fruits like bananas, are rich in magnesium, and their consumption can be beneficial for the general population [34–36]. Serum magnesium levels may be reduced in some diseases, including gastrointestinal disorders, renal failure, endocrine disorders, and metabolic stress [37]. A meta-analysis review found that persistent alcohol use correlates with significantly reduced circulating concentrations of both total and ionized magnesium [38]. In contrast, a previous study has documented a positive and effective role of dark chocolate on serum magnesium [39]. Although diets rich in magnesium, such as those containing green leafy vegetables, nuts, legumes, and whole grains, align with general dietary guidelines, it is important to recognize that the evidence comes from observational studies. While the included cohort studies provide stronger support for a temporal and potentially causal relationship than cross-sectional designs (which remain susceptible to residual confounding and reverse causation). Therefore, suggestions such as increasing magnesium-rich food consumption or population-level dietary policies in this regard in order to directly prevent hyperglycemia, type 2 diabetes, or prediabetes, should be considered preliminary.

Some prior meta-analyses have examined the linkage between magnesium and blood sugar levels [40, 41]. A 2020 dose-response meta-analysis of dietary magnesium intake found that each 100–150 mg/day increment was associated with a 22% lower risk of T2DM [41]. A meta-analysis of clinical trials showed that oral magnesium supplementation improved insulin resistance, and longer-term supplementation reduced glucose levels [40]. Regarding serum magnesium, a 2017 meta-analysis on 4 cohort studies reported a 36% lower risk of T2DM for the highest versus lowest category, although the linear trend per 0.10 mmol/L increase was not significant [42]. In addition, a comprehensive meta-analysis examining the relations between serum magnesium levels and all-cause or cardiovascular mortality in people with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD) found that hypomagnesemia was significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality in these patients [43]. Another meta-analysis conducted in 2021 revealed that individuals diagnosed with prediabetes exhibited markedly reduced serum magnesium levels in comparison to their healthy counterparts [44]. In addition, a meta-analysis on observational studies examined the association between serum magnesium levels and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) revealed that serum magnesium levels were significantly lower in individuals with GDM compared to healthy individuals (standard mean difference (SMD): − 0.35; 95%CI: −0.62, − 0.07, P = 0.013) [45].

According to the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) health definition, a normal serum magnesium level is between 0.85 and 1.10 mmol/L (1.70 and 2.20 mg/dL). Our analyses revealed that in the case of T2DM, the decreasing trend of the odds ratio started at a serum magnesium level of 0.75 mmol/L and reached its lowest value at 0.95 mmol/L, while values above this serum magnesium level were not associated with more risk reduction. In the case of hyperglycemia, the risk was sharply decreased between 0.80 and 1.30 mmol/L, and the lowest risk of hyperglycemia was observed at serum magnesium levels of 1.30 mmol/L. However, these specific threshold values should be interpreted with caution. They represent point estimates derived from a limited number of studies in the dose-response analysis and are not intended as definitive clinical cut‑offs. Reference ranges for serum magnesium vary across laboratories, measurement methods, and populations. From a clinical perspective, it is important to note that short‑term oral magnesium supplementation (⁓12 weeks) raises serum magnesium by only 0.04–0.08 mmol/L on average [46], which is substantially smaller than the 0.20 mmol/L increment examined in this meta‑analysis. This suggests that the larger differences in serum magnesium observed across observational studies likely reflect sustained dietary patterns, rather than effects achievable through brief supplementations. Therefore, for individuals at risk of hyperglycemia or T2DM, emphasis should be placed on magnesium‑rich diets alongside efforts to improve glycemic control and manage underlying conditions that deplete magnesium, rather than on a routine supplementation approach.

Regarding the outcomes examined in the cohort studies, we did not reach a significant relationship for prediabetes, maybe due to the small number of eligible studies (n = 2 studies) or a small number of participants (n = 8,026 participants). This indicates that the current evidence was insufficient to draw firm conclusions regarding prediabetes, and the observed effect should be interpreted with caution. In non-cohort studies, there was a significant association in all three outcomes, observed in a large number of participants. Non-cohort and especially cross-sectional studies have reported large reductions in odds ratios in the case of all three outcomes of interest. However, these reductions might be overestimated. For example, the odds of type 2 diabetes were 66% lower in the highest versus lowest magnesium category in cross‑sectional studies (OR = 0.34), compared with a 33% risk reduction observed in cohort studies (RR = 0.67). This marked discrepancy was also evident for hyperglycemia (57% vs. 33% reduction). This exaggeration might stem from inherent limitations and greater biases present in cross-sectional studies, such as their inability to establish causality and their reliance on snapshot data rather than longitudinal evidence. On the other hand, cohort studies, which tracked individuals over time and could be better suited for assessing causal relationships, have reported effect sizes that were more reliable and accurately reflected the true relationship between variables. This issue could highlight the importance of study design in ensuring the validity and accuracy of research findings. Substantial heterogeneity was observed in cross‑sectional analyses for hyperglycemia and T2DM. After excluding case-control studies and studies conducted on children and the elderly, there was still high heterogeneity. Despite these explanations, residual heterogeneity remained, suggesting that additional unmeasured factors, such as variations in magnesium measurement methods, outcome definitions, and population characteristics, also contributed. In the current meta-analysis, we applied the ROBINS-I checklist to assess the quality of eligible observational studies; this tool was very sensitive and assessed the risk of bias with an extremely rigorous approach. Therefore, most studies had a low risk of bias in most domains (e.g., exposure assessment, selection of participants, outcome measurement, and results reporting), but their overall risk of bias was rated as moderate or severe. This result is largely due to ROBINS-I’s rigorous framework, which is aligned with the standards of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and is particularly sensitive to even minor sources of bias.

Several possible mechanisms could explain the association between serum magnesium and hyperglycemia, T2DM, and prediabetes. Magnesium acts as a cofactor for enzymes and is also involved in insulin signaling. Therefore, reduced magnesium levels could reduce cellular sensitivity to insulin and lead to insulin resistance [47]. In addition, magnesium plays a role in the function of pancreatic beta cells, and a decrease in it can lead to a decrease in the ability of these cells to secrete insulin [48]. Furthermore, reduced magnesium concentrations trigger oxidative stress, mainly by increasing nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase activity, followed by upregulation of the pro-oxidant thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) [49]. Additionally, higher serum magnesium levels are associated with reduced high sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) [50]. Magnesium plays a role in enzymatic reactions of glycolysis as well as in transport of glucose into cells and can help control blood sugar levels [51, 52]. On the other hand, magnesium can reduce levels of stress hormones such as cortisol, which can be effective in controlling blood sugar levels [53]. Finally, magnesium can also affect glucagon secretion and thereby help regulate blood sugar levels [52, 54].

This study has several strengths. This is the first and most comprehensive meta-analysis that examined the association between serum magnesium and hyperglycemia, T2DM, and prediabetes. In addition, dose-response meta-analyses were performed and showed linear associations between serum magnesium and risk of hyperglycemia and T2DM. Several limitations should be considered in this study. Although causality was confirmed in cohort studies, cross-sectional studies likely overestimated the association between serum magnesium levels and our study outcomes. In addition, most of the included studies accounted for important confounders, but some others did not account for these confounders, which might have biased the results. Methodological differences in magnesium measurement might have resulted in heterogeneity between studies; most studies used the calorimetric method, whereas some studies employed atomic absorption spectrophotometry, Colorimetry, ICP-MS, or photometry, which might yield slightly different serum values. Additionally, population characteristics varied substantially across studies, including age range (one cross-sectional study was conducted exclusively on children, another on older adults), geographic region, and baseline health status. Outcome definitions also differed: hyperglycemia was defined by fasting glucose thresholds ranging from 100 to 110 mg/dL, and T2DM definitions incorporated differing combinations of glucose cut-offs, medication use, and ICD codes. Genetic, cultural, and environmental factors, such as differences in diet and lifestyles between regions, might influence the relation [55]; but have not been controlled in eligible studies. The focus of most prospective studies was on European and American countries; this might limit the generalizability of the results to other population groups, especially in regions with different economic and social conditions. Therefore, to generalize the results, further prospective studies in different communities are needed. Furthermore, two cohort studies [14, 30] did not report the number of cases; this omission limits the precision of the pooled risk estimates and precludes a complete assessment of potential publication bias for those analyses. Additionally, although Begg’s and Egger’s tests did not detect significant publication bias for hyperglycemia or T2DM, these tests have limited power in case of small number of cohort studies; therefore, the possibility of publication bias cannot be entirely excluded. The GRADE assessment indicated high certainty of evidence for hyperglycemia and type 2 diabetes in both cohort and non‑cohort analyses. In the GRADE framework, however, high certainty in observational studies could not automatically translate into strong clinical recommendations, because the evidence originated from non‑randomized designs and residual confounding could not be ruled out. Definitive recommendations would require confirmation from large‑scale randomized controlled trials. Finally, due to the use of the ROBINS-I tool, which has strict criteria for assessing risk of bias in observational studies, most included studies had moderate or severe risk of bias.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated significant inverse dose–response associations between serum magnesium and hyperglycemia and type 2 diabetes, while the association with prediabetes was weak and uncertain. Increasing serum magnesium concentration from deficient to sufficient values (> 0.85 mmol/L) was associated with a decreasing trend in hyperglycemia and T2DM risk. Cohort studies provided high-certainty evidence (based on GRADE) for inverse dose-response associations between serum magnesium and both hyperglycemia and type 2 diabetes, but low-certainty evidence was obtained for prediabetes; non-cohort studies similarly yielded high-certainty evidence for hyperglycemia and type 2 diabetes, with moderate-certainty evidence for prediabetes. Clinicians should consider monitoring serum magnesium levels in individuals at high risk of hyperglycemia or type 2 diabetes. Further prospective and well-designed investigations are needed to clarify these results, particularly for prediabetes.
