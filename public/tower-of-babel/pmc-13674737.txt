Probiotics as a Strategy for Mitigating Viral Infections in Poultry: Immunomodulatory Mechanisms and Therapeutic Potential
Authors: Elmer Nayra Ametefe, Daniel Saka, Juliet Nuerkie Anang, Lourraine Sena Doh, Leonard Kofi Adjorlolo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPoultry viral infections remain a persistent challenge for the poultry industry, causing significant economic losses and posing potential threats to human health. The escalating frequency of outbreaks involving pathogens, such as infectious bronchitis virus (IBV), Newcastle disease virus (NDV) and avian influenza virus (AIV), underscores the urgent need for effective interventions. Probiotics have gained attention as a promising strategy to combat these viral infections due to their potential in enhancing immune responses and establishing a healthy gut microbiome. A literature search was carried out on Google Scholar, Scopus and PubMed using key search terms, such as ‘probiotics’, ‘Lactobacillus’, ‘acidophilus’, ‘poultry’, ‘viral infections’, ‘Newcastle Disease Virus (NDV)’ and other relevant terms. Inclusion criteria for manuscripts used were based on the relevance to probiotic‐based interventions in poultry, the focus on immunological mechanisms and the examination of viral pathogens commonly found in West African poultry farming systems. This review explores the role of probiotics in mitigating the effects of NDV, AIV and IBV in poultry, focusing on their immunomodulatory effects and antiviral mechanisms. Lactobacillus acidophilus, L. casei and L. plantarum have shown an interesting promise in countering NDV infections by modulating immunological parameters and enhancing antibody responses. Similarly, these probiotics have exhibited antiviral activity against AIV, promoting immune activation and reducing viral loads. Additionally, L. acidophilus and L. plantarum have emerged as potential adjuvants for IBV vaccines, improving antibody responses and humoural immunity.Keywords: avian viruses, lactic acid bacteria, poultry, probiotics

Introduction

Poultry production is a major component of global food systems, providing an important source of affordable animal protein, income and livelihoods for millions of people (Grace et al. 2024; Nassar 2026). The rapid expansion of poultry production worldwide has, however, been accompanied by persistent outbreaks of viral diseases that threaten flock health, productivity and food security. Viral infections can result in high morbidity and mortality, reduced growth performance, impaired reproductive function, decreased egg production and substantial costs associated with disease control and flock losses (Bose and Siva Kumar 2025). These consequences are particularly important in developing countries, where poultry production is often characterized by smallholder and backyard systems with limited access to veterinary services, vaccines, diagnostic facilities and effective biosecurity measures (Grace et al. 2024). Consequently, developing accessible and complementary strategies for improving poultry resistance to viral infections is an important priority for sustainable poultry production.

Control of poultry viral diseases currently relies primarily on vaccination, biosecurity, surveillance and appropriate flock‐management practices. Vaccination remains one of the most important tools for reducing the severity and transmission of several poultry viral diseases. Nevertheless, vaccination does not always provide complete protection because of antigenic variation, the emergence of new viral strains, differences in vaccine responsiveness and variations in vaccination practices (Abozeid 2023; Hu et al. 2022). In developing countries, animals that have the most contact with humans are poultry and domestic animals (including pets) within their living habitat. This provides favourable conditions for virus evolution and variety, and hence these animals serve as appropriate intermediary hosts for viruses to infect humans (Li et al. 2026). Moreover, maintaining effective biosecurity and vaccination programmes can be particularly challenging in backyard and smallholder production systems (Grace et al. 2024; Okata and Al‐Hassan 2023).

The challenge is further complicated by environmental stressors associated with climate variability and climate change. Poultry is highly sensitive to environmental stress, particularly elevated ambient temperatures which can increase oxidative stress, alter metabolism, disrupt intestinal integrity and impair immune function, potentially increasing susceptibility to infectious diseases (Mpenda et al. 2019). At the same time, changing environmental conditions can influence pathogen ecology, host–pathogen interactions, poultry management practices and the distribution of disease vectors and reservoirs. These interactions are particularly relevant to viral disease control because an already stressed or immunocompromised host may have a reduced capacity to mount effective antiviral responses (Aryal et al. 2025; Ncho 2025). This is a major concern in the poultry industry with respect to viral infections as viruses use proteins to inhibit certain apoptotic agents against virally infected cells. An example of this mechanism is the infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV), which causes clinical disease in young chicks and adult fowl (Gao et al. 2025). It damages immunological tissue, particularly the bursa of Fabricius and bursa‐derived lymphocytes, impairing the host's ability to develop efficient responses to recurrent pathogen infection (Gao et al. 2025; Orakpoghenor et al. 2026). Other viral infections have been shown to impact poultry by similar mechanisms. Therefore, strategies capable of simultaneously supporting intestinal health, maintaining immune competence and improving resilience to environmental stress may offer an important complementary approach to conventional disease‐control measures.

An increasing body of evidence indicates that the gastrointestinal (GI) microbiome is an important component of host immunity and resistance to infectious diseases. The gut microbiota interacts continuously with intestinal epithelial cells and immune cells and contributes to the development and regulation of both innate and adaptive immune responses (Liu et al. 2025). Microbial metabolites and microbial‐associated molecular patterns can influence immune‐cell activation, cytokine production, epithelial barrier integrity and systemic immune function. Consequently, disruption of the normal relationship between the intestinal microbiome and the host can adversely affect immune homeostasis and may compromise antiviral defence (Harper et al. 2021, Nassar et al. 2026; Oke et al.2025). This emerging understanding provides a biological basis for investigating microbiome‐directed interventions as potential tools for improving resistance to viral infections in poultry; however, the mechanisms through which these influence antiviral immunity in poultry remain incompletely understood.

Probiotics are particularly attractive within this context because they can potentially influence both intestinal microbial ecology and host immune function. Probiotics, including selected species of lactic acid bacteria (LAB), can contribute to gut health through competitive exclusion of undesirable microorganisms, production of antimicrobial metabolites, enhancement of intestinal barrier function, modulation of inflammatory responses and regulation of immune‐cell activity (Ishaq et al. 2026; Naeem and Bourassa 2025). Importantly, their potential antiviral effects may extend beyond direct inhibition of viruses. By interacting with intestinal epithelial cells and immune pathways, probiotics may enhance host antiviral defences and improve the ability of the host to respond to viral challenge (Y. Liu et al. 2025; Sayed et al. 2025). Thus, probiotics should not be viewed solely as agents that suppress pathogens directly, but also as biological modulators capable of influencing the host–microbiome–virus interaction. Lactobacillus casei, L. acidophilus and L. plantarum are crucial probiotics for addressing poultry viruses due to their multifaceted benefits. These probiotic strains possess inherent immunomodulatory properties that bolster the avian immune response, enhancing the defence mechanisms against viral infections, such as infectious bronchitis virus (IBV), NCD and avian influenza virus (AIV). They stimulate both innate and adaptive immunity, promoting the production of antiviral cytokines and enhancing phagocytic activity (Yin et al. 2023; S. Zhang et al. 2025). Additionally, these probiotics help maintain gut health by regulating the gut microbiota composition, which plays a pivotal role in the overall immune system (S. Zhang et al. 2025). They create a competitive environment against pathogenic bacteria, reducing the risk of secondary infections and indirectly alleviating viral load (Ishaq et al. 2026; Vinayamohan et al. 2024). Thus incorporating L. casei, L. acidophilus and L. plantarum as probiotic supplements could contribute to enhanced poultry health, reduced viral susceptibility and, ultimately, more sustainable poultry production.

This review focuses on IBV, Newcastle disease virus (NDV) and AIV which are viruses implicated in poultry health. These viruses cause highly contagious and economically devastating diseases, jeopardizing poultry production and food security. IBV leads to respiratory issues and decreased egg production (Falchieri et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2024). NDV also results in severe respiratory and neurological symptoms (Berihulay et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2023), and AIV can mutate into strains that have potential risks against human health (AbuBakar et al. 2023; Mellace et al. 2024). Protecting poultry from these viruses ensures sustainable production, safeguards livelihoods and mitigates the potential spread of zoonotic diseases.

Antiviral drugs against poultry viruses have faced drug resistance, and probiotics have recently become potential preventive control measures to mitigate viral infections in poultry. Probiotic effects are often strain‐specific and may depend on dose, route of administration, host age, diet, environmental conditions, microbiome composition and the characteristics of the viral pathogen (Idowu et al. 2025; Oliveira et al. 2025). The mechanisms through which they influence antiviral immunity in poultry remain incompletely understood, and evidence for probiotic‐mediated protection is uneven across IBV, NDV and AIV.

This review seeks to explore the role these probiotics play in certain viral infections (IBV, AIV and NDV) which are prevalent in the sub‐Saharan region with the aim of providing a better understanding of the mechanisms linking probiotic administration, gut microbiome modulation, immune responses and viral outcomes essential for identifying strains with genuine prophylactic or therapeutic potential.

Methodology

This review was conducted to synthesize knowledge on the use of probiotics as immunomodulatory interventions against prevalent viral infections affecting poultry in West Africa. The literature search focused on identifying both primary and secondary research articles that explore the interaction between probiotics and viral pathogens, with an emphasis on immune responses and potential for sustainable disease control. Relevant literature was retrieved through comprehensive searches of online scientific databases, including Google Scholar, Scopus and PubMed. Key search terms included: ‘probiotics’, ‘Lactobacillus’, ‘acidophilus’, ‘plantarum’, ‘poultry’, ‘viral infections’, ‘Newcastle Disease Virus (NDV)’, ‘Infectious Bronchitis Virus (IBV)’, ‘Avian Influenza Virus (AIV)’, ‘immunomodulation’, ‘immune response’, ‘vaccine’, ‘West Africa’ and ‘gut microbiome’. Boolean operators, such as ‘AND’ and ‘OR’, were used to refine the search and ensure inclusiveness across different thematic areas. After retrieving the initial pool of articles, titles and abstracts were reviewed to exclude irrelevant studies. Full‐text screening was then conducted for articles that passed the initial review. Inclusion criteria were based on the relevance to probiotic‐based interventions in poultry, the focus on immunological mechanisms and the examination of viral pathogens commonly found in West African poultry farming systems. Both experimental and observational studies, as well as prior reviews that added theoretical or contextual depth, were considered. Despite extensive database querying, the review revealed a limited number of high‐quality, region‐specific studies on the subject, underscoring a significant research gap in the application of probiotics for controlling viral infections in poultry within the West African context. This scarcity of literature highlights the urgent need for more targeted primary research to establish context‐specific probiotic strategies and their efficacy in mitigating poultry viral diseases.

Prevalent Avian Viruses in the Sub‐Saharan Region

Sub‐Saharan Africa faces severe economic consequences from viral infections in chickens, including lower productivity, higher production costs and a decline in the region's appeal, to foreign direct investment (Grace et al. 2024; Sadiq and Mohammed 2017). Research in Ethiopia, Ghana, Kenya and Nigeria discovered that avian influenza (AI) outbreaks can lead to significant livelihood losses, hurting both small‐scale and commercial poultry farmers (Birol et al. 2013). In Nigeria, research done by You and Diao (2007) revealed that an epidemic of AI might lead to a 21% decrease in chicken output, resulting in a major economic loss of $250 million for chicken producers.

Among the most prevalent viral diseases is the Newcastle disease (ND), caused by the virulent NDV. This affects various avian species and is characterized by high mortality rates. Concurrently, AI, particularly the highly pathogenic H5N1 strain, presents a grave concern. This viral strain not only jeopardizes poultry health but also possesses zoonotic potential, thereby posing a severe threat to both avian and human populations with potential pandemic implications. Additional noteworthy poultry viruses in the region include infectious bursal disease (IBD), induced by the IBDV and infectious bronchitis (IB), caused by the IBV. These viruses contribute to the overall burden on poultry health and production systems, necessitating comprehensive strategies for mitigation and control.

Newcastle Disease Virus

The first case of this virus was reported in Indonesia in the year 1927; however, the name Newcastle was given because of an outbreak that happened in the city of Newcastle, in 1927 where about 700 adult chickens and chicks of different ages died (Amoia et al. 2021). In Africa, the first reported case was discovered in South Africa (1945); however, similar and closely related symptoms had been discovered in Kenya in the year 1936 (Abolnik 2017). In West Africa, however, the first cases of this disease remain unknown, although predicted, that it likely arrived in this region during and after WWII via wild birds, transportation and poultry commerce, although this has yet to be verified (Dzogbema et al. 2021). Several serological tests conducted years after the first description in South Africa established the occurrence of the disease in several African countries (Dzogbema et al. 2021). After several years of the appearance of the disease, there is still high prevalence of ND in West African countries due to the free‐range farming system. In Ghana, high seroprevalence have been observed in recent times. A study by Cobbinah et al. (2020) found an overall seroprevalence of 69.0% for ND antibodies in chickens. Notably, local chickens raised under extensive farming systems had a significantly higher seroprevalence of 60.56%, compared to those raised under intensive systems, which had a seroprevalence of 8.45%. Moreover, a serological investigation conducted by Boakye et al. (2016) found circulating ND antibodies in local hens and guinea fowls in Kumasi and its environs, with 81.8% of local chickens and 24.2% of guinea fowls testing positive. This demonstrates that ND remains an endemic viral illness in the Ghanaian poultry industry. The prevalence of ND in Nigeria was 15.9% in Oyo and Kano states (Ekiri et al. 2021) and 17% in Abuja (Abraham‐Oyiguh et al. 2014). Ivory Coast has also recorded a seroprevalence of 22% as reported by Kouakou et al. (2015).

Impact of Probiotic LAB on NDV

To reduce the prevalence of NDV in poultry and to reduce the dependence on antibiotics in treating NDV, the efficacy and role of various probiotics against viral infection have been explored. LAB are the leading probiotics in the search for probiotics against NDV. LAB live naturally in the GI tract and have been classified as probiotics and several studies have reported their benefits as intestinal bacteria in mammals. Various species of the Lactobacillus genus have been implicated in limiting the effects of NDV in poultry.

Lactobacillus acidophilus

L. acidophilus has been found to enhance immune function by strengthening the adaptive and innate arms of the immune system. Through in vitro investigations, it has been shown to be a powerful inducer of T helper type 1 (Th1) cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)‐12 and interferon (IFN)‐. L. acidophilus is also implicated in the stimulation of natural killer (NK) cell function (Table 1). A study by Ho et al. (2020) revealed that the oral intake of the probiotic bacterium L. acidophilus strain L‐55 enhanced immune responses. This was marked by a significant increase in lymphocyte and leukocyte counts, following vaccination with live attenuated NDV strains (Ho et al. 2020). Monocyte counts also increased following treatment, regardless of NDV strain inoculation. At 2 weeks following the subsequent NDV vaccine inoculation, there was an observed dose‐dependent increase in NK cell activity. Interferon (IFN)‐mRNA expression was detected 2 weeks after the secondary NDV strain injection (Ho et al. 2020). Moreover, in an investigation by Nouri Gharajalar et al. (2020) where they explored the possible use of Lactobacillus sp. as adjuvants for NDV vaccine, they observed significantly greater immunoglobulin G (IgG) and high NDV antibody levels in Lactobacillus‐treated groups, compared to merely vaccinated and negative control groups. During the vaccination period, antibody levels against NDV rose, particularly in probiotic‐treated groups (Nouri Gharajalar et al. 2020).

Lactobacillus casei

L. casei has been used as an adjuvant for NDV vaccine due to its immunomodulating properties. A variety of recombinant L. casei strains have been investigated as a new vaccination therapy in poultry. Some investigations found that oral administration of NDV haemagglutinin–neuraminidase (HN)‐expressing L. casei boosted immunological responses while not impairing growth performance (Ju et al.2021 ). Orally administered L. casei expressing the HN protein of NDV (Lc‐pPG‐HN) immunization dramatically enhanced body weight (BW) and immunological organs index (Ju et al.2021 ). Lc‐pPG‐HN also improved secretory immunoglobulin A (SIgA) in the jejunum, the relative richness of bacteria in the cecum and the histomorphological development of the small intestine (Table 1) (Ju et al.2021 ). Furthermore, similar enhancing effects were identified with haemagglutination inhibition (HI) antibody titre and serum cytokine expression (Ju et al.2021 ). In a study by Ogawa et al. (2006), they assessed the anti‐BSA IgG concentrations in serum samples from hens immunized orally or subcutaneously with BSA and examined the oral immunoadjuvant effect of L. casei subsp. casei with or without dextran‐feeding. In dextran‐supplemented diet‐fed hens given with L. casei subsp. casei, both kinds of immunization resulted in a considerable rise in anti‐BSA IgG concentrations. However, treatment of L. casei subsp. casei or dextran‐feeding alone resulted in no rise in anti‐BSA IgG concentrations (Ogawa et al. 2006) This investigation showed that prebiotics are very crucial in the immunomodulating activity of probiotics, in this case dextran for L. casei.

Lactobacillus plantarum

L. plantarum, a naturally occurring probiotic in the mouth and gut of mammals has several benefits against pathogenic microbes. It exhibits antimicrobial activity against harmful and spoilage‐causing microorganisms by producing natural compounds, such as bacteriocins, organic acids and analogues of bacteriocins, that suppress their growth and survival (Xu et al. 2020). Several studies have also shown that L. plantarum plays a significant role in the fight against NDV in poultry. A study by Yin et al. (2023) observed that supplementing broiler feed with L. plantarum led to a remarkable increase in antibody production in response to ND vaccination. They also observed by comparing with a control group, L. plantarum GX17 supplementation resulted in larger amounts of CD4 molecule levels in the blood serum on Days 28 and 42 following the booster shot (Table 1) (Yin et al. 2023). Like L. casei, L. plantarum use as an adjuvant for NDV vaccine has been explored, and it has been shown to increase the efficacy of these vaccines by strengthening the immune response to an antigen. Je‐hyun and Chang‐Seon (2022) patented a vaccine formulation or a feed composition against a virus that includes L. plantarum and NDV K148/08 strain. When compared to an inactivated vaccine that does not contain LAB, the vaccine composition has a synergistically high antigen amount reduction impact, and it can have a high ability to suppress the expression of clinical symptoms and/or an ability to inhibit viral release (Je‐hyun and Chang‐seon 2022).

Avian Influenza Virus

There are various strains of the AIV with the most pathogenic ones being H5 and H7 strains. H5N1 caused the first recorded highly pathogenic AI outbreak, and this happened in chickens in Scotland in 1959 and has since been causing outbreaks in several continents with the recent outbreak occurring in North America and Europe (Shi et al. 2023). In Africa, it was first reported in 2006 in Nigeria after which several cases have been reported creating huge losses in the poultry industry on the continent (Ekiri et al. 2021). In Kaduna state, northern Nigeria, a highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) virus of the H5N1 subtype was discovered in chickens. The virus expanded to 25 of Nigeria's 36 states, including the Federal Capital Territory, lasting 21 months. Ghana reported the first HPAI H5N1 infections in poultry in three regions in 2007: Greater Accra, Volta and Brong Ahafo (Asante et al. 2016).

Active influenza surveillance and restricted movement of poultry were instituted to manage the outbreak. However, in 2015, over 30,000 chickens were affected with over 17% mortality because of a resurgence of the viral outbreak (Asante et al. 2016). Burkina Faso's poultry population has also been severely impacted by multiple waves of HPAI H5N1 epizootics. The three different regions, Gomboussougou, Bonyollo and Koubr, detected the highly pathogenic H5N1 in December 2021, after a phylogenetic analysis, the viruses in these three affected regions clustered together within a clad named (2.3.4.4b) making them closely related to viruses identified in Niger and Nigeria (Ouoba et al. 2022). This shows that these viruses are easily transmitted across border countries hence difficult to control making developing effective control strategies against them very crucial. Moreover, the finding raised concerns about the possibility that West Africa will become a new hotspot for the introduction of novel HPAI virus genotypes (Ouoba et al. 2022) The H7 viruses have caused outbreaks in several countries with the first recorded in 1963 among turkeys in England; however, it has rarely been reported in the sub‐Saharan region.

Due to the resistance of AI A viruses to antivirals, there have been explorations for more effective treatments, such as probiotics use due to their safety and affordability as nutraceuticals. Probiotics that produce lactic acid have been shown to be effective in preventing and controlling AIV (Alqazlan et al. 2020).

Impact of Probiotic LAB on AIV

Lactobacillus acidophilus

L. acidophilus has been shown to be an effective antiviral agent against chickens infected with AIVs. As reported in a study by Shojadoost et al. (2019), L acidophilus either in isolation or in combination with Lactobacillus reuteri and Lactobacillus salivarius demonstrated an enhanced antiviral property of chicken macrophages to fight off AIV. This enhancement was accompanied by increased expression of various immune‐related genes, including interferon (IFN), interferon‐cytokine, interferon‐inducible transmembrane protein M3 (IFITM3), interleukin (IL)‐1, 2′,5′‐oligoadenylate synthetase (OAS) and interferon regulatory factor‐7 (IRF7) genes. Additionally, lactobacilli‐treated macrophages produced considerably more IFN‐protein and nitric oxide (NO) (Table 1) and displayed increased surface expression of the CD40 marker, which plays a key role in immune activation (Shojadoost et al. 2019). Moreover, in an investigation by Shojadoost et al. (2019), the effects of L. acidophilus with or without inulin, a prebiotic, on broiler meat quality infected by the H9N2 strain was investigated. They found that probiotics (L. acidophilus), either alone or in combination with prebiotics, improved the physicochemical qualities of chicken breast muscle in both healthy and AIV‐infected hens (Shojadoost et al. 2019). L acidophilus has been shown to stimulate dendritic cells (DCs), and an investigation conducted by X. Gao et al. (2016) elucidated the mechanism of activation. The S‐layer protein from L. acidophilus ATCC 4356 was identified as a new antiviral material (X. Gao et al. 2016). The protein was found to stimulate DCs. The stimulation activated DCs and increased interleukin 10 (IL‐10) release. The protein also showed potential to prevent H9N2 AIV invasion in DCs.

Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (L. plantarum)

The antiviral activity of L. plantarum against some strains of AIV has been investigated in several studies. L. plantarum strain KAU007 was investigated by Rather et al. (2022) for its ability to combat the low‐pathogenic AIV (H9N2). KAU007 demonstrated significant antiviral efficacy against low pathogenic H9N2 and aggressive antioxidant activity, confirming a haemagglutination assay (Rather et al. 2022), and free culture supernatants revealed a dose‐dependent decrease in IL‐6 and IFN‐ levels (Table 1) (Rather et al. 2022). KAU007 could thus be regarded as a possible H9N2 target‐based probiotic. To make vaccines against H9N2 more effective, Sun et al. (2018) developed a vaccine with a Lactobacillus recombinant that activates DCs. They discovered that L. plantarum expressing HA protein and DCpep confers effective protection against G57 strain H9N2 infection by activating DCs via the toll‐like receptor (TLR)‐induced NF‐B pathway and promoting DC migration via the CCR7‐CCL19/CCL21 axis, thereby enhancing immunogen presentation to T and B lymphocytes, skewing T cell polarization towards Th1, Th2 and Treg cells, and evoking more efficient mucosal and adaptive immunity responses (Sun et al. 2018). The role of L. plantarum against the highly pathogenic AI strains H5N1 and H7N1 remains largely unexplored.

Lactobacillus casei

The impact of L. casei on AIVs in poultry has not been fully demonstrated in literature; however, various works have been done in mice where it was shown to mediate high levels of serum IgG and mucosal IgA. Its role in poultry AIVs remains to be explored.

Effect of Probiotic Bacillus Species Against AIV

Bacillus species have been long explored for use as antiviral agents against AIVs; in a study by Lee et al. (2020), the spores of Bacillus subtilis was shown to have enhanced the immune system by increasing the population of B cells and other innate cells in the splenocytes of chickens when administered with inactivated H9N2 and B. subtilis spores (Table 1). There was also an increase in the pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐1β and IL‐6 compared to that in the H9N2‐only group. Moreover, by spraying Bacillus spp. on experimentally infected H9N2 broiler chickens, Rasaei et al. (2023) observed that there was an improved weight gain in chickens sprayed as compared to the control group (which were AIV challenged but no Bacillus sprayed). To develop a convenient and safe vaccine, Mou et al. (2016) created a recombinant B. subtilis strain that expressed the haemagglutinin (HA) protein and then investigated the immunological activity in chicken bone marrow‐derived DCs (BM‐DCs) and the immune response after oral immunization. It was observed that the BM‐DCs increased significantly in immunized chickens as accompanied by expression of TLRs and elevated IgG‐ and IgA‐specific titres in the trachea and serum.

Infectious Bronchitis Virus

IBV is a corona virus that causes acute and highly contagious disease in chickens affecting their upper respiratory tract and the reproductive tract, some strains also cause nephritis (Ennaji et al. 2019). The first case of IBV outbreak in poultry was reported in North Dakota, USA with chickens of all ages considered natural hosts; however, severity was observed to be great in younger chickens (Khataby et al. 2016). In Africa, the first incidences of IBV were recorded in Egypt in the 1950s where the variant isolated was shown by neutralization tests to be closely related to a Dutch variant (de Wit et al. 2022). Moreover, other variants in northern Africa were shown to be closely related to the variants found in Europe (J. J. de Wit et al. 2022). In West Africa, serological evidence of avian virus IBV was initially identified in Nigerian chicken farms between the late 1990s and early 2000s. The prevalence of IBV was found to be very high (84%) (Khataby et al. 2016). In the next few years, new variants which were both genetically and antigenically different from the other known viral strains emerged in Nigeria and other West African countries, like Niger, and the effectiveness of vaccines against these strains was unexplored (Khataby et al. 2016). In Ghana, the first case of IBV was recorded in 2013 by detecting IBV antibodies in trachea swabs of chickens in a respiratory disease outbreak in Ghana, indicating the existence of IBV in commercial poultry (Bali et al. 2022). The detection was more in layers than in broilers with a prevalence of 27% and 24%, respectively, in a total of 440 sera samples collected; however, in 2018, they observed a prevalence of 16% (Ayim‐Akonor et al. 2018).

Impact of Lactobacillus Species on IBV

The effect of Lactobacillus sp. against the IBV in poultry has not been enormous compared to NDV and AIV; however, a few studies have shown the prospect of a few Lactobacillus species as antiviral agents.

Lactobacillus acidophilus

L. acidophilus together with 3 other bacteria (L. casei, Streptococcus faecium and B. subtilis symbionts) (SNB) and yeast derived carbohydrates (YDC) were explored as IBV antiviral agents in poultry as diets by Alizadeh et al. (2017). They found that birds fed YDC had an increased specific antibody response to IBV in the fourth week, whereas birds fed both YDC and SNB had a lower antibody response to IBV in Week 6. Furthermore, the diets had no effect on specific antibody response to sheep red blood cells (SRBC) and bovine serum albumin (BSA), which were both controls (Alizadeh et al. 2017). As a result, they suggest that supplementing the diet with L. acidophilus and other species boosted humoural immunity by boosting serum IgG concentrations (Table 1) (Alizadeh et al. 2017). The antiviral property of L acidophilus alone against IBV has not been explored so there is little or no information about how this species regulate the immune system of poultry against IBV. Moreover, the exact mechanism by which these probiotics increase IgG is not fully understood.

Lactobacillus plantarum

L. plantarum, a probiotic bacterium, has been shown to enhance immunity against IBV in poultry through its immunoadjuvant properties (Guo et al. 2020). Specifically, L. plantarum has been found to increase the production of IL‐17B, a protective cytokine that regulates mucosal inflammation and activates key signalling pathways, such as NF‐κB and JAK‐STAT (Guo et al. 2020). This leads to reduced IBV proliferation and viral loads, indicating enhanced immunoprotection against the virus (Guo et al. 2020). However, the exact mechanism by which L. plantarum activates these immune regulators remains unknown, highlighting the need for further research to explore how this probiotic bacterium prevents IB viral infections.

Lactobacillus casei

The antiviral effect of against IBV remains largely unexplored; however, as stated earlier together with L. acidophilus and B. subtilis, it was shown to boost humoural immunity by increasing IgG concentration against IBV (Alizadeh et al. 2017). The various LABs contain a range of immunostimulatory compounds, such as nucleic acid, peptidoglycan and lipoteichoic acid, which bind to TLR, and muramyl dipeptide, which binds to nod‐like receptors (Table 1) (Darbandi et al. 2021), triggering an immune response that protects poultry from viral infections.

The Impact of Bacillus and Other Probiotic Organisms in Viral Interventions

Probiotics other than Lactobacillus have been shown to offer protection against viruses that affect chickens. According to a study by Rasaei et al. (2023), supplementing broiler chickens with Bacillus probiotics decreased the severity of clinical symptoms and gross lesions associated with H9N2 AIV infection. Furthermore, the quantity, quality and hatchability of eggs were positively affected by B. subtilis and Bacillus amyloliquefaciens (X. Liu et al. 2019; Mazanko et al. 2018). Bacillus probiotics efficiently lower viral load and boost Th1 immune response (Tsai et al. 2021).

Moreover, probiotic yeasts, such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae and its byproducts, have demonstrated the ability to modulate immune responses in birds suffering from illnesses (Muthusamy et al. 2011; Rosen 2007; Świątkiewicz et al. 2014). Broiler diets supplemented with yeast cell wall and mannan oligosaccharide (MOS) showed enhanced immunogenic responses against NDV (Khani et al. 2012; Muthusamy et al. 2011; Oliveira et al. 2009) and AIV (Tohid et al. 2010).

Multiple studies have consistently shown enhanced serum antibody responses against viral diseases when yeast‐based products are added to poultry diets, attributed to the complementary effects of yeast‐derived mannans, glucans and nucleotides (Gao et al. 2008; GHEISARI and KHOLEGHIPOUR 2006; Ghosh et al. 2012; Shashidhara and Devegowda 2003; Tohid et al. 2010).

Conclusion, Limitations and Perspectives

The available evidence suggests that probiotics, particularly selected LAB, have potential to complement conventional strategies for controlling poultry viral infections by enhancing host immune responses, improving vaccine responsiveness and, in some cases, reducing viral replication or disease severity. However, the evidence remains heterogeneous and highly strain‐ and virus‐dependent. The strongest evidence currently relates to immunomodulatory effects and vaccine enhancement, whereas direct protection against specific poultry viruses, particularly IBV and highly pathogenic AIV strains, remains insufficiently characterized. Furthermore, many reported outcomes are based on immune biomarkers rather than clinically relevant endpoints, such as viral shedding, transmission, morbidity and mortality. The limited number of field‐based studies also constrains extrapolation of experimental findings to commercial and smallholder production systems.

Although much of the evidence is limited to studies up until 2020 with relatively limited recent poultry‐specific evidence, future research should prioritize well‐characterized probiotic strains, standardized dosing and administration protocols, mechanistic studies linking microbiome changes to antiviral immunity, and controlled field and challenge studies that evaluate both immunological and virological outcomes. These gaps are critical for determining whether probiotics can provide reproducible prophylactic benefits and, where evidence supports it, therapeutic value against poultry viral diseases.
