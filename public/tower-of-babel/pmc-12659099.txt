Assessment of reliability and concurrent validity of a single trunk-based IMU system to quantify spatiotemporal parameters in healthy individuals
Authors: Simon Zamberger, Lena Wurzinger, Brian Horsak, Bernhard Dumphart, Dieter Pahr, Ursula Ernst, Andrea Zauner-Dungl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCommercially available Inertial Measurement Units (IMU) are gaining popularity in clinical gait analysis. Compared to standard optoelectronic motion capture systems (OMCs), IMUs offer advantages such as lower cost, greater time efficiency, and the ability to be used outside of laboratory settings. IMUs can be attached to various body parts and have already been tested in numerous clinical studies. This study aimed to assess the intra-session reliability and concurrent validity of a trunk-based IMU system (BTS G-Walk®) in healthy individuals compared to an OMC system (VICON®).MethodsTo test the intra-session reliability and concurrent validity of the BTS-G-Walk®, 27 healthy participants were recruited for gait analysis. Both the IMU and OMC systems were used simultaneously to record spatiotemporal gait parameters under a standardized laboratory protocol. Eight spatiotemporal parameters were analyzed, using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC 2,k) and Bland–Altman analysis to evaluate the reliability and concurrent validity.ResultsThe IMU demonstrated excellent intra-session reliability across all parameters (ICC: 0.952–0.995). Concurrent validity was good to excellent for cadence, speed, stride length, and stride duration (ICC: 0.807–0.999), but poor for stance phase, single support, double support, and swing phase (ICC: 0.036–0.136). Bland–Altman analysis showed the lowest mean percentage bias for cadence (− 0.18%) and the highest for double support phase (23.45%).ConclusionThe BTS-G-Walk® delivers reliable values in healthy individuals. Regarding concurrent validity, stride duration and cadence demonstrated the highest agreement with the reference system. Caution is advised when using the IMU in clinical practice, particularly for interpreting parameters dependent on toe-off events, such as gait phase durations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01410-0.

Introduction

In recent years, inertial measurement units (IMUs) have gained increasing importance in clinical gait analysis. These systems, originally used in aircraft and vehicle navigation, combine accelerometers, gyroscopes, and sometimes magnetometers to determine linear accelerations, rotation velocities, and angles. Since these sensors are available in a small, portable format, they can easily be attached to various body parts as a single sensor or combined attachment [1, 2]. Another reason for their increasing popularity is that these wearables are less expensive, easier to apply, and not bound to a laboratory setting, compared to an optoelectronic motion capture (OMC) gait analysis system [3]. One popular and simple method is the attachment of a single IMU to the lower lumbar spine region. Mobbs et al. reviewed previous studies on single, trunk-mounted sensors with respect to their validation and reliability, as well as their application in various clinical contexts (e.g., neurological and orthopedic disorders). According to this review, these systems represent a viable alternative to current quantitative gait analysis methods and are of clinical relevance. However, the authors also underline that further validation studies in long-term, continuous monitoring are needed, particularly involving larger sample sizes or pathological populations [4].

Several clinical studies have already applied the IMU system (BTS G-Walk®), used in this study, to assess spatiotemporal gait parameters [5–28], with some of them focusing on reliability or validity [5–8, 13, 14, 17, 24]. It is essential to ensure that inertial sensors are valid and reliable before recommending their clinical application. Previous findings have demonstrated good reliability in healthy individuals (ICC > 0.84) [5] and individuals with medical conditions (ICC > 0,80) [8, 13, 17]. Regarding concurrent validity, study authors have reported poorer agreement in phase-describing parameters such as stance, single stance, double stance, and swing than in the other spatiotemporal parameters (step length, stride duration, cadence, speed). Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting parameters that define gait cycle phases [5–8].

However, questions remain, as currently only three studies are known to address the reliability and concurrent validity of the BTS G-Walk® system in healthy participants [5, 7, 8]. De Ridder et al. [5] and Viteckova et al. [7] used GAITRite® systems to explore this topic. However, these systems are not considered the gold standard in clinical gait analysis. Moreover, while Bravi et al. [8] evaluated reliability and concurrent validity using an optoelectronic motion capture (OMC) system, their sample size (only ten healthy participants) was notably small. Overall, the body of literature evaluating the BTS G-Walk® remains limited. Existing studies tend to focus on specific aspects of either validity or reliability, often involving small sample sizes or employing reference systems that do not represent the gold standard for validation. Therefore, this study seeks to address these limitations by assessing the reliability and concurrent validity of the trunk-based BTS G-Walk® system using a gold-standard optoelectronic motion capture system and a larger participant sample. The issue of reproducibility is another important issue in clinical studies. Therefore, comparing the results with previous studies should provide greater clarity and contribute to a better understanding of the applicability and limitations of IMU systems in clinical practice.

Methods

Participants

A convenience sample of 27 healthy participants (female: 22, male: 5) with a mean height of 1.69 m (SD 0.08) and a mean body mass of 67 kg (SD 11.4) was recruited. This sample size is in line with methodological guidance suggesting that approximately 25–30 participants are generally sufficient to estimate ICCs with adequate precision in reliability studies, particularly when high ICC values (> 0.80) are expected [29–31]. Participants’ ages ranged from 21 to 59 years with a mean of 36.9 years (SD 9.9). Exclusion criteria included previous injuries of the lower limbs or relevant orthopedic pre-existing conditions that could affect normal gait patterns. Additionally, individuals with severe internal or neurological comorbidities affecting cardiorespiratory fitness or physiological gait were excluded.

All participants provided written informed consent. Ethical approval was obtained from the local ethics committee (registration number: GS4-EK-4/682–2020).

Gait analysis systems

IMU-system

A waist-mounted IMU system was used for this study (BTS Bioengineering Corp., Quincy, USA). The IMU (BTS G-Sensor 2®) consisted of a triaxial accelerometer (multiple sensitivities from 2–16 g), a triaxial gyroscope (multiple sensitivities from 250–2000°/s, 16bit/axes), and a triaxial magnetometer (1200 µT, 13bit). The applied sensor had a size of 7 × 4 × 18 mm (length x width x depth) and a weight of 37 g. For study purposes, the sensor was affixed to the trunk at the level of L5–S1 using double-sided adhesive tape, instead of the manufacturer-recommended elastic belt (Fig. 1). All gait data were sampled at a frequency of 100 Hz. The gait-specific Walk + protocol of the commercially available software (G-Studio®, version 3.5.25) was used. OMC gait analysis

A motion capture system (Vicon Motion Systems Ltd, Oxford Metrics, UK), comprising 16 infrared cameras at a sampling rate of 120 Hz, was used. A full-body marker set with reflective spherical markers was attached bilaterally to each participant using double-sided tape. Marker placement followed the Cleveland Clinic Marker Set guidelines and the Plug-in Gait model for the upper extremities [32] (Fig. 1). The OMC trajectories of all markers were reconstructed and processed using the VICON Nexus analysis software (version 2.15.0, Oxford, UK) and filtered using the integrated Woltring routine with a mean squared error (MSE) value of 15 mm2. Initial contact and foot-off events were detected using the machine learning-based algorithm IntellEvent [33].

Test protocol

The IMU and OMC sensors remained attached throughout the entire gait protocol (Fig. 1). After sensor placement and an acclimatization phase, participants walked a 10-m distance back and forth four to five times at a self-selected speed. Because the IMU G-Walk + software included two gait distances (2 × 10 m) per report, a short break of 15–20 s was taken after every two distances to generate a report and reinitialize the gait protocol, including sensor calibration. In total, each participant completed eight to ten walking trials. Tracking of OMC data did not require any interruption of participants’ gait. The participants had to walk barefoot. Parameters recorded by both systems included cadence (steps/min), speed (m/s), stride length (m), stride duration (s), and the durations of stance, swing, double support, and single support phases (all expressed as percentages of the gait cycle).

Data postprocessing and statistical analysis

All data processing was performed using Python 3.11 (Python Software Foundation, Beaverton, USA). For the OMC system, gait parameters (cadence, speed, stride duration, stride length, duration of stance, swing, first double support, and single support phase) were calculated from the gait events and marker trajectories and aggregated for each participant. To make sure acceleration, deceleration, and turning movements did not affect the results, the first and last 2–3 steps of each of the trials were excluded from the analysis. The spatiotemporal parameters of the IMU system were obtained directly by the device-specific software. For parameters calculable on both sides (stance, swing, double support, single support, stride duration), either the left or right side was randomly selected for analysis. Assumptions of normality for all parameters and their differences between systems were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (as implemented in the SciPy package, version 1.11.1) and with Q–Q plots [34]. As all variables met normality assumptions, parametric tests (ICCs and paired t-tests) were applied for system comparisons. For the statistical analysis, the average value across the eight to ten walking trials per participant was used. Primarily, the two systems were compared using Bland–Altman plots and Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC; 2,k), based on a two-way random effects model with mean measurements and absolute agreement. The same ICC version was used for the assessment of intra-session reliability. ICC values were calculated using the Pingouin package (version 0.5.3) [35].

ICC values were interpreted by recommendations of Koo et al. [36], suggesting values less than 0.5 as poor, values between 0.5 and 0.75 as moderate, values between 0.75 and 0.9 as good, and values higher than 0.9 as excellent. Additionally, 95% confidence intervals were calculated.

For Bland–Altman analysis, the OMC system was used as the reference, and differences are reported as IMU minus OMC values. The mean systematic bias and 95% limits of agreement were calculated in both absolute terms and as percentage bias and visualized using Bland–Altman plots [37]. In addition, paired t-tests were performed for each gait parameter as supplementary checks for systematic bias. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, as ICCs and Bland–Altman plots represent the primary evidence of validity in this study. The percentage bias was calculated as [(IMU value – OMC value)/(OMC value)] × 100%.

Discussion

The study assessed the concurrent validity and intra-session reliability of a commercially available trunk-based IMU system compared to an OMC gait analysis system in 27 healthy participants. Several studies have previously evaluated the concurrent validity of the same IMU sensor (BTS-G-Walk®) either in healthy individuals [5] or as a control group in studies on clinical pathologies [7, 8]. However, these validation studies had shortcomings, including using non-gold standard gait analysis systems [5, 7] or a small sample size [8]. The present study aimed to address these shortcomings to provide clearer recommendations for the clinical applicability of promising IMU devices.

The intra-session reliability of the BTS-G-Walk® in this study was excellent, with high ICC values for all tested spatiotemporal parameters in the study population. These findings are largely consistent with previously reported intra-session reliability in both healthy individuals (ICC > 0.84) [5] and patients after total knee or hip replacement surgery (ICC > 0.6) [8], using the same IMU system. Also, similar results were published for a test–retest setting in cerebral palsy patients (ICC > 0.79) [13] and patients after bimalleolar ankle fracture surgery (ICC > 0.84) [17]. Therefore, the IMU sensor´s intra-session reliability can be considered sufficient in healthy persons and patients with gait pattern alterations.

In terms of concurrent validity, good to excellent ICC values were observed for spatiotemporal parameters such as cadence, speed, stride length, and stride duration. In contrast, poor ICC values were found for gait phase-related parameters, including stance, single support, double support, and swing phase. In Bland–Altman analysis, cadence and stride duration showed the lowest mean percentage bias and the narrowest Limit of Agreement interval. Speed and stride length demonstrated a higher mean systematic percentage error of approximately 2.5–3%, whereas phase-defining parameters demonstrated even greater systematic biases exceeding 3.9%. The relative bias of 23.45% for double support can be misleading, as it mainly reflects the inherently short duration of this phase within the gait cycle. In absolute terms, the mean deviation for double support is comparable to that of the other phase-defining parameters. Consistent with earlier reports [5, 7, 8], our ICC and Bland–Altman results indicate that the validity of gait cycle phase durations (stance, swing, initial double support, single support) remains limited when measured with a trunk-mounted IMU. A possible explanation is the difficulty of accurately detecting toe-off events using IMU systems, which is critical for correctly identifying gait cycle phases. Trojaniello et al. [38] tested a different IMU sensor at various positions on the lower trunk and reported that stride duration and step duration demonstrated acceptable accuracy across all sensor placements. In contrast, stance phase, swing phase, and double support phase did not show the same level of accuracy. The authors suggested that these discrepancies could be due to the smoother movement during final foot contact compared to the initial contact. Moreover, stride duration and cadence are potentially less dependent on the precise detection of toe-off events and can be robustly derived from the strong and regular trunk acceleration signals, which may explain their higher accuracy. Although the relative mean systematic biases of the phase-defining parameters were higher, their limits of agreement were generally narrower compared to parameters such as stride length and speed (except for double support). This indicates good precision, despite the limited validity of these parameters in trunk-based IMU systems. Viteckova et al. [7] also analyzed the possible cause of differences between the systems in phase-defining parameters. They concluded, that a systematic error influenced the discrepancy more than a random error and further that the systematic error in pathologic gait may increase.

Interestingly, among the spatiotemporal parameters, stride duration and cadence yielded better ICC values and smaller Bland–Altman biases compared to speed and stride length. This is partly differing from previously published data. De Ridder et al. [5] showed worse mean percentage bias in cadence and stride duration in Bland-Altmann analyses compared to our results (2.1% vs −0.18%; −1.3% vs 0.4%). Therefore, it remains uncertain whether the observed differences in concurrent validity among these spatiotemporal parameters are genuine or attributable to factors such as differences in study populations, the choice of reference system (GaitRite® vs. VICON®), or the sensor attachment method (elastic belt vs. adhesive tape). However, other study data support our findings, like Vítečková et al. [7] or Bravi et al. [8] who showed the best concurrent validity values for cadence and stride duration, using a GaitRite® respectively an optoelectronic system as reference. Similarly, a concurrent validity study involving patients with Parkinson’s disease [6] showed the smallest group differences in cadence and stride duration, regarding the non-parametric effect size (< 0,2; Cohen [39]). Also, the mean absolute errors were lowest for these parameters, compared to the other spatiotemporal parameters.

In summary, trunk-based IMU systems demonstrate high reliability but limited validity for certain gait parameters. As these sensors are already frequently used in clinical routine [4], the most important question to address is, whether a trunk-based IMU can be recommended for clinical diagnosis or follow-up assessments. Fernandes et al. [40] have published Minimal Detectable Change (MDC) data of gait parameters in a healthy population, using an OMC system. When comparing the present study results with these reference values, the mean systematic bias for speed, stride duration, and stride length was below the published MDC thresholds, supporting the feasibility of using trunk-based IMUs in healthy individuals for these parameters. More important for the clinical application of IMU sensors is the consideration of MDC [41–45] or Minimal Clinical Important Difference (MCID) [46, 47] in persons with gait deviations. Regarding speed, the 0.04 m/s of mean systematic error of the recent study is within the range of small meaningful changes (effect size: 0.2) in elderly (0.04 m/s) and stroke patients (0.06 m/s) after a training program [46] and a Parkinson study (0,08 m/s) [47].

Although the results are partly encouraging, caution is advised when applying trunk-based IMUs in populations with pathological gait patterns. In pathologic gait, variability increases [48] and systematic bias of trunk-based IMU may also increase, as stated for phase-defining parameters [7]. Further research is needed to systematically evaluate the measurement error of trunk-based IMUs in individuals with different gait pathologies and to relate findings to clinically meaningful thresholds such as MDC and MCID.

A key limitation of this study is that the spatiotemporal parameters used for statistical analysis were obtained directly from the proprietary IMU software, for which essential processing details (e.g., gait event detection algorithm, parameter calculation methods, filter type and cut-off, sampling frequency, and drift correction) are not disclosed. As these unknowns may affect accuracy, interpretability, and reproducibility, our findings should be viewed with this in mind. In addition, newer software versions may employ updated algorithms, which could limit comparability across studies.

Another limitation concerns the participant composition (female: 22, male: 5). This imbalance resulted from the recruitment process, as gender balance was not predefined. We do not expect it to have affected the outcomes. However, importantly, van der Kruk et al. [49] found that across biomechanical research, many studies show markedly low female representation, with only a small proportion favoring female participants. Thus, our sample, having a majority of women, helps begin to redress this pervasive gender imbalance in the literature.

Conclusion

The findings confirm that this trunk-based IMU system provides reliable measurements in healthy individuals and partially valid spatiotemporal gait parameters. The most satisfying results for concurrent validity were observed for cadence and stride duration. Caution is warranted when interpreting phase-defining parameters like stance phase, single support, double support, and swing phase due to an increased systematic bias. These limitations should be carefully considered when interpreting results from current and future studies utilizing trunk-based IMU systems. Possible explanations for the deviating values could be difficulties in the accurate detection of toe-off with these trunk-based devices. Future investigations should focus on the evaluation of concurrent validity between trunk-based IMU systems and OMC in persons with gait deviations. Further, the results of these studies should be compared with clinically important parameters like MCD and MCID.
