Determinants of stunting among under-five children in Amhara and Oromia regions, Ethiopia: the linear quantile regression analysis
Authors: Dafa Duge Wachifo, Dereje Danbe Debeko, Zeytu Gashaw Asfaw
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStunting in Ethiopia slightly decreased from 38% in 2016 to 34.4% in 2022. However, with this rate the country still falls behind the targets of the Sustainable Development Goals for 2030. The underlying unobserved factors-whether constant overtime or/and evolving due to random shocks have not yet been identified. Therefore, the current study aimed to identify the determinants of stunting among under-five children by accounting for both time-invariant and time-varying random effects.MethodsData for this study was obtained from the International Food Policy Research Institute. It was collected through three annual follow-up surveys conducted between February 8, 2018, and April 25, 2021, targeting the children of households participating in the Productive Safety Net Programs in the Amhara and Oromia regions. A total of 3,041 households, whose children had at least two waves complete (non-missing) anthropometric measurements, were included in the analysis. The response variable, height-for-age standard score (HAZ), was used. HAZ was computed based on a child’s height (in centimeters), age (in days), and sex, in accordance with the World Health Organization child growth standards. A quantile mixed hidden Markov model was employed.ResultsThere was a significant burden of stunting among children under five in both the Amhara and Oromia regions. However, the situation was more worsening in the Amhara region. Several factors were found to significantly reduce the risk of under-five stunting, including maternal body mass index, maternal education, nutritional counseling, use of iodized salt, increased feeding frequency, and being a female child. Conversely, factors such as small size at birth, age of the child, episodes of illness, and consumption of unhealthy food were associated with increased stunting. For instance, being the female children were associated with a lower likelihood of stunting compared to males, with odds ratios of 0.263 (0.055), 0.164 (0.0544), 0.171 (0.071), and 0.358 (0.0822) at 10th, 25th, 75th and 90th quantiles respectively. Additionally, the mixture probabilities of the first component of latent process increased across quantiles, with 62% of children classified in the first hidden (stunted) state at 90th quantile. Unobserved temporal shocks were found to increase the probability of transitioning into the first hidden state.ConclusionThe study identified both unobserved time-constant and time-varying heterogeneities, as well as several fixed effects. These factors contribute to an increased risk of under-five stunting and raise the probability of transitioning into the stunted state. Without timely and targeted interventions, these effects may continue to exacerbate the burden of stunting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25347-8.Keywords: Height for age standard score, Linear quantile mixed hidden Markov model, Mixture component probabilities, Initial and transition probability

Introduction

Stunting is defined as low height-for-age and it can result in irreversible physical and cognitive impairments, leading to reduced growth, delayed development, and long-term consequences for health, learning capacity, and productivity. Children who are stunted are more likely to face health complications, reduced learning potential, and a higher risk of chronic diseases in adulthood [1, 2]. In 2022, the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), World Health Organization (WHO), and the World Bank Group jointly estimated that 22.3% of under-five children (U5C) globally were stunted. Assia and Africa were the most affected regions, accounting for 52% and 43% of the global burden of stunted children, respectively [3]. Africa is the only continent where the number of stunted children has continued to rise. Between 2000 and 2022, stunting prevalence increased across all UN sub-regions of Africa. For example, in Eastern Africa, the prevalence of stunting among U5C rose from 21.1% in 2000 to 30.6% in 2022 [3, 4]. Stunting in Ethiopia slightly decreased from 38% in 2016 to 34.4% in 2022; however, with this rate the country still falls short of meeting the targets of the Sustainable Development Goals for 2030 [1, 5–7].

Previous studies have primarily used mean regression to identify determinants of under-nutrition, focusing on average effects across the population [8–13]. However, such approaches overlook temporal dynamics and fail to capture variations across different points of nutritional status distribution, particularly among the most or least undernourished children. For skewed and outlier affected data, the mean regression models may not be appropriate choice since it misses important aspects of response [14]. The extreme sensitivity of least squares estimator (LSE) to the modest amounts of outlier contamination makes it a very poor estimator in many non-Gaussian distributions [15]. They introduced quantiles regressions as robust alternatives which are comparably efficiency to estimator for Gaussian models while substantially outperforming the LSE over a wide class of non-Gaussian error distribution [16].

The data used in the current study was extracted from the “Strengthen Productive Safety Net Program Phase-IV (PSNP4) Institutions and Resilience (SPIR) Development Food Security Activity” dataset. The Ethiopia PSNP began in 2005 and operates in rural areas, supporting food insecure households to ensure that their basic needs are met. Ethiopia’s PSNP is the second largest in Africa (after South Africa) and is one of the largest safety net programs in the world. The PSNP has three key components: the (1) direct support program; (2) public works program; and (3) temporary program [17]. The PSNP in Ethiopia has shown potential in addressing acute malnutrition, its impact on chronic malnutrition and stunting remains limited. To maximize its benefits, particularly for nutritional outcomes, a multisectoral and integrated approach is recommended, including health, child feeding, sanitation, hygiene, and economic approaches, to achieve the maximum intended result of the program. Integrated and multisectoral social behavioral change communications on child feeding and care are also crucial to linking the program with the intended nutritional outcomes [18]. To support the implementation of this program World Vision and its partners, Cooperative for Assistance and Relief Everywhere (CARE) and Organization for Rehabilitation and Development in Amhara (ORDA) have been providing services to over 500,000 PNSP clients in 15 food insecure districts in the Amhara and Oromia regional states of Ethiopia. This effort has been funded by USAID’s Bureau for Humanitarian Assistance and in close collaboration with the Government of Ethiopia [20, 22],.This dataset was collected longitudinally through three consecutive survey rounds by the International Food Policy Research Institute (IFPRI). While three studies [19–21] have been published using this dataset, none of them have examined the effects of improved dietary diversity interventions on children’s nutritional status. Therefore, the current study aimed to identify the determinants of stunting among U5C by accounting for both time-invariant and time-varying random effects. The linear quantile mixed hidden Markov model (lqmHMM) was applied for the analysis.

Method

Target population

The target population of the study was U5C from households participating in the PSNP in Amhara and Oromia regional states of Ethiopia.

The design and data source

The dataset used in this study was obtained from the IFPRI. The authors requested access and received the dataset link on Monday May 29, 2023. The data were collected as part of IFPRI’s experimental, quantitative impact evaluation of the SPIR. This evaluation aimed to assess outcomes across several domains, including livelihoods, food security, child nutrition, women’s empowerment, mental health, and intimate partner violence [22]. Initially, 196 kebeles were selected for the study, 115 from the Amhara region and 81 from the Oromia region. However, four kebeles (3 from Amhara region and 1 from Omromia region) were later excluded: two due to the absence of PSNP beneficiaries and two kebeles due to civil unrest. This adjustment resulted in a final sample of 192 kebeles and 3,314 households. The inclusion criteria at baseline were the household must be a PSNP beneficiary, the household must have at least one child aged 0–35 months at baseline, ensuring that the child would be under 60 months at the two-year follow-up, and third the household must have a mother or female caregiver available for interview. Then the baseline, midline and endline survey were conducted respectively during February 8 to April 25, 2018, July 25 to October 23, 2019, and February 8, 2021, to April 25, 2021. Each survey round included three types of questionnaires: household-level interview, a mother or female caregiver questionnaire (focused on child-care and nutrition practices for the designated index child and for the mother), and spouse/partner questionnaire. At a baseline, each household had a child under 36 months designated as a baseline index child, who was the focus of follow-up assessments across the survey rounds [22]. Based on the study’s inclusion criteria, a total of 3,041 households who had a baseline indexed child with at least two waves complete (non-missing) anthropometric measurements of response variables across the survey rounds were included in the final analysis.

Study variables

Dependent variable

The dependent variable in this study is the HAZ, which is used to assess the stunting status of U5C. According to the survey protocols, during each round of data collection, the height or length of the child was measured in centimeters (cm). Each anthropometric measurement was taken twice to ensure accuracy. If the difference between the two measurements was less than or equal to 0.01 cm, both values were recorded as height 1 and height 2. However, if the difference exceeded 0.01 cm, a third measurement was taken. In such cases, the third measurement was considered the correct and final height value for measurement. The HAZ was computed using zscorer function, specifically, employing the add WHO growth reference standard scores (addWGSR) function. This function computes HAZ values based on inputs of the child’s height (in centimeters), age (in days), and sex, using the WHO 2006 Child Growth Standards as the reference. Based on the WHO classification: children with HAZ < −3 standard deviations (SD) were categorized as experiencing severe stunting, children with HAZ between − 3 SD and − 2 SD were classified as stunted and children with HAZ ≥ −2 SD were considered to have normal growth status [1, 2].

The independent variables included in the study were:

Child Related: sex, age, birth weight, illness episodes, duration of breastfeeding, consumption of other than breast milk, frequency of feeding solid/semi-solid food, whether feeding at least five groups among eight food groups, consumption of unhealthy food (UHF), consumption of sugar sweet beverages (SSBs) and immunization status. Mother/Primary caregiver Related: age, body mass index (BMI), marital status, highest education level, place of delivery, attending counseling service on nutrition and breast feeding, childcare activities and number of antenatal cares attended by mother/care giver. Household Related: family size, type of salt used in the household, Zone, and Region. The covariates categories and their coding were summarized as shown in Table 1 below:

Method of data analysis

The linear quantile mixed hidden Markov model

Quantile regression is gradually emerging as a unified statistical methodology for estimating models of conditional quantile functions and it offers a systematic strategy for examining how covariates influence the location, scale, and shape of the entire response distribution by complementing the exclusive focus of classical LSE on the conditional mean [16]. The quantile regression extends the traditional LSE approach, which focuses solely on the conditional mean of the response variable. It estimates conditional quantiles (for example, median, quartiles, or any percentile), allowing for a more complete picture of the relationship between covariates and the response [15]. In longitudinal data, observations from the same individual are typically correlated because they share common, unmeasured characteristics. Ignoring this correlation can lead to biased estimates and invalid inference. Thus previous study introduced a linear quantile mixed model (lqmm) to capture unobserved heterogeneity in the model parameters via individual-specific random coefficients [23]. However, sometimes the assumption of time-constant random coefficients may not hold, and it leads to biased parameter estimates. Other studies were conducted to account this problem was a linear quantile hidden Markov model (lqHMM), where time-varying (discrete) random intercepts capture unobserved dynamics [24, 25],. The more recent study introduced that, in real data applications, unobserved heterogeneity may both evolve and/or stay constant over time and observed individual trajectories could be characterized by temporal shocks and individual heterogeneity [26].

The current study applied a linear quantile regression model that jointly incorporates both time-invariant and time-varying random coefficients, to effectively account for unobserved heterogeneity and within-individual dependence in longitudinal data. The skewness or presence of outliers may be attributed to temporal shocks and/or individual heterogeneity. To appropriately model these complexities, the current study employed the lqmHMM to identify the determinants of HAZ, accounting for both time-constant and time-varying heterogeneity. Software

Data clearance and analysis were performed by SPSS version 27, and R version 4.3.2 with lqmix1.1 package. The standard scores of all anthropometric measure indexes were computed by zscorer 0.3.1 package. The R codes were adapted from manual of lqmix 1.1 Package by [27].

Results and discussion

Descriptive results

Figure 1; Table 2 reveal important characteristics of the shape of the HAZ response distribution. The histogram in Fig. 1 clearly shows that HAZ is not normally distributed. Ideally, standardized variables should follow a normal distribution with a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one. However, in this case, the HAZ variable has a mean of −1.59 and a standard deviation of 2.496, indicating substantial deviation from normality. Furthermore, the descriptive statistics in Table 2 show that both skewness and kurtosis exceed the commonly accepted thresholds for a symmetric distribution, suggesting that the data are skewed and influenced by outliers. These distributional irregularities imply that using mean-based regression models may yield misleading results. Alternative modeling approaches that account for skewness and outliers, such as quantile regression, may therefore be more appropriate for this analysis.

Quantile regressions analysis results

As presented in Table 3, the selection of the optimal lqmHMM for fixed quantile τ ∈ [0,1] was conducted to determine the appropriate number of mixture components (Gτ) and hidden states (mt) for each quantile. The search_lqmix function in R was utilized with the following specifications: number of mixture components G = 3, number of hidden states, m = 3, maximum number of iterations for the EM algorithm set to 1,000 (maxit = 1000), convergence tolerance level (eps = 10− 5), and 40 random starts. The model selection was performed for quantiles 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 0.9. Based on model selection criteria (BIC, AIC and LL), the optimal model identified for the 10th, 75th and 90th quantiles was the lqmHMM with both time-constant and time-varying random coefficients of G = 3, m = 3. For 25th and 50th quantiles, the optimal model was the lqmHMM with time-constant random coefficients of G = 3 and a single hidden state (m = 1). Finally, standard errors were computed using a block-bootstrap procedure with R = 50 replicates for each optimal model. Fixed and random coefficients, along with the latent processes and their corresponding standard errors, were then estimated.

The results indicate that being from zones in the Oromia region, higher maternal BMI, increased frequency of solid/semi-solid food feeding, and being a female child had a positive effect on the child’s HAZ at both the 10th and 25th quantiles. For instance, at the 10th quantile, children from West Arsi and West Hararge zones had HAZ scores that were higher by 1.0841 (SE = 0.5321) and 1.1471 (SE = 0.5082) units, respectively, compared to children from North Wollo (reference category). Similarly, at the 25th quantile, children from East Hararge, West Hararge, and Arsi zones had higher HAZ scores by 2.56 (SE = 0.176), 2.54 (SE = 0.228), and 2.73 (SE = 0.11) units, respectively, than those from North Wollo. Being a female child was associated with significantly higher HAZ scores than being male — by 0.26 (SE = 0.055) at the 10th quantile and 0.1641 (SE = 0.0544) at the 25th quantile. At the 10th quantile, children of mothers with formal education had HAZ scores higher by 0.1526 (SE = 0.0437) than those whose mothers had no formal education. Additionally, at the 25th quantile, children who had not experienced illness (fever, cough, fast breathing, or diarrhea) had HAZ scores higher by 0.1784 (SE = 0.0812) compared to those who had been sick. Table 4 also presents the estimated fixed-effect coefficients and their corresponding standard errors at the median (50th quantile). The results indicate that being from zones in the Oromia region, higher frequency of solid or semi-solid food feeding, and not having recent illness had a positive association with the median HAZ of children. For example, children who were fed solid or semi-solid foods two to three times and four or more times per day had HAZ scores that were higher by 0.238 (SE = 0.1052) and 0.3335 (SE = 0.0799) units, respectively, compared to those fed less than twice per day. Additionally, children who had not suffered from illness (such as fever, cough, fast breathing, or diarrhea) had significantly higher HAZ scores by 0.1549 (SE = 0.0572) than those who had experienced such illnesses. In contrast, children who were born with small birth weight had HAZ scores that were lower by 0.2786 (SE = 0.1147) compared to children born with greater than average birth weight.

Similarly, Table 4 presents the estimated fixed-effect coefficients with corresponding standard errors at the 75th and 90th quantiles. The findings suggest that lack of nutritional counseling is associated with a decrease in HAZ by 0.1403 units (SE = 0.0617) compared to children whose mothers received counseling services. Female children had significantly higher HAZ scores than males, by 0.1713 units (SE = 0.0710) at the 75th quantile and 0.3583 units (SE = 0.0822) at the 90th quantile. Similarly, children who were fed solid or semi-solid foods four or more times per day had HAZ scores higher by 0.1963 units (SE = 0.0897) and 0.2989 units (SE = 0.1350) at the 75th and 90th quantiles, respectively, compared to those fed less than twice per day. Conversely, children aged 2 years and older had significantly lower HAZ scores by 0.218 units (SE = 0.0448) at the 75th quantile and 0.483 units (SE = 0.0728) at the 90th quantile compared to those younger than 2 years. Interestingly, the HAZ scores of children who were fed at least five of the WHO-recommended food groups were lower by 0.2672 units (SE = 0.0848) and 0.2629 units (SE = 0.0883) at the 75th and 90th quantiles, respectively, compared to those who consumed fewer than five food groups. This finding appears counterintuitive and warrants further investigation.

Table 5 presents the estimated time-constant and time-varying random coefficients, along with their corresponding standard errors, across different quantiles. The results indicate that the random intercepts in both time-constant and/or time-varying models had negative effects on HAZ over time at all quantiles, except for the median (50th percentile) and the higher latent states at the 75th and 90th quantiles. This suggests that an increase in unobserved (latent) factors captured by the random intercepts is associated with a decline in HAZ, implying a higher risk of stunting among children under five. In other words, a one-unit increase in the unobserved heterogeneity is linked to lower child growth outcomes, particularly in the lower and middle quantiles. In the lqMM, the time-constant random slopes showed a negative effect on HAZ at the 25th and 50th quantiles, indicating a declining trend over time for children at lower and median HAZ levels. For the linear quantile hidden Markov model (lqmHMM), the random slopes had negative effects only in the lower latent states at the 10th, 75th, and 90th quantiles. This implies that among children in the lower hidden health states, HAZ scores tended to decline over time, while those in higher latent states were less affected or showed more stability.

Discussions

This study aimed to investigate the determinants of stunting among U5C. To capture both time-varying and time-invariant random shocks affecting the HAZ (prevalence of stunting) of U5C, a lqmHMM was applied using a longitudinal dataset. The model parameters were estimated via the EM algorithm, while the corresponding standard errors were obtained using block bootstrap methods. Model selection was guided by standard information criteria, namely, the BIC, AIC, and LL values. Based on these criteria, a lqmm with three mixture components and one hidden state (G = 3, M = 1) was fitted for the 25th and 50th quantiles. In contrast, the lqmHMM with three mixture components and three hidden states (G = 3, M = 3) was selected for the 10th, 75th, and 90th quantiles. The findings of these models are discussed as follows.

Finding of fixed effect

The burden of stunting in both the Amhara and Oromia regions was found to be most pronounced at the 10th and 50th quantiles, with a noticeable reduction at the 25th, 75th, and 90th quantiles. However, the severity of stunting was higher in zones from the Amhara region compared to Oromia at the 10th, 25th, and 50th quantiles, while it declined at the upper quantiles (75th and 90th). This finding aligns with previous studies [9, 12, 29, 30]. The observed difference may be attributed to several contextual factors affecting the Amhara region during the data collection period. These include the civil conflict in northern Ethiopia, widespread locust infestations, and recurring droughts. The zones selected from Amhara were particularly affected by these overlapping shocks, which likely had a more severe impact on child nutritional status in Amhara compared to Oromia [31].

Additionally, maternal BMI showed a positive and significant effect in reducing stunting at the 10th, 25th, and 75th quantiles, consistent with the findings of [9, 12, 14, 30, 32–38]. Similarly, maternal education contributed positively to reducing stunting at the 10th quantile, in line with earlier research [9, 12, 14, 30, 32–38]. This may be because educated mothers are generally more aware of child nutrition, diet diversity, hygiene practices, and appropriate childcare. As a result, they are better equipped to prevent malnutrition and promote healthy growth in their children. Nutritional counseling services and the use of iodized salt at the household level were also associated with a reduction in stunting at the 90th quantile. Moreover, female children exhibited significantly greater resistance to stunting than male children at both lower and upper quantiles (10th, 25th, 75th and 90th) which is supported by the studies in [9, 14, 34, 35, 37, 39]. Cultural effects on gender perceptions may partly explain these differences. In the Ethiopian context, especially in rural communities, boys are often prioritized for what are considered “superior” feeding options. As a result, boys tend to receive complementary foods earlier than girls and boys are often allowed more freedom to play outdoors, increasing their exposure to environmental pathogens [31, 40]. Biological differences may also play a role. Research shows that male placentas are relatively smaller in proportion to birth weight compared to female ones, potentially limiting reserve capacity during periods of nutritional stress. This makes male infants more vulnerable to food shortages [40, 41]. Additionally, girls may have stronger immune responses, particularly in their ability to produce antibodies, which offers them better protection against infection-related malnutrition [42]. In contrast, being two years or older was associated with a statistically significant negative effect on HAZ scores at the 10th, 75th, and 90th quantiles compared to children younger than two years. This may be due to the cessation of breastfeeding after age two, without a corresponding increase in feeding frequency, potentially leading to reduced growth. This finding is consistent with studies by [9, 12, 14, 29, 30, 32, 34–36, 38, 43].

Furthermore, being small at birth was significantly associated with lower HAZ scores at the 10th and 50th quantiles. Surprisingly, children who consumed at least five of the WHO-recommended food groups exhibited lower HAZ scores across all quantiles. This counterintuitive finding may be attributed to factors such as inadequate feeding frequency or insufficient portion sizes per feeding, rather than dietary diversity itself. This interpretation is supported by the positive association observed between feeding frequency and HAZ scores. These results suggest that dietary diversity alone is not sufficient; it must be accompanied by adequate feeding frequency and appropriate portion sizes to support optimal child growth. On the other hand, feeding solid or semi-solid foods at least four times per day had a significant positive impact in reducing stunting across all quantiles. However, the consumption of UHF was associated with a decline in HAZ scores at the 90th quantile. Finally, improvements in children’s health status contributed to a reduction in stunting at the 25th, 50th and 90th quantiles, corroborating findings from previous studies [9, 12, 14, 29, 30, 32, 34–36, 38, 43].

Finding of random effects

Over time, unobserved dynamic random shocks were found to increase under-five stunting across all quantiles, except at the median (50th quantile), where their influence appeared to diminish. Additionally, unobserved time-constant individual-specific heterogeneities significantly increased stunting at the 25th and 50th quantiles, as well as within the lower hidden states at the 10th, 75th, and 90th quantiles. This suggests that individual-specific unobserved factors have a stronger impact in the lower hidden states corresponding to more severe stunting at each quantile. Furthermore, the mixture probability of the first latent component (representing the stunted state) increased progressively with the quantile level. In other words, the proportion of children classified in the stunted component grew as the quantile moved from lower to higher values. Similarly, the initial probability of being in the first hidden state also increased with quantile, indicating that a larger proportion of children began in the stunted state at higher quantiles. Moreover, transitions from higher hidden states to the lower (stunted) hidden state were more frequent than transitions in the opposite direction. This pattern aligns with the findings of [35], but contrasts with those of [34, 39, 43]. These results suggest that unobserved dynamic heterogeneities among U5C increase the likelihood of transitioning into the stunted state over time, underscoring the persistent and accumulating effect of unmeasured risk factors.

The strength of the study

One of the key strengths of this study is the use of a large, high-quality dataset collected by an international institution. The sample includes rural households from five zones affected by chronic food insecurity areas where evidence-based interventions are critically needed. Importantly, the dataset contains longitudinally measured nutritional status data for U5C, allowing for robust modeling of stunting over time. The primary objective of the study was to identify the determinants of U5C, measured by HAZ). Since HAZ scores were not normally distributed, traditional mean regression methods would be inappropriate for such skewed data. To address this, the study employed a lqmHMM, which is well-suited to capturing complex data structures such as time-varying dynamic random shocks and time-constant unobserved individual heterogeneities. Model selection was carried out across fixed quantiles τ ∈ [0,1], using information criteria including the AIC, BIC, and LL. For each quantile, the optimal number of mixture components (Gτ) and hidden states (mτ) was identified. Models with the smallest AIC and BIC, and the highest LL, were selected as optimal. As a result, the lqmHMM was selected as the best-fitting model for the 10th, 75th, and 90th quantiles. For the 25th and 50th quantiles, the lqmm, using three mixture components (G = 3) and a single hidden state (m = 1), was identified as optimal. Standard errors for all final models were computed using a block-bootstrap procedure with R = 50 replicates.

Limitation of the study

This study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the findings may not generalize to all households at the national level or even across the entire study regions because the sample was limited to households participating in the Social Safety Net Program in the Amhara and Oromia regions. As a result, the outcomes may not fully reflect the nutritional realities of the broader population. Nonetheless, this study provides a valuable starting point for national surveys and future research seeking to apply advanced models such as the lqmHMM. Second, the dataset contained a considerable number of missing values in the predictor variables. While this issue was partially addressed through the inclusion of unobserved time-constant and time-varying random effects, the presence of missing data may still affect the stability and precision of the parameter estimates. Lastly, this study employed a univariate analysis, using stunting (HAZ) as the sole response variable. Future research is recommended to explore a multivariate linear quantile regression framework, incorporating additional indicators of malnutrition such as wasting and underweight to provide a more comprehensive understanding of child nutrition dynamics.

Policy implications

The current study revealed that U5Cs in the study area had an increased risk of transitioning to a stunted state than recovering back to healthy state (Table 7). In addition, being male, children above two years of age, mothers who had no education, lower BMI, low weight at birth, episode of illness and living in the Amhara region, particularly in the Waghimra and North Wollo zones, feeding frequency of children less than two per day, were significantly aggravating the risk of stunting among U5C in the study area (Table 4). These results call for planning for policies of intervention on nutrition and dietary improvement strategies like poultry rearing, adopting and scaling up nutrition-sensitive agriculture, increasing productivity in the study areas, and setting other poverty reduction activities to improve nutrition and increase diet diversity in study areas beside PSNP [19] and planning for policies for adult education and training campaigns to boost knowledge and awareness of parents on cultural views of gender, childcare, and dietary diversity, especially fasting time among rural communities in the two regions and beyond [31]. Furthermore, the findings of this study can guide policymakers in developing comprehensive strategies to mitigate the effects of random shocks and ensure sustainable improvements in child nutrition across vulnerable populations. Unless interventions are implemented, it has a high impact on children’s health and development, like irreversible physical and cognitive damage [1, 2, 7].

Conclusions

The study revealed a significant burden of stunting among U5C in both the Amhara and Oromia regions. However, the severity of stunting was found to be more pronounced and worsening in the Amhara region. The analysis identified multiple contributing factors, including unobserved time-constant individual-specific random effects, time-varying random shocks and various fixed effects. These factors contribute to increased stunting and elevate the probability of children transitioning into the stunted state over time. Without timely and targeted interventions, the situation in these regions is likely to deteriorate further.

Recommendations

Based on the findings, the identified risk factors for under-five stunting call for a coordinated effort by both government bodies and non-governmental organizations (NGOs) to design and implement targeted interventions in the study areas. The authors recommend that stakeholders develop responsive strategies to address both dynamic shocks (e.g., drought, conflict, locust infestation, pandemics) and time-constant individual-level heterogeneities. Key recommended actions include raising community awareness on child nutrition and feeding practices, improving dietary diversity and feeding frequency, enhancing child healthcare services to support healthy growth and development, supporting maternal health and education through adult education programs and nutrition-sensitive agriculture, aimed at improving maternal BMI and birth outcomes.
