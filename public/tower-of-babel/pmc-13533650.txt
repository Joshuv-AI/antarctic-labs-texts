Annular Repair Using an All‐Suture Anchor Under Unilateral Biportal Endoscopy: Technical Note and Case Series
Authors: Pengzhi Shi, Jiarui Liu, Chao Li, Mingyu Gao, Sheng Chang, Xuexiao Ma, Hongfei Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveLarge annular defects after lumbar discectomy remain a major risk factor for recurrent herniation, particularly when the annular tear is located adjacent to the bony endplate, for which no well‐established endoscopic repair strategy is currently available. To evaluate the feasibility and early clinical outcomes of a novel endoscopic annular‐repair technique using an all‐suture anchor implanted into the superior surface of the inferior vertebral endplate, performed entirely through unilateral biportal endoscopy (UBE), for closure of large annular defects near the endplate after lumbar discectomy.MethodsWe present a novel annular repair technique performed entirely under UBE using an all‐suture anchor implanted into the superior surface of the inferior vertebral endplate, which is particularly suitable for lumbar disc herniation (LDH) cases involving downward migration of disc fragments. A monofilament shuttle is advanced across the annular tear, enabling passage of high‐strength sutures that approximate the torn annulus to bone. We developed a bone‐to‐annulus repair method in which a monofilament shuttle is passed across the annular tear under direct UBE visualization to deliver high‐strength braided sutures through the defect; these sutures are then secured to an all‐suture anchor placed in the endplate, tension‐tested, and the knot buried within the annulus. The technique was applied in a preliminary consecutive series of five patients presenting with 5–6 mm full‐thickness annular defects discovered intraoperatively during UBE discectomy. Procedural time, intraoperative complications, and early clinical and radiographic outcomes were recorded, follow‐up included one‐month clinical assessment and imaging.ResultsThe annular repair was technically feasible in all five cases, with the repair step adding a mean of 16 min to the standard discectomy. There were no intraoperative complications related to anchor placement or suture passage. All patients reported rapid relief of radicular symptoms and were mobilized early postoperatively. At one‐month follow‐up, clinical examination and imaging demonstrated intact annular repairs with no evidence of recurrent herniation or implant migration.ConclusionThe described UBE all‐suture anchor technique enables direct bone‐to‐annulus fixation and safe closure of moderate (5–6 mm) full‐thickness annular defects through standard endoscopic portals. Early results indicate technical feasibility, safety, and promising short‐term clinical outcomes. Further biomechanical validation and longer‐term, larger‐cohort studies are required to determine its effectiveness in reducing recurrent LDH.Keywords: all‐suture anchor, annular repair, lumbar disc herniation, unilateral biportal endoscopy

Introduction

Lumbar disc herniation (LDH) is the most common degenerative disorder of the spine, with an incidence of approximately 18%–21% [1, 2]. Management strategies include conservative therapy and surgical intervention. Endoscopic discectomy has become the preferred surgical option for LDH; however, recurrent LDH remains a major factor affecting postoperative satisfaction [3]. During spinal endoscopic removal of herniated nucleus pulposus, the locally bulging annulus fibrosus is often excised as part of the herniated fragment, resulting in structural defects of varying sizes. In the short term, the loss of annular integrity eliminates its barrier function, making annular defects the primary mechanical cause of early recurrence [4]. Over time, the defect is gradually filled by scar tissue, forming a biomechanically vulnerable region that predisposes to mid‐ and long‐term recurrence. Published studies report a recurrence rate of 5%–15% following minimally invasive discectomy, with reoperation rates of approximately 5%–19% [5, 6]. Therefore, reducing postoperative recurrence is a key research focus.

Several risk‐factor analyses for LDH recurrence have identified annular defect size as an independent predictor, in addition to patient‐related factors such as age, sex, occupation, and lifestyle [7]. Patients with larger annular defects (≥ 6 mm) after primary discectomy have significantly higher risks of reherniation and revision surgery [8]. Thus, restoring the integrity of the annulus fibrosus is one of the most critical measures to prevent recurrent disc extrusion. Annular repair was first described by Yasargil in 1977. In the following years, several researchers performed animal studies to evaluate the feasibility of annular suturing, demonstrating that annular repair could substantially slow postoperative disc degeneration [9, 10, 11]. Early strategies mainly relied on direct suturing through open or microscopic approaches, but these techniques could not be implemented in a fully endoscopic setting. With advances in surgical instrumentation and visualization, endoscopic annular closure devices have enabled the possibility of repairing annular defects in a minimally invasive environment. Multiple clinical studies have reported that endoscopic annular repair significantly reduces postoperative recurrence of LDH [12, 13].

Conventional annular suturing techniques approximate the torn edges of the annulus using simple sutures, aiming to restore the continuity of the annular wall. However, defects located near the bone–soft tissue junction—particularly in cases of downward‐migrated or sequestered LDH that extend close to the superior or inferior endplate—are often difficult to address using these methods. All‐suture anchors widely used in shoulder and knee ligament reconstruction offer advantages including small diameter, high pullout strength, and secure fixation beneath the cortical bone [14, 15]. These anchors can provide strong biomechanical support comparable to ligament reconstruction [16]. In annular repair, if the annulus can be tensioned and anchored to the bony endplate, this approach may offer greater biomechanical stability and provide an opportunity to enhance early rehabilitation after LDH surgery.

Here, we introduce a novel annular repair technique performed entirely under unilateral biportal endoscopy (UBE). Using an all‐suture anchor (approximately 2.0–2.8 mm in diameter), the anchor is deployed into the superior endplate of the inferior vertebra at the target level. A PDS suture is used as a shuttle to facilitate passage of the anchor suture through the annular defect, thereby creating a robust bone–annulus construct that enables high‐strength closure of the defect. This technique is particularly suitable for LDH cases involving inferiorly migrated or sequestrated disc fragments with annular defects adjacent to the vertebral endplate. The objectives of this study were to: (i) describe the detailed surgical technique of UBE‐assisted annular repair using an all‐suture anchor, including the key technical steps for anchor deployment, suture passage, and annular closure; (ii) evaluate the initial technical feasibility and short‐term clinical outcomes of this technique in patients undergoing endoscopic discectomy with large annular defects; and (iii) discuss the potential indications, technical considerations, and limitations of applying all‐suture anchor fixation for annulus fibrosus–endplate reconstruction in LDH.

Methods

Patient Selection Criteria

Inclusion criteria now include: (1) single‐level LDH confirmed by MRI and clinical correlation; (2) Imaging‐confirmed extruded or sequestered LDH; (3) presence of radicular symptoms refractory to conservative treatment; (4) intraoperative confirmation of a 5–6 mm full‐thickness annular defect; (5) Intraoperative confirmation that the annular defect is located near the superior margin of the endplate. (6) preserved disc height without severe collapse. Exclusion criteria now include: (1) osteoporosis or poor bone quality; (2) segmental instability or spondylolisthesis; (3) prior lumbar surgery at the index level; (4) advanced disc degeneration (e.g., severe collapse or Modic changes); (5) active infection, tumor, or other contraindications to surgery.

During the study period, a total of 10 consecutive patients with symptomatic LDH were initially assessed for eligibility. After screening, five patients were excluded: two because of osteoporosis, two because of Modic changes, and one because of spondylolisthesis. Ultimately, five patients fulfilled the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in this study.

Surgical Technique

Under general anesthesia, the patient is positioned prone on a radiolucent operating table. The target level is identified using a C‐arm fluoroscope by locating the junction between the spinous process and lamina. Based on this reference, two paramedian incisions (approximately 1 cm each) are made along the medial border of the pedicle, spaced 1–1.5 cm apart. Taking a left‐sided approach as an example, the caudal incision serves as the working portal, established using sequential dilators and soft tissue dissectors, while the cranial incision is used for insertion of a 4.0‐mm, 30° endoscope. Continuous saline irrigation is maintained throughout the procedure (approximately 30–40 mmHg), flowing into the surgical field through the viewing portal and exiting via the working portal.

Through the two portals, soft tissue overlying the interlaminar space is cleared using a 30° radiofrequency probe. Ipsilateral laminotomy and partial medial facetectomy are then performed using a high‐speed drill and Kerrison rongeurs to expose the insertion of the ligamentum flavum. After removal of the ligamentum flavum, the dural sac and traversing nerve root are identified. Gentle medial retraction of the nerve root exposes the posterior annulus of the intervertebral disc (Figure 1A). Standard UBE microdiscectomy is subsequently performed using pituitary forceps and curettes to remove herniated nucleus pulposus fragments until adequate neural decompression is achieved. The annular defect is identified using a nerve probe, typically measuring approximately 5–6 mm in height. Throughout the procedure, care is taken to preserve intact annular fibers and protect all neural structures (Figure 1B).

Once the annular defect is confirmed, annular repair is initiated. A PDS suture (Polydioxanone, ETHICON, USA, W9236T) is first loaded into a suture‐passing hook. Under direct endoscopic visualization, the hook is introduced into the intervertebral disc space from outside to inside, penetrating the superior margin of the annular defect (Figure 1C,K). After the tip of the hook enters the central disc space, the PDS suture is advanced through the hook and subsequently retrieved using a curette or grasper, thereby creating a closed shuttle loop across the defect.

Attention is then directed to preparation of the inferior vertebral endplate. A guiding sleeve is positioned 3–5 mm below the superior endplate of the inferior vertebra. A 2.4‐mm drill is advanced through the sleeve to create a pilot hole approximately 10 mm in depth (Figure 1D,L). After removal of the drill, an all‐suture anchor (REJOIN, JoinFix, China, 801102200) is inserted into the prepared hole through the guide sleeve (Figure 1E,M). Following release of the proximal locking mechanism, the inserter is removed without moving the guide sleeve. Tension is applied to the suture limbs to allow the anchor to expand and secure firmly against the cortical bone. After removal of the guide sleeve, additional tension is applied to confirm stable fixation and to ensure smooth sliding of the suture within the anchor (Figure 1F).

Subsequently, one limb of the braided suture from the anchor is tied to the PDS shuttle suture. The PDS suture is then pulled to guide the braided suture through the superior margin of the annular defect (Figure 1G,N). At this stage, one end of the braided suture remains anchored within the vertebral body, while the other end exits through the superior edge of the annular tear (Figure 1H,O).

Finally, the annular defect is closed by tying the braided suture. A knot pusher is used to advance the knot along the working portal and secure it firmly between the vertebral endplate and the annular surface (Figure 1I–J,P). Under endoscopic visualization, appropriate tension is applied to approximate the torn annular tissue tightly against the bone, eliminating any residual gap. Additional half‐hitches are placed to reinforce knot security. Gentle probing is performed to confirm that the annular tissue is firmly anchored without residual separation.

Throughout the repair process, the traversing nerve root is protected using a nerve retractor. Upon completion, the surgical field is irrigated and hemostasis is achieved. Both portals are then closed in a standard layered fashion, with musculofascial and skin closure typically performed using absorbable sutures.

Postoperative Rehabilitation Protocol

To minimize variability in postoperative management and reduce potential confounding factors in outcome assessment, a standardized rehabilitation protocol was implemented for all patients. Early mobilization was encouraged, with patients allowed to ambulate under supervision after drain removal on the first postoperative day. A lumbosacral brace was prescribed for approximately 4 weeks and was recommended during routine daily activities to provide external support and limit excessive spinal motion. During the initial 4–6 weeks, patients were instructed to avoid heavy lifting, repetitive bending, excessive lumbar flexion or rotation, and prolonged sitting. After the first postoperative month, patients were gradually allowed to resume normal daily activities. At this stage, a structured rehabilitation program focusing on lumbar stabilization, core muscle strengthening, and controlled range‐of‐motion exercises was initiated under guidance. This standardized postoperative protocol was applied consistently across all cases to enhance recovery, protect the annular repair site, and ensure comparability of clinical outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

Given the small sample size of this preliminary case series, statistical analysis was limited to exploratory preoperative and postoperative comparisons. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range). Because only five paired observations were available and the distribution of the outcome variables could not be reliably assumed to be normal, preoperative and postoperative clinical scores were compared using a non‐parametric paired test. A two‐sided p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Results

Patient Characteristics

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University (IRB/IEC No. QYFY_WZLL30940). Between January and March 2025, we conducted an initial clinical series involving 5 patients with symptomatic LDH (3 males and 2 females; mean age: 48 years; range: 35–62). The mean body mass index (BMI) was 25.4 kg/m [2]. All patients presented with a single‐level posterior LDH and were noted intraoperatively to have a large annular defect (approximately 5–6 mm in height) during standard UBE discectomy. Treated levels included L4–L5 (3 cases) and L5–S1 (2 cases). Each patient underwent conventional UBE microdiscectomy followed by all‐suture anchor–assisted annular repair as described. No supplemental devices or commercial annular closure implants were used in any case.

Surgical Outcomes

All procedures were completed fully under endoscopic visualization, with no intraoperative complications. The mean estimated blood loss was 50.4 ± 12 mL, and the annular repair step added an average of 16 min to the standard discectomy time. The mean postoperative hospital stay was 6.4 ± 0.5 days. The annular repair step added an average of 16 min to the standard discectomy time. All radicular symptoms were significantly alleviated postoperatively. No patients developed new neurological deficits, and no transfusions were required. All patients were mobilized on the day after surgery and allowed routine daily activities with the use of a lumbosacral brace. No postoperative restrictions beyond our standard discectomy protocol were necessary.

Clinical Outcomes and Radiographic Findings

At 1‐month follow‐up, all patients reported marked improvement in radicular leg pain. The mean leg‐pain VAS score improved from 6.8 ± 1.3 preoperatively to 1.5 ± 0.9 postoperatively (p < 0.0001). The mean Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) improved from 61.1 ± 10.2 to 29.6 ± 5.2 at 1 month (p < 0.001). None of the patients reported recurrent or new radicular symptoms. Neurological examinations were unchanged or improved in all cases.

No patients demonstrated clinical or imaging evidence of recurrent herniation at 1 month. One patient underwent routine MRI for unrelated reasons; both scans confirmed an intact annular repair with no residual herniated material (Figure 2). Radiographs showed no implant migration or adverse osseous reactions around the anchor fixation site. Representative postoperative CT images confirmed satisfactory anchor positioning within the inferior vertebral endplate and no evidence of residual disc fragments or early recurrent herniation (Figure S1).

These preliminary observations suggest that annular repair using an all‐suture anchor under UBE visualization is technically feasible and safe in the short term. In all five cases, vertebral‐to‐annulus fixation was secure, with no early failure or device‐related complications. All patients recovered as expected following standard discectomy, and the anchor‐based repair did not impede early mobilization or result in additional postoperative discomfort. It should be noted that the current outcomes are limited to early postoperative follow‐up. While no cases of recurrent herniation or repair failure were observed within the first month, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary, as longer‐term follow‐up is necessary to determine sustained clinical benefit and structural integrity. Nevertheless, this early experience supports the practicality of the technique and warrants further systematic investigation.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a novel annular repair technique by applying an all‐suture anchor under conventional UBE to reconstruct the annulus fibrosus–bone interface. This technique may provide distinct advantages for annular tears located adjacent to the bony endplate, particularly in cases of inferiorly migrated or sequestrated LDH. Early clinical application demonstrated satisfactory annular closure, and no signs of postoperative recurrence were observed during short‐term follow‐up.

Comparison With Existing Annular Repair Techniques

It is well established that large annular defects (≥ 6 mm) after lumbar discectomy are strongly associated with an increased risk of recurrent LDH [8]. The annulus fibrosus normally serves as a circumferential restraint against nucleus pulposus extrusion; once its integrity is disrupted, the remaining nucleus can reherniate through the defect under physiological loading. The goal of annular repair is therefore to restore this barrier function and facilitate biological healing [17, 18]. Previous attempts at annular closure have included open suture repair, cylindrical plugs, and mesh‐based devices [19, 20, 21]. For example, the bone‐anchored mesh implant Barricaid has demonstrated reduced reherniation rates in patients with large defects; however, it requires an open approach and is frequently combined with fusion [22]. Traditional suturing—whether open or microendoscopic—has been proposed, but reliable closure through a minimally invasive corridor remains technically challenging. In contrast, the UBE approach used in this study allows direct endoscopic visualization and manipulation of the annular defect, providing surgeons with an expanded range of repair options. Traditional endoscopic annular repair relies on a single‐needle piercing technique with a T‐shaped suture anchor to approximate the superior and inferior margins of the defect, followed by knot tying (Figure 3). When the tear originates from the inferior border of the superior vertebra or the superior border of the inferior vertebra, the effectiveness of this conventional approach is frequently suboptimal. Moreover, the closing tension provided by standard sutures is limited, whereas defects at the bone–soft tissue junction typically require sufficient anchorage strength to achieve a secure and durable annular closure.

Restoration of the Annulus–Endplate Interface Using All‐Suture Anchors

All‐suture anchors bring several advantages commonly seen in arthroscopic sports‐medicine implants into the context of spine surgery [23, 24]. They require only a very small drill hole (≈2–3 mm) and leave no bulky hardware behind. Once deployed, the anchor sits entirely within the cancellous bone of the vertebral body, forming a low‐profile construct that is particularly advantageous in the confined working space of endoscopic surgery. The braided ultra–high molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) sutures used in these systems are known for their high tensile strength and reliable knot security, characteristics that have been well established in orthopedic applications such as rotator cuff and tendon‐to‐bone repair [23, 25]. Previous biomechanical studies have demonstrated that all‐suture‐based fixation within cancellous or subcortical bone can provide reliable load‐sharing and biomechanical performance in orthopedic repair settings [26, 27, 28]. Similar fixation principles may apply to vertebral endplate anchoring in the present technique. However, it should be noted that the spinal motion segment is subjected to complex multidirectional and cyclic loading conditions, which may differ from those encountered in tendon‐bone interfaces. Therefore, the long‐term mechanical behavior and fatigue resistance of this anchor‐based repair strategy in the spinal environment remain to be validated through future cadaveric biomechanical studies and finite element analyses.

Biomechanical Considerations

In the present technique, the anchor is deployed into the subcortical cancellous bone of the vertebral endplate, where similar principles of load sharing and suture fixation may be applicable. In our experience, the soft woven anchor toggles beneath the subchondral surface, providing stable fixation without the need for rigid hardware. Compared with conventional bone anchors or plug‐based devices, which often require larger channels (5–7 mm) and may increase the risk of endplate injury, the all‐suture anchor system minimizes structural disruption while maintaining fixation capability. Functionally, this approach enables bone‐to‐annulus fixation analogous to tendon‐to‐bone repair by re‐approximating the torn annular edges to a stable bony substrate, thereby promoting a mechanically favorable environment for healing. The knot is seated flush at the annulus–endplate interface, effectively sealing the defect while avoiding irritation of adjacent neural structures. Our early clinical experience is encouraging. At one‐month follow‐up, all annular defects remained sealed and patients experienced rapid symptom improvement, comparing favorably with historical controls who underwent discectomy alone. Notably, Wang et al. recently reported favorable one‐year outcomes using an arthroscopic bone‐anchor annular repair technique without reoperation [29]. Their method, however, employed a lateral portal and customized instrumentation. The unique advantage of our approach is that it utilizes standard UBE portals and conventional all‐suture anchors, facilitating integration into routine endoscopic workflows.

Surgical Learning Curve

It is important to acknowledge that proficiency in this procedure requires a learning curve, particularly for surgeons who are unfamiliar with endoscopic suturing and anchor‐based fixation techniques. Although the current study was not designed to quantitatively evaluate the learning curve, our initial clinical experience provides preliminary insights into the technical adaptation process. In this series, all procedures were performed by surgeons experienced in UBE surgery but without prior experience with this specific annular repair technique. With increasing familiarity, the operative workflow became more standardized, particularly regarding shuttle placement, anchor deployment, and endoscopic knot management. The primary technical challenges during the early learning phase include precise shuttle placement across the annular defect, accurate positioning and deployment of the all‐suture anchor within the vertebral endplate, and achieving appropriate knot tension under limited endoscopic visualization. In our initial cases, the annular repair procedure prolonged the operative time by approximately 15–20 min compared with standard UBE discectomy. However, with growing surgical experience and technical refinement, surgeons can more reliably identify the optimal anchor position, determine appropriate suture tension, and minimize operative duration. Additionally, as with any new technique, mastering suturing and knot tying under endoscopic conditions requires practice to reduce the risk of failure. The feasibility of annular repair using this technique is closely related to the location and morphology of the defect. Based on our experience, annular defects associated with inferiorly migrated or sequestered disc herniations are most suitable for this approach, as the torn annular edges can be effectively mobilized and approximated toward the inferior endplate. In contrast, defects located near the superior endplate or those with irregular, multidirectional tear patterns may limit the ability of a single‐anchor construct to achieve sufficient tension and stable fixation. Furthermore, very large defects or severely degenerated annular tissue may exceed the mechanical capacity of suture‐based repair alone, potentially compromising fixation stability. These considerations underscore that appropriate patient selection and careful intraoperative assessment of tear morphology are critical determinants of technical success.

Limitations

A limitation of the present study is the relatively short follow‐up duration. Although early clinical improvement and structural integrity of the repair were observed, a 1‐month follow‐up is insufficient to evaluate long‐term outcomes, particularly the prevention of recurrent disc herniation. Given that recurrence may occur months to years after discectomy, caution is warranted when interpreting the current findings. While we did not observe suture cut‐through in our small cohort, the annulus is a relatively soft tissue and may stretch under chronic spinal loading. Whether this repair can withstand long‐term physiological stress remains uncertain. This study was intended as a technical note to demonstrate procedural feasibility and early safety under UBE, rather than to establish definitive clinical efficacy. Ongoing follow‐up of this cohort is being conducted, and future studies with larger sample sizes and extended observation periods (≥ 6–12 months) will be necessary to validate the durability of the repair and its potential role in reducing recurrence rates. Ultimately, controlled clinical trials will be required to determine whether this repair meaningfully reduces long‐term recurrence rates compared with discectomy alone.

Conclusion

We developed a novel endoscopic annular repair technique using all‐suture anchors under UBE guidance. This bone‐to‐annulus fixation method enables closure of lumbar annular defects through minimally invasive portals without the need for open exposure or bulky implants. In our initial cohort of five patients with annular defects measuring 5–6 mm and located near the endplate, this technique achieved safe defect closure and favorable short‐term clinical outcomes. By adapting arthroscopic soft‐tissue anchor technology to the spine, we provide a new tool for annular repair that may reduce the risk of recurrent herniation. Longer‐term follow‐up and further investigation are required to clarify its role in clinical practice.
