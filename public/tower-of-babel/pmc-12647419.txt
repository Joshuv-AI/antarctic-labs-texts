Histone demethylase KDM9 activates the CCND1/AKT pathway to promote the malignant progression of gliomas through interaction with BRD2
Authors: Jin Cheng, Xingzhi Peng, Siyuan Tang, Peijun Zhou, Zhuan Zhou, Zenghui Mao, Dan Li, Lifang Yang, Jinwu Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGliomas are highly aggressive brain tumors with limited treatment options, thus, it is necessary to identify new molecular targets for therapeutic intervention. Epigenetic aberration plays a vital role in the malignant progression of tumors. KDM9 is a histone demethylase, but its function and mechanism in tumors remain unclear. In the present study, we found that KDM9 was highly expressed in malignant gliomas and that it promoted the proliferation, invasion, stem-like characteristics, and temozolomide (TMZ) resistance of tumor cells. Mechanistically, KDM9 mediated the demethylation of H4K20me2 on the CCND1 promoter and interacted with the transcription factor BRD2 to facilitate CCND1 transcription, further enhancing the AKT signaling pathway, thereby promoting the malignant progression and TMZ resistance of glioma cells. In vivo experiments also demonstrated that KDM9 knockdown suppressed xenograft growth and TMZ resistance. Our study clarified the role and mechanism of the epigenetic regulatory molecule KDM9 in the malignant progression and drug resistance of gliomas, thus providing a potential predictive and interventional target for glioma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05900-9.Keywords: KDM9, CCND1, BRD2, Glioma, TMZ sensitivity

Introduction

Glioma is the most common and aggressive malignant tumor of the central nervous system, accounting for over 70% of primary intracranial tumors [1]. Currently, the standard treatment for glioma is surgical resection combined with adjuvant therapy such as chemotherapy (temozolomide, TMZ) and radiotherapy [2]. However, the clinical efficacy of glioma treatment still needs to be improved, as patients have a high recurrence rate and poor prognosis [3]. Therefore, identifying the key molecules and mechanisms driving the malignant progression of glioma is significantly important for providing predictive and intervention targets.

Epigenetic reprogramming is a key driving factor in the malignant progression of tumors [4]. Among them, histone methylation primarily involves the methylation of lysine (K) residues at the N-terminus of the histones H3 and H4, including monomethylation (me1), dimethylation (me2), and trimethylation (me3) [5]. Histone methylation is mainly regulated through histone lysine methyltransferases (KMTs) and lysine demethylases (KDMs), thereby influencing transcriptional regulation [6]. Past research has found that methylation at specific sites can have a dual effect on gene transcription, either suppressing or activating it. Typically, the methylation of H3K9, H3K27, and H4K20 inhibits gene expression, while the methylation of H3K4, H3K36, and H3K79 activates gene transcription [7]. KDMs play a significant role in regulating the proliferation, stemness, and metastasis of tumor cells. For example, KDM6B mediates the demethylation of H3K27me3 at the LDHA promoter and promotes its expression, thereby enhancing the proliferation and metastasis of osteosarcoma [8]. KDM2A suppresses the expression of the tumor suppressor RARRES3 by demethylating H3K36me2 at its promoter, promoting the proliferation and invasion of bladder cancer cells [9]. In colorectal cancer, KDM3 promotes the transcription of β-catenin by demethylating H3K9me2 at its promoter, thus increasing the stemness of tumor cells and promoting malignant progression in colorectal cancer [10].

KDM9 belongs to Jumonji C (JmjC) domain-containing histone demethylase family, which utilizes its catalytic domain to specifically demethylate histone H4 lysine 20 (H4K20me2/me3), and utilizes zinc-binding domains to regulate targeted substrates [11]. In mouse spermatogenesis, KDM9 not only demethylates H4K20me2 but also demethylates H4K20me3 [12]. A recent study found that Linc01711 activates the transcription of Lys phosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 1 (LPCAT1) by specifying the histone modification pattern through recruiting the acetyltransferases HBO and KDM9 to the LPCAT1 promoter, consequently facilitating cholesterol synthesis, thereby promoting gastric cancer progression [13]. Notably, high expression of KDM9 has been reported to be independently associated with poor prognosis in breast cancer and invasive cervical cancer [14, 15]. Recently, a single-cell sequencing data revealed that KDM9 is aberrantly upregulated and exhibiting strong correlations with aggressive clinical phenotypes and immunosuppressive microenvironments [16]. However, the function and mechanism of KDM9 in malignant tumors are not yet clear.

In our previous study, the primary and recurrent samples from six pairs of glioma patients were collected for proteomic analysis, and we found that KDM9 was significantly upregulated in recurrent gliomas [17]. In this study, we found that KDM9 downregulated H4K20me2 methylation and recruited the transcription factor BRD2 to the CCND1 promoter, thus upregulating CCND1 expression, which further activated the AKT signaling pathway, thereby promoting the malignant progression of glioma and reducing TMZ sensitivity.

Materials and methods

Plasmids and reagents

The pcDNA3.1-Flag (#20011) and pcDNA3.1-His (#52534) vectors were obtained from Addgene, while the pLVX-shRNA1 vector (632177) was purchased from Clontech (Mountain View, CA). The pcDNA3.1-KDM9-Flag plasmid (42146), pcDNA3.1-CCND1-Flag plasmid (48980), and pGL3-Basic-CCND1 plasmid (49233) were purchased from Youbio (Changsha, China). The luciferase reporter vector pGL3-Basic (E1751) and renilla luciferase reporter gene control vector pRL-TK (E2241) were obtained from Promega (Madison, WI). The ApaI (1604, Takara, Tokyo, Japan) and EcoRI (1611, Takara) restriction enzymes were used to digest the pcDNA3.1-His vector, and the BRD2 coding sequence was then inserted into the digested vector using homologous recombination reagents (C214, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) to obtain the BRD2 expression plasmid pcDNA3.1-BRD2-His. Using the pcDNA3.1-KDM9-Flag plasmid as a template, the KDM9-MUT (H582A/D584A) mutant plasmid was constructed using mutation reagents (C215, Vazyme). The primer sequences are shown in Table S1. The KDM9 and CCND1 interference sequences, as well as control sequences, were inserted into the pLVX-shRNA1 plasmid digested with BamHI (1605, Takara) and EcoRI. The T4 DNA ligase (2011A, Takara) was used for ligation to obtain the knockdown KDM9 plasmids (shKDM9#1, shKDM9#2), knockdown CCND1 plasmids (shCCND1#1, shCCND1#2), and shNC plasmid. The interference sequences are shown in Table S2. TMZ was purchased from MedChem Express (HY-17364, Monmouth Junction, NJ).

Cell culture and transfection

The normal human brain glial cells HEB; the human glioma cells LN229 (ATCC: CRL-2611), SW1088 (ATCC: HTB-12), and T98G (ATCC: CRL-1690), U118 (ATCC: HTB-15), U251, and human embryonic kidney cells 293 T (ATCC: CRL-11268) were obtained from the Cell Center of Central South University. The TMZ-resistant GBM cell lines T98G-R and U118-R were provided by professor Lunquan Sun (Xiangya Hospital, Central South University) [18]. LN229-luc cells were obtained from iCell Bioscience Inc. (iCell-0125a, Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM (C11995500BT, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (04–001-1ACS, Biological Industries, Kibbutz Beit Haemek, Israel) and incubated in a 5% CO2, 37 °C humidified incubator. Plasmid extraction was performed using the EndoFree Plasmid Maxi Kit (CW2105S, CWBIO, Beijing, China) and plasmid transfection was performed through Neofect (TF201201, Neofect Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For stable cell line construction, shNC, shKDM9#1, and shKDM9#2 plasmids were transfected into 293 T cells using the lentiviral expression system (PT5135-1, Takara) for 48 h. Then, the supernatant was collected and co-cultured with LN229, LN229-luc, and SW1088 cells, and the cells were used with puromycin (p8230, Solarbio, Beijing, China) to obtain the following stable cell lines: LN229-shNC, LN229-shKDM9#1, LN229-shKDM9#2, LN229-luc-shNC, LN229-luc-shKDM9, SW1088-shNC, SW1088-shKDM9#1, and SW1088-shKDM9#2.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (AG21102, Accurate Biotechnology, Changsha, China). According to the manufacturer's instructions, total cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (AU341-02, TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). qPCR was performed using an ABI 7500 instrument (Foster City, CA) with 2 × SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (B21203, Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX). Primer sequences for qPCR are listed in Table S3.

Western blot

Cells were lysed with IP buffer (87787, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with the protease inhibitor cocktail (4693116001, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) to obtain total protein. The protein concentration was measured with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (AR0197, BOSTER Biological Technology, Wuhan, China). Samples were prepared with SDS-PAGE loading buffer (CW0027S, CWBIO) and heated at 100 °C for 10 min. Equal amounts of protein in the samples were separated via SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA). After blocking for 30 min, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The following day, membranes were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, and the protein expression was visualized using the ECL Detection Reagent detection kit (36208-A, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) on an imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). The antibodies used were as follows: KDM9 (BD-PB0935, 1:2000) was purchased from Biodragon (Suzhou, China). Nestin (334753, 1:1000), mTOR (2983S, 1:1000), rabbit IgG-HRP (14708, 1:2000), and mouse IgG-HRP (14709,1:2000) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA). CD133 (18470–1-AP, 1:1000), Histone H4 (16047–1-AP, 1:500), CCND1 (60186–1-Ig, 1:2000), SMAD2 (12570–1-AP, 1:2000), P-AKT (66444–1-IG, 1:1000), AKT (60230–2-IG, 1:1000), Flag-Tag (66008–4-Ig, 1:5000), BRD2 (22236–1-AP, 1:1000), and BRD4 (28486–1-AP, 1:1000) were purchased from Proteintech (Chicago, IL). H4K20me1 (A2370, 1:500), H4K20me3 (A2372,1:500), P-SMAD2 (AP0269, 1:1000), and Actin (AC026, 1:10000) were obtained from ABclonal (Wuhan, China). H4K20me2 (502371, 1:500) was purchased from ZENBIO (Chengdu, China). P-mTOR (AF3308,1:500) was purchased from Affinity (Jiangsu, China).

Immunoprecipitation (IP)

Total cell lysates were extracted using an IP buffer containing cocktail, and 30 μL of magnetic beads (B23201, Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX) was added to 500 μL of IP buffer containing 2 mg of total protein and rotated continuously for 2 h. After the removal of the magnetic beads, the supernatant was incubated with the indicated antibodies overnight; then, 30 μL of magnetic beads was added and rotated continuously for 2 h at 4 °C. The magnetic bead–antibody–protein complexes were washed twice with cold lysate. The supernatant was boiled with 2 × SDS-PAGE buffer for 10 min for subsequent Western blot analysis.

Bioinformatics analysis

The Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA, http://www.cgga.org.cn/) includes the functional genomics data of 2000 Chinese glioma samples. mRNA microarray data and information from 325 clinical glioma patients and 20 normal glial tissue samples were included in our database. Glioma patients were divided into high-KDM9/CCND1 and low-KDM9/CCND1 groups based on the median expression of KDM9/CCND1. Differential expression analysis, survival curve analysis, and molecular correlation analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. The clustering heat maps were created using TBtools software. For protein molecular docking analysis, the AlphaFold3 server was used to model protein complexes, and residues with a pIDDT < 50 were excluded from the analysis of binding sites (per-residue confidence scores). PRODIGY (a computational tool for prediction of binding affinity) was used to calculate the binding affinities of KDM9-BRD2 and KDM9-BRD4.

CCK-8 assay

Cells were planted in 96-well plates and cultured for 24, 48, and 72 h, respectively. CCK-8 solution (C0005, Targetmol, Boston, MA) was added to each well and incubated in the dark for 1 h. The absorbance value of each well was measured at 450 nm using a plate reader (BioTek ELx800, Winooski, VT).

Colony formation assay

The treated cells were collected and inoculated into 6-well plates with an appropriate number of cells, cultured for 2 weeks, fixed in fixative for 15 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (V5265, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) for 15 min, and then the number of clones containing more than 50 cells was counted under a microscope.

Flow cytometry

According to the manufacturer's instructions for the Annexin V-FITC/PI Kit (KGA108, KeyGEN Bio TECH, Nanjing, China), 500μL of binding buffer was added to each tube to suspend the cells; subsequently, 5μL of Annexin V-FITC was added and thoroughly mixed, followed by adding 5μL of PI. The mixture was then incubated at room temperature in the dark for 10 min. The apoptotic cells were detected via flow cytometry (MoFloXDP, Beckman Coulter, Miami, FL) within 1 h, and the data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Wound healing assay

The treated cells were seeded into 6-well plates. When the cells were fully attached, the pipette tip of 200μL was used to make a cross scratch and further cultured in serum-free medium. After 12 h, images were captured under a microscope (DMI3000B, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), and wound healing was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Transwell assay

Transwell invasion assays were performed using 24-well Cell Culture Inserts (353097, Corning, NY) coated with Matrigel (354234, Corning). The cells were added to the upper chamber with serum-free medium, and culture medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h of incubation, non-invaded cells in the upper chamber were removed. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted from three random fields.

Sphere-formation assay

1 × 105 cells were inoculated in ultralow-attachment 6-well plates (3471, Corning) with DMEM/F12 sphere-forming medium (C11330500BT, Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing B27, N-2, EGF, and bFGF (17504044, 17502048, PHG0311, and PHG0368, respectively; Thermo Fisher Scientific). After incubation for 1–2 weeks, cells were counted and photographed under a microscope (DMI3000B, Leica).

Transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA extracted from LN229 cells with KDM9 knockdown and from the control group was sent to QIAGEN Biotechnology (Beijing, China) for transcriptome sequencing (RNA-Seq). The integrity of the total RNA was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Santa Clara, CA) before library construction. The library was subjected to quality control using the Qsep-400 method and then sequenced using a NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-Seq) and ChIP-qPCR

The DNA of LN229 cells was enriched with H4K20me2 antibody (502371, ZENBIO), and the samples were sent to Kang Testing Technology (Wuhan, China) for ChIP-Seq analysis using a DNBSEQ-T7 sequencer (MGI, Shenzhen, China) and RSeQC (version 2.6) software. ChIP-qPCR was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions of the ChIP assay kit (P2078, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). In brief, cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde, and the cross-linked DNA was sonicated to generate 200-1000 bp fragments. After centrifugation, the supernatant was incubated overnight with H4K20me2 antibody. The protein–DNA complexes were de-crosslinked and purified, and enriched DNA fragments were analyzed using qPCR. Primer sequences for ChIP-qPCR are listed in Table S4.

Liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC–MS)

LN229 cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1-KDM9-Flag plasmid for 48 h, and the total protein was lysed with IP buffer containing cocktail. Subsequently, the cell lysates were incubated with magnetic beads and Flag-Tag antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and the magnetic bead–antibody–protein complex was washed with cold lysate, before being sent to PTMbio (Hangzhou, China) for mass spectrometry (TMQ Exactive Plus. Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Dual luciferase reporter assay

pGL3-Basic-CCND1, pRL-TK and other indicated plasmids were co-transfected into cells. Luciferase activity was detected using the Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (RG027, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Relative luciferase activity was analyzed after normalization against quantified pRL-TK activity.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells were transfected with the pcDNA3.1-BRD2-His plasmid, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and blocked in 5% goat serum for 60 min, followed by overnight incubation with the primary antibodies. The primary antibodies concentrations were as follows: KDM9 (1:200, #PA5-59819, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and His-tag (1:200, 66005–1-Ig, Proteinch). The corresponding secondary antibodies were incubated for 2 h, and the images were then observed and captured using a confocal microscope (LSM 510 META, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Animal experiment

To determine the effect of KDM9 on tumor growth, eighteen 5-week-old female nude mice ( BALB/c-nu) were randomly divided into two groups (n=9 per group). Subsequently, 1 × 107 LN229-shKDM9 cells were injected subcutaneously into one group, and LN229-shNC cells into the other group. To clarify the role of KDM9 in tumor TMZ sensitivity, 1 × 107 LN229-shKDM9 or LN229-shNC cells were injected subcutaneously into 12 5-week-old female nude mice to establish xenograft models, respectively; after the tumor grew to 60-80 mm3, 6 mice from each group were intraperitoneally injected with DMSO (100μL) or 100μL TMZ (40 mg/kg) dissolved in DMSO for 5 consecutive days. The tumor volume was measured using a Vernier caliper and calculated with as follows: volume (mm3) = d2 × D/2, where d and D are the short and long diameters, respectively. The nude mice were sacrificed at a certain time, and the subcutaneous tumor tissues were collected and weighed, before being fixed with 10% paraformaldehyde for immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis. For the glioma orthotopic tumor model, 5 × 105 LN229-luc-shNC or LN229-luc -shKDM9 cells in 5 μl of serum-free medium were stereotactically injected into the striatum of each 5-week-old female nude mouse (N = 12). Then, 14 days later, 6 mice from each group were intraperitoneally injected with DMSO or TMZ (40 mg/kg) for 7 consecutive days, and tumor growth was measured 30 days after tumor inoculation using an IVIS Lumina III imaging system (Perkin-Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA). The survival of mice after cell transplantation was recorded and analyzed. At the termination of the experiment, the brains of the mice were collected for H&E staining.

IHC

The tissues of 56 gliomas with the clinical information of patients from the Department of Pathology of Xiangya Hospital (2019 to 2021) [17] and transplanted tumors from animals were collected. According to the manufacturer 's instructions, IHC staining was performed using the universal two-step detection kit (PV-9000, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) and the DAB kit (ZLI-9017, ZSGB-BIO). Sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin (E607317, Sangon bio, Shanghai, China). IHC images captured using the Pannoramic SCAN II whole-slide pathology scanner (3DHISTECH, Hungary). The expression of each protein was semi-quantitatively evaluated using the previously described method [17]. Antibody concentrations were as follows: KDM9 (1:200, #PA5-59819, Invitrogen), CCND1 (1:750, 26939–1-AP, Proteintech), P-AKT (1:100, 66,444–1-IG, Proteintech), CD133 (1:500, 18470–1-AP, Proteintech), and H4K20me2 (1:50, 502371, ZENBIO).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 statistical software. Unless otherwise stated, data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and data were analyzed using two-tailed t-tests. Statistical significance was set at a P value of < 0.05.

Results

KDM9 is highly expressed and associated with recurrence and poor prognosis in glioma

In our previous study, the tandem mass tag (TMT)-based quantitative proteomic analysis of six pairs of primary and recurrent tissues from glioma patients was performed, and the results showed that histone demethylase KDM9 was the most significantly upregulated molecule [17]. We also collected six additional pairs of primary and recurrent tissues from glioma patients (Table S5). The expression of KDM9 in the 6 pairs of tissues was analyzed via Western blot analysis and in the 12 pairs via IHC staining, which confirmed that KDM9 was significantly highly expressed in recurrent glioma tissues compared with primary gliomas (Fig. 1A, B). Further, qPCR and Western blot experiments results showed that KDM9 was highly expressed at the mRNA and protein levels in glioma LN229, SW1088, U251, and T98G cells compared to normal glial cell HEB (Fig. 1C, D). Furthermore, the results from CGGA database analysis revealed that KDM9 was highly expressed in gliomas compared with normal tissues (Fig. 1E), and the expression in recurrent gliomas was also higher than that in primary gliomas (Fig. 1F). Moreover, glioma patients with high expression of KDM9 had a poorer prognosis (Fig. 1G). These results indicate that KDM9 is highly expressed in gliomas and is associated with glioma recurrence and poor prognosis.

KDM9 promotes malignant progression and reduces TMZ sensitivity in glioma

To investigate the function of KDM9 in glioma cells, cell lines with stable knockdown of KDM9 (LN229-shNC, LN229-shKDM9#1, LN229-shKDM9#2, SW1088-shNC, SW1088-shKDM9#1, SW1088-shKDM9#2) were constructed and U251 cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1-KDM9-Flag plasmid (KDM9-OE) for 48 h (Fig. S1A). CCK-8, colony formation, flow cytometry, wound healing, and transwell assay results showed that the knockdown of KDM9 inhibited cell proliferation, migration, and invasion and promoted cell apoptosis. Meanwhile, the overexpression of KDM9 presented contrary results (Fig. 2A, B, Figure S1B-D). Further, the sphere-formation assay and flow cytometry analysis revealed that KDM9 could enhance cell stem-like characteristics (Fig. 2C, Figure S1E). Consistent with this finding, Western blot analysis results showed that KDM9 upregulated the expression of CD133 and Nestin in glioma cells (Fig. 2D). To explore whether KDM9 affects TMZ chemosensitivity, the IC50 value of TMZ in glioma cells was first clarified (Figure S2A, B), and the results of the colony formation assay showed that KDM9 knockdown enhanced TMZ sensitivity compared with the control, while KDM9 overexpression attenuated TMZ sensitivity (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, LN229-shKDM9 cells were used to establish a xenograft model, and the results showed that knocking down KDM9 significantly inhibited tumor growth (Fig. 2F-H). IHC staining of the tumor tissues suggested that Ki67 expression was lower in the knockdown KDM9 group (Fig. 2I). Together, these results demonstrate that KDM9 promotes malignant progression and reduces TMZ sensitivity in glioma.

The tumor-promoting function of KDM9 depends on its demethylase activity

It has been reported that the specific demethylation site of KDM9 in animal cells is H4K20me2/3 [12]. Thus, we performed Western blot analysis, and the results showed that KDM9 could catalyze the demethylation of both H4K20me2 and H4K20me3 in glioma cells, with the demethylation of H4K20me2 being more pronounced, while increasing the level of H4K20me1 (Fig. 3A). Research has shown that the mutation of H582 and D584 residues in KDM9 led to demethylase inactivation [11]. After the transfection of pcDNA3.1-KDM9-Flag (wild-type, WT) and KDM9-MUT (H582A/D584A) plasmids into U251 cells with knockdown of KDM9, we found that enzyme inactivation could eliminate the demethylation of H4K20me2/3 induced by KDM9 (Fig. 3B). Further, functional experiments were performed, and the results indicated that mutations in KDM9 demethylase could significantly weaken the tumor-promoting function of KDM9 (Fig. 3C, D). Similarly, the results of the sphere-formation assay and Western blot analysis indicated that the overexpression of KDM9 enhanced the stem-like characteristics of glioma cells, while this effect was repressed in the mutation group (Fig. 3E, F). Moreover, the colony formation assay results showed that KDM9 overexpression reduced TMZ sensitivity, while mutation attenuated this effect (Fig. 3G). These data suggest that KDM9 induces the demethylation of H4K20me2 to H4K20me1 in glioma cells, and the tumor-promoting function of KDM9 depends on its demethylase activity.

H4K20me2 demethylation mediated by KDM9 in the CCND1 promoter region to promote its transcription

To clarify the target molecules directly regulated by KDM9 in glioma cells, RNA-Seq was performed on LN229-shKDM9 cells, and 2034 differentially expressed genes were screened and identified (P < 0.05, Log Fold change ≥ 1) (Fig. 4A); enrichment analysis of the KEGG revealed that the downstream genes of KDM9 were mainly enriched in the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, with 58 related molecules, including 22 downregulated and 36 upregulated genes (Fig. 4B). Further, the ChIP-Seq assay was performed using H4K20me2 antibody-enriched DNA from LN229 cells, with 8245 potential target genes being obtained (Fig. 4C, Figure S3A). Then, as the demethylation of H4K20 promotes gene expression, 22 downregulated genes enriched in the PI3K/AKT pathway of LN229-shKDM9 cells were selected to intersect with 8245 potential target genes, and the data showed that among the 10 possible target genes identified via integrated analysis of RNA-Seq and ChIP-seq (Fig. 4D, 4E), CGGA database analysis suggested that BCL2L11, BCL2, CCND1, and CREB3L2 in clinical samples were significantly correlated with KDM9 expression (Figure S4). The qPCR assay was performed in glioma cells, and the results showed that although knocking down KDM9 could significantly downregulate the expression of these four molecules in LN229 cells, the overexpression of KDM9 in SW1088 and U251 cells could upregulate the mRNA expression of BCL2L11, BCL2, CCND1, and CREB3L2, while CCND1 exhibited the most pronounced change (Fig. 4F, G) and Western blot experiment results also indicated that KDM9 could increase the protein expression of CCND1 (Fig. 4H). Moreover, the dual luciferase reporter assay revealed that CCND1 luciferase activity decreased after the knockdown of KDM9 in 293 T cells, suggesting that KDM9 could promote CCND1 transcription (Fig. 4I). To verify that KDM9 directly regulates the H4K20me2 level on the CCND1 promoter, the segmented primers spanning the −2000 to + 300 bp region near the transcription start sites (TSS) of CCND1 were designed and ChIP-qPCR experiments were performed (Fig. 4J). The results indicated that only the enrichment of H4K20me2 in the P4 of the CCND1 promoter was enhanced after the knockdown of KDM9, while there was no change in the enrichment of H4K20me2 in the P1-P3 and P5 promoter regions (Fig. 4K, Figure S3B-E). Taken together, KDM9 promotes the transcription of CCND1 by decreasing H4K20me2 methylation on its promoter.

KDM9 promotes glioma malignant progression and reduces TMZ sensitivity through CCND1

CGGA database analysis suggested that CCND1 was highly expressed in gliomas compared to normal tissues and was higher in recurrent samples. Glioma patients with high CCND1 expression had a poorer prognosis (Figure S5A-C). Similarly, qPCR and Western blot assay results showed that compared with HEB cells, the mRNA and protein expressions of CCND1 were highly expressed in glioma cells (Figure S5D, E). To investigate the role of CCND1 in promoting glioma malignant progression and drug resistance mediated by KDM9, rescue experiments were performed by transfecting CCND1 expression plasmids into shKDM9 glioma cells (Fig. 5A). Functional experiments results showed that the overexpression of CCND1 partially restored the suppression of cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, as well as the increase in apoptosis caused by KDM9 knockdown (Fig. 5B, C, Figure S6A-C). Further, the upregulation of CCND1 partially rescued the effects of KDM9 knockdown on stem-like characteristics and TMZ sensitivity in glioma cells (Fig. 5D, E). These results indicate that KDM9 promotes glioma malignant progression and reduces TMZ sensitivity through CCND1.

KDM9 activates the AKT pathway by upregulating CCND1

It has been reported that CCND1 can exert its function by regulating pathways such as SMAD and AKT [19–21]. Elucidating the mechanism through which CCND1 acts in glioma cells, Western blot analysis results showed that after the knockdown of KDM9 in LN229 and SW1088 cells, there was no significant change in the phosphorylation of SMAD2, whereas the phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR were downregulated, and opposite results could be observed after the overexpression of KDM9 in U251 cells (Fig. 6A). The results suggested that CCND1 may regulate the AKT signaling pathway in glioma cells. Further investigation in glioma cells with KDM9 knockdown revealed the inhibition of CCND1, P-AKT, P-mTOR, and CD133 protein expression, while the overexpression of CCND1 could restore the changes in protein expression caused by KDM9 knockdown (Fig. 6B). Notably, inhibition of AKT with MK-2206 markedly reduced the hyperactivation of the p-AKT signaling pathway resulting from KDM9 overexpression (Fig. 6C).

IHC staining was used to analyze tissue sections from 56 glioma patients, and the results showed that the expressions of CCND1, P-AKT, and CD133 were higher in the KDM9 high-expression group (Fig. 6D). The correlation analysis of the IHC staining results indicated a positive correlation between the protein expression of KDM9, CCND1, P-AKT, and CD133 (Fig. 6E). These results, when taken together, indicate that KDM9 promotes malignant tumor progression by upregulating CCND1 to activate the AKT pathway.

KDM9 promotes CCND1 transcription and activates AKT pathway through interaction with BRD2

Studies have shown that demethylases play an important role in regulating the chromatin structure for gene expression. To target specific molecular sequences in the genome, demethylases need to interact with DNA-binding proteins such as transcription factors to exert their function [22–24]. To investigate the transcription factors involved in KDM9’s regulation of CCND1 expression, we performed IP experiments to analyze the proteins interacting with KDM9 using mass spectrometry (Fig. 7A). Analyzing mass spectrometry data revealed that the transcription factors bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4) and bromodomain-containing protein 2 (BRD2) might interact with KDM9 (Fig. 7B). Further, we performed endogenous and exogenous IP experiments, and the results showed that only BRD2 interacted with KDM9 (Fig. 7C, D). Molecular docking analysis consistently predicted a stronger interaction between KDM9 and BRD2 than between KDM9 and BRD4 (Figure S7). Moreover, IF experiments showed significant colocalization of KDM9 and BRD2 in LN229 cells, mainly localized in the nucleus (Fig. 7E). Moreover, ChIP-qPCR experiments showed a decrease in the enrichment of H4K20me2 at the CCND1 P4 promoter after KDM9 knockdown, partially restored via BRD2 overexpression (Fig. 7F). The dual luciferase reporter assay revealed that wild-type KDM9, but not its mutant-type version, enhanced CCND1 transcriptional activity, and BRD2 overexpression partially reversed the decrease in CCND1 transcriptional activity caused by KDM9 knockdown (Fig. 7G). Importantly, silencing of BRD2 markedly inhibited the increased CCND1 promoter activity resulting from KDM9 overexpression, suggesting that BRD2 is essential for KDM9-regulated CCND1 transcription (Fig. 7H). Additionally, Western blot experiments showed that the overexpression of BRD2 could reverse the suppression of CCND1 and CD133 expression and the AKT pathway induced by KDM9 knockdown in glioma cells (Fig. 7I). Taken together, these results indicate that KDM9 promotes the demethylation of H4K20me2 on the CCND1 promoter and enhances the transcription of CCND1 through binding to BRD2, thereby activating the AKT pathway.

KDM9 promotes glioma growth and reduces TMZ sensitivity in vivo

To further clarify the role of KDM9 in glioma TMZ sensitivity, a xenograft model was established using LN229-shKDM9 or LN229-shNC cells. The results showed that the knockdown of KDM9 significantly enhanced glioma TMZ sensitivity compared with the control group (Fig. 8A-C). IHC staining results showed that compared with the control group, inhibiting KDM9 could upregulate the expression of H4K20me2 and downregulate the expression of CCND1, P-AKT, and CD133 (Fig. 8D). We further investigated the effect of KDM9 on TMZ sensitivity in orthotopic tumor models. The results showed that compared with the control group, both KDM9 knockdown and TMZ treatment inhibited tumor growth and substantially prolonged survival, and the combined treatment had a more significant inhibitory effect (Fig. 8E-H). The above results demonstrate that KDM9 enhances glioma growth and reduces TMZ sensitivity by promoting the transcription of CCND1 and further activating the AKT signaling pathway and CD133 expression.

Discussion

H4K20 methylation is one of the earliest identified post-translational modifications of histones, and various methylation states of H4K20 are involved in different biological functions [25]. Approximately 80% of H4 can be modified with H4K20me2, which is the most abundant form of H4K20 [26, 27], participating in DNA damage and replication and cell cycle regulation [28, 29]. H4K20me3 is thought to be associated with senescence, DNA replication, and the maintenance of heterochromatin [25], while H4K20me1 is mainly associated with transcriptional regulation [30, 31]. KDM9 primarily regulates H4K20, catalyzing the conversion of H4K20me2 and H4K20me3 to H4K20me1 during spermatogenesis in mice [12]. In this study, for the first time in tumor cells, we found that KDM9 demethylated H4K20me2 to H4K20me1 and promoted CCND1 transcription, thereby facilitating glioma malignant progression and reducing TMZ sensitivity.

CCND1 is an important cell cycle regulator and oncogene, and it is tightly regulated via epigenetic mechanisms, thereby participating in the malignant progression of tumors. For example, the overexpression of lysyl oxidase-like 2 (LOXL2) blocked CCND1 transcription by reducing H3K36ac acetylation, thereby limiting cancer development in the female reproductive system [32]. In glioblastoma, protein kinase PKM2 promoted CCND1 expression by regulating H3-T11 phosphorylation in the CCND1 promoter region, thereby promoting tumor proliferation and tumorigenesis [33]. KDM6B mediated H3K27me3 the demethylation of the CCND1 promoter, thus upregulating CCND1 expression and promoting the progression of prostate cancer [34]. Significantly, demethylases play a role in the chromatin structure regulation of gene expression, during which they usually interact with transcription factors. For instance, KDM4B interacted with c-Myc and was then recruited to c-Myc binding sites on the promoters of metabolic genes (LDHA, ENO1 and PFK), resulting in chromatin relaxation near c-Myc binding sites in a demethylase-dependent manner, thus promoting metabolic gene transcription and enhancing the Warburg effect to accelerate prostate cancer growth [23]. KDM4A interacted with STAT3 and facilitated the removal of methyl groups from H3K9me3, thereby enhancing the STAT3-mediated transcription of fibrinogen-like protein 1 (FGL1) and further promoting the proliferation and invasion of NSCLC cells [22]. The CCND1 promoter region contains multiple potential cis-regulatory elements that can bind transcription factors, thereby affecting its transcription [35]. Our study found that KDM9 mediated demethylation in the CCND1 promoter region and upregulated CCND1 expression through interaction with the BRD2. This study elucidates the epigenetic regulation mechanisms of CCND1 in glioma.

Studies have shown that CCND1 could promote the malignant progression of tumors through multiple pathways [35]. For example, CCND1 could enhance hexokinase 2 (HK2) activity and participated in aerobic glycolysis, contributing to poor prognosis in multiple myeloma [36]. CCND1 promoted self-renewal and growth in liver cancer stem cells by activating Smad signaling pathways [19]. In glioma, M2-type macrophages inhibited the expression of CCND1 in tumor cells via exosomes carrying miR-15a, thereby downregulating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and suppressing tumor cell migration and invasion [37]. In nasopharyngeal carcinoma, CCND1 activated the PI3K/AKT/c-JUN pathway and promoted downstream signals associated with cell cycle progression and EMT, thereby enhancing tumor cell growth, metastasis, and chemoresistance [21]. Our data reveal that KDM9 is significantly upregulated in TMZ-resistant GBM cell lines, suggesting that KDM9 contributes to TMZ resistance. However, TMZ does not directly alter KDM9 protein expression (Figure S2C, D). The results implied that there are other mechanisms by which KDM9 regulates the sensitivity of temozolomide. Research has shown that activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway promotes TMZ resistance in glioma cells. For example, compounds that inhibit this pathway, such as NVP-BEZ235 and FK228, have been shown to enhance TMZ sensitivity by suppressing AKT/mTOR signaling [38, 39]. XLOC/Sp1/PIK3R2 regulatory axis activates the PI3K/AKT pathway and contributes to TMZ resistance in glioblastoma [40]. And FOXM1-activated NUF2 promotes TMZ resistance in glioma cells by regulating cell proliferation, apoptosis, and autophagy via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway [41]. As shown in Fig. 6, KDM9 activates the AKT pathway by upregulating CCND1. Taken together, these findings strongly suggested that KDM9 enhances TMZ resistance through activation of the AKT signaling pathway.

In conclusion, the current study found that KDM9 could mediate the demethylation of H4K20me2 on the promoter of CCND1, as well as promote CCND1 transcription by interacting with BRD2, further activating the AKT signaling pathway and promoting the malignant progression and reducing TMZ sensitivity of glioma (Fig. 9). This study provides novel experimental evidence for the recurrence mechanism and potential targets for intervention in gliomas.
