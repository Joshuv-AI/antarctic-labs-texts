A multi-modal neuroimaging dataset on long-term cognitive training in school children
Authors: Jiali Mu, Tianyong Xu, Ye Xie, Feiyan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We present a dataset of healthy children (N = 99) to explore the impact of long-term cognitive training on multimodal brain structure, function, and cognitive performance. At the start of primary school, participants were randomly assigned to an experimental group (n = 53), which received five years of structured abacus-based mental calculation (AMC) training from Grade 1 to Grade 5, or a control group (n = 46) without additional training. Neuroimaging data, including resting-state functional MRI and T1-weighted structural MRI, were collected after the first training year (at the start of Grade 2). Behavioral data, including standardized mathematical ability tests and psychological assessments, were collected longitudinally across Grades 2 to 5. To promote open access, the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) formatted data and corresponding quality control reports are available on the Science Data Bank. It offers a unique opportunity to deepen our understanding of how long-term training shapes neural and behavioral development in childhood.

Background & Summary

Cognitive training refers to a structured and goal-oriented set of practice activities designed to enhance individual’s cognitive abilities1,2. Research has demonstrated that cognitive training can significantly effect cognitive performance, as well as the structure and function of the brain, particularly in children, whose brains exhibit heightened plasticity during development3–6. Abacus-based mental calculation (AMC) is a specific skill utilized for mental arithmetic. The execution of this skill relies on visuospatial strategies, finger-motor representations, and higher-order cognitive processes7,8. Through the engagement of multiple cognitive domains, AMC training provides a valuable window for examining how cognitively demanding training may influence the brain and behavior. It has been widely practiced across East Asia in both in-school and after-school settings to enhance children’s mathematical abilities9–11. Previous studies have indicated that AMC training can significantly enhance mathematical abilities12–14, improved executive functions15,16, and even modify brain’s function, structure and networks11,17,18.

Despite the growing interest in cognitive training, the field currently grapples with critical bottlenecks. First, publicly available datasets that integrate neuroimaging and behavioral data on cognitive training are extremely limited, particularly in non-laboratory settings. This lack of open resources significantly hampers reproducibility and cross-study comparisons, which are crucial for advancing the field19,20. Second, most existing research on cognitive training has focused predominantly on adults, with limited data on children, who may experience different developmental trajectories in response to such interventions. Furthermore, the majority of studies are characterized by short-term follow-up periods, failing to capture the long-term effects of cognitive training on both brain structure and function, as well as sustained behavioral outcomes21. Third, there is a notable lack of studies that have implemented ecologically valid, non-laboratory-based cognitive training interventions, particularly those integrated with educational practices over extended periods22,23. To address these critical gaps, our dataset combines longitudinal assessments, multimodal neuroimaging and behavioral data, offering a unique resource to explore the neural and cognitive trajectories associated with AMC training.

To further investigate these questions, we conducted a long-term AMC training study involving multimodal data collection. A total of 99 healthy children were recruited at the beginning of primary school and randomly assigned to two groups: an AMC training group (n = 53) and a control group (n = 46). The AMC group participated in 2 hours of abacus-based training per week, integrated into their school curriculum, from Grades 1 to 5. The control group followed the standard mathematics curriculum without additional training. Neuroimaging data, including resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) and T1-weighted structural MRI, were collected after the first year of training. Behavioral data, including standardized mathematical ability tests and psychological assessments, were collected annually from Grades 2 to 5. This comprehensive study design facilitates the exploration of both short-term and prolonged effects of AMC training on neural mechanisms and cognitive development.

The dataset generated in this study provides a unique resource to bridge a critical gap in understanding the effects of cognitive training on children’s cognitive abilities and brain activity. Its longitudinal design, including mathematical performance data, enables in-depth exploration of brain-behavior relationships. This dataset provides essential insights into the effectiveness of cognitive and contributes to the refinement of educational intervention strategies.

Here, we present the publicly available neuroimaging and behavioral dataset titled “A Multimodal Neuroimaging Dataset on Long-Term Cognitive Training in School Children”, hosted on the Science Data Bank (SciDB, 10.57760/sciencedb.o00133.00034)24. The dataset is meticulously organized in accordance with the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) standard25, ensuring consistency and interoperability with modern neuroimaging analysis pipelines. To date, a portion of this dataset has been utilized in a peer-reviewed publication, which investigated the role of the medial temporal lobe memory system in AMC training26. By making this dataset publicly accessible, we aim to enrich the comprehension of cognitive training and support future research and advancements in related fields of neuroscience and cognitive development.

Methods

Participants and study design

This study recruited 99 children (AMC group: n = 53; control group: n = 46) from the Chinese Abacus Training Project (CATP). Participants were enrolled at the beginning of first grade from an elementary school in Qiqihar, Heilongjiang Province, China, and followed a standardized elementary school curriculum. All participants were of Asian descent and were randomly assigned to the AMC or control group. The two groups did not differ significantly in terms of age (AMC group: M ± SD: 7.98 ± 0.59; control group: M ± SD: 8.02 ± 0.54; t(96) = −0.40, p = 0.64) and gender (AMC: 22 M/30 F; control: 22 M/24 F; χ2(1) = 0.30, p = 0.58)).

The study employed a longitudinal design with four data collection time points spanning five years (Fig. 1). Participants in the AMC group received AMC training in addition to regular mathematics classes, while those in the control group participated in standard mathematics classes supplemented by equivalent-duration conventional learning activities (e.g., arithmetic and reading). However, the study did not include an active control group, a limitation that should be addressed in future work.Fig. 1Study design. Participants were randomized to either the AMC training group or the control group at baseline.

Study design. Participants were randomized to either the AMC training group or the control group at baseline.

To ensure participants’ readiness for the assessments, math ability testing commenced after they had acquired fundamental knowledge of arithmetic operations and visuospatial reasoning. Math ability assessments were conducted at four time points, beginning in second grade (first time point) and continuing through the third, fourth, and fifth grades. Neuroimaging data were acquired at the first time point. Inclusion criteria required participants to have normal or corrected-to-normal vision, no reported history of neurological or psychiatric disorders, and no hearing impairments.

Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or guardians of all participants for their involvement in the study. We further confirm that the parents or guardians explicitly agreed to the publication of anonymized data. The consent process included a detailed explanation of data management—including usage, storage, and sharing in accordance with the approved protocol—to ensure full understanding and agreement. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University, China, and all procedures complied with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

AMC training protocol

Participants in the AMC group underwent up to five years of systematic AMC training. Training was conducted in the same elementary school in Qiqihar, with sessions lasting 2 hours per week during the school year, resulting in approximately 320 hours of training over five years, excluding absences due to vacations, exams, or other school events. The training protocol was designed to systematically develop AMC skills through a stepwise progression of increasing difficulty.

Initially, participants were introduced to the fundamental principles of abacus operation using a traditional Chinese abacus. The abacus consists of a rectangular frame divided into two sections by a horizontal bar (Fig. 2a). The upper section contains one bead per rod, representing the value of five, while the lower section has four beads per rod, each representing a value of one. A simple abacus operation was shown as Fig. 2b. Participants began their training by practicing basic arithmetic operations, including addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division, on a physical abacus to develop foundational calculation skills. As their proficiency improved, they gradually transitioned to performing mental calculations by visualizing bead movements on a “mental abacus”, initially with optional finger movements to assist the visualization process. Over time, participants advanced to performing mental arithmetic exclusively through visualization without physical aids. Training with the physical abacus continued throughout the program to reinforce their skills and enable them to tackle increasingly complex problems. Cognitive assessments

IQ Assessment

Participants’ intelligence quotient (IQ) was assessed using the Chinese version of the Combined Raven’s Test27. Raw scores were standardized according to the age norms for urban Chinese children.

Math ability assessment

Math ability was assessed using the Chinese adaptation of the Heidelberger Rechentest, a standardized test designed to evaluate both arithmetic and visuospatial mathematical skills—two cognitive domains closely linked to AMC training28. The arithmetic component consisted of six time-limited subtests: addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, filling in missing number equations, and number comparisons. The visuospatial math component included five subtests: length estimation, cube counting, number sequencing, object counting, and connecting numbers. Final scores were converted to t-scores based on grade-specific Chinese national norms.

Mathematical performance was assessed at four time points: at the beginning of second grade (after one year of training), at the end of third grade (after three years of training), at the end of fourth grade (after four years of training), and at the end of fifth grade (after five years of training). Baseline testing was not conducted at the start of training because children lacked the necessary knowledge and skills to complete the assessment, making it an unsuitable time for assessment. In the control group, math ability was assessed once in 13 participants, three times in 7 participants, and four times in 26 participants. In the AMC group, math ability was assessed once in 4 participants, twice in 4 participants, three times in 27 participants, and four times in 17 participants (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Distribution of participants’ mathematics test.

Neuroimaging data acquisition

All data were acquired on a 1.5 T magnetic resonance scanner (Achieva, Philips) at the First Hospital of Qiqihar City, Heilongjiang Province, China, with an 8-channel head coil. Resting-state functional MRI data were collected during a 6-minute scan using a single-shot echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence with the following parameters: TR / TE = 2000 ms / 50 ms, flip angle = 90°, FOV = 230 mm × 230 mm, acquisition matrix = 64 × 64, slice thickness = 5 mm, interslice gap = 0.8 mm, 22 interleaved ascending slices covering the whole brain. Participants were instructed to remain still, keep their eyes open, and avoid falling asleep during the scan.

High-resolution T1-weighted structural images were acquired using a 3D fast field echo sequence with the following parameters: TR = 25 ms, TE = 4.6 ms, flip angle = 15°, field of view (FOV) = 256 mm × 256 mm, acquisition matrix = 256 × 256, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm³, 150 sagittal slices.

Data Records

The dataset is stored in the SciDB (10.57760/sciencedb.o00133.00034)24 and organized according to the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS), a standardized framework designed to promote data openness, reproducibility, and interoperability in neuroimaging research25. Access to the dataset requires compliance with the Chinese Color Nest Project (CCNP) standards and adherence to the application process. Specifically, users must download the CCNP-DUA (10.57760/sciencedb.o00133.00020), which outlines the Terms and Conditions, Security Standards, Best Practices, and Recipient Information.

All neuroimaging data were converted from the DICOM format to the standard Neuroimaging Informatics Technology Initiative (NIfTI) format (nii.gz) using dcm2niix. Tabular data are saved as tab-separated values (TSV) files. Metadata descriptor files are formatted in JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) to ensure compatibility with various analysis tools. IQ data, math ability and assessment are stored in the “behavior” folder.

Neuroimaging data are stored in the “MRI” folder, organized by subject into sub- < ID > directories. Each subject’s folder contains two subdirectories: “func” for functional MRI (fMRI) data, including < task_name > _bold.nii.gz and < task_name > _bold.json files, and “anat” for the defaced T1-weighted structural MRI images (.nii.gz). The dataset also includes quality control (QC) reports generated by MRIQC. The data structure is shown in Fig. 4. The participants.tsv file contains each participant’s ID, group assignment, age, and sex. Technical Validation

All collected data are included in the current version of the dataset. In the following sections, we provide a detailed description of the quality assurance procedures. While we recommend excluding certain participants, we acknowledge that exclusion criteria may vary depending on the specific objectives of each study. Therefore, we have opted to include all available data in the dataset release, allowing data users to make exclusion decisions that align with their specific research needs.

Behavioral quality control

The effectiveness of AMC training was assessed using the standardized, time-constrained grading system of the Chinese Abacus and Mental Arithmetic Association, as documented in the data folder. This grading scale ranges from Level 1 to Level 10, with Level 1 representing the highest proficiency. After one year of training, all participants in the AMC group successfully passed the entry-level qualification examination. This timed examination evaluates the ability to perform rapid and accurate consecutive addition and subtraction of three-digit numbers (e.g., 5 + 4 - 2), with a fixed number of problems to solve. By the end of five years of training (at the conclusion of Grade 5), most participants in the AMC group reached high proficiency levels (Level 3 or above) in the qualification examination. This study involves assessments requiring complex arithmetic operations, such as performing consecutive addition and subtraction of eight-digit numbers (e.g., 4235 - 971 + 3 - 60 + 2901 + 56 - 8 + 764). The consistent improvement in participants’ performance on abacus mental calculation tests demonstrates their enhanced computational skills, particularly in rapid and precise numerical processing.

Neuroimaging quality control

All anatomical and functional MRI scans were subjected to rigorous quality control using MRIQC (version 24.1.0), a widely utilized open-source tool designed for automated quality assessment of MRI data. MRIQC generates comprehensive quality reports, which were exported in HTML format for review29. Additionally, the image quality metrics (IQM), derived from the scans, were stored as JSON files in the QC directory located within the root BIDS folder.

Prior to MRIQC processing, all structural T1-weighted images underwent face removal using the pydeface tool (https://github.com/poldracklab/pydeface). A thorough visual inspection confirmed that all facial features were completely removed without cropping any part of the brain. Resting-state fMRI data were preprocessed using the DPABI30 (http://rfmri.org/dpabi) toolbox in MATLAB (version 2021a, MathWorks, Natick, USA). The first five volumes were discarded, leaving 175 time points for analysis. The remaining functional images underwent slice timing and head motion correction, aligned all functional images for each participant to their corresponding T1-weighted structural image. Subsequently, these images were spatially normalized to a Chinese pediatric template31, with a resampled voxel size of 4 × 4 × 4 mm3. To minimize confounding factors, multiple linear regression was applied to remove nuisance variables, including 24 Friston motion parameters, mean signals from white matter and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Spatial smoothing was conducted using a Gaussian kernel with a full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of 6 mm. A band-pass filter (0.01–0.1 Hz) was then applied to reduce physiological noise.

To control for head motion and image quality issues in resting-state fMRI data, participants with a mean frame displacement (meanFD_Jenkinson) exceeding 0.20 mm or poor image quality were excluded, as recommended by DPABI. As a result of the quality assessment, 17 participants were excluded (6 from the AMC group and 11 from the control group), leaving a total of 82 participants for subsequent analyses.

Subsequently, regional homogeneity (ReHo), amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF), and voxel-mirrored homotopic connectivity (VMHC) were computed for each participant using DPABI. Usage Notes

Users seeking to access this dataset24 must first submit a request through the CCNP-DUA (10.57760/sciencedb.o00133.00020), which outlines the Terms and Conditions, Security Standards, Best Practices, and Recipient Information. Once both community review and data request approval are completed, users gain direct access to the datasets for download.

This dataset has been partially utilized in our previously published studies, demonstrating its applicability and scientific value. We strongly encourage other research groups to explore and use this dataset24 in their own studies, provided that proper citation of this article is included. For those interested in additional data sharing or potential collaboration, we welcome you to contact us directly.
