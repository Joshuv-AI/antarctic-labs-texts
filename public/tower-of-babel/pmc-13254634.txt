Successful endoscopic hemostasis of gastric ulcer bleeding in a low-birth-weight neonate
Authors: Shohei Takami, Kohei Kurihara, Rei Yoshida
Published: 2026

Neonatal gastrointestinal bleeding is rare,1 yet it can occasionally be life-threatening. Endoscopic intervention in neonates is technically challenging because of limited device sizes. For effective hemoclip placement and adequate blood suction, an endoscope with a working channel diameter of at least 3.2 mm is desirable. However, conventional therapeutic endoscopes with such working channels have relatively large outer diameters, making their use difficult in neonates. Recently, thin endoscopes equipped with a 3.2-mm working channel have become available,2 enabling therapeutic endoscopic procedures even in small infants. We used this type of endoscope in a low-birth-weight neonate and achieved favorable clinical outcomes.

A male infant weighing 2,480 g was born at 38 weeks of gestation. At 12 hours of life, he developed hematemesis and was transferred to our hospital. Continuous drainage of fresh blood from the gastric tube was observed, indicating bleeding from the upper gastrointestinal tract. Despite continued transfusion of red blood cells (RBCs) at 20 mL/h (8 mL/kg/h) and fresh frozen plasma at 10 mL/h (4 mL/kg/h), the patient developed hemodynamic instability with progressive anemia (hemoglobin level decreased from 11.4 g/dL to 6.7 g/dL). Therefore, the patient required rapid RBC infusion, and endoscopic or surgical hemostasis was considered. At 22 hours of life, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was performed using a thin endoscope under general anesthesia (EG-840TP; Fujifilm; outer diameter, 7.9 mm; working channel, 3.2 mm) with surgical backup for possible laparotomy in case of difficulty with endoscope insertion or failure of endoscopic hemostasis. Endoscopic examination revealed multiple fundal gastric ulcers, with active bleeding from the largest ulcer, measuring 2.5 cm in diameter. Hemostasis was achieved using a hemoclip (HX-610; Olympus), followed by the topical application of thrombin to the ulcer base (Fig. 1). After successful hemostasis, the anemia improved with transfusion. Enteral feeding was resumed on post-procedure day 4, and the patient was discharged on day 15 without recurrence. No procedure-related adverse events, including perforation, rebleeding, or anesthesia-related complications, occurred during or after the procedure.

In this case, because the bleeding was refractory to conservative management and the patient developed hemodynamic instability, endoscopic intervention was prioritized as a less-invasive alternative to surgery. Although endoscopic epinephrine injection and thermocoagulation have been reported as treatment options for neonatal gastrointestinal bleeding,1,3 their application remains limited. Given the thin gastric wall in neonates, mechanical hemostasis using a hemoclip may be more reliable and safer than these procedures. For hemoclip-based hemostasis, endoscopes with a working channel of ≥3.2 mm are desirable to ensure sufficient suction and device operability. However, conventional therapeutic endoscopes with such channels (e.g., Olympus GIF-290T or Fujifilm EG-840T; outer diameter of approximately 10 mm) are generally oversized for neonatal use. Recent developments in thin therapeutic endoscopes have helped overcome these limitations.
