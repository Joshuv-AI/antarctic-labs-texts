Knowledge, attitude, and practice of healthcare workers towards healthcare- associated infections in Jimma University Medical Center, southwestern Ethiopia: using structural equation model
Authors: Etagegn Shacho, Argaw Ambelu, Daniel Yilma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare-associated infections (HCAIs) are a common challenge faced in healthcare facilities, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Evaluating the level of knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) among healthcare personnel regarding HCAI prevention and identifying the relevant factors is important for handling and controlling these infections. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the direct and indirect effects of knowledge, attitude, and practices of healthcare workers (HCW) towards HCAIs prevention in Jimma University Medical Center (JUMC).MethodAn institutional cross-sectional study was conducted from March to April 2022. A total of 262 was sampled from 1354 health professionals working in JUMC using the population proportion stratified random sampling method. The data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to identify the direct and indirect effects of KAP of HCWs on HCAIs.ResultsOf the 262 participants, 55% (n = 144) were nurses and 52.7% were female. The study found that HCW occupational skills (os) had a direct effect on knowledge (k) and attitudes (a) in preventing HCAIs (βos→k = 1.43, Pos→k = 0.004, and βos→a = 0.65, Pos→a = 0.004). HCWs’ practice on HCAIs had an effect (βos→p = -0.79; Pos→p = 0.004). HCWs’ attitudes toward HCAI preventive practice (p) and knowledge had an effect, p-values Pa→p = .002 and Pa→k = .003, respectively. Indirect effects revealed that HCWs’ attitudes towards preventing HCAIs through practice had an impact (γa→k = .426, Pa→k = .003). HCWs’ occupational skills and attitudes towards preventing HCAIs had an effect (γos→p = .523, Pos→p = .002).ConclusionThe finding indicated that attitude and occupational skills can be improved through practice which finally brings a significant improvement in the knowledge of HCWs about HCAIs prevention. Besides, there were direct effects of occupational skills on the practices of HCAIs prevention. This highlights ongoing training and mentoring of HCWs during practice is essential to enhance HCAIs prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-12094-6.

Introduction

Healthcare-associated infections (HCAIs) are infections acquired by patients in hospitals that did not exist when they were admitted [13]. These infections create burdens by impeding the provision of effective healthcare services. They can lead to longer hospital stays, resistance to treatment, worsening of current health issues, increased financial costs for patients, strain on healthcare resources, and even death [65]. Thus, it is important to always follow proper prevention and control measures, particularly for healthcare workers (HCWs), to reduce the risk of HAIs [64]. HCWs knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) influence the prevention of HCAIs [3]. Successful prevention and control procedures should be implemented each time, specifically by HCWs, to reduce the risk of HCAIs [64]. The growing need to train clinicians with adequate KAP to improve patient safety has encouraged research to develop training interventions for medical students [6, 31]. Occupational skills have been recognized as both crucial to patient safety and for highly trained health professionals [7].

The KAP theory model underlines the importance of knowledge and attitude in social decision-making, explains the group of health behavior, and predicts the change of behavior by exploring the KAP of high-risk groups [43]. According to Kelman’s theory of KAP, knowledge is the basis for changing practices, and attitude plays a crucial role in motivating change [64]. The KAP theory condition that health knowledge is the basis for establishing a positive attitude and healthy performance. Attitude is defined as individuals’ feelings concerning a subject and any preconceived ideas they hold about a topic [41]. In this research, attitude refers to the feelings HCWs have towards healthcare-associated infections, as assessed using a questionnaire. Attitudes toward safety issues have a vital role in what is commonly referred to as a hospital’s safety culture [12]. Earlier studies in Ethiopia evaluated the level of KAP towards infection prevention among healthcare workers [27, 52, 59]. Based on the KAP theory, there is a causal link between knowledge, attitudes, and practices. However, these factors are difficult to measure directly. Conventional statistical methods can only identify the factors influencing knowledge, attitudes, and practices without fully explaining the intricate causal relationships at play [67]. Although the classical multivariate statistical method can be the one that identifies the factors that affect KAP, but this could not help elucidate the complex causal relationships involved in the process [1, 58]. A structural equation model (SEM) which is a multivariate statistical skill that includes confirmatory factor analysis, path analysis, and multiple regression analysis is a better way to look at causal relationship. SEM can deal with latent variables and observe indicators and measurement errors [11, 68]. SEM is important for validating variables that cannot directly be observed but that can only be inferred from observable variables and for developing complex patterns of relationships between a multitude of variables and groups [63]. SEM is flexible and important to determine the direct and indirect effects of variables [42], but its applicability in health-related fields is limited [11].

A Belgian study indicated that nurses’ knowledge of prevention of surgical site infection was only 29%, with males performing better [44]. A study in Wuhan revealed that healthcare workers exhibited a notable lack of knowledge regarding infection control practices, despite 60% holding diplomas or higher qualifications [49]. In Uganda, while 90% of healthcare workers demonstrated good knowledge of HAI prevention, only 36% applied this knowledge effectively in practice [37]. Research in Mubende hospital indicated that although 80% of healthcare workers recognized the importance of personal protective equipment (PPE), only 40% consistently washed their hands before aseptic procedures [9]. In Zimbabwe, a concerning 59% of healthcare workers were unaware of what constituted HAIs, highlighting a critical need for education [16].

Previous studies in Ethiopia assessed factors of KAP of healthcare workers towards infection prevention and related factors and found that the magnitude of KAP towards infection prevention among healthcare workers [8, 18, 24]. Available studies in Ethiopia have revealed that a significant number of healthcare workers lack sufficient knowledge about infection prevention [24]. For instance, studies conducted in the Northwest of Ethiopia (54.2%) [27], Addis Ababa, Ethiopia (33.9%) [52], and West Arsi Zone, Ethiopia (36.3%) [46], and in Dessie referral hospital, Northeast Ethiopia (23%) [25] confirmed the existence of a large number of HCWs with insufficient knowledge on infection prevention. In Jimma University Medical Center, high burden and poor discharge outcomes of HAIs have been reported. While many HCWs demonstrate good knowledge and positive attitudes towards infection prevention, practical adherence remains inconsistent, highlighting areas for improvement [5]. Our study was designed to employ structural equation modeling to examine the relationships that existed between the KAP of HCW on prevention of HCAIs. Therefore, the study aimed to identify the associations found between the knowledge, attitudes, and practice of health professionals to HCAIs in JUMC. The specific objectives of the study were to (i identify the relationship that existed between HCW’s KAP with HCAIs; and (ii this study identified the indirect link that existed between the HCW knowledge, attitudes and practices to HCAIs in JUMC.

Materials and methods

Study area and design

A cross-sectional study was carried out among HCWs at JUMC in March and April 2022. All HCWs who were working in JUMC were considered, particularly those in high-risk areas such as the surgical units, intensive care unit (ICU), and neonatology unit. A high-risk area is a working environment in the hospital where inpatients are more susceptible to developing severe illnesses and medical disorders. All HCWs working in JUMC during data collection were included in the study. However, those HCWs who were on annual, maternity, or educational leave and were off work from their duty due to health problems were excluded.

Data collection tool and procedure

The questionnaire was constructed following the structure of Hussein et al. [36], Bayleyegn et al. [10], and Thankam and Goswami [60] centered on demographic, educational, and institutional characteristics of study participants, which could help to determine the level of KAP towards HCAIs preventions. To measure the knowledge of HCW on HCAIs prevention, participants were provided with four open-ended questions. This was developed by considering on mode of transmission and prevention, and type and site of infection. The practice and attitude questionnaires were developed following the related literature [24, 61], and the content was adapted from related studies [27, 59]. Trained nurses managed the data collection procedure. The questionnaire has four sub-sections. The first part of the survey covers the demographic features of healthcare workers (age, sex, ethnicity, profession, year of service, prior training, workload, and availability of HH stations, and colored waste bins). The second part constitutes four questions concerning the knowledge of HCWs on HCAIs prevention. The third part consists of nine questions linked to HCAIs prevention practices. The fourth part contains six questions concerning the attitude of HCWs toward HCAIs prevention. The questionnaires were tested for completeness and reliability. The data collection tool was tested for internal consistency (reliability) using Cronbach’s alpha test.

For each knowledge questions, participants were allowed to give correct answers. The answers to each question were compiled from previous published articles [19, 22, 45, 62]. For each correct response on the knowledge dimension, the study applied based their responses and use the Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5 in which (1 = very poor for those with no correct response, 2 = poor for HCW with one correct response, 3 = fair for having two correct responses, 4 = good for having three correct responses, and 5 = excellent for correct responses of four and above). Higher scores represented better knowledge. The six questions concerning the attitude dimension of HCW towards HCAIs were evaluated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5 (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = not sure, 4 = agree, and 5 = strongly agree). Higher scores indicated a more positive attitude towards to HCAIs [47, 66].

For HCAIs practices, there were nine questions on the practice dimension of HCW which were rated using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5 (1 = never, 2 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 4 = often, and 5 = always) [40].

Underlying conceptual model

HCWs’ KAP has influenced the prevention level of HCAIs [3]. According to Kelman’s theory of KAP, knowledge refers to the basis for changing practices and attitude refers to the energetic force of change. As a result, understanding the KAP of the HCWs concerning HCAIs is central to establishing these measures. Therefore, classifying the factors that have important effects on KAP is significant and can provide information basic for implementing infection prevention and intervention measures by infection prevention and control (IPC) managers. Structural equation modeling

SEM has its root in path analysis and is represented using a drawing of a path diagram [23]. It helps identify latent variables that are believed to exist but cannot be directly observed [17]. SEM was used due to as it has the basic assumption of multivariate normality that assumes all univariate distributions are normal; SEM also assumes no multicollinearity because it constitutes a large number of endogenous and exogenous variables, which increases the possibility of inter-correlations among some of the variables [42]. The present research work applied structural equations and latent variable models to investigate the effects of KAP on the prevention of HCAIs.

Item response theory and confirmatory factor analysis

Item response theory was applied to measure the psychometric properties of clinical measures [51]. It helps to assess the strength of the relationship between the items and the constructs of interest and the availability of information across the latent construct [34].

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) can help examine factor analysis, construct validity, and easy for reliability analysis in several ways. CFA is another kind of SEM related to measurement models [14]. The implementation of CFA is valuable to support the linking items and their particular domains. This permits the fixing of the relationships in the analysis model and presents methods to assess the fit of the planned theoretical model to the collected data [55]. Therefore, we conducted CFA to verify the factorial structure of the KAP towards HCAIs among HCWs. Data analysis

The study used SPSS Amos software version 23 for analysis. The software was employed to identify the relationship strength between the items and the constructs of interest and to confirm the factorial structure of the KAP questionnaires identified in the exploratory factor analysis, respectively.

Numerous indices indicated a good model fit for the construct, and then we included the ratio of chi-square to the degree of freedom (χ2/df) < 5.0, Tucker Lewis Index (TLI > 0.9), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA ≤ 0.08), comparative fit index (CFI > 0.9), normed fit index (NFI > 0.9), the goodness of fit index (GFI > 0.9), incremental fit index (IFI > 0.9), and p > 0.05 for the chi-square test [15, 35]. J. Hair et al. [29], suggested that model fitness should be decided by using at least a minimum of three different indices. A standardized factor loading indicates the presence of a good relationship between items and respective factors as it is greater than 0.5 and has a p-value of less than 0.05 which further shows the validity of the construct. The composite reliability of the domains was computed with a value of 0.7 and above was considered acceptable [32, 50].

Characteristics of respondents

Among the total participants, 52.7% were female. A greater part of the study respondents, 37.4% were in the age group of 26–29 years old, 20.6%, were in the age group of 34–37 and 2.3% were in the age group of greater than 41 years old. Regarding educational status, most of the respondents, 68.3% were master’s degree holders, 24.8% were bachelor’s degrees, 4.6% were specialists and the remaining 2.3% were diploma holders. Regarding professional work experience, 48.1% had less than five years of experience, 45.4% had 6–10 years of experience, and 6.5% had over 10 years of experience. The study results reveal that 75.2% of the participants had workloads that were less than or equal to eight hours per day. In addition, the training access for health professionals was not uniform, with only about 38.9% of nurses having had access to training on infection prevention (Table 3). The level of HCW knowledge about HCAIs varied depending on their years of service. Among HCWs, those with less than 11 years of service (43–55%) have better knowledge of each scale item compared to the rest years of experience. Practices of healthcare workers towards HCAIs

Responses concerning the practice of HCWs were reported in Suplimentary Table 1. The practice of health professionals towards to HCAIs had shown variation.

89% of HCW professionals in each guiding question on HCAIs prevention scored above average(always and often), which were from 51.8% to 85.2%. For instance, HCWs had differences in the frequency of hand hygiene practice after completing the procedure. From total 18.3% of the physican, 8.3% of the nurses, 25% of the lab technicians, 7.4% of the pharmacist and 7.7% of the other health professionals practice it always. For the rest practice guidelines of HCW, we would like to refer Supplementary Table 1.

According to supplementary Table 2, healthcare workers with different years of working experience who always practice hand hygiene after completing procedures were fewer than those who practice it often. In terms of washing hands before and after patient contact, most HCWs practice it often, while those with greater than 10 years of experience practice it sometimes and below. On injection practices, most HCWs, i.e., 67.2%, have less than 11 years of experience. Regarding the use of safety boxes for needle collection after injection, 74.4% of healthcare workers frequently use them after finishing their tasks. Concerning the avoidance of using a swab before an intramuscular injection, 47.6% and 53% of healthcare professionals with 0–5 and 6–10 years of experience, respectively, reported often practicing this after completing the procedure. In contrast, 58.8% of those with over 10 years of experience reported sometimes and below doing this. Changing gloves before handling new patients: 7.3% of individuals in the all-service year category always use new gloves. In contrast, 49.2% of each service year group often change their gloves before meeting a new patient. In the regular use of gowns, most HCWs always, often, or sometimes wear gowns during their working hours. However, a small percentage of HCWs from all service year categories (9.5%) did not wear gowns at all. Concerning the use of gloves for all procedures, the majority of HCWs use them often, with percentages ranging from 51.6%, 58% and 35.3% from those with the least to the most years of service experience.

Discussion

Knowledge is the origin of varying practices, and attitude is the powerful force of change [39]. Thus understanding the KAP of HCWs concerning HCAIs is critical in establishing HCAI prevention measures [65]. This study assessed the effect of KAP on HCAIs among HCWs using latent variables.

In our study, knowledge concerning the modes of transmission of HCAI, methods of prevention of HCAI, common sites of HCAIs, and invasive procedures risk factor aspects of HCAIs were generally above average and following current scientific evidence. In relative terms, the knowledge level of the nurses was average and below on modes of transmission of HCAIs, which constitutes 62.5%, and on common sites of HCAIs that share 64.6% of them. Although the majority of HCWs in the study hospital were aware of the methods of prevention of HCAIs and invasive procedures, there were some areas where their knowledge was low, particularly regarding modes of transmission and sites of HCAIs in the hospital. As a result, the HCW needs to learn and train more to reduce the rate of HCAIs. According to Parmeggiani et al. [48], continuing education is vital to continuing medical advantage in the hospital environment. This study’s findings partly agreed with Sarani et al. [53], who reported that most nurses (43%) had poor knowledge about HCAIs in Iran. The result supports the conclusion of Ghadmgahi et al. [26] on the KAP of nurses concerning HCAIs control in which most nurses have below-average knowledge of HCAIs. The findings of this study indicated that there could be varied according to the education level of HCW. The reason was that respondents with higher education status were more likely to know the modes of transmission and the common site of HCAIs in a hospital environment. This might be due to the direct link between study duration at university and thus those enrolled in university could gain more information on HCAIs [54].

Overall KAP regarding HCAIs were not high across various job categories and levels of experience, similar to studies conducted in Cape Town, South Africa [20], India [57], and Burkina Faso [33]. Although nurses HCW had some awareness of the problem of HAIs, many of them had fewer practices to prevent the transmission of HCAIs. The study findings suggest the existence of deficiencies in training on HCAI both in undergraduate training curricula. In addition, this highlights the absence of in-service training on HCAI for nursing and other medical staff. In addition, one potential reason for the low level of HCAI at JUMC might be the misconception that the hospital environment does not play a major role in infection transmission. The study finding showed that occupational skills had a direct effect on KAP. This implied that HCWs with a great level of occupational skill towards HCAIs prevention have had better knowledge and attitude. Compared with previous studies elsewhere [2, 3, 10, 18, 26], this study finding indicates the direct effects of occupational skills on KAP of HCWs. Our study showed that occupational skills had a direct effect on KAP. This implied that HCWs with a great level of occupational skill towards HCAIs prevention have had better knowledge and attitude. Meanwhile, the level of occupational skill of HCWs was less likely to practice HCAIs prevention. Consistently, the knowledge and practice level of healthcare workers towards HCAIs were significantly associated with their professional status and infection prevention training [8, 24]. Similarly attitude and practice level of healthcare workers towards HCAIs in JUMC were significantly associated with their educational status, which was consistent with study findings in Gonder and Debre-Markos specialized and referral hospitals [10, 18]. This confirmed that KAP had a direct relationship with the education status, professional status, and infection prevention training of HCWs. In the practice domain, education level has a major influence on HCWs’ ability to implement the procedures for preventing and controlling HCAIs. Another positive factor is the in-service training on infection prevention for HCWs. For instance, due to a shortage of proper adherence to techniques on personal protective equipment (PPE) usage, many medical professionals were infected during the COVID-19 pandemic [56]. Thus, adequate and correct use of PPE is the best measure to prevent HCWs from acquiring HCAI diseases [65].

This research applied a structural equation model to assess the KAP impacts on HCAIs in JUMC. Worldwide as well as in Ethiopia, there were sufficient research works addressing the direct impacts of KAP on HCAIs. However, this research tried to investigate the indirect association among knowledge, attitude, and practices on HCAIs using a structural equation model and confirmatory factor analysis. Thus, the factor effect is more powerful than the individual effect for KAP. The model explained that there was a positive direct effect of occupational skills on knowledge and attitude toward HCAIs prevention. Similarly, the attitude and knowledge of HCWs on the practices of HCAIs prevention have a positive direct significant effect. However, occupational skills on the other hand had a negative but direct significant effect on the practices of HCWs towards HCAIs prevention. Similarly, the attitudes of HCWs regarding their knowledge of preventing HCAIs have a negative direct significant association. This implied that increasing the degree of the attitude of HCWs towards HCAIs prevention determines their knowledge level on HCAIs and infection prevention practice. Moreover, improvement of occupational skills has positive and indirect effects on HCWs’ attitudes and practices towards to HCAIs prevention.

The findings help to conclude that increasing the positive attitude and high practices toward HCAIs prevention display an improvement in the practices of infection prevention and to increase the knowledge level of HCAIs. Simultaneously, a decreasing attitude toward HCAIs prevention needs intensive training to upgrade the HCAI’s knowledge.

Although substantial efforts have been prepared to minimize the practicable limitations of this study, health professionals might not give real and open responses to the self-addressed item.

Conclusion

Overall, the HCW of JUMC had a high level of KAP, and the KAP is associated with HCW’s educational level during undergraduate and graduate programs and the level of participation in service training related to HCAIs prevention and control procedures. Both attitude and occupational skills can be improved through practice which finally brings a significant improvement in the knowledge of HCWs about HCAIs prevention. On the other hand, there were direct effects of occupational skills on the practices of HCAIs prevention. This highlights ongoing training and mentoring of HCWs during practice is essential to enhance HCAIs prevention.
