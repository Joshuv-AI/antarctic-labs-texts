Wait-free Replicated Data Types and Fair Reconciliation
Petr Kuznetsov, Maxence Perion, Sara Tucci-Piergiovanni

Abstract
Replication is a standard way to maintain availability of shared data in fault-prone distributed systems. To make sure that the data replicas are up-to-date , they need to synchronize , which typically means engaging the replicas in waiting for coherent responses from each other. The amount of waiting depends on the consistency and availability guarantees we impose on the system. The folklore CAP theory states that strong consistency (intuitively, the set of replicas create an illusion of one correct server) and strong availability (intuitively, the replicas’ states are reachable despite network partitions) cannot be implemented in the same system. A popular way to deal with this impossibility is to relax consistency to be only eventual : the replicas eventually converge to the same state. In return, the replicas can be wait-free , i.e., the clients can get the data from the closest replica without waiting for other ones. Wait-free data replication faces two important challenges. First, the operations issued by the clients may be constantly revoked , i.e., their effects can be repeatedly recomputed due to asynchrony and concurrency. Second, even if some operations eventually stabilize in their effects, a particular client may still experience starvation if, from some point onward, each of its operations is later revoked. In this paper, we address these challenges through a general DAG-based framework for replicated data types, where replicas exchange their local views and merge them using a reconciliation function . Within this framework, we design reconciliation functions that implement a wait-free eventually consistent replicated state machine ensuring both stable convergence and fair progress. Specifically, every replica maintains a growing sequence of client operations, and we guarantee that: (1) all replicas share a common, monotonically growing stable prefix of operations, and (2) no client starves.

Introduction

Sharing data in a consistent and available way is one of the principal challenges in distributed computing.
The only way to ensure availability of data despite failures is to
replicate
it, i.e., to maintain the data in multiple copies and to employ synchronization protocols to make sure that the evolving
replicas
are
up-to-date
.
Synchronization is costly, as it involves reliable dissemination of data copies across the network.
Moreover, preserving availability in the presence of partitions inevitably affect the consistency level.
In particular, if the system is prone to network partitions, one cannot ensure availability and

strong
consistency, making the replicated system indistinguishable from a single correct server
[
5
,
14
]
.
Indeed, partitions are known to be a major source of consistency loss in practical systems
[
1
]
.

Availability is often seen indispensable in practice
[
34
]
: the system is expected to respond quickly, otherwise its clients feel abandoned which negatively affect their perception of the service.
In the common case when partitions are unavoidable, it is natural to resort to weaker consistency criteria, such as
eventual consistency

[
34
,
27
]
.
Intuitively, eventual consistency says that the states of the replicas can diverge arbitrarily for a period of time, as long as this period is finite.
In return for this relaxation of consistency, the shared data can be accessed in the
wait-free
manner
[
16
]
, the local copy of the data may serve and there is no need to query other replicas and wait for their responses.
This approach found numerous applications, under the name of conflict-free replicated data types (CRDT)
[
28
,
4
,
31
,
18
,
26
]
.

The intuitive definition of eventual consistency
[
34
]
requires the replicas to eventually converge to the same state, assuming that there there is a sufficiently long period of
quiescence
, when no new operations on the shared data arrive.
But in practice we would like to ensure some consistency even if new operations never stop arriving.
We address this challenge by maintaining the state of a replica as a DAG (directed acyclic graph) grasping the causal relations of the operations the replica is ”aware of”.
A
reconciliation function
is now used to ”make sense” of this DAG, i.e., to arrange its operations in a totally ordered sequence (a
serialization
), respecting the order of causally related operations and resolving the conflicts for concurrent ones.
Since the DAGs evolve differently at different processes, it is inevitable that some operations in the serialization are later reordered and their
effects
(the responses and the state transitions) are
revoked
.
A natural requirement for a reconciliation function would then be that serializations generated at different replicas should share a monotonically growing
stable prefix
.
The effects of operations that appear in the stable prefix will then eventually get irrevocable.

We face, however, an interesting challenge of
fairness
.
Operations issued by a given client may never land in the stable prefix.
Moreover, since every operation is performed on the
local
state of a replica, and the local state may later evolve, the operation may later be reordered and its effect will change.
This can matter in cases when the desired effect of an operation is only produced in certain states.

Consider, for example an NFS (Network File System) service which offers the functionality of creating and deleting directories.
An operation
\textit{mkdir}(^{\prime}/d2^{\prime},^{\prime}d4^{\prime})
creating a subdirectory of
d2
succeeds only if
d2
is present.
The operation fails if it is ordered after
\textit{rmdir}(^{\prime}/d2^{\prime})
removing
d2
(cf. the example in Figure
1
).
Due to concurrent operations, this can repeat over and over with every operation issued by the client, so the client effectively
starves
.

In this paper, we address the challenges of stable progress and fairness by presenting a wait-free replicated state-machine protocol that guarantees that replicas share a
fair and stable
monotonically growing prefix of operations.

The reconciliation function for fairness iteratively selects certain “leader” vertices in the DAG and appends their causal past to the current history.
To ensure fairness, the leaders are chosen in the round-robin,
à la
breadth first search, fashion based on the commands they issued so far.
Interestingly, this approach shares some similarities with DAG-Rider
[
20
]
and subsequent DAG-based blockchain protocols, albeit in a different, eventually synchronous setting.
To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first to study the notion of fairness (extensively explored in blockchains
[
35
,
9
,
22
,
25
]
) in the context of eventual consistent systems.

To sum up, this paper:

•

introduces the notion of eventually stable and eventually stable and fair replicated state machines;

•

presents a DAG based framework allowing to wait-free compute any replicated data type;

•

proposes a simple asynchronous solution to build an ever-growing stable prefix;

•

proposes an asynchronous solution to build an ever-growing stable prefix that is also fair.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
The system model we consider and the specification of our properties are described respectively in Section
2
and
3
.
We present our DAG-based framework along the reconciliation functions we propose to have an ever-growing stable prefix and an ever-growing fair stable prefix in Section
4
.
We discuss related work in Section
5
.
Section
6
concludes the paper and overviews future work.

Replicas, communication channels.

We consider a set

Let

Since we assume that channels are reliable, we only consider

Stable progress.

The specification above provides a very weak form of progress, comparable with progress requirements of reliable broadcast
[

]

Every command issued by a correct process eventually gets into the local history of every correct process, but we provide no guarantees on the order in which the commands are placed.
Given a history

Let

be the local history of process

As we only consider

Algorithms

We now describe a framework which enables (F-)ESSMR algorithms replicating any given data type
(Q,q_{0},{O},R,\sigma)

This framework is based on a directed acyclic graphs (DAG) collaboratively constructed by the replicas.
Intuitively, the DAG keeps track of issued commands and their causality relations.
A

is then used to totally order the vertices of the DAG and produce the local history.

Related Work

In the shared-memory context, Herlihy
[
16
]
introduced the notion of wait-freedom and proposed a wait-free
universal construction
that constructs a lineraizable
[
17
,
3
]
implementation of any data type.
Lamport
[
24
]
described a partially synchronous fault-tolerant state-machine replication protocol, Castro and Liskov
[
8
]
extended it to the Byzantine setting.

Optimistic replication

[
27
,
34
]
is a popular approach in building scalable distributed systems.
The idea is to sacrifice consistency for the sake of availability in the presence of partitions
[
5
,
14
]
: the data replicas evolve independently of each other and converge to the same state only
eventually
.
This allows the clients of the system to manipulate the data independently of each other, extending the popular progress condition of
wait-freedom

[
16
]
to distributed systems.

Eventual consistency

[
27
]
, the resulting correctness criterion, has found multiple practical instantiations, to name a few: Bayou
[
32
]
, Dynamo
[
11
]
, Cassandra
[
23
]
, numerous implementations of Conflict-free Replicated Data Types (CRDTs)
[
28
]
.

According to the conventional definition of eventual consistency
[
34
]
, ”if no new updates are made to the object, eventually all accesses will return the last updated value”
4
4

4

Verner Vogels, Eventually Consistent - Revisited,
https://www.allthingsdistributed.com/2008/12/eventually_consistent.html
.
Besides being constrained to storage-like systems, this definition also does not account for runs in which updates are constantly arriving.
As a result, it cannot be used to grasp the intuitively desirable property of
fairness
.

Saito and Shapiro
[
27
]
suggested a more general definition of eventual consistency which requires the existence of an ever-growing
stable prefix
(called ”committed prefix”) of issued operations.
Additionally, assuming fault-free systems, it accounts for a form of fairness that requires that ”every issued operation … will eventually be included in the committed prefix”.
However, this, still rather informal, definition does not grasp the aspect of ”local progress”: locally observed effects of operations issued by a process may always be ”revoked” due to concurrency.
Our correctness criterion is defined for generic sequential objects, takes care of all executions, not only ”eventually quiescent” ones, and introduces a stronger notion of fairness which we believe to make sense in practice.

It is important to emphasize that our approach is in spirit close to the original intuition of eventual consistency
[
34
,
27
]
that can be implemented in partition-prone asynchronous systems.
Stronger definitions that require the replicas to eventually evolve in the same way
[
12
,
29
]
can be shown to require some amount of synchrony.

DAG-based blockchains (
[
13
,
20
,
10
,
30
]
, to name a few) have recently gained momentum due to their stable throughput and elegant separation of data dissemination and ordering.
We employ a similar mechanism: the issued commands are maintained in a DAG using reliable broadcast and ordered using a reconciliation function.
Moreover, similar to committed vertices in DAG-based blockchains, once a vertex gets into a stable prefix, all its causal past is also getting stable, which gives us ”stable throughput”: it might take a while for a vertex to get stable because of temporary partitions, but once it does, the whole bunch of its predecessors do.

In
[
2
]
, an eventual stable prefix property for blockchains is stipulated.
It is ensured through a specific “fork choice rule”, which copes with a potentially unbounded number of Byzantine clients.
However,
[
2
]
does not address fairness. Moreover, our framework proposes reconciliation functions that generalize the notion of fork choice rule to arbitrary data types, not just blockchains.

A recent line of work aims to provide
fair ordering consensus
for blockchains implementing SMR, motivated by the risk of manipulation in transaction ordering (e.g. front-running or censorship) which can lead to unfair economic advantages
[
15
]
. The general idea is to observe incoming transactions and reach agreement on a fair ordering, where an ordering is considered fair between any pair of transactions if it preserves their relative order of reception as observed by a majority of (correct) processes
[
22
]
. A central challenge for these protocols is that, even when all processes are correct, they may not be able to agree on a fair total order due to Condorcet cycles in the reception order. As a result, weaker forms of fairness have been proposed, where transactions potentially involved in a Condorcet cycle are included in the same
batch
, avoiding strict ordering among them
[
7
,
21
]
. A more relaxed notion of fairness (sometimes referred to as transaction liveness) only guarantees that a transaction is eventually included in the blockchain. This is typically achieved by aggregating transactions from multiple proposers, either by including several proposals in a single block (e.g., Red Belly
[
9
]
) or by rotating leaders (e.g., HotStuff
[
35
]
, DAG-Rider
[
20
]
). Additionally, in DAG-Rider, the leader does not choose transactions to include in blocks, which further reduces the potential for manipulation.
However, these forms of fairness do not guarantee fairness to clients, as a given client’s transactions might never be executed due to conflicts or system asynchrony
[
25
]
.

6
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we described a protocol for DAG-based eventually consistent replicated state machine.
Every replica maintains a growing sequence of state-machine operations ensuring that: (1) the replicas share a common, monotonically growing stable prefix of operations, and (2) no client starves.
A few interesting questions are left for future work.

A natural extension of this work is to design reconciliation functions that can account for concurrent operations and enforce ordering only when
conflicts
occur.
Indeed, if concurrent operations
commute
, there is no reason to execute them in total order, which may result in more efficient algorithms.
Instead of growing stable prefix, we may then build a growing stable
equivalence class
of operation sequences.

Another desirable feature would be to ensure that the growing stable prefixes are eventually
committed
, so that the client would be informed that the operations in the prefix will no longer be reordered.
However, one cannot do guarantee this in
every
execution without assuming some amount of synchrony
[
12
,
29
]
.
Moreover, if we assume an
eventual failure detector

[
12
]
or partial synchrony
[
29
]
,
we should also ensure that a majority of processes are correct
[
5
,
14
]
.
A popular approach is to resort to
optimistic commitment
, i.e., finding the weakest set of assumptions on the
good
executions where commitment can be achieved.
Combining this with
fairness
, so that every operation issued by a correct process eventually commits, is an interesting challenge.

Finally, an important direction for future work is to extend the approach to tolerate Byzantine failures.
