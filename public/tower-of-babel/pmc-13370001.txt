Molecular characterization of Echinococcus granulosus sensu lato genotypes circulating in small ruminants in north India
Authors: Pallavi Moudgil, Anshu Lohan, Anil K Nehra, Anil Sharma, Aman D Moudgil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe etiological agents of cystic echinococcosis comprise a species complex, Echinococcus granulosus sensu lato (s. l.). Despite high endemicity, the disease remains neglected in most parts of the globe, including India. The present study aimed at investigating the genotypes of Echinococcus granulosus s. l. circulating in sheep and goats in two states (Haryana and Himachal Pradesh) and a union territory (Chandigarh) of north India. Out of 1049 slaughtered small ruminants (n = 314, 394, and 341 from Chandigarh, Himachal Pradesh, and Haryana, respectively), 85 (n = 53, 17, and 15 from Chandigarh, Himachal Pradesh, and Haryana, respectively) exhibited the presence of hydatid cysts. Based on the mitochondrial cox I and ATP6 genes, phylogeny, the population structure and haplotype network analyses were performed. Genotypes G1 and G3, and G6 corresponding to E. granulosus sensu stricto and E. canadensis, respectively, were recorded. Population structure analysis revealed a low nucleotide and high haplotype diversities for the present study isolates, emphasizing a recent population expansion. Cystic echinococcosis is a cyclo-zoonotic parasitic disease caused by Echinococcus granulosus sensu lato (s. l.) species complex1,2. Mitochondrial signatures have revealed the presence of different genotypes or species under E. granulosus s. l. species complex1,3, including E. granulosus sensu stricto (s. s.; genotypes G1 and G3), E. equinus (genotype G4), E. ortleppi (genotype G5), E. canadensis (genotypes G6-8, G10), and E. felidis1,4,5,6. Among all these species, E. granulosus s. s. is ubiquitously distributed and causes most cystic echinococcosis cases in humans worldwide7. Except E. felidis, all other species of this complex are capable of infecting humans3.

Despite being a major veterinary and medical concern throughout the globe, cystic echinococcosis is still among the neglected tropical diseases1,3. The life cycle of E. granulosus s. l. primarily involves dogs and wild canids as definitive hosts and sheep, goats, buffaloes, cattle, equines, yaks, camels, pigs, wild boars, cervids, and moose as intermediate hosts4 except E. felidis3. Cystic echinococcosis is highly endemic in India, with 12% (n = 119320) human cystic echinococcosis cases out of 973,662 global cases recorded annually8.

Various species/genotypes of E. granulosus s. l. differ in host range, pathogenicity, human transmission, and developmental periods in the definitive hosts9. The molecular studies for genetic profiling of cystic echinococcosis in small ruminants in India are very scarce. Only a few studies exhibited the circulation of E. granulosus s. s. (G1 and G3 genotypes)10–12, and E. canadensis (G7 genotype)13 in small ruminants in India. All these previous studies targeted the small ruminant population of an individual state/union territory, including Maharashtra (West India)10, Punjab (North India)11, Jammu & Kashmir (North India)12, and Himachal Pradesh (North India)13. Moreover, a comprehensive study targeting multiple sampling locations (different states/union territories) of different geographies, multiple small ruminant species (sheep and goats due to their different feeding behaviors), multiple mitochondrial genetic markers, and screening of a large number of animals is required to adjudge the endemicity of other genotypes/species. The present study was envisaged to address these research gaps.

Cystic echinococcosis renders significant economic losses due to condemnation of vital visceral organs at post-mortem. Moreover, the disease also causes drop in milk production, poor quality meat, deterioration of wool quality and diminishes its production in small ruminants14. In addition, the disease is associated with delayed growth and overall performance decline in livestock15. Approximately, US$212.35 million annual economic losses were assessed due to cystic echinococcosis in India16.

Population genetic studies reveal pathogen dispersal patterns and assess the evolutionary potential of parasite populations17. It is evident that various cryptic species of E. granulosus s. l. complex possess different morphological, biological, and epidemiological attributes. Therefore, identifying the species/genotypes that are prevalent in endemic areas is crucial for the implementation of the most effective control strategies. Since the Indian subcontinent is considered to be a cradle of E. granulosus s. s., it is essential to evaluate the specific genotypes/species circulating in different domesticated animals (intermediate hosts). Considering the fact, the present comprehensive study aimed at genotyping of E. granulosus s. l. isolates from small ruminants (sheep and goats) in north India (Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, and Chandigarh). Furthermore, the generated sequences were correlated with GenBank-catalogued sequences from the Indian subcontinent based on the mitochondrial cytochrome C oxidase subunit I (cox I) and ATP synthase F0 subunit 6 (ATP6) genes. Additionally, population structure and haplotype network analysis were also performed to assess dispersal patterns of E. granulosus s. l. in small ruminants in the Indian subcontinent.

Materials and methods

Study area and sample collection

The study area involved two geographically distinct states, Haryana (29.0588° N, 76.0856° E), and Himachal Pradesh (32.1024° N, 77.5619° E) and a union territory, Chandigarh (30.7333° N, 76.7794° E) in north India (Fig. 1). The elevation of Haryana and Himachal Pradesh states ranges between 200 and 1200, and 350–7000 m, respectively, above mean sea level. However, the elevation of Chandigarh union territory ranges between 304 and 365 m above mean sea level. The average annual rainfall is 536.5 mm, 1251 mm, and 1110.7 mm, respectively, at Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, and Chandigarh. According to the 20th Livestock Census of India (2019), the total small ruminant (sheep and goats) population in Himachal Pradesh, Haryana, and Chandigarh was approximately 8.74 million. The sample size to assess prevalence for the present study was calculated according to Thrusfield18 with 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and 30% expected prevalence, which came out to be 323. In this cross-sectional study, the vital organs, including the liver, lungs, and heart of slaughtered small ruminants (n = 1049) from Haryana (n = 341), Himachal Pradesh (n = 394), and Chandigarh (n = 314), were screened thoroughly for the presence of hydatid cysts. The hydatid cysts recovered from the organs were incised, cleaned thoroughly with normal saline, and were transported at 4 ℃ to the laboratory for further genotypic studies.

Genomic DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and sequencing

Before further processing, the hydatid cysts were washed thrice with normal saline. The hydatid cysts were screened for their viability (to assess the presence of live protoscolices for further transmission of infection to the definitive hosts) using the Eosin exclusion method. The genomic DNA was extracted from the germinal layers of the viable hydatid cysts (n = 33) using the Qiagen DNeasy Tissue kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The genomic DNA was eluted at 140 µL elution buffer and stored at −20 ℃ till further analysis.

For the genotypic profiling, the amplification of the genomic DNA was performed by targeting the partial mitochondrial cytochrome C oxidase subunit I (cox I)19 and ATP synthase F0 subunit 6 (ATP6) genes20. The details of primers, PCR reaction mixtures, and amplification conditions for both markers are given in Table 1. The amplified products (amplicons) were electrophoresed through 1.25% agarose gel and were visualized using a GelDoc Go Gel Imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). The genomic DNA of Toxocara canis and Taenia hydatigena served as negative controls to adjudge primer specificity for the cox I and ATP6 genes, respectively. However, nuclease-free water served as a non-template control. Approximately 450 and 700 bp amplified products of the cox I and ATP6 genes, respectively, were subjected to customized Sanger sequencing (Barcode BioSciences, Bangalore).

To preclude sequencing error, each isolate was subjected twice to the bidirectional Sanger sequencing using the amplification primers21. Raw sequences were aligned, and non-aligned regions and ends were trimmed to lessen missing data. The analysis was thus performed using BioEdit software, which was also utilized to generate consensus sequences using forward and reverse reads22.

Phylogenetic analysis

The phylogenetic analysis of 23 cox I and 26 ATP6 sequences generated in the present study, along with GenBank-archived cox I (n = 40; Supplementary Table 1) and ATP6 (n = 4; Supplementary Table 2) E. granulosus s. l. sequences from the small ruminants of the Indian subcontinent (India and Pakistan), was performed using the Molecular Evolutionary Genetic Analysis (MEGA) version 11.0.1023. The Tamura-Nei model was found to be the best substitution model to infer evolutionary history for both mitochondrial signatures. Bootstrap analyses were conducted using 1000 replicates with a distance scale estimated at 0.02. Taenia hydatigena sequences (MW305245 for cox I and AB031286 for ATP6 genes, respectively) from the GenBank database were used in the phylogenetic analysis as out-group species to root the tree.

Median joining haplotype network

Based on geographic origin, the relationship among different haplotypes of E. granulosus s. l. for both the genetic signatures (cox I and ATP6) was evaluated using PopART (https://popart.otago.ac.nz)24. GenBank-archived Echinococcus granulosus s. l. sequences with 100% coverage to LC843759 (for cox I) and LC843740 (for ATP6) sequences (generated in the present study) were retrieved and analyzed. To elucidate the interrelationship among different haplotypes corresponding to two countries (India and Pakistan) of the Indian subcontinent, a total of 63 (cox Igene) and 30 (ATP6gene) sequences (including the sequences generated herein), were included in the present study (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). However, the sequences (n = 30) corresponding to the ATP6 gene were only available from India.

Population genetic analyses and genetic differentiation indices

The population genetic differences, neutrality test (Tajima’s D) to test the hypothesis of selective neutrality, and population diversity indices were analyzed for the overall dataset and each country using DnaSPv625. The values of haplotype and nucleotide diversities are expressed with standard deviation. Pairwise genetic difference (FST) and gene flow (Nm) between populations were estimated through Wright’s F statistics using Arlequin 3.5.2 for the genetic differentiation and population structures analysis26. The values of genetic differentiation were considered high, moderate, low, and negligible based on FST values > 0.25, between 0.15 and 0.25, between 0.15 and 0.05, and < 0.05, respectively. Furthermore, the level of gene flow was considered high, intermediate, and low if the values of Nm were recorded as > 1, between 0.25 and 0.99, and < 0.25, respectively. Population-genetic structure within and between countries was investigated by performing an analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) with the Tajima-Nei substitution model at 1000 permutations27.

Results

Prevalence of cystic echinococcosis

Out of 1049 slaughtered small ruminants (n = 314, 394, and 341 from Chandigarh, Himachal Pradesh, and Haryana, respectively) screened, 85 (n = 53, 17, and 15 from Chandigarh, Himachal Pradesh, and Haryana, respectively) exhibited the presence of hydatid cysts. Among 85 hydatid cysts collected from the positive animals, only 33 were found viable through the Eosin exclusion method.

Molecular confirmation

PCR amplification yielded ~ 450 bp and ~ 700 bp fragments for the cox I and ATP6 genes, respectively, from the genomic DNA retrieved from the germinal membranes of the viable hydatid cysts (n = 33) collected during the study. The specificity of the primers was validated by the lack of amplification from Toxocara canis and Taenia hydatigena genomic DNA for the cox I and ATP6 genes, respectively.

Phylogenetic analyses and genotype identification

The NCBI-BLASTn analyses of the consensus sequences generated in this study confirmed the prevalence of E. granulosus sensu lato. The consensus sequences (n = 23 and n = 26 for the cox I and the ATP6 genes, respectively) were submitted to GenBank under the accession numbers LC843759-63, LC842159-61, LC822725-27, LC822526-29, LC820398, and LC819658-64 for the cox I and LC843740-46, LC843434-41, and LC824031-41 for the ATP6 gene. These sequences showed 99.05–100% and 99.10–100% similarity to GenBank-archived E. granulosus s. l. sequences for the cox I and ATP6 genes, respectively. Phylogenetic analysis of the cox I and ATP6 genes (Figs. 2 and 3, respectively) revealed the presence of a large monophyletic clade of E. granulosus s. s. (genotypes G1 and G3) with many sub-clades, exhibiting the presence of micro-variants. However, the sequences corresponding to E. canadensis (genotype G6) were arranged in different clades, supported with high bootstrap values (greater than 80%). Hence, the genotypes recorded in the present study were G1, G3, and G6.

Median joining haplotype network analyses

Analysis of the population structure of E. granulosus s. l. from the Indian subcontinent identified 8 cox I and 5 ATP6 haplotypes (Tables 2 and 3). For the cox I gene, analysis of 63 sequences identified a dominant haplotype (Hap_2) comprising 40 sequences, followed by Hap_1 with 15 sequences. The dominant haplotype Hap_2 constituted the sequences from India (n = 31) and Pakistan (n = 32). Other haplotypes comprised singleton sequences primarily. The remaining haplotypes radiated from this dominant haplotype (Fig. 4). The E. granulosus s. l. sequences generated herein for the cox I gene represented five different haplotypes. Similarly, analysis of 30 mitochondrial ATP6 gene sequences yielded a predominant haplotype (Hap_2), encompassing 19 sequences (Fig. 5). The sequences generated in the present study represented four haplotypes out of five.

Population genetic diversity analyses

Population genetic diversity indices and neutrality test values for the cox I and ATP6 genes of E. granulosus s. l. are summarized in Tables 4 and 5, respectively. For the mitochondrial cox I gene, parameters were inferred for two countries (India and Pakistan) of the Indian subcontinent (Table 4). Whereas population genetic diversity parameters for the ATP6 gene were derived for India only due to the absence of any sequence in the GenBank database from other countries of the Indian subcontinent (Table 5). The overall cox I dataset and the sequences from India and Pakistan showed very low nucleotide diversities (0.013 ± 0.004, 0.019 ± 0.006, and 0.005 ± 0.002, respectively) and high haplotype diversities (0.562 ± 0.061, 0.658 ± 0.066, and 0.546 ± 0.093, respectively). The sequences generated in this study also depicted low nucleotide (0.023 ± 0.008) and high haplotype (0.747 ± 0.062) diversities. Similarly, the overall ATP6 gene dataset and present study sequences displayed low nucleotide (0.022 ± 0.012, and 0.025 ± 0.013, respectively) and high haplotype (0.575 ± 0.093, and 0.588 ± 0.093, respectively) diversities (Table 5). Significant negative values for Tajima’s D were recorded for the overall dataset (−1.918, p < 0.05) and Pakistani (−2.163, p < 0.05) E. granulosus s. l. sequences for the cox I gene. Moreover, for the ATP6 gene, non-significant negative Tajima’s D values (−1.695 and − 1.527, respectively) were recorded for the overall dataset as well as for the sequences generated herein.

Discussion

Various studies have revealed that the Indian subcontinent (especially India and Pakistan) is endemic for E. granulosus sensu stricto. However, a few studies and case reports also highlighted the presence of other species of E. granulosus s. l. complex. This study aimed to evaluate genetic diversity, phylogeny, and haplotype variations in E. granulosus s. l. genotypes circulating in sheep and goats in north India. The mitochondrial signatures have played a significant role in the identification of different genotypes corresponding to different species of E. granulosus s. l. species complex. These markers are effective in adjudging intraspecific variations and population structure differentiations due to the presence of conserved structures, matrilineal inheritance, absence of recombination, and high mutation and evolutionary rates1,4. Earlier, most of the studies on genetic profiling of E. granulosus s. l. in the endemic regions employed mitochondrial cox I and nad1 genes28. However, inconsistencies in genotypic identification (based on the diagnostic positions in these markers) have resulted in the need for a better genetic signatures combination to differentiate between different genotypes/species under the species complex. Hence, two mitochondrial signatures (cox I and ATP6) were employed in the present study to assess genetic diversity and population structure of E. granulosus s. l. genotypes circulating in sheep and goats in north India.

The occurrence of hydatidosis in slaughtered small ruminants was found lower (8.1%) in the present study as compared to other studies (11.47–16.67%) performed in the last decade on small ruminant cystic echinococcosis in India10,11,29, except for the study carried out by Beigh et al., who reported an occurrence of 4.04%12. However, recently a very high 62.9% and 20.9% prevalence of cystic echinococcosis was recorded in sheep and goats in Lebanon30. Furthermore, 11.34% and 2.79% of sheep and goats, respectively, were found infected with cystic echinococcosis in Iran31. On the contrary, a comparatively lower prevalence of cystic echinococcosis was recorded in sheep (5.7% and 7%) and goats (6.3% and 5.5%) in recent studies in Pakistan32,33. Moreover, in another study conducted in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan, a higher prevalence of cystic echinococcosis was recorded in the slaughtered sheep (10%), but lower in goats (5.1%)34. The reason for the variation could be attributed to the geographical differences, as a wider geographical region with significant variations in altitudes, rainfall, and humidity was covered in the present study. Out of these 85 positive animals, only 33 exhibiting the presence of viable cysts (examined through Eosin exclusion method) were subjected to molecular investigations. The molecular investigation and phylogenetics highlighted the presence of three genotypes (G1, G3, and G6) corresponding to E. granulosus s. s. and E. canadensis species. Both these species are implicated in most human cystic echinococcosis cases (88.4 and 7.3%, respectively) worldwide7. Furthermore, the previous studies have exhibited the circulation of E. granulosus s. s. (G1 and G3 genotypes), and E. canadensis (G7 genotype) in small ruminants in India10–13. Hence, the present study reported the circulation of the G6 genotype in small ruminants, in addition to the previously recorded genotypes of the species complex. However, a comprehensive study carried out in north India highlighted the presence of E. granulosus s. s. as the primary etiology of 93.75% of human cystic echinococcosis cases, with a single case associated with the G6 genotype35. Echinococcus granulosus s. s. exhibits cosmopolitan distribution, but E. canadensis (G6 genotype) is primarily associated to areas with camel and goat populations36. The present study area encompasses and borders the camel and goat rearing region of India and hence perpetuates transmission dynamics of this zoonotic genotype due to the presence of definitive and intermediate hosts.

The phylogenetic analysis revealed sequences generated herein clustered with E. granulosus s. s. sequences retrieved from the small ruminants of India and Pakistan (Figs. 2 and 3). However, E. canadensis sequences generated in the present study formed a separate clade due to the absence of any reports of E. canadensis genotype G6 in small ruminants (sheep and goats) from the Indian subcontinent. However, a few previous studies highlighted the presence of G6 genotypes in small ruminants in Iran, Argentina, Mongolia, China, and Sudan37,38. The G6 genotype is reported to possess apparently high affinity for human brain tissues39. In some countries (including Egypt, Mauritania, and Sudan), the G6 genotype of E. granulosus s. l. is the main etiology of human cystic echinococcosis cases39. The clades were clearly differentiated into E. granulosus s. s. and E. canadensis at high bootstrap values. The finding was further supported by the median joining haplotype network, where the haplotypes consisting of E. canadensis sequences were dissociated from haplotypes comprising of E. granulosus s. s. sequences with multiple mutational steps (Figs. 4 and 5).

For genetic studies, haplotype and nucleotide diversities play a crucial role in quantifying genetic variation and provide insights into populations’ evolutionary patterns, such as bottlenecks, expansions, and differentiation40. The high values for haplotype diversities and low values of nucleotide diversities were observed for the complete data set as well as Indian and Pakistani E. granulosus s. l. populations for both the genetic signatures (Tables 4 and 5), which evinced a recent population expansion followed by bottlenecks or the founder effects or genetic drifts26.

The Tajima’s D test relies on the comparison of the segregating nucleotide sites. A negative value is indicative of an influence towards excess of the number of rare alleles, further ascertaining a recent population expansion. However, a positive value indicates an influence towards intermediate frequency alleles35. Hence in the present study, the significant negative Tajima’s D values recorded for the overall dataset as well as Pakistani E. granulosus s. l. populations corresponding to the cox I gene were indicative of either population expansion or purifying selection. This would further result in an excess of rare polymorphism to neutral expectations in sequences21. Moreover, non-significant negative value of Tajima’s D recorded for Indian E. granulosus s. l. populations for both the genetic signatures either indicated a constant population or a slight increase in population26. Low genetic differentiation and high gene flow were also recorded between Indian and Pakistani E. granulosus s. l. populations (Table 6). The observation further evinced evolution as well as unrestricted recurrent gene exchange between populations21,26. The findings were also in corroboration with the observations of AMOVA, where low genetic variations were observed between populations, indicating high gene flow between them. The findings could be associated with rapid geographical expansion of E. granulosus s. l. genotypes/populations with their hosts through trade. Hence, anthropogenic mobility of the infected intermediate hosts might be related to weak genetic differentiation and high gene flow between geographically segregated populations of the Indian subcontinent.

In the Indian subcontinent, the favourable conditions exist for the transmission dynamics of E. granulosus s. l. metacestodes in the intermediate and dead-end hosts, and adults in the definitive hosts13. Moreover, all precipitating factors, including unregulated abattoirs/slaughter slabs and easy access of stray and domesticated dogs to offal, contamination of food and water sources of intermediate and accidental hosts with domesticated and wild canid faeces, societal stratification, and paucity of public awareness, contribute to the completion of E. granulosus s. l. life cycle13. Furthermore, identification of genotypes/species of E. granulosus s. l. circulating in an area is extremely important considering the variable pathogenicity, life cycle patterns, host adaptability, infectivity and other biological characteristics of different genotypes28. Human pathogenic differences were evident for the G1 and G3 (E. granulosus s. s.), and G6 (E. canadensis) genotypes of E. granulosus s. l., but detailed genomic and epidemiological studies on this aspect are warranted40. The G6 genotype has earlier been reported from the human cystic echinococcosis cases in Nepal41, Africa42, Argentina43, and India35. Moreover, the rising cases of cystic echinococcosis due to the G6 genotype in non-endemic areas/regions in India prompt surveillance in imported/exported animals from endemic areas, especially camels and goats. Regarding the other two genotypes, G1 and G3, due to their vast global distribution and highest (88.4%) human7and livestock infections throughout the globe, intervention and control programs should be implemented worldwide targeting the potential definitive and intermediate hosts. Moreover, it is worth noticing that the disease condition is highly endemic in economically constrained/low-income regions with limited diagnostic resources and, hence, diminished evidence-based interventions44. On the contrary, resource-privileged settings also encounter the cases due to abated awareness, eventually prompting misdiagnosis44. Hence, an interdisciplinary approach should be adopted for early appropriate diagnosis, prevention and control, and awareness of disease transmission. The advanced techniques like single tube nested PCR (targeting the cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 gene), having 100 times more sensitivity as compared to conventional PCR to delineate Echinococcus sp., should be employed for an accurate diagnosis as well as epidemiological investigations45. Moreover, cell-free DNA (cfDNA)-based diagnosis of E. granulosus s. l. in sheep sera in the recent past has also furnished the basis for early and more accurate diagnosis of cystic echinococcosis46.

Conclusions

The present study aimed at genotyping the E. granulosus s. l. isolates infecting the small ruminants in north India and highlighted the presence of G1, G3, and G6 genotypes. Furthermore, the findings could be further correlated with a more detailed study involving human cases to understand the pathogenicity and organ tropism of different genotypes (G1, G3, and G6) recorded. Additionally, the study is the first report of G6 genotypes infecting and circulating in small ruminants in India. Low nucleotide and high haplotype diversities for the present study isolates exhibited a recent E. granulosus s. l. population expansion. Further, detailed studies on this aspect are warranted.
