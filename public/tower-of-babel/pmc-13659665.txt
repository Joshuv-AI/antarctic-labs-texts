High intestinal obstruction secondary to peritoneal metastases from invasive lobular breast carcinoma: A case report of an unusual presentation
Authors: Valentina Testini, Umberto Tupputi, Luca Roberto, Giacomo Fascia, Domenico Mannatrizio, Francesco Saverio Guerra, Roberto Bellitti, Vincenzo Dadduzio, Gennaro Gadaleta Caldarola, Giuseppe Guglielmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastrointestinal metastases from breast cancer are uncommon and occur more frequently in invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC), which exhibits a distinctive metastatic pattern involving the peritoneum, mesentery, and gastrointestinal tract. Introduction

Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide. Distant metastases most commonly involve the bones, lungs, liver, and central nervous system. However, invasive lobular carcinoma exhibits a unique metastatic pattern compared with invasive ductal carcinoma, with a greater propensity to involve the gastrointestinal tract, peritoneum, retroperitoneum, and gynecologic organs [1,2].

Peritoneal dissemination may remain clinically silent for prolonged periods or manifest with nonspecific symptoms, occasionally leading to bowel obstruction. We describe an unusual case of proximal intestinal obstruction caused by peritoneal metastases from ILC occurring ten years after treatment of the primary breast malignancy.

Case presentation

A 64-year-old woman presented to the Emergency Department with progressively worsening abdominal pain, persistent nausea, and recurrent episodes of intractable vomiting over the preceding 48 hours, associated with reduced oral intake. Her medical history was significant for bilateral invasive lobular breast carcinoma treated nine years earlier with bilateral mastectomy and axillary lymph node dissection. She had no documented history of metastatic disease and was not receiving oncological treatment at the time of presentation.

On physical examination, the abdomen was soft and non-distended but tender on palpation of the upper quadrants, without signs of peritonitis. Laboratory investigations were unremarkable.

Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT demonstrated multiple ill-defined, enhancing soft-tissue lesions involving the peritoneal reflections and transverse mesocolon. A poorly marginated pseudonodular soft-tissue area was also identified at the mesenteric root, in close anatomical relationship with the third and fourth portions of the duodenum. The lesions produced extrinsic compression of the distal duodenum, accounting for the clinical picture of proximal intestinal obstruction. No definite focal mass or mural thickening involving the duodenal or colonic wall was identified (Fig. 1).

Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT demonstrating infiltrative peritoneal and mesenteric metastatic disease. Axial (A) and coronal (B) contrast-enhanced CT images demonstrate a poorly marginated pseudonodular soft-tissue lesion at the mesenteric root (arrows), in close anatomical relationship with the third and fourth portions of the duodenum, producing extrinsic compression. Axial (C) and coronal (D) images show additional irregular enhancing nodular lesions involving the peritoneal reflections and transverse mesocolon (arrows). No definite focal intramural mass involving the duodenal or colonic wall is identified.

Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy demonstrated a stenosis of the second portion of the duodenum consistent with extrinsic compression, preventing further advancement of the endoscope. Consequently, the fourth portion of the duodenum could not be directly evaluated endoscopically. The endoscopic findings therefore confirmed the presence of a high-grade duodenal obstruction, although direct endoscopic assessment of the distal duodenum was not possible because of the stenosis.

Contrast-enhanced MRI further characterized the abnormal tissue at the mesenteric root and along the transverse mesocolon. The lesions appeared as irregular, poorly defined areas of soft-tissue thickening with heterogeneous post-contrast enhancement. On diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), the lesions demonstrated increased signal intensity, with corresponding low signal intensity on apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps, indicating restricted diffusion. These findings supported the suspicion of metastatic involvement and were concordant with the abnormalities identified on CT (Fig. 2, Fig. 3).

Diffusion-weighted and apparent diffusion coefficient MRI findings of mesenteric and peritoneal metastatic disease. Diffusion-weighted images (A, C) demonstrate increased signal intensity within the poorly delineated lesion at the mesenteric root (A) and within the nodular lesions involving the peritoneal reflections and transverse mesocolon (C). The corresponding ADC maps (B, D) show low signal intensity in the same areas (arrows), consistent with restricted diffusion. The diffusion abnormality corresponds to the infiltrative soft-tissue lesions identified on morphological and contrast-enhanced sequences.

Contrast-enhanced MRI demonstrating infiltrative mesenteric and peritoneal lesions. Post-contrast T1-weighted images demonstrate marked heterogeneous enhancement of the poorly delineated pseudonodular lesion at the mesenteric root (upper image, arrow) and of the irregular nodular lesions involving the transverse mesocolon (lower image, arrows). The lesions show an infiltrative configuration without a well-defined mass and correspond to the abnormalities identified on CT and diffusion-weighted imaging.

18F-FDG PET/CT demonstrated increased FDG uptake corresponding to the abnormal soft-tissue lesions at the mesenteric root and within the transverse mesocolon. The metabolic abnormalities showed an anatomical distribution concordant with the CT and MRI findings and further supported the suspicion of active secondary peritoneal and mesenteric disease (Fig. 4).

18F-FDG PET/CT demonstrating metabolically active mesenteric and peritoneal lesions. Axial PET/CT image (A) demonstrates increased FDG uptake corresponding to the abnormal soft-tissue lesion at the mesenteric root (arrow). Image (B) shows increased FDG uptake corresponding to the nodular soft-tissue involvement of the transverse mesocolon (arrow). The metabolic distribution is concordant with the anatomical abnormalities identified on CT and MRI.

The patient underwent exploratory laparoscopy with multiple biopsies obtained from the transverse mesocolon and mesenteric/peritoneal lesions. Histopathological examination revealed a malignant epithelial neoplasm of mammary lobular origin. Immunohistochemical analysis showed positivity for GATA3 and mammaglobin, supporting the mammary origin of the lesions. The tumor was estrogen receptor (ER) positive in approximately 40% of cells, progesterone receptor (PgR) negative, and showed a Ki-67 proliferation index of 10%. HER2 immunohistochemistry was scored 1+, corresponding to a HER2-low phenotype. These findings were consistent with metastatic invasive lobular carcinoma of breast origin involving the transverse mesocolon.

Discussion

Invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) is characterized by a distinctive pattern of metastatic dissemination that differs from that of invasive carcinoma of no special type. In particular, ILC has a greater propensity to involve the peritoneum, retroperitoneum, and gastrointestinal (GI) tract. This behavior is related, at least in part, to the characteristic loss of E-cadherin-mediated cell adhesion, which favors a diffuse and infiltrative pattern of tumor growth rather than the formation of a discrete mass. Consequently, metastatic ILC may remain clinically and radiologically occult for a prolonged period and may become apparent only when secondary complications develop [3,4].

Although clinically uncommon, autopsy studies suggest that gastrointestinal involvement may be substantially underrecognized, with reported prevalence rates ranging from 4% to 18% among patients with metastatic breast cancer. The stomach and small intestine are the most frequently affected sites, whereas duodenal involvement is relatively uncommon [5,6].

The present case illustrates this characteristic infiltrative behavior. Nine years after bilateral mastectomy and axillary lymph node dissection for ILC, the patient developed symptoms of high intestinal obstruction caused by extrinsic compression of the distal duodenum. CT demonstrated poorly defined enhancing soft-tissue abnormalities involving the mesenteric root, peritoneal reflections, and transverse mesocolon, without a discrete intramural gastrointestinal mass. The distribution and morphology of these abnormalities were suggestive of secondary peritoneal and mesenteric disease.

The absence of a discrete bowel-wall lesion is particularly relevant. Metastatic ILC involving the gastrointestinal tract frequently demonstrates a submucosal or infiltrative growth pattern. As a result, the bowel wall may appear smoothly or diffusely thickened, while the mucosa may remain relatively preserved. In some patients, the metastatic process is predominantly extramural and produces a stricture or obstruction through serosal and muscular infiltration. In our patient, endoscopy demonstrated a severe, non-passable stenosis of the third portion of the duodenum, preventing direct evaluation of the fourth portion [[7], [8], [9]].

CT therefore provided a broader anatomical assessment of the extrinsic involvement of the third and fourth portions [10,11].

MRI provided additional tissue characterization. The lesions at the mesenteric root and transverse mesocolon showed irregular morphology, heterogeneous enhancement, and restricted diffusion, with increased signal intensity on DWI and corresponding low signal intensity on ADC maps. The concordance between CT and MRI supported the interpretation of these abnormalities as infiltrative soft-tissue lesions rather than nonspecific inflammatory or fibrotic changes. MRI may be particularly useful when CT findings are subtle or when additional soft-tissue characterization of infiltrative disease is required.

18F-FDG PET/CT demonstrated increased FDG uptake corresponding to the lesions at the mesenteric root and transverse mesocolon. In this case, the metabolic findings were concordant with the anatomical abnormalities detected on CT and MRI and provided additional support for active metastatic disease. However, FDG PET/CT should be interpreted cautiously in ILC because some ILC metastases may show lower FDG avidity than invasive carcinoma of no special type. Therefore, a negative or only mildly avid PET/CT examination should not by itself exclude metastatic ILC when morphological findings or clinical history are suspicious.

An additional characteristic feature of this case is the long interval between treatment of the primary breast malignancy and development of metastatic disease. Delayed recurrence is well recognized in ILC, and gastrointestinal metastases may occur many years after the initial diagnosis. This emphasizes that a remote history of ILC remains clinically relevant when evaluating unexplained abdominal symptoms, even after a prolonged disease-free interval.

The differential diagnosis of infiltrative peritoneal and mesenteric abnormalities in this setting is broad and includes primary gastrointestinal malignancy with peritoneal dissemination, lymphoma, inflammatory or fibrotic mesenteric disorders, and other metastatic malignancies. The absence of a clear gastrointestinal primary mass, together with the infiltrative distribution of the lesions and the patient's history of ILC, should increase suspicion for metastatic breast carcinoma. Nevertheless, imaging findings alone are often insufficient to establish the diagnosis [2,9].

Histopathological confirmation is therefore essential, particularly when the radiological appearance is atypical or when a primary gastrointestinal malignancy cannot be confidently excluded. In our patient, laparoscopic biopsy of the transverse mesocolon demonstrated metastatic malignant epithelial neoplasia of mammary lobular origin. Immunohistochemical staining was positive for GATA3 and mammaglobin, supporting mammary origin. The tumor was ER-positive in approximately 40% of cells, PgR-negative, with a Ki-67 proliferation index of 10% and HER2 immunohistochemistry scored as 1+ (HER2-low). These findings established the diagnosis of metastatic ILC [12,13].

The present case highlights the complementary role of the different diagnostic modalities. CT was fundamental for identifying the anatomical distribution of the infiltrative disease and demonstrating the relationship between the mesenteric lesion and the distal duodenum. Endoscopy confirmed the obstructive component but was limited by the non-passable stenosis. MRI provided additional morphological and diffusion information, while FDG PET/CT demonstrated metabolic activity within the corresponding lesions. Ultimately, laparoscopy and biopsy were required for definitive diagnosis.

From a clinical perspective, the case emphasizes that bowel obstruction may represent a secondary manifestation of metastatic ILC even in the absence of a discrete gastrointestinal mass. In patients with a previous history of ILC and unexplained obstructive gastrointestinal symptoms, radiologists should carefully evaluate the peritoneum, mesentery, and bowel serosa for subtle infiltrative abnormalities. Recognition of these patterns can facilitate earlier tissue sampling and appropriate immunohistochemical characterization [14,15].

Overall, this case illustrates an uncommon but clinically important presentation of metastatic ILC, in which diffuse peritoneal and mesenteric infiltration produced high-grade duodenal obstruction through extrinsic compression. The combination of a remote history of ILC, infiltrative peritoneal and mesenteric abnormalities without a discrete bowel-wall mass, concordant MRI diffusion abnormalities, and histopathological confirmation should alert clinicians and radiologists to the possibility of late metastatic ILC.

Conclusions

Metastatic invasive lobular breast carcinoma may present years after treatment as diffuse peritoneal and mesenteric disease causing high-grade duodenal obstruction through extrinsic compression, even in the absence of a discrete gastrointestinal mass. In patients with a previous history of ILC, subtle infiltrative abnormalities involving the peritoneum, mesentery, and bowel should therefore be carefully assessed on cross-sectional imaging. CT, MRI, endoscopy, and 18F-FDG PET/CT provide complementary information, but definitive diagnosis requires histopathological and immunohistochemical confirmation. Awareness of this distinctive metastatic pattern is essential to avoid misdiagnosis as a primary gastrointestinal or peritoneal malignancy and to facilitate appropriate oncological management.
