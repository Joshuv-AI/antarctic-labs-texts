Effects of different bleaching methods on the color stability, microhardness, and surface roughness of single-shade composites
Authors: Betül Kübra Kurucu Karadeniz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis in vitro study aimed to evaluate the effects of different bleaching methods on the color stability, microhardness, and surface roughness of single-shade resin composites.MethodsFour single-shade resin composites (Charisma Diamond One, Vittra APS Unique, Omnichroma, Zenchroma) and one universal-shade resin composite (Essentia Universal) were tested. Two hundred disc-shaped specimens were fabricated and stained in a coffee solution for 12 days After staining, specimens were assigned to four groups: at-home bleaching (10% carbamide peroxide), in-office bleaching (40% hydrogen peroxide), laser-assisted bleaching (Er: YAG), and a non-bleached control. Color (ΔE₀₀), surface roughness (Ra), and Vickers microhardness (VHN) were measured at baseline, after staining, immediately post-bleaching, and at 1-week and 1-month follow-ups.ResultsAll bleaching protocols significantly affected surface properties and color stability (p < 0.05). Laser-assisted bleaching induced the greatest surface roughness increase and microhardness reduction (p < 0.05), while at-home bleaching had the least effect. Essentia Universal showed the highest resistance, whereas Charisma Diamond One and Omnichroma were more prone to discoloration and mechanical changes. Microhardness partially recovered over time (p < 0.05), but color and roughness changes persisted.ConclusionsBoth bleaching protocol and composite type influence the performance of single-shade resin composites. These results may guide clinicians in material selection and treatment planning. Essentia Universal showed superior resistance to bleaching-related changes, while laser-assisted bleaching, although effective for whitening, compromised mechanical and esthetic properties.

Introduction

Resin-based composites are frequently selected in restorative dentistry because they offer esthetics, cost-effectiveness, conservative application, and satisfactory mechanical performance [1, 2].Technological progress and rising patient expectations have led to the development of new-generation composites that combine polishability, biocompatibility, and clinical convenience [3–5]. To simplify shade selection and reduce clinical time, universal-shade resin composites have recently been introduced. These materials aim to mimic surrounding dentition with a single shade through optical blending mechanisms [5].

Modifications in the composition of resin composites during manufacturing can influence their surface integrity and response to external factors. Discoloration—one of the most common signs of material aging—may result from intrinsic factors such as resin matrix composition, filler size, and resin–filler interaction, or from extrinsic factors including smoking, oral hygiene, and dietary habits [6–11]. Among common dietary substances, coffee is particularly known for causing pronounced staining of resin-based materials [12–16].

Dental bleaching is widely used to remove extrinsic stains in a minimally invasive manner. At-home bleaching is preferred by patients for its convenience, whereas in-office bleaching provides faster, more controlled outcomes due to higher peroxide concentrations and professional supervision [17]. Laser-assisted bleaching, particularly with Er: YAG systems, has recently gained attention as an adjunctive method, offering enhanced efficiency and reduced pulpal irritation. To further improve bleaching efficacy, light activation systems—including LED and laser-based technologies—have been developed [18–22]. Among these, Er: YAG lasers (2940 nm) are of special interest because of their selective absorption by water-rich gels, enabling effective whitening with minimal risk of pulpal irritation [20, 21].

In addition to color change, bleaching procedures may also induce surface alterations such as increased roughness and decreased hardness, which compromise esthetics and promote plaque retention [23]. While bleaching effects on conventional multi-shade composites have been extensively studied, evidence regarding single-shade materials is scarce. Because these composites rely on optical blending and structural translucency for shade adaptation, their response to bleaching agents may differ from traditional multi-shade composites. Moreover, comparative evaluations of at-home, in-office, and laser-assisted bleaching on single-shade composites are limited, and the influence of Er: YAG laser bleaching on their surface and mechanical properties remains unclear.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first in vitro study to simultaneously evaluate the effects of at-home, in-office, and Er: YAG laser-assisted bleaching on the color stability, surface roughness, and microhardness of single-shade resin composites. By assessing these parameters together, the study provides novel insights into how bleaching protocols influence the clinical performance of contemporary single-shade restorative materials.

Accordingly, the following null hypotheses were tested:H₀₁: The bleaching method would have no significant effect on the color stability, surface roughness, or microhardness of single-shade resin composites.H₀₂: The type of resin composite would not significantly influence the color stability, surface roughness, or microhardness, regardless of the bleaching protocol.

Specimens were assigned to four groups: at-home bleaching (10% carbamide peroxide), in-office bleaching (40% hydrogen peroxide), laser-assisted bleaching (Er: YAG), and a non-bleached control. Color (ΔE₀₀), surface roughness (Ra), and microhardness (VHN) were recorded at baseline (T0), after staining (T1), immediately post-bleaching (T2), 1 week (T3), and 1 month (T4).

The required sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1 (effect size f = 0.40, α = 0.05, power = 0.80), which indicated a minimum of 8 specimens per subgroup. To increase reliability, 10 specimens were prepared for each subgroup, resulting in a total of 200 specimens (5 composite resins × 4 bleaching protocols × 10 specimens).

A total of 200 disc-shaped specimens (n = 40 per resin composite type) were prepared using stainless steel molds (6 mm in diameter, 2 mm in thickness). The composite materials were placed into the molds, and to ensure flat and uniform surfaces on both sides, polyester strips and glass plates were gently pressed onto the top and bottom of each mold.

Polymerization was performed using a Woodpecker light-emitting diode (LED-E) light-curing unit (Woodpecker Medical Instrument Co., Guilin, China) at 1000 mW/cm² for 20 s, in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. The light-curing unit was positioned perpendicular to the specimen surface and kept in direct contact with the glass slide during polymerization.

To ensure uniform surface finishing, all specimens were polished by a single operator using silicon carbide abrasive papers of 400, 600, and 1200 grit under constant water irrigation. Polishing was performed for 10 s per grit at a rotational speed of 150 rpm using a benchtop grinding and polishing unit (Ecomet 6, Buehler, Lake Bluff, USA). Following the procedure, samples were rinsed with distilled water, lightly dried, and their thickness was verified with a digital micrometer (MDC-250, Mitutoyo, Kawasaki, Japan) to confirm dimensional standardization. Thereafter, all specimens were stored in a dark, closed box at room temperature to prevent exposure to ambient light until further processing. After preparation, all specimens were immersed in a coffee solution prepared with Nescafé Classic (Nestlé, Vevey, Switzerland; 1.8 g/150 mL distilled water) at 37 (±) 1 °C for 12 days to simulate extrinsic staining. Each sample was individually placed into a well of a 24-well culture plate to ensure complete submersion. The plates were kept in an incubator at 37 (±) 1 °C throughout the staining period. To maintain consistency and prevent microbial growth, the coffee solution was renewed daily, and the specimens were repositioned to ensure complete exposure. This daily renewal protocol is consistent with procedures described in previous studies [16, 24].

After the staining phase, all samples were rinsed thoroughly with distilled water and gently blotted dry using soft tissue prior to subsequent procedures [25]. Color measurement procedure

Color measurements were standardized by being performed by a single trained investigator under controlled conditions. To ensure consistency, all measurements were conducted using the same spectrophotometer (Spectroshade Micro, MHT, Italy), with the device calibrated before each session The specimens were positioned in a fixed orientation against a standardized white background, and measurements were taken at three predefined points on each sample.To minimize variability, all assessments were repeated three times, and the mean value was used for analysis.

All color measurements were performed by a single trained operator, and intraexaminer repeatability was confirmed by repeating measurements on 10 randomly selected specimens after a one-week interval, yielding an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) above 0.90.

The CIEDE2000 formula uses ΔL, ΔC, and ΔH to express differences in lightness, chroma, and hue. Adjustment factors (SL, SC, SH) and constants KL, KC, and KH (set to 1) were used to control measurement conditions. RT helps correct for interactions between chroma and hue, particularly in bluish tones.

To evaluate the clinical relevance of color change, thresholds for perceptibility (PT = 0.8 ΔE00) and acceptability (AT = 1.8 ΔE00) were used, based on the 50:50% visual assessment criteria. Calculations accounted for discontinuities in hue angle and chroma weighting to ensure accurate interpretation of color differences [30].

Surface roughness and microhardness measurements

Baseline measurements of surface roughness and microhardness were recorded for all specimens.

Measurement of surface roughness

Surface roughness (Ra, µm) was assessed using a contact-type profilometer (Perthometer M1, Mahr, Göttingen, Germany) equipped with a diamond stylus (tip radius 5 μm). The stylus scanned the specimen surface at a constant speed of 0.5 mm/s under a standardized force of 4 mN, with an automatic return function enabled. For each sample, three consecutive measurements were taken in different directions, and the mean Ra value was calculated [31]. Measurements were performed at baseline (T0), immediately after bleaching (T2), one week (T3), and one month (T4). A calibration block was used prior to each session to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Microhardness measurement

The Vickers microhardness tester (HMV-G Micro Vickers Hardness Tester, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) was used to measure the top and bottom surfaces of the specimens. A Vickers diamond pyramid indenter (136°) was applied with a load of 100 g for 15 s [32]. The Vickers hardness number (VHN) was automatically calculated using the device’s digital calculator. For each specimen, three indentations were made at least 1.0 mm apart and ≥ 1.0 mm from the specimen edge, and the mean of these readings was recorded as the final VHN value.Post-bleaching surface roughness and microhardness measurements were repeated immediately (T2), after one week (T3), and after one month (T4).

Measurement time points

Color (ΔE₀₀) was measured at five stages: T0 (baseline), T1 (after staining), T2 (immediately after bleaching), T3 (1-week follow-up), and T4 (1-month follow-up). In contrast, surface roughness (Ra) and Vickers microhardness (VHN) were assessed at four stages: T0 (baseline), T2 (immediately after bleaching), T3 (1-week follow-up), and T4 (1-month follow-up).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were calculated first. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which is more appropriate for small sample sizes. Data that satisfied parametric assumptions were analyzed with repeated-measures ANOVA and two-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests. For non-normally distributed data, the Friedman and Kruskal–Wallis tests were applied, with Dunn’s post hoc correction when appropriate. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient was used to examine relationships between non-normally distributed quantitative variables. For two-way ANOVA, both main effects (bleaching method and composite type) and their interaction were evaluated, and the Results section was revised to explicitly state the significance of these effects. Due to the format of the statistical software output, F-values and degrees of freedom could not be extracted. However, all significance values (p-values) are reported in detail, and the specific tests applied for each dataset are clearly indicated to ensure transparency. A significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted for all analyses.

Results

Color stability (ΔE₀₀)

All composites showed significant discoloration after coffee staining (T1), with ΔE₀₀ values exceeding the acceptability threshold (1.8) (p < 0.001). In-office bleaching achieved the greatest immediate improvement (T2), followed by laser-assisted and at-home protocols. At one month (T4), laser-assisted bleaching maintained the most stable results, particularly for OC and VU. Bleaching significantly increased surface roughness across all composites (p < 0.001). Laser-assisted bleaching produced the highest Ra values, while at-home bleaching resulted in the smallest changes. EU maintained the smoothest surfaces, whereas CDO and OC showed the greatest alterations. Discussion

The present in vitro study investigated the effects of at-home, in-office, and laser-assisted bleaching on the color stability, surface roughness, and microhardness of universal shade resin composites. Significant differences were found among bleaching protocols and composite resin types across all parameters; therefore, the null hypothesis was rejected.

Tooth bleaching has become one of the most popular and conservative methods used to improve the esthetic appearance of tooth color [33]. Bleaching agents commonly utilize hydrogen peroxide (HP) or its derivatives, such as carbamide peroxide (CP), as their active components [34, 35]. The whitening effect occurs through the breakdown of these peroxides into reactive free radicals [36, 37]. These radicals initiate redox reactions that alter the molecular structure of chromogenic compounds within dental tissues, ultimately leading to a visible color change [38, 39].

Statistically significant differences were observed among the tested composite resins regarding initial surface roughness and microhardness values. EU showed the lowest baseline roughness (p < 0.05), while OC and CDO exhibited the highest, with no significant difference between them. Both OC and CDO presented significantly rougher surfaces compared with EU and VU.

Following bleaching, surface roughness increased in all composite resins, with the most pronounced changes in the laser-assisted group. The increase was statistically significant for OC and CDO, which already had higher baseline values and exhibited further degradation. In contrast, EU maintained smoother surfaces, particularly after at-home bleaching, indicating better resistance to peroxide-related alterations.

In contrast to the findings of Altınışık and Özyurt [40], who reported the lowest surface roughness for Omnichroma (OC) and comparable values for Charisma Diamond One (CDO) and Essentia Universal (EU), the present study identified EU as the smoothest material, while OC and CDO exhibited the roughest surfaces. Possible explanations include differences in polishing systems, roughness measurement techniques, and filler characteristics. Variations in specimen preparation, storage, and surface treatment protocols may also have contributed. These findings highlight the need for methodological standardization when comparing surface properties across studies.

In a recent study, it was reported that bleaching procedures performed after discoloration with chlorhexidine significantly increased the surface roughness of universal-shade composites. This finding supports the notion that composite formulation plays a crucial role in determining surface alterations following bleaching [41].

Regarding microhardness, EU consistently showed the highest values, significantly greater than those of the other composite resins (p < 0.05). In contrast, OC exhibited the lowest microhardness at all time points. These results differ from those of Yılmaz Atalı et al. [42], who reported lower microhardness values for EU compared with other composite resins. This variation could stem from differences in experimental conditions, including curing devices, exposure times, and sample preparation protocols. Variables such as curing duration, light intensity, and type of curing unit directly affect the degree of conversion and, consequently, the mechanical properties of resin composites. Therefore, standardized polymerization and testing protocols are essential for meaningful comparisons across studies.

In our study, CDO exhibited the highest color change following coffee immersion, while VU showed the least discoloration. Conversely, an in vitro study by Özdemir et al. [43] reported that VU demonstrated the highest, and Neo Spectra ST the lowest color change after coffee exposure. This variation could stem from methodological differences; in their study, specimens were exposed to coffee for only 24 h, while our protocol involved 12 days of immersion, which may better simulate clinical staining potential. Pedrosa et al. [44] also emphasized that the advanced polymerization system (APS) technology in VU enhances color stability compared with conventional resin matrices. This aligns with our findings and suggests that composite resins incorporating APS technology, such as VU, may offer more favorable long-term esthetic outcomes, particularly for patients at risk of staining.

In the study conducted by Duzyol et al. [45], CDO exhibited the highest color change following coffee immersion, which aligns with the findings of the present study. The authors suggested that the borosilicate glass fillers and the presence of TEGDMA in the resin matrix may contribute to increased water sorption and stain retention. Similarly, in our study, CDO demonstrated the most pronounced discoloration when exposed to coffee, further supporting the influence of material composition on color stability. These findings indicate that clinicians should be cautious when selecting composite resins like CDO for patients with high exposure to staining agents, as their formulation may predispose them to esthetic degradation over time.

In terms of clinical relevance, the 50:50% perceptibility threshold (PT = 0.8 ΔE₀₀) and the 50:50% acceptability threshold (AT = 1.8 ΔE₀₀) are commonly cited in the literature as reference values for evaluating color changes in dental materials [30].

According to these thresholds, VU remained at or near the acceptability limit across most bleaching groups, indicating a level of discoloration that may be clinically tolerable. Although both OC and EU exceeded the acceptability threshold, their ΔE₀₀ values remained below or around the perceptibility limit, suggesting that color changes in these materials may not be easily noticed by patients. In contrast, ZC and particularly CDO demonstrated ΔE₀₀ values well above both thresholds, implying visibly noticeable discoloration that could compromise esthetic outcomes. Therefore, when restoring teeth in highly esthetic zones or in patients with high staining risk, selecting composite resins with lower discoloration potential such as VU may enhance long-term visual satisfaction and reduce the need for restoration replacement.

When evaluating the relationship between baseline surface roughness, microhardness, and coffee-induced discoloration, several notable findings emerged. Composite resins with higher initial surface roughness exhibited more pronounced color changes. In particular, OC and CDO showed the greatest discoloration following coffee immersion. This may be attributed to the increased retention and penetration of coffee particles on rougher surfaces, which can exacerbate staining. These results suggest that achieving a smooth surface finish during restorative procedures may play a critical role in minimizing long-term discoloration of composite restorations.

Composite resins with lower baseline surface roughness demonstrated less discoloration following coffee immersion. In particular, EU and VU, which exhibited the smoothest initial surfaces, showed the lowest ΔE₀₀ values after staining. This may be attributed to reduced adsorption of coffee particles on smoother surfaces, which limits pigment penetration and subsequent color change.

No direct correlation was observed between microhardness and color stability. Although it is generally assumed that composite resins with higher hardness would show better resistance to staining [46], our findings suggest a more complex interaction. EU, which had the highest microhardness, exhibited low discoloration as expected; however, VU, despite its moderate hardness, also showed similarly low ΔE₀₀ values. This indicates that color stability is not solely dependent on hardness, but may also be influenced by other factors such as resin matrix composition and filler characteristics.

Composite resins with lower microhardness values, particularly OC and CDO, exhibited greater susceptibility to discoloration. This may be explained by the fact that softer materials are more prone to absorbing external pigments due to their increased porosity and hydrophilicity, thereby resulting in more pronounced color changes over time.

A previous study reported that OC exhibited the lowest microhardness values among the tested universal composite resins, which was attributed to its specific filler structure and organic resin matrix composition [42]. In contrast, EU demonstrated consistently high microhardness values, with a notable increase over time. This trend may be associated with post-polymerization mechanisms that continue to enhance the material’s mechanical properties after initial curing. Differences in polymerization methods, storage conditions, and surface treatment protocols used across studies could also account for the observed variability in microhardness values. Although microhardness is a critical parameter for evaluating the mechanical durability of resin composites, color stability cannot be explained by hardness alone, as it is influenced by multiple factors including resin chemistry and filler characteristics. Therefore, clinicians should consider not only the mechanical strength but also the compositional characteristics of composite resins—such as filler type and resin matrix—when selecting materials for esthetic restorations, particularly in cases where long-term color stability is essential.

According to the findings of this study, laser-assisted bleaching led to the most significant increase in surface roughness across all tested composite resins. This effect was particularly pronounced in OC and CDO. Although in-office bleaching also increased surface roughness, its impact was less severe compared to the laser-assisted protocol. In EU and VU, changes in surface roughness following in-office bleaching remained relatively minimal. At-home bleaching resulted in the least surface roughness alteration; however, due to prolonged exposure, a slight but statistically significant increase in roughness was observed in OC and ZC. Mendes et al. [47] reported no statistically significant change in surface roughness after bleaching but noted the possibility of irreversible alterations on the resin composite surface. In contrast, our study demonstrated a clear increase in surface roughness, particularly following laser-assisted bleaching, suggesting that this method may induce abrasive effects on the composite surface.

In their study, Markovic et al. [48] investigated the effects of different bleaching agents on the surface roughness of nanohybrid and submicron hybrid resin composites, as well as glass ionomer cements. They reported an increase in surface roughness across all material groups. Similarly, in our study, all tested resin composites exhibited increased surface roughness after bleaching, with the most pronounced changes observed in the laser-assisted bleaching group. While Markovic et al. [48] noted that the highest roughness increase occurred with the 38% hydrogen peroxide group, our findings showed that laser-assisted bleaching led to the greatest surface degradation. This difference may be attributed to the combination of high-energy exposure and the generation of free radicals, which can compromise the integrity of the filler–matrix interface in resin composites.

Laser-assisted bleaching resulted in the greatest reduction in microhardness across all tested resin composites. This effect was particularly prominent in OC and CDO, both of which exhibited significant microhardness loss. In-office bleaching also led to a decrease in microhardness, with the most pronounced reduction observed in EU. At-home bleaching caused the least reduction in microhardness overall; however, statistically significant decreases were still recorded in VU and ZC. These findings suggest that bleaching agents—especially those used in laser-assisted protocols—may compromise the mechanical integrity of resin composite restorations, and therefore, their long-term clinical performance should be carefully considered when choosing bleaching methods for patients with existing resin-based restorations.

In a study conducted by Karataş et al. [49], bleaching-induced microhardness loss was found to be more prominent in microhybrid resin composites. Similarly, in the present study, laser-assisted bleaching resulted, in a significant reduction in microhardness, particularly in OC and CDO. However, unlike Karataş et al. [49] who reported no statistically significant change in surface roughness following bleaching, our findings demonstrated a clear increase in roughness after laser-assisted treatment. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in the composition of the resin composite materials used and the bleaching protocols applied, particularly the use of laser energy in the current study, which may have exerted more pronounced surface effects.

A previous study reported that nanofill and hybrid resin composites containing TEGDMA experienced more pronounced microhardness loss following bleaching [50]. In our study, although all tested composite resins contained TEGDMA, EU exhibited the highest baseline microhardness and the least reduction after bleaching. This suggests that changes in microhardness are not solely influenced by monomer composition, but may also be affected by filler loading, particle characteristics, and polymerization efficiency. Notably, laser-assisted bleaching resulted in the greatest microhardness loss across all resin composites, with OC and CDO showing the most significant reductions.

In the study conducted by Kwon et al. [51], the effects of 15% carbamide peroxide (CP) and 35% hydrogen peroxide (HP) bleaching agents on three nanofill resin composites (Ceram X, Grandio, and Filtek Z350) were evaluated. The authors reported a reduction in microhardness ranging from 8.1% to 10.7% across all materials. In our study, laser-assisted bleaching caused the greatest loss of microhardness, particularly in CDO and OC, while EU exhibited the least reduction. These differences may be attributed to variations in the organic matrix composition, filler content, and polymerization efficiency of the resin composites. While Kwon et al. [51] noted that prolonged application of carbamide peroxide led to increased microhardness loss, our findings suggest that high-energy protocols such as laser-assisted bleaching may cause even greater deterioration of the resin composite structure in a shorter period of time.

In their study, Gurgan et al. [52] compared surface roughness, microhardness, color change, and translucency values among two nanofill resin composites (Filtek Universal Restorative and Estelite Asteria), two nanohybrid resin composites (Charisma Diamond and Neo Spectra ST HV), and one additional nanohybrid resin composite (G-aenial A’CHORD). The authors reported that the control group (G-aenial A’CHORD) and Estelite Asteria exhibited the lowest surface roughness values, which they attributed to their high filler content. Similarly, in our study, EU demonstrated the lowest surface roughness among all tested resin composites, supporting the finding that resin composites with higher filler loading may result in smoother surfaces. However, following bleaching, the most significant increase in surface roughness was observed in OC and CDO, suggesting that bleaching procedures may alter filler–matrix interactions.

Furthermore, Gurgan et al. [52] reported that resin composites with higher filler content (such as Estelite Asteria and G-aenial A’CHORD) exhibited the lowest microhardness values, possibly due to differences in filler characteristics. In contrast, our study found that EU showed the highest baseline microhardness among all tested materials. These discrepancies could be attributed to differences in microhardness testing devices, surface preparation protocols, and variations in resin matrix composition.

In the study conducted by Wongpraparatana et al. [53], changes in surface roughness were evaluated after applying 10% CP and 40% HP bleaching agents to a nanofill composite resin (Filtek Z350) and a resin-modified glass ionomer. The authors reported an increase in surface roughness in both materials following bleaching, although no statistically significant difference was observed between the effects of 10% CP and 40% HP. Similarly, in the present study, all composite resins exhibited increased surface roughness after bleaching, with the most pronounced change observed in the laser-assisted bleaching group. In contrast, both in-office and at-home bleaching led to more limited increases in roughness. The lack of significant differences between bleaching methods in the Wongpraparatana et al. [53] study may support the relatively small differences observed between in-office and at-home groups in our research. However, the marked increase in surface roughness following laser-assisted bleaching suggests that this technique may induce more aggressive surface alterations in composite resins.

According to the findings of the present study, significant differences were observed among the bleaching methods in terms of color change in composite resins. Laser-assisted bleaching resulted in the highest level of whitening across all tested materials. The greatest color improvement was achieved in EU and VU, both of which also demonstrated high post-bleaching color stability. Although in-office bleaching was less effective than the laser protocol, it produced noticeable whitening in OC and ZC. At-home bleaching caused the least color change overall, and while it lightened CDO, the ΔE₀₀ value remained above the clinical acceptability threshold due to the resin composite’s initially high degree of discoloration. Clinically, a ΔE₀₀ value below 1.8 is considered the threshold for acceptability. In several groups treated with laser-assisted and in-office bleaching, ΔE₀₀ values fell below this limit, indicating visually tolerable color changes. However, due to its initially high staining level, the CDO resin composite still exhibited a perceptible discoloration after bleaching. These findings emphasize the importance of selecting both the bleaching method and the restorative material carefully to ensure optimal esthetic outcomes in patients with existing resin composite restorations.

Although different laser systems were evaluated in previous studies compared to the Er: YAG laser used in our study, findings in the literature show similarities with our results. Sürmelioğlu et al. [54] and Hahn et al. [55] reported no statistically significant differences in color change between groups activated with laser and those in which bleaching gel was used without activation. Similarly, Nguyen et al. [20] compared the whitening effectiveness of 35% hydrogen peroxide gels activated with Er: YAG laser and those applied without any activation, and found that the laser activation significantly increased the ΔE value. Furthermore, Ergin et al. [19] investigated the whitening efficacy of Er: YAG laser and LED light sources and found no significant difference in terms of color change. These discrepancies in the literature may be attributed to differences in the types of laser systems used, bleaching agent concentrations, application durations, power settings, and structural characteristics of the tooth surface.

Laser-assisted bleaching appears to intensify both chemical and physical stresses at the composite surface. The rapid photo-thermal activation of hydrogen peroxide by Er: YAG irradiation can accelerate the formation of free radicals, while localized heating and micro-bubble dynamics within the bleaching gel may further compromise the filler–matrix interface. Such processes can weaken silane bonds, promote filler particle loss, and create surface irregularities, leading to increased roughness. In parallel, the organic resin matrix may undergo softening and partial degradation, resulting in reduced microhardness. These findings suggest that the harsher effects of laser-assisted protocols are likely due to the combined influence of accelerated oxidative activity and localized thermal damage, which can magnify the surface and mechanical alterations compared to conventional bleaching methods.

Following the bleaching procedures, notable alterations were observed in the color stability, microhardness, and surface roughness of resin composite materials within the first week. In terms of color change, a slight increase in ΔE₀₀ values was noted in the laser-assisted and in-office bleaching groups, particularly for the CDO and ZC resin composites, indicating decreased color stability. However, EU and VU resin composites maintained color changes within clinically acceptable limits (ΔE₀₀ <1.8).

Regarding microhardness, partial recovery was observed in certain resin composites, potentially due to the gradual reduction in the bleaching agent’s residual effects and the initiation of remineralization processes. OC and CDO groups showed a more noticeable increase in hardness, while changes in EU and VU remained minimal, suggesting that the microhardness response is influenced not only by the bleaching procedure but also by the intrinsic properties of the resin composite.

As for surface roughness, partial surface smoothing was observed in OC and CDO resin composites after one week. In contrast, a slight increase in roughness was detected in EU and VU groups, which may be attributed to the formation of surface microporosities following bleaching.

After one month, the changes in color stability, microhardness, and surface roughness persisted. Color deterioration continued in CDO and ZC resin composites, while EU and VU resin composites remained stable and within clinically acceptable thresholds. Microhardness recovery persisted in OC and CDO groups but was limited in the others. Improvements in surface smoothness continued in OC and CDO resin composites, whereas EU and VU displayed a slight increase in roughness.

The variability between our findings and previous reports can be attributed to differences in bleaching protocols, exposure times, and surface preparation methods. From a clinical perspective, even small increases in roughness or reductions in hardness may predispose restorations to staining, plaque retention, and wear. Although direct clinical evidence is limited, such changes are likely to increase the need for maintenance procedures such as repolishing or repair, which may ultimately influence the long-term survival of restorations.

These findings suggest that physical and esthetic alterations in composite resins may continue up to one month after bleaching. While improvements were noted in surface roughness and color stability for certain materials, the recovery in microhardness remained limited. Clinically, additional polishing procedures may enhance the longevity of restorations, especially in resin composites exhibiting high post-bleaching roughness. The observed partial recovery in hardness implies a reduction in the bleaching agent’s long-term effect, but the continued color change in some materials highlights the need for careful selection based on long-term esthetic performance.

It should be noted that resin composites, unlike dental hard tissues, cannot undergo true remineralization. Previous studies have reported that the application of remineralizing agents such as casein phosphopeptide-amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP) or fluoride can partially recover microhardness loss after bleaching. In the present study, no remineralizing agent was used. Therefore, the partial recovery in microhardness observed over time may be attributed to the dissipation of residual peroxide and water sorption within the resin matrix, rather than actual remineralization.

Accordingly, selecting resin composites with initially higher microhardness and lower discoloration susceptibility is recommended. Furthermore, applying surface protection and polishing protocols post-bleaching may enhance the clinical success and durability of restorations.

This study was conducted under in vitro conditions and does not fully replicate the oral environment, where saliva, enzymatic activity, dietary variations, and mechanical abrasion may influence outcomes. Coffee was the only staining solution tested, and its use may not reflect the effects of other common beverages such as tea, red wine, or soft drinks. The follow-up period was limited to one month, and longer-term evaluations are required to better understand aging behavior. In addition, no mechanical aging protocols such as thermocycling or chewing simulation were included, which may have affected the extrapolation of results. A single polishing technique and curing protocol were applied, and no protective surface interventions (such as repolishing or coating) were performed after bleaching. Finally, the results apply only to the specific composites tested and cannot be generalized to all universal shade materials.

It should also be considered that bleaching-related changes may not remain stable over time. In daily clinical conditions, restorations are continuously exposed to thermal fluctuations, dietary acids, and mechanical wear. These challenges can intensify the effects of bleaching, leading to further surface degradation and progressive color mismatch. Therefore, laboratory simulations of aging, such as thermal or mechanical cycling, are essential to better predict the long-term behavior of bleached composites. From a clinical perspective, this underlines the importance of monitoring patients after bleaching and performing maintenance procedures such as repolishing or applying protective coatings when necessary.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, the performance of universal shade resin composites was significantly influenced by the bleaching method (p < 0.05). Laser-assisted bleaching caused the most adverse effects, increasing surface roughness and reducing microhardness, while at-home bleaching had the least impact. In-office bleaching provided the most immediate whitening.

Among the tested materials, Essentia Universal exhibited superior resistance to discoloration, microhardness loss, and surface degradation, supporting its potential clinical preference in patients undergoing bleaching. In contrast, Omnichroma and Charisma Diamond One were significantly more prone to staining and mechanical compromise, particularly under laser activation.

Although a partial recovery in microhardness was observed over time, surface roughness and color changes persisted, suggesting possible long-term esthetic and functional concerns. Clinically, practitioners are advised to select resin composites with higher baseline hardness and better color stability (e.g., Essentia Universal, Vittra APS) when restorations are likely to be exposed to bleaching procedures. Additionally, performing polishing or surface protection protocols after bleaching may improve restoration longevity andhelp maintain both esthetic appearance and surface integrity over time.
