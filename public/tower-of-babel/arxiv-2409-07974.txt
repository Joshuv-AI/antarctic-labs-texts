Christoffel words, first introduced by E.B. Christoffel
[
2
]
in 1875, are a special class of binary words linked to the theory of continued fractions, and regarded as a finitary analogue of Sturmian sequences. A binary word
w
is a
Christoffel word
if it is Lyndon and cyclically balanced. By a
Christoffel matrix
we shall mean a square matrix representing the lexicographic array of a Christoffel word. More precisely, fix
n\geq 2
and let
({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}
denote the multiplicative group of integers modulo
n
consisting of all integers from
\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
coprime with
n.
For each
m\in({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times},
let
C_{m}(a,b)
denote the
n\times n
matrix whose first row is the Christoffel word
c_{m}(a,b)
of length
n,
on distinct symbols
a
and
b,
beginning in
a
(and hence ending in
b)
and having
m
-many
a
’s and hence
(n-m)
-many
b
’s, and whose subsequent rows consist in the other
n-1
cyclic conjugates of
c_{m}(a,b)
arranged in increasing lexicographic order (relative to the order
a<b).
The quantity
m\in({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}
is called the
type
of the Christoffel matrix
C_{m}(a,b)
(or of the corresponding Christoffel word
c_{m}(a,b))
and is equal to the number of occurrences of the first letter
a
in
c_{m}(a,b).
For example, taking
n=5

C_{2}(a,b)=\begin{pmatrix}a&b&a&b&b\\
a&b&b&a&b\\
b&a&b&a&b\\
b&a&b&b&a\\
b&b&a&b&a\end{pmatrix}

is the
5\times 5
Christoffel matrix of type
2
corresponding to the Christoffel word
ababb.
It also follows that the reverse of
c_{m}(a,b)
is the Christoffel word
c_{n-m}(b,a)
corresponding to the Christoffel matrix
C_{n-m}(b,a).
We remark that the notation
C_{m}(a,b)
(or
c_{m}(a,b))
does not make reference to the size
n
of the matrix (or the length
n
of the Christoffel word) in the same way that the notation
(i,j)
for a
2
-cycle in the symmetric group
S_{n}
does not make reference to the number of letters
n.

We begin by reviewing some basic facts concerning the lexicographic array of a Christoffel word : First, since all rows of
C_{m}(a,b)
are conjugate to one another, it follows that each Christoffel matrix is, up to a permutation of the rows, a circulant matrix
[
6
]
.
We recall the following characterisation of the lexicographic array of a Christoffel word : The lexicographic array of a binary word
w\in\{a,b\}^{n}
having
m
-many
a
’s and
(n-m)
-many
b
’s with
m,n
coprime has the
lexicographic constant shift property
, i.e., each row of the array is a constant shift
p
of the preceding row if and only if it is the lexicographic array of a Christoffel word. Moreover, in this case the constant shift
p
is equal to
m^{-1}\bmod n
(see Theorem C in
[
5
]
). Clearly if each row of a
n\times n
matrix
M
is a shift by
m^{-1}
to the right of the preceding row, then each column of
M
is a shift by
m
downward of the preceding column, and conversely. Hence each column of the matrix
C_{m}(a,b)
is a shift by
m
of the preceding column and of course the first column consists of a block of
a
’s of size
m
followed by a block of
b
’s of size
n-m.
In fact, this column description also characterises Christoffel matrices. To see this, suppose
M
is a
n\times n
matrix with the property that i) column
1
of
M
consists of a block of
a
’s of size
m
followed by a block of
b
’s of size
n-m,
and ii) each subsequent column is a shift downward by
m
of the preceding. Then by ii), each row of
M
is a shift to the right by
m^{-1}
of the preceding row. In particular the rows of
M
are all conjugate to one another. It is also easily verified that the rows of
M
are arranged in lexicographic order relative to the order
a<b.
In fact, any two consecutive rows differ in precisely two consecutive positions : in these positions, the upper row has
ab
while the lower has
ba.
Hence (by Theorem C),
M
is the lexicographic array of a Christoffel word.

Let
R
be an integral domain. We write
0,1
for the additive and multiplicative identity of
R.
In the next lemma we show that Christoffel matrices over
R
commute under multiplication and that the product of two
n\times n
Christoffel matrices over

is a Christoffel matrix over
R

Lemma 1
.

Let
A=C_{m_{1}}(a_{1},b_{1})
and
B=C_{m_{2}}(a_{2},b_{2})
be two Christoffel matrices of size
n
over
R
with

m_{1},m_{2}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}
and
a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}\in R.
Put
r=\left\lceil\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{n}\right\rceil
and
s=\left\lfloor\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{n}\right\rfloor.
Then

AB=BA=C_{m_{3}}(a_{3},b_{3})

where
m_{3}=m_{1}m_{2}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}
(meaning
m_{1}m_{2}=ns+m_{3}),

a_{3}=ra_{1}a_{2}+(m_{1}-r)a_{1}b_{2}+(m_{2}-r)b_{1}a_{2}+(n-(m_{1}+m_{2})+r)b_{1}b_{2}

and

b_{3}=sa_{1}a_{2}+(m_{1}-s)a_{1}b_{2}+(m_{2}-s)b_{1}a_{2}+(n-(m_{1}+m_{2})+s)b_{1}b_{2}.

Moreover, setting
\delta_{i}=a_{i}-b_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}=m_{i}a_{i}+(n-m_{i})b_{i}
(for
i=1,2,3)
we have
\delta_{3}=\delta_{1}\delta_{2}\neq 0
and

\sigma_{3}=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}.

Proof.

We begin by noting that as
R
is commutative, each of
a_{3}
and
b_{3}
given above is invariant under exchange of indices
1
and
2.

Thus it suffices to show that
AB=C_{m_{3}}(a_{3},b_{3}).
Write
A=(a_{i,j}),

B=(b_{i,j})
and
C=(c_{i,j})=AB.

To show that
C=C_{m_{1}m_{2}}(a_{3},b_{3})
, we use the column characterisation of Christoffel matrices mentioned earlier : i)
c_{i,j}=c_{i+m_{1}m_{2},j+1}
for all
i,j
; ii) the first column of the matrix
C
consists of
m_{1}m_{2}\bmod n
consecutive
a_{3}
followed by
(n-m_{1}m_{2})\bmod n
consecutive
b_{3}
; iii)
a_{3}\neq b_{3}.

As for i), using the relations
a_{i,j}=a_{i+m_{1},j+1}
(and hence
a_{i,j}=a_{i+m_{1}m_{2},j+m_{2}})
and
b_{i,j}=b_{i+m_{2},j+1}
we have

c_{i,j}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{i,k}b_{k,j}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{i,k}b_{k+m_{2},j+1}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{i+m_{1}m_{2},k+m_{2}}b_{k+m_{2},j+1}=c_{i+m_{1}m_{2},j+1}

where all indices are taken modulo
n.

We next look at column
1
of the matrix
C.
Fix
1\leq i\leq n
and write row
i
of matrix
A
as
uv
where
u
is the prefix of length
m_{2}
and
v
the suffix of length
n-m_{2}.

Then taking the dot product of row
i
of
A
with column
1
of
B
(which is made up of
m_{2}
consecutive
a_{2}
followed by
(n-m_{2})
consecutive

gives

c_{i,1}=|u|_{a_{1}}a_{1}a_{2}+|v|_{a_{1}}a_{1}b_{2}+|u|_{b_{1}}b_{1}a_{2}+|v|_{b_{1}}b_{1}b_{2}

where as usual
|x|_{a}
denotes the number of occurrences of
a
in

Put
r=\left\lceil\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{n}\right\rceil
and
s=\left\lfloor\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{n}\right\rfloor.

First consider the case in which
u
is
rich
in
a_{1},
meaning
|u|_{a_{1}}=r
; in this case

is poor in
a_{1}
and hence

|v|_{a_{1}}=\left\lfloor\frac{m_{1}|v|}{n}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{m_{1}(n-m_{2})}{n}\right\rfloor=m_{1}-r.

Similarly, as

is rich in

it follows that

is poor in

|u|_{b_{1}}=\left\lfloor\frac{(n-m_{1})|u|}{n}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{(n-m_{1})m_{2}}{n}\right\rfloor=m_{2}-r.

And finally, as

is poor in

it follows that

is rich in

and hence

|v|_{b_{1}}=\left\lceil\frac{(n-m_{1})|v|}{n}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{(n-m_{1})(n-m_{2})}{n}\right\rceil=n-(m_{1}+m_{2})+r.

If on the other hand

is poor in

then a similar calculation shows the same result except with

everywhere replaced by

Thus if the prefix of length
m_{2}
of row

is rich in

then

while if it is poor in

then

Now for any

of length

(since the corresponding suffix of the same length is given by

Thus in particular

i.e., column

of

begins in

Now following

]
, any two consecutive rows of a Christoffel matrix differ precisely in two consecutive positions. When comparing lexicographically the prefixes of length

of the rows of

the switch from rich to poor (in

in other words the position

of the previous column, in column

Finally to see that

we have

\displaystyle\delta_{3}=a_{3}-b_{3}

\displaystyle=(r-s)a_{1}a_{2}+(s-r)a_{1}b_{2}+(s-r)b_{1}a_{2}+(r-s)b_{1}b_{2}

\displaystyle=a_{1}a_{2}-a_{1}b_{2}-b_{1}a_{2}+b_{1}b_{2}

\displaystyle=(a_{1}-b_{1})(a_{2}-b_{2})=\delta_{1}\delta_{2}\neq 0

since
a_{1}\neq b_{1}
and
a_{2}\neq b_{2}.
Finally, to see that
\sigma_{3}=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}
it suffices to multiply out
(m_{1}a_{1}+(n-m_{1})b_{1})(m_{2}a_{2}+(n-m_{2})b_{2})
and use the relations
m_{1}m_{2}=ns+m_{3}
and

∎

If
R
is not an integral domain, then the product of two Christoffel matrices may not be binary - for example, taking

over

we have

C_{1}(2,0)C_{1}(3,0)

The following proposition shows that in general Christoffel matrices are diagonalisable :

Proposition 2
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
m\in({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}.
Let
A=C_{m}(a,b)
be a Christoffel matrix of size
n
with
a,b
belonging to an algebraically closed field
{\mathbb{F}}.
Then the minimal polynomial
m_{A}(x)
divides
(x^{k}-\delta^{k})(x-\sigma)
where
k
is the order of
m,

\delta=a-b
and
\sigma=ma+(n-m)b.
Moreover, if neither
n
nor
k
is divisible by
\text{char}({\mathbb{F}})
(so in particular in characteristic
0),
then
A
is diagonalisable.

Proof.

Put
p(x)=(x^{k}-\delta^{k})(x-\sigma).
Since
m^{k}\equiv 1\bmod n,
by Lemma
1
we have
A^{k}=C_{m^{k}}(c,d)=C_{1}(c,d)
for some
c,d\in{\mathbb{F}}
with
c-d=\delta^{k}.
It follows that
A^{k}-\delta^{k}I=(d)_{n\times n}
the constant matrix of all
d
’s. As each column of the matrix
A-\sigma I
sums to
0,
we deduce that
(A^{k}-\delta^{k}I)(A-\sigma I)=(0)_{n\times n},
i.e.,
p(A)=(0)_{n\times n}.
Thus
m_{A}(x)
divides
p(x)=(x^{k}-\delta^{k})(x-\sigma).
Now assume that neither
n
nor
k
is divisible by
\text{char}({\mathbb{F}}).
If
\sigma^{k}\neq\delta^{k},
then
p(x)
has
k+1
distinct roots, namely the
k
’th roots of
\delta^{k}
(of which there are
k
) and
\sigma.
Since
m_{A}(x)
divides
p(x),
the roots of
m_{A}(x)
are each of multiplicity
1
and hence
A
is diagonalisable.
If on the other hand
\sigma^{k}=\delta^{k},
then we claim that
A^{k}-\delta^{k}I=(0)_{n\times n}.
In fact, setting
B=A^{k}-\delta^{k}I
we have that
B=(d)_{n\times n}
but also
B=A^{k}-\sigma^{k}I.
By Lemma
1
, the row/column sum of
A^{k}
is
\sigma^{k},
and therefore each row/column of
B
sums to
0.

So
nd=0
and since
\text{char}({\mathbb{F}})
does not divide
n
it follows that
d=0
as required. Hence
m_{A}(x)
divides
x^{k}-\delta^{k}
which has
k
distinct roots. Thus again all roots of the minimal polynomial
m_{A}(x)
are simple and hence
A
is diagonalisable.
∎

Remark 1
.

The Christoffel matrix

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}

is diagonalisable in characteristic

but not in characteristic

In fact,

which has distinct roots (
\pm 1)
in characteristic

While in characteristic

and hence

is not diagonalisable.

Remark 2
.

Since all

Christoffel matrices over the complex numbers commute under multiplication (by Lemma
1
) and are diagonalisable (by Proposition
2
), they are simultaneously diagonalisable, i.e., there exists a matrix

such that

is diagonal for every

Christoffel matrix

over
{\mathbb{C}}.
The columns of

constitute a basis for

We now consider the inverse of an invertible Christoffel matrix over an integral domain

The matrix

cf-ed
divides
\det C_{m}(a,b)
which by assumption is a unit in
R,
and hence
cf-ed
is a unit in
R.

Next we claim that
f+d\neq 0.
In fact, suppose to the contrary that
f+d=0.
By Lemma
1

c-d=a-b=f-e,
and hence
c+e=0.

Thus

C_{m}(a,b)\left(C_{m^{-1}}(1,0)+C_{m^{-1}}(0,1)\right)=C_{1}(c,d)+C_{1}(e,f)=C_{1}(c,d)+C_{1}(-c,-d)=(0)_{n\times n}.

But
C_{m^{-1}}(1,0)+C_{m^{-1}}(0,1)
is the constant matrix
(1)_{n\times n}
and hence

C_{m}(a,b)(1)_{n\times n}=(0)_{n\times n}
which is a contradiction since
C_{m}(a,b)
is assumed to be invertible.

We have thus far that the matrix on the right hand side of (
2
) is well defined (meaning that
cf-ed
is a unit) and a Christoffel matrix (meaning that
f(fc-ed)^{-1}\neq-d(fc-ed)^{-1}).

We now show that this matrix is in fact the inverse of
C_{m}(a,b).
First suppose that
df=0.
Then either
d=0
or
f=0.

If
d=0,
then

C_{m}(a,b)C_{m^{-1}}(1,0)=C_{1}(c,0)=cI_{n\times n}

where
I_{n\times n}
denotes the identity matrix over
R.
Also as
d=0
we have that

cf
is a unit (and hence
c
is a unit) and
C_{m}(a,b)^{-1}=C_{m^{-1}}(c^{-1},0)
which is a special case of equation (
2
) when
d=0.
Similarly if
f=0
then we find that
C_{m}(a,b)^{-1}=C_{m^{-1}}(0,e^{-1})
as required.
Finally if
df\neq 0,
then

C_{m}(a,b)C_{m^{-1}}(f,0)=C_{1}(fc,fd)

and

C_{m}(a,b)C_{m^{-1}}(0,-d)=C_{1}(-ed,-fd)

and therefore

C_{m}(a,b)\left(C_{m^{-1}}(f,0)+C_{m^{-1}}(0,-d)\right)=C_{1}(fc-ed,0)

or equivalently

C_{m}(a,b)C_{m^{-1}}(f,-d)=C_{1}(fc-ed,0).

Hence

C_{m}(a,b)^{-1}=C_{m^{-1}}(f(cf-ed)^{-1},-d(fc-ed)^{-1}).

∎

The previous lemma allows us to define an involution on (word) isomorphism classes of Christoffel words of length
n\geq 2.
Let
\omega
be a Christoffel class of length
n
and of type
m\in({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}.
Pick a representative
w
in
\{0,1\}^{n}
having odd many
1
’s (clearly
m
and
n-m
cannot both be even). Then the corresponding Christoffel matrix
C
is invertible over the field
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}=\{0,1\}
and its inverse
C^{-1}
is a Christoffel matrix of type
m^{-1}
corresponding to some Christoffel word
w^{\prime}
over the alphabet
\{0,1\}.
The isomorphism class of
w^{\prime}
is then the so-called
dual
of
\omega
in the sense of
[
1
]
.

Corollary 4
.

Let
R
be an integral domain and
n\geq 2.
Then the set
GC_{n}(R)
of all invertible
n\times n
Christoffel matrices over
R
is an abelian subgroup of
GL_{n}(R).
The subset
H
of
GC_{n}(R)
consisting of all type
1
invertible Christoffel matrices is a subgroup of
GC_{n}(R).
If
R
is a field, then the quotient
GC_{n}(R)/H
is isomorphic to
({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}.

Proof.

It follows from lemmas
1
and
3
that the set of all
n\times n
invertible Christoffel matrices over
R
is closed under multiplication and inverse and hence constitutes an abelian subgroup of
GL_{n}(R).
Define
f:GC_{n}(R)\rightarrow({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}
by
f(C_{m}(a,b))=m.
It follows immediately from Lemma
1
that
f
is a group homomorphism. The kernel of
f
consists precisely of all type
1
invertible matrices over
R
and hence
\mbox{Ker}(f)=H
from which it follows that
H
is a subgroup of
GC_{n}(R).

Now if
R
is a field, then
f
is surjective; to see this, it suffices to show that for each
m\in({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times},
either
C_{m}(1,0)
or
C_{m}(0,1)
(or both) is invertible. In fact, suppose that
C_{m}(1,0)
is not invertible. This implies that the row sum
m1=0.
Since
m
and
n-m
are coprime, it follows from Bézout’s identity that
(n-m)1\neq 0
and hence
C_{m}(0,1)
is invertible. It follows that if
R
is a field, then
GC_{n}(R)/H\simeq({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}.

∎

Now assume that
F
is a finite field. Then as
({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}
is isomorphic to a quotient of the finite abelian group
GC_{n}(F),
it follows that
GC_{n}(F)
contains a subgroup isomorphic to
({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}.
In particular, for each
n\geq 2
the group
({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}
admits a faithful representation in terms of
\varphi(n)
-many invertible
n\times n
Christoffel matrices over
F.
Since every finite abelian group is a subgroup of
({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}
for some
n,
we have

Corollary 5
.

Let
F
be a finite field and
G
a finite abelian group. Then there exists
n\geq 2
and a faithful representation
G\rightarrow GL_{n}(F)
of degree
n
over
F
consisting entirely of invertible Christoffel matrices over
F.
In particular (taking
F={\mathbb{F}}_{2}=\{0,1\}),

G
admits a linear representation over
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
consisting of
|G|
-many distinct
n\times n
invertible Christoffel matrices over
\{0,1\}.

In what follows we compute the general Christoffel group over the field
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
:

Proposition 6
.

Let
n\geq 2
and write
n=2^{k}p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}
with
k,\alpha_{i}\geq 0
and
p_{i}
distinct odd primes. Then

(3)

GC_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\simeq GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\times GC_{p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\times\cdots\times GC_{p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}).

Moreover,
GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2^{k-1}{\mathbb{Z}}
for each
k\geq 1
while
GC_{p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\simeq({\mathbb{Z}}/p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}Z)^{\times}
for each
1\leq i\leq t.

Proof.

First assume
n
is odd, i.e.,
n=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}.
As in Corollary
4
, let
H
denote the subgroup of
GC_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
consisting all type
1
invertible Christoffel matrices. Then
H=\{C_{1}(1,0)\}
since
C_{1}(0,1)
has row sum
n-1\equiv 0\bmod 2
and hence is not invertible.
It follows from Corollary
4
that
GC_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\simeq({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}.
Thus for
n
odd, we have

GC_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\simeq({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}\simeq({\mathbb{Z}}/p_{1}^{\alpha_{i}}Z)^{\times}\times\cdots\times({\mathbb{Z}}/p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}Z)^{\times}\simeq GC_{p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\times\cdots\times GC_{p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})

as required.

Next assume
n
is even and write
n=2^{k}n^{\prime}
with
k\geq 1
and
n^{\prime}=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots p_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}.
In view of the odd case, in order to establish (
3
) it suffices to show that
GC_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\simeq GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\times({\mathbb{Z}}/n^{\prime}Z)^{\times}.
We note that for
n
even, the order of
GC_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
is twice the order of
({\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}.

This is because when
n
is even, the Christoffel matrices
C_{m}(0,1)
and
C_{m}(1,0)
are both invertible (for each
m\in({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}).
For
m\in({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times},
put
a_{m}=C_{m}(1,0)
and
b_{m}=C_{m}(0,1).
Applying Lemma
1
we find :

(4)

a_{m_{1}}a_{m_{2}}=b_{m_{1}}b_{m_{2}}=\begin{cases}a_{m_{1}m_{2}}&\text{if $\left\lfloor\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{n}\right\rfloor$ is even}\\
b_{m_{1}m_{2}}&\text{if $\left\lfloor\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{n}\right\rfloor$ is odd}\end{cases}

and

(5)

a_{m_{1}}b_{m_{2}}=b_{m_{1}}a_{m_{2}}=\begin{cases}b_{m_{1}m_{2}}&\text{if $\left\lfloor\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{n}\right\rfloor$ is even}\\
a_{m_{1}m_{2}}&\text{if $\left\lfloor\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{n}\right\rfloor$ is odd}\end{cases}

where as always the indices are taken modulo
n
in
({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}.
Thus for
n
even,
GC_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})=\{a_{m},b_{m}\}_{m\in({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}}
subject to the above relations. We notice that
a_{1}
is the identity and
b_{1},a_{n-1}
and
b_{n-1}
are each elements of order
2
from which it follows that
CG_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
is never cyclic for
n>2
even.

It is easily checked by induction that for each
j\geq 2,

(6)

(a_{m})^{j}=(b_{m})^{j}=\begin{cases}a_{m^{j}}&\text{if $\left\lfloor\frac{m^{j}}{n}\right\rfloor$ is even}\\
b_{m^{j}}&\text{if $\left\lfloor\frac{m^{j}}{n}\right\rfloor$ is odd}.\end{cases}

It will be convenient to have a general formula for the product
x_{m_{1}}y_{m_{2}}
with
x,y\in\{a,b\}
and
m_{1},m_{2}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}.
To this end, we consider
(\{a,b\},*)
as the cyclic group of order two with
a
being the identity and
*
the group law. Thus
a*a=b*b=a
and
a*b=b*a=b.
Also let
i:\{a,b\}\rightarrow\{a,b\}
denote the involution which exchanges
a
and
b.
Then it is easily verified that
i(x*y)=i(x)*y=x*i(y)
for all
x,y\in\{a,b\},
from which it follows that

(7)

i^{q_{1}+q_{2}}(x*y)=i^{q_{1}}(x)*i^{q_{2}}(y)

for all nonnegative integers
q_{1},q_{2}
and all
x,y\in\{a,b\}.

Lemma 7
.

Let
x_{m_{1}},y_{m_{2}}\in GC_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}).
Then

(8)

x_{m_{1}}y_{m_{2}}=i^{q}(x*y)_{r}

where
m_{1}m_{2}=qn+r
with
r\in({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}.

Proof.

Writing
m_{1}m_{2}=qn+r
with
r\in({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}
so that
q=\left\lfloor\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{n}\right\rfloor,
and using (
4
) and (
5
) we see that if
x=y,
then

x_{m_{1}}y_{m_{2}}=\begin{cases}a_{r}&\text{if $q$ is even}\\
b_{r}&\text{if $q$ is odd}\end{cases}

On the other hand if
x=y
we have

i^{q}(x*y)_{r}=i^{q}(a)_{r}=\begin{cases}a_{r}&\text{if $q$ is even}\\
b_{r}&\text{if $q$ is odd}\end{cases}

A similar calculation handles the case when
x\neq y.

∎

Thus
GC_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})=\{x_{m}\,|\,x\in\{a,b\},\,m\in({\mathbb{Z}}/n^{\prime}{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}\}
and the group law is given by (
8
).

Lemma 8
.

Let

\Psi:GC_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\rightarrow GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\times({\mathbb{Z}}/n^{\prime}Z)^{\times}

be given by

x_{m}\mapsto(i^{q}(x)_{r},r^{\prime})

where
m=q2^{k}+r
and
m=q^{\prime}n^{\prime}+r^{\prime}
with
r\in({\mathbb{Z}}/2^{k}{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}
and
r^{\prime}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/n^{\prime}{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}.
Then
\Psi
is a group isomorphism.

Proof.

We begin by showing that
\Psi
is a group homomorphism. For this it suffices to show that the restriction of
\Psi
to each of the two coordinates is a group homomorphism. By Lemma
1
the restriction of
\Psi
to the second coordinate is a group homomorphism as the type of the product of two Christoffel matrices of size
n
is just the product of the types taken modulo
n.
So let

\Psi_{1}:GC_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\rightarrow GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})

be given by
\Psi_{1}(x_{m})=i^{q}(x)_{r}
where
m=q2^{k}+r
with
r\in({\mathbb{Z}}/2^{k}{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}.
We show
\Psi_{1}
is a group homomorphism. Let
x_{m_{1}},y_{m_{2}}\in GC_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
and write
m_{1}m_{2}=qn+r,

m_{1}=q_{1}2^{k}+r_{1},

m_{2}=q_{2}2^{k}+r_{2},

r_{1}r_{2}=q_{3}2^{k}+r_{3}
and
r=q^{\prime}2^{k}+r^{\prime}
with
r\in({\mathbb{Z}}/nZ)^{\times}
and
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},r^{\prime}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/2^{k}Z)^{\times}.

It follows immediately that
r^{\prime}=r_{3}.
Applying Lemma
7
we have that
x_{m_{1}}y_{m_{2}}=i^{q}(x*y)_{r}.

Therefore

\Psi_{1}(x_{m_{1}}y_{m_{2}})=\Psi_{1}(i^{q}(x*y)_{r})=i^{q^{\prime}}(i^{q}(x*y))_{r^{\prime}}=i^{q+q^{\prime}}(x*y)_{r^{\prime}}.

On the other hand
\Psi_{1}(x_{m_{1}})=i^{q_{1}}(x)_{r_{1}}
and
\Psi_{1}(y_{m_{2}})=i^{q_{2}}(y)_{r_{2}}
and therefore

\Psi_{1}(x_{m_{1}})\Psi_{1}(y_{m_{2}})=i^{q_{1}}(x)_{r_{1}}i^{q_{2}}(y)_{r_{2}}=i^{q_{3}}(i^{q_{1}}(x)*i^{q_{2}}(y))_{r_{3}}=i^{q_{3}}(i^{q_{1}+q_{2}}(x*y))_{r_{3}}=i^{q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3}}(x*y)_{r_{3}}

where equation (
7
) was used for the penultimate equality. Having already established that
r^{\prime}=r_{3},
to show that
\Psi_{1}(x_{m_{1}}y_{m_{2}})=\Psi_{1}(x_{m_{1}})\Psi_{1}(y_{m_{2}})
it remains to show that
q+q^{\prime}\equiv q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3}\bmod 2.
However,

m_{1}m_{2}=qn+r=q(\frac{n}{2^{k}})2^{k}+q^{\prime}2^{k}+r^{\prime}=(q(\frac{n}{2^{k}})+q^{\prime})2^{k}+r^{\prime}.

On the other hand,

m_{1}m_{2}=(q_{1}2^{k}+r_{1})(q_{2}2^{k}+r_{2})=(q_{1}q_{2}2^{k}+q_{1}r_{2}+q_{2}r_{1})2^{k}+r_{1}r_{2}=(q_{1}q_{2}2^{k}+q_{1}r_{2}+q_{2}r_{1}+q_{3})2^{k}+r_{3}

from which it follows that

q(\frac{n}{2^{k}})+q^{\prime}=q_{1}q_{2}2^{k}+q_{1}r_{2}+q_{2}r_{1}+q_{3}.

Now since
\frac{n}{2^{k}},r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are each odd, reducing modulo
2
we find that
q+q^{\prime}\equiv q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3}\bmod 2
as required.

Having shown that
\Psi
is a group homomorphism, let us show that
\Psi
is injective. Assume
x_{m}\in\mbox{Ker}\,\Psi.
Then
\Psi(x_{m})=(a_{1},1)
where
a_{1}
here denotes the identity Christoffel matrix of size
2^{k}.
Then writing
m=q2^{k}+r
we have
a=i^{q}(x)
and
r=1.
Also
m=q^{\prime}n^{\prime}+1.
Thus as
m\equiv 1\bmod 2^{k}
and
m\equiv 1\bmod n^{\prime}
it follows that
m\equiv 1\bmod n
and hence
m=1
and
q=0.
This implies that
a=i^{q}(x)=i^{0}(x)=x
and hence
x_{m}=a_{1}
where this time
a_{1}
denotes the identity Christoffel matrix of size
n.
Hence
\Psi
is injective. To see that
\Psi
is surjective, it suffices to show that
\mbox{Card}\,GC_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})=\mbox{Card}\,(GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\times({\mathbb{Z}}/n^{\prime}Z)^{\times}).
In fact,
\mbox{Card}\,GC_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})=2\,\mbox{Card}\,({\mathbb{Z}}/n^{\prime}{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}
while

\displaystyle\mbox{Card}\,(GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\times({\mathbb{Z}}/n^{\prime}Z)^{\times})

\displaystyle=\mbox{Card}\,\,GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\,\mbox{Card}\,({\mathbb{Z}}/n^{\prime}Z)^{\times}

\displaystyle=2\,\mbox{Card}\,({\mathbb{Z}}/2^{k}Z)^{\times}\,\mbox{Card}\,({\mathbb{Z}}/n^{\prime}Z)^{\times}

\displaystyle=2\,\mbox{Card}\,({\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}

as required. This completes our proof of the lemma. ∎

It remains to compute
GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}).

Lemma 9
.

GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2^{k-1}{\mathbb{Z}}\,
for each
k\geq 1.

Proof.

Recall that for
k\geq 3,

({\mathbb{Z}}/2^{k}{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2^{k-2}{\mathbb{Z}}
(a result due to Gauss) and is generated by
2^{k}-1
(of order 2) and
3
of order
2^{k-2}
(see for example
[
4
]
). In particular, the order of
({\mathbb{Z}}/2^{k}{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}
is
2^{k-1}
and hence the order of
GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})=2^{k}.
We begin by proving the lemma for
k=1,2.
For
k=1
we have
GC_{2}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})=\{a_{1},b_{1}\}
and is hence isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
as required. For
k=2
we have
GC_{4}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})=\{a_{1},b_{1},a_{3},b_{3}\}
and since
GC_{4}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
is not cyclic, it follows that
GC_{4}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
as required. Assume henceforth that
k\geq 3.
We will show that
GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2^{k-1}{\mathbb{Z}}
and generated by the elements
a_{2^{k}-1}
of order
2
and
a_{3}
of order
2^{k-1}.

We begin by showing (by induction on
k)
that for each
k\geq 3
we have
3^{2^{k-2}}=q_{k}2^{k}+1
with
q_{k}
odd. If
k=3,
then
3^{2}=1\cdot 2^{3}+1
so
q_{3}=1.
Now assume
q_{k}=\frac{3^{2^{k-2}}-1}{2^{k}}
is odd and let’s show that

q_{k+1}
is odd. Writing

q_{k+1}=\frac{3^{2^{k-1}}-1}{2^{k+1}}=\frac{(3^{2^{k-2}}-1)}{2^{k}}\cdot\frac{(3^{2^{k-2}}+1)}{2}.

Now the first factor on the right hand side is odd by induction hypothesis. As for the second factor, we have

\frac{3^{2^{k-2}}+1}{2}=\frac{q_{k}2^{k}+1+1}{2}=\frac{q_{k}2^{k}+2}{2}=q_{k}2^{k-1}+1\equiv 1\bmod 2

as required.
It follows from (
6
) that

(a_{3})^{2^{k-2}}=b_{1},
and hence
a_{3}
is of order
2^{k-1}
in
GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
which is half the cardinality of
GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}).
Since
GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
is not cyclic, there can be no element of higher order. Finally as noted earlier,
a_{2^{k}-1}\in GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
is of order
2
and furthermore is not contained in the subgroup
<a_{3}>
of
GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
generated by
a_{3},
for otherwise
2^{k}-1
would belong to the subgroup
<3>
of
({\mathbb{Z}}/2^{k}{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}
generated by
3,
a contradiction. Thus
<a_{3}>\,\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}/2^{k-1}{\mathbb{Z}}
and the quotient
GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})/<a_{3}>\,\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}.

Moreover, as
GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
contains an element of order
2
not in
<a_{3}>
(namely
a_{2^{k}-1}),
it follows that
GC_{2^{k}}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\,\,\times<a_{3}>\,\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2^{k-1}{\mathbb{Z}}.

∎

This completes our proof of Proposition
6
. ∎

We end with a few examples of representations of
({\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}
by Christoffel matrices in
GC_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
:

Example 2
.

Let
n=20.
Using the generators
3
and
19
of
({\mathbb{Z}}/20{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}
we construct different Christoffel representations of
({\mathbb{Z}}/20{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}
contained in
GC_{20}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}).
For example:

<a_{3},a_{19}>=\{a_{1},a_{3},b_{7},a_{9},a_{11},b_{13},a_{17},a_{19}\}

<a_{3},b_{19}>=\{a_{1},a_{3},b_{7},a_{9},b_{11},a_{13},b_{17},b_{19}\}

<b_{3},a_{19}>=\{a_{1},b_{3},a_{7},a_{9},a_{11},a_{13},b_{17},a_{19}\}

<b_{3},b_{19}>=\{a_{1},b_{3},a_{7},a_{9},b_{11},b_{13},a_{17},b_{19}\}

Notice that in each representation we have a Christoffel matrix of each type
m\in({\mathbb{Z}}/20{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}.

Example 3
.

Let
n=8.
Then
GC_{8}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})=\{a_{1},b_{1},a_{3},b_{3},a_{5},b_{5},a_{7},b_{7}\}
and is generated by
a_{3}
and
a_{7}.
Also,
({\mathbb{Z}}/8Z)^{\times},
which is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}},
is isomorphic to the subgroup
\{a_{1},b_{1},a_{7},b_{7}\}
and moreover this is the unique subgroup of
GC_{8}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
isomorphic to
({\mathbb{Z}}/8Z)^{\times}.
The corresponding Christoffel words of length
8
representing
({\mathbb{Z}}/8Z)^{\times}
are
\{10^{7},01^{7},1^{7}0,0^{7}1\}.
Notice only types
1
and
7
occur. In general, for
n\equiv 0\bmod 8
even, we obtain interesting Christoffel representations of
({\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}
over
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}.
By interesting we mean that the representation does not necessarily include a Christoffel matrix of each type
m\in({\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}
as in the case of
n=8.
