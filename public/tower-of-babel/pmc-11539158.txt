Elimination of lymphatic filariasis in Brazil: a public health milestone for citizenship
Authors: Carlos Dornels Freire de Souza, Rodrigo Feliciano do Carmo
Published: 2024

Although the fight against lymphatic filariasis in Brazil dates back to the early 20th century, it was not until the 1950s that systematic interventions were implemented based on the results of hemoscopic surveys conducted throughout the country.1 After more than 70 years, the country was finally certified as having eliminated the disease as a public health problem on September 30, 2024.2 The last confirmed case in Brazil was recorded in 2017 in the metropolitan region of Recife, in northeastern Brazil.

It has been widely documented that the maintenance of the transmission chain in the territory is associated with a higher degree of social vulnerability, which is why it has been included in the list of socially determined diseases (diseases that increase poverty and/or are increased by poverty). This issue has been adequately explored by Durrheim et al. in a text entitled “Lymphatic filariasis endemicity an indicator of poverty?”, in which the authors show that 94% of the countries with the lowest Human Development Index (HDI) are considered endemic for the disease.3

When faced with the juxtaposition of the concept of citizenship, which is an expression of the exercise of democracy and is materialised in the concretisation of civil, political and social rights, and the debilitating impact of lymphatic filariasis (whether physical, mental, or social), a clear conclusion emerges: individuals affected by this disease are hindered in their ability to fully engage in citizenship. In Brazil alone, between 1990 and 2021, data on the global burden of disease show a YLD (Years Lived with Disability) of 108,856.44.4 Thus, the elimination of the disease in Brazil is not only the achievement of an epidemiological goal but also an achievement for the citizenship of the Brazilian people. It is important to remember that elimination does not mean eradication” and, for this reason, filariasis cannot be forgotten–it is still necessary to remain vigilant.

Just as it is impossible to drive without rearview mirrors and windshields, it is equally true that Brazil must make significant progress in addressing other diseases with social determinants. Although it occurred more than a century after the first policies to combat the disease, the achievement of eliminating lymphatic filariasis can serve as an inspiration for health authorities in Brazil and around the world. Finally, we can affirm that all the effort was well worth it.
