Radiomics-based machine learning models for predicting genomic alterations in metastatic prostate cancer using PSMA PET imaging: a pilot study
Authors: Anna Scavuzzo, Giovanni Pasini, Osvaldo G. Perez, Miguel A. Jimenez Rios, Marina Arenas Hernández, Roberto Pedrero-Piedras, Maria Delia Pérez Montiel, Nora Sobrevilla Moreno, Giorgio Russo, Alessandro Stefano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and objectiveGenomic characterization of metastatic prostate cancer (mPCa) plays a pivotal role in guiding precision oncology. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of combining radiomics and clinical data within a machine learning (ML) framework to non-invasively predict key genomic mutations in patients with mPCa undergoing PSMA PET imaging.MethodsA retrospective cohort of 14 mPCa patients who underwent [18 F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT was analysed. Prostate and metastatic lesions were segmented, and radiomics features were extracted. Somatic genomic alterations were obtained from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue samples using FoundationOne CDx testing. Six ML algorithms - Discriminant Analysis, Support Vector Machines, K-Nearest Neighbours, Neural Networks, Random Forest, and Boosting - were trained using a 5-times repeated pipeline with 80/20 train/test split, LASSO feature selection, and 5-fold cross-validation. Model performance was assessed using accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F-score.Key findingsFourteen patients with mPCa were included, and 46 lesions were analysed. Genomic alterations included mutations in TP53, TMPRSS2, PTEN, BRCA1/2, ATM, and others. Owing to data limitations, mutations other than TP53, TMPRSS2, and PTEN were grouped into a composite “OTHER” category. The best-performing clinical-radiomics ML models achieved AUCs of 91.11% (TP53), 84.44% (TMPRSS2), 80.00% (PTEN), and 77.78% (OTHER). Selected feature stability was consistent across repeated runs.Conclusions and clinical implicationsClinical-radiomics ML models based on PSMA PET imaging show promising accuracy in predicting actionable genomic alterations in mPCa. These findings support further investigation into radiogenomics modelling as a complementary, non-invasive tool to inform molecular profiling and treatment stratification.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41824-025-00280-6.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) remains one of the most prevalent malignancies in men worldwide, representing a significant cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality. Among its advanced forms, metastatic PCa (mPCa) poses considerable challenges in diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic management due to its biological heterogeneity and complex mutational landscape. In recent years, the integration of molecular imaging and computational analysis has opened new perspectives for personalized oncology (Bauckneht et al. 1007). Radiomics, a methodology that enables the high-throughput extraction of quantitative features from medical images, has demonstrated its ability to capture intra-tumoral heterogeneity and provide imaging biomarkers with potential prognostic and predictive value (Stefano 2024; Gillies et al. 2016).

Simultaneously, the field of radiogenomics has emerged as a promising frontier, aiming to link imaging phenotypes with genomic signatures. The goal is to develop non-invasive tools that can predict molecular alterations and stratify patients based on their tumour biology, thereby guiding clinical decision-making without the need for repeated biopsies (Sala et al. 2017; Shui et al. 2020). In the context of PCa, radiogenomics approaches have the potential to offer critical insights into the biological behaviour of primary and metastatic lesions, particularly by leveraging advanced imaging modalities such as prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography (PET-PSMA), which has become a cornerstone in PCa imaging due to its high sensitivity and specificity (Evangelista et al. 2022).

Despite the increasing use of radiomics and radiogenomics in various cancer types, their application in mPCa is still in its infancy. Several studies have shown associations between radiomics features and genomic alterations in primary PCa (Ferro et al. 2021; Stoyanova et al. 2016), yet evidence remains limited regarding their role in the metastatic setting. Furthermore, the integration of clinical data, radiomics features, and machine learning (ML) algorithms represents a powerful strategy to develop predictive models capable of anticipating genomic alterations such as TP53, PTEN, TMPRSS2, and others -mutations frequently associated with aggressiveness, resistance to treatment, and prognosis in mPCa (Mateo et al. 2015; Designed Research, 2019; Robinson et al. 2015).

In this study, we aim to investigate the potential of combining radiomics features extracted from 18F-PSMA1007 PET/CT images and clinical data to develop predictive models for somatic genomic mutations in patients with mPCa. A robust ML pipeline was implemented, including k-fold, feature selection and model optimization, to assess the feasibility of non-invasively identifying key genomic profiles. Such an approach may offer substantial clinical value by supporting genomic characterization when tissue sampling is not feasible or when rapid decision-making is required. Ultimately, this work aligns with the broader effort to integrate artificial intelligence (AI) into precision oncology, contributing to the clinical translation of radiogenomics. By demonstrating how imaging-derived data can serve as a surrogate for genetic information, this study could pave the way for new diagnostic paradigms and individualized therapeutic pathways in mPCa.

Patients and methods

Patient cohort and imaging acquisition

This retrospective pilot study included 14 patients diagnosed with mPCa, who underwent [18 F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT imaging. All patients were selected based on the availability of both high-quality imaging data and corresponding histopathological and genomic information. PET/CT scans were acquired following standard institutional imaging protocols, ensuring consistency across the dataset. Patients were stratified according to number of metastases (high volume -defined as the presence of visceral metastases or ≥ 4 bone lesions with ≥1 beyond the vertebral bodies and pelvis- vs. low volume). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the National Cancer Institution-Mexico City research committee (IRB number:0569/2023) and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments.

Image segmentation and radiomics feature extraction

Radiomics feature extraction was performed using LifeX (Nioche et al. 2018) a validated tool compliant with the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI). The prostate gland and metastatic bone lesions were manually segmented by experienced nuclear medicine physicians, for a total of 46 lesions. Image segmentation was performed blinded to genomic data. Segmentations were conducted to capture metabolic characteristics. For each volume of interest (VOI), a comprehensive set of 86 quantitative radiomics features was extracted, including first-order statistics and shape descriptors. Prior to feature extraction, standard preprocessing steps such as resampling to isotropic voxel size, intensity discretization to 64 bins, and grey-level normalization were applied, as recommended by IBSI guidelines.

Genomic data collection and processing

Genomic profiling was performed using Formalin-Fixed, Paraffin-Embedded tumour prostate tissue sample, which were analysed with the FoundationOne CDx assay. This test allowed the identification of somatic alterations across a wide panel of cancer-related genes, as well as genomic signatures including microsatellite instability (MSI), tumour mutational burden (TMB), and loss of heterozygosity. Among the mutations detected were: PIK3CA, PTEN, SOX2, PRKCI, TERC, TMPRSS2, CDKN2A, ATM, BRCA1, BRCA2, RNF 43, MAP2K1, CTNNB1, TERC, AKT1, STAG2, TP53, SPEN, SPOP, FLCN, SRC. To address the low frequency of several genomic alterations, mutations other than TP53, TMPRSS2, and PTEN were grouped into a composite ‘OTHER’ category for analysis purposes. Microsatellite stability and low tumour mutational burden were reported in all patients.

Machine learning workflow

A robust ML pipeline was implemented to assess the predictive value of four clinical characteristics (i.e. Gleason Score, PSA, International Society of Urological Pathology – ISUP - and Tumour Mutational Burden -TMB) and 86 radiomics features in identifying specific genomic mutations using matRadiomics platform (Pasini et al. 2022). Six different ML algorithms were tested: Discriminant Analysis (DA), Support Vector Machines (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbours (KNN), Neural Networks (NN), Random Forest (RF), and Boosting Algorithms (specifically AdaBoostM1, referred to as Boost). The model development process included a five-times repeated holdout strategy, where data were randomly split into 80% training and 20% testing sets. Within each training set, feature selection was performed using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) to reduce feature dimensionality and avoid overfitting. Subsequently, 5-fold cross-validation was used on the training data for hyperparameter tuning and model optimization. Feature selection (LASSO), hyperparameter tuning, and model fitting were performed exclusively within the training folds; the test data were held out and never used during model selection to prevent data leakage. Lesion volume was not included as a covariate in the present models; future studies will adjust for or stratify by volume to disentangle size-related effects from intrinsic radiomic signal.

Model evaluation and performance metrics

Model performance was evaluated on the independent test set of each iteration. The following classification metrics were computed and averaged across repetitions: accuracy, Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC), Sensitivity (also called true positive rate -TPR), Specificity (also called true negative rate - TNR), Precision (also called Positive Predictive Value - PPV), and F1-Score. These metrics provided a comprehensive overview of the discriminative power and robustness of each ML model. Additionally, the stability of the selected radiomics features was assessed across different repetitions to evaluate the consistency of feature selection, as described in (Pasini et al. 2024).

Results

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics

A total of 14 patients with mPCa were retrospectively included in this study. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age at diagnosis was 57.7 years (range: 47–62 years), and all patients were of Mexican origin. A history of radical prostatectomy was reported in 5 patients (28%). At the time of imaging, 8 patients (57%) were classified as having low-volume metastatic disease, while 6 patients (42%) presented with high-volume metastatic disease. The most common metastatic sites analysed were bone (95%) and lymph node (5%). The Gleason score had a median value of 9 (range: 7–9), indicating aggressive disease in most cases. The prostate-specific antigen (PSA) level at diagnosis showed a median value of 991.8 ng/mL, with a wide range (9.2–7414 ng/mL), reflecting significant inter-patient variability in tumour burden. Regarding disease staging, 90% of patients were diagnosed with metastatic prostate cancer, while 10% presented with locally advanced disease at the time of FoundationOne CDx genomic testing. As for therapeutic background, all patients (100%) received androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), including LHRH analogues, enzalutamide, or abiraterone. The RF model was the most stable, with a narrower CI width of 15 points. High performance was also observed with DA and SVM classifiers, all showing AUC values above 90%. It is important to note that several radiomics features—particularly shape-based and texture descriptors—may be influenced by lesion volume. In this study, we did not assess correlations between lesion size and selected features, nor did we adjust the models for volume. This represents a limitation, as some radiomics signatures may reflect size-related effects rather than intrinsic tumour biology. Future studies should include volume as a covariate or perform stratified analyses to account for this potential confounding factor. Hyperparameters selected for each training split of the AdaBoostM1 algorithm are provided in the supplementary material (Tables S1–S12).

Discussion

Our findings show the feasibility and potential of combining radiomics and clinical features to predict key genomic mutations in mPCa patients undergoing PSMA PET/CT imaging. The high performance obtained in TP53 and TMPRSS2 prediction supports the hypothesis that specific radiomics patterns correlate with underlying tumour biology and genetic alterations. Notably, models showed excellent generalization capability, especially for TP53 prediction, with consistently high AUC and F1-score across classifiers.

To our knowledge, this is the first study investigating the specific correlation between TP53 mutations and PET-based radiomics features in PCa. Nevertheless, preliminary studies suggest a potential link between aggressive tumour behaviour - often associated with TP53 alterations - and metabolic heterogeneity observable on PET imaging. Specifically, the TP53 gene is known to be a tumour suppressor, and its mutations are often associated with more aggressive forms of PCa (Maxwell et al. 2022). Although not directly related to radiomics features, this implies that TP53 mutations could influence tumour heterogeneity and, consequently, features detectable by PET imaging. In a recent study based on multiparametric MRI, results showed that TP53 mutations were associated with prognosis and treatment response in PCa. This study involved 388 PCa patients from two independent centres suggesting that MRI radiomics analysis could support the identification of patients carrying TP53 mutations and guide personalized treatment strategies (Chen et al. 2024).

In our study, the results shown in section “Model performance for genomic mutation prediction” strongly support a mutation-specific strategy. Clinical data alone are sufficient and optimal for predicting TP53 and “Other” mutations, while a combined radiomics-clinical model is necessary to obtain the best results for the more complex PTEN and TMPRSS2 mutations. Among the tested ML algorithms, Boosting consistently outperformed other models, likely due to its ability to handle complex, nonlinear feature interactions and ensemble learning capabilities. Importantly, the selection frequency of features across repetitions highlights the robustness and reproducibility of certain radiomics biomarkers, particularly histogram-based intensity descriptors.

The lower performance for PTEN and OTHER mutations may reflect the heterogeneity of these mutations and the limited sample size, which introduces variability and hinders model generalizability. Furthermore, grouping multiple rare mutations into a single category (OTHER) likely diluted the distinctive radiomics signatures. While this aggregation facilitated model training, it may have masked biologically significant differences and reduced interpretability. Future studies should aim to analyse these mutations individually or in biologically consistent subgroups.

Another relevant finding is the contribution of clinical features (e.g., PSA, Gleason Score, TMB), which were frequently selected and contributed significantly to classification performance. This supports the rationale for integrated clinical-radiomics models rather than radiomics-only approaches. Specifically, the frequent selection of features such as PSA, TMB, and entropy suggests a potential link between radiomics descriptors and underlying tumour biology. PSA levels and TMB are established clinical biomarkers that correlate with tumour burden and genomic instability, respectively. Entropy, a measure of image heterogeneity, may reflect intertumoral complexity often associated with TP53 mutations. Their consistent selection across models supports their relevance in capturing biologically meaningful patterns. Although TMB values were uniformly low (0–4), TMB may still contribute complementary signal when combined with radiomics and clinical variables, potentially capturing subtle variability in genomic instability.

The main limitation of this study is the small sample size, being a pilot study, which might impact the statistical power and model generalizability. Indeed, given the limited sample size of only 14 patients and 46 lesions, the statistical power of this study is limited, which could impact the generalizability of the results. Although robust machine learning techniques such as LASSO feature selection and repeated cross-validation were employed to mitigate overfitting, the risk remains due to the high dimensionality of the radiomics data relative to the sample size. Future studies should incorporate larger, externally validated datasets to confirm the reproducibility and robustness of these findings. As this is a pilot study, the scope was intentionally limited to feasibility assessment. Therefore, we did not explore alternative modelling strategies such as Bayesian methods or gradient boosting frameworks, which may be considered in future work once larger datasets become available.

Another limitation of this study is that multiple lesions from the same patient were treated as independent samples. This approach may violate the assumption of independence and artificially inflate the effective sample size, potentially biasing performance estimates. We acknowledge this as an inherent constraint of our pilot design, which aimed primarily to assess feasibility rather than to develop definitive predictive models. Given the small cohort, implementing hierarchical or mixed-effects models, or adopting leave-one-patient-out cross-validation, was not feasible without further reducing statistical power. Nevertheless, we recognize the importance of accounting for intra-patient correlation to improve generalizability. Future studies should incorporate multi-level modelling or patient-level validation strategies, supported by larger, multicentre datasets, to ensure robust and unbiased performance evaluation. In addition, the reproducibility of radiomics features across different segmentations or imaging conditions was not formally assessed. Since manual segmentation can be operator-dependent, future studies should include inter-observer variability analyses and explore automated or semi-automated segmentation methods to improve consistency and reproducibility

Radiogenomics with use PET PSMA imaging seems promising and bewitching methods to predict genomic profile; creating imaging biomarkers might think to replace expensive genetic testing. Given the ongoing challenge associated with genetic testing (elevated cost, invasive procedures and extended turnaround times), its routine use across diverse patient populations remains limited. Radiogenomics has emerged as a compelling non-invasive alternative, enabling the extraction of quantitative imaging biomarkers that exhibit strong correlation with genomic expression patterns. These imaging derived surrogates hold significant potential for predicting therapeutic response, identifying early metastatic risk and guiding precision oncology through individualized treatment stratification (Thenault et al. 2021; Lo Gullo et al. 2019).

In future work, larger multicentric datasets and external validation cohorts will be crucial to confirm these findings and refine the radiogenomics models, possibly enabling non-invasive genomic profiling to support precision oncology strategies in prostate cancer management. While this study demonstrates the feasibility of non-invasive genomic prediction using PSMA PET radiomics, future research should define how such predictions could inform clinical decision-making compared to standard sequencing-based approaches. Prospective studies should evaluate the cost-effectiveness, clinical impact, and integration of radiogenomics models into treatment pathways, especially in settings where tissue sampling is limited or rapid stratification is needed. Previous research has demonstrated that specific histopathological and morphological features can modulate PSMA expression. For instance, ERG positivity has been associated with lower PSMA uptake on [68 Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 PET imaging (Rupp et al. 2024). Infiltrative growth patterns are also linked to reduced PSMA uptake and less diffusion restriction on mpMRI (Laudicella et al. 2022). These findings underscore the importance of integrating histopathological context into radiogenomics analyses to enhance interpretability and biological relevance. Additionally, deep learning models applied to multiparametric MRI have demonstrated the ability to non-invasively predict PSMA-positive prostatic volumes (Laudicella et al. 2024). These complementary imaging approaches suggest that integrating PET/CT and MRI radiomics could provide a more comprehensive assessment of tumour biology and improve the accuracy of genomic prediction models.

Conclusion

As a pilot study, this work is inherently exploratory in nature. The limited sample size and retrospective design serve as a basis for hypothesis generation rather than definitive conclusions. The primary objective was to assess the feasibility of integrating radiomics and clinical data for genomic prediction in mPCa within a limited exploratory framework. The study was not designed to test or compare alternative modelling strategies, which will be explored in future research with larger cohorts.
