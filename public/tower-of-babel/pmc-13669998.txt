Experiences of receiving and delivering a novel complex intervention for type 1 diabetes and disordered eating: Process evaluation of the Safe management of people with Type 1 diabetes and EAting Disorders studY (STEADY)
Authors: Natalie Zaremba, Amy Harrison, Jennie Brown, Divina Pillay, Emmanouela Konstantara, Janet Treasure, David Hopkins, Judith Prins, Bastiaan de Galan, Khalida Ismail, Giesje Nefs, Marietta Stadler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThe Safe management of people with Type 1 diabetes and EAting Disorders studY (STEADY) intervention is a novel cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT) intervention for individuals with type 1 diabetes and disordered eating (T1DE). The aim of this mixed‐method process evaluation is to assess the acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility of STEADY, and explore experiences of both participants and healthcare professionals during therapy delivery.MethodsParticipants who received STEADY (n = 13) and healthcare professionals delivering STEADY therapy (n = 3) completed person‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) to assess the acceptability (AIM), appropriateness (IAM) and feasibility (FIM) of STEADY and took part in semi‐structured interviews to explore experiences, therapeutic processes and implementation considerations. Interviews were analysed using thematic analysis.ResultsPROM scores indicated high ratings across participants (AIM = 18.36 ± 2.01; IAM = 18.18 ± 2.64; FIM = 18.00 ± 2.32) and healthcare professionals (AIM = 17.67 ± 2.08; IAM = 17.33 ± 2.31; FIM = 17.67 ± 2.08), suggesting high likelihood of implementation success. Thematic analysis showed significant overlap in participant and therapist experiences. Key themes included integrated T1DE‐specific CBT delivery, specialist knowledge of T1DE and a strong therapeutic alliance. Both groups emphasised the need for tailored delivery, therapist expertise in T1DE and structural adaptations to standard CBT models. Participant's experiences also included a clear path of therapeutic growth and expectations of therapy. Therapists' experiences included considerations for clinical safeguarding and organisational factors impacting wider implementation.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that STEADY is acceptable, appropriate and feasible for individuals with mild to moderate severity T1DE. The substantial alignment between participant and therapist perspectives offers clear directions for refining the intervention and support implementation in a full‐scale RCT.

INTRODUCTION

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) and disordered eating (T1DE) is a common and serious problem, affecting diabetes management outcomes, acute and long‐term complications and psychosocial outcomes in people with diabetes. 1 , 2 Established interventions for eating disorders are not compatible with diabetes self‐management behaviours, creating challenges for people with T1DE seeking treatment and healthcare professionals (HCPs) delivering care. 3 , 4 The Safe management of people with T1D and EAting Disorders studY (STEADY) is a novel complex intervention to treat T1D and T1DE. Following the development of theoretical models developed from qualitative interviews and blog analyses, 5 , 6 as well as an understanding of challenges faced by HCPs treating people with T1DE, 3 we used Experience‐Based Co‐Design, a participatory action design process bringing together people with lived experience and healthcare professionals to develop STEADY using a cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT) framework integrating diabetes education. 7 , 8 The resulting STEADY ‘toolkit’ is an adaptable and personalised intervention that suits a wide range of T1DE presentations. The STEADY intervention was tested in a feasibility randomised controlled trial (RCT), with the intervention group receiving 12 sessions of the newly developed T1DE‐specific CBT. The feasibility RCT showed proof of concept for feasibility with high intervention completion rates, as well as improvements in self‐reported mental health scores. 9 , 10

STEADY was developed using the Medical Research Council (MRC) Complex Intervention Framework, which highlights the importance of the process evaluation. 7 , 11 , 12 Process evaluations allow researchers and regulators to understand how the context and mechanisms of action influence outcomes and implementation of an intervention. 12 , 13 This can optimise evaluation and refinement of the intervention at the feasibility and piloting stage. 11 , 13 Within this context, the present mixed‐methods study of participant and therapist experiences explores these factors and complements the recently published quantitative evaluation of therapy delivery and audit of therapeutic tools. 14 This will provide a greater understanding of the factors influencing the engagement, delivery and outcomes of the STEADY feasibility RCT, identify barriers and facilitators to wider implementation and inform future refinement of STEADY.

The aim of the mixed‐methods STEADY process evaluation was twofold: first, to quantitatively evaluate the feasibility, acceptability and appropriateness of STEADY, and second, to understand the experiences of receiving and delivering therapy through semi‐structured interviews with participants in the STEADY trial and HCPs delivering therapy.

METHODS

This study received ethical approval from the East of England‐Essex research ethics committee (21/EE/0235). All participants provided written informed consent.

Eligibility

All participants in the STEADY intervention arm were invited to take part in the process evaluation (up to N = 20). HCPs involved in the delivery and supervision of therapy and psychological assessment of participants were invited to take part in the process evaluation (N = 3; one diabetes specialist nurse [DSN] delivering therapy, one clinical psychologist delivering and supervising therapy and conducting psychological assessments, one psychiatrist supervising therapy and conducting psychiatric assessments). The flow of participants through the main STEADY RCT and process evaluation sub‐study is presented in Figure 1. Individuals eligible for the STEADY trial but who declined to take part were eligible for the process evaluation (up to N = 5) 9 ; however, no individuals in this category consented to the sub‐study.

Participant flow diagram of people receiving STEADY therapy (a) and healthcare professionals delivering therapy (b).

Person‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) and quantitative analysis

Three validated PROMs were used to quantitatively measure the acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility of STEADY therapy: the Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM) and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM). 15 Mean scores and standard deviation were calculated. Possible scores for each PROM range from 4 to 20, with scores above 16 indicating a high likelihood of implementation success in each domain. 15

Semi‐structured interviews and qualitative analysis

Semi‐structured interview guides were developed iteratively to focus on the perception of the STEADY therapy, experience of receiving therapy, experience of delivering therapy and practical and structural changes for future iterations of STEADY. The interview schedule for participants who received STEADY included questions on topics of: their initial perceptions of the STEADY intervention, engaging with the therapy content, experiences using the STEADY tools, using STEADY in daily life and the STEADY app.

The interview schedule for HCPs delivering STEADY included questions on the topics of: overall perceptions of the STEADY intervention, delivery of therapy, content and structure of therapy and tools, the STEADY app and clinician platform and future of STEADY and wider implementation.

Interviews were conducted by NZ in‐person or via videocall after the final study visit in the main STEADY trial (STEADY intervention group), and after all participants had completed the study (HCP participants). Interviews lasted approximately 1 h and were audio recorded, transcribed and pseudonymised, so both participant and therapist details were not identifiable without the key file.

Reflexive thematic analysis was conducted in an iterative process. A data‐driven, inductive approach was used initially to capture the experiential aspects and contextual factors, allowing new themes to emerge from the data. A deductive approach was used to collect specific feedback on practical or structural elements. Initial coding was conducted, and themes and subthemes were developed by NZ. The thematic framework was reviewed and revised iteratively with members of the STEADY research team (NZ [mixed‐methods researcher in diabetes and psychology, medical psychology PhD candidate], MS [STEADY chief investigator, clinical academic diabetologist], AH [clinical academic psychologist in eating disorders]) and reviewed and revised further by multidisciplinary researchers and clinicians external to STEADY (GN [clinical academic psychologist specialised in diabetes, mixed‐method expertise], JP [clinical academic psychologist, intervention evaluation expertise] and BdG [clinical academic internist, diabetes expertise]). Researcher reflexivity was an integral part of these discussions, with clinical experiences and interpretations from both diabetologists and psychologists helping to refine codes for both the experiences of participants who received STEADY, as well as the experiences of HCPs and the implementation considerations, from a multi‐country and multidisciplinary perspective. Researchers with theoretical and mixed‐methods expertise helped to shape and structure, refine and reorganise the themes and subthemes. The other authors, mainly clinicians involved in the care of participants and researchers involved in the wider development of STEADY, critically read the ensuing written narrative.

RESULTS

Participants who took part in the STEADY intervention

Descriptives

Thirteen people (12 women, one man, mean ± SD age 40 ± 11 years; diabetes duration 20 ± 13 years; baseline HbA1c 70.8 ± 27.5 mmol/mol/8.6 ± 2.5%; therapy sessions attended 11.7 ± 0.9) who took part in the STEADY intervention group participated in the process evaluation sub‐study interviews. Eleven participants completed the PROMs. Seven participants received STEADY therapy but did not participate in the process evaluation due to withdrawal from the main trial (n = 4) or not consenting to the sub‐study (n = 3).

PROM results

The mean (±SD, min–max, range) PROM scores indicate high acceptability (AIM: 18.36 ± 2.01, 14–20), appropriateness (IAM: 18.18 ± 2.64, 12–20) and feasibility (FIM: 18.00 ± 2.32, 14–20).

Interview themes were organised into two overarching categories: Experiences during STEADY therapy, and Implementation and structural considerations.

Therapeutic growth

Participants had a clear narrative of their path through therapeutic growth (Figure 2a; Table 1a). Participants gained understanding of their problems, where they felt ‘stuck’ and learned about ‘vicious cycles’ throughout therapy. They developed new skills to address their problems and T1DE behaviours (e.g. by challenging unhelpful beliefs). Over time, participants experienced transformative impacts, giving examples of improvements in self‐perception, self‐compassion and resilience. Particularly with diabetes self‐management, participants expressed more confidence and ability to deal with blood glucose fluctuations more effectively, as they received important diabetes education in addition to emotional skills.

Integrated T1DE approach

The integration of T1D and eating disorder specialisms facilitated the therapeutic growth described above, addressing an unmet need in routine services (Figure 2b; Table 1b). CBT‐trained DSN‐delivered therapy allowed participants to use diabetes data therapeutically (e.g. reviewing continuous glucose monitor [CGM] data, making connections with T1DE symptoms), supported diabetes management decisions and provided diabetes education in areas like using diabetes metrics to have a more realistic versus perfectionistic understanding of diabetes management. Some participants described addressing their issues more quickly than in previous therapies due to their therapist's specialist knowledge of T1DE and focus on aspects of therapy that were most meaningful and relevant to them.

Therapeutic relationship

Within the therapeutic relationship, establishing trust was a priority for participants to feel comfortable in therapy, which took time to develop (Figure 2c; Table 1c). For most participants, their therapists' in‐depth understanding of T1DE's diverse presentations and physical and emotional impacts meant that participants felt understood. If a participant felt that trust had been broken or strained with their therapist, it deeply impacted whether they felt safe sharing their experiences or problems and took time to repair. The collaborative approach helped participants feel supported throughout the challenges of therapy and made gradual changes they believed were sustainable. Participants felt their therapist genuinely cared about them, wanted to understand their problems and help and support them in their recovery.

Expectations of therapy

While some participants were familiar with the structure of CBT and welcomed flexibility, others felt less prepared and preferred more explicit rules and pre‐defined structure (Figure 2d; Table 1d). Some participants expected therapy to be less intensive in a research study and were surprised at the depth of STEADY and the progress made in 12 sessions. Clear communication with therapists was important, particularly when participants may have agreed routines or behaviours with their usual care teams that did not align with safety protocols within a trial setting. Emotionally, some participants found the vulnerability of therapy challenging and harder work than expected. Personal circumstances could facilitate or create barriers to engagement, and at times, participants felt unsure of therapy or felt the therapist's time could be used for people with more important problems. Many participants continued to face challenges at the end of therapy and wished for ongoing sessions.

Flexible delivery

Participants felt the amount of flexibility in session scheduling was a facilitator to making therapeutic changes, accommodating the demands of their daily lives (e.g. studies, life events) and the therapeutic context of their sessions, allowing participants to feel supported for a longer duration. Virtual delivery helped fit therapy into participants' routines without booking time off work, childcare, or travel. While several participants felt that 12 sessions were sufficient, most felt that more would be needed to sustain change due to the depth of their problems and the lifelong nature of living with T1D.

Toolkit elements

Participants consistently described CBT tools like behaviour experiments, thought diaries and ‘SMART’ goals (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time‐bound) as important exercises that facilitated change and could be used in and outside of sessions. Strategies to manage difficult emotions, perfectionism, understanding personal values and self‐compassion were key psychoeducational tools. Diabetes education helped participants understand more about diabetes and its impact, including strategies for managing hypoglycaemia and navigating time in range.

Active practice

Conducting active practice between sessions in a structured manner was seen as helpful and engaging for those able to do so; however, some participants found completing exercises between sessions difficult, given the time demands of their daily lives or were unable to complete exercises outside of sessions. Reflection between sessions was common, with some participants using specific prompts, while others used an unstructured approach to reflection or completed structured therapy exercises within sessions.

STEADY app

Most participants downloaded the STEADY app when beginning therapy, but most preferred using other formats for their therapy content, for example, email, writing on paper or in their phone's notes app. The app library was a useful function to have STEADY content in one place and to have a dedicated space to write thought diaries. Suggestions for improving the app included customisable features and notifications, developing the app as a tool for long‐term support and optional linkage with usual care teams.

Lastly, Additions to future content included suggestions such as collaboration with other HCPs or supportive professionals, linking with people in participants' daily life, such as flatmates or partners for additional support, and adding additional educational content, for example, managing T1D during the menstrual cycle and the dawn phenomenon.

HCPs involved in the delivery of therapy and psychological assessment

All eligible HCPs took part in semi‐structured interviews and completed the PROMs.

PROM results

Mean (±SD, min–max range) PROM scores indicate high acceptability (AIM: 17.67 ± 0.2.08. 16–20), appropriateness (IAM: 17.33 ± 2.31, 16–20) and feasibility (FIM: 17.67 ± 2.08, 16–20).

Bespoke therapy

The therapeutic tools were highly versatile, engaging and adaptable to participants' specific presentations and therapy needs, particularly core CBT tools (e.g. behaviour experiments, formulation), emotional regulation techniques and diabetes education (Figure 4a; Table 3a). Behaviour experiments offered a non‐prescriptive approach that gave participants a template for future use in daily life. A focus on meaningful, qualitative goals was found to be more acceptable than a numbered rating scale or strict numerical goals, as it allowed for more flexible thinking and reduced perfectionist or all‐or‐nothing thinking.

Participant engagement with therapy

The therapeutic alliance was an important relational factor in how HCPs understood participants' engagement in therapy (Figure 4b; Table 3b). Building trust over time required consistently proving to participants that they understood the complexity of their unique problems and presentation of T1DE, had the specialist skills and knowledge to address them and compassion for their experiences. Therapist fit was important, with each therapist having distinct experiences and clinical skills to support participants with a specific need. Factors such as comorbidity, traumatic events or ambivalence to therapy meant therapists spent more time preparing participants for therapy and developing the therapeutic formulation, with fewer sessions available for active therapy. Physical wellness impacted engagement and the therapeutic agenda when a participant was unwell or had high glucose during sessions, prioritising managing ketones or participant safety over active therapy. Personal environment, such as workplace flexibility or caring responsibilities, could facilitate or create barriers to engagement with therapy, in addition to life events and disruptions to usual routines and self‐care.

Therapeutic skills

Specialist knowledge of T1DE, in particular diabetes‐specific skills (e.g. understanding sensor data, how diabetes impacts the body, managing insulin changes), informed the therapist's understanding of participants' experiences and guided their approach to therapy (Figure 4c; Table 3c). Clinical judgement was a key requirement for managing medical risk, as well as guiding appropriate changes in diabetes management. Similarly, therapist flexibility was required to be able to manage changing participant needs over the course of therapy or to set shared and sustainable priorities. Cognitive‐behavioural principles were foundational skills therapists used to guide participants throughout therapy, understand the function of their behaviours and build participants' skills in challenging unhelpful thinking and behaviours and building a more compassionate view of self.

Organisational factors

Delivering bespoke STEADY therapy within a research trial presented challenges with randomisation timelines and funding deadlines influencing the pacing of therapy and staff capacity within project timelines (Figure 4d; Table 3d). The clinical judgement and flexibility required to deliver a complex intervention in a cohort with a wide range of T1DE presentations is in opposition to typically rigid and standardised RCT protocols, making standardisation between participants difficult. Twelve sessions are considered a standard course for CBT, but are likely insufficient for people with more clinical complexity in T1DE. The range of therapeutic skills within the multidisciplinary STEADY team was valuable, with the DSN and clinical psychologist able to assess participant needs and consider which therapist was best placed to deliver therapy, and access additional support through weekly meetings with the wider study team. The therapists were able to share skills and provide mutual clinical supervision for the participants under their care, which led to therapists feeling supported and further developing their skills and understanding of T1DE.

Therapist support

Collaborating with participants' usual care teams to share progress and discuss helpful strategies offers an opportunity to support therapists and participants between sessions, after therapy end or times where more clinical contact is important to manage Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) or mental health risk (Figure 5a; Table 4). The STEADY multidisciplinary team has a role in ensuring there is adequate time and breadth of skills required for supervision, handover of participants from baseline to commencement of therapy and maintaining the capacity of therapy and supervisory staff. Assessment of participants at baseline is essential to ensure that T1DE‐specific CBT is an appropriate course of treatment, or whether the participant requires any other support or preparatory work prior to beginning active therapy. Additional support to reduce the administrative load on therapists would be helpful in the future to increase capacity for therapy delivery.

Adaptations from standard CBT therapy

The virtual delivery improved accessibility and allowed both therapists and participants to be flexible (Figure 5b; Table 4). To support virtual delivery, HCPs recommended closer communication with usual care teams to help manage acute risk, and maintaining in‐person baseline assessments with future participants. The complexity of T1DE presentations required flexible changes to the standard CBT approach. As participants' physical health at times required deviation from the standard CBT agenda, the 12‐session duration of therapy requires clinical discretion for participants who have more complex therapeutic needs. This could include preparatory work prior to active therapy, for example, to manage hyperglycaemia prior to sessions, engage the participant in feeling ready to make changes or seek treatment for additional comorbidities. Changes to diabetes care for people with elevated glucose levels must be done gradually to minimise the risk of complications, requiring a longer duration of therapy.

Training and HCP needs for wider implementation

Multidisciplinary team working was essential for therapists to adequately support participants, sharing skills and knowledge as well as clinical supervision, and would be a key consideration for delivering STEADY in the context of existing services (Figure 5c; Table 4). Given that participants may have complex mental or physical health comorbidities in addition to T1DE, a breadth of skills is necessary for appropriate management, and therapists suggested skills in therapeutic approaches beyond CBT may also be useful. HCPs acknowledged that this training is resource‐intensive, which may present barriers to wider implementation. In addition, HCPs delivering therapy require considerable knowledge of T1D and its physical and psychological impacts. HCPs noted that participants appreciated not needing to explain diabetes or its treatment and that a surface‐level knowledge was not sufficient to provide this type of intensive therapeutic support.

Additions and changes to the toolkit

A meaningful, non‐quantitative approach was emphasised, with a focus on behaviour experiments and curiosity over pre‐defined or prescriptive worksheets (Figure 5d; Table 4). Sessions around recovery and relapse management require distinction from standard CBT, as T1D is a lifelong condition and will present challenges beyond recovery from T1DE symptoms or behaviours. The STEADY app and clinician dashboard have the potential to be useful tools for therapists and participants, but require some adaptation, including notifications when sending messages, customisable features and integration with CGM data. The clinician dashboard also has the potential to be an efficient searchable library for therapy tools and education to improve ease of use during therapy sessions. HCPs recommended additional tools for development in future iterations of the toolkit, with topics including technology and emerging treatments, further resources on emotional regulation and embodiment of symptoms, general psychoeducation and family aspects.

DISCUSSION

STEADY participants and therapists reported overall positive experiences with the intervention, with high quantitative ratings of the acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility of the therapy and in their qualitative reports.

Participants' and HCPs' experiences were largely complementary, with similar understandings of how STEADY worked and key aspects important in its delivery. Key contextual factors influencing delivery included participants' personal circumstances, complexity of T1DE presentation and a need for a longer duration of therapy for some participants. Key mechanisms of intervention reported were the integrated T1DE approach and highly flexible, personalised CBT delivery, particularly specialist knowledge of T1DE and strong therapeutic alliance. Implementation was facilitated through flexible approaches tailored to participants' needs, multidisciplinary training and clinical supervision, with clinical discretion to deviate from standard CBT practices and timelines to support participants' needs and reduce the risk of diabetes complications. The considerable overlap in themes between participants and HCPs suggests a shared understanding of the intervention processes and impacts, reflecting high intervention fidelity and a model that resonates with both groups. Both gave specific feedback for future iterations of STEADY and implementation. Our findings align with the MRC's framework of context, mechanisms and implementation, and highlight the importance of evaluating interventions at the feasibility stage. 11 , 13 The consistency between perspectives in our study offers a strong message for future revisions of STEADY and strengthening delivery.

Our results contextualise the findings of the quantitative audit and evaluation of STEADY delivery. 14 The CBT formulation, behaviour experiments and thought diaries were consistently referred to by STEADY participants and therapists in our interviews and were the most frequently recorded tools by therapists in the quantitative audit. 14 Similarly, we also found that managing physical health risks may take priority over standard CBT practices, leading to changes from the set CBT agenda and standard practice in session, or changes to wider therapy structure, such as breaks from active therapy. 14

Key recommendations for wider clinical consideration emerged in this study. Multidisciplinary clinical supervision was a recurring factor to support therapists in managing the variety of complex presentations in STEADY. The DSN and clinical psychologist delivering therapy benefited from clinical supervisory support and were able to share skills and approach a wider range of T1DE‐specific problems. This is important for future practice to build in specific multidisciplinary supervision when treating people with T1DE and other complex comorbidities. Therapist fit is also a key consideration, as therapists' professional experiences were different and complementary in the skills they presented and the types of issues they felt best able to support. While STEADY was not powered to compare DSN‐ and clinical psychologist‐delivered therapy, future trials should explore how therapists with different backgrounds can optimally support people with T1DE alongside multidisciplinary supervision. Considering participant readiness and engaging in preparatory work before therapy can help individuals feel supported and begin CBT at an appropriate time. This may involve STEADY clinicians working to build trust, or usual care teams to better understand and support participants outside therapy. Preparatory work might also include adjustments to diabetes management or other therapies prior to STEADY CBT, and is a key area where usual care teams may be able to support recovery and readiness for future therapy.

HCPs acknowledged that the specialist T1DE training and additional resources may be difficult for teams to access, as most usual care settings do not have the level of specialist staff and capacity to deliver such intensive and flexible therapy. However, this process evaluation also highlights opportunities for HCPs to adapt existing practices to support people with T1DE in routine care. Multidisciplinary team working and effective communication between services, building trusting relationships with patients over time, making small sustainable changes and focusing on meaningful change are transferable approaches that may be useful for healthcare teams working with people with T1DE, diabetes distress or other challenges with diabetes self‐care, and other complex conditions. As these key factors have been highlighted by both people with T1DE as well as HCPs, they are important and highly implementable opportunities to improve care and highlight the importance of person‐centred approaches.

Participants emphasised the importance and novelty of the integrated T1DE approach to therapy. In previous studies, people with T1DE often reported falling between two services without integration and therefore received disjointed and conflicting care. 4 Integrated care also impacted the therapeutic relationship, reflecting literature within the wider population of people receiving therapeutic interventions for eating disorders. While there is mixed evidence on the impact of the therapeutic alliance in different eating disorder populations, 16 , 17 our research highlights factors that may influence the development of the therapeutic relationship in people with T1DE. Given the importance of T1DE integration and specialist knowledge in building trust between participants and therapists in our study, it is likely these factors influence the agreement of goals, tasks and quality of bond between therapist and individual, defining the therapeutic alliance. 18 These factors should be explored in future research and may apply to other interventions requiring specialised knowledge of T1D and other comorbidities.

A key strength of this study is the multidisciplinary team involved in the data analysis and interpretation. The varied clinical and research backgrounds helped to safeguard rigour in the analysis and interpretation of these results. Participant themes were also reviewed by the STEADY patient and public involvement and engagement (PPIE) group members and STEADY co‐design participants. Methodologically, the mixed‐method approach and inclusion of validated PROMs enable comparison to other novel interventions and future iterations of STEADY. Including perspectives from both participants and HCPs provided a holistic understanding of how the intervention works in practice.

A key limitation is that we were unable to recruit people with T1DE who were eligible for STEADY but declined participation in the RCT, as planned in the original protocol. 9 As a result, we do not have data on perceptions of STEADY from people who did not wish to take part. Based on PPIE group discussions and emails during the recruitment phase of STEADY, reasons individuals declined may be (1) not willing to be randomised, (2) past negative experiences with DSNs or diabetes specialists and (3) not ready to engage with therapy. Gaining insight from these individuals in the future could help refine STEADY and better meet the needs of people with T1DE.

The findings of this mixed‐methods process evaluation suggest that STEADY is acceptable, appropriate and feasible to be delivered. Our findings inform the context and mechanisms of therapeutic growth, and implementation considerations that support the further development, testing and future evaluation of the STEADY intervention in a large‐scale RCT.
