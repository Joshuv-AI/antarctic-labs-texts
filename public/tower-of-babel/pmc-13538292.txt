From Gut to Mind: Impact of Probiotics on Depression, Anxiety, Mood, Gut Microbiota, Sleep and Stress—A Systematic Review of RCTs
Authors: Monalisa Das, Nooruddin Thajuddin, Gangatharan Muralitharan, Megha Pundeer, Ramesh Ponnusamy, Ankita Mishra, Priyanka Nayak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProbiotic supplementation may influence mental health through the gut–brain axis, with potential effects on depression, anxiety, sleep, cognition, stress hormones, and gut microbial composition. This systematic review aimed to evaluate the effects of probiotics on psychological, physiological, and gut microbiome-related outcomes across diverse populations.SummaryA comprehensive search of PubMed, MEDLINE, PsycINFO, and ScienceDirect identified randomized controlled trials evaluating probiotic supplementation. Twenty RCTs were included, of which 15 were assessed as having a low risk of bias and five as having a high risk of bias. Commonly assessed outcomes included depression (n = 17), anxiety (n = 10), sleep quality (n = 9), stress (n = 6), cortisol (n = 7), cognitive function (n = 5), quality of life (n = 5), gut microbial composition (n = 11), and other neuroendocrine and inflammatory markers. Overall, probiotics were associated with improvements in depression, anxiety, sleep quality, mood, cognition, quality of life, and beneficial gut microbial populations, although findings for cortisol and stress-related outcomes were inconsistent.Key MessageProbiotic supplementation may provide beneficial effects on psychological well-being and gut microbial composition. However, heterogeneity among interventions, populations, outcome measures, and study quality warrants further well-designed RCTs.Keywords: Gut–brain axis, gut microbiota, depression, anxiety, psychological outcomes

Introduction

The activity of the gut microbiota is intricately connected to central nervous system (CNS) function. Through mechanisms such as neurogenesis, neurotransmission, microglial modulation and behavioural regulation, the gut transmits signals to the brain in both normal and stressful states. 1 The CNS communicates with gut microbiota through endocrine, neural and immune pathways, which modulate brain function, cognition and behaviour. 2 The gut microbiota also modulates the concentration of circulating serotonin, kynurenine, tryptophan and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). It also influences the blood–brain barrier, gut permeability and activation of peripheral immune cells. 3 The gut microbiota has been connected to age-related neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease and mood disorders such as anxiety and depression through recent discoveries of the gut–brain axis, which refers to a reciprocal relationship between the gut and the brain.4–8 The potential of probiotics, which are a component of a balanced diet, for regulating brain health through the microbiota–gut–brain axis has drawn more attention.7, 9 The administration of probiotics is one of the methods of modifying a disturbed microbiome imbalance (gut microbial dysbiosis). 10 Anxiety and depression symptoms can be brought on by pro-inflammatory stimuli. Remarkably, probiotics can raise anti-inflammatory cytokine levels, lower pro-inflammatory cytokine levels and oxidative stress, and regulate the immune system to suppress inflammation.11–15 There is additional evidence that suggests that the pathophysiology of human depression may involve gut bacteria, notably the inflammatory response caused by exposure to lipopolysaccharide.16, 17 Two conflicting assertions are presented by several studies; one states that probiotic supplements have no discernible effect on anxiety symptoms, while another states that probiotics lower anxiety symptoms.18, 19 According to a different study, giving individuals with major depressive disorder (MDD) probiotic supplements for 8 weeks considerably raised their glutathione (GSH), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) levels. 20 Probiotic and vitamin D co-supplementation for 12 weeks reduced several oxidative stress and inflammatory markers in patients with schizophrenia. 21 Studies on animals have demonstrated that probiotic use may alter brain function and lessen symptoms of anxiety or depression.22–24 For example, giving Lactobacillus plantarum PS128 to mice under stress in their early lives may shorten their immobility period in the forced swimming paradigm of depression. 22

This systematic review aims to understand the effectiveness of probiotic supplementation on various measurement scales of depression, anxiety, perceived stress, mood, gut microbiota population and inflammatory markers in adults using randomised controlled trials (RCTs).

Methods

Search and Study Selection

Two independent authors conducted a comprehensive literature search across PubMed, MEDLINE, PsycINFO and ScienceDirect databases for studies published in the last 10 years, applying the RCT filter. The authors constructed the literature search around the keywords (probiotics[MeSH Terms] OR probiotic*[Title/Abstract] OR psychobiotics[Title/Abstract]) AND(anxiety[MeSH Terms] OR depression[MeSH Terms] OR ‘anxiety disorders’[Title/Abstract] OR ‘depressive disorders’[Title/Abstract] OR stress[Title/Abstract] OR mood[Title/Abstract])AND (neurotransmitters[MeSH Terms] OR serotonin[Title/Abstract] OR GABA[Title/Abstract] OR dopamine[Title/Abstract] OR ‘brain-derived neurotrophic factor’[Title/Abstract] OR BDNF[Title/Abstract] OR ‘gut-brain axis’[Title/Abstract] OR ‘microbiota-gut-brain axis’[Title/Abstract]). A total of 227 randomised human trials were identified.

Selection Criteria

We included randomised human clinical trials published in the last decade. Studies that were published in languages other than English, had an age range of less than 20 years, had an intervention lasting less than 2 weeks, had a sample size of less than 20 or lacked depressive, cognitive and stress markers were all excluded. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 20 studies were selected. The PRISMA flow diagram of the study search flowchart is presented in Figure 1.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool was used to assess the RoB in the included RCTs, as only RCTs were considered in this review. The RoB 2 tool evaluates bias across five domains and categorises studies as having a low RoB, some concerns or a high RoB. 25

Various Biomarkers Included.

Healthy Athlete Group (Including Soccer Players): Quero et al. 45 conducted a triple-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled pilot trial in professional soccer players and sedentary students over 4 weeks. Participants received either a symbiotic supplement or a placebo. Results showed reduced cortisol and inflammation in athletes, while sedentary individuals had improved immune parameters. The study suggests that physical activity status influences symbiotic effects. 45

Healthy Pregnant Women: Slykerman et al. 34 performed a study in 380 pregnant women in New Zealand. Participants received Lactobacillus rhamnosus HN001 or a placebo from mid-pregnancy to 6 months postpartum. The probiotic group showed significantly lower postpartum depression and anxiety scores. This suggests that HN001 may help prevent maternal mood disorders. 34 Another study mentioned that Bifidobacterium longum supplementation reduces anxiety and depression during the perinatal period. 42

Diseased Population

Depressed Population: Out of the twenty studies, six were conducted specifically in participants with MDD or depressed populations. The remaining studies focused on other populations, such as healthy adults, pregnant women, older adults, athletes or patients with medical conditions.27–30,35,41 The details of the study are provided in Tables 1 and 2.

Patients with Inflammatory Bowel Disease: Out of the 20 RCTs, only two were conducted in participants with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS).32, 36 Martin et al. 32 conducted an RCT in IBS patients with psychological comorbidities. Probiotic supplementation led to improvements in anxiety and depression symptoms, along with beneficial metabolomic changes. 32 Another RCT was performed in patients with IBS and depression. Participants who received a Lactobacillus-containing cultured milk drink showed significant improvement in depressive symptoms compared with the placebo. 36

Patients with Chronic Kidney Disease: Climent et al. 39 carried out a randomised trial in haemodialysis patients receiving nutritional supplements with probiotics. The intervention improved psychological well-being and offered protective benefits compared to a placebo. 39

Patients with Alzheimer’s Dementia: Hsu et al. 44 conducted a 12-week study in patients with Alzheimer’s dementia. Probiotic supplementation improved cognitive function and was associated with favourable changes in BDNF levels, inflammation and oxidative stress biomarkers compared to controls. 44

Effects of Probiotic Supplementation on Various Biological Markers

Various Depression Rating Scales

Depression has drastically increased in recent years, and in this systematic review, we included 19 studies that used various depression measuring scales, including Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D17/HAM-D), Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS), Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS), Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) and Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale-Revised (CESD-R).

Hamilton Depression Rating Scale-Depression: The HAM-D17 or HDR is now one of the most popular measures for assessing the intensity of depressive symptoms and evaluating the effectiveness of treatment. 46 This systematic review included 20 RCTs, out of which 9 measured the HAM-D17 scale (n = 9). A significant reduction was observed in the probiotic group.28, 29, 32, 39 A few studies did not have a significant difference in the probiotic group.30, 33, 35 Both groups had low baseline scores. 34

Geriatric Depression Scale: One of the most popular depression self-reports available today is the GDS. Only one study included this scale (n = 1). The probiotic group showed reduced depressive symptoms. 26

Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale: MADRS is the world’s most extensively used depression scale in clinical and psychopharmacological depression research. 47 Only two studies included this scale (n = 2). The probiotic group experienced a significant decrease in MADRS values. 27 Both the probiotic and placebo groups showed a reduction in scores. 35

Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale: The primary purpose of the EPDS is to identify depression in expectant and postpartum women. 48 Only two studies (n = 2) have been included in this scale. The probiotic group had lower depression scores.34, 42

Beck Depression Inventory-II: The BDI is one of the most widely used tools for measuring depressive symptoms globally. 49 Two studies measured this scale (n = 2). No significant changes were observed in the probiotic group. 27 BDI-II scores were reduced in the probiotic group. 29

Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale: It is another scale used to measure depression. 50 Only one study included this scale (n = 1) to measure depression. There was a significant reduction in the depression score in the probiotic group. 36

Various Anxiety Rating Scales

Anxiety and depressive disorders affect around 10% of the global population. In this systematic review, 10 studies assessed anxiety symptoms using various standardised scales, including State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale-Anxiety subscale (HADS-A).

State–Trait Anxiety Inventory Scale (STAI or STAI-6): STAI-S is the most commonly used anxiety scale to measure the level of anxiety. 51 Out of 20 RCTs, 8 studies have measured STAI-S (n = 7). The study did not find any changes in the probiotic group. 28 Most of the studies had significantly lower anxiety scores at postpartum compared with placebo.26,27,29,31,34,42

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale-Anxiety Subscale: HADS-A is another anxiety measurement scale that measures the level of anxiety. Three studies have included this scale (n = 3). Significant improvement in the HADS-A scale in the probiotic group.32, 33 No significant changes were observed in the probiotic group. 35

Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7: GAD-7 is a brief, validated, self-reported questionnaire used to screen for and measure the severity of GAD symptoms. Only one of the studies mentions that GAD-7 scores (n = 1) also trended lower in the probiotic group, but the differences were not statistically significant at week 4. 28

Primary Tools Used to Measure Stress Scale

In this systematic review, 10 studies assessed anxiety symptoms using various standardised scales, including the Montreal Imaging Stress Task (MIST) and the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS).

Montreal Imaging Stress Task: MIST is a mental erythrometric task used to analyse mental performance. 52 Only one study has included (n = 1) this study. No significant changes will be observed in the probiotic group. 43

The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-14/PSS): A self-report tool called PSS was created to measure the extent to which respondents thought their lives were disorganised, beyond control and overloaded. 53 Five studies have included this study (n = 5). A significant reduction will be observed in the probiotic supplementation group.27, 33 No significant difference will be observed between the two groups.35, 37 Decreased PSS-14 scores in the probiotic group compared to the placebo group. 34

Sleep Quality

Sleep quality was primarily assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a widely used standardised tool.

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index: Neurocognitive dysfunctions, such as poor impulse control, depression, anxiety, stress and attention deficiencies, are associated with sleep disturbances. In clinical and research contexts, the PSQI plays a significant role in evaluating sleep health. Nine studies have included this scale (n = 9), and most of the studies have shown improvement in sleep quality. Sleep quality will improve in the probiotic group.33,34,37,38,40,45 No significant difference between the probiotic and placebo groups. 35

Moods

The Profile of Mood States: The Profile of Mood States (PoMS) was originally developed to measure mood and feeling states of psychiatric outpatients. 54 Three studies measured this scale (n = 3), and all studies have mentioned that there is an improvement in the PoMS scale. Significant improvement was observed in all subscales in the probiotic group.27, 37, 38

Visual Analogue Scale: The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) is a self-report measurement tool often used in clinical and psychological research to capture subjective experiences, such as pain, mood, anxiety, stress or well-being. 55 Only two studies have measured these scales (n = 2). Improvement in perceived well-being in the probiotic group. 45 VAS score is lower in the probiotic group. 34

Positive and Negative Affect Schedule: The Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) is a self-report questionnaire used to measure mood and emotions. 56 The positive PANAS change score was higher in the placebo group. 34 A significant reduction was observed in PANAS score. 31

Psychological Well-being

Ryff Well-being Scale: The Ryff Well-being Scale (RYFF) is mainly used to measure overall psychological well-being. 57 Only one study (n = 1) has been included, but it does not show any changes that will be observed. 31

Cognitive Functions

Ageing may bring about mild cognitive changes; however, substantial cognitive decline is typically a sign of pathological ageing. In this systematic review, five studies (n = 5) have measured the various cognitive parameters and showed mixed types of responses after probiotic interventions. Two studies showed trends of improvement in cognitive functions in the probiotic group compared to the placebo group.37, 44 Another two studies mentioned that there is an improvement in memory and attention in the probiotic group.26, 37 No significant changes in memory, attention or executive function were observed over the intervention period in the probiotic group. 28 Significant improvement was observed in attention, processing speed and executive function in the probiotic group compared to the placebo. Memory performance showed trends towards improvement. 30

Quality of Life (SF12-MH Scale, SF-36)

SF-36/SF12-MH is a widely used health survey, a validated questionnaire designed to measure health-related questions about quality of life. 58 Five studies have measured quality of life, giving mixed type of responses. Significant improvement in quality of life was observed compared to the placebo.29, 36, 39 No notable changes were observed.31, 35

Stress Hormones and Neurotransmitters

Serum Serotonin Levels: Only one study has measured serotonin concentration (n = 1). Serum serotonin levels significantly increased in the probiotic group. 36

Cortisol Concentration. Cortisol is a major stress hormone; a total of seven studies have included (n = 7), showing mixed responses. Significant reduction of cortisol concentration was observed in the probiotic group.37, 40, 42, 45 No significant changes were observed in cortisol concentration in the probiotic group.34, 36, 43

Adrenocorticotropic Hormone: Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) is another symbol of stress associated with psychological imbalance. Two studies have included ACTH (n = 2). Both studies show a significant reduction of ACTH concentrations in the probiotic group.40, 42

Brain-derived Neurotrophic Factor. Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) plays a crucial role in learning, memory, mood regulation and neuroplasticity. 59 Only three studies have measured serum BDNF concentrations (n = 3). The probiotic group and the placebo group did not show any changes in BDNF levels. 39 Serum BDNF concentration increased.30, 44

Inflammatory Markers

Depression is associated with increased levels of inflammatory markers such as CRP, interleukin (IL)-2 receptors, IL-6, IL-1 and tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α).60, 61 Only four studies have included inflammatory markers, and most have reported a significant decrease in these markers.

Interleukin-1β: IL-1β is a proinflammatory cytokine that mediates immune cell activation, inflammatory signalling and innate and adaptive immunity. Only one study has measured the concentration of IL-1β; levels decreased significantly in the probiotic group. 44

C-reactive Protein: CRP is a marker of inflammation; in this systematic review, only two studies have measured the concentration of CRP. When compared to the placebo group, CRP levels dramatically dropped in the probiotic group. 45 No significant changes were observed in CRP concentration in the probiotic concentration. 28

Interleukin-6: IL-6 is the most important inflammatory marker, and in this systematic review, only two studies have measured the concentration of IL-6. IL-6 levels decreased significantly in the probiotic group.27, 45

Immunoglobulin A: Immunoglobulin A (IgA) provides mucosal immunity by neutralising pathogens. Only one study has measured the concentration of IgA. IgA concentration increased significantly in the probiotic group. 45

Tumour Necrosis Factor-alpha: Only one study has measured significant changes in TNF-α levels in the probiotic group. 27

Discussion

Most studies have shown a reduction in depression and anxiety scales, improved sleep quality, enhanced mood states, improved cognitive functions, improved quality of life and a beneficial gut microbiota population in the probiotic group. Around 11 studies have measured the gut microbial population, and most of the studies have shown an increase in beneficial bacterial species, such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus. Probiotics such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus are thought to have positive impacts on both mental and physical health, including psychiatric symptoms. Healthy volunteers who take probiotic supplements of B. longum and L. helveticus report fewer symptoms of discomfort and lower levels of free cortisol. 65 Human probiotic supplementation is therefore thought to have the ability to improve the gastrointestinal (GI) tract’s bacterial composition and, consequently, behaviour and mental health.66, 67 According to new research, probiotics may significantly affect several brain processes via regulating the gut–brain axis. When Rudzki et al. looked at the benefits of L. plantarum probiotics as an adjunct therapy for cognitive functioning in individuals with MDD, they discovered that the probiotic group had better instantaneous verbal memory recall than the placebo group. 68 Important interactions between probiotics and the brain may be explained by the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis (HPA axis) and inflammatory pathways. Probiotic use altered the HPA axis’s hyperactivity. 69 In rats, treatment with Lactobacillus farciminis or ML-7 decreased endotoxaemia, inhibited the activation of the HPA axis and reduced inflammation in the brain. 11 The afferent and efferent branches of the vagus nerve are essential for two-way communication between the gut and the brain. It may have a role in conditions such as depression by using its afferent fibres to pick up signals from the gut microbiota and send them to the brain. Through vagal efferents, stress-induced brain alterations might exacerbate depression by increasing intestinal permeability and inflammation. Vagus nerve stimulation has been used to treat depression and has anti-inflammatory properties. However, decreased vagal tone is frequently associated with depression, which may be exacerbated by stress. 70 Following a subdiaphragmatic vagotomy, the three bacterial strains or FMT from Chrna7 mutant mice did not produce behavioural issues, body inflammation or loss of synaptic proteins in the prefrontal cortex.71–73L. rhamnosus probiotic supplementation can lower stress-induced corticosterone and anxiety, although vagotomised mice did not exhibit behavioural alterations associated with depression. 74 The communication between the stomach and brain may be influenced by SCFAs, which are created by gut microorganisms through the metabolism of indigestible fibres. By promoting T-cell differentiation, regulating the synthesis of inflammatory cytokines and affecting the creation of serotonin and other neurotransmitters, SCFAs have positive effects on mental health and inflammation. By changing inflammation and neurotransmitter balance, which can affect mood regulation, SCFAs may exacerbate depression. 75 In mice under chronic stress, SCFA administration reduced depressed and anxious behaviour. 76 Gut microbiota may affect the metabolism of tryptophan. 77 Tryptophan is the precursor for 5-HT (serotonin) synthesis by EC cells in the gut and neurons in the enteric system and CNS. 78 Serotonin plays a central role in regulating sleep. Serotonergic signalling influences behavioural states and interacts with brain regions that control the circadian rhythm. 79 Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) plays a key role in regulating anxiety; brain imaging studies have demonstrated reduced benzodiazepine receptor binding on GABA receptors in individuals with anxiety disorders, along with decreased cortical GABA levels as measured by magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS).80–84 In addition, microbes produce gamma-aminobutyric acid in vitro, but the effectiveness varied among different species. 85

This systematic review is unique in several aspects, as it is the first to comprehensively evaluate probiotic supple–mentation involving various strains of Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus and S. thermophilus. The review encompasses studies published over the last 10 years and includes an analysis of various measurement scales of depression, anxiety, perceived stress, mood, sleep quality, quality of life, gut microbiota population and inflammatory markers. All the studies included in the review adhered to a uniform design, comprising an experimental group administered probiotics and a placebo-controlled group. Another RCT mentioned that the probiotic capsule consisted of various strains of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, which had beneficial effects on depressive symptoms and glucose metabolism. 21

Limitations

There are certain restrictions on the current systematic review. First, the validity and generalisability of the findings may be impacted by the included research’s inconsistent sample sizes and demographic distributions. The inclusion of diverse population groups and various strains of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus contributes to the complexity of this study. Additionally, variations in disease conditions and intervention durations may have influenced the outcomes related to various biological markers. Given the heterogeneity among the included studies, the findings of this systematic review should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, the limited use of standardised outcome measures across studies poses challenges for data pooling and restricts the robustness of analytical evaluations.

Conclusion

Most studies have shown a reduction in depression and anxiety scales, improved sleep quality, enhanced mood states, enhanced cognitive functions, improved quality of life and a beneficial gut microbiota population in the probiotic group. Cortisol and various stress scales have mixed responses. The concentration of BDNF will remain unchanged, and proinflammatory cytokines are difficult to measure because of their low sample size. Current evidence suggests that probiotic supplementation improves depressive scales, anxiety scales, sleep index, gut microbial composition, quality of life, cognitive functions and various mood states.
