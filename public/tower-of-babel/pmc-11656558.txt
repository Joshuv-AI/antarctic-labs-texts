Is there a relationship between psychiatrist’s clinical assessment of medication non-adherence and treatment outcomes? Implications for practice
Authors: Graham Danzer, David Sugarbaker, Adriano Zanello, Sam Barkin, Doug Cort
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is considerable research on the ramifications of medication non-adherence for adults with psychotic illnesses. Much of which has tightly controlled designs and strict inclusion/exclusion procedures (i.e., it is less “ecologically valid,” or consistent with real-world challenges in care). The authors sought to determine predictive relationships between psychiatrists’ clinical assessments of non-adherence and treatment outcomes, via a design that would be more applicable to practice.MethodMultiple regression analyses were conducted on non-adherence, symptom severity upon admission, number of recent hospitalizations, and length of hospital stay. The sample consisted of 182 inpatients with psychotic spectrum disorders and significant risk and vulnerability factors. Non-adherence was measured via the psychiatrists’ diagnosis of V15.81. Symptom severity was measured via the 24-item Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS-E).ResultsThere were null findings on non-adherence and BPRS-E pretest score (χ2= 2, p = 0.16), recent hospitalizations (χ2= 1.2, p = 0.27), and length of stay (β = 0.003, p = 0.97). Higher symptom severity predicted a modestly longer length of stay (χ2= .20, p = 0.007), though Bonferroni correction nullified this finding. White/Caucasian participants were far more likely to be non-adherent than black/African-American participants (t = -8.66; p > .00001).ConclusionsNull findings suggest the psychiatrist’s initial, quick-form assessment of non-adherence may not necessarily presume a poor prognosis. Perhaps, because individuals with severe and chronic psychotic disorders may have greater coping, adaptive, and survival skills than is often assumed. In severely under-resourced hospitals, such second thoughts and more reliable information about adherence and contributing factors may improve treatment outcomes.Keywords: Psychotic Disorders, Non-adherence, Schizophrenia, Treatment

Background

Psychotropic medications, mainly antipsychotics, have long been recognized as the gold standard for treating psychotic spectrum disorders [1–4]. Consistent adherence generally facilitates symptom management, improves psychosocial functioning, and reduces risk for re-hospitalization [1, 5]. Medication refusal associates with much higher risk for re-hospitalization, incarceration, substance abuse, cognitive dysfunction, suicide, and early mortality [6].

Nevertheless, individuals with psychotic spectrum disorders often refuse medications, mainly because of side effects and quality-of-life factors [7, 8]. Per the Clinical Antipsychotic Trials of Intervention Effectiveness (CATIE), 74% of over 1400 patients discontinued their medications before the completion of the 18-month study, mainly due to side effects [9]. CATIE was critiqued for not considering psychosocial reasons for discontinuation, including stigma, socioeconomics, and quality-of-life factors [10]. However, follow-up qualitative studies yielded similar findings, regarding the main reasons for non-adherence [4, 11]. First psychotic episode, longitudinal, and meta-analytic studies consistently determined associations between non-adherence, disease progression, and lessening insight into need for treatment [12–15].

A recent study of 29,956 patients prescribed antipsychotics further explored the factors that contribute to non-adherence [6]. Generally consistent with prior research, antipsychotic adherence tended to associate with a multitude of positive outcomes, though only occurred about 40–60% of the time. Non-adherence was more common amongst younger patients, those who were also prescribed a mood stabilizer (which often leads to undesirable sedation and weight gain side effects), and had a significant history of substance abuse, prior suicide attempts, and medical complications. Notably, non-adherence dropped to 7.27% when individuals were prescribed any long-acting injectable, and to 4.77% when they were prescribed Clozaril.

Thus, research generally affirms the overall efficacy of antipsychotic medications, negative longer term outcomes associated with non-adherence, and the potential benefits of switching to long-acting injectables. However, there appear to be no studies of psychiatrist’s clinical impressions of non-adherence and more immediate negative outcomes. Further, treatment research often utilizes highly controlled designs, with strict screening procedures, and adequate collateral data to address potential confounds. While this increases replicability, results may have lesser clinical implications when based on a design that is not reminiscent of the real-world challenges faced by inpatient psychiatrists (i.e., higher reliability, at the expense of lower ecological validity). Unlike in well-controlled research designs, inpatient psychiatrists rely primarily on their clinical judgment to treat high numbers of severely psychotic individuals, often with little-to-no collateral data on their histories of adherence/non-adherence, and pressing needs to reduce symptom acuity and thereby reduce length of stay.

To address these gaps, this research sought to determine, through a design with high ecological validity, whether there may be a significant relationship between the psychiatrist’s clinical impression of non-adherence and immediate outcomes. Ecological validity was increased by using a quick-form assessment of non-adherence and researching a sample of inpatients with psychotic spectrum disorders and significant acuity, chronicity, and vulnerability factors. Such multicollinearity often leads individuals to be screened out of research, though inpatient psychiatrists must still assess and treat them. Thus, the current measure of adherence and sample characteristics better represent the everyday challenges faced in inpatient hospitals.

Methods

The current researchers sought to determine, via multiple regression analyses, whether there were significant predictor relationships between medication non-adherence and greater symptom severity upon admission, a greater number of hospitalizations in the prior 12 months, and longer lengths of inpatient stay (LOS). This involved an analysis of an archival data set from a 2013–2015 inpatient hospital study of symptom reduction and patient satisfaction. Symptom severity was measured via the 24-item Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale, Expanded (BPRS-E). Demographic and clinical data were collected from patient medical records. Data were collected by psychology externs from doctoral programs accredited by the American Psychological Association (APA). Extern training and supervision was overseen by licensed psychologists with expertise in assessment, including but not limited to the BPRS-E. Training consisted of two hours of weekly didactic instruction and one hour of individual supervision and monitoring.

To maximize inter-rater reliability (IRR), externs initially went to inpatient units in pairs and conducted practice interviews on the same patient. Both student interviewers scored the same patient and then returned to group supervision for further review. Paired-practice interviews continued until BPRS-E scores, on each item for each patient, were consistently rated with supervisory approval and no more than one point of difference (per item, per patient). This surpasses the research standard IRR ≥ 0.8 [16, 17]. Two hours of weekly (group and individual) supervision helped to monitor IRR in a manner consistent with the standardization and general usage of the BPRS-E, as discussed in the upcoming “measures” section of this paper.

Setting

The original study was conducted at a locked inpatient psychiatric hospital in a large urban neighborhood in Northern California. The facility contains a 28-bed psychiatric emergency room (PES) and three 21-bed inpatient units. The vast majority of patients are involuntarily transported by the police or emergency medical personnel to PES for observation, assessment, and treatment. All patients are assessed by psychiatrists within 24 h of initial contact at PES. Thereafter, patients may be discharged, placed on 72-h emergent holds, and then released or re-committed for up to 10 days, per California statute. Hospital programming includes multidisciplinary observation, medication management, and various therapeutic services.

Measures

The BPRS-E is used in pharmaceutical and psychological research, both as an assessment tool for schizophrenia and as an outcome measure in inpatient and outpatient settings [18–20]. There are 24-items, each measuring the presence and severity of 24 distinct illness signs and symptoms on Likert scales ranging in severity from 1—7 [21]. Thus, possible scores range from 24 to 168, with higher scores indicating increasingly severe psychopathology [21]. Items 1–12 are based on patient self-report, while items 13–24 are based on clinical observation. BPRS-E interviews usually take about 10 – 40 min, depending on rapport and patient acuity [19]. Manic patients often require more time and redirection [19].

A combined samples replication study of descriptive and chance-corrected coefficients indicated concordant BPRS-E rater agreement > 0.75 on 21 of 24 items, albeit with acceptable weighted kappa values (kw ≥ 0.60) for only 11 items [16]. When measures are administered to severely disordered inpatients, less reliable ratings can be expected.

For the purpose of the present analysis, the psychiatrist’s clinical assessment of non-adherence was operationally defined as the presence or absence of the relevant diagnostic V-code (V15.81). This was chosen as a quick-form indication of non-adherence, which is commonly used in inpatient hospitals due to limited resources. Strengths, limitations, and complications associated with using V15.81 are detailed and discussed later in this paper. Hospital re-admission was defined as the frequency of admissions, at the facility under study, over the past 12 months. Length of Stay (LOS) was the total number of days hospitalized, including in PES.

Participants

For the initial hospital study, participants were randomly selected from a larger pool of inpatients (n = 276) who had been diagnosed by admitting psychiatry with Schizophrenia, Schizoaffective Disorder (of any specifier), Unspecified Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorder, or Major Depressive or Bipolar Disorders with Psychotic Features. For the purpose of this study, diagnoses have been updated to reflect the language of the current Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR). The trans-diagnostic focus of this study is consistent with DSM-5-TR theory of psychosis as a spectrum illness, often with varying clusters of thought, mood, and behavioral abnormalities, and associated features to include low insight into illness and need for treatment, which often leads to non-adherence [22].

Whereas the original hospital study intended to measure symptom severity upon admission and discharge, about 13% (n = 27) of initially interviewed participants were too psychiatrically disorganized to complete the (BPRS-E) posttest, or lucidly refused based on apparent preoccupation with discharge. Similar clinically-related complications have been emphasized in other BPRS-E studies of symptom acuity [23]. Many of the participants in the hospital study did not complete the posttest because they were discharged soon after admission or at times when psychology externs were not available to complete the posttest (n = 57). Thus, the retention rate for this study was approximately 87%. This surpassed the research standard for retention of 70–80% [21, 24]. All participants who completed the pretest assessment of symptom severity, for the original hospital study (n = 182), were included in this analysis.

As detailed in Table 1, the original hospital sample was heterogeneous in terms of age, race, and family involvement. The current sample was largely involuntarily hospitalized, and had high levels of global symptomology, risk for suicide and violence, vulnerability factors, substance abuse, and recent hospitalizations. Whereas such factors may result in exclusion from other studies, greater complexity and heterogeneity helped to move this study closer to approximating the realities of inpatient psychiatric care (i.e., high ecological validity).

Procedure and Ethics

Univariate and logistic regression analyses were conducted on psychiatrist’s clinical assessment of non-adherence (V15.81), symptom severity upon admission (BPRS-E total score), admissions to the facility under study over the past 12 months, and length of stay (LOS). Symptom severity and prior hospitalizations were analyzed separately, in order to differentiate acuity and chronicity factors. As medication adherence/non-adherence, symptom severity, chronicity, and LOS are among the major assessment and outcome measures in inpatient hospitals, analyzing related variables establishes the clinical framework for a good model. It was hypothesized that determining significant relationships between clinical assessments of non-adherence and acuity vs. chronicity factors might yield different implications for inpatient psychiatry practice.

Hypotheses were as follows:

Hypothesis 1) Higher BPRS-E symptom severity upon admission would predict a subsequently admitting psychiatrist’s diagnosis of V15.81 (i.e., assessment of non-adherence).

Hypothesis 2) Higher frequency of hospitalizations (over the past 12 months at the hospital under study) would also predict an admitting psychiatrist’s diagnosis of V15.81.

Hypothesis (3) V15.81 would predict significantly longer LOS.

The original hospital study was approved by its internal Institutional Review Board (IRB). The current research was approved by the IRB of both the hospital and the corresponding author’s doctoral program. All hospital data were coded and aggregated to protect confidentiality.

Extra precautions were taken to ensure informed consent, due to the sample’s high acuity, vulnerability, and rates of non-adherence. Patients were excluded from participation (n = 20) if they displayed significant paranoia when asked to sign the four-page consent form and/or appeared unable to acknowledge and paraphrase what their participation would entail. Patients who were just admitted to the emergency room, had English language barriers, and/or were actively detoxifying from controlled substances were excluded. Interviews were discontinued when participants were so severely incoherent, hyper verbal, or difficult to redirect that the interview appeared likely to extend well beyond the upper end of the usual timeframe.

Consistent with the recommendations of Guarda et al. [25], participants were informed their involvement in the study was voluntary, they were free to discontinue at any time, and their participation or refusal would have no bearing on their inpatient privileges or admission-discharge status. Per the recommendations of Hopko et al. [26] and Ligon and Thyer [27], student interviewers attempted to increase the reliability of symptom assessments by stressing to participants the importance of honest disclosure and generally deferring interviews until after an initial period of treatment.

Results

Logistic regression analyses were conducted to determine whether higher BPRS-E pretest symptom severity and recent hospital re-admissions independently predicted psychiatrist’s clinical assessment of non-adherence, as measured. Based on the standard level of statistical significance (χ2 = 3.84) according to Coolican [16], higher BPRS-E pretest score (χ2 = 2, p = 0.16) and higher frequency of hospital readmissions (χ2 = 1.2, p = 0.27) did not significantly predict non-adherence. A univariate regression analysis was conducted on non-adherence and LOS, which also yielded statistically non-significant findings (β = 0.003, p = 0.97).

Post-Hoc Analyses

As analyses of non-adherence and prior hospital admissions yielded non-significant findings in multiple analyses, those variables were set aside. An additional univariate regression analysis was conducted on BPRS-E pretest and LOS. This was necessary because prior research suggests acutely ill, non-adherent patients are likely to be hospitalized longer [1, 5]. After Bonferroni correction, higher BPRS-E total score did not significantly predict longer LOS.

Additional exploratory analyses were conducted on non-adherence and the 24 BPRS-E items. Individual items may have been less stable measures of psychopathology, particularly in an inpatient psychiatric hospital where participant’s response styles and symptom presentation may be impacted by situational and psychosocial crises. Item level analyses were nevertheless conducted because identifying possible symptom correlates of non-adherence might suggest targets for talk therapeutic treatments, as a pathway to improving adherence.

As noted in Table 5, independent samples t tests yielded statistically significant differences depending on the presence or absence of V15.81, for the items Suspiciousness/paranoia [t(180) = 2.09, p = 0.04] and Conceptual Disorganization/disorganized speech [t(180) = 2.5, p = 0.01]. Thus, there were non-significant differences in the scores on the 22 other major signs and symptoms measured on the BPRS-E, including self-reported Hallucinations and Unusual Thought Content. Family wise error nullified item level findings, though qualitative considerations and implications for further study are presented later in this paper.

Lastly, an independent samples t test was run on medication adherence/non-adherence for Black/African American and White/Caucasian patients. This was necessary to explore the possibility that, perhaps, null findings may have been partially due to bias. White race associated with a far higher likelihood of non-adherence (t = −8.654; p < 0.00001). Underscoring the general problem of non-adherence, about 60% of White participants (n = 57) were non-adherent, as measured, as were 52% of Black participants (n = 79).

Discussion

Identifying possible relationships between psychiatrist’s assessment of non-adherence and treatment outcomes is an important measure in exploring the predictive validity of a common assessment strategy in under-resourced psychiatric hospitals. Due to scarce resources, psychiatrists must quickly assess large numbers of involuntarily hospitalized individuals with severe, chronic, and complex psychotic disorders, often with minimal collateral data, and in scenarios where risk and liability are high. Moreover, prior research suggests that as psychotic spectrum disorders become more severe and chronic, medications are increasingly refused and may even be less effective in some cases. Accordingly, there is a need for a research-informed understanding of the strengths and limitations of daily practice strategies.

The results of the current study suggest marked discrepancies between prior research findings and what would appear to be psychiatrist’s converging clinical assessments of non-adherence. Prior research studies consistently found that non-adherence associated with many negative outcomes related to disease progression, as often manifests in greater symptom acuity (e.g., higher BPRS-E score) and chronicity (e.g., more frequent and longer hospitalizations). However, the current study found that non-adherence, as measured, did not significantly predict any of these major outcomes. The null findings are all the more unexpected because a large proportion of the sample was diagnosed with bipolar disorders and remained severely-extremely mentally ill, even after an initial period of treatment. Accordingly, significant relationships between non-adherence and higher symptom severity upon admission, a higher number of recent hospitalizations, and longer lengths of inpatient stay were anticipated.

Null findings may be informative to the inpatient psychiatrists’ conceptualization, treatment planning, and case formulation. Null findings suggest the psychiatrist’s initial impression of non-adherence, as an indicator of a guarded-poor treatment prognosis, may not necessarily predict negative outcomes. Such a second thought may be important in severely under-resourced public hospital settings that so often re-admit “frequent flyer” type patients with significant substance abuse issues, poverty factors, and low insight. Such difficult cases can bring about a level of cynicism and burn-out that risks sabotage and self-fulfilling prophecies.

Taking the latter possibility a step further, the current null findings may even be encouraging. Perhaps, severely psychotic, involuntarily hospitalized, and non-adherent inpatients have greater strengths and adaptive and survival skills than may often be assumed. Exploration of this alternative hypothesis, during the psychiatrist’s initial assessment, may contribute to improved doctor-patient relationships, which has been found to associate with improved adherence [13].

Prior research findings and the current null findings underscore the importance and complications of inpatient psychiatrists considering long acting injectables, including Clozaril. As detailed in prior studies, individuals with severe psychotic disorders often experience significant disease progression and correspondingly lessening insight into an increasingly clearer need for treatment. While family wise error nullified the results of exploratory analyses, future studies should explore whether select symptom elevations may be driving non-adherence and therefore warrant greater prioritization in talk therapeutic treatment.

As a partial explanation for null findings, it is important to consider possible confounds and covariates. As detailed in Table 1, the hospital sample was racially and ethnically diverse, disproportionately unemployed, afflicted with co-occurring substance use disorders, and had high levels of family involvement (i.e., possible enmeshment). Prior research suggests significant relationships between these psychosocial and vulnerability factors, non-adherence, illness exacerbation, and re-hospitalization [7, 28–32]. Future research should better consider and account for these factors, though this may require greater control and proportionately lessened ecological validity [4, 10, 11].

Additional limitations warrant mention. There is a literature base in support of the reliability and utility of V15.81 [33]. In an inpatient psychiatric hospital, non-adherence generally refers to psychotropic medications, and for individuals with schizophrenia spectrum illnesses, antipsychotics in particular. However, V15.81 is a more general indicator of non-adherence. For example, it can indicate refusal of non-psychiatric medical and substance abuse treatment. Moreover, any two psychiatrists at the hospital under study might not have clinically assessed non-adherence the same way. No information is available on how the psychiatrists diagnosed or ruled-out V15.81. For these reasons, standard measures of adherence are usually preferred for research purposes. Given the presenting limitations, V15.81 may be considered a screener, rather than an adequate substitute for a fuller assessment.

In addition, researching severely and chronically psychotic inpatients, with minimal exclusion criteria, and in a single site poses limitations related to a high amount of uncontrolled variance and lower replicability. This speaks to the general trade-off in conducting a study that seeks high ecological validity. Screening out more complex cases, limiting analyses to individuals prescribed certain classes of medications, and using a standard research design with a fuller and more specific assessment of antipsychotic non-adherence would lessen these limitations (though generate results in a context less reminiscent of the realities of inpatient care). This study is further limited by no available data on which medications were prescribed, how often select medications were refused or complied with, and too few voluntary patients to permit analysis of adherence based on hospital-legal status.

Conclusions

It was unexpected that there were non-significant predictor relationships between the psychiatrists’ clinical assessment of non-adherence and symptom severity upon admission, recent hospitalizations, and length of stay (LOS). Null findings may encourage a second thought about the prognosis of severely psychotic inpatients with histories of non-adherence and significant risk and vulnerability factors. The initial impression of non-adherence may not necessarily predict negative outcomes, especially when patients have greater coping skills, resilience, and other “lived experience” factors than is often assumed.

Faced with enormous time and resource pressures, inpatient psychiatrists must be provided with more reliable information about medication non-adherence and contributing factors (e.g., symptom drivers, side effects, family dynamics, substance abuse). This would be informative to the psychiatrist considering long acting injectables, including Clozaril, for which non-adherence can be life threatening. Identified barriers to adherence could also be prioritized in talk therapeutic treatments. Such alternative engagement strategies may contribute to improved doctor-patient relationships and associated clinical outcomes.
