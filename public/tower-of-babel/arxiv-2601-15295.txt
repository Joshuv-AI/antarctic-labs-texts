Yi Wang, John Joon Young Chung, Melissa Roemmele, Yuqian Sun, Tiffany Wang, Shm Garanganao Almeda, Brett A. Halperin, Yuwen Lu, Max Kreminski

Abstract
. Interactive narrative (IN) authors craft spaces of divergent narrative possibilities for players to explore, with the player’s input determining which narrative possibilities they actually experience. Generative AI can enable new forms of IN by improvisationally expanding on pre-authored content in response to open-ended player input. However, this extrapolation risks widening the gap between author-envisioned and player-experienced stories, potentially limiting the strength of plot progression and the communication of the author’s narrative intent. To bridge the gap, we introduce Elsewise : an authoring tool for AI-based INs that implements a novel Bundled Storyline concept to enhance author’s perception and understanding of the narrative possibility space, allowing authors to explore similarities and differences between possible playthroughs of their IN in terms of open-ended, user-configurable narrative dimensions. A user study (n=12) shows that our approach improves author anticipation of player-experienced narrative, leading to more effective control and exploration of the narrative possibility spaces.

1.
Introduction

Interactive narrative (IN) experiences permit players to explore a space of divergent narrative possibilities crafted by an author, with the player’s input determining which narrative possibilities they will actually encounter

(
Riedl and Bulitko 2013
;
Green and Jenkins 2014
)
. The most prominent form of IN features multiple branching storylines reflecting the consequences of different player actions. A fundamental challenge for IN authoring lies in the tension between two competing needs: the need for coherent expression of the author’s narrative intent (
authorial intent
) and the imperative to immerse audiences in the storyworld by granting them meaningful agency over plot progression (
player agency
)
(
Riedl and Bulitko 2013
)
.

Conventional IN authoring workflows address this challenge by predefining the range of possible player actions and creating storylines tailored to each potential course of action. However, comprehensively authoring appropriate storylines for many different potential sequences of player actions can be prohibitively burdensome, limiting the degree of player choice typically supported by conventional IN systems. Many authoring tools
(
Green et al. 2021
)
have been introduced to help IN authors overcome this limitation, but the authorial burden
(
Jones and Millard 2024
)
associated with high-agency forms of IN remains substantial.

Large language models (LLMs) show potential to transform IN by enabling free-form player input and generating dynamic narrative content tailored to player actions in real-time. Traditional interactive narratives rely exclusively on pre-authored storylines created during development, limiting players to predetermined narrative paths. In contrast, LLM-powered systems can synthesize the author’s narrative intent with player input during gameplay, dynamically generating personalized narrative experiences. Even narrative possibilities not envisioned by the author can be improvised in response to open-ended player input, which significantly enhances player agency.

However, while LLMs can effectively bridge authorial intent and player input
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
, their use in IN also tends to widen the gap between the author’s envisioned narrative experience and the player’s actual experience.
Using LLMs to interpret open-ended player input sacrifices the author’s comprehensive understanding of the range of actions players might perform. Moreover, using LLMs for just-in-time generation of narrative content restricts the author’s direct access to the specific narrative material that players may encounter
(
Lu et al. 2025
)
. Consequently, authors of LLM-based IN struggle to anticipate the storylines that players may actually encounter, resulting in diminished control over player experience. This often leads to narratives that lack meaningful plot progression
(
Tian et al. 2024
;
Chakrabarty et al. 2024
;
Beguš 2024
)
as the combination of player and LLM improvisation drags the storyline away from the intended course, and to failures of expressive communication
(
Louie et al. 2022
)
as players lose touch with the human “hand behind the multiform plot”
(
Murray 1997
, p. 347)
.

To address this challenge, we propose a novel concept of
Bundled Storyline
to aid authors of AI-based INs in making sense of narrative possibility space (Fig.
1
). Our approach builds on insights from prior work in (AI-based) IN authoring
(
Lu et al. 2025
;
Kreminski and Wardrip-Fruin 2018
)
, storyline visualization
(
Chung et al. 2022
;
Masson et al. 2025
)
, and general-purpose tools for visualizing and making sense of possibility spaces
(
Suh et al. 2024
;
Kreminski et al. 2022
;
Chung and Kreminski 2024
;
Smith and Whitehead 2010
)
.
A bundled storyline structure “bundles together” narrative states that are indistinguishable in terms of certain user-configurable narrative dimensions to construct branching visualizations, which illustrate how different possible playthroughs of a single AI-based IN tend to converge and diverge in an abstract narrative state space. Narrative dimensions may be defined manually by an author (Fig.
1
(a)) to assess alignment between player experience and authorial intent, or automatically derived from player-experienced storylines to reveal emergent plot dynamics (Fig.
1
(b)). Additionally, users can combine existing narrative dimensions to cross-reference different aspects of the narrative possibility space (Fig.
1
(c)).

We implement the Bundled Storyline framework in
Elsewise
, an authoring tool for LLM-based INs.
Elsewise
allows authors to define what might happen in an AI-based IN via structured storyworld initialization prompts and a trigger-based rule system adapted from storylets
(
Kreminski and Wardrip-Fruin 2018
)
. Unlike existing authoring tools for AI-based INs
(
Mishra et al. 2025
;
Wu et al. 2025
)
which primarily focus on streamlining the initial implementation of the author’s intended narrative structure, our approach aims to assist IN authors in gradually sculpting the possibility space of player-experienced narratives. Through Bundled Storylines, users can intuitively shape the distribution and trajectories of narrative states to meet their storytelling objectives (Fig.
1
, bottom). A user study (
n=12
) demonstrates that our approach effectively enables authors to better anticipate player-experienced storylines, resulting in more effective control over the narrative possibility space. Additionally, our findings reveal how
Elsewise
facilitates creative exploration in shaping this narrative possibility space.

Our work has three main contributions:

(1)

The concept of
Bundled Storylines
to aid perception and sensemaking of player experienced storylines.

(2)

Elsewise
, an IN authoring tool that visualizes Bundled Storylines and implemented related affordances to support structured exploration and sculpting of AI-based narrative possibility space.

(3)

A user study (n=12) that validates the effectiveness of this approach and sheds lights on how authors perceive, control and explore using our approach.

Altogether, we show how authoring tools can enable authors of AI-based IN to better anticipate, explore, and shape the range of narrative outcomes that players are likely to experience.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
(AI-Based) Interactive Narrative

Constructing a compelling linear story is already a difficult problem of creative
orchestration

(
Talmy 2012
;
Liapis et al. 2018
)
: the high-level plot and character arcs must remain coherent, the low-level details of how the story is told must keep the audience engaged, and all of these facets must work together to effectively communicate the storyteller’s expressive intent
(
Kreminski and Martens 2022
)
. However, the difficulty of conventional linear storytelling is bounded because the author is able to exert precise control over the nature and sequencing of the material presented to the audience. Ultimately, only a single coherent and communicatively effective
storyline
must be conveyed.

Interactivity adds a new dimension of difficulty to storytelling by forcing authors to contend with variation. When there are multiple different ways that a story might unfold based on the player’s choices, it becomes substantially more complicated to anticipate the order of events that each individual player will experience—and therefore to exert control over important experiential qualities such as surprise
(
Bae and Young 2013
)
, pacing
(
Cohn 2013
)
, and tension
(
Kybartas et al. 2020
)
. Difficulties in anticipating and shaping player experience form a significant part of the “authoring problem” as it is commonly discussed in IN research communities
(
Hargood et al. 2023
)
, and two of the most popular design approaches to IN—
emergent narrative

(
Aylett 2000
;
Walsh 2011
;
Ryan et al. 2015
;
Kreminski and Mateas 2021
)
and
drama management

(
Roberts and Isbell 2008
;
Chen et al. 2009
)
—are often framed as different ways of handling the conflict between the unpredictability of the player and the desire to ensure that authorial intent is maintained
(
Aylett 2000
)
.

Broadly, IN authoring can be framed as instantiating not just a single storyline but a
story volume

(
Grinblat 2017
)
: a space of possible storylines that share certain common characteristics, but also admit a progressively wider range of variation as additional narrative branching points
(
Jones 2023
)
or recombinable narrative content units
(
Kreminski and Wardrip-Fruin 2018
)
are introduced. Story volumes are said to be either
closed
(if they admit a finite range of possibilities, with all player-experienced stories ultimately consisting of a valid arrangement of a fixed set of authored content units) or
open
(if the range of possible variations between valid storylines is so wide that the author could not even hope to anticipate all the storylines that an player might experience)
(
Grinblat et al. 2021
)
.

When an IN allows content not directly created by the IN’s author—e.g., open-ended text written by the player, or generated at runtime by an AI model—to form part of a valid storyline, the resulting story volume is necessarily open. Consequently,
AI-based

(
Treanor et al. 2015
)
or
AI-native

(
Sun et al. 2023
)
forms of IN (in which an AI model is invoked at runtime as part of the core storytelling loop) present an especially difficult form of the authoring problem. Building AI-based INs has been argued to require an indirect, experimental approach
(
Thue 2023
)
, in which AI behaviors are painstakingly tested, probed, and adjusted to iteratively assess and broaden the range of situations in which the AI can be expected to do the right thing.

2.2.
Sensemaking Tools for (AI-Based) IN Authoring

IN design was once dominated by the relatively straightforward
branching
approach to content authoring exemplified by popular tools like Twine
(
Klimas 2009
)
. The author of a branching story manually specifies all of the allowable connections between content units, with explicitly defined choice points allowing the player to select which of several possible “branches” the storyline will follow. Due to the ease and utility of visualizing branching structures
(
Green et al. 2021
)
, visualization-based tools for authoring branching stories are now widespread
(
Green et al. 2018
)
. Additionally, a wide variety of analysis tools
(
Partlan et al. 2018
;
Veloso and Prada 2021
;
Mishra et al. 2025
)
have arisen to further support author understanding of how players may experience complex branching stories.

As the ambitions of IN authors grew, the problem of “combinatorial explosion” (exponential growth in the number of content branches needed to account for a linear increase in the depth of player choice)
(
Jones 2023
)
and the desire for greater narrative responsiveness
(
Mason 2021
)
led to the adoption of increasingly modular content structures. The division of story content into more freely reorderable and recombinable units known as
storylets

(
Kreminski and Wardrip-Fruin 2018
)
became an increasingly prevalent alternative to branching narrative authoring, enabling INs to respond more dynamically to a wider range of potential player choices. However, storylet-based authoring also increased the difficulty of anticipating all the possible arrangements of narrative content that players might possibly experience.

The incompatibility of storylet-based authoring with branching storyline visualization, and the need for more powerful sensemaking tools, encouraged the development of new visualization approaches.
State space
visualizations—including the
Ice-Bound
authoring tools
(
Garbe et al. 2014
)
and
Short 2019
’s visualizations of “narrative states”
(
Short 2019
)
—attempt to map out the range of story states that might emerge as a playthrough of the story progresses; to illustrate which content units are available for display at certain points in the overall state space; and to hint at what kinds of content might be needed to improve coverage of the state space, or to steer certain kinds of playthroughs in the right directions. StoryAssembler’s
partial branching
visualizations
(
Garbe et al. 2019
)
simulate many possible playthroughs of the story, then allow authors to investigate the implicit branching structures that emerge in certain subsets of playthroughs. In a similar vein, DendryScope
(
Otto et al. 2023
)
uses playthrough simulation in concert with static analysis techniques to enable reasoning about what content units are and are not allowed to follow one another in certain subsets of the possibility space. All of these tools attempt to help authors reckon with the greater complexity of the story volumes instantiated by storylet-based IN.

Although early AI-based IN experiments like Façade
(
Mateas and Stern 2003
)
and Say Anything
(
Swanson and Gordon 2012
)
had already introduced open-ended text input by players as a possible new type of variation between storylines, it was the emergence of LLM-based IN frameworks like AI Dungeon
(
Walton 2019
)
that popularized runtime narrative content generation as a way to enable even more open-ended AI-based IN
(
Hua and Raley 2020
)
. By throwing the story volume wide open, this form of IN makes it even harder for authors to anticipate and control what players are likely to experience. Nevertheless, a few tools have appeared to help authors work with AI-based IN.
WhatELSE

(
Lu et al. 2025
)
, for instance, distinguishes canonical (“pivot”) storylines envisioned by the author from actual player-experienced (“variant”) storylines, and allows authors to sculpt the entire story volume (or “outline” of the narrative space) defined by an inferred LLM prompt. Furthermore, the Drama Llama
(
Sun et al. 2025
)
and Orchid
(
Wu et al. 2025
)
frameworks both attempt to make LLM-based IN authoring more comprehensible by updating storylet-based authoring affordances for compatibility with open-ended textual storytelling.
Elsewise
builds directly on this line of work.

2.3.
Linear Storyline Visualization

Outside of IN, storyline visualization has also seen some uptake in tools for conventional linear narrative interpretation and construction. For instance, Story Explorer
(
Kim et al. 2017
)
provided a visual toolkit for understanding the flow of time in linear stories told “out of order” (e.g., via flashbacks and flash-forwards). TaleBrush
(
Chung et al. 2022
)
made early use of an LLM to transform hand-drawn story curves (corresponding to the rising and falling fortunes of the story’s main character) into story text. Story Ribbons
(
Yeh et al. 2025
)
is an LLM-powered visual analytics workbench for analyzing how a wide range of story qualities vary over the course of a longform textual story, automatically extracting potential themes of interest but also allowing users to write in their own. And
Masson et al. 2025
’s toolkit for
visual story-writing

(
Masson et al. 2025
)
automatically establishes mappings between narrative text and a variety of structural narrative visualizations via LLM, allowing authors both to understand how their story is currently structured and to change things about their story by directly manipulating the visualizations. Broadly, all of these visualizations bear some resemblance to our approach, but do not provide support for the analysis of INs with multiple valid storylines.

2.4.
Possibility Space Visualization

Expressive range analysis
(ERA) techniques
(
Smith and Whitehead 2010
)
use visualization to assist creators of generative pipelines—e.g., game level generators—in understanding the distribution of outputs that the pipeline can produce. By generating a large number of artifacts and then characterizing each artifact according to a fixed set of automatically assessable quantitative metrics, the distribution of artifacts can be visualized as a set of two-dimensional heatmaps contrasting particular pairs of metrics. These various “slices” through the expressive range can be exhaustively summarized as a
corner plot

(
Summerville 2018
)
; used to manually or automatically tune generator parameters, in order to better align the generator’s output distribution with designer intent
(
Cook et al. 2021
)
; or even combined with trajectory visualization to show how a human interactor moves through a generator’s possibility space
(
Kreminski et al. 2022
;
Alvarez et al. 2022
)
.

With the advent of LLMs, it became possible to perform ERA-like analyses in terms of open-ended conceptual dimensions expressed as brief textual prompts, rather than manually implemented procedural evaluation functions. For example, Luminate
(
Suh et al. 2024
)
enables users to generate and visually organize a range of possible LLM writing suggestions according to a set of LLM-inferred conceptual dimensions. The worldbuilding support tool Patchview
(
Chung and Kreminski 2024
)
similarly enables users to browse and generate world elements (such as characters and storyworld locations) on a visual space of semantic concepts, but with more user agency in configuring the conceptual space and positioning elements.
Elsewise
makes use of related LLM-based ERA affordances, but with one key difference: we apply ERA techniques to the space of possible
narrative states
that may arise within an IN, rather than the range of
artifacts
potentially produced by a procedural generator or incorporated into a larger creative project.

3.
Challenges of AI-Based IN Authoring

Unlike in conventional INs, where possible storylines experienced by the audience are directly created at authoring time, storylines in AI-based INs emerge from the complex play-time interactions between the audience and the AI models in combination with author input, which could deviate from what is conceptually envisioned at authoring time. Mismatch between the two often leads to experiences that lack meaningful plot progression, in which the author’s narrative intents are not sufficiently perceived by the player.

For example, in games like AI Dungeon
(
Walton 2019
;
Hua and Raley 2020
)
, players may begin a fantasy adventure designed around a typical heroic quest in which a princess has been kidnapped by a villain, with the narrative expecting the player to rescue her by confronting the villain at a designated location in town. However, if the player enters this location and defeats the villain prematurely—before the kidnapping occurs—the narrative derails from the intended storyline, leaving the player trapped within purely AI-generated content that lacks deliberate plot progression. To address such scenarios, authors could either guide players toward more narratively relevant actions or design additional branching storylines that accommodate these deviations while maintaining narrative coherence. Both solutions require authors to anticipate potential player actions, a significantly more challenging task than in conventional INs, where player choices are constrained to predetermined options.

To help authors anticipate audience experienced storylines, we distinguish between
intended narrative
(conceptual storylines envisioned by the author) and
experienced narrative
(concrete storylines experienced by the audience)
(
Lu et al. 2025
)
. Intended narratives are often in the form of high-level specifications of desired narrative content possibly conditioned on different narrative states to form branching storylines. Experienced narratives are concrete narrative content experienced by players in actual playthroughs. During gameplay, AI-based IN systems synthesize the author’s specification with player input to generate narrative content presented to the player (e.g., combining them into a prompt for an LLM model). Since player input is unavailable at authoring time, the potential mismatch between the author’s expectation of the player’s action and the actual player’s action often leads to ineffective
control
of the narrative space, such that the intended narrative is only partially reflected in the experienced narrative—or sometimes missed entirely.

Most existing authoring and analysis tools for AI-based INs, such as AI Dungeon
(
Walton 2019
)
, Inworld.ai
(
Inworld 2023
)
,
WhatIf

(
Mishra et al. 2025
)
, Drama Llama
(
Sun et al. 2025
)
, and
Orchid

(
Wu et al. 2025
)
, primarily focus on the authoring and sensemaking of intended narrative content. These tools provide limited—if any—support for helping authors understand the experienced narratives that emerge during actual gameplay. Most provide only basic playtesting capabilities, resulting in highly inefficient
exploration
of the narrative possibility space. Meanwhile, making sense of narrative possibility space presents significant challenges due to its abstract nature: each storyline instance within this space already constitutes a complex object residing in high-dimensional semantic space. Furthermore, authors have diverse analytical needs, typically focusing on narrative aspects that align closely with their specific storytelling approaches and objectives, as well as the content of the story.

This work aims to design authoring tools for AI-based interactive narratives (INs) that provide enhanced control and exploration assistance. We approach this by focusing on the experienced narrative and supporting systematic perception and configurable sensemaking of the possibility space of experienced narratives. We propose the following design goals for such authoring tools:

•

DG1. Player Perspective.
The tool should incorporate player perspectives in the authoring process to make authors aware of potential discrepancies between intended and experienced narrative, and to support the author in improving the alignment of actual experience with intended experience.

•

DG2. Comprehensiveness
To be more comprehensive than simple playtesting, the system should assist authors to
systematically
perceive the possibilities residing in the space of experienced narrative, in helping the author better anticipate the possible storylines a player could experience.

•

DG3. Configurability.
The system should provide customizable affordances to assist authors in sensemaking of the experienced narrative possibility space tailored to their specific storytelling interests and the content of the story.

4.
Bundled Storylines

4.1.
Branching Storylines and Story Volumes

Since IN authors are crafting possibility spaces rather than individual instances of stories, visualization of the possibility space has always been an essential affordance of an IN authoring tool. The possibility space of conventional INs is typically represented as flowchart-like branching storylines
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
. Branching storyline structure represents a narrative possibility space as a graph, where nodes represent different narrative states, and edges represent player actions that can cause a transition from one narrative state to another. The paths in the graph correspond to possible stories a player could experience in a playthrough. Branching storyline structure clearly communicates the
internal dynamics
of the storyworld by specifying all possible events that could happen and their effects on narrative states, with a state machine-like structure. Such dynamics define what is considered narratively meaningful, enabling characterization of similarities and differences between different storylines and direct manipulation of the narrative possibility space.

In AI-based INs, storylines experienced by the players are emergent narratives
(
Aylett 1999
)
resulting from the interaction between the author, the player, and the AI algorithm. The complexity of the AI algorithm, especially the blackbox nature of LLM models, makes it challenging to adopt an interpretable representation of the narrative structure. The space of possible stories that could arise from an emergent narrative system has been characterized as a “story volume”
(
Grinblat 2017
)
, a concept originally established in the context of procedural content generation, to contrast with the idea of single fixed “storylines” as in conventional pre-authored INs, emphasizing the plurality of storylines in a group with no single canonical version. Although "Story volume" captures the emergent nature of storylines from AI-based INs, it abstracts away a structured representation that enables systematic perception and manipulation of the narrative possibility space.

4.2.
Bundled Storyline Visualization for Story Volumes

Although explicitly defined branching storylines are no longer the underlying mechanism for generating narrative instances in AI-based IN systems, a branching visual representation could still serve as a familiar and powerful tool to aid sensemaking of story volumes. We introduce the concept of
Bundled Storylines
, in order to capture both the emergent nature of experienced narrative and the internal dynamics of the storyworld, which help with author’s perception and sensemaking of a narrative possibility space.

An experienced storyline is a sequence of events (“fabula” instead of “sjuzhet”
(
Bal 2009
)
) dynamically constructed by the interactions between the player and the non-player characters’ actions in the storyworld. The event sequence describes how the storyworld changes over time
(
Riedl and Young 2010
)
. Different storylines can thus be viewed as different trajectories in the possibility space
S
of all possible states of the storyworld.

We consequently view an
experienced storyline

T_{i}
as an ordered sequence of
narrative states

\langle s^{i}_{1},s^{i}_{2},\dots,s^{i}_{{\bf t}_{i}}\rangle

({\bf t}_{i}>0)
. Given a set
{\bf T}
of experienced storylines, we assume a global timeline that applies to all the storylines, so that for any
i\neq j
,
s^{i}_{t}
and
s^{j}_{t}
can be meaningfully compared as they are different narrative states at the same timestep. We use
S_{\bf T}
to denote the set of all narrative states from all the storylines in
{\bf T}
. Given a set of
narrative dimensions
representing relevant plot conditions (e.g.,
tension level
,
friendship between two characters
,
whether Aladdin has obtained the magic lamp
, etc.), a possibility space of narrative states can be constructed and used to position each narrative state relative to others based on their value on these dimensions. The experienced storylines (
{\bf T}
) can thus be comprehensively compared as trajectories in this space.

An experienced storyline is represented in different forms, such as a script, a dialog/action history, a sequence of images or even a video. We assume that we can split the representation of a storyline into a sequence of fragments
\langle o^{i}_{1},o^{i}_{2},\dots,o^{i}_{{\bf t}_{i}}\rangle
so that each
o^{i}_{t}
is part of the storyline representation and is describing the latent underlying narrative state
s^{i}_{t}
. We denote the set of all narrative space representations as
O
. Given a narrative dimension
d
(whose value range denoted by
dom(d)
), we assume a function
f_{d}(\cdot):O\rightarrow dom(d)
exists to extract the value of
d
from any narrative state representation.

Although the notion of bundled storylines is in theory generalizable to other story representations, in this paper we focus on textual representation of stories, so that each
o^{i}_{t}
is a text fragment, and the dimension extraction functions
f_{d}(\cdot)
can be implemented with a combination of language models and parameterized prompts. Since we only have access to the textual representations rather than their underlying narrative states, in the rest of the paper we simply use the term “narrative state” to refer to the text representations. We also only consider finite and discrete value range for narrative dimensions.

Bundled Storyline as a Timeline of Single Narrative Dimension (“1D BSVs”)

Given a single narrative dimension
d
, we construct a graph
G_{d}
representing a branching storyline structure as follows:

•

Narrative states that share the same value of
d
at the same timestep are grouped together as a node.
For each
v\in dom(d)
and
t\in\{1,\dots,t_{max}\}
(
t_{max}
represents the maximum timestep),
G_{d}
contains a node corresponding to the set of narrative state representations
O^{v}_{t}=\{o^{i}_{t}\in O_{\bf T}\mid f_{d}(o^{i}_{t})=v\}
, unless the set is empty;

•

Edges represent possible “1-step” transitions between different values
There is an edge from node
O^{v_{1}}_{t}
to
O^{v_{2}}_{t+1}
if there exists
o^{i}_{t}
and
o^{i}_{t+1}
such that
o^{i}_{t}\in O^{v_{1}}_{t}
and
o^{i}_{t+1}\in O^{v_{2}}_{t+1}
.

Intuitively, we “bundle” together all the narrative states at the same timestep sharing the same value in dimension
d
to construct the nodes in the graph, and merging temporal connections sharing both starting and ending bundles. This gives us a
Bundled Storylines
graph of the experienced storylines relative to the dimension
d
.

In our implementation, we use dots of different colors to represent narrative states from different storylines. For each bundled storylines graph, we arrange the dots in a matrix whose columns correspond to the values of dimension
d
(
dom(d)
), and rows correspond to each timestep (
\{1,\dots,t_{max}\}
) - so that dots representing elements of
S^{v}_{t}
are positioned at the row representing timestep
t
and the column representing value
v
of dimension
d
(Fig.
2
(a)). We call this visualization of a single bundled storylines graph a
Bundled Storyline Visualization
, or a (1D) BSV.

4.2.2.
Bundled Storyline with Multiple Narrative Dimensions (“2D BSVs”)

It is theoretically possible to extend the definition of bundled storyline to multiple narrative dimensions. Given a subset of dimensions
D=\{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\}
, the graph
G_{D}
representing a branching storyline structure is constructed as follows:

•

Narrative states that share the same value combination at the same timestep are grouped together as a node.
For each distinct value combination
{\bf v}=v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
(
v_{1}\in dom(d_{1}),\dots,v_{n}\in dom(d_{n})
) and
t\in\{1,\dots,t_{max}\}
,
G_{D}
contains a node corresponding to the set of narrative state representations
O^{\bf v}_{t}=\{o^{i}_{t}\in{\bf O_{T}}\mid f_{d_{1}}(o^{i}_{t})=v_{1},\dots,f_{d_{n}}(o^{i}_{t})=v_{n}\}
, unless the set is empty;

•

Edges represent possible “1-step” transitions between different value combinations.
There is an edge from node
O^{{\bf v}_{1}}_{t}
and
O^{{\bf v}_{2}}_{t+1}
if there exists
o^{i}_{t}
and
o^{i}_{t+1}
such that
o^{i}_{t}\in O^{{\bf v}_{1}}_{t}
and
o^{i}_{t+1}\in O^{{\bf v}_{2}}_{t+1}
.

Intuitively, all narrative states sharing the same value combination on the dimensions are indistinguishable in this particular subspace, forming a “bundle” of narrative states. We call
G_{D}
a Bundled Storyline graph of the experienced storylines relative to the dimension set
D
.

To visualize a bundled storylines graph with multiple narrative dimensions (a
multi-dimensional Bundled Storyline Visualization
), we arrange the dots in a (multi-dimensional) matrix defined by the possible values of the dimensions (Fig.
2
(b)). Dots are grouped at the same position of the matrix if they all share the same dimension value combination corresponding
to the matrix position, regardless of difference on other aspects. We also restrict the number or narrative dimensions to be no more than
2
, due to the visual complexity of higher dimensional matrices.
Conceptually, we can add an additional dimension representing timestep to the matrix to include temporal information in the same view. To retain a 2D visualization, we follow the time flattening technique
(
Bach et al. 2014
)
, and use the size of the dots represent temporal order of the
narrative states (smaller ones are more towards the beginning of the storyline). Admittedly, it is difficult to visually distinguish differences in sizes to quickly get precise temporal relations, but the focus of such 2D BSV views is to show the correlation between multiple narrative dimensions, rather than the temporal progression of a single narrative dimension. We will describe in Section
4.3.2
different ways to incorporate temporal information when cross-referencing multiple narrative dimensions.

Assuming the storylines are representative, we can obtain insights such as: proactive duckling behavior tends to lead to better advantage against goose, while passive duckling behavior tends to lead to worse advantage.

Similar to branching storylines for conventional IN, the bundled storyline representation provides a comprehensive understanding of possible transitions between value combinations of the selected dimensions approximated from the given set of storylines, in a state machine like structure (DG2). When abstracting away each graph node’s internal structure, bundled storyline visualization resembles a branching storyline visualization. However, dynamically deriving the nodes from arbitrary storylines and narrative dimensions allows the branches to emerge from actual playthroughs rather than being predefined by the IN author (DG1).

4.3.
Bundled Storyline Affordances

The previous section defines Bundled Storylines. In this section we explain where the narrative dimensions come from and how they collectively assists perception and sensemaking of the narrative possibility space. We design affordances of Bundled Storylines in support of configurable sensemaking (DG2, DG3), organized in two categories:
dimension extraction
and
dimension crossreferencing
.

4.3.1.
Dimension Extraction

The goal of Bundled Storyline Visualization is to bridge authors’ narrative vision and players’ narrative experience—specifically, to assist the author in understanding the emergent plot, reflecting misalignments between their narrative vision and current audience experience, and finding guidance for further refinement of the narrative space to steer expereinced storylines towards narratively meaningful directions. To this end, the narrative dimensions should be derived from both the author’s narrative vision and player’s narrative experience, with the relative emphasis depending on the authoring approach and analytical objectives. We provide three dimension extraction mechanisms that enable configurable weighting of author vision versus player experience (DG3).

Author vision only
In this case, we prompt the user to add a customized dimension by entering a name denoting the dimension (e.g., “
duckling_courage
”), a line of text describing what this dimension is about (e.g. “
how courageous the duckling feels
”), and the values of the dimension (e.g., “
not_courageous
,
partially
,
courageous
”).

Player experience only
In this case, both the narrative dimension and its values are generated automatically by analyzing the dataset of player experienced storylines through
concept induction

(
Lam et al. 2024
)
, a computational process that produces interpretable high-level concepts from unstructured text (narrative state representations). Since dimensions created in this way are fully derived from the given dataset of player experienced storylines, we also call them “data-derived dimensions” in the rest of the paper.

Mixed
In this case, the narrative dimension and its values are collaboratively defined by both player experienced storylines, and the author. For example, the author names and describes the dimension, then the system extracts categorical values for this dimension with concept induction, or the system extracts narrative dimensions by analyzing the narrative states, then the user defines the categorical values.

Regardless of the dimension
extraction mechanisms, once the dimension and its values are in place, the system will use an LLM model to classify the narrative states using the values of the dimension as labels, to construct the BSV view of this narrative dimension. Note that when the values of the dimensions were generated from experienced storylines, it is usually guaranteed that every value applies to at least one experienced narrative states due to the clustering mechanism. This is not the case when the values are user-defined. For example, it may turn out that the duckling has never felt courageous in any of the playthrough data, so that no narrative state fall into the
courageous
category. However, the dimension value would still be useful if the author desires to refine the game to nudge the duckling towards being more courageous.

The user gets a holistic understanding of the narrative possibility space not from single Bundled Storyline Visulization, but a collection of BSVs presenting information from different aspects (we call this collection a “BSV canvas”). 5.1.
Elsewise
Interactive Stories

The specific type of AI-based IN that a user can create with
Elsewise
is implemented with an LLM driving a series of conversational interactions with a player following specifications from the author about the intended narrative experience. During gameplay, the LLM takes the role of a game master (as in tabletop role-playing games like
Dungeons & Dragons

(
Team et al. 2014
)
), acting as a proxy for the author. We aim to capture the most prominent types of author input seen in existing AI-based IN systems
(
Walton 2019
;
Inworld 2023
;
Sun et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Lu et al. 2025
)
with minimal authorial constructs, resulting in the following two parts of the author specification:

•

A description of the
storyworld
that serves as the long-term context of the interactive experience, which consists of a world description and a set of characters each with a description, all in natural language. One character is marked as the protagonist and is intended to be role-played by the player.

•

A list of
rules
that regulate the playtime behavior of the LLM game master. A rule is defined by a
condition
and an
effect
, both in free-form natural language sentences. As a proxy of the author, the LLM game master is designed to steer the experienced plot towards the effect when the condition is met by the current narrative state.

An instance of the above specification defines a playable interactive story. During gameplay, the LLM game master and the player take turns to contribute to the unfolding story. The LLM story master can generate both narration text and non-protagonist characters’ actions and dialogues, while the player only enters actions and dialogues for the protagonist character. A narrative state in this context is defined as a round of the game (where the game master and the player both take a turn), and contains all the conversation history up until the end of this round. At the end of each round, the system checks if the condition of any rule is met by the current narrative state, which triggers the execution of the effect of the rule.

The next section describes the authoring interface assisting the author in crafting the interactive story specifications.

5.3.
Example Workflow

In this section, we present an example workflow to demonstrate the features described above. Alice is an interactive narrative writer who specializes in designing educational narrative games for children around 10 years old. Alice wants to create an interactive story based on a fairy tale setting, aiming to teach kids that “small act of courage can change everything.” She opens
Elsewise
and first types in a world setting and defines a few characters (Fig
6
(G)). In the story, the player plays as the duckling, who lives in a pond dominated by a bullying goose hoarding all the resources. The third character is the duckling’s mother, who has a protective nature. Alice intends to convey the message by ensuring that player’s courageous behavior leads to a happy ending, so she adds a rule saying “
if the duckling stands up to the goose, all the ducks will join and fight together and reclaim the resources that belong to them.
” Alice thinks this well communicates the message, so she prompts the system to generate the first batch of simulated playthroughs.

First, Alice notices that the rule she added is rarely triggered in the playthroughs, indicating that the player does not tend to stand up to the goose. She suspects the reason is that the dialogue generated for the duck Mom tends to discourage the duckling from standing up based on the character description. To validate this hypothesis, she creates a narrative dimension called “
whether_the_duck_mom_discourages_the_duckling
”.
The resulting BSV indicates this is the case for almost every narrative state. Alice consequently updates the character description of the duck Mon to make her more encouraging.

Alice also prompts the system to extract three key dimensions that help differentiate the playthroughs from each other. The system returns “
duckling_behavior
”, “
goose_aggression_behavior
” and “
location
”. Although lacking significant plot progression, Alice notices one common pattern across most playthroughs: the goose’s aggression behavior usually evolves from threatening to attacking as the story unfolds. This inspires Alice to add another rule to nudge the player towards more courageous behavior: “
if the goose starts attacking, the duck Mom will get injured, and the duckling will become angry, thinking about confronting the goose.
”

Being more satisfied with the story, Alice generates a new batch of simulated playthroughs. Alice notices this batch of playthroughs has a significantly better rule triggering rate. To further investigate the effectiveness of communicating the message, Alice creates two BSVs “
duckling_courage
” and “
duckling_happiness
”. To understand whether these two dimensions are correlated in a meaningful way, Alice combines the two views into a 2D BSV view. The combined view indicates that almost all narrative states where the duckling is courageous lead to a happy narrative state towards the end, which is intended. However, the duckling could also become happy when they have not shown any courageous behavior. By looking at the data-derived dimension “
location
”, Alice realizes that the duckling could escape and find another pond full of resources. To eliminate such undesired storylines, Alice adds a new rule to restrict the story to the pond location “
if the duckling attempts to leave the pond, they will realize that they have to stay at the pond.
” Alice initiates another playthrough simulation. By comparing the narrative state distribution across all these iterations, Alice can clearly see that the modification on the story specification she made helps her make progress in better communication of the message (Fig.
1
, bottom).

7.
Discussion

Our findings suggest that authoring AI-based Interactive Narratives presents unique challenges compared to authoring conventional INs, particularly regarding perception, control, and exploration within the narrative possibility space. While the Bundled Storyline concept and system
Elsewise
represent an initial approach to addressing those challenges, it is valuable to continue the exploration of new tools and paradigms to assist authoring AI-based INs. This section discusses implications from our findings, as well as limitations and possible future directions.

7.1.
The challenge of shaping an unknown possibility space

Interactive narrative authoring presents inherent challenges, as the final narrative experience remains incomplete until audience interaction occurs—yet the audience is typically absent during the authoring process. Authors must therefore work under assumptions about potential audience behaviors. While AI-based interactive narratives offer exciting possibilities for new media forms, the blackbox nature of current AI methods introduces additional uncertainty into the co-creation formula. In our study, both
Elsewise
and baseline received relatively high rating on required mental effort in completing the authoring task (Fig.
9
(c)), as P4 mentioned (“
the mindset required for authoring this kind of IN (AI-based INs) is very different from authoring traditional INs. I’m still adapting to this mindset.
”) The difficulty stems partly from the fundamental nature of the authoring task itself.

Conventional narrative design involves
additively
creating content to expand the possibility space. In contrast, AI-based interactive narrative design requires authors to
subtractively
eliminate undesired content from an initially boundless possibility space. (P4: “
I start to think about restricting the possibilities
”). This requires the author to first have some idea of “what is already there” to even start authoring. P7 explained their thought process when authoring with the baseline “
because I don’t know the specific possibilities, I need to be very comprehensive, I basically first created an action schema in my head for the characters and enumerate one by one all the possible combinations
”. This “action schema” functions as a self-imposed protocol, enabling authors to measure their progress while establishing boundaries of “authorial ownership” between their authored territory and the “wilds” governed entirely by the LLM and player interactions.

Future AI-based IN authoring tools could help addressing the challenges in the following ways:

•

Presenting the narrative possibility space in a perceivable and navigable way.
The concept of bundled storylines is one way to help the author organize the possibilities in the space and imagine the space in a structured manner. However, we also envision other ways of helping the authors understand “what’s already there” before they can effectively subtract or constrain the possibility space. For example, an authoring tool could answer queries probing certain aspects of the possibility space by simulating how the story could unfold (e.g.,“show me 10 different ways the character conversation would escalate into conflict”).

•

Assisting the author in effectively shaping the narrative possibility space.
Our system design does not provide authors with a direct means of controlling the narrative possibility space. When the author sees player-experienced storylines falling into undesired regions of the narrative space, an authoring tool could also help authors herd future similar storylines out of the region or toward a more desired region by generatively suggesting possible ways to revise the author specification.

•

Providing structure and framework for establishing authorial boundaries.
Instead of completely open-ended prompts, the authoring tool could provide more structured templates to help the author specify their mental model of the narrative experience. An authoring tool could also visually distinguish between “authored” and “AI-generated” territory in presenting the narrative possibility space to help the author track the boundary and progress.

•

Guiding authors into new mindsets and reducing cognitive load.
Rather then presenting the authors with a blank canvas, a tool could help structure the authoring process in different phases, for example establishing storyworld settings, exploring possibilities, designing main story events, progressive constraint building and debugging, etc. The tool could automatically detect less “constrained” spaces and generate questions to guide the author in specifying their desired narrative. The workflow could be structured in way where authors gradually increase narrative complexity while validating their constraints at each stage, rather than forcing them to define everything upfront.

7.2.
Playfulness in mixed-initiative design

The reported high enjoyment rating (Fig.
9
(b)) and quotes from participants indicates a perceived
playful

(
Liapis et al. 2023
)
experience when authoring with
Elsewise
. As a mixed-initiative design process, the playfulness helps sustain the author’s motivation when they do not have a full sense of ownership on the final artefact.

The following aspects of our system design shaped the playful experience and can be further explored by future authoring tools for AI-based INs:

•

Challenge from partial control
Some participants found the process engaging because of the reduced control (P10: “
this is fun because you are passively shaping the space rather than having complete control - it’s like a car racing game - you have some level of control of the car, but still restricted by the terrain and other physical factors.
”). This is aligned with existing findings indicating a balanced level of control results in better engagement such as flow theory
(
Czikszentmihalyi 1990
)
.

•

Quick feedback loop
As mentioned in Sec.
6.5.2
, participants are motivated for more design iterations by the quick feedback with simulated playthroughs, and they reported to look forward to seeing how LLM game master and the simulated players respond to their authoring effort. The immediate response gives a strong sense of agency in the authoring process.

•

Exploration
As mentioned in Sec.
6.5.3
, exploring the narrative possibility space to uncover unexpected storylines inspires the authors, and the uncovered value combinations of narrative dimensions trigger intrinsic curiosity about what-if scenarios.

7.3.
Player agency in LLM-based interactive narratives

A primary advantage of LLM-based interactive narratives is their capacity to provide virtually unlimited player agency. Interestingly, when we ask our participants to evaluate the player agency offered by their created interactive stories (Fig.
9
(a)), their assessments were more moderate than theoretical expectations might suggest (Med: 4, across both authoring conditions). Despite the system’s free-form input capabilities and dynamic response generation, participants remained cautious in attributing high levels of genuine player agency to their narratives. P4 directly pointed out “
the freedom a chatbot gives to a player doesn’t necessarily equal to player’s freedom in the game. In a lot of those games, player can play endlessly, but there is no plot progression.
”

When asked about reasons behind their ratings, P7, P8, and P10 also acknowledged that their ratings are relative to the intended storylines they have authored. P10 articulated that when considering this question, they mentally constructed a branching storyline structure based on their authored rules and then evaluated how much freedom this structure offered players. P2 responded with a very low score on player agency (2), saying that “
my story is completely linear
”. Their story contains one key plot event that is almost guaranteed to happen regardless of player actions. They appeared to view the occurrence of this key plot event as the only meaningful direction for plot progression.

These findings align with existing discussions on the distinction between technical affordances and genuine player agency—the recognition that permitting actions within a system does not necessarily confer meaningful agency if those actions lack support from the underlying computational model (
(
Wardrip-Fruin et al. 2009
)
). In conventional interactive narratives, authors usually possess comprehensive understanding of the computational model, enabling them to deliberately shape player agency within their designs. AI-based interactive narratives present a fundamentally different situation: the opaque nature of the underlying computational model obscures this comprehension not only from players, but also authors. Consequently, authors cannot fully determine what constitutes "meaningfully supported" actions, and their conceptual models of agency may diverge significantly from player experience—particularly because emergent interactions between players and the system now constitute a substantial source of perceived meaningfulness. This divergence raises important questions regarding potential disparities in how authors and players conceptualize agency in AI-based interactive narratives and suggests the need for authoring tools that foreground player perspectives on agency affordances and reconcile both parties’ conceptual models of meaningfulness within the game.

7.4.
Towards design support for general AI agents

While this paper discusses the concept of bundled storylines within a digital game context, the framework is theoretically applicable to any interactive medium employing just-in-time response generation. Designers of such systems face the challenge of perceiving, controlling and exploring the possibility space of user experience in interacting with the system. This encompasses AI agents customized for audience interaction and information exchange. In this work, the AI agent functions as a game master, instantiating the game author’s narrative vision in response to specific player actions. More broadly, such agents might serve as virtual customer assistants, virtual therapists, or even generative user interfaces.

Although it is conceptually possible to integrate a bundled storylines based possibility space visualizer to a general AI agent builder such as
(
OpenAI [n. d.]
;
n8n [n. d.]
)
, several challenges emerge: identifying and leveraging meaningful semantic dimensions, designing effective visualization approaches, and adapting control affordances to specific application domains. Task-driven applications present particular difficulties, including representing functional behavior as semantic dimensions, integrating explicit system states with semantic representations, distinguishing between violations of hard constraints and deviations from soft constraints, and accurately simulating user behaviors using real-world data. We hope our initial investigation in the contect of digital games paves the way for exploration of design support for general AI agents via possibility space visualization.

8.
Limitation

We summarize the major limitations of our work as follows:

Player Experience

Due to the limitations in time and resources, this work has primarily focused on the author’s experience. The involvement of the player has been restricted to LLM simulated players. It would be valuable to collect actual human players’ playthrough data and feedback to gain more accurate insights on various aspects such as effectiveness in communicating narrative intents and player perceived in-game agency.

Study Design

To ensure each study session finishes in time, we had to restrict the scale of the stories and the amount of playthrough data to be much smaller than in most real-world use cases. A longitudinal study that investigates how authors would use the system in more realistic projects can be future work. Most of our study participants have experience in authoring INs. Evaluating the system with more diverse groups of participants, especially people with limited AI and IN experience, would help us better understand the usability and generalizability of our system.

Interface Design

Our interface design could be further improved, especially to reduce the visual complexity of the 2D BSVs. Additional functionalities could also greatly improve the usability of the system, such as allowing user-defined player profiles and customizable granularity of dimensions and their value when extracting from playthrough data. Extending our framework to non-categorical dimension values would also be an interesting and meaningful future work.

Technical Method

Since the focus of this work lies in novel interface design, we implemented a simplified version of concept induction
(
Lam et al. 2024
)
for dimension extraction. We did not provide any technical evaluation of our implementation, nor did we explore different strategies for dimension extraction, such as different clustering strategies to examine how the granularity of extracted concepts affect author’s workflow and their created story specifications.

9.
Conclusion

We introduced the novel concept of Bundled Storylines to facilitate comprehensive perception and sensemaking of experienced storylines for AI-based interactive narratives. We implemented the Bundled Storyline framework in the authoring tool
Elsewise
, allowing IN authors to intuitively shape the narrative possibility space defined by user-configurable dimensions. Our user study validated the effectiveness of our approach in helping authors to anticipate possible player-experienced storylines, thereby aiding them in controlling and exploring the narrative possibility space. We believe our work advances AI-bridged IN authoring workflows towards better control and balance between authorial intent and player agency.
