Interfacial spin-order engineering mitigates the permeability-loss trade-off in amorphous soft magnetic composites
Authors: Huaping Ding, Shengxiang Wang, Jie Chen, Fei Hu, Liliang Shao, Haibo Ke, Pan Gong, Congrui Yang, Jiyu Xu, Yuqiang Yan, Jing Zhou, Xing Tong, Xinyun Wang, Weihua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh magnetic permeability and low power loss are critical requirements for amorphous soft magnetic composites in high-frequency electronics. They are also typically mutually exclusive; low power loss requires insulating interfaces between magnetic particles, but conventional insulating interfaces cause magnetic dilution and decoupling, reducing permeability. Here, we introduce an interfacial spin-order engineering strategy that departs from traditional ferromagnetic interface solutions. By exploiting a thermally-electrically activated oxygen migration process, we construct a gradient oxygen-deficient functional layer between heterogeneous interfaces, enabling atomic-scale control of interfacial spin configurations. This engineered interface leads to a 150% increase in magnetic permeability and a 76% reduction in power loss, outperforming state-of-the-art counterparts. Neutron dark-field imaging provides direct, bulk-sensitive evidence that oxygen-defect-mediated spin ordering induces enlarged magnetic domains with intergranular magnetic connectivity. Mechanistically, defect-mediated super-exchange interactions reverse the spin orientation of Ce and Fe atoms at the heterointerface, transforming disordered or antiferromagnetic alignment into ferromagnetic coupling and thereby eliminating magnetic coupling barriers across particles. Our work demonstrates interfacial spin-ordering as a generalizable design principle, mitigating the long-standing permeability-loss trade-off.Subject terms: Magnetic properties and materials, Ferromagnetism

Introduction

Soft magnetic composites (SMCs) serve as essential functional materials in high-frequency electronics, particularly for energy storage, power conversion, and signal filtering applications1,2. Iron-based amorphous soft magnetic composites (ASMCs) represent a prominent class of SMCs distinguished by their unique combination of high saturation magnetization and low coercivity, enabling rapid magnetic flux switching with minimal hysteretic energy dissipation3,4. High magnetic permeability (μ) facilitates efficient magnetic flux conduction, permitting optimized magnetic circuit design and enhanced electromagnetic compatibility in miniaturized devices3; while low core losses (Pcv), particularly at elevated frequencies, significantly reduce operational temperature rise and improve device longevity5,6. However, the concurrent realization of both high μ and low Pcv remains a fundamental materials challenge. Current strategies to suppress eddy-current losses primarily rely on the formation of insulating interparticle boundaries through surface coating technologies2,3. Although these approaches effectively reduce eddy-current losses, they introduce a fundamental trade-off: the insulating interfaces, typically composed of non-magnetic or weakly magnetic materials, inevitably introduce magnetic dilution effects and localized demagnetizing fields that impede domain wall motion5,7. Consequently, these effects ultimately lead to a deterioration in the magnetic permeability. This intrinsic compromise between loss suppression and permeability maintenance forms the thorny materials science dilemma in ASMC development.

The interfacial magnetism dependence of permeability posits that magnetic interfaces exhibit slower permeability decline with increasing interface content compared to non-magnetic counterparts8, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. In ASMCs, amorphous powder particles are separated by insulating interfaces that confine distinct magnetic domains. Ferromagnetic interfaces mitigate magnetic domain pinning and optimize magnetization pathways, thereby improving overall permeability9. This understanding has driven contemporary interface design toward ferromagnetic or ferrimagnetic materials, such as Fe3O410, TiO211, and NiZn ferrite12, which have demonstrated efficacy in improving the magnetic performance of ASMCs. However, a fundamental compromise persists: while oxide-based insulating interfaces effectively suppress eddy current losses, they inherently undermine permeability. This trade-off stems from the fundamental conflict between strong magnetism and electrical insulation in metal oxides13. Classical band theory dictates that high electrical resistivity requires a wide band gap, which represents the minimum energy for electron excitation from the valence to the conduction band14. A larger band gap restricts valence electrons, impeding their thermal excitation into free electrons via thermal excitation. While beneficial for insulation, this property conflicts with the prerequisites for robust magnetism13. According to the Pauli exclusion principle, magnetic moments primarily arise from unpaired electrons, while paired electrons with antiparallel spins cancel magnetic contributions15. Consequently, interface materials necessitate a sufficiently wide band gap to ensure electrical insulation, which concomitantly diminishes the population of unpaired electrons, leading to weak magnetism. This electronic structure incompatibility creates an unavoidable permeability-loss trade-off in ASMCs, rendering conventional interface material substitution ineffective for breakthrough improvements. Therefore, recent interface engineering attempts have shifted away from the traditional pursuit of reconciling insulation and magnetism within a single interface material toward the construction of architected interfaces that enable functional decoupling and synergistic performance16–18. For example, Li et al. propose a bulk/interface insulation strategy to synthesize FeSiAl: Sn/Al2O3 SMCs via mutual atom diffusion at 700 °C19. The formation of FeSiAl: Sn can not only suppress intra-eddy loss by enhancing electrical resistivity, but also reduce hysteresis loss by mitigating lattice distortion. Furthermore, Li et al. utilized TiO2 to form an Al2O3 insulation layer in FeSiAl SMCs via a confined solid-state reaction at 730 °C, which improves homogeneity and lattice matching7. This work innovatively improves electrical insulation to suppress eddy currents while introducing ferromagnetism in oxygen-deficient TiO2. Despite these advances, current studies predominantly rely on high-temperature in-situ chemical reactions to form uniform and complete electrical insulating layers for optimizing resistivity and coercivity. Such processing temperatures, however, exceed the crystallization thresholds of most common Fe-based amorphous soft magnetic alloys, severely limiting their applicability to metastable ASMCs. Consequently, the development of low-temperature strategies capable of constructing magnetically coupled functional interfaces in metastable ASMC systems remains a critical and unresolved challenge.

In this work, we propose an interface spin-order engineering strategy that leverages oxygen-defect modulation to tailor spin orders and magnetic exchange interactions at the atomic scale, as illustrated in Fig. 1b20. Given the fact that the weak-magnetic oxides lead to interfacial magnetic decoupling and discontinuity, the interface becomes the primary source of pinned magnetic domain walls, which degrade permeability. Rather than searching for a single material possessing both insulation and strong magnetism, we decouple these requirements by atomically tailoring the interfacial spin order through oxygen vacancy modulation. This approach constructs a functional gradient layer that establishes a smooth transition in spin configurations between the insulating oxide and the ferromagnetic amorphous matrix, thereby restoring magnetic connectivity across the interface (Fig. 1b). With this goal in mind, we aim to establish a magnetically coupled interface characterized by oxygen gradients. We operationalize this strategy using non-magnetic CeO₂ as the insulating phase, leveraging its propensity for oxygen vacancy formation under the localized high temperatures generated during spark plasma sintering (SPS)21,22. The thermally-electrically activated process controllably extracts oxygen from CeO₂, generating a gradient of oxygen vacancies in the functional layer. These vacancies, in turn, donate electrons that populate localized Ce4f or Fe3d orbitals, promoting long-range spin ordering at heterogeneous interfaces20,23. This defect-mediated magnetism is integrated with the amorphous matrix through a compositionally graded interface. TiO2 and Fe3O4 were selected as comparative benchmarks: TiO2 exemplifies conventional ferromagnetic interface solutions based on confined solid-state reactions, while Fe3O4 represents a standard ferromagnetic insulator. The interfacial microstructure, magnetic domain architecture, and bulk performance are interrogated using spherical aberration-corrected transmission electron microscopy, quantitative neutron dark-field imaging (DFI) based on the modified neutron grating interferometry (nGI), and differential phase contrast (DPC). Furthermore, we unveil the atomic-scale mechanism through density functional theory (DFT) calculations, which reveal a defect-mediated magnetic proximity effect that aligns spins across the heterointerface. This work establishes interfacial spin-order engineering as a design route, moving beyond material selection to active electronic-structure design for overcoming long-standing functional trade-offs in ASMCs.

Results and discussion

Construction of spin-ordering heterointerfaces

The intentional introduction of oxygen vacancies in metal oxides has emerged as a powerful strategy for tailoring functional properties, particularly in magnetic and catalytic applications24. A typical example is the oxygen vacancy-mediated transition of CeO₂ from non-magnetic to weak-magnetic23. While thermal annealing remains the most prevalent method for creating oxygen vacancies, recent advances25,26 demonstrate that electric field-assisted approaches can precisely control vacancy distribution by reducing both formation energy and diffusion barriers27. In this work, we employed SPS to construct oxygen-deficient interfaces through elemental diffusion between the amorphous matrix and oxide precursors (Fig. 1c). As illustrated in Fig. 1c, this process leverages two synergistic effects: (i) high-energy pulsed electrical currents traverse the amorphous powders, generating localized Joule heating at particle contact necks21,28; (ii) non-thermal plasma activation from high-current pulses. This dual mechanism enables precise interface modulations while preserving the bulk amorphous structure. The effectiveness of this method in inducing oxygen vacancies within CeO2 has been confirmed by the EPR-quantified increase in oxygen vacancy concentration (Fig. S1a) and the XPS-quantified transformation of Ce4+ to Ce3+ ions20 (Fig. S1b–d). For details, please refer to Extended Note 2 in the Supplementary Information.

The choice of oxide precursors, TiO₂, CeO₂, and Fe₃O₄ (Fig. S2d–f), was guided by their distinct band gaps (2.6 eV, 1.7 eV, and 0 eV, as shown in Fig. S14), which serve as proxies for electrical resistivity29,30. XRD patterns (Fig. S2a) confirmed the rutile-type, fluorite-type cubic, and inverse spinel structures of TiO₂, CeO₂, and Fe₃O₄, respectively. Magnetic characterization (Fig. S2b, c) revealed non-magnetic behavior for TiO2 and CeO2, while Fe3O4 displayed ferrimagnetism with a saturation magnetization of ~65 A·m2/kg, attributable to electron hopping between Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ at octahedral sites10,31. Higher-resistivity (wider bandgap) oxides like TiO2 exhibit negligible magnetism, while conductive Fe₃O₄ shows strong ferrimagnetism, which highlights a fundamental trade-off between these properties. XRD analysis of Fe-based amorphous powder and its mixtures with three types of oxides demonstrates the retention of the amorphous structure before and after ball milling, as well as the effective blending of oxide powders (Fig. S3a).

Leveraging these intrinsic properties, we engineered heterogeneous interfaces in ASMCs by integrating the oxide coatings. Figure 1d–i and Fig. S4 demonstrate uniform coating and effective isolation in composite powders and bulk ASMCs. The XRD patterns of these as-sintered ASMCs (Fig. S3b) confirm the retention of the macroscopic amorphous structure and the absence of new Bragg diffraction peaks indicative of new phases after SPS. The structural and compositional evolution at various interfaces exhibits contrasting characteristics. The TiO₂-amorphous interface develops a ~ 100 nm thick crystalline layer accompanied by an oxygen-enriched zone (Fig. S5a), as evidenced by SAED analysis identifying the formation of FeB compound—a hard magnetic phase derived from the Fe-based amorphous matrix32. This resulting structure differs from previously reported TiO₂-based interface engineering strategies, such as the confined solid-state reaction approach employed by Li et al.7. The stark structural differences likely originate from the disparate thermal and electrical activation mechanisms inherent to SPS versus conventional annealing. During SPS, high-energy pulsed currents and non-thermal plasma effects generate localized Joule heating at particle contact points, leading to rapid, non-equilibrium interfacial reactions. The high resistivity of TiO₂ exacerbates localized overheating, facilitating uncontrolled Fe-O interdiffusion and crystallization of the amorphous matrix into magnetically hard phases21.

Conversely, the CeO₂-amorphous transition interface is markedly thinner (~40 nm) and composed of two distinct amorphous layers (Fig. 2a): a ~ 20 nm Fe-rich region and a gradient layer, both characterized by halo rings in SAED (region B in Fig. 2a). Unlike the mechanically bonded interfaces produced by conventional cold pressing11,33, this configuration indicates Fe diffusion from the matrix toward CeO₂ (Fig. 2b, c). Elemental profiling via EDS line scanning (Fig. 2d) confirms a progressive variation in Fe and O concentrations across this interface, culminating in a Fe-rich magnetic layer, a feature absent in the TiO₂ and Fe₃O₄ systems. This compositional gradient is a direct consequence of the thermally and electrically activated oxygen release from CeO₂ during SPS, a process that simultaneously generates a high concentration of oxygen vacancies within the CeO₂ lattice. Comparatively, the Fe₃O₄-amorphous interface shares the amorphous nature of the transition layer (~50 nm thick, Fig. S5d), but its composition diverges significantly, exhibiting oxygen enrichment and Fe depletion (Fig. S5e, f), forming Si-O compounds. These observations underscore a critical distinction: while both CeO₂ and Fe₃O₄ form amorphous interfaces, only the CeO₂-amorphous system develops a Fe-rich magnetic layer, contrasting with the oxygen-enriched magnetically dilute layers at Fe₃O₄ and TiO₂ interfaces. The fundamental divergence in interfacial structure can be attributed to the distinct defect formation energetics of the oxide precursors under the non-equilibrium SPS conditions. CeO₂, with its characteristically moderate oxygen vacancy formation energy, undergoes a controlled oxygen release confined to the amorphous state, primarily mediated by Fe diffusion. This process avoids the deleterious formation of hard magnetic crystalline phases and instead establishes a compositionally graded, amorphous transition zone that is pivotal for optimizing magnetic coupling.

Oxygen, with its high electronegativity34, plays a crucial role in modulating the valence states and electronic structures of metallic atoms through its concentration and distribution35,36. The Ce M4,5 edges (Ce4+ and Ce3+) in EELS spectra, corresponding to 4 f orbital transitions, exhibit systematic energy shifts indicative of valence changes. Specifically, Fig. 2e shows a progressive energy decrease in the Ce3+ edge and an increase in the Ce4+ edge when moving from position 5 to 7, indicating a reduction from Ce⁴⁺ to Ce³⁺ due to the formation of oxygen vacancies. This trend reflects an increasing oxygen vacancy concentration in this direction. Concurrently, the oxygen gradient affects Fe electronic states in the amorphous matrix. As shown in Fig. 2f, the Fe L3 peak (Fe2+) shifts to higher energy as oxygen concentration increases (from position 1 to 4), reflecting a gradual transition from Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ as Fe atoms lose electrons. These spectroscopic signatures collectively reveal an interfacial chemical state gradient mediated by oxygen redistribution. We attribute the gradient formation to thermal/field-assisted desorption of lattice oxygen from CeO₂, generating extensive oxygen vacancies.

To verify this mechanism, we performed aberration-corrected TEM analysis along the [0, 0, -1] zone axis (Fig. 2g and S6) and [1, 1, 2] zone axis (Fig. S7), where Ce and O columns are spatially separated for independent analysis. While atomic-resolution imaging was achieved for Ce columns, oxygen column detection remained resolution-limited. We annotate the projected atomic positions based on the fluorite structure of CeO2 (Fig. S6a). Atomic-scale oxygen mapping reveals that certain oxygen columns exhibited diminished intensity or complete absence, indicating a reduced occupancy of oxygen atoms at these sites (Fig. 2g). Line profiles across the white-dashed region (Fig. S6b, c) show pronounced oxygen signal fluctuations versus minimal Ce intensity variations, confirming localized oxygen depletion. Similar results can also be found along [1, 1, 2] (Fig. S7). These multiscale characterizations conclusively demonstrate that under combined thermal activation and electric field, oxygen atoms migrate from CeO₂ lattice sites into the amorphous matrix, establishing the observed gradient oxygen-deficient interface.

Excellent magnetic performances

The interface characteristics are summarized in Fig. 3a, illustrating the differences in structure, magnetism, and the resulting spin orders. To elucidate the role of interfacial engineering, we evaluated their influence on electrical and magnetic properties of ASMCs (Fig. 3). While all oxide interfaces induced a marginal reduction in saturation magnetization (from 156 A·m2/kg to 150 A·m2/kg; Fig. 3b), they markedly improved the frequency stability of magnetic permeability, rendering these composites particularly suitable for mid-to-high frequency applications. The insulating oxide interfaces increased the resistivity of the ASMCs by 1-2 orders of magnitude (from 5.51×10⁻⁴ Ω·cm for FeSiBCCr to 7.56×10⁻² Ω·cm for FeSiBCCr@2 wt.% CeO₂; Table S3). This elevated resistivity effectively suppresses eddy currents at high frequencies, thereby mitigating their detrimental demagnetizing effects on the effective permeability. However, these insulating interfaces, being non- or weakly-magnetic compared to the amorphous matrix, inevitably weaken inter-particle magnetic coupling and increase magnetic reluctance, consequently reducing the permeability. As shown in Fig. 3c, compared to the uninsulated FeSiBCCr sample, oxide interfaces reduce the effective permeability, typically at 10 kHz, and this balance is regulated by the oxide content (as shown in Fig. S8). This study compares the magnetic performances of various ASMCs with 2 wt.% insulation, summarized in Tables S1–3. Notably, the CeO₂-insulated sample achieved a magnetic permeability of ~70 (200 kHz, Fig. 3c), ~11% and ~150% higher than the Fe₃O₄- (~63 at 200 kHz) or TiO2-insulated ASMCs (~28 at 200 kHz), respectively. The superiority of the CeO₂-insulated sample is well outside the conservative measurement uncertainty bound (~2%, Extended note 6). This enhancement in permeability demonstrates the substantial modulation of interparticle magnetic coupling and reduction in magnetic reluctance achieved via interfacial engineering. The DC bias performance, which reflects the stability of permeability under an applied DC magnetic field (Fig. 3d), further highlights interfacial effects. As the DC magnetic field increases, soft magnetic materials tend to saturate, and the permeability significantly decreases due to Barkhausen jumps37 and irreversible domain wall displacement magnetization38. Uninsulated ASMCs exhibited a DC bias of 36%, while TiO₂-insulated ASMCs showed the highest bias (80%), attributed to their lower permeability and larger saturation current. Non-magnetic CeO₂-insulated ASMCs achieved a DC bias of 68%, comparable to magnetic Fe₃O₄-insulated samples, corroborating the superior interfacial design in CeO₂-based systems.

The improvement in magnetic permeability is associated with the smooth movement of magnetic domain walls, which is influenced by interfacial magnetism and spin order. CeO2 engineering promotes a favorable interface structure and magnetic coupling for enhanced domain dynamics. As shown in Fig. S5a, the TiO2-amorphous interfaces develop hard magnetic phases with elevated coercivity and magnetic anisotropy that resist magnetization relative to the amorphous matrix32. At such soft/hard magnetic interfaces, misaligned spins between the hard and soft magnetic regions (Fig. 3a) create an energy barrier that impedes domain wall propagation through pinning effects. The divergent performance outcomes observed between our work versus previous TiO2-FeSiAl interfaces7 are primarily attributable to fundamental differences in interfacial architecture. The controlled oxygen release from TiO₂ yields a coherent FeSiAl/Al-rich/Al2O3/TiO2 multilayered interface, where aluminum diffusion facilitates epitaxial growth of a homogeneous, lattice-matched Al2O3 insulation layer7. The critical disparity lies in the role of aluminum: its presence in FeSiAl acts as a reactant to steer interfacial thermodynamics toward beneficial oxide formation, whereas its absence in our amorphous system allows kinetically driven deterioration under SPS.

In contrast, both the CeO2 and Fe3O4-insulated interfaces (Fig. 2a and Fig. S5d) maintain the amorphous structure with reduced anisotropy that facilitates unimpeded domain motion. Notably, CeO2-insulated samples unexpectedly exhibited the highest magnetic permeability, surpassing Fe3O4-insulated samples despite stronger intrinsic magnetism. Although interfacial oxygen vacancies can induce ferromagnetism in CeO2, this magnetic enhancement is limited, typically ranging from 0.01 to 0.02 A·m²/kg20,23, which is significantly lower than that of Fe3O4 (65 A·m²/kg shown in Fig. S2b). This prompts us to re-examine conventional understanding. Instead, elemental analysis reveals distinct coherence of the magnetic structure at the interface: the CeO2-amorphous interface is a Fe-rich magnetic layer (Fig. 2c), whereas the Fe3O4-amorphous interface is an O-rich magnetically dilute layer (Fig. S5f). The magnetic coherence at these interfaces ensures good magnetic coupling, even though the magnetism of CeO2 is weaker than that of Fe3O4. This defect-mediated engineering for interfacial magnetic continuity regulation demonstrates that optimized spin alignment (Fig. 3a) outweighs absolute magnetic strength in enhancing permeability.

Core losses in ASMCs arise from eddy current and hysteresis losses, governed by resistivity and permeability/dynamic coercivity, respectively. Oxide insulation drastically reduces eddy currents, suppressing high-frequency losses. Here, CeO₂-insulated ASMCs delivered the lowest total loss (86 kW/m³ at 20 mT, 100 kHz, Fig. 3e, f), approximately 80% and 45% lower than TiO₂-insulated (440 kW/m³) and Fe3O4-insulated ASMCs (157.5 kW/m³), respectively. When considering the statistical average performance of multiple samples (Table S2), these two values are 76% and 22%, respectively. This performance gap far exceeds the conservative measurement uncertainty bound (~5%, Extended note 6). Therefore, even when accounting for potential systematic errors, the superior high-frequency performance of the CeO₂-insulated ASMCs remains statistically robust. This apparent discrepancy stems from the disordered and rotationally hindered interfacial spin order between TiO2 and amorphous FeSiBCCr (Fig. 3a), which elevates hysteresis losses by impeding magnetic domain realignment and increasing energy dissipation. Weak-magnetic CeO₂ outperformed strong-magnetic Fe₃O₄ in loss reduction, owing to its synergistic combination of high permeability (optimized spin orders) and exceptional electrical insulation. Their outstanding stability under elevated temperatures further underscores the exceptional performance of the CeO₂-insulated ASMCs, a critical factor for practical applications in high-frequency electronics where components routinely operate at 50–100 °C. Systematic evaluation of the amplitude permeability, core loss, and dynamic coercivity under varying excitations (10–50 mT, 10–200 kHz) reveals minimal performance degradation with increasing temperature up to 100 °C (Figs. S9–12). The amplitude permeability fluctuates within a narrow margin of ±3%, while the core loss and dynamic coercivity vary within +8% to -1%. The exceptional thermal stability ensures reliable and consistent performance of inductive components by mitigating performance drift caused by Joule heating in compact devices. Elevated temperature can induce permeability or loss fluctuations by changing the magnetic domain dynamics. The stable performance demonstrates that the interface defect-promoted spin coupling network exhibits strong resistance to external thermal disturbances. The gradient interface acts as a buffer layer, smoothing out potential magnetic performance gradients caused by temperature variations, avoiding the exacerbation of magnetic domain pinning effects or decoupling phenomena commonly seen at sharp heterogeneous interfaces, thereby ensuring smooth magnetization processes even at high temperatures. Consequently, interface spin-order engineering not only enhances room-temperature performance but also endows the material with robustness to withstand thermal strains in real-world operating environments.

Figure 3g compares the performance of various ASMCs in balancing the permeability-loss trade-off. Uninsulated ASMCs exhibit high permeability, rendering them indispensable for low-frequency applications (e.g., power inductors, electric motors). However, their prohibitive high-frequency losses severely constrain efficiency in modern electronics. While ASMCs mitigate these losses via inherent high resistivity, their permeability remains limited by discontinuous flux paths at insulating interfaces. Traditional high-temperature chemical reaction-based interface engineering strategies7,17,19 are difficult to apply to metastable ASMCs, making it more challenging to optimize the magnetic coupling between insulating interfaces and amorphous matrix. Benchmarked against existing counterparts, CeO₂-insulated ASMCs strike an optimal balance between high permeability and loss minimization (Fig. 3g), underscoring the efficacy of our interfacial engineering strategy. ASMCs with such functional interfaces still have significant potential for performance improvement, as we employed conventional ball-milling coating techniques, which limit the minimum cerium oxide content to 2 wt.%. More importantly, we demonstrate that gradient spin-ordered interfaces can kinetically stabilize magnetic coupling across insulation barriers while preserving electrical isolation, providing a generalizable interfacial design principle for diverse SMCs.

CeO2-isolated ASMCs exhibit exceptional magnetic properties transcending intrinsic electromagnetic characteristics, which can be attributed to interfacial spin configurations and the resulting magnetic domains. The insulating interface isolates the amorphous powder particles from each other, and its inherent weak magnetism reduces the interparticle coupling, forming smaller and irregular cluster-like magnetic domains18. To probe these domain structures, we employed the DFI technique for volume domains characterization owing to neutrons’ strong penetration depth and sensitivity to magnetic moments39,40. When incident neutrons interact with magnetic domains, they undergo magnetic scattering accompanied by phase shifts and interference, producing characteristic scattering patterns41 (Fig. 4a). The DFI signal intensity directly correlates with domain size and spatial distribution42, enabling quantitative structural analysis. Figure 4b reveals that while all three samples show gradual DFI signal decay with increasing autocorrelation length ξ (Fig. 4c), their attenuation rates differ markedly, which reflects intrinsic differences in domain sizes. This ξ-dependence follows established scattering kinematics42: smaller domains generate signals with larger scattering vectors. With increasing distance, neutrons scattered with larger scattering vectors exhibit greater angular deviation, eventually falling outside the detector’s acceptance angle. Consequently, samples with smaller domains demonstrate more rapid DFI signal attenuation over ξ. By fitting the DFI attenuation profiles, we quantitatively determined the size of volumetric magnetic domains42. The TiO2- and Fe3O4-insulated samples exhibit relatively rapid decay, corresponding to domain sizes of 3.54 μm and 3.90 μm, respectively (Fig. 4b). Notably, the CeO2-insulated sample displays the slowest attenuation (Fig. 4b), indicating it maintains the largest average domain size (>3.90 μm) among the three systems.

As an independent variable, domain size influences dynamic magnetization processes (domain wall displacement or rotation), directly determining the magnetic permeability and loss. Recently, Gautam et al. promoted the formation of ~4.8 μm stripe domains in amorphous ribbons by stress-induced weak perpendicular anisotropy, significantly reducing losses by facilitating magnetization rotation43. In powder-based magnetic cores, Bai et al. constructed isolated vortex domains through powder cold-sintering structures to eliminate domain wall resonance and optimize the high-frequency stability of permeability2. Comparing existing studies reveals that their magnetic domain engineering aims to transition from a state of excessively active domain wall motion to suppressing motion/eliminating domain walls, whereas this study shifts from a state of overly suppressed (pinned) domain wall motion to restoring its moderate mobility. Due to magnetic decoupling between particles, the initial magnetic domains in ASMCs are excessively small and fragmented. Through interface spin-order engineering, we enhance the magnetic coupling between particles, thereby expanding the effective domain size, forming more coherent magnetization paths, optimizing the dynamic response of domain walls, and reducing irreversible losses caused by pinning effects. This effectively demonstrates that there is no universal optimal magnetic domain size. For material systems with an initially overly fragmented state, increased domain size holds scientific value equally important to reduced domain size in systems with active domain walls. Together, they form the complete spectrum of magnetic domain engineering.

To establish the correlation between domain sizes and interfacial coupling strength, we utilized DPC imaging to directly visualize the magnetic domain arrangements near different interfaces2. Figure 4d–f illustrate distinct domain structures across the oxide interfaces. At the TiO2-amorphous matrix interface, the formation of a hard magnetic phase induces disordered magnetic domains (Fig. 4d). These domains are resistant to external magnetic fields, acting as pinning sites that degrade magnetic response through domain wall immobilization32. In contrast, the CeO2/Fe3O4-amorphous interface avoids this spin-pinning effect. However, the compositional differences between these interfaces also lead to distinct magnetic domain structures. As shown in Fig. 4e, the gradient interface between CeO2 and the amorphous matrix is Fe-rich, rendering it magnetic, with domain orientations intermediate between those of CeO2 and the amorphous matrix. The oxygen content gradient facilitates smooth spin realignment, bridging the magnetic domain orientations of the amorphous matrix and CeO2 (Fig. 4e). CeO2 exhibits more uniform domain orientations, reflecting strong magnetic moment interactions mediated by oxygen vacancies. This configuration acts as an effective bridge for magnetic coupling across the interface. The Fe3O4-amorphous interface exhibits oxygen-rich magnetically dilute regions (as indicated by the dark contrast in Fig. 4f and Fig. S13), increasing the magnetic coupling barrier and impedance. The CeO2 interface surpasses even the magnetic Fe3O4 interface in promoting strong interparticle coupling. We attribute this superior performance to the optimized spin orders at the CeO2 interface, which appear to improve magnetic coupling efficiency despite its lower saturation magnetization compared to Fe3O4. Consequently, despite the inherent ferromagnetism of Fe3O4, the magnetic properties of Fe3O4-isolated ASMCs are inferior to those isolated by CeO2. Based on the results above, three critical design principles emerge: i) compositional gradient interfaces outperform abrupt transitions; ii) oxygen non-stoichiometry mediates interfacial coupling (vacancies enhance, but excess degrades); iii) hard-magnetic phase formation at sharp interfaces creates detrimental pinning sites.

Defect-mediated spin-ordering mechanisms

The aforementioned experimental results demonstrate a transition from conventional material-centric interfaces (e.g., ferromagnetic Fe₃O₄ or non-magnetic coatings) to defect-mediated spin ordering, resolving the fundamental conflict between high resistivity and strong magnetism in metal oxides. Regarding the formation energy of oxygen vacancies and the evolution mechanism of magnetic moments and electronic structures in three types of oxides, please refer to Extended Note 3 and Fig. S16 in the Supplementary Information. It explains the facile formation of a gradient oxygen-deficient interface and the origin of weak magnetism in otherwise non-magnetic CeO₂. To unravel the atomic-scale underpinnings of the enhanced magnetic coupling observed in our neutron dark-field imaging and electron microscopy, we focus on elucidating the mechanism of defect-induced spin ordering at the heterointerface.

We engineered oxygen defects at the CeO2-matrix interface (Fig. S18a, b) and systematically investigated their impact on the electronic structure and magnetic ordering via DFT calculations. At the heterointerface, the mutual hybridization of orbitals between neighboring atoms results in the formation of a lower-energy molecular orbital, accompanied by interfacial charge transfer44. This restructuring of charges and orbitals induces strong interlayer exchange interactions, extending the magnetically ordered state to adjacent non-magnetic layers and forming interfacial ferromagnetic phases or clusters—a phenomenon known as the magnetic proximity effect45,46. Novel interfacial states that are absent in the bulk material emerge. Without oxygen vacancies (complete interface in Fig. 5a), the high electronegativity of oxygen atoms in CeO2 captures electrons from the adjacent Fe atoms in the amorphous matrix, resulting in electron accumulation on the CeO2 side and depletion on the amorphous side (Fig. 5b). However, when oxygen vacancies are introduced, the reduced electron demand from oxygen vacancies partially restores the charge density on Fe atoms (Fig. 5b). This charge recovery is further supported by the enhanced intensity of Fe 3 d electron states in the DOS curve (Fig. S19d). Vacancy-modified interfaces exhibit spatially extended charge transfer beyond immediate interfacial atoms, indicating long-range electronic restructuring (Fig. 5c), which could potentially alter the magnetic configuration and connectivity around the interface.

Defect-mediated spin ordering mechanisms underlying the fundamental reason for enhanced interfacial coupling.

a Charge density difference (CDD) map of the heterointerface without oxygen vacancy; b CDD map of the heterogeneous interface with oxygen vacancy; c Oxygen vacancy-induced magnetic moment reversal in amorphous interfacial clusters; d Schematic of oxygen-deficient CeO2 interface acting as a magnetic glue to enhance magnetic connectivity in amorphous soft magnetic composites (ASMCs).

The presence of oxygen vacancies significantly enhances the interfacial magnetic moment from 188 μB to 200.19 μB (Figs. S15b, 17, 18c, d), primarily through Fe contributions. This suggests that oxygen vacancies can restructure the exchange interactions among Fe atoms through the magnetic proximity effect. To validate this mechanism, we analyzed the magnetic moment configuration of Fe atoms near the oxygen vacancies (Fig. 5c). While most Fe atoms exhibit upward spins, some near non-magnetic atoms (e.g., boron atoms) show downward spins, forming antiferromagnetic ordering (Fig. 5c, up). Oxygen vacancies reverse these moments, establishing ferromagnetic order (Fig. 5c, down). This means that oxygen vacancies promote the spins of Ce and Fe atoms on both sides to align in a parallel direction. Lack of mutations in the spin direction reduces the chance of magnetic discontinuities, thereby enhancing the exchange coupling between adjacent magnetic domains at the interface. Moreover, the presence of a gradient transition from the Fe-rich region to CeO₂ (Fig. 2) creates a continuously evolving magnetic environment near the interface. This configuration facilitates a smoother alignment and coupling of magnetic moments within the varying chemical and magnetic potential fields, preventing issues like magnetic domain pinning often associated with abrupt interfaces47. This effective utilization of the defect-mediated magnetic proximity effect enhances the overall efficiency and performance of magnetic coupling. This charge redistribution, extending beyond the immediate interface, promotes a parallel alignment of spins between Ce and Fe atoms (Fig. 5c), effectively establishing a ferromagnetic coupling bridge. This DFT-predicted spin alignment mechanism directly explains the experimental observation of smooth magnetic domain bridging at the CeO₂-amorphous interface in DPC images (Fig. 4e), in stark contrast to the pinning effects at the TiO₂ interface (Fig. 4d). Consequently, the defect-mediated magnetic proximity effect revealed by DFT provides the fundamental reason for the enlarged magnetic domains detected by neutron scattering, which underlie the high permeability and low loss.

The interfacial defect engineering strategy employing CeO₂ presents a forgiving pathway for manufacturing, primarily due to its favorable sintering behavior during SPS. CeO₂ exhibits a wider and more forgiving processing window, as its moderate oxygen vacancy formation energy facilitates the desired solid-phase reaction while preserving the integrity of the amorphous matrix, thereby enhancing process yield. This intrinsic characteristic stands in stark contrast to Fe₃O₄, which presents a narrower processing window due to its propensity for excessive oxygen release, often leading to inconsistent interfacial structures and property degradation. Moreover, the formation of the functional gradient interface is an in situ process driven directly by the SPS conditions, eliminating the need for complex multi-step coating or post-annealing treatments that increase manufacturing complexity. Therefore, the combination of a robust sintering window and inherent process simplicity underscores the superior scalability of the CeO₂-insulated ASMCs.

This work demonstrates an approach to reconciling the long-standing permeability-loss trade-off in ASMCs through atomic-scale interfacial spin-order engineering. Moving beyond conventional ferromagnetic interface strategies, we exploit oxygen vacancy modulation to construct a gradient functional layer at heterointerfaces via a thermally-electrically activated oxygen migration process between CeO₂ and the Fe-based amorphous matrix. This approach yields a 150% increase in magnetic permeability and a 76% reduction in core loss, surpassing state-of-the-art counterparts. Mechanistically, our results demonstrate that optimized interfacial spin order, rather than magnetic strength, is the decisive factor governing magnetic coupling. Neutron dark-field imaging (DFI) directly reveals that oxygen-defect-mediated spin coupling leads to enlarged magnetic domains, in contrast to the limited magnetic restoration enabled by traditional ferromagnetic interfaces like Fe3O4. This interfacial spin ordering reconciles the conflicting electronic requirements for insulation and magnetism. This work establishes a framework for interfacial spin-order engineering to optimize multifunctional materials, with implications for high-frequency electronics and energy-efficient magnetic devices. Future efforts could extend this strategy to alternative rare-earth oxides or implement dynamically tunable vacancy gradients for adaptive magnetic responsivity.

Methods

Materials preparation and characterization

The synthesis of Fe-based ASMCs with oxide additives (TiO₂, CeO₂, or Fe₃O₄, average diameter <50 nm, with purity>99% metal basis, received from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.) was performed through a carefully controlled powder metallurgy process. Amorphous Fe78Si13B9C3Cr2 powder (Advanced Technology & Materials Co., Ltd., average diameter 10 μm) was coated with oxide nanopowder at a mass fraction of 2%. The powder mixture underwent low-energy ball milling under optimized conditions: a rotation speed of 300 rpm for 54,000 s with a ball-to-powder ratio of 5:1. Anhydrous ethanol was introduced as a process control agent during milling to ensure effective heat dissipation and promote homogeneous encapsulation of oxide nanoparticles onto the amorphous powder. The milled powder was subsequently consolidated using spark plasma sintering (SPS, LABOX-1575, Sinter Land). Cylindrical compacts with dimensions of φ13 × 6 mm were produced through the following sintering protocol: the powder was subjected to a uniaxial pressure of 600 MPa in a vacuum environment (10 Pa) while being heated to 450 °C at a controlled ramp rate of 0.83 °C/s. The temperature was maintained at 450 °C for 300 s to ensure sufficient densification, followed by furnace cooling to room temperature. For magnetic performance tests, the sintered compacts were precision-machined into toroidal samples using electrical discharge machining. The final ring-shaped specimens had the following dimensions: outer diameter of 13 mm, inner diameter of 8 mm, and thickness of 2 mm.

Phase structure was examined by X-ray diffractometer (XRD, D8-ADVANCE, Bruker) with Cu Kα radiation and LYNXEYE XE-T detector. The morphology and composition analyses were conducted with scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM 7200 F, JEOL) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Titan Cube 80-300, FEI) equipped with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). To examine the interface structure, we prepared site-specific TEM samples via a focused ion beam (FIB, Helios NanoLab 600i, FEI). Atomic-resolution structural and compositional analyses, including electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS), were performed using a spherical aberration-corrected TEM (NEOARM 200 F, JEOL) with an energy resolution of 0.5 eV. Interfacial magnetic domains were characterized using TEM (Talos F200S, Thermofisher) in differential phase contrast (DPC) mode.

Oxygen vacancy formation during sintering was investigated by annealing metallic oxide powders at 450 °C, 500 °C, and 550 °C for 300 s to simulate the sintering profile. The presence and concentration of oxygen vacancies were quantitatively analyzed using X-band continuous-wave electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy (EPR, EMXPLUS, Bruker) with an ER 4119HS cavity. Measurements were conducted at 21 °C with a microwave frequency of 9.3545 GHz, using standard parameters: microwave power of 20 mW, modulation amplitude of 10-4 T, modulation frequency of 100 kHz, and sweep widths of 0.02 T. The chemical states associated with oxygen vacancies were further characterized using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, K-Alpha, Thermo Scientific).

The saturation magnetizations (Ms) of both the powder and sintered composites were measured using a magnetic property measurement system (MPMS, Quantum Design). The sintered composites were machined into toroidal cores with an outer diameter of 13 mm, an inner diameter of 8 mm, and a thickness of 2 mm for subsequent magnetic characterization. The permeability was derived from inductance measurements obtained using a precision impedance analyzer (Keysight E4990A) over a frequency range of 50 Hz to 120 MHz, with a winding turn count of 20. For each permeability measurement, we performed standard open/short circuit calibrations to minimize instrument uncertainty. The total measurement uncertainty for permeability is determined to be ~2% (please refer to Extended Note 6 and Table S4 for details). DC bias characteristics were evaluated using a DC power supply (25 A DC Bias Unit 3265B, WAYNE KERR) by adjusting the input current to vary the superimposed DC magnetic field. Amplitude permeability and high-frequency magnetic losses were measured from room temperature to 100 °C using an AC B-H analyzer (SY-8218, IWATSU) equipped with a temperature chamber. Core loss measurements were performed using the cross-power mode across a frequency range of 10 to 200 kHz and flux densities from 10 to 50 mT. The uncertainty in the loss measurement primarily stems from the phase angle error between the excitation current and the induced voltage. This error is significantly amplified when the phase angle approaches 90°. To ensure high data fidelity, a standard calibration procedure was performed before each measurement, maintaining the instrumental phase angle error at or below 0.05° (Table S6). Furthermore, all measured phase angles corresponding to the reported loss data were kept significantly below 90° (primarily <87°, see Table S5). This combination of a low inherent instrument error and a conservatively large phase angle margin minimizes the systematic uncertainty in core loss to less than 5%. The number of winding turns (20 for both primary and secondary) was selected to generate a sufficiently strong induced voltage signal, ensuring an adequate signal-to-noise ratio under the most demanding test conditions (200 kHz, 50 mT). To mitigate the impact of increased inter-winding stray capacitance associated with this turn count at high frequencies, a twisted-pair (bifilar) winding method was employed. This technique maximizes the magnetic coupling coefficient, minimizes leakage inductance, and promotes a symmetrical distribution of parasitic capacitance, thereby enhancing measurement accuracy. Furthermore, prior to each test, an evaluation was conducted to confirm that the impedance characteristics of all samples remained far from (<10%) the resonance point under all test conditions. To further validate measurement reliability, repeated measurements on the same CeO₂-insulated toroidal core demonstrated excellent repeatability for permeability and core loss (Tables S7, 8). Variable analysis with different winding configurations (10, 15, and 20 turns) was conducted to assess the uncertainty arising from the parasitic effect (Tables S9 and Fig. S20). The total measurement uncertainties for permeability and core loss are ~2% and ~5%, respectively. These results, detailed in Extended Note 6 of the Supplementary Information, support the conservative uncertainty estimates adopted in this work.

Neutron dark-field imaging

To understand the macroscopic magnetic coupling behavior of magnetic toroids, we introduced an advanced DFI technique implemented at the China Spallation Neutron Source (CSNS), which is based on a modified nGI operating in the third Talbot distance48. The method leverages the Talbot effect to adjust the distance between the sample and the detector, enabling the extraction of real-space correlation functions from scattering signals (Fig.4a). The apparatus consists of three key components spaced 1 meter apart: grating G0, grating G1, and analyzer grating G2, with a consistent period of 40 μm. Experiments were conducted using a neutron beam, with a wavelength ranging from 3.5 to 4.5 Å. The magnetic toroid samples were positioned on the platform between gratings G1 and G2. Measurements involved collecting DFI signals at seven distinct correlation lengths ξ ranging from 1.6 to 2.8 μm, each with a 300-s exposure time. The relation between the correlation length ξ and L is given by the formula ξ = λL/p, where L represents the sample-to-detector distance, λ is the neutron wavelength (4 Å), and p denotes the G2 period (40 μm). The variation of the DFI signal with ξ reveals critical information about magnetic domains. Controlled scanning of sample-detector distance enables simultaneous acquisition of macroscopic spatial resolution and microscopic structural information49. For principal details of this technique, please refer to Extended Note 1 in the Supplementary Information. The approach establishes a robust framework for the quantitative evaluation of microstructural heterogeneities spanning multiple length scales50,51, offering new insights into magnetic coupling phenomena in magnetic toroids.

First-principle calculations

To elucidate the impact of oxygen vacancies at interfaces on electronic structure and magnetic properties, DFT calculations were conducted in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)52,53 based on the plane-wave basis sets with the projector augmented-wave method54,55. To investigate distinct mechanisms of oxygen vacancies in bulk oxides versus interfacial regions, we constructed two representative structural models: (1) three oxide models (Fig. S14a–c); (2) Fe78Si13B9C3Cr2/CeO2 heterojunction interfaces (Fig. S18a, b). We quantitatively analyzed differential charge density distributions and magnetic moment variations associated with oxygen vacancy formation.

The amorphous Fe78Si13B9C3Cr2 structure was carefully constructed through a multi-step process. Initially, we developed a doping configuration based on the bulk Fe lattice, followed by Ab Initio Molecular Dynamics (AIMD) simulations to model the thermal annealing process from 1000 K to 300 K. This approach yielded a stoichiometrically accurate slab model of Fe78Si13B9C3Cr2, which underwent subsequent structural re-optimization. For the heterojunction model, we selected the (111) surface of CeO2 to interface with our amorphous structure (Fig. S18).

All DFT calculations adhered to the generalized gradient approximation (GGA), utilizing the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) formulation56. To accurately describe electronic occupancies, we employed Gaussian smearing with a width of 0.05 eV for the Kohn-Sham orbitals. Structural optimizations, including both geometric and lattice parameters, were performed using Γ-centered k-mesh sampling with a 0.04 Å-1 density57. We ensured stringent convergence criteria, with self-consistent calculations reaching an energy threshold of 10⁻⁵ eV. Atomic stress tolerances were maintained below 0.01 eV/Å, guaranteeing precise determinations of equilibrium geometries and lattice constants. Recognizing the importance of weak interactions in our system, we incorporated the DFT + D3 method, applying empirical corrections according to Grimme’s scheme58. Spin polarization methods were implemented to appropriately depict the magnetic behavior of the system. The formation energy of oxygen vacancies was calculated using the formula: Eformation=Evac + EO-Eperf, where Evac denotes the energy of the system with oxygen vacancies, Eperf signifies the total energy of the pristine system, and EO represents the isolated oxygen atom’s energy. Given the substantial influence of strong electronic correlations in transition metals, we employed spin-dependent GGA + U calculations. The Hubbard parameters were carefully selected based on extensive references and verification: U = 5.0 eV for Ce, 5.3 eV for Fe, 4.0 eV for Ti, and 3.7 eV for Cr atoms.
