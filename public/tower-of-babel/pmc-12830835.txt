Inverse decoration of TiOx to NiSn alloy nanoparticles for efficient hydrodeoxygenation of m-cresol
Authors: Feifei Yang, Chengyu Li, Maolin Wang, Shixiang Yu, Jiankang Zhao, Yao Xu, Wei Zhou, Xiaodong Yi, Ding Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hydrodeoxygenation (HDO) of phenolics is crucial for lignin-to-fuel production via fast pyrolysis. The reaction under atmospheric H2 pressure offers compatibility with pyrolysis conditions, high aromatic selectivity and low H2 consumption, but suffers from coking-induced catalyst deactivation. In this work, we report a TiOx-decorated NiSn/SiO2 catalyst (Ti-NiSn/SiO2), achieving 100% deoxygenation selectivity and enhanced stability in m-cresol HDO. The superior performance stems from: (i) the interfacial synergy between TiOx and NiSn alloy that facilitates C-O bond cleavage with a reduced energy barrier; (ii) the geometric isolation and electron-donating properties of Sn, which suppress undesirable side reactions and mitigate coke formation; and (iii) the inverse TiOx decoration strategy, which enables effective interface chemistry while minimizing acidic sites that could otherwise accelerate coking. This study presents a design strategy for constructing inverse oxide–alloy interface catalysts, offering multi-faceted advantages for HDO and other complex catalytic transformations.

Introduction

Fast pyrolysis is an efficient technology on biomass conversion to sustainable liquid fuels, which can produce bio-oil with a yield up to 75 wt%1,2. But the bio-oil presents shortcomings such as high oxygen content and poor stability, requiring hydrodeoxygenation (HDO) treatment to improve its quality before application as fuel. HDO is also a key reaction in catalytic fast pyrolysis, and the development of effective HDO catalysts operable under ambient-pressure conditions could further improve overall process integration (Fig. 1a)3,4. Among common oxygen-containing structures in bio-oil, cleavage of the phenolic C-O bond is the most challenging5. Thus, the development of high-performance phenolic HDO catalysts has attracted widespread attention. Despite this, most reported HDO reactions operate in batch systems or require high H2 pressure, while vapor-phase HDO studies under atmospheric pressure that compatible with pyrolysis conditions are still limited6.Fig. 1Interfacial catalysis strategies for phenolic HDO reactions under atmospheric pressure.a Schematic of HDO units integrated into biomass fast pyrolysis process for biofuel production. b Summary of previous studies on improving metal catalyst performance via interfacial site construction. c Design of the inverse TiOx-NiSn alloy interface with efficient HDO performance and anti-coking stability.

Effective HDO catalysis typically requires not only C-O bond activation but also hydrogenation and dehydrogenation steps. To minimize H2 overconsumption, suppressing aromatic ring hydrogenation is essential. Additionally, the catalyst must inhibit both C-C bond cleavage and coking side reactions to ensure target product selectivity and maintain long-term stability. These requirements are difficult to achieve with mono transition metal catalysts, whereas interfacial structures offer a promising solution due to their multifunctionality and flexible designability (Fig. 1b)7. For instance, numerous studies have reported that constructing metal-acidic/oxophilic site interfaces can significantly enhance both activity and selectivity in phenolic HDO reactions. In such systems, the acid sites can be provided by amorphous SiO2-Al2O3, zeolites, or other acidic solids, while the oxophilic sites usually originate from transition metals or metal oxides with strong oxygen affinity8. On such interfaces, it has been revealed that the phenyl ring preferentially adsorbs on the metal site, while the hydroxyl group of phenols binds to neighboring acidic/oxophilic sites. This synergistic activation leads to a lower energy barrier for C-O cleavage and enhanced HDO activity, in contrast to less selective phenyl ring activation on metal sites alone or the inefficient oxygen activation on acidic/oxophilic sites alone9,10. However, the phenolic reactants tend to form condensed-ring compounds, leading to catalyst deactivation by coking11,12. The acidic/oxophilic sites facilitate the condensation process of phenolic intermediates and thereby accelerate coking13–15. For instance, several studies have reported faster deactivation on acidic/reducible oxide supported catalysts than those on inert SiO2 supported catalysts16,17.

Unlike the above strategies, some researchers have used non-acidic/non-oxophilic materials to construct interface active sites with metals, achieving progress in improving coke resistance (Fig. 1b). Yang et al. reported that a small amount of nitrogen doping in the carbon support reduces the deactivation rate of Fe particles almost by one order of magnitude in m-cresol HDO, which is attributed to the reduced oxygen affinity of Fe by N species18. Teles et al. investigated the influence of Ce/Zr ratio on phenol HDO performance over Pd/CexZr1-xO2 catalysts. They found that Ce-enriched catalysts exhibit higher stability, which is attributed to the reduced oxophilicity19. However, in these cases, the enhanced catalyst stability comes with a substantial sacrifice in activity. Thus, engineering phenolic HDO catalysts with both high efficiency and anti-coking stability under atmospheric pressure remains a significant challenge.

We previously explored bimetallic effects for HDO of m-cresol and related molecules20,21, and observed that while the addition of Sn promoter to Ni/SiO2 catalyst inhibits the furfural HDO reactivity, it is able to enhance the deoxygenation selectivity and relieve coking on the active sites, in stark difference to the acidic/oxophilic site promoted Ni catalysts21. Moreover, Sn alloyed Ni catalysts have also been reported to show enhanced stability in reforming and dehydrogenation reactions, as the electronic interaction between Sn and Ni prevents carbon nucleation on Ni active center22–24.

Building on the aforementioned insights, in this work, we present an inverse TiOₓ decoration strategy on NiSn alloy nanoparticles supported on SiO2 (denoted as Ti-NiSn/SiO2, Fig. 1c), as a distinct and efficient catalyst for the HDO of m-cresol—a representative phenolic compound derived from lignin decomposition. Taking the multiple advantages of (1) interfacial chemistry between TiOx and NiSn alloy in lowering C-O activation barrier, (2) the electronic and geometric modification imparted by Sn to suppress side reaction and mitigate coke deposition, and (3) the inverse strategy to minimize TiOx content and reduce coking, the resulting catalyst exhibits significantly enhanced activity, deoxygenation selectivity, as well as stability for HDO of m-cresol at 350 °C and atmospheric H2 pressure. These improvements are notably superior compared to benchmark catalysts, including Ni/SiO2, NiSn/SiO2, Ni/TiO2, NiSn/TiO2 as well as inverse Ti-Ni/SiO2. The structures of these catalysts were comprehensively investigated through detailed characterization, while the HDO mechanism and the synergistic effect at the TiOx-NiSn alloy interface sites were explored by density functional theory (DFT) calculations to provide critical insights into the structure-activity relationship. This study offers a rational design strategy for developing high-performance and stable HDO catalysts based on earth-abundant elements.

Results

Boosting HDO performance and stability on inverse TiOx-NiSn interface

Firstly, the interfacial effect on m-cresol HDO performance over Ni based catalysts were investigated. Catalysts with 2 wt.% Ni loading on SiO2 and TiO2 were synthesized by incipient wetness impregnation method and compared to NiSn bimetallic catalysts with the same Ni loading. Here SiO2 was selected as an inert support, where HDO is expected to occur solely over the metal/bimetallic surface. In contrast, TiO2 was chosen as an active support where Ti cations at the metal-support interface can participate in the reaction. The basic element loading, crystalline and morphological characterizations of these catalysts are presented in Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Figs. 1–5 and Supplementary Note 1. The gas-phase conversion of m-cresol was studied in a fixed-bed reactor at temperature of 350 °C under atmospheric H2 pressure. As expect20, C-C hydrogenolysis to CH4 and phenol predominantly occurred over deoxygenation to toluene on the mono Ni active sites, leading to poor deoxygenation selectivity of Ni/SiO2 catalyst (Fig. 2a). Ni particles supported on TiO2 exhibited lower overall activity but enhanced yield and selectivity toward toluene compared to those supported on SiO2. Previous studies have shown that this trend in reactivity is the result of strong metal-support interaction (SMSI) between Ni and TiO2, where reduced TiOx species migrated to Ni surface during reduction process25. It prevents sintering of Ni particles, evidenced by their smaller size on TiO2. In addition, the formation of Ni-TiOx interfaces lowers the energy barrier, thereby enhancing deoxygenation activity26. The performance of supported NiSn alloy catalysts is also shown in Fig. 2a. First of all, unlike pure Ni phase that mainly catalyzes C-C hydrogenolysis, the catalysts containing NiSn alloy active phase are highly selective for deoxygenation reaction, even at all conversion levels (Supplementary Fig. 6). This can be attributed to the geometric isolation effect of Sn which suppresses the structure sensitive C-C hydrogenolysis side reaction. Secondly, comparison between Ni/SiO2 and NiSn/SiO2 shows that alloying Ni with Sn reduces the total activity but enhances the toluene yield. Similar to the finding in furfural HDO, the Sn atoms on surface not only occupy the Ni active centers, but also modify their chemical nature, thereby enhancing the activity of Ni in the target deoxygenation reaction21,28. Thirdly, it is interestingly to note that the use of TiO2 support reverses the activity trend between Ni and NiSn sites. The NiSn/TiO2 catalyst shows three times higher m-cresol conversion than that of Ni/TiO2 (Fig. 2a). Last but not least, in contrast to monometallic Ni phase on TiO2, which deters m-cresol conversion relative to Ni/SiO2, the NiSn alloy on TiO2 shows almost three times higher m-cresol conversion than that on SiO2.

Subsequently, m-Cresol and H2 probe characterizations were investigated to elucidate the adsorption and activation of reactants on the active site, which are key factors determining reactivity (Supplementary Figs. 7–9 and Supplementary Discussion 1). In-situ m-cresol adsorption diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) in Supplementary Fig. 7 demonstrates the donor-acceptor interaction between oxygen atom of m-cresol and Lewis acid sites on TiO2, which is stronger than the H-bonding interaction between m-cresol and SiO2 support13,14. Combined with previous DFT studies, the adsorption configurations of m-cresol on metal surface and metal-TiO2 interface are shown in Fig. 2b. m-Cresol binds to metal sites through phenyl ring with the hydroxyl group (-OH) tilting away from surface, while the TiOx at the neighbor of metal sites can interact with the O atom in -OH group26. Such adsorption configuration at the interface site facilities the effective activation of C-O bond in m-cresol, thus resulting in lower energy barrier for deoxygenation reaction (Supplementary Fig. 8). For H2 activation, the hydrogen-deuterium temperature-programed surface reaction (H2-D2 TPSR) indicates that the addition of Sn to Ni/SiO2 significantly prevents H2 dissociation (Supplementary Fig. 9), consistent with previous studies and DFT calculation which showed an enhanced energy barrier for H2 dissociation on the NiSn alloy surface compared to the pure Ni surface21,28. Fortunately, this drawback can be mitigated through the formation of NiSn-TiOx interface, which is also supported by TPR results (Supplementary Fig. 10). All these findings underscore the crucial role of both TiOx and Sn, as well as their synergistic effect in modulating the chemical nature at the interface site, thus leading to enhanced phenolic HDO performance.

The catalytic stability and the oxygen temperature programed oxidation (O2-TPO) profile of spent catalysts are further evaluated and shown in Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 11. The Ni/TiO2 deactivates at a significantly higher rate as compared to Ni/SiO2. Correspondingly, the total coke deposition on Ni/TiO2 is two times higher than that on Ni/SiO2 (Supplementary Table 2). This can be ascribed to the acidic nature of TiO2 support (Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Table 3), which promotes condensation of m-cresol, leading to increased coke formation29. As expected, the introduction of Sn enhances HDO stability on both SiO2 and TiO2 supported Ni catalysts. Therefore, the coking behavior of catalysts in m-cresol HDO can be illustrated. As shown in Fig. 2d, coke formation occurs at two types of active sites: metallic Ni sites and TiOx acid sites. Metal induced coking can be suppressed through the formation of NiSn alloy, while acid induced coking can be mitigated by replacing the acidic TiO2 support with inert SiO2.

The NiSn/SiO2 exhibits the highest anti-coking stability, but its activity remains poor in the absence of TiO2 support. Considering that only the oxophilic TiOx species adjacent to the NiSn alloy contribute to the synergetic activation of m-cresol, while excess acidic sites on TiO2 exacerbate coking, we sought to explore whether a catalyst composed of trace TiOx on NiSn particles could achieve a new level of catalyst performance. The inverse structure was synthesized by slow hydrolysis of tetrabutyl titanate over the as-prepared Ni/SiO2 or NiSn/SiO2 catalysts, as presented in Fig. 2e. The obtained catalyst was named as xTi-M/SiO2 (M = Ni or NiSn), where x refers to the nominal molar ratio of Ti to Ni. O2-TPO analysis of the fresh catalysts indicates that the carbonaceous species from tetrabutyl titanate are almost removed (Supplementary Fig. 13 and Supplementary Table 4). The activity comparison among these catalysts is shown in Fig. 2f. Specifically, over Ni/SiO2, the inverse loading of TiOx caused a decrease in total conversion but an enhanced yield to toluene. This was consistent with the activity trend between Ni/SiO2 and Ni/TiO2, and could also be attributed to the SMSI effect between TiOx and Ni nanoparticles. As expected, more TiOx on Ni/SiO2 leaded to further inhibition in reactivity. Conversely, the decoration of TiOx on NiSn/SiO2 significantly enhanced the deoxygenation reaction, with the m-cresol conversion and toluene yield increased by almost 2.7 times. Notably, its activity was comparable with that of the NiSn/TiO2 catalyst. More interestingly, the 0.5Ti-NiSn/SiO2 catalyst exhibited greater stability (Fig. 2g), with less coke accumulation as indicated by O2-TPO (Supplementary Fig. 13). Combined with NH3 temperature-programmed desorption (NH3-TPD) results in Supplementary Fig. 12, it demonstrates that the inverse TiOx loading outperforms conventional TiO2 support in suppressing acid induced coking.

Elucidating the anti-coking advantage of inverse TiOx-NiSn interface

To further elucidate the anti-coking advantage of inverse TiOx-NiSn interface, the coverage of active sites by coke after m-cresol HDO is investigated by an in-situ probe reaction of isopropanol (Fig. 3a). Generally, metal sites catalyze the dehydrogenation of isopropanol to acetone, while acid/oxophilic site catalyze the dehydration of isopropanol to propene30–32 (Fig. 3b). Therefore, the coke coverage on the metal and TiOx sites can be estimated from the percentage decrease in acetone and propene yields, respectively, after m-cresol HDO. As shown in Fig. 3c, acetone is the main product on Ni/SiO2, indicating that metallic Ni active sites dominate the catalyst surface. During the 2 h HDO test, m-cresol conversion decreased by 51%, closely matching the 52% decrease in acetone yield. These comparable results demonstrate that the isopropanol probe reaction can effectively reflect coke coverage on active sites. On 0.5Ti-Ni/SiO2, propene formation is observed due to the surface acidity provided by TiOx. The 66% decrease in m-cresol conversion is more pronounced than that of Ni/SiO2, consistent with the more severe deactivation observed at higher conversion levels in Fig. 2c. Interestingly, while the acetone yield deceases by 53%, similar to Ni/SiO2, the propene yield decreases only by 27% on 0.5Ti-Ni/SiO2. This observation indicates that coke predominantly covers metallic Ni sites, leading to deactivation in m-cresol HDO, while TiOx sites experience less coverage.

On NiSn/SiO2, propene yield is negligible compared to acetone yield, demonstrating that the Sn promoter does not introduce acid sites. The acetone yield remains constant after 2 h HDO reaction of m-cresol, confirming the inherent anti-coking property of NiSn alloy. After the inverse introduction of TiOx, propene is formed but at a lower yield compared to acetone. The distinct product distributions between 0.5Ti-NiSn/SiO2 and 0.5Ti-Ni/SiO2 during isopropanol reaction may be attributed to different TiOx structures, as evidenced by Raman results (Supplementary Fig. 14). The control experiments on catalysts without Ni or NiSn (Supplementary Fig. 15) further demonstrate that highly dispersed crystalline TiOx provides a higher dehydration activity than the amorphous TiOx. Compared to 0.5Ti-Ni/SiO2, the 2 h m-cresol HDO process has negligible effect on 0.5Ti-NiSn/SiO2, suggesting that NiSn alloy not only possesses intrinsic anti-coking property but also suppresses coke deposition on adjacent TiOx sites. Consequently, the inverse TiOx-NiSn interface enables both high activity and remarkable stability in m-cresol HDO.

Geometric and electronic structure of inverse TiOx-NiSn interface

To understand the efficient m-cresol HDO performance of inverse TiOx-NiSn interface, the catalysts were subjected to detailed characterization.

As noted, the quantified Ti content is 1.03 wt% and 1.13 wt% on the 0.5Ti-Ni/SiO2 and 0.5Ti-NiSn/SiO2 catalysts, respectively, as determined by Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES, Supplementary Table 1). High-angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) corresponding with electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) analysis was performed to visualize the interaction between the TiOx species and the Ni or NiSn nanoparticles. As shown in Fig. 4a and b, a thin TiOx layer partially covered the surface of Ni and NiSn alloy nanoparticles for 0.5Ti-Ni/SiO2 and 0.5Ti-NiSn/SiO2 catalysts, respectively. The TiOx species were clearly dispersed around the Ni or NiSn particles, rather than on the inert SiO2 support, indicating a strong interaction between Ni/NiSn and TiOx after reduction. The TiOx structure on the Ni/NiSn alloy surface was further examined by Raman spectra (Supplementary Fig. 14). For comparison, the Raman spectra of TiO2-supported Ni and NiSn catalysts are also included, which were characterized by the Eg mode of anatase at 143 cm−1 as the strongest signal for both anatase TiO2 and P2533. On the 0.5Ti-Ni/SiO2 catalyst, the Eg mode had blue-shifted to 149 cm−1 and broadened in the full width at half maximum (from ~18 to ~24 cm−1), which was related to phonon confinement and could be ascribed to the decrease of the TiOx thickness with respect to the TiO2 nanoparticles34. However, any Raman signal of TiOx species was undetectable with 0.5Ti-NiSn/SiO2. In case that limited TiOx content on this catalyst caused the weak response to Raman laser, the 1Ti-NiSn/SiO2 with the Ti content being doubled was tested, but still showed no signal. It demonstrated an amorphous structure of TiOx on the NiSn alloy particles35, possibly deriving from the unique interaction between TiOx and NiSn alloy phases.

Possible structure changes of Ni or NiSn alloy after TiOx coating were first examined by XRD. The results showed identical diffraction peaks before and after TiOx loading for both Ni/SiO2 and NiSn/SiO2 catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 16), demonstrating that the bulk active phase of Ni particles or NiSn alloys remains unchanged after TiOx coating. In addition, no TiOx related diffraction peaks were observed due to the high dispersion of TiOx on the surface, consistent with the Raman spectra.

The extended X-ray absorption fine-structure (EXAFS) analysis was further performed to characterize the coordination structure of Ni and NiSn phases in each catalyst. Ni K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge spectrum (XANES) in Supplementary Fig. 17a revealed that the fingerprinting features (including the area of pre-edge and white-line) of all Ni or NiSn-based catalysts, both before and after TiOx coating, resemble those of Ni foil rather than NiO. This indicated almost fully reduction of NiO in each catalyst. The FT k2-weighted Ni K-edge EXAFS further demonstrated an identical coordination environment of Ni atoms between Ni/SiO2 and 0.5Ti-Ni/SiO2, both are metallic Ni particles. While there are Ni-Sn and Ni-Ni coordination in both NiSn/SiO2 and 0.5Ti-NiSn/SiO2, indicating the formation of NiSn alloy in the NiSn-based bimetallic catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 17b, Supplementary Table 5). The above observation confirmed the Ni and NiSn alloy phases remained in the same state before and after TiOx coating, consistent with the XRD results.

Although XRD and EXAFS revealed that the bulk structure of Ni or NiSn alloys remains unchanged after TiOx decoration, subtle differences in the surface structure might not be detectable by these bulk characterization techniques. Surface-sensitive techniques, such as X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and in-situ CO DRIFTS, were therefore crucial to uncover potential modifications at the atomic level on the catalyst’s surface, which could significantly influence catalytic performance. As presented in Fig. 4c–f and Supplementary Figs. 18–19, prior to CO adsorption, the catalyst was in-situ reduced in H2 at 400 °C for 1 h. After exposure to a saturating dose of CO at 30 °C and subsequent purging to remove physically and weakly chemical adsorbed CO, two distinct bands were observed on Ni/SiO2. An intense band centered at 2077 cm−1 and a broad band at ~1940 cm−1 were observed on Ni/SiO2 (Fig. 4c), which were assigned to linearly adsorbed CO or subcarbonyl species atop the unsaturated low-coordinated Ni atom, and bridged adsorbed CO on close-packed Ni sites21,36–40. The decoration of TiOx on Ni surface (0.5Ti-Ni/SiO2) significantly reduced the intensity of linear-adsorbed CO and a slight red shift to 2075 cm−1, while the bridged-CO band remained unaffected (Fig. 4c). This observation suggested that TiOx species preferentially interact with Ni atoms in low-coordinated geometries, coordinating with these sites and altering their electronic properties. The TiOx coverage on Ni site was determined as 53.9% by CO pulsed chemisorption (Supplementary Table 6). The partially electron donation from TiOx to Ni enhanced the CO adsorption strength on Ni (red-shift). Such electron interaction between TiOx and Ni was further verified by XPS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 20) and aligned with previous reports41. The CO adsorption spectra revealed notable differences between Ni/SiO2 and 0.5Ti-Ni/SiO2 (Supplementary Fig. 18). For Ni/SiO2, upon exposure to 5 kPa CO for 1 minute, a band at 2085 cm−1 appeared and intensified over time, eventually blue-shifting to 2090 cm−1 due to dipole-dipole interactions between adsorbed CO molecules. A secondary band centered at 2056 cm−1 emerged and dominated at equilibrium CO pressure. This band, assigned to Ni(CO)4 species physically adsorbed on Ni and/or SiO2 support, disappeared upon Ar flushing, suggesting its formation involved subcarbonyl intermediates (~2090 cm−1) on defected Ni surfaces, which could lead to nickel tetracarbonyl formation36–38,40. Comparative spectra at equilibrium CO pressure (Fig. 4e) demonstrated that TiOx decoration on Ni reduced the number of defect Ni sites, indicated by the diminished intensity of the 2090 cm−1 band. However, the Ni(CO)4 band at 2056 cm−1 intensified and slightly red-shifted to 2054 cm−1, suggesting that the TiOx-modified Ni surface was more active in converting Ni subcarbonyl to Ni(CO)4. The red shift further supported electron transfer from TiOx to the Ni surface. This behavior aligned with previous studies linking Ni(CO)4 formation to deactivation mechanisms in CO hydrogenation, as Ni(CO)4 decomposition could lead to nickel carbide formation and Ni particle sintering42. Consequently, the tendency of TiOx-modified Ni surfaces to promote Ni(CO)4 formation may be a signature for their faster deactivation during HDO reactions (Fig. 2c). In stark contrast, the Ni(CO)4 species were absent in the spectra of NiSn-based catalysts (Fig. 4d and f and Supplementary Fig. 19), which may be related to their anti-coking performance.

Before discussing the difference between TiOx modified Ni/SiO2 and NiSn/SiO2 catalysts, the comparison between Ni/SiO2 and NiSn/SiO2 was firstly revealed. As noted, in contrast to Ni/SiO2 that presented both linear (2077 cm−1) and bridge (1940 cm−1) CO vibration at the steady state of CO adsorption (Fig. 4c), only one symmetrical band centered at 2066 cm−1 (linear adsorbed CO) with a narrow full width at half-maximum (FWHM) of 19.2 cm−1 was observed on NiSn/SiO2, while the bridged adsorbed CO on close-packed Ni sites was completely disappeared (Fig. 4d). Any additional bands were undetectable during the adsorption and desorption process (Supplementary Fig. 19). It indicated a highly uniform and isolated surface Ni sites were formed through alloying with Sn, which was responsible for the removal of C-C hydrogenolysis side reaction that preferred on large Ni ensembles27. The red shift of linear CO band from 2077 to 2066 cm−1 demonstrated the electron transfer from Sn to Ni, agreeing with the XPS observation (Supplementary Fig. 20) and previous studies on NiSn alloy. After decoration of TiOx to NiSn alloy surface, an obvious decreased of CO adsorbed band intensity, as well as a slight red shift to 2064 cm−1 was observed (Fig. 4d). The former was ascribed to the decreased number of exposed Ni sites by TiOx coating, resulting in the 16.9% TiOx coverage on Ni atoms (Supplementary Table 6). The latter phenomenon demonstrated the electron donation from TiOx layer to Ni, which was also verified by XPS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 20). This observation is consistent with the electronic interactions identified in the Ni-based catalysts, further reinforcing the role of the TiOx layer in modifying the electronic structure of Ni and NiSn active phases. In addition, XPS survey scan shows that the surface Ni/Sn ratio is not influenced by the inverse introduction of TiOx species (Supplementary Table 7).

Understanding reactivity and mechanism at the TiOx-NiSn interface

As TiOx-NiSn interface sites were found to have superior reactivity for m-cresol HDO to toluene with respect to the counterpart NiSn alloy sites and the TiOx-Ni interface sites, a DFT study was conducted to explore the catalytic mechanism and reveal the fundamental factors that determine reactivity. To model the NiSn alloy phase on NiSn/SiO2 catalyst, a Ni3Sn(111) surface was selected as suggested by CO-DRIFTS and EXAFS, while Ni(111) surface was used for modeling Ni/SiO2 catalyst. The interface sites were modelled by a Ti3O6 cluster decorated on Ni3Sn(111) or Ni(111) surface, respectively, as the oxide trimer is usually used to model the relatively small oxide particles in the inverse metal oxide-metal system43–46. The optimized structure for the Ti3O6 on Ni(111) surface is a coplanar cyclic conformation where each Ti ion adsorbed at the hollow sites and coordinated with one terminal O binding with Ni at hollow sites, and two bridging O lifting away from surface (Supplementary Fig. 21). Similar adsorption configuration has been observed for Ti3O6 on Ni3Sn(111) surface but with Ti binding atop of a Ni atom while terminal O binding at the bridge sites of Ni due to the geometric isolation effect of Sn (Supplementary Fig. 21). To make oxygen vacancy (Ti cations) at the interface, one terminal O of Ti3O6 cluster was removed by hydrogenating with H. Comparable thermodynamic oxygen vacancy formation energy of 0.88 eV and 0.84 eV were obtained on pure Ni and NiSn alloy surface, while the final stable structure of Ti3O5 on the two surfaces are shown in Supplementary Fig. 21.

The full catalysis cycle, including m-cresol adsorption, C-O cleavage, m-methylphenyl fragment (C7H7*) hydrogenation and oxygen vacancy regeneration steps are investigated (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Figs. 22–25). The energy barrier comparison indicates that C-O cleavage of m-cresol is the rate determine step on all catalysts. In good agreement with previous works, m-cresol prefers flat adsorption on a bridge site of the Ni(111) surface, with the -OH and -CH3 group tilting away from surface (Fig. 5a). As a result, though the entropy-corrected adsorption energy is calculated to be high as −0.81 eV, such adsorption mode on Ni(111) leads to high energy barrier of 1.44 eV and a reaction energy of 0.53 eV (endothermic) for C-O cleavage (Supplementary Fig. 26). In comparison, only the phenyl ring of m-cresol weakly adsorbs to the threefold Ni sites on Ni3Sn(111) surface with positive adsorption energy, where both the C and O atoms in m-cresol are not bond to Sn atoms (Fig. 5a), similar as that of the furfural adsorption on NiSn alloy surface21,28. This configuration with its weak adsorption energy explains the excellent anti-coking ability of NiSn alloy. Despite weaker adsorption of m-cresol on Ni3Sn(111) compared to Ni(111), its O atom is closer to the Ni3Sn(111) surface, with the nearest Ni-O distance of 2.52 Å, lower than the case of 2.68 Å on Ni(111) surface. This can be ascribed to the electron transfer from Sn to Ni, which facilitates the interaction between hydroxyl group and Ni surface. Interestingly, the C-O cleavage barrier is decreased to 0.83 eV with a reaction energy of −0.13 eV (slightly exothermic). It demonstrates that the NiSn alloy surface is more favorable for deoxygenation reaction, agreeing well with the higher toluene yield for m-cresol conversion over NiSn/SiO2 as compared to Ni/SiO2. In stark contrast, the interface sites present a high tendency toward selective C-O bond activation. As seen in Fig. 5a for m-cresol adsorption on Ti3O5/Ni(111) surface, the phenyl ring interacts with the Ni atoms, where O bonds to Ti cation (oxygen vacancy), leading a strong adsorption energy of -1.24 eV, and a longer C-O bond of 1.46 Å as compared to the 1.38 Å for the gas phase m-cresol, indicative of a strong C-O activation. As expected, the C-O scission requires a moderate energy barrier of 0.93 eV with a reaction energy of −0.81 eV (exothermic), seen Supplementary Fig. 26. Similar adsorption configuration of m-cresol has been observed on Ti3O5/Ni3Sn(111) surface (Fig. 5a). Compared to the adsorption on Ni3Sn(111), the three C atoms at the neighbor of -OH in m-cresol becomes closer to the Ni atoms, while the other C atoms slightly lifts away from surface. The O in -OH is bonding with Ti cations nearby, leading a moderate adsorption energy of -0.22 eV and a much longer C-O bond of 1.53 Å, demonstrating a strong tendency toward selective C-O bond activation. The energy barrier of C-O cleavage is further lowered to 0.68 eV, and the reaction becomes much more exothermic showing a reaction energy of −1.10 eV (Supplementary Fig. 26). The above comparison manifests the unique chemistry of TiOx-NiSn alloy interface for m-cresol adsorption and activation, as well as strong tendency toward C-O cleavage, instead of phenyl ring activation, which collaborates to its high catalytic performance for m-cresol HDO.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to develop a cost-effective and efficient catalyst for the hydrodeoxygenation (HDO) of m-cresol, while gaining mechanistic insights into the catalytic behavior. Construction of TiOx-Ni interfacial sites—either by supporting Ni on TiO2 or by decorating TiOx onto Ni nanoparticles—enhanced deoxygenation selectivity but compromised overall reactivity compared to the monometallic Ni/SiO2 catalyst. This performance trade-off is attributed to the SMSI between TiOx and Ni, where partial coverage of Ni surfaces by TiOx suppresses structure-sensitive C-C hydrogenolysis side reactions. At the same time, the interfacial sites promote selective C-O bond activation, resulting in improved deoxygenation activity. However, both Ni/TiO2 and TiOx-Ni/SiO2 catalysts suffer from rapid deactivation, largely due to the acidic nature of TiO2 which facilitates phenolic condensation to form coke, as well as the large Ni ensemble which catalyzes C-C hydrogenolysis side reaction to deposit C.

To overcome this limitation, Sn was introduced as a promoter based on its proven role in mitigating coking in dehydrogenation and reforming reactions. Notably, in contrast to the Ni-TiOx systems where total reactivity is reduced, the combination of TiOx and NiSn alloy nanoparticles—either in the form of NiSn/TiO2 or the inverse TiOx-NiSn/SiO2—exhibited not only excellent activity and 100% deoxygenation selectivity, but also remarkable stability. In particular, the inverse TiOx-NiSn/SiO2 catalyst showed superior resistance to deactivation by coke.

The exceptional catalytic performance of TiOx-NiSn/SiO2 can be attributed to three key factors: (i) the synergetic effect of TiOx and NiSn alloy interface sites for C-O cleavage with low energy barrier; (ii) the geometric isolation and electron donation effect of Sn in preventing C-C hydrogenolysis side reaction and mitigating coke deposition; (iii) the inverse TiOx decoration strategy, which preserves interfacial functionality while minimizing excessive surface acidity that promotes coking. This work demonstrates a rational design of multifunctional metal oxide–alloy interfacial catalysts, offering a promising pathway for the development of efficient and stable catalysts for complex reaction systems.

Methods

Materials

The commercial SiO2 support was purchased from Aladdin Scientific Corp. with SBET of 200 m2/g. The commercial TiO2 support was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (SKU no. 718467, P25 with a mixed phase of anatase and rutile, primary particle size of 21 nm, quoted SBET = 35–65 m2/g). Analytically pure toluene and Ni(NO3)2·6H2O were obtained from Shanghai Hushi Laboratorial Equipment Co., Ltd. Dimethyltin dichloride (98%), triethylamine (99%), ethanol (99.8%), tetrabutyl titanate (99%), m-cresol (99%), and isopropanol (99.5%) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.

Catalyst preparation

Synthesis of Sn/SiO2 and Sn/TiO2: In a typical synthesis, 5.0 g of SiO2 or commercial TiO2 and 0.173 g dimethyltin dichloride were dissolved in 150 mL anhydrous toluene, and stirred at room temperature for 1 h. Then 12.5 mL of triethylamine was drop added, and further continuously stirred for another 2 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the mixture was filtrated and washed with toluene and ethanol, dried at 100 °C overnight, and finally calcined in a muffle furnace at 580 °C for 3 h (after ramping to this temperature with a heating rate of 2 °C/min) to obtain Sn/SiO2 or Sn/TiO2 catalysts. The nominal Sn loading is 1.86 wt.%.

Synthesis of supported Ni catalysts: The supported Ni catalysts were prepared by incipient wetness impregnation method. In a typical synthesis, 0.20 g Ni(NO3)·6H2O was dissolved in a certain amount of distilled water, and drop added to 2.0 g support, i.e., SiO2, TiO2, as well as the above synthesized Sn/SiO2 and Sn/TiO2, respectively. The amount of distilled water used here is 4.0 g for SiO2 based samples, and 0.7 g for TiO2 based samples. The mixture was ground, dried at 60 °C overnight, and calcined at 400 °C for 4 h (heating rate: 2 °C/min) to obtain the supported Ni catalysts of Ni/SiO2, NiSn/SiO2, Ni/TiO2 and NiSn/TiO2, respectively. The nominal Ni loading is fixed at 2 wt.%.

Synthesis of inverse TiOx-Ni/SiO2 and TiOx-NiSn/SiO2 catalysts: Tetrabutyl titanate was used as the TiOx precursor. As the tetrabutyl titanate was easy to be hydrolyzed, we firstly dissolved a calculated amount of tetrabutyl titanate into 4.0 g of ethanol, and then dropped the resulting solution to 2.0 g of as-prepared Ni/SiO2 or NiSn/SiO2 catalyst and impregnated overnight. Here the tetrabutyl titanate amount is 0.116 g when Ti/Ni molar ratio is 0.5. While exposing to the air, the H2O in air would make tetrabutyl titanate be slowly hydrolyzed and deposited as TiOx. The carbon ligand was removed by calcination in air at 300 °C for 1 h (heating rate: 10 °C/min). The catalyst was named as xTi-M/SiO2 (M = Ni or NiSn), where x refers to the nominal molar ratio of Ti to Ni.

Catalyst characterization

The element loading of catalyst was determined by Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES) on Agilent ICP-OES 5110 instrument. Before the measurement, the sample digestion process began with weighing 0.1 g of the sample into a polytetrafluoroethylene digestion tube, followed by the addition of 5 mL of concentrated HNO₃ (65%) and 2–3 mL of HF (40%). Subsequently, the sealed tube underwent microwave-assisted digestion using a programmed temperature protocol: the temperature was first raised to 120 °C within 5 min and held for 5 min, then further increased to 180-200 °C within another 5 minutes and maintained at this range for 30-40 minutes to ensure complete digestion.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of different catalysts were collected on a Rigaku X-ray diffractometer operated at 40 kV and 100 mA, using Cu Kα radiation. Prior to analysis, the catalysts were reduced at 400 °C for 1 h.

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM), and high-angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) together with the elemental mapping images were acquired using a JEOL JEM-2100 microscope operating at 200 kV. Prior to analysis, the catalysts were reduced at 400 °C for 1 h. The electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) was performed on a Nion U-HERMES 100 microscope with a convergence semi-angle of 32 mrad at an accelerating voltage of 60 kV. The Ni-L edge, Ti-L edge and Sn-M edge EELS spectra shown in Fig. 4 were normalized by integrating signals within the energy-loss range of 850-880, 455-475 and 480-520 eV, respectively. The overlapping Ti-L and Sn-M edges were resolved by multiple linear least squares (MLLS) fitting.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on an AXIS Supra instrument by Kratos Analytical Ltd. with Al Kα X-rays (1489.6 eV, 150 W, 50.0 eV pass energy). The sample was firstly pre-reduced at 400 °C for 1 h in a quart tube reactor, and then transferred to a conductive tape in a glove box with Ar atmosphere without exposure to air. Subsequently, the sample was installed in an XPS device using a sealed transport chamber, strictly avoiding air during the whole process. And all the spectra collected were calibrated with amorphous carbon at 284.8 eV.

Raman spectra of ex-situ reduced catalysts were recorded using a Senterra laser confocal Raman spectrometer (Bruker) equipped with excitation wavelength λ = 532 nm.

H2-temperature-programed reduction (H2-TPR) was performed on Micrometrics AutoChem Ⅱ 2910. The catalyst was firstly pretreated at 300 °C for 1 h in He and then cooled to room temperature. Then the gas was switched to 10% H2/Ar. After reaching a stable base line, the sample was heated to 900 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min. The H2 consumption was detected by a thermal conductivity detector.

O2-temperature-programmed oxidation (O2-TPO) measurements were carried out in a reactor with a quartz tube (inner diameter: 6 mm), using 10% O2/Ar gas as oxidant. The mass of the spent catalyst was 50 mg, the gas flow rate was 10 ml/min, and the temperature increase rate was 5 °C/min. Before oxidation, the catalyst was treated in a pure Ar gas atmosphere at 100 °C for 1 h to remove surface-adsorbed gas. The outlet gas was analyzed using the Pfeiffer Omistar quadrupole online MS, observing the key component using the following mass spectrum signals: H2O (m/z = 18), CO (m/z = 28), O2 (m/z = 32), Ar (m/z = 40), CO2 (m/z = 44).

CO pulsed chemisorption was performed on the same instrument. The catalyst was firstly reduced in H2 at 400 °C for 1 h, followed by Ar purging for 0.5 h. After cooling to room temperature, the CO pulsed chemisorption was conducted with 0.5 mL 1%CO/Ar per loop until the adsorption was saturated. The outlet gas was analyzed using the Pfeiffer Omistar quadrupole online MS, observing the CO signal with m/z = 28.

H2-D2 temperature-programed surface reaction (TPSR) experiments were performed on the same system. The fresh sample was reduced in H2 at 400 °C for 1 h, followed by He purging for 0.5 h. After cooling to room temperature, D2 and H2 were then introduced to the system. Until a satisfactory signal/noise ratio, the sample was heated to 200 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min. The m/z values are 2 for H2, 4 for D2, and 3 for HD, respectively.

NH3-TPD characterization was conducted on the Micromeritics AutoChem 2950 HP instrument equipped with a MS detector. The catalyst was firstly reduced in H2 at 400 °C for 1 h, followed by He purging for 0.5 h. After cooling to 50 °C, 10% NH3/He was introduced to the system with the adsorption for 0.5 h. Then the physically adsorbed NH3 was purged with He again for 0.5 h. The sample was heated to 600 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min. The desorption of NH3 is recorded with m/z = 16 in order to exclude the influence of H2O.

In situ Diffuse Reflectance Infrared Fourier Transform spectroscopy of CO adsorption (CO-DRIFTS) was recorded on a Bruker Equinox 55 spectrometer equipped with a mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector. The catalyst was firstly reduced in-situ in the DRIFTS cell in H2 at 400 °C for 1 h, followed by purging with Ar for 0.5 h at the same temperature. The catalyst was then cooled to 30 °C by cooling water. A flow of 5% CO/Ar was then introduced to the system until saturated adsorption, followed by Ar purging to remove the physical adsorbed CO.

The X-ray absorption fine spectroscopy (XAFS) experiment was conducted at the Ni K-edge on beamline 1W1B at Beijing synchrotron radiation facility (BSRF), which operated at 2.5 GeV with a current of 250 mA. A Si(111) double crystal monochromator was used. The photon flux at 11.58 keV was ~1010 photon/s. Data were acquired in fluorescence mode, using Lytle detector. Ni foil and NiO were used as standards. Before measurement, all of the samples were reduced in H2 (30 mL/min) at 400 °C for 1 h, and carefully sealed under argon protection in a glove box. The XAS data were processed and analyzed using Demeter software package47.

Activity test

Catalyst performance was evaluated in a tubular packed bed flow reactor at atmospheric pressure. The catalyst was reduced at 400 °C for 1 h (heating rate: 10 °C/min) in flowing H2 (30 mL/min). After cooling to the reaction temperature, the reactant was injected through a syringe pump (KDS100, KD Scientific) and vaporized under H2 flow at 205 °C before entering the reactor. The outlet was heated to 220 °C before entering the online gas chromatography (Fuli, GC9790Plus) for analyzation and quantification. The typical reaction conditions of m-cresol HDO were: temperature 350 °C, pressure 1 atm, H2/m-cresol molar ratio of 60. The W/F (h) is defined as the ratio of catalyst mass (g) to reactant mass feed flow rate (g/h). The conversion and yield are reported in molcarbon%.

The in-situ probe reaction of isopropanol was conducted using the same reactor. Firstly, the reduced catalyst was subjected to m-cresol HDO reaction at 350 °C for 5 or 125 min, with these times representing the fresh catalyst state and coke deposited catalyst state, respectively. Then the system was purged by N2 (20 ml/min) and cooled to 250 °C. After that, isopropanol was injected through the pump and the yields of acetone and propene were analyzed online at 15 min time on stream (TOS). The conversions of m-cresol and isopropanol are all controlled below 10% by adjusting W/F.

DFT calculation

DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP)48,49. The core and valence electrons were represented by the projector augmented wave (PAW) method and plane-wave basis functions with a kinetic energy cut-off of 500 eV50. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional was used in the calculations51. A Monkhorst-Pack grid of 2 × 2 × 1 was used to sample the surface Brillouin zone, which was deemed appropriate for k-point convergence given the large size of the supercell (>130 atoms). Ground-state atomic geometries were obtained by minimizing the forces on the atoms below 0.02 eV/Å. The transition states were obtained using the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method by relaxing the force below 0.05 eV/Å.

According to the experimental characterization results, the Ni3Sn intermetallic was used to model the NiSn alloy52. For comparison with Ni(111) surface, a more preferential (111) surface was used to model the alloy catalyst surface, with five slabs of a 6×6 unit cell and the top two layers allowed relaxing in the calculations. A distance of 15 Å is added to the vacuum layer in the calculation to avoid the interaction between each slab. To model the TiOx on Ni/NiSn alloy surface, a titanium trimer (Ti3O6 and Ti3O5) was deposited on Ni(111) or Ni3Sn(111) surface41,43. The primary objective of DFT study is to compare the catalytic function of these inverse interfaces against pure Ni and NiSn surfaces, and to identify the core functionality provided by TiOx and Sn species in m-cresol HDO reaction. While the location of TiOx cluster on low-coordinated metal surface, such as step sites, is evidenced by CO-DRIFTS results, the choice of (111) surface models here provides a well-defined benchmark system for investigating the above objective. Further discussion on the validity of the models is provided in the Supplementary Discussion 2 and Supplementary Figs. 27–28.
