Robust joint optical delay and received signal strength positioning for visible light communication systems using particle swarm optimization
Authors: Iván Sánchez, Pablo Palacios Játiva, María Camila Reyes, David Zabala-Blanco, Cesar Azurdia Meza, Ismael Soto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate indoor positioning in Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS)-denied environments remains a critical challenge for next-generation intelligent spaces, industrial automation, and context-aware services. Visible light communication (VLC) and visible light positioning (VLP) have emerged as attractive solutions due to their dual functionality of illumination and communication, immunity to electromagnetic interference, and potential for high-accuracy localization. However, practical VLC/VLP systems are highly sensitive to receiver orientation, LED/front-end nonlinearities, ambient light, multipath reflections, and hardware-related timing offsets and RSS measurement distortions. In this paper, we propose a robust joint optical delay–received signal strength (RSS) positioning framework for VLC systems, where delay-derived pseudorange information and received optical power measurements are fused under a unified weighted negative log-likelihood formulation. Unlike conventional VLP schemes that rely on either RSS-only or pseudorange-only inference under Gaussian assumptions, the proposed approach explicitly accounts for non-Gaussian measurement distortions and per-link timing bias in the optical domain. The user position and per-link timing-bias parameters are jointly estimated through particle swarm optimization (PSO), enabling robust operation in highly nonlinear and multimodal VLC localization landscapes. In the evaluated LOS-dominant scenario, the proposed joint optical delay–RSS estimator achieves an RMSE of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.24\,\textrm{m}$$\end{document} and a 95th percentile error of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.46\,\textrm{m}$$\end{document}, outperforming the Optical pseudorange WNLS, RSS-only Lambertian WNLS, and Joint pseudorange–RSS (PSO + Huber) baselines. This framework provides a calibration-efficient and Lambertian-consistent solution for indoor optical positioning and constitutes a promising basis for accurate localization in smart buildings, hospitals, warehouses, and industrial environments. These results should be interpreted within the simulation-based scope of the present study, and experimental validation remains necessary before drawing stronger deployment-oriented conclusions.

Introduction

Accurate indoor localization in GNSS-denied environments is important for asset tracking, automation, and context-aware services. Visible light communication (VLC) and visible light positioning (VLP) are attractive because deployed LED luminaires can simultaneously support illumination, communication, and localization, especially in environments where RF emissions are constrained1–3.

VLC-based positioning benefits from directional optical propagation and reduced electromagnetic interference, but practical accuracy is strongly affected by receiver orientation, ambient light, partial shadowing, device imperfections, and non-line-of-sight (NLOS) reflections4,5. In addition, recent device-level studies have shown that GaN MQW LEDs exhibit injection-dependent nonlinear behavior in both illumination intensity and electro-optic response, which is not fully captured by a purely geometric Lambertian propagation model6. As a result, the relationship between measured observables and user position is highly nonlinear and often deviates from ideal Gaussian assumptions7–10.

Most existing VLP techniques rely on a dominant observable, such as RSS, image-based measurements, timing-related features or machine learning algorithms11. RSS-based methods exploit LOS channel structure but are sensitive to orientation mismatch, gain uncertainty, and multipath distortion, whereas timing-based methods provide complementary geometric information but are affected by synchronization uncertainty, front-end delays, and measurement bias. Recent implementations include photodiode-array, OCC-based, and learning-driven approaches12–19. PSO-based optimization has also been explored in application-specific VLP settings, including RSSI-based positioning with random-angle interference cancellation and VLC localization in hospital environments5,20.

A promising way to address these limitations is to combine heterogeneous observables within a joint estimation framework. Related multi-sensor and multi-observable studies in indoor positioning have shown clear robustness gains when complementary measurements are fused and bias terms are explicitly modeled21–24. Inspired by this idea, we argue that a similar fusion strategy can be reformulated in the optical domain, where timing-related delay information and optical RSS measurements can be jointly exploited to improve positioning robustness and accuracy. However, this adaptation is not a direct variable substitution. In VLC systems, the RSS observable must be derived from the Lambertian optical channel model, while the timing-related observable must account for optical delay estimation mechanisms and hardware-dependent bias terms. At the same time, it is important to distinguish the propagation model from the electro-optic device model: the present paper focuses on positioning observables and LOS propagation geometry, whereas explicit injection-dependent LED nonlinearity is not modeled through a compact MQW device description. In addition, the error statistics in VLC are often asymmetric or heavy-tailed due to clipping, saturation, ambient illumination, and intermittent LOS blockage, which makes Gaussian-based formulations insufficient.

Motivated by these challenges, this paper proposes a robust joint optical delay–RSS positioning framework for VLC systems. The main idea is to combine delay-derived pseudorange information and optical power measurements in a unified weighted negative log-likelihood formulation, while explicitly accounting for non-Gaussian residuals and per-link timing bias. The resulting optimization problem is highly nonlinear and may contain multiple local minima due to the geometry of the optical channel and the coupling between position, incidence angle, and received power. To address this issue, we adopt particle swarm optimization (PSO) as a global search strategy capable of exploring multimodal cost surfaces without requiring gradient information. This makes the resulting estimator well suited for practical VLC localization scenarios, especially when coarse initialization is available but model mismatch and measurement irregularities remain significant25,26. The novelty claimed here is therefore not the isolated use of PSO, but its integration with a joint optical delay–RSS weighted negative log-likelihood, explicit per-link bias estimation, and robust heavy-tailed residual modeling within a unified VLC positioning framework. It should be emphasized that the present study is methodological and simulation-based. In particular, the optical-delay observable is introduced as a synchronization-assisted pseudorange abstraction to assess the benefit of robust delay–RSS fusion under realistic bias and non-Gaussian disturbances, rather than as an immediately deployable hardware prototype.

This joint formulation is attractive because it combines complementary delay and RSS observables, absorbs per-link timing offsets through explicit bias terms, and uses robust likelihood modeling to tolerate asymmetric and non-Gaussian disturbances. In principle, it is also extensible to three-dimensional positioning, receiver orientation estimation, angle-diversity reception, and hybrid LOS/NLOS conditions9,22.

Methods

System model

In this section, we introduce the considered indoor VLC positioning scenario and the mathematical models associated with the two observables exploited in this work, namely optical delay and RSS. The framework considered here assumes a set of LED luminaires with known locations and a mobile user equipped with a photodetector (PD) receiver whose position is to be estimated.

Optical delay-based observation model

Depending on the implementation, this observable may be obtained from ToA, synchronization-assisted delay estimation, or delay-derived pseudoranging. The use of both observables is motivated by their complementary nature. On the one hand, delay-related measurements provide geometric ranging information that is directly linked to the propagation distance. Localization problem statement

Based on the previous models, the localization task can be stated as the problem of estimating the receiver position from noisy and possibly biased optical measurements. Let18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\boldsymbol{\theta }} = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \textbf{p}, & \text {position-only estimation},\\ {[}{\textbf{p}}^{T},{\textbf{b}}^{T}]^T, & \text {joint position-and-bias estimation}. \end{array}\right. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The estimation problem can then be expressed in general form as19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta }}} = \arg \min _{\boldsymbol{\theta }} \; \mathscr {C}({\boldsymbol{\theta }}), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {C}(\boldsymbol{\theta })$$\end{document} is a cost function constructed from the mismatch between the measured observables and their model-based counterparts.

Since both the RSS and delay models are nonlinear with respect to the receiver position, and because the residual statistics may deviate from ideal Gaussian assumptions, the resulting optimization landscape can be highly nonconvex and multimodal. This motivates the use of a robust joint likelihood-based formulation and a global optimization strategy, which will be developed in the next section.

Proposed joint robust positioning method

This section presents the proposed localization framework based on the joint exploitation of synchronization-assisted optical delay pseudoranges and received optical power measurements. First, the adopted estimation model is introduced. Then, a weighted robust negative log-likelihood cost function is formulated, followed by the PSO-based solution strategy.

Optical delay pseudorange formulation

In practical VLC systems, directly implementing round-trip time (RTT) estimation is not always the most convenient approach due to the stringent timing requirements imposed by the speed of light and the hardware complexity of bidirectional optical timing exchanges. Instead, a more suitable formulation consists of using synchronization-assisted one-way delay estimation, where each LED periodically transmits a known beacon and the receiver estimates the corresponding time of arrival relative to a common timing reference or a pre-established synchronization structure. At meter-scale indoor distances, the corresponding propagation times are on the order of only a few nanoseconds, so a direct experimental implementation would require tightly synchronized transmitters and receivers, high-bandwidth front ends, and specialized timestamping or time-to-digital conversion hardware. For reference, the decimeter-to-centimeter pseudorange disturbances used later in the numerical section correspond to sub-nanosecond residual timing errors; for example, a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.10\,\textrm{m}$$\end{document} bias corresponds to about \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.33\,\textrm{ns}$$\end{document}, while a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.06\,\textrm{m}$$\end{document} scale corresponds to about \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.20\,\textrm{ns}$$\end{document}. The exact beacon bandwidth, waveform, and sampling strategy are implementation dependent and are therefore not fixed in the present system-level study. Consequently, the adopted pseudorange errors should be interpreted as post-synchronization, post-processing residual timing errors rather than as raw sampling intervals. For this reason, the present paper focuses on the system-level estimation model and leaves full hardware realization of the optical-delay block for future work.

For this reason, the timing-related observable considered in this work is modeled as an optical delay pseudorange. Specifically, for the i-th LED, the measured pseudorange is written as20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\rho }_i = c\,\hat{\tau }_i, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\tau }_i$$\end{document} is the estimated optical propagation-related delay and c is the speed of light.

Unlike an ideal geometric range, the measured quantity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\rho }_i$$\end{document} may include hardware- and synchronization-related offsets. Accordingly, the optical delay pseudorange model is expressed as21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\rho }_i = d_i(\textbf{p}) + b_i + n_i^{(\rho )}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_i(\textbf{p})$$\end{document} is the Euclidean distance between the receiver and the i-th LED, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_i$$\end{document} is a bias term accounting for systematic timing offsets, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_i^{(\rho )}$$\end{document} denotes the residual error.

The use of per-link bias terms \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_i$$\end{document} is justified by the fact that different optical transmitters and front-end processing chains may introduce non-identical delays, especially in distributed VLC infrastructures. These biases may originate from driver circuitry, beacon generation latency, clock misalignment, signal thresholding, analog front-end delays, or implementation-dependent timestamping effects. Therefore, the pseudorange model in (21) provides a flexible and physically meaningful abstraction for timing-based VLC positioning.

For each LED transmitter, two heterogeneous observations are available:an optical delay pseudorange measurement \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\rho }_i$$\end{document},an RSS measurement \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{P}_{r,i}$$\end{document}.

an optical delay pseudorange measurement \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\rho }_i$$\end{document},

The model-based counterparts of these observations are23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \rho _i(\boldsymbol{\theta }) = d_i(\textbf{p}) + b_i, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}and24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P_{r,i}(\boldsymbol{\theta }) = P_{t,i} H_i(\textbf{p}), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_i(\textbf{p})$$\end{document} is the VLC channel gain defined in the System Model subsection.

The corresponding residuals are defined as25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} e_i^{(\rho )}(\boldsymbol{\theta }) = \hat{\rho }_i - \rho _i(\boldsymbol{\theta }) = \hat{\rho }_i - d_i(\textbf{p}) - b_i, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}and26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} e_i^{(P)}(\boldsymbol{\theta }) = \hat{P}_{r,i} - P_{r,i}(\boldsymbol{\theta }). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}These residuals reflect the mismatch between the measured optical observables and the position-dependent channel model. Since both observables are affected by nonlinear geometry and practical hardware impairments, the residuals generally exhibit non-Gaussian behavior. As a result, robust likelihood-based modeling is more appropriate than conventional least-squares formulations.

Robust weighted negative log-likelihood formulation

To jointly exploit the two observables, we formulate the localization problem as the minimization of a weighted negative log-likelihood (NLL) cost. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{\rho }(\cdot )$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{P}(\cdot )$$\end{document} denote the probability density functions associated with the pseudorange and RSS residuals, respectively. Without restricting the formulation to a specific residual distribution, the joint cost function is written as27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_{\rho }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_P$$\end{document} are nonnegative weighting factors, and28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {C}_{\rho }(\boldsymbol{\theta }) = \sum _{i=1}^{N} -\log f_{\rho } \!\left( e_i^{(\rho )}(\boldsymbol{\theta }) \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {C}_{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta }) = \sum _{i=1}^{N} -\log f_{P} \!\left( e_i^{(P)}(\boldsymbol{\theta }) influence of the delay and RSS domains. In general, these weights can be selected according to prior measurement confidence, calibration data, estimated variances, or empirical normalization criteria. A practical choice is to define them inversely proportional to the nominal dispersion of each observable, thus preventing either domain from dominating the optimization solely due to scale mismatch.

To make the formulation concrete while preserving robustness, we adopt a generalized heavy-tailed residual model. One suitable option is the Laplacian likelihood, which yields the following cost components:30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {C}_{\rho }(\boldsymbol{\theta }) = \sum _{i=1}^{N} \frac{|e_i^{(\rho )}(\boldsymbol{\theta })|}{\beta _{\rho }}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {C}_{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta }) = \sum _{i=1}^{N} \frac{|e_i^{(P)}(\boldsymbol{\theta })|}{\beta _{P}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _{\rho }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _P$$\end{document} are scale parameters associated with the pseudorange and RSS residuals, respectively.

The adoption of Laplacian-type penalties is well justified in VLC localization because measurement errors may become heavy-tailed under partial LOS blockage, ambient illumination bursts, clipping, gain uncertainty, orientation mismatch, and imperfect threshold-based timing extraction. Compared with quadratic least-squares penalties, the proposed NLL formulation is less sensitive to occasional outliers and therefore better suited for practical optical positioning scenarios.

Accordingly, the final optimization problem is expressed as32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta }} = \arg \min _{\boldsymbol{\theta }\in \Omega } \mathscr {C}(\boldsymbol{\theta }), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where Ω denotes the feasible search region, defined by the room dimensions, admissible receiver height range, and physically plausible bias intervals.

Identifiability considerations

The identifiability of joint position-and-bias estimation depends on both the number of observables and their sensitivity to different subsets of the unknown parameters. In the specific setting evaluated in this paper, the receiver height is fixed and four LEDs are used, so the estimator processes eight scalar observations (four optical pseudoranges and four RSS values) to estimate six unknowns, namely \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[x,y,b_1,b_2,b_3,b_4]^T$$\end{document}. Under these conditions, the problem is not equivalent to unconstrained pseudorange-only trilateration with free per-link biases. If only the pseudorange equations were used, the bias terms could indeed absorb a significant part of the geometric mismatch, making the problem weakly constrained. However, the RSS equations do not depend on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_i$$\end{document}; they constrain only the receiver position through the Lambertian channel geometry. Consequently, the bias variables cannot improve the pseudorange fit without remaining compatible with the RSS measurements. For nondegenerate anchor placement and locally informative RSS gradients, the combined delay–RSS model is therefore locally well constrained in the 2D fixed-height scenario considered here.

In addition, the per-link bias vector is interpreted in this work as a quasi-static nuisance parameter over one estimation batch, not as a fast time-varying random process. Accordingly, the bias vector is re-estimated independently at each receiver location, rather than calibrated once over multiple known points. In this sense, the four-LED configuration should be interpreted as a minimum working setup for the fixed-height 2D case studied here, whereas additional anchors would improve redundancy and numerical robustness in noisier or more general deployments. More general cases, such as fully 3D positioning with unknown height, explicit orientation estimation, or rapidly time-varying per-link biases, would generally require extra observability support, for example through additional anchors, temporal regularization, or prior calibration constraints.

Normalization and practical weight selection

Since optical pseudorange residuals and RSS residuals are expressed in different units, a proper normalization strategy is required to ensure numerical balance in the joint objective. To this end, the weighting coefficients can be selected as33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} w_{\rho } = \frac{1}{\sigma _{\rho }+\epsilon }, \qquad w_{P} = \frac{1}{\sigma _{P}+\epsilon }, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _{\rho }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _P$$\end{document} denote the nominal scales of the corresponding residuals and ε>0 is a small constant introduced to avoid numerical instability.

Under the Laplacian penalties in (30)–(31), a convenient practical default is to let the scale parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _{\rho }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _P$$\end{document} absorb most of the cross-domain normalization, in which case \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_{\rho }=w_P=1$$\end{document} becomes a reasonable choice. If a short calibration batch is available, robust Laplacian-consistent scale estimates can be obtained as34\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\beta }_{\rho } = \frac{\textrm{median}_j\left| r_j^{(\rho )}-\textrm{median}(\{r_j^{(\rho )}\})\right| }{\ln 2}, \qquad \hat{\beta }_{P} = \frac{\textrm{median}_j\left| r_j^{(P)}-\textrm{median}(\{r_j^{(P)}\})\right| }{\ln 2}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_j^{(\rho )}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_j^{(P)}$$\end{document} denote calibration residuals in the delay and RSS domains, respectively. These residuals may be obtained from a short startup calibration stage, from measurements at known reference points, or from a coarse preliminary estimator.

In the numerical evaluation of this paper, the scale parameters are fixed to the nominal disturbance levels used in the synthetic measurement generator, namely \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _{\rho }=0.06\,\textrm{m}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _P=0.08\,P_{r,i}^{\textrm{max}}$$\end{document}, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_{\rho }=w_P=1$$\end{document} is used throughout. Importantly, these quantities are kept fixed across all test points and compared methods rather than tuned per scenario, so that the reported gains are not driven by hyperparameter retuning. If calibration data are not available, (33) provides a conservative alternative based on nominal modality dispersions or sensor specifications.

PSO-based estimation strategy

The optimization problem in (32) is nonlinear and generally nonconvex because both the pseudorange model and the RSS channel gain depend on the receiver position in a highly nonlinear manner. Moreover, the joint inclusion of bias parameters increases the dimensionality of the search space and may introduce multiple local minima. For these reasons, gradient-based solvers may converge to suboptimal solutions, particularly when the initial guess is inaccurate or when the measurements are corrupted by non-Gaussian disturbances.

To address this issue, we employ PSO as a derivative-free global search strategy. PSO is well suited for multimodal optimization problems and has the advantage of being simple to implement while requiring only repeated evaluations of the objective function.

Let the swarm contain S particles. Methodological considerations

The proposed formulation differs from conventional RSS-only VLP methods by incorporating an additional timing-based observable that provides direct geometric information. It also differs from purely range-based optical localization methods by introducing RSS as a complementary source of spatial discrimination through the Lambertian channel response. The resulting framework is particularly attractive in scenarios where one of the observables becomes temporarily less reliable. For example, RSS may be strongly perturbed by receiver tilt or gain uncertainty, whereas pseudorange measurements may remain informative; conversely, when timing estimates become noisy, the RSS observable can still constrain the search space.

Furthermore, the explicit estimation of the bias vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{b}$$\end{document} enhances the practical relevance of the method, since it avoids the need for idealized assumptions regarding perfectly synchronized or delay-free optical hardware. This feature is especially important when considering real VLC implementations in which different luminaires may exhibit unequal processing latencies. Under the adopted system-level assumptions, the combination of robust residual modeling, heterogeneous optical observables, and PSO-based global optimization therefore provides a flexible estimation framework for indoor visible light positioning.

This disturbance model should therefore be interpreted as a phenomenological surrogate at the positioning-observable level. It captures non-Gaussian RSS corruption after propagation and front-end processing, but it does not explicitly reproduce NLOS multipath reflection, severe hard-shadowing events, PD saturation/clipping, or injection-dependent MQW LED nonlinearities, efficiency-droop effects, and waveform-level electro-optic distortion. The particle swarm optimizer uses a swarm size of S=40 particles and a maximum of 120 iterations. To accelerate convergence, the swarm is initialized around the solution of the Optical pseudorange WNLS estimator, in the same spirit as the initialization strategy adopted in the reference paper. This reduces the computational burden and improves the probability of convergence toward the global minimum.

The adopted configuration S=40 and K=120 should be interpreted as a pragmatic compromise between global exploration and computational cost. Under this setting, the worst-case budget is S× K=4800 objective-function evaluations per position estimate. Increasing either the swarm size or the iteration cap improves search diversity only at a linearly increasing computational cost, whereas aggressive reductions in S or K would generally make the search more sensitive to multimodality and local minima. From a convergence standpoint, the Optical pseudorange WNLS solution acts as a geometry-consistent warm start that narrows the effective search region and reduces sensitivity to purely random initialization. Since the absolute wall-clock runtime depends strongly on software implementation, programming language, and hardware parallelism, the present manuscript reports platform-independent evaluation counts and representative asymptotic complexity rather than hardware-specific execution times.

Discussion

The results support three main conclusions. First, joint delay–RSS fusion is beneficial because the pseudorange observable contributes distance-like geometric constraints while RSS adds angle- and gain-dependent discrimination through the Lambertian channel law. Second, explicit bias estimation is important in timing-assisted VLC localization: the bias-sensitivity analysis shows that the advantage of the proposed estimator becomes more pronounced as the bias magnitude increases. Third, the Laplacian NLL formulation improves robustness to non-Gaussian optical disturbances, particularly in the upper tail of the error distribution.

Overall, the obtained sub-meter accuracy is consistent with a structured LOS environment with moderate measurement corruption. At the same time, the results should be interpreted within the methodological scope of the paper: the scenario remains simulation-based and LOS-dominant, and the PSO-based solution trades computational cost for global-search robustness. From a complexity perspective, the proposed estimator is computationally heavier than the WNLS baselines, but it does not incur a higher asymptotic order than Joint pseudorange–RSS (PSO + Huber) and achieves better localization accuracy under the same optimization framework.

Despite these encouraging results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the evaluation is based on a simulated LOS-dominant environment with four anchors and controlled perturbations, which may not fully capture the variability of real deployments. Second, receiver orientation is treated through perturbation tests rather than explicit orientation estimation, leaving room for further improvement in handheld scenarios. Third, although PSO improves robustness in nonconvex settings, its computational cost may increase with larger search spaces and denser anchor deployments. In addition, experimental verification of the synchronization-assisted optical delay component would require dedicated high-resolution timing instrumentation that is beyond the scope of the present study. We also note that there is currently no widely adopted public dataset, to the best of our knowledge, that jointly provides synchronized VLC pseudorange-like delay observations, RSS measurements, and per-link timing-bias effects under a known optical anchor geometry. The present RSS model is also phenomenological with respect to LED device physics: while it captures aggregate distortion at the measurement level, it does not explicitly include the injection-dependent electro-optic nonlinearity of GaN MQW LEDs reported in recent device-level VLC studies6. Finally, the observability arguments of the present estimator rely on a fixed-height 2D configuration with quasi-static per-link biases; extending the same formulation to fully 3D operation, unknown orientation, or rapidly drifting biases would require additional constraints such as more anchors or temporal priors. The current PSO implementation should therefore be interpreted as an algorithmic validation tool rather than as a finalized real-time embedded solution. Experimental validation in real VLC testbeds and complexity-aware implementations therefore remain important next steps.

Conclusion

This paper presented a robust joint optical delay–RSS positioning framework for indoor VLC systems. This approach was developed to address practical VLC localization challenges arising from timing bias, ambient-light perturbations, receiver orientation sensitivity, and aggregate front-end/RSS distortions. To this end, synchronization-assisted optical delay pseudoranges and Lambertian RSS measurements were fused through a weighted negative log-likelihood formulation with explicit per-link bias estimation.

Because the resulting estimation problem is nonlinear and multimodal, particle swarm optimization was adopted to jointly estimate the receiver position and the bias vector without relying on gradient information. This design allows the method to remain effective under non-Gaussian optical measurement disturbances and realistic model mismatch conditions.

The reported numerical results showed that the proposed estimator outperforms representative pseudorange-only, RSS-only, and alternative robust fusion baselines in the considered VLC indoor scenario. In addition to improving the overall error distribution, the method also exhibited stronger robustness under receiver tilt perturbations and increased timing bias. Within the considered LOS-dominant, simulation-based setting, these results confirm that the joint exploitation of optical delay and RSS observables is an effective strategy for improving visible light positioning robustness and accuracy.

Within the adopted modeling assumptions, this framework therefore provides a robust and technically grounded basis for indoor VLC positioning and a promising starting point for future experimental validation in smart and industrial optical wireless environments.

Future work

Future work will focus on extending the proposed framework in several directions. First, explicit receiver orientation estimation should be incorporated in order to better capture practical handheld and mobile-device operation. Second, more advanced optical propagation models including NLOS components and partial blockage effects should be considered. Third, physics-based compact models of LED electro-optic nonlinearity should be integrated into the RSS-generation stage so that injection-dependent device behavior can be propagated to the positioning observables more faithfully. Fourth, the proposed method should be validated experimentally using real VLC testbeds, synchronized optical beacons, and dedicated high-resolution timing hardware for optical delay extraction. Fifth, implementation-oriented acceleration strategies such as parallel particle evaluation, adaptive swarm sizing, and early-stopping criteria should be investigated for real-time optical positioning. Finally, adaptive or learning-assisted weighting and residual modeling strategies may further improve localization robustness in dynamic indoor environments.
