Sex differences in the association of social determinants of health and adverse cardiovascular outcomes in patients with atrial fibrillation
Authors: Yusheng Zhou, Jonathan Houle, Valeria Raparelli, Colleen M Norris, Louise Pilote
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite anticoagulation, patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) experience persistent elevated cardiovascular risk, with conflicting evidence regarding sex-based outcome disparities. Social determinants of health (SDOH)—encompassing economic, psychosocial and environmental factors—demonstrate robust associations with cardiovascular outcomes and exhibit significant sex-specific patterns, yet remain understudied in AF populations. This study aimed to clarify sex differences in the association of SDOH and adverse cardiovascular outcomes in patients with AF.MethodsData came from the UK Biobank. Participants with AF enrolled between 2006 and 2010 were included. SDOH comprised economic, psychosocial and neighbourhood environmental factors. The primary outcome was a composite of major adverse cardiovascular events (ie, stroke/transient ischaemic attack, arterial thromboembolic events, myocardial infarction and cardiovascular mortality) and all-cause mortality. Sex-stratified, Cox proportional hazards models were used.ResultsAmong 3842 participants (mean age 62.5±6.1 years; 35.1% female), males demonstrated higher adverse outcome event rates than females (29.1% vs 21.3%) over median 11.6-year follow-up. Multivariate analyses revealed independent SDOH associations with adverse outcomes, with distinct sex-specific patterns. In male participants, low income (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.55), unemployment (HR 1.28, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.55), living alone (HR 1.29, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.55) and housing insecurity (HR 1.26, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.57) were associated with adverse outcomes, while emotional distress was the only predictor (HR 1.33, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.69) in females.ConclusionsSDOH demonstrate sex-specific associations with adverse cardiovascular outcomes in AF populations. Integration of SDOH into risk prediction algorithms may enhance cardiovascular risk stratification and inform targeted intervention strategies in AF management.Keywords: Atrial Fibrillation, RISK FACTORS, Epidemiology, Women

Introduction

Despite improvements in management and therapeutics, age-adjusted mortality in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) is increasing.1 2 In contemporary trials using direct oral anticoagulants, stroke-related and bleeding-related deaths each accounted for only 6% of all deaths, while other cardiovascular-related deaths accounted for 46% of all deaths.3 Therefore, predicting major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), which include strokes, thromboembolic events, myocardial infarctions, cardiovascular deaths and all-cause mortality, is of the utmost importance.

Females and males with AF differ in many ways. For instance, the incidence of AF in females is lower than in males, but given females with AF live longer, the lifetime risk of AF in both sexes is similar. Females have also been shown to be less likely to be prescribed anticoagulation, to be cardioverted or to undergo catheter ablation.4 Yet, females have been found to experience more severe symptoms than males.5 6 The association between sex and outcomes in AF is controversial. The 2024 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Guidelines represent a shift in sex-based cardiovascular risk assessment, removing female sex from the CHA₂DS₂-VASc scoring system (a widely used stroke risk assessing tool) and introducing the comprehensive CARE (Comorbidity, Anticoagulation, Risk factors, Education) approach for AF management.7 This controversial decision reflects emerging evidence suggesting sex functions as a risk modifier rather than an independent risk factor, though this interpretation remains subject to ongoing clinical debate among experts and requires further validation across diverse patient populations.8 9

These sex differences might be related to unmeasured factors such as social determinants of health (SDOH). Few studies have looked at the impact of SDOH on outcomes in AF but socioeconomic status (SES), education, social support and living in countries with greater gender inequalities were found to be independent predictors of outcomes in AF.10,12 Indeed, SDOH are rarely considered or collected in cardiovascular research13 14 despite being recommended,15 thus limiting the investigation of whether they differentially modify risk between sexes. This study aimed to investigate for sex differences in the associations between SDOH and adverse outcomes in individuals with AF.

Methods

Data and population

Data were obtained from the UK Biobank, a large-scale prospective cohort study of over 500 000 participants aged 37–73 years from 22 centres across England, Scotland and Wales between 2006 and 2010.16 Participants underwent comprehensive baseline assessments at dedicated centres across the UK.17 Follow-up data were collected using electronic health records, primary care data, hospital inpatient registries, death certificate records and self-reported medical conditions. All disease diagnoses were systematically recorded using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes. For the current cohort, participants with a diagnosis of AF from 2 years before enrolment until 2 years after were identified using ICD-10 code I48. This time frame was selected to maximise temporal coherence between AF diagnosis and baseline SDOH measurement (figure 1).

Flow diagram of participant selection for sex-stratified analysis of social determinants of health in UK Biobank cohort. AFib, atrial fibrillation.

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this research. This study used pre-existing deidentified data from the UK Biobank cohort, where participants had provided informed consent for future research use. Given the secondary data analysis design, direct patient involvement in study development was not feasible.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was a composite of MACE and all-cause mortality. MACE included stroke/transient ischaemic attack, arterial thromboembolic events, myocardial infarction and cardiovascular mortality. Time to the composite outcome was defined as the time in days between AF diagnosis and the occurrence of the outcome. Participants who experienced the primary outcome before their AF diagnosis were excluded (figure 1). Study follow-up extended until February 2022.

Social determinants of health

We used the systematic multistep approach proposed by the GOING-FWD (Gender Outcomes INternational Group: to Further Well-being Development) consortium (https://www.mcgill.ca/going-fwd4gender/), which focuses on sex, sociocultural gender and chronic non-communicable diseases. This framework facilitates the identification of sex and gender-related social determinants and health outcomes in large-scale data sets while providing standardised analytical approaches.18 Using this framework, alongside other social determinant frameworks and considering data availability,19,21 we identified 16 baseline variables across three domains: economic factors, psychosocial factors and neighbourhood environment characteristics. Each variable was collected at enrolment and assigned a dichotomous score, with 1 denoting a disadvantageous social condition and 0 denoting an advantageous one (online supplemental table 1).

Covariates

Covariate selection was guided by established cardiovascular risk prediction frameworks and clinical evidence.12 Components of the CHADS₂ score, which are predictive of MACE and all-cause mortality in patients with AF, were included as covariates: history of heart failure, hypertension, age, diabetes and stroke. Additional predictors of MACE and all-cause mortality were also incorporated into the model: chronic kidney disease (stages 3 or higher), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, anticoagulant utilisation, body mass index (BMI), smoking history and alcohol consumption. Age and BMI were treated as continuous variables, while remaining covariates were dichotomised.

Statistical analyses

Patient characteristics were analysed using descriptive statistics; means and SD were used for continuous variables, and frequencies for categorical variables. Baseline characteristics for all participants were stratified by sex and compared using Student’s t-test for continuous variables and χ² test for categorical variables. The composite outcome of MACE and all-cause mortality was analysed using multivariate Cox proportional hazards models. Sex-stratified models included SDOH, adjusting for age, clinical risk factors, anticoagulant use and lifestyle behaviour factors. Results were presented as adjusted HRs with 95% CIs.

To quantify the cumulative impact of the associations between SDOH and the composite outcome, we developed a sex-specific β coefficient weighted composite risk score.22 23 It was based on our a priori hypothesis that SDOH factors would demonstrate differential associations with cardiovascular outcomes between sexes, necessitating distinct weighting schemes.12 Each SDOH variable was dichotomised where higher values represented greater social disadvantage. We obtained β coefficients for each SDOH (comparing disadvantaged vs advantaged levels) from multivariate Cox regression models, adjusting for age, race, clinical factors and lifestyle behaviours. The weighted score was calculated by multiplying each binary SDOH by its corresponding β coefficient, summing, dividing by the total sum of β coefficients and multiplying by 100.23 Higher scores indicated greater social disadvantage. Participants were categorised into tertiles, representing ‘Advantaged’, ‘Neutral’ and ‘Disadvantaged’ groups. Sensitivity analysis employed an unweighted scoring approach to evaluate methodological robustness.

All hypothesis tests were two-sided, and a p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R (V.4.2.0). Missing values were imputed using chained equations, which rely on the distribution of other variables in the data set to impute data.24 25

Results

A total of 3842 patients were included in our analyses (35.1% female sex; mean age 62.5±6.1 years). Over a median follow-up time of 10.1±3.8 years, males experienced a significantly higher incidence of MACE and all-cause mortality compared with females (29.1% vs 21.3%, respectively). Baseline characteristics are shown in table 1. Compared with males, females reported lower rates of smoking (45.8% vs 59.2%) and alcohol consumption (35.1% vs 54.6%). Similarly, the baseline prevalence of multimorbidity (≥2 comorbidities) was significantly lower in females (7.8% vs 10.4%, p=0.013). Females were also less likely to be taking anticoagulants (11.8 vs 17.8) or antiplatelets (32.8 vs 40.7). Risk stratification revealed a predominantly low-risk AF cohort: 20.8% had CHA₂DS₂-VASc scores ≥2. This distribution explains the low anticoagulation utilisation rates and suggests our findings reflect patterns in early-stage or lower-risk AF populations rather than the typical high-risk AF cohorts encountered in contemporary clinical practice (table 1).

Multivariable analysis of the overall cohort demonstrated that male sex was associated with a 48% higher risk of the MACE and all-cause mortality (HR 1.48, 95% CI 1.29 to 1.70). Among SDOH factors, low income, unemployment, living alone, lack of housing ownership, emotional distress and limited access to green space were associated with a higher risk of MACE after adjustment for clinical variables (figure 2).

Forest plot of multivariable predictors for MACE and all-cause mortality. COPD, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; MACE, major adverse cardiovascular events.

Sex-stratified Cox proportional hazards models revealed distinct patterns of associations between SDOH and adverse outcomes (figure 3). Among females, only emotional distress was associated with the outcome (HRfemale 1.33, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.69). In contrast, males demonstrated broader social vulnerability, with multiple SDOH factors associated with the outcome: low income (HRmale 1.30, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.55), unemployment (HRmale 1.28, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.55), living alone (HRmale 1.29, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.55) and not owning a house (HRmale 1.26, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.57). Notably, while emotional distress was a strong predictor in females, it was not in males (p=0.303). Environmental factors such as access to green space showed no significant association with MACE in either group. No statistically significant interactions between sex and SDOH factors were observed.

Forest plot of sex-specific SDOH associations with MACE and all-cause mortality. MACE, major adverse cardiovascular events; SDOH, social determinants of health.

Analysis of individual outcome components revealed that while males had higher rates of myocardial infarction and mortality, there was no significant sex difference in the risk of stroke (online supplemental tables S2 and S3). When SDOH factors were integrated into a weighted risk score, patients in the ‘Disadvantaged’ group demonstrated a 90% higher risk of adverse outcomes compared with the ‘Advantaged’ group (HR 1.90, 95% CI 1.61 to 2.24), while the ‘Neutral’ group exhibited 31% increased risk (HR 1.31, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.55) (online supplemental table S4); no significant interaction between sex and weighted risk score was observed.

Discussion

This analysis of 3842 UK Biobank participants with AF demonstrates that social vulnerability is associated with the risk of MACE and all-cause mortality in a sex-specific manner. While females were more likely to experience economic disadvantage and emotional distress, males exhibited higher rates of social isolation. Notably, male patients with AF experienced more adverse events than their female counterparts, with SDOH burden mediating the higher risk of MACE even after comprehensive adjustment for established cardiovascular risk factors, suggesting that social determinants operate through pathways distinct from traditional clinical predictors.

Non-cardiovascular deaths comprised the largest component of adverse outcomes in both sexes (females: 11.6%, males: 15.9%), exceeding cardiovascular mortality. This aligns with contemporary evidence that non-cardiovascular causes now predominate in anticoagulated AF populations.3 The substantial burden of non-cardiovascular mortality suggests SDOH influence outcomes through broader pathways—such as health behaviours and healthcare access—beyond traditional cardiovascular mechanisms,20 reinforcing the need for comprehensive social risk assessment.

Overall, SDOH remained associated with a higher risk of adverse events even after adjusting for sex and ‘traditional’ cardiovascular risk factors. Though the literature on these associations is scarce, the findings are consistent with reports from a systematic review12 26 and of a US study of over 4500 patients with AF demonstrating that low SES is associated with a 30% higher relative risk of all-cause mortality.27 A Danish study involving more than 150 000 patients with AF also revealed that lower SES was associated with a 64% higher relative mortality risk.28 These previous analyses, however, were not sex-stratified, despite multiple reports showing that SDOH are not distributed equally between females and males and affect them differently.29,31 Our study therefore provides a novel perspective on the poorly understood impact of sex and SDOH on outcomes in AF.

Sex-specific analyses yielded unexpected results. Despite having more unfavourable SDOH profiles than males, females demonstrated a lower risk of MACE and all-cause mortality. Furthermore, fewer SDOH were significantly associated with adverse outcomes in females. Emotional distress only was associated with the outcome in females, while low income, unemployment, living alone and housing insecurity were all associated with the outcomes in males. This finding could be explained by several mechanisms. First, females without AF are known to have a lower lifetime risk of MACE and are known to have longer life expectancy than males.32 The fact that females with AF have historically had higher rates of MACE and all-cause mortality than males may be due to poorer access to care, which has rapidly improved over the last decade.29 33 Those improvements may have been further enhanced by different healthcare engagement patterns between sexes, with women typically demonstrating more proactive health-seeking behaviours and potentially better communication with healthcare providers.30 Second, it is possible that females simply have greater resilience to social disadvantage, with adaptive coping strategies that buffer the impact of disadvantaged SDOH on health outcomes.31 For males, the stronger association between SDOH and adverse outcomes may also stem from different vulnerabilities. For instance, despite higher incomes, higher employment rates and being less likely to live alone overall, lower income, unemployment and living alone were all associated with the outcome in males only. One explanation may be that males have less-developed social support networks than females and, when facing adversity, may have less available help while also being less prone to help-seeking behaviours due to traditional masculine norms.34 Furthermore, males and even more so older males have repeatedly been shown to have poorer emotional coping mechanisms than females.35,37

Our findings must be interpreted within the context of this cohort’s distinctive low-risk cardiovascular profile. Multiple clinical indicators demonstrate substantially lower baseline risk compared with typical AF patient cohorts: mean age of 62.5 years (versus >70 years in UK contemporary AF registries38), and remarkably low anticoagulation rates (11.8% females, 17.8% males) reflecting predominantly low CHA₂DS₂-VASc scores. The UK Biobank’s healthy volunteer selection bias compounds these characteristics, recruiting participants with higher SES and superior baseline health compared with the general population.39,41 This demographic profile likely attenuates SDOH effect estimates and may explain the protective effect of female sex observed in our analysis. Among higher socioeconomic populations, gender-based health disparities may be diminished, whereas socioeconomically disadvantaged populations demonstrate amplified SDOH effects and more pronounced sex-based outcome differences.42 43

Our weighted risk score incorporated multiple SDOH variables to thoroughly explore their cumulative impact on cardiovascular outcomes in patients with AF. While male sex remained independently associated with increased cardiovascular risk, we did not observe a significant sex interaction. It suggests that the magnitude of SDOH impact on outcomes appears similar across sexes at the aggregate level; however, it does not preclude the possibility of structural differences. This risk score approach aligns with emerging research incorporating social determinant data to strengthen risk predictive capabilities. One study created a SDOH score based on the original Framingham risk score and included self-reported SDOH data.44 Two studies also assessed the Area Deprivation Index’s ability to predict cardiovascular disease risk.45 46 Among these studies, very few have addressed potential sex differences in how SDOH differences influence outcomes. Future work should focus on developing more parsimonious sex-specific risk prediction tools that incorporate only the most impactful SDOH factors identified in this exploratory analysis.

Recently, evidence demonstrated a shift in sex-specific AF outcomes. Champsi et al revealed convergent cardiovascular outcomes between sexes in adequately anticoagulated populations, contrasting with historical data showing higher stroke and mortality rates in females.47 This evolution supports the 2024 ESC Guidelines removal of female sex from CHA₂DS₂-VASc scoring, recognising sex as a risk modifier rather than independent factor. Analyses by Tan et al48 and Liu et al49 further demonstrate limitations of traditional risk scores in contemporary populations and evolving AF epidemiology, like machine learning approaches, highlighting the value of incorporating multidimensional factors including social determinants. These advances align and contextualise our sex-specific SDOH findings. Historical observation of female disadvantage in AF outcomes likely reflected unmeasured confounding by social determinants—particularly the economic disadvantage and emotional distress patterns we observed. Such consideration could be integrated into existing frameworks such as the 2024 ESC ‘CARE’ approach for comprehensive AF management.7 While both sexes would benefit from SDOH assessment, interventions addressing social factors may need different emphasis—with particular attention to emotional support for females and individual economic factors for males. Further research should focus on understanding these differential vulnerability patterns and developing targeted interventions that address sex-specific pathways between social determinants and cardiovascular outcomes.

Conclusion

Despite rapid improvements in AF care and therapeutics, patients with AF remain at high risk of MACE and all-cause mortality. Several SDOH were found to be strongly and independently associated with adverse outcomes in AF, more so in males than in females. Therefore, SDOH may allow for more accurate, more equitable, risk prediction models and offer new targets for sex-specific interventions.
