Dirac Representation for Lattice Spin Operators: Spin- 1 / 2 1/2 and Spin- 1 1 cases
Maseim B. Kenmoe

Abstract
We introduce a quantum representation of lattice spin operators (LSOs) by mapping quantum spins to their classical analogues for spin values S = 1 / 2 S=1/2 and S = 1 S=1 . The resulting Dirac (bra–ket) forms are derived from the structure of binary and ternary digit distributions in non-negative integers. The construction requires only an enumeration of the lattice sites and is therefore independent on the spatial dimension and lattice geometry. Analytical expressions for extracting the j j th bit or trit of an integer without performing an explicit binary or ternary decomposition are obtained. As an application of the representation, we analyze the one-dimensional spin- 1 / 2 1/2 XXZ Heisenberg model with open boundaries in a magnetic field in both absence (uniform magnetic field) and presence of disorder (random magnetic field). In the absence of disorder (clean system), we demonstrate that the corresponding eigenvalue problem can be reduced to a tight-binding problem on a graph. For the isotropic case, this is solved analytically without resorting to any spinless transformation or the Bethe Ansatz. In the presence of disorder, a convergent perturbation theory is elaborated. Our analytical results are benchmarked against exact diagonalization for systems up to K ≤ 18 K\leq 18 spins. These computations exploit the global U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) symmetry to block-diagonalize the Hamiltonian and the spin-inversion symmetry to further decompose the zero-magnetization sector. Quantitative agreement between analytical and numerical results is observed.

II
Quantum-to-classical mapping

II.1
Quantum Representation

Consider a lattice of
K
identical spins of size
S
. Each spin lives in a local Hilbert space
\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{(j)}
(with
j=1,\cdots,K
) spanned by the basis vectors
\lvert m_{S}^{(j)}\rangle
(with
-S\leq m_{S}^{(j)}\leq S,
the eigenvalues of spin vectors along the quantization direction). The composite Hilbert space necessary to capture the dynamics of the ensemble is given by the tensor product of local sub-spaces,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{H}}=\bigotimesop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{(j)}.

(1)

Here,
j
labels the sites and should not be interpreted as a
1D
spatial coordinate. For a given lattice, including higher-dimensional lattices, one may introduce a correspondence
j\leftrightarrow\mathbf{R}_{j}
between
j
and the lattice sites. The tensor-product (
1
) and the Dirac representation developed below are invariant under this choice of enumeration. The lattice geometry only enters when interactions between specified sites (neighboring sites for example) are introduced through the Hamiltonian.

This space generated by the construction (
1
) is of dimension
D=d^{K}
with
d=2S+1
and spanned by the basis vectors,

\displaystyle\lvert m_{S}^{(1)}\cdots m_{S}^{(j)}\cdots m_{S}^{(K)}\rangle\equiv\otimes_{j=1}^{K}\lvert m_{S}^{(j)}\rangle.

(2)

These vectors are mutually orthogonal with a unique completeness relation thus forming a complete set of vectors. Denoting as
\hat{\vec{S}}_{j}=[\hat{S}_{j}^{x},\hat{S}_{j}^{y},\hat{S}_{j}^{z}]
the spin vector acting on the local subspace
\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{(j)}
and introducing the corresponding ladder operators
\hat{S}^{\pm}_{j}=\hat{S}_{j}^{x}\pm i\hat{S}_{j}^{y}
(which are non-Hermitian,
(\hat{S}_{j}^{+})^{\dagger}=\hat{S}_{j}^{-}
), then the spin action at a specified site is evaluated through (
\hbar=1
)

\displaystyle\hat{S}^{\pm}_{j}\lvert m_{S}\rangle_{j}=\lambda^{\pm}_{m_{S}^{(j)}}\lvert m_{S}^{(j)}\pm 1\rangle_{j},

(3)

\displaystyle\hat{S}^{z}_{j}\lvert m_{S}\rangle_{j}=m_{S}^{(j)}\lvert m_{S}^{(j)}\rangle_{j},

(4)

with
\lambda^{\pm}_{m_{S}^{(j)}}=[S(S+1)-m_{S}^{(j)}(m_{S}^{(j)}\pm 1)]^{1/2}
as found in many textbooks of quantum mechanics (see for e.g. Ref.
[
51
]
). In the above relations
\lvert m_{S}\rangle_{j}
is a shorthand notation for the basis vector in Eq.(
2
). The extra subscript emphasizes the lattice site on which the operator acts. One easily observes that the action of the lattice ladder spin operators
\hat{S}^{\pm}_{j}
onto a given configuration increases/decreases the
j
th quantum number by a unit. We shall see in our representation that this remark is rather counter-intuitive (see Section
II.2
).

Although the above relations at first glance look simple and elegant as one might expect the action of quantum operators to be, the intrinsic reality is rather complex. Indeed, a concrete Dirac (
“bra”
and
“ket”
) representation inferred from Eqs.(
3
) and (
4
) reads

\displaystyle\hat{S}^{\pm}_{j}=\sumop\displaylimits_{m_{S}^{(1)}=-S}^{S}\cdots\sumop\displaylimits_{m_{S}^{(j)}=-S}^{S}\cdots\sumop\displaylimits_{m_{S}^{(K)}=-S}^{S}\lambda^{\pm}_{m_{S}^{(j)}}

\displaystyle\times\lvert m_{S}^{(1)}\cdots m_{S}^{(j)}\pm 1\cdots m_{S}^{(K)}\rangle\langle m_{S}^{(1)}\cdots m_{S}^{(j)}\cdots m_{S}^{(K)}\rvert,

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{S}^{z}_{j}=\sumop\displaylimits_{m_{S}^{(1)}=-S}^{S}\cdots\sumop\displaylimits_{m_{S}^{(j)}=-S}^{S}\cdots\sumop\displaylimits_{m_{S}^{(K)}=-S}^{S}m_{S}^{(j)}

\displaystyle\times\lvert m_{S}^{(1)}\cdots m_{S}^{(j)}\cdots m_{S}^{(K)}\rangle\langle m_{S}^{(1)}\cdots m_{S}^{(j)}\cdots m_{S}^{(K)}\rvert.

(6)

We adopt the standard convention that
\lambda_{S}^{+}=0
and
\lambda_{-S}^{-}=0
, so that the above relations remain valid at the boundaries of the spectrum. And we see that Eqs.(
3
) and (
4
) concretely involve as many summations as there are spins in the system. They are suitable for analytical treatments of few-body systems as one can manually write down all possible configurations and handle a few summations. This suggests that the full Hilbert space can be constructed. For example, consider a spin-
1/2
three-body system. The Hilbert space is spanned by
2^{3}=8
basis vectors. They can all be manually constructed. Two possible such vectors are
\lvert\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\rangle
and
\lvert-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\rangle
. A matrix element of an observable
\hat{A}
is
\langle-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\rvert\hat{A}\lvert\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\rangle
. We already observe from here that for large spin systems similar notations will be cumbersome involving a large number of summands. This is one of the manifestations of the finite-size effect, a drastic drawback occurring as a consequence of the exponential growth of the Hilbert space with the total number of spins
K
. For numerical analyses, this effect extensively restricts the number of implementable spins in actual computers (
K\leq 24
to the best of our knowledge). While the Hilbert space dimension remains exponential, the Dirac representation significantly simplifies the structure of matrix elements and allows more efficient numerical implementations. Relatively large spin systems can be numerically integrated on small computers (
K\leq 18
, RAM
32
Gb with Mathematica configured with the highly optimized Intel Math Kernel Library (MKL)). Our objective is to show that there exists better alternatives to Eqs.(
5
) and (
6
) easier to handle both analytically and numerically.

II.2
Binary theory

Before diving into the classical representation, let us elaborate on a binary theory for spin-
1/2
systems. To this end, we consider the above example and relabel the basis vectors as
\lvert\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\rangle\equiv\lvert 0\rangle
and
\lvert-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\rangle\equiv\lvert 4\rangle
such that
\langle-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\rvert\hat{A}\lvert\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\rangle\equiv\langle 4\rvert\hat{A}\lvert 0\rangle
. This is the binary representation
[
58
]
. It is already simpler compared to the spin-projection notation in Eq. (
2
). Indeed, qubits are mapped onto their classical counterparts, the classical bits
0
and
1
as
\lvert\frac{1}{2}\rangle\equiv\lvert\uparrow\rangle\to\lvert 0\rangle
and
\lvert-\frac{1}{2}\rangle\equiv\lvert\downarrow\rangle\to\lvert 1\rangle
with
\lvert 0\rangle=[1,0]^{T}
and
\lvert 1\rangle=[0,1]^{T}
. Thus,

\lvert\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\rangle=\lvert 000\rangle=\lvert 0\rangle
,
\lvert-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\rangle=\lvert 100\rangle=\lvert 4\rangle
.

The binary representation is convenient for numerical analysis, exact diagonalization for example. The Hilbert space can easily be constructed by performing binary decomposition of the basis vectors, which in turn facilitates the construction of the Hamiltonian (see
GitHub repository
). We shall show that this representation is not only convenient for numerical simulations but also advantageous for analytical manipulations
[
33
]
. It improves on the readability of observables with consequences on their physical interpretations.

It is very important to emphasize at this point that throughout this work we use the local basis

\lvert\uparrow\rangle=[1,0]^{T}
and
\lvert\downarrow\rangle=[0,1]^{T}
.
In the binary encoding of a configuration
\lvert n\rangle
we assign

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}^{(j)}=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\lvert\uparrow\rangle_{j},\qquad\sigma_{n}^{(j)}=1\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\lvert\downarrow\rangle_{j},

(7)

so that
s_{n}^{(j)z}=\frac{1-2\sigma_{n}^{(j)}}{2}
. With this convention the ladder operators act as
\hat{S}_{j}^{+}\lvert\downarrow\rangle_{j}=\lvert\uparrow\rangle_{j}
and
\hat{S}_{j}^{+}\lvert\uparrow\rangle_{j}=0
, hence
\hat{S}_{j}^{+}
flips the local bit
1\to 0
(and annihilates for
\sigma_{n}^{(j)}=0
), while
\hat{S}_{j}^{-}
flips
0\to 1
(and annihilates for
\sigma_{n}^{(j)}=1
).

We denote as
\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{B}}
the binary space analogue to (
1
) and spanned by
\{\lvert n_{S}\rangle\}_{n_{S}=0}^{D-1}
(basis vectors sorted in lexicographical order). This space is isomorphic to
\bm{\mathcal{H}}
. Indeed, there exists a linear bijective map (i.e. both injective and surjective) between them. This map is injective i.e. every element in
\bm{\mathcal{H}}
has a unique correspondence in
\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{B}}
(one-to-one). The map is surjective (onto) i.e. every element in
\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{B}}
has a unique image in
\bm{\mathcal{H}}
. If we denote as
\sigma_{n_{S}}^{(j)}=\left\{0,1\right\}
the
j
th digit in the binary decomposition of
n_{S}
counting from
j=1
in the reading/coding direction— from the left to the right then,

\displaystyle n_{S}=\left(\sigma_{n_{S}}^{(1)}\cdots\sigma_{n_{S}}^{(j)}\cdots\sigma_{n_{S}}^{(K)}\right)_{2},\quad n_{S}=\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K}\left(\sigma_{n_{S}}^{(j)}\right)q_{j},

(8)

The weight
q_{j}=2^{K-j}
embebded the ordering of the basis vectors and does not encode a physical distance between lattice sites. As a consequence, the binary mapping (
8
) does not require the sites
j
and
j+1
to be spatial neighbors. Spatial adjacency and distances are specified independently by the interaction part of the Hamiltonian.

The weight
q_{j}
is also defined such that the largest number is
\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K}q_{j}=(2^{K}-1)
(number with all digits equals to
1
). For reading/coding direction— from the right to the left
n_{S}=\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K}\left(\sigma_{n_{S}}^{(K+1-j)}\right)r_{j}
where
r_{j}=2^{j}
.

In the first relation in (
8
), subscript
2
refers to the base
b_{2}=\left\{0,1\right\}
. Representations (
8
) are easier to interpret compared to (
2
) which involves alternating
\lvert 1/2\rangle
and
\lvert-1/2\rangle
in a non-trivial manner especially for large systems. Indeed, geometrically, if we define the bits-vector
\bm{\sigma}_{n_{S}}=[\sigma_{n_{S}}^{(1)},\sigma_{n_{S}}^{(2)},\cdots,\sigma_{n_{S}}^{(K)}]
and the weight-vector
\bm{q}=[q_{1},q_{2},\cdots,q_{K}]
(arithmetic basis) we see that
n_{S}=\bm{\sigma}_{n_{S}}\cdot\bm{q}
is a weighted sum of
\sigma_{n_{S}}^{(j)}
i.e. a scalar product (not in the Euclidean sense). The
\sigma_{n_{S}}^{(j)}
being the coefficient of the binary expansion of
n_{S}
determine the presence or absence of
q_{j}
in the weighted sum.

As indicated in Refs.
58
,
30
the binary representation of
n_{S}
encapsulates the exact configuration of spins in the state (
2
) provided that one starts from
n_{S}=0
, thence the quantum-to-classical mapping
m_{S}^{(j)}\to\sigma_{n_{S}}^{(j)}
. Let us now describe in the frame of this mapping how LSOs act onto the basis vectors of
\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{B}}
. With our encoding Eq.(
7
), the action of the
ladder operators acquires a simple binary interpretation.

When
\hat{S}_{j}^{+}
acts on a configuration
\lvert n\rangle
, it acts non-trivially only if the
j
th spin is in the state
\lvert\downarrow\rangle_{j}
, i.e. when
\sigma_{n}^{(j)}=1
.
In that case it flips the local bit
1\to 0
and produces the configuration
\lvert\mu_{j}^{[n]}\rangle
, where
\mu_{j}^{[n]}={\rm\bf flip}(n,j)
denotes the integer obtained after flipping the
j
th digit in the binary representation of
n
(see Refs.
58
,
30
). If instead
\sigma_{n}^{(j)}=0
(spin up), then
\hat{S}_{j}^{+}\lvert n\rangle=0
.

Similarly,
\hat{S}_{j}^{-}
acts non-trivially only when
\sigma_{n}^{(j)}=0
(spin up) in which case it flips the bit
0\to 1
; otherwise it annihilates the state. The diagonal operator
\hat{S}_{j}^{z}
leaves the configuration unchanged and simply multiplies it by the eigenvalue

s_{n}^{(j)z}=(1-2\sigma_{n}^{(j)})/2
. These observations lead to the compact matrix elements
[
68
]

\langle m\rvert\hat{S}_{j}^{\pm}\lvert n\rangle&=s_{n}^{(j)\pm}\,\delta_{m,\mu_{j}^{[n]}},\\
\langle m\rvert\hat{S}_{j}^{z}\lvert n\rangle=s_{n}^{(j)z}\,\delta_{m,n},

(9)

where

s_{n}^{(j)+}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1-2s_{n}^{(j)z}\bigr)=\sigma_{n}^{(j)},\qquad s_{n}^{(j)-}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1+2s_{n}^{(j)z}\bigr)=1-\sigma_{n}^{(j)}.

A few of their properties are displayed in Appendix
B
.

We gain further insight into the
{\bf flip}()
function by noting from Eq.(
8
) that as
\bar{\sigma}_{n}^{\left(j\right)}
denotes the reversed digit of
\sigma_{n}^{\left(j\right)}
then,

\displaystyle\mu_{j}^{\left[n\right]}=n+\left(\bar{\sigma}_{n}^{\left(j\right)}-\sigma_{n}^{\left(j\right)}\right)q_{j}.

(10)

We learn from here that flipping an integer
n
of bit-field length
K
at the
j
th site is equivalent to adding
q_{j}
(if
\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=0
) or subtracting
q_{j}
(if
\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=1
) to the said number. For instance, consider the set of non-negative integers of bit-field length
K=6
i.e.
0\leq n\leq 2^{6}-1
. Consider again
n=5
in the binary basis,
5=\left(000101\right)_{2}
. Let us reverse the second digit counting from the left to right i.e.
j=2
. Because
\sigma_{5}^{\left(2\right)}=0
reversing this digit yields
\left(010101\right)_{2}=21
which is nothing but
5+2^{6-2}=21
ascertaining our assertion (see complete proof in Appendix
B
). In the same vein, it can be proven that
\mu_{j}^{[0]}=q_{j}
and
\mu_{j}^{[D-1]}=D-1-q_{j}
. Similarly, the integer
\bar{n}
obtained after reversing all digits in an integer
n
reads,

\bar{n}=n+\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K}\left(\bar{\sigma}_{n}^{\left(j\right)}-\sigma_{n}^{\left(j\right)}\right)q_{j}=\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K}\bar{\sigma}_{n}^{\left(j\right)}q_{j}=D-1-n

whereby,
\sigma_{\bar{n}}^{\left(j\right)}=\bar{\sigma}_{n}^{\left(j\right)}
.
For even integers, the least significant digit (LSD)
\sigma^{(K)}_{n}=0
,
\mu_{K}^{[n]}=n+1
while for odd integers the LSD is
\sigma^{(K)}_{n}=1
and
\mu_{K}^{[n]}=n-1
. Note that the LSD is the digit at

as we read from the left to the right. Eq.(

) can now be regarded as

\displaystyle\mu_{j}^{\left[n\right]}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}n+q_{j},\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=0\\
n-q_{j},\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=1\end{array}\right.,\quad\mu_{j}^{[\mu_{j}^{\left[n\right]}]}=n.

Then,
n
and
\mu_{j}^{\left[n\right]}
only differ at the
j
th site where a bit is reversed. Remarkably, as for
\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=0
the integer obtained after flipping this bit is

we infer that
\sigma^{(j)}_{n+q_{j}}=1
. Similarly, as
\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=1
and the bit resulting from inversion is

then
\sigma^{(j)}_{n-q_{j}}=0
. Note also that given an integer
q_{j}
(i.e. a number consisting of

everywhere excepted the

th position) it can be verified that

\sigma_{q_{j}}^{(i)}=\delta_{i,j}
.

II.3
Classical representation

Now, based on Eqs.(
9
)-(
9
) and their trivial relations with ladder spin operators and thanks to Eq.(
II.2
), we can now make manifest the promised Dirac representation for spin-
1/2
(See also Ref.
19
),

\hat{S}_{j}^{+}&=\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{D-1}s_{n}^{\left(j\right)-}\lvert n\rangle\langle n+q_{j}\rvert,\\
\hat{S}_{j}^{-}=\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{D-1}s_{n}^{\left(j\right)-}\lvert n+q_{j}\rangle\langle n\rvert,\\
\hat{S}_{j}^{z}=\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{D-1}s_{n}^{\left(j\right)z}\lvert n\rangle\langle n\rvert.

(14)

The appearance of
s_{n}^{(j)-}
in both
\hat{S}_{j}^{+}
and
\hat{S}_{j}^{-}
reflects our encoding

\sigma_{n}^{(j)}=0\leftrightarrow\lvert\uparrow\rangle_{j}
and
\sigma_{n}^{(j)}=1\leftrightarrow\lvert\downarrow\rangle_{j}
. This ensures that
(\hat{S}_{j}^{\pm})^{\dagger}=\hat{S}_{j}^{\mp}
given that the embedded classical objects are real,
(s_{n}^{\left(j\right)\pm})^{\dagger}=s_{n}^{\left(j\right)\pm}
. The summation index
n
is chosen such that the pair
(n,n+q_{j})
represents two configurations that only differ by the
j
th bit. Hence
s_{n}^{(j)-}
projects onto the subset with
\sigma_{n}^{(j)}=0
(spin up), so that
\hat{S}_{j}^{-}
maps
\lvert n\rangle
to
\lvert n+q_{j}\rangle
, while
\hat{S}_{j}^{+}
is the Hermitian conjugate maps from
\lvert n+q_{j}\rangle
back to
\lvert n\rangle
.

A second representation in terms of
s_{n}^{\left(j\right)+}
can be constructed. Indeed, using
\mu_{j}^{[n]}=n\pm q_{j}
together with the conjugation relations

s_{n-q_{j}}^{(j)-}=s_{n}^{(j)+}
and
s_{n+q_{j}}^{(j)+}=s_{n}^{(j)-}
(see Appendix
B
),
one obtains an equivalent representation,

\hat{S}_{j}^{+}&=\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{D-1}s_{n}^{(j)+}\lvert n-q_{j}\rangle\langle n\rvert,\\
\hat{S}_{j}^{-}=\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{D-1}s_{n}^{(j)+}\lvert n\rangle\langle n-q_{j}\rvert.

As promised, the above representations are simpler than their quantum counterparts. Representation of LSOs in terms of classical variables involves a single summation over the entire Hilbert space
\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{B}}
whereas their equivalent quantum counterparts require as many nested summations as the number of spins in the chain and this is clearly a major advantage. While the Hilbert-space dimension remains exponential, the resulting Dirac forms reduce the number of explicit nested sums and yield sparse, structured matrix elements that are advantageous both analytically and numerically.

We emphasize that Eqs.(
14
)-(
14
) contain no assumption regarding the spatial dimension and lattice geometry. As already pinpointed,
j
labels lattice sites. Therefore, our representations apply for one-, two-, and higher-dimensional lattices as well as to arbitrary interaction graphs. Dimensionality and geometry enter only through the choice of interacting site pairs in the Hamiltonian.

It can also be verified that our results do not suffer from any non-locality nor non-Hermiticity and are extendable to higher spin-size (See Section
V

1

su(2)

For a single spin system,

The possibility offered by Eqs.(

It can be seen that our transformations preserve the
su(2)
algebra at the same site. They do not induce any non-locality as in the case of JW transformations nor non-linearity as for HP. They remain local and linear. There is also no constraint related to population occupancy. Let us now clarify what we mean by non-locality and non-linearity. LSOs in the spin representation are local i.e. each operator acts solely on a single site and no coupling to other sites is required. In the case of JW representation, a ladder operator acting at site
j
reads
\hat{S}_{j}^{+}=\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\prodop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{j-1}\exp[i\pi\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{c}_{k}]
where
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}
and
\hat{c}_{k}
are respectively fermion creation and annihilation operators. The factor
\prodop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{j-1}\exp[i\pi\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{c}_{k}]
is a non-local string operator as it involves all fermionic sites from
1
to
j-1
. Non-locality arises as consequence of the fact that the string operator must fulfill the fermionic anti-commutation relations. Non-locality makes it hard to extend JW to higher dimensions. For HP,
\hat{S}_{j}^{+}=\sqrt{2S-\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{a}_{j}}\hat{a}_{j}
where
S
is the spin size,
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}
and
\hat{a}_{j}
are boson creation and annihilation operators respectively. This representation is local but it usage requires approximating the square root. This is done by assuming
S\gg 1
leading to non-linearity in the boson representation. For spin
S=1/2
, this leads to poor convergence of the truncation.

\displaystyle s_{n}^{\left(j\right)\pm}=0,\quad n<0.

(21)

Secondly,

\displaystyle q_{0}=q_{K+1}=q_{K+2}=\cdots=0.

\Theta(x)

\displaystyle\hskip-7.11317pts_{n}^{\left(j\right)z}=\frac{1}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{m=0}^{r_{j}-1}\left(-1\right)^{m}\left(\Theta\left(\mathcal{Q}_{n+1,m}^{(j)}\right)+\Theta\left(\mathcal{Q}_{n+1,m+1}^{(j)}\right)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{j,K},

(24)

and where we have defined

and a smooth regularization of the Heaviside distribution

\displaystyle\Theta\left(x\right)=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\frac{1}{1+e^{-\lambda x}}.

(25)

s_{n}^{\left(j\right)+}

II.4
States representation

As already indicated
\lvert n+q_{j}\rangle
is the state obtained by flipping the
j
th bit in the binary representation of
\lvert n\rangle
provided
s_{n}^{\left(j\right)+}=0
. Therefore
\lvert n+mq_{j}\rangle
could be viewed as the state achieved by flipping the
j
th bit
m
times. If
m
is even, then
s_{n+mq_{j}}^{\left(j\right)+}=s_{n}^{\left(j\right)+}
i.e. the action of flipping a single digit an even number of times takes it back to its initial position preserving the original state. If in opposition
m
is odd, thus
s_{n+mq_{j}}^{\left(j\right)+}=s_{n+q_{j}}^{\left(j\right)+}=s_{n}^{\left(j\right)-}
. As a consequence of these observations, every basis vector can be constructed from
\lvert 0\rangle\equiv\lvert 000\cdots 000\rangle
(vacuum state, all spins up in our encoding) by inverting local spin directions only once as it is done in quantum mechanics by operational application. Thus,

\displaystyle\lvert q_{j}\rangle=\lvert 000\cdots 1_{j}\cdots 000\rangle,

(26)

describes all states with one spin in down state
(K_{\downarrow}=1)
.
For spin-
1/2
, the total magnetization is

\mathcal{J}^{z}=(K_{\uparrow}-K_{\downarrow})/2=K/2-K_{\downarrow}
,
so this sector corresponds to
\mathcal{J}^{z}=K/2-1
(assuming the Hamiltonian possesses a global
U(1)
symmetry and thus conserves
\mathcal{J}^{z}
). The sub-Hilbert space consists of the complete set of basis vectors
\{\lvert q_{j}\rangle\}_{1\leq j\leq K}
equipped with
\langle q_{i}|q_{j}\rangle=\delta_{i,j}
.

Similarly, if from the fully symmetric state one inverts two spins respectively located at positions
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
the resulting state reads,

\displaystyle\lvert q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}\rangle=\lvert 000\cdots 1_{j_{1}}\cdots 1_{j_{2}}\cdots 000\rangle,

(27)

with
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq K
. The sub-Hilbert space in this sector contains
\begin{pmatrix}K\\
2\end{pmatrix}=K(K-1)/2
basis vectors. If we equally assume the canonical ordering
i_{1}<i_{2}
such that the basis vectors are uniquely labeled then

\langle q_{i_{1}}+q_{i_{2}}|q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}\rangle=\delta_{i_{1},j_{1}}\delta_{i_{2},j_{2}}
. The basis vectors (
27
) exactly match the set obtained by addition of two distinct elements in the previous subset. This process can be repeated until the entire set of sub-Hilbert spaces is generated the last but one of which contains
\binom{K}{K-1}=K
states with
K-1
spins in the down state,

\displaystyle\lvert q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K-1}}\rangle=\lvert 1_{j_{1}}1_{j_{2}}\cdots 0\cdots 1_{j_{K-1}}\rangle,

(28)

with
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{K-1}\leq K
. It should be noted that
\lvert q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K}}\rangle=\lvert 1_{j_{1}}1_{j_{2}}\cdots 1_{j_{K}}\rangle\equiv\lvert 2^{K}-1\rangle
. In each sector, the basis vectors normalizes as

\displaystyle\langle q_{i_{1}}+q_{i_{2}}+\cdots+q_{i_{K_{\downarrow}}}|q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K_{\downarrow}}}\rangle=\prodop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{K_{\downarrow}}\delta_{i_{k},j_{k}}.\qquad

(29)

with
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{K_{\downarrow}}
and
j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{K_{\downarrow}}
.
For two distinct sectors, this identity is identically zero.

These observations are useful to evaluate the eigenspectrum of spin Hamiltonians on arbitrary lattices or interaction graphs. In the following Sections, we illustrate this general construction for a
1D
chain.

In the same logic, except for
s_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K}}}^{\left(i\right)+}=1
, it can easily be seen that

&s_{q_{j}}^{\left(i\right)+}=\delta_{i,j},\\
s_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}^{\left(i\right)+}=\delta_{i,j_{1}}+\delta_{i,j_{2}},\\
\vbox{\kern 6.0pt\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}}\\

(30)

Hereby, we verified for further purposes that
\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{K}s_{q_{j}}^{\left(i\right)+}=1
,
\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{K}s_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}^{\left(i\right)+}=2
gradually until
\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{K}s^{\left(i\right)+}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K-1}}}=K-1
. Similarly, using Eq. (

&s_{q_{j}}^{\left(i\right)z}=\frac{1}{2}\bar{\delta}_{i,j},\\
s_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}^{\left(i\right)z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bar{\delta}_{i,j_{1}}+\bar{\delta}_{i,j_{2}}\right),\\
\vbox{\kern 6.0pt\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}}\\

(31)

where we have defined

\displaystyle\bar{\delta}_{i,j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}-1,\quad i=j\\
+1,\quad i\neq j\end{array}\right.,

Importantly,

\displaystyle\bar{\delta}_{i,j}\bar{\delta}_{i,j+1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-1,&\quad i=j\quad\text{or}\quad i=j+1,\\[2.0pt]

in opposition to

as proven below. Thus, because of the constraint

III
Applications: 1
D
Spin-1/2 Integrable
\mathrm{XXZ}
Heisenberg model

After establishing a dimension-independent representation for LSOs, free from any non-locality and non-Hermicity we now consider a
1D
system in order to illustrate its usefulness in an analytically tractable many-body problem. We consider the
1D
spin-
1/2

\mathrm{XXZ}
Heisenberg model with open boundaries in a magnetic field (MF) and compute its eigenspectrum. Two distinct cases are of interest: the case of uniform and random MFs. For uniform MF (integrable model) the eigenspectrum is in general obtained analytically by invoking the Bethe Ansatz
[
11
,
72
]
which amounts to complicated calculations or by resorting to fermionic or bosonic transformations. We demonstrate that our representation transforms the corresponding eigenvalue problem into recurrence equations that can be solved in closed form. In the case of random MF (non-integrable model) a comprehensive perturbation theory is constructed. The prototype Hamiltonian of the study reads
[
22
,
59
]

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sumop\displaylimits_{\alpha=x,y,z}\sumop\displaylimits^{K-1}_{j=1}J_{\alpha}\hat{S}^{\alpha}_{j}\hat{S}^{\alpha}_{j+1}+\sumop\displaylimits^{K}_{j=1}h_{j}\hat{S}^{z}_{j},

(38)

to which we further impose the constraint
J_{x}=J_{y}=J
(anisotropic XXZ spin-
1/2
) with
J
measuring spin-spin interactions;
J_{z}
is the spin anisotropy parameter,
h_{j}
are local magnetic fields applied along the
z
-direction onto individual spins. Our goal is to analyze this model both numerically in the Dirac (binary) representation with the aid of exact diagonalization and analytically without invoking the Bethe Ansatz or any fermionic or bosonic transformation.

III.1
Numerical Treatment

Exact diagonalization (ED) in the model (
38
) is achieved by exploiting two main symmetries: the global
U(1)
and spin-inversion symmetries. These allow us to considerably reduce the size of the Hilbert space. Our choice of spin-spin interaction ensures that
\hat{H}
has a global
U(1)
symmetry thus preserving the total magnetization. In the binary basis, this reads

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{z}=\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{D-1}m_{n}^{z}\lvert n\rangle\langle n\rvert,\quad\left[\hat{H},\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{z}\right]=0.

(39)

Here,
m_{n}^{z}=\sumop\displaylimits^{K}_{j=1}s_{n}^{\left(j\right)z}
is the
n
th magnetic quantum number written in explicit form in (
199
). The conservation law (
39
) decomposes the Hilbert space into disconnected magnetization sectors
[
39
,
18
]
. There are various ways for distinguishing between the sectors. One way consists of labeling them using the total magnetization
\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{z}
in which case
-K/2\leq\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{z}\leq K/2
for even number of spins. An alternative way consists of using the number of spins in either the state
\lvert\uparrow\rangle
or
\lvert\downarrow\rangle
. We label the sectors with the number
K_{\downarrow}
of spins in down state starting from the smallest values;
0\leq K_{\downarrow}\leq K
.
The tensor product of subspaces in (
1
) decomposes into direct sum of subspaces as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{H}}=\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{K_{\downarrow}=0}^{K}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\left(K_{\downarrow}\right)}.

(40)

Each subspace is of dimension
{\rm\bf dim}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\left(K_{\downarrow}\right)}=\begin{pmatrix}K\\
K_{\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}
. This is smaller than the
D=2^{K}
dimension of the original Hilbert space. The Hamiltonian decomposes in a similar fashion. Thanks to this symmetry, states with different value of
\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{z}
are not mixed and the Hamiltonian can be diagonalized independently in each subspace. The gaps between the energy of the fourth excited state and the ground-state energies (
E_{4}-E_{0}
) is calculated as a function of
J/h
and
J_{z}/h
and the results are depicted in Fig.
2
. A similar task is accomplished for
E_{8}-E_{0}
and displayed in Fig.
5
. We observe interesting interference patterns revealing the existence of level-crossings between
E_{4}
and
E_{0}
by linear variation of
J/h
and where the wave functions split and recombine. These calculations can unfortunately only be done for a few number of spins as although exploiting the
U(1)
global symmetry, ED continues suffering from finite-size effects. We invoke an additional symmetry.

In the absence of local magnetic fields (
h_{j}=0
for all
j
), the Hamiltonian acquires an additional global spin-inversion symmetry
[
58
]
. It commutes with the spin-inversion operator,

\displaystyle\hat{Z}=\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{D-1}\lvert D-1-n\rangle\langle n\rvert,\quad\left[\hat{H},\hat{Z}\right]=0.

(41)

Because of the binary encoding basis states,
D-1-n
corresponds to the bitwise complement. Essentially,
\hat{Z}^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{j}^{\pm}\hat{Z}=\hat{S}_{j}^{\mp}
and
\hat{Z}^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{j}^{z}\hat{Z}=-\hat{S}_{j}^{z}
given in addition that
\hat{Z}=\hat{Z}^{-1}=\hat{Z}^{\dagger}
(unitary operator). The operator
\hat{Z}
is an anti-diagonal matrix that has zeros everywhere excepted on the anti-diagonal where it possesses ones. Therefore, given that
\hat{Z}^{2}=\hat{I}
, its eigenvalues are
z=\pm 1
and its normalized eigenvectors are

\displaystyle\lvert n_{z}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert n\rangle+z\lvert D-1-n\rangle).

(42)

They are mutually orthogonal and are equipped with a completeness relation that allows them to form a complete set of vectors,

\displaystyle\langle m_{z^{\prime}}|n_{z}\rangle=\delta_{n,m}\delta_{z,z^{\prime}},\quad\frac{1}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{z=\pm 1}\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{D-1}\lvert n_{z}\rangle\langle n_{z}\rvert=\hat{I}.

(43)

When the global
U(1)
symmetry is implemented, the spin-inversion symmetry is applicable only in the sector
\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{z}=0
(largest sector) where
\hat{Z}\lvert s_{n}^{\left(1\right)z},s_{n}^{\left(2\right)z},\cdots,s_{n}^{\left(K\right)z}\rangle=\lvert-s_{n}^{\left(1\right)z},-s_{n}^{\left(2\right)z},\cdots,-s_{n}^{\left(K\right)z}\rangle
. This permits further fragmentation of this subspace into yet two disconnected sectors associated with the eigenvalues
z
,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\left(\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{z}=0\right)}=\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{+}\bigoplusop\displaylimits\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{-}.

(44)

The projection out of an operator (not necessary a matrix) onto each subspaces can be accomplished with the aid of the projection operator (see also Ref.
[
9
]
)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{I}+z\hat{Z}\right).

(45)

It is Hermitian with properties
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{z}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{z^{\prime}}=\delta_{z,z^{\prime}}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{z}
,
\sumop\displaylimits_{z=\pm}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{z}=\hat{I}
and
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{z}\hat{Z}=z\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{z}
. The projection of a (basis) vector initially in the eigenbasis of
\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{z}
onto each new subspace in (

\displaystyle\lvert n\rangle=\sumop\displaylimits_{z=\pm 1}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{D-1}C_{k_{z},n}\lvert k_{z}\rangle.

(46)

Here, the
C_{k_{z},n}
denoting the projection of
\lvert n\rangle
onto the direction of
\lvert k_{z}\rangle
in the subspace
\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{z}
are determined with the aid of the relations in (
43
) as

\displaystyle C_{k_{z},n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\delta_{k,n}+z\delta_{k,D-1-n}\right).

(47)

We see that the basis vectors mix the two subspaces as indicated by
\lvert n\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sumop\displaylimits_{z=\pm 1}\left(\lvert n_{z}\rangle+z\lvert\left(D-1-n\right)_{z}\rangle\right)
. Every matrix operator
\hat{\mathcal{O}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{n,m=0}^{D-1}\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{n,m}\lvert n\rangle\langle m\rvert
in the eigenspace (
40
) is projected out onto the eigenspaces
\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{z}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{O}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{z^{\prime},z}\sumop\displaylimits_{n,m=0}^{D-1}\left(\langle m_{z^{\prime}}\rvert\hat{\mathcal{O}}\lvert n_{z}\rangle\right)\lvert n_{z}\rangle\langle m_{z^{\prime}}\rvert,

(48)

where

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\langle m_{z^{\prime}}\rvert\hat{\mathcal{O}}\lvert n_{z}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{n,m}+z^{\prime}\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{n,D-1-m}+z\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{D-1-n,m}

\displaystyle+zz^{\prime}\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{D-1-n,D-1-m}\Big),

(49)

are the matrix elements of
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
in the
\{\lvert n_{z}\rangle\}
basis. The
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{\beta,\alpha}
in the left hand side of (
49
) are components of
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
in the eigenbasis (
40
). In general,
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
mixes states with different
z
. When it preserves
\hat{Z}
it does not mix them anymore also falling apart into two disconnected blocks corresponding each to a value of
z
.

These symmetries considerably mitigate finite-size limitations. Indeed, on a small computer with 32Gb of RAM (6-cores Ryzen 5 processor) we have been able on the Mathematica software configured with the highly optimized Intel Math Kernel Library (MKL) to numerically diagonalize (eigenvalues only)
\hat{H}
for systems up to
K\leq 18
spins without resorting to any parallelization. Thanks to the binary representation it only takes a few seconds to build the Hamiltonian as no matrix multiplication is required. Block Hamiltonians are diagonalized one after the other. We expect that further optimization in compiled languages
will allow treatment of even larger systems. The algorithm is available in the following
GitHub repository
.

III.2
Analytical Treatment

After gaining insight into the numerical behavior of the generic model
\hat{H}
, we are now in position to obtain its eigenspectrum analytically. In the representation (
14
)-(
14
) it casts the form
\hat{H}=\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{D-1}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
where

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}\left(\Delta^{(j)+-}_{n}\lvert n-Q_{j}\rangle\langle n\rvert+\Delta^{(j)-+}_{n}\lvert n+Q_{j}\rangle\langle n\rvert\right)+\Delta^{zz}_{n}\lvert n\rangle\langle n\rvert,

(50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}\Delta^{(j)-+}_{n}\left(\lvert n\rangle\langle n+Q_{j}\rvert+\lvert n+Q_{j}\rangle\langle n\rvert\right)+\Delta^{zz}_{n}\lvert n\rangle\langle n\rvert,

and where we have defined

\displaystyle\Delta^{(j)+-}_{n}=\frac{J}{2}s_{n}^{\left(j\right)+}s_{n}^{\left(j+1\right)-},\quad\Delta^{(j)-+}_{n}=\frac{J}{2}s_{n}^{\left(j\right)-}s_{n}^{\left(j+1\right)+},

(51)

and the classical Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\Delta^{zz}_{n}=J_{z}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}s_{n}^{\left(j\right)z}s_{n}^{\left(j+1\right)z}+\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K}{h_{j}s_{n}^{\left(j\right)z}},

(52)

with
Q_{j}=q_{j}-q_{j+1}
. The quantities
\Delta^{(j)+-}_{n}
and
\Delta^{(j)-+}_{n}
encapsulate the microscopical structure of neighboring spins in the configuration
\lvert n\rangle
. For parallel neighboring spins one has
\Delta^{(j)+-}_{n}=\Delta^{(j)-+}_{n}=0
.
For anti-parallel ones, exactly one of the two coefficients is nonzero:

\Delta^{(j)+-}_{n}=J/2
and
\Delta^{(j)-+}_{n}=0
if the local pattern is
(+,-)
,
whereas
\Delta^{(j)-+}_{n}=J/2
and
\Delta^{(j)+-}_{n}=0
if it is
(-,+)
.
Equivalently,
\Delta^{(j)+-}_{n}\,\Delta^{(j)-+}_{n}=0
for all
n
.
Thus, when neighboring spins are simultaneously inverted, this action interchanges the quantities in (
51
),
\Delta^{(j)+-}_{n+Q_{j}}=\Delta^{(j)-+}_{n}
and
\Delta^{(j)-+}_{n-Q_{j}}=\Delta^{(j)+-}_{n}
. These identities are used together with a relabeling of the summation index
n\to n-Q_{j}
in the full sum over
n
, to rewrite the exchange part of
\hat{H}
in the symmetric form in the second line in Eq.(
50
).

Also,
\Delta^{(j)+-}_{0}=\Delta^{(j)+-}_{D-1}=0
and
\Delta^{(j)-+}_{0}=\Delta^{(j)-+}_{D-1}=0
as all adjacent spins in the states
\lvert 0\rangle
and
\lvert D-1\rangle
with extremal values of the total magnetization are parallel. We also observe that
\Delta_{\bar{n}}^{(j)+-}=\Delta_{n}^{(j)-+}
and
\Delta_{\bar{n}}^{(j)-+}=\Delta_{n}^{(j)+-}
for all lattice site.
(\Delta^{(j)+-}_{n})^{2}=\frac{J}{2}\Delta^{(j)+-}_{n}
,
(\Delta^{(j)-+}_{n})^{2}=\frac{J}{2}\Delta^{(j)-+}_{n}
by virtue of Eq.(
180
). Thereby, we infer that
(\Delta^{(j)+-}_{n})^{m}=\left(\frac{J}{2}\right)^{m-1}\Delta^{(j)+-}_{n}
and
(\Delta^{(j)-+}_{n})^{m}=\left(\frac{J}{2}\right)^{m-1}\Delta^{(j)-+}_{n}
.

\Delta^{zz}_{n}
in Eq.(
52
) is nothing but the classical Ising model
[
24
,
69
]
. It corresponds to the non-interacting limit
J=0
of (
50
). When local magnetic fields are randomly distributed as in the MBL case
[
59
,
61
]
, it describes a single-body localization
[
5
,
12
]
. Therefore, in this limit a wide class of problems ranging from magnetism
[
28
]
, phase transitions
[
56
]
, spin glasses
[
47
]
through critical phenomena
[
62
]
can be handled more easily as there is no need anymore to manipulate quantum but classical objects. We have considerably reduced the level of complexity associated with the quantum nature of spin Hamiltonians. This is clearly an advantage of (
52
) over (
38
). As yet another advantage, we will see later in the work that
\Delta^{(j)+-}_{n}
and
\Delta^{(j)-+}_{n}
acquire simple forms when we exploit the global
U(1)
symmetry of
\hat{H}
by sitting in a given sector of the fragmented Hilbert space.
It should be noted for further purposes that
\Delta^{zz}_{0}=J_{z}(K-1)/4+{\bf h}/2
and
\Delta^{zz}_{D-1}=-J_{z}(K-1)/4-{\bf h}/2
with the sample sum
{\bf h}=\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K}h_{j}
.

A special case of paramount interest to be discussed before diving into the fragmented Hilbert space is the case of
perfect
Neel’s states. They correspond to configuration where any adjacent pair of spins are in opposite direction
\lvert\uparrow\downarrow\uparrow\downarrow\cdots\rangle
and
\lvert\downarrow\uparrow\downarrow\uparrow\cdots\rangle
accounting for the state of the first spin. In other words,
s^{\left(j\right)z}_{\rm Neel}=-s^{\left(j+1\right)z}_{\rm Neel}
. As a consequence, the total magnetization
\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{z}=0
; for every pair
s^{\left(j\right)z}_{\rm Neel}s^{\left(j+1\right)z}_{\rm Neel}=-1/4
,
\Delta^{(j)+-}_{\rm Neel}=J/2,\ \Delta^{(j)-+}_{\rm Neel}=0
for
(+,-)
bonds,
while
\Delta^{(j)-+}_{\rm Neel}=J/2,\ \Delta^{(j)+-}_{\rm Neel}=0
for
(-,+)
bonds independently on whether the first component of the chain is found in the state
\lvert\uparrow\rangle
or
\lvert\downarrow\rangle
. For homogeneous local magnetic fields
\Delta^{zz}_{\rm Neel}=-J_{z}\left(K-1\right)/4
. This is an indication that effects of
h
in the sector accommodating
\lvert{\rm Neel}\rangle
are suppressed by mutual compensation of flipped spins i.e.
h\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K}s_{n}^{(j)z}=h\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{z}=0
for all
\lvert n\rangle
. These remarks confirm that Neel’s states (two-fold degenerate) are ground states of the
1D
antiferromagnetic spin-
1/2
Heisenberg model in the classical limit with energy
\Delta^{zz}_{\rm Neel}
[
53
]
. For an even number of spins,
\lvert{\rm Neel}\rangle=\lvert\uparrow\downarrow\uparrow\downarrow\cdots\rangle=\lvert\frac{2}{3}\left(2^{K}-1\right)\rangle
and
\lvert{\rm Neel}\rangle=\lvert\downarrow\uparrow\downarrow\uparrow\cdots\rangle=\lvert\frac{1}{3}\left(2^{K}-1\right)\rangle
.

The central pole of interest in this Section, is the eigenvalue equation
\hat{H}\lvert\psi\rangle=E\lvert\psi\rangle
with eigenvalue
E
and the associated eigenstates
\lvert\psi\rangle
. Our goal is to solve this equation. To this end, we recall that in the absence of spin-spin exchange (
J=0
) spins are in the quantization direction; the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian are
E_{n}=\Delta_{n}^{zz}
whilst the eigenstates are
\lvert n\rangle
. When interactions are turned on (
J\neq 0
) spins are in various superpositions of
\lvert n\rangle
weighted by certain probability amplitudes. The eigenstates of the Hamiltonian can be constructed as,

\displaystyle\lvert\psi\rangle=\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{D-1}C_{n}\lvert n\rangle,

(53)

where
C_{n}
are projections of
\lvert\psi\rangle
onto the direction of
\lvert n\rangle
in the Hilbert space or the probability amplitude of observing spins in the configuration
\lvert n\rangle
. They are subjected to the constraint
\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{D-1}|C_{n}|^{2}=1
. Plugging (
53
) into the eigenvalue equation results into the system of recurrence equations

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}\left(\Delta^{(j)-+}_{n}C_{n+Q_{j}}+\Delta^{(j)+-}_{n}C_{n-Q_{j}}\right)=\left(E-\Delta^{zz}_{n}\right)C_{n}.\quad

(54)

This equation is reminiscent of the tight-binding problem on a graph. Therefore, the complicated problem of a collection of identical spins interacting pairwise on a simple
1D
lattice is transformed into a simple problem of a free spinless particle hopping on a complicated graph as also discussed in Ref.
[
49
]
.

We solve (
54
) analytically by sitting in each sector of the fragmented Hilbert space. We focus exclusively on even
K
as the case of odd
K
follows the same logic. We discriminate between the single-state sectors
K_{\downarrow}=0
and
K_{\downarrow}=K
where (
54
) is easily solved as follows:

Sector
K_{\downarrow}=0
:
This sector has
\begin{pmatrix}K\\
0\end{pmatrix}=1
basis vector with fully aligned spins in the state
\lvert\uparrow\rangle
; the so called
pseudo-vacuum state
. Therefore,
\lvert n\rangle\equiv\lvert 0\rangle
and
\Delta^{(j)+-}_{0}=\Delta^{(j)-+}_{0}=0
. The single eigenstate and eigenvalue of the Hamiltonian are respectively
\lvert\psi^{(0)}_{0}\rangle=\lvert 0\rangle
and
E_{0}^{(0)}=\Delta^{zz}_{0}=J_{z}(K-1)/4+hK/2
. This emerges as a consequence of the fact that
s^{\left(j\right)z}_{0}=1/2
for all
j
. The superscript on the eigenvalue and the eigenfunction refers to
K_{\downarrow}
.

Sector
K_{\downarrow}=K
:
Because this sector has only one state with all spins in the state
\lvert\downarrow\rangle
, same applies. The unique eigenstate and the eigenenergy are respectively
\lvert\psi^{(K)}_{D-1}\rangle=\lvert D-1\rangle
and
E_{D-1}^{(K)}=\Delta^{zz}_{D-1}=-J_{z}(K-1)/4-hK/2
given that
s^{\left(j\right)z}_{D-1}=-1/2
.

Sector
K_{\downarrow}=1
:

All
\binom{K}{1}=K
basis vectors in this sector have exactly one spin in the down state at site
i
, while all other spins are up.
They can all be built from
\lvert 0\rangle
as
\{\lvert q_{i}\rangle\}_{1\leq i\leq K}
(see previous Section) by flipping one spin. The only anti-parallel bonds are
(i-1,i)
and
(i,i+1)
(when they exist); hence only
j=i
contributes to
\Delta^{(j)+-}_{q_{i}}
and only
j=i-1
contributes to
\Delta^{(j)-+}_{q_{i}}
which yields,

\displaystyle\Delta^{(j)+-}_{q_{i}}=\frac{J}{2}\delta_{i,j},\quad\Delta^{(j)-+}_{q_{i}}=\frac{J}{2}\delta_{i,j+1},

(55)

Assuming a homogeneous magnetic field
h_{j}=h
and the convention
s_{n}^{\left(j\right)z}=1/2
and
s_{n}^{\left(j\right)z}=-1/2
for spins in up and down states respectively, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta^{zz}_{q_{i}}=h\left(\frac{K}{2}-1\right).

(56)

Considering now Eqs.(
55
) and (
56
) and because
\Delta^{zz}_{q_{i}}
is independent on
i
it enters Eq.(
54
) as a simple number having only a shift effect. The eigenvalue equation acquires the tight-binding-like form

\displaystyle\frac{J}{2}\left(C_{q_{j-1}}+C_{q_{j+1}}\right)=\left(E-\Delta^{zz}_{q_{i}}\right)C_{q_{j}},

(57)

for
1\leq j\leq K
with constraint
C_{q_{0}}=C_{q_{K+1}}=0
by virtue of (
46
).
Here,
C_{q_{j}}
is the probability amplitude for finding the chain in the configuration
\lvert q_{j}\rangle
. Its governing equation describes an itinerant particle hopping between neighboring sites
j-1
and
j+1
with a hopping integral
J/2
.

Eq.(
57
) can be reduced to a linear three-term recurrence equation by defining
P_{j}=C_{q_{j}}
as
P_{j-1}+P_{j+1}=xP_{j}
with
x=2\left(E-\Delta^{zz}_{q_{i}}\right)/J
. Boundary conditions become
P_{0}=P_{K+1}=0
. This is a state representation as each state
\lvert q_{j}\rangle
is now treated as a point
j
. Upon solving this equation the eigenfunctions read,

\lvert\psi^{\left(1\right)}_{n}\rangle&=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{n}}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K}P_{j}\left(\tilde{E}^{(1)}_{n}\right)\lvert q_{j}\rangle,\\
\mathcal{N}_{n}=\sqrt{\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K}|P_{j}\left(\tilde{E}^{(1)}_{n}\right)|^{2}},

(58)

where
\tilde{E}^{(1)}_{n}=2\left(E^{(1)}_{n}-\Delta^{zz}_{q_{i}}\right)/J
is a dimensionless energy. The Hamiltonian becomes diagonal in the eigenbasis of the corresponding subspace. The superscript refers as usual to the number of spin in down state.

At this stage, it is instructive to comment on the inhomogeneous case
x_{j}\neq x
. Although simple at first glance does not admit a closed-form solution in terms of elementary functions. This is the consequence of the fact that the recurrence equation
P_{j-1}+P_{j+1}=x_{j}P_{j}
can only be solved analytically for restricted cases of sequences
x_{j}
(such as constant, periodic, slowly varying, or perturbative deviations from homogeneity). In this piece of work, this case is addressed perturbatively with remarkable agreement with the data from exact diagonalization (see Section
IV
).

For further purposes, it would be interesting to briefly review how the three-term equation for
P_{j}
is solved in the homogeneous case. Because this equation is subject to open boundary conditions, a general solution can be constructed as
P_{j}=\beta^{j}
(see Ref.
70
). Plugging this back into the equation for
P_{j}
yields the quadratic equation
\beta^{2}-x\beta+1=0
thence the general solution
P_{j}=\phi_{1}\left(\beta_{1}\right)^{j}+\phi_{2}\left(\beta_{2}\right)^{j}
with
\beta_{1,2}=\left(x\pm\sqrt{x^{2}-4}\right)/2
. The boundary conditions teach us that
\phi_{1}=-\phi_{2}
and
\left(\beta_{1}/\beta_{2}\right)^{K+1}=1
. We observe that
\beta_{1}/\beta_{2}
is a root of unity.
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
must be complex which can only be realized if
x^{2}-4<0
. If we choose
x=2\cos\theta
with
0\leq\theta\leq\pi
then
\beta_{1}=e^{i\theta}
and
\beta_{2}=e^{-i\theta}
. Hence,
\left(\beta_{1}/\beta_{2}\right)^{K+1}=e^{2i\pi m}
with
m=1,\cdots,K
and whereby
\beta_{1}/\beta_{2}=e^{2i\theta_{m}}
with
\theta_{m}=\pi m/(K+1)
. Thus,
\beta^{(m)}_{1}=e^{i\theta_{m}}
,
\beta^{(m)}_{2}=e^{-i\theta_{m}}
and
P^{(m)}_{j}=A\sin\left(j\theta_{m}\right)
where
A
is a normalization constant evaluated from (
58
). This process yields the eigenvalues
[
63
,
10
]

\displaystyle E^{\left(1\right)}_{q_{m}}=\Delta^{zz}_{q_{m}}+J\cos\left(\frac{\pi m}{K+1}\right),

(59)

and the one-particle eigenstates

\displaystyle\lvert\psi^{\left(1\right)}_{q_{m}}\rangle=\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K}u_{j,m}\lvert q_{j}\rangle,

(60)

where

\displaystyle u_{j,m}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{K+1}}\sin\left(\frac{\pi j}{K+1}m\right),

(61)

with
1\leq m\leq K
. Note that
u_{j,m}=u_{m,j}
form a structured orthogonal symmetric matrix as explained in Ref.
[
10
]
and
\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{K}u_{i,k}u^{*}_{k,j}=\delta_{i,j}
. The eigenvalues tell us that only the unique spin in down state in the configuration
\lvert q_{j}\rangle_{1\leq j\leq K}
contributes to the energy. The wave function depends only on the total number of spins.

At this stage, we have to indicate that the restriction to open boundary conditions (OBCs) is made only for analytical convenience and is not related to our Dirac representation. For periodic boundary conditions (PBCs), the representation (
14
)-(
14
) remains unchanged. One only has to include the additional bond connecting the sites
j=K
and
j=1
. In the sector

, the recurrence equation

still holds but the OBCs
P_{0}=P_{K+1}=0
are replaced by the periodic boundary condition
P_{j+K}=P_{j}
. The corresponding eigenmodes are therefore the plane waves
P_{j}^{(m)}=Be^{ik_{m}j}
(with
B
a normalization constant that can be determined as in the OBC case presented above). Here,
k_{m}=2\pi m/K
and
m=0,1,\ldots,K-1
, leading in the homogeneous case
J_{z}=0
considered above to

\displaystyle E_{m,\mathrm{PBC}}^{(1)}=\Delta_{q_{m}}^{zz}+J\cos\left(\frac{2\pi m}{K}\right),

(62)

with
\Delta^{zz}_{q_{m}}
in Eq.(
56

results derived above for open chains are exact within the stated assumptions and do not rely on an expansion in the chain length.

Sector

\hskip-14.22636pt\Delta^{(j)+-}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}=\frac{J}{2}\left[\delta_{j_{1},j}\left(1-\delta_{j_{2},j+1}\right)+\delta_{j_{2},j}\left(1-\delta_{j_{1},j+1}\right)\right],\qquad\\

(63)

(64)

It should be noted that under the constraint
i<j

\displaystyle\frac{J}{2}\left[\left(1-\delta_{j_{1}-1,j_{2}}\right)C_{q_{j_{1}-1}+q_{j_{2}}}+\left(1-\delta_{j_{1}+1,j_{2}}\right)C_{q_{j_{1}+1}+q_{j_{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{J}{2}\left[\left(1-\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}-1}\right)C_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}-1}}+\left(1-\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}+1}\right)C_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}+1}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(E-\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}\right)C_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}.

This equation is solved with the conditions
C_{q_{0}+q_{j_{2}}}=C_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{0}}=0
and
C_{q_{K+1}+q_{j_{2}}}=C_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{K+1}}=0
for
j_{1}<j_{2}
. As we did for the second sector, we define the auxiliary variable
P_{j_{1},j_{2}}=C_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}
and obtain the two-dimensional linear recurrence equation

\displaystyle\left[\left(1-\delta_{j_{1}-1,j_{2}}\right)P_{j_{1}-1,j_{2}}+\left(1-\delta_{j_{1}+1,j_{2}}\right)P_{j_{1}+1,j_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left[\left(1-\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}-1}\right)P_{j_{1},j_{2}-1}+\left(1-\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}+1}\right)P_{j_{1},j_{2}+1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle xP_{j_{1},j_{2}},

with boundary conditions
P_{0,j_{2}}=P_{j_{1},0}=P_{K+1,j_{2}}=P_{j_{1},K+1}=0

. Once this equation is solved, the eigenvectors are calculated as

\hskip-14.22636pt\lvert\psi^{\left(2\right)}_{n}\rangle&=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{n}}\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq K}P_{j_{1},j_{2}}\left(\tilde{E}_{n}\right)\lvert q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}\rangle,\\

(67)

By virtue of the condition

it is legitimate to set

(hard-core condition). For

Accordingly, the properly constrained two-particle eigenfunction is given by the Slater determinant as we will see later.
As yet another consequence of this constraint, all Kronecker delta terms in Eq.(

\delta_{j_{1}-1,j_{2}}P_{j_{1}-1,j_{2}}=\delta_{j_{1}+1,j_{2}}P_{j_{1}+1,j_{2}}=0
; this is the consequence of the fact that in such terms every attempt of Kronecker delta to contribute is immediately multiplied by
P_{j,j}=0
. These observations greatly simplify Eq.(
III.2
) leading to,

\displaystyle\left[P_{j_{1}-1,j_{2}}+P_{j_{1}+1,j_{2}}\right]+\left[P_{j_{1},j_{2}-1}+P_{j_{1},j_{2}+1}\right]=xP_{j_{1},j_{2}}.

(68)

Here, it can be observed that the first brackets gather all terms with the same index
j_{2}
while the second one is for those with the same
j_{1}
. It can then be solved by separating the variable as
P_{j_{1},j_{2}}=X_{j_{1}}Y_{j_{2}}
. Inserting this into (
68
) and dividing the resulting equation by
P_{j_{1},j_{2}}
we find that the consistency of the leading equation is achieved when
X_{j_{1}-1}-\eta X_{j_{1}}+X_{j_{1}+1}=0
and
Y_{j_{2}-1}-\left(x-\eta\right)Y_{j_{2}}+Y_{j_{2}+1}=0
with
\eta
an arbitrary parameter. These equations are respectively solved with boundary conditions
X_{0}=X_{K+1}=0
and
Y_{0}=Y_{K+1}=0
. We have then transformed (
68
) into two uncoupled linear recurrence equation each describing the sector
K_{\downarrow}=1
. We obtain the eigenvalues (
1\leq m_{1}<m_{2}\leq K
),

\displaystyle E^{\left(2\right)}_{q_{m_{1}}+q_{m_{2}}}=\Delta^{zz}_{q_{m_{1}}+q_{m_{2}}}+J\cos\left(\frac{\pi m_{1}}{K+1}\right)+J\cos\left(\frac{\pi m_{2}}{K+1}\right),\quad

(69)

and the eigenstates

\displaystyle\lvert\psi^{\left(2\right)}_{m_{1},m_{2}}\rangle=\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq K}u_{j_{1},m_{1}}u_{j_{2},m_{2}}\lvert q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}\rangle.

(70)

However, (
70
) is not the correct expression for eigenstates. Indeed, because local spins are indistinguishable, the wave function should only change by a factor when interchanging particles with spins pointing in the same direction i.e.
\lvert\psi^{\left(2\right)}_{m_{1},m_{2}}\rangle=e^{i\phi}\lvert\psi^{\left(2\right)}_{m_{2},m_{1}}\rangle
. For states in the sectors
K_{\downarrow}=0
and
K_{\downarrow}=K
(fully symmetric states) the phase
\phi
is chosen as
\phi=0
while for states in other sectors, the wave function must be anti-symmetric by interchanging particles. The phase is chosen as
\phi=\pi
and,

\displaystyle\lvert\psi^{\left(2\right)}_{m_{1},m_{2}}\rangle=-\lvert\psi^{\left(2\right)}_{m_{2},m_{1}}\rangle.

(71)

For our construction (
70
), we observe that the two-particle wave function does not fulfill the anti-symmetric requirement (
71
). This problem is circumvented by re-constructing the wave function as the linear combination of the two solutions in (
71
) i.e.

\displaystyle\lvert\psi^{\left(2\right)}_{m_{1},m_{2}}\rangle=\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq K}\mathsf{D}_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{j_{1},j_{2}}\lvert q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}\rangle,

(72)

where

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{j_{1},j_{2}}=\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}u_{j_{1},m_{1}}&u_{j_{1},m_{2}}\\
u_{j_{2},m_{1}}&u_{j_{2},m_{2}}\end{array}\right|=\sumop\displaylimits_{i,j=1}^{2}\epsilon_{i,j}u_{j_{1},m_{i}}u_{j_{2},m_{j}},

is nothing but the two-particle Slater determinant
[
37
]
with
1\leq m_{1}<m_{2}\leq K
. This is also a consequence of the hard-core condition we mentioned earlier. In these expressions
j_{2}=j_{1}+1
such that for a function
g_{j_{1},j_{2}}
the double summation in (
72
) can be viewed as
\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq K}g_{j_{1},j_{2}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{j_{1}=1}^{K}\sumop\displaylimits_{j_{2}=j_{1}+1}^{K}g_{j_{1},j_{2}}
. In Eq.(
III.2
),

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i,j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}+1,&\quad{\rm if}\quad\left(i,j\right)=\left(1,2\right),\\
-1,&\quad{\rm if}\quad\left(i,j\right)=\left(2,1\right),\\
0,&\quad{\rm if}\quad i=j\end{array}\right.

is the two-dimensional Levi-Civita symbol. The Slater determinant vanishes by repetition of at least two indices,
\mathsf{D}_{m,m}^{j_{1},j_{2}}=\mathsf{D}_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{j,j}=\mathsf{D}_{m,m}^{j,j}=0
.

It is also instructive to examine how periodic boundary conditions modify the present
K_{\downarrow}=2
sector. As in the one-spin-down sector, the bulk recurrence equation remains unchanged, while the site indices are now understood modulo
K
, so that sites
K
and

1
become nearest neighbors. In particular, the configuration
\lvert q_{1}+q_{K}\rangle
, which involves the two boundary sites for OBC, becomes a nearest-neighbor configuration on the periodic chain. For
J_{z}=0
, the two-particle amplitudes can be written in the
antisymmetrized form

\displaystyle P_{j_{1},j_{2}}=A\left[e^{i(k_{1}j_{1}+k_{2}j_{2})}-e^{i(k_{2}j_{1}+k_{1}j_{2})}\right],\qquad j_{1}<j_{2},

(80)

which automatically satisfies the hard-core condition
P_{j,j}=0
and
P_{j_{1},j_{2}}=-P_{j_{2},j_{1}}
. The wave-numbers
k_{1,2}
are determined from boundary conditions and
A
from the normalization condition. Substituting into the bulk recurrence equation (
68
) gives

\displaystyle E^{(2)}_{k_{1},k_{2},\rm PBC}=\Delta_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}^{zz}+J\cos k_{1}+J\cos k_{2}.

(81)

Given that transporting one coordinate once around the periodic chain
changes its ordering relative to the other coordinate, the antisymmetric amplitude acquires a minus sign. The periodicity condition therefore becomes

\displaystyle e^{ik_{\ell}K}=-1,\qquad k_{\ell}=\frac{2\pi}{K}\left(m_{\ell}+\frac{1}{2}\right),\qquad\ell=1,2,

(82)

with distinct quantum numbers
m_{1}\neq m_{2}
. Hence, as in the sector
K_{\downarrow}=1
, passing from OBC to PBC requires no modification of the Dirac representation or of the bulk recurrence equations. Only the boundary connectivity and the corresponding momentum quantization are modified.

At this stage, one might be tempted to conjecture about the global structure of the eigensystem. However, in order to ensure a better understanding, it is preferable to first gain further insight into the general structure of the recurrence equation by briefly examining the sector with three spins in the down state.

Sector
K_{\downarrow}=3
:

The sub-Hilbert space corresponds to all possible manners of replacing three spins in a spin chain with
K
identical spins in up state with three spins in down state. It can quickly be verified that

\Delta^{(j)+-}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+q_{j_{3}}}&=\frac{J}{2}\left[\delta_{j_{1},j}\left(1-\delta_{j_{2},j+1}-\delta_{j_{3},j+1}\right)+\delta_{j_{2},j}\left(1-\delta_{j_{1},j+1}-\delta_{j_{3},j+1}\right)+\delta_{j_{3},j}\left(1-\delta_{j_{1},j+1}-\delta_{j_{2},j+1}\right)\right],\\
\Delta^{(j)-+}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+q_{j_{3}}}=\frac{J}{2}\left[\delta_{j_{1},j+1}\left(1-\delta_{j_{2},j}-\delta_{j_{3},j}\right)+\delta_{j_{2},j+1}\left(1-\delta_{j_{1},j}-\delta_{j_{3},j}\right)+\delta_{j_{3},j+1}\left(1-\delta_{j_{1},j}-\delta_{j_{2},j}\right)\right].

(83)

In the same fashion as we did for previous sectors, this leads to the three-dimensional linear recurrence equation

\displaystyle\left[\left(1-\delta_{j_{1}-1,j_{2}}-\delta_{j_{1}-1,j_{3}}\right)P_{j_{1}-1,j_{2},j_{3}}+\left(1-\delta_{j_{1}+1,j_{2}}-\delta_{j_{1}+1,j_{3}}\right)P_{j_{1}+1,j_{2},j_{3}}\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left[\left(1-\delta_{j_{2}-1,j_{1}}-\delta_{j_{2}-1,j_{3}}\right)P_{j_{2}-1,j_{1},j_{3}}+\left(1-\delta_{j_{2}+1,j_{1}}-\delta_{j_{2}+1,j_{3}}\right)P_{j_{2}+1,j_{1},j_{3}}\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left[\left(1-\delta_{j_{3}-1,j_{1}}-\delta_{j_{3}-1,j_{2}}\right)P_{j_{3}-1,j_{1},j_{2}}+\left(1-\delta_{j_{3}+1,j_{1}}-\delta_{j_{3}+1,j_{2}}\right)P_{j_{3}+1,j_{1},j_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle xP_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}.

(84)

This equation is solved with boundary conditions
P_{0,j_{2},j_{3}}=P_{j_{1},0,j_{3}}=P_{j_{1},j_{2},0}=0
,
P_{K+1,j_{2},j_{3}}=P_{j_{1},K+1,j_{3}}=P_{j_{1},j_{2},K+1}=0
and
x=2(E-\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+q_{j_{3}}})/J
. It is important to go deep into this case in order to have a clear comprehension of our ongoing strategy. As in previous cases and by virtue of the assumption
j_{1}<j_{2}<j_{3}
we can assume that
P_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}=0
(hard-core condition) for every repeated (two or more) indices. For example
P_{j_{1},j_{1},j_{3}}=P_{j_{1},j_{1},j_{1}}=P_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{2}}=0
and many more. Hence, all Kronecker delta term in (
84
) vanish leading us to

\displaystyle\left[P_{j_{1}-1,j_{2},j_{3}}+P_{j_{1}+1,j_{2},j_{3}}\right]+\left[P_{j_{1},j_{2}-1,j_{3}}+P_{j_{1},j_{2}+1,j_{3}}\right]

\displaystyle+\left[P_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}-1}+P_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}+1}\right]=xP_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}.

(85)

By separation of variable as we did so far, one achieves a set of three uncoupled linear-recurrence equations that are solved exactly as for the case of
K_{\downarrow}=1
. This task leads to an eigensystem similar to (
69
) and (
70
). Hence,
\lvert\psi^{\left(3\right)}_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}\rangle=\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<j_{3}\leq K}u_{j_{1},m_{1}}u_{j_{2},m_{2}}u_{j_{3},m_{3}}\lvert q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+q_{j_{3}}\rangle.
As already pinpointed, such a wave function does not fulfill the fundamental principle of indistinguishability of particles. Upon overcoming this constraint through the three-particle Slater determinant
[
37
]
we obtain (
1\leq m_{1}<m_{2}<m_{3}\leq K
),

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\lvert\psi^{\left(3\right)}_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}\rangle=\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<j_{3}\leq K}\mathsf{D}_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}^{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}\lvert q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+q_{j_{3}}\rangle,

(86)

where

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}^{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\begin{array}[]{ccc}u_{j_{1},m_{1}}&u_{j_{1},m_{2}}&u_{j_{1},m_{3}}\\
u_{j_{2},m_{1}}&u_{j_{2},m_{2}}&u_{j_{2},m_{3}}\\
u_{j_{3},m_{1}}&u_{j_{3},m_{2}}&u_{j_{3},m_{3}}\end{array}\right|

(91)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{i,j,k=1}^{3}\epsilon_{i,j,k}u_{j_{1},m_{i}}u_{j_{2},m_{j}}u_{j_{3},m_{k}}.

Here,
\epsilon_{i,j,k}
is the three-dimensional Levi-Civita symbol. For a given function
g_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}
the triple summation in (
86
) can be replaced by
\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<j_{3}\leq K}g_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{j_{1}=1}^{K}\sumop\displaylimits_{j_{2}=j_{1}+1}^{K}\sumop\displaylimits_{j_{3}=j_{2}+1}^{K}g_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}
.

\displaystyle\Delta^{(j)+-}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+q_{j_{3}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K_{\downarrow}}}}=\frac{J}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell=1}^{K_{\downarrow}}\delta_{j_{\ell},j}\mathcal{X}_{j_{\ell}+1},\quad

(92)

(93)

\displaystyle\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+q_{j_{3}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K_{\downarrow}}}}=h\left(\frac{K}{2}-K_{\downarrow}\right),

(94)

where we have defined

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{j_{\ell}\pm 1}=1-\sumop\displaylimits_{k\neq\ell}^{K_{\downarrow}}\delta_{j_{k},j_{\ell}\pm 1}.

(95)

Although the Kronecker deltas entering

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell=1}^{K_{\downarrow}}\left(\mathcal{X}_{j_{\ell}-1}P_{j_{\ell}-1,\cdots,j_{K_{\downarrow}}}+\mathcal{X}_{j_{\ell}+1}P_{j_{\ell}+1,\cdots,j_{K_{\downarrow}}}\right)=xP_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{K_{\downarrow}}},

i.e.

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell=1}^{K_{\downarrow}}\left(P_{j_{\ell}-1,\cdots,j_{K_{\downarrow}}}+P_{j_{\ell}+1,\cdots,j_{K_{\downarrow}}}\right)=xP_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{K_{\downarrow}}},

(97)

and is associated with the traditional boundary conditions

P_{0,j_{2},\cdots,j_{K_{\downarrow}}}=P_{j_{1},0,\cdots,j_{K_{\downarrow}}}=\cdots=P_{j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,0}=0

Upon solving Eq.(
97

\displaystyle E_{n}=\Delta^{zz}_{n}+J\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K}s^{(j)+}_{n}\cos\left(\frac{\pi j}{K+1}\right).

(98)

Here,
s^{(j)+}_{n}
and
s^{(j)z}_{n}
are classical spins in (

16

\displaystyle\lvert\psi^{\left(K_{\downarrow}\right)}_{\bf i}\rangle=\sumop\displaylimits_{\bf j}\mathcal{D}^{\left(K_{\downarrow}\right)}_{{\bf i},{\bf j}}\lvert{\bf n_{j}}\rangle,

(99)

where

is the multi-particle Slater determinant

III.3
Comparison with previously published exact results

The above obtained analytical results can directly be compared with previously published exact results for the open spin-
1/2
XXZ chain. This comparison is particularly useful because it provides an independent validation of the recurrence-equation approach derived from our Dirac representation. We consider here the coordinate Bethe-ansatz solution of Kapustin and Skorik
[
31
]
, who
studied an open XXZ chain in boundary magnetic fields. Their
Hamiltonian is written in the convention adopted throughout the present work as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm KS}=2\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}\left(\hat{S}_{j}^{x}\hat{S}_{j+1}^{x}+\hat{S}_{j}^{y}\hat{S}_{j+1}^{y}+\Delta\hat{S}_{j}^{z}\hat{S}_{j+1}^{z}\right)+h_{1}\hat{S}_{1}^{z}+h_{2}\hat{S}_{K}^{z}.

For vanishing boundary fields,
h_{1}=h_{2}=0
, this reduces to the standard open XXZ chain. This Hamiltonian and ours are related through

J=2
,
J_{z}=2\Delta
. Consequently, the
J_{z}=0
analytical results derived above correspond
to the XX limit
\Delta=0
of the Bethe-ansatz solution.

Kapustin and Skorik construct the
n
-magnon eigenstates as
[
31
]

\displaystyle|n\rangle=\sumop\displaylimits_{x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}}f^{(n)}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})|x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle,

(102)

where the coordinate wave function contains all permutations and
sign changes of the magnon momenta
p_{j}
. The corresponding momenta
are determined by the open-chain Bethe equations. In the XX limit

\Delta=0
, and in the absence of boundary fields, these equations
reduce to

\displaystyle e^{2i(K+1)p_{j}}=1,

(103)

and therefore

\displaystyle p_{m}=\frac{\pi m}{K+1},\qquad m=1,\ldots,K.

(104)

Eq.(
104
) is identical to the
quantization condition obtained directly from the Dirac recurrence
equation,
\theta_{m}=\pi m/(K+1)
which led to Eq.(
59
). For a single spin in the down state, the exact Bethe-ansatz energy in
this limit is

\displaystyle E_{\rm BA}^{(1)}=2\cos p_{m}=2\cos\left(\frac{\pi m}{K+1}\right).

(105)

On the other hand, Eq.(
59
) for
h=0
and
J=2
yields,
E_{q_{m}}^{(1)}=J\cos\left(\theta_{m}\right)
telling us that
E_{q_{m}}^{(1)}=E_{\rm BA}^{(1)}
. Thus, the agreement is mode by mode and holds at finite chain length without invoking the thermodynamic limit.

The correspondence extends to the eigenfunctions. The one-particle
wave functions obtained from the recurrence equation are given in Eq.(
61
).
The open-chain Bethe wave function contains the two reflected waves with momenta
p_{m}
and
-p_{m}
. Their appropriate linear combination is proportional to

\displaystyle e^{ip_{m}j}-e^{-ip_{m}j}=2i\sin(p_{m}j),

(106)

and therefore reproduces Eq.(
61
), up to a normalization factor. Hence, both the spectrum and the spatial dependence of the one-magnon eigenstates coincide with the exact known-solution.

The same comparison can be performed in the sector
K_{\downarrow}=2
. For
n=2
,
\Delta=0
, and
h_{1}=h_{2}=0
, the Bethe momenta satisfy

\displaystyle p_{\ell}=\frac{\pi m_{\ell}}{K+1},\qquad 1\leq m_{1}<m_{2}\leq K,

(107)

with
\ell=1,2
. The Bethe-ansatz energy formula then reads,

\displaystyle E_{\rm BA}^{(2)}=2\cos\left(\frac{\pi m_{1}}{K+1}\right)+2\cos\left(\frac{\pi m_{2}}{K+1}\right).

(108)

Our Eq.(
69
) evaluated for
h=0
and
J=2
gives precisely

E_{q_{m_{1}}+q_{m_{2}}}^{(2)}=E_{\rm BA}^{(2)}
. The equality therefore again holds for every allowed pair
(m_{1},m_{2})
at finite
K
.

The agreement also extends to the two-particle wave function. In the
present formulation the hard-core constraint
P_{j,j}=0
is
implemented by the antisymmetrized combination (
III.2
). Using Eq.(
61
), this expression becomes

\displaystyle D_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{j_{1},j_{2}}\propto\sin(p_{1}j_{1})\sin(p_{2}j_{2})-\sin(p_{2}j_{1})\sin(p_{1}j_{2}),\qquad

(109)

which after expressing the sine functions in exponential form, contains precisely the permutations and momentum sign reversals entering the coordinate Bethe-ansatz wave function for an open chain. The two constructions therefore yield the same two-particle eigenspace, up to normalization and overall phase.

These comparisons explicitly establish that the recurrence equations steming from the Dirac representation reproduce the previously known exact open-chain results in the sectors where a direct analytical comparison can be made. This suggests that the same finite-size spectra are recovered here without resorting to Bethe rapidities or solving the coupled Bethe equations. In the XX limit, they follow directly from the finite-dimensional recurrence relations generated by the lattice-spin Dirac representation.

As a yet another benchmark, we considered the
1D
transverse-field Ising model exactly solved by Pfeuty
[
52
]
. This comparison is useful because, unlike the XXZ Hamiltonian considered above, this model does not conserve the total magnetization. It therefore stand as another test-bed for the Dirac representation outside the fixed-
K_{\downarrow}
decomposition used in the preceding sections. Details of our derivations are deferred in Appendix
C
.

IV
Non-integrable version

We have so far discussed the integrable version of (
38
) and got full access to its eigenspectrum in some specific cases. Although this helps gain insight into the physics of spin systems, physics can be radically different when systems are found in more realistic situations. For example spin systems contaminated with sufficiently strong disorder exhibit many-body localization losing their ability to thermalize and explore their entire Hilbert space
[
59
,
61
]
. This leads to a new phase of matter. Understanding this phenomenon requires analyzing energy-level spacing or inverse participation ratios (IPR)
[
64
,
67
]
. The method presented above fails as a consequence of the fact that the
h_{j}
are all distinct and the anisotropy parameter playing a crucial role leads to tight-binding equations that cannot be solved in compact form. It is commonly desired in such cases to use exact-diagonalization (ED) to evaluate the eigenspectrum in order to discriminate between ergodic and MBL phases and to probe precursors of localization and benchmark against ED in spin chains.
As already mentioned the exponential growth of the Hilbert space with the total number of spins, causes finite-size effects dramatically limiting the number of implementable spins. The strongest methods requiring large computer memory and long runtimes can handle only up to
K=24
spins (to the best of our knowledge) provided one can identify and exploit certain symmetries as we did in Section.
III
. Throughout we assume generic continuous disorder, so the unperturbed spectrum is almost surely non-degenerate; near-resonances control the convergence of the expansion.

We propose in this Section to perturbatively calculate the eigenspectrum (up to the fourth order) of the spin-
1/2
random XXZ Heisenberg model (
38
) in an open boundary configuration. Analytical expressions are written down and could be used to dig deeper into MBL. Our results are confirmed with data from ED in small chains and could easily be extended to arbitrary
K
by simply setting the desired value of
K
into our expressions.

A perturbation theory including higher order is elaborated in Appendix
D
. As traditionally required, the Hamiltonian is split into an unperturbed part
\hat{H}_{0}
whose spectrum is well-identified and a perturbed part
\hat{H}_{1}
with respect to a perturbative parameter, say
J
such that
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+J\hat{H}_{1}
. For our case,
\hat{H}_{0}=J_{z}\sumop\displaylimits^{K-1}_{j=1}\hat{S}^{z}_{j}\hat{S}^{z}_{j+1}+\sumop\displaylimits^{K}_{j=1}h_{j}\hat{S}^{z}_{j}
is diagonal with entries
\Delta^{zz}_{n}
in Eq.(
52
) assumed to be real and non-degenerate while
\hat{H}_{1}=\sumop\displaylimits^{K-1}_{j=1}\left(\hat{S}^{x}_{j}\hat{S}^{x}_{j+1}+\hat{S}^{y}_{j}\hat{S}^{y}_{j+1}\right)
with the structured symmetric matrix elements
X_{n,m}=\langle n\rvert\hat{H}_{1}\lvert m\rangle
given by,

\displaystyle X_{n,m}=J^{-1}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}\left(\Delta_{m}^{\left(j\right)+-}\delta_{n,m-Q_{j}}+\Delta_{m}^{\left(j\right)-+}\delta_{n,m+Q_{j}}\right).

(110)

The factor
J^{-1}
is inserted in order to compensate the
J
in
\Delta_{m}^{\left(j\right)+-}
and
\Delta_{m}^{\left(j\right)-+}
such that
X_{n,m}
becomes independent on
J
.
In the Dirac representation, we see that
X_{n,m}
can be regarded as a generalized hopping model where
\Delta_{m}^{\left(j\right)+-}
and
\Delta_{m}^{\left(j\right)-+}
determine the hopping amplitude between neighboring sites separated by
Q_{j}
such that
X_{n,n+Q_{j}}=\Delta_{n+Q_{j}}^{\left(j\right)+-}=\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)-+}
and
X_{n,n-Q_{j}}=\Delta_{n-Q_{j}}^{\left(j\right)-+}=\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)+-}
. Note that
X_{n,n}=0
typically because
Q_{j}>0
and there is no
j
in the interval
1\leq j\leq K-1
for which
\delta_{n,n+Q_{j}}=\delta_{n,n-Q_{j}}=1
.

For our perturbative analysis, we are interested in the energy and wave function of the
n
th configuration determined up to the fourth order as

\displaystyle E_{n}\approx\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{3}J^{k}\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\left(k\right)}+\mathcal{O}\left(J^{4}\right),

(111)

\displaystyle\lvert\psi_{n}\rangle\approx\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{2}J^{k}\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(k\right)}\rangle+\mathcal{O}\left(J^{3}\right).

(112)

Here,
\mathcal{E}^{\left(k\right)}_{n}
and
\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(k\right)}\rangle
are the
k
-order corrections to the energy and eigenstates respectively. The superscripts should not be mistaken for sectors labels and the subscripts
m
do not refer to the magnetic quantum number. Foremost, we start by describing the energy. According to the perturbation theory in Appendix
D
the zero-order contribution to the energy corresponds to the eigenvalue of
\hat{H}_{0}
and reads
\mathcal{E}^{\left(0\right)}_{n}=\Delta^{zz}_{n}
while the first order
\mathcal{E}^{\left(1\right)}_{n}=X_{n,n}=0
for the aforementioned reasons. Other contributions write

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\left(2\right)}_{n}=\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=0\\
m\neq n\end{subarray}}^{D-1}\frac{X_{n,m}X_{m,n}}{\Lambda_{n,m}},

(113)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\left(3\right)}_{n}=\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}=0\\
m_{1}\neq n\end{subarray}}^{D-1}\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{2}=0\\
m_{2}\neq n,\,m_{2}\neq m_{1}\end{subarray}}^{D-1}\frac{X_{n,m_{1}}X_{m_{1},m_{2}}X_{m_{2},n}}{\Lambda_{n,m_{1}}\Lambda_{n,m_{2}}},

(114)

where
\Lambda_{n,m}
denotes the energy difference (gap)
\Lambda_{n,m}=\Delta^{zz}_{n}-\Delta^{zz}_{m}
between the configurations
\lvert n\rangle
and
\lvert m\rangle
. It is also the energy accumulated by the system when transitioning from
\lvert n\rangle
to
\lvert m\rangle
vice versa. Note that when
J=0
(Anderson limit) no transition between the eigenstates of
\hat{H}_{0}
occurs,
E_{n}=\Delta_{n}^{zz}
as all corrections vanish, the system remains in its original configuration.

In the expression for the eigenstates, the zero-order correction corresponds to the eigenstate of
\hat{H}_{0}
namely
\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(0\right)}\rangle=\lvert n\rangle
whereas the first and second order contributions are respectively given by

\displaystyle\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(1\right)}\rangle=\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=0\\
m\neq n\end{subarray}}^{D-1}\frac{X_{n,m}}{\Lambda_{n,m}}\lvert m\rangle,

(115)

and

\displaystyle\hskip-21.33955pt\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(2\right)}\rangle=\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=0\\
m\neq n\end{subarray}}^{D-1}\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}=0\\
m_{1}\neq n\end{subarray}}^{D-1}\frac{X_{n,m_{1}}X_{m_{1},m}}{\Lambda_{n,m_{1}}\Lambda_{n,m}}\lvert m\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=0\\
m\neq n\end{subarray}}^{D-1}\frac{X_{n,m}X_{m,n}}{\Lambda^{2}_{n,m}}\right)\lvert n\rangle.

(116)

Albeit the above expressions can readily be implemented in any symbolic calculator, our main goal is to analytically calculate them and break them down into sectors corresponding to fixed values of the magnetic quantum number.

IV.0.1
Eigenenergies

We start with the eigenenergies. Thanks to the hopping-shaped form of
X_{n,m}
in terms of Kronecker Delta symbols plugging this back into Eq.(
113
) allows us after a few calculations to find that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\left(2\right)}_{n}=J^{-2}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}\left(\frac{\left(\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)-+}\right)^{2}}{\Lambda_{n,n+Q_{j}}}+\frac{\left(\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)+-}\right)^{2}}{\Lambda_{n,n-Q_{j}}}\right).

(117)

Similarly,
\mathcal{E}^{\left(3\right)}_{n}=0
because every application of

\hat{H}_{1}
flips a nearest-neighbor pair, so returning to
\lvert n\rangle
requires an even number of flips (no closed walk of odd length in the configuration graph). As a consequence of these results,
E_{n}\approx\Delta^{zz}_{n}+J^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\left(2\right)}_{n}+\mathcal{O}\left(|J|^{4}\right)
, the energy holds up to the order
\mathcal{O}\left(|J|^{4}\right)
. Note that although
J^{2}\mathcal{E}^{(2)}_{n}
is second order in the perturbation
J\hat{H}_{1}
, the Dirac hopping amplitudes satisfy
\Delta^{(j)\pm\mp}_{n}\propto J
; using the projector identities
(\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)-+})^{2}=\frac{J}{2}(\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)-+})
and
(\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)+-})^{2}=\frac{J}{2}(\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)+-})
as elaborated in the Subsection
III.2
yields an overall correction linear in
J
. Thus,

\displaystyle E_{n}\approx\Delta^{zz}_{n}+\frac{J}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}\left(\frac{\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)-+}}{\Lambda_{n,n+Q_{j}}}+\frac{\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)+-}}{\Lambda_{n,n-Q_{j}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(|J|^{4}\right).

(118)

This result is numerically verified (small chains, generic disorder realizations) and we found a good agreement with ED for
|J|\lesssim 1
as depicted in Fig.
6
. We observe that the eigenenergies converge well to the exact diagonalization results. This is a consequence of the fact that
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(1)}=\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(3)}=0
leading to a high correction. We also observe from Fig.
6
(a)
that for large anisotropy (
J_{z}\gg 1
) when
E_{n}
are sorted in ascending order as a function
n
they depict several clusters where energies are nearly degenerate. This behavior disappears for small and moderate anisotropy as shown in Fig.
6
(c)
.

Despite the above observations, it remains of paramount importance to break (
118
) down into sectors (with a focus on even
K
). To this end, we first prove that for
1\leq K_{\downarrow}\leq K-1
and
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{K_{\downarrow}}\leq K-1
,

\displaystyle\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K_{\downarrow}}}}=\left(\frac{{\bf h}}{2}-\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{K_{\downarrow}}h_{j_{k}}\right)+J_{z}\left(\frac{K-1}{4}-K_{\downarrow}\right)

\displaystyle+J_{z}\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell=1}^{K_{\downarrow}}\left(f_{j_{\ell}}+\sumop\displaylimits_{k\neq\ell}^{K_{\downarrow}}\delta_{j_{k},j_{\ell}+1}\right),

(119)

where
f_{j_{\ell}}=\left(\delta_{j_{\ell},1}-\delta_{j_{\ell},K}\right)/2
is associated with spin chain’s gates;
j_{\ell}=1

(f_{1}=1/2)
and
j_{\ell}=K

(f_{K}=-1/2)
are respectively the left and right gates. The region
1<j<K
is the active domain where
f_{j}=0
.
Expression (
119
) is extended to the last spin i.e.
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{K_{\downarrow}}\leq K
by replacing
f_{j_{\ell}}
by
g_{j_{\ell}}=\left(\delta_{j_{\ell},1}+\delta_{j_{\ell},K}\right)/2
. Defining the occupation number
n_{i}=\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell=1}^{K_{\downarrow}}\delta_{i,j_{\ell}}
we learn that
\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell=1}^{K_{\downarrow}}f_{j_{\ell}}=\left(n_{1}-n_{K}\right)/2
. Similarly,
\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell=1}^{K_{\downarrow}}\sumop\displaylimits_{k\neq\ell}^{K_{\downarrow}}\delta_{j_{k},j_{\ell}+1}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{K-1}n_{i}n_{i+1}
where
n_{i}=(0,1)
.

It should be noted that the second order correction (
117
) has the same structure as (
54
).
\mathcal{E}^{\left(2\right)}_{n}
is therefore expected in each sector to cast the form of the relations for the
P_{j_{1},\cdots,K_{\downarrow}}
as shown below.

Sector
K_{\downarrow}=1
:

In this sector, classical variables are given by Eq.(
55
). The energy of the system reads,

\displaystyle E^{\left(K_{\downarrow}=1\right)}_{q_{j}}\approx\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j}}+\left(W_{j,j-1}+W_{j,j+1}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(J^{4}\right),

(120)

where

\displaystyle\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j}}=\left(\frac{{\bf h}}{2}-h_{j}\right)+J_{z}\left(\frac{K-1}{4}-1\right)+J_{z}g_{j},

(121)

and

\displaystyle W_{i,j}=-\frac{\left(\frac{J}{2}\right)^{2}}{\left(h_{i}-h_{j}\right)-J_{z}\left(g_{i}-g_{j}\right)},

(122)

is defined with the constraints
W_{i,0}=W_{i,K+1}=W_{i,i}=0
. Eq.(
120
) is reminiscent of a
1D
effective tight-binding picture in configuration space for a free traveling particle. In this context,
\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j}}
plays the role of the onsite energy while the dimensionless parameter
W_{i,j}
depicts the interplay between interactions and disorder on one hand, disorder and anisotropy on the other hand and can be interpreted as the transfer rate between the states
\lvert q_{i}\rangle
and
\lvert q_{j}\rangle
. Population transfer is done by successive jumps between neighboring states. These results could be interesting in deciphering the complex mechanism behind MBL in spin chains. For example, for a sample drawn from a uniform distribution, we have calculated the inverse participation ratio (IPR) in Fig.
7
for
500
realizations. We see that the wave function tends to concentrate in certain regions more than others indicating the existence of localization.

Sector
K_{\downarrow}=2
:
In this subspace,
\Delta^{(j)+-}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}
and
\Delta^{(j)-+}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}
are given by (
63
) while

\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}
can easily be inferred from (
119
). As a result of substituting the corresponding expressions into (
118
) one obtains

\displaystyle\left[\left(1-\delta_{j_{1}-1,j_{2}}\right)W_{j_{1}-1,j_{2}}+\left(1-\delta_{j_{1}+1,j_{2}}\right)W_{j_{1}+1,j_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left[\left(1-\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}-1}\right)W_{j_{1},j_{2}-1}+\left(1-\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}+1}\right)W_{j_{1},j_{2}+1}\right]

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\approx E^{\left(K_{\downarrow}=2\right)}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}-\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}},

(123)

where

\displaystyle\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}=\left(\frac{{\bf h}}{2}-\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{2}h_{j_{k}}\right)+J_{z}\left(\frac{K-1}{4}-2\right)

\displaystyle+J_{z}\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell=1}^{2}g_{j_{\ell}}+J_{z}\left(\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}+1}+\delta_{j_{2},j_{1}+1}\right),

(124)

and

W_{j_{1},j_{2}\pm 1}=\frac{\left(\frac{J}{2}\right)^{2}}{\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}-\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}\pm 1}}},\\
W_{j_{1}\pm 1,j_{2}}=\frac{\left(\frac{J}{2}\right)^{2}}{\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}-\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}\pm 1}+q_{j_{2}}}}.

(125)

Here,

\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}-\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}\pm 1}+q_{j_{2}}}=-\left(h_{j_{1}}-h_{j_{1}\pm 1}\right)+J_{z}\left(g_{j_{1}}-g_{j_{1}\pm 1}\right)\\
+J_{z}\left(\delta_{j_{1}+1,j_{2}}-\delta_{j_{1}\pm 2,j_{2}}\right),\quad\\
\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}-\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}\pm 1}}=-\left(h_{j_{2}}-h_{j_{2}\pm 1}\right)+J_{z}\left(g_{j_{2}}-g_{j_{2}\pm 1}\right)\\
+J_{z}\left(\delta_{j_{1}+1,j_{2}}-\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}\pm 2}\right).\quad

(126)

Eq.(
123
) is subject to the constraint
W_{0,j_{2}}=W_{j_{1},0}=W_{j_{1}+K,j_{2}}=W_{j_{1},j_{2}+K}=W_{j,j}=0
. It mimics a two-dimensional semi-classical tight-binding model. Population transfer is equally done by successive jumps between neighboring states.

The energy in other sectors can be evaluated in a similar fashion. However, as suggested earlier, the perturbative energy being of the form (
54
), we have confirmed that in each sector it can be reduced to the tight-binding equation for the
P_{j_{1},\cdots,K_{\downarrow}}
. For instance, (

) in the sector
K_{\downarrow}=1
mimics (

) mimics (

). It is then legitimate to claim without further calculations that in the sector

the energy will be of the form (

\displaystyle E^{\left(K_{\downarrow}\right)}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K_{\downarrow}}}}\approx\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K_{\downarrow}}}}+\hskip 91.04872pt

\displaystyle+\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell=1}^{K_{\downarrow}}\left(\mathcal{X}_{j_{\ell}-1}W_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{\ell}-1,\cdots,j_{K_{\downarrow}}}+\mathcal{X}_{j_{\ell}+1}W_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{\ell}+1,\cdots,j_{K_{\downarrow}}}\right),\qquad

(127)

where

\displaystyle W_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{\ell}\pm 1,\cdots,j_{K_{\downarrow}}}=\frac{\left(\frac{J}{2}\right)^{2}}{\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K_{\downarrow}}}}-\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}\cdots+q_{j_{\ell}\pm 1}+\cdots+q_{j_{K_{\downarrow}}}}}.

The constraints on the

IV.0.2
Eigenstates

For eigenstates, after calculations one obtains at the first order,

\displaystyle\hskip-21.33955pt\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(1\right)}\rangle=\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}\left(\frac{\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)-+}}{\Lambda_{n,n+Q_{j}}}\lvert n+Q_{j}\rangle+\frac{\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)+-}}{\Lambda_{n,n-Q_{j}}}\lvert n-Q_{j}\rangle\right).

(129)

This relation is also of the form (

). Thus, without further calculations and for the same reasons as evoked earlier, it can be broken down into sectors exactly as we did for the energies. In the second and third sectors for example:

\displaystyle\hskip-21.33955pt\lvert\psi_{q_{j}}\rangle\approx\lvert q_{j}\rangle+\left(W_{j,j+1}\lvert q_{j+1}\rangle+W_{j,j-1}\lvert q_{j-1}\rangle\right).

(130)

and

\displaystyle\lvert\psi_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}\rangle\approx\lvert q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}\rangle

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\Big[\left(1-\delta_{j_{1}-1,j_{2}}\right)W_{j_{1}-1,j_{2}}\lvert q_{j_{1}-1}+q_{j_{2}}\rangle

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(1-\delta_{j_{1}+1,j_{2}}\right)W_{j_{1}+1,j_{2}}\lvert q_{j_{1}+1}+q_{j_{2}}\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\Big[\left(1-\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}-1}\right)W_{j_{1},j_{2}-1}\lvert q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}-1}\rangle

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(1-\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}+1}\right)W_{j_{1},j_{2}+1}\lvert q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}+1}\rangle\Big].

The second order correction can be calculated in a similar manner and broken into sectors.
This task is left for future projects.

V
Outlook and perspective: Spin-
1
Case

We have presented in previous Sections the
“bra-ket”
(Dirac representation) for spin-
1/2
lattice operators and discussed a few applications. Because the case of spin-1 follows the same algebraic strategy, we present it here for completeness. In order to avoid lenghtly paper, possible applications are deferred to future projects.

In this case, spin projection along the quantization axis takes three values
+1,0,-1
. These local three-level systems (qutrits) constitute the building blocks of ternary quantum computing. They are known to be robust against external unwanted nuisances, encoding more information (already at room temperature) compared to qubits and offering better security during communication
[
38
,
15
]
. For these reasons it would be crucial to achieve qutrit without ternary decomposition as we did for qubits. This could improve on encrypting information during communications between quantum devices.

Foremost, note that qutrits can also be mapped onto their classical analogue
\sigma_{n}^{(j)}=\{0,1,2\}
(the trits), of the base-
3
. Classical trits cannot be subjected to the principle of quantum superposition nor entanglement in contrast to their quantum analogue. Here,
n
is the positive integer whose decomposition in base
3
encapsulates the exact configuration of the projections
+1,0,-1
. It can exactly be written as in Eq.(
8
) now with
q_{j}=3^{K-j}
and
D=3^{K}
. Without loss of generality, it can be assumed that

\lvert+1\rangle\to\lvert 0\rangle=[1,0,0]^{T}
,
\lvert 0\rangle\to\lvert 1\rangle=[0,1,0]^{T}
and
\lvert-1\rangle\to\lvert 2\rangle=[0,0,1]^{T}
yielding,

\displaystyle\langle m\rvert S^{x}_{j}\lvert n\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\delta_{m,\chi_{j}^{[n,1]}},\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=0,\\
\delta_{m,\chi_{j}^{[n,0]}}+\delta_{m,\chi_{j}^{[n,2]}},\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=1,\\
\delta_{m,\chi_{j}^{[n,1]}},\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=2,\\
\end{array}\right.\qquad

\displaystyle\langle m\rvert S^{y}_{j}\lvert n\rangle=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}-\delta_{m,\chi_{j}^{[n,1]}},\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=0,\\
\delta_{m,\chi_{j}^{[n,0]}}-\delta_{m,\chi_{j}^{[n,2]}},\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=1,\\
\delta_{m,\chi_{j}^{[n,1]}},\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=2,\\
\end{array}\right.\qquad

Here, the integer
\chi_{j}^{[n,a]}
is the number obtained from the ternary decomposition of
n
by replacing the
j
th digit by
a
. For the same reasons as for the spin-
1/2
case, we see that if
\sigma_{n}^{(j)}=0
then replacing this digit by

is equivalent to adding
q_{j}
. Similarly, if
\sigma_{n}^{(j)}=1
then replacing this digit by
0
or
2
is equivalent to subtracting
q_{j}
or adding
q_{j}
respectively. These observations can be translated in a mathematical language as

\displaystyle\chi_{j}^{[n,0]}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}n,\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=0,\\
n+q_{j},\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=1,\\
n+2q_{j},\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=2,\\
\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\chi_{j}^{[n,1]}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}n+q_{j},\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=0,\\
n,\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=1,\\
n-q_{j},\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=2,\\
\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\chi_{j}^{[n,2]}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}n+2q_{j},\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=0,\\
n-q_{j},\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=1,\\
n,\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=2,\\
\end{array}\right.

These functions are defined in the same philosophy as the
{\rm\bf flip}()
function in the spin-
1/2
case. The promised Dirac notations are constructed from Eqs.(
V
) and (
V
) considering Eqs.(
V
)-(
V
). After considerable calculations one obtains

\hat{S}_{j}^{+}&=\sqrt{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{D-1}\Gamma_{n}^{\left(j\right)+}\lvert n-q_{j}\rangle\langle n\rvert,\\
\hat{S}_{j}^{-}=\sqrt{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{D-1}\Gamma_{n}^{\left(j\right)-}\lvert n+q_{j}\rangle\langle n\rvert,\\
\hat{S}_{j}^{z}=\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{D-1}\Gamma_{n}^{\left(j\right)z}\lvert n\rangle\langle n\rvert.

(152)

Although a qutrit can exist in three distinct quantum states, we observe that apart from the
\sqrt{2}
factor Eqs.(

\Gamma_{n}^{\left(j\right)+}=\delta\left(\sigma_{n}^{(j)},1\right)+\delta\left(\sigma_{n}^{(j)},2\right),\\
\Gamma_{n}^{\left(j\right)-}=\delta\left(\sigma_{n}^{(j)},0\right)+\delta\left(\sigma_{n}^{(j)},1\right),\\
\Gamma_{n}^{\left(j\right)z}=\delta\left(\sigma_{n}^{(j)},0\right)-\delta\left(\sigma_{n}^{(j)},2\right).

Here, we have adopted the notation

For all possible values of trits, it can be verified that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n}^{\left(j\right)z}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}+1,\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=0,\\

-1,\quad{\rm if}\quad\sigma^{(j)}_{n}=2.\end{array}\right.

For the same reason as in the spin-
1/2

VI
Conclusion

We have addressed the fundamental problem of constructing suitable representations for lattice spin operators (LSOs) within a fully explicit Dirac (bra–ket) framework. Due to their intrinsic
abstract algebra
Dirac representations provide a natural language for manipulating quantum operators and states. We have constructed compact many-body Dirac representations of LSOs for spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
and spin-
1
systems based on a mapping between quantum spins and classical binary/ternary digit structure. The resulting forms express LSOs through a single summation over the Hilbert space and preserve locality, linearity, and the
su(2)
algebra.
To the best of our knowledge, such explicit many-body Dirac representations have not yet been developed beyond the single-spin case.

Our results have potential for improvement not just in physics but broadly in science as they offer the possibility of getting the
j
th bit/trit of an integer of bit/trit -field length
K
without resorting to a binary/ternary decomposition. The usefulness of our representations are discussed in Physics by examining the spin-
1/2
XXZ Heisenberg model in an open boundary configuration in the presence of a magnetic field. The prototype model is analyzed both numerically and analytically. We have found that binary representation permits a faster numerical construction of the matrix Hamiltonian by avoiding costly matrix–matrix operations and reducing memory overhead. The global
U(1)
symmetry is exploited throughout reducing computational complexity due to the exponential growth of the Hilbert space with the total number of spins. Three representative regimes have been considered: (i) absence of local magnetic field
(h_{j}=0,\,J\neq 0,\,J_{z}\neq 0)
, (ii) uniform magnetic field
(h_{j}=h,\,J\neq 0,\,J_{z}=0)
, and (iii) random magnetic field
(h_{j}\in[-W,W],\,J\neq 0,\,J_{z}\neq 0)
with disorder strength
W
. In case (i), the spin-inversion symmetry in the zero-magnetization sector permit further fragmentation of the sector into two disconnected subspaces, enabling simulations up to
K=18
spins using a Mathematica implementation with Intel MKL (see
GitHub repository
). In case (ii), the associated eigenvalue problem is transformed into a recurrence relation, revealing an effective tight-binding evolution on a graph. In case (iii), the full model is discussed. Perturbative expressions for eigenenergies and eigenvectors are derived and show good agreement with exact diagonalization results.

Before wrapping up it worth underlying that, a few questions asked in this paper are left here without technical answers and for future projects. For example, while the inverse participation ratio (IPR) has been computed, a detailed analysis of localization properties remains open. The clustering of nearly degenerate energy levels in the presence of disorder and the implications of explicit expressions for
E_{q_{j}}
in the context of many-body localization (MBL) also warrant deeper study. It will be interesting in the future to inspect whether our technique applies to long-range interactions and not only nearest-neighbor interactions. It would also be interesting to investigate the possibility of achieving a Dirac representation for lattice Boson operators.

Appendix C
Transverse-field Ising model in the Dirac representation

In this Appendix, we illustrate yet another application of the Dirac representation to a
1D
Ising model with a transverse field. The exact solution to this model is well established
[
52
]
. Our goal is therefore not to provide a new solution but essentially to show how the known solution can directly be derived from a Dirac-basis Schrödinger equation.

C.1
Dirac-basis Schrödinger equation

We consider the following open
1D
transverse-field Ising chain
[
52
]

\displaystyle\hat{H}=h\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K}\hat{S}_{j}^{z}+J\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}\hat{S}_{j}^{x}\hat{S}_{j+1}^{x},

(200)

This model is particularly instructive typically because in contrast to the XXZ model discussed in the main text, the total magnetization is not conserved,

\displaystyle\left[\hat{H},\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{z}\right]\neq 0.

(201)

As a consequence, the fixed-
K_{\downarrow}
decomposition used for the XXZ chain is
no longer applicable. We cannot solve the corresponding recursive equation in each sector associated with a magnetic quantum number. Thus, the change in the block structures is a property of the symmetry of the Hamiltonian and not a limitation inherent to the Dirac representation (
14
).

The prototype Hamiltonian (
200
) nevertheless has a symmetry. Let
K_{\downarrow}
denotes the spin occupation number. In contrast to the longitudinal XXZ model,

\hat{S}_{j}^{x}\hat{S}_{j+1}^{x}=\frac{1}{4}\left(\hat{S}_{j}^{+}\hat{S}_{j+1}^{+}+\hat{S}_{j}^{+}\hat{S}_{j+1}^{-}+\hat{S}_{j}^{-}\hat{S}_{j+1}^{+}+\hat{S}_{j}^{-}\hat{S}_{j+1}^{-}\right)
. The first term annihilates two neighboring occupations whereas the last term creates them. Let
\hat{\Pi}=\left(-1\right)^{K_{\downarrow}}
be a parity operator; its action changes a state just by a sign
\hat{\Pi}\lvert q_{j_{1}}+\cdots+q_{j_{p}}\rangle=\left(-1\right)^{p}\lvert q_{j_{1}}+\cdots+q_{j_{p}}\rangle
. Thus,
\hat{\Pi}\lvert\psi_{\rm even}\rangle=+\lvert\psi_{\rm even}\rangle
,
\hat{\Pi}\lvert\psi_{\rm odd}\rangle=-\lvert\psi_{\rm odd}\rangle
and

\displaystyle\left[\hat{H},\hat{\Pi}\right]=0.

(202)

The parity of
K_{\downarrow}
is conserved meanwhile
K_{\downarrow}
itself is not. The Hilbert space can then be subdivided into even- and odd- subspaces
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\rm even}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\rm odd}
with
\mathcal{H}_{\rm even}=\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{4}\oplus\cdots
while
\mathcal{H}_{\rm odd}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{3}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{5}\oplus\cdots
where
\mathcal{H}_{p}
represents a
p
-state occupation subspace. Although
K_{\downarrow}
is not a conserved quantum number, sectors with the same parity are coupled among themselves whereas sectors of opposite parity remain completely decoupled.

We consider for our demonstration only the even subspace and show that the
2p
-spin probability amplitudes in each subspace can be organized in terms of the two-spin amplitudes. The corresponding two-spin equation takes the form of an algebraic matrix Riccati equation
[
55
]
which can subsequently
be written as a linear eigenvalue problem and solved exactly.

In order to apply our Dirac representation, no modification of the representation is required to construct the Hamiltonian. One simply has to substitute the corresponding Dirac forms of
\hat{S}_{j}^{z}
and
\hat{S}_{j}^{x}
in the model (
200
). The eigenvalue equation is transformed into a bulk recurrence equation by constructing the wave-function as in Eq.(
53
),

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}\Big[\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)++}C_{n-Q_{j}^{+}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)-+}C_{n+Q_{j}^{-}}

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt+\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)+-}C_{n-Q_{j}^{-}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)--}C_{n+Q_{j}^{+}}\Big]=(2E-\Delta_{n}^{z})C_{n}.

(203)

Here,

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)++}=\frac{J}{2}s_{n}^{\left(j\right)+}s_{n}^{\left(j+1\right)+},\quad\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)--}=\frac{J}{2}s_{n}^{\left(j\right)-}s_{n}^{\left(j+1\right)-},

(204)

and

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{zz}=2h\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K}s_{n}^{\left(j\right)z},

(205)

with
\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)+-}
and
\Delta_{n}^{\left(j\right)-+}
given in (
51
). Following the notations in the main text, we defined
Q_{j}^{+}=q_{j}+q_{j+1}
,
Q_{j}^{-}=q_{j}-q_{j+1}
. Eq. (
203
) is our central equation.

C.2
The eigenspectrum

As we restrict ourselves to the even sectors, we can write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lvert\psi_{\rm even}\rangle={}&C_{0}\lvert 0\rangle+\sumop\displaylimits_{j_{1}<j_{2}}C_{j_{1},j_{2}}\lvert q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}\rangle\\
&+\sumop\displaylimits_{j_{1}<j_{2}<j_{3}<j_{4}}C_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}}\lvert q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+q_{j_{3}}+q_{j_{4}}\rangle+\cdots,\end{split}

(206)

where for simplicity,
C_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{2p}}\equiv C_{q_{j_{1}}+\cdots+q_{j_{2p}}}
and can be interpreted as the probability amplitude to observe
2p
spins pointing in the down direction. For instance,
C_{0}
is the probability amplitude to find the system in configuration
\lvert 0\rangle
where no spin points in the down direction. Similarly,
C_{j_{1},j_{2}}
is the one to observe two spins in positions
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
pointing in the down direction. We refer to each fixed-occupation contribution to the wave-function
\lvert\psi_{\rm even}\rangle
as an occupation sector and use the occupation number
K_{\downarrow}
to distinguish between them as in the previous case.

Assuming
C_{0}\neq 0
, it is convenient to introduce

\displaystyle P_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{2p}}=\frac{C_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{2p}}}{C_{0}},\qquad P_{\varnothing}=1.

(207)

As already mentioned in the main text, it has two important properties: it is anti-symmetric i.e. its sign changes by permutation of two particle and vanish when two indices are identical. In a two-particle sector for instance
P_{j_{1},j_{2}}=-P_{j_{2},j_{1}}
and
P_{j,j}=0
.

Sector:
K_{\downarrow}=0
. This is the zero-occupation sector corresponding to
n=0
. In this sector,
\Delta_{0}^{\left(j\right)++}=\Delta_{0}^{\left(j\right)+-}=\Delta_{0}^{\left(j\right)-+}=0
, and
\Delta_{0}^{\left(j\right)--}=J/2.

Equation (
203
) readily gives

\displaystyle 2E-\Delta_{0}^{z}=\frac{J}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}P_{j,j+1}.

(208)

The energy depends only on the
neighboring
two-spin occupation amplitudes
P_{j,j+1}
. This relation plays a key role in our demonstration. It enters the Dirac-basis Schrödinger equation independently on
K_{\downarrow}
. We also show that the whole wave-function depends only on
P_{j_{a},j_{b}}
for
j_{a}<j_{b}
. Knowing the eigenspectrum of the two-spin sector fully solves our problem.

Sector:
K_{\downarrow}=2
. This is the two-particle sector where

n=q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}
with
j_{1}<j_{2}
. Our classical variables acquire the forms,

\hskip-14.22636pt\Delta^{(j)++}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}&=\frac{J}{2}\left[\left(\delta_{j_{1},j}+\delta_{j_{2},j}\right)\left(\delta_{j_{1},j+1}+\delta_{j_{2},j+1}\right)\right],\qquad\\
\Delta^{(j)--}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}=\frac{J}{2}\left[\left(1-\delta_{j_{1},j}-\delta_{j_{2},j}\right)\left(1-\delta_{j_{1},j+1}-\delta_{j_{2},j+1}\right)\right].\qquad

(209)

The terms
\Delta^{(j)+-}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}
and
\Delta^{(j)-+}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}
are given in Eq.(
63
). So, we do not need to calculate the second and third terms in Eq.(
203
). We only need to calculate the first and last terms.
The first term reads,

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}\Delta_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}^{\left(j\right)++}C_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}-q_{j}-q_{j+1}}=\frac{J}{2}\delta_{j_{1}+1,j_{2}}C_{0},

(210)

while the last term is more subtle and requires a special care. The factors in (
209
) vanish in any of the following situations
j=j_{1}
or
j=j_{2}
or
j=j_{1}-1
or
j=j_{2}-1
. Thus,

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}\Delta_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}^{\left(j\right)--}C_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+q_{j}+q_{j+1}}=\frac{J}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\notin\Omega_{2}\end{subarray}}^{K-1}C_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+q_{j}+q_{j+1}},

(211)

where
\Omega_{2}=\{j_{1},j_{2},j_{1}-1,j_{2}-1\}
. The two-occupation equation is achieved by plugging Eqs.(
210
) and (
211
) in Eq.(
203
) while considering (
208
). Dividing the resulting equation by
C_{0}
and finally taking into account the definition (
207
) we achieve,

\displaystyle\delta_{j_{2},j_{1}+1}+\left(\mathcal{L}^{(2)}P\right)_{j_{1},j_{2}}+\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\notin\Omega_{2}\end{subarray}}^{K-1}P_{j_{1},j_{2},j,j+1}=\left(\lambda_{2}+\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}P_{j,j+1}\right)P_{j_{1},j_{2}},

where

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{L}^{(2)}P\right)_{i,j}=P_{i-1,j}+P_{i+1,j}+P_{i,j-1}+P_{i,j+1},

(213)

describes the hopping term (see Eq.(
68
));

\lambda_{2}=2(\Delta_{0}^{zz}-\Delta_{2}^{zz})/J

where
\Delta_{0}^{zz}=hK
and
\Delta_{2}^{zz}\equiv\Delta_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}}^{zz}=h(K-4)
is, in our case, independent on
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
. They can be extracted from Eq.(
119
) in the main text by setting
J_{z}=0
and
h_{j}=h
(homogeneous case).

We see that Eq.(
C.2
) involves a four-spin amplitudes
P_{j_{1},j_{2},j,j+1}
confirming the pattern
0\longleftrightarrow 2\longleftrightarrow 4
predicted by the symmetry of the Hamiltonian. We can now show how they can be
organized in terms of
P_{i,j}
.

Since the transverse field in the Ising model changes the spin occupation number by a pair, the two-occupation amplitude
P_{i,j}
stands for the elementary building block of the even sector. For four-occupied sites with
j_{1}<j_{2}<j_{3}<j_{4}
, there are three distinct ways of partitioning the four sites into two non-overlapping pairs avoiding double-occupation. Anti-symmetrization of these pairings gives,

\displaystyle P_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}}=P_{j_{1},j_{2}}P_{j_{3},j_{4}}-P_{j_{1},j_{3}}P_{j_{2},j_{4}}+P_{j_{1},j_{4}}P_{j_{2},j_{3}}.

(214)

This is known in the Physics of fermionic pairing problem and in Mathematics as the Pfaffian operator
[
32
,
40
]
. For an anti-symmetric
4\times 4
matrix we have,

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\operatorname{Pf}\begin{pmatrix}0&P_{12}&P_{13}&P_{14}\\
-P_{12}&0&P_{23}&P_{24}\\
-P_{13}&-P_{23}&0&P_{34}\\
-P_{14}&-P_{24}&-P_{34}&0\end{pmatrix}=P_{12}P_{34}-P_{13}P_{24}+P_{14}P_{23}.

(215)

and can be denoted in ou case as

\displaystyle P_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}}=\operatorname{Pf}\left[P_{j_{a},j_{b}}\right]_{a,b=1}^{4}.

(216)

We now substitute Eq.(
214
) into the last term in the left hand side of
Eq.(
C.2
). For arbitrary
j
, one has

P_{j_{1},j_{2},j,j+1}=P_{j_{1},j_{2}}P_{j,j+1}-P_{j_{1},j}P_{j_{2},j+1}+P_{j_{1},j+1}P_{j_{2},j}
. This relation confirms that
P_{j_{1},j_{2},j,j+1}
automatically vanishes when any newly created pair overlaps an already occupied site; in other words when two indices are identical (hard-core condition). The restricted sum in Eq.(
C.2
) may therefore be replaced by the full sum,

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\notin\Omega_{2}\end{subarray}}^{K-1}P_{j_{1},j_{2},j,j+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{j_{1},j_{2}}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}P_{j,j+1}

(217)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}P_{j_{1},j}P_{j_{2},j+1}+\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}P_{j_{1},j+1}P_{j_{2},j}.

The first term on the right-hand side cancels exactly the term

P_{j_{1},j_{2}}\sumop\displaylimits_{j}P_{j,j+1}
appearing on the right-hand side of Eq.(
C.2
). We are left
with

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}P_{i,j}=\delta_{j,i+1}+\left(\mathcal{L}^{(2)}P\right)_{i,j}-\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{K-1}P_{i,k}P_{j,k+1}+\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{K-1}P_{i,k+1}P_{j,k}.

Equation (
C.2
) is remarkable because the four-spin amplitudes have disappeared. The same Pfaffian recursion applies successively to the
4
,
6
,
\ldots
occupation sectors.
Thus the complete even-sector hierarchy closes in terms of the two-spin amplitudes
P_{i,j}
.

The structure of Eq.(
C.2

Define the symmetric nearest-neighbor matrix
T_{i,j}=\delta_{i+1,j}+\delta_{i-1,j}

and the antisymmetric nearest-neighbor matrix
A_{i,j}=\delta_{j,i+1}-\delta_{j,i-1}
.
Since
P^{T}=-P
, the hopping terms can be written as
(TP+PT)_{i,j}=P_{i-1,j}+P_{i+1,j}+P_{i,j-1}+P_{i,j+1}
while the nonlinear contribution is

\displaystyle(PAP)_{i,j}=-\sumop\displaylimits_{k}P_{i,k}P_{j,k+1}+\sumop\displaylimits_{k}P_{i,k+1}P_{j,k}.

(219)

Equation (
C.2
) therefore becomes

\displaystyle A+TP+PT+PAP-\lambda_{2}P=0.

(220)

Introducing
B=T-c\hat{I}
where
\hat{I}
is an identity matrix and
c=\lambda_{2}/2
, we obtain the algebraic Riccati equation
[
55
]

\displaystyle A+BP+PB+PAP=0.

(221)

It is worth noting that the Riccati equation has not been introduced as an independent ansatz. It follows directly from the Dirac-basis Schrödinger equation after the Pfaffian structure of the even-sector amplitudes has been taken into account.

Let us briefly describe how Eq.(
221
) can be linearized and the energies obtained. We introduce the
2K\times 2K
symmetric block matrix

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\begin{pmatrix}-B&-A\\
A&B\end{pmatrix},

(222)

where
A^{T}=-A
and
B^{T}=B
. Let
X
and
Y
be two
K\times K
matrices satisfying

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\begin{pmatrix}X\\
Y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}X\\
Y\end{pmatrix}R,

(223)

where
R
is a
K\times K
matrix. This equation is equivalent to
-BX-AY=XR
(i) and
AX+BY=YR
(ii). If
X
is invertible, we define

\displaystyle P=YX^{-1}.

(224)

Using
Y=PX
, equation (i) readily gives
R=X^{-1}(-B-AP)X
.
Substituting this expression into equation (ii) leads precisely to Eq.(
221
) ascertaining a linearization of our algebraic matrix Riccati equation.

Let us show how this relates to the energy. To this end let us introduce the eigenvalues equation,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{x}\\
\hat{y}\end{pmatrix}=\mu\begin{pmatrix}\hat{x}\\
\hat{y}\end{pmatrix},

(225)

where
\hat{x}
and
\hat{y}
are two block vectors and
\mu
the eigenvalue of
\mathcal{M}
.
Taking the trace of the matrix
R
yields,

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}R=-{\rm Tr}B-{\rm Tr}\left(AP\right).

(226)

If we choose the block-matrix vector
\left(X\hskip 5.69046ptY\right)^{\mathcal{T}}
( where
\mathcal{T}
denotes the transpose operation) such that their columns are the
K
independent eigenvectors of
\mathcal{M}
i.e.
\hat{x}_{i}=\sumop\displaylimits_{j}X_{i,j}\lvert j\rangle
and
\hat{y}_{i}=\sumop\displaylimits_{j}Y_{i,j}\lvert j\rangle
then
\mathcal{M}
is diagonal in this basis and
R={\rm diag}\left(\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{K}\right)
i.e. a diagonal matrix with the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}
on the main diagonal. Thus,

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}R=\sumop\displaylimits_{k}\mu_{k}.

(227)

On the other hand

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}B=-\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}{\rm Tr}\hat{I}=-\frac{4hK}{J}=-\frac{4\Delta^{zz}_{0}}{J},

since
{\rm Tr}T=0
. Also,

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left(AP\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{i,j}A_{i,j}P_{j,i}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i,j}\left(\delta_{j,i+1}P_{j,i}-\delta_{j,i-1}P_{j,i}\right)

(228)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{i}P_{i+1,i}-\sumop\displaylimits_{j}P_{j,j+1}=-2\sumop\displaylimits_{j}P_{j,j+1},

since
P_{j,j+1}=-P_{j+1,j}
. Returning to (
226
) equipped with Eqs.(
227
)-(
228
) we obtain,

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{j}P_{j,j+1}=\frac{1}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{k}\mu_{k}-\frac{2\Delta^{zz}_{0}}{J}.

(229)

The desired energy is inferred from Eq.(
208
) as

\displaystyle E=\frac{J}{8}\sumop\displaylimits_{k}\mu_{k}.

(230)

The energy now depends solely on the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}
. We will show that the wave-functions are fully determined by the eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{M}
. The remaining task is to solve Eq.(
225
). We then introduce the auxiliary vectors
\hat{r}=\hat{x}+\hat{y}
and
\hat{s}=\hat{x}-\hat{y}
.
The two block equations immediately give

\mu\hat{r}=(A-B)\hat{s}
and
\mu\hat{s}=-(A+B)\hat{r}
.
Eliminating
\hat{s}
in the equation for
\hat{r}
yields
\mu^{2}\hat{r}=-(A-B)(A+B)\hat{r}=\left(B^{2}-A^{2}+[A,B]\right)\hat{r}
. In the bulk
\left(1<j<K\right)
,
[A,B]=0
. Indeed, the operators
A
and
T
can be rewritten as
A=R-L
and
T=R+L
where
L
and
R
are two shift operators defined such that
\left(Lv\right)_{j}=v_{j-1}
and
\left(Rv\right)_{j}=v_{j+1}
. In the bulk,
RL=LR=\hat{I}
. Since
B=T-c\hat{I}
, one has
{A}B-B{A}=[A,B]=2RL-2LR=0
. This reduces the equation for
\hat{r}
to a nearest-neighbor difference equation,

\displaystyle\mu^{2}\hat{r}_{j}=(c^{2}+4)\hat{r}_{j}-2c(\hat{r}_{j-1}+\hat{r}_{j+1}),

(231)

with boundary conditions
\hat{r}_{K+1}=0
and
\hat{r}_{0}=\lambda\hat{r}_{1}
with
\lambda=2/c=J/2h
. The first boundary condition allows to choose a solution
\hat{r}_{j}=F\sin\left[\left(K+1-j\right)k\right]
(where
F
is a normalization constant) leading to
[
41
,
52
]
,

\displaystyle\mu^{2}_{k}=c^{2}+4-4c\cos k.

(232)

After expressing
c
and
\mu
in terms of the conventional dimensionless parameters in the Ising model, Eq.(
231
) reproduces the standard transverse-field Ising dispersion relation
[
41
,
52
]
. The sign difference of the cosine resulting from the convention used in the Hamiltonian in Eq.(
200
) can equivalently be removed by shifting the momentum as
k\rightarrow\pi-k
.

The second boundary equations determine the allowed momenta. One recovers the standard open-chain quantization condition
[
41
,
52
]

\displaystyle\sin[(K+1)k]=\lambda\sin(Kk).

(233)

Sector:
K_{\downarrow}=2p
. The generalization to an arbitrary even sector is straightforward thanks to the Pfaffians and by using the very same arguments as for
K_{\downarrow}=2
. No additional ingredients or arguments are necessary. Setting
n=\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell=1}^{2p}q_{j_{\ell}}
our central equation (
203
) is transformed with

\displaystyle\Delta^{(j)++}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K_{\downarrow}}}}=\frac{J}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell=1}^{K_{\downarrow}}\sumop\displaylimits_{k\neq\ell}^{K_{\downarrow}}\delta_{j_{\ell},j}\delta_{j_{k},j_{\ell}+1},\quad

(234)

\displaystyle\Delta^{(j)--}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K_{\downarrow}}}}=\frac{J}{2}\left(1-\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell=1}^{K_{\downarrow}}\delta_{j_{\ell},j}\right)\left(1-\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell=1}^{K_{\downarrow}}\delta_{j_{\ell},j+1}\right),\quad

(235)

and

\displaystyle\Delta^{zz}_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K_{\downarrow}}}}=2h\left(\frac{K}{2}-K_{\downarrow}\right).

(236)

So far, the origin of the Pfaffian structure is understood directly from the occupation representation. For
2p
occupied sites the same construction consists of summing over all
(2p-1)!!
perfect pairings with the sign associated with the corresponding permutation. Let

\displaystyle I=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{2p}),\qquad j_{1}<\cdots<j_{2p},

(237)

and assume that the even-sector amplitudes are written as

\displaystyle P_{I}=\operatorname{Pf}\left[P_{j_{a},j_{b}}\right]_{a,b=1}^{2p},

(238)

We show that the complete hierarchy of the
2p
-spin probability amplitudes in the even-sector Schrödinger equations reduces to the equation for the two-spin amplitudes. The standard Pfaffian expansion implies the identity

\displaystyle pP_{I}=\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq a<b\leq 2p}(-1)^{a+b+1}P_{j_{a}j_{b}}P_{I\setminus\{j_{a},j_{b}\}}.

(239)

Here, the notation
P_{I\setminus\{j_{a},j_{b}\}}
should be understood that the indices
j_{a}
and
j_{b}
are removed from the list
I
. The pair-annihilation contribution in Eq. (
203
) can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{I}=\frac{2}{J}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}\Delta_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K_{\downarrow}}}}^{\left(j\right)++}\frac{C_{q_{j_{1}}+q_{j_{2}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K_{\downarrow}}}-q_{j}-q_{j+1}}}{C_{0}}.

(240)

Considering Eq.(
234
), we achieve

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{I}=\sumop\displaylimits_{a<b}(-1)^{a+b+1}A_{j_{a},j_{b}}P_{I\setminus\{j_{a},j_{b}\}},

(241)

Similarly, the hopping contribution satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(2p)}P_{I}=\sumop\displaylimits_{a<b}(-1)^{a+b+1}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(2)}P\right)_{j_{a}j_{b}}P_{I\setminus\{j_{a},j_{b}\}},

(242)

with
\left(\mathcal{L}^{(2)}P\right)_{i,j}
given in E.(
213
)
Terms leading to coincident indices vanish automatically because
of the antisymmetry of the Pfaffian.

Finally, expansion of the Pfaffian after creation of a pair on the
bond
(k,k+1)
yields the last term

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{I}=\frac{2}{J}\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{K-1}\Delta_{q_{j_{1}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K_{\downarrow}}}}^{\left(j\right)--}\frac{C_{q_{j_{1}}+\cdots+q_{j_{K_{\downarrow}}}+q_{j}+q_{j+1}}}{C_{0}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\notin\Omega_{2p}\end{subarray}}^{K-1}P_{I,j,j+1}.

where
\Omega_{2p}=\{j_{a},j_{a}-1\}_{a=1}^{2p}
and

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{k}P_{I,k,k+1}=P_{I}\sumop\displaylimits_{k}P_{k,k+1}+\sumop\displaylimits_{a<b}(-1)^{a+b+1}(PAP)_{j_{a},j_{b}}P_{I\setminus\{j_{a},j_{b}\}}

with
(PAP)_{i,j}
given in Eq.(
219
). If the newly created pair overlaps an already occupied site, the corresponding Pfaffian vanishes. The restricted sum over free bonds can therefore be replaced by the full sum in
Eq.(
C.2
).

Combining Eqs.(
240
), (
242
), and (
C.2
), and using the zero-occupation equation (

), the Schrödinger equation in the
2p
-occupation sector acquires the form
\mathcal{A}_{I}+\mathcal{L}^{\left(2p\right)}P+\mathcal{B}_{I}=\left(2/J\right)\left(2E-\Delta^{zz}_{2p}\right)P_{I}
that is

\displaystyle\lambda_{2p}P_{I}=\sumop\displaylimits_{a<b}(-1)^{a+b+1}\left[A+\mathcal{L}^{(2)}P+PAP\right]_{j_{a},j_{b}}P_{I\setminus\{j_{a},j_{b}\}}.\qquad

(245)

If the two-spin amplitudes satisfy
A+\mathcal{L}^{(2)}P+PAP=\lambda_{2}P
(see Eq.(

)) Eq.(

) becomes

\displaystyle\lambda_{2p}P_{I}=\lambda_{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{a<b}(-1)^{a+b+1}P_{j_{a},j_{b}}P_{I\setminus\{j_{a},j_{b}\}}.

(246)

Using the Pfaffian identity (
239
), we finally obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{2p}=p\lambda_{2}.

(247)

This is precisely consistent with

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}^{z}-\Delta_{2p}^{z}=4hp,

(248)

which independently gives

\displaystyle\lambda_{2p}=\frac{2(\Delta_{0}^{z}-\Delta_{2p}^{z})}{J}=p\lambda_{2}.

(249)

Hence, once the antisymmetric two-spin matrix
P
satisfies Eq.(

), the Pfaffian amplitudes Eq.(

) satisfy all even-sector Schrödinger
equations and this fully determines the wave-function (

).

The last question is how to determine
X
and
Y
in order to have

at hand. Indeed,
\hat{x}
and
\hat{y}
are the columns of
X
and

respectively. We have established that
\hat{r}_{j}(k)=\sin\left[\left(K+1-j\right)k\right]
whereby
\hat{s}_{j}(k)=\left(c\sin\left[\left(K+1-j\right)k\right]-2\sin\left[\left(K-j\right)k\right]\right)/\mu\left(k\right)
. The components of the eigenfunctions are

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{j}\left(k\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(1+\frac{c}{\mu(k)}\right)\sin\left[\left(K+1-j\right)k\right]-\frac{2}{\mu(k)}\sin\left[\left(K-j\right)k\right]\right].

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{j}\left(k\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(1-\frac{c}{\mu(k)}\right)\sin\left[\left(K+1-j\right)k\right]+\frac{2}{\mu(k)}\sin\left[\left(K-j\right)k\right]\right].

P=Y.X^{-1}
can now be completely constructed. Let
k_{1},\cdots k_{K}
be the
K
allowed solutions of Eq.(

). For each

we choose a branch
\mu_{\ell}=\pm\sqrt{c^{2}+4-4c\cos\left(k_{\ell}\right)}
yielding the elements

and
Y_{j,\ell}=\hat{y}_{j}\left(k_{\ell}\right)
. Note that the allowed momenta may include complex solutions, for which the expressions above are understood by analytic continuation. The branch should also be chosen such that
X
is invertible. The normalization of each eigenvector is not important. Multiplying the column by a non-zero constant does not affect

. Therefore
C_{I}=C_{0}\operatorname{Pf}\left(P_{I}\right)
. A Mathematica program that verifies the calculations in this Section is added to our
GitHub repository
.

Appendix D
Perturbation theory

No binary theory is used in this Appendix. According to the traditional perturbation theory, the eigenvalues and eigenstates of a partitioned Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\hat{\Lambda}+\lambda\hat{\Delta}
with
\hat{\Lambda}
a diagonal matrix in the original Hilbert space and
\hat{\Delta}
an off-diagonal matrix obeying
\hat{H}\lvert\psi_{n}\rangle=E_{n}\lvert\psi_{n}\rangle
can be constructed as (see Ref.
[
34
]
)

\displaystyle E_{n}=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{\mathcal{K}}\lambda^{k}\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\left(k\right)}=\epsilon_{n}+\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{\mathcal{K}}\lambda^{k}\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\left(k\right)},

(252)

and

\displaystyle\lvert\psi_{n}\rangle=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{\mathcal{K}}\lambda^{k}\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(k\right)}\rangle=\lvert n\rangle+\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{\mathcal{K}}\lambda^{k}\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(k\right)}\rangle,

(253)

where
\hat{\Lambda}\lvert n\rangle=\epsilon_{n}\lvert n\rangle
with
\mathcal{K}
the order of the correction. We assume a non-degenerate for
\hat{\Lambda}
i.e.
\epsilon_{n}\neq\epsilon_{m}
for
n\neq m
. Here,
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\left(k\right)}
and
\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(k\right)}\rangle
are respectively the
k
th order corrections to the energy and wave function. Plugging Eqs.(
252
) and (
253
) into the eigenvalue equation,

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{\mathcal{K}}\lambda^{k}\left(\hat{\Lambda}+\lambda\hat{\Delta}\right)\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(k\right)}\rangle=\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{\mathcal{K}}\lambda^{k}\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\left(k\right)}\right)\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{\mathcal{K}}\lambda^{k}\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(k\right)}\rangle\right).

(254)

The product of power series in the above equation is reduced to a single power series with aid of the Cauchy product formula. Comparing the left and right hand sides of the resulting equation leads for
k=0
to
\hat{\Lambda}\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(0\right)}\rangle=\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\left(0\right)}\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(0\right)}\rangle
and,

\displaystyle\left(\hat{\Lambda}-\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\left(0\right)}\hat{I}\right)\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(k\right)}\rangle=\sumop\displaylimits_{q=1}^{k}\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\left(q\right)}\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(k-q\right)}\rangle-\hat{\Delta}\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(k-1\right)}\rangle,\quad k\geq 1.\qquad

(255)

We immediately verify from here that
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\left(0\right)}=\epsilon_{n}
and
\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(0\right)}\rangle=\lvert n\rangle
. Eq.(
255
) is further transformed by expanding the correction
\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(k\right)}\rangle
into the eigenbasis of
\hat{\Lambda}
as

\displaystyle\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(k\right)}\rangle=\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{D}A_{j,n}^{\left(k\right)}\lvert j\rangle.

(256)

We observe that the coefficients
A_{j,n}^{\left(k\right)}
of the expansion fully determine the wave function. We show below that they also determine the eigenenergies. Before coming to their evaluation, let us establish a few properties. We note from
\lvert\psi_{n}^{\left(0\right)}\rangle=\lvert n\rangle
that
A_{j,n}^{\left(0\right)}=\delta_{j,n}
(which we label as condition (i)). For normalized wave functions, the requirement
\langle\psi_{n}|\psi_{n}\rangle=1
also reads
\sumop\displaylimits_{k,k^{\prime}=0}^{\mathcal{K}}\lambda^{k+k^{\prime}}\langle\psi_{n}^{\left(k\right)}|\psi_{n}^{\left(k^{\prime}\right)}\rangle=1
. However, this requirement is met for
k=k^{\prime}=0
, therefore,
\sumop\displaylimits_{k+k^{\prime}\geq 1}^{\mathcal{K}}\lambda^{k+k^{\prime}}\langle\psi_{n}^{\left(k\right)}|\psi_{n}^{\left(k^{\prime}\right)}\rangle=0
. Given that the scalar products
\langle\psi_{n}^{\left(k\right)}|\psi_{n}^{\left(k^{\prime}\right)}\rangle
are defined to all be positive, we infer that
\langle\psi_{n}^{\left(k\right)}|\psi_{n}^{\left(k^{\prime}\right)}\rangle=0
for
k+k^{\prime}\geq 1
. Eq.(
256
) teaches us that
\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{D}A_{j,n}^{\left(k^{\prime}\right)}A_{j,n}^{\left(k\right)}=0
for
k+k^{\prime}\geq 1
. By setting
k^{\prime}=0
in the previous relation, we obtain
\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{D}\delta_{j,n}A_{j,n}^{\left(k\right)}=0
i.e.
A_{n,n}^{\left(k\right)}=0
(condition (ii)) which we combine with condition (i) to obtain the important relation
A_{n,n}^{\left(k\right)}=\delta_{k,0}
(condition (iii)).

We can now construct the equation for the coefficients
A_{j,n}^{\left(k\right)}
. This is done by substituting (
256
) into (
255
) and sandwiching the resulting equation from the left with
\langle i\rvert
. This recipe leads us to

\displaystyle A_{i,n}^{\left(k\right)}\left(\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\left(0\right)}-\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\left(0\right)}\right)=\sumop\displaylimits_{q=1}^{k}\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\left(q\right)}A_{i,n}^{\left(k-q\right)}-\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{D}A_{j,n}^{\left(k-1\right)}\hat{\Delta}_{i,j},

(257)

where
\hat{\Delta}_{i,j}=\langle i\rvert\hat{\Delta}\lvert j\rangle
. As mentioned earlier, the coefficients
A_{j,n}^{\left(k\right)}
in this equation fully determine the eigenvalues as well. Indeed, setting
i=n
and considering condition (iii) elaborated above readily yields,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\left(k\right)}=\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{D}A_{j,n}^{\left(k-1\right)}\hat{\Delta}_{n,j}=\left(\hat{\Delta}A^{\left(k-1\right)}\right)_{n,n}.

(258)

Plugging this back into (
257
) followed by a few manipulations and finally changing
i\to j
leads us to

\displaystyle A_{j,n}^{\left(k\right)}=\vartheta_{j,n}\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{q=1}^{k}\left(\hat{\Delta}A^{\left(q-1\right)}\right)_{n,n}A_{j,n}^{\left(k-q\right)}-\left(\hat{\Delta}A^{\left(k-1\right)}_{j,n}\right)\right),

(259)

where we have defined the matrix
\vartheta
with matrix elements

\displaystyle\vartheta_{j,n}=\left(\mathcal{E}_{j}^{\left(0\right)}-\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\left(0\right)}\right)^{-1},\quad\vartheta_{j,j}=0.

(260)

Let us denote as
A^{\left(k\right)}
a matrix whose entries are the desired coefficients. Further inspection of Eq.(
259
) reveals that the former obeys the fixed-point matrix equation,

\displaystyle A^{\left(k\right)}=\vartheta\circ\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{q=1}^{k}A^{\left(k-q\right)}\hat{\mathcal{D}}\left(\hat{\Delta}A^{\left(q-1\right)}\right)-\left(\hat{\Delta}A^{\left(k-1\right)}\right)\right).

(261)

Here, the symbol
\circ
denotes the elements-wise product operator (Hadamard product
[
20
]
). We have defined the operator
\hat{\mathcal{D}}\left(X\right)
which project onto the diagonal part of
X
i.e. the
(\ell,n)
element resulting from its action on a matrix
X
reads
\hat{\mathcal{D}}\left(X\right)_{\ell,n}=\delta_{\ell,n}X_{n,n}
. The eigenvalues and eigenstates are obtained from Eq.(
261
) as

\displaystyle E_{n}=\epsilon_{n}+\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{D}\hat{\Delta}_{n,j}\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{\mathcal{K}}\lambda^{k}A_{j,n}^{\left(k\right)}\right)=\epsilon_{n}+\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{\mathcal{K}}\lambda^{k}\left(\hat{\Delta}A^{\left(k-1\right)}\right)_{n,n},

and

\displaystyle\lvert\psi_{n}\rangle=\lvert n\rangle+\sumop\displaylimits_{j=1}^{D}\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{\mathcal{K}}\lambda^{k}A_{j,n}^{\left(k\right)}\right)\lvert j\rangle.

(263)

For example,

\displaystyle A^{\left(1\right)}=-\left(\vartheta\circ\hat{\Delta}\right),

(264)

\displaystyle A^{\left(2\right)}=-\vartheta\circ\left(\hat{\mathcal{D}}\left(\hat{\Delta}\left(\vartheta\circ\hat{\Delta}\right)\right)-\hat{\Delta}\left(\vartheta\circ\hat{\Delta}\right)\right).

(265)

These formulae are used to build the perturbation theory for random magnetic fields in the main text.
