Exceptionally large winding number of a finite-size topological superconductor Preprint: APS/123-QED
Satoshi Ikegaya, Shingo Kobayashi, Yasuhiro Asano

Abstract
We study finite-size-induced topological phenomena in unconventional superconductors. Specifically, we focus on a thin film with a persistent spin texture, fabricated on a high- T c T_{\text{c}} cuprate d x ​ y d_{xy} -wave superconductors. In two-dimensional d x ​ y d_{xy} -wave superconductors, flat-band Andreev bound states appear at the edges. As the system narrows, these bound states acquire an energy gap due to finite-size hybridization and spin-orbit coupling of the persistent spin texture. This induced gap gives rise to the emergence of a topological phase, characterized by an exceptionally large one-dimensional winding number that scales with the film width. We demonstrate the appearance of highly degenerate zero-energy states, leading to anomalous perfect charge transport in dirty superconducting junctions. These findings provide a promising platform for exploring fascinating topological superconducting phases driven by gapped Andreev bound states.



Introduction and outline.

Topological phases of matter, characterized by nontrivial topological invariants associated with gapped band structures, have been a central focus of condensed matter physics.
A hallmark of these phases, as dictated by the bulk-boundary correspondence, is the emergence of exotic boundary states.

Emergence of zero-energy states.

We describe the present system using a 2D tight-binding model on a square lattice.
A lattice site is indicated by a vector
\bm{r}=j\bm{x}+m\bm{y}
, where
|\bm{x}|=|\bm{y}|=a
.
We apply a periodic boundary condition in the
x
direction, while an open boundary condition is applied in the
y
direction;
the thin film is placed on
1\leq m\leq M
with
W=Ma
representing the width of the system.
The Bogoliubov–de Gennes (BdG) Hamiltonian reads,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&H=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k_{x}}C^{\dagger}_{k_{x}}H_{k_{x}}C_{k_{x}},\\
&C^{\dagger}_{k_{x}}=[c^{\dagger}_{k_{x},\uparrow},c^{\dagger}_{k_{x},\downarrow},c^{\mathrm{T}}_{-k_{x},\uparrow},c^{\mathrm{T}}_{-k_{x},\downarrow}],\\
&c_{k_{x},s}=[c_{k_{x},1,s},c_{k_{x},2,s},\cdots,c_{k_{x},M,s}]^{\mathrm{T}},\\
&H_{k_{x}}=\xi_{k_{x}}\otimes\tau_{z}+\Lambda\otimes s_{z}-\Delta_{k_{x}}\otimes s_{y}\otimes\tau_{y},\\
&\xi_{k_{x}}=-2t\cos(k_x a)-\mu+4t-tA_{+},\\
&\Lambda=\frac{i\lambda}{2}A_{-},\quad\Delta_{k_{x}}=\frac{i\Delta}{2}\sin(k_x a)A_{-},\end{split}

(1)

where
A_{\pm}
is the
(M\times M)
matrices with

\displaystyle(A_{\pm})_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1&\text{for }i=j+1\\
\pm 1&\text{for }i=j-1\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.,

c_{k_{x},m,s}
is an annihilation operator of an electron at
y=ma
with momentum
k_{x}
and spin
s
(
=\uparrow,\downarrow
),

t
denotes the nearest-neighbor hopping integral,

\mu
is the chemical potential,

\lambda
represents the strength of the unidirectional SOC forming the PST,
and
\Delta
denotes the proximity-induced spin-singlet
d_{xy}
-wave pair potential.

\tau_{\nu}
and
s_{\nu}
for
\nu=x,y,z
denote the Pauli matrices in Nambu and spin spaces, respectively.
When we apply a periodic boundary condition in both
x
and
y
directions, the BdG Hamiltonian is rewritten as,

H_{\bm{k}}=\xi_{\bm{k}}\tau_{z}+\lambda\sin(k_y a)s_{z}-\Delta_{\bm{k}}s_{y}\otimes\tau_{y}

with
\xi_{\bm{k}}=-2t\cos(k_x a)-2t\cos(k_y a)-\mu+4t
and
\Delta_{\bm{k}}=\Delta\sin(k_x a)\sin(k_y a)
.
The PST inherently guarantees spin-rotational symmetry

;
kohda_17
:

\displaystyle\left[R_{z},H_{k_{x}}\right]=0,\quad R_{z}=is_{z}\otimes\tau_{z},

(5)

where
R_{z}
describes the spin rotation along the
z
axis.
Therefore, we can decompose the Hamiltonian into two subsectors, each corresponding to a different eigenvalue of
R_{z}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&H=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k_{x},s}C^{\dagger}_{k_{x},s}H_{k_{x},s}C_{k_{x},s},\\
&C^{\dagger}_{k_{x},s}=[c^{\dagger}_{k_{x},s},c^{\mathrm{T}}_{-k_{x},\bar{s}}],\\
&H_{k_{x},s}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\xi_{k_{x}}+\sigma_{s}\Lambda&\sigma_{s}\Delta_{k_{x}}\\
\sigma_{s}\Delta_{k_{x}}&-\xi_{k_{x}}-\sigma_{s}\Lambda\end{array}\right],\end{split}

(6)

where
\bar{s}
represents the opposite spin of
s
, and
\sigma_{s}=+1(-1)
for
s=\uparrow(\downarrow)
.
In the
absence
of the SOC (i.e.,
\lambda=0

belongs to class CI of the AZ classification:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&TH_{k_{x},s}T^{-1}=H_{-k_{x},s},\quad T=\mathcal{K},\\
&CH_{k_{x},s}C^{-1}=-H_{-k_{x},s},\quad C=\tau_{y}\mathcal{K},\\
&\Gamma H_{k_{x},s}\Gamma^{-1}=-H_{k_{x},s},\quad\Gamma=\tau_{y},\end{split}

(7)

where

denotes the complex conjugation operator, and the symmetry operators satisfy

,

, and
\Gamma^{2}=1
.
However, in the

of the SOC (i.e.,
\lambda\neq 0
), the Hamiltonian falls into class AIII, preserving only the chiral symmetry
\Gamma
.
According to the AZ classification
schnyder_08
, a
fully gapped
energy spectrum of

allows us to define the 1D winding number:

\displaystyle N_{\text{1D}}=\frac{i}{4\pi}\sum_{s=\uparrow,\downarrow}\int dk_{x}\mathrm{Tr}[\Gamma H_{k_{x},s}^{-1}\partial_{k_{x}}H_{k_{x},s}]\in\mathbb{Z},

(8)

where the trace is taken over both the Nambu space and the lattice sites along the
y

The unidirectional SOC is essential for defining the 1D winding number

In this case, the Q1D systems are in class DIII, and the AZ classification changes from

Effective theory.

To obtain an intuitive understanding of the numerical results, we derive a low-energy effective Hamiltonian.
For this purpose, we consider the BdG equation in the continuum limit:

\displaystyle H_{s}(k_{x},y)\psi_{s}(k_{x},y)=E\psi_{s}(k_{x},y),

(9)

with

\displaystyle\begin{split}&H_{s}(k_{x},y)=H^{d_{xy}}_{s}(k_{x},y)+H^{\lambda}_{s}(y),\\
&H^{d_{xy}}_{s}(k_{x},y)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\xi(k_{x},y)&\sigma_{s}\Delta(k_{x},y)\\
\sigma_{s}\Delta(k_{x},y)&-\xi(k_{x},y)\end{array}\right],\\
&H^{\lambda}_{s}(y)=\sigma_{s}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Lambda(y)&0\\
0&-\Lambda(y)\end{array}\right],\\
&\xi(k_{x},y)=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\partial_{y}^{2}-\tilde{\mu},\quad\tilde{\mu}=\mu-\frac{\hbar^{2}k_{x}^{2}}{2m},\\
&\Delta(k_{x},y)=-i\frac{\tilde{\Delta}}{k_{F}}\partial_{y},\quad\tilde{\Delta}=\frac{\Delta k_{x}}{k_{F}},\quad\Lambda(y)=-i\tilde{\lambda}\partial_{y},\end{split}

(10)

where
ta^{2}=\hbar^{2}/(2m)
,
\lambda a=\tilde{\lambda}
, and
k_{F}=\sqrt{2m\mu}/\hbar
denotes the Fermi wave number.
We first explore the wave functions of the ABSs in the absence of the SOC, satisfying:

\displaystyle H^{d_{xy}}_{s}(k_{x},y)\psi^{\pm}_{s}(k_{x},y)=\pm\epsilon_{s}(k_{x})\psi^{\pm}_{s}(k_{x},y),

(11)

where
\pm\epsilon_{s}(k_{x})
represents the energy eigenvalues of the ABSs, as also illustrated in Fig.
3
(a).
While obtaining
\epsilon_{s}(k_{x})
and
\psi^{\pm}_{s}(k_{x},y)
across the entire range of
k_{x}
is challenging, as detailed in the SM
sm
,
we can analytically solve the BdG equation at momenta where zero-energy band crossings occur:
H^{d_{xy}}_{s}(k_{n},y)\psi^{\pm}_{s}(k_{n},y)=0
.
Specifically, the zero-energy band crossings occur at momenta:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&k_{n}=\mathrm{sgn}[n]\sqrt{\frac{k_{F}^{2}-q_{n}^{2}}{1+\left(\frac{\Delta}{2\mu}\right)^{2}}},\\
&n=\pm 1,\pm 2,\cdots,\pm n_{c},\end{split}

(12)

with the corresponding zero-energy wave functions:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\psi^{\pm}_{s}(k_{n},y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}e^{\mp i\frac{\pi}{4}}\varphi^{\pm}_{s}(k_{n},y)&\text{for }|n|\in\text{odd}\\
e^{\mp i\frac{\pi}{4}}\varphi^{\mp}_{s}(k_{n},y)&\text{for }|n|\in\text{even}\end{array}\right.,\\
&\varphi^{\pm}_{s}(k_{n},y)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\phi_{s,+}(k_{n},y)\pm\phi_{s,-}(k_{n},y)\\
i\left\{\phi_{s,+}(k_{n},y)\mp\phi_{s,-}(k_{n},y)\right\}\end{array}\right],\\
&\phi_{s,\pm}(k_{n},y)=c_{k_{n},s,\pm}\sin(q_n y)e^{\pm s\kappa_{n}y},\\
&q_{n}=\frac{n\pi}{W},\quad\kappa_{n}=\frac{\Delta}{2\mu}k_{n},\quad c_{s,\pm,n}=\sqrt{\frac{2X_{n}}{W}e^{\mp s\kappa_{n}W}}\\
&X_{n}=\frac{\kappa_{n}W}{\sinh(\kappa_n W)}\left\{1+\left(\frac{\Delta}{2\mu}\right)^{2}\frac{k_{n}^{2}}{q_{n}^{2}}\right\},\end{split}

(13)

where
n_{c}
in the continuum limit is given by
n_{c}=[Wk_{F}/\pi]_{G}
with
[\cdots]_{G}
representing the Gauss symbol, which takes the integer part of the argument.
A detailed derivation for Eq (
13
) is provided in the SM.
Notably, the total number of zero-energy band crossings coincides with the number of propagating channels,
N_{c}=2n_{c}
[see Fig.
3
(a)].
To proceed with our analysis, we treat the SOC,
H^{\lambda}_{s}(y)
, as a perturbation and construct the low-energy effective Hamiltonian as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{s}(k_{x})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A_{++}&A_{+-}\\
A_{-+}&A_{--}\end{array}\right]+O(\lambda^{2}),\\
&A_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}=\int dy\left\{\psi^{\eta}_{s}(k_{x},y)\right\}^{\dagger}H_{s}(k_{x},y)\psi^{\eta^{\prime}}_{s}(k_{x},y),\end{split}

(14)

where
A_{\eta\eta}=\eta\epsilon_{s}(k_{x})
.
In the vicinity of
k_{x}=k_{n}
, we obtain,
H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{s}(k_{n}+\delta k)=H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n,s}(\delta k)+O(\delta k^{2})
, with

\displaystyle\begin{split}&H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n,s}(\delta k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}v_{n}\delta k&m_{n}\\
m_{n}&-v_{n}\delta k\end{array}\right]&\text{for }|n|\in\text{odd}\\
&\\
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}-v_{n}\delta k&-m_{n}\\
-m_{n}&v_{n}\delta k\end{array}\right]&\text{for }|n|\in\text{even}\end{array}\right.,\\
&v_{n}=\frac{\hbar^{2}k_{n}}{m}\left\{1+\left(\frac{\Delta}{2\mu}\right)^{2}\right\}X_{n},\quad m_{n}=\tilde{\lambda}\kappa_{n}X_{n},\end{split}

(15)

where
m_{n}
represents the mass term that opens the energy gap in the ABS spectrum, as depicted in Fig.
3
(b).
A detailed derivation for Eq. (
15
) is provided in the SM.
The low-energy effective Hamiltonian preserves chiral symmetry:

\displaystyle\gamma H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{s}(k_{x})\gamma^{-1}=-H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{s}(k_{x}),\quad\gamma=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-i\\
i&0\end{array}\right],

which allows us to define the winding number:

\displaystyle N_{\text{1D}}^{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{i}{4\pi}\sum_{s}\int dk_{x}\mathrm{Tr}[\gamma\{H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{s}(k_{x})\}^{-1}\partial_{k_{x}}H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{s}(k_{x})].

(18)

On the basis of Ref.
sato_11
, we can compute
N_{\text{1D}}^{\mathrm{eff}}
using only the Hamiltonian in the vicinity of
k_{x}=k_{n}
:

\displaystyle N_{\text{1D}}^{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s}\sum_{n=-n_{c}}^{n_{c}}\mathrm{sgn}[v_{n}]\mathrm{sgn}[m_{n}],

(19)

where the detailed derivation of Eq. (
19
) is provided in the SM.
Applying this formula, we eventually obtain

\displaystyle N_{\text{1D}}^{\mathrm{eff}}=N_{c},

(20)

which shows excellent agreement with the numerical result shown in Fig.
2
(a).
Consequently, our effective theory confirms that the nontrivial topology of the present systems indeed originates from the energy gap of the ABSs.

\lambda=0.5t
(
\lambda=0
).
For the finite-size-induced topological phase, (i.e.,
\lambda=0.5t
), we observe zero-bias conductance quantization at
(2e^{2}/h)N_{\text{1D}}
, independent of
L_{D}
.

Finally, we describe anomalous charge transport induced by the
N_{\text{1D}}
-fold degenerate ZESs.
We consider a junction consisting of three segments:
a ballistic NM segment for
-\infty\leq j<1
, a dirty NM segment for
1\leq j\leq L_{D}/a
, and a SC segment for
L_{D}/a<j\leq\infty
.
For numerical calculations, the BdG Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) is reformulated in a real-space basis by performing a Fourier transformation along the
x
direction:

c_{k_{x},m,s}\rightarrow c_{\bm{r},s}
.
The proximity-induced pair potential is assumed to exist only in the SC segment, i.e.,
j>L_{D}/a
.
For the dirty NM segment, we introduce a non-magnetic random on-site potential:

\displaystyle H_{D}=\sum_{j=1}^{L_{D}/a}\sum_{m,s}v_{\bm{r}}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{r},s}c_{\bm{r},s},

(21)

where
v_{\bm{r}}
is given randomly in the range
-v_{\mathrm{imp}}/2\leq v_{\bm{r}}\leq v_{\mathrm{imp}}/2
.
We calculate the differential conductance at zero temperature on the basis of the Blonder–Tinkham–Klapwijk formalism
klapwijk_82
:

\displaystyle G(eV)=\frac{e^{2}}{h}\sum_{\zeta,\eta}\left[\delta_{\zeta,\eta}-\left|r^{ee}_{\zeta,\eta}\right|^{2}+\left|r^{he}_{\zeta,\eta}\right|^{2}\right]_{E=eV},

(22)

where
r^{ee}_{\zeta,\eta}
and
r^{he}_{\zeta,\eta}
denote a normal and an Andreev reflection coefficient at energy
E
, respectively.
The indices
\zeta
and
\eta
correspond to the outgoing and incoming channels.
These reflection coefficients are calculated using recursive Green’s function techniques
fisher_81
;
ando_91
.
For numerical calculations, the parameters are fixed as
\mu=t
,
\Delta=0.2t
,
v_{\mathrm{imp}}=0.5t
, and
W=30a
.
The conductance is averaged over
5\times 10^{3}
samples with different random potential configurations.

In Fig.
5
(a), we plot the differential conductance at zero bias as a function of the length of the dirty NM segment.
The colored (white) dots correspond to
\lambda=0.5t
(
\lambda=0
).
In the absence of SOC, the zero-bias conductance decreases monotonically with increasing
L_{D}
.
In contrast, for
\lambda=0.5t
, the zero-bias conductance remains perfectly quantized, independent of
L_{D}
:

\displaystyle G(0)=\frac{2e^{2}}{h}\times N_{\text{1D}}=\frac{2e^{2}}{h}\times N_{c},

(23)

where
N_{\text{1D}}=20
for the given parameters.
Moreover, the conductance spectrum for
\lambda=0.5t
exhibits a prominent zero-bias peak, as shown in Fig.
5
(b).
This perfect conductance quantization suggests that the
N_{\text{1D}}
-fold degenerate ZESs, initially localized at the junction interface,
penetrate into the dirty NM segment while retaining their high degree of degeneracy.
Therefore, they form perfect transmission channels within the dirty NM-SC junction
ikegaya_15
;
ikegaya_16(1)
.
This unusual superconducting proximity effect, accompanied by the penetration of topologically protected ZESs, is analogous to the anomalous proximity effect observed in 2D nodal
p_{x}
-wave SCs
tanaka_04
;
tanaka_05(1)
.
As a result, we propose a striking perfect charge transport that clearly manifests the presence of highly degenerate ZESs.

Summary.

In summary, we investigate a novel topological superconducting phase emerging in a Q1D system, where a unidirectional SOC potential of a PST coexists with a
d_{xy}
-wave pair potential.
Finite-size effects lead to hybridization of the flat-band ABSs, resulting in multiple band crossings.
Introducing the unidirectional SOC opens a full energy gap in the ABS spectrum and gives rise to an exceptionally large winding number,
N_{\text{1D}}
.
Numerical calculations demonstrate the emergence of topologically protected
N_{\text{1D}}
-fold degenerate ZESs at both ends of the Q1D system.
Furthermore, a distinctive perfect transport phenomenon serves as an unambiguous signature of these finite-size-induced topological phases.
These findings offer new avenues for studying topological superconducting phases driven by gapped ABSs.

I
Zero-energy Band crossings protected by inversion symmetry

In this section, we discuss the relationship between the zero-energy band crossings in Fig. 1(c) and inversion symmetry of the Hamiltonian.
We consider the Bogoliubov–de Gennes (BdG) Hamiltonian in Eq. (4) of the main text:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&H_{k_{x},s}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\xi_{k_{x}}+\sigma_{s}\Lambda&\sigma_{s}\Delta_{k_{x}}\\
\sigma_{s}\Delta_{k_{x}}&-\xi_{k_{x}}-\sigma_{s}\Lambda\end{array}\right],\\
&\xi_{k_{x}}=-2t\cos(k_x a)-\mu+4t-tA_{+},\\
&\Lambda=\frac{i\lambda}{2}A_{-},\quad\Delta_{k_{x}}=\frac{i\Delta}{2}\sin(k_x a)A_{-},\end{split}

(24)

where
A_{\pm}
is the
(M\times M)
matrices with

\displaystyle(A_{\pm})_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1&\text{for }i=j+1\\
\pm 1&\text{for }i=j-1\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right..

In the
absence
of the spin-orbit coupling (SOC) potential (i.e.,
\lambda=0
), the Hamiltonian
H_{k_{x},s}
preserves inversion symmetry:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&PH_{k_{x},s}P^{-1}=H_{-k_{x},s},\quad P=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{y}&0\\
0&P_{y}\end{array}\right],\\
&(P_{y})_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1&\text{for }i=M-j+1\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right..\end{split}

(28)

This Hamiltonian also preserves particle-hole-like symmetry:

\displaystyle C_{P}H_{k_{x},s}C_{P}^{-1}=-H_{-k_{x},s},\quad C_{P}=\Xi\mathcal{K},\quad\Xi=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&P_{y}\\
P_{y}&0\end{array}\right],

where
\mathcal{K}
denotes the complex conjugation operator, and the operator
C_{P}
satisfies
C_{P}^{2}=+1
.
Combining these symmetries, we find
CP
-like symmetry as:

\displaystyle U_{CP}H^{\mathrm{T}}_{k_{x},s}U_{CP}^{-1}=-H_{k_{x},s},\quad U_{CP}=\Xi P^{\ast}=\tau_{x},

(31)

where
(C_{P}P)^{2}=U_{CP}U_{CP}^{\ast}=+1
and hence
U_{CP}^{\mathrm{T}}=U_{CP}
.
Following Refs.
kobayashi_14
;
timm_17
, we define a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
invariant that characterizes the zero-energy band crossings in the spectrum of
H_{k_{x},s}
at
\lambda=0
.
Since
U_{CP}
is a symmetric matrix, it can be decomposed as
U_{CP}=VQV^{\mathrm{T}}
, where
Q
is a diagonal matrix and
V
is a unitary matrix.
We define
\Omega=\sqrt{Q}^{\dagger}V^{\dagger}
, where
\sqrt{Q}
is well-defined due to the diagonal nature of
Q
.
Using
\Omega
, we rewrite Eq. (
31
) as:

\displaystyle\Omega^{\ast}H^{\mathrm{T}}_{k_{x},s}\Omega^{\mathrm{T}}=-\Omega H_{k_{x},s}\Omega^{\dagger}.

(32)

As a result, we find that the transformed Hamiltonian,
\tilde{H}_{k_{x},s}=\Omega H_{k_{x},s}\Omega^{\dagger}
, becomes an antisymmetric matrix:

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{k_{x},s}^{\mathrm{T}}=-\tilde{H}_{k_{x},s}.

(33)

The antisymmetric nature of
\tilde{H}_{k_{x},s}
allows us to define a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
invariant using its Pfaffian:

\displaystyle(-1)^{\nu}=\mathrm{sgn}\left[\mathrm{Pf}(\tilde{H}_{k^{\prime},s})\mathrm{Pf}(\tilde{H}_{k^{\prime\prime},s})\right]

(34)

where
k^{\prime}
and
k^{\prime\prime}
are two arbitrary momenta.
When
\nu=1
mod
2
, there is an odd number of zero-energy band crossings between
k^{\prime}
and
k^{\prime\prime}

kobayashi_14
;
timm_17
.
Thus, the presence of zero-energy band crossings in the present system is protected by a nontrivial
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
invariant:

\displaystyle\mathrm{sgn}\left[\mathrm{Pf}(\tilde{H}_{k_{n}+\delta k,s})\mathrm{Pf}(\tilde{H}_{k_{n}-\delta k,s})\right]=-1

(35)

where
k_{n}
denotes the momenta at which zero-energy band crossing occurs and
\delta k\ll 1
.
These band crossings can only be removed by perturbations that break the
CP
-like symmetry in Eq. (
31
).
In our model, such symmetry breaking is achieved by introducing the SOC term (i.e.,
\lambda\neq 0
), which inherently breaks the inversion symmetry in Eq. (
28
).

II
Effective Theory

II.1
Low-energy effective Hamiltonian

We derive an effective Hamiltonian for a quasi-one dimensional system with a unidirectional SOC potential of a persistent spin texture and a
d_{xy}
-wave pair potential.
We begin with the BdG equation in Eq. (7) of the main text:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&H_{s}(k_{x},y)\psi_{s}(k_{x},y)=E\psi_{s}(k_{x},y),\\
&H_{s}(k_{x},y)=H^{d_{xy}}_{s}(k_{x},y)+H^{\lambda}_{s}(y),\\
&H^{d_{xy}}_{s}(k_{x},y)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\xi(k_{x},y)&\sigma_{s}\Delta(k_{x},y)\\
\sigma_{s}\Delta(k_{x},y)&-\xi(k_{x},y)\end{array}\right],\\
&H^{\lambda}_{s}(y)=\sigma_{s}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Lambda(y)&0\\
0&-\Lambda(y)\end{array}\right],\\
&\xi(k_{x},y)=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\partial_{y}^{2}-\tilde{\mu},\quad\tilde{\mu}=\mu-\frac{\hbar^{2}k_{x}^{2}}{2m},\\
&\Delta(k_{x},y)=-i\frac{\tilde{\Delta}}{k_{F}}\partial_{y},\quad\tilde{\Delta}=\frac{\Delta}{k_{F}},\quad\Lambda(y)=-i\tilde{\lambda}\partial_{y},\end{split}

(36)

where
\sigma_{s}=+1(-1)
for
s=\uparrow(\downarrow)
.
The Hamiltonian preserves chiral symmetry:

\displaystyle\Gamma H_{s}(k_{x},y)\Gamma^{-1}=-H_{s}(k_{x},y),\quad\Gamma=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-i\\
i&0\end{array}\right].

For later convenience, we transform the Hamiltonian into the chiral basis:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&U_{\Gamma}H_{s}(k_{x},y)U^{\dagger}_{\Gamma}=Q_{s}(k_{x},y)=Q^{d_{xy}}_{s}(k_{x},y)+Q^{\lambda}_{s}(y),\\
&U_{\Gamma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-i\\
i&-1\end{array}\right],\\
&Q^{d_{xy}}_{s}(k_{x},y)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-i\xi(k_{x},y)-\sigma_{s}\Delta(k_{x},y)\\
i\xi(k_{x},y)-\sigma_{s}\Delta(k_{x},y)&0\end{array}\right],\\
&Q^{\lambda}_{s}(y)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-i\sigma_{s}\Lambda(y)\\
i\sigma_{s}\Lambda(y)&0\end{array}\right].\end{split}

(39)

We first examine zero-energy states in the absence of the SOC (i.e.,
\lambda=0
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}&Q^{d_{xy}}_{s}(k_{x},y)\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\phi_{s,+}(k_{x},y)\\
0\end{array}\right]=0,\\
&Q^{d_{xy}}_{s}(k_{x},y)\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0\\
\phi_{s,-}(k_{x},y)\end{array}\right]=0,\end{split}

(40)

where
\phi_{s,\pm}(k_{x},y)
satisfies:

\displaystyle\left\{\pm i\xi(k_{x},y)-\sigma_{s}\Delta(k_{x},y)\right\}\phi_{s,\pm}(k_{x},y)=0.

(41)

A general solution takes the form:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\phi_{s,\pm}(k_{x},y)=(ae^{iqy}+be^{-iqy})e^{\pm\sigma_{s}\tilde{\kappa}y},\\
&q=\sqrt{\tilde{k}_{F}^{2}-\tilde{\kappa}^{2}},\quad\tilde{k}_{F}=\frac{\sqrt{2m\tilde{\mu}}}{\hbar},\quad\tilde{\kappa}=\frac{m\tilde{\Delta}}{\hbar^{2}k_{F}}.\end{split}

(42)

Applying the boundary condition
\phi_{s,\pm}(k_{x},0)=0
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\phi_{s,\pm}(k_{x},y)=c\sin(q y)e^{\pm\sigma_{s}\tilde{\kappa}y}.

(43)

Moreover, the boundary condition,
\phi_{s,\pm}(k_{x},W)\propto\sin(qW)=0
, leads to

\displaystyle q=q_{n}=\frac{n\pi}{W},\qquad

\displaystyle n=\pm 1,\pm 2,\cdots.

(44)

From
q=\sqrt{\tilde{k}_{F}^{2}-\tilde{\kappa}^{2}}
, we find the momenta at which zero-energy state exist:

\displaystyle k_{x}=k_{n}=\mathrm{sgn}[n]\sqrt{\frac{k_{F}^{2}-q_{n}^{2}}{1+\left(\frac{\Delta}{2\mu}\right)^{2}}}.

(45)

Since
k_{n}
must be real, the integer number
n
is restricted to

\displaystyle n=\pm 1,\pm 2,\cdots,\pm n_{c}

(46)

where
n_{c}=[Wk_{F}/\pi]_{G}
represents the number of propagating channels per spin.
From the normalization condition:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{W}dy\phi^{\ast}_{s,\pm}(k_{x},y)\phi_{s,\pm}(k_{x},y)=1,

(47)

we obtain:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\phi_{s,\pm}(k_{n},y)=c_{k_{n},s,\pm}\sin(q_n y)e^{\pm s\kappa_{n}y},\\
&c_{s,\pm,n}=\sqrt{\frac{2X_{n}}{W}e^{\mp s\kappa_{n}W}},\quad X_{n}=\frac{\kappa_{n}W}{\sinh(\kappa_n W)}\left\{1+\left(\frac{\Delta}{2\mu}\right)^{2}\frac{k_{n}^{2}}{q_{n}^{2}}\right\}.\end{split}

(48)

Since
\phi_{s,+}(k_{n},y)
and
\phi_{s,-}(k_{n},y)
are degenerate, we can construct alternative expressions for the zero-energy states by taking linear combinations of these functions.
Here, we construct the wave function of the zero-energy states,
\tilde{\psi}^{\pm}_{s}(k_{n},y)=\sum_{a=\pm}c_{\pm,a}\phi_{s,a}(k_{n},y)
, to satisfy:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{W}dy\left\{\tilde{\psi}^{\pm}_{s}(k_{n},y)\right\}^{\dagger}Q^{d_{xy}}_{s}(k_{n}+\delta k,y)\tilde{\psi}^{\pm}_{s}(k_{n},y)=\pm\epsilon_{s}(k_{n}+\delta k)

(49)

with

\displaystyle\epsilon_{s}(k_{n}+\delta k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\mathrm{sgn}[n]|v_{n}|\delta k+O(\delta k^{2})&\text{for }|n|\in\text{odd}\\
&\\
-\mathrm{sgn}[n]|v_{n}|\delta k+O(\delta k^{2})&\text{for }|n|\in\text{even}\end{array}\right.,

where
\pm\epsilon_{s}(k_{x})
denotes the energy eigenvalues of the Andreev bound states (ABSs).
Equation (
II.1
) means that
\epsilon_{s}(k_{x})
forms a continuous spectrum with respect to
k_{x}
, as illustrated in Fig. 4(a) of the main text.
This is achieved by the wave function:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\tilde{\psi}^{\pm}_{s}(k_{n},y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}e^{\mp i\frac{\pi}{4}}\tilde{\varphi}^{\pm}_{s}(k_{n},y)&\text{for }|n|\in\text{odd}\\
e^{\mp i\frac{\pi}{4}}\tilde{\varphi}^{\mp}_{s}(k_{n},y)&\text{for }|n|\in\text{even}\end{array}\right.,\\
&\tilde{\varphi}^{\pm}_{s}(k_{n},y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\phi_{s,+}(k_{n},y)\\
\pm i\phi_{s,-}(k_{n},y)\end{array}\right],\end{split}

(53)

where we specifically obtain:

\displaystyle v_{n}=\frac{\hbar^{2}k_{n}}{m}\left\{1+\left(\frac{\Delta}{2\mu}\right)^{2}\right\}X_{n}.

(54)

In the main text, we discuss the wave function in the original basis:

\displaystyle\psi^{\pm}_{s}(k_{n},y)=U^{\dagger}_{\Gamma}\tilde{\psi}^{\pm}_{s}(k_{n},y).

(55)

To proceed with our analysis, we treat the spin-orbit coupling,
H^{\lambda}_{s}(y)
, as a perturbation and construct the low-energy effective Hamiltonian as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{s}(k_{x})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A_{++}&A_{+-}\\
A_{-+}&A_{--}\end{array}\right]+O(\lambda^{2}),\\
&A_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}=\int_{0}^{W}dy\left\{\psi^{\eta}_{s}(k_{x},y)\right\}^{\dagger}H_{s}(k_{x},y)\psi^{\eta^{\prime}}_{s}(k_{x},y),\end{split}

(56)

where
\psi^{\pm}_{s}(k_{x},y)
represents the wave function of the ABSs at
\lambda=0
, and
A_{\eta\eta}=\eta\epsilon_{s}(k_{x})
.
In the vicinity of
k_{x}=k_{n}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{s}(k_{n}+\delta k)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{W}dy\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\left\{\tilde{\psi}^{+}_{s}(k_{n},y)\right\}^{\dagger}Q_{s}(k_{n}+\delta k,y)\tilde{\psi}^{+}_{s}(k_{n},y)&\left\{\tilde{\psi}^{+}_{s}(k_{n},y)\right\}^{\dagger}Q_{s}(k_{n}+\delta k,y)\tilde{\psi}^{-}_{s}(k_{n},y)\\
\left\{\tilde{\psi}^{-}_{s}(k_{n},y)\right\}^{\dagger}Q_{s}(k_{n}+\delta k,y)\tilde{\psi}^{+}_{s}(k_{n},y)&\left\{\tilde{\psi}^{-}_{s}(k_{n},y)\right\}^{\dagger}Q_{s}(k_{n}+\delta k,y)\tilde{\psi}^{-}_{s}(k_{n},y)\end{array}\right]+O(\delta k^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n,s}(\delta k)+O(\delta k^{2}),

(59)

with

\displaystyle\begin{split}&H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{n,s}(\delta k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}v_{n}\delta k&m_{n}\\
m_{n}&-v_{n}\delta k\end{array}\right]&\text{for }|n|\in\text{odd}\\
&\\
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}-v_{n}\delta k&-m_{n}\\
-m_{n}&v_{n}\delta k\end{array}\right]&\text{for }|n|\in\text{even}\end{array}\right.,\\
&m_{n}=\tilde{\lambda}\kappa_{n}X_{n},\end{split}

(60)

which is equivalent to Eq. (13) in the main text.

II.2
Winding number

Next, we compute the one-dimensional winding number of the effective Hamiltonian.
By using an appropriate expression of
\psi^{\pm}_{s}(k_{x},y)
, we can always construct the effective Hamiltonian preserving chiral symmetry as:

\displaystyle\gamma H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{s}(k_{x})\gamma^{-1}=-H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{s}(k_{x}),\quad\gamma=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-i\\
i&0\end{array}\right],

which allows us to define the winding number:

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{1D}}^{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{i}{4\pi}\sum_{s=\uparrow,\downarrow}\int dk_{x}\mathrm{Tr}[\gamma\{H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{s}(k_{x})\}^{-1}\partial_{k_{x}}H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{s}(k_{x})].

(63)

The effective Hamiltonian can be generally deformed into the chiral basis as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&U_{\gamma}H^{\mathrm{eff}}_{s}(k_{x})U^{\dagger}_{\gamma}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&q_{s}(k_{x})\\
q^{\ast}_{s}(k_{x})&0\end{array}\right],\\
&U_{\gamma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-i\\
i&-1\end{array}\right],\end{split}

(64)

where

\displaystyle q_{s}(k_{n}+\delta k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-iv_{n}\delta k-m_{n}&\text{for }|n|\in\text{odd}\\
&\\
iv_{n}\delta k+m_{n}&\text{for }|n|\in\text{even}\end{array}\right..

As shown in Ref. [
sato_11
], the winding number can be further simplified to:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&N_{\mathrm{1D}}^{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=\uparrow,\downarrow}\sum_{C(k_{x})=0}\mathrm{sgn}[\partial_{k_{x}}C(k_{x})]\mathrm{sgn}[R(k_{x})],\\
&R(k_{x})=\mathrm{Re}[q_{s}(k_{x})],\quad C(k_{x})=\mathrm{Im}[q_{s}(k_{x})],\end{split}

(68)

where the summation
\sum_{C(k_{x})=0}
is taken for
k_{x}
satisfying
C(k_{x})=0
.
Using this formula, we eventually obtain

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{1D}}^{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=\uparrow,\downarrow}\sum_{n=-n_{c}}^{n_{c}}\mathrm{sgn}[v_{n}]\mathrm{sgn}[m_{n}]=N_{c},

(69)

which is also shown in the main text.
