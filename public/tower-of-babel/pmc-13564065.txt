Variation in and relation between morphological, physicochemical and Ayurvedic characteristics for the standards of Terminalia chebula Retz. (Haritaki) from different geographical regions
Authors: Pramod D Khobragade, Shiramji G Jyotishi, Pandya Tarulata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVariation is the common phenomena in natural specimens; but is within certain limit and may show some association between various characteristics. The present study forms the basis of standardization for such natural specimens. Terminalia chebula Retz. (Haritaki), one of the most useful Ayurvedic drugs has been worked out for the same aspect.ObjectiveTo obtain the range of variations and relation between different characteristics of T. chebula fruit collected from different geographical regions of India; to compare Ayurvedic parameters as mentioned for standards with physicochemical and morphological characteristics of T. chebula fruit; and to develop standards for T. chebula fruit accordingly.Materials and methodsTen samples were obtained by coning and quarting technique from about 3000 fruits obtained from ten different geographical regions, pan India; including one sample of T. pallida for determining variations and relation between usual parameters used for standards; along with assay of chemical group “total phenolic” calorimetrically; instead of any single active principle alone. Ayurvedic parameters as ‘Nimmjate’ (sinking in water) has also been obtained. Interrelation and correlation between morphological, physicochemical and Ayurvedic parameters were studied.ResultLarge variations, which are far from existing texts were found. Quality of the fruits as mentioned in Ayurvedic texts for the drug is not available.ConclusionAyurvedic parameters can be applicable only if the drug is self-collected to avoid any malpractice. The standard of the drugs as obtained here are far from that are reported in various recent texts.Keywords: Terminalia chebula Retz, Terminalia pallida, Haritaki, Nimajjate, Herbal standards

Introduction

The Ayurvedic drugs are derived from the nature, may be with varied origins viz plant, animal or minerals, leading to large variations in the samples collected from different regions, seasons, and irregularity in maturity. Considering all these facts, Ayurvedic classical texts have already mentioned their quality as grahya-agrahya and it's a matter of study in current times.

In the modern era, as the commercialization advanced, human chain of cultivators, collectors, manufacturers, vendors, retailers, consumers and physicians got involved in it; increasing the chances of supply of substandard or duplicate, adulterated, deteriorated material as against the ancient practice of collection by the vaidyas themselves. As per the textual direction, it should be collected keeping the following in mind. Various Nighantus mentioned guidelines for ideal fruit of Haritaki to be taken (Grahya) for therapeutic uses, it should be just mature, round or oval(Aayatkar) in shape, oleaginous, heavy that sinks in water, naturally matured, naturally sheded from tree and falls on the ground, weighing 24 g (2 karsh), not contaminated by any kind of insects (Jantujagdha), not burned in wildfire (Davadagdha), not shed in water (Jalesthiham), not shed on mud (Pankesthitam), not grown in salty soil (Ushare) and not cracked (Bhinnam) fruits [[1], [2], [3]].

Apart from this, the Ayurvedic classics also mention the variations in the quality of Dravya (drug) in different geographical regions.It is also mentioned that,the drug from particular region is to be used for the patient of that region even if he/she temporally shifted to other region [4]; World Health Organization (WHO) made it mandatory all over the world to mention the standards of the product to be consumed by humans; hence it became essential to develop standards of Ayurvedic products as per the modern parameters.

Thus, the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India (API) Committee decided few parameters to be developed as the standards of Ayurvedic drugs, accordingly monographs are published in the various volumes of API. While going through the monographs, provided in the API, there seems a need of further study to strengthen the basic purpose of the API, and further the legal notice given in the API also writes as “If considered necessary these standards can be amended and the Chairman of the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia Committee is authorized to issue such amendments:” [5]. Whereas the monographs given in Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India (API), Govt. of India are with an question mark for the number of samples and their geographical locations.

To fill these gaps , few more parameters and inter-relation of the values becomes necessary. Accordingly, our aim was to determine specifically characterized values for various parameters alongwith some additional parameters.

Though, the monographs in the API gives the standards for Ayurvedic drugs, but the drugs mainly define the botanical perspective for the part of the plant used. For example, in the case of fruit, the fruit as a whole is considered for the description and values of the parameters [6]. It is all right if the whole fruit including peduncles and seed is used as drugs, but it is erroneous where only a part of the fruit is used, for example, if it is rind only, as in case of Haritaki. The same thing is seen in The Siddha Pharmacopoeia of India (SPI Vol I 2008). The quality standard of Indian medicinal plants published by Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) includes the monograph of Haritaki (rind), but it is mainly focused on High performance thin layer chromatography (HPTLC) with microscopy [7]. Similarly, The Hong Kong Chinese Materia Medica Standards (HKCMMS Vol 6, 2013) mentions the standards for fruit excluding kernels. All these texts define Haritaki as pericarp of the fruit; whereas the rind that is used as medicine is only the outer pericarp and not the inner hard endocarp. Hence here it seems necessary to have the standards of Haritaki (rind); with this view, the standards of rind of T. chebula are determined here.

Materials and methods

1.Collection of samples [Supplementary file-Figs. 1.1 to 1.12]:

Collection of samples [Supplementary file-Figs. 1.1 to 1.12]:

Specimen materials (about 3000 fruits) for the study were collected from different regions pan India and were coded as follows:

A –Junner-Pune (Maharashtra), B – Gwalier (Madhapradesh), C– Chennai (Tamilnadu), D-Karad, (Maharashtra), E–Shri-shail-herb, Nagpur (Maharashtra), F-Anand, (Gujarat), G–Jandya (Jammu), H-Udupi, (Karnataka), I-Gadchiroli, (Maharashtra), J- Tirupati, (Andharapradesh), Samples A, B, C, and E were collected from market while D, F, G, H, I and J from field. Samples I, H, and G were immature.

2.Authentication of sample

Authentication of sample

Field samples were authenticated from Foundation for Revitalisation of Local Health Traditions (FRLHT), Bangalore.

Sample F –Voucher herbarium specimen FRLHT Col. No. 120027; Sample H - Voucher specimen FRLHT Col. No.122015; Sample I - Voucher specimen FRLHT Col. No. 122014 and Sample J – Voucher specimen FRLHT Col. No. 120026 (sample J was identified as Terminalia pallida).

3.Selection of sample:(cone and quartening method) [8].

Selection of sample:(cone and quartening method) [8].

The samples were randomly divided into approximately two equal parts (heaps).

One of the parts (approximately 1 kg) was heaped (cone) and spread out in a round tray with equal thickness of the fruit layers and was divided into 4 parts by cross lines. Two opposite parts were taken (approximately half kg). Thus, selected fruits were again taken in another tray and divided into four parts as above and two opposite parts were again selected (approximately 250 g). The fruits were randomly selected from this portion for various studies.

4.Sinking in water:

Sinking in water:

About 5 L of water was taken in a trough having diameter about 20 cm. Randomly selected 27 fruits were dropped in it, shaked and rubbed with hand toremove any air bubble adhering to it. Total numbers of floating and sunkun fruits were recorded to calculate sinked fruits percentage.

Result

The samples out of 3000 fruits from different geographical regions vary largely with each other, as percentage of fruit that sink in water was found to be as low as 14.81 % for sample D (field, mature) and the highest value was 100 % for sample I (field, immature). Loss on drying (LoD) varies from 7.42 (Sample H) to 9.85 (Sample G). These two parameters show positive correlation (r = 0.7864, p < 0.02, n = 9) (Fig. 4).

Diagram showing dimensions and weight (lowest, highest, lowest mean and highest mean) of Haritaki fruits.

In case of the dimensions, the lowest readings are 1.16 cm (sample I) for length, 0.9 cm (sample F) for width, 0.68 cm (sample F) for height and the highest readings for the same are 8.32 cm (sample G), 3.84 cm (sample G), 3.67 cm (sample G) respectively. Similarly, lowest value of mean for the above parameters are 2.71 ± 0.33 cm (sample I), 1.46 ± 0.19 cm (sample F), 1.34 ± 0.20 cm (sample F); whereas the highest values for the same are 5.95 ± 1.25 cm (Sample G), 2.38 ± 0.37 cm (sample E), 1.86 ± 0.32 cm (sample E) respectively. Applying one-way ANNOVA (F value: for length = 154.135, P-value = 0.00; for width = 62.009, P-value = 0.00; for height = 29.888, P-value = 0.00), the variation was confirmed to be statistically significant.

For weight, the lowest readings are 0.77 g for fruit, 0.19 g for seed with endocarp and 0.42 g for rind (all for sample F) while the highest readings for the same are 17.19 g (sample G), 4.96 g (sample H) and 14.21g (sample G) respectively. Similarly, lowest value of mean for the same are 2.47 ± 0.35 g (sample D), 0.64 ± 0.28 g (sample F), 1.62 ± 0.26 g(sample D), whereas the highest values are 10.48 ± 3.13 g (sample G), 3.08 ± 0.69 g (sample E) and 8.59 ± 2.48 g (sample G) respectively.

The highest total ash value, water soluble ash value, acid insoluble ash value and acid soluble ash value obtained were 3.46 % (sample F, the sample H out ranges the same to 5.13 %), 2.61 % (sample F, the sample H out ranges the same to 4.83 %), 1.42 % (sample A) and 0.86 % (sample F) respectively. Whereas, the lowest value for the same parameters was 2.08 % (sample B and C), 0.71 % (sample A), 0.00 % (samples C, D, F,H, I and J), 0.29 % (sample C) respectively.

The highest water extractive value found was 70.47 % (sample C) and that for alcohol extractive value was 55.39 % and the lowest value for the same was 55.10 % and 25.78 % (sample F, the sample H out ranges the same to 8.86 %), respectively.

Phenolic content showed the highest value as 33.81 % (sample C) and the lowest for the same was found to be 24.81 (sample G, sample H had 21.66 % (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Standards of a material define the substance qualitatively and quantitatively, and it should be kept in mind that, one should not neglect the possiblility of identification of another specimen complying with same parameters Ayurvedic classics have already paid due attention towards standards of the raw drugs and their formulations, but it was with respect to personal collection and natural conditions. In the present era, due to human handling, we need to pay the attention towards standards,which can be done by studying the physico-chemical parameters of the raw drugs and their formulations. The ayurvedic standards and related methods from Ayurveda need to be treated equally important as that of modern physico-chemical & analytical methods while establishing quality and purity of any drug or formulation.Beginning our study with the Ayurvedic standards and classical method of standardization and keeping in mind, the fact that Ayurvedic standard "Nimmajate" and "LoD" has positive correlation which can be misused in market.

According to Ayurvedic standards (Grahya) fruit should weigh, two Karsh i.e. two Tola (24 g) [11], but the heaviest fruit among randomly selected 540 fruits, from the total 3000 fruits selected pan India was only17.19 g.

Some values found in literature show very low range as 2.0–3.5 cm for length, 1.3–2.5 cm for width in API [6],and 3.5–4.0 cm for length and 1.5–2.0 cm for width according to Database of medicinal plants by Central Council for Research in Ayurvedic Sciences (CCRAS) as well as Quality standards of medicinal plants by ICMR [12,13]; as against the observed value in this study i.e. 1.6 cm (sample I) to 8.32 cm (sample G) for length, 0.9 cm (sample F) to 3.84 cm (sample G) for width.

Different types of Haritaki according to shape are Jeevantee, Abhya, Amrita, Pootana, Rohini, Vijaya and Chetaki [14], while as per size of the fruit are Survari harade, Rangari harade, Bala harade, Java harade [15]Futher, according to different stages of maturity of fruits, they are small Myrobalan, large Myrobalan, yellow Myrobalanas as mentioned in Ayurvedic literature []. Considering this fact Haritaki samples were collected from different geographical regions pan India and analyzed for their standards. Although, we didn't categorised our samples to be belonging to particular type(since it wasn't a part of our study), but considering the fact of different types, values for different parameters were obtained.

The low ash value as obtained is indicative of comparatively less mineral content, whereas better water extractive value shows higher quantity of primary metabolites along with tannins and medium alcohol extractive values, exhibiting likewise secondary metabolites content.

Assay is an essential part of standardization and not only that of a single active principle but that of the complete chemical group of compounds as an approach towards holistic study. The presence of a single chemical component, whether it is present in mature or immature, may differ quantitatively and qualitatively, and only that is not so important, when the whole drugs is used. Mature specimens definitely differ qualitatively from the immature one (as seen in the observations also, same is the chances with the marketed sample too).

As tannins, the polyphenolics are soluble compounds in water; the increase and decrease of the water extractive value and the phenolic content are found to be qualitatively parallel. The phenolic contents of different samples are quantitatively very close to their alcoholic extractive value, which shows that the alcohol extract mainly contains phenolics including tannins.

Another important fact to be considered is from commercial point of view, that the fully matured and dried sample with less percentage of moisture and sinking in water quality, is less profitable or costlier.

It is also remarkable that T. pallida matches with T. chebula in all aspects of standards pertaining to different parameters which were studied.

Conclusion

The test “Nimajjate” (sinking in water) is applicable only to self-collected samples maintaining all other conditions as per text. The availability of Grahya fruits with 24 gm as mentioned in the text is not possible. The region wise variation in the Dravya is the indicative of its used in the patients belonging to that region as mentioned in Ayurveda. Different ash values follow a definite ratio in general. So also, the ratio between alcohol extractive value and water extractive value also has a definite ratio. Values for T. pallida are within the same range as that of T. chebula.
