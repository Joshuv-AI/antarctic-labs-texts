Hiroaki Ito, Niels Langeveld, Jörg Thuswaldner

Abstract
. We study the Reverse algorithm, a multidimensional continued fraction algorithm, which is not unimodular. We show that the Reverse algorithm is ergodic and, by proving that its second Lyapunov exponent is negative, that it is a.e. exponentially convergent. In addition to that, we attach substitutions to this algorithm and study the S S -adic languages generated by sequences of these substitutions. The negativity of the second Lyapunov exponent implies that almost all of these languages are balanced. By a thorough study of the combinatorics of the substitutions, we are even able to obtain a concrete generic family of balanced languages that is characterized in terms of a simple condition on the underlying sequence of substitutions.

Introduction

In 1991, Arnoux and Rauzy
[
3
]
invented a new 2-dimensional continued fraction algorithm that is defined on a subset of the positive cone
\Lambda=\{[x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2}]\in\mathbb{P}^{2}\,:\,x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}>0\}

F_{\mathrm{AR}}([x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2}])=[x_{0}-x_{1}-x_{2}:x_{1}:x_{2}]

It is easy to see that this procedure does not always result in a vector that is contained in
\Lambda
. Therefore, in general, one cannot iterate the map
F_{\mathrm{AR}}
on
\Lambda

is the well-known

. We can attach

with the Arnoux-Rauzy algorithm. Negativity of the second Lyapunov exponent entails that these substitutions have nice properties. For example, in
[

,

The problem with the domain of definition of

Let

be given. If there is a positive matrix
M
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
M=M_{a_{n}}\cdots M_{a_{n+r}}
holds for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then
(M_{a_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
admits a generalized right eigenvector
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
.

It is easy to see that the condition of the lemma certainly holds if
(M_{a_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

is primitive and recurrent.

We assume throughout the paper that
(\Delta,f)
admits an invariant measure that is equivalent to the Lebesgue measure on
\Delta
.

The transposition used in (
2.2
) comes from the fact that we are interested in the
linear cocycle
of the algorithm
(\Delta,f)
, which is given by
A
. Indeed,

A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})=A(f^{n-1}\mathbf{x})\cdots A(f\mathbf{x})\,A(\mathbf{x}),

satisfies the
cocycle property

A^{(m+n)}(\mathbf{x})=A^{(m)}(f^{n}\mathbf{x})\,A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})
.
For a linear cocycle we can define Lyapunov exponents. In particular, a real number
\lambda
is called a Lyapunov exponent for
A
at
\mathbf{x}
, if there exists
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log{\|A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{v}\|}=\lambda.

The limit does not depend on the norm and, regarded as a function in
\mathbf{v}
, attains at most
3
distinct values
\lambda_{1}(A,\mathbf{x})\geq\lambda_{2}(A,\mathbf{x})\geq\lambda_{3}(A,\mathbf{x})
. If
(\Delta,f)
is ergodic, these values are the same for a.e.
\mathbf{x}\in\Delta
and we call these common values
\lambda_{1}(A)\geq\lambda_{2}(A)\geq\lambda_{3}(A)
the Lyapunov exponents for
A
. For details on Lyapunov exponents we refer for instance to
[
1
]
. Lyapunov exponents, in particular, the second Lyapunov exponent, are intimately related to the convergence behavior of
(\Delta,f)
, see e.g.
[
15
,
10
]
.

A cylinder of rank
n
in
\Delta
is a set

(2.3)

\Delta(a_{0}a_{1}\cdots a_{n-1}):=\{\mathbf{x}\in\Delta\;:\;\mathbf{x}\in\Delta(a_{0}),\,f(\mathbf{x})\in\Delta(a_{1}),\,\dots,\,f^{n-1}(\mathbf{x})\in\Delta(a_{n-1})\}

with
a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1}\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
.
Then the cylinder
\Delta(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1})
containing
\mathbf{x}
has the row vectors of
A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})
as vertices. According to the definition of
A
we have

A^{(n)}(\Delta(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1}))=\{^{t}(M_{a_{0}}\cdots M_{a_{n-1}})\}
,
a singleton, which we will often identify with the single matrix
{}^{t}(M_{a_{0}}\cdots M_{a_{n-1}})
it contains.

Let us recall some further facts from the general theory of multidimensional continued fraction algorithms and formulate them for the
2
-dimensional case.

Definition 2.3

(Topological convergence)
.

Let
(\Delta,f)
be a
2
-dimensional continued fraction algorithm and let
\mathbf{x}=\,^{t}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})\in\Delta
.
Choose
a_{0}a_{1}\cdots\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\mathbf{x}\in\Delta(a_{0}a_{1}\cdots a_{n-1})
holds for each
n\geq 0
.
If the diameters
d(\Delta(a_{0}a_{1}\cdots a_{n-1}))
of
\Delta(a_{0}a_{1}\cdots a_{n-1})
satisfy

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d(\Delta(a_{0}a_{1}\cdots a_{n-1}))=0

we say that
(\Delta,f)
is
topologically convergent
at
\mathbf{x}
.

It is easy to see that topological convergence at
\mathbf{x}
is equivalent to the fact that

(M_{a_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
has a generalized right eigenvector. A much stronger notion of convergence is the following one.

Definition 2.4

(Exponential convergence)
.

Let
(\Delta,f)
be a
2
-dimensional continued fraction algorithm and let
\mathbf{x}=\,^{t}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})\in\Delta
be arbitrary. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let the entries of the cocycle matrix be given by

(2.4)

A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{pmatrix}p_{00}^{(n)}&p_{01}^{(n)}&p_{02}^{(n)}\\
p_{10}^{(n)}&p_{11}^{(n)}&p_{12}^{(n)}\\
p_{20}^{(n)}\textbf{}&p_{21}^{(n)}&p_{22}^{(n)}\end{pmatrix}

(we suppress the dependency of the entries on
\mathbf{x}
for notational convenience). For
i\in\{0,1,2\}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
set
p_{i}^{(n)}=p_{i0}^{(n)}+p_{i1}^{(n)}+p_{i2}^{(n)}
. We say that
\Big(\frac{p_{i1}^{(n)}}{p_{i}^{(n)}},\frac{p_{i2}^{(n)}}{p_{i}^{(n)}}\Big)
is
exponentially convergent
to
({x_{1}},{x_{2}})
if there exists a constant
\alpha>1
such that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\big\|\big({p_{i1}^{(n)}},{p_{i2}^{(n)}}\big)-{p_{i}^{(n)}}({x_{1}},{x_{2}})\big\|_{\infty}<\big(p_{i}^{(n)}\big)^{1-\alpha}

holds for each
i\in\{0,1,2\}
all
n
large enough. If (
2.5
) holds for a.e.
\mathbf{x}\in\Delta
then we say that
(\Delta,f)
is
exponentially convergent
.

Let
(\Delta,f)
be a
2
-dimensional continued fraction algorithm. The supremum
\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})
of all
\alpha
satisfying (
2.5
) for each
i\in\{0,1,2\}
and all
n
large enough is called the uniform approximation exponent of
\mathbf{x}
for the algorithm
(\Delta,f)
. It is known that, under mild conditions (including ergodicity), the uniform approximation exponent
\eta^{*}
of
(\Delta,f)
satisfies

(2.6)

\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})=1-\frac{\lambda_{2}(A)}{\lambda_{1}(A)}

for almost all
\mathbf{x}\in\Delta
. In particular, if
\lambda_{2}(A)<0
then
(\Delta,f)
is exponentially convergent (see for instance
[
15
, Theorem 4.1]
). As stated in
Proposition

4.2
below, these conditions are satisfied for the Reverse algorithm.

We need the following consequence of topological convergence.

Proposition 2.5

(
[
23
, Theorem 4]
)
.

Let
(\Delta,f)
be a
2
-dimensional continued fraction algorithm and let
\mathcal{B}^{(s)}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the cylinders in
\Delta
of order
s
. If
(\Delta,f)
is topologically convergent everywhere, then

\bigvee_{s=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{B}^{(s)}
equals the
\sigma
-algebra of Borel sets on
\Delta
.

Recall that a
2
-dimensional continued fraction algorithm
(\Delta,f)
is called full if for each word
a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1}
we have
f^{n}(\Delta(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1}))=\Delta
.
To prove ergodicity of the Reverse algorithm we will use the following criterion that goes back to Rényi
[
22
]
. To be precise, the following proposition is an improvement of Rényi’s criterion due to Schweiger; details can be found in
[
24
]
.

Proposition 2.6

(Rényi 1957;
[
23
, Theorems 8 and 6]
)
.

Let
(\Delta,f)
be a
2
-dimensional continued fraction algorithm that satisfies the following conditions.

(a)

\mathrm{Leb}(\Delta)<\infty
.

(b)

The algorithm is full.

(c)

\bigvee_{s=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{B}^{(s)}
equals the
\sigma
-algebra of Borel sets on
\Delta
.

(d)

There exists a constant
C
such that for all admissible words
a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1}
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Delta}\omega(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1};\mathbf{x})\leq C\cdot\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\Delta}\omega(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1};\mathbf{x}),

where
\omega(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1};\mathbf{x})
is the Jacobian of the inverse branch of
f^{n}
that maps onto the cylinder
\Delta(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1})
.

Then,
f
is ergodic and admits a finite invariant measure
\mu
absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. This measure is unique up to a scalar factor.

Condition (d) of
Proposition

2.6
is called Rényi’s condition and
C
is called Rényi constant for
f
.
When applying Rényi’s criterion, we will use the following representation of the Jacobian of a
2
-dimensional continued fraction algorithm.

Lemma 2.7

(Schweiger,
[
23
, Lemma 25 in Chapter 11]
)
.

The Jacobian for a
2
-dimensional continued fraction algorithm
(\Delta,f)
is given by

\displaystyle\omega(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1};\;^{t}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}))=\frac{\det{A^{(n)}(\Delta(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1}))}}{\big(\|A^{(n)}_{0}\|_{1}x_{0}+\|A^{(n)}_{1}\|_{1}x_{1}+\|A^{(n)}_{2}\|_{1}x_{2}\big)^{3}},

where
A^{(n)}_{0},A^{(n)}_{1},A^{(n)}_{2}
are the row vectors of
A^{(n)}(\Delta(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1}))
.

3.
Ergodicity

The Reverse algorithm is a
2
-dimensional continued fraction algorithm in the sense of
Definition

2.1
. Indeed, it corresponds to the cocycle
A
given in (
1.3
). In this section, we prove the ergodicity of the Reverse algorithm
(\Delta,f_{\mathrm{R}})
with respect to the measure
\mu
with density
h
given in (
1.7
).
We first introduce the following notation. Let
A^{(n)}_{0},A^{(n)}_{1},A^{(n)}_{2}
be the row vectors of
A^{n}(\Delta(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1}))
. Choose
\pi_{0}(n),\pi_{1}(n),\pi_{2}(n)
in a way that
\{\pi_{0}(n),\pi_{1}(n),\pi_{2}(n)\}=\{0,1,2\}
and
\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{0}(n)}\|_{1}\leq\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{1}(n)}\|_{1}\leq\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{2}(n)}\|_{1}
holds. To simplify notation, we write
A^{(n)}_{\pi_{a}}
instead of
A^{(n)}_{\pi_{a}(n)}
.

When proving ergodicity of
(\Delta,f_{\mathrm{R}})
, the first idea is to establish the conditions of
Proposition

2.6
for
(\Delta,f_{\mathrm{R}})
directly. Conditions (a) and (b) are immediately satisfied. To prove (d), let

\displaystyle C(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1})=\frac{\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Delta}\omega(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1};\mathbf{x})}{\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\Delta}\omega(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1};\mathbf{x})}.

We need to show that
C
is bounded. Since the supremum and the infimum in the definition of
C(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1})
range over the whole set
\Delta
,
Lemma

2.7
yields

(3.1)

C(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1})=\bigg(\frac{\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{2}}\|_{1}}{\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{0}}\|_{1}}\bigg)^{3}.

Since

{}^{t}M^{n}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
n&1&0\\
n&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix},

we have
C(1^{n})=(n+1)^{3}
.
Therefore, there exists no Rényi constant for all cylinders and, hence, we cannot prove (d) directly. Following the ideas of
[
23
]
, we will first show that the conditions of
Proposition

2.6
are satisfied for a so-called jump transformation (see, for instance,
[
23
, Definition 26]
). In particular, we consider the jump transformation
f_{\mathrm{R}}^{*}
of
f_{\mathrm{R}}
on
\Delta(4)
, which avoids
a_{n}\in\{1,2,3\}
, i.e., we set

\tau(\mathbf{x})=1+\inf\{n\geq 0\,:\,f_{\mathrm{R}}^{n}(\mathbf{x})\in\Delta(4)\}

and define

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{R}}^{*}(\mathbf{x})=f_{\mathrm{R}}^{\tau(\mathbf{x})}(\mathbf{x}).

Because the Rauzy gasket has zero Lebesgue measure (see
[
5
, Section 6.3]
), for Lebesgue almost every
\mathbf{x}\in\Delta
,
a_{n}(\mathbf{x})=4
holds for infinitely many choices of
n
. Thus, the jump transformation
f_{\mathrm{R}}^{*}
is well-defined a.e. on
\Delta
and satisfies conditions (a) and (b) by definition.

Before we go on, we establish an auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

We have
\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{0}}\|_{1}+\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{1}}\|_{1}>\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{2}}\|_{1}
.

Proof.

We prove this by induction. Since the induction start trivially holds we continue with the induction step. By the symmetry of this algorithm, we may restrict ourselves to
a_{n}\in\{1,4\}
. For
a_{n}=1
we start with

{}^{t}\!A^{n}(\Delta(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1},1))=M_{a_{0}}\cdots M_{a_{n-1}}M_{1}=\big(A^{(n)}_{0},A^{(n)}_{0}+A^{(n)}_{1},A^{(n)}_{0}+A^{(n)}_{2}\big).

Since all occurring matrices are positive, the
1
-norm behaves nicely w.r.t. addition we gain

\displaystyle\|A^{(n+1)}_{\pi_{0}}\|_{1}+\|A^{(n+1)}_{\pi_{1}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|A^{(n)}_{0}\|_{1}+(\|A^{(n)}_{0}\|_{1}+\min\{\|A^{(n)}_{1}\|_{1},~\|A^{(n)}_{2}\|_{1}\})

\displaystyle>2\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{0}}\|_{1}+\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{1}}\|_{1}>\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{0}}\|_{1}+\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{2}}\|_{1}=\|A^{(n+1)}_{\pi_{2}}\|_{1}.

Similarly, for
a_{n}=4
we get from

\displaystyle M_{a_{1}}\cdots M_{a_{n-1}}M_{4}=\big(A^{(n)}_{1}+A^{(n)}_{2},A^{(n)}_{0}+A^{(n)}_{2},A^{(n)}_{0}+A^{(n)}_{1}\big)

that

\displaystyle\|A^{(n+1)}_{\pi_{0}}\|_{1}+\|A^{(n+1)}_{\pi_{1}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{0}}\|_{1}+\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{1}}\|_{1}+\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{0}}\|_{1}+\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{2}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle>\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{1}}\|_{1}+\|A^{(n)}_{\pi_{2}}\|_{1}=\|A^{(n+1)}_{\pi_{2}}\|_{1}.\qed

We also need a.e. topological convergence of
(\Delta,f_{\mathrm{R}}^{*})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The dynamical system
(\Delta,f_{\mathrm{R}}^{*})
is topological convergent almost everywhere.

Proof.

We first establish a.e. topological convergence of
(\Delta,f_{\mathrm{R}})
. By the Poincaré recurrence theorem, for almost all
\mathbf{x}\in\Delta
, the sequence
(M_{a_{n}})_{n\geq 0}=(\,^{t}\!A(f_{\mathrm{R}}^{n}(\mathbf{x})))_{n\geq 0}
of matrices is recurrent. Moreover, since the Lebesgue measure of the Rauzy gasket is zero,
M_{a_{n}}=M_{4}
holds for infinitely many
n
for a.e.
\mathbf{x}
. Thus, by recurrence, there is
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and a block
(N_{1},\ldots,N_{\ell-1})
such that
(M_{a_{n}},\ldots,M_{a_{n+\ell}})=(M_{4},N_{1},\ldots,N_{\ell-1},M_{4})
holds for infinitely many
n
. It is easy to check that
M_{4}N_{1}\cdots N_{\ell-1}M_{4}
is a positive matrix, hence,
(M_{a_{n}})_{n\geq 0}
is primitive. Thus, the conditions of
Lemma

2.2
hold a.e. and topological convergence a.e. of
(\Delta,f_{\mathrm{R}})
follows from this proposition. Because
(\Delta,f_{\mathrm{R}}^{*})
is an acceleration of
(\Delta,f_{\mathrm{R}})
, it is
a fortiori
toplogically convergent a.e. as well, and the lemma is proved.
∎

An alternative proof of Lemma
3.2

could be done by using ideas from
[
19
]
. Indeed, for each word
w_{1}\cdots w_{k}
ending with the letter
4
and containing two other digits, one can show that the cylinder
\Delta(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1}w_{1}\cdots w_{k})
has at most
\frac{2}{3}
times the diameter of
\Delta(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1})
. Since our proof is much shorter, we refrain from giving the technical details.

The next lemma contains Rényi’s condition for
(\Delta,f_{\mathrm{R}}^{*})
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Renyi’s condition is satisfied for
\omega(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1},4;x)
if
a_{n}=4
.
In particular, we have

C(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1},4)<2^{3}
.

Proof.

Let
M_{a_{0}}\cdots M_{a_{n-1}}=\big(A_{0}^{(n)},A_{1}^{(n)},A_{2}^{(n)}\big)
. Then,

\displaystyle M_{a_{1}}\cdots M_{a_{n-1}}M_{4}=\big(A_{1}^{(n)}+A_{2}^{(n)},A_{0}^{(n)}+A_{2}^{(n)},A_{0}^{(n)}+A_{1}^{(n)}\big).

Thus, by (
3.1
) and
Lemma

3.1

\displaystyle C(a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1},4)=\left(\frac{\|A_{\pi_{1}}^{(n)}+A_{\pi_{2}}^{(n)}\|_{1}}{\|A_{\pi_{0}}^{(n)}+A_{\pi_{1}}^{(n)}\|_{1}}\right)^{3}=\left(\frac{\|A_{\pi_{1}}^{(n)}\|_{1}+\|A_{\pi_{2}}^{(n)}\|_{1}}{\|A_{\pi_{0}}^{(n)}\|_{1}+\|A_{\pi_{1}}^{(n)}\|_{1}}\right)^{3}<2^{3}.\qquad\qed

Proposition 3.4
.

The jump transformation
f_{\mathrm{R}}^{*}
satisfies the Rényi condition.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of
Lemma

3.3
.
∎

We are now ready to prove our ergodicity result.

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

We first prove ergodicity of
(\Delta,f_{\mathrm{R}}^{*})
. To this end, we check the conditions of
Proposition

2.6
. By definition,
(\Delta,f_{\mathrm{R}}^{*})
satisfies conditions (a) and (b). It follows from

3.2
that the dynamical system

is topological convergent a.e. Thus, by

, the cylinders generate the

-algebra of Borel sets on a subset

of

is ergodic (on

and, hence, on

) and admits a finite invariant measure that is equivalent to the Lebesgue measure. Since ergodicity of

implies ergodicity of

4.
The second Lyapunov exponent

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.4
. Let
A
be the cocycle of an ergodic
2
-dimensional continued fraction algorithm
(\Delta,f)
. As mentioned in Section
2
, the Lyapunov exponents
\lambda_{1}(A)\geq\lambda_{2}(A)\geq\lambda_{3}(A)
are related to the convergence behavior of the algorithm
(\Delta,f)
. In particular, under mild conditions,
\lambda_{2}(A)<0
implies exponential convergence of the algorithm. Since it is easier to estimate the largest Lyapunov exponent of a cocycle rather than estimating the second largest one, in a first step we will define a cocycle
D
whose largest Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{1}(D)
contains information on the quality of the approximations of the algorithm.
The idea for this cocycle goes back to
[
15
]
, however, since our algorithm is unsorted and its projectivized version is normalized by the
1
-norm, the details are different in our situation.

Let
(\Delta,f)
be a
2
-dimensional continued fraction algorithm with cocycle
A
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let the entries of the cocycle matrix
A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})
be given as in (
2.4
) and recall that
p_{i}^{(n)}=p_{i0}^{(n)}+p_{i1}^{(n)}+p_{i2}^{(n)}
for
i\in\{0,1,2\}
. For
\mathbf{x}=\,^{t}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})\in\Delta
define the matrices

\displaystyle\Pi

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&0\\
-1&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad H(\mathbf{x})=\begin{pmatrix}-{x_{1}}&-{x_{2}}\\
1-{x_{1}}&-{x_{2}}\\
-{x_{1}}&1-{x_{2}}\end{pmatrix},

and

\displaystyle D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})=\Pi A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})H(\mathbf{x})=\begin{pmatrix}p_{11}^{(n)}-p_{01}^{(n)}-(p_{1}^{(n)}-p_{0}^{(n)})x_{1}&p_{12}^{(n)}-p_{02}^{(n)}-(p_{1}^{(n)}-p_{0}^{(n)})x_{2}\\
p_{21}^{(n)}-p_{01}^{(n)}-(p_{2}^{(n)}-p_{0}^{(n)})x_{1}&p_{22}^{(n)}-p_{02}^{(n)}-(p_{2}^{(n)}-p_{0}^{(n)})x_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

It turns out that
D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})
has the cocycle property. To show this, we first claim that

(4.1)

H(f^{n}\mathbf{x})\,\Pi\,A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})H(\mathbf{x})=A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})H(\mathbf{x}).

To prove this, let
I_{3}
be the
3\times 3
identity matrix and
O_{3}
the
3\times 3
zero matrix and observe that
I_{3}-H(f^{n}\mathbf{x})\,\Pi=\,^{t}(f^{n}(\mathbf{x}),f^{n}(\mathbf{x}),f^{n}(\mathbf{x}))
and
{}^{t}\!f^{n}(\mathbf{x})A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})H(\mathbf{x})=\,^{t}\mathbf{x}\,H(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}
, and, hence,

(I_{3}-H(f^{n}\mathbf{x})\,\Pi)A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})H(\mathbf{x})=\,^{t}(f^{n}(\mathbf{x}),f^{n}(\mathbf{x}),f^{n}(\mathbf{x}))A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})H(\mathbf{x})=\,^{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})H(\mathbf{x})=O_{3}.

Using (
4.1
) we obtain that

D^{(m)}(f^{n}\mathbf{x})D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})=\Pi\,A^{(m)}(f^{n}\mathbf{x})A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})H(\mathbf{x})=\Pi\,A^{(m+n)}(\mathbf{x})H(\mathbf{x})=D^{(m+n)}(\mathbf{x}),

thus
D
is a cocycle of
(\Delta,f)
.

In the following result, for two sequences
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
the notation
a_{n}\ll b_{n}
indicates that there is
C>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a_{n}\leq Cb_{n}
holds for
n\geq N
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(\Delta,f)
be an ergodic
2
-dimensional continued fraction algorithm with cocycle
A
.
Let
\delta_{i}(M)
be the
i
-th largest singular value of a
3\times 3
matrix
M

(1\leq i\leq 3)
. Then the inequality
\delta_{2}(A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x}))\ll\delta_{1}(D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x}))
holds uniformly for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Delta
. Therefore,
\lambda_{2}(A)\leq\lambda_{1}(D)
.

The reverse inequalities can also be shown, but are not needed for our purposes.

Proof.

Let
\|\cdot\|
be an arbitrary norm. Let
\mathbf{x}\in\Delta
and recall that
D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})=\Pi A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})H(\mathbf{x})
. As in the proof of
[
10
, Lemma 4.2]
, we can establish the lemma by mapping the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
into
\mathbb{R}^{2}
step by step via the matrices
H(\mathbf{x})
,
A^{n}(\mathbf{x})
, and
\Pi
, and keep track the lengths of the semi-axes of the occurring ellipses. First, setting
\mathbf{x}=\,^{t}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
, we see that for
{}^{t}(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
we get

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{x})\cdot\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-x_{1}v_{1}-x_{2}v_{2}\\
v_{1}-x_{1}v_{1}-x_{2}v_{2}\\
v_{2}-x_{1}v_{1}-x_{2}v_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Thus
E_{1}=H(\mathbf{x})\mathbb{S}^{1}
is an ellipse whose semi-axes
\mathbf{a}_{1}^{(1)},\mathbf{a}_{2}^{(1)}
satisfy
1\ll\|\mathbf{a}_{1}^{(1)}\|
and
1\ll\|\mathbf{a}_{2}^{(1)}\|
, where the implied constants can be chosen independently of
\mathbf{x}
. Because
\mathbf{x}\in\Delta
we see that
E_{1}
is contained in the orthogonal complement
\mathbf{x}^{\bot}
of
\mathbf{x}
. By definition,
A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})
maps
E_{1}
to an ellipse
E_{2}=A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})H(\mathbf{x})\mathbb{S}^{1}\subset\mathbf{y}^{\bot}
, where
\mathbf{y}=f^{n}(\mathbf{x})\in\Delta
. Moreover, by the definition of the singular value of a matrix, this entails that the semi-axes
\mathbf{a}_{1}^{(2)},\mathbf{a}_{2}^{(2)}
of
E_{2}
satisfy

(4.2)

\delta_{2}(A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x}))\ll\|\mathbf{a}_{1}^{(2)}\|,\quad\text{and}\quad\delta_{3}(A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x}))\ll\|\mathbf{a}_{2}^{(2)}\|,

where the implied constants can again be chosen independently of
\mathbf{x}
.
Thus it remains to show that
\|\Pi\mathbf{w}\|_{\infty}\geq\frac{1}{3}
for each
\mathbf{w}=\,^{t}(w_{0},w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathbf{y}^{\bot}
with
\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\infty}=1
. To see this note that

\displaystyle\Pi\cdot\begin{pmatrix}w_{0}\\
w_{1}\\
w_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}w_{1}-w_{0}\\
w_{2}-w_{0}\end{pmatrix}.

Assume on the contrary that
\|\Pi\mathbf{w}\|_{\infty}<\frac{1}{3}
for some
\mathbf{w}=\,^{t}(w_{0},w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathbf{y}^{\bot}
with
\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\infty}=1
.
Because
\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\infty}=1
we have
|w_{i}|=1
for at least one
i\in\{0,1,2\}
. Moreover,
\|\Pi\mathbf{w}\|_{\infty}<\frac{1}{3}
implies that
|w_{j}-w_{k}|<\frac{2}{3}
for all
j,k\in\{0,1,2\}
. Therefore, because
\mathbf{y}=\,^{t}(y_{0},y_{1},y_{2})\in\Delta
,

|y_{0}w_{0}+y_{1}w_{1}+y_{2}w_{2}|\geq|y_{0}+y_{1}+y_{2}|-\frac{2}{3}|y_{0}+y_{1}+y_{2}|=1-\frac{2}{3}=\frac{1}{3}.

This contradicts the fact that
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbf{y}^{\bot}
and, hence,
\|\Pi\mathbf{w}\|_{\infty}\geq\frac{1}{3}
for each
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbf{y}^{\bot}
with
\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\infty}=1
. Together with (
4.2
) this implies that the ellipse
E_{3}=\Pi A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})H(\mathbf{x})\mathbb{S}^{1}=D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\mathbb{S}^{1}
has semi-axes
\mathbf{a}_{1}^{(3)},\mathbf{a}_{2}^{(3)}
satisfying

\delta_{2}(A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x}))\ll\|\mathbf{a}_{1}^{(3)}\|
and
\delta_{3}(A^{(n)}(\mathbf{x}))\ll\|\mathbf{a}_{2}^{(3)}\|
,
where the implied constants can again be chosen independently of
\mathbf{x}
. This proves the statement on the singular values. By the definition of Lyapunov exponents, this implies that
\lambda_{2}(A)\leq\lambda_{1}(D)
.
∎

Let
(\Delta,f)
be an ergodic
2
-dimensional continued fraction algorithm. We relate exponential convergence of
(\Delta,f)
to the cocycle
D
as follows. Let
\mathbf{x}=\,^{t}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})\in\Delta
be arbitrary. By the mapping properties of the matrix
\Pi
on
\mathbf{y}^{\bot}
for
\mathbf{y}\in\Delta
that we discussed in the proof of Lemma
4.1
, we know that

\displaystyle\max\big\{\big\|\big({p_{i1}^{(n)}},{p_{i2}^{(n)}}\big)-{p_{i}^{(n)}}({x_{1}},{x_{2}})\big\|_{\infty}\;:\;i\in\{0,1,2\}\big\}\qquad\hbox{and}

\displaystyle\max\big\{\big\|\big({p_{i1}^{(n)}-p_{01}^{(n)}},{p_{i2}^{(n)}-p_{02}^{(n)}}\big)-(p_{i}^{(n)}-p_{0}^{(n)})({x_{1}},{x_{2}})\big\|_{\infty}\;:\;i\in\{1,2\}\big\}

are at most a factor
\frac{1}{3}
apart from each other. Thus, the criterion for exponential convergence contained in (
2.5
) is equivalent to the fact that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\big\|\big({p_{i1}^{(n)}-p_{01}^{(n)}},{p_{i2}^{(n)}-p_{02}^{(n)}}\big)-(p_{i}^{(n)}-p_{0}^{(n)})({x_{1}},{x_{2}})\big\|_{\infty}<\big(p_{i}^{(n)}\big)^{1-\alpha}

holds for each
i\in\{1,2\}
when
n
is large enough. Therefore, the uniform approximation exponent
\eta^{*}
defined in (
2.6
) can also be defined in terms of the entries of
D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})
.

According to the following proposition, the uniform approximation exponent of the Reverse algorithm can be expressed in terms of its Lyapunov exponents (see for instance
[
15
, Theorem 4.1]
whose conditions are satisfied for the Reverse algorithm according to the results that we established in Section
3
).

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\lambda_{1}(A)\geq\lambda_{2}(A)\geq\lambda_{3}(A)
be the Lyapunov exponents of the cocycle
A
of the Reverse algorithm
(\Delta,f_{\mathrm{R}})
. Then the uniform approximation exponent
\eta^{*}
of
(\Delta,f_{\mathrm{R}})
satisfies

\eta^{*}(\mathbf{x})=1-\frac{\lambda_{2}(A)}{\lambda_{1}(A)}

for almost all
\mathbf{x}\in\Delta
. In particular, if
\lambda_{2}(A)<0
then the Reverse algorithm is a.e. exponentially convergent.

In order to prove Theorem
1.4
, we have to establish a negative upper bound for
\lambda_{2}(A)
for the Reverse algorithm. By Lemma
4.1
, it suffices to provide such a bound for the first Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{1}(D)
of the cocycle
D
. Since the 1-norm is submultiplicative, the cocycle property of
D
implies that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\log{\|D^{(n+m)}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}}\leq\log{\|D^{(m)}(f^{n}\mathbf{x})\|_{1}}+\log{\|D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}}.

This subadditivity property of
D
allows us to apply the following classical result.

Proposition 4.3

(Kingman’s Subadditive Ergodic Theorem)
.

Let
T
be a measure-preserving transformation on the probability space
(\Omega,\nu)
, and let
(g_{n})_{{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}}
be a sequence of

L^{1}
-functions such that
g_{{n+m}}(x)\leq g_{n}(x)+g_{m}(T^{n}x)
(subadditivity relation). Then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{g_{n}(x)}{n}=:g(x)
holds
\nu
-a.e.,
where
g(x)
is
T
-invariant. If
T
is ergodic, then
g(x)
is constant.

Using
Proposition

4.3
and (
4.4
) we gain

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(D)=\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{n}\int_{\Delta}\log{\|D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}}d\mu(\mathbf{x}),

and, hence, the following result holds.

Lemma 4.4

(
[
13
, Theorem 3]
)
.

Let
(\Delta,f)
be an ergodic
2
-dimensional continued fraction algorithm with cocycle
A
. We have
\lambda_{2}(A)<0
if and only if there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}

such that

(4.5)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\Delta}\log{\|D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}d\mu(\mathbf{x})}<0.

We are now ready to give the proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

We split the integral in (
4.5
) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{\Delta}\log{\|D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}d\mu(\mathbf{x})}

\displaystyle=I_{1}(n)+I_{2}(n)

with

\displaystyle I_{1}(n)=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}\Delta(i^{n})}\log{\|D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}d\mu(\mathbf{x})},\quad I_{2}(n)=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\Delta\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}\Delta(i^{n})}\log{\|D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}d\mu(\mathbf{x})}.

According to
Lemma

4.4
we have to estimate
I_{1}(n)
and
I_{2}(n)
for a suitable
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we set

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{1}(n):

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\Delta(i^{n})}\log{\|D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}}\int_{\Delta(i^{n})}d\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{n}\log{\Big(\frac{2n(n-1)}{n+1}-1\Big)}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n+1}}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n+1}}\frac{4}{\pi^{2}(1-x)(1-y)(x+y)}dydx

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{2}(n):=

\displaystyle\frac{4}{\pi^{2}n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\{1,2,3,4\}^{n}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}\{i\}^{n},\\
\max\{\log{\|D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}}\,:\,\mathbf{x}\in\Delta(w)\}>0\end{subarray}}\bigg(\prod_{j=0}^{2}\max\Big\{\frac{1}{(1-x_{j})}\,:\,\mathbf{x}\in\Delta(w)\Big\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\cdot\mathrm{Leb}(\Delta(w))\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\Delta(w)}\log{\|D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}}

\displaystyle+\frac{4}{\pi^{2}n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\{1,2,3,4\}^{n}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}\{i\}^{n},\\
\max\{\log{\|D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}}\,:\,\mathbf{x}\in\Delta(w)\}<0\end{subarray}}\bigg(\prod_{j=0}^{2}\min\Big\{\frac{1}{(1-x_{j})}\,:\,\mathbf{x}\in\Delta(w)\Big\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\hskip 147.95424pt\cdot\mathrm{Leb}(\Delta(w))\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\Delta(w)}\log{\|D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}}.

The sum of the integrals of the maximum of
\log{\|D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}}
on each cylinder set
\Delta(w)
,
w\in\{1,2,3,4\}^{n}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}\{i\}^{n}
, is larger than or equal to
I_{2}
. Furthermore, when the maximum of
\log{\|D^{(n)}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}}
is positive, choosing the largest density of the measure
\mu
on each cylinder yields a quantity larger than or equal to
I_{2}
; when the maximum of one is negative, selecting the smallest density of the measure
\mu
on each cylinder yields a quantity larger than or equal to
I_{2}
.
Taking this into account we conclude that
I_{j}(n)\leq\mathbb{L}_{j}(n)

for
j\in\{1,2\}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We got the following estimates by computer calculation (with floating point error handling):

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{1}(12)<0.024002,\quad\mathbb{L}_{2}(12)<-0.044610.

Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}\int_{\Delta}\log{\|D^{(12)}(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}}d\mu(\mathbf{x})<\mathbb{L}_{1}(12)+\mathbb{L}_{2}(12)<-0.020608

and the proof is finished.
∎

5.
Balancedness

In this section, we will prove the balancedness result contained in
Theorem

1.5
.
Roughly speaking we show that balance properties of a set of substitutions (in the sense of
Proposition

5.2
) lead to balance properties of the languages of the directed sequences formed by these substitutions.
We start with two results on the behavior of letter balancedness under the action of the substitutions (
1.8
) of the Reverse algorithm.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\sigma\in\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}\}
and let
(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathcal{A}^{*}\times\mathcal{A}^{*}
with
|v_{1}|=|v_{2}|
be
C
-letter balanced. If
|\sigma(v_{1})|\leq|\sigma(v_{2})|
and if
w
is a factor of
\sigma(v_{2})
with
|w|=|\sigma(v_{1})|
then the pair
(\sigma(v_{1}),w)
is
C
-letter balanced.

Proof.

By symmetry we may assume w.l.o.g. that
\sigma=\sigma_{1}
.
Because
|\sigma(v_{1})|\leq|\sigma(v_{2})|
,
C
-letter balancedness of
(v_{1},v_{2})
implies that there is
d_{1}\in\{0,\ldots,C\}
such that
|v_{1}|_{1}=|v_{2}|_{1}+d_{1}
and, hence,
|\sigma(v_{1})|+d_{1}=|\sigma(v_{2})|
. By
C
-letter balancedness of
(v_{1},v_{2})
and because
|v_{1}|=|v_{2}|
there is
d_{2}\in\{-C,\ldots,C-d_{1}\}
such that

\begin{split}|v_{1}|_{2}&=|v_{2}|_{2}+d_{2},\\
|v_{1}|_{3}&=|v_{2}|_{3}-d_{1}-d_{2}.\end{split}

By the definition of
\sigma=\sigma_{1}
this yields

\begin{split}|\sigma(v_{1})|_{1}&=|\sigma(v_{2})|_{1},\\
|\sigma(v_{1})|_{2}&=|\sigma(v_{2})|_{2}+d_{2},\\
|\sigma(v_{1})|_{3}&=|\sigma(v_{2})|_{3}-d_{1}-d_{2}.\end{split}

Because
w\prec\sigma(v_{2})
with
|w|=|\sigma(v_{2})|-d_{1}
this implies

\begin{split}|w|_{1}\leq|\sigma(v_{1})|_{1}&\leq|w|_{1}+d_{1},\\
d_{2}+|w|_{2}\leq|\sigma(v_{1})|_{2}&\leq|w|_{2}+d_{1}+d_{2},\\
-d_{1}-d_{2}+|w|_{3}\leq|\sigma(v_{1})|_{3}&\leq|w|_{3}-d_{2}.\end{split}

Since
|d_{1}|\leq C
,
|d_{2}|\leq C
, and
|d_{1}+d_{2}|\leq C
the result follows.
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
be given. If
u
is
C
-letter balanced then
\sigma_{i}(u)
is
(C+4)
-letter balanced for
i\in\{1,2,3\}
and
(C+2)
-letter balanced for
i=4
.

Proof.

First we consider the case
i\in\{1,2,3\}
and set
\sigma=\sigma_{i}
. We have to show that each pair of factors
(w_{1},w_{2})
of
\sigma(u)
with
|w_{1}|=|w_{2}|
is
(C+4)
-letter balanced. There are factors
v_{1},v_{2}
of
u
and words
a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}
of length at most
1
such that

(5.1)

w_{1}=a_{1}\sigma(v_{1})b_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad w_{2}=a_{2}\sigma(v_{2})b_{2}.

Thus
-2\leq|\sigma(v_{1})|-|\sigma(v_{2})|\leq 2
and we may assume w.l.o.g. that
|\sigma(v_{1})|\leq|\sigma(v_{2})|
. We claim that
(\sigma(v_{1}),\sigma(v_{2}))
is
(C+2)
-letter balanced. To prove this claim we distinguish two cases.

Case (i):
|v_{1}|<|v_{2}|
. In this case there exists
v_{1}^{\prime}
with
v_{1}\prec v_{1}^{\prime}\prec u
and
|v_{1}^{\prime}|=|v_{2}|
.
If
|\sigma(v_{1}^{\prime})|<|\sigma(v_{2})|
the only possibility is
|\sigma(v_{1}^{\prime})|=|\sigma(v_{1})|+1
and
|\sigma(v_{2})|=|\sigma(v_{1})|+2
. We may apply
Lemma

5.1
to see that for a factor
w
of
\sigma(v_{2})
with
|w|=|\sigma(v_{1}^{\prime})|
the pair
(\sigma(v_{1}^{\prime}),w)
is
C
-letter balanced. Because
|\sigma(v_{1})|+1=|\sigma(v_{1}^{\prime})|=|w|<|\sigma(v_{2})|=|\sigma(v_{1})|+2
, this implies that
(\sigma(v_{1}),\sigma(v_{2}))
is
(C+2)
-letter balanced.
If
|\sigma(v_{1}^{\prime})|\geq|\sigma(v_{2})|
, we may again apply
Lemma

5.1
to see that for a factor
w
of
\sigma(v_{1}^{\prime})
with
|w|=|\sigma(v_{2})|
the pair
(w,\sigma(v_{2}))
is
C
-letter balanced. Because
|\sigma(v_{1})|\leq|w|\leq|\sigma(v_{1})|+2
we may choose
w
in a way that
\sigma(v_{1})\prec w
and, hence,
(\sigma(v_{1}),\sigma(v_{2}))
is
(C+2)
-letter balanced.

Case (ii):
|v_{1}|\geq|v_{2}|
. In this case there exists
v_{2}^{\prime}
with
v_{2}\prec v_{2}^{\prime}\prec u
and
|v_{2}^{\prime}|=|v_{1}|
. Since
|\sigma(v_{1})|\leq|\sigma(v_{2})|\leq|\sigma(v_{2}^{\prime})|
, we may apply
Lemma

5.1
to see that for a factor
w
of
\sigma(v_{2}^{\prime})
with
|w|=|\sigma(v_{1})|
the pair
(\sigma(v_{1}),w)
is
C
-letter balanced. If we choose
w
in a way that
w\prec\sigma(v_{2})
it follows from
|\sigma(v_{2})|-2\leq|w|\leq|\sigma(v_{2})|
that
(\sigma(v_{1}),\sigma(v_{2}))
is
(C+2)
-letter balanced.

Summing up we proved that
(\sigma(v_{1}),\sigma(v_{2}))
is
(C+2)
-letter balanced. Thus we see from (
5.1
) that the pair
(w_{1},w_{2})
is
(C+4)
-letter balanced and the result is proved for
i\in\{1,2,3\}
.

It remains to deal with the case
i=4
. We have to show that each pair of factors
(w_{1},w_{2})
of
\sigma_{4}(u)
with
|w_{1}|=|w_{2}|
is
(C+2)
-letter balanced. There are factors
v_{1},v_{2}
of
u
with
|v_{1}|=|v_{2}|
and words
a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}
with
|a_{1}|\leq 1
,
|b_{1}|\leq 1
,
|a_{2}|+|b_{2}|\leq 2
such that

(5.2)

w_{1}=a_{1}\sigma_{4}(v_{1})b_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad w_{2}=a_{2}\sigma_{4}(v_{2})b_{2}.

Thus it is enough to show that
(\sigma_{4}(v_{1}),\sigma_{4}(v_{2}))
is
C
-letter balanced.
By the definition of
\sigma_{4}
we have

|\sigma_{4}(v_{\ell})|_{1}=|v_{\ell}|_{2}+|v_{\ell}|_{3},\quad|\sigma_{4}(v_{\ell})|_{2}=|v_{\ell}|_{3}+|v_{\ell}|_{1},\quad|\sigma_{4}(v_{\ell})|_{3}=|v_{\ell}|_{1}+|v_{\ell}|_{2}\qquad(\ell\in\{1,2\}),

which implies that
|\sigma_{4}(v_{\ell})|_{j}-|\sigma_{4}(v_{\ell})|_{k}=|v_{\ell}|_{k}-|v_{\ell}|_{j}
for all
\ell\in\{1,2\}
and all
j,k\in\mathcal{A}
. Thus we see from (
5.2
) that the pair
(w_{1},w_{2})
is
(C+2)
-letter balanced because
(\sigma_{4}(v_{1}),\sigma_{4}(v_{2}))
is
C
-letter balanced.
∎

is known to be related to projection properties of its abelianization
\mathbf{l}(w)
. To state the according results we use notation from

]
. In particular, for

(5.3)

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}}:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathbf{x}-\frac{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\rangle}\mathbf{v}.

Set
\mathbf{1}=\,^{t}(1,1,1)
.
We continue with the relation between letter balancedness and projections of abelianizations.
It is an immediate consequence of the proof of

Lemma 5.3

(see

Let

(i)

Let

–

If
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is

-letter balanced then

\|\pi_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{1}}(\mathbf{l}(u))\|_{\infty}\leq C

–

\|\pi_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{1}}(\mathbf{l}(u))\|_{\infty}\leq C

\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}

2C

(ii)

Let
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 0}\in\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3},\sigma_{4}\}^{\mathbb{N}}

\mathbf{u}
. In all that follows, we will denote the associated sequence of incidence matrices by

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 0}\in\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3},\sigma_{4}\}^{\mathbb{N}}
be a directive sequence with sequence of incidence matrices
(\bar{B}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. If

\bar{B}_{{[n-3,n+3)}}=(B_{\sigma_{1}}B_{\sigma_{2}}B_{\sigma_{3}})^{2}

then
\|\pi_{\mathbf{u}_{n},\mathbf{1}_{n}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{11}{7}
and
\|\pi_{\mathbf{u}_{n},\mathbf{1}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{11}{7}
.

Proof.

Because
\bar{B}_{{[n-3,n+3)}}=(B_{\sigma_{1}}B_{\sigma_{2}}B_{\sigma_{3}})^{2}
we have, by definition,

(5.4)

\begin{split}\mathbf{u}_{n}&\in\,B_{\sigma_{1}}B_{\sigma_{2}}B_{\sigma_{3}}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{>0}=\mathop{span}\,(^{t}(4,2,1),\,^{t}(3,2,1),\,^{t}(2,1,1))_{>0},\\
\mathbf{1}_{n}&\in\,^{t}(B_{\sigma_{1}}B_{\sigma_{2}}B_{\sigma_{3}})\mathbb{R}^{3}_{>0}=\mathop{span}\,(^{t}(4,3,2),\,^{t}(2,2,1),\,^{t}(1,1,1))_{>0}.\end{split}

To prove the theorem we have to maximize
\|\pi_{\mathbf{u}_{n},\mathbf{1}_{n}}(\mathbf{x})\|_{\infty}
