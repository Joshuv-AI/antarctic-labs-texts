Breaking barriers: challenges faced by the older adults in accessing primary healthcare in a developing country
Authors: Vahid Yazdi-Feyzabadi, Mahin Sabermahani, Vahidreza Borhaninejad, Mahla Iranmanesh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGiven the growing older adults, ensuring their access to primary and basic healthcare services is crucial. Older adults face significant challenges in accessing care, making the develop and implementation of targeted programs vital. This study aims to explore accessibility barriers of the older adults to primary care in urban health centers of an area of Iran.MethodsThis study employed a qualitative content analysis approach using five focus group discussions with both service providers and selected older adults from the primary health care registration system. Data were analyzed using inductive data-driven coding and conventional content analysis.ResultsFour categories and ten sub-categories emerged: accessibility, availability, affordability, and system management and policy. Key barriers included: (1) geographic and physical inaccessibility, particularly for those with mobility issues; (2) service limitations such as insufficient specialist care, lack of home visits, and mismatch between services and older adults’ needs; (3) high out-of-pocket costs, even for insured individuals; and (4) mismanagement in coordination and planning, workforce burnout, poor interdepartmental coordination, and inadequate specialized training. Older adults also expressed limited awareness of services and mistrust in public systems. Healthcare providers highlighted system-level issues, such as weak information systems and a lack of supportive infrastructure. The perspectives of both groups revealed deep alignment in perceived barriers.ConclusionsAddressing the multi-level barriers faced by older adults in accessing primary care requires coordinated reforms in service delivery, financing, human resources, and infrastructure. Key interventions include investment in public transportation, expansion of home-based healthcare services, and adjusting healthcare center operating hours and staffing schedules to better accommodate the needs of older individuals. Additionally, providing daily mental health services with experienced professionals, rather than only occasional availability could, enhance care quality. These targeted strategies are essential for responding to the unique and complex needs of the elderly population and ensuring equitable access to primary healthcare in resource-constrained settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06712-z.

Background

Aging is a biological process that begins in the fourth decade of life, gradually reducing an individual’s physical, mental, social, and overall functional capacities [1]. In Iran, the aging population is increasing at a particularly rapid rate. By 2050, people aged 60 and older will account for 31% of Iran’s population, around 29 million, nearly four times the level in 2015. Iran is expected to become the second fastest-aging country in the world after South Korea. The ongoing demographic shift is expected to generate substantial challenges for strategic health system planning and policy development [2]. Considering the accelerating increase in the older population, ensuring equitable and timely access to comprehensive, high-quality preventive services and primary healthcare for older adults constitutes an essential imperative for health policymakers and practitioners [3–5].

Older adults are among the most costly users of healthcare systems and health insurance providers, mainly due to chronic and underlying health conditions [3, 6, 7]. As people age, there is a greater demand for medical services, care, and medication [3, 8, 9]. While many older adults seek healthcare at hospitals and medical centers, some face significant barriers to access due to illness, disability, limited mobility, or living in isolation [10, 11]. Access to healthcare services for the older adults is a significant public health challenge. This issue can lead to delayed diagnoses, lack of timely screening and treatment, increased healthcare costs, and reduced quality of life for older individuals [4, 12, 13].

In Iran, the healthcare delivery system is structured at three levels: national, university-based, and district levels. At the national level, the Ministry of Health and Medical Education (MoHME) oversees healthcare. Universities and faculties of medical sciences operate at the provincial level, while the district health network is the smallest independent unit within the healthcare system, covering comprehensive health service centers. These centers, which can be urban, rural, or urban-rural, handle referrals related to prevention, care, and targeted diseases, both communicable and non-communicable [14]. These facilities provide various services, including dietary regulation (nutritional counseling and dietary planning), mental health, social health support, addiction counseling, oral and dental health, developmental disorders, environmental and occupational health, and management of the catchment area’s overall health. They also supervise health posts and health houses in their coverage areas [15–17].

One of the ongoing initiatives at these centers is the Integrated Care for Older Adults Program, which focuses on early detection, risk assessment, and appropriate care [18]. However, despite the prioritization of older adults’ care in national health policies, actual service coverage remains insufficient. Many elderly individuals either do not attend these centers or do so irregularly, often without systematic follow-up. Evidence indicates that the provision of health services for older adults is inadequate [19]. Therefore, understanding the barriers that hinder older adults’ access to primary healthcare, from both the perspectives of older individuals and healthcare providers, is crucial for informing timely interventions to enhance service delivery. Despite existing programs, significant gaps remain in comprehensively identifying these barriers. This study aims to fill this gap by exploring the challenges older adults face in accessing healthcare services in comprehensive health centers within one of Iran’s largest metropolitan areas.

Context of primary health care and aging in Iran

In Iran, primary healthcare is structured through a hierarchical system that caters to both rural and urban populations. The implementation of an integrated healthcare program for the older adults commenced as a national pilot in Zarand county in 2010, expanding to Ravar county at the non-physician level by 2012. Subsequently, training for this program extended to all university-covered counties, excluding Kerman, from 2014, with nationwide rollout occurring in early 2015.

Since 2017, the integrated healthcare program for the older adults has been integrated into the SIB system, acting as a tool for service providers. This integration ensures that comprehensive care for the older adults is available across all service units, adhering to standardized service packages outlined by the SIB system. Concurrently, the “Integrated Care for Older Adults Health” service package has been implemented in medical universities nationwide, involving both non-physician and physician caregivers.

A nationwide screening of the older adults, identified as a vulnerable group, was conducted in November-December 2020. This initiative aimed to categorize the older adults based on their level of susceptibility within the community, distinguishing between very high, high, moderate, low, and minimal risk individuals. In Kerman, 10.75% of older adult’s individuals are covered by university services, with the county boasting nine urban comprehensive health service centers, thirteen urban-rural comprehensive health service centers, and ten rural centers.

In 2023, non-physician caregivers provided care for 26.9% of the older adults in Kerman. When older adults visit community health workers (CHWs) in rural areas or family health caregivers (FHCs) in urban settings, they receive specialized integrated care packages tailored for the older adults. These caregivers assess various health conditions and conduct early diagnosis screenings based on guidelines outlined in the integrated care package. Evaluation criteria include risk factors, disease symptoms, and diagnostic tests, with further comprehensive diagnostic procedures employed when necessary [20].

Methods

Study design

This study utilized a qualitative design with content analysis approach to identify and explore the views of older adults and healthcare providers regarding barriers to accessing public health, primary care, and preventive services within the context and framework of Iran’s primary healthcare system. Qualitative content analysis is an established research method that enables the systematic and contextual interpretation of textual data through a structured process of coding, categorization, and theme development [21].

Participants and recruitment

In 2023, five focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted in Kerman, the capital city of Kerman Province, which is the largest province in Iran. Kerman, a major city in southeast Iran, had an estimated urban population of 683,900 according to the Iran Statistics Center in 2022.

Older adults and healthcare providers participated in separate FGDs to capture distinct perspectives relevant to the study. Older adults, as healthcare recipients, provide insights about their experiences and barriers to care, while providers offer professional views on service delivery and challenges. Separating the groups creates a comfortable space for open discussion, minimizing bias and enhancing the richness and authenticity of the data.

Four FGDs involved older adults (n = 40), defined as individuals aged 60 years and above, who were purposefully selected to ensure maximum variation in terms of age, gender, marital status, and healthcare utilization history recorded in the SIB system. Integrated Health Record System, locally known as SIB (Samaneh-ye Yekparche-ye Behdasht), an electronic platform launched by Iran’s MoHME to manage primary healthcare data across the country. This system is the primary health care information system in Iran for recording primary healthcare, public health, and preventive care services, covering for the entire population. Recruitment letters were sent to four relevant urban comprehensive health service centers involved in the study, and all of them responded positively and cooperated in facilitating participant recruitment.

One FGD involved healthcare providers (n = 13), including physicians, midwives, nurses, infrastructure and allied health professionals working in primary care settings, as well as infrastructure specialists from network development and information technology (IT) units. All were purposively selected based on their experience and role in healthcare delivery.

Older adults and healthcare providers were purposefully selected using a maximum variation strategy. Older adults and healthcare providers were purposefully selected using a maximum variation strategy. For the older adults’ group, participants were chosen based on two main criteria: first, individuals aged 60 and above; second, elderly individuals whose information was registered in the SIB system but who either did not visit healthcare facilities at all or did not engage in regular follow-up care as recommended by preventive care programs. All older adults were recruited via phone invitation, with permission from affiliated urban comprehensive health services centers and health posts. The older adult participants included 25 females and 15 males, with a mean age of 69.0 years.

For the providers’ group, participants were purposively selected to ensure maximum variation in their educational and professional backgrounds. The group comprised 13 experts, including three general practitioners (GPs), and six professionals from diverse fields including nutrition, mental health, midwifery, nursing, and family health, all affiliated with urban comprehensive health services centers and their subordinate health posts. Additionally, the group included two experts specializing in aging health, one expert from the network development unit, and one information technology (IT) specialist.

Data collection

FGDs were chosen as the data collection method to elicit and explore perspectives from multiple stakeholders, as well as their interactions and collective development of ideas. Each FGD with the older adults included participants from various geographic areas, encompassing a wide range of socio-economic statuses. This diversity allowed for a wide range of perspectives among different socio-economic groups. Similarly, healthcare providers participating in a single FGD were selected from different urban comprehensive health services centers and their affiliated health posts, serving populations with diverse socio-economic backgrounds. Recruitment letters were sent to all providers working in these urban comprehensive health services centers and their affiliated health centers.

All FGDs were conducted in urban comprehensive health service centers by three trained researchers (VYF, MI, and MS). VYF served as the moderator of the sessions, MI acted as the note-taker, and MS was responsible for coordinating the focus group discussions.

A semi-structured interview guide was used to focus the discussions on barriers to initial access, continuity of visits, and any negative experiences related to primary and preventive healthcare. Separate guides were developed for older adults and healthcare providers. For older adults, questions explored their awareness of available services, reasons for irregular or missed visits, and perceived obstacles to care. After one FGD were completed, the guide was slightly revised to incorporate a hypothetical scenario: participants were asked how they would plan and deliver preventive services if they were in charge of a local health center. This modification aimed to elicit deeper insights into their perceived priorities, system gaps, and community-oriented solutions.

The interview guide for healthcare providers focused on their professional experiences in delivering services to older adults, challenges in outreach and follow-up, and organizational barriers within the health system. Both guides were reviewed by experts in primary healthcare and geriatrics, and minor revisions were made based on pilot feedback. The interview guides used for this study has been developed specifically for this research and is uploaded as a supplementary file. All sessions were audio-taped and transcribed verbatim into typed transcripts using Microsoft Word 2016. Each session lasted 90 to 135 min, with an average duration of 110 min. The four focus group discussions with older adults included 10, 5, 12, and 13 participants, lasting 90, 105, 120, and 135 min, respectively. The single provider FGD involved 13 participants and lasted 110 min.

Data analysis

The data analysis process followed a conventional content analysis approach [22], employing an inductive, data-driven approach guided by Graneheim and Lundman’s framework in a five-step process. Initially, FGDs were digitally recorded and transcribed verbatim. Subsequently, all transcripts were imported into MAXQDA Analytics Pro 2018 (VERBI GmbH Release 18.2.0 Berlin) to facilitate simultaneous data processing and analysis. The transcripts underwent multiple readings to identify meaningful units within the text. These meaning units were then abstracted and assigned codes, which were subsequently organized into subcategories and categories based on conceptual and semantic similarities. Through comparisons and contrasts among the codes, overarching themes were derived, representing the key findings of the study. This inductive coding approach ensured a comprehensive analysis and interpretation of the qualitative data, enhancing the rigor and reliability of the study’s findings. Given the two groups of participants, healthcare providers and aging care recipients, both common and divergent themes from healthcare providers and recipients were analyzed to capture shared and differing perspectives.

Trustworthiness

Throughout the data analysis process, rigorous steps were taken to enhance the trustworthiness and reproducibility of the research findings, aligning with Lincoln and Guba’s criteria for trustworthiness. Credibility was ensured through a meticulously designed research approach, which included well-defined criteria for purposeful and maximum variation sampling, interviews, and text analysis procedures. Additionally, considerable time was dedicated to each focus group discussion (FGD) to foster trust and empathy between the elders and researchers. Immediate transcription of audio-taped FGDs and thorough immersion in data coding and analysis after each session ensured a comprehensive representation of participants’ voices. This process extended over six months from February to August 2023, providing prolonged engagement and allowing for a deeper understanding of the data.

Researcher triangulation further bolstered credibility, with a diverse four-member research team, comprising experts in health policy, health management, gerontology, and managing supervisor in older adults’ primary care services, collaborating during both data collection and analysis phases. Consensus was reached on interpretations through discussions among team members, and evocative descriptions were crafted to vividly capture the experiences of healthcare providers and aging service recipients.

Provider member-checking was employed to validate analytic categories, interpretations, and conclusions drawn from the transcribed content. A negative case analysis was also conducted to scrutinize deviant cases, potentially refining and confirming emerging patterns from the data. Confirmability was addressed through an audit trail documenting critical analysis decisions and interpretations. Independent coding by two researchers, followed by in-person reconciliation of any discrepancies and discussions with a third researcher to achieve consensus on all codes, further ensured the reliability of the analysis. Alternative explanations for the findings were explored by revisiting the data.

Finally, transferability was enhanced by providing detailed descriptions of the findings and the context of primary healthcare in Iran, specifically focusing on older adults’ preventive care services. These thick descriptions enable others to assess the applicability of the findings to different contexts.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Kerman University of Medical Sciences with registration ID IR.KMU.REC.1402.492. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants before each session. Prior to each focus group discussion, verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants after providing them with a full explanation of the study objectives, data collection procedures, and their right to withdraw at any time without any consequences. Participation was entirely voluntary. All interviews were conducted in a private and respectful environment, ensuring confidentiality and anonymity. No identifiable personal information was recorded or stored. Audio recordings and transcripts were securely stored and only accessible to the research team. Given the inclusion of older adults, additional care such as boredom, tiredness, and any discomfort related to psychological well-being or physical mobility was taken to ensure that participants clearly understood the purpose of the research and felt comfortable sharing their perspectives.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

The four focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted with older adult participants from four different areas, each representing varied socio-economic and cultural backgrounds. The results of the study revealed that, approximately two-thirds of the older adult participants in the FGDs were female. Moreover, more than half of the older adult participants were widowed, having lost their spouses. The majority of participants had an education level below a diploma or possessed low educational attainment, with only about one-fifth having achieved an academic education level.

Regarding employment status, about half of the older adult participants were homemakers, meaning they were primarily responsible for unpaid domestic and caregiving tasks, while over one-third were retired, receiving a pension. Merely 15% were still employed. Notably, around 80% of the older adults reported having a caregiver assisting them with activities of daily living. Approximately one-fifth of the older adults lived alone, while about two-thirds resided with their spouses. Those living with children were the least represented group.

Challenges faced by the older adults in accessing primary healthcare in a developing country

Category one: accessibility

Participants identified various dimensions of accessibility that hinder older adults’ ability to engage with primary healthcare services. These included geographical proximity, time-related convenience, availability of essential services, physical infrastructure and age-friendly adaptations, as well as access to medicines, equipment, and basic medical supplies. Both older adults and providers described overlapping concerns, though their emphasis sometimes differed. They emphasized that access to services should be simple, direct, and fair for all, especially for the elderly and other vulnerable groups.

Geographical accessibility

Geographical accessibility for the older adults concerns the proximity or travel time from their residence to essential healthcare facilities, which significantly affects their well-being. As people age, their reliance on healthcare and community resources increases. Both older adults and providers reported that the physical distance between older adults’ homes and comprehensive health service centers and health posts, combined with poor transport options, and poor facility visibility, posed major challenges to access. A healthcare provider stated:“… I have trouble moving around because of leg pain and getting transport isn’t easy. It’s really hard to get to health centers, especially out here in the suburbs where it takes forever to get anywhere.” (FGD1-P4).

“… I have trouble moving around because of leg pain and getting transport isn’t easy. It’s really hard to get to health centers, especially out here in the suburbs where it takes forever to get anywhere.” (FGD1-P4).

One of the key challenges faced by older adults when traveling from their homes to urban healthcare centers is limited transportation options that restrict their mobility. A male older adult noted that:“… Not everyone like me can easily walk to the health centers. Some places are just too crowded, and it’s really hard for both private and public transport to get through. I wish the centers were closer or that there were better transportation options available for our older adults like us.” (FGD3-P5).

“… Not everyone like me can easily walk to the health centers. Some places are just too crowded, and it’s really hard for both private and public transport to get through. I wish the centers were closer or that there were better transportation options available for our older adults like us.” (FGD3-P5).

Time accessibility

Time accessibility poses significant challenges for older adults accessing comprehensive health service centers and health posts as identified by older adults and healthcare providers through four distinct issues: no scheduled programs for service provision, inappropriate working hours, conflicts between older adults’ daily commitments and center hours, and congestion leading to lengthy waiting times.

Older adults highlighted the absence of scheduled programs tailored specifically for older adults as a discouraging their attendance at comprehensive health service centers and health posts. One female older adult emphasized:“… As an older person, with all the daily stuff like taking care of my grandkids and managing the house, and sometimes my kids aren’t around to help, I really wish they’d set specific days and times just for us. That way, we could plan ahead and make sure we get the care we need without all the hassle.” (FGD2- P1).

“… As an older person, with all the daily stuff like taking care of my grandkids and managing the house, and sometimes my kids aren’t around to help, I really wish they’d set specific days and times just for us. That way, we could plan ahead and make sure we get the care we need without all the hassle.” (FGD2- P1).

A primary healthcare provider also stated that:" We lack a fixed schedule for older adults with reserved care days. Their irregular visits mean mental health professionals or nutritionists are often unavailable. Despite efforts to set specific appointment days, older adults don’t always follow them consistently.” (FGD5-P9).

" We lack a fixed schedule for older adults with reserved care days. Their irregular visits mean mental health professionals or nutritionists are often unavailable. Despite efforts to set specific appointment days, older adults don’t always follow them consistently.” (FGD5-P9).

Healthcare providers, especially those working extended hours at these centers, commonly experience occupational burnout. This study underscored that burnout caused by prolonged working hours is perceived by providers as a barrier to older adults’ access to health services. One of the healthcare providers stated:“If you look at our centers, about five to six staff members handle the workload every year. This results in burnout for the clinic manager, and I can’t find the energy to allocate time for the staff anymore because I feel exhausted.” (FGD5-P7).

“If you look at our centers, about five to six staff members handle the workload every year. This results in burnout for the clinic manager, and I can’t find the energy to allocate time for the staff anymore because I feel exhausted.” (FGD5-P7).

In this regard, many older adults believed that conflicts between their daily activities and the operating hours of healthcare centers, particularly the busy morning shift, pose substantial obstacles. Since comprehensive health service centers and health posts typically operate during morning shift or standard business hours, this schedule may not align with older adults’ other responsibilities. One female older adult declared that:“The services available to the older adults mostly happen during standard office hours. Many older adults like me need assistance to attend, often relying on companions or caring for their grandchildren. However, their children are usually busy with work during those hours, so the older adults end up prioritizing childcare duties over getting healthcare services.” (FGD1-P5).

“The services available to the older adults mostly happen during standard office hours. Many older adults like me need assistance to attend, often relying on companions or caring for their grandchildren. However, their children are usually busy with work during those hours, so the older adults end up prioritizing childcare duties over getting healthcare services.” (FGD1-P5).

Another female older adult stated:“I really wish I could get services in the afternoon too, because my duties in the morning keep me from going to the center when it’s open.” (FGD4-P8).

“I really wish I could get services in the afternoon too, because my duties in the morning keep me from going to the center when it’s open.” (FGD4-P8).

Additionally, from the perspective of older adults, long wait times and overcrowding are especially challenging due to mobility issues and other health problems. Service providers also recognized this factor as a key barrier. An older adult stated that:“At times, you visit the center, and it’s packed, forcing you to wait hours for a simple blood pressure test. It’s just not worth our time to wait around for these services.” (FGD3-P3).

“At times, you visit the center, and it’s packed, forcing you to wait hours for a simple blood pressure test. It’s just not worth our time to wait around for these services.” (FGD3-P3).

A healthcare provider also highlighted that:“We serve different groups of care recipients, and we try to follow an appointment system. But often, due to overcrowding and the high number of people seeking care, it’s hard to respond to everyone promptly within a short time.” (FGD5-P6).

Service accessibility

Access to healthcare services for older adults is often restricted by physical, cognitive, and socio-economic barriers. In this study, participants, both older adults and healthcare providers, identified seven key challenges undermining access to care: (1) limitations in service provision to disabled older adults, (2) insufficient availability of laboratory, pharmaceutical, and dental services, (3) limited access to specialist physicians, (4) misalignment between service packages and the needs of older adults, and (5) inadequate provision of preventive services before old age.

Both older adults and healthcare providers frequently mentioned that physical disabilities in result of multi-morbidities and chronic conditions, such as visual loss and mobility impairments, hinder older adults from attending healthcare centers. Additionally, older adults living alone expressed heightened challenges and health concerns, often feeling vulnerable due to limited support from family members.

One male older adult expressed that:“Services like checking blood pressure at home should be available because some older adults just can’t make it to the center on their own. For example, my wife can’t walk even ten meters without my help since her diabetes has affected her eyesight. She can’t even recognize me from close up. Lots of other older women have the same problem and can’t go to the centers alone.” (FGD1, P7).

“Services like checking blood pressure at home should be available because some older adults just can’t make it to the center on their own. For example, my wife can’t walk even ten meters without my help since her diabetes has affected her eyesight. She can’t even recognize me from close up. Lots of other older women have the same problem and can’t go to the centers alone.” (FGD1, P7).

From the providers’ perspective, the insufficient availability of home care services further exposes these individuals to an increased risk of hospitalization.

A primary healthcare provider stated:“… Out of 250 older adults who could use home care, only about 5–10% are currently receive these services. We need teams to provide in-home support.” (FGD5-P3).

“… Out of 250 older adults who could use home care, only about 5–10% are currently receive these services. We need teams to provide in-home support.” (FGD5-P3).

Another healthcare provider also declared that:“A person who is 78 years old, with 25% kidney function, diabetes, injects insulin, and suffers from high blood pressure cannot go to a health center. A team should visit such individuals in their neighborhoods and provide care on-site.” (FGD5, P2).

“A person who is 78 years old, with 25% kidney function, diabetes, injects insulin, and suffers from high blood pressure cannot go to a health center. A team should visit such individuals in their neighborhoods and provide care on-site.” (FGD5, P2).

Healthcare providers expressed concern over the lack of essential supportive services, such as laboratory tests, pharmacies, medication counseling, and dental care, within the centers. One of the primary healthcare providers expressed that:“It is necessary to include lab tests in the aging service package or have a pharmacy inside the center so that older adults can receive their medications immediately. Previously, we had a referral form for lab tests, but the program was canceled.” (FGD5, P1).

“It is necessary to include lab tests in the aging service package or have a pharmacy inside the center so that older adults can receive their medications immediately. Previously, we had a referral form for lab tests, but the program was canceled.” (FGD5, P1).

Another healthcare provider also declared that:“Older adults don’t receive proper medication counseling at these centers, so important details like how often to take vitamin D or other medications are often missed, leading to medication errors.” (FGD5, P7).

“Older adults don’t receive proper medication counseling at these centers, so important details like how often to take vitamin D or other medications are often missed, leading to medication errors.” (FGD5, P7).

Some older adult participants noted the absence of specialist physicians in most health centers, which limited the quality and comprehensiveness of care for older adults. They emphasized that older adults often require specialized care under the supervision of a specialist physician due to mobility issues and various illnesses. One female older adult stated that:“Health posts lack specialists. I believe specialist physicians provide more comprehensive services and give you what is needed for your health, like blood pressure or diabetes checks or vaccinations.” (FGD 2, P 6).

“Health posts lack specialists. I believe specialist physicians provide more comprehensive services and give you what is needed for your health, like blood pressure or diabetes checks or vaccinations.” (FGD 2, P 6).

Several older adults indicated that current service packages do not reflect their medical and functional needs. While general services like vaccinations and nutritional counseling are available, more advanced care, such as minor surgeries or cardiology services, is lacking. One male older adult noted that:“Older adult individuals have contributed significantly to this society, dedicating 30 years of their lives, and they deserve proper care. The services provided to them do not align with their needs. Many older adult individuals now suffer from high blood pressure, and many have devices. The center only provides two supplement pills, checks blood pressure, and the needs of the older adults are different. If the government has a service package for this group, it should be tailored to their needs and conditions.” (FGD 2, P1).

“Older adult individuals have contributed significantly to this society, dedicating 30 years of their lives, and they deserve proper care. The services provided to them do not align with their needs. Many older adult individuals now suffer from high blood pressure, and many have devices. The center only provides two supplement pills, checks blood pressure, and the needs of the older adults are different. If the government has a service package for this group, it should be tailored to their needs and conditions.” (FGD 2, P1).

Some older adults perceived health centers as being primarily focused on maternal and child care, leading them to avoid seeking services for themselves. One male older adult expressed that:“As a 60-year-old person, I’ve never felt these centers are for me. I think they’re for children and pregnant mothers—because that’s how it was when I was younger.” (FGD3, P1).

“As a 60-year-old person, I’ve never felt these centers are for me. I think they’re for children and pregnant mothers—because that’s how it was when I was younger.” (FGD3, P1).

Primary healthcare providers highlighted the insufficient implementation of preventive interventions during earlier life stages, particularly for conditions prevalent among older adults, such as cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and age-related disorders. They noted that many chronic conditions could be prevented if preventive services were more effectively targeted toward younger and middle-aged populations. One primary healthcare provider declared that:“Old age is not the time to start prevention. It’s better to deliver these services earlier so people adopt healthier lifestyles before they age. Unfortunately, the preventive services for our youth are weak.” (FGD5, P1).

“Old age is not the time to start prevention. It’s better to deliver these services earlier so people adopt healthier lifestyles before they age. Unfortunately, the preventive services for our youth are weak.” (FGD5, P1).

Physical accessibility and adaptation

This sub-category captures the physical and infrastructural barriers that hinder older adults’ ability to access primary healthcare centers. Physical accessibility to healthcare services is essential for ensuring timely and effective medical care. Findings from both older adults and primary healthcare providers indicate challenges related to unfriendly facility design, poor building maintenance, inadequate space or privacy, and insufficient communication access. While both groups emphasized similar concerns, healthcare providers tended to focus more on systemic infrastructure and operational shortcomings, whereas older adults focused on the direct impacts of poor physical conditions on their care experiences. Participants frequently mentioned that healthcare centers lacked elder-friendly architectural features. Buildings often resembled residential homes, had multiple staircases, or lacked ramps and elevators. This made physical navigation particularly difficult for older adults with mobility issues. These challenges can lead to delays in care, decreased patient satisfaction, and increased healthcare costs. One male older adult stated:“Upon entering these centers, they do not evoke a sense of being healthcare facilities… For example, one center has stairs that make it hard for older adults to enter.” (FGD 4, P 1).

“Upon entering these centers, they do not evoke a sense of being healthcare facilities… For example, one center has stairs that make it hard for older adults to enter.” (FGD 4, P 1).

In this regard, also one of the community health workers stated that:“… The building should be constructed to meet the needs of older adults, not in a way that they cannot enter.” (FGD5-P11).

“… The building should be constructed to meet the needs of older adults, not in a way that they cannot enter.” (FGD5-P11).

Primary healthcare providers expressed concerns about deteriorating infrastructure, including some worn-out buildings, unsafe physical conditions, and outdated designs that did not meet service standards. To address building deterioration and improve service quality, regular maintenance and repairs are essential. Additionally, ensuring adequate and up-to-date equipment and technologies, along with attention to proper building design and safety measures, is crucial. One of the general physicians remarked that:“Some centers are so old and worn out that they don’t have the conditions of a comprehensive center… not safe nor properly structured.” (FGD 5, P4).

“Some centers are so old and worn out that they don’t have the conditions of a comprehensive center… not safe nor properly structured.” (FGD 5, P4).

Several healthcare providers highlighted a lack of sufficient space to accommodate multiple care functions such as examination, education, and rehabilitation. Overcrowded rooms compromised both care quality and patient privacy.“Physical space is very inappropriate and really small. It’s so cramped that I can’t even put two beds in one room.” (FGD5, P2).

“Physical space is very inappropriate and really small. It’s so cramped that I can’t even put two beds in one room.” (FGD5, P2).

Older adults also expressed reluctance to receive sensitive services, particularly mental health consultations, in shared or public areas, citing discomfort and fear of stigma. One female older adult declared that:“There was no private place to talk to the mental health expert. People were around, and I didn’t want others to know I had come for such services. So, I left without saying anything.” (FGD3, P4).

“There was no private place to talk to the mental health expert. People were around, and I didn’t want others to know I had come for such services. So, I left without saying anything.” (FGD3, P4).

Another barrier identified was the lack of effective communication infrastructure, particularly telephone systems. Providers explained that they faced difficulties contacting older adults, while older participants reported not receiving follow-up calls or outreach. One of the healthcare providers stated that:“We now have problems with the phone; for example, we have a telephone line that is really unresponsive.” (FGD5, P7).

“We now have problems with the phone; for example, we have a telephone line that is really unresponsive.” (FGD5, P7).

Another primary healthcare provider stated:“They should have their phones and addresses, which they probably do, but they don’t call… There should be a way to contact those who cannot visit.” (FGD5, P10).

“They should have their phones and addresses, which they probably do, but they don’t call… There should be a way to contact those who cannot visit.” (FGD5, P10).

Access to medicines, equipment, and medical supplies

This sub-category highlights the logistical and operational limitations that reduce the effectiveness of primary healthcare service delivery. Older adults and healthcare providers jointly identified critical issues such as shortages in essential medications, malfunctioning or outdated equipment, and inadequacy in basic medical supplies needed for diagnosis and treatment.

Older adults reported repeated experiences of not receiving their prescribed medications due to supply issues. Providers noted frequent stockouts, especially for essential chronic disease medications. These shortages lead to care disruption, delayed treatment, and added out-of-pocket expenses. One male older adult remarked that:“My distress is the non-availability of medications for this group… I had to buy half of my prescription on my own because the pharmacies said they were out.” (FGD1, P5).

“My distress is the non-availability of medications for this group… I had to buy half of my prescription on my own because the pharmacies said they were out.” (FGD1, P5).

Both groups raised concerns about the inadequacy of medical tools. Providers emphasized that many centers were equipped with outdated or poorly calibrated instruments, which affected diagnostic accuracy and undermined trust. Additionally, the quantity of essential equipment was often insufficient for the size of the population served, leading to delays in care and service interruptions. One of the healthcare providers highlighted:“Our devices, both blood pressure monitors and scales, are not calibrated… This creates errors in the charts and reduces trust.” (FGD5, P5).

“Our devices, both blood pressure monitors and scales, are not calibrated… This creates errors in the charts and reduces trust.” (FGD5, P5).

Another provider also declared that:“The equipment in the centers is not proportional to the population… If a scale breaks, there is no replacement that day.” (FGD5, P8).

“The equipment in the centers is not proportional to the population… If a scale breaks, there is no replacement that day.” (FGD5, P8).

Category two: availability

The category of availability encompasses system-level and provider-side limitations that affect the reach, consistency, and quality of services delivered to older adults. This section presents three sub-categories based on data from older adults and primary healthcare providers as follows:

Health communication and cultural awareness

This sub-category relates to informational and sociocultural barriers that influence older adults’ awareness and trust in primary healthcare services. Barriers in this sub-category relate to a lack of awareness about available services, cultural misunderstandings, and community mistrust. Older adults expressed uncertainty about the existence or eligibility for services, while providers acknowledged difficulties in health promotion and outreach.

Older adults frequently reported unawareness of available services or eligibility for them. Providers acknowledged this issue, attributing it to weak outreach strategies.“I’ve lived here for years but didn’t know there was a health center nearby. No one ever told me.” (FGD3, P2).

“I’ve lived here for years but didn’t know there was a health center nearby. No one ever told me.” (FGD3, P2).

Another adult older also stated that:“Many still think these centers are only for children and mothers. We, who don’t have small children, thought of checking if they also serve the older adults. As a result, we were not informed.” (FGD4, P3).

“Many still think these centers are only for children and mothers. We, who don’t have small children, thought of checking if they also serve the older adults. As a result, we were not informed.” (FGD4, P3).

Providers observed that preventive care was not prioritized by either the health system or the older population. One of primary healthcare providers expressed:“They only go when they’re sick. Prevention doesn’t seem important to them.” (FGD5, P3).

“They only go when they’re sick. Prevention doesn’t seem important to them.” (FGD5, P3).

Another provider also remarked that:“Preventive healthcare is not prioritized in our system. I know a seventy-seven-year-old who has had diabetes for over 20 years, and yet no doctor ever explained to them the risks of kidney damage or vision loss. We wait for complications to appear instead of educating people in advance.” (FGD5, P7).

“Preventive healthcare is not prioritized in our system. I know a seventy-seven-year-old who has had diabetes for over 20 years, and yet no doctor ever explained to them the risks of kidney damage or vision loss. We wait for complications to appear instead of educating people in advance.” (FGD5, P7).

Healthcare providers also believed that many older adults often overlook preventive services, perceiving healthcare as meaningful only when it involves treatment or medication. In this regard, one of the general physicians stated that:“Older adults mainly visit for treatment when they are ill and don’t pay attention to prevention. Our services are preventive, but they only come to us when they’re already sick and don’t see our services as real care.” (FGD2, P3).

“Older adults mainly visit for treatment when they are ill and don’t pay attention to prevention. Our services are preventive, but they only come to us when they’re already sick and don’t see our services as real care.” (FGD2, P3).

Both groups noted a shift toward private clinics, often due to mistrust in the public sector. Older adults attributed this to prior negative experiences in service quality, while providers cited systemic distrust due to inadequate equipment and technology, and an inability to meet community needs.

One of the older adults declared:“We prefer private centers because they offer better care. For example, a local clinic was known for prioritizing older adults and provided excellent, attentive service.” (FGD4, P2).

“We prefer private centers because they offer better care. For example, a local clinic was known for prioritizing older adults and provided excellent, attentive service.” (FGD4, P2).

Another older adult stated:“My neighbor told me not to go there—said they don’t treat older people well.” (FGD4, P4).

“My neighbor told me not to go there—said they don’t treat older people well.” (FGD4, P4).

One of the healthcare providers remarked that:“People just don’t trust the government system anymore. They’d rather pay more at a private clinic than deal with the confusion at public centers.” (FGD5, P1).

“People just don’t trust the government system anymore. They’d rather pay more at a private clinic than deal with the confusion at public centers.” (FGD5, P1).

Some participants noted that cultural differences and perceived discomfort in multicultural care settings may discourage some older adults from attending comprehensive health centers. In particular, concerns related to unfamiliar customs, hygiene practices, or interpersonal comfort were mentioned as reasons for avoidance. These perceptions, though not universal, suggest the need for culturally sensitive communication and strategies that promote inclusivity and trust. One of the healthcare providers stated that:“Some older adults feel uncomfortable visiting when non-Iranian clients are present. They even avoid lying on beds others have used, often due to concerns about hygiene and cultural differences” (FGD2, P1).

“Some older adults feel uncomfortable visiting when non-Iranian clients are present. They even avoid lying on beds others have used, often due to concerns about hygiene and cultural differences” (FGD2, P1).

Health information management system

This sub-category pertains to technical and functional limitations of the national electronic health record system in primary healthcare settings (SIB), as experienced by providers. Older adults did not directly reference this issue. These include incomplete or outdated population data, underutilized health data, lack of offline access, user-unfriendly information system, and poor alignment with public health guidelines.

Providers reported that many records were inaccurate as they are not updated, complicating patient tracking. Because census data and population records are not updated regularly, especially with people moving around, we often find mismatches in the system.“Some older adults show up with different phone numbers or addresses, and the system either doesn’t recognize them or lists them as first-time visitors, even though they’ve received services before.” (FGD5-P8).

“Some older adults show up with different phone numbers or addresses, and the system either doesn’t recognize them or lists them as first-time visitors, even though they’ve received services before.” (FGD5-P8).

Inadequate utilization of information in decision-making and planning in managerial levels presents multifaceted challenges in health policy formulation and implementation. Although data was available, it was underutilized due to lack of training or system constraints.“If the information in the SIB system is not used in decision-making and policy-making, it’s like driving with your eyes closed—making it difficult to predict and prevent diseases, which can ultimately lead to widespread health crises.” (FGD5, P11).

“If the information in the SIB system is not used in decision-making and policy-making, it’s like driving with your eyes closed—making it difficult to predict and prevent diseases, which can ultimately lead to widespread health crises.” (FGD5, P11).

Internet interruptions and slow speeds pose significant challenges in delivering services through health systems like the SIB system. In areas with poor internet coverage, accessing the SIB system becomes difficult, limiting its functionality and use. The lack of an offline platform, combined with frequent internet disruptions, worsens these issues by preventing access to the system when connectivity is lost, leading to service interruptions, technical problems, and increased user complaints.“We don’t have easy access to patients’ previous history in the SIB system. To see it, we have to navigate through several menus, and sometimes the internet connection is cut off. The internet is so slow that it takes a long time to open pages. When the internet disconnects, which happens frequently, the system becomes unusable and causes significant delays. (FGD5, P10)

“We don’t have easy access to patients’ previous history in the SIB system. To see it, we have to navigate through several menus, and sometimes the internet connection is cut off. The internet is so slow that it takes a long time to open pages. When the internet disconnects, which happens frequently, the system becomes unusable and causes significant delays. (FGD5, P10)

Users of the SIB system face significant challenges related to the time-consuming nature of data registration and the lack of user-friendly, customizable reports. These issues slow down workflows, especially in areas like aging care, and reduce the system’s efficiency. The inability to tailor reports to specific needs further limits usability, making it difficult for healthcare providers to retrieve meaningful information quickly. Overall, the system’s design neglects user needs, leading to dissatisfaction and decreased effectiveness in daily operations.“The SIB system is time-consuming and not user-friendly, forcing us to fill unnecessary fields that distract from patient care. It’s designed more for supervisors than for those working in the field, making information retrieval difficult. Overall, it neglects the needs of physicians and caregivers, leading to frustration and added challenges.” (FGD5, P4).

“The SIB system is time-consuming and not user-friendly, forcing us to fill unnecessary fields that distract from patient care. It’s designed more for supervisors than for those working in the field, making information retrieval difficult. Overall, it neglects the needs of physicians and caregivers, leading to frustration and added challenges.” (FGD5, P4).

Guidelines and protocols for follow-up care and service packages are occasionally updated, but the SIB system does not always reflect these updates promptly. This delay negatively impacts the quality of care and service delivery. The system’s incompatibility with the latest guidelines creates discrepancies and dissatisfaction among users, highlighting the need to improve the updating process and better integrate current protocols into the system.“The SIB system is not up to date, and the guidelines say one thing, while the SIB system says another. This creates contradictions, and during supervision, the inspector may criticize by saying, ‘I am providing care based on the system.” (FGD5, P2).

“The SIB system is not up to date, and the guidelines say one thing, while the SIB system says another. This creates contradictions, and during supervision, the inspector may criticize by saying, ‘I am providing care based on the system.” (FGD5, P2).

Human resources capacity and readiness

This sub-category includes barriers stemming from inadequate staffing, training, and provider-patient relationships. These challenges significantly affect the quality and accessibility of services for older adults. While providers focused on organizational and workload issues, older adults emphasized interpersonal aspects. From the providers’ perspective, the primary concerns centered around structural and organizational issues such as insufficient training, heavy workloads, lack of specialized staff, and poor job conditions. Providers highlighted how these systemic constraints limit their capacity to deliver comprehensive and effective care for older adults. In contrast, older adults emphasized interpersonal aspects, particularly the quality of communication and respect they receive during care encounters. They expressed a need for providers to demonstrate empathy, listen attentively, and communicate in an age-sensitive manner.

Providers reported a lack of formal education and preparation specific to geriatric care, often learning skills informally on the job. Older adults, meanwhile, noticed that providers often failed to communicate appropriately with their age group, sometimes treating them in a condescending manner. Training in comprehensive health centers was often repetitive, outdated, and lacked effective evaluation methods, leading to poor engagement and limited impact. Providers struggled to communicate appropriately with older adults and faced challenges tailoring recommendations, particularly nutritional advice, to the financial realities of elderly patients. Additionally, the absence of organizational socialization processes for new staff reduced their understanding of community needs and weakened the effectiveness of care delivery.

One of the health providers declared that:“There’s no specific training for elderly care. We just learn on the job.” (FGD5, P6).

“There’s no specific training for elderly care. We just learn on the job.” (FGD5, P6).

In this regard, one of the older adults stated:“They don’t know how to talk to older people. They treat us like we’re children.” (FGD4, P9).

“They don’t know how to talk to older people. They treat us like we’re children.” (FGD4, P9).

Training programs were described as repetitive and outdated, failing to respond to the real demands of geriatric care. Providers noted a lack of feedback and follow-up to update their skills.“They train us, yes, but it’s always the same outdated material. No feedback, no follow-up.” (FGD5, P7).

“They train us, yes, but it’s always the same outdated material. No feedback, no follow-up.” (FGD5, P7).

Providers highlighted a disconnect between training content and the environmental needs based on lived realities of patients, particularly those facing financial hardship. While nutrition counseling is offered, they emphasized that recommendations often fail to translate into practice due to patients’ limited purchasing power.“We need to learn how to tailor our nutritional counseling to different socioeconomic conditions. For example, when we talk to an unemployed older adult who can’t afford meat or dairy, we should be able to suggest affordable alternatives that still meet their dietary needs. Otherwise, our advice isn’t practical.” FGD5, P1)

“We need to learn how to tailor our nutritional counseling to different socioeconomic conditions. For example, when we talk to an unemployed older adult who can’t afford meat or dairy, we should be able to suggest affordable alternatives that still meet their dietary needs. Otherwise, our advice isn’t practical.” FGD5, P1)

There was a notable shortage of full-time specialized staff, particularly clinical psychologists and nutritionists. While some limited access to these specialists exists through comprehensive health centers, the primary care bases themselves do not have dedicated full-time specialists. These specialists serve multiple subordinate bases within the centers, resulting in limited and indirect access for older adults.“We don’t have a nutritionist or psychologist assigned. It’s all left to the general staff. (FGD5-P2)

“We don’t have a nutritionist or psychologist assigned. It’s all left to the general staff. (FGD5-P2)

In this regard, one of the older adults declared that:“I need to talk to a psychology specialist and get counseling. Private clinics are very expensive, so I want to receive the service here, but they say the specialist is not available full-time.” (FGD2, P4).

“I need to talk to a psychology specialist and get counseling. Private clinics are very expensive, so I want to receive the service here, but they say the specialist is not available full-time.” (FGD2, P4).

Older adults frequently raised concerns about the lack of empathy and respectful communication from providers. They expressed a desire to be listened to and treated with dignity rather than being rushed or dismissed.“They don’t look at us. They just say ‘go here, go there.’ We want someone to listen, not just check a box.” (FGD2, P3).

“They don’t look at us. They just say ‘go here, go there.’ We want someone to listen, not just check a box.” (FGD2, P3).

Another older adult stated that:“Some center staff lack the skills to interact with the older adults, and this interaction may discourage the older adults from seeking services on subsequent visits” (FGD2, P1).

“Some center staff lack the skills to interact with the older adults, and this interaction may discourage the older adults from seeking services on subsequent visits” (FGD2, P1).

Providers described overwhelming caseloads that affect both the quality of care they can offer and their own morale, making it difficult to provide focused attention on complex older adult needs. The high population density assigned to healthcare providers and physicians generates substantial workload pressures, limiting the time available per patient. They reported an increase in patient load per physician from 4,000 to 6,000 individuals, further intensifying the workload challenge.“You can’t expect one provider to cover general care, mental health, and nutrition in one shift for all age groups.” (FGD, P2).

We train them, and just as they settle in, they’re gone. It’s especially hard in remote areas.” (FGD5-P5).

“Most staff are here for their two-year service, then they leave. We train them, and just as they settle in, they’re gone. It’s especially hard in remote areas.” (FGD5-P5).

Job insecurity, lack of benefits, and inadequate pay were cited as major factors diminishing providers’ motivation and commitment to their roles.“Why stay in a job with no contract, no benefits, and low pay?” (FGD5, P5).

Category three: affordability

Affordability was identified as a major barrier to healthcare access, particularly for older adults, many of whom experience declining income post-retirement. This sub-category reflects both provider and recipient perspectives. Older adults mainly focused on the burden of out-of-pocket payments for medications, visits, and transportation. Even when services were technically available, indirect costs often rendered them inaccessible.

Healthcare providers highlighted the socioeconomic vulnerability of their older clients and the structural limitations of the health system, particularly inadequate insurance coverage and the lack of free or subsidized services in public centers, as critical impediments to care.

While service packages exist, their utilization remains suboptimal due to unaffordable associated costs, such as medications not fully covered by basic insurance or the inability to pay for transportation to clinics. In some cases, older adults opt for social insurance clinics, which provide a more financially accessible alternative due to free visits and medication. One of the healthcare providers stated that:“Some of the older adults under our coverage really have financial problems and cannot afford the medical costs. They even have problems affording their medications. We had an older adult person who didn’t have money for transportation costs to come to the clinic, even if all services were provided to him for free. Sometimes they say, can we receive a package of food!” (FGD5, P5).

“Some of the older adults under our coverage really have financial problems and cannot afford the medical costs. They even have problems affording their medications. We had an older adult person who didn’t have money for transportation costs to come to the clinic, even if all services were provided to him for free. Sometimes they say, can we receive a package of food!” (FGD5, P5).

A male older adult declared that:“I couldn’t afford to buy the full dose of Plavix 75 prescribed for a year, even though it was covered by insurance. I always had to settle for purchasing only half the amount because I didn’t have enough money.” (FGD1, P5).

This includes a free visit, and then medication is also provided for free. Perhaps this is one reason why individuals don’t have much interest in coming to comprehensive health service centers.” (FGD5, P3).

Category four: system management and policy

Planning and coordination

Effective health system management relies on strategic planning, timely policy implementation, and strong coordination across units. However, participants, particularly healthcare providers, highlighted systemic shortcomings that hinder efficient and responsive service delivery. These challenges are not rooted in individual capacities, but rather in structural and managerial limitations. Recurring issues included top-down planning without local consultation, delays in implementing national guidelines, fragmented responsibilities across departments, inadequate infrastructure for new programs, and weak operational follow-through.

While providers mainly emphasized managerial inefficiencies, such as lack of clear direction, poor coordination between departments, and insufficient readiness before program roll-out, older adults indirectly experienced these gaps in the form of inconsistent access, reliance on external clinics (e.g., social insurance), and limited responsiveness of the system.

Providers described that many initiatives are designed centrally with limited input from frontline staff, resulting in systems and programs that are poorly adapted to local needs. One of the healthcare providers stated that:“In the design of the system, there was no consultative approach because neither the medical section is suitable based on it, nor is the physical activity section. It seems that during the design, the approach was from the ministry levels towards the centers. But it would have been better to have a bottom-up approach based on environmental needs.” (FGD5, P2).

“In the design of the system, there was no consultative approach because neither the medical section is suitable based on it, nor is the physical activity section. It seems that during the design, the approach was from the ministry levels towards the centers. But it would have been better to have a bottom-up approach based on environmental needs.” (FGD5, P2).

Participants raised concerns about the slow and inconsistent dissemination of MohME guidelines, which affects program clarity and compliance.“We need to look for solutions to ensure timely dissemination of guidelines because these delays are not beneficial to the system at all. They lead to confusion, slow down the process, reduce efficiency, and sometimes result in non-compliance with new guidelines, creating legal problems.” (FGD5, P4).

This includes a free visit, and then medication is also provided for free. Perhaps this is one reason why individuals don’t have much interest in coming to comprehensive health service centers.” (FGD5, P3).

Discussion

The findings of this study shed light on multiple, interrelated barriers limiting older adults’ access to primary healthcare services, highlighting the need for comprehensive strategies to improve equity in access and quality of care. The barriers identified accessibility, service availability, affordability, and system management and policy can interact and compound, intensifying the difficulties faced by older adults seeking timely care. They also reflect broader patterns seen in similar low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) with constrained healthcare infrastructure.

Geographical accessibility emerged as a significant obstacle, particularly for older adults living in suburban and rural areas, due to long distances to healthcare facilities. Results from a study conducted in Iran similarly indicate that, despite overall improvements, significant portions of the elderly population still lack adequate access to nearby health centers, particularly in certain regions where geographic barriers and limited transportation options persist [23]. A study in Bangladesh similarly found that long distances to healthcare centers left many older adults without accessible services in their communities [24]. Moreover, results of a systematic review found that most studies found that patients living farther from healthcare facilities had worse health outcomes, though a few reported the opposite or no link. Overall, distance may negatively impact health and should be considered in planning healthcare locations [25]. Limited home care services, telecare, and transportation constraints further worsen these challenges. Prior research has shown that geographical barriers can significantly affect the health and well-being of older adults [26, 27]. Another study indicates that living in areas with more social and healthcare resources leads to better mental and physical health outcomes for older adults. In the area of geographical access, a key concern was the limited availability of home care services, which many older adults considered essential for meeting their needs. Home-based primary care provides ongoing, team-based primary care services at home. It helps bring essential care to people who cannot easily visit clinics, such as homebound older adults, by shifting primary care from outpatient settings to their homes [28]. To make these services affordable, they should be covered by health insurance. As a study conducted in United Sates concluded that although many older adults could pay for two years of home care by selling all their assets, those with serious disabilities often cannot, and future cost increases may make home care even less affordable [29]. Addressing these geographical barriers requires targeted interventions such as improving transportation options and increasing the availability of home care services, especially for older adult individuals living alone.

Time accessibility was identified as another critical issue, with challenges including the lack of scheduled programs for service provision and extended working hours of healthcare centers. A qualitative study conducted in Iran also highlighted that time-related access barrier significantly affects healthcare utilization [30]. In Iran, incompatible center hours with daily routines and caregiving responsibilities prevent older adults from attending, and many expressed a desire for scheduled, organized services. Ensuring timely and convenient access is crucial for older patients, as studies indicate that improving the timeliness of care and minimizing delays can lead to better health outcomes and well-being in older adults. Similarly, a review of healthcare access for older Australians found that accommodation issues, such as long waiting times to see general practitioners or specialists, pose a significant barrier for the elderly seeking care [31]. To enhance time accessibility, it is recommend extending or optimizing service hours to better fit older adults’ schedules and addressing provider burnout through workforce management and support, ensuring sufficient staffing and rested providers to reduce wait times.

Service accessibility refers to the availability of appropriate healthcare services for older adults, and our findings revealed several gaps. Participants highlighted the limited provision of specialized geriatric services and a mismatch between the standard service packages and the actual needs of older patients. Preventive services such as routine screenings and specialized chronic disease management were also reported as inadequately covered. These challenges are consistent with other research which health systems in many countries are not yet well adapted to the complex, chronic needs of an aging population, tending to focus on single diseases rather than comprehensive person-centered care [27]. In Iran, for example, studies identified the lack of tailored national programs for elderly care, absence of standard care plans, and “undiscovered” chronic conditions in older people as major issues, essentially confirming gaps in preventive and specialized services [32, 33]. Addressing service accessibility requires customizing healthcare offerings to the unique needs of older adults. This might include establishing more supportive geriatric clinics and trained gerontology teams, expanding preventive care, and ensuring service packages are aligned with older adults’ expectations.

Physical accessibility, the ability of older adults to reach and use healthcare facilities, is another critical dimension. Our study found that poorly designed or maintained facilities (e.g., buildings with structural deterioration, lack of ramps or elevators, and insufficient clinic space) create significant barriers for older patients. Another study conducted in Iran concluded that some defects including the lack of resources and facilities (the shortage of employees; lack of suitable physical space and sufficient medical equipment; lack of sufficient budget al.location) act as barriers to receive integrated care [34]. Improving physical accessibility will require concerted efforts in health system planning and design: ensuring that clinics are elder-friendly (e.g., wheelchair accessible, with clear signage and appropriate waiting areas), keeping buildings well-maintained, and providing adequate space to accommodate assistive devices and caregivers.

Access to essential medications, medical equipment, and supplies emerged as a serious concern in our study. Participants noted that older patients often could not obtain the medications they needed, and that some clinics were equipped with outdated or insufficient medical devices, impeding proper care. These issues are commonly reported in other studies as well [32, 33]. For example, a narrative review of medication use in older adults identified accessibility problems – alongside non-adherence and polypharmacy – as one of the main medication-related challenges for seniors [35]. Addressing these gaps will require proactive measures by healthcare authorities: ensuring an adequate and steady supply of essential medications at clinics and pharmacies that serve older populations, updating medical equipment in healthcare centers (e.g., providing functional blood pressure monitors, glucometers, physiotherapy apparatus, etc.), and possibly subsidizing or covering the costs of critical supplies for low-income elderly patients.

Effective healthcare for older adults depends not only on services and infrastructure, but also on the competencies of healthcare providers. Our study uncovered several challenges in the training and development of the healthcare workforce. Participants pointed out that much of the training content for staff was overly repetitive and not sufficiently tailored to geriatric care needs. They also noted deficiencies in how training programs were evaluated and a lack of coherence in the training process, for example, inconsistent curricula and little integration across different professional groups. An Iranian study of nurses, for instance, reported an educational gap in geriatric care, citing a lack of training on principles of older adult care in nursing curricula and insufficient on-the-job training in how to communicate with and care for elderly patients [36]. On a broader scale, a recent scoping review on community health workers (CHWs) and interprofessional education found that building on existing knowledge and skills is key to accelerating workforce development [37]. The review suggests that applying a “fit-for-purpose” approach – essentially customizing training based on what health workers already know and what competencies they need, can fast-track the ability of CHWs to participate in interprofessional teams. Our findings underscore the importance of strengthening training programs through up-to-date gerontology courses, improved evaluation and feedback mechanisms, and ensuring that all levels of staff, including doctors, nurses, and community health workers, are adequately prepared.

Financial constraints are a significant barrier that surfaced in our study, particularly affecting older adults of low socioeconomic status or those with inadequate health insurance coverage. Even when services exist, many seniors cannot access them due to out-of-pocket costs. Affordability is frequently cited as a problem for older patients, often exacerbated by the fact that they have multiple co-existing conditions requiring numerous healthcare visits and medications [31]. Concurrent studies in Iran have similarly found that many services ostensibly available to older people go underutilized because of cost and coverage gaps [33, 34]. Addressing affordability challenges requires a multi-pronged approach, including the expansion of insurance coverage and the implementation of financial assistance programs for low-income older adult individuals.

Strong stewardship and leadership are essential for a well-functioning healthcare system, especially when coordinating services for a demographic group with complex needs like older adults. Participants noted delays in the dissemination and implementation of clinical guidelines for elder care, as well as weak collaboration between different departments and sectors involved in providing services to older people. These issues resonate with broader analyses of health system governance in Iran. A recent qualitative study on Iran’s elderly care system identified the absence of a single empowered stewardship body for elder care and a lack of integration across various levels of care (from primary health centers to specialized services) as major challenges [38]. Strengthening these leadership structures can address systemic weaknesses; experts argue that better governance is urgently needed to reform elder care in Iran.

This study had several limitations. First, because it was conducted in only one district in Iran, the findings may not be generalizable to other areas. Although primary healthcare services for older adults in Iran are mostly centralized and similar across regions, differences in socio-cultural, economic, and healthcare system factors in other locations could affect access to care in different ways. However, using a constructivist approach allowed us to explore this case in depth and provide a detailed description that can help transfer findings to similar contexts. Second, since this study relied on face-to-face FGDs, older adults with severe mobility issues or multiple serious health conditions could not participate. Also, older adults who were not registered in the SIB system, often due to moving within cities, were not included. Still, by selecting participants from different socio-economic backgrounds who interact with other older adults, we believe the barriers identified reflect a range of perspectives. Future research should include older adults with severe disabilities to better understand their unique challenges. Using FGDs with both healthcare providers and older adults provided valuable insights into their experiences and views. This approach helped us gain a deeper and more complete understanding of the barriers faced by older adults in accessing primary healthcare services. Another potential limitation is that some participants may have adjusted their responses during focus groups because of social or cultural expectations. To reduce this risk, researchers emphasized confidentiality, created a comfortable discussion environment, and encouraged participants to share their honest experiences and opinions.

Conclusions

This qualitative study identified four main categories—accessibility, availability, affordability, and system management and policy, comprising multiple sub-categories that together illustrate the diverse and interconnected barriers faced by older adults in accessing primary healthcare services. Older adults reported substantial challenges related to geographical distance, inadequate transportation, inappropriate service hours, long waiting times, unfriendly physical environments, and insufficient communication or awareness of available services. These barriers were compounded by financial hardships, limited insurance coverage for essential medications, and high out-of-pocket costs, which often restricted their ability to seek timely care.

From the perspective of healthcare providers, inadequate human resources capacity, poor financial incentives, outdated or ineffective training, and shortcomings in the health information management system emerged as primary obstacles to delivering effective care. System-level issues, including fragmented planning, delays in guideline dissemination, insufficient infrastructure, and weak interdepartmental coordination, further undermined efforts to provide integrated, patient-centered services for older adults.

Addressing these multifaceted barriers will require comprehensive strategies: investing in accessible and age-friendly transportation, expanding service availability through flexible hours and home-based care, strengthening insurance coverage to reduce financial burdens, enhancing specialized training for providers, and improving data systems for better planning and responsiveness. These findings provide valuable insights for policymakers, planners, and health system leaders to design targeted interventions that can advance equitable, effective, and dignified care for the growing older adult population in Iran and comparable low- and middle-income settings.
