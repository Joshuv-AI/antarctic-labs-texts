Matrix Control Barrier Functions
Pio Ong, Yicheng Xu, Ryan M. Bena, Faryar Jabbari, Aaron D. Ames

Abstract
This paper generalizes the control barrier function framework by replacing scalar-valued functions with matrix-valued ones. Specifically, we develop barrier conditions for safe sets defined by matrix inequalities—both semidefinite and indefinite. Matrix inequalities can be used to describe a richer class of safe sets, including nonsmooth ones. The safety filters constructed from our proposed matrix control barrier functions via semidefinite programming (CBF-SDP) are shown to be continuous. Our matrix formulation naturally provides a continuous safety filter for Boolean-based control barrier functions, notably for disjunctions (OR), without relaxing the safe set. We illustrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework with applications in drone network connectivity maintenance and nonsmooth obstacle avoidance, both in simulations and hardware experiments.



I
Introduction

Dynamic safety is increasingly recognized as essential in modern control systems alongside stability. While convergence to the equilibrium is important, it is equally critical that safety constraints are respected throughout the entire trajectory. Control barrier functions (CBFs)
[
1
,
2
]
have emerged as an effective framework for enforcing safety during system evolution. A key advantage of CBFs is their integration into the safety filters
[
3
]
as constraints in a quadratic program (QP), which facilitates the integration of multiple safety and stability requirements and enables fast online computation. The success of CBFs coincided with the rise of real-time QP solvers. Today, with advances in semidefinite program (SDP) solvers and computational hardware, it becomes practical to move beyond scalar-valued barrier functions and define richer safe sets via matrix inequalities, motivating the development of
matrix control barrier functions
(MCBF).

The original CBF framework relies on Nagumo’s theorem (see
[
4
]
or
[
5
, Ch. 4.2]
) to ensure forward invariance of safe sets. To use the result, safety-critical controllers must render the closed-loop dynamics locally Lipschitz. This requirement motivated substantial research into the regularity of safety filters derived from the QP formulation, which is the most common form of CBF controller. For single-constraint QPs, it is simple to guarantee (local) Lipschitz continuity by examining closed-form solutions, while for multiple-constraint QPs, continuity and Lipschitz property have been studied using tools from set-valued analysis and parametric optimization
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. Recently, the work
[
10
]
compiles different sufficient conditions, such as the linear independence constraint qualification (LICQ), which guarantees continuity and Lipschitz properties for QP-based safety filters.

In contrast, the modern CBF framework does not rely on Nagumo’s theorem. Instead, it requires that the CBF condition hold in a neighborhood of the safe set, enabling safety guarantees from continuous safety filters. This relaxation is particularly important when extending the control formulation beyond QPs. While the most prominent form of CBF-based controllers relies on the QP formulation, the use of other convex optimization classes has also emerged. For instance, measurement-robust CBFs
[
11
]
yield barrier constraints that require second-order cone programs (SOCPs) to formulate control laws. The matrix-valued CBFs proposed in this paper are enforced via SDPs, where Lipschitz continuity guarantees are even more difficult to establish.

Matrix inequalities provide a natural way to describe nonsmooth safe sets, including those defined by Boolean combinations of constraints. Due to their complexity, work on nonsmooth safe sets is relatively sparse. The paper
[
12
]
formalizes a nonsmooth barrier function framework that offers barrier conditions for verifying forward invariance. The work was motivated by the natural nonsmoothness arising from combining multiple CBFs into one via Boolean operations, both for conjunction (AND) and disjunction (OR). Unfortunately, it does not provide a controller construction method for control systems.
Specifically for Boolean-based CBFs,
[
13
]
proposes using soft-min and soft-max functions to enable the formulation of safety filters with continuity properties. This approach, however, conservatively alters the safe set.

The motivation of this work arises from the problem of connectivity maintenance in multi-robot systems. In such settings, CBFs are especially attractive because they allow the integration of a connectivity constraint alongside other objectives without requiring a complete co-design of the underlying control law. Approaches for connectivity maintenance can be categorized into local and global. Local approaches
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
address the problem by reasoning about the initial network configuration; on the other hand, global approaches
[
17
,
18
]
rely on algebraic graph theory, where the Fiedler eigenvalue
[
19
,
20
]
must remain positive at all times to ensure connectivity. Unfortunately, eigenvalues are nonsmooth functions of matrices, complicating the design of continuous control laws. Our prior work
[
18
]
attempted to resolve this issue by carefully designing the safety filter constraints to ensure continuity, drawing on nonsmooth analysis tools. In contrast, the matrix CBF proposed herein offers a simpler systematic approach to designing safety filters to address these nonsmooth connectivity constraints.

Statement of Contributions:
This paper develops the general framework of
matrix control barrier functions
. We formulate safety constraints described by matrix-valued functions rather than scalar ones, investigating both semidefinite and indefinite cases. To this end, we develop barrier conditions that ensure set forward invariance and their counterparts for control systems that ensure control invariance. We propose optimization-based controllers via semidefinite programming (CBF-SDP) and provide the technical analysis of their continuity properties and the bounds they enforce.

Compared to existing approaches, the proposed framework offers several important advantages. Throughout the paper, we investigate the special case with diagonal matrices and discuss how MCBFs can address Boolean compositions of multiple safety constraints. MCBFs directly handle the nonsmooth nature of the problem without resorting to soft-min or soft-max approximations that conservatively relax the original safe set. In particular, the framework elegantly offers the CBF-SDP solution for disjunctive combinations of safety constraints, which was not previously possible. In connectivity maintenance problems, where prior work relied on nested optimization schemes to address nonsmoothness, our approach formulates the problem as a single SDP, simplifying both analysis and implementation. We validate our MCBF framework by applying the proposed results on the connectivity maintenance problem, both in simulation and experiment.

II-B

Barrier Condition

Consider the nonlinear autonomous system:

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}\in\real^{n}
is the system state and
\mathbf{F}:\real^{n}\rightarrow\real^{n}
is the system dynamics. For
safety
, we are interested in verifying that the state trajectories
t\rightarrow\mathbf{x}(t)
remain inside a safety constraint defined as a sublevel set of the function
\psi:\real^{n}\rightarrow\real
:

\mathcal{S}=\big\{\mathbf{x}\in\real^{n}\;|\;\psi(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\big\},

(2)

at all times. In other words, we want the set
\mathcal{S}
to be forward invariant.

Definition 1
.

(Forward Invariance):

A set
\mathcal{C}\subset\real^{n}
is
forward invariant
for system (
1
) if, for initial conditions
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
, all system trajectories
t\rightarrow\mathbf{x}(t)
remain inside the set
\mathcal{C}
for all time
t\geq 0
. The set
\mathcal{C}
is
safe
if it is also a subset of the safety constraint
\mathcal{S}
.
\diamond

The idea behind a safe set is that it provides a safe operating region, from which the system can be initialized without violating the safety constraint. In the barrier function framework, we construct the safe set using a continuously differentiable function
h:\real^{n}\rightarrow\real
as:

\mathcal{C}=\big\{\mathbf{x}\in\real^{n}\;|\;h(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\big\},

(3)

typically with
h(\mathbf{x})\geq\psi(\mathbf{x}),\forall\mathbf{x}
to ensure
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{S}
. Forward invariance, on the other hand, can be established using the barrier condition.

Lemma 1
.

(Barrier Condition):

Consider the autonomous system (
1
) with a continuous dynamics
\mathbf{F}
and the set
\mathcal{C}
in (
3
). If there exists a locally Lipschitz function
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
such that:

L_{\mathbf{F}}h(\mathbf{x})\geq-\alpha(h(\mathbf{x}))

(4)

for all
\mathbf{x}
in an open neighborhood
\mathcal{E}\supset\mathcal{C}
, then set
\mathcal{C}
is forward invariant for the system.
\blacksquare

We adopt the above version of barrier conditions from
[
12
]
because it only requires the system dynamics to be continuous, rather than locally Lipschitz.
This is made possible by requiring the barrier condition to hold over an open neighborhood set
\mathcal{E}
, instead of
\mathcal{C}
, so that the result does not need to rely on Nagumo’s theorem. This distinction is particularly relevant for control systems.

II-C

Control Barrier Function

We consider the nonlinear control-affine system:

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}

(5)

where
\mathbf{x}\in\real^{n}
is the system state,
\mathbf{u}\in\real^{m}
is the control input,
\mathbf{f}:\real^{n}\rightarrow\real^{n}
is the system drift, and
\mathbf{g}:\real^{n}\rightarrow\real^{n\times m}
is the control matrix.

For control systems, the control input
\mathbf{u}
provides additional flexibility for maintaining safety of the system. Analogous to forward invariance for autonomous system, we rely on the concept of control invariance.

Definition 2
.

(Control Invariance):

A set
\mathcal{C}
is (forward)
control invariant
for system (
5
) if, for any initial condition
\mathbf{x}(0)\in\mathcal{C}
, the system trajectories
t\rightarrow\mathbf{x}(t)
can be maintained inside the set
\mathcal{C}
using some corresponding control input
t\rightarrow\mathbf{u}(t)
. The set
\mathcal{C}
is
safe
if it is also a subset of the safety constraint
\mathcal{S}
.
\diamond

Building on the concept of barrier conditions, control barrier functions are useful for verifying control invariance of a set.

Definition 3
.

(Control Barrier Functions):

A continuously differentiable function
h:\real^{n}\rightarrow\real
is called a
control barrier function
(CBF) for system (
5
) if there exists a locally Lipschitz function
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
such that, for each
\mathbf{x}
in the set
\mathcal{C}
defined in (
3
), there exists a
\mathbf{u}\in\real^{m}
satisfying:

\underbrace{L_{\mathbf{f}}h(\mathbf{x})+L_{\mathbf{g}}h(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}}_{\dot{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})}>-\alpha(h(\mathbf{x})).

(6)

The idea behind CBFs is that they ensure the feasibility of designing a controller to satisfy (
4
). However, the safety result from Lemma
1
additionally requires that the controller be continuous and that the barrier condition be satisfied at all points in some neighborhood
\mathcal{E}
outside of
\mathcal{C}
. To this end, the modern definition of a CBF uses a strict inequality, as in (
6
), to facilitate the design of controllers that satisfy these condition.

The safety filter framework is one of the main features of CBFs. With CBFs
\{h_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p}
, we may construct an optimization-based controller to simultaneously satisfy all constraints:

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})=\argmin_{\mathbf{u}\in\real^{m}}\quad

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{k}_{\operatorname{d}}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}

(7)

s.t.

\displaystyle\dot{h}_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\geq-\alpha(h_{i}(\mathbf{x})),~\forall i\in[p]

where
\mathbf{k}_{\operatorname{d}}:\real^{n}\rightarrow\real^{m}
is a desired continuous controller, without considering safety. The controller (
7
) effectively chooses the control input
\mathbf{u}
closest to the desired value
\mathbf{k}_{\operatorname{d}}(\mathbf{x})
while respecting the safety constraints given by the CBFs.

If the optimization is feasible

(Lipschitz controllers)

III
Semidefinite Matrix Constraints

We introduce matrix-valued barrier functions that enable a richer representation of safety constraints. Rather than using a scalar-valued function, we construct the safe set via a continuously differentiable matrix-valued function
\mathbf{H}:\real^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{p}
:

\mathcal{C}=\big\{\mathbf{x}\in\real^{n}\;|\;\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\succeq 0\big\}

(8)

To handle matrix barrier functions, we first introduce the following notation for the entry-wise Lie derivative matrix
\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{H}:\real^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{p}
along a vector field
\mathbf{F}
as:

[\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{H}]_{ij}(\mathbf{x})=L_{\mathbf{F}}H_{ij}(\mathbf{x}),\forall i,j\in[p].

Using this notation, we present a barrier condition that maintains matrix semidefiniteness as follows.

Proposition 1
.

(Exponential Semidefinite Matrix Barrier Condition):

Consider the autonomous system (
1
) with a continuous vector field
\mathbf{F}
and the set
\mathcal{C}
in (
8
). If the following barrier condition holds with a positive constant
c_{\alpha}>0
:

\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\succeq-c_{\alpha}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})

(9)

for all
\mathbf{x}
in an open neighborhood
\mathcal{E}\supset\mathcal{C}
, then set
\mathcal{C}
is forward invariant for the system.

Proof.

For any given vector
\mathbf{v}\in\real^{p}
, we define an auxiliary function:

\xi_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{v}

(10)

Letting
t\rightarrow\mathbf{x}(t)
be any solution from an initial condition
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(\xi_{\mathbf{v}}\circ\mathbf{x})(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{v}^{\top}\frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf{H}\circ\mathbf{x})(t)\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}(t))\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle\geq-c_{\alpha}\mathbf{v}^{\top}(\mathbf{H}\circ\mathbf{x})(t)\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=-c_{\alpha}(\xi_{\mathbf{v}}\circ\mathbf{x})(t)

where we have used the matrix inequality (
9
) to derive the inequality for all time
t
such that
\mathbf{x}(t)\in\mathcal{E}
.

Suppose there exists a solution
\mathbf{x}(t)
starting from
\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
yet
\mathbf{x}(t^{*})\not\in\mathcal{C}
at some time
t^{*}\geq 0
. Then there exists
\mathbf{v}^{*}
such that
\xi_{\mathbf{v}^{*}}(\mathbf{x}(t^{*}))<0
. By continuity of the solution, there exists a time interval
[t_{\partial\mathcal{C}},t_{\mathcal{E}}]\subseteq[t_{0},t^{*}]
such that
\xi_{\mathbf{v}^{*}}(\mathbf{x}(t_{\partial\mathcal{C}}))=0
and
\xi_{\mathbf{v}^{*}}(\mathbf{x}(t))<0
for all
t\in(t_{\partial\mathcal{C}},t_{\mathcal{E}}]
which, by the definition of th positive definiteness, indicates
\mathbf{x}(t)\in\mathcal{E}\setminus\mathcal{C},\forall t\in(t_{\partial\mathcal{C}},t_{\mathcal{E}}]
. However, the inequality above suggests
\xi_{\mathbf{v}^{*}}\circ\mathbf{x}
must be increasing during time
t\in(t_{\partial\mathcal{C}},t_{\mathcal{E}})
, which is a contradiction since
\xi_{\mathbf{v}^{*}}(\mathbf{x}(t_{\mathcal{E}}))<0=\xi_{\mathbf{v}^{*}}(\mathbf{x}(t_{\partial\mathcal{C}}))
.
∎

Definition 4
.

(Exponential MCBF):

A continuously differentiable function
\mathbf{H}:\real^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{p}

exponential matrix control barrier function
(exponential MCBF) for system (

c_{\alpha}>0
such that, for each
\mathbf{x}
in the set
\mathcal{C}
defined in (
8

\mathbf{u}\in\real^{m}
satisfying:

\underbrace{\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{f}}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{g}_{i}}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})u_{i}}_{\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})}\succ-c_{\alpha}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}).

(11)

where
\mathbf{g}_{i}
is the

\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})
.
\diamond

The definition of MCBFs with a strictly positive definite condition helps ensure the existence of a continuous controller, establishing safety of the set

Theorem 1
.

(Safety from exponential MCBF):

Consider the control-affine system (
5
) and the set
\mathcal{C}
in (
8
). If
\mathbf{H}
is an exponential MCBF, then set
\mathcal{C}
is control invariant. In particular, the CBF-based semidefinite programming (CBF-SDP) safety filter:

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})=\argmin_{\mathbf{u}\in\real^{m}}\quad

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{k}_{\operatorname{d}}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}\hfill

s.t.

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\succeq-c_{\alpha}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})

(12)

is continuous for all
\mathbf{x}
in some neighborhood
\mathcal{E}
of
\mathcal{C}
. Consequently, the state-feedback
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})
renders set
\mathcal{C}
forward invariant for the closed-loop system.

Proof.

Define the set-valued map:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\big\{\mathbf{u}\in\real^{m}\;|\;\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})+c_{\alpha}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\succeq\mathbf{0}\big\}

\displaystyle=\big\{\mathbf{u}\in\real^{m}\;|\;\phi_{1}\big(\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})+c_{\alpha}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\big)\geq 0\big\}.

where
\phi_{1}
is the function returning the minimum eigenvalue of a given matrix. Since the matrices are entry-wise continuously differentiable and the eigenvalue function
\phi_{1}
is globally Lipschitz (see e.g.,
[
16
, Thm. 1]
and
[
21
, Thm. 2.4]
), the resulting function
\phi_{1}\circ(\dot{\mathbf{H}}+c_{\alpha}\mathbf{H})
is continuous. As such, not only does the map have a nonempty interior for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}
from the definition of MCBF, but it also has a nonempty interior on some neighborhood
\mathcal{E}\supset\mathcal{C}
from continuity. Hence, we conclude the map
\mathcal{U}
is lower semicontinuous on
\mathcal{E}
because it has a nonempty interior, is convex-valued, and the function
\phi_{1}\circ(\dot{\mathbf{H}}+c_{\alpha}\mathbf{H})
defining it is continuous, see
[
22
, Lem. 5.2]
. After substituting
\Delta\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{k}_{\operatorname{d}}(\mathbf{x})
as a new variable, we have a minimal selection formulation:

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})=\argmin_{\mathbf{u}\in\real^{m}}\quad

\displaystyle\|\Delta\mathbf{u}\|^{2}\hfill

s.t.

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(\dot{\mathbf{H}}\big(\mathbf{x},\Delta\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{k}_{\operatorname{d}}(\mathbf{x}))+c_{\alpha}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\big)\geq 0.

Here, the constraint remains lower semicontinuous, convex-valued, and closed-valued. Therefore, from
[
9
, Prop. 2.19]
, its minimal selection on a Euclidean space is continuous for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{E}
, implying
\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})=\Delta\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{k}_{\operatorname{d}}(\mathbf{x})
is also continuous, as desired.

Forward invariance of the closed-loop system
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})
follows from Lemma
1
, and control invariance follows from the existence of the control signal
t\rightarrow\mathbf{u}(t)=\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}(t))
, concluding the proof.

III-A

Spectral Analysis

For the standard CBF condition (
4
), we can derive an exponential bound on the evolution of
h
along the trajectory. This occurs when
\alpha(h(\mathbf{x}))=c_{\alpha}h(\mathbf{x})
is chosen as a linear function with
c_{\alpha}>0
. That is, we have
h(\mathbf{x}(t))\geq h(\mathbf{x}_{0})e^{-c_{\alpha}t}
from an initial condition
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\real^{n}
. Incidentally, we also refer to our MCBF in (
11
) as exponential, despite
\mathbf{H}
not being scalar-valued. This is motivated by how the evolution of its eigenvalues can be similarly exponentially bounded.

For each
\mathbf{x}\in\real^{n}
,
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})
is a symmetric matrix and thus has real eigenvalues. Formally, we use
\phi_{j}:\mathbb{S}^{p}\rightarrow\real
to denote the function mapping a symmetric matrix
\mathbf{H}
to its
j
-th smallest eigenvalue. At the same time, we use
\lambda_{j}:\real^{n}\rightarrow\real
for the state-dependent eigenvalue function through a function composition:
\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{j}(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}))
. Without loss of generality, we define
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
so that they are in an ascending order:

\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{x})\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{x}),~\forall\mathbf{x}\in\real^{n}.

(13)

We note importantly that the function
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
is not differentiable at points where the eigenvalue is not simple. Therefore, we rely on nonsmooth analysis to bound its rate of change along an autonomous system (
1
) as:

\frac{d}{dt}(\lambda_{j}\circ\mathbf{x})(t)\in\partial\phi_{j}(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}(t)))\cdot\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}(t)),~a.e.~t\geq 0,

with its weak set-valued Lie derivative
[
12
, Lem. 1 and Rmk. 1]
, constructed by the generalized gradient set
\partial\phi_{j}
. Note that the left hand side is also guaranteed to be absolutely continuous. For the right hand side, we have used the nonsmooth chain rule
[
23
, Thm. 2.3.10]
for the weak set-valued Lie derivative since we know the generalized gradient of an eigenvalue of a symmetric matrix function, cf.
[
16
]
:

\partial\phi_{j}(\mathbf{H})=\text{co}\big\{\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\in\mathbb{S}^{p}\;|\;\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}=\phi_{j}(\mathbf{H})\mathbf{v},~\|\mathbf{v}\|=1\big\}.

Since the vectors
\mathbf{v}
’s defining in the set above are essentially the normalized eigenvectors associated with
\phi_{j}
, we have the following result.

Proposition 2
.

(Exponential Bound on Eigenvalues):
Consider the autonomous system (
1
) with continuous dynamics
\mathbf{F}
and the set
\mathcal{C}
in (
8
). If the matrix barrier condition (
9
) holds, then the eigenvalues can be bounded as:

\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x}(t))\geq\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{0})e^{-c_{\alpha}t},~\forall i\in[p],

(14)

at almost every time
t\geq 0
, along any Carathéodory solution starting from
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{A}(t)\in\partial\phi_{j}(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}(t))
be such that

\frac{d}{dt}(\lambda_{j}\circ\mathbf{x})(t)=\mathbf{A}(t)\cdot\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}(t)))

at almost every time
t\geq 0
. Since
\mathbf{A}
is an element in the convex hull, there may not be an eigenvector
\mathbf{v}
such that
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}
. Therefore, we use the Carathéodory theorem of convex hulls
[
24
, Thm. 17.1]
to deduce the existence of vectors
\{\mathbf{v}_{s}(t)\}_{s=1}^{(p^{2}+p)/2}
such that

\mathbf{A}(t)=\sum_{s=1}^{(p^{2}+p)/2}a_{s}(t)\mathbf{v}_{s}(t)\mathbf{v}_{s}(t)^{\top}

where
\sum a_{s}(t)=1
and each
\mathbf{v}_{s}
is a normalized eigenvector associated with
\phi_{j}(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}))
. Using this fact, we bound:

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{s=1}^{(p^{2}+p)/2}a_{s}\mathbf{v}_{s}\mathbf{v}_{s}^{\top}\Big)\cdot\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{(p^{2}+p)/2}a_{s}\big(\mathbf{v}_{s}\mathbf{v}_{s}^{\top}\cdot\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{(p^{2}+p)/2}a_{s}\big(\mathbf{v}_{s}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{v}_{s}\big)

\displaystyle\geq-c_{\alpha}\sum_{s=1}^{(p^{2}+p)/2}a_{s}\big(\mathbf{v}_{s}^{\top}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{v}_{s}\big)

\displaystyle=-c_{\alpha}\sum_{s=1}^{(p^{2}+p)/2}a_{s}\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{x})=-c_{\alpha}\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{x})

where we have dropped the dependencies on
t
for compactness of the presentation. Hence, the comparison lemma ensures the evolution bound (
14
), concluding the proof.
∎

Proposition
2
shows that the proposed matrix barrier condition (
9
) enforces bounds on all eigenvalues of
\mathbf{H}
. The bounds suggest a degree of conservatism in the approach, since ensuring only the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
nonnegative is sufficient (and necessary) to keep
\mathbf{H}
positive semidefinite. Nevertheless, it is typically difficult to handle the nonsmooth nature associated with eigenvalues, and our approach offers a simple way to address it. We further expand on this point by focusing our discussion on diagonal matrices.

III-B

Diagonal Matrix Constraints

Diagonal matrices are special cases of symmetric matrices
\mathbf{H}
. When
\mathbf{H}
is diagonal, the safe set
\mathcal{C}
in (
8
) can be equivalently represented as:

\mathcal{C}=\big\{\mathbf{x}\in\real^{n}\;|\;\mathbf{H}_{ii}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0,\forall i\in[p]\big\}.

Here, each
\mathbf{H}_{ii}
can be viewed as an individual scalar-valued CBF because the matrix barrier condition (
11
) is equivalent to

\dot{\mathbf{H}}_{ii}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\geq-c_{\alpha}\mathbf{H}_{ii}(\mathbf{x}),~\forall i\in[p],

which is precisely what we would have when considering multiple exponential CBFs with linear
\alpha
simultaneously. The following corollary makes this observation formal.

Corollary 1
.

(Multiple CBFs via MCBF):
Consider the control-affine system (
5
). Given a set of control barrier functions
\{h_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p}
with
\alpha_{i}(r)=c_{\alpha}r
for all
i\in[p]
, let a diagonal matrix
\mathbf{H}
be constructed with
\mathbf{H}_{ii}(\mathbf{x})=h_{i}(\mathbf{x})
. Then
\mathbf{H}
is an exponential MCBF, and the safety filters (
7
) and (
1
) are equivalent and continuous on
\mathcal{C}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{p}\mathcal{C}_{i}
defined in (
8
). As a result, the state-feedback
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})
renders set
\mathcal{C}
forward invariant for the closed-loop system.
\blacksquare

Corollary
1
formalizes the common strategy of using multiple CBFs to simultaneously handle multiple safety constraints. While it is generally understood that all barrier conditions should all hold together, a frequently overlooked detail is that they only need to do so on a neighborhood of the intersection
\bigcap_{i=1}^{p}\mathcal{C}_{i}
. It is unnecessary to verify that each CBF hold over its entire individual safe set
\mathcal{C}_{i}
. Although this nuance is familiar to many practitioners, it is rarely stated formally. Our diagonal MCBF formulation makes this composition process explicit and provides a formal justification for this widely used strategy.

Another related strategy for simultaneously addressing multiple safety constraints is through Boolean-based CBFs
[
12
]
, which also connects to our diagonal matrix formulation. In particular, the smallest eigenvalue of a diagonal matrix
\mathbf{H}
is:

\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{x})=\min_{i\in[p]}\mathbf{H}_{ii}(\mathbf{x})\triangleq h_{\min}(\mathbf{x}).

Instead of considering all CBFs at the same time, we may choose to only focus on the one with the smallest value. This particular approach corresponds to the conjunctive (AND) Boolean composition discussed in
[
12
]
. To address the nonsmoothness introduced when the index of the smallest CBF switches,
[
12
]
proposes a barrier condition that essentially considers the worst-case
\frac{d}{dt}h_{\min}(\mathbf{x}(t))
. However, such formulation does not yield continuity in the optimization-based controller, see
[
18
]
for an example.

Our MCBF formulation offers one approach to design continuous controllers for Boolean-based CBFs
[
12
]
without relaxing the safe set using soft-min or soft-max approximation like in
[
13
]
. Undeniably, as suggested in Corollary
1
, our proposed formulation recovers the safety filter (
7
) we typically use for dealing with multiple CBFs. However, as we shall discuss in later section, the novelty of our approach manifests when we consider disjunctive (OR) Boolean compositions.

III-C

Lipschitz Controllers

This paper does not establish Lipschitz guarantees for the CBF-SDP (
1
). Regularity of an optimization-based controller is an active field of research. For instance, it was only recently rediscovered that a CBF-QP with multiple CBF inequalities (
7
) is locally Lipschitz under the linear independence constraint qualification (LICQ) assumption
[
10
]
. At the same time, there is little work on the regularity of more complex optimization-based controller, such as second-order cone programming (SOCP) formulations for measurement-robust CBF
[
11
]
. Given the inherent difficulty of identifying conditions that guarantee Lipschitz continuity—even for the diagonal case—we decide to leave it as an open problem and a part of our future investigations on MCBFs. Instead, we provide here a result establishing the existence of a smooth (though not necessarily optimization-based) controller associated with a MCBF.

Proposition 3
.

Consider the control-affine system (
5
) and the set
\mathcal{C}
in (
8
). If
\mathbf{H}
is an exponential MCBF, then there exists a smooth controller
\mathbf{k}:\real^{n}\rightarrow\real^{m}
such that
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})
satisfies (
11
) for all
\mathbf{x}
in an open neighborhood
\mathcal{E}\supset\mathcal{C}
. Consequently, set
\mathcal{C}
is control invariant for the system.

Proof.

From the definition of an exponential CBF, there exists a function
\mathbf{x}\rightarrow\mathbf{u}^{*}(\mathbf{x})
, not necessarily continuous, such that
\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}^{*}(\mathbf{x}))\succ-c_{\alpha}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})
. Then because the function
\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})+c_{\alpha}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})
is continuous in
\mathbf{x}
, there exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{x})
, for each
\mathbf{x}
, such that
\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{u}^{*}(\mathbf{x}))+c_{\alpha}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
remains positive definite for all
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{x})
. Since the
\mathbf{u}^{*}(\mathbf{x})
is a feasible control in its corresponding neighborhood
\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{x})
, we can invoke the arguments of Artstein’s theorem
[
25
, Thm. 4.1]
(see also
[
18
, Lem. 6.5]
) to deduce the existence of a smooth controller through a finite partition of unity, relying on the fact that a LMI is a convex constraint.
∎

Proposition
3
relies on the Artstein’s theorem to non-constructively establish the existence of a smooth controller. This result shows that smooth controllers are theoretically achievable. While challenging, identifying conditions guaranteeing Lipschitz continuity for optimization-based controllers is a valuable and promising area for future research.

IV
Indefinite Matrix Constraints

Often times, safety requirements involve maintaining state trajectories outside a given set, e.g., obstacle avoidance, rather than inside the set. For scalar-valued CBFs, there is no distinction between the two scenarios because CBFs can be trivially reformulated with a negation. However, a negation to a matrix requires extra care.

IV-A

Disjunctive Boolean on CBFs

We begin with a motivating example. Consider a cylinder described by the following three inequalities:

\displaystyle x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\leq 1~\text{and}~-1\leq x_{3}\leq 1.

To keep state trajectories within this cylinder, we can rely on the positive semidefinite constraint formulation developed in this paper. On the other hand, when this cylinder represents an obstacle, we would like the state trajectories to remain outside of it. This leads to a safety constraint given by the logical negation of the cylinder representation:

\displaystyle x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\geq 1~\text{or}~x_{3}\leq-1~\text{or}~1\leq x_{3},

introducing OR Booleans among the inequalities. To tackle this problem using the standard CBF approach, the common practice is to smoothly approximate the safe set with a soft-max function on all three constraints, see
[
13
]
.

From the new perspective using a matrix representation, we may also represent the negation of a (open) cylinder with an indefinite inequality:

-\begin{bmatrix}1-x_{1}&x_{2}&0&0\\
x_{2}&1+x_{1}&0&0\\
0&0&1-x_{3}&0\\
0&0&0&1+x_{3}\end{bmatrix}\not\prec~\mathbf{0}.

Motivated by this class of problems, we develop a MCBF framework to deal with such constraints.

IV-B

Indefinite CBFs

In this section, we consider safe sets given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\big\{\mathbf{x}\in\real^{n}\;|\;\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\not\prec\mathbf{0}\big\}

\displaystyle=\big\{\mathbf{x}\in\real^{n}\;|\;\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\big\}

(15)

The equivalent eigenvalue formulation facilitates our subsequent result for indefinite matrices.

Proposition 4
.

(Indefinite Matrix Barrier Condition):

Consider the autonomous system (
1
) with a continuous vector field
\mathbf{F}
and the set
\mathcal{C}
in (
IV-B
). If there exists a function
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
and
c_{\perp}\geq 0
such that:

\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\succeq-\alpha\big(\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{x})\big)\mathbf{I}_{p\times p}-c_{\perp}\big(\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{I}_{p\times p}-\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\big),

(16)

for all
\mathbf{x}
in an open neighborhood
\mathcal{E}\supset\mathcal{C}
, then the following bound holds:

\frac{d}{dt}(\lambda_{p}\circ\mathbf{x})(t)\geq-\alpha\big((\lambda_{p}\circ\mathbf{x})(t)\big)

(17)

at almost every time
t\geq 0
along any Carathéodory solution starting from
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
. As a consequence, set
\mathcal{C}
is forward invariant for the system.

Proof.

Following the proof of Proposition
2
, we may bound the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
along the trajectory at almost every time
t\geq 0
as:

~\frac{d}{dt}(\lambda_{j}\circ\mathbf{x})(t)\geq-\alpha\big((\lambda_{p}\circ\mathbf{x})(t)\big)-c_{\perp}\big(\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{x})-\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{x})\big).

(18)

In particular, we derive (
17
) for the largest eigenvalue with
j=p
.
Hence, from the comparison lemma with any initial condition
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
, the eigenvalue
\lambda_{p}
must remain nonnegative at all time, preventing
\mathbf{H}
from becoming negative definite.
∎

The key idea behind the matrix inequality in (
16
) is to isolate the largest eigenvalue and prevent it from becoming negative. This can, in fact, be established even without the last term in the inequality. The last term, however, helps relax the barrier condition further. Without it, the inequality would also unnecessarily bound the evolution of all other eigenvalues, as evident in (
18
). In a graceful manner, the relaxation vanishes as
\lambda_{j}
approaches
\lambda_{p}
.

Note importantly that the barrier condition avoids nonsmoothness of
\lambda_{p}
through relying on
\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{H}
rather than
\frac{d}{dt}(\lambda_{p}\circ\mathbf{x})
directly. This formulation lays the foundation for the MCBF framework that facilitates the synthesis of a continuous controller.

Definition 5
.

(Indefinite MCBF):

A continuously differentiable function
\mathbf{H}:\real^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{p}
is called an
indefinite matrix control barrier function
(indefinite MCBF) for system (
5
) if there exists a function
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
and
c_{\perp}\geq 0
such that, for each
\mathbf{x}
in the set
\mathcal{C}
defined in (
IV-B
), there exists a
\mathbf{u}\in\real^{m}
satisfying:

\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\succ-\alpha\big(\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{x})\big)\mathbf{I}_{p\times p}-c_{\perp}\big(\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{I}_{p\times p}-\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\big).

(19)

Much like the semidefinite case, indefinite MCBFs facilitate the construction of a continuous controller.

Theorem 2
.

(Safety from Indefinite MCBF):
Consider the control-affine system (
5
) and the set
\mathcal{C}
in (
IV-B
).
If
\mathbf{H}
is an indefinite MCBF, then set
\mathcal{C}
is control invariant. In particular, the CBF-SDP:

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})=\argmin_{\mathbf{u}\in\real^{m}}\quad

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{k}_{\operatorname{d}}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}

(20)

s.t.

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\succeq-\alpha(\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{x}))\mathbf{I}_{p\times p}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-c_{\perp}\left(\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{I}_{p\times p}-\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\right)

is continuous for all
\mathbf{x}
in some neighborhood
\mathcal{E}
of

\blacksquare

Theorem
2
provides a continuous controller for dealing with indefinite matrix safety constraints. We omit the proof for the theorem because of its similarities to the one for Theorem
1
. An important application of indefinite MCBFs is on a diagonal matrix constraint constructed by multiple CBFs.

Corollary 2
.

(OR Boolean CBFs via MCBF):
Consider the control-affine system (
5
). Given a set of control barrier functions
\{h_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p}
with some corresponding
\alpha_{i}\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
for each
i\in[p]
, let a diagonal matrix
\mathbf{H}
be constructed with
\mathbf{H}_{ii}(\mathbf{x})=-h_{i}(\mathbf{x})
. If
\mathbf{H}
is an indefinite MCBF with
\alpha(r)\triangleq\max_{i\in[p]}\alpha_{i}(r)
and some constant
c_{\perp}\geq 0
on the set
\mathcal{C}=\cup_{i=1}^{p}\mathcal{C}_{i}

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})

\blacksquare

Our MCBF formulation enables the synthesis of continuous controllers that handle nonsmooth Boolean-based constraints. In particular, Corollary
2
offers a mechanism for addressing OR Boolean constraints where it suffices to maintain the positivity of any one CBF at a given time. In addition, the approach provides a bound on the evolution of the maximum eigenvalue
\lambda_{p}
as in (
17
). This coincides with the CBF that has a largest value at a given state, i.e.,
h_{\max}(\mathbf{x})\triangleq\max_{i\in[p]}h_{i}(\mathbf{x})
. Notice that the bound (
17
) obtained involves the more general class-
\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
function, rather than a linear one, because we deal directly with eigenvalues. This insight motivates the developments in the next section.

V
General Matrix Control Barrier Functions

A limitation of exponential MCBFs developed earlier is that their construction (
11
) only permits a linear relationship between
\dot{\mathbf{H}}
and
\mathbf{H}
.
This section formulates the general class of MCBFs that allows the use of class-
\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
functions.

V-A

Smallest Eigenvalue MCBF

Building on the development from the last section, we propose a barrier condition that maintains positivity of the lowest eigenvalue.

Proposition 5
.

(Smallest Eigenvalue Matrix Barrier Condition):

Consider the autonomous system (
1
) with a continuous vector field
\mathbf{F}
and the set
\mathcal{C}
in (
8
). If there exists a function
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
and
c_{\perp}\geq 0
such that:

\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{H}(x)\succeq-\alpha\big(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{x})\big)\mathbf{I}_{p\times p}-c_{\perp}\big(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})-\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{I}_{p\times p}\big)

(21)

for all
\mathbf{x}
in an open neighborhood
\mathcal{E}\supset\mathcal{C}
, then the following bound holds:

\frac{d}{dt}(\lambda_{1}\circ\mathbf{x})(t)\geq-\alpha\big((\lambda_{1}\circ\mathbf{x})(t)\big).

(22)

at almost every time
t\geq 0
along any Carathéodory solution starting from
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
. As a consequence, set
\mathcal{C}
is forward invariant for the system.
\blacksquare

While the barrier condition proposed in Proposition
5
successfully introduces a class-
\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
function to the bound, it only provides a bound on the smallest eigenvalue. With a slight adjustment of setting
c_{\perp}=0
, bounds can be developed for other eigenvalues since
\alpha(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{x}))\leq\alpha(\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{x}))
for any
j\in[p]
. However, such an approach is inherently conservative as it essentially bounds the evolution of all eigenvalues with the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
rather than directly with each
\lambda_{j}
. To this end, we omit the result on the corresponding MCBF associated with this barrier condition.

V-B

General Matrix CBFs

The more appropriate approach to bounding each eigenvalue involves matrix diagonalization through spectral decomposition. For a given symmetric matrix
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})
, we have:

\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}

where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbf{x})
is a diagonal matrix constructed from eigenvalues,
\Lambda_{ii}(\mathbf{x})=\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x})
, and
\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x})
is a matrix constructed from concatenating corresponding eigenvectors. Once diagonalized, we may apply the class-
\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
function on each eigenvalue. We denote with
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})
the resulting matrix from applying the function
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
element-wise on the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\mathbf{x})
:

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\triangleq{\operatorname{diag}}\big(\alpha(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{x})),\dots,\alpha(\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{x}))\big).

Then we use the following notation for applying a class-
\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
function to a matrix:

\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}))\triangleq\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x})^{\top},

(23)

for which we can develop a barrier condition that mirrors the scalar case.

Proposition 6
.

(Matrix Barrier Condition):

Consider the autonomous system (
1
) with a continuous vector field
\mathbf{F}
and the set
\mathcal{C}
in (
8
). If there exists a function
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
such that:

\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{H}(x)\succeq-\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}))

(24)

for all
\mathbf{x}
in an open neighborhood
\mathcal{E}\supset\mathcal{C}
, then the following bound holds:

\frac{d}{dt}(\lambda_{j}\circ\mathbf{x})(t)\geq-\alpha((\lambda_{j}\circ\mathbf{x})(t)),\forall j\in[p],

(25)

at almost every time
t\geq 0
along any Carathéodory solution starting from
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
. As a consequence, set
\mathcal{C}
is forward invariant for the system.

Proof.

Since the matrix
\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x})
is orthonormal at each
\mathbf{x}
, we can deduce the evolution bound (
25
) using the logic of the proof for Proposition
2
.
∎

Proposition
6
lays the necessary groundwork for the development of a general class of MCBFs that can impose a class-
\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
function bound on all of the eigenvalues. Note importantly that the class-
\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
function must be uniform for all eigenvalues, unlike in the diagonal case.

Remark 2
.

(Uniform class-
\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
function):

Diagonal matrices enjoy the luxury of selecting different
\alpha_{i}
for each eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
. For these matrices, it is possible to correspond the eigenvalues with eigenvectors. However, the correspondence is unclear for general symmetric matrices, especially when there are repeated eigenvalues. By imposing different
\alpha_{i}
, repeated eigenvalues scale to different values. The uniqueness of the resulting matrix
\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}))
requires careful labeling of eigenvalues and associated eigenvectors. In some cases, this is possible, but we choose to avoid this for simplicity.
\bullet

Definition 6
.

(Matrix CBF):

A continuously differentiable function
\mathbf{H}:\real^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{p}
is called a
matrix control barrier function
(MCBF) for system (
5
) if there exists a function
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
such that, for each
\mathbf{x}
in the set
\mathcal{C}
defined in (
8
), there exists a
\mathbf{u}\in\real^{m}
satisfying:

\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\succ-\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})).

(26)

with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}))
defined in (
23
).

The introduction of the eigenvector matrix
\mathbf{V}
raises an important concern regarding the continuity of the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\circ\mathbf{H}
. Such a guarantee facilitates establishing the lower semicontinuity of the constraint set generated by the MCBF, thereby ensuring continuity of the resulting safety filter.
The following result resolves this issue.

Lemma 2
.

(Continuity of the Matrix Class-
\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
Functions):
Let the matrix-valued function
\mathbf{H}
be continuous and
\alpha
be a class-
\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
function. The matrix-valued function
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\circ\mathbf{H}
is continuous if
\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}_{j=1}^{p}
are selected orthonormal.

Proof.

First, we write the matrix function as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha(\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{x}))\mathbf{v}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{v}_{j}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha(\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{x}))\mathbf{P}_{j}(\mathbf{x})

in terms of functions
\mathbf{P}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\triangleq\mathbf{v}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{v}_{j}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}
. The matrix
\mathbf{P}_{j}
is precisely the eigenspace projector when
\lambda_{j}
has a multiplicity of one. For higher multiplicity,
\mathbf{P}_{j}
is not unique, so depending on the selection of
\mathbf{v}_{j}
, it may not be continuous. Because
\mathbf{v}_{j}
are orthonormal and span the entire eigenspace, the eigenspace projector is then given by the sum of the projectors with the same eigenvalue, i.e.,
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J(\mathbf{x})}}\mathbf{P}_{j}(\mathbf{x})
where
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{x})=\{i\in[p]\;|\;\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{x})\}
. From
[
26
, Chapter 2 Sec. 1]
(see the subsection 8 for a brief summary), the total projector
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J(\mathbf{x})}}\mathbf{P}_{j}(\mathbf{x})
is holomorphic at
\mathbf{x}
and adopts smoothness of
\mathbf{H}
on the manifold where total eigenvalue multiplicity
|\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{x})|
is constant. Therefore, since
\alpha(\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x}))=\alpha(\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{x}))
for all
i\in\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{x})
, we may group
\mathbf{P}_{j}(\mathbf{x})
’s with the summation
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{x})}\mathbf{P}_{j}(\mathbf{x})
to deduce continuity of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\circ\mathbf{H}
as desired.
∎

With this continuity result, the safety result follows with a similar proof to earlier theorems on safety.

.

(Safety from MCBF):
Consider the control-affine system (
5
) and the set
\mathcal{C}
in (
8
).
If
\mathbf{H}
is an MCBF, then set
\mathcal{C}
is control invariant. In particular, the CBF-SDP:

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})=\argmin_{\mathbf{u}\in\real^{m}}\quad

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{k}_{\operatorname{d}}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}

(27)

s.t.

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\succeq-\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}))

is continuous for all
\mathbf{x}
in some neighborhood
\mathcal{E}
of
\mathcal{C}
. Consequently, the state-feedback
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})
renders set
\mathcal{C}
forward invariant for the closed-loop system.
\blacksquare

3
guarantees safety with a continuous CBF-SDP safety filter. We would like to emphasize that the function
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
does not apply the class-
\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}
function
\alpha
on

VI
Further Discussions

In this section, we offer several analytical insights and possible refinements of the proposed MCBF framework. These include a less conservative barrier condition, a more general form of the safety filter, and a comparison between matrix- and scalar-valued representations of safe sets. While we do not provide the full technical details, we consider these discussions to be an important complement to the main results.

VI-A

Less Conservative Barrier Condition

The barrier condition proposed in (
24
) is not the least conservative formulation possible. By Sylvester’s criterion
[
27
, Thm. 7.2.5]
, a matrix is positive semidefinite if and only if all of its leading principal minors
2
2

2

Leading principal minors are the determinants of the upper left
j\times j
corner submatrices for all
j\in[p]
.
are nonnegative. This, in turn, implies that all upper left
j\times j
corner submatrices must be positive semidefinite. Consequently, the matrix inequality in (
24
) is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{[j]}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{f}}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{V}_{[j]}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\succeq-{\operatorname{diag}}\big(\alpha(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{x})),\dots,\alpha(\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{x}))\big)

for all
j\in[p]
, where the matrix
\mathbf{V}_{[j]}(\mathbf{x})
is the concatenation of only the orthonormal eigenvectors associated with the smallest
j
eigenvalues. Here, the minimum eigenvalue of the left hand side can be interpreted as the worst-case rate of change among the
j
smallest eigenvalues. Thus, we can further relax the condition by replacing the right hand side with:

\mathbf{V}_{[j]}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{f}}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{V}_{[j]}(\mathbf{x})\succeq-\alpha(\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{x}))\mathbf{I}_{p\times p}

(28)

while maintaining safety guarantees.

The condition (
28
) is equivalent to the barrier condition proposed in the previous work
[
18
]
. However, that work formulated the control barrier function as a hierarchical optimization and did not make a connection to LMIs, which greatly reduce computational overhead during implementation. From a computational standpoint, the barrier condition (
28
) scales poorly with matrix size because it contains multiple LMIs. In contrast, the CBF (
27
) proposed in this paper requires a single LMI. For this reason, we omit the full technical treatment of this alternative.

VI-B

Safety Filter Cost Functions

Beyond the choice of barrier conditions, the cost function plays a key role in the behavior of safety filters. The standard safety filter cost function encodes minimal deviations from desired control inputs. For this particular cost function, we have established the continuity of the safety filter induced by a MCBF. However, this cost function can be generalized as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})=\argmin_{\mathbf{u}\in\real^{m}}\quad

\displaystyle J(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})

s.t.

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\succeq-\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}))

with some cost function
J:\real^{n}\times\real^{m}\rightarrow\real
. In this case, continuity of the safety filter can be established if
J
is continuous in
\mathbf{x}
and strictly convex in
\mathbf{u}
(or simply if the sub-level sets of
J
is compact for each
\mathbf{x}
). We omit the result due to the increased complexity in the proof, cf.
[
18
]
.

VI-C

Schur’s Complement Reformulation

Schur’s complement is often useful for reformulating matrix safety constraints as scalar ones, and vice versa. We briefly investigate here the differences between the two representations. While both representations of the safe set are equivalent, the resulting CBF constraints are not, as illustrated with the following spherical constraint example.

Consider the control-affine system (
5
) with a simple spherical constraint of radius
R
centered at
\mathbf{x}_{\operatorname{o}}
. There are two equivalent representations:

\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\operatorname{o}}\|^{2}\leq R^{2}\iff\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n\times n}&\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\operatorname{o}}\\
(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\operatorname{o}})^{\top}&R^{2}\end{bmatrix}\succeq\mathbf{0}

On the one hand, the standard exponential CBF approach gives the following constraint:

-2(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\operatorname{o}})^{\top}\big(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}\big)\geq-c_{\alpha}(R^{2}-\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\operatorname{o}}\|^{2}),

which can be algebraically rearranged into:

c_{\alpha}R^{2}-\frac{1}{c_{\alpha}}\|c_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\operatorname{o}})+\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}\|^{2}\\
\geq-\frac{1}{c_{\alpha}}\|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}\|^{2}.

On the other hand, the MCBF approach from Sec.
III
yields:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}&\dot{\mathbf{x}}\\
\dot{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}&0\end{bmatrix}\succeq-c_{\alpha}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n\times n}&\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\operatorname{o}}\\
(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\operatorname{o}})^{\top}&R^{2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\iff c_{\alpha}R^{2}-\frac{1}{c_{\alpha}}\|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}+c_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\operatorname{o}})\|^{2}\geq 0,

which differs by the extra term
(1/c_{\alpha})\|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}\|^{2}
. This difference hints at a potentially useful generalization, whose implications are left for future work.

VII
Applications

The MCBF formulation offers safe control solutions for a broader class of safety problems. This section demonstrates its versatility through discussions of specific applications.

VII-A

Localization via Nonlinear Least Squares

In many robotic systems, the full state
\mathbf{x}
is not directly measured. Instead, we have sensor measurements
\mathbf{y}
through an output of some function
\mathbf{m}:\real^{n}\rightarrow\real^{p}
:

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{x}).

One approach for computing a state estimation
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\real^{n}
from measurements is through solving nonlinear least square (NLS) problems:

\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\real^{n}}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2},

a method increasingly used in robotics in fields such as SLAM, pose graph optimization, and sensor fusion. One recent work
[
28
]
has begun to explore how control barrier functions can be used to ensure NLS remains well-posed during robot operation.

In particular, the Hessian matrix of the cost function:

\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})=\nabla^{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}

must remain positive definite as the robot navigates through its environment. This would ensure the optimization problem admits a unique solution; otherwise, the resulting estimates may be ambiguous or discontinuous. The work
[
28
]
approaches this problem using standard CBFs on the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{H}
. As a result, additional constraints must be imposed to avoid the possibility of nonsmoothness. In contrast, our MCBF formulation handles such matrix inequalities directly and smoothly, allowing for the construction of a continuous safety filter that ensures well-posedness of the localization problem. Due to technical considerations beyond the scope of this paper, the full technical treatment of this application will appear in a separate paper.

VII-B

Collision Avoidance

Our formulation expands the range of geometrical objects that CBFs can handle in collision avoidance tasks for robotic applications. Even when we limit the matrix
\mathbf{H}
to be linear in the state
\mathbf{x}
, the set
\mathcal{C}
in (
8
) describes a spectrahedron. This class of objects contains many familiar and useful shapes such as polyhedra, hyperspheres, cylinders, and cones. It also encompasses interesting objects such as elliptopes and objects whose shadows are ellipses with multiple foci (m-ellipses). As an example, Fig.
1
depicts the elliptope defined by:

\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\real^{3}\middle|\begin{bmatrix}1&x_{1}&x_{2}\\
x_{1}&1&x_{3}\\
x_{2}&x_{3}&1\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0\right\}.

(29)

We hope the generalization to matrix inequalities will enable more sophisticated analytical representations of real-world obstacles.

VII-C

Connectivity Maintenance

An immediate application of MCBFs is the task of connectivity maintenance in a multi-robot system. We consider a group of
p
robot agents. Let
\mathbf{x}_{i}
be the state of the
i
-th robot and
\mathbf{x}
be the aggregate of all the states evolving according to the control-affine dynamics (
5
). One key safety constraint concerns the communication capability between robots.

We use a weighted adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}:\real^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{p}
to describe connectivity between robots. Note here we only consider the case of symmetric adjacency matrices, which correspond to undirected communication graphs. In particular, the entry
A_{ij}(\mathbf{x})>0
if and only if robots
i
and
j
are connected. The Laplacian matrix is defined as
\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{x})\triangleq\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x})
, where
\mathbf{D}_{ii}(\mathbf{x})\triangleq\sum_{j\in[p]}\mathbf{A}_{ij}(\mathbf{x})
is the degree matrix. By construction, the smallest eigenvalue
\phi_{1}(\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{x}))\equiv 0
is always zero, with an eigenvector being a vector of ones
\mathbf{1}_{p}
. More importantly, the second smallest eigenvalue
\phi_{2}(\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{x}))
is useful for describing the connectivity of the robot network. A network is connected (i.e., there is a communication
path
between any pair of robots) if and only if
\phi_{2}(\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{x}))>0
.

Connectivity can be enforced using a MCBF. By proposing:

\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{\varepsilon}{p}\mathbf{1}_{p}\mathbf{1}_{p}^{\top}-\varepsilon\mathbf{I}_{p\times p}

(30)

with
\varepsilon\geq 0
, the set
\mathcal{C}
comprises only states
\mathbf{x}
for which the network is connected. If
\mathbf{H}
is an MCBF, we can design a safety filter to maintain the robot network connectivity. The CBF approach enables a simple integration of the connectivity constraint that minimally interferes with other robot tasks. In addition, our matrix-based formulation avoids the nonsmooth issues associated with eigenvalues (see
[
18
]
for the discussion on this issue).

Experiment Results

Following our successful simulated demonstrations, we physically implemented the aforementioned connectivity maintenance scenario on a swarm of Crazyflie 2.1+ UAVs. We used an OptiTrack motion capture system to perform localization at 240 Hz, and we executed the control laws in (

Simultaneously, we generated a vertical velocity command through a decoupled altitude tracking controller, leveraging the same control law as in (

After converting these 3-D velocities to acceleration commands (by backstepping through an additional proportional feedback loop), we radioed them to each UAV in real-time, where they were tracked at 1000 Hz by an onboard low-level quaternion-based attitude controller. IX
Conclusion

This paper generalizes the control barrier function framework to accommodate matrix-valued functions. We have considered both the semidefinite and indefinite constraints on these matrix functions. We established matrix barrier conditions for both autonomous and control systems, and proved the continuity of the CBF-SDP controller derived from the proposed MCBFs. The framework was demonstrated through a multi-robot network connectivity maintenance task, validated in both simulation and quadrotor experiments. Our future work focuses on both theoretical development and practical applications, such as identifying sufficient conditions for local Lipschitz continuity of the CBF-SDP controllers, and exploring applications beyond connectivity maintenance, including Boolean compositions of scalar-valued CBFs.
