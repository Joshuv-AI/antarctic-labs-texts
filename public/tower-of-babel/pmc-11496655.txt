Potential toxicity of Graphene (Oxide) quantum dots to human intestinal fatty acid binding protein (HIFABP) via obstructing the protein’s openings
Authors: Yanbo Luo, Zonglin Gu, Jose Manuel Perez-Aguilar, Yuqi Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Graphene quantum dots (GQDs) have garnered significant attention across numerous fields due to their ultrasmall size and exceptional properties. However, their extensive applications may lead to environmental exposure and subsequent uptake by humans. Yet, conflicting reports exist regarding the potential toxicity of GQDs based on experimental investigations. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of GQD biosafety requires further microscopic and molecular-level investigations. In this study, we employed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to explore the interactions between GQDs and graphene oxide quantum dots (GOQDs) with a protein model, the human intestinal fatty acid binding protein (HIFABP), that plays a crucial role in mediating the carrier of fatty acids in the intestine. Our MD simulation results reveal that GQDs can be adsorbed on the opening of HIFABP, which serves as an entrance for the fatty acid molecules into the protein’s interior cavity. This adsorption has the potential to obstruct the opening of HIFABP, leading to the loss of its normal biological function and ultimately resulting in toxicity. The adsorption of GQDs is driven by a combination of van der Waals (vdW), π-π stacking, cation-π, and hydrophobic interactions. Similarly, GOQDs also exhibit the ability to block the opening of HIFABP, thereby potentially causing toxicity. The blockage of GOQDs to HIFABP is guided by a combination of vdW, Coulomb, π-π stacking, and hydrophobic interactions. These findings not only highlight the potential harmful effects of GQDs on HIFABP but also elucidate the underlying molecular mechanism, which provides crucial insights into GQD toxicology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73037-z.

Introduction

Since their groundbreaking discoveries of graphene, carbon nanotube (CNT) and graphene quantum dot (GQD)1–3, carbon nanomaterials have garnered immense attention across multiple scientific fields4–9, owing to their remarkable properties10–16. These nanomaterials exhibit unique characteristics such as high surface area, excellent electrical conductivity, superior mechanical strength, and exceptional chemical stability to mentioned a few. Among the diverse range of carbon nanomaterials, GQDs stand out due to their distinctive quantum confinement effects, tunable bandgap, and exceptional photoluminescence properties17–20. With their size in the nanometer range and outstanding properties, GQDs have emerged as a promising candidate for applications spanning electronics, optics, devices, biology, and medicine21–25. In the area of electronics, GQDs have enabled the development of flexible and transparent conductive films, high-performance transistors, and ultrafast electronic devices26–28. Their exceptional electrical properties make them ideal candidates for next-generation electronics, paving the way for advancements in wearable technology, flexible displays, and high-speed communication systems29. GQDs, with their size-dependent photoluminescence behavior, have found applications in bioimaging, fluorescent probes, and light-emitting diodes (LEDs)30,31. Their tunable emission wavelengths and excellent photostability make them versatile tools in biological research and medical diagnostics, promising significant advancements in personalized medicine and healthcare. Notably, the 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded to three scientists for their pioneering discoveries and synthesis of quantum dots, further enhancing the possibility of GQDs as a focal point of future research.

While the widespread applications of GQDs hold tremendous promise, investigations regarding their environmental exposure and potential toxicity have emerged as research focus, because an extensive utilization of GQDs might cause problematic environmental pollution. Of particular concern is the potential for human exposure to GQDs through ingestion, inhalation, or dermal contact. However, recent reports present conflicting findings regarding the potential toxicity of GQDs based on experimental investigations(describe the experimental investigations) with some studies even suggesting low or even no GQDs toxicity. For instance, Chong et al.32 assessed the in vitro and in vivo toxicity of GQDs using various analytical techniques. Their results indicated minimal cytotoxicity of GQDs, attributed to their ultra-small size and high oxygen content. Similarly, investigations by Zhang et al.33 into the reproductive and offspring health of mammals showed no significant effects on the frequency and timing of sexual behaviors in male mice following exposure to GQDs through oral gavage or intravenous injection. Furthermore, Jiang et al.34 demonstrated weak toxicity of luminescent GQDs towards HeLa cells and zebrafish embryos. Moreover, some theoretical investigations showed the advantageous effect of nanoscale-sized graphene sheet in treating SARS-CoV-2 viral infection via the inhibiting of the interaction between the Receptor-Binding Domain and the ACE2 receptor35. However, Sengupta et al.36 highlighted the nuanced nature of GQD toxicity, emphasizing factors such as size, concentration, surface chemistry, and doping as crucial determinants. Some studies37,38 have underscored potential toxic effects of GQDs, including dopaminergic neurodegeneration, induction of apoptosis, autophagy, and inflammatory responses. Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation of the toxicity profile of GQDs, especially from a microscopic/molecular level, is essential for a thorough understanding of their biosafety.

Some evidences have been demonstrated that GQDs may possibly accumulate in intestinal tract39. Such accumulation may also possibly cause extensive exposure of GQDs to components in the intestinal tract, yielding their direct contact and the corresponding harmful effect. However, limited efforts have been devoted to exploring the potential toxicity of GQDs to the different components in intestinal tract, e.g., proteins. In this study, using a molecular dynamics simulation approach, a member of the family of intracellular lipid binding proteins, the human intestinal fatty acid binding protein (HIFABP), is selected as a typical intestinal protein to investigate the possible binding and repercussions of GQDs. The HIFABP is expressed in epithelial cells of the mucosal layer of the small intestine and exhibits essential metabolic and structural functions. HIFABP has the primary function of carry fatty acid molecules through the aqueous cellular environment and hence, it is involved in various processes including fatty acid uptake, transport, and oxidation. HIFABP plays the central role of lipid chaperon in lipid-mediated biological processes and systemic metabolic homeostasis through the regulation of diverse lipid-related signaling pathways40. Structurally, the HIFABP displays a three-dimensional fold featuring an antiparallel β-clam motif formed by ten β-strands. The tertiary structure of HIFABP contains a cavity that exhibits high affinity for fatty acids allowing the binding and transport of such molecules36. Our MD simulations reveal that GQD can partially seal up the HIFABP’s portal that allows the access and egress of the fatty acid molecules resulting in an obstruction to its interior with the concomitant loss of function of this carrier protein, i.e., possibly generating citotoxicity. In addition, we also examine the potential influence of GOQD to HIFABP, a chemically modified oxygen-containing GDQ. Our results also demonstrates a similar obstruction of the HIFABP entrance by GOQDs. The mechanistic analyses suggest that the vdW interactions drive the binding of either GQD or GOQD to the HIFABP portal. Our results not only reveal the potential toxicity of both GQD and GOQD to HIFABP, but also uncover the corresponding molecular determinants of such harmful features of the nanomaterials, which may direct their possible future applications.

Methods

Two simulation boxes were constructed as shown in Fig. S1, wherein the GQD or GOQD was initially put on the HIFABP. The initial distance between GQD/GOQD and HIFABP was set to over 2 nm to avoid initial biased interactions. The GQD and GOQD shared the same carbon layer, whereas the only difference was the oxygen-containing groups on the GOQD systems. The combinations of GQD/HIFABP and GOQD/HIFABP were dissolved into the water boxes, and then 0.15 M NaCl was added in each box. The tertiary structure of HIFABP was obtained from the protein data bank with PDB accession code of 1kzw41. An additional simulation system comprised of 10 GQD around one HIFABP aiming at investigate how the GQD with higher concentration affected HIFABP. The simulation details were listed in Table S1.

We performed the simulations with the GROMACS software package (version 2018)42 using the CHARMM27 force field43. The force field parameters for GQD and GOQD were obtained from a previous study34. The VMD software package44 was used to analyze and visualize the simulation conformations and trajectories. The TIP3P water model was adopted to describe the water molecules45. The temperature was maintained at 300 K using a v-rescale thermostat46 and pressure was kept at 1 atm using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat47. The long-range electrostatic interactions were treated with the PME method48,49, and the vdW interactions were calculated with a cutoff distance of 1.2 nm. All bonds associated with hydrogen atoms were maintained constant at their equilibrium values with the LINCS algorithm50, while the water geometry was also constrained using the SETTLE algorithm51. During the production runs, a time step of 2.0 fs was used, and data were collected every 10 ps. Each system was investigated for 20 independent trajectories with each of 100-ns duration.

Result

HIFABP comprises an empty cavity surrounded by β-strands, serving as a carrier that accommodates and facilitates the transport of fatty acid molecules. The protein features two openings, through which fatty acids can access to the cavity. We conducted 20 parallel simulations of the GQD/HIFABP system. Figure 1 depicting the final conformations of six systems, after 100-ns trajectory, where the GQD places near one of the portals of HIFABP (top portal as presented in Fig. 1). Notably, five conformations suggest that GQD almost enters into the interior of the HIFABP cavity (Fig. 1a–e). From a top viewpoint, we observed that GQD almost completely covers the openings of the HIFABP cavity. When binding to the protein openings, GQD exhibits a vertical orientation relative to the entrance of the protein. Additionally, Fig. 1f shows that GQD is able to only touched one β-strand located at the portal slightly covering the entrance of the protein’s cavity. In addition, to further investigate how GQDs in high concentration affects HIFABP, we conducted an additional simulation where 10 GQDs, rather than a single molecule as in the previous calculations, were initially placed around the HIFABP structure; the simulation results are illustrated in Figure S2. Clearly, the GQD molecules form a cluster that completely blocks the opening of HIFABP, supporting the occlusion effect of the carbon-based nanostructure. Given the importance of the portals to the normal physiological function of this protein, the presence of GQD may have a detrimental effect on HIFABP by obstructing its primary function as a fatty acid carrier.

We then plotted the contact probability isosurface of GQD to HIFABP by averaging all twenty parallel simulations, as shown in Fig. 2. Residues near one of the portals of HIFABP (illustrated by the left panel in Fig. 2a,b) exhibit a relatively high contact probability, indicating the preferential adsorption of GQD to this particular opening. This suggests that GQD is likely to preferentially obstruct this portal (top portal) of HIFABP. We also noted that residues near the second portal (bottom portal as presented in Fig. 1) also show relatively high contact probability with GQD. However, upon examining the final binding conformations of the rest of the 14 systems (Figure S3), only in one trajectory the occlusion of the second (bottom) HIFABP portal by GQD is observed, as shown in Figure S3h. The binding positions of the GQD systems in the other 13 trajectories is located at the side of the HIFABP structure rather than binding to either of the two openings. These findings suggest that GQD could bind to both portals of HIFABP resulting in the obstruction to the protein’s cavity entrance.

We further analyzed the residues with contact probability by GQD over 0.15, as shown in Fig. 3. These residues include Phe2, Phe47, Phe62, Leu64, Leu84, Asn87, Lys88, Glu107, and Ile108. Notably, six hydrophobic residues (Phe2, Phe47, Phe62, Leu64, Leu84, and Ile108) account for 66.7% of these important residues. This indicates that hydrophobic interactions play a significant role in guiding the GQD binding to HIFABP. Additionally, three aromatic residues (Phe2, Phe47, and Phe62) are associated with the highly contacted residues at the interface. Given that previous studies52 have demonstrated that aromatic residues can form π-π stacking interactions during the binding of graphene and aromatic residues in proteins, the π-π stacking interaction between these three aromatic residues and GQD also plays an important role in GQD-HIFABP binding. Furthermore, a basic residue, Lys88, located in the top portal, shows high probability during GQD binding to HIFABP. The attractive force of this basic residue to GQD is attributed not only to the long side chain of lysine, which can generate a large dispersion interaction (i.e., vdW interaction) with GQD53 but also to the cation-π interactions, as demonstrated by previous studies54,55. These results suggest that the complex interplay of hydrophobic, π-π stacking, cation-π, and vdW interactions drive the binding of GQD to HIFABP.

To further elucidate the binding dynamics, we selected one typical trajectory to analyze the time-evolution vdW interaction energy between GQD and HIFABP, and plotted two conformations at two key time points (Fig. 4). Specifically, at t = 7 ns, GQD briefly touches HIFABP, but they separate thereafter. At t = 32.5 ns, GQD reaches the (top) portal of HIFABP, and the binding between GQD and HIFABP approaches a metastable state, accompanied by an increase in the vdW interaction energy so as to reach − 195.6 kJ/mol. At that same time, residues Phe2, Phe47, Phe62, and Leu64 are largely involved with the interfacial binding. This state persists until 48.0 ns. From around 32.5 ns to 48.0 ns, GQD further moves towards the interior of the HIFABP cavity. Meanwhile, the vdW interaction energy increases to reach a value of -242.5 kJ/mol, and the main interfacial residues involved in the binding are Phe2, Phe47, Leu84, Asn87, Lys88, Glu107, and Ile108. We have illustrated the binding interface between GQD and two aromatic residues, i.e., Phe2 and Phe47, as shown in Fig. S4. The phenyl rings of the aromatic residues directly contact the GQD forming a face-to-face pattern via π−π stacking interactions. Such interactions provide a strong favorable attractive force contributing significantly to the protein-GQD binding, resembling the findings in previous studies56,57. As expected, the dominant hydrophobic and aromatic residues suggest the critical role of hydrophobic and π-π stacking interactions during the GQD and HIFABP binding. In addition, the involvement of Lys88 in the protein-GQD binding also suggests an important contribution of cation-π interactions. Therefore, the obstruction of GQD to the HIFABP portal is driven by the interplay of hydrophobic, π-π stacking, cation-π, and vdW interactions.

The summarized high contact probability profile for GOQD is illustrated in Fig. 7, wherein residues with contact probability larger than 0.15 are selected. These nine residues include Ala1, Phe2, Gln42, Phe47, Leu64, Leu84, Gly86, Asn87, and Lys88. Out of the nine residues, there are five hydrophobic residues (Ala1, Phe2, Phe47, Leu64, and Leu84) and within these, two are aromatic residues (Phe2 and Phe47). Considering that GOQD exposes some phenyl rings on its surface (as shown in Fig. S1), hydrophobic and π-π stacking interactions are considered to largely contribute to GOQD-HIFABP binding. Additionally, since the GOQD structure bears some negatively charged local regions due to the oxygen-containing groups, the electrostatic interaction between the basic residue Lys88 and the negatively charged groups may also contribute to strengthen the GOQD binding to HIFABP (more discussion below).

To further elucidate the binding dynamics, we selected one typical trajectory to analyze the interaction energies (including vdW, Coulomb, and total energies) between the GOQD nanostructure and HIFABP and plotted two conformations at two key time points (Fig. 8). At around 20 ns, GOQD quickly binds to the HIFABP (top) portal with one basal surface of GOQD contacting the HIFABP structure. Accordingly, the vdW and Coulomb energies sharply increase to -72.8 and − 84.3 kJ/mol, respectively, suggesting that both vdW and Coulomb interactions are responsible for the original binding of GOQD to HIFABP. The interfacial binding residues are primarily Gly86, Asn87, and Lys88. The basic residue Lys88 contains a positively charged sidechain, which can form a strong electrostatic interaction with the negatively charged groups on GOQD. This binding pattern undergoes long-time fluctuation. Around 72 ns, GOQD undergoes a 90-degree rotation, resulting in an orientation of GOQD that positions the nanostructure vertically towards the HIFABP portal. At the same time, the interaction energies change to -150.3 kJ/mol (vdW) and − 18.8 kJ/mol (Coulomb). The increment in the vdW energy is attributed to the participation of more contacting residues (Ala1, Phe2, Gln42, Phe47, Leu64, Leu84, and Asn87) in the interaction with GOQD. The loss of interaction involving residue Lys88 leads to the weakened in the value of the Coulomb energy. However, the increase in the interactions related with aromatic (Phe2 and Phe47) and hydrophobic residues (Ala1, Phe2, Phe47, Leu64, and Leu84) augments the contributions of the π-π stacking and hydrophobic interactions between these residues and the aromatic region of GOQD, which also aids in the binding of GOQD to the HIFABP portal. These findings suggest that the obstruction of the GOQD nanostructure to the HIFABP portal is driven by the interplay of vdW, Coulomb, π-π stacking, and hydrophobic interactions.

The results from the MD simulations regarding the possible occlusion of the HIFABP portal by the GQD and GOQD nanostructures do not consider the involvement of the complex physiological milieu surrounding the target protein, i.e., the HIFABP. These shortcomings, which mainly arise by the limitations in the current computational infrastructure, which is unable to handle such systems due to the exceedingly high number of atoms as well as the prohibitively long simulation times, precludes the integration of all the components that may influence the interaction between either GQD or GOQD and HIFABP. Nonetheless, the results presented here offer mechanistic details regarding the interaction of either GQD or GOQD and HIFABP, that could be further assessed using other biophysical characterization. Additionally, it brings caution to the use of such nanostructures by analyzing in detail their interaction with a prototypical protein present in the in epithelial cells of the small intestine. Hence, even though there are biomolecular components that may influence the interaction of the GQD or GOQD with HIFABP, it is likely that the nature of the binding described here will remain valid.

Conclusion

In summary, we construct two simulation systems, GQD/HIFABP and GOQD/HIFABP, to investigate the potential impact of both nanostructure on HIFABP using MD simulations. Our results demonstrate that GQD can bind to the portal of HIFABP, obstructing the entrance of fatty acid molecules and thus, inhibiting the normal physiological function of this carrier protein. The binding of GQD to the HIFABP portal is mediated by vdW, π-π stacking, cation-π, and hydrophobic interactions. Similarly, GOQD also binds to the same portal of HIFABP, suggesting a similar deleterious mechanism of this nanostructure. The adsorption of GOQD to the HIFABP portal is governed by vdW, Coulomb, π-π stacking, and hydrophobic interactions. Overall, our findings not only highlight the perturbation to the carrier function of HIFABP by GQD and GOQD that may result in their potential toxicity but also elucidate the underlying molecular interacting mechanism, which underscores and warns of the detrimental impact of these nanomaterials in future applications.
