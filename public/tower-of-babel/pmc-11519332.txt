JK-1, a useful erythroleukemic cell line model to study a controlled erythroid differentiation from progenitors to terminal erythropoiesis
Authors: Sylvain Metral, Sandrine Genetet, Benoît Gamain, Isabelle Mouro-Chanteloup
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNew hematopoietic cell models have recently emerged through immortalization of CD34 cells to study and understand various molecular mechanisms of erythropoiesis. Here, we characterize the JK-1 CML-derived cell line, previously shown to spontaneously differentiate without cytokines. Using an epigenetic differentiation inhibitor that keeps JK-1 in an early differentiation phase, we characterized 2 progenitor stages: BFU-E JK-1 and CFU-E JK-1 with CD34+/CD36− and CD34−/CD36 + phenotypes respectively. Then, using the PFI-1 inducer known to synchronously control the terminal differentiation of JK-1 cells, 5 precursor stages were obtained (ProE, Baso1-2, PolyC, OrthoC) and characterized via cell morphology, CD49a and Band3 markers. Enlarged phenotyping was carried out for the earlier phase, and expression kinetics of membrane proteins such as RhAG, RhD/CE, CD47, DARC and CD44 were performed on each stage of the terminal phase. Furthermore, since JK-1 offers the unique property of covering a broad spectrum of differentiation stages, we explored deeper the GATA2/GATA1 and the non-erythroid/erythroid spectrin ‘switching’. The possibility of obtaining large quantities of JK-1 cells at each stage of differentiation, as shown in this study, as well as the potential to genetically modify these cells, via CrisprCas9, makes their use of considerable interest for studying pathologies occurring during erythropoiesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76897-7.Keywords: Erythropoiesis, Progenitor, Precursor, JK-1 cells, Expression switchingSubject terms: Biological techniques, Cell biology

Introduction

In recent years, considerable progress has been made in differentiating erythroid precursors ex vivo to obtain immature RBCs. For this purpose, new hematopoietic cell lines have been described : Bel-A, Hudep, Hidep, EJ, PBiEPC and CD34iEPC1–4. These immortalized cell lines are derived from genetically modified Human hematopoietic stem cells (HSC) isolated from either bone marrow (Bel-A), umbilical cord blood (Hudep), Induced Pluripotent Stem (IPS) cells (Hidep) or peripheral blood mononuclear cells PBMC (EJ, PBiEPC and CD34iEPC)1–4. With respect to the differentiation process, similar experimental approaches have been developed based on previous protocols described for CD34 + cells, including the use of cytokines: Stem Cell Factor (SCF) and Erythropoietin (EPO), dexamethasone, with or without doxycycline to obtain undifferentiated or differentiating immortalized cells, respectively5. Although the differentiation stages can be well controlled following such procedures, the use of these factors is costly and therefore severely limits large-scale production, particularly of cells at the end of differentiation which, as expected, are no longer able to divide. In contrast, compared to these cell lines that show low productivity and high production costs, the well-known erythroid cell lines derived from leukemia, such as K562 cells (from chronic myeloid leukemia CML) or HEL cells (from acute myeloid leukemia AML) are still commonly used as erythroid models, since they place themselves in a less expensive context6–8. However, derived from leukemia, these cells can sometimes display non-erythroid markers such as megakaryocytic markers that do not allow them to be identified at a precise stage of erythroid differentiation, and can also be poorly committed to a complete differentiation pathway. Moreover, they have complex karyotypes since HEL exhibit a modal chromosome number of 66 and K562 are considered to display a triploid karyotype (hypotriploidy)6,8.

Here, we performed an enlarged characterization of the JK-1 cell line that was isolated 30 years ago from a patient with CML in erythroid crisis and described, at that time, as the first established human erythroid cell line which spontaneously undergoes terminal differentiation, producing hemoglobin in ex vivo culture, as assessed by their benzidine-positive ratio9,10. JK-1 cells display an interesting karyotype: in addition to the Philadelphia chromosome with the BCR/ ABL gene rearrangement common to 90% of CMLs (but with two translocations in the case of JK-1), these cells exhibit a hyper diploidy with 48 chromosomes, of which only 2 (chr. 8 and 22) are in triplicate9,11. This feature gives these cells a definite advantage for genetic manipulations.

Renewed interest in JK-1 cells has also recently been revealed in their use as a model to study the infection by the malaria parasites Plasmodium falciparum via the CD44 or Basigin (BSG) receptors or Plasmodium vivax via the transferrin receptor (TfR-1)12. In parasitology studies, JK-1 cells show strengths and limitations compared to models of immortalized cell lines13.

Using JK-1 cells for infection studies, Kanjee et al. previously performed epigenetic screens to identify inhibitors that maintain an undifferentiated state but also compounds that induce synchronous differentiation14. To block the cells in their spontaneous differentiation, they identified tranylcypromine (TC), a specific inhibitor of lysine demethylase 1 (LSD1), capable of maintaining cell growth in an undifferentiated state (glycophorin A-negative population)15,16. On the other hand, to induce differentiation, they identified several inducers, including PFI-1, a dihydroquinazolin-2-one inhibitor that blocks the interaction of bromo and extra terminal (BET) (BRD) proteins with acetylated histone tails. They showed that PFI-1 treated JK-1 cells results in a homogenous population of Glycophorin A-positive cells and CD34-cells17,18.

In parasitology studies, changes in the expression of only some known host receptors (Glycophorin A, Glycophorin C, and CR1) were investigated but the different stages of erythroid differentiation of these cells, either maintained in TC to proliferate or induced by PFI-1 to differentiate synchronously, had not yet been fully studied using tools commonly used in hematology14.

The present study provides valuable hematological information on the erythroleukemic cell line JK-1 under proliferating and differentiating conditions, respectively and successively using TC and PFI-1. Taking advantage of specific and well-known markers currently used in erythropoiesis investigations, we were able to characterize the JK-1 cells in detail during their early and terminal differentiation phases by flow cytometry corroborated by microscopy analysis of cells stained with May-Grünwald Giemsa (MGG) and by Western blot analysis19,20.

Altogether, covering the erythropoeisis from progenitors (Burst Forming Unit Erythroblasts (BFU-E) and Colony Forming Unit Erythroblasts (CFU-E)) to the 5 stages of the terminal phase of erythropoiesis (Pro Erythroblasts (ProE), Basophilic Erythroblasts1 (Baso1), Basophilic Erythroblasts2 (Baso2), PolyChromatophilic Erythroblasts (PolyC) and OrthoChromatophilic Erythroblasts (OrthoC) before enucleation), the present study allowed a discrimination and isolation of differentiated JK-1 cells at each stage of the erythroid differentiation.

Results

Identification of progenitors in early phase of JK-1 differentiation

We first assess the impact of tranylcypromine (TC), a chemical agent known to be involved in reprogramming somatic cells into IPS cells, on JK-1 cells. When TC was added to the medium, JK-1 cells were shown to be glycophorin A (GPA) negative (Fig. 1A upper graph), as previously described14,21. Furthermore, using antibodies against two well-established and commonly used markers (CD34 and CD36) of human hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, the GPA-negative JK-1 cells could be split into two main cell types (Fig. 1A lower graph): cells expressing CD34 and negative for CD36 and other cells expressing CD36 and negative for CD34, that we named BFU-E JK-1 and CFU-E JK-1, respectively, by homology with the phenotype of the well-known erythroid progenitors BFU-E and CFU-E22. As long as JK-1 cells were cultured in presence of TC, the rate of cell multiplication in 72 h was 2.3+/− 0.35 (not shown) with a constant proportion in the culture of 59.63%+/− 8.81 CD34− CD36 + JK-1 (CFU-E JK-1) and 19%+/−8.22 of CD34 + CD36− JK-1 (BFU-E JK-1). Focusing on cells with high expression levels of either CD34 or CD36, cell sorting was performed to isolate BFU-E JK-1 and CFU-E JK-1 cells, showing the absence of hemoglobin expression and similar aspect under May-Grünwald Giemsa (MGG) staining (Supplementary Fig. S1A and B).

Following the identification of BFU-E and CFU-E in JK-1 cells cultured in the presence of TC, we assessed the fate of both progenitors under the continuous pressure of TC for 7 days. To this end, a stringent cell sorting was performed and the isolated BFU-E and CFU-E JK-1 cells were re-cultured in the presence of TC. As expected, both types of progenitors were GPA-negative (Fig. 1B, upper part), although a very slight leak of GPA expressing cells could be observed at days 4 and 7 from CFU-E JK-1 (Fig. 1C, upper part). In cultures of sorted BFU-E JK-1, a phenotypic change could be observed over time, with the emergence of increasing CFU-E JK-1 (5.89% and 36.4% at days 4 and 7, respectively) (Fig. 1B, lower part and Fig. 1D, left graph). Interestingly, from sorted CFU-E JK-1, BFU-E appeared at day 4 of cell culture and, although low in quantity, progressively increased over time (Fig. 1C, lower part and Fig. 1D, right graph).

Identification of erythroid precursors during the terminal differentiation of JK-1 cells

Following the addition of the PFI-1 inducer to JK-1 cells previously maintained undifferentiated by TC (Day 0), we assessed the multiplication rate (MR) during the differentiating process that takes place over 2 weeks, on each day of passage by the ratio of the quantity of cells compared to day 0 (Supplementary Fig. S2A). The growth of differentiating cells can then be divided into two phases. In the first phase (days 0 to 8), during which cells are subcultured every two days at a concentration of 0.5 to 1 million cells/mL, a two-day latency period marked by a low MR followed by exponential growth is observed. In the second phase (days 8 to 18), during which cells are subcultured every two days at a concentration of 10 million cells/mL to limit cell death, particularly visible at day 14, an expected decrease of MR for differentiated cells is observed.

The effect of PFI-1 was analyzed using flow cytometry by comparing the expression of chosen markers during differentiation (at days 2, 4, 7, 10 and 14) in absence or presence of PFI-1. Anti-Glycophorin A and anti-Band3 antibodies were used to visualize the appearance of specific markers of the terminal phase of erythropoiesis19. Conversely anti-CD49d was used to test for integrin subunit alpha 4, a marker known to decrease during the last stage of erythroid differentiation19. Flow cytometry dot plot graphs showed the expression of GPA in both conditions (-PFI-1 and + PFI-1), under either spontaneous or induced erythroid differentiation (Supplementary Fig. S3). GPA expression increases heterogeneously in absence of inducer (Supplementary Fig. S3A) and homogeneously in its presence (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Similarly, undifferentiated cells (Band3- CD49d+) at day 16 can be observed in spontaneous condition of differentiation (Supplementary Fig. S4A) while the presence of PFI-1 resulted in a homogeneous differentiation of cells over time, showing synchronization with 98.53% +/− 1.847 of Band3 + CD49d- cells on day 14 (Supplementary Fig. S4B).

JK-1 differentiation under PFI-1 for 17 days was studied in greater detail. Flow cytometry dot plot graphs, with FSC (diffracted light) and SSC (side scatter), revealed an expected size decrease over time and a decrease of the internal granularity (Fig. 2A upper part). Furthermore, an increase of GPA expression was observed (Supplementary Fig. S2B lower graphs). From day 14, a significant proportion of dying cells was detected (12.9% and 50% at days 14 and 17, respectively) (visualized by a gate Fig. 2A upper part).

Band3 and CD49d expression levels during differentiation represented on the x and y axes respectively were then assessed (Fig. 2A lower part) to allow the distinction between the different erythroid populations. This commonly used representation in the field of erythropoiesis allowed us to apply a mask similar to what is commonly used for CD34 + cells analysis during erythroid differentiation to each graph obtained at different differentiation times under PFI-1 on GPA + cells19. This mask was designed to allow distinction between ProE (GPA +, CD49d high, band3-), Baso1 (GPA +, CD49d high, Band3 low), Baso2 (GPA +, CD49d high, Band3 med), PolyC (GPA +, CD49d med, Band3 high) and OrthoC (GPA +, CD49d low, band3 high). The percentage of JK-1 precursors at each differentiation stage could be estimated among GPA + cells at different days after PFI-1 addition (Fig. 2A lower part).

To validate the mask on differentiating JK-1 cells and then confirm the corresponding precursor type, cell sorting was performed using the mask as specific gates to isolate ProE, Baso1, Baso2 and PolyC JK-1 cells on days 2, 4, 7 and 10 respectively. OrthoC JK-1 cells were also isolated from the corresponding gate, on a population of cells differentiating for 14 days, since cells differentiated for 17 days contain a substantial proportion of dead cells. After centrifugation, these sorted cells exhibited pink to red color (Supplementary Fig. S1C), in agreement with the increasing synthesis of hemoglobin during JK-1 differentiation and consistent with the assumed type of precursors.

On sorted cells from day 2, 4, 7, 10 and 14 that were spun onto slides by cytospin and stained using MGG, morphological features conformed to what was expected for each precursor (Fig. 2B). The cell nucleus decreased and condensed in the last stages of differentiation (PolyC JK-1 and OrthoC JK-1). The cytoplasm, by its size increase due to the decrease of the nucleus during the JK-1 erythroid differentiation and by its change in color that is consistent with its transition from basic to acid form (from dark purple in the ProE JK-1, Baso 1 and 2 JK-1 to light purple in the PolyC JK-1 and transparent in OrthoC), also attests to the production of erythroid JK-1 precursors that can be clearly identified on the basis of morphological criteria.

Characterization of the different phases of differentiation by protein expression analysis

An extended phenotype of BFU-E JK-1 (CD34+/CD36−), CFU-E JK-1 (CD34−/CD36+) and intermediate JK-1 progenitors (CD34+/CD36+) (Fig. 3A) was performed by flow cytometry using selected markers, CD45, CD123 (IL3R), CD201 (EPCR), CD117 (c-kit), CD105 (endoglin) and CD38 (Fig. 3B), previously used to better characterized BFU-E and CFU-E progenitors20,23–28. CD105 and CD45 were found to be expressed on 100% of the 3 distinct types of progenitors, albeit with a slight decrease of CD45 expressing CFU-E JK-1. While these markers were found to be expressed on all cells, a variation in their expression level can be seen depending on the progenitor type (Table S5). The percentage of CD201 and CD117 positive cells remained stable for CD201 and for CD117 in the 3 types of progenitors. Regarding CD123 and CD38, the ratio of positive cells clearly decreased during differentiation of progenitor cells until no CD38-positive CFU-E JK-1 cells.

Dynamic changes in the surface expression of well-known erythroid proteins were also investigated by flow cytometry during the terminal differentiation of PFI-1-treated JK-1 cells. CD47 and CD44 two proteins that are not only present at the erythrocyte membrane but also ubiquitously expressed, were tested in the 5 stages of the terminal differentiation of JK-1 cells (ProE JK-1, Baso1 JK-1, Baso2 JK-1, PolyC JK-1, OrthoC JK-1)29,30. Similarly, the Duffy antigen/chemokine receptor (DARC) allowing P. vivax entry into reticulocytes was analyzed, as well as proteins of the Rh complex, RhAG, RhD and RhCE carrying the Rhesus blood group antigens, as expression of these proteins is controlled by erythroid-specific transcription factors31–36. JK-1 cells separately incubated in the presence of PFI-1 for 2, 5, 7, 12 and 14 days, and pooled, in order to obtain precursors of the 5 terminal stages of differentiation in a single tube, were tested with antibodies recognizing the selected membrane proteins. Using the mask described in the previous paragraph and applied to the pooled cells (Fig. 3C) after their labelling with anti-CD49d and anti-Band3, it was possible to estimate the expression levels of the membrane proteins at each stage and thus to monitor changes in their expression during differentiation. Membrane expression kinetics of the Rh complex and DARC proteins were increased (Fig. 3D and E), while those of CD47 and CD44 proteins were decreased (Fig. 3E), in particular from the Baso2 stage. For the Rh complex, a delay is observed between the expression of RhAG and that of both RhD and RhCE proteins. RhAG is present as soon as the ProE stage while RhD and RhCE are essentially detected from the baso2 stage when RhAG expression has doubled compared with its expression at the ProE stage (Fig. 3D).

Western blot analysis of the expression kinetics of several markers during different stages of the JK-1 differentiation. Cell lysates of 107 JK-1 cells sorted at different stages of differentiation of phase 1 (progenitors BFU-E and CFU-E) and phase 2 (precursors, ProE, Baso1, Baso2, PolyC, OrthoC) were loaded on a 4–20% SDS-PAGE from Bio-Rad and transferred onto nitrocellulose membrane subsequently incubated with antibodies that allow the detection of commonly used markers for erythroid differentiation study (CD34, GPA, Band3) and the expression of beta actin (A) and antibodies that highlight a ‘switching’ in expression of GATA transcription factors (GATA1 and GATA2) and alpha-spectrin skeleton proteins (non-erythroid α II spectrin or fodrin and erythroid αβ I spectrin) (B) during the erythroid differentiation of JK-1 cells. *, *#, ** stand for bands corresponding to monomeric, heterodimeric (GPA/GPB) and homodimeric forms of GPA.

Expression kinetics of several markers of erythropoiesis were also investigated by western blot analysis using cell lysates of JK-1 cells sorted at different stages of differentiation of phase 1 (progenitors) and phase 2 (precursors), from BFU-E JK-1 to OrthoC JK-1. Consistent with the flow cytometry results shown above, CD34 expression was only detected in BFU-E JK-1, while both GPA and Band3 expression increased during terminal differentiation of precursors (Fig. 4A). The expression of Band3 was delayed in time compared to that of GPA, in agreement with previous results in erythroid differentiation from CD34 + cells37. More interestingly, using multiplex fluorescent western blots and specific primary antibodies, a ‘switching’ in expression of GATA transcription factors and alpha-spectrin skeleton proteins during the erythroid differentiation of JK-1 cells was highlighted (Fig. 4B). Thus, GATA2 was highly expressed in BFU-E JK-1 and CFU-E JK-1 progenitors and its expression declined in JK-1 precursors, whereas GATA1, weakly expressed in JK-1 progenitors and known to be essential for erythroid differentiation, increased along with the erythroid differentiation, although reduced in late phase 2. Likewise, non-erythroid alpha-spectrin, also called alpha-fodrin, was only expressed in BFU-E JK-1 and CFU-E JK-1 progenitors, while erythroid alpha-spectrin was highly expressed in precursors, although reduced in OrthoC JK-1.

Discusssion

In the present paper, new highlights on in vitro erythroid differentiation are presented from a focused study on the JK-1 erythroid cell line derived from a CML and previously shown to undergo erythroid terminal differentiation spontaneously9. Among other well-known erythroid cell lines, immortal JK-1 erythroleukemia line displays a unique cytokine-free differentiation feature, which is also a major advantage compared to immortalized hematopoietic cell lines that require expensive SCF and EPO for their in vitro erythroid lineage commitment. In addition, a controlled use of these JK-1 cells offers the possibility of access to large quantities of cells at different stages of differentiation for phenotypic and functional studies.

Recently, this property of spontaneous differentiation has been used by Kanjee et al., to study the erythroid receptors for P. falciparum parasite14. During their investigation, the authors identified specific drugs allowing either the maintenance of cells in an undifferentiated state or inducing a synchronous erythroid differentiation from these undifferentiated cells. At the time, the aim was to isolate enough differentiated cells expressing erythroid receptors to investigate the impact of surface protein expression invalidations on infection by several strains of the parasite. Consequently, JK-1 cells maintained in TC were barely characterized. Moreover, the synchronized erythroid differentiation of JK-1 has to date been essentially assessed by the expression of Glycophorin A, and isolation of differentiated cells was only performed based on cell size using FACS (FSC and SSC parameters) and by Percoll–PBS gradient.

In the present study, using the previously described drugs, the two phases (undifferentiated/GPA-negative and differentiated/GPA-positive) as well as the different stages of the terminal erythropoiesis of JK-1 cells were investigated from a hematological point of view, employing tools commonly used to characterize progenitors and precursors of erythroid cells.

JK-1 cells maintained undifferentiated by TC were analyzed, according to their expression of CD36 and CD34, giving rise to CD34 + CD36− and CD34− CD36 + named in the present paper BFU-E JK-1 and CFU-E JK-1 cells respectively, by analogy with the well-known erythroid progenitors20.

When GPA-negative JK-1 cells were sorted and grown for seven days in a culture medium containing TC, an expected evolution of BFU-E JK-1 to CFU-E JK-1 cells was observed. More interestingly, the CFU-E JK-1 cells that could not continue their differentiation course into ProE due to the presence of a differentiation-blocking molecule, are suspected of being recycled into BFU-E JK-1 cells, in accordance with the observation of an increasing, albeit small, quantity, of this progenitor type at days 4 and 7. This was also consistent with the constant proportion of each progenitor found in the culture of unsorted JK-1 cells, the BFU-E JK-1 progenitor representing about 20% of JK-1 cells as long as TC is present, without any increase in dying cells. While such a mechanism for reprogramming CFU-E into BFU-E has not yet been elucidated, a partial role of TC in reprogramming somatic cells into induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) was previously described, showing the possibility of reprogramming differentiated cells without genetic modification, but with only a combination of specific small molecules added in the culture medium21,38.

The present study also allowed a detailed characterization of subpopulations of BFU-E JK-1 and CFU-E JK-1 cells. Using an antibody recognizing the IL3 receptor (CD123), 50% of IL-3R-positive BFU-E could be detected. Interestingly, this marker could not be observed in primary cells of the erythroid lineage isolated from bone marrow of healthy individuals. Moreover, only few BFU-E and no CFU-E of the erythroid lineage were revealed among IL-3R + isolated progenitor cells, this marker being, however, highly expressed in B-lymphoid and myeloid progenitors and found in CFU-GM (Granulocyte/Macrophage)20. This indicates that the CD123 + subpopulation present in JK-1 progenitors may originate from even earlier progenitors that may not be committed to the erythroid lineage. Similarly, the detection of CD38 in a small proportion of JK-1 progenitors, particularly in BFU-E JK-1, strongly suggests the presence in these CD34 + cells of a subset displaying a highly primitive phenotype23,28,39. This subset could be associated with MEPs (megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitors) previously described as cells at an earlier stage than BFU-E and probably having the ability to be differentiated into precursors of megakaryocytes. In the present study, JK-1 cells were shown to be able to grow over a long period in culture medium containing TC, to display expected features but also rare primitive phenotypes of progenitors and, importantly, to expand with a rate higher than two-fold per 72 h of culture40. This unique property offers the possibility, in future studies, of isolating a large number of cells belonging to subpopulations of interest, even minor ones.

Importantly, JK-1 cells maintained in TC were still able to differentiate into erythroid precursors, spontaneously or after synchronization with PFI-1, as previously described14. However, the originality here is the use, for the first time on JK-1 cells, of antibodies detecting the conventional differentiation markers for the study of the terminal phase of erythropoiesis. These are not only anti-GPA but also antibodies raised against two markers known either to decrease (CD49d, the integrin subunit alpha 4) or increase (Band3, erythroid anion exchanger 1, AE1 or SLC4A1) during the last stage of erythroid differentiation19. Flow cytometry analysis confirmed synchronization of the erythroid differentiation of JK-1 cells first treated by TC and then induced by PFI-1 14. More interestingly, the opposite evolution of CD49d and Band3 expression over the time of synchronized differentiation was similar to that described on EPO-differentiated CD34 + primary cells purified from human cord blood or bone marrow19. This allowed the application of a mask, as previously implemented for the study of the differentiation of primary CD34 + cells, leading to several gates of JK-1 cells homogeneously expressing these markers at different days after the addition of PFI-119,37. Compared to in vitro differentiating primary CD34 + cells or genetically immortalized cell lines from CD34 +, 5 stages of erythroid differentiation were identified in JK-1 differentiating cells (ProE JK-1, Baso1 JK-1, Baso2 JK-1, PolyC JK-1 and OrthoC JK-1), but in EPO-free conditions41.

Undifferentiated immortal JK-1 cells that can be easily expanded into large quantities, as discussed above, and from which an erythroid differentiation can be easily controlled, represent an ideal model for the isolation of precursors at different stage of erythroid differentiation. Moreover, the quantity of each isolated JK-1 cell type produced is remarkably high, making it possible to carry out a large number of phenotypic studies as well as functional testing. Surface expression of several transmembrane proteins was assessed in this study by flow cytometry on JK-1 cells from ProE to the OrthoE stage of precursors. Regarding the time course of the Rh complex expression during JK-1 differentiation, RhAG protein was found to be present earlier when compared to the expression of the two other members of the complex, RhCE carrying the Rhc antigen and RhD, as previously described for in vitro differentiating primary CD34 + cells19,42. When Kanjee et al., performed surface proteomics on late PolyC JK-1 cells, they also identify RhAG and RhCE but not RhD, probably because of the high identity (97%) between the RHD and the RHCE genes, inviting caution when analyzing proteomic data14. However, it will be definitely interesting to undertake an extensive and comparative proteomic study not limited to late PolyC JK-1 cells alone, but including all progenitor and precursor stages, in order to better understand the gene expression regulations involved during erythropoiesis. With access to stages of both phases, early erythropoiesis and terminal differentiation of JK-1, a first mechanism corresponding to a ‘switching’ between non-erythroid and erythroid genes expression was highlighted in this study. GATA factor ‘switching’ from GATA2 to GATA1 that contributes to erythroid differentiation was previously demonstrated from embryos and yolk sac mouse hematopoietic cells43. Here, GATA ‘switching’ was observed in differentiating JK-1. Similarly and consequently, Spectrin ’switching’ from non-erythroid Spectrin (fodrin) to erythroid Spectrin was also revealed from JK-1 erythropoiesis44. It would therefore be interesting to use differentiating JK-1 cells in the future to study this ‘switching’ mechanism extended to other essential families of proteins (membrane, skeletal or cytosolic) with one of their members known for erythroid-specific expression due the binding of transcription factor GATA1 to the promotor of their gene.

In conclusion, JK-1 is a useful erythroid cell line, easy to handle and displaying an interesting ability to provide both early erythroid progenitors and, following their synchronized commitment into the terminal erythroid differentiation, precursors with similar characteristics when compared to isolated primary CD34 +.

All of these properties open the way not only to studies of infection by different parasites such as P. falciparum and P. vivax, after Crispr-cas9 invalidation of certain receptors, but also for investigation of viral infections12,14. While Takahashi et al., 1993 showed that DNA replication of parvovirus B19 can occur in JK-1 in vitro, more recent studies indicated that pure populations of CD36 + Erythroid Progenitor Cells (EPCs) expanded and differentiated from CD34 + HSCs were permissive to B19V infection45,46. These studies could be advantageously supplemented by B19 infection tests of CD36 + progenitors isolated from JK-1 cells treated by TC to block spontaneous differentiation. Having an access to the transition between BFU JK-1 and CFU JK-1 would also be a definite advantage for the study of Diamond-Blackfan Anemia, with the possibility of carrying out knock-in by Crispr-cas9 to introduce the mutations responsible for the disease and thus establishing models to better understand the pathology47. Moreover, by using Crispr-cas9 to generate, during the progenitor stages of JK-1, mutated transgenes coding for membrane proteins and by temporally regulating expression of these mutants at selected stages of differentiation in JK-1 precursors, an in-depth study of the impact of these mutations on the assembly of membrane complexes will be enabled. In this context, the use of JK-1 cells whose synchronized differentiation can be controlled will certainly prove interesting for the study of membrane pathologies associated with hemolytic anemia such as spherocytosis, elliptocytosis, dehydrated or hyperhydrated stomatocytosis48.

Methods

JK-1 cell culture

The erythroid cell line JK-1 was obtained from the Leibniz Institute DSMZ-German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures (DSMZ no.: ACC 347) and cultured as described in Supplementary Information.

Trans-2-phenylcyclopropylamine/HCl (also referred to as TranylCypromine or TC from Sigma Aldrich, St Quentin Fallavier, France) was added to stabilize cells in an undifferentiated state (Day0) as described in Supplementary Information. For differentiation of the JK-1 cell line, cells were grown in the same medium but with PFI-1 (Sigma Aldrich, St Quentin Fallavier, France) instead of TC as described in Supplementary Information.

Flow cytometry analysis of progenitors and precursors

Cells grown in the presence of TC were analyzed for simultaneous cell surface expression of CD34, CD36 and GPA by flow cytometry using specific antibodies detailed in the Supplementary Information (Table S1).

For extended phenotyping of BFU-E JK-1 and CFU-E JK-1 progenitor cells, combined with the use of anti-CD34 and anti-CD36 antibodies, 6 other markers of differentiation were tested: CD117 (c-kit), CD201 (EPCR), CD105 (endoglin), CD45, CD123 (IL3R) and CD38 (Table S1).

Cells grown in the presence of the PFI-1 inducer were analyzed by flow cytometry at day 0, 4, 6, 7, 8, 10, 14 and 16 of the differentiation for the simultaneous cell surface expression of Integrin alpha4, GPA and Band3, using anti-CD49d, anti-CD235a and BRIC6 antibodies respectively (Table S1) as described in Supplementary Information.

For kinetics of membrane protein expression during the terminal differentiation, previously described antibodies recognizing RhAG, RhD, Rhc, DARC, CD47, CD44 proteins were used (Table S2). For this study, the expression of each membrane protein was tested from JK-1 cells separately differentiating with PFI-1 for 2, 4, 7, 10 and 14 days that were pooled in order to get, in one tube, 1 million of JK-1 cells displaying the 5 precursor stages: ProE, Baso1, Baso2, PolyC and OrthoC. Each JK-1 pool tube was also incubated with anti-CD49d-APC, BRIC6-PE and anti-GPA-PEcy7 antibodies described above in order to identify, using a mask, the different precursor stages and assess the expression level of the investigated membrane protein at each stage.

Cytospin and slide preparation for May-Grünwald Giemsa (MGG) staining

105 cells in 100 µL of PBS-D were spinned onto slides using the Thermo Scientific Shandon 4 Cytospin with a speed of 700 rpm for 2 min. The cells on slides were fixed with methanol and stained with May-Grünwald Giemsa (MGG) for 20 min. Cells were visualized by image acquisition from a Leica DM2000 inverted microscope.

JK-1 cell sorting

From progenitor JK-1 cells that were maintained at Day0 of erythroid differentiation by TC, gated CD34+/CD36− and CD34−/CD36 + cells among selected GPA negative cells were sorted for MGG staining, flow cytometry analysis or for culturing in presence of TC for at least 7 days.

Erythroid precursors of the terminal differentiation of JK-1 cells were also sorted from cells grown in the presence of PFI-1 for 2, 4, 7, 10 and 14-days using gates to select viable (7AAD negative) and GPA positive cells and a mask allowing the selection of cells expressing several expression levels of CD49d and Band3 in agreement with the 5 stages (ProE, Baso1, Baso2, PolyC, OrthoC). The sorted cells were then tested by MGG staining or flow cytometry analysis.

Cell sorting processes were carried out using a SONY MA900 sorter at speeds of 3000–4000 events per second with a 100 μm nozzle and purity mode.

Western blot analysis

Cell lysates from JK-1 sorted at the different stages of differentiation were tested by western blot as described in Supplementary Information using antibodies anti-CD34, anti-GPA, anti-Band3, anti-actin (Table S3) as well as antibodies raised against proteins of the GATA and spectrin families (Table S3) known to be differentially expressed during erythropoiesis.

For Chemiluminescence detection, except for the anti-actin antibody that is directly coupled with HRP, detection of the other primary antibodies was performed by incubating membranes with appropriate secondary antibodies (Table S3) for 1 h at Room Temperature (RT). Revelation was performed using the ECL™ Prime Kit (Cytiva Amersham™, Vélizy Villacoublay, France).

For the multiplex fluorescent western blotting, primary (Overnight at 4 °C – 2 h at RT) and secondary antibodies (30 min at RT) (Table S3) were used.

Revelations were performed using the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Marne La Coquette, France).
