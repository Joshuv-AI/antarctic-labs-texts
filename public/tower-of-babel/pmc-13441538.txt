Detection of sex chromosome-specific DNA sequences by polymerase chain reaction using hair DNA in cattle
Authors: Toma HAMANO, Tomomi KANAZAWA, Toru TAKAHASHI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To establish a simpler polymerase chain reaction (PCR) strategy for detecting sex chromosome-specific DNA sequences in cattle, we verified the feasibility of using hair-derived DNA as a PCR template. In the control steer, multiplex PCR using hair-derived DNA resulted in the amplification of two amplicons: one derived from the X chromosome-harboring 1.715 satellite DNA (365 bp) and another from the Y chromosome-harboring btDYZ-1 sequence (199 bp). In heterosexual twin females with XX/XY chimerism confirmed by chromosome analyses, multiplex PCR analyses with hair DNA determined both 365 and 199 bp amplicons in five of the six cases. In the remaining case, multiplex PCR analysis using hair DNA revealed the amplification of only the 365-bp product; however, using singleplex PCR, the 199-bp product was successfully amplified from hair DNA. The results of the present study suggest that sex chromosome-specific DNA sequences can be detected by PCR using bovine hair-derived DNA.

Several reproductive disorders in cattle, including freemartin [1], XY-female [2], and hermaphroditism [3], have been reported to be associated with abnormalities of sex chromosome karyotypes. In the past, cytogenetic analysis of the metaphase plates of chromosomal specimens was the only way to detect abnormal karyotypes [4]. With the introduction of PCR, the amplification of sex chromosome-specific DNA sequences has become widely used as a key step in analyses, from single-cell blastomeres [5] to individual cattle [6]. In the clinical diagnosis of congenital karyotype abnormalities in cattle, peripheral blood leukocytes are used to prepare metaphase specimens for chromosome analysis and genomic DNA for PCR [6]. In practice, blood collection and subsequent transport to the laboratory are laborious and subject to constraints, such as refrigeration and time from collection to analysis.

Bovine genomic testing has been widely adopted over the last two decades as a technique for predicting performance later in life. In 2008, the principal DNA sources for cattle genotyping in the United States were semen from bull sires and blood from cows. However, as of 2010, most DNA has been collected from hair for convenience [7].

Human hair samples including the roots and shafts contain minute but detectable amounts of genomic DNA [8]. Previous studies have shown that genomic DNA from human keratinized cells located in the hair shaft is highly degraded and generally only approximately 100 base pairs (bp) in size. Despite the low yield and poor quality of hair DNA, hair samples remain an attractive choice for PCR amplification in domestic animals because of their non-invasive nature and ready availability.

Currently, there are no reports of congenitally abnormal karyotypes in cattle diagnosed by PCR amplification of hair-derived DNA. Thus, a PCR-based approach using hair DNA may be a simple technique for identifying abnormal karyotypes in cattle. To evaluate the feasibility of sex-specific DNA amplification from hair-derived DNA in cattle, hair-derived DNA was extracted and subjected to PCR to detect the X chromosome-derived 1.715 satellite DNA [9] and Y chromosome-derived btDYZ-1 [10] sequences. This study aimed to establish a diagnostic procedure for abnormal karyotypes based on non-invasive sampling.

Niku et al. reported that the proportion of male-derived cells among non-hematopoietic cells in various tissues of bovine heterosexual twin females was < 1%; however, the ratio in hair was not examined [11]. Niku et al. [11] suggested that XY cells contribute less to non-hematopoietic tissues than circulating leukocytes do in bovine heterosexual twin females. Fujishiro et al. reported that a Y chromosome-specific btDYZ-1 sequence could be detected with an XY chimerism ratio of 0.08% [6]. It is plausible that the results obtained in the present study were near the threshold between the sufficient (case nos. 1–5) and insufficient (case no. 6) contributions of XY cells in hair.

However, singleplex PCR targeting a single amplicon in each reaction yielded either 365- or 199-bp amplicons in case 6 (Figs. 2D and 2E). This result suggests that the singleplex PCR strategy focusing on a single amplicon has the significant advantage of reducing the enzymatic burden of amplification.

The results of singleplex PCR suggest that a combinatorial improvement in sample collection, DNA extraction methods, and target sequence selection and amplification reaction can enhance the reliability of PCR-based diagnosis.

In conclusion, the results of this study suggest that sex chromosome-specific DNA sequences can be detected by PCR using hair-derived DNA from cattle. However, in the context of diagnosing freemartin syndrome in heterosexual twin females, both the low contribution of male cells to non-hematopoietic lineages and the high level of DNA degradation in the keratinized hair shaft may lead to insufficient sensitivity.

Materials and Methods

Unless otherwise noted, all reagents used in this study were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corpration (Osaka, Japan).

The design of the animal experiments was approved by the Ethical Review Board of Iwate University (A202304). For the experiments in control steers and heifers, animal handling was conducted on a commercial dairy farm in Takizawa, Iwate Prefecture, Japan, from October 2024 to March 2025. The Holstein-Friesian steers and heifers used in the present study were fed milk replacer, synthetic milk, and hay according to their stage of growth. Blood samples were collected from the calves via venipuncture into heparinized tubes and transferred to the laboratory under refrigerated conditions. Hair samples were collected by hand-plucking from the back after gentle brushing to remove loose hair. The collected hair samples were then transferred to ambient temperature.

Blood and hair samples of heterosexual twin female dairy cattle were obtained by veterinary clinicians during routine practice in the Tohoku region of Japan. The collected samples were shipped via a refrigerated courier to the laboratory the day after collection.

Throughout the study, blood-derived DNA was extracted from heparinized whole blood using the ISOSPIN Blood and Plasma DNA Kit (Nippon Gene, Toyama, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the experiments in case nos. 1–3 of heterosexual twin females, hair-derived DNA was extracted from whole hairs or both halves of cut hairs using the same ISOSPIN kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the remaining (nos. 4–6 of heterosexual twin females, control steers, and control heifers, hair-derived DNA was extracted using an ISOHAIR kit (Nippon Gene) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Isolated DNA samples were stored at –30°C until used for PCR analyses.

Two sets of PCR primers were designed to amplify sex chromosome-specific sequences: the X chromosome-specific 1.715 satellite DNA (5′- tggaagcaaagaaccccgct-3′, 5′- aagccctcgagagaccttct-3′) and the Y chromosome specific btDYZ-1 sequence (5′-gtgcacgcgattctcacctct-3′, 5′- acacacacacacgacaaaatattg-3′), yielding products of 365 and 199 bp, respectively [6]. Primers were synthesized by Eurofins Genomics (Tokyo, Japan).

PCR amplification was carried out in 0.2-ml tubes containing 12.5 µl Q5 High fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), 5 µl primer mix (each of the four primers at 10 µM), and 7.5 µl template (containing isolated DNA ranging from 21 to 402 ng). The amplification cycle was as follows: 98°C for 2 min for initial denaturation, 98°C for 10 sec for cycling denaturation, 56°C for 30 sec for annealing, 72°C for 30 sec for extension, and 72°C for 10 min for the final extension. Thirty-five amplification cycles from denaturation to extension were performed using an automated thermal cycler (Dice, TaKaRa Bio, Shiga, Japan). After cycling, PCR mixtures were subjected to 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, stained with ethidium bromide, and viewed under UV irradiation.

For singleplex PCR in case no. 6, primers targeting either 1.715 satellite DNA or btDYZ-1 were added in separate reactions using the same amounts of hair DNA templates as in the multiplex reaction.

Collected whole blood was cultured with RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and phytohemagglutinin-M (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 38°C for 3 days in an atmosphere of 5% CO2 in air under humidified conditions. On the fourth day of culture, cells were treated with 10 µg/ml colcemid (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 90 min, further treated with 0.075 M potassium chloride as a hypotonic treatment, and then fixed with Carnoy’s solution. The fixed cells were dropped onto glass slides, allowed to dry, and then stained with Giemsa solution. At least 20 metaphase plates per case were examined under a light microscope.
