Evidence-based design of a patient-specific quality assurance program through retrospective analysis of pretreatment and in vivo results
Authors: Esther Decabooter, Sebastiaan M.J.J.G. Nijsten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeAs radiotherapy techniques become increasingly complex, patient-specific quality assurance (PSQA) must evolve to detect a broad spectrum of potential failure modes across the treatment chain. This study presents a extensive evaluation of a multi-modal PSQA program by assessing its ability to detect clinically relevant failure modes and quantifying its efficacy.Materials and MethodsA five-year retrospective analysis was conducted of an integrated PSQA program implemented at Maastro, combining secondary dose calculations, log-file based verification, and Electronic Portal Dosimetry (EPD) in both pretreatment and in vivo workflows. The efficacy of individual QA methods and the overall program was quantified using positive predictive values (PPVs). PSQA outcomes were linked to potential improvements in treatment planning strategies and delivery techniques.ResultsA total of 18,558 treatment plans were analyzed in the pretreatment workflow and 1817 treatment plans in the in vivo workflow. Nearly all high-priority failure modes could be detected except machine output miscalibration. Combining PSQA methods substantially improved PPVs across failure categories. The in vivo workflow flagged the most failure modes and additionally gives insight into possible improvements in planning strategies or treatment techniques.ConclusionsThis retrospective analysis demonstrated that a comprehensive framework incorporating independent, purpose-specific PSQA methods is essential to detect a wide range of treatment deviations. Combining complementary methods improves the reliability and interpretability of results. The clinical impact of a PSQA program can be further enhanced by adopting more clinically meaningful evaluation metrics and using the results to guide the optimization of new treatment strategies.

Introduction

As radiotherapy treatment modalities get increasingly more complex, patient-specific quality assurance (PSQA) becomes more important in ensuring accurate and safe dose delivery. Conventional phantom measurement based PSQA approaches including ionization chamber measurements [1], [2], [3], [4], film measurements [5], [6], [7] or electronic portal imaging device (EPID) measurements [8], [9], [10], [11] have historically formed the basis for verifying treatment plans prior to delivery. However, the introduction of highly modulated techniques including volumetrically modulated arc therapy (VMAT), stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) and adaptive radiotherapy have increased the number and diversity of possible failure modes. These methods alone may not adequately capture the full spectrum of potential failure modes. Advances in cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) and the availability of on-board EPID have enabled the development of in vivo dose verification techniques, providing an additional and complementary layer of quality assurance during treatment delivery [12], [13], [14]. As a result, PSQA is no longer limited to pretreatment plan validation but can also be used to monitor the accuracy and consistency of dose delivery throughout the entire treatment course.

This study presents a retrospective analysis of PSQA data over the last five years. Retrospective analyses of PSQA data remain scarce, with most large-scale efforts concentrating on treatment delivery assessed through a single PSQA modality [15], [16]. This PSQA program integrates multiple PSQA components currently used in clinical practice: secondary dose calculations (SDC) using an independent dose calculation algorithm, dose verification based on machine log files (LOG), and Electronic Portal Dosimetry (EPD) both pretreatment and in vivo. These in-house developed PSQA methods are integrated into an automated framework. While PSQA is widely implemented, its ability to consistently detect clinically meaningful errors, its diagnostic value, and its contribution to improving patient outcomes are still under active investigation [17].

The aim of this study was to evaluate an institutional PSQA program through three objectives. First, the current program was assessed whether it identifies clinically relevant failure modes using a large retrospective analysis of PSQA results. Second, the effectiveness of the PSQA program was quantified by determining the positive predictive values (PPVs) of individual PSQA methods and the overall workflow, thereby identifying how reliably detected deviations represent true errors. Third, it was explored how PSQA can improve treatment planning strategies and delivery techniques. Ultimately, this work aimed to provide a critical evaluation of a complete multi-modal PSQA program and evidence-based insights to support the optimization of future PSQA strategies.

Materials and methods

PSQA program

Fig. 1 illustrates the in-house developed PSQA program containing both a pretreatment plan validation workflow and an in vivo treatment delivery verification workflow. All PSQA methods are based on three-dimensional (3D) dose reconstructions derived from either predicted or measured two-dimensional portal dose images (PDIs). The reconstructed dose reconstructions are compared to either the treatment planning dose or SDC using a global 3D gamma analysis. Failed results are reviewed by a medical physicist to determine whether corrective actions or plan adaptations are required.Fig. 1Illustration PSQA program.Fig. 1

Illustration PSQA program.

Pretreatment plan validation comprises three PSQA methods: a secondary dose calculation on a planning CT (SDCPCT), a dose verification based on machine log files (LOG), and EPD using pretreatment acquired portal images (EPDP). For all pretreatment methods, the dose is compared to the treatment planning dose. Log file PSQA results are in turn used to identify interrupted deliveries and erroneous EPID acquisitions.

In vivo treatment dose verification was performed throughout treatment and consists out of two secondary dose reconstructions based on kV Cone Beam CT scans using 1) the predicted 2D portal dose images based on the original treatment plan (SDCCBCT) or 2) the measured transit relative portal dose images (EPDT). Each SDCCBCT dose was compared to the SDCPCT dose to minimize dose differences due to 3D dose calculation algorithms. A single fraction with a failed SDCCBCT resulted in the entire plan being marked as failed for this PSQA method. For patients passing SDCCBCTs, EPDT measurements were further evaluated. Transit portal dose verification is performed for a selection of patients treated with a curative intent during the first three fractions of the treatment course and then once a week. Additional technical details are provided in the Supplementary Materials A.

Evaluation methods

A retrospective analysis of the pretreatment workflow was performed on 18,558 treatment plans delivered between January 2020 and December 2024 at Maastro (IRB PO0593). Treatment plans with failed secondary dose calculations were excluded from subsequent LOG or EPDP evaluations. Consequently, LOG or EPDP analysis where available for 14,380 plans and 11,885 plans, respectively. The in vivo workflow was evaluated between February 2024 and December 2024 and included 13,658 SDCCBCT evaluations from 1817 unique treatment plans. Plans with failed SDCCBCT results were excluded from EPDT evaluation, leaving 1432 plans for analysis. Treatment site, technique, and machine distributions are provided in Fig. 2. All treatment plans were generated using Eclipse treatment planning system (TPS) using either the Acuros (v15.5.11 or v16.1.0) or AAA (v15.5.11 or v16.1.0) dose calculation algorithm (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA). Treatments were delivered on TrueBeam systems equipped with High-Definition or Millennium MLCs (2020–2024), as well as Halcyon and Ethos systems (2024) (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA). PSQA findings were compared with high-risk failure modes identified by the ESTRO-AAPM task group [18]. Detection of these failure modes was used as a measure of PSQA program completeness.Fig. 2Illustration of the patient population evaluated using the PSQA program for pretreatment (5 years data) and in vivo plans (1 year data). Program efficacy of the program was assessed using positive predictive value (PPV) calculated for each individual PSQA method individually and for combined workflow. The PPV is calculated as the percentage of true positive results relative to the total number of failed results. Failure was classified into four predefined failure categories: treatment plan generation, plan transfer and deliverability, inter- and intrafractional dose delivery. A PSQA failure attributed to the category under evaluation was considered a true positive; all others were classified as false positive.

Finally, PSQA outcomes were linked to potential improvements in treatment planning and delivery techniques by identifying recurring deviation patterns that could initiate protocol adaptations, planning strategies, and technological optimizations.

Results

Overall, 1.29% of treatment plans (N = 239) failed SDC evaluation (Fig. 3). False-positive results occurred in 0.55% of plans (N = 102) due to PSQA framework limitations, including limited portal dose image field of view (FOV), absence of electron contamination in the secondary dose algorithm and differences in HU binning discrepancies between the planning CT and CBCT. AAA-related dose calculation inaccuracies accounted for 0.39% (N = 72) of the plans, comprising dose overestimation in bone structures (0.32%, N = 59) and underestimation in lung tissue (0.07%, N = 13). Omission of immobilization devices during planning affected 0.21% of the plans (N = 39) while Acuros systematically underestimated dose for jaw-tracked fields smaller than 3 × 3 cm2 in 0.14% of plans (N = 26).Fig. 3Overview of pretreatment plan validation results and failure modes (SDCPCT: Secondary Dose Calculation on the planning CT scan; LOG: dose verification based on machine log files; EPDP: pretreatment Electronic Portal Dosimetry). Log file analysis identified dose delivery discrepancies in 26 of 14,380 evaluated plans. Most failed LOG reports coincided with passed SDC results showing low agreement scores, suggesting dose calculation uncertainties, 7 of these plans were calculated with AAA. Only 9 failed LOG plans also failed the EPDP PSQA, combining both combined dose calculation and dose delivery uncertainties. An additional 107 plans had a failed EPDP result, typically involving heterogeneous and complex fluence patterns. In the Supplementary Materials (S1) an example of a case failing both LOG and EPDP is provided. No protocol based PSQA was performed for 21.2% of LOG evaluations and for 34.7% of EPDP according to protocol (e.g. palliation or phantom QA).

SDCCBCT identified no clinically relevant deviations in 78.8% of treatment plans (Fig. 4). At least one failing fraction was observed in 21.2% of plans (N = 385), with patient alignment errors being the most common failure mode (9.1%, N = 166), predominantly in breast treatments. Additional alignment-related deviations were observed for lung treatments (N = 28), extremities (N = 21), bolus placement (N = 17), and shoulder positioning (N = 12). CBCT imaging limitations generated false positives in 5.1% of cases, primarily due to limited FOV (N = 53), HU differences between planning CT and CBCT (N = 31), and prosthesis-related artefacts (N = 8). The third category encompassed dose differences due to interfractional anatomical changes which were further subdivided into systematic and random differences. Systematic anatomical changes were observed in 3.9% of plans and included atelectasis or tumor shrinkage (N = 51), weight fluctuations (N = 11) and breast swelling or seroma formation (N = 9). Random anatomical variations occurred in 2.9% of plans and were mainly associated with diaphragm motion (N = 33) and variable abdominal air pockets (N = 20). Procedural deviations, such as excessive 4D-CT belt compression or omission of rectal balloons, were observed infrequently. Intrafractional motion affected only 0.5% of plans (N = 9), occurring in breast breath-hold treatments (N = 3), head-and-neck treatments with head displacements (N = 3), and abdominal treatments with variable air content (N = 3).Fig. 4Overview of in vivo treatment dose verification results and failure modes (SDCCBCT: Secondary Dose Calculation on a CBCT scan; EPDT: in vivo Electronic Portal Dosimetry). Discussion

This large-scale retrospective study showed that combining SDC, log file analysis, EPD, and in vivo dosimetry enables detection of complementary failure modes throughout the radiotherapy workflow. In vivo PSQA identified the most clinically relevant deviations, while pretreatment methods were most effective for planning and deliverability issues. Despite limited PPVs of individual methods, their combination improved error detection and interpretation, supporting the use of integrated PSQA frameworks to enhance radiotherapy quality and safety.

The PSQA program identified failure modes across the entire radiotherapy workflow. Consistent with the ESTRO-AAPM task group ranking, limitations of TPS dose calculation algorithms were among the most important findings. The observed dose overestimation in bone and underestimation in lung tissue by AAA agrees with previous reports [19]. In addition, a small subset of plans showed systematic dose underestimation when jaw tracking was applied to small fields, confirming earlier findings by Swinnen et al. [20]. Despite internal guidelines discouraging jaw tracking for very small fields, PSQA identified plans that did not adhere to this recommendation. Furthermore, 0.21% of plans failed due to omission of immobilization devices during treatment planning. Inclusion of these devices in the SDCPCT evaluation [21] revealed clinically relevant dose discrepancies, resulting in a protocol change requiring their consideration during treatment planning.

Another failure mode involved inaccuracies in TPS input data, including multi leaf collimator (MLC) modeling, dynamic leaf gap (DLG) values, MLC transmission values, percentage depth dose (PDD) curves, tissue-maximum-ratio (TMR), off-axis factors (OAF), etc. A distinction was made between errors arising from inconsistencies during measurement of these data and errors introduced during TPS commissioning. PDIs were generated from the same measurement data used by the TPS. This caused the PSQA methods SDCP, LOG and SDCCBCT to miss errors in measurement of the TPS data. Since the EPD methods, both pretreatment and in vivo, made use of an independent fluence measurement, these PSQA methods could flag this failure mode. This reinforces the need for accurate acquisition of TPS input data and for PSQA methods that are fully independent of TPS commissioning. As noted by Wong et al., PSQA effectiveness diminishes when both systems rely on the same underlying data [22]. An improvement to the framework could be the adoption of a direct 3D forward dose calculation instead of using predicted 2D PDIs for the SDC and LOG PSQA methods, using independently measured input data. The use of standardized “golden beam data” rather than site-specific commissioning measurements may further reduce variability and enhance reliability [23]. The failure mode that could not be found by the PSQA framework is the output calibration. Since a daily calibration of our EPD panel neglecting linac output variations is performed, the impact of output variations on the portal dose measurements is removed (< 1%). This failure mode in return could be found by performing regular machine output measurements. Thus, combining a PSQA program with rigorous machine QA can be crucial to ensure a complete and robust PSQA program.

A severe failure mode can be a mismatch between planned and delivered MLC positions. By combining LOG PSQA with EPID measurements, a distinction could be made between plan transfer errors, incomplete EPD acquisitions, and deliverability issues e.g. overmodulated MLC sequences. EPD and LOG PSQA continue to be valuable tools when implementing new treatment techniques and evaluating emerging platforms. Additionally, correlating EPD PSQA outcomes with plan complexity metrics may offer predictive insight into deliverability during treatment planning, an avenue for future research [24], [25], [26]. Other high severity failure modes (including incorrect energy selection, partial delivery, or incorrect MU) could also be detected through EPD and/or LOG PSQA. Although LOG PSQA offers a less labor-intensive alternative to measurement based PSQA, not all delivery issues can be captured by this method alone. Uncertainties such as erroneous beam tuning and slowly changing beam properties can only be found with an independent fluence measurement.

The most frequently observed failure modes were identified by the in vivo PSQA methods. Esposito et al. reported out-of-tolerance rates for EPID-based in vivo dosimetry of 5–18% depending on technique and system, which is consistent with the 21.2% of plans showing at least one failed SDCCBCT in the cohort [27]. In vivo PSQA provided valuable insight into how interfractional and intrafractional variations influence the delivered dose. Although most finding are reported in literature [27], [28], [29], the in vivo PSQA results offer an efficient way for detecting and mitigating such uncertainties during the treatment. The incidence of positioning-related deviations (~9%) and anatomical changes (~3–7%) observed in the cohort is consistent with previously reported in vivo dosimetry studies, where positioning errors were reduced from approximately 10% to 3–5% and anatomical variation from ~10% to ~3–4% following workflow adaptations [30]. Despite established clinical protocols, patient-specific errors (such as the omission of a rectal balloon during treatment) can still occur and may substantially affect treatment outcome. The EPDT findings highlight the limited, yet clinically relevant role of intrafractional motion monitoring, particularly in anatomically mobile regions or motion-sensitive protocols. As these results are evaluated only after treatment delivery, their immediate clinical actionability is more limited for hypofractionated treatments than for hyperfractionated treatments. Real-time evaluation would be required to enable adaptive intervention during treatment, but can give insight for protocol improvements.

The PPV provided insight into the reliability of a positive PSQA result. As shown in Table 2, the PPVs of the individual PSQA methods across the different failure mode categories were generally very low. This is primarily explained by the low prevalence of true failures: only a small subset of plans exhibited failing PSQA results. The low prevalence reflects the improvements in treatment delivery techniques driven by insights from the PSQA program. For the treatment plan generation and interfractional dose delivery categories, the PPVs are equal to those of the overall program, as false positives from other failure modes cannot occur. In the case of SDCCBCT, each dose was compared to a SDCPCT, thereby minimizing differences related to three-dimensional dose calculation algorithms. In contrast, substantial increases in PPV are observed between plan transfer and deliverability, and intrafractional dose delivery. Both categories are characterized by a low prevalence, meaning that most detected failures are attributable to other failure modes rather than the one being tested. Although EPDT can detect all failure categories, its PPVs remain low, as the results may reflect multiple underlying error sources. Consequently, interpretation is challenging when this method is used in isolation, since dose differences arising from different failure modes may compensate for each other and obscure the true cause. The higher PPV observed for the PSQA program demonstrated that combining multiple methods enhances the robustness of error detection and improved the interpretability of the results. PPVs can additionally be increased by improving PSQA methods and hence reducing the number of false positives. E.g. using more accurate Monte Carlo algorithms and Hypersight CBCT technology with extended FOV can further enable reliable dose evaluation [31]. Artificial intelligence may strengthen future PSQA frameworks by enabling deeper analysis of complex datasets, improving failure mode identification and supporting clinical decision making [32], [33], [34]. The PPV should be interpreted in the context of the applied gamma criteria and pass rate thresholds. Stricter thresholds typically increase sensitivity but may lead to more false positives and a lower PPV, whereas less stringent thresholds can improve PPV at the cost of increased false negatives.

Evaluating PSQA results allow for identifying limitations within the radiotherapy workflow but also in supporting the safe implementation and evaluation of new technologies in clinical practice [28]. For example, surface tracking may improve setup accuracy in breast and lung treatments when arm positioning often leads to dose discrepancies [35], [36]. Offline adaptation may be appropriate for interfractional changes such as tumor regression or weight loss [29], whereas online adaptation may be required for intrafractional variations such as air pocket movement [37], [38]. Within the current framework, treatment plans are primarily flagged based on gamma analysis. While the standard (3%, 3 mm) criteria were applied, tailoring gamma criteria per treatment indication, such as (5%, 1 mm) for stereotactic treatments, may provide additional value. The gamma metric is sensitive to dose discrepancies but not always linked directly to clinical relevance. The integration of more clinically meaningful endpoints, such as dose–volume histogram parameters or normal tissue complication probability metrics, could enhance decision making. In addition, dose accumulation approaches may provide a more complete understanding of the impact of interfractional variations. To correctly evaluate the impact of PSQA results, good registration of adaptation rates and reasons for the adaptation should be tracked.

This large-scale retrospective analysis demonstrates that complementary pretreatment and in vivo PSQA methods detect different classes of treatment deviations and provide distinct but valuable information. Maintaining methodological independence while integrating these approaches within a coordinated framework improves the interpretation of QA findings, facilitates the identification of underlying causes, and supports targeted corrective actions. Together, these results underscore the importance of a comprehensive PSQA strategy for optimizing patient treatments and ensuring robust quality assurance in modern radiotherapy.
