Making the Most of a Transplant ID Conference: A Practical Guide for Trainees and Early‐Career Faculty
Authors: Chelsea A Gorsline, Rebecca N Kumar, Denise J McCulloch, Maheen Z Abidi, Ajit P Limaye, Courtney E Harris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAttending conferences is a foundational component of professional growth in transplant infectious diseases (TIDs), yet trainees and early‐career faculty often face an overwhelming array of meeting options and limited guidance on how to maximize the experience. This report outlines practical strategies for selecting the most impactful conferences, preparing effectively, engaging with poster sessions, cultivating meaningful professional connections, and incorporating rest and reflection into conference travel. Unlike previous discussions hosted by the TID Early Career Network (TxIDECN), which typically occurred over social media, this was a live expert panel with audience participation at the 2025 Fred Hutch Symposium on Infectious Diseases in the Immunocompromised Host on May 13, 2025, in Seattle, Washington. Here, we summarize that discussion with actionable tools to navigate conferences intentionally and translate these experiences into sustained academic and career development for emerging TID professionals. Keywords: conference, networking, trainee, transplant infectious diseases

Introduction

Transplant infectious diseases (TID) offers numerous opportunities for professional development through conferences spanning solid organ transplant (SOT), cellular therapy, infectious disease, microbiology, infection prevention, and pharmacy. Because time away from clinical duties and financial constraints often limit conference attendance, trainees and early‐career members must be strategic in selecting the right conferences to attend and intentional in maximizing the benefits of the conferences they attend.

To help address this need, the TID Early Career Network (TxIDECN) (@TxID_Fellows on X; @txidfellows.bsky.social on BlueSky) hosted a live panel discussion at the biennial Fred Hutch Symposium on Infectious Diseases in the Immunocompromised Host on May 13, 2025, in Seattle, Washington. This interactive session was targeted to trainees and focused on six key questions created by TxIDECN (C.E.H., C.G., and R.N.K.) that were addressed to the expert panel (D.J.M., M.Z.A., and A.P.L.), with active audience participation (see Appendix in the Supporting Information).

Here, we review practical strategies for selecting the right conference and effective preconference preparation, engaging with poster sessions and presenters, networking with intention, navigating conferences as an introvert, and balancing conference activities with personal time.

When selecting a conference, attendees should consider what to prioritize; for instance, is the goal primarily for learning, networking, and/or socializing with colleagues? A summary of factors to consider when selecting a conference is reviewed in Table 2. For those early in their career, one of the most important considerations may be expanding clinical knowledge. For example, TID clinicians may consider attending the International Society of Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) annual meeting instead of the American Transplant Congress (ATC) if their center is predominantly a thoracic transplant center. In addition, when reviewing a conference's program, consider what meetings may be occurring during the conference, such as guideline panel meetings or society working groups. These sessions provide opportunities for early engagement with committees and specialty groups.

Presenting one's own research or supporting the presentation of a mentee (fellow, resident, or medical student) can also shape conference selection. As trainees transition to faculty and faculty roles evolve, conference attendance may better align with mentorship responsibilities.

Finally, logistics, finances, and feasibility matter. International conferences require additional travel time, financial investment, and coordination of family responsibilities such as childcare. These responsibilities affect the length of a trip, the number of trips per year, and the distance feasible for travel. In addition, attending conferences can be financially challenging for trainees. To counteract this, many conferences offer travel grants to support trainees or those presenting abstracts. We recommend considering hotel sharing with a colleague or staying at other lodging not affiliated with the conference, such as rental properties that are convenient yet more affordable.

Preparing for a Conference

Preparing for a conference requires a thoughtful balance of priorities, including academic learning, networking, presentation responsibilities, and—when relevant—moderating or leading sessions. For many trainees and early‐career faculty, conferences are a limited opportunity to meet collaborators, connect with mentors, and engage with emerging science. Because these compete for time and attention, advance planning is essential. Effective preparation includes reviewing the conference schedule, identifying relevant sessions, arranging meetings with collaborators, and setting realistic goals for networking and scientific engagement. With an organized plan, attendees can better ensure that the experience is both productive and personally fulfilling. This can be divided into preparing for the scientific content and preparing to network.

Once a conference has been selected, preparation is essential to maximize the academic and networking value. If a conference is held in a different time zone, consider incremental adjustments to sleep leading up to travel. Regarding scientific content, abstracts are typically made available in the month leading up to the conference. Reviewing abstracts in advance allows attendees to identify posters and oral sessions that align with their interests. This is particularly important for large conferences, such as IDWeek or the ATC, as multiple sessions occurring simultaneously may pique your interest. Many conferences have mobile applications with options to build a customized schedule. Attendees may also create their own schedule, such as on paper or an electronic calendar, and we recommend the inclusion of session locations and room numbers when creating these. In addition, be aware that meetings not affiliated with the conference need to be considered when making plans. If there are two sessions that are of equal interest, consider if there is an option to view sessions later via an online platform, or one more likely to be attended by individuals with whom to network or collaborate. In addition, allow for flexibility should new or interesting opportunities arise.

Networking preparation is an equally important priority before arriving at the conference [1]. Contacting mentors, collaborators, former co‐fellows, and friends in the field prior to the conference increases the likelihood of meeting as schedules become busy once sessions begin. These meetings can occur during academic programming, which is acceptable and often necessary. The chance to build professional relationships is limited and harder to replicate after the conference ends, and sessions may be viewed after the conference ends through virtual platforms. Similarly, connecting with former classmates, co‐residents, or co‐fellows, at the expense of attending a session is also okay. These opportunities provide an opportunity to discuss a variety of aspects of work, such as navigating difficult situations at work or challenges in balancing work and home life [2]. Networking can also occur briefly before and after sessions, or over coffee, drinks, or a meal. Prioritizing and nurturing social support systems, such as mentorship and friendship, can also help prevent physician burnout [3].

Engaging With Poster Presenters and Speakers

Once at the conference, it is time to enjoy the scientific content! While general sessions may provide overviews and updates on a given topic, the newest research is typically found at the oral abstract sessions and the poster hall.

Poster sessions represent one of the richest sources of new scientific information at conferences, yet the number of posters and the brief time for viewing can be overwhelming without planning. In addition, given the limited time of the poster sessions, it is impossible to engage with every poster and presenter meaningfully. Lacking a plan can result in aimless meandering or unintended diversions while missing out on content of interest. However, some work ahead of time to prepare will help prevent this.

Prior to the session, such as a few days in advance or while traveling, we recommend reviewing poster session abstracts to prioritize those of interest. Categorizing abstracts into tiers of importance (e.g., “must see,” “good to see,” and “lower interest”) can help ensure the most valuable content is not missed due to time constraints or unplanned interruptions. Consider a critical review of the abstracts to have more meaningful interactions with presenters.

Flexibility during poster sessions is needed. If there is a poster that sparks interest but was not previously reviewed or time is limited, consider reaching out to the presenter via email, social media, platforms such as LinkedIn, or the conference's application.

For trainees and early‐career faculty, we highly encourage participation in poster walks. Poster walks are typically arranged in advance with a specific focus, such as a topic or trainee presentations. In this format, several people visit posters together, which allows the presenter to give their pitch and interact with the group [4]. Poster walks may pair trainees or early‐career faculty with senior faculty who may act as a model for critical appraisal and professional communication to those more junior, in addition to valuable feedback for those presenting.

Engaging with speakers after their presentations is a highly effective networking strategy. Given the limited time to ask questions during the session, approaching speakers at the end of the session conveys genuine interest and is a natural bridge to further conversation. Many presenters, regardless of seniority, appreciate how their work resonates with others. If providing a compliment, we recommend identifying specific details of interest to show true engagement with the content.

Networking

Conferences offer an unparalleled opportunity for networking. Making meaningful connections with attendees requires genuine interactions, with quality over quantity mattering the most.

As mentioned in Section 3, communicating with potential collaborators ahead of time may help with networking. Cold emailing someone before a conference is reasonable. Many senior faculty have tightly scheduled itineraries, so proactive emails are often necessary to secure time for discussion.

Another way to facilitate networking is to attend with someone more senior, as they can make introductions to new people. Mentees should feel empowered to communicate their networking goals explicitly, including asking to join mentor gatherings when appropriate and requesting help with introductions. If attending a conference with a senior faculty and they strike up a conversation with someone they know well, it is recommended to introduce oneself.

Importantly, reintroducing oneself to someone previously met is appropriate; the TID community is expanding, and forgetting names or faces is common and rarely personal.

If seeking a job, networking at conferences can yield new connections. Even when an institution is not actively hiring, a positive, brief interaction may prompt faculty to consider future opportunities or to connect a trainee with another institution. Most people are excited to connect trainees and young faculty with resources and people who can help. Consider inviting potential employers to poster presentations or talks that one is giving before the conference to showcase research accomplishments. Trainees may also solicit help from mentors to connect with those attending from institutions with open positions, facilitating introductions that make in‐person conversations easier to arrange.

Tips for Meeting New People

Although there is no shortage of opportunities to meet people at conferences, this can be challenging for some people. Networking with strangers may feel unnatural, and the multitude of interpersonal engagements can be draining. Attending conferences requires going outside of one's comfort zone as networking is a large part of the experience, but this can be bolstered by intentional strategies.

It can be intimidating to attend a conference without knowing other attendees. However, TID is a welcoming community, so attendees can start by reintroducing themselves to people they have met in other settings. Structured events such as mentor–mentee gatherings or networking receptions can provide a supportive environment with built‐in prompts that ease conversation. Setting a personal goal, such as speaking with one new person per event, can be an achievable way to build confidence. Make sure to pay attention to verbal and nonverbal cues when introducing yourself and talking with someone new. In the unlikely event someone is dismissive or uninterested in speaking, consider trying again with someone else.

One effective strategy is to attend a conference with someone with a network, like a mentor, or an outgoing peer [5]. Going to a conference with a mentor may make some trainees and junior faculty feel like they are shadowing, and it is easy to worry about being a tag‐along in these situations. However, most mentors view this dynamic as a core part of their role and expect to involve mentees in conversations and introductions. Because of the two‐way relationship of mentor/mentee, mentees should be open about how a mentor can best help them before the conference—including asking if they can be included in smaller group gatherings or making specific introductions. In addition, practicing these traits in specific settings such as at work or conferences can allow them to become more natural.

Imposter syndrome may further complicate networking, as many trainees feel uncertain about initiating conversations or fear appearing inexperienced. Up to 22%–75% of trainees experience imposter syndrome [6]. In addition, women may be more likely to experience imposter syndrome, both in the general population and in medicine [7, 8]. It can affect trainees’ ability to engage in new challenges and may result in more passive behavior during conferences. All panelists acknowledged experiencing imposter syndrome at multiple points in their career, consistent with evidence that even senior faculty in academic medicine can experience it. However, branching out and speaking to others about common interests can be a way to ease into conversation with other conference participants. Approaching another junior attendee, especially someone who is also alone or observing a session quietly, can be a lower‐stakes entry point. In many cases, these encounters lead to meaningful professional connections.

Balancing Conference Activities With Personal Time

Perfect attendance at a conference is not required. Conferences can be a marathon learning and networking crammed into a short time frame can be exhausting for anyone, physically and emotionally. Thus, it is important to take time for oneself while at conferences, which is important for sustained engagement. This is especially true for introverts who benefit from time to recharge following activities that require intense social engagement [5].

For those with childcare responsibilities, it may be a rare time away from home without children. Intentionally scheduling personal time—such as visiting a museum, exploring the city, dining at a new restaurant, or simply relaxing—can be restorative. However, self‐care activities to rejuvenate oneself can help anyone be more present during the academic and networking aspects of the conference.

Alternatively, if traveling with family, it is okay to take time to make shared memories. If able, extending a trip beyond the conference dates allows opportunities for more relaxed travel and exploring. This can be particularly helpful for international trips, where the cost of additional local travel is often minimal compared to the long‐haul flight itself.

After the Conference

A frequently asked question to our panelists outside of this event is how to manage content learned and connections made during conferences. We recommend at the end of the conference, perhaps during travels home, to curate high‐yield information and important contacts into a list of highlights from the conference. This is best done when the information is fresh and should include any other relevant details. Similarly, if new collaborators or other contacts are identified at a conference, a highlight list may also include action items and a timeline for these to occur.

Conclusion

Conferences offer trainees and early‐career faculty an exciting opportunity to engage in scientific discovery, professional development, and community building. Even for those beginning their career in Transplant ID, there are plenty of opportunities to make meaningful connections and impactful learning experiences are readily achievable with thoughtful preparation and a willingness to engage.
