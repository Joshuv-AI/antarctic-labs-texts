Phosphatidylserine exposure on tolerogenic nanoparticle correlate with oral tolerance
Authors: Vincent Chak, Maya Bybel, Sujay Harne, Jason G. Kay, Elizabeth Wohlfert, Sathy V Balu-Iyer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Unwanted immune responses against therapeutic proteins compromise treatment safety and therapeutic efficacy in patients. To mitigate these immune responses, we developed phosphatidylserine (PS)-containing lipid nanoparticles that mimic the surface properties of apoptotic cells, thereby converting an immunogen into a tolerogen. Previous studies have shown that mice prophylactically treated with PS nanoparticles- either double-chain PS or single-chain PS (LysoPS), administered intravenously or subcutaneously, suppressed humoral immune responses against therapeutic proteins such as Factor VIII (FVIII) and α-Glucosidase (GAA). However, only LysoPS successfully induced oral tolerance, whereas double-chain PS did not. This suggests that the structure of PS alters immune responses when given orally. To understand the structure of PS on the impacts on oral tolerance, we formulated dimyristoyl phosphatidylcholine (DMPC) nanoparticles containing PS variants that differed in the number of acyl chains, degree of unsaturation, and carbon chain length. These structural modifications affected PS surface exposure on the nanoparticles, which in turn modulated their tolerogenic potential. Using ovalbumin (OVA) as a model antigen, we evaluated immune responses in the presence or absence of different PS nanoparticles. Our data revealed that PS structure significantly influenced its surface exposure, which inversely correlated with OVA-specific IgG1 antibody titers and directly correlated with the frequency of T helper 3 cells (Th3). These findings highlight a structure–function relationship in PS-mediated oral tolerance, demonstrating that specific PS configurations can enhance tolerogenic outcomes by increasing PS surface exposure. This insight supports the rational design of PS-based oral immunotherapies to treat clinical conditions involving unwanted immune responses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32211-7.

Introduction

Immunogenicity against therapeutic proteins compromises both the safety and efficacy of life-saving therapies in patients1,2. For example, patients with Hemophilia A (HA), a genetic disorder caused by missing or defective factor VIII (FVIII), require lifelong administration of exogenous FVIII to maintain normal blood clotting activity. However, approximately 30% of these patients develop neutralizing antibodies (nAbs) against FVIII, which neutralize the therapeutic efficacy of FVIII and impair blood clotting activities3. The resulting loss of functional FVIII activity place patients at high risk of uncontrolled bleeding-even from minor injuries-despite receiving therapeutic doses. Although various strategies have been proposed to mitigate unwanted immune responses causing the formation of nAbs, these strategies were unsustainable or ineffective4. For instance, HA patients with nAbs are commonly treated with high doses of FVIII to induce immune tolerance5. However, this approach is prohibitively expensive (approximately $750,000 per patient per year) and, the financial burden significantly diminished the quality of life in these patients6. The immunogenicity issue is even more challenging in some disease conditions. Pompe disease is a lysosomal storage disorder caused by a deficiency of acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA), an enzyme that breaks down glycogen into glucose7. In the absence of this enzyme, glycogen accumulates in lysosomes, leading to a loss of muscle contraction and affecting several organs such as lungs, heart and various other body tissues8. Currently, enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) using recombinant human GAA is the first line of therapy, and clinical studies have shown that this therapy improves ventilator-free survival, cardiomyopathy, growth, and motor function in infantile-onset Pompe patients9–11. However, approximately 90% of patients with Pompe disease who receive ERT develop neutralizing antibodies, compromising the safety and efficacy of this life-saving treatment11,12. Once a sustained response is established, reversal is often very difficult. The clinical options that are available compromise the patient’s immune system leading to conditions where only palliative care is provided, with an average life span of less than 3 years. Thus, there is an urgent need for novel strategies to prevent unwanted immune responses in these patients.

Phosphatidylserine (PS) is a phospholipid present in the inner leaflet of the cell membrane. When cells undergo apoptosis, PS flips from the inner leaflet to the outer leaflet of the cell membrane, to provide “eat me” and “ignore me” signals to the antigen presenting cells (APCs)13,14. APCs that receive these signals can silently remove cell debris without further immunological consequence. Leveraging these natural properties of PS, we developed PS containing lipid nanoparticles associated with therapeutic antigens, which mimic the surface properties of apoptotic cells and deliver therapeutic antigens into the body while avoiding autoimmune reactions. Previously, our lab demonstrated that mice prophylactically treated with double-chain PS associated with FVIII- administered subcutaneously or intravenously-successfully suppressed the development of anti-FVIII nAb titers, even after FVIII rechallenge15,16. Importantly, only mice prophylactically treated with PS-FVIII suppressed anti-FVIII nAb development, whereas mice treated with a combination of dexamethasone (Dex) and FVIII developed FVIII-specific nAbs once treatment ceased. These data indicate that mice treated with PS-FVIII induced immunological tolerance and memory, whereas mice treated with Dex-FVIII did not15. Notably, in the same study, hyporesponsiveness was not observed for FVIII associated with charge-matched phosphatidylglycerol (PG-FVIII) or neutral phosphatidylcholine (PC-FVIII), indicating that this tolerogenic effect was specific to PS-FVIII.

Furthermore, our lab identified specific structural features of PS that are critical for PS-mediated immune tolerance. Specifically, we found that mono-acyl chain of PS (LysoPS), when incorporated into DMPC nanoparticles, successfully induced immune tolerance upon intravenous or subcutaneous administration, using FVIII or acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA) as model antigens17,18. This finding is comparable to our previous observation that double-chain PS also induces immune tolerance. However, when we switched the route of administration to the oral route, only LysoPS nanoparticles effectively induced antigen specific oral tolerance, and suppressed antibody development, whereas double-chain PS did not19. Furthermore, we have also previously established that 30% LysoPS:70% PC is optimal for tolerance induction as we increase LysoPS concentration from 5–30% increase tolerance and optimal for its stability20,21.These findings highlight the critical role of PS structure in modulating oral tolerance, likely through interactions with PS receptors such as Tim-4. As the role of PS compared to other negatively charged lipid as well as the required LysoPS percentage in PC bilayer is already established, the focus of this manuscript is to understand structural changes in LysoPS in terms of chain length and unsaturation for tolerance induction.

To further investigate the structural and mechanistic basis of PS mediated oral tolerance, we examined the structure–function relationship of PS. We hypothesized that the PS structure affects the biophysical properties of nanoparticles- particularly PS exposure on the outer leaflet-which subsequently influences their tolerogenic potential. To test this hypothesis, we constructed a series of DMPC nanoparticles containing PS variants differing in acyl chain number, degree of unsaturation, and carbon chain length. These PS nanoparticles were evaluated for their ability to modulate immune responses. Our results showed that PS exposure is critical for the induction of oral tolerance, as it correlated with suppression of humoral immune responses and reduced plasma cell generation. These tolerogenic effects were further supported by the induction of regulatory T cells and enhanced downstream activation of transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β).

Results

The impact of PS structure on biophysical properties

PS structure on lamellar properties

To investigate the impact of PS structure on lamellar and phase properties of DMPC bilayer, we constructed nanoparticles with various PS species. These nanoparticles were labeled with diphenylhexatriene (DPH) and analyzed using temperature-dependent fluorescence anisotropy. DPH is a fluorescent probe that partitions in the hydrophobic region of the bilayer and senses changes in acyl chain mobility22. At low temperatures, lipid nanoparticles are in the gel phase, characterized by minimal mobility and higher anisotropy values. As the temperature increases, the lipid nanoparticles undergo a phase transition from the gel phase to the liquid-crystalline state, characterized by increased mobility and lower anisotropy values23. The phase transition temperature (Tm), which marks the shift from gel to liquid crystalline phase, is defined as the temperature at which there is a 50% reduction in the DPH anisotropy value. Thus, DPH fluorescence anisotropy serves as a sensitive tool to assess lamellar phase properties, membrane fluidity, and stability. In this study, we formulated lipid nanoparticles with 30 mol% of the following PS species: 18:0 LysoPS, 18:1 LysoPS, 18:2 LysoPS, 22:1 LysoPS, or double-chain PS with 70% DMPC lipid. 30 mol% of PS was chosen for optimal tolerogenic potential and stability24. These PS nanoparticles differed in the numbers of carbon atoms in the acyl chain, degrees of unsaturation, and the number of acyl chains. We then examined changes in lamellarity using DPH anisotropy (Fig. 1A, B). Our data showed that DMPC lipid nanoparticles alone displayed a phase transition temperature around 23 °C, which is consistent with previously reported values25. When we incorporated double-chain PS into DMPC lipid nanoparticle, phase transition temperature decreased without discernible phase transition peak. Meanwhile, the addition of 18:1 LysoPS, 22:1 LysoPS or 18:2 LysoPS also lowered the phase transition temperature to approximately 16 °C, but to a lesser extent than double-chain PS. Inclusion of 18:0 LysoPS, which lacks unsaturation, led to a broadening of the transition peak with only a minimal shift in Tm. These results suggest that all PS lipids partitioned in the bilayer and were miscible with DMPC, with no evidence of lipid phase separation. Additionally, our data also showed that double-chain PS had the most significant effect on membrane fluidity with abolishment of the transition peak observed at 30% mol, while 18:0 LysoPS had the least impact. These observations align with our differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis, which also showed a reduction in phase transition temperature upon PS partition (Fig. 1C). PS headgroup exposure depends on PS structure

The impact of PS structure on nanoparticle exposure was assessed using PSvue550, a PS-binding fluorescent probe that binds to PS with high affinity and emits strong fluorescence. An increase in fluorescence intensity directly correlates with a greater PS exposure. Our data showed that DMPC control, a neutral lipid, did not exhibit changes in the fluorescence signal after adding PSvue at any lipid concentration tested (Fig. 1D). In contrast, all PS-containing lipid nanoparticles displayed strong fluorescence emission, indicating that PSvue bound to PS exposed on the nanoparticle surface. Among the PS-containing nanoparticles, 18:1 LysoPS had the highest level of PS exposure, followed by 18:0 LysoPS and 18:2 LysoPS, then 22:1 LysoPS, with double-chain PS showing the lowest PS exposure.

PS exposure correlated with tolerance potential

The impact of PS structure on oral tolerance induction was investigated in Swiss Webster mice using OVA as a model antigen and systemic antibody levels were measured. To eliminate nanoparticle size as a variable in oral tolerance, all PS nanoparticles were uniformly sized with an average diameter of 100 nm using high-pressure extrusion. An association study showed that at least 70 ± 10% of OVA protein were associated with PS nanoparticles, without protein-lipid separation. Cryo-TEM images were captured for double-chain PS and 18:1 LysoPS nanoparticles. Both PS nanoparticle formulations displayed lamellar and vesicle morphology (Supplementary Figure S1). Furthermore, we did not observe any correlation between this biophysical characteristic and oral tolerance induction (Table 1). In this study, outlined in Fig. 2A, mice were prophylactically treated with buffer or 1 μg of OVA protein in the presence or absence of PS nanoparticles for nine weeks and subsequently rechallenged with 2 µg of OVA subcutaneously starting in week six. At the end of the study, our data showed that mice prophylactically treated with 18:0 LysoPS and 18:1 LysoPS had lower OVA-specific IgG1 antibody titers compared to all other treatment groups, with 18:1 LysoPS slightly lower than 18:0 LysoPS. Among the anti-OVA antibody responses, IgG1 was the dominant isotype and was specifically assessed in this study26,27. Moreover, as the number of carbons in the acyl-chains (18:1 LysoPS vs 22:1 LysoPS) or the number of acyl-chains (18:1 LysoPS vs double chain PS) increased, OVA-specific IgG1 titers also increased (Fig. 2B). These findings demonstrate that PS structure significantly influences the tolerogenic potential of PS-containing nanoparticles, with 18:1 LysoPS showing the highest tolerogenic efficiency in suppressing antibody production. Meanwhile, OVA-specific IgG2a antibody levels were comparable across all treatment groups, suggesting that mice treated with 18:1 LysoPS did not display a Th1- or Th2-associated class switch in their immune response (Supplementary Fig. 3, 8). However, OVA-specific IgG1 antibodies remained detectable, suggesting that mice treated with 18:1 LysoPS still generated a limited immune response against OVA. However, similar immunization schedule with LysoPS vesicle containing FVIII or GAA in Hemophilia A mice or GAA -/- mice showed antigen specific non-responsiveness19. To assess the immunological impact of LysoPS-OVA treatment in mice, we measured the frequency of plasma cells in bone marrow using flow cytometry. Plasma cells residing in the bone marrow represent long-lived plasma cells, which are responsible for sustaining antigen-specific IgG antibody responses28. Therefore, measuring the frequency of bone marrow plasma cells provides an indication of how LysoPS treatment modulates B-cell differentiation. In this study, we collected cells from bone marrow at the end of the study. These cells were stained for B220 and CD138 biomarkers, a phenotype of plasma B cells, using flow cytometry. Our data showed that mice treated with 18:1 LysoPS suppressed the frequency of plasma cells in the bone marrow, with a mean frequency of 11% ± 5.96 (Mean ± SD), compared to mice treated with buffer (18.8% ± 8.35), free soluble OVA (Free OVA) (26.2% ± 10.0), double chain PS-OVA (20.7% ± 16.7), 18:2 LysoPS (17.1% ± 6.15), and 22:1 LysoPS (21.9% ± 7.83) (Fig. 2C). Additionally, the frequency of plasma cells correlated with PS exposure. Specifically, higher PS exposure corresponded to lower OVA-specific antibody titers and plasma cell frequency. In contrast, double-chain PS, which had the lowest PS exposure (Fig. 1D), correlated with the highest OVA-specific antibody titers and plasma cell frequency in PS-treated mice.

PS exposure induced Th3 cells for tolerogenic responses

To investigate the mechanism of LysoPS in mediating oral tolerance, we collected splenocytes and measured the frequency of latency-associated peptide (Lap +) expression in T cells at the end of the study. Splenocytes were analyzed for T-cell populations that were negative for Foxp3 expression but positive for LAP expression (Supplementary Fig. 5). Th3 are a subtype of Tregs that contributes to mucosal immune tolerance and can secrete high levels of immunoregulatory cytokine TGF-β. Our data showed that mice pretreated with 18:1 LysoPS generated the highest frequency of Th3 cells in both CD4 + and CD8 + T cells (Fig. 2D,E). Additionally, the frequency of Th3 cells was found to be inversely correlated with the frequency of plasma cells and OVA specific IgG1 antibody titers. These findings indicate that LysoPS induces Th3 cells to promote tolerance responses and that the magnitude of tolerance is associated with PS exposure.

LysoPS induced elevated TGF-β cytokine for oral tolerance

To investigate the role of TGF-β in 18:1 LysoPS mediated oral tolerance, we collected mesenteric lymph nodes 4 weeks after prophylactic treatment and performed RNA sequence for the Tgfb1 gene. Given that 18:1 LysoPS increased the frequency of Th3 cells, which are known to secrete high levels of the immunoregulatory cytokine TGF-β, 18:1 LysoPS might induce oral tolerance through the secretion of TGF-β. Our data showed that mice treated with 18:1 LysoPS increased TGF-β expression compared to FreeOVA or buffer treated animals (Fig. 3). These findings support our hypothesis that 18:1 LysoPS promotes TGF-β secretion as a potential mechanism of inducing tolerance responses. Moreover, increase in TGF-β levels might promote activation and differentiation of Th17 responses, a pro-inflammatory T cells that specialize to secrete IL17 to drive inflammatory immune responses. Therefore, we also measured the concentration of IL17, using multiplex assay from Sigma-Aldrich (Supplementary Figure S8) in this study. We found that mice treated with LysoPS showed lower levels of IL-17 cytokines, with a mean value of 0.1683 ± 0.1554 pg/ml (mean ± SD), compared to buffer controls (0.4443 ± 0.5108 pg/ml) or FreeOVA (0.4925 ± 0.5364 pg/ml), suggesting that LysoPS induced elevated TGF-β cytokine for immunosuppressive response and did not promote pro-inflammatory Th-17 response. Although the differences were not statistically significant, the findings are still biologically relevant because LysoPS promotes tolerogenic rather than inflammatory immune responses. The lower IL-17 levels suggest that the observed increase in Tgfb gene expression is unlikely to drive a Th17-mediated proinflammatory response. Discussion

The PS lipid nanoparticle platform harnesses the intrinsic ability of PS to convert an immunogen into a tolerogen, thereby inducing oral tolerance to multiple protein antigens19,29. Importantly, PS nanoparticles induce immune tolerance only occurred when antigen associated with PS nanoparticles, as PS nanoparticles alone did not modulate immune responses in dendritic cells30,31. Oral administration offers several advantages over subcutaneous or intravenous routes as tolerance is the default immune mechanism by oral route. In addition to being a user friendly and needle-free route of administration32, it allows PS formulation to directly engage with gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT), one of the largest lymphatic tissues in the body for tolerance induction33. The large surface area of the GALT allows PS nanoparticles to engage with antigen presenting cells, facilitating cellular signaling pathways that may enhance downstream tolerogenic responses. More importantly, GALT contains a specialized immune cell population, CD103-expressing dendritic cells, a specialized immune cell favors tolerance induction. CD103 dendritic cells can naturally convert retinol into retinoic acid, combination with TGF-β, promotes Treg generation and enhances TGF-β signaling34,35. Therefore, switching the route of administration to oral delivery may enhance the tolerogenic potential of the PS nanoparticle platform. However, in our previous study, we showed that only mice treated with single acyl-chain PS (LysoPS) induced oral tolerance, whereas mice treated with double chain PS did not19. Since we have previously shown that both double chain PS and LysoPS are stable and remain intact reaching GALT after oral administration19, the observed difference in their tolerogenic effects suggests that PS structure plays a critical role in mediating oral tolerance.

To further understand the impact of PS structure on biophysical properties, we conducted a series of biophysical analyses on PS nanoparticles formulated with various PS phospholipids. Our data showed that structural variation in PS significantly impacted the biophysical properties of the nanoparticles, particularly size, membrane fluidity and PS surface exposure. Specifically, we observed that the number of acyl chains (single vs double) had the greatest impact on biophysical properties, followed by the number of carbon atoms (18 vs 22) in the acyl chains and then the degree of unsaturation (0 vs 1). When double-chain PS was incorporated into DMPC liposome, the negatively charged PS increased membrane fluidity and PS exposure (Fig. 1). Reducing the number of acyl chains from double to single by replacing double-chain PS with 18:1 LysoPS changed the molecular shape of PS from cylindrical to conical, further increasing PS exposure. This PS lipid structure also showed the highest PS exposure among all PS species tested in this study. These changes in biophysical properties may be attributed to a kink introduced by the unsaturation in the acyl chain, which reduced the acyl chain length matching with the 14:0 DMPC chains. The mismatch between PS and 14:0 DMPC likely enhanced the miscibility of the binary lipid and increased vesicle curvature due to the conical shape of LysoPS, thereby promoting the translocation of PS from the inner to the outer leaflet. When 18:1 LysoPS was replaced with 18:0 LysoPS or 22:1 LysoPS, a reduction in unsaturation with 18:0 or an increase in chain length 22:1 resulted in acyl chain mismatch, causing packing defects and interdigitation of the bilayer, which led to a reduction in PS exposure compared to 18:1 LysoPS. Overall, these findings demonstrate that PS structure strongly influences the biophysical properties of PS nanoparticles, particularly PS surface exposure, which is sensitive for the recognition by T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 4 (Tim-4) receptors expressed on APCs for tolerance responses36.

The Tim-4 receptor is one of the PS receptors expressed on APCs and is involved in the regulation of immune tolerance36,37. It has been reported that PS binding to the Tim-4 receptor is sensitive to the PS exposure and surface charge density, thereby modulating immune response, including neutrophil clearance and the resolution of inflammation38,39. PS mediated immune tolerance via Tim-4 receptors was also demonstrated in our previous study. We showed that mice pretreated with anti-Tim-4 functional blocking antibody reversed PS mediated tolerance effects17, indicating that the mechanism of PS-induced oral tolerance requires engagement of PS with Tim-4 receptors on APCs. Tim-4 receptors is sensitive to the surface density of the PS-expressing cells in facilitating downstream tolerogenic responses40. Studies conducted by Tietjen et al. have shown that PS nanoparticles with 30% PS had sixfold stronger binding to the Tim-4 receptors than those with 10% PS nanoparticles. This suggests that PS exposure plays a critical role in facilitating selective recognition by the APCs and the induction of immunological tolerance. Therefore, we conducted an in-vivo study, to demonstrate the connection between PS exposure, constructed from various PS nanoparticles, and oral tolerance. Our data showed that PS exposure directly correlated with OVA-specific IgG antibody titers and the frequency of antibody-secreting plasma cells in the bone marrow, while inversely correlated with the frequency of Th3 cells. Together, these results demonstrate that the structure of the PS phospholipid modulates PS surface exposure, which in turn influences Tim-4 engagement and downstream tolerogenic signaling.

Th3 cells are a subset of the regulatory T cell population, characterized by the absence of Foxp3 expression and the presence of latent TGF-β on their surface41. These cells express abundant Lap protein expression on the cells surface, allowing them to rapidly secrete TGF-β cytokine in response to tolerance signals. The role of Th3 cells in the peripheral tolerance was first reported in a murine model of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE)42,43, and they were later shown to contribute to oral tolerance as well44. Moreover, studies have reported that induced Th3 cells suppress naïve CD4+ T cell proliferation and, when adoptively transferred in vivo, inhibit allergic eosinophilia and Th2 cytokine expression in the lung45. The role of Th3 cells was also observed in our previous studies and in this current work19. Mice treated with PS nanoparticles increased the frequency of Th3 cells and this frequency correlated with PS exposure. Specifically, 18:1 LysoPS treatment resulted in the highest frequency of Th3 cells and the greatest PS exposure, whereas double-chain PS nanoparticles showed the lowest levels of both. This trend was particularly evident in CD4⁺ Th3 cells, suggesting that this subset is especially sensitive to PS nanoparticle-induced tolerance. Besides, we also observed similar trends in the frequency of CD8⁺ Foxp3⁻ Lap⁺ (CD8⁺Th3-like) cells. CD8⁺Th3-like cells represent a minor subset of TGF-β producing regulatory T cells, and their functions remain less well characterized. Studies have shown that CD8⁺Foxp3⁻ regulatory T cells can suppress effector T-cell responses independent of cytotoxicity. These cells express low levels of cytolytic molecules such as Granzyme A, Granzyme B, and Perforin, and do not induce apoptosis of responder T cells or antigen-presenting cells when co-cultured with them, suggesting a non-cytolytic, TGF-β-mediated suppressive mechanism46. However, it remains unclear the specific role of CD4⁺Foxp3⁻ Tregs and CD8⁺Foxp3⁻ Tregs in PS mediated oral tolerance. In addition, Foxp3⁺ Tregs represent another regulatory T cell subset that is critical for maintaining immune homeostasis. Multiple studies have reported their role in promoting immune tolerance and suppressing pro-inflammatory immune responses47,48. However, in this study, the frequency of Foxp3⁺ Tregs in the spleen was comparable across treatment groups, suggesting that this subset is not likely a primary driver of the systemic immune tolerance mediated by LysoPS (Supplementary Figure S6).

TGF-β is central to both the induction and suppressive function of Th3 cells. To further understand the role of TGF-β in LysoPS mediated oral tolerance, we conducted an additional experiment and measured Tgfb1 RNA expression in the mesenteric lymph nodes (MLN) 4 weeks after LysoPS-OVA treatment. We found that mice treated with LysoPS increased Tgfb1 expression compared to mice treated with buffer or FreeOVA, suggesting that TGF-β is involved in PS mediated oral tolerance. TGF-β is an immunoregulatory cytokine that contributes to multiple downstream tolerogenic responses. In dendritic cells or macrophages, TGF-β engages with TGF-β receptors and promotes a tolerogenic phenotype. It impairs antigen presentation by downregulating major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules and reduces the expression of IL-12 and co-stimulatory molecules such as CD40, CD80, CD83, and CD8649. In B cells, TGF-β decreases B lymphocyte Ig secretion by inhibiting the synthesis of Ig mRNA and inhibiting the switch from the membrane form to the secreted forms of mu and gamma mRNA50. It also suppresses the production of isotypes such as IgM or IgG while promoting the generation of Bregs51. In T cells, TGF-β serves as a key immunoregulatory cytokine that can direct T-cell differentiation toward either tolerogenic or inflammatory lineages, promoting the development of Tregs or, in the presence of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 or IL-21, inducing Th17 cells52. While Tregs regulate the magnitude of immune responses, Th17 cells are pro-inflammatory lymphocytes that mediate host defense against bacteria and fungi through IL-17 secretion. However, although not statistically significant, we observed that mice treated with LysoPS showed lower levels of IL-17 cytokines, compared to buffer controls or FreeOVA (Figure S1). This suggests that LysoPS promoted TGF-β secretion, which in turn promoted Tregs generation, potentially mediated by Th3 cells, for downstream tolerogenic responses.

To explore the mechanism of LysoPS-mediated oral tolerance, we also examined the potential contribution of Th1/Th2 immune polarization in PS-mediated tolerance. Previous studies have demonstrated that low-dose antigen administration can rebalance Th1/Th2 responses and promote the induction of oral tolerance53,54. Th1 responses are typically characterized by elevated IgG2a antibodies and increased secretion of IFN-γ and IL-12p70, whereas Th2 responses are associated with IgG1 antibodies and elevated levels of IL-5, and IL-1355–57. Mice treated with free OVA displayed a Th2-skewed response but with 18:1 LysoPS treatment we did not observe a clear trend either in Th1 or Th2 immune response, suggesting PS mediated oral response is likely via Tregs generation.

The structure and biophysical correlation of PS to oral tolerance led to the development of a novel oral immunotherapy platform. Lipid nanoparticle containing 18:1 LysoPS displayed the highest PS exposure and effective oral tolerance in terms of reduced humoral response with a reduction in long lived plasma cells and higher Tregs.

The Endotoxin level was monitored using Endosafe Endochrome-K endotoxin assay kit. Preparation of nanoparticles

PS nanoparticles were prepared at a 30:70 molar ratio of PS phospholipid to 14:0 DMPC phospholipid using the thin film method as previously described 17. The film was rehydrated in a 5 mM citrate buffer at pH 4.0 or in 25 mM Tris-NaCl pH 7.4 buffer for PSvue assay and extruded six times through a polycarbonate membrane with 100 nm pore size using a high-pressure extruder. The final lipid concentration and recovery was confirmed via a standard phosphate assay 58. The OVA protein to lipid molar ratio was maintained at 1:1000. OVA was associated with PS lipid nanoparticles by incubating the nanoparticles with OVA at 37 °C for 30 min in the same buffer. The size of nanoparticle confirmed was measured using Nicomp 380 Submicron Particle Sizer. (Entegris, Santa Barbara, CA). Data was analyzed using the intensity-weighted Gaussian analysis. Nanoparticle morphology was examined by cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) in Glacios cryo-electron microscope equipped with a Falcon 4 Direct Electron Detector at the Hauptman-Woodward Medical Research Institute.

DPH-Fluorescence anisotropy

To label nanoparticles with DPH, 0.54 μM of the fluorescent probe was incubated with 1µMol/ml of various PS nanoparticles in 5 mM citrate buffer, pH 4.0, at 37 °C in the water bath for 15 min. DPH labeled PS nanoparticles, were placed in a 1 mL, Y-shape, quartz cuvette and equilibrated to desired temperature for at least 7 min. The excitation monochromator was set at 355 nm, and emission was monitored at 435 nm using a PTI-Quantamaster fluorescence spectrophotometer (Photon Technology International, Lawrenceville, NJ) connected to an external water bath (VWR Scientific, NY). Average anisotropy values were recorded at a range of temperatures from (10 °C to 40 °C) spanning the phase transition of base lipid DMPC.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

Phase transition temperatures of various PS nanoparticles were measured using a DSC 4000 calorimeter (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA). A total lipid concentration of 40 µmol/ml of PS nanoparticles was loaded onto aluminum pans and sealed for DSC analysis. A pan with equal volume of citrate buffer was added as the internal reference. Samples were set to stabilize at 5 °C for 10 min and then heated from 5 °C to 40 °C at the heating rate of 2.5 °C/min.

Association efficiency (%)

The association efficiency was determined by separating free OVA from nanoparticle associated protein using Sephadex G-200 size-exclusion chromatography. This gel filtration chromatography has a filtration range between 5 to 600 kDa and is suitable for the separation of OVA. Samples passed through the column with 5 mM citrate buffer as mobile phase and samples were collected using a fraction collector. Each faction was equivalent to 15 drops. (~ 550 μl). To determine the fractions containing lipid particles, PS-nanoparticles were labeled with 0.05% Rhodamine-PE fluorescent dye. The protein was released from the nanoparticle using 0.5% Tween 20, vortexed for 10 s, and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. Protein quantification was performed using microBCA assay. Determination of PS exposure

A fluorescence probe that binds to PS specifically was titrated with various concentrations of PS nanoparticles to evaluate the PS exposure on the surface. 1 µM of PSvue was added to increasing concentration of nanoparticles from 0 to 200 µM in Tris buffer (pH 7.4) and samples were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min to allow proper interaction of the probe with the particle. The fluorescence intensity was measured using a SpectraMax i3 Multi-Mode Microplate reader (Molecular Device, Sunnyvale, CA) at an emission wavelength of 610 nm with excitation set at 550 nm. Fluorescence intensity was normalized using the emission intensity of PSvue alone in the absence of lipids and the data was plotted with the changes in fluorescence intensity as a function of lipid concentration. Data were fitted in GraphPad Prism software using a single site-total binding model with nonlinear least squares fitting to evaluate PS surface exposure.

Immune tolerance study

Swiss Webster mice are outbred animal with great genetic diversity which provides translational relevance in immune responses observed in human population. Both male and female Swiss Webster mice (n = 8/group) were prophylactically dosed with citrate buffer, 1 μg of OVA (FreeOVA), or 1 μg of OVA with various PS nanoparticles via oral gavage for 9 weeks as described previously 19. On week 6, all mice were rechallenged with 4 weekly subcutaneous re-challenge injections of 2 μg OVA in 25 mM Tris-NaCl buffer (pH 7.4), 48 h after oral immunization. Blood samples were collected at week 5 (before the rechallenge), week 8 (3 weeks after the rechallenge), and terminal to capture development of anti-OVA IgG1 antibody titers and cytokine development. One week after the last re-challenge, spleen, and bone marrow were collected. Plasma was collected in 10% v/v acid-citrate-dextrose solution for the determination of antibody titers. Cytokines were measured in week 8 blood sample using MCYTOMAG-70 K 15 plex Multiplex Assay from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO), following the manufacture protocol.

Anti-OVA antibody ELISA

Anti-OVA IgG1, IgG2a, IgM antibody titers were determined using an in-house developed indirect ELISA method, as previously described59. Briefly, 5 μg/ml of OVA protein was coated on the NUNC Maxisorp 96-well ELISA plate and incubated at 4◦C for 18 h. The coated ELISA plate was washed 4 times with 1 × PBS + 0.05% (%v/v) Tween 20 using HydroFlex™ Plus microplate washer (Tecan, AU). After washing, the plate was blocked with 300 μl/well of 5% of BSA in PBS for 1 h at 37◦C. After blocking, the plate was washed 4 times and then incubated with samples and standard for 2 h at room temperature. This was followed by another washing step. Then the detection antibodies (anti-fc IgG1-HRP antibody, anti-fc IgG2a-HRP antibody 1:4000 or anti-fc IgM-HRP antibody 1:4000) were added to the plate and incubated for 1 h at room temperature, followed by washing steps. TMB was added and incubated in the dark for 30 min at room temperature to develop color. The reaction was stopped by adding 1N HCL and absorbance data were measured at 450 nm. Titer measurements were obtained using anti-OVA IgG1 or anti-OVA IgG2a standard, whereas titer measurements of anti-OVA IgM antibody using the Frey method60. Inter-plate variability was presented in supplementary Table S1. A non-linear sigmoidal four-parameter logistic (4PL) model, with concentration as the X-variable, was used to fit the standard curve and interpolate sample antibody titers.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were obtained from organs by mechanical disruption. Briefly, tissues were gently pressed between glass slides in media to release cells, including red blood cells and lymphocytes. The resulting cell suspensions were filtered through a 100-µm cell strainer, followed by red blood cell lysis. Then the single cell suspension was blocked with anti-mouse CD16/32 and stained with dye (Ghost Dye™ Violet 450), CD3-APC-Cy7(17A2, Cytek bioscience), CD4-FITC (RM4-5, Cytek bioscience), CD8a- PE-Cy7 (53–6.7, Cytek bioscience), CD25- PerCp-Cy5.5 (3C7, Biolegend), Lap-APC (TW7-16B4, BioLegend). Cells were fixed, permeabilizated and stained with Foxp3-Alexa Fluor 700 (FJK-16 s, eBioscience). Cell suspensions were harvested from the spleen and bone marrow and stained with viability dye (Ghost Dye™ Violet 450), B220-APC (RA3-6B2, Cytek bioscience), and CD138-BV605(281–2, BioLegend). All flow cytometry results were obtained using a BD LSR Fortessa flow cytometer (Pittsburgh, PA) and data was analyzed using FlowJo v10 (Ashland, OR). Fluorescence Minus One (FMO) method was used for gating strategy.

RNA sequencing

A separate animal study was conducted with six mice per group. Each group was treated with buffer, FreeOVA, or 18:1 LysoPS-OVA for four weeks. At the end of the study, MLN were harvested from each of the animals at the end of the study. MLN were digested with RNase and collagenase to release cells from the MLN. Cells within the group were pooled and dead cells were removed Dead Cell Removal Kit (Miltenyi bioscience) and conducted single RNA cells sequencing using Chromium Single Cell universal 3’ Gene Expression (10 × Genomic). We present data as a total RNA expression from all the immune cells population expressing cells expressing Ptprc (Cd45), for total Tgfb1 gene expression after treatment.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc, La Jolla, CA) version 10.0. One-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc analysis on the original or log-transformed values were performed to detect significant differences (P < 0.05) as indicated.
