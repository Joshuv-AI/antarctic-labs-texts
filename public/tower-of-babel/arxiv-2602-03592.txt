Abstract
Transcendental Liouvillian extensions are differential fields, in which one can model poly-logarithmic, hyperexponential, and trigonometric functions, logarithmic integrals, and their (nested) rational expressions. For such an extension ( F , ′ ) (F,\,^{\prime}) with the subfield C C of constants, we construct a complementary subspace W W for the C C -subspace of derivatives in F F , and develop an algorithm that, for every f ∈ F f\in F , computes a pair ( g , r ) ∈ F × W (g,r)\in F\times W such that f = g ′ + r f=g^{\prime}+r . Moreover, f f is a derivative in F F if and only if r = 0 r=0 . The algorithm enables us to determine elementary integrability over F F by computing parametric logarithmic parts, and leads to a reduction-based approach to constructing telescopers for functions that can be represented by elements in F F .

A classical topic of computer algebra is to express indefinite integrals in closed forms.
Historical developments and fundamental results on this topic are reviewed in
[

]
.
On the computational side, the most systematic developments are due to Risch
[

,

]

and researchers who have clarified, improved, extended and implemented Risch’s algorithm

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

.

For an elementary function, Risch’s algorithm
determines whether it has an elementary integral, and computes such an integral if there exists one.

Example 1.1
.

Let

\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{x\log(x)^{3}+1}{x\log(x)}
and
\displaystyle g(x)=\dfrac{x\log(x)^{3}+1}{(x+3)\log(x)}.

By Risch’s algorithm,

\int f=x\log(x)^{2}-2x\log(x)+2x+\log(\log(x))\quad\text{and}\quad\int g=\int\dfrac{x\log(x)^{3}+1}{(x+3)\log(x)}.

The algorithm yields an elementary integral of
f
, and reveals that
g
has no elementary integral.

The most basic routine in Risch’s algorithm is the Hermite-Ostrogradsky reduction for rational functions
[
26
,
29
]
.
We refer to
[
10
, Chapter 2]
and
[
23
, Chapter 11]
for its modern presentations.

Example 1.2
.

Let
C
be a field of characteristic zero.
For every element
f\in C(x)
, the Hermite-Ostrogradsky reduction computes
g,s\in C(x)
such that

f=g^{\prime}+s,

where
′
stands for the usual derivation
d/dx
, and

s
is a proper fraction with a squarefree denominator.
Moreover,

f\in C(x)^{\prime}
if and only if
s=0
, where
C(x)^{\prime}
denotes the set of derivatives in
C(x)
.

Let
S_{x}
be the set
\{s\in C(x)\mid\text{$s$ is proper and has a squarefree denominator}\}
.
Then
S_{x}
is a
C
-linear subspace of
C(x)
and
C(x)=C(x)^{\prime}\oplus S_{x}
. The projection from
C(x)
to
S_{x}
with respect to the direct sum
is a complete reduction for
C(x)^{\prime}
in
C(x)
according to
[
27
, Definition 5.67]
.
In this paper, complete reductions are for images of linear operators. The following definition is
equivalent to
[
27
, Definition 5.67]
, but more convenient
for the present paper.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
U
be a
C
-linear space and
\rho
be a linear operator on
U
.
Another linear operator
\phi
on
U
is called a complete reduction
for the pair
(U,\rho)
if
U=\operatorname{im}(\rho)\oplus\operatorname{im}(\phi)
and
\phi
is the projection from
U
to
\operatorname{im}(\phi)
with respect to the direct sum.

Another equivalent definition is that a linear operator
\phi
on
U
is a complete reduction for
(U,\rho)
if

\phi^{2}=\phi
and
\ker(\phi)=\operatorname{im}(\rho)
.

The next definition describes how a complete reduction decomposes an element additively.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
\phi
be a complete reduction for
(U,\rho)
and
u\in U
. We call
\phi(u)
the remainder of
u
with respect to
\phi
.
Let
v\in U
be such that
u=\rho(v)+\phi(u)
. Then
(v,\phi(u))
is called a reduction pair of
u
with respect to
\phi

or an R-pair with respect to
\phi
for brevity.

The Hermite-Ostrogradsky reduction not only induces a complete reduction for
(C(x),\,^{\prime})
, but also yields an algorithm
for computing
the corresponding R-pairs.
An algorithm based on the reduction is developed in
[
3
]
to construct telescopers for bivariate rational functions.
It avoids the costly computation of certificates, and has motivated a rapid development of complete reductions and additive decompositions
in various settings.

Example 1.5
.

Let
h
be a nonzero rational function in
C(x)
, and

U
be the space spanned by the hyperexponential function
\exp(\int h)
over
C(x)
.
For every element
f\in U
,
the Hermite reduction for hyperexponential functions in
[
4
]
is a complete reduction for
(U,\,d/dx)
.

Recall that a
derivation

{}^{\prime}:R\rightarrow R
on a commutative ring
R
is an additive map satisfying the usual Leibniz’s rule:

(ab)^{\prime}=ba^{\prime}+ab^{\prime}
for all
a,b\in R
.
The pair
(R,\,^{\prime})
is called a
differential ring
and a
differential field
if
R
is a field.
The set
\{c\in R\mid c^{\prime}=0\}
forms a subring whose elements are called
constants
.
In particular, the subring becomes a subfield if
R
is a field.

Let
(R,\,^{\prime})
be a differential ring whose subring
C
of constants is a field. Then
R
is a
C
-linear space.
Complete reductions have been established for such differential rings with instances of
R
including
the field of differential fractions
[
6
]
, the field of algebraic functions
[
15
]
,
(sub)rings of D-finite functions
[
16
,
37
,
12
]
, exponential
towers
[
22
]
and primitive towers
[
20
]
.
A complete reduction for the pair
(C(x),L)
is developed in
[
5
]
,
where
L
denotes a linear differential operator.
The reader is referred to
[
7
,
14
]
for complete reductions related to symbolic summation.

A complete reduction for
(R,\,^{\prime})
decomposes an element
f
as
g^{\prime}+r
for some
g,r\in R
such that
r
is minimal
with respect to a partial ordering defined in
[
22
, Section 1.1]
.
In particular,
f

is a derivative in
R
if and only if
r=0
.
So a complete reduction for
(R,\,^{\prime})
is an additive decomposition in the sense given by
[
13
,
21
]
.
It provides us with an alternative and possibly more informative way to express integrals.

Example 1.6
.

Let
f
and
g
be the same as in Example
1.1
. They can be regarded as elements of

{\mathbb{Q}}(x,\log(x))
, which is a differential field with derivation
{}^{\prime}=d/dx
.

The complete reduction in
[
20
]
yields

f(x)=h(x)^{\prime}+\frac{1}{x\log(x)}\quad\text{and}\quad g(x)=h(x)^{\prime}+\frac{1}{(x+3)\log(x)}-\frac{3\log(x)^{2}}{x+3},

where
h(x)=x\log(x)^{2}-2x\log(x)+2x
.

Note that
f(x)
is the sum of a derivative in
{\mathbb{Q}}(x,\log(x))
and
a remainder
1/(x\log(x))
. The remainder has an elementary integral
\log(\log(x))
.
In general, the elementary integrability of remainders in logarithmic extensions can be determined
by computing parametric logarithmic parts by
[
20
, Theorem 5.3]
.
On the other hand,
g(x)
is the sum of a derivative in
{\mathbb{Q}}(x,\log(x))
and a remainder, which is not elementarily
integrable. Unlike in Example
1.1
, the complete reduction renders us a maximal integrable part
h(x)
of
g(x)
in
{\mathbb{Q}}(x,\log(x))
.
The above two decompositions of
f
and
g
can also be obtained from
the algorithms in
[
13
,
21
]
.

Now, let us define transcendental Liouvillian extensions. Such extensions are more general than transcendental elementary extensions
(see
[
10
, Definition 5.1.4]
), and do not contain any complicated reduced elements (see
[
10
, Definition 3.5.2]
).

Definition 1.7
.

Let
C
be a field of characteristic zero. A differential field
C(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
with derivation
′

is called a transcendental Liouvillian extension of
C
if

(i)

t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
are algebraically independent over
C
,

(ii)

either
t_{i}^{\prime}
or
t_{i}^{\prime}/t_{i}\in C(t_{1},\ldots,t_{i-1})
for all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
,

(iii)

C
is the subfield of constants in
C(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
.

Our initial attempts to inductively construct a complete reduction for derivatives in a transcendental Liouvillian extension
revealed the necessity of a more general inductive framework (see Example
3.1
).
A similar phenomenon also occurs in the inductive proof of the main theorem in
[
32
]
.
In fact, the main theorem has two assertions. The first is of interest in elementary integration,
and the second serves to facilitate the proof.
In the second assertion, Risch writes down a differential equation

y^{\prime}+fy=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}g_{i},

(1)

where
f
and the
g_{i}
’s belong to a differential field
F
, and the
c_{i}
’s are constants of
F
.
The equation has been called the (parametric) Risch differential equation in the literature.

To develop complete reductions for symbolic integration, we define Risch operators using the homogeneous part of (
1
).

Definition 1.8
.

Let
(F,\,^{\prime})
be a differential field and
h\in F
. The map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}{\mathcal{R}}_{h}:&F&\rightarrow&F\\
&y&\mapsto&y^{\prime}+hy.\end{array}

is called the Risch operator associated to
h
.

The letter “
f
” is replaced with “
h
” in the above definition because
f
usually stands for an arbitrary element of a differential field
in this paper.
Risch operators are linear over the subfield of constants in
F
. In particular,
{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
is the derivation operator on
F
.

Assume that there is a complete reduction
\phi_{f}
for
(F,{\mathcal{R}}_{f})
, where
f
is given in the left-hand side of (
1
). Then
each
g_{i}
in the right-hand side of (
1
) has an R-pair
(p_{i},q_{i})
with respect to
\phi_{f}
. Therefore, (
1
) can be rewritten as

{\mathcal{R}}_{f}(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\left({\mathcal{R}}_{f}(p_{i})+q_{i}\right)
, which is equivalent to

{\mathcal{R}}_{f}\left(y-\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}p_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}q_{i}.

It follows from Definition
1.3
that there exist
y\in F
and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}\in C
such that (
1
) holds
if and only if
\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}q_{i}=0
and
{\mathcal{R}}_{f}\left(y-\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}p_{i}\right)=0
. The first constraint leads
to a linear system over the subfield
C
of constants in
F
, and the second amounts to determining the kernel of
{\mathcal{R}}_{f}
.
In other words, the complete reduction finds all solutions of (
1
) in
F

by solving a linear algebraic system over
C
and
y^{\prime}+fy=0
in
F
.

Let
F_{n}=C(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
be a transcendental Liouvillian extension of
C
with
n>0
, and set
F_{0}=C
.
In this paper, we construct a complete reduction
\phi_{n,h}
for
(F_{n},{\mathcal{R}}_{h})
, where
h\in F_{n}
, in the sense that
we fix a complement of
\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{R}}_{h})
in
F_{n}
with the corresponding projection
\phi_{n,h}
from
F_{n}
to the complement,
and develop an algorithm for computing R-pairs with respect to
\phi_{n,h}
.
Setting
h=0
yields a complete reduction
\phi_{n,0}
for
(F_{n},\,^{\prime})
. In doing so, we
generalize
the complete reductions in Examples
1.2
,
1.5
, and those in
[
22
,
20
]
in one fell swoop.

Our construction is based on the assumption
that there is a complete reduction for
(F_{n-1},{\mathcal{R}}_{z})
for all
z\in F_{n-1}
.
It proceeds in three steps:

Outline 1.9
.

1.

Reduce the problem of constructing a complete reduction for
(F_{n},{\mathcal{R}}_{h})
to that
for
(F_{n},{\mathcal{R}}_{\xi})
, where
\xi\in F_{n}
is
t_{n}
-normalized
(see Proposition
3.3
).

2.

Reduce the problem of constructing a complete reduction for
(F_{n},{\mathcal{R}}_{\xi})
to that
for

(R_{n},{\mathcal{P}}_{\xi})
, where
R_{n}
is either
F_{n-1}[t_{n}]
or
F_{n-1}[t_{n},t_{n}^{-1}]
, and
{\mathcal{P}}_{\xi}

is the
companion operator of
{\mathcal{R}}_{\xi}
(see Definition
3.4
and Proposition
3.5
).

3.

Construct a complete reduction for
(R_{n},{\mathcal{P}}_{\xi})
(see Theorem
7.1
).

The idea for the first two steps can be traced back to
[
18
, Condition b) on page 905]
and
[
10
, Section 6.1]
,
in which the problem of solving Risch differential equations in a monomial extension is transformed to that in its subring of reduced elements.
Similar transformations are also given in
[
24
,
11
]
, which can, respectively, be viewed as a differential analogue and a
generalization of the minimal decomposition for hypergeometric terms
in
[
2
,
1
]
.

The idea for step 3 is to construct an auxiliary subspace
A_{\xi}
such that
R_{n}=\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{P}}_{\xi})+A_{\xi}
,
and compute an echelon basis of
\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{P}}_{\xi})\cap A_{\xi}
. The basis enables us to construct
a complement of
\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{P}}_{\xi})
in a dual manner (see Section
2.3
).
Although this idea
has appeared in

[
4
, Section 4.1]
,
[
22
, Chaper 4]
and
[
20
, Section 3]
in one form or another,
a comprehensive generalization is required, because
F_{n}
is more involved than
the differential fields appearing therein. Step 3 is
different from the way to handle elements of
R_{n}
in Risch’s algorithm.
It is also the most technical part in this paper.

Applications of the complete reduction in Theorem
7.1
include two algorithms: one is for in-field integration,
and the other for elementary integration.
In addition, the complete reduction leads to a reduction-based approach to
constructing telescopers (up to a given order) for functions that can be interpreted as elements in a transcendental
Liouvillian extension.

The next example illustrates an application of the complete reduction to in-field integration.

Example 1.10
.

Let

f=\dfrac{x}{1+\exp(x)}\cdot\exp\left(\dfrac{x}{1+\exp(x)}\right),

which can be regarded as an element in the transcendental elementary extension
{\mathbb{C}}(x,t,y)
of
{\mathbb{C}}
, where

t=\exp(x)
and
y=\exp\left(\dfrac{x}{1+\exp(x)}\right)
.
In fact,
f=\dfrac{xy}{1+\exp(x)}
, which belongs to
{\mathbb{C}}(x,t)
.
We determine whether
f
has an integral in the extension
{\mathbb{C}}(x,t,y)
.

The complete reduction in this paper confirms that

is in-field integrable, and finds that

\int f=-\bigl(1+\exp(-x)\bigr)\cdot\exp\left(\dfrac{x}{1+\exp(x)}\right).

The
int()

Example 1.11
.

2.1
Basic terminologies

Besides the usual notation in textbooks, we let
{\mathbb{N}}
,
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
{\mathbb{N}}^{-}
be the sets of positive, nonnegative and negative integers, respectively.
Set

[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
[n]_{0}:=\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.
For an abelian group
(G,+,0)
,
G\setminus\{0\}
is denoted by
G^{\times}
.

All the fields are of
characteristic zero in the sequel.
Let
F
be a field and
p\in F[t]^{\times}
. The degree and leading coefficient of
p
are denoted
by
\deg_{t}(p)
and
\operatorname{lc}_{t}(p)
, respectively. In addition,
\deg_{t}(0):=-\infty
and
\operatorname{lc}_{t}(0):=0
.
We set
F[t]_{<d}:=\{p\in F[t]\mid\deg_{t}(p)<d\}
for all
d\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.

The ring of Laurent polynomials in
t
over
F
is denoted by
F[t,t^{-1}]
.
Let
q\in F[t,t^{-1}]
be of the form

q_{k}t^{k}+q_{k-1}t^{k-1}+\cdots+q_{l+1}t^{l+1}+q_{l}t^{l},

where
k,l\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
k\geq l
,
q_{k},q_{k-1},\ldots,q_{l+1},q_{l}\in F
, and
q_{k}q_{l}\neq 0
.
The
head degree, head coefficient, tail degree and tail coefficient
of
q
are
defined to be
k
,
q_{k}
,
l
and
q_{l}
,
denoted by
\operatorname{hdeg}_{t}(q)
,
\operatorname{hc}_{t}(q)
,
\operatorname{tdeg}_{t}(q)
and
\operatorname{tc}_{t}(q)
, respectively.
In addition,
\operatorname{hdeg}_{t}(0):=-\infty
,
\operatorname{hc}_{t}(0)=0
,
\operatorname{tdeg}_{t}(0):=\infty
, and
\operatorname{tc}_{t}(0):=0
.
Since
F[t,t^{-1}]=F[t]\oplus t^{-1}F[t^{-1}]
,
we set
q^{+}
and
q^{-}
to be the respective projections of
q
to
F[t]
and
t^{-1}F[t^{-1}]
.
For
Q\subset F[t,t^{-1}]
and
l\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, the set
Q_{>l}
denotes
the set of elements in
Q
whose tail degrees are greater than
l
.

For an element
f
of
F(t)
, its numerator and denominator are denoted by
\operatorname{num}_{t}(f)
and
\operatorname{den}_{t}(f)
, respectively.
Moreover,
\operatorname{den}_{t}(f)
is set to be monic. We say that
f
is
t
-proper
if the degree of
\operatorname{num}_{t}(f)

is less than that of
\operatorname{den}_{t}(f)
.

The subscript
t
will be omitted for brevity when the indeterminate
t
is clear from context.

Let
f\in F(t)^{\times}
with
a=\operatorname{num}(f)
and
b=\operatorname{den}(f)
. For
p\in F[t]
with
\deg(p)>0
,
the
order
of
f
at
p
, denoted by
\nu_{p}(f)
, is defined to be
-m
if
p
is of multiplicity
m>0

in
b
. Otherwise, the order is the multiplicity of
p
in
a
.
In addition,
\nu_{p}(0)
is set to be
\infty
.
The
order of
f
at infinity
is
\deg(b)-\deg(a)
and denoted by
\nu_{\infty}(f)
.

Let
(F,\,^{\prime})
be a differential field.
We denote
\{f^{\prime}\mid f\in F\}
by
F^{\prime}
, which is a linear space over the subfield of constants
in
F
.
An element of
F
is called a
logarithmic derivative

if it is equal to
g^{\prime}/g
for some
g\in F^{\times}
.
A differential field
(E,\,\delta)
is called a
differential field extension
of
(F,\,^{\prime})
if
F
is a subfield of
E
and
\delta|_{F}=\,^{\prime}
.
The derivation

when there is no confusion arising.

Let

be a differential field extension of

and

.
Then

(resp.

An element

A monomial

over

and

have the same subfield of constants. By Definition

In the rest of this section, we let

be a differential field,

be its subfield of constants,

Set

Some basic facts about generators in a transcendental Liouvillian extension are collected in the following lemma for a convenience of later references.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

be regular over
F
,
d\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
u\in F
and
p\in F[t]
.

(i)

If
d>0
and
t
is primitive over
F
, then
\left(ut^{d}\right)^{\prime}=u^{\prime}t^{d}+udt^{\prime}t^{d-1}

whose degree is equal to
d
if
u
is not a constant, and
d-1
, otherwise.

(ii)

If
t
is hyperexponential over
F
, then
\left(ut^{d}\right)^{\prime}=\left(u^{\prime}+ud(t^{\prime}/t)\right)t^{d}

whose head and tail degrees are both equal to
d
.

(iii)

If
t
is primitive over
F
, then
p
is normal if and only if
p
is squarefree.

(iv)

If
t
is hyperexponential over
F
, then
p
is normal if and only if
p
is squarefree and
t\nmid p
.

Proof.

(i) By Leibniz’s rule,
\left(ut^{d}\right)^{\prime}=u^{\prime}t^{d}+udt^{\prime}t^{d-1}
. Since
t
is regular over
F
,
we have that
t^{\prime}\in F^{\times}
, which implies that the degree of
\left(ut^{d}\right)^{\prime}
is at least
d-1
.

(ii) Similarly, Leibniz’s rule implies that
\left(ut^{d}\right)^{\prime}=\left(u^{\prime}+ud(t^{\prime}/t)\right)t^{d}
,
which is nonzero because
t
is regular. Since
t^{\prime}/t\in F
, both head and tail degrees of
\left(ut^{d}\right)^{\prime}
are equal to
d
.

(iii) and (iv) hold by
[
10
, Lemma 3.4.4, Theorems 5.1.1 and 5.1.2]
.
∎

2.2
Residues and elementary integration

The reader is referred to
[
10
, Section 4.4]
for the definition of residues.
Let us mention two well-known facts on residues: First, all residues of an element in
F[t]
are zero by the definition.
Second,
all residues of a logarithmic derivative in
F(t)
are integers by
[
10
, Corollary 4.4.2 (iii)]
.

The next two lemmas are useful to prove Theorem
8.3
, which enables us to determine elementary integrability of remainders.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
t
be regular over
F
,
p\in F[t]^{\times}
with
d=\deg(p)
and
p_{d}=\operatorname{lc}(p)
.

(i)

If
t
is primitive over
F
, then
p^{\prime}/p=s+p_{d}^{\prime}/p_{d}
for some
s\in S_{t}
.

(ii)

If
t
is hyperexponential over
F
, then
p^{\prime}/p=s+p_{d}^{\prime}/p_{d}+dt^{\prime}/t

for some
s\in S_{t}
.

(iii)

All residues of
s
appearing in (i) or (ii) are integers.

Proof.

(i) Let
q=p_{d}^{-1}p
. Then
p^{\prime}/p=p_{d}^{\prime}/p_{d}+q^{\prime}/q
by
the logarithmic derivative identity in
[
10
, Exercis 3.1]
,
So
q^{\prime}/q\in S_{t}
by Lemma
2.1
(i) and (iii).
The conclusion holds by setting
s:=q^{\prime}/q
.

(ii) There exists an integer
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and a monic polynomial
r\in F[t]
with
t\nmid r
such that
p=p_{d}t^{m}r
.
Again, the logarithmic derivative identity implies that
p^{\prime}/p=p_{d}^{\prime}/p_{d}+mt^{\prime}/t+r^{\prime}/r
.
It follows from
t^{\prime}/t\in F
, Lemma
2.1
(ii) and (iv) that
s:=r^{\prime}/r-(d-m)t^{\prime}/t
is an element of
S_{t}
.
Furthermore,
p^{\prime}/p=p_{d}^{\prime}/p_{d}+dt^{\prime}/t+s
.

(iii) The
t
-simple element
s
in (i) or (ii) is the difference of
p^{\prime}/p
and an element in
F
.
Since all residues of a logarithmic derivative are integers, so are the residues of
s
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
t
be regular over
F
and
f\in S_{t}^{\times}
. Assume that
C
is algebraically closed, and that

t
is either primitive or hyperexponential over
F
. If
all residues of
f
belong to
C
, then
f
has an elementary integral over
F
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
be the distinct roots of
\operatorname{den}(f)
in the algebraic closure of
F
.
Since all residues of
f
in
\overline{F}
are constants,
there exist
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\in C
and
p\in F[t]
such that

f=c_{1}\frac{(t-\alpha_{1})^{\prime}}{t-\alpha_{1}}+\cdots+c_{k}\frac{(t-\alpha_{k})^{\prime}}{t-\alpha_{k}}+p

by
[
19
, Lemma 3.1 (i)]
. If
t
is primitive over
F
, then
p=0
by
f\in S_{t}
and Lemma
2.1
(i).
If
t
is hyperexponential over
F
, then
p=ct^{\prime}/t
for some
c\in C
by
f\in S_{t}
and Lemma
2.1
(ii).
Thus,
f
has an elementary integral over
F
.
∎

2.3
A dual presentation for complementary subspaces

Let
U
be a
C
-linear space. Every subspace of
U
is the intersection of kernels of some linear functions on
U
.
Such an intersection is understood as a dual presentation of the subspace.

For a nonempty subset
S
of
U
,

\operatorname{span}_{C}(S)
stands for the
C
-subspace spanned by
S
. In addition, the empty set spans
\{0\}
.
Let
\Theta
be a basis of
U
. For
\theta\in\Theta
,
\theta^{*}
stands for the linear
function on
U
that maps
\theta
to
1
and other elements of
\Theta
to
0
.
The basis
\Theta
is said to be
effective
if
there are two algorithms available: one finds an element
\theta\in\Theta
such that
\theta^{*}({\bf u})\neq 0
for all
{\bf u}\in U^{\times}
,
and the other computes
\theta^{*}({\bf v})
for all
{\bf v}\in U
and
\theta\in\Theta
(see
[
20
, Section 2.2]
).

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\Theta
be given above,
V
be a subspace of
U
and
{\mathbb{I}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.
A basis
\{{\bf v}_{i}\mid i\in{\mathbb{I}}\}
of
V
is called an echelon basis of
V
with respect to
\Theta
if, for each
i\in{\mathbb{I}}
, there
exists an element
\theta_{i}\in\Theta
such that
{\bf v}_{i}\notin\ker(\theta_{i}^{*})
for all
i\in{\mathbb{I}}
and

{\bf v}_{j}\in\ker(\theta_{i}^{*})
for all
j\in{\mathbb{I}}
with
j<i
.
In this case,
we also call
\{{\bf v}_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}
an echelon basis of
V
with pivots
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}
.

Intuitively, an echelon basis
\{{\bf v}_{i}\mid i\in{\mathbb{I}}\}
of
V
with pivots
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}
is a basis of
V
,
in which
\theta_{i}
appears in the
C
-linear combination of
{\bf v}_{i}
in terms of elements in
\Theta
, but it
does not appear in the
C
-linear combination of
{\bf v}_{j}
in terms of the same elements for all
j<i
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
U
be a
C
-linear space and
\Theta
be a basis of
U
. Let
V
be a subspace of
U
and

\{{\bf v}_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}
be an echelon basis of
V
with pivots
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}
.
Then
U=V\oplus\left(\cap_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}\ker(\theta_{i}^{*})\right).

Proof.

Let
W=\cap_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}\ker(\theta_{i}^{*})
.
For every
{\bf u}\in U
,
{\bf u}
is a linear combination of elements in
\Theta
, in which a finite number of elements in
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}

appear effectively. Eliminating these pivots by the echelon basis successively, we find
{\bf v}\in V
such that

{\bf u}-{\bf v}\in W
. It follows that
U=V+W
.

Suppose that
{\bf w}
is a nonzero element of
V\cap W
. Then it is a
C
-linear combination of elements in the echelon basis.
So
{\bf w}
does not belong to
\ker(\theta_{j}^{*})
, where
j
is the maximal index such that the
j
th element in the basis appears in
the linear combination.
Accordingly,
{\bf w}\notin W
. It follows that
V\cap W=\{0\}
, a contradiction. Hence,
V\cap W=\{0\}
and
W
is a complement of
V
.
∎

Example 2.6
.

Let
U=C[x]
with a
C
-basis
\Theta=\{x^{i}\mid i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}.
Let
V
be the ideal generated by
x^{2}+1
in
C[x]
.
Then
V
has an echelon basis
\{x^{i+2}+x^{i}\mid i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
with pivots
\{x^{i+2}\mid i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}.

By Lemma
2.5
,
a complement of
V
in
U
is
\cap_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\ker(\theta_{i}^{*})
with
\theta_{i}=x^{i+2}
, which is
equal to
C[x]_{<2}
.

When developing complete reductions for a transcendental Liouvillian extension with a Risch operator,
we face situations, in which a
C
-space
U
, an effective basis
\Theta
, and a subspace
V_{1}
are given,
but no echelon basis of
V_{1}
is known. To construct a complement of
V_{1}
in
U
, we seek another subspace
V_{2}

such that
U=V_{1}+V_{2}
and that
V_{1}\cap V_{2}
has an echelon
C
-basis.
Then a complement of
V_{1}
in
U
can be obtained from the next lemma.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
U
and
\Theta
be given as in Lemma
2.5
, and
let
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
be two subspaces of
U
such that
U=V_{1}+V_{2}
.
If
V_{1}\cap V_{2}
has an echelon basis with pivots
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}
, then

V_{2}\cap\big(\mathop{\cap}\limits_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}\ker(\theta_{i}^{*})\big)

is a complement of
V_{1}
in
U
.

Proof.

Let
W=\cap_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}\ker(\theta_{i}^{*})
. Then
U=V_{1}\cap V_{2}+W
by Lemma
2.5
.
Since
V_{1}\cap V_{2}\subset V_{2}
, we have

V_{2}=V_{1}\cap V_{2}+V_{2}\cap W
by the modular law of subspaces. Then

U=V_{1}+V_{2}=V_{1}+V_{1}\cap V_{2}+V_{2}\cap W=V_{1}+V_{2}\cap W.

The conclusion follows immediately from
V_{1}\cap V_{2}\cap W=\{0\}
.
∎

Example 2.8
.

Let
U=C[x]
with a
C
-basis
\Theta=\{x^{i}\mid i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}.
Let
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
be the ideals generated by
x^{2}+1
and
x^{2}-1
in
C[x]
,
respectively. Then
U=V_{1}+V_{2}
and

V_{1}\cap V_{2}
is the ideal generated by
x^{4}-1
. The intersection has an echelon
basis
\{x^{i+4}-x^{i}\mid i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
with pivots
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
, where
\theta_{i}=x^{i+4}
.
It follows from Lemma
2.7
that a complement of
V_{1}
is

V_{2}\cap\left(\cap_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\ker\left(\theta_{i}^{*}\right)\right),
which is equal to

\operatorname{span}_{C}\{x^{2}-1,x^{3}-x\}
.

As mentioned in Section
1
, we consider linear spaces equipped with an operator
\rho
.
Reductions usually take place in or modulo a subspace of
\operatorname{im}(\rho)
.
This motivates us to define
the notion of echelon sequences so as to find pre-images under
\rho
without any additional effort.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
U
be a
C
-linear space,
\rho
be a linear operator on
U
,
\Theta
be a basis of
U
, and
V
be a subspace contained in
\operatorname{im}(\rho)
.
Assume that
\{{\bf v}_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}
is an echelon basis of
V
with pivots
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}
, where
\theta_{i}\in\Theta
.
If
{\bf v}_{i}=\rho({\bf u}_{i})
for some
{\bf u}_{i}\in U
and for every
i\in{\mathbb{I}}
, then we call

\{({\bf u}_{i},{\bf v}_{i},\theta_{i})\}_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}
an echelon sequence of
V
with respect to
(U,\rho)
and
\Theta
.

Example 2.10
.

Let
U
,
\Theta
and
V
be the same as those in Example
2.6
, and
\rho:U\rightarrow U
map
p
to
(x^{2}+1)p

for all
p\in U
. Then
V=\operatorname{im}(\rho)
and has an echelon sequence

\left\{\left(x^{i},x^{i+2}+x^{i},x^{i+2}\right)\right\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}.

The last lemma is a preparation for computing R-pairs (see Definition
1.4
).

Lemma 2.11
.

With the notation introduced in Definition
2.9
, we further assume that
\Theta
is effective. If
\{({\bf u}_{i},{\bf v}_{i},\theta_{i})\}_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}
is an echelon sequence of
V
with respect to
(U,\rho)
and
\Theta
,
then, for every
{\bf x}\in U
, one can compute
{\bf y}\in U
and
{\bf z}\in V
such that

{\bf z}=\rho({\bf y})
and
{\bf x}-{\bf z}\in\cap_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}\ker\left(\theta_{i}^{*}\right)
.

Proof.

Since
\Theta
is effective, one can express
{\bf x}
as a linear combination of elements in
\Theta
.

Let
\Theta_{{\bf x}}
be the finite set consisting of all the pivots appearing in the linear combination of
{\bf x}
in terms of elements in
\Theta
.
It suffices to set both
{\bf y}
and
{\bf z}
to be zero if
\Theta_{{\bf x}}=\emptyset
.
Otherwise, let
j
be the maximal index such that
\theta_{j}\in\Theta_{{\bf x}}
.
Set
c_{{\bf x}}:=\theta_{j}^{*}({\bf x})
and
c_{j}:=\theta_{j}^{*}({\bf v}_{j})
.
Then
c_{j}\neq 0
. Set
{\bf y}_{j}:=c_{{\bf x}}c_{j}^{-1}{\bf u}_{j}
and
{\bf z}_{j}=c_{{\bf x}}c_{j}^{-1}{\bf v}_{j}
.
Then
{\bf z}_{j}=\rho({\bf y}_{j})
, and
{\bf x}-{\bf z}_{j}\in\ker(\theta_{i}^{*})
for all

by Definition

.
The lemma follows from a straightforward induction on

.
∎

3
Normalization and companion operators

Similar to the previous section, we let
(F,\,^{\prime})
be a differential field,
C
be the subfield of constants
in
F
, and
t
be a monomial over
F
.

Our first attempt was to construct a complete reduction for
(F(t),\,^{\prime})
under the assumption that there is a complete reduction
for
(F,\,^{\prime})
. But such an assumption is insufficient as long as
t
is a hyperexponential monomial.

Example 3.1
.

Let
t
be both regular and hyperexponential. Set
h=t^{\prime}/t
.
For an element
u\in F
, we determine whether
ut\in F(t)^{\prime}
.
By an order argument,
ut\in F(t)^{\prime}
if and only if
ut\in F[t,t^{-1}]^{\prime}
.
Then
ut\in F(t)^{\prime}
if and only if

ut=(vt)^{\prime}
for some
v\in F
by Lemma
2.1
(ii).
In other words,
v
is a solution of the Risch differential equation

y^{\prime}+hy=u.

So complete reductions for
(F(t),\,^{\prime})
are linked with Risch differential equations inevitably.

We are going to construct a complete reduction
\phi_{h}
for
(F(t),{\mathcal{R}}_{h})
for all
h\in F(t)

under the assumption that a complete reduction for
(F,{\mathcal{R}}_{z})
is available for every element
z\in F
,
where
{\mathcal{R}}_{h}:F(t)\rightarrow F(t)
and
{\mathcal{R}}_{z}:F\rightarrow F
are Risch operators given in Definition
1.8
.

Example 3.2
.

Let
F=C
and
c\in C
. The Risch operator
{\mathcal{R}}_{c}:C\rightarrow C
is given by
y\mapsto cy

for all
y\in C
. Then
\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{R}}_{c})=C
if
c\neq 0
, and
\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{R}}_{c})=\{0\}
if
c=0
.
Hence,
the complete reduction for
(C,{\mathcal{R}}_{c})
is the zero map if
c\neq 0
. Otherwise, it is the identity map.

An element
h
of
F(t)
is
t
-normalized
if

\gcd\left(\operatorname{num}(h)-i\operatorname{den}(h)^{\prime},\operatorname{den}(h)\right)=1

for all
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
(see
[
11
, Section 3.2]
). The gcd-condition is equivalent to
that any nonzero residue of
h
is not an integer by
[
10
, Theorem 4.4.3]
. All elements of
F
are
t
-normalized.

This normalization simplifies our induction significantly in three aspects:

(i)

It suffices to construct complete reductions for the images of Risch operators associated to
t
-normalized elements
(see Proposition
3.3
).

(ii)

t
-Normalized elements enable us to transform the problem of constructing complete reductions in
F(t)
to that in
F\langle t\rangle

(see Proposition
3.5
).

(iii)

The Risch operator associated to a
t
-normalized element
h
is injective if
h\in F(t)\setminus F
, and
t
is regular
and is either primitive or hyperexponential over
F

(see Lemma
4.4
).

An element of
F(t)
is said to be
weakly
t
-normalized
if it does not have any positive integer residue
(see
[
10
, Section 6.1]
). Note that (i) and (ii) given above hold for weakly
t
-normalized elements, but (iii) hinges on
the assumption that
h
does not have any residue in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}
.

For every element
h\in F(t)
, there exists a pair
(\xi,\eta)\in F(t)\times F(t)^{\times}
such that

\xi
is
t
-normalized, and
h=\xi+\eta^{\prime}/\eta
. We call
(\xi,\eta)
a
normal form
of
h
. Normal forms are not unique. Among them, the canonical one is computed by
Algorithm
GKS
in
[
11
, Section 3.2]
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
h\in F(t)
with a normal form
(\xi,\eta)
.
Then the following assertions hold.

(i)

{\mathcal{R}}_{h}=\eta^{-1}\circ{\mathcal{R}}_{\xi}\circ\eta
, where
\eta
is understood as the map with
f\mapsto\eta f
for all
f\in F(t)
.

(ii)

Assume further that
\phi_{\xi}
is a complete reduction for
(F(t),{\mathcal{R}}_{\xi})
.
Then
\phi_{h}:=\eta^{-1}\circ\phi_{\xi}\circ\eta
is a complete reduction for
(F(t),{\mathcal{R}}_{h})
.

(iii)

For all
f\in F(t)
,

\left(\eta^{-1}g,\,\eta^{-1}r\right)
is an R-pair of
f
with respect to

\phi_{h}
if
\left(g,\,r\right)
is an R-pair of
\eta f
with respect to
\phi_{\xi}
.

Proof.

(i) For all
f\in F(t)
,
\eta^{-1}\circ{\mathcal{R}}_{\xi}\circ\eta(f)=\eta^{-1}\left((\eta f)^{\prime}+\xi(\eta f)\right)
.
Then
\eta^{-1}\circ{\mathcal{R}}_{\xi}\circ\eta(f)={\mathcal{R}}_{h}(f)
by
h=\xi+\eta^{\prime}/\eta
and a straightforward calculation.

(ii) Since
\phi_{\xi}
is an idempotent, so is
\phi_{h}
. Hence,
\operatorname{im}(\phi_{h})
is a complement of its kernel in
F(t)
.
It suffices to verify that
\ker(\phi_{h})=\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{R}}_{h})
. By (i),

\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{R}}_{h})=\eta^{-1}\cdot\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{R}}_{\xi})
.
By
\phi_{h}=\eta^{-1}\circ\phi_{\xi}\circ\eta
, we have
\ker(\phi_{h})=\eta^{-1}\cdot\ker(\phi_{\xi}).

Thus,
\ker(\phi_{h})=\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{R}}_{h})

by
\ker(\phi_{\xi})=\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{R}}_{\xi})
.

(iii) Let
(g,r)
be an R-pair of
\eta f
with respect to
\phi_{\xi}
.
It follows from
\eta f={\mathcal{R}}_{\xi}(g)+r
and

that

f={\mathcal{R}}_{h}(\eta^{-1}g)+\eta^{-1}r,

which, together with
\eta^{-1}r\in\operatorname{im}(\phi_{h})
, implies that
\left(\eta^{-1}g,\,\eta^{-1}r\right)
is an R-pair of
f

with respect to
\phi_{h}
.
∎

In the rest of this section, we let
h\in F(t)
be
t
-normalized,
a=\operatorname{num}(h)
and
b=\operatorname{den}(h)
. Set

S_{t,h}:=\{s\in S_{t}\mid\gcd(\operatorname{den}(s),b)=1\},

(2)

which is also a
C
-subspace.
For all

, Algorithm
GKSR
in
[

, Section 3.4]
computes
(g,r,s)\in F(t)\times F\langle t\rangle\times S_{t,h}

such that

f={\mathcal{R}}_{h}(g)+\frac{r}{b}+s.

(3)

Moreover,

in the above equality is unique. Therefore,

F(t)=\left(\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{R}}_{h})+b^{-1}\cdot F\langle t\rangle\right)\oplus S_{t,h}.

(4)

This direct sum generalizes

induced by Algorithm

in
[

, Section 5.6]
.
It remains to find a

-subspace

Such a subspace is found via a polynomial reduction map in
[

, Section 4.1]
when

.
This motivates us to define the notion of companion operators.

Definition 3.4
.

The map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}{\mathcal{P}}_{h}:&F\langle t\rangle&\rightarrow&F\langle t\rangle\\
&r&\mapsto&br^{\prime}+ar.\end{array}

is called the companion operator of
{\mathcal{R}}_{h}
.

The following proposition reduces the problem of constructing a complete reduction for
(F(t),{\mathcal{R}}_{h})

to that for
(F\langle t\rangle,{\mathcal{P}}_{h})
.

Proposition 3.5
.

If
W
is a complement of
\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{P}}_{h})
in
F\langle t\rangle
, then

F(t)=\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{R}}_{h})\oplus\left(b^{-1}\cdot W\oplus S_{t,h}\right).

Proof.

By (
4
),

F(t)=\left(\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{R}}_{h})+b^{-1}\cdot\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{P}}_{h})+b^{-1}\cdot W\right)\oplus S_{t,h}.

Then

F(t)=\left(\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{R}}_{h})+b^{-1}\cdot W\right)\oplus S_{t,h}

by
b^{-1}\cdot\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{P}}_{h})\subset\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{R}}_{h})
.
It remains to show that
\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{R}}_{h})\cap\left(b^{-1}\cdot W\right)=\{0\}
.
Assume that
v\in\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{R}}_{h})\cap\left(b^{-1}\cdot W\right)
. Then
v=w/b
for some
w\in W
, and
there exists
g\in F(t)
such that
bg^{\prime}+ag=w
.
Since
h
is
t
-normalized, we have
g\in F\langle t\rangle
by either
[
10
, Theorem 6.1.2]

or
[
11
, Theorem 3.12 (iii)]
. Hence,
w\in\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{P}}_{h})
.
By
\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{P}}_{h})\cap W=\{0\}
,
w
is equal to
0
, and so is
v
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

With the notation and direct sum given in the above proposition, we see that the projection

\Phi_{h,W}
from
F(t)
to
(b^{-1}\cdot W\oplus S_{t,h})
is a complete reduction for
(F(t),{\mathcal{R}}_{h})
.
For every element

, an R-pair of
f
with respect to
\Phi_{h,W}
can be computed as follows.

Let
(g,r,s)\in F(t)\times F\langle t\rangle\times S_{t,h}
satisfy (
3
), and
\pi_{h,W}
be the projection from
F\langle t\rangle
to
W

with respect to
F\langle t\rangle=\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{P}}_{h})\oplus W
. Then
\pi_{h,W}
is a complete reduction for
(F\langle t\rangle,{\mathcal{P}}_{h})
.

Let
(u,v)
be an R-pair of
r
with respect to
\pi_{h,W}
. It follows from (

),

r={\mathcal{P}}_{h}(u)+v
and
{\mathcal{R}}_{h}(u)=b^{-1}{\mathcal{P}}_{h}(u)
that

f={\mathcal{R}}_{h}(g)+b^{-1}({\mathcal{P}}_{h}(u)+v)+s={\mathcal{R}}_{h}(g+u)+b^{-1}v+s.

Since

belongs to
(b^{-1}\cdot W\oplus S_{t,h})
, an R-pair of

with respect to

is

.

Remark 3.7
.

If

, then the direct sum in Proposition

which leads to a complete reduction for derivatives in

.

4
Induction hypothesis

By Proposition

and Remark

,
our task boils down to constructing a complete reduction for

It is realistic due to equation (5.1) in

Convention 4.1
.

In the next two sections, the following are assumed.

(i)

Let

.
For

(ii)

Let

be a

-normalized element of

.

(iii)

Set

,

,

and

if it is nonzero.

(iv)

Let

be an effective basis of

over

.

Unless mentioned otherwise, all linear spaces in the rest of this paper are regarded as linear spaces over

,
and a basis means a

-basis.
Note that

may stand for the Risch operator associated to

on either

or

when

belongs to

.
We do not distinguish them, because the former is the restriction of the latter to

.

Hypothesis 4.2
.

In the next two sections, we assume that there is a map that assigns to each element

of
F
a
complete reduction
\phi_{z}
for
(F,{\mathcal{R}}_{z})
.

By the hypothesis,
\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{R}}_{z})\cap\operatorname{im}(\phi_{z})=\{0\}
for all

, and every element

has an

(g,r)
with respect to
\phi_{z}
, that is,

and

(see Definition

).

The base case of our induction is given by the following example.

Example 4.3
.

Let
F=C
.
By Example
3.2
, the map in Hypothesis
4.2
assigns to zero the identity operator
and to every element of
C^{\times}
the zero operator

We show that one can construct a complete reduction
for
(F\langle t\rangle,{\mathcal{P}}_{h})
in three steps:

1.

Construct an auxiliary subspace

such that

.

Compute an echelon sequence of

.

Determine a complement

of

in

.

Auxiliary subspaces come from our attempt to simplify elements of

A basis of

.
One is to recognize logarithmic derivatives in

(see

, §5.12]
). The other is

Although such a basis may not be finite, its regular shape described in Proposition

As the final preparation for the next two sections, we present a lemma

Lemma 4.4
.

If

, then both
{\mathcal{R}}_{h}
and

are

Proof.

Suppose that

. Then

by Definition

.
The logarithmic derivative identity implies that

is a

-linear combination of logarithmic derivatives of polynomials in

.
Since

It follows that

Thus,

is injective, because it is

restricted to

.
∎

5
The primitive case

In this section,

is assumed to be primitive. Then

.
We define a

\begin{array}[]{cccc}{\mathcal{L}}:&F&\rightarrow&F\\
&z&\mapsto&b_{m}z^{\prime}+a_{m}z\end{array}

(5)

to describe the coefficient of the possibly highest power in

\operatorname{im}({\mathcal{P}}_{h})
. More precisely,
let

with

(i) and Definition

It follows that

{\mathcal{P}}_{h}(f)\equiv{\mathcal{L}}(f_{d})t^{m+d}\mod F[t]_{<m+d}.

(6)

Remark 5.1
.

By Convention

and (

), the following two assertions hold.

(i)

If

, then

is injective.

(ii)

If

. By Definition

,

.

The next lemma is will be frequently used in constructing echelon sequences.

Lemma 5.2
.

If
u\in\ker({\mathcal{L}})^{\times}
, then
\phi_{a_{m}}(ut^{\prime})\neq 0
.

Proof.

Since
\ker({\mathcal{L}})\neq\{0\}
, we have that

by Remark

(i), and

by Remark

(ii).
Suppose that

. Then

.
Hence, there exists

such that

On the other hand,

by

.
The two equalities involving

imply that

.
∎

5.1
Auxiliary subspaces

,

and

Case 1.
If

(7)

If

Proof.

Let

.

Proposition 5.9
.

I_{h}=\{0\}
if and only if

. In particular,
I_{h}=\{0\}
if

Proof.

Assume that

Let

for some

.
By Lemma

,

and so is

. Thus,
I_{h}=\{0\}
.

Conversely, assume that
I_{h}=\{0\}
. Let

. Then
{\mathcal{P}}_{h}(u)\in F[t]_{<m}
by (

),
and, hence,

by Definition

.
Consequently,
{\mathcal{P}}_{h}(u)=0
by

. It remains to show

.

If

, then

by Lemma

. Otherwise,

. Then

,

and

. It follows from

and

. Since

, it has an R-pair

. Then

and

.

,

, implies that

.

by

. Then

.

Definition 5.10

8

Similar to the previous section, we let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
F_{n}=C(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
be a transcendental Liouvillian extension.
In addition,
C
is denoted by
F_{0}
during an induction or a recursion.
For all
i\in[n]_{0}
, we let
\psi_{i}
be the complete reduction
\phi_{i,0}
for
(F_{i},\,^{\prime})
given in the proof of Theorem
7.1
.

To determine elementary integrability, we need to distinguish primitive generators from hyperexponential ones.
Set

{\mathbb{P}}:=\{i\in[n]\mid t_{i}^{\prime}\in F_{i-1}\}
and
{\mathbb{H}}:=[n]\setminus{\mathbb{P}}.

Moreover,

P:=\operatorname{span}_{C}\{\psi_{i-1}(t_{i}^{\prime})\mid i\in{\mathbb{P}}\}\quad\text{and}\quad H:=\operatorname{span}_{C}\{\psi_{j-1}(t_{j}^{\prime}/t_{j})\mid j\in{\mathbb{H}}\}.

The following two technical lemmas present some useful properties of remainders.

Lemma 8.1
.

For
i\in[n]_{0}
and
f\in F_{i}
,
\psi_{n}(f)-\psi_{i}(f)\in P.

Proof.

Let
k\geq i
. By Example
5.11
and Corollary
5.22
, we have that

\psi_{k+1}(f)-\psi_{k}(f)\in P
if
k+1\in{\mathbb{P}}
.
By Corollary
6.17
,
\psi_{k+1}(f)-\psi_{k}(f)=0
if
k+1\in{\mathbb{H}}
.
It follows from

\psi_{n}(f)-\psi_{i}(f)=\sum_{k=i}^{n-1}\left(\psi_{k+1}(f)-\psi_{k}(f)\right)

that
\psi_{n}(f)-\psi_{i}(f)\in P.

∎

Let
S_{i}
be the set of
t_{i}
-simple elements in
F_{i-1}(t_{i})
for all
i\in[n]
, that is,
S_{i}:=S_{t_{i}}
. Set
S:=S_{1}+\cdots+S_{n}
. The sum is direct. An element of
F_{n}
is said to be

if it belongs to
S
.

Lemma 8.2
.

For every element
s\in S
,
s-\psi_{n}(s)\in P
.

Proof.

Let
s=\sum_{i\in[n]}s_{i}
, where
s_{i}\in S_{i}
. By Proposition
3.5
and Remark
3.6
,
\psi_{i}(s_{i})=s_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
. Then

s-\psi_{n}(s)=\sum_{i\in[n]}\left(\psi_{i}(s_{i})-\psi_{n}(s_{i})\right)

by
\psi_{n}(s)=\sum_{i\in[n]}\psi_{n}(s_{i})
.
The lemma follows from Lemma
8.1
.
∎

Next, we define residues of elements in
S
without referring to a particular generator of
F_{n}
.
For a simple element
s
, there exists a unique element

for each

such that

Regarding

as an element of

, we say that a residue of

is a

.

We are ready to generalize
[

, Theorem 5.3]
from primitive towers to

.

Theorem 8.3
.

Let

be algebraically closed. Then
an element

of

has an elementary integral over

if and only if

(i)

there exists a simple element
s
such that

\psi_{n}(f)\equiv s\mod H+P,
and

(ii)

all residues of

belong to

.

Proof.

Assume that both (i) and (ii) hold. By (ii) and Lemma
2.3
,

s
has an elementary integral over

. By (i), it suffices to show that every element of

has an elementary integral over
F_{n}
.
If

, then
t_{i}^{\prime}/t_{i}=u_{i}^{\prime}+\psi_{i-1}(t_{i}^{\prime}/t_{i})
for some

, which implies that

\psi_{i-1}(t_{i}^{\prime}/t_{i})
is the derivative of
\log(t_{i})-u_{i}
.
If

.

.

has an elementary integral over

, and so does

.

Conversely, assume that
f
has an elementary integral over
F_{n}
.
Then there exists a
C
-linear combination

such that

.
Since

, we have

. It suffices to show that

satisfies (i) and (ii).

By Lemma

and the logarithmic derivative identity, there exists

such that

10
Concluding remarks

In this paper, a complete reduction is constructed for
(F,{\mathcal{R}}_{h})
, where
F
is a transcendental Liouvillian extension and
{\mathcal{R}}_{h}
is the
Risch operator associated to an element
h
of
F
. The complete reduction yields an algorithm for in-field integration directly, and
leads to a new algorithm for computing elementary integrals over
F
.

There are at least two challenging problems concerning complete reductions for symbolic integration. One is to develop a reduction-based
algorithm for computing telescopers for elements in
F
without a given priori order bound. This would require a criterion
on the existence of telescopers for elements in
F
. The other is how to adapt remainders of the complete reduction for algebraic functions
in
[
15
]
so as to compute elementary integrals over algebraic-function fields more efficiently.
