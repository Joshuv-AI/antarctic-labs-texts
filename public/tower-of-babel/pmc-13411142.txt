Respondent fatigue in clinical practice: exploring the challenges of increasing PROMS and PREMS utilization in a tertiary hospital
Authors: Emmanuel Gimenez, Ana Martí, Alexandra Tanja Stamm, Rosa Ramos, Yolima Cossio-Gil, Carolina Watson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe growing adoption of patient-reported measures (PRMs) in routine clinical practice has raised concerns about ‘respondent fatigue’, which leads patients to not complete questionnaires. Respondent fatigue poses risks not only for patient follow-up but also for data quality and overall experience. The aim is to evaluate the frequency of patients receiving PRMs potentially leading to respondent fatigue, considering both patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) in a tertiary Spanish hospital. Based on all questionnaires sent to patients in 2022, we quantified risk-inducing situations that could influence respondent fatigue in routine clinical practice.MethodsThe analysis included all adult patients attending the hospital in 2022. Information systems data were used to estimate the frequency of patient visits across various healthcare settings. The analysis assessed the frequency of annual multiple encounters, involving a selected set of 8 clinical conditions and 2 programs, mirroring real-world uses of PREMs and PROMs in both inpatient and outpatient care. By integrating both datasets, we evaluated the probability of co-occurrence and potential overlap between clinical conditions and/or healthcare settings requiring the administration of patient reported measures.ResultsThis study revealed a significantly high frequency of patient contacts for PRM collection, totalling 322,039 interactions in 2022, with an average of 2.81 contacts per year per PRMs collection. Among nearly 50,000 patients with four or more healthcare interactions, the average number of PRM collection contacts reached 8,03. For selected programs involving PROM collection, 25,016 patients enrolled in a single program had an average of 2.49 contacts per year, while 4,068 and 519 patients enrolled in two or more than three programs experienced averages of 4.46 and 6.52 contacts, respectively. These results highlight the burden of frequent PRM collection and the associated risk of respondent fatigue. Background

For many years, patient-reported measures (PRMs) have been used primarily in clinical research studies. However, recent evidence suggests that PRMs can also be valuable in routine clinical practice and support the implementation of value-based healthcare [1]. Recently, a taxonomy was proposed [2] that identifies five types of uses for PRMs in clinical practice: need assessment, shared decision-making, symptom management, outcome assessment, and quality improvement. Despite several limitations, such as subjectivity bias, PRMs can complement clinical measures and lead to improved patient outcomes [3].

In the literature, various barriers related to the use of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) have been identified. They can include barriers related to clinician perception, such as lack of awareness of PROMs, preference for physiological measures, and difficulty in translating data interpretation into clinical actions [4].

Guiding protocols and systematic recommendations on how to implement PROMs per disease in hospitals have been published in the literature [5]. Various strategies have been proposed to improve the response rate of PROMs, such as sending pre-notifications, email invitations, or reminders, which have been linked with increased answer rates higher than 10% (from approximately 27% to nearly 40% depending on the scenario) [6]. The implementation of PROMs can be centralized by acute or chronic clinical conditions, by procedures, or by the type of population or be left to the discretion of professionals to send questionnaires. In the context of gathering insights from patients about their experiences, also known as patient-reported experience measures (PREMs), strategies may vary depending on the goals and objectives of the healthcare organization. If the aim is to capture the patient experience across different stages of care, strategies may focus on different care settings. Conversely, if the organization’s objective is to gather insights from patients with specific health conditions, PREM strategies may be implemented with a condition-specific focus [7].

Several initiatives have focused on identifying the most relevant outcomes and measures for patients with different health conditions, including the selection of traditional Clinical Outcomes Assessment (COAs) and emphasizing PROMs and PREMs tailored to specific conditions [8–10] (ii). The primary goal of these initiatives is to ensure a patient-centered approach to outcome measurement, highlighting the use of patient-reported outcomes. Although some adult patients utilizing patient reported questionnaires may be monitored for a single condition, a significant proportion of the adult population who could benefit from these tools may be affected by multimorbidity [11]. The prevalence of multimorbidity, defined as the presence of two or more concurrent diseases, varies considerably across different studies and age groups. In a study conducted in 2021 [12] among European community-dwelling adults, the prevalence of multimorbidity was 28.2% among men and 34.5% among women. Additionally, a systematic review in 2011 [13] already showed that the prevalence of multimorbidity ranged from approximately 20% to 30% in the overall population but significantly increased among older individuals, ranging from 55% to 98%. Similarly, in 2017, a substantial prevalence of multimorbidity among the older population was reported [14], with a considerable proportion of individuals having multiple diseases across various categories: 88.6% had diseases in two or more categories, 73.2% in three or more, and 55.8% in four or more. This high prevalence of multimorbidity presents challenges in terms of the number of times patients are requested to complete PRMs, particularly for patients with multiple chronic conditions or a combination of chronic and acute conditions. Consequently, there is a potential risk of overlap and respondent fatigue due to the repeated administration of PRMs at multiple timepoints, which are necessary for comprehensive follow-up and monitoring of patient, highlighting the need for strategies that address the complexities of managing multiple conditions while leveraging PROMs and PREMs effectively.

Thus, the increasingly widespread use of PRMs may lead to the emergence of respondent fatigue, a phenomenon to be taken into consideration. Respondent fatigue is understood as the phenomenon by which participants become tired or disinterested during the completion of a survey [15]. Careless responses can occur when many questionnaires or similar questions are delivered at close periods of time or, simply, if questionnaires are too long [16]. Increasing questionnaire fatigue leads to less precise answers, so-called straight-line answers [17], or even premature drop out, while decreasing the quality of the data collected and leading to obvious biases in outcomes [18]. Therefore, questionnaire fatigue is a critical element to consider when designing and implementing the collection of outcomes at the individual patient and population levels; this is why tertiary hospitals wonder how to develop PROM and PREM strategies that can balance relevant data collection and questionnaire fatigue in daily practice.

Importantly, integrating a respondent fatigue mitigation approach within management strategies at clinical centers is of prime importance. Among others, three factors related to respondent fatigue should be considered [19].

These factors extend beyond the realm of clinical trials and are becoming increasingly relevant in real-world data collected in the daily practice of clinical care.

In this scientific article, our aim was to evaluate the frequency of potential factors that may contribute to respondent fatigue linked with the utilization of PRMs in routine daily clinical practice at a tertiary healthcare institution with a significant catchment area (> 450,000).

This assessment is based on the number of questionnaires sent in 2022, inspiring the research of critical factors that might influence respondent fatigue. Specifically, we focus on quantifying the frequency of situations that could pose a risk and potentially lead to respondent fatigue, considering scenarios such as (1) multiple patient visits across diverse settings incorporating ad hoc PREMs, (2) the management of multiple health conditions requiring RPM administration, and (3) the simultaneous occurrence of both circumstances.

The results of this research provide insights into the frequency of these events and can inform clinical practice in managing patients with multiple conditions requiring PROMs/PREMs to prevent or minimize respondent fatigue and patient burden.

Methods

PRM implementation strategy at the center

The strategy for the collection of PROMs and PREMs at the hospital has been designed to ensure comprehensive and unified data collection as part of routine clinical practice. This approach was implemented in a tertiary hospital in Spain. PROM and PREM data are routinely collected and integrated into patient care and follow-up processes, rather than being collected specifically for research purposes. Patient participation is voluntary, and the questionnaires are an integral part of routine care, with no additional incentives provided for completing them.

Historically, PREMs were obtained through separate satisfaction audit surveys, but since June 2021, there has been a shift towards consolidating the collection of PREMs through a single digital platform that sends a message to the patient’s phone after hospitalization, allowing patients to rate their experience in a pseudonymized manner. While the PREM data can be linked to specific care areas of the hospital, they cannot be traced back to individual patients, ensuring confidentiality. The platform incorporates 8 PREMs, each tailored to specific care settings within the hospital campus, including face-to-face consultations, inpatient admissions, emergency visits without admission, and external radiology tests. PREM questionnaires include less than 16 items and are designed to address the specific context and needs of the hospital, without relying on internationally validated instruments.

Similarly, PROMs are collected through a multichannel platform integrated with the hospital’s electronic health record (EHR). This platform enables PROM collection by specific clinical condition, ensuring a systematic and standardized approach to data collection. For this study, 10 real-world clinical conditions and transversal hospital projects (hereafter referred to as programs) were selected, which already include the consolidated collection of PROM data. These programs include acute coronary syndrome, cataracts, diabetes, heart failure, inflammatory bowel disease, localized prostate cancer, malignant breast cancer, stroke, enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS), and frequent emergency department users. The specific PROMs used for each program, the number of PROMs collected, as well as the timepoints collection for their administration throughout the healthcare process, are detailed in Supplementary Index Table 1. PROM questionnaires typically range from 5 to 20 items and are internationally validated instruments, ensuring reliability and comparability across different settings.

Design and data collection

In this study, we explored the potential factors that influence respondent fatigue associated with the implementation of PRMs in daily activities at a tertiary Spanish hospital. With over 70,000 inpatient discharges and nearly 1 million outpatient visits per year, the hospital has a catchment area of almost 450,000 inhabitants. The hospital offers over 60 medical services for both acute and chronic patients and employs staff of over 9,000 professionals.

The study sample for this retrospective analysis comprised all adult patients who attended our hospital throughout the year 2022. This included over 322,039 patients who utilized healthcare resources at the hospital during this period. Data were collected across various healthcare settings, such as outpatient clinics, emergency departments, hospitalizations, and day hospitals, providing a comprehensive overview of patient interactions.

Data analysis

A retrospective analysis of patient records from 2022 was conducted to estimate the frequency with which patients visited different healthcare settings that involved the delivery of PREMs, such as outpatient clinics, emergency departments, hospitalizations, and day hospitals, over the course of a year. This analysis generated a comprehensive table that displays the number of times each patient attended each healthcare setting. Subsequently, a hypothetical strategy to mitigate fatigue was implemented, limiting the delivery of PREMs to once per year per healthcare facility (specifically for urgencies, hospitalization, outpatient consultations, etc.). This approach allowed us to examine the potential impact of the mitigation strategy on the resulting table of binary values, indicating patient attendance at particular healthcare settings.

Afterwards, the frequency of multiple encounters per year for patients with various clinical conditions or programs that require the use of PROMS in both inpatient and outpatient settings was assessed. This was done through a retrospective analysis of patient records from 2022, specifically focusing on the 10 selected programs. To determine how often data are collected and the number of questionnaires for PRMs received by each patient within a year, we detailed the PRM utilization of PRMs in the selected programs.

Finally, we integrated data from the previous two analysis steps to evaluate the probability of co-occurrence and potential overlap between different conditions and/or healthcare settings that require the administration of PROMs and PREMs.

Using all this information, patients were categorized based on the frequency of their PRM collection contacts, considering the number of data collection timepoints. This approach allowed us to present the results as percentages, visually illustrating the trajectory of the number of contacts for PROM collection by patient within a year, with a focus on specific patient archetype(s).

Statistical software (R) was used to conduct this analysis. Finally, we used visualization software [24] to create a graphical representation of the pathway of the number of interactions (visits and PROM collection) for an archetypal patient. Overall, this methodology allows us to gain insights into patient‒PRM interactions in a real-world scenario, enabling us to measure the frequency of PROM collection contacts that patients would typically receive without measures to prevent respondent fatigue.

Discussion

Our study delved into the implementation of Patient-Reported Measures (PRMs) within a tertiary hospital, focusing on potential factors that may contribute to respondent fatigue. The investigation explored the hospital’s value-driven initiatives, involving the measurement of PROMs based on clinical conditions or programs and PREMs across diverse healthcare settings. Our findings indicate several factors that may increase the likelihood of respondent fatigue in this context: (1) extensive patient contact, (2) the presence of multiple pathologies, and (3) the comprehensive PRM implementation that includes both PROMs and PREMs.

Result 1: high numbers of contacts across various healthcare settings

Our initial results underscore the prevalence of a high number of patient contacts across various healthcare settings. This finding illuminates the challenges posed by the sheer volume of interactions, signalling a potential contributor to respondent fatigue within the hospital’s dynamic environment.

Our results revealed a high frequency of 322,039 patient contacts in different hospital settings, with an average of 2.56 contacts per year; however, even more importantly, nearly 50,000 patients would have received almost three times this number of contacts in 2022. This high frequency of patient contact in hospitals’ health-resource settings aligns with previous research on this topic. For example, data on outpatient contacts per year from the WHO [25] demonstrated a similar pattern, indicating the potential burden placed on respondents. This substantial frequency of interactions raises concerns about respondent fatigue, as the repetitive nature of data collection may lead to decreased response quality and engagement. These findings are consistent with the concept of “survey burden” discussed in recent studies [26, 27], reflecting that excessive survey demands can lead to respondent fatigue and decreased data accuracy.

Mitigation measures are needed to manage the rapid growth in PRM use, aligning them with implementation mapping. While the present study focuses on PROM and PREM use in tertiary hospital settings, it is important to recognize that primary care also contributes significantly to the growing use of PRMs [28], as well as other healthcare sectors and research contexts, potentially increasing fatigue. Given these multiple sources of data collection, mitigation strategies become even more crucial to reduce fatigue.

To address the risk of respondent fatigue, healthcare organizations should proactively implement mitigation strategies. For example, limiting the number of administrations of PREMs to once every 3 months or one year for each contact setting may help alleviate the burden on patients. As stated, by adopting the second option of this approach, the average number of PREMs per patient could be reduced from 2.56 to 1.58 in our centre. This approach is aligned with those suggested by previous studies and previously reviewed [29], who presented survey strategies to increase participant rates.

However, coordinating a centralized system for collecting PROMs and PREMs across different departments is indeed challenging, especially considering the possibility of incompatible systems. As addressed further below, hospitals may need to adopt an integrated IT platform capable of consolidating data from various departments. Recent evidence suggests that utilizing interoperable systems can allow for streamlined data collection [30].

Result 2: multiple pathologies and follow-up with proms

Result 2 highlights the significant impact of managing multiple pathologies and the associated follow-up with PROMs. As patients navigate through various conditions, the intensified frequency of data collection emerges as a crucial factor, accentuating the complexity and burden of the patient journey.

Our analysis revealed that, following our value-based healthcare strategy in a selection of 8 diseases and 2 programs, 4,527 out of 29,603 patients who would receive PROMs, will receive it more than once. This finding emphasizes the starting potential burden of administering PROMs in an increasing multimorbid population. If we were to apply PROMs to all health conditions, as proposed by initiatives such as ICHOM with PROMs for 40 pathologies, the impact of questionnaire burden would be even more significant.

As a result of the conducted research and the increasing risk of respondent fatigue due to the growing number of health conditions requiring PRMs, we propose four main solutions to address these challenges, based on both our practical experience and existing evidence:

implementing a unified IT system that streamlines data collection. Evidence from studies on healthcare data systems suggests that integrated IT systems can significantly contribute to more efficient and effective PRM administration [31]. A full integration approach to PROMs collection, as previously described [32], is particularly effective for managing PRMs. By streamlining data collection, healthcare providers can more effectively manage the collection of PROMs and PREMs, which reduces the burden of both patients and clinicians. Integrated IT systems also enhance the efficiency of data use, ensuring that valuable insights from PRMs are accessible for decision-making in a timely manner. In addition, with respect to PROMs, to address the increasing number of items in various programs, a potential solution proposed in the literature is the use of computerized adaptive testing (CAT) questionnaires [40]. However, it is important to note that CAT questionnaires entail the risk of losing specific unexpected individual data, and they are not currently used in our hospital. Furthermore, strategic planning and the coordination of data collection efforts are crucial for optimizing the efficiency and minimizing the duplication of questionnaires. All these actions help mitigate respondent fatigue and improve the overall quality of the collected data.

Result 3: elevated total frequency with integrated PRM vision and respondent fatigue risk

Result 3 delves into the integrated analysis of both PROMs and PREMs, revealing an elevated total frequency. This integrated vision reveals a heightened risk of respondent fatigue, especially among patients with multiple data collection contacts. Potential fatigue is not only influenced by the number of contacts but also compounded by the use of multiple questionnaires, highlighting the need for a holistic approach in understanding respondent burden. As shown in our data, each timepoint often involves more than one questionnaire, which further intensifies the burden, particularly for multimorbid patients enrolled in various programs.

Our analysis revealed significant findings concerning the frequency of patient interactions within the healthcare system. Patients who had multiple visits reached averages of 7–8 contacts, indicating a potential burden and impact of questionnaire fatigue. Given that patients with four or more annual interactions with healthcare professionals represent a significant portion of the increasingly multimorbid patient population, the cumulative impact of repeated questionnaires should be carefully considered.

The increased frequency of interactions among these patients raises questions about the potential implications for respondent fatigue and, consequently, the reliability and validity of the collected data. When respondents are repeatedly engaged and asked to provide feedback, there is a risk of diminishing response quality and participant engagement [15], which can compromise the integrity of the data collected.

The incremental adoption of both PROMs and PREMs has attracted considerable attention. To address this, clear policies on survey frequency and efficacy reminder systems [41] are crucial. One strategy to address the issue of frequency is to double the frequency of patient contact, allowing for an assessment of potential questionnaire overload. For example, the questionnaire can be administered every two months for patients who visit the hospital monthly. This approach, proposed in the literature [42], aims to manage respondent burden. Another mitigation strategy could involve limiting the number of items in the questionnaires with a reference number of 15 items suggested based on feedback from clinical practice and professional consensus. However, further evidence from empirical studies is needed to determine the optimal number of items that balance completeness with patient burden.

One limitation of this study is the absence of an analysis of potential overlaps between healthcare settings within the hospital reference territory. Future research should consider this aspect to provide a more comprehensive understanding of respondent fatigue management. Another limitation is that while we discussed strategies to minimize respondent fatigue, our study did not delve deeply into patient perspectives and preferences regarding questionnaire administration. A more comprehensive understanding of patient preferences could provide valuable insights for tailoring fatigue mitigation strategies to individual needs. Additionally, the lack of control or knowledge regarding possible research initiatives collecting patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs), whether through paper-based methods or dedicated data collection tools, poses an additional concern. This circumstance, particularly in the context of a university hospital, could exacerbate the frequency of information collection and intensify the potential burden on patients, thereby amplifying the challenges associated with respondent fatigue.

The current analysis is limited to the hospital setting and does not include data from PROMs and PREMs collected at the PHC level, and the patient journey often extends beyond the hospital to include primary healthcare (PHC) visits, where additional questionnaires could be administered. Incorporating both care levels would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the patient journey. This aspect is an important area for future research and will be explored in subsequent studies to offer a more complete view of the patient experience across all stages of care.

For future considerations, it would be valuable to further investigate direct patient fatigue perception and its relationship with other factors, such as age, sex or years and having one or more clinical conditions. Further analysis is also needed to assess the corresponding impact on outcomes, satisfaction, and engagement in healthcare settings and then provide valuable insights for planning truly person-centred value-based healthcare via PRM. This research could also reveal how questionnaire fatigue truly affects patients’ experiences and help them find more ways to mitigate negative effects. Additionally, new studies could focus on building standard PRM administration protocols to strike a balance between collecting valuable patient-reported data and avoiding excessive patient burden. These guidelines could also help healthcare providers optimize data collection and ensure both the meaningfulness and effectiveness of PRM. Furthermore, future research should expand analyses with other integrated-strategy PREMs that could occur, such as blood extraction contacts or PROMs such as other diseases or programs. Finally, we could integrate the PRMs strategy with primary care settings. In this sense, a project has been started in our integrated area where patients psoriatic arthritis is detected among psoriasis patients through PURE-4 questionnaires and patients are derived from primary care to avoid advanced complications referrals (example of the A at the aforementioned MATRICS framework), and viceversa, our hospital usually prescribes through an own app but prescribes through the main app used by primary care when a disease is expected to be followed by this care level. By optimizing data collection coordination with more studies, healthcare organizations can mitigate respondent burden and improve the overall quality of collected data.

Importantly, the landscape of PROM collection in our hospital has evolved since this study was conducted. While PROMs were previously collected primarily within research frameworks [43–45] and rarely as part of routine clinical practice, a significant change has been implemented. Since January 2023, our hospital has been gradually introducing a comprehensive and unified platform for systematic PROM collection. This new platform enables the deployment of PROMs specific to various pathologies, allowing for a more targeted approach to measuring patient outcomes. This development represents a shift towards integrating patient-reported data more consistently into clinical practice, which may influence the frequency and nature of PROM administration in the future.

Conclusion

The implementation of patient-reported measures (PRMs) in healthcare settings raises concerns about respondent fatigue and its associated implications. Careful consideration of respondent fatigue is essential, particularly in the shift towards a patient-centred, value-based healthcare model. Regular assessment and mitigation strategies are vital to minimize questionnaire fatigue and ensure data validity. Five possible strategies might include sharing knowledge, appropriate PRM selection for clinical practice, providing thoughtful timing of questionnaires, selecting relevant PRMs for clinical practice, and maximizing their appropriate use in patient care. A comprehensive understanding of all patient-reported data is necessary to evaluate fatigue risk.

Our study contributes to the existing body of research by acknowledging respondent burden challenges and drawing from the literature. By implementing measures to minimize respondent fatigue, healthcare institutions can increase the reliability and impact of research findings, ultimately improving patient care and outcomes.

In conclusion, fostering a patient-centered culture through knowledge sharing during PRM implementation is key to mitigating respondent fatigue. Healthcare organizations must recognize and address fatigue risk to ensure the success and efficiency of these initiatives and, above all, to enhance the overall patient experience.
