Excess nitric oxide alters cellular pH to restrict salicylic acid movement and systemic immunity
Authors: Fan Xia, Huazhen Liu, Keshun Yu, Xinyu Yuan, Aardra Kachroo, Pradeep Kachroo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plants carrying a mutation in S-nitrosoglutathione reductase 1 (GSNOR1) accumulate high nitric oxide (NO) and exhibit impaired immunity characterized by reduced pathogen-responsive salicylic acid (SA) accumulation and failure to activate normal SA signaling. We show that this reduced SA responsiveness in gsnor1 plants arises from impaired vascular-associated movement of SA, thereby compromising systemic acquired resistance (SAR). Elevated NO perturbs cellular pH homeostasis, acidifying the apoplast and alkalinizing the cytosol, which likely interferes with phloem-associated SA movement and renders gsnor1 plants unresponsive to foliar SA. In contrast, SA supplied via root drench restores SA signaling and SAR in gsnor1, likely because sustained xylem delivery bypasses the mutant’s defect in SA entry into the symplast. NO-mediated modulation of pH and its downstream effects on solute and ion transport in mammals suggest a conserved role for NO in regulating transport processes across biological systems. Our study provides previously unknown insights into how spatial NO gradients fine-tune immune signaling in plants and potentially across the organismal scale.

INTRODUCTION

Nitric oxide (NO) is an essential signal regulator of various physiological processes, including growth, development, and stress responses, in both plants and animals (1–3). In mammals, NO is synthesized primarily by NO synthase (NOS), which catalyzes the conversion of l-arginine to l-citrulline, generating NO as a byproduct (4). Although animal NOS inhibitors seem to affect NO production in plants, no canonical NOS gene has been identified in plants. Genetic, biochemical, and pharmacological studies suggest that nitrate reductase and NO-associated 1 (NOA1) enzymes play key roles in plant NO production (5–7). NOA1, a chloroplastic guanosine triphosphatase (8, 9), contributes to NO synthesis or accumulation through an as-yet-unknown mechanism. Levels of the fatty acid oleic acid, in turn, affect NO levels due to its regulation of NOA1; oleic acid binds the NOA1 protein and promotes its degradation (8).

The unpaired electron of NO makes it highly reactive, enabling interaction with superoxide anions (O2-) and other biological molecules. This in turn can result in the formation of reactive nitrogen species such as peroxynitrite, S-nitrosothiols (like S-nitrosoglutathione, GSNO), nitroxyl, nitrosonium cation, and higher oxides of nitrogen (10). Of these, GSNO is formed through the reaction of NO with glutathione. This reaction is considered reversible and enzyme independent. GSNO plays a pivotal role in mediating protein S-nitrosylation, serving as a relatively stable form that can transport NO bioactivity throughout the cell. Hence, GSNO serves as a major bioactive reservoir of NO across various organisms (11). The breakdown of GSNO is catalyzed by the conserved enzyme GSNO reductase (GSNOR) (12). In animals, GSNOR is involved in regulating blood pressure and stress responses. In plants, GSNOR has been implicated in immune responses, hormone signaling, and various aspects of growth and development (13–16).

The Arabidopsis gsnor1 mutant exhibits altered disease resistance to various pathogens (12). Similarly, silencing of the GSNOR1 gene in tomato affects defense responses, indicating that GSNOR1 plays a conserved role across different plant species (17). Compared to wild-type plants, the gsnor1 mutant accumulates substantially lower levels of salicylic acid (SA) after pathogen infection (13). The gsnor1 plants are also not responsive to exogenous SA, and this response is thought to be associated with GSNO-mediated S-nitrosylation of the nonexpressor of pathogenesis-related 1 (NPR1) protein, a key regulator of SA signaling (18). The transcription factor TGA1, another important component of SA signaling, also undergoes GSNO-mediated S-nitrosylation during pathogen defense (19). These findings suggest that GSNOR1 plays a key regulatory role in SA signaling. Beyond its role as a local and systemic signaling molecule, SA also exhibits direct antimicrobial and antibiofilm activities in the leaf apoplast, including during age-related resistance (20, 21). These local apoplastic functions likely complement SA’s symplastic signaling role that also governs cellular uptake and phloem loading examined here.

NO is rapidly produced in response to pathogen infection and is considered an important early signal in plant immune responses (22–29). For example, the Arabidopsis nia1 nia2 mutant, which has reduced NO levels, fails to develop a normal hypersensitive response against Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato (Pst) and is more susceptible to Botrytis cinerea (30, 31). In addition, noa1 nia plants, which are significantly impaired in pathogen-induced NO production, are impaired in systemic acquired resistance (SAR), a form of broad-spectrum immunity that protects against secondary infections (32–34). Notably, NO regulates SAR in a dose-dependent manner, with both low and high NO levels compromising SAR (35). In this study, we examined why elevated NO levels impair SAR and found that high NO levels impair cellular pH and disrupt SA transport, which is essential for SAR (36, 37).

RESULTS

gsnor1-mediated NO accumulation is independent of NOA and NIA

To characterize the molecular mechanisms underlying the NO-mediated suppression of SAR, we assessed SAR in gsnor1 plants, which contain elevated levels of NO (18, 35). For the SAR assays, the lower three leaves of wild-type (Col-0), gsnor1-3, and the allelic mutant par2-1 plants were infiltrated with either MgCl2 (mock) or the avirulent pathogen Pst avrRpm1 (13, 38). Two days later, three upper leaves of these plants were inoculated with the virulent pathogen Pst DC3000. Pst DC3000–infected leaf samples were collected at 0 and 3 days postinoculation (dpi) to monitor bacterial growth. Consistent with previous findings, SAR was compromised in both gsnor1 and par2 (Fig. 1, A and B). The gsnor1 plants expressing a wild-type GSNOR1 copy were restored in SAR (Fig. 1C), indicating that the compromised SAR phenotype is specifically associated with the gsnor1 mutation.

To examine whether the elevated NO levels in gsnor1 mutants are associated with the compromised SAR phenotype, we conducted an epistatic analysis between gsnor1 and genes involved in NO biosynthesis and/or accumulation. In plants, NO biosynthesis and accumulation are primarily regulated by nitrate reductase (NIA) and the NOA1 protein (5, 6). Previously, we demonstrated that NIA genes function redundantly with NOA1 to regulate the SAR phenotype (35). On this basis, we generated gsnor1 noa1 and gsnor1 noa1 nia2 mutants to assess their ability to accumulate NO and induce SAR. The gsnor1 noa1 double mutant exhibited morphological defects of both gsnor1 and noa1 parents and contained high NO levels (Fig. 1, D and E). In contrast, the gsnor1 noa1 nia2 triple mutant displayed severe developmental abnormalities and reduced seed set, and contained high NO levels (Fig. 1, D and E). Consistent with their high NO content, gsnor1 noa1 plants were compromised in SAR (Fig. 1F). We were unable to assess SAR in the gsnor1 noa1 nia2 triple mutants due to their severe developmental phenotype. These results suggest that the elevated NO levels observed in gsnor1 plants are independent of regulation by NOA1 or NIA2.

AzA and G3P levels are not affected in the gsnor1 plants

In the SAR signaling pathway, AzA (azelaic acid) and G3P (glycerol-3-phosphate) function downstream of the NO-ROS (reactive oxygen species) feed-back loop (37, 39, 40). Similar to NO, ROS contributes to SAR in a dose-dependent manner, and both NO and ROS positively regulate AzA production by facilitating the cleavage of the C9 double bond in C18 fatty acids (35). We tested whether the elevated NO levels in gsnor1 plants affected their ROS, AzA, or G3P levels. The gsnor1 plants contained wild type–like levels of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), AzA, and G3P both before and after infection with Pst avrRpm1 (fig. S1, A to C). Moreover, the gsnor1 rbohD plants were SAR compromised (fig. S1D). We next evaluated whether the gsnor1 mutation alters AZI1 (AzA-induced 1) and DIR1 (defective in induced resistance 1) protein levels, as these lipid transfer proteins have been suggested to function in a positive feedback loop with G3P (41–45). Overexpression of AZI1 or DIR1 has been reported to enhance SAR in wild-type plants, and each protein can functionally compensate for the other in azi1 or dir1 mutants, respectively (43). For this, we crossed the gsnor1 mutant with 35S-AZI1-MYC and 35S-DIR1-GFP (green fluorescent protein) plants (Col-0 background) (46). Analysis of AZI1-MYC and DIR1-GFP in the gsnor1 35S-AZI1-MYC or gsnor1 35S-DIR1-GFP plants, respectively, showed that these plants contained similar levels of AZI1 and DIR1 as wild-type plants expressing 35S-AZI1-MYC or 35S-DIR1-GFP (fig. S1, E and F). Furthermore, the gsnor1 35S-AZI1-MYC or gsnor1 35S-DIR1-GFP plants were not restored in SAR, and exogenous application of AzA or G3P did not induce SAR in gsnor1 plants (fig. S1, G and H). While this experiment demonstrates that AZI1 and DIR1 overexpression does not restore SAR in gsnor1, the constitutively high protein levels in these lines limit our ability to assess whether the gsnor1 mutation itself alters endogenous AZI1 or DIR1 abundance. We therefore interpret these findings as supportive of SAR impairment in gsnor1 but not definitive evidence regarding AZI1 or DIR1 regulation in this background.

The gsnor1 mutation is epistatic to camta1/2/3

As shown before, the pathogen-inoculated gsnor1 plants accumulated significantly less SA and SA glucoside (SAG) than wild-type plants (Fig. 2, A and B). Incidentally, the dgd1-1 mutant, which is defective in the biosynthesis of the chloroplastic membrane lipid digalactosyldiacylglycerol (DGDG), also contains reduced SA and exhibits stunting. Therefore, we investigated the potential phenotypic overlap between gsnor1 and dgd1. A total lipid profile comparison of wild-type (Col-0), gsnor1, and dgd1-1 plants revealed multiple DGDG subspecies significantly reduced in dgd1-1 but not in gsnor1 (Fig. 2, C and D; table S1; and fig. S2). Notably, dgd1-1 plants had over twofold differences in 31 lipid species compared to Col-0 (table S1), whereas gsnor1 plants showed differences in only two lipid species (table S1). These results indicate that the dgd1 mutation exerts a more pronounced impact on the overall lipid profile. This aligns with cluster analysis results, where dgd1-1 emerged as an outlier, distinct from wild-type and gsnor1, which grouped closely together (Fig. 2, C and D). This supports the notion that the reduced SA levels in gsnor1 plants are not associated with defects in galactolipid or other lipid species pools.

SA levels are regulated by the transcription factors SARD1 (SAR-deficient 1) and CBP60g (calmodulin-binding protein 60 g), which regulate the expression of ICS1 that encodes the SA biosynthesis enzyme isochorismate synthase. Notably, the Pst-avrRpm1–infected gsnor1 plants showed significantly reduced expression of SARD1 and CBP60g (Fig. 3, A and B), which correlates with their reduced ICS1 expression (Fig. 3C) and, in turn, their lower pathogen-responsive SA levels (Fig. 2, A and B). SARD1/CBP60g also regulate the expression of ALD1 (AGD2-like defense response 1) (47), which encodes the aminotransferase that catalyzes the biosynthesis of another SAR regulator, pipecolic acid (Pip) (48). The gsnor1 plants showed significantly lower expression of ALD1 after pathogen infection (Fig. 3D), which in turn correlated with their reduced Pip levels (Fig. 3E). Consistent with their reduced Pip levels, the gsnor1 plants also contained reduced levels of the Pip-derived SAR regulator, N-hydroxy Pip (NHP) (49) (Fig. 3F).

In addition to SARD1and CBP60g, the TGA transcription factors TGA1 and TGA4 also positively regulate SA biosynthesis. On the other hand, calmodulin-binding transcription activator (CAMTA) transcription factors negatively regulate SARD1 expression, and thereby SA and Pip biosynthesis (50–52). Unlike SARD1/CBP60g, the gsnor1 plants expressed WT-like levels of TGA1, TGA4, and CAMTA3 in pathogen-infected leaves (Fig. 3, G to I). This indicates that TGA1, TGA4, and CAMTA3 are unlikely to be associated with the reduced pathogen-responsive SA accumulation in gsnor1 plants.

There are six CAMTA isoforms in Arabidopsis, which are thought to act redundantly (53–55). Of these, the camta 2/3 double and camta 1/2/3 triple mutants exhibit constitutive defense activation and contain increased levels of SA and Pip (52, 56). We examined whether the mutations in CAMTA genes up-regulate SARD1 expression and thereby affect the SAR phenotype in gsnor1 plants. For this, we generated the gsnor1 camta1/2/3 quadruple mutant. gsnor1 was epistatic to camta1/2/3 and suppressed the stunted morphology and constitutive cell death phenotypes as well as their high H2O2, SA, Pip levels, and elevated SARD1 expression (Fig. 4, A to E). In contrast, the gsnor1 camta1/2/3 quadruple mutant contained elevated NO levels similar to the camta1/2/3 triple mutant (Fig. 4F). gsnor1 camta1/2/3 was also compromised in SAR (Fig. 4G). Together, these results suggest that the gsnor1 mutation is epistatic to camta1/2/3 and that GSNOR1 plays a major role in the regulation of SARD1 expression, and in turn SARD1-mediated SA and Pip biosynthesis. Notably, although gsnor1 suppressed the elevated SA and Pip levels of camta1/2/3, the gsnor1 camta2/3 and gsnor1 camta1/2/3 mutants still accumulated higher-than-wild-type levels of SA and Pip while exhibiting wild-type–like morphology (Fig. 4, A to D) (52, 56).

The gsnor1 plants are defective in SA accumulation

Although the gsnor1 plants accumulated SA, Pip, and NHP in response to pathogen infection, these levels are significantly lower than those observed in wild-type plants (Fig. 2, A, B, E, and F). To determine whether reduced SA, Pip, or NHP levels contributed to the defective SAR in gsnor1 plants, we assessed SAR in response to localized application of SA, Pip, or NHP. Exogenously applied Pip, NHP, or SA conferred SAR in wild-type plants but not in gsnor1 (Fig. 5, A and B), suggesting that the SAR defect in the gsnor1 plants was not related to their lower than wild-type levels of pathogen-responsive SA, Pip, or NHP. We next tested whether the gsnor1 mutation affected accumulation of SAR-associated chemicals. To test this, we quantified levels of SA, G3P, and NHP in the petiole exudate (PEX) collected from mock- or pathogen (Pst avrRpm1)–infected plants. PEX from pathogen-infected gsnor1 plants contained wild type–like levels of G3P (Fig. 5C). In contrast, their PEX SA levels were significantly lower than those in wild-type plants (Fig. 5D). We were unable to detect NHP in the PEX of either wild-type or gsnor1 plants. Previously, we suggested that apoplastic accumulation of SA is an early step preceding SA accumulation in infected leaves (36). This conclusion was based on the timing of SA detection in the apoplast relative to total leaf SA levels, although cytosolic SA was not directly measured. In addition, we showed that a portion of cellular SA accumulates in the cuticle wax and that this process is driven by cellular water potential (36). While tga1 tga4 mutants accumulated significantly less SA in local leaves, they maintained normal SA levels in PEX (fig. S3), suggesting that SA is still loaded into the phloem and exported from the induced leaf despite reduced local accumulation (36). In contrast, pathogen-infected gsnor1 plants contained significantly reduced SA in their apoplastic compartment (Fig. 5E) as well as in cuticular wax (Fig. 5F). The reduced PEX SA in sard1 cbp60g double mutant plants reflected their overall reduced SA levels (fig. S3C). Together, these observations suggest that gsnor1 plants are unable to accumulate normal SA levels in PEX. Consistently, introducing gsnor1 into the camta2,3 background, despite its elevated leaf and PEX SA (Fig. 4, C and D, and fig. S3, D and E), still impaired PEX SA levels in gsnor1 camta plants, suggesting a defect in transport capacity rather than SA availability. However, the defective PEX SA levels in gsnor1 plants was not associated with reduced water potential or impaired cuticular permeability because gsnor1 plants showed wild type–like water potential (fig. S4A). The gsnor1 leaves were also not permeable to the hydrophilic dye toluidine blue (fig. S4B) and exhibited normal chlorophyll leaching (fig. S4C), suggesting an intact cuticle.

To probe the step(s) at which the gsnor1 mutant is impaired, we applied SA or 14C-SA locally to leaves and monitored recovery in the treated tissue and PEX 24 hours posttreatment. Because exogenous SA is introduced directly into the intercellular space, these assays do not measure endogenous SA flux during SAR; rather, they serve as a proxy for apoplast-to-symplast uptake and/or phloem loading. Under these conditions, the gsnor1 mutant displayed significantly reduced SA in distal exudates and reduced incorporation into the treated tissue, which we interpret as consistent with a defect in cellular uptake and/or phloem loading, not as a definitive measurement of endogenous SA transport in vivo (Fig. 5, G and H). Because distal Pip accumulation requires intact SA and G3P signaling (57), we quantified Pip in untreated distal leaves. Pip was reduced in the gsnor1 plants (Fig. 5I); however, given the attenuated ALD1 expression in this background, reduced Pip may also reflect limited biosynthetic capacity in addition to diminished SA-dependent signaling. We therefore present the Pip data as supportive of compromised systemic signaling while acknowledging that ALD1 availability is a plausible contributing factor.

The gsnor1 plants are competent to generate the TAS3a SAR signal

Previously, we showed that SAR relies on multiple signals, including SA, G3P, and the early RNA signal TAS3 (Trans-acting small-interfering RNA 3a) and that proper SA accumulation is required for G3P and TAS3a to confer SAR (36, 45, 58, 59). To determine whether this relationship holds in gsnor1, we first examined TAS3a levels. gsnor1 plants accumulated wild-type levels of TAS3a (fig. S5A), consistent with the idea that their SA levels exceed the threshold required for TAS3a stability (59). TAS3a undergoes phased cleavage following pathogen infection to generate small RNAs that travel systemically to activate SAR. To test whether this transport step remains functional, we locally applied a fluorescein-labeled small RNA (D7-FAM) and found that its movement to distal tissues was normal in gsnor1 (fig. S5B). Thus, impaired SA accumulation in gsnor1 does not interfere with long-distance movement of the small RNA SAR signal.

Although SA levels in gsnor1 PEX are reduced relative to wild type, they are not absent. Together with the intact G3P and TAS3a signals present in gsnor1, the PEX collected from gsnor1 was sufficient to activate SAR in Col-0 (fig. S5C), which retains functional SA transport and perception. However, when gsnor1 was used as the recipient, neither Col-0 PEX nor gsnor1 PEX was able to trigger SAR. This suggests that although gsnor1 plants generate the necessary SAR signals, they cannot respond to them because defective SA transport prevents proper signal perception. These findings support a model in which gsnor1 maintains signal production (G3P, TAS3a, and small-RNA mobility) but fails in the SA transport–dependent perception step required for SAR activation.

Increased NO levels inhibit SA uptake

The fact that exogenous application of an NO donor above 300 μM inhibits SAR (35), together with the result that gsnor1 plants accumulate elevated levels of NO, prompted us to test whether higher NO levels inhibit SA uptake. We assayed the uptake of 14C-SA into protoplasts prepared from Col-0 leaves. The freshly prepared protoplasts were incubated with various concentrations of the NO donor DETA-NONOate or the N2O donor Sulfo-NONOate and 14C-SA, followed by three washes before quantifying 14C-SA levels. The untreated and Sulfo-NONOate–treated protoplasts showed similar uptake of SA, but DETA-NONOate repressed SA uptake in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 6A, left). Incubation of DETA-NONOate with SA did not alter SA levels or its structure based on mass spectral analysis (fig. S6). This suggests that reduced import of SA in DETA-NONOate–treated protoplasts was not due to chemical alteration of 14C-SA. We further confirmed this by assaying the uptake of deuterated SA, which likewise showed a significant reduction in protoplasts treated with 1 mM DETA-NONOate (Fig. 6A, right). Unlike the NO donor, protoplasts treated with H2O2 showed normal accumulation and uptake of 14C-SA (Fig. 6B). Next, we assayed SA accumulation in Col-0 leaves pretreated with DETA-NONOate or Sulfo-NONOate for 12 hours before infection with Pst avrRpm1. Mock (MgCl2) and Pst avrRpm1 were used as controls. PEX from pathogen-infected leaves that were pretreated with DETA-NONOate contained significantly reduced SA compared to PEX from leaves that were only pathogen infected or pretreated with Sulfo-NONOate before pathogen infection (Fig. 6C). Together, these results support the notion that increased NO levels inhibit SA movement and that the high NO in gsnor1 plants was likely responsible for their impaired SAR.

Because SA uptake is pH dependent (36, 60), these NO-associated pH shifts likely impair the foliar uptake and intracellular distribution of SA during SAR establishment. To quantify pH changes in vivo, we used Arabidopsis lines expressing the genetically encoded pHusion sensor targeted to either the cytosol or the apoplast (61). The pHusion construct is a tandem fusion of enhanced GFP (EGFP) and monomeric red fluorescent protein (mRFP1), whose fluorescence properties differ in their sensitivity to pH. EGFP fluorescence decreases under acidic conditions and increases under more alkaline conditions, whereas mRFP1 is relatively pH insensitive within the physiological range and therefore serves as an internal reference. Consequently, the GFP/mRFP fluorescence ratio provides a robust ratiometric measure of local pH: Higher ratios indicate more alkaline environments, while lower ratios indicate increased acidity. We crossed these pHusion reporters into the gsnor1 background and observed a significant shift in the GFP/mRFP ratio compared to wild type. Specifically, gsnor1 leaves exhibited a more acidic apoplast and a more alkaline cytosol (Fig. 6D), indicating a disruption of pH homeostasis across the plasma membrane. To test whether plasma-membrane H+–adenosine triphosphatases (ATPases) underlie the SA uptake/transport phenotypes, we isolated homozygous mutants for AHA1, AHA2, AHA4, and AHA5 in the Col-0 background. In SAR assays, aha2, aha4, and aha5 displayed wild-type SAR, whereas aha1 showed a reproducible SAR defect accompanied by a significant reduction in SA in the PEX (Fig. 6, E and F). The aha1 leaves accumulated wild-type levels of free SA and SAG (Fig. 6F), suggesting that AHA1 function contributes to the accumulation of SA in SAR-induced leaf exudates and to SA movement during SAR. Additional detailed analyses will be necessary to delineate the specific contribution of AHA1 to SA transport.

Impaired foliar SA uptake is responsible for the SA-insensitive phenotype of gsnor1 plants

Earlier work has shown that gsnor1 plants are partially insensitive to SA and unable to induce wild type–like levels of the SA marker gene PR-1 (18). Consistently, exogenous SA application resulted in significantly lower PR-1 induction in gsnor1 plants (Fig. 7A). To investigate whether this partial insensitivity was linked to a defect in SA uptake, we sprayed Col-0 and gsnor1 plants with 500 μM SA and analyzed total and cuticular SA levels 24 hours posttreatment. While both genotypes contained comparable amounts of leaf SA in response to exogenous SA application (foliar spray) (Fig. 7B), gsnor1 plants contained substantially higher levels of SA in the cuticular wax (Fig. 7C). Because SA sprays left disproportionately high amounts of SA in the cuticular wax of gsnor1 (Fig. 7C), we considered the possibility that the mutant’s apparent insensitivity to exogenous SA results from a defect in foliar uptake or partitioning (and/or impaired phloem loading), rather than from an inability to respond to SA once it enters the symplast. To bypass the cuticle barrier and the leaf-surface loading step, we applied SA via soil drench, a route that delivers SA to the shoot primarily through root uptake and xylem transport. To confirm that soil-applied SA indeed enters the xylem stream, we quantified SA in guttation droplets, a xylem-derived fluid exuded through hydathodes (62), and observed an ∼18-fold increase in SA in SA-treated wild-type plants compared with water controls (Fig. 7, D and E, and fig. S7, A and B). This verified that soil drench effectively loads SA into the xylem and justified its use to assess SA responsiveness independent of foliar uptake constraints. Untargeted metabolomics of guttation fluid further supported a systemic SA response: Of the 94 detected chemical entities, 7 were exclusive to SA-treated plants and 1 was reduced >twofold relative to water (table S2). Notably, four of the seven induced features were identified as glucosinolates, metabolites known to accumulate in response to SA (63). Together, these data indicate robust xylem-mediated movement of exogenous SA and concomitant SA-driven metabolic remodeling evident in the guttation fluid, reinforcing a functional role for SA at hydathodes (64).

For xylem-transported SA to elicit a cellular response such as PR-1 induction, it must cross plasma membranes at several points along the root-shoot axis, including during root uptake, symplastic passage through the endodermis, loading into xylem parenchyma/pericycle cells, and finally upon arrival in the leaf. Because gsnor1 exhibits a defect in foliar SA import, we anticipated that SA transported from the roots might also be affected. Supporting this idea, guttation fluid from gsnor1 plants contained markedly less SA than that from wild type (Fig. 7, D and E). Although guttation SA reflects xylem content, this reduction does not distinguish whether the limitation arises during root uptake, endodermal passage, xylem loading, or the subsequent apoplastic-symplastic transitions that occur as SA moves through the plant. To further assess root-to-shoot transport, we conducted a time-course analysis using root-fed deuterated SA (fig. S7C). In contrast to endogenous SA in guttation fluid, the reduction in deuterated SA detected in leaves was modest, and levels of deuterated SAG were comparable to wild type. This suggests that enough SA still reaches the leaf symplast to support normal metabolic conversion (Fig. 7, F and G, and fig. S7C). A plausible explanation is that continuous SA availability in the soil, together with the absence of a cuticle on roots (fig. S7, D and E) (65), permits sufficient membrane uptake and delivery to meet the intracellular threshold for signaling. Consistent with this, PR-1 expression in gsnor1 lagged behind wild type at early time points but reached wild-type levels by 24 hours (Fig. 7H and fig. S7F). Together, these data indicate that while gsnor1 shows reduced overall SA delivery, the major functional defect lies in the impaired foliar import of SA into leaf cells. Consistent with restored systemic signaling, soil-drench SA also enabled distal Pip accumulation after localized avrRpm1 inoculation (Fig. 7I) and induced SAR in mutant plants (Fig. 7J). Collectively, these findings indicate that the mutant’s SA-insensitive phenotype primarily reflects reduced foliar uptake and phloem loading/long-distance movement of SA, rather than impaired NPR1-dependent perception (18), and they underscore the centrality of SA transport in SAR.

NO overproducer nox1 exhibits gsnor1-like defects in SAR but displays distinct metabolic profiles

To determine whether elevated NO alone is sufficient to disrupt SA accumulation and SAR, we examined the nox1 mutant, an NO overproducer previously shown to exhibit reduced SA levels after pathogen infection (24, 25). Like gsnor1, nox1 plants were compromised in SAR (Fig. 8A). Consistent with this, the amount of SA that accumulated into PEX was also significantly less in nox1 (Figs. 8B). Although local SA and PEX-associated SA levels varied somewhat across experimental replicates (fig. S8), likely reflecting the growth variability characteristic of nox1, the overall trend was consistent with previous reports demonstrating reduced SA accumulation in nox1 (24).

Despite these shared deficiencies, nox1 and gsnor1 differ markedly in the subcellular distribution of NO (Fig. 8C). Confocal imaging revealed that NO accumulates predominantly within chloroplasts in nox1, whereas gsnor1 exhibits elevated NO both inside and outside chloroplasts (Fig. 8C). These differences in NO localization likely contribute to the distinct growth and morphological phenotypes of the two mutants. Consistent with these observations, metabolomic analysis demonstrated that nox1 and gsnor1 occupy different metabolic spaces: gsnor1 clustered with wild-type plants, whereas the nox1 gsnor1 double mutant grouped with nox1, underscoring the dominant metabolic influence of nox1-type NO overproduction (Fig. 8, D and E, and table S3).

Together, these findings indicate that excess NO perturbs SA accumulation and SAR irrespective of its source but that the physiological and metabolic consequences differ depending on how and where NO accumulates. While both mutants overproduce NO and exhibit reduced SA accumulation into PEX and impaired SAR, nox1 and gsnor1 remain clearly distinguishable in their subcellular NO distribution patterns and metabolomic profiles. These observations are consistent with recent work showing that genotypes with altered NO levels display distinct physiological outputs and metabolic states (27), reinforcing that the magnitude and spatial pattern of NO accumulation shape downstream immune and metabolic responses.

DISCUSSION

Our study reveals a mechanism by which GSNOR1 regulates SAR by modulating NO and SA accumulation. While previous research has established that NO influences plant immunity in a dose-dependent manner, with elevated NO levels suppressing SAR (35), our findings advance this knowledge by showing that excessive NO disrupts the accumulation of SA in distal tissues, a process critical for SAR activation. These results reiterate and expand on our previous report demonstrating that defective SA accumulation in cuticle mutants accounts for their compromised SAR responses (36). Notably, the gsnor1 mutants display intact cuticle structures, indicating that GSNOR1 regulates SAR through a mechanism independent of cuticle formation, specifically by modulating the spatial distribution of SA.

Our results consistently show that SA is readily detectable in PEX, whereas NHP is below the detection limit under our experimental conditions. These findings are consistent with a large body of prior literature that demonstrates reproducible detection of SA in PEX from Arabidopsis and other species (66–70). In contrast, Cao et al. (71) reported detection of NHP in PEX but did not detect SA. This discrepancy may reflect technical differences. Our platforms have consistently reproduced SA detection across dozens of independent experiments performed by multiple researchers, aligning with earlier reports of SA accumulation in PEX. Similarly, several studies have reported that NHP and Pip are not detectable in exudates (41, 72). While we cannot fully explain the basis for Cao et al.’s (71) findings, our results support the conclusion that SA is a major signal present in PEX, while NHP levels are extremely low or absent.

While definitively establishing the route of vascular entry for small molecules in vivo remains challenging, small molecules can access the phloem through both apoplastic and symplastic pathways, including via plasmodesmata connecting companion cells and sieve elements. Notably, prior work has shown that inhibition of plasmodesmatal transport does not measurably impair SA movement during SAR (58), supporting a predominant role for apoplastic and membrane transport processes. While a minor contribution from symplastic connectivity cannot be fully excluded, our data and prior findings are most consistent with a model in which SA movement is largely mediated through pH-dependent membrane transport and regulated partitioning across cellular compartments.

This behavior is consistent with well-established pH- and H+-dependent mechanisms governing weak-acid movement in plants (73). As a weak acid (pKa ∼2.97, where Ka is the acid dissociation constant), SA exists in a protonated, membrane-permeable form in the mildly acidic apoplast and becomes deprotonated and effectively trapped upon entry into the cytosol. Classical studies in Ricinus communis demonstrated that transport of weak acids is strongly pH dependent (60), with uptake enhanced under acidic extracellular conditions (74–77). In agreement with this framework, perturbation of proton gradients, including in our own assays (36), results in reduced SA uptake and movement, indicating that SA movement involves H+-coupled processes.

A particularly notable finding of this study is that the gsnor1 mutant is specifically defective in the accumulation of SA in SAR-induced exudates and in SAR-induced distal leaves. Multiple independent observations support this conclusion: (i) SA levels in PEX, an experimental proxy for phloem sap, are markedly reduced in gsnor1; (ii) locally applied SA shows diminished movement away from the application site; and (iii) exogenous SA applied through the soil, but not through the leaf, restores PR-1 induction and SAR in gsnor1. Together, these results highlight a defect in foliar import and/or phloem loading of SA rather than in SA perception or signaling. For xylem-transported SA to elicit a defense response, it must cross plasma membranes at several points along the root-shoot axis, including during initial root uptake across the epidermis and cortex, during symplastic passage through the endodermis, upon loading into xylem parenchyma/pericycle cells, and again upon arrival in the leaf. Soil-applied SA therefore provides multiple opportunities for SA to bypass the foliar transport defect in gsnor1, allowing sufficient SA to reach the leaf symplast to induce PR-1 and reconstitute SAR. In contrast, foliar SA relies on direct import into leaf cells followed by accumulation in the phloem, and this proximal step appears compromised in gsnor1. The combined dataset thus suggests that gsnor1 is impaired in the membrane-transport processes governing foliar SA uptake and long-distance phloem transport but retains sufficient root-to-shoot SA movement to support delayed yet functional immune activation. This is a notable finding because it challenges the prevailing assumption that SAR defects in gsnor1 arise mainly from NPR1 dysfunction (18). The restoration of both PR-1 expression and SAR following soil-drench SA treatment demonstrates that NPR1 remains functional in gsnor1. Instead, our results identify a defect in active SA accumulation that prevents immune activation in distal tissues.

A key mechanistic insight from our study is that gsnor1 plants exhibit an acidic apoplast and a more alkaline cytosol, as demonstrated using genetically encoded pH sensors (61). Because SA transporters operate within defined pH windows (36, 60, 78), this disruption in pH homeostasis is predicted to interfere with SA movement and compromise SAR. Consistent with this idea, the plasma-membrane H+-ATPase mutant aha1, which contributes to apoplastic and cytosolic pH regulation, also exhibited reduced PEX-associated SA and a measurable defect in SAR. However, PEX SA levels in aha1 remained above mock, and SAR impairment was only partial, indicating that AHA1 is not the sole determinant of SA transport. Other AHA isoforms (AHA2, AHA4, and AHA5), despite their established roles in growth, stomatal regulation, and immunity (79, 80), did not show reduced SA uptake or substantial SAR defects, further suggesting that additional components participate in SA transport.

These observations point toward other plasma-membrane transport proteins as the more proximal regulators of SA flux, likely an H+-coupled SA importer and/or an SA efflux mechanism. Candidate families include NPF, MATE, and ABCG transporters, whose activities are inherently pH dependent and thus sensitive to NO-induced apoplastic acidification and cytosolic alkalinization. Mechanistically, we propose two nonexclusive routes by which elevated NO generates the observed physiology: (i) primary inhibition of H+-coupled SA import, limiting proton cotransport and thereby reinforcing cytosolic alkalinization and apoplastic acidification, even under conditions that would normally favor weak-acid diffusion; and (ii) NO-mediated alterations in membrane ion fluxes (e.g., K+ or anion conductances) that diminish the proton-motive force, secondarily reducing carrier capacity and impairing SA movement into the phloem. Together, this framework explains the counterintuitive outcome that gsnor1 and aha1 show reduced SA uptake despite an acidic apoplast and highlights specific plasma-membrane transport proteins as testable candidates underlying pH-dependent SA transport. In humans, NO and pH are similarly intertwined; acidic and alkaline conditions have opposing effects on NOS activity and thereby NO-mediated vasodilation (81). In addition, macrophage-mediated inflammation is modulated through NO-pH interactions (82). Moreover, pH also influences neurotransmitter transport in the human nervous system, just as it modulates SA transport in plants (83, 84). Together, these findings suggest that NO-pH interactions represent a conserved regulatory mechanism that affects metabolite transport and signaling across biological kingdoms.

Last, our findings highlight a dual and dose-dependent role of NO in plant immunity, wherein NO can either promote or suppress defense responses depending on its concentration and spatial distribution (18, 35). An additional layer through which elevated NO may influence systemic immunity involves potential redox regulation of the transcription factor SARD1, as suggested by recent work (85). Although the precise relationship between SARD1 S-nitrosylation, its transcriptional regulation, and the activation of downstream pathways such as CBP60g remains incompletely understood, our data are consistent with a model in which excessive NO perturbs both SA transport and the transcriptional reinforcement of SA/Pip/NHP biosynthesis. This possibility highlights an additional mechanistic axis through which disrupted NO homeostasis may weaken systemic immunity in gsnor1.

Notably, this NO buildup occurs independently of NOA1 and NIA, the two canonical sources of NO in plants (5, 6), suggesting that alternative biosynthetic routes or residual NO production, combined with defective NO turnover, contribute to the elevated NO levels in gsnor1. Strikingly, this phenomenon parallels well-established principles in human physiology. In both systems, maintaining NO homeostasis is essential, as excessive NO alters membrane transport processes and perturbs signaling networks. In humans, dysregulated NO impairs neurotransmitter trafficking and synaptic activity in Alzheimer’s disease (86) and contributes to endothelial dysfunction that limits the transport of vasoactive molecules in vascular disorders (87). These cross-kingdom similarities suggest a conserved biological principle: Elevated NO, regardless of origin or context, can interfere with membrane transport and thereby disrupt transport-dependent signaling pathways. Our work positions gsnor1 as a model for dissecting how NO-driven alterations in transport processes reshape immune signaling in plants, with conceptual parallels to NO-mediated pathophysiology in animals.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant growth conditions and genetic analysis

Plants were grown in MTPS 144 Conviron (Winnipeg, MB, Canada) walk-in or MTR-30 reach-in chambers set at 22°C, 65% relative humidity. These chambers were equipped with cool white, fluorescent bulbs (Sylvania, FO96/841/XP/ECO) or T6/T8 LED lights. The photon flux density during the day period ranged from 109 to 120 μmol m−2 s−1, as measured using a digital light meter (Phytotronic Inc., MO), a LI-COR-180 spectroradiometer (LI-COR Inc.), and the chamber microprocessor. Growth chambers were programmed for 12-hour day length. Light intensity across wavelengths was measured using a LI-COR-180 spectroradiometer. Genotypes and primers used in this study are listed in tables S4 and S5.

Plants were grown on autoclaved Pro-Mix soil (Premier Horticulture Inc., PA, USA). The soil was fertilized once using Peter’s 20:10:20 peat lite special general fertilizer that contained 8.1% ammoniacal nitrogen and 11.9% nitrate nitrogen (JR Peters Inc., PA, USA). The plants were irrigated using deionized or tap water. The gsnor1-3 and par2 plants used here are described before (13, 38). Unless noted otherwise, all experiments were performed at 30 to 32 days postsowing. The same plant age was used for every experiment (pathogen assays, metabolite measurements, petiole-exudate collections, and imaging).

Generation of transgenic plants

For transgenic overexpression of GSNOR1, the cDNA spanning the coding region was cloned into pGWB5 vector, which after confirmation of the DNA sequence was transformed into gsnor1 plants. The transgenic plants were selected on plates containing kanamycin (50 μg/ml) and hygromycin (17 μg/ml).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Small-scale extraction of RNA from two or three leaves (per sample) was performed with the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and concentration were determined by gel electrophoresis and determination of absorbance at 260 nm. Reverse transcription (RT) and first-strand cDNA synthesis were carried out using Superscript II (Invitrogen, CA). Quantitative RT–polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was carried out as described before (88). Each sample was run in triplicates and ACTINII (At3g18780) expression levels were used as internal control for normalization. Cycle threshold values were calculated by SDS 2.3 software.

Protein extraction and immunoblot analysis

Proteins were extracted in buffer containing 50 mM tris-HCl (pH7.5), 10% glycerol, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 5 mM EDTA, 5 mM dithiothreitol, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Protein concentration was measured by the Bio-Rad protein assay (Bio-Rad, CA). For Ponceau-S staining, polyvinylidene difluoride membranes were incubated in Ponceau-S solution [40% methanol (v/v), 15% acetic acid (v/v), and 0.25% Ponceau-S (w/v)]. The membranes were destained using deionized water. Proteins (∼100 μg) were fractionated on a 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel and subjected to immunoblot analysis using α-GFP or α-MYC antibodies. Immunoblots were developed using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (Roche).

Pathogen infection and collection of PEX and apoplastic fluid

Inoculations with Pst DC 3000 or Pst-expressing avrRpm1 were conducted as described before (89). The bacterial cultures were grown overnight in King’s B medium containing rifampicin and/or kanamycin. For analysis of SAR, the primary leaves were inoculated with MgCl2 or the avrRpm1 bacteria [107 colony-forming units (CFUs) ml−1], and, 48 hours later, the systemic leaves were inoculated with vir bacteria (105 CFU ml−1). Unless noted otherwise, samples from the systemic leaves were harvested at 3 dpi. PEX were collected in diethyl pyrocarbonate–treated water as described earlier (35, 45, 90, 91). PEX was collected for 24 hours and assayed for bacterial growth to ensure that it did not contain any viable bacteria. Petioles were recut under sterile water, incubated 1 mM EDTA (pH 7.5) for 10 to 20 min to reduce sieve-plate sealing, then rinsed twice, and transferred to 1 mM EDTA for the main collection. Exudation proceeded for 10 to 24 hours in the MTPS 144 growth chamber programmed for 12-hour day length. Apoplastic fluid collection was carried out as described before (92). Briefly, detached leaves were infiltrated with sterile water, blotted, and centrifuged 800 to 1000g, 10 min, 4°C with lamina facing down. Protein in PEX and apoplastic fluids was measured by Bradford assays. Protein yields from PEX and apoplastic fluid were 2 to 12 and 0.3 to 0.6 μg/μl, respectively.

ROS staining

The leaves were placed in six-well plates containing 2 to 5 ml of DAB (3,3′-diaminobenzidine) staining solution [DAB (1 mg/ml), 200 mM Na2HPO4 (pH 3.0), and 1 Tween 20 (μl/ml)]. A gentle vacuum was applied to facilitate penetration of the staining solution into the leaf tissues. The plates were incubated at room temperature for 6 to 8 hours on an orbital shaker set at 150 rpm (Varion Inc., model T, New Jersey, USA). The staining solution was discarded, a destaining solution containing ethanol:acetic acid:glycerol (3:1:1) was added to the leaves, and the plates were boiled in a water bath for 5 to 10 min. The leaf samples were mounted on slides using glycerol and photographed using an AxioCam camera (Zeiss, Germany).

Trypan blue staining

The leaves were vacuum infiltrated with trypan blue staining solution (10 mg of trypan blue in 10 ml of acidic phenol, 10 ml of glycerol, and 20 ml of sterile water). The samples were placed in a water bath (65°C) for 10 min and incubated at room temperature for 8 to 12 hours. The samples were destained using chloral hydrate (25 g/10 ml of sterile water; Sigma-Aldrich), mounted on slides, and observed for cell death with a compound microscope. The samples were photographed using an AxioCam camera (Zeiss, Germany).

Chemical and RNA treatments

SA, G3P, AzA, NHP, and Pip treatments were carried out by using 500, 100, 1000, 100, and 1000 μM solutions, respectively. DETA-NONOate {(Z)-1-[N-(2-aminoethyl)-N-(2-ammonioethyl) amino] diazen-1-ium-1,2-diolate} and Sulfo-NONOate (hydroxydiazenesulfonic acid 1-oxide) were prepared and diluted in water and used at indicated concentrations. All dilutions were freshly prepared before performing biological experiments.

G3P, SA, NHP, and Pip quantifications

G3P and AzA quantifications were carried out as described earlier (45, 93), and otherwise mentioned, all quantifications were done 24 hours posttreatment. SA and SAG were extracted and measured from ∼0.1 g of fresh weight leaf tissue, as described before (94). Pip quantifications were carried out using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) (57, 95). For quantification of SA and AzA in PEX, the samples were dried under nitrogen, suspended in acetonitrile (ACN) and derivatized with N-methyl-N-(tert-butyldimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide containing 1% tert-butyldimethylchlorosilane. Derivatized samples were analyzed by GC-MS, and SA/AzA levels were quantified using external standard curves and the MassHunter Quantitative Analysis software (Agilent Technologies).

For quantification of G3P in PEX, the 24-hour samples were dried under nitrogen, suspended in ACN, and derivatized with MSTFA [N-methyl-N-(trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide] [containing 1% TMCS (trimethylchlorosilane] and analyzed by GC-MS. For total NHP quantification, 10 mg of lyophilized leaf tissue sampled 24 hours postinoculation was ground using liquid nitrogen in a 1.5-ml microfuge tube and suspended in 1 ml of 80% methanol containing 1 μg of anisic acid as an internal standard. The extract was vortexed at 4°C for 10 min and centrifuged at 6000g for 10 min. The supernatant was then transferred to a 13 mm–by–100 mm glass tube and dried under a stream of nitrogen gas. The dry pellet was resuspended in 200 μl of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 5.0), transferred to a fresh 1.5-ml microfuge tube, and centrifuged at 6000g for 20 min to remove chlorophyll. The supernatant was transferred to a GC vial and reacted with 15 U of β-galactosidase at 30°C for 1 hour. The reaction was then dried under nitrogen gas, suspended in 50 μl of ACN, and derivatized with 50 μl of MSTFA (containing 1% TMCS) at 65°C for 1 hour. NHP levels were analyzed and quantified using GC-MS. Both GC-MS and liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) (see below) platforms were used to assay NHP levels in PEX.

Analysis of wax and cutin components

For analysis of the wax components, 500 mg of 4-week-old leaves were immersed in 10 ml of chloroform for 10 s. The leaves, sampled 24 hours posttreatments, were rinsed once more with 10 ml of chloroform for 10 s. An internal standard (3 μg of n-tetracosane) was added, and the extract was evaporated under a gentle steam of nitrogen. The samples were derivatized using diazomethane followed by 100 μl of pyridine and 100 μl of acetic anhydride, and the sealed tubes were incubated for 1 hour at 60°C. The samples were again dried under a gentle stream of nitrogen and dissolved in 0.5 ml of heptane:toluene (1:1. v/v). The solution was washed with 400 μl of 1% NaHCO3 before GC-MS analysis.

D7-FAM transport assays

FAM (6-carboxyfluorescein)–tagged D7 RNA (D7-FAM, synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies or Eurofins Genomics) was dissolved in ribonuclease-free water. The abaxial surface of 3-week-old Arabidopsis leaves was infiltrated with 5 to 10 μM D7-FAM. The plants were then kept in a growth chamber with 14-hour light and 10-hour dark photoperiods, and the infiltrated (local) uninfiltrated (distal) leaves were analyzed 4 hour posttreatment by confocal microscopy using 488-nm laser (59).

SA transport assays

For SA transport, [14C] SA (1 μCi/ml, specific activity 38 mCi/mmol; PerkinElmer Inc.) was suspended in 10 mM MgCl2 and used for infiltrations. The resulting solution contained 20 μM of SA and was injected into abaxial surface of 4-week-old Arabidopsis leaves. Three leaves per plant were infiltrated with ∼0.05 ml of 14C-SA solution. The plants were then kept in a growth chamber set at 12-hour light and 12-hour dark photoperiods and sampled 24 hours posttreatments. The leaf samples were extracted using SA extraction method described before (94). The samples were quantified using a liquid scintillation counter.

Protoplast 14C-SA and deuterated-SA transport assays

Protoplasts were prepared as described before (36), quantified using a hemocytometer, and suspended at a concentration of 106/ml. For 14C-SA uptake assays, 200 μl of freshly prepared protoplast were incubated with 2 μM 14C-SA for 1 hour in W5 buffer {154 mM NaCl, 125 mM CaCl2, 5 mM KCl, and 2 mM MES [2-(N-morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid] (pH 5.7)}. The protoplasts were pelleted by centrifugation at 100g for 3 min and washed four times with W5 buffer. Radioactivity in the protoplast and the supernatant was measured using a liquid scintillation counter. For deuterated-SA uptake assays, protoplasts were incubated with 50 μM D4-SA for 1 hour under identical buffer and centrifugation conditions. After washing, protoplast pellets and pooled supernatants were collected for LC-MS analysis (see below) of deuterated-SA abundance to quantify SA uptake.

Lipid analysis

For lipid profiles ∼100 mg of leaves were extracted as described (96). The extracts were chromatographed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC coupled to an Agilent 6546 time-of-flight mass spectrometer. A 1.8-μm, 2.1 mm–by–50 mm Zorbax Eclipse Plus C18 column was used for reverse phase chromatography with 20-min binary gradient consisting of ACN-water (60:40, v/v; A) and isopropanol-ACN (90/10, v/v; B). Both A and B solutions contained 10 mM ammonium formate. The following gradient was used for separation with a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min: 0 to 5.5 min (30% B), 5.5 to 11.50 min (10 to 100% B), and 11.60 to 16 min (100 to 10% B). The MS was run in positive and negative electrospray ionization mode with the following parameters: gas temperature, 300°C; drying gas flow rate, 12 liters/min; nebulizer, 35 psi; sheath gas temperature, 275°C; sheath gas flow 12 liters/min; fragmentor voltage, 120 V; skimmer voltage, 65 V; nozzle voltage, 750 V; and capillary voltage, 3500 V. Data were analyzed using Lipid Annotator, MassHunter-Profinder, and -Mass Profiler Professional softwares.

Untargeted metabolite profiling

Guttation fluid collected from Col-0 or gsnor1 plants were chromatographed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC coupled to an Agilent 6546 time-of-flight mass spectrometer. A 150 mm–by–4.6 mm Luna Omega 3 μM polar C18 column was used for reverse phase chromatography with water containing 0.1% formic acid (FA) as mobile phase A and ACN containing 0.1% FA as mobile phase B. The following gradient was used for separation with a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min: 0 to 5.5 min (10% B), 5.5 to 11.50 min (10 to 100% B), and 11.60 to 16 min (100 to 10% B). The MS was run in negative mode with the following parameters: gas temperature, 300°C; drying gas flow rate, 12 liter/min; nebulizer, 35 psi; sheath gas temperature, 275°C; sheath gas flow 12 liter/min; fragmentor voltage, 120 V; skimmer voltage, 65 V; nozzle voltage, 0 V; capillary voltage, 3500 V. Data were analyzed using MassHunter-Profinder and -Mass Profiler Professional software.

NO staining

NO staining of leaves was carried out as described before (8). NO staining of roots was performed by incubated these in a solution containing 0.1 mM CaCl2, 10 mM KCl, 10 mM MES-tris (pH 5.6) (incubating solution) for 1 hour, followed by staining with 10 μM DAF-2DA (4,5-diaminofluorescein diacetate, Molecular Probe) for 45 min. The tissues were washed twice with incubating solution and observed under Olympus FV3000 laser-scanning confocal microscope using 488-nm laser.

Water potential, toluidine blue staining, and chlorophyll leaching assay

Water potential was quantified using a pressure chamber (model 3000, Soil moisture Equipment Corp., Santa Barbara, CA USA) as described previously (36). Toluidine blue staining was carried out as described earlier (97). Briefly, 7 μl of 0.05% (w/v) toluidine blue water solution was spotted on the adaxial side of the leaves. After 5 min, stained leaves were detached from plants and washed gently with water to remove excess toluidine blue; the leaves were then ready for imaging. Chlorophyll leaching assay was conducted as previously described (89).

Confocal microscopy and analysis of pH sensor plants

For confocal imaging, samples were scanned on an Olympus FV1000 microscope (Olympus America, Melvile, NY). Analysis of pHusion transgenics was carried out as described before (61). EGFP excitation was performed at 488 nm, and emission was captured in the 500- to 550-nm range. mRFP1 was excited at either 558 or 585 nm, with fluorescence detection set between 600 and 630 nm. Images were background-subtracted, and the per-pixel GFP/mRFP ratio computed within apoplastic regions of interest (cell wall space). Higher ratios reflect alkaline apoplast; lower ratios reflect acidic apoplast. Olympus FLUOVIEW 1.5 was used to control the microscope, image acquisition, and the export of TIFF files.

Statistics and reproducibility

For pathogen assays, ∼16 plants/genotype/treatment were analyzed in a single experiment. At least three to four biological replicates/genotype/treatment were plated. For metabolite quantification, ∼12 plants/genotype/treatment were analyzed in each experiment. NO staining and confocal analysis was evaluated in at least 7 to 10 independent root or leaf samples per genotype, with 5 to 7 fields of view examined per sample. Experiments were repeated at least two-three times with a different set of plants as indicated in the figure legends. Unless otherwise mentioned error bars indicate SD. Comparisons between two groups used two-sided Student’s t tests. Statistical analyses were performed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) as appropriate to the experimental design, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical tests were conducted on biological replicate values, and the number of biological replicates (n) is reported in each figure.
