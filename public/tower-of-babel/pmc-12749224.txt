Chronic alcohol consumption disrupts the integrity of the blood-brain barrier through the gut-brain axis
Authors: Chenguang Li, Hongxuan Wang, Xinrou Lin, Guanyong Zeng, Xiangpen Li, Wanru Chen, Hengjian Lu, Jingrui Pan, Xiaoni Zhang, Xiaoming Rong, Lei He, Ying Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chronic alcohol consumption can lead to disruption of the blood-brain barrier. The gut-brain axis may be involved in this pathological process. We investigated the gut microbiota of 30 healthy individuals and 30 alcohol use disorder (AUD) patients and found that at the genus level, AUD patients had decreased Faecalibacterium and increased Streptococcus. Liquid chromatography mass spectrometer (LC-MS/MS) revealed that 604 metabolites were upregulated and 606 were downregulated in AUD patients with cognitive impairment, compared to healthy controls. Chronic alcohol consumption led to cognitive decline in mice, with increased 20 kDa FITC-dextran leakage in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and hippocampus, and decreased expression of ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-5. Transplantation of feces from AUD patients into germ-free mice resulted in increased 20 kDa FITC-dextran leakage in PFC and hippocampus, and decreased expression of ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-5, compared to mice receiving feces from healthy individuals. Administration of Faecalibacterium prausnitzii to chronically alcohol-fed mice improved cognitive function, reduced 20 kDa FITC-dextran leakage in PFC and hippocampus, and increased the expression of ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-5. Chronic alcohol consumption can disrupt the blood-brain barrier through the gut-brain axis. Faecalibacterium prausnitzii can improve alcohol-induced blood-brain barrier disruption and cognitive impairment.

Introduction

Chronic alcohol consumption is now considered to be a major risk factor for the global disease burden. The number of drinkers and the amount of alcohol consumption have been continuously increasing globally, and the trend is expected to continue1. Furthermore, the population of drinkers is becoming younger2.

On the one hand, previous studies think ethanol can cause damage to the central nervous system, because it can directly cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB)3. On the other hand, chronic alcohol consumption can lead to BBB disruption. Autopsy of chronic alcohol consumption patients has revealed pathological changes, such as perivascular microhemorrhage and thickened vessel walls4. Individuals with alcohol use disorder were found decline of the basal lamina protein, collagen-IV, and claudin-5 in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex5. Chronic alcohol exposure in the substantia nigra can also lead to changes in the vascular basement membrane6. However, there is some controversy over whether chronic alcohol consumption causes BBB disruption. One study using Evans blue leakage, gadolinium-positive signal leakage, and fibrinogen deposition has not detected impairment of BBB integrity in animal models of chronic alcohol consumption7. Furthermore, clinical studies have found that abnormal intracranial diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) signals caused by chronic alcohol consumption can still be observed and continue to increase even one year after abstinence8. While DTI is often used to detect white matter fibers, it has previously been reported to have some correlation with BBB opening9. Therefore, we believe that the integrity of the BBB is not only the beginning of alcohol-induced central nervous system damage, but also a key factor affecting the prognosis of the nervous system after abstinence.

The maintenance of BBB integrity is crucial for the homeostasis of the central nervous system, and disruption of the BBB is closely related to many neurological diseases10. In recent years, in the field of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) research, some scholars have proposed the “vascular hypothesis”, believing that the integrity of the BBB is an early predictor of cognitive impairment independent of Aβ and pTau11,12. Hence, there has been a growing focus on investigating BBB disruption in the context of chronic alcohol-induced neurological damage. This disruption is recognized as a key factor contributing to the cognitive dysfunction and neurological deficits observed in high-risk populations affected by alcohol abuse13. Previous studies have reported that reactive oxygen species (ROS)14, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)15, and neuroinflammation16 may play critical roles in the mechanisms of alcohol-induced BBB disruption, but they have not yet been applied in clinical treatment.

The concept of the gut-brain axis has also been widely recognized17,18. Previous studies have reported that chronic alcohol consumption can lead to changes in the gut microbiome19. However, the gut microbiome is greatly influenced by many factors, such as ethnicity, dietary habits, age, amount of alcohol consumption, and type of alcohol consumed. Therefore, the impact of chronic alcohol consumption on the gut microbiome still needs to be further verified in different populations. In addition, the gut microbiome can influence the integrity of the BBB by regulating related metabolites, neural pathways, and endocrine pathways20,21. Currently, there are no animal experiments, such as fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), that demonstrate the role of the gut–brain axis in BBB disruption caused by chronic alcohol consumption.

Therefore, we hypothesize that chronic alcohol consumption may affect the integrity of the BBB through the gut-brain axis.

Results

Alterations in intestinal microbiota and plasma metabolites of AUD patients

The study included 30 healthy controls and 30 AUD patients. All participants were male, and the clinical information for both groups is shown in Table 1. Within 30 AUD patients, the median of alcohol drink history was 10.50 years, and the mean of alcohol consumption was 176.30 gram/day. There were no significant differences in age between the two groups (40.00 (28–57) vs. 47.00 (31–52), P = 0.200). To further identify the specific differences in microbiota between the two groups, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) revealed that at the family level, compared to the healthy controls, the abundance of Ruminococcaceae in the AUD patients decreased, while Streptococcaceae and Enterobacteriaceae increased. At the genus level, compared to the healthy controls, the abundance of Faecalibacterium decreased, while Streptococcus increased in the AUD patients (Fig. 1D, E). Additionally, the bar charts in Fig. 1F–H illustrate the significantly reduced abundance of certain microbiota in the AUD patients compared to the healthy controls.

LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on plasma metabolites from both groups. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed differences in the composition of metabolites between the two groups (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, to screen for differential metabolites between the two groups, we established an orthogonal projections to latent structures-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) model (Fig. 2B), which was validated to be reliable (R2Y = 0.987, Q2Y = 0.843). Based on the OPLS-DA results, using the variable importance in projection (VIP) from the obtained multivariate analysis OPLS-DA model, we preliminarily selected metabolites that differed between the two groups. Relative to healthy controls, AUD patients showed upregulation of 604 metabolites and downregulation of 606 metabolites, which were visualized in a volcano plot (Fig. 2C). And it was showed in the volcano plot, N (4)-Oleylcytosine arabinoside, DG (14:0/15:0/0:0), Mosinone A, benzoyl-l-arg and Lividomycin B were the five lowest P value differential metabolites. And dTDP-4-oxo-2-deoxy-beta-L-xylose, Ancymidol, ( + )-(R)-2-(2,4-Difluorophenyl)-1-(3-((E)-4-(2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropoxy)styryl)-1,2,4-triazol-1-yl)-3-(1,2,4-triazol-1-yl)propan-2-ol) and dTDP-4-dimethylamino-4,6-dideoxy-5-C-methyl-D-allose were markedly increased in AUD patients. The top 10 upregulated and downregulated differential metabolites were presented in a bar chart (Fig. 2D). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of differential metabolites between the two groups revealed that these metabolites were mainly enriched in pathways related to lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and bile acid secretion (Fig. 2E).

Furthermore, a correlation analysis was conducted between the top ten upregulated or downregulated differential metabolites from the two groups and the main differential microbial communities identified by LEfSe (linear discriminant analysis value (LDA) > 3.5, at the genus level), as shown in Fig. 2F. From the correlation heatmap, it can be seen that besides Faecalibacterium, which shows a significant correlation with multiple major differential metabolites, UBA1819, Streptococcus, Ligilactobacillus, Enterobacter, Desulfovibrio, Collinsella, and Colidextribacter also have significant correlations with several major differential metabolites. In contrast, unclassified_Clostridia_UCG-014, Ruminococcus_gnavus_group, Parabacteroides, Lactobacillus, and Klebsiella are only significantly correlated with a smaller number of major differential metabolites.

Chronic alcohol consumption induces cognitive impairment and BBB disruption in mice

Following the mice experimental design outlined in Fig. 3A, behavioral experiments were conducted in the 7th week after 6 weeks of continuous alcohol consumption. In the Morris water maze (MWM) test, during the initial 5 days of the spatial acquisition phase, the latency gradually decreased for both groups of mice. However, compared to the control group, the EtOH group exhibited a smaller reduction in latency, with this difference reaching statistical significance on the fifth day (P = 0.006, Fig. 3B). During the sixth day’s probe trial phase, mice in the EtOH group showed longer latencies (24.10 ± 12.02 s vs. 11.20 ± 7.53 s, P = 0.004) and fewer platform crossings (1.40 ± 0.70 vs. 3.90 ± 1.52, P < 0.01) compared to the control group (Fig. 3C–E). In the novel object recognition (NOR) test, the EtOH group spent proportionally less time exploring the novel object compared to the control group (55.43 ± 8.40 vs. 71.90 ± 4.46, P < 0.01, Fig. 3F). Observation of mouse brain slices pre-treated with 20 kDa FITC-dextran under a fluorescent microscope revealed a noticeable increase in FITC-dextran leakage in the EtOH group compared to the control group. This phenomenon was observed in the prefrontal cortex and the hippocampus (Fig. 3G). Furthermore, we examined the expression of endothelial cell tight junction proteins closely associated with the BBB. Western blot analysis revealed a significant decrease in the expression of ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-5 in the prefrontal cortex of the EtOH group relative to the control group (ZO-1 P < 0.05, occludin P < 0.01, claudin-5 P = 0.302). Similarly, in the hippocampus, the EtOH group exhibited decreased expression of ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-5 compared to the control group (ZO-1 P < 0.05, occludin P = 0.281, claudin-5 P < 0.01) (Fig. 3H). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining revealed a significant downregulation of ZO-1 and occludin expression in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampal regions of the EtOH group compared to the control group (Fig. 3I). These findings indicate that chronic alcohol consumption leads to BBB disruption in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus of mice.

Faecalibacterium prausnitzii ameliorates chronic alcohol consumption induced cognitive impairment and BBB disruption

So, we considered the potential protective effect of Faecalibacterium against chronic alcohol consumption induced cognitive impairment and BBB disruption. In the MWM test, during the initial 5 days of the spatial acquisition phase, the latency gradually decreased in both groups of mice. However, compared to the EtOH+NS group, the mice in the EtOH+Faecali group exhibited a greater reduction in latency, although this difference did not reach statistical significance during the first five days (Fig. 5B). This phenomenon was observed in the prefrontal cortex and the hippocampus (Fig. 5G). These findings indicate that Faecalibacterium prausnitzii improves BBB integrity in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus of mice affected by chronic alcohol consumption.

Faecalibacterium prausnitzii can regulate the gut microbiota and the short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) in chronic alcohol consumption mice

Following a 2-week administration of Faecalibacterium prausnitzii or saline by gavage, fecal samples from the two groups of mice were subjected to 16S rDNA gene sequence analysis and SCFA analysis using Gas Chromatography Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS/MS).

Discussion

The altered gut microbiota and plasma metabolites in AUD patients have been observed. Through animal experiments, it has been confirmed that chronic alcohol consumption can lead to cognitive impairment and BBB disruption in mice. Additionally, through FMT experiments of germ-free mouse, it has been discovered for the first time that alcohol, by altering the gut microbiota, can lead to BBB disruption. Faecalibacterium prausnitzii has been shown to ameliorate cognitive impairment and BBB disruption caused by chronic alcohol consumption.

Comparing clinical Information between individuals with AUD and healthy populations, we have observed that AUD patients exhibit certain cognitive impairments, along with manifestations of anxiety, depression, and sleep disturbances, consistent with previous studies22–25. Blood tests have revealed anemia and decreased platelet count in AUD patients, with the latter being common among male drinkers26. In terms of biochemical markers, we have found elevated levels of AST, GGT, and DBIL in AUD patients, indicating a certain impact of chronic alcohol consumption on liver and bile excretion. Additionally, we have also observed higher blood glucose levels in AUD patients, suggesting a potential association with alcohol’s regulation of glucose metabolism26,27.

Through gut microbiota analysis of individuals with AUD and healthy populations, we have discovered that alcohol does not alter the abundance and diversity of gut microbiota, but it does change the composition of the gut microbiota. In order to screen for potential microbiota that can ameliorate alcohol-induced BBB disruption and cognitive impairment, we analyzed the differential microbiota between the two groups. We found that the microbiota increased by alcohol, including Streptococcaceae (family level), Enterobacteriaceae (family level), and Streptococcus (genus level), are mainly associated with intestinal infections and inflammation. The decreased microbiota primarily includes Ruminococcaceae (family level), Faecalibacterium (genus and species level), and Megasphaera (species level). Although Ruminococcaceae represents the most significantly different microbiota, previous reports have indicated that its metabolite isoamylamine (IAA) can pass through the BBB, leading to cognitive impairment28. Faecalibacterium is one of the extensively studied microbiota, primarily regulating the metabolism of various inflammatory pathway metabolites such as butyrate, salicylic acid, and shikimic acid, which may consequently have a regulatory effect on the BBB29. Although Megamonas was detected at the species level with differences, it could not be specifically classified, and no differences were detected at the genus level. Additionally, through Inter-group abundance bar chart (Fig. 1H), it is evident that although there are differences in Megamonas between the two groups, its abundance is extremely low in most individuals. Therefore, we speculate that Faecalibacterium may have a protective effect against alcohol-induced BBB disruption and cognitive impairment. However, 16S-rDNA sequence analysis of feces from germ-free mice did not always yield consistent results. Previous studies analyzing germ-free mice that received fecal microbiota transplants found, when compared to their hosts, only 47% of the human gut microbes could be re-established in mice at the species level, among which more than 1/3 underwent significant changes. This difference dues to the diet, genetic, behavioral, physiological, and anatomical differences between the guts of mice and humans30. Therefore, further experimental validation is needed to determine whether Faecalibacterium can ameliorate BBB disruption induced by chronic alcohol consumption. Besides, before exploring this issue further, we need to first verify whether alcohol leads to BBB disruption and cognitive impairment, and whether there is a causal relationship between gut microbiota and BBB disruption.

The current debate over alcohol-induced BBB disruption may be related to alcohol consumption levels and the methods used to detect BBB damage. Previous studies have reported that chronic alcohol consumption did not show significant Evans blue extravasation in brain tissue, but did show leakage of 20 kDa FITC-dextran in different parts of the brain. Considering that common inflammatory cytokines have molecular weights around 14–20 kDa, it is suggested that alcohol-induced BBB disruption, although mild, may lead to secondary central nervous system inflammatory damage31. Therefore, our study used 20 kDa FITC-dextran to detect the presence of BBB disruption. Additionally, our study also examined the endothelial cell tight junction proteins closely related to the BBB: ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-5, where ZO-1 is a cytoplasmic protein and occludin and claudin-5 are transmembrane proteins10. Through animal experiments, we confirmed that chronic alcohol consumption can lead to BBB disruption in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus of mice.

Currently, there is no study confirming that alcohol can disrupt the BBB by altering the gut microbiota. Previous studies have indicated that gut microbiota may affect the BBB through neural pathways, immune pathways, neuroendocrine pathways, metabolic pathways, and inflammatory pathways20,21. While previous studies have shown that chronic alcohol consumption can lead to alterations in gut microbiota and disruption of the BBB32, none have been able to directly demonstrate the correlation between the gut microbiota and BBB. Therefore, we investigated whether alcohol could regulate the BBB through the gut microbiota by transplanting the fecal microbiota of AUD and healthy individuals into germ-free mice, which is an excellent model for understanding the correlation to which the intestinal microbiota impacts on disease33. It is worth noting that the gut microbiota itself plays a role in the formation and regulation of the BBB, and compared to normal mice, germ-free mice lacking normal gut microbiota have increased BBB permeability34. Through FMT in germ-free mice, we found that compared to germ-free mice receiving FMT from healthy individuals, those receiving FMT from individuals with AUD patients showed more FITC-dextran leakage in the prefrontal cortex and the hippocampus, along with reduced expression of endothelial cell tight junction proteins. To the best of our knowledge, this study provides the first direct evidence that chronic alcohol consumption affects the integrity of the BBB through the gut microbiota.

Faecalibacterium is a genus of gram-positive, obligate anaerobic bacteria that has been reported to be associated with various diseases, particularly in the nervous system, and has been linked to psychiatric disorders, Parkinson’s disease (PD), AD, autism, and epilepsy. Among the species within this genus, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii is the earliest discovered and most extensively studied, earning the title of “next-generation probiotic”. Faecalibacterium prausnitzii is involved in the regulation of butyrate salt, MAM, NF-kB, Dact3, DP8 Tregs, and IL-1029. Previous study has also shown a significant decrease in SCFAs, particularly butyric acid levels, in the feces of chronic alcoholics35. Through animal experiments, we have found that Faecalibacterium prausnitzii can significantly improve the cognitive impairment and BBB disruption caused by chronic alcohol consumption, indicating its potential therapeutic value in alcohol-induced brain diseases. Additionally, through targeted metabolomic analysis of the feces, we discovered that Faecalibacterium prausnitzii can increase the levels of three SCFAs—butyric acid, valeric acid, and caproic acid. SCFAs have previously been shown to have a protective effect on both the intestinal barrier and the BBB36, and butyric acid has been found to improve alcohol-induced cognitive impairment37. Germ-free mice, lacking gut microbiota, exhibit higher BBB permeability compared to conventional mice. However, transplanting fecal matter from conventional mice or introducing SCFA-producing bacterial strains (Clostridium tyrobutyricum and Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron) for two consecutive weeks, or administering sodium butyrate for three consecutive days, can reduce BBB permeability in germ-free mice34. Previous studies have shown that SCFAs can restore tight junctions between brain endothelial cells through G protein-coupled receptor-mediated effects, inhibition of histone deacetylases, and regulation of NF-κB and other various signaling pathways. Furthermore, SCFAs indirectly modulate BBB integrity by regulating systemic inflammation, microglia, and astrocyte activity38. Therefore, we believe that Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, by regulating the levels of gut SCFAs, can repair the BBB disruption caused by chronic alcohol consumption, thus improving the cognitive impairment associated with chronic alcohol consumption. However, its sensitivity to oxygen and bile acids limits its clinical application. In recent years, research has shown that co-culturing Faecalibacterium prausnitzii with Desulfovibrio piger can significantly increase the activity of Faecalibacterium prausnitzii39.

The limitations of this study are as follows: (1) Due to the majority of AUD patients in our country being male2, the study included only male AUD patients. Previous studies have reported that chronic alcohol consumption does not significantly disrupt the BBB in female mice, and it is suggested that estrogen may have a protective effect on the BBB40, Therefore, further investigation is needed to explore whether alcohol affects the BBB and cognitive impairment in females through alterations in the gut microbiota. (2) Because germ-free mice have poor adaptability to a normal environment and are prone to changes in gut microbiota, this study did not conduct behavioral experiments on germ-free mice after FMT, thus failing to confirm whether alcohol-induced changes in the gut microbiota lead to cognitive impairment. (3) Although we compared the differences in plasma metabolites between AUD patients and healthy individuals, the relevance of these differing metabolites to the BBB and cognitive impairment has not been previously reported, necessitating further exploration. (4) Due to the fact that blood samples from AUD patients were collected more than 24 h after their last drinking session, the study did not measure the blood ethanol concentration (BEC) for comparison with the animal model. This issue may require further experimental investigation. (5) Due to our prior consideration that the composition of gut microbiota in mouse feces differs significantly from that of humans, the study did not detect the fecal gut microbiota of mice in the EtOH group and the control group, nor did it compare the former two with the feces of AUD patients and healthy individuals.

In summary, chronic alcohol consumption leads to changes in gut microbiota and plasma metabolites. It results in the disruption of the BBB and cognitive impairment in mice. The gut microbiota is involved in the alcohol-induced disruption of the BBB. Faecalibacterium prausnitzii can ameliorate alcohol-induced disruption of the BBB and improve cognitive impairment caused by chronic alcohol consumption.

Methods

Participants

A total of thirty patients diagnosed with AUD were recruited as participants. Patients were diagnosed with AUD by medical doctors at the Baiyun Psychological Hospital according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5). We recruited 30 healthy members at the Jianghai Street community health service center as healthy controls. Participants were excluded if they had other neuropsychiatric diseases, infectious diseases, neoplastic disease, autoimmune disorders, digestive diseases, or administered with antibiotics, probiotics, or prebiotics within 4 weeks. It was reported that the gut microbiota can be changed after 4 weeks of abstinent41. So, the AUD patients had more than 4 weeks of abstinent were also excluded. All healthy members and AUD patients have completed the questionnaire assessment, and blood and stool samples have been collected.

Informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study complied with all relevant ethical regulations and was approved by the ethics committee of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University with the ethical number SYSEC-KY-KS-2019007. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Animals

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) male C57BL/6 J mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were obtained from Yancheng Bioscience in Guangzhou, China. The mice were allocated to various experimental groups through random assignment. Germ-free (GF) male C57BL/6 J mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were bred and housed at Shenzhen Gnotobio Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

All experimental procedures of SPF mice were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee at Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat-Sen University, under the ethical number AP20220218. All experimental procedures involving GF mice were approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee at Shenzhen Gnotobio Biotechnology Co., Ltd, under the ethical number JTAW20230223-1. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Alcohol administration

In order to simulate a model of chronic alcohol intake, the mice received either water (control) or alcohol (25% ethanol, 4 g/kg)42–44, administered in equivalent volumes via oral gavage on a daily basis for 6 consecutive weeks. Each mouse received an average of 0.12 g/day of ethanol. Following the 6-week alcohol exposure protocol, the mice were subjected to behavioral tests or sacrificed 24 h after the final gavage.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)

For FMT experiments, pooled fecal pellets were homogenized in anaerobic saline solution and filtered with filter bag three times. Then we collected the supernatant stool homogenates of the mixed liquor suspended solid after centrifugation twice. GF mice were subjected to gavage with 200 μL stool homogenates twice in a week. 1 week after FMT, stool samples were collected for 16S rDNA gene sequence analysis. Then the mice were kept bred and housed for the next 5 weeks.

Bacterial strains and culture conditions, and bacterial colonization

Commercial strains of Faecalibacterium prausnitzii A2-165 (DSMZ 17677) was purchased from Mingzhou Bioscience, Ningbo, China. Faecalibacterium prausnitzii was cultured under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 24 h in modified reinforced clostridial (MRC) broth within a sterile test tube. After centrifugation at 4 °C for 10 min at 8000 rpm, the bacteria were washed with phosphate-buffered saline and then resuspended in sterile saline solution containing 0.5% L-cysteine. The bacteria were administered orally (1 × 109 CFU) in 200 μL sterile saline for 14 consecutive days14.

16S rDNA gene sequence analysis

Fecal samples were used to extract bacterial DNA employing the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit. PCR amplification targeted the variable region 3–4 (V3–V4) of the 16S rRNA gene using barcoded specific bacterial primers. Any potential chimeras were then excluded, and the resulting sequences were clustered into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). ASVs were obtained by denoising the sequences using the dada2 method in the QIIME2 software. Taxonomy was assigned to all ASVs through a search against the Silva databases using QIIME software.

Metabolomics analysis

Untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed using a liquid chromatography mass spectrometer (LC-MS/MS) system. The mass spectrometer was operated in both positive and negative ionization modes for comprehensive analysis. Following normalization of the original peak area data with the total peak area, subsequent analysis was performed.

The resulting supernatant was then transferred to a vial for subsequent analysis using gas chromatography mass spectrometry (GC-MS/MS). The GC analysis was performed using a Trace 1300 gas chromatograph (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with a capillary column Agilent HP-INNOWAX and helium as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL/min.

Animal behavior tests

The MWM setup consisted of a circular pool filled with water. A circular platform, submerged 1 cm below the water surface, was positioned in the southeast as previously described45. The MWM procedure involved two phases: (1) Spatial acquisition phase; (2) Probe trial phase. The relevant data were captured using a tracking system (DigBehv Systems, Jilang Software Technology Co., LTD, Shanghai, China) (n = 10 per group).

The Novel Object Recognition (NOR) task spanned 3 consecutive days following a protocol outlined in previous research46, with 24 h intervals between trials. The task comprised the following phases: (1) Habituation phase; (2) Training phase; (3) Test phase. The results are reported using the recognition index (RI). All activities were monitored and recorded using a tracking system (DigBehv Systems, Jilang Software Technology Co., LTD, Shanghai, China) (n = 10 per group). The ratio was calculated as the time explored the novel object / total object.

FITC-dextran permeability assay

BBB permeability assay were measured as previously described47 with modifications. In brief, 20 kDa FITC-dextran (F6057, Sigma, Germany) was diluted in PBS, and administered via angular vein (i.v.) at 75 mg/kg body weight mice, 30 min before sacrificed. Then mice were sacrificed by transcardial perfusion with saline and 4% PFA under pentobarbital anesthesia. The brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA for 8 h and dehydration in gradient sucrose. After that, the brains were embedded in OTC, and 10 μm sagittal slices were obtained with a cryotome (Leica CM1950). Fluorescent signals were examined using fluorescence microscope (Zesis Imager D2). (n = 5–6 per group).

Western blotting

Proteins extracted from the prefrontal cortex or hippocampus were lysed using extraction buffer (Beyotime, China). To identify and quantify the proteins, 50 μg of total proteins were separated using SDS-polyacrylamide gels, then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Merck Millipore, USA), and blocked with 5% non-fat milk. The membranes were then exposed to primary antibodies against ZO-1 (13663S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), occludin (331500, Invitrogen, USA), claudin-5 (352500, Invitrogen, USA), and GAPDH (60004-1, Proteintech, China), followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. The signal was detected using touch chemiluminescence imaging technology (e-Blot) and analyzed with Image J. (n = 5-6 per group).

Immunofluorescence staining

The tissue preparation was same as the part of FITC-dextran permeability assay. Double immunofluorescence was performed in order to evaluate the expression of TJs. Sections were first incubated with a rabbit anti-ZO-1 antibody (13663S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), and combined with a mouse anti-occludin antibody (331500, Invitrogen, USA) at 4 °C overnight. And then incubated with species-specific Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody and Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody for 1 h. Afterward, the sections were prepared and subjected to analysis using a fluorescence microscope. (Zesis Imager D2). (n = 5-6 per group).

Statistics and Reproducibility

We included 30 healthy individuals and 30 AUD patients for statistical analysis. For the animal experiments, the number of replicates for germ-free mice was 5, and for SPF mice was 6. All data were analyzed with RStudio (R version 4.4.1). Student’s two-tailed t test or Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between two groups when the data were normally distributed or abnormally distributed, respectively. Data were expressed as the mean ± SEM. A two-tailed P-value of 0.05 or less was taken to infer statistical significance.
