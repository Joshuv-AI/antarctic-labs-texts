Womens experiences of disrespectful maternity care and its consequences during childbirth in Spain
Authors: Julián Rodríguez-Almagro, Antonio Hernández-Martínez, Inmaculada Ortiz Esquinas, Ana Rubio Alvárez, Ana Ballesta Castillejos, Miriam Donate Manzanares
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDisrespectful care during childbirth may adversely affect women’s well-being and their relationship with health services. This explanatory sequential mixed-methods study explored women’s perceptions and reported consequences of disrespectful treatment during childbirth in Spain. In the quantitative phase, 1,579 women completed the Childbirth Abuse and Respect Evaluation Maternal Questionnaire (CARE-MQ), a validated instrument that assesses self-reported experiences of abuse and/or disrespectful treatment during childbirth. In the qualitative phase, 20 women who reported such experiences participated in semi-structured interviews. Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive and bivariate analyses, and qualitative data were analysed thematically. The mean CARE-MQ score was 6.84 (SD 9.61). Although most women scored 0 on most items, relevant proportions reported negative experiences, particularly lack of support with infant care or breastfeeding (20.4%) and lack of respect for the birth plan when possible or lack of explanation of alternatives (13.8%). Higher CARE-MQ scores were observed in association with induction of labour, neuraxial analgesia, emergency caesarean section, severe perineal trauma, absence of skin-to-skin contact, and longer hospital stay. Qualitative findings described non-consented procedures, poor communication, lack of empathy, feelings of infantilization and helplessness, and subsequent distrust toward healthcare services. These findings highlight the need to strengthen respectful maternity care, informed decision-making, and professional training in woman-centred childbirth care.Keywords: Obstetric violence, Disrespectful care, Maternal health, Humanized obstetric care, Emotional well-beingSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Obstetric violence has emerged as a global public health problem that violates the rights and dignity of women during one of the most significant moments of their lives1,2. This phenomenon, characterized by dehumanizing treatment, verbal and physical abuse, and lack of informed consent, is a type of gender-based violence that occurs in healthcare settings during childbirth and the postpartum period1,2. Obstetric violence varies considerably in its prevalence and forms, affecting both high-income and low- and middle-income countries. In Latin America, a recent study found that 44% of women in Argentina experienced it, while 33% in Mexico reported experiencing this type of abuse1,2. These rates suggest that abusive practices in birthing settings are persistent and strongly linked to sociocultural and institutional factors that perpetuate structural violence in obstetric care3.

The World Health Organization (WHO) does not explicitly use the term “obstetric violence” but recognizes the problem of mistreatment during childbirth. It identifies forms such as disrespect, verbal and physical abuse, discrimination, and procedures without consent, and promotes respectful maternity care focused on dignity, privacy, informed choice, and continuous support4,5.

Several authors have used the term obstetric violence to describe mistreatment, non-consented procedures, verbal abuse, and dehumanizing practices experienced during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period6,7.

Disrespectful childbirth care has been described as involving dehumanizing practices, lack of informed consent, and inadequate communication, often including non-consensual procedures and unnecessary interventions8,9. These experiences are frequently linked to a lack of empathy and a mechanistic approach to care in which women’s needs are not adequately considered1,10.

The consequences of these experiences can be long-lasting, affecting women’s emotional well-being, perception of motherhood, and future healthcare decisions11,12. Previous studies have reported increased levels of anxiety, post-traumatic stress symptoms, and mistrust toward healthcare services following such experiences10.

The impact of obstetric violence extends beyond immediate physical effects, affecting women’s long-term mental health. Women who experience such situations report anxiety, post-traumatic stress symptoms, and mistrust toward healthcare services, which may influence future care-seeking13. Qualitative studies have also documented psychological trauma associated with non-consensual interventions and invasive practices during childbirth11.

Previous research has also highlighted the importance of addressing these experiences through improved professional training, communication skills, and respect for women’s autonomy14. The World Health Organization identifies mistreatment during childbirth as a significant barrier to safe and respectful maternity care and calls for policies that promote dignity and woman-centered care15.

Obstetric violence has also been conceptualized as a manifestation of structural violence within healthcare systems, reflecting broader patterns of dehumanization and unequal power dynamics1,16 These factors contribute not only to negative childbirth experiences but also to long-term psychological and physical consequences16.

Previous studies have reported that non-consensual and dehumanizing childbirth experiences are associated with increased post-traumatic stress symptoms and may negatively influence women’s future interactions with healthcare services17. Such experiences have also been described as reinforcing perceptions of depersonalized care, where women feel treated as “cases” rather than individuals17.

However, existing research has largely examined either the prevalence of disrespectful maternity care or qualitative accounts of women’s experiences, with limited integration of both perspectives. This limits a comprehensive understanding of both the magnitude of the problem and its subjective impact. Therefore, there is a need for mixed-methods studies that combine quantitative and qualitative approaches to better understand this phenomenon.

This study aimed to explore and analyze the perceptions and consequences of disrespectful and abusive treatment during childbirth using an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design. The quantitative phase examined the frequency and characteristics of self-reported disrespectful care, while the qualitative phase explored women’s experiences and their perceived impact on physical and psychological well-being.

Methods

Design and participants

This study was designed as a mixed-method study using an explanatory sequential design, structured in three phases. This approach provides a comprehensive view of this phenomenon by combining quantitative and qualitative data, providing rich and contextually grounded results18,19. This approach seeks to offer a comprehensive perspective of the situation, generating knowledge that contributes to improving maternal health policies and promoting practices that respect the autonomy and dignity of women in childbirth18,20,21.

Initially, quantitative data obtained through questionnaires provided an overview of the prevalence and characteristics of experiences of dehumanizing treatment. The qualitative phase focused on in-depth interviews to explore women’s subjective experiences, examining how they perceive the abuse and how it affects their physical and psychological well-being9,22. Finally, the results were integrated in a third phase.

This approach allows for a holistic understanding of obstetric violence, considering both the quantifiable aspects of the phenomenon and personal narratives that reflect its emotional and social impact. Ultimately, this study aims to contribute to the design of women-centered health interventions and policies that promote more respectful and humane treatment in obstetric care, preventing future cases of violence and improving the quality of the birth experience for women in all contexts15,23.

Phase 1: quantitative data collection and analysis

In the quantitative phase, we aimed to describe the frequency and distribution of self-reported experiences of disrespectful or inadequate care during childbirth and to explore whether CARE-MQ scores varied according to selected sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. For this purpose, participants completed the Childbirth Abuse and Respect Evaluation Maternal Questionnaire (CARE-MQ), together with additional sociodemographic and obstetric questions. The CARE-MQ is a validated instrument developed to assess women’s perception of abuse and/or disrespectful treatment during childbirth. It includes 20 items scored from 0 to 3, with a theoretical total score ranging from 0 to 60; higher scores indicate a greater burden of negatively perceived experiences during childbirth care. In its validation study, the CARE-MQ showed adequate psychometric performance, including satisfactory content validity, a four-factor structure explaining 55.16% of the variance, good convergent validity, excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.903), and excellent temporal stability (ICC = 0.927). In the present study, the instrument was used to assess self-reported experiences of disrespectful or inadequate care during childbirth rather than as a direct measure of the prevalence of obstetric violence. This quantitative strand was descriptive and exploratory and was intended to provide an overall view of the phenomenon and to inform the subsequent qualitative phase12.

The items assessed aspects such as verbal abuse, physical coercion, lack of informed consent, and perceptions of treatment received by healthcare personnel. This approach allows for the collection of data that statistically represents the phenomenon, providing a solid foundation for the subsequent qualitative phase24.

The quantitative data were analyzed using statistical tools to identify patterns of abuse and relevant correlations between variables. They served as a guide for selecting participants for inclusion in the qualitative phase, allowing for purposive sampling of the most representative or complex cases25.

The sequential approach ensured that the qualitative research complemented and enriched the quantitative findings, facilitating a more holistic interpretation of the phenomenon under study26.

Participant selection

The sample was selected using convenience sampling and targeted women who had given birth in the previous two years. To disseminate the questionnaire, contact was made with associations related to pregnancy, childbirth, and postpartum, as well as with breastfeeding and parenting support groups throughout Spain. The questionnaire was also distributed through midwives’ associations so that it could reach women through their members. The recruitment period was established between September and December 2023. Eligible participants were women aged 18 years or older and able to complete the questionnaire in Spanish.

Data collection

The questionnaire included sociodemographic variables such as age, educational level, marital status, and medical history. In addition, the questionnaire was divided into sections covering specific aspects of the obstetric experience, including:

Birth context: type of birth, duration of the birth process, and procedures done.

Birth context: type of birth, duration of the birth process, and procedures done.

Treatment received: perception of respect and empathy received from healthcare staff.

Medical procedures: frequency and context of non-consented interventions, such as vaginal examinations or cesareans.

This framework allowed for a detailed understanding of the characteristics of disrespectful treatment in the obstetric setting, from factors related to the birth context to specific aspects of treatment by health professionals.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the sample characteristics and CARE-MQ responses. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means and standard deviations. CARE-MQ total scores were treated as a continuous variable in the bivariate analyses. The mean CARE-MQ score should be interpreted with caution due to the skewed distribution of responses, with a high proportion of zero scores across items. We then explored whether total CARE-MQ scores varied according to selected sociodemographic, obstetric, and neonatal variables using bivariate comparisons. These analyses were planned as descriptive and exploratory, with the aim of identifying patterns that could help contextualize women’s reported experiences and inform the subsequent qualitative strand, rather than to develop an adjusted explanatory model. Therefore, all reported associations are unadjusted and should be interpreted as exploratory associations rather than causal relationships.

The quantitative phase thus provides a solid foundation for the study, allowing for the identification of trends and magnitudes of the problem. These preliminary findings guided the qualitative phase, where the participants’ individual experiences were explored in greater depth, facilitating a comprehensive and detailed approach to the phenomenon of disrespectful treatment in obstetric care12.

Phase 2: qualitative data collection and analysis

In keeping with an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design, the qualitative phase was conducted after the quantitative phase to explore in greater depth the meanings and contexts underlying the patterns identified in the survey data. The qualitative phase focused on an in-depth exploration of participants’ experiences, perceptions, and interpretations of disrespectful treatment during childbirth.

Participants were purposively selected to reflect a range of childbirth experiences, including variation in type of birth, labour onset, perception of obstetric violence, and newborn admission27. Inclusion criteria included women aged 18 years or older with direct experience relevant to the study topic. Participants in the qualitative phase were recruited independently of the quantitative sample, which is consistent with an explanatory sequential design in which the qualitative strand aims to deepen and interpret quantitative findings without requiring identical samples.

Data collection was conducted through semi-structured individual interviews carried out by trained members of the research team using a predefined interview guide. Interview topics included women’s understanding of disrespectful or abusive maternity care, specific experiences during labour and birth, perceived emotional and physical consequences, communication with healthcare professionals, and suggestions for improving childbirth care. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and conducted until thematic saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new relevant themes emerged.

Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis following the approach described by Braun and Clarke28. This involved familiarization with the data, generation of initial codes, identification of themes, and refinement and definition of final themes. Transcripts were imported into NVivo software29 to facilitate data management and coding. An inductive approach was used, allowing themes to emerge from the data.

To enhance the credibility and rigor of the analysis, two researchers independently coded the transcripts. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus within the research team. Reflexivity was maintained throughout the process, with researchers critically reflecting on potential biases to ensure that the findings were grounded in participants’ experiences.

Phase 3: data integration

In an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design, integration of quantitative and qualitative data is essential to achieve a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon under study30. In this study, integration primarily occurred at the interpretation stage, following established methodological frameworks for explanatory sequential mixed-methods designs, where findings from both phases were brought together to provide a more nuanced understanding of women’s experiences of disrespectful care during childbirth and their consequences.

The quantitative phase provided an overview of the frequency and characteristics of self-reported experiences of disrespectful or dehumanizing care. These findings informed the purposive sampling of participants for the qualitative phase, allowing the inclusion of women with diverse experiences and contexts.

The qualitative phase generated in-depth narratives that helped contextualize and explain the patterns identified in the quantitative data. Integration was conducted at two complementary levels. At the results level, qualitative findings were used to illustrate and deepen the quantitative patterns, providing concrete examples of the types of experiences reported and their perceived impact. At the interpretation level, qualitative insights were used to refine and expand the understanding of the phenomenon, offering a more comprehensive interpretation of the quantitative results18,31.

To support this process, qualitative themes were systematically compared with quantitative trends to identify areas of convergence and divergence. For example, higher CARE-MQ scores were reflected in qualitative accounts describing non-consented procedures, poor communication, limited empathy, and long-term emotional consequences. This integrative approach allowed the development of a coherent narrative linking statistical patterns with lived experiences, thereby strengthening the explanatory power of the study31,32.

Integration was undertaken collaboratively by members of the research team with experience in both quantitative and qualitative methods. This process involved iterative discussion and comparison of findings from both strands, helping to enhance analytical rigor and reduce the influence of single-researcher interpretation.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Mancha-Centro Hospital (reference 197-C) and subsequently ratified by the Reina Sofía University Hospital of Córdoba (reference 5615) and the Integrated Care Management of Ciudad Real University Hospital (reference C-600). All participants received written information about the study objectives, procedures, and data use, and provided written informed consent prior to questionnaire completion or interview participation.

Results

A total of 1,579 women correctly completed the questionnaire. The mean age was 33.29 years (SD 3.96). Of these, the majority were primiparous (n = 1,240, 78.5%), had a normal vaginal birth (n = 899, 56.9%), had spontaneous onset of labor (n = 868, 55%), and used regional analgesia (n = 1,309, 82.9%). Regarding the newborns, a minority required admission to a neonatal unit at birth (n = 213, 13.5%), and the majority were exclusively breastfed (n = 1,113, 70.5%). The mean CARE-MQ score in the sample was 6.84 (SD 9.61). However, because responses were highly concentrated at 0 for most items, this mean should be interpreted cautiously. It does not reflect a typical experience across the entire sample, but rather summarizes the overall burden of negatively perceived experiences reported during childbirth care. Despite the predominance of 0 scores, every type of potentially disrespectful or inadequate care included in the questionnaire was reported by some women, and some participants indicated that these experiences had affected them to varying degrees. Relationship between perceptions of inappropriate treatment and the variables studied

Bivariate analyses found statistically significant associations between CARE-MQ scores and some of the variables included in the study. Scores were statistically higher in the following cases (p ≤ 0.05): live birth (6.85 vs. 3.8), and conversely, women who experienced intrauterine fetal death reported lower CARE-MQ scores, twin pregnancy (12.04 vs. 6.76), induced labour (7.85 vs. 6.01), birth plan not respected (20.24 vs. 3.4), episiotomy (8.69 vs. 6.33), severe tear (10.25 vs. 6.66), neonatal admission (9.98 vs. 6.35), maternal readmission (11.98 vs. 6.69), subsequent maternal surgery (12.1 vs. 6.52), emergency caesarean Sect. (11.99 vs. 4.35), neuraxial analgesia (7 vs. 5.59), hospital stay of 4 days or more (11.18 vs. 4.74), absence of skin-to-skin contact (14.65 vs. 4.27), and lack of exclusive breastfeeding at discharge (7.92 vs. 6.12). As these analyses were exploratory and unadjusted, these findings should be interpreted as associations only and not as causal relationships.

Obstetric Violence from the Women’s Perspective

Four overarching themes were identified through the analysis of the semi-structured interviews (n = 20), capturing how participants understood, experienced, and responded to disrespectful maternity care. Participants conceptualized obstetric violence as both physical and psychological abuse occurring during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period (Theme 1). They also described specific lived experiences illustrating these practices (Theme 2), reflected on their consequences across multiple domains of life (Theme 3), and proposed potential solutions to address the problem (Theme 4).

Themes, Categories, Relevant Meaning Units, and Quotes from the Qualitative Phase.

Theme 1 – definition of obstetric violence

Participants described obstetric violence as a set of practices characterized by a lack of respect for women’s autonomy and dignity within maternity care. Central to their definitions was the absence of informed consent, with women emphasizing that procedures were often carried out without their authorization or without adequate information (1a; 1b). As one participant stated, “Procedures without consent by health professionals toward women” (1a), highlighting a perceived violation of their autonomy.

Another key element was the lack of individualized care, with participants describing experiences in which their preferences and personal circumstances were not taken into account. This was frequently expressed through feelings of depersonalization, as illustrated by statements such as “Treating us like just another number” (2a) and “Not taking into account the woman’s wishes regarding her pregnancy and her birth” (2b).

Participants also identified the use of non-recommended or potentially harmful practices as a defining feature of obstetric violence. These included interventions perceived as unnecessary, inappropriate, or even dangerous for the mother or baby (3a; 3b; 3c), reinforcing the perception of care that is not aligned with current evidence-based standards.

Finally, women highlighted the manipulation of decision-making processes, describing situations in which information was withheld, distorted, or presented in a biased manner to influence their choices. As one participant stated, this could involve being “subjected to some maneuver, coerced, manipulated, forced […] without being informed or, even when informed, that the information is biased” (4a).

Taken together, these definitions reflect a shared understanding of obstetric violence as a multifaceted phenomenon involving violations of autonomy, lack of individualized care, inappropriate clinical practices, and imbalanced power dynamics between healthcare professionals and patients.

Theme 2 – experiences of obstetric violence

Participants described experiences of obstetric violence as occurring across two interrelated dimensions: inadequate clinical care, often involving unnecessary or non-recommended procedures, and problematic interpersonal interactions with healthcare professionals.

In relation to clinical care, women reported multiple interventions perceived as unnecessary, outdated, or not aligned with current evidence-based practices. These included enforced immobility during labour (5a; 5b), restrictions on food and fluid intake (5a), and the imposition of birth positions without allowing women to choose (5i). Participants also described more invasive practices, such as the Kristeller maneuver (5f), early umbilical cord clamping (5a), artificial rupture of membranes without explanation (5c; 5 g), episiotomies (5 g), and repeated vaginal examinations performed without consent (5d). In some cases, these experiences extended to highly distressing situations, including dehumanized cesarean sections, separation from the newborn, and being left unattended during critical moments (5 h; 5i). As one participant described, “They broke my waters without telling me and very roughly; I said they were hurting me and they kept going” (5c), illustrating both the physical and emotional impact of these practices.

Across these accounts, a consistent element was the absence of informed consent and explanation. Women frequently emphasized that these procedures were not only perceived as unnecessary but were also carried out without adequate communication or opportunity for decision-making (5c; 5 g).

Alongside these clinical practices, participants described experiences of inadequate interpersonal relationships with healthcare professionals. These were characterized by perceived manipulation, abuse of authority, and coercion in decision-making processes. Women reported feeling pressured or frightened into accepting interventions, as reflected in statements such as being “scared so I’d agree to the induction of my labor” (6b) or being warned about severe risks in a way that limited their ability to make autonomous decisions (6a).

A lack of clear, accessible, and sufficient information was a recurring theme, with participants describing situations in which they did not understand what was happening or why certain interventions were performed (6e; 6f; 6 g; 6i). The presence of multiple unidentified professionals in the room further contributed to feelings of discomfort and loss of control (6e; 6f). In addition, women highlighted a lack of empathy and sensitivity in communication, describing interactions that made them feel infantilized, dismissed, or disrespected (6d; 6 h).

Taken together, these findings suggest that experiences of obstetric violence are not limited to specific clinical practices but are deeply shaped by the quality of interpersonal interactions, communication, and respect for women’s autonomy during childbirth.

Theme 3 – consequences of the experience of obstetric violence

Participants described a wide range of consequences affecting multiple areas of their daily lives, highlighting the enduring impact of these experiences beyond the immediate childbirth event.

At an emotional level, women reported feelings of infantilization and objectification, often linked to not being listened to or treated as individuals with agency (7a; 7c). These experiences were frequently associated with negative emotions such as anger, sadness, and guilt, particularly in relation to the perception that a meaningful and anticipated life event had been negatively altered. As one participant expressed, “I felt like a rag doll […] they stole a moment you spend nine months dreaming about” (7a). For some, however, these experiences also led to a process of personal reflection and empowerment, with participants describing a greater sense of assertiveness in future healthcare encounters (7d).

Beyond the immediate emotional impact, participants described a negative predisposition toward future interactions with healthcare professionals. Experiences of obstetric violence were associated with a loss of trust in the healthcare system, leading some women to approach future care with fear or skepticism (8c; 8 d), and in some cases to actively avoid the facility where the experience occurred or to request transfer to another hospital (8a; 8b).

These experiences also had implications at the professional level. Some participants reported disruptions to their working lives, including withdrawing from employment due to emotional distress or separation-related anxiety following childbirth (9a). In addition, one participant who was herself a healthcare professional described feeling judged by colleagues when sharing her experience, highlighting the potential stigma associated with speaking about obstetric violence (9b).

The family environment was also affected. Participants described situations of misunderstanding or lack of validation from partners or relatives (10b), and in some cases reported that these experiences contributed to relationship breakdown (10a). Conversely, some women described strengthened relationships when their partners provided support and understanding (10c). In addition, for some participants, the experience influenced future reproductive decisions, including reluctance to have more children (10a).

Finally, participants reported consequences affecting different aspects of health and well-being. These included mental health difficulties such as anxiety and depressive symptoms (11a; 11b), persistent physical pain related to childbirth interventions (11c), early cessation of breastfeeding (11b), and changes in sexual well-being following the experience (11d).

Taken together, these findings indicate that the consequences of obstetric violence extend beyond the clinical setting, affecting emotional well-being, healthcare engagement, social relationships, and long-term health outcomes.

Theme 4 – solutions proposed by users

Participants proposed a range of strategies to address obstetric violence, reflecting both individual and systemic levels of change.

A first set of recommendations focused on the need to recognize and make visible the problem. Women emphasized that obstetric violence is often minimized or treated as isolated incidents, rather than acknowledged as a widespread and structural issue. As one participant noted, “if it isn’t acknowledged it doesn’t exist” (12b). For this reason, participants highlighted the importance of naming the phenomenon and raising awareness as a fundamental step toward change (12b; 12c).

A second key area concerned professional training and standardization of care. Participants stressed the need for continuous education, updating of knowledge, and implementation of evidence-based protocols (12a; 12b; 12c). They also pointed to the unacceptable variability in clinical practice across institutions and professionals, suggesting that the quality of care should not depend on chance or on the specific setting in which childbirth occurs (12b).

Respect for women’s autonomy emerged as another central element. Participants emphasized the importance of providing clear, accessible, and continuous information throughout care, enabling women to make informed decisions about their bodies and childbirth experience (13a; 13b; 13e). This was closely linked to the need to recognize women as capable, autonomous individuals, rather than passive recipients of care (13c; 13 d; 13f).

Participants also highlighted the importance of humanizing healthcare practices, particularly through empathy, individualized care, and respectful communication. They emphasized the need to eliminate abusive or authoritarian behaviors and to foster a more supportive and person-centered approach (14a; 14b; 14c; 14 d). One specific example frequently mentioned was the need to humanize cesarean sections, allowing the presence of a companion and immediate skin-to-skin contact with the newborn (14a).

Finally, participants identified the need for broader social and systemic change, including increased awareness among both healthcare professionals and the general population, improved knowledge of patients’ rights, and the development of policies and oversight mechanisms to ensure respectful care (15a; 15b; 15c; 15 d).

Taken together, these proposals highlight that addressing obstetric violence requires a multifaceted approach, combining professional training, organizational changes, and broader cultural and structural transformations.

In addition, the integration of quantitative and qualitative findings provides a more comprehensive understanding of women’s experiences of disrespectful maternity care. Quantitative results identified patterns of higher CARE-MQ scores in relation to specific clinical and obstetric factors, while qualitative findings illustrated how these experiences were perceived and interpreted by women, particularly in relation to non-consented procedures, poor communication, limited empathy, and loss of autonomy. This integrative perspective highlights how statistical patterns are reflected in lived experiences, reinforcing the relevance of both strands in understanding the phenomenon.

Discussion

The integration of quantitative and qualitative findings provides a comprehensive understanding of women’s experiences of disrespectful maternity care during childbirth. In particular, higher CARE-MQ scores were reflected in women’s narratives of non-consented procedures, poor communication, limited empathy, and long-term emotional consequences.

Our findings show that, although most women did not report negative experiences in response to most CARE-MQ items, a relevant proportion described situations of disrespectful or dehumanizing care with a meaningful emotional impact. Item-level results and qualitative narratives were broadly consistent in identifying lack of informed consent and communication-related aspects as central elements. The proportion of women reporting a negative impact varied across items, ranging from 1% for physical aggression to 20.4% for lack of support with infant care or feeding concerns, figures that were lower than those reported in some previous studies2–34.

Findings from the quantitative and qualitative phases were consistent. Participants in the qualitative strand demonstrated awareness of this type of inappropriate treatment and described it in ways that closely align with definitions provided by official bodies4,5,35 and previous research on obstetric violence6,7,34. Overall, these accounts characterized the phenomenon as inappropriate care marked by lack of informed consent, limited individualization of care, use of non-recommended practices, and biased information that may influence women’s decision-making.

In this study, the term “obstetric violence” is used in accordance with existing literature and public health discourse, whereas the quantitative component specifically assessed women’s self-reported experiences of disrespectful or inadequate maternity care through the CARE-MQ instrument, rather than establishing a direct prevalence of obstetric violence.

The quantitative findings were supported by the qualitative accounts, which provided detailed descriptions of the reported experiences. The CARE-MQ proved to be a useful instrument for capturing women’s self-reported experiences of disrespectful or inadequate care during childbirth, rather than as a direct measure of obstetric violence, as its domains were reflected in participants’ narratives. Taken together, these findings suggest that experiences of disrespectful care were primarily related to communication and information, protection of privacy, professional support, and the quality of the interpersonal relationship between healthcare professionals and patients.

Higher CARE-MQ scores were observed in association with childbirth-related complications and interventions, including induced labour, neuraxial analgesia, longer hospital stay, severe perineal injury, instrumental delivery, and emergency cesarean section, as well as postoperative complications such as surgical interventions or readmission. Similar patterns have been reported in previous studies, where increased intervention has been associated with lower maternal satisfaction36,37. These findings suggest that, in contexts of greater clinical complexity, particular attention should be paid to communication, informed consent, and supportive care to mitigate the potential negative impact on women’s experiences.

These quantitative associations were consistent with the qualitative narratives, in which women who experienced more interventionist or clinically complex childbirth situations frequently described feelings of loss of control, insufficient information, non-consented procedures, and limited emotional support. In particular, participants linked emergency interventions and prolonged hospital-related experiences with poorer communication, reduced participation in decision-making, and perceptions of depersonalized care.

Findings related to perinatal outcomes should be interpreted with caution. In this study, women who experienced intrauterine fetal death reported lower CARE-MQ scores. This may reflect recent efforts to improve professional training in perinatal bereavement and communication in these contexts38, which could contribute to more supportive care experiences. However, given the observational nature of the study, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Experiencing these situations was described by participants as being associated with a loss of trust in the healthcare system. These findings are consistent with previous research reporting similar consequences following experiences of disrespectful or abusive maternity care41,42. In our qualitative data, participants described a negative predisposition toward future encounters with healthcare professionals, which may lead to mistrust and, in some cases, delayed care-seeking, with potential implications for health outcomes41,42.

Participants also reported impacts on their social and family environments. Some described feeling misunderstood or unsupported by their partners or relatives, which affected personal relationships39–41. In contrast, one participant described a process of personal growth following the experience, suggesting that responses to these situations may vary across individuals.

At the health level, participants reported consequences affecting multiple dimensions of well-being. Psychological effects were the most frequently described, including anxiety and depressive symptoms, which are commonly reported in the literature35,41–43. Physical consequences were also identified, such as prolonged pain related to pelvic floor injuries or invasive procedures40. In addition, participants described early cessation of breastfeeding40 and changes in sexual well-being, findings that are consistent with previous studies13,17.

Notably, Morris et al.41 have described parallels between obstetric violence and sexual abuse, identifying similarities in both experiences and their consequences. These parallels are supported by our findings and reinforce the potential severity of the impact of such experiences.

Participants identified several key areas for intervention to address obstetric violence, including professional training and awareness, respect for patient autonomy, humanization of care, and broader social and systemic change.

Recognition and visibility of the problem were highlighted as essential first steps. As suggested in previous research, identifying and naming the phenomenon is necessary to enable the development of effective strategies to address it16. In this context, lack of awareness or denial among healthcare professionals may hinder the implementation of appropriate protocols and care practices.

Several interventions have been proposed to improve care and reduce experiences of obstetric violence. These include training healthcare professionals in communication skills and respect for patient autonomy44–49, improving infrastructure and organizational conditions47, and implementing humanization strategies50. Examples of such measures include facilitating visits to obstetric or delivery units51 and enhancing childbirth preparation programs51. In addition, increasing the presence of companions during childbirth and promoting privacy have been associated with improved care experiences44–49.

Notably, some interventions have been associated with substantial reductions in the perceived prevalence of obstetric violence. For example, Mihret et al.45 reported a decrease from 71.8% to 15.9% following the implementation of targeted improvements in care practices.

A key strength of this study is the explanatory sequential mixed-methods design, which enabled the integration of quantitative breadth with qualitative depth, providing a comprehensive understanding of women’s experiences of disrespectful care during childbirth and its perceived consequences. The use of the CARE-MQ, a validated instrument specifically designed to assess self-reported experiences of abuse and/or disrespectful treatment during childbirth, further strengthens the quantitative component.

However, several limitations should be considered. The study relied on retrospective self-reported data, which may be subject to recall bias, although childbirth experiences are often vividly remembered. In addition, the quantitative and qualitative strands did not include identical participants; while this is methodologically consistent with an explanatory sequential design, it may limit direct comparability between datasets. The quantitative analyses were exploratory and unadjusted, and therefore the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal. Finally, the use of non-probability sampling limits the generalizability of the findings, and the results may therefore not be representative of or generalizable to all childbirth experiences in Spain.

This study shows that, although not all women reported negative childbirth experiences, a substantial proportion described disrespectful or dehumanizing care with meaningful emotional, relational, and healthcare-related consequences. The quantitative findings identified higher CARE-MQ scores in association with specific clinical and obstetric conditions, while the qualitative findings provided insight into how these experiences were perceived, particularly in relation to lack of informed consent, poor communication, limited empathy, and loss of autonomy.

These findings highlight the need to further strengthen respectful maternity care by improving communication, supporting informed decision-making, and reinforcing training in evidence-based, woman-centred practices. Addressing these areas is essential to improving women’s childbirth experiences and fostering greater trust in maternity services.
