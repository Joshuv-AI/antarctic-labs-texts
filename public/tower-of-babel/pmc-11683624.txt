Peng–Robinson or Redlich–Kwong? Twu or Soave α-Function? Which Combination of Cubic Equation of State (CEoS) and α-Function Produces More Accurate and Consistent Results for Pure Components
Authors: Twaha Mohamed, Janusz Kozinski, Francisco Ramos-Pallares
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study tested the accuracy and thermodynamic consistency of four CEoS/α-function models. The objective was to find the most suitable CEoS/α-function combo for producing accurate and consistent physical and derivative properties for nonpolar, polar and hydrogen bonding components at subcritical conditions. The models tested were PR-Twu, PR-Soave, RK-Twu, and RK-Soave. The first term in the model’s name refers to the CEoS used: Peng–Robinson (PR) or Redlich–Kwong (RK). The second term indicates the α-function used, i.e., Twu’s or Soave’s. The models were tested on a data set containing saturation pressure, enthalpy of vaporization and saturated liquid heat capacity of 147 pure components classified as polar, nonpolar, and hydrogen bonding. The three Twu α-function parameters were fitted to data and constrained to produce thermodynamic consistent values across the phase diagram; and, the Soave α-function parameter was predicted from a well-known correlation. The thermodynamic consistency of the models was assessed by calculating the Waring number and the saturated liquid speed of sound of 147 and 79 pure components, respectively. The results showed that PR-Twu and RK-Twu produced more accurate pure component properties compared to those from PR-Soave and RK-Soave. However, there was not a significant difference between the performance of PR-Twu and RK-Twu for calculating pure component properties. The same result was obtained when comparing PR-Soave and RK-Soave. Interestingly, the consistency analysis showed that only PR-Twu and PR-Soave produced consistent Waring number trends for components with acentric factors below 0.7. It was also observed that the saturated liquid speed of sound calculated from all four models tested was not accurate as the models cannot produce precise −(∂P∂v)T and liquid volumes. Besides, using volume translation is detrimental to the accuracy of the calculated saturated liquid speed of sound. The most accurate and consistent model was PR-Twu; however, caution should be exercised when modeling the saturated liquid heat capacity of hydrogen bonding components.

Introduction

Cubic equations of state (CEoS) play a very important role in process design, simulation and optimization because of their simplicity, accuracy, and computational efficiency.1,2 A CEoS calculates the total pressure of a system from contributions arising from repulsive and attractive interactions between molecules. The term accounting for repulsive interactions (or repulsive term) is derived from the kinetic theory of hard spheres3−5 and consequently is theoretically sound. However, the term accounting for attractive interactions (attractive term) is empirical and needs to be adjusted to improve the performance of the CEoS in matching pure component saturation pressure. This adjustment is carried out by introducing a temperature-dependent, component-specific parameter known as the α parameter. The α parameter is calculated by fitting the CEoS to experimental pure component vapor pressure (Psat), enthalpy of vaporization (ΔHvap) and saturated liquid heat capacity (Cpsat).6−8 The empirical expression for the calculation of the α parameter proposed by Soave9 has been adopted as the basis for calculations using the Redlich and Kwong (RK)10 and the Peng and Robinson (PR)11 CEoS, because of its simplicity and predictive capability. However, reported results suggest that the Soave’s α-function does not produce accurate Psat results for polar components,12−14 and is inconsistent at high temperatures.2,15,16 Numerous α-functions have been proposed and extensive reviews have been reported elsewhere.2,14,15 Among those α-functions, the expression proposed by Twu13 has been used in engineering calculations because of its accuracy and consistency. A brief review of the Soave and Twu α-functions is presented here as they were used in this study.

The α-function proposed by Soave9 calculates α using the pure component’s acentric factor and reduced temperature (Tr) as inputs. This α-function is empirical and was obtained by plotting the square root of the fitted α parameters for 9 components (linear/branched alkanes, and aromatics) versus the square root of the reduced temperature. The resulting trends were linear and constrained to produce a value of α = 1 at Tr = 1 to ensure the CEoS fits the critical point as required. The slope of the lines, also known as the m parameter, was component-specific and a generalized correlation for its calculation was proposed. This generalized correlation is a quadratic polynomial of the acentric factor with coefficients fitted to match the saturation pressure at Tr = 0.7. The RK CEoS when combined with the Soave α-function is commonly referred to as the Soave–Redlich–Kwong (SRK) CEoS.9,10 Peng and Robinson11 used the same α-function proposed by Soave but the coefficients of the quadratic polynomial were fitted to match pure component saturation pressure from the normal boiling point up to the critical point. The motivation behind this fitting approach was to improve the performance of the PR CEoS at high temperatures. These original expressions for the calculation of the slope of the Soave’s α-function, or m parameter, have been modified through the years to improve their accuracy.

Graboski and Daubert17 updated the original Peng and Robinson expression for the calculation of m by fitting the vapor pressure data of 41 pure components including alkanes (C1–C20), olefins (C2–C10), cycloalkanes (C3–C10), aromatics (C6–C10), ammonia, carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, sulfur dioxide, nitrogen, and water. According to Graboski and Daubert, the PR CEoS with the updated expression for m produced more accurate vapor–liquid partitioning coefficients than those from the same CEoS with the original expression for m; however, the authors did not present a comparison of the deviations between the updated and original expression for m. Peng and Robinson18 updated their original expression for m by proposing a piecewise function for its calculation according to the acentric factor of the component. The updated piecewise function produced results with lower deviations for heavy hydrocarbons with an acentric factors higher than 0.491 compared to those from the original expression for m.19,20 Piña-Martinez et al.21 proposed an updated expression for the calculation of m for the RK and PR CEoS by fitting these equations to match pseudo experimental (rather than experimental) saturation pressure, enthalpy of vaporization and saturated liquid heat capacity of 1,721 pure compounds, including hydrocarbons, alkanes, aromatics, esters, ethers, nitrogen compounds, acids, alcohol, peroxides, polyfunctional, and silanes/siloxanes. Piña-Martinez et al. reports that using the updated expressions for m produced lower deviations than those obtained when the original expressions were used, especially for components with acentric factors higher than 0.7. Despite the simplicity of the Soave α-function, this function is not flexible enough to capture the properties of polar and hydrogen-bonding components2,14 and does not produce consistent results at extreme temperatures as reported by a handful of authors.2,15,16,22

Twu et al.13 developed a 3-parameter exponential α-function that produced more accurate results for polar components than the Soave α-function. The Twu α-function is temperature-dependent and uses three component-specific parameters as inputs. Twu et al.13 proposed an α-function that satisfied the following thermodynamic consistency constraints regarding the value of α: (1) that it is always positive; (2) that α equals unity at the critical point; and (3) that α approaches zero as the temperature approaches infinity. Twu et al. reported three component-specific parameters for nine polar (acetone, methanol, ethanol, water, ethylene glycol, diethylene glycol, and triethylene glycol) and twenty-two nonpolar (linear alkanes C1–C20, cyclohexane, and benzene) components. All the three component-specific parameters were calculated by fitting the RK CEoS with the Twu α-function to saturation pressure and saturated liquid heat capacity data. Only heat capacity data at low temperatures was used in the fitting. Twu et al. claimed that the proposed α-function fitted the saturation pressure of pure components from the triple point to the critical point within the error of the measurement.13 The main disadvantage of the Twu α-function is that there is not a predictive correlation for the three component-specific parameters. This limitation makes the equation less attractive for general use in engineering calculations. However, several studies have investigated the Twu α-function due to the function’s accuracy and flexibility.14,15,23,24

In addition to the higher accuracy and flexibility of the Twu α-function, compared to that of Soave’s, the main advantage of the Twu α-function is that it can be easily constrained to ensure thermodynamic consistency across the entire phase diagram. Le Guennec et al.15 reported a set of conditions that any α-function must satisfy to produce consistent thermodynamic results. These authors showed that the Twu α-function can be fully constrained to ensure thermodynamic consistency, but the Soave α-function cannot be constrained and therefore produces inconsistent results at high temperatures. Le Guennec et al.15 calculated the three component-specific parameters of the Twu α-function for 1197 components from different chemical families including alkanes, alkynes, aromatics, ethers, esters, acids, ketones, aldehydes, alcohols, sulfur and nitrogen-containing compounds. The three component-specific parameters were calculated by fitting the RK and the PR CEoS to pseudoexperimental vapor pressure, enthalpy of vaporization and/or saturated liquid heat capacity when available. Le Guennec et al. used a constrained optimization algorithm to ensure the fitted Twu α-function parameters satisfied the proposed thermodynamic consistency conditions. Bell et al.,6 using a similar constrained optimization algorithm, calculated consistent Twu α-function parameters for 2500 pure components from the same chemical families as those investigated by Le Guennec et al. The Twu α-function parameters reported by Bell et al. were obtained by fitting the PR CEoS to actual experimental pure component vapor pressure, enthalpy of vaporization and/or saturated liquid heat capacity rather than pseudoexperimental data as done by Le Guennec et al. Both, Le Guennec et al. and Bell et al., used a weighted objective function and set the weighting factors for saturation pressure and enthalpy of vaporization equal to one and adjusted the weighting factor for the saturated liquid heat capacity to produce fitted properties. When the weighting factor for the saturated heat capacity was set to one, the accuracy of the fitted vapor pressure and enthalpy of vaporization was typically low because CEoSs are not suited to accurately fit saturated liquid heat capacities as noted by Piña-Martinez et al.8

To date, one of the few available comparisons between the RK and the PR CEoS with α parameter calculated from the Soave and Twu α-functions has been published by Piña-Martinez et al.25 Piña-Martinez et al. reported deviations between fitted and pseudoexperimental pure component saturation pressure, enthalpy of vaporization and saturated liquid heat capacity data and concluded that coupling either the RK or PR CEoS with the Twu α-function produces lower deviations compared to those when coupled with the Soave α-function. This conclusion was drawn by comparing the deviations for 1800 pure components from assorted chemical families including alkanes, aromatics, alcohols, ketones, aldehydes, amines, amides, etc. However, lower deviations do not necessarily imply a model is capable of producing property trends consistent with those experimentally measured. Therefore, in this study, the performance of the RK or PR CEoS was not only assessed based on fitting capabilities and deviations but also by assessing the thermodynamic consistency of the calculated property trends as well as the consistency of derivative properties such as the saturated liquid speed of sound and the Waring number (Section 3.2). The purpose was to gain a better understanding of the quality, reliability and performance of CEoS.

The objectives of this study were to (1) fit the RK and PR CEoS with α parameters from the Twu α-function; (2) compare the deviations and trends produced by the RK and PR CEoS with α parameter from the Twu and Soave α-functions on pure component saturation pressure (Psat), enthalpy of vaporization (ΔHvap) and saturated liquid heat capacity (Cpsat); and, (3) assess the consistency of the results by calculating derivative properties such as the Waring number and the saturated liquid speed of sound. To do so, the three component-specific parameters of the Twu α-function were calculated by fitting the RK and PR CEoS to experimental saturation pressure, enthalpy of vaporization and saturated liquid heat capacity of 147 pure components (objective 1). Saturated liquid density was not used for fitting because CEoS are unable to accurately match this property unless a temperature dependent volume translation expression is used. These expressions are thermodynamically inconsistent as they produce incorrect isotherms as well as negative heat capacities as reported by Jaubert et al.7 Most of the experimental data were collected from the NIST database,26 with some components utilizing supplementary ΔHvap and Cpsat data from the databases compiled by Zábranský et al.;27 and Acree and Chickos.28 The Soave α-function was used as reported by Piña-Martinez et al.21 To help in the analysis of the results the pure components were classified into three groups: Group 1, nonpolar components (50 components); Group 2, polar components (61 components); and Group 3, hydrogen-bonding components (36 components). The saturation pressure, enthalpy of vaporization, and saturated liquid heat capacity were calculated using the RK and PR CEoS coupled with the corresponding α parameter from the Twu and Soave α-functions; the resulting deviations were tabulated; and the experimental and calculated trends were compared (objective 2). Finally, the consistency of the models was assessed by applying a qualitative test based on the Waring number and by examining the calculated and experimental saturated liquid speeds of sound (objective 3). The experimental saturated liquid speed of sound data used in this study were collected from the NIST Database.26

Data Sets

Three data sets were used in this study: one Development data set and two Test Data sets. The Development Data set was used to fit the three component-specific parameters of the Twu α-function (eq 7) for the PR-Twu and RK-Twu models. The Test Data sets were used to assess the performance of the models (PR-Twu, RK-Twu, PR-Soave, and RK-Soave) in predicting derivative thermodynamic properties such as the Waring number (Test Data set 1) and the saturated liquid speed of sound (Test Data set 2).

Development Data Set

The data in this data set was used for fitting the three component-specific parameters, α1, α2, and α3, in eq 7, for the PR-Twu and RK-Twu models. It contains the experimental saturation pressure (Psat), enthalpy of vaporization (ΔHvap) and saturated liquid heat capacity (Cpsat) of 16 n-alkanes, 7 branched alkanes, 12 cyclic alkanes, 15 aromatics, 9 heterocyclic sulfur-containing compounds, 14 thiols, 13 pyridines, 3 aldehydes, 16 ketones, 6 nitriles, 9 amines, 3 heterocyclic nitrogen-containing compounds, 12 alcohols, 10 carboxylic acids, water, and ammonia. Most of the experimental data were collected from the NIST database,26 with some components utilizing supplementary ΔHvap and Cpsat data from the databases compiled by Zábranský et al.;27 and, Acree and Chickos.28 The PR-Twu and RK-Twu models were fitted to the data using the optimization procedure described in Section 2.2. Pure component inputs properties required for the flash algorithm described in Section 2.1, such as Pc, Tc, and the component-specific coefficients needed for the calculation of Cpidealgas (eq 20), were collected from the Yaws’ Handbook of Thermodynamic and Physical Properties of Chemical Compounds.31 Values of the pure component’s acentric factor, ω, were also included in this data set as they were used as an input for the calculation of the parameter m (eqs 5 and 6) for PR-Soave and RK-Soave models. The acentric factors were also collected from the Yaws’ Handbook of Thermodynamic and Physical Properties of Chemical Compounds.31 All the data in the Development Data set were screened to detect and eliminate obvious outliers using a quantile regression algorithm implemented in MATLAB.32 Additionally, manual screening was performed to ensure all obvious outliers were eliminated. To aid in the analysis of the results, the components in this data set were classified into three groups based on the nature of their intermolecular interactions: (1) Group 1: nonpolar, (2) Group 2: polar, and (3) Group 3: hydrogen bonding components. Test Data Sets: Assessment of the Thermodynamic Consistency of the Models

The thermodynamic consistency of the PR-Twu, PR-Soave, RK-Twu, and RK-Soave models was assessed by the calculation of the Waring number and the saturated liquid speed of sound for the pure components in Test Data sets 1 and 2, respectively.

Test Data Set 1: The Waring Number

This data set includes the same 147 components included in the Development Data set. For each one of the components in this test data set, the Waring Number (W) was calculated at reduced temperatures from 0.3 to 1 according to the following expression:3730where the derivative on the right-hand side of eq 30 was calculated from the PR-Twu, PR-Soave, RK-Twu, and RK-Soave models. The calculated Waring number was then plotted against the reduced temperature and the resulting trend was used to assess the thermodynamic consistency of the models. A model produces a consistent Waring Number trend for a given component if it is convex, continuous, and shows a minimum at a temperature below the critical point.6,38−41 The temperature at which the minimum value of the Waring number is found is known as the Waring temperature (TW). The reciprocal of TW is the condition at which the inflection point in a log vapor pressure-inverse temperature curve is observed.42 Therefore, the analysis of the Waring number is an indirect approach for testing the thermodynamic consistency of CEoS-calculated vapor pressures. In addition, the values of TW reported by Velasco et al.40 for 105 pure components were also included in this test data set for comparison purposes. The results of the analysis of the Waring Number for the components in the Test Data set 1 is presented in Section 4.5.1.

Test Data Set 2: Saturated Liquid Speed of Sound

This data set contains the experimental saturated liquid speed of sound for 79 pure components classified as nonpolar (Group 1), polar (Group 2), and hydrogen bonding (Group 3). Results

The following nomenclature was used for the models tested in this study: PR-Twu, PR-Soave, RK-Twu, and RK-Soave. The first term in the model’s name refers to the CEoS used: Peng–Robinson (PR) or Redlich–Kwong (RK). The second term indicates the α-function used: Twu’s (eq 7) or Soave’s (eq 4). The three component-specific parameters in the Twu α-function were calculated by fitting PR-Twu and RK-Twu to experimental saturation pressure, enthalpy of vaporization and saturated liquid heat capacity as described in Section 2. All the fitted Twu α-function parameters are summarized in the Supporting Information. In the case of the PR-Soave and RK-Soave models, the component-specific parameter m, required as input for the Soave α-function (eq 4), was predicted from the expressions proposed by Piña-Martinez et al. (eqs 5 and 6) using the component’s acentric factor as input. The performance of the PR-Twu, PR-Soave, RK-Twu, and RK-Soave on calculating pure component properties is presented first and then a discussion on their consistency, assessed by the calculation of the Waring number and saturated liquid speed of sound, is presented. PR-Twu and RK-Twu fitted the Cpsat of the components in Group 1 with an overall AARD of 2.7 and 2.8%, respectively; and the PR-Soave and RK-Soave predicted the Cpsat with an overall AARD of 6.9 and 5.0%, respectively.

The results of this study indicate that the Twu α-function is more adequate than the Soave α-function to capture the effect of hydrogen bonding on Cpsat when hydrogen bonding is the dominant intermolecular interaction as in the case of ammonia, water, and low molecular weight alcohols such as methanol and ethanol (see Figure 5). This observation does not imply that the Twu α-function captures hydrogen bonding but rather produces a more appropriate α-parameter for low carbon number alcohols, water and ammonia. In the case of higher carbon number alcohols, the Twu and Soave α-functions do not produce accurate results because, as suggested by Cerdeiriña et al.,34 Bureš et al.,52 and Zábranský et al.,36 these alcohols do not only interact through hydrogen bonding but also through stronger dispersion forces resulting from the longer paraffinic chains. This combination of hydrogen bonding and stronger dispersion forces might produce clusters with the paraffinic chains oriented toward the center and hydroxyl groups at the outer surface of the cluster interacting through hydrogen bonding with other hydroxyl groups in other clusters. This nonrandom arrangement renders the repulsive-attraction interaction approach of CEoS unsuitable; with the α-function unable to correct this fundamental deficiency. More sophisticated models that account for self-association, such as SAFT53 and CPA54 should be used for alcohols.

In general, the four models tested in this study could capture the correct ΔHvap trends for all the hydrogen-bonding components in Group 3. PR-Twu and RK-Twu produced trends that exhibit a minimum in the Cpsat curve, Figure 6b; and, PR-Soave and RK-Soave produced results with significantly higher deviations although no minimum in the Cpsat curve was detected. These results suggest that the Twu and Soave α-functions are not adequate to qualitatively capture the Cpsat of hydrogen-bonding components; therefore, these α-functions should be fitted to only Psat and ΔHvap data in the case of hydrogen-bonding components.

Saturated Liquid Speed of Sound

Figure 10 shows the different contributions to the saturated liquid speed of sound of n-pentane calculated from the untranslated PR-Twu, PR-Soave, RK-Twu, and RK-Soave models, as described in eq 34, Section 3.2.2. As seen in Figure 10, the most important contributions are those arising from the pressure derivative, −(∂P∂v)T, and from the squared saturated liquid volume term, v2/MW. At reduced temperatures below 0.8, the contribution arising from the Cp/Cv term has a value close to zero and therefore has a negligible contribution to the calculated speed of sound. The calculated –(∂P∂v)T decreases rapidly with the reduced temperature whereas the value of v2/MW slightly increases with the reduced temperature. Comparing −(∂P∂v)T and the squared speed of sound, u2, versus temperature, it is clear that both trends are very similar in shape suggesting that the saturated liquid speed of sound is qualitatively controlled by −(∂P∂v)T with v2/MW acting as a correction factor that shifts the magnitude of −(∂P∂v)T to lower values. Therefore, the consistent and accurate calculation of the saturated liquid speed of sound is highly dependent on the accurate calculation of −(∂P∂v)T and the saturated liquid molar volume.

Calculated contributions to the saturated liquid speed of sound of n-pentane according to eq 34.

Figure 11 shows experimental and calculated −(∂P∂v)T and untranslated saturated liquid speed of sound for n-pentane (nonpolar, Group 1, Panel a) and methanol (hydrogen-bonding, Group 3, Panel b) from Test Data set 2. The experimental −(∂P∂v)T used here was calculated using the experimental saturated liquid isothermal compressibility coefficient and density from the NIST Database.26 As discussed in Section 3.2.2, −(∂P∂v)T is not affected by volume translation but the saturated liquid speed of sound is affected by volume translation. In the case of n-pentane, it was found that the four models tested (PR-Twu, PR-Soave, RK-Twu, and RK-Soave) underpredict −(∂P∂v)T; and, for methanol, −(∂P∂v)T was overpredicted, Figure 11. Despite the calculated −(∂P∂v)T trend for both components is qualitatively correct, a close examination of the calculated saturated liquid speed of sound (Figure 11) indicates some inconsistencies. At reduced temperatures below 0.9, the experimental saturated liquid speed of sound decreases almost linearly with the temperature whereas the calculated values decrease exponentially with the temperature. This result, and the analysis of Figure 10, indicates that the calculated −(∂P∂v)T decreases too rapidly with the temperature producing an inconsistent trend for the calculated saturated liquid speed of sound as seen in Figure 11 for n-pentane and methanol. In general, for all the components in the Test Data set 2, the four models tested (PR-Twu, PR-Soave, RK-Twu, and RK-Soave) produced one of the following results for the saturated liquid speed of sound: (1) a trend that crossed the experimental data, as seen for n-pentane in Figure 11a; or, (2) overprediction (or underprediction) as seen for methanol in Figure 11b. The reason behind these results are the inaccuracies in the calculated −(∂P∂v)T that, as previously described, is qualitatively controlling the saturated liquid speed of sound trend.

Conclusions

Four cubic equation of state (CEoS) models were tested in this study for the calculation of the saturation pressure (Psat), enthalpy of vaporization (ΔHvap) and saturated liquid heat capacity (Cpsat) of pure components at subcritical conditions. Additionally, the thermodynamic consistency of the models was assessed by calculating the Waring number and the saturated liquid speed of sound of pure components. The four models tested were: PR-Twu, PR-Soave, RK-Twu, and RK-Soave. The first term in the model’s name refers to the CEoS used: Peng–Robinson (PR) or Redlich–Kwong (RK). The second term indicates the α-function used. The models were tested in a Development Data set containing the Psat, ΔHvap, and Cpsat of 147 pure components classified into three groups: Group 1, nonpolar components (50 components); Group 2, polar components (61 components); and Group 3, hydrogen-bonding components (36 components). For PR-Twu and RK-Twu, the three component-specific parameters of the Twu α-function, eq 7, were calculated by fitting the models to the data in the Development Data set. The calculated Twu α-function parameters are applicable across the entire phase diagram as they were constrained to the thermodynamic consistency requirements proposed by Le Guennec et al.15 (eqs 23–26). For PR-Soave and RK-Soave, The component-specific parameter of the Soave α-function, eq 4, was predicted using the correlations proposed by Piña-Martinez et al.,8eqs 5 and 6. The thermodynamic consistency of the four models was assessed by calculating the Waring number of 147 pure components (Test Data set 1) and the saturated liquid speed of sound of 79 pure components (Test Data set 2).
