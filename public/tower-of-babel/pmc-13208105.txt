Moisture-dependent modulation of powder, structural, pasting, and gel properties of whole acorn flour (Quercus wutaishanica Blume) by heat-moisture treatment
Authors: Meiling Ren, Jie Yang, Cailin Niu, Peiling Liu, Guohua Zhao, Fayin Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acorn flours are valued for nutrition and health benefits. This study investigated heat-moisture treatment (HMT) at 100 °C for 4 h on acorn flour properties using varying moisture contents (15–35 g/100 g). HMT minimally altered microstructure but changed particle size distribution, increasing aggregation, bulk/tapped densities, and flowability with rising moisture content. It shifted acorn starch from C- to A + V-type, reducing crystallinity (34.42% to 21.88%), short-range order (R1047/1022 from 1.26 to 0.83), and mass fractal dimension (2.52 to 1.84). HMT increased β-sheet content and starch–lipid complexing index, while decreasing swelling power and solubility. Pasting viscosities of acorn flour increased or retained after HMT at moisture content of 15–20 g/100 g, but declined at higher moisture levels. HMT-treated flours yielded softer, more elastic gels with reduced hardness (82.63 g to 19.45 g) and increased springiness (0.47 to 0.90) versus untreated flour. Overall, HMT enhanced acorn flour's structural and physicochemical properties, improving gel texture and viscoelasticity.

Introduction

Acorns (Quercus spp.), the fruits of oaks, are produced by approximately 600 species and predominantly distributed in Asia, Europe, North Africa, and North America (Inácio et al., 2024; Szabłowska & Tańska, 2021). Among them, at least 50 species (including their variants) are edible (Dordevic et al., 2025; Inácio et al., 2024; Szabłowska & Tańska, 2021). China's oak forests cover about 13% of its total forest area, producing 6–7 million tons of acorns annually, mainly from Q. wutaishanica Blume, Q. mongolica Fisch. ex Ledeb., and Q. variabilis Blume (Wang et al., 2023). Portugal's cork oak (Q. suber L.) and holm oak (Q. ilex L.) produce around 437,300 tons annually, while Spain's holm oaks yield between 120.4 and 674.3 kg/ha per year (Inácio et al., 2024). Acorns are single-seeded nuts with a hard shell, measuring 1.4–5.14 cm in length and weighing 1.2–16.4 g, each containing an embryo without endosperm at maturity (Inácio et al., 2024; Shakuri et al., 2025).

Acorns are rich in carbohydrates (32.7–91.92 g/100 g, mainly starch), lipids (1.1–31.3 g/100 g), and protein (2.3–8.6 g/100 g). They also contain minerals (Na, K, P, Ca, Mg, Fe, Zn, Cu), vitamins (A, B, C), and phenolics (0.98–1.43 g/100 g) (Dordevic et al., 2025; Inácio et al., 2024; Shakuri et al., 2025; Szabłowska & Tańska, 2021). Traditionally eaten raw (e.g., a sweet acorn oak: Q. ilex subsp. rotundifolia), boiled (e.g., acorn mush known as “wiiwish” or “shawii”), or roasted (e.g., as coffee surrogate), they are now commonly processed into flour for foods like noodles, bread, biscuits, drinks and jellies (often called “xiangzi tofu” in China) (Dordevic et al., 2025; Szabłowska & Tańska, 2021; Szabłowska & Tańska, 2025a). Meanwhile, acorn flour shows promise for gluten-free products in improving texture and nutrition (Konya & Aktaş, 2024; Szabłowska & Tańska, 2021). Yet, its application faces limitations. Szabłowska and Tańska (2025a) reported that substituting wheat flour with ≥30% acorn flour reduced dough extensibility by up to 56% and bread volume by 40%–52% (Szabłowska & Tańska, 2025a). Similarly, Konya and Aktaş (2024) observed that starch-based noodles with >30% acorn flour exhibited elevated cooking loss (4.29% to 9.28%) and reduced firmness (142.3 g to 107.46 g). These challenges necessitate targeted processing strategies to optimize acorn flour's food applications.

Thus far, several pretreatments have been applied to acorn flour to enhance its properties, including roasting, germination, fermentation, microwave processing, and superheated steam treatment (Amina et al., 2018; Belmouloud et al., 2024; Mokhtari et al., 2025; Szabłowska and Tańska, 2021, Szabłowska and Tańska, 2025b). For instance, germination reduced the starch content and bulk density of acorn flour while boosting the foaming capacity and swelling power (Amina et al., 2018). Fermentation of acorn flour increased protein content (by 15%) and antioxidant activity (by 40%), but decreased swelling capacity (by 14.5%) and oil retention (by 31.03%) (Belmouloud et al., 2024). Microwave treatment of hydrated acorn flour (flour-to-water mixture = 1: 1, w/v) released bound phenolics, improving antioxidant properties and acorn flour muffin volume (by 6.31 cm3) (Szabłowska & Tańska, 2025b). Superheated steam processing markedly enhanced the volume, texture, and crust color of acorn flour-fortified rice cake (Mokhtari et al., 2025).

Heat-moisture treatment (HMT) has been widely used to enhance the techno-functional properties of various starches and flours. Typically conducted at 84–140 °C with moisture levels of 10–35 g/100 g for 1–16 h (Fonseca et al., 2021), HMT has demonstrated significant effects. For instance, Shi et al. (2022) reported that HMT of wheat flour (15–25 g/100 g moisture, 100 °C, 1 h) strengthened starch-protein interactions, improving the viscoelasticity of wheat flour gels. Similarly, Gu, Chen, et al. (2024) found that HMT (28.2 g/100 g moisture, 1.5 h, 80–120 °C) increased starch crystallinity and digestion resistance in high-amylose maize flour. Guo et al. (2024) also noted the elevated dietary fiber content (7.06–13.42 g/100 g) in chestnut flour treated with HMT (20 g/100 g moisture, 105 °C, 2 h), which promoted metabolites beneficial for lipid regulation and obesity resistance.

To date, some studies have investigated the effect of HMT on isolated acorn starch (Chen et al., 2025; Molavi et al., 2018). These studies have shown that HMT could reduce solubility, improve gel hardness and freeze–thaw stability, and increase slowly digestible starch and resistant starch contents of acorn starch. Unlike isolated starch, acorn flour is a complex, application-focused whole-flour material. Acorn flour is abundant not only in starch but also in proteins, lipids, and dietary fibers. These components are likely to work synergistically to drive structural changes during HMT, ultimately exerting a collective influence on the flour's properties. In particular, acorn flour is relatively rich in endogenous lipids. Beltrão Martins et al. (2020) reported 8.5 g/100 g lipids in acorn flour, about 2.5 and 6.54 times the levels in buckwheat and rice flours, respectively. Its lipid fraction is mainly composed of C16–C18 fatty acids, including oleic, linoleic, α-linolenic, and palmitic acids (Inácio et al., 2024), which might contribute to a different response to HMT compared with other starch-based systems. In addition, moisture could regulate the mobility of molecular chains and structural reorganization during the HMT process (Fonseca et al., 2021). However, how moisture level regulates starch rearrangement, starch–lipid/protein interactions, and processing properties in acorn flour remains unclear. In the present study, acorn flour was subjected to HMT at 100 °C for 4 h with varying moisture contents (15–35 g/100 g). The powder, structural, pasting, and gelation properties of the treated acorn flour were analyzed. The results could offer valuable insights for optimizing acorn flour processing and its applications in the food industry.

Materials and methods

Materials

Dried acorns (Q. wutaishanica Blume) were purchased from Luoyang Acorn E-Commerce Co., Ltd. (Luoyang, Henan, China). All the chemical reagents and solvents used were of analytical grade.

Preparation of acorn flour

The dried acorns were dehulled manually with a regular kitchen knife, milled using a grinding machine (MFJ-W153, Beijing Liven Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and sieved through an 80-mesh sieve. The acorn flour samples were stored at 4 °C prior to use. The proximate composition (moisture, protein, fat, and ash) of acorn flour was determined following AOAC (2005) methods, and the total carbohydrate content was calculated by difference. Starch content and amylose content were analyzed using the iodine-binding colorimetric method in ISO/TR 6647–2: 2011, with absorbance measured at 720 nm. The chemical composition of acorn flour is listed in Table S1.

Heat-moisture treatment of acorn flour

HMT of acorn flour was performed according to Vu et al. (2017), with some modifications. Briefly, 50 g of acorn flour was weighed and put into a retort pouch. Distilled water was then sprayed on the flour to adjust the moisture content to 15, 20, 25, 30 and 35 g/100 g (wet basis), respectively. The amount of distilled water required was calculated using Eq. (1):(1)ma=m×Mt−Mi/100−Mtwhere ma was the mass of distilled water added (g); m was the initial mass of acorn flour (g); Mt was the target moisture content and Mi was the initial moisture content of acorn flour (10.34 g/100 g, wet basis), respectively. During water addition, the flour and water were thoroughly mixed to ensure uniform moisture distribution. The retort pouches were vacuum sealed and equilibrated at 4 °C for 24 h, and subsequently treated with atmospheric steam in a conventional covered steamer at approximately 100 °C for 4 h, while the temperature was monitored using a thermometer. After cooling to room temperature, the samples were taken out from the retort pouches and dried in a hot air oven (DHG-9140, Shanghai Qixin Scientific Instrument Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 45 °C for 24 h. The dried samples were subjected to pestle grinding and sieved (80-mesh) to obtain the HMT acorn flours. These samples were labeled as HMT-15, HMT-20, HMT-25, HMT-30, and HMT-35, respectively, corresponding to the moisture levels. The native acorn flour was labeled as NAF.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

The morphology and microstructure of the flours were characterized using a scanning electron microscope (Phenom Pro Suite, Phenom-World BV, Eindhoven, the Netherlands). Prior to the test, the flour samples were mounted on carbon tape and sputter-coated with gold. The observation was performed at an accelerating voltage of 10.0 kV, and SEM images were captured at a magnification of 1000 ×, 5000 × and 8000 ×, respectively.

Particle size analysis

The particle size of the acorn flour was determined using a laser particle sizer (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern Instruments Ltd., Malvern, UK) according to Ahmed and Ejaz (2025). The acorn flour was suspended in distilled water to achieve a suspension shading coefficient between 8% and 20%. The refractive index of the dispersing medium was set to 1.33. Following the measurement, the D10, D50 and D90 values were recorded, representing the particle diameters at 10%, 50%, and 90% of the cumulative volume distribution, respectively. The particle size dispersion (Span) was calculated with Eq. (2).(2)Span=D90−D10/D50

Packing properties test

The bulk density (ρB) and tapped density (ρT) were measured with some modifications using a method of Fernando and Manthey (2021). The acorn flour (m1, g) was poured into a measuring cylinder (50 mL) through a glass funnel and the initial volume (V1, mL) was recorded. The cylinder was then tapped repeatedly until the volume no longer decreased, and the final volume (V2, mL) was recorded. The ρB and ρT were calculated with Eq. (3) and Eq. (4), respectively.(3)ρBg/cm3=m1/V1(4)ρTg/cm3=m1/V2

Angle of repose and slide

The angle of repose and slide were measured by the method of Fernando and Manthey (2021). The funnel was vertically fixed 1 cm above the test platform. The acorn flour was slowly poured into the funnel until it accumulated into a cone-shaped pile whose peak reached the funnel's opening. The angle of repose was calculated with Eq. (5):(5)Angle of repose°=arctanH/R

where H (cm) and R (cm) were the height and radius of the powder cone, respectively.

The acorn flour was poured on a glass plate (10 cm × 20 cm) and the plate was slowly tilted until the particles began to slide. The angle of slide was calculated with Eq. (6).(6)Angle of slide°=arcsinH/L

where L (cm) and H (cm) were plate length and the vertical distance between the top of the inclined and horizontal planes, respectively.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The crystalline structure of the starch in acorn flour samples was analyzed using an XRD instrument (X'Pert 3 Powder, PANalytical B.V., the Netherlands). XRD patterns were recorded at 40 kV and 40 mA with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å). The diffraction angle (2θ) was measured in the range of 4°–40° with a scanning rate of 4°/min. The relative crystallinity (RC) and V-type crystallinity (RC-V) were determined by peak fitting using MDI Jade 6 software (Materials Data Inc., Livermore, CA, USA) according to Gu, Cheng, et al. (2024).

Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS)

SAXS experiments were carried out on the BL16B1 beamline using a Pilatus 2MF detector at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (Shanghai Advanced Research Institute, Shanghai, China). Silver behenate was used as the calibration standard for the SAXS scattering vector. Prior to measurement, 0.8 g of the acorn flour sample was mixed with 1.2 mL of distilled water to make a slurry and equilibrated at room temperature for 24 h, and was vortexed immediately before measurement to obtain a homogeneous suspension. Immediately prior to measurement, the slurry was vortexed at 2000 rpm for 15 s, after which it was transferred to a 2-mm-thick sample cell and exposed to a monochromatic X-ray beam (λ = 1.24 Å) for 30 s, with a sample-to-detector distance of 1900 mm. The scattering vector (q = 4π sinθ / λ, where 2θ was the scattering angle) ranged from 0.15 to 1.5 nm−1, and scattering intensities were recorded using a photodiode to obtain SAXS data. FIT2D.exe software (Version 10.132, European Synchrotron Radiation Facility, Grenoble, France) was used to convert the 2D images into 1D profiles, from which parameters such as the radius of gyration (Rg), fractal dimension (Dm), and lamellar thickness (dBragg) were calculated by the method of Li et al. (2024).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

An attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectrometer (Spectrum 100, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to record the infrared spectra of the samples. Prior to analysis, the samples were sieved through a 200-mesh sieve and equilibrated for 24 h at room temperature. The spectra were collected in the wavenumber range of 4000–600 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and the scanning times of 16 scans. Baseline correction, smoothing, and absorbance normalization of the spectra were performed using OMNIC software (Version 9.2, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madison, WI, USA). This was followed by spectral deconvolution with an enhancement factor of 1.9 and a bandwidth of 40 cm−1. The absorbance ratios (R1047/1022 and R1022/995) were calculated based on the peak absorbance values at 1047 cm−1, 1022 cm−1 and 995 cm−1. The protein secondary structure composition was determined by analyzing the amide I band (1600–1700 cm−1). The band was deconvoluted and curve-fitted using PeakFit software (v4.12, SeaSolve Software Inc., San Jose, CA, USA).

Starch-lipid complexing index (CI)

The starch-lipid complexing index was determined by the method of Gu, Fang, et al. (2024). 0.2 g of the sample was suspended in 10 mL distilled water and subjected to gelatinization in a 95 °C water bath for 1 h. After cooling at room temperature for 10 min, the suspension was centrifuged at 4000 ×g for 10 min, and 50 μL of the supernatant was taken out and reacted with 2 mL of iodine solution (ten-fold diluted from 1.3% I₂ and 2% KI). The absorbance at 690 nm was measured, and the CI value was calculated according to Eq. (7):(7)CI%=100×ANAF−AHMT/ANAF

where ANAF and AHMT are the absorbance values of native and HMT-treated acorn flour, respectively.

Swelling power (SP) and solubility (SOL)

Samples (1.0 g) were mixed with 20 mL distilled water and incubated in a water bath for 30 min with gentle stirring at 25, 55 and 85 °C, respectively. The samples were then taken out from the water bath and centrifuged at 3000 ×g for 30 min using a high-speed refrigerated benchtop centrifuge (H1850R, Hunan Xiangyi Laboratory Instrument Development Co., Ltd., Hunan, China). The supernatant was collected and dried in an oven at 105 °C to a constant weight. SOL and SP were calculated using Eqs. (8), (9), respectively (Asranudin et al., 2021).(8)SOL%=m2/m0×100(9)SPg/g=m3/m0×1−SOL

where m0 (g) was the initial weight of sample (1.0 g), m2 (g) was the dry weight of the supernatant, and m3 (g) was the weight of the wet sediment.

Rapid Visco-analyzer (RVA)

The pasting properties of the samples were determined by a Rapid Visco-Analyzer (RVA-TecMaster, Perten Scientific Instruments Ltd., Stockholm, Sweden), using the method by Gu et al. (2024b) with some modifications. 3 g of sample (dry basis) and 25 g of distilled water were weighed and added to an RVA canister. The test procedure involved stirring at 960 r/min for the initial 10 s at 50 °C, stirring at 160 r/min for 1 min at 50 °C, heating from 50 °C to 95 °C at a rate of 12 °C/min, holding at 95 °C for 2.5 min, and subsequently back to 50 °C at the same cooling rate.

Dynamic rheological properties

The rheological properties of acorn flour pastes were analyzed using the method described by Zhang et al. (2023) with slight modification. After the RVA pasting program was completed, the paste sample was immediately collected and then transferred to the measurement platform of a rheometer (DHR-2, TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA) equipped with a parallel plate (40 mm in diameter) set at a 1.0 mm gap for rheological property analysis. A strain sweep test (0.01–1000%) was conducted at 25 °C and 1 Hz to determine the linear viscoelastic region (LVR) of all samples. After that, a frequency sweep test was performed at a strain of 1% (within the LVR) over the angular frequency range of 0.1–100 rad/s.

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

The TPA of the acorn flour gels was carried out using a texture analyzer (TA. XT plus, Stable Micro Systems, Godalming, Surrey, UK) by the method of Ungureanu-Iuga and Mironeasa (2023) with slight modification. Prior to test, the weighed acorn flour was mixed with distilled water (1,6, w/w) and heated at 95 °C for 30 min. The resulting acorn paste was poured into silicone rubber molds (3.5 cm in diameter) and stored at 4 °C for 24 h to form the gels. Each gel was equilibrated at room temperature for 1 h. The measurement was performed using a P/0.5 probe with the following parameters: compression ratio, 50%; pre-test, test, and post-test speeds, 1.0 mm/s; test height, 5 mm; trigger force, 5.0 g; interval between two compression cycles, 3 s.

Water distribution

The water distribution in the acorn flour gel was determined using a low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMI20-060 V-I, Suzhou Niumag Analytical Instrument Co., Ltd., Suzhou, Jiangsu, China) based on transverse relaxation time (T₂). The acorn flour gel prepared in Section 2.15 was wrapped with plastic film, placed into a 45 mm NMR tube, and analyzed for T₂ relaxation using the Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill mode. The operating parameters were as the following: spectral frequency = 333.333 kHz, recycle delay = 2000, number of scans = 4, echo time = 0.12 ms, and number of echoes = 18,000. Each treatment group was analyzed in triplicate using independently prepared samples, and each sample was measured twice.

Syneresis determination

The syneresis of acorn flour gel was measured following the method of Yarlina et al. (2025) with some modification. The acorn flour gel prepared in Section 2.15 was placed in a plastic centrifuge tube, sealed, and frozen at −18 °C for 24 h, then defrosted in a 25 °C water bath for 2 h. The tube was centrifuged at 3000 ×g for 20 min. The supernatant was weighed and the syneresis rate was calculated according to Eq. (10).(10)Syneresis%=mi−mp/mi×100

where mi (g) was the initial weight of the gel, mp (g) was the weight of the supernatant.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate using independently prepared samples unless otherwise stated. The data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) with one-way analysis of variance, and significance was set at P < 0.05. All figures were plotted using Origin 2022 software (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA).

Results and discussion

Morphological characteristics

As disclosed by SEM (Fig. S1, magnification 1000×), the native acorn flour (NAF) consisted of particles with varying sizes. As examined more closely (Fig. S2, magnification 3000×), the microstructure of acorn flour was rough, including liberated starch granules and debris or tissue fragments distributed on and around its surface. In contrast, the surface of isolated acorn starch was smooth as reported by He et al. (2022). In comparison to NAF (Fig. 1), the microscopic morphology of acorn flour did not significantly change following HMT at moisture contents of 15–25 g/100 g (HMT-15, HMT-20 and HMT-25). However, the particle agglomeration occurred in the flour treated at higher moisture content (HMT-30 and HMT-35). As observed at greater magnification (8000×), acorn flours treated at higher moisture contents exhibited more irregular shape and rougher surface of the particles (Fig. 1). The results indicated that increasing moisture content during HMT facilitated starch swelling and gelatinization and amylose leaching driven by the thermal force, and consequently leading to particle agglomeration and the loss in surface integrity of the particles (Gu et al., 2024a). Particle size distribution was measured to evaluate changes in the particle size and aggregation of acorn flour after HMT. As shown in Fig. S3, acorn flours showed a broad unimodal profile with three humps in the ranges of 3.12–30 μm, 30–100 μm, and > 100 μm, respectively. The region in 3.12–30 μm was dominated by starch granules liberated from the cotyledons, the region in 30–100 μm was attributed to fine cotyledonary tissue fragments, and the >100 μm part was designated to coarse cotyledon fragments that were partially disrupted during milling (Ahmed & Ejaz, 2025). HMT-treated acorn flours showed a decrease in the volume (%) of particles >100 μm and an increase in 30–100 μm particle fraction, indicating that HMT promoted the disintegration of cotyledonary tissue. However, two opposite trends were observed for the fraction in 3.12–30 μm: the volume (%) increased when the acorn flour was HMT-treated under low-moisture conditions (15 and 20 g/100 g), but it decreased when the moisture level was elevated to 25–35 g/100 g. The results indicated that HMT and post-process (the subsequent grinding) altered the particle size distribution of acorn flour. The structure of cotyledon fragments might be altered during HMT under low-moisture conditions, making the fragments more susceptible to breakage during subsequent grinding. Conversely, at high moisture levels (25–35 g/100 g), starch swelling and partial gelatinization might enhance the cohesion among the coexisting components, leading to a shift toward larger particle size and a decrease in the volume (%) of particles in the region of 3.12–30 μm (Zou et al., 2024). D10, D50, and D90 represent that 10%, 50%, 90% of the total particles are smaller than this size, respectively (Fernando & Manthey, 2021). As shown in Table 1, HMT-20 showed a significant decrease in D10 (P < 0.05), with a reduction of 8.17% when compared with NAF, and HMT at low moisture levels (15–25 g/100 g) led to a significant decrease in D50 (P < 0.05), with 21.40% decrease for HMT-20. By contrast, D50 of acorn flour significantly increased (P < 0.05) at higher moisture levels (≥ 30 g/100 g), with an 8.39% rise in maximum. In addition, D90 increased when HMT was performed under moisture content ≥25 g/100 g. It might be ascribed to partial starch gelatinization, protein denaturation, and starch granule interactions ultimately leading to particle aggregation and the increases in D50 and D90 (Zou et al., 2024). Pasqualone et al. (2019) reported that acorn flour from Quercus ilex L., Quercus suber L., and Quercus coccifera L. was mainly composed of coarse particles, with about 70% of particles larger than 300 μm and the highest proportion observed in the >425 μm fraction. In contrast, He et al. (2022) reported that 82.25% of the particles in acorn flour (Quercus wutaishanica Bl.) fell within 5–25 μm. The differences among studies might be attributed to variations in acorn species, flour composition, milling conditions, sieving procedures, and measurement methods. Span indicates the width of particle size distribution (Ahmed & Ejaz, 2025). The broad profiles of HMT-treated flours in Fig. S3 were reflected by Span values, with HMT-20 showing the maximal Span value. Powder properties

Packing properties

The packing properties of acorn flours, expressed as ρB and ρT, are presented in Table 1. These parameters are crucial for powder handling operations, including storage, handling, transportation, and compression in solid dosage manufacturing. ρB represents the mass per unit volume of a powder in its freely settled state, including interparticle voids. As shown in Table 1, HMT resulted in a marginal effect on ρB except for HMT-35, of which a ρB value was 35.71% greater than that of NAF. This might be ascribed to particle enlargement, which was evidenced by the significant increases in D50, thereby enhancing the gravitational packing efficiency. In contrast, finer particles, owing to their higher specific surface area and greater air resistance, tended to entrap more air during pouring, resulting in a looser bulk structure (Fernando & Manthey, 2021). Malunga et al. (2025) reported that canary seed (Phalaris canariensis L.) flour subjected to HMT under 17–18 g/100 g moisture content increased the ρB from 0.55 to 0.61 g/cm3, which was partly due to granule aggregation. ρT is defined as the mass per unit volume after mechanical tapping. Among HMT flours, ρT progressively increased with increasing moisture content. The ρT value of HMT-35 was 30.56% greater than that of NAF. The increase in ρT was probably associated with partial starch gelatinization and flour particle agglomeration induced by HMT, which consequently altered the particle size, shape, and surface properties of acorn flour, leading to tighter powder packing (Fernando & Manthey, 2021).

Flowability properties

Flowability properties are critical for powder transport, storage, feeding, and ensuring uniform mixing of ingredients during processing (Fernando & Manthey, 2021). The angle of repose and the angle of slide are commonly used as indications of flowability. The angle of repose characterizes powder flowability under free-flowing and uncompacted conditions, reflecting the equilibrium between particle kinetic energy and interparticle cohesive forces during heap formation. As displayed in Table 1, the angle of repose of the acorn flour increased by HMT, indicating reduced flowability. It increased with the increase of HMT moisture content. The angle of repose of HMT-30 was 22.84% greater than that of NAF. It was probably due to the increase in particle roughness and specific surface area promoted mechanical bridging and interparticle cohesion (McGuire et al., 2022). Milkias et al. (2024) indicated powders with an angle of repose greater than 45° as having poor flowability, and they reported that the angle of repose of wheat flour was 40.61°. In this study, acorn flour (angles of repose 30.57°–37.63°) exhibited good powder flowability. Notably, HMT-30 and HMT-35 showed values closer to those reported for wheat flour, suggesting their potential miscibility with wheat flour. Since flour miscibility is also affected by particle size, density, morphology, and composition, the angle of repose should only be considered a preliminary indicator. Besides, the angle of slide characterizes the flowability of powders on an inclined surface, being mainly related to particle adhesion. As shown in Table 1, the angle of slide showed no significant difference among NAF, HMT-15, HMT-20, and HMT-25. In contrast, the slide angle of acorn flour treated by HMT at 30 g/100 g moisture (HMT-30) was significantly greater than that of NAF (P < 0.05). The result was likely due to HMT-induced alterations in particle surface properties, which strengthened interparticle cohesion and limited particle mobility.

Structural characteristics

Crystalline structure of starch

Starch, accounting for 85.41 g/100 g, is the predominant component of acorn flour (Table S1). XRD was used to determine the crystallinity of starch, providing insights into its long-range order. NAF (Fig. 2A) showed its peaks at 5.6°, 15°, 17.1°, 18° and 23° (2θ), which depicted a typical C-type starch crystalline pattern. He et al. (2022) reported that acorn flour had diffraction peaks at 5.6°, 15°, 17°, 18°, 20° and 23° (2θ), respectively, and displayed a C-type crystalline pattern. After HMT, acorn flour exhibited peaks at 15°, 17.1°, 18°, 20°, and 23°, suggesting a shift from C-type to A + V-type crystalline pattern. The peak at 20° was a characteristic peak of V-type crystalline associated with the formation of amylose-lipid complexes (Zhou et al., 2025). The RC and RC-V of starch in NAF and HMT-treated acorn flours are presented in Table 2. In comparison to NAF, HMT samples exhibited significantly lower RC values (P < 0.05), which decreased progressively with increasing moisture content, with reductions ranging from 10.18% to 57.34%. The decrease in crystallinity was probably attributed to the excessive swelling and subsequent rupture of starch granules induced by HMT under high moisture content. Gu et al. (2024a) found that, when the moisture content during the treatment increased from 13 g/100 g to 40 g/100 g, the RC of mung bean flour was decreased by 0.49%–13.11%. The RC reduction in HMT-treated acorn flour (protein 2.01%) was greater than in mung bean flour (protein 28.88%). It was mainly due to its low protein content, which offered less structural support to starch granules and facilitated granule swelling and crystalline disruption. RC-V increased significantly (P < 0.05) with increasing moisture content in HMT-treated samples, whereas no V-type crystalline peak was detected in NAF (Table 2). Lamellar structure of starch

SAXS is sensitive to the nanoscale lamellar structure of starch, capable of revealing lamellar regularity, lamellar repeat distance, and structural compactness by detecting the electron density contrast between crystalline and amorphous lamellae. The combined use of SAXS and XRD allowed for a more comprehensive analysis of the HMT-induced structural changes in acorn flour. Fig. 2B shows the one-dimensional (1D) SAXS curves of scattered intensity (I) versus the scattering vector (q). NAF showed a typical peak around 0.64–0.67 nm−1 in its 1D curve. By HMT with increasing moisture content, the peak gradually broadened and nearly disappeared under HMT-35, indicating a decline in the regularity of lamellar arrangement within starch granules (Niu et al., 2020). HMT-treated acorn flours exhibited greater scattering peak intensity than NAF and the intensity increased with moisture content up to 30 g/100 g, but decreased at 35 g/100 g. These findings suggested that HMT disrupted the original semicrystalline lamellar structure, lowering lamellar order and altering electron density contrast (Niu et al., 2020). Similarly, Liu et al. (2019) reported that the peak intensity of HMT-treated highland barley starch increased as the moisture content elevated from 15 g/100 g to 25 g/100 g but decreased with 30 g/100 g moisture.

To enhance the visibility of the scattering peak, Lorentz correction was applied to the SAXS data (Fig. S4A). The thickness of the semi-crystalline lamellae was expressed using the Bragg lamellar repeat distance (dBragg), which was calculated according to Bragg's law (dBragg=2π/qmax, where qmax was the position of the scattering peak). The dBragg of starch in HMT-15 significantly decreased (P < 0.05) in comparison to NAF (Table 2), likely due to thermal disruption of the crystalline structure. However, the dBragg increased significantly (P < 0.05) when the moisture content ≥30 g/100 g. In this situation, the swelling of the amorphous lamellae, the formation of starch-lipid complexes, or the dissociation of the double helix tail might occur to the largest extent (Gu et al., 2024c; Li et al., 2024).

According to Guinier's law (Iq=I0e−Rg2q23, ln I(q) ∼ q2), the approximate value of the radius of gyration (Rg=−3α) of the acorn starch lamellar structure was derived from the slope (α) obtained by linear fitting over 0.4 < q2 < 0.8 nm−2 in the Guinier plot (Fig. S4B). Rg value is an indicator of the size of the scattering structure and used to reveal the change in lamellar structure during HMT. The Rg value of HMT-treated acorn flours with moisture content above 20 g/100 g was significantly greater than that of NAF (P < 0.05), which was likely associated with the swelling of the amorphous regions during HMT. With increasing moisture content from 20 to 35 g/100 g, the Rg firstly increased and then decreased, reaching a maximum in HMT-25 (Table 2). The decrease in Rg at moisture contents above 25 g/100 g might be associated with intensified structural rearrangement under higher moisture conditions.

The fractal dimension reflects the self-similarity of the material structure and is derived from the absolute value of the slope (α) of the low q region in the double-log scattering curve (Fig. S4C). The slope follows the power-law relationship (I(q) = I0q-α). It was used to describe the mass compactness or surface roughness of starch granules (Zhang et al., 2024). For surface fractals (3 < α < 4), the fractal dimension was calculated as Dₛ = 6 − α; for mass fractals (1 < α < 3), it was calculated as Dₘ = α. The starch in acorn flour had a mass fractal structure (Table 2), which characterized the compactness of the internal starch structure (Zhang et al., 2024). The mass fractal dimension of starch in HMT-treated acorn flours was lower than that in NAF, indicating that HMT decreased the compactness of the internal structure of acorn starch.

Short-range ordered structure of starch

The short-range order of acorn flours was characterized using FTIR, and the results are illustrated in Fig. 2C–D and Table 2. The major band positions and overall shapes of bands (Fig. 2C) were highly similar among all the samples, indicating that HMT did not induce substantial chemical structural changes. The 800–1200 cm−1 region of FTIR corresponds to the fingerprint region of starch, which reflects the stretching vibrations of C—C, C-OH, and C—H bonds (Gu et al., 2024b). Particularly, the FTIR absorption at 995 cm−1 is associated with the helical order of starch, while the absorption peaks at 1022 cm−1 and 1047 cm−1 are indicative of the structural characteristics of the amorphous and crystalline regions, respectively (Fig. 2D). The absorbance ratios of 1022/995 cm−1 and 1047/1022 cm−1 were adopted to evaluate the degree of double helix and degree of short-range order, respectively (Zhang et al., 2022). The R1022/995 value of HMT-15 was comparable to that of NAF. At moisture contents of 20–35 g/100 g, this value increased by 12.3%–36.8% (P < 0.05), suggesting that HMT promoted the dissociation of starch double helices under these conditions (Table 2). Meanwhile, the R1047/1022 values of HMT-treated acorn flours were lower than those of NAF (P < 0.05), with reductions ranging from 5.6%–34.1%. The results indicated a downward trend in short-range order with increasing moisture content, which was probably due to the combined effect of heat and moisture during HMT, which disrupted hydrogen bonds, unwound the amylopectin double helices, and increased molecular disorder in the starch components that were prone to gelatinization (Zhang et al., 2022).

CI

Lipids could insert into the starch hydrophobic helix cavity to form starch-lipid complexes, predominantly with amylose, as the short branches of amylopectin hinder single-helix formation (Zhou et al., 2025). The CI was used to evaluate the extent of complex formation, as amylose-lipid interactions reduced the iodine-binding capacity of amylose. As shown in Table 2, the CI of HMT-treated acorn flours significantly increased when the moisture content was above 20 g/100 g. With increasing moisture content during HMT, starch granule swelling and amylose leaching were facilitated, and more sites for lipid binding were exposed (Gu et al., 2024a). Meanwhile, higher moisture content could enhance the mobility of amylose, amylopectin, and free lipids during HMT, thereby facilitating starch-lipid complex formation (Chang et al., 2014). To be specific, the CI of HMT-25, HMT-30, and HMT-35 was 6.77, 9.12, and 10.06 times greater than that of HMT-15, respectively. The acorn flour used in this study had a relatively high lipid content (8.59%) and low protein content (2.01%) and therefore, the variations in CI of HMT samples were mainly driven by the synergistic effect of moisture and lipid contents. Gu et al. (2024a) reported that CI of mung bean flour (lipid 0.93%; amylose 34.75%) increased markedly from 1.84% to 45.06% after heat treatment (100 °C) at different moisture content (13–70 g/100 g). They attributed the increase in CI primarily to the help of increasing amount of water, which disrupted hydrogen bonds between amylose and amylopectin, promoting amylose leaching and complexation with lipids. Besides, the mobility of starch-lipid chains was enhanced under higher moisture, facilitating more ordered arrangements.

SP and SOL

As shown in Fig. 3A, the SP of NAF increased progressively as temperature increased from 25 °C to 85 °C. However, HMT-treated flours showed a significant decrease in SP at the same temperature compared to NAF. Meanwhile, the SP was progressively lowered as moisture content increased during HMT. The decline in SP value was mainly attributed to the amylose-lipid complexes, which lowered the water permeability of the starch granules (Zhou et al., 2025). Asranudin et al. (2021) reported that HMT-treated purple yam flour showed a decrease in SP as the moisture content increased (15–30 g/100 g), which was attributed to three main factors: (i) the interactions of amylose-amylose and amylose-amylopectin were reinforced during HMT; (ii) the availability of hydroxyl groups for hydration was reduced by HMT treatment; and (iii) protein denaturation might prevent interaction with water. Molavi et al. As shown in Fig. 3B, the SOL of acorn flours had a similar trend to SP. The HMT process resulted in a reduction of acorn flour's SOL, with SOL decreasing progressively as the moisture content increased during HMT process. The SOL of acorn flours might be associated with the leaching of amylose and solubilization of other solutes. The results indicated that the amylose leaching was suppressed, which was probably attributed to a denser internal starch molecular arrangement under greater moisture content that restricted amylose diffusion, as well as the steric hindrance induced by endogenous proteins and lipids and amylose-lipid complex formation (Bhat & Arya, 2020).

Pasting properties

During cooking and/or processing, the pasting of acorn flour plays a key role that influences the overall quality of its formulated products. The pasting property of acorn flours was measured by RVA and the profiles are illustrated in Fig. 3C, which indicates a viscosity development during the heating and cooling of acorn flour-water blend with a programmed manner. Pasting parameters, such as pasting temperature (PT), peak (PV), trough (TV), final (FV), breakdown (BD = PV - TV), and setback (SB = FV - TV) viscosities were recorded in Table 3. As shown in Fig. 3C, the pasting curves of NAF, HMT-15, and HMT-20 were similar and closely overlapped, while the curves corresponding to HMT-25, HMT-30, and HMT-35 were distinctly positioned below them. The curves of HMT-30 and HMT-35 almost overlapped and they kept an upward tendency and did not show an obvious pasting peak. Compared with NAF, HMT-treated flours showed a significant (P < 0.05) increase in PT from 75.58 °C to 82.85 °C (Table 3). It suggested that HMT could enhance the resistance of starch granules (before gelatinization) to heat. Asranudin et al. (2023) reported that the PT of purple yam flours increased by HMT (100 °C, 3 h) with increasing the moisture content (from 15 to 30 g/100 g), which was due to enhanced ordering of polymer chains through interactions among amylose, amylopectin, and amylose-fatty acid complexes. Meanwhile, HMT showed a significant effect on pasting viscosities. Compared with NAF, HMT-treated flours increased PV and TV values when the moisture content was 15 g/100 g but progressively decreased the values as the moisture content increased. HMT-15 showed greatest PV and TV among them, indicating that HMT at low moisture content (15 g/100 g) promoted the swelling of starch granules before their disruption upon heating. However, HMT at the moisture content >15 g/100 g improved the granular integrity by forming a protective layer on the outer shell of the starch granules, which prevented the water penetration and amylose leaching, and consequently lowered SP and SOL (as indicated in Fig. 3A&B), and resulted in a decrease in PV and TV (Zhang et al., 2022). BD followed a similar trend, which attained maximal value for HMT-15 but showed a significant decrease in HMT-30 and HMT-35 (by 73.82% and 67.08%, respectively) compared with NAF. Decreasing BD values implied that the starch granules of HMT acorn flours treated in high moisture content resisted deformation under shear and heat, and were resistant to disintegration by reinforcing the intra-granular binding forces (Asranudin et al., 2023). The increasing CI (as indicated in Table 2) also impeded the rupture of acorn starch granules (Guo et al., 2024). Besides, HMT significantly (P < 0.05) increased the subsequent FV and SB when the flours treated at low moisture contents (15–20 g/100 g) but it significantly (P < 0.05) decreased FV and SB when the flours treated at high moisture contents (25–35 g/100 g). The results indicated that HMT at 15–20 g/100 g moisture promoted the rearrangement of amylose in the paste of acorn flour upon cooling. Small deformation rheological properties

The formation of starch gel network was closely related to the reorganization after pasting of acorn flours. The rheological properties of cooled pastes were evaluated by frequency sweep tests, in which the storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G″) and the loss tangent (tan δG″/ G′) were used to characterize the rigidity and tenacity of the gel network under small-amplitude oscillatory deformation (Fig. 3D–F). Generally, G′ and G″ represent the elastic and viscous characteristics of the system, respectively (Zhang et al., 2023). The G′ and G″ of cooled pastes demonstrated an increase with the rise in angular frequency within the frequency range for the testing. Meanwhile, G′ values of all samples were consistently higher than G″ within the range of 0.1–100 rad/s, suggesting a solid-like behavior (typical weak gels) (Zhang et al., 2023). In comparison with NAF, the HMT samples had greater G′ and G″, indicating that HMT strengthened the viscoelastic network of the system under small deformation. The G′ values followed the sequence of NAF < HMT-15 < HMT-35 < HMT-30 < HMT-25 < HMT-20, reaching a maximum at 20 g/100 g moisture content, which corresponded to the lowest BD value (as indicated in Table 3). The G″ values showed a similar trend, with the order of NAF < HMT-35 ≈ HMT-30 ≈ HMT-25 < HMT-20 ≈ HMT-15, reflecting enhanced viscous and energy-dissipating properties when the acorn flours were treated under low moisture conditions. Ungureanu-Iuga and Mironeasa (2023) reported that the dough from HMT-treated wheat flour showed an increase in both G′ and G″, explaining that the dough strengthening was probably attributed to the aggregation of gluten proteins and the modification of starch structure and hydrophobicity during HMT. The remnants of swollen starch granules also played a key role, as the particle rigidity, swollen granule integrity, and the extent of granule swelling contributed to the formation of a close-packed particle gel network, thereby reinforcing the rheological properties (Renzetti et al., 2025). Tan δ reflects the dynamic viscoelasticity of the system, with higher values indicating greater viscosity and fluidity, and lower values reflecting greater elasticity and solid-like behavior (Shi et al., 2022). In this study, all samples (Fig. 3F) showed tan δ values less than 1, indicating that the acorn flour gel was more elastic (Zhang et al., 2023). With increasing angular frequency, tan δ of NAF remained nearly constant across the entire frequency range, whereas tan δ of HMT-treated acorn flour samples exhibited a pronounced upward trend, indicating that the network of HMT samples was less stable or more structurally heterogeneous compared to NAF.

Gel properties

TPA

Acorn flour gels were formed by the reassociation of gelatinized starch molecules during cooling, with leached amylose forming the continuous phase network and swollen starch granules and their fragments embedded in it as filler/supporting structural units. Other components, such as proteins and lipids, also participated in the network formation (Liu et al., 2026). Table 4 shows the TPA results of gel samples prepared from the native and HMT-treated acorn flour, including hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, chewiness and resilience. Hardness refers to the force needed to break a food sample. The gel hardness of HMT-treated samples (> 15 g/100 g moisture) significantly (P < 0.05) decreased compared with that of NAF, whereas G′ increased (Fig. 3D). HMT induced internal starch-chain rearrangement and promoted amylose–lipid complex formation, thereby restricting granule swelling (Fig. 3A) and reducing amylose leaching (decreased SOL in Fig. 3B). As a result, the HMT flour gels contained more rigid granule remnants but a less well-developed continuous phase network. This structural reorganization increased the gel's resistance to small-deformation shear, as reflected by the higher G′ values, but weakened the resistance of the gel under TPA compression, leading to lower hardness. Springiness represents the rate at which a deformed food returns to its original shape after the applied force is removed. HMT-treated acorn flour gels exhibited markedly higher springiness than NAF gels, which was consistent with the observed increase in G′ (Fig. 3D). Cohesiveness reflects the compactness of the gels. HMT did not significantly change the cohesiveness of acorn flour gels compared with NAF. Chewiness reflects the energy required during mastication to disintegrate semi-solid foods before swallowing. The chewiness exhibited no significant change when the flour was treated at 15–20 g/100 g moisture, but showed a significant decline when the moisture content was 25–35 g/100 g, which was related to the observed reduction in gel hardness (Table 4). Resilience reflects the ability of the gel to recover its original form after deformation. HMT led to a significant decrease in resilience values (Table 4), indicating a reduced instantaneous recovery capacity of the gel. This was associated with greater energy dissipation during compression, as indicated by the higher G″ values. Water distribution

The transverse relaxation time (T2) reflects the interaction strength between water molecules and the gel matrix, with a higher T2 value denoting greater water mobility (Sun et al., 2025). The T2 relaxation spectra of the acorn flour gel samples are illustrated in Fig. 4 and corresponding parameters are summarized in Table 4. The relaxation components T21 (0.01–10 ms), T22 (10–430 ms), and T23 (430–10,000 ms) correspond to bound water, immobilized water, and free water, respectively (Gu et al., 2024a). Fig. 4 showed a heterogeneous distribution of water states in the acorn flour gels. The immobilized water (as indicated by T22), which was limited by gel network structure, dominated in the gels. In the region of T21, it showed two peaks, i.e., T21a & T21b, suggesting the presence of tightly bound water and weakly bound water, respectively. Meanwhile, HMT-treated samples showed a more obvious T23 peak than NAF, indicating more free water in the continuous phase of the acorn flour gels. As shown in Table 4, HMT showed no significant effect on T21a. However, HMT samples had lower T21b relaxation time than NAF. T21b progressively declined as the moisture content during HMT increased. This trend indicated that HMT resulted in an increase in bound water in the acorn flour gels. As indicated by the decreasing T22, HMT increased interaction strength between water molecules and the gel matrix. The maximal T23 was observed in NAF. However, HMT led to a decrease in T23 and it gradually decreased as the moisture content increased. The normalized peak area reflected the proportion of each water population. As shown in Table 4, A21a was not significantly impacted by HMT. However, A21b and A22 of HMT-treated flour samples progressively declined and A23 of HMT-treated flour samples increased as the moisture content increased. The results suggested that HMT led to an increase in free water outside the gel matrix and an increase in interaction strength of immobilized water and the gel matrix. HMT-induced biopolymer chain rearrangement and strengthened starch-protein and starch-lipid interactions constrained the mobility of water that remained within the gel matrix, resulting in residual water more tightly bound. The increase in free water might be attributed to two factors: (i) starch-protein interactions occupied the hydrogen-bonding sites for water (Table 2); (ii) increased amylose-lipid complex formation (Table 2) reduced the available amylose, weakening the gel network and promoting water mobility (Gu et al., 2024a).Fig. 4The LF-NMR relaxation spectra of acorn flour gels. Syneresis

Syneresis reflects the gel stability and water-holding capacity during storage and thawing. In one freeze-thawing cycle, the syneresis value of NAF gel was 42.32% (Table 4). However, the syneresis value was observed to progressively increase with the increase in moisture content during HMT (Table 4). The results showed that HMT led to a decrease in gel stability and water-holding capacity of acorn flour gels. The increase in syneresis was mainly attributed to the gel network structure change induced by HMT. HMT promoted amylose-lipid complex formation and restricted amylose leaching, with more swollen or partially intact granules retained. These changes resulted in a heterogeneous gel network with weakened water retention (increased A23), making it prone to water separation during freeze–thawing. Meanwhile, the retained granules and amylose–lipid complexes could immobilize water molecules (decreased T21b and T22) and inhibit amylose rearrangement during freeze–thawing, thereby reduce the syneresis (Liu et al., 2026). An overall increase in syneresis indicated that the adverse effect of HMT on gel network formation predominated. Similarly, Yarlina et al. (2025) found that HMT (30 g/100 g moisture, 85 °C, 18 h) increased the syneresis of spirulina-soybean tempeh flour gels from 73.28% to 77.50%.

Given the above property variations, the application potential of HMT-treated acorn flour depended on moisture level. HMT-15 could be the optimal compromise condition for products requiring thickening capacity or gel firmness, such as porridge, jelly and noodles, as it showed higher PV, FV and springiness, with gel hardness and syneresis comparable to NAF. HMT-20 could be more suitable for products requiring superior elasticity and structural stability, as it exhibited the highest G′ and springiness, retained moderate hardness and chewiness, showed a PV close to NAF, and had lower BD. It thus held promise for elastic gel foods including jelly candies and chewy starch gels. Heat-moisture treatment at higher moisture levels (25–35 g/100 g) produced softer gels that retained relatively high springiness, suggesting potential for soft-textured foods or texture-modifying ingredients, but their application might be limited by higher syneresis, lower PV, and poorer powder flowability.

Conclusions

In this study, HMT (100 °C, 4 h) was applied to acorn flour with varying moisture contents (15–35 g/100 g) and the results showed that the effects of HMT on physicochemical properties of acorn flour were dependent on the moisture content. As the moisture content increased, HMT progressively disrupted the ordered structure of starch in acorn flour, promoted the formation of starch-lipid complexes, and induced structural rearrangement of proteins. These structural changes consequently reduced swelling power and solubility, and altered the pasting and gel properties of acorn flour. When acorn flour was subjected to HMT, pasting viscosities were increased or maintained at moisture contents of 15–20 g/100 g, yet decreased progressively with further increases in moisture contents. HMT-treated acorn flour produced softer, more elastic gels with enhanced viscous response. HMT reduced T2 values of acorn flour gels, inhibited the bound water transforming to free water. HMT increased the A23 values of acorn flour gels, enhanced the mobility of water molecules in the continuous phase of the gel, which was further reflected by the increase in syneresis of HMT-treated acorn flour gels. The study highlights the potential of HMT to improve the softness and elasticity of acorn flour gels and enables its broader use in foods with desired texture properties. Notably, heat-moisture treatment with moisture content of 15 g/100 g was identified as the optimal condition. HMT-15 exhibited higher PV, FV, and springiness, while its gel hardness and syneresis were comparable to those of NAF, indicating promising applicability in porridge, jelly, and noodle products. Future work will involve lipid profiling to clarify the mechanisms underlying heat-moisture treatment and validate the optimal processing conditions in targeted food systems.
