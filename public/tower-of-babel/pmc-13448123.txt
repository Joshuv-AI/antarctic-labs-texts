A Commentary on “A Nationwide Registry Study Comparing Long-term Risk of Adjacent Segment Disease Requiring Reoperation Following Anterior Cervical Discectomy and Fusion Versus Cervical Disc Arthroplasty”
Authors: Samuel K Cho
Published: 2026

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) has long been the workhorse for degenerative cervical disc disease. However, the specter of adjacent segment disease (ASD) remains a persistent clinical concern. While cervical disc arthroplasty (CDA) emerged as a motion-preserving alternative theoretically poised to mitigate biomechanical stress at adjacent levels [1], the literature has presented conflicting evidence regarding its long-term efficacy in preventing ASD [2,3].

In this issue of Neurospine, the authors [4] present a compelling, nationwide retrospective cohort study utilizing the Swedish Spine Registry (Swespine) to evaluate the long-term risk of ASD requiring reoperation following ACDF versus CDA. The scale and longitudinal nature of this study are its greatest strengths. Analyzing 9,338 patients—comprising 9,005 ACDF and 333 CDA procedures—over an 18.8-year period is a monumental task that provides much-needed real-world epidemiological data.

The findings of this study provide a tempered perspective on historical benchmarks. The study demonstrates that the cumulative incidence of clinically significant ASD requiring reoperation was 4.9% at 5 years and 10.4% at 10 years. While this is notably lower than some foundational studies that estimated symptomatic ASD incidence to reach up to 26% over a decade [5], this difference is significantly influenced by the study’s strict outcome definition. Since the registry data only captured cases that required subsequent surgical intervention 12 months after the index operation, it omits asymptomatic radiological ASD and conservatively managed ASD. Despite this, the lower incidence is a reassuring data point for counseling patients, suggesting that the true burden of ASD severe enough to necessitate secondary surgery is relatively modest.

The core comparison between CDA and ACDF yields nuanced insights. The multivariable Cox regression demonstrated a significantly lower hazard of ASD for CDA (hazard ratio 0.61). Yet, as the authors rightly acknowledge, the absolute risk difference is small. Furthermore, a propensity score-matched sensitivity analysis failed to show a statistically significant difference in ASD risk between the 2 procedures. This loss of significance likely stems from the reduced sample size in the matched cohort—which consisted of 325 CDA patients matched to 650 ACDF patients—but it also underscores the inherent difficulty in directly comparing these techniques without randomized control over strict indications.

The demographic disparities between the 2 surgical groups highlight classic selection bias in spine surgery:

• The CDA cohort was significantly younger, with a mean age of 45.6 years compared to 51.9 years for the ACDF group.

• Patients receiving CDA were predominantly treated for radiculopathy (74% vs. 57%).

• The CDA group overwhelmingly underwent single-level procedures (74% vs. 58%).

Interestingly, the study found that increasing age and multilevel procedures were associated with a lower risk of ASD [4]. The lower risk in older patients might reflect a higher threshold for reoperation or lower functional demands, while the protective effect of multilevel surgery likely relates to fewer remaining adjacent segments being left at risk. Conversely, patients with myelopathy or stenosis faced a 22% higher hazard for ASD compared to those with radiculopathy, possibly reflecting a more systemic degenerative cascade and the biomechanical strain of extensive decompressions.

In the ongoing global discourse on degenerative cervical spine pathology, this study reinforces that while CDA is a viable and potentially protective alternative, ACDF remains an exceptionally reliable procedure with a lower long-term surgical ASD burden than historically feared. The surgical decision must remain highly individualized, weighing the patient’s specific pathology, age, and functional goals against the modest absolute risk reduction offered by arthroplasty.
