Yixin Tongbi formula as a therapeutic approach for atherosclerosis by targeting inflammatory mediators and metalloproteinases
Authors: Qian Nie, Haseeb Sattar, Tao Leng, Rui Wang, Ailing Huang, Xie Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the molecular mechanisms of Yixin Tongbi Formula (YTF) in intervening in atherosclerotic plaques. Thirty-six healthy SD rats were randomly divided into six groups: normal control, model control, low-dose YTF, medium-dose YTF, high-dose YTF, and Atorvastatin groups, with six rats in each. All groups, except for the normal control, were administered a high-fat diet mixed with Vitamin D3 to establish an atherosclerosis (AS) model. After successful model establishment, confirmed by sampling three rats, the treatment groups were administered 10 g/kg, 20 g/kg, and 40 g/kg of YTF, respectively, and the Atorvastatin group received 5 mg/kg of Atorvastatin, all once daily for 14 consecutive days. Compared to the normal control group, the model control group showed significant thickening of the aortic walls, and increased levels of TC, TG, HDL, and LDL in serum, as well as elevated levels of MMP-2, MMP-9, IL-6, TNF-α, and ICAM-1 in aortic tissues. Expression of Bax in myocardial tissue increased, while Bcl-2 decreased. These changes were completely reversed by treatment with YTF, with the high-dose group showing the most significant effects. YTF inhibits the development of AS by suppressing inflammation and cell apoptosis and improving plaque stability.Keywords: Yixin tongbi, Atherosclerosis, Metalloproteinases, Statins, Chinese medicineSubject terms: Drug discovery, Cardiology, Medical research

Introduction

With rising living standards, atherosclerosis (AS) has become a major risk factor and the principal pathological basis for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events. Over recent decades, AS has increasingly drawn attention, with its prevalence projected to rise by 18% by 20301,2. AS is typically categorized based on the location of lesions into carotid atherosclerosis (CAS), coronary AS, and lower limb AS, among others, with carotid arteries being one of the most commonly affected sites. Notably, extracranial carotid AS is closely associated with cerebral infarction in 20–25% of patients. AS is a leading cause of adult cardiovascular diseases, leading to myocardial infarction or stroke, and ultimately death. Diseases associated with AS, such as ischemic heart disease and stroke, have high incidence and mortality rates and often poor prognoses, imposing significant physical and economic burdens on patients3. Thus, exploring the pathogenesis and effective treatment methods of AS is of great importance.

The formation of AS plaques and the dislodging or rupture of vulnerable plaques, accompanied by platelet aggregation and thrombosis, are key mechanisms in the pathogenesis of clinical cardiovascular diseases. AS plaques primarily consist of a fibrous cap and lipid core, characterized by the proliferation of smooth muscle cells (SMCs) and infiltration of inflammatory cells. Numerous studies have identified AS as a chronic inflammatory disease, with inflammation playing a critical role throughout its development and closely associated with various complications4,5. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), particularly important in matrix degradation, facilitate the migration of immune-related macrophages and vascular SMCs within the intima by degrading the endothelial basement membrane. Macrophages invade tissues via various pathways and can damage AS plaques, highlighting that MMPs can exacerbate the severity of AS plaques by modulating cellular immunity6.

In recent years, an increasing number of clinical studies have explored new anti-AS therapies based on anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory approaches. These studies have shown that Western medicines are effective in regulating lipids, reducing inflammation, and preventing platelet aggregation; however, they come with drawbacks such as muscle pain, hepatic and renal toxicity, increased transaminases, high production costs, and singular therapeutic targets. In contrast, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has demonstrated clear efficacy in treating AS with fewer side effects7. From a holistic perspective, TCM can impact multiple aspects of disease progression. Currently, TCM’s role in preventing and treating AS through immune-inflammatory pathways is a focal area of research. Many studies have found that qi deficiency and phlegm stasis are common symptoms in CAS patients, often treated with methods that invigorate blood, remove stasis, dispel phlegm, and boost qi8.

The YTF, composed of Astragalus membranaceus, Salvia miltiorrhiza, Ligusticum chuanxiong, and Allium macrostemon, is used clinically. Astragalus replenishes qi and vital energy, promotes blood circulation, and transforms phlegm; Salvia miltiorrhiza invigorates blood and dispels stasis; Ligusticum chuanxiong moves qi and invigorates blood while relieving pain; Allium macrostemon aids in qi regulation and disperses clumps. Collectively, these herbs work together to replenish qi, nourish blood, invigorate circulation, and alleviate symptoms of qi deficiency and blood stasis. Clinical studies have confirmed the effectiveness of YTF in preventing and treating coronary AS diseases and atrial fibrillation combined with coronary heart disease. However, the mechanisms by which YTF treats AS remain unclear. Therefore, this study aims to explore the efficacy and molecular mechanisms of YTF in treating AS based on levels of inflammatory factors and MMPs, providing a theoretical basis for the clinical treatment of AS with TCM9.

Methods

YTF Decoction Preparation

Preparation of Experimental Drugs YTF was prepared by weighing and mixing 30 g of Astragalus membranaceus, 30 g of Salvia miltiorrhiza, 18 g of Ligusticum chuanxiong, and 18 g of Allium macrostemon. The mixture was heated in a water bath concentrated to a final concentration of 2 g/mL of raw herbs and stored at 4 °C for future use. The doses of Yixin Tongmai Decoction (10 g/kg, 20 g/kg, 40 g/kg) were chosen based on previous studies17.

Grouping, modelling, and intervention treatment

All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The experimental procedure, modelling, and interventional treatment were approved and carried out after approval from the Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine ethical and research committee.

Establishment of the atherosclerosis model

36 SD rats (190 to 200 g) were acclimatized for one week and then randomly divided into a normal control group (6 rats) and a model group (30 rats). The model of atherosclerosis in rats was established using a high-fat diet mixed with vitamin D3 administered via gavage. To establish a rapid AS model, rats were given 7000U/g of vitamin D3 for three consecutive days combined with a high-fat diet, followed by a high-fat diet al.one for 18 more days, totaling 21 days24. The successful establishment of the model was confirmed by observing significant thickening of the abdominal aortic walls via H&E staining.

Animal grouping and medication administration

After successful modelling, the 30 rats were further divided into a model control group, a low-dose YTF group, a medium-dose YTF group, a high-dose YTF group, and an Atorvastatin group, with six rats in each group. Following modelling, treatments were initiated: low, medium, and high doses of YTF were administered at 10 g/kg, 20 g/kg, and 40 g/kg, respectively; the Atorvastatin group received 5 mg/kg of Atorvastatin; both the model and normal control groups received a placebo, administered once daily for 14 consecutive days.

Histological examination of aortic tissues

24 h after the last dose, rats were euthanised by carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation technique by gradual fill method, and CO2 was introduced at a displacement rate of 30% of the chamber volume per minute, followed by cervical dislocation according to the AVMA guidelines21. Aortic tissues were collected and fixed in 4% neutral formaldehyde. Following dehydration in graded alcohol, clearing in xylene, and embedding in paraffin, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Changes in the overall structure and morphology of arterial plaques were observed under a microscope, photographed, and recorded.

Biochemical analysis

Post-treatment, abdominal aortic blood was collected. After centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 15 min, the serum was analyzed for total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) using an automatic biochemical analyzer.

ELISA measurement of inflammatory markers in aortic tissue

Following euthanasia, aortic tissues (20–30 mg) were collected and homogenised in PBS containing protease inhibitors. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) were measured using ELISA kits.

Western blot analysis of protein expression

After euthanasia, myocardial and aortic tissues were collected, and total proteins were extracted and quantified using a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against β-actin, MMP-2, MMP-9, Bax, and Bcl-2 overnight at 4 °C. Following secondary antibody incubation and washing, protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence. Images were captured using a Bio-Rad imaging system, and densitometric analysis was performed using Image-Pro Plus software, with β-actin serving as a loading control.

Statistical methods

Data were analyzed using SPSS 20.0 software and presented as mean ± SD. Differences between groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA, and the least significant difference (LSD) test was used for post-hoc comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The thickness of the aortic wall in H&E-stained sections was quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, USA).

Experimental results

Impact of YTF on atherosclerotic plaque formation in rats

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed significant thickening of the aortic walls in the model control group compared to the normal control. The outer layer was thicker with no significant necrosis or infiltration of inflammatory cells. The media layer showed extensive necrosis, with disrupted elastic fibers, disorganized structure, necrotic smooth muscle cells, nuclear pyknosis, karyorrhexis, and cytoplasmic dissolution, leading to localized calcification. The inner layer was notably thickened, forming large calcified areas surrounded by proliferating fibrous tissue and numerous chondrocytes, with endothelial cell loss and elastic fiber disruption, indicating severe pathological deterioration post-modeling. Compared to the model control, both the high-dose YTF and Atorvastatin groups showed a thicker outer layer primarily composed of collagen fibers without significant necrosis or inflammatory cell infiltration; the media layer was more loosely organized, creating more spaces; the intima was thinner, with some endothelial cells sloughing off into the lumen. These changes were milder in these groups than in the model control, with the medium-dose YTF group showing intermediate improvements and the low-dose group exhibiting slight pathological changes (See Fig. 1).

Effects of YTF on serum lipids in atherosclerotic rats

Compared to the normal control group, the model control group exhibited increased levels of total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) (P < 0.01). The high and medium doses of YTF and the Atorvastatin group had lower levels of TC, TG, HDL, and LDL compared to the model control group (P < 0.05). The low-dose YTF group showed no significant difference in these parameters compared to the model control group (P > 0.05) (See Fig. 2).

Impact of YTF on inflammatory markers IL-6, TNF-α, and ICAM-1 in atherosclerotic rats

Compared to the normal control group, levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and ICAM-1 were elevated in the aortic tissues of the model control group (P < 0.01). The medium and high doses of YTF and the Atorvastatin group showed lower levels of these markers compared to the model control group (P < 0.01). The low-dose YTF group had lower levels of IL-6 and ICAM-1 compared to the model control group (P < 0.05), with no significant difference in TNF-α levels between the two groups (P > 0.05) (See Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study determines that YTF improves lipid profiles, reduces vascular inflammation, and inhibits matrix remodeling in AS model rats. YTF significantly reduced IL-6, TNF-α, and ICAM-1 levels, consistent with previous studies highlighting their role in AS pathogenesis22. Additionally, YTF suppressed MMP-2/9 expression, aligning with previously established studies, linking these enzymes to plaque vulnerability23. By reducing apoptosis, such as lowering Bax, higher Bcl-2 expression, YTF can provide vascular protection. Collectively, these findings suggest YTF exerts multi-targeted protective effects in AS.

YTF is known for its efficacy in invigorating qi, promoting blood circulation, and alleviating pain. Modern pharmacological studies have demonstrated its protective roles in anti-thrombosis, reducing whole blood viscosity, and preventing platelet aggregation. Atherosclerosis (AS) is the most common and crucial form of arteriosclerotic vascular disease, characterized mainly by the thickening of the arterial intima and the formation of atheromatous plaques10. The development of these plaques is a process filled with inflammatory responses, occurring throughout various stages of AS, where inflammatory factors play a pivotal role11. Among them, IL-6 and TNF-α, secreted by macrophages, endothelial cells, and smooth muscle cells, promote inflammation. IL-6 can induce acute inflammatory responses downstream in AS, reducing the activation of plasminogen which leads to continual platelet aggregation and increased thrombosis. TNF-α, associated with infections and inflammatory responses, can penetrate the vascular intima, activate macrophages, and form foam cells or lipid pools. ICAM-1, primarily secreted by arterial endothelial cells, when downregulated, can effectively alleviate the formation of AS12,13. This study found that YTF significantly reduces the levels of these pro-inflammatory factors, indicating its anti-inflammatory properties14. YTF may inhibit the activity of the IκB kinase (IKK) complex, preventing the phosphorylation and degradation of IκB proteins and NF-κB retained in the cytoplasm and cannot enter the nucleus to initiate the transcription of pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β), thereby reducing the release of inflammatory mediators. The formation of AS is closely related to abnormal lipid levels, which may be an independent risk factor. LDL and TC are key markers for diagnosing AS15. Lipid levels, including TC, TG, HDL, and LDL, reflect the health of blood vessels where LDL can oxidize into ox-LDL after endothelial damage, stimulating the production of a large number of inflammatory factors, thus causing vascular constriction and endothelial dysfunction16. On the other hand, HDL has roles in reducing cholesterol levels and anti-oxidation and anti-inflammatory actions. Lipid dysregulation can lead to various diseases. Patients with lipid abnormalities have a higher incidence of AS, and excessive deposition of TC and cholesterol in the intima and media is a pathological basis for AS17. High cholesterol levels significantly influence the formation of AS, and reducing cholesterol can decrease the relative risk of stroke. Research indicates that matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) reflect the pathophysiology of vulnerable plaques well and can be used to predict plaque vulnerability18. Levels and expression intensities of MMPs such as MMP-1, MMP-2, and MMP-9 can determine the severity of vulnerable plaques. MMP-2 and MMP-9, which can degrade collagen and elastin in the extracellular matrix, play crucial roles in the synthesis and degradation balance of the ECM, closely related to plaque formation and stability19. Studies have found that MMP-9 contributes to AS development through the mediation of vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, apoptosis, and degradation of the extracellular matrix, closely linked to the thickening of the intima and instability of carotid artery plaques20. High expression levels of MMP-9 can serve as indicators of the stability of internal carotid AS. MMP-2 plays an important role in the pathological process of unstable plaque formation and is considered a target molecule for treating AS. Inhibiting the expression of MMP-2/MMP-9 can reduce inflammatory responses, thereby enhancing the stability of atherosclerotic plaques. This study employed a method of gavage with high-fat vitamin D3 mixed feed to establish an AS model in rats, finding significant thickening of the aortic walls post-modeling and upregulated expression of MMP-2 and MMP-9 in aortic tissues, with an increased ratio of cell apoptosis in myocardial tissues. Treatment with different doses of YTF resulted in varied degrees of reduction in MMP content and apoptosis rates, with the high-dose group showing the best therapeutic effect.

Conclusion

In conclusion, YTF effectively reduced the thickness of the aortic walls in AS rats while suppressing inflammation and cell apoptosis. It significantly improved the lipid metabolism, suppressed the expression of metalloproteinases, MMP-2/MMP-9, and reduced the pro-inflammatory markers, i.e. IL-6, ICAM-1 and TNF-α, thus enhancing the plaque stability and reducing apoptosis by lowering Bax and increasing Bcl-2 expression. These findings suggest that YTF exerts therapeutic effects through its anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and matrix-stabilizing mechanisms. Further studies and human clinical trials may be useful to confirm the effectiveness of YTF in patients suffering from coronary artery plaques.

Limitations

Although this study provides new insights into the mechanisms of Yixin Tongmai Decoction in treating AS, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively small, with only 6 rats in each group, which may affect the statistical power of the results. Future studies should consider increasing the sample size to enhance the reliability and generalizability of the findings. Second, the experimental duration was relatively short, lasting only 14 days, which may not be sufficient to fully evaluate the long-term effects of Yixin Tongmai Decoction on AS plaques. Future research should extend the experimental period to observe the therapeutic effects and potential side effects of Yixin Tongmai Decoction over a longer timeframe. Additionally, there are significant differences between the animal model used in this study and human AS pathology. The rat AS model was induced primarily through a high-fat diet and vitamin D3 supplementation, which, while capable of simulating certain features of AS, still differs significantly from the complex pathological processes of human AS, such as plaque instability and the diversity of inflammatory responses. Therefore, caution is needed when directly applying these findings to humans. Future studies should incorporate the pathological characteristics of human AS to further validate the efficacy and safety of Yixin Tongmai Decoction in clinical settings.
