Long-term immune protection after infant hepatitis B vaccination in children
Authors: Rodman Tarigan Girsang, Eddy Fadlyana, Muhammad Gilang Dwi Putra, Kusnandi Rusmil, Budi Setiabudiawan, Cissy B Kartasamita, Tressia Febfi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection in endemic regions often begins in infancy and persists throughout life. Although anti-HBs antibody levels may decline over time, long-term protection usually remains. The me chanisms underlying this sustained protection are not fully understood. Evidence from studies conducted up to 20 ypost-vaccination indicates that booster doses are unnecessary for immunocompetent individuals who completed the full vaccination series as recommended. Therefore, reviewing HBV vaccination studies in children is crucial to update current knowledge on long-term immunity and evaluate the program’s strengths and weaknesses. This literature review analyzed studies published from 2013 to 2023 using PubMed, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect, and Scopus databases with relevant keywords. Selection followed PRISMA guidelines. Twenty-seven studies demonstrated protective effects exceeding 10 y, though antibody levels were lower in adolescents. Booster vaccination during adolescence may be beneficial, especially for those born to HBsAg-positive mothers.KEYWORDS: Hepatitis B, vaccine in children, long-term protection

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a major global public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, contributing substantially to chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma.1–3 HBV is a partially double-stranded DNA virus of the Hepadnaviridae family that primarily infects hepatocytes, entering cells via the sodium taurocholate co-transporting polypeptide (NTCP) receptor.4 After entry, viral DNA is transported to the nucleus, forming covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA), which serves as a template for viral transcription and replication.4,5 The immune response to HBV involves both innate and adaptive mechanisms: cytotoxic T lymphocytes target infected hepatocytes, while B cells produce neutralizing anti-HBs antibodies that confer protective immunity.6 Chronic infection develops when the immune response fails to eliminate infected cells or when viral replication overwhelms host defenses, leading to persistent hepatitis, liver inflammation, and progressive fibrosis.3,7 Infant vaccination induces both humoral and cellular immunity, preventing viral entry, replication, and subsequent chronic liver disease.8

Chronic HBV infection in endemic areas usually begins in infancy and early childhood and continues throughout life. Most chronically HBV-infected children initially show a state of immune tolerance, undergo an immune clearance phase with varying degrees of liver damage during or after puberty, and enter an inactivity phase after HBV e antigen (HBeAg) seroconversion.2 Cirrhosis and HCC may develop later. An estimated 3.6% of the global population (248 million individuals) are HBV surface antigen (HBsAg) positive.3

HBV vaccination is highly effective. However, vaccination failures have been reported, which can result in acute and chronic infections. Infections shortly after vaccination likely reflect primary vaccination failures due to non-response, whereas infections that occur decades later reflect secondary vaccination failures due to weakened immunity.4 An anti-HBs level ≥10 mIU/mL is expected after a primary HBV vaccination series or a booster HBV vaccine dose if the anti-HBs level falls below that threshold. It is widely accepted as a post-inoculation defense marker. Immunity against HBV protects against infection and disease.5–7 Protection against infection is based on anti-HBs levels >10 mIU/mL. After HBV vaccination, the proportion of individuals with an anti-HBs level ≥10 mIU/mL highly depends on the elapsed time from primary immunization.3–5

An individual whose anti-HBs level falls below 10 mIU/mL is no longer protected against HBV infection.2 However, they are not at risk of liver disease, provided they have HBsAg-specific immunological memory. Memory persists beyond the presence of anti-HBs and protects against clinically relevant disease, prolonged viremia, and chronic infections.3,4 An anamnestic anti-HBs response after a booster vaccine dose indicates specific memory after HBV vaccination. This booster dose rapidly increases anti-HBs levels in most individuals who were successfully vaccinated initially. The persistence of immunological memory also appears to be related to time after primary vaccination.3

Review studies of the HBV vaccine in children are needed to establish an update concerning the knowledge of this method for long-term protection and review its strengths and weaknesses. This study aims to provide a comprehensive evaluation of long-term hepatitis B vaccine efficacy, supporting evidence-based recommendations for booster administration and program optimization globally and in endemic regions.

Materials and methods

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed using the electronic databases PubMed, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect, and Scopus. The search terms included combinations of: “hepatitis B vaccine,” “long-term protection of hepatitis B vaccine,” and “hepatitis B vaccine in children.” Boolean operators (AND, OR) were applied to refine search results. To ensure completeness, references cited in included full-text articles were manually screened to identify additional relevant studies not captured by the database search. The search was conducted independently by three authors between January 2024–March 2024 and results were exported into a reference manager EndNote for deduplication.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligibility of studies was determined using the PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome, Study design) in accordance with PRISMA guidelines.1

Inclusion criteria including children receiving a primary HBV vaccination series, children receiving an HBV booster dose, children with HBV infection prior to vaccination and young adults with documented HBV vaccination history. Exclusion criteria including case reports, meeting abstracts, conference reports, editorial comments, reviews, including systematic reviews and meta-analyses, articles with irrelevant outcomes or results, duplicates of previously published studies, articles not published in English and articles inaccessible in full text.

Literature review process

After removing duplicates and excluded studies, titles and abstracts were screened independently by three authors for relevance. Full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were then retrieved and reviewed. For each included study, the following data were extracted systematically into a predesigned spreadsheet: Author(s) and year of publication, Country of origin, Study design, Sample size, Mean follow-up duration (months), Primary outcomes: overall success rate of HBV vaccination, Secondary outcomes: HBV recurrence or breakthrough infection. Disagreements during screening or data extraction were resolved through discussion among all authors. The study selection process was documented using a PRISMA flow diagram, showing the number of records identified, screened, excluded, and finally included for qualitative synthesis (Figure 1). This ensures transparency and reproducibility for readers attempting to replicate the review process.

PRISMA diagram describing the literature search process for literature selection and review.

Study selection and data extraction

Three authors reviewed the title and abstract of each candidate study from the literature search and, if necessary, their full text to determine its eligibility for inclusion in the quality evidence synthesis. Disagreements were settled through discussion by all authors. The data from eligible studies were extracted into a summary table. Each included study was evaluated for methodological quality using an appropriate tool (e.g., Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for randomized trials or Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for observational studies). Studies were assigned scores for selection bias, comparability, and outcome assessment to determine risk of bias and confidence in the evidence.

Results

The Google Scholar, PubMed, Science Direct, and Scopus databases were searched using the keywords ‘long-term hepatitis B vaccine protection,’ ‘long-term hepatitis B vaccine protection in neonatal,’ ‘long-term Hepatitis B vaccine protection in infant,’ ‘long-term hepatitis B vaccine protection in children,’ and ‘long-term Hepatitis B vaccine protection in adolescent,’ yielding 90,743 results. Next, articles that were duplicates, irrelevant, or published over 10 y ago were excluded, yielding 500 articles for further analysis. Then, case reports, abstracts, meeting reports, conference reports, editorial comments, reviews, non-primary HBV vaccine studies, and studies not listing outcomes were excluded, yielding 27 articles for further analysis. The studies included in the literature review comprised 48,978 participants who received a targeted or universal HBV vaccine.

Of the 27 studies that met the inclusion criteria (Table 1), most originated from Asia, including China, Malaysia, Thailand, Taiwan, Hong Kong, South Korea, and Singapore. The age of their participants varied substantially from 1 to ≥20 y. Sixteen were cross-sectional seroprevalence survey studies evaluating the efficacy of universal vaccination. Six cross-sectional studies only evaluated targeted vaccination, of which two used a repeated cross-sectional design. Five cross-sectional studies evaluated both targeted and universal vaccination.

Long-term HBV vaccination studies.

We examined 14 studies reporting the anti-HBs positivity rate at specific ages or follow-up times to assess efficacy in protecting children from HBV infection (Table 2). Ang et al. studied 1,200 subjects divided equally into three age groups (1–6, 7–12, and 13–17 y). The subjects were tested for HBsAg and anti-HBsAg. About 40% had an anti-HBs level ≥10 mIU/mL; the antibody prevalence decreased significantly from 63.8% in children aged 1–6 y to 32.8% in children aged 7–12 y and 23.5% in children aged 13–17 y (P < .0005).4 Similarly, Gilca et al. found that more than 94% of subjects had detectable antibodies in all three study groups, with the seroprotective titer decreasing significantly with age at five (88.2%), 10 (86.4%), and 15 (76.7%) years post-primary vaccination (p = .0025).15 Kim et al. in a large cohort study, reported that 62.6% of children born before 2005 had anti-HBs ≥10 IU/L, whereas 78.1% of children born after 2005 maintained seroprotection.20

Discussion

This review situates hepatitis B virus (HBV) immunization within the broader framework of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3, which targets reductions in infant and child mortality and the elimination of viral hepatitis as a public health threat by 2030.1,2 Despite the adoption of the World Health Organization’s (WHO) Global Health Sector Strategy on viral hepatitis in 2016 and the establishment of national hepatitis control programs, Indonesia remains off track to achieve HBV elimination targets according to Polaris Observatory estimates.3,4 Persistently suboptimal immunization coverage, widening inequities across provinces, and disruptions during the COVID-19 pandemic underscore the need for strengthened, evidence-informed policy responses.12,13,27

We examined four studies that compared vaccine groups (Table 4). Avdicova et al. compared two vaccine groups, reporting different means for each. Ng et al. showed an appreciable difference between groups based on the universal vaccination program. The positive rate was >80% in the non-universal vaccination group but only 33.8% in the universal vaccination group. Romano et al. reported a higher positive response in the adolescent group than in the infant group. Zanetti et al. compared two vaccine types, showing a higher positive response in the Infanrix-Hexa group (81.3%).

Indonesia introduced universal childhood immunization through the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) in 1977, with hepatitis B included as a core antigen.10,11 Nevertheless, a substantial proportion of children have not completed the basic immunization schedule, and the proportion of zero-dose and under-immunized children has increased in recent years. National data indicate that nearly half of Indonesian children did not complete basic immunization in 2012, with a further decline reported in 2018, and only around one-third of provinces achieving high coverage.12 These gaps reflect multifactorial barriers, including healthcare access, socioeconomic disparities, parental education, misinformation, and sociocultural or religious considerations.10–12 The decline in coverage during the COVID-19 pandemic further highlights system fragility and the importance of resilient primary healthcare systems and effective catch-up strategies.13

Evidence synthesized in this review demonstrates that universal HBV immunization is effective in inducing protective immunity in childhood. Across studies reporting age-specific anti-HBs positivity, the majority of vaccinated children achieved antibody levels ≥10 mIU/mL, indicating seroprotection.2,4–6,8,11,15,20,25 However, several studies consistently showed a decline in measurable anti-HBs titers beginning approximately 6–10 y after primary vaccination.4,6,15 This decline, observed across diverse settings and vaccine formulations, reflects the natural waning of circulating antibodies rather than a loss of immune memory.30

Importantly, long-term follow-up studies indicate that primary HBV vaccination confers durable protection against clinically significant infection for decades, with evidence of protection extending beyond 30 y in both children and adults.30,31 Individuals with anti-HBs levels <10 mIU/mL or undetectable titers frequently demonstrate a rapid anamnestic response upon booster challenge, suggesting preserved immunological memory.31 Breakthrough infections with severe clinical outcomes such as acute hepatitis B or progression to chronic carriage remain exceedingly rare among successfully vaccinated individuals. On this basis, routine booster doses are not recommended for the general population who completed a full primary vaccination series in infancy.30–32

Nevertheless, specific high-risk groups may warrant tailored strategies. Studies from endemic settings indicate that individuals born to HBsAg-positive mothers, particularly those with high maternal viral loads, may have a higher risk of HBV infection later in life despite infant vaccination.33–36 Evidence from China suggests that an adolescent booster dose can enhance protection in these high-risk cohorts, especially when anti-HBs titers fall below 10 mIU/mL prior to sexual debut.33,34 These findings support a risk-based rather than universal booster policy, aligned with WHO recommendations that emphasize targeted interventions for populations at increased risk of exposure.30,32

The effectiveness of HBV immunization programs is also influenced by vaccine formulation and program implementation. Comparative studies identified differences in immunogenicity between vaccine products, exemplified by lower long-term antibody responses associated with Hexavac, which subsequently led to its withdrawal in several countries.16 Additionally, socioeconomic context appears to modify program effectiveness in some settings. Studies from China reported lower seroprotection in regions with limited resources and healthcare access,37,38 although this association was not uniformly observed, as other studies found no significant correlation between socioeconomic factors and protective antibody levels.39

Two studies comparing vaccination groups across different cities or districts demonstrated considerable variation in seroprotection rates, reflecting local epidemiology, vaccine delivery, and healthcare infrastructure. In Egypt, Salama et al. reported no significant differences between urban and rural areas, with anti-HBs positivity ranging from 16.3% in Mansoura City to 26.4% in Talkha Village.23 This suggests that within this study population, access to vaccination and adherence to immunization schedules were relatively uniform across districts.

In contrast, Wang et al. reported marked differences among districts in China, with anti-HBs positivity ranging from 42.0% in Longan District to 80.95% in Tongde District.24 The highest seroprotection was observed in Tongde District (80.95%), likely reflecting better vaccine program implementation, higher coverage, and stronger follow-up systems in this area. Conversely, districts with lower positivity rates may have faced challenges such as limited healthcare access, socioeconomic constraints, or suboptimal cold-chain maintenance, which can reduce vaccine immunogenicity.

Overall, the higher positive rate observed in certain districts in China compared with Egypt may be explained by differences in vaccination program efficiency, population density, health system infrastructure, and local public health initiatives. While socioeconomic factors did not correlate significantly with protective antibody levels in Egypt, in China, areas with limited resources and healthcare access showed lower seroprotection, indicating that program quality and delivery efficiency can be as important as socioeconomic status in determining long-term vaccine-induced immunity.23,24 These findings emphasize that regional variability must be considered when assessing long-term hepatitis B vaccine effectiveness. Public health authorities should account for local differences in healthcare access, follow-up monitoring, and program implementation to optimize booster strategies and maintain adequate population immunity.

Taken together, these findings reinforce that universal HBV immunization remains the cornerstone of hepatitis B elimination efforts. In Indonesia, priorities should include improving timely birth-dose coverage, ensuring completion of the full vaccine schedule, strengthening maternal screening for HBsAg, and optimizing perinatal interventions such as antiviral therapy for highly viremic mothers and combined active–passive immunization for exposed neonates.30 Enhanced surveillance systems are needed to monitor long-term vaccine effectiveness, population immunity, and epidemiological trends, particularly as vaccinated cohorts age into adolescence and adulthood.31

This review has several limitations. The number of studies assessing long-term protection in children remains limited, and substantial heterogeneity exists in study design, age at assessment, follow-up duration, vaccine type, and laboratory thresholds for anti-HBs measurement. Most included studies were cross-sectional, which limits causal inference and precise estimation of antibody decay kinetics.2,4–6,15 These factors reduce direct comparability across studies and constrain the generalizability of pooled conclusions. Future research should focus on prospective longitudinal cohort studies tracking both humoral and cellular immunity over decades to better understand protection dynamics. Studies assessing booster efficacy, timing, and cost-effectiveness in high-risk populations will inform national and global immunization policy. Furthermore, integration of regional serosurveillance data can help identify areas with waning immunity or suboptimal coverage, guiding targeted interventions. Advanced modeling could predict the impact of booster strategies on HBV incidence and long-term liver disease outcomes. Finally, public health initiatives should incorporate community education and healthcare infrastructure improvements to ensure equitable vaccine access and long-term protection, especially in endemic regions.2,8,15,20

In conclusion, universal HBV vaccination in childhood provides robust and long-lasting protection against infection and severe disease, even as measurable antibody levels decline over time. For Indonesia to achieve HBV elimination by 2030, renewed commitment is required to close immunization gaps, address geographic and socioeconomic inequities, and implement targeted strategies for high-risk populations, supported by sustained surveillance and health system strengthening.1–4,30

Conclusion

We found that all 27 studies included in this review showed protection more than 10 y after vaccination with an anti-HBs level of 10 IU/L. Most studies showed a lower anti-HBs level in adolescents. A booster is needed during adolescence because of the lower anti-HBS level, especially for children born to HBsAg-positive mothers. Age, vaccine, vaccine type, and socioeconomic factors affected protection. Future studies require standardized anti-HBs groupings to provide commensurable results in every country. We recommend conducting a multicenter research study on long-term HBV protection to better understand protective antibody concentrations after HBV vaccination and further research on the HBV booster in adolescents. It remains imperative to target the elimination of HBV infection worldwide.
