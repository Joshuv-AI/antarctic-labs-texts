Performance of large language models in endodontics: accuracy, consistency, and benchmarking with consensus guidelines
Authors: Soner Sismanoglu, Vasfiye Isik, Mehmet Baybora Kayahan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLarge language model (LLM)-based chatbots are increasingly used in healthcare, yet their diagnostic accuracy, consistency, and temporal stability in endodontics remain insufficiently evaluated. This study aimed to assess and compare the performance of LLM-based chatbots using established international clinical guidelines.MethodsA diagnostic accuracy study with a repeated-measures design was conducted. Two LLMs (ChatGPT-5 and Gemini-2.5 Flash) were evaluated using 200 structured yes/no items derived from consensus-based international position statements covering the full scope of endodontic practice. Each model was tested weekly over three consecutive sessions, yielding 600 responses per model. Accuracy was assessed against reference answers, consistency was analyzed using Fleiss’ κ and Cohen’s κ, and logistic regression was performed to evaluate the effects of model type, week, and clinical domain.ResultsChatGPT demonstrated significantly higher overall accuracy than Gemini (92.8% vs. 84.8%; OR = 2.37; p = 0.004) and near-perfect reproducibility (κ = 0.95), whereas Gemini showed fair agreement (κ = 0.38). Both models performed well in structured clinical domains but showed reduced accuracy in areas related to restoration and pulpal disease management.ConclusionsLLM-based chatbots show potential as decision support and educational tools in endodontics. However, performance limitations in complex clinical domains highlight the continued need for expert oversight in clinical decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08269-8.Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, ChatGPT, Position Statements, Gemini, Large Language Models

Background

The use of artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare has evolved from an experimental concept to practical applications in many medical disciplines. In dentistry, AI technologies have demonstrated significant potential in image analysis, diagnostic support, treatment planning, and educational applications [1–3]. Machine learning algorithms, particularly deep learning networks, have achieved accuracy levels comparable to or exceeding those of human professionals in various diagnostic tasks, such as detecting dental caries, periapical lesions, and root fractures [4]. Recent bibliometric analyses reveal that research in this field has shown exponential growth, increasing from a single article published in 2000 to 120 articles in 2023 [5]. Therefore, evaluating the performance of chatbots in endodontics has emerged as a critical research priority. Previous studies have highlighted both their potential and limitations [6, 7]. A previous study examined the validity and reliability of AI chatbots as a source of endodontic information, reporting that GPT-3.5 performed better than other chatbots but showed only 60% validity (defined as the degree to which chatbot responses align with evidence-based knowledge) when evaluated with strict clinical criteria [8]. Similarly, another study evaluated ChatGPT-4 and reported a 57% accuracy rate for yes/no questions related to endodontics, noting that this highlights the ongoing challenges in clinical decision-making tasks [9]. More recent comparative studies have expanded the scope to evaluate multiple platforms simultaneously and reported significant performance differences between systems; accuracy rates ranged from 48% to 97.7%, depending on model type and question complexity [10, 11]. To ensure a broad and standardised reference framework, this study evaluated chatbot performance against consensus-based position statements that cover core domains of endodontic practice [12–22].

The development of robust evaluation frameworks for AI systems in healthcare is becoming increasingly urgent as these technologies approach clinical applications. Recent research highlights the need for comprehensive evaluation strategies that encompass not only accuracy but also clinical appropriateness, safety, and real-world applicability [23]. Current approaches often consider multiple dimensions such as accuracy, completeness, clinical validity, and response consistency [6]. Furthermore, the importance of repeated measurements and temporal stability is highlighted; as AI systems in healthcare must demonstrate reliable performance across different clinical scenarios and over time [24].

Despite growing interest, comprehensive and standardized studies evaluating chatbot performance in endodontics by referencing established clinical guidelines remain insufficient. Previous studies have typically examined a single platform or used limited question sets that do not reflect the breadth of endodontic practice. This study systematically compares the two leading platforms, ChatGPT and Gemini, using a comprehensive set of consensus-based position statements as the reference framework, aiming to address this gap. Employing such documents as benchmarks aligns the evaluation with expert consensus, current best evidence, and internationally recognised practices. It should be noted that evaluating chatbot responses against clinical guidelines primarily reflects concordance with established recommendations rather than the ability to perform contextual clinical reasoning or decision-making in real patients. This distinction is essential for the appropriate interpretation of accuracy metrics in LLM-based studies and has been emphasized in both general medical AI evaluation frameworks and previous endodontic AI literature [6, 8, 9, 24].

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate and compare the accuracy and consistency of responses generated by ChatGPT and Gemini when answering yes/no questions derived from the ten position statements of the European Society of Endodontology (ESE) during consecutive testing sessions over a three-week period. The secondary objectives are to assess the temporal stability of each chatbot, identify the clinical areas where performance differences are most pronounced, and determine the overall reliability of these AI systems as a source of endodontic knowledge. Given the limited and variable findings in previous large language models (LLM) studies in endodontics and the relatively recent development of both platforms, the following hypotheses are proposed: (1) There will be no significant difference in overall accuracy between ChatGPT and Gemini in their responses to questions derived from ESE position statements; (2) both chatbots will demonstrate similar levels of temporal stability and consistency over the three-week testing period; and (3) performance will not show meaningful differences across clinical domains, with both systems exhibiting comparable accuracy across all ESE position statements, including complex topics such as restorations and pulpal disease management.

Materials and methods

Study design and objective

This diagnostic accuracy study has been written in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Diagnostic Accuracy Studies in Endodontics (PRIDASE) 2024 guidelines. In the present context, the term ‘diagnostic accuracy’ is used in a methodological sense to describe the degree of concordance between chatbot responses and reference answers derived from consensus-based clinical guidelines using dichotomous items. This study does not evaluate clinical diagnoses in patients or real-world diagnostic performance. This study is a diagnostic accuracy study designed to evaluate the performance of LLM-based chatbots in endodontics, incorporating repeated measurements. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Human participants were not involved, and no ethical committee approval was required as no patient data or animals were used. The primary objective was to evaluate the accuracy of ChatGPT (version 5) and Gemini (version 2.5 Flash) when responding to items derived from the ten position statements of the ESE and to determine the consistency of their responses in repeated applications. All chatbot evaluations were conducted using the versions available through their respective official web-based interfaces during the data collection period (August-September 2025). ChatGPT-5 was accessed via the Pro subscription (chat.openai.com), and Gemini-2.5 Flash was accessed via the Advanced subscription (gemini.google.com). Both platforms were used with their default generation settings; no system prompt modifications, custom instructions, or parameter adjustments (e.g., temperature, top-p) were applied, as these parameters are not user-configurable through the standard web interfaces. No model version updates were released on either platform during the three-week data collection period. Given the continuous updates of large language models, the findings represent a time-specific snapshot of model performance rather than fixed or permanent capabilities.

Consensus-based international position statements were selected as the reference framework, as they provide evidence-informed guidance widely adopted in clinical endodontic practice [12–22]. By converting the recommendations in these statements into structured yes/no items, the aim was to compare chatbot responses against established professional standards rather than individual expert opinions. This conversion process involves a deliberate simplification of the original guideline content, which facilitates objective and reproducible scoring but inherently limits the clinical complexity that can be assessed through this format.

To illustrate this conversion process, the following examples demonstrate how guideline recommendations were transformed into yes/no items: (1) The ESE antibiotics statement [20] specifies that systemic antibiotics are indicated only in cases with systemic involvement such as elevated body temperature, malaise, or lymphadenopathy; this was converted to: ‘Is systemic antibiotic treatment indicated for symptomatic irreversible pulpitis without systemic involvement?’ (expected answer: No). (2) The ESE deep caries statement [17] recommends selective carious-tissue removal to firm dentine for deep caries lesions; this yielded the item: ‘Is selective carious-tissue removal (to soft/firm dentine) recommended for reversible pulpitis?’ (expected answer: Yes). (3) The ESE restoration statement [15] notes that ‘wherever possible, clinicians should make every effort to obtain an optimal ferrule’; this was converted to: ‘Is a 2 mm ferrule height generally recommended for root-filled teeth?’ (expected answer: Yes). These examples demonstrate that while some conversions are straightforward, others involve a degree of simplification of conditional language inherent to the binary format.

Each chatbot was tested with the same set of 200 items once a week for three consecutive weeks, thus obtaining a dataset consisting of repeated measurements. This design allowed not only a cross-sectional assessment of accuracy at a specific point in time but also the measurement of stability and reproducibility over time.

Item development and validation

The questionnaire was developed through a structured process to ensure content validity and clinical applicability. Statements covering areas such as “The use of antibiotics in endodontics”, “The use of CBCT in endodontics”, “External cervical resorption”, “Revitalization procedures”, “Root resorption”, “Endodontic management of traumatized permanent teeth”, “The restoration of root filled teeth”, and “Treatment of pulpal and apical disease” were taken directly from each of the ten ESE position statements and converted into yes/no items. This process resulted in 20 questions for each statement, for a total of 200 questions. This approach ensured that all items were based directly on official documents and avoided additional interpretation.

Two senior experts—one in endodontics and the other in restorative dentistry, each with over 10 years of clinical and academic experience—independently reviewed all questions. Their evaluations focused on the clarity of the statements, clinical appropriateness, and alignment with the relevant ESE statement. The same experts also verified that the correct answers directly matched with the source documents. In case of disagreement, the item and reference answer were discussed again; if no consensus was reached, the issue was referred to a third, more experienced senior author, who made the final decision. This process ensured reproducibility in the dataset by minimizing subjective interpretations. The yes/no format was deliberately chosen because it allows for objective scoring and reduces the uncertainty created by partially correct answers seen in open-ended formats.

Obtaining chatbot sessions and responses

Both chatbots were queried via their official web interfaces. To ensure session independence, all interactions were conducted in “temporary chat” (incognito) mode, available on both platforms. This feature prevents the storage of previous interactions, ensuring that each session starts without contextual memory.

All sessions were conducted by a single experienced operator to ensure standardization. Before each weekly round, 200 items were randomly sorted using Excel, and this procedure was applied to prevent any order effect. The same randomized set was then presented separately to each chatbot, and the process was repeated for three consecutive weeks; thus, a total of 600 responses were obtained for each chatbot. A neutral instruction was given at the beginning of each session: “I will ask you yes/no questions; please respond only with “Yes or ‘No’.” Each item was asked in the following format: “[Question]? Only Yes or No.” Responses were immediately recorded in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA), and double-checked at the end of each session to minimize transcription errors. This protocol was designed to create a controlled and repeatable testing environment similar to structured multiple-choice exams, but adapted for chatbot evaluation.

Assessment of accuracy and consistency

Each chatbot response was compared to the reference response obtained from the ESE statements. Correct responses were coded as “1” and incorrect responses as “0”. Accuracy was calculated as the percentage of correct responses out of 200 items each week and was also divided into subgroups according to the statements.

Consistency was assessed by examining the stability of responses to the same items over three weeks. Agreement was measured using Fleiss’ κ for all weeks and Cohen’s κ for pairwise comparisons (Week 1–2, Week 2–3, Week 1–3). κ values were interpreted according to Landis & Koch criteria, and percentage agreement was also reported.

Statistical analysis

Accuracy values were expressed as percentages with 95% confidence intervals and were calculated using the exact binomial method. Cochran’s Q test was applied to matched binary data to evaluate the accuracy variation between weeks for each chatbot. When a significant difference was detected, the McNemar test was used for pairwise comparisons. Direct comparisons between ChatGPT and Gemini within the same week and for each statement domain were also analyzed using the McNemar test. The McNemar test was used as it is appropriate for paired nominal data and enables comparison of dichotomous (yes/no) responses generated by different models for the same clinical questions, while accounting for the paired structure of the data.

Consistency was also examined using Fleiss’ κ for three-week measurements and Cohen’s κ for pairwise comparisons (Week 1–2, Week 2–3, Week 1–3). κ values were interpreted according to Landis and Koch criteria, and percentage agreement was also reported to provide descriptive context.

A logistic regression model was established to examine in more detail the effect of model type, week, and statement category on response accuracy. The dependent variable was correct/incorrect response, while the independent variables were chatbot (ChatGPT vs. Gemini), week (1–3), and statement category (10 fields). Results were reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals. Robust standard errors clustered at the item level were used to account for the dependency structure arising from repeated measurements across weeks and across models for the same item. This approach addresses within-item correlation without imposing distributional assumptions on random effects and was preferred over mixed-effects modeling as it provides consistent variance estimates under a broader range of dependency structures.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1, R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). Goodness-of-fit statistics were obtained using the irr package, Cochran’s Q and McNemar tests using the DescTools package, and regression analyses using the stats and sandwich packages. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Overall accuracy

Each week, the same 200 yes/no items were administered, yielding a total of 600 responses per model over three weeks. ChatGPT consistently demonstrated high performance with a correct response rate of 93.5% in Week 1, 92.5% in Week 2, and 92.5% in Week 3; these results correspond to an overall accuracy of 92.8% (95% CI: 90.5–94.6). Gemini showed lower values, starting at 81.5% in Week 1, increasing slightly to 87.0% in Week 2, and 86.0% in Week 3. The overall accuracy was calculated as 84.8% (95% CI: 81.7–87.5). The accuracy values of both models remained stable over the three-week period. Detailed accuracy results are also summarized in Table 1. Discussion

This study comparatively evaluates the performance of ChatGPT and Gemini in endodontics using the complete set of ten ESE position statements as a reference framework, thereby covering a broader scope of clinical domains and incorporating temporal stability assessment alongside accuracy, which has not been addressed collectively in previous studies. The primary objective was to examine the accuracy and consistency of these LLMs when responding to yes/no questions derived from the ten position statements of the ESE during three consecutive weekly test sessions. The results show that the null hypothesis was rejected based on the observed data. Contrary to the initial assumption of equal performance, ChatGPT demonstrated significantly higher accuracy compared to Gemini (92.8% vs. 84.8%; p = 0.004), with an odds ratio (OR) of 2.37 in favor of ChatGPT. ChatGPT demonstrated consistently higher accuracy across the three testing weeks. Furthermore, the second hypothesis, the assumption of similar temporal stability, was also rejected. ChatGPT showed near-perfect consistency with Fleiss’ κ = 0.95 (“almost perfect” agreement), whereas Gemini showed only weak agreement with κ = 0.38. These substantial differences in reproducibility are noteworthy, although it should be acknowledged that the dichotomous yes/no format under controlled conditions may favor higher agreement metrics compared to more complex response formats. Nevertheless, consistent performance over time remains a key requirement for AI tools in healthcare applications [24]. It should be noted that these consistency results reflect short-term temporal stability under controlled testing conditions. Given the continuous updates and dynamic nature of large language models, our findings should be interpreted as time-specific rather than indicative of long-term model stability. In addition, the third hypothesis, the expectation of similar performance across clinical domains, was also rejected. Significant differences were observed, with both models showing the lowest accuracy in the “The restoration of root filled teeth” domain (OR = 0.24; p = 0.038). While ChatGPT achieved perfect accuracy in the “The use of antibiotics in endodontics” and “External cervical resorption” domains, both models struggled in “The restoration of root filled teeth” and “Treatment of pulpal and apical disease”.

The accuracy rates observed in the present study are higher than those reported in previous endodontic AI studies, although direct comparisons should be interpreted with caution given differences in question format, reference standards, and the specific model versions evaluated. A previous study reported that GPT-3.5 demonstrated only 60% validity when evaluated with strict clinical criteria [8]. Another study noted that ChatGPT-4 achieved a 57% accuracy rate on yes/no questions [9]. In contrast, ChatGPT’s 92.8% accuracy in our study can be explained by updated model versions, a structured yes/no format, and the use of established clinical guidelines as references rather than individual expert opinions. Moreover, one comparative study reported that accuracy varied between 48% and 97.7% across different chatbot systems in the context of endodontic surgery [10], while another study demonstrated ChatGPT’s consistent superiority over Google Bard in evidence-based dental practice [25]. The temporal stability findings in this study contribute to the endodontic AI literature, as very few studies have examined the reliability of LLMs in repeated applications. However, it should be noted that stability was evaluated over a three-week period under controlled experimental conditions, which may not reflect the models’ long-term performance evolution. ChatGPT’s near-perfect reproducibility (κ = 0.95) contrasts sharply with Gemini’s variability (κ = 0.38), addressing fundamental concerns about AI reliability in clinical practice [6].

The observed performance differences are consistent with broader trends in the field of medical AI. Recent reviews report that ChatGPT performs better than alternative LLMs in many healthcare domains [26]. Similarly, field-based trends are also consistent with general patterns in medical AI. Systems generally perform well in areas with clearly defined protocols, but their accuracy decreases in scenarios requiring more complex clinical reasoning [27]. This explains why both models performed well in “The use of CBCT in endodontics” and “The use of antibiotics in endodontics” statements but struggled with “The restoration of root filled teeth” and “Treatment of pulpal and apical disease”.

Examining individual items provides further insight into the nature of these performance differences. In the restoration domain [15], when asked ‘Is cuspal coverage mandatory for all root-filled teeth with cracks?’ (expected answer: Yes), ChatGPT correctly responded ‘Yes’ across all three weeks, whereas Gemini consistently answered ‘No.’ Similarly, for the item ‘Does ferrule thickness (2 mm+) matter as much as height for survival?’ (expected answer: Yes), ChatGPT answered correctly in all sessions, while Gemini’s responses fluctuated between ‘No,’ ‘Yes,’ and ‘No’ across the three weeks, exemplifying temporal inconsistency. Conversely, when asked ‘Are metal posts contraindicated in modern adhesive dentistry?’ (expected answer: No), Gemini correctly responded ‘No’ in all sessions while ChatGPT consistently answered ‘Yes,’ demonstrating that performance differences are not unidirectional. In the pulpal disease domain [13], the item ‘Does the guideline endorse partial pulpotomy as a valid treatment for mature teeth with spontaneous pain?’ (expected answer: Yes) proved challenging for both models: ChatGPT responded ‘No’ in all three weeks, and Gemini’s answers shifted from ‘No’ in the first week to ‘Yes’ in the subsequent weeks.

ChatGPT’s demonstrated superior accuracy and consistency indicate potential as an educational and informational support tool in endodontics, particularly for familiarization with evidence-based clinical guidelines. However, any outputs should be regarded as supportive rather than prescriptive, with final clinical responsibility resting with qualified dental professionals. It should be noted that findings derived from binary yes/no question formats primarily reflect concordance with established guidelines rather than the full complexity of real-world clinical decision-making. Its consistent performance in the areas of antibiotic use and external cervical resorption reflects its success in areas managed by well-defined protocols [20, 21]. However, the lower accuracy observed in the areas of “The restoration of root filled teeth” and “Treatment of pulpal and apical disease” highlights the continuing importance of expert supervision [13, 15]. Recent developments emphasize the need for rigorous validation prior to clinical application. The American Dental Association (ADA) has published standards for AI validation in dentistry (ANSI/ADA Standard No. 1110-1:2025). These standards highlight data labelling, data collection, and external validation criteria. While our findings support ChatGPT’s potential as an educational and training tool, its limitations in complex areas once again demonstrate that AI should play a complementary role to clinical expertise, not replace it. Regulatory bodies also emphasize the importance of reliability in AI-based medical tools, and continuous verification of performance and transparent reporting are increasingly demanded. In this context, while ChatGPT’s temporal stability may offer an advantage in terms of educational application potential, Gemini’s variable responses indicate a need for further improvement for regulatory acceptance. In real-world endodontic settings, inaccurate or inconsistent chatbot responses may influence patient or clinician behavior, potentially leading to delayed referral, inappropriate self-management, or deviation from evidence-based guidelines, particularly in time-sensitive clinical scenarios. Such risks underscore the need for cautious integration of AI tools into clinical workflows, with clear emphasis on human oversight and guideline-based decision-making.

The accuracy rates obtained in this study also support the integration of ChatGPT into endodontic education. Recent research on AI in dental curricula has shown mixed results; some studies have reported improved diagnostic performance, while others have emphasized the need for careful integration [28]. ChatGPT’s reliable interpretation of ESE guidelines can support student learning and continuous professional development. However, performance limitations in complex areas should be considered. “The restoration of root filled teeth” and “Treatment of pulpal and apical disease” showed the highest error rates, indicating a need for greater human oversight in these areas. This aligns with educational research emphasizing the importance of critical thinking and advising against overreliance on AI [28].

Several limitations should be considered. While the yes/no format allows for objective scoring, it may not reflect the full complexity of clinical reasoning [1]. Additionally, the binary nature of this format may artificially inflate consistency metrics, such as kappa coefficients, as the limited response options reduce variability and increase the likelihood of chance agreement, which should be considered when interpreting the reported reproducibility values. The assessment against guidelines is based solely on documentation rather than patient outcomes; therefore, concordance with clinical guidelines should not be equated with clinical validity or therapeutic effectiveness in real patients, limiting the generalizability of the findings to real-world clinical practice. In addition, because large language model training datasets are not fully transparent, prior exposure of the models to ESE position statements or derivative content cannot be excluded. However, as the models were not directed to respond based on any specific guideline, providing accurate responses required retrieving and synthesizing relevant information from a vast and potentially conflicting training corpus, which may reflect information retrieval capacity beyond simple memorization. Furthermore, the model versions tested represent snapshots of rapidly evolving technologies obtained during a specific data collection period (August-September 2025), and the reported accuracy and consistency values should not be interpreted as fixed or permanent model capabilities. Re-evaluation will be necessary as these systems continue to evolve. Moreover, only two LLM platforms were evaluated in this study. Although ChatGPT and Gemini were selected as the two most widely used and publicly accessible commercial chatbot systems at the time of data collection, the exclusion of other platforms such as Claude (Anthropic, San Francisco, CA, USA), Copilot (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA), and open-source LLMs limits the generalizability of comparative conclusions. Future studies should adopt more complex question formats, such as case-based and multi-stage reasoning tasks, to better reflect real clinical practice, and could incorporate multiple operators to further assess procedural robustness. Longitudinal studies tracking the evolution of LLMs and broader comparative analyses using additional platforms are also needed. Investigating the most appropriate methods for integrating into clinical workflows and clinician-AI interaction will be critical for successful implementation [24].

Conclusion

This study demonstrated significant performance differences between ChatGPT and Gemini in responding to dichotomous items derived from consensus-based endodontic clinical guidelines. ChatGPT exhibited higher accuracy (92.8% vs. 84.8%) and almost perfect consistency (κ = 0.95 vs. κ = 0.38). Hypotheses of equal performance were rejected, supporting ChatGPT’s potential benefits in educational and training contexts, particularly for familiarization with evidence-based clinical guidelines. However, performance limitations in complex areas such as “The restoration of root filled teeth” and “Treatment of pulpal and apical disease” underscore the importance of expert oversight. Consistency differences highlight temporal stability as a critical dimension in medical AI evaluation. Overall, this study provides a methodological framework for benchmarking LLM performance against established endodontic guidelines and offers a basis for future research aimed at exploring the role of AI in endodontic education, training, and guideline dissemination.
