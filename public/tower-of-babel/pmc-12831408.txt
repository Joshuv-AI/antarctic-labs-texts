Biomarker study on pneumonitis in durvalumab maintenance therapy after chemoradiotherapy for stage III non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Ryo Yano, Takaaki Tokito, Kenta Murotani, Daiki Murata, Shingo Tsuneyoshi, Hidenobu Ishii, Koichi Azuma, Tomoaki Hoshino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe standard treatment for stage III non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is platinum-based concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT), followed by durvalumab (anti-programmed death ligand-1 antibody) maintenance therapy. Biomarkers related to immune-related pneumonitis during durvalumab maintenance therapy after CCRT for stage III NSCLC have not yet been identified.MethodsWe evaluated 88 cytokines/chemokines at three time points, before concurrent chemoradiotherapy (pre-CCRT), post-radiotherapy, and six weeks after durvalumab administration, using a multiplex assay (Bio-Plex system) in 29 patients who provided consent for the blood biomarker study, categorizing them into Grade 0–1 and Grade 2 or higher pneumonitis groups.ResultsAt pre-CCRT, no significant changes were observed; however, at post-radiotherapy, significant changes in TNFRSF8/sCD30 were observed in the group with Grade 2 or higher pneumonitis. Moreover, six weeks after durvalumab administration, significant changes were observed in TNFRSF8/sCD30, in addition to TNFSF12, IL-1Ra, CXCL9/CXCL10/CXCL11, IFN-γ, and CCL19.ConclusionAlthough no biomarkers predicting Grade 2 or higher pneumonitis before CCRT were identified, the relationship between radiotherapy and TNFRSF8/sCD30 as well as the relationship between durvalumab administration and various cytokines/chemokines is reported for the first time in stage III NSCLC. In particular, the CXCL family, including CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11, is considered highly significant in pneumonitis with durvalumab maintenance therapy after CCRT for stage III NSCLC. Further large-scale studies are needed to establish biomarkers for patients with NSCLC who receive durvalumab maintenance therapy after CCRT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15467-0.

Introduction

The standard treatment for stage III non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is platinum-based concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT), followed by durvalumab (anti-programmed death ligand-1 [PD-L1] antibody) maintenance therapy [1]. However, the 5-year survival rate for unresectable NSCLC remains unsatisfactory at approximately 40%, and side effects such as radiation pneumonitis and immune-related adverse events (irAEs) develop [2].

Pneumonitis is the primary cause of durvalumab discontinuation [1], and a short duration of durvalumab consolidation therapy after CCRT due to adverse events (AEs) has been reported to result in poor prognosis [3]. Therefore, complete durvalumab maintenance therapy is desirable. In real-world data of 225 cases in Japan, the incidence of Grade 2 or higher pneumonitis was reported to be 34%, with Grade 3 or higher incidence being 7% [4]. Currently, the factors that predict immune-related pulmonary inflammation are not well established, and various studies are underway.

Early prediction of pneumonitis is essential to facilitate the timely and appropriate administration of durvalumab maintenance therapy. Many studies have reported the usefulness of krevs von den lungen-6 (KL-6) in predicting pneumonitis prior to radiation therapy in lung cancer [5, 6]. It has also been reported that KL-6 is significantly elevated in immuno-related pneumonitis in lung cancer [7]. However, since KL-6 is associated not only with pneumonitis, but also with the presence of lung cancer itself, the predictive performance of KL-6 before treatment may be insufficient [8]. IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α and TGF-β have been reported as biomarkers for pneumonitis after thoracic radiotherapy [4, 9, 10].

Predictive biomarkers for pneumonitis during durvalumab maintenance therapy following CCRT have not been clearly defined. To address this issue, a prospective observational study was conducted to identify biomarkers associated with Grade ≥ 2 pneumonitis in patients with stage III NSCLC receiving durvalumab maintenance therapy after CCRT.

Materials and methods

Participants

A total of 77 patients with stage IIIB to IIIC NSCLC who underwent curative CCRT at our institution between May 2018 and November 2021 were prospectively enrolled. Lung cancer staging was performed using the 8th edition of the TNM classification by the Union for International Cancer Control. The study was conducted in accordance with the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kurume University Hospital (IRB No. 18168).

Patient characteristics

Prior to the start of CCRT, the following information was obtained from medical records: age, sex, smoking history, performance status (PS), histological type, genetic mutations, staging, PD-L1 expression, combination chemotherapy regimen, radiation technique, V5 and V20, mean lung dose (MLD), presence or absence of durvalumab maintenance therapy, and respiratory function test results—including percentage of predicted vital capacity (%VC), percentage of forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1%), and diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (%DLCO). In addition, in this study, pneumonitis was defined as non-infectious pneumonitis, including radiation-related, immune-related, or both. Pneumonitis was evaluated based on the international classification of idiopathic interstitial pneumonias by the American Thoracic Society, European Respiratory Society, Japanese Respiratory Society, and Latin American Thoracic Association (ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT) [11], with chest X-ray and HRCT findings at the onset of pneumonitis independently assessed by two respiratory specialists. Pneumonitis was graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0.

Measurement of soluble immune mediators in plasma

Plasma samples were collected at three time points: before concurrent chemoradiotherapy (pre-CCRT), after completion of radiotherapy, and six weeks after initiation of durvalumab maintenance therapy (Fig. 1A). The six-week time point was selected because there is no established schedule for post-ICI blood sampling, and this interval aligns with tumor assessment schedules used in clinical trials and previous studies [10, 12]. All blood samples were heparinized, centrifuged at 1600 g for 15 min, and the plasma supernatants were transferred into new tubes and stored at –80 °C until analysis. Prior to measurement, plasma samples were thawed at room temperature. Plasma levels of soluble immune mediators were quantified once for each patient using a bead-based multiplex assay (Bio-Plex 200 system; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the assay, 100 µL aliquots of two-fold diluted plasma were analyzed using commercially available kits (Bio-Rad Laboratories) covering 88 soluble immune mediators, including cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors.

The panel included IL-1Ra, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-9, IL-10, IL-11, IL-12 (p40), IL-12 (p70), IL-13, IL-15, IL-16, IL-17A, IL-19, IL-20, IL-22, IL-26, IL-27, IL-28A, IL-29, IL-32, IL-34, IL-35, IFN-α2, IFN-β, IFN-γ, TNF-α, GM-CSF, 6Ckine, BCA-1, CTACK, ENA-78, Eotaxin, Eotaxin-2, Eotaxin-3, Fractalkine, GCP-2, Gro-α, CXCL2 (Gro-β), IP-10, I-TAC, MIP-1α, MIP-1δ, MIP-3α, MIP-3β, MPIF-1, SCYB16, SDF-1 α + β, TARC, TECK, MCP-1, MCP-2, MCP-3, MCP-4, MDC, MIF, MIG, VEGF, APRIL, BAFF, sCD30, sCD163, Chitinase 3-like-1, gp130, sIL-6Rα, LIGHT, MMP-1, MMP-2, MMP-3, Osteocalcin, Osteopontin, Pentraxin-3, sTNF-R1, sTNF-R2, TSLP, TWEAK, VEGF, and TGF-β (1, 2, 3).

Changes during treatment were calculated as differences in mediator levels between pre-CCRT, post-radiotherapy, and six weeks after durvalumab initiation.

Statistical analysis

Univariate analysis was conducted based on the 29 patients who underwent blood biomarker sampling at pre-CCRT, post-radiotherapy, and at 6 weeks after durvalumab administration to compare the low-grade (Grade < 2) and high-grade (Grade 2 or higher) groups. Continuous variables were summarized as median (Q1-Q3) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and proportions and were analyzed using Fisher's exact test. Univariate logistic regression was performed with pneumonitis severity classified as low- or high-grade as the dependent variable and blood biomarkers as independent variables. For relevant biomarkers, smoothing spline analysis was performed for nonparametric estimation of the mean structure, with biomarker measurements as the dependent variable and the time point as the independent variable. As this was an exploratory study, no adjustments were made for the multiplicity of tests. A two-sided p-value of 0.05 was considered significant, and analyses were performed using JMP version 17.0 and R 4.2.1.

Results

Patient characteristics

Patient enrollment began in May 2018, and the final analysis was completed in March 2023. The follow-up period for the study population was 4 years 10 months. Of the 77 patients, 50 were included after excluding 27 patients who did not undergo durvalumab maintenance therapy. All patients received 60 Gy of radiation using three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy. Of the 50 patients who received durvalumab maintenance therapy, 29 consented to participate in the blood biomarker study (Fig. 1A), Blood samples were collected pre-CCRT, post-radiotherapy, and 6 weeks after durvalumab administration (Fig. 1B). Among the 29 patients, 19 had low-grade pneumonitis (no pneumonitis, 3 cases; Grade 1, 16 cases), whereas 10 had Grade 2 or higher pneumonitis (Grade 2, 8 cases; Grade 3, 2 cases). The interval between the completion of chemoradiotherapy and the onset of pneumonitis ranged from 14 to 169 days, with a median duration of 53 days. Pneumonitis developing after the completion of CCRT but before the start of durvalumab maintenance therapy would be diagnosed as radiation pneumonitis; however, there were no such cases in the present study.

All patients had a PS 0–1. The histological diagnoses included 15 cases of adenocarcinoma, 10 cases of squamous cell carcinoma, and 4 cases of NSCLC. Genetic mutation analysis revealed seven cases with EGFR mutations (three cases with 19del, three cases with L858R, and one case with 20 ins) and one case with ALK mutations. The PD-L1 expression status was as follows: 10 cases with 0%, five cases with 1–49%, four cases with ≥ 50%, and 10 cases with no data available. Regarding chemotherapy, 25 patients received platinum-based combination therapy and four patients received carboplatin monotherapy. The median V5 was 42% (range: 14–66%), median V20 was 25% (range: 7–40%), and median MLD was 13% (range: 4–21%). In the respiratory function tests, 17 patients had %VC ≥ 80%, and 12 patients had no available data. For FEV1%, three patients had ≥ 70%, 14 patients had < 70%, and 12 patients had no data available. Analysis of biomarkers

Discussion

The aim of this study was to explore biomarkers that could predict Grade 2 or higher immune-related pneumonitis in patients with stage III NSCLC receiving durvalumab maintenance therapy after CCRT. At pre-CCRT, no biomarkers found significant differences. However, only TNFRSF8 was significantly elevated at the point of post-radiotherapy in the high-grade pneumonitis group. TNFRSF8 is a member of the receptor family for TNF-α and is one of the receptors that specifically bind to TNF-α [13]. TNF-α is a cytokine that plays an important role in regulating immune responses and inflammatory reactions, and is known as an inflammatory cytokine. The early release of TNF-α during thoracic cancer radiotherapy plays an important role in radiation pneumonitis, and its inhibition may achieve radiation protection for the lungs [14]. Therefore, we considered that increased TNFRSF8 expression may be associated with radiation exposure.

In addition, we identified TNFSF12 as a newly implicated factor in Grade 2 or higher pneumonitis. TNFSF12 is a TNF ligand associated with inflammation, fibrosis, and angiogenesis. It plays a particularly important role in the immune system and inflammatory responses [15–17]. In a study exploring predictive factors for irAEs, including pneumonitis, in urothelial carcinoma treated with ICIs, associations were found between elevated pretreatment levels of TNFSF12 and the occurrence of irAEs [18]. It is speculated that inflammation mediated by TNFSF12 may be related to the occurrence of irAEs; however, to our knowledge, there are no reports demonstrating an association between TNFSF12 levels during treatment and the occurrence of irAEs. In our study, there was no significant difference in TNFSF12 levels before durvalumab treatment; however, a significant decrease was observed 6 weeks after durvalumab administration in high-grade pneumonitis. The above results suggest that the TNF pathway may be more affected by radiotherapy than by ICI therapy in high-grade pneumonitis in patients receiving CCRT followed by PD-L1 therapy. These findings suggest that the TNF pathway, including TNFRSF8 and TNFSF12, may serve as a predictor of pneumonitis during durvalumab maintenance therapy following chemoradiotherapy in patients with stage III NSCLC, particularly in those associated with radiation therapy.

At the point of post-radiotherapy, the level of IL-1Ra appeared to peak in the high-grade pneumonitis group. IL-1Ra is an IL-1 receptor antagonist [19]. IL-1Ra is considered part of a genetic signature that predicts toxicity in patients who have recently received ICI therapy [20, 21]. Additionally, there has been a report of an increase in IL-1Ra concurrent with the onset of symptomatic pneumonitis in patients with melanoma treated with axillary lymph nodes radiation therapy followed by ICI [22].

These results suggest that IL-1Ra may play an important role in pneumonitis during durvalumab maintenance therapy after chemoradiotherapy for stage III NSCLC.

In the high-grade pneumonitis group, significant increases in CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, and IFN-γ were first observed after radiotherapy, with peaks detected 6 weeks after initiating durvalumab. CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 mediate immune cell recruitment to sites of infection and inflammation [23] and are primarily secreted in response to IFN-γ by monocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and cancer cells [24]. Radiation-induced cellular damage contributes to the release of multiple cytokines, including IFN-γ, TGF-β, and IL-6, thereby inducing pulmonary injury [25]. In murine models, chemokines such as CXCL10 are upregulated after radiation exposure [26]. In the present context, IFN-γ released due to radiation-induced lung injury may have activated macrophages and T cells, leading to the induction of various chemokines, including CXCL10. In our study, IFN-γ, CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 remained elevated at 6 weeks after durvalumab initiation in the high-grade pneumonitis group, suggesting a synergistic effect between radiation-induced injury and PD-L1 blockade. Several studies have suggested that multiple cytokines and chemokines, including members of the interleukin and CXCL families, are predictive of irAEs such as pneumonitis [27, 28]. CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 have been reported as biomarkers of autoimmune inflammation in patients with collagen vascular disease-ILD and interstitial pneumonia with autoimmune features, and may also be implicated in the development of pneumonitis [29]. Previous reports have shown a significant increase in CXCL9 levels at 2–3 weeks and at 6 weeks following the onset of irAEs, such as pneumonitis [30]. Furthermore, previous study in renal cell carcinoma have demonstrated a significant association between CXCL10 and grade 2 or higher irAEs, including pneumonitis [31]. This raises the possibility that CXCL10 may also play a role in irAEs in lung cancer. In solid tumors treated with ICIs, significant increases in CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 were observed at the onset of irAEs [32]. Our group has also reported CXCL9 as a potential biomarker for ICI-related ILD [33]. Taken together, these findings suggest that IFN-γ, CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 may serve as predictive biomarkers for pneumonitis during durvalumab maintenance therapy after CCRT in patients with stage III NSCLC.

In our cohort, the observed sequential elevation of TNFRSF8 following radiotherapy and of TNFSF12, IL-1Ra, IFN-γ, and CXCL9/10/11 after durvalumab initiation suggests a mechanistic cascade linking radiation-induced tissue injury with immune checkpoint–related inflammation. TNFRSF8 may reflect TNF-α–driven immune activation induced by radiation exposure, whereas TNFSF12 modulates fibroinflammatory remodeling and amplifies downstream cytokine signaling. IFN-γ, released by activated macrophages and T cells, in turn induces CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11, which recruit additional effector T cells to the lung parenchyma. The sustained activation of this TNF–IFN–CXCL axis could promote immune cell infiltration and cytokine amplification, may ultimately contributing to pneumonitis in patients receiving CCRT followed by PD-L1 blockade.

In our study, CCL19 levels were slightly elevated during CCRT, followed by durvalumab treatment. CCL19 is a homeostatic chemokine that is abundantly expressed in the thymus and lymph nodes and primarily regulates the transport of immune cells. CC L family mainly exert chemotactic effects by attracting monocytes, dendritic cells, and T lymphocytes, which are involved in inflammatory reactions and antiviral effects. Radiation stimulates the production of these chemokines. The relationship between CCL19 and irAEs, including pneumonitis, remains unclear, and further studies are warranted to elucidate the relationship between CC chemokines and ICI. The present study suggests a possible association between CCL19 and pneumonitis during durvalumab maintenance therapy after chemoradiotherapy for stage III NSCLC.

The current study had several limitations. First, it employed a single-center design and the small number of patients is a limitation. Specifically, only 29 patients (37% of the total population) underwent blood biomarker analysis, and among them, only 10 developed Grade ≥ 2 pneumonitis. This limited sample size may have reduced the statistical power of our analyses. Additionally, in our study, several chemotherapy regimens—including platinum monotherapy and platinum-based combination regimens—were used during chemoradiotherapy prior to the transition to durvalumab maintenance therapy. This heterogeneity may have served as a potential confounder. Therefore, a larger prospective study is warranted to validate our findings. Next, in this study, the onset of pneumonitis occurred at a median of 53 days (range, 14–169 days) after the completion of radiotherapy, which is close to the period when pneumonitis commonly develops and to the timing of the third cytokine measurement in this study. However, for cases in which pneumonitis developed after the median onset time, the cytokine dynamics may not have been fully captured at their peak. This limitation may have affected the predictive accuracy of the pneumonitis biomarkers presented in this study and is therefore considered one of the study’s limitations. Third, because this study was exploratory in nature, no adjustment for multiplicity was applied. However, for reference, we performed a Benjamini–Hochberg correction, and no cytokines met the threshold of False Discovery Rate (FDR) < 0.05 (the FDR values are provided in the supplementary materials). Therefore, the overall type I error may have been inflated, and the conclusions should be regarded as tentative. The variables identified as being potentially associated with the outcomes warrant further validation in an independent cohort to confirm their reproducibility. Finally, in this study, smoothing spline curves were applied to visualize temporal changes in biomarker levels. However, the distributions and scatter observed in the boxplots differed from the averaged smoothing spline curves, which may influence the interpretation of the results. This discrepancy should be considered when interpreting the findings, as it may reflect limitations of the smoothing spline approach in representing data with heterogeneous distributions.

Conclusion

Although no biomarkers predicting Grade 2 or higher pneumonitis before CCRT were identified, the relationship between radiation therapy and TNFRSF8/sCD30, as well as the relationship between durvalumab administration and TNFSF12, IL-1Ra, CXCL9/CXCL10/CXCL11, IFN-γ, and CCL19 were demonstrated in stage III NSCLC treated with CCRT followed by durvalumab.
