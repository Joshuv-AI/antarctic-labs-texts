Ets2 Is an Activity‐Dependent Transcriptional Regulator of a Fast‐Twitch Muscle Program
Authors: Zaheer A. Rana, Jo C. Bruusgaard, Jens R. Wendland, Ivan Winje, Ingrid M. Egner, Irina Karavanova, Einar Eftestøl, Detlef Vullhorst, Kristian Gundersen, Andres Buonanno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe functional properties of excitable cells, including skeletal muscle, are regulated by electrical activity. Distinct motoneuron firing patterns control contraction speed, strength, and endurance of fast‐ and slow‐twitch myofibers by activating transcriptional programs that specify contractile and metabolic protein isoforms. Although transcriptional regulators of slow muscle adaptation are well characterized, less is known about activity‐dependent mechanisms controlling expression of fast muscle genes. We previously showed that the quail fast troponin I (TnIf) intronic regulatory element (FIRE) is both necessary and sufficient for fast‐muscle‐specific transcription and responds selectively to brief, high‐frequency (150 Hz) stimulation rather than to sustained low‐frequency (20 Hz) activity. In this study, using mRNA microarray analysis of muscles subjected to different stimulation patterns, we identified E26 transformation‐specific homolog 2 (Ets2) as a transcription factor rapidly and selectively induced by fast‐patterned activity. Ets2 accumulates in nuclei of slow soleus muscle following fast, but not slow, stimulation, and it is expressed more abundantly in fast muscle. Ets2 binds a consensus Ets site within the TnIf FIRE, and mutation of this site abolishes enhancer activation by fast‐patterned activity. Furthermore, Ets2 knockdown reduces transcription from both a trimerized Ets2‐responsive reporter and a TnIf FIRE reporter specifically in fast muscles. To our knowledge, these findings identify Ets2 as the first transcription factor shown to selectively promote fast muscle gene expression in response to fast‐patterned activity. Together with NFATc1, Ets2 may contribute to a dynamic regulatory mechanism that enables skeletal muscles to adapt their functional properties to changing physiological demands.

Introduction

How excitable tissues adapt to a changing environment remains a central scientific question with important implications for understanding basic biological processes that span from evolution to brain function. Similar to how distinct patterns of neuronal activity differentially modify synaptic strength between neurons in the brain, mechanisms important for memory and learning, physical exercise modulates the contractile and metabolic properties of skeletal muscles that are necessary for adapting to changing demands for strength, speed, and endurance. Whereas intrinsic genetic programs are important for the initial differentiation of distinct skeletal muscle types during embryonic and neonatal development prior to the maturation of motor inputs [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8], adult muscles retain a degree of plasticity that is critical for the adaptation of slow and fast muscle types to changing environmental demands [6, 9, 10, 11]. Importantly, muscle disuse and aging result in muscle atrophy [12, 13, 14] and, conversely, physical exercise improves insulin sensitivity [15] and is associated with a lower risk for depression [16].

The firing properties of motoneurons are closely matched to the contractile and metabolic properties of adult skeletal muscles. Motor units that are recruited to maintain posture fire in long trains at lower frequencies (13–20 Hz) and are fatigue‐resistant due to their oxidative nature, whereas motor units used to generate rapid movements and force burst rarely and at higher frequencies (50–250 Hz) and tend to fatigue quickly [17]. Electrical activity per se, in absence of motoneuron innervation, is instructive (see Discussion). Direct stimulation of denervated rodent muscles with activity patterns mimicking either slow or fast motoneuron firing patterns is sufficient to regulate the contractile and metabolic properties of skeletal muscles [18, 19, 20], and electrical stimulation of dissociated myogenic cultures in vitro in absence of neurons is sufficient to change expression of contractile proteins [21, 22, 23, 24]. In rodents and humans, electrical stimulation mimics the effects of exercise, and it can also ameliorate skeletal muscle atrophy resulting from disuse, aging, and microgravity (see [12, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30]).

Investigations into the transcriptional regulation of Troponin I (TnI) isoforms have served as an excellent model to understand how activity regulates the plastic contractile properties of skeletal muscles because, in contrast to the numerous genes that encode myosin heavy chain (MHC) isoforms (Myh1, Myh2, Myh4, Myh7), there are only two skeletal muscle TnI genes (Tnni1; slow and Tnni2; fast) that are tightly regulated by either tonic, low frequency (slow‐patterned) or infrequent, high‐frequency (fast‐patterned) electrical activity, respectively [31, 32]. Prior studies using transgenic mice identified a 128 base‐pair (bp) TnIs slow upstream regulatory element (SURE) and a 144 bp TnIf fast intronic regulatory element (FIRE) that are sufficient to drive fiber type‐specific transcription in adult muscles [33, 34, 35]. However, TnI FIRE DNA element(s) necessary to regulate transcription in response to fast‐patterned activity remain unknown.

With a few exceptions (see Discussion), little is known about transcription factors regulating the fast developmental program [36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41] and its activity dependence [42, 43]. Fast muscles are relatively inactive, as compared to their slow muscle counterparts that can contract thousands of times in the course of a day [17], which has led some to suggest that the fast regulatory program could constitute a default pathway similar to the one operant in neonatal or denervated muscles (see [11]). However, depolarization of adult muscles with fast or slow stimulation patterns mimicking motoneuron activity differentially regulates TnIf and TnIs mRNAs [31] and their promoters [44]. Infrequent fast‐frequency stimulation (150 Hz) regulates TnIf transcription differently than denervation [34, 44], indicating the existence of a sporadically active fast‐frequency program, rather than a default program, important for determining muscle twitch duration. In this study, we combined single‐cell in vivo time‐lapse imaging of TnIf reporter‐transfected myofibers and in situ transcriptional analysis to identify Ets2 as a novel activity‐dependent transcription factor responding selectively to fast‐patterned activity. As Ets factors generally function in complex with other transcription factors (see Discussion), our findings suggest that Ets2 forms ternary complexes with other transcription factors required for conferring skeletal muscle specificity to selectively regulate transcription of Tnni2, and potentially other myofibril genes, in response to fast‐patterned activity in fast‐twitch muscles.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Surgical procedures were performed on adult male Wistar rats (200–300 g). Surgery and stimulation experiments were performed as previously described [43] and according to the approved NIH Animal Study Proposal (ASP# 05‐032) and Norwegian Animal Research Authority guidelines. Animals were euthanized by CO2 inhalation and cervical dislocation after anesthesia.

RNA Expression Analysis on Microarrays

Affymetrix gene chip analyses were done on rat EDL and soleus muscles stimulated with mismatching activity patterns for 0, 3, 6, 12 or 24 h using slow (20 Hz pulse train for 10 s every 30 s) and fast (150 Hz burst for 0.167 s every 15 min) stimuli, respectively, as described previously [43]. Briefly, a silicon cuff connected to Teflon‐coated steel electrodes (AS632, Cooner Sales, CA) was placed around the right leg sciatic nerve, which innervates the lower leg muscles, including the EDL and Sol muscles. The nerve was cut proximally to the electrodes in order to avoid reflexes. For each time point (0, 3, 6, 12, or 24 h), three Sol and EDL muscles were harvested (total of 30 muscles) and snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen until use. Frozen muscles were pulverized, RNA extracted using TRI Reagent (Ambion), followed by further purification on CsCl gradients as described [31]. Starting from 5ug of total RNA, cDNA was synthesized and hybridized onto Affymetrix GeneChip Rat Genome 230 2.0 Arrays (with 31 000 probe sets), following the manufacturer's instructions. Array data were analyzed with the GeneChip Operating Software (GCOS) v1.1.1, which revealed 6689 probe sets that hybridized with transcripts in all 30 microarrays. Log2‐transformed gene expression data from control (t = 0) and stimulated Sol and EDL muscles were analyzed for correlation tests to derive Pearson coefficients. We further analyzed those probe sets with correlation coefficients > 0.9 that showed activity‐dependent increases in Sol, but not EDL, muscles in response fast‐patterned activity and were significant after Bonferroni correction (p < 3.7E−6). A total of 31 probe sets, one encoding Ets2, were identified after applying these stringent criteria.

RNA Analysis by Semi‐Quantitative RT‐PCR

RNA from nerve‐stimulated Sol and EDL muscles was isolated with TRI Reagent (Ambion) and reversed transcribed with Superscript III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen) using random primers. All RT‐PCR reactions were performed on a Roche Lightcycler 480 instrument, using 1.5.0 Lightcycler software and the LightCycler FastStart SYBR Green Master kit (Roche Applied Science); β‐actin was used for normalization. The primers were as follows: Ets2 fw, 5′‐GGA GCA GTC TGT ATG AGG TT‐3′ and Ets2 rev, 5′‐CAA AGG CTC TTT ATG AAT AT‐3′; β‐Actin fw, 5′‐ACC AAC TGG GAC GAT ATG GAG AAG A‐3′ and β‐Actin rev, 5′‐CGC ACG ATT TCC CTC TCA GC‐3′. Amplification of Ets2 consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 10 min followed by 10 s every cycle, 58°C for 20 s, and 72°C for 30 s. For β‐Actin the annealing temperature was optimized to 55°C. A final concentration of 3 mM Mg2+ was used for all reactions. Melting curves showed a single peak, affirming the absence of other contaminating products or primer‐dimers. Amplification coefficients were similar for Ets2 and β‐actin (1.95 and 1.96 respectively). Crossing points for Ets2 and for β‐actin were typically cycles 23 and 17, respectively.

DNA Constructs

TnIf2100 was subcloned into pEGFP (Clontech) as previously described [44], and into pGL3 to drive expression of firefly luciferase. Deletions and point mutations were introduced using site‐directed mutagenesis (QuickChange II, Stratagene) and verified by sequencing. The EGFP‐tagged Ets2 expression construct was kindly provided by Dr. Sugimoto [45]. A trimerized Ets luciferase reporter construct (3xEts2‐Luciferase) was generated by inserting three copies of a bona fide Ets site (5′‐CGAGCAGGAAGTTCGA‐3′) into the IL2 minimal promoter between −326 and +45.

In Vivo Transfection of Adult Muscle Fibers

Reporter and expression constructs used for transfection of adult muscles by electroporation were prepared free of endotoxins, as described [34]. Troponin‐driven luciferase and GFP reporter constructs in saline were directly injected into Sol and EDL muscles (50 μL of 0.5 mg/mL DNA solution). For imaging of Ets2‐EGFP, the transfection conditions were adjusted to favor DNA uptake by superficial fibers by bathing the muscles (50 μL of a 0.5 mg/mL DNA solution), rather than injecting prior to electroporation. Muscles were electroporated after 30 s by applying 5 pulse trains, consisting of 1000 symmetrical 200‐ms bipolar pulses with an amplitude of 50 V (~150 V/cm), using two 1‐mm silver wire electrodes spaced at 3 mm and connected to a Pulsar 6 bp‐a/s stimulator (Frederick Haer & Co, ME) placed on each side of the muscle to generate an electrical field perpendicular the long axis of muscles.

In Vivo Imaging of Ets2‐EGFP in Adult Stimulated or Denervated Muscles

Adult EDL and Sol muscles were transfected with the Ets2‐EGFP expression construct, as described above, and muscles were stimulated via the intact nerve with slow (20 Hz train for 10 s, every 30 s) or fast (150 Hz trains of 25 pulses, every 15 s) patterns previously shown to cause fiber‐type transitions [20]. Denervation was done by surgically exposing the sciatic nerve in the thigh and then removing a 1‐cm‐long section of the nerve to prevent reinnervation. The subcellular distribution of fluorescent Ets2‐EGFP was analyzed in living myofibers, before and after 48 h stimulation using the original confocal imaging approaches developed by Lichtman and colleagues [46, 47] and modified as we previously described [44, 48]. Muscles were exposed for 1 h to a Hoechst 33342 dye solution in the live animal but, in contrast to the previous protocols, surgically removed and placed in a petri dish containing oxygenated Ringer's solution. Hoechst dye was used to mark all myonuclei and to determine Ets2‐EGFP nuclear/cytoplasmic ratios. Within 20 min, confocal z‐stacks (Olympus FluoView FV1000) were obtained from fiber segments, and later analyzed in ImageJ (Rasband, W.S., ImageJ, U.S. National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA, http://imagej.nih.gov/ij/, 1997–2011). The nuclear/cytoplasmic ratio of Ets2‐EGFP was determined by measuring fluorescence intensity in whole nuclei relative to an equal area of adjacent cytoplasm. All measurements were performed on 16‐bit TIFF images. Each data point represents one fiber, and 2–5 ratios were calculated per fiber.

EMSAs

Nuclear extracts were obtained from Ets2 over‐expressing C2C12 myotubes using the NE‐PER extraction kit (Pierce) mixed in equal amounts with Jurkat nuclear extracts (Santa Cruz; sc‐2132). Probes for EMSAs were generated from ds‐oligos (Ets2: GATCTCGAGCAGGAAGTTCGA, mutEts2: GATCTCGAGCAAGAAGTTCGA, FIRE: TGGGGGGGGAAAGTGCTTCTAA and mutFIRE: TGGGGGGGCAAAGTGCTTCTAA) labeled with [γ‐32P]‐ATP and polynucleotide kinase and purified on acrylamide gels. Radiolabeled probes (2500 cpm) and nuclear extracts (2 μg) were incubated at +4°C for 25 min before loading onto a 5% polyacrylamide gel. EMSAs were run at +4°C for 2.5 h at 170 V.

siRNA

EDL and soleus muscles were co‐transfected by electroporation as described above with specific and validated Ets‐targeting or negative control siRNAs (2 μg/0.15 nmole; Thermo Fisher Scientifically, formerly Ambion) and TnIf2100‐luc or 3xEts2‐luc reporter constructs (20 μg). Muscles were harvested after 4 days and homogenized for subsequent luciferase measurement as described above. Effects of siRNAs peaked at 4 days post‐transfection as established in preliminary standardizing tests.

Results

Expression Profile of Skeletal Muscles Stimulated With Different Electrical Activity Patterns

With the goal of identifying transcription factors that selectively and rapidly respond to activity to regulate the fast activity muscle program, we used microarray profiling to globally analyze changes in transcript levels in the rat fast extensor digitorum longus (EDL) and slow soleus (Sol) muscles stimulated for 0, 3, 6, 12, or 24 h with “mismatching” activity patterns that elicit fiber‐type transitions (i.e., EDL stimulated tonically at 20 Hz; Sol stimulated infrequently at 150 Hz). From the 6689 probe sets that hybridized with cDNAs from 30 stimulated Sol and EDL muscles (see Methods and Supporting Information), we identified 31 transcripts that showed significant changes after Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons in mRNA levels that increased in Sol muscles stimulated with fast‐patterned activity, but were unchanged or decreased in EDL muscles stimulated with slow‐patterned activity (Table 1). Of these 31 transcripts, three encode for transcription regulatory proteins: nuclear receptor coactivator 1 (Nco1), nuclear receptor interacting protein 1 (Nrip1) and E twenty‐six 2 (Ets2). Interestingly, both Ncoa1 and Nrip1 do not bind directly to DNA elements; instead, they modulate transcription by forming complexes with nuclear receptors (see [49]).

We therefore focused our studies on the transcription factor Ets2 because its expression was selectively and highly upregulated by fast‐patterned electrical stimulation (Figure 1A) and, as demonstrated below, the TnIf FIRE harbors a canonical Ets DNA binding site. Independent confirmation of the microarray findings for Ets2 was obtained using semi‐quantitative RT‐PCR (Figure 1B), which showed that fast‐patterned stimulation (48 h) of the Sol augments relative Ets2 transcript levels by 3.5‐fold (0.006 to 0.014 arbitrary units, p < 0.01), whereas slow‐patterned stimulation of the EDL reduces Ets levels by 3‐fold (0.01 to 0.002 arbitrary units, p < 0.001). Interestingly, 150 Hz stimulation of the EDL further augments Ets2 transcript levels by 1.8‐fold (0.011 to 0.019, p < 0.001). Importantly, Ets2 steady‐state mRNA levels are higher in adult EDL than in Sol muscle (Figure 1A,B), and its activity‐dependent increase by fast‐patterned activity precedes regulation of mRNAs encoding structural proteins like TnIf (Figure 1C) and TnIs (Figure 1D) that are regulated by more prolonged stimulation times [31, 44].

Ets2 Protein Translocation Between Cytoplasm and Nucleus Is Activity‐ and Pattern‐Dependent

Because cytoplasmic‐nuclear shuttling of Ets factors couples extracellular signals to transcriptional responses in non‐excitable cultured cells [45], we determined the nuclear/cytoplasmic fluorescence ratio of enhanced GFP‐tagged Ets2 (Ets2‐EGFP) in transfected EDL (Figure 2A,C) and Sol (Figure 2A,D) myofibers using confocal microscopy. Imaging was performed on intact muscles within 20 min from dissection (see Methods). As shown in Figure 2A, Ets2‐EGFP accumulates in EDL myonuclei, and the nuclear/cytoplasmic ratio in EDL is significantly higher than in Sol muscle (Figure 2B; 2.49 ± 0.09 vs. 0.96 ± 0.04; p = 0.006). To assess the potential role of motoneuron innervation in regulating the subcellular distribution of Ets2, we denervated muscles to acutely remove the source of neuron‐elicited activity. Consistent with a role of intrinsic motoneuron activity (Figure 2C,D), EDL denervation reduced the average nuclear/cytoplasmic ratio from 2.49 ± 0.09 to 1.9 ± 0.15; (Figure 2E; p < 0.001) whereas Sol denervation had little effect (Figure 2F: 0.96 ± 0.05 to 1.1 ± 0.07). Importantly, high‐frequency stimulation through the sciatic nerve for 48 h increased the Sol Ets2‐EGFP nuclear/cytoplasmic ratio from 0.96 ± 0.04 to 1.42 ± 0.07 (Figure 2F; p < 0.001) but had no effect in the EDL (Figure 2E), underscoring the requirement of fast‐patterned activity for maintaining Ets2 in adult muscle nuclei. Transfection with a construct expressing unfused EGFP showed no change in nuclear/cytoplasmic ratio neither in EDL nor Sol, nor after denervation of these muscles (Figure S1). Interestingly, transcriptional autoregulation of Ets2, as reported for Ets1 [50, 51], could function as an important mechanism to stabilize higher Ets2 mRNA expression in fast muscles (Figure 1) while retaining a dynamic responsiveness to activity (see Discussion).

An Ets Binding Site in the TnI Fast Enhancer Is Necessary for Transcription in Fast Muscle

The TnI SURE and FIRE exhibit a conserved spatial organization of core cis‐elements, namely an E box, MEF‐2, SP1 and CAGG sites necessary to drive high transcription levels selectively in skeletal muscles [33, 52], suggesting combinatorial interactions of transcription factors in both enhancers are required for fiber‐type specificity. However, the factor(s) necessary to drive fast activity‐dependent transcription of TnI FIRE are unknown. Bioinformatics analysis (TransFac) for potential regulatory elements in the quail TnIf FIRE identified a sequence (5′‐GGGAAAG‐3′) conforming to the canonical Ets binding site (5′‐GGAA/T‐3′) that resides between cis‐elements for SP1 and SRF/MEF2—factors known to form ternary complexes with Ets‐domain proteins [53]. Therefore, to map the region required for fast‐muscle transcription and determine if sequences encompassing the putative Ets‐site contribute to fast TnI FIRE specificity, we had to resort to generating TnI FIRE/SURE chimeric enhancer reporter constructs that retain cis‐acting sequences shared by both enhancers (i.e., E‐box, MEF2, SP1, CAGG) that we previously demonstrated are required to mediate general skeletal muscle transcription in transgenic mice [54] and transfected rat muscles [34, 44]. A diagram of the TnI FIRE, SURE and FIRE/SURE chimeric enhancers, cloned in the context of the TnIf2100 promoter and used to analyze luciferase reporter levels in transfected EDL and Sol adult rat muscles, is shown in Figure 3A. Consistent with our earlier studies [44], we found that the relative transcription levels driven by the TnIf2100 construct containing the full‐length FIRE enhancer are approximately 10‐fold higher in the EDL than in Sol muscles (Figure 3B, left). By contrast, the same TnIf2100 construct, in which the fast TnI FIRE 144‐bp enhancer was replaced by the slow TnI SURE 128‐bp enhancer, is expressed preferentially in the Sol muscle (Figure 3B, right). Similar to the TnI FIRE, a chimeric reporter construct (FIRE/SURE 724) that retains the E‐Box, MEF2, SP1 and the potential Ets binding site from FIRE, continues to be transcribed preferentially in the fast EDL muscle. However, swapping the region encompassing the FIRE Ets site with the corresponding region of SURE (FIRE/SURE 694), which retains the E‐box and MEF2 sites from the TnIf FIRE but eliminates the putative Ets‐site, switches preferential transcription to the slow Sol muscle (Figure 3C). Taken together, these results indicate DNA sequences harboring the putative Ets‐site in the TnI FIRE are necessary for the transcription of TnIf preferentially in fast muscle.

Ets2 Binds Directly to a Site in the TnI FIRE That Is Necessary to Selectively Respond to Fast‐Patterned Activity

Using electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) with wild‐type and mutated double‐stranded oligonucleotide probes corresponding to the canonical immunoglobulin kappa (Igκ) or the putative TnIf Ets binding site (5′‐GGGAAAG‐3′), we found that nuclear extracts from C2C12 myotubes expressing Ets2 directly bind to both elements (Figure 4A). EMSA competition assays with an excess of unlabeled double‐stranded probes corresponding to the Igκ and TnI FIRE Ets sites, or probes harboring a mutation in the consensus Ets binding site, demonstrated that the observed shifts are specific for Ets binding (Figure 4A).

Next, we used a quantitative in vivo fluorescence imaging technique to measure transcription in live adult muscles [34, 44] to investigate if the bona fide Ets binding site is required for the activity‐dependent transcription of TnIf. Transcription of EGFP driven by either wild‐type (Figure 4B; TnI2100) or mutant (Figure 4C; TnI2100mutEts2) TnIf reporter constructs was measured in transfected and denervated Sol muscles before and after direct stimulation with slow‐ or fast‐patterned activity. Strikingly, a 2‐bp mutation in the Ets2 site reduces transcription by approximately 90% in response to fast activity (Figure 4D: TnI2100 123% ± 23% vs. TnI2100mut 6% ± 6%, p < 0.001) but largely fails to respond to slow‐patterned stimulation (TnI2100 −17% ± −3% vs. TnI2100mut slow 12% ± 4%). These findings are consistent with data from muscles transfected with the luciferase reporter constructs harboring chimeric fast‐slow enhancers (Figure 3), and they emphasize the critical role of this bona fide Ets site for regulating TnIf transcription in fast‐twitch muscles in response selectively to fast‐patterned activity.

Ets2 Knock‐Down Down‐Regulates Transcription in Fast‐Twitch, but Not Slow‐Twitch, Muscles

To functionally test for Ets2‐driven transcriptional activity in adult skeletal muscles and to confirm that Ets2 activity was effectively reduced by “target‐verified” siRNAs (see Methods), we began by co‐transfecting muscles with a luciferase reporter construct (3xEts2‐luc) driven by a trimerized Ets site (see Methods). The use of trimerized sites is important as Ets factors on their own, when not associated with other transcription factors, are known to bind DNA cis‐elements only weakly (see [53, 55, 56]). Consistent with the results above, transcription driven by the 3xEts2‐luc construct is approximately 2‐fold higher in the EDL than in Sol muscle (Figure 5A: EDL 100% ± 14% vs. Sol 40% ± 4%, p < 0.003). Importantly, co‐transfection of the EDL with the 3xEts2‐luc (Figure 5A) or the TnIf FIRE (Figure 5B) reporter construct with either of the two independent Ets2‐targeting siRNAs (siRNA‐a, siRNA‐b) reduced luciferase activity. Transcription from 3xEts2‐luc was reduced by approximately 60% (Figure 5A; Con: 100% ± 14% vs. siRNA‐a: 39% ± 3%, p < 0.01) and from the TnIf FIRE by approximately 50% (Figure 5B; Con: 100% ± 10% vs. siRNA‐a: 52% ± 5%, p < 0.03). By contrast, co‐transfection of an Ets1 siRNA (siRNA‐nc) with either the 3xEts2‐luc or TnIf FIRE‐luc reporter constructs in the EDL had no significant effect on luciferase levels (Figure 5A,B). In addition, Ets1 and Ets2 siRNAs had no significant effect in Sol muscles co‐transfected with either the 3xEts2‐luc or TnIf FIRE‐luc reporter constructs. These results are consistent with the preferential localization of Ets2 in fast muscle myonuclei, where it can contribute to the activity‐dependent transcription of Tnni2 and potentially other genes encoding fast muscle myofibril proteins.

Discussion

Ets2 has not previously been implicated in the regulation of muscle properties. Here we demonstrate that Ets2 binds to a site in the TnIf FIRE enhancer that is necessary to increase transcription of this isoform in adult fast‐twitch muscle in response to fast‐patterned activity. Ets2 protein accumulates selectively in innervated myofiber nuclei of the fast EDL muscle, and stimulation of denervated EDL and Sol muscles in vivo with infrequent high‐frequency activity, a fast pattern that induces slow‐to‐fast myofiber transitions, selectively increases the nuclear accumulation of Ets2. By contrast, Ets2 nuclear levels are low in innervated Sol myofibers and stimulation of denervated muscle with persistent low‐frequency activity, a pattern that promotes fast‐to‐slow transitions, does not increase Ets2 nuclear levels. Moreover, transcription from a reporter construct driven by a trimerized Ets2 cis‐acting element is more active in EDL than Sol muscle, and knockdown of Ets2, but not Ets1, selectively downregulates reporter transcription driven by either the Ets2 trimerized cis‐acting element or by the TnIf promoter in fast muscle. As discussed below, to our knowledge, Ets2 is the first factor identified to selectively increase transcription of a fast muscle gene in response to fast‐patterned activity, and it is likely to function in combination with other factors to regulate the plasticity of adult skeletal muscle.

Electrical Stimulation Parameters Differentially Regulate Skeletal Muscle Properties

Both the amount of activity and instantaneous frequency play a crucial role in affecting the transcriptome and different aspects of muscle phenotype in rodents, such as muscle fiber‐type transitions, twitch speed/duration and glycolytic/oxidative properties and endurance (rev. [9, 10, 11, 57, 58, 59]); epigenetic effects can also contribute to muscle plasticity [60]. The effects of electrical activity mimic those of exercise, including in humans, where neuromuscular electrical stimulation was found to activate about 4000 differentially expressed genes of which 80% overlap with muscles activated by exercise [26]. Fast‐to‐slow and slow‐to‐fast fiber‐type transitions require coordinated changes in the transcription of numerous genes encoding distinct sarcomeric proteins, such as those that comprise thick (i.e., MHCs, MLCs) and thin (Troponin‐I, ‐C and ‐T) filaments (rev. [9, 11, 57]). Importantly, different parameters of motoneuron activity, such as instantaneous frequency, inter‐burst frequency and total number of impulses, differentially regulate the contractile properties of skeletal muscles and the expression of genes expressing distinct MHC and TnI isoforms (see [6, 9, 10, 11]). It is therefore important to emphasize that, although the stimulation parameters used in this study are effective in regulating the nuclear residence of Ets2 and activity‐dependent transcription of TnI FIRE, different parameters may be necessary to enhance transcription of genes encoding other fast muscle proteins that contribute to skeletal muscle properties (i.e., contraction speed, endurance), and that extrinsic stimulation of muscles does not necessarily represent voluntary exercise.

Transcriptional Programs Mediating Fast‐Patterned Activity Regulation Are Poorly Understood

Compared to studies on the transcriptional mechanisms and signaling pathways that regulate the expression of slow contractile proteins and metabolic properties, little is known about factors regulating the fast muscle developmental program and activity‐dependent slow‐to‐fast myofiber transitions. Signaling pathways [21, 22, 42, 61, 62, 63, 64, 65, 66], and transcription factors that regulate the slow developmental muscle program [54, 67, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72, 73, 74] and activity‐dependent fast‐to‐slow transitions have been identified (rev. [11, 75, 76, 77]). Specifically, in the case of the TnI genes, a bicoid‐like site within the TnI SURE that binds the general transcription factor 3 (GTF3)/muscle TFII‐I repeat domain‐containing protein 1 (MusTRD1) is necessary to restrict TnIs transcription to slow muscles during development [54, 78], and a nuclear factor of activated T‐cells c1 (NFATc1) site is necessary to repress TnIs transcription in muscles stimulated with fast‐patterned activity during fast‐to‐slow transitions [34], through a mechanism shown to require repression of MyoD [79].

To our knowledge, Six1‐Eya1 [38] and MyoD [42, 43] are the only transcription factors shown to activate expression of fast muscle genes in adult muscle when overexpressed. The sine oculis Six1 and Six4 homeoproteins, originally identified by their direct binding to MEF3 [38] and TREX [37] cis‐acting elements in the glycolytic aldolase and creatine kinase enhancers, were the first factors shown to selectively regulate the fast muscle program in the developing mouse primary myotome [39, 41]. Over‐expression of Six1 can promote a shift from slow‐to‐fast muscle properties by directly binding MEF3 sites in the Myh4 (MHC‐IIB) and Myh1 (MHC‐IIX) promoters to increase transcription of these sarcomeric proteins and, in addition, increases expression of a long intergenic non‐coding RNA (linc‐MYH) through a MEF3 site located in the fast Myh locus control element that indirectly suppresses expression of slow contractile genes [36]. While Six1 directly binds MEF3 sites on fast Myh promoters and other sarcomeric genes, in contrast to Ets2, there is no evidence it regulates the Tnni2 FIRE. Moreover, whereas ablation of Six1 and Six4 resulted in the downregulation of sarcomeric transcripts (i.e., Myh1, Myh4, Tnnt2, Tnnc2), there was no evidence for effects on Tnni2 expression [39]. Moreover, inspection of transcripts regulated by conditional Six1 ablation or by long intergenic non‐coding MHC knockdown [36] did not reveal effects on Ets2 or TnIf transcript levels. The fact that Six1 regulates contractile and metabolic genes during embryonic development [39, 41], prior to the emergence of patterned motoneuron activity [80], suggests it acts as a developmental factor that functions independently of fast‐patterned motoneuron activity. On the other hand, myogenic factors like MyoD are unlikely to determine TnIf FIRE fast‐specific transcription because the FIRE/SURE expression construct harboring the E‐box from TnIs continues to be transcribed in fast muscles (see Figure 3); however, the E‐box and MEF2 cis‐acting motifs are critical for skeletal muscle‐specific transcription [31, 33, 52]. Therefore, it will be interesting to investigate in the future if Six, MyoD and Ets2 regulatory networks interact to coordinate expression of different sarcomeric and metabolic proteins in adult muscles in response to fast‐patterned activity. This is physiologically important because high‐frequency stimulation, independently of its amount, regulates twitch duration that determines the twitch‐to‐tetanus fusion frequency. In order to ensure efficient force regulation in motor units, it is important that the summation frequency is matched to the prevailing firing frequency of the motoneurons (rev. [11]).

Muscle Plasticity Operates Within a Dynamic Range

An important aspect of muscle adaptation is that it function within a dynamic range that is responsive to changing requirements. In this regard it is important that transcripts encoding activity‐responsive factors, such as NFATc1 and Ets2 (and potentially Six1‐Eya1), are expressed in both slow‐ and fast‐twitch myofibers. However, histological analysis of innervated skeletal muscle shows that NFATc1 selectively accumulates in myonuclei of slow‐twitch myofibers [8, 81], whereas Ets2 (Figure 2) and Six1/Eya1 [38] predominantly accumulate in fast‐twitch myofiber myonuclei. In a manner that is complementary to Ets2, the accumulation of the transcription factor NFATc1 in myonuclei selectively requires tonic slow‐patterned activity, whereas fast‐pattern bursts are ineffective [8, 34, 81]. NFATc1 function requires sustained intracellular calcium levels and activation of calcineurin [61, 82, 83], which indirectly increase transcription of the slow MHC [84, 85]. Importantly, activity‐dependent myofiber transitions (i.e., fast‐ to slow‐twitch) require both coordinated activation of genes that match the prevailing stimulus pattern and the repression of genes that do not. An interesting example of this is NFATc1 which, in addition to promoting the slow program, also represses transcription of TnIf in slow‐twitch muscles in response to slow‐pattern activity by directly binding to a site in the TnIs SURE [34]. Moreover, this unexpected repressive effect of NFATc1 may also indirectly downregulate other sarcomeric and glycolytic genes by blocking p300 and MyoD interactions on the promoters of fast‐muscle genes [79].

Like NFATc1, that can either stimulate (slow isoforms) or repress (fast isoforms) expression of genes to promote slow muscle properties [34, 61, 79, 85], our data suggest that similarly Ets2 may differentially regulate genes depending on ternary complexes it forms with other factors [53, 55, 56, 86, 87] to retain or adopt fast contractile properties in adult skeletal muscles. As summarized in Figure 6, we speculate that the activity‐dependent accumulation of NFATc1 and Ets2 in slow and fast myonuclei in response to slow‐ and fast‐patterned motoneuron stimuli, respectively, is critical for modulating plastic properties of adult muscles in response to changing demands. The fact that both NFATc1 and Ets2 are expressed in all muscles, but require activity for their nuclear accumulation, is important because it allows for the dynamic regulation of target genes in response to changing activity. Moreover, the recent studies demonstrating that factors augmenting the expression of either fast or slow sarcomeric genes can, in turn, promote the expression of non‐coding intragenic or intronic regulatory RNAs that coordinately repress slow or fast genes [36, 91, 92, 93], respectively, begin to elucidate mechanisms of how regulatory programs can function reciprocally to facilitate fiber‐type transitions that underlie muscle plasticity.
