SLC25A12 mitigates mitochondrial dysfunction in myoblast senescence, and alleviates cuproptosis-related changes under copper stress
Authors: Shengwei Wang, Weigen Wu, Hua Yin, Qiushuo Chen, Ling Zhang, Wen He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenia is an age-related muscle wasting condition that currently lacks specific diagnostic biomarkers and effective treatments. This study aimed to identify mitochondria-related biomarkers for sarcopenia via integrated multi-omics.MethodsHuman transcriptomic datasets and machine learning algorithms were integrated to screen core mitochondria-related biomarkers for sarcopenia. In vivo validation was performed utilizing 24-month-old naturally aging mice exhibiting sarcopenia features. In vitro, SLC25A12 was either overexpressed or silenced in C2C12 myoblasts subjected to D-galactose-induced senescence or copper stress. Pathophysiological changes and molecular mechanisms were subsequently evaluated via immunofluorescence, mitochondrial functional assays, and Western blotting. Additionally, single-cell RNA sequencing combined with a Geneformer model was employed in MuSCs for in silico perturbation analysis.ResultsTranscriptomic and machine learning analyses identified SLC25A12 as a core mitochondria-related gene, exhibiting consistent downregulation in both human sarcopenia cohorts and 24-month-old aging mice. In vitro, SLC25A12 overexpression in C2C12 myoblasts significantly ameliorated senescence induced by D-galactose and preserved mitochondrial function, highlighted by increased EdU incorporation and restored membrane potential. Furthermore, under copper stress, elevated SLC25A12 levels effectively alleviated cuproptosis-related alterations, including restricting mitochondrial copper overload, suppressing DLAT oligomerization, and partially restoring impaired EdU incorporation. Conversely, SLC25A12 knockdown consistently aggravated these phenotypes in both models. Notably, in silico Geneformer-based perturbation of SLC25A12 in MuSCs predicted its regulatory roles in cellular senescence, mitochondrial function, and copper homeostasis. In vitro experiments further demonstrated that SLC25A12 overexpression partially restored suppressed PI3K/AKT signaling under senescent stress conditions.ConclusionSLC25A12 expression ameliorates myoblast senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction, while also attenuating cuproptosis-related changes under copper stress. SLC25A12 knockdown exacerbates these phenotypes. Taken together, SLC25A12 represents a potential regulator and therapeutic target for skeletal muscle aging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00918-w.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is an age-related progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass and function [1]. It represents a major and growing global health concern, affecting approximately 10% of individuals over 60 years worldwide [2]. Patients with sarcopenia experience impaired mobility and reduced quality of life [3], along with an increased risk of comorbidities, including fractures, frailty, and mortality [4]. Beyond its clinical burden, sarcopenia imposes a substantial economic impact, costing the United States healthcare system over $18.5 billion annually [5]. The pathogenesis of sarcopenia is multifactorial, involving neuromuscular degeneration, dysregulated protein turnover, and chronic inflammation [6]. Driven by global aging, the escalating prevalence of sarcopenia demands novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Among the diverse pathological mechanisms implicated in sarcopenia, mitochondrial dysfunction has been demonstrated to be closely associated with its pathogenesis [7]. As key regulators of cellular energy metabolism, redox homeostasis, and metabolic signaling, mitochondria are essential for maintaining skeletal muscle integrity and function [8, 9]. Aging disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis through impaired biogenesis and defective mitophagy, leading to excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, mitochondrial DNA damage, and progressive muscle atrophy [10, 11]. Beyond bioenergetic dysfunction, mitochondria also participate in intracellular metal homeostasis [12]. Emerging evidence suggests that mitochondrial cuproptosis-associated alterations may also be involved in sarcopenia progression [13, 14]. Although compelling evidence links mitochondrial dysfunction to sarcopenia [15], systematic approaches for identifying and validating mitochondria-related biomarkers involved in skeletal muscle aging remain limited. Furthermore, there is currently a lack of effective pharmacological therapies for sarcopenia [16]. Together, these gaps underscore the urgent need to identify novel molecular targets and clarify the underlying disease mechanisms.

This study aimed to identify mitochondria-related biomarkers associated with sarcopenia through integrated transcriptomic and machine learning (ML) analyses, with a particular focus on SLC25A12, a cross-species dysregulated gene. We further investigated the role of SLC25A12 in mitochondrial dysfunction and senescence using combined animal and cellular models. In addition, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis combined with in silico perturbation was performed to further characterize the biological features associated with SLC25A12 in skeletal muscle aging. Collectively, this study may provide novel insights into the potential role of SLC25A12 in muscle aging.

Methods

Data availability

The microarray datasets GSE1428, GSE38718, and GSE8479 from the Gene Expression Omnibus database served as the discovery cohort [17–19]. Dataset GSE25941 from the identical database functioned as an independent external validation cohort [20], detailed in Table 1. During data integration, the ComBat algorithm in the ‘sva’ package eliminated batch effects across platforms within the discovery cohort. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) evaluated the batch effect correction efficacy prior to downstream analyses. The final analytical cohort comprised 45 sarcopenia samples and 50 control samples. The scRNA-seq data were retrieved from the Human Muscle Ageing Cell Atlas (HMA) database (https://db.cngb.org/cdcp/hlma/) under the accession numbers CNP0004394, CNP0004395, CNP0004494, and CNP0004495 [21]. Furthermore, the mitochondria-related genes (MRGs) investigated in this study were sourced from the MitoCarta 3.0 database (https://www.broadinstitute.org/mitocarta/mitocarta30-inventory-mammalian-mitochondrial-proteins-and-pathways).

DEGs analysis and functional enrichment

Differential expression analysis of the merged dataset was conducted utilizing the ‘limma’ package [22]. DEGs were defined as those with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| > 0.5. Following this, functional enrichment analyses comprising Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were executed on the DEGs via the ‘clusterProfiler’ package [23]. The significance thresholds were established at a P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25.

GSEA and GSVA

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the ‘clusterProfiler’ package to evaluate MSigDB gene sets within the merged dataset [24]. This analysis aimed to uncover phenotypic differences between high and low risk cohorts. Gene set sizes were restricted between five and 500 genes. Significant enrichment was defined as q-value < 0.25 and p-value < 0.05 following adjustment via the Benjamini and Hochberg method. Additionally, gene set variation analysis was utilized as a nonparametric approach to assess functional enrichment differences between sarcopenia and normal control samples [25], with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

The WGCNA network was performed using the ‘WGCNA’ package to identify gene modules associated with sarcopenia [26]. An optimal soft threshold power was determined based on the scale-free topology criterion to construct a topological overlap matrix. Modules comprising a minimum of 50 genes were identified utilizing a hierarchical clustering tree approach. Key genes were subsequently extracted from the module exhibiting the strongest correlation with sarcopenia.

Evaluation of immune infiltration and correlation analysis

The immune cell infiltration profile of sarcopenia was quantified utilizing both single sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) and the CIBERSORT algorithm. The differential abundance of infiltrating immune cells between disease and healthy control groups was statistically evaluated via the Wilcoxon test. To investigate the potential regulatory role of the candidate diagnostic biomarker in the immune microenvironment, Spearman correlation analysis was performed to determine the statistical association between the biomarker expression and the proportions of distinct immune cell populations.

Screening of key mitochondrial genes based on machine learning algorithms

To identify and validate mitochondrial diagnostic biomarkers for sarcopenia, three datasets (GSE1428, GSE38718, and GSE8479) were merged into a combined dataset to serve as the discovery cohort for training five ML algorithms. Only the candidate genes identified by all algorithms were retained for subsequent evaluation. Meanwhile, GSE25941 was designated as an independent external validation set to test the extracted features. First, a Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression model was constructed via the ‘glmnet’ package [27], utilizing 10-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal penalty coefficient and selecting genes with non-zero coefficients as robust features. Second, Support Vector Machine Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE) was executed using the ‘e1071’ and ‘caret’ packages with a 5-fold cross-validation approach [28], strictly defining the feature retention threshold at the minimum error rate point. Third, the random forest algorithm was implemented via the ‘randomForest’ package. As previously described [29], we optimized an initial 500-tree model based on the minimal error rate, retaining candidate genes with a MeanDecreaseGini score strictly greater than 1. Fourth, the ‘Boruta’ package facilitated the Boruta algorithm with a maximum of 500 iterations, officially confirming features only when their Z-scores significantly outperformed random shadow attributes [30]. Finally, an eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) architecture was deployed using the ‘xgboost’ package with carefully fixed hyperparameters to prevent overfitting, including a learning rate of 0.1, a maximum depth of 9, a subsample ratio of 0.7, and 200 training iterations [31]. Consistent with previous studies [32], this gradient boosting model filtered features based on the Gain metric to isolate those with positive contribution scores.

Following integration of candidate genes identified by multiple machine learning methods, their diagnostic performance was systematically evaluated in both the combined discovery cohort and an independent external validation cohort. The diagnostic performance was thoroughly measured using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy. To prioritize biologically fundamental drivers over species-specific secondary alterations, a data-driven cross-species filter was applied to ensure the evolutionary conservation of the target. Ultimately, only the target demonstrating consistent transcriptomic alterations across both human and murine sarcopenia models was selected for definitive mechanistic validation.

Pathway analysis

Pathway activities were inferred from human and mouse transcriptomic data using the ‘decoupleR’ package [33]. The t-values from differential expression analysis were utilized as the input summary statistics. The PROGENy database was employed as the prior knowledge network to provide regulatory weights. Final pathway scores were calculated by integrating the input t-values with the network weights across different groups.

scRNA-seq analysis

Pre-annotated scRNA-seq data from the HMA database, comprising 31 samples with 12 controls and 19 cases, were filtered for quality control, with doublets and ambient RNA removed using the ‘DoubletFinder’ and ‘SoupX’ packages, respectively. In silico SLC25A12 perturbation, encompassing virtual knockout and overexpression, was implemented utilizing a fine-tuned 12-layer Geneformer model [34]. Downstream pathway alterations within muscle stem cells (MuSCs) were subsequently evaluated via GSEA, with P-values adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Detailed quality control criteria, model fine-tuning hyperparameters, and simulation-specific parameters are provided in the Supplementary material 1.

Mice

Three 6-month-old and three 24-month-old male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. and maintained under standard SPF conditions with free access to food and water. The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangzhou Lingfu Top Biotech Co., Ltd. approved all procedures under protocol TOP-2SL-GZ251103.

Grip strength test

Grip strength was evaluated as previously described [35]. Mice were placed horizontally on a grid and steadily pulled backward by the tail until release. The average of three consecutive trials was calculated and normalized to body weight.

The cross-sectional area of muscle fibers

Harvested tibialis anterior muscles underwent standard HE staining. The cross-sectional area (CSA) of 50 random myofibers per mouse was quantified from transverse sections via ImageJ, utilizing image scale bars for spatial calibration.

RNA sequencing

Transcriptomic profiling of murine gastrocnemius muscles from both groups was conducted via the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. To accommodate the limited sample size, the differential expression threshold was adjusted to |log2 FC| > 0.25 and adjusted P < 0.05, a strategy minimizing false negatives while retaining key biological signals [36].

C2C12 cell culture and treatment

The mouse C2C12 myoblast cell line was obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology (Wuhan, China) and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and a 1% penicillin and streptomycin mixture. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Stable SLC25A12 overexpression was achieved via lentiviral transduction. Recombinant lentiviruses, generated in HEK293T cells using vectors (Sangon Biotech, China), were used to infect C2C12 cells followed by puromycin selection. For gene knockdown, SLC25A12-specific siRNA (Sangon Biotech, China) was transfected into C2C12 cells using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (13778150, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

To systematically evaluate the role of SLC25A12, cells were subjected to two independent stress models. For the senescence model, C2C12 cells were treated with 30 g/L D-galactose (D-gal) for 48 h as previously described [37]. Although this in vitro approach is not a classic cellular model of sarcopenia, it is widely utilized to mimic cellular senescence driven by oxidative stress [37–39]. It provides a reliable method to preliminarily explore the intrinsic pathways governing the senescence of myoblasts. For the copper stress model, cells were exposed to a mixture of 40 nM elesclomol (HY-12040; MedChemExpress, China) and 10 µM CuCl₂ (C3279; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), hereafter referred to as ES-Cu, for 2 h followed by medium replacement, as described previously [40]. To inhibit PI3K signaling, cells were treated with 10 µM LY294002 (HY-10108; MedChemExpress, China).

Within each model, cells were divided into distinct experimental groups based on Slc25a12 modulation: Vector, OE-Slc25a12, si-NC, and si-Slc25a12. These respective groups were evaluated with or without subsequent D-gal or ES-Cu treatment.

Western blotting and qRT-PCR

Total RNA from murine gastrocnemius muscles and C2C12 cells was isolated utilizing Beyozol (R0011, Beyotime, China). One-step qRT-PCR was conducted with the BeyoFast SYBR Green One-Step qRT-PCR Kit (D7268S, Beyotime, China), and relative mRNA levels were quantified via the 2^−ΔΔCt method normalized to Gapdh. For protein analysis, total lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and electrotransferred onto membranes. Blocked blots were probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies. Protein signals were visualized using a BeyoECL Star kit (P0018S, Beyotime, China). Detailed primer sequences and antibody specifications are provided in the Supplementary material 1.

Immunofluorescence

To assess cellular proliferation, C2C12 cells were incubated with 10 µM EdU for 2 h. Subsequent fixation, permeabilization, and the Click reaction were performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (C10337, Invitrogen, USA). In a separate experiment to evaluate DNA damage, cells were stained utilizing a DNA Damage Assay Kit by γ-H2AX Immunofluorescence (C2038S, Beyotime, China). Briefly, following standard fixation and permeabilization, all subsequent staining procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For both assays, nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342, and fluorescent images were acquired utilizing a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope.

Intracellular copper content and distribution

To quantify overall intracellular copper levels, sonicated C2C12 homogenates were analyzed using a specific colorimetric copper detection kit (MAK127, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Following manufacturer protocols, the optical density of the reaction mixtures was recorded at 359 nm using a microplate reader, and final concentrations were extrapolated from a standard curve. Additionally, to visualize the subcellular distribution of labile copper, living myoblasts were incubated with the fluorescent probe Coppersensor-1 (CS1, HY-141511, MedChemExpress, China) and subsequently examined utilizing the Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope.

Detection of mitochondrial function

Mitochondrial functional integrity and morphology were evaluated using a suite of fluorescent probes following standard manufacturer protocols. Briefly, mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was determined via a JC-1 assay kit (C2006, Beyotime, China), quantifying depolarization by the red-to-green fluorescence intensity ratio. Furthermore, MPTP opening was evaluated utilizing an MPTP assay kit (C2009S, Beyotime, China), which employs Calcein AM fluorescence combined with Co2 + cytosolic quenching. Additionally, intramitochondrial superoxide production and structural alterations were visualized using MitoSOX Red (M36008, Invitrogen, USA) and Mito-Tracker Green (C1996S, Beyotime, China), respectively. All fluorescent images were acquired utilizing a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope.

Measurement of adenosine triphosphate (ATP)

Following C2C12 cell lysis and centrifugation, supernatant ATP concentrations were determined utilizing a specific ATP assay kit (S0026, Beyotime, China) via luminescence detection in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was executed utilizing R software and GraphPad Prism. Data are denoted as mean ± SD. Two-group comparisons were performed via an unpaired t-test or Wilcoxon test as appropriate. For multiple group evaluations, one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post hoc analysis was utilized. Results were deemed statistically significant at P < 0.05.

GO and KEGG analyses revealed significant DEG enrichment in metabolic and structural processes, including monosaccharide and carbon metabolism (Fig. 1C). GSEA utilizing C2 reference sets was conducted, revealing a significant upregulation of aging, senescence, and copper homeostasis modules concurrent with a marked downregulation of mitochondrial functions including the citrate cycle and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 1D). Subsequent hallmark GSEA corroborated the induction of apoptosis, TNFA signaling via NFkB, and P53 cascades (Fig. 1E), paralleled by a substantial inhibition of oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism, and peroxisomal pathways (Fig. 1F). These specific pathway dynamics were consistently validated by GSVA (Fig. 1G and H), which emphasized the robust inflammatory and apoptotic activities alongside the concurrent suppression of mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Collectively, this comprehensive transcriptomic profiling strongly links sarcopenia progression to the intertwined pathological events of mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, and cuproptosis.

WGCNA and key module identification

WGCNA was performed to construct a scale free coexpression network. According to scale independence and mean connectivity, a soft power threshold of β = 6 was selected (Fig. 2A). Genes were assigned into modules and subsequently merged based on eigengene clustering (Fig. 2B) and hierarchical clustering (Fig. 2C). Module trait analysis revealed that the turquoise module had the most significant negative correlation with sarcopenia, presenting a correlation coefficient of -0.67 and a p value of 2e-13 (Fig. 2D). Within this turquoise module comprising 1622 genes, module membership and gene significance exhibited a robust positive correlation with a correlation coefficient of 0.76 and a p value less than 1e-200 (Fig. 2E). As detailed in Fig. 2F, intersecting the turquoise module genes, DEGs, and MRGs identified 30 differentially expressed mitochondria-related genes (DEMRGs). These intersected genes were subsequently extracted for downstream analyses, including the evaluation of immune infiltration patterns and the screening of diagnostic biomarkers via machine learning algorithms.

Mitochondrial genes correlate with distinct immune infiltration patterns in sarcopenia

Evaluation of the immune microenvironment via ssGSEA and CIBERSORT revealed significant immune dysregulation in tissues exhibiting sarcopenia features. Specifically, ssGSEA showed increased CD56bright NK cells, central memory CD4 T cells, and macrophages, but decreased NK cells (Figure S2A). CIBERSORT concurrently highlighted enriched follicular helper T cells and resting mast cells, alongside reduced neutrophils (Figure S2B). Furthermore, Spearman analysis demonstrated extensive correlations between the 30 DEMRGs and these immune cells. Notably, SLC25A12 correlated negatively with macrophages and effector memory CD8 T cells, but positively with Type 17 T helper cells, suggesting their immunomodulatory roles in sarcopenia (Figure S2C).

Machine learning identifies SLC25A12 and CISD1 as core mitochondrial genes in sarcopenia

To identify robust diagnostic biomarkers for sarcopenia, five complementary machine learning algorithms were applied to the 30 mitochondria-related DEMRGs. Only the consensus candidate genes that passed the screening of all five algorithms were ultimately retained. The LASSO algorithm retained 10 candidate genes using ten-fold cross-validation (Fig. 3A and B), whereas Random Forest identified 13 informative genes based on feature importance (Fig. 3C and D). SVM-RFE further selected an optimal subset of five genes with the highest classification performance (Fig. 3E and F). XGBoost and Boruta identified 27 and 26 candidate genes, respectively, based on feature importance analysis (Figs. 3G and H). Intersection analysis of the candidate genes identified by all five algorithms ultimately revealed SLC25A12 and CISD1 as the two consensus biomarkers (Fig. 3I).

Initial evaluation in the discovery cohort demonstrated excellent diagnostic efficacy, yielding an AUC value of 0.889 for SLC25A12 and 0.928 for CISD1 (Fig. 3J). However, independent external validation revealed the superior generalization capability of SLC25A12. Specifically, SLC25A12 maintained a robust area under the curve value of 0.898, notably outperforming the decreased value of 0.822 for CISD1 (Fig. 3K). Comprehensive diagnostic metrics further solidified this advantage. In the external validation set, the specificity and accuracy of CISD1 plummeted to 0.667 and 0.833 respectively, whereas SLC25A12 demonstrated exceptional stability by maintaining a robust specificity of 0.867 and a superior accuracy of 0.889 (Table S2). To identify the most evolutionarily conserved target for further investigation in skeletal muscle aging, these candidates were further evaluated in a sarcopenic mouse model.

Cross-species validation identifies SLC25A12 as a conserved mitochondrial gene in sarcopenia

We evaluated the sarcopenia phenotype during natural aging by comparing 6-month-old control mice and 24-month-old aging mice, utilizing the latter as a widely accepted in vivo sarcopenia model [41]. Aging mice exhibited a significantly reduced skeletal muscle myofiber CSA (Figure S3A), accompanied by decreased grip strength (Figure S3B). Furthermore, the expression levels of muscle atrophy markers MAFbx and MuRF1 alongside senescence markers p53 and p21 were significantly upregulated at both protein and mRNA levels in the aging group (Figure S3F and G). Collectively, these data support 24-month-old aging mice as a sarcopenic model.

To explore molecular alterations, we performed transcriptomic analysis of mouse gastrocnemius muscle tissue. The analysis identified 559 upregulated genes and 594 downregulated genes (Table S3). A volcano plot and a heatmap visualized these differential expression patterns (Figure S3C and D). Although ML analysis of human datasets initially identified both targets (Figure S4A), CISD1 was not differentially expressed in the aging mouse model (Figure S4B). In contrast, SLC25A12 demonstrated consistent downregulation across both species (Figure S3E). In vivo validation showed reduced SLC25A12 protein abundance and significantly decreased mRNA expression in the skeletal muscle of aging mice, whereas CISD1 showed no obvious changes at either the protein or mRNA level (Figure S3F and G). Therefore, we adopted a data-driven, post hoc strategy for final target selection rather than relying on a pre specified hypothesis. CISD1 was excluded due to its poor generalization in the external clinical dataset and its absence of differential expression in aging mice, suggesting it likely represents a cohort or species-specific secondary alteration. In contrast, SLC25A12 exhibited remarkable diagnostic stability in independent clinical cohorts alongside a highly conserved downregulation across species. This dual validation supports SLC25A12 as the potential central molecule of muscle atrophy for subsequent functional studies.

To strengthen the continuity between our human transcriptomic findings and in vivo pathology, we evaluated the immune landscape in aging mice. Notably, the infiltration level of NK cells exhibited a significant downward trend in sarcopenic mice, mirroring the human data (Figure S3H). Furthermore, SLC25A12 expression maintained a consistently observed positive correlation with Type 17 T helper cells across both species (Figure S3I). These in vivo features support that the role of SLC25A12 in sarcopenia involves the conserved remodeling of the local immune microenvironment, alongside its intracellular mitochondrial functions.

SLC25A12 modulates senescence-associated markers in C2C12 myoblasts exposed to D-gal

In order to investigate the role of SLC25A12 in senescent myoblasts, C2C12 cells were exposed to D-gal following previously established protocols [37]. Consistent with the in vivo findings, D-gal exposure markedly reduced SLC25A12 expression in C2C12 myoblasts. SLC25A12 overexpression efficiently restored its expression and attenuated the D-gal triggered upregulation of the senescence-associated markers p53 and p21 at both the protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 4A and B). Conversely, siRNA mediated knockdown further decreased SLC25A12 expression and subsequently enhanced the expression of these senescence-associated markers at the protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 4C and D). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed the corresponding changes in SLC25A12 protein expression following overexpression and knockdown (Fig. 4E and F), validating the successful establishment of gain and loss of function models for subsequent functional studies.

SLC25A12 overexpression ameliorates senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction in myoblasts

To assess the impact of SLC25A12 on cell proliferation and DNA damage in the presence of D-gal, we measured EdU incorporation and γ-H2AX expression. Cell proliferation was reduced following D-gal treatment, and this reduction was partially rescued by OE-Slc25a12 (Fig. 5A). Meanwhile, D-gal induced an accumulation of DNA damage, as indicated by a higher proportion of γ-H2AX positive cells. Correspondingly, OE-Slc25a12 treatment partially mitigated this DNA damage (Fig. 5B).

Furthermore, we investigated mitochondrial function using JC-1, ATP levels, MitoSOX, and Calcein AM staining. The mitochondrial membrane potential was impaired in the Vector + D-gal group compared to the Vector group, whereas OE-Slc25a12 partially restored this potential (Fig. 5C). Interestingly, OE-Slc25a12 was able to protect cells from reduced ATP production (Fig. 5D) and increased mitochondrial ROS generation caused by D-gal (Fig. 5E). The reduction in Calcein AM fluorescence induced by D-gal, a marker of mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening, was also partially reversed by OE-Slc25a12 (Fig. 5F).

Finally, we evaluated mitochondrial dynamics. Incubation with D-gal promoted mitochondrial fission, evidenced by upregulated DRP1 protein and mRNA levels, alongside downregulated MFN1 and MFN2 levels (Fig. 5G and H). Notably, OE-Slc25a12 partially suppressed these abnormal fission trends, restoring the balance of fusion and fission markers. Collectively, these findings suggest that OE-Slc25a12 protects against D-gal-induced cellular senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction.

SLC25A12 knockdown exacerbates senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction in myoblasts

To further validate the intrinsic requirement of SLC25A12, we evaluated the effects of its knockdown on cell proliferation and DNA damage. Following D-gal exposure, cell proliferation was inhibited, and the transfection of si-Slc25a12 further aggravated this reduction compared to the si-NC group (Fig. 6A). Concurrently, D-gal induced a marked accumulation of DNA damage, and SLC25A12 silencing exacerbated this phenotype, resulting in a significantly higher proportion of γ-H2AX positive cells (Fig. 6B).

Furthermore, we assessed the impact of SLC25A12 deficiency on mitochondrial homeostasis. The mitochondrial membrane potential was impaired upon D-gal exposure, and this loss was markedly worsened in the si-Slc25a12 group (Fig. 6C). Additionally, SLC25A12 depletion further decreased cellular ATP production and increased mitochondrial ROS generation in cells exposed to D-gal (Fig. 6D and E). The reduction in Calcein AM fluorescence was also more pronounced following SLC25A12 silencing, indicating an intensified opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (Fig. 6F).

While incubation with D-gal inherently promoted mitochondrial fission, SLC25A12 knockdown further upregulated DRP1 expression and simultaneously exacerbated the downregulation of MFN1 and MFN2 at both the protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 6G and H). Collectively, these loss-of-function findings suggest that SLC25A12 deficiency exacerbates cellular senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction in cells exposed to D-gal.

SLC25A12 contributes to cuproptosis-related changes in myoblasts

Transcriptomic profiling revealed disrupted copper homeostasis in sarcopenic muscle, prompting investigation of SLC25A12 in cuproptosis regulation. C2C12 myoblasts were exposed to ES-Cu to establish a copper stress model [40], in which DLAT oligomerization was markedly induced. This effect was attenuated by SLC25A12 overexpression and aggravated by its knockdown (Fig. 7A and B). Consistently, immunofluorescence confirmed reduced DLAT punctate aggregation upon SLC25A12 overexpression (Fig. 7C), whereas silencing exacerbated this phenotype (Fig. 7D).

Functionally, ES-Cu markedly suppressed myoblast proliferation, which was partially rescued by SLC25A12 overexpression (Fig. 7E), while knockdown further impaired proliferative capacity (Fig. 7F). Mechanistically, SLC25A12 was associated with alterations in intracellular copper homeostasis. Overexpression limited mitochondrial labile copper accumulation (Fig. 7G), whereas knockdown intensified copper overload (Fig. 7H). SLC25A12 partially restores PI3K/AKT signaling under senescent stress

To explore the downstream mechanisms of SLC25A12, we analyzed skeletal muscle transcriptomes from control and sarcopenic mice. As shown in Figure S5A, GSVA revealed a significant inhibition of the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade in sarcopenic mice, which was corroborated by decoupleR analysis (Figure S5B and C). Crucially, this finding is consistent with our human cohort data demonstrating similar pathway inhibition (Fig. 1H), indicating that PI3K/AKT suppression represents a conserved transcriptomic feature across species. Furthermore, stratification of the human sarcopenia cohort into high and low SLC25A12 expression groups demonstrated significantly higher PI3K/AKT signaling activity in samples with high SLC25A12 expression (Fig. 8A and B). DecoupleR analysis further supported these findings by demonstrating enhanced PI3K pathway activity (Fig. 8C). The major genes contributing to the inferred pathway activity are shown in Fig. 8D.

To achieve higher biological resolution, the investigation was subsequently extended to the single-cell level. Figure 8E presents a comprehensive cell annotation map depicting the cellular landscape. To explore the potential impact of SLC25A12 perturbation in this single-cell context, the Geneformer model was employed for in silico simulation in MuSCs. Notably, following both simulated overexpression and knockdown of SLC25A12, the myogenic regulator MYF5 was consistently ranked as the top predicted responsive gene, as visualized in the effect size scatter plots (Fig. 8F). GSEA of the simulated perturbation outputs further suggested a potential involvement of PI3K/AKT signaling (Fig. 8G). In addition, the in silico perturbations were predicted to affect gene sets associated with oxidative phosphorylation, metal ion binding by metallothioneins, copper homeostasis, and signatures of cellular aging and senescence.

To validate these predictions, we performed in vitro experiments utilizing C2C12 myoblasts. As shown in Fig. 8H, SLC25A12 overexpression did not alter basal PI3K and AKT phosphorylation or MYF5 expression under normal conditions. D-gal exposure markedly suppressed PI3K/AKT signaling and MYF5 expression. Crucially, SLC25A12 overexpression partially restored these levels, whereas its knockdown exacerbated the inhibition. These findings suggest that SLC25A12 modulates PI3K/AKT signaling and MYF5 under senescent conditions. Given that PI3K/AKT signaling contributes to the regulation of MYF5 expression [42–44], we used the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 to test whether SLC25A12-mediated restoration of MYF5 depends on this pathway. LY294002 attenuated the restoration of PI3K/AKT signaling by SLC25A12 overexpression and reduced MYF5 restoration. These findings indicate that SLC25A12 partially restores PI3K/AKT signaling in senescent myoblasts and that restoration of MYF5 expression is partially dependent on this pathway.

Discussion

The progressive decline in skeletal muscle mass and function associated with aging is a characteristic feature of sarcopenia, which severely compromises the quality of life in the elderly [4]. Mitochondrial dysfunction is closely associated with sarcopenia and age-related decline [7, 45], contributing to impaired biogenesis, altered dynamics, defective autophagy, and excessive ROS production [46, 47]. These defects exacerbate cellular energy deficits and trigger signaling pathways that accelerate protein degradation while inhibiting protein synthesis, ultimately driving muscle wasting [48, 49]. Crucially, mitochondria also mediate cuproptosis, a distinct cell death driven by copper accumulation and lipoylated protein aggregation [50]. Building upon this, we hypothesized that the mitochondria-associated gene SLC25A12 might serve as a key link between energy metabolism and trace element homeostasis during the progression of muscle aging.

This study demonstrates a significant downregulation of SLC25A12 in both human and murine sarcopenia. Mechanistically, restoration of SLC25A12 partially improved mitochondrial homeostasis, supporting cellular energy production, promoting myoblast proliferation, and attenuating senescence-associated markers. Conversely, targeted silencing of SLC25A12 exacerbated these pathological declines. In parallel, SLC25A12 upregulation alleviated cellular alterations associated with cuproptosis, including reduced intracellular copper accumulation and DLAT aggregation, whereas its depletion exacerbated these changes (Fig. 9). Functionally, these molecular alterations were accompanied by corresponding changes in proliferative capacity assessed by EdU staining, with SLC25A12 overexpression partially restoring proliferation under copper stress, while knockdown further suppressed it. Importantly, empirical observations from both overexpression and knockdown models were consistent with the single-cell in silico perturbation results. Specifically, within a senescent cellular context induced by D-gal, SLC25A12 partially restored PI3K/AKT signaling activity and MYF5 expression. Given that impaired PI3K/AKT signaling and decreased expression of MYF5, a key myogenic regulator influenced by PI3K/AKT signaling, are characteristic features of muscle aging [44, 51], our findings suggest that SLC25A12 may attenuate senescence-associated signaling dysfunction. Taken together, these findings suggest that SLC25A12 may act as a regulator of muscle aging, supporting its potential as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target.

SLC25A12 encodes the mitochondrial aspartate glutamate carrier 1. It fundamentally drives the malate-aspartate shuttle to transfer cytosolic NADH reducing equivalents into the mitochondrial matrix [52, 53]. SLC25A12 deficiency primarily affects the central nervous system and muscles [54, 55]. In the nervous system, SLC25A12 deficiency often leads to neurodevelopmental defects, including hypomyelination and increased autism risk [56, 57]. Beyond these neurological impacts, emerging evidence increasingly highlights the physiological significance of SLC25A12 in skeletal muscle. Specifically, its deficiency perturbs energy homeostasis and impairs metabolic coupling, with congenital hypotonia serving as one of the primary clinical manifestations [58]. Jalil et al. demonstrated that this pathological state is primarily driven by a compromised malate-aspartate shuttle, which leads to a marked reduction in ATP output [59]. SLC25A12 deficiency disrupts muscle redox balance, driving a highly oxidized and proinflammatory microenvironment that manifests as inflammatory myopathy [60]. Consistent with this inflammatory shift, our analysis revealed that SLC25A12 maintains a conserved cross-species correlation with NK cell infiltration and Type 17 T helper cell abundance. Because mitochondrial dysfunction is closely linked to the reprogramming of immune metabolism [61, 62], SLC25A12-mediated metabolic regulation may contribute to shaping the muscle immune landscape. Furthermore, this metabolic imbalance also increases cellular susceptibility to specific metabolic cell death. Yan et al. reported that in sarcopenic cell models, the downregulation of SLC25A12 is accompanied by the elevated expression of cuproptosis markers SLC31A1 and FDX1, hinting at an underlying mechanistic link between SLC25A12 and cuproptosis [38]. Nonetheless, the role of SLC25A12 in myoblast senescence remains unclear. To our knowledge, this study provides initial evidence that SLC25A12 is involved in the regulation of myoblast senescence. Specifically, its restoration partially improves mitochondrial dysfunction and restores suppressed PI3K/AKT signaling in senescent cells, while also alleviating cuproptosis-related cellular alterations.

Supported by convergent evidence from transcriptomic, single-cell, in silico, and in vitro analyses, our findings suggest that SLC25A12 contributes to the regulation of PI3K/AKT signaling and downstream MYF5 expression. This regulation appears to be specific to senescent conditions. While SLC25A12 overexpression did not affect basal signaling, it partially restored PI3K/AKT activity and MYF5 expression suppressed by D-gal exposure, whereas knockdown further aggravated this suppression. The observation that the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 partially blocked this SLC25A12-mediated restoration of MYF5 suggests that this specific molecular change is partially dependent on the PI3K/AKT pathway. Unlike established mitochondrial regulators such as PGC-1α and SIRT family proteins, which primarily influence mitochondrial homeostasis through transcriptional programs or enzymatic signaling [63, 64], SLC25A12 functions as a mitochondrial metabolite transporter within the malate-aspartate shuttle. Therefore, its influence on PI3K/AKT signaling is unlikely to arise from direct pathway regulation but is more likely to reflect preservation of mitochondrial metabolic homeostasis under senescent stress.

The PI3K/AKT pathway is essential for skeletal muscle homeostasis and mitochondrial function [8, 65], and its suppression directly accelerates the progression of muscle aging [66]. Accordingly, we hypothesize that SLC25A12 may preserve PI3K/AKT signaling through indirect mechanisms associated with improved mitochondrial function, such as restoration of NADH flux or attenuation of ROS accumulation, thereby mitigating senescence-associated changes. Preservation of PI3K/AKT signaling may partially contribute to the maintenance of downstream myogenic programs, including MYF5 expression. Furthermore, Gao et al. recently demonstrated that PI3K/AKT pathway activation effectively inhibits cuproptosis [67], a process known to depend on mitochondrial metabolism and tricarboxylic acid cycle protein homeostasis [50]. Together with our findings, this raises the possibility that SLC25A12-mediated maintenance of tricarboxylic acid cycle flux and redox balance may indirectly contribute to the preservation of PI3K/AKT signaling and mitochondrial homeostasis under copper stress. Accordingly, intracellular copper accumulation and DLAT aggregation may be reduced, thereby attenuating cuproptosis-related cellular alterations. These mechanistic links remain speculative and require further experimental validation.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although convergent computational and experimental evidence suggests that the PI3K/AKT cascade is involved in the protective effects of SLC25A12 against myoblast senescence, the precise regulatory mechanisms remain unclear. Current measurements, including intracellular ATP levels and MFN1/MFN2 expression, are insufficient to fully characterize the global mitochondrial profile, and non-mitochondrial specificity controls were not comprehensively included. Future targeted studies incorporating metabolic flux tracing and Seahorse metabolic analysis, together with perturbation experiments targeting specific pathways, are required to evaluate mitochondrial dependency and to further delineate the specificity of these mechanisms. Second, although our data suggest a close association between SLC25A12 and mitochondrial copper homeostasis, the molecular mechanisms linking the malate-aspartate shuttle to copper homeostasis remain to be defined. Without direct structural evidence, the proposed connection between SLC25A12 and cuproptosis should be interpreted as a preliminary hypothesis rather than a definitive conclusion. Future studies incorporating metal tracing and protein interaction assays are required to validate this regulatory axis. Third, while myoblasts treated with D-gal offer a valuable platform to preliminarily explore senescence driven by oxidative stress, this system cannot fully substitute for a validated model of sarcopenia. It lacks the complex systemic environment and mature contractile apparatus of clinical muscle wasting. Mechanistic interpretations of SLC25A12 from this model should therefore be limited to myoblast senescence. In addition, the relatively small animal cohort and absence of in vivo genetic manipulation further limit the robustness of causal inference. Future studies utilizing more mature cellular models and comprehensive animal cohorts with specific genetic interventions are required to validate these findings in systemic sarcopenia.

In conclusion, this study identifies SLC25A12 as a key mitochondria-related gene implicated in muscle aging. Restoring its expression partially ameliorates senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction. In parallel, it is associated with reduced cuproptosis-related alterations under copper stress, including decreased DLAT oligomerization, intracellular copper accumulation, and impaired proliferative capacity. Therefore, SLC25A12 represents a potential therapeutic target for skeletal muscle aging.
