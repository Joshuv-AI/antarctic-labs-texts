Social inequities in injury-attributable emergency department visits across Alberta: an ecological study
Authors: Claire Benny, Roman Pabayo, Colleen Drul, Kathy L Belton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInjury is a leading cause of death and disability in Canada, and injury rates are disproportionately higher in Alberta. Limited evidence suggests that social inequities, including income inequality, may contribute to injury risk through differential exposure to hazards and unequal access to prevention and care. The objective of this study is to examine the ecological association between income inequality and rates of injury-attributable emergency department (ED) visits in Alberta from 2013 to 2022.MethodsData on social inequities were from the 2016 Census and injury-attributable ED data were from Alberta Health (2013–2022). We analysed the association between income inequality and injury mechanisms (eg, falls, poisoning) using negative binomial regression models.ResultsOver the 10-year study period, there were 5.37 million injury-attributable ED visits in Alberta, most commonly from falls and being struck by or against objects, with moderate variation in social inequities across forward sortation areas. Higher income inequality was significantly associated with higher rates of several injury mechanisms, including burns (incidence rate ratio (IRR): 1.08, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.14), poisoning (IRR: 1.18, 95 % CI 1.10 to 1.26) and environmental injuries (IRR: 1.11, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.2). Higher income inequality was associated with a reduced risk of drownings (IRR: 0.93, 95% CI 0.90 to 0.97), falls (IRR: 0.82, 95% CI 0.79 to 0.85) and motor vehicle collisions (IRR: 0.90, 95% CI 0.87 to 0.93).ConclusionsThe results highlight that income inequality was associated with a variety of injury outcomes and overall the findings were mixed, with some risk of injuries increasing and some decreasing with higher levels of income inequality. These findings may lend strength to discussion on integrating equity-informed approaches to injury prevention strategies. Policies that improve neighbourhood safety and enhance equitable access to healthcare may help reduce the disproportionate injury burden experienced by populations experiencing social inequity.

Introduction

Injuries are a leading cause of death in Canadians and are a major driver of falling life expectancy and disability-adjusted life years,1 but injuries are not distributed equally across the Canadian landscape. For example, 20% of hospitalisations attributable to injuries occur in the prairie provinces and injury rates in the prairies are elevated compared with the Canadian average (830 per 100 000 population vs 732 per 100 000 population).1 Alberta represents the second highest rates of injury hospitalisation per capita in Canada, second only to Saskatchewan,1 and despite ongoing injury prevention efforts, rates of injury-attributable emergency department (ED) visits remain stable over the past decade.2 The total economic cost of injuries annually is nearly $30 billion and 25% of these costs are incurred in Alberta, despite Albertans accounting for only 11% of the Canadian population.34 These statistics highlight potential inequities in injury risk and severity in Alberta.

Injury risk is incurred unequally across many demographic groups and social inequities play a critical role in shaping injury risk, treatment and recovery. Existing evidence has highlighted some associations between social inequities and injury-related outcomes (eg, mortality,5 disability6),7 which may arise from geographic and structural inequities in risk, and potentially resulting from inequities in access to services, safety education and financial resources to support injury prevention measures.8 For example, in more disadvantaged areas, it is likely that the use of safety equipment like helmets or personal protective equipment may be more limited.9 Addressing such inequities requires strengthening prevention strategies and emergency response, expanding telemedicine and mobile health services and implementing community-tailored injury prevention programmes to reach hard-to-reach populations. Improving our understanding of the social inequities in injury can inform these targeted prevention programmes that reduce such inequities and ultimately prevent injuries and improve post-injury outcomes. Programmes targeted to specific groups or locations may help to bolster the success of injury prevention programming.

One key measure of social inequity is income inequality, which is characterised by the gap between the highest and the lowest earners within a given geographical area.8 The income inequality hypothesis suggests that in addition to absolute income, the distribution of incomes of those in your social environment can independently impact health. Three main mechanisms may explain how income inequality may contribute to adverse health, and, namely, injury.10 First, the divestment in human capital mechanism postulates that in areas with higher income inequality, there are more likely to be divestments in human capital programmes such as in education and healthcare. For example, in higher income inequality spaces, if there is less investment in injury prevention efforts, we may see greater risk of injury of all types.9 Social cohesion is typically lower in higher income inequality areas because people may be less likely to trust or connect with those around them. People lacking in social connections and in social isolation may experience increased risky behaviours, such as substance use,11 which may exacerbate the risk of injury. Finally, the theory of social comparison mechanism alludes that comparisons between people living in more unequal areas are greater, and people may develop feelings of shame if they have relatively more or relatively less than those around them. Ultimately, these feelings may manifest in poor mental health and avoiding health-seeking behaviours or support,12 which can result in increased risk of injury.

Few existing studies have investigated the association between social inequities and injury outcomes. Namely, a 2002 review highlighted that early literature demonstrated consistent links between social inequities and higher homicide non-fatal unintentional injury rates, but the topic has seldom since been explored.13 Social determinants of health, the social environment and injury risk and severity have changed greatly over the past 25 years,14 highlighting the need to update this body of literature. Namely, inequities have deepened as a result of inflation, rising political division and public health and other disasters, representing a growing need to reassess the ways in which social inequities contribute to injury risk.

While social inequities, such as income inequality, likely impact injury risk and severity, existing evidence is limited and outdated. This study aims to provide a foundation for understanding the ecological association between income inequality, other indicators of social inequity and injury-attributable ED visits in Alberta.

Methods

Data source

Data were obtained from Alberta Health for all ED visits that were determined to be due to injury based on the presence of an external cause of injury code [International Classification of Diseases – 10th revision, Canadian version (ICD-10-CA) codes V01–V98 (any position)] between 1 January 2013 and 31 December 2022.15 These visits were anonymised and sent to the research team at the U of A. These data contained information on the date of injury, diagnosis codes assigned to the visits and the first three digits of the patient’s postal code to determine the region within Alberta. The Alberta Hospital Morbidity file was linked with the Alberta healthcare Insurance Plan (AHCIP) Population Registration Data file to identify visits belonging to Alberta residents (at the time of visit or following fiscal year end).16 Forward sortation areas (FSAs) were assigned based on the postal code of the patient at the time of the healthcare encounter. Two FSAs were removed due to low ED counts. A scrambled identifier was used to identify unique individuals within the data. Each record was assigned a mechanism of injury based on the first listed external cause of injury code. This external cause of injury code was categorised into 29 mechanisms of injury.17 See online supplemental appendix I for ICD-10 codes for included outcomes, which were selected based on the most common mechanisms of injury as identified in the existing literature.1819 The Alberta Health data were then linked to Canadian Census data from 2016,20 approximating the mid-point of the study period. FSA-level data were used in an effort to use the most proximal geographic information, while still representing the area-level social inequities associated with injury rates. FSAs represent community-level geographic units that reflect social, economic and environmental conditions of where people live.

Sample

The sample included all Alberta residents who visited EDs in Alberta with injury diagnoses. The population excluded members of the Armed Forces, Royal Canadian Mountain Police, inmates in Federal Penitentiaries and those who have opted out of the AHCIP. ED visits by Alberta residents in the Lloydminster hospital, which is located on the border of Saskatchewan and Alberta are not included. These data are based on individual ED visits rather than individual patients, therefore multiple visits for the same patient for the same injury are reflected as additional observations. Outside of exclusions, the dataset represented virtually all ED visits noted to be attributable to unintentional injuries in Alberta and any observations with missing data were removed from the data before the research team receipt, thus case-complete data were analysed.

Measures

Outcome

The primary outcome in this study was unintentional injury-attributable ED visits (2013–2022). Injury outcomes were selected based on Association of Public Health Epidemiologists in Ontario recommended ICD-10-CA codes for injury core indicators18 and were aligned with existing studies. Outcomes included cuts/piercings, burns, drownings, falls, poisonings, suffocation, being struck by/against, environmental injuries and those injuries resulting from motor vehicle collisions in traffic. Injury outcomes were treated as count data and were reported at the FSA-level.

Exposure

The primary exposure measure was income inequality at the FSA-level, which was measured using the Gini coefficient, and z-transformed to improve interpretability aligned with existing studies.2122

Covariates

Covariates included the year of injury-attributable ED visit and other factors may confound the association between income inequality and unintentional injury risk. We adjusted for the proportion of people having completed post-secondary education, the proportion of renters, the proportion of residents receiving government transfer income, the proportion of lone parent households, the proportion of racialised households (as per Census definition), the proportion of residents identifying as recent immigrants (in the past 5 years), the proportion of males, the proportion of people aged 18 and under, the proportion of people aged 65 and older and the median after-tax household income within each FSA. Covariates were z-transformed, aligned with existing studies, for ease of interpretation.

Statistical analysis

Frequencies and proportions of FSA-level variables are reported. The analysis is ecological in nature and spans the years of 2013–2022. Negative binomial regression models were used to assess the association between FSA-level income inequality, other markers of social inequity and FSA-level injury outcomes. Negative binomial regression was selected over Poisson regression due to detected overdispersion in the count data with population counts of each FSA serving as the term. Model estimates were presented as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% CIs, representing the relative change in injury rates associated with a one-unit increase in each covariate. Each injury outcome was tested in separate models.

Sensitivity analyses

An interaction between median after-tax household income and each injury outcome was tested to identify joint effects of relative income inequality and absolute income on unintentional injury risk. Negative binomial regression was used for these models and estimates are presented as IRRs and 95% CIs, similar to the primary analyses. We used an additive interaction framework to quantify the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and 95% CIs. With this method, we assessed whether the joint effects of median household income and income inequality on injury risk exceeded (super-additive interaction), equalled or fell below (sub-additive interaction) what would be expected from the sum of their independent effects.23

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Sensitivity analyses

Across all injury mechanisms, multiplicative interactions between income inequality and median after-tax household income were statistically significant. When assessing the additive interaction, RERI estimates were all negative and statistically significant, indicating consistent sub-additive interaction between income inequality and median after-tax household income. For example, the RERI for cut/pierce injuries was −0.051 (95% CI −0.090 to −0.012), for burns −0.099 (95% CI −0.145 to −0.053) and for poisoning −0.159 (95% CI −0.213 to −0.105). Similar sub-additive interactions were found for suffocation, struck-by/against injuries and environmental injuries, and greater detail is available in table 3.

Discussion

The results of this study highlight associations between higher income inequality and increased risk of ED visits attributable to burns, poisoning and environmental injuries, and reduced risk of drownings, falls and motor vehicle collisions at the FSA-level in Alberta. Namely, areas with higher median after-tax household income and higher proportions of racialised households had lower rates of injuries overall. FSAs with higher proportions of immigrant households were associated with elevated rates of injury outcomes, which is surprising, considering that, at the individual-level, immigrant households typically experience lower rates of injury compared with non-immigrant counterparts.24 Notably, FSAs with higher proportions of people who completed post-secondary education had higher rates of cuts/piercings, burns, drowning, suffocation, environmental injuries and motor vehicle collisions, counter to individual-level evidence that highlights that higher education is associated with lower risk of injury.5 Importantly, the discordance between individual-level study findings and our area-level measures (eg, related to immigration and education) may be reflective of nuances at the individual- (eg, risk-taking or health-seeking behaviours2526) or community-level (eg, availability of healthcare services27) that are not captured in the FSA-level data. Consequently, our findings can only be interpreted at the FSA-level and are not reflective of individual-level behaviours or accessibility.

Across all injury mechanisms examined, there was a clear multiplicative interaction between income inequality and median after-tax household income. However, using an additive interaction approach, we identified a potential sub-additive effect, meaning that income inequality and median after-tax household income did not directly compound one another. The multiplicative combined effect of higher inequality and lower income on injury risk was smaller than would be expected if added together, suggesting that these socioeconomic conditions do not amplify each other’s influence, but instead contribute risk independently. This aligns with existing research that highlights that, while related, measures of income inequality and absolute income impact health risk independently. Thus, FSAs facing both higher inequality and low median household income may face comparatively less marginal injury risk than those facing the full independent effect of income inequality and median after-tax household income.

This study fills critical gaps in the existing evidence, by investigating associations between income inequality, other social inequities and injury risk in the general population, in focusing on a space with disproportionate burden of injury (Alberta), and by analysing injury trends over a 10-year period, enabling assessment of persistent social gradients in injury risk across time. Additionally, by using FSA-level measures, this study captures localised variation in socioeconomic and demographic conditions that may shape injury risk, to allow for understanding of how inequities manifest within communities. These findings of this study reinforce the need to situate injury prevention within a broader framework of social determinants of health, emphasising that reducing inequities may be useful for preventing injuries. The study findings also highlight that injury is not distributed randomly within the population, but is patterned by underlying social and structural inequities. Populations experiencing socioeconomic marginalisation or structural racism bear a disproportionate burden of preventable injuries and adverse outcomes following trauma. Compounded barriers to care, recovery and prevention in disadvantaged communities result in an augmented risk of disability, longer time-to recovery, chronic pain and mental health issues, all of which may persist throughout the lifespan.

This study is not without limitations. Injury data were from administrative ED records, which captures injuries resulting in hospital presentation. This likely underestimates the true burden of injury, as less severe cases or those managed outside of hospital settings are not captured in the data. Patterns of healthcare utilisation also differ by social position and population-based research in Canada has highlighted that socially disadvantaged populations may be more likely to seek care in EDs,28 which could bias associations between social inequities and reported injury rates, which may result in an overrepresentation of ED visits in more disadvantaged groups. That said, the opposite is true for other socially disadvantaged populations due to cultural or language barriers (eg, newcomers to Canada),29 thus the true magnitude of this bias is unclear. Additionally, the analysis is ecological and conducted at the FSA level; as such, associations observed between income inequality and injury ED visits will not reflect relationships at the individual level. The results of this study should be interpreted with the ecological design in mind to avoid ecological fallacy.30 Finally, although many variables that may confound the association between income inequality and injury rates were included, residual confounding likely remains. Indicators such as alcohol outlet density, availability of injury prevention programmes and safety enforcement were not available in the data, and thus were omitted from the analysis. Thus, residual confounding may have implications for our results, given that each of the aforementioned measures are likely associated with the exposure (income inequality) and each of the injury outcomes. Future research should consider integrating more aspects of the physical environment and programming into analyses where available.

This study showed that social inequities at the FSA level were generally associated with injury risk, with the strongest positive associations in areas characterised by greater income inequality and larger immigrant populations. Protective associations with higher income and racialised concentrated communities may reflect more and/or better FSA-level services. Together, these results underscore the importance of social inequities in population-based injury prevention strategies in Canada. Policies that improve distribution of income (eg, increases to minimum wage), access to injury prevention programming, bolster neighbourhood safety and enhance equitable access to healthcare may help reduce the disproportionate injury burden experienced by those in more socially disadvantaged areas. Integrating social equity considerations into injury prevention may be a cornerstone for narrowing disparities in injury outcomes.
