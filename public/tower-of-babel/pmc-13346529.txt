Radioprotective potential of Ophioparma ventosa (L.) Norman extract against UV-C-induced damage in Allium cepa
Authors: Oksal Macar, Tuğçe Kalefetoğlu Macar, Kadir Kınalıoğlu, Kültiğin Çavuşoğlu, Emine Yalçın
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ultraviolet-C (UV-C) radiation is capable of inducing severe physiological, cytogenetic, and biochemical disturbances in living organisms. Increasing exposure to artificial UV-C sources has intensified the search for natural radioprotective agents. Thanks to their unique antioxidant compounds and natural UV-filtering metabolites, lichens are able to withstand high levels of ultraviolet radiation. This study evaluated the protective potential of Ophioparma ventosa (L.) Norman lichen extract against adverse effects of UV-C in Allium cepa L., a well-established bioindicator for genotoxicity and oxidative stress assessment. Onion bulbs were exposed to 254-nm UV-C radiation alone or in combination with two concentrations of aqueous extract of O. ventosa (0.5 g/L and 1.0 g/L) for 72 h. Physiological parameters including germination percentage, root elongation, and weight gain were assessed. Genotoxic effects were evaluated using mitotic index (MI), micronucleus (MN) frequency, chromosomal aberrations (CAs), and DNA fragmentation (Comet assay). Malondialdehyde (MDA), proline levels, superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activities, and chlorophyll a and b contents were determined as oxidative stress markers. In addition, meristematic cell integrity was examined microscopically, and phenolic constituents of the extract were characterized by LC-MS/MS. UV-C exposure suppressed root growth, reduced MI, increased MN and CAs frequencies, elevated DNA fragmentation, and enhanced oxidative stress biomarkers, confirming its strong cytotoxic and genotoxic potential. Applied doses of the O. ventosa extract alone did not induce toxicity on A. cepa. In contrast, co-application of O. ventosa extract significantly mitigated these adverse effects in a dose-dependent manner. LC-MS/MS analysis revealed the presence of phenolic compounds, including p-coumaric acid, gallic acid, vanillin, gentisic acid and taxifolin, which likely contribute to the observed antioxidant and protective effects. Findings of the present study demonstrate that O. ventosa possesses radioprotective capacity against UV-C toxicity and highlight its potential as a natural bioresource for mitigating radiation-related oxidative and genotoxic stress in plant systems.

Introduction

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation is an important part of the light spectrum and is emitted by the Sun in substantial quantities. Although sunlight is an essential component of life on Earth, exposure to UV radiation can be harmful for all organisms, as it has the potential to damage DNA, induce mutations, and cancer1. There are two primary mechanisms by which ultraviolet radiation can damage genetic material: First, it can directly form covalent bonds between adjacent pyrimidine bases; second, it can indirectly intensify cellular stress by increasing the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS)2,3.

Solar UV radiation is usually classified into three bands: UV-A (315–400 nm), UV-B (280–315 nm), and UV-C (200–280 nm). Nearly all UV-C radiation is absorbed by the stratospheric ozone layer before arriving at surface of the Earth. Nonetheless, humans may still encounter UV-C radiation from artificial light sources4. Because the 254-nm wavelength close to the maximum absorption peak of DNA (253.7 nm), UV-C is highly effective at inactivating microorganisms and is therefore widely used in sterilization technologies5. Despite the practical uses, UV-C radiation has risks; it is cytotoxic, and may even contribute to tumor development6. Its detrimental effects arise from its ability to induce photodimer formation at dipyrimidine sites and to stimulate the production of ROS, leading to both direct and oxidative damage in DNA and other essential biomolecules3,7. In light of these detrimental effects, the depletion of the ozone layer as well as increased exposure to artificial light sources have made UV-C radiation a significant topic for concern in recent years. Although UV-C does not normally constitute a significant component of solar radiation at the Earth’s surface, it remains biologically relevant because humans and other organisms may increasingly encounter it through artificial sources used in disinfection and sterilization systems. Moreover, owing to its strong interaction with DNA and its well-documented capacity to induce reproducible oxidative and genotoxic stress, 254-nm UV-C serves as a useful experimental model for investigating radiation-induced damage and testing protective interventions. While this model reflects controlled acute exposure rather than typical environmental conditions, it provides a practical framework for mechanistic evaluation of radioprotective potential.

Lichens are symbiotic organisms consisting of a photobiont (green algae or cyanobacteria), and a mycobiont where the fungus supplies water, structural support, and essential minerals to its photosynthetic partner8. Lichens are well-known for their remarkable tolerance to high levels of ultraviolet radiation, which shows how well they have adapted to harsh conditions9. Many lichen species can survive long periods of exposure to UV-C and other types of radiation10. This resilience is strongly associated with structural and biochemical strategies that enhance photoprotection11. In particular, the lichen cortex contains various secondary metabolites, such as parietin, melanin, and usnic acid- that function as natural UV filters, effectively absorbing or screening harmful wavelengths, especially within the UV12. Their distinctive biochemical arsenal and specialized biological processes are considered strong candidates for developing protective agents against radioactive stress. Ophioparma ventosa (L.) Norman is a crustose lichen commonly found in arctic-alpine regions, where it endures intense solar radiation13. Because of its natural resilience to intense UV radiation, O. ventosa represents a valuable source of protective metabolites capable of mitigating UV-C-induced damage.

Plant-based biomonitoring approaches make use of microscopic evaluation of dividing cells, where irregularities in mitosis and the resulting chromosomal disorders provide clear evidence of genotoxic influences14. The Allium assay is a well-established plant test system widely used to evaluate genotoxicity, cytotoxicity, and oxidative stress caused by environmental pollutants and physical agents15. Its meristematic root cells are highly sensitive to DNA-damaging factors, chromosomal abnormalities, and reduced mitotic activity in Allium cepa, providing measurable markers of cellular damage16. Therefore, this model is suitable for determining whether antioxidant-rich lichen extracts can mitigate UV-induced cytogenetic and oxidative damage.

This study examined the potential radioprotective role of O. ventosa extract against toxicity induced by UV-C radiation in A. cepa root meristem cells. To understand this potential, some key parameters such as germination rate, root length, and biomass gain were evaluated. Alterations in the mitotic index (MI), micronucleus (MN) formation, chromosomal abnormalities (CA), and DNA fragmentation (Comet assay) were examined to reveal possible genotoxic effects. A series of oxidative stress biochemical indicators were also evaluated, including superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) enzyme activities, malondialdehyde (MDA) content, proline levels, and chlorophyll a and b contents. Additionally, cross-sections of the roots were microscopically observed to determine the degree of structural damage in the meristematic tissue. The phenolic composition of the O. ventosa extract was also identified to determine the active constituents that might have been responsible for its protective and antioxidative effects.

Although lichens are recognized as rich sources of UV-screening and antioxidant metabolites, their potential as natural radioprotective agents against UV-C-induced damage in plant systems remains largely unexplored. Based on this, we hypothesized that the phenolic-rich extract of O. ventosa, a lichen naturally adapted to harsh radiation environments, could alleviate UV-C induced physiological, cytogenetic, biochemical, and anatomical damage in A. cepa through antioxidant-mediated mechanisms. This study investigated whether O. ventosa exhibits radioprotective activity against UV-C-induced toxicity and whether this protection may be associated with its phenolic composition. To test this hypothesis, we evaluated the protective effects of O. ventosa extract by examining growth-related physiological responses, genotoxic and oxidative stress markers, meristematic cell damage, and the phenolic constituents potentially underlying these effects.

Materials and methods

Preparation of lichen extract and experimental design

The A. cepa L. bulbs used for the study were sourced from a local agricultural product supplier in Giresun, Türkiye. In November 2022, thalli of O. ventosa (L.) Norman were gathered from the eastern hillsides of the Polot Plateau (40°39′ N, 40°01′ E; 2450 m elevation) located in the Araklı district of Trabzon Province, Türkiye. The material was authenticated by Prof. Dr. Kadir Kınalıoğlu (Botany Division, Department of Biology, Giresun University), who verified the species using the criteria defined by Brodo et al.17. A voucher sample labelled OVN-2023 was archived in the herbarium of Giresun University, where portions of the specimen were stored under appropriate archival conditions. All stages of collection and use of biological material adhered to the relevant ethical and legal frameworks.

Collected lichen materials were rinsed to remove the particles and left to dry away from direct sunlight for one week. Desiccated lichens were ground into a fine powder with a mechanical grinder, and extracts were prepared for use in the treatments. A series of pre-germination tests were conducted to determine the extract doses to be used in the experiments. Preliminary tests identified two concentrations that were well tolerated by the bulbs but could still induce significant physiological and cellular responses. Therefore, the doses of 0.5 g/L and 1 g/L were selected as sublethal and biologically active levels for subsequent analyses. This dose-selection strategy is consistent with earlier lichen extract studies, in which preliminary trials were used to establish concentrations that are non-toxic yet biologically informative18,19.

A. cepa bulbs were placed in glass beakers so that they would be in contact with the relevant treatment solutions throughout the duration of the experiment. The beakers were maintained under dark conditions at room temperature (23 ± 2 °C) for three mitotic cycles (72 h) of the root meristem. For the experiment, six experimental groups were arranged, each comprising 50 A. cepa bulbs. The first group served as the control and was exposed to tap water throughout the experimental period. The remaining groups were treated as follows: 0.5 g/L O. ventosa extract (OVE1), 1 g/L O. ventosa extract (OVE2), 254-nm UV-C radiation (UV), 0.5 g/L O. ventosa extract + UV-C radiation (OVE1-UV) and 1 g/L O. ventosa extract + UV-C radiation (OVE2-UV) (Table 1). UV-C irradiation was applied using a hand-held UV lamp (Mineralite UVGL-58, UVP Inc., USA). According to the method described by Çavuşoğlu et al.20, UV lamp was positioned 20 cm above the beakers, and irradiation was continued for three days. During experimental period, the bulbs were kept in their respective experimental solutions to ensure that the chemical treatment and UV-C stress acted simultaneously. At the same time, a subgroup of some bulbs was continued to receive the corresponding treatment solutions until the exposure period reached 144 h. This subgroup was left unharvested to allow normal development of green aerial tissues, which were later used for chlorophyll analysis.

Experimental design.

Determination of physiological growth alteration

At the end of the three-day rooting period, the A. cepa bulbs and their roots were rinsed thoroughly with tap water to remove any extract residues. Bulbs with clean, healthy roots exceeding 1 cm in length were classified as rooted and used in the analyses. Germination ratio for each group was calculated as percentages using the following Eq. 121.

Germination (%) = (Number of germinated bulbs / Total bulbs tested) × 100 (1).

Root elongation was evaluated by randomly selecting 10 bulbs from each group. For each bulb, the average length of the roots was first calculated individually (10 adventitious roots), followed by the calculation of the mean root elongation for each group using these data. Measurements were performed with a digital micrometer with an accuracy of 0.01 cm. Average weight gain of the bulbs was determined using 10 randomly selected bulbs per group. Bulb weights were measured before and after treatments using an analytical balance (0.01 g precision), and weight gain was calculated as the difference between these values.

Determination of genotoxic effects

Genotoxic effects of the UV-C were assessed by analyzing the MI value, MN frequency, and formation of CAs. Slide preparation and microscopic observations were conducted following the protocol of Ayhan et al.22. For cytogenetic preparations, approximately 1 cm of the root tips of A. cepa were excised. The root tips were initially fixed in Clarke’s fixative (3:1 ethanol: glacial acetic acid) for 2 h, followed by immersion in 96% ethanol for 10 min. After fixation, the tissues were rinsed with distilled water and were hydrolyzed in 1 N HCl at 60 °C for 13 min in a water bath. Following hydrolysis, distilled water was used to wash the roots before 1% acetocarmine staining was applied. Then, the apical 5 mm part of stained root tip was placed on a slide with a drop of 45% acetic acid and gently squashed under a coverslip to ensure an even distribution of meristematic cells. The prepared slides were subsequently examined and photographed under a research microscope at 500× magnification.

For each group, ten preparation slides were made. For quantifying MN and CAs, 100 mitotic cells were randomly evaluated on each slide, giving a total of 1,000 mitotic cells per treatment group. MN were identified based on the criteria of Fenech et al.23, where small, clearly defined chromatin bodies that are separate from the primary nucleus are classified as MN. In addition to these parameters, cells exhibiting chromosomal abnormalities were systematically recorded and collectively classified as chromosomal aberrations (CAs). The frequencies of MN and CAs were expressed as the percentage of affected cells among the total mitotic cells counted. Similarly, for determining MI, 1,000 mitotic cells were randomly evaluated on each slide, giving a total of 10,000 mitotic cells per treatment group.

The alkaline comet assay was conducted with minor modifications following the previously established protocol of Chakraborty et al.24 Root meristems were excised and homogenized in ice-cold Tris buffer (400 mM, pH 7.5) to obtain nuclear suspensions. Microscope slides were initially pre-coated with 1% normal melting point agarose and allowed to dry for 16 h at room temperature. Subsequently, 40 µL of the nuclear suspension was mixed with 1% low melting point (LMA) agarose and immediately layered onto the pre-coated slides. After placing a coverslip, the agarose was allowed to solidify on a chilled surface, ensuring uniform thickness and proper embedding of nuclei. The coverslip was then gently removed, and a final protective layer of 0.5% low melting point agarose (80 µL) was applied to stabilize the gel and prevent damage during subsequent processing steps.

Following gel solidification, slides were put in freshly prepared alkaline unwinding buffer containing 300 mM NaOH and 1 mM Na₂EDTA (pH > 13) for 15 min to allow DNA denaturation and strand unwinding. Electrophoresis was carried out at 4 °C for 20 min under an electric field strength of 0.7 V/cm. After electrophoresis, the slides were neutralized using Tris buffer (pH 7.5). They were then stained with ethidium bromide, rinsed with distilled water, and mounted with coverslips.

DNA migrations were photographed using a fluorescence microscope. For each experimental group, DNA damage was quantified in 1,000 randomly selected nuclei using CASP image analysis software, and results were expressed as percentage tail DNA, which is a sensitive indicator of DNA strand breaks. DNA damage levels were classified into five categories. Groups with ≤ 5% tail DNA were considered to have little or no damage. Values between 5.1% and 20% indicated low damage, 20.1–40% moderate damage, and 40.1–75% high damage. Percentages ≥ 75.1% were classified as severe DNA damage. The pronounced tail DNA values exceeding 75% observed in the UV-treated groups classified severe DNA fragmentation25.

Determination of biochemical effects

Determination of MDA content

Oxidative stress-related membrane damage of cell in A. cepa was assessed by MDA content, a well-known marker of lipid peroxidation. MDA levels were evaluated using a modified thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method based on Unyayar et al.26. Root tissues (0.5 g) were homogenized in 1 mL of 5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was mixed in a 1:1 ratio with 20% TCA containing 0.5% TBA and incubated in a boiling water bath for 25 min. The reaction was immediately terminated by cooling the tubes in an ice bath, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. Absorbance of the clear supernatant was measured at 532 nm, and MDA levels were expressed as µM g-1 fresh weight (FW).

Preparation of extracts for SOD and CAT determination

The activities of SOD and CAT enzymes, which constitute major components of the antioxidant defense system, were measured as indicators of the enzymatic response to oxidative stress. The same extraction protocol was employed to prepare enzyme extracts for SOD and CAT activity analyses, following Zou et al.27. Briefly, 0.5 g of root tissue was homogenized in 5 mL of ice-cold sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), and the homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was used as the extract for enzyme assays.

Determination of SOD activity

SOD activity was determined according to the method of Beauchamp and Fridovich28, based on the inhibition of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) photoreduction. The reaction mixture contained sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), methionine, NBT, EDTA-Na₂, riboflavin, polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), and deionized water. The reaction was initiated by adding 0.01 mL of enzyme extract, and the tubes were exposed to light for 10 min under two 15 W fluorescent lamps. The reaction was ended by placing the tubes to darkness for 15 min, and absorbance was measured at 560 nm. SOD activity was expressed as units per mg fresh weight (U/mg FW).

Determination of CAT activity

Activity of CAT enzyme was measured following the method of Beers and Sizer29 by monitoring the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). The reaction mixture consisted of sodium phosphate buffer, H₂O₂, and deionized water. The reaction was started by the addition of 0.2 mL of enzyme extract, and the decrease in absorbance was recorded at 240 nm. Catalase activity was expressed as OD240nm/min./g FW.

Determination of proline content

Proline content was quantified following the method described by Bates et al.30 After the exposure period, 0.5 g of fresh onion root tissue was carefully homogenized in 10 mL of 3% sulfosalicylic acid using a mortar and pestle. The homogenate was then filtered with Whatman No. 2 filter paper to obtain a clear extract. For color development, 2 mL of the filtrate was mixed with 2 mL of acid-ninhydrin and 2 mL of glacial acetic acid. The reaction mixture was incubated in a water bath at 100 °C for 1 h and subsequently cooled in an ice bath to terminate the reaction.

Following cooling, 4 mL of toluene was added to each sample, and the mixture was vigorously shaken for 15–20 s to allow phase separation. The upper toluene phase containing the chromophore was carefully collected, and its absorbance was measured at 520 nm using toluene as the blank. Proline concentration was determined from a standard calibration curve and calculated using Eq. 2. Results were expressed as µmol proline per gram fresh weight (FW).

Proline (µmol/g FW) = [(C × V) / 115.5] / (FW × 5) (2).

C is the proline concentration (µg mL⁻¹), V is the toluene volume (mL), and FW is the fresh weight of the root sample (g).

Determination of Chlorophyll a and Chlorophyll b Contents

At the end of the extended 144 h of exposure period, chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b contents were determined in fresh leaves. Pigment extraction and quantification were carried out following a modified protocol adapted from Kaydan et al.31. Briefly, 0.2 g of fresh leaf tissue was finely homogenized in 80% acetone and allowed to extract in 5 mL of the same solvent under dark conditions for seven days. After extraction, the homogenate was filtered to remove pigment-free residues, and the filtrate was supplemented with an additional 5 mL of 80% acetone. To minimize solvent loss, the extract was immediately centrifuged at 3,000 rpm, and the supernatant was carefully collected. This extraction step was repeated once more using an additional 5 mL of 80% acetone, and the resulting supernatants were pooled.

Absorbance values of the final extract were read at 645 nm and 663 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Chlorophyll a and b concentrations were subsequently calculated according to the equations described by Witham et al.32 (Eqs. 3 and 4).

Chlorophyll a (µg g⁻¹ FW) = (12.7 × OD₆₆₃ − 2.69 × OD₆₄₅) × (V / (1000 × FW)) (3).

Chlorophyll b (µg g⁻¹ FW) = (22.9 × OD₆₄₅ − 4.68 × OD₆₆₃) × (V / (1000 × FW)) (4).

OD represents the optical density measured at the indicated wavelengths (nm), V is the final volume of the extract (mL), and FW is the fresh weight of the leaf tissue (g).

Determination of meristematic cell damage

Meristematic damage was assessed using ten randomly selected rooted onion bulbs from each experimental group. Cross-sections were manually prepared from adventitious root tips, and ten permanent slides were made per group. The preparations were stained with 1% methylene blue and examined under a research light microscope at 400× magnification. Meristematic cells were classified into four categories: undamaged, slightly damaged, moderately damaged, and severely damaged33.

Determination of phenolic composition

Determination of phenolic compounds in O. ventosa was carried out using an LC-MS/MS analytical platform at Hitit University Scientific and Technical Research Center (HÜBTUAM). For extraction, 0.5 g of each sample was treated with 10 mL of a methanol-dichloromethane solvent mixture (8:2, v/v) and subjected to ultrasonic extraction in a KUDOS ultrasonic bath for 2 h. Following extraction, the extracts were filtered through 0.45 μm syringe filters and transferred into analysis vials. Phenolic compound contents were quantified and expressed as micrograms per gram of plant material (µg g-1).

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences among experimental groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple range test, and statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05. Independent onion bulbs exposed to each treatment were considered biological replicates.

Results and discussion

Table 2 summarizes the physiological toxicity of UV-C radiation in A. cepa root meristem cells and the protective effect of O. ventosa extract against this toxic effect. All bulbs were rooted in the OVE1 and OVE2 groups that received only O. ventosa extract, similar to those in the control group. In addition, the mean values of root elongation and weight gain of these groups were not statistically different from the control group values (p > 0.05). On the other hand, UV-C exposure reduced the rooting percentage of the UV group to 65%. Additionally, root elongation and weight gain values of this group decreased by 81.8% and 81.1%, respectively, compared to the control group. In the OVE1-UV and OVE2-UV groups treated with O. ventosa extract simultaneously with UV-C application, the rooting percentage was determined as 72% and 84%, respectively. Accordingly, O. ventosa application reduced but did not completely prevent UV-C induced rooting inhibition. A comparable outcome was also observed in terms of root elongation and weight gain. The root elongation of OVE1-UV and OVE2-UV groups was 2.4 and 3.0 times that of the UV group, respectively. In comparison with the UV group, the weight gain of the OVE1-UV increased by 84.9% and that of the OVE2-UV group by 174.1%. These findings revealed that both application doses of O. ventosa extract applied alone did not cause physiological damage to A. cepa. However, when lichen extract was administered simultaneously with UV-C, it exhibited a mitigating effect on the physiological toxicity induced by UV. This effect was observed to be dose-dependent.

In the literature, there are few studies showing that lichen extracts or microorganisms associated with lichens can have an effect on plant growth and development. Our findings were consistent with those of Kalefetoğlu Macar et al.18, who reported that Ramalina farinacea extract alleviated nickel-induced growth retardation in A. cepa in a dose-dependent manner. Additionally, Aslan et al.34 reported that various lichen extracts used as plant nutrients increased plant height, the content of certain nutrients, dry matter ratio, plant weight, and amino acid levels in Zea mays. Among the other lichen extracts, Lobaria pulmonaria extract was the most effective factor in promoting growth. In a separate study, researchers isolated a Bacillus licheniformis strain from the Collema undulatum lichen species and observed that it increased the length of corn roots, stems, and leaves, as well as leaf chlorophyll levels35.

UV-C radiation has been employed in numerous plant species to mitigate the development of diseases, functioning as both a disinfectant and a stimulator of plant defense systems36. However, as revealed by Bhatt et al.37, the stimulatory effect of UV-C application on different varieties of the Amaranthus hypochondriacus plant can turn into an inhibitory effect depending on both the plant variety and the duration of application. Our findings corroborate the findings of the prior study of Çavuşoğlu et al.20, which demonstrated that 254-nm UV-C radiation led to a reduction in root length, overall weight gain, and rooting percentage in A. cepa. Additionally, Otake et al.38 demonstrated that exposure to 254-nm and 222-nm radiation resulted in reduced leaf and root development and biomass loss in Arabidopsis. Moreover, UV-C treatment was reported to reduce total dry matter, leaf area and photosynthetic activity in the ornamental plant Catharanthus roseus39.

The inhibition of growth-related processes in A. cepa by UV-C may be related to numerous factors. Indeed, proteins, lipids, DNA, and essential functions such as photosynthesis are the primary targets of UV radiation in plant cells. Moreover, it has been reported that mitochondria and chloroplasts generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) in response to UV radiation40. ROS-induced oxidative stress has been demonstrated to result in membrane damage and the development of genotoxicity, which is associated with the inhibition of cell proliferation or the induction of cell death20. Furthermore, UV-C radiation can directly affect DNA, causing the formation of pyrimidine dimers, which can interfere with vital biological processes by inhibiting cell division and protein synthesis41. UV-C may suppress mitotic activity, which plays an important role in growth, by disrupting microtubule organization, altering water permeability and turgor, or reducing indole acetic acid hormone16,42.

Meristematic cells in plant roots exhibit a unique vulnerability to external influences due to their constant mitotic activity and absence of cellular differentiation. These characteristics render them excellent biological materials for cytogenetic evaluations43. In this study, MI, MN, CAs, and Comet assays were employed to examine the genotoxic effects of UV-C and lichen extract on A. cepa root meristem cells (Tables 3 and 4; Fig. 1). No genotoxic alterations were observed in the OVE1 and OVE2 groups, similar to the control (p > 0.05) (Tables 3 and 4). This finding indicates that the lichen extract is cytogenetically safe at both application doses. On the other hand, UV-C radiation caused a remarkable suppression of cell division and a dramatic increase in genetic damage.

MI is a key indicator of cell division and reflects how strongly cytotoxic stress interferes with mitosis. In the UV group, the level of MI decreased to approximately half of the control value (Table 3). Such a dramatic decrease suggests that many meristematic cells were unable to initiate or finish mitosis20. Since cell division is closely associated with root development, the decrease in MI indicates that UV-C radiation primarily inhibits root elongation by suppressing cell proliferation rather than by inducing cell death. Similarly, de Souza et al.44 demonstrated that exposure to 254 nm UV-C decreased both MI and radicle formation in Allium seeds, confirming its cytotoxic effect, which is consistent with our findings in Allium bulbs. Furthermore, in a study conducted by Bajaj et al.45, A. cepa root meristem cells were exposed to UV-C radiation at a wavelength of 254 nm for periods ranging from half an hour to four hours, and it was determined that even a half-hour exposure caused a significant decrease in MI. UV-C exposure significantly increased MN and CAs frequencies while decreasing MI compared to the control group (p < 0.05) (Table 3). The number of MN rose from almost negligible levels (0.50 ± 0.53) to 80.8 ± 5.05 following UV-C exposure, indicating a substantial genotoxic stress (Table 3; Fig. 1a).

MN is a small, separate nuclear structure that arises from whole chromosomes or broken chromosome fragments which fail to rejoin the main nucleus during mitosis46. On the other hand, the Comet assay has become a widely used system for identifying single-strand breaks, cross-linked DNA, and alkali-sensitive regions within individual cells. In this system, breaks in the DNA loosen the supercoil, causing DNA strands to extend outside the nucleus in an electric field, forming a Comet tail47. A positive relationship between MN frequency in plants and Comet assay-based DNA damage measures has been confirmed in numerous studies25,48. It has also been reported that DNA breaks lead to a drastic accumulation in MN, acentric fragments and lagging chromosomes49,50.

In the present study, UV-C administration led to notable alterations in the Comet assay parameters (Table 4). Representative Comet assay micrographs illustrating DNA damage patterns in different treatment groups are presented in Fig. 2. In the control, OVE1 and OVE2 groups, head DNA percentages were high (95.6–99.9%) and tail DNA percentages were low (0.10–4.40%), suggesting that the lichen extract alone did not cause cytogenetic damage. A decrease in the head DNA ratio indicates that part of the DNA has migrated to the “tail” side. The tail DNA ratio was used to establish the Comet scale, which indicates DNA damage. Considering this scale, DNA damage in the control and O. ventosa-treated groups was “negligible or minimal” (Table 4). In contrast, DNA damage in the UV group was classified as “high damage” when considering the tail DNA ratio, which was determined to be 46.5%. The tail length and tail moment in this group were also the highest among the other groups. A long tail indicates how far the DNA fragments extend into the “tail” section and suggests further DNA breaks51. On the other hand, an increase in tail moment reflects the severity of the genotoxic effect52.

Another genotoxic effect associated with UV-C exposure was the formation of numerous CAs, which were not frequently observed in the control, OVE1, and OVE2 groups (Table 3). Fragment (Table 3; Fig. 1b), sticky chromosome (Table 3; Fig. 1c), vagrant chromosome (Table 3; Fig. 1d), unequal distribution of chromatin (Table 3; Fig. 1e), bridge (Table 3; Fig. 1f), binuclear cell (Table 3; Fig. 1g), and irregular mitosis (Table 3; Fig. 1h) were the most prevalent CA types seen in the UV group. The most common CA, chromosome fragment, is an indicator of chromosome breaks and may be the result of anaphase or telophase bridges53. Fragments may also occur simultaneously with loss-of-function defects in spindle fibers18. The occurrence of DNA strand breaks and the inadequacy of DNA repair mechanisms in eliminating DNA errors have also been confirmed by the Comet assay, which monitors the formation of tail DNA percentage (Table 4). Stickiness is an error associated with spindle fiber abnormalities that can lead to cell death as an irreversible CA54. As the second most frequent CA in the UV group (Table 3), sticky chromosomes indicate severe disruption of mitotic division. The binding of the ends of chromosomes that have lost their telomeres results in sticky chromosome formation. Chromosome condensation, nucleoprotein dissolution, and DNA depolymerization may also be among the causes of stickiness. Bridge formation may occur between chromosomes that cannot be separated due to stickiness55. Bridge was one of the CA types frequently observed in the UV group (Table 3) and may be associated with para-centered inversions, fusion events, and replication enzyme defects, in addition to breakage and adhesion events56. The vagrant chromosome, which was quite abundant in the UV group (Table 3), also results from a failure in the organization and function of the spindle apparatus. The vagrant chromosome moves toward the poles of the dividing cell before its own chromosome set. This error generally results in unequal distribution of chromosomes in the daughter cells55. Another type of cell with two nuclei (binuclear) (Table 3), which was one of the most common CA types in the UV group, usually develops in association with problems such as severe DNA damage, disruption of microtubule organization, and failure of spindle fibers to function, and as a result, cells that have failed to undergo cytokinesis become binuclear57.

In the OVE1-UV and OVE2-UV groups, co-treatment with O. ventosa extract partially restored mitotic activity and reduced genotoxicity compared with the UV group. Specifically, MI and head DNA (%) increased, whereas MN and CA frequencies as well as tail DNA (%) decreased, indicating an attenuation of UV-C-induced DNA damage in a dose-dependent manner (Table 3). The OVE2-UV group showed results closer to the control values than the OVE1-UV group in terms of all these parameters.

The biochemical changes induced by UV-C treatment in A. cepa root meristem cells were evaluated through activities of SOD and CAT enzymes and levels of MDA, proline, and chlorophyll (a and b) contents (Table 5). Mean values for these parameters did not show a statistically significant difference between the control, OVE1, and OVE2 groups (p > 0.05). On the other hand, MDA content of the UV-C treated group was 3.07 times that of the control group. In addition, SOD and CAT activities exhibited 2.11-fold and 2.87-fold increase, respectively, in the UV group compared to the control group. Furthermore, proline content significantly increased in the UV group compared to the control (p < 0.05), showing a 2.50-fold elevation.

It has been previously reported that UV-C application accelerates antioxidant enzyme activities by causing the formation of radicals such as superoxide and hydrogen peroxide58. These oxidant substances are not only molecules that attack membranes, proteins, or DNA, but also signals that regulate growth and development, metabolite synthesis, and stress responses in plants59. Indeed, disruption of the balance between radical scavenging and ROS production can lead to oxidative stress, and consequently, signaling activity may be replaced by damage60. The destructive effect of UV radiation on membranes may develop due to free radicals or as a result of the direct absorption of these rays by the double bonds of unsaturated fatty acids61. Free radicals target polyunsaturated fatty acids, initiating a chain reaction called lipid peroxidation, which damages membranes. MDA begins to accumulate as an indicator of oxidative degradation of membrane lipids62. Previous studies have shown that exposure to UV-C radiation leads to increased ROS production in plants, resulting in lipid peroxidation and enhanced MDA accumulation20,58,63. SOD and CAT are enzymes that scavenge superoxide and hydrogen peroxide radicals, respectively, and work as a team of antioxidant systems. The concurrent elevation in SOD and CAT activities along with MDA content may indicate that UV-C exposure triggered excessive ROS production and an enzymatic antioxidant response. Our results confirm previous studies that stress associated with UV-C exposure leads to SOD and CAT activation in A. cepa root meristem cells16,20. Ultimately, when applied in controlled and low doses to plants for pathogen suppression, hormetic effects, or post-harvest disinfection64,65, UV-C radiation can become harmful depending on the dose and duration of exposure. Centeno-Rodríguez et al.66 demonstrated that the oxidative signal induced by low-dose controlled UV-C application in broccoli sprouts activates enzymatic and non-enzymatic defense mechanisms, including CAT activity and proline biosynthesis, to increase antioxidant capacity. It was also reported that both lipid peroxidation and proline accumulation were enhanced in Oryza sativa, Brassica juncea, and Vigna radiata following UV irradiation, and proline accumulation increased in parallel with UV application time67. Similarly, another defense activated in A. cepa cells in response to UV exposure was an increase in proline in our study (Table 5). It has been reported that exogenously applied proline protects growth processes, strengthens antioxidant defense, upregulates genes involved in stress response, and suppresses oxidative stress in plants68.

Among the photosynthetic pigments involved in the conversion of sunlight into chemical energy, chlorophyll a is the primary molecule that absorbs red and blue light, while chlorophyll b extends the range of absorption and increases light utilization efficiency69. The destructive effect of UV radiation on chlorophyll pigment varies depending on the type of plant and its growing conditions61. UV-C application resulted in a 64.70% and 83.26% decrease in chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b levels, respectively, compared to the control group (Table 5). In Portulaca oleracea leaves, increasing UV-C intensities reduced chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b contents, accompanied by elevated indicators of oxidative stress, such as CAT and peroxidase activities70. Moreover, Sinaga et al.71 demonstrated that long-time UV-C exposure was destructive for chlorophyll pigments in Brassica oleraceae. UV-C is a high-energy radiation and may have caused the destruction of chlorophyll pigment by increasing the production of ROS, especially singlet oxygen, in chloroplasts72. ROS may also result in oxidative destabilization of the interaction of chlorophylls with proteins and peroxidation of thylakoid membrane lipids73. According to Gill and Tuteja74, stress conditions can suppress the activity of enzymes involved in chlorophyll pigment biosynthesis. The significant decrease in chlorophyll b levels indicates the possibility of an imbalance in the light-harvesting complex proteins.

Simultaneous application of O. ventosa extract with UV-C decreased MDA and proline levels as well as antioxidant enzyme activities, while increasing chlorophyll pigment levels in the OVE1-UV and OVE2-UV groups compared to the UV group (Table 5). Our findings were consistent with previous reports indicating that extracts from various lichens mitigate oxidative stress associated with various abiotic stressors, including UV radiation18,19,75.

Investigation of meristematic cell damages revealed that UV-C (254 nm) radiation triggered injuries in the A. cepa root meristems (Table 6). The UV group displayed severe epidermal cell damage (Fig. 3b), flattened nucleus (Fig. 3d), cortex cell damage (Fig. 3g), and cortex wall thickening (Fig. 3h), while giant nuclei (Fig. 3e) were moderately frequent. The results obtained in this study are consistent with those of previous studies, which have indicated that UV-C causes structural defects in the root meristem tissue and cells of A. cepa16,20. Çavuşoğlu et al.20 noted that exposure to 254-nm UV-C radiation induces pronounced oxidative stress in A. cepa meristematic cells, resulting in ultrastructural damage such as membrane disruption, epidermal and cortical tissue degeneration, nuclear abnormalities, and the formation of necrotic zones. On the other hand, thickening of the cell walls in cortical and conductive tissues can be interpreted as a defense response, reflecting structural reinforcement against penetrating radiation20.

However, the fact that no detectable damage was observed in the OVE1 and OVE2 groups (Fig. 3a, c and f), where O. ventosa extract was applied alone (Table 6) indicates that the extract does not exhibit harmful effects on A. cepa meristem at the concentrations applied. Notably, co-treatment markedly reduced UV-C damage in a concentration-dependent manner. OVE2-UV reduced damage largely to slight or no damage, with giant nucleus completely normalized. These trends suggest a dose-dependent protective effect of O. ventosa extract against UV-C.

When all physiological, genotoxic, and biochemical findings were considered together with meristematic cell integrity, the extract did not show any toxic effects on A. cepa at the tested concentrations. This is consistent with previous studies showing that lichen extracts such as R. farinacea and Ramalina pollinaria did not induce phytotoxic or cytogenetic damage in A. cepa when applied alone, but rather showed protective effects against abiotic stressors18,19. Instead, the results suggest that it has radioprotective potential, likely due to its antioxidant and bioactive phytochemical content. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have reported that extracts of R. farinacea18 and R. pollinaria19 alleviate heavy metal induced toxicity in A. cepa, mainly by reducing ROS generation and protecting cellular integrity with their rich phenolic content. Similarly, lycopene, a strong antioxidant effectively attenuated 254-nm UV-C-induced oxidative and cytogenetic injury in A. cepa roots16.

Lichens generally live in areas exposed to excessive light, so they are frequently exposed to high levels of UV radiation. If this excess energy is not mitigated, it increases ROS formation, which attacks the photosynthetic apparatus, leading to photoinhibition and photo-oxidative stress76. For this reason, phenols play a vital role in lichens, both in UV screening and in combating the resulting ROS8. The specific phytochemical profile of O. ventosa correlates with the above-mentioned physiological and biochemical results obtained in this study. The high concentration of p-coumaric acid is of particular significance; its well-documented role in neutralizing ROS suggests it may be a primary factor in mitigating the UV-C induced damage observed in A. cepa cells77. Gallic acid, the second most abundant phenol in O. ventosa extract, is also a potent phenolic antioxidant and is widely reported to exhibit strong radical scavenging and metal chelation properties. Similarly, other phenols in O. ventosa extract, such as vanillin, gentisic acid, taxifolin, naringenin, and galangin, are known to modulate oxidative stress directly through antioxidant mechanisms or indirectly by regulating cellular defense pathways, even at low concentrations78. Such flavonoids may exert synergistic or complementary biological effects within complex phytochemicals in the lichen79. The rich phenolic composition of the extract is consistent with the biochemical responses observed in this study. The function of phenolic compounds as ROS scavengers may explain the decrease in lipid peroxidation (MDA) and proline accumulation caused by UV exposure in the OVE1-UV and OVE2-UV groups. Lichens have various phenolic secondary metabolites. These compounds’ multiple phenolic –OH groups confer strong antioxidant chemistry: they can directly scavenge reactive species and chelate metal ions, interrupting oxidative chain reactions80. They also enhance endogenous defenses (e.g. upregulating SOD, CAT, glutathione) and mitigate genotoxic endpoints81. For example, a phenolic-rich extract of the lichen R. pollinaria dramatically reduced A. cepa root lipid peroxidation and DNA fragmentation under metal-induced stress, illustrating how lichen phenols protect plant tissues19. The exogenous antioxidant support provided by phenols appears to support the endogenous enzymatic defense system, including SOD and CAT. Concurrently, the improvement of chlorophyll content was demonstrated to offer effective protection against UV-induced photo-oxidative damage. In addition to antioxidant-mediated protection, part of this effect may also be related to UV-screening or absorption properties of lichen metabolites, which may function in a sunscreen-like manner by reducing radiation penetration8,12. However, because UV absorption was not directly measured, this mechanism remains a plausible interpretation requiring further study.

Findings of the study suggest that the protective action of O. ventosa cannot be explained solely by a dose-dependent response. Its mitigating effect may also be associated with multiple complementary mechanisms, including scavenging of ROS, support of antioxidant defenses, maintenance of chromatin stability, and reduction of oxidative DNA damage. The simultaneous improvement observed in cytogenetic, biochemical, and meristematic parameters suggests that the extract did not act only through a quantitative dose effect, but may have contributed to a broader modulation of cellular stress responses. Collectively, the present results provide evidence that O. ventosa extract may function as a natural protectant against UV-C–induced meristematic damage, highlighting its potential application as a plant-based radioprotective agent.

Conclusion

Exposure to 254-nm UV-C radiation caused clear and multi-layered damage in A. cepa. In contrast, co-treatment with O. ventosa extract alleviated many of these harmful effects. Extract alone did not result in detectable toxicity under the tested conditions, suggesting a favorable biological profile at the applied concentrations. The observed radioprotective effect is likely linked to the phenolic profile of the O. ventosa extract, characterized by p-coumaric acid and complemented by gallic acid and minor flavonoids. These compounds are widely recognized for their synergistic antioxidant capacities and support the observed radioprotective effect.

The findings suggest that O. ventosa extract has potential to mitigate UV-C induced stress in plant systems. Beyond its relevance to stress physiology, these findings may provide a basis for future studies exploring plant-derived approaches to mitigate radiation-induced damage. A limitation of the present study is the absence of a reference antioxidant control for direct efficacy comparison. However, future research should focus on determining individual bioactive components, identifying their specific molecular modes of action, evaluating their efficacy in different biological models, as well as incorporating established antioxidant standards for comparison.
