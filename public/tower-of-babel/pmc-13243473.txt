Influence of graphene oxide (GO) on thermo-mechanical performance of fly ash-steel slag based geopolymer composite: an experimental and numerical approach
Authors: Ranjit Singh, Ashwin Raut, Supriya Janga, Adeolu Adediran, Shaswat Kumar Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study demonstrates the feasibility of incorporating graphene oxide (GO) as an additive to enhance the mechanical and thermal properties of fly ash (FA) and steel slag (SS)-based geopolymer composites. A series of experiments and numerical simulations was conducted to evaluate the effect of GO on the material’s thermo-mechanical behaviour. Heat transfer in both solid and fluid domains was analysed using ANSYS Fluent, whereas structural deformation and stress distribution were evaluated through static structural analysis using ANSYS Mechanical. The results showed that adding GO reduced the setting time and flowability, while it enhanced the strength and microstructure. There was an increase in 32.5% strength for the GO-modified geopolymer along with an increase in the bulk density (2298 kg/m³), thermal conductivity (1.85 W/mK), and heat capacity (6.92 MJ/m³K). While subjected to thermal exposure, the GO-modified specimens showed lower thermal deformation by approximately 20% and limited thermal stress to around 2 MPa compared to the reference mix, as determined by numerical simulation. The enhancement in the developed geopolymer was due to the GO’s micro-filling, nucleation, and nano-reinforcement effects, which enhanced matrix densification and thermal stability. The findings of the study will contribute to the understanding of the influence of GO in geopolymer composites and offer valuable guidance for improving the low and high-temperature performance of FA-SS-based geopolymer composites containing GO in construction applications.

Introduction

The production and widespread use of ordinary Portland cement (OPC) is well-known for their significant environmental impacts, particularly due to the high emission of greenhouse gases (GHGs) during its manufacturing stage1. These emissions contribute substantially to global warming and have adverse effects on the earth’s ecosystem. Given the urgent need to address these environmental concerns, the scientific community has been actively searching for alternatives to reduce or eliminate the dependency on OPC for the synthesis of construction materials. In recent years, numerous researchers have made strides in identifying and providing alternatives that can partially or completely replace OPC2–5. While some progress has been achieved, OPC-based materials continue to dominate the construction industry due to their widespread availability and established performance standards. To completely phase out OPC-based products, it is essential to identify a material that not only matches but exceeds the performance of OPC across multiple dimensions while being developed with relative ease.

One major drawback of OPC-based materials is their vulnerability to fire. As temperatures rise, their strength gradually declines at different stages6,7. The extent of damage largely depends on the intensity and duration of heat exposure. Up to 300 °C (572 °F), the impact is relatively minor, though some moisture loss may occur, leading to small cracks. Between 300 °C and 450 °C (572 °F to 842 °F), calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) starts to dehydrate, causing noticeable strength reduction and increased cracking due to thermal expansion differences8. When temperatures reach 450 °C to 600 °C (842 °F to 1112 °F), calcium hydroxide (CH) begins to break down, leading to even greater strength loss and a higher risk of spalling, as vaporized water builds internal pressure9,10. This structural deterioration highlights a critical weakness in traditional cement-based materials, emphasizing the need for alternatives with better fire resistance. Given both environmental and thermal concerns, attention has shifted toward geopolymer-based materials as a promising solution. These materials not only offer enhanced strength and durability, but also significantly lower carbon emissions compared to conventional cement11,12. However, despite their potential, there are still challenges to overcome before geopolymers can fully replace OPC in construction.

Previous studies on high-temperature behaviour of geopolymers

Research on GPC has shown promising results at high temperatures, making it a viable alternative to OPC13,14. Several studies15–19 have explored its behaviour under extreme heat, focusing on factors such as raw material composition, curing conditions, and thermal resistance. For instance, Jiang et al. (2020) examined the performance of geopolymer pastes made from Class F and Class-C fly ash (FA) at temperatures ranging from 100 °C to 1200 °C16. Their findings revealed that Class-F FA-based geopolymers performed better at temperatures below 500 °C, while Class-C FA-based geopolymers retained more strength at higher temperatures. This suggests that selecting the right type of FA is crucial for improving the thermal durability of geopolymer materials.

Previously, various researchers have worked on geopolymer-based material at elevated temperature conditions. To mention some, Ren et al. (2016) explored the behaviour of geopolymer concrete made from blast furnace slag (BFS) and FA between 200 °C and 800 °C under different cooling regimes20. They found 600 °C to be a critical point where significant weight loss and strength degradation occurred. It should be noted that the precursor material chosen for geopolymers substantially affect its thermal stability. Geopolymers dominated with BFS as a major precursor essentially develops C-S-H-type gels as the primary binding gel similar to that of OPC-based materials, thus shows similar thermal performances21.

Curing conditions also influence GPC’s thermal performance. Amer Hassan et al. (2020) found that ambient-cured GPC gained strength at temperatures up to 200 °C, outperforming heat-cured GPC in strength retention due to a more extended geopolymerization process15. This demonstrates the benefits of ambient curing in improving GPC’s thermal resistance. Additionally, a review by Rihan et al. (2024) discussed the mechanical properties of GPC at high temperatures, noting that residual compressive strength generally increases up to 150–350 °C but decreases beyond 400 °C due to microcrack formation22. Notably, most of the GPC samples considered for review experienced a reduction in strength above 300 °C due to thermal stress. The thermal stress in the concrete is caused by the temperature gradient; hence, the thermal properties of concrete have significant impact on its endurance strength after the heat exposure.

Over the course of a couple of decades, research in the domain of geopolymer production has primarily focused on optimising mixes and exploring various ingredient combinations. Several raw materials have been tested, including fly ash23, silica fume24, rice husk ash25, slag26,27, ferrochrome waste28, laterite soil29, and red mud30. All these aluminosilicate-dominated materials are activated using alkaline solutions such as sodium silicate or sodium hydroxide to form a stable geopolymer structure31. Furthermore, some studies are also focusing on mechanochemical activations of aluminosilicates to reduce the dosages of alkaline activators for more sustainable geopolymer recipes32,33. Despite these advancements, significant challenges remain in designing geopolymer formulations that meet the performance requirements of industrial applications. Ongoing and future research continues to focus on fine-tuning material compositions and processing conditions to fully realize the potential of geopolymer-based materials for different industrial applications.

Role of nanomaterials and graphene oxide in geopolymers

With the recent advancements in nanotechnology, the usage of nanomaterials to improve the performance of geopolymer composites has been tried34. Recent studies have explored the incorporation of nanomaterials, such as nano-silica35, carbon nanotubes (CNTs)36, and graphene-based additives37, to enhance the thermal, mechanical, and microstructural performance of geopolymer. Among these, graphene oxide (GO) has attracted considerable attention due to its exceptional mechanical properties, including a high aspect ratio, a large specific surface area (700–1500 m2/g), and a high elastic modulus (23–42 GPa)37. Studies have demonstrated that incorporating GO into geopolymer composites—typically at dosages of less than 1%—improves flexural strength, reduces porosity, and enhances microstructural integrity38. GO’s effectiveness stems from its ability to form strong covalent bonds between carbon atoms, which reinforce the matrix and improve resistance to deformation. Additionally, GO accelerates the formation of aluminosilicate networks, which strengthens the geopolymerization and hydration processes, further improving the mechanical properties of the composite39. This has positioned GO as a key nanomaterial in the ongoing development of high-performance geopolymer composites. In addition to GO, other nanomaterials have been explored for their ability to improve the structural properties of geopolymers. These materials act as both fillers and bridges within the geopolymer matrix, reducing porosity and enhancing durability40. By accelerating the formation of aluminosilicate networks, nanomaterials improve the overall hydration process and contribute to stronger, more resilient composites.

Research gap and objective

The existing literature highlights that the effects of precursor type and curing conditions on geopolymer performance are well-documented. However, there has been a lack of research on the combined thermo-mechanical performance of fly ash-steel slag (FA-SS) geopolymers reinforced with graphene oxide (GO). Most of the earlier studies have focused on BFS slag blends and the use of SS in geopolymer studies remain limited.

Furthermore, the performance of GO-modified composites under high-temperature conditions remains unclear, particularly because GO begins to decompose above 600 °C41. Furthermore, the studies lack integration of experimental testing with numerical thermal-stress simulations, resulting in an insufficient understanding of the mechanisms of deformation and stress distribution. Hence, to address these gaps, this focuses on the mechanical, thermal, and microstructural performance of GO-reinforced FA-SS geopolymers, and it validates the thermal response through numerical analysis. The research aims to develop optimized geopolymer formulations with improved thermo-mechanical performance and enhanced sustainability for high-temperature construction applications. This is intended to be achieved through both experimental and simulation-based studies. The thermal performance of the newly developed GO-based geopolymer is analysed computationally using ANSYS software. The heat transfer analysis in the solid and fluid domain is performed using ANSYS Fluent and the results were coupled to the static structural analysis for the calculation of thermal stress and deformation. The verification of thermal endurance of newly developed geopolymer’s strength at high temperatures is compared with other existing geopolymers and cement composite (CC) blocks.

Materials and methods

The synthesis of GO-based geopolymer composites involves several key ingredients, including steel slag (SS) and fly ash (FA), which serve as solid precursors. The FA was sourced from the Dr. Narla Tata Rao Thermal Power Plant, Vijayawada, Andhra Pradesh, India, and was classified as Class F according to ASTM standards42. The SS, generated as a by-product of the electric arc furnace steelmaking process, was obtained from the Vizag Steel Plant, Visakhapatnam, Andhra Pradesh, India. Additionally, crusher dust (CD) is used as a filler material. The activation process relies on sodium silicate (NaS) and sodium hydroxide (NaH) as alkali activators, while graphene oxide (GO) is incorporated as a nano-additive to enhance performance. Figure 1 presents Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) images of SS and FA, highlighting their distinct morphologies. SS particles appear irregular, angular, and rough in texture, whereas FA particles exhibit spherical shapes as reported in several earlier studies43. The FESEM images were captured at different magnifications—1000x for SS and 20,000x for FA—clearly showing the coarser nature of SS, which may impact its reactivity. To achieve the desired fineness, both SS and FA were oven-dried at 110 °C to eliminate moisture before being sieved through a 90 μm mesh.

The physical properties and chemical composition of FA and SS are discussed in this section. The XRF results of FA suggests the presence of high amount of silica (SiO2: 60.19%) and alumina (Al2O3: 28.09%), along with small quantities of Fe2O3 (4.66%), CaO (1.25%), MgO (0.53%), K2O (1.47%), TiO2 (1.99%), and P2O5 (0.48%). The SS chemical composition shows a high amount of CaO (38.08%) and SiO2 (37.04%), with these major oxides, MgO (7.69%), and Al2O3 (12.10%) are also present. Further, SS consists of a small amount of Fe2O3 (0.74%), K2O (0.64%), TiO2 (0.57%), and Na2O (0.20%). Based on the chemical composition, it can be interpreted that the FA provides the necessary aluminosilicate for the synthesis of N-A-S-H gel, while the calcium-rich SS provides early development of C-A-S-H or C-(N)-A-S-H-type gels3,44. The physical properties of the procured materials (i.e. FA and SS) have specific gravity of 1.98 and 3.21, respectively. The combination of finer aluminosilicate-rich FA with coarser calcium-rich SS enabled a harmonious dissolution process, effective gel development, and enhanced matrix densification.

Crusher dust (CD), sourced from local quarries outside the city, was limited to a particle size of 2.38 mm. The alkali activators, NaH and NaS, were obtained from authorized local suppliers. The graphene oxide (GO) used in this study was procured as a commercial powder from a certified supplier (Shilpa Enterprises). According to the supplier’s technical data sheet, the GO was synthesized using the modified Hummers method. The material consists mainly of carbon and oxygen with a C: O ratio between 2.1 and 2.9, containing approximately 77% carbon, 22% oxygen, and about 1% trace elements, with a carbon purity close to 99%. The GO has a bulk density of approximately 0.48 g/cm³ and exhibits a high aspect ratio with lateral dimensions of about 5–10 μm. It typically consists of 1–3 layers with a thickness of around 2–4 nm. The development of GO-incorporated geopolymer began with preparing an alkaline solution by mixing NaS and NaH in a 2.8:1 ratio. This solution was prepared a day before casting to ensure proper activation. The casting process started with batching the materials according to the specified mix design. The solid-to-liquid ratio (including steel slag, fly ash, and crusher dust to the combined NaS-NaH solution, and water) was maintained at 1.8. The solid components (SS, FA, and CD) were mixed in a 0.35:0.35:0.30 ratio. Graphene oxide (GO) was introduced into the mix according to the design mix. The dosage of GO was decided based on the previous studies that suggested below 1% by weight enhances strength, reduces porosity, and improves microstructural densification, while higher dosages may lead to agglomeration and loss of workability38. Therefore, the research adopts GO in the range of 0.25–1.25% as it is commonly reported to be the optimum in GO-modified geopolymer and cementitious systems. Table 1 presents the material mix design.

Initially, the solid ingredients were dry mixed for 2–5 min. Then, the alkaline solution was introduced, and the mixture was blended for another 5–10 min in a rotary mixer until it became homogeneous. This procedure was followed consistently for all batches. Once fully mixed, the wet geopolymer was poured into moulds of different sizes, depending on testing requirements. The samples were then cured in an oven at 60 °C before undergoing various performance evaluations.

To evaluate the strength of the GO-based geopolymer, compressive strength tests were conducted at 7- and 28-days following ASTM C109 guidelines45. For testing, the geopolymer mixes were poured into moulds of 50 mm × 50 mm × 50 mm. They were then vibrated for two to three minutes to eliminate air pockets before undergoing thermal curing at 60 °C for 24 h. After this initial curing phase, the specimens were stored under ambient conditions until they reached the designated testing age. A digital compression testing machine (2000 kN, CTM) was used, and the results were averaged from three samples per test. The loading rate was maintained at 0.75 kN/s using a 2000 kN compression testing apparatus.

Beyond mechanical strength, the material underwent physical property tests to assess apparent porosity, bulk density, and water absorption. These tests were performed on 100 mm × 100 mm × 100 mm samples following ASTM C20-00 standards46.

The thermal conductivity of the geopolymer was measured using a hot guarded plate apparatus. In this test, the top plate was heated to a specified temperature, generating heat transfer through the sample. The sample was positioned between two plates to ensure unidirectional heat flow, with thermocouples monitoring heat distribution at multiple points. A data logger recorded the thermal readings throughout the experiment.

To assess the workability of FA-SS geopolymers, the flow value method as specified in ASTM C1437-20 was used47. The setting time, which is an essential factor influencing workability, strength development, and curing behaviour was measured using Vicat’s apparatus as per48.

After heating, the furnace was switched off, and the specimens were allowed to cool naturally at a rate of approximately 5–6 °C/min. Understanding these properties is crucial in evaluating the material’s behaviour under high temperatures and determining its suitability for real-world applications.

The thermal conductivity of the geopolymer specimens was measured using a steady-state guarded hot plate setup following the procedure specified in ASTM C17749. This standard provides consistent test conditions for determining the thermal conductivity of solid materials under steady-state heat flow. Meanwhile, the coefficient of thermal expansion, which measures how much the material expands or contracts with temperature changes was determined using dilatometry in accordance with ASTM E228-0650. This property is critical for preventing thermal cracking and ensuring the long-term stability of structures exposed to fluctuating temperatures.

The modulus of elasticity, a key indicator of stiffness and deformation resistance, was evaluated using ASTM C46951. This test helps determine how well the material retains its structural integrity under thermal and mechanical stress. Additionally, Poisson’s ratio, which describes how a material deforms in different directions under stress, was measured following ASTM D303952. This parameter is particularly important in understanding how the material responds to loading conditions and potential thermal stresses.

Computational analysis

The present study focuses on the thermal analysis of the GO-based geopolymer with a comparison of other blocks for thermal propagation, deformation, and thermal stress using ANSYS mechanical static structural analysis. The heat transfer analysis is performed by using ANSYS Fluent under incompressible laminar flow conditions for the temperature propagation in the solid and fluid domains. The temperature data is exported to ANSYS static structural analysis for the calculation of thermal deformation and induced thermal stress. The heat propagation through the solid block is simulated using the conduction equation. Furthermore, the Navier-Stokes equation, along with the energy equation, is solved in the fluid domain (Air). The numerical analysis is conducted under the assumption that material properties do not vary with temperature. However, future work will focus on incorporating temperature-dependent properties to enable thermal analysis and transient thermo-mechanical coupling. Results and discussion

Compressive strength

The 7-day and 28-day compressive strengths of the developed geopolymer incorporating various dosages of GO are presented in the Fig. 6. At 7 days, the compressive strengths recorded were 33.9 MPa, 37.5 MPa, 42.1 MPa, 44.9 MPa, and 45.9 MPa for GO dosages of 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1% by weight, respectively. This data shows that the early strength of the FA-SS-based geopolymer increases steadily with the addition of GO. Similarly, the 28-day compressive strength of the geopolymer samples increased with increase in GO content, with values of 54.71 MPa, 58.9 MPa, 64.9 MPa, 68.2 MPa, and 72.5 MPa recorded for GO dosages of 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1% by weight, respectively. The strength gain in geopolymer materials is primarily attributed to the geopolymerization/hydration reactions. In this process, aluminosilicate-rich materials like FA and SS in our case, react with alkaline activators. These activators dissolve the aluminosilicate compounds, releasing silicate (SiO4) and aluminate (AlO4) tetrahedra species into the pore solution. Further, these species polymerize to form a dense, three-dimensional aluminosilicate gel network, which binds the material, leading to early strength development and further strength gain over time. Additionally, the incorporation of SS in the mix resulted in better compressive strength compared to glass powder, as observed in an earlier study by the authors8. This is due to the higher calcium content (Ca⁺) in SS, which promotes the formation of calcium-containing N-A-S-H gels, specifically calcium (sodium) aluminate silicate hydrate (C-(N)-A-S-H)/N-(C)-A-S-H-type gels. These gels are reported to be highly crosslinked and performs better than the only N-A-S-H or C-A-S-H type gels within the mix, enhancing the overall strength of the geopolymer44,60.

The increase in strength observed in GO incorporated GPC specimens is largely due to the chemical and physical interactions between the geopolymer matrix and GO. The wrinkled surface structure of GO nanosheets creates mechanical interlocking with the geopolymer binder, which contributes to the improvement in strength61. Additionally, GO acts as a filler and nucleation site within the GPC, creating a denser structure and enhancing compressive strength, particularly in the early stages of curing40. The wrinkled morphology and increased surface area of GO nanosheets enhance the interconnectedness within the geopolymer mixture, accelerating the hydration and solidification processes. This results in the development of a robust three-dimensional network structure during the initial reaction phase, as noted by Liu et al. (2020)62. Furthermore, the inclusion of GO within the geopolymer matrix acts as a flexible nano fibre, effectively reducing the propagation of micro-cracks under external stress as observed by FESEM results (Please see the FESEM section) and earlier reported literature63.

Flow value

Understanding the workability of geopolymer composite is essential for ensuring ease of handling during construction operations. Poor workability can lead to significant challenges during the construction process, such as difficulty in placing and compacting the material. One common method to measure the workability of geopolymer composite is by evaluating its flow value. Experimental results showed that the flow values for geopolymer composite were 121%, 110%, 95%, 89%, and 81% for GO dosages of 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1% by weight, respectively (Fig. 7). These results indicate that the flow value decreases progressively as the GO content increases. This reduction in workability can be attributed to the large surface area of the GO nanosheets, which absorb a significant amount of water from the mixture. As a result, the paste becomes more viscous and stickier, limiting the flowability of the geopolymer mixture. Additionally, the introduction of nanoparticles like GO increases the mixture’s resistance to friction, further decreasing its ability to flow smoothly. Similar behaviour was observed by Li et al. in their study on GO-modified metakaolin-based geopolymer paste, where the incorporation of GO also led to reduced workability due to increased internal friction and water absorption64. This highlights a common trend in geopolymer systems modified with nanoparticles: while they may enhance mechanical properties, they can also adversely affect the material’s fresh state workability, requiring adjustments in mix design, water content or addition of chemical superplasticizers to compensate for these negative effects.

Setting time

The setting of geopolymer composite is a critical factor that influences both its workability and long-term performance. It dictates the time available for the material to be applied and shaped, ensuring that it can be effectively worked with before hardening begins.

Experimental results show that the setting times (final) for geopolymer samples were 489 min, 441 min, 423 min, 398 min, and 361 min for GO dosages of 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1% by weight, respectively. This data indicates that as the dosage of GO increases, the setting time decreases progressively. This reduction in setting time can be attributed to the interaction of GO with the geopolymer matrix. Two possible hypotheses were proposed by the authors based on the earlier literature and observations. Firstly, GO has a high surface area, which absorbs water from the mixture, making the Composite more viscous and denser. The decrease in water content within the mixture accelerates the setting process, shortening both the initial and final setting times. The amount of water in the geopolymer mixture plays a key role in determining the setting time. As observed in previous studies, the incorporation of nanoparticles, such as GO, tends to reduce the setting time by absorbing free water and thus accelerating the geopolymerization, which would otherwise prolong the hydration or geopolymerization process64. Second, GO is known to work as a nucleation site for growth of hydrates which might fasten the development of hydrates, causing the mix set earlier as compared to its counterpart without any GO.

These observations align with the current findings, demonstrating that while the addition of GO can improve certain mechanical properties, it requires careful control of the mixture’s water content and the use of regarding admixtures to avoid excessively rapid setting, which could hinder workability and handling during construction. However, the setting time of all reported mixtures was around or close to 6 h, which is generally considered acceptable for typical construction practices. Furthermore, for applications such as repair works or rapid construction where earlier setting is desirable, GO may serve as an effective additive to accelerate the setting process.

Residual compressive strength

The evaluation of residual compressive strength in geopolymer composites exposed to elevated temperatures was conducted at 200 °C, 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C (Fig. 8). The results demonstrate distinct trends in strength behaviour at different temperature levels, as influenced by GO incorporation. At 200 °C, the residual strength ratios relative to the original 28-day strength were 108.2% (0% GO), 105.6% (0.25% GO), 107.2% (0.5% GO), 107.9% (0.75% GO), and 109.2% (1% GO), confirming that mild heating further densified the matrix. This strength gain is attributed to the continued polymerization of the geopolymer matrix, where the formation of aluminosilicate structures such as (Al-O-Si-O-)Na⁺, (Al-O-Si-O-Si-O-)Na⁺, and (Si-O-Al-O-Si-O-Si-O-)Na⁺ is enhanced through sintering. This process strengthens the bonds between aluminosilicate units, creating a denser and more resilient structure. The compressive strength before exposure developed at ambient temperatures provides a well-bonded matrix, and the additional heat-induced sintering at 200 °C further reinforces these structures. The strength gain was consistent across all samples, with GO-modified geopolymer exhibiting superior residual compressive strength due to the improved pore structure and increased thermal resistance imparted by GO.

At 400 °C, the residual strength ratio dropped to 72.7%, 71.4%, 72.7%, 73.2%, and 74.0% for 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1% GO, respectively. The decrease in strength is primarily due to water evaporation from the geopolymer matrix at this temperature21. As water evaporates, voids are created within the structure, increasing porosity and weakening the overall matrix. The formation of these pores leads to the development of microcracks, which compromise the internal bonding and mechanical integrity of the geopolymer. Thermal stress and shrinkage contribute to further deterioration of strength, particularly in samples with higher GO content. However, GO-modified geopolymers still showed better compressive strength compared to those without GO. This can be attributed to the reinforcing effect of GO nanosheets, which help reduce crack propagation during thermal exposure.

Further deterioration in compressive strength was observed at 600 °C, with values of 31.22 MPa, 33.25 MPa, 39.68 MPa, 40.90 MPa, and 43.87 MPa for GO levels of 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1% by weight, respectively. The residual strength ratio further declined to 57.1%, 56.4%, 61.1%, 60.0%, and 60.5% for 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1% GO mixes. At this temperature, the expansion of silicates during secondary reactions causes the formation of microcracks, which weaken the geopolymer matrix. The inclusion of GO helps delay some of this deterioration by bridging microcracks and enhancing early-age densification, which contributes to improved mechanical and thermal stability within this temperature range.

However, upon exposure to a 600 °C temperature, GO begins to decompose, affecting its effectiveness as an active nano-reinforcement. As it is reported earlier that GO has an early carbon combustion temperature range of 550–616 °C than that of its high-pure counterpart graphene (G) (650–713 °C)41. This decomposition leads to surface cracking and a reduction in strength. Therefore, the performance of GO-modified samples at 800 °C should not be attributed to intact GO particles, but rather to the residual benefits they provided earlier in the reaction process. These residual effects include the denser matrix formed during curing, the micro-filling action of GO, and a barrier-type behaviour created by the compacted structure. As a result, even though GO is no longer structurally active at 800 °C, the samples still retain slightly higher strength than the control mix due to the improved matrix developed before GO decomposition.

At 800 °C, the compressive strength dropped significantly for all samples, with values of 23.32 MPa, 25.36 MPa, 28.54 MPa, 32.11 MPa, and 33.85 MPa for GO levels of 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1% by weight, respectively. The residual strength ratio decreased sharply to 42.6%, 43.1%, 43.9%, 47.0%, and 46.7% for 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1% GO, respectively. The strength degradation at this temperature exceeded 50% relative to the ambient condition. This decline is mainly due to shrinkage caused by thermal incompatibility, chemical changes, and increased pore pressure. At such high temperatures, the differences in thermal expansion between phases become more pronounced, resulting in microstructural damage and a significant loss of strength. Although GO initially enhances resistance to thermal stress, its influence diminishes at these extreme temperatures due to decomposition, leading to increased cracking and weakening of the geopolymer matrix64.

In summary, the residual compressive strength of geopolymer Composite is significantly influenced by temperature exposure, with an initial increase at 200 °C due to enhanced geopolymerization and sintering, followed by a progressive decrease at higher temperatures as water loss, pore formation, and thermal stress take effect. GO-modified geopolymers exhibit enhanced thermal resistance and strength retention compared to unmodified samples; however, the degradation of GO at higher temperatures limits its reinforcing capabilities beyond 600 °C64.

Based on the experimental results, the optimal GO dosage in this study is 1%, as it produced the highest compressive strength, the greatest strength retention at elevated temperatures, and the most compact microstructure. Lower dosages (0.25–0.75%) improved performance but did not match the enhancement observed at 1%. No negative effects, such as severe agglomeration or loss of mechanical performance, were observed at a dosage of 1%, indicating that this level offers the most effective reinforcement among those tested, Fig. 8.

Table 3 shows the effects of GO on the thermo-mechanical performance developed specimens. The bulk density increases from 2216 kg/m³ for the control mix to 2298 kg/m³ with GO inclusion, showcasing a denser and more compact microstructure resulting in the improved particle packing and bonding. The thermal conductivity also shows a marginal increase, from 1.77 W/mK in the FA-SS mix to 1.85 W/mK in the GO-modified composite, while remaining significantly lower than that of conventional cement composite (CC), which records 2.58 W/mK. The results shows that the GO-enhanced geopolymer maintains good insulation capability despite the moderate conductivity increase.

Although GO-based materials possess high intrinsic thermal conductivity, their behaviour inside the geopolymer matrix differs considerably. The small GO dosage used (0.25–1.25%) does not create a continuous conductive network; instead, the sheets remain well dispersed, leading to phonon scattering and reduced heat transfer. GO also refines pore structure by filling micro-voids, thereby limiting heat movement through interconnected air-filled pores. Furthermore, interfacial thermal resistance at the GO-gel interface further restricts heat flow. These combined effects explain why the overall bulk thermal conductivity of the composite remains relatively low despite the conductive nature of GO.

In contrast to nano-scale additives such as GO, several studies have reported that macro-scale additives, particularly natural fibres, can reduce the thermal conductivity of cementitious and alkali-activated composites65,66. This reduction is generally attributed to the increased porosity and air entrainment introduced by fibre reinforcement, which interrupts heat conduction pathways and improves thermal insulation. Therefore, while GO slightly increases thermal conductivity due to matrix densification and improved particle packing, macro-scale fibre additives typically enhance insulation performance by increasing internal voids and limiting heat transfer. This comparison highlights how the scale and type of reinforcement influence thermal transport behaviour in composite materials.

The heat capacity of the FA-SS-GO mix is significantly higher than that of both the FA-SS mix and conventional composite, increasing from 5.49 MJ/m³K to 6.92 MJ/m³K, while conventional composite has only 3.65 MJ/m³K. This enhanced heat capacity implies that the GO-modified mix can store more thermal energy, which is beneficial for reducing thermal stresses under fluctuating temperatures. The modulus of elasticity also benefits from the addition of GO, rising from 33.5 GPa in the FA-SS mix to 38.38 GPa, compared to 25.97 GPa for conventional composite. This higher stiffness in the GO-modified mix indicates improved resistance to deformation, which contributes to the material’s structural integrity under load.

Poisson’s ratio decreases slightly with the addition of GO, from 0.182 to 0.175, indicating a reduced tendency for lateral expansion under compressive load, which enhances dimensional stability. Conventional composite, in comparison, has a higher Poisson’s ratio of 0.215. Additionally, the coefficient of thermal expansion increases modestly with GO, from 6.25 × 10⁻6/°C to 6.89 × 10⁻6/°C, remaining lower than the 9.87 × 10⁻6/°C of conventional composite. This lower coefficient suggests reduced expansion and contraction under temperature fluctuations, lowering the risk of thermal cracking and contributing to the durability of the GO-modified mix.

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM)

To gain deeper insight into the microstructural characteristics of nano-based geopolymer Composite (see Fig. 15), FESEM was conducted on samples containing FA and SS, as well as those incorporating GO. The analysis focused on morphological variations, the extent of geopolymerization, and phase distribution within the matrix.

At room temperature, FESEM micrographs revealed that the geopolymer gel contained a significant number of GO particles with irregular morphologies, smooth surfaces, and sharp edges. Additionally, numerous unreacted spherical FA particles and irregularly shaped SS particles were observed embedded within the gel phase, indicating the gradual dissolution of the precursor materials. The gel phase was also noted to grow around these particles, further confirming ongoing reaction and microstructural development. Moreover, comparison of the SEM images of mixtures with and without GO shows that the GO-containing sample exhibits fewer unreacted FA particles, suggesting that GO addition enhances the reaction and promotes higher gel formation.

The SEM image confirms that the dissolution of aluminosilicate species from FA and SS led to the formation of amorphous aluminosilicate C-(N)-A-S-H-type gels, which acted as the primary binding phase in the matrix3,44. The addition of GO nanoparticles enhanced this structure by improving mix homogeneity and producing a denser microstructure, which results in the increased compressive strength observed in GO-modified samples. The GO particles also provided effective nucleation sites during the early stages of geopolymerization, as their oxygen-containing functional groups (–OH, –COOH) interacted with dissolved silicate and aluminate species, accelerating gel formation and contributing to a more compact matrix67. At later stages, the well-dispersed GO acted as nanoscale reinforcements that bridged microcracks and improved the integrity of the gel network, enhancing resistance to both mechanical and thermal stresses. These mechanisms are consistent with the FESEM observations, which show reduced porosity and fewer unreacted particles in GO-modified samples.

Upon exposure to elevated temperatures up to 800 °C, significant morphological changes were observed. FESEM images revealed interface cracks due to weak mechanical interlocking between FA, SS, and the geopolymer gel, leading to a decline in mechanical properties. This effect was more pronounced in mixes without GO. However, the melting behaviour of SS contributed to microstructural densification, enabling GO-reinforced specimens to retain more of their mechanical strength. This suggests that GO enhances thermal stability and crack resistance due to its nano-fibre reinforcement effect.

The GO-modified geopolymer shows similar crack-bridging behaviour as that of carbon-based nanomaterials, particularly multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs)58. The recent work by Filazi et al., (2025)36 suggests enhanced crack resistance and energy dissipation by nano-scale fibers which can be compared with the nano material (graphene oxide) used in this study. Further, research by Li et al., (2021) have also demonstrated the improved structural damage control due to their crack-arresting capability by MWCNTs68. Comparing the previous studies to the GO incorporation, the nano material shows matrix densification and crack control through micro-filling and nucleation effects during geopolymerization.

Furthermore, few researches have adopted alternative reinforcement strategies to enhance the performance of geopolymer. The work by Filazi et al., (2024) discusses on the use of hybrid systems incorporating Bacillus subtilis and PET fibres31. Both these materials improve mechanical performance through microbial-induced calcium carbonate precipitation, which densifies the matrix and fills microcracks, while PET fibres improve crack resistance through fibre-bridging mechanisms. The comparison of GO with bio-fibre hybrid systems is crucial to understand the advantage of nanoscale filling, nucleation effects, and micro-crack bridging, resulting in improved matrix compactness and thermo-mechanical stability.

Despite these improvements, FESEM analysis confirmed that specimens exposed to 800 °C developed a highly porous structure, resulting in a significant strength reduction. The increased porosity was likely due to the decomposition of hydration products and the separation of FA and SS particles from the geopolymer matrix due to thermal expansion. While GO addition improved homogeneity, geopolymerization, and high-temperature stability, extreme heat exposure still led to material degradation. Further refinements in mix design, curing conditions, and reinforcement strategies may help mitigate porosity and enhance long-term durability in high-temperature applications.

Limitations

This study demonstrates the positive influence of GO on the thermo-mechanical performance of geopolymer specimens, particularly in enhancing thermal resilience. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the experimental investigation was restricted to a relatively narrow GO dosage range (0.25–1.25%), selected based on prior literature. The effects of higher dosages remain unexplored and warrant further examination. Second, all tests were performed under static loading conditions with temperature exposure limited to 800 °C. Future studies should consider cyclic thermal loading and exposure to higher temperatures to better simulate real service conditions.

The fresh properties of geopolymers were also found to deteriorate with increasing GO content, primarily due to increased water demand and the strong tendency of GO to agglomerate. To mitigate these effects, future formulations could explore optimized ultrasonic or mechanical dispersion protocols to ensure uniform GO distribution, the use of superplasticizers to improve flowability, or adjustments to the alkaline activator composition to balance reactivity and workability. Additionally, the high cost of GO represents a key limitation, potentially hindering large-scale industrial adoption. A thorough assessment of the economic feasibility of GO-modified geopolymers is therefore essential, particularly in comparison with other nano-additives or low-cost natural fibres.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the significant advancements achieved by incorporating graphene oxide (GO) into fly ash-steel slag-based geopolymer composite, focusing on its mechanical, thermal, and structural performance. The research highlights the material’s enhanced compressive strength, superior thermal resistance, and improved insulation properties, making it a promising alternative for high-performance applications under extreme conditions. While GO addition brings notable improvements, it also introduces challenges in workability and setting time, emphasizing the need for balanced mix designs. Overall, this study contributes to the development of sustainable and durable construction materials, paving the way for eco-friendly solutions in the construction industry.
