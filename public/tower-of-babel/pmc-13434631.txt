Effect of biomass management strategies on carbon and nitrogen supply in legume and legume–grass mixtures under organic arable farming
Authors: Aušra Arlauskienė, Viktorija Gecaitė, Monika Toleikienė, Lina Šarūnaitė
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Legumes can provide nitrogen through biological fixation, but retaining it in the soil for subsequent crop production must also be ensured. In this study, we assessed the effects of biomass management using forage legumes (Trifolium pratense L., Medicago sativa L.), as well as their mixtures with festulolium (× Festulolium), under organic arable farming conditions. Three biomass management treatments were applied: complete removal, partial removal with mulching, and full mulching The focus was on the mulch and root biomass and quality, decomposition dynamics, and changes in soil mineral nitrogen (SMN) and soil organic carbon (SOC) content. Results showed that the biomass C:N ratio was lowest in legumes, highest in festulolium, and intermediate in mixtures. Root biomass was greatest in legume–festulolium mixtures. Full mulching reduced the mulch C:N ratio, whereas the root C:N remained relatively stable and higher. Legume mulch (C:N ≤ 16) released 33–54% of N during the growing season, indicating that autumn SMN largely depended on root and litter decomposition. Mixtures maintained higher SMN than festulolium monocultures but less than pure legumes. The persistence of SMN until spring was influenced by both mulching practices and meteorological conditions. The greatest losses occurred when biomass was removed from the field or under full mulching. Partial removal with mulching reduced SMN losses and supported a more stable SOC.

Introduction

Soil organic carbon status is an important indicator of soil quality and productivity. Organic carbon improves soil structure, stabilizes soil aggregates, increases soil water retention and aeration, and improves nutrient availability1. It also improves long-term yield2, which is particularly important in organic farming systems, where nutrient cycling relies on natural sources and biological processes3,4. Plants are the main source of soil organic carbon, which originates from above- and below ground parts of plants (dead roots, root exudates, the effects of root respiration, etc.)5,6. Root systems are essential, as they not only accumulate carbon but also determine its stabilization or loss7.

Studies have indicated that intensively tilled soils and high rates of N fertilizer application reduce potential productivity by reducing soil organic carbon, which is a barrier to sustainable agriculture8,9. New organic farming practices are, therefore, needed for sustainable food production. One such option is forage legumes, which represent a useful agronomic solution for two main reasons: they increase organic soil carbon and provide nitrogen to plants10,11. Perennial legumes accumulate more carbon in their roots than cereals12; moreover, they fix atmospheric nitrogen and improve the chemical, physical, and biological properties of the soil13. Recently, legume–grass mixtures have garnered increasing attention as they exhibit additional functional properties. Such mixtures can improve forage quality, enhance productivity, and stimulate soil microbial activity and nutrient availability14,15. They also ensure greater carbon sequestration in roots and rhizomes compared with monocultures15,16. In Lithuania, combinations of at least two species—legumes and grasses—have enriched soil with carbon compounds more effectively over a short period of time16. However, the interaction between grasses and legumes is highly complex, and their effects depend on species identity17 and the proportion of plants in the mixture14.

In arable farming systems, the aboveground biomass of legumes and legume–grass mixtures can be used as marketable products, such as in forage, protein, or biogas production. However, in small farms, such products are not widely available, and therefore, this practice is uncommon. Much more frequently, the aboveground biomass is used as green manure to return nutrients to the soil18, enrich it with biologically fixed nitrogen (BFN) and organic matter19.

A common practice is the incorporation of legume biomass before winter cereal sowing, but this poses several challenges: large amounts of biomass are difficult to incorporate, plowing accelerates the mineralization of accumulated organic matter, and the released nitrogen often does not coincide with the nitrogen demand of winter cereals in autumn. Moreover, the large biomass input can lead to increased soil mineral N levels during the non-vegetation period, thereby raising the risk of nitrate leaching20,21. Nitrate losses are among the most pressing agri-environmental problems worldwide22. Studies show that this risk is lower in legume–grass mixtures compared with their monocultures23. To reduce these risks, various green manure management techniques are applied, including incorporation into the soil, surface mulching, strip mulching, or biomass transfer to another field24,25.

Among these techniques, mulching stands out as a practice that regulates cutting time and frequency, improves the synchronization of nitrogen release with crop demands, and avoids sudden surpluses of mineral nitrogen.

Mulching is recognized as a sustainable agricultural practice in arable systems, providing not only short-term benefits such as moisture retention, weed suppression, and biodiversity habitat but also long-term climate benefits through improved soil carbon sequestration19,26–28. Green manures improve SOC accumulation in the soil by increasing plant residues and microbial necrosis, regulating microbial life strategies in wheat and green manure production systems29,30. Mulch incorporated into the soil as green manure can improve plant nutrition conditions and yield24,31. However, the effect of mulch depends on biomass quality, the C:N ratio, plant species, climate, and soil conditions32. Improperly selected mulch can cause nitrogen immobilization and increase the incidence of diseases or weed proliferation28. In addition, the decomposition of legume roots and residues reduces greenhouse gas emissions and improves the overall resilience of the system through stable soil aggregation33. Therefore, mulching with forage legumes and their mixtures with grasses can be regarded as a promising yet still insufficiently studied practice, potentially optimizing soil carbon and nitrogen balance in organic farming systems, although our understanding remains incomplete.

In response to climate change, it is essential to develop crop management solutions within organic farming systems that optimize the soil carbon and nitrogen balance. Numerous studies have been conducted on green manures. However, with changing technologies, our understanding of the mechanisms of N and C dynamics interacting with the soil remains incomplete. This study is distinctive in that it includes an examination of the effects of different biomass management strategies (time, mass) on forage crops’ biomass traits, their mineralization, and the impact on soil organic carbon and mineral nitrogen dynamic. We hypothesize that (i) mulching of aboveground biomass during the growing season affects its decomposition and influences root biomass, chemical composition, and nitrogen release in organically managed arable soils; (ii) autumn mulching of legume–grass mixtures reduces soil mineral nitrogen losses and improves the synchronization of nitrogen release with the nutrient demands of subsequent crops; and (iii) mulching of legume–grass mixtures increases soil organic carbon by enhancing residue retention and carbon inputs, compared to biomass removal from the field. The objectives of this study were to assess: (i) mulch and root biomass production and quality of forage legumes and their mixtures with festulolium; (ii) mulch decomposition dynamics; and (iii) the resulting changes in soil mineral nitrogen and organic carbon under organic arable farming conditions.

Materials and methods

Site and soil properties

Research was performed in the northern part of Central Lithuania’s lowland at the Lithuanian Research Centre for Agriculture and Forestry’s Joniškėlis Experimental Station (56° 21′ N, 24° 10′E) in 2007–2010. The parental rock of the experimental site is limnoglacial clay on morenic loam, according to the current classification—Endocalcari Endohypogleyic Cambisol, Cmg-n-w-can (WRB 2015). The soil texture is clay loam (at a depth of 0–25 cm) on silty clay (at a depth of 26–76 cm), with deeper lying sandy loam (at a depth of 77–135 cm). The topsoil layer (0–25 cm) contains 27.0, 50.2 and 22.7% clay, silt and sand, respectively. Bulk density of the ploughlayer is 1.5 Mg m− 3, total porosity 41–43%. Agrochemical characteristics of the 0–25 cm ploughlayer: pHKCl – 6.6, mobile P2O5 and K2O – 142 and 223 mg kg–1 of soil DM respectively, Ntotal – 1.26 g kg− 1 and Corg. – 16.7 g kg− 1.

Experimental designs and details

The field experiment was conducted in 2007–2009 and repeated in 2009–2011. The experiments were conducted in the following crop rotation sequence: barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) + undersown legumes and legume–festulolium mixtures (year of introduction) → legumes and legume–festulolium mixtures → winter wheat (Triticum aestivum L.). For the design of the field experiment, Factor A was the type of legumes and legume–festulolium mixtures and Factor B was the legume biomass and legume–festulolium mixtures management strategies (Table 1). The control treatments were Fs without mulching. The experimental plots were laid out in a randomized complete two-factor block design in four replicates (n = 15, N = 60). Individual plot size was 5 × 16 m for 80 m2. In the year the experiments were established (2007 and 2009), spring barley (variety ‘Ūla’) was undersown with festulolium, legumes, and legume–festulolium mixtures in compliance with the experimental design. The intergeneric hybrid festulolium variety ‘Punia’ was sown at a seeding rate of 620 seeds m–2, the red clover variety ‘Vyliai’ was sown at a seeding rate of 750 seeds m–2, the lucerne variety ‘Birutė’ was sown at a seeding rate of 750 seeds m− 2. The seeding rate of the mixtures consisted of 200 seeds m–2 of festulolium and 500 seeds m–2 of forage legumes. The seeds of forage legumes were not inoculated. All plant varieties used in the study were developed by the Departments of Cereal and Grass Breeding at the Institute of Agriculture, LAMMC, and were used in the experiments.

After harvesting spring barley, the straw was chopped and spread, and the legume, festulolium, and mixtures of the two were left to grow. In 2008 and 2010, the year of festulolium, legumes, and legume–festulolium mixture cultivation, herbage was cut twice for forage and removed for feed at the flowering stages (WM). For full mulching (4 M), forage crops were cut four times at the bud formation stage and left on the soil surface (Table 2). When forage crops were partially removed with mulching (2 M), the first cut was removed from the field and used as forage, while the second and third cuts were mulched. Mulching differed in terms of mulch mass, number, frequency, and timing of applications; therefore, we defined it as extensive (2 M) and intensive (4 M) mulching.

Meteorological conditions of Experiment I. In 2007, after sowing legumes and festulolium in May–June, moisture and temperature conditions were sufficient for the germination of forage crops. In July, precipitation exceeded the standard climate norm (SCN) by 37.8 mm. Autumn, and winter were warmer than usual. In 2008, the summer was slightly cooler, characterized by a dry May and ending with excessive moisture in August, when precipitation exceeded the SCN by 48.6 mm. Due to the drier conditions in September, winter wheat sown after grass ploughing emerged and developed slowly. Autumn was warm and prolonged, with excessive precipitation recorded in October. Winter conditions were warmer and milder compared with the SCN. Overall, the study period can be considered close to optimal for plant growth conditions.

Meteorological conditions of Experiment II. In the establishment year of Experiment II (2009), April and May—when forage legumes and festulolium were germinating—were warmer and drier than the SCN. Plant development was promoted by warmer weather in June and July, when precipitation exceeded the SCN by 21.5 and 38.4 mm, respectively. January was colder than usual. In spring 2010, the average daily air temperature was close to the SCN. In May, excessive precipitation accelerated forage crop growth. July and August were unusually hot. Dry conditions persisted until mid-autumn. In September and October, precipitation was 2.0–2.1 times lower than the SCN. November was very warm and humid. During winter, air temperature was variable: the beginning and end of winter were cooler, whereas January was warmer than the SCN. Overall, the period of Experiment II can be described as meteorologically contrasting.

Sampling and analysis of plants

Before each mulching event (2 M— two times and 4 M— four times), the aboveground biomass of festulolium, legumes, and legume–festulolium mixtures was determined (Table 2). Sampling of aboveground biomass occurred at four randomly chosen squares of 0.25 m2 in each plot that were cut to ground level leaving a 5 cm stubble. In the samples, plants were identified based on morphological traits (leaves, stems, flowers), and species were separated manually34. Each fraction (legumes, festulolium, weeds) was weighed and dried separately. The article reports the total mulch mass, defined as the sum of the aboveground biomass of festulolium and legumes from all cuts, depending on the treatment. When biomass was removed from the field, aboveground biomass data are not presented in this article.

Before the incorporation of forage crops into the soil, belowground plant biomass was determined. To determine the root (belowground) biomass of plants, soil blocks 0.25 × 0.25 × 0.24 m in size were dug out in the plots of each treatment, and these were replicated three times. Each soil block containing roots was placed into a 12 L container, filled with water, and left for 24 h. On the following day, large, intact roots of lucerne, clover, and festulolium visible to the naked eye were carefully separated from the soil and rinsed two to three times with clean water. The remaining soil in the 12 L containers was washed using a 1.0 mm mesh sieve, on which fine roots, mulch, and other organic residues were retained35. These data are not presented in this article.

Plant samples were prepared for the determination of dry matter (DM), C, and N concentrations. Dried plant samples (at 60 °C 48 h) were ground using an ultra-centrifugal mill ZM 200 (Retsch, Haan, Germany) equipped with a 1 mm mesh size sieve and analyzed for total organic carbon (C) content by using the wet chemistry method of dichromate oxidation with spectrophotometry at a 590 nm wavelength and using glucose as a standard (UV/Vis Cary 50, Varian Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA). Total nitrogen was analyzed using the Kjeldahl method with a spectrophotometric measuring procedure at a wavelength of 655 nm (UV/Vis Cary 50, Varian Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA). In mixed crops, C and N were determined separately in festulolium and legume biomass samples (for each cut separately). The ratio of C to N was calculated. When calculating the C:N ratio of legumes and festulolium mixture, a weighted average was used.

Litter bag studies

Studies to determine the decomposition intensity of forage crop mulch were carried out using the litterbag method2,36. Samples (25 g of fresh natural moisture mass) of festulolium, red clover, and lucerne were placed in polychlorvinyl mesh bags (20 × 15 cm) with a mesh diameter ≈ 1.0 mm after each mulching treatment and after determination of the dry matter content and C and N concentrations of each. Bags containing festulolium, clover, and lucerne mulch were placed in the corresponding plant plots arranged in a randomized order. At different times, the bags were placed on the soil surface and secured in place to simulate the spreading of the mulch during each cutting. The bags were placed at an equal distance (4 m) from the edge of the experimental strip, in a single row, in 4 replicates. The number of bags depended on the number of mulching events and repetitions. Before plowing, all the bags were collected at the same time, the mass of mulch (remaining after mulching) was weighed, and the dry matter and C and N concentrations were determined. This method quantifies changes in chemical composition during decomposition by comparing initial and final concentrations of elements in plant residues36,37. The reduction in C and N concentrations was calculated according to the formula:

MC =100-((Cb/Ca)*100) and MN =100-((Nb/Na)*100),

where MC and MN are the reduction of C and N during mulching, Ca and Na are the concentration of C and N before mulching, Cb and Nb are the concentration of C and N in the mulch before grass ploughing in. C and N of the mulch were determined by the above-mentioned methods.

Sampling and analysis of soil

Soil samples for agrochemical characterization were collected with a drill from the 0–25 cm soil layer prior to the establishment of the experiments. Mobile phosphorus (P2O5) and mobile potassium (K2O) were determined according to Egner–Riehm–Domingo (A-L) method38. Total nitrogen (Ntot) was determined by the Kjeldahl method using the spectrophotometric procedure at the wavelength of 655 nm (UV/Vis Cary 50, Varian Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA) after reaction with salicylate and hypochlorite ions in alkaline solution in the presence of sodium nitroferricyanide39, soil pH—by the potentiometric method in 1 M KCl (1:5, w:v) in accordance with ISO 10,390.

Soil samples for the determination of soil organic carbon (SOC) content (% of soil DM) and characteristics of humus fractions were collected from the 0–25 cm layer upon completion of the field experiment. Composite soil samples were taken from ten sites within each plot. Soil samples were air-dried, crushed, and sieved through a 1 mm sieve, manually removing visible roots and plant residues. Soil organic carbon content was determined using the modified Tyurin method, based on wet chemical oxidation. Soil samples were oxidized in a solution of potassium dichromate in sulfuric acid with external heating at 160 °C for 30 min in 100 mL flasks. The resulting solutions were measured using a Carry 50 (VARIAN) spectrophotometer at 590 nm using glucose standards40,41. Carbonates in the studied Endocalcari Endohypogleyic Cambisol occur below 40 cm depth; therefore, soil inorganic carbon was not present in the sampled 0–25 cm layer and was not determined.

Humus substances were dissolved in a solution of sodium chloride, and from the extracts, humic acids were precipitated with mineral acid solutions and separated from fulvic acids. Applying this method, three humic fractions and four fractions of fulvic acids were found in the soil humus fraction42. Humification was expressed as the percentage (by mass) of carbon in soil total organic carbon. The humification degree was classified as high (> 30%), average (20–30%), or low (< 20%)43. Another crucial indicator of humus quality is the ratio of humic acids to fulvic acids (HR:FR). An increase in the relative proportion of humic acids in relation to other humic substances is a desirable development, which is valuable from an agronomic and environmental point of view43. Soil mineral nitrogen (NO3-N + NH4-N) was determined in the year of forage crops cultivation after plowing in late autumn and spring after resumption of winter wheat vegetation (Exp. I −12.11.2008 and 17.04.2009; Exp. II—18.10.2010 and 13.04.2011). Composite soil samples for mineral nitrogen (SMN) determination were taken from three sites within each plot and from two soil layers: 0–30 and 30–60 cm. Each soil sample was well crushed and mixed. A composite soil sample of the 0–60 cm layer for each plot was made by mixing two samples in equal parts from the 0–30 and 30–60 cm layers. The concentrations of soil N-NO3 were determined by the potentiometric method in a 1% extract of KAl(SO4)2 × 12H2O (1:2.5, w:v), and soil N-NH4 using spectrophotometric measurement at a wavelength of 655 nm in a 1 M KCl extract (1:2.5, w:v)44. All concentrations of elements and compounds of plant and soil were expressed on a DM basis.

Methods of statistical data analysis

Statistical analysis revealed an interaction between years. Therefore, plant and soil data from each experiment were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess the effects of crop type and biomass management. The two main factors for statistical analysis were Factor A: the type of legumes and legume–festulolium mixtures, and Factor B: biomass management strategies. Significant differences among factors and their interactions were assessed with an F-test at p < 0.05 and p < 0.01 levels. Post hoc comparisons were carried out using Tukey’s test at p < 0.05, where means sharing the same letter are not significantly different. The correlation between N release and the C:N ratio of the mulch was assessed using correlation and regression analysis. Statistical significance was evaluated at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using Statistica software, version 9.4 (StatSoft Inc., Tulsa, OK, USA).

Results

Mulch of forage crop and its quality

Mulch biomass: The average of experiments I and II indicated that a similar amount of field-fresh biomass was applied as mulch (5623 and 5297 kg ha⁻¹ DM, respectively). When mulched twice, the mulch mass averaged 58.8% (Exp. I) and 69.1% (Exp. II) of the mulch mass mulched four times (Fig. 1). When evaluating festulolium, forage legumes, and mixtures of the two, it was found that festulolium had the lowest mulch mass, irrespective of the mulching intensity. The mass of forage legumes and their mixtures with festulolium was significantly higher. When mulching extensively (2 M), the mulch mass did not differ between forage crop species (Exp. I), or lucerne and its mixture with festulolium had a significantly higher mulch mass than red clover and its mixture (Exp. II). However, in the intensive mulching (4 M), the differences were only apparent in Exp. I. The highest mulch biomass was that of red clover and its mixture with festulolium (4 M).

Forage legumes showed a decrease in N concentration when grown in combination with festulolium. Red clover was found to be less sensitive to the decrease in N concentration than lucerne. When mulching was applied four times, the nitrogen concentration in the mulch increased. The organic carbon (C) concentration of plant mulch biomass was significantly increased via forage crop species and mulching intensity interactions (p < 0.01) or by crop species alone (p < 0.01) (Table 4). In both experiments, red clover, regardless of mulching intensity, accumulated significantly more C compared with festulolium.

Although less C was accumulated in the mulch of the plants in Exp. II (3.0% compared with Exp. I), the differences were less consistent, with an upward trend of C accumulation in RC mulch.

Similarly, the C:N ratio, a key factor influencing mulch mineralization, also exhibited variation. In both experiments, the value of C:N varied similarly. In Exp. I, the C:N ratio of RC and RC + Fs mulches did not differ, or was significantly higher than that of pure clover mulches. By contrast, the C:N ratio of L + Fs mulch was significantly higher when cultivated in mixtures with festulolium. Compared with the mulch of festulolium grown in a pure stand, forage legume mulch, irrespective of the method of cultivation, had a substantially lower value for this indicator: red clover—15.6–18.1 and lucerne—14.7–16.5.

Mass and quality of forage crops roots

Number of roots: Despite the same seed rate, the experiments differed regarding plant species density. The pure stand in Experiment 1 had more festulolium and fewer legumes than in Experiment 2. In the mixtures of Experiment I, legumes accounted for 45% of the total number of plants, whereas in Experiment II, they accounted for 63% of the total number of plants. This could be due to the meteorological conditions when festulolium did not germinate and overwinter well due to large temperature fluctuations in February–March.

Root mass: The root mass of the plants depended on the interaction between forage crop species and mulching intensity (p < 0.01). It was found that the root mass of plants in Exp. I was on average 21.2% higher than that in Exp. II (Table 5). When the plant biomass was removed from the field, lucerne (Experiments I and II) and the mixture of red clover with festulolium (Experiment II) significantly produced a higher root mass compared to pure festulolium stands. Both experiments differed in the patterns of root variation. Based on the mean data from Experiment I, the highest root mass was found in pure festulolium and in its mixtures with lucerne, irrespective of the use of aboveground biomass. In experiment II, lucerne and the mixtures L + Fs and RC + Fs- showed a higher root mass. Root mass also depended on mulching. In Exp. I, the root mass increased with mulching (4 M) Fs, RC + Fs, and L + Fs and decreased with mulching RC and L compared with without mulching. According to the data derived from experiment II, the highest root mass was obtained with extensive mulching (except for L + Fs). Mulching the aboveground mass of the RC resulted in a decrease (Exp. I) or insignificant change (Exp. II) in root mass. In lucerne, mulching of the ground mass was also not favorable for root mass (Exp. I—2 M and 4 M, Exp. II − 4 M).

In the case of festulolium mixtures with legumes, the Exp. I data showed that mulching increased root mass. Differences were more pronounced in mixtures with lucerne than with clover. Root data for Exp. II mixtures were less consistent. Mulching the mixture of festulolium with red clover four times did not change the root mass significantly, whereas the mixture with lucerne tended to increase it.

The contribution of roots to the total organic material input (mulch + roots) was influenced by plant species and mulching intensity. The greatest root share was observed in pure festulolium (2 M) and its mixtures with legumes, whereas intensive mulching consistently reduced the relative proportion of roots within the total organic input. This reduction may have implications for soil C and N balance, as lower root contributions can decrease belowground carbon inputs and alter nutrient cycling dynamics.

Mineralisation of plant mulch and roots

Mulch decomposition intensity: Litter bag studies were used to assess C and N variation during the growing seasons of red clover, lucerne, and festulolium mulches spread on the soil surface. Figure 2 shows C and N reduction (RC and RN) in mulch. Extensive mulching (2 M) resulted in a greater decrease in C than N in all mulches (Fig. 3a). The greatest decrease in C and N was observed in the first mulch, which had been left on the soil surface for the longest time (except L). The second mulch of red clover and the second one of festulolium decomposed more slowly. While there was little difference in the reduction of C between these mulches, the red clover mulch showed a threefold greater reduction in N than the festulolium mulch. There was little difference between the first and second lucerne mulches. A comparison of all mulches showed that the first mulch of red clover decomposed the fastest.

The evaluation of intensive mulching (4 M) showed that all mulches differed in the intensity of decomposition (Fig. 3b). The highest C and the lowest N were released from festulolium mulches. The first and second red clover mulches decomposed intensively, with the highest release of C and N. Later, N release was reduced by half in the red clover mulch. In contrast to the lucerne mulch, the reduction of C and N was consistent, i.e., the reduction of C and N was the highest in the third and fourth mulches. Comparing all mulches, it was found that the first and second red clover mulches and the third and fourth lucerne mulches were the fastest to decompose.

Soil mineral nitrogen (SMN) The intensity of decomposition of plant residues and mulch was indicated by the amount of SMN in the 0–60 cm layer in late autumn when winter wheat was grown after roots and plant residues had been incorporated into the soil and in spring, when winter cereals resumed growth. On average, Exp. II plots contained 31.0% more SMN than Exp. I plots (Table 7).

In both experiments, the SMN content was significantly correlated with the interaction between forage crop species and the intensity of aboveground mass mulching (p < 0.01). The cultivation of legumes (RC, L) and the removal of their harvest from the field for fodder significantly increased the amount of SMN in the soil (from 2.0 to 3.5 times). The cultivation of legumes in mixtures with Fs tended to reduce the SMN content compared with pure forage legumes. Especially in Exp. I, where the density of festulolium was higher.

Discussion

Forage crops biomass, quality, and mineralization The mass of mulch depends on the type of plant, the cutting frequency, and the plant’s ability to regrow quickly after cutting. Festulolium in pure stands usually produces a lower biomass due to nitrogen limitations. However, when grown in mixtures with legumes, total yields can increase significantly due to nitrogen transfer and additional resource use45, which occurred during our study (Fig. 1). In addition, different mixtures of forage crops produce a denser canopy, better soil cover, and more efficient use of resources, resulting in a total yield increase of around 13% compared with a single crop46.

It has been shown that less frequent cutting allows plants to accumulate more biomass, while intensive cutting can reduce overall yields47, but the results depend on local conditions and the species composition of the sward. This is because plants may not have enough time to fully regrow and replenish their nutrient reserves, which ultimately reduces overall yield48,49. In our study, a pure lucerne stand produced lower yields and was more susceptible to intensive mulching compared with clover. This can be explained by the fact that lucerne is a longer growing crop (reaching full productivity in 3–4 years) and is not yet sufficiently established in its first year. Mulching was more favorable for it in the second half of the growing season (2 M) (Fig. 1). The ecological needs and agro-ecological contribution of red clover indicate its greater suitability for short-term cultivation26.

The N concentration of the aboveground mass depends on the plant species, stage of development, soil, meteorological conditions, and the agrotechniques of cultivation50. Maximum nitrogen supply and retention in the soil can be achieved by cultivating forage legumes51. The biomass of legume–grass mixtures accumulate less N, but the reduction is not as large (Table 4). This is also confirmed with data from other studies26,52. Our study showed that intensive mulching increased or had no significant effect on C and N concentrations (Table 4). It has been suggested that mulching red clover mass can reduce biological N fixation (BFN) and input in soil53–55. This effect is mainly due to the reduced supply of carbohydrates to the nodule bacteria, while limiting the energy available for symbiotic nitrogen fixation56. According to Hatch et al. (2007)53, mulching legume–grass mixtures reduce clover populations and BFN capacity. In cut/mulch, the red clover–ryegrass–cocksfoot mixture had the least dry matter and N yields of all red clover-based leys51. Reduced N₂ fixation BFN (60–70%) due to frequent defoliation is another reason why intensive mulching should be applied with caution, balancing the quality of the mulch supply with the ecological demand for nitrogen54,55.

The release of nitrogen from the mulch depends on the weight of the plant and its contact with the soil57 and its chemical composition58. Researchers generally emphasize the key role of the initial carbon to nitrogen ratio (C: N) in controlling decomposition and nitrogen release59,60. It has been found that using mulch with a low C: N ratio (< 20) determines faster N release compared with mulch with a higher C: N ratio (> 20) 8). Legume mulches decomposed much faster and caused a higher yield and N uptake of the following crop than non-legume mulches59–61. Our study showed that the mulch of festulolium grown in mixtures with legumes had a significantly lower C: N ratio (Table 4) compared with the monoculture. This may have contributed to microbial N immobilization during festulolium mulch breakdown, or to the utilization of free nitrogen released from legume mulch by the growing plants, particularly festulolium. The data from the modeling study showed that (Figs. 2 and 3), the decomposition intensity of the mulch depended on the C: N value and mulching time. A value of this index ≤ 16 determined a 33–54% reduction in the legume mulch (Fig. 2). The first mulch, which was left on the soil surface for the longest time, was the most decomposed, while the fourth mulch was the slowest (option 4 M) to decompose, as well as mulching in the second half of the summer (2 M) caused the slowest decomposition. The size of mulch particles also affects decomposition, possibly leading to delayed mineralization62.

Halde and Entz (2016)63 state that mulch released a significant amount (46.4%) of the initial nitrogen content after just 30 days for all plant species and mulch application rates combined. Early mulching results in nitrogen-enriched, fast-decomposing mulch, while late mulching provides more biomass but results in a higher C: N ratio and slower decomposition58. The decomposition time after mulch application can also affect nutrient release, as readily degradable compounds are depleted over time61. Nitrogen release can be slowed down and delayed by mulches with a wide C: N ratio, which occurs when a mixture of legumes and non-legumes is used as mulch or when biomass is mulched when the plant is at a later stage of development. Microbiological immobilization of N then takes place. The chemical quality of organic matter regarding three C pools (labile, intermediate, and recalcitrant compounds) was observed to be important in regulating the response of microbial N immobilization in the short-term64. The presence of wheat straw in the mulch can provide a long-term source of nitrogen for the plant and reduce the risk of nitrogen leaching from the rapidly decomposing low C: N ratio mulch59. Mulch decomposition is not a continuous process but occurs in successive pulses corresponding to favorable hydrothermal conditions57. Factors related to the soil microenvironment, such as soil porosity, pH, and soil water content, influence the decomposition of residues and nutrient cycling by regulating microbial succession and activity in the soil13.

It has been stated that belowground plant parts contribute more to the stabilization of soil organic carbon than aboveground parts5,65. This indicates that the main plant compounds reaching the soil are structural constituents5. In pure legume stands, mulching had a more negative effect on the root mass of lucerne than that of red clover (Table 5). When grown in mixtures with festulolium, the negative effect of mulching on root mass was less pronounced. In our study, the C: N value of the forage legume root index was close to humification. In lucerne, root C: N was higher than that of RC. Intensive mulching tended to reduce the C: N value (Table 7). Intensive harvesting, grazing, or defoliation can reduce the root mass of plants66,67 and has a negative effect on the accumulation of carbohydrates in roots, which interferes with regrowth68,69. Xu et al. (2021)69 also highlighted the strong interaction between carbon accumulation in the below-ground and above-ground parts of plants. These findings underline the need to manage both the frequency of cutting and the proportion of plant species in the mixture to maintain root system function and ensure stable plant yield. This indicates that most of N was released from the rhizodeposition and roots.

Changes in SMN in late autumn and spring In our study, forage crops and their mulch were plowed in late summer, and winter wheat was sown in the second half of September. After removing the aboveground biomass from the field, the highest SMN content was found after pure legumes or their mixtures with festulolium (Fig. 4). This indicates that most of N was released from the rhizodeposition and roots. It is estimated that the rhizodeposition flux represents 20 to 50% of the net root production70.Similar results were also obtained by other researchers71. Studies on above- and belowground biomass decomposition have shown that roots released less nitrogen (27.3–50.7% of initial N) than shoots (63.4–70.0% of initial N), depending on the plant species50. That most of N is released in the first four weeks, with additional release between weeks 4 and 1072. When the plant material is young and N-rich, and mineralization of biomass is rapid5; however, our mulch mass had already partially decomposed. In most cases, extensive mulching tended to reduce SMN, while intensive mulching tended to increase it. Growing legumes in mixtures also contributed to the reduction in autumn SMN, but not as markedly as the intensity of mulching (Fig. 4). Grasses generally have a higher carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, which leads to nitrogen immobilization51. Studies have shown that extensive mulching, in contrast to intensive mulching, does not cause excess N. Extensive mulching significantly reduced the SMN pool (Exp I—after L, L + Fs and Exp II—after pure legume) compared to intensive mulching.

In most cases, it is not possible to utilize green manure N in autumn due to low N demand of the crop (winter wheat)20. According to Kostensalo et al. (2024)73, soil nitrate nitrogen concentration in autumn is an excellent predictor of N leaching. In spring, the mineral N content decreased by 9.9% or 18.7%, depending on the winter conditions (Fig. 4). In clay loam Cambisol, when the temperature is below 0 °C, SMN can be retained until spring (even nitrogen from legumes). Contrary to the findings of other researchers74, the lowest SMN was recorded when the biomass was removed from the field, whereas in the mulched plots, it showed a consistent increase. This can be explained by the nitrogen accumulated in the rhizosphere of legumes. However, fluctuations between positive and negative temperatures during winter may promote SMN losses in the soil of intensively mulched plots. However, in extensively mulched plots, SMN may increase due to re-immobilization.

Soil organic carbon: Soil organic matter results from the biotransformation of inputs by plants (mainly belowground inputs)29,75. The mean residence time (MRT) of root-derived carbon in the soil is almost 2.4 times longer than that of aboveground-derived carbon, which may be related to stronger physical protection by soil minerals and aggregates76. In general, plant roots control and influence soil organic carbon (SOC) dynamics and accumulation. Plant influence and variation are determined by their morphological, phenological, and physiological characteristics39,77. Roots, plant residues, and by-products of micro-organisms (bacteria and fungi) and invertebrates contribute to the formation of aggregates that store soil organic carbon33. Chang et al. (2024)78 emphasized that biological factors played a more decisive role in SOC accumulation than physical protection. It is argued that amino sugars and the amount of the clay fraction, combined with parameters related to the input of organic matter (root mass and amount of the particulate organic matter fraction), accounted for more than 90% of the variability in SOC stocks79. In our study, the highest SOC was found after the cultivation of festulolium. Legumes and their mixtures with festulolium increased mobile humic acids. To more thoroughly assess the variation of SOC in the short term, we examined the humic substances and determined the degree of humification and the ratio of humic acids to fulvic acids (HR: FR) (Table 7). The higher these two ratios, the stronger the humification processes in the soil and the greater the chances of retaining humus. Mobile humic substances stimulate microbial activity in the soil5. Lucerne was found to have the greatest positive effect on the humification of plant residues. Other researchers have reported that in semi-arid lucerne grasslands, specific abiotic (soil phosphorus and moisture) and biotic (plant species diversity) factors can control the content of organic matter (SOC) and total N80. Extensive mulching tended to increase the amount of SOC compounds and the humification index.

Studies in different climatic zones have confirmed that mulching or cover crops statistically increase SOC27. Cover cropping and leaving residues in the field have been shown to increase SOC stocks by an average of 7–15% compared with systems without mulching81. Other researchers have reported that cover crops resulted in a 15.5% increase in soil SOC in the topsoil (i.e., at depths ≤ 30 cm), indicating that approximately 0.16 ± 0.06 Pg of carbon could be sequestered per year82. The highest increases in soil organic carbon were observed in fertile soils and under long-term cover crop cultivation83. Forage crops bases new ways of incorporating them into cropping systems (e.g., living mulch, green manure) and a better quantification of the combinations of ecosystem services they provide.

Although the Tyurin wet oxidation method may slightly underestimate soil organic carbon due to incomplete oxidation of more stable fractions84, all samples were analyzed using a consistent standardized protocol, so relative differences among treatments remain reliable and do not affect the interpretation of treatment effects or conclusions on soil organic carbon dynamics.

Conclusions and research perspectives

This study demonstrated that the use of forage legumes and their mixtures with festulolium, combined with aboveground biomass mulching, significantly influenced soil carbon and nitrogen dynamics in organic arable farming. The C: N ratio of mulch and root biomass varied similarly, being the lowest in legumes, highest in festulolium, and intermediate in mixtures. Intensive mulching reduced the mulch C: N ratio. Due to lower C and especially N concentrations, the root C: N was higher than that of mulch and changed little or tended to decrease under intensive mulching. Root biomass was greatest in legume mixtures with festuloliumMulch with a C: N ratio ≤ 16 released 33–54% of nitrogen during the vegetation period. In late autumn, forage crop biomass—either removed from the field or extensively mulched—accounted for the majority of SMN. This indicating that soil mineral nitrogen (SMN) largely depended on the decomposition of roots, litter, and rhizodeposition. Legume and festulolium mixtures resulted in higher SMN than festulolium alone but significantly lower SMN compared with the corresponding legumes. The persistence of SMN in the soil until spring was influenced not only by the management practices used but also by meteorological conditions. In spring, after vegetation resumed, the greatest SMN decrease (compared with autumn) was observed when forage crop biomass was removed from the field and in unfavorable years also under intensive mulching. The extensive biomass management strategy resulted in the lowest SMN losses during the non-vegetation period, thereby promoting a more stable carbon soil and nitrogen balance. However, in clay loam soil, higher SMN levels in spring can also be observed after incorporation of legumes when their biomass is mulched on the soil surface.

Intensive mulching of pure legume biomass accelerated organic matter mineralization and nutrient turnover, yet the temporal release of nitrogen from plant residues and roots (WM) was not optimally synchronized with the peak nitrogen demand of winter wheat in early spring. The humification indexwas significantly increased in systems incorporating lucerne under extensive mulching, underscoring its root role in enhancing organic matter stabilization. Future research should explore a broader diversity of forage legume–festulolium mixtures, alongside alternative strategies for processing and managing aboveground biomass. Particular attention should be paid to optimizing the synchrony between nitrogen release from plant residues and the nutrient uptake dynamics of subsequent crops using no-till technologies. The data obtained contributes to a deeper mechanistic understanding of nitrogen and carbon cycling in organic farming systems and supports the development of improved agronomic practices aimed at enhancing nutrient use efficiency and soil sustainability.
