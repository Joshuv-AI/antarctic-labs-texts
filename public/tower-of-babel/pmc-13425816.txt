Prospective Development of a Whole-Brain Connectivity and Electric Field–Guided Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation Atlas for Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Symptoms
Authors: Sneha Chandrashekar, Grant Brighter, Ivy Sun, Hannah Gura, Milan Patel, David F Gregory, Kevin G Lynch, Audreyana Jagger-Rickels, Desmond J Oathes, Yvette I Sheline, Lily Brown, William P Milberg, Catherine B Fortier, Michael Esterman, Nicholas L Balderston
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough trauma-focused psychotherapy remains the first-line treatment for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), 14% to 35% of individuals with PTSD fail to achieve symptom remission. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) has shown promise as an adjunctive intervention, but its efficacy for PTSD remains inconsistent and may be better informed by the incorporation of brain-based signals to guide stimulation. To address this, we developed a connectivity-based modeling framework to identify functional networks that predict symptom severity and to estimate where stimulation would most effectively reduce PTSD symptoms.MethodsWe used clinical, structural, and resting-state functional data from 350 veterans with and without PTSD. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to functional connectivity matrices to reduce dimensionality, and resulting component scores were entered into a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression to identify connections associated with symptom severity. To simulate TMS effects, we generated electric field models for 200 randomly distributed cortical sites per participant and used these distributions to estimate connectivity changes proportional to the modeled fields. The estimated changes were combined with PCA regression weights to predict stimulation-induced symptom changes at each site and were mapped across the cortex to create a whole-brain TMS targeting atlas.ResultsThe model identified 2 broad and opposing patterns. Stimulation expected to increase connectivity in the left prefrontal cortex was predicted to reduce symptoms, whereas stimulation expected to decrease connectivity in bilateral parietal and occipital regions was also predicted to reduce symptoms.ConclusionsThese findings provide a framework for individualized TMS targeting in PTSD and generate potential therapeutic targets for future clinical trials.Keywords: CAPS, E-field monitoring, Precision medicine, PTSD, rs-fMRI, TMS

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a psychiatric condition marked by symptoms including intrusive memories of traumatic events, alterations in arousal, avoidance of trauma-related cues, and negative changes in mood and cognition (1). Approximately 7% to 9% of individuals in the United States develop PTSD during their lifetime, with rates increasing to ∼20% among military veterans (2,3). Standard treatments including pharmacotherapy and trauma-focused psychotherapy show wide variation in effectiveness (4), and many patients with PTSD do not achieve remission (5). Therefore, there is a need for novel treatment approaches that can work as either standalone second-line treatments or adjuncts to current psychotherapy/pharmacotherapy regimens.

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is a noninvasive technique that uses magnetic pulses to influence neural activity (6). The Food and Drug Administration has approved TMS to treat psychiatric disorders such as major depressive disorder (MDD) and obsessive-compulsive disorder. TMS protocols for PTSD are still under investigation, with many clinical studies adapting MDD TMS protocols to treat comorbid PTSD and MDD (7). However, clinical outcomes from these PTSD TMS studies have been inconsistent, suggesting that traditional TMS targeting approaches, mainly based on fixed anatomical landmarks of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), may not effectively engage the neural circuits disrupted in PTSD (8, 9, 10).

Neuroimaging suggests that PTSD involves disruptions across large-scale brain networks (11). Specifically, PTSD is associated with altered connectivity across networks such as the default mode network (DMN), executive control network, and salience network (SN) (9,12,13). However, network connectivity varies between individuals and can affect the presentation and severity of symptoms (14). Studies have demonstrated distinct subtypes of PTSD (e.g., impaired executive function subtype and decreased ventral attention network subtype) that exhibit different patterns of connectivity and are associated with variability in symptom severity and treatment response (15,16). Beyond categorical subtypes, PTSD symptoms may also be organized along fear-related symptoms and more generalized emotional pain, which are also associated with distinct patterns of network interactions (17). Both high-frequency and low-frequency repetitive TMS (rTMS) targeting the right dlPFC (18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25) have shown therapeutic benefit despite potentially opposing within-session mechanisms of action (26) and limited data characterizing the long-term effects of these stimulation patterns on connectivity. This seemingly contradictory finding could potentially be explained by the current theory that PTSD may involve multiple symptom-dependent patterns of prefrontal-limbic circuit dysfunction (13,27).

Furthermore, TMS protocols often target skull-based anatomical landmarks without addressing neuroanatomical differences across individuals and downstream connectivity effects of TMS. Polysynaptic signal transduction of the TMS pulse is key to the mechanism of action of TMS (28,29), and work with resting-state connectivity clearly shows that network changes are widely distributed (30,31). Recent work has shown that network changes are proportional to the distribution of the electric field (E-field) (32) and associated with clinical outcomes (33). Combining functional connectivity–based targeting and E-field modeling is a step toward precise and mechanistical treatments.

Our group previously developed a connectivity-based E-field modeling framework that integrates resting-state functional connectivity, simulated E-fields, and dimensional symptom data to predict treatment response in depression (34). The approach modeled changes in connectivity associated with the E-field for a given stimulation site to determine the degree to which these changes correlate with symptoms and compare these predicted changes across sites. This modeling approach made predictions about the localization of stimulation sites within the left dlPFC that were consistent with previous clinical trials, showing that sites where the E-field–modeled connectivity changes were most strongly associated with depression symptoms were those that had been previously shown to be effective at treating depression (34). While these results offer preliminary evidence supporting this modeling approach, there has yet to be a clinical trial directly testing the assumptions of this model. While it may seem that the next logical step would be to use our model for targeting in a prospective clinical trial aimed at improving depression symptoms, we believe that the clinical gains would be marginal compared with existing targeting approaches. The goal of the current study was to determine the extent to which this model could be used for target discovery, using PTSD as a model system, with 2 overarching objectives. The first objective was to identify network-level predictors of symptom severity. The second objective was to generate a whole-brain atlas of predicted TMS targets most likely to reduce PTSD symptoms. Critically, the current work is not an empirical test of the model. Instead, the current study is intended to support future symptom-guided randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Methods and Materials

Participants

Data for this study were drawn from the TRACTS (Translational Research Center for TBI and Stress Disorders) longitudinal cohort (35). Participants were U.S. service members and veterans who were deployed or to be deployed post-9/11 in operations in support of the Global War on Terror. Recruitment occurred through outreach at yellow ribbon events, task force meetings, and other military-related events in New England, in addition to flyers, word of mouth, and referrals at the Veterans Affairs Boston Healthcare System (VA BHS). General Procedures

All study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Human Studies Research at the VA BHS. Participants provided informed consent prior to participation. Participants completed a baseline evaluation that included biomedical, background and health data, trauma assessment, and psychiatric assessment, as well as neuropsychological and self-report indices and neuroimaging. Participants were compensated for time and travel. The analysis workflow is shown in Figure 1. See the Supplement for additional details related to the scanning protocol and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) preprocessing.

Preprocessing pipeline for the resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging data. Data were preprocessed using afni_proc. The cleaned time series were downsampled to the Gordon atlas. Pairwise Pearson’s correlations were calculated between each region and every other region. These correlation data were vectorized, catonated across participants, and reduced using a principal component analysis (PCA), resulting in PCA weightings for each participant × component combination and item loadings for each connection × component combination. These PCA weightings and item loadings were then used in the analysis described in Figure 3.

Target Localization

For each participant, 200 randomized targets were generated across the mantle of the cortex. We chose not to cluster the randomized simulation sites around a priori stimulation regions because the primary goal of the current study was to use archival data and a bottom-up data-driven approach to discover potential new targets for intervention. Tissues from the SimNIBS mesh were converted to stl files, and the coordinates from each vertex were extracted (Figure 2). Coordinates from the gray matter surface defined the target floor. Skin coordinates above this floor were randomly sorted, and 200 vertices were selected for each participant and used as hypothetical stimulation sites (see Figure 3). To create the cortical atlas, these sites were then projected to the nearest gray matter vertex using the knnsearch function in MATLAB (version R2023A; The MathWorks, Inc.).

Preprocessing pipeline for the structural magnetic resonance imaging data. T1s were segmented using mri2mesh. Two hundred randomly selected targets were selected from vertices distributed across the cortical mantle and projected to the scalp. Electric field (E-field) models were calculated for each site. The E-field models were then downsampled to the Gordon atlas. The downsampled E-field models were then converted to E-field matrices by averaging the E-field magnitude in each region with the magnitude in each other region. These matrices were then used in the analysis described in Figure 3.

E-Field Calculations

We modeled the E-field distribution using a figure-of-eight coil definition file distributed with the SimNIBS software package (version 4.1.0a1; Medtronic_MCF_B65.ccd). The roll and pitch of the coil were defined perpendicular to the cortex at each site of stimulation. For each of the 200 sites, we calculated 3 separate models with evenly spaced yaw orientations centered on the scalp target, tangential to the scalp surface. We chose to calculate 3 models per site with varying yaw orientations because it was unclear how to define the yaw orientation at each site using an a priori approach. Choosing the yaw orientation that maximizes the E-field magnitude at the cortical target is widely used in studies using E-field modeling to determine coil angle (36, 37, 38, 39, 40). Then, we selected the model that maximized the E-field magnitude at the corresponding cortical vertex (37,41). The resulting maps were then downsampled to the Gordon atlas (42) (see Figure 4). To identify regions most strongly influenced by stimulation, the vector was sorted by E-field magnitude to resemble a scree plot and thresholded at the point farthest away from the hypotenuse connecting the first and last values in the plot (32,34). Our model uses these pairwise correlations to predict symptoms, so it was not only important to model the E-field at each region but also at each pair of regions. To do this, we converted the single vector of E-field values (i.e., to values across regions) to a matrix (i.e., average values across pairs of regions), which was then used to model predicted changes in connectivity. The downsampled and thresholded vector was expanded into a 333 × 333 matrix with each entry representing the average induced current across a pair of regions shown in Figure 2A (32,34). This matrix provided an estimate of how stimulation at a given site and orientation could modulate functional connectivity between parcels.

Principal Component Analysis Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator Regression

We used principal component analysis (PCA) least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (lasso) regression to model the relationship between functional connectivity and symptoms while reducing noise and minimizing collinearity in the imaging data. First, a PCA was conducted on the functional connectivity matrices to decompose the data into orthogonal components that capture the largest sources of variance. Next, component scores from the PCA were entered into a lasso regression to predict PTSD severity (indexed by total Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-IV [CAPS-IV] scores). We used 10-fold cross validation to select the optimal regularization parameter (λ). We selected the λ value where reductions in the mean square error (MSE) plateaued (i.e., the maximal decrease in MSE across λ values). Then, we multiplied the resulting regression coefficients by the item loadings from the PCA to generate a weighting matrix that reflects the degree to which any given connection predicts PTSD symptoms.

Symptom Correlate Model

For each site, we then calculated the degree to which predicted changes in connectivity associated with the distribution of the E-field generated predictions for how PTSD symptoms might change following a therapeutic course of TMS (32,34). The predicted impact of TMS on symptom severity was estimated by integrating the participants’ individualized E-field matrix, functional connectivity matrix, and group-level coefficients from the PCA regression linking connectivity with symptoms (34). These values were combined to generate a participant-specific prediction of neuromodulatory effect (34). The procedure was repeated across all candidate stimulation sites. The resulting predictions were spatially smoothed using a Gaussian kernel (full width at half maximum [FWHM] = 4 mm) to produce a continuous heatmap representation (see Figure 2B). Each entry in the heatmap reflected the predicted relative change in symptom severity for a given stimulation site and orientation, prior to scaling by a constant factor.

Group-Level Whole-Brain Analysis

To evaluate whether the modeling approach predicted significant symptom changes at a group level, we performed a 1-sample t test at each voxel in the cortical mantle using the AFNI tool 3dttest++ (https://afni.nimh.nih.gov/pub/dist/doc/program_help/3dttest++.html). Predicted symptom changes were defined as the difference between posttreatment and baseline symptom scores estimated by the model. Significant negative t values indicated a predicted group-level decrease in symptoms, whereas the null hypothesis for each site combination was that no change would be predicted. The resulting T scores were visualized in heatmaps at the voxel level.

Results

PCA-Lasso Regression

We used a PCA to decompose the resting-state connectivity data into orthogonal components (43). These components were then entered into a lasso in relation to total CAPS scores, the dependent variable. We used 10-fold cross-validation and selected a λ value at the point where reductions in MSE reached an asymptote. Based on this approach, we selected a λ value of 1.58, resulting in an MSE ± SE value of 1121.55 ± 46.22 (see Figure 2C). This yielded 122 nonzero coefficients being included in the model. The PCs corresponding to these coefficients accounted for 50.33% of the variability in the resting-state connectivity data. The fitted coefficients from the lasso regression were then combined with the item loadings from the PCA using matrix multiplication. The resulting 333 × 333 matrix represents the degree to which each pairwise connection contributes to the model for evaluating associations with CAPS scores.

Discussion

In this study, we used archival structural MRI and fMRI data to prospectively map the potential TMS sites that modulate resting-state functional connectivity associated with PTSD symptoms. We implemented an exploratory, dimensional, whole-brain approach to both characterize symptom-relevant networks and define potential TMS stimulation sites. We used a PCA combined with a lasso regression to identify networks associated with PTSD symptoms and E-field modeling to predict how TMS may modulate PTSD symptoms through changes in connectivity. We simulated stimulation sites across the cortex and found 2 patterns in the distribution of predicted PTSD symptom change scores as shown in Figure 5. First, our model predicted that simulated sites over the left PFC would significantly reduce PTSD symptoms for stimulation patterns that facilitated functional connectivity. Second, our model predicted that simulated sites over bilateral occipital and parietal regions would significantly reduce PTSD symptoms for stimulation patterns that diminished functional connectivity. Additionally, we used symptom measures to cluster participants into low, medium, and high severity groups. Our model predicted differential benefits across these clusters, with the low and medium severity groups predicted to benefit most from stimulation to the occipital and parietal sites, while the high severity group was predicted to benefit most from stimulation to the left prefrontal and anterior temporal sites (see Supplement).

The current model is based on 2 fundamental assumptions. First, the model assumes that TMS impacts psychiatric symptoms by modulating brain connectivity (44). Support for this assumption comes from recent studies that have used prospective and retrospective image-guided targeting algorithms to personalize the selection of TMS targets for depression (45). Retrospective analyses of anatomically targeted stimulation showed better clinical outcomes when the left dlPFC site was stimulated closer to a personalized region maximally anticorrelated with the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC), suggesting that functional connectivity may help guide optimal treatment response (46). Furthermore, prospective studies have demonstrated that patients randomly assigned to receive stimulation at connectivity-guided, personalized TMS sites had better outcomes than those who received stimulation at standardized (i.e., not personalized) defined sites (47). However, it should be noted that the added benefit gained from personalized functional targeting can be modest, especially in situations where effective group-level clinical targets exist, as is true for depression (48, 49, 50). The model’s second assumption is that changes in brain connectivity induced by TMS are proportional to the distribution of energy introduced into the cortical gray by the TMS pulse. Our group has done preliminary work showing that there are systematic changes in resting-state connectivity following intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) and that the spatial distribution of these changes is proportional to the spatial distribution of the E-field model for the site of stimulation (32).

This work builds on our previous proof-of-concept study in an anxious depression cohort (34). In this previous study, we used a similar PCA regression approach to predict Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) and Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) scores based on predicted connectivity changes associated with stimulation sites positioned along the anterior to posterior axis of the left dlPFC. We found systematic variability in predicted MADRS/HAMD scores as a function of both the position of the simulated site and the orientation of the coil at that site. At the group level, the optimal stimulation site was proximal to the Beam/F3 coordinates used in clinical practice (34). At the single-participant level, the optimal personalized stimulation site was proximal to the dlPFC site maximally anticorrelated with the sgACC (34). Both findings show that our model identifies optimized sites that have demonstrated clinical efficacy, lending preliminary support to the overall modeling approach (34). In the current study, we applied the same approach with a focus on novel target discovery by expanding beyond the left dlPFC to search across the entire cortical mantle. This expanded search not only identified potential targets in regions with known clinical targets (i.e., the left dlPFC and dorsomedial PFC) but also identified novel parietal and occipital targets.

Historically, the primary clinical target for PTSD has been the left dlPFC as PTSD patients with comorbid depression tend to show improvements in both depression and PTSD symptoms with standard TMS treatment (51, 52, 53, 54). Additionally, connectivity-guided personalization seems to increase the efficacy of left dlPFC stimulation for PTSD (55). Consistent with these studies, our TMS targeting atlas predicts symptom improvements with excitatory stimulation to the left dlPFC. However, our atlas does not predict a significant change in total CAPS scores following excitatory or inhibitory stimulation to the right dlPFC. Several clinical trials have investigated right dlPFC stimulation to treat PTSD (18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25), but no consistent relationship between stimulation pattern and outcomes has emerged. In one trial, 1-Hz stimulation to the right dlPFC resulted in reductions in CAPS and PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) scores (19, 20, 21, 22), while other mechanistically opposing stimulation protocols using iTBS (23, 24, 25) and 20 Hz (18) also showed a reduction in PCL-5 scores. Finally, in a recent double-blind RCT, van Rooij et al. (56) found that right dlPFC 1-Hz rTMS targeted using amygdala connectivity reduced amygdala reactivity and hyperarousal in patients with PTSD. Together, these results suggest that additional work needs to be done to characterize the discordant findings from both the left and right dlPFC, and systematic studies need to be conducted to directly compare the efficacy of left and right dlPFC sites. The medial PFC (mPFC) is another emerging treatment target for PTSD as a regulator of fear responses and emotional regulation. In one study, 18-Hz deep TMS was delivered to the mPFC using the Brainsway H7 coil combined with a script-driven imagery task and was associated with improved outcomes in the sham group compared with the active treatment group (57). However, our atlas predicts a significant effect of mPFC stimulation on CAPS scores but suggests that protocols designed to decrease connectivity rather than increase connectivity may result in decreases in CAPS scores.

Our approach differs from other connectivity-guided targeting approaches in that it is not based on an a priori model for circuit dysfunction, such as hyper-/hypoconnectivity between the dlPFC and the sgACC (46). Instead, it derives the connectivity-guided target based on empirical estimates of the associations between connectivity and symptoms. In this way, it is similar to the lesion mapping approach adopted by Siddiqi et al. (58, 59, 60), which uses focal brain lesions, deep brain targets, and TMS outcomes to identify common circuits associated with symptoms such as depression (58,59) or PTSD (60). However, it differs from both previous connectivity-guided targeting approaches and the lesion mapping approach in that it uses E-field modeling to estimate TMS-induced changes in whole-brain functional connectivity and then generates a predicted symptom change score from these estimated changes. Accordingly, the atlas generated in Figure 5 does not represent a circuit as in the work by Siddiqi et al. (58,59). Instead, it represents the predicted efficacy of stimulation delivered at the sites plotted on the cortical surface. These 2 aspects of our model make this approach generalizable to other symptom dimensions.

Future Directions

The results of the current work suggest that changing connectivity proportional to the E-field distribution over left prefrontal and bilateral occipital sites may result in changes in PTSD symptoms. While there is some evidence that iTBS-induced changes in connectivity show spatial correlations with the E-field distribution at the site of stimulation, the evidence for this relationship is much weaker for other forms of stimulation such as continuous TBS (cTBS) or rTMS. Additionally, it is important to note that the current results are still at the proof-of-concept stage and can only be used to predict differences in treatment outcomes as a function of target location. Without additional data to characterize the pre-post changes in functional connectivity for various patterns and doses of TMS, it is not possible to make accurate predictions regarding the direction or magnitude of potential treatment effects. Accordingly, the critical next step will be to test predictions of this model in a separate cohort in an RCT. Additional discussion of this and other future directions can be found in the Supplement.

Strengths and Limitations

In the current study, we present a novel use of our E-field and connectivity-guided TMS targeting algorithm to create a novel whole-brain atlas of prospective TMS targets to reduce CAPS scores. The benefit of this modeling approach includes the generation of a whole-brain targeting atlas to guide target discovery for novel psychiatric indications. The participant clustering results from the current work and the individualized targeting results from our previous proof-of-concept study also suggest that the model can be used to optimize and personalize TMS targeting at the individual level (34). The model is generalizable, symptom specific, and requires only a single normative relationship to map connectivity/symptom correlations. Also, the model uses a novel PCA-lasso regression approach to maximize on-target effects and makes specific predictions about how to optimize the TMS parameter selection (e.g., TBS pattern) to modulate network connectivity to maximally reduce symptoms. Combined with future work showing exactly how TMS modulates connectivity, this model may be able to make specific quantitative predictions about the magnitude of symptom changes following neuromodulatory TMS and how specific protocol parameters might impact these changes.

Despite these strengths, there are some methodological characteristics of the current model that should be considered. In the previous proof-of-concept study, we related connectivity and symptoms using a standard PCA regression and included components in the model based on how much variability in connectivity they accounted for rather than how much variability in symptoms they accounted for. In contrast, in the current work, we used a lasso regression to weight components from the PCA and selected a λ value that balanced model simplicity with prediction accuracy. In the current approach, the weighting of the connectivity components depends on how well they predict symptoms in the selected lasso regression model. As a result, our current model necessarily weights on-target effects more strongly than off-target effects, which could have 2 potential downstream effects. First, it could lead to different components (i.e., functional networks) being selected for different symptoms, thus influencing the potential targeting effects. Second, by excluding components that do not contribute to the symptom prediction model, this targeting approach may ignore off-target effects. Despite this potential limitation, we view this trade-off as acceptable because the excluded components in the target discovery model were not significantly related to the predicted symptom variation and include components that can have either a positive or a negative influence. Accordingly, we predict that the off-target components excluded from the current targeting approach represent noise rather than effects that would likely affect the model. For targeting, we used the most inferior point of the cortical mantle to define the lower bounds of the targets. We understand that this overly simplistic heuristic results in simulations that most likely would not be real-world targets such as the eyes and ear lobes. We chose not to exclude these simulation sites so that we could present a comprehensive atlas of predicted symptom changes across the entire cortex. Future use of this approach in a prospective manner would necessitate clinical decision making to balance predicted treatment response and overall target feasibility and tolerability.

Another limitation is that there is currently no generalized model capable of predicting changes in connectivity following neuromodulatory TMS. Previously, we used E-field modeling to estimate the changes in connectivity following a short 3-day course of iTBS in patients with depression (32). We found that the distribution of changes in resting-state connectivity following iTBS was significantly more correlated with the distribution of the E-field estimated for the actual TMS coil position relative to an unstimulated control site. However, this preliminary study did not account for the effects of dose or pattern, which may influence the strength, distribution, and direction of changes resulting from the TMS delivered. Our laboratory is currently collecting data in a large multiday TMS trial where resting-state connectivity data are collected before and after three 5-train TBS sessions. This project, which we have named the Philadelphia Theta Burst Cohort, will eventually include 4 scans each from approximately 200 healthy participants receiving either cTBS or iTBS to the left dlPFC. By including both dose and pattern as variables in this project, we will be able to use this dataset to refine the current prediction model and make precise, testable predictions about how specific patterns and doses of TBS will modulate specific symptoms in prospectively targeted TMS clinical trials.

Finally, it is important to note that the current work is based on archival data from the TRACTS cohort, which could be considered a limiting factor for several reasons. First, this cohort was recruited from a military population, and additional studies should be conducted with nonmilitary samples to examine the degree to which these findings generalize. Second, this cohort was oversampled for males, making the degree to which these samples generalize to females with PTSD unclear. Third, the data for this cohort were collected from February 2010 to January 2021, and the scanner hardware and fMRI sequences have advanced substantially since these data were collected. Fourth, this work does not factor in concurrent treatment or medication status, which could affect targeting. Future studies replicating this work should be conducted with more representative samples using updated hardware and software for data collection. We also understand that current treatments may impact the relationship between symptoms and connectivity and may thus impact targeting. Future studies, especially those using functional imaging for individualized TMS targeting, should incorporate treatment and medication status as a moderator variable.

Conclusions

The current study demonstrates that integrating E-field modeling with individual connectivity and symptom prediction provides a powerful model for identifying TMS targets in PTSD. By generating a whole-brain atlas of stimulation sites and linking these to specific symptom profiles, the model moves beyond fixed anatomical coordinates and offers a scalable approach for individualized neuromodulation. We identified that stimulation that increased connectivity in the left PFC was predicted to reduce symptoms, whereas stimulation that decreased connectivity in bilateral parietal and occipital regions were also predicted to reduce symptoms.

Although further validation is required, the current findings establish a foundation for future targeting approaches that incorporate individualized network structure, symptom dimensionality, and stimulation parameters. As future work is conducted to clarify how dose and pattern influence connectivity changes, this model may support testable predictions regarding symptom improvement. Ultimately, this model has the potential to guide personalized clinical interventions and advance the development of circuit-informed treatments for PTSD.
