Correlation of Scaphotrapezoid Contact Pressures With Degree of Trapezoid Resection: Does Eliminating Contact Matter?
Authors: Hayden Anz, Jordan Roylance, Sabir Ismaily, Shuyang Han, Peter Gibson, James Saucedo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeScaphotrapeziotrapezoid (STT) arthritis is the third most common hand arthritis in the United States. In the setting of thumb carpometacarpal arthroplasty, varying degrees of partial excision of the proximal trapezoid have been suggested. We sought to quantify how much resection was adequate to prevent scaphotrapezoid (ST) impingement through a biomechanical cadaveric study. It was hypothesized that the commonly suggested 2 mm of trapezoid excision would be inadequate to prevent ST impingement in all planes of motion.MethodsEight fresh-frozen cadaveric upper extremities were screened for normal STT morphology for inclusion. Each extremity was prepared for testing with the forearm transfixed in neutral rotation, then mounted in a servohydraulic testing system. A motion capture system was used to evaluate the range of motion. A dorsal approach to the thumb carpometacarpal was performed with trapezium excision. A Tekscan sensor was secured within the ST joint, and the following conditions were tested: 45N in terminal flexion, 45N in terminal extension, 89N in terminal extension, and 45N in radial deviation. Flexion and extension loads were applied axially in line with the forearm, and radial deviation was applied 90° tangentially to the forearm. Tests were repeated after incremental 2-mm trapezoid resections up to 6 mm.Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, followed by Kruskal–Wallis and Dunn’s post hoc tests for nonparametric comparisons.ResultsNo significant ST joint contact occurred in extension at any resection depth. A statistically significant decrease in contact pressures required a 4-mm resection (P = .008) in flexion and a 6-mm resection (P = .028) in radial deviation.ConclusionsSignificant reduction in ST contact pressures in flexion and radial deviation requires 4–6 mm of resection. There is no significant ST impingement in wrist extension. Achieving impingement-free motion in flexion and radial deviation requires more resection than previously believed.Clinical relevanceIf the goal of ST arthritis management through partial trapezoid resection is impingement-free range of motion, then at least 4–6 mm of trapezoid must be resected. Our data may support investigating alternative interventions for the management of ST arthritis.Key words: Basal thumb arthritis, Scaphotrapezoid arthritis, STT arthritis, Wrist arthritis

Scaphotrapeziotrapezoid (STT) osteoarthritis is the third most common hand arthritis in the United States.1 Scaphotrapeziotrapezoid arthritis is visible radiographically in between 11% and 64% of patients, yet the true prevalence remains unknown.1 Concomitant thumb carpometacarpal joint and STT osteoarthritis is relatively common and has been identified intraoperatively in 62% of patients undergoing trapezium excision.1,2 When identified, intraoperatively, a section of proximal trapezoid is often excised, with the amount ranging from 2 mm to one-third the observed height to decompress the scaphotrapezoid (ST) joint.2,3 Persistent contact between the distal pole of the scaphoid can then be assessed with certain stress maneuvers, including axial compression along the second ray or through ranging the wrist. However, it is unclear whether an additional trapezoid should be excised in the presence of persistent or observed impingement. Quantifying contact pressures at the ST joint after serial resection and in various wrist positions may help us understand whether observed contact generates clinically concerning contact pressures.

A number of studies have investigated the safety of trapezoid excision. For instance, Wright et al4 investigated index metacarpal loading following partial trapezoid excision and found that one may safely excise up to 50% of the trapezoid without altering the index metacarpal’s ability to endure axial load. However, this study was only performed in the “power grip” position (20° of wrist extension and 5° of ulnar deviation) and did not investigate contact pressures in this plane under load. Additionally, Ferikes et al3 investigated impingement in wrist flexion and radial deviation, yet the data collection method was subjective. Therefore, the adequacy of trapezoid excision for the treatment of STT arthritis under load with contact pressures has not been investigated. Further investigation of the contact pressures across the ST joint following partial trapezoid excision in wrist flexion, extension, and radial deviation may be warranted.

This study evaluates whether 2-mm-partial trapezoid excision prevents ST impingement under load in wrist flexion, extension, and radial deviation. We hypothesized that a 2-mm trapezoid excision is insufficient to prevent ST impingement in all planes of motion under load.

Materials and Methods

Eight fresh-frozen cadaveric upper extremities were obtained for the purpose of this study. The specimens were screened radiographically for STT and basilar thumb arthritis and were excluded if the morphology of the scaphotrapezoid joint, trapeziotrapezoid joint, or trapeziometacarpal joint was altered. Each extremity was skeletonized 10 cm proximal to the wrist crease, and the radius and ulna were fixed to each other with screws. An osteotomy was then performed distal to the bicipital tuberosity. Each limb was then potted in epoxy resin to facilitate subsequent load application (Fig. 1). A custom jig was fashioned to hold the limb in the appropriate position within a servohydraulic test system (Material Testing Machine [MTS] 858, MTS Systems). For each specimen, a dorsal approach to the thumb was performed through which the trapezium was excised. A force sensor (Tekscan FlexiForce A201) was calibrated prior to each session, as described by the manufacturer, then placed within the ST joint (Fig. 2). To track the joint motion using a motion capture system (Vicon), retroflective markers were also placed at 3 points: proximal on the radial border of the radial shaft, on the radial border of the radial styloid, and at the radial border of the distal index metacarpal (Fig. 3). Contact pressure at the ST joint was tested across 4 different conditions: 45N in terminal flexion, 45N in terminal extension, 89N in terminal extension, and 45N in terminal radial deviation. 45N approximated a typical force used during intraoperative evaluation, and 89N approximated the force across the wrist during a pushup.5 The MTS system was used for load application, and the maximal load for each condition was cycled five times (Fig. 3). Force was applied axially in line with the forearm with the flat plate centered over the metacarpals, both volarly and dorsally to keep the force centered in the hand (Fig. 4). To test radial deviation, a load cell (Futek) was attached to the index metacarpal, and a jig was used to prevent flexion or extension at the wrist. Force was then applied directly radial to the forearm in line with the hand. Data were recorded continuously on the motion capture system as well as the force sensor throughout each test. Following each test, a 2-mm partial trapezoid resection was performed using an oscillating saw (Stryker), and resection depth was confirmed using a caliper. Tests were repeated for each resection up to 6 mm of resection. All statistical analyses were performed in SPSS 26.0 (IBM). The results are presented as mean ± SD. The normality of data distribution was examined using the Shapiro and Wilk test. The data were analyzed using nonparametric statistics by Kruskal–Wallis with Dunnett–Bonferroni multiple comparisons. The results were considered significant with a P < .05. A post hoc power analysis was performed, demonstrating that 8 specimens are adequate to detect differences in force at terminal flexion and terminal radial deviation.

Figure 5 presents the average changes in contact force at the ST joint under a 45N load in terminal flexion. Compared with the intact state (13.2N), the contact forces were significantly lower when the partial trapezoid excision depth reached 4 mm (1.5N, P = .008) and 6 mm (0N, P = .002), but not at 2 mm (7.7N, P > .99). Similar findings were observed under 45N load with radial deviation (Fig. 6), with 6-mm excision reaching a significant difference (P = .028) compared with the 2-mm condition (P > .99).

Discussion

In this cadaveric study, we evaluated the effects of partial trapezoid excision depths on ST joint impingement under load in different wrist positions. Our results indicate that a 6-mm resection is required to prevent ST impingement, which was much larger than the current clinical practice. Previous studies have reported that 2 mm of resection is typically adequate, whereas more recent biomechanics studies recommended resecting at least one-third of the trapezoid to prevent impingement.2,3 However, the earlier in vitro studies demonstrated good pain relief with only 2 mm of trapezoid resection.2 Our results do suggest that the highest forces occur under load in radial deviation. This is consistent with previous literature that indicates that an oblique osteotomy may be better suited to prevent contact in radial deviation than the transverse osteotomy that we examined.3

Partial trapezoid excision is a mainstay in the treatment of both isolated STT arthritis and STT arthritis associated with thumb CMC arthritis, with patients achieving excellent functional results.2,6,7 Multiple biomechanical studies have been performed supporting that longitudinal stability of the second ray is maintained following partial trapezoid excision.3,4 Clinically, however, trapezoid excision has been found to have some morbidity. Renfree et al7 evaluated the clinical results in a series of patients treated for STT arthritis with concomitant thumb CMC arthritis. The patients received a trapeziectomy with abductor pollicis longus suspensionplasty in addition to no treatment, partial trapezoid excision, or complete trapezoid excision. It was found that 29% of patients who underwent partial trapezoid excision had radiolunate angles >15°, and proximal index migration occurred in both the complete and partial trapezoid excision groups, with 9.5% patients in the partial trapezoid excision group experiencing symptomatic migration and none in the no treatment group. Of the 7 patients who experienced symptomatic migration, 4 required a secondary procedure for index and middle finger CMC arthrodesis.7 Although there was a subset of patients that had radiographic or symptomatic complications, pain scores were improved in the partial and complete trapezoid excision groups compared with the no treatment group who did not have intraoperative findings of ST arthritis.7 Thus, despite decades of overall good clinical results, when critically examined, this procedure can still be associated with complications necessitating revision procedures.

The findings of this study could represent an alternative direction of investigation in the treatment of STT arthritis following trapeziectomy for thumb CMC arthritis. Traditionally, it is believed that pain generated in osteoarthritis is secondary to the articulation of 2 bones with worn or damaged cartilage. Our results suggest that articulation of the ST joint persists after a 2-mm partial trapezoid excision. This suggests that the excellent pain relief reported by Tomaino et al.2 may not solely be explained by resection arthroplasty. We hypothesize that this may be related to alternative mechanisms, such as joint capsule denervation, rather than the elimination of impingement.

This concept has been popularized in the treatment of thumb CMC arthritis.8, 9, 10, 11, 12 For thumb CMC arthritis, branches of the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve, palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve, and radial sensory nerve have been identified as providing sensation to the thumb CMC joint.9 These branches are divided to denervate the joint capsule, providing durable pain relief for many patients while, critically, maintaining the mechanical stability of the joint.8, 9, 10, 11, 12 No cadaveric study has been performed to evaluate the innervation of the scaphotrapezoid joint, nor has a clinical study evaluating the effects of thumb CMC joint denervation on scaphotrapezoid arthritis. Given the proximity of these joints, it would be reasonable to assume that the 2 joints may share a similar innervation pattern, although further study is needed to closely examine this.

There are several potential limitations in this study, given its cadaveric nature. Most notably, the study was unable to assess pain, and there is no literature available detailing what levels of contact pressure are associated with arthritic pain. However, clinical studies detailing pain relief after different degrees of resection suggest that contact elimination may not be the sole mechanism for pain relief.1,2,6,7,13 Additionally, gripping is the common pain-inducing activity for those with STT arthritis, yet we were unable to replicate gripping activities in our model to evaluate contact pressures during this motion. We did note that there was a considerable decrease in the average contact pressure in flexion after a 2-mm resection, but data variability did skew the average. This could be improved with a larger sample size. We also observed higher degrees of resection required to avoid impingement in radial deviation, which would have implications for patients engaged in racket sports, such as pickleball, if the elimination of contact were the goal of resection. Additionally, the small sample size (n = 8) may limit generalizability. Finally, the cadaveric specimens used had normal STT articulations. Other periarticular changes associated with arthritis may impact initial contact pressures; however, it is unlikely that this would affect contact following the first resection.

Despite this, our findings support the need for further understanding of the etiology of pain associated with STT arthritis. One possible mechanism for pain relief for STT arthritis may be STT joint denervation. This could be a less mechanically disruptive solution to pain relief for STT arthritis, as it has been for thumb CMC arthritis. Alternatively, observation of persistent contact or impingement of the ST joint intraoperatively in certain planes of motion may not require further resection. Future studies should investigate ST joint innervation and the efficacy of denervation for STT arthritis pain relief.

We sought to investigate the degree of trapezoid resection necessary to significantly decrease contact between the scaphoid and trapezoid for STT arthritis. We hypothesized that 2 mm of trapezoid excision would not be adequate to prevent impingement in all planes of motion and found that a resection of at least 4 mm was necessary to significantly decrease ST contact pressures. These findings could alter our understanding of pain relief in STT arthritis and guide further investigations for alternative methods of managing this condition.
