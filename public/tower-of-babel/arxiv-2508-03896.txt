Abstract
The accurate labeling of datasets is often both costly and time-consuming. Given an unlabeled dataset, programmatic weak supervision obtains probabilistic predictions for the labels by leveraging multiple weak labeling functions (LFs) that provide rough guesses for labels. Weak LFs commonly provide guesses with assorted types and unknown interdependences that can result in unreliable predictions. Furthermore, existing techniques for programmatic weak supervision cannot provide assessments for the reliability of the probabilistic predictions for labels. This paper presents a methodology for programmatic weak supervision that can provide confidence intervals for label probabilities and obtain more reliable predictions. In particular, the methods proposed use uncertainty sets of distributions that encapsulate the information provided by LFs with unrestricted behavior and typology. Experiments on multiple benchmark datasets show the improvement of the presented methods over the state-of-the-art and the practicality of the confidence intervals presented.

This paper presents a methodology for programmatic weak supervision that can use general
LF
to provide confidence intervals for label probabilities together with more reliable probabilistic predictions (see Fig.
1

Given the uncertainty set, the proposed methodology obtains probabilistic predictions by minimizing the worst-case expected log-loss and provides confidence intervals given by the worst-case probabilities over groups of instances. The use of uncertainty sets allows the proposed methods to assess the reliability of probabilistic predictions by considering all the distributions that are consistent with the LFs’ observed behavior.
Specifically, the main contributions of the paper are as follows.

•

We develop programmatic weak supervision methods that use an uncertainty set of distributions to encapsulate the information provided by
LF
with unrestricted behavior and typology.

•

We present methods that provide confidence intervals for the label probabilities corresponding to any group of instances. In addition, the theoretical results presented characterize the validity and optimality of such confidence intervals.

•

We develop techniques that use general
LF
to obtain probabilistic predictions that minimize worst-case expected losses. In addition, the theoretical results characterize the reliability of such predictions, which are always included in the confidence intervals.

•

We experimentally show the practicality of the confidence intervals presented and the improvement provided by the methods proposed in comparison with existing approaches.

II
Methodology for programmatic weak supervision based on uncertainty sets

This section describes the problem formulation and the proposed uncertainty sets for programmatic weak supervision.

II-A

Problem formulation

Given a dataset composed by
n
instances with unknown labels
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n}\in\mathcal{Y}~=\{1,2,\ldots,T\}
, programmatic weak supervision aims to use the outputs (guesses) of
m

LF
to obtain probabilistic predictions for the label of each instance. The array composed by the guesses of the
m

LF
at an instance is denoted by
\Lambda=[L^{1},L^{2},\ldots,L^{m}]
, where
L^{j}
denotes the guess of the
j
-th
LF
. Each array
\Lambda
is composed by the outputs of multiple
LF
that can provide guesses with assorted types such as labels (
L^{j}\in\mathcal{Y}
), abstentions (
L^{j}=\mbox{`?'}
), or probabilities
(
L^{j}=\hat{p}(1),\hat{p}(2),\ldots,\hat{p}(T)
)
(see Fig.
1
). In addition, for a specific instance
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, we denote by
\Lambda_{i}=[L_{i}^{1},L_{i}^{2},\ldots,L_{i}^{m}]
the array given by the guesses for instance
i
.

Programmatic weak supervision aims to use the LFs’ guesses
\Lambda_{i}
to obtain probabilistic predictions
\mathrm{h}_{i}
for the label
y
of each instance
i
. The probabilistic prediction for each instance
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
is denoted by
\mathrm{h}_{i}
and given by
\mathrm{h}_{i}~=~[\mathrm{h}_{i}(1),\mathrm{h}_{i}(2),\ldots,\mathrm{h}_{i}(T)]^{\top}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
, with
\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
the set of probability distributions on
\mathcal{Y}
. The reliability of each probabilistic prediction
\mathrm{h}_{i}
can be roughly assessed by the
0-1 loss

\ell(\mathrm{h}_{i},y_{i})=\mathbb{I}\{\arg\max_{y}\mathrm{h}_{i}(y)\neq y_{i}\}

or by a score tailored for probabilities, such as the log-loss

\ell(\mathrm{h}_{i},y_{i})=-\log(\mathrm{h}_{i}(y_{i}))

or the Brier-loss
[
28
]

\ell(\mathrm{h}_{i},y_{i})=(1-\mathrm{h}_{i}(y_{i}))^{2}.

Likewise, the expected loss at the
i
-th instance with respect to probability distribution
\mathrm{p}_{i}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
is given by

\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{p}_{i}}\ell(\mathrm{h}_{i},y)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\ell(\mathrm{h}_{i},y)\mathrm{p}_{i}(y).

The reliability of probabilistic predictions can be also assessed by their similarity to the actual label probabilities for any group of instances. Specifically, let
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
be a group of instances and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
be a label, the actual probability (proportion) of label
y
for instances in
\mathcal{I}
is given by

\mathrm{p}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathbb{I}\{y_{i}=y\}

while the predicted probability of label
y
for instances in
\mathcal{I}
is given by

\mathrm{h}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathrm{h}_{i}(y).

Ideally, we would like that
\mathrm{h}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)\approx\mathrm{p}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)
for any group
\mathcal{I}
and label
y
, and a significant difference between
\mathrm{h}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)
and
\mathrm{p}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)
implies that the probabilistic predictions for label
y
are unreliable in group
\mathcal{I}
.

For each group
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
and label
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, the actual probability
\mathrm{p}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)
takes an unknown value in
[0,1]
. The methods presented in the following use the information provided by the
LF
to determine a confidence interval for such probability, denoted by
[\,\underline{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)\,,\,\overline{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)\,]
. Notice that the methods that obtain confidence intervals in binary classification
[
25
,
26
]
also provide intervals corresponding to groups of instances.

II-B

Uncertainty sets that encapsulate the information of general LFs

The proposed methodology for programmatic weak supervision is based on encapsulating the information provided by general
LF
into an uncertainty set of distributions.

Uncertainty sets are given by constraints on expectations of a feature mapping
\Phi(\Lambda,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=\Big\{

\displaystyle\mathrm{p}_{1},\mathrm{p}_{2},\ldots,\mathrm{p}_{n}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}):

(1)

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{p}_{i}}\big\{\Phi(\Lambda_{i},y)\big\}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\Big|\preceq\boldsymbol{\lambda}\Big\}

where
\preceq
and
|\cdot|
denote component-wise inequalities and absolute values, vector
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes an estimate of the feature mapping’s expectation
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi(\Lambda_{i},y_{i})
, and vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\succeq\mathbf{0}
assesses the error in the expectation estimate. Similar uncertainty sets of distributions have been recently used to develop minimax approaches for supervised classification
[
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
.

The feature mapping
\Phi(\Lambda,y)
allows us to encode multiple characteristics related to the
LF
. For instance, if
L^{1},L^{2},L^{3}\in\mathcal{Y}
are the outputs provided by 3
LF
that guess labels, their fit with labels can be encoded by the feature mapping

\displaystyle\Phi(\Lambda,y)=\big[

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}\{L^{1}\neq y\},\mathbb{I}\{L^{2}\neq y\},\mathbb{I}\{L^{3}\neq y\}\big]^{\top}

(2)

for which the expectation
\boldsymbol{\tau}
corresponds to error probabilities for the different
LF
. On the other hand, if
L^{1}\in\mathcal{Y}
,
L^{2}\in\mathcal{Y}\cup\{\mbox{`?'}\}
, and
L^{3}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
are the outputs provided by 3
LF
that guess labels or label probabilities and may abstain at certain instances, their fit with labels and disagreements can be encoded by the feature mapping

\displaystyle\Phi(\Lambda,y)=\big[

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}\{L^{1}\neq y\},\mathbb{I}\{L^{2}\neq y\},\mathbb{I}\{L^{2}\neq\mbox{`?'}\},

\displaystyle(1-L^{3}(y))^{2},\mathbb{I}\{L^{1}\neq L^{2}\},1-L^{3}(L^{1})\big]^{\top}

for which the expectation
\boldsymbol{\tau}
corresponds to error and abstention probabilities together with expected scores and disagreements. In practice, the feature mapping used can be tailored to the
LF
and information available, and
Section
V
below discusses multiple choices for feature mappings components and corresponding expectation estimates.

In general, the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
in (
1
) contains all the probability distributions for labels that are consistent with the
LF
’ observed behavior, as measured by the feature mapping. The next sections show how such uncertainty sets can be used to obtain confidence intervals and probabilistic predictions.

III
Confidence intervals for label probabilities using uncertainty sets

This section shows how to obtain confidence intervals for the actual label probabilities from the information encapsulated in the uncertainty set.

Given an uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
as in (1), for any group of instances
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
and label
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, a confidence interval
[\,\underline{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)\,,\,\overline{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)\,]
for the actual label probability
\mathrm{p}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)
can be obtained using the optimum values

\displaystyle\overline{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)

\displaystyle=\underset{(\mathrm{p}_{1},\mathrm{p}_{2},\ldots,\mathrm{p}_{n})\in\mathcal{U}}{\max}\;\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathrm{p}_{i}(y)

(3)

\displaystyle\underline{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)

\displaystyle=\underset{(\mathrm{p}_{1},\mathrm{p}_{2},\ldots,\mathrm{p}_{n})\in\mathcal{U}}{\min}\;\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathrm{p}_{i}(y).

(4)

The upper
\overline{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)
and the lower
\underline{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)
bounds represent, respectively, the maximum and minimum possible values allowed by
\mathcal{U}
for the average probability of label
y
for instances in
\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\;\hskip 0.0pt

\displaystyle-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}|\boldsymbol{\mu}|

(5)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{\tilde{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\Big\{\Phi(\Lambda_{i},\tilde{y})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\frac{n}{|\mathcal{I}|}\mathbb{I}\{i\in\mathcal{I},\tilde{y}=y\}\Big\}

\displaystyle\max_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\;

\displaystyle-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}|\boldsymbol{\mu}|

(6)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min_{\tilde{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\,\Big\{\Phi(\Lambda_{i},\tilde{y})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\frac{n}{|\mathcal{I}|}\mathbb{I}\{i\in\mathcal{I},\tilde{y}=y\}\Big\}

that correspond to the Lagrange dual of optimization problems in (

The theorem above shows that each confidence interval is given by the optimum values of the optimization problems (
5
) and (
6
). These optimization problems can be efficiently addressed in practice since they are equivalent to linear optimization problems (defining new variables for the terms in the inner max and min in (
5
) and (
6
)). Furthermore, optimization problems (
5
) and (
6
) are also amenable for stochastic subgradient methods for convex optimization. In particular, the
LF
’ outputs corresponding to each instance

can provide subgradients for each term in the sums in (
5

6
) without the need to know the corresponding actual label. In addition, such optimization problems often have sparse solutions since the term
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}|\boldsymbol{\mu}|
imposes an L1-regularization that penalizes feature components with high errors in expectation estimates.

The optimal values
\overline{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)
and
\underline{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)
directly provide a valid confidence interval for label probabilities if the error in the expectation estimate is not underestimated,
i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\succeq|\boldsymbol{\tau}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}|
. In other cases,
\overline{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)
and
\underline{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)
still provide approximate confidence intervals as long as the underestimation
|\boldsymbol{\tau}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}|-\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is not substantial. Notice that the validity of the confidence intervals presented is independent of the instances’ group and label considered, and only depends on the assessment for the error in expectation estimates.

Theorem
1
shows that the best value for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is the smallest value that ensures that the confidence intervals are reliable. This would occur by setting
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=|\boldsymbol{\tau}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}|
. If
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is underestimated i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\preceq|\boldsymbol{\tau}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}|
, the error in the confidence intervals is at most
\||\boldsymbol{\tau}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}|-\boldsymbol{\lambda}\|\max(\|\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\|,\|\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\|)
as shown in equation (
7
). In practice, the value of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
can be given by the an estimate of the error (
|\boldsymbol{\tau}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}|
) such as the standard error.

The main factors that determine the width of confidence intervals are the number of instances in the group and the size of the uncertainty set. More accurate expectation estimates, additional feature components, and small values of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
result in a reduced uncertainty set. In particular, adding feature components can shrink the uncertainty set if the new constrains added are informative (non-redundant features). Such uncertainty sets result in reduced feasible sets in (
3
) and (
4
) that lead to tighter confidence intervals. In addition, for sizable groups of instances
\mathcal{I}
, the constraints in (
3
) and (
4
) more strongly affect the objective values leading to tighter confidence intervals.

III-A

Practical benefits of the confidence intervals presented

The intervals
[\,\underline{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)\,,\,\overline{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathcal{I}}(y)\,]
can serve to assess the reliability of probabilistic predictions and the adequacy of
LF
for different labels and groups of instances.

Confidence intervals for groups of instances with similar probabilistic predictions can be used to identify miscalibrated, overconfident, and underconfident predictions. For example, instances can be grouped for each label
y\in\mathcal{Y}
by using the probabilistic predictions as

\mathcal{I}_{p,{y}}=\Big\{i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}:\mathrm{h}_{i}(y)\geq p\Big\}

(9)

with
p\in[0,1]
.

This type of groups can serve to assess the reliability of probabilistic predictions. For instance, a confidence interval of
[\,0.5\,,\,0.7\,]
, for a group
\mathcal{I}_{p,y}
as in (
9
) with
p=0.9
and
y=1
, would indicate that probabilistic predictions larger than
0.9
are highly unreliable for label
1
, and that the threshold
0.9
is inadequate to regard a label
1
as correct.

Confidence intervals of groups of instances with similar
LF
’ outputs can be used to identify instances for which additional information is needed to reliable predict labels. For example, instances can be grouped by using the the proportion of
LF
that predict the label
y\in\mathcal{Y}
as

\mathcal{I}_{r,y}=\Big\{i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\colon\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}\big\{L_{i}^{j}=y\}\geq r\Big\}

(10)

with
r\in[0,1]
.

This type of groups can serve to assess the adequacy of
LF
.
For instance, a confidence interval of
[\,0.5\,,\,0.95\,]
, for a group
\mathcal{I}_{r,y}
as in (
10
) with
r=0.9
and
y=1
, would indicate that the
LF
used are inadequate to accurately predict label
1
, prompting the need to obtain additional
LF
or improve their prediction quality for label
1
.

The proposed confidence intervals have simultaneous coverage probability for all groups and labels since the validity of all intervals only depends on the assessment of errors in expectations estimates (
|\boldsymbol{\tau}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}|-\boldsymbol{\lambda}
). This capability is important in practice since it enables the usage of general groups of instances, even groups chosen using confidence intervals of other groups. The process of identifying informative groups of instances can be iterative, focusing on the examination of overlapping groups and subgroups. For instance, after obtaining a confidence interval for a group
\mathcal{I}
, we may want to examine the confidence intervals for subgroups
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{I}
or for groups similar to
\mathcal{I}
. These procedures can help identify specific issues, such as challenging instances for labeling and the need for specific
LF
for certain instances.

III-B

Optimality of confidence intervals

Theorem
1
shows that the validity of the presented confidence intervals is given by the assessment of error estimates (

IV
Minimax probabilistic predictions using uncertainty sets

This section shows how the uncertainty sets can also be
used to obtain more reliable probabilistic predictions. Given the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
in (
1
), the proposed methodology obtains probabilistic predictions
(\mathrm{h}_{1},\mathrm{h}_{2},\ldots,\mathrm{h}_{n})
by minimizing the worst-case expected log-loss over all instances. These probabilities are solutions to the minimax problem

\underset{(\mathrm{h}_{1},\mathrm{h}_{2},\ldots,\mathrm{h}_{n})}{\min}\,\,\underset{(\mathrm{p}_{1},\mathrm{p}_{2},\ldots,\mathrm{p}_{n})\in\mathcal{U}}{\max}\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{p}_{i}}\{\ell(\mathrm{h}_{i},y)\}

(23)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{p}}\{\ell(\mathrm{h},y)\}
denotes the expected log-loss of
\mathrm{h}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
with respect to
\mathrm{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{p}}\{\ell(\mathrm{h},y)\}=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\ell(\mathrm{h},y)\mathrm{p}(y)=-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\log(\mathrm{h}(y))\mathrm{p}(y).

The usage of log-loss is adequate not only for analytical convenience but also because the log-loss is a strictly proper score for probabilities
[
34
]
. In particular, the usage of such loss ensures that the probabilistic predictions are always contained in the confidence intervals provided, as shown in Theorem
4
below.

The following result shows that the minimax probabilities can be determined by solving the optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\;\;

\displaystyle-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}|\boldsymbol{\mu}|

(24)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\Big(\sum_{\tilde{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp\left\{\Phi(\Lambda_{i},\tilde{y})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}\right\}\Big).

V
Feature mapping components and expectation estimates for general
LF

Let
\Phi(\Lambda,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the feature mapping considered, its different components
\Phi^{(s)}
, for
s=1,2,\ldots,d
, allow us to encode multiple characteristics related to the
LF
. Some components can describe the fit of the guesses provided by different
LF
. In particular, if the
j
-th
LF
guesses labels, components given by

\Phi^{(s)}(L^{j},y)=\mathbb{I}\{L^{j}\neq y\}

can encode the
LF
’s error and components given by

\Phi^{(s)}(L^{j},y)=\mathbb{I}\{L^{j}\neq\mbox{`?'}\}

can encode the abstention probability. In addition, if the
j
-th
LF
guesses label probabilities, components as

\Phi^{(s)}(L^{j},y)=(1-L^{j}(y))^{2}

can encode the
LF
’s Brier score and components as

\Phi^{(s)}(L^{j},y)=-\log(L^{j}(y))

can encode the
LF
’s log score.

Other components can describe the observed interdependencies of
LF
. In particular, the disagreement between the
j
-th and
k
-th
LF
can be encoded using

\Phi^{(s)}(L^{j},L^{k})=\mathbb{I}\{L^{j}\neq L^{k}\}

for cases
where both
LF
guess labels, using

\Phi^{(s)}(L^{j},L^{k})=1-L^{k}(L^{j})

for cases where the
j
-th
LF
guesses labels and the other guesses label probabilities,
or

\Phi^{(s)}(L^{j},L^{k})=\|L^{j}-L^{k}\|

for cases
where both guess label probabilities. Furthermore, the
disagreement among more than two
LF
can be encoded similarly, as well as that with respect to an expert predictor obtained from multiple
LF
.
Specifically, if
\text{MV}(\Lambda)
denotes the
MV
(
MV
) of the
LF
’ guesses
\Lambda=L^{1},L^{2},\ldots,L^{m}
, that is

\text{MV}(\Lambda)\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}\{L^{j}=y\}

its disagreement with the
j
-th
LF
can be encoded as

\Phi^{(s)}(L^{j},\Lambda)=\mathbb{I}\{L^{j}\neq\text{MV}(\Lambda)\}.

Expectation estimates
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for feature mappings can be directly obtained using the unlabeled dataset, prior knowledge, or a reduced set of labels. Specifically, the expectations corresponding to features that do not depend on labels (e.g., abstentions probabilities and disagreements) can be computed exactly just by evaluating the
LF
in a large enough number of unlabeled instances. For example, the expectation

\tau^{(s)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi^{(s)}(L^{j}_{i},L^{k}_{i})

can be computed by evaluating
L^{j}_{i}
and
L^{k}_{i}
for
i=1,2,\ldots n
, so that
\widehat{\tau}^{(s)}=\tau^{(s)}
and
\lambda^{(s)}=0
. The estimates corresponding to features
\Phi^{(s)}(L^{j},y)
that encode
LF
’ errors or scores can be directly obtained from prior knowledge of the
LF
’ performance or by using a reduced set of labels. Specifically, the expectation

\tau^{(s)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi^{(s)}(L^{j}_{i},y_{i})

can be estimated using labeled instances in a reduced subset
\mathcal{J}
through the sample mean

\widehat{\tau}^{(s)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{J}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{J}}\Phi^{(s)}(L^{j}_{i},y_{i})

and the corresponding
\lambda^{(s)}
can be taken to be the standard error of such sample mean.

In practice, the feature components are chosen based on the
LF
’ types and the sources of information available. The next result shows that the usage of components given by disagreements also enables to encode characteristics for which we may not have explicit expectation estimates.

Theorem 5
.

The error probabilities and expected disagreement corresponding with the
j
-th and
k
-th
LF
for
j,k\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
satisfy

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{j}\neq y_{i}\}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{k}\neq y_{i}\}\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{j}\neq L_{i}^{k}\}}{n}.

Proof.

The result is obtained since the disagreement between pairs of
LF
satisfies the triangle inequality, that is for any two
LF

L^{j}
and
L^{k}
we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{j}\neq y_{i}\}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{k}\neq y_{i}\}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{j}\neq L_{i}^{k}\}.

(37)

where
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{j}\neq L_{i}^{k}\}
is the disagreement between
L^{j}
and
L^{k}
, and
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{j}\neq y_{i}\}
and
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{k}\neq y_{i}\}
are the errors of
L^{j}
and
L^{k}
, respectively. Such inequality in (
37
) is directly obtained because

\{L^{j}\neq L^{k}\}\subseteq\{L^{j}\neq y\}\cup\{L^{k}\neq y\}

since if the
j
-th and
k
-th LFs provide different outputs, at least one of them have to provide an output different to
y
.

Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{j}\neq y_{i}\}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{k}\neq y_{i}\}\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{j}\neq L_{i}^{k}\}}{n}

because inequality in (
37
) implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{j}\neq y_{i}\}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{k}\neq y_{i}\}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{j}\neq L_{i}^{k}\}

and, reversing the roles of
j
-th and
k
-th
LF
in (
37
), we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{k}\neq y_{i}\}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{j}\neq y_{i}\}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_{i}^{j}\neq L_{i}^{k}\}.

∎

The theorem above shows that estimates for expected disagreements together with estimates for error probabilities also serve to constrain other error probabilities.
Such theorem is stated in terms of pairs of
LF
but analogous results are satisfied in other cases. For instance, estimates for the error probability of the
MV
and its disagreement with individual
LF
also constrain the errors of the
LF
even if they are not explicitly estimated.

The proposed methodology can utilize assorted types of feature mappings depending on the prior knowledge avaible and the outputs of the LFs. For instance, if a set of labeled instances is available and the outputs of the LFs are labels, it is appropriate to use feature mappings that encode the error behavior of the LFs (e.g.,
\Phi^{(s)}(L^{j},y)=\mathbb{I}\{L^{j}\neq y\}
), as the expectation can be estimated from the labeled data. In contrast, if no labeled data is available and the outputs of the LFs are probabilities, it is appropriate to use feature mappings based on disagreements between pairs of LFs (e.g.,
\Phi^{(s)}(L^{j},L^{k})=\|L^{j}-L^{k}\|
), as the expectation can be computed just from the outputs of the LFs.

VII
Conclusion

Reliable probabilistic predictions provided by programmatic weak supervision can significantly improve the cost-efficiency of labeling processes. The new methodology presented in the paper can assess the reliability of probabilistic predictions in terms of confidence intervals with simultaneous coverage probability. In addition, the methods presented result in more reliable probabilistic predictions that minimize the worst-case expected loss. Using multiple benchmark datasets, the numerical results show the performance improvement with respect to the state-of-the-art, and the practicality of the presented confidence intervals for label probabilities. The novel methodology presented can lead to more reliable labeling methods that leverage general types of programmatic weak supervision and provide additional levels of uncertainty awareness.

Limitations and future work:
The confidence intervals presented require to solve two optimization problems per group of instances and label examined. However, this limitation is alleviated by the fact that such problems can be solved very efficiently. Future work could explore stochastic gradient descent-based techniques to more efficiently solve the optimization problems for a sizable number of groups. To the best of our knowledge, the techniques presented are the first to obtain confidence intervals for probabilistic predictions in programmatic weak supervision that do not require assumptions for the LFs’ behavior. The price to pay for such generality is that the confidence intervals obtained are not tight for small groups of instances or in scenarios with few LFs. Future work could explore adaptive grouping strategies that can accommodate low-data regimes and identify the need for additional LFs in certain instances.

Appendix A
Strong duality lemma

Some of the proofs of the results in the paper make use of Lagrange duality for linear optimization problems over probability distributions. The next lemma provides the duality result needed for such proofs.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a non-empty uncertainty set given by (
1
). For any function
w:\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have that

\displaystyle\max_{(\mathrm{p}_{1},\mathrm{p}_{2},\ldots,\mathrm{p}_{n})\in\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{p}_{i}}w(i,y)=\min_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}|\boldsymbol{\mu}|

(38)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\underset{y\in\mathcal{Y}}{\max}\ \big\{\Phi(\Lambda_{i},y)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}+w(i,y)\big\}.

Proof.

The optimization problem in the left-hand-side of (
38
) can be written as

\displaystyle\underset{(\mathrm{p}_{1},\mathrm{p}_{2},\ldots,\mathrm{p}_{n})\in\mathcal{U}}{\max}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{p}_{i}}w(i,y)

(39)

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccl}=&\underset{\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n}}{\max}&\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{+}(\mathbf{p_{i}})\\
&\mbox{s.t.}&\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}\preceq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\top}\Phi(\Lambda_{i},\cdot)\preceq\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}\end{array}

where
\mathbf{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|}
and
\mathbf{w}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
denote the vectors given by the probability distributions
\{\mathrm{p}_{i}\}
and functions
\{w(i,\cdot)\}
, and function
I_{+}
is given by

I_{+}(\mathbf{p})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\mbox{if }\mathbf{p}\succeq\mathbf{0}\mbox{ and }\mathbf{p}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=1\\
\infty&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Optimization problem (
39
) has Lagrange dual

\begin{array}[]{ccl}&\underset{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}}{\min}&-\big(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}\big)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}+\big(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}\big)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}\\
&&+f^{*}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{1}\frac{(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2})}{n},\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{2}\frac{(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2})}{n},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{n}\frac{(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2})}{n})\\
&\mbox{s.t.}&\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}\succeq\mathbf{0}\end{array}

where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{i}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
denotes the matrix with rows
\Phi(\Lambda_{i},y)^{\top}
for
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, and
f^{*}
is the conjugate function of

f(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n})=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{+}(\mathbf{p}_{i})

for
\mathbf{w}_{i}=[w(i,1),w(i,1),\ldots,w(i,T)]^{\top}
. Hence, we have that

\displaystyle f^{*}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{n})=

\displaystyle\sup_{(\mathrm{p}_{1},\mathrm{p}_{2},\ldots,\mathrm{p}_{n})\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{i}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\|\mathbf{u}_{i}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{w}_{i}\big\|_{\infty}.

Therefore, the Lagrange dual above becomes

\begin{array}[]{ccl}&\underset{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}}{\min}&-\big(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}\big)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}+\big(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}\big)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}\\
&&+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\underset{y\in\mathcal{Y}}{\max}\{\Phi(\Lambda_{i},y)^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2})+w(i,y)\}\\
&\mbox{s.t.}&\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}\succeq\mathbf{0}.\end{array}

It is easy to see that the solution of such optimization problem
\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1},\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{2}
satisfies that
\bar{\mu}_{1}^{(i)}\bar{\mu}_{2}^{(i)}=0
for any
i
such that
\lambda^{(i)}>0
. Then
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{2})=\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}|\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{2}|
and taking
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}
the Lagrange dual above is equivalent to the right-hand-side of (
38
).

Then, the result is obtained because strong duality is satisfied since the constraints in (
39
) are affine and
\mathcal{U}
is non-empty.
