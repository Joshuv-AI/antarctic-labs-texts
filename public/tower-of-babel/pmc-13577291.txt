Participation in Continuing Professional Development Among Radiographers in Lilongwe District, Malawi
Authors: Peter Bajinyumwe Shaba, Mbuyiselwa Radebe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Continuing Professional Development (CPD) enables healthcare workers to continuously update their medical knowledge, skills, and clinical practices, ultimately enhancing patient care, improving health outcomes, and maintaining professional competence. However, a significant gap remains in the literature regarding the specific challenges that affect radiographers’ engagement in CPD from the perspectives of key stakeholders.Purpose: This study explored challenges to CPD participation in radiography from the triadic perspectives of radiographers, professional body, and employers.Methods: Utilizing a qualitative, multiple-case design in Lilongwe District, Malawi, a multi-stage purposive sampling matrix recruited participants across primary, secondary, and tertiary healthcare tiers. Verbatim data were subjected to thematic analysis and systematic horizontal and vertical cross-case comparisons via a master synthesis matrix.Results: Cross-case synthesis demonstrated that radiography CPD participation is heavily modulated by institutional healthcare tier rather than individual motivation. Disparate codes consolidated into a multi-level framework revealing critical vertical misalignments between macro-level regulatory standards, meso-level institutional resource constraints, and micro-level clinical workloads.Conclusion: Low radiography CPD engagement is a structural consequence of vertical misalignments across macro, meso, and micro strata. These findings establish a contextualized diagnostic baseline to inform future targeted clinical interventions and broader multi-district investigations.

Introduction

Continuing Professional Development (CPD), also referred to as Continuing Medical Education (CME), is a proactive, structured approach that healthcare practitioners utilise to enhance their skills, knowledge, and expertise beyond their initial training [1,2]. It serves as a cornerstone for career progression, enabling health professionals to consistently deliver high-quality patient care [3,4]. Ultimately, CPD ensures the continuous advancement of competencies and aptitudes throughout a clinician’s career, allowing professionals to maintain and elevate their clinical practice [5,6].

Employers and professional bodies serve as the twin institutional pillars governing and enabling radiographer participation in Continuing Professional Development (CPD) [5]. In the context of health workforce development in Malawi, employers control the operational environment by managing budgets, staff rosters, and educational leave, which directly determines a radiographer’s practical access to training opportunities. Conversely, professional associations such as the Association of Radiographers in serve as the academic and professional custodians of the discipline. This body provides training activities that enable radiographers to accumulate the annual CPD credits required for practicing licence renewal with the regulatory body, the Medical Council of Malawi (MCM). Consequently, while professional bodies establish the guidelines and mandates for lifelong learning among their members, employers must provide the vital infrastructure and resource allocations required to translate these professional demands into clinical reality [1,2]. Continuing Professional Development helps radiographers adapt to changing healthcare landscape, update skills in emerging modalities and comply with regulatory requirements thereby demonstrating professionalism, enhanced career prospects and delivery of high quality, evidence based imaging services [3,4]. Employers value CPD as beneficial to organisations as it encourages employees to engage in lifelong learning and proactiveness in offering appropriate services [5]. Professional bodies consider CPD engagement as an important tool in monitoring that registered members maintain competence through reflection, review and documentation of learning as a means of keeping their licensure [6–9].

Several studies conducted across the broader African region have revealed that radiographers face numerous challenges when participating in CPD activities [4,6]. While literature reviews have identified generic challenges [4] and barriers hindering participation in other countries [10,11], a distinct localised gap remains. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no qualitative study has investigated the challenges to radiographers’ engagement in CPD activities from the simultaneous perspectives of radiographers, employers, and the professional body in Malawi. Such information could inform stakeholders of the precise challenges experienced within the profession, ultimately guiding the development of targeted strategies to alleviate these barriers. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the challenges to CPD engagement in radiography from the perspective of radiographers, their professional body, and employers in Malawi.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This investigation adopted a qualitative, multiple-case embedded design to examine the systemic, environmental, and structural factors influencing Continuing Professional Development (CPD) participation among radiographers [12]. A case study framework is uniquely suited for this research as it facilitates an in-depth, contextual exploration of a contemporary phenomenon within its real-world environment, particularly where the boundaries between the phenomenon and context are highly integrated [13–15]. To operationalise this design into a rigorous bounded system, the research established a distinct case-level architecture rather than treating the data as a unified descriptive pool [8,16]. The overarching case boundaries were defined as the professional radiography ecosystem within the public healthcare network of the Lilongwe District in Malawi, bounded both geographically and institutionally. Within this macro-system, three distinct embedded cases were explicitly constituted based on the architectural tier of healthcare delivery to examine how varying institutional infrastructures modulate CPD compliance.

Case one comprised the tertiary hospital setting, representing the macro-level institutional environment; case two comprised the secondary hospital setting, representing the meso-level institutional environment; and case three evaluated the primary hospital setting to capture the micro-level institutional context. Each tier operated under vastly different resource allocations, patient volumes, and staffing quotas [12,17,18]. Additionally, the professional body perspective, captured via total population sampling of the chairperson of the radiographers’ professional body, functioned as an external analytical anchor to cross-examine localised institutional findings against national policies [19]. By framing the different hospital tiers as individual embedded cases, the researcher could look past individual human behaviours and explicitly examine the systemic, administrative, and structural environments that restrict or facilitate professional development. Data collection was carried out through individual, face-to-face, semi-structured interviews.

Study Setting

The study was conducted within the Lilongwe District, the capital city region of Malawi, a landlocked country in Southeast Africa [14]. Lilongwe contains 15 hospitals with radiology departments that are structurally categorised into tertiary, secondary, and primary levels of care [1,5]. The association of radiographers in Malawi serves as the sole professional body for radiographers in the country, overseeing radiography practice and regulatory compliance with CPD registration for its members. This study was executed across three distinct tiers of the healthcare delivery system, stratifying participants into primary, secondary, and tertiary care settings. To ensure rigorous case construction and clear boundaries, a multi-stage purposive sampling matrix was implemented, strictly excluding facilities outside the Lilongwe District and non-radiography professional bodies.

Participants

Three distinct participant cohorts specifically, radiographers, institutional employers, and a professional body representative across primary, secondary, and tertiary hospital tiers were eligible to participate in the study. Conversely, healthcare workers from other professional associations or facilities located outside the Lilongwe District were excluded. A multi-stage purposive sampling strategy dictated the selection of both facilities and participants [15]. Initially, a maximum variation sampling protocol was implemented to select one tertiary, one secondary, and one primary healthcare facility within the Lilongwe District, thereby establishing clear institutional case boundaries. Subsequently, homogeneous sampling was utilised within these designated strata to recruit a tightly defined cohort of radiographers and hospital employers. Concurrently, total population sampling was executed to recruit the singular representative from the radiographers’ professional body.

The final purposive sample comprised 15 radiographers, three employers, and one professional association representative. All participants possessed a minimum institutional tenure of one year to ensure robust, context-specific institutional memory. Recruitment and data collection followed a structured, cohort-specific approach. The radiographer cohort was contacted via electronic mail containing a formal study outline and an information letter, after which the researcher scheduled individual interviews. Similarly, the researcher directly approached and coordinated interviews with the employer cohort. For the professional body, institutional permission was first secured from the radiographers’ association before the designated representative was approached, provided with a comprehensive information letter, and engaged in a formal research meeting.

Data Collection

Individual, face-to-face, semi-structured interviews were conducted in English with all participants. Data collection was facilitated through cohort-specific interview guides, with a dedicated instrument developed for each of the three stakeholder groups to ensure contextual relevance informed by existing literature [16,20,21]. This multi-perspectival approach allowed the researcher to investigate CPD participation from divergent operational standpoints. The sessions were audio-recorded using a digital voice recorder, with each interview lasting 30 to 60 minutes in a private setting. These in-depth interviews with radiographers, employers, and the professional body representative were conducted until data saturation was achieved, meaning that interviews within the cases yielded repetitive structural patterns and no new codes emerged.

To ensure trustworthiness, strategies promoting credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability were rigorously employed. Credibility was established through prolonged engagement, persistent observation, and member-checking, wherein preliminary findings were shared with participants at the end of the study to validate the data. Dependability was maintained by establishing a clear audit trail through a systematic and transparent report of the data collection and analysis procedures. Confirmability was ensured through the transparent documentation of the research process, the maintenance of a reflexive approach to limit researcher bias, and by anchoring all findings directly to verbatim participant transcripts [16,22]. Finally, transferability was enhanced through a detailed discussion of the study boundaries, institutional restrictions, participant demographics, and the length and frequency of data collection sessions, enabling future researchers to assess the applicability of these findings to similar sub-Saharan contexts.

Data Analysis

Qualitative data were analysed thematically by integrating Tesch’s (1990) and Saldaña’s (2016) frameworks, transitioning from an independent within-case analysis to a structured cross-case synthesis [22–24]. During the within-case phase, radiographer transcripts were segregated strictly by institutional case boundaries to recursively analyse text, observations, and field notes for initial patterns (Tesch, 1990). Transcripts across the three tiers were coded separately using Saldaña’s (2016) first- and second-cycle coding methods specifically descriptive, in vivo, pattern, and axial coding thereby preserving institutional identity and mapping the unique thematic boundaries of each environment [21,25]. Concurrently, the professional body representative’s transcript was treated as a standalone, macro-level stakeholder source and coded independently to prevent regulatory perspectives from overshadowing ground-level data.

This independent coding process was replicated chronologically across the tertiary, secondary, and primary hospital cases, ensuring each tier’s unique context was preserved on its own terms prior to comparison [21]. Rather than establishing a shared coding framework a priori, an inductive framework emerged directly from the raw text across stakeholder groups. During the cross-case synthesis, a comparative thematic matrix served as a shared analytical tool to systematically cross-examine codes across cases and stakeholders. This matrix categorised findings into convergent themes (systemic CPD barriers experienced uniformly across the Lilongwe District) and divergent themes (tier-specific barriers varying by institutional size, geography, and resource allocation).

Themes and sub-themes were recursively refined and finalised using Tesch’s (1990) framework. At this stage, professional body data were integrated into the matrix as a macro-regulatory structural mirror to contrast national policy expectations against localised institutional realities [21,26,27]. To finalise the themes, a constant comparative analysis was performed by moving recursively between the shared matrix and raw transcripts to ensure interpretive accuracy. Results

To systematically elucidate the multi-layered dynamics governing Continuing Professional Development (CPD) engagement, cross-case comparisons were executed using qualitative synthesis across three distinct stakeholder strata [28]. Following the initial within-case thematic analysis of individual transcripts, a horizontal and vertical comparative analysis was performed via a master cross-case framework to map data points within and across the participant cohorts: radiographers (n = 15), institutional employers (n = 3), and the professional body representative (n = 1). This comparative trajectory prioritised data triangulation to evaluate thematic convergence and divergence regarding the challenges, motivations, and institutional mechanisms driving CPD involvement [29]. The cross-case thematic synthesis revealed that CPD participation among radiographers in the Lilongwe District is heavily modulated by their institutional healthcare tier, rather than being a singular product of individual motivation. Through this iterative process of constant comparison, disparate codes were consolidated into robust, overarching categories, thereby validating the structural configuration of the finalised three-tiered analytical matrix presented in Table 1.

Theme 1: Personal and Professional Challenges (Micro-Level)

The individual radiographer operates at the front lines of clinical care, where immediate personal and professional impediments directly restrict active participation in Continuing Professional Development (CPD). Time constraints and clinical workload pressures represent a primary obstacle, characterised by an acute deficit of available hours during the working day to allocate to educational advancement. This sub-theme was derived from participant descriptions of high patient volumes, understaffed shifts, and a relentless operational pace across all hospital tiers. Radiographers reported that their entire shift is consumed by routine diagnostic imaging tasks, leaving no opportunity for protected study or asynchronous learning. A strong bidirectional relationship exists between this micro-level challenge and the meso-level organisational environment, as institutional staff shortages and competing operational priorities directly cause the intense workload pressures that suppress individual CPD participation. … we are already few, so when someone goes out to attend learning activities it means large workload for the remaining person … (Radiographer 5, 30–39 years, male)

… we are already few, so when someone goes out to attend learning activities it means large workload for the remaining person … (Radiographer 5, 30–39 years, male)

Deficiencies in personal motivation and professional interest function as an internal psychological barrier, characterised by apathy or diminished enthusiasm towards pursuing professional development opportunities. The data revealed that many radiographers view CPD as a bureaucratic box-checking exercise rather than a meaningful path to clinical excellence, which severely limits voluntary engagement. This lack of drive was inductively tied to chronic burnout and a perceived absence of professional progression. Temporally and causally, this deficiency does not exist in a vacuum; it is directly exacerbated by the meso-level deficiency in managerial support and professional incentives. When institutions fail to recognise or reward educational achievements, radiographers lose the intrinsic professional interest required to actively seek out and participate in optional training. … there is no motivation, if there were some kinds of reward for radiographers when participating in CPD activities, people should have been competing, in such a way a lot of radiographers would be participating in CPD activities … (Radiographer 6, 30–39 years, male) … sometimes you feel you have to attended to CPD activity, but then considering that the previous one was not up to expectation, so most likely I will not go to attend the current one … (Radiographer 9, 30–39 years, male)

… there is no motivation, if there were some kinds of reward for radiographers when participating in CPD activities, people should have been competing, in such a way a lot of radiographers would be participating in CPD activities … (Radiographer 6, 30–39 years, male)

… sometimes you feel you have to attended to CPD activity, but then considering that the previous one was not up to expectation, so most likely I will not go to attend the current one … (Radiographer 9, 30–39 years, male)

Furthermore, individual radiographers frequently contend with insufficient foundational knowledge and technical skills, manifesting as gaps in primary training and an inability to operate specialised, modern imaging modalities without direct guidance. The data derived this sub-theme from accounts where radiographers felt ill-prepared to handle advanced software, digital modalities, or specialised equipment like computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanners. This micro-level deficit discourages active participation in advanced clinical workshops, as participants feel cognitively overwhelmed by the material. This links directly to the macro-level regulatory body and systemic institutional support deficits, where the overarching education and regulatory systems fail to provide standardised bridging courses, thereby leaving individual radiographers ill-equipped to participate in higher-level CPD. … sometimes as you practice you find things that you did not learn in school, however, to find information and learn it’s a challenge … (Radiographer 1, 20–29 years, male)

… sometimes as you practice you find things that you did not learn in school, however, to find information and learn it’s a challenge … (Radiographer 1, 20–29 years, male)

Finally, personal financial constraints and economic burdens place a substantial strain on the individual’s private finances, introducing severe affordability barriers to CPD participation. This sub-theme emerged from data detailing out-of-pocket expenses required for course registration fees, learning materials, internet data bundles, and travel to training venues. This micro-level economic burden acts as a direct deterrent to regular engagement and is heavily reinforced by both meso- and macro-level factors, stemming from inadequate institutional funding for employee education and the professional body’s failure to offer subsidised, cost-free compliance pathways. … I encountered financial limitations, for example if there are some research disseminations which are paid CPD activities, I most times find that I cannot manage financially to sponsor myself, I don’t attend … (Radiographer 9, 30–39 years, male)

… I encountered financial limitations, for example if there are some research disseminations which are paid CPD activities, I most times find that I cannot manage financially to sponsor myself, I don’t attend … (Radiographer 9, 30–39 years, male)

Theme 2: Organisational and Institutional Challenges (Meso-Level)

At the meso-level, structural, administrative, and cultural dynamics within the employing healthcare facilities shape the immediate environment, dictating how resources and administrative protocols either facilitate or hinder staff participation in CPD. Inadequate facility resources and infrastructure deficits compromise both daily clinical operations and educational access. This sub-theme was derived from data detailing frequent power outages, equipment downtime, unreliable hospital internet connectivity, and a lack of dedicated physical spaces for learning. This institutional deficit directly undermines participation by cutting off the digital infrastructure needed for online courses and seminars. Furthermore, it links to macro-level financial constraints, demonstrating how national budget deficits trickle down to cripple local learning environments. … we are not a cost centre, as such whenever there are CPD activities that require finding we are unable to support officers, in addition we also don’t have dedicated rooms with good internet connection where clinical staff can conduct their CPDs’.’ (Employer 1, 30–39 years)

… we are not a cost centre, as such whenever there are CPD activities that require finding we are unable to support officers, in addition we also don’t have dedicated rooms with good internet connection where clinical staff can conduct their CPDs’.’ (Employer 1, 30–39 years)

This problem is compounded by a deficiency in managerial support and professional incentives, as hospital leadership and departmental managers regularly fail to encourage, facilitate, or reward CPD participation. The data derived this sub-theme from administrative actions, such as denying study leave requests, and the total absence of career promotion or financial rewards linked to educational compliance. This meso-level failure reinforces the micro-level deficit in personal motivation: when management treats CPD participation as an inconvenient disruption to the daily roster rather than an institutional asset, radiographers respond by withdrawing their professional interest and scaling back their involvement. … As a hospital we are supposed to reward materially those who engage in CPD, but due to insufficient funds we mostly give verbal appraisal which does not necessarily motivate those who do not engage in teaching others …. (Employer 1, 30–39 years, male)

… As a hospital we are supposed to reward materially those who engage in CPD, but due to insufficient funds we mostly give verbal appraisal which does not necessarily motivate those who do not engage in teaching others …. (Employer 1, 30–39 years, male)

Inefficient internal CPD policies and institutional procedures further hinder professional development because employing facilities lack clear, fair, and structured mechanisms to regulate and support staff education. This sub-theme emerged from accounts of opaque, inequitable selection processes for training opportunities, disorganised internal rosters that fail to allocate protected learning hours, and a total absence of localised tracking systems. This procedural inefficiency prevents equal participation, as access to professional development often depends on subjective managerial approval rather than standardised merit. This is heavily tied to the macro-level deficiency in compliance monitoring, where the lack of regulatory auditing permits highly disorganised, ad-hoc internal systems at the hospital level. Suboptimal stakeholder collaboration at the facility level undermines collective learning and interprofessional participation through fragmented communication and a lack of teamwork between radiographers and other health professionals. The data derived this sub-theme from reports of isolated departmental silos and a failure to share knowledge. This localised fragmentation mirrors the macro-level multi-stakeholder disconnect, proving that the lack of unified national engagement actively repeats itself at the institutional level, preventing radiographers from participating in cross-functional clinical learning communities. … Most times radiology department members do not attend morning reports where we do have presentations, they avoid making presentations … (Employer 2, 30–39 years, male)

These structural issues cultivate a counterproductive workplace culture and competing operational priorities, where the dominant institutional ethos prioritises rapid patient throughput and immediate clinical outputs over long-term educational investments. This sub-theme was derived from accounts where radiographers were actively discouraged from participating in clinical updates because it would leave an imaging room uncovered. This creates a cultural dynamic where pursuing professional growth is stigmatised as abandoning one’s immediate operational duties, directly fuelling the micro-level time constraints that suppress radiographers’ participation. … we have inadequate members of radiographers, so when we receive invitation for them to go for training as management we think about the patients first and at times, we don’t allow them to attend … (Employer 1, 30–39 years, male)

Deficiencies in CPD policy implementation and compliance monitoring further weaken the system, as the professional body lacks the administrative capacity or infrastructure to consistently roll out educational policies and track participation. The data derived this sub-theme from reports of lengthy delays in points updates, confusing guidelines, and an unpredictable auditing process. This macro-level failure directly accommodates meso-level inefficient institutional procedures; without clear, consistent monitoring from the state regulator, local hospital administrations face no pressure to standardise or support internal training pathways. within criterion for having radiographer registered with the association, we are not regarding CPD as a requirement … we don’t have clear CPD guidelines or clear penalties that are put in black and white that if a member doesn’t comply to CPD requirements, these are the consequences. In summary, the empirical findings demonstrate that radiographers’ participation in continuing professional development (CPD) is governed by an interdependent, multi-layered ecosystem of barriers. An analysis of these challenges reveals that low participation rates are not merely a function of individual omission, but rather the direct consequence of a fragmented framework that fails to reconcile statutory mandates with localised clinical realities.

Discussion

This study explored the complex challenges to continuing professional development (CPD) participation and engagement within Malawian radiography practice by evaluating and integrating the triadic perspectives of radiographers, institutional employers, and the professional body. Rather than revealing isolated operational friction, the synthesised findings demonstrate a highly interdependent, multi-layered ecosystem of challenges that directly suppress professional participation. This multi-stakeholder assessment underscores the urgent need for a unified, coordinated approach to reverse declining CPD participation rates and mitigate the systemic challenges that stifle sustainable learning cultures within low-resource healthcare structures [19,30,31].

A critical point of convergence across all three stakeholder groups is the collective acknowledgement that acute financial and accessibility challenges severely restrict individual capability to participate in CPD initiatives [2,4,30]. Radiographers, employers, and the professional body representative uniformly identified geographical isolation, limited digital infrastructure, and a lack of centralised educational funds as pervasive structural challenges that flatten participation opportunities [4,32]. Furthermore, all groups agreed that communication and inter-organisational coordination between the regulator, the professional body, and academic institutions are deeply compromised. This lack of stakeholder collaboration serves as a profound structural challenge that undermines the development and implementation of accessible CPD pathways, resulting in a fragmented educational landscape characterised by poorly synchronised and underfunded CPD activities that discourage professional attendance [14,30,33–35].

Despite shared agreement on overarching resource deficits, significant divergence emerged regarding the perceived root causes of low CPD participation. Individual radiographers framed participation challenges primarily through the lens of survival and immediate operational exhaustion, emphasising that extreme clinical workload pressures, severe staffing shortages, and unmitigated out-of-pocket economic burdens naturally force them to prioritise daily work-life balance and acute patient throughput over educational involvement [4,6,8,33,35–37]. In stark contrast, institutional employers demonstrated an attribution bias, frequently associating low radiographer participation with individual attitudinal challenges, such as deficiencies in intrinsic motivation and professional interest [38]. This localised micro-meso conflict is further complicated by the macro-level professional body, which faces its own structural challenges. The professional body representative focused heavily on macro-regulatory policy implementation challenges, highlighting that the absence of a guiding national framework makes it impossible to deliver clear participation guidance and monitor compliance for its membership a dilemma heavily exacerbated by severe macro-level financial constraints and a lack of partner support to sustain active participation [7,8,19,26,27,37,39].

The operational implications of these diverging stakeholder perspectives are profound and directly manifest as a paralysing cycle of institutional complacency that gridlocks CPD participation. Because employers perceive low participation as a personal attitudinal failure rather than a symptom of systemic challenges, healthcare facilities regularly fail to prioritise CPD support, allocate dedicated learning resources, or foster an enabling workplace culture that accommodates continuous learning needs [4–6,11,33]. Simultaneously, this organisational gridlock is exacerbated by counterproductive regulatory flexibility. While regulatory protocols mandate a strict point-accumulation system for annual professional licence renewal, administrative gaps permit non-compliant radiographers to register successfully without achieving the minimum participation criteria. This lack of regulatory enforcement and accountability completely undermines the imperative for active participation, permitting a culture of complacency and signalling to both employers and radiographers that professional development challenges can be ignored since the activities are treated as optional bureaucratic formalities rather than clinical necessities [1,4].

While existing sub-Saharan literature thoroughly documents isolated instances of health workforce underfunding and resource constraints [30,32,35], this study contributes novel empirical knowledge by uncovering the precise mechanism of vertical system failure that shuts down participation within low-resource radiography tiers. This research demonstrates that depressed participation and individual radiographer apathy are manufactured consequences of structural alignment challenges rather than an organic disposition. Specifically, it models how a lack of funding and regulatory inconsistency at the macro-level directly permit chaotic, ad-hoc scheduling at the meso-level, which inevitably collapses into personal financial and time scarcity challenges at the micro-level. By detailing how regulatory institutions subvert their own standards to maintain licencing numbers, this study moves beyond generic calls for more funding to prove that transforming participation rates requires a multi-tiered, coordinated overhaul. Secure partnerships standardised local tracking networks, targeted motivational strategies, and strict regulatory accountability must be implemented simultaneously to successfully transform CPD from a punitive, unworkable bureaucratic mandate into an accessible and sustainable vehicle for clinical excellence, active professional participation, and workforce retention.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides localised qualitative insights into the multifaceted challenges surrounding Continuing Professional Development (CPD) participation in radiography by triangulating the perspectives of radiographers, institutional employers, and a professional body representative within the Lilongwe District. The findings reveal that individual radiographers face immediate, micro-level participation challenges, including clinical time scarcity, out-of-pocket economic burdens, and low motivation. Conversely, the institutional employers interviewed are constrained by meso-level operational challenges, such as severe staffing shortages and high patient volumes, which restrict their capacity to allocate resources or provide protected leave for professional learning. At the macro-level, the professional body representative highlighted systemic challenges rooted in chronic underfunding and regulatory policy gaps that undermine consistent CPD implementation.

Given the study’s qualitative design and localised geographic scope, the findings reflect contextualised, triadic perceptions rather than a comprehensive national consensus. Nevertheless, the vertical structural misalignments identified suggest that future interventions must move away from viewing low participation as an isolated individual failure, focusing instead on reconciling macro-level regulatory licencing standards with micro-level clinical workloads. To build upon these insights, future research utilising broader multi-district or mixed-methods designs is needed to evaluate CPD challenges across a wider, comprehensive representation of professional body, regulatory, academic, and healthcare administration stakeholders.

Strengths and Limitations

A primary methodological strength of this investigation lies in the comprehensive triangulation of data achieved by recruiting participants across three stakeholder strata and from all three tiers of the healthcare delivery system, which significantly enhances data credibility. However, some limitations must be acknowledged.

First, restricting the geographical scope to a single district, the Lilongwe District inherently limits the immediate transferability of the findings. The documented systemic, organisational, and individual dynamics are deeply bound to the specific institutional and socioeconomic profiles of Lilongwe’s healthcare facilities and may not fully reflect Continuing Professional Development (CPD) participation realities across Malawi.

Second, stakeholder representation relied on a varied cohort that indexed more heavily on radiographers than on institutional employers and regulatory personnel. Although this was a methodologically intentional choice aimed at grounding the analysis in clinical realities, the smaller sample size of management and professional body voices introduces a potential interpretive risk during thematic synthesis. Specifically, the dominant volume of radiographer data may have inadvertently skewed the interpretative weight towards clinical grievances, potentially under-representing the administrative constraints faced by employers.

Finally, a notable limitation is the reliance on a single professional body participant to capture the macro-level perspective. Bounding the regulatory voice to one representative carries distinct operational implication, as their views may not fully encapsulate all internal perspectives within the professional body. This constraint may affect the interpretation of systemic findings by presenting the macro-regulatory landscape as a monolithic viewpoint rather than a complex, multi-faceted regulatory institution, thereby limiting the study’s capacity to dissect internal macro-level friction.

To mitigate these combined limitations, the research design leveraged horizontal and vertical cross-case triangulation, evaluating discrepancies between strata not as analytical errors but as vital qualitative indicators that exposed the exact operational rifts between regulatory expectations and frontline clinical survival. Within these parameters, this study provides an essential diagnostic baseline to be expanded upon by future multi-district investigations.
