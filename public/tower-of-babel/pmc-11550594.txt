Elucidating the hysteresis effect in printed flexible perovskite solar cells with SnO2 quantum dot- and PCBM-based electron transport layers
Authors: Yerassyl Yerlanuly, Erik O Shalenov, Hryhorii P Parkhomenko, Muhammad Salman Kiani, Zarina Kukhayeva, Annie Ng, Askhat N Jumabekov
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecently, flexible perovskite solar cells (FPSCs) fabricated using solution-processed printing techniques have garnered significant attention. However, challenges remain in achieving cost-effective, scalable manufacturing under ambient conditions and ensuring stable, efficient devices. This study focuses on fabricating printed FPSCs using the slot-die coating technique and examines the impact of SnO2 quantum dot (QD) and (6,6)-Phenyl C61 butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) based electron transport layers (ETLs) on device performance and hysteresis. Experimentally results show that SnO2 QD-based devices exhibited favorable photovoltaic properties but significant hysteresis compared to PCBM-based devices. Numerical simulations have shown that the hysteresis effect in devices is influenced not only by the higher concentration of mobile ions in the perovskite layer of PCBM-based devices compared to SnO2 QD-based devices, but also by the more effective redistribution of these ions during forward and reverse J-V scans. The results provide insights into the behavior of printed FPSCs with different ETLs, contributing to the development of high-performance, hysteresis-free printed FPSCs.Keywords: Slot-die coating, Flexible and printed perovskite solar cells, Electron transport layer, Hysteresis index

Introduction

One of the key advantages of perovskites over classic inorganic semiconductor materials is their capability for low-temperature solution processing and the use of various deposition techniques, enabling the printing of perovskite thin films on a wide range of rigid and flexible substrates [1,2]. This promises production of low-cost printed flexible perovskite solar cells (FPSCs) in large-scale [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. There has been a significant advancement in developing various approaches for mass production of printed FPSCs. This mainly involves sheet-to-sheet (S2S) and/or roll-to-roll (R2R) manufacturing of devices using screen, gravure, relief, and inkjet printing as well as spray-coating, blade-coating, and slot-die coating techniques [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. Among them, the slot-die coating technique is one of the attractive ones as it provides a unique combination of minimum material waste, high-degree control over film thickness, enclosed reservoir for ink, and both S2S and R2R processing for high-throughput manufacturing [13,14]. To date, a maximum power conversion efficiency (PCE) of around 21 % has been achieved with inkjet printing [15] and 22.3 % slot-die coating techniques [16].

Perovskites are known to exhibit ion migration phenomena [[17], [18], [19]]. This is related to immobilization and diffusion of positively and negatively charged ions in the volume of perovskites [20]. In perovskite solar cells (PSCs), this leads to pile-up of mobile ions at the perovskite/charge transporting layer (CTL) interfaces. This results in hysteresis behavior of current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics of devices [17,19,[21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]]. While this process is inherent to many perovskite materials, it poses a potential barrier for efficient and stable operation of PSCs. The accumulated ions at the perovskite/CTL interfaces accelerate emission-free recombination of charge carriers, which also leads to rapid degradation of PSCs [28]. Achieving a better understanding of this process, its impact on the performance and stability of PSCs, and finding the ways to control it is of key importance for various applications of this technology [27,[29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37]].

There are several reports that explain the hysteresis effect in PSCs based on slow drift and diffusion of ionic defects at room temperature [38,39]. Also, the hysteresis effect in PSCs strongly depends on the perovskite/CTL interface properties and choice of CTL material [[40], [41]]. For instance, in the work by Hou et al. [42] the use of (6,6)-Phenyl C61 butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) as ETL was shown to reduce hysteresis effect in PSCs. Xu et al. [43] has shown that suppression of ion migration in PCBM-based PSCs is due to low density of interfacial defects at the ETL/perovskite interface. In SnO2-based PSCs, on the other hand, low hysteresis effect is attributed to more efficient charge extraction at the ETL/perovskite interface [44]. Additionally, report by Tumen-Ulzii et al. [30] suggests that the presence of hydroxyl (-OH) groups on the surface of SnO2-based ETLs is one possible source of ionic localization in PSCs. Overall, these studies indicate that the mechanisms governing the hysteresis effect in PSCs can be rather specific, depending on the choice of ETL material. Thus, unravelling the relation of hysteresis effect in PSCs to the nature and properties of ETLs is of significant importance both from the device physics and manufacturing perspectives. Thus, this paper focuses on the experimental and theoretical analysis of the hysteresis effect in printed FPSCs, utilizing SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based ETLs. The obtained results provide valuable insights into the origin of the hysteresis effect in these devices.

Experimental part

Materials

All the chemicals and reagents were used as received without any further purification. Flexible polyethylene terephthalate (PET, thickness 125 μm) substrates with a layer of patterned transparent conducting indium-tin-oxide (ITO) (thickness 150 nm, sheet resistance – 45 Ω □−1) were purchased from Mekoprint. Methylammonium iodide (MAI, 99.995 %) was purchased from GreatCell Solar. Lead iodide (PbI2, 99 %), bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (Li TFSI, 99 %), 4-tert-butylpyridine (4-tBP, 98 %), methylamine solution (MA, in absolute ethanol 33 wt%), acetonitrile (ACN, 99.8 %), chlorobenzene (CB, 99.8 %), acetone (99.9 %), and 2-propanol (IPA, 99.5 %) were purchased from Merck [6,6].-phenyl C61 butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM, >99.5 %) and 2,2′,7,7′-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamino)-9,9′-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD, 99.5 %) were purchased from Lumtec. Gold and silver pellets (Au (99.99 %) and Ag (99.99 %)) were purchased from Kurt J. Lesker.

Device fabrication

Fabrication of n-i-p structured printed FPSCs

The PET/ITO substrates were cleaned by sequential 10-min sonication in a detergent-water solution, deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol. After drying the substrates with a stream of compressed air, they underwent ultraviolet (UV)-ozone treatment for 15 min. The cleaned PET/ITO substrates were attached to the drum of a slot-die coater (NanoRoll coater, FOM Technologies, Denmark) with a set temperature of 25 °C (Fig. S1a in Supporting Information). The functional layers of printed FPSCs were coated using a slot-die head with a 19 mm wide meniscus guide.

Two different ETL inks were used in the experiments. For the first set of devices, a water-based SnO2 QD ink was slot-die coated on clean PET/ITO substrates at a pump rate of 0.25 mL min−1 and a coating speed of 0.8 m min−1. This was followed by a 30-min annealing of the substrates at 130 °C. The method for the synthesis of SnO2 QDs and the main steps for ink preparation with them are described in our previous work [45]. For the second set of devices, a chlorobenzene-based PCBM ink (1 mg mL−1) was slot-die coated on clean PET/ITO substrates at a pump rate of 0.1 mL min−1 and a coating speed of 1 m min−1. Then, the substrates were annealed for 10 min at 100 °C. In the next step, a perovskite (MAPbI3) ink, which was prepared by dissolving 1 mol of MAI (159 mg) and 1 mol of PbI2 (461 mg) in 0.7 mL of MA and 0.7 mL of ACN, was slot-die coated on PET/ITO/ETL substrates at a pump rate of 0.15 mL min−1 and a coating speed of 0.8 m min−1. Afterward, the substrates were annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. Fig. S1a and b in Supporting Information show the photographs of the substrates after the perovskite layer coating step.

Next, a layer of Spiro-OMeTAD (45.5 mg mL−1 in chlorobenzene) was slot-die coated on PET/ITO/ETL/MAPbI3 substrates at a pump rate of 0.1 mL min−1 and a coating speed of 0.8 m min−1. After this coating step, the color of the coated layers had a purple shine (see Figs. S1c and d). The HTL solution was doped with 29 μL of 4-tBP and 17.5 μL of LiTFSI stock solution (520 mg mL−1 in ACN). The preparation of the perovskite and Spiro-OMeTAD inks was done in a nitrogen-filled glove box. All three device functional layers were printed in air at a temperature and relative humidity of 18–21 °C and ∼27 %, respectively. Finally, 80 nm gold top contacts were deposited on PET/ITO/ETL/MAPbI3/Spiro-OMeTAD substrates through a shadow mask using a thermal evaporator (Mini SPECTROS, Kurt J. Lesker, USA). The deposition rate for the top Au contacts was 0.1 nm s−1, and the base pressure of the deposition chamber was 10−6 Torr. The photographs of the fabricated printed FPSCs are shown in Figs. S1c and d. The active area of devices was 0.1 cm2. The illuminated area of the FPSCs was defined using an aperture mask of 0.0314 cm2.

Fabrication of electron-only (n-i-n structured) devices

The fabrication process for electron-only devices with a PET/ITO/(SnO2 QD- or PCBM-based bottom ETL)/MAPbI3/PCBM-based top ETL/Ag structure was similar to the process for conventional (n-i-p structured) printed FPSCs. The main difference lies in slot-die coating the top ETL using PCBM ink with a higher concentration (10 mg mL−1 in CB). The slot-die coating parameters for the top ETL were 0.15 mL min−1 and 0.8 m min−1 for the pump rate and coating speed, respectively. After this step, the substrates were annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. Finally, the top Ag contacts of the devices were deposited using the thermal evaporation method with the same parameters as for the top Au contacts (see above).

Device characterizations and measurements

X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku Smartlab, Japan) analysis was used to study the structural properties of printed perovskite layers. Cross-section images of slot-die-coated device functional layers were obtained using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Crossbeam 540, Zeiss, Germany). An ultraviolet–visible spectrometer (UV–Vis, Lambda 1050, PerkinElmer, USA) was employed to analyze the optical properties of printed thin films. The photoluminescence (PL) spectra of smaples were obtained using a FLS 1000 spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments, UK). The main photovoltaic parameters of the fabricated printed FPSCs were investigated using a solar simulator (ORIEL Sol3A, Newport, USA). The spectral responses of devices were measured using an external quantum efficiency (EQE) measurement system (ORIEL IQE 200, Newport, USA). A system for measuring induced current by a laser beam (Professional LBIC, InfinityPV, Denmark) was utilized to assess the active area of devices. An impedance and intensity-modulated voltage and photocurrent spectroscopy system (Autolab PGSTAT302N, Metrohm, Switzerland) was employed to analyze the properties of the fabricated devices.

Computer simulation and modelling

Computer simulation and modeling experiments were conducted using the MATLAB computational platform, employing a drift-diffusion model for PSCs developed by Calado et al. [46]. This model enables the simulation of one-dimensional ordered semiconductor devices with mixed ionic-electronic conducting layers, incorporating up to four charge carrier species, including electrons, holes, and up to two ionic species. Additionally, the model features a discrete interlayer interface approach, eliminating the need for boundary conditions at material interfaces and introducing interface-specific properties. A brief description of the model is provided in the Supporting Information. The thickness of the functional layers of printed FPSCs, determined from SEM cross-section images (see Fig. S2 in Supporting Information), and their physical parameters used in simulation experiments are presented in Tables S1 and S2 in Supporting Information.

Results and discussion

The structure of printed FPSCs with SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based ETLs is presented in Fig. 1a. Fig. 1b–e displays the statistical data for the solar cell performance parameters of 24 devices fabricated within the framework of this work. The solar cell parameters obtained from reverse J-V scan measurements indicate that the best performance was observed in SnO2 QD-based devices, with average values of open-circuit voltage (VOC), short-circuit current density (JSC), fill factor (FF), and power conversion efficiency (PCE) around 0.94 V, 15.25 mA cm−2, 65.2 %, and 9.31 %, respectively. In forward J-V scan measurements, the average VOC, JSC, and FF in SnO2 QD-based devices were lower, with values of 0.83 V, 13.53 mA cm−2, and 38.94 %, respectively, resulting in an average PCE of around 4.40 %.

(a) Structure of printed FPSCs. Statistical data for the photovoltaic parameters including (b) JSC, (c) VOC, (d) FF, (e) PCE, and (f) HI for SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based devices. The designations ‘RS-' and ‘FS-' in the graphs signify the reverse and forward J-V scan directions, respectively. The shaded columns in the graphs represent the standard deviation.

For PCBM-based devices, the average values of VOC, JSC, FF, and PCE for reverse J-V scan measurements were around 0.93 V, 10.88 mA cm−2, 50.95 %, and 5.18 %, respectively. In forward J-V scan measurements, the average VOC and JSC of PCBM-based devices remained stable, with values around 0.93 V and 10.42 mA cm−2, respectively. However, the average FF was slightly lower, with a value of around 45 %. Thus, in forward J-V scan measurements, the average PCE in these devices was around 4.35 %.

Comparison of the average reverse and forward J-V scan PCE values for both SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based devices shows that the hysteresis effect was present in both device types. However, the average value of the hysteresis index (HI) in SnO2 QD-based devices (∼0.53) was significantly higher than in PCBM-based devices (∼0.16), as shown in Fig. 1f. The estimated average photovoltaic parameters of the devices are presented in Table S3 in the Supporting Information.

The EQE spectra of the devices with different ETLs are shown in Fig. S3. The EQE spectra for SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based devices have a similar shape. However, the EQE values for the SnO2 QD-based device are higher, resulting in higher integrated short-circuit current. The JSC estimated from the EQE spectra show similar values to the ones obtained from J-V measurements (see Table S3).

In the next step, we investigated the origin of the observed performance disparity between SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based devices. To assess this, open-circuit voltage decay (OCVD) measurements were performed to estimate the electron lifetime (τ) in the perovskite layer of the fabricated devices [[47], [48], [49]]. Fig. 2a illustrates the electron lifetime curves of devices as functions of photovoltage. A comparison of the curves reveals that, at equal potentials, τ in the SnO2 QD-based device is longer. This suggests that the charge carrier recombination rate in the perovskite layer of SnO2 QD-based devices is lower than in PCBM-based devices [50,51]. This implies that the density of defects in the perovskite layer of SnO2 QD-based devices is also lower than in PCBM-based devices.

(a) Electron lifetime as a function of photovoltage in printed FPSCs with SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based ETLs. (b) Dark J-V curves of electron-only devices with SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based ETLs.

To confirm this observation, electron-only devices were fabricated using SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based ETLs, and dark J-V curves of the device were analyzed (Fig. 2b) to quantify the density of trap states (ntraps) and charge carrier (electron) mobility (μ) in the perovskite layer [[52], [53], [54]]. The estimated values for ntraps in the SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based electron-only devices were around 4.1 × 1015 and 8.5 × 1015 cm−3, respectively. The estimated values for μ in SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based electron-only devices were 1.2 × 10−2 and 4.9 × 10−3 cm2 V−1 s−1, respectively. Contrasting the estimated ntraps and μ values in the SnO2 QD-based electron-only device with those in the PCBM-based one indicates that the perovskite layer in the former has indeed a better quality. This is further confirmed by the analysis of the XRD patterns of slot-die coated MAPbI3 films on SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based ETLs, where the average perovskite crystallite size in the former is considerably larger than in the latter (see Fig. S4 in Supporting Information). The photoluminescence (PL) results reveal that the PL intensity of the perovskite film on PCBM-based ETL is significantly higher than that of on SnO2 QDs-based one (see Fig. S5). This suggests that the charge extraction efficiency of SnO2 QD-based ETLs is superior to that of PCBM-based ETLs, highlighting a distinct difference in the properties of the ETL/perovskite interface. The corresponding absorption spectra are presented in Fig. S6.

Additionally, it should be noted that the observed relatively low performance of the PCBM-based devices compared to those with SnO2 QD-based ETLs is also due to a slightly lower transmittance of PCBM-based ETLs as opposed to SnO2 QD-based ETLs, as shown in Fig. S7 in the Supporting Information.

To further analyze the behavior of the fabricated printed FPSCs, we numerically simulated the experimentally obtained J-V curves (see Fig. 3) using computer models for SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based devices. The photovoltaic parameters extracted from both the experimental and simulated J-V curves are detailed in Table 1. It is important to note that the experimental J-V curves selected for numerical simulation closely align with the average performance of SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based devices (see Table S3 in the Supporting Information). This careful selection ensures that the computer models for SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based devices closely represent real experimental devices, ensuring fairness in the comparison of theoretical results.

The device parameters obtained from the computer simulation experiments (refer to Table S2 in the Supporting Information) indicate that the electronic properties of the perovskite layer in SnO2 QD-based devices are considerably superior to those in PCBM-based devices. For instance, the electron lifetime (τ) and charge carrier diffusion length (Ld) in the perovskite layer of SnO2 QD-based devices are higher than in PCBM-based devices by factors of approximately 1.4 and 2, respectively. Additionally, the surface recombination of charge carriers in SnO2 QD-based devices is lower than in PCBM-based devices by a factor of 2. These findings are somewhat consistent with the results of OCVD and electron-only device measurements, as well as XRD analysis of perovskite films on PET/ITO/SnO2 and PET/ITO/PCBM (see Fig. 2 and Fig. S4 in the Supporting Information).

In order to analyze the concentration of mobile ions in the perovskite layer of SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based devices, additional computer simulation and modeling experiments were conducted. We calculated the concentration distributions of mobile cations (c), anions (a), and their difference (Nion = c - a) for short-circuit (SC), maximum power (MP), and open-circuit (OC) conditions with consideration of both forward and reverse J-V scan cases (see Fig. 4).

Spatial distribution of mobile cations ((a) and (b)), anions ((c) and (d)), and their difference ((e) and (f)) in the perovskite layer of SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based printed FPSCs.

A comparison of the concentration distributions of c, a, and Nion in SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based devices for corresponding conditions indicates that the overall trends for these quantities are somewhat similar. At SC and MP, concentrations of c and a are higher at the perovskite/HTL interface (perovskite layer thickness = 400 nm) and ETL/perovskite interface (perovskite layer thickness = 0 nm), respectively. However, at OC, the situation reverses with concentrations of c and a becoming higher at the ETL/perovskite and perovskite/HTL interfaces, respectively (see Fig. 4a–d) [Ref.]. This trend is further evident in Fig. 4e–f, where the concentration distributions of Nion are shown for all three conditions. Nonetheless, a visible difference between the concentration distributions of c, a, and Nion in SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based devices is that in the former case, the concentration distributions of c, a, and Nion (see Fig. 4a–c, and e) differ visibly for forward and reverse J-V scan cases. This indicates that during forward and reverse J-V scans, the mobile ions in the perovskite layer of SnO2 QD-based devices undergo noticeable redistribution. This redistribution also has an impact on the concentration distributions of electrons and holes in the perovskite layer (see Fig. S8 in the Supporting Information).

Further analysis of the c and a concertation distribution curves in SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based devices (see Fig. 4a–d) shows that the concertation of mobile ions in PCBM-based devices is an order of magnitude higher than in SnO2 QD-based device. This could be due to higher defect density in the perovskite layer of PCBM-based devices, which can enable a larger number of mobile ions in the perovskite layer [[55], [56], [57]]. Contrasting the Nion concentration distribution curves in SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based devices (see Fig. 4e–f) shows that the magnitudes of mobile ion concentrations at the ETL/perovskite and perovskite/HTL interfaces are considerably larger in PCBM-based devices. This can be attributed to a smaller work function of PCBM-based ETL as compared to SnO2 QD-based ETL [58,59]. Indeed, since both devices employ the same type of HTL, it is expected that the built-in field in PCBM-based devices is higher than in SnO2 QD-based devices. This can result in accumulation of larger number of mobile ions at the ETL/perovskite and perovskite/HTL interfaces in PCBM-based devices as compared to SnO2 QD-based devices. A drawback of this can be reduced charge carrier extraction in PCBM-based devices [46,[60], [61], [62]]. This is evident from the electron and hole concentration distribution curves in SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based devices shown in Fig. S8 in Supporting Information in which the concentrations of electrons and holes in the perovskite layer of PCBM-based devices is higher as compared to SnO2 QD-based devices. This is also consistent with the experimentally obtained results in which the average JSC in values in PCBM-based devices is considerably lower than in SnO2 QD-based devices (see Table S3 in Supporting Information) even if the difference in transmittance of SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based ETLs is taken into account (see Fig. S7 in Supporting Information).

Fig. 5 shows the calculated electric field distributions in SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based devices at the SC, MP, and OC conditions both for forward and reverse J-V scan cases. Here, the electric field distributions were obtained when mobile ions are present (indicated by ‘w/’) and absent (indicated by ‘w/o’) in the perovskite layer of devices. The graphs show that at SC condition, there is a considerable built-in field in the perovskite layer in the absence of mobile ions. However, in the presence of mobile ions, the built-in filed in the perovskite layer quickly dissipates, which is due to accumulation of a and c at the ETL/perovskite and perovskite/HTL interfaces, respectively (see Fig. 4). The dissipation of the built-in filed in PCBM-based devices occurs slightly faster as compared to SnO2 QD-based devices. This is an expected result, considering that the concentration of mobile ions in the perovskite layer of PCBM-based devices is an order of magnitude higher than in that of SnO2 QD-based devices (see Fig. 4b and d). Nevertheless, it appears that in both devices, the overall built-in field across the thickness of the perovskite layer is negligible except for the thin regions near ETL and HTL. This is shown more explicitly in Fig. S9 in Supporting Information. A negligible built-in field in the bulk of the perovskite layer indicates that the drift components of the electron and hole currents are also negligible i.e., the total current in devices is mainly due to diffusion of electrons and holes toward their respective charge collecting layers. Fig. 5a also shows that when the mobile ions are taken into account, the electric field distribution curves in the SnO2 QD-based device are visibly different for forward and reverse J-V scan cases. This is again due to noticeable redistribution of mobile ions in the perovskite layer of SnO2 QD-based during forward and reverse J-V scans.

Spatial distribution of electric field in printed FPSCs with (a) SnO2 QD- and (b) PCBM-based ETLs.

Overall, the theoretical analysis of SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based printed FPSCs suggests that the level of hysteresis effect in devices depends not only on the concentration of mobile ions in the perovskite layer but also on the rate of their redistribution during forward and reverse J-V scans. The rate of mobile ion redistribution may strongly depend on the quality of the perovskite layer (e.g., polycrystallinity, density of grain boundaries, etc.). Films with higher crystallinity and fewer grain boundaries may assist the motion of mobile ions across the film, whereas films with lower crystallinity and more grain boundaries may hinder it. This could be one of the possible reasons why HI in our SnO2 QD-based devices is higher than in PCBM-based devices despite the lower concentration of mobile ions in the former. This is schematically depicted in Fig. 6, where the redistribution of mobile ions during reverse and forward scans in SnO2 QD-based devices takes place more noticeably than in PCBM-based devices.

Conclusions

In conclusion, printed FPSCs were fabricated using the slot-die coating technique, and the effects of SnO2 QD- and PCBM-based ETLs on device performance were investigated. Devices with SnO2 QD-based ETLs achieved an average PCE of 9.3 %, while those with PCBM-based ETLs achieved 5.2 %. The hysteresis index (HI) was higher in SnO2 QD-based devices (0.531) compared to PCBM-based devices (0.156). This better photovoltaic performance in SnO2 QD-based devices is attributed to their superior optical transmittance and higher quality perovskite layer, as evidenced by XRD, PL, SEM, OCVD, and dark current analyses. Numerical simulations indicated that the concentration of mobile ions in the perovskite layer of PCBM-based devices is an order of magnitude higher than in SnO2 QD-based devices. The higher HI in SnO2 QD-based devices is due to more effective redistribution of mobile ions during J-V scans. These findings highlight the interplay between ETLs and perovskite film quality, providing insights for developing efficient, hysteresis-free printed FPSCs.
