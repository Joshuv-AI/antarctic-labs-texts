Modeling anesthetic-drug label detection for low-resource operating rooms
Authors: Solomon Nsumba, Flavia Delmira Ninsiima, Mary Juliet Nampawu, Peter Nabende, Joyce Nakatumba-Nabende
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedication errors during surgical procedures pose significant risks to patient safety, particularly in low-resource healthcare settings. In this study, we present the development and evaluation of computer vision models for detecting clinical errors related to syringe and vial swaps during anesthetic drug administration at the Comprehensive Rehabilitation Services for People with Disability in Uganda (CoRSU Hospital). Leveraging data collected through wearable head-mounted cameras, we created a custom dataset comprising annotated images of syringes and vials labeled with a diverse range of drugs used in the operating room. Three deep learning-based object detection architectures were trained: YOLOv11n, YOLOv12n, and RT-DETR large. The RT-DETR model achieved marginally higher performance, with a precision of 0.89, recall of 0.85, and mAP50 of 0.86, outperforming both YOLO variants across key metrics. The models provide a foundation for real-time monitoring tools aimed at reducing medication errors and improving clinical safety protocols in resource-constrained surgical environments.Keywords: Medical error detection, Computer vision, Object detection, Anesthesiology

Introduction

Medication errors are a significant cause of preventable patient harm in clinical settings, with studies estimating that as many as 251,000 deaths annually in the United States alone are attributable to such errors [1]. These incidents are especially critical in anesthesiology, where intravenous drug administration must occur under high-pressure and time-sensitive conditions [2]. Common among these are vial swap errors in which an incorrect drug is drawn into a syringe and syringe swap errors in which a correctly labeled syringe is mistakenly administered [3]. These two types alone account for roughly 40% of all drug-related errors observed in hospitals [4].

To mitigate such risks, traditional strategies such as color coded labeling, barcode scanning systems, and double-check protocols have been widely adopted. Color-coded labeling has improved visual drug identification [5], while barcode scanning systems ensure drug verification during medication administration. However, compliance with barcode scanning remains inconsistent, often due to time constraints and workflow interruptions [6, 7]. Double-check systems, where two healthcare professionals verify the medication, though effective, are time intensive and susceptible to human oversight [8]. These limitations, coupled with cognitive overload and deviations from safety protocols, make traditional error prevention strategies insufficient in fully eliminating clinical errors.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) and computer vision have emerged as promising technologies for error detection in healthcare. Object detection algorithms such as YOLO [9] and Faster R-CNN [10] have been deployed in real-time analysis of drug preparation and administration. In this study [11], an enhanced version of YOLO was integrated with a Vision Transformer (ViT) to improve label detection accuracy under challenging clinical conditions. These models were trained to recognize syringe and vial labels even in varied lighting conditions, label occlusions, and amidst non-standardized drug packaging. The ViT architecture was particularly beneficial in capturing contextual cues in complex visual scenes, thereby improving classification robustness.

Wearable technologies have also shown promise in clinical environments, offering unobtrusive ways to collect procedural data and enhance patient safety. Wearable cameras, in particular, have been used for surgical skill assessment, infection control, and anesthesia workflow monitoring [11]. This study builds upon this work by deploying a head-mounted camera system to record drug preparation activities in real-time at CoRSU Hospital in Uganda. Unlike prior studies [11] conducted in high-resource settings like the United States, the Ugandan clinical environment presented unique challenges—including differences in drug labeling conventions, availability, and operating room configurations.

Beyond error prevention, such systems can support drug usage tracking, supply chain monitoring, training of anesthesia providers, and integration into hospital safety programs. This highlights the potential for impact well beyond the operating room.

Existing studies in high-resource hospitals have typically assumed standardized drug labeling, uniform operating room layouts, and access to advanced infrastructure. Such assumptions limit their applicability in settings where workflows are variable and resources are constrained. In contrast, our work directly targets low-resource environments, where labeling practices differ, lighting conditions fluctuate, and safety protocols are harder to enforce. By addressing these challenges, we position our system as a practical solution for contexts that have been largely overlooked in previous research.

To capture the complementary strengths needed for clinical deployment, we selected the YOLOv11-nano, YOLOv12-nano, and RT-DETR-large models. YOLOv11-nano and YOLOv12-nano were chosen for their real-time inference capability and low computational demands, which are critical for integration into low-resource operating room settings without requiring advanced hardware. In contrast, RT-DETR-large was selected as a state-of-the-art transformer-based model that provides higher accuracy in complex and cluttered visual environments, serving as a benchmark for performance. Together, these models allow us to evaluate the trade-off between speed and accuracy, which is central to determining the most practical approach for real-world clinical use.

This work represents one of the first AI-powered systems for clinical error detection adapted to the realities of low-resource surgical environments. Unlike prior studies in high-income settings, our approach addresses challenges of non-standard labeling, variable lighting, and workflow constraints.

Advantages of the proposed system: The proposed approach provides an unobtrusive, real-time, and context-aware safety net for anesthetic drug administration in resource-constrained surgical environments.

Our findings align with and extend recent work such as [11], who applied YOLO-based models in high-resource hospitals, and [12], who demonstrated the value of structured annotation pipelines in medical video. By adapting similar approaches to a resource-constrained setting, we demonstrate that high-performance error detection is feasible even without standardized infrastructure.

Related work

Two families of object detection models are central to this work: YOLO and RT-DETR. YOLO (You Only Look Once) is a single-stage object detection framework that divides an image into grids and simultaneously predicts bounding boxes and class probabilities, making it highly suitable for real-time detection [9]. The nano variant of YOLOv11 offers a lightweight architecture optimized for speed, which is advantageous in time-critical clinical environments. YOLOv12 further refines this design with architectural and training improvements that target both accuracy and efficiency [13, 14]. In contrast, RT-DETR (Real-Time Detection Transformer) employs a transformer-based encoder with self-attention mechanisms to capture long-range dependencies across the image [15]. This enables it to perform well in complex and cluttered scenes, such as operating rooms with multiple overlapping objects. These complementary characteristics—speed in YOLO and contextual robustness in RT-DETR—explain our choice to benchmark all three models in the context of anesthetic error detection. The latest YOLO variants also incorporate residual connections, feature pyramid networks, and advanced augmentations to further improve performance [13].

Computer vision (CV) and artificial intelligence (AI) applications have seen considerable growth in healthcare, particularly in enhancing patient safety and clinical workflow optimization. In high-acuity environments such as Intensive Care Units (ICUs), vision-based systems have been used to monitor compliance with safety protocols. Automated systems have been developed to evaluate hand hygiene practices [16] and to track patient mobility and activity levels in ICUs using deep learning and pervasive sensing [17, 18]. These studies reinforce the broader potential of computer vision in healthcare and motivate the extension of such approaches to medication safety in low-resource anesthesia practice.

In surgical settings, CV applications have focused on skill and phase assessment. Tool detection models assess surgical proficiency [19], and automation pipelines support structured self-evaluation [20]. Video-based systems have enabled intraoperative phase recognition [21, 22], cancer detection, and monitoring of auditory and visual cues for compliance and teaching [23, 24]. These advances reinforce the value of video analytics in promoting patient safety.

However, the use of CV and AI for the direct detection of medical errors, particularly medication errors, remains underexplored. One of the most cited works in this domain is Chan et al. (2024) [11], who deployed AI-enabled wearable cameras to detect clinical medication errors during anesthetic drug administration. Their model integrated an enhanced YOLOv5 with Vision Transformers (ViT), trained on a dataset collected from high-resource hospital environments in the U.S. While their results were promising in terms of label detection under varied lighting and occlusion conditions, the system relied on standardized drug labeling and fixed OR configurations, limiting its generalizability to more diverse or low-resource settings.

Most datasets used in these studies are proprietary and collected under highly controlled environments, restricting reproducibility and external validation. Public datasets like the MEDS dataset (focused on medication detection) and synthetic datasets for surgical scenes exist, but they often lack representation of diverse healthcare contexts, such as those in low- and middle-income countries. Models such as Faster R-CNN, YOLO variants, and DETR have shown promise in object detection tasks but require adaptation to operate effectively in dynamic, resource-constrained environments with non-standard drug packaging and frequent workflow interruptions.

In the field of anesthesiology, most vision-based systems center on the analysis of ultrasound images for neuraxial and intravascular access [25, 26]. A notable exception includes a syringe classification and volume estimation tool [27], although its reliance on a static background limits applicability in real-world OR deployments. This leaves a critical gap in AI-powered systems designed to monitor and verify anesthetic drug preparation in situ.

Efficient and secure annotation of clinical video remains a bottleneck in medical computer vision. CVAT (Computer Vision Annotation Tool) has emerged as a valuable platform for annotating sensitive data, offering a cost-effective and privacy-conscious alternative to platforms like Mechanical Turk. Its medical applications include endoscopic video annotation for simulated neurosurgical procedures [28], while non-medical use cases span odometer reading recognition [29] and sports analytics [30].

Efforts to reduce medication errors through improved labeling systems have also shown promise. For example, the Codonics Safe Label System improved label visibility and compliance [4], but such systems rely on structured workflows and standardized setups, which are often not present in low-resource settings.

Despite these advancements, no prior work has developed a real-time AI system for medication error detection using wearable cameras in low-resource surgical environments. Our work addresses this gap by proposing a novel computer vision-based error detection pipeline, adapted for clinical contexts where drug labeling standards, lighting conditions, and resource availability differ markedly from high-income hospital environments. Methods

Dataset collection

The dataset was collected as part of the study on intelligent wearable systems to detect medication errors at the CoRSU Rehabilitation Hospital. The images were acquired using a head-mounted GoPro HERO camera, selected for its high-definition 4K video capabilities, wide-angle lens, and rugged design, making it suitable for deployment in an Operating Room environment. The study received ethical approval from the Mildmay Uganda Research Ethics Committee (MUREC) under reference number MUREC-2024–378, and was subsequently cleared for national registration with the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology (UNCST). All procedures followed the approved protocol and ensured the protection of participant privacy and informed consent as per institutional and national ethical guidelines. The GoPro was securely mounted on a forehead strap worn by anesthesia providers, centered on the midline approximately 2–3 cm above the glabella. The camera was angled downward by about 20 to capture the working area where syringe preparation and vial handling occurred, with the roll constrained within to minimize lateral drift. Recordings were captured at 4K resolution using the wide field of view setting, stabilization enabled, and automatic white balance. This configuration ensured hands-free operation and consistent field-of-view coverage across all sessions. To promote reproducibility, a standardized setup checklist was followed before each recording to confirm mounting height, angle, and positioning consistency. The GoPro’s performance was evaluated under different operating room conditions, including bright illumination from overhead surgical lamps and dimmer ambient light. Its wide dynamic range and image stabilization allowed reliable capture of syringe and vial labels across these lighting scenarios. Alternative devices such as the Axon Flex 2, Microsoft HoloLens, and Google Glass were also considered. The Axon Flex 2 provided adequate head-mount ergonomics but lacked a wide-angle lens necessary to cover the full syringe preparation area. HoloLens and Google Glass offered advanced features (e.g., heads-up display, audio), which were unnecessary for this study and raised privacy concerns. Their heavier form factor also introduced potential comfort issues for prolonged use. Based on these evaluations, the GoPro HERO was selected as the most suitable device, balancing image quality, robustness, and unobtrusiveness for low-resource operating room environments. Data was stored on an encrypted SD card during recording sessions and subsequently transferred to a password-protected and encrypted server for secure storage and processing. The deployment of the head-mounted GoPro minimized interference with routine clinical workflows while ensuring a comprehensive and unobtrusive data collection process. The image in Fig. 1 captures an example of the actual setup used during data acquisition, where the clinician interacts with syringes while wearing the recording device. This setup allowed for a naturalistic and minimally disruptive observation of label-handling practices. The collected dataset serves as a critical resource for training machine learning models to detect medication errors based on syringe and vial label interactions.

Dataset annotation

The annotation process was conducted using the Computer Vision Annotation Tool (CVAT) [31] to label the collected image dataset. A structured project was created under a dedicated CVAT organization to manage the annotation workflow. Within this framework, image annotation tasks were assigned to four trained annotators, each responsible for labeling specific subsets of the dataset. The annotated images were securely stored on a Google Cloud Storage (GCS) bucket, which was directly linked to CVAT. This integration enabled seamless access and streamlined data handling during the annotation process. Annotators were instructed to identify and label key objects of interest, which included syringes, vials, syringe drug labels, and vial drug labels for all the drugs under study. Bounding boxes were manually drawn around these objects to ensure precise localization and clear differentiation. An example of this annotation task is shown in Fig. 3, where bounding boxes highlight labeled syringes and drug names captured from the head-mounted camera’s point of view. To ensure the highest quality of the annotations, all completed jobs underwent a two-stage review process. Initially, two dedicated reviewers assessed the annotations for labeling accuracy and consistency. Following this, a panel of four anesthesiologist reviewers independently verified that the bounding boxes were correctly placed and accurately labeled according to the visible drug information. Any inconsistencies or mistakes identified at this stage were resolved collaboratively, and final corrections were approved before export.The validated annotations were exported in YOLO format, a format widely adopted in object detection workflows, ensuring smooth integration with machine learning training pipelines.

Training RT-DETR for syringe and vial drug label detection

To detect syringe drug labels and vial drug labels in clinical images, we employed the Realtime Detection Transformer (RT-DETR), a state-of-the-art framework for real-time object detection. RT-DETR leverages the power of transformer architectures—originally designed for natural language processing—to model long-range dependencies and spatial relationships within images, making it highly effective for dense object detection tasks [15]. The RT-DETR training process began with the preparation of a labeled dataset, where each object in an image was annotated with bounding boxes and corresponding class labels (e.g.,

Propofol_Syringe_Label, Adrenaline_Vial_Label). The dataset was partitioned into 50% for training and 50% for validation using an alternating sampling strategy, where every other frame was assigned to either the training or validation set. This approach was chosen due to the sequential nature of the data collected from video streams, ensuring that the model was exposed to representative samples from the entire dataset. Standard random splits would have introduced class imbalance and overfitting risks, given the clustered appearance of similar samples in consecutive frames. Data augmentation techniques such as random cropping, color jittering, horizontal flipping, and scaling were applied to enhance the model’s ability to generalize across different environments and lighting conditions. The model was then fine-tuned using a pretrained RT-DETR backbone, with training conducted over multiple epochs until convergence. The augmentation parameters were chosen to reflect real-world variability in the dataset. Small-angle rotations () accounted for natural tilts in syringe and vial orientation, brightness and contrast jittering addressed variable lighting in the operating room, and random scaling/cropping simulated distance changes and partial occlusions due to hand movements. Mosaic augmentation further increased contextual diversity by combining multiple samples. These ranges were selected to enhance model robustness without introducing unrealistic artifacts beyond those observed in the collected clinical data. RT-DETR follows a transformer-based architecture that consists of two primary components:

Backbone Network: A convolutional neural network (CNN) backbone, such as ResNet or a vision transformer (ViT), is employed to extract features from the input image. The extracted feature map serves as the input to the transformer encoder [32].

Backbone Network: A convolutional neural network (CNN) backbone, such as ResNet or a vision transformer (ViT), is employed to extract features from the input image. The extracted feature map serves as the input to the transformer encoder [32].

Transformer Encoder: The encoder employs multi-head self-attention mechanisms to model long-range dependencies and spatial relationships across the entire image. This attention mechanism allows the model to focus on the most relevant regions for object detection. The encoder is designed to work with a set of learnable object queries that interact with the input features, allowing for the detection of objects at different scales and locations [33, 34].

For training, the optimizer and the learning rate were automatically selected by the Ultralytics training framework. The adaptive optimizer AdamW, which is commonly employed for transformer-based models, was selected with an initial learning rate of 0.001. No manual learning rate decay or annealing schedule was applied; instead, the framework’s adaptive learning strategy ensured stable convergence within 20 epochs. The batch size was set to 16. Training was carried out over multiple epochs (i.e., 20 epochs). The number of training epochs was set to 20 based on empirical observation: the model reached convergence on the validation loss and mean Average Precision (mAP) metrics within this range, with no substantial gains observed beyond 20 epochs. To reduce overfitting risks, we incorporated transfer learning from COCO-pretrained weights, employed a 50/50 train–validation split designed to minimize temporal redundancy, and applied extensive data augmentation. The loss function was monitored during training, and the model’s performance was evaluated periodically using metrics such as mean Average Precision (mAP) and precision–recall curves on the validation set. After training, the model’s predictions are refined using Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) to remove duplicate detections. The NMS algorithm suppresses overlapping bounding boxes by retaining only those with the highest confidence scores above a defined threshold. Additionally, the confidence threshold was adjusted to ensure that only highly confident detections were kept for final model output.

These architectures build on previous YOLO designs by introducing significant enhancements in accuracy and inference speed [13, 14].

The training process began with a labeled dataset containing bounding box annotations and corresponding class labels. The dataset was divided into 50% for training and 50% for validation to ensure reliable model evaluation.

Experimental setup

The training and inference experiments for RT-DETR, YOLOv11n, and YOLOv12n models were performed on an NVIDIA A100 GPU with 40GB of VRAM, accessed via Google Colab Pro+. The software environment was based on Python 3.10.12, PyTorch 2.0.1, and the Ultralytics YOLO and RT-DETR implementations [35].

Results

Performance analysis of RT-DETR and YOLO models

We implemented the code for YOLOv11n, YOLOv12n, and RT-DETR (large) from the Ultralytics repository [35]. We decided to implement transfer learning and initialize our models with pre-trained weights to then begin training on the custom data set. These weights are from a model trained on the COCO dataset. We then compared which model is optimal for our use case given the trade-off between inference speed and mAP50 on the validation set. The mean Average Precision (mAP) at an Intersection-over-Union (IoU) threshold of 0.5 (mAP@50) metric averages the precision across all object classes, where a detection is considered correct if the predicted bounding box overlaps with the ground truth by at least 50%. Higher mAP@50 values indicate more accurate detection performance. YOLOv12n offers a modest but consistent improvement over YOLOv11n across all summary metrics, with gains of approximately 0.01 in precision, 0.013 in recall, 0.01 in mAP50, and 0.006 in mAP50–95. This suggests that the architectural refinements in YOLOv12 translate into measurable accuracy benefits while remaining within the nano-scale regime.

We also observe that YOLOv11n and RT-DETR infer at 0.7 and 3.8 milliseconds per image respectively on the validation set. However, our original goal is to reach an average inference speed between 15 to 30 frames for 1080p. We tested the RT-DETR large-size model on multiple 1080p HD videos and observed an average total speed (pre-process speed (2.4 ms) + inference speed (561.2 ms) + post-process speed (0.2 ms)) of 563.8 ms (1 frame per second). When testing the YOLO nano-size model on multiple 2.7k resolution HD videos, we observe an average total speed (pre-process speed (2.4 ms) + inference speed (53.7 ms) + post-process speed (0.2 ms)) of 56.3 ms (17 frames per second), which aligns with our primary objective. Although we did not perform a full timing sweep for YOLOv12n, its nano-scale architecture is comparable to YOLOv11n, so we expect its runtime characteristics to remain within the same real-time operating regime. This leads to our selection of the YOLO family, and particularly the nano variants, as strong candidates for deployment, with RT-DETR serving as a high-accuracy reference.

Discussion

The development of AI-powered clinical error detection systems for CoRSU Hospital in Uganda represents a critical step toward mitigating medication errors, particularly within the high-risk domain of anesthesiology. Building upon prior work conducted in the United States [11], this study adapts and extends those methodologies to a resource-constrained healthcare setting. The findings demonstrate the feasibility and potential of leveraging artificial intelligence and computer vision technologies to enhance patient safety in diverse clinical environments.

While full deployment into clinical workflows is yet to occur, the successful training and validation of these models using locally collected datasets underscore their relevance and transferability to low-resource contexts. This work illustrates the promise of scalable, cost-effective solutions that can be integrated into hospital systems with minimal disruption. It also emphasizes the importance of context-aware adaptation in global health AI initiatives and highlights the value of cross-institutional collaboration in addressing systemic healthcare challenges.

Key findings and implications

Our findings indicate that all three models—RT-DETR, YOLOv11n, and YOLOv12n—are effective in detecting syringe and vial label errors, with RT-DETR showing the highest accuracy in complex scenes. However, the lightweight YOLO variants offer superior inference speed, making them more suitable for real-time applications, aligning with our primary objective of achieving a balance between accuracy and speed. The deployment of wearable cameras, specifically the head-mounted GoPro system, proved to be minimally disruptive to clinical workflows while providing comprehensive data collection. This is particularly important in resource-limited settings where workflow efficiency is critical.

The study also highlights the importance of model adaptability to different clinical environments. The variations in drug availability, labeling conventions, and operating room layouts in Uganda necessitated modifications to the training strategies and dataset expansion. This adaptability is crucial for the broader applicability of AI systems in global healthcare settings, where clinical practices and resources can vary significantly.

Practical implications in healthcare

The performance metrics observed in this study have direct consequences for clinical practice. In real-world anesthetic workflows, high recall is critical to ensure that potential drug errors are rarely missed, thereby safeguarding patient safety. Similarly, high precision minimizes false alarms, which is essential to prevent workflow interruptions and alarm fatigue among anesthesia providers. While the RT-DETR model demonstrated the highest accuracy, its low inference speed (1 frame per second) makes it impractical for real-time use in the operating room. In contrast, the YOLO nano models achieved near-comparable accuracy but with real-time inference capability (17 frames per second for YOLOv11n), enabling timely feedback during drug preparation and administration. This trade-off underscores that in healthcare, particularly in low-resource settings, speed and reliability of detection are as important as accuracy, making the YOLOv11n and YOLOv12n models more suitable for integration into routine clinical workflows.

Societal and economic impact

The deployment of AI-powered medication error detection systems in developing regions carries important societal and economic implications. In many low-resource healthcare settings, adverse drug events not only compromise patient safety but also contribute to extended hospital stays, higher treatment costs, and increased mortality rates. By reducing medication errors in anesthesia practice, our system has the potential to lower these costs and improve clinical efficiency, thereby alleviating some of the financial strain on both healthcare institutions and patients. Beyond direct clinical benefits, improving trust in surgical care through error reduction can enhance community confidence in health services, encourage care-seeking behavior, and contribute to broader public health gains in resource-constrained settings.

Limitations and future directions

While the results are promising, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted in a single hospital, and the generalizability of the findings to other settings may be limited. Future research should involve multiple healthcare facilities to validate the system’s effectiveness across diverse clinical environments. Importantly, although the AI models were successfully developed and validated using locally collected data, they have not yet been deployed in live hospital settings. Future work should prioritize real-world deployment and clinical integration to assess the system’s performance under routine operating conditions. This includes evaluating its impact on clinical workflows, medication error rates, and overall patient safety. Although some drug classes (e.g., dexamethasone, atracurium) had fewer than 70 annotated instances, no explicit class rebalancing or weighting was applied. Instead, we relied on strong data augmentation and the models’ intrinsic loss functions (focal/CIoU) to reduce imbalance effects. Future dataset expansions will aim to increase representation of rare classes. Our benchmarking emphasizes YOLOv11n and RT-DETR with a single YOLOv8 baseline trained under a standardized 20-epoch protocol. We did not conduct exhaustive comparisons against older two-stage or earlier YOLO variants (e.g., Faster R-CNN, YOLOv5). Prior work generally reports these architectures as lower or comparable in performance relative to modern YOLO and transformer detectors under similar conditions. A broader, carefully tuned baseline sweep would strengthen comparative analysis and is a priority for future work. Another critical area for future exploration is the integration of AI systems with existing hospital information systems. Such integration could enable real-time error detection and immediate feedback to clinicians, thereby enhancing clinical decision-making. Additionally, the acceptability and usability of wearable camera systems among healthcare providers remain essential for successful implementation. Future studies should incorporate qualitative assessments to better understand user experience, privacy concerns, and workflow compatibility, ensuring that these technologies are not only effective but also practical and well-received in everyday clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and validated an AI-powered framework for the real-time detection of anesthetic drug errors, explicitly designed for the challenges of low-resource clinical settings. Our work directly addressed its primary goals: achieving operational feasibility, ensuring adaptability to non-standard conditions, and maintaining high accuracy.

First, we established that real-time performance is attainable. The YOLOv11-nano model achieved an inference speed of 17 FPS on high-resolution video, meeting our target for real-time clinical deployment without requiring specialized computational infrastructure, and the YOLOv12-nano model follows the same nano-scale design philosophy. Second, the system was specifically adapted to the realities of a low-resource environment. By training on a custom, locally collected dataset featuring non-standard drug labels and variable lighting, and by employing robust data augmentation, our models learned to perform under the exact challenging conditions where traditional, standardized systems fail. Finally, the models demonstrated high accuracy, with our RT-DETR-large model achieving a mean average precision (mAP50) of 0.86, a precision of 0.89, and a recall of 0.85, and the best-performing YOLO variant (YOLOv12n) achieving an mAP50 of 0.85 with precision 0.87 and recall 0.83.

When contextualized against the state-of-the-art, our results are particularly significant. While prior work, such as [11] reported a higher mAP50 (0.92) using a YOLOv5-ViT architecture, their model was trained and tested in a high-resource setting with standardized labels and controlled conditions. Our model’s competitive performance (0.86 mAP50) was achieved under significantly more challenging and variable real-world constraints. This demonstrates that high-performance AI error detection is not solely dependent on standardized infrastructure but can be effectively adapted to diverse and resource-constrained clinical environments. In conclusion, this work provides a critical proof-of-concept for scalable, cost-effective, and context-aware AI solutions in global health. The successful use of wearable cameras and deep learning models tailored to local conditions at CoRSU Hospital highlights a viable pathway toward reducing medication errors and improving patient safety in operating rooms worldwide. Future work will focus on live clinical integration and exploring the system’s impact on error rates and workflow efficiency.
