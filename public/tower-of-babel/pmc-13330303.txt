Tumor-enriched circRNA TAFCR1 drives cancer progression and metastasis by disrupting UBE1-dependent TRIM21-mediated CD44 ubiquitination
Authors: Rilan Bai, Yan Li, Lei Zhou, Yu Yu, Xue Wen, Hui Li, Yijing Zhao, Andrew R. Hoffman, Wei Li, Jifan Hu, Jiuwei Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lipid metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer, driving tumorigenesis, progression, metastasis, and chemoresistance. Fatty acid synthase (FASN) is a key enzyme in this pathway. Here, we identified a novel exonic circular RNA (circRNA) within the FASN locus, termed TAFCR1 (tumor-associated FASN circular RNA 1). TAFCR1 comprises partial exon 20 (20 L), exons 21, 22, and partial 23 (23 S) of the FASN gene, generated through MBNL1-mediated back-splicing via short Alu motifs at the cyclization junction. TAFCR1 was tumor-specifically upregulated and promoted proliferation, invasion, and migration of cancer cells in vitro. In vivo, TAFCR1 knockdown suppressed tumor growth and metastasis, and prolonging survival in xenograft and lung metastasis models. Mechanistically, TAFCR1 functions as a specific RNA modulator that binds the ubiquitin-activating enzyme E1 (UBE1/UBA1), thereby disrupting UBE1-dependent, TRIM21-mediated CD44 ubiquitination and upregulating the oncogenic signaling molecule CD44. The E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM21 serves as a critical mediator regulating CD44 ubiquitination by binding to the CD44 intracellular domain (CD44-ICD) and catalyzing K48-linked polyubiquitination of CD44 at K695. Upregulated CD44 further activates the PI3K/AKT pathway by suppressing the tumor suppressor PHLPP1. This cascade subsequently enhances the expression of stemness factor SOX2, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers (vimentin, N-cadherin, and fibronectin), and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9), ultimately promoting cancer metastasis. Collectively, our findings establish TAFCR1 as a promising therapeutic target for cancers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40364-026-00946-8.

Introduction

Lipid metabolism is a critical pathway in cancer metabolic reprogramming. It not only provides energy and biosynthetic precursors for tumor proliferation and metastasis but also generates lipid second messengers that drive oncogenic signaling [1]. Lipid metabolic reprogramming supports tumorigenesis, progression, metastasis, and chemoresistance [2]. Fatty acid synthase (FASN) is a key enzyme in this pathway, conferring energetic flexibility to meet the high metabolic demands of tumor cells [1, 3]. FASN also promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and regulates cell cycle progression [4, 5]. Its upregulation correlates with tumor development, poor prognosis, and therapeutic resistance [4, 5], particularly in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and colorectal cancer (CRC). Although FASN protein function has been extensively characterized—including roles in growth signaling, metabolism, and immunity [4] —clinical development of FASN inhibitors remains challenging. This is attributable to the ubiquitous expression of FASN across normal cells, tumor cells, and immune cells, leading to on-target toxicities such as severe weight loss and altered food intake in murine models [6], as well as off-target effects. Furthermore, compensatory activation of metabolic bypass pathways often confers resistance to metabolic-targeted therapies, even in combination regimens. Nevertheless, elucidating the mechanisms underlying dysregulated FASN expression remains essential for developing effective cancer therapies targeting metabolic reprogramming.

Emerging evidence indicates that lipid metabolic reprogramming in cancer is regulated by dysregulated non-coding RNAs, including long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) and circular RNAs (circRNAs) [2]. CircRNAs represent a novel class of bifunctional RNAs with non-coding or limited protein-coding potential, characterized by covalently closed single-stranded structures [7, 8]. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that circRNAs are widely expressed in malignant tumors, exhibiting high stability, evolutionary conservation, and tissue- or cell-specific expression patterns, thereby contributing to tumorigenesis and progression [9–11]. Aberrantly expressed circRNAs promote cancer progression by modulating multiple signaling pathways [12, 13]. The interaction between circRNAs and RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) has garnered significant attention, particularly competitive binding that restricts RBP function [14]. Additionally, certain circRNAs serve as translational templates or scaffolds for proteins, or regulate RNA splicing and transcription [15–17]. CircRNAs can also function as competitive endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) [16]. Although numerous circRNAs associated with various malignancies have been identified, additional functionally critical circRNAs likely remain undiscovered. Identifying therapeutic targets within the FASN-mediated metabolic reprogramming pathway holds significant promise for improving cancer treatment.

In this study, we identified a novel circRNA within the FASN locus, termed TAFCR1 (tumor-associated FASN circular RNA 1), through RNA-seq analysis of hepatoma HepG2 cells. TAFCR1, whose activity is distinct from FASN mRNA and other FASN-derived circRNAs, exhibited specific enrichment in hepatoma cells and was associated with tumor metastasis. TAFCR1 was specifically overexpressed in cancer cells, particularly hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and colorectal cancer (CRC). We investigated the biogenesis mechanism of TAFCR1 and characterized its functional role through in vitro and in vivo experiments. Mechanistically, TAFCR1 promotes cancer metastasis by modulating the ubiquitin-dependent degradation of the cancer stem cell factor CD44, representing a noncanonical function of the FASN gene distinct from its canonical role in lipid metabolism. Notably, unlike the ubiquitously expressed FASN protein, TAFCR1 exhibits cancer-specific expression, rendering it a promising precision therapeutic target for HCC and CRC.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cultures

Cell lines included normal liver cells (THLE-2, THLE-3, HHL-5), hepatoma lines (HepG2, Huh7, HCCLM3, SMMC-7721), normal colorectal epithelial cells (NCM460, HIEC-6), colorectal cancer lines (HRT18, HCT116, LOVO, SW620, SW480), and other cancer lines (MCF-7, HeLa, A549). Human embryonic kidney 293T cells were used for virus packaging, as well as intracellular co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and ubiquitination assays. HIEC-6 was purchased from Shanghai Fuheng Biotechnology and cultured in Opti-MEM™ I (Gibco); all other lines were from ATCC and maintained in DMEM with 10% FBS (CellMax) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Hanbio). Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and tested negative for mycoplasma.

Cell transfection

siRNAs targeting CD44, UBE1, and PHLPP1, along with negative control (NC), were synthesized by Jin Tuos (Wuhan). Transfection was performed using jetPRIME (Polyplus) when cells reached 30–50% confluence. For overexpression, TAFCR1 was cloned into the fifth-generation circRNA expression vector pLC5-ciR, and CD44 was cloned into the lentiviral vector pCDH. TAFCR1 shRNA plasmids (sh1, sh2) were constructed in lenti-pGreenPuro-EGFP. Lentiviral particles were produced in 293T cells using jetPRIME and used to infect HepG2 and HRT18 cells. Stable lines were selected with puromycin (Biofroxx) and validated by qRT-PCR. For reporter and co-IP assays, plasmids were transiently transfected at ~ 90% confluence; cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection. HA-tagged ubiquitin mutants (HA-Ub-K48, HA-Ub-K63) and CD44 lysine-to-arginine mutant (K679/680/681R triple mutant, K695R, K705R, K715R, K739R) were obtained from GeneCloudBiotech (Guangzhou, China). All relevant sequences are provided in Table S1.

RNA and cDNA extraction, linear RNA digestion and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol (Sigma-Aldrich) and quantified by spectrophotometry (NanoVue). Reverse transcription was performed using Hifair® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yeasen) with gDNA digester. circRNA and mRNA expression were analyzed by qRT-PCR (CFX384, Bio-Rad): 95 °C for 2 min; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 30 s; melting curve 95 °C for 10 s. circRNAs were enriched by RNase R digestion (3 U/µg, 37 °C for 15 min). Conventional PCR was performed using 2× Taq PCR StarMix (Genstar): 94 °C for 2 min; 32 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 1 min; final extension 72 °C for 5 min. Relative expression was calculated by 2^(−ΔΔCt), normalized to GAPDH (circRNAs), ACTB (mRNA), or U6 (nuclear RNA). Primer sequences are in Table S2. Plasmids were purified using an endotoxin-free kit (TIANGEN).

Immunoblotting and analysis

Cell pellets were lysed in Western/IP lysis buffer (NCM, Suzhou) with PMSF. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay (Beyotime). Samples were prepared in 5× or 1× SDS-PAGE loading buffer and boiled at 100 °C for 10–15 min. Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were separated using the Nucleoprotein and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime). Proteins were separated on precast gels (Omni-Easy™, Yamei) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 2 h, incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, and then with secondary antibody for 2 h. Signals were visualized using ECL (NcmECL Ultra, NCM) and quantified by ImageJ, normalized to internal reference.

Cell function assays

(A) CCK-8 proliferation assay: Cells were seeded at 2 × 10³/well in 96-well plates and incubated for 24–96 h. At each time point, CCK-8 solution (Beyotime) was added and incubated for 2–4 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured (BioTek) to generate proliferation curves. (B) Colony formation: Cells were plated at 1,000–2,500/well in 6-well plates and cultured for 10–14 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and quantified using ImageJ. (C) Transwell migration/invasion: For migration, 5–10 × 10⁴ cells in serum-free medium were seeded into Transwell inserts (Corning) in 24-well plates with complete medium. After 48 h, inserts were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and quantified. For invasion, membranes were precoated with Matrigel (200–300 µg/mL) prior to seeding. (D) Wound healing: Confluent cells in 6-well plates were scratched using a pipette tip, washed, and incubated in serum-free medium. The same field was imaged at 0 and 48 h, and closure quantified using ImageJ.

RNA-fluorescence in situ hybridization

RNA-FISH was performed using a circRNA/miRNA FISH kit (Geneseed Biotech, Guangzhou) with a Cy3-labeled TAFCR1 probe. For cell samples, cells on coverslips (70–80% confluence) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 (15 min, room temperature), prehybridized at 37 °C for 1 h, and hybridized with probe at 37 °C for 18–24 h. For FFPE tissue Sect. (5 μm), samples were deparaffinized, rehydrated, treated with proteinase K (10 µg/mL in 10 mM Tris/1 mM EDTA, 37 °C, 20 min), and hybridized at 37 °C for 18–24 h. After hybridization, samples were washed with graded SSC (2×, 1×, and 0.5×) at 40 °C (10 min each), counterstained with DAPI, and imaged by confocal microscopy. RNase A-treated and scramble probe controls were included to confirm RNA-specific hybridization.

RNA pull-down assay

Wild-type and mutant DNA sequences containing the T7 promoter were synthesized as DNA templates. Biotin-labeled RNA was generated by in vitro transcription at 37 °C for 2 h using T7 RNA polymerase and a Biotin-NTP Mix containing Biotin-16-UTP. Residual DNA template was removed by incubation with RNase-free DNase I at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by purification and quantification of the RNA. For RNA pull-down assays, cells were lysed on ice for 10 min in buffer supplemented with RNase and protease inhibitors, then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. An aliquot of supernatant was retained as Input, and the remainder was transferred to an RNase-free tube. Pretreated beads were divided equally into two tubes: RPD (target probe) and NRPD (control probe). Beads were incubated with probes at 4 °C with rotation (10 rpm) for 30 min, followed by addition of cell lysate supernatant and further rotation at 4 °C for 1 h. Beads were collected by brief vortexing (3–4 times), and bound proteins were eluted in 5× SDS-PAGE loading buffer for Western blot or mass spectrometry analysis, alongside Input samples.

Silver staining assay for proteins

Protein bands were visualized using the Protein Silver Stain Kit (Yeasen, Shanghai) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Gels were sequentially treated with fixative, sensitizer, silver stain, developer, and stop solution. Stained bands were photographed and subjected to mass spectrometry analysis.

Mass spectrometry (MS) assay

Mass spectrometry was performed by Guangzhou Geneseed Biotech Co., Ltd. using a Q Exactive™ hybrid quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled to a Dionex Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano UPLC system. Peptides were desalted using Nanosep® Centrifugal Devices (Pall), dissolved in 0.1% formic acid/2% acetonitrile, and loaded onto a reversed-phase C18 column (75 μm × 150 mm, 2 μm, 100 Å). The gradient was 5–50% solvent B (0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile) over 40 min, then increased to 90% in 5 min and held for 5 min at 300 nL/min. Peptides were ionized by nano-ESI at 2.0 kV and analyzed by data-dependent acquisition (one MS scan at 70,000 resolution; 20 MS/MS scans at 17,500 resolution; NCE 27; dynamic exclusion 30 s). Protein identification was performed using MASCOT against the UniProt Human database (release 2023_05).

RNA binding protein immunoprecipitation assay

RIP assays were performed using the PureBinding® RNA Immunoprecipitation Kit (Geneseed Biotech, Guangzhou). Cells were lysed in RIP buffer with protease and RNase inhibitors, centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and supernatant retained as Input. For immunoprecipitation, 5 µg of target antibody or IgG control was prebound to protein A/G beads at 4 °C for 1–2 h, then incubated with cell lysate at 4 °C for 2 h to overnight. After washing, bound RNA was isolated and analyzed by RT-PCR. Relative enrichment was calculated as 2^(−ΔΔCt), where ΔΔCt=(CtIP-CtInput)-(CtIgG-CtInput). The resulting value represents the fold enrichment of target RNA in the IP sample relative to the IgG control, normalized to Input.

Co-immunoprecipitation

IP lysis buffer: 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 250 mM NaCl, 3 mM EDTA, 5 mM EGTA, 0.5% NP-40. For endogenous co-IP, cells were lysed in IP buffer with protease inhibitors at 4 °C for 1 h, centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min, and supernatants retained as Input. Remaining lysate was divided into target antibody (5 µg) and IgG control groups, incubated at 4 °C for 2 h, followed by Protein A/G beads (20 µL, Thermo) overnight. For exogenous co-IP, 293T cells were transfected 48 h prior to lysis; pretreated affinity gel (Anti-Flag M2 or anti-HA) was added directly to lysate and incubated overnight. Beads were washed four times with IP buffer/1 mM PMSF, and proteins eluted in SDS-PAGE loading buffer at 100 °C.

Ubiquitination assay

1% SDS lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 0.5 mM EDTA, 1% SDS) and 0.3% NP-40 dilution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.3% NP-40) were prepared with 1 mM DTT added immediately before use. 293T cells were transiently transfected with Flag-tagged CD44 and HA-tagged ubiquitin plasmids and incubated for 48 h (with 50 µM MG132 added 6 h prior to collection where indicated). Cells were lysed in 1% SDS buffer by boiling at 99 °C, then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant was diluted with 0.3% NP-40 buffer to reduce SDS to 0.1%. An aliquot was retained as Input, and anti-flag M2 affinity gel was added to bind target proteins. After overnight incubation at 4 °C with rotation, Beads were washed three times with 0.3% NP-40 buffer by brief centrifugation and proteins were eluted in 1× loading buffer at 100 °C. Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes for immunoblotting analysis. Ubiquitination levels of target proteins, linkage-specific polyubiquitin chains (K48- and K63-linked), and modification sites (K679/680/681R, K695R, K705R, K715R, K739R) were detected using corresponding antibodies. All experiments were performed with independent biological replicates to ensure reproducibility.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were cultured on confocal dishes or plates as required, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and blocked in rapid blocking solution for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After washing, nuclei were stained with DAPI (Beyotime, Shanghai) for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Fluorescence was visualized using an Olympus inverted fluorescence microscope.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic protein and RNA extraction

Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were prepared using the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai) with 1 mM PMSF and 2 U/µL RNase inhibitor. Cell pellets were resuspended in 200 µL cytoplasmic extraction reagent A, incubated on ice for 10–15 min, then mixed with 10 µL reagent B and centrifuged at 12,000–16,000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant (cytoplasmic fraction) was collected for protein and RNA analysis. Pellets were resuspended in 50 µL nuclear extraction reagent, incubated on ice for 30 min, and centrifuged at 12,000–16,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant (nuclear fraction) was similarly collected. Protein samples were prepared in 5× SDS-PAGE loading buffer at 100 °C; RNA was extracted from cytoplasmic fractions using TRIzol.

Construction of animal model

Four-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (15–18 g; Beijing Vital River) were housed under SPF conditions at the Animal Center of the Institute of Translational Medicine, First Hospital of Jilin University. Animal studies were approved by the Ethics Committee (approval no. JDYY20240244) and conducted in accordance with NIH guidelines. Stably transduced HRT18 and HepG2 cells (shNC and TAFCR1 sh2) were used for xenograft studies. Subcutaneous tumor model: 1 × 10⁶ cells in 100 µL PBS were injected subcutaneously into the left axilla. Tumor dimensions and body weight were measured every 3–4 days. At week 6 or when tumor size reached endpoint, mice were euthanized; tumors were resected, photographed, and weighed. Tumor volume was calculated as (length × width²)/2. Tail vein metastasis model: 1 × 10⁶ cells were injected via tail vein. Body weight was monitored every 4 days. At week 6, one-third of mice were sacrificed to assess lung metastasis; the remaining mice were observed for survival.

Immunohistochemistry

Excised tumors were fixed in 4% formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), CD44, and Ki-67 staining were performed by Wuhan Seville Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Stained sections were examined by light microscopy.

Molecular docking and protein docking

The full-length tertiary structure of TAFCR1 was modeled de novo using the 3dRNA/DNA structure prediction tool based on its RNA sequence, while the complete three-dimensional structures of UBE1, human TRIM21, and CD44 were all predicted using AlphaFold. RNA-binding proteins were predicted using RBPDB (http://rbpdb.ccbr.utoronto.ca). Protein-RNA and protein-protein docking were performed using HDOCK (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn) and ZDOCK (https://zdock.umassmed.edu), respectively, and the resulting complex structures were visualized in PyMOL. Protein-protein restrained docking was performed on the HADDOCK platform, with the intracellular region of CD44 (residues 671–742) specifically designated as the interaction interface for targeted interaction simulation and analysis. Docking scores for the TAFCR1-UBE1 complex were calculated using the iterative scoring functions ITScorePR (for protein-RNA) and ITScorePP (for protein-protein), with confidence scores derived as Confidence_score = 1.0/[1.0 + e^(0.02 × (Docking_Score + 150))]. Complex stability was subsequently assessed using PDBePISA (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/msd-srv/prot_int).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Additional analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel and ImageJ. Group comparisons were assessed by Student’s t-test or one-/two-way ANOVA. ns = not significan, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

Results

Discovery and characterization of cancer metastasis-associated circRNA TAFCR1

Identification and molecular characterization of TAFCR1

FASN is a pivotal enzyme in cancer lipid metabolic reprogramming. To identify FASN regulators in tumors, we analyzed circRNAs by comparing RNA-seq data between hepatocellular carcinoma HepG2 cells and normal hepatic cells [18]. HepG2 cells showed significant enrichment at FASN exons 20, 21, 22, and 23 compared with normal cells (Fig. 1A, red arrows), suggesting a novel circRNA within the FASN locus. To verify this, we designed divergent primers specific to circular transcripts—exon 21 downstream and exon 22 upstream primers—for RT-PCR (Fig. 1B). A single PCR product was detected in HepG2 cells and confirmed by Sanger sequencing as a previously unidentified 787 bp circRNA formed by back-splicing of exons 21, 22, and partial exons 20 and 23 (Fig. 1C). We designated the partial exons as 20 L (long exon 20) and 23 S (short exon 23). RT-PCR sequencing confirmed TAFCR1 formation via back-splicing, consistent with IGV analysis. This circRNA was absent from public circRNA databases, likely due to its tumor-specific upregulation and unique partial back-splicing of exon 23. We named it tumor-associated FASN circular RNA 1 (TAFCR1) to distinguish it from other FASN.

Next, we designed convergent primers for linear FASN mRNA and divergent primers for TAFCR1, using genomic DNA (gDNA) and cDNA as templates. As expected, TAFCR1 was amplified only from cDNA, not gDNA (Fig. 1D). To further validate TAFCR1, cDNA was treated with RNase R prior to PCR. TAFCR1 was resistant to RNase R digestion, whereas linear FASN mRNA was markedly reduced (Figs. 1E, S1A), confirming the higher stability of circular TAFCR1 relative to its linear counterpart.

Elucidation of TAFCR1 circularization mechanisms

To elucidate the looping mechanism of TAFCR1, we examined cis-elements proximal to the back-splicing site. Both exon 20 L and exon 23 S contain a pair of short Alu motifs (GGAGGCTGC) at the cyclization site (Figs. 1 C, S1B). This motif aligns significantly with circRNA consensus sequences [19], suggesting Alu-mediated cyclization (Figs. S1B-S1C). Using RBPDB, we identified MBNL1 as a potential RNA-binding protein interacting with this Alu element (Fig. S1D). MBNL1 is associated with the lymph node metastasis of hepatocellular carcinoma based on TCGA data (Fig. S1E) and can regulate alternative splicing and circRNA biogenesis through repetitive RNA binding [7, 20]. We therefore examined whether MBNL1 promotes TAFCR1 formation.

First, we predicted the Alu-MBNL1 interaction structure using RNAfold and RNAcomposer for secondary/tertiary structure modeling, followed by molecular docking via HDOCK and visualization in PyMOL (Fig. 1F). The docking score (-240.59) and confidence score (0.7320) indicated stable complex formation. Second, RIP assay with MBNL1-specific antibodies confirmed Alu motif enrichment in HepG2 cells compared to IgG control (Fig. 1G). Finally, MBNL1 knockdown by siRNA significantly reduced TAFCR1 levels (Figs. 1H, S1F). Collectively, these findings indicate that MBNL1 facilitates TAFCR1 biogenesis through Alu motifs in exons 20 L and 23 S (Figs. 1 C, S1C).

Expression profiling and subcellular localization of TAFCR1

We examined TAFCR1 expression in tumor cells and patient tissues to further establish its cancer-associated characteristics. First, we performed RT-qPCR across multiple cancer cell lines, including lung, breast, ovarian, pancreatic, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and colorectal cancer (CRC). TAFCR1 was upregulated in all tumor lines, particularly HCC and CRC cells (Fig. S1G). We therefore focused on these two cancer types and analyzed available cell lines (Fig. S1H). TAFCR1 expression was significantly higher in CRC cells (HRT18, HCT116, SW480, LOVO and SW620) and HCC cells (HepG2, Huh7, SMMC-7721 and HCCLM3), normal colorectal epithelial cells (NCM460, HIEC-6), colorectal cancer lines (HRT18, HCT116,) compared to their respective normal counterparts (HIEC-6 and HHL-5), and was not elevated in normal colonic epithelial (NCM460) or hepatic cells (THLE-3, HHL-5) (Fig. 1I). Based on these results, HepG2 and HRT18, which showed the highest TAFCR1 expression, were selected for subsequent experiments.

Additionally, we collected clinical data from 20 colorectal cancer patients and performed RNA-FISH to examine TAFCR1 expression in paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues. TAFCR1 expression was markedly elevated in tumor tissues compared with adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 1J). Patients were subsequently stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on TAFCR1 levels (Fig. 1K). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed a significant difference in median overall survival (OS) between the two groups (54.97 months vs. not reached [NR]; HR: 0.19, 95% CI: 0.05–0.72; P = 0.018) (Fig. 1L), suggesting that high TAFCR1 expression may serve as a biomarker for poorer prognosis in colorectal cancer patients.

To determine TAFCR1 subcellular localization, we performed RT-qPCR on cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions from HepG2 and HRT18 cells, using GAPDH and U6 as controls. TAFCR1 was detected in both compartments (Fig. 1M). RNA-FISH confirmed this dual localization (Figs. 1N, S1I). These data indicate that TAFCR1 is highly stable and significantly upregulated in tumor cells.

TAFCR1 promotes cell proliferation, migration and invasion of tumor cells

To examine TAFCR1 function, we constructed knockdown and overexpression vectors. The knockdown vector contained shRNAs targeting the TAFCR1 circular junction. For overexpression, full-length TAFCR1 cDNA was cloned into the circRNA lentiviral vector pLC5-ciR. HRT18 and HepG2 cells were transduced with lentiviruses carrying shRNAs (TAFCR1sh1, TAFCR1sh2) or TAFCR1 cDNA (TAFCR1-OE), and TAFCR1 levels were verified by RT-qPCR (Fig. 2A). Notably, TAFCR1 manipulation did not affect linear FASN mRNA expression.

We next examined the effect of TAFCR1 on malignant phenotypes, including proliferation, migration, and invasion. CCK-8 and colony formation assays showed that TAFCR1 knockdown significantly inhibited cell proliferation, whereas overexpression enhanced it (Fig. 2B C, S2A). Similarly, wound healing and transwell assays revealed that TAFCR1 knockdown reduced cell migration and invasion, while overexpression promoted these processes (Fig. 2D and E, S2B-S2C). Collectively, these data indicate that TAFCR1 acts as an oncogenic circRNA promoting tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.

TAFCR1 drives tumor metastasis through the CD44-SOX2-EMT pathways

We then investigated the molecular mechanism by which TAFCR1 promotes tumor growth and metastasis. Given that EMT and cell adhesion pathways are critical for tumor invasion and metastasis, we examined EMT-related gene expression by RT-qPCR and Western blot in TAFCR1-modulated HRT18 and HepG2 cells. MMP9, Vimentin, N-cadherin, and FN1 were significantly downregulated upon TAFCR1 knockdown and upregulated upon overexpression at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3A and C). These data suggest that TAFCR1 promotes tumor invasion and metastasis through EMT and MMP-associated pathways.

Screening for regulatory factors in these pathways revealed that stem cell factors CD44 and SOX2 correlate closely with TAFCR1 abundance. CD44, a well-known cancer stem cell marker and transmembrane receptor, plays a critical role in cell-cell interaction, adhesion, and migration [21]. Analysis of TCGA LIHC data showed that CD44 expression affects patient survival and lymph node metastasis (Fig. S3A). CD44 signaling modulates the tumor microenvironment and promotes EMT through cell trafficking, lymph node homing, ECM adhesion, and cytokine coordination [22], and serves as a cancer stem cell marker in breast and prostate cancers [23]. Among stemness-related genes affecting tumor proliferation and metastasis, SOX2 is a key transcription factor maintaining stem cell multipotency [24, 25]. Aberrant SOX2 expression is associated with tumorigenesis, differentiation, metastasis, and poor prognosis, promoting tumor growth through multiple pathways [25, 26]. We therefore examined whether TAFCR1 regulates these factors in HRT18 and HepG2 cells. TAFCR1 knockdown and overexpression significantly altered CD44 protein levels without affecting its mRNA (Fig. 3D and E, S3B), whereas SOX2 was changed at both levels. These data suggest CD44 and SOX2 as downstream targets of TAFCR1.

To test this hypothesis, we knocked down CD44 and examined SOX2 and EMT-related molecule expression. Western blot and RT-qPCR showed that SOX2, EMT markers (Vimentin, N-cadherin, FN1), and MMP9 were consistently downregulated at both protein and mRNA levels in CD44-depleted cells (Fig. 3F and G, S3B). These data support a TAFCR1-CD44-SOX2 axis underlying TAFCR1 function in our model.

Analysis 48 h post-transfection showed that restored TAFCR1 expression rescued CD44, SOX2, and EMT-related proteins (Vimentin, N-cadherin, FN1) as well as MMP9 (Figs. 4B, S4A).

TAFCR1 stabilizes CD44 by antagonizing UBE1-mediated ubiquitination

TAFCR1 inhibits CD44 ubiquitination and degradation

We next investigated how TAFCR1 regulates CD44 protein stability. Since TAFCR1 knockdown or overexpression altered CD44 protein levels without affecting mRNA expression, we examined protein degradation pathways using cycloheximide (CHX, protein synthesis inhibitor), MG132 (proteasome inhibitor), and chloroquine (CQ, autophagy inhibitor). CHX treatment revealed that TAFCR1 knockdown significantly accelerated CD44 protein degradation (Fig. 5A and B), indicating that TAFCR1 protects CD44 from degradation. Notably, MG132 abolished the difference in CD44 degradation between TAFCR1 knockdown and control cells, whereas CQ had no effect. These results suggest that TAFCR1 inhibits CD44 degradation through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway.

To verify this ubiquitination effect, we performed ubiquitination assay in vitro using ubiquitin (Ub) plasmids. Specifically, HA-tagged ubiquitin plasmid (Ub-HA) and related plasmids were transiently transferred into 293T cells, with or without MG132. Enrichment of proteins in cell lysates was performed by affinity gels. HA-tag enriched ubiquitin-proteins were measured by Western blot. In the CD44 ubiquitination assay, the ladder concentration increased when proteasome inhibitor MG132 was added (Fig. 5C). Thus, CD44 can be ubiquitinated and degraded through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Notably, when TAFCR1-OE plasmids were transfected (Relative levels of overexpression of TAFCR1 in 293T cells are shown in Fig. S1G), The ladder concentration showed a decreasing trend. (Fig. 5C). These data suggest that TAFCR1 inhibits the ubiquitination level of the CD44 molecule.

TAFCR1 binds UBE1 to modulate CD44 ubiquitination

We then performed RNA pulldown coupled with mass spectrometry (MS) to identify ubiquitination-related enzymes involved in TAFCR1-mediated CD44 regulation (Fig. S5A). Biotin-labeled TAFCR1 probes were generated by T7 transcription and used to enrich TAFCR1-binding proteins, which were subsequently analyzed by silver staining and mass spectrometry (MS). This analysis identified ubiquitin-activating enzyme 1 (UBE1) (Fig. S5B), an E1 enzyme essential for initiating most cellular ubiquitination reactions [27]. To validate this interaction, we performed RIP assays in HRT18 TAFCR1-OE cells using UBE1-specific antibodies (Figs. 5D, S5C). TAFCR1 enrichment was 10-15-fold higher in UBE1 IP relative to IgG control, confirming TAFCR1-UBE1 interaction.

To clarify this, we investigated the interaction and binding sites between TAFCR1 and UBE1. The molecular docking results are shown in Fig. 5E. Based on the Docking Score (-328.63) and Confidence Score (0.9727), the complex model exhibited stable binding with high reliability. The interacting residues are detailed in Table S3, revealing that the circRNA-protein complex formed 6 electrostatic interactions, 3 hydrogen bonds, and 2 Pi-Sigma interactions. Next, based on the specific binding sites of circRNA, wild-type (target) and mutant probes (Fig. 5F) were designed for RNA pull-down assays. As shown in Fig. 5G, the wild-type probe group containing the target circRNA TAFCR1 successfully pulled down UBE1 protein, whereas the mutant probe group failed to retrieve the target protein. Therefore, we reasonably speculate that the binding sites between TAFCR1 and UBE1 involve multi-site interactions as listed in Table S3, encompassing diverse molecular forces that ensure the stability and specificity of the complex structure.

We subsequently performed co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) to examine CD44-UBE1 interactions in HRT18 and HepG2 cells. CD44 co-precipitated with UBE1 (Figs. 6 A-6B, S6A), and immunofluorescence confirmed their cytoplasmic co-localization (Fig. 6C, S6B). We next performed protein docking analysis using ZDOCK to predict the CD44-UBE1 interaction interface. The complex structure was simulated in PyMOL and evaluated using PDBePISA. The complex exhibited a large interaction area (975.7 Å²) and a negative interface free energy (ΔG = − 9.2 kcal/mol), with a PISA biounit probability of 0.094 (threshold < 0.5), predicting a stable CD44-UBE1 interaction (Figs. S6C-6D). To determine whether this interaction affects CD44 ubiquitination, we performed ubiquitination assays in vitro. Combined with MG132 treatment to block protein degradation. UBE1 overexpression increased CD44 ubiquitination (Fig. 6D, right lane 3), whereas TAFCR1 overexpression reversed this effect (Fig. 6D, right lane 4). These data indicate that UBE1 promotes CD44 ubiquitination, which is antagonized by TAFCR1.

TAFCR1 disrupts the UBE1-CD44 interaction

Does TAFCR1 binding to UBE1 affect the UBE1-CD44 interaction? To address this question, we first analyzed the structural stability of UBE1-TAFCR1 and UBE1-CD44 complexes through molecular dockingand performed quantitative assessment using PDBePISA. Notably, the UBE1-TAFCR1 interaction exhibited a larger interface area (2447.4 Å² vs. 975.7 Å²) and a more negative binding free energy (ΔG = -25.7 vs. -9.2 kcal/mol) (Figs. S6C-6D), reflecting stronger protein-circRNA binding affinity. These findings indicate that TAFCR1 represents a preferred binding partner for UBE1 under physiological conditions. We next verified whether TAFCR1 affects UBE1-CD44 binding in 293T cells using exogenous Co-IP assays. In cells overexpressing TAFCR1, Flag-affinity precipitation of CD44-Flag co-purified significantly less HA-tagged UBE1 compared to controls (Fig. 6E). Conversely, HA-affinity precipitation of UBE1-HA co-purified significantly less CD44-Flag in the TAFCR1-OE group (Fig. 6E).

These results demonstrate that TAFCR1 negatively regulates the interaction between UBE1 and CD44. Notably, UBE1, functioning as an E1 enzyme without intrinsic substrate recognition capacity, is unable to mediate CD44 ubiquitination on its own. We therefore hypothesize that UBE1-CD44 interaction may serve as a prerequisite for CD44 entry into the ubiquitination pathway, poising the substrate in a ubiquitination-competent state. However, the definitive substrate recognition and catalytic execution of ubiquitination are conferred by E3 ligases, which determine target specificity and mediate ubiquitin transfer to the substrate. Identifying the specific E3 ligase responsible for CD44 ubiquitination is therefore essential to fully elucidate the regulatory mechanism underlying TAFCR1-mediated CD44 stabilization.

TRIM21 acts as an E3 ubiquitin ligase mediating K48-linked CD44 ubiquitination

To identify the functional E3 component responsible for direct CD44 recognition and ubiquitination, we performed immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry (IP-MS) using CD44 as bait. MS analysis revealed significant enrichment of TRIM21, a RING domain-containing E3 ubiquitin ligase. To investigate whether TRIM21 regulates ubiquitination of CD44 protein, we co-transfected CD44-Flag, HA-Ub, and TRIM21-Myc plasmids into 293T cells, and detected the ubiquitination level of CD44 through co-immunoprecipitation combined with Western blot. The results demonstrated that TRIM21 interacted with CD44 in cells, and overexpression of TRIM21 significantly enhanced the ubiquitination signal of CD44 (Anti-HA smear) (Fig. 6F), indicating that TRIM21 binds to CD44 and promotes its ubiquitination modification.

This finding suggests that UBE1, CD44, and TRIM21 operate within the same ubiquitination axis, with UBE1 governing ubiquitin activation and TRIM21 executing substrate recognition and transfer. Although TRIM21 has been implicated in the regulation of diverse cancer substrates [28, 29], its direct ubiquitination of CD44 remains uncharacterized. Based on this proteomic evidence, we posit that TRIM21 constitutes the cognate E3 enzyme mediating CD44 ubiquitination. To validate this, we co-transfected CD44-Flag, TRIM21-Myc, and ubiquitin mutants (HA-Ub-WT, HA-Ub-K48-only [K48O], or HA-Ub-K63-only [K63O]) into 293T cells, treated with MG132 and Bafilomycin A1 to block degradation, and performed anti-Flag immunoprecipitation followed by Western blot. CD44 exhibited strong ubiquitination with HA-Ub-WT and HA-Ub-K48-only, whereas the HA-Ub-K63-only signal was markedly attenuated. These results demonstrate that TRIM21 specifically catalyzes K48-linked, rather than K63-linked, ubiquitination of CD44 (Fig. 6G).

CD44 is a transmembrane protein composed of an extracellular domain (ECD), a transmembrane domain (TMD), and an intracellular domain (ICD). A recent study demonstrated that proteolytic cleavage releases the ICD, which then translocates to the nucleus to activate gene expression and promote tumor metastasis [23]. Given the cytoplasmic localization of TRIM21 and the progressively recognized functional importance of the ICD segment, we further investigated the specific role of TRIM21 in regulating CD44-ICD. To identify the lysine residue(s) essential for TRIM21-mediated K48-linked ubiquitination of CD44, we generated a series of CD44-ICD lysine-to-arginine single mutants and a K679/680/681R triple mutant (Fig. 6H), and performed immunoprecipitation assays in 293T cells. Overexpression of TRIM21 significantly enhanced K48 ubiquitination of wild-type CD44. Notably, the K695R mutation, but not other single mutations, markedly attenuated TRIM21-induced CD44 ubiquitination, indicating that K695 is the critical functional site for TRIM21-mediated K48-linked ubiquitination of CD44 (Fig. 6I).

Knockdown or inhibition of UBE1 increases expression of CD44 and downstream SOX2 and EMT-related proteins

Consistently, treatment with TAK-243, a mechanism-based E1 inhibitor, dose-dependently reduced UBE1 expression while increasing CD44, SOX2, and EMT-related proteins (Fig. 7D-E), confirming UBE1’s critical role in CD44 ubiquitination and degradation.

In the lung metastasis model, 4–6-week-old immunocompromised BALB/c nude mice received tail vein injection of HRT18 or HepG2 cells stably expressing shNC or TAFCR1-sh2 (n = 12 per group; Fig. 9E). Body weight was monitored regularly. At week 6, four mice (1/3) from each group were randomly selected for lung metastasis assessment, while the remaining mice (2/3) were used for survival analysis. TAFCR1 knockdown significantly reduced lung metastasis in the HRT18 group (p < 0.001; Fig. 9F).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that TAFCR1 knockdown significantly prolonged survival compared to controls (Fig. 9G). Collectively, these findings establish TAFCR1 as an oncogenic circRNA that promotes tumorigenesis and metastasis in vivo, and demonstrate that its depletion impairs tumor progression and improves survival.

Discussion

The FASN enzyme is a central regulator of lipid metabolism critical for the growth and survival of tumors with lipogenic phenotypes [4]. Here, we identified TAFCR1, a novel exonic circRNA embedded within the FASN gene locus. TAFCR1 comprises exons 21, 22, and portions of exons 23 and 20. Distinct from conventional circRNAs, TAFCR1 is generated through base-pairing between short Alu motifs located in exons 23 S and 20 L, with MBNL1 serving as a critical trans-acting factor in this cyclization process.

TAFCR1 is significantly upregulated in hepatocellular carcinoma and colorectal cancer, promoting tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, TAFCR1 functions as an RNA regulator that interacts with UBE1 to negatively modulate the UBE1-CD44 interaction, thereby impairing TRIM21-mediated substrate recognition and ubiquitin-dependent degradation of CD44. As two critical enzymes governing CD44 ubiquitination-dependent degradation, UBE1 and TRIM21 cooperatively control CD44 expression levels in tumor cells. By antagonizing this process, TAFCR1 stabilizes CD44, leading to accumulation of this cancer stem cell marker. Elevated CD44 subsequently activates the PI3K/AKT pathway via suppression of the tumor suppressor PHLPP1, ultimately upregulating SOX2 and EMT/MMPs to drive HCC and CRC progression (Fig. 10). Collectively, our findings establish TAFCR1 as a UBE1-docking circRNA that controls CD44 stability, and identify the TAFCR1-UBE1-CD44 axis as a promising therapeutic target for liver and colorectal cancers.

Cluster of differentiation 44 (CD44), a cancer stem cell marker, regulates tumor metastasis and maintains tumor stemness. CD44 is aberrantly upregulated in various malignancies, including breast cancer [30], lung adenocarcinoma [31], colorectal cancer [32], prostate cancer [33], and renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [34], contributing to tumorigenesis and cancer progression [21]. As a stem cell-associated protein, CD44 maintains cancer stem cell populations in breast and prostate adenocarcinomas [23, 35] and modulates cell adhesion, tumor cell metastasis, and signal transduction pathways [36] through EMT-related molecules, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [37], cycle-related proteins [38], glycolysis-related genes [39], the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [38], and the PI3K-Akt pathway [40]. EMT is widely recognized as a key driver of metastasis formation [41, 42], with CD44-mediated EMT regulation being essential for tumor invasion. Increasing evidence indicates shared molecular features between tumor stemness and EMT, wherein cancer cells acquire stem-like properties through EMT that facilitate metastasis [43, 44]. For instance, SOX2 overexpression promotes metastasis in breast and prostate cancers via EMT [45, 46], establishing the SOX2-EMT axis as central to invasion and metastasis in multiple cancers [25, 47]. These correlations underscore the need to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms linking CD44, tumor stemness, EMT, and tumor progression.

Our data demonstrate that TAFCR1 critically regulates intracellular CD44 at the post-transcriptional level, significantly affecting CD44 protein abundance without altering its mRNA expression. Using small-molecule inhibitors and ubiquitination assays, we confirmed that CD44 undergoes ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS)-mediated degradation [48]. As the primary protein degradation pathway, UPS governs diverse biological processes including proteostasis, cell cycle regulation, DNA repair, and transcriptional control [49–54]. TAFCR1 substantially modulates CD44 ubiquitination, suggesting that the TAFCR1-CD44 axis represents a promising therapeutic target for cancer intervention.

How does TAFCR1 affect CD44 ubiquitination and protein expression? Increasing evidence indicates that circRNAs harbor protein-binding sites. Cytoplasmic circRNAs can function as protein chaperones or form circRNA-protein complexes to regulate target protein expression and biological functions [55–57]. Previous studies have demonstrated critical mechanisms by which circRNAs interact with RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) to influence disease progression. For instance, circMTO1 binds TRAF4 and prevents its interaction with Eg5, thereby inhibiting Eg5 translation [58]. Similarly, circACTN4 directly binds FUBP1 in breast cancer, blocking the FUBP1-FIR interaction and activating MYC transcription to promote cancer progression [59]. Studies have also preliminarily explored how circRNAs affect ubiquitination-mediated degradation by binding ubiquitin-related proteins. For instance, circWSB1 is substantially upregulated in breast cancer and competitively binds the deubiquitinating enzyme USP10, hindering the USP10-p53 interaction and leading to p53 degradation and cancer progression [60]. Additionally, emerging evidence demonstrates that circRNAs can influence substrate ubiquitination by regulating UBE1-protein interactions [61, 62].

In our model, TAFCR1 binds UBE1 through a cooperative network of multiple binding sites, involving 6 electrostatic interactions, 3 hydrogen bonds, and 2 Pi-Sigma interactions. This multi-valent binding mode ensures both the thermodynamic stability and binding specificity of the circRNA-protein complex, minimizing off-target interactions. Through this interaction, TAFCR1 negatively modulates the inducible UBE1-CD44 interaction. Given that UBE1 alone cannot mediate substrate-specific ubiquitination, we hypothesize that its inducible interaction with CD44 serves to license the substrate for entry into the ubiquitination pathway, establishing a ubiquitination-competent configuration. TAFCR1 may impinge upon CD44 ubiquitination through two non-mutually exclusive mechanisms: (i) Allosteric regulation: induction of conformational changes in UBE1 that modulate the UBE1-CD44 interaction while suppressing ubiquitin activation and impairing downstream cascade propagation, ultimately attenuating E3-mediated substrate ubiquitination and stabilizing CD44; (ii) Competitive inhibition: direct competition with CD44 for UBE1 binding, thereby precluding effective substrate engagement at the entry point of the ubiquitination cascade and rendering E3 ligase-bound CD44 catalytically inert. Future studies are needed to investigate these two mechanisms in more detail. Nonetheless, our findings underscore the important role of circRNA-UBE1 interactions in tumor development and warrant future investigation.

Despite these challenges, we identified the E3 ligase TRIM21 as the key mediator of CD44 ubiquitination. TRIM21 binds to the CD44 intracellular domain (CD44-ICD) and catalyzes K48-linked polyubiquitination of CD44 at K695. This finding is essential to fully elucidate the regulatory mechanism of TAFCR1-mediated CD44 stabilization. Importantly, this study identifies UBE1 and TRIM21 as two critical enzymes governing CD44 ubiquitination-dependent degradation, which cooperatively regulate CD44 expression levels in tumor cells. Future investigations are warranted to dissect the molecular basis underlying UBE1-CD44 and TRIM21-CD44 interactions, and delineate the complete UBE1-E2-TRIM21 ubiquitination cascade.

Another key objective of our study was to investigate the role of CD44 in tumor development. Previous studies have shown that invasive and metastatic tumors overexpress stem cell-specific markers, including CD44, NANOG, SOX2, and OCT4 [63]. Although the critical roles of CD44 and SOX2 in regulating EMT during tumor progression have been established, their underlying mechanisms remain largely unclear. Here, we delineated the interrelationship among CD44, SOX2, and EMT. We show that CD44-mediated inhibition of the tumor suppressor PHLPP1 represents a plausible mechanism for PI3K/AKT pathway activation. This activation subsequently upregulates SOX2 and promotes the expression of EMT-related proteins (vimentin, N-cadherin, and FN1) and MMP9, ultimately enhancing tumor proliferation, invasion, and metastasis.

The PI3K/AKT pathway is frequently hyperactivated in various human cancers and plays a pivotal role in cancer stemness [64–67]. CD44 has also been reported to promote HAS2 expression through PI3K/AKT activation, which further enhances CD44-mediated PI3K/AKT signaling, creating a positive feedback loop that drives tumor cell resistance and survival [68]. While our data support a functional link where CD44 suppresses PHLPP1 to sustain PI3K/AKT signaling, we acknowledge that epistasis analyses or genetic rescue experiments were not performed. Thus, whether PHLPP1 is the necessary and sufficient mediator of CD44-regulated AKT phosphorylation requires further validation. Nevertheless, evidence regarding the relationship between circRNAs and PI3K/AKT signaling in HCC and CRC remains limited, and our study delineates a comprehensive regulatory axis: TAFCR1-CD44-PI3K/AKT-SOX2, which plays a critical role in tumor development. Importantly, we identify TAFCR1 as a key upstream regulator of CD44 that controls the activity of this axis in cancer metastasis. Therefore, targeting the TAFCR1-CD44 axis may offer novel therapeutic strategies for cancer treatment.

Our study introduces a novel concept: the FASN gene possesses dual pathway functions through its canonical FASN protein and non-canonical TAFCR1 circRNA. Previous studies have established that the FASN gene participates in tumor lipid metabolism, oncogenic signaling, and immune regulation within the tumor microenvironment via FASN enzyme activity [1, 4]. However, FASN protein is widely expressed across various normal cells, including immune cells, presenting a significant challenge for targeted therapeutic development. This study demonstrates that the FASN gene may also contribute to cancer metastasis through a non-canonical circRNA pathway. TAFCR1 is generated from back-splicing of FASN gene exons and functions through a dual mechanism. First, TAFCR1 acts as an RNA regulator by binding UBE1, thereby modulating substrate protein ubiquitin-proteasome degradation—a non-canonical function distinct from the classical enzymatic activity of FASN protein. Second, we report for the first time that TAFCR1 regulates the cancer stemness markers CD44 and SOX2, an effect not previously attributed to FASN protein. By modulating CD44 expression, TAFCR1 subsequently influences SOX2, EMT-related proteins, and MMPs, thereby promoting tumor proliferation and metastasis. Critically, TAFCR1 exhibits cancer-specific expression, unlike the ubiquitous FASN protein. Thus, this unique mechanism governing protein post-translational modification and tumor stemness regulation may provide a precise therapeutic target for cancer treatment, warranting further investigation.

While the present study establishes TAFCR1 as a critical regulator of metastasis through the UBE1-TRIM21-CD44 and PI3K/AKT-SOX2 axes, we acknowledge that our findings remain at the mechanistic and preclinical level. An expanded study with larger animal samples would further strengthen the statistical power of the data in the in vivo findings. The therapeutic efficacy of combining TAFCR1 inhibition with UBE1 or CD44 inhibitors, as well as the optimization of dosing regimens and assessment of potential toxicities, awaits future investigation. Notably, the UBE1 inhibitor TAK-243 has demonstrated a tolerable safety profile in phase I trials (NCT02045095), though its clinical efficacy in solid tumors remains limited. CD44-targeted strategies, including antibody-, small-molecule-, and peptide-based approaches, are currently under preclinical development for solid tumors. Nevertheless, the TAFCR1-UBE1/CD44-PI3K/AKT axis identified herein provides a rationale for combination strategies, laying the groundwork for future therapeutic applications integrating TAFCR1 targeting with UBE1 or CD44 inhibitors.
