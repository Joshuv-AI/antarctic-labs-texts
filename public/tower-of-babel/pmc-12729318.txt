Enhancing socioemotional competencies: protocol for a three-arm cluster randomized controlled trial of a daily program for third graders and educators
Authors: Barbara Hanisch-Cerda, Diego Benitez-Yavar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOver the past two decades, there has been a growing emphasis on noncognitive skills, particularly socioemotional competence (SEC), in educational research. The SEC encompasses personal characteristics such as motivation, personality traits, preferences, and values, which are shaped by both socialization and genetic influences. Socioemotional learning (SEL) is recognized as a key process through which individuals develop these competencies, impacting their academic and societal outcomes. The significance of the SEC is highlighted by neurobiological findings linking environmental factors to cognitive and behavioral outcomes, as well as economic research showing its positive effects on educational attainment, labor productivity, and civic participation.MethodsThis research aims to evaluate the impact of a novel SEL program on third- and fourth-grade students and their teachers using a three-arm cluster randomized controlled trial. The program includes a daily manualized 10-min curriculum for students and an additional professional development component for teachers. The study’s objectives include examining its effects on students’ and teachers’ SEC and analyzing the program’s efficiency. The study will involve 30 schools in Chile that are randomly assigned to either the SEL curriculum, the curriculum with professional development, or a control group. The data will be collected through surveys at baseline, post-intervention, and follow-up.DiscussionThe evidence in the field shows almost no impact evaluations of programs supporting the socioemotional competencies of children in Chile and almost no evaluations of programs supporting the socioemotional development of teachers. This study will provide evidence for the effectiveness of an SEL program for children in the 3rd and 4th grades and their teachers.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT06515444. Registered on July 22, 2024.

Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Over the last 20 years, there has been increasing emphasis on studying noncognitive skills or socioemotional skills in educational research. These terms are widely used to cover a wide range of personal characteristics, such as motivation, personality traits, preferences, and values [28]. Some common characteristics are that they are not measured by typical cognitive ability tests; they are shaped partially by socialization and learning and partially by genetic influences; and they manifest in relatively consistent behavioral, cognitive, and affective patterns [26, 53]. A more applied and in-context reference to the terms is socioemotional competence (SEC hereafter), defined as “the capacity to coordinate cognition, affect and behavior so that individuals can thrive in diverse cultures and contexts and achieve specific tasks and positive developmental outcomes” [32]. There is wide consensus that inter- and intrapersonal skills and attitudes are involved. The Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL) has identified five core socioemotional competence clusters: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making.

The science of learning and economics of education has increased the relevance of socioemotional competence both for learning and other societal outcomes. Figure 1 presents the interplay between these core themes. Paths A and B illustrate the impact of socioemotional competence on learning and other societal outcomes. Findings from neurobiology present evidence to substantiate path A. They have illuminated how environmental factors such as poverty, exposure to stress, or experiencing trauma affect children’s development of brain systems that support neurocognitive functions such as language, memory, and self-regulation and therefore affect their behavior, lifelong learning, and health [9, 22, 27, 39, 50, 52]. Findings from the economics of education argue for path B. Their evidence consistently indicates positive relationships between noncognitive skills and societal outcomes such as educational attainment [16, 19, 30], labor productivity and adult earnings [8, 20, 36], and civic participation [1].Fig. 1Interplay between SEC and learning and societal outcomes (adapted by authors from Lechner, Anger & Rammstedt, 2019)

Knowledge of the impact of socioemotional competence on learning and other societal outcomes has led educators to pursue new strategies to support the social and emotional competence of children as well as their academic learning. Socioemotional learning (SEL hereafter; path C in Fig. 1) is the process through which individuals acquire socioemotional competence, the “process through which all young people and adults acquire and apply the knowledge, skills, and attitudes to develop healthy identities, manage emotions and achieve personal and collective goals, feel and show empathy for others, establish and maintain supportive relationships, and make responsible and caring decisions” [38].

Although there is no clear consensus about what social and emotional skills and attitudes are most important, how they should be fostered, and how they should be measured [6], SEL programs are proliferating worldwide [2, 14, 47]. This is now more than ever due to the COVID-19 pandemic. The emergence of mental health needs in children, young people, and adults has reached a crisis [21, 55]. SEL programs are increasingly recognized as valuable tools for enhancing students’ emotional and behavioral health and preventing mental health issues from developing.

SEL programs refer to curricula organized into learning experiences sequenced in such a way that they respectfully consider the development of individuals from childhood to adulthood and focus on developing the socioemotional competence of children and adults in relationally healthy places [14]. Addressing one or more domains: cognitive, emotional, social, value, perspective, or identity [6]. SEL programs can have different shapes and targets: stand-alone interventions, integrated approaches between SELs and academics, systemic approximations within schools, individual targeting and others considering the whole community, some focused on students and others on employees, and some led by school counselors and others by teachers or SEL facilitators ([2, 14].

With the increase in SEL programs, research evidence is also expanding in terms of short- and long-term impacts, equity, and efficiency. There is enough evidence to demonstrate that social and emotional skills are malleable and teachable, both in formal and informal educational settings [25]. The meta-analysis of Durlak and colleagues reviews the impact of 213 studies involving school-based universal SEL programs that include over 270,000 students in K-12. The meta-analysis suggested that children who participate in SEL programs significantly improve their social and emotional skills; improve their attitudes about themselves, others, and the school; improve their classroom behavior; improve their academic performance; and reduce their conduct problems and emotional distress [13]. This evidence is consistent with the findings of a meta-analysis published by Sklad and colleagues 1 year later [48]. A meta-analysis of the follow-up effects of 82 different SEL programs, which included more than 97,000 students, revealed that the impacts on academic performance, emotional and social skills, and attitudes are long-lasting and global [49]. This same study presented evidence that the benefits of the programs were the same regardless of the socioeconomic background of the children, the students’ race, and the school location. The value of an SEL program should also be viewed in terms of the relationship between the benefits it generates and the costs it has. An economic evaluation of six SEL programs revealed that the programs were efficient, with a very high benefit–cost ratio of 11:1, i.e., 11 dollars in return for every dollar spent [4]. Research on the impact of SEL on students’ SEC and academic achievement is strongly supported by causal identification strategies, with several exemplary randomized controlled trials. Nevertheless, such research is not without limitations, as studies based on relatively small and specific groups make it difficult to generalize to larger populations and may not be replicable.

While research on the impact of SEL programs on students’ outcomes is well established, research on the impact of SEL programs on teacher outcomes is still limited. Teachers with high SEC are more likely to stay in the classroom longer than those with low SEC. Specifically, teachers who perceive themselves as being able to develop and manage nurturing relationships with their students, who serve as role models for children, and who regulate their emotions are better able to protect themselves from burnout [23]. Research on the impact of SEL on teachers is mainly correlational, limiting the ability of these studies to make causal claims regarding the link between SEL programs and teacher SEC.

In addition to the impact of SEL programs on children and teachers and their equity and efficiency, follow-up inquiries are needed to determine the characteristics of SEL programs that have proven to be effective. A review of the literature on SEL programs for students aged 3 to 8 years undertaken by the Institute of Educational Sciences [40] identified three key components of effective SEL programs: (i) they use a combination of techniques focused on skills and the environment, meaning that the skills are not only taught in the classroom but also that those targeted skills are vertically and horizontally aligned with the curriculum, school policies, and other school regulations and practices; (ii) there is a sequenced, active, focused, and explicit program to develop skills in the classroom; and (iii) training and technical assistance for teachers is provided. The content of effective SEL programs includes strategies for a positive classroom climate (modifying physical space, management strategies, and supporting emotionally positive environments); instructional strategies that promote SEL, such as modeling, reactions to students’ emotions and interactions, and explicit teaching about emotions and relationships; and strategies to build SEC in teachers, such as direct training, supervision, relationship building, and stress-reduction techniques [12, 24, 41].

Trial design {8}

This project is a quantitative study developed to evaluate whether the 10-min curriculum and an expanded version with professional development activities for teachers or counselors, 10-min plus, in the third grade would improve students’ and teachers’ socioemotional competence. This is a randomized controlled trial (school-randomized) design because it is an appropriate and robust research technique [37]. The study design involves 30 schools in Chile, matched on socioeconomic measures, size, and urban/rural conditions, which will be assigned randomly to 1 of 3 conditions: (a) Ten schools will receive only the 10-min SEL curriculum taught by teachers or school counselors during one school year. (b) Ten schools will receive 10-min plus during one school year. (c) Ten schools will serve as “delayed program” controls and will have the opportunity to receive the program (10-min plus) after the final follow-up. To examine the durability of the effects of the SEL intervention on students, the first experimental condition (10-min only) will be randomly divided into two groups: one will receive the intervention for 1 year only, whereas the second will receive the intervention for two consecutive years.

Surveys will be used to collect data on student, classroom, and teacher outcomes in the 30 schools in fall of year 1 (baseline), spring of year 1 (first follow-up), and fourth-grade students in the same schools in spring of year 2 (final follow-up). Who will take informed consent? {26a}

Schools will be evaluated for eligibility to participate in the study based on the criteria outlined above. Once deemed eligible, the principals of the selected schools will receive detailed information about the study. After the school authorities agree to participate and sign a form, teachers will be invited to participate in a 1:1 meeting where the study and intervention will be explained. After the teachers agree, parents of the students will be notified about the study and informed that the 10-min program will be incorporated into the school curriculum. Finally, students will be informed about the study and asked to provide their assent by signing a form confirming their willingness to participate.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

To evaluate the effectiveness of the 10-min program, the control group will serve as a comparison group. They will continue with their standard “Orientation” class, which follows the Ministry of Education’s curriculum and covers topics like socioemotional learning. This allows us to determine if the 10-min program provides any benefits beyond the typical “Orientation” class instruction. Both groups will receive the “Orientation” class; the 10-min program is an added element for the intervention group.

Intervention description {11a}

The 10-min program offers third- and fourth-grade manualized curricula and professional development to help transform schools by strengthening students’ and teachers’ socioemotional competence. It encompasses two components: a manualized curriculum focused on children’s SEC and professional development focused on teachers’ SEC.

The curriculum component offers 10-min daily doses of activities targeting the development of eight socioemotional competences in children: (i) emotional knowledge and management, (ii) developing a healthy identity, (iii) establishing and maintaining positive relationships, (iv) feeling and showing empathy, (v) establishing and reaching goals, (vi) responsible decision-making, (vii) contributing to the community, and (viii) developing spirituality. This is done through evidence-based activities. The activities do not require the teacher to plan or prepare material for the students. Each activity is accompanied by guidelines for the teacher to develop such socioemotional competences in children through that activity. If the activities require any specification about including children with special needs, those are included in the guidelines.

The activities are presented in cards (Fig. 2 presents the unpublished version of 10-min for students from prekindergarten to 2nd grade), which the teacher can randomly select from a box available in the classroom. Along with each activity, on the other side of the card, the teacher is provided with brief information about how the activity develops specific SECs based on evidence. This box with activities can be used in one, two, or more years with the students (up to 4 years where the level of the activities is appropriate). The program is designed to be implemented on (almost) every school day throughout the academic year. Specifically, the curriculum includes 180 unique activities, enabling teachers to select one per day over the course of a full school year (approximately 180 days).Fig. 2Photo montage 10 min of material (version prek-2; unpublished)

Photo montage 10 min of material (version prek-2; unpublished)

While the structure encourages daily use, flexibility is built into the program: teachers may adjust implementation to fit their classroom schedule or student needs. At the same time, implementation fidelity will be monitored through teacher self-reports to ensure consistent use and to document any adaptations across contexts.

The rationale of this component is that Chilean teachers admit that they do not have the required skills and tools to address students’ SEC [5], that their preservice training does not include specific knowledge and competencies on children’s SEC or its development [34], and that when teachers are asked, the most frequent professional development training needed is on the SEC [3]. Furthermore, teachers do not have enough time to plan a class, implement, generate material, or evaluate the SEC of students (Cabezas et al., 2017), and the current available time is limited to 0.5 weekly hours in the orientation class. In addition to the mental health crisis that children and teachers are facing, we need to find a fast easy-to-scale solution to teach both students and teachers simultaneously to help address the mental health problems of students and teachers.

The professional development (PD) component is a 1-h dose of activities targeting each of the eight SECs on which the curriculum focuses. The focus of PD is not to teach about SEC development in children but to develop SEC in teachers. Thus, this is an 8-h PD program distributed throughout the academic year.

The rationale of this component is that it is difficult for a teacher to teach a child SEC if they score low in such SEC.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

Students in the intervention group, as the program is integrated into the school curriculum, will participate in all program sessions. While they are required to remain in the classroom during these sessions, they are free to withdraw from the study at any time and for any reason, without facing any consequences. If they choose to withdraw, their information and collected data will be excluded from analysis. Meanwhile, the control group will maintain its status throughout the duration of the trial.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

Adherence to the 10-min program will be monitored by research assistants from the research team. Teachers will complete a monthly survey providing information on student attendance and the quality of program delivery. Research assistants will maintain close communication with school authorities and teachers, regularly monitoring the program’s progress through study visits.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

The exclusion criterion specifies that daily manualized interventions cannot be implemented in control schools. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of the 10-min program compared to the business-as-usual (BAU) approach in Chile. As noted, the typical practice does not involve the implementation of daily manualized interventions.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

This item is not applicable, as the trial does not involve clinical risk or potential for adverse effects. However, all participating schools will be offered access to the manualized curriculum after the end of the trial. Specifically, schools in the control group will be invited to implement the intervention once they have completed the final follow-up assessment. The materials will remain available for continued use beyond the trial period.

Outcomes {12}

The primary outcome of the trial at the student level is student’s behavior as measured by the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) developed by Goodman (1997) at the post-intervention timepoints: the end of the first school year (9 months after baseline), which is the primary endpoint, and the end of the second school year (21 months after baseline). The SDQ evaluates internalizing and externalizing behavior problems as well as prosocial behavior. The version for children aged 9–15 was validated in Chile by Gaete et al. [18], with internal consistency fluctuating between 0.76 and 0.85. The SDQ was selected as the primary outcome because it aligns with the main objectives of the 10-min intervention and was used to calculate the required sample size.

The primary outcome of the trial at the teacher level is the teacher’s socioemotional competency as measured by the Socioemotional Competence Inventory. This inventory was developed by Mikulic and colleagues [33] (reliability = 0.89). This is a 72-item inventory that assesses self-efficacy, optimism, emotional regulation, assertiveness, emotional expression, emotional awareness, empathy, prosociality, and autonomy.

Sample size {14}

Power analyses were conducted to estimate a suitable sample size. The starting point for calculations was a two-level model: school and students. It was assumed that 1–5 classes would participate per school, classes would contain 20 students, the recruitment rate would be 70%, and the attrition rate would be 10% in the follow-up measures of year 2. Based on these assumptions, a total of 12 to 63 students per school would participate. The intracluster correlation coefficient between schools for one of the outcome measures was assumed to be 0.15, as there is evidence for the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) already validated with Chilean students [17, 18]. The effect size between the intervention and control groups was expected to be 0.25 or 0.3, in line with empirical evidence that shows effect sizes ranging from small to moderate [14]. In a two-level design using a significance level of 0.05 and requiring 80% power, a sample size ranging between 600 and 750 students is reasonable, which, in terms of schools, corresponds to a sample that ranges between 28 and 53. For the sample calculation, Optimal Design Software [45] was used.

While some studies have reported positive effects of SEL interventions on teacher outcomes, the evidence base remains limited and heterogeneous [23, 47]. Therefore, precise sample size estimates for teacher-level effects are not feasible at this time. Teacher socioemotional competence is included as a co-primary outcome to be explored within the available sample, acknowledging the importance of these outcomes despite the lack of robust prior effect size estimates.

Recruitment {15}

To ensure adequate school enrollment and achieve the target sample size, we will employ a multi-pronged approach. This includes collaborating with municipal authorities to facilitate contact with schools within their jurisdiction and direct outreach by research assistants to school authorities, providing detailed information about the study’s objectives, requirements, and timeline. Our established collaborative relationships with municipalities and schools in Chile will significantly facilitate this recruitment process.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Following the successful recruitment of participating schools within each municipality and the acquisition of all necessary consents from teachers, parents, and students, a baseline evaluation will be conducted. Subsequently, randomization will be carried out independently within each municipality. Schools will be assigned equally across the three trial arms (curriculum only, curriculum plus, and control) using a simple computer-generated randomization sequence in Microsoft Excel. This procedure ensures balance within each municipality while accommodating the staggered rollout of recruitment across municipalities.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

Allocation concealment was maintained by separating the recruitment and randomization processes. Local authorities, working with designated research team members, were responsible for recruiting schools and collecting consents. The team member who conducted the randomization was not involved in recruitment and conducted the randomization only after all participating schools in a municipality had completed baseline data collection.

Implementation {16c}

After randomization, research assistants informed schools of their group assignment via email and/or telephone. Group assignments will be maintained confidentially to the data analyst.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

This is a single-blind trial. Outcome assessors and data analysts will be blinded to group assignments. Outcome assessors will not be informed of school allocation and will be instructed not to inquire about group status during assessments. The data analyst will receive a masked dataset with no group identifiers. Participants (schools, teachers, students) will be aware of their allocation due to the nature of the intervention, which does not allow blinding.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

This trial is open label for schools and participants, with blinding restricted to outcome assessors and data analysts. Unblinding is not feasible in this context. However, the nature of the intervention necessitates participant awareness of their school’s allocation.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

Instruments will be administered to students, parents, and teachers according to standardized protocols. Research assistants will receive training on the administration and scoring of all instruments. Parents and teachers’ data will be collected electronically to minimize data entry errors. Students’ data will be collected manually, and the instrument will be read aloud by the research assistant and will be filled out by students paced by the research assistant, to minimize the risk of having children not understanding the questions and/or not responding to them. Data entry will be double/checked for accuracy.

All the instruments that have not been validated in Chile will need to be contextualized for the Chilean population. To achieve this, a seven-step contextualization process will be carried out (Oxford MeasurEd, 2022): 1. Convene a focus group (psychometrician, subject matter experts, individuals familiar with the students and their context). 2. The focus group reviews the items and decides which elements should be discarded, edited, or retained. 3. The focus group considered whether the items were relevant to the students’ experience (i.e., terminology used, scenarios, expressions). 4. Develop administration plans and processes. 5. The focus group results were analyzed to produce contextualized/adapted instruments. 6. Qualitative piloting of the instruments was conducted with respondents. All these steps will be done before baseline evaluation. 7. The instruments will be quantitatively piloted. There may be iterations back to step 5 to review and make modifications if necessary.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

During the recruitment process, schools, students, and their families will be thoroughly informed about the study’s structure and requirements, with a strong emphasis on the importance of completing follow-up activities. From the start of the 10-min assessments and activities, teachers will receive regular reminders about the value of their active participation throughout the project. Throughout the follow-up period, researchers will track responses and, if necessary, reach out to schools and participants to ensure follow-up activities are completed.

Data management and confidentiality {19, 27}

Secure storage of data: All data from teachers and parents will be collected through Google Forms and linked to a restricted-access Google Drive account. For child participants, data will be collected using paper-based assessments administered by research assistants.

After completion, paper forms will be double-entered into Stata (version SE 19.5) by two independent research assistants to ensure accuracy. Discrepancies between entries will be automatically flagged and resolved through comparison with the original paper copy.

Each participant will be assigned an encrypted ID number to anonymize responses. Original paper copies of the instruments, along with the list linking participant names to ID numbers, will be securely filed and stored under lock and key in a self-storage facility. After the study is completed, all data will continue to be securely stored, and identifying information will be destroyed according to a pre-determined schedule outlined in the research protocol.

Encrypted ID: All data will be stored using a unique study identification code for each participant. The key linking participants to their identification codes will only be accessible to the designated research team, including the principal investigator, research assistants, and the statistician.

Controlled access to the database: Access to the database will be restricted to the principal investigator, research assistants responsible for data entry, and the statistician. All collected data (whether from Google Forms or paper-based assessments) will be compiled and processed using Stata for statistical analysis.

Signed confidentiality agreements: All individuals with access to the dataset will be required to sign a confidentiality agreement to ensure the protection of identifying information.

No identifiable data in publications: Identifiable participant information will not appear in any publications or study reports.

Clear informed consent: The informed consent process will clearly describe confidentiality risks, explain who will have access to the data, outline the circumstances under which data may be shared, and detail the measures in place to protect participant privacy.

Plans for collection, laboratory evaluation, and storage of biological specimens for genetic or molecular analysis in this trial/future use {33}

Not applicable. No biological specimens will be collected in this trial.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

The basic research design compares students in the treatment and control groups, and the randomization ensures that the assignment of schools to the treatment group is independent of the school and student characteristics. Intervention adherence and delivery fidelity will be monitored through monthly teacher surveys. Teachers in the intervention arms will complete short questionnaires at the end of each month, reporting on the number of 10-min activities implemented, perceived student engagement, any adaptations made, and contextual factors affecting delivery. These surveys are designed to provide insight into implementation patterns without adding significant burden to teachers. While fidelity measures will not be used to adjust the primary outcome analysis, they will be summarized descriptively to assess variation in delivery across schools and trial arms. Exploratory analyses may be conducted to examine associations between fidelity indicators and outcomes, but these will be interpreted with caution and clearly distinguished from the primary impact estimates.

Interim analyses {21b}

No interim analyses will be conducted while the trial is active.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}

All planned analyses will occur after completion of the intervention and follow-up periods, as part of the overall evaluation of outcomes.

Subgroup analyses will be performed to explore (i) gender, (ii) rural/urban status, and (iii) student body composition according to socioeconomic background. The statistical approach for subgroup analyses will follow the same procedures as mentioned in the previous section.

Social desirability bias can be a threat to the internal validity of a study. To address potential social desirability bias, which can lead to overestimation or underestimation of some of the treatment effects, two scales will be used, one for adults and one for children. For adult bias, the module Crowne-Marlowe [11] will be used in its validated version with Spanish speakers and an internal consistency of 0.89 [7]. For children’s bias, the Children’s Social Desirability Scale will be used (CSD-S; [35]), a scale that has been validated in Spanish-speaking countries with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.7 [29]. If the treatment effects are driven by those individuals with higher desirability bias at baseline, then the effects are over- or underestimated. However, if the treatment effects are driven by individuals with lower desirability bias, then social desirability bias concerns will be alleviated.

All subgroup categories will be defined based on baseline data collected prior to randomization. The subgroup analyses will test whether differences in outcome between trial arms vary across these subgroups. These analyses will be conducted only on outcome data and will not influence or adjust the delivery of the intervention.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

All outcome analyses will follow an intention-to-treat (ITT) approach, meaning participants will be analyzed in the group to which their school was originally randomized, regardless of their level of protocol adherence or exposure to the intervention. This strategy preserves the integrity of the randomization process and mitigates the risk of bias due to differential attrition.

We anticipate a low to moderate level of missing outcome data of teachers and students due to student or teacher absenteeism, attrition, or incomplete questionnaires, and moderate levels of missing outcome data among parents. Missing data will be minimized through several strategies. Students will be encouraged to complete the entire questionnaire, and questionnaires will be revised immediately after completion; if any questions are left unanswered, students will be asked to provide the missing responses at that time. To address this, we will apply multiple imputation techniques under the assumption that data are missing at random. The imputation models will incorporate relevant baseline variables and available outcome data to estimate missing values.

In addition, we will conduct sensitivity analyses comparing imputed datasets with complete-case results to assess the robustness of our findings. Reasons for missing data and attrition will be recorded and reported by trial arm to support transparency and interpretation.

Plans to give access to the full protocol, participant-level data, and statistical code {31c}

The datasets generated during the current study will be made available in the international database repository known as the UK Data Archive.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of the coordinating center and trial steering committee {5d}

This study is conducted exclusively at Universidad de los Andes, Chile. The principal investigator is responsible for the overall management and execution of the trial, overseeing its daily operations, providing direct supervision, and ensuring its smooth functioning. The study coordinator manages trial registration, coordinates site visits, and ensures timely reporting. Research assistants provide daily support to schools, including obtaining informed consent, managing procedures according to the protocol, and addressing any questions or concerns from schools, participants, or their families.

The study team will meet weekly to ensure continuous coordination throughout the trial. A formal trial steering committee has not been convened. Given the low risk, school-based nature of the intervention, and the close internal oversight by the core study team, a separate TSC was not deemed necessary. Independent oversight is provided by the Ethical Scientific Committee of Universidad de los Andes, which reviews and monitors the study to ensure compliance with ethical and regulatory standards.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role and reporting structure {21a}

No data monitoring committee has been established for this trial. This decision is based on the low-risk nature of the intervention, which involves daily 10-min socioemotional activities implemented by classroom teachers in school settings. The trial poses no anticipated physical or psychological harm to participants and does not include safety critical outcomes that would require periodic review by an independent monitoring body.

Instead, oversight responsibilities are fulfilled by the Ethical Scientific Committee of Universidad de los Andes. This committee reviews the study annually, including audits of study procedures and protocol adherence. It is also responsible for approving any protocol amendments. For more information about its guidelines, inquiries can be directed via email to cec@uandes.cl.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

No formal adverse event monitoring system will be implemented for this trial. The intervention involves low-risk, non-invasive socioemotional activities delivered by classroom teachers, with no expected physical, psychological, or social harm to participants. Given the educational and non-clinical nature of the trial, adverse events are not anticipated.

That said, any concerns or unintended effects reported by participants, teachers, or school staff will be documented by research assistants and reviewed by the principal investigator. Any serious concerns will be promptly reported to the Ethical Scientific Committee of Universidad de los Andes, which oversees the ethical conduct of the study.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

The principal investigator provides biannual progress reports on the trial to the Ethical Scientific Committee of Universidad de los Andes, which then conducts follow-up audits at least once a year.

Plans for communicating important protocol amendments to relevant parties (e.g., trial participants, ethical committees) {25}

All proposals for substantial amendments will be submitted to the Ethical Scientific Committee of Universidad de los Andes for approval. If the amendments affect participants in any way, they will be informed about the changes, including any additional consent forms, if necessary. The online trial registration will be updated accordingly.

Dissemination plans {31a}

The findings of this research will be published in international peer-reviewed journals and presented at conferences. An executive summary of the findings will be provided to school authorities. Seminars will be organized with local communities to disseminate the results to teachers, parents, and students.

Discussion

Over the last decade, there has been increasing interest in the development of interventions that address socioemotional competencies in children and adults. The reviews of the evidence show few impact evaluations of programs supporting the socioemotional competencies of children in Chile and almost no evaluations of programs supporting the socioemotional development of teachers.

This study will provide evidence for the effect of the 10-min program for children in the 3rd and 4th grades and their teachers. The data will be used not only to explore the impact of the intervention on both children and teachers but also both at the same time. The findings will be examined alongside evidence from other studies that have examined new approaches to supporting children’s and teachers’ socioemotional competence.
