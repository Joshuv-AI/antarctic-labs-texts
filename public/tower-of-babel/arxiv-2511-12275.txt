A Stochastic Genetic Interacting Particle Method for Reaction-Diffusion-Advection Equations
Boyi Hu, Zhongjian Wang, Jack Xin, Zhiwen Zhang

Abstract
We develop and analyze a stochastic genetic interacting particle method (SGIP) for reaction-diffusion-advection (RDA) equations. The method employs operator splitting to approximate the advection-diffusion and reaction processes, treating the former by particle drift-diffusion and the latter by exact or implicit integration of reaction dynamics over bins where particle density is estimated by a histogram. A key innovation is the incorporation of adaptive resampling to close the loop of particle and density field description of solutions, mimicking mutation in genetics. Resampling is also critical for maintaining long-term stability by redistributing particles with the evolving density field. We provide a comprehensive error analysis, and establish rigorous convergence bounds under appropriate regularity assumptions. Numerical experiments from one to three space dimensions demonstrate the method’s effectiveness across various reaction types (FKPP, cubic, Arrhenius) and flow configurations (shear, cellular, cat’s eye, ABC flows), showing excellent agreement with finite difference method (FDM) while offering computational advantages for complex flow geometries and higher-dimensional problems.



Keywords:
Stochastic particle method, resampling, reaction-diffusion-advection equations, operator splitting, convergence analysis, 3D simulations.

Introduction

Reaction-Diffusion-Advection (RDA) equations play a fundamental role in modeling numerous physical, biological, and chemical processes. The general form of RDA equations is given by:

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{v}u)=D\Delta u+r(u)

(1)

where
u(\mathbf{x},t)
represents the concentration or density field,
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)
is ambient fluid flow field,

is molecular diffusion coefficient, and

is reaction nonlinearity. The versatility of RDA equations is reflected in their wide-ranging applications
[

[

, while developmental biology employs Turing’s reaction-diffusion model to explain morphological patterning
[
33

. The framework also extends to materials science for simulating phase separation via the Cahn-Hilliard equation
[
4

, and to finance, where it underpins the Black-Scholes model for option pricing
[

, collectively underscoring its broad utility

The development of efficient numerical methods for these equations remains an active research area, with challenges from nonlinearities, multiscale phenomena, and high-dimensionality. The Finite Difference Method (FDM) is well-suited for simple geometries, with established stability and convergence properties

Particle (Lagrangian) techniques present a mesh-free alternative to handle large deformation (e.g. sharp internal layers) and high-dimensional problems. In the 1980’s,

In the absence of low order reactions, the current authors

In the spirit of particle-field representation, the stochastic genetic interacting particle (SGIP) method developed in this work provides an efficient and robust framework for RDA equations in several space dimensions. It is a natural extension of the SGIP
[
22
,
37
]
built from the Feynman-Kac semigroup for linear RDA equations.
The algorithm consists of a 3-step operator-splitting to decompose the RDA process: drift-diffusion, density update and particle resampling. The advection-diffusion is handled by
drift-diffusion (Ito equation) of particles.
The reaction process is approximated by going between particles and density: the density field is a histogram counting particle positions over bins, and is evolved in time via reaction ODEs by either an exact (e.g. for FKPP) or a stable implicit integration (Algorithm

[
22
,
37
]

drift-diffusion and resampling
steps without relying on density field,
due to reaction rate being a known potential function.
The resampling step here mimics mutation in genetics in that particles are more likely to survive in a bin
to the next generation
if the bin has more particles (is more healthy) than its neighbors. Resampling is also critical for long-term stability and computational efficiency by maintaining a well-balanced representation of the evolving solution, i.e. keeping variance in check over time. Our SGIP framework here is supported by a rigorous convergence analysis under appropriate regularity assumptions, which establishes consistent accuracy controlled by time step, spatial resolution, and the number of particles.

Our numerical experiments comprehensively demonstrate SGIP’s capabilities across various dimensions and complexities. In 1D scenarios, SGIP shows excellent agreement with FDM for different reaction types (FKPP, cubic, and Arrhenius)
[
10
,
1
]
, accurately capturing propagation speeds and front shapes. The method effectively resolves a spectrum of complex flow configurations, from fundamental 2D shear, cellular and cat’s eye flows to challenging 3D Arnold-Beltrami-Childress (ABC) flows
[
27
,
7
,
25
,
9
,
36
]
, even when coupled with Lipschitz-continuous reaction terms. In these scenarios, particularly in 3D, SGIP maintains high accuracy while offering significant computational advantages over traditional mesh-based methods like the FDM. As the diffusion coefficient decreases and advection dominates, the SGIP framework remains stable and efficient, in stark contrast to FDM, which suffers from prohibitive computational costs and severe numerical instability under such conditions.

The SGIP method provides a simple-to-program, scalable, low-cost and stable framework for high-dimensional RDA problems. Its mesh-free formulation bypasses the geometric constraints of grid-based methods, allowing it to handle complex flows and large gradients of solutions with ease. This capability is augmented by favorable computational scaling in high dimensions and long-term stability, thanks to adaptive resampling. These combined strengths allow SGIP to open new avenues for simulating complex multiscale phenomena in physical and biological systems, particularly in scenarios involving intricate flow geometries and requiring long-time integration.

2
SGIP Algorithm

In this section, we will present the SGIP algorithm for solving the reaction-diffusion-advection (RDA) equations.
The mathematical foundation of our method is the operator-splitting technique. We consider the RDA equation as an abstract evolution equation:

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{R})u,\quad u(0)=u_{0}

(2)

where
\mathcal{A}u=\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{v}u)+D\Delta u
is the advection-diffusion operator and
\mathcal{R}u=r(u)
is the reaction operator. Following the Lie-Trotter splitting scheme, we approximate the solution evolution by decoupling the operators:

u_{n+1}\approx\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\Delta t}\circ\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\Delta t}(u_{n})

(3)

Here,
u_{n}
denotes the numerical approximation of
u(\mathbf{x},t_{n})
at time
t_{n}
, and
u_{n+1}
denotes the approximation at the next time level
t_{n+1}=t_{n}+\Delta t
. This splitting enables us to employ specialized numerical techniques for each physical process, ensuring its long-term stability and efficiency.

For the advection-diffusion part
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\Delta t}
, we employ the stochastic particle method, which turns out to be stable in various high-dimensional advection-dominant problems. For the reaction
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\Delta t}
counterpart, we apply a density-based approach, which recovers the density
u
from the particle formulation and approximates the reaction
\mathcal{R}
by resampling. Details of each part of the algorithm are listed in the following subsections.

2.1
Initialization

Let
\Omega=[-L,L]^{d}
be the computational domain. We approximate the density field
u(\mathbf{x},t)
using an empirical measure of
N
particles:

u(\mathbf{x},t)\approx\frac{M_{0}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{X}^{i}(t))

(4)

where
M_{0}=\int_{\Omega}u_{0}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}
is the initial total mass and
\{\mathbf{X}^{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}
are particle positions.
The initial particle positions at
t_{0}=0
are sampled from the normalized initial distribution:

\mathbf{X}_{0}^{i}\sim\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{u_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{M_{0}},\quad i=1,\dots,N

(5)

Particle masses are initialized uniformly according to:

m_{0}^{i}=\frac{M_{0}}{N},\quad i=1,\dots,N.

(6)

In the temporal direction, we assume that the computational time range
[0,T]
is partitioned into time steps by
\{t_{n}\}_{n=0}^{n_{T}}
, where
t_{0}=0
and
t_{n_{T}}=T
. We consider the uniform time step, i.e., there exists
\Delta t=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
for
n=0,\dots,n_{T}-1
. For ease of presenting our algorithm, we will use a slight abuse of notation. We will represent the density
u
at time
t_{n}
as
u_{n}
, and the particle positions at time
t_{n}
as
\{\mathbf{X}_{n}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Similarly, particle masses at time
t_{n}
are denoted as
m_{n}^{i}
.

2.2
Advection-Diffusion Step

The advection-diffusion sub-step
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\Delta t}
advances the solution from
t_{n}
to an intermediate time. Each particle follows the stochastic differential equation:

d\mathbf{X}^{i}=\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{X}^{i},t)dt+\sqrt{2D}d\mathbf{W}^{i}(t)

(7)

where
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)
is the velocity field and
\mathbf{W}^{i}(t)
are independent Brownian motions.

Discretized using the Euler-Maruyama scheme over the time interval
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
:

\mathbf{X}_{*}^{i}=\mathbf{X}_{n}^{i}+\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{X}_{n}^{i},t_{n})\Delta t+\sqrt{2D\Delta t}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{i}

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
are independent standard normal random variables, and
\mathbf{X}_{*}^{i}
denotes the intermediate particle positions.

2.3
Reaction Step

The reaction sub-step
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\Delta t}
handles the nonlinear reaction term from the intermediate state to time
t_{n+1}
. This step consists of two main components: density estimation on a Eulerian grid, followed by reaction integration.

2.3.1
Density Estimation

To evaluate the reaction terms, we first reconstruct the density field from the particle distribution. The domain
\Omega=[-L,L]^{d}
is discretized into
M^{d}
uniform bins with centers
\{\mathbf{x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M^{d}}
and bin size
\Delta x=2L/M
. The density in each bin is estimated by counting particles and summing their masses:

\hat{u}_{*}^{j}=\frac{1}{(\Delta x)^{d}}\sum_{i:\mathbf{X}_{*}^{i}\in\text{bin}_{j}}m_{n}^{i}

(9)

This provides a piecewise constant approximation of the density field at the intermediate state, which serves as the initial condition for the reaction integration.

2.3.2
Reaction Integration

Given the density estimates
\{\hat{u}_{*}^{j}\}
, we solve the reaction equation
\partial_{t}u=r(u)
over the time interval
\Delta t
independently in each bin. We consider two cases based on the form of the reaction term:

Closed-form Solutions

For certain reaction terms, exact integration is possible. A prominent example is the FKPP reaction:

r(u)=u(1-u)

(10)

which admits the exact solution:

\hat{u}_{n+1}^{j}=\frac{\hat{u}_{*}^{j}e^{\Delta t}}{1+\hat{u}_{*}^{j}(e^{\Delta t}-1)}

(11)

Other examples include linear reactions
r(u)=\lambda u
, which yield exponential growth/decay
\hat{u}_{n+1}^{j}=\hat{u}_{*}^{j}e^{\lambda\Delta t}
.

General Reaction Terms

For reaction terms without closed-form solutions, such as:

•

Cubic reaction:
r(u)=u^{2}(1-u)
,

•

Arrhenius-type reaction:
r(u)=e^{-E/u}(1-u)
with activation energy
E>0
,

•

Polynomial reactions:
r(u)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}a_{k}u^{k}
,

we employ implicit time integration methods to maintain numerical stability. The backward Euler scheme reads:

\frac{\hat{u}_{n+1}^{j}-\hat{u}_{*}^{j}}{\Delta t}=r(\hat{u}_{n+1}^{j})

(12)

which is solved numerically using Newton’s method. For stiff problems, higher-order implicit methods such as Crank-Nicolson
[
8
]
or BDF2
[
12
]
can be employed.

The reaction integration is performed independently in each spatial bin, making this step highly parallelizable. After reaction integration, we obtain the updated density field
\{\hat{u}_{n+1}^{j}\}
at time
t_{n+1}
.

Following the reaction integration that produces the updated density field

3
Error Analysis of the SGIP method for RDA Equation

In this section, we present a rigorous error analysis for the SGIP algorithm applied to the RDA equation. The analysis systematically decomposes the total error into four fundamental components: splitting error, particle approximation error, spatial discretization error, and sampling error. We establish precise bounds for each component and combine them to obtain the overall error estimate.

Let
u(t)
denote the exact solution of the FKPP equation,
u^{\mathrm{Lie}}(t)
denote the exact Lie splitting solution (Eqs.(
2
)(
3
)), and
\widetilde{u}_{n}
denote the numerical SGIP solution at time
t_{n}=n\Delta t
.

We analyze the local error of the SGIP by decomposing it into distinct components corresponding to different stages of the algorithm. The one-step error at time
t_{n+1}
admits the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|\tilde{u}_{n+1}-u_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}&\leq\underbrace{\|\tilde{u}_{n+1}-\tilde{u}_{n+1}^{\text{Lie}}\|_{L^{2}}}_{\varepsilon_{\text{num}}}+\underbrace{\|\tilde{u}_{n+1}^{\text{Lie}}-u_{n+1}^{\text{Lie}}\|_{L^{2}}}_{\varepsilon_{\text{propag}}}+\underbrace{\|u_{n+1}^{\text{Lie}}-u_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}}_{\varepsilon_{\text{split}}}\end{aligned}

(15)

where
u^{\mathrm{Lie}}_{n+1}=S_{\mathcal{R}}(\Delta t)S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)u_{n}
is the Lie splitting solution from the previous exact solution
u_{n}
. Here,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}
represents the splitting error due to operator splitting, while
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{num}}
captures the numerical implementation error arising from the particle discretization, and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{propag}}
denotes the error from propagating the numerical solution over one time step using the splitting scheme.

The numerical implementation error
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{num}}
can be further decomposed by tracing through the algorithmic stages:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{num}}

\displaystyle=\|\tilde{u}_{n+1}-\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{Lie}}_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\|S_{\mathcal{R}}(\Delta t)(\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{D}}_{n+1})-S_{\mathcal{R}}(\Delta t)S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\tilde{u}_{n}\|_{L^{2}}}_{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dens}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\|\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1}-S_{\mathcal{R}}(\Delta t)(\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{D}}_{n+1})\|_{L^{2}}}_{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{react}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\|\tilde{u}_{n+1}-\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}}_{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{resamp}}}

(16)

where the intermediate quantities are:

•

\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{D}}_{n+1}
: numerical density after particle advection-diffusion and binning,

•

\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1}
: numerical density after reaction integration step,

•

\widetilde{u}_{n+1}
: final numerical solution after resampling step.

The numerical implementation error comprises four distinct components:
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dens}}
represents the density estimation error from particle advection-diffusion and spatial binning;
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{react}}
corresponds to the reaction integration error from numerically solving the reaction ODEs; and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{resamp}}
captures the resampling error introduced during particle redistribution.

To establish rigorous error bounds, we introduce the following technical assumptions:

Assumption 1

(Regularity and boundedness)
.

The solution and operators satisfy:

1.

Initial regularity:
The initial data
u_{0}
belongs to the Sobolev space
H^{s}(\Omega)
with
s>\frac{d}{2}+3
, ensuring sufficient spatial regularity for the error analysis.

2.

Velocity field regularity:
The velocity field
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)
is uniformly Lipschitz continuous in space:

|\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)-\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{y},t)|\leq L_{v}|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\Omega,t\in[0,T],

(17)

and is uniformly bounded:
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times[0,T])}\leq M_{1}
.

3.

Boundedness:
The exact solution
u(t,\cdot)
remains uniformly bounded in the interval
[0,1]
for all
t\in[0,T]
, and maintains smoothness throughout the time evolution.

4.

Semigroup contractivity:
The boundary conditions (Neumann, periodic, or decay conditions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) are such that the advection-diffusion semigroup
S_{\mathcal{A}}(t)=e^{t\mathcal{A}}
is contractive in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, i.e.,

\|S_{\mathcal{A}}(t)u\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|u\|_{L^{2}}\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

(18)

5.

Reaction term regularity:
The reaction function
r(u)
satisfies the Lipschitz condition

|r(u)-r(v)|\leq L_{r}|u-v|\quad\text{for all }u,v\in[0,u_{\max}],

(19)

and is twice continuously differentiable with bounded second derivative
|r^{\prime\prime}(u)|\leq M_{2}
, ensuring well-posedness of the reaction steps.

Assumption 2

(CFL-like Condition)
.

In the SGIP algorithm, we assume that the bin size
\Delta x
approaches 0 faster than the time step
\Delta t
approaches 0. Additionally, the number of particles
N
is assumed to grow sufficiently fast relative to
\Delta x
and
\Delta t
.

More formally, as
\Delta t\to 0
,
\Delta x\to 0
, and
N\to\infty
, there exist parameters
\kappa,\nu
such that:

\displaystyle\kappa:=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t},\ \kappa\to 0,\qquad\nu:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\Delta x^{d}}\Delta t},\ \nu\to 0.

(20)

We first present the main convergence theorem, with its proof relying on the technical lemmas established in the subsequent subsection.

Theorem 3

(Global error bound)
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, for
n\Delta t\leq T
, the global error of the SGIP algorithm satisfies:

\sup_{0\leq n\leq T/\Delta t}\mathbb{E}\,\|\widetilde{u}_{n}-u_{n}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{T}\left(\Delta t+(N\Delta x^{d}\Delta t)^{-1/2}+\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\right)

(21)

where
C_{T}
depends on final time
T
, diffusion parameter
D
, and solution regularity, but is independent of time step
\Delta t
, bin size
\Delta x
, and the number of particles
N
.

The proof follows from the subsequent lemmas that bound each error component.

Lemma 1

(Propagation error estimate)
.

Under Assumption
1
, the propagation error satisfies:

\|S_{\mathcal{R}}(\Delta t)S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\tilde{u}_{n}-S_{\mathcal{R}}(\Delta t)S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)u_{n}\|_{L^{2}}\leq(1+C_{1}\Delta t)\|\tilde{u}_{n}-u_{n}\|_{L^{2}}

(22)

where
C_{1}
depends on the Lipschitz constants of the advection-diffusion and reaction operators.

Proof.

We analyze the propagation error by considering each operator separately.

For the advection-diffusion operator
\mathcal{A}u=-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{v}u)+D\Delta u
, consider two solutions
u(t)=S_{\mathcal{A}}(t)u_{0}
and
v(t)=S_{\mathcal{A}}(t)v_{0}
with difference
w=u-v
. Then:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}w=-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{v}w)+D\Delta w

(23)

Taking the
L^{2}
inner product with
w
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle w,-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{v}w)\rangle+D\langle w,\Delta w\rangle

(24)

For the advection term, using integration by parts and the boundedness of
\mathbf{v}
:

\displaystyle|\langle w,-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{v}w)\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq G_{1}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{D}{2}\|\Delta w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(25)

where
G_{1}
depends on the diffusion coefficient
D
and the domain regularity.

For the diffusion term:

\displaystyle D\langle w,\Delta w\rangle

\displaystyle=-D\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 0

(26)

Thus:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2G_{1}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(27)

By Gronwall’s inequality:

\displaystyle\|w(\Delta t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{2G_{1}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{\infty}}\Delta t}\|w(0)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(28)

So for small
\Delta t
:

\displaystyle\|S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)u_{0}-S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)v_{0}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq(1+L_{A}\Delta t)\|u_{0}-v_{0}\|_{L^{2}}

(29)

where
L_{A}=2G_{1}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

For the reaction equation
\partial_{t}u=r(u)
, consider two solutions
u(t)
and
v(t)
with difference
w=u-v
. Then:

\displaystyle\frac{dw}{dt}

\displaystyle=r(u)-r(v)

(30)

Using the Lipschitz condition
|r(u)-r(v)|\leq L_{r}|u-v|
in Assumption
1
:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{d}{dt}\|w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right|

\displaystyle=2\left|\int w(r(u)-r(v))dx\right|\leq 2L_{r}\|w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(31)

By Gronwall’s inequality:

\displaystyle\|w(\Delta t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{2L_{r}\Delta t}\|w(0)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(32)

So for small
\Delta t
:

\displaystyle\|S_{\mathcal{R}}(\Delta t)u_{0}-S_{\mathcal{R}}(\Delta t)v_{0}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq(1+L_{R}\Delta t)\|u_{0}-v_{0}\|_{L^{2}}

(33)

where
L_{R}=2L_{r}
.

Combining both estimates:

\displaystyle\|S_{\mathcal{R}}(\Delta t)S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\tilde{u}_{n}-S_{\mathcal{R}}(\Delta t)S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)u_{n}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq(1+L_{R}\Delta t)\|S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\tilde{u}_{n}-S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)u_{n}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq(1+L_{R}\Delta t)(1+L_{A}\Delta t)\|\tilde{u}_{n}-u_{n}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq(1+C_{1}\Delta t)\|\tilde{u}_{n}-u_{n}\|_{L^{2}}

(34)

where
C_{1}=L_{A}+L_{R}+O(\Delta t)
.
∎

Lemma 2

(Splitting error estimate)
.

Under Assumption
1
, the Lie splitting error satisfies:

\mathbb{E}\,\|u^{\mathrm{Lie}}_{n+1}-u_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{2}\Delta t^{2}

(35)

where
C_{2}
depends on the commutator
[\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}]
and the solution regularity.

Proof.

Using the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula
[
2
,
5
,
13
]
for operator splitting, we have the formal expansion:

S_{\mathcal{R}}(\Delta t)\circ S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)-S_{\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{R}}(\Delta t)=\tfrac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}[\mathcal{R},\mathcal{A}]+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})

(36)

where
[\mathcal{R},\mathcal{A}]u=r^{\prime}(u)\mathcal{A}u-\mathcal{A}(r(u))
is the commutator expression for the reaction and advection-diffusion operators.

For the specific operators
\mathcal{R}u=r(u)
and
\mathcal{A}u=\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{v}u)+D\Delta u
, we compute the commutator explicitly:

\displaystyle[\mathcal{R},\mathcal{A}]u

\displaystyle=r^{\prime}(u)\mathcal{A}u-\mathcal{A}(r(u))

\displaystyle=r^{\prime}(u)[\nabla(\mathbf{v}u)+D\Delta u]-\left[\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{v}r(u))+D\Delta r(u)\right]

\displaystyle=r^{\prime}(u)\nabla(\mathbf{v}u)+Dr^{\prime}(u)\Delta u

\displaystyle\quad-\left[r(u)\nabla\mathbf{v}+r^{\prime}(u)\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla u+Dr^{\prime}(u)\Delta u+Dr^{\prime\prime}(u)|\nabla u|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=r^{\prime}(u)\left[\nabla(\mathbf{v}u)-\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla u\right]-r(u)\nabla\mathbf{v}-Dr^{\prime\prime}(u)|\nabla u|^{2}

\displaystyle=r^{\prime}(u)u\nabla\mathbf{v}-r(u)\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}-Dr^{\prime\prime}(u)|\nabla u|^{2}

(37)

Under Assumption
1
, using Hölder’s inequality and Sobolev embedding theorem, we can obtain:

\displaystyle\|[\mathcal{R},\mathcal{A}]u\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|r^{\prime}(u)u\nabla\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{2}}+\|r(u)\nabla\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{2}}+D\|r^{\prime\prime}(u)|\nabla u|^{2}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|r^{\prime}(u)\|_{L^{\infty}}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{\infty}}|\Omega|^{1/2}+\|r(u)\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{\infty}}|\Omega|^{1/2}+D\|r^{\prime\prime}(u)\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{4}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq G_{2}(\|\mathbf{v}\|_{W^{1,\infty}},\|r\|_{C^{2}},\|u\|_{H^{s}})

(38)

with the constant
G_{2}
depending on
|\Omega|
,
D
, and the Sobolev embedding constants.

The local error bound of order
\Delta t^{2}
follows immediately from the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula.
∎

Lemma 3

(Density estimation error)
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, the density estimation error satisfies:

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{D}}_{n+1}-S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\widetilde{u}_{n}\|_{L^{2}}\right]\leq C_{3}(N\Delta x^{d})^{-1/2}+C_{4}\Delta x

(39)

where the constants
C_{3},C_{4}
depend on the domain size
L
, dimension
d
, diffusion coefficient
D
, the total mass at
t_{n}

\widetilde{m}_{n}=\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}
, and the regularity parameters from Assumption
1
.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{M}_{n}=\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}
be the computed total mass at time
t_{n}
, which contains
error from previous steps. The density estimate in bin
j
after the advection-diffusion step is:

\hat{u}_{j}=\frac{\widetilde{m}_{n}\cdot K_{j}}{(\Delta x)^{d}}=\frac{\widetilde{M}_{n}}{N(\Delta x)^{d}}\cdot K_{j}

(40)

where
\widetilde{m}_{n}=\widetilde{M}_{n}/N
is the particle mass at time
t_{n}
and
K_{j}
is the number of particles in bin
j
.

The density estimation error consists of two components: statistical sampling error, and spatial discretization error.

1. Statistical sampling error:

Let
p_{j}^{\mathrm{true}}=\int_{\mathrm{bin}_{j}}\frac{S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\widetilde{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})}{\widetilde{M}_{n}}d\mathbf{x}
be the probability that a particle, starting from the initial distribution
\widetilde{u}_{n}
and following the exact advection-diffusion dynamics, falls in bin
j
.

More precisely, consider particles
\mathbf{X}^{i}(t)
satisfying the exact SDE:

d\mathbf{X}^{i}(t)=\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{X}^{i}(t),t)dt+\sqrt{2D}d\mathbf{W}_{i}(t),\quad\text{for }t\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}]

(41)

with initial distribution
\mathbf{X}^{i}(t_{n})\sim\widetilde{u}_{n}
. Then:

p_{j}^{\mathrm{true}}=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{X}^{i}(t_{n+1})\in\mathrm{bin}_{j}\mid\mathbf{X}^{i}(t_{n})\sim\widetilde{u}_{n}\right)

(42)

The expected number of particles in bin
j
is
\mathbb{E}[K_{j}]=Np_{j}^{\mathrm{true}}
, so the expected density is:

\mathbb{E}[\hat{u}_{j}]=\frac{\widetilde{m}_{n}}{N(\Delta x)^{d}}\cdot\mathbb{E}[K_{j}]=\frac{\widetilde{m}_{n}}{N(\Delta x)^{d}}\cdot Np_{j}^{\mathrm{true}}=\frac{\widetilde{m}_{n}\cdot p_{j}^{\mathrm{true}}}{(\Delta x)^{d}}

(43)

The variance of the density estimate is:

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{u}_{j})=\left(\frac{\widetilde{m}_{n}}{N(\Delta x)^{d}}\right)^{2}\cdot\mathrm{Var}(K_{j})\leq\left(\frac{\widetilde{m}_{n}}{N(\Delta x)^{d}}\right)^{2}\cdot Np_{j}^{\mathrm{true}}(1-p_{j}^{\mathrm{true}})

(44)

Using
p_{j}^{\mathrm{true}}\leq\frac{\|S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\widetilde{u}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}}(\Delta x)^{d}}{\widetilde{m}_{n}}
, we get:

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{u}_{j})\leq\frac{\widetilde{m}_{n}\|S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\widetilde{u}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}}}{N(\Delta x)^{d}}

(45)

Summing over all bins:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{M^{d}}\Delta x^{d}|\hat{u}_{j}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{u}_{j}]|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{M^{d}}\Delta x^{d}\cdot\mathrm{Var}(\hat{u}_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M^{d}}\Delta x^{d}\cdot\frac{\widetilde{m}_{n}\|S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\widetilde{u}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}}}{N(\Delta x)^{d}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\widetilde{m}_{n}\|S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\widetilde{u}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}}(2L)^{d}}{N(\Delta x)^{d}}

(46)

Taking square roots:

\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{M^{d}}\Delta x^{d}|\hat{u}_{j}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{u}_{j}]|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}\leq\frac{C_{3}}{\sqrt{N}\Delta x^{d/2}}

(47)

where
C_{3}=\sqrt{\widetilde{m}_{n}\|S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\widetilde{u}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}}(2L)^{d}}
.

2. Spatial discretization error:

Let
u_{j}^{*}=S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\widetilde{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
be the true density at the
j^{th}
-bin center
\mathbf{x}_{j}
. The bias due to spatial discretization is:

|\mathbb{E}[\hat{u}_{j}]-u_{j}^{*}|=\left|\frac{\widetilde{m}_{n}\cdot p_{j}^{\mathrm{true}}}{(\Delta x)^{d}}-S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\widetilde{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\right|

(48)

Note that:

\frac{\widetilde{m}_{n}\cdot p_{j}^{\mathrm{true}}}{(\Delta x)^{d}}=\frac{1}{(\Delta x)^{d}}\int_{\mathrm{bin}_{j}}S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\widetilde{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}

(49)

By Taylor expansion and the smoothness of
S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\widetilde{u}_{n}
under Assumption
1
:

\left|\frac{1}{(\Delta x)^{d}}\int_{\mathrm{bin}_{j}}S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\widetilde{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}-S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\widetilde{u}_{n}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\right|\leq G_{3}\Delta x

(50)

Thus, the
L^{2}
spatial bias is:

\left(\sum_{j=1}^{M^{d}}\Delta x^{d}|\mathbb{E}[\hat{u}_{j}]-u_{j}^{*}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq C_{4}\Delta x

(51)

where
C_{4}
depends on the second derivatives of
S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\widetilde{u}_{n}
and the domain size.

Combining both error components Eqs.(
47
)(
51
):

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{D}}_{n+1}-S_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta t)\widetilde{u}_{n}\|_{L^{2}}\right]\leq C_{3}(N\Delta x^{d})^{-1/2}+C_{4}\Delta x

(52)

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2

(On the particle motion discretization error)
.

The particle motion discretization error from the Euler-Maruyama scheme, which would contribute a
\Delta t
term to the density estimation error, is intentionally omitted from Lemma
3
. This error arises from the discrepancy between the numerical particle positions
\mathbf{X}_{i}^{*}
(following Euler-Maruyama) and the exact positions
\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\text{true}}(t_{n+1})
(following the exact SDE dynamics).

However, this error can be treated as an accumulation term and is absorbed into the propagation error estimate in Lemma
1
. Specifically, the
\Delta t
coefficient in the propagation error bound
(1+C_{1}\Delta t)
implicitly incorporates the effects of particle motion discretization.

Lemma 4

(Reaction integration error)
.

Under Assumption
1
, the reaction integration error satisfies:

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{react}}=\|\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1}-S_{\mathcal{R}}(\Delta t)(\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{D}}_{n+1})\|_{L^{2}}\leq\begin{cases}0,&\text{(exact integration)}\\
C_{5}\Delta t^{p},&\text{(numerical integration, order $p$)}\end{cases}

(53)

where
C_{5}
depends on the Lipschitz constant of
r(u)
and the boundedness of higher derivatives under Assumption
1
.

Proof.

Case 1: Exact integration

For reactions with closed-form solutions (e.g., FKPP), the algorithm implements the exact solution operator:

\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1}=S_{\mathcal{R}}(\Delta t)(\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{D}}_{n+1})\quad\Rightarrow\quad\varepsilon_{\mathrm{react}}=0

(54)

Case 2: Numerical integration

For general reactions, using a numerical method of order
p
(e.g., implicit Euler:
p=1
, Crank-Nicolson:
p=2
) yields:

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{react}}\leq C_{5}\Delta t^{p}.

(55)

∎

Lemma 5

(Resampling error)
.

Under Assumption
1
, the resampling error satisfies:

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{resamp}}]=\mathbb{E}[\|\widetilde{u}_{n+1}-\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}]\leq C_{6}(N\Delta x^{d})^{-1/2}

(56)

where the constant
C_{6}
depends on the domain size
L
, dimension
d
, and the total mass
M
.

Proof.

The resampling step converts the continuous density field
\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1}
back to a particle representation. This process introduces statistical sampling error:

The resampling step generates
N
new particles according to the multinomial distribution with probabilities:

p_{j}=\frac{\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1,j}\cdot(\Delta x)^{d}}{M}

(57)

where
M=\sum_{j=1}^{M^{d}}\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1,j}\cdot(\Delta x)^{d}
is the total mass.

Let
n_{j}
be the number of particles assigned to bin
j
with
\sum_{j=1}^{M^{d}}n_{j}=N
. The new density estimate after resampling is:

\widetilde{u}_{n+1,j}=\frac{M\cdot n_{j}}{N(\Delta x)^{d}}

(58)

The expected value is:

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{u}_{n+1,j}]=\frac{M}{N(\Delta x)^{d}}\cdot\mathbb{E}[n_{j}]=\frac{M}{N(\Delta x)^{d}}\cdot Np_{j}=\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1,j}

(59)

so the resampling is unbiased.

The variance is:

\mathrm{Var}(\widetilde{u}_{n+1,j})=\left(\frac{M}{N(\Delta x)^{d}}\right)^{2}\cdot\mathrm{Var}(n_{j})=\left(\frac{M}{N(\Delta x)^{d}}\right)^{2}\cdot Np_{j}(1-p_{j})

(60)

Using
p_{j}\leq\frac{\|\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1}\|_{L^{\infty}}(\Delta x)^{d}}{M}
, we get:

\mathrm{Var}(\widetilde{u}_{n+1,j})\leq\frac{M\|\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1}\|_{L^{\infty}}}{N(\Delta x)^{d}}

(61)

Summing over all bins:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{M^{d}}\Delta x^{d}|\widetilde{u}_{n+1,j}-\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1,j}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M^{d}}\Delta x^{d}\cdot\frac{M\|\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1}\|_{L^{\infty}}}{N(\Delta x)^{d}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M\|\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1}\|_{L^{\infty}}(2L)^{d}}{N(\Delta x)^{d}}

Taking square roots:

\mathbb{E}[\|\widetilde{u}_{n+1}-\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}]\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{N}\Delta x^{d/2}}=C_{6}(N\Delta x^{d})^{-1/2}

(62)

where
C_{6}=\sqrt{M\|\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1}\|_{L^{\infty}}(2L)^{d}}
.

Under Assumption
1
,
M
and
\|\widetilde{u}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n+1}\|_{L^{\infty}}
are uniformly bounded, ensuring
C_{6}
is finite.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove Theorem
3
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

From Eqs.(
15
)(
3
), and all the lemmas above, we can obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,\|\widetilde{u}_{n+1}-u_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{propag}}]+\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dens}}]+\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{react}}]+\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{resamp}}]+\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{split}}]

\displaystyle\leq(1+C_{1}\Delta t)\mathbb{E}\,\|\tilde{u}_{n}-u_{n}\|_{L^{2}}+C_{3}(N\Delta x^{d})^{-1/2}+C_{4}\Delta x

\displaystyle\quad+C_{5}\Delta t^{p}+C_{6}(N\Delta x^{d})^{-1/2}+C_{2}\Delta t^{2}

(63)

For the optimal case where we use exact reaction integration (
p\to\infty
) or second-order methods (
p=2
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,\|\widetilde{u}_{n+1}-u_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}\leq(1+C_{1}\Delta t)\mathbb{E}\,\|\tilde{u}_{n}-u_{n}\|_{L^{2}}+C_{4}\Delta x+C_{7}\Delta t^{2}+C_{8}(N\Delta x^{d})^{-1/2}

(64)

where
C_{7}=C_{2}+C_{4}
,
C_{8}=C_{3}+C_{6}
.

Since the initial condition is sampled exactly from
u_{0}
, we have
\varepsilon_{0}=0
. By the discrete Gronwall’s inequality, for
n\Delta t\leq T
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,\|\tilde{u}_{n}-u_{n}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{C_{1}T}-1}{C_{1}}\left(C_{7}(N\Delta x^{d}\Delta t)^{-1/2}+C_{8}\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}+C_{2}\Delta t\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{T}\left(\Delta t+(N\Delta x^{d}\Delta t)^{-1/2}+\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\right)

(65)

where
C_{T}=\frac{e^{C_{1}T}-1}{C_{1}}\max(C_{7},C_{8},C_{2})
. This completes the proof of Theorem
3
.

5
Conclusion

We have presented the SGIP method, a novel stochastic genetic interacting particle algorithm for solving reaction-diffusion-advection equations. The method’s effectiveness stems from its integrated design that combines three key components: operator splitting for process decoupling, stochastic particle dynamics for transport, and adaptive resampling for long-term stability.

The main theoretical contribution is a rigorous convergence analysis, where we prove that under appropriate assumptions, the method achieves consistent accuracy across various dimensionalities and problem configurations. This provides a solid mathematical foundation for the proposed methodology. Numerical experiments demonstrate SGIP’s capability in handling diverse challenges: from various reaction types (FKPP, cubic, Arrhenius) to complex flow configurations (shear, cellular, ABC flows) across 1D, 2D, and 3D domains. The Lagrangian formulation naturally handles sharp gradients and complex geometries without requiring specialized discretization techniques. Particularly in higher dimensions, the method offers computational advantages over traditional Eulerian approaches by avoiding the curse of dimensionality while maintaining accuracy with relatively large time steps.

The adaptive resampling strategy proves crucial for maintaining numerical stability and computational efficiency over long-time integration. Through intelligent particle redistribution guided by Eulerian density reconstruction, SGIP maintains an optimal balance between statistical accuracy and computational efficiency. Future work will focus on extending SGIP to multi-species systems, and implementing the fast adaptive algorithm discussed in Remark
1
to concentrate computational resources in active transition regions for enhanced computational efficiency in higher dimensions.
