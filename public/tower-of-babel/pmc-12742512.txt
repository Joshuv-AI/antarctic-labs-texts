Genetic applications in arthroplasty: A review
Authors: Jaclyn A. Konopka, Theodor Di Pauli von Treuheim, Lefko Charalambous, Ran Schwarzkopf, Joshua Rozell, Claudette Lajam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetics is a burgeoning field within adult reconstructive surgery. Genome-wide sequencing has identified genetic variants found to be associated with not only the development of osteoarthritis but also arthroplasty-related complications, such as aseptic loosening, prosthetic joint infection, arthrofibrosis, and postoperative pain. Examples include newer technology, such as next-generation sequencing, in diagnosing culture-negative prosthetic joint infection. Genetics drives new therapeutic technologies, such as gene therapy, gene-editing, and bacteriophage treatment. Although still rare, a handful of phase 3 clinical trials of gene therapy for osteoarthritis have begun to demonstrate efficacy with low-risk profiles. As the field continues to grow, public and professional buy-in as well as cost present challenges.

Introduction

As the population ages, disease burden from orthopedic pathology will continue to rise.1 Although orthopedic disorders are common and disabling, they are rarely lethal and have, therefore, not been prioritized for applications in gene therapy.1 However, over the past decade, the role for genetics has expanded for management of hip and knee arthritic conditions, including arthroplasty. Osteoarthritis (OA), aseptic implant loosening, prosthetic joint infection (PJI), arthrofibrosis, the inflammatory response to metal ion disease, heterotopic ossification (HO), and postoperative pain are all known to be genetically linked. In the future, it might be possible to screen patients preoperatively for risk of infection, aseptic loosening, or arthrofibrosis. There is evidence that genetically engineered bacteriophages have the potential to eliminate the need for 2-stage exchange for PJI. Although many genetic applications are experimental and expensive, the field is undoubtedly growing. There are already genetic applications in the mainstream, such as next-generation sequencing (NGS), to help diagnose infection.

Two main methods can be used to identify whether certain pathology has a genetic link. One of the methods is to study outcomes for twin studies, comparing those of identical twins with identical deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) with those of fraternal twins who have unique DNA sequences.2 The other method includes genome wide sequencing (GWS), which has become increasingly popular over the past decade and is used to quantify and correlate genetic markers with various pathologies. In GWS, DNA is extracted from cells in a blood or tissue sample and then broken down into smaller pieces, which are then sent through a sequencing machine. The machine uses “NGS” to mix fluorescently labeled nucleotides with the DNA fragments so that these fluorescent nucleotides can then bind to their corresponding nucleotides on the DNA. The machine then analyzes the order of the different colored fluorescent signals to output the entire sequence.3

Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are what make GWS so powerful. By sequencing the entire genome, researchers can identify SNPs and see how they correlate with disease (Fig. 1). These SNPs can be used to develop prevention, diagnosis, and treatment. SNPs have been instrumental in advancing novel treatments and prevention strategies in the field of arthroplasty. Conditions including OA, aseptic loosening, PJI, and postoperative pain have seen the most genetic applicability. This article will review the current state of genetic knowledge of these conditions, discuss how these strategies work, and highlight landmark ongoing clinical trials and future projections.

SNPs involve a germline substitution of single base pairs present in the DNA of 1% or more of the population. DNA sequencing can identify patterns of SNPs that can be used as markers for certain diseases or conditions. SNP, single-nucleotide polymorphisms.

Genetics of Osteoarthritis

OA is a complex inflammatory and metabolic syndrome resulting in the degradation of articular cartilage, local inflammation, and pain through the release of metalloproteinases and inflammatory cytokines.4,5 There are over 100 different SNPs associated with OA, which vary significantly based on joint and cellular function.6 These genetic variants translate to a 40–65% hereditary risk of developing OA, with similar rates demonstrated in inflammatory arthropathies like rheumatoid arthritis and psoriatic arthritis.7,8

Gene therapy—osteoarthritis

Medical applications of gene therapy are increasing, with projected utilization reaching a million patients in the year 2035.9 Gene therapy is the process of manipulating gene expression in a host through transcriptional or translational transfer of genetic material that integrates into the host's genome, typically through a viral vector.10 In the knee or hip, this can be accomplished either by directly injecting agents into the joint to modify cells in vivo or indirectly by modifying cells in vitro and then delivering them to the recipient’s synovium.4 OA provides an appealing target for gene therapy because joints are discrete spaces that can be easily accessed. First attempts at gene delivery to joints were made in the 1990s. To date, this domain has yielded the most robust aggregate of clinical trials on gene therapy of all musculoskeletal conditions.1

For OA, the goal of gene therapy is to overexpress therapeutic genes involved in chondrocyte proliferation and extracellular matrix production and downgrade detrimental genes involved in chondrocyte senescence and joint inflammation, catabolism, and matrix-degrading enzymes.4 Current clinical gene therapy trials aim at overexpressing the anti-inflammatory genes, transforming growth factor (TGF)-beta, Interleukin (IL)-10, and IL-1 antagonists, with the goal of limiting pain and decreasing cartilage wear.4 Historical attempts of systemic administration or articular injections of these mediators have provided limited efficacy because of rapid clearance of these drugs, low target concentrations, and short-lived local therapeutic effects.1 Unlike systemic administration of these agents, gene therapy provides a sustained, localized expression of the treatment modality.4

There have been 12 gene therapy clinical trials for the treatment of OA and degenerative arthropathies, including 5 phase 1, 6 phase 2, and 1 phase 3 trial.4 These have targeted upregulation of anti-inflammatory cytokines TGF-beta1, Nkx3.2, and IL-10 and downregulation of the proinflammatory cytokine IL-1 Ra.4 These proteins are also ones that have been found to have SNPs predisposing to OA.6 To date, 1 phase 1 trial was suspended because of persistent injection site reactions that were treated conservatively.1 The other trials have reported “related” adverse events that were found not to be attributed to the therapy, including 1 fatality from histoplasmosis. One gene therapy drug, called Invossa, was approved in South Korea in 2017, where chondrocytes were transduced with a retrovirus carrying TGF-beta1 ex vivo and injected into the knee joints of 163 patients with Kellgren-Lawrence grade 3 OA.1,11 The researchers followed patients for 52 weeks and found significant improvements in pain and functional scores which persisted 12 months after injection. Complications were minimal and transient but included peripheral edema, arthralgia, joint swelling, and injection site pain.1,11 This drug, however, was suspended after it was discovered that the modified cells were not chondrocytes, but rather human embryonic kidney epithelial cells.1 The ACTIVION trial (Kolon TissueGene, Rockville, Maryland) is the only phase 3 clinical trial in the United States. This is a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study aiming to enroll 510 patients with KL grade 2 and 3 knee OA. Very similar to the Invossa drug, they are using a retrovirus vector to overexpress TGF-beta and are evaluating patient-reported outcome measures, biochemical markers, and magnetic resonance imaging over 1 year. Results have yet to be released.1,11–15 Various limitations to the therapeutic commercialization of gene therapy for OA still exist. Continued research into the long-term effectiveness and adverse effects of these treatments is still needed, and the high cost of gene therapy cannot be overlooked. Invossa, the only commercialized OA gene therapy drug, costs $4400 and other gene therapy drugs were significantly higher from $425,000 for Voretigene Neparvovec (a gene therapy drug for congenital retinal disease) to $2,100,000 for Zolgensma (a gene therapy drug for spinal muscular atrophy). OA is a nonfatal pathology. Therefore, the adverse effect profile for OA gene therapy needs to be very closely monitored to prevent significant harm.

Gene-editing—osteoarthritis

Although current OA gene therapies have relied on transduction of 1 gene, future strategies with a multigene regulation approach will allow for simultaneous pain control and dynamic cartilage degradation cessation.1,16 This strategy addresses the multifactorial nature of OA progression. OA is not halted by continuous inhibition of proinflammatory mediators, nor activation of anti-inflammatory mediators, but rather by dynamic cytokine homeostasis.1,17 Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR)/Cas9 is a promising gene-editing technology that possesses self-feedback gene regulation.1 The dynamic balance that CRISPR can impart is exemplified by recent research into the mechanisms of nerve growth factor (NGF). Although NGF is upregulated in OA, suppressing NGF also leads to bone destruction, making NGF inhibition alone undesirable.18 Zhao et al. injected an adeno-associated virus vector expressing CRISPR/Cas9 components to simultaneously delete NGF, matrix metalloproteinase 13, and IL-1beta. This combination successfully alleviated pain symptoms without structural damage in a post-traumatic murine mouse OA model.16 The future of OA gene therapy will, therefore, likely depend on clinical trial outcomes using gene-editing technologies or through combination gene therapy that simultaneously targets multiple cellular pathways.

Genetics of Aseptic Loosening

Aseptic loosening is one of the leading causes for revision surgery in total joint arthroplasty (TJA) and is characterized by chronic peri-implant inflammation, leading to osteolysis and subsequent failure at the bone-implant interface.19,20 A genetic link predisposing to aseptic loosening has been investigated because its incidence cannot solely be explained by environmental factors, implant material, presence of metal or polyethylene debris, or surgical component positioning.21,22 Koks et al.22 reviewed 423 patients undergoing TJA and found 52 SNPs to be associated with aseptic loosening, 4 of which directly correlated with the amount of time to loosening. One SNP was identified on the gene CERK, which encodes the CLERK protein, a protein involved in the pathway regulating the antiproliferative actions of vitamin D3. This SNP was found to impose a 12.6 odds ratio for developing aseptic loosening. Another SNP on the gene PAPPA, which encodes a metalloprotease involved in bone formation, was found to impose a 14.0 odds ratio. A SNP in the IFIT2/IFIT3 gene, which is known to be involved in the innate immune response and in inflammatory processes, was found to impose a staggering 21.6 odds ratio for aseptic loosening. These results support the notion that genetic makeup can significantly influence risk for this complication. No research has yet elucidated a mechanistic pathway by which these genes directly affect aseptic loosening; nevertheless, there are ongoing investigations using gene therapy to address this complication. However, having identified SNPs that increase the risk of aseptic loosening, there may be merit in using this technology for preoperative patient screening. Knowledge of increased risk can enhance patient counseling and perhaps prompt different surgical techniques and implant selection. Once identified, these patients may require closer follow-up after surgery. However, gene applications remain theoretical because no trials have investigated the number needed to treat and cost associated with screening.

Gene therapy—aseptic loosening

Gene therapy offers opportunities to manage aseptic loosening. Wang et al.23 investigated gene therapy for the treatment of debris-induced aseptic loosening in a mouse tibia model implanted with a titanium alloy pin. They transduced cells ex vivo with a retroviral vector encoding IL-1 receptor antagonist, as well as an adeno associated virus vector encoding osteoprotegerin. They injected these cells into mouse knee joints. They reported synergistic effects of combination gene therapy leading to decreased peri-implant pseudomembrane thickness, increased pullout strength, increased bone mineral density, and improved bony architecture on micoCT at the bone-implant interface. These efforts paved the way for clinical trials using gene therapy to address aseptic loosening.

Although most gene therapy research for aseptic loosening is preclinical, there is 1 Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved phase 1 clinical trial in the United States that has been completed. De Poorter et al.24 used gene therapy to treat patients with known aseptic loosening who had significant medical comorbidities impeding formal revision surgery. They developed an adenovirus vector that delivered complementary DNA to cells within the pseudomembrane surrounding loose implants, leading to pseudomembrane degradation. This void was then filled with a bone cement solution that hardened in situ, thus generating a new bone-implant interface and securing the prosthesis (Fig. 2). This procedure was performed on 12 patients who reported improvements in walking distance, independence, and pain. However, the underlying cause of loosening is not addressed, increasing the likelihood of recurring symptoms down the road. The researchers advocate that in a low functioning, frail, elderly patient, a percutaneous means of decreasing pain that has minimal side effects could be helpful. Skeptics question whether adequate void filling can be achieved, which has stalled subsequent phase 2 and 3 clinical trials using this methodology.1

(A) For a loose implant in a patient who cannot tolerate revision surgery, adenovirus vector and cDNA are injected into the pseudomembrane on days 1 and 3, degrading the membrane and creating a new space for cement injection. (B) Bone cement is injected on days 5–7 to enhance fixation. cDNA, complementary DNA.

Genetics of Prosthetic Joint Infections

PJI is a devastating complication of TJA, leading to significant increases in morbidity and mortality.25 Although there has been an emphasis on the identification and optimization of modifiable clinical risk factors for PJI, investigators have only recently begun to consider genetic influences on the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of PJI. In their 2022 meta-analysis, Hijazi et al.26 identified various genetic polymorphisms associated with PJI. They highlighted several cytokine-related genes with polymorphisms that were significantly associated with PJI including tumor necrosis factor-α, IL-6, GCSF3R, IL-1 repeat number-variable number tandem repeat, and IL-1B. Furthermore, protein-related and enzyme-related genes, such as mannose-binding lectin (MBL) and MBL2, as well as a receptor-related gene, vitamin D receptor, were also found to correlate with PJI development. These genes are implicated in a variety of pathways, from inflammatory cascades to vitamin D metabolism. They present opportunities for additional research into treatment modalities and PJI patient-specific risk profiling.

Next-generation sequencing—prosthetic joint infection diagnosis

Aspiration and culture of synovial fluid has classically been the primary means of PJI diagnosis but is less helpful in the diagnosis of fastidious organisms, with culture-negative PJI rates quoted to be as high as 50%.27 NGS is a novel technology that can be used to sequence and amplify DNA/ribonucelic acid fragments of bacteria in a sample simultaneously, enabling the creation of a comprehensive microbial profile.28 NGS is particularly advantageous for culture-negative PJI and to diagnose less common strains of bacteria such as Pasteurella multocida, Mycobacterium houstonense, Coxiella burnetti, and Streptococcus canis.4,27,29,30

In the most comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis to date, Hantouly et al.31 compared the diagnostic performance of synovial NGS with traditional culture methods in diagnosing PJI. They reported a pooled sensitivity of NGS of 94% with a specificity of 89%. In comparison, culture methods had a pooled sensitivity of 70% and specificity of 94%. Areas under the curve analyses are used to convey the accuracy of a test, and NGS also commanded a higher diagnostic accuracy at 91.9% compared with 80.5% for culture. Similarly, Tan et al.28 published a study in 2024 comparing the sensitivity and specificity of NGS versus culture for the diagnosis of PJI from different types of tissue samples. In their analysis, NGS yielded a sensitivity of 93% and specificity of 94% across all specimen types, significantly outperforming culture methods, which had a sensitivity of 72% and specificity of 100%. When comparing across various types of tissue samples, the sensitivity and specificity of NGS were 83% and 94% for synovial fluid, 90% and 94% for prosthetic sonicate fluid, and 81% and 100% for periprosthetic tissue, respectively. Collectively, they determined that prosthetic sonicate fluid yielded the highest pathogen detection rate, with greater sensitivity and comparable specificity to culture. NGS was also found to be superior (sensitivity 92%) to culture (sensitivity 62.3%) in diagnosing PJI if antibiotics were administered within 2 weeks.28

Azad et al.32 evaluated the PJI diagnostic performance of the BioFire Joint Infection Panel (BFJIP) compared with NGS. The BFJIP is a multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based test recently approved by the US Food and Drug Administration that is becoming increasingly popular at various institutions. It simultaneously detects 15 gram-positive bacteria, 14 gram-negative bacteria, 2 yeast, and 8 antimicrobial resistance gene targets with a run time of about 1 hour. Sensitivities for diagnosing PJI were 56% for BFJIP and 93% for NGS, whereas specificities were 100% for BFJIP and 94% for NGS. Of note, a limitation of the BFJIP is that it does not include Staphylococcus epidermidis, which is at least partially responsible for the decreased sensitivity of the test. Overall, the study concluded that the BFJIP is a useful adjunct in certain PJI diagnostic algorithms but overall, less diagnostically effective than NGS.

By contrast, some studies found NGS to be inferior to culture. Using Musculoskeletal Infection Society criteria as the reference standard, Kildow et al.33 found that NGS yielded a sensitivity of 61% and specificity of 90%, whereas culture methods produced a higher sensitivity of 77% and specificity of 95%, with PCR having the lowest sensitivity at 18%. Although it is accepted that NGS is a highly sensitive and specific means for diagnosing PJI, the literature remains inconclusive regarding the true prognostic value, superiority, efficacy, value, and cost-effectiveness of NGS compared with culture methods. The cost-effectiveness of NGS was evaluated further by Torchia et al.34 who used a Markov state-transition model to analyze incremental cost-effectiveness ratios with a willingness-to-pay threshold of $100,000 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY). One-way sensitivity analyses demonstrated that NGS was cost-effective under the following conditions: (1) the pretest probability of PJI was >45.5%, (2) sensitivity of NGS was >70.0%, (3) specificity of NGS was >94.1%, and (4) cost per test was <$3915. Under these assumptions, the incremental cost-effectiveness ratios for culture compared with NGS was $422,784 per QALY, indicating that NGS is more cost-effective than culture. The authors, therefore, concluded that NGS is more cost-effective than culture in situations where the pretest probability of PJI is relatively high.

Bacteriophage treatment—prosthetic joint infection

Another genetic clinical opportunity in the treatment of PJI is through bacteriophages. Bacteriophages are bacterial viruses that target and invade bacterial cells by injecting phage DNA.35 Each bacteriophage is specific to a type of bacteria. Phages work by attaching to bacteria. Once attached, DNA is injected which integrates with the bacterial DNA. This leads to the formation of new bacteriophages which then go on to lyse and kill the bacterial host from within36 (Fig. 3). Bacteriophages have garnered special interest in the treatment of PJI because (1) they only target bacterial cells and avoid human cells, (2) they can be genetically engineered for targeted applications, (3) their mechanism is not susceptible to the development of resistance, and (4) they can disrupt biofilms.36–38 Antibiotics conversely are very susceptible to the development of resistance and generally target many types of bacteria. Antibiotics are systemically absorbed while phages cannot survive outside the proximity of the infection, where their bacterial targets are located. This is also why antibiotics typically present with more systemic side effects than bacteriophages.39

Stages of bacteriophage action. 1. The phage attaches to the target bacteria. 2. Phage DNA is injected, reprogramming the bacteria to produce more phage. 3. Phages replicate within the bacteria. 4. The bacterial host is lysed and killed. Released phage are able to act on more bacteria.

The discovery and early application of bacteriophages is generally credited to Drs. Twort and d’Herelle.35 A majority of early work on human applications for bacteriophages hails from Eastern Europe, but Dr. d'Herelle's laboratory produced at least 5 commercially available bacteriophage preparations for use in humans.35 Phages were very successful in treating dysentery in the early 1900s, saving many lives. Ironically, phages are generally harvested from sewage because human feces contain a substantial number of bacteriophages. With the advent of antibiotics, the bacteriophage industry waned and was used only in Eastern Europe. With antibiotic resistance on the rise, bacteriophages are regaining popularity.40

Two promising appeals of bacteriophages are (1) the targeted eradication of bacterial biofilm and (2) for prophylaxis against PJI by coating implants with bacteriophages at the time of implantation.37 Bacteria naturally produce biofilms which consist of an extracellular matrix of exopolysaccharides, proteins, teichoic acids, lipids, and extracellular DNA.40,41 The bacteria subsequently embed themselves in this film and enter a metabolically inactive state, diminishing their susceptibility to antibiotics. Moreover, the biofilm itself is a protective barrier that is partially impermeable to antibiotics, further impeding antibiotic penetration and efficacy.42 Conveniently, some phages produce endolysins and/or depolymerases, which are capable of degrading biofilms, making them a promising application for PJI.43 Kaur et al.44 investigated the adjunctive use of bacteriophage and linezolid to prevent bacterial colonization and biofilm formation on orthopedic implants. Bacteriophages were more effective than linezolid antibiotic treatment in successfully reducing Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) colonization on K-wires coated with a film impregnated with antibiotics and/or bacteriophage; however, bacteriophages were most effective when combined with antibiotics. This holds true in many other studies, where the ultimate benefit appears to come by combining antibiotics and phages together.45,46

Formal clinical trials regarding the efficacy of bacteriophages for treatment of PJI have been limited. There is currently one phase 2 double-blind, randomized, controlled clinical trial underway in France looking at the effectiveness of adding phage therapy to debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention and antibiotics for MRSA PJI. Included patients were all more than 3 months postoperative. This trial is slated to end in 2025.38 In Russia, Fedorov et al. performed a nonrandomized, prospective, open-label study with historical controls, including 45 patients with hip PJI who were followed for 12 months after single-stage revision surgery. They found that the group treated with antibiotics and phages had a significantly lower relapse rate (4.5%) compared with the control group treated with antibiotics alone (36.4%). This translates to a roughly 12-fold lower risk of relapse with bacteriophages added to treatment. However, this study is limited in that it included both acute and chronic PJI, no 2-stage exchanges, and inconsistency across antibiotics used.47 To date, there are currently no randomized clinical trials in the United States using bacteriophages for PJI treatment except for a trial that was withdrawn multiple times by the sponsor Adaptive Phage Therapeutics (APT, Gaithersburg, Maryland) after their acquisition by BiomX (Gaithersburg, Maryland).48

Other studies evaluating the use of bacteriophages have looked at their use for bone and joint infections (BJI) combined. Suh et al.38 explored 33 bone and joint infections cases treated with bacteriophages, of which 29 (87%) achieved clinical success. Four cases (12%) experienced relapse—2 with the same and 2 with different organisms. Despite this, all but 1 infection eventually achieved clinical resolution without additional surgery or phage treatments. The remaining literature primarily consists of case reports. A systematic review published in 2023 gathered data from all previous case reports using bacteriophages to treat PJI. Most patients had failed multiple revision surgeries and long-term antibiotic treatment. The researchers found that phages were highly effective in these case reports, with all patients cleared of infection at an average l4 month follow-up and mild reversible transaminitis as the only reported side effect.36

Although bacteriophages are not yet fully approved in the United States, the literature exemplifies how physicians today in the United States could use bacteriophage treatment for patients with complex PJI who have failed multiple surgeries and antibiotic regimens. Notably, the process remains cumbersome. Surgeons would require both FDA approval and Institutional Review Board expanded access for “compassionate use approval” to use phages.45 One illustrative case report describes in detail the process of how the authors were able to offer bacteriophage treatment to a patient at Mayo Clinic Hospital with a history of 11 prior re-revisions and recurrent PJI relapses.45 The researchers checked the bank of phages available in the PhageBank available through APT/BiomX but could not find one that lysed the bacteria causing the infection. Therefore, they performed de novo isolation of the appropriate phage from environmental samples. APT then isolated, cleaned, and replicated the phages before clinical treatment. After phage and antibiotic treatment, the patient was reported to be clinically free of infection at 34-week follow-up with no side effects, thereby postponing the need for amputation.

Although phage therapy is an increasingly popular frontier for the treatment of PJI, it is not without risk. Liu et al.49 produced the most comprehensive review of phage therapy risks to date. From a toxicity perspective, phage therapy is well-tolerated with a low-risk profile. The most common risks included secondary endotoxin toxicity from bacteriolysis, infusion-related hypersensitivity reactions, and other immunologic reactions as the phages are technically immunogenic biological entities. They concluded that overall risks from phage therapy were mild and that serious adverse events were extremely rare.

Genetics of Arthrofibrosis, Inflammatory Response to Metallosis, and Heterotopic Ossification

Arthrofibrosis has an estimated heritability of 28–32% and contributes to 28% of 90-day readmissions, 10% of all revisions within 5 years of total knee arthroplasty (TKA), and a 7.5× higher cost of care.50–52 Campbell et al.53 used GWS to evaluate the genetic expression of the posterior capsule tissue of patients with and without knee flexion contractures, documenting 4 genes (CSN1S1, CHAD, Sox9, and Cyr61) to be overexpressed in patients with contractures. All of these genes are known to be associated with extracellular matrix generation pathways and tissue fibrosis. Various studies have also shown that patients with a history of fibrotic disorders are also at a higher risk of manipulation under anaesthesia or lysis of adhesions after TKA including those with a history of keloid scars, adhesive capsulitis, Dupuytren disease, Ledderhose disease, and Peyronie disease.50,54,55

It has also been shown that mutations in the calpain gene are associated with an increased risk of revision in metal on metal total hip arthroplasties. The calpain gene kgp9316441 SNP was associated with a more robust inflammatory response with increased macrophage activation in patients presenting for revision secondary to metal ion disease.56 For HO, variations in the secreted frizzled-related protein-3 on the frizzled-related protein gene was shown to be associated with an increased risk of HO after total hip arthroplasty. Secreted frizzled-related protein-3 is known to inhibit proteins that increase new bone formation.57 Although the research on the genetics of arthrofibrosis, HO, and metallosis in TJA remain extremely limited, these studies suggest that patients who are found to screen positive for such diseases may benefit from focused preoperative counseling about their risks. Postoperative management may be adjusted such as to add more rigorous postoperative physical therapy for those at a high risk of arthrofibrosis.

Genetics of Postoperative Pain

Many studies have demonstrated a genetic component of postoperative pain because of differences in neurotransmitters, the inflammatory response, and serotonin pathways, all of which have downstream effects on pain response, perception, and emotional satisfaction.58 There are also important differences in metabolism which may affect the effectiveness of commonly used medications. Tramadol, for instance, is a mainstay centrally acting analgesic used to manage pain after arthroplasty. It is metabolized by the liver to yield its active metabolite by various cytochrome P450 enzymes.59

Many opioids like tramadol and oxycodone do not function until after they are metabolized by the liver. Because there are many genetic variations in CYPD6, the main enzyme for metabolism, there are also individual differences in the rate at which these medications are metabolized into their active forms. The prevalence of ultra-fast metabolizers also varies among ethnicities, and it has been shown that >10% of individuals who are Oceanic, Northern African, Middle Eastern, Ashkenazi Jews, or Puerto Rican are ultra-fast metabolizers.58 Therefore, when taking tramadol or oxycodone, an ultra-fast metabolizer would be significantly more likely to develop toxicity, whereas slow metabolizers may get minimal or no pain relief. Preoperative screening through GWS would involve identifying patients who are at a risk of toxicity or inefficacy with standard pain regimens. Personalized pain management strategies could be used to improve pain alleviation, patient satisfaction, progress with physical therapy, length of stay, and limit unplanned visits because of inadequately controlled pain.

Challenges With Genetic Advances

There remain many obstacles limiting genetic advancements and clinical applications in TJA. The relationship between genetics and complex pathologies like OA and PJI are incompletely understood. Such conditions are a mix of genetic and environmental influences. The most difficult question is to understand the balance between genetic predisposition and environmental factors for each individual. Genomic databases of variants for OA, PJI, and other pathologies are far from complete. These could prove to be extremely useful for screening in the future, particularly with the expanding use of artificial intelligence in managing genomic databases. The clinical efficacy and safety of genetic treatments such as bacteriophages and gene therapy continue to be explored. Although not currently applicable to TJA patients, gene silencing tools, such as siRNA, gene editing tools such as CRISPR, and gene delivery tools, may have potential in the clinical setting.

The FDA approval process is both costly and slow, presenting additional obstacles for translating genetic treatments to patients care.60 Pharmaceutical companies are disincentivized to pursue genetic treatments because of the high liability and extensive amount of research and development needed to bring them to market.60 Furthermore, arthroplasty-related pathologies and complications are generally not life threatening, which skews the risk-benefit ratio and impedes expedient clinical translation. There is considerable stigma and fear related to gene therapy and gene editing. Hence, it will require substantial effort to overcome beliefs that these technologies should be reserved for incurable and lethal disease.1

Another obvious constraint is cost. On average, analysis of a single sample through GWS costs between $399 and $999. One company, MicroGenDx, advertises lower costs to hospitals at $299 with a 3 day turnaround (2020 Diagnostics).61 Therefore, meticulous patient selection, as opposed to universal screening, would be favorable in the current clinical climate. Gene therapeutics is also costly. Wong et al.9 performed a simulation using 109 late-stage gene therapy clinical trials underway before 2020 to project the QALY and cost. They concluded that gene therapeutics will conservatively cost $43,110 per unit QALY, which is almost 3 times the annual expenditure of patients with cancer in the United States between 2010 and 2014. For instance, the approved OA gene therapy drug, Invossa, was sold in Korea for $4,400 and would likely be more expensive in the United States secondary to the substantial regulatory burden for drug approval.60 Gene therapy costs for spinal muscular atrophy with the drug Zolgensma have been reported to be as high as $2.1 million.9 There is no publicly available US quote for the cost of bacteriophage therapy. However, in the Republic of Georgia, phage therapy prices range between $3000 and $20,000.38 Like with other treatments, most of the cost of drugs in the United States relates to the high expense required to bring these therapies to market—research, development, clinical trials, and the regulatory process.

Accessibility to these advanced genetic interventions for diverse patient populations is also a concern. Affordability for lower-income patients will present one challenge. Notably, most of the genetic studies included in this review were performed primarily in Caucasian populations. Findings and effectiveness may be different for other ethnic and racial groups. There are ethical barriers related to the sequencing of an entire genome and for gene editing because information yielded from this process may include genetic tendencies and disorders not related to the primary reason for the test. These are tricky questions that are hotly debated.

Conclusion

Although many factors influence the care of OA, PJI, and other pathologies affecting patients who are candidates for TJA, evolving evidence supports a potential use of genetics and genetic interventions to enhance treatments and outcomes. The timeline to streamlined implementation remains questionable, but most would agree that genetic advances in TJA are growing and provide opportunities for additional studies to analyze the use of genetics to improve the care of patients. Next-generation sequencing may soon rival culture for the standard of care of PJI. Diagnosis and advancement in gene therapy, gene-editing technologies like CRISPR, or bacteriophage therapy may 1 day become commonplace. Although a majority of current investigations are preclinical, there are a subset of phase 3 clinical trials evaluating the efficacy of such novel treatments with promising results and low-risk profiles. Additional multicenter studies, clinical trials, and public buy-in are essential to propel this field into mainstay arthroplasty clinical practice.
