Neuromelioidosis presenting with the 'tunnel sign': A case report
Authors: Kamani Naresh Babu, M Keerthi, K Sai Kiran, M Narendhar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 31 yr old male came with complaints of fever of 2 weeks duration with headache. Later he developed right hemiparesis with dysarthria followed by left hemiparesis with anarthria. On examination there was spastic quadriparesis. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) brain contrast showed multiple ring-enhancing lesions of 2–4 cm size in bilateral cerebral hemispheres with central diffusion restriction. On coronal contrast sequence there was a thick-walled tubular curvilinear enhancing lesion traversing through the corpus callosum. Two sets of blood cultures were obtained before initiation of empirical antituberculous therapy, which was started along with dexamethasone. The patient's sensorium gradually deteriorated over 4 days and elective intubation was done. Repeat CT brain scan showed worsening of oedema with midline shift. Neurosurgery consultation was taken, and the patient was taken for decompressive craniectomy. Despite decompressive craniectomy, the patient succumbed to the illness on the 6th day of hospital admission. Blood cultures subsequently grew Burkholderia pseudomallei, supporting the diagnosis of neuromelioidosis. Although the diagnosis was supported by characteristic neuroimaging findings and positive blood cultures, direct microbiological confirmation from the intracranial lesion was unavailable.Keywords: Burkholderia pseudomallei, Neuromelioidosis, Microabscesses

Introduction

Burkholderia pseudomallei is a facultative, soil-dwelling, Gram-negative bacillus responsible for melioidosis, an infection endemic to several regions of Southeast Asia, including India, with increased incidence during the monsoon season [1,2]. Human infection occurs through inhalation or ingestion of contaminated soil or water, or via direct inoculation through minor skin

abrasions. Individuals with uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, chronic renal or pulmonary disease, alcohol dependence, or immunosuppression are particularly susceptible [3].

Melioidosis may manifest as a localised infection involving a single organ system—most commonly the lungs, genitourinary tract, bones, joints, or central nervous system—or as a disseminated multisystem disease [4]. Neurological involvement, termed neuromelioidosis, is uncommon, accounting for approximately 5% of reported cases. Despite its rarity, neuromelioidosis carries

a high mortality rate approaching 25% and is associated with significant neurological morbidity among survivors [4,5].

Clinical manifestations are varied and include encephalitis, headache, fever, seizures, altered consciousness, features of raised intracranial pressure, cerebellar signs, cranial nerve palsies, and focal neurological deficits [4].

Neuroimaging findings reported in neuromelioidosis include multiple cerebral microabscesses (typically less than 2 cm), meningitis, skull osteomyelitis, and extra-axial collections [4,5].

This case report aims to highlight the characteristic tunnel sign in neuromelioidosis and emphasize its value as an early neuroimaging clue to this rare but life-threatening infection.

Case details

A 31 yr old male, farmer by occupation with no prior co-morbidities, came with chief complaints of fever of 2 weeks duration, which was moderate to high-grade, associated with chills and rigors with holocranial dull aching headache. After 2 weeks of fever he developed gradual-onset weakness of right upper limb and lower limb - predominantly distal weakness associated with slurred speech which was accompanied with in 3 days by weakness of the left upper and lower limb and complete loss of speech. There was no history of seizures, vomitings, altered sensorium. There were no other associated systemic symptoms. On initial examination patient was conscious, obeying stage 3 commands with anarthria. Motor examination revealed spastic quadriparesis with hyperreflexia with extensor plantars. There was no neck stiffness. Based on history of fever with subacute quadriparesis with dysarthria later progressing to anarthria, infectious etiology was thought first. Hemogram showed a neutrophilic leucocytosis. Liver and renal functions were normal. Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) serology was negative. Neuroimaging with non-contrast computed tomography (CT) scan showed left frontoparietal hypodensity with mass effect. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) brain plain showed multiple T1 hypointense and T2/FLAIR hyperintense (Fig. 1) lesions scattered in both cerebral hemispheres (more on left side). The hyperintensities were tracking along the white matter tracts from left to right side. Contrast-enhanced MRI revealed multiple ring-enhancing lesions of 2–4 cm size in bilateral cerebral hemispheres more on left than right side. The lesions in the left frontoparietal region are conglomerated (Fig. 3). On diffusion MRI most of the lesions showed central diffusion restriction, suggestive of multiple brain abscesses (Fig. 2). On coronal contrast sequence there was a thick-walled tubular curvilinear enhancing lesion extending from the left cerebral hemisphere, traversing through the corpus callosum to the right resembling a Tunnel (Fig. 4). Two sets of blood cultures were sent before starting antituberculosis treatment (Isoniazid 5 mg/kg/day with pyridoxine, rifampicin 10 mg/kg/day, ethambutol 15 mg/kg/day, pyrazinamide 20 mg/kg/day) with dexamethasone 0.4 mg/kg/day, as tuberculosis is more common in our region and imaging is suggestive of conglomerated lesions seen in CNS tuberculosis. Lumbar puncture was deferred in view of mass effect. Patient sensorium gradually deteriorated over 4 days in our hospital and to protect airway, elective intubation was done. Repeat CT brain showed worsening cerebral oedema with midline shift. In view of impending cerebral herniation, the patient underwent emergency decompressive craniectomy as a life-saving measure. No biopsy or sampling of the intracranial lesion was performed, as the procedure was undertaken solely for decompression. The patient developed haemodynamic instability during surgery and, despite intensive supportive management, succumbed on the sixth day of hospital admission. Two days later, both sets of blood cultures grew Burkholderia pseudomallei, supporting the diagnosis of neuromelioidosis.

Discussion

A characteristic imaging feature of cerebral melioidosis is its tendency to spread along white matter pathways, involving both projection and commissural fibers, with extension to the contralateral hemisphere [6]. The disease process frequently originates in one cerebral hemisphere and progresses across the corpus callosum to the opposite side. Involvement of both supratentorial and infratentorial white matter has been documented, with occasional extension into the spinal cord [7,8].

Chadwick et al. highlighted acute frontal and maxillary sinusitis as an associated finding in neuromelioidosis, suggesting possible contiguous spread or a portal of entry [9]. Another distinctive feature described on MRI is continuous intra-axial extension of infection from supratentorial white matter into the brainstem, resulting in the formation of microabscesses aligned along white matter tracts [10].

Radiologically, cerebral melioidosis commonly presents as single or multiple pyogenic microabscesses measuring less than 2 cm, often associated with surrounding vasogenic edema and mass effect. On CT, these lesions appear hypodense, whereas MRI typically demonstrates T2/FLAIR hyperintensity and T1 hypointensity with central diffusion restriction on diffusion-weighted imaging [5,11]. Contrast-enhanced MRI may reveal various enhancement patterns, including cystic, nodular, linear, or coalescent ring-enhancing lesions [11]. Spread along white matter tracts and cranial nerve pathways has also been described [6,12].

Extra-axial involvement may be seen in the form of leptomeningeal enhancement, subdural empyema, epidural collections, calvarial or vertebral osteomyelitis, subgaleal abscesses, and associated paranasal sinusitis [11].

The tunnel sign, characterized by tubular or curvilinear enhancement on contrast MRI, was initially described as a specific radiological feature of cerebral sparganosis [10,13]. Similar appearances have since been reported in cerebral melioidosis, as demonstrated in the present case, and in cerebral listeriosis [[13], [14], [15]]. This imaging pattern likely reflects tract-based intraparenchymal spread of infection. When identified, the tunnel sign may therefore serve as a valuable diagnostic clue pointing toward a limited group of infectious etiologies. In the present case, empirical antituberculous therapy was initiated because the patient was from a tuberculosis-endemic region and presented with a subacute febrile illness and multiple conglomerated ring-enhancing cerebral lesions, which are characteristic imaging features of central nervous system tuberculosis. At the time of treatment initiation, the diagnosis of neuromelioidosis had not been considered, and blood culture results were still pending. The subsequent isolation of Burkholderia pseudomallei from two sets of blood cultures, together with the characteristic neuroimaging findings and compatible clinical presentation, made neuromelioidosis the most likely underlying aetiology. This case highlights the diagnostic challenge posed by neuromelioidosis in regions where tuberculosis is highly prevalent and emphasises the importance of considering melioidosis in the differential diagnosis of multiple cerebral abscesses, particularly when characteristic neuroimaging features such as the tunnel sign are present.

Limitations

As this is a single case report, the findings cannot be generalised. In addition to the absence of direct microbiological or histopathological confirmation from the intracranial lesion, cerebrospinal fluid examination could not be performed because of significant mass effect, and no autopsy was available for pathological confirmation. Furthermore, because the patient deteriorated rapidly and succumbed despite emergency decompressive craniectomy, the therapeutic response to targeted anti-melioidosis treatment could not be assessed.

Conclusion

The clinical presentation of cerebral melioidosis is often nonspecific and may closely mimic other central nervous system infections, making diagnosis challenging. Neuroimaging plays a crucial role in raising suspicion for this entity. The presence of multiple small ring-enhancing microabscesses, particularly those less than 2 cm in size, along with tubular or curvilinear enhancement tracking along white matter tracts, cranial nerve nuclei, or cranial

nerves, should prompt consideration of neuromelioidosis.Recognition of the tunnel sign may provide an additional and potentially specific imaging clue. When present, this sign should raise suspicion for a narrow differential diagnosis that includes neuromelioidosis, cerebral listeriosis, and sparganosis, thereby facilitating early diagnosis and appropriate management.
