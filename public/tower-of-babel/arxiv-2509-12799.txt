Levinson’s theorem for dissipative systems, or how to count the asymptotically disappearing states
A. Alexander, J. Faupin, S. Richard

Abstract
We consider dissipative Schrödinger operators of the form H = − Δ + V ⁡ ( x ) H=-\Delta+V(x) on 𝖫 2 ​ ( ℝ 3 ) \mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}) , with V ⁡ ( x ) V(x) a complex, bounded and decaying potential with a non-positive imaginary part. We prove a topological version of Levinson’s theorem corresponding to an index theorem for the discrete, complex spectrum of H H .

Introduction

Every quantum system is dissipative, to some extent, in the sense that part of its energy may be irreversibly transferred to the environment.
There are various ways to mathematically represent a dissipative quantum system.
In the theory of open quantum systems, see for example
[
3
,
10
]
, the system of interest is coupled to a reservoir
representing the environment. The latter can be modeled, for instance, by a bosonic field.
A celebrated effective description of the evolution of dissipative quantum systems,
obtained by first tracing out the degrees of freedom of the environment, is given by Lindblad’s master equation
[
27
]
.
In this setting the mixed states of the system are associated to density matrices – positive trace class operators of trace
1
–
and the generator of the dynamics, the Lindbladian, acts on the Banach space of trace class operators.
Another simple, phenomenological way to describe a particular dissipative quantum system, given by a neutron interacting with a fixed nucleus,
was introduced in
[
17
]
. It is now called the nuclear optical model. Here the dynamics of the neutron is generated
by a Schrödinger operator

H=H_{0}+V

(1.1)

on
\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, where
H_{0}=-\Delta
corresponds to the free, kinetic energy of the neutron (in suitable units)
and
V
is a complex potential with non-positive imaginary part corresponding to the effective interaction
between the neutron and the nucleus.
A feature of the model is that it allows for a description of the phenomenon of capture of the neutron by the nucleus,
leading to the formation of a compound nucleus.
The form of the potential
V
is deduced from the scattering data obtained from experiments.
We refer to
[
21
,
16
]
for extensions of the model to more complex situations.
In principle, one can similarly represent the dynamics of a dissipative quantum system,
which can be captured by another system, by a semi-group of contractions generated
by a dissipative operator in the Hilbert space corresponding to the pure states of the system.

The spectral and scattering theory of dissipative operators in Hilbert spaces,
in relation with the nuclear optical model, have been studied by many authors.
We refer, among others, to the works of Martin
[
29
]
, Davies
[
4
,
5
]
and Neidhardt
[
31
]

for general results in an abstract setting, Mochizuki
[
30
]
, Simon
[
39
]
, Wang
[
41
,
42
]
, Wang and Zhu
[
43
]

for dissipative Schödinger operators of the form (
1.1
), and

[
12
,
14
,
15
]
for more recent developments.
Let us also mention
[
11
,
14
]
for applications to the scattering theory of the Lindblad master equation,
and
[
13
,
18
]
for extensions to non-dissipative operators still related to complex Schrödinger operators
and to the nuclear optical model.

In dissipative quantum scattering theory, the wave operators are defined by the time-dependent expressions

W_{-}:=\mathop{\hbox{\rm s-}\lim}\nolimits_{t\to\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{itH_{0}}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad W_{+}:=\mathop{\hbox{\rm s-}\lim}\nolimits_{t\to\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{itH^{*}}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH_{0}},

(1.2)

where
H^{*}=H_{0}+\overline{V}
stands for the adjoint of
H
, whenever these strong limits exist.
In this case, the scattering operator is defined by the relation

S\equiv S(H,H_{0}):=W_{+}^{*}W_{-}.

(1.3)

It will be recalled in the next section that these three operators are contractions. Since
S
commutes with
H_{0}
,
it can be diagonalized in the sense that, for a.e.
\lambda>0
and
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
,

[\mathscr{F}_{0}S\mathscr{F}_{0}^{*}\varphi](\lambda,\omega)=\big[S(\lambda)\varphi(\lambda)\big](\omega),

where
\mathscr{F}_{0}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathrm{d}\lambda;\mathfrak{h})
is a unitary map satisfying

\big[[\mathscr{F}_{0}H_{0}\mathscr{F}_{0}^{*}\varphi](\lambda)\big](\omega)=\lambda[\varphi(\lambda)](\omega),

for suitable
\varphi\in\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathrm{d}\lambda;\mathfrak{h})
, with
\mathfrak{h}:=\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
.
We refer again to the next section for more details.
The scattering matrix
S(\lambda)
, as an operator acting on
\mathfrak{h}
, is also a contraction.
In particular, contrary to the self-adjoint setting,
S(\lambda)
may not be invertible.
An energy
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
such that
S(\lambda)
is not invertible is called a spectral singularity.
In our context, it corresponds to a real resonance for the Schrödinger operator
H
.
In the sequel we shall assume that all
\lambda>0
are regular points for
H
,
i.e. that
S(\lambda)
is always invertible.

As in the works
[
14
,
15
]
, we introduce two subspaces specific to dissipative systems, namely

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)

\displaystyle:=\big\{f\in\mathcal{H}\mid\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH}f\|=0\big\},\quad\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H^{*}):=\big\{f\in\mathcal{H}\mid\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{itH^{*}}f\|=0\big\},

(1.4)

and call them the spaces of
asymptotically disappearing states
for
H
and
H^{*}
, respectively.
For the nuclear optical model, these spaces play an important role as the normalized vectors in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)

correspond to the initial states of the neutron which lead to an absorption by the nucleus,
asymptotically as
t\to\infty
.
One can verify that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)
contains the discrete spectral subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{disc}}(H)
, defined as the closure of the vector space spanned by the generalized
eigenstates of
H
associated to a complex eigenvalue with negative imaginary part.
However, in general,
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)
may contain further states (unless
V
is compactly supported, for instance; see
[
14
,
15
]
).
We refer again to the next section for a more detailed discussion on the spectrum and spectral subspaces for
H
and
H^{*}
.

Back to the self-adjoint setting, it is known that the number of bound states of the perturbed system
can be evaluated on scattering data. This relation bears the name of
Levinson’s theorem

and was discovered by Levinson in
[
26
]
in the context of
a Schrödinger operator with a spherically symmetric potential.
Similar investigations have then been generalized in numerous papers, and
we only mention the two review papers
[
28
,
36
]
which contain several
earlier references. On a very specific example, the self-adjoint setting
was extended in
[
32
]
where it is shown that complex eigenvalues can also
be counted thanks to Levinson’s theorem. For more general dissipative systems,
it was not known if an analog of Levinson’s theorem holds, and if it does
what exactly is counted ?

As shown in the following sections, not only Levinson’s theorem holds for dissipative systems in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
but a precise formula can be exhibited, namely

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\lambda\;\!\left(\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)^{-1}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)+c\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)=-{\mathrm{dim}}\big(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)\big),

with
c=\frac{i}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}x\;\!V(x)
, and the integral on the l.h.s. understood as an improper Riemann integral.
The precise statement is given in Theorem
4.9
.
It means that the dimension of the
vector space generated by the asymptotically disappearing states can be computed based on the scattering
matrix only. Note that, in this specific setting, no correction due to threshold effects appears (also called
half-bound states). On the other hand, the factor
c\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is quite common for scattering
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, even if
V
is real-valued.

An interesting consequence of the previous equality, which appears to be new for non-compactly supported potentials,
is that under our assumptions we have
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{disc}}(H)
,
namely the asymptotically disappearing states coincide with the linear combinations of generalized eigenstates of
H
.
This follows from the fact
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)
is finite-dimensional, by the previous equality.

The proof of the above equality is provided in Section
4
. It relies both on a
C^{*}
-algebraic
framework and on some deep analysis for the computation of a generalized winding number.
The key ingredient is an explicit formula for the wave operator
W_{-}
which is derived in Section
3
.
Finally, general information on scattering theory in the dissipative setting and for Schrödinger operators
is provided in Section
2
. The reader familiar with some of these topics can easily skip the corresponding section.
However, we provide all details, since dissipative systems have been less studied than their self-adjoint counterpart.

2
Framework and scattering theory

In the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}:=\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
we consider the dissipative operator
H
defined by

H:=H_{0}+V

where
H_{0}
corresponds to the Laplace operator
-\Delta
and where
V
is the multiplication operator by a measurable and bounded function
V:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying
\Im V\leq 0
. Here
\Im V\leq 0
means that the imaginary part of
V(x)
is non-positive for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Additional decay conditions on
V
will be imposed on due time.
Clearly, since
V
is bounded, the domain of
H
corresponds to the domain of
H_{0}
, namely the
second Sobolev space on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

We recall that an operator
A
is called dissipative if
\Im(\langle f,Af\rangle)\leq 0
for all
f\in\mathcal{D}(A)
, and maximal dissipative if it is dissipative and has no proper dissipative extension. We denote by
\mathbb{C}_{-}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,\Im z\leq 0\}
the complex lower-half plane.

We begin with the following two easy lemmas whose proofs are recalled for completeness.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
V:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}_{-}
is a bounded, measurable function. The following properties hold.

(i)

H
is a maximal dissipative operator on
\mathcal{H}
with domain

\mathcal{D}(H)=\mathcal{D}(H_{0}).

(ii)

The operator
-iH
generates a strongly continuous group
\{\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
which satisfies

\big\|\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH}\big\|\leq 1\,\hbox{ if }\,t\geq 0,\qquad\big\|\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH}\big\|\leq\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{\|V\|_{L^{\infty}}|t|}\,\hbox{ if }\,t\leq 0.

In particular,
-iH
generates the strongly continuous semigroup of contractions
\{\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH}\}_{t\geq 0}
.

(iii)

The adjoint of
H
is

H^{*}=H_{0}+\overline{V},

with domain
\mathcal{D}(H^{*})=\mathcal{D}(H_{0})
, where
\overline{V}
is the complex conjugate of
V
. Moreover,
iH^{*}
generates of a strongly continuous group
\{\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{itH^{*}}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
such that
\{\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{itH^{*}}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a semigroup of contractions.

Proof.

(i)
Since
V
is bounded, the operator
H
is closed with domain
\mathcal{D}(H)=\mathcal{D}(H_{0})
. Moreover,
H
is dissipative since, for all
f\in\mathcal{D}(H)
,

\Im(\langle f,Hf\rangle)=\Im(\langle f,Vf\rangle)\leq 0,

by assumption. To verify that
H
is maximal dissipative, it then suffices to show that
\mathop{\mathsf{Ran}}\nolimits(H-i\lambda)=\mathcal{H}
for some
\lambda>0
, see
[
34
, Corol. p. 201]
. This easily follows from the identity

H-i\lambda=H_{0}-i\lambda+V=(H_{0}-i\lambda)\big(1+(H_{0}-i\lambda)^{-1}V\big).

Indeed, since
H_{0}
is self-adjoint,
(H_{0}-i\lambda)
is invertible, and we have the estimate
\|(H_{0}-i\lambda)^{-1}V\|\leq\lambda^{-1}\|V\|_{L^{\infty}}
which implies that
1+(H_{0}-i\lambda)^{-1}V
is also invertible for
\lambda>\|V\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

(ii)
Since
H_{0}
is self-adjoint,
-iH_{0}
generates the strongly continuous unitary group
\{\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH_{0}}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
. Hence, since
V
is bounded, a perturbation argument (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Thm. 11.4.1]
) shows that
-iH
generates a strongly continuous group
\{\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
satisfying

\big\|\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH}\big\|\leq\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{\|V\|_{L^{\infty}}|t|},

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. The fact that
\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH}
is a contraction for
t\geq 0
is a consequence of the fact that
H
is maximal dissipative (see e.g.
[
7
, Thm. 10.4.2]
).

(iii)
It easily follows from the definition of
H^{*}
and direct computations that
H^{*}=H_{0}+\overline{V}
with domain
\mathcal{D}(H^{*})=\mathcal{D}(H_{0})
. The same argument as in
(ii)
then yields
(iii)
.
∎

The next lemma concerns the spectrum of
H
. Since
H
is dissipative, one easily verifies that its spectrum,
\sigma(H)
, is contained in the lower half-plane
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid\Im z\leq 0\}
. We now assume that
V
vanishes at
\infty
and that
\Im V<0
on a non-trivial open set. The essential spectrum of
H
is defined by

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(H):=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mid H-\lambda\text{ is not Fredholm}\},

while the discrete spectrum is

\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(H):=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mid\lambda\text{ is an isolated eigenvalue of }H\text{ and }{\mathrm{dim}}\,\mathop{\mathsf{Ran}}\nolimits(\Pi_{\lambda}(H))<\infty\},

where
\Pi_{\lambda}(H)=(2i\pi)^{-1}\int_{\gamma}(z-H)^{-1}\mathrm{d}z
stands for the usual Riesz projection associated to
\lambda
, with
\gamma
a circle oriented counterclockwise and centered at
\lambda
, with sufficiently small radius.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that
V:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}_{-}
is a bounded, measurable function such that
V(x)\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
and
\Im V<0
on a non-trivial open set. The following properties hold:

(i)

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(H)=\sigma(H_{0})=[0,\infty)
.

(ii)

\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(H)=\sigma(H)\setminus\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(H)
. Moreover
\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(H)
consists of isolated eigenvalues with non-positive imaginary parts, of finite algebraic multiplicities, and that may only accumulate at
\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(H)
.

(iii)

H
does not have real eigenvalues.

Proof.

Since
V
is bounded and
V(x)\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
,
V
is a relatively compact perturbation of the self-adjoint operator
H_{0}
. This implies that
\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(H)=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(H_{0})
and hence
(i)
since
H_{0}=-\Delta
.

For
(ii)
, by using
[
35
, Cor. 2 of Thm. XIII.14]
and
[
8
, Thms IX.1.6 and
IX.2.1]
, it follows that the different definitions of essential spectra for non self-adjoint operators considered in
[
8
, Chap. IX]
all coincide. In particular we have
\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(H)=\sigma(H)\setminus\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(H)
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(H)
is at most countable and can only accumulate at points of
\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(H)
.
It remains to show that any eigenvalue in
\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(H)
has a finite algebraic multiplicity. Let
\lambda\in\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(H)
. Since the range of the Riesz projection
\Pi_{\lambda}(H)
is finite,
[
35
, Thm. XII.5 (d)]
yields that
\mathop{\mathsf{Ran}}\nolimits(\Pi_{\lambda}(H))
is spanned by the generalized eigenvectors of
H

associated to
\lambda
, i.e., by the vectors
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(H^{k})
such that
(H-\lambda)^{k}\varphi=0
for some integer
k
, which in turn implies that
{\mathrm{dim}}\,\mathop{\mathsf{Ran}}\nolimits(\Pi_{\lambda}(H))
coincides with the algebraic multiplicity of
\lambda
.

For
(iii)
, suppose that
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is an eigenvalue of
H
and let
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(H)
,
\varphi\neq 0
, be such that
(H-\lambda)\varphi=0
. Taking the scalar product with
\varphi
and then the imaginary part, we obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}x\,(\Im V)(x)|\varphi(x)|^{2}=0,

and hence, since
\Im V\leq 0
,
\varphi=0
on
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits(\Im V)
. Since
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits(\Im V)
contains a non-trivial open set, by the unique continuation principle (see e.g.
[
35
, Thm. XIII.63]
), this yields
\varphi=0
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Several spectral subspaces can be naturally introduced for dissipative operators in Hilbert spaces. We refer to
[
13
]
for a thorough discussion in the more general context of non-self-adjoint operators. Here we only mention the discrete spectral subspace

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{disc}}(H):=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\nolimits\big\{\varphi\in\mathop{\mathsf{Ran}}\nolimits(\Pi_{\lambda}(H))\mid\lambda\in\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(H)\big\}^{\mathrm{cl}},

where for a set
\Omega
,
\Omega^{\mathrm{cl}}
stands for the closure of
\Omega
. Recalling that the space of asymptotically disappearing states
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)
has been introduced in (
1.4
), we have the following easy lemma which follows directly from
[
13
, Prop. 5.6]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
V:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}_{-}
is a bounded, measurable function such that
V(x)\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
and
\Im V<0
on a non-trivial open set. Then

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{disc}}(H)\subset\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H).

It is also proved in
[
14
, Thm. 6.1]
that the equality
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{disc}}(H)=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)
holds when
V
is compactly supported
and
0
is neither an eigenvalue nor a resonance of
-\Delta+\Re V
.
Our main result provides different conditions ensuring that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{disc}}(H)=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)
, using the following easy lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Under the conditions of Lemma
2.3
and if
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)
is finite dimensional, then

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{disc}}(H)=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H).

Proof.

Clearly,
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)
is an invariant subspace for
H
. Denote by
\tilde{H}
the restriction of
H
to
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)
. For all
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)
, we have
\|\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-it\tilde{H}}\varphi\|=\|\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH}\varphi\|\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.
Since
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)
is finite dimensional, it follows from Liapunov’s Theorem, see e.g.
[
9
, Thm. 2.10]
, that
all eigenvalues of
\tilde{H}
have a negative imaginary part.
Therefore, any
\varphi
in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)
is a finite linear combination of generalized eigenstates of
\tilde{H}
, and hence of
H
,
associated to eigenvalues with negative imaginary parts.
In other words,
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)\subset\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{disc}}(H)
. Together with Lemma
2.3
, this gives the desired equality.
∎

Our aim is to study the wave operator and the scattering operators for the pair
(H,H_{0})
.
Recall that for dissipative operators they are defined by the time-dependent expressions (
1.2
) and (
1.3
).
By Lemma
2.1
, when they exist, these operators are contractions.
For subsequent investigations it will be necessary to have stationary expressions for these operators.
For that purpose and for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
let us denote by

R_{0}(z)
the resolvent
(H_{0}-z)^{-1}
of
H_{0}
. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
\varepsilon>0
we also set
R(\lambda+i\varepsilon)
for
(H-\lambda-i\varepsilon)^{-1}

and
R^{*}(\lambda-i\varepsilon)
for
(H^{*}-\lambda+i\varepsilon)^{-1}
.
The proof of the next lemma is fairly standard for self-adjoint operators. It is not difficult to adapt it to the setting studied in this paper. For the convenience of the reader, we provide the details.

Lemma 2.5
.

If the wave operators
W_{\pm}
exist, then for any
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
the following equalities hold:

\langle W_{+}f,g\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\langle R_{0}(\lambda+i\varepsilon)f,R(\lambda+i\varepsilon)g\rangle

and

\langle W_{-}f,g\rangle=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\langle R_{0}(\lambda-i\varepsilon)f,R^{*}(\lambda-i\varepsilon)g\rangle.

Proof.

We give the proof for
W_{+}
, the proof for
W_{-}
is similar.
Since
\{\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a contraction semigroup and
\{\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH_{0}}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is a unitary group, we have

R(\lambda+i\varepsilon)f=i\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-\varepsilon t}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-it(H-\lambda)}f,\qquad R_{0}(\lambda+i\varepsilon)f=i\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-\varepsilon t}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-it(H_{0}-\lambda)}f,

for all
\varepsilon>0
and
f\in\mathcal{H}
with
\|f\|=1
, and

\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\|R(\lambda+i\varepsilon)f\|^{2}\leq 1,\qquad\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\|R_{0}(\lambda+i\varepsilon)f\|^{2}=1.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
. Using Lebesgue’s dominated convergence Theorem, we write

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\langle R_{0}(\lambda+i\varepsilon)f,R(\lambda+i\varepsilon)g\rangle=\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-\delta\lambda^{2}}\langle R_{0}(\lambda+i\varepsilon)f,R(\lambda+i\varepsilon)g\rangle,

(2.1)

and then compute, for all
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-\delta\lambda^{2}}\langle R_{0}(\lambda+i\varepsilon)f,R(\lambda+i\varepsilon)g\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-\delta\lambda^{2}}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-\varepsilon(t+t^{\prime})}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{i\lambda(t^{\prime}-t)}\langle\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH_{0}}f,\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-it^{\prime}H}g\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\int_{-t}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-\delta\lambda^{2}}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-2\varepsilon t}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-\varepsilon s}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{i\lambda s}\langle\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH_{0}}f,\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i(t+s)H}g\rangle,

where we used the change of variables
t^{\prime}=t+s
in the last equality. Using Fubini’s Theorem and computing the integral in
\lambda
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-\delta\lambda^{2}}\langle R_{0}(\lambda+i\varepsilon)f,R(\lambda+i\varepsilon)g\rangle

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}(2\delta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\int_{-t}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-s^{2}/(4\delta)}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-2\varepsilon t}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-\varepsilon s}\langle\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH_{0}}f,\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i(t+s)H}g\rangle

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}2^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\int_{-(4\delta)^{-1/2}t}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-\tau^{2}}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-2\varepsilon t}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-(4\delta)^{1/2}\varepsilon\tau}\langle\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH_{0}}f,\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i(4\delta)^{1/2}\tau H}g\rangle,

where we used the change of variables

. Applying again Lebesgue’s dominated convergence Theorem and then computing the integral in

, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-\delta\lambda^{2}}\langle R_{0}(\lambda+i\varepsilon)f,R(\lambda+i\varepsilon)g\rangle

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-2\varepsilon t}\langle\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH_{0}}f,\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH}g\rangle.

Inserting this into (
2.1
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\langle R_{0}(\lambda+i\varepsilon)f,R(\lambda+i\varepsilon)g\rangle

\displaystyle=2\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-2\varepsilon t}\langle\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH_{0}}f,\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH}g\rangle

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-2s}\langle\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i\varepsilon^{-1}sH_{0}}f,\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i\varepsilon^{-1}sH}g\rangle

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-2s}\langle\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{i\varepsilon^{-1}sH^{*}}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i\varepsilon^{-1}sH_{0}}f,g\rangle,

by the change of variables
s=\varepsilon t
. A last application of Lebesgue’s dominated convergence Theorem gives the result.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Suppose that

is a bounded, measurable function satisfying (

\mathop{\mathsf{Ran}}\nolimits(W_{+})^{{\mathrm{cl}}}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)^{\perp},\qquad\mathop{\mathsf{Ran}}\nolimits(W_{-})^{{\mathrm{cl}}}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H^{*})^{\perp}.

Proof.

We prove the lemma for
W_{+}
, the proof for
W_{-}
is identical.
Writing

\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{itH^{*}}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH_{0}}\varphi=\varphi+i\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{i\tau H^{*}}\overline{u}v^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i\tau H_{0}}\varphi,

for any

, one sees that it suffices to show that the integral converges as

, we have

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{i\tau H^{*}}\overline{u}v^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i\tau H_{0}}\varphi\Big\|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\psi\in\mathcal{H},\|\psi\|=1}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}\tau\big|\big\langle v\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i\tau H}\psi,\overline{u}v\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{i\tau H_{0}}\varphi\big\rangle\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\psi\in\mathcal{H},\|\psi\|=1}\Big(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}\tau\big\|v\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i\tau H}\psi\big\|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}\tau\big\|v\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{i\tau H_{0}}\varphi\big\|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(2.6)

where we used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in the second inequality. Since
\alpha>1
in (
2.3
), it is well-known,
using the explicit expression of the kernel of

, that there exists

such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\|v\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{\pm i\tau H_{0}}\varphi\|^{2}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\|vR_{0}(\lambda\mp i0)\varphi\|^{2}\leq C_{v}\|\varphi\|^{2}.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\|v\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i\tau H}\psi\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\|vR(\lambda+i0)\psi\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\|vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)\psi\|^{2}+\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\|vR(\lambda+i0)vuvR_{0}(\lambda+i0)\psi\|^{2}.

2.6

the estimate (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\|v\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i\tau H}\psi\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}_{v}\|\psi\|^{2},

(2.8)

2.7
) and (
2.8
),

[
14

is only a contraction (not a unitary operator) and hence it may not be invertible on

. An energy

such that

is not invertible is called a spectral singularity. The reader is referred to
[
14
,
15
,
13
]
, where the notion of spectral singularity plays a central role, for various characterizations of this notion in an abstract context. Note that the previous lemma shows that the threshold energy
0
cannot be a spectral singularity if
V
decays fast enough; see also
[
41
, Thm. 1.1]
or
[
13
, Prop. 3.7]
.
A characterization of spectral singularity in terms of real resonance is also possible, see
for example
[
13
, Thm. 3.6]
.

In the next statement we provide sufficient conditions for the invertibility of the scattering
matrix.

Lemma 2.10
.

Suppose that
V:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}_{-}
is a bounded, measurable function satisfying (
2.3
) with
\alpha>3
and
\Im V<0
on a non-trivial open set.

\lambda>0
be such that the operator
\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v

is invertible in
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Then the operator
S(\lambda)
is invertible in
\mathscr{B}(\mathfrak{h})
,
and the map

[0,\infty)\ni\lambda\mapsto S(\lambda)^{-1}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathfrak{h})

is continuous. Moreover, if
\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v

is invertible in
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
for all
\lambda>0
, then
S
is invertible in
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
W_{\pm}
have closed ranges.

Proof.

We firstly recall the relation
2\pi i\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)=R_{0}(\lambda+i0)-R_{0}(\lambda-i0)
and make the computation

\displaystyle S(\lambda)\big(1+2\pi i\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*})

\displaystyle=1-2\pi i\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}+2\pi i\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}

\displaystyle-(2\pi i)^{2}\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}

\displaystyle=1-2\pi i\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\big(\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}-\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v\big)^{-1}\big)v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}

\displaystyle-2\pi i\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}v\big(R_{0}(\lambda+i0)-R_{0}(\lambda-i0)\big)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}

\displaystyle=1,

so that
1+2\pi i\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}
is a right inverse for

. A similar calculation shows that it is a left inverse and so
S(\lambda)
is invertible with inverse given by

\displaystyle S(\lambda)^{-1}

\displaystyle=1+2\pi i\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}.

If
\bar{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v
is invertible for all
\lambda>0
, then it follows from Lemma
2.6
that the map

[0,\infty)\ni\lambda\mapsto\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v\big)^{-1}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})

is continuous. Together with Lemma
2.8
, this proves the continuity of

on
[0,\infty)
.
Arguing as in the proof of Lemma
2.9

as

.
Therefore

is bounded on

, which implies that

is invertible in
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

Finally, to prove that

has a closed range, it suffices to write, for any

\displaystyle\|W_{-}\varphi\|\geq\|W_{+}^{*}W_{-}\varphi\|=\|S\varphi\|\geq\|S^{-1}\|^{-1}\|\varphi\|,

where we used that

4
Levinson’s theorem

Motivated by the expressions obtained in the previous section, let us introduce
a
C^{*}
-algebraic framework.
Note that since Theorem
3.3
holds under the conditions
\alpha>7
in (
2.3
) and
\Im V<0
on a non-trivial open set, we shall assume these assumptions throughout this section. Clearly, some of the statements could be obtained under weaker conditions.

Recall that
A
denotes the generator of dilations in
\mathcal{H}
while
H_{0}
stands for the Laplace
operator. If we set
B:=\frac{1}{2}\ln(H_{0})
, then the operators
A
and
B
satisfy the important relation
[iB,A]=1
, or more precisely

\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{itB}A\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itB}=A+t,\qquad\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{isA}B\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-isA}=B-s.

We also recall that the spectrum of
A
is
\mathbb{R}
, and that the spectrum of
H_{0}
is
[0,\infty)
.
We then set

\mathscr{E}:=C^{*}\Big(\eta(A)\psi(H_{0})\mid\eta\in C\big([-\infty,\infty];\mathbb{C}\big)\hbox{ and }\psi\in C\big([0,\infty);\mathscr{K}(\mathfrak{h})\big)\Big)^{+}

where the exponent
+
means that
\mathbb{C}
times the identity has been added to the algebra.
In the context of Schrödinger operators in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
this
C^{*}
-algebra has already been introduced
and studied in
[
25
, Sec. 4]
.

Let us state a few results about this algebra. It is known that
\mathscr{K}(\mathcal{H})\subset\mathscr{E}
, and
since
\mathscr{K}(\mathcal{H})
is an ideal in
\mathscr{E}
the quotient algebra can be computed. One has

\mathscr{E}/\mathscr{K}(\mathcal{H})\cong C\big(\square;\mathscr{K}(\mathfrak{h})\big)^{+},

where
\square
denotes the edges of a square. More precisely, if we set

q:\mathscr{E}\to C\big(\square;\mathscr{K}(\mathfrak{h})\big)^{+}

for the quotient map, and if we consider a typical element of
\mathscr{E}
of the form
\eta(A)\psi(H_{0})

one has

q\big(\eta(A)\psi(H_{0})\big)=\Big(\eta(\cdot)\psi(0),\eta(+\infty)\psi(\cdot),\eta(\cdot)\psi(+\infty),\eta(-\infty)\psi(\cdot)\Big)

where (by convention) we started by the edge on the left of the square and list the elements clockwise.
Note also that continuity holds at the four corners, as it can easily be checked.

Since the three algebras can be fitted in the short-exact sequence

0\to\mathscr{K}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathscr{E}\to C\big(\square;\mathscr{K}(\mathfrak{h})\big)^{+}\to 0,

the index map in
K
-theory maps the
K_{1}
-group of
C\big(\square;\mathscr{K}(\mathfrak{h})\big)^{+}

to the
K_{0}
-group of
\mathscr{K}(\mathcal{H})
.
It is well-known that both groups can be identified with
\mathbb{Z}
, with the usual trace for the former.
For the latter, the winding number of the pointwise determinant induces the isomorphism.
However, a careful use of this functional is necessary, as it will appear later in the application.

Our interest in the algebra
\mathscr{E}
is that the wave operator
W_{-}
belongs to it, as a
consequence of Theorem
3.3
. Indeed, the scattering operator can be thought as an operator
of the form
S(H_{0})
with a function

[0,\infty)\ni\lambda\mapsto S(\lambda)\in\mathscr{K}(\mathfrak{h})

continuous and having limits at
0
and at
+\infty
.
It then follows that the image of
W_{-}
in the quotient algebra can be computed and one infers that

q(W_{-})=\big(1,S(\cdot),1,1\big)

(4.1)

where the facts that
S(0)=1
and
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}S(\lambda)=1
(see Lemma
2.9
) have been taken into account.
If in addition
S(\cdot)
is invertible, we can deduce two complementary sets of information:

(i)

Since the element
q(W_{-})
computed in (
4.1
) is invertible, it follows that the operator
W_{-}
is a Fredholm operator (Atkinson’s theorem, see for example
[
33
, Prop. 3.3.11]
) and that its range is closed.
Let us then define the orthogonal projection
P_{-}
by

P_{-}\mathcal{H}=\mathop{\mathsf{Ran}}\nolimits(W_{-})^{\bot}.

(4.2)

Under our current set of assumptions (recalled below) this projection is finite dimensional.

(ii)

The element
q(W_{-})
defines an element
[q(W_{-})]_{1}
of the
K_{1}
-group of the
C^{*}
-algebra
C\big(\square;\mathscr{K}(\mathfrak{h})\big)^{+}
.
Consequently, it follows that this element is related to the Fredholm index of
W_{-}
by the relation

-{\mathrm{dim}}(P_{-})=\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{-})=\big(K_{0}(\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits)\circ\delta_{1}\big)([q(W_{-})]_{1}),

(4.3)

where the first equality follows from the injectivity of
W_{-}
given by Lemma
2.7
, and where
\delta_{1}
stands for the index map in
K
-theory.
We refer to
[
38
, Prop. 9.4.2]
for the second equality and for more explanations.

Our final aim is to get an analytic formula for the computation of (
4.3
).
This will be evaluated with an expression of the form

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\lambda\;\!\left(\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)^{-1}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)+c\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)

for a suitable constant
c
, and whenever
S(\lambda)^{-1}
makes sense.
Here and in the sequel, any integrals of this type should be understood as improper Riemann integrals.
Note that previous investigations of this type made use of the spectral shift function for the pair
(H,H_{0})
. Since
H
is not self-adjoint, this tool seems no longer available to us. Nevertheless, enough of the elements of the proof can still be used to construct an integral formula for the index.

First we analyze some properties of the determinant of the scattering matrix.
Recall from Lemma
2.9
that
S(\lambda)-1\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathfrak{h})
for all
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
and that the map
\lambda\mapsto S(\lambda)-1
is continuously differentiable in
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathfrak{h})
, with
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathfrak{h})
the set of trace-class operator on the Hilbert space
\mathfrak{h}
.
Thus we infer from
[
19
, Equation IV.1.14]
that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\ln\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)^{-1}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)

for all
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
, whenever
S(\lambda)
is invertible.

The following is another straightforward adaptation of
[
45
, Prop. 9.1.1, 9.1.2 and 9.1.3]
.
For its statement we use the notation

D_{2}(z):={\det}_{2}\big(1+uvR_{0}(z)v\big).

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose that
V:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}_{-}
is a bounded, measurable function satisfying (
2.3
) with
\alpha>7
and
\Im V<0
on a non-trivial open set.
Assume also that the operator
\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v
is invertible in
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
for any
\lambda>0
.
Then the function
z\mapsto D_{2}(z)
is analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus[0,\infty)
. In addition, this function is continuous for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[0,\infty)
up to the cut along
[0,\infty)
, with the point
z=0
possibly excluded. For
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
the function
\lambda\mapsto D_{2}(\lambda\pm i0)
does not have any zeros. Furthermore, we have

\lim_{|z|\to\infty}D_{2}(z)=1

uniformly in
\arg(z)
.

Since we suppose that
\alpha>7
in (
2.3
),
V
is clearly integrable. We then define the constant

c:=\frac{2\pi i\mathop{\mathrm{Vol}}\nolimits(\mathbb{S}^{2})}{4(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}x\;\!V(x)=\frac{i}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}x\;\!V(x).

(4.4)

Our first task is to show the integrability of the map
\lambda\mapsto\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\ln\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)+c\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.
Since
V
is not real-valued, this result is not available in the literature.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that
V:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}_{-}
is a bounded, measurable function satisfying (
2.3
) with
\alpha>7
and
\Im V<0
on a non-trivial open set.
Assume also that the operator
\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v

is invertible in
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
for any
\lambda>0
.
Then the function
(0,\infty)\ni\lambda\mapsto\ln\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)
is in
C^{1}\big((0,\infty)\big)
and

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(\ln\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)+2c\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=m

(4.5)

for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

The proof of the differentiability claim can be directly copied from the proof of
[
45
, Thm. 9.1.18]
, using Lemmas
2.9
and
2.10
.
For the second statement, consider
z,\overline{z}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(H)
,
and observe that the following identity holds:

\displaystyle(1+uvR_{0}(z)v)^{-1}(1+uvR_{0}(\overline{z})v)

\displaystyle=(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(z)v)^{-1}(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\overline{z})v)

\displaystyle=1-(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(z)v)^{-1}v\big(R_{0}(z)-R_{0}(\overline{z})\big)v.

By Lemma
2.6
we have that the above function is continuous and can be extended to
z=\lambda\pm i0
for
\lambda>0
. Recall the relation
2\pi i\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)=\big(R_{0}(\lambda+i0)-R_{0}(\lambda-i0)\big)
. Then we find, using also the relation
\det(1+AB)=\det(1+BA)
, that

\displaystyle\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)

\displaystyle=\det\big(1-2\pi i\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}\big)

\displaystyle=\det\big((1+uvR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v)^{-1}(1+uvR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\det_{2}(1+uvR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v)}{\det_{2}(1+uvR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v)}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(uv(R_{0}(\lambda+i0)-R_{0}(\lambda-i0))v)},

where in the last line we have used
[
20
, Lem. III.4]
. Since
D_{2}(\lambda\pm i0)\to 1
as
\lambda\to\infty
by Proposition
4.1
, the high-energy behavior of
\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)
is fully determined by the term
\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(uv(R_{0}(\lambda+i0)-R_{0}(\lambda-i0))v)}
. We can compute this trace explicitly, namely

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\Big(uv\big(R_{0}(\lambda+i0)-R_{0}(\lambda-i0)\big)v\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi i\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{\mathrm{Vol}}\nolimits(\mathbb{S}^{2})}{2(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}x\;\!V(x)=2c\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}},

(4.6)

leading easily to the statement.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

We can fix a branch of the meromorphic function
\ln\det_{2}\big(1+uvR_{0}(z)v\big)
by the condition

\displaystyle\lim_{|z|\to\infty}\arg\Big({\det}_{2}\big(1+uvR_{0}(z)v\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=0.

Fixing this branch gives
m=0
in Lemma
4.2
and we take this convention for the rest of this paper.

Lemma 4.4
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
4.2
, for any
\Lambda>0
the map
\lambda\mapsto\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\ln\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)+c\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is integrable on
(0,\Lambda)
and
\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\mathrm{d}\lambda\big(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\ln\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)+c\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big)
, with
c
defined in (
4.4
), converges as
\Lambda\to\infty
.

The following proof is an analogue of
[
1
, Lem. 4.12]
valid for usual self-ajdoint Schrödinger operators.

Proof.

We first check integrability in a neighbourhood of zero. Recall that we have the equality

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\ln\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)^{-1}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)

for
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
.
Recall also that

\displaystyle S(\lambda)

\displaystyle=1-2\pi i\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}.

The operator
\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathfrak{h})
has Hilbert-Schmidt norm

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-3}\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|v(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}x=C\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}},

with the same calculation applying to the adjoint also, from which we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}},\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\|v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}\|_{2}\leq C\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}.

A similar calculation shows that

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{F}_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda)v\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}(2\pi)^{-3}\lambda^{-\frac{3}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|v(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{4}(2\pi)^{-3}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|\langle x,\omega\rangle|^{2}|v(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=C_{1}\lambda^{-\frac{3}{2}}+C_{2}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Thus for sufficiently small
\lambda
we have the estimates

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{F}_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda)v\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda^{-\frac{3}{4}},\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\|v\mathscr{F}_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda)^{*}\|_{2}\leq C\lambda^{-\frac{3}{4}}.

Then we can compute that

\displaystyle S^{\prime}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=-2\pi i\Big(\mathscr{F}_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}+\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda)^{*}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)^{2}v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}\Big).

For sufficiently small
\lambda
, the operators

\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)^{2}v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}

are uniformly bounded (see Lemma
2.6
for the resolvents, and use the well-known fact that
vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)^{2}v
is bounded if
\alpha>2
in (
2.3
) which follows from the explicit expression of the kernel of
R_{0}(\lambda+i0)
). Then we can use Hölder’s inequality for Schatten norms to make the estimate

\displaystyle\big\|S^{\prime}(\lambda)\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi\big\|\mathscr{F}_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}\big\|_{1}+2\pi\big\|\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda)^{*}\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle+2\pi\big\|\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)^{2}v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi\|\mathscr{F}_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda)v\|_{2}\big\|\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}\big\|_{\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})}\|v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}\|_{2}

\displaystyle+2\pi\|\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\|_{2}\big\|\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}\big\|_{\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})}\|v\mathscr{F}_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda)^{*}\|_{2}

\displaystyle+2\pi\big\|\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)v\|_{2}\big\|\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)^{2}v\big(\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v\big)^{-1}\big\|_{\infty}\|v\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}+C_{2}\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}},

for some positive constant
C_{1},C_{2}
. It remains to recall that
\lambda\mapsto S(\lambda)^{-1}
is bounded on
[0,\infty)
in the operator norm by Lemma
2.10
. Thus we find using again Hölder’s inequality for Schatten norms that for sufficiently small
\lambda
we have the estimate

\displaystyle\big|\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)^{-1}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\|S(\lambda)^{-1}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\|_{1}\leq\|S(\lambda)^{-1}\|_{\mathscr{B}(\mathfrak{h})}\|S^{\prime}(\lambda)\|_{1}\leq C\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}

for some
C>0
, which is integrable near zero.

By Lemma
4.2
, for any
\Lambda>0
the map
(0,\Lambda)\ni\lambda\mapsto\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\ln\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)+c\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is continuous (and in fact the derivative of a continuous function) and so by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus,

\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\mathrm{d}\lambda\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\ln\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)+c\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)<\infty.

(4.7)

The limit of Equation (
4.5
) shows that the integral in Equation (
4.7
) converges as
\Lambda\to\infty
.
∎

Since the map
\lambda\mapsto\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)
does not necessarily converge as
\lambda\to\infty
, some regularisation is required. For
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
, define
A(\lambda)\in\mathscr{B}(\mathfrak{h})
by

A(\lambda):=4\tan^{-1}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)V\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}.

This operator is trace class, by assuming
\alpha>3
in (
2.3
).
Indeed, based on the definition of
\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)
, we can write explicitly the integral kernel of
A(\lambda)
as

\displaystyle A(\lambda,\theta,\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=2\tan^{-1}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}(2\pi)^{-3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\langle\theta-\theta^{\prime},x\rangle}V(x).

Integrating along the diagonal we find

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\big(A(\lambda)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\tan^{-1}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{\mathrm{Vol}}\nolimits(\mathbb{S}^{2})}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}x\;\!V(x)=\frac{4}{\pi i}\tan^{-1}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}c.

In addition, by the properties of the map
\lambda\mapsto\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)
already mentioned in Lemma
2.8
we infer that the map
\lambda\mapsto A(\lambda)\in\mathscr{B}(\mathfrak{h})
is continuous and has norm limits

\mathop{\hbox{\rm u-}\lim}\nolimits_{\lambda\to 0}A(\lambda)=\mathop{\hbox{\rm u-}\lim}\nolimits_{\lambda\to\infty}A(\lambda)=0.

As a consequence, we also see that the map
\lambda\mapsto\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\big(A(\lambda)\big)
is continuous with
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\big(A(\lambda)\big)=0
and

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\left(i\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\big(A(\lambda)\big)-\frac{2\pi i\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{\mathrm{Vol}}\nolimits(\mathbb{S}^{2})}{2(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}x\;\!V(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\left(i\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\big(A(\lambda)\big)-2c\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)=0.

(4.8)

Based on these observations, we now define for
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
the operator

\displaystyle\beta(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{iA(\lambda)}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathfrak{h}),

which satisfies
\det\big(\beta(\lambda)\big)=\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{i\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(A(\lambda))}
for all
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
.
By construction we have the norm limits

\mathop{\hbox{\rm u-}\lim}\nolimits_{\lambda\to 0}\beta(\lambda)=\mathop{\hbox{\rm u-}\lim}\nolimits_{\lambda\to\infty}\beta(\lambda)=1.

The operator
\beta(\lambda)
is invertible for all
\lambda
with
\beta(\lambda)^{-1}=\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-iA(\lambda)}
. Such results are not true in the trace norm. We also define the operator
W_{\beta}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
by the equality

W_{\beta}=1+\big\{\tfrac{1}{2}\big(1-\tanh(2\pi A_{+})-i\cosh(2\pi A_{+})^{-1}\big)\otimes 1_{\mathcal{H}}\big\}(\beta(L)-1).

Lemma 4.5
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
4.2
, the operator
W_{\beta}
is a Fredholm operator in
\mathscr{B}(\mathfrak{h})

satisfying
\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{\beta})=0
.

A similar statement has been proved in
[
2
, Lem. 5.7]
to which we refer for the details.
For the next statement, we define the operator
W_{S}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
by

W_{S}=1+\big\{\tfrac{1}{2}\big(1-\tanh(2\pi A_{+})-i\cosh(2\pi A_{+})^{-1}\big)\otimes 1_{\mathcal{H}}\big\}(S(L)-1).

Since
S(0)=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}S(\lambda)=1
, the operator
W_{S}
is also
a Fredholm operator, as shown in
[
2
, Lem. 5.4]
.
We can then deduce the following statement:

Lemma 4.6
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
4.2
,
the Fredholm operators
W_{S}
and
W_{\beta}
satisfy

\ W_{S}W_{\beta}-W_{S\beta}\in\mathscr{K}(\mathscr{H})\

and

\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{S})=\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{S\beta}).

Proof.

The equality
W_{S}W_{\beta}=W_{S\beta}
up to compact operators follows from the proof of
[
1
, Lem. 3.4]
, see also
[
2
, Lem. 5.8]
for a similar statement.
The index claim follows from the fact that
\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{\beta})=0
and the composition rule for the Fredholm index.
∎

The next statement shows that the operator
\beta
provides the correct regularisation for the operator
S
, and consequently
W_{\beta}
will provide the correct regularisation
to the operator
W_{S}
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
4.2
,
the map
\lambda\mapsto\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)\det\big(\beta(\lambda)\big)
satisfies

\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)\det\big(\beta(\lambda)\big)=1

and

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)\det\big(\beta(\lambda)\big)=1.

Proof.

The result follows essentially from Lemma
4.2
. Using Equations (
4.6
) and (
4.8
) we find

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)\det\big(\beta(\lambda)\big)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\frac{\det_{2}(1+uvR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v)}{\det_{2}(1+uvR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v)}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\big(uv(R_{0}(\lambda+i0)-R_{0}(\lambda-i0))v)}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{i\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(A(\lambda))}

\displaystyle=1.

Since
S(0)=\beta(0)=1
we finally infer that
\det\big(S(0)\big)\det\big(\beta(0)\big)=1
.
∎

We can thus state the main result of this section.

Proposition 4.8
.

Suppose that
V:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}_{-}
is a bounded, measurable function satisfying (
2.3
) with
\alpha>7
and
\Im V<0
on a non-trivial open set.
Assume also that the operator
\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v
is invertible in
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
for any
\lambda>0
.
Then the following equality holds:

\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{-})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\lambda\;\!\left(\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)^{-1}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)+c\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)

where
c
is defined in (
4.4
).

Proof.

By Theorem
3.3
and Lemma
4.6
one has

\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{-})=\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{S})=\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{S\beta}).

Note that by
[
40
, Thm. 3.5(a)]
we have
\det\big(S(\lambda)\beta(\lambda)\big)=\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)\det\big(\beta(\lambda)\big)
. By Lemma
4.7
, the map
\lambda\mapsto\det\big(S(\lambda)\beta(\lambda)\big)
defines a loop, and using Gohberg-Kreĭn theory we can compute the index of
W_{S\beta}
as

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{S\beta})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{Wind}}\nolimits\Big(\lambda\mapsto\det\big(S(\lambda)\beta(\lambda)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\lambda\;\!\frac{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\big[\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)\det\big(\beta(\lambda)\big)\big]}{\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)\det\big(\beta(\lambda)\big)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\lambda\;\!\Big(\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)^{-1}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)+i\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\big(A(\lambda)\big)\Big).

By Lemma
4.4
,
\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\ln\det\big(S(\lambda)\big)+c\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)
is finite for all
\Lambda>0
and converges as
\Lambda\to\infty
. Noting also that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\lambda\;\!\left(i\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\big(A(\lambda)\big)-c\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

we have the desired result.
∎

To strengthen the previous result, we can use the description of
\mathop{\mathsf{Ran}}\nolimits(W_{-})
given by Lemma
2.7
. Thus, coming back to the definition of the projection
P_{-}
given in (
4.2
) one has

P_{-}\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H^{*}).

In addition, it is easily observed from the definition of these spaces in (
1.4
), by complex conjugation,
that

{\mathrm{dim}}\big(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H^{*})\big)={\mathrm{dim}}\big(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)\big).

Thus, collecting the content of Proposition
4.8
together with (
4.2
), we finally deduce
our main result:

Theorem 4.9
.

Suppose that
V:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}_{-}
is a bounded, measurable function satisfying (
2.3
) with
\alpha>7
and
\Im V<0
on a non-trivial open set.
Assume also that the operator
\overline{u}+vR_{0}(\lambda-i0)v
is invertible in
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
for any
\lambda>0
. Then one has

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\lambda\;\!\left(\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)^{-1}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)+c\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)=-\#\sigma_{\mathrm{ads}}(H),

where
c
is defined in (
4.4
) and
\#\sigma_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)={\mathrm{dim}}\big(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)\big)
.

Recalling from Lemma
2.4
that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{disc}}(H)
if
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)
is finite-dimensional, we directly deduce from Theorem
4.9
the following statement:

Corollary 4.10
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
4.9
, we have

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ads}}(H)=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{disc}}(H).
