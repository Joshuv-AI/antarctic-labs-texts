Convergence of Regret Matching in Potential Games and Constrained Optimization
Ioannis Anagnostides, Emanuel Tewolde, Brian Hu Zhang, Ioannis Panageas, Vincent Conitzer, Tuomas Sandholm

Abstract
Regret matching ( RM ) —and its modern variants—is a foundational online algorithm that has been at the heart of many AI breakthrough results in solving benchmark zero-sum games, such as poker. Yet, surprisingly little is known so far in theory about its convergence beyond two-player zero-sum games. For example, whether regret matching converges to Nash equilibria in potential games has been an open problem for two decades. Even beyond games, one could try to use RM variants for general constrained optimization problems. Recent empirical evidence suggests that they—particularly regret matching + ( RM + \texttt{RM}^{+} ) —attain strong performance on benchmark constrained optimization problems, outperforming traditional gradient descent-type algorithms. We show that alternating RM + \texttt{RM}^{+} converges to an ϵ \epsilon -KKT point after O ϵ ​ ( 1 / ϵ 4 ) O_{\epsilon}(1/\epsilon^{4}) iterations, establishing for the first time that it is a sound and fast first-order optimizer. Our argument relates the KKT gap to the accumulated regret , two quantities that are entirely disparate in general but interact in an intriguing way in our setting, so much so that when regrets are bounded, our complexity bound improves all the way to O ϵ ​ ( 1 / ϵ 2 ) O_{\epsilon}(1/\epsilon^{2}) . From a technical standpoint, while RM + \texttt{RM}^{+} does not have the usual one-step improvement property in general, we show that it does in a certain region that the algorithm will quickly reach and remain in thereafter. In sharp contrast, our second main result establishes a lower bound: RM , with or without alternation, can take an exponential number of iterations to reach a crude approximate solution even in two-player potential games. This represents the first worst-case separation between RM and RM + \texttt{RM}^{+} . Our lower bound shows that convergence to coarse correlated equilibria in potential games is exponentially faster than convergence to Nash equilibria.



1
Introduction

Regret matching
is a foundational online algorithm for minimizing
regret
. It was famously introduced by
Hart and Mas-Colell 2000
, although its conception can be traced much further back to the seminal
approachability
framework of
Blackwell 1956
, which lay the groundwork for online learning and regret minimization. As the name suggests, regret matching prescribes playing each action with probability proportional to the (nonnegative) regret accumulated by that action. Its appeal lies in its simplicity and scalability, being both
parameter free
and
scale invariant
.

Regret matching—and modern versions thereof—has been at the forefront of equilibrium computation in massive two-player zero-sum games. A notable variant with strong empirical performance is
regret matching
+
, introduced by
Tammelin 2014
; the only difference is that it truncates all negative coordinates of the regret vector to zero in every iteration. Even so, this variant is typically far superior than its predecessor, and was a central component in AI poker breakthroughs
[
Bowling et al. 2015
,
Brown and Sandholm 2017
,
Brown and Sandholm 2019b
,
Moravčík et al. 2017
]
and a more recent superhuman agent for dark chess
[
Zhang and Sandholm 2025
]
.

As such, the regret matching family of algorithms has rightfully been the subject of intense study in contemporary research. Much of this focus has been confined to two-player zero-sum games, where minimizing regret translates to convergence—of the
average
strategies—to minimax (equivalently, Nash) equilibria
[
Freund and Schapire 1999
]
. More broadly, in general-sum games, no-regret algorithms guarantee convergence to the set of
coarse correlated equilibria

[
Moulin and Vial 1978
]
—a more permissive concept than Nash equilibria.

In this paper, we examine the convergence of regret matching and its variants in the seminal class of
potential games
, and, more broadly, nonconvex optimization constrained over a product of simplices. Surprisingly little is known about this question even though it was identified early on as an important open question in this space
[
Kleinberg et al. 2009
,
Marden et al. 2007
]
. Recent empirical evidence brings this question to the fore again:
Tewolde et al. 2025a
showed that the regret matching family—and especially regret matching
+
—attains strong performance on a benchmark suite of constrained optimization problems, significantly outperforming gradient descent-type algorithms. Yet, there is no theory to suggest that regret matching will even asymptotically converge to approximate KKT points in constrained optimization, which are tantamount to Nash equilibria when dealing specifically with potential games. We fill this gap in this paper.

1.1
Our results

We analyze the convergence of regret matching
(\texttt{RM})
and regret matching
(\texttt{RM}^{+})
in the general class of (nonconvex) optimization problems constrained over a product of probability simplices. This encompasses as a special case Nash equilibria in potential games when the objective is multilinear. More broadly, to have a unifying treatment of both settings, we think of each probability simplex as being controlled by a single player who is observing the corresponding part of the gradient.

We mostly focus on the alternating version of
\texttt{RM}^{+}
, whereby players update their strategies one after the other, akin to coordinate descent. Our main result for
\texttt{RM}^{+}
is summarized below.

Theorem 1.1
.

Alternating
\texttt{RM}^{+}
converges to an
\epsilon
-KKT point of any optimization problem over a product of simplices after
O_{\epsilon}(1/\epsilon^{4})
iterations.

This theorem confirms that
\texttt{RM}^{+}
is a sound and efficient first-order optimizer, lending further credence to the empirical results of
Tewolde et al. 2025a
. We hope that
Theorem

1.1
will help cement
\texttt{RM}^{+}
in the optimization arsenal going forward.

Our argument proceeds by parameterizing the rate of convergence of
\texttt{RM}^{+}
as a function of the accumulated regret, so much so that if the regret with respect to each individual simplex remains bounded, the rate is improved all the way to
T^{-1/2}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that the regret of
\texttt{RM}^{+}
on each individual simplex grows as at most
T^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in[0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}]
. Then
\texttt{RM}^{+}
converges to an
\epsilon
-KKT point after
O_{\epsilon}(1/\epsilon^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{1-\alpha}}})
iterations.

\texttt{RM}^{+}
always guarantees regret growing as
\sqrt{T}
, so
Theorem

1.1
is implied by
Theorem

1.2
. What makes the latter theorem surprising is that, in general, regret is a fundamentally disparate property compared to KKT gap: as we point out in
Proposition

3.2
, a sequence can incur zero regret while having an
\Omega(1)
KKT gap in each iteration. Even so,
Theorem

1.2
directly relates the KKT gap in terms of the regret. In particular, the non-asymptotic rate of
Theorem

1.1
is a consequence of the fact that
\texttt{RM}^{+}
has the no-regret property! In the special case of potential games, regret is known to bound the rate of convergence to
coarse correlated equilibria
;
Theorem

1.2
shows for the first time that regret can also dictate the rate of convergence to Nash equilibria.

On a similar vein, a further important consequence of
Theorem

1.2
is that, in
symmetric
potential games,
simultaneous

\texttt{RM}^{+}
converges under a symmetric initialization.

Corollary 1.3
.

Simultaneous
\texttt{RM}^{+}
converges to an
\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium of any symmetric potential game. Furthermore, if convergence to CCE happens at a rate of
T^{-(1-\alpha)}
, for some
\alpha\in[0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}]
, the rate of convergence to Nash equilibria is no slower than
T^{-\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}
.

From a technical standpoint, the key challenge is that
\texttt{RM}^{+}
does
not
have a one-step improvement property: even if one initializes
\texttt{RM}^{+}
close to a KKT point,
\texttt{RM}^{+}
can still grossly overshoot. And, of course, it is a parameter-free algorithm, so the usual treatment of gradient descent-type algorithms that relies on appropriately tuning the learning rate falls short. In this context, our starting observation is that, at least when the utility function is linear,
\texttt{RM}^{+}
is bound to improve the utility, although the improvement is inversely proportional to the norm of the regret vector (
Lemma

3.3
). This key property already suffices to show that alternating
\texttt{RM}^{+}
will converge to Nash equilibria in potential games. For the more challenging setting where the objective is not multilinear, we first show that one-step improvement holds conditional on the norm of the regret vector being
sufficiently large
(
Lemma

3.7
). To conclude the argument, we combine this property with the crucial insight that the
\ell_{2}
norm of the regret vector is
monotonically increasing
proportionally to the KKT gap (
Lemma

3.8
). This means that
\texttt{RM}^{+}
will never get stuck in a cycle: the regret vector would quickly grow in norm, at which point the one-step improvement promised by
Lemma

3.7
kicks in.

We remark that for potential games and constrained optimization over a single simplex, the
O_{\epsilon}(1/\epsilon^{4})
bound holds no matter how the regrets in
\texttt{RM}^{+}
are initialized. For constrained optimization over multiple simplices, we obtain the same rate by suitably initializing the regret vectors (
Corollary

3.11
); for the usual parameter-free version of
\texttt{RM}^{+}
, in which the regret vectors are initialized at zero, we can only guarantee an inferior bound growing as
O_{\epsilon}(1/\epsilon^{8})
(
Theorem

3.12
).

Does
RM
share the same convergence properties as
\texttt{RM}^{+}
? As a reminder, the only difference is that
RM
refrains from truncating negative regrets to zero. Even so, we find that this seemingly innocuous difference gives rise to an exponential gap in the performance of
RM

vis-à-vis

\texttt{RM}^{+}
, manifested even in two-player identical-interest games—a special case of potential games (
Figure

1
).

Theorem 1.4
.

There is a two-player
m\times m
identical-interest game where
RM
, with or without alternation, requires
m^{\Omega(m)}
iterations to converge to an
m^{-\Theta(1)}
-approximate Nash equilibrium.

This lower bound holds not just under an adversarial initialization, but also when players initially mix uniformly at random, which is the most common initialization in practice.

Theorem

1.4
constitutes the first worst-case separation—let alone an exponential one—between
RM
and
\texttt{RM}^{+}
. Indeed, in zero-sum games, it is known that
RM
and
\texttt{RM}^{+}
both attain a rate no faster than
T^{-1/2}

[
Farina et al. 2023
]
, even though
\texttt{RM}^{+}
typically performs much better in practice.
Theorem

1.4
provides further justification for opting for
\texttt{RM}^{+}
instead of
RM
, albeit in a fundamentally different setting.

The basic flaw of
RM
that underpins
Theorem

1.4
is that, even with a linear utility, it is not guaranteed to improve the utility even when it has a large best-response gap; specifically, as we show in
Lemma

3.6
, the improvement is conditional on a good-enough action having nonnegative regret. But herein lies the problem: it could take many iterations before the regret resurfaces to being positive. What happens in the construction behind
Theorem

1.4
is that it takes longer and longer—exponentially so—for the regret of the unique good-enough action to be positive; before then,
RM
is entirely stalled without making any progress. At the same time,
RM
is guaranteed to converge to the set of coarse correlated equilibria (CCE) at a rate of
T^{-1/2}
, simply because it always has the no-regret property. This leads to the following interesting consequence.

Corollary 1.5
.

There is a class of two-player potential games in which
RM
converges to an
\epsilon
-CCE in
O_{\epsilon}(1/\epsilon^{2})
rounds but it takes
\exp(\Omega(1/\epsilon))
rounds to converge to an
\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium.

To be clear, convergence to a CCE is meant in terms of the average correlated distribution of play, whereas convergence to Nash equilibria is in terms of the individual iterates produced by
RM
.

.

2.1
Potential games and constrained optimization

Normal-form games

Our first key focus in this paper is on
potential games
, which we represent in the usual normal form. Here, we have
n
players, each of whom is to select an action
a_{i}
from a finite set
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
, with
m_{i}\coloneqq|{\mathcal{A}}_{i}|
and
m=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}m_{i}
. Under a joint action profile
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\times\dots\times{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
, each player
i\in[n]
receives a payoff given by a
utility function

u_{i}:(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\mapsto u_{i}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}
with range bounded by
1
. A player
i\in[n]
can randomize by specifying a
mixed strategy

{\bm{x}}_{i}\in\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{i})\coloneqq\{{\bm{x}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}^{{\mathcal{A}}_{i}}:\sum_{a_{i}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{i}}{\bm{x}}_{i}[a_{i}]=1\}

i
strives to maximize its
expected
utility, given by
u_{i}({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})\coloneqq\sum_{(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\times\dots\times{\mathcal{A}}_{n}}u_{i}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\prod_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}{\bm{x}}_{i^{\prime}}[a_{i^{\prime}}]
. A key fact is that the expected utility is
multilinear
, in that
u_{i}({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})=\langle{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{-i})\rangle
for some utility vector
{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{-i})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathcal{A}}_{i}}
that does not depend on
{\bm{x}}_{i}
; namely,
{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{-i})=(\sum_{(a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1},a_{i+1},\dots,a_{n})}u_{i}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\prod_{i^{\prime}\neq i}{\bm{x}}_{i^{\prime}}[a_{i^{\prime}}])_{a_{i}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{i}}
. Here and throughout, we use the shorthand notation
{\bm{x}}_{-i}=({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{i-1},{\bm{x}}_{i+1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})
, while we recall that
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product. Further, we use the shorthand notation
\mathsf{BRGap}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{u}}_{i})\coloneqq\max_{{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\in\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{i})}\langle{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}-{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{u}}_{i}\rangle
for the best-response gap.

The predominant solution concept in game theory is the
Nash equilibrium

[
Nash 1950
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A strategy profile
({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})\in\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{1})\times\dots\times\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{n})
is an
\epsilon

if for any player

u_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime},{\bm{x}}_{-i})\leq u_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{-i})+\epsilon.

A standard relaxation of the Nash equilibrium is the

(
Definition

[

]
, our focus is on

—equivalently,

[

]

Potential games

This is a seminal class that goes back to the work of
Rosenthal 1973
. The defining property is the admission of a global, player-independent function—the
potential
—whose difference reflects the benefit of any unilateral deviation.

Definition 2.2

(Potential game)
.

An
n

if there exists a function
\Phi:{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\times\dots\times{\mathcal{A}}_{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that for any player
i\in[n]

\bm{a}_{-i}\in\bigtimes_{i^{\prime}\neq i}{\mathcal{A}}_{i^{\prime}}

\Phi(a_{i}^{\prime},\bm{a}_{-i})-\Phi(a_{i},\bm{a}_{-i})=u_{i}(a_{i}^{\prime},\bm{a}_{-i})-u_{i}(a_{i},\bm{a}_{-i}).

(1)

With an overload in notation, we also denote by
\Phi:\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{1})\times\dots\times\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{n})

\Phi({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\sim({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})}\Phi(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})

for any strategies
{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\in\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{i})
and
{\bm{x}}_{-i}\in\bigtimes_{i^{\prime}\neq i}\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{i^{\prime}})
.

A special case of a potential game worth noting is an

game, which means that
u_{1}({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})=\dots=u_{n}({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})

m_{2}=|{\mathcal{A}}_{2}|

{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{-i})=\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{i}}\Phi({\bm{x}})

A (mixed) Nash equilibrium in potential games is amenable to (projected) gradient descent, but is likely hard to compute when the precision

Online learning and regret matching

Simultaneous and alternating updates

We are interested in the convergence of
RM
and
\texttt{RM}^{+}
when used by all players; in the constrained optimization setting, we think of having one player acting on each simplex, in direct correspondence with potential games. In this setting, the sequence of utilities
({\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)})_{t=1}^{T}
given as input to player
i\in[n]
is determined by the strategies of the other players. If the updates are
simultaneous
, we have
{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)}=\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{i}}u({\bm{x}}^{(t)})
for each player
i\in[n]
. (In potential games, the potential function
\Phi
plays the role of
u
, so that
{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)}={\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{-i}^{(t)})
.) In the alternating setting, we go through the players in a round-robin fashion
i=1,\dots,n
. In the
lazy
version of the update, for a fixed precision
\epsilon>0
, we first compute
{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)}=\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{i}}u({\bm{x}}_{i^{\prime}<i}^{(t+1)},{\bm{x}}_{i^{\prime}\geq i}^{(t)})
. If the best-response gap of player
i\in[n]
is already at most
\epsilon
, we refrain from updating that player, so that
{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t+1)}\coloneqq{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)}
. Otherwise, the player updates its strategy to
{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t+1)}
using
{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)}
. We refer to this scheme as
\epsilon
-lazy alternating
updates; one limitation of this lazy variant is that it is not an “anytime algorithm,” in that one needs to specify the precision beforehand. In the more common, non-lazy version of alternation, a player is updated regardless of the best-response gap; in the sequel, we obtain convergence results for both variants. Alternation speeds up performance, at least in zero-sum games
[
Tammelin 2014
]
, and has been the subject of much recent research
[
Wibisono et al. 2022
,
Cevher et al. 2023
,
Nan et al. 2025
]
.

3
Convergence of regret matching
+

In this section, we analyze the convergence of
\texttt{RM}^{+}
in potential games (
Section

3.1
), and more broadly, constrained optimization (
Section

3.2
). A central theme in our analysis of
RM
and
\texttt{RM}^{+}
is a recurring connection between regret and convergence to KKT points.

Before we proceed, it is worth highlighting that, in general, the no-regret property is fundamentally different from convergence to KKT points in
nonconvex problems
. To begin with, we point out that when the underlying function to be maximized,
u
, is concave, then the no-regret property does imply convergence to a global optimum, from Jensen’s inequality.

Proposition 3.1

(Under concavity, no-regret implies convergence)
.

Let
u
be a smooth concave function. If an online algorithm observes the sequence of utilities
(\nabla u({\bm{x}}^{(t)}))_{t=1}^{T}
, then
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}u({\bm{x}}^{(t)})\geq\max_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}u({\bm{x}})-\frac{1}{T}\mathsf{Reg}^{(T)}
, where
\mathsf{Reg}^{(T)}
is the regret of the algorithm per (
3
).

Thus, if the algorithm has vanishing average regret,
u({\bm{x}}^{(t))})
converges to
\max_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}u({\bm{x}})
(in density). But beyond concave problems, no-regret algorithms do not necessarily guarantee convergence even to a KKT point, as we point out below.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
T=0\mod 4
, there exists a polynomial function
u
in
[0,1]
and a sequence of points
(x^{(t)})_{t=1}^{T}
such that

•

the regret of the sequence with respect to
(\nabla u(x^{(t)}))_{t=1}^{T}
is zero, while

•

every point in the sequence has an
\Omega(1)
KKT gap with respect to the function
u
.

This is based on the
4
-cycle
0.6\rightarrow 0.7\rightarrow 0.4\rightarrow 0.3\rightarrow 0.6
. If the gradients observed at those points are
0.6\mapsto 2,0.7\mapsto-1,0.4\mapsto-2,0.3\mapsto 1
, it follows that i)
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nabla u(x^{(t)})=0
and ii)
\sum_{t=1}^{T}x^{(t)}\nabla u(x^{(t)})=0
, which in turn implies that this sequence incurs zero regret. But, by construction, the gradients at those interior points have a large magnitude, which in turn implies that the KKT gap is large. (That the average is a local minimum is coincidental.) A polynomial consistent with the above gradients is
90x-298.\bar{3}x^{2}+416.\bar{6}x^{3}-208.\bar{3}x^{4}
, leading to
Proposition

3.2
; we note that the above sequence of iterates is not realizable through an algorithm such as gradient descent.

3.1
Potential games

We first analyze convergence in potential games. A key property, which paves the way for
Theorem

3.4
, is that, for a fixed utility vector,
\texttt{RM}^{+}
has a one-step improvement property; the lemma below takes the perspective of a single, arbitrary player in the game.

Lemma 3.3

(One-step improvement for
\texttt{RM}^{+}
)
.

For any
\bm{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\geq 0}
and
{\bm{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{A}}
, we define
{\bm{x}}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{\bm{r}}}{{\|\bm{r}\|_{1}}}
; if
\bm{r}=\bm{0}
,
{\bm{x}}\in\Delta({\mathcal{A}})
can be arbitrary. If
\bm{r}^{\prime}\coloneqq[\bm{r}+{\bm{u}}-\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle\bm{1}]^{+}\neq\bm{0}
and
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{\bm{r}^{\prime}}}{{\|\bm{r}^{\prime}\|_{1}}}
, then

\langle{\bm{x}}^{\prime}-{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{\|\bm{r}^{\prime}\|_{1}}\left(\max_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\bm{u}}[a]-\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{\|{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\|_{1}}\mathsf{BRGap}({\bm{x}},{\bm{u}})^{2}.

(4)

If
\bm{r}^{\prime}=\bm{0}
, then
\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle=\langle{\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{u}}\rangle\geq\max_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\bm{u}}[a]
.

The left-hand side of (
4
) reflects the improvement in utility obtained by updating
{\bm{x}}
to
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}
; in particular,
\max_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\bm{u}}[a]-\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle
is the best-response gap of
{\bm{x}}
with respect to
{\bm{u}}
.
Lemma

3.3
implies that the utility is monotonically increasing—unless the current strategy is already a best response to
{\bm{u}}
. Furthermore, so long as the regret vector is
small enough
, the improvement is bound to be substantial, being proportional to the squared best-response gap. It is worth noting that
Lemma

3.3
holds no matter the initial regret vector
\bm{r}
, subject to
\bm{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
; this invariance always holds for
\texttt{RM}^{+}
(by definition of the algorithm in
Algorithm

2
), but that is not so for
RM
(
cf.

Lemma

3.6
).

The proof of
Lemma

3.3
proceeds by expressing (
4
) in terms of the regret vectors and performing simple algebraic manipulations; it appears in
Section

C.1
. Furthermore, as we point out in
Lemma

C.4
,
Lemma

3.3
is in a certain sense tight.

Convergence in potential games

We now employ
Lemma

3.3
to show that alternating
\texttt{RM}^{+}
quickly converges to approximate Nash equilibria in potential games. Using the fact that the game admits a potential (per
Definition

2.2
), we have that for any round
t\in[T]
,
\Phi({\bm{x}}_{1}^{(t+1)},\dots,{\bm{x}}^{(t+1)}_{n})-\Phi({\bm{x}}_{1}^{(t)},\dots,{\bm{x}}^{(t)}_{n})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\|\bm{r}^{(t)}_{i}\|_{1}}\mathsf{BRGap}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)})^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{\mathsf{BRGap}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)})>\epsilon\}
, where we used
Lemma

3.3
together with the assumption that only players with more than
\epsilon
best-response gap update their strategies. The telescopic summation over
t=1,\dots,T
yields

\Phi_{\mathsf{range}}\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{1}}\mathsf{BRGap}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)})^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{\mathsf{BRGap}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)})>\epsilon\},

(5)

where
\Phi_{\mathsf{range}}
denotes the range of the potential function. If in every round
t\in[T]
there is a player
i\in[n]
such that
\mathsf{BRGap}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)})>\epsilon
, we have
\Phi_{\mathsf{range}}\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{m\sqrt{t}}\epsilon^{2}\geq\frac{1}{m}\epsilon^{2}\sqrt{T}
, where we used that
\|\bm{r}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{m}\|\bm{r}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{2}\leq m\sqrt{T}
(
Proposition

2.3
). We thus arrive at the following result.

Theorem 3.4
.

In any potential game,
\epsilon
-lazy alternating
\texttt{RM}^{+}
requires at most
1+\frac{(m\Phi_{\mathsf{range}})^{2}}{\epsilon^{4}}
rounds to converge to an
\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium. More broadly, if
\|\bm{r}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{1}\leq C(n,m)t^{\alpha}
for all
i\in[n]
and some
\alpha\in[0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}]
, it requires
1+\frac{(C(n,m)\Phi_{\mathsf{range}})^{\beta}}{\epsilon^{2\beta}}
rounds, where
\beta\coloneqq\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-\alpha}}
.

This provides a convergence rate of
T^{-1/4}
to Nash equilibria. Notwithstanding
Proposition

3.2
, an intriguing aspect of
Theorem

3.4
is that it
connects convergence to Nash equilibria to the regret
of
\texttt{RM}^{+}
. In particular, if
\texttt{RM}^{+}
did not have the no-regret property, meaning that
\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{1}=\Omega(t)
, one could only prove an exponential bound since
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nicefrac{{1}}{{t}}=\Theta(\log T)
. At the other end of the spectrum, when each player accumulates constant regret,
Theorem

3.4
implies an improved convergence rate of
T^{-1/2}
. It is an open question whether
\texttt{RM}^{+}
, with or without alternation, can experience
\Omega(\sqrt{T})
regret in potential games.

One caveat of lazy alternating
\texttt{RM}^{+}
prescribed by
Theorem

3.4
is that the desired precision should be known in advance in order to execute the algorithm. We address this limitation in
Theorem

C.7
, where we show that the usual, non-lazy version also converges after
O_{\epsilon}(1/\epsilon^{4})
rounds, albeit at the cost of introducing an additional dependence in the total number of iterations.

Faster rates using discounting

Next, we refine
Theorem

3.4
through the use of
discounted

\texttt{RM}^{+}
, which means that the regret vector is multiplied by a discount factor
\alpha^{(t)}\in(0,1]
in each round; we spell out
\texttt{DRM}^{+}
in
Algorithm

3
. This class of algorithms was introduced by
Brown and Sandholm 2019a
, who showed that discounting drastically improves empirical performance in zero-sum games. Our next result shows that
\texttt{DRM}^{+}
with geometric discounting, that is,
\alpha^{(t)}=1-\gamma
for some time-invariant
\gamma\in(0,1)
, attains a rate of
T^{-1/2}
to Nash equilibria in potential games; this is considerably faster than the
T^{-1/4}
rate for alternating
\texttt{RM}^{+}
guaranteed by
Theorem

3.4
. The basic reason is that
\texttt{DRM}^{+}
—with geometric discounting—maintains the norm of the regret vector bounded by
\sqrt{\nicefrac{{m}}{{\gamma}}}
(
Lemma

C.2
and
Corollary

C.3
), while still enjoying the one-step improvement property of
Lemma

3.3
.

Corollary 3.5
.

In any potential game,
\epsilon
-lazy alternating
\texttt{DRM}^{+}
with discount factor
1-\gamma\in(0,1)
requires at most
1+\frac{m\Phi_{\mathsf{range}}}{\epsilon^{2}\sqrt{\gamma}}
rounds to converge to an
\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium.

To our knowledge, this is the first time that discounting yields a provable, worst-case improvement over the bound obtained for non-discounted
\texttt{RM}^{+}
.

Regret matching

Before we switch gears to the more general constrained optimization setting, it is instructive to examine the behavior of
RM
. It turns out that one can adjust
Lemma

3.3
, but with a crucial caveat: the one-step improvement property is now only
conditional
, as specified below.

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
\bm{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\geq 0}
and
{\bm{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{A}}
, we define
{\bm{x}}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{\bm{\theta}}}{{\|\bm{\theta}\|_{1}}}
, where
\bm{\theta}\coloneqq\max(\bm{r},\bm{0})
; if
\bm{\theta}=\bm{0}
,
{\bm{x}}\in\Delta({\mathcal{A}})
can be arbitrary. If
\bm{r}^{\prime}\coloneqq\bm{r}+{\bm{u}}-\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle\bm{1}
and
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}}{{\|\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\|_{1}}}
, where
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}=\max(\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{0})\neq\bm{0}
, then

\langle{\bm{x}}^{\prime}-{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{\|\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\|_{1}}\|\bm{\theta}^{\prime}-\bm{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{\|\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\|_{1}}\left({\bm{u}}[a]-\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\bm{r}[a]\geq 0\right\},

(6)

where
a\in\argmax_{a^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\bm{u}}[a^{\prime}]
. If
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}=\bm{0}
, then
\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle=\langle{\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{u}}\rangle\geq{\bm{u}}[a]
.

We see that
RM
’s one-step improvement is conditional on the regret accumulated thus far by a best-response action to be nonnegative. This is not an artifact of our analysis; it alludes to a fundamental discrepancy between
RM
and
\texttt{RM}^{+}
that will be formally established later on (
Theorem

4.4
). The main issue with
RM
can be seen as follows. If we consider a utility vector
{\bm{u}}=(1,0)
and the initial regret vector is, say,
(-R,R)
, it will take
RM
many iterations—proportionally to the magnitude of
R>0
—to finally change strategies, although this will eventually happen with a stationary utility.

3.2
Constrained optimization and simultaneous updates

We now treat the more general setting where we are maximizing an
L
-smooth function
u
.

Single simplex

We begin with the special case of a single probability simplex,
{\mathcal{X}}=\Delta({\mathcal{A}})
. Our first goal is to adapt
Lemma

3.3
. The key challenge is that
\texttt{RM}^{+}
does
not
have a one-step improvement, unlike algorithms such as gradient descent (for a small enough learning rate), even if one initializes
\texttt{RM}^{+}
close to a KKT point. But we observe that if the norm of the regret vector is
large enough
—having small regrets is an obstacle here, in contrast to
Section

3.1
—we are guaranteed a one-step improvement in terms of the value of the function (
Lemma

3.7
).

To do so, we will use the basic quadratic bound, which yields
u({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\geq u({\bm{x}})+\langle\nabla u({\bm{x}}),{\bm{x}}^{\prime}-{\bm{x}}\rangle-\frac{L}{2}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}
; we think of
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}
as the updated strategy starting from
{\bm{x}}
. Using a slight refinement of
Lemma

3.3
, we first have the lower bound
\langle{\bm{x}}^{\prime}-{\bm{x}},\nabla u({\bm{x}})\rangle\geq\frac{1}{\|\bm{r}^{\prime}\|_{1}}\|\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}
(
Lemma

C.5
).

Also, we observe that
\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq\|\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}\|_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\|\bm{r}\|_{1}}+\frac{1}{\|\bm{r}^{\prime}\|_{1}}\right)
(
Lemma

C.6
). We are now ready to establish a
conditional
one-step improvement when the regret vector has a
sufficiently large
norm.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
u
be an
L
-smooth function over
\Delta({\mathcal{A}})
. For any
\bm{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\geq 0}
with
{\bm{r}}\neq\bm{0}
, we define
{\bm{x}}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{\bm{r}}}{{\|\bm{r}\|_{1}}}
. Further, let
\bm{r}^{\prime}\coloneqq[\bm{r}+\nabla u({\bm{x}})-\langle{\bm{x}},\nabla u({\bm{x}})\rangle\bm{1}]^{+}\neq\bm{0}
and
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{\bm{r}^{\prime}}}{{\|\bm{r}^{\prime}\|_{1}}}
. If
\|\bm{r}^{\prime}\|_{2}\geq\max\{2m,9mL\}
, then

u({\bm{x}}^{\prime})-u({\bm{x}})\geq\frac{1}{2\|\bm{r}^{\prime}\|_{1}}\left(\max_{\bm{x}^{\star}\in\Delta({\mathcal{A}})}\langle\bm{x}^{\star}-{\bm{x}},\nabla u({\bm{x}})\rangle\right)^{2}.

Lemma

3.7
only shows a one-step improvement so long as the norm of the regret vector is large enough. But how can we guarantee that? It would seem possible that
\texttt{RM}^{+}
ends up cycling in perpetuity under a regret vector with small norm. The following lemma shows that cannot happen; it turns out that maintaining this monotonicity property for the norm of the regret vector is crucial for designing an optimal variant of
\texttt{RM}^{+}
in zero-sum games
[
Zhang et al. 2025
]
.

Lemma 3.8
.

For any
t
,
\texttt{RM}^{+}
guarantees
\|{\bm{r}}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\|{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\|[{\bm{g}}^{(t)}]^{+}\|_{2}^{2}
, where
{\bm{g}}^{(t)}\coloneqq\nabla u({\bm{x}}^{(t)})-\langle\nabla u({\bm{x}}^{(t)}),{\bm{x}}^{(t)}\rangle\bm{1}
is the instantaneous regret vector at round
t
.

In particular,

\|{\bm{r}}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\|{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\|[{\bm{g}}^{(t)}]_{+}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\|{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\mathsf{KKTGap}({\bm{x}}^{(t)})^{2}

since
\|[{\bm{g}}^{(t)}]^{+}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\mathsf{KKTGap}({\bm{x}}^{(t)})^{2}
. That is, not only is the
\ell_{2}
norm of the regret vector nondecreasing, but the increase is at least
\mathsf{KKTGap}({\bm{x}}^{(t)})^{2}
at each round
t\in[T]
. Combining with
Lemma

3.7
yields the following.

Theorem 3.9

(Single simplex)
.

Let
u
be an
L
-smooth function in
\Delta({\mathcal{A}})\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with range
u_{\textsf{range}}
and
R\coloneqq\max\{2m,9mL\}
.
\texttt{RM}^{+}
requires at most
1+\frac{(m(2u_{\textsf{range}}+R^{2}))^{2}}{\epsilon^{4}}
rounds to reach an
\epsilon
-KKT point.

Simultaneous updates in symmetric potential games

We now use
Theorem

3.9
to prove convergence of
simultaneous

\texttt{RM}^{+}
in
symmetric
potential games; our earlier result in
Theorem

3.4
shows convergence for arbitrary potential games but for the alternating version. The symmetry assumption here means that
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}={\mathcal{A}}_{1}=\dots={\mathcal{A}}_{n}={\mathcal{A}}
and
{\bm{u}}_{1}({\bm{x}}_{-1})={\bm{u}}_{2}({\bm{x}}_{-2})=\dots={\bm{u}}_{n}({\bm{x}}_{-n})
when
{\bm{x}}_{1}={\bm{x}}_{2}=\dots={\bm{x}}_{n}
. It is further assumed that all players initialize from the same strategy, so that the previous property implies that, inductively, it will be the case that
{\bm{x}}_{1}^{(t)}={\bm{x}}_{2}^{(t)}=\dots={\bm{x}}_{n}^{(t)}
for all
t
under simultaneous updates because players observe exactly the same utility vector. A simple example of this is a two-player game with a common, symmetric payoff matrix
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}
. Then
{\bm{u}}_{1}({\bm{x}}_{2})=\mathbf{A}{\bm{x}}_{2}
and
{\bm{u}}_{2}({\bm{x}}_{1})=\mathbf{A}{\bm{x}}_{1}
, so the previous assumption is satisfied.

Corollary 3.10
.

In any symmetric potential game, simultaneous
\texttt{RM}^{+}
converges to an
\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium after
O_{\epsilon}(1/\epsilon^{4})
rounds. In particular, if convergence to the set of CCE happens at a rate of
T^{-(1-\alpha)}
, for some
\alpha\in[0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}]
, the rate of convergence to Nash equilibria is no worse than
T^{-\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}
.

Indeed, running simultaneous
\texttt{RM}^{+}
in a symmetric game is equivalent to running
\texttt{RM}^{+}
on the function
\Delta({\mathcal{A}})\ni{\bm{x}}\mapsto\Phi({\bm{x}},\dots,{\bm{x}})
, as we formalize in
Appendix

C
(
cf.

Tewolde et al. 2025b
);
Corollary

3.10
thereby follows from
Theorem

3.9
pertaining to
\texttt{RM}^{+}
executed on a single simplex.

Multiple simplices

We now have the necessary tools to analyze the general case where we maximize
u
over a product of simplices. Similarly to
Theorem

3.4
, we run alternating
\texttt{RM}^{+}
, thinking of every individual simplex as being controlled by a single player; this is akin to coordinate descent.

Corollary 3.11
.

If
u
is an
L
-smooth function in
\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{1})\times\dots\times\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{n})
with range
u_{\textsf{range}}
,
\epsilon
-lazy alternating
\texttt{RM}^{+}
initialized at
{\bm{r}}^{(0)}_{i}=\max\{2\sqrt{m_{i}},9\sqrt{m_{i}}L\}\bm{1}
for each player
i\in[n]
requires at most
1+\frac{4n^{4}m^{2}u_{\textsf{range}}^{2}}{\epsilon^{4}}
rounds to reach an
\epsilon
-KKT point of
u
.

The proof follows directly from
Lemma

3.7
together with a telescopic summation. The non-lazy version admits a qualitatively similar bound, following
Theorem

C.7
.

One caveat of
Corollary

3.11
is that the regret vector of each player needs to be initialized at a specific threshold. Our next result addresses this limitation by analyzing the usual parameter-free and scale-invariant version of
\texttt{RM}^{+}
, at the cost of introducing a worse dependence on
1/\epsilon
.

Theorem 3.12
.

If
u
is an
L
-smooth function in
\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{1})\times\dots\times\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{n})
with range
u_{\textsf{range}}
,
\epsilon
-lazy alternating
\texttt{RM}^{+}
requires at most
O_{\epsilon}(1/\epsilon^{8})
rounds to reach an
\epsilon
-KKT point of
u
.

The proof again leverages
Lemma

3.8
to show that the number of rounds in which active players have a regret vector with norm smaller than what prescribed by
Lemma

3.7
is bounded by
O_{\epsilon}(1/\epsilon^{2})
. It then appropriately bounds the number of rounds it takes in between such rounds to arrive at the bound of
Theorem

3.12
.

4
Exponential lower bounds for regret matching

In stark contrast, we show that
RM
, with or without alternation, can take exponentially many rounds to reach an approximate Nash equilibrium even in two-player identical-interest games. The underlying class of games is based on the one considered by
Panageas et al. 2023a
, who treated fictitious play. Specifically, for
m=4,6,\dots
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
we define the matrix
\mathbf{A}_{m,k}
per the recursion

{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}\ni\mathbf{A}_{m,k}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}k+1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&&&&k+4\\
\vdots&&\mathbf{A}_{m-2,k+4}&&\vdots\\
0&&&&0\\
k+2&0&\cdots&0&k+3\end{bmatrix},\text{ where }\mathbf{A}_{2,k}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}k+1&0\\
k+2&k+3\end{bmatrix}.

(An illustrative example appears in
Section

C.2
.) For any even dimension
m
, we define
\mathbf{A}\coloneqq\mathbf{A}_{m,0}
, with maximum entry
2m-1
. Further, we define, for
1\leq a_{1}\leq m+1
and
1\leq a_{2}\leq m+1
,

{\mathbb{R}}^{(m+1)\times(m+1)}\ni\mathbf{B}[a_{1},a_{2}]\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathbf{A}[a_{1},a_{2}]&\text{if }a_{1}\leq m\text{ and }a_{2}\leq m;\\
1/2&\text{if }(a_{1}=m+1\text{ and }a_{2}=1)\text{ or }(a_{1}=1\text{ and }a_{2}=m+1);\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(7)

The action sets of the two players are
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}=[m+1]={\mathcal{A}}_{2}
. We assume that

is initialized at the pure strategy
(m+1,m+1)
;

shows how to adapt the lower bound when

is initialized at the uniform random strategy (

), which is more common. We recall that one round includes one update from each player, which for now is assumed to be made in a simultaneous fashion. For a payoff
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we denote by

the row and column index, respectively, corresponding to
k

\mathbf{A}
.

We begin by stating a basic invariance concerning the behavior of
RM
when executed on the game (
7
).

Property 4.1
.

After the first round both players play the first action. Thereupon, either the players play with probability
1

(a_{1}(k),a_{2}(k))
, or, when
k
is odd, only Player 1 (respectively, Player 2 when
k
is even) mixes between
a_{1}(k)
and
a_{1}(k+1)
(respectively,
a_{2}(k)
and
a_{2}(k+1)
). If a row or a column stops being played, it will never be played henceforth. An action profile
(a_{1}(k+1),a_{2}(k+1))
is played with positive probability only if
(a_{1}(k),a_{2}(k))
was played at some previous round.

We prove this property inductively in
Section

C.2
. We take it for granted in what follows.

In accordance with
4.1
, for
k\geq 2
, we define
\underline{t_{k}}
to be the first round in which the action profile corresponding to payoff
k
is played with positive probability and
\overline{t_{k}}
the last round before the action profile corresponding to payoff
k+1
is played with positive probability. We then define
T_{k}\coloneqq\overline{t_{k}}-\underline{t_{k}}+1
to be the number of rounds corresponding to the period
[\underline{t_{k}},\overline{t_{k}}]
.

We also define
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}(k)\coloneqq\{a_{1}(k^{\prime}):2m-1\geq k^{\prime}\geq k\}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}(k)\coloneqq\{a_{2}(k^{\prime}):2m-1\geq k^{\prime}\geq k\}
. These are the rows and columns, respectively, that will be played after the action profile corresponding to
k
starts being played. The next crucial lemma shows that before an action becomes desirable, it will have accumulated very negative regret in the previous rounds.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any even
k\geq 4
, let
{\bm{r}}_{1}^{(\overline{t_{k-2}})}[a_{1}]
be the regret of Player 1 with respect to any action
a_{1}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}(k)
. Then
{\bm{r}}_{1}^{(\overline{t_{k-2}})}[a_{1}]\leq-\sum_{l=2}^{k-2}(l-1)T_{l}
. Similarly, for any odd
k\geq 5

{\bm{r}}_{2}^{(\overline{t_{k-2}})}[a_{2}]
is the regret of Player 2 with respect to any action
a_{2}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{2}(k)
,
{\bm{r}}_{2}^{(\overline{t_{k-2}})}[a_{2}]\leq-\sum_{l=2}^{k-2}(l-1)T_{l}
.

At the same time, when an action has very negative regret, it will take a long time before that action gets played with positive probability, as formalized below.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any even
k\geq 4

T_{k}\geq-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{r}}_{2}^{(\overline{t_{k-1}})}[a_{2}(k+1)]

T_{k}\geq-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{r}}_{1}^{(\overline{t_{k-1}})}[a_{1}(k+1)]

By

T_{k}\geq\sum_{l=2}^{k-1}\frac{l-1}{2}T_{l}
for any

for all

.

Moreover, it takes as least

rounds to converge to an NE with approximation gap at most
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2m+2}}
(

The same reasoning directly applies to alternating

Alternating

5
Future research

Our paper sheds new light on the convergence properties of regret matching(
+
) in constrained optimization problems in general, and potential games in particular. We showed that alternating
\texttt{RM}^{+}
is a sound and fast first-order optimizer; on the flip side,
RM
can be exponentially slow even in two-player identical-interest games.

Several interesting questions remain open. Does
simultaneous

\texttt{RM}^{+}
always achieve fast convergence? Our current approach can only handle symmetric potential games (
Corollary

3.10
), in which case the analysis reduces to polynomial optimization over a single simplex. It would also be interesting to understand whether alternating
\texttt{RM}^{+}
can experience
\Omega(\sqrt{T})
regret in potential games; this is known to be the case in zero-sum games
[
Farina et al. 2023
]
, but remains unclear for the class of potential games. In light of
Theorem

3.4
, any improvement over the
\sqrt{T}
barrier would automatically translate into a faster convergence rate to Nash equilibria. Finally, does
RM
asymptotically converge even under alternating updates?

Further related work

Much of the existing research on regret matching revolves around zero-sum games. Many variants have been proposed over the years to speed up its convergence
[
Xu et al. 2024b
,
Cai et al. 2025
,
Chakrabarti et al. 2024
,
Meng et al. 2025
,
Farina et al. 2021
,
Tammelin 2014
,
Brown and Sandholm 2019a
]
. Some notable variations that have considerably improved performance are
predictive

RM
and
\texttt{RM}^{+}

[
Farina et al. 2021
]
, which rely on predicting the next utility, and
discounted

RM
and
\texttt{RM}^{+}

[
Brown and Sandholm 2019a
,
Zhang et al. 2024
,
Xu et al. 2024a
]
, where one dynamically discounts the accumulated regret; in a similar vein, our work shows that a discounted variant of
\texttt{RM}^{+}
achieves a better convergence upper bound than
\texttt{RM}^{+}
in our setting (
Corollary

3.5
). It must be stressed that the focus of all that prior work was on zero-sum games. Constrained optimization is a fundamentally different problem. For one, in zero-sum games, it is only the average strategy of

and
\texttt{RM}^{+}
that converges, not the last iterate
[
Farina et al. 2023
]
.

The recent paper of
Tewolde et al. 2025a
demonstrated that the regret matching family is a formidable first-order optimizer in constrained optimization problems. In particular, their focus was on (single-player) imperfect-recall problems, which are tantamount to general polynomial optimization problems over a product of simplices. Interestingly, many of the trends observed in zero-sum games are actually reversed in constrained optimization. For example, the predictive versions of
RM
and
\texttt{RM}^{+}
generally performed worse than their non-predictive counterparts. One trend that did persist was the superiority of
\texttt{RM}^{+}
over
RM
. It is also worth mentioning an earlier work by
Ma and Gerber 2014
that also reported fast empirical convergence in a certain class of congestion games. Yet, there was hitherto no theoretical understanding of those algorithms in this setting. The main precursors of our work are the paper of
Hart and Mas-Colell 2003
, which established asymptotic convergence in discrete time but for a somewhat artificial variant of regret matching, and the paper of
Marden et al. 2007
, which analyzed asymptotically a certain variant of regret matching that aggressively discounts the regrets (
cf.

Corollary

3.5
).

An interesting result that sheds light on
RM
and
\texttt{RM}^{+}
is by
Farina et al. 2021
, who showed that
RM
can be obtained by running
follow the regularized leader (
FTRL
)
in a certain lifted space, whereas
\texttt{RM}^{+}
can be obtained through
mirror descent (
MD
)
in the same space; this is despite the fact that, unlike
FTRL
and
MD
,
RM
and
\texttt{RM}^{+}
are both parameter free. On a related note,
Cai et al. 2024
showed that only forgetful algorithms—closer to
MD
than to
FTRL
—can attain fast last-iterate convergence. Our exponential separation of
RM
and
\texttt{RM}^{+}
echoes their finding, although in a different setting and class of algorithms.

Zooming out of the
RM
family, understanding the convergence of no-regret dynamics in potential games has been a popular research topic
[
Kleinberg et al. 2009
,
Héliou et al. 2017
,
Palaiopanos et al. 2017

Panageas et al. 2023b
,
Cui et al. 2022

. Our research also relates to parameter-free optimization; for example, we refer to

Coarse correlated equilibria

For completeness, we provide the definition of a coarse correlated equilibrium
[
Moulin and Vial 1978
]
, which is a relaxation of correlated equilibria
[
Aumann 1974
]
. The key connection that relates to our results is that if all players in a normal-form game have sublinear regret, the average correlated distribution of play converges to the set of coarse correlated equilibria. In particular, the rate of convergence is driven by the maximum of the players’ regrets (
Proposition

B.2
).

Definition B.1

(Coarse correlated equilibrium)
.

Consider an
n
-player game in normal form. A correlated distribution
\mu\in\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{1}\times\dots\times{\mathcal{A}}_{n})
is an
\epsilon
-coarse correlated equilibrium (CCE)
if for any player
i\in[n]
and deviation
a_{i}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
,

\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\sim\mu}u_{i}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\geq\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbb{E}}_{(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\sim\mu}u_{i}(a_{i}^{\prime},a_{-i})-\epsilon.

Proposition B.2
.

If each player
i\in[n]
observes the sequence of utilities
({\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}^{(t)}_{-i}))_{t=1}^{T}
, the average correlated distribution of play is an
\epsilon
-CCE with
\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{T}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathsf{Reg}_{i}^{(T)}
, where
\mathsf{Reg}_{i}^{(T)}
is the regret of the
i
th player.

This connection holds for simultaneous updates. It is unclear if and how it can be extended under alternating updates. For the special case of potential games and
\texttt{RM}^{+}
, which is our main focus here, we are indeed able to establish convergence to the set of CCEs even under alternating updates by bounding the path length of the players’ strategies (
Remark

C.9
).

Other notions of regret

Section

2.2
introduced the usual notion of regret used in online linear optimization. For a constrained optimization problem with respect to a differentiable function
u
, we have
\mathsf{Reg}^{(T)}=\max_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{x}}^{\prime}-{\bm{x}}^{(t)},\nabla_{\bm{x}}u({\bm{x}}^{(t)})\rangle
. This is a linearized version of
\max_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(u({\bm{x}}^{\prime})-u({\bm{x}}^{(t)}))
. Minimizing the latter notion is computationally intractable unless one places restrictive assumptions on
u
.
Hazan et al. 2017
(
cf.

Angelopoulos et al. 2025
) introduced the notion of “local regret,” and showed that having sublinear local regret implies that a randomly selected iterate will be an approximate stationary point; this is in stark contrast to regret as defined in
Section

2.2
(
Proposition

3.2
).

C.5
is identical to that of
Lemma

3.3
.

The next elementary lemma shows that, so long as the norm of the regret vector is not too small, closeness in regrets implies closeness in strategies.

Lemma C.6
.

For
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\geq 0}\ni{\bm{r}},{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\neq\bm{0}
, let
{\bm{x}}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{{\bm{r}}}}{{\|{\bm{r}}\|_{1}}}
and
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{{\bm{r}}^{\prime}}}{{\|{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\|_{1}}}
. Then

\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq\|\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}\|_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\|\bm{r}\|_{1}}+\frac{1}{\|\bm{r}^{\prime}\|_{1}}\right).

Proof.

The term
{\bm{x}}[a]-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}[a]
can be expressed, for any
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
, as

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{r}[a]}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}}\bm{r}[a^{\prime}]}-\frac{\bm{r}^{\prime}[a]}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}}\bm{r}^{\prime}[a^{\prime}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}}(\bm{r}[a]\bm{r}^{\prime}[a^{\prime}]-\bm{r}^{\prime}[a]\bm{r}[a^{\prime}])}{\|\bm{r}\|_{1}\|\bm{r}^{\prime}\|_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}}(\bm{r}[a](\bm{r}^{\prime}[a^{\prime}]-\bm{r}[a^{\prime}])+\bm{r}[a^{\prime}](\bm{r}[a]-\bm{r}^{\prime}[a]))}{\|\bm{r}\|_{1}\|\bm{r}^{\prime}\|_{1}},

and the claim follows.
∎

With those two helper lemmas in hand, we are now ready to analyze (non-lazy) alternating
\texttt{RM}^{+}
in potential games.

Theorem C.7
.

In any potential game with utilities in
[0,1]
, alternating
\texttt{RM}^{+}
requires at most
2\lceil\frac{625m^{4}n^{4}\Phi_{\mathsf{range}}^{2}}{\delta^{4}\epsilon^{4}}\rceil
rounds to converge to an
\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium, where
\delta_{i}\coloneqq\mathsf{BRGap}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{(1)},{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(1)})>0
and
\delta\coloneqq\min_{1\leq i\leq n}\delta_{i}
.

The assumption that
\delta_{i}>0
is without any loss in the following sense. The analysis requires that
\|{\bm{r}}^{(t)}_{i}\|_{2}>0
for all
i\in[n]
and any sufficiently large
t
. By
Lemma

3.8
, it suffices if a player incurs a positive best-response gap
at some
time. In the contrary case, if a player always has zero best-response gap, it will always play the same strategy (by definition of
\texttt{RM}^{+}
in
Algorithm

2
), thereby reducing to a potential game with
n-1
players. We further remark that, in accordance with
Theorem

3.4
, the bound in
Theorem

C.7
can be similarly parameterized in terms of the maximum regret incurred by a player. A more pedantic point about
Theorem

C.7
is that utilities are taken to be in
[0,1]
, while in the rest of the paper we only assume that the range is bounded by
1
; this innocuous assumption is used in
C.8
below.

Proof of
Theorem

C.7
.

By
Lemma

C.5
, the telescopic summation after
T
rounds yields

\Phi_{\mathsf{range}}\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{1}}\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t)}-{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{\max_{i}\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t)}-{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}.

(10)

Since
\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{1}\leq m\sqrt{t}
for any player
i\in[n]
, it follows that
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq m\Phi_{\mathsf{range}}\sqrt{T}
. As a result, after at most
2\lceil\nicefrac{{m^{2}\Phi_{\mathsf{range}}^{2}}}{{\epsilon^{4}}}\rceil
rounds, there will be a time
t
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t)}-{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}
, which in turn implies
\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t)}-{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t-1)}\|_{2},\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
for all
i\in[n]
. Now, by assumption, we know that
\|{\bm{r}}^{(1)}_{i}\|_{2}\geq\delta_{i}>0
for all
i\in[n]
. By the monotonicity property of the regret vector (
Lemma

3.8
, proven in the sequel), it follows that
\|{\bm{r}}^{(t)}_{i}\|_{2}\geq\delta_{i}>0
for all
t\in[T+1]
and
i\in[n]
. Consequently, applying
Lemma

C.6
, we have
\|{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{1}\leq 2\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{1}/\delta_{i}\leq 2\sqrt{m}\epsilon/\delta_{i}
since
\|\cdot\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{m}\|\cdot\|_{2}
. Next, we will make use of the following simple claim.

Claim C.8
.

Consider a normal-form game with utilities in
[0,1]
. For any two joint strategies
({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})
and
({\bm{x}}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\prime})
, it holds that
\|{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{-i})-{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{-i}^{\prime})\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}\|{\bm{x}}_{i^{\prime}}-{\bm{x}}_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}\|_{1}
for any player
i\in[n]
.

Proof.

We fix a player
i\in[n]
. We have

\displaystyle\|{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{-i})-{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{-i}^{\prime})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{\bm{a}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\times\dots\times{\mathcal{A}}_{n}}u_{i}(\cdot,\bm{a}_{-i})\left(\prod_{i^{\prime}\neq i}{\bm{x}}_{i^{\prime}}[a_{i^{\prime}}]-\prod_{i^{\prime}\neq i}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}[a_{i^{\prime}}]\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sum_{\bm{a}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\times\dots\times{\mathcal{A}}_{n}}\left(\prod_{i^{\prime}\neq i}{\bm{x}}_{i^{\prime}}[a_{i^{\prime}}]-\prod_{i^{\prime}\neq i}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}[a_{i^{\prime}}]\right)\right|

(11)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}\|{\bm{x}}_{i^{\prime}}-{\bm{x}}_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}\|_{1},

(12)

where (
11
) uses triangle inequality together with the assumption that
|u_{i}(\cdot)|\leq 1
, and (
12
) uses a bound on the total variation distance of a product distribution in terms of the sum of the total variation distances of its marginals
[
Hoeffding and Wolfowitz 1958
]
.
∎

Using this lemma, we now observe that
\|{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)}-{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{-i}^{(t)})\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{i^{\prime}<i}\|{\bm{x}}^{(t+1)}_{i^{\prime}}-{\bm{x}}^{(t)}_{i^{\prime}}\|_{1}\leq\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}\|{\bm{x}}^{(t+1)}_{i^{\prime}}-{\bm{x}}^{(t)}_{i^{\prime}}\|_{1}\leq 2n\sqrt{m}\epsilon/\delta
for each
i\in[n]
, where
\delta=\min_{1\leq i\leq n}\delta_{i}
. Furthermore, in view of the fact that
\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t)}-{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
, it follows that
\|{\bm{u}}^{(t)}_{i}\|_{\infty}-\langle{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)}\rangle\leq\epsilon
. Thus,

\displaystyle\|{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}^{(t)}_{-i})\|_{\infty}-\langle{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}^{(t)}_{-i})\rangle

\displaystyle=\|{\bm{u}}^{(t)}_{i}\|_{\infty}-\langle{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)}\rangle+\|{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}^{(t)}_{-i})\|_{\infty}-\|{\bm{u}}^{(t)}_{i}\|_{\infty}+\langle{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}^{(t)}_{i}-{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}^{(t)}_{-i})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|{\bm{u}}^{(t)}_{i}\|_{\infty}-\langle{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)}\rangle+2\|{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)}-{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{-i}^{(t)})\|_{\infty}

(13)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\left(1+\frac{4n\sqrt{m}}{\delta}\right)\leq\epsilon\left(5\frac{n\sqrt{m}}{\delta}\right).

where (
13
) follows from the fact that
\langle{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}^{(t)}_{i}-{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}^{(t)}_{-i})\rangle\leq\|{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{1}\|{\bm{u}}^{(t)}_{i}-{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}^{(t)}_{-i})\|_{\infty}\leq\|{\bm{u}}^{(t)}_{i}-{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}^{(t)}_{-i})\|_{\infty}
since
\|{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{1}=1
. We conclude that
({\bm{x}}_{1}^{(t)},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(t)})
is an
\epsilon(\nicefrac{{5n\sqrt{m}}}{{\delta}})
-Nash equilibrium; rescaling
\epsilon
leads to the claim.
∎

Remark C.9

(Convergence to CCE under alternating updates)
.

The folk connection linking no-regret learning and coarse correlated equilibria (
Proposition

B.2
) is predicated on the dynamics being executed in a simultaneous fashion. We observe that, in potential games, even alternating
\texttt{RM}^{+}
converges to the set of CCEs in the following sense.
Lemma

C.6
together with (
10
) imply that
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)}\|^{2}_{1}=O_{T}(\sqrt{T})
. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in turn yields

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{1}\right)^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}1^{2}}=O_{T}(T^{3/4}).

(14)

Now, by the fact that
\texttt{RM}^{+}
has the no-regret property (
Proposition

2.3
),
\max_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{i}\in\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{i})}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}-{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)}\rangle=O_{T}(\sqrt{T})
. Further, by
C.8
and (
14
),
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}^{(t)}_{-i})-{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}\|{\bm{x}}_{i^{\prime}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{x}}_{i^{\prime}}^{(t)}\|_{1}=O_{T}(T^{3/4})
. As a result, we conclude that, for any player
i\in[n]
,

\max_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}_{i}\in\Delta({\mathcal{A}}_{i})}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}-{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{-i}^{(t)})\rangle=O_{T}(T^{3/4}).

This implies that the correlated distribution
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)}
is an
\epsilon
-CCE for some
\epsilon=O_{T}(T^{-1/4})
(
Proposition

B.2
); here,
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)}
denotes the product distribution induced by
({\bm{x}}_{1}^{(t)},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n}^{(t)})
.

Moving on, a further implication of
Lemma

C.4
is that having a large regret vector can slow down
\texttt{RM}^{+}
. Employing discounting, in the form of
\texttt{DRM}^{+}
(
Algorithm

3
), addresses this deficiency in potential games, as we prove below. It is worth stressing that while discounting is sensible when employing alternating
\texttt{RM}^{+}
in potential games, this is not the case in more general constrained optimization problems; there, having a regret vector with a
small
norm can impede convergence (
cf.

Lemma

3.7
).

See
3.5

Proof.

Lemma

3.3
can be directly adjusted for
\texttt{DRM}^{+}
: the updated strategy
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}=\nicefrac{{{\bm{r}}^{\prime}}}{{\|{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\|_{1}}}
, where
\bm{r}^{\prime}=\alpha[\bm{r}+{\bm{u}}-\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle\bm{1}]^{+}\neq\bm{0}
, remains the same since we only rescaled the regret vector by
\alpha
. That is, for any
{\bm{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathcal{A}}}
,

\langle{\bm{x}}^{\prime}-{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{\|\bm{r}^{\prime}\|_{1}}\left(\max_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\bm{u}}[a]-\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle\right)^{2}.

The key difference compared to
Theorem

3.4
is that we now maintain the invariance
\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\nicefrac{{m}}{{\gamma}}}
for all
i\in[n]
and
t\in[T]
(
Corollary

C.3
). Consequently,

\Phi_{\mathsf{range}}\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\|{\bm{r}}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{1}}\mathsf{BRGap}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)})^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{\mathsf{BRGap}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)})>\epsilon\}.

If in every round
t\in[T]
there is a player
i\in[n]
such that
\mathsf{BRGap}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}_{i}^{(t)})>\epsilon
, we have
\Phi_{\mathsf{range}}\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\nicefrac{{\sqrt{\gamma}}}{{m}})\epsilon^{2}
, so
T\leq\frac{m\Phi_{\mathsf{range}}}{\epsilon^{2}\sqrt{\gamma}}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Unlike
\texttt{RM}^{+}
and
\texttt{DRM}^{+}
,
RM
only has a
conditional
one-step improvement because the regret vector can have negative coordinates.

See
3.6

Proof.

We define
\bm{\delta}\coloneqq\bm{\theta}^{\prime}-\bm{\theta}
. Following the proof of
Lemma

3.3
, it suffices to show that

\sum_{a^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}}\bm{\theta}[a^{\prime}]\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\bm{\delta}[a]({\bm{u}}[a]-\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle)\geq\sum_{a^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}}\bm{\theta}[a^{\prime}]\left(\max_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\bm{u}}[a]-\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle\right)^{2}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\bm{r}[a]\geq 0\right\}.

(15)

For an action
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
, we consider the following cases.

•

If
{\bm{u}}[a]-\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle\geq 0
,

–

if
\bm{r}[a]\geq 0
, we have
\bm{\delta}[a]={\bm{u}}[a]-\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle
.

–

If
\bm{r}[a]<0
, it follows that
\bm{\delta}[a]\geq 0
; in particular,
\bm{\delta}[a]=0
if
\bm{r}^{\prime}[a]\leq 0
and
\bm{\delta}[a]>0
otherwise. As a result,
\bm{\delta}[a]({\bm{u}}[a]-\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle)\geq 0
.

•

If
{\bm{u}}[a]-\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle<0
,

–

if
\bm{r}[a]\leq 0
, we have
\bm{\delta}[a]=0
since
\bm{\theta}[a]=0=\bm{\theta}^{\prime}[a]
.

–

if
\bm{r}[a]>0

\bm{\delta}[a]<0
. Again, we have
\bm{\delta}[a]({\bm{u}}[a]-\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}\rangle)\geq 0
.

Combining those items, (

) follows.
∎

We now turn to the more general setting of constrained optimization. First, combining

Proof.

Using the quadratic bound for

, we have

\displaystyle u({\bm{x}}^{\prime})-u({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle\geq\langle\nabla u({\bm{x}}),{\bm{x}}^{\prime}-{\bm{x}}\rangle-\frac{L}{2}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\|{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\|_{1}}\|{\bm{r}}-{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{L}{2}\|{\bm{r}}-{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\|_{1}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\|{\bm{r}}\|_{1}}+\frac{1}{\|{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\|_{1}}\right)^{2}

(16)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\|{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\|_{1}}\|{\bm{r}}-{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{9mL}{2\|{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\|_{1}^{2}}\|{\bm{r}}-{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}

(17)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\|{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\|_{1}}\|{\bm{r}}-{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2},

(18)

where (
16
) uses the one-step improvement property (
Lemma

C.5
) applied for
{\bm{u}}\coloneqq\nabla u({\bm{x}})
together with

C.6
; (
17

\|\bm{r}\|_{1}\geq\|\bm{r}^{\prime}\|_{1}-m\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{r}^{\prime}\|_{1}

\|{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\|_{1}\geq\|{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\|_{2}\geq 2m

for all

) follows from the assumption that

.
∎

To make use of
Lemma

3.7
, we next establish that the
\ell_{2}
norm of the regret vector under
\texttt{RM}^{+}
is nondecreasing.

See
3.8

Proof.

We have
{\bm{r}}^{(t)}-{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)}=\max({\bm{g}}^{(t)},-{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)})
(element-wise), so

\|{\bm{r}}^{(t)}-{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}=\|\max({\bm{g}}^{(t)},-{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)})\|_{2}\geq\|[{\bm{g}}^{(t)}]^{+}\|_{2}.

Further,
\langle{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)},{\bm{r}}^{(t)}-{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)}\rangle=\langle{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)},\max({\bm{g}}^{(t)},-{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)})\rangle\geq\langle{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)},{\bm{g}}^{(t)}\rangle=0
, where we used the fact that
{\bm{r}}^{t}\geq\bm{0}
, coordinate-wise. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|{\bm{r}}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}=\|{\bm{r}}^{(t)}-{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)}+{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|{\bm{r}}^{(t)}-{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\|{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}+2\langle{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)},{\bm{r}}^{(t)}-{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\|{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\|[{\bm{g}}^{(t)}]^{+}\|_{2}^{2},

as claimed.

Armed with
Lemmas

3.7
and
3.8
, we can now prove

3.9
.

See
3.9

Proof.

Let
t_{c}\in[T]
be the largest
t
such that
\|{\bm{r}}^{(t)}\|_{2}<\max\{2m,9mL\}=R
. By

3.8
,

\|{\bm{r}}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\|{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\|[{\bm{g}}^{(t)}]_{+}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\|{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\mathsf{KKTGap}({\bm{x}}^{(t)})^{2};

so,

\sum_{t=1}^{t_{c}}\mathsf{KKTGap}({\bm{x}}^{(t)})^{2}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{t_{c}}(\|{\bm{r}}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}-\|{\bm{r}}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2})=\|{\bm{r}}^{(t_{c})}\|_{2}^{2}\leq R^{2}.

(19)

Further, for any
t\geq t_{c}+1

\|{\bm{r}}^{(t)}\|_{2}\geq R
since the
\ell_{2}

. Thus, by

3.7
,

\sum_{t=t_{c}+1}^{T}\frac{1}{2\|{\bm{r}}^{(t)}\|_{1}}\mathsf{KKTGap}({\bm{x}}^{(t)})^{2}\leq u({\bm{x}}^{(T+1)})-u({\bm{x}}^{(t_{c}+1)})\leq u_{\textsf{range}}.

(20)

19
) and (

), together with the fact that

,

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{m\sqrt{t}}\mathsf{KKTGap}({\bm{x}}^{(t)})^{2}\leq 2u_{\textsf{range}}+R^{2}.

Next, we use
Theorem

3.9
to establish that even
simultaneous

\texttt{RM}^{+}
converges in symmetric potential games, as long as the players have the same initialization.

See
3.10

Proof.

We will argue that simultaneous
\texttt{RM}^{+}
, under the same initialization, in a symmetric game is tantamount to running
\texttt{RM}^{+}
with respect to the function
\Delta({\mathcal{A}})\ni{\bm{x}}\mapsto\Phi({\bm{x}},\dots,{\bm{x}})
, where
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}=\dots={\mathcal{A}}_{n}={\mathcal{A}}
. We first claim that for any
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
,

\frac{\partial\Phi({\bm{x}},\dots,{\bm{x}})}{\partial{\bm{x}}[a]}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\Phi({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{i}[a]}\bigg\mid_{{\bm{x}}_{1}=\dots={\bm{x}}_{n}={\bm{x}}}.

(21)

By definition,
\Phi({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})
is multilinear. Let us consider a monomial of
\Phi
of the form

m_{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{x}}_{i}[a_{i}]

for some joint action
{\bm{a}}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\times\dots\times{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial m_{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}},\dots,{\bm{x}})}{\partial{\bm{x}}[a]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bm{x}}[a]}\left(\prod_{a^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}({\bm{x}}[a^{\prime}])^{d(a^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}d(a)({\bm{x}}[a])^{d(a)-1}\prod_{a^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\setminus\{a\}}({\bm{x}}[a^{\prime}])^{d(a^{\prime})}&\text{if }a\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\text{ and}\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(22)

where
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}(\bm{a})=\{a^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}:\exists i\in[n]\text{ such that }a^{\prime}=a_{i}\}
and degrees
d(a^{\prime})=|\{i\in[n]:a_{i}=a^{\prime}\}|\geq 1
for
a^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
. Further,

\frac{\partial m_{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{i}[a]}=\begin{cases}\prod_{i^{\prime}\neq i}{\bm{x}}_{i^{\prime}}[a_{i^{\prime}}]&\text{if }a=a_{i}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In particular,

\frac{\partial m_{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{i}[a]}\bigg\mid_{{\bm{x}}_{1}=\dots={\bm{x}}_{n}}=\mathbbm{1}\{a=a_{i}\}{\bm{x}}[a]^{d(a)-1}\prod_{a^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\setminus\{a\}}({\bm{x}}[a^{\prime}])^{d(a^{\prime})}.

As a result,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial m_{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{i}[a]}\bigg\mid_{{\bm{x}}_{1}=\dots={\bm{x}}_{n}}=\mathbbm{1}\{a\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\}d(a){\bm{x}}[a]^{d(a)-1}\prod_{a^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\setminus\{a\}}({\bm{x}}[a^{\prime}])^{d(a^{\prime})},

which matches the expression in (
22
). That is, we have shown that for any
\bm{a}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\times\dots\times{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
,

\frac{\partial m_{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}},\dots,{\bm{x}})}{\partial{\bm{x}}[a]}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial m_{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{i}[a]}\bigg\mid_{{\bm{x}}_{1}=\dots={\bm{x}}_{n}}.

Since
\Phi({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})=\sum_{\bm{a}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\times\dots\times{\mathcal{A}}_{n}}\Phi(\bm{a})m_{\bm{a}}({\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})
, (
21
) follows by linearity. Moreover, the symmetry assumption concerning the potential
\Phi
tells us that

Lemma C.13

Uniform initialization

So far, our lower bound assumes that one can initialize
RM
arbitrarily. A more common initialization prescribes randomizing uniformly at random. In what follows, we point out that the same argument works by considering a different common payoff matrix; namely,

{\mathbb{R}}^{(2m)\times(2m)}\ni\tilde{\mathbf{B}}[a_{1},a_{2}]\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathbf{A}[a_{1},a_{2}]&\text{if }1\leq a_{1}\leq m\text{ and }1\leq a_{2}\leq m;\\
1-\frac{1}{m}&\text{if }a_{1}=1\text{ and }m+1\leq a_{2}\leq 2m;\\
1-\frac{3}{m}&\text{if }m+1\leq a_{1}\leq 2m\text{ and }a_{2}=1;\\
-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{a_{2}^{\prime}=1}^{m}\mathbf{A}[a_{1},a_{2}^{\prime}]&\text{if }2\leq a_{1}\leq m\text{ and }m+1\leq a_{2}\leq 2m;\\
-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{a_{1}^{\prime}=1}^{m}\mathbf{A}[a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}]&\text{if }m+1\leq a_{1}\leq 2m\text{ and }2\leq a_{2}\leq m;\\
\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{a_{1}^{\prime}=1}^{m}\sum_{a_{2}^{\prime}=1}^{m}\mathbf{A}[a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime}]-\frac{2}{m}&\text{if }m+1\leq a_{1}\leq 2m\text{ and }m+1\leq a_{2}\leq 2m.\end{cases}

By assumption,
{\bm{x}}_{1}^{(1)}
and
{\bm{x}}_{2}^{(1)}
are the uniform distributions over
[2m]
. We observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{a_{1}=1}^{2m}\sum_{a_{2}=1}^{2m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}[a_{1},a_{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{1}=1}^{m}\sum_{a_{2}=1}^{m}\mathbf{A}[a_{1},a_{2}]+m\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)+m\left(1-\frac{3}{m}\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{a_{1}=2}^{m}\sum_{a_{2}=1}^{m}\mathbf{A}[a_{1},a_{2}]-\sum_{a_{1}=1}^{m}\sum_{a_{2}=2}^{m}\mathbf{A}[a_{1},a_{2}]+\sum_{a_{1}=1}^{m}\sum_{a_{2}=1}^{m}\mathbf{A}[a_{1},a_{2}]-2m

\displaystyle=2\sum_{a_{1}=1}^{m}\sum_{a_{2}=1}^{m}\mathbf{A}[a_{1},a_{2}]-\sum_{a_{1}=2}^{m}\sum_{a_{2}=1}^{m}\mathbf{A}[a_{1},a_{2}]-1-\sum_{a_{1}=1}^{m}\sum_{a_{2}=2}^{m}\mathbf{A}[a_{1},a_{2}]-3=0

since

and

{\bm{u}}_{1}^{(1)}=\tilde{\mathbf{B}}{\bm{x}}_{2}^{(1)}
and
{\bm{u}}_{2}^{(1)}=\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{1}^{(1)}

{\bm{u}}_{1}^{(1)}[a_{1}]=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{a_{2}=1}^{m}\mathbf{A}[a_{1}=1,a_{2}]+\frac{1}{2m}m\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)=\frac{1}{2}&\text{if }a_{1}=1;\\
\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{a_{2}=1}^{m}\mathbf{A}[a_{1},a_{2}]-\frac{1}{2m}m\frac{1}{m}\sum_{a_{2}=1}^{m}\mathbf{A}[a_{1},a_{2}]=0&\text{if }2\leq a_{1}\leq m;\\

Since

{\bm{r}}_{1}^{(1)}[a_{1}]=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}&\text{if }a_{1}=1;\\

Similarly, we have

{\bm{r}}_{2}^{(1)}[a_{2}]=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}&\text{if }a_{2}=1;\\

As a result, the second round sees both players playing the first action with probability 1. In view of the fact that

and
