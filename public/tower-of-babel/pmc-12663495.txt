Pectins extracted from cranberry pomace: A study in physicochemical, structural, functional properties and the application in peach preservation
Authors: Fan Wang, Jun Yang, Xu Yang, Xiquan Li, Xiuling Zhang, Zhili Pan, Chen Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCranberry pomace was utilized to extract pectins using various methods (water, oxalic acid, and natural deep eutectic solvent (NADES)) with or without ultrasound assistance. Ultrasound-assisted NADES extraction (US-NCPP) yielded the highest pectin content (5.53 %) and the highest galacturonic acid (GalA) content (83.20 %), indicating superior purity. US-NCPP also exhibited outstanding antioxidant activity, with the lowest IC50 values for both ABTS (0.55 mg/mL) and DPPH (0.21 mg/mL) radical scavenging. The degree of esterification (DE) and FTIR analysis revealed that all pectins were high-methoxyl (DE: 71.58–85.21 %). Furthermore, an edible film fabricated from US-NCPP significantly enhanced the postharvest quality of peach fruit during an 8-day storage period. Compared to the control, the film extended the shelf life of peach samples from 4 d to 8 d at room temperature. These findings demonstrate that US-NCPP is a highly effective strategy for obtaining high-quality, antioxidant-rich pectin from cranberry pomace, which shows great potential as a bioactive film for fruit preservation.Keywords: Cranberry pomace, Pectin extraction, Structure, Antioxidant activity, Peach preservation

Introduction

Cranberries are rich in nutrients, however, due to the sourness and astringency of their fresh fruits, they are rarely eaten directly and are often processed into products such as juice, wine and jam (Blumberg et al., 2013). Therefore, cranberry residue has become the main by-product of cranberry processing. Spadoni Andreani, Karboune, and Liu (2021) reported that pectin polysaccharides accounted for more than 90 % of the entire carbohydrate content of cranberry pomace. The extraction and utilization of pectin from cranberry residue have become a research hotspot. The typical industrial method of pectin extraction is inorganic acid extraction, and strongly acidic conditions tend to cause equipment corrosion and environmental pollution (Cui et al., 2021). To mitigate these negative effects and improve the environmental sustainability of pectin production, researchers are increasingly focusing on green extraction methods.

Water, organic acids, and natural deep eutectic solvents have been widely used as mild extractants for pectin (Ly & Sothornvit, 2024; Rodsamran & Sothornvit, 2019b). Among them, NADES, a novel green extractant with a low melting point composed of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, has gained attention because of its nontoxicity, biodegradability, thermal stability, low volatility, and cost effectiveness (Zdanowicz, Wilpiszewska, & Spychaj, 2018). Moreover, ultrasonication is often used as a green physical method to assist in extracting pectins. Numerous studies have shown that ultrasonic cavitation can disintegrate solids and disrupt cell walls and is an effective method for the green modification of pectins (Abou-Elseoud, Hassan, & Hassan, 2021; Sabater, Sabater, Olano, Montilla, & Corzo, 2020; Shivamathi et al., 2019).

The films and emulsion made with pectin were also successfully used as a coating to preserve the commercial quality of fresh vegetables in postharvest storage (Rodsamran & Sothornvit, 2019a). Pectin based film is known as “green” packaging, compared with traditional synthetic polymers, it has certain advantages, such as low production cost, biodegradable, renewable, can be independently produced film or combined with other biopolymers, contains active ingredients, extend the shelf life of packaged food (Meerasri & Sothornvit, 2023; Rodsamran & Sothornvit, 2019c), is the ideal material for the development of bio-based film for food packaging. However, the beneficial effect of cranberry pomace pectin (CPP) as a coating on fruit preservation has not been reported.

In this study, the physicochemical components, structural characteristics and functional properties of cps extracted by different extraction methods, as well as the influence of CPP on the preservation of peaches, were systematically investigated. This work aims to enrich the sources of pectin in the processing of fruit matrix, explore the potential applications of pectin in food preservation, and provide new knowledge for improving the comprehensive utilization rate of the by-product cranberry residue under the premise of environmental friendliness.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemical reagents

Cranberry pomace was purchased from Heilongjiang Bei'an Nongken Tianyunshan Products Co., Ltd. The cranberry pomace was enzyme inactivated in a boiling water bath for 5 min and dried at 55 °C. The samples were subsequently crushed into powder via a grinder, passed through a 60-mesh sieve and set aside. Commercial apple pectin (CAP), monosaccharide standard and ascorbic acid (VC) was provided by Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), 1,1-Diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and 2,2-azinobis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulphonic acid) (ABTS) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co., Ltd. All other chemical reagents were of analytical grade.

Pectin extraction

Hot water extraction

The extraction was determined according to the methods of Yuliarti, Goh, Matia-Merino, Mawson, and Brennan (2015) with some modifications. The cranberry pomace powder was mixed with deionized water at a ratio of 1:20 (w/v) and then heated in a water bath at 80 °C with continuous stirring for 1.5 h. The mixture was then allowed to cool to 25 °C before being centrifuged for 15 min at 7500 ×g (THR16A, Yingtai Instrument Co., Ltd., China). A rotary evaporator (RE-5203, Shanghai Yarong Biochemical Instrument Factory, China) was then used to concentrate the supernatant to only 1/3 of its initial volume. The concentrated solution was mixed with 1.5 times anhydrous ethanol, incubated at 4 °C for 12 h, and then centrifuged to obtain the crude pectin extract.

Hot oxalic acid extraction and ultrasound-assisted oxalic acid extraction

The extraction was determined according to the methods of (Peng et al., 2022b) with some modifications. The powder was homogeneously mixed with an appropriate amount of deionized water. A 1 mol/L oxalic acid solution was added dropwise to the mixture to achieve a final pH of 1.7 and a material–liquid ratio of 1:20 (w/v). The pH adjusted samples were subjected to ultrasonication treatment times of 20 min (3 s on and 3 s off) that was controlled by a sonicator (VC750, SONICS, USA) with an outpower 400 W and frequency of 15 kHz. The samples treated with and without ultrasound were both heated and stirred in a water bath (80 °C, 1.5 h). The rest of the procedure was the same as that in section 2.2.1.

NADES extraction and ultrasonic-assisted NADES extraction

The extraction was determined according to the methods of Chen et al. (2022) with some modifications. For the preparation of the NADES, choline chloride (CC) and oxalic acid (OA) were mixed at a 1:2 M ratio and heated in a water bath (80 °C) until a clear and transparent stable solution formed at 25 °C, and water (30 %, v/v) was added to the solution. Dried cranberry pomace powder and NADESs were mixed evenly (1:20, w/v), The samples were subjected to ultrasonication treatment times of 20 min (3 s on and 3 s off) that was controlled by a sonicator (VC750, SONICS, USA) with an outpower 400 W and frequency of 15 kHz. The sample treated with and without ultrasound were both heated and stirred in a water bath (80 °C) for 1.5 h, and the remainder of the extraction process was the same as that described in section 2.2.1.

Monosaccharide composition

The monosaccharide composition was determined according to the methods of Min Yu, Zhou, Guo, Zheng, and Zhang (2021) with some modifications. Briefly, 1 mL pectin solution (5 mg/mL) was prepared, mixed with 1 mL trifluoroacetic acid solution (4 mol/L), and hydrolyzed at 120 °C for 2 h. The hydrolysis solution was cooled and spun dry with a rotary evaporator. Two milliliters of methanol was added, and the mixture was spun dry; this process was repeated 3 times. Then, deionized water was added to fully dissolve the pectin solution to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. One milliliter of the hydrolysis mixture was mixed with 1 mL of 0.3 M NaOH, 1 mL of 0.5 M 1-phenyl-3-methyl-5-pyrazolone (PMP)-methanol solution was added, and the mixture was heated at 70 °C for 60 min in a water bath. When the mixture cooled, 1 mL of 0.3 M HCl solution was added to neutralize it. Two milliliters of chloroform was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 30 s. The chloroform layer was discarded after centrifugation, which was repeated three times, the mixture was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane, and high-performance liquid chromatography (1260II Prime, Agilent, USA) with an ultraviolet detector was used. The chromatographic column used was an InfinityLab Poroahell C18 (4.6 mm × 150 mm, 4 μm). The detection wavelength, injection volume, and column temperature were 250 nm, 20 μL, and 25 °C, respectively. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.85) and acetonitrile (82:18, v/v). The flow rate was 1 mL/min. Structural characterization of the CPPs

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

The FTIR analysis was based on the method of W. W. X. M. Y. X. Y. C. Z. J. T. D. D. L, 2015with some modifications. The samples were homogeneously mixed with KBr powder at a ratio of 1:100 (w/w) and pressed into tablets. The functional group structures of the freeze-dried samples were subsequently analyzed via Fourier infrared spectroscopy (IRTracer-100, Shimadzu, Japan). The scanning wavelength range was 4000 cm−1-500 cm−1, and the scanning fraction rate was set to 2 cm−1 with 16 scans.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

The crystallinity of pectin was analyzed via X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns measured with an X-ray diffraction (XRD) diffractometer (D/MAX 2500 V, Rigaku, Japan). The measurement parameters were set as follows: the voltage was 40 kV, the current was 40 mA, the diffraction angle scan range was set to 10–50°, and the scan angle was set to 0.02° (Hoque et al., 2025).

1H NMR

Pectin lyophilized samples were fully dissolved in 99.9 % D2O and adjusted to 5 mg/mL. The 1H NMR spectra were measured using a fully digital superconducting NMR spectrometer at 80 °C (Avance NEO 600 M, Bruker Switzerland Ltd., Switzerland) with 256 scans (Benmebarek et al., 2024).

Morphological analysis

A small amount of pectin was evenly glued on the sample holder with conductive adhesive and sprayed with gold. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, S-3400 N, Hitachi, Japan) was then used to observe the surface morphology. The electron microscope acceleration voltage was 5 kV, and the magnifications were 500 × and 5.0 k × (Li, Zhang, Zhang, Liu, & Zhu, 2022).

Rheological properties

The rheological properties of the pectin solution were analyzed via a modular rotational rheometer (HAAKE MARS60, Thermo Fisher Scientific, China). Pectin was dissolved in deionized water and added to a 2 % (w/v) solution. The solution was then poured onto a 60 mm parallel plate. The apparent viscosity of the pectin solution was measured at shear rates ranging from 0.1 to 1000 s−1 (C. Wang et al., 2020).

Thermal analysis

The thermal properties of pectin were determined via differential scanning calorimetry (DSC250, TA Instruments, USA). A 5.0 mg sample was weighed and placed in an aluminum pan, with an empty pan serving as a control, and the measurement temperature was between 25 °C and 300 °C; the sample was heated at 10 °C/min and protected with nitrogen (Wani & Uppaluri, 2023).

The application of US-NCPP in peach preservation

Preparation of US-NCPP-based films

Pectin-based films of cranberry residue were prepared via the solution-casting method (X. Li et al., 2024). The pectin was dissolved in distilled water (3 %, w/v) and stirred continuously at 55 °C until the pectin was completely dissolved. Glycerin (30 % (w/w)) pectin mass was added to the pectin solution and stirred evenly to obtain the membrane mixture. The film-forming solution was poured into polyethylene plates at a volume of 0.45 g/cm2, left to stand at room temperature, dried and then stored in a desiccator (40 °C, 40 % RH) to prepare US-NCPP-based biofilms. The prepared US-NCPP-based film was used to wrap peaches, and its preservation effect on the peaches was studied.

Effects of the US-NCPP-based film on the storage quality of peach fruit

Okubo peaches with the same color, size and maturity and without mechanical damage or pests were selected for this preservation test. Peaches were divided into two groups: one group was freshly preserved with the US-NCPP-based film, and the other group without the film was used as the control group. For each treatment group, 30 peaches were used. Each quality parameter (weight loss, firmness, etc.) was measured destructively on 5 randomly selected peaches from each group at each sampling time point. Therefore, all measurements were performed with n = 5 biological replicates, and the results are presented as the mean of these 5 replicates ± standard deviation. Peaches were stored at room temperature at 25 ± 2 °C, the storage period was 8 days, and the index was measured every 2 days.

The firmness of the peaches was determined by a hardness tester. The total soluble solid (TSS) content was determined by a handheld refractometer. The weight loss was determined by the percentage of weight loss concerning day 0. The respiration intensity was measured via the titration method (Guerra et al., 2023). The malondialdehyde (MDA) content was determined via the thiobarbituric acid method (Elbarbary & Mostafa, 2014). The polyphenol oxidase activity was measured following the methods of Jiang et al. (2020).

Data analysis

At least three parallel groups were measured for each experiment, and their means and standard deviations were calculated. The experimental data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations and were analyzed via one-way ANOVA with SPSS 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA) and Tukey's test (P < 0.05). Origin Pro 2021 (Origin Lab Inc., Northampton, Massachusetts, USA) was used to plot the data.

Results and discussion

Physicochemical properties

As shown in Table 1, the yields of the five pectins from cranberry pomace ranged from 4.08 % to 5.53 %, among which US-NCPP had the highest yield (5.53 %). The protein content was reported because it is a key indicator of pectin purity and is closely linked to its functional properties (Ke, Lin, & Zhao, 2023). Pectin often contains proteinaceous material as a common impurity. The significant variation in protein content among our samples (Table 1) directly demonstrates that the extraction method selectively co-extracts or removes non-pectin compounds, thereby influencing the purity of the final product. US-OCPP had the highest protein content (5.24 %), followed by OCPP (4.16 %). The lower protein content in NCPP and US-NCPP may be because the acidic environment created by natural deep eutectic solvents is stronger than that created by oxalic acid solutions, which destroys the structure of the proteins. Among the CPPs, WCPP had the lowest total phenol content (6.24 mg/g), whereas US-NCPP and NCPP had higher total phenol contents (14.42 mg/g and 11.19 mg/g, respectively). In addition, ultrasonic-assisted treatment helps to enhance the solvent extraction of pectin from fruits, which is evident in citrus fruits, apples, and others(Zhang et al., 2013a; Zheng, Zeng, Kan, & Zhang, 2018).

Color

Some pigments in cranberry pomace were insoluble in ethanol or water, and these pigments were left in the pectin during the extraction of pectin, giving it a specific color.

Conventional methods, employing harsh conditions, generally produce darker pectin. Specifically, hot water extraction induces severe non-enzymatic browning due to high temperature, explaining its darkest color. Conversely, oxalic acid creates an acidic environment that stabilizes cranberry's native red anthocyanins, leading to a distinct reddish hue. In contrast, green methods using NADES offer superior color preservation. The inherent selectivity of the NADES solvent minimizes the co-extraction of dark impurities, resulting in a much lighter product.

As shown in Table 1, NCPP had the highest ΔL* value among CPPs, indicating that the pectin was brighter and whiter, and the lowest ΔL* value for WCPP, indicating that the pectin was darker. All pectin samples had positive Δa* values, indicating a reddish color, and US-OCPP had the highest Δa* value, indicating the greatest reddening. Δb* values for NCPP, US-NCPP, and US-OCPP were significantly higher than this for WCPP, indicating that these three pectins showed a darker yellow color. ΔE⁎ indicated the total color difference of each pectin sample, and the ΔE⁎ values of CPPs were in the order of WCPP > OCPP > US-OCPP > US-NCPP > NCPP, with NCPP having the lightest color and WCPP having the darkest color.

Monosaccharide composition

The GalA contents of the CPPs were much greater than those of the other monosaccharide fractions, accounting for 47.34 %–83.20 % (Table 1). This occurred because the main structural domains of pectin, HG, RG-I, and RG-II, are dominated by GalA, which is an important constituent of pectin. Among the three extraction methods without ultrasound-assisted treatment, NCPP had the highest GalA content, indicating high purity and that the HG structural domain may contain a relatively long backbone. In contrast, pectin extracted with water had the lowest GalA content. Moreover, the GalA contents of US-OCPP and US-NCPP were obviously greater than those of OCPP and NCPP, demonstrating that the pectin purity of cranberry pomace pectin could be improved by ultrasound-assisted treatment. In addition, the contents of Gal, Ara and Rha, the major neutral sugar components in the RG-I side chain, were greater in all the pectin samples. The low levels of xylose, mannose, and glucose in CPPs may be due to the other cell wall polysaccharides that were isolated during the extraction of pectin, such as cellulose and hemicellulose.

Notably, the (Gal + Ara)/Rha value can reflect the side-chain length. The (Gal + Ara)/Rha of OCPP (4.35 ± 0.15) was significantly greater than that of NCPP (3.38 ± 0.10). In addition, the (Gal + Ara)/Rha values of both US-OCPP and US-NCPP after sonication were lower than those of pectin without sonication, indicating that sonication may degrade the RG-I side chain length of pectin, resulting in shorter branches of the RG-I structural domain. This was consistent with the results that reported that acid extraction resulted in a higher (Gal + Ara)/Rha molar ratio than did DES-extracted pectin, where the side chains of pectin molecules were degraded by sonication, thus increasing the GalA content (Min Yu, 2021). The Rha/GalA value can reflect the main structural domains constituting pectin, and pectin with a ratio in the range of 0.05–1 is considered to be dominated by the RG-I structural domain (Schols & Voragen, 1996). The Rha/GalA ratios of the CPPs ranged from 0.03 to 0.04, and the HG structural domains of the CPPs ranged from 94.85 % to 96.73 %, indicating that the CPPs were dominated by the HG structural domain with a long skeleton.

DE

Based on the data presented in Table 1, the extraction method significantly influences the degree of esterification (DE) of cranberry pomace pectin. Among the different methods, NCPP, extracted using a natural deep eutectic solvent (NADES), exhibited the highest DE value (85.21 %), indicating a highly esterified pectin structure. In contrast, ultrasound-assisted extraction, particularly with oxalic acid (US-OCPP), resulted in the lowest DE (71.58 %), suggesting that ultrasonic treatment in combination with oxalic acid promotes de-esterification. The conventional oxalic acid extraction (OCPP, 77.87 % DE) also showed a lower DE compared to water extraction (WCPP, 82.30 %), implying that acidic conditions may contribute to partial de-esterification. S. Chen et al. (2022) extracted pectin from mango peels with natural deep eutectic solvents and reported that pectin extracted with natural deep eutectic solvents had a higher DE value than that extracted with hydrochloric acid. Interestingly, ultrasound-assisted NADES extraction (US-NCPP) led to a moderate DE value (72.75 %), which is higher than that of US-OCPP but lower than that of its non-sonicated counterpart (NCPP). This indicates that while ultrasound generally reduces DE, the choice of solvent plays a critical role in modulating the extent of de-esterification. Overall, these findings highlight that both the solvent nature and the use of physical assistance such as ultrasound can markedly alter the esterification profile of pectin, potentially affecting its functional properties. Notably, ultrasound treatment also reduced the DE of pectins, which was consistent with the findings of Chen et al. (2021).

Molecular weight

Table 1 showed the molecular weights of the CPPs. The extraction method profoundly influenced the molecular weight profiles of the obtained pectins. Conventional water extraction (WCPP) yielded pectin with the highest molecular weight (Mw = 1.24 × 107 g/mol), while the use of acidic and NADES-based methods, particularly without ultrasound, resulted in a significant reduction in Mw. The lowest molecular weights were observed for NCPP and US-NCPP (0.12 × 107 and 0.11 × 107 g/mol, respectively), indicating that the natural deep eutectic solvent (CC:OA) was highly effective in depolymerizing the pectin chains, a process that was only slightly intensified by ultrasound assistance. Interestingly, ultrasound had a more pronounced depolymerizing effect when combined with oxalic acid, reducing the Mw from 0.90 × 107 g/mol (OCPP) to 0.47 × 107 g/mol (US-OCPP). Notably, the Mw and Mn values of OCPP and NCPP were higher than those of US-OCPP and US-NCPP, respectively. A similar result was reported by Chen, Qi, Zhu, and Wang (2019). This trend is further corroborated by the polydispersity index (Mw/Mn), where US-NCPP exhibited an extremely high value of 70, suggesting a very heterogeneous distribution of chain lengths, likely due to non-random cleavage of the pectin backbone during the combined ultrasound-NADES treatment. In summary, the molecular weight decreased in the order of WCPP > OCPP > US-OCPP > NCPP ≈ US-NCPP, clearly demonstrating that solvent choice and the application of ultrasound are critical factors controlling the extent of pectin depolymerization.

FTIR analysis

The FTIR spectra of the CPPs are shown in Fig. 1 A. The spectra of the CPPs were similar and contained almost the same functional groups and structures. The absorption peaks could be observed at 3700–2800 cm−1 and 1800–1600 cm−1 for all the CPPs. The stretching vibration of O—H produced a broad peak near 3404 cm−1, whereas the stretching vibration of C—H produced a modest absorption peak near 2929 cm−1. These two peaks are thought to represent typical pectin peaks, and the peak areas at approximately 1745 cm−1 and 1626 cm−1 allowed for the calculation of the DE values (Pappas, 2004). The peak area at 1626 cm−1 was markedly weaker than that at 1740 cm−1 for the CPPs. Therefore, all the pectin samples contained high methoxyl pectin. These structural differences highlighted by FTIR are crucial as they directly relate to the functional properties of pectin, such as gelation capacity and emulsifying activity, which in turn influence its potential application in food coatings.

XRD analysis

Based on the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of cranberry pomace pectin (CPPs) presented in Fig. 1B, the broad and diffuse halo observed in the patterns, typically centered around 20°(2θ), is characteristic of the amorphous nature of pectic polysaccharides. All the CPPs presented a diffraction peak at approximately 22° (Fig. 1B). This was attributed to the partial crystallization of the pectin structure, which indicated the amorphous or semicrystalline behavior of the pectin. Similar results were reported by Rahmani, Khodaiyan, Kazemi, and Sharifan (2020).

The differences in the intensity and broadness of this amorphous halo among the samples extracted by different methods suggest variations in the degree of structural disorder and possibly the level of intermolecular interactions.

The lack of sharp, crystalline peaks across all methods confirms the absence of a long-range ordered structure, which is consistent with the typical structure of pectin. However, the observable differences in the amorphous background highlight how the choice of extraction technique can modify the short-range molecular order and the physical state of the extracted CPPs, which may subsequently affect their functional properties, such as solubility, gel-forming ability, and performance as edible coatings as investigated in later figures.

1H NMR analysis

The effect of different extraction methods on the 1H NMR spectra of CPP was shown in Fig. 1C, the spectral differences observed among the samples extracted by different methods reflect significant variations in their molecular structure and composition (Ezzati, Ayaseh, Ghanbarzadeh, & Heshmati, 2020). The region between 3.0 and 5.5 ppm is characteristic of the proton signals from the sugar ring systems of pectin polysaccharides. Heterohead proton signal peaks were observed in both the δ 4.90–5.60 and δ 4.30–4.90 chemical shift intervals, indicating the presence of both α- and β-configuration sugar residues in cranberry pomace pectin. The strong signal appearing at 3.74 ppm was attributed to methoxy (-OCH3) bound to the galacturonic acid carboxyl group, while the proton peak appearing near δ 1.24 ppm was derived from the C-6 methyl proton signal of L-rhamnose (W. Wang et al., 2016). Near 2.0 ppm, the two signals were from acetyl groups (-COCH3) bound on 2-O- and 3-O-GalA, which were weak in the cranberry pomace pectin samples due to their low degree of acetylation. Proton signals for non-esterified galacturonic acid residues such as H-1 (5.02 ppm) and H-5 (4.90 ppm) were observed near 5.0 ppm (Asgari, Labbafi, Khodaiyan, Kazemi, & Hosseini, 2020). The presence of a signal peak at 3.30 ppm indicates the presence of unsubstituted rhamnose residues in the pectin, which was in agreement with that described by Renard, Crépeau, and Thibault (1995). These structural differences revealed by1H NMR are critical, as the degree of esterification and acetylation directly affects the gelation mechanism, solubility, and ultimately the functional performance of CPPs in applications such as the edible coatings demonstrated in subsequent storage studies.

SEM analysis

SEM was used to examine the surface morphology of the CPPs at two different magnifications (500× and 5000×). As shown in Fig. 2, the morphology of the CPPs differed significantly. At 500× magnification, the surface structure of WCPP had many holes and depressions, most likely because water extraction hindered the aggregation of pectin chains. The OCPPs presented a tight and uneven surface morphology and a thick lamella. In contrast, NCPP had a loose, uniform, thin lamellar structure with slight breakage. Compared with those extracted without ultrasound assistance, US-OCPP and US-NCPP presented different structural features, indicating that ultrasound treatment could affect the morphology of pectins, which was consistent with the findings of a previous study (X. Chen et al., 2019). US-OCPPs presented leaf-like fragments with wrinkled and porous surfaces. US-NCPP showed filamentous structures, possibly because ultrasonic cavitation disrupted the cross-linking between pectin and other molecular substrates, causing the gradual fragmentation of pectin spheres, leading to the extension and aggregation of molecular chains to form filaments, which then twisted and aggregated with each other to form a network structure (Kang, Hua, Yang, Chen, & Yang, 2015).

The SEM morphological properties of CPPs (500 × and 5000×).

At a greater magnification (5000×), OCPP resulted in a smoother surface with some round pores of different sizes. Furthermore, the surface morphologies of NCPP, US-OCPP, and US-NCPP had particles of different sizes and shapes. The solubility of pectin may be correlated with the size distribution of the particles on its surface (Liew, Ngoh, Yusoff, & Teoh, 2016). NCPP and US-NCPP had smaller particle distributions than did US-OCPP, which may favor their solubility.

Rheological properties

Fig. 1D showed the relationship between the shear rate and apparent viscosity of CPPs prepared as 2 % (w/v) solutions. With increased shear rates (0.1–1000 s−1), the apparent viscosities of the five pectin solutions decreased, especially in the lower shear rate range (< 300 s−1), indicating their typical shear-thinning behavior and non-Newtonian pseudoplastic fluid characteristics (Liew et al., 2016). The shear thinning behavior was due to the entanglement of pectin macromolecular chains, and with increasing shear rate, the intermolecular entanglement decreased, and the flow resistance weakened, leading to a decrease in the apparent viscosity of the pectin solutions (Z. Li et al., 2022). For all the CPPs, the apparent viscosity of WCPP was the highest (4.87 Pa·s), and that of US-NCPP was the lowest (0.33 Pa·s) when the shear rate was 0 s−1. Yuliarti et al. (2015) reported that water-extracted gold kiwifruit pectin also had the highest viscosity compared with enzyme and acid extracts. With increasing shear rate, WCPP and OCPP decreased rapidly and exhibited significant shear thinning behavior. The apparent viscosities of the pectin samples gradually converged when the shear rate exceeded 500 s-1. NCPP and US-NCPP had strong shear resistance. This finding was in agreement with that of S. Chen et al. (2022), who reported that mango peel pectins extracted with natural deep eutectic solvents were less viscous and more stable than pectins extracted with hydrochloric acid. This may be related to the ability of natural deep eutectic solvents to hinder the cross-linking of biomolecules. Notably, pectin solutions without ultrasound-assisted extraction had higher viscosity and more pronounced shear thinning behavior than those with ultrasound-assisted extraction did, especially for oxalic acid-extracted pectin. The molecular weight is related to the apparent viscosity of the pectin solution (Cui et al., 2021). The CPPs were compared in terms of molecular weight and apparent viscosity, and it was shown that the higher the molecular weight is, the greater the apparent viscosity. Therefore, the ultrasound-assisted treatment reduced the viscosity of the pectin solutions, probably because the low molecular weight reduced the intermolecular entanglement and interaction of pectin.

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant capacity of the CPPs was determined by preparing different concentrations of pectin solutions and measuring the ability of the pectin solutions to scavenge ABTS and DPPH radicals. As shown in Fig. 3, the antioxidant activity of the CPPs increased when the concentration of ABTS in the pectin solution ranged from 0.2 to 1.6 mg/mL (Fig.3 A) and the concentration of DPPH ranged from 0.1 to 0.6 mg/mL (Fig.3B), indicating concentration-dependent characteristics.

(A) Scavenging activities of CPPs on ABTS radical. (B) Scavenging activities of CPPs on DPPH radical. (C) The thermal properties of CPPs. Different letters above the bars indicate significant differences (p < 0.05) among the different extraction methods for each concentration, as determined by ANOVA.

The rate of ABTS radical scavenging over the measured concentration range is shown in Fig. 3 A. NCPP and US-NCPP had a greater ability to scavenge ABTS radicals than the other three pectin solutions did. The IC50 values for WCPP, OCPP, NCPP, US-OCPP, and US-NCPP were 0.98, 0.82, 0.66, 0.70, and 0.55, respectively. DPPH (Fig. 3B) also showed a similar trend to that of ABTS, with IC50 values of 0.29, 0.28, 0.24, 0.26, and 0.21, respectively, indicating a positive effect of CC-OA solvent on the ABTS and DPPH clearance of cranberry pomace pectin. The extraction methods lead to differences in total phenol content, which in turn result in differences in antioxidant activity. This phenomenon supports the fact that the antioxidant activity exhibited by pectin extracted from cranberry residue is related to its co-extracted and closely bound phenolic substances. Moreover, the antioxidant activity of pectins is related to their molecular weight and GalA content, which has been confirmed by numerous studies (Dengyue Sun & Zhu, 2020; Liu et al., 2022). Moreover, the molecular weight of pectin was negatively correlated with its antioxidant activity, whereas the GalA content was positively correlated. The lower Mw and higher GalA content of pectin could promote the release of hydroxyl groups to increase the antioxidant capacity. NCPP and US-NCPP had lower molecular weights and higher GalA and total phenol contents, which may be the reason for their better antioxidant activity. In addition, ultrasound-assisted treatment increased the ability of pectin to scavenge ABTS and DPPH radicals, resulting in higher antioxidant activity in US-OCPP and US-NCPP than in OCPP and NCPP, respectively. The relatively higher antioxidant activity of these samples may also be related to their Mw, GalA and total phenol content.

Thermal properties

The thermal properties of the CPPs were analyzed via differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) from 25 °C to 300 °C. As shown in Fig. 3C, all these CPPs presented endothermic and exothermic peaks. Water loss, hydrogen bonding between galacturonic acid units, and conformational changes in the GalA ring may have been responsible for the endothermic peaks that were visible near 170 °C (Chen, Liu, Zhang, Jiang, & Li, 2022). The exothermic peaks appeared near 240 °C, indicating the onset of thermal degradation. The maximum exothermic peaks were observed at 250.53 °C, 252.54 °C, 236.86 °C, 249.15 °C and 244.39 °C for WCPP, OCPP, NCPP, US-OCPP and US-NCPP, respectively. Among all the pectin samples, WCPP, OCPP, and US-OCPP presented greater thermal stability. Moreover, according to the thermal degradation temperature of commercial pectins mentioned by Dranca, Vargas, and Oroian (2020), all the pectin samples except NCPP had higher degradation temperatures than did commercial citrus pectin (243 °C), which indicated their potential for thermal processing. On the other hand, NCPP exhibited a narrower exothermic peak than the other pectin samples did, which indicated an ordered molecular arrangement and tight molecular weight distribution (Deng et al., 2020).

Effects of US-NCPP films on the storage quality of peach fruit

Fruit firmness is the main physical index that affects consumer acceptance. Peaches rapidly lose their firmness during the aging process, which is an important reason for their short postharvest life and susceptibility to fungal contamination. The softening of fruit is related to the degradation of the cell wall caused by water loss, the respiration of fruit and the destruction of the tissue structure by the mold (Gull et al., 2021). As shown in Fig. 4 A, the firmness of the peaches tended to decrease during storage. On the 8th day of storage, the firmness of peach fruits in the control group and US-NCPP group were 3.45 N and 4.69 N, respectively, decreased by 7.67 N, 6.53 N and 4.35 N, respectively. Compared with the control group, the US-NCPP experimental group significantly inhibited the decrease in peach firmness. These results indicated that the cranberry pomace pectin-based film could significantly improve the firmness loss of peaches during storage and effectively slow the softening process of peaches.

Firmness (A), TSS (B), Weight loss (C) values and Respiration intensity (D) of peach coated with extracted cranberry pomace pectin during storage. Different letters indicate significant differences (p < 0.05) at different storage times or among different treatment groups.

The total soluble solid content refers to the total sugar content of soluble metabolites, which mainly includes glucose, fructose and sucrose. In general, owing to fruit ripening and aging, metabolic processes continue during postharvest storage, converting starches and organic acids in the fruit into sugars for metabolic processes, and the total soluble solid content of the postharvest fruit gradually increases during ripening, leading to increased total soluble solid content. On the other hand, the continuous consumption of nutrients as breath continues leads to its gradual reduction (Pan et al., 2019). As shown in Fig. 4B, during the storage period, the total soluble solid content of the peaches in both the control group and the experimental group first increased but then decreased. On the 4th day of storage, the content of soluble solids in the control group reached the highest value (12.76 %), whereas in the US-NCPP group, the content of total soluble solids reached the highest value (12.98 %) on the 6th day of storage, which slowed the accumulation rate of soluble solids during the ripening process of the peach fruit. On the 8th day of storage, the total soluble solids content of the US-NCPP group was 12.22 %, which was significantly greater than that of the control group (11.14 %), which better maintained the total soluble solids content of the peaches during storage.

The weight loss of fruit after harvest is caused mainly by transpiration and respiration. Peach skin is thin and can easily lose water, resulting in rapid deterioration. As shown in Fig. 4C, the weight loss rate of the peaches in each group tended to increase during storage. This is mainly because respiration caused the loss of water and dry matter in peaches. On the 8th day of storage, the weight loss rate of the peaches in the US-NCPP group (28.00 %) was significantly lower than that in the control group (32.46 %). Compared with that of the control group, the weight loss rate of the peaches in the US-NCPP group after storage was reduced by 4.46 %, indicating that the pectin-based film of cranberry pomace acted as a barrier, limiting the speed of respiration and water transfer. This maintained the stability of the internal environment of the peaches.

The respiration intensity of fruits and vegetables is closely related to their storability. The higher the respiration intensity of fruits and vegetables is, the weaker their storage. As shown in Fig. 4D, the respiration intensity of the peaches in both the control group and the experimental group tended to first increase but then decrease during storage. The respiratory rate of fruits increased sharply at the beginning, peaked on the 4th day of storage, and then decreased rapidly until the end of storage, indicating a typical postharvest respiratory rate change trend. Compared with those in the control group, the respiration rates of the peaches in the US-NCPP experimental group were lower (P < 0.05). On the 8th day of storage, the respiration intensities of the control group and US-NCPP group were 46.03 mg/(kg·h) and 39.80 mg/(kg·h), respectively. The US-NCPP effectively inhibited the increase in the respiration rate during peach fruit storage and reduced the gas exchange between the peaches and the surrounding atmosphere, thus slowing the ripening process of the peaches.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) is one of the main byproducts of lipid peroxidation in cell membranes and is often used as an index to reflect the degree of lipid oxidation in cell membranes. The accumulation of malondialdehyde in fruits and vegetables during storage severely affects their storage life and quality. As shown in Fig. 5 A, the content of malondialdehyde in the peaches of both the control group and the experimental group tended to increase during storage, which may have been due to the increase in the generation of oxygen free radicals, resulting in an increase in the malondialdehyde content of the membrane lipid peroxidation products. On the 8th day of storage, the malondialdehyde contents of the peaches in the control group and US-NCPP group were 2.43 μmol/g and 1.38 μmol/g, respectively. The content of malondialdehyde in the control group was significantly greater than that in the US-NCPP group. These findings indicated that the membrane treatment effectively delayed the increase in the malondialdehyde content during peach storage.

The Malondialdehyde (A) and Polyphenol oxidase activity (B) values of peach coated with extracted cranberry pomace pectin during storage. Different letters indicate significant differences (p < 0.05) at different storage times or among different treatment groups.

The oxidation of phenolic substrates catalyzed by polyphenol oxidase is one of the important reasons for the postharvest browning of fruits and vegetables. The degree of surface browning of peach fruit is related to the activity of polyphenol oxidase(Li et al., 2019). As shown in Fig. 5B, the activity of polyphenol oxidase in the peaches in the control group and experimental group gradually increased with increasing storage time. At the end of storage, the activities of polyphenol oxidase in the control group and US-NCPP group were 298.71 U/g and 273.21 U/g, respectively. Compared with the activity of polyphenol oxidase in peaches stored on day 0, after 8 days of storage, the activity of polyphenol oxidase in peaches in the control group increased by 32.64 %, whereas that in the US-NCPP group increased by 21.31 %. Compared with those in the control group, the activity of polyphenol oxidase in peach fruits was significantly lower in the US-NCPP group, thereby reducing surface browning caused by the increase in the activity of polyphenol oxidase.

The changes of peaches in the control group and the US-NCPP treated group during storage were shown in Fig. 6. On the 4th day of storage, peaches in the control group began to rot, while those in the US-NCPP group remained intact. On the 8th day, due to water loss, the surface of peaches in the US-NCPP group crumpled and began to deteriorate. The results showed that US-NCPP based film had remarkable effect on peach preservation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that the extraction method significantly influences the properties of cranberry pomace pectins (CPPs). The ultrasound-assisted natural deep eutectic solvent (US-NADES) method proved most effective, yielding a high-methoxyl pectin (US-NCPP) with superior galacturonic acid content and antioxidant activity. When applied as an edible coating, US-NCPP effectively maintained peach quality and extended the shelf life at room temperature from 4 to 8 days by mitigating water loss, softening, and oxidative deterioration. These findings highlight the potential of cranberry pomace as a valuable source of functional pectin for food preservation applications. Future studies should focus on scaling up the extraction process and evaluating the economic feasibility and safety of NADES-extracted pectins for commercial applications.
