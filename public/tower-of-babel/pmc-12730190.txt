Impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on influenza vaccination coverage in Latin America: Southern Cone, Argentina, Chile, and Uruguay
Authors: Pablo Bonvehi, Ruby Trejo Varon, Carlos Espinal, Helena Brenes Chacon, Francisco Becerra-Posada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective.Analyze the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on influenza vaccination coverage in some countries in the Americas.Methods.This descriptive ecological study included Argentina, Chile, and Uruguay and analyzed information on influenza vaccination coverage in the countries during 2018–2023. The study reports annual estimates of influenza vaccination coverage expressed as a percentage of the population. The sources used were those reported by the Pan American Health Organization/World Health Organization and the ministries of health of the countries studied.Results.Comparatively, vaccination coverage rates according to risk groups among Argentina, Chile, and Uruguay show a downward trend without reaching optimal vaccination coverage for 2018 to 2023. Increased vaccination rates were reported in 2020 for all age and risk groups, except in Uruguay for children under 5 years of age. Among pregnant women, Argentina reported higher coverage in 2020 with 77%, Chile 90% (2018), and Uruguay 55% (2020). Among health workers, all three countries reached their highest coverage in 2020 (100%) but reported a decrease for 2022.Conclusion.The studied countries have observed a decrease in post-COVID-19 influenza vaccination coverage. Therefore, they are working to achieve optimal coverage, not only in influenza risk groups but across the entire population. Data on influenza vaccination coverage are limited in some cases. The availability of new vaccines can provide greater immunogenicity, efficacy, and effectiveness. However, they represent a challenge for the Expanded Program on Immunization, which requires cost-effectiveness studies to evaluate new vaccines while experiencing restricted national budgets.

Influenza vaccination is a challenge for many reasons: annual vaccine program implementation, identification of the most suitable time for implementation, definition of high-risk groups, availability of vaccines at the appropriate time, and access to the correct type of vaccine, among others. The accessibility to and the introduction of new vaccines or vaccines with higher immunogenicity using preexisting or new platforms that could provide better protection also represents a challenge for national immunization programs.

Data gathering for influenza vaccination before and during the COVID-19 pandemic has been complex, and in some cases, unavailable. Estimation of vaccine coverage among different groups is also a challenge, especially when the target population is not well known. For example, some countries do not have certain data of the number of patients with underlying conditions to estimate adequate coverage in these groups. In pediatrics, this analysis often is less complex since most countries have accurate estimated data of different age cohorts. However, a challenge exists related to the lack of a registry of administered vaccine doses in older children and in those that receive primary vaccination at an older age (1).

The COVID-19 pandemic had an important impact on vaccination programs, including for the seasonal influenza vaccine. In Argentina, Chile, and Uruguay, an increase in influenza vaccine administered doses was observed as a result of several factors: lack of the COVID-19 vaccine at the beginning of the pandemic; a potential cross-protection for SARS-CoV-2 infection with the influenza vaccine; and the avoidance of creating an additional burden on health systems by preventing influenza-associated cases, medical consultations, hospitalizations, and deaths (2).

The pandemic also disrupted the traditional seasonality of influenza virus circulation. In Argentina, for example, an unusual surge of influenza A (H3N2) occurred in early 2022 during the summer months (3), followed by reduced activity in winter and sustained influenza B circulation throughout the year. Such shifts may have reduced the effectiveness of influenza vaccination in the region and globally.

Nevertheless, it is known that a decrease in coverage was observed in 2020 and 2021, with a recovery in Argentina and Uruguay during 2022. Changes in the number of influenza cases could also be affected by other external factors, including the isolation of infected people and contacts, reduced collective activities, the restriction of group activities, and migratory controls among populations. Annual data regarding the circulation of influenza before and during the pandemic, and during the pandemic, were closely followed. We see that as a good practice to be continued (4).

Changes in the influenza virus circulation were reflected in the burden of disease, with lower reported rates of hospitalizations and mortality. An interaction between the influenza virus, influenza vaccine, and COVID-19 has been reported, with an apparent association between the lower risk of severe COVID-19 and influenza vaccination. A 10% increase in influenza vaccine coverage was associated with a 28% decrease in mortality associated with COVID-19 in the elderly. A study of 92 664 COVID-19 patients documented an 8% reduction in intensive care unit admissions, an 18% reduction in ventilatory assistance, and a 17% reduction in mortality in those vaccinated against influenza (5). A potential immune modulation role is suspected.

CHALLENGES OF INFLUENZA VACCINATION IN THE REGION

Influenza vaccine introduction in South America and around the world has been demonstrated to be effective in reducing morbidity and mortality in high-risk groups. Vaccination coverage changed over the study period, with a progressive increase before 2020, a considerable decrease by 2021, and an apparent recovery by 2022 and 2023 (6). Nevertheless, data in the region are scarce.

The region faces several limitations to accomplish adequate vaccine coverage. Different health policies could become barriers for universal vaccination. Health care personnel and patient perception of disease can impact vaccine acceptance and a campaign’s success. The goals to achieve better coverage include improvements in vaccine access and availability, incorporation of influenza vaccine to regular immunization programs, availability of better registration, and inclusion of new risk groups among immunization strategies.

There are several differences among influenza vaccination programs in South America. Although the defined high-risk groups for influenza vaccination are similar among countries, they may differ. Also, given the specific policies for each country, there are several differences in implementation strategies and coverage rates.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This descriptive ecological study seeks to analyze the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on influenza vaccination coverage in Latin America, specifically in Argentina, Chile, and Uruguay. The information analyzed corresponded to administrative coverage of influenza vaccination in the study countries during the years 2018 to 2023. Coverage rates are expressed as a percentage of the corresponding population. Estimates of influenza vaccination coverage are for annual vaccination programs. These sources corresponded to those officially reported by the Pan American Health Organization/World Health Organization (PAHO/WHO) and the ministries of health of the countries that are part of the research.

Vaccination coverage estimates were calculated using the administrative data on doses administered, obtained from the ministry of health, and population denominators based on national census projections, as reported by PAHO/WHO. These estimates may differ slightly from those presented in the ministry of health annual campaign reports, which use population denominators from internal health services. Small discrepancies between the tables reflect the use of these different population bases.

RESULTS

Chile

In Chile, each year since 1982, the immunization department of the ministry of health organized an influenza vaccination campaign with regional governments, health services, and health providers. The campaign starts in March and reaches coverage close to 85% on high-risk groups by the end of the year (7).

Due to demographic and epidemiological changes and scientific advances on the safety and immunogenicity of influenza vaccination, the number of doses in the country is modified annually. High-risk populations and vaccination objectives are dynamic, and coverage may be impacted, as shown in Figure 1 (7, 8).

Uruguay

In Uruguay, the influenza vaccination program has been developed in different stages to cover all groups of interest. For 2023, high-risk groups in Uruguay include the following: adults older than 65 years of age, residents and workers in long-term care facilities, pregnant women and mothers to infants younger than 6 months of age, inmates, refugees, health care workers, and poultry farmers. In the second stage of vaccination, children younger than 5 years old, adolescents, and adults with comorbidities are included. In the final stage of vaccination, the general population is allowed to be vaccinated, even if they do not belong to the vaccination priority groups (13). For this period, a trivalent vaccine is used with the formulation recommended by the WHO.

Vaccine coverage in Uruguay has changed through time (14). The vaccination rates between 2018 and 2023 are shown in Table 2. In 2014, vaccine coverage for children younger than 2 years was 47%, 82% in those between 2 and 5 years of age, 30% for adults, and 46% for health care providers (1, 10, 15). Data for patients with underlying conditions are not available for the period.

Argentina

Vaccination against influenza started in Argentina in 1993 and was consolidated by 2010 after the H1N1 influenza pandemic through the national vaccination campaign, using mono- and trivalent formulations. By 2011, the influenza vaccine was added to the national vaccination program, with the objective to decrease hospitalizations, complications, sequelae, and mortality.

The targeted population for 2023 includes health care workers, pregnant women and those in the first 10 days after pregnancy, infants between 6 and 24 months of age, adults with underlying conditions (chronic lung diseases, cardiac disease, immunodeficiencies, oncologic and hematologic diseases, obesity, diabetes, chronic renal disease, neuromuscular diseases, severe congenital malformations, and people living with high-risk patients), and the elderly. Argentina uses a trivalent formulation, as recommended by WHO.

Vaccine coverage in Argentina has also changed over time, and pre- and post-pandemic coverages have evolved. In 2010, coverages over 85% were reported in all risk groups, reaching 99% in health personnel and children and adults with comorbidities. An increased tendency is observed in coverage reports between 2011 and 2023 in most risk groups according to data provided by the ministry of health. In 2014, 50% coverage was reported in children younger than 2 years of age, in contrast with 75% by 2019. By 2014, 90% coverage was reported for the elderly, 84% for health care workers, 35% for patients with comorbidities, and 96% for women during pregnancy (1, 15).

Variation in the vaccination coverage is evident. In Argentina, a documented false perception of the influenza burden of disease has spread over the years, making it difficult to improve the vaccine coverage in different risk groups (Table 2) (16).

Comparatively, vaccination coverage according to risk groups in Argentina, Chile, and Uruguay from 2018 to 2023 shows a decreasing trend without achieving optimal vaccination coverage. In Argentina, in 2018, coverage for children under 5 years of age decreased from 70% in 2018 to 39% in 2022 and 63% in 2023. For the same study period, in children under 5 years of age, vaccination coverage in Chile decreased from 71% in 2018 to 69% in 2022 and 70% in 2023. Finally, in Uruguay, vaccination coverage decreased from 24% in 2018 to 2% in 2022 and 20% in 2023. For older persons, Argentina reported 103% coverage in 2018, 76% in 2022, and 12% in 2023. Chile showed appropriate coverage in this group in 2020 with 85% coverage, 70% coverage in 2022, and 65% coverage in 2023. Uruguay showed a low coverage of 32% for this group in 2018 and 33% in 2022 and 2023. We note an increase in vaccination rates in 2020 for all age groups and risk groups, except in Uruguay in children under 5 years of age (7%). For the pregnant women group, Argentina reported greater coverage in 2020, with 77%, Chile reported 90% coverage in 2018, and Uruguay reported 55% coverage in 2020. In the health care workers group, the three countries reached their highest coverage in 2020 with over 100% coverage reported, and they reported a decrease by 2022, with the sharpest decrease in Uruguay, which went from 136% in 2020 to 22% in 2022 and 19% in 2023 (Table 2). High vaccination coverage is evident in health workers. The results show that it is important to strengthen actions to achieve an increase in coverage, especially in the at-risk population.

Between 2018 and 2023, Argentina, Chile, and Uruguay used similar influenza vaccines based on the WHO recommendations for the Southern Hemisphere. All three countries employed inactivated vaccines formulated in embryonated eggs, containing influenza A(H1N1)pdm09, A(H3N2), and B (Victoria and/or Yamagata lineages) strains. Differences among countries were mostly related to the formulation type and vaccine supplier, rather than antigenic composition: trivalent vaccines were used in 2018, while quadrivalent vaccines were used from 2019 through 2023 (17).

DISCUSSION

Argentina, Chile, and Uruguay report that they have a current influenza vaccination policy in their countries, which includes risk groups (18). Regular efforts to improve influenza vaccination coverage in Southern Cone countries are underway. Argentina, Chile, and Uruguay are undertaking efforts to improve vaccination with the objective to increase vaccine coverage. Access to vaccines, vaccine availability for high-risk groups, broader campaigns, and a more precise registry are necessary to accomplish this goal.

Chile, in its quest to strengthen actions to protect the population and as a result of the increase in influenza cases so far in 2024, as well as the viral circulation in unusual times, expanded vaccine coverage to the entire population beginning August 1, 2024 (19). The possibility of continuing to use or introduce vaccines with higher immunogenicity is important in vulnerable populations. For this purpose, cost-effective studies and the logistics for implementation should be considered.

Because the influenza vaccine remains in high demand, it is hard to predict the number of doses to be used. The demand varies depending on many circumstances, and planning starts one year before the campaign begins. The reports show the doses that were applied; hence, we can see the variability in the different target groups each country has defined.

Educational campaigns and improvements in the participation of certain groups of medical specialists to reach targeted populations (i.e., obstetricians, cardiologists, pneumologists, and geriatricians) might contribute to better coverage.

Starting in 2022, six countries in the region, including Chile and Uruguay, have been collecting and analyzing surveillance and immunization data on severe acute respiratory infection (SARI) to improve the effectiveness of influenza and COVID-19 vaccines against hospitalizations (18).

Some reported influenza vaccination coverage rates exceeded 100% for certain risk groups and years. According to PAHO 2021 methodological guidelines and published literature, such figures may arise from the underestimation of target population denominators, vaccine availability, vaccination of individuals outside the reported jurisdiction, or duplication in administrative records rather than true over-coverage. These values highlight limitations in the accuracy of administrative data and should be interpreted with caution when comparing coverage trends across years or between countries (19).

The 2030 Immunization Agenda, which was adopted by all countries, refers to its mission as “a global strategy to leave no one behind.” Among its objectives, the agenda has to increase equitable access to new and existing vaccines and their use, as well as the contribution to universal coverage and sustainable development. Therefore, governments must unite efforts and continue to work together on vaccination campaigns adapted to the community to meet the goals in vaccination coverage (20).

National influenza vaccination programs in Argentina, Chile, and Uruguay use registry systems to monitor coverage and dose traceability. Argentina’s Federal Nominal Vaccination Registry (NOMIVAC) and Chile’s National Immunization Registry (RNI) are interoperable electronic systems with nominal records, while Uruguay employs an Information System for Vaccines (SIV) complemented by on-site documentation. These registries are key tools to ensure data quality and to support surveillance and evaluation of seasonal influenza campaigns across the region.

The observed differences in coverage between countries may also reflect variations in vaccination registration and reporting systems.

Although this study describes changes in influenza vaccination coverage before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic, no statistical test was applied to assess whether the observed variations represented significant differences relative to historical trends. The analysis was designed as a descriptive ecological study using secondary administrative data from PAHO/WHO and national health authorities. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as indicative of trends rather than inferential conclusions.

CONCLUSION

The influenza vaccine is the most effective way to prevent the disease and the resulting complications, especially in high-risk groups. Although the countries studied have incorporated the flu vaccine into their vaccination schedules and worked to continue vaccination services during the COVID-19 pandemic [including Argentina and Chile, which have incorporated longer “winter campaigns” lasting more than 3 months in order to strengthen actions (18)], suboptimal vaccination coverage is evident. Therefore, a review of actions is suggested to improve the vaccination coverage.

Constant monitoring of vaccination coverage and the identification of factors related to nonvaccination or reluctance allow efforts to be directed toward promoting specific actions and ensuring that the population is vaccinated, helping to reduce morbidity and mortality caused by this disease. The risk groups established (such as pregnant women, older adults, children under 5 years of age, and patients with chronic diseases), given the probability of developing the disease in a serious manner or death, had fluctuating coverage in all countries and all were below optimal coverage. Uruguay, in particular, reported low coverage percentages for all groups.

A comprehensive analysis on morbidity and mortality associated with influenza and COVID-19 in the Region of the Americas (2010 to 2023), carried out by PAHO (21), preliminarily showed that 70% of the region’s population reported about 350 000 influenza-related hospitalizations annually. The results indicate approximately 55 million cases of mild influenza illness per year in the Americas, urging governments to improve vaccination coverage and reduce the burden of this disease in the region.

The influenza vaccine is a biological medicine that is necessary throughout the life cycle. Immunizing the mother during pregnancy provides transplacental protection up to 6 months; from 6 months onward, the baby can receive the vaccine on its regular schedule; after 5 years and in adulthood, although it is recommended for risk groups, it can be applied at all ages, offering immunity through annual reapplication.

Since 2020, COVID-19 surveillance has been gradually integrated into ongoing surveillance of SARI and influenza-like illnesses (ILI), allowing for comprehensive analysis and monitoring of the behavior of influenza and influenza-like viruses (22).

According to a regional report (23), the activities of ILI and severe SARI have shown a general downward trend. Influenza activity has remained below the epidemic threshold in all countries. During this period, according to the report, the predominant influenza viruses have been type A(H3N2), followed by A(H1N1)pdm09 and B/Victoria. Influenza activity in the study countries has shown that Argentina is at epidemic levels, showing a slight increase in the last two epidemiological weeks. In Chile, influenza remains below the epidemic threshold; however, it has shown a slight increase in recent years. In Uruguay, influenza and SARS-CoV-2 activity have remained at low levels.

It is important to work together to develop electronic vaccination records and digital certificates, which can facilitate population monitoring and the strengthening of joint actions towards improving vaccination coverage. Estimations of updated population denominators are needed to adequately assess coverage by the countries’ ministries of health.
