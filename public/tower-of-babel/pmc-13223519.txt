Surgical management of thoracic impalement injuries in sub-Saharan Africa: A three-case series from Benin
Authors: Abdel Kémal Bori Bata, Ahmad Ibrahim, Mahugnon Emmanuel Martino Gbessi, Désiré Nékoua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thoracic impalement injuries remain rare but dramatic, and represent a major challenge in low-resource settings. We report the successful surgical management of three male patients, aged 22 to 56 years, treated managed at our tertiary center in Benin between June 2022 and March 2023. Two cases involved isolated thoracic impalement, and one involved a thoracoabdominal injury. This case series illustrates the complexity of managing these traumas in the sub-Saharan context and underscores the pressing need to improve technical infrastructure and improved access to emergency thoracic care.

Introduction

Impalement injuries occur when a large foreign object penetrates a body cavity or limb and remains in situ [1]. Thoracic impalement is rare in civilian settings and is associated with high mortality due to frequent multivisceral involvement [2], [3]. We present three cases successfully managed at our institution between June 2022 and March 2023, to highlight the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of such injuries in a sub-Saharan, resource-limited environment.

Case presentations

Case 1

A 56-year-old right-handed male with no known medical history was admitted for a penetrating injury to the right hemithorax following a motorcycle collision involving impalement by a bundle of wood. The foreign object had been removed at the scene. He presented two hour post-trauma in a pre-shock state: blood pressure 96/52 mmHg, heart rate 115 bpm, respiratory rate 28 bpm, oxygen saturation 93% on room air.

Local examination revealed a non-sucking oval wound (10 cm long) in the right deltopectoral groove (Fig. 1A–B). The right hemithorax showed diffuse subcutaneous emphysema, rib fractures (ribs 2–4), and clinical signs of hemothorax. Neurological and systemic examinations were unremarkable. Initial management included vascular resuscitation and a compressive hemostatic dressing, which stabilized the patient hemodynamically.Fig. 1A. Chest X-ray showed subcutaneous emphysema, bifocal fractures of ribs 2–4 on the right, and a moderate right-sided hemothorax. Surgical exploration was delayed 24 h due to financial constraints and revealed right pectoral muscle laceration, transection of a collateral branch of the lateral thoracic artery, a 5 cm pleural breach, pulmonary contusion of the right upper lobe, and a 750 mL hemothorax (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Intraoperative view: blood and air bubbles emerging from the pleural dome.Fig. 2

Management included thoracic drainage, hemostatic repair, and layered chest wall closure. Postoperative recovery was favorable, despite transient oxygen requirement. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 20 with a prescription for pulmonary rehabilitation. At 30 months of follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with complete functional recovery. He had resumed his normal daily activities without respiratory limitation or chronic pain. Clinical examination was unremarkable, and no late complications such as chronic infection, chest wall instability, or respiratory dysfunction were observed.

Case 2

A 50-year-old male with no prior medical history was admitted for right thoracic impalement following a motorcycle crash into a stationary truck transporting teak branches. A 4 cm diameter branch had embedded into his right hemithorax and broken 40 cm proximal to the entry point.

He presented with hemodynamic stability. Examination showed an 8 cm penetrating wound above the right nipple. The object's trajectory was oblique, forming a 45° angle, with the proximal end palpable in the right laterocervical region (Fig. 3). Upper right limb neurovascular function was intact. Chest X-ray (Fig. 4) revealed extrapleural penetration without pneumothorax or rib fracture. Surgery was performed 48 hour post-trauma. A descending oblique incision was made along the subclavian axis. A double osteotomy of the right clavicle allowed mobilization of the middle segment of the clavicle, exposing the neurovascular structures. Brachial plexus branches were visualized and stretched anteriorly to the foreign body. Neurolysis was performed to protect the nerves and facilitate exposure of the teak branch. Extraction required multiple technical maneuvers, including weakening the wood with a 4.5 mm drill, use of an oscillating saw, and an osteotome. The branch was removed in two segments—distal via the entry wound and proximal via the surgical approach. A subclavian vein laceration was repaired. Clavicular stabilization was achieved using a Kirschner wire.

Postoperatively, the patient developed venous congestion in the right hand and partial brachial plexus injury, resulting in monoparesis (muscle strength 2/5) with paresthesia. He was managed with physiotherapy and symptomatic treatment (Fig. 6). At 15 months of follow-up, the patient showed marked neurological and functional improvement. The initial monoparesis of the right upper limb had significantly regressed following rehabilitation, with recovery of muscle strength to near-normal levels. Case 3

A 22-year-old male presented with left thoracic impalement by wooden fragments following a motorcycle–tricycle collision. On admission, he was hemodynamically and respiratory stable. Local examination revealed an embedded wooden fragment in a 3 cm wound below the left nipple, associated with subcutaneous emphysema and clinical signs of left hemothorax (Fig. 7). Signs of peritoneal irritation were also noted.Fig. 7Preoperative image of left thoracoabdominal impalement.Fig. 7

Preoperative image of left thoracoabdominal impalement.

Imaging (X-ray and CT) showed pulmonary contusion, left hemopneumothorax, diaphragmatic injury, perisplenic effusion, hepatic contusion, and hemoperitoneum.

A left posterolateral thoracotomy revealed a 20 cm wooden fragment piercing the left diaphragmatic dome (Fig. 8). An oval, contused-edged diaphragmatic breach (3 cm) with omental protrusion was observed. The omentum was reduced into the abdominal cavity, and the diaphragm repaired with separate Mersuture® no.3 stitches. The pleural cavity was irrigated and drained, followed by layered chest closure.Fig. 8Left posterolateral thoracotomy revealing the foreign body in the thoracic cavity.Fig. 8

Left posterolateral thoracotomy revealing the foreign body in the thoracic cavity.

Subsequent laparotomy revealed two 2 cm wounds on the anterior gastric wall, a second posterior diaphragmatic breach, and herniated omentum in the thoracic cavity. Surgical interventions included partial omentectomy, gastric wound repair, diaphragmatic suturing, left subhepatic drainage, and meticulous hemostatic closure.

Postoperative course was complicated by pyothorax and suture dehiscence on day 10, requiring reoperation on day 16. The subsequent recovery was favorable. At 13 months of follow-up, the patient had fully recovered from the initial injuries and postoperative complications. He remained clinically stable, with no evidence of recurrent infection, diaphragmatic dysfunction, or digestive complications. The patient had returned to normal physical activity without limitation.

Discussion

Thoracic impalement injuries predominantly affect young individuals, as illustrated in our series (ages 22–56). All cases resulted from road traffic accidents—the most commonly reported mechanism in the literature [1], [4]. Impalements may be overt (with visible protrusion) or occult. All our cases involved visible impalements, facilitating prompt diagnosis and specialized surgical referral [2].

Although rare, thoracic impalement injuries occur more frequently on the right side than on the left, as the presence of the heart and great vessels in the left hemithorax often renders left-sided injuries fatal [5]. This trend was reflected in our series: two patients had right-sided injuries, and the left-sided case had no cardiac involvement.

Based on trauma dynamics, two impalement types exist: Type I, where a moving body strikes a fixed object (as in our series); and Type II, involving a mobile object penetrating a stationary body [1], [5], [6].

From a therapeutic standpoint, there is broad consensus that the impaling object should not be removed at the scene, due to its potential tamponade effect; premature removal may indeed lead to massive hemorrhage [1], [5]. In one of our cases, the object was extracted prior to hospital admission, and this patient was the only one to present with hemodynamic instability.

Imaging should not delay surgical management unless the patient is stable; contrast-enhanced CT scan aid surgical planning and limit unnecessary incisions [2], [5], [6]. This approach was adopted in our hemodynamically stable patients [2], [6]. Each impalement requires tailored management through optimal multidisciplinary coordination [6], [7].

Surgical approaches include thoracotomy or sternotomy for mediastinal involvement [2], [8]. Incisions should encompass both entry and exit points for vascular control [5]. Suspected major vascular injuries may require extracorporeal circulation support [2], [3]. Incision strategy depends on object trajectory [1]. After extraction, extensive debridement, lavage, and broad-spectrum antibiotics are essential. Closure depends on tissue loss and may require flap coverage [5].

In our series, one patient underwent thoracotomy, one deltopectoral incision, and one simple extraction with drainage. Video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) may reduce morbidity and length of stay in selected cases [2], [8].

Prognostically, thoracic impalement injuries are associated with a high mortality rate, estimated at 40% [2]. However, certain factors are predictive of a favorable outcome, including younger age, isolated pulmonary injuries, and right-sided involvement [3]. These factors may explain the absence of mortality observed in our series.

Conclusion

Thoracic impalement injuries are exceptionally rare and dramatic surgical emergencies that demand swift, coordinated, and multidisciplinary management. The fundamental principle remains unchanged: the impaling object should not be manipulated or removed outside the operating room unless absolutely necessary for life-saving purposes, such as transport or emergent cardiothoracic assessment. The successful surgical management of these cases in our setting underscores both the feasibility and the critical importance of providing urgent thoracic surgery in sub-Saharan Africa. It further advocates for strengthening surgical infrastructure and emergency technical capacity across sub-Saharan Africa.
