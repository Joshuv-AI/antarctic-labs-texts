Advancing rice breeding for drought tolerance: a comprehensive study of traditional and mutant lines through agronomic performance and drought tolerance indices
Authors: Mohammad Taher Hallajian, Ali Akbar Ebadi, Mojtaba Kordrostami
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDrought stress is a critical challenge to rice production, necessitating the development of drought-tolerant genotypes. This study aimed to evaluate the drought tolerance of rice genotypes, including traditional parental lines (Hashemi, Khazar, Fajr, and Tarom Mahalli) and their corresponding mutant lines, under normal and drought stress conditions.MethodsAgronomic traits such as plant height, spike number, spike length, seed fertility, and yield were analyzed under both conditions. The performance of these genotypes was further assessed using drought tolerance indices. Statistical methods including cluster analysis and principal component analysis (PCA) were applied to identify the most resilient genotypes.ResultsMutant lines demonstrated superior drought resilience compared to their parental counterparts. Specifically, genotypes like TM-230-VE-7-5-1, TM-B-2-1-E, and HM-250-7-6 exhibited higher yields and better stability of key traits under stress conditions. Cluster analysis and PCA emphasized the strong performance of TM-230-VE-7-5-1, which emerged as the most drought-tolerant genotype, excelling across various drought tolerance indices.ConclusionsThe selected mutant lines, particularly TM-230-VE-7-5-1, showed significant potential for breeding programs aimed at improving drought tolerance in rice. These findings have substantial implications for enhancing rice production in drought-prone regions, contributing to sustainable agricultural practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05771-5.

Introduction

People in many countries consume rice (Oryza sativa L.), an economically important crop with nutritional diversification, which forms the basis of the diet for more than half of the world’s population [1]. Drought significantly affects rice, one of the most crucial food crops in the lowland rice ecosystem [2]. Drought is one of the prime abiotic stresses globally, causing significant reductions in rice yield by affecting many morphological and physiological aspects of the plant from anthesis to maturity [3]. A successful breeding program depends on the genetic diversity of a crop for achieving the goals of improving high-yielding rice varieties with enhanced grain micronutrients [4], even under unfavorable weather conditions.

In recent years, mutation breeding has emerged as a powerful tool in crop improvement, particularly in enhancing traits like drought tolerance [5]. Mutation breeding uses physical or chemical mutagens to cause genetic variations, and then it picks out traits that are desirable [6]. This approach generates new genetic variability, which can be crucial in developing crops with enhanced resilience to abiotic stresses, such as drought [7]. Mutant lines can exhibit a wide range of beneficial traits, including improved yield, disease resistance, and stress tolerance, making mutation breeding an essential strategy in modern agriculture [8].

Previous research has identified several drought-tolerant rice genotypes through conventional breeding and molecular approaches. For instance, Kumar et al. [9] identified four rice genotypes with favorable grain yield under drought stress during the reproductive stage. Singh et al. [10] used drought tolerance indices to find three drought-tolerant genotypes in the reproductive stage, both in drought stress conditions and controlled conditions during Kharif. Similarly, Safaei Chaeikar et al. [11] found genotypes such as Nemat, Sepidrood, IR64, Bejar, and IR50 to have the highest tolerance to drought stress, with the highest paddy yields ranging from 4.03 to 7.07 tons per hectare. Danesh Gilevaei et al. [12] conducted an investigation into 150 pure lines resulting from a cross between two Iranian rice varieties, identifying several high-yielding and drought-tolerant lines suitable for use in breeding programs. Giasi Oskoei et al. [13] conducted an evaluation of 15 genotypes under both stressed and non-stressed conditions, identifying cultivars such as Garde, Sarkho, Sangjo, Tarom Mahali, and Tarom Amiri as the most tolerant while identifying some others as sensitive to drought stress. Tabkhkar et al. [14] evaluated 83 diverse rice genotypes and found IR83750-131-1, Sangjo, Sepidrood, Zenith, and IR58 to have higher drought tolerance. Despite significant advancements in rice breeding, the development of high-yielding, drought-tolerant rice varieties remains a critical challenge [15]. While traditional breeding methods have identified several drought-tolerant genotypes, they often compromise yield or lack widespread adaptation to diverse environmental conditions [2]. Moreover, the genetic diversity available within cultivated rice is limited, constraining the potential for further improvements [1].

Mutation breeding presents an opportunity to introduce novel genetic variations that are not present in the existing gene pool [16]. However, there is a paucity of research on the long-term performance and stability of mutant rice lines under prolonged drought stress conditions. Moreover, specific traits such as plant height, spike number, seed fertility, and yield components have not been thoroughly investigated in mutant lines under extended drought stress. Understanding how these traits contribute to drought tolerance is essential for breeding programs [17]. Previous studies have focused on short-term drought stress or have not extensively evaluated the agronomic and physiological traits that contribute to drought resilience [11, 12].

Therefore, this study aimed to fill these knowledge gaps by developing and evaluating high-yielding, drought-tolerant mutant rice lines using mutation breeding techniques. We extended the drought stress period to 35 days, significantly longer than in many previous studies, to rigorously assess the mutants’ performance under severe stress conditions. By analyzing a comprehensive set of agronomic traits and applying multiple drought tolerance indices, we sought to identify mutant genotypes that combine high yield potential with enhanced drought resilience, thereby contributing to sustainable rice production in drought-prone regions.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

In this study, we used a diverse set of rice genotypes, including two well-known Iranian commercial rice landraces—Tarom Mahalli and Hashemi—alongside five other commercial Iranian rice cultivars: Khazar, Fajr, Ali Kazemi, Sepidrood, and Sangjoo. Additionally, we included a high-yielding international standard cultivar, referred to as HP. Furthermore, 56 promising mutant rice lines were evaluated, consisting of 13 lines derived from Tarom Mahalli, 31 lines from Hashemi, 10 lines from Khazar, and two lines from Fajr. These mutant lines were generated through gamma irradiation in 2009 and were selected from the M6 population. The initial evaluation of drought tolerance in these mutant populations was conducted between 2010 and 2013 at the Rice Research Institute of Iran, Rasht [18]. During this period, the severity of drought stress was imposed for an average of two to three weeks starting from the onset of flowering. All 56 mutant lines demonstrated drought tolerance and favorable yields under drought conditions in previous studies, making them suitable candidates for further evaluation.

Evaluation of tolerance levels under 35-day drought stress and normal conditions

To identify the most drought-tolerant lines with superior qualitative and quantitative traits, we extended the drought stress period to 35 days in the experimental farm for 2 years (Fig. 1). The field capacity of the soil was determined prior to the experiment using the gravimetric method. During the application of drought stress, soil moisture levels were monitored twice weekly using a soil moisture meter. The drought stress was imposed by withholding irrigation, allowing the soil moisture to decrease to approximately 50% of the field capacity, which is considered a moderate to severe drought stress level for rice cultivation. After bringing the soil moisture to field capacity, water stress was imposed approximately two weeks before flowering and continued until three weeks after flowering. Control plots received regular irrigation to maintain field capacity conditions. The study involved 64 rice genotypes, which were evaluated in a three-replicate Square Lattice 8 × 8 design.Fig. 1Experimental rice field conditions one month after the onset of drought stress, illustrating the phenotypic differences among the genotypes under study. After 2 year experiments, to assess the yield stability of 14 selected mutant drought-tolerant lines, a randomized complete block design (RCBD) was employed for 2 years. The RCBD experiment also included three parental cultivars (Hashemi, Tarom Mahalli, and Khazar) and a new commercial cultivar, Gilaneh, which are among the most renowned Iranian commercial rice landraces and cultivars.

Analysis and comparison of yield and yield components under drought stress and normal conditions

A comprehensive evaluation of agronomical traits and yield components was conducted under both drought stress and normal conditions. The traits assessed included:

Agronomical traits: Plant height, internode length. Yield and yield components: Spike number, spike length, spike weight, number of filled seeds, number of empty seeds, fertility percentage, 1000 seed weight, seed length, seed width, and seed length/width ratio.

Although specific data on days to 50% flowering (DFF) and days to maturity (DM) were not recorded, field observations indicated minimal variation in flowering onset among the genotypes under both normal and drought stress conditions.

In the following year, the same traits were assessed in the 14 selected drought-tolerant mutant lines using a three-replicate RCBD under both drought stress and normal conditions.

While physiological traits like root depth, leaf rolling, leaf area, stomatal conductance, and relative water content are important indicators of plant responses to drought stress [2], our study focused on drought tolerance indices for several reasons: (a) Drought tolerance indices, such as MP, GMP, and STI, integrate the effects of drought stress on yield and yield components, providing a comprehensive measure of a genotype’s performance under both stress and non-stress conditions [15]. (b) These indices are directly related to agronomic performance, which is crucial for breeders aiming to improve crop productivity under drought [17]. They help identify genotypes that maintain high yields in drought conditions, which is the ultimate goal for farmers and breeders [31]. (c) Drought tolerance is a complex trait influenced by various physiological and morphological factors [32]. Drought tolerance indices capture the cumulative effect of these factors on yield, offering a holistic understanding of plant performance that individual physiological traits may not fully reveal [33]. (d) Measuring physiological traits like root architecture or stomatal conductance can be time-consuming and resource-intensive, especially in large field trials [34]. Drought tolerance indices allow for efficient screening of numerous genotypes based on readily measurable agronomic data [9]. Therefore, we selected drought tolerance indices as they provide a reliable, integrative, and practical approach to assess the drought tolerance of our mutant lines.

Also, the decision to use multiple indices rather than only 2 or 3 was based on the understanding that drought tolerance is a complex trait influenced by various physiological and genetic factors [10]. Some indices, like SSI, focus on relative performance under stress compared to non-stress conditions, while others, like STI and GMP, identify genotypes with high yield potential under both conditions [35]. By employing a suite of indices, we aimed to capture the multidimensional nature of drought tolerance, reduce the risk of misclassification due to reliance on a single index, and increase the reliability of our genotype selection for breeding programs.

Experimental data analysis

ANOVA was performed using the general linear model (GLM) procedure in SAS software (version 9.4). Homogeneity of error variances across years was assessed using Bartlett’s test. Critical difference (CD) at the 5% significance level and coefficient of variation (CV) were calculated for each trait to evaluate the precision of the experiments.

Data analysis was performed using SAS software version 9.1. The Least Significant Difference (LSD) test was applied to compare the means of the studied traits (the mean of 2 years). The statistical analysis focused on identifying significant differences between genotypes under drought stress and normal conditions, enabling the selection of the most promising drought-tolerant lines with superior agronomic and yield-related traits.

PCA was performed using R software (version 4.0.3) with the ‘prcomp’ function. Prior to PCA, all variables were standardized (mean-centered and scaled to unit variance) to ensure that each trait contributed equally to the analysis. The PCA aimed to reduce the dimensionality of the data while retaining most of the variation present in the dataset. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) were used to create a biplot for visualizing the relationships among genotypes and traits.

Hierarchical cluster analysis was conducted using the Ward’s minimum variance method to group genotypes based on their agronomic traits and drought tolerance indices. The Euclidean distance metric was used to measure the dissimilarity between genotypes. The ‘hclust’ and ‘dist’ functions in R were utilized for this analysis. The optimal number of clusters was determined by examining the dendrogram and using the ‘elbow method,’ which involves identifying the point where adding another cluster does not significantly reduce the within-cluster sum of squares. All statistical analyses were performed in R software (version 4.0.3). The packages ‘stats’, ‘ggplot2’, and ‘factoextra’ were used for PCA and cluster visualization.

Results

To determine the significance of the differences among the genotypes under both normal and drought stress conditions, we performed a detailed analysis of variance (ANOVA) for each experiment. The ANOVA was conducted separately for each trait measured during the two-year evaluation period. The initial experiment involved 64 rice genotypes evaluated in a three-replicate Square Lattice 8 × 8 design. The analysis under both normal and drought stress conditions revealed significant differences across the genotypes for most traits (Table S1). Under normal conditions, traits such as plant height (PH), internode length (INL), spike number (SPN), spike length (SPL), number of full seeds (NFS), percentage of fertility (PF), and yield (Y) showed highly significant (p < 0.01) genotype effects, indicating considerable variability among the genotypes. The significant differences suggest that the genotypes responded differently to the normal growing environment, highlighting their genetic diversity. Similarly, under drought stress conditions, several traits exhibited significant variation among genotypes. Plant height (PH), spike length (SPL), number of empty seeds (NES), percentage of fertility (PF), and yield (Y) showed significant (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01) genotype effects, suggesting that drought stress conditions had a strong impact on these traits. The variation under stress conditions indicates differences in drought tolerance among the genotypes, which is crucial for selecting drought-resilient varieties. The replication effect was not significant for most traits in both environments, except for a few traits under normal and drought conditions, such as number of full seeds (NFS) and yield (Y), where significant replication effects were observed. This indicates a consistency in the environmental conditions across replications, leading to stable performance of the genotypes in most traits.

For the second experiment, 14 selected mutant drought-tolerant lines and their parental lines were evaluated using a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications over two years. The combined analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed significant effects for the majority of agronomic traits studied under both normal and drought stress conditions (Table S2). For plant height (PH), significant variation was found among genotypes (F-value = 15.67, P < 0.0001), indicating a considerable level of genetic variability under different environmental conditions. No significant interaction was found between genotype and year for PH, suggesting that the genotypic performance remained consistent across years. Similar results were observed for other traits, including internode length (INL), spike number (SPN), spike length (SPL), and yield (Y), where genotype effects were significant (P < 0.05), indicating substantial genetic differences among the studied entries.

The least significant difference (LSD) values and coefficient of variation (CV%) for each agronomic trait under normal and drought stress conditions are summarized in Table S3. The LSD (5%) values indicated the minimum difference required to declare two genotype means significantly different at a 95% confidence level. For instance, for plant height, the LSD was 4.87 under normal conditions and 6.25 under drought stress, highlighting the greater variability under stress. The CV% values ranged from 9.15% for the SL/SW ratio under normal conditions to 23.18% for the number of empty spikelets (NES) under drought stress, indicating varying precision in the experimental conditions. Traits like spike length (SPL) and hundred seed weight (HSW) exhibited relatively low CV%, suggesting good experimental reliability, while traits such as NES and number of fertile spikelets (NFS) had higher CV%, indicating greater environmental influence or variability in measurement.

The comparison between the wild types Hashemi (H), Khazar (Kh), Fajr (F), and Tarom Mahalli (TM) and their corresponding mutants (Tarom Mutant (TM), Hashemi Mutant (HM), Fajr Mutant (FM), and Khazar Mutant (KM)) across various agronomic traits under normal and drought stress conditions provided significant insights into how these genotypes respond to environmental stress, particularly water scarcity (Table 1). The analysis of traits such as plant height (PH), internode length (INL), spike number (SPN), spike length (SPL), the number of full seeds (NFS), the number of empty seeds (NES), percentage of fertility (PF), hundred seed weight (HSW), seed length (SL), seed width (SW), seed length/width ratio (SL/SW), and yield (Y) revealed a complex interplay between genetic makeup and environmental stress that influences the performance and adaptability of these rice genotypes. Starting with plant height (PH), wild types such as Hashemi and Tarom Mahalli typically exhibited taller plants under normal conditions, reaching up to 145 cm. This height advantage is likely due to their genetic background, which has been optimized over generations in favorable environments. However, under drought stress, these wild types experienced a significant reduction in height. Hashemi, for instance, decreased from 145 cm to 128 cm, while Tarom Mahalli was reduced to 134 cm, representing an 11.7% and 7.6% reduction, respectively. This substantial decrease suggests that the wild types are more susceptible to water stress, possibly due to the larger biomass of the wild types, which may require more water to maintain. On the other hand, mutants such as HM-250-42-1-E and TM-B-2-1-E showed better retention of their height under drought conditions.

These mutants, which were specifically bred for resilience, exhibited smaller reductions in plant height, indicating that they have developed mechanisms to cope with water stress more effectively. The ability to maintain height under drought conditions is crucial, as it often correlates with overall plant vigor and the ability to compete for sunlight.

Internode length (INL), another important trait, showed a similar trend. In normal conditions, wild types like Tarom Mahalli had a longer internode length, reaching up to 63.2 cm, which could be beneficial for light capture and resource allocation in non-stress environments. However, under drought stress, this trait saw a significant reduction, with Tarom Mahalli’s internode length decreasing to 56.7 cm. This reduction may be a physiological response to conserve water and reduce transpiration. In contrast, Tarom Mahalli mutants, particularly TM-B-2-1-E, exhibited more stable internode lengths under drought stress. This stability in internode length among mutants suggests that they may possess more efficient water use efficiency or deeper root systems that allow them to access water from deeper soil layers, thus maintaining growth and development even under limited water availability.

The spike number (SPN), which directly correlates with the potential grain yield, also experienced a notable reduction under drought stress across both wild types and mutants. For example, Fajr (F) had 15 spikes under normal conditions, but this number significantly decreased under drought conditions. This reduction in spike number is critical as it directly impacts the potential grain yield. Interestingly, Fajr mutants, such as FM-200-E6, maintained a relatively higher spike number despite the stress, indicating that the mutants might have better drought adaptation mechanisms that allow them to sustain reproductive structures under adverse conditions. This ability to maintain a higher number of spikes under stress is particularly valuable as it could lead to a less severe reduction in yield under drought conditions.

Spike length (SPL) is another trait that diminished under drought stress. Wild types such as Sepidrood (Se) typically had longer spike lengths under normal conditions, with Sepidrood reaching 27 cm. However, under drought stress, this length decreased, with Sepidrood’s spike length dropped to 24.9 cm. The reduction in spike length under drought stress could be due to the plant’s reduced ability to allocate resources to reproductive growth when water is scarce. On the other hand, mutants such as HM-250-7-6 showed only minor reductions in spike length, demonstrating better drought resilience. Maintaining spike length under drought conditions is advantageous as it can lead to a higher number of grains per spike, which is crucial for sustaining yield under water-limited conditions.

The number of full seeds (NFS) and the number of empty seeds (NES) are critical indicators of reproductive success under drought conditions. Wild types generally produced more full seeds under normal conditions due to their optimized genetic background for fertile environments. For example, Khazar (Kh) produced 115 full seeds under normal conditions, a number that significantly drops to 32 seeds under drought stress. This reduction highlights the impact of water stress on the plant’s ability to produce viable seeds. In contrast, Khazar mutants such as KM-200-19-1, although starting with fewer full seeds, experienced less severe reductions under drought conditions, suggesting a better ability to maintain seed production when water is limited. The number of empty seeds dramatically increases in wild types under drought conditions, with Khazar showing an increase from 35 to 76 empty seeds. In contrast, the mutants exhibited smaller increases in the number of empty seeds, which highlights their relative drought tolerance. The ability to maintain a higher percentage of full seeds and fewer empty seeds under drought stress is critical for ensuring crop yield and quality.

Percentage of fertility (PF), which reflects the plant’s reproductive success, is another trait that experienced significant reductions under drought stress. Wild types such as Ali Kazemi (AK) typically exhibited high fertility under normal conditions, with a fertility percentage of 93.3%. However, under drought stress, this percentage drops sharply to 40.8%. This decline in fertility is a clear indication of the adverse effects of water stress on the plant’s reproductive organs and processes. In comparison, mutants like HM-250-42-1-E-1 retain higher fertility percentages under drought stress, which suggests that these mutants have developed mechanisms to protect their reproductive structures from the effects of drought, possibly through enhanced water use efficiency or stress tolerance pathways.

One hundred seed weight (HSW), which is a critical determinant of grain yield and quality, generally decreased under drought conditions. Wild types like Hashemi typically had a higher 100 seed weight under normal conditions, with Hashemi showing a seed weight of 2.6 g. However, under drought stress, this weight decreased to 2.14 g. This reduction in seed weight under drought conditions is likely due to the plant’s inability to allocate sufficient resources to grain filling when water is scarce. In contrast, Hashemi mutants like HM-250-12-1 retained more of their seed weight under drought stress, with only a slight reduction from 2.57 g to 2.04 g. This ability to maintain seed weight under drought conditions is advantageous as it directly impacts the overall grain yield and quality.

Seed length (SL) and seed width (SW) are also impacted by drought stress, with both dimensions typically decreasing under water-limited conditions. For instance, Fajr (F) showed a significant reduction in seed length and width under drought stress, which could negatively impact the overall seed quality and marketability. However, its mutants, such as FM-200-E6, tend to maintain a more stable seed size, indicating that the mutants may have more robust seed morphology under stress conditions. The ability to maintain seed dimensions under drought is important for ensuring that the grain meets quality standards, which is crucial for both market acceptance and farmer profitability.

The seed length/width ratio (SL/SW) generally increases under drought conditions, reflecting changes in seed shape due to differential reductions in length and width. Wild types like Sepidrood showed a more pronounced change in this ratio, indicating a greater impact of drought on seed morphology. In contrast, mutants exhibited less variation in this ratio, suggesting that they might retain their seed shape more consistently under stress. Maintaining a consistent seed shape under drought conditions is beneficial as it ensures uniformity in grain size, which is an important quality trait for both consumers and processors.

Finally, yield (Y) is perhaps the most critical trait, as it directly reflects the overall productivity of the plant. Yield was severely impacted by drought across all genotypes, with wild types like Hashemi and Tarom Mahalli experiencing drastic reductions. For instance, Hashemi’s yield droped from 3686.5 kg/ha under normal conditions to just 546.5 kg/ha under drought stress, representing an over 85% reduction. Tarom Mahalli showed a similar pattern, with yield decreasing from 3646.5 kg/ha to 375.5 kg/ha, highlighting the severe impact of drought on these traditionally high-yielding varieties. In contrast, mutants such as TM-B-19-1-E and HM-250-42-1-E-1, although starting with lower yields under normal conditions, showed relatively smaller reductions under drought stress. For example, TM-B-19-1-E’s yield decreases from 5243.5 kg/ha to 2068 kg/ha, which, while significant, is less drastic compared to the wild types. This suggests that these mutants may be better suited for cultivation in drought-prone areas, as they maintain more stable yields under stress conditions.

Overall, Drought stress has had a profound impact on all measured traits, particularly yield, seed fertility, and seed weight, limiting growth potential and reproductive success, which culminated in significant yield reductions across genotypes. Among the genotypes, M6 (TM-B-2-1-E), M32 (HM-250-7-6), and M41 (HM-300-3-1) showed better resilience to drought stress, maintaining relatively stable traits and lower yield reductions. In contrast, M53 (KM-200-43-1-E), M49 (KM-200-19-1), and M50 (KM-200-19-2) were the most affected, exhibiting significant reductions in both vegetative and reproductive traits, resulting in much lower yields under drought conditions. These findings highlight the importance of selecting genotypes with enhanced drought resistance for breeding programs aimed at improving rice productivity under water-limited conditions.

After evaluation of yield and yield components of studied genotypes in 35 days drought stress and normal conditions, 14 mutant drought-tolerant promising lines including 6 promising lines with origin of Tarom Mahalli, 7 promising lines with origin of Hashemi and only a promising line with origin of Khazar were selected as plant materials for final selection of high yielding drought-tolerant promising lines with superior quantitative and qualitative traits in next year (Table 2). The analysis of key agronomic traits—plant height (PH), internode length (INL), spike number (SPN), spike length (SPL), number of full seeds (NFS), number of empty seeds (NES), percentage of fertility (PF), hundred seed weight (HSW), seed length (SL), seed width (SW), and yield (Y)—under both normal and drought stress conditions reveals significant differences in the performance of parental rice lines (Hashemi (H), Khazar (Kh), Tarom Mahalli (TM), and the control variety Gilaneh (G)) and their corresponding mutant lines. This comprehensive trait-by-trait analysis highlighted the resilience of the mutant lines compared to their respective parental lines, particularly in response to drought stress.

Starting with plant height (PH), the parental lines generally exhibited strong growth under normal conditions, with Hashemi reaching 156.3 cm, Khazar at 133 cm, and Tarom Mahalli at a towering 168.7 cm, the tallest among the examined lines. However, under drought stress, these heights decreased, with Hashemi reducing slightly to 157.3 cm, Khazar to 127 cm, and Tarom Mahalli to 153.7 cm. In contrast, the mutant lines often showed better retention of plant height under drought stress. For example, HM-250-7-6 retains a height of 147.3 cm, and HM-250-12-1 maintains 146 cm, indicating that these Hashemi mutants are more resilient to water scarcity. Similarly, KM-200-4-2-E holded a plant height of 125.3 cm under drought, showing stability despite the stress, while TM-250-10-7-1 and TM-B-2-1-E maintain heights of 139 cm and 131.3 cm, respectively, outperforming the parental Tarom Mahalli line in terms of height retention under drought conditions.

Internode length (INL) is another critical trait that reveals the impact of drought stress on plant structure. Under normal conditions, Hashemi showed an internode length of 45 cm, which slightly increased to 47.7 cm under drought, a likely physiological response to maintain structural integrity despite stress. Khazar and Tarom Mahalli, however, showed reductions in internode length under drought, with Khazar decreasing from 44.5 cm to 39 cm, and Tarom Mahalli from 44.7 cm to 44 cm. The mutants, in contrast, demonstrated greater stability. HM-250-6-6 retained an internode length of 44 cm under drought, closely matching its normal condition performance, while HM-250-E-1-1 maintains 41.3 cm. KM-200-4-2-E showed a slight reduction to 38 cm under drought but remains relatively stable. Mutants from Tarom Mahalli, such as TM-250-10-7-1 and TM-B-2-1-E, also exhibited resilience with internode lengths of 35 cm and 35.7 cm under drought, indicating better structural stability compared to their parental line.

In terms of spike number (SPN), the parental lines showed a decrease under drought conditions, which directly impacts potential grain yield. Hashemi, which produced 15 spikes per plant under normal conditions, saw this number drop under drought, as does Khazar, which decreased from 12 to 13 spikes. Tarom Mahalli also experienced a reduction, maintaining 15 spikes under normal conditions but dropping to 18 spikes under drought. Gilaneh, although a control variety, maintained a relatively high spike number of 20 under normal conditions, showing some stability under drought stress. The mutants, however, generally outperform their parental lines in maintaining spike number. HM-250-7-6 retained 21 spikes under drought, and HM-250-12-1 also maintains 21 spikes, indicating strong resilience. KM-200-4-2-E shows better performance with 15 spikes under drought, higher than Khazar. Similarly, TM-250-10-7-1 and TM-B-2-1-E retain 18 and 19 spikes under drought, respectively, showing better retention of this crucial trait compared to the parental Tarom Mahalli line.

Spike length (SPL), which influences the number of grains per spike, also showed variability under drought stress. Hashemi, with a spike length of 30.5 cm under normal conditions, saw a slight decrease to 30 cm under drought. Khazar’s spike length reduced more significantly, from 30.2 cm to 28.3 cm, while Tarom Mahalli’s spike length decreased from 25.8 cm to 27.5 cm. Gilaneh, with a shorter spike length of 22.8 cm, remained relatively stable under drought conditions. The mutant lines, however, generally showed better retention of spike length. HM-250-E-1-1 retained a spike length of 31.3 cm under drought, outperforming Hashemi, and HM-250-E-3-2 maintains 30.2 cm, showing good stability. KM-200-4-2-E retained a spike length of 28.5 cm under drought, indicating resilience compared to Khazar. Tarom Mahalli mutants like TM-250-10-7-1 and TM-B-2-1-E maintained spike lengths of 24.5 cm and 27.7 cm under drought, respectively, indicating better performance than the parent line under stress conditions.

The number of full seeds (NFS), a key indicator of reproductive success, decreased significantly in parental lines under drought stress. Hashemi’s number of full seeds droped from 105 under normal conditions to 51 under drought, while Khazar’s decreased from 97 to 44. Tarom Mahalli, which produced 116 full seeds under normal conditions, saw a reduction to 52 under drought. Gilaneh, with 138 full seeds under normal conditions, reduced to 63 under drought. In contrast, mutant lines generally retained a higher number of full seeds under drought conditions. HM-250-E-1-1 maintained 96 full seeds, significantly better than Hashemi, and HM-250-7-6 retained 79 full seeds. KM-200-4-2-E showed a slight improvement over Khazar, maintaining 48 full seeds under drought. Tarom Mahalli mutants, particularly TM-250-10-7-1 and TM-B-2-1-E, retained 74 and 90 full seeds under drought, respectively, indicating superior reproductive resilience compared to the parent line.

Conversely, the number of empty seeds (NES) tended to increase under drought stress, reflecting the negative impact of water scarcity on seed fertility. Hashemi’s number of empty seeds increased from 15 under normal conditions to 31 under drought, while Khazar’s increased significantly from 31 to 60. Tarom Mahalli also saw a substantial increase from 16 to 53 empty seeds under drought stress. Gilaneh’s empty seeds increased from 26 to 39 under drought. The mutant lines generally performed better, showing a lower increase in the number of empty seeds. HM-250-E-1-1 showed better tolerance with only 18 empty seeds under drought, compared to Hashemi, and HM-250-6-6 also performed well with 28 empty seeds. KM-200-4-2-E retained 62 empty seeds under drought, slightly higher but still stable compared to Khazar. Tarom Mahalli mutants like TM-250-10-7-1 and TM-B-2-1-E also showed resilience, with 15 and 18 empty seeds under drought, respectively, indicating a lower increase compared to the parent line.

Percentage of fertility (PF), which reflects the overall reproductive success of the plant, decreased significantly in parental lines under drought stress. Hashemi’s fertility percentage droped from 87.3% under normal conditions to 62.6% under drought, while Khazar’s decreased more drastically from 75.7 to 42.6%. Tarom Mahalli’s fertility percentage decreased from 87.8 to 49.7% under drought stress. Gilaneh, although showing some reduction, maintains a relatively stable fertility percentage of 84.3% under normal conditions and slightly reduced under drought. Mutant lines generally showed better retention of fertility under drought conditions. HM-250-E-1-1 maintained a higher fertility percentage of 84.3% under drought, significantly better than Hashemi, and HM-250-6-6 also shows better retention with 72.5% under drought. KM-200-4-2-E retained a fertility percentage of 43.7% under drought, slightly better than Khazar. Tarom Mahalli mutants like TM-250-10-7-1 and TM-B-2-1-E also performed well, maintaining fertility percentages of 84% and 77.6% under drought, respectively, outperforming the parental line.

The 100 seed weight (HSW), an important determinant of grain yield and quality, decreased in parental lines under drought stress. Hashemi’s hundred seed weight droped from 2.48 g under normal conditions to 2.2 g under drought, while Khazar’s decreased from 2.28 g to 2.1 g. Tarom Mahalli’s hundred seed weight also reduced slightly under drought stress. Gilaneh maintained a hundred seed weight of 2.4 g under normal conditions, slightly decreasing under drought. Mutant lines generally retained higher hundred seed weights under drought. HM-250-E-1-1 maintains 2.42 g under drought, showing better retention than Hashemi, and HM-250-6-6 also performed well with 2.51 g under drought. KM-200-4-2-E maintained 2.1 g under drought, showing stability compared to Khazar. Tarom Mahalli mutants like TM-250-10-7-1 and TM-B-2-1-E also showed good retention of hundred seed weight under drought conditions, maintaining relatively stable weights.

Seed length (SL) and seed width (SW) also showed reductions under drought stress in parental lines. Hashemi’s seed length decreased from 11.07 mm under normal conditions to 9.9 mm under drought, while its seed width decreased from 2.4 mm to 2.3 mm. Khazar’s seed length decreased from 9.43 mm to 9.13 mm, and its seed width reduced from 2.3 mm to 2.1 mm under drought. Tarom Mahalli showed a slight reduction in seed length and width under drought stress. Gilaneh maintained relatively stable seed dimensions under drought conditions. Mutant lines, however, generally showed better retention of seed dimensions. HM-250-E-1-1 retained a seed length of 10.2 mm under drought, with a stable seed width of 2.67 mm. HM-250-12-1 also maintained good seed dimensions under drought. KM-200-4-2-E retained stable seed dimensions under drought with slight reductions, indicating resilience. Tarom Mahalli mutants like TM-250-10-7-1 and TM-B-2-1-E showed good retention of seed dimensions under drought, maintaining relatively stable seed lengths and widths.

Finally, yield (Y), the most critical trait, showed significant reductions in parental lines under drought stress. Hashemi’s yield droped dramatically from 4021.7 kg/ha under normal conditions to 642.7 kg/ha under drought, indicating high sensitivity. Khazar’s yield decreased from 4157.3 kg/ha to 620.7 kg/ha under drought, showing a similar reduction. Tarom Mahalli’s yield decreased from 3596.7 kg/ha to 859.3 kg/ha under drought, indicating considerable sensitivity to water stress. Gilaneh, although showing a reduction, maintained a relatively stable yield of 975.7 kg/ha under drought conditions. In contrast, mutant lines generally retained higher yields under drought. HM-250-E-1-1 maintained a yield of 2375 kg/ha under drought, significantly better than Hashemi. HM-250-7-6 also performed well with a yield of 1637.7 kg/ha under drought. KM-200-4-2-E retained a yield of 726.3 kg/ha under drought, showing slight improvement over Khazar. Tarom Mahalli mutants like TM-230-VE-7-5-1 and TM-250-10-7-1 also showed strong performance, maintaining yields of 2788.3 kg/ha and 2178.3 kg/ha under drought, respectively, significantly outperforming the parent line.

Based on above results, the parental lines—Hashemi, Khazar, and Tarom Mahalli—while performing well under normal conditions, exhibited significant reductions in key traits such as yield, fertility, and seed quality under drought stress. In contrast, their mutant lines often showed enhanced drought tolerance, maintaining higher yields, better fertility, and more stable seed dimensions under stress. Notably, Hashemi mutants such as HM-250-E-1-1 and Tarom Mahalli’s TM-230-VE-7-5-1 demonstrated significant improvements in drought resilience, making them valuable candidates for breeding programs focused on enhancing drought tolerance in rice. The Gilaneh variety, serving as a control, showed moderate reductions under drought but generally lower sensitivity compared to the other parental lines. These findings underscore the importance of mutant lines in developing rice varieties that can sustain productivity under water-limited conditions, contributing to food security in regions prone to drought.

Analysis of drought tolerance indices

The analysis of drought tolerance indices across various rice genotypes revealed significant differences in their ability to maintain yield and adapt under drought stress (Table 3). Among the genotypes, TM-230-VE-7-5-1 stood out as the most drought-tolerant, consistently ranking first across multiple indices. It achieved the highest mean productivity (MP) of 3851.50 and geometric mean productivity (GMP) of 3701.85, indicating robust performance under both normal and stress conditions. Its low stress susceptibility index (SSI) of 0.69 and tolerance index (TOL) of 2126.40 further emphasize its resilience, showing minimal yield reduction under drought. Discussion

The comparison between the wild types (Hashemi, Khazar, Fajr, and Tarom Mahalli) and their corresponding mutants Tarom Mutant, Hashemi Mutant, Fajr Mutant, and Khazar Mutant) across various agronomic traits under normal and drought stress conditions revealed significant insights into how these genotypes respond to environmental stress, particularly water scarcity. Wild types, such as Hashemi and Tarom Mahalli, typically exhibited taller plants, longer internode lengths, and higher spike numbers under normal conditions, but these advantages were significantly reduced under drought stress, indicating their susceptibility to water scarcity [11]. In contrast, mutants like HM-250-42-1-E and TM-B-2-1-E demonstrated better retention of plant height and internode length, suggesting improved water use efficiency and deeper root systems [2, 36]. Similarly, while wild types experienced severe reductions in spike number, spike length, and seed fertility under drought conditions, mutants like FM-200-E6 and HM-250-7-6 maintained more stable reproductive traits, indicating their better adaptation to drought [3]. The number of full seeds decreased, and the number of empty seeds increased significantly in wild types under stress, while mutants showed smaller changes, reflecting their relative drought tolerance [37]. Additionally, fertility percentage and hundred seed weight decreased sharply in wild types, but mutants like HM-250-12-1 retained higher values, suggesting they have mechanisms to protect reproductive success and grain filling under drought [38]. Seed dimensions, including length and width, also decreased in wild types, but mutants exhibited more stable seed morphology, maintaining consistent seed shapes under stress [39]. Yield, the most critical trait, was drastically reduced in wild types, with Hashemi and Tarom Mahalli showing over 85% reductions, while mutants like TM-B-19-1-E and HM-250-42-1-E-1 had relatively smaller yield losses, highlighting their potential for cultivation in drought-prone areas [40].

The evaluation of rice genotypes through comprehensive yield and component analysis under both normal and drought stress conditions, coupled with the exploration of drought tolerance indices, cluster analysis, and Principal Component Analysis (PCA), provided a robust framework for understanding the genotypic responses to water scarcity and the potential for breeding drought-tolerant rice varieties [41, 42]. The results from these studies highlight the intricate interplay between genetic makeup and environmental stress, revealing that certain genotypes possess intrinsic resilience mechanisms that allow them to maintain productivity under adverse conditions [43], while others are significantly compromised by drought, resulting in substantial yield losses.

The analysis of key agronomic traits clearly delineates the performance disparities between the wild-type parental lines (Hashemi, Khazar, Fajr, and Tarom Mahalli) and their corresponding mutant lines [44]. Under normal conditions, the parental lines generally exceled in most traits, exhibiting optimal growth and reproductive success, which is a reflection of their adaptation to non-stress environments [33]. For instance, Hashemi, with its towering height of 156.3 cm and high fertility percentage, represented the typical high-performing variety under favorable conditions [11]. However, when subjected to drought stress, these parental lines exhibit significant reductions in key traits, most notably in yield, fertility, and seed quality, underscoring their vulnerability to water scarcity [45]. The substantial yield reductions observed in Hashemi and Tarom Mahalli, for example, underscored the detrimental impact of drought on traditionally high-yielding varieties, with Hashemi’s yield plummeting by over 85%, signaling its high sensitivity to drought [46].

In contrast, the mutant lines often demonstrated enhanced resilience under drought stress, maintaining higher yields and more stable trait expressions compared to their parental counterparts [15]. This resilience is particularly evident in mutants such as HM-250-E-1-1, TM-230-VE-7-5-1, and TM-B-2-1-E, which consistently outperform their respective parental lines in drought conditions [47]. These mutants exhibited smaller reductions in plant height, internode length, spike number, and fertility percentage, suggesting that they possess adaptive mechanisms, possibly related to more efficient water use, deeper root systems, or better stress signaling pathways, that allow them to cope with limited water availability more effectively [48, 49]. For example, HM-250-E-1-1 retained a yield of 2375 kg/ha under drought, which, while lower than its performance in normal conditions, still represents a significant improvement over Hashemi’s yield under the same stress, demonstrating its potential as a drought-tolerant cultivar [17].

The qualitative field observations suggested minimal differences in flowering times among the genotypes, and the application of drought stress at the onset of flowering for each genotype ensured that all plants experienced stress during the critical reproductive stage. Therefore, it is reasonable to infer that the enhanced drought tolerance observed in mutant lines is likely attributable to intrinsic genetic improvements affecting physiological and biochemical responses to drought stress rather than a phenological escape mechanism [38].

Previous studies have demonstrated that drought tolerance can be conferred by genetic factors that enhance root architecture, improve water use efficiency, maintain cellular membrane integrity, and bolster antioxidant defense systems [10, 32, 50]. The mutant lines in our study may possess such traits, contributing to their superior performance under drought conditions.

The cluster analysis under normal and drought conditions further illuminated the genotypic variability and its adaptive responses to environmental stress [51]. Under normal conditions, genotypes clustered into groups based on their high yield and strong agronomic traits, with Cluster 4 and Cluster 2, containing genotypes like TM-230-VE-7-5-1 and TM-B-2-1-E, showing the most promising performances. These clusters highlight genotypes that are not only high-yielding but also possess desirable traits such as high fertility and optimal seed dimensions, making them ideal candidates for breeding programs focused on enhancing productivity [1]. However, under drought stress, the clustering shifts, with genotypes that maintain higher yields and better stress tolerance clustering together, such as those in Cluster 5, which included TM-230-VE-7-5-1 and HM-250-E-1-1. This shift in clustering underscored the importance of stress tolerance traits in determining genotype performance under adverse conditions and highlighted the potential of these mutants to serve as resilient cultivars in drought-prone areas [17, 18].

The analysis of drought tolerance indices further solidified the understanding of genotypic resilience, providing quantitative measures of how each genotype responded to water stress [1]. Genotypes like TM-230-VE-7-5-1 and TM-B-2-1-E, which consistently rank at the top across various indices such as MP, GMP, and STI, showing a remarkable ability to maintain productivity under stress, with minimal reductions in yield [52]. Their low SSI values further highlighted their robustness, indicating that these genotypes are less affected by drought compared to others [11]. In contrast, traditional varieties like Khazar, Hashemi, and Gilaneh, which ranked lower on these indices, exhibit significant vulnerabilities, as evidenced by their high SSI values and poor performance in STI and YI [12]. These findings emphasize the need for genetic improvements in these traditional varieties to enhance drought tolerance and ensure stable production in regions prone to water scarcity [36].

The PCA complemented these findings by offering a visual representation of the genotypic variability and the key traits driving these differences [51]. The PCA biplot revealed that genotypes like TM-230-VE-7-5-1 and TM-230-VE-8-4-1, which were positioned positively on the first principal component (PC1), were strongly associated with higher grain yield metrics such as GMP and MP. This association indicates their superior performance under both stress and non-stress conditions, making them ideal candidates for cultivation in diverse environments [53]. Conversely, genotypes like Hashemi and Khazar, positioned negatively on PC1, reflect different trait profiles, likely due to their higher susceptibility to stress. The prominence of vectors for variables like GMP, HARM, and MP in the biplot underscores their critical role in genotype differentiation, highlighting these traits as key indicators of drought resilience [54].

The negative association of stress susceptibility (SSI) with yield-related traits, as indicated by the PCA, further highlights the trade-offs in genotype performance under drought conditions [15]. Genotypes with higher SSI values, such as Hashemi and Khazar, tended to experience greater yield reductions under stress, reflecting their inability to cope with adverse conditions effectively [11]. On the other hand, genotypes with lower SSI values, like TM-230-VE-7-5-1, show a more balanced performance, maintaining high yields despite the stress. This balance between stress susceptibility and yield maintenance is crucial for developing rice varieties that can thrive in drought-prone regions, where water availability is often unpredictable [52].

The integration of these analyses—trait evaluation, cluster analysis, drought tolerance indices, and PCA—provided a comprehensive understanding of the genotypic responses to drought stress, highlighting the strengths and weaknesses of each genotype [55]. The consistent performance of genotypes like TM-230-VE-7-5-1, TM-B-2-1-E, and HM-250-E-1-1 across these analyses suggests that they possess a combination of genetic traits that confer superior drought tolerance, making them prime candidates for breeding programs aimed at improving rice productivity under water-limited conditions. These genotypes not only maintain higher yields under stress but also exhibit stability in key agronomic traits, which is essential for ensuring food security in regions affected by climate change and increasing water scarcity [3, 18].

Conversely, the poor performance of traditional varieties like Khazar, Hashemi, and Gilaneh underlines the urgent need for genetic enhancement. These varieties, while performing well under optimal conditions, are highly susceptible to drought, as indicated by their significant yield reductions and poor rankings in drought tolerance indices [11]. The development of new rice varieties that incorporate the drought-resistant traits of the mutant lines, combined with the desirable qualities of traditional varieties, could offer a solution to the challenges posed by climate change [2]. This approach would ensure that rice production remains stable even in the face of increasing environmental stress, contributing to global food security [15, 52].

Conclusion

This study aimed to evaluate the drought tolerance and agronomic performance of traditional rice parental lines and their corresponding mutant genotypes under prolonged drought stress. By analyzing comprehensive agronomic traits and applying multiple drought tolerance indices, we identified mutant lines, particularly TM-230-VE-7-5-1 and TM-B-2-1-E, that demonstrated enhanced resilience and maintained higher yields under drought conditions. These findings suggest that mutation breeding can effectively introduce drought tolerance traits while sustaining high yield potential.

Our results contribute valuable insights into breeding strategies for developing drought-tolerant rice varieties, which is crucial for food security in drought-prone regions. However, the study was conducted in a specific environmental context, and further multi-location trials are recommended to validate the performance of these mutant lines across diverse conditions. Future research should incorporate detailed phenological measurements, including days to flowering and maturity, to fully elucidate the mechanisms of drought tolerance in rice genotypes. Integrating phenological data with physiological, molecular, and agronomic assessments will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the strategies employed by different genotypes to cope with drought stress.

In conclusion, the identified mutant genotypes hold significant promise for enhancing rice resilience to drought, offering a practical pathway toward sustainable rice production amid the challenges posed by climate change.
