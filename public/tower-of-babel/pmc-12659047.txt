Responses of soil nutrients, enzyme activities, and soil microbial carbon metabolic activity of Cunninghamia lanceolata to acid rain stress and post-stress recovery dynamics
Authors: Xuanran Yu, Chong Li, Xingyu Zhang, Hui Nie, Jingyi Zeng, Jie Lin, Xin Liu, Jinchi Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcid rain pollution has historically been a serious issue in Southern China, where the dominant acid rain anion is SO42−. In recent decades, China has undertaken multiple measures to reduce acid deposition, leading to significant decreases in SO2 emissions year on year. However, understanding ecological effects during and after acid rain remains important, especially for other rapidly industrializing regions worldwide. Therefore, it is important to study changes in the ecological effects between acid rain deposition periods and the absence of acid rain. For this study, we designed a one-year simulated acid rain experiment. Subsequently, after the simulated acid rain was stopped, we conducted a nine-month continuous monitoring of the relevant experimental plots. We investigated soil nutrients, soil enzyme activities, and soil microbial carbon metabolic activity. The results revealed that acid rain had no significant effects on TN, AP, AK; however, it was difficult to return soil nutrients to their original status once the simulated acid rain was stopped. In addition, the acid rain treatments decreased the activities of soil enzymes, particularly under the high intensity acid rain treatment. Soil enzyme activities (except for sucrase activity under the high intensity acid rain treatment) reverted to their original status once the acid rain was stopped. Further, the moderate and high intensity acid rain treatments negatively affected the activities of soil microbial carbon metabolic. When the simulated acid rain was stopped for the CK treatment, soil microbial carbon metabolic activity recovered over time. During the simulated acid rain treatment and its cessation, four main categories (carbohydrates, carboxylic acids, amino acids, and polymers) were dominantly utilized. The utilization of L-Asparagine and Tween40 resulted in changes in the peak values of the C substrate groups during the non-acid rain period. Finally, the soil enzyme activities were intimately associated with the utilization of carbohydrates, carboxylic acid, amino acids, and phenols. Our results suggested that short-term acid rain contamination showed potential for recovery, but the process occurred over an extended time period.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12862-025-02466-9.Keywords: Acid rain, Soil nutrient, Soil enzyme activity, Acid rain and non-acid rain periods

Introduction

Pollution via acid rain is a serious global scale ecological challenge [1], as the areas affected by acid rain continue to increase due to worldwide industrialization, as well as economic and population growth [2]. Historically, China experienced severe acid rain following Europe and North America [3–5], particularly in Southern China [6] where the dominant anion was SO42− [7, 8] due to significant SO2 and NOX emissions [9, 10]. Recent strict emission controls have substantially reduced acid deposition, yet lessons from China remain highly relevant to regions facing similar industrialization pressures.

Acid rain has resulted in myriad ecological consequences, with plants being the first species to be affected [11]. Acid rain affects plant growth [12], photosynthesis [2, 13], litter decomposition [14], and alters antioxidant metabolism [15]. However, soil is the ultimate recipient of acid rain; thus, the underground components of ecosystems are likely more sensitive to acid rain than the surface components [16]. Moreover, plant growth also depends on the supply of nutrients and water from the soil [5, 17]. Soil microbial activity may be directly impacted by increased H+ input to soil brought on by acid deposition [18]. Further, base cations (such as K+, Mg2+, and Ca2+) will compete with H+ from acid deposition for replacement, increasing base cation leaching and reducing soil buffering capacity. Soil buffering capacity, often associated with “soil alkalinity”, is primarily determined by carbonate, bicarbonate, and hydroxide compounds that neutralize exogenous H⁺— higher alkalinity slows pH decline and mitigates acid-induced nutrient loss [19, 20].Soil enzyme activities affected by acid deposition [21], and also catalyze the biogeochemical processes in soil [22]. Soil nutrients and enzyme activities are indicators that can be employed to evaluate soil quality [14, 23], which are also a direct expression of soil microbial communities to metabolic requirements [24, 25]. Therefore, it is important to investigate and quantify changes in these indicators.

At present, China has undertaken multiple measures to minimize sulfur oxides in the air, including the closing of high-emission factories [4], development of cleaner production technologies [26], and limiting of industrial emissions [8], which have significantly reduced the deposition of SO2 year on year [4, 21]. This trend shows that the biological recovery of soil ecosystems from acid deposition is about to enter a critical phase. However, many studies focused on the effects of acid rain. It was unknown how the soils would react once the acid rain stopped, in particular how the soil nutrients, enzyme activity, and microbial diversity would change. To explore the responses of soils to acid rain and the absence thereof, we established a series of experiments to measure these indices. These findings will help inform subsequent research on the ecosystem recovery after acid rain while also providing fundamental information for both of these states. Furthermore, the study will provide environmental response experience for countries and regions in the process of industrialization.

Materials and methods

Study site and experimental treatment

The study site was located at the Nanjing Tongshan Forest Farm (31°37′N, 118°51′E), and the 12 random experimental plots were located in a pure Cunninghamia lanceolata plantation. The climate is subtropical monsoonal, with an average annual temperature of 16.5 °C. The total number of hours of sunshine each year is 2199.5. The summer rainy season lasts from June to August, and the average annual precipitation was 1117.2 mm. The annual average pH value of precipitation is 5.15 [27], and the annual frequency of acid rain (the sum of all acid rain events divided by the sum of all rainfall events) is roughly 55.8% [21]. We selected three acid rain intensities: SA4.5 (pH 4.5), SA3.5 (pH 3.5), SA2.5 (pH 2.5), and CK (pH 6), with three replicates for every treatment. The simulated acid rain consisted of 0.5 mol L− 1 H2SO4 and 0.5 mol L− 1 HNO3, where the molar ratio of H2SO4 to HNO3 was 5:1, which was based on the composition of rainfall in Nanjing [5, 21].During the four seasons of spring (March-May), summer (June-August), autumn (September-November), and winter (December–February), the acid rain solution was sprinkled on each plot twice a month. The monthly proportions of the simulated acid rain, which totaled 62.07 mm, were based on the monthly precipitation in the past two decades. Based on the ratios of average monthly precipitation to annual precipitation, 5.55% of the precipitation amount was applied to each plot. Spring, summer, autumn, and winter’s quantities were respectively 12.79 mm (20.62% of total amounts), 32.35 mm (52.11%), 9.35 mm (15.08%), and 7.57 mm (12.19%). The simulated acid rain treatment will be terminated after one year, followed by a 9-month continuous monitoring of the experimental plots.

Soil Samples were collected 3 times during the 1-year simulated acid rain period, once every 4 months, abbreviated as B1, B2, and B3; and 3 times during the 9-month plot monitoring period, once every 3 months, abbreviated as M1, M2, and M3. Soil samples were taken from the 0–10 cm soil depth layer after the surface litter and humus layers had been removed. After that, they were placed inside a transportable, chilled box and brought straight to the lab, where they were sieved through a 2 mm sieve to get rid of any leaves, plant roots, pebbles, and stones. For the purpose of analyzing the soil nutrient and soil enzyme activity, a portion of the soil samples were air-dried, and a different fraction was examined using the Biolog Eco technique.

Soil nutrient and soil enzyme activity analysis

The soil pH was determined using a PB-10 pH meter (Sartorius GmbH, Göttingen, Germany) after agitating for 1 h [5]. Once the soil samples were ground further, the TN was determined using an elemental analyzer (Vario EL III, ELementar, Germany). The AP was determined using the molybdate-blue colorimetric method [28], whereas the AK was determined using a flame photometer [5]. The soil urease activity was determined by measuring the quantity of ammonium released from the soil [29]. The soil phosphatase activity was measured using the phenyl phosphate disodium colorimetric method [30], whereas the soil sucrase activity was determined via 3,5-diyl salicylic acid colorimetry [31].

Biolog-ECO analysis

The capacity of soil microbial communities to utilize carbon sources was determined by the Biolog-ECO method. The steps in the analysis were as follows: Ten grams of dry dirt worth of fresh soil samples were placed in 150 mL triangle flasks. The bottles were filled with a total of 100 mL of sterile, ultra-pure water, sealed with sealing film, and shaken for 30 min at 250 rpm on a shaking bed. To obtain the soil bacterial suspension, the bottles were shaken and then allowed to stand for 15 min. On a very clean work surface, the suspension was diluted 103 times with sterile water before being added in 125 L portions to the Biolog Eco ecological plate. Following inoculation, the Eco plate was kept in an incubator for 10 days (25 °C) of continuous culture while the light absorption value (wavelength 590 nm) was recorded every 24 h. After 240 h, the average well color development (AWCD) values were continuously rising. Therefore, the OD value at 240 h was utilized to gauge how well soil microbes were utilizing carbon sources. The specific equation of Shannon and Simpson diversity referred to previous study.

Data analysis

We employed one-way ANOVA (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Ill., USA) to examine the impacts of acid rain treatments and the absence of acid rain on soil nutrients, soil enzyme activities, and soil microbial carbon metabolic activity. A heatmap of the 31 carbon sources was created by the Heml 1.0 (http://hemi.biocuckoo.org/). Correlation analysis was performed by R to elucidate the correlations between soil nutrients, enzyme activities, and six C groups, and additional charts were created by Origin 2015 (OriginLab Corporation, USA).

Results

Soil nutrients

During the simulated acid rain period, the soil pH of each of the acid rain treatments was significantly lower than that of the CK treatment (p < 0.05), where lower pH resulted in higher acidity (Fig. 1A). When the acid rain was stopped, there was no significant difference between SA4.5 and CK in M1, and there were no significant differences between SA3.5, SA4.5, and CK in M2. In addition, there were no significant differences between all acid rain treatments in M3.

For the soil TN and AP content (Fig. 1B, C), there were no significant differences between all acid rain treatments in B1 and B3. In the absence of acid rain, the soil TN and AP contents of the SA3.5, SA2.5 were significantly lower than for the CK. However, for TN (p < 0.05), there was no significant difference between the SA4.5 and CK in M3. The soil AK content of the SA4.5 treatment was higher than CK in B2 and B3 (Fig. 1D). When the addition of acid rain was stopped, the soil AK contents of the SA3.5 and SA2.5 treatments were significantly lower than the CK treatment (p < 0.05).

Meanwhile, for soil pH (Fig. 1A), within the CK treatment, no significant differences were observed among B1, B2, and B3 (p > 0.05); however, in SA2.5, pH decreased significantly from B1 (4.8) to B3 (4.2) (p < 0.05), and recovered to 4.7 by M3. For AP (Fig. 1C), SA3.5 showed a continuous decline from B1 (3.2 mg/kg) to M1 (2.1 mg/kg) (p < 0.05), and no recovery was observed by M3.

Soil enzyme activity

The pattern of soil urease activity was similar to the soil pH between acid rain treatments (Fig. 2A). When the acid rain was halted, there were no significant differences between all of the acid rain treatments in M2 and M3. The soil acid phosphatase activity in the SA2.5 treatment was significantly lower than that of the CK treatment during acid rain treatment period (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2B), and soil acid phosphatase activity of the SA4.5 treatment was significantly higher than those of the other treatments in B3 (p < 0.05). When the acid rain treatments were stopped, the soil acid phosphatase activity for the SA2.5 treatment was significantly lower than that for the other treatments in M1 and M2(p < 0.05). However, there were no significant differences between all treatments.

For soil sucrose activities, there were no significant differences between the SA4.5 and CK treatments during the acid rain treatment period (Fig. 2C), where the soil sucrose activities in the SA2.5 and SA3.5 were significantly lower than that for the CK treatments (p < 0.05). The AWCD values of the different treatments increased with longer cultural time. During the acid rain treatment period, the AWCD values for all treatments were lower than for CK in B1 (Fig. 3A). When the acid rain was stopped, the AWCD value for the SA2.5 treatment was lower than for the CK in M1 (Fig. 3D). However, the AWCD values for all acid rain treatments were higher than for CK in M2 and M3 (Fig. 3E, F), particularly the SA3.5 treatment.

Discussion

The final recipient of acid rain is soil, and soil acidity is directly reflected in soil pH. In this investigation, the pH of the soil dropped after acid deposition, particularly in the high intensity (pH = 2.5) treatment; however, the pH of the soil progressively increased once the acid rain was stopped. This might have been caused by the soil’s acid-buffering capacity, which is generally referred to as “soil alkalinity“ [4, 34, 35]. This capacity primarily relies on compounds such as carbonates, bicarbonates, and hydroxides to neutralize introduced acidity, and it also contributes to eliminating larger H+ ion inputs [36]. As a crucial factor for maintaining stable soil conditions conducive to plant growth, soil alkalinity directly influences the rate and magnitude of pH changes: higher alkalinity means a slower and smaller shift in soil pH when acids are introduced. Moreover, acid rain might increase the soil TN, AP, and AK contents [4, 5]; however, in this study, the effects of acid rain were weakened, as acid rain enriched with N may have served as a fertilizer [37]. Another factor might have been the leaching of P during the acid rain treatments [38]. When the acid rain treatments were stopped, the exogenous N disappeared. It was difficult to recover from the inhibitory effects of high and moderate intensity acid rain (pH = 2.5, 3.5). However, for weakly acidic (pH = 4.5) treatments, the TN content could gradually recover, where the trend for the AK was similar to TN. Moreover, the AP content did not recover to its original state, which indicated that the P has been leached and Pearson’s correlation analysis also verified this conclusion.

The results showed that high and moderate intensity treatments considerably decreased the enzyme activity in the soil. The acid phosphatase activity did, however, rise in response to the modest acidity therapy. This phenomena might have been connected to the control of soil nitrogen absorption [39], and Pearson’s correlation analysis also confirmed this conclusion. Urease and acid phosphatase returned to their previous levels when the acid rain treatments were stopped, however sucrase activity did not. This was due to the high intensity acid rain. In contrast to the other enzymes examined in this work, this result showed that the sucrase activity was significantly more responsive to high intensity acid rain treatments.

Soil enzyme activities are commonly interpreted as indicators of the activities of soil microbes [40, 41]. AWCD values may also be used to indicate the activities of soil microbes [42]. Soil microbial activities can either be reduced [43], increased [44], or unaffected [45]under the deposition of acid rain. In this study, the application of acid rain initially reduced soil microbial carbon metabolic activity. This might be because the pH dropped quickly, limiting the growth of soil microbes [4]. Additionally, alterations in root exudates brought on by acid rain may potentially account for a decline in soil microbial activity [46]. As acid rain continues to be deposited, however, modest acid loading can encourage soil microbial activity and soil microbial carbon metabolic activity. This scenario occurs when the benefits of S and N fertilizer outweigh the drawbacks of a fast declining pH [46].

Soil microbial carbon metabolic activity rose with longer recovery durations after the acid rain ended, surpassing the control in M3 and reaching their greatest levels for the moderate intensity acid rain treatment. This could have been due to the fact that the levels of S and N gradually dropped off after the acid rain treatments were terminated, with the SA3.5 treatment having higher levels than the SA4.5 treatment, further supporting the aforementioned conclusion. Following the end of acid rain, the soil microbial carbon metabolic activity was successfully recovered overall.

AWCD values may also be employed to indicate the capacity of soil microorganisms to utilize various carbon sources [47]. The utilization of six C groups were altered due to changing environmental conditions [48, 49]. Acid rain can primarily alter the utilization of carbohydrates, carboxylic acids, and amino acids [46]. For this study, the carbon resources utilized by soil microbes were primarily carbohydrates, carboxylic acid, amino acids, and polymers for the acid rain treatments and their cessation. The acid rain treatments changed not only the six C groups, but also the 31 specific carbon sources. During the acid rain treatment period, the main 31 specific carbon resources used by soil microbes always changed. However, the utilization of L-Asparagine, and Tween40, resulted in changes in the peak values of the C substrate groups once the acid rain was halted, and the utilization of other specific carbon sources also always changed. These results are not similar to previous studies [4], which may have been because various tree species grew under different soil conditions; thus, different microbial communities evolved [50].

Correlation analysis showed that the consumption of specific C groups was closely connected to the activities of soil enzymes, AP, and AK, but not to the utilization of the other six C groups. It was shown that although acid rain does not directly alter the consumption of carbon sources by reducing acidity, it does so indirectly by affecting the nutrients and enzyme activity that are present in the soil. These outcomes agreed with the findings of other investigations [51].

It should be noted that the simulated acid rain treatments proceeded for only one year, and were monitored for only nine months following the cessation of the treatments. The AWCD under the SA3.5 treatment was highest when the acid rain treatments stopped. However, if the recovery experiment were to continue for another year or two, the AWCD under the SA2.5 treatment might have been higher than those of the other treatments. Therefore, new and longer experiments should be designed to comprehensively investigate the effects and aftereffects of acid rain treatments. It is also noteworthy that the research findings hold certain reference significance for regions around the world currently undergoing rapid industrialization, while in most areas of modern China over the past two years, such situations have been gradually improved.

Conclusion

During the acid rain treatment period we observed decreased soil enzyme activities, particularly under the high intensity acid rain treatment, which also reduced the AK content. Following the cessation of acid rain, the soil nutrients did not revert to their original status; however, the soil enzyme activities (except for the sucrase activity under the high intensity acid rain treatment) returned to their original states. The moderate and high intensity acid rain treatments also negatively affected the activities of soil microbial carbon metabolic; however, the impacts of the weak intensity acid rain treatments were opposite. The soil microbial activities managed to recover over time, once the acid rain was stopped, particularly from the moderate intensity acid rain treatments. The main carbon resources used by soil microbes were primarily carbohydrates, carboxylic acid, amino acids, and polymers during the acid rain treatments and once they were stopped. The activities of soil enzymes were closely related to the utilization of carbohydrates, carboxylic acid, amino acids, and phenols. In summary, short-term acid rain pollution has the potential to be remedied, albeit over a significant timeline.
