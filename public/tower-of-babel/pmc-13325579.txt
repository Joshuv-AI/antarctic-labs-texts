A human–AI collaborative workflow for improved gross tumor volume delineation in esophageal cancer radiotherapy
Authors: Hongfei Sun, Jie Li, Yufen Liu, Fan Meng, Ziqi An, Yixin Cui, Bo Lv, Guangjin Chai, Lecheng Jia, Zihan Shi, Meixi Liu, Bingqi Zhu, Jie Gong, Lina Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEsophageal cancer tumors exhibit complex and variable distribution. Due to differences in clinical experience, junior oncologists often show less accuracy in gross tumor volume (GTV) delineation compared to their senior counterparts. Deep learning methods have the potential to assist junior oncologists in improving the accuracy of GTV delineation. This study proposes a human–AI collaborative workflow for esophageal cancer radiotherapy and explores its clinical feasibility.MethodsA retrospective collection of 730 esophageal cancer radiotherapy cases from our center was divided into training and testing sets at a 4:1 ratio. The human–AI collaborative workflow involves the following steps: (1) developing an automatic GTV delineation model based on planning CT images and obtaining an AI-prompt; (2) junior oncologists manually refining the GTVs generated by the model to serve as human-guided prompt; (3) using both prompts in combination with planning CT images to build two automatic refinement models; and (4) junior oncologists comparing the refined GTVs obtained by the above models, ultimately submitting the finalized GTVs for expert review by senior oncologists. Three junior oncologists from our center independently completed the manual tasks in steps 2 and 4, and the GTVs derived from the new approach were compared for accuracy against the ground truth delineated by senior oncologists.ResultsThe results from the testing set indicated that after manual refinement by the three junior oncologists, the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) between the automatic GTV delineation (step 1) and the ground truth increased from an initial value of 0.6538 ± 0.2022 to 0.7109 ± 0.1958, 0.7253 ± 0.1632, and 0.7236 ± 0.1965. Furthermore, by combining manual refinement, automatic refinement, and decision-making, the accuracy of the GTV delineation further improved to 0.7552 ± 0.1366, 0.7671 ± 0.1190, and 0.7757 ± 0.1354. These results demonstrate that the new approach significantly enhances the accuracy and stability of esophageal cancer GTV delineation.ConclusionWith the support of the human–AI collaborative workflow, junior oncologists can delineate esophageal cancer GTV contours with improved accuracy, providing a reliable foundation for the integration of AI in clinical radiotherapy. The study also demonstrates that, in the current trend toward radiotherapy automation, clinical oncologists remain an indispensable and vital part of the process.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02400-5.Keywords: Automatic GTV delineation, Automatic refinement, Esophageal cancer

Introduction

Esophageal cancer is a common malignant tumor of the digestive tract with persistently high incidence and mortality rates. In China, it ranks sixth in incidence and fifth in mortality among all cancers, and remains within the top five cancers in men for both metrics [1, 2]. Based on comprehensive assessments of patients and tumor characteristics, multidisciplinary team (MDT)–based strategies are recommended to formulate optimal treatment plans and to make rational use of available modalities, thereby improving survival and treatment outcomes [3]. Among these modalities, radiotherapy plays an important role in MDT-based management of esophageal cancer. Accurate delineation of the gross tumor volume (GTV) is a critical step in radiotherapy, as its precision directly affects therapeutic efficacy [4–6]. However, due to the anatomical variability of tumor location and size, GTV delineation for esophageal cancer is often challenging. Furthermore, variations in clinical experience may lead to considerable inter-observer differences between junior and senior radiation oncologists, introducing subjectivity into manual contouring.

With the development of artificial intelligence (AI) in medicine, deep-learning-based automatic tumor delineation has gained increasing attention [7–10]. These models learn nonlinear mappings between medical images and manually delineated ground truths through retrospective training and can automatically generate tumor contours for new patients. Depending on the imaging modalities used, existing deep-learning methods for esophageal cancer GTV delineation mainly fall into two categories. First, models based on PET-CT [11, 12]. Jin et al. proposed a DeepTarget model with progressive semantically nested network (PSNN) and spatial distance encoding. Validated on 148 esophageal cancer cases, their method effectively captured spatial contextual features across PET and CT modalities, improving the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of GTV delineation by approximately 8.7% and reducing the 95th percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95) by about 32.9 mm compared with baseline models [13]. Building upon this work, Ye et al. further demonstrated the clinical feasibility of DeepTarget, achieving an average DSC of 0.83 on a 104-case validation set [14]. However, PET-CT is not routinely available in radiotherapy workflows, and its high cost and registration uncertainty limit the widespread clinical application of such dual-modality models. Second, models trained solely on planning CT [15, 16]. Planning CT is the standard imaging modality for radiotherapy planning and is universally accessible; thus, developing a high-precision single-modality GTV delineation model holds strong clinical relevance. Recent studies using U-Net, Transformer, and their variants as backbones have achieved mean DSC values around 0.70 for esophageal cancer GTV delineation [17, 18]. Yet, in low-contrast regions, such as tumor–esophageal wall or mediastinal interfaces, single-modality models often suffer from over- or under-delineation errors, limiting their direct clinical usability.

To further enhance geometric accuracy and boundary consistency of automatic delineation, deep-learning–based contour refinement strategies have recently emerged. These approaches stepwisely refine preliminary contours toward ground-truth [19, 20]. Unlike end-to-end networks, refinement frameworks use the coarse delineation as prior information to guide secondary correction. For instance, an active contour model (ACM) based automatic contour refinement (ACR) method improved the mean DSC of abdominal organs with major delineation errors from 0.34 to 0.59, and that of minor-error cases from 0.72 to 0.79 [21]. Following this concept, Ding et al. proposed a deep-learning ACR (DL-ACR) network using U-Net as the backbone for intestinal contouring. The pipeline first applied ACM-ACR to correct major errors, then used DL-ACR to further refine minor errors to clinically acceptable accuracy. In their test set, 44% of major-error contours were converted to minor errors and 5% to acceptable results; subsequently, DL-ACR upgraded 49% of minor-error contours to acceptable ones, substantially reducing manual workload [22]. These studies demonstrate that contour refinement can improve accuracy beyond traditional end-to-end learning, offering potential for esophageal cancer GTV delineation. Nonetheless, the gains achieved by fully automated refinement on single-modality CT remain limited. Incorporating human-corrected slices as interactive guidance into model training has been shown to further improve esophageal cancer GTV delineation accuracy by approximately 7.35% [23]. Similarly, Wang et al. introduced the DeepIGeoS framework, a multi-stage pipeline of “automatic coarse segmentation–manual correction–conditional refinement”, which achieved high accuracy for brain tumor delineation (DSC = 89.93 ± 6.49%) [24]. These findings suggest that human-guided refinement can outperform fully automatic refinement.

Taken together, we aimed to explore whether refinement strategies driven by human-interactive cues could improve deep-learning–based GTV delineation for esophageal cancer using single-modality planning CT. Inspired by the clinical workflow of “junior delineation–senior review”, we propose a novel human–AI collaborative workflow for planning-CT-based esophageal cancer GTV contouring in radiotherapy. The workflow adopts the widely used nnU-Net architecture as the foundation for both initial delineation and refinement, simulates real-world clinical interaction scenarios, and evaluates its practical effectiveness under various levels of junior physician intervention [25].

Materials and methods

Details of datasets

This retrospective study included 730 patients with esophageal cancer who underwent radiotherapy at Xijing Hospital. Among them, 587 cases were used for model development and 143 cases were reserved for testing. For each patient, planning CT scans were collected. All images were free from metallic or motion artifacts and were acquired using a Brilliance Big Bore CT scanner (Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) with a voxel spacing of 1 × 1 × 5 mm3 and image dimensions ranging from (480–500) × (480–500) × (83–102). The ground-truth GTV contours were independently reviewed by two senior radiation oncologists, each with more than 10 years of clinical experience in esophageal cancer radiotherapy. Delineation was performed in accordance with established clinical practice and relevant international guidelines, including NCCN recommendations and contouring principles derived from RTOG guidelines. In cases where discrepancies were identified, a consensus discussion was conducted between the two experts to reach agreement, and the finalized contours were used as the reference standard for model training and evaluation. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Xijing Hospital (Approval No. KY20253188-1).

Human–AI collaborative workflow for GTV delineation

Based solely on the planning CT images, a human–AI collaborative workflow was developed for GTV delineation in esophageal cancer radiotherapy. The central concept was to initiate from an automatic delineation and, through minimal human intervention, iteratively achieve contours that better approximate the expert-defined ground truth via a structured process of automatic delineation and prompt-guided refinement. The flow chart is shown in Fig. 1.

Human-AI collaborate workflow for esophageal cancer GTV delineation

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the human–AI collaborative workflow begins with an initial automatic delineation model that generates a preliminary GTV contour using the planning CT volume as the sole input. This initial contour serves as a common starting point for subsequent refinement. Following this step, two prompt-guided refinement pathways are executed in parallel. In the fully automatic pathway, the initial automatic contour is directly used as an AI prompt mask. In the collaborative pathway, a junior radiation oncologist manually refines the initial contour once to generate a human prompt mask. For each case, the same junior radiation oncologist who performed the manual refinement also conducted the subsequent case-level decision-making. Importantly, these two pathways differ only in the source of the prompt information and share the same refinement architecture. In the refinement stage, the planning CT volume is reintroduced and concatenated with either the AI prompt mask or the human prompt mask to form the input to the prompt-guided refinement model. This design ensures that both global anatomical context from the CT and localized guidance from the prompt mask jointly contribute to the refinement process. The refinement model produces two candidate GTVs corresponding to AI-guided and human-guided refinement, respectively. In the final decision-making stage, the junior oncologist performs a side-by-side comparison of these refined contours and selects the more anatomically plausible result, which is subsequently submitted for senior oncologist review and approval.

Model architecture and training details

Both the initial delineation and prompt-guided refinement stages in the proposed human–AI collaborative workflow were implemented using the self-configuring nnU-Net architecture [26]. No external or large-scale pre-training was performed in this study. All nnU-Net models were trained from scratch using only the esophageal cancer dataset collected at our center, and all network parameters were optimized exclusively on the training data. We adopted the 3D full-resolution nnU-Net configuration, which employs a U-Net–based encoder–decoder structure with skip connections. The architecture was selected because of its robustness, reproducibility, and prior validation in esophageal cancer GTV delineation [16]. The nnU-Net framework automatically adapts key hyperparameters to the dataset characteristics, including voxel spacing, resampling strategy and patch size. In our experiments, the batch size was configured as 2 and remained consistent across all experiments performed on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU (24 GB memory). For the initial delineation stage, the model input consisted of a single-channel planning CT volume. For the prompt-guided refinement stage, an additional channel containing the AI-generated or manually refined prompt mask was concatenated with the CT volume as input. The automatic refinement model shared the same network architecture and training hyperparameters as the initial delineation model. No architectural modifications or hyperparameter adjustments were introduced between the two stages. Image preprocessing followed the default nnU-Net pipeline, including B-spline interpolation for resampling, Z-score normalization, and extensive data augmentation.

Evaluation methods

Model performance was quantitatively evaluated by comparing the predicted GTVs against the reference manual contours using multiple complementary metrics [27–31]. Geometric accuracy was assessed through overlap-based indices, including the DSC and the Intersection over Union (IoU). To further capture boundary precision, surface distance–based metrics were computed, including the HD95 and the average symmetric surface distance (ASSD), both reflecting the contour proximity between automatic and manual delineations. Lower HD95 and ASSD values indicate improved surface alignment and reduced delineation error at tumor margins. Normalized Surface Dice (NSD) was additionally used to evaluate clinically acceptable boundary agreement, as it provides improved robustness to outlier surface deviations. All statistical analyses were performed using Python-based packages, with quantitative results reported as mean ± standard deviation. Comparative evaluations were conducted across different methods to determine whether the human-AI collaborative workflow significantly improved delineation accuracy. Statistical comparisons between methods were performed using paired t-tests, and corresponding p values and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Clinical interpretability was additionally considered by qualitatively inspecting representative slices to ensure the refined contours adhered to anatomical plausibility. For qualitative visualization, representative axial slices were selected based on an objective criterion: the slice corresponding to the largest cross-sectional tumor region in the ground-truth contour. This standardized selection approach was adopted to minimize potential selection bias and ensure anatomical representativeness.

Results

Patient characteristics

The esophageal cancer dataset used in this study was retrospectively collected from cases treated at Xijing Hospital between 2016 and 2022. The median ages in the training and testing cohorts were 67 and 66 years, respectively, with a higher proportion of male patients in both groups. Regarding tumor location, the distribution in the training cohort included 2.56% cervical, 18.06% upper thoracic, 37.31% middle thoracic, and 42.08% lower thoracic cases, a pattern that was similarly observed in the testing cohort, where mid- and lower-thoracic tumors predominated. The median tumor length was 5 cm in both datasets. In terms of tumor stage, T3 and T4 lesions accounted for the majority of cases in both cohorts. Comparison of automatic and manual delineation with manual refinement

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed method, we compared three automatic delineation models, manual delineation by junior oncologists, and manual refinement using nnU-Net delineated contours as prompts. Among the automatic delineation models, nnU-Net achieved a mean DSC of 0.6538 ± 0.2022, an IoU of 0.5174 ± 0.1961, an NSD of 0.5751 ± 0.1946, an HD95 of 15.50 ± 12.64 mm, and an ASSD of 4.19 ± 5.19 mm. The state-of-the-art architectures ECA-UNet and nnFormer demonstrated comparable delineation performance. However, nnU-Net demonstrated substantially higher computational efficiency, with an average inference time of approximately 15 seconds per case, compared to 47 seconds for ECA-UNet and 161 seconds for nnFormer. These results confirm that nnU-Net provides competitive delineation accuracy while maintaining substantially higher computational efficiency, supporting its selection as the backbone architecture in this study. Manual delineation by junior oncologists outperformed automatic delineation methods, but the margin was limited.

Manual refinement using nnU-Net delineated contours as prompts significantly improved accuracy compared with automatic or manual delineation alone. Mean DSC values increased to 0.7109, 0.7253, and 0.7236, corresponding to relative improvements of approximately 7% compared with the baseline nnU-Net results. IoU values similarly increased to 0.5795, 0.5901, and 0.5976, indicating improved volumetric agreement. Surface distance–based metrics also showed substantial improvements following prompt-guided refinement. NSD values increased compared with both automatic and manual delineation methods, reflecting improved boundary agreement within clinically acceptable tolerances. In addition, HD95 decreased to 7.98, 7.99, and 7.72 mm, representing approximately a 50% reduction compared with baseline automatic delineation. Similarly, ASSD decreased to 2.93, 2.59, and 2.70 mm, indicating improved boundary precision. Overall, these results demonstrate that while automatic and manual delineation provide competitive baseline performance, manual refinement incorporating AI-assisted input significantly enhances both accuracy and boundary consistency. Comparison of different workflow configurations for GTV delineation

After obtaining both AI- and human-prompt information, three primary workflow configurations were compared: (1) fully automated workflow, where the AI-generated contour was directly used as a prompt for automatic refinement; (2) human–AI semi-collaborative workflow, where three junior oncologists independently refined the AI contours, and their manually corrected contours were used as prompts for further automatic refinement; and (3) human–AI fully collaborative workflow, where the junior oncologist selected the superior contour between the fully automated and semi-collaborative outputs for each case, yielding the final submission. To further evaluate whether performance gains in the fully collaborative workflow were attributable to meaningful decision-making rather than arbitrary selection, an additional control condition was introduced. In this random selection workflow, one of the two refined contours (AI-guided or human-guided) was randomly chosen for each case, simulating a non-informed selection process. For overlap-based metrics, the fully automated workflow achieved mean DSC and IoU values of 0.7459 ± 0.1383 and 0.6157 ± 0.1497, respectively. When introducing manually refined prompts, the three semi-collaborative workflows yielded mean DSCs of 0.7127, 0.7277, and 0.7232, with corresponding IoUs of 0.5815, 0.5971, and 0.5958, which remained lower than the fully automated baseline. In contrast, the fully collaborative workflow demonstrated consistent improvements in overlap-based metrics. Mean DSC values increased to 0.7552, 0.7671, and 0.7757, with corresponding IoUs of 0.6226, 0.6345, and 0.6500, respectively. To assess whether these gains could be attributed solely to selection effects, the random selection workflow was evaluated. This workflow achieved intermediate performance, with mean DSC values of 0.7303, 0.7400, and 0.7417, and IoUs of 0.5979, 0.6055, and 0.6112. Notably, the fully collaborative workflow consistently outperformed random selection across overlap-based metrics, confirming that human-guided selection provides meaningful improvements beyond arbitrary choice. A similar pattern was observed for surface distance–based metrics. The fully automated workflow achieved a mean NSD of 0.6685 ± 0.1540, which increased to 0.6854, 0.6897, and 0.6992 in the fully collaborative workflow, compared with intermediate values of 0.6548, 0.6619, and 0.6627 in the random selection control. Likewise, HD95 values decreased to 6.47 ± 10.06, 6.03 ± 8.09, and 5.54 ± 7.95 mm, while ASSD values were reduced to 2.18 ± 4.10, 2.04 ± 3.66, and 1.95 ± 4.01 mm. These improvements in surface-based metrics further support the effectiveness of human-guided refinement in enhancing boundary accuracy.

Notably, the “AI reference rate”—defined as the proportion of cases where the junior oncologist selected the fully automated result—was 30%, 31%, and 34% for the three raters, respectively. This indicates that in approximately two-thirds of the cases, human-guided refinement provided superior anatomical plausibility, while the automated output remained preferable in the remaining cases. To further characterize the tumor features associated with clinician preference for fully automated results, a stratified analysis was performed based on tumor anatomical location and T stage (Table 4). The results showed that AI reference cases were predominantly observed in advanced-stage tumors. Specifically, T3 and T4 tumors accounted for the majority of AI reference cases, representing approximately 81% (35/43), 84% (38/45), and 86% (42/49) of AI reference selections for the three junior oncologists, respectively. This finding may reflect the increased complexity of advanced-stage tumors, where junior oncologists may have greater uncertainty in defining tumor boundaries. In such cases, the fully automated workflow model may provide consistent structural guidance, leading clinicians to preferentially accept the automated result rather than perform additional manual refinement. With respect to tumor location, AI reference cases were most frequently associated with tumors located in the lower esophagus, which accounted for the largest proportion across all raters. For tumors located in the lower esophagus, junior oncologists may have greater uncertainty when attempting manual refinement due to limited clinical experience and therefore may be more inclined to accept the automated contour if it appears anatomically reasonable. These findings suggest that clinician experience plays an important role in determining when human–AI collaboration provides additional benefit, and highlight the potential of AI-assisted delineation to support less experienced clinicians in routine clinical practice.

Time cost analysis of different delineation methods

To evaluate the efficiency and clinical feasibility of the proposed collaborative workflow, the average delineation time per case was measured across the 143-case test cohort and compared among manual delineation, fully automated delineation, and the human–AI fully collaborative workflow (Table 5). The manual delineation required an average of 13.0, 12.5, and 11.0 minutes per case for Junior oncologists A, B, and C, respectively. In comparison, the human–AI fully collaborative workflow reduced the average time to 6.0, 5.0, and 4.5 minutes per case, corresponding to a reduction of more than 50% relative to manual delineation. The fully automated workflow required only 0.5 minutes per case, reflecting its computational efficiency. However, as demonstrated in Table 3, the human–AI fully collaborative workflow achieved substantially higher accuracy compared to the fully automated approach. These findings indicate that the human–AI fully collaborative workflow significantly improves delineation efficiency while maintaining higher accuracy, supporting its practical applicability in routine clinical radiotherapy practice.

Intra-group comparison based on the human–AI collaborative workflow

To further evaluate whether the proposed human–AI collaborative workflow could improve the delineation performance of junior oncologist, intra-group comparisons were conducted between the first and second manual refinements, separated by a one-month interval. Supplementary Fig. S1 and S2 present paired comparisons of DSC and HD95 among the AI delineation, first refinement, and second refinement. Scatterplots demonstrate that for most cases, both manual refinements improved accuracy relative to the AI baseline. However, a few inverse outliers (cases with decreased DSC or increased HD95) were also observed, indicating limited benefit from human–AI collaborative workflow in certain complex cases.

Supplementary Fig. S3 further illustrates direct comparisons between the first and second manual refinements. Quantitatively, the second refinement achieved a higher mean DSC of 0.7571 ± 0.1368 compared to 0.7109 ± 0.1958 after the first refinement, while HD95 decreased from 7.98 ± 10.47 mm to 5.77 ± 8.55 mm. Overall, both accuracy and stability improved following exposure to the human–AI collaborative workflow. Difference maps revealed that in most cases, ΔDSC values were positive (105/143), and ΔHD95 values were negative (79/143), confirming general improvement trends. These findings suggest that the human–AI collaborative workflow can enhance the overall contouring capability of junior oncologist. Nevertheless, its benefit remains limited for particularly challenging cases, in which expert supervision and final review remain indispensable.

Discussion

This study proposes and validates a human–AI collaborative workflow for GTV delineation in esophageal cancer radiotherapy, comprising the steps of “automatic delineation → prompt guidance → automatic refinement → decision-making → expert review.” Compared with traditional end-to-end automatic delineation models, our results demonstrate that introducing dual prompts and individualized decision-making can effectively compensate for the limitations of automatic models in identifying low-contrast, elongated tumor boundaries, leading to more accurate outputs. More importantly, this process retains the clinician’s leading role: with AI-assisted automatic delineation, junior oncologists are able to focus on challenging regions, and, by integrating AI results with clinical experience, make comprehensive judgments on the automatic refinement results based on different prompts to generate GTV contours that better meet clinical standards.

We assessed the usability and improvement pathway of automatic GTV delineation for esophageal cancer using the single-modality planning CT within a human–AI collaborative framework. Even with the highly adaptable nnU-Net model, which showed promising self-configuration capabilities, the model still exhibited insufficient accuracy and unstable boundary determination for esophageal cancer GTV. Its automatic delineation performance did not meet the standards required for clinical radiotherapy applications. Additionally, we observed that junior oncologists struggled to accurately identify issues such as over-delineation and under-delineation in some cases. In these uncertain regions, they might inadvertently perpetuate or amplify the model’s errors, leading to recurring delineation issues in subsequent automatic refinement steps, or even model degradation.

To address these challenges, we proposed a “prompt guidance and decision-making” paradigm. On one hand, additional prompt guidance was incorporated into the automatic delineation model, enabling directed learning at low-confidence boundaries rather than uniform learning. On the other hand, at the workflow level, we preserved both the manual refinement and automatic delineation results, allowing for case-by-case decision-making, which reduced error correction rates and avoided the risks of mis-correction associated with a single path. The results also indicate that when automatic delineation, manual refinement, automatic refinement, and oncologist interpretation are integrated, the delineated GTVs are closer to the ground truth. More importantly, the human–AI collaborative workflow demonstrates that junior oncologists are not replaceable “outer rings,” but rather, an integral part of the process as the “inner ring.” By interacting effectively with AI models, they are better able to control the axial range, wall interface, and longitudinal continuity of the GTV, which enhances the overall stability and accuracy of their delineation.

The intra-group comparison of GTV delineation performed by a single junior oncologist on two separate refinements further supports the potential of the human–AI collaborative workflow to enhance the delineation skills of junior oncologists. Junior oncologists primarily addressed apparent issues such as longitudinal under- or over-extension and noticeable boundary shifts during their first refinement. The human–AI collaborative workflow helped them achieve a clearer understanding of the target region’s finer details, resulting in measurable improvements in most cases after one month. While some cases showed reverse effects in the second round of refinement, the differences in DSC values remained within 0.1, indicating that these differences were not statistically significant. Moreover, the results also underscore the need to avoid excessive modification during manual refinement, as over-adjustment may lead to errors. A comprehensive assessment should incorporate patient clinical data, imaging information, and AI-derived contours, with collaborative input when necessary to reduce the risk of mis-delineation.

While this study has preliminarily demonstrated the effectiveness of the human–AI collaborative workflow for GTV delineation in esophageal cancer, several limitations remain. First, the study was based on single-center retrospective data, lacking external validation from other centers. Second, only planning CT single-modality imaging was considered, without further investigation into the application of the human–AI workflow in multi-modality PET/CT for GTV delineation in esophageal cancer. Third, the study only involved automatic delineation models based on imaging data, excluding other diagnostic information such as text. As a result, the contribution of the model within the workflow is somewhat limited. Future research will expand the dataset for multi-center external testing and prospective validation, incorporating multi-modality PET/CT imaging and large language models (LLMs) to further enhance the accuracy of automatic delineation models. This will ensure that the human–AI collaborative workflow can be more effectively applied to esophageal cancer radiotherapy, and gradually extended to other cancer types [32, 33]. Fourth, recent studies have highlighted the value of incorporating clinically meaningful priors into medical imaging AI. For example, Zhang et al. proposed an adaptive dual-axis style-based recalibration framework using global and pixel-wise clinical style information, while a related study introduced a region-based integration-and-recalibration attention mechanism to emphasize clinically informative regional representations [34, 35]. These studies reflect a broader trend toward clinically guided feature modulation in medical AI, which may further improve the robustness of human–AI collaborative GTV delineation in future work. Fifth, qualitative evaluation based on representative axial slices may not fully reflect longitudinal performance, which will be addressed by future volumetric assessment. Finally, it should be noted that the observed performance improvement after a one-month interval may be partially influenced by recall bias, as the junior oncologist was exposed to the same set of cases during the first refinement. Although the interval duration and the relatively large number of cases reduce the likelihood of precise case-level memory, we cannot fully exclude residual familiarity effects. Future prospective studies with independent case cohorts or randomized case orders would be valuable to more definitively distinguish genuine skill enhancement from potential memory-related effects.

Conclusion

This study proposes a human–AI collaborative workflow for esophageal cancer GTV delineation based on planning CT. Through an interactive paradigm of automatic delineation, manual refinement, automatic refinement, and oncologist interpretation, this workflow addresses the labor-intensive nature of traditional manual delineation, while overcoming the limitations of fully automated GTV delineation models that are not directly applicable to radiotherapy. Furthermore, it provides a novel approach for the integration of AI models into clinical workflows for tumor delineation.
