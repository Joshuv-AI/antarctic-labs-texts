Cloning Quantum Channels
Pavel Sekatski, Yelena Guryanova, Naga Bhavya Teja Kothakonda, Michalis Skotiniotis

Abstract
We consider the problem of deterministically cloning quantum channels with respect to the best attainable rate and the highest quality, so-called optimal cloning . We demonstrate that cloning quantum states is, in-fact, equivalent to cloning the trash-and-replace channel and therefore the former is a special case of the more general problem. By appealing to higher-order quantum operations (quantum processes) we construct a unified framework to deal with the most general cloning tasks and establish necessary conditions for a family of channels to exhibit super-replication—a quadratic cloning rate with vanishing error. We find that noisy phase-gate channels satisfy these conditions, and we construct the explicit super-replicating process for the task. Conversely, we find that the criteria are not met by the full set of noisy unitary gates; classical noise channels; or amplitude damping channels, whose respective cloning rates are at most linear. In this paradigm, we not only derive new results, but also refigure known ones. We derive a strong converse for state cloning, and for unitary channels we construct an alternative super-replication process to that of Dür et al. Dür et al. 2015 and Chiribella et al. Chiribella et al. 2015 based on a measure-and-prepare process, which allows us to establish a direct connection between optimal channel cloning and Bayesian channel estimation. Finally we give an SDP algorithm to search for optimal cloning processes and study the advantage of coherent vs measure-and-prepare protocols on concrete examples.

The

Given access to
N

ideal copies of the same resource. The performance of such a map depends crucially on how the quality of the

;

;

II.1
Mathematical Preliminaries and Notation

Let us begin by establishing some important definitions and notation.
Quantities (particularly distances) written in sans-serif font will denote the optimized value of the function they pertain to.
The set of all linear operators acting on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. Density operators
in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
are written using Greek letters; uppercase Greek letters denote rank-one density operators,
i.e.,
\Psi=\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi},\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}
, while lowercase Greek letters will denote general density
operators. Positive operator valued measures (POVMs) are denoted as
\{{\rm E}_{\ell}\,|{\rm E}_{\ell}\geq 0\,,\sum_{\ell}{\rm E}_{\ell}=\mathds{1}\}
. Quantum channels are completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) linear maps
that take operators from
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
to
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
, and map density operators to density operators.
Quantum channels are indicated with calligraphic upper case Latin characters, i.e.,

\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\!\to\!\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
, apart from the identity channel
which is written

{\rm id}
. So as to avoid
notation clutter we shall use the short hand
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}_{2}):=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\!\to\!\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
.
Quantum instruments, denoted as
\{\mathcal{I}_{\ell}\}
, are collections of CP maps such that
\sum_{\ell}\mathcal{I}_{\ell}
is
also TP.

Central to the following discussion are superchannels
Chiribella et al. 2008b
;
Chiribella et al. 2008c
;
Chiribella et al. 2009
, also
known as processes or higher-order operations
(terms we will use interchangeably). These are linear maps
that take quantum channels from
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}_{2})
to
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm in}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}_{\rm out})
.
A process has three properties: it is trace preserving preserving (TPP) (transforms trace preserving maps to trace
preserving maps); it completely positive preserving (CPP)
(transforms completely positive maps to completely positive maps); it is completely completely positive preserving (CCPP) (the process remains CPP if the channels that it acts on are trivially extended).

We shall denote quantum processes using
boldface uppercase Latin characters
\mathbf{P}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}_{2})\!\to\!\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm in}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}_{\rm out})
. A specific case
({\rm\dim}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm in})=1)
are state producing process that map CPTP maps
to states and will be denoted with the uppercase Latin character

\mathbf{S}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}_{2})\!\to\!\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm out})
. We shall also consider processes which map channels
to quantum instruments, denoted as
\{\mathbf{M}_{\ell}\}
, where each

\mathbf{M}_{\ell}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}_{2})\!\to\!\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm in}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}_{\rm out})
maps CPTP maps to CP maps
and
\mathbf{M}=\sum_{\ell}\mathbf{M}_{\ell}
returns a TP map. Finally, a specific case of these superchannels

({\rm\dim}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm in})={\rm\dim}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm out})=1)
are measurement processes (also known as channel POVMs),

\{\mathbf{E}_{\ell}\,|\mathbf{E}_{\ell}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}_{2})\!\to\!\mathds{R}\}
which map channels to real
numbers—the probability of the classical output
\ell
1
1

1

All measurement processes can be e.g. realized

\mathbf{E}_{\ell}[\cdot]={\rm tr}\,{\rm E}_{\ell}\,\mathbf{S}[\cdot]
as a state producing process
\mathbf{S}

followed by a measurement of the output system represented by a POVM
\{{\rm E}_{\ell}\}
.
.

Particular care must be taken when considering processes that take
N>1
channels as inputs
\mathbf{P}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}_{2})^{\otimes N}\!\to\!\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm in}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}_{\rm out})
. Such
processes can be divided into two distinct classes: causally ordered and non-causally ordered. The former is comprised of
processes that operate on
N
channels in sequence or in parallel and admit a circuit
implementation
Chiribella et al. 2008c
;
Gour and Winter 2019
, while the latter is a more general class and consists of processes that operate on the

N
channels in an indefinite causal order (see
Oreshkov et al. 2012
;
Chiribella et al. 2013b
for examples). For more
details on the characterization of quantum processes we refer the reader to the recent
review
Milz and Quintino 2024
.

For both classes we employ the notation
\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{E}^{\times N}]

to indicate that the process may use the
N
input channels in an arbitrary fashion, i.e.,
with
physical access
to
N
queries of the channel
\mathcal{E}
and not simply a single query of the channel

\mathcal{E}^{\otimes N}
.

Finally, for the purposes of this paper, we shall assign particular understanding to
measure-and-prepare
processes.
We define these to be a measurement process
\{\mathbf{E}_{\ell}\}
followed by the implementation of a channel
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}
conditional on the observed classical outcome
\ell
, i.e.,
\mathbf{P}_{\rm M\&P}[\mathcal{E}^{\times N}]=\sum_{\ell}\mathbf{E}_{\ell}[\mathcal{E}^{\times N}]\,\mathcal{P}_{\ell}.
In our work, processes that are not measure-and-prepare are termed
coherent
. See e.g.
Chen and Chitambar 2020
for a more fine-grained characterization.

II.2
Quantum Cloning and Replication

Consider a family of quantum states
\mathscr{S}\subset\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. An
N\!\to\!M
quantum cloning map takes as
input
N
copies of some state
\rho\in\mathscr{S}
and produces
M>N
copies. Hence, an
N\!\to\!M
cloning map
is described by a CPTP map
\mathcal{P}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}^{\otimes M})
, illustrated in
Fig.
1
a. The performance of the cloning map strongly depends on how one chooses to quantify
the quality of the copies. By and large the most widely used figure of merit is the
fidelity
, which
for two quantum states,
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, is defined
Nielsen and Chuang 2000

F(\rho,\sigma):=\tr|\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma}|={\tr\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho}\,\sigma\sqrt{\rho}}}\,.

(1)

We can quantify the performance of a cloning map based on the
global fidelity
between the
output
\mathcal{P}[\rho^{\otimes N}]
and the ideal target state
\rho^{\otimes M}

Werner 1998
;
Chefles and Barnett 1999
;
Bruß et al. 2000
, or based on the
per-copy fidelity
between any one of the outputs

\sigma_{i}=\tr_{\neg i}\,\mathcal{P}[\rho^{\otimes N}]
and
\rho

Buzek and Hillery 1996
;
Gisin and Massar 1997
;
Bruss et al. 1998
;
Bruß et al. 2000
.
Cloning maps with high global fidelity automatically yield high per-copy fidelity (but not vice versa),
and for this reason we focus on cloning tasks where the figure of merit is the global fidelity from hereon in.

The precise prescription for the optimal cloning map depends strongly on the figure of merit used. For

N\!\to\!M
state cloning, optimal cloning maps have been constructed for the
worst case

fidelity
Werner 1998

\mathsf{F}^{\mathscr{S}}(N,M):=\inf_{\rho\in\mathscr{S}}\,F\left(\mathcal{P}\left[\rho^{\otimes N}\right],\rho^{\otimes M}\right)\,,

(2)

as well as for the
average fidelity

Gisin and Massar 1997
;
Bruß et al. 2000
;
D’Ariano and Macchiavello 2003

\max_{\mathcal{P}}\int_{\mathscr{S}}\,p(\rho)\,F\left(\mathcal{P}\left[\rho^{\otimes N}\right],\rho^{\otimes M}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\rho
where
p(\rho)
denotes any prior knowledge regarding the state
\rho\in\mathscr{S}
to be cloned.
Observe that the average fidelity is always greater or equal to the worst case one. Furthermore, in the former,
the optimal map depends on the choice of the distribution
p(\rho)
therefore, in this work, we focus on the
worst case fidelity. Whatever figure of merit is chosen the task of designing the optimal cloning map reduces to
finding the CPTP map
\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}^{\otimes M})
that optimizes the figure of merit.
Notice that the optimization problem depends only on
N
,
M
, and
\mathscr{S}
. Hereafter we shall suppress the
dependence on
N
and
M
and simply write
\mathsf{F}
to denote the optimized cloning fidelity. Optimal quantum
cloning maps have been constructed for the set of all pure states of dimension

d

Buzek and Hillery 1996
;
Gisin and Massar 1997
;
Werner 1998
, as well as for the so-called phase covariant set of pure states for
dimensions
d=2,3

Bruß et al. 2000
;
D’Ariano and Macchiavello 2003
(the set of states that produce a uniform probability
distribution when measured in the computational basis).

Restricting the set of input states that the map has to clone is akin to relaxing the problem. Another
relaxation of the cloning task is to allow for
probabilistic
cloning maps. Such maps are known to produce
higher fidelity copies than their deterministic counterparts, albeit at the cost of producing no clones with a
finite probability
Duan and Guo 1998
;
Pati 1999
;
Chefles and Barnett 1999
;
Chiribella et al. 2013a
. The more general case of cloning mixed
states (also known as
broadcasting
) is less studied, but nevertheless some results have been
obtained
Barnum et al. 1996
;
D’Ariano et al. 2005
;
Chiribella et al. 2007
; for further reading one may consult reviews on
cloning here
Scarani et al. 2005
;
Luo 2010
;
Fan et al. 2014
.

Aside from cloning states, it is also of interest to consider whether it is possible to clone dynamical quantum
resources such as unitary gates or quantum channels. Consider a family of quantum channels
\mathscr{C}
and a
cloning
process
which attempts to transform
N
copies of
\mathcal{E}\in\mathscr{C}
into
M>N
approximate
copies. This transformation is solicited by a process
\mathbf{P}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N})\!\to\!\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes M}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}^{\otimes M})
that uses
N
copies of
\mathcal{E}
to produce a CPTP map

\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{E}^{\times N}]
acting on
M
quantum systems (see Fig.
1
). Recall that such
processes can be causally or non-causally ordered and,
regardless of the classification,
can be thought of as a quantum algorithm
whose inputs and outputs consist of
M
quantum systems, and which uses
N
copies of the unknown channel as
oracle queries.

Just as for state cloning, the optimal superchannel for cloning quantum channels depends strongly on the figure
of merit one uses. There is no clear cut notion of distance between two quantum channels with most measures
defined indirectly via the effects such channels have on quantum states. Here
we adopt two measures; the first is the Choi-Jamiołkowski fidelity which, for two
channels
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}_{2})
, is defined
Raginsky 2001

F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}):=F(\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{A}],\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{B}]),

(3)

where
{\mathtt{CJ}}[\mathcal{A}]:=({\rm id}\otimes\mathcal{A})\left[\outerproduct{\Phi^+}{\Phi^+}\right]
with
\ket{\Phi^+}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{d}}}\,\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\ket{ii}
. Observe that if either of the channels is unitary,
Eq. (
3
) corresponds to the
entanglement
(or process) fidelity
Schumacher 1996
;
Chuang and and
Nielsen 1997
,
and that in this case there is a one-to-one relationship between the process fidelity and the
average

fidelity
Horodecki et al. 1999
;
Nielsen 2002
. The second figure of merit we shall use is the diamond (or worst-case) fidelity

F_{\diamond}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}):=\min_{\Psi}F\big(({\rm id}\otimes\mathcal{A})[\Psi],({\rm id}\otimes\mathcal{B})[\Psi]]\big),

(4)

where the minimization is taken over all pure states
\Psi\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1^{\prime}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{1})
of the extended system.
By definition it holds that
F_{\text{CJ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\geq F_{\diamond}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
. Analogous to the case of state
cloning, given
(N,M)
the optimal superchannel is the one that optimizes the chosen figure of merit

\mathsf{F}^{\mathscr{C}}_{\bullet}(N,M)=\underset{\mathbf{P}}{\max}\,\underset{\mathcal{E}\in\mathscr{C}}{\inf}\,F_{\bullet}\big({\mathbf{P}}[\mathcal{E}^{\times N}],\mathcal{E}^{\otimes M}\big).

(5)

Note the immediate property
\mathsf{F}^{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm CJ}\geq\mathsf{F}^{\mathscr{C}}_{\diamond}
. From now on we
will drop the explicit dependence on
N
and
M
from Eq. (
5
) to avoid notational clutter.

With these definitions in place the following proposition establishes a connection between cloning quantum states
and cloning of the corresponding
trash-and-replace
channel.

Proposition 1

(Equivalence of cloning states and cloning trash-and-replace channels)
.

Let
\mathscr{S}\subset\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
be a set of states and
\mathscr{C}=\{\mathcal{T}_{\rho}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}\!\to\!\mathcal{H})\,|\mathcal{T}_{\rho}[\,\cdot\,]=\rho,\,\rho\in\mathscr{S}\}
the set of corresponding trash-and-replace channels. It holds that

F^{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm CJ}=F_{\diamond}^{\mathscr{C}}=F^{\mathscr{S}}\,,

(6)

for which any
N\!\to\!M
cloning process
\mathcal{P}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}^{\otimes M})
and cloning
superchannel
\mathbf{P}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N})\!\to\!\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes M}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}^{\otimes M})
are related by
\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{T}_{\rho}^{\times N}]=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}[\rho^{\otimes N}]}
.

Proof sketch.
The proof consists of two parts (see App.
A
for details). First, Lemma
6
demonstrates that the three affine sets resulting from the contraction of a parallel, sequential or non-causally ordered process with any number of trash-and-replace channels are, in fact, identical and recover the full set of CPTP maps
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{P}}[\,\cdot\otimes\rho^{\otimes N}]:=\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{T}_{\rho}^{\times N}][\,\cdot\,]:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes M}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}^{\otimes M})
. Second, we show that for any process
\mathbf{P}

that tries to clone
N
trash-and-replace channels (
\mathcal{T}_{\rho}^{\times N}
) there exists a corresponding state cloning map
\mathcal{P}
.
Conversely, for any state cloning map there exists a cloning process
that attempts to clone the trash-and-replace
channel. The proof is completed by showing that equivalence of all fidelities for the particular choice
\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{T}_{\rho}^{\times N}]=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}[\rho^{\otimes N}]}
. We emphasize that the result holds for all processes: parallel, sequential and non-causally ordered and that the later two classes offer no advantage in any task performed on trash-and-
replace channels.

\square

A closely related task to cloning is
replication
. A replication protocol uses
N
copies of a quantum
resource to produce the maximum number
M>N
copies with fidelity at least
1-\epsilon
,
\epsilon>0
.
Formally, a replication process for quantum channels corresponds to the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathsf{M}^{\mathscr{C}}_{\bullet}(N,\epsilon)=\,\underset{\mathbf{P}}{\textrm{max}}\quad&M\\
\textrm{ s.t. }&\;\underset{\mathcal{E}\in\mathscr{C}}{\textrm{inf}}\,\mathsf{F}^{\mathscr{C}}_{\bullet}\big(\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{E}^{\times N}],\mathcal{E}^{\otimes M}\big)\geq 1-\epsilon\,.\end{split}

(7)

Specifically, what is of interest in a replication process is the
replication rate
,

\mathsf{R}^{\mathscr{C}}_{\bullet}(\epsilon)=\sup\,r\quad\textrm{ s.t.}\quad\lim_{N\!\to\!\infty}\frac{\mathsf{M}^{\mathscr{C}}_{\bullet}(N,\epsilon)}{N^{r}}>0\,,

(8)

which quantifies how the number of high-fidelity clones scales in the large
N
limit. If, for any set of quantum
resources, a replication protocol exists whose rate is
\mathsf{R}(\epsilon)=2,\,\forall\epsilon>0

then we say that these resources can be
super-replicated
.

For the set of pure states
\mathscr{S}
, it follows from the expression of the optimal universal cloning
map
Werner 1998
, that for
all states
the replication rate is at most linear, i.e.,

\mathsf{R}(\epsilon)=1,\,\forall\epsilon
. In
Chiribella et al. 2013a
this was shown to be the maximal rate for
deterministic cloning of quantum states, and in section Sec.
III.1.1
we will
present an alternative derivation. In contrast, it is known that the set of phase covariant states can be

probabilistically
replicated with a rate
\mathsf{R}(\epsilon)=2,\,\forall\epsilon
with the probability
of successful replication decreasing exponentially with the number of replicated copies

M

Chiribella et al. 2013a
. Quite remarkably, the set of unitary gates forming a representation of the
unitary groups
\mathrm{U}(1)
and
\mathrm{SU}(2)
can be deterministically replicated at a rate

\mathsf{R}(\epsilon)=2,\,\forall\epsilon>0

Dür et al. 2015
;
Chiribella et al. 2015
.

Here we consider deterministic cloning and replication of a general set of quantum channels beyond the case of
trash-and-replace and unitary channels. In particular, for continuously parametrized sets of channels we study
the asymptotic replication rate, and ask in what instances super-replication is possible. Note that whilst the
fidelity is as good a quantifier of performance as any other, it is not a distance as it fails to satisfy the
triangle inequality. However, a proper distance measure can be obtained from the fidelity quite simply as

D_{\bullet}:=\arccos F_{\bullet}\,\in\,[0,\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}]\,.

(9)

In particular, for states
D(\rho,\sigma)=\arccos F(\rho,\sigma)
(Eq. (
1
)) is the Bures (or
quantum) angle
Bures 1969
;
Uhlmann 1976
. We stress that the distance
D_{\diamond}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
between two channels as defined above is not to be confused with the
diamond distance

d_{\diamond}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}):=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{B}\|_{\diamond}

(10)

induced by the diamond norm, although, the two are related by the Fuchs-van der Graaf
inequality
Fuchs and van de
Graaf 1999
,
1-\cos D_{\diamond}\leq d_{\diamond}\leq\sin D_{\diamond}
. Equivalently to
Eq. (
5
), we denote the optimized (minimal) cloning distance as
\mathsf{D}^{\mathscr{C}}_{\bullet}
or

\mathsf{d}^{\mathscr{C}}_{\diamond}
.

III
Upper bounds on cloning and replication rates

Trying to solve the optimization problem in Eq. (
5
) directly is, in general, not an easy task (see
Section
IV
). In this section we derive upper bounds on the channel cloning rates and
investigate when super-replication is possible by exploring a connection to two closely related tasks: quantum
channel discrimination (Sec.
III.1
) and quantum metrology
(Sec.
III.2
). In Sec.
III.1.1
we use these bounds to prove that deterministic super-
replication of any continuous set of quantum states is impossible, whilst in Sec.
III.1.2

we show that super-replication of the full set of quantum gates becomes impossible in the presence of any
noise, however weak.

III.1
Bounds from binary channel discrimination

Let
\mathcal{E}_{0},\,\mathcal{E}_{1}\in\mathscr{C}
be two channels that we wish to replicate and

Corollary 2.2

III.2
Bounds from channel estimation

Whilst the geometric bound in Eq. (
15
) holds in general, its usefulness relies on our
ability to compute the optimal channel discrimination distance
\mathsf{D}^{(N)}(\mathcal{E}_{0},\mathcal{E}_{1})
. For states and
unitary channels the optimal discrimination distance was easy to compute, however for more general
families of channels this is no longer the case. In this section, we use powerful techniques from the theory of
quantum channel estimation
Kurdziałek et al. 2023
to provide a rigorous upper bound on the optimal discrimination
distance for any family of continuously parametrized channels
\mathscr{C}
. We then provide necessary conditions
for when a set of channels
\mathscr{C}
can be deterministically super-replicated and identify general families of
channels for which super-replication is impossible.

The key insight for establishing our general upper bound comes from the
quantum Fisher information

(QFI)
Braunstein and Caves 1994
. Let
\mathscr{S}=\{\rho_{x}\,|\,x\in\mathbb{R}\}
be a smooth one-parameter
family of quantum states. The QFI is proportional to the square of the susceptibility of the distance (Bures
angle) defined in Eq. (
9
)

\mathrm{QFI}(\rho_{x}):=4\left(\frac{D(\rho_{x},\rho_{x+\mathrm{d}x})}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^{2}=8\frac{1-F(\rho_{x},\rho_{x+\mathrm{d}x})}{\differential x^{2}}\,.

(21)

Now consider a smooth curve
\{\mathcal{E}_{x}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}\!\to\!\mathcal{H})\,|\,x\in[a,b]\subset\mathds{R}\}
connecting two
quantum channels
\mathcal{E}_{a}
with
\mathcal{E}_{b}
, and let
\mathbf{S}
be the state-producing process that maximizes the
channel discrimination distance
\mathsf{D}^{(N)}(\mathcal{E}_{a},\mathcal{E}_{b})=D\left(\mathbf{S}[\mathcal{E}_{a}^{\times N}],\mathbf{S}[\mathcal{E}_{b}^{\times N}]\right)
. By the triangle inequality we have

\begin{split}\mathsf{D}^{(N)}(\mathcal{E}_{a},\mathcal{E}_{b})&\leq\int_{a}^{b}\,D\left(\mathbf{S}[\mathcal{E}_{x}^{\times N}],\mathbf{S}[\mathcal{E}_{x+\mathrm{d}x}^{\times N}]\right)\differential x\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{\mathrm{QFI}\left(\mathbf{S}[\mathcal{E}_{x}^{\times N}]\right)}\,\differential x\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{\max_{\mathbf{S}}\mathrm{QFI}\left(\mathbf{S}[\mathcal{E}_{x}^{\times N}]\right)}\,\differential x\\
&:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{\mathsf{QFI}^{(N)}\left(\mathcal{E}_{x}\right)}\,\differential x\end{split}

(22)

where the inequality in the third line follows from relaxing the assumption that
\mathbf{S}
maximizes the
discrimination distance between
\mathcal{E}_{a},\mathcal{E}_{b}
to one where
\mathbf{S}
maximizes the discrimination distance
between
\mathcal{E}_{x}
and
\mathcal{E}_{x+\mathrm{d}x}
, and we have defined the maximized QFI in the last line. The latter
quantifies the precision with which one can estimate the localized parameter
x
of a quantum channel, when
having access to
N
copies of it, via an estimation process (typically restricted to be
causal)
Demkowicz-Dobrzański and Maccone 2014
;
Zhou and Jiang 2021
;
Kurdziałek et al. 2023
. Denoting by
\{K_{n}(x):\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}\!\to\!\mathcal{H})\,|\,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
the set of Kraus operators of the channel
\mathcal{E}_{x}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{E}_{x}[\,\cdot\,]=\sum_{n}K_{n}(x)\,\cdot\,K_{n}^{\dagger}(x)
, and defining the operators

\begin{split}\alpha(x)&:=\sum_{n}\,\dot{K}_{n}^{\dagger}(x)\dot{K}_{n}(x)\\
\beta(x)&:=\sum_{n}\dot{K}_{n}^{\dagger}(x)K_{n}(x)\,,\end{split}

(23)

with
\dot{K}_{n}(x):=\frac{\mathrm{d}K^{\dagger}_{n}(x)}{\mathrm{d}x}
, the maximized QFI under any causally ordered state-producing
process
\mathbf{S}
in Eq. (
22
) can be upper bounded as
Kurdziałek et al. 2023

\begin{split}\mathsf{QFI}^{(N)}(\mathcal{E}_{x})&\leq 4f_{N}(\mathcal{E}_{x}),\quad\mathrm{where}\\
f_{N}(\mathcal{E}_{x})&:=\min\,N\sqrt{\|\alpha(x)\|}\\
&\qquad\times\,\left((N-1)\|\beta(x)\|+\sqrt{\|\alpha(x)\|}\right)\,,\end{split}

(24)

where the minimization is over all Kraus representations
\{K_{n}(x):\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}\!\to\!\mathcal{H})\,|\,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
and their derivatives. We formalize the above analysis into the following proposition (see also
Uhlmann 1992
;
Taddei et al. 2013
;
Albarelli and Demkowicz-Dobrzański 2022
).

Proposition 3

(Bound on optimal channel discrimination from channel estimation)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{x}
with
x\in[a,b]\subset\mathds{R}
be a smooth curve in the
set of channels. For all causally ordered state-producing processes
\mathbf{S}
, the optimal
N
-copy channel
discrimination distance in Eq. (
12
) satisfies

\mathsf{D}^{(N)}\left(\mathcal{E}_{a},\mathcal{E}_{b}\right)\,\leq\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{{f}_{N}(\mathcal{E}_{x})}\,\differential x\,,

(25)

where
f_{N}(\mathcal{E}_{x})
in given in Eq. (
24
). Consequently, the optimal diamond distance in
Eq. (
13
) satisfies

\mathsf{d}_{\diamond}^{(N)}(\mathcal{E}_{a},\mathcal{E}_{b})\leq\sin\left(\int_{a}^{b}\,\sqrt{{f}_{N}(\mathcal{E}_{x})}\,\differential x\right)\,.

(26)

Proposition
3
provides an easily computable upper bound on the
N
-copy
channel discrimination task in terms of the single-copy quantity
f_{N}(\mathcal{E}_{x})
and as such we believe that it is of
independent interest beyond the cloning task considered here.
The same proposition can also be used in conjunction with
Proposition

to imply the following bound on the optimal cloning distance
for all causally ordered processes

\mathsf{D}^{\mathscr{C}}_{\bullet}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\bullet}(\mathcal{E}_{a}^{\otimes M},\mathcal{E}_{b}^{\otimes M})-\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{f_{N}(\mathcal{E}_{x})}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\,.

(27)

In turn, this expression can be used to establish necessary conditions for channel super-replication. The
following proposition, whose proof can be found in App.

III.2.1
No super-linear replication of “classical noise” channels

Corollary 4.1

(No super-replication for classical noise channels)
.

Let
\mathscr{C}

\{\mathcal{E}_{x}\,|\,x\in[0,\delta)\}\subset\mathscr{C}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{x}[\,\cdot\,]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}K_{k}(x)\,\cdot\,K_{k}^{\dagger}(x)

\displaystyle K_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=a_{k}(x)G_{k}\qquad a_{k}(x)\in\mathds{R},\,G_{k}\in L(\mathcal{H})\,,

(34)

\mathscr{C}

Proof.

It is straightforward to see that

\displaystyle\beta(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\dot{K}_{k}^{\dagger}(x)\,K_{k}(x)=\sum_{k}\dot{a}_{k}(x)a_{k}(x)G_{k}^{\dagger}G_{k}

such that

\displaystyle D_{\rm CJ}(\mathbf{P}^{\prime}[(\mathcal{N}\circ\mathcal{U})^{\times N}],(\mathcal{N}\circ\mathcal{U})^{\otimes M})

\displaystyle\leq D_{\rm CJ}(\mathbf{P}[(\mathcal{N}\circ\mathcal{U})^{\times N}],\mathcal{U}^{\otimes M})

(42)

which follows from the monotonicity of the Bures angle. But by Corollary
4.2
the left-hand
side of Eq. (
III.2.2
) cannot be made arbitrarily small and thus, super-replicating
N
noisy
gates into
M>N
perfect copies of gates is not possible.

IV
Cloning processes

In this section we study the performance of various cloning and replication processes. First we show that the
task of finding the optimal cloning processes for a given family of channels, can be approximated with a
semidefinite program (SDP) (Sec.
IV.1
).
Although the SDP is the most general numerical formulation of the problem, it suffers from computational limitations that severely restrict the application of the approach beyond moderate values of

N
and
M
.

In Sec.
IV.2
we embark on a more instructive understanding of optimal cloning for large
N,M
values by honing in on a particular class of measure-and-prepare processes, which are fairly straightforward to analyze.

We highlight the natural connection
between
these processes and the Bayesian channel estimation task, and establish quantitative bounds relating optimal
(measure-and-prepare) cloning fidelity with several score functions used in Bayesian channel estimation. With
this in hand, in Secs.
IV.2.1
and
IV.2.2
we show that super-replication of all
qubit unitaries
\mathscr{C}\simeq\mathrm{SU}(2)
and all qubit phase gates
\mathscr{C}\simeq\mathrm{U}(1)
can be
realized with measure-and-prepare processes. This provides an alternative to the coherent super-replication
process of Refs.
Dür et al. 2015
;
Chiribella et al. 2015
and shows that super-replication of these families of channels is
a direct consequence of being able to estimate them with an error that decays quadratically with
N
. Note that this shows the measure-and-prepare protocols give the optimal
scaling
of
M
with
N
, but
not the exact prefactor. Away from asymptotics there are other known results:
for

1\!\to\!2
cloning of unitary gates, it was shown that coherent processes are strictly optimal, outperforming
any processes which attempt to learn the action of the unitary
Chiribella et al. 2008a
.

Before discussing more involved cloning processes let us quickly introduce a naive, but pretty general cloning
strategy that we call the
dummy
process. It consists of applying the given channels
\mathcal{E}\in\mathscr{C}
on
the first
N
systems and applying a fixed dummy channel
\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum}
on the remaining
M-N
systems. The
optimal Choi-Jamiołkowski cloning fidelity for the dummy processes is given by

\mathsf{F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{C|\rm dum}}:=\left(\max_{\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum}}\min_{\mathcal{E}\in\mathscr{C}}F_{\text{CJ}}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum})\right)^{M-N}\,.

(43)

Note that the minmax problem—equivalent to finding the best dummy channel—is
independent of
N
and
M
(see the examples in Secs.
V.3
and
V.4
). In particular, by choosing
the trash-and-replace channel
\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum}=\mathcal{T}_{\nicefrac{{\mathds{1}}}{{d}}}
and noting that
F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{T}_{\nicefrac{{\mathds{1}}}{{d}}},\mathcal{E})=\frac{1}{d}\tr\sqrt{\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{E}]}\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}
is saturated by
unitaries
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{U}
, we obtain the following expression

\mathsf{F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{C|\rm dum}}\geq\mathsf{F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{\rm CPTP|\rm dum}}=(\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}})^{M-N}

(44)

as a baseline for future comparisons. Since the cloning fidelity decays exponentially, the dummy process is only
good when
M-N=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Indeed, even in the linear regime
M=(1+\lambda)N
, its asymptotic replication
rate is zero for any
\lambda>0
.

IV.1
Optimal processes via Semidefinite Programming

Numerically solving the worst case cloning fidelity optimization in Eq. (
5

(i)
Formulating the domain constraining the variable
\mathbf{P}
is
a priori
not easy.
(ii)
The figure of merit
F_{\rm CJ}\big({\mathbf{P}}[\mathcal{E}^{\times N}],\mathcal{E}^{\otimes M}\big)
is nonlinear.
(iii)
It is a minmax problem on a continuous set.
We now discuss how these challenges can be overcome in order to approximate
\mathsf{F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{C}}
numerically. We will keep the discussion at a conceptual level with all technical details deferred to App.
H
.

(i)
The sets of all (parallel, sequential or non-causal) processes
{\mathbf{P}}
can be represented
Milz and Quintino 2024
by a matrix variable subject to positive semidefinite constraints, such that
\mathtt{CJ}[{\mathbf{P}}[\mathcal{E}^{\times N}]]
is linear in the variable.

(ii)

The fidelity between any two density matrices
\rho
and
\sigma
can be expressed as an SDP
Skrzypczyk and Cavalcanti 2023
by introducing a slack variable
Y

\displaystyle F(\rho,\sigma)=\;\max_{Y}

\displaystyle\quad\tr\tfrac{1}{2}(Y+Y^{\dagger})

(45)

subject to

\displaystyle\quad\begin{pmatrix}\rho&Y\\
Y^{\dagger}&\sigma\end{pmatrix}\geq 0.

(iii)
To approximate
\mathsf{F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{C}}
we can choose a discrete net
\mathscr{N}:=\{\mathcal{E}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{H}

inside the continuous set
\mathscr{N}\subset\mathscr{C}
, such that
\mathsf{F}^{\mathscr{N}}_{\rm CJ}\approx\mathsf{F}^{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm CJ}
approximates the optimal cloning fidelity from above. In turn, a cloning process

\mathbf{P}
achieves
\mathsf{F}^{\mathscr{N}}_{\rm CJ}\geq x
if it fulfills the fidelity constraints

F\big({\mathbf{P}}[\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\times N}],\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\otimes M}\big)\geq x\;\forall\,i\in\{1,\ldots,H\}
, which
changes the optimization problem above to a
feasibility
problem.
Following the above discussion, such a process can be found by the SDP

find

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{P},\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{H}

(46)

subject to

\displaystyle\quad{\rm tr}\tfrac{1}{2}(Y_{i}+Y_{i}^{\dagger})\geq x

\displaystyle\quad\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathtt{CJ}[{\mathbf{P}}[\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\times N}]]&Y_{i}\\
Y_{i}^{\dagger}&\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\otimes M}]\end{array}\right)\geq 0.

\displaystyle\quad\textrm{ process constraints on }\mathbf{P}

where we introduced
H
slack variables
Y_{i}
. Conversely, showing that the program (
46
) is infeasible implies
\mathsf{F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{C}}\leq\mathsf{F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{N}}<x
for the considered class of processes. It is also worth mentioning that once the optimal cloning process
\mathbf{P}
for
\mathscr{N}
is found, one can easily improve the approximation of the worst case fidelity it achieves on the whole set
\mathscr{C}
, e.g. by increasing the net size.

Finally, we note that the SDP approach can also be used to bound the best cloning fidelity achievable by the measure-and-prepare processes introduced in Section
IV.2
. This is done by imposing a positive partial transpose constraint on the matrix variable representing the process, as explained in App.
H
.

IV.2
Measure-and-prepare cloning

Measure-and-prepare
processes
consist of two steps: (i) a measurement process
\{\mathbf{E}_{\ell}\}
that consumes
N
copies of the input
channel to produce a classical outcome
\ell
with probability
\mathbf{E}_{\ell}[\mathcal{E}^{\times N}]
, and (ii) a global channel preparation
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}^{(M)}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes M}\!\to\!\mathcal{H}^{\otimes M})
conditioned on
the value
\ell
. These processes have the general form
\mathbf{P}_{\rm M\&P}[\mathcal{E}^{\times N}]=\sum_{\ell}\mathbf{E}_{\ell}[\mathcal{E}^{\times N}]\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}^{(M)}
and the associated optimal cloning fidelity

\mathsf{F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{C|\rm M\&P}}:=\max_{\{\mathbf{E}_{\ell},\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}\}}\min_{\mathcal{E}\in\mathscr{C}}F_{\text{CJ}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{\rm M\&P}[\mathcal{E}^{\times N}],\mathcal{E}^{\otimes M}\right)\,.

(50)

While this is the most general form of a measure-and-prepare process,
it is very natural to consider a more
restricted set of
estimate-and-prepare
processes, which first
estimate
the channel, using a
process
\{\mathbf{E}_{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}\}
, outputting an estimator
\hat{\mathcal{E}}
(a classical description of the
corresponding channel) with corresponding probability distribution
{\rm Pr}^{(N)}(\hat{\mathcal{E}}|\mathcal{E})=\mathbf{E}_{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}[\mathcal{E}^{\times N}]
, and then prepare
M
copies of the estimated channel

\mathbf{P}_{\rm E\&P}[\mathcal{E}^{\times N}]=\sum_{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}{\rm Pr}^{(N)}(\hat{\mathcal{E}}|\mathcal{E})\,\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{\otimes M}\,.

(51)

We will focus on such processes in the rest of this section.
Using the concavity of the fidelity we can write

F_{\text{CJ}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{\rm E\&P}[\mathcal{E}^{\times N}],\mathcal{E}^{\otimes M}\right)\geq\sum_{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}{\rm Pr}^{(N)}(\hat{\mathcal{E}}|\mathcal{E})(F_{\text{CJ}}(\hat{\mathcal{E}},\mathcal{E}))^{M},
from which it follows that the optimal measure-and-prepare
cloning fidelity satisfies

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{C|\rm M\&P}}\geq\max_{\{\mathbf{E}_{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}\}}\min_{\mathcal{E}\in\mathscr{C}}\sum_{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}{\rm Pr}^{(N)}(\hat{\mathcal{E}}|\mathcal{E})\,(F_{\text{CJ}}(\hat{\mathcal{E}},\mathcal{E}))^{M}.

(52)

The optimization on the right-hand side defines the optimal worst case
estimation score for the
M
-dependent score function
(F_{\text{CJ}}(\hat{\mathcal{E}},\mathcal{E}))^{M}
,
and is an instance of the channel estimation task studied in Bayesian quantum
metrology.

To push the connection with Bayesian estimation further using Jensen’s inequality for the expected values

\mathds{E}[F_{\rm CJ}^{M}]\geq\mathds{E}[F_{\rm CJ}^{2}]^{\nicefrac{{M}}{{2}}}\geq\mathds{E}[F_{\rm CJ}]^{M}
, gives the inequalities

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{C|\rm M\&P}}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\max_{\{\mathbf{E}_{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}\}}\min_{\mathcal{E}\in\mathscr{C}}\sum_{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}{\rm Pr}^{(N)}(\hat{\mathcal{E}}|\mathcal{E})(F_{\text{CJ}}(\hat{\mathcal{E}},\mathcal{E}))^{2}\right)^{\nicefrac{{M}}{{2}}}

(53)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\max_{\{\mathbf{E}_{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}\}}\min_{\mathcal{E}\in\mathscr{C}}\sum_{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}{\rm Pr}^{(N)}(\hat{\mathcal{E}}|\mathcal{E})F_{\text{CJ}}(\hat{\mathcal{E}},\mathcal{E})\right)^{M}.

(54)

Both score functions
F_{\text{CJ}}(\hat{\mathcal{E}},\mathcal{E})^{2}
and
F_{\text{CJ}}(\hat{\mathcal{E}},\mathcal{E})
are commonly used in the literature on channel
estimation, (see the next sections for examples). Furthermore, let the channel family
\mathscr{C}
be smoothly
parameterized by a vector of real parameters
\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{C}}\subset\mathds{R}^{n}
from a manifold

\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{C}}
, such that

F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{E}_{\bm{\theta}_{1}},\mathcal{E}_{\bm{\theta}_{2}})\geq 1-g\|\bm{\theta}_{1}-\bm{\theta}_{2}\|^{2},\quad g>0\,,

(55)

where
\|\bm{\theta}_{1}-\bm{\theta}_{2}\|
is the Euclidean norm. Using Eq. (
54
) with
(1-x^{2})^{M}\geq 1-Mx^{2}
we then obtain

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{C|\rm M\&P}}\geq 1-gM\,\mathsf{MSE}^{\mathscr{C}}\qquad\text{with}

\displaystyle\mathsf{MSE}^{\mathscr{C}}:=\max_{\{\mathbf{E}_{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}\}}\min_{\mathcal{E}\in\mathscr{C}}\sum_{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}{\rm Pr}^{(N)}(\hat{\mathcal{E}}|\mathcal{E})\,\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}\|^{2}\,,

(56)

where we identified
\hat{\mathcal{E}}=\mathcal{E}_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}
. Here,
\mathsf{MSE}^{\mathscr{C}}
is the worst case mean
squared error, with respect to the parametrization of the channels we introduced.

The inequalities above establish a relation between channel estimation and cloning, dual to the QFI-based
lower bounds derived in Sec.
III.2
. Here, we have shown that channel estimation protocols can
be directly used to define measure-and-prepare cloning processes, with readily available relations of Eqs.
(
52
-
54
,
IV.2
) between the natural figures of merit.

This connection is particularly interesting when considering the asymptotic replication rate. For instance,
assume that for a family of channels there is a known estimation protocol achieving super-linear mean squared
error

\mathsf{MSE}^{\mathscr{C}}=\frac{\gamma}{N^{1+\epsilon}}\,.

(57)

Then this protocol directly induces a measure-and-prepare cloning process with a worst case fidelity
exceeding

\mathsf{F}^{\mathscr{C}|\rm M\&P}_{\rm CJ}\geq 1-g\,\gamma\frac{M}{N^{1+\epsilon}}\,,

(58)

i.e., a super-linear replication rate. In the following two sections we show that the super-replication results
for all qubit gates and qubit phase gates, established in
Chiribella et al. 2015
;
Dür et al. 2015
with coherent processes
(summarized in Sec.
V.1
), can also be realized with measure-and-prepare processes.

IV.2.1
Measure-and-prepare super-replication of all qubit unitries.

The task of estimating an unknown qubit unitary

\mathscr{U}_{2}=\{\mathcal{U}[\cdot]:=U\cdot U^{{\dagger}}|U\in{\rm SU}(2)\}

(59)

has been studied extensively
Hayashi 2006
;
Chiribella et al. 2005
. The optimal strategy probes
N
copies
of the gate
\mathcal{U}
in parallel using
N
spin-
1/2
systems prepared in the state

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\beta_{\frac{N}{2}}\ket{\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=0(1/2)}^{\frac{N}{2}-1}\frac{\beta_{j}}{\sqrt{2j+1}}\sum_{m=-j}^{j}\ket{j,m}\otimes\ket{j,\alpha(m)}

(60)

where
j
denotes the total spin of the
N
spin-
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
systems,
m
its
z
-component and

\alpha(m)
is the multiplicity index. The coefficients
\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=0(1/2)}^{N/2}
depend on the score
function used to quantify the estimation task. The most common score function used in the literature is the
square of the process fidelity
4
4

4

We note that in
Hayashi 2006
the cost function is the
infidelity
1-F_{\mathrm{CJ}}(\mathcal{U},\hat{\mathcal{U}})^{2}

F_{\mathrm{CJ}}(\mathcal{U},\hat{\mathcal{U}})^{2}
between the true unitary gate

\mathcal{U}
and the estimate
\hat{\mathcal{U}}
. The average fidelity of estimation is, in the limit of large
N
,

\int\differential\mathcal{U}\int\differential\hat{\mathcal{U}}\,{\rm Pr}^{(N)}(\hat{\mathcal{U}}|\mathcal{U})\,F_{\mathrm{CJ}}(\mathcal{U},\hat{\mathcal{U}})^{2}=1-\frac{\pi^{2}}{N^{2}}\,,

(61)

and is achieved by the covariant POVM with density
\{{\rm E}_{\hat{U}}=\hat{U}^{\otimes N}\outerproduct{e}{e}\hat{U}^{\dagger\otimes N}\}
, whose fiducial element is given by

\displaystyle\ket{e}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{N+1}\ket{\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=0(1/2)}^{\frac{N}{2}-1}\sqrt{2j+1}\sum_{m=-j}^{j}\ket{j,m}\otimes\ket{j,\alpha(m)}\,.

(62)

By construction, the protocol is symmetric and yields the same average squared process fidelity for all

U\in\mathrm{SU}(2)
. Hence, the worst case squared process fidelity is equal to the average squared process
fidelity.

Using Eq. (
53
) we find that the cloning process using the estimation strategy described above
achieves a worst case cloning fidelity

\mathsf{F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{U}_{2}|\rm M\&P}\geq\left(1-\frac{\pi^{2}}{N^{2}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{M}}{{2}}}\,.

(63)

Thus for
M=N^{2-\delta}
, the worst case process fidelity of such an estimate and prepare strategy tends to
unity in the limit
N\to\infty
, for any
\delta>0
. We believe that similar estimate and prepare processes are
capable of super-replication also for gates in
\mathrm{SU}(d)
.

IV.2.2
Measure-and-prepare super-replication of all qubit phase gates.

We now consider an estimate and prepare strategy for cloning the set

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{2}

\displaystyle:=\big\{\mathcal{U}_{\theta}\,|\,\theta\in[0,2\pi)\big\}

(64)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\theta}[\,\cdot\,]

\displaystyle=U_{\theta}[\,\cdot\,]U_{\theta}^{\dagger}:=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta\sigma_{z}}\cdot e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta\sigma_{z}}.

(65)

The optimal estimation strategy has been determined in
Berry and Wiseman 2000
, although here we closely follow
the notation established in
Bartlett et al. 2007
. The optimal estimation strategy consists of probing
the
N
unitary gates in parallel using the entangled state

\ket{\Psi_N}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N+2}}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\,\sin\left(\frac{(k+1)\pi}{N+2}\right)\ket{{\rm D}_k^N}\,.

(66)

where
\ket{\rm{D}_k^N}
denote the permutationally symmetric state of
N
spin-1/2 systems,
k
of which are in
an excited state—the so-called Dicke states
Dicke 1954
. The relevant score function is the alignment
fidelity
f(\hat{\theta},\theta)=\cos^{2}\left((\theta-\hat{\theta})/2\right)
. Just as in the case of
\mathrm{SU}(2)
estimation, the average alignment fidelity is achieved by the covariant POVM with density
\{{\rm E}_{\hat{\theta}}=U_{\hat{\theta}}^{\otimes N}\outerproduct{e}{e}U_{\hat{\theta}}^{\dagger\otimes N}\}
, where

\ket{e}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\ket{{\rm D}_k^N}
, and reads, in the limit of large
N
,

\displaystyle\int\differential\hat{\theta}\,{\rm Pr}^{(N)}(\hat{\theta}|\theta)\,f(\hat{\theta},\theta)\,=1-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4N^{2}}\,.

(67)

The protocol is symmetric by construction, hence the worst case alignment fidelity is equal to the average
alignment fidelity.

Noting that
f(\hat{\theta},\theta)=\frac{1}{2}(1+F_{\mathrm{CJ}})
, and using Eq. (
54
), it follows that
a cloning process based on the above estimation strategy yields

\mathsf{F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{P}_{2}|\rm M\&P}\geq\left(1-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2N^{2}}\right)^{M},

(68)

which, for
M=N^{2-\delta}
and in the limit of large
N
, tends to unity for any
\delta>0
, demonstrating super-replication. Note that the rhs of Eq. (
68
) is always larger than the rhs of
Eq. (
63
), which is consistent with the fact that cloning phase gates is by definition simpler than
cloning all
\mathrm{SU}(2)
gates.

V
Examples of channel cloning

In this section we study the possibility of replicating non-unitary channels. Specifically, in
Sec.
V.2
we consider noisy phase gates on qubits, where the noise consists of a known Pauli

\sigma_{x}
channel that occurs either before or after the application of the gate. For both these channels we
provide an explicit cloning process—utilizing quantum error mitigation techniques—that allows for
super-replication. In Secs.
V.3
and
V.4
we consider families of Pauli-noise and amplitude damping
channels for which, by Proposition
4
, super-replication is not possible.
Nevertheless we study the performance of several cloning processes, among which are an error mitigation process, and
a measure-and-prepare process, and derive bounds on their cloning performance.

We start by summarizing the coherent parallel cloning processes for unitary gates
Dür et al. 2015
;
Chiribella et al. 2015
.

V.1
Coherent processes for unitary gates

For completeness, we now briefly review the coherent cloning process of
Dür et al. 2015
for the qubit phase gates

\mathscr{P}_{2}
(Eq.
64
). We will denote the
M
input qubits
\bm{S}=S_{1}\dots S_{M}
, their
computational basis states
\ket{\bm k}_{\bm{S}}
, where
\bm{k}
is bitstrings with Hamming weight
|\bm{k}|
.
First, an isometry
\mathcal{V}[\,\cdot_{\bm{S}}]=V(\,\cdot_{\bm{S}}\otimes\outerproduct{\bm 0}{\bm 0}_{\bm{A}})V^{\dagger}

is applied to the
M
input qubits, by preparing
N
auxiliary qubits
\bm{A}=A_{1}\dots A_{N}
in the state
\ket{\bm 0}_{\bm{A}}
and performing a global
unitary operation such that
V:\ket{\bm k}_{\bm{S}}\ket{\bm 0}_{\bm{A}}\mapsto\ket{\bm k}_{\bm{S}}\otimes\ket{{\rm Unary}(n_{|\bm k|}) }_{\bm{A}}
with

\displaystyle\ket{{\rm Unary}(n)}:=\begin{cases}\ket{1}^{\otimes n}\ket{0}^{\otimes(N-n)}&0\leq n\leq N\\
\ket{0}^{\otimes N}&{\rm otherwise}\end{cases}

and
n_{|\bm{k}|}:=\left\lfloor|\bm{k}|-\nicefrac{{(M-N)}}{{2}}\right\rfloor
.
Then, one applies the
N
copies of the gate
\mathcal{U}_{\theta}
onto
the auxiliary qubits
\bm{A}
before uncomputing the unitary and tracing out the
auxiliary qubits with the channel
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}[\,\cdot_{\bm{S}\bm{A}}]=\tr_{\bm{A}}\left(V^{\dagger}\,\cdot_{\bm{S}\bm{A}}\,V\right)
.
The channel realized by this process
\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{U}_{\theta}^{\times N}]=\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}\circ\big(\mathds{1}_{\bm{S}}\otimes(\mathcal{U}_{\theta}^{\otimes N})_{\bm{A}}\big)\circ\mathcal{V}
imprints the correct phases on
the subspace spanned by all computational basis states
\ket{\bm k}_{\bm{S}}
with
\big|\nicefrac{{M}}{{2}}-|\bm{k}|\big|\leq\nicefrac{{N}}{{2}}
. Crucially, for large
M=N^{2-\delta}

\ket{\Phi^+}^{\otimes M}
is
supported on this subspace with unit probability, guaranteeing
\mathsf{F}^{\mathscr{P}_{2}}_{\rm CJ}\to 1
. In
contrast, the phases outside of this subspace are in general wrong, which is easy to detect by probing the
channel
\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{U}_{\theta}^{\times N}]
with a state outside of the subspace and consistent with

\mathsf{F}^{\mathscr{P}_{2}}_{\diamond}\to 0
implied by Corollary
2.2
.

The coherent cloning process of
Chiribella et al. 2015
is based on a similar idea. In the case of qubits
\mathscr{U}_{2}
(spin-
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
systems), it correctly imprints the
action of the unknown gates in all subspaces with total spin less than
\nicefrac{{N}}{{2}}
, and can be realized efficiently with the help of the Schur
transform circuit
Bacon et al. 2006
. Again, the same argument about
\mathsf{F}^{\mathscr{U}_{d}}_{\rm CJ}\to 1
and

\mathsf{F}^{\mathscr{U}_{d}}_{\diamond}\to 0
can be made for
M=N^{2-\delta}.

V.3
Pauli-noise channels

Next, let us consider the set of Pauli-noise channels

\mathscr{P}=\Big\{\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}[\cdot]=\sum_{j=0}^{3}p_{j}\,\sigma_{j}\cdot\sigma_{i}\Big|p_{j}\geq 0,\sum_{j}p_{j}=1\Big\},

(79)

whose
Choi-Jamiołkowski
states are given by

\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}]=\sum_{j=0}^{3}p_{j}\,\outerproduct{\Phi_i}{\Phi_i}
with

\ket{\Phi_j}=(\mathds{1}\otimes\sigma_{j})\ket{\Phi^+}
. The Pauli-noise channels are a specific example of “classical”
noise channels, hence by Corollary
4.1
they cannot be replicated
beyond a linear rate.

The
Choi-Jamiołkowski
fidelity between two Pauli noise channels reads

F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}},\mathcal{N}_{\bm{q}})=\sum_{j=0}^{3}\sqrt{p_{j}\,q_{j}}.

(80)

As
\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}]
is a mixture of fixed states, it is not difficult to see that the optimal dummy
channel in Eq. (
43
) is
\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum}=\mathcal{N}_{\nicefrac{{\bm{1}}}{{\bm{4}}}}=\mathcal{T}_{\nicefrac{{\mathds{1}}}{{2}}}
, leading to the dummy cloning fidelity of
\mathsf{F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{P}|\rm dum}=\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\right)^{M-N}
, tight with the expression for the full set of channels in Eq. (
44
).

Before discussing better replication processes, consider the following proposition
guaranteeing that optimal cloning of Pauli-noise channels is realized by a measure-and-prepare process.

Proposition 5
.

For any subset of Pauli-noise channels
\mathscr{P}
in Eq. (
79
) coherent processes bring no advantage over measure-and-prepare processes (for any task).

Proof.

Let
\{\mathbf{N}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{3}
be the following measure-and-prepare process using a single qubit channel

\mathbf{N}_{j}[\mathcal{E}]=\bra{\Phi_j}({\rm id}\otimes\mathcal{E})[\Phi^{+}]\ket{\Phi_j}\,\mathcal{P}_{j},

(81)

with the unitary maps
\mathcal{P}_{j}[\cdot]:=\sigma_{j}\cdot\sigma_{j}

It consists of using the input channel to prepare the state
({\rm id}\otimes\mathcal{E})[\Phi^{+}]
, measuring this state in the Bell basis, and implementing the unitary
channel
\mathcal{P}_{j}
. When combined with a Pauli-noise channel the process gives

\mathbf{N}_{j}[\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}][\cdot]=p_{j}\,\sigma_{j}\cdot\sigma_{j}.

(82)

Hence, upon discarding the classical output the process reproduces the input channel

\mathbf{N}[\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}]:=\sum_{j}\mathbf{N}_{j}[\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}]=\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}
.

Now, let
\mathbf{P}
be any process on multiple qubit channels. Combing it with (potentially different) Pauli noise channels one finds

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[\bigtimes_{k}\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}^{(k)}}]=\mathbf{P}\big[\bigtimes_{k}\mathbf{N}[\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}^{(k)}}]\big]:=\mathbf{P}^{\prime}[\bigtimes_{k}\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}^{(k)}}],

(83)

where the process
\mathbf{P}^{\prime}
, obtained by combining
\mathbf{P}
with copies of
\mathbf{N}
, is measure-and-prepare. Hence, any possible process on Pauli-noise channels, can be realized in this fashion. The result directly generalizes to any set of channels left invariant by a fixed measure-and-prepare process.
∎

A convenient property of Pauli noise is that all Kraus operators

K_{j}(\bm{p})=\sqrt{p_{j}}\,\sigma_{j}
in Eq. (
79
) are proportional to
identity when squared. Hence the probability of the corresponding events—labeled by
j
—is independent of the state on which the channel acts. It follows that the best a measurement process can do with
N
Pauli-noise channels is to perfectly distinguish all the events labeled by the string
\bm{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{N})
. In other words, the optimal measurement process is
\{\mathbf{E}_{\bm{j}}\}
such that

\mathbf{E}_{\bm{j}}[\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}^{\times N}]=\prod_{k=0}^{3}(p_{k})^{t_{k}(\bm{j})},

(84)

where
t_{k}(\bm{j})
is the number of values
j_{i}=k
in the string
\bm{j}
.
This measurement process can be realized via applying
\{\mathbf{N}_{j}\}
in Eq.(
81
) on each Pauli-noise channel in parallel.

Finding the optimal channel estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}^{(M)}_{\bm{j}}
in Eq. (
51
)
is more challenging. Instead, we now focus on the specific construction

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}^{\times N}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{j}}\mathbf{E}_{\bm{j}}[\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}^{\times N}]\,\widehat{\mathcal{N}}^{(M)}_{\bm{j}}\qquad\text{with}

(85)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{N}}^{(M)}_{\bm{j}}

\displaystyle:=\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{P}_{j_{i}}\right)\otimes\mathcal{N}_{\hat{\bm{p}}(\bm{j})}^{\otimes(M-N)},

(86)

where
\hat{\bm{p}}(\bm{j})
is an estimator of the parameter
p
, which only depends on the totals
\bm{t}:=(t_{0}(\bm{j}),\dots,t_{3}(\bm{j}))
. The idea of this strategy is as follows: upon discarding the classical register
\bm{j}
it
implements the perfect copies of the channel
\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}=\mathbf{N}[\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}]
on
the first
N
systems, while at the same time it estimates the value
\bm{p}
and
implements the estimated channels
\mathcal{N}_{\hat{\bm{p}}}
on the remaining
M-N
systems.

Using the fact that the four states
\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{P}_{j}]
are pure and orthogonal, the fidelity of
\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}^{\times N}]
with
M
ideal copies
\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}^{\otimes M}
of the channel is found to be (see App.
F.1
)

\displaystyle F_{\rm CJ}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}^{\times N}],\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}^{\otimes M})

(87)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{t}}{\rm Pr}_{\rm Mult}(\bm{t}|N,\bm{p})(F_{\rm CJ}\left(\mathcal{N}_{\hat{\bm{p}}(\bm{t})},\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}\right))^{M-N},

where
{\rm Pr}_{\rm Mult}(\bm{t}|N,\bm{p})=\binom{N}{t_{0},t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}}\prod_{k=0}^{3}(p_{k})^{t_{k}}

is the multinational distribution.

In App.
F.2
we lower bound the value of the coherent cloning fidelity in
Eq. (
87
) for the specific choice of the (unbiased) estimator

\hat{p}_{j}(\bm{t})=\frac{t_{j}}{N}
. First, using the inequality
\mathds{E}[\sqrt{X}]\geq\sqrt{pN}\left(1-\frac{1-p}{2pN}\right)
for binomial random variables

X\sim{\rm Bin}(N,p)
we show that

{\rm F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{P}}\geq\left(1-\nicefrac{{3}}{{2N}}\right)^{M-N}
.
Second, for a restricted family of channels
\mathscr{P}=\{\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}|p_{j}\geq\epsilon,\sum_{j}p_{j}=1\}
with some
\epsilon>0

\mathsf{D}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{P}}\leq\arccos(\exp(-\nicefrac{3}{8}\, \lambda))\,.

(88)

Conversely, the lower bound
\mathsf{D}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{P}}\geq A\left(\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\right)

When restricting to bit-flip channels
\mathscr{X}=\{\mathcal{X}_{p}|p\in[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]\}
in Eq. (

V.4.1
AD channels can not be super-replicated

With the help of Proposition
4
it is immediate
to see that AD channels can not be super-replicated. Indeed, with the
derivatives of the natural Kraus operators

\dot{K}_{0}^{\dagger}({\gamma})=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-\gamma}}\outerproduct{1}{1},\quad\dot{K}_{1}^{\dagger}({\zeta})=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\gamma}}\outerproduct{1}{0}

(92)

\beta({\gamma})=\dot{K}_{0}^{\dagger}({\gamma})K_{0}({\gamma})+\dot{K}_{1}^{\dagger}({\gamma})K_{1}({\gamma})=0
which rules out the possibility of super-linear
replication.
In addition, we have

\frac{\mathsf{QFI}^{(N)}(\mathcal{E}_{\gamma})}{N}\leq 4\|\alpha({\gamma})\|=\frac{1}{\gamma(1-\gamma)}.

(93)

For the QFI of the
Choi-Jamiołkowski
states combining Eqs. (
21
) and (
91

{\rm QFI}(\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}])=\frac{1}{2\gamma(1-\gamma)}
.

Hence, for a linear rate
M=(1+\lambda)N
the optimal cloning distance is bounded by

\mathsf{D}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{D}}\geq A\left(\frac{2}{1+\lambda}\right).

(94)

This bound resembles the one obtained for Pauli noise channels in Eq. (
38

. This comes from the fact
that probing AD channels in parallel through their Choi-Jamiołkowski states is
in fact suboptimal by a factor of two from the QFI perspective (it is not
difficult to see

5

F(\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}[\outerproduct{1}{1}],\mathcal{E}_{{\gamma}^{\prime}}[\outerproduct{1}{1}])=\sqrt{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}+\sqrt{(1-\gamma)(1-\gamma^{\prime})}

that probing the AD channels with the state

V.4.2
Estimate-and-prepare cloning of AD channels

A simple estimation process for the AD channels, maximizing the Fisher information for all
\gamma
, consists of
probing each copy
\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}
with the state
\ket{1}
, and measuring the output system in the computation basis
\{\ket{s\oplus 1}\}_{s=0,1}
, such that the output
s
corresponds to the application of the Kraus operator
K_{s}
, and
s=1
occurs with probability
\gamma
.

For
N
copies of the channel, the sum of the outcomes
t:=\absolutevalue{\bf s}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}
follows the binomial distribution
{\rm Pr}_{\rm Bin}(t|N,{\gamma})
. Now consider estimate-and-prepare cloning processes, that prepare
\{\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\hat{\gamma}(t)}^{\otimes M}\}_{t=0}^{N}
for all possible values
t
. This family of processes achieves the fidelity

\displaystyle{\rm F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{D}|\rm E\&P}\!\!=\!\min_{{\gamma}\in[0,1]}F_{\rm CJ}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{N}{\rm Pr}_{\rm Bin}(t|N,{\gamma})\mathcal{E}_{\hat{\gamma}(t)}^{\otimes M},\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\otimes M}\right)

with the binomial distribution
{\rm Pr}_{\rm Bin}(t|N,p):=\binom{N}{t}p^{t}(1-p)^{N-t}
,
for any choice of the estimator
\hat{\gamma}(t)
. As the fidelity is nonlinear this expression is very challenging to analyze, even numerically. Instead in Section
V.4.4
we analyze the lower bound

\displaystyle{\rm F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{D}|\rm E\&P}\geq\!\min_{{\gamma}\in[0,1]}\sum_{t=0}^{N}{\rm Pr}_{\rm Bin}(t|N,{\gamma})F_{\rm CJ}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\hat{\gamma}(t)},\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}\right)^{M},

(95)

obtained by concavity of
F_{\rm CJ}
. Before doing so, let us introduce a closely related coherent process.

V.4.3
Coherent cloning of AD channels

Just like for the bit-flip noise the two branches of the AD channel, i.e. the CP maps

\mathcal{K}_{s|\gamma}[\,\cdot\,]:=K_{s}(\gamma)\cdot K_{s}^{{\dagger}}(\gamma)\quad\text{with}\quad s=0,1,

(96)

have a different parity and can be detected with the process
\{\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{s}\}
with
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{0}=\mathbf{M}_{0}
and

\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{1}=\mathcal{X}_{1}\circ\mathbf{M}_{1}
, illustrated in Fig.
3
. Indeed, one can verify (see App.
E
) that when used on
\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}
the process returns
\{\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{s}[\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}]=\mathcal{K}_{s|\gamma}\}
.
This is a quantum instrument allowing one to
execute
the unknown channel
\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{0}[\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}]+\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{1}[\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}]
(upon discarding
s
) while at the same time
estimating
the parameter
\gamma
through the classical output
s
. With this in mind, we now construct a coherent cloning process, illustrated in Fig.
3
c.

The cloning process
\mathbf{P}
first applies the instruments
\{\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{s_{i}}[\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}]\}
on the first
N
input qubits. Depending on the value
t=\absolutevalue{\bm s}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}
observed, it applies the fixed AD channel
\mathcal{E}_{\hat{{\gamma}}(t)}
on the remaining
M-N
qubits, and then discards the classical register
\bf s
.
It follows that the result of this process is the
M
-qubit CPTP map

\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\times N}]=\!\sum_{s_{1},\dots,s_{N}}\!\left(\mathcal{K}_{s_{1}|\gamma}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{K}_{s_{N}|\gamma}\otimes(\mathcal{E}_{\hat{{\gamma}}(\bf s)})^{\otimes(M-N)}\right)

(97)

and it remains to find the optimal estimator
\hat{\gamma}(t)
for each
N
and
M
.

To do so we first compute the fidelity achieved by this process for all values of the parameter
\gamma
. Observe that here the maps
\mathcal{K}_{s|\gamma}
are not applied on
\ket{1}
but on half of the maximally entangled state
\ket{\Phi^+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{00}+\ket{11})
. The output
s
thus takes the value
s=0
with probably
\|\mathds{1}\otimes K_{0}({\gamma})\ket{\Phi^+}\|^{2}=1-\frac{\gamma}{2}
and the value
s=1
with probability
\|\mathds{1}\otimes K_{1}({\gamma})\ket{\Phi^+}\|^{2}=\frac{\gamma}{2}
.
Hence the sum
t=\|\bm{s}|=\sum_{i}s_{i}
follows the binomial distribution
{\rm Pr}_{\rm Bin}(t|N,\nicefrac{{{\gamma}}}{{2}})
.

This allows one (see App.
G.2
) to obtain the following expression for the fidelity
F_{\rm CJ}(\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\times N}],\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\otimes M})=\sum_{t=0}^{N}{\rm Pr}_{\rm Bin}(t|N,\nicefrac{{{\gamma}}}{{2}})F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{E}_{\hat{\gamma}(t)},\mathcal{E}_{\gamma})^{M-N}

and shows that the coherent process achieves

\displaystyle{\rm F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{D}|\rm coh}

VI
Summary and conclusion

This work extends the notion of cloning and replication beyond quantum states
and gates to the most general transformations of quantum theory: quantum
channels. By demonstrating the equivalence between cloning of states and
cloning the respective trash-and-replace channels (Proposition
1
) we are able to formulate cloning and replication of all quantum
resources under a unified framework,

cast

in the language of quantum processes.
By employing a geometric approach we establish a connection between cloning and the fundamental tasks of binary channel discrimination and channel estimation, which allows us to derive upper bounds on the performance of any cloning and
replication process (Propositions
2

and
3
). Independently of cloning, we
believe the latter to be of great interest for binary channel discrimination.

We also establish the necessary conditions for a family of channels to exhibit
super-replication (Proposition
4
) and prove
strong converses on the deterministic replication rates for any continuous
family of states (Corollary
2.1
), quantum gates under the
diamond distance (Corollary
2.2
), and parametric
families of “classical” noise channels (Corollary
4.1
).
For all of the above we provide rigorous upper bounds on the cloning fidelity in
the limit of large
N
and
M
. Moreover, while the full set of
d
-dimensional
unitaries admits super-replication, we show that even infinitesimal noise
reduces the rate to linear (Corollary
4.2
).

Motivated by the converse question, in Section
IV
, we devise
general techniques to approximate the optimal cloning process for a given set of
channels. Building on the results
of
Milz and Quintino 2024
;
Skrzypczyk and Cavalcanti 2023
we show in Section
IV.1

that the search of the optimal cloning process can be approximated by a semi-
definite program (SDP), and we illustrate this approach with
1\!\to\!2

cloning for the bit-flip and amplitude-damping channels. We concluded that, for the amplitude-damping channels, the optimal process found by the SDP outperforms all measure-and-prepare process and also the coherent hand-crafted process. We also study the performance of measure-and-prepare processes (Section
IV.2
) which enables us to draw a quantitative connection between the tasks of cloning and Bayesian channel estimation. Somewhat surprisingly, we find that
super-replication of qubit gates, demonstrated in Refs.
Dür et al. 2015
;
Chiribella et al. 2015
with
coherent
processes, can also be realized by measure-and-prepare process Sections

IV.2.1
and
IV.2.2
), and can
be viewed as a consequence of the quadratic scaling of precision in the corresponding Bayesian estimation
tasks
Berry and Wiseman 2000
;
Chiribella et al. 2005
;
Hayashi 2006
.

Finally, in Section
V
, we study cloning process for several
specific families of quantum channels: noisy phase gates—where the noise acts
either before or after the application of the phase gate—Pauli-noise channels
and amplitude-damping channels. We show that for the first two cases
super-replication is possible, the first instance of a non-unitary channel where this is possible. In contrast, both Pauli-noise and amplitude-damping channels can
only be replicated at a linear rate. For Pauli-noise channels, we prove that coherent processes offer no advantage over measure-and-prepare processes in
any

task (Proposition
5
), and discuss the asymptotic performance of such a process. The case of amplitude-damping channels is more subtle, and we focus on the
comparisons between two hand-crafted processes. .

Our findings raise several interesting questions for future research. Whilst
Proposition
4
establishes necessary
conditions for super-replication it is worth noting that we do not know whether
these are also sufficient. In particular, the proposition was derived
via a construction in the
local neighborhood
\differential\mathscr{C}=\{\mathcal{E}_{x}|x\in[0,\delta)\}\subset\mathscr{C}
of a channel
\mathcal{E}_{x}
and
by studying the scaling of the (optimal

N
-copy)
\mathsf{QFI}^{(N)}(\mathcal{E}_{x})
. Yet, even when all such subsets
\differential\mathscr{C}

can be super-replicated the processes may be different and thus it is not clear if
super-replication is achievable across the whole set
\mathscr{C}
with a single process. Answering this
question requires consideration of the global properties of the set.
Interestingly, a sufficient condition for super-replication is offered by the
estimate-and-prepare processes discussed in Sec.
IV.2
—it is
possible if the channels
\mathscr{C}
can be
estimated
with the worst-case
error that decays as
N^{2}
, for an appropriate cost function. Establishing
closed-form sufficient conditions for such Heisenberg scaling in Bayesian estimation task and for channel super-replication is an interesting open question.

Another interesting direction for future research, is to rephrase the question
of replication/cloning at the level of continuous semi-group dynamics. For instance, does there exist a process that, given access to a system evolving under a given parametrized family of Liouvillian dynamics for a total time
t
, simulate its evolution for a time
t^{\prime}>t
. For Hamiltonian dynamics this can be seen as an instance of the task of transforming Hamiltonian eigenvalues
Odake et al. 2025
, while nothing seems to be known for the case of open-system dynamics.

Optimization of the bound

In this section for all
0<x,y<\infty

\mathrm{f}(x,y):=\max_{R\geq 0}\left(\arccos\left(\exp[-Rx]\right)-\sqrt{2Ry}\right).

(137)

Define a new variable
\zeta:=\exp\left[-Rx\right]\in[0,1]
which allows us to rewrite the maximization as

\displaystyle\mathrm{f}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{g}(\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}):=\max_{\zeta\in[0,1]}g(\zeta;Z)

\displaystyle g(\zeta;Z)

\displaystyle:=\left(\arccos\left(\zeta\right)-\sqrt{2Z\ln(\nicefrac{1}{\zeta})}\right)

(138)

with
0<Z=\nicefrac{{y}}{{x}}<\infty
. Note that the function
g(\zeta;Z)
assumes the values
-\infty

and
0
on the boundaries of the domain (
\zeta=0,1
respectively), and is smooth on the open interval
(0,1)
.
Therefore its maximum is attained at the boundary or at a local extremum satisfying

\displaystyle\frac{\differential}{\differential\zeta}g(\zeta;Z)=0\quad

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^{2}}}=\frac{\sqrt{2Z}}{2\zeta\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{1}{\zeta}\right)}}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{Z}{\zeta^{2}}-Z+\log(Z)=\log(\frac{Z}{\zeta^2}).

(139)

Exponentiating both sides of the equation gives

\exp(\frac{Z}{\zeta^2})\exp(-Z)Z=\frac{Z}{\zeta^{2}}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad(-\frac{Z}{\zeta^{2}})\exp(-\frac{Z}{\zeta^2})=-Z\exp(-Z),\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad y\,e^{y}=-Z\exp(-Z)

(140)

after the change of variable
y=-\frac{Z}{\zeta^{2}}
. Note that the right hand side in
the Eq. (
140
) satisfies
-\frac{1}{e}\leq-Z\exp(-Z)\leq 0
, and the equation is known to
have two real solutions given by the two branches
(k=0,-1)
of the Lambert W function
W_{k}

y=W_{0}(-Z\exp(-Z))\,,\qquad y=W_{-1}(-Z\exp(-Z))\,.

(141)

Transforming the variables back gives

\zeta=\sqrt{-\frac{Z}{W_{0}(-Z\exp(-Z))}}\,\qquad\zeta=\sqrt{-\frac{Z}{W_{-1}(-Z\exp(-Z))}}\,.

(142)

As
\zeta\in[0,1]
only two solutions are possible

\zeta=1\qquad\text{or}\qquad\zeta=\sqrt{-\frac{Z}{W_{-1}(-Z\exp(-Z))}}\quad\text{if}\quad Z\leq 1.

(143)

The solution
\zeta=1
corresponds gives the trivial value
g(1;Z)=0
. Hence, the unique nontrivial solution is
only possible if
Z\leq 1
, i.e.,

\mathrm{g}(Z)=\begin{cases}0&Z\geq 1\\
\arccos\zeta(Z)-\sqrt{2Z\ln(\frac{1}{\zeta(Z)})}&Z<1\,.\end{cases}

(144)

It follows that the solutions of
A(Z)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{g}(Z)
are given by

A(Z)=\begin{cases}0&Z\geq 1\\
\frac{1}{2}\left(\arccos\zeta(Z)-\sqrt{2Z\ln(\frac{1}{\zeta(Z)})}\right)&Z<1\end{cases}\qquad\text{with}\qquad\zeta(Z):=\sqrt{-\frac{Z}{W_{-1}(-Z\exp(-Z))}}\,.

(145)

To see that
A(Z)
(or
\mathrm{g}(Z)
) is a monotonically decreasing function of
Z
, note that
g(\zeta;Z)
is
monotonically decreasing in
Z
for all
\zeta

Following the beginning of the proof in the main text, consider the curve

inside our set of channels. We now show that we can choose

which does not give the desired result. However, exploiting the gauge freedom we can chose any other Kraus

where we have collected the
n

and
L_{2}

\mathcal{N}

(152)

L_{1}^{\dagger}L_{1}

L_{2}^{\dagger}L_{2}

we obtain

\displaystyle F_{\rm CJ}(\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}^{\times N}],\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}^{\otimes M})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{t}}{\rm Pr}_{\rm Mult}(\bm{t}|N,\bm{p})\,F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{N}_{\hat{\bm{p}}(\bm{t})},\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}})^{M-N}\,.

(176)

F.2
Computing the asymptotic cloning fidelity of the coherent process

In this section we lower bound the cloning fidelity of the Pauli-noise channels
(Section
V.3
)

\displaystyle{\rm F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{C}|\rm M\&P}=\min_{\bm{p}}\sum_{\bm{t}}{\rm Pr}_{\rm Mult}(\bm{t}|N,\bm{p})\,F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{N}_{\hat{\bm{p}}(\bm{t})},\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}})^{M-N}

(177)

for the specific choice of the estimator
\hat{p}_{k}(\bm{t})=\frac{t_{k}}{N}
. To do so we will lower bound the final
fidelity for all values of the parameter
\bm{p}
, using two approaches.

First, consider the following lower bound given by Jensen’s inequality

\displaystyle F_{\rm CJ}(\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}^{\times N}],\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}^{\otimes M})

\displaystyle\geq\left(\sum_{\bm{t}}{\rm Pr}_{\rm Mult}(\bm{t}|N,\bm{p})\,F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{N}_{\hat{\bm{p}}(\bm{t})},\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}})\right)^{M-N}

(178)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{3}\sqrt{p_{k}\,\hat{p}_{k}(\bm{t})}\right]^{M-N},

(179)

where we used Eq. (
80
) for the single copy fidelity. Here the four random variables
N\hat{p}_{k}(\bm{t})\sim{\rm Bin}(N,p_{k})
are binomially distributed. To lower bound the last
expression we can thus use the following inequality for a binomially distributed random variable
X\sim{\rm Bin}(N,p)

\mathds{E}[\sqrt{X}]\geq\sqrt{pN}\left(1-\frac{1-p}{2pN}\right),

(180)

which is derived in App.
F.3
following
Gurel-Gurevich 2012
. Plugging Eq. (
180
)
into Eq. (
179
) one immediately obtains

\displaystyle F_{\rm CJ}(\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}^{\times N}],\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}^{\otimes M})

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\sum_{k=0}^{3}\frac{1-p_{k}}{2N}\right)^{M-N}=\left(1-\frac{3}{2N}\right)^{M-N}\,\forall\bm{p}\quad\implies\quad\mathsf{F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{P}}\geq\left(1-\frac{3}{2N}\right)^{M-N}.

(181)

Finally, considering a smaller family of Pauli-noise channels
\mathscr{P}=\{\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}}|\sum p_{j}=1,p_{j}\geq\epsilon\}
for
some
\epsilon>0
, we obtain a tight expression for the asymptotic worst-case alignment fidelity

\sum_{\bm{t}}{\rm Pr}_{\rm Mult}(\bm{t}|N,\bm{p})\,F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{N}_{\hat{\bm{p}}(\bm{t})},\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}})=1-\frac{3}{8N}\,.

(182)

To do so we note that in the large
N
limit the distribution of
\hat{\bm{p}}(\bm{t})
concentrates around the mean value

\mathds{E}[\hat{\bm{p}}(\bm{t})]=\bm{p}
and we can expand
F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{N}_{\hat{\bm{p}}},\mathcal{X}_{\bm{p}})=1-\sum_{k}\frac{(\hat{p}_{k}-p_{k})^{2}}{8p_{k}}+\mathcal{O}(\|\hat{\bm{p}}-\bm{p}\|^{3})
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{t}}{\rm Pr}_{\rm Mult}(\bm{t}|N,\bm{p})\,F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{N}_{\hat{\bm{p}}(\bm{t})},\mathcal{N}_{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{k=0}^{3}\frac{\sum_{\bm{t}}{\rm Pr}_{\rm Mult}(\bm{t}|N,\bm{p})(\hat{p}_{k}N-p_{k}N)^{2}}{8N^{2}p_{k}}

(183)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{k=0}^{3}\frac{(1-p_{k})}{8N}=1-\frac{3}{8N},

(184)

where the sum in the first line is nothing more than the second moment of the binomial distribution
{\rm Bin}(N,p_{k})
, given by
Np_{k}(1-p_{k})
. In the asymptotic limit with
M=(1+\lambda)N
we thus find for any restricted set of Pauli-channels the cloning fidelity satisfies

\mathsf{F}_{\rm CJ}^{\mathscr{P}}\geq\exp(-\frac{3}{8} \lambda).

(185)

F.3
Square root of a binomial random variable

For completeness, in this section we summarize the demonstration of the bound

\mathds{E}[\sqrt{X}]\geq\sqrt{pN}\left(1-\frac{1-p}{2pN}\right)

(186)

for a binomial random variabel
X\sim{\rm Bin}(N,p)
, given in the discussion
Gurel-Gurevich 2012
on
Mathoverflow. First, for
x\geq 0
consider the inequality

\sqrt{x}\geq 1-\frac{(1-x)}{2}-\frac{(1-x)^{2}}{2}\Longleftrightarrow 2\sqrt{x}\geq(3-x)x\Longleftrightarrow 2-3(\sqrt{x})+(\sqrt{x})^{3}\geq 0\Longleftrightarrow(\sqrt{x}-1)^{2}(\sqrt{x}+2)\geq 0.

(187)

With its help, for any nonnegative random variable we can write

\displaystyle\frac{\mathds{E}[\sqrt{X}]}{\sqrt{\mathds{E}[X]}}=\mathds{E}\left[\sqrt{\frac{X}{\mathds{E}[X]}}\right]\geq\mathds{E}\left[1-\frac{(1-X/\mathds{E}[X])}{2}-\frac{(1-X/\mathds{E}[X])^{2}}{2}\right]=1-\frac{\mathds{E}[X^{2}]-\mathds{E}[X]^{2}}{2\,\mathds{E}[X]^{2}}

(188)

Plugging in
\mathds{E}[X]=pN
and
\mathds{E}[X^{2}]=p\left(pN^{2}-pN+N\right)
gives
Eq. (
186
).

Appendix G
Cloning of amplitude-damping channels

G.1
The dummy process for amplitude-damping channels

Recall that the Kraus operators of the amplitude-damping channels
\mathcal{E}_{\gamma},\,{\gamma}\in[0,1]
are given by

K_{0}({\gamma})=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sqrt{1-{\gamma}}\end{pmatrix},\quad K_{1}({\gamma})=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt{{\gamma}}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\,

(189)

in the computational basis. In order to derive the best dummy cloning process we need to find the channel

\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum}
maximizing

\min_{{\gamma}\in[0,1]}F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{E}_{\gamma},\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum}).

(190)

For simplicity, let us first relax this problem by only minimizing the Choi fidelity with repect to the extremal
amplitude-damping channels
\mathcal{E}_{0}=\text{id}
and
\mathcal{E}_{1}
(we show later that this actually leads to the optimal
solution of the full problem), i.e.

\min\left\{F_{\rm CJ}(\text{id},\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum}),F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum})\right\}.

(191)

Consider the Choi-Jamiołkowski states associated to these three channels. For the target channels we have

\mathtt{CJ}[\text{id}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\frac{1}{2}&&&\frac{1}{2}\\
&0&&\\
&&0&\\
\frac{1}{2}&&&\frac{1}{2}\end{array}\right)\qquad\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{E}_{1}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\frac{1}{2}&&&\\
&0&&\\
&&\frac{1}{2}&\\
&&&0\end{array}\right)\,.

(192)

The most general Choi-Jamiołkowski state that can be associated to the dummy channel is of the form

\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\frac{p}{2}&\dots&\frac{b}{2}&\frac{a}{2}\\
\dots&\frac{1-p}{2}&\dots&\dots\\
\frac{b^{*}}{2}&\dots&\frac{1-q}{2}&\dots\\
\frac{a^{*}}{2}&\dots&\dots&\frac{q}{2}\end{array}\right),

(193)

with
0\leq p,q\leq 1
and
|a|,|b|\leq 1
, where we only specified the elements that will be important for the
following discussion. Note that the state must satisfy
\tr_{S}\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum}]=\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}
which
is readily guaranteed by the chosen parametrization. In addition, it must be positive semi-definite
\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum}]\geq 0
which in particular implies
|a|^{2}\leq pq
and
|b|^{2}\leq p(1-q)
. Since both target
states are real, we can assume that
a
and
b
are also real without loss of generality.

Let us now compute the target fidelities, starting with the identity channel. We find

\displaystyle F_{\rm CJ}({\rm id},\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum})

\displaystyle=\tr|\sqrt{\mathtt{CJ}[{\rm id}]}\sqrt{\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum}]}|=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{p+q+2a}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{p+q+2\sqrt{pq}}

(194)

where the last bound is saturated by setting
a=\sqrt{pq}
. For
\mathcal{E}_{1}
we find

\displaystyle F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum})

\displaystyle=\tr|\sqrt{\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{E}_{1}]}\sqrt{\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum}]}|

(195)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{p+1-q-\sqrt{4b^{2}+(p+1-q)^{2}}}+\sqrt{p-1-q+\sqrt{4b^{2}+(p+1-q)^{2}}}}{2\sqrt{2}}

(196)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{p+1-q}}{2},

(197)

where the last inequality is tight for
b=0
. Note that the upper-bounds can be attained simultaneously, without
compromising the positivity of
\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum}]
. Hence, this is the optimal choice for the parameters

a
and
b
, and it remains to optimize over
p
and
q
. As both expressions we computed are increasing with

p
, we set
p=1
as the optimal choice. This leads to

\displaystyle F_{\rm CJ}({\rm id},\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1+2\sqrt{q}+q}

(198)

\displaystyle F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\sqrt{1-q}\right)\,

(199)

and their minimum occurs at
q=\frac{1}{2}

F_{\rm CJ}({\rm id},\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum})=F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum})=\frac{2+\sqrt{2}}{4}.

(200)

We have thus shown that the optimal Choi-Jamiołkowski states for the relaxed problem is

\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\frac{1}{2}&&&\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\\
&0&&\\
&&\frac{1}{4}&\\
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}&&&\frac{1}{4}\end{array}\right),

(201)

with the dummy channel corresponding to an AD channel with
\gamma=1/2
.
Finally, it is straightforward to check that

F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{E}_{\gamma},\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum})\geq F_{\rm CJ}({\rm id},\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum})=F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum})=\frac{2+\sqrt{2}}{4},

(202)

which implies that the dummy channel we constructed is also optimal for the original problem, and

\max_{\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum}}\min_{{\gamma}\in[0,1]}F_{\rm CJ}(\mathcal{E}_{\gamma},\mathcal{E}_{\rm dum})=\frac{2+\sqrt{2}}{4}.

(203)

G.2
Computing the fidelity of the coherent process for cloning AD channels

Here we compute the cloning fidelity
F_{\rm CJ}(\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\times N}],\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{M})
for the coherent
AD cloning process discussed in Sec.
V.4.3
. In the main text we have established that the
process returns the
M
-qubit CPTP map

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\times N}]

\displaystyle=\!\sum_{s_{1},\dots,s_{N}}\!\left(\mathcal{K}_{s_{1}|\gamma}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{K}_{s_{N}|\gamma}\otimes(\mathcal{E}_{\hat{{\gamma}}(\absolutevalue{\bf s})})^{\otimes(M-N)}\right)

\displaystyle=\!\sum_{s_{1},\dots,s_{M}}\!\left(\mathcal{K}_{s_{1}|\gamma}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{K}_{s_{N}|\gamma}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{s_{N+1}|\hat{\gamma}(\absolutevalue{\bf s})}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{K}_{s_{M}|\hat{\gamma}(\absolutevalue{\bf s})}\right)

(204)

where
\absolutevalue{\bf s}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}
. Henceforth we shall write
\hat{\gamma}(\absolutevalue{\bf s})=\hat{\gamma}
for short.
Analogously the ideal channel
\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\otimes M}
can be expanded as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\otimes M}

\displaystyle=\!\sum_{s_{1},\dots,s_{M}}\!\left(\mathcal{K}_{s_{1}|\gamma}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{K}_{s_{N}|\gamma}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{s_{N+1}|\gamma}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{K}_{s_{M}|\gamma}\right)\,.

(205)

For both channels we find that the corresponding Choi-Jamiołkowski states read

\displaystyle\mathtt{CJ}\big[\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\times N}]\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{1},\dots,s_{M}}\!\left(\Psi_{s_{1}|\gamma}\otimes\dots\otimes\Psi_{s_{N}|\gamma}\otimes\Psi_{s_{N+1}|\hat{\gamma}}\otimes\dots\otimes\Psi_{s_{M}|\hat{\gamma}}\right)

\displaystyle\mathtt{CJ}\big[\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\otimes M}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{1},\dots,s_{M}}\!\left(\Psi_{s_{1}|\gamma}\otimes\dots\otimes\Psi_{s_{N}|\gamma}\otimes\Psi_{s_{N+1}|\gamma}\otimes\dots\otimes\Psi_{s_{M}|\gamma}\right)

(206)

where
\Psi_{s|\gamma}=\outerproduct{\Psi_{s|\gamma}}{\Psi_{s|\gamma}}
with

\ket{\Psi_{s|\gamma}}=(K_{s}(\gamma)\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\Phi^+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{cases}\ket{00}+\sqrt{1-\gamma}\ket{11}&s=0\\
\sqrt{\gamma}\ket{01}&s=1\end{cases}.

(207)

Crucially, because of their parity for different values for
s
,
\innerproduct{\Psi_{0|\gamma}}{\Psi_{1|\gamma'}}=0

for all
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
. This allows us to write

\displaystyle\left|\sqrt{\mathtt{CJ}\big[\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\times N}]\big]}\sqrt{\mathtt{CJ}\big[\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\otimes M}\big]}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{1},\dots,s_{M}}\!\left(\Psi_{s_{1}|\gamma}\otimes\dots\otimes\Psi_{s_{N}|\gamma}\otimes\left|\sqrt{\Psi_{s_{N+1}|\hat{\gamma}}}\sqrt{\Psi_{s_{N+1}|\gamma}}\right|\otimes\dots\otimes\left|\sqrt{\Psi_{s_{M}|\hat{\gamma}}}\sqrt{\Psi_{s_{M}|\gamma}}\right|\right).

(208)

Then, for
{\rm Pr}^{(1)}(s|\gamma)=\tr\Psi_{s|\gamma}=\begin{cases}1-\frac{\gamma}{2}&s=0\\
\frac{\gamma}{2}&s=1\end{cases}
, we get

\displaystyle F_{\rm CJ}(\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\times N}],\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{M})

\displaystyle=\tr\left|\sqrt{\mathtt{CJ}\big[\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\times N}]\big]}\sqrt{\mathtt{CJ}\big[\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}^{\otimes M}\big]}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{1},\dots,s_{M}}\!\left({\rm Pr}^{(1)}(s_{1}|\gamma)\times\dots\times{\rm Pr}^{(1)}(s_{N}|\gamma)\times\tr\left|\sqrt{\Psi_{s_{N+1}|\hat{\gamma}}}\sqrt{\Psi_{s_{N+1}|\gamma}}\right|\times\dots\times\tr\left|\sqrt{\Psi_{s_{M}|\hat{\gamma}}}\sqrt{\Psi_{s_{M}|\gamma}}\right|\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{1},\dots,s_{N}}\!\left({\rm Pr}^{(1)}(s_{1}|\gamma)\times\dots\times{\rm Pr}^{(1)}(s_{N}|\gamma)\times\left(F_{\text{CJ}}(\mathcal{E}_{\hat{\gamma}(t)},\mathcal{E}_{\gamma})^{M-N}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{N}\,{\rm Pr}_{\rm Bin}(t|N,\nicefrac{{\gamma}}{{2}})\,F_{\text{CJ}}(\mathcal{E}_{\hat{\gamma}(t)},\mathcal{E}_{\gamma})^{M-N}\,

(209)

with the binomial distribution
{\rm Pr}_{\rm Bin}(t|N,\nicefrac{{\gamma}}{{2}})
.

H.2
Constraining measure-and-prepare processes

Consider the physical realization (Fig.
7
) of the
Choi-Jamiołkowski
state
\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{S}]
representing
a process
\mathbf{P}
. It is obvious that for a measure-and-prepare process, the representing
Choi-Jamiołkowski
state is
separable across the bi-partition
\mathcal{H}_{\vec{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\vec{2}}\,|\,\mathcal{H}_{\rm in}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\rm out}
—
only classical information can be exchanged between these systems. Separability of
\mathtt{CJ}[\mathcal{S}]

implies a positive partial transpose. Hence all measure-and-prepare processes satisfy the SDP
constraint

\mathbf{P}\,\text{ is M\&P}\quad\implies\quad\overline{\mathtt{CJ}}[\mathcal{S}]^{\rm T(\mathcal{H}_{\rm in},\mathcal{H}_{\rm out})}\geq 0,

(227)

which can be directly plugged in the SDP of Eq. (
222
). This is of course a relaxation of the
measure-and-prepare set of processes, but can nevertheless be useful to upper-bound their performance in a given
task.

H.3
Cloning
1\to 2
copies of the amplitude-damping map

Numerically we tested the qubit amplitude-damping channel
\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}[\,\cdot\,]:=K_{0}({\gamma})\cdot K_{0}^{\dagger}({\gamma})+K_{1}({\gamma})\cdot K_{1}^{\dagger}({\gamma})
with Kraus operators given by

\displaystyle K_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sqrt{1-\gamma}\end{pmatrix},\quad K_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt{\gamma}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(228)

Note the natural asymmetry of the problem at the end points:

\displaystyle\gamma=0\qquad

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal{E}_{0}={\rm id},

(229)

\displaystyle\gamma=1\qquad

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal{E}_{1}=\mathcal{T}_{\outerproduct{0}{0}}

(230)

where
\mathcal{T}_{\outerproduct{0}{0}}
trashes any input and returns
\outerproduct{0}{0}
. In other words, the extremal points
are a unitary map and the
trash-and-replace
map. These extremal points will prove to be the problematic
ones in the numerics (see Table
1
). For the superchannel that clones
1\to 2
copies of the qubit
amplitude-damping channel
d_{\rm in}=d_{\rm out}=4
, the superchannel acts on the following spaces

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}:(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}))\longrightarrow(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 4})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 4})),

(231)

and its corresponding respresenting map (the induced linear CP map on
Choi
operators) by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2})\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 4}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 4}),

(232)

i.e., a map which takes dimension
d=4
matrices on its input to
d=16
matrices on its output. The
Choi of this map is defined as the action on two copies of the input space

\displaystyle\overline{\mathtt{CJ}}[\mathcal{S}]=\mathds{1}\otimes\mathcal{S}\big(\sum_{ij}\ket{i}\!\ket{j}\!\bra{i}\!\bra{j}\big)=\sum_{ij}\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes\mathcal{S}(\outerproduct{i}{j})\quad\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2})\otimes\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 4}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 4}).

(233)

Thus, the
Choi-Jamiołkowski
operator of the representing map
\overline{\mathtt{CJ}}[\mathcal{S}]
for
1\to 2
cloning is a matrix of
dimension
d=2\cdot 2\cdot 4\cdot 4=64
.
