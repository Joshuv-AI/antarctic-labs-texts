Burden of anaemia in displaced women and children in refugee settings worldwide, update for 2017–2024: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Thomas Charles McHale, Terry N Theuri, Oleg O Bilukha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo determine the prevalence of anaemia in women and children in refugee settings globally.DesignPopulation-representative cross-sectional surveys were conducted by the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) and partner organisations.SettingRefugee sites in East and Southern Africa (ESA), West and Central Africa (WCA) and South Asia.ParticipantsUNHCR conducted routine programmatic surveys evaluating the needs of non-pregnant women and children in refugee sites, globally.Outcome measureHaemoglobin levels were measured using the HemoCue Hb 301+System (Fischer Scientific, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, USA). Anaemia was defined as mild, moderate or severe based on the 2011 WHO guidelines.ResultsThe median total prevalence of anaemia in children was 41.5%. WCA had the highest median total prevalence of anaemia in children (56.8%), and South Asia had the lowest (37.6%). Globally, the median total prevalence of anaemia in non-pregnant women of reproductive age was 28.2%. WCA had the highest median total prevalence of anaemia in women (39.4%), and ESA had the lowest (27.7%). Anaemia is highly correlated between women and children, even though the prevalence is much lower in women. The mean total prevalence of anaemia in children increased by 15.9% (95% CI 2.04% to 29.7%) in WCA but decreased by 7.9% (95% CI −20.1% to 4.24%) in ESA after the COVID-19 pandemic.ConclusionsWCA have particularly high rates of anaemia, worsening since 2020. ESA, as well as South Asia, have seen reductions in anaemia in recent years. There is wide variability within refugee settings, indicating variability in the implementation of successful interventions to reduce and prevent anaemia. Improving feeding practices and preventing and treating anaemia-causing infections may improve prevalence of anaemia.Keywords: Anaemia, Nutrition, Refugees, Africa South of the Sahara, Community child health

Background

Anaemia is a common health problem globally and is particularly prevalent in women and children. Recent systematic reviews have demonstrated that the global burden of anaemia remains alarmingly high, despite improvements in diagnostics, therapeutics and nutrition.1 2 In 2019, there were 1.8 billion total cases of anaemia globally, with a population ratio of 23 000/100 000.2 These systematic reviews found that, globally, in 2019, approximately 40% of all children under 5 years1 and about 30% of women of reproductive age had anaemia.2 3 Sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia are the most affected regions.4 The causes of anaemia are many and complex, but include modifiable aetiologies like micronutrient deficiencies and infections, and unmodifiable aetiologies like thalassaemia and sickle cell disease.1 2 5 In 2023, the WHO published a framework for accelerating anaemia reduction, noting that the COVID-19 pandemic and humanitarian crises had exacerbated the global burden of anaemia.4 The report also noted that progress toward the Sustainable Development Goals of 2030, regarding anaemia, had stalled, demanding new and reinvigorated approaches, such as optimising service delivery and prioritising key preventive and therapeutic actions.4 Displaced populations may be at higher risk for nutritional deficiencies and infections, and are less likely to access the care needed for diagnosis of haemoglobinopathies, making them more vulnerable to the underlying aetiologies of anaemia.1 6 However, a review of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) data from the years 2013–2016 showed that the prevalence of anaemia in non-pregnant refugee women and children was similar to estimates in the host populations.7 Globally, the prevalence of anaemia is higher in developing countries, and surveys have shown that individuals from lower socioeconomic strata have higher rates of anaemia.2 5

Anaemia accounted for an estimated 50.3 million years lived with disability in 2019.2 People living in refugee settings are particularly susceptible to many of the underlying aetiologies of anaemia. Iron deficiency anaemia, which accounts for over half of anaemia cases globally, is associated with reduced work capacity, immune dysfunction, gastrointestinal disturbances and an increased risk of Helicobacter pylori infection. Iron deficiency anaemia is even more common in children and women, accounting for ~65% of all aetiologies of anaemia in children <5 years old and 50–60% in women of reproductive age.2 In children and infants, iron deficiency anaemia has been linked to poor health outcomes, including reduced motor, socioeconomic and cognitive function.8,11 In pregnant women, anaemia is associated with premature rupture of membranes, preterm delivery, low birth weight and neonatal asphyxia.12 13 Other modifiable causes of anaemia in children and women of reproductive age are infections such as malaria, schistosomiasis and hookworm. In 2019, malaria caused 464 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) globally, and malaria is one of the leading causes of anaemia in sub-Saharan Africa and Asia.2 14 Hookworm was estimated to cause about 4 million DALYs in 2010,15 and schistosomiasis causes around 1.5 million DALYs annually.16 Among non-modifiable aetiologies, sickle cell disease alone accounts for over 3 million DALYs, and other haemoglobinopathies nearly 3 million DALYs.17

In 2019, Kay et al published a global analysis of anaemia in refugee settings.7 The data, which covered the period from 2013 to 2016, were collected from surveys conducted in refugee sites by UNHCR and partner agencies and included haemoglobin measurements in children aged 6–59 months and non-pregnant women aged 15–49 years. The study found that the burden of anaemia was high among the refugee site population, but not different from global estimates of the general population or the host populations of the sites. Regional rates of anaemia were also similar to those reported in host populations, with the exception of South Asia, where anaemia in refugee sites was lower than that observed in surveys in host populations, though with only a small number of surveys in South Asia. Children had a particularly high total prevalence of anaemia at 44%, and 19% had moderate/severe anaemia.7 They also found that the prevalence of anaemia among children compared with prevalence among non-pregnant women in the same setting was highly correlated.7

In 2020, as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic, UNHCR services for refugees were significantly impacted.18 For example, lockdowns reduced the operational hours of UNHCR health facilities; Rwanda and Uganda saw an 8–9% reduction in outpatient consultations.18 In South Sudan, there was a 51% reduction in children enrolled in treatment for severe acute malnutrition.18 These disruptions to health facilities and services for children were likely to worsen health outcomes for refugees living in refugee settings. Given these disruptions and ongoing changes in refugee populations, we endeavoured to reassess the prevalence of anaemia in refugee sites in children aged 6–59 months and non-pregnant women, aged 15–49 years using the same data sources as Kay et al.7 We compared the overall and regional prevalences of anaemia in the years before and after 2020 to evaluate how anaemia has changed in refugee populations since the COVID-19 pandemic. Finally, we evaluated the correlation between women and children of haemoglobin measurements and anaemia for each survey. Although anaemia is determined from the haemoglobin measurements, these indicators have different population distributions, so we evaluated them in tandem.

Methods

Data sources

UNHCR and its partners conducted programmatic population-representative cross-sectional surveys in refugee settings between 2017 and 2024. Surveys were conducted in: Sudan, South Sudan, Tanzania, Uganda, Rwanda, Kenya, Djibouti, Eritrea, Ethiopia, the Republic of the Congo, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Mauritania, Chad, Bangladesh and Nepal. These surveys were conducted using the Standardised Expanded Nutrition Survey (SENS) guidelines and tool.19 20 SENS guidelines were developed by UNHCR in collaboration with experts in the field of nutrition and public health and are internationally recognised for anthropometric assessments in refugee settings. Surveys that were missing haemoglobin measurements were excluded from the analysis. For small camps, surveys were conducted on the entire population. Otherwise, simple, systematic or cluster random sampling was employed, depending on the quality of available household data. Sample sizes were calculated to determine the prevalence of children aged 6–59 months with global acute malnutrition. The frequency of surveys in each site varied depending on UNHCR assessments, but many sites had multiple surveys conducted throughout the study period. Verbal informed consent was obtained from each household before proceeding. The HemoCue Hb 301+System (Fischer Scientific, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, USA) was used to measure haemoglobin. Capillary blood was drawn from the ring finger of the non-dominant hand and immediately placed directly into a microcuvette and tested on the HemoCue 301.21 The HemoCue has been used in low-resource settings and validated in comparison to laboratory reference standards using venous blood.21 22 Haemoglobin measurements <40 g/L or >180 g/L were excluded from the analysis, as these are likely to be invalid results.22

Data analysis

Severity of anaemia was classified using the haemoglobin concentrations defined by the 2011 WHO guidelines for haemoglobin cut-offs to define anaemia.23 For children aged 6–59 months, haemoglobin concentration <110 g/L was classified as mild anaemia, <100 g/L as moderate anaemia and <70 g/L as severe anaemia. For non-pregnant women aged 15–49 years, haemoglobin concentration of <120 g/L was classified as mild anaemia, <110 g/L as moderate anaemia and <80 g/L as severe anaemia. Total haemoglobin concentrations were adjusted according to elevation of the household following the 2011 WHO guidelines for haemoglobin measurement in settings greater than 1000 m above sea level.23

Surveys were separated into those that collected haemoglobin in children only, in women only, or in both women and children. We calculated the mean haemoglobin and determined the per cent prevalence of total anaemia, as well as mild, moderate and severe anaemia, for each survey. Since the prevalence of severe anaemia was 0.3% for children and 0.4% for women, we combined categories of moderate and severe anaemia for the analysis. The median and IQR of each anaemia indicator and per cent prevalence were determined from the survey-level data. If the total prevalence of anaemia for any given survey was >40%, then the representative population was classified as having a severe public health problem, per WHO guidelines.23 We classified surveys based on the country of the refugee setting where the survey was conducted and by the three UNICEF geographical regions where the surveys were conducted. Ethiopia (in the East and Southern Africa (ESA) region) and Chad (in the West and Central Africa (WCA) region) were classified separately from their regions, given the large number of surveys from these two countries.

We approached the analysis of the median haemoglobin and median prevalence of anaemia in two ways. We determined the median haemoglobin and median prevalence of anaemia for each survey. In the first approach, for sites with multiple surveys conducted at the same site over the study period, we analysed each survey at each site independently. Second, we determined the median haemoglobin and median prevalence of anaemia for each site across the entire study period, if multiple surveys were conducted. For each region, we measured the survey-level variance in prevalence of anaemia by calculating the coefficient of variance (CV), or the ratio of the SD to the mean. For surveys that measured haemoglobin in both women and children, we used a Pearson correlation coefficient to determine whether there was a correlation in haemoglobin levels and anaemia prevalence between these two population groups. Since no surveys were conducted in 2020, we calculated the changes in site-specific haemoglobin and prevalence of anaemia during the periods 2017–2019 to 2021–2024. South Asia was excluded from these analyses given the paucity of data from this region. All data aggregation, cleaning and analyses were performed using RStudio V.2024.04.1.

The project was reviewed according to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention human-subjects protection procedures and determined to not constitute human-subjects research under 45CFR46, thus ethical approval was not obtained.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this study.

Results

There were 273 surveys with haemoglobin measurements in children aged 6–59 months and 250 surveys with haemoglobin measurements in non-pregnant women aged 15–49 years in refugee settings between 2017 and 2024 (online supplemental tables 1,2). 243 surveys had haemoglobin measurements for both children and non-pregnant adult women. Surveys were conducted in 15 countries. Ethiopia and Chad had the largest number of surveys conducted, with Ethiopia having 68 surveys in children and 70 in women, while Chad had 74 surveys conducted in children and 74 surveys in women. South Asia had the fewest number of surveys (nine), with Nepal conducting one survey in children and women and Bangladesh conducting six surveys in children and two surveys in women. There was a preponderance of surveys conducted in ESA (excluding Ethiopia), with 111 surveys conducted in children and 89 surveys in women. Meanwhile, only 13 surveys were conducted in children and 11 in women in WCA (excluding Chad).

Anaemia in children

We found that in children aged 6–59 months, the overall median total prevalence of anaemia was 41.5% and the median prevalence of moderate/severe anaemia was 17.8% (table 1). Geographically, the prevalence of both total and moderate/severe anaemia was highest in WCA (56.8% and 32.9%, respectively) and Chad (46.8% and 20.6%, respectively). The total prevalence of anaemia and moderate/severe anaemia was similar in both ESA, as well as Ethiopia, 39% and ~16%, respectively. South Asia had a relatively small number of surveys, but it had the lowest prevalence of total anaemia, at 37.6%, although the CI overlapped with other regions. The percentage of surveys in which the population had a severe public health problem from anaemia was high in all regions. Almost all surveys in WCA (92.3%) demonstrated a severe public health problem, followed by Chad, with 73.0% of surveys. About half of the surveys showed a severe public health problem in Ethiopia and ESA.

Anaemia in women

We found that in non-pregnant women aged 15–49 years, the overall median prevalence of total anaemia was 28.2% and the median prevalence of moderate/severe anaemia was 11.2%, which was lower than in children (table 2). The prevalence of both total and moderate/severe anaemia remained highest in WCA (39.4% and 15.1%, respectively), although Chad had a lower prevalence of both total and moderate/severe anaemia (29.9% and 11.5%, respectively). ESA, as well as Ethiopia, had a lower prevalence: 25–28% total and 10–11% moderate/severe anaemia. South Asia had a prevalence of total anaemia of 30.6%. Overall, the percentage of surveys indicating a severe public health problem from anaemia was lower than that for children, with a total of 16.5% across all surveys. However, WCA had a sharply higher percentage of surveys with a severe public health problem (53.8%).

Changes in anaemia before and after 2020

In children, we found that overall there were only small changes from 2017 to 2019 compared with 2021–2024 but there were marked regional changes. We found an increase of 4.35% (95% CI −10.1% to 18.1%) in the mean total prevalence of anaemia from 2017–2019 to 2021–2024 (figure 2 and online supplemental table 3). WCA had an increase of 15.9% (95% CI 2.04% to 29.7%) and ESA had a decrease of 7.9% (−20.1% to 4.24%). Similarly, in non-pregnant women of reproductive age, there was essentially no change in the total prevalence of anaemia with a decrease of 1.12% (95% CI −14.1 to 11.9%). However, for women in WCA, there was an increase of 14% (95% CI 7.8% to 20.2%) and ESA had a decrease of 14.7% (95% CI −24.5% to −4.88%).

Correlations of prevalence of anaemia and mean haemoglobin level between women and children

While the prevalence of anaemia was lower in women than in children, we found that there was a high degree of survey-level correlation between women and children for prevalence of total anaemia, moderate/severe anaemia and mean haemoglobin (figure 3). The Pearson correlation coefficient for total anaemia was 0.684 and for moderate/severe anaemia was 0.594 (online supplemental table 4). Correlation was highest in WCA, while in ESA, Ethiopia and Chad, it was similar to the overall correlation. We did not assess the correlation in South Asia given the limited number of surveys.

Discussion

In this analysis of cross-sectional surveys conducted in refugee sites in ESA, WCA and South Asia, we found evidence that children and women in refugee sites have an overall low haemoglobin concentration and high prevalence of anaemia, although the burden of severe anaemia was low. Children, aged 6–59 months, had a median prevalence of total anaemia of 42%, while non-pregnant women of reproductive age (15–49 years) had a prevalence of total anaemia of 28%, both of which are similar to the WHO global prevalences of anaemia in these populations. Globally, according to the WHO, 40% of children aged 6–59 months, and 30.5% of non-pregnant women aged 15–49 years have anaemia.24 25 Since the most disruptive period of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, our study found that the prevalence of total anaemia in children increased by 4.35%. Except for ESA, all regions and countries, including Ethiopia, had increases in prevalence of anaemia in children after 2020 compared with before. In 2020, UNHCR suspended data collection involving in-person activities, including population-based surveys.18 These surveys (SENS) are used to plan and implement interventions in situations where health and nutrition, especially in children, are deteriorating.19 These disruptions made it more difficult for UNHCR to identify and respond to health issues in refugee sites in 2020.18 There were also restrictions on in-person activities, including delivery of nutrition assistance in camps.18 UNHCR reported a broad swath of disruptions to health services in 2020, including vaccination programmes, outpatient and inpatient services and enrolment in treatment for severe acute malnutrition, though specific country and regional-level disruptions are not described.18 Thus, given our findings of worsening anaemia in refugee populations, one plausible explanation is that these disruptions led to an interruption in the progress of anaemia prevention and reduction. However, our limited analysis could not account for competing explanations, such as conflict and instability in these regions leading to worsening displacement, food insecurity and vulnerability to anaemia-inducing infections.26 27

We did note that there were regional differences in the prevalence of anaemia in refugee sites, which also varied from host populations. Surveyed children in refugee settings in Chad and WCA, which mostly consisted of refugees in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and the Republic of the Congo, had particularly high rates of anaemia, with a total prevalence of nearly 50% or higher. However, according to WHO in 2019, the population prevalence of anaemia among children in Central Africa was 64%.24 Meanwhile, for refugees in Ethiopia and ESA, the median total prevalence of anaemia was ~39%, while the population prevalence in children was ~50%.24 In South Asia, hosting mostly refugees from Nepal and Bangladesh, it was even lower (37.6%), even though the population prevalence of anaemia in children in South Asia was 51%. Refugee women in the sub-Saharan Africa regions and Ethiopia had a similar prevalence of total anaemia to host populations.25 However, in Chad, refugee women had a prevalence of 30% compared with 46% population prevalence reported by the WHO in 2019.24 25 These findings indicate that while refugee settings tended to have a lower prevalence of anaemia compared with host populations, anaemia remains a dire issue in resource-limited settings.

In 2019, Kay et al published an analysis of anaemia in refugee sites observed between 2013 and 2016 based on similar UNHCR data (ie, SENS surveys).7 Since our analysis used the same UNHCR SENS surveys, we can compare how the prevalence of anaemia has changed since 2013–2016, to complement our own analysis of change over time within our study. Overall, the prevalence of total anaemia in children was 2.4% lower in our study compared with Kay et al, and the prevalence of moderate/severe anaemia was 1.3% lower (online supplemental table 5).7 In women, the prevalence of total anaemia and moderate/severe anaemia changed by <0.5% compared with Kay et al.7 Surprisingly, for both children and women, the prevalence of total anaemia increased in Chad (8.2% in children and 11.7% in women) while decreasing in the rest of WCA (1.2% in children and 11.3% in women).7 However, in both ESA, as well as in Ethiopia, the prevalence of total anaemia decreased by ~5% in children and 1% in women compared with Kay et al.7 In Ethiopia, the prevalence of anaemia decreased by 5% in children and women compared with Kay et al.7

Overall, in the refugee sites included in this study, these findings indicate that globally, the prevalence of anaemia has been relatively stable, but there have been marked changes in the prevalence of anaemia at the regional and country level across these broad periods. We also confirmed the previous findings of Kay et al that there is stark variability in the prevalence of anaemia within countries and regions (figure 1).7 However, Kay et al reported that the prevalence of anaemia in refugee sites was similar to population prevalences. In contrast, we found that most regions had a lower prevalence of anaemia in refugee sites than population prevalences. Although WCA had an increasing prevalence of total anaemia after the disruptions in 2020, the overall numbers have improved (decreased by 1.2% in children and 11.3% in women) since the 2013–2016 period, as outlined above. However, the prevalence of anaemia among refugee populations in Chad has had marked worsening since 2013–2016, which was exacerbated after 2020. Although the prevalence of anaemia increased in Ethiopia after 2020, the overall prevalence has still improved in Ethiopia and the rest of ESA since the 2013–2016 period. As we have described, there are greater challenges in WCA given the underlying prevalence of anaemia in the population, the differing feeding practices that may increase nutrient deficiency and the prevalence of anaemia-inducing infections, like soil-transmitted helminths and malaria. However, there has been slow but incremental progress in reducing the prevalence of anaemia in ESA, even despite disruptions to services during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Our study did not collect enough information about refugee services and health practices to pinpoint exactly why these differences occurred. However, to try to understand these differences, it is first necessary to understand the underlying aetiologies of anaemia in refugee settings. Nutritional deficiencies (primarily iron and vitamin A) are the most common reason for anaemia globally.2 Other important causes include infections like schistosomiasis, hookworm and malaria, as well as haemoglobinopathies.2 In cross-sectional studies conducted in refugee sites in Ethiopia and Somalia, risk factors for anaemia included a lack of iron supplementation, a lack of deworming medication, diarrhoea and fever.6 28 A recent study evaluating the feeding practices of children in refugee sites found that in refugee populations in sub-Saharan Africa, infants were more likely to initiate and exclusively breastfeed through the first 6 months and continue breastfeeding up to 23 months of life than were infants in host populations.29 However, they also found that refugee children in ESA were far more likely to initiate and exclusively breastfeed up to 8 months than were refugee children in WCA (85% vs 38% initiate breastfeeding and 84% vs 56% exclusively breastfeed, respectively).29 Breastfeeding provides protective factors for preventing and reducing anaemia. Although iron concentration in breast milk is low,30 exclusively breastfeeding has been demonstrated to prevent iron deficiency for the first 4 months of life.31 In settings with poor sanitation and hygiene, the use of bottles may be a source of infection, which is a risk factor for anaemia.32 Poor sanitation has specifically been linked to iron deficiency anaemia,33 suggesting that breastfeeding may be protective when water sanitation quality is poor. The differences in feeding practices in refugee children between ESA, compared with WCA, may explain a large degree of the higher overall prevalence of anaemia in WCA. Given the generally lower prevalence of anaemia in refugee settings compared with host populations, it is surprising to note that the same study found that refugee children were less likely to be introduced to semisolid food by 6–8 months than children in the host populations, and had very low access to flesh foods.29,1 While significant progress has been made,34 the prevalence and intensity of soil-transmitted helminth infections remain higher in WCA compared with ESA.35 In a recent study, the pooled prevalence of malaria in sub-Saharan Africa was 26%, but Niger and Nigeria ranged 28–39% while Uganda and Tanzania ranged 7–20%.36 This combination of differing infant and young child feeding practices and varying prevalences of blood-consuming parasites creates greater barriers to anaemia control in WCA and suggests the need for a tailored approach to anaemia control in this region. In the USA, up to 87% of resettled refugees are infected with soil-transmitted helminths, which include hookworm and schistosomiasis.37 In 2021, malaria was the second most common cause of morbidity among refugees and was responsible for 8% of deaths in refugees.38 Malaria infections can be reduced with relatively low-cost insecticide-treated bed nets and indoor residual spraying.38 Infections can also be mitigated with access to rapid diagnostics and effective therapeutics. Soil-transmitted helminths can be controlled with simple mass drug administration among children. The WHO framework for accelerating anaemia reduction noted that, although iron-deficiency anaemia is a predominant aetiology globally, specific communities may have different underlying aetiologies, requiring tailored interventions.4 Given the variability we have described between and within regions, it is unlikely that a one-size-fits-all approach is the best way to address anaemia in refugee sites. Surveys that better characterise the underlying aetiology of anaemia which can be used to tailor future interventions at a site-specific level, may be needed to target improvement in control of these conditions that lead to higher prevalence of anaemia.

Our study is subject to several limitations. First, it is limited by the use of SENS surveys, which lack contextual variables that would help to understand why the prevalence of anaemia has changed differentially within individual refugee settings as well as at the country and regional levels. Collection of additional variables, such as complete blood count to determine the type of anaemia, iron studies, screening for infections that could lead to anaemia and survey-level infant and young child feeding practices would allow us to better understand why prevalence of anaemia before and after 2020 changed in opposite directions in East and West Africa. Second, the limited number of surveys conducted in South Asia makes it difficult to draw conclusions about how anaemia is changing in refugee settings in that region. Finally, some SENS surveys conducted by UNHCR were excluded from this analysis because they did not collect haemoglobin.

Conclusion

In summary, this analysis adds to the limited body of evidence describing the prevalence of anaemia in children and non-pregnant displaced women, in refugee settings. This provides an important update to the analysis covering the period from 2013 to 2016 and provides a longitudinal update in the changes that have occurred since that time, including before and following health service disruptions related to COVID-19. The overall rates of anaemia were high and remained high in children since the prior study, but important regional changes have occurred. While the overall prevalence of anaemia is lower in women compared with children, it is still concerning and has only minimally changed from the prior study. However, we did find that in comparison to the WHO estimates of anaemia in 2019, refugee settings tended to have a lower prevalence of anaemia than host populations. We also found that there have been large changes at the country and regional levels, with WCA and Chad having the largest increases in anaemia over time while ESA have shown modest improvements. This study lays the groundwork for further analyses which would elucidate the differences in the underlying causes of anaemia and identify the reasons for the differential findings in refugee and non-refugee settings. This would allow for improved and targeted prevention and treatment of anaemia. These data could better guide UNHCR and country’s ministries of health on how to provide the most appropriate interventions in their respective refugee sites.
