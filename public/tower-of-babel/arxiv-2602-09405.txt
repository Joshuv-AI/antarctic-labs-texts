Is Memorization Helpful or Harmful? Prior Information Sets the Threshold
Chen Cheng, Rina Foygel Barber

Abstract
We examine the connection between training error and generalization error for arbitrary estimating procedures, working in an overparameterized linear model under general priors in a Bayesian setup. We find determining factors inherent to the prior distribution π \pi , giving explicit conditions under which optimal generalization necessitates that the training error be (i) near interpolating relative to the noise size (i.e., memorization is necessary), or (ii) close to the noise level (i.e., overfitting is harmful). Remarkably, these phenomena occur when the noise reaches thresholds determined by the Fisher information and the variance parameters of the prior π \pi .



1
Introduction

In this work, we consider the question of
overfitting
in high-dimensional statistical models: should we allow our trained model to overfit to the training data, perhaps even interpolating the data perfectly, or should we instead constrain the training process to return low-complexity models that avoid overfitting? This core question lies at the heart of statistics and machine learning in practice, and has received substantial attention in the theoretical literature as well, over several decades. Despite its importance and ubiquity, however, this question is still not fully understood: there are a range of findings that are apparently at odds with each other.
In classical statistical machine learning, it is standard to train a model that exhibits some intrinsic low-dimensional structure, such as sparsity or low-rank, to ensure that we avoid overfitting
[
35
,
10
,
11
]
.
On the other hand, recent work by
12
suggests that, in an overparameterized regime, memorization is
necessary
for generalization—that is, it is necessary to choose a model that overfits, in order to achieve optimal prediction error.
And, another line of literature on the “benign overfitting” phenomenon can be viewed as lying in between these two extremes, establishing that overfitting is not harmful (but, perhaps not necessary) for good predictive performance
[
6
,
7
,
5
,
21
,
13
]
. How can we reconcile these different views?

We examine this question from the perspective of a Bayesian framework, in the context of a specific problem: high-dimensional linear regression. Concretely, we consider the model

y=X\theta+\mathsf{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{n}),

where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is drawn from a prior
\pi
, and where
d\geq n
(the overparameterized regime).
In this model, an overfitted estimator
\widehat{\theta}
is one that has (mean square) training error substantially below the noise level
\sigma^{2}
.
At a high level, our findings reveal that the question of whether overfitting is necessary or is instead harmful for optimal predictive peformance depends entirely on the nature of the prior
\pi
. If
\pi
encodes some sort of latent low-dimensional structure (for instance, encouraging approximately sparse
\theta
) then overfitting is harmful, while if
\pi
is uninformative then overfitting becomes necessary. Strikingly, our findings suggest the (asymptotically optimal) determining factors delineating these scenarios for the prior
\pi
with a differentiable density
p>0
are the Fisher information and variance parameters:

2.1
Problem setup

We first specify our problem setting and introduce the corresponding quantities of interest.

Overparameterized linear model under a general prior
\pi
.

Consider a design matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, whose rows are vectors
x_{1}^{\top},\cdots,x_{n}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The design matrix may be fixed or random, as we carry out all our calculations conditional on
X
. We assume that
d\geq n
(the overparameterized regime), and that
X
has full row rank (almost surely).

Given a prior
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a noise level
\sigma^{2}>0
, we assume the following model for the observed response vector
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

The Bayesian model
\mathscr{M}_{X}(\pi,\sigma^{2})
:
y=X\theta+\sigma\tau
where
(\theta,\tau)\sim\pi\times\mathsf{N}(0,I_{n})
.

That is, this is a Gaussian linear model, with coefficients
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
sampled from the prior
\pi
.

Training error and prediction error.

We study estimating
\theta
of the linear model given the design matrix
X
and the observation
y
. Given an estimator
\widehat{\theta}=\widehat{\theta}(X,y)
, we define its expected training error as

\mathsf{Train}(\widehat{\theta})=\mathbb{E}\left[{\frac{1}{n}\left\|{X\widehat{\theta}-y}\right\|^{2}\mid X}\right],

where implicitly, the expected value is computed with respect to the model
\mathscr{M}_{X}(\pi,\sigma^{2})
. The training error will typically take values in the range
[0,\sigma^{2}]
. Indeed,
any estimator
\widehat{\theta}
that interpolates the data will have
\mathsf{Train}(\widehat{\theta})=0
—that is, perfect memorization. On the other hand, an estimator with zero overfitting will have
\mathsf{Train}(\widehat{\theta})=\sigma^{2}
(i.e., the true noise level).

We can also define its prediction error at a new test point
x^{\prime}
as
\mathbb{E}\left[{(x^{\prime}{}^{\top}\widehat{\theta}-x^{\prime}{}^{\top}\theta)^{2}\mid X}\right]
. In particular, if
x^{\prime}
is random and mean-zero, and is independent from the training data
y
, we can equivalently define the prediction error as

\mathsf{Pred}_{\Sigma}(\widehat{\theta})=\mathbb{E}\left[{\left\|{\widehat{\theta}-\theta}\right\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\mid X}\right],

where
\Sigma=\textnormal{Cov}(x^{\prime})
is the covariance of the random test point, and where
\|v\|_{\Sigma}:=\sqrt{v^{\top}\Sigma v}
. (If
\Sigma=I_{d}
, then this is simply the mean-squared error (MSE) of the estimator
\widehat{\theta}
.)
We will also write

\mathsf{Cost}(\widehat{\theta})=\mathsf{Pred}_{\Sigma}(\widehat{\theta})-\mathsf{Pred}_{\Sigma}^{*}\textnormal{ \ where \ }\mathsf{Pred}_{\Sigma}^{*}=\inf_{\widehat{\theta}}\mathsf{Pred}_{\Sigma}(\widehat{\theta}),

i.e., this measures the excess prediction error of the estimator
\widehat{\theta}
.
2
2

2

Since
X
is treated as fixed (i.e., we condition on
X
throughout), we should interpret the definition of
\mathsf{Pred}_{\Sigma}^{*}
as computing an infimum over all possible maps
y\mapsto\widehat{\theta}(X,y)
, i.e., functions
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As a technical note, we will assume throughout that any estimator
\widehat{\theta}
considered in this paper is square-integrable (with respect to the distribution of its input, the observed response
y
), so that all defined measures of training and prediction error are finite.

The key question of this paper is the following: what is the relationship between the training error and the prediction error of an estimator—and, to ensure optimal predictive performance (i.e., minimal prediction error), what training error should we aim for?
In particular, we are interested in identifying settings where these interesting phenomena may occur:

•

Settings where
memorization is necessary
for generalization: when is it true that any estimator
\widehat{\theta}
that is (near) optimal for predictive error, must have training error close to zero, i.e.,

\mathsf{Cost}_{\Sigma}(\widehat{\theta})\approx 0\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathsf{Train}(\widehat{\theta})\leq o(\sigma^{2})\ ?

•

Settings where
overfitting is harmful
for generalization: when is it true that any estimator
\widehat{\theta}
that is (near) optimal for predictive error, must have training error close to
\sigma^{2}
, i.e.,

\mathsf{Cost}_{\Sigma}(\widehat{\theta})\approx 0\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathsf{Train}(\widehat{\theta})\geq\sigma^{2}-o(\sigma^{2})\ ?

2.2
Comparing to the Bayes-optimal estimator

Under our assumption of the Bayesian model
\mathscr{M}_{X}(\pi,\sigma^{2})
, the Bayes estimator is given by the posterior mean,

\widehat{\theta}_{\mathsf{B}}(X,y)=\mathbb{E}[\theta\mid X,y].

The following proposition demonstrates that
\widehat{\theta}_{\mathsf{B}}
plays a crucial role for the motivating questions of this paper.

Proposition 1
.

Under the setting and notation above, for any positive definite
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
\widehat{\theta}_{\mathsf{B}}
achieves the optimal prediction error, i.e.,

\mathsf{Pred}_{\Sigma}(\widehat{\theta}_{\mathsf{B}})=\inf_{\widehat{\theta}}\mathsf{Pred}_{\Sigma}(\widehat{\theta})=\mathsf{Pred}_{\Sigma}^{*}.

Moreover, letting
\lambda_{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\|X\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}
, for any estimator
\widehat{\theta}
, its excess prediction error satisfies

\mathsf{Cost}_{\Sigma}(\widehat{\theta})=\mathsf{Pred}_{\Sigma}(\widehat{\theta})-\mathsf{Pred}_{\Sigma}^{*}=\mathbb{E}\left[{\left\|{\widehat{\theta}-\widehat{\theta}_{\mathsf{B}}}\right\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\mid X}\right]\geq\lambda_{\Sigma}^{-1}\left({\sqrt{\mathsf{Train}(\widehat{\theta})}-\sqrt{\mathsf{Train}(\widehat{\theta}_{\mathsf{B}})}}\right)^{2}.

In other words, this standard result tells us that the Bayes estimator is optimal for prediction error, regardless of
\Sigma
. Moreover, the cost of choosing a different estimator (in terms of excess prediction error) can be lower bounded via the difference in training errors,
\mathsf{Train}(\widehat{\theta})
versus
\mathsf{Train}(\widehat{\theta}_{\mathsf{B}})
. In particular, any estimator
\widehat{\theta}
that is close to optimal in terms of prediction error, must have a training error that is similar to that of the Bayes estimator:

\mathsf{Cost}_{\Sigma}(\widehat{\theta})\approx 0\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathsf{Train}(\widehat{\theta})\approx\mathsf{Train}(\widehat{\theta}_{\mathsf{B}}).

2.4.1
The role of the prior: connections to effective dimension

In the results above, we have seen that if
\sigma^{2}
is close to zero then memorization appears necessary, while if
\sigma^{2}
is large then overfitting is harmful. How does this relate to the connection between overfitting and model complexity? In particular, we might expect the following:

•

If
\theta
is assumed to lie in a low-complexity model class (e.g., under sparsity), then any accurate estimator
\widehat{\theta}
should have minimal overfitting.

•

On the other hand, if we are in a truly high-dimensional setting (with no assumptions such as sparsity that would induce low-dimensional structure), then we may prefer to overfit, i.e., “benign overfitting”.

In the Bayesian setting considered here, our results can be related to these principles by considering the parameters
\mathsf{V}_{\pi}
and
\mathsf{J}_{\pi}^{-1}
. Essentially, these parameters capture a notion of
effective dimension
.

For intuition, suppose
n=d
and
X
is the identity.
First, suppose our prior
\pi
is quite flat and uninformative. In this case, we would expect
\mathsf{V}_{\pi}
and
\mathsf{J}_{\pi}^{-1}
to both be
O(1)
(for instance, if
\pi=\mathsf{N}(0,I_{d})
then
\mathsf{V}_{\pi}=\mathsf{J}_{\pi}^{-1}=1
). In particular, if the noise level
\sigma^{2}
is
o(1)
then memorization becomes necessary: Corollary
2.1
tells us that cost is high for any estimator with training error
\gtrsim\sigma^{4}
.

At the other extreme, we can consider a setting where
\pi
encodes some knowledge of low-dimensional structure in
\theta
—for instance,
\pi
may be concentrated near some low-dimensional subspace or manifold in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In such a setting, we would expect that overfitting would be harmful, since an optimal estimator should lie near this low-dimensional region and therefore would not have the capacity to overfit. For this type of prior
\pi
,
\mathsf{J}_{\pi}^{-1}
would typically be quite low, and the regime in Corollary
2.1
(i.e., the regime in which our results show that memorization is necessary) becomes negligible. However, there is a subtlety here: if
\sigma^{2}
is extremely close to zero (i.e.,
\sigma^{2}\leq\mathsf{J}_{\pi}^{-1}=o(1)
), then we again find that memorization is necessary. This is an artifact of our Bayesian framework: even if
\pi
is strongly concentrated around some low-dimensional region within
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, since we require
\pi
to have a positive and differetiable density, if the noise level is extremely low then
\pi
is still effectively constant within any sufficiently small neighborhood. Thus if
\sigma^{2}
is extremely small,
\pi
again acts (locally) as an uninformative prior (see Figure
2
for an illustration). On the other hand, if we choose
\pi
to be a distribution that places all its mass on a low-dimensional support (and therefore,
\pi
cannot have a density), this phenomenon would not occur—but this is beyond the scope of our framework.

In the next section, we explore these ideas through concrete examples to demonstrate the role of effective dimension in this problem.

in particular), we see that memorization is necessary when the noise is not too high, since
\mathsf{V}_{\pi}
and
\mathsf{J}_{\pi}
are
O(1)
. We can interpret this finding as showing that, when
\pi
is essentially uninformative, we are in a genuinely overparameterized regime: in the absence of any implicit structural knowledge of
\theta
, overfitting is unavoidably necessary, and strong predictive performance requires memorization.

3.2
Approximately-low-rank Gaussian prior

In the second example, we begin to investigate scenarios characterized by an underlying underparameterized structure, in which the model lacks sufficient capacity to overfit the noise. To capture this structure while still ensure
X\theta
has a density
p
, we consider an approximately low-dimensional prior:

\displaystyle\pi=\pi(n)=\mathsf{N}(0,\Omega(n)),\qquad\Omega=\Omega(n)=\frac{1-\eta}{r}\begin{bmatrix}I_{r}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}+\frac{\eta}{d}I_{d},\qquad 0<\eta\leq 1.

(7)

For small
\eta
, this prior is approximately supported on a low-rank subspace (spanned by the first
r
basis vectors). As
\eta\to 0+
, if
r<n
then the model approaches an underdetermined linear model (where
\mathsf{Train}(\widehat{\theta})
cannot be zero for any estimator), while if
r\geq n
then the model essentially approaches the same setting as the isotropic Gaussian example of Section
3.1
, except with
r
in place of
d
.

Proposition 3
.

Let Assumption
1
hold,
r/n\to\rho\in(0,+\infty)
, and
\pi
defined as in (
7
) above. Define the constant
C=C(\gamma,\rho,\eta)=\eta^{2}(\gamma-\rho-1)+\eta(\gamma\rho-\rho^{2}-\rho)+\gamma(\rho-1)
. For
\sigma^{2}>0
and
\Sigma=I_{d}
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}_{\pi}\to 1,\qquad\mathsf{J}_{\pi}\to\frac{-C+\sqrt{C^{2}+4\eta(\gamma-1)\gamma(\eta+\rho)}}{2\eta(\gamma-1)},\qquad\lambda_{\Sigma}\to(1+\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}

almost surely.
In particular, we have the following limits as
\eta\to 0+
:

Underparameterization limit: if
\rho<1
,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\mathsf{J}_{\pi}=\frac{\gamma(1-\rho)}{\eta(\gamma-1)}+o(\eta^{-1});

Interpolation threshold limit: if
\rho=1
,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\mathsf{J}_{\pi}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\eta(\gamma-1)}}+o(\eta^{-\frac{1}{2}});

Overparameterization limit: if
\rho>1
,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\mathsf{J}_{\pi}=\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}+o(1).

In the regime
\rho>1
(i.e.,
r>n
), even as
\eta\to 0+
the model is still overparameterized, and essentially reduces to the same result as the isotropic Gaussian, in Proposition
2
: we have
\mathsf{J}_{\pi}\to\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}
in place of
\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}
, since the effective dimension is

instead of

2.1
apply as soon as
\sigma^{2}\leq\mathsf{J}_{\pi}^{-1}\asymp 1
.

On the other hand, if
\rho<1
(i.e.,
r<n
), we see different behavior. Indeed, if
\eta
is close to zero (i.e., the prior is strongly concentrated near the low-rank subspace), then if the noise
\sigma^{2}
is not too small, intuitively we would expect overfitting to be harmful since we have knowledge of low-rank structure in
\theta
(as expressed by
\pi

This is confirmed by the theory: by Proposition
3
and Corollary
2.1
, as
\eta\to 0+
we see that memorization is necessary once
\sigma^{2}\leq\mathsf{J}_{\pi}^{-1}=O(\eta)

\eta\to 0+
then any positive noise level will not fall into this regime.

For comparison, we can consider a prior with exact low-rank structure,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline qn:pi_{w}hen_{e}ta=0}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{r})^{\top}\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I_{r}/r),\qquad(\theta_{r+1},\dots,\theta_{d})=0,

Proposition 4

Let Assumption
1

3.3
Mixture of approximately-sparse priors

We now move beyond the Gaussian prior setting, in which the absence of a closed-form expression for
\nabla\log p(y)
makes it challenging for exact evaluation of the key Fisher information quantity
\mathsf{J}_{\pi}
. Nevertheless, in certain cases we can still derive bounds that characterize its behavior. In particular, we consider the mixture of the collection of perturbed
K
-sparse priors:

\displaystyle\pi=\pi(n)=\frac{1}{\binom{d}{K}}\sum_{S\subset[d],|S|=K}\mathsf{N}(0,\Omega_{S}(n)),\qquad\Omega_{S}=\Omega_{S}(n)=\frac{1-\eta}{K}\sum_{i\in S}e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}+\frac{\eta}{d}I_{d}.

(9)

This prior is appropriate for a setting where we believe
\theta
is
K

X\theta
induced by

then has the density

\displaystyle p(y)=\frac{1}{\binom{d}{K}}\sum_{S\subset[d],|S|=K}\phi(y;0,X\Omega_{S}X^{\top}),

where
\phi(\cdot\,;\,\mu,\Sigma)
denotes the density of the
\mathsf{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
distribution.

Proposition 5
.

Let Assumption
1
hold, and
\pi
defined as in (
9
) above. Assume also that
K/n\to 0
. For
\sigma^{2}>0
and
\Sigma=I_{d}
, we have almost surely

\frac{1}{\eta}\cdot e\left({1-\frac{1}{\gamma}}\right)^{\gamma-1}\leq\lim\inf_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{J}_{\pi}\leq\lim\sup_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{J}_{\pi}\leq\frac{\gamma}{\eta(\gamma-1)}.

In addition, when
\gamma\to+\infty
, both sides converge to

Assuming

ensures that, under the prior

, the model is essentially low-dimensional. Similarly to the results of Section

(for the underparameterized case

considered there), here we see that, provided
\eta\approx 0
—i.e., the prior is strongly concentrated on approximately-sparse values of
\theta
—the results only suggest that memorization is necessary if the noise level
\sigma^{2}
is extremely small.

5
Discussion

Our results, extending the memorization phenomenon in
12
from Gaussian priors to general priors, provide summarizing parameters
\mathsf{J}_{\pi}^{-1}
and
\mathsf{V}_{\pi}
, which are intrinsic to the underlying parameter distribution
\pi
and capture a notion of effective dimension. These parameters yield asymptotically optimal guaranties for characterizing when interpolation is necessary or harmful. Furthermore, Theorem
1
does not rely on proportional asymptotics nor assume a high-dimensional ambient space, and thus holds in a fully general setting.

For linear models, the hypothesis class consists essentially of the parametric family
\mathcal{F}_{\Theta}=\{x^{\top}\theta\mid\theta\in\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
. It is of considerable interest to extend the present analysis to more general model classes
\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}=\{f_{\lambda}\}
, where
\Lambda
may be either a parametric or a nonparametric index set; it would also be valuable to investigate analogous phenomena outside the Bayesian framework without imposing a prior distribution on
\Theta
.

Finally, the role of
\lambda_{\Sigma}
is inherently tied to the finite-dimensional ambient space
d<\infty
. In Hilbert spaces
[
5
,
13
]
, one has
\lambda_{\Sigma}=\infty
, while the cited works show that benign overfitting occurs for arbitrary noise levels in such settings. A future theory of memorization in Hilbert spaces will therefore require a fundamentally different analytical approach.

Lemma A.1

Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}X_{2}X_{3}X_{4}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}X_{2}]\mathbb{E}[X_{3}X_{4}]+\mathbb{E}[X_{1}X_{3}]\mathbb{E}[X_{2}X_{4}]+\mathbb{E}[X_{1}X_{4}]\mathbb{E}[X_{2}X_{3}].

The next result is

[

[

34

[
14

Lemma A.2

(Fisher information inequality)

For two independent vectors

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[{\left\|{\nabla\log(p*q)(Z)}\right\|^{2}}\right]}\geq\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[{\left\|{\nabla\log p(X)}\right\|^{2}}\right]}+\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{Y}\left[{\left\|{\nabla\log q(Y)}\right\|^{2}}\right]}.

Lemma B.1

(Variational principle of the Fisher information)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the family of all compactly supported and smooth vector fields from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then for all
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{J}(t)=\sup_{b\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\left\{-\frac{2}{n}\int p_{t}(y)\nabla\cdot b(y)\mathrm{d}y-\frac{1}{n}\int p_{t}(y)\left\|{b(y)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y\right\}.

Proof

By nonnegativity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int p(y)\left\|{b(y)-\nabla\log p_{t}(y)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y=\epsilon_{b}\geq 0,

we immediately have
\mathsf{J}(t)\geq\epsilon_{b}+\frac{2}{n}\int p_{t}(y)b(y)\cdot\nabla\log p_{t}(y)\mathrm{d}y-\frac{1}{n}\int p_{t}(y)\left\|{b(y)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y
. While
b
is smooth and compactly supported, we can integrate by parts and obtain

\displaystyle\frac{2}{n}\int p_{t}(y)b(y)\cdot\nabla\log p_{t}(y)\mathrm{d}y=\frac{2}{n}\int b(y)\cdot\nabla p_{t}(y)\mathrm{d}y=-\frac{2}{n}\int p_{t}(y)\nabla\cdot b(y)\mathrm{d}y.

Here
\nabla\cdot b(y):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}b(y)
denotes the divergence of
b(y)
. Thus taking supremum over all
b\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle\mathsf{J}(t)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{b\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\left\{\epsilon_{b}-\frac{2}{n}\int p_{t}(y)\nabla\cdot b(y)\mathrm{d}y-\frac{1}{n}\int p_{t}(y)\left\|{b(y)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{b\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\left\{-\frac{2}{n}\int p_{t}(y)\nabla\cdot b(y)\mathrm{d}y-\frac{1}{n}\int p_{t}(y)\left\|{b(y)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y\right\}.

The equality holds since
\epsilon_{b}
can be arbitrarily small.
∎

We are now ready to show lower semi-continuity using Lemma

p_{t}(y)\mathrm{d}y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}p(y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0+}\left({-\frac{2}{n}\int p_{t}(y)\nabla\cdot b(y)\mathrm{d}y-\frac{1}{n}\int p_{t}(y)\left\|{b(y)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{n}\int p(y)\nabla\cdot b(y)\mathrm{d}y-\frac{1}{n}\int p(y)\left\|{b(y)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y.

Thus

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to 0+}\mathsf{J}(t)\geq-\frac{2}{n}\int p(y)\nabla\cdot b(y)\mathrm{d}y-\frac{1}{n}\int p(y)\left\|{b(y)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y.

Taking supremum on both sides for

yields

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to 0+}\mathsf{J}(t)\geq\mathsf{J}(0).

(14)

We show

C.3
Proof for the mixture of approximately-sparse priors

We provide the proofs in three parts. In the first part we upper bound the Fisher information by convexity. In the second part we utilize the isoperimetric lower bound from Lemma
A.3
.

Part I: Upper bound by convexity of Fisher information.

For a mixture of density
p(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}p_{i}(y)
where Fisher information exists for each individual
p_{i}
. We can upper bound pointwise by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla p(y)}\right\|^{2}=\left\|{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\nabla p_{i}(y)}\right\|^{2}\leq\left({\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}p_{i}(y)}\right)\cdot\left({\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\frac{\left\|{\nabla p_{i}(y)}\right\|^{2}}{p_{i}(y)}}\right)=p(y)\cdot\left({\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\frac{\left\|{\nabla p_{i}(y)}\right\|^{2}}{p_{i}(y)}}\right).

Apply the pointwise bound to the mixture considered in the example with
m=\binom{d}{K}
,
\alpha_{S}=\binom{d}{K}^{-1}
and
p_{S}=\phi(y;0,X\Omega_{S}X^{\top})
and we have

\displaystyle\frac{\left\|{\nabla p(y)}\right\|^{2}}{p(y)}\leq\sum_{S}\alpha_{S}\frac{\left\|{\nabla p_{S}(y)}\right\|^{2}}{p_{S}(y)}=\frac{1}{\binom{d}{K}}\sum_{S}\frac{\left\|{\nabla\phi(y;0,X\Omega_{S}X^{\top})}\right\|^{2}}{\phi(y;0,X\Omega_{S}X^{\top})}.

Integrating over
y
yields

\displaystyle\mathsf{J}_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\left\|{\nabla p(y)}\right\|^{2}}{p(y)}\mathrm{d}y\leq\int\frac{1}{\binom{d}{K}}\sum_{S}\frac{\left\|{\nabla\phi(y;0,X\Omega_{S}X^{\top})}\right\|^{2}}{\phi(y;0,X\Omega_{S}X^{\top})}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{d}{K}}\sum_{S}\int\left\|{\nabla\log\phi(y;0,X\Omega_{S}X^{\top})}\right\|^{2}\phi(y;0,X\Omega_{S}X^{\top})\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{d}{K}}\sum_{S}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathsf{N}(0,X\Omega_{S}X^{\top})}\left[{\left\|{\nabla\log\phi(y;0,X\Omega_{S}X^{\top})}\right\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{d}{K}}\sum_{S}\Tr\left({\left({X\Omega_{S}X^{\top}}\right)^{-1}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\Tr\left({(XX^{\top}/d)^{-1}}\right).

In the last inequality, we use the uniform lower bound
\Omega_{S}\succeq\eta I_{d}/d
. Let
\mu_{n}
be the e.s.d. of
XX^{\top}/d
,
\mu_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\mu_{\mathsf{MP},\gamma}
by Lemma
A.4
, and by the resolvent identity at
z\to i\cdot 0+
in
[
2
, Lemma 3.11]
we conclude

\displaystyle\mathsf{J}_{\pi}\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\Tr\left({(XX^{\top}/d)^{-1}}\right)=\frac{1}{\eta}\int\frac{1}{\lambda}\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}\lambda)\to\frac{1}{\eta}\int\frac{1}{\lambda}\mu_{\mathsf{MP},\gamma}(\mathrm{d}\lambda)\to\frac{\gamma}{\eta(\gamma-1)}.

We thus obtain the upper bound

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\mathsf{J}_{\pi}\leq\frac{\gamma}{\eta(\gamma-1)}.

Part II: Lower bound by entropic isoperimetric inequality.

We begin by introducing the necessary notation that we will leverage to derive upper and lower bounds for
\mathsf{J}_{\pi}
. Define the differential entropy for
\mathsf{Ent}_{\pi}
for
y=X\theta
by
\mathsf{Ent}_{\pi}=\mathbb{E}\left[{-\log p(X\theta)}\right]>0
(by Jensen’s inequality). With a slight abuse of notations, we also denote
S
by the discrete random variable uniformly drawn from all
\binom{d}{k}
subsets of size
K
of
[d]
. Then we can view the distribution of
y
as drawing
S
first and sampling
y\mid S\sim\mathsf{N}(0,X\Omega_{S}X^{\top})
. We can then define the conditional entropy and mutual information as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Ent}_{\pi\mid S}=\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[{\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi\mid S}\left[{-\log\phi(y\mid S;0,X\Omega_{S}X^{\top})}\right]}\right],\qquad\mathscr{I}(y;S)=\mathsf{Ent}_{\pi}-\mathsf{Ent}_{\pi\mid S}.

Combine the following explicit formula for the differential entropy for multivariate Gaussians, and the familiar entropy bound for the mutual information of a discrete random variable,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Ent}_{\pi\mid S}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{d}{K}}\sum_{S}\left({\frac{n}{2}\log 2\pi+\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\det X\Omega_{S}X^{\top}}\right)=\frac{n}{2}+\frac{n}{2}\log 2\pi+\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\binom{d}{K}}\sum_{S}\log\det\left({X\Omega_{S}X^{\top}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}(y;S)

\displaystyle\leq\mathscr{H}(S)=\log\binom{d}{K}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(i)}}}{{\leq}}K\log\frac{d}{K}+K,

where we use the Stirling’s bound
K!\geq(K/e)^{K}
in (i); we can thus upper bound

\displaystyle\mathsf{Ent}_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Ent}_{\pi\mid S}+\mathscr{I}(y;S)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{2}+\frac{n}{2}\log 2\pi+K\log\frac{d}{K}+K+\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\binom{d}{K}}\sum_{S}\log\det\left({X\Omega_{S}X^{\top}}\right).

We are now ready to apply the entropic isoperimetric inequality in Lemma
A.3
on
\mathsf{J}_{\pi}
, which implies

\displaystyle\mathsf{J}_{\pi}=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[{\left\|{\nabla\log p(y)}\right\|^{2}}\right]\geq 2\pi e\cdot\exp\left\{{-\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[{-\log p(y)}\right]}\right\}=\exp\left\{{\log(2\pi)+1-\frac{2}{n}\mathsf{Ent}_{\pi}}\right\}.

Comibining with the entropy bound yields

\displaystyle\mathsf{J}_{\pi}\geq\exp\left\{{-\frac{2K}{n}\log\frac{d}{K}-\frac{2K}{n}-\frac{1}{\binom{d}{K}}\sum_{S}\frac{1}{n}\log\det(X\Omega_{S}X^{\top})}\right\}.

(16)

Utilizing the special structure of

, we can further upper bound the determinant by the following lemma.

Lemma C.1
.

For all

, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\det(X\Omega_{S}X^{\top})\leq\left({1-\frac{K}{n}}\right)\log\eta+\frac{K}{n}\log\frac{d}{K}+\frac{1}{n}\log\det\left({\frac{XX^{\top}}{d}}\right).

Proof

Denote by

the
K

whose indices belong to
S

\displaystyle\det\left({X\Omega_{S}X^{\top}}\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left({\frac{1-\eta}{K}X_{S}X_{S}^{\top}+\frac{\eta}{d}XX^{\top}}\right)

\displaystyle=\eta^{n}\cdot\det\left({\frac{XX^{\top}}{d}}\right)\cdot\det\left({I_{d}+\frac{(1-\eta)d}{\eta K}(XX^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}X_{S}X_{S}^{\top}(XX^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}}\right)

where in the last inequality, we use

∎

Applying Lemma

\displaystyle\mathsf{J}_{\pi}

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left\{{-\left({1-\frac{K}{n}}\right)\log\eta-\frac{3K}{n}\log\frac{d}{K}-\frac{2K}{n}-\frac{1}{n}\log\det\left({\frac{XX^{\top}}{d}}\right)}\right\}.

We can once more exploit the convergence to the Marchenko–Pastur law as in the previous part, so that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\det\left({\frac{XX^{\top}}{d}}\right)=\int\log\lambda\cdot\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}\lambda)\to\int\log\lambda\cdot\mu_{\mathsf{MP},\gamma}(\mathrm{d}\lambda)=\left({1-\gamma}\right)\cdot\log\left({1-\gamma^{-1}}\right)-1.

The last equality is from
