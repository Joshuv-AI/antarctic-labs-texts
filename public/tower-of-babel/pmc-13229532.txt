Utilization of adjunctive anti-seizure medications to facilitate chronic benzodiazepine discontinuation
Authors: Michael R. Dueno, Gavin T. Howington, Melody Ryan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionWhen discontinuing benzodiazepines, treating withdrawal symptoms can be challenging. Some anti-seizure medications (ASM) may mitigate withdrawal symptoms. This article reviews the literature regarding adjunctive ASM use during benzodiazepine tapering and discontinuation.MethodsA PubMed search focusing on the use of ASMs as adjunctive agents to mitigate benzodiazepine withdrawal symptoms was conducted. Key search teams identified relevant articles, and the references of included articles were evaluated to identify potential additional publications for inclusion. In total, 21 articles met the inclusion criteria, and 44 articles were excluded for various reasons.ResultsSeveral clinical trials, observational studies, case reports, and case series were identified. Carbamazepine was more effective than placebo at 3 months (74% benzodiazepine free vs 52% for placebo). Oxcarbazepine enabled successful rapid detoxification with minimal withdrawal symptoms in a small, noncontrolled study. Valproate, pregabalin, and gabapentin showed improvements in benzodiazepine-free rates, but the results were inconsistent.DiscussionEvidence for ASM in benzodiazepine withdrawal is limited, emphasizing the need for provider discretion. Additional larger clinical trials are warranted to identify optimal agents to mitigate withdrawal symptoms and enhance successful benzodiazepine cessation.

Introduction

Benzodiazepines are widely prescribed for anxiety, insomnia, and a range of neurological and psychiatric conditions. However, long-term use is associated with significant risks, particularly the development of physical dependence and withdrawal. Recurrent benzodiazepine use rates during discontinuation exceed 50% in some studies,1 and abrupt cessation can lead to withdrawal in most people who use them chronically. Approximately 40% to 60% of individuals taking benzodiazepines for longer than 6 months experience moderate to severe withdrawal symptoms, while others exhibit milder withdrawal.2 In a survey of more than 1000 individuals, commonly reported symptoms included anxiety (50.2%), difficulty concentrating (49.7%), insomnia (49.0%), cardiac rhythm abnormalities (30.7%), hallucinations (6.1%), and seizures (4.6%).3 Protracted withdrawal, defined as symptoms persisting beyond 1 year, occurs in an estimated 15% to 44% of people who use benzodiazepines chronically.2

A recent Joint Clinical Practice Guideline on Benzodiazepine Tapering developed by 10 major professional societies and endorsed by organizations, including the American Academy of Neurology and American Geriatrics Society, emphasizes that physical dependence is expected with regular benzodiazepine use and is distinct from substance use disorder.4 The guideline strongly recommends against abrupt discontinuation in physically dependent patients, recommending tapering strategies with dose reductions of 5% to 10% every 2 to 4 weeks.4 Physical dependence can develop within weeks of benzodiazepine use, and the risk of developing benzodiazepine withdrawal is highly dependent on the duration of use, frequency of use per week, and total daily dose. Specifically, moderate daily dose (estimated as 10-15 mg diazepam equivalents) and high daily dose (estimated at >15 mg diazepam equivalents) are more associated with the potential for clinical withdrawal symptoms.4

Although guidelines for tapering benzodiazepines exist,4,5 clinical experience demonstrates that discontinuation is often unsuccessful. Withdrawal symptoms are a significant concern when discontinuing benzodiazepines, potentially hindering cessation. In an outpatient observational study, 63 patients underwent a 25% weekly benzodiazepine dose reduction; 90% of those patients experienced mild to moderate withdrawal.6 Complete cessation failed in 42% of those using short-acting and 32% of individuals using long-acting benzodiazepines. Alcohol consumption and female sex were factors decreasing the success of discontinuation and increasing withdrawal severity.6

Some anti-seizure medications (ASM) show promise in mitigating benzodiazepine withdrawal symptoms due to their effects on gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and its receptors.7-11 When GABA binds to GABA(A), a chloride ion channel opens, leading to hyperpolarization of the neuron, reducing its excitability.12,13 Benzodiazepines enhance GABA’s inhibitory effects on the GABA(A) receptor by binding to allosteric sites, increasing the frequency of chloride channel opening. This enhances neuronal inhibition, leading to the sedative and anxiolytic effects of benzodiazepines.12,13 It is theorized that GABA modulation with ASM will decrease withdrawal symptoms.

Systematic reviews have found insufficient evidence to include specific adjunctive therapy recommendations in clinical guidelines, leaving these decisions to individual clinicians. The Cochrane review by Baandrup et al14 analyzed 35 trials with 2295 participants but found no single intervention tested in more than 4 trials. The quality of evidence was rated low or very low due to small study sizes, inconsistent findings, and poor study design, which prevented firm recommendations on pharmacological interventions for discontinuation.14 Similarly, Brandt et al5 found various adjunctive pharmacotherapies employed, including the anti-seizure medications carbamazepine, valproate, gabapentin, and pregabalin, and did not offer recommendations concerning ASM use in benzodiazepine discontinuation. These gaps in recommendations show a need for analysis of the available data on ASMs as adjunctive therapy.

Clinicians face the following dilemma: Treating benzodiazepine discontinuation (or withdrawal symptoms) with ASM may aid benzodiazepine tapering but may also increase adverse effects and costs. Given these limitations, this review examines the available evidence for ASM use during benzodiazepine taper, providing clinicians with practical guidance on agent selection, dosing, and expected outcomes.

Methods

A PubMed search for articles published before October 28, 2025, focused on the use of ASM for benzodiazepine discontinuation. The search keywords included “benzodiazepine withdrawal” or “benzodiazepine discontinuation” or “benzodiazepine tapering” or “benzodiazepine cessation” and “anticonvulsants” or “anti-seizure medications” or “antiepileptic drugs” or “carbamazepine” or “oxcarbazepine” or “eslicarbazepine” or “valproate” or “valproic acid” or “divalproex” or “gabapentin” or “gabapentin enacarbil” or “pregabalin” or “gabapentinoids” or “levetiracetam” or “brivaracetam” or “phenobarbital” or “phenytoin” or “fosphenytoin” or “lamotrigine” or “topiramate” or “zonisamide” or “tiagabine” or “vigabatrin” or “lacosamide” or “perampinel” or “rufinamide” or “felbamate” or “ethosuximide” or “stiripentol” or “adjunctive therapy.” Additional relevant articles were identified from the references of the retrieved papers (this accounted for 6 of the articles included). Articles that did not include the success of benzodiazepine discontinuation, review articles, non-English language articles, articles that used animal models, and articles that did not mention the use of adjunctive ASMs were excluded. Additionally, the search identified several articles off topic relative to the purpose of this article; these were excluded as well. To avoid confounding, articles in the setting of alcohol withdrawal syndrome were also excluded. A flow diagram describing the inclusion and exclusion criteria can be referenced (Figure).

Carbamazepine and Oxcarbazepine

Carbamazepine

A case series of four geriatric patients suggested that carbamazepine, in addition to an alprazolam taper, supported successful discontinuation.15 The specific carbamazepine regimen used was 400 mg/day in divided doses given as either 100 mg 4 times per day or 200 mg 2 times per day, depending on patient tolerability.15 After alprazolam was successfully discontinued, carbamazepine was gradually tapered over a 2- to 6-day period, with the duration dependent upon the presence of withdrawal symptoms.15

In another small case series, 9 patients transitioning from an inpatient psychiatric unit to an outpatient substance use disorder clinic received carbamazepine adjunctively during benzodiazepine tapering.16 The specific benzodiazepine and duration of exposure varied between patients. Carbamazepine was initiated either before or during each patient’s benzodiazepine cessation. Doses were individualized based on withdrawal severity, with those experiencing more symptoms receiving higher doses of carbamazepine and a slower taper. On day 1, patients were given two 200-mg doses of carbamazepine, with additional benzodiazepine orders on standby for withdrawal. None required extra benzodiazepines. Carbamazepine adjustments ranged from 0 to 800 mg/day, tapers lasted 1 to 6 days (mean 4.2 days). Carbamazepine was discontinued 3 to 14 days after taper completion. Long-term efficacy of the reduced benzodiazepine dose at follow-up was not assessed.16

A prospective, controlled, comparative cohort study of 18 benzodiazepine-dependent inpatients compared tapering with and without carbamazepine.17 The control group (N = 9) underwent benzodiazepine tapering without adjunctive treatment, and the study group (N = 9) received carbamazepine adjunctively. All patients transitioned to diazepam with equivalency based on their daily benzodiazepine dose. Diazepam was reduced by 5 mg every 3 days starting on day 4 until withdrawal symptoms were resolved. Carbamazepine was introduced on day 0, with doses increasing from 200 to 300 mg on day 1, 300 to 400 mg on day 2, and 400 to 600 mg from day 4 onward. Promethazine was available for sleep. Carbamazepine was discontinued 2 weeks after the benzodiazepine taper was completed. Using the Benzodiazepine Withdrawal Symptoms Questionnaire, results demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in withdrawal symptoms in the study group (P<0.05), with a notable decrease from days 9 to 12. Withdrawal symptoms over a 20-day period, plus the time required to discontinue benzodiazepine use, were also significantly reduced in the treatment group (P<0.05).17

In a randomized, double-blind clinical trial, 40 patients who had previous trouble discontinuing their long-term daily benzodiazepine were randomized to carbamazepine (200-300 mg/day) or placebo.18 After 5 weeks, benzodiazepines were tapered by 25% weekly. A significantly higher number of the carbamazepine group remained benzodiazepine-free (95%) versus 62% of the placebo group (P<0.03). At 3 months, 74% of the carbamazepine group remained benzodiazepine-free compared with 52% of the placebo group (P<0.03). The carbamazepine group reported a modest reduction in withdrawal severity by self-report and on the Physician Withdrawal Checklist one week after discontinuing the benzodiazepine compared with the placebo group. These findings suggest that carbamazepine as an adjunctive therapy may mitigate benzodiazepine withdrawal symptoms, increasing benzodiazepine taper success.18

Another randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial studied individuals prescribed alprazolam for general anxiety disorder (n = 35) or panic disorder (n = 36).19 Alprazolam was initiated at 0.5 mg twice daily and titrated to 4 mg/day for the general anxiety group and up to 10 mg/day for the panic disorder group over 9 to 11 weeks. Then, patients received either placebo or carbamazepine while tapering alprazolam. The alprazolam dose was reduced by 25% every 3 days, with adjunct treatment added 1 week following the first reduction. Investigators found 52% of the patients successfully discontinued alprazolam, but only 37% maintained a 4-week alprazolam-free period. Specifically, in the panic disorder subgroup of patients, carbamazepine led to an increased frequency of alprazolam discontinuation with a lower dropout rate as compared with placebo (53% vs 88%; P = 0.05).

Oxcarbazepine

A case series of three patients published by Croissant and colleagues20 describes successful discontinuation of benzodiazepines with the use of adjunctive oxcarbazepine. Each patient was managed within an inpatient facility, and the entire stay lasted 17 to 25 days. Oxcarbazepine was initiated at 150 mg/day and uptitrated over a 12-day period to a maximally tolerated dose of 1200 mg/day.20 Simultaneously, the patient’s benzodiazepine was down-titrated over an 8- to 15-day period, dependent upon the initial diazepam equivalent dose. All 3 patients successfully discontinued their benzodiazepine and tolerated oxcarbazepine, with minimal to no reported issues.20

Another case series conducted by Croissant et al21 investigated the tolerability and efficacy of adjunctive oxcarbazepine for benzodiazepine discontinuation. Participants met the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 4th Edition criteria for benzodiazepine dependence and had unsuccessfully attempted to discontinue their benzodiazepine.21 Patients were categorized into either a high-dose (>30 mg/day diazepam equivalents) or low-dose benzodiazepine group (≤30 mg/day diazepam equivalents). The low-dose group tapered 5 mg/day of diazepam equivalents every 2 days, while the high-dose group reduced by 25% every 2 days, completing the taper over approximately 8 days. Oxcarbazepine was initiated at 150 mg/day on day 1, increasing by 150 mg/day for 4 days, then every 2 days until reaching 1200 mg/day. All 10 patients successfully and rapidly tapered off benzodiazepines with minimal to no withdrawal symptoms.21

Valproic Acid

McElroy et al22 reported a case of a 23-year-old patient prescribed 90 mg/day of diazepam. The patient was transitioned to clonazepam and initiated on valproate sodium, requiring 2250 mg/day to alleviate withdrawal symptoms. Panic attacks and general anxiety symptoms resolved.22 A case series described 4 patients taking chronic benzodiazepines who began valproate sodium 1200 mg/day after experiencing severe withdrawal while on tapering therapy. The addition of valproate sodium mitigated withdrawal symptoms.23

A randomized, open-label trial of 30 subjects who were opioid-and benzodiazepine-dependent assessed valproate’s effect on benzodiazepine withdrawal using Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment Scale-Benzodiazepine (CIWA-B) scores.24 Subjects were randomized to the valproate sodium 20 mg/kg/day group or a control group and underwent a 3-week benzodiazepine taper. The median benzodiazepine dose at baseline was 60-mg diazepam equivalents for the valproate group and 30-mg diazepam equivalents in the placebo group. All patients were converted to equivalent doses of diazepam with the maximum dose of 80-mg diazepam daily. The diazepam equivalent dose was decreased by 10 mg daily to 40 mg/day, then by 5 mg/day. Valproate sodium was gradually reduced during week 3. CIWA-B scores were recorded daily; mean CIWA-B scores for days 15 to 20 were 5.2 (standard deviation [SD] 3.7) for the valproate sodium (n = 14) and 6.3 (SD 3.9) for placebo (n = 15) (P = 0.77).24 Overall, there was no statistically significant difference in withdrawal severity reported.

The first randomized, controlled trial with valproate sodium included 78 participants chronically prescribed benzodiazepines.25 Subjects were assigned in a double-blind manner to trazodone, (dosing range 100 to 500 mg/day; n = 41), valproate sodium (dosing range 500 to 2500 mg/day; n = 19), or placebo (dosing range 2 to 10 capsules; n = 18). Treatment began 1 to 2 weeks before a benzodiazepine taper was initiated, which reduced the dose by 25% weekly for 4 weeks, followed by discontinuation. Adjunctive treatment continued for 7 weeks post-taper. Five weeks after discontinuation, 63 patients remained in the study (81% retention). Benzodiazepine-free rates were highest with valproate (79%) compared with trazodone (64%) or placebo (31%; P<0.05). At 3 months, 54 patients remained (69% retention rate), with 32 remaining benzodiazepine-free; 79% of those given valproate, 53% of those given trazodone, and 50% of those given placebo (P<0.23).25

Gabapentinoids

Gabapentin

A retrospective, cohort study assessed whether gabapentin benefited hospitalized patients undergoing benzodiazepine withdrawal.26 The treatment group (n = 57) received gabapentin for generalized anxiety or neuropathic pain with lorazepam. The control group (n = 115) received only lorazepam for benzodiazepine withdrawal. The outcomes were length of stay and total benzodiazepine exposure during hospitalization. The average gabapentin dose was 300 mg (range, 107-888 mg). The gabapentin group had shorter hospital stays (7.49 vs 20.90 days; P<0.0001) and lower benzodiazepine exposure measured as lorazepam equivalents (14.50 vs 59.60 mg; P<0.0001).26 This study demonstrated that adjunctive gabapentin effectively reduced hospital length of stay and total benzodiazepine exposure.

A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot trial enrolled 19 patients maintained on methadone with benzodiazepine abuse or dependence in an 8-week outpatient study.27 Participants used benzodiazepines at least 4 days per week and received manual-guided supportive psychotherapy. Gabapentin was titrated over 2 weeks to a maximum dose of 1200 mg 3 times daily. Primary outcomes included benzodiazepine use assessed by urine toxicology and self-report, and withdrawal symptoms using the CIWA-B. Sixteen participants had post-randomization data with 50% retention at week 8. The mean gabapentin dose was 2666 mg/day (SD ± 1446 mg). No significant between-group differences were found in benzodiazepine use (P = 0.745), abstinent days per week (P = 0.811), or CIWA-B (P = 0.913).27 This study showed that gabapentin was not effective at helping patients reduce their benzodiazepine use when compared with placebo.

Pregabalin

Evidence for pregabalin includes a case report, a case series, an observational study, and a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. Biermann and colleagues28 describe a case report in which pregabalin (300 mg/day) was used successfully to lessen the symptoms of benzodiazepine withdrawal as a slow tapper was performed (over 45 days). The patient was able to stop their benzodiazepine with manageable withdrawal symptoms. A 2008 case series of 4 patients suggests that adjunctive pregabalin (225-600 mg/day) over 5 weeks helps to mitigate withdrawal symptoms.29

A multicenter, prospective, uncontrolled, observational study enrolled 282 participants chronically taking benzodiazepines who presented for detoxification. Patients received either pregabalin monotherapy (n = 175) or pregabalin in combination with another medication for 12 weeks.30 Common adjuncts included diazepam (n = 29), clonazepam (n = 17), mirtazapine (n = 13), alprazolam (n = 10), and lorazepam (n = 10). Pregabalin doses varied from 25 to 450 mg/day (mean 315 mg/day recorded at week 12). Tapering method, the benzodiazepine, and the selection and dose of adjunctive medications, if any, were at the treating physician’s discretion. The success rate of benzodiazepine discontinuation was 49% for pregabalin monotherapy and 58% for combination therapy (P = 0.1263).30 The findings were not statistically significant, indicating that the addition of various benzodiazepines or mirtazapine did not affect benzodiazepine discontinuation compared with pregabalin alone.

In a randomized, double-blind clinical trial, patients with general anxiety disorder who had used benzodiazepines for 8 to 52 weeks were randomized to pregabalin 300 to 600 mg/day (n = 56) or placebo (n = 50).31 Both groups tapered off benzodiazepines at a 25% dose reduction per week over a maximum of 5 weeks. Those patients who were benzodiazepine free after the taper were administered pregabalin (n = 30) or placebo (n = 19) for 6 weeks and then tapered over 1week. After finishing pregabalin, Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A) scores decreased (−2.0) compared with an increase with placebo (+2.8; P = 0.0008). Physician Withdrawal Checklist (PWC) scores were also lower with pregabalin (6.5 versus 10.3; p = 0.01). Although more patients remained benzodiazepine-free at the end of 6 weeks with pregabalin than with placebo (51.4% vs 37.0%), the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.284).31 Pregabalin significantly improved PWC and HAM-A scores but did not significantly increase cessation success.

Alternate Adjunctive Anti-Seizure Medications That Require Further Research

Levetiracetam, phenobarbital, lamotrigine, and topiramate yielded promising results in murine experiments or small-scale case studies.32-38 However, larger studies focused on benzodiazepine withdrawal are needed before recommending these therapies.

Discussion

Several clinical trials evaluated ASMs as adjuncts to benzodiazepine tapering. All were small, and the methodologies varied, making it difficult to draw firm conclusions.

Carbamazepine and Oxcarbazepine

The strongest evidence supports carbamazepine and potentially oxcarbazepine. In addition to blocking voltage-gated sodium channels, carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine exhibit GABA-agonist activity, leading to muscle relaxation and sedation.7,8 Hence, there is interest in using carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine as adjuncts to benzodiazepine tapering. A case series conducted by Ries et al16 demonstrated promise for carbamazepine but was flawed by a small sample size and varied initial benzodiazepine. Later, Garcia-Borreguero and colleagues17 published a prospective comparative cohort study of carbamazepine. Although this study indicated that carbamazepine reduces withdrawal symptoms, the small sample size necessitates a larger cohort to further evaluate whether carbamazepine can demonstrate a reduction in withdrawal symptoms and sustain benzodiazepine-free periods beyond 2 weeks. One trial showed 95% of patients were benzodiazepine-free 5 weeks after the benzodiazepine taper and that 74% remained benzodiazepine-free at 3 months when given carbamazepine, compared with 62% and 52% for placebo, suggesting carbamazepine has efficacy beyond the immediate withdrawal period.18 However, it is important to note that the sample size of this study was small, limiting validity. Lastly, the randomized, placebo-controlled trial conducted by Klein et al19 showed successful discontinuation of alprazolam when adjunctive carbamazepine was used, but this was temporary as only 37% remained alprazolam-free at 4 weeks. Additionally, only 1 patient received “high-dose” (>4 mg/day) alprazolam in the panic disorder subgroup, who successfully discontinued alprazolam. Also, instead of enrolling participants with a history of chronic benzodiazepine usage, this study initiated alprazolam upon enrollment and subsequently tapered alprazolam, limiting external validity. Furthermore, carbamazepine was not initiated simultaneously with the taper. Oxcarbazepine also demonstrated potential efficacy. A case series evaluating adjunctive oxcarbazepine demonstrated successful discontinuation of benzodiazepines.20 However, it is difficult to draw conclusions based on the sample size (n = 3) and the uncontrolled nature of the study. Croissant et al21 later showed that adjunctive oxcarbazepine may be useful in facilitating benzodiazepine discontinuation. However, while promising, the same sample size of this study was small (n = 10), more robust trials are needed to confirm the results before the routine use of oxcarbazepine as an adjunctive agent.

Both carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine demonstrated efficacy in mitigating benzodiazepine withdrawal symptoms and facilitating successful discontinuation in small studies. Clinicians may consider the use of either agent initiated either prior to or simultaneous with a benzodiazepine taper for suitable patients to improve successful discontinuation.

Valproic Acid

Valproic acid indirectly boosts GABA(A) receptor activation by elevating brain GABA levels. It triggers GABA synthesis via glutamic acid decarboxylase and decreases GABA degradation.9 An in vitro study found valproic acid prolongs GABA-mediate synaptic currents.39 However, studies evaluating the use of valproate as an adjunctive agent have demonstrated inconsistent results. Case reports suggested efficacy at 1200 to 2250 mg daily, and a trial conducted by Vorma et al24 assessed valproate’s effect between groups on CIWA-B scores. The CIWA-B uses a scoring system with a maximum of 80 points. Scores ranging from 8 to 10 indicate minimal to mild withdrawal, 10 to 15 moderate withdrawal, and 15 or higher severe withdrawal.24 Overall, the trial demonstrated that adjunctive valproate did not change withdrawal severity. However, it is crucial to highlight that in this study, at baseline, the groups were not receiving equivalent benzodiazepine doses. Additionally, the first randomized controlled trial with valproate demonstrated high benzodiazepine-free rates (79% vs 31% for placebo) 5weeks post-discontinuation, but this was not sustained at 3 months.25 Although this trial was limited by a small sample size and possible confounders, the study suggests valproate may support short-term benzodiazepine discontinuation success.

Despite promising case reports of valproate’s efficacy in managing benzodiazepine withdrawal, mixed results were seen in clinical trials.22,23 One double-blind, placebo-controlled study showed reduced withdrawal severity with valproate treatment,25 but another did not.24 Owing to the constraints of this limited data, a recommendation for or against valproate cannot be made.

Gabapentinoids

In animal studies, gabapentin does not inhibit GABA reuptake or metabolism.10 It increases intraneuronal GABA concentrations but shows no conclusive activity at GABA(A) nor GABA(B) receptors.10 Pregabalin also does not bind to GABA(A), GABA(B), nor benzodiazepine receptors.11 It does not enhance GABA(A) response in cultured neurons, alter rat brain GABA concentration, nor affect GABA uptake or degradation.11 Continuous pregabalin exposure increases the GABA transporter protein density and enhances functional GABA transport rates in cultured neurons.11 The precise mechanism of gabapentinoids for adjunctive treatment of benzodiazepine withdrawal is unclear because they unconventionally potentiate GABA, but they do not act on the same sites as traditional benzodiazepines. Leung and colleagues26 conducted a retrospective cohort study that demonstrated gabapentin may be useful at reducing hospital length of stay and benzodiazepine exposure. However, a few notable weaknesses of the study include missing baseline benzodiazepine dose, retrospective design, and lack of objective measures of withdrawal. Mariani et al27 conducted a randomized controlled trial comparing gabapentin with placebo. The authors ultimately found that gabapentin was not effective. However, the sample size of this trial was small (n = 19), and generalizability is limited due to concurrent methadone use in the study population. Pregabalin also demonstrated potential efficacy. A multicenter, prospective, observational trial conducted by Bobes and colleagues30 evaluating pregabalin demonstrated no difference in benzodiazepine discontinuation. However, the study lacked power and lacked a control group without pregabalin, limiting its ability to evaluate efficacy. The results do not enhance understanding of pregabalin as an adjunct to benzodiazepine tapering. Lastly, Hadley et al31 compared pregabalin with placebo in a randomized and blinded fashion. The authors concluded that pregabalin improved withdrawal symptoms but did not increase discontinuation success. Gabapentin and pregabalin demonstrated some potential, decreasing hospital stay and total benzodiazepine exposure, and improved PWC and HAM-A scores.28,32 However, the lack of information regarding baseline benzodiazepine requirements and simultaneous administration protocols (gabapentin) and no statistical significance in benzodiazepine discontinuation rate (pregabalin) restrict the promise in clinical efficacy. Although gabapentinoids show promise for managing benzodiazepine withdrawal, the addiction risk associated with gabapentinoids, and the lack of quality clinical trials limit the ability to make a firm recommendation.

Alternate Adjunctive Anti-Seizure Medications That Require Further Research

Other ASMs, such as lamotrigine, levetiracetam, topiramate, and phenobarbital, have too little data to make a recommendation. Limitations of this review include small trial sample sizes, variation in benzodiazepine use across populations studied, and inconsistent outcome measures across the literature, making it difficult to comprehensively assess ASM efficacy in benzodiazepine discontinuation or in reducing withdrawal symptoms.

Conclusion

Carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine are potential options to use as adjunctive therapy during benzodiazepine tapering, considering the preponderance of clinical data. Carbamazepine appears most effective when initiated at 200 mg twice daily and titrated to 200 to 800 mg daily based on tolerance, ideally allowing time to achieve steady state concentrations before beginning the benzodiazepine taper. Oxcarbazepine demonstrated success when initiated concurrently with tapering, using a titration of 150 mg daily, increased by 150 mg daily for 4 days, then every 2 days to reach 1200 mg daily.17,18,21 Both agents may offer added benefit from continuation 2 to 3 weeks post-cessation.

Alternative options include gabapentin, pregabalin, or valproate; however, supporting evidence is limited. Patient-specific factors and clinical judgement should guide their use. Additionally, high-quality research is warranted to identify the optimal agent to mitigate withdrawal symptoms and enhance the likelihood of successful benzodiazepine cessation.
