Esophageal diversion after esophageal catastrophe: from institutional experience to collective progress
Authors: Yota Suzuki, Evan T. Alicuben, James D. Luketich, Omar Awais
Published: 2026

In our recent manuscript in the Journal of Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery (1), we reported our institutional experience with esophageal diversion for complex esophageal disease. As a tertiary referral center for complex esophageal disorders, the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center has managed some of the most challenging elective and urgent esophageal pathologies. In this setting, although infrequently utilized, esophageal diversion has remained an important option. While diversion is often considered a “bailout” procedure in a crisis, it can also provide an opportunity for safe two-stage reconstruction and create a pathway toward functional recovery that would not be possible otherwise.

We are grateful for the editorial board and the thoughtful commentaries from two highly respected esophageal surgery programs, the University of Southern California and the Mayo Clinic (2,3). Their perspectives place our findings in a broader clinical and technical context and, we believe, further emphasize the importance of sharing experience with this uncommon but consequential operation.

The University of Southern California group provided a broad review of the role of diversion in the contemporary management of esophageal perforation (2). This perspective is important because diversion should not be regarded as the primary treatment for most perforations. Similar to other programs, our approach has evolved toward esophageal stenting with drainage (4), as well as selective primary repair in patients with timely presentation and favorable anatomy. These organ-preserving strategies are often preferable when they can achieve reliable source control and a predictable recovery course.

Nevertheless, esophageal diversion remains essential in selected patients with devastating injury, uncontrolled sepsis, hostile tissue planes, or failure of less invasive initial management. Although the management pathway is complex, diversion offers a reliable means of source control without the immediate risk of an anastomotic failure. One of the major contributions of our report, therefore, was not simply to describe operative outcomes, but to characterize the expected clinical course after diversion, including recovery and reconstruction. Because diversion is rare, underreporting of its outcomes and timeline remains a barrier for surgeons, patients, and families, particularly given its substantial effect on quality of life. Although regionalization and referral to tertiary centers should be considered when feasible, clinicians who manage esophageal disease should understand the full pathway and expected outcomes of diversion.

The Mayo Clinic group offered valuable perspectives on operative technique, patient management, and institutional practice (3). We particularly appreciated their discussion of technical variations, such as the location of the cervical esophagostomy and the use of an alternative conduit. Their prior description of complex esophageal reconstruction highlights important points: in this area, careful attention to technical details and institutional experience still carries substantial weight beyond what can be captured by available evidence alone (5). Sharing these management principles is therefore valuable for both surgeons and patients.

The choice of reconstructive conduit is another important area of debate. In our series, although the number of patients was limited, colon interposition was associated with a high leak rate and considerable morbidity. We regard this as an area for improvement in our program. These outcomes likely reflect the complexity of the operation, the need for multiple anastomoses, and the improved but still imperfect physiologic condition of many patients undergoing reconstruction after diversion. We also recognize that good results have been reported with supercharged jejunal interposition (5) and supercharged colon conduits (6), which may offer a pathway to improved outcomes in selected patients. That said, we believe gastric conduit should remain the preferred option in most cases when it is available and preserved, because of its relative technical simplicity and the fewer anastomoses required. Likely because of these factors, although cervical anastomotic leak was not uncommon in our report, most leaks had a limited clinical impact after reconstruction with a gastric conduit.

In conclusion, our study describes outcomes after esophageal diversion in a difficult and uncommon clinical setting. We hope these data provide practical guidance for surgeons facing these challenging scenarios, while overall outcomes remain in need of improvement. In addition to data and evidence, management narratives from experienced centers are essential for improving care across the field. We again thank the authors of both commentaries for their thoughtful insights and appreciate the opportunity to respond.
