On the minimal area of quadrangles circumscribed about planar convex bodies
Ferenc Fodor, Florian Grundbacher

Abstract
. We show that every planar convex body is contained in a quadrangle whose area is less than ( 1 − 2.6 ⋅ 10 − 7 ) ​ 2 (1-2.6\cdot 10^{-7})\sqrt{2} times the area of the original convex body, improving the best known upper bound by W. Kuperberg.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
52A27, 52A10, 52A40

1.
Introduction and Results

It is one of the classical problems in discrete geometry to investigate the approximation properties of planar convex figures by convex
n
-gons.
This problem is not only interesting for its own sake, but it also plays an important role in the theory of packing and covering, see L. Fejes Tóth
[
6
]
, and in computational geometry, for references, see Hong et al.
[
9
]
.

Let
K
be a convex body (compact convex set with non-empty interior) in the Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For
n\geq 3
, let
C_{n}(K)
(
I_{n}(K)
) be a convex polygon with at most
n
sides containing
K
(contained in
K
) that has minimal (maximal) area.
Let us denote the area of (Lebesgue) measurable sets by
|\cdot|
. The quantities

c_{n}=\inf\frac{|C_{n}(K)|}{|K|},

and

i_{n}=\sup\frac{|I_{n}(K)|}{|K|},

where the infimum and the supremum are taken over all convex bodies
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, have been studied extensively. It can be shown by standard compactness arguments that both
c_{n}
and
i_{n}
are, in fact, attained. In the inscribed case, Sas
[
14
]
proved that

i_{n}=\frac{n}{2\pi}\sin\frac{2\pi}{n},

where equality holds if and only if
K
is an ellipse. As is too common in approximation problems, we know less about the circumscribed case. The only
c_{i}
whose exact value is known is
c_{3}=2
, and the extremal figures are parallelograms; see Gross
[
8
]
, Eggleston
[
4
]
, and Chakerian
[
2
]
. The upper estimate
c_{4}\leq\sqrt{2}
was proved by Chakerian and Lange
[
1
]
(see also Chakerian
[
2
]
). W. Kuperberg
[
11
]
proved that
c_{4}<\sqrt{2}
, but did not give an explicit upper bound less than
\sqrt{2}
. Kuperberg conjectured that the extremal figures are the affine regular pentagons and that
c_{4}=\frac{3}{\sqrt{5}}
. Hong et al.
[
9
]
proved that every unit-area convex
pentagon is contained in a convex quadrangle of area at most
\frac{3}{\sqrt{5}}
, and that equality holds only for affine regular pentagons, thus
c_{4}\geq\frac{3}{\sqrt{5}}
.
However,
c_{4}
is still unknown for general planar convex bodies
K
, and not even an explicit
\varepsilon>0
has been found such that
c_{4}\leq(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{2}
for all
K
.

We note that in the special case when
K
is centrally symmetric, Petty
[
12
]
proved that the area of the minimum-area parallelogram circumscribed about
K
is at most
\frac{4}{3}|K|
, with equality only for affine regular hexagons. Since minimum-area circumscribed quadrangles around centrally symmetric planar convex bodies are also centrally symmetric with the same centre,
c_{4}=\frac{4}{3}
for such planar convex bodies (see also Pełcziński and Szarek
[
13
]
).

For
5\leq n\leq 11
, the currently known best upper bound is
c_{n}\leq\sec\frac{\pi}{n}
due to Ismailescu
[
10
]
, who improved on an earlier bound by Chakerian
[
2
]
. For
n\geq 12
, L. Fejes Tóth’s bounds
[
5
]
are still the best:

\frac{n}{\pi}\tan\frac{\pi}{n}\leq c_{n}\leq\frac{n-2}{\pi}\tan\frac{\pi}{n-2}.

For more information and references about related results, we suggest the reader consult the Notes section of L. Fejes Tóth’s seminal book
[
6
]
, the
Lagerungen
, which has recently been translated into English by G. Fejes Tóth and W. Kuperberg and which contains an up-to-date survey on the current state of the art on problems mentioned in the book. We also refer to the introduction of Hong et al.
[
9
]
for an overview of results on approximations of convex
m
-gons by
n
-gons.

Our main theorem adds to Kuperberg’s result
[
11
]
in which it is proved that
c_{4}<\sqrt{2}
.
Our goal is to find an
\varepsilon>0
such that for every planar convex body
K
there exists a quadrangle
Q
such that
K\subset Q
and
|Q|\leq(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{2}\,|K|
. We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex body.
Then there exists a quadrangle
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
K\subset Q
and
|Q|<(1-2.6\cdot 10^{-7})\sqrt{2}\,|K|
.

The methods we employ can be considered rather elementary,
and our ideas are based on an approach by Ismailescu
[
10
]
.
He also gave a proof of the fact that
c_{4}\leq\sqrt{2}
,
and it turns out that using appropriate stability improvements in his arguments
allows us to find an explicit
\varepsilon
as described above.

In Section
2
, we describe Ismailescu’s proof with some modifications. Section
3
contains some geometric preparations. We prove Theorem
1.1
in Section
4
.

Original Proof by Ismailescu

Let us first introduce the necessary notation.
For
X,Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

, and
\rho\in\mathbb{R}

we write
X+Y:=\{x+y:x\in X,y\in Y\}

and

The

\rho

are given by
v+X=\{v\}+X
and
\rho X:=\{\rho x:x\in X\}
, respectively.
The convex hull of
X
is abbreviated as
\conv(X)
.
We denote the maximum norm on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty}
, with associated unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}
.

Second, we discuss the existence of minimum-area quadrangles circumscribed about a planar convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Note that such a quadrangle always has to be convex.
Moreover, it is not difficult to see that there exist some points
v^{1},\ldots,v^{4}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
K\subset Q:=\conv(\{v^{1},\ldots,v^{4}\})
and
Q
has minimum possible area.
If
Q
has precisely three vertices, then all of them must belong to
K
, that is,
K=Q
.
Otherwise, we could separate
K
from any vertex of
Q
that
K
does not contain
and intersect
Q
with the separating half-space containing
K

to obtain another convex hull of at most four points with smaller area than
Q
.
If
Q
has at most two vertices, then
|Q|=|K|=0
, which cannot happen since
K
has non-empty interior.
Either way, the factor
\sqrt{2}
can be reduced to
1
.
Throughout the paper, we may therefore always assume that
Q
is a proper convex quadrangle, that is,
Q
has exactly four vertices.

Next, we require the following two results.
The first can be found, for example, in
[
6
]
*p. 9.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex body and
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
a proper quadrangle
such that
Q
is a minimum-area circumscribed quadrangle of
K
.
Then all midpoints of the edges of
Q
belong to
K
.

The second result is known as Varignon’s theorem.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
Q
be a proper convex quadrangle and
P\subset Q
be a convex quadrangle with vertices at the midpoints of the edges of
Q
.
Then
P
is a parallelogram, and
|Q|=2\,|P|
.

We now turn to the proof by Ismailescu.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex body.
Then there exists a quadrangle
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
K\subset Q
and
|Q|\leq\sqrt{2}\,|K|
.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{c(3-4\delta)(v^{1}_{1}-1)}{5(1-2\delta)}=\zeta_{c,\delta}(v_{1}^{1}).

Since
-\frac{c}{2}\leq v^{1}_{1}
, the claim now follows from (
2
).
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We verify two more preliminary lemmas before proving the main theorem.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a proper convex quadrangle such that the midpoints of its edges are the vertices of
\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}
.
Then
Q\subset 3\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}
.

Proof.

Assume, to the contrary,
that
Q\not\subset 3\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}
.
Then
Q
must have a vertex
v\notin 3\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}
.
Since the vertices of
\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}
are boundary points of
Q
,
we see that
Q
is contained in the union of the slabs

\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:-1\leq x_{1}\leq 1\}
and
\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:-1\leq x_{2}\leq 1\}
.
In particular, one coordinate of
v
must have an absolute value of at most
1
.
Therefore, we may assume that
|v_{1}|\leq 1
and
v_{2}>3
.

Now, let
u
and
w
be the vertices of
Q
that are connected to
v
via edges of
Q
.
We may assume

\frac{v+u}{2}=(1,1)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{v+w}{2}=(-1,1).

Then
u_{2},w_{2}<-1
and
u_{1},-w_{1}\geq 1
, where we cannot have both
u_{1}=1
and
w_{1}=-1
.
But then one of
u
and
w
has two coordinates with an absolute value larger than
1
,
which is a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
v\in R\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}
for some
R>0
.
Then for any
r\in[0,R+1]
, we have

\left(1-\frac{r}{R+1}\right)v+\frac{r}{R+1}\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}\subset\conv(\{v\}\cup\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2})\cap(v+r\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}).

Proof.

The triangle inequality yields for any
u\in\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}
that

\displaystyle\left\|\left(1-\frac{r}{R+1}\right)v+\frac{r}{R+1}u-v\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{R+1}\left\|u-v\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r}{R+1}(\left\|u\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|v\right\|_{\infty})\leq\frac{r}{R+1}(1+R)=r.

This clearly suffices to verify the claim.
∎

We are now finally ready to verify the initially discussed improvement of the factor
\sqrt{2}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We reuse the notation introduced in the proof of Proposition
2.3
.
We shall distinguish a few cases where we introduce constants for each individual step.
These constants are chosen explicitly only at the end of the proof to allow us to improve the upper bound on the ratio of areas in the theorem.

First, assume that
|Q^{\prime}|>c_{1}|Q|
for some
c_{1}\geq 1
.
Then (
1
) gives

\sqrt{c_{1}}\,|Q|<\sqrt{|Q|\,|Q^{\prime}|}\leq\sqrt{2}\,|K|
and in particular

|Q|<\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{1}}}\sqrt{2}\,|K|.

(3)

For the following considerations, we assume
|Q^{\prime}|\leq c_{1}|Q|=8c_{1}
.

Next, suppose that
x>c_{2}y
for some
c_{2}\geq 1
.
Since clearly
y\geq 2
, we have

|\mathcal{O}|^{2}-|P|\,|Q^{\prime}|=x^{2}+2xy+y^{2}-4xy=(x-y)^{2}>(c_{2}-1)^{2}y^{2}\geq 4(c_{2}-1)^{2}.

From
|P|=4
and
|Q^{\prime}|\leq 8c_{1}
, it follows that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{O}|

\displaystyle>\sqrt{|P|\,|Q^{\prime}|+4(c_{2}-1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{|P|\,|Q^{\prime}|\left(1+\frac{4(c_{2}-1)^{2}}{|P|\,|Q^{\prime}|}\right)}\geq\sqrt{|P|\,|Q^{\prime}|\left(1+\frac{(c_{2}-1)^{2}}{8c_{1}}\right)}.

Thus, (
1
) shows

|Q|\leq\sqrt{2\,|P|\,|Q^{\prime}|}<\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\nicefrac{{(c_{2}-1)^{2}}}{{8c_{1}}}}}\sqrt{2}\,|\mathcal{O}|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\nicefrac{{(c_{2}-1)^{2}}}{{8c_{1}}}}}\sqrt{2}\,|K|.

(4)

From now on, we assume that
x\leq c_{2}y
.
Consequently, we have that

\frac{x^{2}}{c_{2}}\leq xy=|Q^{\prime}|\leq 8c_{1}

and in particular
x\leq\sqrt{8c_{1}c_{2}}
.
Analogously, we may assume that
y\leq\sqrt{8c_{1}c_{2}}
.

Next, suppose that there exists some
v\in K
such that
v+r\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}
does not meet
\mathcal{O}
for some
r>0
.
Lemma
4.1
shows that
Q\subset 3\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}
,
so
v\in K\subset Q
yields in particular
v\in 3\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}
.
Moreover,
\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}=P\subset\mathcal{O}
is true as well, which implies
r\leq 2
.
Hence, Lemma
4.2
shows that
K\supset\conv(\{v\}\cup\mathcal{O})

contains a translated copy of
\frac{r}{4}\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}
that does not intersect
\mathcal{O}
.
Therefore,
|\mathcal{O}|\leq|Q|=8
yields

\left(1+\frac{r^{2}}{32}\right)|\mathcal{O}|\leq|\mathcal{O}|+\frac{r^{2}}{32}|Q|=|\mathcal{O}|+\frac{r^{2}}{4}=|\mathcal{O}|+\left|\frac{r}{4}\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}\right|<|K|,

and by (
1
) further

|Q|\leq\sqrt{2}\,|\mathcal{O}|<\frac{1}{1+\nicefrac{{r^{2}}}{{32}}}\sqrt{2}\,|K|.

(5)

From now on, we assume that
v+r\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}
meets
\mathcal{O}
for all
v\in K
.
In particular, we have

K\subset\mathcal{O}+r\mathbb{B}_{\infty}^{2}\subset(1+r)\mathcal{O}.

Finally, Lemma
3.1
shows for any
c_{3}\geq\sqrt{8c_{1}c_{2}}\geq x,y
and
\delta\in[0,\frac{1}{10}]

that there exists a quadrangle
\tilde{Q}
with
\mathcal{O}\subset\tilde{Q}
and

|\tilde{Q}|\leq\max\left\{\frac{c_{3}(c_{3}(1+\delta)+2\delta)}{1+2\delta},\zeta_{c_{3},\delta}\left(-\frac{c_{3}}{2}\right)\right\}.

Since
K\subset(1+r)\tilde{Q}
and
Q
is a minimum-area quadrangle containing
K
with
|Q|=8
,
we must have

\displaystyle 8

\displaystyle\leq|(1+r)\tilde{Q}|=(1+r)^{2}|\tilde{Q}|

\displaystyle\leq(1+r)^{2}\max\left\{\frac{c_{3}(c_{3}(1+\delta)+2\delta)}{1+2\delta},\zeta_{c_{3},\delta}\left(-\frac{c_{3}}{2}\right)\right\}=:C(c_{3},r,\delta).

Consequently, if
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
,
c_{3}
,
r
, and
\delta
are chosen such that

\sqrt{8c_{1}c_{2}}\leq c_{3}
and

,
then one of the above assumptions must be wrong,
that is, one of (
3
), (
4
), and (
5
) must be true.

We now explicitly choose

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=1+5.3\cdot 10^{-7},

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=1+\sqrt{8c_{1}(c_{1}-1)}<1+2.06\cdot 10^{-3},\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\sqrt{32\left(\sqrt{c_{1}}-1\right)}<2.913\cdot 10^{-3},

in which case the upper bounds in (

) coincide.
We obtain
\sqrt{8c_{1}c_{2}}<2.83134=:c_{3}

and choose
\delta=2.824\cdot 10^{-2}
.
In this case,

\frac{c_{3}(c_{3}+\delta(2+c_{3}))}{1+2\delta}<7.95359

and with (

) also

\zeta_{c_{3},\delta}\left(-\frac{c_{3}}{2}\right)<7.95359.

Finally,

In summary, we have found an admissible choice of

,
c_{2}
,

,

, and

,
for which

\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{1}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\nicefrac{{(c_{2}-1)^{2}}}{{8c_{1}}}}}=\frac{1}{1+\nicefrac{{r^{2}}}{{32}}}<0.99999974=1-2.6\cdot 10^{-7}.

This concludes the proof as discussed above.
∎

5.
Concluding Remarks

We would like to point out the (absurdly) large gap between the conjectured value of
c_{4}
by Kuperberg
[
11
]
(which is the same as the established lower bound by Hong et al.
[
9
]
) and the upper bound in Theorem
1.1
.
It is clear that there is room for improvement. We note that with careful tweaking of the argument and by distinguishing more cases, one could probably improve the upper bound by some orders of magnitude at the cost of making the paper less readable. However, the size of the gap remains. This reminds us of the packing density of the regular tetrahedron, which has long been known to be less than
1
as regular tetrahedra do not tile space, see
[
6
]
*Section 16.8 for history of the problem and references. However, the best known upper bound is
(1-2.6\cdot 10^{-25})
by Gravel, Elser, and Kallus
[
7
]
, while the best construction, by Chen, Engel, and Glotzer
[
3
]
, yields
0.856347\ldots
, with a huge gap between them. Determining the exact value of
c_{4}
is probably not as formidable as that of the packing density of the regular tetrahedron (for which there is not even an explicit conjecture) but being the ”simplest” case of a long-standing approximation problem it certainly deserves further attention.
