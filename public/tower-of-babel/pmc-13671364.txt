A suzetrigine-based multimodal analgesic protocol in spine surgery: a single-center retrospective cohort study of opioid consumption, pain, and length of stay
Authors: Teja Yeramosu, Gabriella A Perez, Huniya Masood, Janeliz Mercado, Allison L Williams, Andrew P Alvarez, Oscar Santalo, Daniel R Fassett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Patients receiving suzetrigine had lower directly observed 48-hour opioid consumption after spine surgery.•The lower 48-hour opioid consumption was supported by an all-patient sensitivity analysis.•After multivariable adjustment, suzetrigine was not independently associated with 48-hour opioid consumption.•Day-of-surgery and discharge opioid consumption were also lower with suzetrigine, while pain scores and length of stay were similar between groups.•These findings support further prospective, multicenter evaluation of suzetrigine as part of multimodal analgesia after spine surgery.Keywords: Suzetrigine, NaV1.8 inhibitor, Nonopioid analgesic, Opioid-sparing, Multimodal analgesia, Spine surgery, Postoperative pain, Length of stay, Enhanced recovery

Introduction

Within the past 2 decades, prescription opioid use in the United States has risen dramatically, alongside growing concern regarding its contribution to misuse and dependence [1,2]. Among all surgical subspecialties, spine surgery carries one of the highest burdens of postoperative pain and, consequently, one of the highest rates of opioid utilization [3]. Patients undergoing spinal decompression and instrumented fusion routinely require opioid analgesia for days to weeks following surgery, with approximately 2%–8% of previously opioid-naïve patients progressing to long-term opioid use after spine surgery [[4], [5], [6]].

Beyond the risk of prolonged use, excessive postoperative opioid exposure is independently associated with worse clinical outcomes. Patients prescribed more than 225 morphine milligram equivalents (MME) postoperatively report lower satisfaction, worse functional outcomes, and higher rates of opioid dependence at 90 days [7]. In addition, prior research has demonstrated that for every 100 MME increase in cumulative postoperative opioid exposure, hospital length of stay increases by 1.1 days [8]. This relationship is further compounded by opioid-related adverse effects, including respiratory depression, nausea, constipation, sedation, and falls [9]. Collectively, these findings have driven considerable interest in strategies aimed at reducing postoperative opioid reliance without compromising analgesia.

Multimodal analgesia (MMA) protocols have been the primary strategy for achieving this goal. Regimens combining nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), acetaminophen, gabapentinoids, muscle relaxants, and regional anesthesia techniques have been associated with reduced postoperative opioid use, earlier mobilization, and shorter hospital stays [10,11]. A recent propensity-matched analysis demonstrated that an opioid-sparing anesthesia protocol in adult spinal deformity surgery reduced postoperative pain, opioid consumption, LOS, and opioid-related complications [12]. Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols built around these strategies have become standard at many spine centers [13]. However, while MMA protocols have been refined over the past 2 decades, the core pharmacologic agents available to spine surgeons, acetaminophen, NSAIDs, gabapentinoids, and opioids, have remained largely unchanged.

Suzetrigine (Journavx, Vertex Pharmaceuticals) is a first-in-class, oral, selective inhibitor of the voltage-gated sodium channel NaV1.8, approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in January 2025 for the treatment of moderate-to-severe acute pain in adults [14,15]. NaV1.8 is selectively expressed on peripheral nociceptors, and suzetrigine blocks pain signal transmission without central nervous system effects, thereby avoiding the sedation, euphoria, and addictive potential of opioids. In 2 Phase 3 trials involving postoperative pain following abdominoplasty and bunionectomy, suzetrigine demonstrated superiority over placebo and noninferiority to hydrocodone-acetaminophen [14].

However, whether a peripheral pain signal inhibitor can meaningfully reduce opioid requirements in spine surgery, where postoperative pain involves deep tissue disruption, bone work, and instrumentation, and likely exceeds the original models in which suzetrigine was studied, remains unknown. The purpose of this study was to assess the impact of adding suzetrigine to a standard multimodal analgesic protocol on opioid consumption, pain scores, and length of stay following spine surgery at a single institution. It was hypothesized that the addition of suzetrigine to a standard multimodal analgesic protocol would result in a measurable reduction in postoperative opioid consumption and length of stay (LOS) without compromising recovery.

Methods

Study design and data source

This was a single-center, retrospective, observational cohort study using a prepost protocol implementation design from January 1, 2025, to March 31, 2026. Patients were identified using EPIC SlicerDicer reporting, and data were obtained through electronic medical record review. This study protocol was deemed exempt by the institutional review board as a Quality Improvement and Quality Assurance (QI/QA) study. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [16].

Adult patients (18 years of age or older) were eligible for inclusion if they underwent elective inpatient spine surgery by the senior author during the study period. The suzetrigine cohort comprised 50 consecutive patients who underwent surgery after protocol implementation and received uninterrupted suzetrigine therapy throughout hospitalization. Patients received suzetrigine 50 mg orally every 12 hours during hospitalization; a 100-mg loading dose was administered preoperatively or on postoperative day (POD) 0 at the surgeon's discretion. The median number of 50-mg doses administered was 5 (range, 1–20), reflecting variation in length of stay.

The control cohort comprised 50 patients who underwent comparable procedures before protocol implementation and did not receive suzetrigine as part of their postoperative analgesic regimen. Postoperatively, all patients were weight-bearing as tolerated and participated in physical therapy in accordance with the institution's standard spine surgery pathway.

Variables

Demographic data abstracted included age, sex, race/ethnicity, and body mass index (BMI). Clinical variables included baseline pain score (numeric rating scale [NRS], 0-10), patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) use, and comorbidity burden, as measured by the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), which was recalculated from individual component diagnoses using standard Charlson weights. A history of chronic opioid use was evaluated for all patients and defined as at least 60 morphine milligram equivalents (MME) daily for at least 7 consecutive days before admission, based on available data from the state prescription drug monitoring program. Smoking status was not recorded as a discrete variable and therefore could not be included as a covariate. Institutional protocol required preoperative smoking cessation.

Surgical characteristics were classified from operative notes. The spinal region was categorized as cervical, lumbosacral, thoracic, or multi-region, and the surgical approach was classified as anterior, posterior, or combined anterior-posterior. Procedures involving hardware placement or fusion, including anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), posterior instrumented fusion, interbody fusion, and arthroplasty, were classified as instrumented. Only pure decompression procedures (laminectomy, discectomy, or foraminotomy) and hardware removal without new fusion were classified as noninstrumented. Procedure complexity was categorized as decompression only, ACDF/anterior fusion, posterior fusion, interbody fusion, combined anterior-posterior, or revision/removal.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was total MME administered within 48 hours of surgery. Secondary outcomes included MME on POD 0, MME on the day of discharge, average NRS pain scores on POD 0, at 48 hours, and at discharge, postoperative hospital length of stay (LOS), and bowel function. In-hospital falls and 30-day all-cause readmission were also recorded.

Bowel-movement counts were abstracted from routine nursing documentation. Absence of bowel-movement documentation was treated as missing rather than as zero bowel movements. Similarly, pain scores not documented in the medical record were treated as missing and not imputed as zero.

All opioid medications were converted to MME using standard conversion factors [17]. POD 0 MME reflected opioids administered in the postoperative period and did not include intraoperative or anesthesia-administered medications.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent t-tests. Non-normally distributed variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are reported as n (%) and compared using chi-square or Fisher's exact test. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d. Analyses used available cases for each outcome.

Multivariable linear regression was performed for directly observed total MME at 48 hours, pain score at 48 hours, and LOS, adjusting for age, BMI, baseline pain score, chronic opioid use, instrumented fusion, and race (White vs. non-White). These covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance [[18], [19], [20]]. Multivariable models used complete cases for all included variables. Patients with declined or unreported race/ethnicity were treated as missing for the race covariate rather than classified as non-White. Interbody fusion and the number of vertebral levels, which had been included in earlier exploratory model specifications, were omitted from the final models to limit model dimensionality.

Prespecified subgroup analyses compared outcomes stratified by chronic opioid use, instrumentation, spinal region, and procedure type. All subgroup analyses were considered exploratory. Given the number of subgroup comparisons and the limited sample size, no correction for multiple comparisons was applied, and subgroup findings are reported as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. For subgroup analyses of 48-hour MME, only patients with a directly observed cumulative 0-48-hour MME value were included, consistent with the primary endpoint analysis.

The primary 48-hour MME analysis was restricted to patients with a directly observed cumulative 0-48-hour opioid total and included 44 suzetrigine and 43 control patients. Six suzetrigine and 7 control patients were discharged before completion of the 48-hour postoperative window. Because these patients had a shorter observation period, their through-discharge opioid totals were not treated as directly observed 48-hour values in the primary analysis. To evaluate the potential effect of excluding early-discharge patients, an all-patient sensitivity analysis included all 100 patients. For patients discharged before 48 hours, the total cumulative inpatient opioid dose administered from surgery through discharge was used as their observed postoperative opioid exposure. A second sensitivity analysis used discharge-day MME for the early-discharge patients.

Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with VIFs > 5 considered indicative of problematic multicollinearity. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 (2-sided). Analyses were performed using Python version 3.13.5, with SciPy version 1.17.0 and statsmodels version 0.14.6.

Surgical characteristics

The distribution of spinal region did not differ between cohorts (p = .539). Cervical procedures comprised 22.0% of the suzetrigine cohort and 34.0% of controls; lumbosacral procedures, 52.0% and 48.0%; thoracic procedures, 2.0% and 2.0%; and multi-region procedures, 24.0% and 16.0%, respectively. Instrumented procedures represented 76.0% of the suzetrigine cohort and 80.0% of controls (p = .810). The remaining procedures were noninstrumented decompression or hardware-removal procedures.

Secondary outcomes

Exploratory secondary analyses showed lower opioid consumption in the suzetrigine cohort on postoperative day (POD) 0 and at discharge. Median POD 0 MME was 5.0 (IQR, 0.0–10.0) versus 10.0 (IQR, 4.0–20.0) (p = .010; d = −0.56), and median discharge MME was 5.0 (IQR, 0.0–15.0) versus 15.0 (IQR, 2.8–30.0) (p = .014; d = −0.48). Pain scores did not differ at POD 0 (median, 4.0 vs. 4.6; p = .072), 48 hours (3.7 vs. 4.6; p = .108), or discharge (3.6 vs. 4.0; p = .352).

Postoperative length of stay was similar between groups (median, 3.0 [IQR, 2.0–4.0] days in both cohorts; p = .171; d = −0.33). The number of documented bowel movements did not differ (median, 1.0 vs. 0.0; p = .168), nor did bowel-regimen utilization (92.0% vs. 96.0%; p = .678). In-hospital falls occurred in 1 patient in each cohort (2.0% vs. 2.0%; p = 1.000), and 30-day readmission rates were 4.0% and 6.0%, respectively (p = 1.000).

In the multivariable model for directly observed 48-hour MME (n = 83; R² = 0.257), the suzetrigine treatment coefficient was −9.45 MME (95% CI, −19.50 to 0.61; p = .065) and did not reach statistical significance after adjustment for age, BMI, baseline pain, chronic opioid use, instrumented fusion, and race (Figure; Supplementary Table 2). Baseline pain score was associated with greater 48-hour opioid consumption (β = 2.43 MME per NRS point; p = .004).

Discussion

Spine surgery is frequently associated with substantial postoperative pain and opioid exposure despite widespread use of multimodal analgesia. Postoperative pain management after spine surgery requires effective analgesia while minimizing opioid exposure, underscoring the need for nonopioid agents that can complement multimodal analgesic pathways. This study compared postoperative opioid consumption, pain scores, length of stay, and short-term clinical outcomes between patients receiving suzetrigine as part of a multimodal analgesic protocol and patients managed without suzetrigine after spine surgery. The principal findings were that patients receiving suzetrigine had lower directly observed cumulative 48-hour opioid consumption, as well as lower opioid consumption on postoperative day (POD) 0 and on the day of discharge. There were no significant differences in documented pain scores, postoperative length of stay, bowel function, in-hospital falls, or 30-day readmission. Although the unadjusted 48-hour MME comparison favored suzetrigine, this association was not statistically significant after adjustment for measured baseline and surgical characteristics.

Our findings contribute to the limited but growing literature on suzetrigine as a nonopioid analgesic. Prior Phase 3 trials demonstrated efficacy for acute postoperative pain after abdominoplasty and bunionectomy, but spine surgery presents a distinct pain profile related to muscle dissection, osseous work, neural decompression, and instrumentation [14]. Suzetrigine selectively inhibits NaV1.8 on peripheral nociceptors without the central nervous system effects associated with opioids [14,15], and a recent review of postoperative pain management in spine surgery identified this mechanism as a promising addition to multimodal analgesic pathways [21]. Our study suggests that these potential opioid-sparing effects may also extend to patients undergoing spine surgery.

One notable finding was the lower cumulative 48-hour MME among patients receiving suzetrigine. This finding was supported by the all-patient sensitivity analysis. However, the treatment association was not statistically significant after adjustment for measured covariates. In the adjusted model, higher baseline pain was independently associated with greater 48-hour opioid consumption, consistent with prior literature [19].

This study also determined that patients receiving suzetrigine required fewer opioids on POD 0 and on the day of discharge. This early opioid-sparing signal may be clinically relevant beyond the immediate postoperative period. Yang et al. demonstrated in a cohort of 1,294 patients that poor pain control in the first 24 hours after elective spine surgery was independently associated with failure to achieve meaningful improvement in disability scores at up to 2 years of follow-up [22]. This finding suggests that interventions targeting immediate postoperative pain may have implications for long-term patient-reported outcomes. Additionally, several studies have established that opioid consumption in the first 24 hours after surgery is an important predictor of postoperative opioid use trajectories. In a prospective cohort study of patients undergoing abdominal, joint, and spine surgery, MME per 24 hours of hospitalization was the primary predictor of whether patients followed a “decreasing” or “persistent” opioid use trajectory, with the latter group having 3.7-fold greater odds of opioid use at 6 months [23]. Similarly, total inpatient MME has been shown to be a significant predictor of long-term opioid use following lumbar interbody fusion [24].

In this study, patients receiving suzetrigine who underwent cervical procedures had lower POD 0 opioid consumption than patients who did not receive suzetrigine. This finding may reflect the fact that cervical procedures, particularly ACDF and cervical laminectomy, tend to produce moderate rather than severe postoperative pain compared with multi-level lumbar fusions, especially when performed through an anterior approach [25,26]. In this setting, a peripheral pain signal inhibitor may be sufficient to displace a meaningful portion of opioid need. While this subgroup was small and these results should be interpreted with caution, they suggest that patients with cervical spine disease may warrant particular consideration for suzetrigine-inclusive protocols in future prospective studies.

A related finding was observed in noninstrumented procedures. Moran et al. recently reported that among opioid-naïve patients undergoing outpatient spine surgery, endoscopic procedures had the lowest average opioid consumption (48.6 MME), and 17.5% of all patients required no postoperative opioids when managed with a multimodal preoperative regimen [27]. These types of spine procedures are increasingly performed in outpatient or ambulatory settings, where reducing opioid requirements may have practical implications for same-day discharge eligibility and postoperative monitoring.

Opioid-naïve patients represent an important population in spine surgery because minimizing initial perioperative opioid exposure may reduce the risk of persistent postoperative use [28]. In our study, among opioid-naïve patients, those receiving suzetrigine had lower POD0 opioid consumption (5.0 vs. 10.0 MME; p = .055) and a shorter median LOS (2 vs. 4 days; p = .091) compared with controls, although neither difference reached statistical significance. Although the direction of the findings in our cohort suggests that opioid-naïve patients may warrant further study as a population potentially responsive to nonopioid analgesic strategies, these exploratory results require confirmation in larger prospective cohorts.

Beyond opioid dependence, reducing perioperative opioid exposure may also mitigate common opioid-induced adverse effects that complicate postoperative recovery. Constipation and sedation-related falls are well-documented sequelae of opioid use [29]. In the current study, bowel function, fall rates, and 30-day readmission rates did not differ between cohorts, and readmission events were not associated with the analgesic protocol. However, the small sample size and low baseline event rates likely limited the ability to detect such differences. Whether the magnitude of opioid reduction observed with suzetrigine is sufficient to translate into fewer opioid-related adverse events warrants investigation in larger cohorts.

Overall, the findings from this study support continued investigation of suzetrigine as part of multimodal analgesia after spine surgery. Opioid prescribing after common spine procedures has declined substantially with increasing adoption of multimodal analgesia, enhanced recovery pathways, and prescribing restrictions [3,30,31]. Suzetrigine offers a mechanistically distinct approach that may complement existing nonopioid agents rather than duplicate their effects [14,15,21]. Larger prospective studies are needed to determine whether the opioid-sparing associations observed in this cohort persist across procedure types and translate into meaningful longer-term clinical benefits.

This study is not without its limitations. Its retrospective prepost design and lack of randomization introduce the potential for temporal confounding, selection bias, and unmeasured differences between cohorts. Race/ethnicity differed between groups, and residual confounding remains possible despite multivariable adjustment. In addition, all procedures were performed by a single surgeon at a single center, which may limit generalizability. The primary 48-hour MME analysis included only patients with directly observed cumulative opioid data through 48 hours and therefore remains susceptible to informative censoring related to early discharge. The all-patient sensitivity analysis retained early-discharge patients, using their actual cumulative inpatient opioid exposure through discharge; however, these values reflect shorter observation intervals and are not equivalent to directly observed 48-hour totals. Pain scores and bowel movement data were derived from routine nursing documentation, and undocumented values were treated as missing rather than zero. Concurrent patient-level use of other nonopioid medications, including NSAIDs, acetaminophen, and gabapentinoids, was not captured and may have differed between study periods. The modest sample size also limited power for subgroup analyses, and no correction for multiple comparisons was applied to exploratory outcomes. Finally, the study did not evaluate postdischarge opioid prescriptions, longer-term opioid use, patient-reported satisfaction, functional recovery, or cost-effectiveness. Despite these limitations, this study provides early clinical data on suzetrigine as part of a multimodal analgesic protocol after spine surgery.

Conclusions

In this retrospective single-surgeon cohort, patients receiving suzetrigine had lower unadjusted cumulative 48-hour, POD 0, and discharge opioid consumption compared with patients who did not receive suzetrigine, while documented pain scores and postoperative length of stay were similar between groups. The 48-hour treatment association was not statistically significant after multivariable adjustment. These findings suggest that suzetrigine may have an opioid-sparing role within multimodal analgesia after spine surgery, but prospective multicenter studies with standardized dosing and complete postoperative opioid ascertainment are needed to confirm these results and define the patients most likely to benefit.
