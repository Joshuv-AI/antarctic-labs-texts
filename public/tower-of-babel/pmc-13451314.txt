Data-driven optimization of mechanical performance and durability of cementitious composites in acidic environments
Authors: Danial Nasr, Rezvan Babagoli, Nematollah Soleymani Dashtaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The durability of cementitious materials in aggressive acidic environments remains a critical challenge for infrastructure systems, primarily due to the complex and nonlinear interactions between mixture design parameters and degradation mechanisms. Despite extensive experimental investigations, the predictive understanding of these interactions and their combined influence on mechanical performance is still limited. In this study, an integrated experimental–computational framework is developed to systematically evaluate and predict the mechanical behavior of cementitious composites under acidic exposure conditions (pH = 3 and 6). A comprehensive experimental program was conducted to measure compressive, tensile, and flexural strengths at different curing ages, alongside durability-related indicators.To address the limitations of conventional empirical approaches, several machine learning models including. Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, and Elastic Net were implemented to capture the nonlinear relationships between input variables and performance responses. Among these, the Random Forest model exhibited the highest predictive accuracy (R2 up to 0.95), demonstrating its robustness in modeling complex material behavior. Experimental results showed that compressive strength increased from approximately 20 MPa to 60 MPa with curing time. Exposure to acidic conditions resulted in a reduction of mechanical properties by approximately 20–30%. The results reveal that the interaction between mixture composition and exposure conditions governs performance more significantly than individual parameters alone, while excessive incorporation of supplementary constituents may reduce long-term efficiency due to diminished matrix reactivity. The developed machine learning model achieved a high prediction accuracy with a coefficient of determination (R2) of up to 0.95. The prediction errors were limited to approximately 2.0 MPa for compressive strength and 0.10 MPa for tensile and flexural strength, indicating high reliability. Feature importance analysis further identifies curing time and environmental conditions as the dominant controlling factors. The proposed framework provides new insights into the data-driven design of durable cementitious systems and offers a reliable basis for optimizing material performance in aggressive service environments, particularly for infrastructure applications such as pavements and industrial structures. Sensitivity analysis indicated that curing time was the most influential parameter (45%), followed by environmental condition (35%) and material composition (20%).

Introduction

The most popular building material in the world, concrete, is unstable over geological durations and is not intrinsically unbreakable. Over time, the calcium, silicon, iron, and aluminum compounds within its structure decompose into more stable forms such as limestone, clay, and silica sand. Consequently, engineers aim to enhance the durability of concrete to extend the service life of structures and minimize lifecycle costs associated with repairs and maintenance1. Given the ever-increasing use of concrete in infrastructure projects, including dams, investigating its performance in harmful environments—particularly acidic conditions—is essential. This research seeks to understand the factors influencing the durability of concrete in acidic environments, considering its inherent alkaline nature2.

Fiber-reinforced concrete (FRC) represents a significant advancement in composite materials, where fibers play a critical role in enhancing the tensile and compressive strength of concrete. Extensive studies have demonstrated that FRC exhibits superior damage control properties under dynamic loading compared to conventional concrete. Its excellent integrity, continuity, and energy absorption capacity make it an ideal material for producing curved, resistant surfaces and applications requiring impact resistance. The high compressive strength of FRC is achieved through optimal particle distribution and homogeneous mixing. Today, advanced countries utilize various fiber types, including glass, polypropylene, steel, and even carbon fibers, to produce FRC for diverse industrial applications3.

In highly hydrated concrete, low-solubility calcium hydrates remain stable within the alkaline environment of concrete, where the pH of the pore solution typically ranges between 12.5 and 13.5, depending on the concentrations of K +, Na +, and -OH ions. Under these conditions, hydration products maintain chemical equilibrium with the pore solution. However, any disturbance to this balance, particularly a reduction in pH below 12.5, compromises the stability of these compounds. Consequently, natural waters, industrial effluents, and urban wastewater pose significant threats to concrete durability. The failure rate is strongly influenced by both the pH level and the permeability of the concrete; in low-permeability concrete with a pH above 6, the risk of degradation is minimal4. Furthermore, a decrease in Ca2⁺ ion concentration in the pore solution disrupts the chemical equilibrium, leading to the decomposition of calcium hydroxide and subsequently the C-S–H gel5.

The unique nature of the anion in sulfuric acid (the sulfate ion) results in a distinct damage mechanism compared to other acids. This specificity is particularly critical for important concrete structures such as sewage pipes, water and wastewater treatment facilities, and sulfuric acid production units in petrochemical plants, which are frequently exposed to corrosion caused by sulfuric acid attack. Consequently, a separate and detailed examination of the damage mechanisms resulting from the interaction between sulfuric acid and concrete components is essential for developing stable and durable structures6.

The potential for severe damage in structures exposed to sulfuric acid has prompted extensive research aimed at enhancing the durability of concrete in such harmful environments. Cement hydration products, including lime crystals and calcium silicate hydrate (C-S–H) gel, are particularly vulnerable to sulfuric acid attack. Consequently, researchers have recommended minimizing cement usage to mitigate these effects8. Pozzolanic materials, such as natural pozzolans and iron-smelting furnace slag, offer an effective solution by reducing lime content and improving concrete permeability through their reactive properties9. Studies have consistently demonstrated that the incorporation of these cement substitutes enhances the durability of concrete in sulfuric acid environments10. However, conflicting results have been reported by some researchers who observed negative impacts of cement substitutes on durability11. This discrepancy can be attributed to variations in the physical characteristics, chemical composition, fineness, and pozzolanic activity of the substitute materials, as well as differences in testing conditions.

In addition to the use of cement substitutes, alternative strategies to improve concrete resistance against sulfuric acid include the incorporation of polymer materials12 and the substitution of conventional aggregates with acid-resistant options, such as silica-based aggregates instead of lime-based aggregates13. It is worth noting that the neutralizing effect of lime aggregates may contribute to positive outcomes in certain cases, providing a comparative advantage over siliceous stone materials14. These findings highlight the importance of carefully selecting materials and testing conditions to optimize concrete performance in acidic environments.

Glass fiber reinforced concrete (GFRC)

Glass fiber reinforced concrete (GFRC) is a type of fiber-reinforced composite material designed to enhance the mechanical performance and durability of conventional concrete. It typically consists of cement, fine aggregates, glass fibers, and chemical additives, forming a homogeneous matrix with improved tensile strength, crack resistance, and durability15.

Due to its high strength-to-weight ratio and formability, GFRC is widely used in architectural applications, particularly for facade systems and thin structural elements with thicknesses as low as 10 mm. Compared to conventional concrete, GFRC enables the production of lightweight, complex, and curved components while maintaining adequate mechanical performance, making it suitable for modern construction requirements16.

Despite these advantages, the performance of GFRC is highly influenced by environmental conditions, especially in aggressive environments such as acidic exposure, which can significantly affect its long-term mechanical properties. Therefore, understanding and predicting the behavior of GFRC under varying curing times and environmental conditions is essential for reliable structural applications.

Glass fibers

Glass fibers differ from polymeric fibers in that they possess a three-dimensional network structure rather than a chain-like molecular arrangement, which contributes to their relatively high tensile strength among non-metallic fibers. They are typically manufactured from molten glass into continuous filaments with diameters ranging from 5 to 25 μm, providing uniformity and flexibility suitable for reinforcement applications17.

Due to their favorable properties, including high tensile strength, good chemical resistance, thermal stability, and relatively low cost, glass fibers are widely used as reinforcing agents in cement-based composites such as GFRC. Among the various types of glass fibers, alkali-resistant (AR) glass is particularly important in cementitious environments due to its enhanced durability under alkaline conditions.

Despite these advantages, glass fibers exhibit certain limitations, including brittleness, relatively low modulus compared to advanced fibers (e.g., carbon fibers), and sensitivity to mechanical damage during handling. These characteristics can influence the long-term performance of GFRC, particularly under aggressive environmental conditions18.

Iron blast furnace slag

Slag is a latent hydraulic material that exhibits cementitious behavior when activated in an alkaline environment. Among various types, ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBFS) is widely used in cement-based composites due to its glassy and amorphous structure formed through rapid cooling, which enhances its reactivity19.

The incorporation of slag in concrete mixtures has been shown to improve durability-related properties, including reduced permeability, enhanced resistance to chloride penetration and sulfate attack, and mitigation of alkali–silica reactions. In addition, slag contributes to lower heat of hydration and improved long-term mechanical performance, although early-age strength development is typically slower compared to conventional cement systems20,21.

From an environmental perspective, partial replacement of cement with slag significantly reduces CO₂ emissions and energy consumption associated with cement production. Therefore, slag is widely recognized as a sustainable supplementary cementitious material in modern concrete technology.

Despite these advantages, the performance of slag-containing systems is highly dependent on curing conditions and environmental exposure. In aggressive environments, such as acidic media, the long-term mechanical behavior may vary significantly, highlighting the need for accurate predictive models.

Literature review

Moradian et al.22 explored the impact of various pozzolanic additives on sulfuric acid resistance. Sulfuric acid poses a significant threat to the durability of industrial structures. Their findings revealed that pozzolan incorporation is an effective strategy for enhancing concrete strength in acidic conditions. Samples containing metakaolin exhibited notable weight gain due to ongoing hydration reactions and pore filling with sulfate attack products. Despite prolonged exposure (up to 200 days), these samples gained weight rather than losing it. Zeolite-treated samples accumulated higher surface sulfate levels but minimal sulfate penetration depth22.

Al-Swaidani et al. (2015) predicted and investigated the compressive strength and corrosion resistance of natural pozzolan-containing concrete. Although early-age compressive strength was lower compared to ordinary Portland cement concrete, pozzolan inclusion significantly reduced chloride ion permeability and extended service life in chloride-prone environments by 2–3 times23.

Bondar et al.24 evaluated geopolymer concrete made from alkali-active natural pozzolan under sulfate attack. X-ray diffraction analysis revealed that silica-alumina-based geopolymers exhibited a 19.4% reduction in compressive strength and 0.074% expansion after exposure to sodium and magnesium sulfate solutions24.

Barbhuiya and Kumala (2017) studied concrete stability in acidic environments. Concrete mixtures containing ≥ 30% ash showed a 10% reduction in compressive strength under sulfuric acid exposure but exhibited less weight loss compared to non-ash mixtures. Nitric acid exposure resulted in porous surfaces with salt by-products formed via the reaction of acid with calcium hydroxide25.

Investigating the durability properties of mortar and concrete using pozzolans as a partial replacement of cement has been one of the critical issues for researchers in the past few decades.

The effects of blast furnace slag and waste fiberglass (WFG) on the characteristics of new composite materials were examined by Fanache et al.26. In this investigation, concrete class C16/20 was employed as the basic material, including 0.35–0.7% WFG and 2–10% slag by weight, while washed river aggregates were partially substituted with WFG at different ratios. In a certified laboratory, the final composites’ density, workability, and compressive strength were assessed. The results indicated that increasing WFG content reduced the density of the composites, which ranged from 2358 to 2405 kg/m3—higher than the control sample but lighter due to the lower density of WFG compared to natural coarse aggregates. While the addition of WFG improved certain mechanical properties, it also increased non-homogeneity in the mixture. Optimal performance was achieved with 0.25% WFG and 5% slag, yielding a slight increase in compressive strength. Conversely, incorporating 10% slag led to a 4.2% reduction in compressive strength compared to the reference samples. The study highlights the potential economic and environmental benefits of utilizing WFG and slag in concrete production, including reduced waste disposal costs and lower production expenses. These findings underscore the importance of optimizing WFG and slag proportions to enhance material performance while promoting sustainable construction practices.

Yahyaee et al.27 optimized concrete pavements by incorporating steel and carbon fibers with Ground Granulated Blast Furnace Slag (GGBS). Using 25 mix designs (0.40 water-to-cement ratio, up to 50% GGBS, and 0.5–1.5% fiber content), they evaluated mechanical and durability properties, CO₂ emissions, and cost. While GGBS reduced 28-day compressive strength, the G50S1.5 mix (50% GGBS, 1.5% steel fibers) had only a 5.5% strength reduction at 28 days but a 38% increase at 90 days, outperforming carbon fibers due to better distribution. G50S0.5 achieved a 40.34% CO₂ reduction, while GGBS lowered costs; however, carbon fibers significantly increased them (e.g., G30C1.5 by 208.5%). The study concluded that GGBS with steel fibers enhances strength, durability, and sustainability, making it ideal for pavements, whereas carbon fibers are less viable due to high costs and inferior performance.

In order to improve sustainability and ductility, Zhao et al.28 looked into alkali-activated fiber-reinforced composites (AAFRCs) employing steel slag (SS) as a precursor. The impact of SS as a partial substitute (0–25%) for fly ash (FA) and ground granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBS) on mechanical characteristics was investigated. Three-point bending and single crack tensile testing, as well as tests on compressive and tensile strength, cracking behavior, and meso-scale analysis, were carried out. Tensile strength of 4.86 MPa, tensile strain capacity of 3.94%, and compressive strength of 93 MPa were all attained by the ideal mix, which comprised 15% SS. Reduced mechanical performance resulted from higher SS replacement (> 15%). While SS replacement had a limited effect on cracking characteristics, it weakened fiber-matrix interfacial bonding, improving ductility. SEM analysis confirmed minimal microstructural changes at 15% SS replacement.

Benemaran et al.29 studied the use of alkali-resistant glass fiber (AR-GF) and silica fume (SF) to improve recycled aggregate concrete (RAC), where 100% of natural aggregates were replaced with recycled ones. AR-GF was added at 1–3% by volume, and SF replaced 5–15% of cement weight. Across 384 samples tested at 7, 14, 28, and 56 days, results showed that SF and AR-GF enhanced compressive and tensile strength in single or combined mixtures compared to conventional RAC. However, AR-GF additions above 2% reduced strength. Using TOPSIS analysis, the M15G2 mix (15% SF and 2% AR-GF) was identified as the optimal design, achieving the highest performance score. Microstructural analysis via SEM confirmed improved bonding and reduced defects. Thus, the combination of 15% SF and 2% AR-GF was deemed the best solution for enhancing RAC’s mechanical properties and microstructure.

Abbas et al.30 examined the durability performance of concrete incorporating electronic-waste aggregates combined with different strengthening strategies such as silica fume, polymer latex, and synthetic fibers. Results indicated that the simultaneous use of silica fume and fibers can significantly reduce porosity, water absorption, and sorptivity while improving resistance to acid, chloride, and sulfate attacks. The improvements were mainly attributed to matrix densification and crack-control mechanisms produced by the mineral–fiber synergy. These findings highlight that combining supplementary cementitious materials with fibers can effectively compensate for the weaknesses of recycled aggregates. Nevertheless, most studies focus on waste-aggregate systems, with limited investigation of similar synergistic mechanisms in slag-based or chemically activated binders under aggressive exposure conditions.

The study performed by Adil khan et al.31 applied gene expression programming (GEP) and multi-expression programming (MEP) to predict the depth of wear in fly ash-based concrete using a dataset of 216 records. The GEP model achieved high accuracy (R > 0.989), and SHAP analysis identified testing time and concrete age as the most influential parameters. These machine learning models offer a cost-effective alternative to destructive laboratory tests, supporting the sustainable use of fly ash in construction.

The study performed by Ali et al.32 applied GEP, MEP, XGBoost, and Bayesian models to predict the compressive strength of steel-fiber-reinforced concrete under high temperatures using 307 data points. The GEP model demonstrated the most consistent accuracy across training, validation, and testing phases (R ≈ 0.96–0.97), while SHAP analysis identified temperature as the most influential parameter. The proposed models offer a reliable, cost-effective alternative to physical testing for assessing SFRC performance in fire or high-temperature conditions.

The study performed by ali et al.33 developed GEP, MEP, and XGBoost models to predict the compressive strength of steel-fiber-reinforced concrete under combined extreme conditions (200–1200°C and strain rates from 10⁻5 to 102/s) using 307 experimental data points. The GEP model achieved the highest and most consistent accuracy (R ≈ 0.96 across all subsets), with global sensitivity analysis identifying temperature and strain rate as the most influential parameters. A simplified empirical equation was also derived from GEP, offering a reliable and cost-effective alternative to experimental testing for SFRC performance in fire and blast scenarios.

The study performed by Ali et al.34 employed GEP and MEP to predict the maximum displacement of steel-fiber-reinforced concrete members under blast loading using 107 experimental data points. The GEP model demonstrated superior predictive accuracy (R ≈ 0.96–0.97 across training, validation, and testing), while MEP showed reasonable but comparatively lower performance. Parametric analysis highlighted the influence of fiber properties on SFRC behavior, providing a reliable and simplified approach for optimizing blast-resistant structural design.

The study performed by Ali et al.35 experimentally investigated hybrid fiber-reinforced concrete (HFRC) exposed to temperatures of 200–800°C for durations up to 120 min, finding that dynamic compressive strength initially increases up to 200 °C before declining, with optimal performance at 400 °C and 1.5% synthetic fiber content. An XGBoost model developed using 213 data points achieved high predictive accuracy (R = 0.998 training, 0.899 testing), with global sensitivity analysis identifying temperature and high strain rate as the most influential parameters. The combined experimental and machine learning approach provides valuable insights and predictive tools for designing fire-resistant HFRC infrastructure under extreme thermal and dynamic conditions.

The study performed by Ali et al.36 developed a machine learning framework using Random Forest and XGBoost to predict CO₂ emissions of steel-fiber-reinforced concrete under coupled thermal (200–1200°C) and strain rate (10⁻5–102/s) conditions, moving beyond traditional static life-cycle assessments. The XGBoost model achieved excellent accuracy (R2 = 0.987, RMSE = 8.90 kg CO₂e/m3), with SHAP analysis identifying temperature, cement content, and supplementary cementitious materials as the most influential variables. The study introduces the concept of “emission signatures” linking CO₂ output to mechanical performance under extreme events, providing a scalable tool for designing low-carbon, resilient infrastructure.

The study performed by Ali et al.37 developed an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) model with three hidden layers to predict the maximum displacement of steel-fiber-reinforced concrete tunnel structures under blast loading, using a combined dataset of 299 points (120 experimental + 179 numerical simulations). The optimized ANN achieved high predictive accuracy with R2 values of 0.983 (training), 0.956 (validation), and 0.948 (testing), with RMSE ranging from 2.12–3.14 mm. Sensitivity analysis identified peak overpressure and standoff distance as the most influential parameters, offering a computationally efficient tool for designing blast-resilient underground infrastructure.

In the study performed by Das et al.38,39 a novel strategy for in-situ assessment of healing-induced recovery of mechanical properties (HIRMP) based on the ratio of areas under the stress–strain curve and acoustic emission signals was developed. Experimental results under different healing environments, fiber contents, and pre-damage levels show that this approach is more consistent and comprehensive than conventional methods.

In the study performed by Das et al.40–45 develops high-performance natural seawater coral sand engineered cementitious composites (HP-NSCS-ECC) with outstanding early-age and mechanical properties, achieving 8–10% tensile strain, > 90 Mpa compressive strength, and crack widths below 80 μm. Seawater mixing accelerates hydration and matrix densification, eliminates air-drying shrinkage, and induces a beneficial self-stressing effect, while chloride ingress resistance is enhanced by 40–50% compared to conventional ECC. With only 1.5% PE fibers, the material integrates high strength, exceptional ductility, shrinkage mitigation, and durability, offering a sustainable solution for marine and island construction.

This study performed by Sahani et al.46,47 produces eco-friendly plastic sand bricks from non-segregated waste plastics and river sand, with the 1p:4s mix ratio achieving optimal properties: 8.64 Mpa compressive strength, 0.37% water absorption, and 1.05 Mpa tensile strength. Production costs are under USD 0.10 per brick, upcycling ~ 1 kg of plastic waste each. Compared to conventional bricks, these bricks cut CO₂ emissions by 70–85%, water use by 95%, and costs by 30–50%. Plastic sand bricks offer a viable, sustainable alternative for construction while reducing plastic waste accumulation.

Another study by Zhou et al.48 reviews the application of industrial sludge (IS) and municipal sludge (MS) in sustainable construction materials, focusing on their source characteristics, chemical composition, effects on material properties, and environmental safety. Processing methods such as grinding, calcination, and alkali activation enhance sludge reactivity and material strength, while sludge incorporation reduces carbon emissions—though heavy metal leaching under acidic conditions remains a key durability concern. Future research should prioritize sludge stabilization, mix optimization, and environmental impact assessments, with marine sludge and engineered cementitious composites (ECCs) identified as promising directions.

Although slag-based systems have been widely reported to enhance durability due to pore refinement and reduced permeability, conflicting findings exist regarding their performance under acidic environments. Several studies have demonstrated improved resistance to acid attack attributed to a denser microstructure and reduced ion ingress. However, other investigations have reported increased vulnerability, primarily due to the reduced calcium hydroxide content and the susceptibility of calcium-silicate-hydrate (C–S–H) phases to decalcification under low pH conditions. These contradictory observations suggest that the performance of slag-modified systems is highly dependent on exposure severity, slag replacement level, and chemical environment49.

Similarly, the incorporation of fibers has been generally associated with improved mechanical performance and crack resistance. Nevertheless, under chemically aggressive environments, particularly acidic exposure, the effectiveness of fibers becomes less consistent. While fibers can delay crack propagation and reduce permeability pathways, several studies have reported degradation of fiber–matrix interfacial bonding, leading to reduced load transfer efficiency and potential loss of durability. This indicates that the beneficial role of fibers may be compromised under long-term chemical attack conditions.

A critical limitation in the existing literature is that most studies have investigated slag modification, fiber reinforcement, and environmental exposure independently, without systematically addressing their coupled interactions. In reality, these factors are strongly interdependent, as slag-induced microstructural changes influence fiber–matrix bonding, while acidic environments simultaneously affect both the matrix and the interfacial transition zone.

Furthermore, although recent machine learning-based studies have improved the prediction of durability-related properties, they are typically limited to conventional material systems and do not incorporate multi-factor interactions involving fibers, industrial by-products, and aggressive chemical exposure. As a result, the nonlinear and high-dimensional relationships governing degradation mechanisms remain insufficiently captured.

Based on the above inconsistencies and limitations, a fundamental research question arises:

How do glass fibers and IBFS interact under acidic environments, and can their coupled effects on durability be reliably quantified and predicted using data-driven approaches?

Addressing this question requires an integrated experimental and modeling framework capable of capturing the nonlinear interactions among material composition, reinforcement mechanisms, and environmental exposure.

Recent advances in machine learning (ML) have significantly improved the prediction of engineering properties of cementitious composites. For example, Ahmad et al.50 developed ML models for estimating the compressive strength of sustainable concrete containing waste-derived materials, demonstrating the potential of data-driven approaches for mechanical performance prediction. Likewise, De-Prado-Gil et al.51 applied ensemble learning techniques to estimate the strength of fiber-reinforced concrete mixtures, while Nguyen et al. investigated ML-based prediction of durability-related properties in slag-modified cementitious systems. Although these studies demonstrated promising predictive capability, they primarily focused on isolated mechanical or durability indicators, and none addressed the coupled interaction between supplementary cementitious materials, fiber reinforcement, and chemically aggressive environments.

In addition to the experimental objectives, this study addresses a set of machine learning-oriented research questions aimed at developing a reliable and interpretable predictive framework:Can machine learning models accurately predict durability performance under varying acidic exposure conditions (e.g., different pH levels and exposure durations) using a limited experimental dataset?Which input variables (e.g., IBFS content, fiber dosage, pH level, and exposure time) exert the most significant influence on durability degradation, and can these effects be interpreted in a physically meaningful manner?How do different ML algorithms compare in terms of predictive accuracy, generalization capability, and interpretability when applied to multi-variable degradation systems?

In systems containing induction blast furnace slag (IBFS) and glass fibers, durability under acidic exposure is governed by complex nonlinear interactions involving pore refinement, fiber–matrix interfacial degradation, and matrix dissolution mechanisms. These coupled phenomena cannot be adequately captured through conventional empirical analyses or ML models trained on limited variables. Despite the growing use of ML in cement-based materials research, an integrated predictive framework that simultaneously considers IBFS dosage, glass fiber reinforcement, acidic attack conditions, and durability response remains absent in the literature.

Accordingly, this study aims to fill this gap by combining experimental investigation with machine learning techniques to model the durability performance of glass-fiber-reinforced IBFS composites under acidic environments. The proposed framework provides a more comprehensive understanding of the coupled degradation mechanisms and offers a reliable predictive tool for sustainable material design.

Current research evaluates the use of iron blast furnace slag (IBFS) at varying percentages as a partial cement replacement to enhance the durability of concrete in acidic environments. Additionally, the influence of glass fibers at different percentages on both ordinary concrete and IBFS-blended concrete was examined. To evaluate the chemical effects of acids on concrete performance, two types of acid environments—strong (pH = 3) and weak (pH = 6)—were employed. In order to evaluate the performance and durability of mixtures, compressive strength, tensile strength, flexural strength and RCPT tests were performed.

Although previous studies have examined slag-based cementitious systems or fiber-reinforced concrete separately, limited research has addressed their combined performance under aggressive acidic exposure. Moreover, most available works rely solely on experimental observations and lack predictive tools for mixture design. Therefore, this study aims to (i) experimentally evaluate the coupled effects of IBFS replacement and glass-fiber addition on mechanical performance and durability under acidic conditions, and (ii) develop interpretable machine-learning models capable of predicting strength development and identifying dominant controlling variables. By integrating material optimization with data-driven prediction, this research provides new insight into the design of durable and sustainable concrete for aggressive environments.

Tests were carried out on both new and tough concrete to assess the performance and longevity of the different concrete mix types. To ensure uniform dispersion of glass fibers, a controlled mixing procedure was adopted. Fibers were gradually added to the mixture to prevent agglomeration, and sufficient mixing time was applied to achieve a homogeneous distribution. This approach minimizes the risk of fiber clustering and ensures consistent mechanical performance across specimens.

Curing of samples

Following molding, the specimens were treated for 24 h in the laboratory at a controlled temperature of 23 ± 2 °C under wet conditions. After this initial curing period, the samples were demolded and subsequently stored in water at a temperature of 20 ± 2 °C for further curing until the designated testing age.

For samples intended for exposure to acidic environments, protective capping was applied prior to placement in the acid bath. Capping involves coating two opposing faces of the concrete specimen with a protective material, such as sulfur or resin, to prevent corrosion from the acidic solution. In this study, resin was used for the capping process. After capping, the specimens were immersed in the acidic pool (sulfuric acid with specified pH levels) until the relevant tests were conducted.

For each mixture and testing condition, three replicate specimens were prepared and tested to ensure statistical reliability, and the reported results represent average values. Mechanical properties were evaluated at curing ages of 7, 28, and 91 days to capture both early-age and long-term performance.

The selected pH levels (pH = 3 and pH = 6) were chosen to represent different levels of acidic exposure conditions. A pH value of 3 corresponds to highly aggressive environments such as industrial effluents and wastewater systems, while pH 6 represents mildly acidic environmental conditions. This range enables the assessment of material performance under varying degrees of chemical aggressiveness.

Specimens were immersed in acidic solutions with controlled pH levels, and the pH was regularly monitored throughout the exposure period using a calibrated pH meter.

To maintain consistent chemical conditions and avoid deviations caused by ongoing reactions between the specimens and the solution, the acidic medium was periodically refreshed at predetermined intervals. This approach ensured a stable exposure environment and minimized variability in degradation conditions.

The selected exposure durations represent accelerated laboratory conditions intended to simulate long-term degradation processes within a shorter time frame. Such conditions are commonly adopted to replicate environments encountered in real-world applications, including wastewater systems, industrial infrastructures, and regions exposed to acid rain.

Although laboratory exposure cannot fully replicate field complexity, the adopted protocol provides a controlled framework for evaluating material performance under sustained acidic attack and enables meaningful comparison across different mixture compositions.

The adopted exposure protocol ensures both experimental reproducibility and relevance to real-world aggressive environments.

Experimental program

Given that the objective of this research is to compare the mechanical properties and durability of identical concrete designs under normal water curing and acidic exposure conditions, all test conditions were standardized, including the load application speed on the samples. Prior to testing, samples were removed from the water and acid baths and allowed to dry in open air to eliminate surface moisture. During testing, the smoothest surfaces of the samples were positioned between the jaws of the testing device to ensure consistent contact. Following these preparatory steps, the testing device was activated, and force was applied to the sample.

A digital testing machine was employed to measure compressive, tensile, and flexural strengths. This device displayed the applied force numerically in real-time during the test, with the final value recorded as the ultimate strength of the sample in kilograms per square centimeter (kg/cm2). These values were subsequently converted to megapascals (MPa) for standardization and reported in the results tables. Additionally, weight loss and electrical resistance (RCPT permeability) tests were conducted to evaluate the durability aspects of the samples.

Compressive, tensile and bending strength

To conduct the tests, samples were first dried and then subjected to the capping process. Capping is performed to protect the two faces of the concrete that come into contact with the machine’s loading plates, preventing corrosion during testing. While both sulfur and resin can be used for capping, resin is typically preferred due to its lower cost and ease of application compared to sulfur. The capping process involves applying wide paper glue to the desired face of the sample, followed by pouring resin over it. After 24 h of drying, the same procedure is repeated for the opposite face. It is important to note that any small holes or imperfections on the concrete surface must be filled with resin to prevent sulfuric acid from penetrating the concrete through these areas.

Following the capping operation, the specimens were submersed in water and acidic solutions for curing periods of 7, 28, and 91 days. The pH of the acidic solutions was monitored daily using a pH meter, and adjustments were made as necessary to maintain the desired pH levels. Compressive, tensile, and flexural strength tests were conducted on the samples at each curing age (7, 28, and 91 days) in accordance with relevant standards. Finally, the results obtained from the two environments—normal water and acidic conditions—were compared, and the changes in resistance were quantified.

Compressive strength tests were conducted in accordance with ASTM C3954, while splitting tensile strength tests followed ASTM C49655. Flexural strength tests were performed based on ASTM C7856. All tests were carried out under controlled laboratory conditions to ensure consistency and reliability of the results.

Rapid chloride permeability (RCPT) test

The Rapid Chloride Permeability Test (RCPT) was conducted in accordance with ASTM C120257 to evaluate the resistance of concrete to chloride ion penetration. Prediction models

The selection of machine learning models in this study was not based solely on predictive performance but also on their suitability for handling nonlinear interactions, robustness to limited datasets, and interpretability. Random Forest (RF) was employed as a primary model due to its ensemble-based structure, which reduces overfitting and enhances generalization in small-to-medium datasets. Moreover, RF provides relatively transparent feature importance measures, allowing the identification of key variables influencing durability behavior. In contrast, gradient boosting models such as XGBoost, although often achieving high predictive accuracy, involve more complex parameter tuning and may reduce interpretability due to their sequential learning structure. Therefore, comparing RF with other models enables a balanced evaluation between accuracy and physical insight.

This approach ensures that the selected model not only predicts durability reliably but also contributes to a better understanding of the underlying degradation mechanisms.

All input variables were normalized prior to model training to ensure numerical stability. The dataset contains no missing values due to the controlled experimental design. Outliers were evaluated through residual analysis, and no data points were excluded to preserve the physical integrity of the dataset.

It should be noted that the dataset used in this study is relatively limited in size, which may affect the generalization capability of the machine learning models. To address this issue, appropriate validation strategies were employed to reduce overfitting and improve model reliability.

Furthermore, the selected models were chosen for their suitability for small-to-medium datasets and their ability to capture nonlinear relationships. Despite the satisfactory predictive performance, the results should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger and more diverse datasets are recommended to further improve model generalization and validate the findings.

To ensure model robustness, a validation strategy based on progressive data partitioning and learning curve analysis was implemented. The results show convergence between training and validation errors at approximately 80% of the dataset, indicating strong generalization capability without overfitting.

Results and discussion

Compressive strength

Compressive strength is a critical parameter in the design of concrete structures, influenced primarily by factors such as the water-to-cement ratio, degree of compaction, and age of the concrete. Secondary factors include the weight ratio of aggregates, aggregate quality and size, loading speed, sample dimensions, storage conditions, and the performance of the testing equipment. In this study, cubic specimens measuring 150 × 150 × 150 mm were used to evaluate compressive strength. The experiments were performed at an age of 7 days, with a constant tensile load applied at a uniform rate until failure, allowing the compressive resistance of the samples to be determined. The results indicate that, at early immersion ages in sulfuric acid, the samples containing slag (G40GF0) and glass fibers (G0GF1) exhibit the best resistance to acidic environments. The reduction in strength observed for the G60GF0 sample compared to the G40GF0 sample at 28 days can be attributed to the excessive consumption of calcium hydroxide and cement paste by the higher slag content. At higher slag percentages, a greater proportion of pozzolan remains inactive within the concrete matrix, effectively acting as a neutral filler. As sulfuric acid attacks the concrete surface and penetrates inward, it begins to bypass the inert aggregates. In this scenario, the inactive pozzolan offers no resistance to the acid, facilitating deeper penetration and exacerbating degradation.

The increase in compressive strength observed in mixtures containing IBFS can be attributed to secondary hydration reactions that generate additional C–S–H gel and refine the pore structure. This densification reduces internal voids and improves stress distribution under loading. However, at higher replacement levels, strength reduction may occur due to slower early hydration and dilution of clinker content. The presence of glass fibers does not directly increase compressive strength but contributes indirectly by restricting microcrack propagation, which delays failure and stabilizes the load-bearing matrix.

The observed reduction in strength at fiber contents exceeding 1% can be attributed to several interacting factors. At higher dosages, achieving uniform fiber dispersion becomes increasingly difficult, leading to fiber agglomeration and the formation of localized weak zones within the matrix. In addition, increased fiber content reduces workability, which may result in inadequate compaction and higher air void content. These defects negatively affect the mechanical integrity of the composite. Furthermore, excessive fiber concentration can weaken the fiber–matrix interfacial transition zone (ITZ), limiting efficient stress transfer and reducing the overall reinforcing efficiency. As a result, the beneficial crack-bridging effect of fibers is outweighed by these adverse microstructural effects at higher fiber contents.

Density of samples after 7 day curing.

Density of samples after 28 day curing.

Density of samples after 91 day curing.

Acid attack mainly proceeds through dissolution of calcium hydroxide and decalcification of hydration products. Mixtures containing IBFS show improved resistance because slag reduces Ca(OH)₂ content and promotes formation of chemically more stable hydration phases. The denser microstructure further slows acid penetration. Glass fibers improve resistance by controlling crack propagation and limiting transport channels that accelerate acid ingress.

This phenomenon can be explained by the nature of the aggregates used, which are silica-based and remain neutral in sulfuric acid environments. These aggregates do not react with the acid, leaving only a small portion of the cement paste to participate in the corrosion process. As the acid penetrates the cement paste surrounding the aggregates, the bond between the inert aggregates and the degraded cement paste weakens, causing the aggregates to dislodge with minimal force. This detachment not only contributes to severe weight loss but also exposes new surfaces for acid attack, accelerating the degradation process. The heterogeneity between the cement paste and aggregates further facilitates acid penetration along their interfaces. During testing, it was observed that corrosion by-products detached from the sample surfaces and settled at the bottom of the containers.

As shown in Figs. 12, 13, and 14, the addition of 3% glass fibers results in a significant reduction in density compared to the control sample throughout the entire exposure period in the aggressive acidic environment. Notably, the G20GF0 sample demonstrated improved performance relative to the control sample, with weight losses of 1.98% and 1.93% in sulfuric acid solutions at pH = 3 and pH = 6, respectively. While the incorporation of 1% glass fibers induces only a slight decrease in density, higher percentages of glass fibers (e.g., 3%) lead to a marked reduction in density, underscoring their effectiveness in enhancing resistance to acidic attack.

Figures 12, 13, and 14 further illustrate that the inclusion of glass fibers reduces overall density. Specifically, the G0GF3 sample, containing 3% glass fibers, exhibited the highest weight losses of 2.3% and 4.2% in acidic environments at pH = 3 and pH = 6, respectively. These findings highlight the role of glass fibers in mitigating degradation and improving the durability of concrete exposed to corrosive conditions.

Conclusion

This study investigates the impact of mineral additives, specifically iron-smelting furnace slag and glass fibers used as cement substitutes, on the durability of ordinary concrete samples exposed to sulfuric acid solutions with pH levels of 3 and 6.
