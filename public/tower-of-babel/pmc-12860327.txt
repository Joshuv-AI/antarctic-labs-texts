Recurrent subgaleal abscess following cervicofacial necrotizing fasciitis: A case report
Authors: Emin Tabipoğlu, Ayşe Gül Özer, Fatih Bera Gürtaş, Efecan Çekiç, Mustafa Berker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Subgaleal abscess (SGA) is an uncommon but potentially life-threatening condition characterized by pus accumulation between the galea aponeurotica and skull periosteum. Necrotizing fasciitis (NF) of the head and neck is an aggressive infection with high morbidity and mortality. While SGAs have been reported following trauma, neurosurgical interventions, or regional infections, recurrent SGA secondary to cervicofacial NF (CNF) has not previously been documented.Case Description: We present the case of a 75-year-old woman with diabetes mellitus and multiple comorbidities who developed CNF complicated by recurrent SGA. Despite initial drainage procedures, the patient experienced repeated relapses due to capsule formation. Definitive resolution was achieved only after complete capsule excision, in addition to culture-directed antibiotic therapy.Conclusion: Recurrent SGA complicating CNF is exceedingly rare. This case underscores the importance of early recognition, thorough surgical intervention including capsule excision, and vigilant follow-up to achieve durable infection control in patients with significant comorbidities.

INTRODUCTİON

Subgaleal abscesses (SGAs) are rare but clinically significant entities. They usually occur after trauma, neurosurgical procedures, or through contiguous spread from adjacent infections, and carry a risk of severe sequelae such as cranial osteomyelitis, epidural abscess, or intracranial extension. [2,3] Necrotizing fasciitis (NF) is an aggressive soft-tissue infection characterized by rapid fascial and subcutaneous tissue necrosis. Mortality rates for cervicofacial NF (CNF) range between 18% and 40%, despite modern advances in imaging, surgical technique, and antimicrobial therapy.[4] Diabetes mellitus and other chronic comorbidities are recognized risk factors for both NF and poor wound healing outcomes.[6] Although isolated reports exist of SGA associated with scalp infections or otogenic sources, no cases of recurrent SGA secondary to CNF have been published to date. Here, we present a rare case of recurrent SGA complicating CNF, with emphasis on the pathophysiology of recurrence and the role of capsule excision in definitive management.[8]

CASE PRESENTATİON

A 75-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, and hypertension presented to the emergency department with a 2-week history of the right facial and cervical swelling and 5 days of severe pain. She denied any recent trauma, odontogenic infection, or upper respiratory symptoms. One week prior, she had received a single intramuscular dose of ceftriaxone as an outpatient. Initial Evaluation and First Intervention: On examination, there was non-erythematous, non-fluctuant swelling extending from the right preauricular area to the lateral neck. Laboratory tests [Table 1] demonstrated leukocytosis, elevated C-reactive protein, and increased erythrocyte sedimentation rate.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance ımaging (MRI) revealed NF of the right cervicofacial region with possible mandibular condyle osteomyelitis [Figure 1 and Table 1]. The patient was empirically started on meropenem (1 g IV every 8 h) and teicoplanin (6 mg/kg IV once daily) and was taken to the operating room (OR) for neck exploration, abscess drainage, and tracheostomy for airway protection. Intraoperative cultures grew Streptococcus constellatus, and antibiotic therapy was de-escalated to piperacillin-tazobactam (4.5 g IV every 6 h) plus tigecycline (100 mg IV loading dose, then 50 mg IV every 12 h). First Recurrence and Second Drainage: Three days later, the patient developed a tense, fluctuant swelling over the right temporal-occipital region without erythema or fever. Under local anesthesia, a 1.5 cm incision was made above the right ear, and 60–70 mL of yellow purulent fluid was aspirated [Figure 2a]. The cavity was irrigated with saline, vancomycin powder was applied subcutaneously, and two Hemovac drains were placed. Post-procedurally, antibiotic therapy was changed to sulbactamampicillin (3 g IV every 6 h). Two days afterward, swelling recurred despite the Hemovac drains. The patient returned to the OR; the same incision was reopened, the Hemovac drains were exchanged for a Jackson-Pratt drain, and daily dressings with a cohesive bandage were applied. Sulbactamampicillin was discontinued on postoperative day 5, and oral moxifloxacin 400 mg once daily was initiated. Second Recurrence and Definitive Surgery: On day 10, swelling reappeared in the same region. Under general anesthesia, the prior temporal-occipital incision was extended, and a 2 cm zygomatic incision was added. Approximately 100 mL of pus and organized fibrous membranes were encountered. Using curettes and Adson-Madson forceps, the capsule was completely excised, with extension into the frontal compartment to remove adherent membrane. The field was irrigated with saline and hydrogen peroxide, and a new Jackson-Pratt drain was placed.

Outcome: The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful. The Jackson-Pratt drain was removed on postoperative day 7. At 1-month follow-up, there was no clinical or radiologic evidence of recurrence [Figure 2b].

DISCUSSION

CNF is a rare but life-threatening soft-tissue infection characterized by rapid progression along fascial planes, leading to extensive necrosis of subcutaneous tissue and fascia.[2,3] The presented case of a 75-year-old woman with diabetes mellitus and multiple comorbidities who developed recurrent SGA as a complication of CNF highlights several critical clinical and therapeutic considerations. This case is particularly notable as the first reported instance of recurrent SGA secondary to CNF, underscoring the complexity of managing such infections in patients with significant predisposing factors.[4] The discussion integrates insights from contemporary literature to contextualize the pathophysiology, diagnostic challenges, and optimal management strategies for this rare clinical entity. CNF is an aggressive infection with a predilection for rapid dissemination through the relatively avascular fascial planes of the head and neck, which facilitates bacterial proliferation- and toxin-mediated tissue destruction.[3,5] Diabetes mellitus, as seen in our patient, is a well-documented risk factor for CNF, contributing to impaired immune responses and poor wound healing.[6,7] The presence of comorbidities such as coronary artery disease and hypertension further exacerbates the risk of adverse outcomes by compromising tissue perfusion and immune function.[1,8] In this case, the microbiological isolate, S. constellatus, a member of the Streptococcus anginosus group, aligns with prior reports linking this organism to deep-seated abscesses in the head and neck due to its propensity for abscess formation and polymicrobial synergy.[9,11] The S. anginosus group is known for its ability to form encapsulated collections, which likely contributed to the recurrent nature of the SGA in our patient.[11,12] The subgaleal space, located between the galea aponeurotica and skull periosteum, is a unique anatomical compartment that allows extensive pus accumulation due to its loose connective tissue.[13,14] This anatomical predisposition, combined with the aggressive spread of CNF, facilitated the development of a large SGA in our patient. The recurrent nature of the abscess was likely driven by the formation of a fibrous capsule, which acted as a nidus for persistent infection by shielding bacteria from both host immune responses and systemic antibiotics.[15,16] Scalp abscesses are uncommon infections that typically arise after local trauma or surgery, or by contiguous spread from adjacent craniofacial foci (e.g., sinusitis, otogenic, or cutaneous sources). Clinically, they present with localized pain, tenderness, fluctuant swelling, and erythema; fever may be absent, especially in elderly or comorbid patients. MRI/contrast-enhanced imaging usually demonstrates a rim-enhancing cystic collection within the scalp layers; importantly, progression to SGA can occur because the loose areolar tissue between the galea aponeurotica and the pericranium permits rapid spread. Major complications include cranial osteomyelitis, subperiosteal collections, and – less commonly – subdural/epidural extension or intracranial spread. Management hinges on early surgical drainage/debridement combined with culture-directed antibiotics; delayed or incomplete drainage increases the risk of recurrence and deeper extension. In recurrent or encapsulated cases (as in our patient), definitive control often requires capsule excision in addition to antimicrobial therapy.[1]

This phenomenon is consistent with literature emphasizing that encapsulated abscesses in the head and neck require complete surgical excision to prevent relapse.[7,17] Early diagnosis of CNF and its complications, such as SGA, is critical but challenging due to the nonspecific initial presentation, which may mimic less severe conditions such as cellulitis or simple abscess.[3,18] In our patient, the absence of erythema and fever during the initial recurrence underscores the potential for subtle clinical signs to delay recognition.[4] Imaging played a pivotal role in both diagnosis and management. Contrast-enhanced CT provided rapid identification of the fluid collection and suggested mandibular condyle osteomyelitis, while MRI offered superior resolution for delineating fibrous membranes and guiding surgical planning.[13,19] These findings align with recent literature advocating for multimodal imaging in head and neck infections to assess the extent of disease and detect complications such as osteomyelitis or intracranial extension.[5,20] The Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis (LRINEC) score, though not explicitly used in this case, could have supported early suspicion of CNF based on the patient’s leukocytosis, elevated C-reactive protein, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate.[21] However, the LRINEC score’s utility in the head and neck region is less established compared to extremity infections, highlighting the need for clinical vigilance and imaging in atypical presentations.[22]

The management of CNF and its complications requires a multimodal approach encompassing early surgical debridement, culture-directed antimicrobial therapy, and optimization of comorbidities.[2,17] In our case, initial surgical intervention involved neck exploration, abscess drainage, and tracheostomy for airway protection, consistent with guidelines emphasizing aggressive debridement as the cornerstone of CNF treatment.[3,23] The recurrence of SGA despite initial drainage and appropriate antibiotics underscores the limitations of drainage alone in managing encapsulated collections.[15] The formation of a fibrous capsule, as observed intraoperatively, prevented complete evacuation and impaired antibiotic penetration, necessitating complete capsule excision during the definitive surgery.[7,16] The use of negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) has emerged as a promising adjunct in managing complex wounds associated with CNF, as demonstrated in recent case reports.[24,25] NPWT enhances wound perfusion, removes exudate, and promotes granulation tissue formation, potentially reducing the risk of recurrence.[26] Although NPWT was not utilized in our case, its application could have been considered to support wound healing post-capsule excision, particularly given the patient’s comorbidities.[24] Future studies should explore the role of NPWT in recurrent SGA to optimize outcomes in similar cases. Antimicrobial therapy in CNF must initially target polymicrobial flora, including Gram-positive, Gram-negative, and anaerobic organisms, until culture results guide de-escalation.[11,17] Our patient’s transition from broad-spectrum antibiotics (meropenem and teicoplanin) to culture-directed therapy (piperacillin-tazobactam and tigecycline, later sulbactam-ampicillin and moxifloxacin) reflects best practices outlined by the Infectious Diseases Society of America.[17] However, the persistence of infection despite appropriate antibiotics highlights the critical role of surgical intervention in addressing encapsulated collections.[7] Compared to prior reports, our case is unique in documenting recurrent SGA as a complication of CNF.[4] Salloum et al. described an infected subgaleal hematoma in a pediatric patient that resolved with early drainage, contrasting with our patient’s recurrent course.[16] Timilsina et al. reported a de novo SGA complicated by osteomyelitis and epidural abscess, requiring prolonged antibiotics and surgical drainage, but without recurrence.[21] The recurrent nature of our patient’s SGA likely stemmed from the combination of CNF’s aggressive pathophysiology and the patient’s diabetic status, which impaired immune clearance and promoted capsule formation.[6,15] Recent literature emphasizes the high mortality rates of CNF (18–40%) and the importance of early intervention to prevent complications such as descending mediastinitis or septic shock.[2,8] Our patient’s survival and eventual resolution of infection highlight the efficacy of a staged approach involving repeated surgical interventions and culture-directed therapy.[17] However, the need for multiple procedures underscores the challenge of managing recurrent infections in the presence of comorbidities and encapsulated collections.[16] This case highlights several key clinical lessons. First, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for CNF in patients with atypical presentations, particularly those with diabetes or other comorbidities.[6] Second, imaging (CT and MRI) is indispensable for diagnosing complications such as SGA and guiding surgical planning.[19] Third, capsule excision should be prioritized in recurrent abscesses to eliminate the nidus of infection, as drainage alone is often insufficient.[7,16] Finally, the integration of advanced wound management techniques, such as NPWT, warrants further investigation in the context of head and neck infections.[24,26] Future research should focus on elucidating the mechanisms of capsule formation in SGA and identifying predictors of recurrence. Multicenter studies could provide insights into the optimal timing and techniques for capsule excision, as well as the role of adjunctive therapies such as NPWT or hyperbaric oxygen in preventing relapse.[25] In addition, the development of refined diagnostic tools, such as enhanced imaging protocols or biomarkers, could improve early detection of CNF and its complications, potentially reducing morbidity and mortality.[20,22] Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) has also been reported as a useful adjunct in necrotizing infections and refractory cranial osteomyelitis. By increasing tissue oxygen tension, HBOT enhances leukocyte function, promotes angiogenesis, and augments the bactericidal activity of certain antibiotics. It can suppress anaerobic bacterial growth and accelerate wound healing, thereby reducing the risk of recurrence in scalp and cranial infections. Although HBOT was not applied in our patient, its role should be considered in selected cases where conventional surgical and antimicrobial measures are insufficient.[10]

CONCLUSION

This case of recurrent SGA complicating CNF illustrates the challenges of managing complex head and neck infections in patients with significant comorbidities. The critical role of complete capsule excision, supported by culture-directed antibiotics and vigilant follow-up, cannot be overstated. By integrating insights from contemporary literature, this case underscores the need for a multimodal, aggressive approach to achieve durable infection control and highlights the importance of further research into optimizing management strategies for this rare but devastating condition.
