Variable range hopping in self-encapsulated carbon features by internal resin direct laser writing carbonization method
Authors: Zhufeng Jiang, Guo Li, Zhengmou Jiang, Xuchu Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The internal resin direct laser writing carbonization (IR-DLWc) method is a technique for in-situ fabrication of self-encapsulated carbon structures inside polyimide via laser. This study investigates the temperature-dependent sheet resistance of IR-DLWc samples of four process conditions and conventional DLWc samples of two process conditions using the three-dimensional variable range hopping (3D-VRH) conduction model. The results show that IR-DLWc samples exhibit significantly higher T0 values than conventional DLWc samples, indicating more localized electronic states. For the first time, the conductivity decay of both IR-DLWc and conventional DLWc samples was monitored over a 4-year period. The conventional DLWc samples exposed to air showed rapid initial conductivity decay, which slowed over time. In contrast, the IR-DLWc and UV-glue-encapsulated DLWc samples displayed a slower initial decay but continued to decay at a faster rate over time, suggesting complex interactions between air and laser-induced carbon materials. The study also suggests that T0, an important indicator for assessing hopping barriers and structural disorder affecting electron transport, can serve as a viable metric for characterizing disorder degree in IR-DLWc materials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23564-0.

Introduction

The direct laser writing carbonization (DLWc) technology is a process in which polymer substrates undergo in-situ thermal decomposition and carbonization under laser irradiation to form patterned carbon features1–3. It offers high precision, low cost, and flexible patterning, making it widely used in the fabrication of carbon-based devices. However, traditional DLWc methods have limitations regarding the ability to carbonize arrays only on the surface of polymers. The internal resin direct laser writing carbonization (IR-DLWc) method, developed in the author’s previous work, is a novel and efficient approach that enables the fabrication of carbon arrays/patterns sealed within the interior of a thin polyimide (PI) film through a one-step in-situ process without any additional packaging procedures4,5, which has shown flexible and versatile preparation of carbon-based functional devices and wearable electronics. However, so far, the charge transport behavior has lacked sufficient investigation, which is crucial solid-state physics information. Historically, the academic community has built on various studies on the electrical properties of carbon materials to develop deeper research and achieve significant results in numerous applications—such as carbon material transistors, transparent conductive electronics, field emission devices, energy storage devices, electrochemical, and electrode materials6. This paper focuses on exploring the electrical properties of IR-DLWc carbon lines, elucidating their charge transport mechanisms to lay the groundwork for the future application of IR-DLWc carbon structures in broader fields.

It is generally considered that PI undergoes a transformation from insulator to semiconductor to conductor during the thermal decomposition process. Our team’s previous research indicates that the IR-DLWc process involves a low-temperature pyrolysis carbonization under sealed conditions with significant pressure4,5. The products are not highly graphitized and may contain heteroatoms, vacancies, and disordered defects. Studies by Mott and others7–9 have shown that these heterogeneous and defective structures cause electron wave function localization, resulting in complex conductive behaviors such as phonon-assisted variable range hopping (VRH)9–19. Extensive research8,15–17,19–26 has shown that VRH is a common conduction mechanism in carbon materials with different microstructures, explaining the negative temperature coefficient of PI pyrolysis carbon product resistance and why the higher the pyrolysis temperature of PI, the lower the temperature dependence of its electrical properties.

Traditionally, the academic study of carbon materials has relied heavily on FTIR, Raman spectroscopy, and EDX analysis to identify microstructures. But these methods have their limitations. For example, the author’s team found that IR-DLWc samples prepared under the same laser scanning speed but different powers, despite having significant differences in room temperature conductivity and VRH behaviors, however, showing hardly any difference in their FTIR, Raman, and EDX analysis results. This is because these traditional characterization tools cannot fully represent the material’s electronic structure, especially the degree of electron localization. Charge transport behavior is closely related to the material’s detailed chemical/physical structure. By specifically studying the material’s charge transport behavior, one can extract material attribute information that infrared, Raman spectroscopy, and energy spectrum analysis may not or find difficult to detect, such as the material’s electron localization length or the density of states near the Fermi level. Therefore, to systematically study the processing-structure-performance rules of IR-DLWc materials and understand their charge transport behavior, it is necessary to conduct extensive variable-temperature electrical testing.

This paper uses samples prepared under the upper and lower boundaries of power conditions regarding the processing parameter window as the subject of study. By setting up a liquid nitrogen-level low-temperature experimental apparatus, it compares the variable-temperature electrical conductivity behavior of IR-DLWc and traditional DLWc carbon lines, confirming that 3D-VRH is the dominant conduction mechanism in IR-DLWc self-encapsulated carbon materials. By using a fixed beam movement speed to change the radiation power, the carbonization degree of carbon lines written by IR-DLWc and traditional DLWc is controlled to study the changes in VRH parameters of samples with different degrees of carbonization. The results suggest that the main changes in the electrical properties of IR-DLWc carbon products are likely due to an increase in carrier concentration, which is also reflected in the changes in the density of electronic states near the Fermi level in solids, but the degree of electron localization remains strong, so the localization lengthξis consistently small and does not change dramatically. Based on this, further combining the morphological characterization and analysis of the samples, the relationship between the VRH model’s Mott characteristic temperature T0 and pre-factor R0 with the laser processing conditions was established, demonstrating the potential use of T0 as an important indicator for measuring the disorder degree and hopping barriers in characterizing the disordered structures of IR-DLWc carbon materials. And at last, this study conducted a four-year continuous monitoring of the conductivity decay of IR-DLWc and traditional DLWc samples. To ensure the reliability of the experimental data, the sample used for VRH testing is the same as the ones used for conductance decay characterization.

Experimental section

Preparation and morphological characterization of IR-DLWc and DLWc samples

Figure 1a displays the schematic diagram of the DLWc process, and Fig. 1b for IR-DLWc process. During IR-DLWc, a 125 μm thick polymer precursor, Kapton® PI film, is sandwiched between a ZnSe optical window and a glass support substrate. The laser writing operation is conducted using a SCE4030 laser cutter equipped with a Synard® CO2 laser with a wavelength of 10.6 μm. The CO2 laser beam reaches the PI layer through the optical path inside the ZnSe medium, initiating a photothermal effect. The temperature field is constrained by the thermal dissipation of the ZnSe, forming a photothermal source confined within the PI film and inducing the decomposition and carbonization of PI. Notably, to prevent the ZnSe window from dislodging, it is secured using a clamp with a force of about 10 N, ensuring the ZnSe is pressed tightly above the PI. Before experiments, all components are meticulously cleaned with isopropanol and ethanol. The dimensions of the PI film used for laser carbonization are 35 mm in length, 25 mm in width, and 125 μm in thickness. The ZnSe optical window has a diameter of 20 mm and a thickness of 2 mm, with an anti-reflective coating on the side facing the laser source to reduce laser loss. During the IR-DLWc line drawing process, the laser power varies from 3.5 to 4.1 W, and the laser beam moves at a constant speed of 30 mm/s. For comparative experiments, traditional DLWc samples are prepared with laser powers of 1.2–2.5 W and a fixed scanning speed of 15 mm/s. Optical characterization of the samples is performed using a Mshot® MP41 polarizing microscope.

Temperature-dependent electrical performance experiments of IR-DLWc and DLWc samples

The samples are connected via copper wires, and silver paste is applied for the measurement of their electrical properties using a two-wire method. For traditional DLWc samples, electrodes are directly attached to the exposed carbon areas, whereas the electrode preparation for IR-DLWc is slightly more complex. Initially, the PI covering at the predetermined electrode positions is ablated using traditional DLWc processes to expose the IR-DLWc embedded material beneath for electrode fabrication. As shown in Fig. 1c, the variable temperature electrical properties of the IR-DLWc and traditional DLWc carbon lines are measured using a custom-built liquid nitrogen cooling and resistance measurement system. This cooling device consists of a highly conductive copper tube suspended within an insulating setup, where the sample and a thermocouple are fixed at the same position on the inner wall of the copper tube. The sample temperature is controlled by adjusting the distance between the bottom of the copper tube and the liquid nitrogen level. At the start of the electrical property measurements, the bottom of the copper tube just touches the liquid nitrogen surface, i.e., the distance is zero. As time progresses, the slow evaporation of liquid nitrogen increases this distance, thereby causing a corresponding rise in the sample temperature. During this process, the resistance of the sample and the temperature of the thermocouple are recorded in real-time by a Keithley 3706 A (digital multimeter, DMM), with a sampling rate of 12 readings per minute. Initially, the device is calibrated using the thermocouple to ensure accurate temperature measurements at the sample’s position inside the copper tube. Without liquid nitrogen, the sample is at room temperature, and the thermocouple reads room temperature; when filled with liquid nitrogen and the copper tube’s bottom just touches the liquid nitrogen surface, the thermocouple shows a temperature of 78 K, consistent with the boiling point of liquid nitrogen at 77 K. The excellent thermal conductivity of the copper ensures a uniform temperature environment for the sample. Therefore, the electrical property data obtained from liquid nitrogen to room temperature in this experiment are reliable. The calibration results indicate that the temperature difference between the sample and its surroundings has a negligible impact on the variable temperature resistance measurements. Additionally, due to the effective insulation of the cooling device, the evaporation rate of liquid nitrogen is low (over 12 h), resulting in a slow change in the distance between the copper tube’s bottom and the liquid nitrogen surface. This slow change allows the sample to be considered in thermal equilibrium at each measurement temperature, helping to minimize measurement errors.

Conductivity decay monitoring of IR-DLWc and DLWc samples

To evaluate whether the intrinsic in-situ encapsulation of IR‑DLWc yields improved electrical stability, a conductivity decay monitoring experiment was conducted on IR‑DLWc and DLWc samples. The IR‑DLWc sample was fabricated at a laser power of 3.9 W and scan speed of 30 mm/s, corresponding to the processing condition indicated by the second blue arrow from the right in the process‑window shown in Fig. 1c. The conventional DLWc sample was fabricated at 1.2 W and 10 mm/s, corresponding to the leftmost arrow in Fig. 1c. Notably, to examine whether UV-glue sealing could improve the electrical stability of carbon, two conventional DLWc replicate samples were prepared: one left fully exposed to air (as usual), and another sealed with UV-glue immediately after fabrication and wiring for isolating it from air. For all three samples, a stopwatch timeline was initiated immediately after fabrication, and the samples were promptly connected to a digital multimeter (DMM) for overnight conductivity decay monitoring at approximately 6 s intervals to track changes in electrical performance over time. Subsequently, single‑point resistance was recorded once daily for the first three days, then every few days over the following month, and thereafter at intervals of several weeks to several months.

Variable range hopping conduction mechanism of IR-DLWc and DLWc samples

Carbon materials fabricated through various techniques, due to their multiscale defect structures and disordered arrangement of carbon atoms, often have their electron transport mechanisms dominated by the variable range hopping (VRH) conduction mechanism. In the VRH conduction process, when the electron localization is not very strong, the energy imparted by lattice vibrations may cause electrons to hop from one localized state to another. Crucially, these hops follow the principle of minimal energy rather than minimal distance19. Moreover, at lower temperatures, electrons are more likely to jump to further positions in search of the lowest energy state20–26. This process displays characteristic temperature dependencies, which typically manifest as a straight line on a logarithmic scale8.

Schematic diagram of the charge transport mechanism of VRH27.

Figure 3 illustrates the schematic diagram of carrier transport via Variable Range Hopping (VRH) in disordered conductive materials. In these materials, disorder can lead to Anderson localization27, a phenomenon where the wave functions of electrons become localized. It is well known that when the degree of localization is not very strong, electrons localized below the Fermi level can gain sufficient energy through phonon coupling to jump across the soft band gap between different localized states, thus enabling carrier transport7–9. This type of conduction mechanism, influenced by various physical factors, follows the principle of minimal energy for carrier transition paths28, rather than necessarily jumping to the nearest location. This is known as the variable range hopping29–35. The VRH model, based on this mechanism, is used to describe the behavior of carrier transport via phonon-assisted hops between different localized states within the band gap of amorphous semiconductor materials. According to Mott’s derivation for variable range hopping model (VRH)30, the temperature dependence of the electrical conductivity can be expressed as:

(5b) to perform a linear fit on the temperature-dependent resistance data of IR-DLWc and traditional DLWc samples presented in Fig. 4, the slope and intercept values can be used to calculate the corresponding Mott characteristic temperature T0 and pre-factor R0. Figure 5b,c display the relationships of T0 and R0 with normalized laser power conditions for IR-DLWc and traditional DLWc samples within a reasonable laser processing power window. As shown in Fig. 5b, the IR-DLWc samples produced near the lower boundary of the processing window exhibit the highest T0 values, approximately 3.9 × 105 K. With increasing laser power, T0 initially decreases rapidly and then stabilizes as it approaches the upper boundary of the processing window. The carbon lines produced at the upper boundary have the lowest T0 values, approximately 2.4 × 104 K. Regarding DLWc, in contrast, even for the samples prepared with the highest and lowest permissible laser energies, T0 values only increases from 4 × 103 to 6 × 103 K, which are substantially lower than those of IR-DLWc carbon lines. The comparison of T0 values indicates that the carbon materials produced by IR-DLWc have a lower degree of carbonization and a lower order of carbon structure, resulting in a higher degree of electron localization. This leads to much higher carrier hopping barriers compared to those in traditional DLWc carbon products, thus resulting in higher T0 values. According to formula (1c), T0 is inversely proportional to ξ 3and has a cubic dependence, making it highly sensitive to the values of ξ.

The author’s previous studies reveal that for all conductive IR-DLWc samples, under various scanning speeds and regardless of increases in laser power—even to the extent that it damages the PI cover and ZnSe—no significant changes were observed in the Raman signals of the IR-DLWc carbon materials. It is reasonable to speculate that in the IR-DLWc process, even though the carbon product’s Raman signals do not change noticeably when processing conditions approach the upper limit of the window, the PI has already formed condensed-ring conjugated structures due to molecular chain breaks and recombination at the initial stages of thermal degradation. This results in an increase in the localization scale of the electron wave function, reflected in the decreasing behavior of T0 with increasing laser power. Furthermore, as the laser processing power approaches the upper boundary of the window, the PI’s upper skin layer becomes thinner, and the heat transfer efficiency dominated by ZnSe increases, thus limiting the temperature rise in the carbonized regions of PI. This causes the trend of carbonization to slow down after PI reaches a certain degree of degradation or carbonization, leading to T0 initially dropping sharply and then gradually stabilizing. Compared to T0, the pre-factor R0 of the IR-DLWc and traditional DLWc carbon lines shows no significant differences and decreases slowly with increasing laser power, as shown in Fig. 5c. This is primarily because T0 is inversely proportional to N(EF)ξ3, while R0 is inversely proportional to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sqrt[2]{{N(E_{F} )\xi }}$$\end{document}, making R0 less sensitive to changes in ξ than T0. Additionally, the similar R0 values between IR-DLWc and DLWc carbon lines also indicate that there are no significant differences in the N(EF) values between DLWc and IR-DLWc carbon products, and these do not change noticeably with laser power.

It is worth noting that although all four IR-DLWc samples and the DLWc sample prepared with low laser power (1.2 W, 15 mm/s) exhibit different T0 values, their Raman signatures are highly similar and difficult to distinguish, as shown in Fig. 6a. All IR-DLWc samples display Raman spectra consisting of two broad, overlapping features—the G band at 1595 cm−1 and the D band at 1350 cm−1—indicating that, under these conditions, the IR-DLWc products are composed predominantly of disordered, weakly graphitized sp2 carbon. This spectral signature resembles that of products obtained by direct pyrolysis and carbonization of PI at relatively low temperatures5 and is consistent with previous reports on IR-DLWc dots4.

By contrast, the two conventional DLWc samples used for comparison show markedly different Raman responses. The spectrum of the low-power sample (1.2 W, 15 mm/s) is very similar to that of the IR-DLWc samples and difficult to distinguish from them, whereas the spectrum of the high-power sample (2.5 W, 15 mm/s) displays the characteristic features of defect-rich graphene. These results demonstrate the feasibility of using T0—an important indicator of the degree of disorder in electron transport and the height of hopping barriers—to characterize structural disorder in IR-DLWc carbon materials, and even suggest its potential to detect differences in material properties that are not readily discernible by Raman spectroscopy5.

Further analysis of the localization length ξ is conducted. From Eqs. (1c) and (3b), it is known that the three important parameters in the VRH model—density of states at the Fermi level N(EF), localization length ξ, and the phonon frequency νph associated with the hopping process—can be calculated by specifying one parameter and using the experimentally measured values of T0 and σ0 to derive the other two. Considering the significant variation in the sp2/sp3 hybridization ratio of carbon atoms in carbon materials prepared by different processing methods and conditions, the difference in Fermi levels is also substantial39, which means that the density of states (DOS) near the Fermi level is not a fixed value, and thus it is not suitable to use it as a basis for calculations. In solid-state physics, the localization length ξ represents the decay distance of the localized electron wave function; thus, the smaller the value of ξ, the stronger the electron localization. For computational convenience, the localization length is often fixed in studies of carbon materials’ electronic structure and electrical performance. For example, in the works of Murakami40 and Wan38, the typical localization length of amorphous carbon reported in literature is taken as ξ = 12Å to calculate the other parameters in their VRH model of carbon materials. However, some studies argue that for amorphous carbons with different chemical compositions, especially samples with significant changes in electrical properties, the size of sp2 carbon atom clusters and the corresponding variation in localization length ξ may differ by more than an order of magnitude41, so ξ should not be treated as a fixed value. For the VRH conduction mechanism, the energy required for electron transitions is related to the coupling phonon frequency. According to the Einstein model of temperature-dependent specific heat, the phonon frequency of a solid material determines its apparent heat capacity. Considering that the specific heat capacities of PI and pure graphite differ by only 30%, it is reasonable to treat the phonon frequency in the 3D-VRH model as a fixed value for simplicity in calculations.

During the calculations, the conversion between σ0 and R0 in formula Eq. (3b) was achieved through image processing software, which measured the cross-sectional area and porosity of the carbon lines from IR-DLWc and DLWc (illustrated in Fig. 7). The conductivity was then calculated using the sheet resistance values, with the numerical values for cross-sectional area and porosity also listed in Table 1. However, it is worth noting that the samples prepared by the methods described in this paper exhibit non-uniformity. Additionally, the coupling level between phonons and electron hopping processes, as well as values of ξ and N(EF), are subject to changes with varying temperatures, which means the calculation results should be taken as indicative rather than definitive.

Although the results are questionable due to sample preparation errors and computational simplifications, it is evident from Table 1 that the localization length ξ of the carbon materials produced by IR-DLWc is somewhat dependent on the processing parameters. As the laser power increases, the ξ value of the carbon lines grows from about 0.8 nm near the lower boundary of the reasonable laser power window to approximately 2.6 nm near the upper boundary. As mentioned earlier, an increase in ξ reflects the growth in the size of the condensed-ring conjugated structures produced by the thermal decomposition of PI with increasing laser power. Unlike IR-DLWc, the ξ values for DLWc carbon lines do not show an increasing trend with higher laser power. This phenomenon is not significant and may be due to cumulative errors in experiments and calculations; if there is a causal relationship, it might be because under high-power processing conditions for DLWc, the intense reactions in the laser-irradiated area produce a large amount of pyrolysis gases in a very short time, leaving numerous defects from the removal of heteroatoms and leading to Anderson localization, which results in shorter ξ values. Literature shows that42 conventional heating methods for carbonizing/graphitizing PI can change the carrier concentration and mobility of the carbon products. In the early stages of pyrolysis, as the degree of carbonization increases, the concentration of localized carriers gradually increases; in the later stages, carrier mobility significantly improves. This is likely because the time scale of conventional thermal treatment is relatively long, and the pyrolysis process is milder, resulting in far fewer defects, impurities, and cracks in the carbon/graphite products compared to those produced by IR-DLWc/DLWc. For instance, Wan et al.38 reported that the T0 of polyimide pyrolysis products treated at 800 °C in a nitrogen atmosphere was 6.4 × 105 K, similar to the T0 for samples near the lower boundary of the IR-DLWc window, yet their conductivity was much higher than that of carbon materials produced by the IR-DLWc process. These results suggest that the presence of defects, impurities, and various heterogenous structures in IR-DLWc samples reduces their conductivity. Unlike localization length ξ, the density of states (DOS) at the Fermi level, N(EF), does not show a clear dependence on the laser processing conditions. This is mainly because regardless of the DLWc or IR-DLWc process conditions, the final products are composed of different sizes of sp2-bonded carbon atoms forming conjugated structures, and their basic structural units (or Brillouin Zones) do not undergo significant changes, so the variations in N༈EF༉are not very pronounced.

The results indicate that for both conventional DLWc and IR-DLWc samples, their conductive behavior fundamentally stems from various intrinsic conjugated structures undergoing the Variable Range Hopping (VRH) effect under certain conditions. To examine the temporal stability of these conductive structures, this study conducted a long-term monitoring of conductivity decay over a period of four years for representative IR-DLWc conductivity samples (prepared under conditions of V = 30 mm/s, P = 3.9 W) and conventional DLWc conductivity samples (prepared under conditions of V = 15 mm/s, P = 1.2 W, either exposed to air or immediately encapsulated with UV-curing adhesive post-preparation). The results are quite intriguing. Before 2000 s, a rapid decline in the conductivity of the exposed DLWc sample (colored blue) can be observed, with its decay rate being the fastest among the three samples, highlighting the destructive effect of the atmospheric environment on conductivity. However, possibly due to the oxidation reaction approaching saturation afterward, the rate of decay then slows down, exhibiting a “fast-then-slow” pattern in conductivity decay. In contrast, the IR-DLWc sample (colored red) and the conventional DLWc sample sealed with UV glue (colored black) show a slower rate of conductivity decay before 2000 s compared to the unsealed conventional DLWc sample. But after that period, their decay continues without the slowdown observed in the exposed DLWc sample, eventually resulting in their decay surpassing that of the sample exposed to air.

The author believes that these results may suggest that the reaction between the air and the laser-carbonized PI samples is very complex and dual-natured. Firstly, it may oxidize and destroy some of the conjugated groups that facilitate electrical conductivity, causing a rapid decline in conductivity under atmospheric conditions in a short time frame. However, on the other hand, it may promote the transition of other types of metastable conductive structures into more stable states, thereby delaying the decay of conductivity when observed over a very long time scale. Samples of conventional DLWc and IR-DLWc sealed with UV glue might contain metastable condensed-ring structures produced during laser treatment of PI. Due to the short duration of the laser-induced thermal degradation, these structures are inherently metastable. Even without the effect of air oxidation, they may gradually transition to thermodynamically more stable states with poor conductivity over a longer time scale. These are only preliminary speculations, and the specific details depend on further detailed analysis by subsequent researchers.

Conclusions

This study adjusts the carbonization level of IR-DLWc and traditional DLWc written samples by changing the radiation power with a fixed moving speed. Using variable temperature electrical performance testing, we analyzed the charge transfer behavior and mechanisms in carbon materials processed by IR-DLWc and traditional DLWc. The results indicate that within a reasonable laser power processing window, the charge transfer behavior of carbon lines prepared by both IR-DLWc and DLWc conforms to the 3D-VRH model. The electron transmission mechanism is limited by variable range hopping and electron localization. Compared to DLWc carbon materials, the IR-DLWc prepared carbon materials show a significantly higher Mott characteristic temperature T0, representing the barrier for carrier hopping, while the pre-factor R0 did not show significant differences. Since the thermal degradation of IR-DLWc is still at a very early stage, it is reasonable to speculate that the main changes in the electrical properties of the IR-DLWc carbon products are likely due to increased carrier concentration. This is also reflected in the change in the density of electronic states near the Fermi level in solids, although the degree of electron localization remains strong, thus the localization length ξ is consistently small and does not vary dramatically. By integrating the existing research on the electrical properties of carbon materials and the variable temperature electrical performance results of this study, it is evident that the defect structures present in IR-DLWc carbon materials, such as micro and nano-cracks, significantly affect their electrical properties. Finally, the electrical performance stability of the samples was characterized, and preliminary discussions on the results were conducted. The findings and methods of this paper provide valuable insights into the electronic structure of carbon materials repared by IR-DLWc, furthering the development and optimization of IR-DLWc technology and the future development of multifunctional devices based on IR-DLWc.
