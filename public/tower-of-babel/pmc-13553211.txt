Orbital Engineering of π–d Conjugation in One‐Dimensional Metal–Organic Materials for Lithium‐Ion Batteries: A DFT Study
Authors: Mingli Li, Di Zhao, Lei Zhang, Pan Yang, Feixue Han, Zhenzhen Wu, Shanqing Zhang, Yun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding and tuning π–d conjugation is essential for advancing metal–organic materials in metal‐ion batteries. Here, we applied density functional theory (DFT) to systematically investigate π–d conjugation in metal–organic systems. Using a Ni‐based metal–organic polymer as the model system, our results show that strong π–d conjugation originates from effective hybridization between Ni 3d orbitals and ligand π orbitals mediated by bridging groups. Among the three bridging groups (─O, ─S, and ─NH), S provides the strongest coupling due to favorable orbital overlap. Ligand functionalization further modulates this interaction via energy level alignment, where electron‐withdrawing groups enhance π–d hybridization and charge delocalization, while electron‐donating groups weaken it. These factors collectively influence Li adsorption and electrochemical performance. This study establishes a unified design strategy combining bridge atom selection and functional group tuning to optimize π–d conjugation for high‐ performance metal–organic electrode materials.

Introduction

Lithium‐ion batteries (LIBs) remain the dominant power source for portable electronics, electric vehicles, and grid‐scale energy storage, where the performance ceiling is increasingly set by electrode materials [1, 2, 3]. Beyond conventional inorganic compounds, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) and metal–organic polymers (MOPs) have attracted growing attention as a structurally tunable and electronically active class of electrode candidates owing to their modular architectures, well‐defined coordination environments, and abundant redox‐active sites that can be precisely engineered through molecular design [4, 5, 6]. However, most traditional MOFs and MOPs are constrained by localized electronic states and poor intrinsic conductivity, which severely limit their rate capability and restrict their practical application in rechargeable batteries [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12].

To address these limitations, recent efforts have shifted toward conductive low‐dimensional MOFs and MOPs, particularly two‐dimensional and one‐dimensional systems built from conjugated organic ligands and transition‐metal nodes [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Compared with their three‐dimensional counterparts, low‐dimensional MOFs and MOPs feature well‐defined metal–ligand motifs and continuous, structurally simple charge‐transport pathways, which promote electronic delocalization across the framework and enable intrinsic electrical conductivity rarely achieved in conventional MOFs and MOPs [18, 19, 20]. More importantly, at the atomic scale, low‐dimensional MOFs are constructed through well‐defined coordination interactions between metal nodes, bridging groups, and conjugated organic ligands, forming ordered frameworks that facilitate tunable electronic coupling and structural precision [21, 22]. Their clean and modular architectures allow the role of individual chemical modifications, either at the metal node or on the organic ligand, to be unambiguously identified [23, 24]. This clarity makes it possible to establish structure–property relationships with molecular precision [25]. The electronic properties of these materials are largely governed by π–d conjugation, which is formed through the hybridization of the d orbitals of the transition‐metal center with the p z orbitals of the conjugated organic ligands and bridging groups [26, 29]. This π–d conjugation allows electron delocalization across the framework, endowing the system with intrinsic electrical conductivity and reshaping the frontier orbital energies that govern ion adsorption, charge transfer, and redox behavior [30, 31, 32, 33]. Understanding and tuning π–d conjugation is therefore essential for moving the design of low‐dimensional metal–organic electrodes from trial‐and‐error screening toward predictable, descriptor‐based engineering.

Despite the recognized role of π–d conjugation in low‐dimensional MOFs and MOPs, the way in which chemical modifications tune this interaction in a predictable manner remains poorly understood. Three simple chemical strategies can modify π–d conjugation without changing the overall framework, including varying the metal node, optimizing the bridging group [34], and functionalizing the conjugated organic ligand [16, 35]. Among these approaches, variation of the metal node has been extensively explored [22, 25], whereas strategies involving bridging groups and ligand functionalization have received comparatively limited attention. The bridging group changes the energy and spatial overlap between the d orbitals of the metal node and p orbitals of the ligand [36, 37], while functionalization of the ligand shifts the π‐electron density and the frontier orbital energies through inductive and resonance effects [26]. However, there remains a lack of systematic understanding that directly correlates these diverse chemical modifications with lithium storage behavior within a single low‐dimensional platform, underscoring the need for the present study.

In this work, we employ density functional theory (DFT) to systematically investigate the nature and tunability of π–d conjugation in low‐dimensional MOFs/MOPs. Building upon our previous study of the 1D Ni‐based MOP system [25], we examine the influence of heteroatom bridge species and ligand functionalization on the electronic structure, orbital hybridization, and charge‐transport characteristics. Ni was selected as it demonstrated the highest performance among the metals investigated [25]. Through this approach, we aim to elucidate the fundamental mechanisms governing π–d conjugation and establish a theoretical framework for the rational design of advanced metal–organic electrode materials, demonstrating how the selection of bridging groups and ligand functionalization can be used to modulate electrochemical performance.

Computational Details

All of the spin‐polarized DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) using the projector‐augmented wave (PAW) method [38, 39, 40, 41]. Because of the strong on‐site Coulomb repulsion between the d electrons of Ni atoms, the DFT + U method with U = 4.0 eV was used to describe the electronic properties of all Ni‐MOPs [25, 42, 43]. For the electron–electron exchange and correlation interactions, the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional, a form of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA), was used throughout [44]. The plane‐wave kinetic energy cutoff was set to 520 eV, and the Brillouin zone was sampled using a gamma‐centered (5 × 1 × 1) Monkhorst–Pack k‐point grid for both geometry optimization and electronic structure calculations [44]. There are vacuum layers of 15 Å in the y and z directions for the MOF structures. The convergence criteria for total energy and ionic forces were set to 10−5 eV and 0.02 eV Å−1. Besides, the DFT‐D3 method was considered in all calculations to account for the van der Waals interaction [45]. Postprocessing of the VASP output was carried out using the VASPKIT code [46]. All images were made by visualization for electronic and structural analysis (VESTA) [47].

The average adsorption energy of the Li atoms on the Ni‐MOP monolayer is defined as follows [48]:

(1) Eads=ELixNi‐MOP−ENi‐MOP−xELix where ELixNi‐MOP, ENi‐MOP, and ELi represent the total energies of the Ni‐MOP monolayer adsorbed by xLi atoms, the Ni‐MOP monolayer, and the single Li atom in its corresponding bulk structure, respectively.

The open‐circuit voltage (OCV) of the process can be estimated by dint of the equation given below [49]:

(2) OCV=ELix2Ni‐MOP−ELix1Ni‐MOP−(x2−x1)ELi(x2−x1)e where ELix1Ni‐MOP and ELix2Ni‐MOP are the total energies of the system with x 1 and x 2 adsorbed Li atoms, respectively. Here, x represents the number of Li atoms, and e is the elementary charge.

The formation energy of Ni‐MOP (ΔE F) can be calculated based on the following equation [50]:

(3) ΔEF=ENi‐MOP−∑nMEM∑nM where E Ni‐MOP is the energy of the Ni‐MOP, E M is the energy of the isolated atom of each element M, and n M is the number of atoms of each element M, respectively.

Results and Discussion

Evidence and Origin of π–d Conjugation in Ni‐MOP

To confirm the existence of π–d conjugation in this one‐dimensional Ni‐MOP system and to investigate its fundamental properties, an analysis of its electrical properties was conducted. As shown in Figure 1a, the schematic of the coordination environment and the optimized geometry of the Ni‐MOP repeating unit are presented. The Ni center is coordinated by four sulfur atoms that bridge conjugated organic ligands composed of benzene rings. This forms a one‐dimensional conjugated backbone in which the metal node and ligand are directly connected through S atoms. The planar geometry and short metal–ligand linkage favor orbital overlap between the transition metal and the π‐conjugated ligand. The structural arrangement clearly suggests a potential pathway for electronic communication along the framework, where the S bridge serves as an intermediate coupling center between the Ni atom and the carbon‐based π system.

(a) Chemical structure and side view of Ni‐MOP. Brown: C, yellow: S, green: Ni, and white: H. (b) PDOS of Ni‐MOP. The Fermi level is set to 0. (c) Illustration of π–d conjugation in Ni‐MOP. (d) ELF of Ni‐MOP.

The electronic origin of this interaction is revealed by the projected density of states (PDOS), which gives an orbital‐resolved picture of how electron‐donating and electron‐withdrawing substituents influence the hybridization between the Ni 3d orbitals and the p z orbitals of the ligand. The PDOS patterns shown in Figure 1b reveal a significant overlap between the Ni 3d orbitals and the p orbitals of the bridging S and the conjugated C atoms in the energy window near the Fermi level. Specifically, the d xz and d yz orbitals of the Ni node exhibit pronounced hybridization with the p z orbitals of S atoms below the Fermi level, while contributions from C p z states extend across a wider energy range. This continuous distribution of states across the metal and ligand components indicates a strong electronic coupling rather than isolated atomic contributions. The presence of a hybridized orbital character near the Fermi level confirms that electrons can delocalize over both the Ni center and the conjugated ligand, which is a defining feature of π–d conjugation. Figure 1c schematically illustrates the orbital interactions responsible for the conjugation. The out‐of‐plane p z orbitals of the conjugated carbon atoms and sulfur bridge atoms interact with the partially filled Ni d xz and d yz orbitals. This symmetry‐compatible overlap enables effective π–d coupling across the metal–ligand interface. Importantly, the S atoms act as electronic mediators, extending the conjugation pathway while also influencing the strength of orbital hybridization through their electronegativity and orbital size.

The real‐space manifestation of this delocalization is visualized in the electron localization function (ELF), as shown in Figure 1d. The ELF contour plot provides a direct visualization of how different substituents modulate the spatial distribution and delocalization of electronic states involved in π–d conjugation [51]. The ELF is not confined to either the metal center or the ligand but instead extends continuously across the Ni–S–C framework. High ELF regions are observed along the covalent bonding direction between Ni and S atoms as well as across the conjugated ligand, confirming the presence of an interconnected electronic network. This spatial continuity of ELF provides another direct piece of evidence of charge delocalization enabled by π–d conjugation.

Impact of Bridging Groups on π–d Conjugation

To elucidate the role of bridging atoms in affecting π–d conjugation, the adsorption of Li atoms on the Ni‐based MOP was systematically investigated with three different groups (X = ─NH, ─O, and ─S) (Figures 2a and S1–S3). All three Ni‐MOPs have negative formation energies (see Figure S4), indicating that their formation is thermodynamically favorable. Moreover, these Ni‐MOPs have also been experimentally synthesized in previous studies, further supporting the experimental feasibility of the investigated structures [52, 53, 54]. The adsorption site was identified in our previous study [25]. Figure 2b shows that the average adsorption energy (E ads) becomes progressively more negative from Ni─4NH (−0.13 eV) to Ni─4O (−0.53 eV) and further to Ni─4S (−1.43 eV). It demonstrates that the bridging coordination atom has a strong effect on the ability of Li adsorption. The much weaker adsorption in the ─NH system suggests limited electronic interaction with Li, whereas the strong adsorption in the S‐bridged system points to enhanced charge‐transfer capability. Since Li adsorption is closely coupled to the electronic structure near the Fermi level, this result implies that π–d conjugation is substantially strengthened when moving from ─NH to ─S.

(a) Chemical structure of Ni─4X (X = ─NH, ─O, ─S) with the adsorbed Li atom. (b) Lithium adsorption energies on Ni─4X MOPs. (c) PDOS for Ni─4X. (d) HOMO–LUMO characteristics for Ni─4X MOPs with the iso‐surface value of 1.5 × 10−9.

To gain deeper insight into the electronic origin of this trend, the PDOS results are analyzed (Figure 2c). In the Ni─4NH system, the Ni 3d orbitals exhibit strong localization, with limited overlap with the ligand p orbitals. In particular, there is almost no significant hybridization near the Fermi level. Although orbital coupling is somewhat enhanced in the Ni─4O system, the interaction with the Ni 3d orbitals remains relatively limited due to the lower energy levels and strong localization of the O 2p orbitals. The PDOS suggests that ─NH and ─O bridges lead to weaker hybridization and more localized electronic states. In contrast, the Ni─4S system exhibits extensive hybridization between the Ni 3d and S 3p states over a broad energy window around the Fermi level. The S p z orbitals exhibit strong coupling with Ni d xz /d yz states, forming a continuous density distribution that bridges the metal center and the ligand π system. This enhanced orbital overlap is attributed to the larger spatial extent and more suitable energy level of S 3p orbitals, which provide an optimal balance of electronegativity and orbital diffuseness to facilitate effective π–d interaction. The projected crystal orbital Hamilton population (pCOHP) analysis also reveals that the S bridging atom has the strongest bonding strength with Ni, as evidenced by the most negative ICOHP value of −2.32 eV, compared with those of Ni─4O and Ni─4NH MOPs (Figures S5–S7). As a result, the Ni─4S system exhibits the most pronounced electronic delocalization among the three systems.

This trend is further supported by the analysis of frontier energy levels. The HOMO‐LUMO gap decreases from 1.84 eV in both Ni─4NH and Ni─4O to 1.05 eV in Ni‐4S (Figure 2d). Meanwhile, the HOMO level rises from −5.82 eV in Ni─4O to −4.96 eV in the Ni─4S system, and the LUMO drops from −1.97 to −3.91 eV. The narrowing of the gap indicates stronger metal–ligand orbital mixing and a higher degree of delocalization of the boundary states. Moreover, the lower LUMO in Ni─4S facilitates electron acceptance from the adsorbed Li atom. Therefore, the Ni─4S MOP can show a stronger Li adsorption performance. These results demonstrate that the bridging group exerts a profound influence on π–d conjugation by modulating orbital energy alignment, spatial overlap, and electronic delocalization.

Tuning π–d Conjugation via Ligand Functionalization

As illustrated in Figures 3a and S8–S11, a series of functional groups with varying electron‐donating and electron‐withdrawing capabilities (R = ─NH2, ─CH3, ─H, ─Cl, ─NO2) are introduced onto the conjugated ligand framework of the S‐bridged Ni‐MOP system. This approach allows systematic tuning of the ligand π‐electron density and its energy alignment with Ni d‐orbitals without altering the primary coordination geometry. The impact of functional groups is reflected in Li adsorption behavior. As shown in Figure 3b, a clear trend is observed in which the adsorption energy becomes progressively more negative when transitioning from electron‐donating to electron‐withdrawing substituents. Specifically, E ads decreases from −1.09 eV (4S─NH2) and −1.32 eV (4S─CH3) to −1.42 eV (4S─H),−1.43 eV (4S─Cl) and reaches −1.87 eV for the strongly electron‐withdrawing ─NO2 group. This trend indicates that electron‐withdrawing groups significantly enhance the Li‐binding strength, which can be attributed to their ability to lower the electron density of the ligand and promote charge transfer from Li to the framework. From the perspective of π–d conjugation, this suggests that functional groups can effectively regulate the electronic environment of the conjugated system and thus influence its interaction with charge carriers. Furthermore, all functionalized structures exhibit negative formation energies, indicating that their formation is thermodynamically allowed (Figure S12). The low formation energies indicate that all functionalized MOPs can be experimentally synthesized with good stability.

(a) Chemical structure of Ni─4S MOPs with different functional groups (R = ─NH2, ─CH3, ─H, ─Cl, ─NO2). (b) Lithium adsorption energies on the different functionalized Ni─4S MOPs. (c) Simulated open‐circuit voltage of different functionalized Ni─4S MOPs. (d) Average voltage of different functionalized Ni─4S MOPs.

To further evaluate the electrochemical implications, the OCV profiles as a function of Li coverage are presented. As depicted in Figure 3c, systems functionalized with electron‐withdrawing groups exhibit consistently higher voltage plateaus compared to those with electron‐donating groups. Notably, the 4S─NO2 system delivers the highest voltage, reaching approximately 1.25 V at low coverage, while electron‐donating groups such as ─NH2 result in lower voltages (~0.9–1.0 V). This behavior reflects the stabilization of the lithiated states in the presence of electron‐withdrawing substituents, which effectively shift the redox potentials to higher values. In contrast, electron‐donating groups increase the electron density of the framework, thereby weakening Li binding and lowering the operating voltage. This trend is more clearly summarized in Figure 3d, which compares the average voltages of all systems. A monotonic decrease in average voltage is observed from 4S─NO2 (1.25 V) to 4S─NH2 (0.91 V), following the order of decreasing electron‐withdrawing strength. This result highlights a direct correlation between the electronic nature of functional groups and the electrochemical performance of the material.

The comparison of frontier orbital energies for the five functionalized frameworks is summarized in Figure 4. As the substituent changes from electron‐donating to electron‐withdrawing, both the HOMO and the LUMO are pushed to lower energies. The LUMO drops monotonically from −3.50 eV in 4S─NH2 to −3.60 eV in 4S─CH3, −3.91 eV in 4S─H, −3.96 eV in 4S─Cl, and −4.49 eV in 4S─NO2, while the HOMO shifts from −3.87 to −5.72 eV over the same series. The lowered LUMO of the electron‐withdrawing systems strengthens the ability of the framework to accept electrons from the adsorbed Li atom, providing a direct electronic explanation for the systematically enhanced Li binding and higher operating voltages [55, 56].

Electronic Origin of Functional Group Effects

To elucidate the microscopic origin of the functional group effects discussed above, the real‐space distribution of the electronic states across the substituted frameworks is visualized by the ELF maps. As shown in Figure 5, the ELF is mainly distributed along the Ni–S–C backbone for all systems, confirming that the conjugated pathway established in Figure 1d remains intact upon functionalization. However, significant differences around the aromatic rings and the functional groups emerge in the degree of delocalization and intensity of electron density, depending on the nature of the functional group.

ELF contour plots of (a) 4S─NH2, (b) 4S─CH3, (c) and (d) 4S─H, (e) 4S─Cl, and (f) 4S─NO2 MOPs.

For the electron‐donating 4S─NH2 and 4S─CH3 systems, stronger ELF features are found on the benzene rings, reflecting the extra electron density pushed into the ligand π system by ─NH2 and ─CH3 (Figure 5a,b). Moreover, the 4S─H system shows a similar pattern along the backbone but with more uniform ELF on the benzene rings, serving as a clean reference (Figure 5c,d). For the mildly electron‐withdrawing 4S─Cl system shown in Figure ‍5e, the ELF along the backbone remains close to that of 4S─H but spreads slightly toward the ring edges, consistent with the weak electron withdrawal of Cl. In the strongly electron‐withdrawing 4S─NO2 system, the ELF spreads more widely over the framework, and clear features appear around the nitro group, linked to the N─O bonds and oxygen lone pairs. This broader distribution reflects the strong electron withdrawal by ─NO2, which moves electron density away from the benzene rings (Figure 5f).

Orbital‐Level Understanding of Functional Group Effects

To further understand the effect of ligand functionalization for π–‍d conjugation, the PDOS for these five functionalized systems is analyzed and is shown in Figure 6. A clear evolution in orbital coupling is observed when moving from electron‐donating to electron‐withdrawing substituents. For systems with electron‐donating substituents such as 4S─NH2 and 4S─CH3, the Ni 3d states, mainly d xz and d yz , are relatively narrow and show limited overlap with the S p z and C p z states near the Fermi level (Figure 6a). The p z states of the ligands shift to higher energies, reducing the energy match with the Ni d‐states. The π–d hybridization is therefore weakened, and the electronic states remain comparatively confined within either the metal center or the ligand backbone. This behavior is consistent with the higher ELF density localized on the benzene rings shown in Figure 5 and the weaker E ads and lower voltages in Figure 3b.

PDOS of (a) Ni‐MOPs with different electron‐donating groups and (b) Ni‐MOPs with different electron‐withdrawing groups.

As the substituent changes from the unsubstituted 4S─H to the slightly electron‐withdrawing 4S─Cl, the orbital overlap gradually increases (Figure 6b). Within the energy window near the Fermi level, the Ni d states broaden and mix more strongly with the S p z states, while the contribution from the C p z states also extend into this window, indicating enhanced electronic coupling along the whole framework. This intermediate state reflects a more favorable energy alignment between the metal and ligand orbitals, leading to a moderate enhancement of π–d conjugation. The most significant change occurs in the strong electron‐withdrawing 4S─NO2 system. In this system, the p z state of the ligand shifts toward lower energies, significantly enhancing its overlap with the Ni 3d state. The PDOS plot reveals extensive hybridization among the Ni d xz /d yz states, S p z states, and C p z states over a broad energy range near the Fermi level. The boundary between the metal and ligand states becomes blurred, indicating the formation of strongly coupled frontier states. This enhanced hybridization directly reflects the strengthening of π–d conjugation.

The evolution of the PDOS features reveals two ways in which substituents regulate π–d conjugation. First, electron‐withdrawing groups lower the energy of the ligand p z states and bring them closer to the Ni d states, improving the energy match required for hybridization. Second, the resulting d–p overlap broadens and extends over a wider energy window, indicating stronger orbital delocalization across the framework. Together, these effects strengthen the Li–MOP interaction and account for the systematic enhancement of Li adsorption.

Conclusion

In this work, we conducted DFT calculations to investigate the origin and tunability of π–d conjugation in 1D Ni‐MOPs. The combined first‐principles results lead to a set of design principles for tuning π–d conjugation in low‐dimensional MOF and MOP systems. First, the bridging group plays a decisive role in determining the metal–ligand orbital overlap. Atoms with larger and more diffuse p orbitals, such as ─S, match the transition‐metal d states more effectively and form stronger, more continuous π–d conjugation pathways than the more contracted ─NH and ─O. Second, ligand functionalization tunes the energy alignment between the metal and ligand orbitals. Electron‐withdrawing groups lower the ligand p z energies and bring them closer to the metal d states, improving the orbital coupling, whereas electron‐donating groups raise the ligand energies and weaken the conjugation. Third, stronger π–d conjugation is reflected in both real‐space electron delocalization and energy‐space orbital mixing, which together enhance charge transfer and strengthen ion adsorption. These principles indicate that the strongest π–d conjugation, and therefore the strongest Li binding, is achieved by combining a diffuse bridging atom for effective orbital overlap with an electron‐withdrawing substituent for favorable energy alignment. This dual strategy, acting respectively on the metal–ligand bonding and on the ligand electronic environment, provides a general and predictive framework for designing low‐dimensional metal–organic electrodes with tunable lithium storage performance.
