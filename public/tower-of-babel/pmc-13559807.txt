Small ethmoid mucocele with intracranial extension associated with fibrous dysplasia: A rare case report
Authors: Luong Trung Vu, Dung Trung Dao, Le Cong Dinh, Hong Thị Nguyen, Nguyen Khoi Vu, Hao Thi Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ethmoid mucoceles are expansile, benign cystic lesions characterized by slow growth, predominantly affecting the frontal sinus, followed by the ethmoid sinus, and infrequently the sphenoid and maxillary sinuses. Intracranial involvement is rare and is typically associated with large or longstanding lesions. We present a case involving a 34-year-old immunocompetent woman who experienced non-specific facial pain subsequent to an influenza infection without exhibiting typical sinonasal or neurological symptoms. Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging revealed a small ethmoid sinus lesion (∼13-17 mm) with erosion of the anterior skull base and intracranial extension, resulting in mild compression of the frontal lobe without associated edema or neurological deficits. The patient underwent endoscopic sinus surgery, which resulted in complete drainage of the lesion. Histopathological analysis revealed the presence of coexisting fibrous dysplasia. The postoperative course was uneventful, with no evidence of complications or recurrence on follow-up examination. This case underscores that intracranial extension of an ethmoid sinus mucocele may occur even in small, clinically silent lesions, particularly in the presence of underlying bony abnormalities, such as fibrous dysplasia. Early multimodal imaging is crucial for diagnosis, and minimally invasive endoscopic management can yield favorable outcomes in appropriately selected patients.

Introduction

A sinonasal mucocele is a slowly progressing, benign cystic lesion formed by the obstruction of the natural sinus ostium, which leads to the accumulation of mucoid secretions and the gradual expansion of the sinus. This process induces bone pushing, erosion and destruction, allowing the lesion to extend into adjacent structures. Among the paranasal sinuses, the frontal sinus is the most frequently affected site, whereas ethmoid sinus involvement is less common but holds distinct clinical significance due to its intimate anatomical relationship with the orbit and the anterior skull base. Cribriform plate and ethmoid roof are thin, easily damaged structures; therefore, even small ethmoid sinus mucocele lesions can push through the ethmoid plate into the intracranial cavity and predisposing the lesion to intracranial extension when intra-sinus pressure remains elevated over a prolonged period [1,2]. Although the majority of the sinonasal mucoceles grow slowly and remain benign, intracranial extension is a rare complication that can lead to life-threatening conditions such as meningitis, brain abscesses, or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage [3].

In this report, we present a case of an ethmoid mucocele with intracranial extension, characterized by its notably small size despite having already caused skull base erosion and intracranial invasion, occurring concurrently with fibrous dysplasia. The patient did not exhibit the common syndromes associated with this condition, and the intracranial extension of the ethmoid mucocele was identified incidentally. Although larger mucoceles are more frequently associated with intracranial extension due to prolonged expansion and pressure-induced bone erosion, lesion size alone is not the sole determinant. Even though the mucocele is benign and progresses slowly, it may extend intracranially, particularly when located adjacent to inherently thin regions of the skull base, such as the cribriform plate or sphenoid sinus walls, or in the presence of pre-existing bony dehiscence. Therefore, our case demonstrates that intracranial extension results from a combination of chronic pressure effects and local anatomical vulnerability rather than size alone [3,4]. This report underscores the critical importance of multimodal diagnostic imaging for early diagnosis in recent clinical management strategies.

Case report

A 34-year-old female patient, with a history of good health and no previous occurrences of sinusitis, maxillofacial trauma, or surgical interventions, presented with symptoms following an influenza A infection 1 month prior to admission. She experienced right-sided facial pain, occasional bilateral temporal pain, and a sensation of postnasal drip. The patient did not report rhinorrhea, hyposmia, nasal congestion, blurred vision, or diplopia. An otorhinolaryngological endoscopic examination revealed mucopurulent discharge and polypoid tissue in the right sphenoethmoidal recess; the nasal septum was deviated to the left with edematous mucosa, although no frank pus was observed on the left side (see Fig. 1). The patient was diagnosed with right sphenoid sinusitis and a suspected left ethmoid mucocele, warranting surgical intervention. During the interim period prior to surgery, she received medical treatment comprising oral antibiotics, nasal saline irrigations, and topical corticosteroid sprays. Following 1 month of medical management, her facial pain and headaches were completely alleviated, and the polypoid lesion in the right sphenoethmoidal recess was no longer detectable.

The patient subsequently underwent functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS), which included a left nasal septoplasty, right sphenoidotomy, and left ethmoidectomy and maxillary antrostomy. Utilizing a curette, the ethmoid bulla was opened to access the anterior ethmoid cells, proceeding through the basal lamella to the posterior ethmoid, where a smooth, hard, bulging, spherical mass with a little hypervascular surface, highly suggestive of a mucocele, was encountered (Fig. 5). During the surgery, because we did not have a navigation system, we mainly relied on images and anatomical landmarks during the operation to determine the tumor's location. There were no signs of communication with the cerebrospinal fluid space that was confirmed during the surgery. In addition, there was no need to repair the skull base, no meningeal damage, and no CSF leakage. All bony partitions between the ethmoid air cells were meticulously removed. Upon opening the mucocele, an edematous mucosal lining and clear yellow fluid were observed inside. A bone fragment from the mucocele wall was sent for histopathological examination, and the results were consistent with fibrous dysplasia (Fig. 6). Radiographic findings were suggestive of atypical fibrous dysplasia (FD), showing only irregular cortical thickening; characteristic craniofacial FD lesions were not detected on the images. Discussion

Factors predisposing to paranasal sinuses mucoceles include previous sinus surgery, facial trauma, fibrosis, inflammation, polyposis, or mass effects from malignancy, osteoma, fibrous dysplasia, or Paget’s disease [1]. Though benign and slow growing, mucoceles can distort anatomy and pressure adjacent structures. Due to paranasal sinuses proximity to the orbit, mucoceles may affect orbital bones and eyes, causing symptoms like headache, facial pressure, nasal congestion, proptosis, diplopia, eyelid swelling, restricted motility, and optic neuropathy. Intracranial mucoceles often present with headaches, neurological deficits, or complications like abscesses or meningitis. However, the patient in this study showed no neurological symptoms despite MRI evidence of brain compression, highlighting the condition's silent progression and risk of unnoticed damage leading to irreversible deficits or infections. Thus, CT and MRI are crucial for early detection, especially when symptoms are atypical or absent. CT scans assess bony changes and skull base defects, while MRI distinguishes mucoceles from tumors or abscesses and evaluates intracranial involvement. In this case, hyperintense signals on T1W and T2W images indicated high-protein content, consistent with advanced mucoceles. The absence of parenchymal inflammation ruled out abscesses. MRI is recommended when CT shows skull base defects, even in asymptomatic patients, to avoid missing intracranial extensions [5,6].

Intracranial extension of ethmoid mucoceles is traditionally attributed to large or long-standing lesions [[7], [8], [9]]. The mechanisms driving bone erosion in these cases are hypothesized to involve a combination of mechanical and biochemical pathways: continuous mucus accumulation increases intra-sinus pressure, inducing stress-related bone remodeling, while chronic inflammation triggers the release of cytokines, collagenases, and prostaglandins that stimulate osteoclastic bone resorption [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Through this process, chronic progressive expansion rather than true tissue invasion, causes gradual thinning and erosion of the sinus walls. In our case, however, a relatively small mucocele (∼13-17 mm) extensively eroded the ethmoid roof and breached the anterior cranial fossa. Histopathological evaluation confirmed concurrent craniofacial FD. We hypothesize that FD played a dual predisposing role: first, structural bony deformities mechanically obstructed the sinus ostium to precipitate mucocele formation [3,4,9]; second, altered bone architecture redirected the expansion vector toward the skull base rather than allowing typical dilation within the sinus lumen. Because the cribriform plate, fovea ethmoidalis, and lateral lamella represent inherently thin, highly variable regions of the cranial base [11] localized pressure and osteolysis at these sites can drive intracranial penetration regardless of absolute tumor volume [3,[12], [13], [14]]. Despite anterior cranial fossa invasion, the patient was successfully treated via endoscopic sinus surgery alone without skull base reconstruction [[15], [16], [17]]. The dura mater remained intact with no CSF leak, supporting the hypothesis that dural condition rather than the radiological size of the bone defects the primary determinant governing surgical reconstruction requirements.

Historically, in terms of treatment, intracranial extending mucoceles were predominantly managed through open surgical approaches combined with extensive neurosurgical interventions. However, with the rapid evolution of endoscopy, endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS) is now widely considered the first-line treatment modality [[18], [19], [20], [21]], even in cases presenting with intracranial extension lacking severe acute complications. In our case, a purely endoscopic approach allowed for the effective and safe drainage of the mucocele without the morbidity associated with a craniotomy. This is in perfect accordance with current trends in the management of anterior skull base lesions, where advancing endoscopic techniques permit minimally invasive treatments with profoundly high efficacy. The patient's highly favorable postoperative course, with no documented CSF leaks or neurological complications, unequivocally confirms that the chosen therapeutic modality was appropriate. Nevertheless, it is imperative to note that mucoceles carry a recognized risk of recurrence if surgical drainage is inadequate; thus, stringent long-term follow-up is mandatory.

Conclusion

Ethmoid mucocele with intracranial extension represents a rare pathological condition that can progress insidiously, often resulting in a significant discrepancy between clinical symptoms and the actual extent of the structural lesion. This case clearly demonstrates that a small lesion size does not eliminate the inherent risk of skull base erosion and intracranial invasion, particularly when associated with underlying bony structural abnormalities such as fibrous dysplasia. Diagnostic imaging, particularly the combined use of CT and MRI, is crucial for the early detection and precise assessment of invasiveness. Endoscopic sinus surgery is an effective and exceedingly safe treatment modality in appropriately selected cases, significantly reducing the necessity for agressively invasive surgical interventions.
