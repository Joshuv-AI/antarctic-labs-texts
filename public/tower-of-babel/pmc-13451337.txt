Ice-free vitrification of embryos and its impact on post-warming outcomes
Authors: Abdallah Abdelhady, Jingzhi Zhang, Chia-Kuan Wu, Yoke Lee Lee, Meihong Shi, Patrick L. Crane, Mariana Diel de Amorim, Ian R. Porter, Qiudi Zheng, Robert E. Thorne, Jingyue Ellie Duan, Soon Hon Cheong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vitrification protocols are widely used for mammalian embryo cryopreservation. Post-warming outcomes are impaired by cryodamage from crystalline intracellular ice, whose formation depends on both the cooling and warming rates. Here, time-resolved X-ray diffraction measurements on bovine embryos indicated that current standard tools and protocols achieved ice-free cooling, but that intracellular ice always formed during warming. Increasing cooling rates by a factor of ~ 30 over current standard rates allowed ice-free cooling and warming, even when cryoprotectant concentrations were reduced by 30%. Embryos cooled at ultrafast rates exhibited survival and developmental competence rates close to those of unvitrified controls and significantly better than those cooled at standard rates. Increased global H3K9me3 intensity was found with both standard and ultrafast cooling relative to controls, while DNA methylation (5mC and 5hmC) was comparable for all groups. Transcriptomic analysis indicated adhesion and cell-junction assembly genes were downregulated while DNA damage repair pathway genes were upregulated with standard cooling relative to controls, and cell-development and differentiation pathway genes were downregulated with standard cooling relative to ultrafast cooling. Embryos vitrified with the ultrafast cool protocol retain full developmental potential and can establish successful pregnancies. Overall, increased cooling rates improved post-cryopreservation outcomes in part by minimizing ice formation, minimizing DNA damage, and preserving gene expression related to embryonic developmental functions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46755-9.

Introduction

Cryopreservation of oocytes and embryos is an essential technology in clinical assisted reproduction of animals, in preserving valuable genetics for agriculture or research, and in conserving species biodiversity. Successful cryopreservation depends on minimizing cryodamage, particularly damage associated with the formation of intracellular ice1. Ice formation during cooling and warming is influenced by the concentration of free water within a cell and by the time interval that the cell remains in the temperature range where ice nucleation and/or growth rates are large2. With sufficiently fast cooling, ice nucleation can be minimized and ice growth can be outrun, leaving intracellular solvent in a largely vitrified state.

In standard vitrification or “fast-cooling” protocols, embryos are soaked in high concentrations of non-penetrating and penetrating cryoprotective agents (CPAs) that remove and replace water within their cells, reducing the free water fraction and cooling rates required to prevent ice formation. Minimizing free water fractions must be balanced against damage from CPA toxicity and osmotic shock during soaks3,4. Samples are then placed on a support, hand-plunged in liquid nitrogen (LN2)5,6 and subsequently hand-inserted into warming media for recovery. Ice forms much more readily during warming than during cooling7 and this ice may often be responsible for most cryodamage8,9. The amount of ice formed during warming depends in part on the amount of ice nucleation during cooling and so can be decreased with faster cooling7,8. Both large cooling rates and large warming rates are thus essential to minimize intracellular ice formation while using minimally toxic CPA concentrations.

Previous advances in vitrification technology have increased cooling and warming rates by reducing the total thermal mass of both the embryo and its support/holder and by enabling direct contact of the embryo with LN2 and warming solution, maximizing heat transfer rates. Among commercial vitrification systems, the open Cryotop™ method (Kitazato Biopharma) is likely the most widely used and has been reported to deliver the highest cooling and warming rates, estimated at around 23,000 °C/min10 and 42,000 °C/min, respectively11, for 110–120 µm diameter oocytes.

Ice formation is also a major issue in protein cryocrystallography, where ice damages protein crystals and generates interfering X-ray diffraction12,13. The range of protein crystal sizes (~ 5–500 μm) overlaps with that of mammalian oocytes (80–130 μm) and blastocyst-stage embryos (~ 100–200 μm). Nylon loops used to hold protein crystals have been used in bovine embryo cryopreservation, yielding good post-thaw development outcomes14. By using microfabricated thin-film crystallography supports and an automated LN2-based crystallography cryocooler, cooling rates of ~ 300,000–600,000 °C/min are obtained for blastocyst-sized samples8. These tools from cryocrystallography provide a platform for exploring the impact of ultrafast cooling on embryo cryopreservation outcomes.

Detecting and quantifying intracellular ice in embryos using optical imaging is challenging, particularly in bovine embryos due to their dark, lipid-rich cytoplasm. Synchrotron-based X-ray diffraction provides a sensitive method for detecting ice in aqueous solutions and protein crystals8,13. We recently used time-resolved X-ray diffraction to quantify ice formed after cooling and during warming8 in bovine oocytes and how the amount and grain size of ice depends on cooling rates and CPA concentrations.

The effect of cryodamage is usually assessed by quantifying the post-warming embryo survival rate and the achievement of developmental milestones15. Sublethal cryodamage causes delays in reaching those milestones. For example, embryos commonly have a collapsed blastocoele cavity following CPA exposure and vitrification16. Embryos whose cavities re-expand faster after thawing have a higher likelihood of establishing pregnancies16,17. Sublethal cryodamage can delay resumption of cell division and hatching and increase the number and proportion of apoptotic cells within the embryo18.

Transcriptomic analysis using RNA sequencing provides a sensitive method for evaluating post-thaw embryo function. Previous studies have identified the impact of standard vitrification protocols on transcriptomics in multiple mammalian models, including murine, porcine, and bovine oocytes and preimplantation embryos. In the murine model, cooling and thawing did not induce significant transcriptomic changes in MII oocytes19 and embryos20 compared to fresh or soaked but not cooled embryos, though stress-related genes were upregulated at 3 h post-warming in pronuclear stage embryos. In porcine blastocysts, vitrification protocols caused transcriptomic alterations involved in osmotic stress, hormone responses, and developmental growth21. Studies of bovine morula22, blastocysts23, and elongation embryos24 using microarray and RNA-seq analysis have reported impaired expression of genes involved in apoptosis, cell cycle, structure, signaling, differentiation, adherent junctions, lipid metabolism, and oxidation.

Vitrification protocol induced cellular stress including DNA damage can trigger epigenetic modifications that influence genome stability and embryonic development25. One prominent epigenetic response to DNA damage is the enrichment of Histone 3 Lysine 9 (H3K9me3), a repressive heterochromatin mark26, which helps maintain genome stability and protect DNA from damage26. H3K9me3 also plays essential roles in maintaining cell identity and silencing lineage-inappropriate genes during mouse embryo development27 and in embryonic stem cells28–30. In addition to histone modifications, DNA methylation marks such as 5-methylcytosine (5mC) and 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) are dynamically regulated during development and in response to environmental stressors. While 5mC at promoters is typically associated with gene silencing, 5hmC represents an intermediate in active DNA demethylation and is often linked to gene activation31,32. Recent studies have shown that 5hmC specifically accumulates at DNA damage sites in both human and mouse embryonic stem (ES) cells32, suggesting a functional role in coordinating the DNA repair process and preserving genome integrity.

In this study, we evaluated intracellular ice formed during cooling, ice growth during warming, post-warming developmental, global epigenetic mark (H3K9me3, 5mC and 5hmC) levels, and transcriptomic profiles in bovine embryos vitrified using either a standard Cryotop-based protocols (Standard Cool, SC) or using a cryocrystallography protocol (Ultrafast Cool, UFC) that gives much higher cooling rates. Outcomes were compared with those from unfrozen controls. Our results show that the Ultrafast Cool protocol effectively prevents ice formation during both cooling and warming and yields developmental outcomes comparable to those of unfrozen controls, significantly surpassing those obtained using the Standard protocols. Immunostaining revealed a significant increase in the repressive histone mark H3K9me3 in both Standard and Ultrafast groups compared to the Control, whereas changes in 5mC and 5hmC were less pronounced. Transcriptomic profiling showed that embryos from the Ultrafast Cool group exhibited fewer deviations from Controls than those from the Standard Cool group, though some perturbations remained. Transferred embryos from the Ultrafast Cool group led to successful pregnancies, confirming developmental competence. Together, these findings establish ultrafast cooling as a novel, ice-free vitrification approach that minimizes cryodamage and preserves developmental and molecular integrity in bovine embryos.

After re-expansion, the next milestone in embryo development is hatching from the zona pellucida. The proportion of embryos that hatched was significantly lower in the Standard Cool group (77.6%) compared with the Control (92.0%) and Ultrafast Cool (87.3%) groups. (Fig. 2C and D). Moreover, the Standard Cool group required a much longer time (2,055 min) for hatching compared with the Ultrafast Cool (1,394 min) and Control (1,353 min) groups. (Table 1).

Embryo death and degeneration was also assessed by morphology and movement (Fig. 2E and F and Table 1). Dead and degenerated embryos were assessed within 48 h after warming. The proportion of embryos that were degenerated was similar for all the groups: 2.0% in the Control group, 1.4% in Ultrafast Cool group, and 5.3% in Standard Cool group (Table 1).

Standard cooling yields more frequent blastocoel collapse and cell apoptosis

Embryos that re-expanded were analyzed for the number of times their blastocoel collapsed after re-expansion and before hatching (Fig. S1), as repeated collapses are associated with reduced embryo quality and lower implantation potential33,34. The Control and Ultrafast Cool group embryos averaged one blastocoel collapse while Standard Cool group embryos averaged two blastocoel collapses, highlighting a negative impact of the Standard Cool protocol on embryo quality.

In the comparison between the Standard Cool and the Control group, 110 genes were differentially expressed, with 93 genes down-regulated and 17 up-regulated in the Standard Cool group (Supplementary Table 3, Fig. 5A). Notably, Forkhead box protein 3 (FOXA3), known for its critical role in gluconeogenic gene regulation35, emerged as the most downregulated (fold change) in the Standard Cool group relative to the Control group. In contrast, DNA demethylase Tet methylcytosine dioxygenase 2 (TET2) was significantly upregulated in Standard Cool compared to Control (Fig. 5A). GO analysis revealed that gene functions in cellular activities including synapse assembly, neuron migration, cell–cell adhesion via plasma-membrane adhesion molecules, and cell junction assembly were predominantly upregulated in the Standard Cool group, while cellular extravasation, cell adhesion, and thymus development were downregulated.

In the comparison between the Ultrafast Cool and Control groups, 72 genes were differentially expressed, with 32 genes down regulated and 40 upregulated in the Ultrafast Cool group (Fig. 5B and Table 2). Phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH), key to the de novo serine biosynthesis pathway36, and mitochondrial translocator protein (TSPO)37 were downregulated in the Ultrafast Cool relative to the Control group. In contrast, threonyl-tRNA synthetase 3 (TARS3) was the most upregulated in Ultrafast Cool group. Previous studies suggested depletion of TARS3 impacts metabolism and muscle development, leading to development retardation38. Furthermore, genes for cyclic-nucleotide-mediated signaling, neuron migration, second messenger-mediated signaling, and cell migration were upregulated in Ultrafast Cool compared to Control group, while pigment biosynthesis, metabolic processes, cellular lipid metabolic processes, and carboxylic acid biosynthesis processes were downregulated in the Ultrafast Cool group (Fig. 5B).

Comparing the Standard Cool and Ultrafast Cool groups, 100 genes were differentially expressed, with Standard Cool showing 66 down-regulated and 34 up-regulated genes (Fig. 5C, Table 2). TSPO, known for its role in cholesterol importation and other hormone biosynthesis processes39, was downregulated in the Ultrafast Cool group compared to Controls but exhibited the most significant upregulation (fold change) in the Standard Cool when compared to the Ultrafast Cool group, indicating cholesterol-related functions were impacted in the Ultrafast Cool group. Conversely, the potassium voltage-gated channel subfamily H member 4 (KCNH4), involved in neurotransmitter release and neuronal excitability, was most significantly downregulated (fold change) in the Standard Cool group. GO analysis revealed that signal transduction in response to DNA damage, lipid transport, and localization processes were upregulated while system process, axon guidance, central nervous system neuron differentiation, and neuron projection guidance were downregulated in the Standard Cool group relative to the Ultrafast Cool group (Fig. 5C).

Comparison of DEGs across cryopreservation methods

To evaluate the shared or distinct impacts of different cryopreservation methods on embryos, we identified overlapping DEGs via intersecting gene sets based on the direction of up- or down-regulation across each pair-wise comparison (Fig. S4). Our analysis identified the largest number (n = 16) of shared downregulated DEGs in Standard Cool when compared to Control and Ultrafast Cool groups including ASGR1, EPH86, and ASTN2. Five genes were upregulated in Ultrafast Cool compared to Control and Standard Cool, including CFAP210, NDST3, SYT17, TGM1, and C14H8orf88. Four genes were downregulated in both Standard Cool and Ultrafast Cool compared to Control, including ALAS2, ASIP, CYP4F2, and PINLYP.

To gain more global insight into gene expression patterns in embryos subjected to different cryopreservation protocols, we conducted k-means (k = 5) cluster analysis using all DEGs identified in this study (n = 282, Fig. 6 and Table S5). The DEGs were grouped into 5 clusters, each with distinct GO enrichment profiles. DEGs in Cluster 5 (gene size = 45) were only elevated in the Standard Cool group, enriching signal transduction in response to DNA damage and lipid localization, consistent with the lower developmental rate observed in the Standard Cool group compared to Ultrafast and Control Groups. DEGs in Cluster 4 (gene size = 60) were enriched in the Ultrafast Cool group, including gene function in the integrin-mediated signaling pathway and in cell–cell adhesion. Cluster 3 (gene size = 27) contained genes upregulated in both the Ultrafast Cool and Standard Cool groups relative to the Control group, enriching the cellular response to nitrogen compounds, skeletal muscle cell differentiation, and cyclic purine nucleotide metabolic process. Pregnancy confirmation from Ultrafast Cool protocol embryos

To assess the developmental competence of embryos vitrified using the Ultrafast Cool protocol, embryos were transferred into recipient cows seven days after observed natural estrus as detected by thermal imaging. Three Ultrafast Cool group embryos were successfully transferred into three recipients, and two became pregnant. Pregnancy was confirmed using ultrasonography (Fig. S5) at 26 days after embryo transfer into the recipient (pregnancy day 33). This demonstrates that embryos vitrified with our Ultrafast Cool protocol retain full developmental potential and can establish successful pregnancies.

Next, X-ray diffraction patterns were recorded during warming in a room temperature N2 gas stream. (Fig. 7E–H, K, and L). In each case, the pattern corresponding to the time at which ice diffraction (if ever present) was strongest or the pattern at 50 ms if no ice formed is shown. No ice was observed during warming for embryos in the Ultrafast Cool group with permeating CPA concentrations between 100 and 80% (Fig. 7E–F), faint and diffuse rings indicating a small ice fraction were observed at 60% (Fig. 7G), and strong ice rings were observed at 50% (Fig. 7H). For the Standard Cool group, strong ice diffraction was observed during warming at all permeating CPA concentrations (Fig. 7K and L).

This difference in ice formation is related to the very different cooling and warming times when using Standard Cool vs Ultrafast Cool protocols. As shown in Fig. 7, peak ice diffraction intensities/maximum ice fractions occurred at times after the start of warming of ~ 50–60 ms for embryos on crystallography supports vs 260–300 ms for embryos on Cryotops, when both were warmed in identical warm gas streams. All ice diffraction disappeared ~ 80 ± 23 ms after the start of warming for embryos on crystallography supports vs 410 ± 62 ms on Cryotops, corresponding to average warming rates from ~ 100 K to ~ 273 K of ~ 130,000 °C/min and ~ 25,000 °C/min, respectively. Cooling rates using Cryotops in the Standard Cool protocol are roughly a factor of 20–30 smaller than when using cryocrystallography tools in the Ultrafast Cool protocol.

Note that the ice diffraction results during warming do not directly translate to the post-thaw development and RNA-seq results described above, as the warming methods and rates were different. Gas stream warming, which was fully automated, likely gave more reproducible warming than is achieved in standard manual warming in shallow thawing-solution-filled wells.

Discussion

We have compared the post-thaw survival rate, re-expansion rate, and viability of bovine embryos subject to standard cryopreservation protocols and to protocols using tools from cryocrystallography that deliver ultrafast cooling rates and somewhat larger warming rates. Post-thaw outcomes were generally good for all treatment groups. However, the Ultrafast Cool protocol improved several outcomes compared with the Standard Cool protocol, yielding significantly higher hatching rates, significantly lower times to re-expansion and hatching, and significantly lower fractions of apoptotic cells. By most metrics, outcomes with the Ultrafast Cool group were similar to those with the Control group.

Previous studies of human blastocysts subject to vitrification and warming have found that shorter times to re-expansion correlate with earlier hatching and better pregnancy outcomes16,17, and that earlier post-thaw hatching correlates with better developmental competence40,41. Human embryos with fewer blastocoel collapses have been shown to yield a higher live birth rate than embryos with multiple blastocoel collapses33,42. As bovine blastocysts in the Ultrafast Cool group showed faster re-expansion and hatching and fewer blastocoel collapses, they may be expected to yield better pregnancy outcomes as well.

During early embryo development, epigenetic reprogramming is tightly tuned and highly dynamic. Cryopreservation processes occurring in specific stages could introduce distinct disruptions of this process and alter developmental outcomes43–46. In early stage bovine embryos, the histone mark H3K9me3 remains stable until the 8-cell stage and then the intensity decreases, consistent with its role in heterochromatin establishment and transcription repression47. Here we observed significantly elevated levels of H3K9me3 in both the SC and UFC groups, while levels of DNA methylation marks 5mC and 5hmCs remained unchanged. This suggests that vitrification leads to disruption of histone modification, regardless of cooling rate, perhaps by interfering with timely removal of repressive histone marks essential for development. In a recent study in mice, vitrification of 8-cell stage embryos led to significantly increased levels of histone marks H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H4K12ac, and H4K16ac44 associated with active transcription, implying an open chromatin structure allowing transcriptional activities following thawing. In bovine embryos prepared from vitrified oocytes, H3K9me3 levels remained stable until the 8-cell stage and then increased significantly at the blastocyst stage46. Other studies have reported reduced levels of 5mC and H3K9me3 in cleavage-stage embryos derived from vitrified oocytes46. Interestingly, elevated H3K9me3 levels have also been observed in reprogramming-resistant regions of Somatic Cell Nuclear Transfer (SCNT) mouse embryos at the 2-cell stage compared to normally fertilized counterparts48. Overexpression of H3K9me3 demethylase Kdm4d improved the blastocyst rate in the SCNT embryos, highlighting H3K9me3 as a potential barrier to successful epigenetic reprogramming. Taken together, the elevated H3K9me3 level in both SC and UFC groups suggests a common epigenetic response to the vitrification process, independent of the cooling rate. These findings highlight the need for further investigation using single-base resolution genomic methods such as Cleavage Under Targets & Tagmentation (CUT&Tag) sequencing to elucidate how cryopreservation alters histone modification dynamics and impacts embryonic developmental competence.

Changes in epigenetic regulation post cryopreservation may contribute to perturbations in gene expression profile. Our transcriptomics analysis indicates substantial differential expression of genes related to lipid metabolism, cellular functions, and DNA damage repair.

Vitrification of morula-stage bovine embryos was found to perturb expression of genes enriched in cell membranes and to impair lipid metabolism functions22. Comparing embryos prepared using vitrification and slow cooling protocols (the latter inducing extracellular ice formation), expression of cytochrome P450 family 11 subfamily A member 1 (CYP11A1) was downregulated22. CYP11A1 is predominantly involved in steroid biosynthesis, pregnenolone biosynthesis, and eicosanoid signaling22. In our study, a gene from cytochrome P450 family 4 subfamily F member 2 (CYP4F2) was downregulated in both Standard Cool and Ultrafast Cool groups relative to the Control group. Furthermore, among the DEGs in the Standard Cool group relative to the Control group, Asialoglycoprotein receptor 1 (ASGR1) and Enoyl-CoA Hydratase 1 (ECH1) were significantly downregulated. Proteins encoded by CYP4F2, ASGR1, and ECH1 are involved in lipid metabolism which mediates lysosomal degradation of glycoproteins49 and peroxisomal lipid metabolism50, respectively. Disrupted expression of these three genes may indicate disruption in lipid metabolism induced by both cryopreservation methods.

Our Gene Ontology analysis indicates that cell–cell adhesion via plasma-membrane adhesion molecules and cell junction assembly were upregulated, and cell adhesion was downregulated in the Standard Cool group compared with the Control. Among genes associated with cell membrane functions, Astrotactin 2 (ASTN2), which regulates endosomal trafficking machinery51, was downregulated in the Standard Cool group relative to both the Ultrafast Cool and Control groups. Adhesion G protein-coupled receptor L3 (ADGRL3), an integral component of plasma membrane involved in calcium ion binding activities and nervous system development52, was upregulated in Standard Cool relative to Control. These results suggest that cryodamage affecting lipid metabolism and cell membrane functions may contribute to inferior post-thaw outcomes and that the Standard Cool and Ultrafast Cool protocols have distinct impacts on these cellular processes.

Small GTPases of the Rho family regulate the actin cytoskeleton, thereby controlling cell motility and migration and contributing to embryonic development53. Rho GTPases including RhoA, Rac1 and Cdc42 were found to be indispensable during early embryo development in mice18,53,54. For example, inhibition of Rho signal transduction was associated with failures of blastocoel formation and trophectoderm junction assembly and with early embryo lethality53. Inhibition of Rho-associated coiled-coil kinase has been linked to improved embryo competence following vitrification and warming18,54. Here, Rho guanine nucleotide exchange factors, Pleckstrin Homology and RhoGEF Domain Containing G7 (PLEKHG7), were significantly downregulated in the Standard Cool group compared with the Control group. PLEKHG7 contains guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) domains, which are known to activate Cdc42. Cdc42 is essential for formation of the cytoplasmic actin bundle in cell–cell contact during mouse early development55. Abnormal expression of PLEKHF7 in the Standard Cool group may contribute to the observed lower hatching rate for this group.

Cryopreservation is known to induce DNA damage, perhaps due to toxicity of cryoprotective agents and to injury from ice formation, compromising developmental capability in bovine embryos22,56. In our GO analysis, signal transduction in response to DNA damage was upregulated in the Standard Cool group compared with the Ultrafast Cool group. Specifically, ankryrin repeat domain 1 (ANKRD1) and cyclin D1 (CCND1) were among the most significantly upregulated genes in the Standard Cool group compared to the Ultrafast Cool group. ANKRD1 can be induced under hypoxia conditions and in response to DNA damage, interacting with P53 to regulate the DNA damage response57. In human cells, CCND1 is predominantly enriched in cancer cells58, where its degradation is critical for cell cycle arrest in response to DNA damage58. Cyclin dependent kinase 5 (CDK5), involved in repairing disordered DNA in cancer cells via modulation of STAT3-Eme1 pathway at the replication fork59, was also downregulated in the Standard Cool group compared with both the Control and Ultrafast Cool groups. Together, these findings suggest that elevated expression of DNA damage response pathways in the Standard Cool group may contribute to higher rates of cell apoptosis and reduced blastocyst quality in this group.

The Ultrafast Cool group overall shows developmental outcomes that are close to or indistinguishable from those of unfrozen Controls, and substantially better than those obtained in the Standard Cool group. In the Ultrafast Cool group, manual plunging in LN2 is replaced with automated plunging using a cryocrystallography instrument, and Cryotops are replaced by crystallography supports. Cooling rates delivered by the automated cryocrystallography instrument for otherwise identical small (< 200 µm thick) samples are roughly an order of magnitude larger than those achieved in standard manual cooling practice and have far less variability. Our time-resolved X-ray diffraction data indicate that warming rates in an identical gas stream are roughly 5 times larger for embryos on crystallography supports versus Cryotops due to much lower thermal mass and more complete exposure of embryos to the warming fluid. Cooling rates under otherwise identical conditions (manual or automated) when using crystallography supports should be larger by a similar factor. Much larger (~ 30 ×) cooling rates and larger (~ 5 ×) warming rates in the Ultrafast Cool group together contribute to improved cryopreservation outcomes. With standard concentrations of penetrating and non-penetrating cryoprotectants, cooling rates achieved in both Standard Cool and Ultrafast Cool protocols are adequate to prevent substantial ice fractions from forming within bovine embryos during cooling (although variability in cooling rates and perhaps ice formation will be much larger in Standard cooling.) During warming at rates comparable to those achieved in standard warming protocols, substantial ice fractions (likely involving most or all “free” water8,60) transiently appear in Standard Cool samples. Ultrafast Cool samples remain ice free.

This difference in ice formed during warming likely explains the difference in damage caused by the cryopreservation cycle, due to differences in both cooling and warming rates7,8. Ice nuclei always form during cooling, even though their small size and volume fraction may render them undetectable. During warming, these nuclei grow rapidly, and additional nuclei may form and grow. The density of nuclei available for growth during warming is roughly proportional to the cooling time, and so for similar warming times longer cooling times give larger ice fractions. Shorter warming times reduce the time for ice growth and give smaller ice fractions.

Differences between the Ultrafast and Control groups in developmental outcomes and in patterns of gene expression may arise from non-optimal and variable warming rates in standard protocols, in which embryos are inserted into thawing solution at 37 °C that is held in shallow (few mm deep) wells. Large improvements in both the rate and reproducibility of warming are possible8. These may further close the gap in outcomes when using fresh versus cryopreserved embryos, not just for bovine embryos but for all CPA-permeable mammalian embryos.

Conclusion

Here we have described a comprehensive study of post-thaw developmental parameters and transcriptome changes of in vitro produced bovine embryos that were vitrified using Standard and Ultrafast Cool protocols and warmed using standard protocols. The results illustrate how large increases in cooling rate (and small increases in warming rate) can positively impact developmental and transcriptomic outcomes and lead to successful pregnancies. Further optimization of cooling rates and especially warming rates and reductions in penetrating CPA concentrations needed to prevent ice formation during cooling and warming are feasible. These may further narrow the developmental gap between fresh and cryopreserved embryos of many mammalian species.

Methods

In vitro embryo production

Bovine embryos were produced from slaughterhouse ovaries cultured in commercial media (IVF Bioscience) and fertilized using cryopreserved semen from three proven bulls as previously described18. Briefly, good quality cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs), defined as those with dark homogeneous cytoplasm and at least 2 intact layers of cumulus cells, were matured in IVM media (BO-IVM™, IVF Bioscience) and incubated at 38.5° C for 22 h in a humidified air atmosphere supplemented with 5% CO2. Fertilization was accomplished by adding bull sperm at a concentration of 106/mL and incubated in IVF media for 18 h at 38.5 °C in a humidified atmosphere supplemented with 5% CO2. After fertilization, presumptive zygotes were denuded of cumulus cells and cultured in IVC media (BO-IVC™, IVF Bioscience) in a humidified atmosphere of 5% O2, 5% CO2, and 90% N2 at 38.5 °C for 7 days. Cleavage rate was assessed at day 2 following fertilization and blastocyst rate was assessed at day 7. Blastocysts were selected based on International Embryo Technology Society (IETS) criteria and first grade stage 6 and 7 blastocysts were selected for the experiment.

Experimental design

Embryos in the Control group were soaked in equilibration, vitrification, and warming solutions with no cryopreservation as a positive control. Standard Cool group embryos were processed using the standard Cryotop vitrification protocol, while Ultrafast Cool group embryos were processed using cryocrystallography tools (Fig. 8). Embryos in both the SC and UFC groups used the same soaking protocols as the CTRL group, and both were thawed using the same (standard) protocol. Details are described below.Fig. 8Experimental Design. Bovine blastocyst-stage embryos were randomly assigned to three groups: Control, Standard Cooling, and Ultrafast Cooling groups to execute protocols as shown. All groups were then compared using the methods shown at right, including post-warming developmental assessment, cell apoptosis evaluation, global epigenetic marks staining, and RNA sequencing.

Experimental Design. Bovine blastocyst-stage embryos were randomly assigned to three groups: Control, Standard Cooling, and Ultrafast Cooling groups to execute protocols as shown. All groups were then compared using the methods shown at right, including post-warming developmental assessment, cell apoptosis evaluation, global epigenetic marks staining, and RNA sequencing.

Non-frozen control group (CTRL)

Embryos in this group were soaked in equilibration solution (7.5% ethylene glycol, 7.5% dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO), in media M199 with 20% fetal calf serum) for 13 min, transferred into vitrification solution, (15% ethylene glycol, 15% DMSO, in media M199 with 20% fetal calf serum and 0.5 M sucrose) and soaked for 1 min at room temperature. Soaked embryos were loaded onto a Cryotop and then immediately de-loaded into warming solution 1 (Base media containing 1 M sucrose) for 1 min, soaked in warming solution 2 (Base media containing 0.5 M sucrose) for 3 min, soaked in warming solution 3 (Base media containing 0.25 M sucrose) for 5 min, and then soaked in base media for 5 min. Embryos were loaded into a plate containing in vitro culture (IVC) media and incubated for 48 h in a humidified atmosphere of 5% O2, 5% CO2, and 90% N2 at 38.5 °C, while being imaged using a time-lapse video recording system.

Standard Cool group (SC)

Embryos in this group were soaked in equilibration solution for 13 min and then soaked in vitrification solution for 1 min at room temperature. Each embryo was then loaded onto a Cryotop™ (Kitazato), taking care to minimize the surrounding volume of vitrification solution. Embryos were then plunged by hand into liquid nitrogen and stored until warming.

Ultrafast Cool group (UFC)

Embryos in this group were soaked in equilibration solution for 13 min and in vitrification solution for 1 min at room temperature. They were then loaded onto one of two cryocrystallography supports: smaller embryos were loaded on Microloops LD100 (with 100 µm apertures) and larger embryos were loaded on Microloops LD150 (MiTeGen). Microloops support the embryo using a microfabricated 10 µm thick polymer loop (rather than a ~ 90 μm thick solid polymer as in Cryotops), which minimizes thermal mass and surrounding liquid and maximizes contact of cooling and warming fluids with the embryo. Embryos on Microloops were then plunged into liquid nitrogen and stored in cryocrystallography “pucks” using an automated cryocooling instrument developed for cryocrystallography (Nanuq™, MiTeGen). This instrument gives cooling rates of ~ 600,000 °C/minute for ~ 100–125 um samples.

Thawing of Standard Cool group (SC) and ultrafast Cool group (UFC)

For the SC group, the Cryotop™ (Kitazato) was removed from the LN2 and its sample-holding tip was immediately inserted into 3 mL of warming solution I (1 M sucrose) held in a 35 mm petri dish. The Cryotop was swirled in the solution until the sample had fallen off its tip and was fully thawed. Samples were left in warming solution I for 1 min then moved to warming solution II (0.5 M sucrose) for 3 min and then to warming solution III (0.25 M sucrose) for 5 min and then soaked in base media for 5 min. All solutions were warmed to 38.5 °C. During each transfer, the media surrounding and transferred with the sample with the cells was minimized. After each transfer, the solution was swirled several times using a pulled pipette to ensure fast and uniform penetration into the sample.

For the UFC group, samples on crystallography supports were retrieved one at a time from a storage puck submerged in LN2 using a magnetic wand and then immediately immersed in 3 mL of warming solution I in a 35 mm petri dish. The sample support was swirled in the solution until the sample had fallen off its tip and was fully thawed. Subsequent post-thaw steps were completed as described for the SC group.

Assessment of post-warming embryo outcomes

A total of 1,029 COCs were selected for in vitro embryo production and use in the post-thaw time lapse culture portion of the study, yielding 875 cleaved embryos (85.1%) on day 2 and 350 blastocysts (34%) on day 7. Between 70 and 100 blastocysts were assigned to each treatment group based on the block design (Table 1), with no significant differences in embryo stage between groups (p = 0.8312). An additional 69 blastocysts that re-expanded and hatched after post-thaw culture were evaluated using TUNEL and Hoechst 33342 staining for total cell count, apoptotic count, and apoptotic index.

TUNEL staining

Apoptotic cells were identified using the TUNEL in situ cell death detection kit (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). Post-thaw embryos were cultured for 48 h. Only embryos that re-expanded and hatched were selected for TUNEL staining, to avoid skewing the proportion of apoptotic cells in each embryo by including non-viable embryos. Selected embryos were washed in PBS with 1 mg/mL polyvinylpyrrolidone (Sigma-Aldrich), fixed in 4% (v/v) paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA) in PBS for 1 h, permeabilized in 0.2% (v/v) Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS with 1 mg/mL polyvinylpyrrolidone for 30 min, all at room temperature. Embryos at 37 ºC were then stained with TUNEL for 1 h and then by Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 15 min. Embryos were mounted onto poly-L-lysine coated slides (VWR), flattened with a cover slip, and imaged using a BZ-X800 fluorescent microscope (Keyence, Itasca, IL, USA) with an excitation wavelength of 360 nm and emission wavelength of 460 nm for Hoechst 33342 to identify the total number of cells, and with an excitation wavelength of 470 nm and emission wavelength of 525 nm for TUNEL to identify apoptotic cells. The apoptotic index was calculated as the ratio (in percent) of apoptotic cells to the total cell count of each embryo. Individual embryos were used as the experimental unit for fluorescent microscopy analysis.

Epigenetic staining

A total of 19, 18, and 15 re-expanded blastocysts in CTRL, SC, and UFC groups, respectively, were selected for H3K9me3 immunofluorescence staining. Blastocysts were fixed with 4% PFA in 0.01% polyvinyl acetate (PVA)/PBS for 10 min at room temperature, then transferred into 0.5% TritonX100/PVA/PBS for permeabilization for 1 h at room temperature, followed by the blocking step in 3%BSA/0.1%TritonX-100/PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Next, samples were incubated in primary antibody (1:500, rabbit, Abcam Ab8898) at 37 °C for 1 h. Secondary antibody (1:100, Abcam ab150077) incubation was processed at 37 °C for 1 h followed by Hoechst 33342 staining and mounting.

For 5mC and 5hmC IF staining, 17, 22, and 17 blastocysts from CTRL, SC, and UFC groups, respectively, were used. Same steps were taken for embryo fixation and permeabilization as described above. After permeabilization, blastocysts were treated with 2 N HCl for 12 min at room temperature, followed by 0.25% Trypsin–EDTA (Invitrogen) digestion at 37 °C for 5 min followed by blocking step in 3%BSA/0.1%TritonX-100/PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were then incubated in primary antibody mix against distinct host (5mC: 1:500, mouse, Diagenode C15200081-10; 5hmC: 1:500, rabbit, Abcam Ab2630381) at 4 °C overnight and in secondary antibody (Alexa 488, Abcam ab150077, 1:100, rabbit; Alexa 647, Abcam, ab150115, 1:100, mouse) at 4 °C for 1 h, followed by Hoechst 33342 staining and mounting.

RNA extraction

From 3 replicates for each of the three embryo groups studied (Control, Standard Cool and Ultrafast Cool), pools of 5–10 embryos that hatched during the first 24 h post thaw were collected and RNA extracted and sent to the Cornell Bio-informatics facility for library preparation and sequencing. Total RNA extraction was performed using the PicoPure RNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the kit’s recommended protocol. Briefly, embryos were transferred to the extraction buffer and kept at 42 °C for 30 min on a pre-heated heater block and then centrifuged at 800 g for two min. Before loading the cell lysate into the purification column, the column was pre-conditioned by adding 250 μl of conditioning buffer, incubated for 5 min at room temperature and then centrifuged at 16,000 g for 1 min. To prepare the cell extract sample, 10 μl of ethanol was added to the cell extract and the mixture loaded into the purification column. 100 μl of Wash Buffer 1 was pipetted into the purification column and the column centrifuged for 1 min at 8,000 g. Then 100 μl of Wash Buffer 2 was pipetted into the purification column and the column centrifuged for 1 min at 8,000 g, followed by another 100 μl of Wash Buffer 2 and centrifugation for 2 min at 16,000 g. The purification column was then transferred to a new 0.5 ml microcentrifuge tube provided in the kit and 15 μl Elution Buffer was pipetted directly onto the membrane of the purification column. The column was incubated for 1 min at room temperature, centrifuged for 1 min at 1,000 g to distribute the Elution Buffer within the column, and then centrifuged for 1 min at 16,000 g to elute the RNA.

RNA quality control and poly-A RNA isolation

RNA sample quality was confirmed using a Qubit (RNA HS kit; Thermo Fisher) to determine concentration and a Fragment Analyzer (Agilent) to determine RNA integrity. Each sample had an RNA integrity number (RIN) greater than 6, confirming high quality. Then Poly A + RNA was isolated with the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (New England Biolabs).

Illumina library preparation and Illumina sequencing

TruSeq-barcoded RNAseq libraries were generated with the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs). Each library was quantified with a Qubit HS dsDNA assay kit (Thermo Fisher) and the size distribution was determined with a Fragment Analyzer (Agilent) prior to pooling. Libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina instrument. At least 10 M reads were generated per library.

RNA sequencing data analysis

Raw sequencing FASTQ files were processed by trimming reads for low quality and adaptor sequences with TrimGalore (v0.6.0)61, which is a wrapper for cutadapt62 and fastQC. The reads were aligned using STAR (v2.7.0e)63 to the bovine reference genome (Bos_taurus/ARS-UCD1.2_GCA_002263795.2 reference genome). R packages limma64 and sva65 were used to remove batch effects in the three replicates, DESeq2 (v1.26.0)49 to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), and lfcshrink was used to generate normalized counts and statistical analysis of differential gene expression66. The DEGs were identified in the pair-wise manner using the criteria of Fold Change (FC) > 2, Padj < 0.05. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis was applied to the whole DEG list and in a pair-wise manner using the ComplexHeatmap67 package in R. Functional analysis including Gene Ontology enrichment was performed and incorporated besides the clustering heatmap using ClusterGV68 in R. Finally, the intersections of DEGs among different conditions were plotted using UpsetR69 package in R.

Statistical analysis

To calculate the apoptotic cell count and index based on TUNEL staining results, total cell count and apoptotic cell count data were assumed to arise from Poisson distributions and were log-transformed before being analyzed with linear regression. Differences between groups were determined using Tukey’s HSD post hoc test. The apoptotic index was not normally distributed and was analyzed using the Steel–Dwass test to determine differences between groups.

All statistical analysis of post-thaw blastocyst outcomes was performed using JMP Pro version 16 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Logistic regression assessed differences in the proportion of embryos that re-expanded, hatched, or degenerated across treatment groups, with replicates included as a covariate. Cox’s proportional hazard model was used to evaluate the time to re-expansion, hatching, or degeneration, and failure plots were generated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. The non-parametric Steel–Dwass multiple comparison test was used to assess the number of times the blastocoel cavity collapsed per embryo by group.

To quantify the immunofluorescence staining intensity, the regions of interest of the embryo nucleus were selected by Hochest overlay. Mean gray values of two random background regions were selected, averaged and subtracted from the ROI mean gray value to rule out the background noise. Pairwise T-test was conducted to assess the significance of the difference of IF intensity level.

Embryo transfer experiment

The animal work was done on a private farm under IACUC protocol 2018–0058. Ultrafast cooled blastocysts were thawed, re-expanded, and selected for transfer into recipient animals seven days after the onset of natural estrus, as determined by visual heat detection. The recipients were around 2-year-old, non-lactating Holstein housed in freestall barns at the farm designated for embryo transfer procedures. The recipients were palpated, and the presence of a Corpus Luteum (CL) was confirmed on the day of embryo transfer. The embryos were transferred into the cranial 1/3 of the uterine horn ipsilateral to the CL using a deep chamber embryo transfer gun for 0.25 mL straws with a plastic tip side delivery embryo transfer sheath (WTA Technologies, 5552 Raymond Stotzer Pkwy. Ste 124, College Station, TX 77,845). Pregnancy was assessed 26 days post-transfer (corresponding to day 33 of gestation) via transrectal ultrasonography.

X-ray diffraction observation of ice formation in bovine blastocysts

Bovine blastocysts soaked in 0.5 M sucrose and between 50 and 100% of the standard concentration of penetrating cryoprotectants used in vitrification solutions (15% v/v ethylene glycol, 15% v/v DMSO) were prepared in two groups. In the first, blastocysts were placed on thin (90 um thick) CryoTops, adapted to fit into crystallography bases, and plunge cooled by hand in LN2 according to standard protocols. In the second, blastocysts were placed on thin-film crystallography supports (MicroLoops or MicroMounts, 150 µm apertures) held in crystallography bases and plunge cooled in LN2 using an automated instrument developed for cryocrystallography (NANUQ, MiTeGen). As discussed elsewhere, cold samples were transferred to cryocrystallography sample cassettes (UniPucks) and the cassettes transferred to cryogenic containers for storage and transport to the X-ray source. X-ray data collection was performed using beamline 7IDB2 at the Cornell High-Energy Synchrotron Source (CHESS). The cassettes were loaded into the beamline’s cryogenic automounter. Each sample was transferred to the beamline goniometer stage, where it was maintained in a vitrified state by a T = 100 K N2 gas cryostream, centered in the X-ray beam, and oriented with the plane of support perpendicular to the beam. X-ray data collection was automated. First, with the sample at T = 100 K, 10 frames (images of the X-ray diffraction patterns) with exposures of 0.1 s per frame were recorded by the X-ray detector. These frames characterized ice formed during cooling and present in the cold sample and also verified that the sample was stationary in the cold gas stream. At the end of the 10th frame the cold gas stream was blocked using an air blade and, simultaneously, a stream of room temperature (20–25 °C) N2 gas was directed at the sample to warm it. As the sample warmed, 200 frames with exposure times of 0.01 s per frame (2 s total) were collected. Concurrent with x-ray data collection, optical images of the sample were acquired at 100 fps, matching the x-ray frame rate. The room temperature gas reached the sample ~ 20 ms after the switch from cooling to warming gas flows. Sample warming times were estimated from the evolution of ice diffraction patterns and from optical images of the sample as described previously8.
