Estimating stationary mass, frequency by frequency


Abstract
Suppose we observe a trajectory of length n n from an exponentially α \alpha -mixing stochastic process over a finite but potentially large state space. We consider the problem of estimating the probability mass placed by the stationary distribution of any such process on elements that occur with a certain frequency in the observed sequence. We estimate this vector of probabilities in total variation distance, showing universal consistency in n n and recovering known results for i.i.d. sequences as special cases. Our proposed methodology—implementable in linear time—carefully combines the plug-in (or empirical) estimator with a recently-proposed modification of the Good–Turing estimator called WingIt , which was originally developed for Markovian sequences. En route to controlling the error of our estimator, we develop new performance bounds on WingIt and the plug-in estimator for exponentially α \alpha -mixing stochastic processes. Importantly, the extensively used method of Poissonization can no longer be applied in our non i.i.d. setting, and so we develop complementary tools—including concentration inequalities for a natural self-normalized statistic of mixing sequences—that may prove independently useful in the design and analysis of estimators for related problems. Simulation studies corroborate our theoretical findings.

Estimating the stationary distribution of a stochastic process from an

Other estimators that see appealing empirical performance are absolute discounting

While the aforementioned papers have provided a detailed understanding of the i.i.d. setting, many real-world stochastic processes exhibit temporal dependencies. Examples abound in natural language processing, where sequences of words are sometimes better captured through hidden Markov modeling, and genomics, where DNA sequences are formed from interdependent base pairs. These dependencies in the stochastic process pose significant challenges for estimation of the stationary distribution. Indeed, the naive application of estimators such as Good–Turing is no longer valid even when the stochastic process is Markovian
(
Chandra et al., 2024
)
and different algorithmic tools are required for addressing temporal dependence
(
Hao et al., 2018

Skorski, 2020
;
Chandra et al., 2024

Pananjady et al. (2024)

windowed Good–Turing

Some extensions were also provided for estimation of the “small-count” probability, i.e., the mass placed by the distribution on elements occurring
at most
a certain number of times in the observed sequence. However, these results do not imply a satisfactory bound on distribution estimation. In particular, we still do not have an estimator for the stationary distribution in a frequency-by-frequency sense that estimates the vector of probabilities
(M^{\pi}_{\zeta})_{\zeta=0}^{n}
consistently in any natural divergence measure.
Finally, we mention that besides frequency-by-frequency stationary distribution estimation, researchers have studied various learning problems involving other parameters of Markov chains
(
Hao et al., 2018
;
Han et al., 2023
;
Wolfer and Kontorovich, 2019

.

In this paper, we propose and theoretically analyze an estimator for the stationary distribution in a frequency-by-frequency sense. Specifically, we consider a stochastic process

Background and problem formulation

As mentioned before, we are interested in estimating the stationary distribution of a mixing stochastic process
X^{n}
. In this section, we formally set up the assumptions on our stochastic process as well as the metric in which we wish to perform estimation.

2.1
Model for the stochastic process

We assume that the sequence
X^{n}:=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
is an ergodic stochastic process defined over a finite (and possibly unknown) state space
\mathcal{X}
. The unique stationary distribution of this ergodic process is denoted by
\pi
and we assume that the process is initialized at stationarity
2
2

2

This assumption can easily be relaxed by allowing for a burn-in period.
with
X_{1}\sim\pi
.

We also assume that the stochastic process is exponentially
\alpha
-mixing, which ensures that the dependencies between lagged past and future observations in the process decay exponentially with the time lag.
Formally, for an ergodic stochastic process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
, define its
\alpha
-mixing coefficient as

\displaystyle\alpha(\tau)\coloneqq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\;\sup\big\{|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)|:A\in\sigma(X_{t}^{-}),B\in\sigma(X_{t+\tau}^{+})\big\},

(1a)

where at time
t
, we use
\sigma(X_{t}^{-})
to denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the past RVs
(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{t})
, and
\sigma(X_{t+\tau}^{+})
to denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the future RVs
(X_{t+\tau},X_{t+\tau+1},\ldots)
.
Then, the stochastic process is said to satisfy
exponential
\alpha
-mixing
if there exist constants
\mu>0
and
\rho\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\alpha(\tau)\leq\mu\rho^{\tau}\quad\text{for all }\tau\geq 1.

(1b)

We also define the mixing time of such a process to level
\epsilon\in(0,1)
as

\displaystyle\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon)\coloneqq\min\{\tau\in\mathbb{N}:\alpha(\tau)\leq\epsilon\}.

(2)

Note that for an exponentially mixing process satisfying Eq. (
1b
), we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon)\leq\frac{\log(\mu/\epsilon)}{\log(1/\rho)}.

(3)

The assumption of exponential
\alpha
-mixing is satisfied by a wide range of stochastic processes, making it a versatile and practical modeling choice. Below, we discuss some examples:

1.

I.I.D. sequences:
IID sequences are special cases of
\alpha
-mixing sequences. Since all samples are i.i.d., we have from Eq. (
1a
) that
\alpha(\tau)=0
for all
\tau\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, from the definition of mixing time in Eq. (
2
) we obtain
\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon)=1
for all
\epsilon>0
.

2.

Finite-State Ergodic Markov Chains:

Any ergodic Markov chain on a finite state space satisfies the exponential
\alpha
-mixing condition (
1b
). In particular, the parameters
(\mu,\rho)
depend on the size of the state space and the spectral gap of the Markov transition matrix
(
Levin and Peres, 2017
, Theorem 4.9)
.

3.

Hidden Markov Models (HMMs):

In HMMs, the observations depend on a latent Markov chain. When the latent Markov chain
(H_{t})_{t\geq 1}
is exponentially
\alpha
-mixing and the observed process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 1}
is some measurable function
f_{t}
of
H_{t}
and another exponentially mixing Markov process
W_{t}
, then
X_{t}=f_{t}(H_{t},W_{t})
is also exponentially
\alpha
-mixing
(
Doukhan, 2012
)
.

4.

Random duplication model:

Suppose i.i.d. samples from
\pi
are input to a random duplication model
(
Chandra and Thangaraj, 2024
)
,
which for each input
x
, outputs the duplicated sequence
(\underbrace{x,\ldots,x}_{k\text{ times}})
with probability
\alpha
, and
x
otherwise.
The output process is ergodic and satisfies exponentially
\alpha
-mixing, and the mixing time satisfies
\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon)=k
for all
\epsilon>0
.

2.2
Frequency-by-frequency estimation of the stationary distribution

The total variation (TV) distance between two probability mass functions
p
and
q
defined on a common probability space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F})
is given by

d_{\mathsf{TV}}(p,q)=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}}|p(A)-q(A)|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|p_{x}-q_{x}|,

where we have used the shorthand
p_{x}=p(\{x\})
and
q_{x}=q(\{x\})
.

Recall that our goal is to estimate the vector of probabilities
(M^{\pi}_{\zeta})_{\zeta=0}^{n}
consistently in total variation, and thereby the stationary distribution
\pi
.
Let us set up this problem formally. For each
\zeta=0,1,\ldots,n
, define

\displaystyle M^{\pi}_{\zeta}(X^{n}):=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\pi_{x}\cdot{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}=\zeta\right\}}

(4)

as the mass placed by
\pi
on elements occurring exactly
\zeta
times in the observed sequence
X^{n}
.
Note that each scalar
M^{\pi}_{\zeta}(X^{n})
is a random functional, since it depends both on the underlying distribution and on the realized sequence
X^{n}
. Denote the vector of count probabilities by

\displaystyle M^{\pi}(X^{n})=(M^{\pi}_{0}(X^{n}),M^{\pi}_{1}(X^{n}),\ldots,M^{\pi}_{n}(X^{n})),

(5)

which is a random vector taking values in the probability simplex
\Delta(\{0,1,\ldots,n\})
.

Our goal is to develop an estimator of the random vector
M^{\pi}(X^{n})
as a functional of only the observed sequence
X^{n}
. In particular, we seek to design an estimator
\widehat{M}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\Delta(\{0,1,\ldots,n\})
and measure its error using total variation distance. Specifically, define the risk

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}(M^{\pi},\widehat{M}):={\mathbb{E}}\left[d_{\mathsf{TV}}(M^{\pi}(X^{n}),\widehat{M}(X^{n}))\right],

(6)

where the expectation is taken over
X^{n}
and any additional randomness in the estimator.

While the problem of estimating the vector
M^{\pi}
is interesting in itself, it is worth pausing to ask in what sense this yields an estimator for the stationary distribution
\pi
. To describe this, we recall the notion of a
natural
estimator due to
Orlitsky and Suresh (2015)
, which is an estimator of
\pi
that assigns the same probability to all symbols appearing with the same frequency in
X^{n}
. Formally, define the set of all natural estimators

\mathcal{Q}^{\mathsf{nat}}:=\{q:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\Delta(\mathcal{X})\mid q_{x}=q_{y}\text{ if }N_{x}(X^{n})=N_{y}(X^{n})\}.

Note that natural estimators are indeed intuitive, since if we know only that a sequence is exponentially
\alpha
-mixing and therefore reaches stationarity, then it is
natural
to assign the same probability to symbols that occur an equal number of times.
In particular, any estimator
\widehat{M}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\Delta(\{0,1,\ldots,n\})
of the count probability vector can be used to generate an estimator
\widehat{q}
for the stationary distribution by dividing the mass
\widehat{M}_{\zeta}
equally among all elements of
\mathcal{X}
that occur
\zeta
times in
X^{n}
.
The estimator
\widehat{q}
is natural by definition. The following lemma shows that this estimator is competitive with respect to the class of natural estimators
\mathcal{Q}^{\mathsf{nat}}
.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose
\widehat{q}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\Delta(\mathcal{X})
is generated from an estimator
\widehat{M}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\Delta(\{0,1,\ldots,n\})
by splitting the mass
\widehat{M}_{\zeta}
equally among all elements that occur exactly
\zeta
times in the sequence
X^{n}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\pi,\widehat{q}(X^{n}))\leq 2\cdot\inf_{q\in\mathcal{Q}^{\mathsf{nat}}}d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\pi,q)+d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\widehat{M}(X^{n}),M^{\pi}(X^{n})).

(7)

Note that the infimum on the RHS is taken over all natural estimators, including those that have perfect knowledge of
\pi
but are constrained to assign the same probability to symbols appearing the same number of times in
X^{n}
. We thus have an
oracle inequality
with approximation factor
2
, and estimating the vector of count probabilities automatically yields a good estimator of the stationary distribution. As an aside, we note that such a competitive relation was proved for the KL-divergence by
Orlitsky and Suresh (2015)
, who showed an approximation constant of
1
instead of as above. A corresponding result for the TV distance has not appeared before (to our knowledge).

3
Methodology

We now turn our attention to the estimation problem set up above. Our distribution estimation algorithm is based on a careful combination of the
WingIt
estimator
(
Pananjady et al., 2024
)
for low-frequency symbols and the plug-in estimator for high-frequency symbols. Let us begin by describing these two estimators.

3.1
WingIt
Estimator

The
WingIt
estimator generalizes the Good-Turing estimator’s leave-one-out technique to provide a “leave-a-window-out” framework for estimation with dependent data.
To describe this framework, we define some additional notation. For each index
i\in[n]
in the sequence, define the following index sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{i}=\{k\in[n]:|k-i|<\tau\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{I}_{i}=[n]\setminus\mathcal{D}_{i}.

(8)

For a suitable choice of
\tau
, the set of indices
\mathcal{D}_{i}
represents the “dependent set”, in that the random variables
\{X_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{D}_{i}}
may depend strongly on the random variable
X_{i}
. Conversely,
\mathcal{I}_{i}
represents the “independent set”: If
\tau
is chosen large enough and the stochastic process is mixing, then we expect the random variables indexed by the set
\mathcal{I}_{i}
to be approximately independent of
X_{i}
.
With this notation in hand, we define the
exact-count
estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{M}_{\tau,\zeta}^{(i)}:={\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{X_{i}}(X_{\mathcal{I}_{i}})=\zeta\right\}},\;\;\text{ and the averaged estimator }\;\;\widehat{M}_{\textsc{WingIt},\zeta}(\tau)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{M}_{\tau,\zeta}^{(i)},

(9)

where
\widehat{M}_{\textsc{WingIt},\zeta}(\tau)
is the total probability mass assigned to symbols appearing
\zeta
times in the sample by the
WingIt
estimator. Note that substituting
\tau=1
yields the Good–Turing estimator. We note that the definitions of the exact-count
WingIt
estimators in our work differ slightly from those in
Pananjady et al. (2024)
. Specifically, while
Pananjady et al. (2024)
were concerned with estimating the
small-count
probability given by
M^{\pi}_{\leq\zeta}=\sum_{s=0}^{\zeta}M^{\pi}_{s}
, which corresponds to the total mass of elements appearing up to
\zeta
times, our focus is on estimating the exact count probabilities
M^{\pi}_{\zeta}
, which are objects that are more directly useful for distribution estimation.

3.2
Plug-in estimator

Recall that we denote by
\varphi_{\zeta}
the number of symbols appearing
\zeta
times in
X^{n}
. Then, the plug-in (or empirical) estimator assigns the following total mass to all symbols with multiplicity
\zeta
:

\displaystyle\widehat{M}_{\textsc{PI},\zeta}=\varphi_{\zeta}\cdot\frac{\zeta}{n}.

(10)

Clearly, the plug-in estimator (simultaneously for all values of
\zeta
) is linear-time implementable.

When a particular symbol occurs sufficiently many times, the law of large numbers

(
)

guarantees that the relative frequency of the symbol in the sample
X^{n}
provides a reliable estimate of its stationary probability.
Therefore, we expect this estimator to be accurate for sufficiently large frequencies
\zeta
. Conversely, when
\zeta
is small, the plug-in estimator
\widehat{M}_{\textsc{PI},\zeta}
will typically underestimate
M^{\pi}_{\zeta}
.

3.3
Combined estimator

To leverage the complementary strengths of the
WingIt
estimator (which we expect to be inaccurate for large
\zeta
) and the plug-in estimator (which we expect to be inaccurate when
\zeta
is small), we define a hybrid estimator that switches between the
WingIt
estimator and the plug-in estimator based on the symbol multiplicity
\zeta
. Specifically, our estimator takes the following form:

\displaystyle\widehat{M}_{\zeta}(\tau;\overline{\zeta})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\nu^{-1}\cdot\widehat{M}_{\textsc{WingIt},\zeta}(\tau)\quad&\text{ if }\zeta\leq\overline{\zeta}\\
\nu^{-1}\cdot\widehat{M}_{\textsc{PI},\zeta}&\text{ if }\zeta>\overline{\zeta},\end{cases}

(11)

where
\overline{\zeta}\in\mathbb{N}
is some parameter to be chosen, and
\nu
is chosen to be a normalizing constant to ensure that
\sum_{\zeta=0}^{n}\widehat{M}_{\zeta}(\tau)=1
.
Note that
\overline{\zeta}
determines the
transition point
, or the threshold for transitioning from the
WingIt
to the plug-in estimator, and we make a specific choice of
\overline{\zeta}
in stating our theorem to follow. When
\overline{\zeta}
and
\tau
are clear from context, we often use the shorthand
\widehat{M}_{\zeta}\coloneqq\widehat{M}_{\zeta}(\tau;\overline{\zeta})
to denote the scalar estimate and
\widehat{M}\coloneqq(\widehat{M}_{\zeta}(\tau;\overline{\zeta}))_{\zeta=0}^{n}
to denote its vector counterpart.

The combined estimator can be implemented in linear time using the linear time implementation of the plug-in estimator and that of the
WingIt
estimator in Algorithm
1
.

4
Main results

Our main result is a bound on the TV error attained by our hybrid estimator
\widehat{M}(\tau;\overline{\zeta})
.

Theorem 1
.

There exists a universal positive constant
C
such that if we choose the window size
\tau\geq\mathsf{t_{mix}}(n^{-5})
and transition point
\overline{\zeta}=\lfloor n^{1/3}\rfloor-1
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}(M^{\pi},\widehat{M}(\tau;\overline{\zeta}))\leq C\cdot\left(\frac{\sqrt{\tau\log\left(Cn\right)}}{n^{1/6}}\right).

A few remarks are in order. First, in the special case of an i.i.d. sequence we have
\mathsf{t_{mix}}(n^{-5})=1
,
so we can set
\tau=1
and our proposed estimator reduces to the combination of the Good–Turing and plug-in estimators studied in
Acharya et al. (2013)
. In this case, Theorem
1
recovers the TV guarantee of
Acharya et al. (2013)
, but without requiring Poissonization (see the discussion below).

Second, note that by the discussion in Section
2.1
, we can always choose the window size
\tau=\log(n^{5}\mu)/\log(1/\rho)
in a general mixing stochastic process satisfying Eq. (
1b
). This parameter depends logarithmically on
n
and also depends on the mixing parameters
\mu
and
\rho
, and yields the bound (ignoring
\log\log
factors)

{\mathbb{E}}_{X^{n}}\left[d_{\mathsf{TV}}(M^{\pi}(X^{n}),\widehat{M}(\tau;\overline{\zeta}))\right]\lesssim\left(\frac{\log^{3}(n^{5}\mu)/\log^{3}(1/\rho)}{n}\right)^{1/6}

uniformly over all exponentially
\alpha
-mixing stochastic processes satisfying Eq. (
1b
).

Third, we highlight that our proof technique necessarily departs from the ones in
Acharya et al. (2013)
and
Orlitsky and Suresh (2015)
, which rely on Poissonization. Poissonization essentially translates a sequence of fixed length into a sequence where the total number of samples itself is an independent Poisson random variable, which in turn renders the multiplicity of each individual symbol independent.
While powerful, Poissonization requires independent random variables in the original sequence, and can no longer be used in our setting.
Instead, our techniques to bound the error of the
WingIt
estimator are based on the leave-a-window-out interpretation of the estimator, and careful control of its error in terms of appropriately defined indicator random variables.
To handle the error of the plug-in estimator, we use a careful blocking technique
and prove a self-normalized concentration inequality for mixing sequences — see
Lemma
2
. Our control on the error of both estimators is in terms of fine-grained quantities — see Propositions
1
and
2
.

As a side remark, we note that
Acharya et al. (2013)
and
Orlitsky and Suresh (2015)
also analyze the error of their estimator in KL-divergence, and show that it achieves a rate of
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/3})
for IID sequences.
By applying the reverse Pinsker inequality
(
Sason, 2015
, Theorem 1)
to Theorem
1
above, one can show that a minor modification of our estimator

0

\zeta

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}((\tau^{3}/n)^{1/6})

As mentioned above, the proof of our main result relies on a concentration inequality for a self-normalized statistic of exponentially
\alpha

Lemma 2
.

Fix
\delta,\epsilon>0
and let
U^{n}\coloneqq(U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{n})
denote a sequence of random variables from an ergodic, stochastic process that is exponentially
\alpha
-mixing with parameters
\mu
and
\rho
, with each
U_{j}\in[0,B]
almost surely.
Recall the definition of mixing time from Eq. (
2
).

(a) For each fixed
\tau\geq\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon/n)
, the following Bernstein-type inequality holds:

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}}[U_{1}]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{4\tau v^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{4B\tau\log(1/\delta)}{3n}

with probability at least
1-4\delta-\epsilon
. Here
v^{2}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\mathsf{var}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}U_{j}\right)
is the normalized block variance.

(b) In addition, if
\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}\geq 36B\log(2/\delta)\cdot\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon/n)
and
n\geq 24\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon/n)
, then the following holds:

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}}[U_{1}]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}\right|\leq 82\cdot\frac{\sqrt{B\log(2/\delta)\cdot(\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j})\cdot\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon/n)}}{n}

with probability at least
1-10\delta-2\epsilon
.

On the one hand, it is worth comparing the Bernstein bound in Lemma
2
(a) to the Bernstein bound for exponentially
\alpha
-mixing sequences given in
Merlevède et al. (2009, Theorem 2)
. The authors in that paper show that if
U^{n}
is
\alpha
-mixing and satisfies
\alpha(\tau)\leq\exp(-2c\tau)
, then defining

r^{2}:=\sup_{i}\left(\mathsf{var}(U_{i})+2\sum_{j>i}|\cov(U_{i},U_{j})|\right),

we have the tail bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|{\mathbb{E}}[U_{1}]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}\right|>t\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{Cnt^{2}}{r^{2}+\frac{B^{2}}{n}+B\log(n)^{2}t}\right),

(12)

where
C
is an unspecified constant that depends on the parameter
c
defined in assuming a bound on
\alpha(\tau)
.
By comparison, Lemma
2
(a) makes the weaker
\alpha
-mixing assumption (
1a
) and implies the following tail bound in terms of the mixing time:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|{\mathbb{E}}[U_{1}]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}\right|>t\right)\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}}{4r^{2}+\frac{4\tau B}{3}t}\right)+\epsilon.

(13)

In stating Eq. (
13
), we have used the fact that
\tau v^{2}\leq r^{2}
, which is evident from the following sequence of calculations:

\displaystyle v^{2}=\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\mathsf{var}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}U_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mathsf{var}(U_{i})+2\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\sum_{j>i}^{\tau}\cov(U_{i},U_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tau}{\tau^{2}}\sup_{i}\left(\mathsf{var}(U_{i})+2\sum_{j>i}|\cov(U_{i},U_{j})|\right).

Comparing Eqs. (
13
) and (
12
), we see that our bound is stated explicitly in terms of mixing times and may be more user-friendly under the mixing assumption (
1a
).

On the other hand, Lemma
2
(b) should be viewed as a concentration inequality on a self-normalized statistic constructed from a mixing sequence — note that the constants
(24,36,82)
appearing in the theorem are not sharp and can likely be improved. A consequence of this result — stated in a form that is typical in this literature
(
de la Peña et al., 2004
)
— is that for an exponentially
\alpha
-mixing sequence
U^{n}
satisfying Eq. (
1a
) with
U_{j}\in[0,B]
a.s. for all
j\in[n]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\left|{\mathbb{E}}[U_{1}]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}\right|}{\sqrt{\frac{B\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}}{n}}}>82\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)\cdot\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon/n)}{n}}\text{ and }\frac{B\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}}{n}>\frac{36B^{2}\log(2/\delta)\cdot\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon/n)}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq 10\delta+2\epsilon.

Such a self-normalized concentration inequality for exponentially
\alpha
-mixing sequences may be of independent interest.

We conclude this section with the two key propositions that we prove, which provide bounds—for each
\zeta
—on the error made by both the plug-in and
WingIt
estimators in approximating
M^{\pi}_{\zeta}
.

Proposition 1
.

Consider any fixed
\delta,\epsilon>0
and let
\tau_{0}:=\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon/n^{2})
for convenience. There is a universal positive constant
C
such that if
n\geq 24\tau_{0}
, then for each

\zeta\geq\max\Big\{36\tau_{0}\log(22n/\delta),1+\sqrt{(4+8\tau_{0}\log(11n/\delta)}+\frac{4\tau_{0}\log(11n/\delta)}{3}\Big\},

we have

\displaystyle\left|M^{\pi}_{\zeta}-\widehat{M}_{\textsc{PI},\zeta}\right|\leq C\left(\frac{\sqrt{\zeta\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon/n^{2})}\cdot\varphi_{\zeta}(X^{n})}{n}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{Cn}{\delta}\right)}\right)

with probability at least
1-\delta-3\epsilon
.

Proposition 2
.

There exists a universal positive constant
C
such that the following holds for all
\zeta
.
If the window size is chosen such that
\tau\geq\mathsf{t_{mix}}(n^{-2})
, then we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|M_{\zeta}^{\pi}(X^{n})-\widehat{M}_{\textsc{WingIt},\zeta}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{n}}\Bigg(\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}[M^{\pi}_{\zeta}]}+\sqrt{\zeta\log(2\tau){\mathbb{E}}[M^{\pi}_{\zeta}]}+\sqrt{\sum_{u=1}^{4\tau-2}\frac{(\zeta+u)}{u}{\mathbb{E}}\left[M^{\pi}_{\zeta+u}\right]}\Bigg)+C\frac{(\zeta+1)\tau}{n}.

It is worth comparing Proposition
2
to the analogous bound for missing mass with
\zeta=0

(
Pananjady et al., 2024
)
.
Substituting
\zeta=0
in the above bound and ignoring the final (lower order) term, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|M_{0}^{\pi}(X^{n})-\widehat{M}_{\textsc{WingIt},0}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{n}}\left(\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}[M^{\pi}_{0}]}+\sqrt{\sum_{u=1}^{4\tau-2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[M^{\pi}_{u}\right]}\right).

Because
\sum_{u=1}^{4\tau-2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[M^{\pi}_{u}\right]\leq 1
, this recovers the minimax rate of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\tau/n})
given by
Chandra et al. (2022)
.
Moreover, the bound above is strictly stronger, since the RHS can be significantly smaller when the quantities
\{\mathbb{E}[M^{\pi}_{u}]\}_{u=0}^{4\tau-2}
are small. Note that these are the expected count probability masses for the stationary distribution
\pi
— if the small count mass of the stationary distribution is small (i.e. most symbols appear many times), then our bound shows that
WingIt
incurs a smaller error for missing mass estimation. This adaptivity is a distinct advantage of our approach.
In contrast, the
\mathsf{MSE}
bound provided in
Pananjady et al. (2024)
captures the correct scaling in terms of
\tau
and
n
, but is not adaptive to this specific behavior of the stationary distribution
\pi
.
This adaptivity property plays a central role in our final bound in Theorem
1
, since the errors incurred across all the
\zeta
values are aggregated to produce the claimed bound on TV error.

5
Numerical experiments

In this section, we provide a set of simulations on synthetically constructed Markov chains in order to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed estimator. We first describe the experimental set-up and discuss some of the estimators used in practice. We then compare the performance of these estimators against the proposed hybrid estimator.

5.1
Experimental Setup

We perform experiments on samples that we generate from different types of
\alpha
-mixing processes. Specifically, we instantiate the stationary distribution of the mixing sequence from one of five different distributions. These distributions include: the uniform distribution, a step distribution with half of the symbols occurring with probability
1/(2|\mathcal{X}|)
and the other half of symbols occurring with probability
3/(2|\mathcal{X}|)
, a Dirichlet distribution with the concentration parameter
1.5
, and Zipf distributions with parameters
1.1
and
1.5
. The Zipf distribution with parameter
a
assigns the probability
p(i)\propto i^{-a}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. We then layer a
random duplication model
on the i.i.d. samples generated from the stationary distribution to obtain the final
\alpha
-mixing sequence. This process is described below. For notational convenience, we define
\mathsf{T_{mix}}\coloneqq\mathsf{t_{mix}}(1/4)
, where
\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon)
is defined in Eq. (
2
).

1.

First, we generate
n_{0}
i.i.d. samples from the underlying stationary distribution. The number of symbols is set as
|\mathcal{X}|=n_{0}
. Thus, as we increase the number of samples, the number of symbols increases proportionally.

2.

We then use a geometric duplication model in order to generate a mixing sequence from the i.i.d. samples. Specifically, for each sample in the i.i.d. sequence, we draw a geometric random variable with mean
n_{0}^{c}
(for some
c>0
), which determines how many times that symbol is duplicated in the final sequence. This processcan be viewed as a draw from a sticky Markov chain
(
Chandra et al., 2022
)
in which the probability of observing an i.i.d. sample from the stationary distribution is given by
1/n_{0}^{c}
. The mixing time,
\mathsf{T_{mix}}
, for this sequence is given by
\mathsf{T_{mix}}=\mathcal{O}(n_{0}^{c})

(
Pananjady et al., 2024
)
. Since the duplication factors are random, the total length of the resulting sequence, denoted as
n
, is also a random variable with expected value
\mathbb{E}[n]=n_{0}^{c+1}
.

We compare the performance of our proposed estimator with several existing estimators that were originally designed for i.i.d. sequences. Specifically, we include the Good–Turing + Plug-in estimator analyzed by
Acharya et al. (2013)
, as well as a family of popular add-
\beta
estimators defined as:

\displaystyle\widehat{M}_{\zeta}(\beta)=\frac{\varphi_{\zeta}(\zeta+\beta(\zeta))}{\nu},

where
\nu
is a normalizing constant chosen to ensure that the estimated probabilities sum to
1
. Different choices of the function
\beta(\zeta)
yield well-known estimators:

•

Laplace estimator

(
Laplace, 1814
)
:
\beta(\zeta)=1
for all
\zeta
,

•

Krichevsky–Trofimov estimator

(
Krichevsky and Trofimov, 1981
)
:
\beta(\zeta)=0.5
for all
\zeta
,

•

Braess–Sauer estimator

(
Braess and Sauer, 2004
)
:
\beta(0)=0.5
,
\beta(1)=1
, and
\beta(\zeta)=0.75
for all
\zeta>1
.

An important detail is that the normalization constant

for the add-

estimators depends on the alphabet size

. For the uniform, step, and Dirichlet distributions, the alphabet size is finite and set to

\widehat{q}

Let

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\pi,\widehat{q}\right)\leq d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\pi,q^{\pi})+d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\widehat{q},q^{\pi}).

(15)

We also have the following:

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\pi,q^{\pi})

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\pi,q^{*})+d_{\mathsf{TV}}(q^{*},q^{\pi})\overset{{\sf(i)}}{=}d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\pi,q^{*})+d_{\mathsf{TV}}(M^{*}(X^{n}),M^{\pi}(X^{n})),

in the appendix, since both

are natural estimators.

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(M^{\pi}(X^{n}),M^{*}(X^{n})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u=0}^{n}|M^{\pi}_{u}(X^{n})-M^{*}_{u}(X^{n})|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u=0}^{n}\left|\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}=u\right\}}(\pi_{x}-q^{*}_{x})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u=0}^{n}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}=u\right\}}\left|\pi_{x}-q^{*}_{x}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\left|\pi_{x}-q^{*}_{x}\right|\sum_{u=0}^{n}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}=u\right\}}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{{\sf(i)}}{=}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left|\pi_{x}-q^{*}_{x}\right|=d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\pi,q^{*}),

where step
{\sf(i)}
follows because

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\pi,\widehat{q})\leq 2\cdot d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\pi,q^{*})+d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\widehat{q},q^{\pi})\overset{{\sf(i)}}{=}2\cdot d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\pi,q^{*})+d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\widehat{M},M^{\pi}),

where step
{\sf(i)}

Applying Proposition

, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\zeta=0}^{\overline{\zeta}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|M_{\zeta}^{\pi}(X^{n})-\widehat{M}_{\textsc{WingIt},\zeta}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\zeta=0}^{\overline{\zeta}}\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{n}}\left(\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}[M^{\pi}_{\zeta}]}+\sqrt{\zeta\log(2\tau){\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}=\zeta\right\}}\right]}+\sqrt{\sum_{u=1}^{4\tau-2}\frac{(\zeta+u)}{u}{\mathbb{E}}\left[M^{\pi}_{\zeta+u}\right]}\right)+\sum_{\zeta=0}^{\overline{\zeta}}\frac{(\zeta+1)\tau}{n}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{n}(\overline{\zeta}+1)}+\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{n}}\underbrace{\sum_{\zeta=0}^{\overline{\zeta}}\sqrt{\zeta\log(2\tau){\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}=\zeta\right\}}\right]}}_{E_{1}}+\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{n}}\underbrace{\sum_{\zeta=0}^{\overline{\zeta}}\sqrt{\sum_{u=1}^{4\tau-2}\frac{(\zeta+u)}{u}{\mathbb{E}}\left[M^{\pi}_{\zeta+u}\right]}}_{E_{2}}+\frac{(\overline{\zeta}+1)^{2}\tau}{n}.

The first term in the above inequality is lower order compared to
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
. Thus we now focus on providing the bounds for

and
E_{2}
separately. We have

\displaystyle E_{1}=\sum_{\zeta=0}^{\overline{\zeta}}\sqrt{\zeta\log(2\tau){\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}=\zeta\right\}}\right]}\overset{{\sf(i)}}{\leq}(\overline{\zeta}+1)\sqrt{\log(2\tau)},

where step
{\sf(i)}
follows because the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact
\sum_{\zeta=0}^{\overline{\zeta}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}=\zeta\right\}}\right]\leq 1
.
For the term
E_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\zeta=0}^{\overline{\zeta}}\sqrt{\sum_{u=1}^{4\tau-2}\frac{(\zeta+u)}{u}{\mathbb{E}}\left[M^{\pi}_{\zeta+u}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\zeta=0}^{\overline{\zeta}}\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{u=1}^{4\tau-2}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}=\zeta+u\right\}}\frac{(\zeta+u)}{u}\right]}

\displaystyle\overset{{\sf(i)}}{\leq}\sqrt{\sum_{\zeta=0}^{\overline{\zeta}}1}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{\zeta=0}^{\overline{\zeta}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{u=1}^{4\tau-2}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}=\zeta+u\right\}}\frac{(\zeta+u)}{u}\right]}

where step
{\sf(i)}
follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Continuing, we have

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\overline{\zeta}+1}\cdot\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{u=1}^{4\tau-2}(1/u)\sum_{\zeta=0}^{\overline{\zeta}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}=\zeta+u\right\}}(\zeta+u)\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\overline{\zeta}+1}\cdot\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{u=1}^{4\tau-2}\frac{(\overline{\zeta}+u)}{u}\right]}\leq\sqrt{(\overline{\zeta}+1)(\overline{\zeta}+4\tau-2)\log(4\tau)}.

Putting together the pieces, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\zeta=0}^{\overline{\zeta}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|M_{\zeta}^{\pi}(X^{n})-\widehat{M}_{\textsc{WingIt},\zeta}\right|\right]\lesssim(\overline{\zeta}+1)\sqrt{\frac{\tau\log(2\tau)}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{n}}\sqrt{(\overline{\zeta}+1)(\overline{\zeta}+4\tau-2)\log(4\tau)}+\frac{(\overline{\zeta}+1)^{2}\tau}{n}.

In the statement of Theorem
1
, we set the value
\overline{\zeta}+1=n^{1/3}
. Since we have assumed that
n^{1/3}\gtrsim\tau\log(Cn)
, we can write
(\overline{\zeta}+4\tau-2)\lesssim\overline{\zeta}+1
and substituting above yields

\displaystyle\sum_{\zeta=0}^{\overline{\zeta}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|M_{\zeta}^{\pi}(X^{n})-\widehat{M}_{\textsc{WingIt},\zeta}\right|\right]\lesssim(\overline{\zeta}+1)\sqrt{\frac{\tau\log(4\tau)}{n}}+\frac{(\overline{\zeta}+1)^{2}\tau}{n}.

Bounding plug-in error:

For bounding the error of the plug-in estimator one thing to note is that we use this estimator only for
\zeta\geq\overline{\zeta}+1=n^{1/3}
. Applying the statement of Proposition
1
, the following holds for each
\zeta\geq\overline{\zeta}+1
provided that
\tau\geq\mathsf{t_{mix}}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{n^{2}}\right)
:

\displaystyle\left|M^{\pi}_{\zeta}(X^{n})-\widehat{M}_{\textsc{PI},\zeta}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\tau\zeta}\cdot\varphi_{\zeta}(X^{n})}{n}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{Cn}{\delta}\right)}.

probability at least
1-\delta-3\epsilon
.

Taking the union bound of the above inequality for all
\zeta\geq\overline{\zeta}+1
we have

\displaystyle\left|M^{\pi}_{\zeta}(X^{n})-\widehat{M}_{\textsc{PI},\zeta}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\tau\zeta}\cdot\varphi_{\zeta}(X^{n})}{n}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{Cn}{\delta}\right)}.

probability at least
1-n\delta-3n\epsilon
. Substituting the above inequality into the error for the plug-in estimator we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\zeta=\overline{\zeta}+1}^{n}\left|M^{\pi}_{\zeta}(X^{n})-\widehat{M}_{\textsc{PI},\zeta}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\zeta=\overline{\zeta}+1}^{n}\frac{\sqrt{\tau\zeta}\cdot\varphi_{\zeta}(X^{n})}{n}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{Cn}{\delta}\right)}

\displaystyle\overset{{\sf(i)}}{\leq}\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{\overline{\zeta}+1}}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{Cn}{\delta}\right)},

probability at least
1-n\delta-3n\epsilon
. Above, step
{\sf(i)}
follows from Eq. (
16
). Substituting
\epsilon^{\prime}=3n\epsilon
and
\delta^{\prime}=n\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\zeta=\overline{\zeta}+1}^{n}\left|M^{\pi}_{\zeta}(X^{n})-\widehat{M}_{\textsc{PI},\zeta}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{\overline{\zeta}+1}}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{Cn^{2}}{\delta^{\prime}}\right)},

with probability at least
1-\delta^{\prime}-\epsilon^{\prime}
.
Choosing
\epsilon^{\prime}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}
and then integrating the tail bound we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\zeta=\overline{\zeta}+1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left|M^{\pi}_{\zeta}(X^{n})-\widehat{M}_{\textsc{PI},\zeta}\right|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{\overline{\zeta}+1}}\sqrt{\log\left(Cn^{2}\right)}.

(17)

Recall that Proposition
1
requires
\tau\geq\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon/n^{2})
. We set
\epsilon=\epsilon^{\prime}/3n
and finally substitute
\epsilon^{\prime}=1/n^{2}
. Thus we require the condition
\tau\geq\mathsf{t_{mix}}(1/n^{5})
.

Combining the pieces:

Combining the two bounds, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[d_{\mathsf{TV}}(M^{\pi}(X^{n}),\widehat{M}(\tau))\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim(\overline{\zeta}+1)\sqrt{\frac{\tau\log(4\tau)}{n}}+\frac{(\overline{\zeta}+1)^{2}\tau}{n}+\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{\overline{\zeta}+1}}\sqrt{\log\left(Cn^{2}\right)}.

Note that we have set
\overline{\zeta}+1=n^{1/3}
and also that
n\geq C^{\prime}\tau^{3}\log^{3}(Cn)
for a large enough constant
C^{\prime}
. This allows us to simplify the above expression and obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[d_{\mathsf{TV}}(M^{\pi}(X^{n}),\widehat{M}(\tau))\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\tau\log(Cn)}}{n^{1/6}},

as claimed.
We conclude by proving claim (
16
).

Proof of claim (
16
):

First, note that
n=\sum_{\zeta=0}^{n}\zeta\varphi_{\zeta}
. Part (a) of the inequality then follows by noting that

\displaystyle n\geq\sum_{\zeta=\overline{\zeta}+1}^{n}\zeta\varphi_{\zeta}\geq(\overline{\zeta}+1)\sum_{\zeta=\overline{\zeta}+1}^{n}\varphi_{\zeta}.

(18)

To prove part (b), we use the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\overline{\zeta}+1}^{n}\varphi_{\zeta}\sqrt{\zeta}=\sum_{\zeta=\overline{\zeta}+1}^{n}\sqrt{\varphi_{\zeta}}\sqrt{\zeta\varphi_{\zeta}}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{\zeta=\overline{\zeta}+1}^{n}\varphi_{\zeta}}\sqrt{\sum_{\zeta=\overline{\zeta}+1}^{n}\zeta\varphi_{\zeta}}.

Using Eq. (
18

\displaystyle\sum_{\zeta=\overline{\zeta}+1}^{n}\varphi_{\zeta}\sqrt{\zeta}\leq\sqrt{\frac{n}{\overline{\zeta}+1}}\cdot\sqrt{n}=\frac{n}{\sqrt{\overline{\zeta}+1}},

which completes the proof of the claim and therefore the proof of Theorem

and the true count probability mass
M^{\pi}_{\zeta}
for sufficiently large values of count
\zeta
. Also recall the shorthand notation
\tau_{0}=\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon/n^{2})
. We denote by
\mathcal{X}_{\zeta}
the set of all symbols appearing exactly
\zeta
times in
X^{n}
and by
\mathcal{X}_{\geq\zeta}
the set of all symbols appearing at least
\zeta
times in
X^{n}
. Clearly, we have
\mathcal{X}_{\zeta}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{\geq\zeta}
. Using this fact and the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left|M^{\pi}_{\zeta}-\widehat{M}_{\textsc{PI},\zeta}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|M^{\pi}_{\zeta}-\frac{\zeta\varphi_{\zeta}}{n}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}(X^{n})=\zeta\right\}}\left(\pi_{x}-\frac{\zeta}{n}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}(X^{n})=\zeta\right\}}\left|\pi_{x}-\frac{N_{x}(X^{n})}{n}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\geq\zeta}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}(X^{n})=\zeta\right\}}\left|\pi_{x}-\frac{N_{x}(X^{n})}{n}\right|.

(29)

At this juncture, one possible approach is to prove a high-probability bound on
the deviation term
\left|\pi_{x}-\frac{N_{x}(X^{n})}{n}\right|
for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and then take a union bound over the state space. However, this would induce a dependence on
|\mathcal{X}|
and the resulting bound would no longer be universal in
n
. Consequently, we reduce the sum over
\mathcal{X}_{\geq\zeta}
to the sum over a deterministic set of size at most
\mathcal{O}(n)
using the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

Recall that
\tau_{0}=\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon/n^{2})
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists x\in\mathcal{X}:N_{x}(X^{n})\geq 1+\sqrt{(4+8\tau_{0})\log(n/\delta)}+\frac{4\tau_{0}\log(n/\delta)}{3}\text{ and }\pi_{x}\leq 1/n\right)\leq\delta+\epsilon.

Lemma
3
is proved in Section
6.4.1
.
Let us also define the deterministic set

\mathcal{Y}\coloneqq\Bigg\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\pi_{x}\geq\frac{1}{n}\Bigg\}.

By Lemma
3
, we have that for any
\zeta\geq 1+\sqrt{(4+8\tau_{0})\log(n/\delta)}+\frac{4\tau_{0}\log(n/\delta)}{3}
, the inclusion

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\geq\zeta}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}\text{ holds with probability at least }1-\delta-\epsilon.

(30)

Next, we observe that
N_{x}(X^{n})
can be written as follows:

\displaystyle N_{x}(X^{n})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{I}\left\{X_{j}=x\right\}},

which takes the form of a sum of indicator random variables over the sequence
X^{n}
.
Since
\mathcal{Y}
contains at most
n
(nonrandom) elements, we may apply the union bound over these elements in conjunction with Lemma
2
.
This implies that the following event occurs
uniformly
over all
x\in\mathcal{Y}
with probability at least
1-10\delta-2\epsilon
(note that we defined
\tau_{0}=\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon/n^{2})
):

\displaystyle\left|\pi_{x}-\frac{N_{x}(X^{n})}{n}\right|\lesssim\begin{cases}\frac{\sqrt{\tau_{0}N_{x}(X^{n})\log(2n/\delta)}}{n}\quad&\text{ if }N_{x}(X^{n})\geq 36\tau_{0}\log(2n/\delta)\\
\sqrt{\frac{4\pi_{x}(1+2\tau_{0})\log(n/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{4\tau_{0}\log(n/\delta)}{3n}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(31)

We make particular use of the first case in the proof — the second case is stated for completeness. Working now on the intersection of the high-probability events in (
30
) and (
31
), which in turn occurs with probability at least
1-11\delta-3\epsilon
, and considering

\zeta\geq\max\left\{36\tau_{0}\log(2n/\delta),1+\sqrt{(4+8\tau_{0})\log(n/\delta)}+\frac{4\tau_{0}\log(n/\delta)}{3}\right\},

we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\geq\zeta}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}(X^{n})=\zeta\right\}}\left|\pi_{x}-\frac{N_{x}(X^{n})}{n}\right|

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\geq\zeta}\cap\mathcal{Y}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}(X^{n})=\zeta\right\}}\left|\pi_{x}-\frac{N_{x}(X^{n})}{n}\right|+\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\geq\zeta}\cap\mathcal{Y}^{c}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}(X^{n})=\zeta\right\}}\left|\pi_{x}-\frac{N_{x}(X^{n})}{n}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\overset{{\sf(i)}}{=}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\geq\zeta}\cap\mathcal{Y}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}(X^{n})=\zeta\right\}}\left|\pi_{x}-\frac{N_{x}(X^{n})}{n}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\overset{{\sf(ii)}}{\lesssim}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\geq\zeta}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}(X^{n})=\zeta\right\}}\frac{\sqrt{\tau_{0}N_{x}(X^{n})\log(2n/\delta)}}{n}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}(X^{n})=\zeta\right\}}\frac{\sqrt{\tau_{0}N_{x}(X^{n})\log(2n/\delta)}}{n},

where step
{\sf(i)}
follows by Eq. (
30
) and step
{\sf(ii)}
by Eq. (
31
).

Now, using the fact that

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}(X^{n})=\zeta\right\}}\sqrt{N_{x}(X^{n})}=\varphi_{\zeta}(X^{n})\sqrt{\zeta}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\left|M^{\pi}_{\zeta}-\widehat{M}_{\textsc{PI},\zeta}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\tau_{0}}}{n}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{2n}{\delta}\right)}\varphi_{\zeta}(X^{n})\sqrt{\zeta}

(32)

on the same event occurring with probability at least
1-11\delta-3\epsilon
. Adjusting constants in the definitions of
\delta
and
\epsilon
and substituting the value of
\tau_{0}
yields the statement of the proposition.
∎

6.4.1
Proof of Lemma
3

In order to prove this lemma, we start by carefully partitioning the state space. We number the elements in
\mathcal{X}
as
1,\ldots,|\mathcal{X}|
and assume without loss of generality that they are ordered such that
\pi_{1}\leq\pi_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\pi_{|\mathcal{X}|}
. Let us define
j^{*}+1\coloneqq\min\{i:\pi_{i}>1/n\}
. Also define the cumulative probabilities
c_{i}=\sum_{m=1}^{i}\pi_{i}
, and let
i_{k}:=\min\{i:c_{i}\geq k/n\}
denote a sequence of
n+1
indices with
i_{0}\coloneqq 0
.
Now, partition the first
j^{*}
indices into disjoint sets

T_{1},\ldots,T_{L}
given by

\displaystyle T_{\ell}=\{i_{\ell-1}+1,\ldots,i_{\ell}\},

(33)

where the set is considered to be empty if
i_{\ell}=i_{\ell-1}
, and
L
is defined such that
i_{L}=j^{*}
.
We overload notation and denote by
\pi_{T_{\ell}}
the total stationary mass of all symbols appearing in the partition
T_{\ell}
, i.e.
\pi_{T_{\ell}}:=\sum_{x\in T_{\ell}}\pi_{x}
.
Note by the definition of
\{i_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
and
\{T_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
that each block
T_{\ell}
contains elements whose stationary probabilities sum up to at most
1/n
. In other words, we have
\pi_{T_{\ell}}\leq 1/n
for all
\ell\in[L]
. Thus, we have

\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\pi_{T_{\ell}}\leq\frac{L}{n}\leq 1,

which implies that
L\leq n
.

Next, we define

\displaystyle N_{T_{\ell}}(X^{n})\coloneqq\sum_{x\in T_{\ell}}N_{x}(X^{n}).

We also set
\zeta_{0}:=1+\sqrt{(4+8\tau_{0})\log(n/\delta)}+\frac{4\tau_{0}\log(n/\delta)}{3}
, where we defined
\tau_{0}:=\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon/n^{2})
in the statement of Lemma
3
. By construction of the partitions
\{T_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists x:N_{x}(X^{n})\geq\zeta_{0}\text{ and }\pi_{x}\leq 1/n\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\exists x\in\cup_{\ell=1}^{L}T_{\ell}:N_{x}(X^{n})\geq\zeta_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\ell\in[L]:N_{T_{\ell}}(X^{n})\geq\zeta_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbb{P}\left(N_{T_{\ell}}(X^{n})\geq\zeta_{0}\right).

(34)

It remains to characterize each of the terms
\mathbb{P}\left(N_{T_{\ell}}(X^{n})\geq\zeta_{0}\right)
, for which we will use the statement of Bernstein’s inequality from Lemma
2
(a).
Consider the random variable

B_{i}={\mathbb{I}\left\{X_{i}\in T_{\ell}\right\}}-\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}\in T_{\ell}\right).

We note that
|B_{i}|\leq 1
and
{\mathbb{E}}[B_{i}]=0
. Applying the Bernstein inequality from Lemma
2
(a) — in its tail bound form — to the process
(B_{1},\ldots,B_{n})
with
\tau_{0}=\mathsf{t_{mix}}(\epsilon/n^{2})
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}\geq t\right)\leq 4\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{4n\tau_{0}v^{2}+\frac{4\tau_{0}}{3}t}\right)+\frac{\epsilon}{n}

(35)

for any
t>0
.
Recall from the statement of the lemma that
v^{2}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\tau_{0}^{2}}\mathsf{var}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\tau_{0}}B_{j}\right)=\frac{1}{\tau_{0}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{\tau_{0}}\mathsf{var}\left(B_{j}\right)+\frac{2}{\tau_{0}^{2}}\sum_{j>i}^{\tau_{0}}\cov(B_{i},B_{j})
.
We have

\displaystyle\mathsf{var}(B_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X_{i}\in T_{\ell})-\mathbb{P}(X_{i}\in T_{\ell})^{2}\leq 1/n\text{ and }

\displaystyle\sum_{j>i}^{\tau_{0}}\cov(B_{i},B_{j})

\displaystyle\overset{{\sf(i)}}{\leq}\sum_{j>i}^{\tau_{0}}\sqrt{\mathsf{var}(B_{i})\mathsf{var}(B_{j})}\leq\frac{\tau^{2}_{0}}{n},

where step
{\sf(i)}
follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
This directly yields

v^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n\tau_{0}}+\frac{2}{n}\implies 4n\tau_{0}v^{2}\leq 4(1+2\tau_{0}).

Continuing from Eq. (
35
) and substituting the definition of the random variables
\{B_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{I}\left\{X_{i}\in T_{\ell}\right\}}\geq t+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}\in T_{\ell}\right)\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{4+8\tau_{0}+\frac{4\tau_{0}}{3}t}\right)+\frac{\epsilon}{n}.

Next we note that, because the sets
\{T_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
are disjoint, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\{X_{i}\in T_{\ell}\}\leq 1
.
From this, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(N_{T_{\ell}}(X^{n})\geq t+1\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{4+8\tau_{0}+\frac{4\tau_{0}}{3}t}\right)+\frac{\epsilon}{n}.

Substituting
t=\sqrt{(4+8\tau_{0})\log(n/\delta)}+\frac{4\tau_{0}\log(n/\delta)}{3}
in the above yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N_{T_{\ell}}(X^{n})\geq 1+\sqrt{(4+8\tau_{0})\log(n/\delta)}+\frac{4\tau_{0}\log(n/\delta)}{3}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(N_{T_{\ell}}(X^{n})\geq\zeta_{0}\right)\leq\frac{\delta+\epsilon}{n}.

(36)

Combining Ineqs. (
36
) and (
34
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists x:N_{x}(X^{n})\geq\zeta_{0}\text{ and }\pi_{x}\leq 1/n\right)\leq L\left(\frac{\delta+\epsilon}{n}\right)\overset{{\sf(i)}}{\leq}\delta+\epsilon,

where step
{\sf(i)}
follows because we established that
L\leq n
.
This completes the proof of the lemma. ∎

6.5
Proof of Proposition
2

In this section, we bound the error of the
WingIt
estimator for small frequencies. We first derive bounds on the mean squared error (
\mathsf{MSE}
) for each
\zeta
, and then use these to obtain bounds on the
\ell_{1}
error.
Throughout this proof, note that
\tau
should be thought of as an arbitrary integer that divides
n
, with
n_{0}=n/(2\tau)
.

As in
Pananjady et al. (2024)
, we can relate the
\mathsf{MSE}
of the
WingIt
estimator to a “skipped” version as below:

\displaystyle\mathsf{MSE}\left(M_{\zeta}^{\pi}(X^{n}),\widehat{M}_{\textsc{WingIt},\zeta}(\tau)\right)\leq\frac{1}{2\tau}\sum_{\ell=0}^{2\tau-1}{\mathbb{E}}\left(M_{\zeta}^{\pi}(X^{n})-\widehat{M}_{\textsc{WingIt},\zeta}(\tau;\ell)\right)^{2},

(37)

where

\displaystyle\widehat{M}_{\textsc{WingIt},\zeta}(\tau;\ell):=\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}\widehat{M}_{\tau,\zeta}^{(2\tau j-\ell)}\text{ for each }\ell=0,\ldots,2\tau-1.

(38)

\widehat{M}_{\textsc{WingIt},\zeta}(\tau;\ell)
represents the skipped estimator used in our analysis.

We now focus on bounding the term
{\mathbb{E}}\left(M_{\zeta}^{\pi}(X^{n})-\widehat{M}_{\textsc{WingIt},\zeta}(\tau;0)\right)^{2}
. An
\mathsf{MSE}
bound proven for
\ell=0
can be identically transferred to bound the
\mathsf{MSE}
of
\widehat{M}_{\textsc{WingIt},\zeta}(\tau;\ell)
for any
\ell=0,\ldots,2\tau-1

(
Pananjady et al., 2024
, Proposition 5)
. Thus, without loss of generality, we address the case
\ell=0
and analyze the estimator
\widehat{M}_{\textsc{WingIt}}(\tau;0)
. Consequently, we use the shorthand
\mathcal{B}_{j}:=\mathcal{B}_{j,0}
and
\mathcal{H}_{j}:=\mathcal{H}_{j,0}
. For more details and a pictorial representation of the skipped estimators and the corresponding “dependent” and “independent” sets, refer to
Pananjady et al. (2024, Section 7.1)
.

Adding and subtracting the term
{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\sim\pi\\
Y\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}X^{n}\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}})=\zeta\right\}}
to the quantity of interest given by
\frac{1}{2}|\widehat{M}_{\textsc{WingIt},\zeta}(\tau;0)-M^{\pi}_{\zeta}(X^{n})|^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left|\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{X_{2\tau j}}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}})=\zeta\right\}}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\sim\pi\\
Y\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}X^{n}\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X^{n})=\zeta\right\}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\underbrace{\left|\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}\left({\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\sim\pi\\
Y\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}X^{n}\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}})=\zeta\right\}}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\sim\pi\\
Y\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}X^{n}\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X^{n})=\zeta\right\}}\right)\right|^{2}}_{T_{1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\underbrace{\left|\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}\left({\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{X_{2\tau j}}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}})=\zeta\right\}}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\sim\pi\\
Y\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}X^{n}\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}})=\zeta\right\}}\right)\right|^{2}}_{T_{2}}.

The rest of the proof proceeds by bounding
{\mathbb{E}}[T_{1}]
and
{\mathbb{E}}[T_{2}]
.

Bounding
{\mathbb{E}}[T_{1}]
:

In order to bound
T_{1}
we define the random variable
P_{j}={\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}})=\zeta\right\}}-{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X^{n})=\zeta\right\}}
. We have

\displaystyle T_{1}=\frac{1}{n_{0}^{2}}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\end{subarray}}P_{j}\right|^{2}.

We obtain
P_{j}\leq{\mathbb{I}\left\{Y\in X_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\right\}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}})=\zeta\right\}}\leq 1
in a manner similar to the argument in
(
Pananjady et al., 2024
, Lemma 12)
. We briefly outline the argument here for completeness.
Since
P_{j}\in\{-1,0,1\}
and
{\mathbb{I}\left\{Y\in X_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\right\}}\cdot{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}})=\zeta\right\}}\in\{0,1\}
, the inequality is only non-trivial for the case where
P_{j}=1
.
In this case, we have
N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}})=\zeta\text{ and }N_{Y}(X^{n})\neq\zeta
, which implies that
Y\in X_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}
. Thus, we have
{\mathbb{I}\left\{Y\in X_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\right\}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}})=\zeta\right\}}=1
and so the above inequality holds.

Since the blocks
\{\mathcal{B}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{0}}
are non-overlapping, we have

\bigsqcup_{j^{\prime}\in[n_{0}]\setminus j}\mathcal{B}_{j^{\prime}}\subset\mathcal{H}_{j}.

Now, suppose that for some
j
we have
{\mathbb{I}\left\{Y\in X_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\right\}}\cdot{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}})=\zeta\right\}}=1
.
This means that
Y
occurs at least once in
\mathcal{B}_{j}
, but its number of occurrences outside of
\mathcal{B}_{j}
is equal to
\zeta
.
Then, we have

\sum_{j^{\prime}\in[n_{0}]\setminus j}{\mathbb{I}\left\{Y\in X_{\mathcal{B}_{j^{\prime}}}\right\}}\cdot{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j^{\prime}}})=\zeta\right\}}\leq\sum_{j^{\prime}\in[n_{0}]\setminus j}{\mathbb{I}\left\{Y\in X_{\mathcal{B}_{j^{\prime}}}\right\}}\leq N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}})=\zeta.

Putting these pieces together yields
\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}P_{j}\leq\zeta+1
point-wise.
Ultimately, this yields

\displaystyle T_{1}=\frac{1}{n_{0}^{2}}\left({\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}P_{j}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\frac{\zeta+1}{n_{0}}\right)^{2},

(39)

and so
{\mathbb{E}}[T_{1}]\leq\left(\frac{\tau(\zeta+1)}{n}\right)^{2}
.
This ultimately turns out to be a lower-order term and so this worst-case analysis suffices.

Bounding
{\mathbb{E}}[T_{2}]
:

The term
T_{2}
, which captures a certain conditional variance, will be the dominant term in the analysis. First, we have

\displaystyle T_{2}=\frac{1}{n_{0}^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{0}}Z_{j}Z_{k},

(40)

where we define

Z_{j}:={\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{X_{2j\tau}}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}})=\zeta\right\}}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\sim\pi\\
Y\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}X^{n}\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}})=\zeta\right\}}\text{ for all }j\in[n_{0}].

We thus have

\displaystyle T_{2}=\frac{1}{n_{0}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}Z_{j}^{2}+\frac{1}{n_{0}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n_{0}}Z_{j}Z_{k}.

We first bound the cross terms in
T_{2}
. We define the following random variables for each
j,k\in[n_{0}]
with
j\neq k
and conditioned on a random variable
Y\sim\pi
:

\displaystyle Q_{j,k}\coloneqq{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}})=\zeta\right\}}-{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{k}})=\zeta\right\}}.

(41)

Observe that
Q_{j,k}\in[-1,1]
point-wise and so
|Q_{j,k}|\in[0,1]
point-wise. The following lemma relates the terms
Z_{j}Z_{k}
and
|Q_{j,k}|+|Q_{k,j}|
in expectation.

Lemma 4
.

For each
j\neq k
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{j}Z_{k}]\leq 4{\mathbb{E}}[|Q_{j,k}|]+4{\mathbb{E}}[|Q_{k,j}|]+12\mu\rho^{\tau},

where
Q_{j,k}
is defined in Eq. (
41
).

We take Lemma
4
as given for the moment and prove it in Section
6.5.1
.
Further, auxiliary Lemma
10
yields the following point-wise bound on
|Q_{j,k}|
for any
Y\in\mathcal{X}
:

\displaystyle|Q_{j,k}|\leq{\mathbb{I}\left\{Y\in X_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\right\}}\cdot\left({\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{k}})=\zeta\right\}}+{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}})=\zeta\right\}}\right).

Define the shorthand

\displaystyle R_{Y}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n_{0}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{Y\in X_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\right\}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{k}})=\zeta\right\}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n_{0}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{Y\in X_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\right\}}\cdot{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}})=\zeta\right\}}.

(42)

Combining Lemma
4
and Eq. (
42
), we obtain the following bound for the cross terms in
T_{2}
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{0}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n_{0}}{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{j}Z_{k}]\overset{(i)}{\leq}\frac{8}{n_{0}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}[R_{Y}]+12\mu\rho^{\tau}.

It remains to provide a satisfactory bound for
{\mathbb{E}}[R_{Y}]
, which is the technical crux of our analysis.
The following lemma provides an adaptive, point-wise bound on
R_{Y}
.

Lemma 5
.

Consider the quantity
R_{Y}
defined in Eq. (
42
).
For all
Y\in\mathcal{X}

7
Discussion

We have provided a flexible estimator and analysis of the vector of count probabilities
M^{\pi}(X^{n})
(and therefore, stationary distribution estimation) of any stationary exponentially
\alpha
-mixing stochastic process.
An explicit construction for the IID case
(
Acharya et al., 2013
)
reveals that our estimation error rate
is sharp in its dependence on
n
; we conjecture that the dependence on
\mathsf{t_{mix}}
is tight for our estimator, but showing this remains open. While we have obtained high-probability bounds on the error of the plug-in estimator, our bounds on the
WingIt
are in expectation — obtaining corresponding high-probability bounds would be an interesting follow-up problem.
Finally, our approach heavily uses the fact that TV decomposes as an
\ell_{1}
norm of frequency-by-frequency errors; obtaining corresponding analysis for the KL-divergence is an intriguing direction for future work.

More broadly, we believe that the de-correlation devices introduced through our design and analysis of the
WingIt
and plug-in estimators for sequences of random variables may find applications in related problems, such as stochastic optimization
(
Mou et al., 2024
;
Li et al., 2023
)
, uncertainty quantification with dependent data
(
Xu and Xie, 2023
;
Agrawal and Maguluri, 2024
)
, and property testing problems for stochastic processes
(
Beran, 1992
;
Kalai and Vempala, 2024
)
.

A.1
Lemmas related to the TV distance

The following lemma relates the TV distance of two natural estimators/distributions to the TV distance between their count probability masses.

Lemma 7
.

Let us denote by
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
any two natural distributions over
\mathcal{X}
. For a given sequence
X^{n}
, denote their vector of count probability masses as
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
respectively. Then, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}(R_{1},R_{2})=d_{\mathsf{TV}}(M_{1},M_{2}).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}(R_{1},R_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left|R_{1}(x)-R_{2}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\overset{{\sf(i)}}{=}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{u=0}^{n}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}=u\right\}}\frac{\left|M_{u,1}-M_{u,2}\right|}{\varphi_{u}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u=0}^{n}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}=u\right\}}\frac{\left|M_{u,1}-M_{u,2}\right|}{\varphi_{u}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u=0}^{n}\left|M_{u,1}-M_{u,2}\right|=d_{\mathsf{TV}}(M_{1},M_{2}),

where step
{\sf(i)}
uses fact that both
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are natural distributions.
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

The following lemma relates the total variation (TV) distance between the normalized and unnormalized versions of the vector of count probabilities for our estimator from
M^{\pi}
. This result allows us to simplify our analysis by exclusively working with the unnormalized estimator in all our proofs.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\widehat{M}^{\textsc{u}}
denote a vector of nonnegative entries indexed by
\zeta=\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
, and define
\nu\coloneqq\sum_{\zeta=0}^{n}\widehat{M}^{\textsc{u}}_{\zeta}>0
. Define the normalized estimator
\widehat{M}=\widehat{M}^{\textsc{u}}/\nu
as a vector on the simplex
\Delta(\{0,1,\ldots,n\})
. Let
M^{\pi}
be the vector of count probability masses of the stationary distribution
\pi
. Then the following holds:

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\widehat{M},M^{\pi})\leq\|\widehat{M}^{\textsc{u}}-M^{\pi}\|_{1}.

Proof.

Using the definition
\nu\coloneqq\sum_{\zeta=0}^{n}\widehat{M}^{\textsc{u}}_{\zeta}
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\widehat{M},M^{\pi})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\zeta=0}^{n}\left|\widehat{M}_{\zeta}-M^{\pi}_{\zeta}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\zeta=0}^{n}\left|\frac{\widehat{M}_{\zeta}^{\textsc{u}}}{\nu}-\widehat{M}_{\zeta}^{\textsc{u}}+\widehat{M}_{\zeta}^{\textsc{u}}-M^{\pi}_{\zeta}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\zeta=0}^{n}\widehat{M}_{\zeta}^{\textsc{u}}\left|\frac{1}{\nu}-1\right|+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\zeta=0}^{n}\left|\widehat{M}_{\zeta}^{\textsc{u}}-M^{\pi}_{\zeta}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{|\nu-1|}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\widehat{M}^{\textsc{u}}-M^{\pi}\|_{1}.

It remains to bound the term
|\nu-1|
, which we do below:

\displaystyle|\nu-1|=\left|\sum_{\zeta=0}^{n}(\widehat{M}_{\zeta}^{\textsc{u}}-M^{\pi}_{\zeta})\right|\leq\sum_{\zeta=0}^{n}|\widehat{M}_{\zeta}^{\textsc{u}}-M^{\pi}_{\zeta}|=\|\widehat{M}^{\textsc{u}}-M^{\pi}\|_{1}.

Putting together the pieces completes the proof.
∎

A.2
Lemmas concerning indicator random variables

In this section, we state and prove two simple lemmas concerning indicator random variables.

Lemma 9
.

For any symbol
x\in\mathcal{X}
, let
Q_{j,k}
be defined as in Eq. (
41
). Then, we have

\displaystyle Q_{j,k}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{I}\left\{x\in X_{\mathcal{H}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{j}}\right\}}\cdot{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}})=\zeta\right\}}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{I}\left\{x\in X_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\right\}}\cdot{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}})=\zeta\right\}}.

Proof.

The only case when this inequality can be violated is when
Q_{j,k}=1
and
{\mathbb{I}\left\{x\in X_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\right\}}\cdot{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}})=\zeta\right\}}=0
. In every other case the inequality is satisfied since
Q_{j,k}\in\{-1,0,1\}
and
{\mathbb{I}\left\{x\in X_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\right\}}\cdot{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{x}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}})=\zeta\right\}}\in\{0,1\}
. Let us thus analyze the case when the equality is violated and show that this cannot happen.
Note that
Q_{j,k}=1
implies

\displaystyle N_{x}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}})=\zeta\text{ and }N_{x}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{k}})\neq\zeta.

We can write the set
X_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
as the union of the disjoint sets
X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}}
and
X_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle N_{x}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{k}})=N_{x}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}})+N_{x}(X_{\mathcal{B}_{j}})=\zeta+N_{x}(X_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}).

Since
N_{x}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}})=\zeta
and
N_{x}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{k}})\neq\zeta
, we can be sure that the symbol
x\in X_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}
. This implies that
{\mathbb{I}\left\{x\in X_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\right\}}\cdot{\mathbb{I}\left\{N_{Y}(X_{\mathcal{H}_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}})=\zeta\right\}}=1
. Hence, the inequality given in the statement of the lemma is always satisfied.
∎

Lemma 10
.

For any fixed symbol
x\in\mathcal{X}
, let

be defined as in Eq. (

\displaystyle|Q_{j,k}|

, we have

.

A.3

Lemma 11
.

For each
i\in[n]

\displaystyle Z_{i}

\displaystyle=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{i-\tau},X_{i},X_{i+\tau},X_{i+\tau+1},\ldots,X_{n}),

X_{t}

\alpha
-mixing with parameters
\mu
and
\rho
(see Eq. (

.

f

|{\mathbb{E}}[f(Z_{i})-f(Z^{\prime}_{i})]|\leq 3\mu\cdot\rho^{\tau}.

We prove the bound on total variation, noting that the consequence for bounded functions

We have

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}(Z_{i},Z_{i}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sup_{S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{X}}\big|\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in S_{1},\ldots,X_{i-\tau}\in S_{i-\tau},X_{i}\in S_{i},X_{i+\tau}\in S_{i+\tau},\ldots,X_{n}\in S_{n})

Lemma 12
