New treatments in myositis on the horizon
Authors: Ingrid E Lundberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM), or briefly myositis, are a heterogeneous group of rare autoimmune diseases with high morbidity and mortality. Current treatment is based on high doses of glucocorticoids over long periods of time in combination with immunosuppressive drugs, with varying and often disappointing results. Therefore, there is a high unmet need for new therapies. Subgrouping patients with IIM based on clinical, histopathological and autoantibody profiles has facilitated identification of predominant molecular pathways, and this knowledge has been applied in recent successful clinical trials, and more trials are in the pipeline. This is exciting and promising news indicating possibilities to improve health and life for patients with IIM.

INTRODUCTION

The idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIMs), or briefly myositis, are a heterogeneous group of rare autoimmune diseases with skeletal muscle inflammation leading to muscle weakness as a shared clinical manifestation. Other organs are frequently involved, such as the skin, joints, lungs, gastrointestinal tract, and the heart [1]. IIM is a chronic condition with high morbidity and mortality. Based on differences in clinical and histopathological features of the muscles, the IIMs have for many years been subgrouped into dermatomyositis (DM), polymyositis, and inclusion body myositis (IBM); however, there are some diverging outcomes within these subgroups [1]. Autoantibodies are present in up to 80% of patients with IIM. Identification of autoantibodies that are specific for myositis, so-called myositis-specific autoantibodies (MSA), has helped us to identify new subgroups of IIM, including immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) and antisynthetase syndrome (ASyS) with more homogeneous clinical and histopathological features than the traditional subgroups. The new subgrouping of IIM has identified different molecular pathways dominating in different subgroups of IIM, and this information is now often reflected in the inclusion criteria for clinical trials including patients with IIM.

TREATMENT

Treatment of IIM is based on high doses of glucocorticoids over a long period of time in combination with immunosuppressive drugs. Few controlled trials have been undertaken, and treatment is based on case series and expert opinion [1]. Most often used first-line drugs are methotrexate, azathioprine, and mycophenolate mofetil, and as second-line treatment calcineurin inhibitors, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg), cyclophosphamide, and rituximab; however, with varying and often disappointing results. Thus, there is a high unmet need for new therapies. A special case is the subgroup IBM, where no effective pharmacological treatment is available. Therefore, this subgroup will not be subject to further discussion in this report.

Over the past few years, a number of clinical trials have been initiated for patients with IIM by pharmaceutical companies, and the first results are emerging. A crucial step in the development of clinical trials was the development of clinical outcome measures and a total improvement score (TIS), now often used as a primary outcome in clinical trials [2]. The TIS score is based on 6-item core set measures of disease activity; the patient’s and physician’s global assessment of disease activity using a visual analogue scale (VAS) score of 0 to 10; the rheumatoid arthritis (RA) health assessment questionnaire (HAQ); a test of muscle strength (manual muscle test in 8 muscle groups [MMT-8]), serum levels of muscle enzymes, eg, creatine phosphokinase (CK) levels; and an extra-muscular disease activity score on a VAS score of 0 to 10 [3]. The TIS score is calculated from the absolute % change for each variable, and improvement in the different domains gives different points. Maximum TIS score is 100 points. In consensus, experts involved in the development of the TIS score suggested a definition of minimal improvement if the TIS score reached 20, moderate improvement if the TIS score was ≥40, and major improvement if the TIS score was ≥60.

The first placebo-controlled trial in patients with IIM that reached its primary endpoint was with high-dose IVIg in refractory DM [4]. Over the past years, an improved knowledge of molecular pathogenesis has been achieved, importantly showing dominating pathways of the immune system in different subgroups of IIM. This knowledge has now been utilised in recently published and ongoing trials, with promising results. Important immune pathways that have been identified in patients with IIM include B cells, autoantibodies, T cells, the complement system, interferons (IFNs), and muscle fibre regeneration [5], opening possibilities for targeted therapies in comparison to previously often-used broad-spectrum immunosuppression with frequent unwanted side effects.

The first promising trials are targeting IFNs, where previous studies have demonstrated a high IFN signature in the blood and muscles of patients with DM and polymyositis compared with healthy controls [6]. Notably, a difference in IFN1/IFN2 pathways has been observed between subgroups of IIM [7]. Thus, type 1 IFN gene signature (IFNGS) (RNA sequencing [RNAseq]) was particularly high in patients with DM and moderate in patients with ASyS but low in patients with IMNM, whereas type 2 IFNGS was upregulated in both patients with DM and ASyS but with lower levels than type 1 IFNGS [7]. A role for the IFN system in DM was also supported by the positive results using an IFN beta-specific monoclonal antibody, dazukibart, which resulted in improved skin score, cutaneous dermatomyositis disease area and severity index (CDASI) score, in a double-blind randomised controlled trial (RCT) phase 2 trial. A phase 3 multicentre double-blind placebo RCT is ongoing in patients with active DM or polymyositis with muscle involvement. Case series including patients with DM have reported beneficial effects of anifrolumab, a type 1 IFN receptor antagonist approved for severe systemic lupus erythematosus [8]. A multicentre, double-blind, phase 3 study is ongoing in patients with DM and polymyositis (including ASyS) (NCT06455449).

Tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2) and Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) mediate signal transduction of cytokines, including types 1 and 2 IFN, which provide a therapeutic rationale for treatment with JAK and TYK inhibitors in patients with DM. This is supported by the positive results from a case series using the JAK inhibitor tofacitinib [9] and the recently published RCT using brepocitinib, a selective TYK2/JAK1 inhibitor in patients with refractory DM, the VALOR (Study to Investigate the Efficacy and Safety of Brepocitinib in Adults with Dermatomyositis) study [10]. This is the largest controlled trial in patients with IIM so far, including 241 patients with DM with active skin and muscle disease. The primary endpoint was improvement of the TIS at 52 weeks, which was significantly higher in the arm treated with brepocitinib 30 mg/d (TIS score, 46.5), thus reaching the definition of “moderate improvement” compared with placebo (TIS score, 31.2, corresponding to “minimal improvement”). Another clinically relevant outcome was that improvement in the brepocitinib 30 mg arm was also recorded after reducing the glucocorticoid dose to <2.5 mg per day or to complete cessation. The VALOR study is a successful example of precision medicine targeting types 1 and 2 IFNs in DM associated with increased IFN signatures in muscle and skin, thereby providing additional support for a role of these IFNs in the pathophysiology of DM.

Another promising treatment approach is the use of compounds targeting the neonatal Fc gamma receptor; for example, efgartigimod PH20, a human immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) antibody Fc fragment that binds the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn). In the RCT ALKIVIA (Efgartigimod PH20 SC in Adults With Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy: A Phase 2 Randomized, Placebo-Controlled Trial), including active, refractory IIM, a statistically significantly higher TIS score was reached after 24 weeks in the active treatment arm with subcutaneous efgartigimod PH20 (TIS score, 50.45) compared with placebo (TIS score, 35.65) (P < .0004) [11]. A phase 3 trial is ongoing.

Other exciting novel therapies are the CD19 chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapies. Very promising results have been reported from case reports and basket trials using this approach to deplete B cells in patients with antisynthetase syndrome, IMNM, and DM [12]. Several trials are ongoing, including patients with autoantibody-positive IIM using autologous or allogeneic CAR T cells. More results can be expected within the coming years. The promising results so far using this novel treatment give hope to reach a cure in these severe diseases.

Yet another exciting novel treatment targeting specific immune cells is the use of bispecific antibodies, or T cell engagers (TCE). A case series including 5 patients with the IIM subgroup anti-Jo1-positive antisynthetase syndrome (ASyS) using the CD19xCD3 TCE blinatumomab was recently reported with promising results [13]. Induction therapy with blinatumomab induced a temporary improvement of disease activity in muscle and lungs and decreased autoantibody titers, but prolonged disease control was achieved when followed by maintenance therapy with rituximab. Adverse events included cytokine release syndrome (CRS) up to grade 3 in 2 of 5 patients with ASyS and respiratory infections. Thus, blinatumomab may offer potential as a rescue therapy for patients with treatment-refractory ASyS.

In conclusion, we are currently in an exciting era with the rapid development of new possible therapies for patients with IIM. This advancement has been possible due to the improved knowledge of molecular pathways in different subgroups of IIM and is an example of successful precision medicine. For the future, we expect more trials with positive results leading to more and better therapies for patients with IIM. In the ideal world, we want to identify the right treatment for the individual patient in the early disease phase, but for this we need more research, including identification of prognostic biomarkers.
