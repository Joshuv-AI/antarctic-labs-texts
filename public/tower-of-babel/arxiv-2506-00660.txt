Differential Privacy for Deep Learning in Medicine
Marziyeh Mohammadi, Mohsen Vejdanihemmat, Mahshad Lotfinia, Mirabela Rusu, Daniel Truhn, Andreas Maier, Soroosh Tayebi Arasteh

Abstract
Differential privacy (DP) is a key technique for protecting sensitive patient data in medical deep learning (DL). As clinical models grow more data-dependent, balancing privacy with utility and fairness has become a critical challenge. This scoping review synthesizes recent developments in applying DP to medical DL, with a particular focus on DP-SGD and alternative mechanisms across centralized and federated settings. Using a structured search strategy, we identified 74 studies published up to March 2025. Our analysis spans diverse data modalities, training setups, and downstream tasks, and highlights the tradeoffs between privacy guarantees, model accuracy, and subgroup fairness. We find that while DP—especially at strong privacy budgets—can preserve performance in well-structured imaging tasks, severe degradation often occurs under strict privacy, particularly in underrepresented or complex modalities. Furthermore, privacy-induced performance gaps disproportionately affect demographic subgroups, with fairness impacts varying by data type and task. A small subset of studies explicitly addresses these tradeoffs through subgroup analysis or fairness metrics, but most omit them entirely. Beyond DP-SGD, emerging approaches leverage alternative mechanisms, generative models, and hybrid federated designs, though reporting remains inconsistent. We conclude by outlining key gaps in fairness auditing, standardization, and evaluation protocols, offering guidance for future work toward equitable and clinically robust privacy-preserving DL systems in medicine.



1
Introduction

Deep learning (DL) has become a cornerstone of modern medical artificial intelligence (AI), driving advances in diagnostic imaging, survival prediction, biosignal analysis, and electronic health record (EHR) modeling
Rajpurkar et al. 2022
;
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2024
;
Haug and Drazen 2023
. However, the deployment of DL models in clinical settings raises critical concerns regarding patient privacy
Kaissis et al. 2021
. Medical data are inherently sensitive, and DL models trained on such data are vulnerable to various privacy attacks—including membership inference, model inversion, and identity disclosure—potentially exposing the presence or characteristics of individual patients in the training set
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2024
;
Usynin et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Haim et al. 2022
;
Carlini et al. 2023
.

Differential privacy (DP)
Dwork and Roth
provides formal guarantees to protect individuals within a dataset by bounding the influence of any single record on the model’s output. In DL, this is commonly implemented via differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD)
Abadi et al. 2016
, which injects noise into clipped gradients during training (Fig.
1
). Despite its theoretical strength, practical deployment of DP in healthcare DL introduces key trade-offs: noise reduces model utility and may disproportionately affect underrepresented subgroups, potentially reinforcing existing healthcare inequities
Tran et al. 2021
;
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2023
;
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2024
.

To better understand the state of DP in clinical DL, we conduct a comprehensive scoping review of empirical studies that apply DP mechanisms in medical contexts. While several prior reviews address DP broadly or focus on applications in generic domains, no existing synthesis systematically evaluates how DP interacts with clinical data, model design, privacy-utility trade-offs, fairness impacts, or adversarial robustness.

To guide our review, we investigate the following research questions:

RQ1:

To what extent do medical DL models leak sensitive information, and under what conditions are privacy breaches most likely?

RQ2:

How effectively do DP-based methods mitigate membership inference and other privacy attacks in healthcare applications?

RQ3:

What are the empirical impacts of DP on model performance, fairness, and clinical applicability in real-world scenarios?

This scoping review aims to critically synthesize current knowledge, identify key challenges and opportunities, and provide practical guidance for future research and clinical practice involving privacy-preserving DL models in medicine.
In the following sections, we first review the applications of DP across clinical DL tasks, covering data modalities, training settings, and privacy accounting methods. We then analyze how architectural choices, normalization strategies, and pretraining affect the privacy-utility tradeoff, with a focus on model performance across varying privacy budgets. Next, we examine fairness impacts, highlighting subgroup disparities, the metrics used to evaluate them, and gaps in current practices. We also survey alternative privacy mechanisms beyond DP-SGD, including federated learning (FL), local perturbation, and hybrid methods. Finally, we synthesize empirical findings on privacy attacks and defenses, before concluding with a discussion of key limitations, open challenges, and recommendations for building robust, equitable, and privacy-preserving AI systems in medicine.

(a)

2
Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines
Tricco et al. 2018
;
Page et al. 2021
to ensure methodological rigor and transparency
Buess et al. 2025
. The complete PRISMA-ScR checklist is provided in Supplementary Table 1. The methodological approach to data collection, article selection, and results synthesis is described in detail below.

2.1
Eligibility Criteria

Any study was eligible if it was published on or before March 1, 2025, and explicitly focused on empirical applications of DP in conjunction with DL methods for healthcare-related tasks. Eligible articles included original peer-reviewed journal papers, conference proceedings, and impactful preprints in English. Studies purely theoretical in nature or without explicit medical or clinical applications were excluded. Review articles, editorials, and commentaries were also excluded to focus exclusively on primary empirical contributions.

2.2
Information Sources

We systematically searched four electronic databases: PubMed, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, and Web of Science. Database queries were performed on March 1, 2025, and search results were imported into Rayyan software
Ouzzani et al. 2016
, a tool designed to facilitate the systematic screening of literature and duplicate removal. A detailed description of the database-specific search queries is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

2.3
Search Strategy

The search strategy was carefully designed to identify publications at the intersection of DP, DL, and medical applications. Specifically, the search strings combined three primary keyword groups: (1) differential privacy techniques (e.g., “differential privacy,” “differentially private,” “DP-SGD”), (2) deep learning and related machine learning methodologies (e.g., “deep learning,” “machine learning,” “neural network,” “artificial intelligence”), and (3) healthcare and clinical applications (e.g., “medical,” “clinical,” “healthcare,” “biomedical,” “EHR,” “imaging,” “radiology,” “text,” “speech”).

Each database query was adapted to database-specific indexing conventions to maximize search sensitivity. The comprehensive queries and exact search strings used, along with the number of articles retrieved from each database, are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Additionally, manual searches were conducted by screening reference lists from key articles and reviewing recent preprints and related studies to capture any relevant works missed by the database searches.

2.4
Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The literature selection involved multiple screening steps: (1) Initial screening included automated duplicate removal and manual title and abstract screening in Rayyan. Articles were excluded if they lacked relevance to DP or healthcare applications. (2) Full-text reviews were conducted on the remaining articles to ensure alignment with inclusion criteria, specifically the empirical application of DP with DL techniques in medical contexts. Articles with purely theoretical content, lacking empirical evaluation, or applied to non-healthcare domains were excluded. The selection process ensured proportional representation across different medical application domains, including medical imaging, electronic health records, predictive analytics, and personalized medicine.

Synthesis of Results

3
Included studies

A total of 4,242 records were retrieved through systematic searches across four databases: PubMed, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, and Web of Science. After automated and manual removal of duplicates, 2,600 unique records remained. During the initial title and abstract screening phase, 2,529 articles were excluded based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, primarily due to lack of relevance to medical applications or absence of DP components.

The remaining 71 articles underwent full-text screening to assess their eligibility and methodological alignment with the review objectives. At this stage, additional exclusions were made based on insufficient empirical content, non-healthcare focus, or redundancy in covered topics, resulting in the exclusion of 20 more papers.

Ultimately, 51 studies met all criteria and were included from the database search. To supplement this, 23 additional relevant studies were identified through manual searching of reference lists and preprint repositories, ensuring coverage of the most recent and impactful contributions. 4
Theoretical Background

We begin by establishing the core concepts and notation that form the basis of our discussion on DP in DL, followed by a detailed exposition of relevant privacy mechanisms and their integration into DL workflows.

Throughout this paper, we use uppercase bold letters (e.g.,
\mathbf{X}
) for matrices, lowercase bold letters (e.g.,
\mathbf{w}
) for vectors, uppercase letters (e.g.,
D
) for datasets, and lowercase letters (e.g.,
x
) for scalar variables or individual data samples.

4.1
Differential Privacy Definitions

DP was introduced by Dwork et al.
Dwork and Roth 2014
as a mathematically rigorous framework to limit the influence of any single individual’s data on the output of a computation. This protection is achieved by injecting controlled noise into a computation’s output, thereby masking the presence or absence of individual records.

Consider two neighboring datasets
D
and
D^{\prime}
, differing by a single entry. A randomized algorithm
M
is said to satisfy
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP if for all possible outputs
O
,

\Pr[M(D)\in O]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\cdot\Pr[M(D^{\prime})\in O]+\delta

(1)

Here,
\varepsilon
controls the worst-case multiplicative difference in output probabilities between
D
and
D^{\prime}
, while
\delta
allows for a small additive probability of failure. When
\delta=0
, we recover the original, stricter definition of
\varepsilon
-DP.

In practice,
\delta
is often set inversely proportional to dataset size (
\delta\sim 1/n
), ensuring that the likelihood of privacy failure remains negligible. Together, the
(\varepsilon,\delta)
pair quantifies the privacy guarantee, with smaller values implying stronger protection.

Rényi differential privacy (RDP)
Mironov 2017
is a generalization of DP that leverages Rényi divergence
Rényi 1961
to provide tighter composition guarantees. The Rényi divergence of order
\alpha>1
between the output distributions of
M
on
D
and
D^{\prime}
is defined as:

D_{\alpha}(M(D)\|M(D^{\prime}))=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{o\sim M(D^{\prime})}\left[\left(\frac{P_{M(D)}(o)}{P_{M(D^{\prime})}(o)}\right)^{\alpha}\right]

(2)

A mechanism satisfies
(\alpha,\varepsilon)
-RDP if this divergence is bounded by
\varepsilon
for all adjacent
D
and
D^{\prime}
:

D_{\alpha}\big(M(D)\,\|\,M(D^{\prime})\big)\leq\varepsilon

(3)

Smaller values of
\alpha
make the analysis more sensitive to small distributional differences, while larger values emphasize worst-case behavior. RDP can be converted to
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP using analytical bounds, and is widely used for accounting in iterative training.

The amount of noise required to achieve DP depends on the sensitivity of the function
f
being computed, which quantifies how much
f
can change when a single input is modified.

For vector-valued outputs, the
\ell_{2}
-sensitivity of
f
is:

\Delta_{2}f=\max_{\text{neighboring }D,D^{\prime}}\|f(D)-f(D^{\prime})\|_{2}

(4)

This sensitivity bounds the required noise scale in mechanisms such as the Gaussian and Laplace mechanisms.

For real-valued outputs, the Gaussian mechanism adds noise
\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
to
f(D)
:

\text{Release: }f(D)+\eta

(5)

To achieve
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP, the standard deviation
\sigma
must satisfy:

\sigma\geq\frac{\Delta_{2}f\cdot\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}}{\epsilon}

(6)

For
\epsilon
-DP, the Laplace mechanism adds noise from
\mathrm{Lap}(0,b)
, where:

b=\frac{\Delta_{1}f}{\epsilon}

(7)

Here,
\Delta_{1}f
is the
\ell_{1}
-sensitivity of
f
.

When the output is non-numeric (e.g., a selected category), the exponential mechanism selects an output
r
from a range
\mathcal{R}
using a utility function
u(D,r)
:

\Pr[M(D)=r]\propto\exp\left(\frac{\epsilon\cdot u(D,r)}{2\Delta u}\right)

(8)

with sensitivity:

\Delta u=\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\max_{D,D^{\prime}}|u(D,r)-u(D^{\prime},r)|

(9)

DP mechanisms can be applied at various stages of the learning pipeline, including the input
Schein et al. 2019
;
McKenna et al. 2022
;
Feyisetan et al. 2020
, training process
Abadi et al. 2016
;
Chaudhuri and Hsu 2011
, and model output
Dwork and Feldman 2018
;
Nissim et al. 2007
. Among these, training-time DP has been shown to yield better accuracy and robustness
van der Maaten and Hannun 2020
.

4.2
Deep Learning and Optimization via SGD

DL refers to models that learn hierarchical representations from data via neural networks with multiple layers
LeCun et al. 2015
;
Schmidhuber 2015
. Training these models involves minimizing an empirical risk objective:

J(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}L(\boldsymbol{\theta};x_{i},y_{i})

(10)

where
L
is the loss function and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
the model parameters. This is typically optimized using stochastic gradient descent (SGD).

Variants of SGD differ by the batch size used in each update. In practice, mini-batch SGD is most common:

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-\eta\cdot\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t};x_{i_{j}},y_{i_{j}})

(11)

where
\eta
is the learning rate and
m
is the mini-batch size. This formulation enables efficient training with good generalization.

4.3
Applying Privacy in Deep Neural Networks

Deep neural networks are vulnerable to privacy attacks targeting the input data, gradients, or model parameters
Rigaki and Garcia 2023
. Gradients are particularly sensitive, especially in federated settings where they are transmitted across devices
McMahan et al. 2017
. Since gradients are computed directly from the input data, they can leak private information under adversarial scrutiny.

A common defense is to inject noise into gradients during training. DP-SGD
Abadi et al. 2016
achieves this by modifying the gradient computation at each iteration. For each example
i
in a mini-batch
\mathcal{B}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{i}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta};x_{i},y_{i})

(12)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}_{i}\cdot\min\left(1,\frac{C}{\|\mathbf{g}_{i}\|_{2}}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{g}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{i}

(14)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{g}}

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathbf{g}}+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}C^{2}\mathbf{I})

(15)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}-\eta\hat{\mathbf{g}}

(16)

Here,
C
is the clipping norm and
\sigma
is the noise multiplier. Fig.
4
illustrates this process. Clipping limits any individual’s contribution, while the added noise ensures DP guarantees.

Variants such as dynamic DP-SGD
Du et al. 2021
adapt
C
and
\sigma
over time to improve utility during training. Other refinements target sparse gradients, which arise in applications like language models. A recent method
Ghazi et al. 2024
improves DP-SGD efficiency by focusing on non-zero gradient components, offering better performance in high-dimensional or sparse settings.

Overview of the DP-SGD training process.
(a)
In each iteration, a mini-batch of data is sampled.
(b)
Per-sample gradients are computed and clipped to a fixed norm to bound sensitivity.
(c)
Noise—typically Gaussian—is added to the aggregated clipped gradients to enforce differential privacy.
(d)
The model is updated using the noisy gradients. This process is repeated over multiple iterations, yielding a trained model with formal privacy guarantees.

4.4
Private Aggregation of Teacher Ensembles

Private aggregation of teacher ensembles (PATE), introduced by Papernot et al.
Papernot et al. 2016
, is a differentially private learning framework designed to protect sensitive training data by aggregating predictions from multiple independently trained models, referred to as ”teachers.” Each teacher model
T_{k}
is trained on a disjoint subset
D_{k}
of a sensitive dataset:

D=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}D_{k},\quad D_{i}\cap D_{j}=\emptyset\text{ for }i\neq j

(17)

Given an input
x
, each teacher
T_{k}
produces a class prediction
T_{k}(x)\in\{1,\dots,C\}
, and votes are tallied across the ensemble:

v_{c}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{I}[T_{k}(x)=c]

(18)

To preserve privacy, Laplace noise is added to the vote counts:

\tilde{v}_{c}(x)=v_{c}(x)+\text{Lap}(1/\lambda)

(19)

Here,
\lambda
controls the privacy level, with smaller values inducing stronger noise and stronger privacy.

A student model
S
is then trained using public unlabeled data
D_{\text{public}}
, where noisy labels are obtained by querying the teacher ensemble. The final label assigned to each sample is:

\hat{y}(x)=\arg\max_{c}\tilde{v}_{c}(x)

(20)

The student is trained to minimize a supervised loss using the noisy labels:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{student}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell(S(x_{i}),\hat{y}(x_{i}))

(21)

The total privacy loss is tracked using a privacy accountant, such as the moments accountant
Abadi et al. 2016
, ensuring that the cumulative privacy budget
\epsilon
is not exceeded.

Compared to DP-SGD, which perturbs gradients within a single model during training, PATE decouples private learning from student training by using noise-perturbed knowledge transfer. PATE generally performs well in semi-supervised settings or when class imbalance can be mitigated via teacher ensembling. For example, a comparative study
Ganev 2021
showed that PATE-based GAN training often yields better utility-privacy tradeoffs than DP-SGD in cases where sensitive training data can be partitioned effectively.

4.5
Alternative Methods for Privacy Preservation

Beyond DP-SGD and PATE, several additional strategies have been developed to support privacy-preserving DL. One widely adopted framework is federated learning (FL), which enables collaborative model training across decentralized institutions or devices without requiring raw data to leave local storage
McMahan et al. 2017
. FL can be implemented in centralized configurations—where local updates are sent to a coordinating server—or in decentralized topologies, where clients communicate directly with peers based on a predefined graph structure
Bharati et al. 2022
. FL is increasingly used in clinical AI
Kaissis et al. 2021
;
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2023a
;
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2023b
;
Riess et al. 2025
, and is often combined with DP by adding noise to model updates. In such settings, privacy can be ensured either through centralized mechanisms—where noise is added after server aggregation—or local mechanisms, where clients inject noise prior to communication
Wei et al. 2020
;
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2023
.

Another major line of research involves secure multiparty computation, originally introduced by Yao
Yao 1982
. It enables multiple parties to jointly compute functions over private inputs without revealing those inputs to each other. In DL, secure multiparty computation has been used to train or evaluate models securely across institutional boundaries
Cramer et al. 2015
. Some recent works combine secure multiparty computation with DP
Das et al. 2025
;
Pentyala et al. 2022
, adding noise either at each training iteration or once at the end of training. These hybrid designs differ in trade-offs: for instance,
Das et al. 2025
focuses on communication efficiency through incremental protection, while
Pentyala et al. 2022
emphasizes accuracy preservation by applying noise post hoc.

Several methods aim to protect privacy by modifying gradients during training. These include clipping, pruning, masking, or injecting noise into gradients to obscure sensitive signals
Kim and Park 2024
. While some evidence suggests that such gradient masking can limit leakage without harming utility, other studies have shown that these defenses can be circumvented by more sophisticated adversaries
Athalye et al. 2018
.

An alternative line of defense involves perturbing internal feature representations rather than gradients. These feature-level defenses add noise or transformations at intermediate network layers, reducing the risk of inversion or memorization
Jeddi et al. 2020
;
Borkar et al. 2020
;
Zhang and Wang 2019
.

Synthetic data generation offers another privacy-preserving strategy. In this approach, a generative model (e.g., GAN or diffusion model) is trained on private data—sometimes under DP constraints—and is then used to produce synthetic datasets for downstream model training
Bao et al. 2023
;
Montoya Perez et al. 2024
. This decouples the final model from direct exposure to sensitive records, reducing re-identification risk.

Classical data anonymization methods are still used in some clinical contexts. These include
k
-anonymity
Samarati 2002
,
l
-diversity
Machanavajjhala et al. 2007
, and
t
-closeness
Li et al. 2006
, which aim to ensure that individual records are indistinguishable within equivalence classes. However, these approaches lack formal guarantees and are often vulnerable to auxiliary information attacks.

Another direction of research considers how architectural choices impact privacy. For example, empirical studies suggest that transformer-based models tend to leak more information than convolutional neural networks (CNNs), possibly due to higher capacity and attention mechanisms
Zhang et al. 2024
. Components such as normalization layers, activation functions, and attention structures can influence a model’s vulnerability to privacy attacks.

Finally, cryptographic tools such as homomorphic encryption and secure aggregation are increasingly applied to protect data during training and communication
Zhang and Li 2024
. In FL, for instance, gradients can be encrypted locally before transmission, adding an additional layer of protection against malicious servers or eavesdroppers.

These alternative mechanisms illustrate the diversity of tools available for protecting patient data in clinical machine learning. While our review focuses primarily on DP-SGD and PATE due to their theoretical foundations and empirical uptake, the broader ecosystem of privacy-preserving techniques—including encryption, secure computation, gradient manipulation, and architectural design—offers complementary strategies that may be better suited for specific tasks or deployment environments.

5.2.3
Data Augmentation Practices for DP-SGD

Data augmentation, a staple of conventional DL, is rarely applied under DP-SGD. Among reviewed studies, only two used basic affine transformations—one for brain MRI classification
Mehmood et al. 2024

;

This reluctance stems from the risk of increasing gradient variance, which exacerbates training instability under noisy DP updates. Augmentations can alter input distributions in ways that interfere with the gradient signal, especially in small medical datasets with high class imbalance

.

The lack of systematic evaluation limits conclusions about which augmentations, if any, are compatible with DP-SGD. There is a clear need for controlled experiments testing standard and domain-specific augmentation strategies under DP conditions.

5.2.4
Pretraining and Transfer Learning for DP-SGD

Pretraining is a consistent and effective strategy for mitigating performance loss under DP-SGD. By starting from pretrained weights, models avoid the fragile convergence dynamics associated with noisy training from scratch.

Tayebi Arasteh et al.
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2024
demonstrated that DP-SGD models trained from scratch failed to learn meaningful features, whereas pretrained ResNet-9 models retained high accuracy. In a related study, pretrained models on chest X-ray and CT were shown to maintain near non-private performance under tight
\epsilon
budgets
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2024
. Pretraining on general-domain speech corpora also improved DP training on pathological speech classification
Arasteh et al. 2024
, despite cross-domain mismatch. Similarly, ImageNet-initialized models improved performance in brain MRI classification under DP
Mehmood et al. 2024
.

Pretraining is not only helpful but often necessary for training deeper or more expressive models under DP-SGD. Explicit reporting and standardization of pretraining strategies is vital for reproducibility and benchmarking.

5.3.1
Performance at Extremely Strong Privacy Levels

At very low privacy budgets (
\epsilon\approx 1
), the magnitude of injected noise often leads to significant performance degradation. For example, Tayebi Arasteh et al.
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2024
observed an AUC drop from 89.7% to 84.0% on chest X-rays and from 99.7% to 92.0% on abdominal CT when applying
\epsilon=0.5
. In pathological speech classification, accuracy fell from 99.1% to 88.3% at
\epsilon=0.9

Arasteh et al. 2024
, and in dermoscopy, accuracy declined from 69.1% to 43% at
\epsilon=3

Guo et al. 2022
. Similar trends were reported for generative tasks, such as cardiac MRI synthesis, where Fréchet inception distance (FID) worsened substantially under
\epsilon=1

Daum et al. 2025
.

However, there are exceptions. In a multi-institutional study on domain transfer, Tayebi Arasteh et al.
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2023
showed that external validation performance remained nearly unchanged across
\epsilon\approx 1
–10. This suggests that DP noise may act as a regularizer, benefiting generalization even as in-domain performance drops.

5.3.2
Utility Under Strong Privacy Levels: A Modality-Specific Perspective

Most reviewed studies operate in the strong privacy regime (
\epsilon\approx 5
–10), where performance generally remains close to non-private baselines. For instance, chest X-ray AUC dropped modestly from 89.7% to 87.4% at
\epsilon=7.9
, and CT classification remained above 99% even at
\epsilon=8

Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2024
. In MRI
Mehmood et al. 2024
and genomic data
Khanna et al. 2022
, performance losses at
\epsilon=10
were similarly modest.

Nonetheless, more fragile domains show greater vulnerability to DP noise. Studies in histopathology
Adnan et al. 2022
, dermoscopy
Guo et al. 2022
, and speech
Arasteh et al. 2024
report larger performance drops, reflecting higher input variance, smaller datasets, and more complex learning tasks. Tabular and sensor-based applications—e.g., in mental health or activity monitoring—also showed significant utility loss at lower
\epsilon
values
Aziz et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2024
.

These trends emphasize the importance of modality-specific tuning: modalities like chest imaging are more robust under DP-SGD, while others require additional regularization, pretraining, or architectural adaptation to maintain clinical viability.

While most studies report
\epsilon
, the value of
\delta
—which bounds the probability of privacy failure—is often missing or inconsistently stated. Best practice recommends
\delta\leq 1/n
, where
n
is the dataset size. Many studies follow this, setting
\delta
between
10^{-5}
and
10^{-6}
, but others omit it or use heuristic values without justification.

Reporting gaps extend beyond privacy parameters. Performance under multiple
\epsilon
levels is rarely benchmarked, and core implementation details—such as clipping norm, noise multiplier, accounting method (e.g., RDP or moments accountant), and number of training epochs—are frequently missing. This limits reproducibility and cross-study comparison.

This issue is particularly acute in FL settings, where DP is often applied to distributed model updates. Although FL introduces additional privacy through decentralization, few studies evaluate how DP noise affects utility across varying budgets in such settings, hindering generalizability.

To address these gaps, we recommend several reporting standards for future work. (1) Researchers should report both non-private and private model performance to provide clear context for evaluating the impact of privacy mechanisms. (2) The values of key privacy parameters, including
\epsilon
,
\delta
, and the accounting method used (e.g., RDP or moments accountant), should be explicitly stated to ensure interpretability and comparability. (3) Performance metrics should be reported across multiple
\epsilon
levels to characterize the trade-off between privacy and utility. (4) Finally, studies should document core model and training details, including architecture type, dataset size, pretraining strategies, and other relevant hyperparameters. Adopting these practices will improve transparency, foster reproducibility, and support more rigorous benchmarking of privacy-preserving models for clinical deployment.

Progress will require treating fairness as a first-class design goal in DP pipelines, and systematically reporting subgroup performance across varying privacy levels.

6.2
Fairness Metrics and Their Application

Fairness auditing is essential when applying DP to medical DL, as noise can distort performance differently across groups
Ziller et al. 2024
. Reviewed studies primarily assess group-level metrics based on protected attributes such as age, sex, or race. Table
4
summarizes the main metrics used.

Most fairness evaluations focus on group-level comparisons, typically assessing disparities across sex, age, or race. The most frequently reported metric is statistical parity difference (PtD)
Dwork et al. 2012
;
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2024
;
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2023
;
Arasteh et al. 2024
;
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2024
;
Xing et al. 2025
, which measures the difference in the proportion of positive predictions between two subgroups:

\text{PtD}=\Pr(\hat{Y}=1\mid A=a)-\Pr(\hat{Y}=1\mid A=b)

(22)

Here,
\hat{Y}
is the model prediction,
A
is a protected attribute (e.g., sex), and
a
,
b
are two group values (e.g., male and female).
A related metric is the disparate impact ratio (DIR)
Commission 1978
;
Feldman et al. 2015
, which compares the rates of favorable outcomes as a ratio:

\text{DIR}=\frac{\Pr(\hat{Y}=1\mid A=a)}{\Pr(\hat{Y}=1\mid A=b)}

(23)

Another widely used metric is the equal opportunity difference
Hardt et al. 2016
;
Arasteh et al. 2024
, which focuses on differences in true positive rates (TPRs) between groups:

\Delta\text{EqOp}=\Pr(\hat{Y}=1\mid Y=1,A=a)-\Pr(\hat{Y}=1\mid Y=1,A=b)

(24)

In this case,
Y
is the ground truth label, with
Y=1
indicating a positive case. Equalized odds
Hardt et al. 2016
extends this concept by considering both true positive and false positive rates:

\displaystyle\Delta\text{EOD}=\

\displaystyle\left|\Pr(\hat{Y}=1\mid Y=1,A=a)-\Pr(\hat{Y}=1\mid Y=1,A=b)\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\Pr(\hat{Y}=1\mid Y=0,A=a)-\Pr(\hat{Y}=1\mid Y=0,A=b)\right|

(25)

Predictive equality
Hardt et al. 2016
, by contrast, focuses only on disparities in false positive rates:

\Delta\text{FPR}=\Pr(\hat{Y}=1\mid Y=0,A=a)-\Pr(\hat{Y}=1\mid Y=0,A=b)

(26)

Treatment equality
Corbett-Davies et al. 2017
compares the balance between false negatives (FN) and false positives (FP) across groups:

\text{Treatment Equality}=\frac{\text{FN}_{a}/\text{FP}_{a}}{\text{FN}_{b}/\text{FP}_{b}}

(27)

In addition to general-purpose fairness metrics, several studies employed domain-specific measures such as underdiagnosis rate
Obermeyer et al. 2019
(differences in false negative rates across groups), overdiagnosis rate (false positives), and subgroup AUC
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2024
;
Arasteh et al. 2024
;
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2023
. While these do not require formal equations, they are particularly important in clinical settings, where different types of errors carry different consequences for patient care.

While these metrics are useful, they must be interpreted in context. For example, parity in outcomes across age groups may be inappropriate when prevalence rates differ biologically. Furthermore, DP-induced noise may distort probability calibration, reducing the reliability of threshold-based or probabilistic fairness measures.

To ensure robustness, future studies should adopt a standardized approach to fairness auditing under DP: (1) report multiple fairness metrics to capture different dimensions of disparity; (2) justify the choice of protected attributes based on clinical relevance; (3) disaggregate results by subgroup across multiple
\epsilon
values; and (4) apply calibration or post hoc adjustments when using probability-based metrics. The development of benchmark datasets with detailed demographic annotations is also critical to advance fair, privacy-preserving AI in medicine.

Membership inference attacks (MIAs) attempt to infer whether a specific sample was part of the model’s training dataset—a particularly serious risk in healthcare, where inclusion in a disease cohort may itself reveal sensitive clinical information. Multiple studies applied DP-SGD to protect against MIAs in classification tasks using imaging, sensor, and tabular data

;

;

;

. These defenses typically introduced Gaussian noise into gradient updates, reducing attack success rates even at moderate
\epsilon

However, the strength of protection varied across settings. Fan et al.
Fan and Bonomi 2023
noted that class imbalance and shallow architectures increased MIA vulnerability under DP, emphasizing the need to consider model and data characteristics when tuning privacy parameters. Almadhoun et al.
Almadhoun et al. 2020
confirmed that even with DP-SGD, certain MIAs could still succeed under weak noise or limited clipping, highlighting the importance of robust DP configurations.

Beyond MIAs, several studies evaluated inversion-based threats, where attackers attempt to reconstruct sensitive inputs—such as medical images or EHR records—by analyzing shared gradients. These attacks are especially relevant in federated learning, where gradient exchange is common. For example, Hatamizadeh et al.
Hatamizadeh et al. 2023
and Liangrui et al.
Pan et al. 2024
demonstrated that DP-SGD defenses could substantially degrade inversion quality but not eliminate leakage entirely. Ziller et al.
Ziller et al. 2024
observed similar results in reconstruction attacks on multi-institutional imaging models.

Alternative perturbation schemes, such as weight noise
Díaz et al. 2025
or complex-valued gradients
Riess et al. 2025
, were also explored as mitigation strategies. Still, these methods remain under-tested and lack standardized evaluation criteria, especially under stronger adversary assumptions (e.g., white-box gradient access).

LDP, which perturbs data before model access, was investigated in a few studies (e.g.,
Alsenani 2025
;
Gwon et al. 2024
;
Tramèr et al. 2015
). While theoretically strong, LDP was shown to be insufficient in practice against sophisticated attacks, including attribute inference and partial MIAs. These findings underscore that DP defenses must often extend beyond input perturbation, especially in distributed learning settings.

A recurring recommendation across studies is the use of hybrid defenses—combining DP with cryptographic methods like secure aggregation or multiparty computation
Alsenani 2025
;
Wu et al. 2021
. These layered strategies offer stronger guarantees but introduce practical constraints, including communication overhead and increased system complexity.

Despite growing interest in attack resilience, several gaps remain. First, the majority of studies focus on a narrow set of attacks—most commonly MIAs—while overlooking others like training-time poisoning or client-level inference in federated setups. Second, many reports lack standardized threat models or omit key experimental details (e.g., batch size, gradient access scope), limiting reproducibility. Third, defenses are rarely evaluated under varying
\epsilon
levels, making it difficult to assess tradeoffs between privacy strength and robustness.

DP offers a foundational layer of protection against common inference attacks in medical DL. However, achieving effective defense requires not only tuning
\epsilon
, but also understanding how model architecture, data distribution, and perturbation method interact with adversarial goals. Future work should prioritize comprehensive, reproducible evaluations that benchmark attack resilience under clinically realistic settings and privacy constraints.

8
Discussion

This scoping review synthesizes recent advances in applying DP to medical DL, with a particular focus on subgroup fairness, attack resilience, utility tradeoffs, and alternative privacy mechanisms. Across 74 studies, we observed growing maturity in technical implementations, but uneven adoption of standardized evaluation protocols and fairness-aware design practices.

Despite the theoretical strengths of DP
Dwork and Roth
, practical implementations face well-documented tradeoffs. Performance consistently degrades under stricter privacy budgets, particularly at
\epsilon\approx 1
, where noise injection frequently leads to convergence failure or sharp accuracy declines
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2022
;
Arasteh et al. 2024
. However, several studies demonstrated that with strong pretraining
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2024
;
Mehmood et al. 2024
, stable architectures
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2023
;
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2024
;
Kaess et al. 2025
, and careful normalization
Arasteh et al. 2024
, models can maintain clinically acceptable utility even under strong privacy guarantees (
\epsilon\approx
10). Imaging tasks, particularly chest X-ray and CT classification, showed resilience to DP noise
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2023
;
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2024
, while domains such as histopathology
Adnan et al. 2022
, dermoscopy
Guo et al. 2022
, and pathological speech
Arasteh et al. 2024
proved more vulnerable, largely due to smaller datasets or higher input variability.

Fairness remains underexplored relative to utility. A handful of studies—most notably those by Tayebi Arasteh et al.
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2023
;
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2024
;
Arasteh et al. 2024
—conducted thorough evaluations of subgroup disparities under varying privacy levels, revealing that DP can exacerbate demographic gaps unless explicitly mitigated. However, fairness is rarely treated as a core training objective. Intersectional effects are largely absent, and fairness in regression or survival tasks remains rarely assessed
Fan and Bonomi 2023
;
Raghavan Veeraragavan et al. 2024
. Fairness-aware optimization methods such as reweighting or constraint-based training are virtually unexplored in the current literature. Metrics like subgroup AUC, underdiagnosis rate, and statistical parity difference have been used to track disparities
Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2023
;
Kaess et al. 2025
;
Chin-Cheong et al. 2020
, but no consensus exists on which are most clinically meaningful or how to interpret them under noisy predictions.

Alternative privacy mechanisms beyond DP-SGD—including Laplace and exponential noise
Li et al. 2021
;
Aziz et al. 2022
;
Fedeli et al. 2021
, federated aggregation
Alsenani 2025
;
Gong et al. 2023
;
Yuan et al. 2019
, and local perturbation
Gwon et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2021
—offer increased flexibility and often better alignment with deployment constraints. Generative models, such as GANs and diffusion models, were applied to EHR and medical image synthesis under DP constraints
He et al. 2024
;
Fang et al. 2022
;
Sun et al. 2023
. However, many studies omit essential parameters like
(\epsilon,\delta)
or the privacy accountant used, complicating reproducibility and comparison.

We also reviewed the threat landscape for privacy-preserving models. Empirical evaluations show that DP can significantly mitigate attacks such as membership inference and gradient inversion
Shiri et al. 2024
;
Fan and Bonomi 2023
;
Hatamizadeh et al. 2023
;
Ziller et al. 2024
, but protections are highly context-dependent. Fan et al.
Fan and Bonomi 2023
emphasized that class imbalance and shallow architectures increased vulnerability. Almadhoun et al.
Almadhoun et al. 2020
demonstrated residual attack success under weak noise, even with DP-SGD. Some studies also evaluated reconstruction attacks in FL
Pan et al. 2024
;
Truhn et al. 2024
, identity and attribute disclosure risks
Sun et al. 2023
, and defenses using weight perturbation
Díaz et al. 2025
or complex-valued networks
Riess et al. 2025
. Importantly, LDP alone was shown to be insufficient under adversarial conditions
Gwon et al. 2024
;
Tramèr et al. 2015
.

This review has several limitations. While our search covered major venues and was supplemented by manual inclusion of key preprints, the rapid pace of publication means that recent work may have been missed. In addition, while we systematically included all eligible studies applying DP to medical DL, some clinical areas—such as genomics
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Niinimäki et al. 2019
or survival modeling
Fan and Bonomi 2023
;
Raghavan Veeraragavan et al. 2024
—are still underrepresented in the literature itself. Finally, our review focused primarily on technical methods and performance outcomes; perspectives from clinicians, patients, and data custodians were outside the scope but remain essential for translating privacy-preserving models into practice.

Several future directions emerge. First, fairness evaluations should become standard practice in DP studies, ideally spanning multiple
\epsilon
levels and well-justified subgroup definitions. Second, benchmark datasets with demographic annotations and support for intersectional analysis would facilitate robust, reproducible fairness evaluations. Third, new methods that integrate privacy and fairness directly into training—such as group-aware DP optimizers or adaptive clipping—could help ensure that protections do not exacerbate health inequities. Fourth, clearer documentation of DP parameters, accountant types, and performance tradeoffs will improve transparency and support rigorous benchmarking.

Differential privacy offers a robust framework for protecting individual data in clinical AI models. However, privacy alone does not guarantee equitable or reliable deployment. A shift toward integrated design—where privacy, fairness, and utility are optimized together—is essential for developing trustworthy, real-world-ready medical AI. This review provides a foundation for that goal, clarifying what is already known and identifying what remains to be addressed.
