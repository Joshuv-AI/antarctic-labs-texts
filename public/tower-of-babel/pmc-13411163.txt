Coumarin linker facilitates regulation of CRISPR/Cas9 activity using visible light
Authors: Ian McClain, Bhoomika Pandit, Maksim Royzen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Photochemical control of CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology is an important tool for a variety biochemical applications. In the past, either photo-activation or photo-inactivation has been achieved using UV-light responsive chromophores, which are not ideal for in vivo applications. In this work, we describe a coumarin containing phosphoramidite that can be site-specifically incorporated into guide RNA using solid phase synthesis. It will facilitate regulation of Cas9-enabled nuclease activity using visible light. As a proof-of-concept, we synthesized guide RNA targeting the GFP gene. Regulation of nuclease activity was shown in solution, as well as in live GFP-expressing HEK293 cells.

Introduction

During the past decade, there has been a great deal of innovation in CRISPR/Cas9-based gene editing technology.1,2 In particular, optical control of nuclease activity has been extensively explored towards minimizing off-target effects and to control gene functions with spatiotemporal precision both in cells and live organisms. This has been pursued either by installing light-responsive modalities onto Cas9 or its gene-editing partner, single guide RNA (sgRNA). For example, Zhou W. et al. installed photocaged uridine and guanosine residues into the guide sequence of sgRNA (Fig. 1A).3 The construct remained inactive until irradiated with 365 nm light. The authors were able to control gene expression with spatiotemporal precision in HEK293 cells and zebrafish thus showing that the system is a useful tool for developmental biology. A different approach was described by Sun Y.-J. et al. who crosslinked sgRNA using light-sensitive nitroaryl groups (Fig. 1A).4 The conformationally restricted sgRNA was shown to be inactive in cells and mouse zygotes until irradiated with 365 nm light. Lastly, Carlson-Stevermer, J., et al. reported a robust gene-editing system that can be inactivated using 345 nm light (Fig. 1A).5 They utilized a light sensitive group as a linker that was site-specifically installed into sgRNA. The authors used UV light to limit gene-editing to a narrow spatiotemporal window that allowed to lower off-target effects. Similar systems for optical control of CRISPR/Cas9 have been reported by Bhatia, Ha and Tang groups.6–8

(A) Previously reported sgRNAs containing nitroaryl-based groups that can be photocleaved using UV light; (B) the current design of sgRNA containing coumarin-based linker that is responsive to visible light.

All of the technologies described above were responsive to UV-light, which is not ideal for biological applications. Our goal was to address this by developing a system which can be controlled using visible light. To achieve this, we decided to utilize the previously described photocleavable coumarin.9,10 Yamazoe et al. incorporated a similar photocleavable dye into caged morpholino oligonucleotides that were used for sequential gene silencing.11 Heckel and coworkers reported an analogous coumarin linker to achieve oligonucleotide fragmentation.12 Wang et al. described application of a photolabile coumarin linker for gene editing using a three-component crRNA–tracrRNA–Cas9 system.13 We designed a coumarin containing linker that was site-specifically installed into sgRNA using solid phase synthesis. Its placement was carefully chosen to ensure minimal disruption of proper Cas9 binding and activity which is on par with the native system. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 1B, photoirradiation with 456 nm light results in degradation of sgRNA and subsequent inhibition of nuclease activity. A similar approach to install a coumarin-based linker into sgRNA was recently published.14 In contrast to this report, our linker synthesis is more concise, and we were able to achieve regulation CRISPR activity in live mammalian cells.

Results and discussion

Our goal was to site-specifically install a photolabile coumarin linker into sgRNA using solid phase synthesis. To achieve this, we designed the phosphoramidite 5, whose synthesis is shown in Fig. 2. The synthesis of the coumarin linker commenced with the previously reported compound 1 that was prepared using the published method.15 The alcohol group was protected with TBS-Cl in the presence of imidazole. Hydroboration–oxidation of 2 using BH3–Me2S and H2O2, yielded compound 3.11 The primary alcohol was protected with DMT-Cl, while the TBS group was cleaved using TBAF. Lastly, the phosphoramidite 5 was formed upon addition of CEP-Cl. The phosphoramidite 5 was site-specifically incorporated into sgRNA using standard solid phase synthesis.

Synthesis of the phosphoramidite 5 that will enable site-specific incorporation of coumarin-based photocleavable group into sgRNA. (a) TBS-Cl; (b); BH3–Me2S and H2O2; (c) DMT-Cl, pyridine; followed by TBAF; (d) CEP-Cl, DIPEA, DMAP, CH2Cl2.

Upon completion of the chemical synthesis, the phosphoramidite 5 was incorporated into sgRNA targeting linearized eGFP-N1 plasmid. Placement of the photolabile linker is very important because it could potentially perturb the sgRNA–Cas9 complex and could negatively impact nuclease activity. Our group, as well as others reported that tetraloop is generally permissive to chemical modifications.16,17 In fact, it was reported to tolerate incorporation of an entire aptamer without significant perturbation of catalytic activity.18 Meanwhile, Carlson-Stevermer et al. reported that position 57 of sgRNA (stem loop 1) is also permissive to modifications.5 The authors incorporated a nitroaryl-based photolabile linker (Fig. 1A) at that position and found the resulting construct to be fully functional. Based on these reports, we decided to construct the sgRNA2 illustrated in Fig. 3. As a control, we also synthesized sgRNA1 that does not have any modifications.

Incorporation of photolabile coumarin into two positions of sgRNA2.

The synthesized sgRNAs were purified by preparative PAGE. Their purity was assessed by analytical PAGE (Fig. 4A, lanes 2–5) and ESI-MS (Fig. 4B and C). The deconvoluted mass spectra of sgRNA1 and sgRNA2, matched the calculated m/z values of 32 911.8 and 33 340.5, respectively. Then, we assessed in vitro photocleavage of sgRNA2. It was irradiated with using Kessil lamp (456 nm) at 100% power setting for 5, 10 and 20 min and analyzed by analytical PAGE. As shown in Fig. 4A, lanes 6–8, the sgRNA2 was efficiently cleaved after photo-irradiation. Multiple bands are observed on the gel, as photo-irradiation will cleave either one or both coumarin linker, potentially resulting in seven different oligonucleotide fragments.

We tested ability of sgRNA1 and sgRNA2 to cut linearized eGFP-N1 plasmid in the presence of Cas9. After 16 h of treatment the experiments were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis, shown in Fig. 5, lanes 3 and 4. The results suggest that modification of sgRNA with two coumarin groups did not significantly perturb its functionality, as the nuclease activity levels of sgRNA1 and sgRNA2 seem to be very similar. Subsequently, both sgRNAs were irradiated with 456 nm light for 20 min and their activity was re-assessed under the same conditions. As expected, the activity of the unmodified sgRNA1 was not affected at all (Fig. 5, lane 5). Meanwhile, activity of sgRNA2 significantly lowered, presumably due to photocleavage (Fig. 5, lane 6). Afterwards, we repeated CRISPR experiment, while irradiating the reaction mixture at different timepoints (2 h and 4 h). The results are shown in Fig. 5, lanes 7–10. We observed a small decrease in nuclease activity of sgRNA2 after photoirradiation.

Control of CRISPR–Cas9 activity was subsequently assessed in GFP-expressing HEK239 cells. We followed the strategy described by Yin et al. to ensure post-transfection stability of sgRNAs that might be susceptible to nuclease degradation inside the cells. The sgRNAs were modified to include site-specific 2′-OMe modifications at positions that were reported to be compatible with binding between sgRNA and Cas9.19 We synthesized sgRNA3, as a positive control, having the sequence:

5′-GGGCGAGGAGCUGUUCACCGGUUUUAGagcuagaaauagcaaGUUaAaAuAaggcuaGUccGUUAucAAcuugaaaaagugGcaccgagucggugcuuuuU-3′. Capital letters indicate unmodified nucleotides, while lowercase correspond to nucleotides containing 2′-OMe groups. We also synthesized sgRNA4 that had the same sequence and that incorporated two coumarins at the same locations as in sgRNA2. sgRNA3 and sgRNA4 were purified by preparative PAGE, shown in Fig. S1. We tested ability of sgRNA3 and sgRNA4 to cut linearized eGFP-N1 plasmid in the presence of Cas9. The results, shown in Fig. S2, confirmed that 2′-OMe modifications had minimal impact on the nuclease activity, which was analogous to sgRNA1 and sgRNA2.

GFP-expressing HEK293 cells were co-transfected with sgRNA3 or sgRNA4 and commercially available mRNA that encodes the Cas9 gene for 72 h. After the transfection, the media was replaced with fresh DMEM and the cells were allowed to grow for additional 48 h. GFP expression was analyzed by flow cytometry and compared to the untreated cells (Fig. 6). Treatment with sgRNA3 resulted in marked increase of weakly fluorescent subpopulation of cells (Fig. 6A). As expected, analogous outcome was observed when the cells were transfected with sgRNA4 (Fig. 6B). To illustrate control of nuclease activity, the cells were once again transfected with sgRNA4 and mRNA that encodes the Cas9 gene. One hour into the transfection period, the cells were irradiated with 456 nm light for 10 minutes. The transfection was subsequently continued for another 71 h. Afterwards, the media was replaced with fresh DMEM and the cells were allowed to grow for additional 48 h. GFP expression was once again analyzed by flow cytometry and compared to the untreated cells (Fig. 6C). We observed a much smaller effect on the expression of GFP due to inactivation of sgRNA4 using light. Fig. 6D shows quantitative analysis of the subpopulation of weakly fluorescent cells as percentage of the total cells.

To confirm that irradiation with 456 nm light does not impact GFP expression, we carried out a control experiment using the unmodified sgRNA3. GFP-expressing HEK293 cells were co-transfected with sgRNA3 and commercially available mRNA that encodes the Cas9 gene for 72 h. One hour into the transfection period, the cells were irradiated with 456 nm light for 10 minutes. The transfection was subsequently continued for another 71 h. Afterwards, the media was replaced with fresh DMEM and the cells were allowed to grow for additional 48 h. GFP expression was once again analyzed by flow cytometry and compared to the cells treated with sgRNA3, but without irradiation with 456 nm light. As illustrated in Fig. S3, the flow cytometry histograms look very similar suggesting that exposure to 456 nm light had little impact on the expression of GFP.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a method to optically control CRISPR–Cas9 activity using site-specific modification of sgRNA with coumarin dyes. The placement of coumarin was carefully chosen to minimally impact the native binding between sgRNA and Cas9. We have shown that coumarin-modified sgRNA was functional in solution and was capable of cutting plasmid DNA in the presence of Cas9. However, brief irradiation with 456 nm light resulted in sgRNA truncation and inhibition of its activity. In contrast to previously described constructs capable of optical control of nuclease activity, our system can be controlled using visible light which is compatible with live cells. This was illustrated by successful targeting of GFP in HEK293 cells. We foresee that our system will be useful for experiments in which CRISPR–Cas9 activity persists for a long time post-transfection. This has been shown to happen when Cas9 is transfected in the form of mRNA and when sgRNA is chemically modified to prevent nuclease degradation.20 Prolonged CRISPR–Cas9 activity could potentially be toxic to cells.21,22 Our system will allow for inactivation of coumarin-modified sgRNA at the right moment in time.
