Machine learning–based prediction of postoperative continuous renal replacement therapy initiation after open thoracoabdominal aortic repair
Authors: Kai Xu, Jiang Liu, Yumeng Ji, Jian Song, Shiqi Gao, Chenyu Zhou, Juntao Qiu, Cuntao Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) initiation for severe acute kidney injury is a clinically consequential complication after open thoracoabdominal aortic repair. We aimed to develop an interpretable machine-learning (ML) model for predicting postoperative CRRT in this high-risk population.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included consecutive adult patients who underwent open thoracoabdominal aortic repair between January 2010 and December 2025. After exclusions, 372 patients were analyzed, including 66 (17.7%) who initiated postoperative CRRT. The cohort was randomly divided into a training set (75%, n = 278) and an internal test set (25%, n = 94). After multistep feature selection, six routinely available perioperative predictors were retained. Eight ML models and a baseline logistic regression model were developed. Model interpretability was assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).ResultsIn bootstrap internal validation, Light Gradient Boosting Machine showed the highest optimism-corrected AUC of 0.814 (95% CI, 0.773–0.856) and the lowest optimism-corrected Brier score of 0.131 (95% CI, 0.112–0.150). Background

Open thoracoabdominal aortic repair (TAAR) remains a cornerstone treatment for complex thoracoabdominal aortic disease, but it is associated with substantial physiologic stress, including aortic cross-clamping, renal and visceral ischemia–reperfusion, major transfusion, and marked hemodynamic instability [1]. As a result, postoperative acute kidney injury (AKI) is a major complication in this setting. Prior studies have shown that AKI after TAAR is associated with worse early and mid-term outcomes, underscoring the clinical importance of perioperative renal injury in this high-risk population [2, 3].

Among renal endpoints, postoperative CRRT represents a severe and clinically consequential form of postoperative kidney injury, as it is associated with markedly increased mortality, prolonged intensive care utilization, and greater resource consumption. Early identification of patients at high risk for postoperative CRRT could therefore support perioperative decision-making, optimization of renal protection strategies, intensified postoperative surveillance, and timely preparation for continuous renal replacement therapy. Traditional cardiac surgery risk tools, such as the Cleveland Clinic score and Mehta score, were not developed specifically for TAAR and their performance in external populations has been only modest. In addition, conventional regression-based models are constrained by prespecified linear assumptions and may not adequately capture the complex nonlinear interactions among baseline renal reserve, aortic pathology, ischemic burden, transfusion exposure, and perioperative hemodynamics that characterize TAAR [1, 4, 5].

Machine learning offers a flexible framework for modeling complex nonlinear relationships and feature interactions, and recent cardiac surgery studies have shown its potential for AKI prediction, especially when paired with interpretable methods such as SHAP [6, 7]. However, dedicated models for predicting postoperative CRRT after TAAR remain limited. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and compare multiple machine-learning models for predicting postoperative CRRT after TAAR and to interpret the selected model using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).

Methods

Study design and patient population

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at Fuwai Hospital, Beijing, China. All consecutive adult patients who underwent TAAR between January 2010 and December 2025 were enrolled. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital (No. 2022 − 1745) on April 7, 2022. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki, as amended in 2024.

Patients with thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm or dissecting aneurysm involving Crawford extent I–IV who underwent open surgical repair were eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria were prespecified: (1) age < 18 years; (2) preoperative end-stage renal disease (maintenance dialysis); (3) early postoperative death (< 48 h); and (4) incomplete perioperative data (missing key pre- or intraoperative variables). After applying these criteria, 372 patients were included in the final analysis, while 31 were excluded. Clinical data were retrospectively retrieved from the hospital information systems. Outcome definitions

The primary outcome was the initiation of continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) during the index postoperative hospitalization after TAAR. In our institution, CRRT was initiated after comprehensive clinical assessment by the intensive care team in consultation with the surgical team and/or nephrologists. The main indications included severe postoperative AKI accompanied by one or more of the following conditions: refractory oliguria or anuria despite optimization of hemodynamics and volume status, fluid overload unresponsive to diuretic therapy, severe or persistent hyperkalemia, severe metabolic acidosis, uremic complications, or progressive azotemia in the setting of postoperative hemodynamic instability. Because patients undergoing open TAAR are frequently hemodynamically unstable in the early postoperative period, CRRT was generally preferred over intermittent hemodialysis when renal replacement therapy was required.

CRRT included continuous venovenous hemofiltration (CVVH), continuous venovenous hemodialysis (CVVHD), or continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF) using standard anticoagulation practices [8]. Review of hospitalization records, renal replacement therapy orders, procedure records, and nephrology consultation records confirmed that no patient received IHD alone during the index hospitalization. All patients who required acute postoperative RRT initially received CRRT and were therefore captured by the primary endpoint; among the 66 patients who initiated CRRT, 7 were subsequently transitioned to IHD after hemodynamic stabilization. Thus, redefining the endpoint as initiation of any RRT would not alter the event classification. Nevertheless, the primary endpoint represents observed postoperative CRRT initiation under the clinical practices of our institution rather than the biological need for any form of RRT. Although the major conventional indications for CRRT remained broadly consistent during the study period, CRRT initiation was a clinician-adjudicated treatment decision, and potential changes in ICU practice and treatment thresholds over time could not be fully excluded.

Secondary outcomes included AKI and in-hospital mortality. AKI was defined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria as any of the following: an increase in serum creatinine (SCr) of at least 0.3 mg/dL (26.5 µmol/L) within 48 h, an increase in SCr to at least 1.5 times baseline within 7 days, or urine output less than 0.5 mL/kg/h for at least 6 h [9]. In-hospital mortality was defined as all-cause death occurring during the index hospitalization after surgery and before discharge.

Adjudication of CRRT indications among in-hospital deaths

For patients who died during hospitalization, two clinicians independently reviewed medical records to assess whether CRRT indications had been met before death, based on urine output, renal function, metabolic status, volume status, treatment response, and clinical decisions. Disagreements were resolved by consensus, and reasons for not initiating CRRT were recorded when applicable.

Operative technique

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia through a left thoracoabdominal incision with the patient in a lateral decubitus–twist position. The operative strategy was selected according to aortic extent, anatomical characteristics, and surgeon preference. In our center, open TAAR was most commonly performed using a normothermic iliac perfusion strategy, whereas partial cardiopulmonary bypass or deep hypothermic circulatory arrest was used in selected patients when clinically indicated. Further technical details have been reported previously [10].

Candidate predictors

Candidate predictors included routinely available preoperative and intraoperative variables. Preoperative variables encompassed demographic characteristics, comorbidities, aortic pathology–related features, and laboratory parameters, whereas intraoperative variables included procedural characteristics, transfusion-related variables, and perfusion/metabolic indicators. For laboratory variables with multiple preoperative measurements, the value from the last preoperative assessment was used for analysis. Maximum intraoperative lactate was defined as the highest lactate value recorded during the operative period and was therefore available at the end of surgery or immediately postoperatively. Crawford extent was initially recorded as a four-category anatomical variable (extents I–IV). In the final prediction models, it was represented as a binary indicator for Crawford extent II, coded as 1 for extent II and 0 for extents I, III, or IV.

Data preprocessing

After cohort assembly, variables with > 30% missingness were excluded before model development (Supplementary Table S3). The remaining dataset was then randomly split into training and internal test sets stratified by postoperative CRRT status.

To avoid information leakage, all subsequent data-dependent preprocessing was performed exclusively within the training set, including multiple imputation, winsorization [11], scaling, VIF-based collinearity screening, Boruta and LASSO feature selection, and hyperparameter tuning. Missing values in the remaining predictors were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) with five imputations. The imputation model included all candidate predictors retained after missingness screening and available at the intended prediction time point. Continuous variables were imputed using predictive mean matching, binary variables using logistic regression, and unordered categorical variables using polytomous logistic regression. The primary outcome, postoperative CRRT, was complete and was not imputed. In the training set, postoperative CRRT was included only as an auxiliary variable in the imputation model to preserve predictor–outcome associations. In the internal test set, postoperative CRRT status was not used during imputation and was reserved only for final performance evaluation.

The imputation specification and preprocessing parameters derived from the training set were subsequently applied to the internal test set without using test-set outcome information. Model development was performed across the five imputed training datasets, and predicted probabilities from the corresponding imputed test datasets were averaged to obtain final test-set predictions. Final performance metrics were calculated using these averaged predicted probabilities.

Feature selection

A total of 88 candidate variables were initially assessed for missingness. Five variables with more than 30% missingness were excluded before model development, leaving 83 variables for subsequent screening. To reduce redundancy among highly correlated predictors, variance inflation factor (VIF)-based collinearity assessment was performed exclusively within the imputed training data. The internal test set was not used for VIF screening, Boruta selection, LASSO selection, or final predictor determination.

Boruta and LASSO regression were then applied within the training set as complementary feature-selection methods. Boruta was used to identify variables with robust predictive importance, whereas LASSO was used to obtain a sparse predictor set under regularization. The final predictors were selected by integrating the results of Boruta and LASSO with prespecified clinical and methodological considerations, including availability at the intended prediction time point, avoidance of post-outcome information, redundancy with other predictors, event-per-variable considerations, stability across feature-selection methods, and clinical interpretability.

Given the limited number of postoperative CRRT events, the final model was intentionally restricted to six predictors to maintain parsimony and reduce the risk of overfitting. The final predictors were surgery duration, D-dimer, serum creatinine, intraoperative red blood cell transfusion, maximum intraoperative lactate, and Crawford extent II. The complete feature-selection process is summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

Model development and validation

The final predictor set identified in the feature selection process was fixed before model development and used consistently across all models. The dataset was randomly split into a training set (75%) and an internal test set (25%), stratified by postoperative CRRT status.

Eight machine-learning algorithms were developed, including elastic net (Enet), decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), support vector machine (SVM), k-nearest neighbors (kNN), and multilayer perceptron (MLP). Multivariable logistic regression served as the baseline model. Training and hyperparameter tuning were conducted exclusively within the training set using stratified 10-fold cross-validation, whereas the held-out test set, derived from the same single-center cohort, was reserved exclusively for random-split internal evaluation.

Postoperative CRRT was defined as the positive class for all performance metrics. For each model, the classification threshold was determined exclusively from cross-validated out-of-fold predicted probabilities in the training set by maximizing Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). The selected model-specific threshold was fixed before application to the held-out internal test set, and test-set outcomes were not used for threshold selection. Discrimination and classification performance in the held-out internal test set were evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), F1 score, Cohen’s kappa, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) [12]. Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, F1 score, Cohen’s kappa, and MCC were calculated using the corresponding model-specific threshold, whereas AUC was calculated from continuous predicted probabilities.

To reduce reliance on a single random split, bootstrap internal validation with optimism correction was additionally performed using 1000 bootstrap resamples. Calibration was assessed using calibration plots, calibration intercept, calibration slope, and Brier score. The final model for interpretation was selected according to discrimination, classification performance, optimism-corrected performance, calibration, and clinical interpretability, rather than a single test-set metric alone. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) was used for post hoc interpretation of the selected model [13].

Sensitivity analyses

Two sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, patients who died before CRRT initiation were excluded, while retaining the original data split and predictors. Second, a composite outcome of CRRT initiation or death after documented fulfillment of the prespecified CRRT indications was used. In both analyses, feature selection was unchanged, whereas imputation, preprocessing, model fitting, tuning, and threshold selection were repeated within the training set, with performance evaluated in the corresponding test set.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and median (interquartile range [IQR]) for non-normally distributed data, while categorical variables were presented as number (percentage). Group comparisons were performed using the Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using SPSS Statistics version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Model development was performed using R version 4.2.1 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline characteristics

As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 403 patients who underwent open thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm repair between January 2010 and December 2025 were initially screened. After exclusion of 31 patients, 372 patients were included in the final analysis. The cohort was then randomly divided into a training set (n = 278, 75%) and an internal test set (n = 94, 25%).

Among the 372 patients, 66 (17.7%) initiated postoperative CRRT. The mean age was 41.4 ± 11.6 years, and 73.1% were male. Compared with the non-CRRT group, patients requiring CRRT were older and more likely to have false lumen thrombosis, hypertension, and Crawford extent II, with higher preoperative neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, SCr, D-dimer, and HbA1c levels, whereas Marfan syndrome was less frequent (Table 1). Intraoperatively, the CRRT group had longer surgery duration, higher maximum lactate, lower hemoglobin, greater blood loss, and greater RBC transfusion requirements. Selective renal perfusion and cerebrospinal fluid drainage were also more common in the CRRT group (Table 2). Other baseline and operative characteristics were generally comparable between groups. Among the 19 patients who died during the index hospitalization, eight died after CRRT initiation, whereas 11 died without prior CRRT initiation. Among the eight patients who received CRRT, the median time from surgery to CRRT initiation was 18 h (IQR, 11–30 h). The median interval from CRRT initiation to death was 3.3 days (IQR, 2.8–8.9 days). Among the 11 patients who died without prior CRRT initiation, the median time from surgery to death was 3 days (IQR, 3–10 days). Detailed chart review indicated that two of these 11 patients had met the prespecified institutional indications for CRRT before death, whereas no prespecified CRRT indication was documented before death in the remaining nine patients. Patient-level timing, biochemical findings, clinical context, and adjudicated CRRT-indication status are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

In the sensitivity analysis excluding the 11 patients who died before CRRT initiation, LightGBM achieved an AUC of 0.834 (95% CI, 0.717–0.926). In the sensitivity analysis using the composite endpoint of CRRT initiation or death after fulfillment of CRRT indications, LightGBM achieved an AUC of 0.837 (95% CI, 0.737–0.921). Discrimination, classification performance, and overall model ranking were broadly consistent with those observed in the primary analysis (Supplementary Tables S5 and S6).

ML model construction

After missingness screening, five variables with more than 30% missingness were excluded, and 83 variables were retained for VIF-based collinearity assessment. After VIF-based screening, 59 variables were retained for feature selection. All subsequent feature-selection procedures were performed within the training set only. Boruta identified 10 important features (Fig. 2A), with surgery duration showing the highest importance. LASSO was used as a complementary approach, and the coefficient path and top 10 retained variables are shown in Fig. 2B and C. By integrating Boruta and LASSO results with clinical availability, redundancy among predictors, event-per-variable considerations, and interpretability, six predictors were retained for model development: surgery duration, D-dimer, serum creatinine, intraoperative RBC transfusion, maximum intraoperative lactate, and the Crawford extent II indicator. The full feature-selection process and the rationale for final predictor inclusion or exclusion are shown in Supplementary Table S3.

Feature selection process. (A) Boruta feature importance after collinearity screening. (B) LASSO coefficient profiles. (C) Variables retained by LASSO. The final six predictors were surgery duration, D-dimer, serum creatinine, intraoperative red blood cell transfusion, maximum intraoperative lactate, and Crawford extent II

Using the final six-variable predictor set, eight machine-learning models and a baseline multivariable logistic regression model were trained in the training set. Model tuning was performed using cross-validation, and final performance was assessed in the internal test set.

Model performance comparison

Overall, the machine-learning models showed heterogeneous performance for predicting postoperative CRRT. In the internal test set, LightGBM achieved the numerically highest AUC of 0.833 (95% CI, 0.716–0.925), followed closely by SVM (0.830; 95% CI, 0.731–0.914) and elastic net (0.825; 95% CI, 0.714–0.931) (Fig. 3). Although kNN yielded the highest F1 score (0.585), Cohen’s kappa (0.474), and MCC (0.486), LightGBM showed favorable overall performance across discrimination and calibration metrics. These findings indicate that model ranking was metric-dependent and should be interpreted cautiously. Model performance in the internal test set is summarized in Table 3.

Bootstrap internal validation with optimism correction was further performed using 1000 bootstrap resamples. All resamples were successful. As shown in Supplementary Table S1, LightGBM showed the highest optimism-corrected AUC of 0.814 (95% CI, 0.773–0.856) and the lowest optimism-corrected Brier score of 0.131 (95% CI, 0.112–0.150). Elastic net, random forest, kNN, SVM, XGBoost, logistic regression, decision tree, and MLP showed lower optimism-corrected AUCs. These findings suggest that model performance was attenuated after correction for optimism. In this internally validated dataset, LightGBM showed the highest optimism-corrected AUC and the lowest optimism-corrected Brier score; however, the performance differences among several models were modest.

Quantitative calibration metrics are shown in Supplementary Table S2. Calibration intercepts were generally close to zero across most models, suggesting no marked systematic underestimation or overestimation of the average predicted risk. Calibration slopes varied across algorithms. LightGBM showed a calibration intercept close to zero but a calibration slope below 1, indicating some over-dispersion of predicted probabilities in the internal test set. Calibration plots further showed that LightGBM, logistic regression, SVM, and kNN were generally well calibrated, whereas RF, DT, and MLP deviated more from the ideal line (Fig. 4). Taken together, LightGBM was selected for SHAP-based interpretation because it showed the highest optimism-corrected AUC and the lowest optimism-corrected Brier score in this internally validated dataset and remained clinically interpretable. However, model ranking may vary across cohorts, performance metrics, and clinical settings; therefore, external validation and potential recalibration are required.

Model interpretation

To further interpret the final LightGBM model, SHAP analyses were performed (Figs. 5 and 6). As shown in Fig. 5A, Surgery Duration had the greatest overall contribution to model output, followed by D-dimer, Scr, intraoperative RBC transfusion (Intraop_RBC), intraoperative maximum lactate (Intraop_Max_Lactate), and Crawford extent II. The SHAP summary plot (Fig. 5B) demonstrated that higher values of Surgery Duration, D-dimer, SCr, intraoperative RBC transfusion, and intraoperative maximum lactate were generally associated with positive SHAP values, indicating an increased predicted risk of postoperative CRRT. In contrast, lower values of these variables were more often associated with negative SHAP values, suggesting reduced predicted risk. The contribution of Crawford extent II was smaller but remained directionally associated with a higher predicted probability of CRRT.

(B) SHAP summary plot illustrating the distribution of SHAP values for each predictor across all patients. (C) Representative individual SHAP force plot showing how each predictor contributed to the final prediction for a single patient

Feature effect plots showing the relationship between predictor values and model output in the final LightGBM model. Each panel illustrates the association between the actual value of a predictor and its corresponding effect on the model prediction

An individual-level SHAP force plot (Fig. 5C) illustrated how model predictions were driven by the combined effects of multiple variables. In the representative case shown, prolonged surgery duration, higher intraoperative RBC transfusion, and Crawford extent II increased the predicted risk, whereas lower D-dimer, lower Scr, and lower intraoperative maximum lactate reduced the prediction, yielding an overall predicted probability close to the model baseline expectation.

Detailed SHAP analysis further suggested approximate model-derived inflection ranges in which the predicted risk of postoperative CRRT increased more steeply, including surgery duration > 600 min, preoperative D-dimer > 5 µg/mL, SCr > 80–100 µmol/L, intraoperative RBC transfusion > 5 units, and maximum intraoperative lactate > 5–7 mmol/L (Fig. 6). These values were derived from the internal model explanation and should be interpreted only as exploratory patterns of model behavior. They do not represent validated clinical cutoffs, intervention targets, or treatment thresholds, and their clinical utility requires further confirmation in external cohorts.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and evaluated multiple ML models for predicting postoperative CRRT after TAAR. Among the tested algorithms, LightGBM showed the highest optimism-corrected AUC and the lowest optimism-corrected Brier score in this internally validated dataset and was therefore selected for SHAP-based interpretation. However, the differences among several models were modest, and model ranking may vary depending on the validation cohort, performance metric, and case mix. Therefore, the present model should be interpreted as an exploratory risk-stratification tool rather than a validated clinical decision model. In addition, SHAP analysis enhanced model interpretability by identifying the perioperative variables that contributed most to model output, namely surgery duration, D-dimer, SCr, intraoperative RBC transfusion, maximum intraoperative lactate, and Crawford extent II. Together, these findings suggest that a parsimonious ML model based on routinely available perioperative variables may provide useful risk stratification for severe postoperative renal dysfunction in this high-risk surgical population.

Postoperative CRRT for severe AKI is one of the most severe complications after TAAR because it reflects not only renal injury itself but also the cumulative burden of extensive aortic reconstruction, ischemia–reperfusion, systemic inflammation, transfusion exposure, and perioperative hemodynamic instability [14]. Compared with routine cardiac surgery, open thoracoabdominal repair poses distinct renal threats, including prolonged aortic manipulation, visceral and renal perfusion challenges, substantial blood loss, and high metabolic stress. The incidence of KDIGO-defined AKI in our cohort was high. Overall, 254 patients (68.3%) developed postoperative AKI, including 87 with stage 1, 59 with stage 2, and 108 with stage 3 AKI. Although this incidence appears higher than that reported in some open TAAR series, it is broadly comparable to the high AKI burden reported in Chinese cohorts of surgically treated complex aortic dissection [15]. A recent systematic review of kidney protection during thoracoabdominal aortic surgery emphasized that postoperative AKI and the need for renal replacement therapy remain major concerns despite modern organ-protection strategies [3]. Likewise, contemporary cardiac surgery literature consistently shows that cardiac surgery-associated acute kidney injury (CSA-AKI) is associated with increased mortality, longer hospitalization, and greater resource utilization [1].

Our cohort was substantially younger than those in many Western series of open TAAR, with a mean age of 41.4 years [16]. This age distribution likely reflects the specific disease composition and referral pattern of our cohort rather than a single factor alone. First, Marfan syndrome was present in 27% of patients, which likely contributed to the younger overall age profile. Second, chronic aortic dissection accounted for the majority of cases, in contrast to many Western open TAAR series that include older patients with predominantly degenerative thoracoabdominal aneurysms [17]. This pattern is broadly consistent with Chinese registry data showing younger age at presentation among patients with aortic dissection. Third, chronic dissecting thoracoabdominal disease and Crawford extent II repair predominated in our cohort, suggesting a tertiary referral population enriched for extensive and complex aortic disease [18]. Notably, Marfan syndrome was less frequent in the CRRT group than in the non-CRRT group, indicating that it contributed to the demographic profile of the cohort but was unlikely to be the main determinant of postoperative CRRT risk. Although these patients were relatively young, they were still exposed to substantial operative complexity, ischemia–reperfusion burden, transfusion requirements, and perioperative physiologic stress, which may partly explain the observed burden of severe postoperative renal dysfunction.

Another important finding is that several machine-learning models showed favorable discrimination compared with the baseline logistic regression model, although not all algorithms performed better. In particular, DT and MLP showed limited discrimination, likely reflecting the small number of outcome events, the restricted six-variable predictor set, and the susceptibility of single-tree or neural-network models to instability in modest-sized datasets. This result is biologically and methodologically plausible. Postoperative CRRT after TAAR is unlikely to be driven by a single linear pathway; rather, it probably arises from complex interactions among preoperative renal reserve, disease burden, intraoperative ischemic stress, blood loss, transfusion exposure, and tissue hypoperfusion. ML methods are well suited to capture such nonlinear associations and higher-order interactions without requiring strict prespecification of functional form. Recent studies in cardiac surgery populations have similarly shown that ML approaches can provide good predictive performance for postoperative AKI and may outperform or complement traditional regression-based tools. At the same time, these advantages should not be overstated: model performance remains highly dependent on data quality, sample size, case mix, and external validation [19, 20].

Our findings should therefore be interpreted pragmatically. The value of machine learning in this setting is not that it replaces clinical reasoning, but that it may improve risk estimation in a scenario where relationships between predictors and outcome are inherently complex. Importantly, we used only six final predictors, which preserved model parsimony while still allowing a gradient-boosting model to exploit nonlinear structure in the data. This balance between simplicity and predictive flexibility is likely one reason why LightGBM performed well in our cohort.

The SHAP analysis provided clinically plausible explanations for the final model. Surgery duration emerged as the most important predictor, which is consistent with the concept that prolonged operative time reflects greater procedural complexity, longer exposure to hemodynamic perturbation, more extensive tissue injury, and potentially longer periods of renal and visceral ischemic stress. In the feature effect plots, the predicted CRRT risk rose more steeply when surgery duration exceeded approximately 600 min, suggesting that operative burden is a major determinant of severe postoperative renal dysfunction [3, 21, 22].

Preoperative SCr was another dominant predictor. This finding is intuitive, as reduced baseline renal reserve makes patients more vulnerable to perioperative ischemia–reperfusion injury, inflammatory injury, and nephrotoxic insults [23]. D-dimer also ranked highly in the model. In the setting of thoracoabdominal aortic disease, elevated D-dimer reflects activation of coagulation and fibrinolysis and is frequently increased in aortic dissection; higher levels have also been associated with adverse outcomes in acute aortic disease [24, 25]. In this context, a higher D-dimer level may plausibly indicate greater thrombus burden, more active coagulation–fibrinolytic turnover, or more extensive aortic pathology, all of which may correlate with operative complexity and systemic stress. This interpretation is partly supported by prior work showing that D-dimer levels vary across aortic dissection subtypes and may carry diagnostic and risk-stratification information [26]. Although D-dimer is not a conventional kidney biomarker, its importance in our model suggests that disease burden before surgery may contribute meaningfully to postoperative renal risk.

Among intraoperative variables, RBC transfusion and maximum lactate were strongly associated with model output. Greater transfusion requirement likely reflects a combination of bleeding severity, surgical complexity, inflammation, and hemolysis-related renal stress; recent cardiac surgery data likewise support an association between perioperative RBC transfusion and postoperative AKI [27]. Elevated lactate is a clinically familiar marker of hypoperfusion and metabolic stress, and recent studies in cardiac surgery populations have also reported an association between intraoperative hyperlactatemia and postoperative AKI [28]. Crawford extent II showed a more modest but directionally consistent association with increased risk, which may reflect the broader extent of aortic involvement and the greater technical burden typically associated with this anatomy. More broadly, these interpretations are consistent with current understanding of CSA-AKI pathophysiology, in which preexisting renal vulnerability, ischemia–reperfusion, inflammation, and perfusion failure act together rather than independently; this framework also aligns with recent reviews focused on kidney injury during thoracoabdominal aortic surgery [1, 21].

Importantly, the SHAP-derived threshold-like values should be interpreted as approximate model-derived inflection ranges rather than absolute treatment cutoffs. These ranges were derived from internal model explanation and have not been externally validated. Therefore, they should not be used as clinical thresholds, intervention targets, or standalone reference values for decision-making. Instead, they should be viewed as exploratory patterns that help illustrate how the model captured nonlinear risk gradients across perioperative variables.

From a clinical perspective, the intended prediction time point of the model is at the end of surgery or immediately after surgery, when all six predictors are available. Therefore, the model is not designed for purely preoperative risk prediction. Instead, it may help identify patients at high risk for postoperative CRRT and support timely ICU surveillance, nephrology consultation, hemodynamic optimization, avoidance of nephrotoxic exposures, and preparation for CRRT readiness.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective single-center study, and selection bias and information bias cannot be excluded. Second, although we attempted to reduce overfitting through predictor reduction, cross-validation, held-out test-set evaluation, 95% confidence intervals, and bootstrap optimism correction, the sample size was modest and the number of postoperative CRRT events remained limited. The internal test set contained only a small number of CRRT events, resulting in uncertainty around several performance estimates. Therefore, the predicted probabilities should be interpreted cautiously and may require recalibration before clinical implementation. Third, the model lacked external validation, and its generalizability to other centers, surgical strategies, and patient populations remains uncertain. Fourth, the long study period may have introduced temporal heterogeneity. Although the major indications for CRRT remained broadly consistent, evolving evidence regarding renal replacement therapy timing and changes in ICU practice, perioperative management, and clinician thresholds may have influenced CRRT initiation. Because CRRT initiation is a clinically adjudicated treatment endpoint rather than a purely objective biological endpoint, temporal variation in treatment thresholds may have introduced outcome misclassification and affected model performance and calibration. Future prospective studies using standardized CRRT initiation criteria are needed to validate the present findings. Fifth, some potentially relevant variables may have been unavailable or incompletely captured in historical records. Sixth, death before CRRT initiation represented a competing event in 11 patients. We addressed this issue through patient-level adjudication of CRRT indications and two sensitivity analyses, including exclusion of patients who died before CRRT initiation and use of a composite endpoint comprising CRRT initiation or death after documented fulfillment of CRRT indications. The findings were broadly consistent with the primary analysis. Nevertheless, the primary prediction framework treated the outcome as binary and did not explicitly model the timing of CRRT initiation, death, or discharge. Future studies with complete time-to-event data should therefore consider formal competing-risk approaches. Finally, SHAP improves interpretability at the model level, but it does not establish causality; the identified predictors should therefore be interpreted as contributors to model prediction rather than proven mechanistic determinants.

Conclusion

A parsimonious LightGBM model based on six routinely available perioperative variables showed favorable internally validated performance for predicting postoperative CRRT after TAAR and was selected for SHAP-based interpretation. SHAP analysis improved interpretability and identified clinically plausible contributors to predicted risk. However, given the retrospective single-center design, limited number of events, modest performance differences among models, and lack of external validation, this model should be considered exploratory and requires further external validation and potential recalibration before clinical implementation.
