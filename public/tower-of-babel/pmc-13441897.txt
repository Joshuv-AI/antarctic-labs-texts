Ethanolamine as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for depressive disorder
Authors: Shintaro Ogawa, Kotaro Hattori, Shinsuke Hidese, Daimei Sasayama, Miho Ota, Tomoko Miyakawa, Megumi Tatsumi, Ryo Matsumura, Sumiko Yoshida, Takamasa Noda, Minoru Takebayashi, Wataru Omori, Kei Itagaki, Naoto Kajitani, Mami Okada-Tsuchioka, Akitoyo Hishimoto, Shuken Boku, Tadasu Horai, Akira Monji, Yoshito Mizoguchi, Hiroshi Tateishi, Toru Murakawa-Hirachi, Reiji Yoshimura, Ryohei Igata, Yoshiharu Kim, Hiroaki Hori, Hiroshi Kunugi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe investigated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) ethanolamine (EA) levels in major depressive disorder (MDD), to validate our previous findings on EA (Ogawa et al., 2015) and broaden its biological context and translational relevance. Using human samples (sets A–C) and animal models, we explored the implications for replication, translational potential, therapeutic development, and mechanistic insight. In set A (n = 380), CSF EA levels were low in patients with MDD (P = 0.00047, Cohen’s d = −0.59), negatively correlating with depression severity (Spearman’s ρ = −0.29, P = 0.00015) and positively correlating with CSF homovanillic acid (partial r = 0.40, P = 6.7E − 8) and 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid levels (partial r = 0.26, P = 0.00064). Patients with moderate-to-severe depression showed large effects regardless of medication (Cohen’s d = −1.27 to −1.14). In set B (n = 13), CSF EA levels were significantly increased in patients who received electroconvulsive therapy (P = 0.0071; Cohen’s d = 0.90), which was linked to Hamilton Depression Rating Scale subscale score recovery. In set C (n = 66), a novel multicenter-collected sample set, CSF EA levels were low in patients with MDD (P = 0.0037; Cohen’s d = −0.91). Rats receiving 0.5 or 1.0 mg/kg lipopolysaccharide intraperitoneally for 7 days showed significantly reduced CSF EA levels (P = 0.014; Cohen’s d = −1.52 and 0.00020; d = 2.31, respectively) and depressive-, anxiety-, and anhedonia-like behaviors. Rats orally administered EA for 4 weeks showed significant antidepressant-like behaviors (P = 0.016; Cohen’s d = −1.36). Proteomic and bioinformatic analyses revealed 40 proteins that were significantly correlated with CSF EA levels. The top hit protein was CHL1 (partial r = 0.42, P = 1.5E − 9), and the axon guidance pathway was the most enriched (P = 3.4E − 20). EA is a potential CSF biomarker for mental state evaluation, treatment response, subtyping, and validation of MDD animal models and a promising research tool for developing a new classification framework for psychiatric disorders and novel therapeutic strategies.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Depression, Diagnostic markers, Biological techniques

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a common disease with an estimated global prevalence of 4.4% [1]. Although candidate markers related to pathological hypotheses, such as excitation/inhibition disturbances, monoaminergic systems, inflammation, neuroimaging, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, blood metabolome, and gut microbiota, have been investigated [2–5], establishing practical biomarkers remains challenging owing to the elusive mechanisms of MDD. Thus, there is a need to identify biomarkers of MDD that can be used in daily clinical settings. Considering the high prevalence of MDD, there is an urgent need to establish functional biomarkers of MDD for diagnosis, state evaluation, treatment response, and subtyping to facilitate a novel biology-oriented classification for psychiatric disorders [6] or endophenotype-oriented translational research [7].

Our study group was the first to report the significant reduction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) ethanolamine (EA) levels in patients with MDD compared with healthy controls [8]. Remarkably, CSF EA levels were associated with depression severity (i.e., depressed, but not remitted, patients with MDD had lower CSF EA levels than healthy controls), suggesting CSF EA as a potential state-dependent marker for MDD or its subtype).

EA (C2H7NO; also known as monoethanolamine, 2-aminoethanol, or 2-aminoethyl alcohol) is a metabolite that exists in all living species, from bacteria to humans [9]. It belongs to the class of ethanolamines (including di- and triethanolamines) and is composed of ethane with an amino and a hydroxy substituent [10]. EA is essential for life as a crucial structural component of cells, a substrate for enzymatic reactions, and a precursor for the production of bioactive molecules [11–13]. It is distributed in every cell in the human body as the head group of phosphatidylethanolamine and other lipids [13], including bioactive lipid mediators [12]. EA plasmalogens are essential components of mammalian cellular membranes [11], and EA is also present as free EA at varying concentrations in bodily fluids [13].

The present study aimed to obtain further evidence on the role of EA in MDD pathophysiology. We attempted to answer the following unsolved questions: 1) Does our previous finding of reduced CSF EA levels in patients with MDD hold true in an independent novel sample set? 2) Across psychiatric disorders, is CSF EA reduction specific to MDD? 3) Do CSF EA levels correlate with clinical variables, such as CSF monoamine metabolites, in a larger sample set? 4) Do CSF EA levels respond to a therapeutic intervention in a state-dependent manner? 5) Do animal models of depressive disorder have low levels of CSF EA, and how does subchronic exogenous EA administration influence their behaviors? 6) Which biological pathway is related to CSF EA reduction in the central nervous system (CNS)?

Materials and methods

Participants

We used three clinical sample sets (A–C). Set A (n = 380) comprised individuals with MDD, bipolar disorder, or schizophrenia as well as healthy controls (Table 1). The participants were recruited through advertisements placed in free local magazines, posted on our website, and displayed at the National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry (NCNP) Hospital (Tokyo, Japan), among whom 52 patients with MDD and 54 healthy controls overlapped with our previous study [8]. Set B (n = 13) comprised patients with MDD who underwent electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) at the NCNP Hospital to determine changes in the CSF EA levels after the treatment. Set C (n = 66) comprised psychiatric patients and healthy controls recruited from four institutions in Japan other than the NCNP for the replication of our previously reported results: Kobe University (Ethics Committee approval no. 180071 and 170185), Saga University (2012-05-02 and 2015-12-02), Kure Medical Center (Hiroshima) (26-51 and 26-52), and the University of Occupational and Environmental Health (Fukuoka) (H27-243). This study was performed per the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee at the NCNP (XX-305, A2014-141, and A2019-092). All subjects provided written informed consent. Detailed information is described in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Clinical assessments

The Japanese version of the 21-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) was used to evaluate depressive symptoms [14, 15], and the six subscale and total scores were then analyzed according to a previous study [16]. Manic and schizophrenic symptoms were evaluated using the Japanese versions of the Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) [17, 18] and the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) [19, 20], respectively. BPRS scoring was performed based on clinical information obtained through interviews by a trained psychiatrist or psychologist. Daily doses of psychotropic medications were converted into equivalent doses using published guidelines [21]. Detailed information is provided in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

CSF collection in human subjects

CSF samples were collected as previously described [22]. Detailed information is provided in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Review Committee for Animal Experimentation at the National Institute of Neuroscience, NCNP (approval numbers 2017019, 2020018R2, and 2022042). We conducted two experiments in rats. In Experiment 1, 10-week-old male F344/NSlc rats underwent daily intraperitoneal administration of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) solution (0.5 or 1.0 mg/kg body weight [B.W.]) or saline during the light period for 7 days, followed by 3 consecutive days of behavioral tests. The behavioral tests included the forced swim test (FST), open field test (OFT), and sucrose preference test (SPT) to assess depression-like behavior, anxiety-like behavior, and anhedonia, respectively. In Experiment 2, 6-week-old male F344/NSlc rats received water containing 0.1% v/v EA or EA-free water via a drinking bottle for 4 weeks, followed by behavioral testing (FST, OFT, and SPT). At the end of the experiments, CSF samples were collected from all animals. A detailed description of the animal experiments and the rationale for selecting the LPS administration model are provided in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Determination of EA and other biomolecule levels

The levels of EA and other amino acid-related molecules were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography, as previously described [8]. Detailed information is provided in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Proteomics analysis

Proteomics analyses using the SomaScan Platform (https://somalogic.com/somascan-platform/) were conducted for a subset of subjects in sample set A (n = 191), which was chosen based on patients with greater HAM-D scores as well as age- and sex-matched controls. A detailed description is provided in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Gene ontology enrichment analysis

We used Metascape (https://metascape.org/) to identify enriched pathways and functional characteristics of proteins related to CSF EA levels. Detailed information is provided in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v25.0 statistical software (IBM Japan, Tokyo, Japan). Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or mean ± standard error of the mean for human and animal data, respectively. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed between groups for human or animal data using the Bonferroni or Dunnett test, respectively. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. In the proteomics SomaScan analysis, the P value considered significant was adjusted according to the number of proteins. Detailed information is provided in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Results

Sample set A

Association between CSF EA levels and demographic and clinical variables

CSF EA levels were significantly higher in men than in women (13.65 ± 2.46 vs. 12.63 ± 2.55 μM; P = 0.000091; Cohen’s d = 0.41). There was a significantly negative correlation between CSF EA levels and daily doses of antipsychotics (ρ = −0.22, P = 0.010), antiparkinsonian drugs (ρ = −0.32, P = 0.016), and sodium valproate (ρ = −0.41, P = 0.011), but not antidepressants (P = 0.52), benzodiazepine derivatives (P = 0.16), or lithium (P = 0.62) (Table 1). Demographic tables for the female and male subgroups are shown in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, respectively. The male subgroup showed a significantly negative correlation between CSF EA levels and body mass index (BMI) (ρ = −0.23, P = 0.0012) (Table S2).

Association between CSF EA levels and clinical symptoms

The CSF EA levels were significantly negatively correlated with the HAM-D total scores (ρ = −0.29, P = 0.00015; Table 1 and Supplementary Figure S1,A) and subscale scores of Core (ρ = −0.26, P = 0.00054; Figure S1,B), Sleep (ρ = −0.18, P = 0.017; Figure S1,C), Activity (ρ = −0.28, P = 0.00024; Figure S1,D), Psychic Anxiety (ρ = −0.16, P = 0.034; Figure S1,E), and Somatic Anxiety (ρ = −0.28, P = 0.00023; Figure S1,F) but not with that of Delusion (ρ = −0.14, P = 0.076; Figure S1,G). These nonparametric correlation analyses were also conducted separately for the female and male subgroups (Supplementary Figures S2 and S3, respectively). The findings revealed a similar association between EA and depressive symptoms between the sexes, as measured by the HAM-D (Figures S2,E–K and S3,E–K).

Furthermore, CSF EA levels were not significantly correlated with manic symptoms (YMRS scores) or psychotic symptoms (BPRS scores) in the entire cohort (ρ = −0.033, P = 0.80, Figure S1,H; ρ = −0.0017, P = 0.99, Figure S1,I, respectively), female patients (ρ = 0.18, P = 0.39, Figure S2,L; ρ = −0.040, P = 0.83, Figure S2,M, respectively), or male patients (ρ = −0.097, P = 0.58, Figure S3,L; ρ = 0.024, P = 0.86, Figure S3,M, respectively).

Specificity of CSF EA reduction across diagnostic groups

We compared CSF EA levels across diagnostic groups using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), controlling for age, sex, and BMI. Patients with MDD were stratified by remission or nonremission based on a HAM-D cutoff score ≤7 [23]. The CSF EA levels were significantly lower in depressed patients with MDD (12.32 ± 2.37 μM; P = 0.000052; Cohen’s d = −0.69) and schizophrenia (12.59 ± 2.56 μM; P = 0.00011; Cohen’s d = −0.63) than in healthy controls (14.07 ± 2.24 μM) but not in patients with remitted MDD (13.54 ± 2.44 μM; P = 1.0; Cohen’s d = −0.19) or bipolar disorder (13.14 ± 2.84 μM; P = 0.19; Cohen’s d = −0.34) (Fig. 1A). However, although Riedel et al. [23] suggested a cutoff of HAM-D ≤ 9 for remission in bipolar depression, the subsample of patients with bipolar disorder with HAM-D scores ≥ 10 (n = 31) had significantly lower CSF EA levels than the healthy controls (12.48 ± 2.61 μM; P = 0.030; Cohen’s d = −0.59).

Because CSF EA levels were significantly correlated with daily doses of antipsychotics, antiparkinsonian drugs, and sodium valproate (Table 1), we repeated the ANCOVA with the addition of those drug doses as covariates (i.e., daily doses of antipsychotics, antiparkinsonian drugs, and sodium valproate). This resulted in the CSF EA levels retaining statistical significance in the depressed MDD group (P = 0.00047; Cohen’s d = −0.59, additionally controlling for medication) but not in the schizophrenia or bipolar disorder groups (P = 1.0 for both, additionally controlling for medication). In the subsamples of bipolar patients with HAM-D scores ≥ 10, the difference in CSF EA levels compared with controls was no longer significant (P = 0.21; Cohen’s d = −0.44).

Furthermore, we conducted a stratified analysis of patients with MDD based on medication status and depression severity based on the correlation of CSF EA levels with both factors (Table 1). Moreover, medication status and depression severity were also associated and likely confounded (Supplementary Table S3). Compared with healthy controls, medicated patients with mild depression showed significantly reduced CSF EA levels (P = 0.00065; Cohen’s d = −0.83), whereas unmedicated patients with mild depression did not (P = 1.0; Cohen’s d = 0.38). Patients with moderate-to-severe depression showed significantly decreased CSF EA levels regardless of medication status (medicated: P = 0.000021; Cohen’s d = −1.14; unmedicated: P = 0.026; Cohen’s d = −1.27).

CSF EA levels and monoamine metabolites

We previously reported a positive correlation between CSF EA levels and monoamine metabolites [8]. Thus, we aimed to replicate our findings in a relatively large sample set (n = 173: schizophrenia, 10; nonremitted MDD, 17; bipolar disorder, 8; MDD in remission, 18; and healthy controls, 120) who were not receiving any psychotropic medication. Nonparametric partial correlation tests controlling for age, sex, and BMI revealed that CSF EA levels had a significantly positive correlation with the dopamine metabolite homovanillic acid (HVA; r = 0.40, P = 6.7E − 8) and the serotonin metabolite 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA; r = 0.26, P = 0.00064) but not with the noradrenaline metabolite 3-methoxy-4-hydroxyphenylethyleneglycol (MHPG; r = 0.11, P = 0.15) (Fig. 1B–D).

Patients may exhibit specific associations between CSF EA and monoamine metabolite levels relevant to their pathology, as previously reported [24]. Accordingly, we conducted the same partial correlation analyses controlling for age, sex, and BMI only among patients who were not under psychotropic medication (n = 53: schizophrenia, 10; non-remitted MDD, 17; bipolar disorder, 8; and MDD in remission, 18). The correlation coefficients as effect sizes (ES) were similar to those obtained when both medication-free patients and healthy controls were included. The correlation coefficient was significantly highest for HVA (r = 0.38, P = 0.0066), followed by 5-HIAA (r = 0.19, P = 0.19) and MHPG (r = 0.13, P = 0.39) (Fig. 1E–G).

Sample set B

Effect of ECT on CSF EA levels

We assessed the changes in CSF EA levels before and after ECT sessions in 13 patients with MDD (demographic and clinical information in Supplementary Table S4). The levels were significantly increased after ECT (pre-ECT: 10.51 ± 2.76 µM vs. post-ECT: 11.85 ± 2.59 μM, P = 0.0071, Cohen’s d = 0.90; Fig. 2A). Subsequently, we analyzed these data using nonparametric Kendall rank correlation test to investigate the relationships between changes in CSF EA levels and HAM-D scores following ECT. The changes in CSF EA levels were not significantly correlated with the changes in HAM-D total scores (Kendall’s τ = −0.22, P = 0.30; Fig. 2B) or with five of the six subscale scores (τ = −0.33–0.17, P = 0.13–0.46) but were significantly correlated with the changes in the HAM-D Somatic Anxiety subscale scores (τ = −0.43, P = 0.046; Fig. 2C).

Sample set C

Collaborative multicenter study

To confirm our observation in the NCNP, we organized a collaborative study with four research groups from institutions other than the NCNP (demographic information is presented in Supplementary Table S5). Patients with MDD showed significantly reduced CSF EA levels compared with healthy controls (12.22 ± 2.12 μM vs. 14.56 ± 2.30 μM; P = 0.0037; Cohen’s d = −0.91; Fig. 2D). Moreover, patients with psychiatric disorders other than MDD (bipolar disorder, 8; schizophrenia, 3) showed lower CSF EA levels (11.82 ± 2.40 μM) than the healthy controls (P = 0.031; Cohen’s d = −0.94; Fig. 2D), based on ANCOVA controlling for age and sex.

During Experiment 2, the average volume of drinking water was 23.7 ± 0.7 mL of water containing 0.1% v/v EA compared with 25.1 ± 0.6 mL of EA-free water (P = 0.14; Cohen’s d = −0.78). The rats that consumed 1 mL of water containing 0.1% v/v EA were estimated to ingest 1 µL of EA daily. The daily average EA consumption was 124.5 µL/kg B.W. (range: 107.3–146.2) in the EA-administered group (Fig. 3F), which did not affect their growth (Fig. 3F). Proteomics and bioinformatics analyses

Proteomics correlation and gene ontology enrichment analysis

To determine the biological factors and pathways in the CNS associated with CSF EA levels, we first used a proteomics approach with SomaScan to perform a nonparametric partial correlation analysis of CSF EA levels in a subset of sample set A (n = 191), controlling for age, sex, and BMI. When the significant P value was set at 4.4E − 5 (0.05 / 1128 proteins) to correct for multiple testing, 40 proteins showed significant correlations with CSF EA levels (P = 1.5E − 9 to 4.0E − 5; Table 2). These proteins included cell adhesion molecules such as netrin, ephrin, neurexin, and fibronectin. The top-hit protein (r = 0.42, P = 1.5E − 9, n = 191) was CHL1 (UniProt accession number: O00533: neural cell adhesion molecule L1-like protein) [25].

To identify potential biological enrichment pathways related to CSF EA levels, we performed a gene ontology enrichment analysis using Metascape based on the 40 proteins identified as significantly correlated with CSF EA levels. The axon guidance pathway (including neuron projection guidance, axon development, axonogenesis, neuron projection development, nervous system development, and cell morphogenesis-related subpathways, such as cell morphogenesis involved in neuron differentiation, neuron projection morphogenesis, plasma membrane bounded cell projection morphogenesis, cell projection morphogenesis, and cell morphogenesis) was of particular high statistical significance (P = 3.4E − 20), followed by the response to elevated platelet cytosolic Ca2+ pathway (P = 8.2E − 12). Collectively, CSF EA signaling may be closely related to the pathways of neuronal axon development in the CNS.

Discussion

Our initial comparisons across psychiatric disorders to evaluate the specificity of CSF EA reduction revealed a statistically significant reduction not only in patients with MDD but also in patients with schizophrenia. However, after adjustment for medication, the difference between schizophrenic patients and controls was no longer significant, which may be due, at least in part, to the medications. Furthermore, the CSF EA levels correlated with HAM-D scores but not YMRS or BPRS scores, suggesting that the CSF EA reduction may be specific to MDD symptoms.

We obtained solid evidence for a correlation between the levels of CSF EA and the monoamine metabolites HVA and 5-HIAA. Serotonin and dopamine are widely known as crucial for regulating neuropsychological functions, such as emotion, motivation, and cognition [26, 27], and are also involved in pathophysiological brain networks in psychiatric disorders [28]. In a report on patients with MDD and a meta-analysis of previous studies that were performed by our study group, we found that patients with MDD showed reduced levels of CSF HVA [24, 29] but not CSF 5-HIAA or MHPG. Consistent with our previous observations, the statistical significance of the correlation between CSF HVA and EA levels in our current study was in the order of four digits stronger than the CSF 5-HIAA level, and the correlation coefficient for the former was higher than the latter (r: 0.40 vs. 0.26). Therefore, our results reinforced the robust association between CSF EA and HVA levels, suggesting the involvement of dopaminergic relationship in MDD pathophysiology.

Our investigation into whether treatment intervention, such as ECT, influenced CSF EA levels revealed a significant increase in CSF EA levels following treatment. Furthermore, the magnitude of changes in CSF EA levels was linked with treatment response in the HAM-D Somatic Anxiety subscale scores. Although this association was consistent with our previous report [8], the HAM-D total score did not reach statistical significance, which was thought to be due to the small size of sample set B (n = 13). Our results suggest that CSF EA may be a useful state-dependent marker of depressive symptoms. Previously, our research group reported that patients with MDD undergoing ECT treatment showed significant volume increases after sessions in several gray matter regions of the brain, such as the bilateral medial temporal cortices, inferior temporal cortices, and right anterior cingulate [30]. EA is a major component of phospholipids comprising the cell membrane covering the cell body, dendrites, synapses, and axons. Thus, increased CSF EA levels after ECT may contribute to the increased volume of gray matter.

We investigated whether our observation of reduced CSF EA levels in depressed patients [8] could be replicated using samples collected from four other institutions (set C). The results obtained further supported our evidence of the reduction of CSF EA levels in MDD. Although patients with other psychiatric disorders also showed lower CSF EA levels than controls in the sample set, the use of psychotropic medications might have confounded the obtained result as observed in sample set A, but we were unable to investigate this possibility due to the inaccuracy of the available medication information.

We used a rat model of depressive disorder to perform experiments investigating whether CSF EA could be a translatable biomarker. We observed significantly reduced CSF EA levels in rats that were repeatedly administered LPS and exhibited depressive-, anxiety-, and anhedonia-like behaviors. Therefore, CSF EA may be a useful biomarker for animal models of depression or the successful induction of depression-like pathology in animals. Moreover, we observed that rats orally administered EA for 4 weeks exhibited significant antidepressant-like behavior. This suggests that the oral intake of EA may have antidepressant-like properties and may be a candidate novel antidepressant. Although EA exhibits irritant properties in its pure chemical form, it has been demonstrated that it is not cytotoxic at food concentrations [31]. Further investigations are necessary to evaluate its safety and explore possible sources of natural ingredients or alternative forms, such as EA derivatives.

Finally, we conducted proteomics and bioinformatics analyses to elucidate the biological pathways likely related to CSF EA levels. The correlation analysis of CSF EA levels using SomaScan data revealed statistically significant results for 40 proteins related to neuronal functions, such as cell adhesion, axon guidance, growth factors, and various other factors. The top hit protein was CHL1, an extracellular matrix and cell adhesion protein that plays a role in nervous system development and synaptic plasticity [25]. Notably, CHL1 was suggested as a putative selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressant-response biomarker [32, 33]. Gene ontology enrichment analysis of the 40 proteins revealed enrichment of the axon guidance pathway, including axon-, neuron projection-, and cell morphogenesis-related subpathways, with conspicuously high levels of statistical significance. In fact, the correlation test results using SomaScan data included several netrin-, ephrin-, and cell adhesion-related molecules, all of which are synergistically involved in axon-related signals [34, 35]. Therefore, we hypothesize that CSF EA levels are related to signaling in the axonal development, and this may partly explain the regional increase in the brain gray matter volume after ECT [30]. However, because the axon-related subpathway contains several different factors and signaling molecules, further investigation of the detailed roles of CSF EA in that pathway is necessary.

A hybrid of the minimum ES (effect size) plus P value (MESP) approach described by Goodman et al. has been proposed [36] to preclude issues arising from differences between statistical and biological significance or relevance and the associated risk of false discovery in assessments of depression(-like) syndromes. Carvalho et al. [37] summarized the possible peripheral biomarkers for depressive disorder. The significant and low-biased peripheral markers included, for example, eicosapentaenoic acid (ES = −0.33), F2-isoprostanes (ES = 0.46), GDNF (ES = −0.68), oxidative stress (ES = −0.59), and sIL-2R (ES = 0.56), which represent small to medium effect sizes. Therefore, as a potential biomarker for MDD, the effect size of CSF EA may not be particularly large. However, we clarified its specificity to MDD (ES = −0.59) and its association with disease severity (ρ = −0.29). Additionally, patients with moderate-to-severe depression showed large effects regardless of psychotropic medication (ES = −1.27 to −1.14). Furthermore, we demonstrated that ECT significantly affects CSF EA levels (ES = 0.90), to a greater extent than previously reported plasma BDNF levels (ES = 0.72) [38]. Therefore, the advantages and strengths of CSF EA as a biomarker for MDD lie in its specificity for depression diagnosis and symptoms. Moreover, CSF EA may be specifically relevant to the mechanisms of depressive disorder. Further studies should be conducted to verify this.

The results of LPS administration in rats are comparable to those of a meta-analysis on the effect sizes of LPS-induced depression-like behavior in mice. Compared with a single administration of LPS (ES = 1.78), 5 days of 1 mg/kg LPS administration in C57BL/6 mice increased immobility time in the FST with a larger ES of 2.76 standardized mean difference (SMD) [39]. This finding supports the repeated administration paradigm of LPS. In our study, rats in the 7-day LPS administration model showed a large ES in immobility time in the FST (Cohen’s d = 1.52). Regarding the therapeutic effect of EA, Kryst et al. [40] found naïve mice did not show any behavioral alterations with chronic fluoxetine treatment (SMD = 0.01). Since our study revealed significant changes in immobility time on the FST with a large effect size in EA-treated naïve rats (ES = −1.36), the effect might be larger than that of fluoxetine or may reflect different biological mechanisms of depression. Furthermore, a large effect size was observed in chronic fluoxetine-treated stress-exposed animals (ES = −1.86) [40]. Therefore, the EA effect may be comparable to that of fluoxetine. Further studies using other depression-model animals will be necessary to verify this hypothesis.

A negative association may exist between CSF EA and depression severity (Table 1 and Supplementary Figs. S1–S3). In sample set B, patients who received ECT showed significantly higher CSF EA levels, while changes in the HAM-D Somatic Anxiety subscale scores were significantly and negatively correlated with changes in CSF EA levels, despite the small sample size. Moreover, a stratified analysis revealed that patients with mild depression who were not receiving psychotropic medication had significantly higher CSF EA levels than those with moderate-to-severe depression. Therefore, CSF EA may serve as a suitable state-dependent marker for depressive symptoms.

The results of this study indicate that EA is not strongly associated with central nervous system (CNS) inflammation. Thus, the decreased CSF EA levels in LPS-treated mice may not be directly caused by LPS exposure but rather by physiological pathways linked to depression-like behavior. First, representative pro-inflammatory cytokines in CSF, such as interleukin (IL)-1β (P = 0.35), IL-6 (P = 0.11), tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α (P = 0.081), and interferon-γ (P = 0.78), were not significantly correlated (i.e., P < 4.43 × 10⁻⁵) with CSF EA levels. Correlation analyses revealed significant associations between CSF EA and several TNF superfamily proteins. Notably, TNFSF12 (TWEAK) regulates diverse processes (e.g., cell proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, apoptosis, and inflammation) and exerts tissue-protective effects [41]. Similarly, although TNFRSF21 (death receptor 6; DR6) is pro-apoptotic, it promotes blood–brain barrier (BBB) formation during development [42]. Metascape gene ontology analysis identified APP, ECM1, FN1, and SERPINA1 as inflammation-related; however, their corresponding proteins also participate in multiple non-inflammatory pathways. CSF galectin-3 exhibited a near-significant negative association with EA (Table 2). Produced mainly by macrophages, galectin-3 mediates pro-inflammatory signaling and metabolic dysregulation, including impaired insulin action [43]. Conversely, TIMP3, an inhibitor of TNF-α–converting enzyme (TACE/ADAM17) and a regulator of TNF-α [44], was positively correlated with EA. Collectively, these findings indicate that any link between EA and inflammation is likely indirect and may involve metabolic intermediates, such as insulin resistance, a key feature common among psychiatric disorders. The potential role of EA in these integrative pathways thus warrants further investigation.

Among the proteins identified in our correlation analyses using proteome data, several interesting ones appear to be linked to BBB functions. For example, CSF EA was associated with CSF proteins, such as netrin, ephrin, ICAM, and ECM, which are important regulators of the BBB. Furthermore, CSF EA was also significantly correlated with CSF VEGF and TNFRSF21 (death receptor 6; DR6), a regulator of CNS-specific angiogenesis and an essential factor in VEGF-mediated JNK activation [45]. Although TNFRSF21 (DR6) is pro-apoptotic, it also promotes BBB formation during development. Conversely, its suppression impairs BBB integrity in the presence of amyloid-β25-35 oligomers [42]. Through the JNK pathway, TNFRSF21 (DR6) upregulates the expression of BBB functional proteins [42] and induces endothelial sprouting [45]. Therefore, our findings indicate the potential link of EA to BBB function. However, further studies are required to verify this.

The sources and fates of brain EA have not been fully elucidated. Because mammals cannot synthesize EA, it is obtained from dietary sources as free EA or in the form of EA-containing glycerophospholipids such as phosphatidylethanolamine, which is degraded to yield glycerol, fatty acids, and EA [13]. An in vitro study demonstrated that fibroblast cells with a homozygous mutation in SLC44A1 (also known as choline transporter-like protein 1, CTL1), which encodes a transporter for EA and choline, showed significantly diminished SLC44A1 expression and phosphatidylethanolamine accumulation [46]. CTL1 is expressed in endothelial cells at the BBB [47], and several ATP-binding cassette transporters regulate EA-containing phospholipids at the BBB [48]. Therefore, the total cerebral EA amount is affected by the transporter functions at the BBB, dietary content, and phospholipid metabolism.

A previous study on rats demonstrated that the intraperitoneal administration of EA hydrochloride significantly affected EA levels in the blood, brain tissue, and extracellular fluid, as well as brain phospholipid levels [49]. This implies that EA from peripheral sources permeates the BBB and is associated with phospholipid metabolism in the brain considering that we obtained a similar result in this study. Notably, EA itself is caustic, so a safer form is required to avoid the possibility of harmful side effects caused by its supplementation when developing a new therapeutic approach that could be used in daily life in humans. Foods such as cheese, wine, fermented vegetables, egg yolk, and fish contain relatively plentiful amounts of EA [50–53], and these amounts are all lower than the lowest concentration reported as cytotoxic [31]. EA can be derived from sources in the human body, such as the degradation of EA-containing phospholipids [13, 54] and degradation of N-acylethanolamines [55], which are cell membrane components and lipid mediators.

EA-containing phospholipids are the major lipid components of myelin [56], which ensheathes neuronal axons. Furthermore, EA phospholipids account for 14.9% and 22.7% of lipids in the white and gray matter of the brain, respectively [56]. Phospholipid-related mechanisms with reduced CSF EA levels may be promising, and the results of this study may be attributed to the signaling in neuronal structural components such as myelin and axon. We recently reported reduced EA phospholipid levels in the peripheral circulation of patients with mood disorders [57], and the findings of our collaborative group also supported the evidence of altered phospholipid dynamics in patients with MDD by focusing on lysophosphatidic acid signaling [58, 59]. Thus, EA-containing phospholipids may also be related to MDD pathophysiology.

Molecular biosciences have slowly but steadily elucidated the function of EA, including the identification of novel EA transporters [60]. Lipids and bioactive molecules that contain ethanolamine have attracted increasing attention for their various roles in diverse body and brain functions. The relationships between the biological functions of EA and the mechanisms of depression have psychiatric relevance. By combining evidence obtained from clinical settings and basic studies, the roles of EA in the pathophysiology of MDD may be more effectively elucidated. However, an interpretive gap remains between the two fields. Bridging this gap, by making use of EA as a translatable marker, could clarify as yet unknown functions of EA in depression. Promoting translational research would effectively establish the synergistic link between basic and clinical evidence. Such an approach would further validate EA signaling as a suitable therapeutic target in MDD.

This study has several limitations. First, we were unable to examine the associations between CSF EA and diet or exercise in the human sample sets because such detailed information were not collected from the participants. Furthermore, BMI data and other demographic and clinical information, such as disease severity or medication status, were lacking in sample set C. These parameters, apart from BMI, may also influence EA levels. Thus, the associations between EA levels and these detailed variables, including basic lifestyle parameters, should be extensively investigated in future studies. Second, we used only male rats in our animal experiments. However, recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have provided quantitative insights. In behavioral measures of anxiety, although the estrous stage significantly affects both rats and mice, anxiety-like behavior is higher during metestrus/diestrus (lower ovarian hormones) than during proestrus (higher ovarian hormones; Hedges’s g = 0.44 in rats; g = 0.43 in mice); however, these effect sizes are considered small [61]. The same group also reported that male rodents show greater variability than unstaged females [62], indicating that estrous-cycle staging is not essential for reducing behavioral variability, as males exhibit greater variability than females regardless of staging [61, 62]. Nevertheless, the estrous cycle remains a crucial variable in essential female physiological processes. When it may influence the data, the underlying sources of the ensuing variability must be identified. Sex-specific physiological factors may also influence EA. For instance, the FST demonstrated that female rats exhibited a stronger response to lower doses of antidepressants than males [63]. Hence, future studies should evaluate both sexes to clarify the effects of EA on depression-like behavior, which is further justified by the higher prevalence of depressive disorders in women than in men. Third, in the animal study in Experiment 2, although EA demonstrated possible antidepressant-like properties in the FST, the behavioral variables in the OFT and SPT did not differ from those observed in the control animals. Thus, CNS EA may be mainly related to depression-like behaviors. Furthermore, we used naïve rats in Experiment 2. Porsolt et al., who developed the FST paradigm, also used naïve rats in their initial study [64] to screen for antidepressant-like properties, which has since been replicated in subsequent studies [40]. Notably, the forced swim paradigm itself is considered a model for inducing depression-like behavior that explored the concepts of “learned helplessness” and “behavioral despair” [65]. Therefore, the FST may serve as a behavioral test for depression-like phenotypes, as applied in Experiment 1 of the current study, and simultaneously represents an inherent animal model of depression. Thus, we cautiously propose that the obtained results partly reflect antidepressant-like effects. However, we believe that additional depression models such as chronic corticosterone treatment or restraint stress models, should be employed to further examine the efficacy of EA administration in future studies. Fourth, the use of animal models for depression-like behavior has inherent limitations. Currently, no consensus or universal standard has been established regarding the definition, composition, or duration of the observable behavioral phenomena that should be termed “depressive-like” in laboratory animals. The Research Domain Criteria framework developed by the National Institute of Mental Health provides a conceptual basis for cross-species translation based on the shared phylogenetic origin and the interspecies conservation of biological and neuroanatomical structures necessary for complex emotions, cognition, and behaviors [6]. This framework also hypothesizes that depressive behaviors are at least partially comparable between species such as humans and rodents [7]. Based on this concept, von Mücke-Heim et al. [66] proposed an algorithm for assessing depression-like syndromes in translational neuropsychiatry using the following criteria: (i) a minimum duration of phenotype (i.e., 10 months in humans can be translated to 52–176.5 h in mice); (ii) significant socio-functional impairment; (iii) core biological features; and (iv) necessary depressive-like symptoms. These criteria should ideally be combined with a statistical algorithm that applies a minimum threshold for both statistical significance and effect size (P ≤ 0.05, together with a moderate effect size) to define each depression-like syndrome criterion [66]. This multidimensional approach, which also considers the effect size may facilitate the development of a more valid, reliable, and reproducible syndrome definition and phenotyping standard for animal depression models. However, although the sucrose preference test, FST, and other anxiety-related tests conducted in our study all showed statistical significance and large effect sizes, they were limited in scope. Future studies should thus incorporate such systematic, multidimensional approaches to generate more robust evidence. Finally, we obtained evidence of CSF EA reduction in patients with MDD, the underlying mechanisms of which may be linked to axonal signaling pathways. However, the pathophysiological role of EA in depression remains unclear. Although correlative findings have revealed possible mechanisms linking CSF EA with biological pathways, further studies involving experimental manipulation that lowers EA levels are needed. Key targets include known EA transporters, such as CTL1 (SLC44A1), an EA transporter expressed at the blood–brain barrier (BBB), as well as the more recently identified feline leukemia virus subgroup C receptor-related proteins 1 and 2 (FLVCR1 and FLVCR2) [60]. Manipulating these transporters could control regional EA concentrations, enabling the direct study of the effects and roles of EA, including those on behavior and neuronal structures. Furthermore, the in vivo dynamics of EA, particularly its distribution and accumulation in the body, may be elucidated through radioisotope labeling and positron emission tomography studies. Obtaining such macroscopic and microscopic data is essential for achieving a complete understanding of the roles of EA in the body.

In conclusion, we obtained convincing evidence of low CSF EA levels in patients with MDD in two independent sample sets. We also demonstrated that CSF EA levels were related to brain monoaminergic systems, particularly to the dopamine metabolite HVA. CSF EA levels are state-dependent (i.e., restoration of levels after ECT) and are associated with axonal development pathways in the brain. Moreover, we showed that CSF EA can be a translatable marker in a rat model of LPS-induced depression and highlighted the possibility of EA administration as a candidate novel antidepressant treatment. However, the causality and pathophysiological roles of EA in depression should be investigated in future studies, with a focus on several bioactive molecules, such as phospholipids and lipid mediators that are related to axonal and myelin functions, as well as on the manipulation of transporters, distribution, and dynamics of EA in the body. Furthermore, CSF EA may be a novel research tool for developing a new classification framework for psychiatric disorders and novel therapeutic strategies.
