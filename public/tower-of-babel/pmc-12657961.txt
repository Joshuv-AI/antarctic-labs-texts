Soil total nitrogen prediction based on multi-temporal synthetic Sentinel-2 images under different land use types
Authors: Yi-lin Ouyang, Wei Wu, Hong-bin Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTotal soil nitrogen (TN) is a key indicator for plant growth, and its accurate prediction is crucial for agricultural production and environmental management. This study aims to enhance prediction accuracy of TN by integrating multi-temporal synthetic imagery with environmental data, combined with machine learning methods. Seven single-temporal Sentinel-2 images from 2019 to 2020 were acquired, and four types of multi-temporal synthetic imagery were generated using maximum, minimum, mean, and median synthesis methods. Spectral bands and vegetation indices were separately extracted from these images. Extreme gradient boosting algorithm was then employed to predict TN content using these derived data from remote sensing, topographic and climatic data at four spatial resolutions (10 m, 20 m, 30 m and 60 m). Results indicate significant model performance variations across land use types: single-temporal imagery achieved highest prediction accuracy in orchards (R² = 0.73), while multi-temporal mean synthetic imagery performed best in dry land (R² = 0.63) and paddy fields (R² = 0.61). Spectral bands (particularly B8 and B11) were identified as the most critical predictive variables. This study demonstrates the application value of multi-temporal remote sensing synthesis technology in soil total nitrogen monitoring, providing a technical pathway for advancing precision agriculture and sustainable land management.Keywords: Multi-temporal synthetic, Sentinel-2 images, Extreme gradient boosting, Soil property prediction, Land useSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

As a fundamental part of ecosystems, soil delivers key nutrients for plants1. Among them, soil nitrogen is indispensable for plant growth, fertility maintenance and biomass accumulation2,3. Therefore, the balance of soil nitrogen content is crucial. Nitrogen deficiency can severely limit crop yield and quality, threatening food security, whereas excess nitrogen often leads to serious environmental consequences, including eutrophication of water bodies and emissions of nitrogen-based greenhouse gases4. Soil total nitrogen (TN) as a measure of soil nitrogen content directly reflects the total storage and supply potential of nitrogen1. Hence, understanding its spatial distribution is a prerequisite for informed decision-making. Consequently, effective monitoring of TN content and the accurate, efficient acquisition of its spatial distribution information—soil TN mapping—are crucial for precision agricultural management and environmental sustainability. Traditional soil mapping relies primarily on field surveys, which are time-consuming and costly5. Thus, there is an urgent need to identify a more efficient and reliable method for predicting the spatial variation of soil TN. Reflectance spectroscopy can solve this challenge to a certain extent.

Scholars typically employ laboratory spectroscopy to predict soil total nitrogen content under highly controlled conditions6,7. However, since this method excludes the influence of key field variables such as moisture, illumination, and vegetation cover, its application remains confined to small-scale studies6,8. Consequently, models developed under idealized laboratory conditions often prove difficult to generalize to large-scale soil monitoring scenarios where these confounding factors are present. In contrast, remote sensing technology operates under actual field conditions, providing practical solutions for regional-scale assessments9.

Remote sensing technology provides robust support for soil property research through its extensive coverage and continuous monitoring capabilities10. Nevertheless, in densely vegetated areas, thick canopy layers make it difficult to directly obtain soil spectral signals for soil property inversion. This challenge can be addressed by employing vegetation indices as proxy indicators: since vegetation physiology is influenced by soil nitrogen content11, these indices can indirectly reflect the spatial distribution patterns of soil total nitrogen12–14. To further enhance prediction accuracy, environmental auxiliary variables that influence soil property changes (e.g., topography and climate) can be incorporated for comprehensive analysis15–17.

Traditional single-temporal remote sensing imagery is susceptible to short-term fluctuations, making it difficult to capture stable spectral characteristics of soil properties18. Multi-temporal remote sensing data overcomes this by constructing long-term sequences19. This approach captures seasonal patterns and vegetation dynamics, thus significantly enhancing prediction accuracy and reliability20–22. Currently, the application of multi-temporal data is becoming increasingly widespread. However, most studies still focus on analyzing individual images from different moments. In contrast, multi-temporal synthetic imagery offers a new approach: performing statistical operations (such as taking the median or mean) on all temporal observations at each pixel location to generate a novel synthetic image that comprehensively reflects the overall condition across the entire period. This approach compresses and integrates complex temporal information into a unified data layer. Luo et al.18 found that the median synthetic image in the prediction of SOM showed the highest accuracy. However, the applicability of this approach to prediction of other soil properties need further explored and investigated.

In addition, model resolution has a significant impact on the application of remotely sensed data in soil property mapping. Different resolutions offer distinct advantages: high-resolution data excels at capturing spatial heterogeneity in complex terrain, while medium-to-low resolution imagery better characterizes macroscale patterns across large areas23–25. Therefore, multi-scale data comparison is essential for determining the optimal resolution for model development25.

Land use changes significantly affect soil TN content. Changes in land use types can alter soil properties and the quantity of soil organic matter26,27, subsequently affecting soil nitrogen components, and ultimately exerting significant impacts on soil nitrogen cycling as well as the content and distribution of TN. Therefore, we hypothesize that modeling different land use types separately can improve the prediction accuracy of soil nitrogen.

To achieve the goal of digital soil mapping of soil properties, the methods commonly employed by researchers include traditional geostatistical methods and machine learning approaches. Compared to traditional geostatistical methods such as Kriging interpolation, machine learning approaches are better suited for handling nonlinear relationships and can effectively overcome the issue of insufficient prediction accuracy in complex terrain19. Among numerous machine learning approaches, the XGBoost algorithm is highly favored for its outstanding performance. This algorithm not only efficiently handles high-dimensional features but also effectively suppresses overfitting through regularization techniques, thereby enhancing model generalization capabilities8,28,29. Therefore, this study adopts XGBoost as the core modeling method.

To overcome challenges posed by vegetation cover, this study introduces an innovative approach utilizing multi-temporal synthetic imagery, employing vegetation indices as key proxy variables. This method establishes an indirect inversion mechanism linking “soil nitrogen content – vegetation growth – spectral response.” This study aims to (1) investigate improvement in prediction accuracy achieved through single land use type modeling and identify reasons for model performance variations across different land use types; (2) evaluate the feasibility of multi-scale modeling and multi-temporal synthetic imagery techniques for predicting soil total nitrogen; (3) analyze differences in the importance of predictive variables across various land-use types. The results will offer effective support for the application of multi-temporal remote sensing data in agricultural and environmental management.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study area is located in Kaizhou District, Chongqing, southwestern China(Fig. 1). It lies on the eastern edge of the Sichuan Basin (107°55’48’’−108°36’45’’E, 30°49’33’’−31°17’ 44’’), and covers 2059.51 km2. Its topography is complex, characterized by hilly and mountainous terrain with continuous mountain ranges, presenting a significant altitude difference from 111 to 1356 m(Fig. 1a). Climate type is subtropical monsoon humid climate, featuring four distinct seasons: warm winters with early springs, hot and humid summers, and cool, dry autumns. Land use types are diverse, with orchards, dry fields, and paddy fields being the primary types. Specifically, orchards cover an area of 83.95 km2, accounting for 4.08%; dry fields cover 435.43 km2, accounting for 21.14%; and paddy fields occupy 197.11 km2, representing 9.57% of total area (Fig. 1c).

Soil collection and preparation

According to the relevant standards of DZ/T0295-2016 Specification of Land Quality Geochemical Assessment, 329 topsoil soil samples were collected from cultivated land in Kaizhou District, Chongqing Municipality in 2019. Among these, 54 samples from orchard, 133 from dry land, and 142 from paddy field. (Fig. 1a)During the sampling process, an “S”-shaped pattern was used for site layout. Within a 50-meter radius around each sampling point, five surface soil samples at a depth of 0–20 cm were collected in a radial distribution30.The land use type and soil type at each sub-sampling point were basically consistent. These samples were then mixed into one composite sample, with a total weight exceeding 1.5 kg, and placed into a specialized sampling bag. The collected soil samples were air-dried in the laboratory, cleared of debris and stones, then ground and passed through a 2 mm sieve before being bottled and stored for soil property analysis. The soil total nitrogen was determined using the Kjeldahl acid-digestion method31.

Data acquisition and processing

Sentinel-2 image

Sentinel-2 Images Collection and Preprocessing.

Sentinel-2 Images Collection and Preprocessing.

The study area has diverse elevation levels and slope variations, cloud cover may obscure critical areas, increasing the difficulty of identifying land features such as vegetation coverage and land use types.To ensure the acquisition of high-quality remote sensing images and guarantee the reliability and accuracy of the subsequent data analysis, we screened and adopted Sentinel-2 multi-temporal satellite images with less than 10% cloud cover and similar time sampling. Due to persistent high cloud cover in the region, only seven valid images were successfully acquired, spanning the period from 2019 to 2020 and covering all stages of vegetation growth. The specific acquisition dates are as follows: April 24, 2019; June 3, 2019; August 17, 2019; September 26, 2019; December 10, 2019; March 19, 2020; and November 14, 2020. All data were obtained from European Space Agency (ESA) platforms.

Each image includes 13 spectral bands with resolutions ranging from 10 to 60 m. The images are Level-2 A (L2A) products, which means they have already undergone atmospheric correction and do not require further processing. For each image, three bands with 60 m spatial resolution (B1, B9, B10) were removed, retaining the higher-resolution 10 m bands (B2, B3, B4, B8) and 20 m bands (B5, B6, B7, B8A, B11, B12) (Table 1). In SNAP software, bilinear interpolation was used to resample the retained 10 m and 20 m bands to resolutions of 10 m, 20 m, 30 m, and 60 m, respectively.

Multi-temporal Synthetic Images.

To comprehensively capture spectral-temporal features associated with soil total nitrogen changes, we constructed a multi-temporal dataset. For each retained spectral band, four new images were synthesized by calculating the maximum, minimum, mean, and median values of each pixel across seven observation dates from 2019 to 2020. This process ultimately formed a composite dataset containing the original seven temporal images and four synthesized images.

Spectral Indices: Using SNAP software, twenty-one spectral indices were calculated for each of the eleven images (seven original images and four Synthetic images). These included four soil radiometric indices, fifteen vegetation radiometric indices, and two water radiometric indices(Table 2). The indices selection references prior research whose environmental conditions closely resemble those of the study area8,23.

Topographic, climatic and land use data

A 30 m×30 m grid Digital Elevation Model (DEM) was obtained from NASA Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) 1 Arc-Second Global (https://www.usgs.gov/). Based on the acquired DEM data, the following topographic auxiliary variables were calculated in SAGA GIS: Elevation, Slope, Aspect, Valley Depth (VD), Topographic Wetness Index (TWI), Length-Slope Factor (LS-Factor) and Channel Network Base Level (CNBL).

The monthly mean climate data from 2010 to 2019 used in this study contains mean temperature (T), mean precipitation (P), mean surface soil moisture (SM) and mean evapotranspiration (ET) with a spatial resolution of 1 km. The dataset was provided by National Tibetan Plateau (http://data.tpdc.ac.cn). These variables are fundamental drivers of soil biogeochemical processes which directly influence the spatial distribution of soil total nitrogen.

The land use data were extracted from the 1:10,000 scale land use map of 2019 obtained from the Ministry of Natural Resources of the People’s Republic of China (http://www.mnr.gov.cn, accessed on 13 August 2024). Significant ecological differences across various land use types (such as cropland and orchards) represent one of the primary drivers of spatial heterogeneity in soil nitrogen.

Statistical analysis

The differences in total nitrogen content across various land use types were analyzed using one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and multiple comparisons (least significant difference test, LSD), with a confidence interval of 0.05. The above statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS V. 25 software.

Modeling process

In this study, multi-temporal Sentinel-2 images were synthesized based on maximum, minimum, mean, and median values. For seven single-temporal Sentinel-2 images and four composited images, we constructed a predictor variable dataset containing topographic and climatic indices, band data and spectral indices. Models with twelve different input combinations were established separately for orchards, dry land, paddy fields, and total study area (Table 3).

Modeling techniques

the eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) is a highly efficient machine learning algorithm based on the Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT) framework32, renowned for its exceptional predictive performance and computational efficiency33. This algorithm effectively prevents overfitting by incorporating a regularization term into its objective function34 and achieves rapid convergence through second-order Taylor approximation optimization35. This study employs XGBoost to construct a soil total nitrogen prediction model in Python 3.9.

To identify key environmental drivers, we utilize its built-in “gain” metric to quantify the relative importance of individual features.This metric effectively characterizes the relative importance of predictive variables by measuring the average reduction in loss achieved through feature splitting36.

Model performance assessment

In the XGBoost model, we selected four parameters: learning rate (Eta), maximum tree depth (Max_Depth), minimum child weight (Min_Child_Weight), and regression lambda (Lambda). Their ranges were determined based on prior research8,23(Table 4). We randomly selected 80% of the samples as the training set and 20% as the test set. Five-fold cross-validation was performed on the training set to optimize the model parameters. Specifically, the training set was divided into five folds, with four folds successively used for training and parameter tuning, while the remaining fold was reserved as the validation set. After five iterations, the optimal parameters were determined based on the average performance on the validation set.

Image synthesis

The results of different image synthesis modes (maximum, minimum, mean, and median synthesis) are shown in Fig. 3. Results from different synthesis methods exhibit significant visual differences. The imagery from (a) maximum synthesis and (b) minimum synthesis show the greatest tonal variation, indicating their outcomes are heavily dependent on specific surface conditions during particular phases and thus fail to effectively represent typical spectral characteristics across the entire period. In contrast, (c) mean synthesis and (d) median synthesis achieve a visual balance, with tones falling between the two extremes. Mean synthesis preserves richer surface texture details, while median synthesis yields smoother, more homogeneous results. Both methods effectively capture the most representative stable surface conditions by integrating multi-temporal information. Furthermore, we observed that maximum synthesis and mean synthesis are susceptible to the influence of clouds and shadows, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies18. Although scattered cloud cover exists in both images, it is confined to non-agricultural areas. All soil sampling points in this study were located within cloud-free agricultural fields, ensuring the reliability of modeling data.

The most significant finding is that models trained on individual land use types consistently demonstrated significantly higher prediction accuracy than models trained on the total area (maximum R² values of 0.73, 0.63, and 0.61 for orchard, dry land, and paddy field respectively, compared to 0.44 for the total area model). Prediction errors (MAE, RMSE) for models tailored to specific land use types were also significantly lower. For example, the MAE of the optimal orchard model (0.09 g/kg) and RMSE (0.11 g/kg) are both significantly lower than those of the full-area model (MAE: 0.25 g/kg, RMSE: 0.33 g/kg).

This conclusion is robustly supported by the scatter plots of Fig. 5, which detail the relationship between predicted and actual values for the optimal models. Overall, data points across all scatter plots cluster near the 1:1 true line, indicating high predictive accuracy. The consistent distribution of data points between training and test sets confirms the models’ strong generalization capabilities. Moreover, the performance gap between training and test sets remained minimal across all scenarios. This demonstrates that model parameter optimization via five-fold cross-validation effectively prevented overfitting, confirming the model’s reliable predictive capability on unseen data.

In addition, accuracy of the predictions varies among models constructed at different resolutions. The prediction effectiveness also varies under different parameter combinations. For example, in the orchard environment, Model D, constructed using parameters extracted from a single image captured in August 2019, demonstrated the best predictive performance, achieving test R² of 0.73 and MAE of 0.09 g/kg. In contrast, Model J based on multi-temporal mean synthesis imagery delivered the best performance in dry land and paddy field, achieving test R² values of 0.63 and 0.61, respectively, with MAE of 0.16 and 0.21 g/kg. Model J also produced the optimal results for total area prediction (test R² = 0.44, MAE = 0.25 g/kg). The twelve models established in this study can be categorized into three types, namely: single-temporal image data model, multi-temporal synthetic image data model and, multi-temporal image data model. The prediction accuracy of single-temporal models varies significantly. For instance, at 10 m resolution, the best R² value for orchard single-temporal models reached 0.73, while the worst was only 0.32. However, multi-temporal synthesis models demonstrated higher and more stable prediction accuracy, particularly the mean and median synthesis approaches. Among the five multi-temporal synthesis models tested, the mean synthesis model achieved the highest accuracy and stability, followed by the median synthesis model, while the minimum synthesis model performed the worst. Notably, transitioning from the best single-temporal model to the optimal multi-temporal synthesis model led to enhanced prediction accuracy and reduced errors, especially for dry land and paddy field: For dry land, R2 increased by 0.14,while MAE and RMSE decreased by 0.02 g/kg and 0.04 g/kg separately. For paddy field, R2 increased by 0.11,while MAE and RMSE decreased by 0.05 g/kg and 0.02 g/kg separately.

Discussion

Comparison of model performance

Impact of different land use types on TN content prediction

This study established independent prediction models for orchards, dry fields, and paddy fields, respectively. Results indicate that modeling for a single land use type significantly outperforms unified models covering all land use types. In addition, the three land use types differed in prediction accuracy, with orchard prediction accuracy significantly higher than that of dry land and paddy field. This indicates that TN prediction accuracy varies with land use type41.

Across different land use types, significant variations in topographic features, vegetation structure, and management practices result in distinct spatial distributions of soil nitrogen and vegetation spectral response mechanisms42–44. Addressing this characteristic, this study employs separate modeling approaches for each land use type. The core advantage of this method lies in its ability to adjust and optimize model parameters based on the environmental traits of different land categories, thereby significantly enhancing inversion accuracy. Conversely, unified modeling approaches, unable to effectively coordinate the inherent heterogeneity across land types, attempt to characterize all land use systems with a single model. This often obscures the relationship between key characteristics and nitrogen content, resulting in increased prediction bias.

The study area features complex hilly and mountainous topography with intricate terrain conditions and pronounced monsoon climate characteristics. This geographical context profoundly influences the coupling relationships among topography, climate, vegetation, and soil across different land use types, leading to significant variations in TN prediction accuracy between orchards, dry fields, and paddy fields. This difference is primarily attributed to the following reasons:

Orchard prediction accuracy was highest, primarily benefiting from its superior topography and vegetation conditions. Orchard areas predominantly occupy gentle slopes with minimal topographic variation and reduced erosion, resulting in relatively stable spatial nitrogen distribution. Furthermore, the study area primarily cultivates citrus, a single crop type with uniform vegetation structure and continuous canopy cover, yielding high-quality spectral signals45.

Prediction accuracy for dry land was relatively lower due to significant challenges from topography, precipitation, and vegetation variability. It extensively distributed across hilly slopes, where steep gradients exacerbate soil erosion and nitrogen loss46. Crop rotation systems cause continuous changes in vegetation cover and types. Single-temporal model fitting results indicate significantly higher accuracy in March and April compared to August and September. This discrepancy arises from increased summer precipitation promoting nitrogen migration, thereby amplifying uncertainty47.

Although paddy fields are predominantly located in flat terrain, they are influenced by monsoon rainfall and seasonal flooding management. In March and April, fields are either dry or have only shallow water, effectively capturing soil and seedling information. However, significantly increased rainfall in August and September intensifies spatial heterogeneity and dynamic changes in nitrogen through leaching, further reducing model prediction accuracy47. Concurrently, this period features the deepest flooding in paddy fields, where water absorption and reflection interfere with the effective extraction of nitrogen indicator signals48. Consequently, prediction performance in paddy fields is relatively poor.

This study preliminary demonstrates that using remote sensing data to separately model orchard, dry land, and paddy field can achieve higher prediction accuracy. However, further research is needed to verify whether this method is applicable to other land use types and larger regions.

Application of multi-scale modeling in TN prediction

In this study, we constructed predictive models at multiple spatial scales to estimate TN content in orchard, dry lands, and paddy field. The results indicate that predictive capabilities of models vary across different modeling resolutions. Overall, prediction accuracy exhibits a gradient decline as the resolution decreases. Specifically, the model achieves its highest prediction accuracy at 10 m resolution and its poorest performance at 60 m resolution. This performance disparity primarily stems from the inevitable information loss and noise introduction during the downsampling process49. It is important to note that the 10 m resolution dataset includes components upsampled from the 20 m band. The upsampling process does not generate new spatial details or information. Therefore, the performance advantage of the 10 m resolution data set does not stem from details created by upsampling. Rather, it arises from the fact that this data set maximally preserves the spectral integrity of the native high-resolution bands. Furthermore, all bands are uniformly mapped onto a grid with optimal information density, resulting in minimal degradation. When data is downsampled to coarser resolutions, the pixel blending effect causes irreversible loss of fine-grained information during pixel aggregation while introducing data noise. This results in coarse model input features and obscures the influence of terrain and vegetation50. These factors collectively contribute to the gradient decline in prediction performance. However, It is worth noting that the difference in accuracy between 10 m and 20 m resolution is minimal at the overall regional scale compared to modeling each land use type individually. This phenomenon may be related to diversity of land use types, complexity of topography, and larger regional scale across total area51,52. Specific reasons may include the fact that this significant difference in accuracy in individual land types stems primarily from the ability of high-resolution data to accurately capture small-scale features (e.g., differences in fruit tree monoculture fertilizer application, dry land strip-seeding patterns, paddy field ridge effects, etc.). However, for large regions, TN prediction is relatively influenced by macro factors such as climate, topography and land use type, and 20 m resolution can adequately characterize these large-scale features. Therefore, when extended to full-region scale modeling, the accuracy advantage of 10 m resolution is significantly reduced. Consequently, when selecting modeling resolutions, it is necessary to consider the specific study area and objectives, balancing the relationship between local details and overall characteristics to achieve optimal prediction performance.

It must be acknowledged that while upsampling 20 m band data to 10 m resolution is a necessary step in constructing spatially aligned multi-scale datasets, it is fundamentally a methodological compromise. This process does not generate new spatial information and carries the risk of overestimating spatial resolution confidence in quantitative analyses. Future research can circumvent this limitation by employing data fusion techniques that integrate genuinely high-resolution data or by strictly conducting analyses at the original resolution of the bands.

Feasibility of multi-temporal synthetic images in TN prediction

Remote sensing images capture surface information at a specific moment, and data from different moments may exhibit varying spectral characteristics due to differences in lighting, crop and vegetation growth stages, and soil moisture conditions. Vegetation growth status is closely related to soil TN content, and vegetation growth exhibits distinct seasonality53. Multi-temporal data, by integrating information from multiple moments, can capture the dynamic changes in vegetation growth, eliminate seasonal biases inherent in single-temporal data, and provide more comprehensive and richer spectral information54. This study compared effectiveness of single-temporal and multi-temporal remote sensing information in predicting soil TN content. The results show that, for dry land, paddy field, and at the total area scale, the best model prediction accuracy using multi-temporal data is higher than that using single-temporal data. This demonstrates feasibility of utilizing multi-temporal remote sensing information to improve soil TN content prediction. However, this approach is not universally applicable across all land use types. For example, in orchard, the best model for predicting TN content is based on single-temporal remote sensing data, specifically Sentinel-2 images acquired in August 2019. This phenomenon may be related to the unique characteristics of orchards, vegetation growth cycles, and spectral features. August is typically a stable period after fertilization in orchards, during which the spatial distribution of soil TN content is relatively stable and easier to predict using remote sensing images. More importantly, in most orchards, August is the fruit expansion or ripening stage, when vegetation coverage is high, and the leaf area index reaches its peak55. The spectral characteristics of orchard vegetation (e.g., near-infrared reflectance) exhibit the strongest correlation with soil TN content. It indicates that selecting appropriate remote sensing data time points is crucial for improving soil TN content prediction accuracy across different land use types.

In this study, synthetic image construction technique was used in processing multi-temporal remote sensing data, aiming to improve correlation between TN content and band and optical indices. According to the results in Fig. 4, mean synthetic model showed the best performance in terms of accuracy in dry land, paddy field and total area, followed by the median synthetic model, while the maximum and minimum synthetic models as well as the full-parameter combination model had relatively low accuracy. This finding is similar to the results of previous studies: Lou et al. 18 similarly observed in their study on the prediction of soil organic matter content using Landsat-8 imagery that the median synthesis method performed the best, followed by the mean synthesis, while the extreme value synthesis method was less effective. Similarly, Gasmi et al. 56 confirmed that the model constructed based on the mean spectral reflectance of the bare period imagery exhibited optimal performance in the prediction of soil clay content. Together, these consistent findings suggest that image synthesis methods based on centralized trend statistics (especially mean and median) can effectively suppress noise interference and retain stable surface feature information, thus significantly improving the accuracy of soil property prediction models. In contrast, the extreme value synthesis method is prone to introduce noise interference due to its over-sensitivity to outliers, leading to a decrease in prediction performance. This pattern was verified in the prediction tasks of different soil attributes (total nitrogen, organic matter, and clay content), which highlights the importance of the choice of synthesis methods for remote sensing soil prediction.

Importance of predictor variables

Topography directly regulates the distribution and accumulation of soil properties by influencing the transport of water, solutes and sediments57. Climatic factors further influence the dynamics of total soil N by modulating biogeochemical cycling processes58,59. Despite the significant association of topography and climate with TN content, model predictions relying solely on these environmental variables are still of limited accuracy. When optical remote sensing image bands and spectral indices were introduced into the model, the model’s accuracy significantly improved60,61. Unlike topography and climate factors that directly drive the spatial distribution of soil total nitrogen, remote sensing imagery provides an indirect yet powerful means to infer total nitrogen content by capturing the effects of soil nitrogen on vegetation. Sentinel-2 multispectral bands can detect specific surface properties62. The spectral indices derived from these bands, particularly vegetation indices, exhibit high sensitivity to vegetation cover, biomass, and health status—parameters strongly regulated by soil nitrogen content13. Therefore, we employ remote sensing signals as surrogate indicators of vegetation response to soil total nitrogen conditions. The original bands and spectral indices complement each other to comprehensively characterize land surface conditions63. When integrated with direct drivers such as topography and climate factors, they significantly enhance models’ predictive power for soil total nitrogen content.

In this study, the contributions of three land categories—bands B8 and B11—are relatively high. This is attributed to their complementarity and universality in reflecting vegetation information and soil characteristics. The B8 band indicates soil nitrogen by directly reflecting vegetation biomass and health status, while the B11 band indirectly reflects soil nitrogen through soil organic matter and moisture conditions64. This synergistic effect makes B8 and B11 key factors in predicting soil total nitrogen content.

However, due to differing ground conditions across three types of land use, the importance of predictive variables varies within models. For instance, in orchards, temperature had a very significant importance, scoring second only to B8. Orchard ecosystems include abundant organic matter inputs and complex root secretions. In orchards, large amounts of organic matter are imported annually through leaf litter, pruning debris, and fruit drop, and the rate of decomposition of this organic matter is highly dependent on temperature65. At the same time, fruit tree roots secrete a variety of organic acids and enzymes, which are temperature-sensitive and directly stimulate microbial nitrogen transformation activity66. Therefore, temperature is particularly important for predicting total nitrogen (TN) content in orchards, and its contribution is higher than in dry land and paddy fields. In contrast, in dry land systems, precipitation is the important factor predicting total nitrogen content. This is because key nitrogen cycling processes—such as nitrogen leaching and runoff losses—are primarily regulated by precipitation47. While water stress may promote aerobic microbial activity under certain conditions, precipitation-driven nitrogen losses and turnover processes exert an overwhelming influence on the total nitrogen pool, thereby masking the independent contributions of other climatic factors (such as temperature) in the model. In dry land, GEMI contributes more because it effectively distinguishes between soil backgrounds and vegetation information in regions with low vegetation cover67. In such environments, GEMI better represents the relationship between vegetation and soil nitrogen. Paddy soils, which are often saturated, benefit most from TSAVI, as it efficiently overcomes spectral interference caused by high moisture levels and low vegetation cover68, making it the highest scoring method in paddy fields. Among terrain factors, Aspect only influences TN predictions in orchards but has little to no impact on dry fields and paddy fields. Aspect affects TN prediction in orchards by modulating light, temperature, and water distribution, significantly influencing vegetation growth and soil nitrogen content. Conversely, in dry fields and paddy fields, the influence of aspect is diminished by low vegetation cover and high moisture levels, resulting in negligible effects on TN predictions. These differences highlight the complexity of terrain, climate, vegetation, and soil conditions across different land use types, as well as their unique mechanisms affecting spatial distribution of soil TN content.

Conclusion

This study investigated feasibility of using multi-scale modeling and multi-temporal synthetic remote sensing images to predict soil TN content in a vegetated region. The study was based on single-temporal Sentinel-2 optical satellite images, synthesized them based on maximum, minimum, mean and median values, and extracted their band data and spectral indices, and constructed a prediction model together with auxiliary DEM data and climatic data. The study also analyzed influence of land use types on soil TN prediction and importance of optical data under different land use types. The results show that the highest accuracy was achieved using single-temporal image for orchard, and mean multi-temporal synthetic images for dry land and paddy field. Among three land use types, orchard prediction accuracy (R2 = 0.73) > dry land (R2 = 0.63) > paddy field (R2 = 0.61) > total area (R2 = 0.42), and the contribution of the importance in the prediction: spectral bands > optical indices > topographic parameters > climatic factors. For different scales, prediction effects at 10 m, 20 m, 30 m and 60 m resolutions in three land use types showed a gradient decreasing trend, while the prediction at 20 m resolution in the total area approximates the prediction at 10 m resolution.

Based on these results, we conclude that multi-temporal synthesis images achieve better prediction accuracy than single-temporal images under certain land use types, with mean and median synthesis demonstrating particularly superior performance. Modeling for individual land-use types may further improve the prediction accuracy of TN content. Meanwhile, multi-scale modeling helps to improve prediction of TN content and enables more desirable TN prediction at medium and high resolution compared to coarse resolution.

This study demonstrates the potential of utilizing the XGBoost algorithm to predict soil total nitrogen content based on multi-temporal synthetic imagery. However, the computational cost associated with processing the high-dimensional data may limit the method’s application to large-scale datasets. Future work will explore more efficient algorithms and validate the method’s effectiveness across diverse geographical environments and land use conditions.
