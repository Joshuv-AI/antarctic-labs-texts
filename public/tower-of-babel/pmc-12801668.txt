Background

The incidence of violence against Emergency Medical Technicians (EMTs) in Ghana is still not well understood, and it is a problem that is frequently ignored. Moreover, there are no guidelines on how to handle and lessen violence in pre-hospital emergency healthcare for certain professional communities [1, [2]. EMTs are often the first to respond to pre-hospital emergencies, placing them in potentially hazardous situations. A few studies have brought attention to the seriousness of the problem by highlighting the high occurrence of violence against EMTs [1, 3, 4]. EMTs are vital members of the healthcare team who respond quickly to emergencies by delivering pre-hospital treatment. In times of emergency, such as accidents, medical emergencies, fires, or natural disasters, their responsibilities encompass patient evaluation, stabilisation, and transportation. However, violence against EMTs has gained international attention in recent years. Over 3500 incidents of assault resulting in injury against EMS personnel were reported in the United States in 2016 [5]. Many nations have strengthened emergency personnel’s legal safeguards in response to the increase in violence. For instance, in high-risk locations, especially in areas impacted by political turmoil and gang-related violence, South Africa mandated the use of armed escorts for ambulance staff [6]. The understanding that violence impedes the delivery of life-saving treatments has led to greater emphasis on safeguarding EMTs, both for their safety and for the wider public health ramifications. Patients, their families, healthcare professionals, including physicians and nurses, bystanders, and other people present at the emergency site are among those who harm EMTs [7]. The quality and promptness of the emergency medical care that EMTs deliver are jeopardised by this violence and put them at risk. This issue must be resolved, not just to safeguard EMTs but also to guarantee efficient emergency response and quality patient treatment.

The dire situation of EMTs in Africa is similar to the difficulties encountered worldwide, especially in situations where resources are scarce or politics are unstable [6, 8]. Tensions in these situations can flare up quickly and result in violent attacks against EMTs. Research indicates that EMTs frequently come across violence throughout Africa, particularly in areas that are prone to civil unrest or conflict [8]. The risk of violence is further increased by the combination of high-stress circumstances and scarce resources, which makes it challenging for EMTs to deliver essential care. Notwithstanding multiple reports of attacks in different regions, violence against EMTs remains an under-recognised issue in Ghana. During a routine patient transfer in 2021 in Ghana, one crew member was sexually abused, and an EMT was tragically shot and killed [9]. Another incident involved gunmen ambushing EMTs in Binduri in the Upper East region, Ghana, while they were transferring a victim of the Bawku Chieftaincy clashes. These illustrations highlight the grave risks that EMTs in Ghana must contend with. In spite of these accounts, no systematic study has been carried out to determine the scope and character of this violence.

The issue of violence against Emergency Medical Technicians (EMTs) in Ghana is largely unresolved, due to media reporting and anecdotal data. While there is a significant amount of research on violence against healthcare professionals in hospital settings, the pre-hospital environment, where EMTs operate, is often overlooked. This gap in research leaves policymakers without the necessary data to address the specific risks faced by EMTs. In Ghana, the National Ambulance Service (NAS) Act 1041, passed in 2020, mandates the training and deployment of EMTs to provide pre-hospital emergency care. However, without adequate protection measures in place, these professionals remain vulnerable to attacks, especially in high-risk environments such as road accidents, public disturbances, and conflict zones.

In this study, we aim to fill the gap in understanding the nature of violence against EMTs in the National Ambulance Service, Ghana. We assessed the types of violence encountered by EMTs, the specific emergency scenarios that are more likely to trigger violent incidents, and how these affect the quality of care provided. The research focused on various emergencies, including road traffic accidents, political gatherings, domestic emergencies, extreme violence, structural collapses, fires, and inter-hospital transfers. Understanding the patterns and causes of violence will help identify mitigation strategies, such as enhanced security measures, training for EMTs on how to handle volatile situations, and stronger legal protections. The findings will enhance understanding of workplace violence in healthcare and offer actionable insights for improving the safety of EMTs. Ensuring EMTs’ safety enhances the quality of emergency medical care, improving patient outcomes and service delivery. Protecting EMTs is crucial for maintaining service integrity and effective treatment. The lack of research on violence against EMTs in Ghana is a pressing issue, particularly in light of the growing demand for emergency services. Addressing this gap can help develop protective measures, improve patient outcomes, and enhance Ghana’s emergency healthcare system’s effectiveness. The study is divided into five sections: an introduction was presented in sections “Background”, “Hypothesis development” contains a literature review and research hypothesis, section “Data and method” contains the methodology, data analysis was presented in sections “Results”, “Discussion” contains the discussion and conclusions, and recommendations were presented in section “Conclusions and policy recommendations”.

Hypothesis development

Emergency Medical Technicians (EMTs) play a crucial role in the healthcare system, especially in emergencies. However, violence against these professionals has become a pressing issue worldwide. This literature review looks into the significant impact of emergencies on the types of violence directed at EMTs, the association between perpetrators and the nature of these violent attacks, and the mediating role of individuals committing violence in this academic work. Violence against EMTs is a significant global concern, with numerous studies highlighting their frequent exposure to violent situations during emergency calls. Factors contributing to this violence include high-stress levels among the public and relatives of patients, pressure on medical staff, and the patients’ conditions. Research by Bigham et al. [10] and Pourshaikhian et al. [11] indicates that substance abuse, psychiatric emergencies, and incidents in high-crime areas are more likely to lead to violence against EMTs. Additionally, Gormley et al. [12] found that specific emergencies, such as road traffic crashes, political gatherings, and structural collapses, are associated with an increased risk of violence against EMTs. These scenarios can heighten emotions and escalate violent incidents, as noted by Maguire et al. [13]. To address these risks, it is crucial to examine the relationship between various types of emergencies and the violence faced by EMTs in Ghana. Below is the initial hypothesis developed for investigation;

H1

The types of emergencies significantly influence the types of violence committed against EMTs

Understanding the nature of attacks on EMTs requires identifying the perpetrators, who often include patients, their relatives, doctors, nurses, or the general public, each exhibiting different violence patterns [14]. Research by Maguire et al. [13] in the United States and Suserud et al. [15] in Sweden indicates that the relationship between the attacker and the EMT significantly influences the type of violence experienced. EMTs are particularly vulnerable to physical violence from bystanders or relatives during road traffic accidents or political events, often due to perceptions of delayed response or political affiliations, as noted in research by Tshitangano et al. [4] in South Africa. Additionally, doctors and nurses under pressure may verbally attack EMTs, attributing frustrations related to patient care delays or complications to them, which can heighten the stress faced by EMS personnel [10]. Recognising the differences between physical and verbal violence based on the identity of the perpetrators is crucial for developing effective violence prevention strategies for EMS workers. Understanding who is responsible for these attacks can inform tailored interventions to protect EMTs. In this regard, it is hypothesised that;

H2

Perpetrators of violence significantly influences the types of violent attacks

The relationship between the perpetrators of violence and the emergencies in which the incidents occur is not usually predictable. Understanding the various groups of perpetrators of violence against EMTs in specific emergencies is essential. During emergencies such as RTAs, political gatherings, or disaster scenes, patients or the general public may become agitated and aggressive, leading to violent encounters [5]. These scenes often fuel intense emotions such as fear, anxiety, and frustration in patients and their relatives [13]. It is also suggested by Bowers et al. [16], in the United Kingdom, that an emergency can influence who becomes a perpetrator of violence. A study conducted in Nigeria, by Ndukwe et al. [17], which examined attacks of violence against ambulance personnel, has shown that patients and their relatives often transfer their frustration onto EMTs due to perceived inadequacies in care or delays at health facilities. This frustration can escalate during inter- and intra-hospital referrals, where long wait times and communication breakdowns contribute to aggressive behaviour directed at emergency medical personnel [18]. Research by Chapman et al. [19] and Koritsas et al. [20] suggests that these emergencies with heightened emotions can affect patient care. This association underscores the importance of targeted training for EMTs to handle specific types of emergencies and the individuals they encounter. Consequently, we hypothesise that;

H3

Perpetrators of violence significantly influence the type of emergencies that attract violence

The study of mediation in emergencies highlights the influence of perpetrators on the escalation of violence against EMTs, a factor that has been largely overlooked in research. Patterson et al. [21] found that emergency environments often induce stress and urgency, which can trigger violence. Suserud et al. [15] noted that perpetrators can transform non-violent situations into attacks on emergency service providers. The nature of violence faced by EMTs is often linked to the context and the role of the perpetrator; for example, Hill and Boyle [22] indicated that physical violence is more likely in high-stress environments like large gatherings or crime-prone areas. Additionally, conflicts among healthcare professionals can lead to verbal abuse, as described by Pourshaikhian et al. [11], where communication may become aggressive or demeaning. Gormley et al. [23] emphasised the importance of understanding the role of perpetrators in predicting violence types, which can aid EMTs, EMS managers, and policymakers in developing preventive strategies. H4

The types of emergencies that attracted violence significantly mediate the relationship between perpetrators of violence and the types of violent attacks

The literature review highlights several research gaps regarding violence against Emergency Medical Technicians in Ghana. Despite global studies highlighting violence in high-stress environments, there is a lack of research specifically addressing this issue within the Ghanaian context. Existing data is primarily anecdotal, with no comprehensive studies exploring the extent and nature of violence among pre-hospital emergency care providers. Research has not investigated the profiles of perpetrators in Ghana or the specific emergencies that may trigger violent behaviour. However, the current study extends the mediation model of perpetrators in examining the relationship between emergencies and types of violence within the context of pre-hospital emergency care delivery. In Ghana, research has not thoroughly examined the role of perpetrators in mediating and escalating violence in emergency healthcare situations. Addressing these gaps is crucial for developing informed policies and interventions to safeguard EMTs in Ghana. Moreover, the Occupational Health and Safety Theory (OHST) is also relevant for framing violence as a workplace hazard affecting EMTs’ wellbeing, safety, and performance.

Data and method

Population and sample

The research methods used were based on a quantitative study relative to violence against Emergency Medical Technicians (EMTs) in Ghana. The research approach adopted was deductive, as the study relied on numbers and statistics to answer the research questions through description, quantification, and calculation of facts.

The study population consists of employees of the Ghana National Ambulance Service (NAS), which is a critical component of Ghana’s healthcare system, providing emergency medical services across the country. The NAS employs trained paramedics, EMTs, and support staff to provide pre-hospital care. With 297 stations, 356 ambulances, 3473 EMTs, and 16 dispatch centres, NAS has expanded [24]. There are sixteen regional offices, each with their dispatch centre and overseen by a regional administrator, who is typically an emergency medical technician (EMT). The total population of EMTs within the NAS is 2112 nationwide as of 2020. We applied the Yamane formula to calculate the sample size [25]. The study used a 95% confidence level and p = 0.05 to determine a sample of 336 EMTs. We proportionally selected 126 EMTs from Greater Accra, 139 from Ashanti, and 44 from the Northern Region.

Sampling and data collection

We adopted a multistage sampling technique to ensure a representative yet targeted selection of respondents for the study. In the first stage, purposive sampling was used to select the three regions—Greater Accra, Ashanti, and the Northern Region—based on their relatively high number of ambulance stations and their consistent record of emergency medical cases over the years [24]. These regions were deemed most suitable for the study due to their operational significance within the National Ambulance Service and their potential to yield rich data for assessing violence against EMTs. This approach ensured that the study captured diverse emergency response settings with varying population densities and operational demands. In the second stage, we selected EMTs from various stations in the three regions using a simple random sampling method. To accomplish this, we obtained a sampling frame containing a list of respondents/EMTs. Then, a balloting approach was used to select prospective respondents from the sampling frame in all the stations in each region. Each station has a crew of six EMTs; we selected three EMTs from each station in the region. We wrote their staff IDs on a piece of paper, folded them, and randomly selected three to respond to the questionnaire. If the respondent drawn from the box was not available to respond to the research assistant, it was immediately replaced by drawing another respondent from the box until the required number was achieved. The characteristics of the respondents are presented in the next section.

The majority of the EMTs were male (70.2%), with females accounting for 29.8% of the sample (Appendix 1). The largest age group is between 29 and 38 years, followed by those between 39 and 48 (24.4%) and 18–28 (15.5%). The Ashanti region has the highest proportion of EMTs at 48.2%, followed by Greater Accra at 37.5% and the Northern region at 14.3%. The majority of EMTs are senior EMTs, accounting for 37.8%, followed by EMT-basic at 31% and advanced at 17%. Most EMTs have 6–10 years of experience (42.3%), with the least common roles being principal AEMT/health tutor, senior AEMT, and paramedic. About half of the respondents have secondary education (49.4%), while 47.3% have tertiary education and 3.3% have lower secondary education (drivers). The majority of EMTs work in Operations (non-driver) at 67%, followed by Operations (driver) at 19%, Administration at 8.3%, and Dispatch at 5.7%.

Measures

We structured the questionnaire into four sections; section “Background” contains the demographic characteristics of the respondents, section “Hypothesis development” contains the types of physical and non-physical violence attacks, section “Data and method” has variables on what emergencies are likely to attract violence, and the last section “Results” has variables on perpetrators who have the potential of causing violence attacks against EMTs. We used a Likert scale for respondents to rate their experiences in sections “Data and method” to “Results”, using 1 = not frequent, 2 = less frequent, 3 = moderately frequent, 4 = frequent, and 5 = very frequent. We reviewed the questionnaire and conducted a pilot survey with 18 EMTs to assess the comprehension and precision of the questions. The questions were adopted from previous literature [1, 13, 26, 27] that have been implemented in different aspects of emergency healthcare delivery. We designed Google Forms and used it to collect the data.

Data analysis

We analysed the data in this work using Smart Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM). We chose this approach due to its appropriateness for the data qualities and its efficient execution of moderation and mediation analysis [28]. The PLS-SEMs extensive analytical skills have made it popular in research sectors such as human resource management, marketing, and others [3, 29]. According to Hair et al. [30], PLS-SEM is very useful for predicting the effects of dependent variables. Davari and Rezazadeh [31] agreed and said that PLS-SEM is good at predicting many equations in a study model and showing how variables interact with each other. Hair et al. [32] conducted a study using literature studies, actual examples, and simulations to demonstrate that negative perceptions of PLS-SEMs capabilities are overstated, indicating that PLS-SEM still produces unbiased estimates. PLS-SEM is a second-generation data exploration technique that focuses on linear and additive causal relationships [33]. The technique aims to explore the connections between constructs, rendering it suitable for the analysis of complex models with latent variables. The approach is capable of examining both direct and indirect paths, especially when latent components are difficult to detect. Inner and outer model analyses are used in SEM and its version, PLS-SEM, to find the relationships between independent and dependent variables, as well as between latent constructs and the things that can be seen that show them. The Smart PLS Vinzi et al. [34] promotes variance-based analysis, which is consistent with PLS-SEMs focus on variance. This methodological approach improves the study’s ability to conduct a thorough and robust analysis, making it a dependable instrument for investigating complex interactions in management and health sciences. Also, the analysis included the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure, and the Chi-Square, inner, and outer models in Table 1. Model I (Table 5) cover the generic variables collected via the questionnaire, providing a comprehensive overview of the factors impacting the research components. In contrast, Model II (Table 6), also known as the reduced model, focuses on variables that are most important to the hypotheses. It refines the study by focusing on the key links under inquiry, allowing for simpler interpretation and hypothesis testing.

To ensure discriminant validity, each latent unobserved variable must have a greater outer-loading (bolded) than it’s cross-loading (with other measures) (Table 1).

Study full Model I hypothesis

The Smart PLS4 used bootstrapping to calculate route coefficients and t-values for direct and mediated correlations. The present study proposes 12 hypotheses, which are direct relationships in Table 5 and depicted in the path analysis in Fig. 2 below. The study findings show that the 10 dimensions of emergencies that attract non-physical violence, as well as the characteristics of those who commit non-physical violence, have a direct, favourable, and substantial influence on both physical and non-physical violence types, except dimensions [10] and [19] (Table 5). Although the result is not statistically significant (Beta = −0.0477), (p > 0.05), the negative beta coefficient suggests that as the involvement of persons in committing non-physical violence increases, reported physical violence slightly decreases. The hypotheses’ analysis reveals that most of the proposed relationships are statistically significant, as indicated by the failure to reject the null hypothesis in most cases. Specifically, emergencies like extreme violence scenes, inter/intra-hospital referrals, and so on can lead to non-physical violence (at, making offensive jokes with sexual overtones, being called names, etc.) with a statistically significant coefficient (H1: β = 0.1907, p = 0.00382) and also to physical violence (being raped, stabbed, etc.) with a coefficient (H2:β = 0.2111, p = 0.02933) and was found to be a major factor in causing physical violence on EMTs. Similarly, emergencies that attract physical violence have the potential to cause non-physical violence with a coefficient (H3: β = 0.1573, p = 0.03198).

Structural Model

The study also found that people who committed non-physical violence had a significant effect on emergencies that involved both non-physical (H5:β = 0.4739, p < 0.0001) and physical violence (H6: β = 0.1728, p = 0.01545) violence, as well as on the different kinds of non-physical violence (H7: β = 0.3426, p < 0.0001). Furthermore, those who committed physical violence have the potential to be significantly affected by emergencies that attract both non-physical (H9: β = 0.2324, p = 0.00186) and physical violence (H10: β = 0.5168, p < 0.0001), in addition to impacting both types of non-physical (H11: β = 0.2378, p = 0.00072) and the various physical violence (H12: β = 0.3926, p = 0.00137). However, H4 (emergencies attracting physical violence leading to physical violence) and H8 (persons committing non-physical violence leading to physical violence) rejected the null hypothesis, indicating a lack of statistical support for these specific relationships. The results show the significant impact of emergencies and the role of individuals in the occurrence of non-physical and physical violence, except in the two instances mentioned.

Discussion

The assessed violence against emergency medical technicians (EMTs) in Ghana National Ambulance Service (NAS) focuses on the impact of emergencies on types of violence, the association between perpetrators and types of violence, the relationship between perpetrators and emergencies leading to violence, and the mediation role of perpetrators in the relationship between emergencies and types of violence against EMTs. According to the study, emergencies with the potential for violence have a significant impact on the types of violence experienced by EMTs. On average, high-stress scenarios such as political gatherings, structural collapses, fires, extreme violence, road traffic accidents, domestic emergencies, and natural disasters increase the likelihood of EMTs encountering various forms of violence by about 32.5%. These include physical violence (e.g., being hit, kicked, pushed, stabbed, or bitten) and non-physical violence (e.g., threats, cursing, or name-calling). These findings are consistent with the General Aggression Model (GAM), which explains that aggression is caused by the interaction of situational inputs (such as high-stress emergency conditions) and internal states (such as anger or frustration), resulting in aggressive behaviour based on individual characteristics and contextual cues [24].

The findings were in line with a study by Taylor et al. [26] that EMTs are usually exposed to various forms of violence from patients, patients’ relatives, bystanders, or members of the general public. Additionally, high-crime areas, psychiatric emergencies, and substance abuse cases are major contributors to violence against EMTs [10, 11]. Moreover, the identity of the perpetrators, including patients’ relatives and members of the general public, plays a crucial role in violence during emergencies, like road traffic accidents, domestic emergencies, and political gatherings. This current study found that violent attacks caused by these perpetrators on EMTs are about 68.4% on average. This lends credence to the GAM paradigm, which holds that when some individuals are exposed to emotionally charged surroundings (such as mass casualty events or politically contentious circumstances), they are more inclined to externalise hostility. Gormley et al. [12] research revealed that emergency responders have indicated that they are more likely to be attacked during emergency response. Individuals or youth of political parties in Ghana are involved in violence to promote their party’s agendas. At times, this application of violence against their political opponents escalates, and the emergency service providers get attacked while responding to the situation to de-escalate and provide medical care to the injured victims [41]. In 2021, an EMT was shot dead, and another crew member was sexually assaulted while en route with a case from Akuse Government Hospital to Eastern Regional Hospital [9]. EMTs were also attacked in Binduri in the Upper East of Ghana, who were transporting a victim of the Bawku Chieftaincy conflicts from Bawku Presbyterian Hospital to Bolgatanga.

The common forms of violence that were reported by EMS personnel (61 to 93%) in various states in America included verbal abuse, physical violence, and intimidation [42]. Other forms of violence include sexual harassment and sexual assault. These patterns are not unique to Ghana. The violent attacks on EMTs in South Africa have escalated, leading to the introduction of an armed escort protocol for ambulance personnel in certain areas in the Western Cape and Cape Town [6]. These incidents highlight the broader occupational risk that emergency medical technicians face around the world. In line with the Occupational Health and Safety Theory (OHST), such violence constitutes a workplace hazard, threatening the well-being, mental health, and operational capacity of EMTs. As frontline health workers responsible for on-scene patient care and transport, EMTs face unique exposure to volatile environments that differ from those encountered by other health professionals. This necessitates proactive safety policies, including legal protections, training in de-escalation, and provision of psychosocial support.

These attacks target EMTs, frontline health workers who play a critical role in the health sector by providing timely, safe, on-scene patient assessment, stabilisation, and medical transport of patients [43]. It causes them to face difficult and different challenges compared to other health professionals, such as emotional and physical stress or burnout [44]. This has made some countries, like South Africa, in Africa, pass laws seeking to protect EMTs against violence by implementing harder punishment for attackers of EMTs or emergency responders.

Furthermore, the identity of the perpetrators influences about 51.5% of the types of violence that EMTs endure, including physical violence (e.g., being hit, kicked, pushed, stabbed, or bitten) and non-physical violence (e.g., threats, cursing, or name-calling). A study by Ndukwe et al. [17] in Nigeria found that attacks of violence against ambulance personnel have shown that patients and their relatives often transfer their frustration onto EMTs due to perceived inadequacies in care or delays at health facilities’ emergencies often escalate into violence due to high stress levels, intense pressure on doctors or nurses, and the condition of patients [4]. The study conducted by Maguire et al. [13] revealed that the relationship between the attacker and the victim (EMT) significantly influences the type of violence that occurs. EMTs responding to road traffic accidents or political gatherings may face physical violence from bystanders or patients’ relatives due to perceived delayed response or association with a specific political faction [13–15]. Furthermore, doctors and nurses under intense pressure in their facilities direct verbal attacks on EMTs during referral cases [18].

These emergencies heightened emotional effects on patient care [19, 20]. This association emphasises the importance of targeted training for EMTs to handle specific types of emergencies and the people they encounter. Hostility in emergency medical services (EMS) often arises from frustrations over delays in patient care or misplaced blame for complications, which can intensify the stress EMS personnel face and negatively affect their performance [19].

These findings support the mediation model tested in this study, which found that emergencies mediate the relationship between perpetrators and the type of violence experienced by EMTs, accounting for approximately 22.2%. This implies that the nature and intensity of the emergency can influence not only whether violence occurs, but who is likely to perpetrate it and how it manifests. In high-stakes emergencies—such as road traffic accidents or politically motivated violence—individuals may act aggressively toward EMTs out of fear, confusion, or misdirected blame [5, 13, 45]. Patterson et al. [21] emphasised that the high-pressure environment of emergency medicine increases the potential for violent behaviour, especially when roles are misunderstood or expectations are unmet. This also underscores GAMs focus on violent behaviour as context-dependent, with contextual stresses (such as chaos, perceived loss, or threat) triggering emotional states that increase the likelihood of violence. Furthermore, intra- and inter-hospital referral disagreements can result in verbal abuse from facility-based doctors, highlighting the complex character of violence in EMS [11]. Finally, understanding the role of perpetrators as mediators in the escalation of violence, as emphasised by Gormley et al. [23], is critical for developing tailored prevention strategies for EMTs, EMS managers, and policymakers.

Conclusions and policy recommendations

This study addressed the serious issue of violence against Emergency Medical Technicians (EMTs) in Ghana’s National Ambulance Service (NAS), providing vital insights into the forms, settings, and predictors of such violence, as well as potential mitigation strategies. Three types of violence were identified: physical violence, verbal abuse, and sexual assault. Physical violence frequently entails actions like assaulting EMTs or striking ambulances, and verbal abuse includes intimidation, name-calling, and threats. Indecent assault is nevertheless a source of worry, notwithstanding its decreased frequency. Using a Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) approach, the study analysed the relationships among the types of violence experienced, the emergency contexts, and the perpetrators involved. The findings revealed that both physical and non-physical violence were significantly associated with specific emergencies, with patients and their relatives being the most frequent offenders. Importantly, the results indicated that the identity of the perpetrator plays a mediating role in the link between the emergency context and the form of violence encountered. This highlights the complex and dynamic nature of violence within the pre-hospital care environment. The prevalence of these attacks, particularly from members of the public, marks the urgent need for systemic interventions. The study also uncovered specific emergency scenarios that increase the likelihood of violence against EMTs, identifying road traffic accidents, hospital referrals, and domestic emergencies as particularly dangerous contexts. These findings emphasise the need for tailored training and preparedness strategies to protect EMTs during high-risk interventions. Respondents endorsed effective measures for mitigating violence, such as defensive tactical training, management of aggressive behaviour, and verbal de-escalation techniques, while also highlighting the importance of awareness of gang dynamics and management of bystander interference. Addressing violence against EMTs in Ghana is imperative for the safety of personnel and the effective delivery of emergency medical services. The study suggests that the National Association of Emergency Medical Technicians should establish protocols requiring police presence before dispatching EMTs to potentially violent situations to enhance safety and preparedness. A national reporting system for documenting violence against EMTs should be implemented in collaboration with law enforcement agencies. NAS should be integrated into Regional and District Security Councils (RESEC and DISEC) to stay informed about local security situations. Management should prioritise addressing EMTs’ concerns about violence by developing policies that safeguard them and provide clarity on handling violent encounters. Regular assessments for mental health conditions like anxiety, depression, and PTSD should be conducted. The Escaping Violence Encounters for EMTs program should be incorporated into the training curriculum to equip EMTs with skills to defuse and escape violent situations.

In the future, research should examine how violence affects EMTs’ mental health over time and how well the measures taken to reduce this violence work, aiming to develop a lasting, evidence-based plan to protect EMTs in Ghana. Additionally, a study should be done in referral hospitals and the National Ambulance Service (NAS) to understand the challenges EMTs face with referrals and other related issues. Again, concerning doctors or nurses as perpetrators of violence against EMTs, research should be conducted for referral hospitals and the National Ambulance Service (NAS) to identify challenges of referrals and other factors involving EMTs.

Limitations

Participants or EMTs who remained at a particular station for six months or more were included in the study, while those who had either recently arrived or were transferred to the study area and had stayed for less than six months were excluded from the interviews. This decision was made under the premise that such individuals might lack sufficient knowledge regarding the pertinent issues, as they could either be newly employed staff or may have been interviewed in different regions within the selected areas during the study period. Even though SmartPLS does not provide global goodness-of-fit indices like those used in covariance-based SEM (e.g., RMSEA, CFI), which may restrict the full evaluation of model fit. We used Harman’s single-factor test, which revealed that no one factor explained the bulk of the variation, suggesting that CMB was not a serious worry [46].
