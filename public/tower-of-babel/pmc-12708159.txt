Gracilis Muscle Flap for Ankle Soft Tissue Reconstruction: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Postoperative Outcomes
Authors: John Milkovich, Shaishav Datta, Blake Murphy, Joseph Catapano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The gracilis muscle flap is valuable as a reconstructive option because of its reliable anatomy and minimal donor-site morbidity. The aim of this systematic review was to evaluate acute perioperative complications and long-term outcomes of the gracilis muscle flap in distal lower limb reconstruction following trauma, infections, or oncological resections.Methods:This review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023486475) and adhered to PRISMA guidelines. All primary studies on gracilis flaps for lower leg soft tissue reconstruction were included, excluding those with flap recipient sites above the knee, isolated tendon repair, or nonprimary research articles. Searches were performed across Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, and PubMed.Results:Fifty-eight articles involving 1232 patients who underwent lower extremity reconstruction with a gracilis flap were included. Indications for surgery were trauma (n = 146), diabetes (n = 116), oncological (n = 31), infection (n = 27), other (n = 2), and heterogeneous (n = 910). A total of 397 (32.2%) complications were reported. In heterogeneous studies, pooled proportions were 0.22 for acute postoperative complications, 0.17 for recipient-site events, and 0.12 for donor-site morbidity, with oncological and infection cases showing the highest complication rates. The most common complications for each category were total flap loss (79, 6.4%), recipient infection (14, 1.1%), and donor-site scarring (14, 1.1%).Conclusions:The gracilis flap is a reliable option for reconstructing a wide array of soft tissue defects of the distal lower extremity, with modifications to increase its coverage area. The long-term functional and aesthetic outcomes after gracilis flap reconstruction are undescribed.

Takeaways

Question: What are the acute perioperative complications and long-term outcomes of the gracilis muscle flap in lower limb reconstruction following trauma, infections, or oncological resections?

Findings: From 58 articles, a total of 397 (32.2%) complications were reported: 234 (19.2%) acute perioperative, 127 (10.3%) recipient site, and 36 (2.9%) donor site. The most common complications for each category were total flap loss (79, 6.4%), recipient-site infection (14, 1.1%), and donor-site scarring (14, 1.1%).

Meaning: The gracilis flap is a reliable option for reconstructing a wide array of soft tissue defects of the lower extremity, with modifications to increase its coverage area.

INTRODUCTION

Lower limb reconstruction is a challenging reconstructive endeavor, particularly following trauma or oncological resections. Although a systematic approach to lower limb reconstruction has long been advocated, reconstructive decisions are often dependent on the clinical expertise of the surgeon, which can vary greatly. These decisions can critically impact psychosocial outcomes, rehabilitation, and donor- and recipient-site complications, wherein failure can result in amputation.1

When free tissue transfer is indicated for limb salvage, reconstruction can be achieved with myogenous, myocutaneous, or fasciocutaneous flaps. Myogenous flaps provide better contouring of soft tissue defects while providing robust vascular coverage over exposed hardware or joints, and may create a more inconspicuous scar at the donor site.2 However, a skin graft is required to cover the muscle, which may lead to more conspicuous scarring at the recipient site. Myocutaneous and fasciocutaneous flaps tend to be bulkier with more conspicuous donor sites; however, they provide cutaneous coverage at the donor site, which may be preferred aesthetically.3 No statistically significant differences exist with respect to total flap failure, reoperation, and limb salvage between myogenous and fasciocutaneous free flaps for traumatic lower limb reconstruction.3

Commonly used muscle flaps for reconstruction of the lower limb include the latissimus dorsi, rectus abdominis, and gracilis muscles.2 In addition to surgeon comfort and expertise, muscle flap selection is often dependent on the size of the defect and the pedicle length required. Although the latissimus and rectus abdominis muscle flaps are better suited for large defects, the gracilis muscle is often ideal for lower limb reconstruction because of its narrow width and long length. Features of the gracilis that make it favorable include (1) a long arterial pedicle (range, 8–12 cm), (2) reliable anatomy of vascular supply, (3) an inconspicuous donor-site scar, and (4) minimal functional donor-site morbidity.4,5 The gracilis muscle flap has a mean length of 28 cm, which permits adequate coverage in distal leg wound reconstruction.4–6 Often, the width of the muscle can be variably expanded by incising the epimysium.

We conducted a systematic review to provide an update on the existing literature around the use of the gracilis muscle flap in distal lower leg reconstruction. The objective of this systematic review was to specifically examine the acute perioperative complications and long-term outcomes of the gracilis muscle flap when used in lower limb reconstruction.

METHODS

This study was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023486475) and concords with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses reporting guidelines.7,8 The search strategy was developed in consultation with an expert medical librarian at the University of Toronto. Randomized control trials, cross-sectional studies, case reports/series, and cohort studies that described the use of the gracilis muscle flap for the reconstruction of soft tissue defects in the distal third portion of the lower limb were included. Published studies were limited to English-language articles published between 1946 and May 22, 2024.

The search was executed on May 22, 2024, in Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and PubMed. The final search strategy can be found in Supplemental Digital Content 1. (See appendix, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays search strategy for Ovid MEDLINE, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E535.) Two reviewers (J.M. and S.D.) developed and piloted a series of screening questions before independently screening all titles and abstracts. Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (B.M. or J.C.) or through consensus to determine eligibility for full-text review. Full-text articles identified as eligible were extracted by 2 reviewers (J.M. and S.D.). The risk of bias assessment was completed by a single reviewer (J.M.) and verified by a second reviewer (S.D.). A third reviewer (B.M. or J.C.) resolved any disagreements. The risk of bias was stratified by the proportion of “yes” scores: higher (≤49%), moderate (50%–79%), and low (≥80%).9

Study characteristics and outcomes were narratively summarized, whereby quantitative results were combined holistically and by the following etiologic subgroups: diabetes, infection, oncology, trauma, and heterogeneous. Within each subgroup, a proportional meta-analysis was performed to estimate pooled complication rates (acute postoperative complications, recipient-site complications, and donor-site morbidity). Event counts and sample sizes from each study were logit-transformed and pooled via fixed-effects inverse-variance models, where study weights were calculated as the inverse of the within-study variance. Pooled logit estimates were back-transformed to proportions, and heterogeneity was calculated using the Cochran Q test and the I2 statistic. Forest plots and funnel plots were generated for each outcome by etiology subgroup. Statistical analyses were conducted in R (v4.2.2) using the metafor package.

RESULTS

There were 58 studies that were included in this review. Figure 1 presents the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flowchart for the review process. Most studies were published in the United States (15, 25.9%), Germany (9, 15.5%), and Switzerland (9, 15.5%). Most included articles are case series (37, 63.8%), followed by case reports (11, 19.0%) and cohort studies (9, 15.5%). (See appendix, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays journal details, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E536.) Only 5 (8.6%) of the studies were prospective in nature. Most studies had a low risk of bias (average 88.2% with a range of 37.5%–100%). All 11 case reports were classified as low risk, in contrast to 86.5% of 37 case series and 55.6% of cohort studies. Only 1 cohort study scored a high risk of bias. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 3, which displays Joanna Briggs Institute risk of bias, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E537.) In the included studies, 1232 participants were documented to have received a gracilis muscle flap for distal lower extremity reconstruction. The average sample size was 21.2 participants, ranging from a single case study to 204 participants.10 Most participants were men (66.2%) for the studies that reported sex, and they were, on average, 50.13 years of age (range, 6–92 y). Ethnicity and body mass index were largely underreported, with only 3 and 6 studies providing relevant data, respectively.4,7,11–17 Approximately 70% of patients (ie, 858 participants) had at least 1 comorbidity, whereas more than half of the studies (36, 62.1%) reported prior procedures performed on their participants. The most common comorbidities were diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and peripheral arterial disease. Most participants were nonsmokers (42, 72.4%). (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 4, which displays patient demographics, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E538.) The gracilis muscle flap was used in distal lower extremity reconstruction for the following etiologies: trauma (20, 34.5%), infection (6, 10.3%), diabetes (5, 8.6%), oncological (2, 3.4%), and other (2, 3.4%), including resection of massive ectopic calcification and treatment of recalcitrant chronic venous ulcers. Twenty-one (36.2%) studies reported a heterogeneous set of etiologies. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 5, which displays surgery and outcomes, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E539.)

Trauma

Twenty studies (34.5%) reported a total of 146 (11.9%) patients who underwent distal lower extremity reconstruction with a gracilis muscle free flap posttrauma.12–31 Most of the patients were men (122, 83.6%), and the average age was 39.23 years (range, 6–76 y). Trauma involved road traffic accidents, workplace injuries, falls, crush injuries, and gunshot wounds. Injuries were localized to the distal third of the lower leg, ankle, and foot, including the Achilles tendon, calcaneus, forefoot, and lateral foot, with presentations of subcutaneous degloving, compartment loss, and bilateral foot through-and-through gunshot wounds. Eight (40%) studies reported the Gustilo classification of the injury, ranging from IIIB to IIIC.12,14,17,19,27,28,31

The length of surgery ranged from 65 to 630 minutes; however, only 5 studies reported this metric. Wait time to surgery varied: 8 studies performed surgery immediately in several cases (ie, 70 min to 48 h postinjury), 3 studies performed surgery subacutely (ie, 3–7 d), and 5 studies reported intermediate and delayed treatment (16 d to 8.1 wk postinjury). The flap size ranged from 25–160 cm2, with 1 study reporting a flap volume range of 120–288 cm3. The anterior tibial artery, posterior tibial artery, dorsalis pedis artery, and their accompanying venae comitantes, and the peronea arteria magna (1 case involving end-to-side microanastomosis),19 were used as recipient vessels. Hospital stay ranged from 10 days to 1.1 years, and patients were followed up for, on average, 17.2 months (range, 20 d to 47 mo).

Infection

Six studies reported 27 cases of infection-related lower extremity reconstruction with a gracilis muscle free flap.32–37 Most of the patients were men (18, 66.7%), with an average age of 45.4 years (range, 9–91 y). A notable primary case involved Fusobacterium varium,37 whereas secondary infections were precipitated by complications related to trauma, diabetic ulcers, and ischemic gangrene. Most injury sites were located at the distal third of the leg, predominantly the ankle, heel, and sole of the foot.

The length of surgery ranged from 30 minutes to harvest the flap (not the entire operative time) to 3.5 hours; however, only 2 studies reported this metric. Time to surgery also varied greatly from 3 days after admission to 6 months, 23 years, and 28 years after the initial presentation in 1 cohort; the significant delay period can be explained by persistent osteomyelitis caused by an open complex fracture of the os calcis.34,37 The average flap size was 84.0 cm2 (range, 9–300 cm2), with the posterior tibial artery as the recipient vessel in most cases. Hospital stays ranged from 8 to 79 days, and patients were followed up, on average, for 3.14 years.

Diabetes

Five studies, including 116 patients, reported surgical outcomes from distal lower extremity reconstruction with a gracilis free muscle flap for diabetic wound–related complications.38–42 Eighty-nine (76.7%) patients were men, and their average age was 56.21 years (range, 21–90 y). All the patients were treated for complications related to chronic diabetic foot ulcers located at the ankle, forefoot, dorsum, heel, or toes. In 1 study, Wagner classification was used to grade the foot ulcers of its 45 participants: grade 2 (13, 28%), grade 3 (9, 20%), and grade 4 (23, 51.1%).41

One study reported the average length of surgery to be 240 minutes (SD, 30 min),42 with procedures occurring within 48 hours41 or within 7–10 days of admission.42 The flap size ranged from 66 to 130 cm2, and the recipient vessels comprised the dorsal pedis, posterior tibial, anterior tibial, and first metatarsal arteries. All studies reported follow-up time, which averaged 20.83 months (range, 6–150 mo).

Oncology

Four studies of 31 patients reported the reconstruction of the distal lower extremity after oncological resection with the gracilis muscle free flap.43–46 Most of the patients were women, and the average age was 52.3 years (range, 18–81 y). Twelve (38.7%) patients underwent radiotherapy before surgery. Cancer type and location of the reconstruction varied among all portions of the distal third of the leg.

Most of the reconstructive procedures (24 cases) were performed immediately following cancer resection,45,46 with 4 cases delayed more than 72 hours,46 and 7 cases delayed an average of 6 weeks after the completion of postresection radiotherapy.43 One study of 18 patients reported the average flap size to be 104 cm2 (range, 20–200 cm2).46 Only 1 study reported a single hospital stay of 16 days for the treatment of a pleomorphic leiomyosarcoma of the ankle,44 and the average follow-up time was 31.1 months (range, 14–53 mo).

Unusual Cases

Two unusual cases for gracilis muscle flap use in the reconstruction of the distal lower extremity were included. Schmitt et al47 described a 53-year-old woman who underwent reconstruction after resection of a massive ectopic calcification following tibial fracture and compartment syndrome. The surgery occurred 34 years after the initial injury at the distal third of the lower left leg. Moreover, Weinzweig et al48 reported a 32-year-old man who underwent lower leg reconstruction as a long-term cure for a recalcitrant chronic venous ulcer. Both cases resolved without complication at 18 and 35 months of follow-up, respectively.

Heterogeneous

Twenty-one (36.2%) studies included 910 patients who underwent gracilis muscle free flap reconstruction for a wide array of etiologies that were not specific to the outcome.2,4–6,10,11,49–63 Most of the patients were men (65.0%), and their average age was 51.9 years (range, 5–88 y). Four studies reported Gustilo type, which included IA to IIIC injuries.2,6,59,61 The location of the reconstruction varied at the distal lower extremity level.

The timing of interventions varied widely, with some performed immediately after tumor resection, debridement, or fracture stabilization,2,58,61 whereas others occurred over a range of 2 days to 30 years. Average durations included 23.1 days (range, 2–66 d), with acute injuries treated at 7.9 days (2–20 d), and posttraumatic infections at 3.13 years (11 d to 30 y). Timing was often categorized as within 7 days (6 cases), 8–30 days (11 cases), 31–90 days (4 cases), or beyond 90 days (3 cases).59

The length of the procedures also varied. One study reported mean times of 327.4 minutes for men and 332.3 minutes for women.4 When comparing a single-vein to double-vein anastomosis, the mean length of surgery was 301 minutes for the single-vein group and 324 minutes for the double-vein group.57 The overall average flap size was 114.50 cm2 (range, 3.6–400 cm2); notably, a large cohort study of 204 patients reported an average flap size of 68.51 cm2 (15–150 cm2) for small-to-medium soft tissue defects, and 200.85 cm2 (150–400 cm2) for large tissue defects.10 Another large study (n = 152) reported differences in flap size between sex, finding that men required 192.8 cm2 and women required 173.3 cm2 for the harvested flaps.4 Regarding blood supply, the posterior tibial vessels were most commonly used, followed by the anterior tibial and dorsalis pedis arteries. Other vessels, including the genicular and medial plantar arteries, were also used when the tibial or peroneal arteries were not available. Hospital stay was largely underreported, whereas the average follow-up time for patients was approximately 11.82 months (range, 1 mo to 7 y).

Surgical Outcomes and Meta-analysis

Among the 1232 cases, 397 (32.2%) complications were documented, including 234 (19.0%) at the acute perioperative stage; 127 (10.3%) at the recipient site, and 36 (2.9%) at the donor site. The most frequent acute perioperative complications were total flap loss (79, 6.4%), partial flap loss (44, 3.6%), venous thrombosis (36, 2.9%), hematoma (33, 2.7%), and arterial thrombosis (22, 1.8%). A significant proportion of recipient-site complications were not specified (70, 5.7%); however, infection (14, 1.1%), graft loss (12, 1.0%), and bulky flap (12, 1.0%) were the most common. Finally, at the donor site, the majority of complications were associated with suboptimal scarring (14, 1.1%). A breakdown of the complications is shown in Table 1.

For the heterogeneous cohort studies where the etiology could not be disentangled from the outcome, the pooled proportion for acute postoperative complications was 0.22 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.19–0.25), for recipient-site events was 0.17 (95% CI: 0.14–0.21), and for donor-site morbidity was 0.12 (95% CI: 0.08–0.17) (Table 2). The greatest pooled proportions of acute complications were reported in oncological (0.29, 95% CI: 0.19–0.46) and infection (0.25, 95% CI: 0.12–0.45) cases; the other proportions were lower than the heterogeneous group. Similarly, the largest pooled proportions of recipient-site events were observed in oncological (0.37, 95% CI: 0.22–0.54), infection (0.34, 95% CI: 0.18–0.56), and traumatic cases (0.21, 95% CI: 0.13–0.31). Finally, the pooled proportions of donor-site morbidity were lower across all specific etiologies compared with the heterogeneous group. Overall, most I² values were less than 50%, and all funnel plots appeared relatively symmetrical, suggesting low heterogeneity and a low likelihood of publication bias. DISCUSSION

This systematic review highlighted the versatility of the gracilis free muscle flap in distal lower extremity reconstruction. The muscle has an average mass of 133.2 g, with a mean length of 28 cm and a width of 4–5 cm, making it a reliable myogenous or myocutaneous free flap for the reconstruction of various distal third leg wounds.4–6 Based on our findings, gracilis muscle flaps were most often used to reconstruct defect sizes ranging from 3.6 to 400 cm². Despite the gracilis muscle’s shorter pedicle length, vein grafts were not commonly reported in the included studies; however, when necessary, vein grafting remains a reliable option, as demonstrated in other lower extremity free tissue transfers.64 The use of spreaded gracilis flaps, where epimysial dissection was performed to increase the muscle’s coverage area, has proven reliable for defects exceeding 150 cm2 with major complication rates similar between small-to-medium and large defect groups (10.91% and 15.72%, respectively).10 As such, a treatment algorithm that advises nonspreaded gracilis flaps for deep lower limb defects more than 150 cm2 and spreaded gracilis flaps for superficial lower limb defects between 150 and 350 cm2 was proposed.10 Patient height, weight, body mass index, and age were found to be correlated with the mass of the gracilis flap, which thereby offers proxy patient-specific measurements that can aid in preoperative planning and flap selection for distal lower extremity reconstruction.5

Several findings from this review emphasize the gracilis flap’s reliability in the hospital setting and limitations. A comparison of 1 versus 2 venous anastomoses showed no significant impact on complication rates or flap survival, although a single venous anastomosis understandably led to reduced operative times.57 Furthermore, gracilis flaps demonstrated lower major complication rates compared with alternatives, such as anterolateral thigh flaps.57 Additionally, the gracilis free flap was effective in covering challenging sarcoma-related soft tissue defects, which were subjected to high-dose chemoradiation and wide surgical margins.46 However, certain risk factors for surgical complications were highlighted with this procedure. These included tobacco use, which led to complete superficial necrosis of a free gracilis flap in 1 patient,14 as well as psychiatric illness, which contributed to the late avulsion of a free flap in another patient.27

However, there is a gap in knowledge regarding the long-term outcomes, particularly the potential for atrophy of the gracilis muscle postreconstruction and how this impacts patient function. None of the reviewed studies provided data on the long-term stability of flap size or alterations in its volume. Future studies should incorporate ankle circumference evaluations to assess flap bulk and its influence on the ability of patients to fit into shoes. Further, despite the strengths of the gracilis flap in lower extremity reconstruction, free-style local perforator flaps should also be considered for smaller defects.26

Limitations

The limitations of the study include language bias and publication bias. First, non-English articles were excluded because they were not the authors’ primary language. Second, publication bias arises from included articles being more likely to report positive findings, which can skew results by omitting negative or inconclusive findings that are not publishable.

CONCLUSIONS

This is the largest known systematic review and meta-analysis to date that showcases the gracilis flap as a reliable choice for small-to-medium lower extremity reconstructions, particularly the ankle, with modifications expanding its applicability to larger defects. Although short-term results are promising, further research addressing long-term outcomes, particularly muscle size stability and functional implications, is warranted.
