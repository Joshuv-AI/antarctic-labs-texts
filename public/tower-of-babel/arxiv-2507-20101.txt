Matters arising: Tunnelling photons pose no challenge to Bohmian machanics
Yun-Fei Wang, Xiao-Yu Wang, Hui Wang, Chao-Yang Lu

Abstract




Bohmian mechanics (BM) is an alternative interpretation of quantum mechanics, firstly proposed by de Broglie in 1927
De27
and further developed by Bohm in 1952
Bohm52
. It is widely believed that BM has the same prediction on experimental outcomes with textbook Copenhagen interpretation because they obey the same fundamental Schrödinger equation in the non-relativistic regime.

However, a recent experiment by Sharoglazova et al.
S25
reports an apparent discrepancy between measured energy-velocity relationships and Bohmian predictions in specific regimes. The authors contend this mismatch “casts doubt on whether the guiding equation accurately captures the temporal dynamics of the scattering process”.

In their experiment, the authors employ a high-finesse microcavity containing a dye medium. Within this cavity, the engineered height difference is implemented for the purpose of measuring speed of photons. First, a linear ramp in the main waveguide is designed to control the initial kinetic energy of photons by changing the position along this ramp at which the dye is optically pumped. Subsequently, the emitted photons impinge a constant step potential
V_{0}
, where another auxiliary waveguide starts. This auxiliary waveguide evanescently couples to the main waveguide with a fixed coupling strength
J_{0}
. This system is modeled by two coupled Schrödinger equations

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi_{m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\frac{d^{2}\psi_{m}}{dx^{2}}+V_{0}\psi_{m}+\hbar J_{0}(\psi_{a}-\psi_{m}),

(1)

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi_{a}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\frac{d^{2}\psi_{a}}{dx^{2}}+V_{0}\psi_{a}+\hbar J_{0}(\psi_{m}-\psi_{a}),

where
m
is the effective mass of photons considering the longitudinal resonance energy
E_{z}
using Einstein energy-mass equivalence
E_{z}=m\tilde{c}^{2}
(
\tilde{c}
denotes the photon speed in the medium),
\psi_{m}
and
\psi_{a}
are wavefunctions in the main and auxiliary waveguides, respectively.

The discrepancy of these two measured speeds happens when

, where
\Delta=E-V_{0}+\hbar J_{0}
. The observed

, while measured

is very close to zero. Therefore, the authors claim that this experiment raises concern on the correctness of Bohmian mechanics.

Here we reanalyze the physical process in this experiment and demonstrate that it poses no challenge to BM. First, the two measured speeds represent fundamentally distinct physical quantities; comparing them is physically unjustified and cannot serve as evidence favoring the Copenhagen interpretation over BM. Second, we theoretically prove that both the widely-held Copenhagen interpretation and BM yield identical predictions for photon tunnelling in coupled waveguides, thereby resolving the contradiction raised by Ref.
S25
.

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{2m|c_{0}|^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}(|\Delta+\hbar J_{0}|-2\sqrt{\text{Max}\{\Delta^{2}-(\hbar J_{0})^{2},0\}}

\displaystyle+|\Delta-\hbar J_{0}|)^{2}x^{2}+O(x^{3}).

The authors in Ref.
S25
model
\rho_{a}
as
\rho_{a}=(J_{0}x/v)^{2}
. Though they emphasize this speed

“represent the the local speed of particles directly behind the potential step”, we still have concerns whether this is acceptable. Even if accepted,

cannot be directly compared to the Bohmian velocity (

is ambiguous—does it include a

is strictly along the

v

v_{s}
to ensure consistency with probability conservation.

Next, we derive the Bohmian velocity
v_{s}
from continuity equation:

v_{s}(x)=\frac{\hbar}{m}\text{Im}\frac{\psi^{*}\partial_{x}\psi}{\psi^{*}\psi}
. Using the solution in equation
3
,
v_{s,m}(x)
and

, all velocities vanish. This is expected: the wavefunction decays exponentially (purely evanescent), indicating photons are stationary along
x

|\psi_{a}|^{2}
and
|\psi_{m}|^{2}
persist in the waveguides, enabling tunnelling despite zero net flow.

To definitively demonstrate this experiment poses no challenge to BM, we prove BM yields identical photon populations
\rho_{a}
to the Schrödinger solution (equation
4
; full derivation in Methods). First, substituting the wavefunction in equations
15
into the Schrödinger equations yields new quantum Hamilton-Jacobi equations
16
and continuity equations
17
. The continuity equations contain a tunnelling current term
j_{0}=2J_{0}R_{a}R_{m}\text{sin}(S_{a}-S_{m})
, which accounts for inter-waveguide quantum tunnelling while preserving probability conservation. Solving the reduced continuity equation
\partial_{x}(\rho_{a,B}v_{a,x})+j_{0}=0
with BM-derived
v_{a,x}
and
j_{0}
gives
\rho_{a,B}
, which is presented in equation

in Methods. Subsequent analysis confirms exact agreement with the Schrödinger-predicted
\rho_{a}
(equation
4
). This result indicates that (i) the Bohmian velocity
v_{a,x}
generates the same
\rho_{a}
as standard Schrödinger equation, and (ii) both interpretations predict identical tunnelling dynamics, refuting claims in Ref.
S25
.

In conclusion, the experiment in Ref.
S25
poses no challenge to BM. The mistakes in Ref.
S25
primarily stem from misinterpreting the semi-classical speed
v
(equation
2
) and Bohmian velocity
v_{s}
as comparable quantities. We point out that these represent fundamentally distinct physical entities, their discrepancy cannot validate claims against BM. Furthermore, solving for
\rho_{a}
through both Schrödinger equation and continuity equation in BM yields identical results. This confirms no interpretational distinction exists between Copenhagen and BM for quantum tunneling phenomena. In other words, any velocity defined from
\rho_{a}
must be identical in both frameworks, meanwhile satisfy continuity equation. Last but not least,
v_{s}=0
in the region of
\Delta<-\hbar J_{0}
is physically expected. From the perspective of BM, photons are indeed at rest in the step potential along
x
direction, but by no means it imply that quantum tunnelling does not occur. In fact, non-vanishing probability of evanescent wavefunction still allows quantum tunnelling to come up.

Methods

Deriving the
\psi_{m}
,
\psi_{a}
solution from Schrödinger equation

The two coupled Schrödinger equations
1
in the main text satisfy:

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}(\psi_{m}+\psi_{a})=

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{d^{2}(\psi_{m}+\psi_{a})}{dx^{2}}+V_{0}(\psi_{m}+\psi_{a}),

(5)

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}(\psi_{m}-\psi_{a})=

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{d^{2}(\psi_{m}-\psi_{a})}{dx^{2}}

\displaystyle+V_{0}(\psi_{m}-\psi_{a})-2\hbar J_{0}(\psi_{m}-\psi_{a}).

We get two solutions:

\displaystyle\psi_{m}+\psi_{a}

\displaystyle=A_{+}e^{ik_{+}x}+B_{+}e^{-ik_{+}x},

(6)

\displaystyle\psi_{m}-\psi_{a}

\displaystyle=A_{-}e^{ik_{-}x}+B_{-}e^{-k_{-}x},

where

\displaystyle k_{+}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2m(E-V_{0})}{\hbar^{2}}}=\sqrt{\frac{2m(\Delta-\hbar J_{0})}{\hbar^{2}}},

(7)

\displaystyle k_{-}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2m(E-V_{0}+2\hbar J_{0})}{\hbar^{2}}}=\sqrt{\frac{2m(\Delta+\hbar J_{0})}{\hbar^{2}}},

\Delta=E-V_{0}+\hbar J_{0}
, and
A_{i},B_{i}
is the amplitude of each transmission mode.

Subsequently, the solution of
\psi_{m}
and
\psi_{a}
is:

\displaystyle\psi_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(A_{+}e^{ik_{+}x}+A_{-}e^{ik_{-}x}+B_{+}e^{-ik_{+}x}+B_{-}e^{-ik_{-}x}),

(8)

\displaystyle\psi_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(A_{+}e^{ik_{+}x}-A_{-}e^{ik_{-}x}+B_{+}e^{-ik_{+}x}-B_{-}e^{-ik_{-}x}).

According to the initial conditions that the initial auxiliary mode is zero and there is no back propagation mode, we have the following:

\displaystyle A_{+}

\displaystyle=c_{0},

(9)

\displaystyle A_{-}

\displaystyle=c_{0},

\displaystyle B_{+}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle B_{-}

\displaystyle=0.

Finally two transmitted wavefunctions are given by:

\displaystyle\psi_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{0}}{2}(e^{ik_{+}x}+e^{ik_{-}x}),

(10)

\displaystyle\psi_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{0}}{2}(e^{ik_{+}x}-e^{ik_{-}x}).

This result indicates that two modes (
k_{+}
and
k_{-}
) exist in two coupled waveguides with different energies.

\displaystyle\rho_{a}=

\displaystyle\frac{|c_{0}|^{2}}{4}(e^{i(k_{+}-k_{+}^{*})x}-e^{i(k_{-}-k_{+}^{*})x}

(11)

\displaystyle-e^{i(k_{+}-k_{-}^{*})x}+e^{i(k_{-}-k_{-}^{*})x}).

When
x
is small, we can expand
\rho_{a}
at

\displaystyle\rho_{a}=

\displaystyle-\frac{|c_{0}|^{2}}{8}((k_{+}-k_{+}^{*})^{2}+(k_{-}-k_{+}^{*})^{2}

(12)

\displaystyle+(k_{+}-k_{-}^{*})^{2}+(k_{-}-k_{-}^{*})^{2})x^{2}+O(x^{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m|c_{0}|^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}}(|\Delta+\hbar J_{0}|-2\sqrt{\text{Max}\{\Delta^{2}-(\hbar J_{0})^{2},0\}}

Similar to Ref.

\displaystyle+O(x^{3}).

If

Bohmian mechanics for quantum tunnelling

Since the Schrodinger equations are not in standard form, the Bohmian mechanics equations for quantum tunnelling between two coupled waveguides should be rederived.

We start from the wavefunction in the forms of:

\displaystyle\psi_{m}=R_{m}e^{iS_{m}},

(15)

\displaystyle\psi_{a}=R_{a}e^{iS_{a}},

and substitute them into
1
. The real parts correspond to two equations:

\displaystyle-\hbar\partial_{t}S_{m}=

\displaystyle\frac{(\hbar\partial_{x}S_{m})^{2}}{2m}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}R_{m}}{R_{m}}+V_{0}

(16)

\displaystyle+\hbar J_{0}\left(\frac{R_{a}}{R_{m}}\text{cos}(S_{a}-S_{m})-1\right),

\displaystyle-\hbar\partial_{t}S_{a}=

\displaystyle\frac{(\hbar\partial_{x}S_{a})^{2}}{2m}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}R_{a}}{R_{a}}+V_{0}

\displaystyle+\hbar J_{0}\left(\frac{R_{m}}{R_{a}}\text{cos}(S_{m}-S_{a})-1\right),

which are the Hamilton-Jacobi equations of the hidden variable particles. Here,
-\hbar\partial_{t}S_{i}
is the total energy of particles of these two modes,
\frac{(\hbar\partial_{x}S_{i})^{2}}{2m}
is the kinetic energy,
-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}R_{i}}{R_{i}}
is the quantum potential,
V_{0}
is the external potential, and
\hbar J_{0}(\frac{R_{j}}{R_{i}}\text{cos}(S_{j}-S_{i})-1)
is the coupling energy between these two modes.

The imagine parts also correspond to two equations:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(R^{2}_{m})+\partial_{x}\left(\frac{R^{2}_{m}\hbar\partial_{x}S_{m}}{m}\right)+2J_{0}R_{a}R_{m}\text{sin}(S_{m}-S_{a})=0,

(17)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(R^{2}_{a})+\partial_{x}\left(\frac{R^{2}_{a}\hbar\partial_{x}S_{a}}{m}\right)+2J_{0}R_{m}R_{a}\text{sin}(S_{a}-S_{m})=0,

which are the continuity equations for two coupled waveguide system. Noting that (i) another term appears in continuity equations, which describe the coupling between two waveguides; and (ii) the
x
-direction velocity of these two modes can still be written as:

\displaystyle v_{x,m}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{m}\partial_{x}S_{m}=\frac{\hbar}{m}\text{Im}\frac{\psi^{*}_{m}\partial_{x}\psi_{m}}{\psi^{*}_{m}\psi_{m}},

(18)

\displaystyle v_{x,a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{m}\partial_{x}S_{a}=\frac{\hbar}{m}\text{Im}\frac{\psi^{*}_{a}\partial_{x}\psi_{a}}{\psi^{*}_{a}\psi_{a}}.

Solving
\rho_{a,B}
using Bohmian mechanics

Since the transmitted wave is monochromatic wave, the time evolution part is
e^{-i\omega t}
, so
\rho
doesn’t contain
t
, namely
\partial_{t}\rho=0
. The remaining part of the continuity equation of auxiliary mode is :

\partial_{x}(\rho_{a,B}v_{a,x})+j_{0}=0,

(19)

where
j_{0}=2J_{0}R_{m}R_{a}\text{sin}(S_{a}-S_{m})=-iJ_{0}(\psi_{a}\psi^{*}_{m}-\psi^{*}_{a}\psi_{m})
.

When
x
is sufficiently small, a Taylor expansion of
v_{a,x}
and
j_{0}
at
x=0
leads to:

\displaystyle v_{a,x}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{m}\text{Im}(i\frac{k_{-}+k_{+}}{2}+O(x^{1})),

(20)

\displaystyle j_{0}

\displaystyle=|c_{0}|^{2}(\frac{J_{0}}{2}(-k_{-}-k_{-}^{*}+k_{+}+k_{+}^{*})x+O(x^{2})).

Substituting them into the continuity equation
19
, we have:

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\left[\rho_{a,B}\frac{\hbar}{m}\text{Im}\left(i\frac{k_{-}+k_{+}}{2}+O(x^{1})\right)\right]

(21)

\displaystyle+|c_{0}|^{2}\frac{J_{0}}{2}(-k_{-}-k_{-}^{*}+k_{+}+k_{+}^{*})x+O(x^{2})=0.

After integration, we have:

\displaystyle\rho_{a,B}

\displaystyle=|c_{0}|^{2}\frac{\frac{J_{0}}{4}(k_{-}+k_{-}^{*}-k_{+}-k_{+}^{*})x^{2}+O(x^{3})}{\frac{\hbar}{m}\text{Im}\left(i\frac{k_{-}+k_{+}}{2}+O(x^{1})\right)},

(22)

\displaystyle=|c_{0}|^{2}\frac{mJ_{0}}{\hbar}\frac{k_{-}+k_{-}^{*}-k_{+}-k_{+}^{*}}{k_{-}+k_{-}^{*}+k_{+}+k_{+}^{*}}x^{2}+O(x^{3}).

When
\Delta>\hbar J_{0}
, equation
22
becomes:

\displaystyle\rho_{a,B}

\displaystyle=|c_{0}|^{2}\frac{mJ_{0}}{\hbar}\frac{\sqrt{\Delta+\hbar J_{0}}-\sqrt{\Delta-\hbar J_{0}}}{\sqrt{\Delta+\hbar J_{0}}+\sqrt{\Delta-\hbar J_{0}}}x^{2}+O(x^{3})

(23)

\displaystyle=2m|c_{0}|^{2}\frac{J_{0}^{2}}{(\sqrt{\Delta+\hbar J_{0}}+\sqrt{\Delta-\hbar J_{0}})^{2}}x^{2}+O(x^{3}).

When
|\Delta|<\hbar J_{0}
, equation
22
becomes:

\rho_{a}=|c_{0}|^{2}\frac{mJ_{0}}{\hbar}x^{2}+O(x^{3}).

(24)

When
\Delta<-\hbar J_{0}
, we perform the analytic continuation:

\Delta=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\Delta+i\epsilon,

(25)

where
\epsilon
is a small value. Noting this is a common technique in quantum field theory, which involves selecting a complex part approaching zero through analytic continuation of real-valued quantities. The rationale here lies in the fact that, even in the case of evanescent waves where the average value of
v_{x}
vanishes, the velocities of individual hidden variable particles conform to a distribution centered around these averages. Furthermore, this distribution must satisfy the equilibrium condition in a perturbative sense, where the infinitesimal imagine part corresponds to an infinitesimal perturbation of the propagation modes. Consequently, the particle number distribution is still required to obey the continuity equation under this velocity-dependent framework.

From
\sqrt{x+iy}=\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}+x}{2}}+i\text{sgn}(y)\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}-x}{2}}
(here we take the positive sign, corresponding to the physically reasonable value in physics, i.e., the evanescent wave solution), we have:

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}(\sqrt{\Delta+\hbar J_{0}+i\epsilon}+(\sqrt{\Delta+\hbar J_{0}+i\epsilon})^{*})

(26)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}2\sqrt{\frac{|\Delta+\hbar J_{0}|(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2|\Delta+\hbar J_{0}|^{2}}+O(\epsilon^{4}))+\Delta+\hbar J_{0}}{2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{|\Delta+\hbar J_{0}|}+O(\epsilon^{4})},

and

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}(\sqrt{\Delta-\hbar J_{0}+i\epsilon}+(\sqrt{\Delta-\hbar J_{0}+i\epsilon})^{*})

(27)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}2\sqrt{\frac{|\Delta-\hbar J_{0}|(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2|\Delta-\hbar J_{0}|^{2}}+O(\epsilon^{4}))+\Delta-\hbar J_{0}}{2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{|\Delta-\hbar J_{0}|}+O(\epsilon^{4})},

so equation
22
becomes:

\displaystyle\rho_{a}

\displaystyle=|c_{0}|^{2}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{mJ_{0}}{\hbar}\frac{\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{|\Delta+\hbar J_{0}|}}-\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{|\Delta-\hbar J_{0}|}}}{\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{|\Delta+\hbar J_{0}|}}+\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{|\Delta-\hbar J_{0}|}}}x^{2}+O(x^{3})

(28)

\displaystyle=|c_{0}|^{2}\frac{mJ_{0}}{\hbar}\frac{\sqrt{|\Delta-\hbar J_{0}|}-\sqrt{|\Delta+\hbar J_{0}|}}{\sqrt{|\Delta-\hbar J_{0}|}+\sqrt{|\Delta+\hbar J_{0}|}}x^{2}+O(x^{3})

\displaystyle=2m|c_{0}|^{2}\frac{J_{0}^{2}}{(\sqrt{|\Delta+\hbar J_{0}|}+\sqrt{|\Delta-\hbar J_{0}|})^{2}}x^{2}+O(x^{3}).

Hereto, we see that all these results are consistent with what we derived from Schrödinger equation. There is no any distinction between Copenhagen interpretion and BM on the problem of quantum tunnelling. Any velocity defined from
\rho_{a}
must be identical in both frameworks, and they must satisfy continuity equation.
