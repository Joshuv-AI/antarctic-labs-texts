The Complexity of Deciding Characteristic Formulae Modulo Nested Simulation (extended abstract)
Luca Aceto, Antonis Achilleos, Aggeliki Chalki, Anna Ingólfsdóttir

Abstract
This paper studies the complexity of determining whether a formula in the modal logics characterizing the nested-simulation semantics is characteristic for some process, which is equivalent to determining whether the formula is satisfiable and prime. The main results are that the problem of determining whether a formula is prime in the modal logic characterizing the 2-nested-simulation preorder is 𝖼𝗈𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{coNP} -complete and is 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} -complete in the case of the n n -nested-simulation preorder, when n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 . This establishes that deciding characteristic formulae for the n n -nested simulation semantics is 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} -complete, when n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 . In the case of the 2-nested simulation semantics, that problem lies in the complexity class 𝖣𝖯 \mathsf{DP} , which consists of languages that can be expressed as the intersection of one language in 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} and of one in 𝖼𝗈𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{coNP} .

Introduction

Since the pioneering work by Hennessy and Milner
[
21
,
23
]
, behavioural equivalences and preorders over processes have been given logical characterizations, typically using some modal logic—see, for instance,
[
17
]
for a survey. Such characterizations state that two processes are related by some behavioural equivalence if, and only if, they satisfy the same properties expressible in some logic.
A formula
\varphi
is characteristic for a process
p
with respect to some behavioural equivalence or preorder
\lesssim
if every process
q
satisfies
\varphi
exactly when
p\lesssim q
holds.
Thus, characteristic formulae provide a complete logical description of processes up to some notion of behavioural equivalence or preorder. As shown in, e.g.,
[
5
,
8
,
13
,
18
,
26
]
, the logics that underlie classic modal characterization theorems for equivalences and preorders over processes allow one to express characteristic formulae. Moreover, the procedures for constructing characteristic formulae presented in those works
reduce equivalence and preorder checking to model checking—see
[
15
]
for an application of this approach.
The converse reduction from model checking problems to equivalence and preorder checking problems is only possible when the logical specifications are characteristic formulae
[
12
]
. This raises the natural question of how to determine whether a specification, expressed as a modal formula, is characteristic for some process and of the computational complexity of that problem.
In
[
5
,
6
,
12
]
, it was shown that characteristic formulae coincide with those that are both
satisfiable and prime
. (A formula is prime if whenever it entails a disjunction
\varphi_{1}\vee\varphi_{2}
, then it entails
\varphi_{1}
or
\varphi_{2}
.) So, determining whether a formula is characteristic is equivalent to checking its satisfiability and primality.

In the setting of the modal logics that characterize bisimilarity,
the problem of checking whether a formula is characteristic has the same complexity as validity checking, which is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete for Hennessy-Milner logic (
\mathbf{HML}
) and
\mathsf{EXP}
-complete for its extension with fixed-point operators and the
\mu
-calculus
[
4
,
9
]
. In
[
2
]
, we studied the complexity of the problem modulo the simulation-based semantics considered by van Glabbeek in his seminal linear-time/branching-time spectrum
[
17
]
. In op. cit., we identified the complexity of satisfiability and primality for fragments of
\mathbf{HML}
that characterize various relations in the spectrum, delineating the boundary between the logics for which those problems can be solved in polynomial time and the logics for which they are computationally hard.
Both satisfiablity and primality checking are decidable in polynomial time for simulation, complete simulation, and ready simulation when the set of actions has constant size. Computational hardness already manifests itself in ready simulation semantics
[
11
,
22
]
when the size of the action set is not a constant: in this case, the problems of checking satisfiability and primality for formulae in the logic characterizing the ready simulation preorder are
\mathsf{NP}
-complete and
\mathsf{coNP}
-complete, respectively. In the presence of at least two actions, for the logic characterizing the 2-nested-simulation preorder, satisfiability and primality checking are
\mathsf{NP}
-complete and
\mathsf{coNP}
-hard, respectively, while deciding whether a formula is characteristic is
\mathsf{US}
-hard
[
10
]
(that is, it is at least as hard as the problem of deciding whether a given Boolean formula has exactly one satisfying truth assignment). Moreover, all three problems—satisfiability and primality checking, and deciding characteristic formulae—are
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hard in the modal logic for the 3-nested-simulation preorder
[
19
]
. Additionally, deciding characteristic formulae modulo the equivalence relations induced by the 2-nested and 3-nested-simulation preorders is
\mathsf{coNP}
-hard and
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hard, respectively.

The family of nested-simulation equivalences and preorders were introduced by Groote and Vaandrager in
[
19
]

The work presented in
[

did not provide upper bounds on the complexity of the aforementioned problems for the family of
n
-nested-simulation semantics with

n
-nested-simulation preorders, with

, is in
\mathsf{coNP}
and
\mathsf{PSPACE}
, respectively. These results, combined with previously-known lower bounds, imply that the problem is
\mathsf{coNP}
-complete and
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete, respectively (Theorems
19
and
21
). Taking into account the complexity of the satisfiability problem for the respective logics, we show that deciding characteristic formulae for the
n
-nested-simulation semantics is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete when
n\geq 3
(Corollaries
17
and
20
) and is in the complexity class
\mathsf{DP}
for the 2-nested-simulation semantics (Corollary

Our algorithms
are based on two families of two-player, zero-sum games that are initiated on a modal formula
\varphi
(Section
3
). In the first type of game, defined for every
n\geq 1
, player
B
constructs two structures corresponding to two arbitrary processes that satisfy
\varphi
and player
A
wins if she manages to show that the first process is
n
-nested-simulated by the second one. These games are used to determine whether all processes satisfying

\varphi
are equivalent modulo the
n
-nested-simulation equivalence, and thus whether
\varphi

is characteristic modulo the
n
-nested-simulation equivalence relation. The second class of games, introduced for
n\geq 3
, is designed so that when a game is initiated on a satisfiable formula
\varphi
, a winning strategy for player

is equivalent to
\varphi
being prime, and hence characteristic, modulo the
n
-nested-simulation preorder. By adapting the algorithms that arise from these games, we provide
\mathsf{coNP}
algorithms for the case of the 2-nested-simulation preorder and equivalence relation in Section
4

Preliminaries

Concurrency theory and logic

In this paper, we model processes as finite, loop-free
labelled transition systems
(LTS). A finite LTS is a triple
\mathcal{S}=(\mathtt{Proc},\mathtt{Act},\longrightarrow)
, where
\mathtt{Proc}
is a finite set of states (or processes),
\mathtt{Act}
is a finite, non-empty set of actions and
{\longrightarrow}\subseteq\mathtt{Proc}\times\mathtt{Act}\times\mathtt{Proc}
is a transition relation. As usual, we use
p\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\scriptsize$a$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q
instead of
(p,a,q)\in{\longrightarrow}
. For each
t\in\mathtt{Act}^{*}
, we write
p\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\scriptsize$t$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q
to mean that there is a sequence of transitions labelled with
t
starting from
p
and ending at
q
. An LTS is
loop-free
iff
p\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\scriptsize$t$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}}p
holds only when
t
is the empty trace
\varepsilon
. A process
q
is
reachable
from
p
if
p\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\scriptsize$t$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q
, for some
t\in\mathtt{Act}^{*}
.
We define the
size
of an LTS
\mathcal{S}=(\mathtt{Proc},\mathtt{Act},\longrightarrow)
, denoted
|\mathcal{S}|
, to be
|\mathtt{Proc}|+|{\longrightarrow}|
. The
size of a process

p\in\mathtt{Proc}
, denoted
|p|
, is the cardinality of
\mathrm{reach}(p)=\{q\penalty\ |\penalty\ q\text{ is reachable from }p\}
plus the cardinality of the set
\longrightarrow
restricted to
\mathrm{reach}(p)
.
A sequence of actions
t\in\mathtt{Act}^{*}
is a trace of
p
if there is a
q
such that
p\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\scriptsize$t$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q
.
The
depth
of a finite, loop-free process
p
, denoted
0pt(p)
, is the length of a longest trace
t
of
p
.
In what follows, we shall often describe finite, loop-free processes using the fragment of Milner’s CCS
[
24
]
given by the grammar

p::=\mathtt{0}\penalty\ \mid\penalty\ a.p\penalty\ \mid\penalty\ p+p
,
where
a\in\mathtt{Act}
. For each action

and terms
p,p^{\prime}
, we write
p\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\scriptsize$a$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}}p^{\prime}
iff
(i)
p=a.p^{\prime}
or
(ii)

, for some
p_{1},p_{2}
, and
p_{1}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\scriptsize$a$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}}p^{\prime}
or
p_{2}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\scriptsize$a$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}}p^{\prime}
holds.

Truth in an LTS

\displaystyle p\models\langle a\rangle\varphi\text{ iff there is

\displaystyle p\models[a]\varphi\text{ iff for all }p\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\scriptsize$a$}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}}q\text{ it holds that }q\models\varphi.

If

An
alternating Turing machine
is a non-deterministic Turing machine whose set of states is partitioned into existential and universal states. An existential state is accepting if at least one of its transitions leads to an accepting state. In contrast, a universal state is accepting only if all its transitions lead to accepting states. The machine as a whole accepts an input if its initial state is accepting. The complexity class
\mathsf{AP}
is the class of languages accepted by polynomial-time alternating Turing machines.
An
oracle Turing machine
is a Turing machine that has access to an oracle—a “black box” capable of solving a specific computational problem in a single operation. An oracle Turing machine can perform all the usual operations of a Turing machine, and can also query the oracle to obtain a solution to any instance of the computational problem for that oracle. We use
\textsf{C}^{\textsf{C'}[\text{poly}]}
to denote the complexity class of languages decidable by an algorithm in class
C
that makes polynomially many oracle calls to a language in
C’
. Note, for example, that
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}^{{\mathsf{PSPACE}}[\text{poly}]}={\mathsf{PSPACE}}
, since a polynomial-space oracle Turing machine can simulate any
\mathsf{PSPACE}
oracle query by solving the problem itself in polynomial space.

Consider now two-player games that have the following characteristics: they are
zero-sum
(that is, player one’s gain is equivalent to player two’s loss),

(meaning that, at every point in the game, each player is fully aware of all events that have previously occurred),
polynomial-depth
(i.e. the games proceed for a number of rounds that is polynomial in the input size), and
computationally bounded
(that is, for each round, the computation performed by a player can be simulated by a Turing machine in polynomial time in the input size). For two-player games that have all four characteristics described above, there is a polynomial-time alternating Turing machine that decides whether one of the players has a winning strategy
[

p_{1}

states that witness the failure of
r_{1}\lesssim_{S}r_{2}

\mathcal{L}_{2S}

Algorithm
\mathtt{Prime_{2S}}
decides whether its input
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{2S}
is prime:
\varphi
is prime iff every execution of
\mathtt{Prime_{2S}}(\varphi)
accepts. This algorithm runs
\mathtt{ConPro}(\varphi)
twice. If
\mathtt{ConPro}(\varphi)
fails to return an output,
\mathtt{Prime_{2S}}(\varphi)
rejects at line 5—this line deals with unsatisfiability. For every two processes
p_{1},p_{2}
that satisfy
\varphi
, at line 7,
\mathtt{Prime_{2S}}(\varphi)
constructs their maximal lower bound, denoted
\mathrm{mlb}_{\lesssim_{2S}}(p_{1},p_{2})
, which is a process
g
that is 2-nested-simulated by both
p_{i}
’s and
r\lesssim_{2S}g
, for every process
r
such that
r\lesssim_{2S}p_{i}
. Processes
p_{1},p_{2}
have a maximal lower bound iff
p_{1}\equiv_{S}p_{2}
.
In case
\mathrm{mlb}_{\lesssim_{2S}}(p_{1},p_{2})
does not exist, the algorithm discovers two processes satisfying
\varphi
such that there is no process that is 2-nested-simulated by both of them and it rejects the input—
\varphi
is not prime. On the other hand, if
\mathrm{mlb}_{\lesssim_{2S}}(p_{1},p_{2})
exists, then there is a process that is 2-nested simulated by both
p_{i}
’s and it can be constructed in polynomial time. It remains to check whether
\mathrm{mlb}_{\lesssim_{2S}}(p_{1},p_{2})
satisfies
\varphi
. If so, then the second condition described above is met, and the algorithm accepts. If
\mathrm{mlb}_{\lesssim_{2S}}(p_{1},p_{2})\not\models\varphi
it can be shown that there is no process
r
satisfying
\varphi
that is 2-nested-simulated by both
p_{i}
’s, and the algorithm rejects at line 10. This establishes Theorem
21
. By slightly adjusting
\mathtt{Prime_{2S}}
, we can show that deciding whether all processes satisfying a formula in
\mathcal{L}_{2S}
are 2-nested-simulation equivalent is in
\mathsf{coNP}
. By these results and the
\mathsf{NP}
-completeness of the satisfiability problem for
\mathcal{L}_{2S}
, we obtain the upper bound given in Corollary
22
.
