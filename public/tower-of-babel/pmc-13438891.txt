Relationship Between Sexual Attitude and Sexual Function Among Iranian Women and Men During Pregnancy: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Sepideh Mashayekh‐Amiri, Mobina Ahmadi, Elnaz Hemati, Mojgan Mirghafourvand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSexual function is considered an essential criterion for enhancing the quality of life, encompassing not only the genital component but also mental and physical factors. The present study was conducted to determine the relationship between sexual attitude and sexual function among Iranian women and men during pregnancy.MethodsA total of 404 participants, including 240 pregnant women and 164 spouses, who visited health centers affiliated with Tabriz University of Medical Sciences between February 2023 and August 2023. To collect data, the study utilized the socio‐demographic questionnaire, the Attitude Scale toward Sexuality during Pregnancy (AStSdP), the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI), and the International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF). The Pearson correlation test was used for bivariate analysis, while a General Linear Model (GLM) was applied for multivariate analysis.ResultsA total of 404 participants were included in the study, with a response rate of 100% (240/240) in women and 68.3% (164/240) in men. The results indicated that 65% (95% CI: 59.0%–71.0%) of women (156/240) and 15.9% (95% CI: 10.3%–21.4%) of men (26/164) experienced sexual dysfunction. According to Pearson correlation test, there was a significant positive correlation between the total sexual attitude scores and its subscales with the total sexual function scores among couples (p < 0.001). The results of the GLM, indicated that total sexual attitude score, marital satisfaction, and job were significantly associated with sexual function scores in both women and men.ConclusionThe findings of the present study showed that nearly half of the couples had a positive sexual attitude toward sexual activity during pregnancy. However, a high prevalence of sexual dysfunction was observed, providing accurate information and counseling by reproductive health and midwifery specialists may be associated with improved sexual function during pregnancy.Keywords: pregnancy, sexual behavior, sexual health, sexual partners

Introduction

Sexuality is a multidimensional experience (genital, physical, and mental) that is considered an essential and inherent aspect of human existence, encouraging the pursuit of love, intimacy, sexual relations, and closeness [1]. Sexual function is also a crucial part of an individual's personality and is a fundamental cornerstone in strengthening couple relationships, significantly impacting quality of life [2]. Sexual function is defined as the body's response during different phases of the sexual cycle (excitement, plateau, orgasm, and resolution). It is characterized not only by the absence of dysfunction in these stages but also by subjective satisfaction with the frequency and outcomes of sexual behavior for both the individual and their partner [3]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), safe sexual function is not merely the absence of dysfunction but rather a state of physical, emotional, psychological, and social well‐being related to sexuality [4].

Sexual function is a dynamic component that evolves and is influenced by context and the partner involved [5]. Women's sexuality undergoes significant changes during different biological phases of life, including pregnancy. Pregnancy is a unique physiological and psychological experience that may affect couples’ relationships, challenge marital and sexual bonds, and alter sexual dynamics [6]. During this period, factors such as decreased self‐perceived attractiveness, changes in body image, fear of harming the fetus, concerns about miscarriage, and the risk of preterm birth can negatively impact women's sexual responses [7]. Similarly, men's fears of causing harm to their partner or the baby or inducing labor may also affect their sexual responses, ultimately leading to a decline in couples’ overall sexual function [8]. These concerns can result in sexual avoidance and feelings of guilt regarding intimacy during pregnancy [9].

Sexual function indicators during pregnancy significantly decline, especially in the third trimester. The frequency of sexual activity during pregnancy is generally low and reaches its lowest levels in both men and women during the third trimester [2]. Men also experience hormonal changes during their partner's pregnancy, including lower testosterone and cortisol levels and higher estrogen levels. Reduced testosterone levels are associated with a decline in sexual desire for many men by the end of their partner's second trimester. Therefore, sexual dysfunction during pregnancy is not an unexpected phenomenon [10]. In this regard, a systematic review by Ouyaba et al. on the prevalence of sexual dysfunction in pregnant women and its associated factors revealed that 69.7% of pregnant women suffer from sexual dysfunction during pregnancy. Furthermore, factors such as maternal age, husband's age, and the duration of marriage were negatively correlated with sexual dysfunction scores, while the level of education showed a positive correlation [11].

Sexuality is a central organizing structure that encompasses attitudes, behaviors, and values related to gender, sexual identity, eroticism, intimacy, pleasure, sexual desire, and reproduction [12]. Studies indicate that most sexual problems during pregnancy stem from attitudes, taboos, lack of awareness, and fear. Among the key determinants of sexual function during pregnancy are attitudes toward sexual activity during pregnancy, health‐related quality of life (HRQoL), and adverse symptoms during sexual activity [13]. Among these factors, attitudes toward sexual activity during pregnancy play a particularly significant role. Attitude can be defined as a psychological tendency expressed by evaluating an entity or object as favorable or unfavorable to a certain degree [14].

Sexual attitudes are beliefs with a heavy emotional weight that influence how individuals respond positively or negatively to sexual stimuli [15]. These attitudes, which manifest in an individual's sexual behavior, are shaped by family and cultural perspectives on sexual desires, previous sexual experiences, and sexual education. They are also related to demographic characteristics, especially gender, education level, and race/ethnicity [16]. In this regard, the study by Altınayak et al. on attitudes toward sexual function during pregnancy showed that 51.7% of pregnant women had a positive attitude toward sexual function during pregnancy [17]. Additionally, the study by Jamali et al. on sexual activity and male sexual attitudes during their wives’ pregnancy revealed that approximately 62.2% of men had a negative attitude toward sexual desires [18]. Another study by Dikmen et al. found that six out of ten pregnant women had a positive attitude toward sexual function during pregnancy. Sexual self‐awareness, sexual shyness, and the husband's education level influenced pregnant women's attitudes toward sexual desires during pregnancy [19]. Furthermore, the study by Silva et al. showed that sexual attitudes are influenced by demographic variables such as age, duration of marriage, and education level [1].

Transformative approaches to sexual health are essential for achieving comprehensive sexual and reproductive rights, advancing health and gender justice, and protecting bodily autonomy throughout life. Since sexual relations are considered a taboo subject in many countries, including Iran, individuals are often reluctant to discuss their sexual problems. As a result, sexual dysfunction is often underdiagnosed and undertreated compared to its actual prevalence [20]. Furthermore, much of the existing evidence has examined sexual health in a reductionist manner, focusing primarily on women's health and sexually transmitted infections. This has led to the neglect of sexual well‐being and health in couples, particularly men [21]. Therefore, more significant efforts are needed to prioritize sexual health and well‐being within the broader framework of sexual and reproductive health to strengthen equitable and inclusive healthcare systems for all individuals. Studies on the relationship between sexual attitudes and sexual function in couples, especially during pregnancy, are limited [7]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first Iranian couple‐based study to determine the relationship between sexual attitudes and sexual function in Iranian women and men during pregnancy using the AStSdP. We hypothesized that positive sexual attitudes are associated with higher sexual function scores in both women and men during pregnancy.

Methods

Study Design

This cross‐sectional study was conducted on 404 Iranian women and men (240 pregnant women and 164 spouses) who visited health centers affiliated with Tabriz University of Medical Sciences. The study employed cluster random sampling after obtaining approval from the Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (IR. TBZMED. REC.1401.393). The study followed all ethical principles from February 2023 to August 2023. This study has been reported following the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cross‐sectional studies [22].

Participants and Data Collection

Inclusion criteria for the study included residence in Tabriz, singleton pregnancy, being a primiparous or multiparous woman, and having a stable relationship and cohabitation with the spouse. Exclusion criteria included a self‐reported history of sexual dysfunction, a history of depression in either partner based on self‐report, the use of antidepressant medications (such as tricyclic antidepressants [amitriptyline, clomipramine, dosulepin, doxepin, imipramine, lofepramine, nortriptyline, trazodone], serotonin reuptake inhibitors [citalopram, fluoxetine, paroxetine, sertraline], monoamine oxidase inhibitors [phenelzine, tranylcypromine], serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors [reboxetine, venlafaxine]), occurrence of a stressful event such as the death of a first‐degree family member or the diagnosis of a terminal or incurable illness for a family member in the past 3 months, drug use by either partner, obstetric complications such as placenta previa, abruption, preeclampsia, high‐risk pregnancies (e.g., diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, or a dead or malformed fetus), and the presence of chronic diseases in men such as diabetes and cardiovascular diseases.

In the present study, a cluster random sampling method was used. Each health center was considered one cluster. A total of 92 health centers were available in the study region. First, one quarter of these centers (23 centers) were randomly selected using the website www.random.org. A probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling method was used to determine the sample size per cluster. Accordingly, the number of participants selected from each health center was proportional to the total number of pregnant women covered by that center, followed by random selection of individuals. Then, the researcher visited the selected centers and extracted a list of pregnant women and their phone numbers from the SIB system (Integrated Health System). Women were then randomly selected from the prepared list. The researcher contacted the women chosen by phone and screened them along with their spouses according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. If they met the study's inclusion criteria, the researcher provided them with information about the research process and the confidentiality of their information, and participation in the survey was offered to them. If both partners agreed to participate, they were asked to attend the health center at a specified time. During the visit, informed consent was obtained from the participants. The objectives and methods of the study were thoroughly explained to all eligible couples, and if they were willing to participate, written informed consent was obtained. Then, the sociodemographic checklist, the Attitude Scale toward Sexuality during Pregnancy (AStSdP), the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI), and the International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF) were completed anonymously. If male participants did not attend the health centers with their spouses, women were asked to provide the questionnaires to their husbands and return them to the researcher during their next visit for prenatal care at the respective health centers. It is important to note that this indirect interaction with men, in cases where they did not attend the health center, the inability of the researcher to persuade men to complete the questionnaire, work commitments of men, and feelings of shame and embarrassment regarding sexual issues in Iranian culture may have negatively impacted the response rate from men, leading to a lower response rate compared to women in the present study.

Measures

The four questionnaires used in the present study included: Sociodemographic Checklist, AStSdP, FSFI, and IIEF.

Sociodemographic Checklist

The sociodemographic checklist included information such as age, age at marriage, duration of marriage, gestational age, gravidity, education level, job, adequacy of monthly income, marital satisfaction, tobacco use, alcohol consumption, chronic diseases, and history of sexual disorders in the couple. This questionnaire aimed to collect essential demographic and social data to help analyze the factors that might influence sexual attitudes and functions in couples during pregnancy.

AStSdP

This tool was developed by Yılmaz Sezer and colleagues (2021) in Turkey and contains 34 items with three factors, designed to assess the sexual attitudes of couples (pregnant women and their husbands) during pregnancy (23). The items encompass the cognitive, emotional, and behavioral aspects of sexual attitudes. The factors of the questionnaire include Dysfunctional Beliefs and Values about Sexuality during Pregnancy (10 items), Approving Sexuality during Pregnancy (15 items), and Anxiety about Sexual Intercourse during Pregnancy (9 items). The scoring range for the first domain is 10–50, for the second domain is 15–75, and for the third domain is 9–45. Fourteen items reflect a positive attitude (1, 2, 5, 6, 11, 14, 20, 21, 23, 24, 28, 31, 32, 33, and 34), while twenty items reflect a negative attitude (3, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 13, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 22, 25, 26, 27, 29, and 30). Negative attitude items are reverse‐scored. The 34 items of the questionnaire are completed using a five‐point Likert scale (Strongly Disagree (1), Disagree (2), Neutral (3), Agree (4), Strongly Agree (5)). The minimum score on this questionnaire is 34, and the maximum score is 170. The cutoff point of the scale is 111.5, meaning that individuals who score above this point (AStSdP score ≥ 111.5) are interpreted as having a positive sexual attitude toward intercourse during pregnancy. In the first domain (dysfunctional beliefs and values about sexual activity during pregnancy [DBV]), a higher score reflects a more positive attitude toward sexual desires in terms of beliefs and values. In the second domain (ASP), a higher score reflects a more positive attitude toward sexual desires during pregnancy. Similarly, in the third domain (anxiety about sexual intercourse during pregnancy [ASI]), a higher score indicates less concern about intercourse during pregnancy. Overall, higher scores indicate a more positive attitude toward sexual activity during pregnancy. The validity and reliability of this tool in the Turkish population were confirmed with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.90 [23]. In the present study, the validity and reliability of the AStSdP were assessed, with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.78 and an intra‐class correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.85.

FSFI

This questionnaire was developed by Rosen and colleagues in 2000 [24]. The FSFI is a valid and reliable measure for assessing women's sexual function. This questionnaire is a patient‐reported outcome measure (PROM) that contains 19 questions, covering six domains of female sexual function: desire (questions 1 and 2, factor 0.6), arousal (questions 3–6, factor 0.3), lubrication (questions 7–10, factor 0.3), orgasm (questions 11–13, factor 0.4), satisfaction (questions 14–16, factor 0.4), and pain (questions 17–19, factor 0.4), which assess the past 4 weeks. Regarding scoring, since the number of questions in each domain is not equal, the scores for each domain's questions are first summed and then multiplied by the respective factor for that domain. Questions 1, 2, 15, and 16 are scored from 1 to 5, while the other items are scored from 0 to 5. After adjusting the scores for each domain, the maximum score for each domain becomes 6, and the total score for the scale becomes 36. The total score range is from 2 to 36, with a cutoff point of 26.55. Women with scores of 26.55 or lower (≤ 26.55) are considered to have sexual dysfunction. The validity and reliability of the Iranian version of FSFI were confirmed by Fakhri and colleagues with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient for each domain of the FSFI (ranging from 0.73 to 0.86) [25].

IIEF

This questionnaire was developed by Rosen and colleagues in 1997 [26]. The IIEF is a multidimensional self‐reporting tool widely used to assess men's sexual function over the past month, and it is currently available in 32 languages worldwide. The questionnaire consists of 15 items across five domains. The five domains of the IIEF are erectile function (items 1–5 and 15), orgasmic function (items 9 and 10), sexual desire (items 11 and 12), satisfaction with sexual intercourse (items 6–8), and overall satisfaction (items 13 and 14). Responses to the questions are given on a 6‐point Likert scale (for items 1–10) or a 5‐point scale (for items 11–15). Higher scores indicate better sexual function. The minimum and maximum scores range from 15 to 75. Scores for each domain are obtained by adding the individual item scores within that domain. The severity of sexual dysfunction is determined based on the score obtained, with the following classification: 1–10 indicates severe dysfunction, 11–16 indicates moderate dysfunction, 17–21 indicates mild to moderate dysfunction, 22–25 indicates mild dysfunction, and 26–30 indicates no sexual dysfunction. The validity and reliability of this questionnaire in Iran have been confirmed by Pakpour and colleagues with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient for each domain of the IIEF (ranging from 0.85 to 0.96) [27].

Sample Size

To determine the sample size in the present study, the sample size was assessed based on two variables: sexual attitude and sexual function. The sample size was determined based on the study by Shayan et al. [28], considering a significance level of 5%, a power of 95%, and a correlation coefficient of 0.29. The sample size was set at 120 participants; however, due to cluster sampling (Design effect = 2), the final sample size was considered to be 240 couples.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS software (Version 26.0, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for data analysis. In descriptive statistics, frequency and percentage were used to describe categorical variables. The mean and standard deviation were used to describe continuous variables. Visual inspection and skewness and kurtosis were examined to assess the normality of quantitative data, indicating a normal distribution of the data. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using skewness and kurtosis. Based on the accepted thresholds (skewness < |2| and kurtosis < |7 | ), all variables were approximately normally distributed. Specifically, skewness values ranged from –0.909 to –0.128, and kurtosis values ranged from –0.454 to +0.263. Therefore, parametric tests were used for analysis.

To determine the relationship between sexual attitudes and their dimensions with sexual function in couples, Pearson correlation was used in the bivariate analysis, and the general linear model was applied in multivariate analysis. Prior to running the General Linear Model (GLM), the assumptions of linearity (scatter plots), multicollinearity (VIF < 2.5), and homoscedasticity (plot of standardized residuals vs. predicted values) were tested and confirmed. For this purpose, first, the relationship between the couples’ sociodemographic characteristics and sexual function scores was assessed using the independent t‐test and one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA). In the next step, all variables that had a significant relationship with sexual function (p < 0.1), along with the sexual attitude variable, were included in the GLM. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For the correlation analyses presented in Table 3, which involved multiple comparisons, a sensitivity analysis was performed using the Benjamini‐Hochberg correction to control the false discovery rate (FDR) at 0.05. All associations that were significant before correction (p < 0.05), remained significant after FDR adjustment (q < 0.05). To address the clustering effect of the sampling design, we used cluster‐robust standard errors in the GLM.

Ethical Consideration

The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (IR. TBZMED. REC.1401.393). All ethical principles were followed, including obtaining written informed consent from all participants (pregnant women and their spouses), providing comprehensive explanations by the researcher about the objectives, methods, and reasons for conducting the research, ensuring the possibility of withdrawal from the study at any stage, and assuring participants of the confidentiality of their information.

Results

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics of the Iranian Couples

A total of 404 participants (240 pregnant women and 164 men whose partners were pregnant) with a response rate of 84.2% (404/480) were randomly selected to participate in the present study from February 2023 to August 2023. The mean (SD) age and age at marriage for the women were 26.6 (6.7) years and 19.8 (4.7) years, respectively. The mean (SD) gestational age was 26.3 (7.3) weeks, with more than half of the women (55.4%, 133/240) being in the second trimester of pregnancy. Additionally, more than half of the women (53.8%, 129/240) reported having experienced one pregnancy. Over a quarter of the women (36.7%) had a high school diploma. The majority (95%) were homemakers. More than half of the women (53.3%) were somewhat satisfied with their monthly income. Furthermore, more than two‐thirds of the women (81.3%) expressed complete satisfaction with their marital life. A history of sexual disorders and chronic diseases was reported by 38.8% and 38.3% of the women, respectively. For the men, the mean (SD) age and age at marriage were 31.7 (6.2) years and 25.1 (4.8) years, respectively. More than a quarter of the men had a university education (27.5%), and nearly half (44.5%) had a self‐employed job. More than half of the men (52.4%) expressed moderate satisfaction with their income. The majority (88.4%) were fully satisfied with their marital life. Additionally, the rates of cigarette consumption, chronic diseases, and a history of sexual disorders in men were 37.8%, 43.9%, and 50%, respectively (Table 1).

Female and Male Sexual Attitude and Sexual Function Status

The mean (SD) total score of the AStSdP scale for women and men in the present study was 117.41 (19.43) and 109.47 (21.22), respectively, within a score range of 34–170. Both women and men had the highest mean score in the DBV subscale and the lowest mean score in the ASI subscale. The mean (SD) total score of the FSFI scale for women was 21.25 (9.37), with a score range of 2–36. Women had the highest mean score in the Orgasm subscale and the lowest score in the Arousal subscale. The mean (SD) total score of the IIEF scale for men was 48.84 (19.36), within a score range of 15–75. Men had the highest mean score in the Overall Satisfaction subscale and the lowest in the Intercourse Satisfaction subscale (Table 2).

Relationship Between Sexual Attitude and Sexual Function Among Iranian Couples

Based on the results of the Pearson correlation test, a significant positive correlation was observed between the total scores of sexual attitude in women and its subscales with the total scores of sexual function, sexual desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, and pain (p < 0.001). Similarly, a significant positive correlation was found between the total scores of sexual attitude in men and its subscales with the total scores of sexual function, erectile function, orgasmic function, sexual desire, sexual satisfaction, and overall satisfaction (p < 0.001). As shown in Table 3, there was a significant positive correlation between total sexual attitude and total sexual function scores in both women (r = 0.418, 95% CI: 0.297–0.531, p < 0.001) and men (r = 0.456, 95% CI: 0.323–0.575, p < 0.001) (Table 3).

The results of the bivariate tests showed that there was a significant statistical relationship between the total sexual function scores of women and their job (p = 0.023), marital satisfaction (p < 0.001), and alcohol consumption (p = 0.057). The results of the GLM, after controlling for demographic and social variables, indicated that the total sexual attitude scores, marital satisfaction, and job were significantly associated with women's sexual function scores. Specifically, for each unit increase in sexual attitude scores, sexual function scores in women (β [95% CI]: 0.21 [0.16–0.27]; p < 0.001) and men (β [95% CI]: 0.37 [0.23–0.51]; p< 0.001) increased. Additionally, women who expressed complete satisfaction with their marital life had significantly higher sexual function scores compared to those who were dissatisfied (β [95% CI]: 3.95 [1.03–6.87]; p= 0.008). Homemakers, in comparison to employed women, had significantly lower sexual function scores (β [95% CI]: −9.27 [−15.64 to −2.89]; p = 0.005). Regarding men's sexual function, there were statistically significant relationships between job (p = 0.099) and marital satisfaction (p = 0.001) with their total sexual function scores. According to the GLM, unemployed men had significantly lower sexual function scores compared to those with self‐employed jobs (β [95% CI]: −9.40 [−25.23–6.43]; p = 0.039). Furthermore, men who were satisfied with their marital life had significantly higher sexual function scores compared to those dissatisfied (β [95% CI]: 14.13 [5.33–22.94]; p = 0.002) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study, to the best of our knowledge, is the first to investigate the relationship between sexual attitudes and sexual function in Iranian couples during pregnancy. The results indicated that nearly half of the couples (48.2% of women [116/240] and 61.7% of men [101/164]) had a positive sexual attitude toward sexual activity during pregnancy. Among the subscales of sexual attitude, the highest score was related to DBV, while the lowest score was related to ASI. Despite this, 65% of women and 15.9% of men reported sexual dysfunction. Among the sexual function subscales, women had the highest average score in the Orgasm subscale and the lowest in the Arousal subscale. Meanwhile, men had the highest average score in the Overall Satisfaction subscale and the lowest in the Intercourse Satisfaction subscale. Sexual attitudes and their subscales were significantly and positively correlated with sexual function and its subscales. Increased sexual function in both women and men in this study was associated with higher total sexual attitude scores, being employed, and marital satisfaction. Beyond statistical significance, the clinical significance of these findings warrants consideration. The effect sizes observed for the relationship between sexual attitude and sexual function (r = 0.418 for women, r = 0.456 for men) indicate moderate to large effects, suggesting that improving sexual attitudes may translate into clinically meaningful improvements in sexual function.

In this study, the results showed that nearly half of the couples had a positive attitude towards sexual activity during pregnancy, with the mean scores for the overall sexual attitude of women and men being 117.41 and 109.47, respectively. In line with the current study's findings, a survey by Altinayak et al., which aimed to examine the sexual attitudes of 290 pregnant women, showed that the mean sexual attitude score of pregnant women was 113.58, with 51.7% of the women having a positive attitude towards sexual activity during pregnancy [18]. In another study by Kirca et al., which aimed to assess the sexual attitudes and sexual functions of 200 Turkish and Syrian pregnant women, the mean sexual attitude scores for Turkish and Syrian women were 123.77 and 94.74, respectively.

The highest score was related to DBV, while the lowest score was related to ASI, which aligns with the results of the current study (29). Although nearly half of the couples had a positive attitude towards sexual activity during pregnancy, the highest score was related to dysfunctional beliefs and values about sexual activity during pregnancy. The majority of the couples believed that sexual intercourse during pregnancy was unethical, conflicted with the role of motherhood, led to feelings of shame, and could harm the fetus, which is consistent with the findings of Shayan et al. [28]. In the present study, the prevalence of sexual dysfunction in pregnant women was 65% (156/240), while it was 15.9% (26/164) in their partners. The mean sexual function scores for women and men were 21.25 and 48.84, respectively. The current study's findings are comparable to the survey conducted by Masha et al. [29], which found the mean sexual function score for Turkish women to be 20.83, and for Iranian men, it was 48.00 [30]. The large discrepancy in sexual dysfunction prevalence between women (65%) and men (15.9%) during pregnancy may be explained by several factors. Women experience significant physiological changes during pregnancy, including increased abdominal volume, postural imbalances, back pain, fatigue, and fear of childbirth, all of which can make sexual intercourse less appealing and more difficult [19]. In women, the highest mean score was observed in the Orgasm subscale, while the lowest was in the Arousal subscale.

One possible explanation is that while pregnant women may maintain the ability to achieve orgasm, the physiological and psychological changes of pregnancy (e.g., body image concerns, fear of miscarriage, and vaginal dryness) may specifically hinder the arousal phase of the sexual response cycle [6]. In men, the highest mean score was in the Overall satisfaction subscale, while the lowest was in the Intercourse satisfaction subscale. This pattern may indicate that men derive satisfaction from non‐intercourse aspects of intimacy during their partner's pregnancy, such as emotional closeness and non‐penetrative sexual activities [6].

The prevalence of sexual dysfunction in women in this study aligns with findings from studies in Türkiye (76.4%) [31], the Middle East (65%) [32], Spain (65%) [33], and Turkey (87.4%) [34]. Despite using the same evaluation tool (FSFI) with the same thresholds, literature data show a wide variation in the prevalence of sexual dysfunction, ranging from 65% to 87.4%. This difference could be attributed to each country's religious, social, and cultural context [35]. In this context, the study by Guermazi et al., which aimed to examine the prevalence of sexual dysfunction and beliefs about sexual intercourse during pregnancy, found the prevalence of sexual dysfunction in pregnant women to be 70.6% [36]. On the other hand, another study by Khalesi et al. on the impact of pregnancy on couples’ sexual function indicated that over 60% of pregnant women experienced sexual dysfunction during pregnancy, which is in line with the findings of the current study [2].

One possible mechanism for the occurrence of sexual dysfunction in women during pregnancy is the increase in abdominal volume and the weight of the fetus, which can lead to imbalances and compensatory postural changes. As a result, women may have to start using muscles that were rarely engaged before pregnancy, which may be accompanied by back pain, which is a specific symptom marking the end of the pregnancy period. Additionally, fatigue, anxiety, and natural fear associated with the impending childbirth can make sexual intercourse less appealing to pregnant women [37]. A Scoping Review of qualitative research on pursuing sexual and loving relationships revealed that the sexual experiences of male partners have been mainly overlooked [38]. Most studies on sexual relations during pregnancy and postpartum have focused on women. In men, natural reductions in testosterone and cortisol and increases in estrogen, along with feelings of guilt, fears of harming the fetus or partner, and concerns about inducing labor, may be associated with decreased sexual desire [39].

The results of the present study showed that the sexual attitudes of couples and their subscales were directly and significantly related to sexual function and its subscales. Specifically, higher sexual attitude scores were associated with higher sexual function scores. According to the study by Sierra et al., which aimed to examine the relationship between sexual attitudes (general and specific) and various components of sexual function (desire, arousal, erection, ability to achieve orgasm, and orgasm satisfaction) in 2000 participants in Spain (1044 women and 956 men), sexual function for both men and women was essentially explained by having a positive sexual attitude. In other words, having a positive sexual attitude toward sexual fantasies was associated with greater sexual desire and arousal, better lubrication in women/erection in men, the ability to achieve orgasm, and greater satisfaction with orgasm [40]. In this regard, another study in Iran by Shayan et al. on 260 pregnant Iranian women also found a significant direct correlation between sexual function and sexual attitude [28].

In identifying factors associated with sexual function in Iranian couples, the results of the present study indicated that marital satisfaction and job were related to sexual function in Iranian couples. The moderate to large effect size for marital satisfaction in men (Cohen's d = 0.73) supports the clinical importance of assessing and addressing relationship satisfaction in prenatal sexual health interventions. Many factors influence marital satisfaction, one of the most important being safe and enjoyable sexual relationships, as mentioned in numerous studies [41]. Women and men who reported full marital satisfaction, compared to dissatisfied women and men, had significantly higher sexual function scores.

In this regard, the study by Emami et al., aimed at predicting marital satisfaction based on sexual dysfunction in couples on the brink of divorce, conducted on 187 Iranian couples (369 participants), showed that sexual satisfaction and desire in women, as well as satisfactory sexual function for men, can predict marital satisfaction [42]. Another study by Gholami et al. aimed at investigating the relationship between marital satisfaction and sexual dysfunction in women with type 2 diabetes, indicated a significant positive correlation between marital satisfaction and sexual dysfunction [43]. In men, the study by Mohseni et al., conducted on 204 Iranian men, showed a significant positive relationship between marital satisfaction and sexual function [44]. Marital satisfaction and intimacy may be considered a correlate of sexual desire in couples, shaping sexual experiences and predicting sexual function. Couples who have higher satisfaction in their relationships tend to derive more pleasure from sexual relations, which is associated with stronger orgasm, more intense ejaculation, and ultimately, happier and more satisfying relationships [41].

Homemakers and unemployed men, compared to employed women and men with self‐employed jobs, scored significantly lower in sexual function. Notably, the large effect size for homemakers compared to employed women (Cohen's d = 0.99) indicates that employment status has a substantial clinical impact on women's sexual function during pregnancy. The study by Enas et al., aimed at examining the impact of employment on women's sexual function in Egypt, showed that employed women had better sexual function than unemployed women. Employed women, compared to unemployed women, reported a higher frequency of sexual intercourse (60.2% vs. 39.4%), greater spontaneous desire (41% vs. 34.7%), and better ability to reach orgasm (38.2% vs. 12.7%) [45].

In this context, the study by Nikbina et al. on the relationship between job type and women's sexual function and satisfaction in 685 Iranian women found a significant relationship between job type and lubrication, satisfaction, pain during intercourse, overall sexual function scores, and sexual satisfaction. Overall, sexual function and sexual satisfaction scores were significantly lower in homemakers compared to women with healthcare, educational, and administrative jobs [46]. The higher scores of employed women on the scale, one possible interpretation is that these women, due to employment and financial security, enjoy their marriages more. Furthermore, employed women can connect better with their spouses due to the interpersonal skills and experience gained through work [47]. In men, the study by Pasha et al., aimed at evaluating sexual dysfunction and associated risk factors in male partners of infertile couples using the IIEF tool in 204 Iranian men, showed a significant correlation between employment status and sexual dysfunction. Specifically, the level of sexual dysfunction was lower in employed men than in unemployed men [48]. Among men, stable employment and higher social status may coincide with enhanced self‐confidence and mental relaxation, factors that have been associated with sexual satisfaction in cross‐sectional studies. Additionally, having a permanent job and steady income may facilitate marital life and, as a result, increase sexual satisfaction [48, 49].

Strength and Limitations

To our knowledge, this study is the first to explore the relationship between sexual attitudes and sexual function during pregnancy in Iranian couples. The large sample size (n = 404) and the use of the AStSdP tool, specifically designed to assess sexual attitudes in couples by encompassing cognitive, emotional, and behavioral aspects for the first time in Iran, are other strengths of this study. Although many studies have been conducted on sexual attitudes and sexual function, these studies have mainly focused on women, often neglecting the role of men in this context. Therefore, examining sexual attitudes and sexual function in couples (women and their partners) is another strength of the present study.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes establishing causal relationships between sexual attitudes and sexual function, and raises the possibility of reverse causality (i.e., better sexual function during pregnancy might lead to more positive sexual attitudes rather than the reverse). Therefore, longitudinal and prospective studies are needed. Second, accessing male partners was challenging, and only 164 out of 240 men (68.3%) completed the questionnaires, which may have introduced selection bias. Despite our efforts to recruit both partners, a considerable number of husbands either declined to participate or did not accompany their wives to the health center during the data collection period. Third, social desirability bias may have been particularly strong given the sensitive nature of sexual issues in Iranian culture and the taboo surrounding discussions of sexuality during pregnancy; the reliance on self‐reported questionnaires, especially those completed indirectly by men through their wives, likely exacerbated this bias. Consequently, our findings may be subject to overreporting of positive sexual attitudes and underreporting of sexual dysfunction and negative attitudes, particularly among male participants.

Fourth, and most importantly, although we collected data from both partners and describe our sample as “Iranian couples,” our analytical approach treated the data as independent by performing separate analyses for women and men, without accounting for the inherent interdependence between partners’ responses. This represents a major limitation, as dyadic data require analytical methods such as the Actor‐Partner Interdependence Model (APIM) or multilevel modeling to properly account for non‐independence.

Conclusions

The findings of the present study indicated that nearly half of the couples (48.2% of women and 61.7% of men) had a positive sexual attitude toward sexual activity during pregnancy. However, 65% of women and 15.9% of men also experienced sexual dysfunction. Given the relatively high prevalence of sexual dysfunction among women during pregnancy and the fact that nearly half of the couples hold negative attitudes toward sexual activity during pregnancy. Due to the cross‐sectional design, causal inferences cannot be made, and these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to determine whether providing accurate information and counseling can effectively dispel misconceptions, correct sexual beliefs, and improve sexual function during pregnancy. At this stage, the findings may be valuable for generating hypotheses to inform future interventions aimed at improving sexual health and sexual function, sexual education, and new counseling methods, ultimately improving family stability and, in turn, benefiting society.
