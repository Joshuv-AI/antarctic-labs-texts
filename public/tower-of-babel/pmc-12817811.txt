Medication Adjustment After Bariatric and Metabolic Surgery: A Review of Current Evidence and Clinical Practice Trends
Authors: Ji-Hyeon Park, Sojung Kim, Ho Seok Seo, Kyo Young Song, Han Hong Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bariatric and metabolic surgery (BMS) is the most effective treatment for severe obesity and its related comorbidities. Rapid metabolic improvement following surgery frequently leads to the reduction or discontinuation of medications for obesity-related comorbidities. However, there are no established guidelines regarding timing or criteria for dose adjustment. A narrative review examined major clinical trials, meta-analyses, and society guidelines relevant to BMS. Evidence from previous and recent studies was synthesized to summarize pharmacokinetic alterations, disease-specific medication adjustments, and long-term deprescription patterns following BMS. Postoperative anatomical changes—including reduced gastric surface area, intestinal bypass, and altered acidity—affect the absorption of many oral drugs, requiring early dose adjustment. For type 2 diabetes mellitus, patients using <30 units/day of basal insulin can discontinue insulin after surgery, whereas those using ≥30 units/day typically require a 50–80% dose reduction. Most patients taking a single oral hypoglycemic agent may stop medication, while metformin monotherapy is recommended when glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) <9% and dual therapy when HbA1c ≥9%. In hypertension, discontinuation of one antihypertensive drug usually results in approximate 10 mmHg reduction in systolic blood pressure. Diuretics should be withheld for the first 2 weeks postoperatively to prevent dehydration and excessive volume loss. For dyslipidemia, lipid-lowering agents are adjusted according to postoperative lipid profile changes and restarted if necessary. In psychiatric disorders, early resumption of preoperative medications is recommended to prevent withdrawal symptoms or relapse. BMS enables early medication reduction across comorbidities, yet individualized pharmacologic management remains essential to sustain metabolic improvement and long-term disease control.

INTRODUCTION

Obesity has become one of the most prevalent chronic diseases worldwide, and its clinical importance is less about excess weight per se than about the cluster of metabolic and organ complications that accompany it [1]. Candidates for bariatric and metabolic surgery (BMS) typically have obesity complicated by type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, obstructive sleep apnea, and often metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease, with a subset also carrying psychiatric disorders. In this group, lifestyle therapy and modern drugs frequently produce only short-lived control, so treatment is progressively intensified. On this background, BMS has become the most dependable option for durable weight loss and broader metabolic benefit [23].

An important feature of BMS is the tempo of change. After procedures such as Roux-en-Y gastric bypass or sleeve gastrectomy, insulin sensitivity improves within days [45], caloric intake falls abruptly [6], and blood pressure begins to decline as intravascular volume contracts and sympathetic drive is reduced [7]. Lipid parameters improve more slowly but still within months rather than years [8]. In other words, the underlying pathophysiology is corrected faster than in almost any medical weight-loss strategy. Yet patients typically arrive in the operating room with chronic prescriptions that were calibrated to their preoperative, highly insulin-resistant, volume-expanded, dyslipidemic state.

If those medications are continued unchanged, the success of surgery can paradoxically create harm: the same insulin regimen that barely controlled hyperglycemia a month earlier can now provoke symptomatic hypoglycemia, and a diuretic-ACE inhibitor combination that was appropriate for obesity-associated hypertension can cause postoperative dizziness, prerenal azotemia, or even syncope in a patient who is drinking only small volumes.

Despite this, postoperative medication management is often under-described in surgical reports and even in some clinical guidelines. Perioperative instructions tend to focus on thromboprophylaxis, ulcer prevention, and micronutrient supplementation, while leaving day-to-day decisions about antihyperglycemic, antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and psychotropic drugs to individual clinicians [910]. This leads to wide variation in practice. for example, insulin may be withdrawn while sulfonylureas are continued, or all antihypertensives may be stopped despite cardiovascular or renal comorbidity. A more structured, procedure-specific approach is therefore warranted.

The purpose of this review is to outline such an approach. We first describe the pharmacokinetic and physiologic alterations that follow common BMS, because these alterations explain why certain oral formulations fail. We then discuss, in turn, the 4 clinical domains in which medication adjustment is most consequential—diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and psychiatric disease. For each domain we emphasize 2 questions: (1) how quickly does the disease state itself improve after surgery, and (2) what is the safest way to deprescribe without losing control? Finally, we propose a follow-up schedule that synchronizes drug adjustments with the typical postoperative recovery and weight-loss curve.

PHARMACOLOGIC CONSIDERATIONS AFTER BMS

All BMS impose, to varying degrees, a new anatomy on the upper gastrointestinal tract [11]. Reduced gastric volume, altered gastric pH, and accelerated gastric emptying are common even after purely restrictive procedures such as sleeve gastrectomy (Table 1). In these restrictive operations, the stomach is smaller but the normal continuity of the duodenum and proximal jejunum is preserved; most drugs still reach their usual absorption sites, but they spend less time in the stomach and encounter a less acidic environment, which can shorten the window for tablet disintegration and reduce the solubility of acid-dependent compounds. Gastric bypass and other malabsorptive procedures additionally divert nutrients and medications away from the duodenum and proximal jejunum, which are the preferred sites of absorption for many drugs [1213]. Here, tablets may enter the mid–small bowel before they are fully disintegrated, and the loss of duodenal and proximal jejunal surface area, as well as altered mixing with bile and pancreatic secretions, can further lower and destabilize drug exposure. Under these circumstances, tablets that require prolonged gastric residence to disintegrate, or that depend on an acidic milieu, may exhibit reduced bioavailability. Extended-release and enteric-coated formulations are particularly vulnerable because their release mechanisms were engineered for an intact stomach and small bowel [14].

These anatomic changes occur in parallel with early postoperative dietary restriction (Table 1). For the first one to 2 weeks, patients ingest liquids or small, soft meals and often do not meet their usual fluid targets. Even in an otherwise healthy individual, such a regimen produces a modest contraction of extracellular fluid volume. When combined with preexisting diuretic therapy or aggressive renin–angiotensin system blockade, this seemingly mild volume loss is enough to precipitate orthostatic hypotension or prerenal azotemia. Thus, the pharmacokinetic changes (reduced and less predictable oral absorption) and the pharmacodynamic changes (a greater effect of the same dose in a relatively hypovolemic patient) often reinforce each other [1516].

For these reasons, several general principles can be stated at the outset. In the immediate postoperative period, large tablets should be replaced with liquid, soluble, or crushable formulations whenever feasible (Table 1). Extended-release products should be avoided or converted to immediate-release forms until oral intake and gastrointestinal transit stabilize. For a small subset of critical medications—antipsychotics, antiepileptics, or medications for Parkinson’s disease—non-oral routes (transdermal, subcutaneous, intramuscular depot) should be considered proactively [1718]. Applying these principles before discharge prevents many of the ad hoc phone calls and emergency visits that otherwise occur in the first month.

ADJUSTMENT OF ANTIDIABETIC THERAPY

Type 2 diabetes is the clearest example of a condition in which surgery outpaces medication. Insulin resistance falls rapidly after BMS because of decreased adipose-tissue inflammation, changes in gut hormones, and, quite simply, lower carbohydrate intake [1920]. Accordingly, if the preoperative antidiabetic regimen is continued unchanged during the immediate postoperative hospitalization, there is a high likelihood of hypoglycemia.

First, insulin requires separate adjustment because its demand falls most abruptly after surgery. A practical and safe approach is to stratify patients according to their preoperative insulin requirement [21]. If the patient was using <30 units/day before surgery, basal insulin can usually be withheld on postoperative day 0–1, with bedside capillary glucose checks every 4–6 hours and only correctional (sliding-scale) insulin given for values above the target range (often 140–180 mg/dL); a practical scheme is to administer 1 unit of rapid-acting insulin for every 40 mg/dL above 140 mg/dL. If the preoperative basal dose was ≥30 units/day, basal insulin should be restarted at about 50–80% of the previous dose, again with close monitoring and down-titration as oral intake advances. Scheduled prandial insulin is generally unnecessary while the diet consists of clear or full liquids and should either be omitted or replaced with correctional dosing (Table 2). The goal in this early phase is “safe control”—preventing hypoglycemia while avoiding sustained hyperglycemia—rather than tight normalization [182122232425].

Among the oral antihyperglycemic agents, in the immediate postoperative period, the priority is to stop the agents most likely to cause harm under conditions of low intake and rapidly improving insulin sensitivity. Secretagogues—particularly sulfonylureas—should be discontinued first, because they pair 2 strong hypoglycemia drivers: reduced oral intake and enhanced insulin action. SGLT2 inhibitors should also be withheld briefly, since early after surgery patients are relatively volume-depleted and this class can rarely precipitate euglycemic ketoacidosis. Other oral agents, such as metformin or DPP-4 inhibitors, can be considered later according to intake and renal function, but sulfonylureas and SGLT2 inhibitors are the ones that require routine early interruption (Table 2) [112126].

For noninsulin therapy, postoperative management can be simplified in many patients. Individuals who were taking a single oral antidiabetic agent before surgery can often have that medication discontinued after the procedure, provided that early postoperative glucose values remain within the target range. In patients who required more than one oral agent preoperatively, metformin is the preferred drug to continue as single-agent therapy when glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) is <9%, given its favorable safety profile and lack of hypoglycemia. When HbA1c is ≥9% or when glycemic control remains suboptimal, a second agent may be added to metformin, preferably one with demonstrated cardiovascular risk–reduction benefits. Agents that increase the risk of hypoglycemia should generally be avoided in this early period (Table 2). Continuous follow-up is still required, as diabetes is a progressive disease and medication needs may re-emerge over time [182223242527].

Follow-up in the first year should be structured. Hemoglobin A1c and fasting glucose should be reassessed at 3 and 6 months, aiming for an A1C <6.5% (or an individualized target if there are comorbidities) (Table 2). Many patients at these visits will meet criteria for diabetes remission or will at least qualify for deintensification of therapy. When stable control at the target A1C is confirmed, it is reasonable to taper and discontinue antidiabetic agents, or to continue it only when there is a separate cardiovascular or hepatic indication [27].

ADJUSTMENT OF ANTIHYPERTENSIVE THERAPY

Blood pressure tends to decline in parallel with early weight loss and fluid restriction. If diuretics, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), calcium-channel blockers, and beta-blockers are all continued unchanged, blood pressure may fall below the patient’s autoregulatory range, producing fatigue, dizziness, and falls [28]. Patients are typically on a strictly liquid diet for the first 1–2 weeks and are relatively volume depleted; in this setting, discontinuing just one outpatient antihypertensive agent can produce an additional ≈10-mmHg fall in systolic blood pressure at the 2-week visit (Table 3) [11]. For that reason, diuretics are the first drugs to stop or suspend in the early phase in order to avoid aggravating postoperative dehydration. Other agents (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, calcium-channel blockers, β-blockers) can generally be continued but at reduced doses, with daily blood-pressure monitoring until values stabilize. In patients with diabetes or kidney disease, continuation of an ACE inhibitor or ARB at low dose may still be appropriate for renoprotective effects [1018232429].

MANAGEMENT OF LIPID-LOWERING THERAPY

Unlike glucose and blood pressure, serum lipids typically improve over months rather than days [303132]. Therefore, immediate discontinuation of statins or fibrates is rarely justified. The safer approach is to maintain preoperative lipid-lowering therapy through the early recovery period, then to remeasure lipid profiles at 3, 6, and 12 months (Table 3) [101821]. If the original indication was primary prevention in the context of morbid obesity and the lipid profile has normalized in parallel with weight loss, dose reduction or discontinuation can be considered. If the indication was secondary prevention, therapy should generally be continued regardless of weight change [33].

PSYCHIATRIC AND OTHER CHRONIC MEDICATIONS

A substantial proportion of BMS candidates are treated for depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, or psychotic illnesses [3435]. Abrupt cessation around surgery risks withdrawal, symptom rebound, and nonadherence [36]. At the same time, several psychotropics are marketed primarily in extended-release tablet form, which is suboptimal in the early post-bariatric anatomy [37]. The preferred strategy is to restart psychotropic medications early, but in immediate-release, liquid, or crushable forms (Table 3) [91838]. For patients in whom treatment interruption would be particularly dangerous, long-acting injectable formulations can be used to bypass gastrointestinal absorption entirely. Close collaboration with psychiatry is advisable, especially in the first month, because affective symptoms can be misattributed to the normal postsurgical adjustment period.

ETHNIC GENERALIZABILITY OF POSTOPERATIVE MEDICATION ADJUSTMENT STRATEGIES

Although much of the evidence informing postoperative medication adjustment derives from Western cohorts, several studies suggest that the metabolic benefits of BMS are broadly consistent across ethnic groups [3940]. Systematic reviews and large observational studies have shown that racial minorities often experience slightly less weight loss than non-Hispanic White patients, yet remission of major comorbidities such as type 2 diabetes and hypertension is generally similar across racial groups [40]. Furthermore, Asian-dominant series and nationwide Korean data demonstrate substantial improvements in glycemic control and high rates of discontinuation of antidiabetic, antihypertensive, and lipid-lowering medications after BMS [41], indicating that the core deprescribing patterns are not confined to Western populations. Because the strategies outlined in this review are based on shared postoperative physiological and pharmacokinetic changes rather than ethnicity-specific factors, they are likely applicable across diverse populations, although further prospective validation in non-Western cohorts remains desirable.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS FOR POSTOPERATIVE MEDICATION ADJUSTMENT

Recent advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI)-integrated wearable devices have accelerated interest in digital health and digital therapeutics across clinical medicine [42]. In BMS, however, wearable technologies have so far been used primarily to track weight loss, physical activity, and overall nutritional status, while continuous glucose monitoring has been utilized to detect and manage post-bariatric hypoglycemia [434445]. To date, we were unable to identify studies in which AI-integrated wearable systems are used directly to guide postoperative adjustment or tapering of medications for diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, or psychiatric disorders. As these technologies continue to evolve and generate increasingly rich continuous biosignal data, there is potential for future integration into decision-support tools that enable more personalized deprescribing strategies after BMS. Nevertheless, this remains an important area for future investigation.

CONCLUSION

BMS succeeds precisely because it makes the patient metabolically “new” in a short time. If clinicians continue to treat the postoperative patient as though they were still metabolically old—insulin-resistant, volume-expanded, and dyslipidemic—then preventable drug-related complications will occur. A deliberate, disease-by-disease, and procedure-aware medication plan avoids this. Such a plan starts with simple formulation changes, proceeds to early de-escalation of diabetes and blood-pressure therapies, and ends with medium-term reassessment of preventive drugs. Above all, it is multidisciplinary: surgeons, endocrinologists, cardiologists, psychiatrists, and pharmacists must share the same understanding of how fast the physiology changes after surgery. When they do, medication adjustment becomes not an afterthought but an integral part of metabolic surgery care.
