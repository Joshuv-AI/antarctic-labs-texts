Safety and Clinical Outcomes of Endovascular Thrombectomy in Very Elderly Patients (≥80 Years) with Acute Ischemic Stroke: A Prospective Multicenter Cohort Study in Vietnam
Authors: Trinh Kim Thi Ngo, Thang Cong Tran, Thang Huy Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence on the safety and clinical outcomes of endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) in very elderly patients with acute ischemic stroke remains limited, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.PurposeTo evaluate the safety and clinical outcomes of EVT in patients aged ≥80 years with acute ischemic stroke.Patients and methodsThis prospective multicenter cohort study was conducted at three major stroke centers in Vietnam between 2024 and 2025. Patients aged ≥80 years with acute ischemic stroke due to anterior circulation large vessel occlusion were enrolled. The primary outcome was functional independence at 90 days. Secondary outcomes included malignant cerebral edema, intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), and mortality. Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsA total of 370 patients were included (190 EVT, 180 non-EVT). In adjusted analyses, EVT was associated with a higher likelihood of functional independence at 90 days (aOR 2.84, 95% CI 1.38–5.87; p=0.005) and a lower risk of malignant cerebral edema during hospitalization (aOR 0.50, 95% CI 0.27–0.93; p=0.029). EVT was also associated with a higher risk of any intracranial hemorrhage (aOR 4.78, 95% CI 2.90–7.89; p<0.001) and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (aOR 4.11, 95% CI 1.42–11.91; p=0.009). Although mortality at 90 days was numerically lower in the EVT group, the association did not reach statistical significance after adjustment (aOR 0.78, 95% CI 0.49–1.24; p=0.29).ConclusionIn very elderly patients with acute ischemic stroke, EVT was associated with improved functional outcomes but an increased risk of intracranial hemorrhage, without a significant increase in mortality. These findings support individualized treatment decision-making regarding EVT in carefully selected patients aged ≥80 years and highlight the importance of greater inclusion of very elderly populations in future clinical trials.Keywords: acute ischemic stroke, intracranial hemorrhage, endovascular thrombectomy, very elderly, clinical outcomes, Vietnam

Introduction

Population aging is occurring rapidly globally, leading to a significant increase in the number of very elderly patients (≥80 years old) hospitalized for acute ischemic stroke. According to the GBD 2019 analysis, stroke remains one of the leading causes of death and disability, with the burden of disease increasingly concentrated in the elderly population.1 However, the very elderly patient group is very limitedly represented in clinical trials of endovascular thrombectomy (EVT), and the evidence regarding the safety and efficacy of EVT in this population is primarily based on observational studies.2–4

The HERMES collaboration pooling patient-level data from five trials (MR CLEAN, ESCAPE, REVASCAT, SWIFT PRIME, and EXTEND IA) showed the benefits of EVT in patients with acute ischaemic stroke caused by occlusion of the proximal anterior circulation, however, the average age of the study participants was only about 65–70 years, and the number of participants aged 80 years or older was very small.2–4 The HERMES analysis showed that EVT can still provide functional benefits in very elderly individuals, but its absolute effectiveness decreases and the risk of complications increases with age.4 Furthermore, the majority of available data were from high-income countries, while evidence from lower-middle-income countries, particularly in Southeast Asia, remains limited.5

In clinical practice, advanced age is often considered a poor prognostic factor, leading to a cautious approach to EVT in the very elderly. However, from a geriatric perspective, chronological age may not fully reflect an individual’s biological recovery capacity. Many studies showed that post-stroke outcomes in the very elderly are significantly influenced by pre-stroke functional and frailty status, in addition to stroke severity and imaging characteristics.6–9 Pre-stroke functional status and frailty have been shown to be independent predictors of mortality and poor functional outcome after stroke, even after adjusting for the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and imaging factors.7–10 These factors can determine a patient’s recovery capacity, even when technically optimal reperfusion is achieved.

In Vietnam, the burden of stroke is rapidly increasing along with the aging population. However, there is very limited published data on EVT in patients aged 80 years and above with acute ischemic stroke due to anterior circulation artery occlusion. Therefore, this study was conducted to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of EVT in this group of very elderly patients. The results of this study will contribute to providing more practical evidence from Vietnam and support a more individualized approach to the management of acute stroke in very elderly patients.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This multicenter prospective cohort study was conducted from January 2024 to December 2025 at three major stroke centers in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam (People’s Hospital 115, Military Hospital 175, and University Medical Center Ho Chi Minh City). These three stroke centers are capable of endovascular intervention and have achieved Diamond Status in the World Stroke Organization’s Angels Awards program, reflecting a high level of compliance with international standards for admission, imaging diagnosis, reperfusion, and comprehensive care for acute stroke patients.

All eligible patients were consecutively recruited to minimize sampling bias. Patients were included in the study if they met all of the following criteria: (1) age ≥80 years at the time of admission; (2) diagnosed with acute ischemic stroke; (3) evidence of occlusion in the anterior circulation on CTA or MRA, involving the intracranial internal carotid artery and/or the middle cerebral artery (M1 or proximal M2 segment); and (4) admitted within a timeframe that allows for clinical practice-based assessment and consideration of reperfusion therapy. Exclusion criteria included: (1) primary hemorrhagic stroke or non-ischemic stroke, (2) lack of outcome data during hospitalization or at 90 days.

EVT indication, intervention technique selection, and post-procedure care were performed according to routine clinical practice at each center, in accordance with the guidelines of the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association11 and the European Stroke Organisation12 on the treatment of acute ischemic stroke. Based on the treatment received at each center, participants were classified into two groups: the EVT group - those who underwent EVT, with or without intravenous thrombolysis, and the non-EVT group - those who did not receive EVT due to medical contraindications, late presentation outside the treatment window, or refusal of intervention by family/patients after adequate counseling. The study investigators had no role in the treatment for these patients. Treatment decisions were made by multidisciplinary stroke teams based on clinical presentation, imaging findings, estimated procedural risk, comorbidities, pre-stroke functional status, and discussions with patients’ family members or legal representatives regarding the expected risks and benefits of treatment.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committees of the University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City (reference number 130/HDDD-DHYD, dated 16 January 2024), the People’s Hospital 115 (reference number 310/BVND115-NCKH, dated 29 January 2024), and the Military Hospital 175 (reference number 1326/GCN-HDDD, dated 4 April 2024). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample Size Justification

Based on previous reports in very elderly patients, an absolute difference of approximately 15% in functional recovery between the EVT and non-EVT groups was assumed.11,13,14 With an α = 0.05 and a power of 80%, the estimated sample size was at least 150 patients for each group.

Variable Definition

Safety Outcomes

The safety outcomes in this study included mortality, intracranial hemorrhage, symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, and malignant cerebral edema during hospitalization.

Intracranial hemorrhages (ICH) were morphologically classified according to the ECASS system as follows: HI-1 (small punctate hemorrhages along the infarct margins), HI-2 (confluenced punctate hemorrhages within the infarct without mass effect), PH-1 (hematoma occupying ≤30% of the infarct), and PH-2 (hematoma occupying >30% of the infarct with mass effect).15

Symptomatic ICH was defined according to ECASS II criteria as ICH on imaging accompanied by clinical deterioration (increase ≥4 NIHSS points) or death within 24 ± 6 hours after intervention.

Malignant cerebral edema was defined as progressive cerebral edema on imaging accompanied by decreased consciousness and/or requiring surgical intervention (decompression craniotomy), consistent with classic descriptions of extensive cerebral infarction causing compression and increased intracranial pressure in international studies and recommendations.16,17

Clinical Outcomes

The clinical outcomes of EVT included functional independence and mortality at 90 days.

Functional independence at 90 days was defined as a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) of 0 to 2, consistent with baseline EVT trials and HERMES pooled analysis.3,4 The mRS scores were assessed during in-person follow-up visits or via telephone interviews when participants were unable to attend the hospitals. Assessments were conducted by research staff trained in mRS and independent from the treatment decision-making process. Although complete blinding to treatment allocation could not be guaranteed because of the observational design of the study, structured mRS interviews were used to improve consistency and reliability among assessors.18

Mortality at 90 days was recorded during hospitalization and within 90 days after admission.

Other Stroke-Related Variables

Stroke severity at admission was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), with a total score from 0 to 42; higher scores reflect more severe neurological deficits. The NIHSS is widely used in clinical practice and stroke research as a standardized indicator for initial severity stratification and outcome prediction.12,19

Anterior circulation artery occlusion was identified on computed tomography angiography (CTA) or magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), including occlusion of the intracranial internal carotid artery (ICA) and/or the middle cerebral artery (M1 segment or proximal M2 segment). Tandem occlusion was defined as significant stenosis or occlusion of the extracranial internal carotid artery accompanied by a concomitant intracranial arterial occlusion, typically involving the ICA terminus or the M1 segment.

The extent of early brain injury was assessed using the Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) based on non-contrast CT at admission.20 ASPECTS is a 0-to-10 scale, where lower scores reflect a larger early infarct area within the middle cerebral artery territory. ASPECTS is usually treated as a continuous variable in analysis models or as a binary variable (≥6 vs <6) in EVT trials and international guidelines. In this study, ASPECTS was recorded by the treating physician at the time of admission.4,11,12,20

The time of stroke onset was determined by the last-known-well (LKW), which is the last time the patient was recorded as being neurologically normal.11 Standardized time metrics in stroke emergency care recorded prospectively include: onset-to-door, door-to-imaging, door-to-needle (for patients receiving intravenous thrombolysis), door-to-groin puncture, and groin-to-reperfusion. These milestones reflected the efficiency of the emergency procedure and the quality of acute stroke care at each center, consistent with the quality indicators recommended in international guidelines.11,12

The treatment window was determined according to current recommendations: intravenous thrombolysis should be administered within 4.5 hours of LKW,11 endovascular thrombectomy within 6 hours in patients with vessel occlusion who meet imaging criteria11,12 and may be extended to 24 hours in certain cases if imaging shows a mismatch between the clinical deficit and the infarct core according to the DAWN and DEFUSE 3 trials.13,14 These definitions and selection strategies are consistent with the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association and European Stroke Organisation guidelines for the management of acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion.

The degree of reperfusion was assessed on post-procedure cerebral angiography using the Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (TICI) score,21 with classifications from 0 to 3. Successful reperfusion was defined as TICI 2b–3, consistent with baseline EVT trials and international reporting practices.4,22 A TICI score of 3 was considered complete reperfusion. To increase consistency across centers, TICI was recorded based on the interventional physician’s assessment at the time of the procedure; if necessary, angiographic images were cross-reviewed according to internal quality control procedures.

Post-intervention image evaluation and follow-up: All patients underwent follow-up non-contrast CT scans of the brain after 24 ± 6 hours, or sooner if clinical deterioration occurred, to assess intracranial hemorrhage and the extent of infarction progression. In cases of suspected re-occlusion or vascular complications, additional CTA was performed as clinically indicated.

Other Covariates

Frailty: Frailty was assessed using the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS)23 upon admission. CFS is a 9-point scale, from 1 (very healthy) to 9 (terminal), reflecting overall biological reserves and tolerance to acute stress. In analysis, CFS was treated as a continuous variable; it was also grouped (CFS ≤4 vs. ≥5) in exploratory analyses to stratify clinical impairment.

Comorbidities: Data on comorbidities were documented from the medical records, using a predefined list including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, coronary heart disease, heart failure, cancer, chronic kidney disease, peripheral artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Statistical Analysis

To examine the association between EVT and study outcomes (functional independence at 90 days, mortality at 90 days, mortality during hospitalization, malignant cerebral edema, any intracranial hemorrhage, and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage), multivariable logistic regression models were performed with EVT as the primary predictor of interest.

The models were adjusted for clinically relevant covariates selected a priori based on stroke severity, imaging characteristics, and geriatric vulnerability, including age, sex, baseline NIHSS, ASPECTS, intravenous thrombolysis use, and Clinical Frailty Scale score. Additional variables such as time-to-treatment metrics, vessel occlusion site, and pre-stroke functional status assessed by the pre-stroke modified Rankin Scale (mRS) were reviewed during model development. However, pre-stroke mRS had a restricted distribution in this cohort and was not retained in the final primary multivariable models to preserve model stability and avoid overfitting.

Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and median (interquartile range) when appropriate. Ordinal variables such as NIHSS and ASPECTS were additionally summarized using median and interquartile range values for descriptive purposes. Comparisons between groups were performed using the independent t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous and ordinal variables, and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Two-tailed P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Participant Characteristics

There were 370 participants. The study flow chart is presented in Figure 1. They had a mean age of 85.3 ± 4.5 years, and 65.9% were female. Of these 370 participants, 190 (51.4%) received EVT, and 180 (48.6%) received standard medical management. There was no significant difference in age between the two groups. Participants in the EVT group had higher prevalence of underweight (9.5% vs 3.3%; p=0.056) and smoking (11.1% vs 5.6%; p=0.056). The mean CFS score was 4.9 ± 1.2, with no difference between the two groups (p=0.195).

Procedure Characteristics and Outcomes of Reperfusion Therapy in the EVT Group

Among participants receiving EVT (n = 190), the most common site of occlusion was the middle cerebral artery (M1) (48.4%), followed by the internal carotid artery (27.9%), the proximal M2 segment (15.3%), and tandem lesions (8.4%). Successful reperfusion (TICI ≥2b) was achieved in 76.3% of cases, with 31.6% achieving complete reperfusion (TICI 3). The mean door-to-groin puncture time was 223.65 ± 125.41 (minutes), and the mean groin-to-reperfusion time was 56.97 ± 26.92 (minutes). Stent retriever was the main technique used (64.7%), followed by stent retriever combined with aspiration (22.6%), and aspiration only (11.1%). Balloon angioplasty and/or stenting were performed in only 1.6% of the participants. Most procedures were performed without general anesthesia (96.8%). Compared to the non-EVT group, participants who received EVT had lower rates of mortality during hospitalization (22.1% vs 32.2%, p=0.029) and malignant cerebral edema (10.5% vs 21.1%, p=0.005). Discussion

In this multicenter cohort study of 370 patients aged 80 and above with acute ischemic stroke due to anterior circulation artery occlusion, EVT was associated with a higher likelihood of functional independence (mRS 0–2) at 90 days. EVT was also associated with a lower risk of malignant cerebral edema during hospitalization, although the rate of intracranial hemorrhage, including symptomatic hemorrhage, was significantly higher.

Numerous observational studies and meta-analyses have demonstrated that EVT may improve functional outcomes for very elderly patients. While large randomized controlled trials may not always adequately represent the population aged 80 and above, recent studies have expanded eligibility criteria to include patients with larger infarct cores and extended time windows. These studies still demonstrated that EVT provides functional benefits compared to medical treatment alone, particularly for groups with favorable imaging characteristics and high baseline scores on the NIHSS scale.4,13,24–26 Our results are consistent with these data, showing a significant increase in the odds of achieving mRS 0–2 after EVT. The underlying biological mechanism of this benefit mainly involves the preservation of the ischemic penumbra and the limitation of the progression of the infarct core after early revascularization, particularly in large anterior circulation occlusions, where the penumbra volume often predominates over the initial necrotic area.13,14 This effect has been clearly demonstrated in selective perfusion imaging trials, showing that EVT slows infarct progression and improves functional outcomes compared to medical treatment alone. However, in very elderly individuals, these benefits are significantly reduced due to a decline in neural reserve and collateral circulation, which are associated with vascular aging and microcirculatory dysfunction. This leads to poorer tolerance to ischemia and a narrower biological window for effective reperfusion.27,28

Although EVT improved the likelihood of achieving functional independence at 90 days, mortality at 90 days remained high in both groups and did not differ significantly after multivariable adjustment. A possible explanation is that mortality in very elderly patients may not only be influenced by factors like infarct size or revascularization but also involves a combination of multimorbidity, frailty, nutrition, polypharmacy, and medication adherence.29–31

According to the AHA/ASA 2026 guideline for the early management of patients with acute ischemic stroke, advanced age should not be considered an absolute exclusion criterion for reperfusion therapies.25 Instead, treatment decisions should be based on the presence of salvageable brain tissue, the time window since onset, and the identification of large vessel occlusion by imaging, rather than age alone. EVT is recommended as the standard treatment within a ≤6-hour window and may be extended to 24 hours in cases selected by perfusion imaging. Furthermore, the 2026 guidelines emphasized the central role of the stroke care system, including pre-hospital triage and prioritization of direct transport to EVT-capable centers, in order to shorten the door-to-groin puncture time. These systemic strategies are considered key to improving functional outcomes, particularly in very elderly patients with reduced biological reserves.25

Our data showed that compared with medical treatment, EVT was associated with a significantly higher rate of intracranial hemorrhage, including symptomatic hemorrhage. This observation aligns with studies conducted in Europe and North America, which found that patients aged 80 years and older have a higher risk of hemorrhagic transformation following reperfusion. This increased risk is believed to be associated with factors such as vessel wall vulnerability, blood-brain barrier dysfunction, and an exaggerated response to reperfusion, all of which may be influenced by the effects of vascular aging.32

In our study, EVT was associated with reduced malignant cerebral edema during hospitalization. This suggests that revascularization offers early biological benefits by limiting the progressive spread of the infarct. This finding is consistent with recent trials involving imaging selection and acute stroke interventions with extended time windows. These trials demonstrate that timely reperfusion can help preserve salvageable brain tissue and improve the distribution of modified Rankin Scale scores, even in the presence of an increased risk of hemorrhage.26,33,34

The study was carried out at three major stroke centers in Vietnam and included high-quality clinical data. Our study provides real-world experience of current practices in this very high-risk elderly population, which are often under-represented in clinical trials. Another key strength of this study is the use of the Clinical Frailty Scale to assess the overall level of frailty. This approach emphasizes “biological age” and functional reserve rather than chronological age. There is increasing evidence that frailty independently predicts poor outcomes after a stroke and after EVT. In our multivariate logistic regression analysis, EVT demonstrated an independent association with favorable functional outcomes, even after adjusting for the CFS score. This finding suggests that frailty alone should not automatically exclude carefully selected very elderly patients from consideration for EVT. Instead, it should be used as a tool for assessing the potential benefits and risks, guiding discussions about prognosis with patients and their families. This approach aligns with contemporary care practices for the elderly, where treatment decisions are tailored based on biological age and functional capacity rather than chronological age alone.29,31 Therefore, EVT should be considered a vital part of a comprehensive care approach. It is essential to integrate comprehensive geriatric care, which includes early swallowing screenings, aspiration prevention, early mobilization, delirium management, nutritional optimization, and pneumonia prevention strategies. These supportive measures are important for improving functional recovery after reperfusion therapy. This chain-based approach, which spans from pre-hospital care to rehabilitation, aligns with the AHA/ASA 2026 Guideline. These guidelines emphasized the importance of organized stroke management, reducing treatment delays, and enhancing post-acute care to optimize outcomes, especially for populations with reduced biological reserves.25

Our study has several limitations. First, due to its observational cohort design, residual confounding related to treatment selection cannot be completely excluded despite multivariable adjustment for key baseline variables, including age, sex, baseline NIHSS, ASPECTS, intravenous thrombolysis use, and Clinical Frailty Scale score. Although pre-stroke mRS data were available, this variable had a restricted distribution in the present cohort and was not retained in the final primary multivariable models; therefore, residual confounding related to baseline functional status cannot be completely excluded. Patients undergoing EVT had higher baseline ASPECTS scores, suggesting more favorable imaging profiles and smaller infarct cores at presentation, which may have contributed to selection bias. In addition, subgroup analyses according to ASPECTS categories and intravenous thrombolysis status were not performed because of the limited number of participants and outcome events within several subgroups. Therefore, the present study could not fully evaluate whether the association between EVT and clinical outcomes differed according to baseline imaging severity or thrombolysis status, which may limit the broader applicability of the findings. Furthermore, the overall 90-day mortality remained high in both treatment groups, reflecting the substantial vulnerability and comorbidity burden of very elderly patients with acute ischemic stroke. Finally, we focused primarily on functional and mortality outcomes and did not systematically evaluate quality of life or cognitive outcomes, which may be important in future studies involving very elderly populations.

Conclusion

Compared with standard medical management, EVT was associated with improved functional outcomes at 90 days among patients aged ≥80 years with acute ischemic stroke due to anterior circulation artery occlusion, although no significant reduction in mortality was observed after multivariable adjustment. EVT was also associated with an increased risk of intracranial hemorrhage, underscoring the need for careful patient selection in this vulnerable population. Taken together, these findings support a more individualized approach to treatment decision-making in very elderly patients, integrating clinical status, imaging characteristics, frailty burden, and expected treatment-related risks rather than relying on chronological age alone.
