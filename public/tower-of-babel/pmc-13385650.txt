C2orf74 orchestrates germ-Leydig crosstalk to inhibit white adipose tissue browning in male mice
Authors: Li Zhao, Jialuo Han, Xinxin Wang, Qin Li, Qinghua Shi, Fangbiao Tao, Pilong Li, Chengxin Zhang, Qiang Liu, Juan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sex differences in obesity are well recognized; however, the identification of sex-specific obesity genes and the mechanisms through which they affect obesity development remain elusive. Here, we identify a germ-cell-specific gene C2orf74, whose expression is responsive to high-fat diet (HFD) and promotes HFD-induced obesity in male mice by restraining lipolysis and limiting the browning of white adipocytes through suppression of androgen receptor signaling, but not in females. Additionally, C2orf74’s expression increases with aging, contributing to aging-related obesity and metabolic comorbidities in chow-fed male mice. We demonstrate that C2orf74 is an ER-residing transmembrane protein that anchors and stabilizes dolichol phosphate mannose synthase 1 (Dpm1) on the ER membrane, facilitating Dpm1-mediated glycosylation and secretion of Bpifa3 from germ cells. As a paracrine regulator, Bpifa3 transcriptionally suppresses the expression of testosterone biosynthesis enzymes in Leydig cells. Therapeutically, we demonstrate that antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) targeting C2orf74 protects male mice from HFD-induced obesity. Thus, our study defines a role for germ-Leydig cell crosstalk, mediated by C2orf74, in the white adipocytes browning in both age-associated and diet-induced obesity.

Introduction

Although obesity affects both males and females, there is a well-established sexual dimorphism in its development and associated metabolic complications1–4. This disparity may be partially due to differences in how males and females respond to sustained dietary fat intake, with males exhibiting greater obesifying effects than females, including enhanced lipid deposition and obesity5–7. Despite clear physiological differences between men and women, the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely understood. While there is growing recognition of sex differences in obesity, the development of sex-sensitive therapeutic strategies remains limited due to an incomplete understanding of the mechanisms driving distinct obesity patterns in males and females.

Genetic factors play a significant role in obesity, with heritability estimates ranging between 40 and 70%8–10. The concordance for obesity strongly correlates with the degree of genetic relatedness, and various measures of obesity, such as BMI, subscapular skinfold thickness, waist circumference, and waist-hip ratio, also show high heritability11–13. Thus, genetic components largely determine an individual’s susceptibility to obesity and response to an obesogenic environment9. Broadly, the genetic origins of obesity can be categorized into three areas: genes regulating food intake, genes controlling lipid metabolism in adipocytes, and genes influencing mitochondrial biogenesis and adaptive thermogenesis8. However, the identification of genetic risk factors contributing to distinct obesity patterns in males and females remains largely elusive.

White adipose tissue (WAT) is a major type of adipose tissue in mammals, whose traditional role is energy storage14. In contrast, brown adipose tissue (BAT) burns energy to produce heat15–17. White adipocytes can exhibit intermediary characteristics between white and brown adipocytes in response to environmental stimuli through browning, a process associated with augmented non-shivering thermogenesis and metabolic capacity18–21. Adipokines, such as FGF21, apelin, and leptin, secreted factors, such as IL-10, IL-27, and irisin, have been shown to promote adipocyte browning22–29. In addition, environmental stimuli, such as cold exposure, also induce this process30. Since adipose browning is beneficial to counteracting obesity; understanding its role in distinct obesity patterns in males and females remains a critical yet unresolved question.

In this study, we identify a germ-cell-enriched gene C2orf74, whose expression is responsive to both aging and HFD. We demonstrate that C2orf74 promotes aging-related and HFD-induced obesity in male mice by restraining heat production, energy expenditure, and the browning of white adipocytes through the inhibition of testosterone biogenesis and androgen receptor (AR) signaling. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that C2orf74 is an ER membrane–resident protein that physically anchors Dpm1 to the ER membrane, thereby preventing its destabilization and degradation. Stabilized Dpm1 promotes the synthesis of dolichol phosphate mannose (Dol-P-Man), a critical donor substrate required for protein glycosylation. This glycosylation activity is necessary for the production and secretion of Bpifa3 from germ cells. Secreted Bpifa3 is subsequently taken up by neighboring Leydig cells, where it suppresses the transcription of key enzymes involved in testosterone biosynthesis in a paracrine manner. Therapeutically, ASO-mediated reduction of C2orf74 alleviates HFD-induced obesity by enhancing the browning of white adipocytes. Taken together, our findings establish C2orf74 as a key regulator of male obesity, functioning through the suppression of testosterone biosynthesis and the restraint of testosterone-AR signaling-mediated adipocyte browning. These insights position C2orf74 as a potential therapeutic target for combating obesity and its associated metabolic comorbidities.

Results

The testis-enriched C2orf74 promotes high-fat diet-induced obesity in male mice

Testis-enriched genes often function in spermatogenesis and/or sperm function; however, several testis-enriched genes have been shown as dispensable for male fertility31–35. C2orf74 is a previously identified testis-enriched gene, yet has no obvious impact on male fertility31. We conducted qPCR and immunoblotting analyses of various mouse tissues, which confirmed that C2orf74 is exclusively expressed in male mouse testis (Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Testes comprise multiple cell types, including germ cells, somatic Sertoli cells, and interstitial Leydig cells; qPCR following enzyme decomposition and Percoll gradient centrifugation of dissected testis samples showed that C2orf74 is only expressed in germ cells (Supplementary Fig. 1c). We generated C2orf74 knockout (KO) mice by deleting the entire genomic sequence, and the knockout was confirmed by genotyping and immunoblotting (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). We examined genome-wide association studies (GWAS) human datasets (ukb-b-17462, ukb-e-278, and ebi-a-GCST-010118) and noted associations for single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in human C2ORF74 with obesity, hyperlipidemia, as well as type 2 diabetes (Supplementary Fig. 1i). Moreover, we found that C2orf74 protein levels are responsive to high-fat diet (HFD) feeding in mice, wherein HFD is a diet consisting of 60% of total calories consumed from fat36. Immunoblotting of testes extracts showed that C2orf74 protein levels were significantly increased in response to HFD in a time-dependent manner, reaching a maximum at 10 weeks, with no further increase observed at later time points (Supplementary Fig. 1j). Accordingly, an HFD feeding duration of 12 weeks was used for subsequent studies. Moreover, a switch of diet from HFD to a normal chow diet (NCD) led to a significant reduction in C2orf74 protein levels compared to continuous HFD feeding (Supplementary Fig. 1k). Despite the marked increase in C2orf74 protein levels, its mRNA levels remained unaltered (Fig. 1c, d). Notably, mice fed an HFD displayed significantly more weight gain than mice fed an NCD (Fig. 1e). These findings suggest that C2orf74 protein levels are positively associated with HFD-induced obesity.

To investigate the impact of C2orf74 on HFD-induced obesity, we next monitored WT and C2orf74 KO male mice fed an HFD for 12 weeks. We found that C2orf74 KO mice showed significantly less body weight gain as compared to littermate WT mice (Fig. 1f, g), suggesting that C2orf74 promotes HFD-induced obesity. Note that no differences in body weight gain were observed between female C2orf74 KO and WT mice fed an HFD (Supplementary Fig. 1l), and there were no differences in body weight gain in males or females of either genotype upon feeding a NCD (Supplementary Fig. 1m, n). In addition to the obese phenotype, we observed that HFD-fed C2orf74 KO males (Fig. 1h, i), but not females (Supplementary Fig. 1o, p), showed significantly lower levels of triglycerides (TG) and cholesterol in serum compared to WT mice, and the C2orf74 KO males exhibited significant improvements in both glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity (Fig. 1j, k). C2orf74 KO male mice fed an HFD showed significantly less liver steatosis compared to WT male mice, which is supported by lower hepatic TG contents in C2orf74 KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 1q–s). These findings suggest that C2orf74 promotes obesity and other metabolic comorbidities in HFD-fed male mice.

C2orf74 promotes lipid accumulation in white adipocytes in male mice fed a high-fat diet

To investigate how C2orf74 promotes obesity in males, we examined the fat and lean mass by conducting an MRI-based body composition analysis at the end of the HFD experiment (12 weeks). Our data revealed that the C2orf74 KO male mice had significantly less fat mass than WT mice (Fig. 1l); there was no difference in the lean mass (Fig. 1m). Nor were there any differences in fat or lean mass between C2orf74 KO and WT male mice fed an NCD or between C2orf74 KO and WT female mice fed an HFD (Supplementary Fig. 2a–d). Dissection of adipose tissue showed that the HFD-fed C2orf74 KO mice had obviously smaller epididymal WAT (epWAT) and subcutaneous WAT (scWAT) deposits than WT mice (Fig. 1n and Supplementary Fig. 2e), and weighing showed a significantly less weight for both tissues in the C2orf74 KO mice (Fig. 1o and Supplementary Fig. 2f–h). Histological analysis not only showed a smaller adipocyte size in C2orf74 KO mice than that in WT mice, but also demonstrated multilocular depot morphology in some of the adipocytes in C2orf74 KO mice (Fig. 1p and Supplementary Fig. 2i, j). Bioluminescent assay of epWAT and scWAT extracts revealed significant reductions of TG and cholesterol content in both tissues in HFD-fed C2orf74 KO mice compared to WT littermates (Fig. 1q, r and Supplementary Fig. 2k, l). These findings indicate that decreased body weight gain observed for HFD-fed C2orf74 KO male mice is likely attributed to a reduction in white adipose depots.

To confirm that germ-cell-expressed C2orf74 promotes obesity-related phenotypes in male mice fed an HFD, we next generated germ-cell-specific C2orf74 knockout mice, by crossing loxP C2orf74 mice, wherein exon 3 and exon 4 were flanked with loxP sites (C2orf74 flox/flox), with stimulated by retinoic acid gene 8 (Stra8) driven Cre transgenic mice. This crossing strategy results in C2orf74 flox/flox Stra8+/− mice (C2orf74 cKO) or C2orf74 flox/flox Stra8−/− mice (control) (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). We found that C2orf74 cKO male mice showed similar resistance to HFD-induced obesity, including less body weight gain, less fat mass, less weight of epWAT and scWAT, and smaller adipocyte size, as compared to control male mice (Supplementary Fig. 3c–h). The C2orf74 cKO mice also had improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity compared to control mice (Supplementary Fig. 3i, j). These findings further support that germ-cell-expressed C2orf74 promotes HFD-induced obesity in male mice through increasing lipid accumulation in white adipocytes.

C2orf74 restrains energy expenditure in male mice

Obesity is the result of an energy imbalance caused by an increased ratio of caloric intake to energy expenditure37,38. As calorie intake is informatively reflected by food intake and intestinal absorption39, we first examined food intake: there was no difference between C2orf74 KO and WT mice (Fig. 2a). Nor were there differences in the levels of hypothalamic peptides known to control food intake (Fig. 2b)40–42. Prior to assessing intestinal lipid absorption by enterocytes, we confirmed that there were no differences in intestinal villi length between C2orf74 KO and WT mice (Fig. 2c). Upon gavaging mice with olive oil, we found no differences at any examined time point for serum TG, total TG across examined times (represented by the area under the curve, AUC), lipid accumulation in both enterocytes and the lamina propria between WT and C2orf74 KO mice (Fig. 2d, e), indicating no difference in the enterocytic lipid absorption rate. We next investigated the impact of C2orf74 on energy expenditure using metabolic chambers. We detected a significantly higher oxygen consumption rate (VO2) and higher carbon dioxide production rate (VCO2) in HFD-fed C2orf74 KO males than in HFD-fed WT males (Fig. 2f, g). The energy expenditure (EE) (computed based on VO2 and VCO2) was significantly increased in C2orf74 KO mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 2h). Of note, we confirmed that there was no difference in overall physical activity between C2orf74 KO and WT mice (Fig. 2i). Moreover, we analyzed adaptive thermogenesis and found that C2orf74 KO mice were less sensitive to cold-induced hypothermia and more tolerant of cold than WT littermates when fed an HFD (Fig. 2j), suggesting that the increased energy expenditure in C2orf74 KO mice is attributable to increased heat production. The elevation in energy expenditure was also evident in C2orf74 cKO versus control mice (Supplementary Fig. 3k–n). To prevent the confounding effects of sub-thermoneutral housing, we next housed WT and C2orf74 KO mice in thermoneutral chambers. Similarly, we found that C2orf74 KO mice showed significantly less body weight gain than their littermate WT controls fed an HFD (Fig. 2k). Metabolic profiling indicated that C2orf74 KO mice showed significantly increased energy expenditure compared to WT mice (Fig. 2l). These findings indicate that C2orf74 promotes HFD-induced obesity by decreasing heat production and reducing energy expenditure in mice.

C2orf74 limits the browning of white adipocytes

To investigate what elicits the above-observed changes in heat production and energy expenditure in C2orf74 KO versus WT mice, we conducted RNA sequencing of the aforementioned epWAT and scWAT and noted global transcriptional changes in both, albeit with more extensive changes in epWAT than in scWAT (2407 DEGs in epWAT vs. 1255 DEGs in scWAT) (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis based on RNA-seq data showed that the significantly upregulated genes in both epWAT and scWAT of C2orf74 KO versus WT mice exhibited an enriched functional annotation related to thermogenesis (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 4c), also supporting that increased energy expenditure in C2orf74 KO mice is owing to increased thermogenesis in white adipocytes. In addition to thermogenesis, significantly upregulated genes in epWAT also showed enriched functional annotation related to lipolysis, fatty acid oxidation, and mitochondrial respiration (Fig. 3a). The increases in these gene expressions were validated by qPCR and immunoblotting analysis (Fig. 3b, c and Supplementary Fig. 4d). Of note, increased lipolysis in the WAT of C2orf74 KO mice supported the observation of less lipid accumulation in their white adipocytes. To determine the impact of C2orf74 on mitochondrial respiration, we analyzed mitochondrial respiration in adipose tissues by employing a high-resolution respirometry (OROBOROS Oxygraph-2k). By analyzing respiratory exchange ratio (RER) for metabolic profiles, we found that RER was around 0.7 for both WT and C2orf74 KO mice fed an HFD (Supplementary Fig. 4f), indicating that fat is the predominant fuel source43. By analyzing metabolic data according to published formula44–47, we demonstrated that C2orf74 KO mice showed a significant increase in lipid oxidation compared to WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 4g). In addition, combined usage of palmitate and etomoxir not only reduced the palmitate-induced increase in basal oxygen flux, but also abolished the difference in maximal oxygen flux of WATs between C2orf74 KO and WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 4h–m). These findings suggest that C2orf74 deficiency promotes respiration through increasing fatty acid β-oxidation and thermogenesis in white adipocytes. Taken together, these data indicate an occurrence of white adipocyte browning and formation of beige adipocytes, which is a type of thermogenic adipocyte, in C2orf74 KO mice.

Mammals have two types of thermogenic adipocytes: brown adipocytes and beige adipocytes48,49. Levels of mitochondrial uncoupling protein 1 (Ucp1), which dissipate energy in the form of heat, are used as a marker for thermogenesis capacity50. We detected no difference in Ucp1 levels in brown adipocytes between C2orf74 KO and WT mice (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 5a), indicating no difference in the Ucp1-dependent thermogenesis for brown adipocytes. This is supported by no difference in BAT temperature between WT and C2orf74 KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 5b). In addition, we examined Ucp1-independent thermogenesis, such as creatine and calcium futile cycling, in brown adipocytes and again detected no difference between C2orf74 KO and WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 5c–e). In addition, Serca2 and sarcolipin expression was assessed in skeletal muscles; no difference between WT and C2orf74 KO mice indicates that muscle-based thermogenesis does not contribute to the observed changes in overall thermogenesis (Supplementary Fig. 5f, g). By conducting qPCR/immunohistochemistry/immunoblotting, we detected a significant increase in Ucp1 expression in both epWAT and scWAT of C2orf74 KO mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 3b, f, g and Supplementary Fig. 5h–l), suggesting an increase in the thermogenesis capacity for white adipocytes in C2orf74 KO mice. Moreover, using an adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated approach to achieve adipose-specific Ucp1 knockdown in C2orf74 KO male mice51, we observed significantly increased body weight gain accompanied by reduced energy expenditure (Supplementary Fig. 5m–p), supporting the involvement of a Ucp1-dependent mechanism. These lines of evidence further support an increase in thermogenesis capacity and an elicitation of browning for white adipocytes in C2orf74 KO mice. Thus, C2orf74 restrains the browning of white adipocytes.

C2orf74 limits the browning of white adipocytes through testosterone-AR signaling

Recalling that C2orf74 is specifically expressed in the testis, an endocrine gland that produces testosterone. To determine the impact of C2orf74 on testosterone levels, we examined testosterone levels for C2orf74 KO and WT mice and found that both serum and intratesticular testosterone was significantly increased in C2orf74 KO mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 6a). Notably, levels of testosterone-derived 5α-dihydrotestosterone (DHT) and 17β-estradiol (E2) did not differ between WT and C2orf74 KO mice fed an HFD, consistent with the unchanged expression of Cyp19a1 (aromatase), which catalyzes the conversion of testosterone to E2 (Supplementary Fig. 6b–e). In addition, serum luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels were unchanged, ruling out involvement of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis feedback (Supplementary Fig. 6f, g).

The actions of testosterone on adipose tissue are mediated via the androgen receptor (AR) and testosterone-AR signaling52–54. To investigate whether C2orf74 regulates the browning of white adipocytes and obese phenotype through AR signaling, we gavaged C2orf74 KO and WT mice fed an HFD with a known AR antagonist enzalutamide to block AR signaling. Both enzalutamide and HFD treatments started at the age of 8 weeks and lasted for 12 weeks. Molecular analysis indicated that epWAT of C2orf74 KO mice treated with enzalutamide showed a significant reduction in thermogenesis, lipolysis, fatty acid oxidation, and mitochondrial respiration, compared to that of C2orf74 KO mice treated with DMSO (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 6h). In support of the observed reduction in thermogenesis by qPCR analysis, we detected a significant decrease in Ucp1 expression in both epWAT and scWAT of C2orf74 KO mice treated with enzalutamide compared to that of C2orf74 KO mice treated with DMSO (Supplementary Fig. 6i). These findings suggest that C2orf74 restrains thermogenesis and browning of white adipocytes through suppressing AR signaling. We next examined the obese phenotype and found that C2orf74 KO mice treated with enzalutamide showed a significantly increased body weight gain, increased weight of WATs, increased adipocyte size, as well as increased serum TG level, compared to C2orf74 KO mice treated with DMSO (Fig. 3j–m and Supplementary Fig. 6j–l).

To further strengthen our conclusion that C2orf74 restrains white adipocyte browning by suppressing AR signaling, we conducted AR knockdown (KD) in the epWAT of C2orf74 KO male mice using an AAV-mediated approach to deliver AR-targeting shRNA or control shRNA. We found that AR shRNA significantly reduced the expression of Ucp1 in the epWAT of C2orf74 KO mice compared to control shRNA (Supplementary Fig. 6m, n). In addition, AR KD significantly decreased the expression of other thermogenic genes, including Ucp3, Adrb1, Adrb2, Adrb3, Ybx2, Gadd45g, and Ffar4 (Supplementary Fig. 6m), indicating reduced adipocyte browning. Taken together, these findings suggest that C2orf74 deficiency promotes adipose browning through the activation of androgen-AR signaling. Therefore, C2orf74 restrains the browning of white adipocytes and contributes to HFD-induced obesity by inhibiting testosterone-AR signaling.

To investigate how testosterone biosynthesis is increased in C2orf74 KO mice, we conducted RNA-seq of Leydig cells isolated from WT and C2orf74 KO testes. The purity of Leydig cells was assessed by measuring cell-type-specific marker gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 7a). GO analysis indicated that the significantly upregulated genes in C2orf74 KO versus WT testes were enriched for functional annotations related to the terms “metabolism of steroids” and “steroid biosynthetic process” (Fig. 4a). To explore how germ-cell-expressed C2orf74 suppresses testosterone biosynthesis in Leydig cells, we isolated germ and Leydig cells from testes, cultured them in vitro, and treated WT Leydig cells with conditioned medium derived from C2orf74 KO or WT germ cell cultures (Fig. 4f). Both qPCR and immunoblotting analyses revealed significantly higher levels of testosterone biosynthesis genes in Leydig cells treated with conditioned medium derived from C2orf74 KO germ cells (Fig. 4g, h). Consistently, testosterone released by Leydig cell cultures treated with C2orf74 KO germ cell-derived medium was significantly elevated compared to those treated with WT conditioned medium (Fig. 4i). These findings suggest that germ-cell-expressed C2orf74 limits testosterone biosynthesis in Leydig cells through substance(s) secreted by germ cells.

Exosomes are membrane-derived vesicles known as important mediators of intercellular communication60,61. We next isolated exosomes from the medium of WT and C2orf74 KO germ cell cultures and treated WT Leydig cells with the purified exosomes (Supplementary Fig. 7b). No differences in the expression levels of testosterone biosynthesis genes were detected (Supplementary Fig. 7c–e), suggesting that germ cell-secreted exosomes have no impact on testosterone biosynthesis in Leydig cells. Therefore, we speculated that C2orf74 likely reduces testosterone biosynthesis in Leydig cells, probably through germ cell-secreted protein(s).

Ideally, a germ cell secretome analysis would help identify the secreted protein(s) influencing the testosterone biosynthesis pathway. However, primary germ cells did not survive in serum-free medium. As an alternative, we conducted proteomics on germ cells isolated from WT and C2orf74 KO mice, focusing on differentially expressed secretory proteins (Supplementary Fig. 7f). The top five most differentially expressed secretory proteins were indicated in the volcano plot: Entpd6, Wfdc3, Bpifa3, Cst8, and Mif (Fig. 4j). Among these, Entpd6, Wfdc3, Bpifa3, and Cst8 were significantly decreased, while of Mif levels were significantly increased in C2orf74 KO germ cells compared to WT (Fig. 4j). To validate their secretion, we conducted plasmid transfection for gene expression, followed by protein detection in the cultured medium, confirming that these proteins indeed are secreted (Supplementary Fig. 7g).

The expression of major testosterone biosynthesis genes is coordinately regulated by steroidogenic factor 1 (SF-1), a transcription factor belonging to the nuclear hormone receptor subfamily62. To evaluate the transcriptional activity of testosterone biosynthesis genes, we constructed a luciferase reporter containing SF-1-responsive elements. 293 T cells were transfected with this reporter, followed by the addition of conditioned medium derived from 293 T cells overexpressing Entpd6, Wfdc3, Bpifa3, Cst8, or Mif. Notably, medium derived from Bpifa3-overexpressing cells significantly reduced luciferase activity, while medium from cells overexpressing other genes had no impact (Fig. 4k). In contrast, Bpifa3-immunodepleted conditioned medium markedly enhanced luciferase activity (Supplementary Fig. 7h, i). Moreover, treatment with Bpifa3-containing medium resulted in decreased SF-1 occupancy at the promoter region of these steroidogenic genes compared to control medium (Supplementary Fig. 7j), suggesting that secreted Bpifa3 suppresses the transcription of testosterone biosynthesis genes. Furthermore, analysis of Bpifa3 protein levels revealed a significant reduction in C2orf74 KO germ cells and C2orf74 KO germ cell-derived conditioned medium compared to their respective controls (Fig. 4l, m), indicating that C2orf74 reduces testosterone biosynthesis in Leydig cells through germ cell-secreted Bpifa3.

To validate the effect of secreted Bpifa3 on the testosterone biosynthetic pathway in Leydig cells, we treated WT Leydig cells with control medium or medium derived from 293T cells overexpressing Bpifa3. The expression of testosterone biosynthesis genes was significantly reduced in Leydig cells exposed to Bpifa3-containing medium compared to those treated with control medium (Fig. 4n). Importantly, treatment with Bpifa3-immunodepleted medium largely reversed this reduction, indicating that secreted Bpifa3 suppresses testosterone biosynthesis (Supplementary Fig. 7k–m). To determine whether secreted Bpifa3 is taken up by Leydig cells, conditioned medium derived from 293T cells expressing Bpifa3-FLAG was applied to cultured Leydig cells. Bpifa3-FLAG protein was detected in Leydig cell lysates, indicating that secreted Bpifa3 is taken up by Leydig cells and exerts a function in transcriptional repression (Supplementary Fig. 7n). Of note, Bpifa3 levels were under detection in plasma, indicating that the biological function of Bpifa3 is likely restricted to local paracrine signaling within the testicular microenvironment (Supplementary Fig. 7o).

To establish a causal role for Bpifa3 in regulating testosterone biosynthesis, we performed Bpifa3 knockdown in the testes of HFD-fed WT mice using rete testis-delivered ASO technology (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). We found that Bpifa3 knockdown significantly upregulated the expression of testosterone biosynthetic enzymes and enhanced intratesticular and serum testosterone levels (Supplementary Fig. 8c–e). Consequently, Bpifa3 knockdown increased Ucp1 protein levels in white adipose tissues and increased EE in mice, indicating the promotion of white adipocyte browning (Supplementary Fig. 8f, g). Phenotypically, Bpifa3 knockdown reduced body weight gain compared with controls (Supplementary Fig. 8h). We also demonstrate that Bpifa3-mediated regulation of testosterone production is independent of HPG axis feedback (Supplementary Fig. 8i, j). Together, these findings demonstrate that Bpifa3 as a downstream effector of C2orf74 within the germ-Leydig-adipose regulatory axis, promoting HFD-induced obesity by restraining testosterone biosynthesis and limiting white adipose browning.

ER transmembrane protein C2orf74 anchors Dpm1 to the ER for Bpifa3 glycosylation

To investigate how C2orf74 regulates Bpifa3 levels, we first examined Bpifa3 mRNA levels in germ cells and detected no differences between WT and C2orf74 KO germ cells (Fig. 4o), suggesting that C2orf74 regulates Bpifa3 expression at posttranscriptional levels. Notably, Bpifa3 protein was detected in testis homogenates and 293T cell lysates at an approximate molecular weight of 34 kD, which exceeds its calculated molecular weight of 25.5 kD (Fig. 4p and Supplementary Fig. 7g), indicating the presence of potential posttranslational modifications.

In eukaryotic cells, most secreted proteins undergo maturation in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), following translocation from ribosomes63. KEGG analysis of differentially expressed proteins in C2orf74 KO versus WT germ cells revealed enriched annotations related to “Protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum” and “N-glycan biosynthesis” (Supplementary Fig. 8k), suggesting the involvement of glycosylation in Bpifa3 modification. We next treated testis homogenates with N-glycosidase (PNGase F), a glycosidase that removes N-linked glycans from glycoproteins64. We found that Bpifa3 protein exhibited a molecular weight shift from ~34 kD to ~26 kD in the presence of PNGase F, while it remained at ~34 kD in the absence of PNGase F (Fig. 4p). Similarly, treatment of cell homogenates from Bpifa3-overexpressing cells with PNGase F resulted in a comparable molecular weight shift of Bpifa3 from ~34 kD to ~26 kD (Supplementary Fig. 8l). These findings support that Bpifa3 undergoes glycosylation modification.

Since Bpifa3 is detectable only in its glycosylated form (~34 kD), we next sought to determine whether glycosylation affects its protein stability. 293 T cells transfected with the Bpifa3 plasmid were treated with the N-linked glycosylation inhibitor tunicamycin to produce non-glycosylated Bpifa3 or with DMSO to detect glycosylated Bpifa3. Cycloheximide chase assays showed that non-glycosylated Bpifa3 (~26 kD) degraded significantly faster than its glycosylated counterpart (~34 kD) (Fig. 4q), suggesting that glycosylation enhances Bpifa3 stability by preventing its degradation. We also generated a glycosylation-deficient mutant, in which asparagine (N) residues within the predicted N-glycosylation sites were substituted with glutamine (Q) (Supplementary Fig. 8m). Immunoblotting revealed that Mutant-Bpifa3 migrated predominantly at ~26 kD, corresponding to the non-glycosylated form (Supplementary Fig. 8n). Mutant-Bpifa3 exhibited more rapid degradation and a significantly shorter half-life than WT-Bpifa3 (Supplementary Fig. 8n), supporting the conclusion that glycosylation is critical for Bpifa3 protein stability. Furthermore, Bpifa3 protein was undetected in conditioned medium from Mutant-Bpifa3-expressing cells, whereas abundant Bpifa3 was detected in medium from WT-Bpifa3-expressing cells (Supplementary Fig. 8o). These findings indicate that N-glycosylation is required to maintain Bpifa3 stability and secretion.

Among the 13 significantly downregulated proteins related to glycosylation in C2orf74 KO, Dolichol phosphate mannose synthase 1 (Dpm1) showed the most pronounced reduction (dropped by 80%) compared to WT testes (Fig. 5b). Dpm1 is essential for the N-glycosylation process, facilitating the transfer of mannose from GDP-mannose to dolichol monophosphate to form dolichol phosphate mannose (Dol-P-Man), a key glycosylation substrate65,67–70. Immunoblotting of isolated germ cells further confirmed significantly lower Dpm1 levels in C2orf74 KO germ cells than in WT germ cells (Fig. 5c). To establish a causal relationship between Dpm1 and Bpifa3, we conducted Dpm1 knockdown in GC-2spd cells and measured Bpifa3 protein levels in both cell lysate and conditioned medium. Bpifa3 levels were significantly reduced in Dpm1 KD cells and their conditioned medium compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 8p). These findings suggest that C2orf74 controls Bpifa3 production and secretion through Dpm1-mediated glycosylation.

Dpm1-mediated formation of the donor substrate Dol-P-Man occurs on the cytoplasm side of the ER membrane65,71. Since Dpm1 lacks a transmembrane domain, an ER-residing protein is required to tether Dpm1 to the ER membrane71–73. Based on TMHMM prediction, C2orf74 contains a transmembrane domain (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0/). We experimentally confirmed its ER localization by detecting co-localization of C2orf74 and Calnexin (CALX), an ER membrane marker, in 293 T cells (Fig. 5d). Immunostaining further revealed co-localization of C2orf74 with Dpm1 in 293 T cells (Fig. 5e). Additionally, co-immunoprecipitation (IP) assay using 293 T cells co-expressing C2orf74-FLAG and Dpm1-HA supported a direct interaction between C2orf74 and Dpm1 (Fig. 5f). These findings suggest that C2orf74 serves as an anchor to tether Dpm1 to the ER membrane.

TMHMM also predicted that the C-terminus of C2orf74 faces the cytoplasm side of the ER, where Dpm1 facilitates Dol-P-Man formation72. To investigate whether C2orf74 tethers Dpm1 through its C-terminus, we generated a C2orf74 truncation mutant lacking 73 amino acids at its C-terminus (Mutant-C2orf74), while retaining its transmembrane domain on the N-terminus (Fig. 5g). We found that although Mutant-C2orf74 localized to the ER membrane, it failed to interact with Dpm1 (Fig. 5h, i). These findings support that C2orf74 tethers Dpm1 to the ER membrane through its C-terminus.

In addition to the lack of interaction between Dpm1 and Mutant-C2orf74, immunostaining showed a significant reduction in Dpm1 signals on the ER in the presence of Mutant-C2orf74 (Fig. 5j). This led us to investigate whether C2orf74 and its ER localization are necessary for stabilizing Dpm1. We transfected 293T cells with the Dpm1-HA plasmid for expression, followed by co-transfection with either Mutant-C2orf74-FLAG or full-length C2orf74-FLAG. Using a cycloheximide chase assay, we found that Dpm1 degraded significantly faster in cells expressing Mutant-C2orf74-FLAG compared to those expressing full-length C2orf74-FLAG (Fig. 5k). These results suggest that both C2orf74 and its ER localization are required for stabilizing Dpm1. Taken together, C2orf74 tethers Dpm1 to the ER membrane, ensuring its stability. ER-localized Dpm1 facilitates the glycosylation of Bpifa3, which is required for maintaining Bpifa3 production and secretion.

We next conducted a rescue experiment by re-expressing C2orf74-FLAG specifically in germ cells in C2orf74 KO male mice using the Stra8 promoter (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Phenotypically, reintroduction of C2orf74 in the C2orf74 KO background led to significantly increased body weight increment, elevated fat mass, and reduced energy expenditure compared to control C2orf74 KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 9b–e). Of note, lean mass remained relatively constant between two groups (Supplementary Fig. 9c). Consistently, Ucp1 expression was reduced in both scWAT and epWAT of C2orf74 KO mice following C2orf74 re-expression, indicating impaired white adipocyte browning (Supplementary Fig. 9f). At molecular level, reintroduction of C2orf74 in C2orf74 KO mice resulted in increased protein levels of Dpm1 and Bpifa3 relative to control C2orf74 KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Correspondingly, serum testosterone levels were significantly reduced in C2orf74-re-expressing mice (Supplementary Fig. 9g). Together, this gain-of-function model indicates that the C2orf74-Dpm1-Bpifa3 axis regulates obesity-related phenotypes by limiting energy expenditure and white adipocyte browning.

Phenotypically, WT mice injected with ASO-C2orf74 exhibited significantly less body weight gain compared to control ASO-treated mice (Fig. 6e), suggesting that ASO-C2orf74 mice are resistant to HFD-induced obesity. Additionally, ASO-C2orf74-treated mice displayed significantly increased energy expenditure compared to control ASO mice (Supplementary Fig. 9h, i). Of note, serum testosterone levels were significantly higher in ASO-C2orf74-treated mice compared to control ASO-treated mice, whereas serum LH and FSH did not differ between groups (Supplementary Fig. 9j–l). We further observed that ASO-C2orf74-treated mice exhibited a significant reduction in both Dpm1 and Bpifa3 levels compared to controls (Fig. 6f). Furthermore, these mice showed markedly increased expression of thermogenic genes, including Ucp1, Ucp3, Pgc1α, Adrb1, Adrb2, and Adrb3 in their epWAT (Fig. 6g), indicating enhanced thermogenic capacity. Immunohistochemistry analysis confirmed this effect by revealing significantly increased Ucp1 signals in the white adipocytes of ASO-C2orf74-treated mice (Fig. 6h, i). In addition to increased thermogenesis, ASO-C2orf74-treated mice also showed increased markers of lipolysis, enhanced mitochondrial respiration, and elevated fatty acid oxidation in white adipocytes compared to control mice (Fig. 6j, k), further supporting that ASO-C2orf74 promotes white adipocyte browning. Aiming to investigate the preventive potential of ASO, WT mice were simultaneously administered ASO-C2orf74 and HFD feeding. We found that ASO-C2orf74 significantly reduced the body weight increment compared to control ASO (Supplementary Fig. 9m). These results suggest that reducing C2orf74 through ASO-based therapy may serve as both a therapeutic and preventive approach for treating HFD-induced obesity in males.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that ER-residing C2orf74 tethers and stabilizes Dpm1, which facilitates Bpifa3 glycosylation, ensuring its stability and secretion in germ cells. As a paracrine regulator, Bpifa3 suppresses testosterone biosynthesis in neighboring Leydig cells, influencing testosterone-mediated metabolic effects by inhibiting the browning of white adipocytes.

Aging-induced C2orf74 promotes the development of aging-related obesity and other metabolic comorbidities

Aging is a well-established risk factor for metabolic syndromes, contributing to increased prevalence of obesity, dyslipidemia, glucose intolerance, and other related comorbidities74. We next determined age-dependent changes in the testicular expression of C2orf74, Dpm1, and Bpifa3 in mice fed an NCD and found that the protein levels of all three proteins increased progressively across developmental, adult, and aging stages, whereas their mRNA levels remained relatively constant throughout adult and aging (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Fig. 10a–c). To directly assess the functional relevance of this pathway during aging, we aged both WT and C2orf74 KO male mice to reach 21 months while maintaining them on a NCD, and demonstrated a significantly higher serum testosterone levels in C2orf74 KO mice compared to age-matched WT mice (Fig. 7c). Consistently, the expression of testosterone biosynthesis genes was significantly upregulated in the testes of C2orf74 KO mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 7d). Additionally, we detected a significant reduction in both Dpm1 and Bpifa3 protein levels in 21-month-old C2orf74 KO mice compared to age-matched WT mice (Fig. 7d). These findings suggest that aging-induced C2orf74 controls Dpm1-mediated production of Bpifa3, which suppresses the transcription of testosterone biosynthesis genes.

By evaluating the obese-related phenotypes of those 21-month-old mice, we found that C2orf74 KO mice had a significantly lower body weight compared to WT mice (Fig. 7e). Both epWAT and scWAT mass were significantly reduced in aged C2orf74 KO mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 7f). Histological analysis further revealed that adipocyte sizes in both epWAT and scWAT were significantly smaller in aged C2orf74 KO mice than in age-matched WT mice (Fig. 7g, h). These findings indicate that C2orf74 promotes aging-related obesity. Moreover, serum TG and cholesterol levels were significantly lower in aged C2orf74 KO mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 7i, j). Similarly, TG and cholesterol levels were significantly reduced in the epWAT of aged C2orf74 KO mice relative to WT controls (Fig. 7k, l). These findings suggest that aging-induced C2orf74 contributes to the development of metabolic comorbidities, while loss of C2orf74 protects against age-related obesity and associated metabolic dysfunction.

Progressive dysfunction of adipocytes is considered a hallmark of normal aging, with beige adipocytes exhibiting a decline in quantity and distribution over time75. However, the mechanisms underlying these age-related changes remain unclear. To investigate the role of C2orf74 in these processes, we examined the expression of genes related to thermogenesis, lipolysis, and mitochondrial respiration in 21-month-old C2orf74 KO and WT mice. We detected significantly higher expression of genes related to Ucp1-dependent thermogenesis, including Ucp1, Ucp3, Pgc1α, Adrb1, Adrb2, and Adrb3 in the WAT of C2orf74 KO mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 7m and Supplementary Fig. 10d, e). In contrast, no differences were detected in genes associated with Ucp1-independent thermogenesis (Supplementary Fig. 10f, g). In addition, genes involved in lipolysis and fatty acid oxidation, including Hsl, Atgl, Cpt2, Echs1, and Eci1, and mitochondrial respiratory, function, including Cox1, Cox2, Cytb, Nd2, Nd5, and Atp6, were significantly upregulated in the epWAT of aged C2orf74 KO mice compared to age-matched WT mice (Fig. 7n, o). Of note, no differences were detected in Ucp1-dependent and Ucp1-independent thermogenesis in the BAT of aged C2orf74 KO and WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 10h, i). These findings suggest that aging-induced C2orf74 suppresses the browning of white adipocytes, contributing to age-related obesity and metabolic dysfunction.

To investigate whether reduced C2orf74 expression could alleviate age-related metabolic phenotypes, we specifically selected aged mice that exhibited pronounced body weight gain (body weight >35 g and at least 50% higher than 2-month-old mice). These mice also displayed increased fat mass, elevated serum cholesterol and TG levels compared to young controls, indicating obesity-associated metabolic abnormalities (Supplementary Fig. 10j–l). These mice were administered either control or C2orf74 ASO via rete testis injection (Supplementary Fig. 10m). Longitudinal body weight analysis revealed a significant reduction in body weight in C2orf74 ASO-treated mice compared to control ASO-treated mice as early as 1 week after the second ASO injection (Supplementary Fig. 10n). In addition, C2orf74 ASO-treated mice showed increased energy expenditure relative to controls (Supplementary Fig. 10o). Consistently, Ucp1 expression in both epWAT and scWAT was significantly increased in C2orf74 ASO-treated mice, indicating enhanced white adipocyte browning (Supplementary Fig. 10p). Together, these results demonstrate that reducing testicular C2orf74 expression in aged mice with metabolic dysfunction improves metabolic parameters.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the aging-associated increase in C2orf74 may contribute to obesity and metabolic comorbidities by altering the quantity and characteristics of white adipocytes. Specifically, C2orf74 restricts the browning of white adipocytes, limits lipid catabolism and fatty acid oxidation, and restrains mitochondrial respiration, ultimately contributing to metabolic decline during aging (Fig. 7p).

Discussion

Increased dietary fat intake is strongly associated with body weight gain, leading to obesity and related metabolic disorders; however, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear76–78. Here, we demonstrate that a high-fat diet (HFD) induces the expression of C2orf74, which promotes lipid deposition and contributes to obesity. Notably, C2orf74 KO mice exhibit resistance to HFD-induced obesity by enhancing lipid catabolism, promoting the browning of white adipocytes, and increasing energy dissipation. Thus, we identify C2orf74 as a causal gene in obesity, whose expression is responsive to dietary fat intake, and define its regulatory role in lipid deposition and metabolic dysfunction. Moreover, our findings suggest that targeting C2orf74 may represent a promising therapeutic approach for combating HFD-induced obesity.

The upregulation of C2orf74 occurs in response to both HFD and aging, suggesting a conserved stress-responsive mechanism. Notably, this increase is observed at the protein level but not at the mRNA level, indicating posttranscriptional regulation. This expression pattern is shared between HFD- and aging-induced C2orf74 upregulation and likely reflects altered translational efficiency of C2orf74 mRNA and/or changes in C2orf74 protein stability.

Mechanistically, we demonstrate that germ cell-derived C2orf74 limits white adipocyte browning by restraining testosterone production in Leydig cells through local intercellular signaling, which subsequently mediates long-range inter-organ communication between the testis and adipose tissue. Notably, both local intercellular and long-range inter-organ communication are well-recognized mechanisms in metabolic regulation. For example, M2 macrophages within adipose tissue promote beige adipogenesis and enhance energy expenditure by locally suppressing the transcription factor Ets1 in white adipocytes via local paracrine signaling, leading to increased mitochondrial content and adipocyte browning79. In addition, Irisin, a PGC1α-dependent myokine cleaved from FNDC5 in skeletal muscle, acts on WAT to stimulate Ucp1 expression and energy expenditure through inter-organ communication28.

Although human C2ORF74 exhibits a broader distribution of expression, its expression remains relatively high in the testis (The Human Protein Atlas), suggesting that its testicular function may be conserved between mice and humans. Notably, associations between human C2ORF74 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and obesity, hyperlipidemia, and type 2 diabetes in human metabolic datasets further support a regulatory role for C2ORF74 in obesity and related metabolic traits in humans. Beyond the testis, C2ORF74 is also expressed in metabolically active tissues such as liver, adipose tissue, and muscle, indicating that C2ORF74 may be involved in a complex regulatory network governing systemic metabolism.

Sex influences metabolic disorders; for example, diabetes is more prevalent in men than in women80. Similarly, in almost all animal models, males and females exhibit distinct metabolic responses to sustained HFD intake80. Females tend to be more resistant to the obesifying effects than males, though the underlying mechanism remains elusive80. Here, we identify C2orf74 as an obesity-associated gene, which is specifically expressed in the testes of male mice, with no detectable expression in females. Given the demonstrated role of C2orf74 in obesity and its upregulation in response to HFD, we speculate that C2orf74 gene contributes to the distinct metabolic responses observed in male mice under dietary stress.

Testosterone, the primary male sex hormone produced in male testes, plays a crucial role in carbohydrate, lipid, and protein metabolism81,82. Testosterone deficiency is linked not only to obesity but also to metabolic disorders, such as glucose metabolism disturbance, impaired insulin sensitivity, and dyslipidemia83–86. Notably, testosterone replacement therapy has demonstrated a beneficial effect in obesity treatment87–89. In this study, we found that C2orf74 KO mice displayed increased serum testosterone levels, which promote lipolysis, the browning of white adipocytes, and energy dissipation—ultimately conferring resistance to obesity through AR signaling. Furthermore, blocking AR signaling could reverse these metabolic effects in C2orf74 KO mice, further supporting that C2orf74 restricts lipolysis, adipocyte browning, and energy dissipation through suppressing testosterone biogenesis and AR signaling.

Although serum testosterone levels are elevated in HFD-fed C2orf74 KO mice, this increase does not translate into changes in lean mass or BAT. Several factors may account for this observation. In contrast to supraphysiological testosterone supplementation90–93, the elevation observed in our model is endogenous and of moderate magnitude. Increases within the low-to-physiological-range may be sufficient to influence adipocyte fate but insufficient to induce muscle lean mass changes or alter BAT activity93. In addition, the metabolic effects of testosterone are highly context-dependent. HFD-associated factors, including inflammation and metabolic dysfunction, may attenuate tissue responsiveness to testosterone, thereby limiting downstream anabolic or thermogenic outcomes. Notably, adipocyte plasticity appears to be more sensitive to endocrine modulation than organ-level remodeling, which may explain the selective effects observed in our study. Together, our findings suggest that C2orf74 primarily regulates adipocyte plasticity rather than global anabolic outcomes.

The role of testosterone in thermogenesis appears to be controversial. Administration of dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), an adrenal steroid, significantly increases the expression of thermogenic genes such as Ucp1 and enhances thermogenic activity in both BAT and WAT in Otsuka Long Evans Fatty (OLETF) rats, an obesity animal model94. Conversely, androgen depletion via castration has been associated with increased Ucp1 expression in WAT depots in both rats and mice95,96. However, it is important to note that surgical removal of the testes not only depletes testosterone but also alters the levels of other hormones, such as progesterone, which is known to regulate Ucp1 expression97,98. Thus, castration-induced Ucp1 upregulation may not be solely attributed to changes in testosterone and androgen-AR signaling95,96,99.

Notably, discrepancies also exist regarding the effect of androgen and AR signaling on thermogenesis and adipocyte browning. In vitro studies generally support an inhibitory role of androgen-AR signaling in regulating Ucp1 expression97,98,100. The discrepancies may come from experimental models used: in vitro cell culture is a simplified paradigm and less capable of replicating the complex conditions occurring in vivo101. In vivo findings vary depending on the experimental model. AR full KO mice exhibit reduced Ucp1 expression in their WAT depots, consistent with decreased oxygen consumption observed in these mice52,53. Fan et al. further identified an androgen response element in the Ucp1 promoter and demonstrated a stimulation of the Ucp1 promoter by dihydrotestosterone (DHT)52, supporting a role for androgen-AR signaling in promoting thermogenesis and adipocyte browning. Conversely, adipocyte-specific AR KO mice show increased Ucp1 expression in their WATs, along with elevated PGC1α, a transcriptional activator for Ucp1 in the epWAT102,103. The variability in thermogenic phenotypes across different AR KO models suggests that the impact of AR signaling on thermogenesis may be tissue-specific and dependent on the extent of AR depletion. Interestingly, HFD has been shown to suppress castration-induced increase in Ucp1 expression in scWAT96, suggesting that dietary intake is a significant confounding factor when evaluating the impact of androgen and/or AR signaling on thermogenesis capacity. Our findings likely reflect a combined effect of both androgen-AR signaling and dietary food.

Adipose tissue is a highly dynamic organ with remarkably morphological and functional plasticity, allowing for reversible adaptations in their morphology and functions104–106. White adipocytes can undergo a transformation into beige adipocytes through exposure to certain stimuli, such as cold, diet, and medication19,25,107–109. This process is known as browning, characterized by increased energy expenditure, fat multilocularization, and upregulation of thermogenic genes18,110–112. Given its potential to enhance energy dissipation, the browning of white adipocytes has emerged as a promising therapeutic target for obesity and metabolic disorders. Several strategies aiming to promote this process have been proposed, including the injection of engineered anti-inflammatory macrophages and brown adipocyte transplantation113,114. In this study, we found that C2orf74 promotes HFD-induced obesity by restraining the browning of white adipocytes, whereas C2orf74 deficiency confers resistance to HFD-induced obesity by enhancing this process. Notably, ASO therapy targeting C2orf74 effectively reversed obese phenotypes in WT mice subjected to HFD feeding, highlighting C2orf74 ASO as a potential therapeutic approach for HFD-induced obesity and related metabolic diseases.

Adipocyte browning is a multidimensional and context-dependent process. In our study, WT and C2orf74 KO mice were examined under HFD conditions, indicating that the observed effects of AR signaling on browning phenotypes are influenced by this specific metabolic environment. Notably, the same nuclear receptor can exert distinct effects depending on cellular context, posttranslational modifications, and interacting co-regulators. Thus, AR signaling may regulate different layers of the browning program depending on the physiological context. In our model, the regulation effect appears to be mainly mediated through the Ucp1-dependent thermogenic execution pathway.

Recruitment of thermogenic capacity occurs not only during cold acclimation but also in response to caloric overfeeding. HFD can stimulate sympathetic tone and increase Ucp1 expression in brown/beige adipocytes. However, it remains unclear whether the increase in sympathetic activity under thermoneutral conditions is sufficient to activate Ucp1 to a degree that meaningfully affects whole-body energy expenditure. Indeed, conflicting results have been reported regarding the contribution of Ucp1 to energy balance regulation and diet-induced obesity at thermoneutrality115–118. In this study, C2orf74 KO mice exhibit persistent resistance to diet-induced obesity compared to WT controls under both cold and thermoneutral conditions, accompanied by sustained elevation of Ucp1 levels in white adipose tissues under both environmental settings. Furthermore, adipose-specific Ucp1 knockdown in C2orf74 KO mice results in increased body weight gain accompanied by reduced energy expenditure, further supporting the involvement of a Ucp1-dependent mechanism. Notably, additional Ucp1-independent mechanisms, such as enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis and activation119, may also contribute to the observed phenotypes.

Testosterone is biosynthesized primarily in Leydig cells, a process traditionally regulated by the HPG axis120. The testis is stimulated by pituitary gonadotropins, including LH and FSH, both of which are secreted in response to hypothalamic gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH). LH acts on the Leydig cells to produce testosterone, while FSH supports spermatogenesis either independently or synergistically with testosterone121,122. Beyond the classic HPG axis, additional regulatory mechanisms may involve paracrine interactions between germ cells and neighboring somatic Sertoli cells and/or interstitial Leydig cells. Such local intratesticular regulation is well-documented in spermatogenesis, where Leydig cell-produced testosterone acts on Sertoli cells to stimulate seminiferous tubule fluid production and promote spermatogenesis123,124. However, how germ cells influence Leydig cells and impact testosterone production remains elusive. By examining the cellular distribution of C2orf74 within the testes, we found that C2orf74 is predominantly localized in germ cells, where it stabilizes Bpifa3 protein by promoting its glycosylation. This modification enhances Bpifa3 secretion, allowing its uptake by Leydig cells, where Bpifa3 transcriptionally suppresses the expression of testosterone biosynthesis enzymes and inhibits testosterone production. Thus, our study defines a newly identified paracrine regulation pathway for testosterone biogenesis.

Adipocytes undergo profound changes with aging, including reduced lipolysis, diminished browning of white adipocytes, and impaired mitochondrial respiratory function74,75. However, the underlying triggers for these changes remain unclear. Our study demonstrates that aging induces the expression of C2orf74, which promotes obesity by suppressing thermogenesis, lipolysis, fatty acid oxidation, and mitochondrial respiration in white adipocytes. These findings identify C2orf74 as a potential therapeutic target for both high-fat-induced obesity and aging-related obesity.

Methods

Experimental model

Animals

C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories. C2orf74 homozygous knockout (KO) mice and C2orf74flox/flox mice were generated by Biocytogen. Briefly, C2orf74 KO mice were generated using the TALEN genome editing technique to delete the entire genomic sequence. C2orf74flox/flox mice were generated by flanking exon 3 and exon 4 of C2orf74 with loxP sites. Stra8 cre transgenic mice were originally from Jackson Lab. Mice were housed on a 12/12 h light-dark cycle at 21 ± 1 °C and 55–60% humidity with free access to food and water. Male mice were fed either a normal chow diet (NCD; 10% kcal from fat, WQJX Bio-technology) or a high-fat diet (HFD; 60% kcal from fat, Trophic Animal Feed High-tech, TP23510)125. Diet feeding was initiated at 8 weeks of age and continued for the indicated durations. Unless otherwise stated, mice were housed at room temperature (21–22 °C); thermoneutral housing (30 °C) was used for specific experiments as indicated. Animals were randomly assigned to each experimental group and assessed in a randomized order. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Animal Studies Committee at the University of Science and Technology of China.

Cell lines

293T (ATCC, CRL-3216), derived from a female donor, and GC-2spd cells (ATCC, CRL-2196), derived from a male donor, were cultured under standard conditions in DMEM (GIBCO) plus 10% FBS (BI) and 1% streptomycin/penicillin (Invitrogen) at 37 °C with 5% humidified CO2.

Method details

Plasmid construction

The coding sequences of C2orf74, Dpm1, Entpd6, Wfdc3, Bpifa3, Cst8, and Mif were PCR amplified from mouse testis cDNA and subcloned into pcDNA3.1 vector (Invitrogen), with additional FLAG tag on the C-terminus of C2orf74, Entpd6, Wfdc3, Bpifa3, Cst8, and Mif, HA tag on the N-terminus of Dpm1. C2orf74 truncation fragments with a FLAG tag on the C-terminus were subcloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector. The shRNA sequences for Dpm1 (TRCN0000111998) and a negative control shRNA (SHC002) were obtained from the MISSION shRNA Library (Sigma), and subcloned into miRZip lentivector (System Biosciences). All the cloning primers were included in Supplementary Data 1.

Metabolic cages

The energy expenditure of mice was measured by the Promethion Metabolic Cage System (Sable Systems). Mice were housed individually in metabolic chambers for 2 days with free access to food and water prior to data collection126. Each mouse was monitored continuously for 24 h. Oxygen consumption (VO2), carbon dioxide expiration (VCO2) levels were obtained 13 times per hour per mouse. Physical activity was assessed by measuring the distance traveled within the beam break system. Energy expenditure (EE) is calculated using the Weir equation127 and normalized to the lean mass. All measurements were performed at constant temperature (22 °C) on a 12/12 h light/dark cycle.

Body composition analysis

Fat and lean mass in mice were determined by Bruker’s Whole Body Composition Analyzer (Bruker Optics, The Woodlands, TX) with a measuring frequency of 7.5 mHz. The body weight of each mouse was recorded prior to body composition measurement. Each mouse was gently placed into a holder without anesthesia, and the restraining disc was adjusted carefully to restrict movement while allowing normal respiration. The holder with mice was inserted into the instrument for fat and lean mass measurement, and each mouse was returned to its home cage once the results were displayed.

Glucose tolerance test (GTT) and Insulin tolerance test (ITT)

For GTT, mice were fasted overnight, and glucose was administered intraperitoneally. For ITT, mice were fasted 6 h and insulin was administered intraperitoneally. The tail blood was collected at 0, 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min after insulin administration and blood glucose levels were measured using a glucometer (Abbott, FreeStyle Optium Neo), which has a maximum detectable value of 27.8 mmol/L, or a glucometer (Roche, ACCU-CHEK Active), which has a maximum detectable value of 33.33 mmol/L.

Rectal temperature measurement during cold exposure

Mice were placed at 4 °C with free access to water and food. Body temperatures were measured by insertion of a thermometer probe (RWD) into the rectum up to 2 cm. Rectal temperatures were recorded every 2 h over a period of 6 h.

Absorption of dietary fat

HFD-fed WT and C2orf74 KO mice were provided with olive oil through intragastric gavage as described previously128–130. The intestinal absorption of dietary fat was determined by both measuring serum TG and conducting Oil red O staining for the proximal jejunum. Briefly, mice were fasted overnight and then given 1 g/kg poloxamer 407 intraperitoneally to deactivate the endogenous lipase. Mouse tail blood was collected an hour later, and the baseline TG level was measured. This was followed by intragastric administration of 200 μL olive oil. Tail blood was collected at 1, 2, 4, 6 h after the gavage, and TG levels were measured accordingly. Two hours after the gavage of olive oil, the proximal jejunum was collected, washed with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4 °C. The fixed samples were then incubated in 30% sucrose for 24 h at 4 °C and embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT Compound. The proximal jejunum was sectioned to 10 μm using a microtome (Leica), and sections were stained with 0.5% Oil Red O. The images were captured by microscopy equipped with a CCD (Thunder).

H&E staining and immunohistochemistry

Mouse adipose tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C immediately after tissue dissection, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned to a thickness of 7 μm using a microtome (Leica). Sections were stained with hematoxylin (Sigma) for 3 min, followed by eosin for 15 min (Sigma). The images were captured using an optical microscope equipped with a CCD camera (Thunder).

For immunohistochemistry (IHC), paraffin sections were subjected to dewaxing and rehydration, followed by antigen retrieval (citrate buffer, boiled at 95 °C for 10 min and cooled to room temperature) and treatment with 3% H2O2 for 10 min to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. Subsequently, tissue sections were subjected to TBST washes and then blocked with 5% goat serum for 1 hour. The sections were then incubated with primary antibody for 2 h, followed by secondary antibody for 1 h and detection using DAB kit (Proteintech). Images were captured using an optical microscope equipped with a CCD camera (Thunder).

β-oxidation measurement

The contribution of β-oxidation to mitochondrial respiration was calculated based on oxygen flux in the presence of exogenous substrate palmitate and/or fatty acid oxidation inhibitor etomoxir using an Oroboros Oxygraph-2k system. White adipose tissues were minced into pieces and transferred to the 37 °C chamber and incubated with oxidation assay medium (Mir05 buffer supplemented with 2 mM L-carnitine, 0.1 mM malate, 0.05 mM coenzyme A, 1 mM DTT, and 2 mM ATP). About 2 μM digitonin was titrated into the chamber to permeabilize the plasma membrane. Samples were pre-incubated with/without etomoxir (40 μM) for 30 min, followed by the addition of palmitate-BSA (200 μM). Oxygen flux was measured after equilibration, and oxygen flux difference (ΔO2 flux) was calculated to represent the capacity of β-oxidation.

Triglycerides (TG), cholesterol, and testosterone (T) measurements

Blood samples were obtained from mice that fasted overnight and placed on ice for 2 h, followed by centrifugation at 3000×g for 30 min at 4 °C. The resulting serum supernatant was transferred to a new tube and frozen at −80 °C for triglyceride (TG), cholesterol and testosterone measurement in serum.

TG contents in serum and tissues were determined using Triglycerides Enzymatic assay kits (APPLYGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For cholesterol measurements, tissue samples were homogenized in a lysis buffer (1% Triton X-100 in PBS supplemented with 1% proteinase inhibitor) and centrifuged at 18,800×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected for cholesterol assay. Cholesterol levels were determined by Amplex Red Cholesterol Assay Kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Testosterone content was measured by an ELISA kit (Elabscience) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Leydig cell isolation

Leydig cells were isolated from testes as previously described131, with modifications. Briefly, mice were euthanized, and testes were promptly dissected and washed with PBS. The testes were cautiously decapsulated, then incubated with 1 mg/mL collagenase IV in DMEM/F12 with gentle shaking at 37 °C for 10 min, and the digestion was stopped by the addition of cold DMEM/F12. The interstitial cells were isolated from seminiferous tubules through gravity sedimentation, followed by centrifugation at 300×g for 6 min. The crude interstitial cells were then resuspended in 2 mL DMEM/F12 supplemented with 5% FBS and filtered through a 200-mesh strainer. To obtain purified Leydig cells, a discontinuous Percoll gradient comprising layers of 30% (2 mL), 40% (4 mL), 50% (2 mL), and 60% (2 mL) Percoll dissolved in HBSS was prepared. The interstitial cell suspension was loaded onto the top of 30% Percoll and centrifuged at 500×g for 30 min at 4 °C. The fraction containing Leydig cells (50–60%) was collected, washed with PBS, and centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min to obtain Leydig cell pellets.

Germ cell isolation

Germ cells were obtained from the seminiferous tubules of mouse testes using stepwise enzymatic digestion as previously described132, with modifications. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under anesthesia, and testes were rapidly dissected with the white membrane removed. Testes were then placed in digestion solution 1, which contains DMEM/F12 supplemented with 1 mg/mL collagenase IV (Nordmark) and 0.2 mg/mL DNase I (Sangon), and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min with gentle shaking to ensure uniform digestion. Subsequently, cold DMEM/F12 was added to terminate the digestion. The seminiferous tubules were carefully dispersed using fine forceps under a dissecting microscope and washed three times with DMEM/F12 to remove interstitial cells and a small number of peritubular cells. To further remove peritubular cells, the tubules were placed in digestion solution 2, which contains DMEM/F12 supplemented with 1 mg/mL collagenase IV, 0.5 mg/mL DNase I, and 400 U/mL hyaluronidase (Sangon). The digestion and termination procedures were the same as described above. After washing, the tubules were subjected to digestion solution 3, which contains 0.1% trypsin, 1 mg/mL collagenase IV, 0.5 mg/mL DNase I, and 400 U/mL hyaluronidase. When the seminiferous tubules were visibly dissociated, the digestion was terminated with a culture medium containing serum (15% FBS), and further dispersed by pipetting and filtered through a 75 μm and a 40 μm cell strainer sequentially, followed by centrifugation and washing with PBS three times. The resulting cell pellets were considered germ cells.

Creatine kinase activity

Creatine kinase activity was assessed using the Creatine Kinase (CK) Activity Assay Kit (Elabscience) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Administration of enzalutamide

The androgen receptor (AR) antagonist, enzalutamide (MCE, HY-70002) was initially dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock solution. This stock solution was then diluted to a working solution with a final vehicle composition of 10% DMSO, 40% PEG 300, and 50% saline. Enzalutamide was administered to mice via oral gavage at a dose of 30 mg/kg body weight, three times per week, for 12 consecutive weeks.

High-resolution respirometry

Mitochondrial respiration in adipose tissue explants was assessed using the Oroboros Oxygraph-2k system, and data were analyzed with DatLab8 software (Oroboros Instruments, Innsbruck, Austria)133–135. Briefly, freshly isolated adipose tissues (~50 mg) were minced in Mir05 Buffer (20 mM HEPES, 110 mM sucrose, 10 mM KH2PO4, 20 mM taurine, 60 mM lactobionic acid, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM EGTA, 1 mg/mL fatty acid-free BSA, pH 7.1) supplemented with 1.5 mM octanoyl-l-carnitine, 5 mM pyruvate, 2 mM malate, and 10 mM glutamate at a 37 °C chamber. Plasma membrane was permeabilized with 2 µM digitonin, and tissue samples were continuously stirred at 750 rpm for equilibration. Real-time oxygen flux was recorded under basal conditions and following sequential additions of oligomycin (5 µM), FCCP (titrated to 2–8 µM until maximal mitochondrial electron transfer capacity was reached), and rotenone (1 µM)/antimycin A (5 µM).

Epididymal sperm count

Male mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation after being anesthetized. The epididymides were then removed and minced in 1 mL of prewarmed phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The minced tissue was subsequently incubated at 37 °C for 20 min to release sperm. After the incubation, sperm samples were diluted 1:50 in 1 mL of warmed PBS. Sperm counts were performed using a hemocytometer, and the average count was calculated based on three repetitions for each mouse.

Immunofluorescence staining

293T cells seeded on a coverslip were washed three times with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min136. Cells were permeabilized with PBS plus 0.2% Triton X-100 and blocked with 2% BSA in PBS for 30 min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies diluted in 0.5% BSA containing 0.1% Triton X-100 overnight at 4 °C. Cells were rinsed three times and incubated with Alexa-conjugated secondary antibody (Invitrogen). Fluorescence signals were captured with a Leica TCS SPE microscope equipped with DFC 365 FX Digital Camera.

Mice were perfused transcardially with PBS when anesthetized using 1% pentobarbital sodium. Mouse testes were isolated and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C137. Testes were then incubated in 30% sucrose for 24 h at 4 °C and embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT Compound. Testes were sectioned at 20-μm thickness using a microtome (Leica), and cryostat sections were mounted on a glass slide for immunofluorescence staining. The testis section was washed with PBS plus 0.25% Triton X-100 and blocked with 2% BSA in PBS for 45 min. Testis sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by Alexa-conjugated secondary antibody (Invitrogen). Fluorescence signals were captured with a Leica TCS SPE microscope equipped with a DFC 365 FX Digital Camera.

Immunoblotting and densitometric analysis

The tissues or cells were homogenized in a lysis buffer (PBS containing 1% Triton X-100, 1% proteinase inhibitors, and 1% phosphatase inhibitors), followed by sonication and centrifugation at 18,800×g for 10 min at 4 °C138. The supernatant was collected, and protein concentration was determined by the BCA Protein assay kit (Pierce). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight and secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The immunoblots were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence (Pierce) using ChemiScope (CLiNX). Immunoreactive bands were quantified by Fiji software (NIH). Antibodies are listed in Supplementary Data 2.

RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and quantitative PCR (qPCR) analyses

Total RNA was extracted from tissue or cell using Trizol (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions139. About 500 ng of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using HiScript II Reverse Transcription System (Vazyme). Diluted cDNA was used as a template for qPCR analysis with AceQTM qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme) on LightCycler 96 system (Roche, version 1.1) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The results of qPCR were analyzed using the CT method. The primers used in qPCR are listed in Supplementary Data 1.

4D label-free quantitative proteomics analysis

Protein extraction and digestion

Germ cells isolated from WT and C2orf74 KO mice were lysed in SDT buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6) for total protein extraction. The protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad, USA). Protein digestion by trypsin was conducted in accordance with the filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) procedure described by Matthias Mann140. The digested peptides were then purified on C18 cartridges (Empore™ SPE Cartridges C18 (standard density), bed I.D. 7 mm, volume 3 mL, Sigma), concentrated using vacuum centrifugation, and reconstituted in 40 µL of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid.

LC-MS/MS analysis

The LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted using a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometry system (Bruker) coupled with Nanoelute (Bruker). Peptides were loaded onto a C18-reversed phase analytical column (Thermo Scientific Easy Column) with dimensions of 25 cm length, 75 mm inner diameter, and 1.9 μm resin, using 95% buffer A (0.1% Formic acid in water) as the mobile phase. Separation was achieved by a linear gradient of buffer B (99.9% acetonitrile and 0.1% Formic acid) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ion mode, with an electrospray voltage of 1.5 kV applied. The TOF detector was used to analyze both precursor and fragment ions across a mass range of m/z 100–1700. The timsTOF Pro was operated in parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (PASEF) mode, with the following parameters for data collection: an ion mobility coefficient (1/K0) range of 0.6 to 1.6 Vs cm2, 1 MS scan, and 10 MS/MS PASEF scans. Active exclusion was enabled with a release time of 24 s.

Identification and quantitation of proteins

MS raw data were analyzed using MaxQuant (v1.6.14; APTBIO) for protein identification and quantification.

RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted from white adipose tissue or isolated Leydig cells using Trizol (Invitrogen), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and its concentration was assessed using the Onedrop spectrophotometer (WINS). RNA-seq cDNA libraries were constructed using VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, total mRNA was enriched by Oligo (dT) beads, then was fragmented and converted into double-stranded cDNA using random hexamer primers and reverse transcriptase. The cDNA was purified and end-repaired, followed by adapter ligation and PCR amplification. The library was purified and quantified before being sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq system using 150 nt pair-end reads (Annoroad).

ASO rete injection in vivo

Microinjection of ASOs into the testis was conducted as previously described, with modifications141,142. Mice were anesthetized, and the testis was exposed through a small incision in the abdomen. 0.02% trypan blue was added to ASOs to monitor the process of filling of the seminiferous tubules. The injection volume was approximately 10 μL with a total amount of 3 nmol. Saline was added to keep the tissue moist during the process of injection process. The testis was returned to the abdominal cavity after injection, and incisions were closed with sutures when both sides of the testes were finished with injection. ASOs used in this study were chemically modified with 5’-LNA and 3’-cholesterol to improve the biodistribution and stability143.

Generation of adeno-associated virus and adipose tissue injection

The adeno-associated virus (AAV) was prepared according to previously published144. Briefly, target sequences in AAV vector, pHelper, and AAV serotype 2/8 packing plasmids were transfected into 293 T cells in a ratio of 2:1:1 with polyethylenimine (PEI, Sigma). 24 h after transfection, the culture medium was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS. 72 h after transfection, both cells and medium were harvested for AAV purification. AAV particles were released from cells with freezing/thawing cycles, followed by incubation with 50 U/ml benzonase nuclease (MKbio) and 10 U/ml RNase I (Genstonebio) at 37 °C for 30 min. The incubation continued for another 30 min upon addition of 0.5% sodium deoxycholate (Sigma), followed by centrifugation at 2500×g for 30 min to remove cell debris. 40% PEG8000 and 2.5 M NaCl was added to the supernatant to precipitate the virus, and the viral pellet was resuspended in PBS gently, followed by chloroform and (NH4)2SO4 extraction to remove contaminated proteins. Viral titer was determined using a qPCR-based approach. AAV virus was microinjected into 5 distinct spots of an epididymal fat pad, delivering a total of 1.2 × 1011 viral genomes (VG). RNA and protein assays for adipose tissues were performed 4 weeks after the injection.

Statistical analysis

The results were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) for all samples included in each analysis. Statistical significance was determined using GraphPad Prism (Version 9) or IBM SPSS. All experimental findings were confirmed by independent repetitions, and sample sizes were specified in the figure legends. All quantitative data were analyzed blindly without knowledge of sample or animal group identity.
