Highly sensitive X-ray responsive molecular switches
Authors: Jiangang Li, Kuanjian Wei, Xiangmei Liu, Zijian Zhou, Peiling Dai, Jiacheng Shen, Xiuwen Xu, Mingye Zhu, Mengzhu Wang, Yuchang Wang, Yongjing Deng, Shujuan Liu, Yun Ma, Qiang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractX-ray-triggered molecular switching promises remote control behind physical barriers, yet existing radioswitches typically require multi-gray doses to reach a photostationary state. Here we route X-ray energy through triplet excitons to enable low-dose switching. An efficient scintillator acts as a triplet sensitizer, transferring energy to a photoswitch with lower triplet energy via triplet-triplet energy transfer (TTET). Flexible films combining a Cu2I2(POP)2 scintillator with hydrazone switch 1-I reach a photostationary state at ~0.18 Gy and show a detection limit near 0.01 mGy, about two orders of magnitude lower than previous systems. HPLC quantification, radical-scavenger tests, and time-resolved spectroscopy support a TTET-dominated mechanism and establish a design rule based on triplet-level matching. Isomerization quenches fluorescence, enabling optical readout for high-resolution radiography (3251 dpi) with pattern retention for at least two months. In this work, we show that penetrating X-rays enable practical control of molecular functions in previously inaccessible complex environments.Subject terms: Chemical physics, Metamaterials, Sensors and biosensors

Introduction

Molecular switches have emerged as powerful and versatile dynamic tools, allowing for the remote and reversible control of their structural configurations, physical properties and chemical reactivity1–4. The past decades have witnessed the rapid development of photoswitches, including azobenzenes5–7, diarylethenes8–10, spiropyrans11–13, and hydrazones14–16, for various applications in biomedicine5,6,17–19, molecular electronics7,20–22, catalysis23–26, and nanotechnology27–29. These advancements highlight the critical need to expand and enhance the existing toolbox of molecular switches, optimizing their photoresponsive performance to boost the field forward and unlock novel functionalities. Recent efforts have extended the response wavelengths of photoswitches from the commonly utilized ultraviolet region to the visible and even near-infrared regions7,30–32, significantly broadening their applicability. Nevertheless, the limited penetration depth of light presents a significant challenge for inducing molecular isomerization in scenarios obstructed by physical barriers.

In contrast, X-rays possessing superior penetration capabilities33–37, offer a compelling avenue for triggering molecular isomerization (termed radio-switching) across physical barriers. This distinct advantage renders X-ray-responsive switches (radio-switches) with significant potential in applications requiring deep actuation, such as non-destructive testing37,38, information storage within protected media39, and targeted drug release36,40–42. However, realizing efficient radio-switches has proven exceptionally challenging. The low X-ray absorption cross-section of typical organic molecules results in extremely poor sensitivity, often necessitating high radiation doses to achieve a photostationary state43–45. Covalently attaching high-Z atoms to the switch only marginally improves absorption while often perturbing the delicate photo-isomerisation pathways46–48. Previous work determined that indirect strategies that harness X-ray-generated free radicals introduce a host of undesirable side reactions and still demand multi-gray doses, for example, about 20 Gy for an azobenzene-Gd chelate49. Consequently, the development of general strategies to achieve highly sensitive radio-switches, operating at dramatically lower doses, is paramount to enable their translation into practical applications.

In this work, we route X-ray energy through triplet excitons rather than radical chemistry to drive the isomerization of molecular switches. Molecular scintillators can generate abundant triplet excitons under X-ray irradiation, a behavior analogous to organic electroluminescence50. When the triplet level of a scintillator is higher than that of a photoswitch, triplet to triplet energy transfer (TTET) can populate the triplet state of the switch and drive isomerization30. Decoupling X-ray absorption and switching into a two-component system provides a simple and effective design rule based on triplet-level matching. We implement this concept by pairing a molecular scintillator with a photoswitch whose triplet level lies below that of the sensitizer. Flexible films reach a photostationary mixture at about 0.18 Gy and show a detection limit near 0.01 mGy, demonstrating a reduction of nearly two orders of magnitude compared to the reported system49. Isomerization quenches fluorescence and enables high-resolution, rewritable radiography on flexible substrates with patterns that are stable for months under ambient conditions. Because the governing criterion is triplet-level alignment, the approach is general and can be extended to other scintillators and switch families to deliver low-dose radioswitching in complex environments.

Results

Design

The strategy of scintillator-sensitized energy transfer requires a sensitizer with two key features: (i) high X-ray scintillation efficiency to ensure sufficient triplet population, thereby enhancing the TTET process efficiency; and (ii) a high triplet excited state energy level to match the energy requirements of the photoswitchable molecules. To meet these criteria, we sought a material exhibiting both high X-ray scintillation efficiency and a sufficiently high T1 energy level as the energy donor. The copper(I) iodide cluster complex, Cu2I2(POP)2 (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 1), which we synthesized, proved to be an exceptional candidate. It displayed a high radioluminescence (RL) light yield of 17,090 ± 700 photons MeV−1 and a substantial triplet energy level (T1 = 2.54 eV, derived from its phosphorescence peak at 488 nm) (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Figs. 3, 4). In addition, Cu2I2(POP)2 demonstrates high stability under prolonged UV exposure, ambient storage, continuous X-ray irradiation up to 20 Gy, and repeated irradiation cycles (Supplementary Fig. 5). These results highlight its robust structural integrity and photophysical reliability, underscoring its potential for practical applications.

For the photoswitchable acceptor, we selected the hydrazone derivative ethyl (Z)−2-(4-(dimethylamino)phenyl)−2-(2-(4-iodophenyl)hydrazineylidene)acetate (1-I, Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 2). This molecule exhibits efficient photoisomerization, demonstrated by a quantum yield (QY) of 22.98 ± 1.47% for the Z to E conversion (in air-saturated toluene upon 440 nm irradiation) (Supplementary Figs. 6–8). The isomerization also results in a significant fluorescence quenching both in toluene solution (on/off ratio of 7.69) and PMMA film (on/off ratio of 3.80), offering a clear and convenient readout signal (Supplementary Fig. 9). Furthermore, its exceptionally long thermal isomerization half-life (τ1/2 = 303.65 years in toluene-d8) ensures the stability of the metastable state, making it suitable for long-term recording applications (Supplementary Figs. 10–15). The strategic incorporation of an iodine atom into 1-I aimed at two key advantages. One is to potentially enhance its intersystem crossing (ISC) efficiency, thereby promoting triplet state population crucial for the TTET pathway. The other is to increase the intrinsic X-ray absorption cross-section of photoswitch30,43. To verify the energy-level alignment, we recorded their PL spectra at both room temperature and 80 K. Only singlet (S1) emission was observed, located at 514 nm (2.41 eV) for 1-I (Supplementary Fig. 16). Accordingly, time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) calculations confirmed the estimated T1 energy level of 1-I to be 2.00 eV (Supplementary Table 2). This value is significantly lower than the T1 of Cu2I2(POP)2 (2.54 eV), indicating a thermodynamically favorable TTET process.

Scintillator-sensitized radio-switching

We confirmed the photoisomerization behavior of 1-I under direct light irradiation. Under 440 nm light, typically exciting the S1 state, 1-I in PMMA film underwent isomerization, reaching a photostationary state within approximately 90 s, accompanied by a significant decrease in its fluorescence at 516 nm (Supplementary Fig. 9, Supplementary Table 3). However, consistent with the challenges faced by conventional radio-switches, direct X-ray irradiation of a PMMA film containing only 1-I did not induce observable isomerization or fluorescence change, even at substantial doses (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 17). This highlights the inefficiency of direct X-ray absorption by the organic switch molecule.

In contrast, after optimizing the doping ratio and the host matrix (Supplementary Figs. 18, 19), the Cu2I2(POP)2/1-I film exhibits pronounced isomerization under X-ray irradiation. As shown in Fig. 2b, c, the PL intensity of 1-I at 516 nm decreased sharply by approximately 5.5-fold after a cumulative X-ray dose of only 0.18 Gy, substantially lower than the doses required for other reported systems (Supplementary Table 4). Concurrently, the fluorescence quantum yield dropped from 18.43% to 0.27% (Supplementary Fig. 20, Supplementary Table 5). The stark contrast between the inert response of pure 1-I and the pronounced dose-dependent switching in the composite film highlights the crucial role of the scintillator in mediating energy transfer. These results highlight the ability of the TTET-based strategy to achieve effective switching at low radiation doses.

Notably, the dose required to reach photostationary state represents a two-order-of-magnitude reduction compared to previously reported radio-switch systems requiring doses around 20 Gy49,51. The system exhibited remarkable sensitivity, with a calculated limit of detection for X-rays determined to be 0.01 mGy (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, heating the irradiated film at 353.15 K led to thermal back-isomerization, gradually restoring the initial fluorescence intensity over 40 min (Fig. 2e). The system also demonstrated good photostability and fatigue resistance under repeated X-ray irradiation and thermal annealing cycles, with minimal degradation observed over ten cycles (Fig. 2f), indicating its suitability for reusable recording applications.

To confirm that the photoluminescence changes are linked to genuine isomerization, we quantified the E/Z composition by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) on films dissolved immediately after X-ray irradiation. Two well-resolved peaks at retention times 15.02 min (E) and 18.69 min (Z) were assigned by co-injection with standards (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 21a). The non-irradiated film was Z-rich with E/Z = 9/91. The composition shifted monotonically with dose and reached a photostationary state at 0.18 Gy with E/Z = 81/19 (Fig. 3a). No additional peaks above the detection threshold were observed. Heating the E-rich film at 353.15 K drove thermal back-isomerization toward Z, giving E/Z = 20/80 after 40 min (Fig. 3b). The HPLC trends mirror the fluorescence recovery in Fig. 2e and align with the intrinsic thermal stability of the hydrazone switch, establishing a direct chemical readout of the X-ray-induced switching.

Elucidation of the triplet-triplet energy transfer mechanism

To elucidate the X-ray-triggered switching mechanism, we performed a series of spectroscopic and control experiments. First, we probed the involvement of the triplet state in the isomerization of 1-I itself. The influence of molecular oxygen, an efficient triplet quencher, on the photoisomerization QY of 1-I was studied in toluene solution under 440 nm light. In an air-saturated solution, where oxygen can quench triplets, leaving singlet pathways primarily, the QY was 22.98 ± 1.47%. Upon purging with nitrogen to remove oxygen, the QY significantly increased to 32.99 ± 1.95%. Conversely, bubbling with oxygen reduced the QY to 12.02 ± 1.02% (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Figs. 6–8). This pronounced oxygen dependence provides strong evidence that the triplet excited state of 1-I is indeed involved and contributes significantly to its photoisomerization process.

With the role of the triplet state established, we then sought direct evidence for TTET from the Cu2I2(POP)2 sensitizer to the 1-I acceptor. We monitored the phosphorescence lifetime (τ0) of Cu2I2(POP)2/1-I at 483 nm in thin films containing increasing concentrations of 1-I. As shown in Fig. 3d, the triplet lifetime of Cu2I2(POP)2/1-I systematically decreased from its intrinsic value of 10.1 μs to 9.81, 9.53, 8.71, 8.15, 7.48, and 6.69 μs as the 1-I concentration increased (0.5% to 10% weight ratio). This concentration-dependent quenching is indicative of energy transfer. A linear Stern-Volmer relationship was observed (Supplementary Fig. 22), yielding a TTET rate constant (kTTET) of 1.81 × 1010 M−1·s−1. This large effective quenching/transfer constant indicates highly efficient triplet quenching/transfer in the condensed film, consistent with the pronounced donor lifetime shortening52–55. This confirms efficient energy transfer from the triplet state of the sensitizer to that of the acceptor.

Finally, to rule out contributions from radiolysis-derived radicals, including •O2-, •OOR, •OH, and •NO, to the switching process, we incorporated the radical scavenger p-benzoquinone into the Cu2I2(POP)2/1-I films (0.1 wt%). After X-ray exposure at 0.18 Gy, the scavenger-doped films displayed a fluorescence quenching response that was indistinguishable from the undoped controls within experimental error (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 21b). The persistence of switching in the presence of an efficient radical trap indicates that radical-mediated pathways do not make a measurable contribution under our conditions.

Taken together, the oxygen-dependence of the QY, the direct observation of lifetime quenching, and the ineffectiveness of the radical scavenger provide a self-consistent and compelling picture. We thus confirm that the high sensitivity of our system originates from a clean, TTET-dominated mechanism, where X-ray energy is efficiently transferred from the scintillator Cu2I2(POP)2 to the switch 1-I via triplet states (Fig. 1).

Structure-property relationship in photoswitches

To further elucidate the pivotal role of triplet excited states in photoswitching processes, we synthesized two model compounds, (Z)-2-(2-(4-bromophenyl)hydrazineylidene)-2-(4-(dimethylamino)phenyl)acetate (1-Br) and (Z)-2-(4-(dimethylamino)phenyl)-2-(2-phenylhydrazineylidene)acetate (1-H). TDDFT calculations revealed that substituting iodine (I) with bromine (Br) or hydrogen (H) had minimal impact on the triplet energy levels (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Table 2). In contrast, the calculated spin-orbit coupling matrix elements (SOCME) increased markedly with the atomic number of the substituent, from 1.4370 cm−1 for 1-H to 5.0832 cm−1 for 1-Br, and reaching 9.0637 cm−1 for 1-I (Fig. 4b). This clear trend highlights the heavy-atom effect of iodine, which significantly enhances spin-orbit coupling and thus facilitates more efficient ISC to the triplet state. The much larger SOCME of 1-I therefore provides a mechanistic basis for its triplet population and more efficient photoswitching behavior.

Subsequent control experiments further supported the involvement of triplet states. The photoisomerization quantum yields (QY) of 1-H and 1-Br were measured under air-, N2-, and O2-saturated conditions. The measured QYs were 15.04% ± 1.12% and 20.60% ± 1.34% in air, 19.88% ± 1.55% and 27.88% ± 1.29% in nitrogen, and 9.43% ± 0.50% and 10.34% ± 0.55% in oxygen for 1-H and 1-Br, respectively (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Figs. 23–28). Compared with the iodine-substituted system, oxygen quenching exerts a smaller effect on 1-H and 1-Br, consistent with their less efficient ISC and reduced triplet-state involvement. Furthermore, the TTET rate constants for 1-H and 1-Br were calculated to be 9.68 × 109 M−1·s−1 and 1.54 × 1010 M−1·s−1, respectively, both lower than that of 1-I (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 29). The fabricated X-ray responsive films Cu2I2(POP)2/1-H and Cu2I2(POP)2/1-Br required higher X-ray irradiation doses of 335.80 mGy and 244.21 mGy, respectively, to reach the photostationary states, with LoD determined to be 1.44 mGy and 0.60 mGy, respectively (Fig. 4e–h). These findings suggest the importance of efficient triplet energy transfer in the photoisomerization of these systems. It is crucial to design photoswitchable molecules that can be effectively isomerized by the triplet excited state to develop highly sensitive X-ray responsive switches.

High-resolution and flexible X-ray imaging

The remarkable sensitivity and fluorescence contrast of the Cu2I2(POP)2/1-I film enabled its use as a novel X-ray imaging recording medium. The prepared films were highly transparent (>58% transmittance in 500–800 nm) and exhibited uniform fluorescence before irradiation (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Figs. 30, 31). We successfully recorded the internal circuitry (0.33 mm lines) of a 1.1 cm × 1.1 cm chip by exposing the film through the chip to X-rays and subsequently reading the fluorescence pattern under UV light (Fig. 5b). High-resolution radiographic patterns were obtained, as demonstrated by imaging a 400-mesh copper grid (10 μm line width, 60 μm aperture), which were clearly resolved using confocal microscopy (Fig. 5c, d). Quantitative assessment using a USAF 1951 resolution test chart confirmed a spatial resolution capability of up to 3251 dpi (approximately 7.8 μm line pairs) (Supplementary Fig. 32). The flexibility of film allowed conformal contact with curved objects (Fig. 5e), enabling clear recording of the complex internal structure of an electronic circuit board (Fig. 5f, left), whereas conventional scintillator-based imaging (requiring real-time camera capture) produced only an overlapping projection (Fig. 5f, right). Importantly, the recorded patterns, contrast between irradiated and non-irradiated areas, remained stable and clearly visible for over two months under ambient conditions (Supplementary Fig. 33).

This TTET-based radio-switch film offers a distinct advantage over conventional X-ray imaging methods. Unlike scintillator screens requiring coupling with expensive cameras or electronics (e.g., CMOS or TFT) for real-time readout, this system functions as a passive recording medium. The X-ray dose information is stored chemically as the isomer ratio and can be read out using a simple UV lamp. This eliminates the need for complex imaging setups during exposure and avoids the cumbersome wet processing steps associated with traditional X-ray film. The operation simplicity, cost-effectiveness, flexibility, high resolution, and long-term storage capability make this system a promising platform for applications ranging from non-destructive testing in resource-limited settings to specialized radiographic recording tasks.

Discussion

In summary, we have shown that pairing an X-ray scintillator with a photoswitch allows radioswitching at low dose by routing energy through triplet excitons. Triplet-level alignment between sensitizer and switch enables TTET and drives isomerization in the solid state. In films that combine a Cu2I2(POP)2 scintillator with a hydrazone switch 1-I, the composition reaches a photostationary mixture at about 0.18 Gy and the limit of detection is near 0.01 mGy. HPLC quantification, radical-scavenger controls and time-resolved measurements are consistent with a TTET-dominated pathway rather than radical chemistry.

Our findings establish a simple and powerful design rule for creating sensitive radioswitches. When the triplet energy of the scintillator exceeds that of the switch, efficient population of the switch triplet state becomes possible, and radioswitching follows. Because triplet energies can be estimated by computation and measured from phosphorescence, the components can be selected prospectively. This modular view decouples X-ray absorption from isomerization and should be transferable to other scintillators and switch families. Isomerization quenches fluorescence and creates high-contrast patterns that can be written by X-rays and read with ultraviolet light. The films are flexible, give high spatial resolution and retain patterns for months under ambient conditions. In settings where simple write-read workflows are valued, the ability to store dose information chemically without cameras or wet processing offers a practical alternative to conventional imaging.

In addition to triplet-level matching, the host polymer plays a practical role in determining whether TTET sensitization translates into large switching contrast in the solid state. While rigid aromatic scintillating hosts such as PS and PVT can in principle, improve X-ray harvesting, our control experiments indicate that such rigid, π-rich microenvironments strongly suppress hydrazone isomerization in the solid state, leading to markedly reduced optical modulation under both visible-light excitation and X-ray exposure (Supplementary Figs. 18, 19). We attribute this suppression to reduced free volume and stronger host-guest interactions that hinder the conformational changes required for Z ↔ E interconversion. In contrast, PMMA provides an optically transparent and comparatively permissive microenvironment that supports efficient isomerization while keeping background luminescence low, making it well suited for passive write-read recording. These observations highlight that, for switch families requiring molecular motion, selecting a host that balances X-ray harvesting with sufficient local mobility/free volume is essential for maximizing radioswitching performance.

The donor lifetime shortening provides a convenient measure of triplet quenching/transfer in dispersed polymer films; however, it does not imply that all sensitizer triplets are transferred with unit probability. In the present architecture, TTET is expected to proceed via a short-range Dexter-type mechanism, so only donor-acceptor pairs within sufficient proximity contribute efficiently. In addition, radiative/nonradiative decay of the sensitizer competes with TTET, and increasing photoswitch loading beyond an optimum can introduce inner-filter effects, self-quenching and/or micro-aggregation that reduce the observable fluorescence modulation. Consistent with these considerations, the composition maximizing optical readout contrast does not necessarily coincide with the composition maximizing donor quenching. Therefore, the selected formulation represents a practical balance between efficient triplet sensitization and high-contrast readout in flexible films.

Future work exploring biocompatibility and device durability under various environmental conditions will be crucial for defining specific application spaces. From a materials-design perspective, TTET utilization could be further improved by strategies that increase donor-acceptor encounter probability without sacrificing optical transparency and readout contrast. Overall, triplet-level matching provides a general route to X-ray-responsive molecular control at low dose. By separating the roles of X-ray absorption and isomerization, this strategy provides a clear route to extend radioswitching beyond the specific system studied and into complex environments that demand deep penetration and simple operation.

Methods

Preparation of X-ray-responsive film

To prepare the film, Cu2I2(POP)2 (20 mg) was first dispersed in 1 mL of chloroform and dispersed by ultrasonication. The corresponding hydrazone compound was then added, followed by 1 mL of a PMMA chloroform solution (80 mg/mL). The resulting mixture was stirred vigorously for 4 h to ensure homogeneous blending. The resulting solution was then deposited on the surface of a quartz sheet and covered to minimize airflow, allowing for slow evaporation. The obtained film was annealed at 50 °C for 30 min to form a uniform X-ray responsive film.

X-ray imaging

For planar imaging, the objects to be measured were placed above the X-ray-responsive film and exposed under a Mini-X2 X-ray tube at an operating voltage of 50 kV and current of 70 μA. For curved-surface imaging, the X-ray-responsive film was conformally attached to the flexible device and mounted on a motorized stage capable of 360° rotation. The rotation speed was set to 5° per second, and a total of seven rotations were performed under the same experimental conditions. In all experiments, the distance between the objects and the X-ray source was fixed at 3 cm, corresponding to a dose rate of approximately 28 mGy·s-1.

After the irradiation setup was completed, the samples were illuminated using a handheld UV lamp equipped with an 8‑Watt halogen tube (λmax = 365 nm), and images were captured using an Apple iPhone 15 Pro Max.
