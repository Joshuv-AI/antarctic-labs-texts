Improving social functioning in people with substance use and mental health disorders using virtual reality (ROPVR): study protocol for a multi-center, pragmatic randomized controlled trial
Authors: Anne Berit Bech, Theresa Friederike Wechsler, Andreas Mühlberger, Lars Lien, Marja Leonhardt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn individuals with substance use disorders (SUDs), impairment of social, occupational, and/or recreational functioning is common. Social functioning encompasses social skills, social behavior, and cognitive skills, which are relevant for establishing meaningful relationships and effective functioning across different domains in life. Introduction

Background and rationale

In individuals with substance use disorders (SUD), impairment of social, occupational, and/or recreational functioning is common and included as diagnostic criteria [1]. Additionally, people with SUD often experience comorbid mental health disorders (MHD) [2–4], many of them also associated with functional impairment. No uniform definition of social functioning exists [5, 6], but it is generally agreed that social functioning encompasses social skills, social behavior, and cognitive skills [6], which are essential for establishing meaningful relationships and for effective functioning across different domains in life.

Social functioning impairments may include problems with assertiveness and interpersonal problem solving. Substances like opioids and stimulants can impair social behaviors, especially interpersonal trust and mental perspective taking [7]. In addition, some people with SUD may have marked deficits in specific social skills for handling situations in which substances are offered or substance use takes place [8]. The relationship between SUD and deficits in social skills is complex and bidirectional and often understudied in substances other than alcohol. Social skills deficit and SUD may act in a reciprocal way, where social skills deficit may feed substance use and vice versa [8]. Thus interventions to improve social functioning are an important treatment target in people with SUD.

The use of immersive virtual reality training (VR training) has shown promising results in the assessment and treatment of MHDs and SUDs [9–12]. There are several VR studies on (psycho)social functioning and social skills training in psychotic disorders [12–17] and autism spectrum disorders [9, 18–21], as well as for other disorders like social anxiety disorder [22]. Interventions in these VR studies typically aim at improving cognitive, social, communication, and/or vocational skills, with preliminary evidence of effect and generalizability to real-world settings [14]. However, across VR studies, the quality of evidence varies, with high variability in methodological rigor and application maturity. There is a lack of well-designed randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with larger samples, and side effects are rarely reported [9, 10, 13, 14, 16, 23]. Research on the use of VR to improve social functioning in people with SUD is scarce.

This RCT is part of a larger project called «Virtual Reality (VR) as a facilitator for participation in society among persons with mental health/substance use disorders». The overall project comprises three work packages: explorative studies, a development study, and this evaluation study (RCT). Based on the exploratory studies in work package 1 [24, 25], relevant VR learning content was developed in work package 2, resulting in three learning modules addressing social cognition, social communication skills, and social competence.

This paper describes the study protocol for ROPVR, an RCT that aims to determine the effectiveness of VR social functioning training in improving functionality in people with SUD receiving in-patient SUD treatment.

Objectives

The primary objective (O1) is to determine the effectiveness of tailored VR social functioning training as an adjunct to in-patient SUD treatment compared to in-patient SUD treatment alone (treatment as usual [TAU]) in improving functionality from before (T0) to 6 months post-intervention (T2). We are interested in group*time interaction effects, indicating between-group differences in change over time. As primary hypothesis (H1), we expect a significantly higher increase in the level of functioning in the intervention compared to the control group from before (T0) to 6 months follow-up (T2), measured by World Health Organization Disability Assessment Scale (WHODAS) 2.0 global score (primary outcome).

As secondary hypothesis (H2), we expect a significantly higher increase in the intervention compared to the control group from baseline (T0) to 6 months (T2) in the WHODAS domains “getting along” (H2a) and “social participation” (H2b). As further secondary outcomes, we exploratorily examine time*group interaction effects in WHODAS 2.0 domain scores cognition (understanding and communicating), mobility (moving and getting around), self-care (hygiene, dressing, eating and staying alone), and life activities (domestic responsibilities, leisure, work and school), as well as further additional aspects of social functioning, such as social inclusion (Mini-SCOPE), resilience (CD-RISK 10), practical and social functioning (PSF), and quality of life (EQ-5D).

The secondary objective (O2) is to evaluate differences in the short time changes in functioning from before (T0) to 6 weeks (T1) in the experimental compared to the control group. The primary and secondary outcomes correspond to the outcomes for the primary objective. As hypothesis, we expect a significantly higher short time increase in the intervention compared to the control group from baseline (T0) to 6 weeks (T1) regarding functionality (WHODAS 2.0 global score; H3), as well as in the WHODAS domain scores “getting along” (H4a) and “social participation” (H4b). Exploratorily, we also examine social inclusion (Mini-SCOPE), resilience (CD-RISK 10), PSF, quality of life (EQ-5D), and the rest of the WHODAS domains.

The third objective (O3) is to explore the cost-effectiveness of SUD treatment plus VR training compared to SUD treatment alone using WHODAS 2.0 global score and subscale scores, the Treatment Inventory of Cost in Patients with mental disorders (TIC-P), and quality of life questionnaire (EQ-5D) before (T0) and at 6 months (T2) and 12 months (T3).

Additionally, as explorative question, we aim to explore any harms or side effects (cybersickness or other harms) using VR training in people with SUD.

Trial design

The study is designed as a two-arm, statistician-blinded, pragmatic, multi-cite, superiority RCT. Eligible participants will be randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group using 1:1 allocation. The control group receives standard in-patient SUD treatment (TAU), while the intervention group receives VR training (8 sessions) as an adjunct to standard in-patient SUD treatment. Participation in this study will not affect the participant’s ordinary SUD treatment. The study has a within-between-subject (mixed) design with the group assignment as between-subject and time points of assessment as within-subject variable. Time points of assessments for O1 are before randomization (T0) and at 6 months (T2) (H1, H2a, and H2b), for O2 before randomization (T0) and at 6 weeks (T1) (H3, H4a, and H4b), and for O3 before randomization (T0), at 6 months (T2), and at 12 months (T3).

This protocol follows the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines [26].

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting

Participants in the present study will be recruited from short- and long-term specialist-level SUD treatment units in South-East Norway. In Norway, SUD treatment is provided through public health care services, or private institutions with an agreement with the regional health authorities. Treatment is generally subsidized and free of charge for the patient.

Who will take informed consent

Patients will first be given information about the study in ordinary joint meetings in each clinic. If interested, clinicians will refer patients to the researchers who will assess eligibility and take informed consent. Eligible patients that agree to participate will go through an informed consent procedure, which involves personal meetings with the researchers, and being provided with both verbal and written information regarding the intervention, potential side effects and risks, and study design. The patient information sheet/consent form is in plain language to ensure that patients understand what participation entails and that participation is voluntary.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data

There are no additional consent provisions as participant data will only be used for the purpose of the ROPVR trial.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators

The standard SUD treatment plus VR intervention will be compared with standard SUD treatment (TAU). In Norway, SUD treatment clinics offer patient-centered, interdisciplinary, holistic treatment focusing on physical, mental, and social problems. This may include (but are not restricted to) psychotherapy, individual and/or group sessions, medication, physical activities, and family or social network involvement. The main aim of this study is to assess whether VR training as an adjunct to standard SUD treatment offers any additional benefits. With TAU as a comparator condition, we ensure that all participants receive some form of active treatment. Other benefits include the possibility of increased retention in the TAU group, and higher generalizability of study results.

Intervention description

Participants randomized to the intervention arm will attend VR sessions twice a week for 4–6 weeks, i.e., eight sessions in total. They will be placed into eight different virtual environments with increasing complexity, where they will be confronted with normal everyday social situations in city and work environments. The order of the eight sessions is fixed.

The module learning content, gained from previous explorative studies among the target group [25, 27], is displayed in Table 1. To enhance learning, VR content is organized into three sequential learning modules comprising distinct micro-learning units. This allows the user to acquire and practice skills individually and subsequently integrate these individual skills into social behaviors that are adapted to the specific context.

Modules and learning content

In the first VR session, participants will receive instructions on how to use the head-mounted display and navigating the scenarios. In three scenarios (1A, 1B, and 3A), participants are only observers, while in the remaining they can choose between several responses. These response options will be displayed on the screen, and participants can select their preferred response by pointing their finger to the chosen response alternative. Patients will be encouraged to verbalize their selected response. In module 3, scenarios 3A and 3B are completed in the same session (session eight).

VR sessions last up to 45 min. Participants can experience the scenario several times during the session. Participants wear the head-mounted display for 5–10 min, and the rest of the session is used to reflect on the content together with the VR therapist. In addition to VR, a semi-structured topic guide for the VR therapists has been developed, with questions related to the participants’ interpretation and understanding of the scenarios, including interpretation of verbal and non-verbal communication and cues, and in what ways the scenarios are relevant for their current situation. The aim of this conversation is to reinforce the learning objectives. As this conversation is framed around the participant’s life and experiences, some individual tailoring is necessary. No documentation is produced from the VR sessions, other than attendance.

The intervention will be delivered by one of three trained VR therapists across all treatment units (the main researcher or one of the two study nurses). The three VR therapists received training in how to use the VR equipment before recruitment started. The main researcher has extensive clinical experience (< 25 years) from the fields of mental health and addiction treatment. The three VR therapists regularly reflect together on their use of and experience with the topic guide.

We will use an MSI Raider GE78HX with a GeForce RTX4080 12 GB GPU, running Windows 11, Unreal Engine 5.4, and the Meta Quest Link app, with a Meta Quest Pro as a PCVR headset connected via a Meta Quest Link cable. The setup works 100% locally, and no Wi-Fi or internet connection is used on the head-mounted display or PC. The VR program itself does not store any data and will discard all that has happened inside the program when it is restarted.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions

The participants can withdraw or discontinue the trial at any time during the study without further explanation. Any unexpected or adverse effects influenced or not influenced by the study content will be monitored and reported according to standard operating procedures.

If eligibility criteria are not fulfilled any more, or if participants receiving the intervention experience severe side effects, e.g., severe persistent cybersickness, or not tolerable fears concerning VR glasses or VR content, discontinuing allocated interventions will be discussed with the participant and research team. Participants discontinuing the intervention continue with TAU. As long as participants who discontinue do not withdraw from the study, they will be followed up for outcomes according to their allocation.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions

The modules have been co-designed with a user representative and pre-tested by end-users (persons in recovery from MHD/SUD) to ensure that the scenarios are relevant. Participants will be reminded about appointments by clinical staff at the units, and by text message, if needed. Attendance is recorded. Changes in scheduled appointments are to be expected, as the practical realities of long-term in-patient treatment can be unpredictable. Some flexibility is therefore needed when VR sessions are scheduled. If the participants drop out or miss appointments, they will be contacted by either clinical staff in person, or by text message or phone to make new appointments. Participants will be considered to have completed the intervention if they attend a minimum of six out of the eight scheduled VR sessions. There will be regular staff meetings in each clinic.

To reduce drop-out, participants in the control group are offered one VR-session with VR 360° nature videos in head-mounted display, which do not require interacting or social functioning training. All participants will be provided with a gift card worth 250 NOK after assessment at T2.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial

Participants in the control group cannot receive the intervention (VR training) for the duration of the trial. There are no other restrictions. During the trial, all participants will receive ordinary TAU in an in-patient treatment setting.

Provisions for post-trial care

All participants continue with TAU post-trial.

Outcomes

Outcomes will be assessed longitudinally at four assessment points (within-subjects).

Baseline assessment

Baseline assessments include patient demographics and characteristics, diagnostic information from medical journals, and cognitive status. Since cognitive impairment may affect learning in VR training, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCa) will be used to assess cognitive status at baseline (T0). MoCa is validated as a sensitive tool for detection of mild cognitive impairment and is used both in clinical practice and research [28]. MoCa score will be used descriptively, and associations with the global WHODAS score at 6 months follow-up score will be explored.

Primary outcome measure for O1 and O2

The primary outcome measure for the primary (and secondary) objective (O1 and O2) is functionality measured with the 36-item WHODAS 2.0 global score [29]. WHODAS 2.0 measures health and disability in adults and captures the level of functioning in six domains: cognition, mobility, self-care, getting along, life activities, and participation. Higher WHODAS 2.0 global score indicates more problems. WHODAS covers all diseases, including physical, mental, and substance use disorders. WHODAS is suitable for measuring the clinical effectiveness of interventions, has excellent psychometric properties, and is easy to use [29]. For O1 and O2, the WHODAS global score will be assessed at T0, T1, and T2. For the third objective (O3), WHODAS will also be measured at T3.

Secondary outcome measures for O1 and O2

Secondary outcome measures for the primary (and secondary) objective (O1 and O2) include WHODAS domains scores “getting along” and “social participation” [29]. They are assessed at T0 (O1 and O2), T1 (O2), and T2 (O1).

Exploratory outcomes for O1 and O2

Exploratory outcome measures for O1 and O3 include WHODAS domain scores “cognition,” “mobility,” “self-care,” and “life activities” [29]; social participation measured by Mini-SCOPE [30, 31]; practical and social functioning (PSF) [32]; resilience measured by the Connor-David Resilience Scale 10 (CD-RISK 10) [33, 34]; and quality of life (EQ-5D) [35]. They are assessed at T0 (O1 and O2), T1 (O2), and T2 (O1). For the third objective (O3), EQ-5D will also be measured at T3.

Outcome measure for O3

For the third objective (O3), the TIC-P [36], WHODAS 2.0 ([29]; see also primary outcome for O1 and O2), and EQ-5D ([35]; see also exploratory outcomes for O1 and O2) will be used as outcomes in the health economic evaluation and will be assessed at T0, T2, and T3.

Further exploratory outcomes

After each VR session, cybersickness symptoms scores [37] and any other self-reported side effects of the VR intervention will be explored.

Sample size

Sample size estimation is based on the methods from a study from the mental health field, where WHODAS is used as primary outcome [38], assuming the same standard deviation (SD) of 16, power of 80%, and significance level of 5%. To estimate variance components for patients and clinics, intraclass correlations (ICC) estimates of 0.50 for the measurements within patients and 0.05 for the measurements within clinics with 5 participating clinics were used. Simulations computed using the R environment and the lme4 and simr library (R Core Team, 2021) (N = 1000) resulted in a sample size of 41 patients per arm and a total of 82 patients is needed to observe time*group interaction effect with no difference between the intervention and the control group before the intervention and an absolute difference of 10 points between the intervention and the control group at 6-month follow-up (i.e., WHODAS global 2.0 scores of 50 in the control group vs. 40 in the intervention group). Since attrition up to 20% should be expected [14], we aim to recruit 51 patients in each arm, i.e., 102 participants in total. The assumed 10-point difference corresponds to a standardized effect size of d = 0.63 based on the SD of 16. To date, no universally accepted minimally clinically important difference exists for WHODAS 2.0 (36-item). Because there are no directly comparable studies, our power analysis requiring a power of 80% used the study by Asher et al. [38] and leads to a sample size that detects the assumed medium effect size. The probability of detecting a smaller effect size, even if it is clinically significant, is therefore lower. However, in the case of a smaller effect size, we will exploratory apply Bayesian statistics to estimate whether the result points to either no effect or to a potential effect of the intervention.

Recruitment

The proportion of women with SUD varies according to specific substances and regions, but generally the male–female ratio is 2:1 [39]. Although both men and women with SUD have shown interest and participated in pre-tests, targeted recruitment of women may be needed in the RCT to get a representative study sample.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation

Participants will be randomly assigned to either the control or VR intervention group with a 1:1 allocation as per a computer-generated randomization schedule.

Concealment mechanism

Knowledge of the next assignment can introduce bias due to selective enrolment of participants [40]. Therefore, random permuted blocks of varying length (4 or 6) will be used to avoid prediction of next allocation [41]. To conceal allocation from participants and those enrolling participants in the study, administrative staff at Innlandet Hospital Trust will handle the randomization list.

Implementation

Administrative staff, in cooperation with a statistician, who is not involved in the enrolment or assignment of participants will generate the allocation sequence. To ensure allocation concealment, researchers will contact administrative staff at Innlandet Hospital Trust when baseline assessments are done and be informed about assignment to interventions. Subsequently, the researchers will inform the patient about group allocation.

Assignment of intervention: blinding

Who will be blinded

This study is statistician-blinded. Participants, the main researcher, and the study nurses will be partially blinded, because baseline assessments will be done before allocation.

Procedure for unblinding if needed

Not applicable, since group allocation is known after baseline assessments.

Data collection, management, and confidentiality

The assessments and data management will be carried out by the main researcher/study nurse(s). Privacy, confidentiality, and information security are important aspects of research ethics [42]. Data will be stored according to Innlandet Hospital Trust’s procedures in secure locations on the Innlandet Hospital Trust’s research server, with restricted access. Any paper-based data will contain study ID only and will be stored in locked archives at Innlandet Hospital Trust, with restricted access.

Plans for promoting participant retention and complete follow-up

There is a risk of lost-to-follow-up at T2/T3 for patients who have completed in-patient treatment. Follow-up at T2 and T3 will be done as telephone interviews if in-patient treatment is completed. In-patient treatment is usually followed by out-patient treatment. Therefore, all participants will be asked to provide their contact details as well as contact details for one or several individuals (in municipal health or social service and/or next of kin) we can contact to make appointments after completion or discharge from in-patient treatment.

Plans for collection, laboratory evaluation, and storage of biological specimen

Not applicable, as biological specimens are not collected.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes

To assess the effectiveness of the VR intervention, analysis will be intention-to-treat (ITT). The ITT approach ensures that all participants are analyzed according to the allocated treatment to which they were originally randomized, regardless of whether they completed the intervention as per protocol or not [43].

All analysis for hypothesis testing will be two‐sided, and significance is set at p < 0.05. For hypothesis with two outcome measures (H2a/H2b and H3a/H3b), the alpha-level will be corrected to p < 0.025. Descriptive statistics will be used to describe patient characteristics and outcome variables. Frequencies of categorical variables will be compared using a Pearson’s chi-square test or a Fisher’s exact test. To compare the means of continuous variables, we will use Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test (normally or non-normally distributed variables, respectively).

To evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention (O1 and O2), linear mixed models will be used to test for significantly stronger increases in outcome variables over time (T0 to T1:H3, H4a/b; T0 to T2: H1, H2a/b) in the intervention compared to the control group (time*group interaction effects), while controlling for different treatment units (included as additional level within a multilevel approach).

Results will be reported as minimally adjusted mean differences and effect sizes defined as standardized mean differences with 95% confidence interval (CI). Minimally adjusted models will include patients and clinics as random effects, and group and their interaction as fixed effects. For sensitivity analysis, we will conduct fully adjusted models including variables associated with drop-out (p < 0.10) and demographic and clinical variables seemingly unbalanced between groups at baseline.

For the health economic evaluation (O3), seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) models will be fitted to each dataset to obtain adjusted estimates of total costs and quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) for each treatment arm. These SUR models will adjust the total costs and QALYs for baseline characteristics, such as age and gender, and for baseline imbalances in costs and health-related quality of life.

To describe potential side effects in the intervention group, descriptive statistics will be used.

Interim analysis

Interim analysis will not be performed due to resource constraints.

Methods for additional analyses

The primary analyses will be followed up with sensitivity analysis on completers (per-protocol analyses) for the main outcome variables. Participants will be included in the per protocol analysis if they attended at a minimum of six VR sessions. The same statistical methods as for the ITT analyses will be applied. Furthermore, we will compare dropouts between groups, gender, and clinics, and will analyze whether baseline variables could predict dropout by mixed model regression analysis. Additionally, to analyze whether sociodemographic or clinical variables (age, sex, MoCa, drug use characteristics) mediate changes in WHODAS, mixed model regression analysis will be conducted.

Finally, we will calculate Bayes factors (BF01) to quantify support of the null hypotheses (H0) for nonsignificant results including gender and clinic as covariates. Bayesian analysis will be conducted in JASP (Version 0.16.4; JASP Team, 2022). Resulting Bayes factors will be interpreted according to the categories of Jeffreys (1961) [44].

Statistical methods to handle missing data

For ITT analysis, missing data will be addressed using appropriate statistical methods, such as multiple imputations if possible or last observation carried forward (LOCF) [41]. Sensitivity analyses for handling missing data will be provided.

To address missing data on resource use and quality of life scores in the health evaluation, multivariate imputation by chained equations will be implemented. Multiple imputation will be combined with nonparametric bootstrapping to account for skewness, non-normality, and the correlation between costs and QALYs. Single missing values in the other questionnaires will be replaced by mean value of the scale (for up to 2 missing values). Otherwise, LOCF will be used.

Data monitoring committee

The coordination and execution of the monitoring will be carried out by a monitor appointed by Innlandet Hospital Trust. The monitor will verify adherence to the study protocol. Adverse events reporting and harms

The study is approved by relevant authorities. People who use drugs are often considered a vulnerable group. Although there might be situations where their ability to give informed consent is affected (e.g., intoxications), people who use drugs are generally able to give informed consent. Individuals belonging to vulnerable groups not only face an elevated risk of exploitation, but they also have a disproportionately higher likelihood of missing out on the benefits associated with research participation [45]. Therefore, vulnerable groups should be included in research as long as there is no substantial reason to exclude them.

Any side effects of the VR intervention or harm is continuously monitored and reported according to standard operating procedures. Cybersickness due to VR exposure is one potential side effect. Typical symptoms, such as nausea, oculomotor discomfort, and disorientation, may interfere or limit participants’ motivation and ability to engage in VR training [37, 46, 47]. There is a need for improved detection and reporting of VR-related harm or adverse effects that may lead to drop-out or clinical worsening [48].

To reduce the possibility of cybersickness, the time spent in the head-mounted display is short (< 10 min), and participants may sit during sessions. Participants will be monitored by the VR therapist for any reactions or signs of distress during sessions. If we discover or the participant reports symptoms of cybersickness, he or she will be asked to determine whether VR training should be discontinued. See also “Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions” section.

For documentation of possible side effects and reporting, we will measure cybersickness using Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ) [49] after each VR session. In addition to the SSQ, participants will be asked after each VR session about symptom exacerbation and/or increased drug craving that might need follow-up from clinicians from the units after the VR session. If needed, follow-up is arranged.

In people with SUD, mental disorders, suicide, overdose and physical diseases such as hepatitis C, chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder, or cardiovascular diseases occur more frequently than in the general population. Any adverse event, whether related to the VR training or not (e.g., emotional arousal, drug craving or relapse to substance use, drop-out from in-patient treatment, deterioration of mental disorders, or reactivation of traumatic memories) will be reported as soon as possible by the participating units to the project leader.

A serious adverse event is an event that results in death, is life-threatening, or requires inpatient or prolonged hospitalization [26]. Serious adverse events in this patient group may include fatal or non-fatal overdose, suicide or suicide attempts, death by other causes, or hospitalization (other than long-term SUD treatment) due to physical or mental disorders or substance use. Any serious adverse events will be reported immediately according to the trial protocol and Innlandet Hospital’s research guidelines. Adverse and serious adverse events will be recorded. The relationship between any adverse events and the trial procedures will be assessed by the research team in cooperation with clinicians from the participating units.

Plans for communicating important protocol amendments to relevant parties

Major protocol amendments or changes will be sent as soon as possible to the regional ethics committee, Data Protection Officials, and participating treatment units. Major changes after publication of the protocol will be corrected according to the journal’s guidelines. All study sites will be regularly updated via e-mail and verbally by the project leader and/or the researchers.

Dissemination plans

The protocol and results of the trial will be published. The study will be reported following the CONSORT guideline [50]. Results will also be presented for the participating clinics and at national and international conferences.

Discussion

A wide range of treatment interventions and tailored approaches for SUD need to be developed [51]. Evidence suggests that VR training can enhance patients’ social skills and social functioning and thereby improve relations and promote community participation in people with SUD. VR training may offer a safe environment for patients to practice and learn in a standardized manner. However, research gaps exist. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that aims to explore the use of VR in improving social functioning and social participation in people with SUD. Thus the results of this trial can provide valuable new insights for clinicians and health authorities in Norway and abroad.

The main strength of this study is the participatory design, where a representative of an end-user organization (person with lived experience) has been involved in research and the development of VR content, in line with recommendations for VR development [52, 53].

Trial status

Recruitment started on April 1, 2025, and ends when target sample is reached, or on December 31, 2026, at the latest. This is protocol version 2. Version 1 (dated October 28, 2024) was sent to the Research Ethic Committee in October 2024. In version 2, the description of outcomes and statistical analysis has been revised.
