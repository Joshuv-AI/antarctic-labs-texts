Expired dapagliflozin as a promising corrosion inhibitor for copper in 1.0 M nitric acid: experimental and computational validation
Authors: Metwally Abdallah, Nizar El Guesmi, Arej S Al-Gorair, Badreah A Al Jahdaly, Hanaa Hawsawi, Salih S Al-Juaid, Mohamed Sobhi, Huda Fish, Salah Abd El Wanees, Abdelkader Zarrouk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe efficacy of expired dapagliflozin (DAP) as a sustainable and cost-effective corrosion inhibitor for copper (Cu) in 1.0 M HNO₃ was investigated using a combination of experimental and theoretical approaches. Chemical and electrochemical methods were applied to assess the anticorrosion efficacy over a range of concentrations and temperatures. The results demonstrated that the anticorrosion efficiency of expired DAP is significant and increases with increasing inhibitor concentration but decreases as the temperature rises. This indicates that the inhibition process is primarily governed by the physical adsorption of expired DAP molecules onto the Cu surface. However, the observed change in βₐ suggests that the adsorption is not purely physical in nature but rather involves a mixed physical and chemical adsorption mechanism. Potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) results indicate that expired DAP functions as a mixed-type inhibitor, effectively suppressing both anodic metal dissolution and cathodic reduction reactions. Moreover, a pronounced positive shift in the pitting potential (Eₚiₜₜ) was observed, indicating a significant enhancement in resistance to pitting corrosion. The thermodynamic parameters associated with both activation and adsorption processes were evaluated and analyzed, offering deeper insight into the corrosion inhibition mechanism. The inhibition effect of expired DAP is attributed to the formation of a stable complex between DAP molecules and Cu²⁺ ions adsorbed on the metal surface. Conductometric titration indicates a 1:1 stoichiometric ratio for the Cu²⁺–DAP complex. The adsorption of this complex reduces the corrosion rate and enhances inhibition efficiency. Theoretical calculations further confirm that DAP exhibits a strong tendency to absorb onto the Cu surface, reflecting its remarkable inhibitory potential. Good agreement between the theoretical predictions and the experimental results highlights the consistency of the applied approaches and strengthens confidence in the reported results.Keywords: Copper, Dapagliflozin, Corrosion inhibitor, Adsorption, PittingSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

There are numerous industrial applications for Cu and its alloys, such as heat exchangers, electrical components, desalination plants, and chemical processing machinery, due to their remarkable electrical conductivity and thermal properties1–3. However, Cu is quite vulnerable to corrosion in acidic environments, particularly in industrial procedures like pickling, scaling, and acid scrubbing. Acidic media, such as nitric acid, promote the dissolution of Cu, leading to material deterioration, monetary losses, and possible risks to safety4,5. One of the most feasible and effective strategies to minimize Cu corrosion in acidic circumstances is to apply a corrosion inhibitor.

Most corrosion inhibitors used to mitigate the corrosion of Cu in acidic solutions are organic compounds, which have attracted considerable interest due to their high inhibition efficiency, cost, and ease of use6–16. These compounds usually have heteroatoms (such as N, O, and S), along with aromatic rings and π-electron systems that aid in their adsorption onto the Cu surface. This adsorption leads to the formation of a protective film that effectively blocks active corrosion sites, thereby reducing both the anodic dissolution of Cu and the cathodic hydrogen evolution reactions. This adsorption produces a protective covering that effectively inhibits active corrosion sites, reducing both the anodic dissolution of Cu and the cathodic hydrogen evolution processes17–19.

Recent studies have demonstrated that drug compounds represent a promising class of corrosion inhibitors for metals and alloys in acidic environments20–25. These compounds possess complicated molecular structures enriched with heteroatoms and various functional groups, which act as effective adsorption centers on the metal surface. This adsorption facilitates the formation of a protective barrier that effectively reduces corrosion processes. Furthermore, compared to traditional synthetic organic inhibitors, many drug molecules are non-toxic, biodegradable, cost-effective, and environmentally benign, making them appealing options for corrosion inhibition.

Numerous investigations have demonstrated that pharmaceuticals, including anticancer, antifungal, antibiotic, and anti-inflammatory medications, considerably minimize copper corrosion in acidic environments26–29. The ability of these drugs to adsorb onto the copper surface through chemical (coordinate bonding) and/or physical (electrostatic) interactions usually explains their inhibitory effects. In addition to π-electron systems in aromatic rings, heteroatoms boost their adsorption propensity and aid in the construction of a barrier layer that suppresses active corrosion sites. Furthermore, the adsorption behavior of these compounds is often influenced by their molecular structure, functional groups, and solution factors, including temperature and pH27,28.

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the potential use of expired dapagliflozin (DAP) as a cost-effective, safe, and environmentally friendly corrosion inhibitor for copper in 1.0 M HNO₃ solution. Although this drug is no longer suitable for therapeutic use after expiration, it may be repurposed for alternative applications such as corrosion inhibition. This approach offers an economically viable solution while also addressing environmental concerns by reducing the costs and potential impacts associated with the disposal of expired pharmaceuticals.

The inhibition performance of DAP was evaluated using weight loss measurements in combination with three electrochemical techniques. To further elucidate the anticorrosion mechanism, activation parameters and adsorption characteristics were determined and systematically analyzed. In addition, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to investigate the inhibitor’s electronic structure, adsorption behavior, and protective properties. This integrated experimental and theoretical approach provides a comprehensive understanding of the interactions between DAP species and the Cu surface, offering valuable insights for the development of efficient and sustainable corrosion inhibition strategies.

Experimental methods

Materials

The metal under inquiry was Cu (Merck, purity 99.93%), with the elemental composition (%) of 0.029, Fe, 0.023 Pb, 0.011 Ni, 0.005 Si, and the balance of quantity Cu.

Measurements

Weight loss (WL) measurements

For the WL technique, Cu sheets of 1.0 × 2.0 × 0.6 cm were utilized. The Cu sheets were polished using sequential emery papers of different grades (300 to 1500) prior to any experiments. Before being submerged in the testing medium, they were cleaned with double-distilled water, cleansed with acetone, and dried. The main corrosive medium (blank) in every study was a 1.0 M HNO3 solution prepared with double-distilled water. The Cu sheets were precisely weighed and then placed in a 100 mL beaker filled with 1.0 M HNO3, with and without the inclusion of various amounts of the expired DAP. The samples were thoroughly cleaned, dried, and carefully weighed again after being submerged for 12 h. As stated earlier, the same procedure was performed at certain temperatures between 303 and 333 K30,31.

Electrochemical measurements

A copper (Cu) rod with an exposed surface area of 0.38 cm² was used as the working electrode in three electrochemical techniques: potentiodynamic polarization (PDP), potentiodynamic anodic polarization (PDAP), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). Before each experiment, the Cu rods were prepared according to the WL procedure.

A platinum (Pt) wire and a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) were used as the auxiliary (counter) and reference electrodes, respectively, while the Cu electrode served as the working electrode (WE) in a conventional three-electrode electrochemical cell. All electrochemical measurements were conducted at 25 ± 1 °C. Potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) and potentiodynamic anodic polarization (PDAP) measurements were performed using a PS remote potentiostat controlled by PS6 software, with scan rates of 2 mV s⁻¹ and 0.1 mV s⁻¹, respectively. The corrosion parameters, including corrosion potential (Ecorr), corrosion current density (icorr), and anodic (βₐ) and cathodic (β) Tafel slopes, were obtained from the PDP curves by extrapolating the linear portions of the anodic and cathodic branches to their intersection point.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were carried out using an AutoLab 30 potentiostat (Metrohm) at open circuit potential, applying a sinusoidal AC perturbation of 5 mV over a frequency range from 100 kHz to 10 mHz. A thermostatically controlled circulating bath was used to maintain the temperature at 25 ± 1 °C throughout the experiments. Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS program.

Conductometric titrations were conducted as previously mentioned32,33. They were performed using a calibrated conductivity meter (YSI model 32 conductance meter) with a cell constant of 1.6. A DAP solution (5.0 × 10− 4 M, 50 ml) was titrated with a Cu²⁺ ions solution (2.0 × 10− 3 M), prepared by dilution of a freshly made stock solution (1.0 × 10− 2 M) from a copper (II) salt in deionized water.

The Cu²⁺ ions solution was added incrementally (0.10–0.20 mL), and the conductivity was measured after each addition. A blank titration of Cu²⁺ ions into the solvent (in the absence of DAP) was conducted under identical conditions to correct for dilution and background conductivity effects.

Inhibitor

Expired dapagliflozin (DAP) was utilized as a corrosion inhibitor in this study. The compound was manufactured by AstraZeneca (Egypt). Its molecular weight is 503.0 g mol⁻¹, and its chemical formula is C₂₄H₃₅ClO₉. The chemical structure of dapagliflozin is presented in Scheme 1. Stock solutions of expired DAP were prepared using double-distilled water, and the inhibitor was tested over a concentration range of 100–300 mg L⁻¹.

Computational details

Geometry optimizations and electronic structure calculations of Dapagliflozin (DAP) were carried out within the framework of density functional theory using the Gaussian 09 software. The hybrid B3LYP functional was consistently applied, as it has demonstrated strong performance in the characterization of organic corrosion inhibitors35,36. A 6-311G(d, p) basis set, incorporating polarization functions on all atoms, was used in all computations. This basis set is well-suited for capturing the electronic structure of DAP, including the contributions of its heteroatom-containing moieties. All geometries were fully optimized without symmetry restrictions, enabling the DAP molecule to relax to its most stable configuration. The characterization of the stationary points was achieved through vibrational frequency calculations; the lack of imaginary frequencies confirmed that the optimized structures represent a true minimum on the potential energy surface. The calculations were performed in the gas phase. While implicit solvation models such as PCM (water) may be employed, the gas-phase framework remains widely used in corrosion inhibition studies for determining global reactivity descriptors and frontier molecular orbital energies, thereby enabling meaningful comparison with previously published results on analogous compounds.

Thereafter, the frontier molecular orbital energies (EHOMO and ELUMO) were used to evaluate a series of global reactivity descriptors through their fundamental relationships. The ionization potential (I) and electron affinity (A) were approximated as −EHOMO and −ELUMO, respectively, while the energy gap (ΔE) was calculated as ELUMO − EHOMO. Elevated temperatures’ effects on anticorrosion performance

The impact of elevated temperature on the corrosion behavior of Cu in uninhibited 1.0 M HNO3 and in the occurrence of varying concentrations of expired DAP was examined over the temperature range of 293 K and 323 K. The obtained values of Rcorr, θ, and % AE(WL) are provided in Table 3. Increasing the temperature leads to an increase in Rcorr, while θ and % AE(WL) decrease. This trend clarifies that higher temperatures promote the desorption of DAP molecules from the Cu surface, thereby reducing the inhibition efficiency. Such behavior indicates that the inhibition process is predominantly governed by the physical adsorption of expired DAP molecules onto the Cu surface. However, the observed variation in βₐ suggests that the adsorption is not purely physical in nature but rather involves a mixed adsorption mechanism. Accordingly, the inhibitory mechanism can be described as predominantly physisorption, with a contribution from chemisorption, in agreement with both thermodynamic and electrochemical results.

Considering these findings, the Ea* values rise progressively with the rising concentration of expired DAP and are higher than in a free 1.0 M HNO₃ solution, suggesting that the corrosion reaction requires a greater energy barrier to proceed and the Cu corrosion is governed by activation-controlled processes. This verifies that the utilized DAP molecules adsorb on the Cu surface. This adsorption prevents the Cu surface from directly interacting with the aggressive nitric acid by creating an adherent coating that covers the active corrosion sites. As a result, the corrosion reaction becomes energetically less favorable, which is reflected in the higher activation energy values. Additionally, the adsorption of expired DAP increases the thickness of the electrical double layer at the Cu/solution interface, creating an adhesive barrier that hinders mass and charge transfer. Consequently, the transit of corrosive species and electron transfer are inhibited, indicating that expired DAP has an efficient inhibitory effect through surface adsorption and barrier film development62,63.

Figure 9 depicts the plots of log Rcorr/T versus 1/T for the dissolution of Cu in 1.0 M HNO₃ with and without certain concentrations of expired DAP. A linear relationship was obtained, from which the thermodynamic parameters ΔH* and ΔS* were computed. The estimated value of ΔH* for Cu corrosion in 1.0 M HNO3 was 17.23 kJ mol− 1. When expired DAP was present, ΔH* increased to 24.89, 28.72, 30.63, 34.45, and 39.21 kJ mol⁻¹ at expired DAP concentrations of 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 mg L⁻¹, respectively. The positive values of ΔH* argue that the corrosion of Cu in 1.0 M HNO₃ is an endothermic process, implying that Cu dissolution is thermodynamically less favorable. Moreover, the progressive increase in ΔH* with increasing concentrations of expired DAP suggests that the drug’s presence hinders the corrosion process, thereby enhancing the protection of Cu corrosion.

The calculated values of ΔS* for Cu corrosion in 1.0 M HNO₃ were − 377.11 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. In the presence of expired dapagliflozin, ΔS* became more negative, with values of − 410.88, − 432.22, − 465.24, − 491.34, and − 518.81 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ at inhibitor concentrations of 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 mg L⁻¹, respectively. The negative sign of ΔS* indicates a decrease in randomness, suggesting that the freedom of the DAP molecules in the inhibited system is significantly reduced due to their adsorption onto the Cu surface in the presence of expired DAP. This decrease in entropy reflects the formation of a more ordered system, as inhibitor molecules become confined at the Cu/solution interface rather than freely dispersed in the bulk solution. Such behavior is characteristic of adsorption-controlled corrosion inhibition processes, where the formation of an adsorbed layer promotes an ordered arrangement of DAP molecules on the metal surface.

Furthermore, the increasingly negative ΔS* values indicate that the activated complex formed during the rate-determining step is more ordered than the initial reactants. This ordering arises not only from an association mechanism but also from (i) adsorption of DAP molecules forming a compact surface film, (ii) displacement of water molecules from the Cu/solution interface, and (iii) possible surface complexation between DAP and Cu atoms. These combined effects significantly restrict molecular mobility, leading to the observed reduction in entropy.

Overall, the entropy results provide strong evidence that expired DAP acts as an efficient corrosion inhibitor by facilitating the formation of a stable adsorbed layer on the Cu surface. Consistent with previous reports, this protective layer reduces the corrosion rate by limiting molecular mobility, increasing system order, and shifting the reaction pathway toward a more ordered activated complex63.

Anticorrosion mechanism

The anticorrosion efficacy of Cu surface in 1.0 M HNO3 solution in the presence of expired DAP molecules was evaluated using various electrochemical and gravimetric techniques. All corrosion parameters obtained from these techniques confirm the anticorrosion effect of DAP. The key factors governing anti-corrosive efficacy include inhibitor concentration, temperature, adsorption strength on the metal surface, molecular structure, and the nature of the corrosive medium.

The anticorrosion mechanism can be explained through the adsorption of expired DAP at the Cu/solution interface. The extent of adsorption is influenced by charge density, molecular size of the inhibitor, the number and nature of active binding sites on the Cu surface, the strength of interaction between the drug and the metal surface, the availability of electron density at adsorption centers, and the possible formation of a metal–inhibitor complex70.

Expired DAP molecules preferentially displace water molecules and adsorb onto the active sites of the Cu surface. Electron-donating functional groups such as –OH and –CH₃, along with π-electron systems and heteroatoms like oxygen, facilitate donor–acceptor interactions with vacant orbitals of Cu. This interaction results in strong adsorption and the formation of a compact, adherent protective layer that effectively isolates the Cu surface from the aggressive nitric acid medium. Consequently, the inhibition efficiency increases with increasing concentration of expired DAP.

It is important to note that the expired dapagliflozin (DAP) used in this study may not retain a fully identical chemical composition to that of the fresh drug due to possible degradation processes such as hydrolysis, oxidation, or structural rearrangement during storage beyond the expiration date. In the absence of detailed analytical characterization (e.g., HPLC, LC–MS, NMR, or FTIR), the expired material is therefore considered as a mixture of the parent compound and its potential degradation products, which are also likely to retain similar functional moieties. From a corrosion inhibition perspective, the adsorption process is primarily governed by electron-rich centers such as oxygen atoms and π-electrons, enabling donor–acceptor interactions with the copper surface. Accordingly, the observed inhibition efficiency is attributed to the collective adsorption behavior of these species rather than assuming the intact molecular structure of dapagliflozin alone.

The significant anticorrosion efficacy of expired DAP can be attributed to the possible complex formation between Cu2+ ions and expired DAP in aqueous solution. A conductometric study was performed to clarify the mechanism of complex formation71. This technique is particularly sensitive to changes in ionic concentration and mobility arising from coordination processes72.

Figure 12 displays the variation in specific conductivity, corrected for dilution, upon incremental additions of 1 × 10⁻³ M expired DAP to a Cu²⁺ solution. The observed change in slope at the equivalence point confirms the formation of a stable 1:1 Cu (II)–ligand complex.

In the current system, the evolution of conductivity is dominated by coordination interactions between Cu²⁺ ions and expired DAP molecules. As the ligand is introduced, Cu²⁺ ions interact with oxygen-containing functional groups within the DAP structure, particularly hydroxyl groups associated with the glycosidic and alicyclic moieties73. This coordination process is accompanied by partial proton release from the hydroxyl groups, leading to the generation of additional H⁺ ions in solution.

Before the equivalence point, a steady increase in conductivity is observed. This behavior reflects the gradual formation of Cu²⁺–DAP coordination species, together with the contribution of liberated protons to charge transport. At the equivalent point, a distinct change in slope occurs, indicating completion of the complexation process. Beyond this point, conductivity rises sharply due to the accumulation of excess, non-coordinated Cu²⁺ ions in solution74.

The conductometric results provide indirect support for the proposed corrosion inhibition mechanism of Cu in 1.0 M HNO3 in the occurrence of DAP to give just a suggestion for the possibility of Cu (II)–DAP complex formation. In the absence of an inhibitor, Cu undergoes anodic dissolution in the acidic medium, producing Cu²⁺ ions that remain freely mobile in solution and contribute to rapid corrosion.

When expired DAP is introduced, inhibition occurs primarily through coordination and surface adsorption processes. The strong affinity of DAP for Cu²⁺ ions, as demonstrated by conductometric titration, leads to the formation of a stable metal–ligand complex in solution. This complex reduces the concentration of free Cu²⁺ ions and promotes adsorption onto the Cu surface.

Adsorption of the Cu–DAP complex is facilitated by donor–acceptor interactions between electron-rich oxygen atoms and vacant copper orbitals, as well as by additional electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions within the acidic nitrate environment. These interactions successfully separate the Cu surface from the corrosive medium by producing a tight and sticky interfacial layer. Moreover, this adsorbed layer functions as an engineered interface, where the electronic environment of the Cu surface is modified, leading to suppression of charge transfer reactions associated with anodic dissolution74.

The presence of this protective layer hinders the access of aggressive species to active anodic sites and suppresses the release of Cu²⁺ ions into solution. Consequently, both the rate of Cu dissolution and the overall corrosion current are significantly reduced. The anticorrosion efficacy increases with expired DAP concentration, consistent with adsorption-controlled protection.

Quantum-chemical analysis

The efficiency of corrosion inhibitors is frequently assessed using Density Functional Theory (DFT), a key quantum chemical method. It enables a detailed understanding of the electronic structure variations that govern inhibition performance76. In particular, the DFT/B3LYP approach is considered a dependable and extensively adopted method for modeling and predicting the behavior of organic corrosion inhibitors76–80. Moreover, using DFT-based computations, the inhibitor’s structural characteristics during the corrosion process and its interactions with the metal surface were systematically analyzed. Consequently, DFT represents an essential theoretical approach for examining frontier molecular orbitals, supporting the interpretation of experimental results, and elucidating the mechanism of adsorption at the surface/inhibitor interface.

Figure 13 displays the optimized conformation of dapagliflozin (DAP) molecule and the corresponding spatial distributions of its frontier HOMO and LUMO orbitals. The optimized structure reveals that DAP adopts a non-planar conformation. The observed variations in bond lengths and angles are strongly related to this non-planar arrangement, which influences the overall structural parameters of the molecule. The HOMO is characterized by a localized distribution of electron density over the heteroatoms and aromatic moieties, pointing out the regions that interact by donating electrons to Cu’s vacant d-orbitals. This electron-donating ability facilitates the development of coordinating bonds between the metal surface and the inhibitor. Conversely, the LUMO distribution highlights the electron-accepting regions of the molecule, supporting the possibility of back-donation from filled Cu orbitals into the antibonding orbitals of DAP. Such donor–acceptor interactions contribute significantly to the stabilization of the adsorbed layer.

The electrophilicity index (ω = 2.199 eV) reflects the balanced donor–acceptor character of DAP, enabling strong adsorption on the Cu surface through combined σ-electron donation from heteroatoms and π-back donation from the metal, thereby improving the effectiveness of corrosion inhibition and surface coverage. The electro-donating power (ω⁻ = 4.210 eV) is considerably higher than the electro-accepting power (ω⁺), confirming that DAP predominantly behaves as an electron donor. This pronounced donating ability reflects its strong nucleophilic character and high tendency to transfer electron density to an electron-deficient surface. Such behavior is particularly favorable for adsorption on Cu, where the inhibitor can donate electrons through the oxygen atom lone pairs and from its π-conjugated system to the vacant or partially filled orbitals of the Cu atoms. Such electron donation leads to the establishment of coordinate bonds and strengthens donor–acceptor interactions at the metal–solution interface. Consequently, the preferential electron-donating nature of DAP enhances its adsorption stability and contributes significantly to its corrosion anti-corrosive efficacy. The high Δω value (5.046 eV) confirms the pronounced electron-donating nature of DAP. The calculated ΔEb–d (− 0.647 eV) is negative, demonstrating that back-donation from the metal to the inhibitor is energetically favorable. Negative ΔEb–d values are commonly associated with stable chemisorption, suggesting that DAP adsorption occurs via partial charge redistribution and donor–acceptor interactions at the Cu interface.

Compared to these inhibitors, DAP demonstrates comparable performance (91.1%), even though it was tested in nitric acid rather than hydrochloric acid. While the imidazothiazole and piperazine derivatives show slightly higher efficiencies, DAP still performs competitively, particularly considering the aggressive nature of nitric acid media. Its performance is comparable to linezolid and slightly lower than piperacillin, but superior to several other drug-based inhibitors tested under similar or less aggressive conditions.

An important observation from Table 6 is that although some inhibitors achieve high efficiencies at very low molar concentrations (≈ 10⁻⁴ M), DAP attains 91.1% inhibition at 300 mg L⁻¹, which can be attributed to its multiple functional groups (hydroxyl, ether, and aromatic moieties) that promote strong synergistic adsorption through heteroatom electron donation, π–electron contributions and probable electrostatic interactions within the acidic medium.

The data in Table 6 clearly demonstrates that Dapagliflozin (DAP) acts as a potent corrosion inhibitor for Cu under acidic conditions, with inhibition efficiency comparable to or exceeding many previously reported inhibitors. Its strong performance, particularly in nitric acid, underscores the impact of its molecular structure and electronic attributes in enabling stable adsorption and protective film formation on the Cu surface.

Conclusion

Expired dapagliflozin (DAP) was found to be an effective corrosion inhibitor for copper in 1.0 M HNO₃ solution. The inhibition efficiency obtained from different techniques increased with decreasing temperature and increasing inhibitor concentration, indicating stronger adsorption at higher surface coverage.

Potentiodynamic polarization results revealed that expired DAP acts as a mixed-type inhibitor and effectively suppresses pitting corrosion by shifting the pitting potential toward more positive values. Conductometric titration results suggested that the inhibition performance is associated with the formation of a stable complex between DAP species and Cu²⁺ ions, which adsorb on the copper surface. This adsorption of the complex reduces the corrosion rate and enhances the overall protective efficiency.

The adsorption process was found to follow the Langmuir adsorption isotherm, indicating monolayer adsorption on a homogeneous surface. Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations further confirmed that the adsorption of DAP on the Cu surface occurs through donor–acceptor interactions involving electron-rich sites of the inhibitor and vacant orbitals of copper.

Moreover, the good agreement between experimental techniques (weight loss, potentiodynamic polarization, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy) and theoretical DFT results supports the reliability and consistency of the proposed inhibition mechanism.
