Angstrom-fluidic chemical synapses for accurate cancer diagnosis
Authors: Jing Zhao, Qun Ma, Xueqin Luo, Hong Liu, Shijun Xu, Gangping Lian, Yang Liu, Huageng Liang, Lei Zhou, Meihua Lin, Wei Guo, Fan Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial chemical synapses, which specifically identify, transmit, and process molecular information, find promising applications in precision medical diagnosis, neural-electronic interface, and in-memory computing. However, to implement biomarker-triggered neuronal excitability modulation with artificial iontronic devices remains a significant challenge. Herein, we demonstrate a capture DNA integrated angstrom-fluidic chemical synapse in which the intramembrane ionic conductance can be switched between excitatory and inhibitory states by specific DNA-target interactions on the outer membrane surface. Experimental results and theoretical calculations unveil that capture of specific biomarker results in a bidirectional space charge polarization, and establishes opposite local concentration gradient at the membrane surface. Driven by this reversible concentration gradient, cation influx or efflux modulate the number density of ionic charge carriers inside the membrane, analogy to the hyperpolarization and depolarization modes of biological chemical synapses. Using a convolutional neural network algorithm to process the ionic conductance enhancement and depletion signals, we develop a diagnostic approach for early prostate cancer with 100% accuracy for both retrospective analysis of 105 clinical specimens, and prospective double-blind trials (n = 10). This work sheds light on artificial chemical synapses based medical diagnosis, and provides a blueprint for neural-like iontronic network for chemical information processing.

Introduction

Neural signals are generated, conducted, and processed through the interplay of chemical and electrical synapses1–4. Neurotransmitters released from the presynaptic terminal of the chemical synapse bind to specific receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, which triggers the opening or closing of excitable ion channels therein, and thus leads to the lateral propagation of action potentials (Fig. 1A)5–8. This process is widely involved in, for example, sensory processing, motor coordination, and cognitive functions9,10. From a molecular perspective, K⁺ efflux from the cell leads to an intra-cellular hyperpolarization, and makes the membrane potential more negative, termed as an inhibitory state (Fig. 1A). It elevates the threshold for action potential, and thereby suppresses the neuronal excitability11. Correspondingly, Na⁺ influx to the cell causes intra-cellular depolarization, and renders the membrane potential less negative, which is closer to the action potential threshold, and thus promotes the neuronal excitability, termed as an excitatory state (Fig. 1A)12. Dynamic switching between the inhibitory and the excitatory states enables tunable and bidirectional signal propagation that is fundamental for synaptic plasticity, adaptive information processing, and complex behavioral coordination13–16.Fig. 1Bioinspired design of an artificial chemical synapse.A Scheme of the biological chemical synapse (BCS). Inspired by biological chemical synapses, artificial chemical synapses constructed with electronic or ionic devices, emulate key functions such as molecular recognition17–19, transmission20,21, and processing22,23. By bridging biological and artificial systems, artificial chemical synapses leverage ionic or molecular charge carriers to mediate and transmit biochemical signals, establishing a foundation for real-time signal transduction and seamless biological interface24–26. Within this context, artificial iontronic devices demonstrate proficiency in regulating ion transport and electrochemical signals, closely mimicking dynamic biological signal transmission27–30. Of note, the state-of-the-art artificial iontronic devices are proficient in processing unidirectional signal transduction by precisely switching between two metastable states31. However, achieving biomolecule-triggered three-state switching remains a significant challenge for artificial iontronic devices to emulate the bidirectional signal propagation characteristic of neural systems.

Furthermore, to endow artificial systems with more advanced intelligence for precision medical diagnosis, recently, the integration of machine learning algorithms shows considerable promise32–35. Machine learning algorithms enable multidimensional analysis of complex biomolecular data, integrating features such as RNA, proteins, and metabolites to improve diagnostic accuracy and specificity36,37. For example, Das et al. utilize ion-sensitive devices combined with an artificial neural network to achieve advanced data analytics38. The extensive datasets collected from the sensors are transformed into multiple features of merit, which serve as input signals for the neural network. Each data point is assigned a weight, and multi-feature normalization is employed to eliminate dimensional discrepancies between features, thereby improving the efficiency and accuracy of the system. This machine-learning-assisted analytical framework provides critical insights for the development of artificial chemical synapse devices for bidirectional signal processing, a critical step toward creating more adaptive, precise, and responsive diagnostic systems.

Herein, inspired by the extracellular membrane triggered and lateral membrane transmission modes of action potential along biological chemical synapses, we demonstrate an excitatory and inhibitory states switchable angstrom-fluidic chemical synapse (AFCS) that regulates the intramembrane ionic conductance in multi-layered graphene oxide membrane (GOM) by specific DNA-target interactions on the outer membrane surface (Fig. 1B). Experimental results and theoretical calculations reveal that converting multi-dimensional biomolecular information, such as molecular type, charge, and concentration, creates bidirectional space-charge polarization and establishes an opposite cation concentration gradient at the membrane surface. Driven by this reversible and localized concentration gradient, cation influx or efflux from outside the membrane modulates the number density of ionic charge carriers inside the membrane, achieving biomarker-triggered neuronal excitability modulation. Following this principle, the synapse-mimetic ionic current signals from real clinical specimens of early prostate cancer can be further processed with a machine learning algorithm to achieve neural-like diagnosis (Fig. 1C). Confusion matrix analysis demonstrates 100% accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and precision. In prospective double-blind trials, our system identifies prostate cancer patients with 100% accuracy.

Results and discussion

Designing artificial chemical synapses

Following the similarities in structure and signal transmission mode with biological chemical synapses (Fig. 1A), we design artificial chemical synapses based on a strip of reconstructed GOM with an interconnected network of laminar ionic and fluidic channels (Fig. 1B). The GOM was fabricated by vacuum filtration of liquid exfoliated GO nanosheets, followed by a mild thermal annealing process to improve water stability39,40 (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed that the GOM shows a relatively smooth surface morphology (Supplementary Fig. 3A), and a dense layered structure on the cross section (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Its overall thickness is approximately 5 μm. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis indicates that the interlayer spacing (d) of a wet GOM is about 11.7 Å (Supplementary Fig. 4). Considering the monolayer thickness of a GO nanosheet (~3.4 Å), the resulting lamellar channels possess an effective hydrated height of ~8.3 Å. From separate water permeation experiments, we confirm that water can permeate through the GOM (Supplementary Fig. 5), indicating that the hydrated membrane provides sufficient free volume for the diffusion of water as well as common hydrated ions such as K⁺ (hydrated radius ~3.3 Å), thereby enabling efficient ionic transport in the device41,42.

To enable biomolecular recognition as an artificial chemical synapse, the outer surface of the GOM was chemically modified with single-stranded DNA probes (Fig. 2A and Methods). A 32-mer DNA aptamer is first used as the capture probe to demonstrate the versatility of the DNA modification strategy, which specifically binds to the highly conserved biomarker prostate-specific antigen (PSA) associated with prostate cancer. After DNA modification, the surface morphology of the DNA-on-GOM becomes wrinkled under SEM observation, but no evident change can be found on the layered cross section (Fig. 2B). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements further show that peak-shaped nanostructures appear on the surface of GOM after DNA modification (Fig. 2C). The mean height of the peak structure is approximately 9.8 nm, with a lateral size ranging from 22 to 25 nm. In comparison, the root mean square roughness of the unmodified GOM is merely 0.3 nm.

To further confirm the successful DNA modification on the GOM surface, the capture DNA strands were labeled with a fluorescent dye (cyanine 3, shortened as Cy3) on their 3’ terminus. As shown in Fig. 2D, surface fluorescence imaging observes a strong fluorescent signal from the surface of the DNA-on-GOM, while the unmodified GOM exhibits negligible fluorescent noise.

Under X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), the 2P energy spectra of phosphorus element further confirm the presence of capture DNA after chemical modification (Supplementary Fig. 6). More importantly, through an element analysis of the phosphorus element (characteristics of DNA) and carbon element (characteristics of the GO backbone) in the depth-direction of the DNA-on-GOM, one can get the depth-profiling of the DNA modification. As shown in Fig. 2E, the DNA components distribute mainly at the outer surface and the near-surface region of about 20 nm in depth.

After DNA modification, XRD analysis shows a prominent diffraction peak centered at approximately 7.6° (Supplementary Fig. 4), corresponding to an estimated channel height of about 8.3 Å (Fig. 2F). Compared to the unmodified GOM, the change in the interlayer distance is negligible. This is probably due to the fact that the DNA modification takes place merely at the outer surface and in very few parts of the stacked GO layers.

In addition, after DNA modification, the top surface of the GOM becomes more hydrophilic reflected by the change in surface contact angle (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Fig. 2F). A significant increase from −20.3 to −30.0 mV in the surface zeta potential of the GOM is also found upon DNA modification (Fig. 2F). These phenomena can be explained by the negatively charged and hydrophilic nature of the DNA backbone.

A confocal laser scanning microscope (LSCM) is further employed to characterize the space charge distribution near the outer surface upon DNA modification. The DNA-on-GOM was incubated in a solution of positively charged fluorescent dye, Rhodamine 6 G (R6G), for LSCM imaging. As shown in Fig. 2G, the fluorescence intensity from the DNA-on-GOM is significantly stronger than that from the unmodified GOM. This result suggests that the adsorption capacity of the positively charged R6G dyes is greatly enhanced by the modification of capture DNA. Interestingly, the fluorescent signal from the DNA-on-GOM further extends to the outer space of about 20 nm in height beyond the membrane surface. Considering the fact that the length of a 32-mer DNA strand is about 11 nm in ionic solution44, the extended fluorescent region is due to the stretched space charge toward the bulk solution.

Fundamental ion transport properties

To fabricate a testing device for lateral ion transport, a strip of outer-surface unmodified GOM (5 mm × 10 mm) was first encapsulated by an epoxy matrix to avoid swelling of the interlayer distance upon hydration45–47. Then, the epoxy confined GOM was sealed in a transparent polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) elastomer (Fig. 3A)48. Afterward, two side reservoirs of about 100 μL in volume were carved out in the PDMS substrate, exposing the two lateral ends of the GO strip to electrolyte solution. Ag/AgCl electrodes were used to record the lateral ion transport signals through the GOM. This design improves the utilization efficiency of the testing liquid samples, and thus, yields a highly efficient sensing interface for the trace analytes in clinical samples40. Moreover, the capture DNA probes with contour lengths of more than ten nanometers extends the scope of the native surface charges on GOM to the vertical dimension, forming a space charge region. It facilitates the specific capture of biological target molecules from the testing liquid samples, and thus, functions as a biomolecular-triggered switch for transmembrane ion transport through the interlayer angstrom-scale fluidic channels. These characteristics explain why the device is termed an artificial chemical synapse.

To investigate the ion transport properties, current-voltage measurements were carried out in a 1 × 10−6 M of unbuffered potassium chloride (KCl) solutions. It was crucial to consistently maintain the inlet window interface incubated with the 1 × 10−6 M KCl solution as the blank solution. Typical results are shown in Fig. 3B. Both pristine and DNA-modified GOMs show linear current-voltage responses under a scanning voltage of 0 to 1.0 V. After DNA modification, the lateral ionic conductance increases remarkably from 0.1 μS in the initial state to 0.8 μS in the stand-by state. Here, we define an ON-OFF ratio (Istand-by state – Iinitial state)/(Iinitial state × 100%) to measure the ionic conductance enhancement between the initial and the stand-by states, where Istand-by state and Iinitial state are the ionic conductance at +1.0 V before and after capture DNA modification, respectively. The 8-fold increment suggests that the extended space charge region beyond the outer membrane surface due to graft of capture DNA carries huge implications to the lateral ion conduction in the beneath interlayer angstrom-channels.

To understand the role of the DNA probes, the space-charge-induced ionic conductance enhancement is further investigated by modulating the DNA grafting density. As shown in Fig. 3C, the ON-OFF ratio shows a strong positive correlation with the content of DNA probes used in the chemical modification steps. In the DNA-on-GOM structure, the negatively charged DNA strands would accumulate cations in the near-surface space charge region49. Analogy to the biological chemical synapse (Fig. 1A), this local cation concentration difference would lead to influx of cations to the intra-GOM side, and promote the concentration of ionic charge carriers in the beneath interlayer channels, termed space charge polarization50. Therefore, under high DNA grafting density, the enhanced space charge polarization would consequently promote the lateral ionic conductance through the underlying GOM.

To further support the observed ionic conductance enhancement, which is caused by the DNA-induced space charge polarization, electroneutral peptide nucleic acid (PNA) probes were covalently attached onto the outer surface of GOM, instead of DNA. From the results shown in Supplementary Fig. 9, one can see that the modification of neutral PNA molecules does not show a clear influence on the overall lateral ionic conductance through the GOM, and the increment is no more than 0.05%. This evidence suggests that the charge property of the modified molecular components is crucial to the switching behavior in transmembrane ionic conductance.

In addition, to get an optimal testing condition for biochemical analysis, concentration-dependent ionic conductance switching behavior is further investigated. By keeping the modified DNA concentration to 10−6 M, the electrolyte concentration in the two side reservoirs was varied from 10−6 to 10−3 M. From the results shown in Fig. 3D, one can see that a substantial ON-OFF ratio can be obtained merely in the low-concentration range. Therefore, we select the electrolyte concentration of 10−6 M for subsequent bioanalytical tests. Moreover, to evaluate the storage stability of the DNA-on-GOM devices, the samples were stored at 4 °C for 14 days and subsequently subjected to ionic current measurements. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 10, the ionic conductance exhibited negligible variation throughout the storage period, indicating that the DNA-on-GOM devices maintained stable and reproducible electrochemical performance under refrigerated conditions.

Biomarker-triggered excitatory and inhibitory states

To explore biomarker-triggered excitatory and inhibitory ion transport states, three prostate cancer-associated biomarkers51 were selected as a testbed: the positively charged PSA protein (isoelectric point 7.2)52, DNA analogs of prostate cancer antigen 3 (PCA3, a noncoding RNA), and microRNA-198 (miRNA-198), as shown in Fig. 4A and Supplementary Table 1. The current-voltage measurements of DNA-on-GOM before and after the input of 10 ng/mL PSA protein are shown in Fig. 4B. The ionic conductance decreases from 0.8 μS in the stand-by state to 0.6 μS in the inhibitory state, due to the reduction of the surface space charge by the positively charged PSA (Supplementary Fig. 11). Surface zeta potential measurements (Supplementary Fig. 12) confirm this reduction, indicating depolarization of the outer surface of GOM. Moreover, the ionic conductance decreases progressively with increasing PSA concentrations (0.1–30 ng/mL) (Supplementary Fig. 13), demonstrating the tunability of the inhibitory current via surface depolarization. The inhibitory state of DNA-on-GOM is quantitatively characterized using the inhibitory index (β): Inhibitory index = (Iinhibitory state–Istand-by state)/Istand-by state × 100%, where Iinhibitory state and Istand-by state are the ionic conductance intensities at a bias of +1.0 V before and after the input of PSA proteins, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4C, the inhibitory index is gradually increased with PSA concentration, highlighting the PSA-induced upregulation of the inhibitory function. Notably, the specificity of the aptamer binding is confirmed, as no significant change in the inhibitory index is observed with hemoglobin (HB), glucose oxidase (GOD), casein (CS), and lysozyme (LZ), even at concentrations 10 times higher than PSA (Supplementary Fig. 14 and Fig. 4D). Moreover, to evaluate the storage stability of sensing performance, the DNA-on-GOM devices were stored at 4 °C for 14 days and periodically tested for PSA detection. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 15, both the PSA-responsive ionic conductance and the derived inhibitory index remained essentially unchanged over the storage period, demonstrating that the devices preserve stable and reliable detection capability over extended storage.

In another way, when PCA3 or miRNA-198 (at 10–8 M) is input, the negatively charged nucleic acid molecules hybridize with their complementary DNA probes on the GOM surface (Fig. 4A). Upon input of PCA3, hybridization with complementary ssDNA probes on the outer surface of the GOM increase ionic conductance from 0.3 μS in the stand-by state to 0.7 μS in the excitatory state (Fig. 4E). Similarly, when miRNA-198 is input, the ionic conductance is elevated from 0.4 μS in the stand-by state to 0.8 μS in the excitatory state. Zeta potential measurements (Supplementary Fig. 12) confirm hyperpolarization of the outer surface of GOM, facilitating the transition from a stand-by state to an excitatory state.

To investigate the tunable excitatory current of DNA-on-GOM, the ionic conductance is observed to increase with the concentration of PCA3 or miRNA-198 (10–11 to 10–7 M) (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17), further demonstrating its modulation through surface hyperpolarization. The excitatory state of DNA-on-GOM is quantitatively characterized using the excitatory index (β): Excitatory index = (Iexcitatory state–Istand-by state)/Istand-by state × 100%, where Iexcitatory state and Istand-by state are the ionic conductance intensities at a bias of +1.0 V before and after the input of PCA3 (or miRNA-198), respectively. Figure 4F shows that the excitatory index gradually increases with the concentration of PCA3 (or miRNA-198), indicating the upregulation of the excitatory function. Interestingly, when equivalent concentrations of PCA3 and miRNA-198 are input, the excitatory index for PCA3 is higher than that for miRNA-198. This is likely due to the greater efficiency and stability of DNA-DNA hybridization compared to RNA-DNA hybridization53.

Moreover, due to the specificity of Watson-Crick base pairing54,55, only perfectly matched PCA3 and miRNA-198 sequences significantly modulate the space charge on the GOM surface, inducing an increase in ionic conductance (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19). Specifically, the excitatory current index for PCA3 is 8.7 times higher than its negative control, while miRNA-198 exhibits an 8.1-fold increase over its negative control (Fig. 4G). In conclusion, DNA-on-GOM successfully mimics the inhibitory and excitatory functions of biological chemical synapses, enabling the transition between three states through space charge polarization in response to biomarker inputs. Therefore, the developed device shows promise for the specific detection of prostate cancer-relevant proteins, RNA, and microRNA56.

Computational visualization of space-charge-regulated cation influx/efflux

To obtain an in-depth understanding of the intramembrane ionic conductance switched between excitatory and inhibitory states by bidirectional space charges polarization, a theoretical analysis and calculation were performed based on a coarse-grained Poisson-Nernst-Planck (PNP) model (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. 20)57. The coarse-grained particles were used to simulate the capture DNA structures on the outer membrane surface. The diameter of coarse-grained particles was set as 2 nm because a 6-mer of DNA is 2.04 nm (Fig. 5A). The space charge region was filled with these particles with a spacing of 2 nm. In order to simplify the simulation calculation, the diameter and length of the GOM main channel were set as 2 nm and 32 nm, respectively. By fixing the charge density on GOM surface (–0.01 C/m2, see calculation in Supplementary part 19.3) and increasing the charge density on DNA-filled region (σ < –0.05 C/m2), corresponding to enhanced hyperpolarization at the space charge region on the outer surface of the membrane, the intramembrane ionic conductance rises from 0.17 μS to 0.64 μS gradually (Fig. 5B, square), representing an excitatory state. By contrast, decreasing the charge density (–0.05 C/m2 < σ < 0 C/m2) on the DNA-filled region enhances depolarization at the outer surface, leading to a decline in intramembrane ionic conductance (from 0.17 μS to 0.05 μS) (Fig. 5B, triangle), representing an inhibitory state. The calculation results demonstrate consistent trends with experimental observations (Fig. 4) and provide insights into the effect of the intramembrane ionic conductance switching between excitatory and inhibitory states by bidirectional space charge polarization, indicating that the model is valid.

The calculation results further indicate that the variation in intramembrane ionic conductance is attributed to the modulation of the number density of cationic charge carriers inside the main channel under bidirectional space charge polarization at the outer membrane surface (Fig. 5C). Increasing the charge density on DNA-filled region (–0.07 C/m2) induces space charge hyperpolarization at the outer surface, leading to higher cation concentration in the main channel compared to the stand-by state (Fig. 5C, blue line). Conversely, decreasing the charge density on the DNA-filled region (–0.03 C/m2) promotes depolarization at the outer surface, which reduces the cation concentration in the main channel compared to the stand-by state (Fig. 5C, pink line).

Further analysis of the source of cations in the main channel shows that space charge hyperpolarization leads to the accumulation of more cations in the space charge region, establishing a localized concentration gradient at the GOM surface (Fig. 5D, left). The cation concentration outside the membrane is higher than that inside the membrane. The direction of current density flow indicates that localized concentration gradient drives the cation influx from outside the membrane (Fig. 5E, left), and increases the number density of ionic charge carriers inside the main channel, mimicking the depolarization mode of biological chemical synapses (with Na+ influx in living systems)61,62.

Likewise, space charge depolarization leads to a higher cation concentration inside the main channel compared to outside the membrane (Fig. 5D, right), creating an opposite localized concentration gradient. This results in cation efflux out of the main channel (Fig. 5E, right), simulating the hyperpolarization mode of biological chemical synapses (with K+ efflux in living systems)63,64. Consequently, the number density of ionic charge carriers inside the membrane decreases, inducing an inhibitory state in ionic conductance.

Prostate cancer diagnosis using clinical specimens

Before applying the device to clinical specimens, we first validated its stability and anti-interference performance in complex serum environments (Supplementary Fig. 21). The device generated nearly identical excitatory indices in serum and in buffer, even in the presence of a ten-fold excess of non-complementary DNA interferent, confirming that matrix effects do not compromise the sensing output.

To investigate the feasibility as a diagnostic tool, multiple biomarkers were extracted from the whole blood specimens of prostate cancer patients for analysis. Furthermore, we statistically analyzed the ionic current values of the clinical specimens (Fig. 6C). For the PSA-specific device, the mean current exhibits a significant decrease when exposed to patient specimens (0.80 ± 0.05) compared to healthy controls (0.88 ± 0.01, p = 0.0003). In contrast, the PCA3-specific device exhibits a marked increase in mean current for patient specimens (0.57 ± 0.11) compared to healthy controls (0.34 ± 0.02, p < 0.0001). Similarly, the miRNA-198-specific device shows a significant increase in mean current for patient specimens (0.51 ± 0.05) compared to healthy controls (0.44 ± 0.01, p = 0.0001). Statistical analysis reveals significant differences in the responses of all three devices, which effectively distinguish prostate cancer patients from healthy individuals. These results indicated that all three biomarkers are overexpressed in prostate cancer patients, consistent with the theory of gene upregulation in cancer66.

To quantify the diagnostic potential, the ionic current values are converted into inhibitory and excitatory indices (Fig. 6D). For the PSA-specific device, a significant increase in the inhibitory index is observed in serum specimens from prostate cancer patients, with β values ranging from –22 to –40%, while serum specimens from healthy controls show β values near –7%. Similarly, the PCA3-specific and miRNA-198-specific devices exhibit a pronounced increase in excitatory indices when exposed to total RNA extracted from patient specimens, with β values ranging from 30 to 90%. In contrast, RNA specimens from healthy controls generate excitatory indices near 15%. Notably, each patient exhibits varied expression levels of prostate cancer-associated protein, RNA, and miRNA, highlighting the diversity of prostate cancer phenotypes67. The resultant heat map classifies patients and healthy controls, achieving prostate cancer diagnosis; however, the overlap between patient and healthy control specimens for single biomarkers highlights the limitations of single-target analysis, indicating the need for multiple biomarkers combinatorial diagnostic analysis to improve classification accuracy37.

These weights are adjusted during training and optimized using backpropagation to minimize classification errors68. Next, a training cohort comprising 75 clinical whole blood specimens (52 prostate cancer patients and 23 healthy individuals) is utilized to evaluate the practical application potential. The data are shuffled randomly to ensure that each batch of the dataset consists of both healthy sample-derived data and prostate cancer specimen-derived data72. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves are constructed using the NN algorithm and biomarker features. As shown in Fig. 7B, the area under the curve (AUC) for the multiple biomarkers is 1.000, surpassing the AUCs of single biomarkers (PSA: 0.903, PCA3: 0.916, miRNA-198: 0.864) and dual biomarker combinations (PSA + PCA3: 0.952, PSA + miRNA-198: 0.962, PCA3 + miRNA-198: 0.959). These results indicate that the combination of multiple biomarkers in the NN algorithm outperforms single and dual biomarker approaches, fully leveraging the integrated features of all three biomarkers to achieve superior diagnostic accuracy.

To evaluate the practical potential in cancer diagnosis, we investigate the correlation among the expression levels of three biomarkers. The predicted and actual accuracy of the NN algorithm, which utilizes these three biomarker signatures as inputs, is summarized in a confusion matrix (Fig. 7C). Notably, the combination of multiple biomarkers in the NN algorithm for diagnosing prostate cancer (1.00 AUC, 100% sensitivity, 100% specificity, and 100% precision) affords better diagnostic ability than individual or dual biomarker features (Supplementary Fig. 22). By employing the NN algorithm, we achieve a diagnostic accuracy of 100% for prostate cancer (Fig. 7D).

Utilizing a highly accurate NN algorithm and a logistic regression model73, the optimal weights for the three biomarkers in the combinational diagnosis of prostate cancer are determined. These weights are as follows: PSA (W1 = 0.480), PCA3 (W2 = 0.393), and miRNA-198 (W3 = 0.127), as shown in Fig. 7E. In the computational classifier, weight analysis distinguishes between prostate cancer patients and healthy individuals through a weighted summation. The magnitude of each weight reflects the relative importance of the corresponding biomarker74. A larger weight indicates a greater contribution of that biomarker to the classification process75.

To justify the validity, the optimal weights are applied to the three biomarker features from an independent validation set. This set consists of 30 age-matched clinical specimens, including 20 confirmed prostate cancer patients and 10 healthy individuals (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 23). By performing weighted summation, the data are converted into a weighted signal change ratio. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 24, this method achieves 100% classification accuracy in distinguishing prostate cancer patients from healthy individuals. The optimal cut-off value is 6.3% (determined using Youden’s index based on the training cohort)72, confirming the success and effectiveness of the assigned biomarker weights.

The optimal weights derived from the NN model (Fig. 7E) are applied to classify prostate cancer patients and healthy individuals in prospective double-blind trials. Specifically, serum and total RNA are extracted from 10 unknown whole blood clinical specimens. As shown in Fig. 7F, inhibitory and excitatory indices for the three biomarkers are generated. These indices exhibit considerable variability across specimens, reflecting differences in biomarker expression levels among individuals.

The weighted signal change ratio is subsequently calculated by combining these indices using the pre-determined optimal weights. As shown in Fig. 7G, this ratio is plotted for the 10 clinical specimens, with a cut-off value of 6.3% applied to differentiate prostate cancer patients from healthy individuals. This ratio separates prostate cancer patients (7 specimens) from healthy individuals (3 specimens) with a 100% accuracy, demonstrating precise classification accuracy (Supplementary Fig. 25). The results are fully consistent with clinical diagnoses (Supplementary Table 4). In contrast, using single or dual biomarker features results in accuracy values of 93.8–96.7% (Supplementary Fig. 26), further emphasizing the critical importance of combinatorial biomarker weighting. These results demonstrate the potential of combining multiple biomarker analysis with the NN algorithm to enhance diagnostic performance in early-stage prostate cancer.

Discussion

In summary, we develop a capture DNA-integrated AFCS capable of biomarker-triggered bidirectional ionic conductance switching. Molecular recognition occurring at the macroscopically accessible outer membrane surface induces space charge polarization, which modulates the intramembrane ionic carrier density within angstrom-scale channels. This coupling between outer-interface chemistry and confined ion transport generates excitatory- and inhibitory-like conductance states analogous to synaptic depolarization and hyperpolarization (Supplementary Table 5)76,77. Integration of three-biomarker ionic outputs with machine-learning analysis achieves 100% diagnostic accuracy for early-stage prostate cancer in both retrospective and prospective evaluations.

Mechanistically, this study demonstrates that quantitative regulation of confined ion transport can be achieved through reconfiguration of interfacial charge landscapes without altering geometric confinement. Unlike conventional nanofluidic strategies that position recognition elements inside nanochannels78–80, post-assembly functionalization at the outer surface preserves sub-nanometer interlayer integrity while improving probe accessibility and reaction kinetics81. The immobilized DNA probes establish a spatially extended charge distribution that dynamically redistributes ionic carriers within the membrane. As a result, subtle biochemical interactions are converted into amplified and low-noise transmembrane ionic signals through space charge modulation.

From a device-physics perspective, the AFCS operates as a chemically gated ionic transistor in which molecular recognition governs ion transport via space charge polarization. In contrast to electronic field-effect transistors that modulate electronic carriers under external gate bias82, and to memristive systems governed by history-dependent conductance evolution83, the AFCS relies on chemically induced ionic redistribution. This mechanism enables bidirectional ionic conductance modulation within a single platform, transforming conventional monotonic ionic readout into a synaptic signal-processing mode with intrinsic dual-polarity contrast28,84. Such dual-polarity behavior provides internal signal differentiation without external gate operation, offering a biomimetic route for chemical information processing. These mechanistic features translate into system-level performance. The angstrom-fluidic architecture supports stable operation in aqueous biological environments while avoiding the nanofabrication complexity and environmental sensitivity of electronic transistor-based platforms85,86. Through space charge-mediated amplification of interfacial perturbations, the AFCS achieves a limit of detection of 0.1 ng/mL for PSA and picomolar sensitivity for nucleic acid biomarkers, demonstrating performance comparable to or exceeding representative field-effect transistor- and memristor-based biosensors (Supplementary Table 6)87–103. The platform maintains storage stability for over 14 days (Supplementary Fig. 15), exhibits robust inter-batch reproducibility with a coefficient of variation of 5.3% (Supplementary Fig. 27), and enables signal readout within 20 min. Importantly, the high-fidelity ionic outputs support accurate multidimensional classification using only three biomarkers, highlighting the synergy between iontronic signal transduction and machine-learning-assisted diagnosis.

Beyond prostate cancer detection, the central principle established here, namely, coupling outer-interface molecular recognition with angstrom-confined space charge regulation, provides a generalizable framework for chemical-to-ionic signal conversion. Future work may focus on programmable modulation of outer-surface charge density using structurally defined DNA assemblies and hierarchical nucleic acid architectures, enabling quantitative tuning of polarization amplitude and carrier redistribution104,105. Integration of multiple AFCS units into series and parallel ionic circuits could further facilitate higher-order information processing, laying a foundation for artificial nanofluidic neural networks that combine biomimetic sensing with ionic neuromorphic computation106,107.

Methods

Materials

Pristine graphene oxide nanosheet was purchased from XFNANO Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). KCl and polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) were obtained from Aladdin Reagent (Shanghai, China). 1-3-(N, N-Dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC, ≥98%) and N-Hydroxysulfosuccinimide (NHS, ≥98.0%) were purchased from Aladdin Chemistry Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Rhodamine 6 G was purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). DNA was synthesized by Hippo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). PNA was synthesized by Hangzhou Taihe Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China).

Characterizations

SEM images were collected with a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SU8010, HITACHI). Surface image and height analysis of the GOM were performed using a Bruker MultiMode 8 atomic force microscope in tapping mode. The fluorescence images were captured by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, LSM880, Carl Zeiss AG) equipped with a FemtoSecond Laser (Coherent Inc.). Elemental and functional group analysis of the products was performed by XPS (Thermo, ESCALAB 250Xi). X-ray diffraction (XRD; Bruker, D8-Advance) analysis was conducted using an X-ray diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å). Contact angles of viscous liquids on different surfaces were measured with a contact angle goniometer (DSA100). The Zeta potential of solids is tested by VisioLab for SurPASS by Anton Paar GmbH.

Covalent DNA modification

The GOM outer surface was treated with 10 μL of 1 ng/mL EDC/NHS solution and 100 μL of 1 μM single-stranded DNA probes (prepared from a 100 μM stock, stored at 4 °C, centrifuged at 16770 × g, and diluted with ultra-pure water) at 25 °C for carboxyl group activation and DNA modification. The amide bond reaction was allowed to proceed for 20 min. After the reaction, the surface was washed three times with 1 μM KCl solution to remove unreacted DNA probes.

Model calculation

The simulation system contains of two electrolyte reservoirs connected by the GOM main channel (Supplementary Fig. 20). To gain an affordable computation scale, a coarse-grained particle with a diameter of 2 nm and an interstitial space of 2 nm is used to simulate the space charge region formed by the capture DNA on the GOM top surface, while a 2 nm-width, 32 nm-length main channel is used to simulate the GOM. Details of the model parameters in Supplementary Materials, part 19.

Dilution of biomarker specimens for measurements

PSA protein (initial concentration: 100 μg in powder form) was dissolved in 0.01 M PBS buffer (pH 7.4) and diluted to concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 50 ng/mL. The diluted PSA solutions were stored at –20 °C to prevent degradation from repeated freeze-thaw cycles. For nucleic acid biomarkers, PCA3 analogs and miRNA-198, as well as their corresponding negative controls, were first dissolved in nuclease-free water and then diluted in 10 mM Tris-HCl buffer (1 mM EDTA, pH 7.4) to final concentrations ranging from 10–12 M to 10–7 M. Aliquots of 20 μL were prepared for each sample and stored at –20 °C until use.

Human participant recruitment

Clinical samples were collected from the Department of Urology, Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, including 72 patients with prostate cancer, 33 age-matched healthy volunteers, and 10 samples from a prospective double-blind cohort. Details of all samples are provided in Supplementary Tables 2–4. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (approval number: 2020(0266)). All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the approved protocols and relevant ethical guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection.

Processing of whole blood samples for serum and plasma separation

Since clinical whole blood contains numerous cellular components, separation is required prior to biomarker analysis. Briefly, whole blood samples were collected and allowed to coagulate at room temperature for 30–60 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of whole blood was centrifuged at 27 × g for 5 min at room temperature. The resulting serum supernatant was carefully collected and stored at –80 °C for subsequent PSA analysis. The remaining blood fraction was processed further for plasma collection and RNA extraction.

PSA detection from serum

For PSA detection, 50 μL of the stored serum was diluted with an equivalent volume of 0.01 M PBS (pH 7.4) (resulting in a 1:1 dilution) prior to incubation on the device. This mild dilution was employed to mitigate potential matrix effects and ensure consistent assay performance while still operating within a clinically relevant matrix.

Total RNA extraction from Whole blood

Total RNA was extracted from the plasma fraction using the Spin Column Blood Total RNA Purification Kit. The extracted RNA was treated with RNase-Free DNase to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. The RNA purity was verified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), showing an OD₂₆₀/OD₂₈₀ ratio of 2.0, confirming that the extracted RNA was of sufficient purity for the intended electrical analyses. The purified RNA was stabilized in RNA-EZ reagent, aliquoted, and stored at –80 °C. Both PCA3 and miRNA-198 real sample tests were performed using the total RNA solution obtained from this process.

Electrical measurements

Ionic current signals were recorded using a Keithley 6487 picoammeter (Keithley Instruments, Cleveland, OH). Current-voltage curves were obtained over a potential range of –1.0 to +1.0 V with a scan rate of 0.05 V/s and a step size of 0.1 V. Prior to measurement, the inlet window on the top surface of the DNA-functionalized GO membrane was replaced with biomarker solutions of varying concentrations and incubated for 20 min to allow sufficient binding and ionic current response. For clinical specimen testing, the inlet window was filled with 50 μL of clinical serum samples or 20 μL of total RNA extraction solution. After incubation, the inlet window was rinsed three times with 1 × 10–6 M KCl solution to remove non-specifically bound species. All electrical measurements were then performed in fresh 1 × 10–6 M unbuffered KCl (pH~6), a condition under which PSA exhibits a net positive charge, as confirmed by the zeta potential measurements (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Machine learning for prostate cancer diagnosis and classification

In this study, an artificial neural network (NN) model was constructed to integrate the excitatory and inhibitory indices derived from the three prostate cancer–associated biomarkers. For each clinical specimen, the quantitative output of each biomarker was represented as βᵢ, and the three biomarker indices were assembled into a feature vector β = [β₁, β₂, β₃] that served as the input layer of the NN. To ensure a model architecture with low complexity and minimal risk of overfitting, the network was designed with a single hidden layer containing only a small number of adjustable parameters, enabling the model to capture nonlinear relationships among biomarkers while avoiding unnecessary model capacity. A total of 115 clinical specimens were used in the machine learning analysis. These specimens were randomly assigned to three independent cohorts, including a 75-sample training cohort used to optimize the model parameters, a 30-sample validation cohort used to determine the optimal classification threshold based on the ROC curve and Youden’s index, and a 10-sample double-blind test cohort reserved exclusively for final performance evaluation. The training procedure involved iteratively updating the model parameters using standard backpropagation until convergence. The validation cohort was not used during parameter optimization and served solely to determine the classification cut-off based on the NN output, ensuring that threshold determination was unbiased by the training process. After the model architecture, weight parameters, and cut-off values were fixed, the NN was applied without modification to the double-blind test cohort in order to rigorously assess diagnostic performance in an unbiased setting. This three-stage workflow, comprising separate training, validation, and double-blind testing, provides a robust framework for minimizing overfitting and ensuring objective evaluation of model generalizability. All machine-learning analyses were performed in the IBM SPSS Statistics environment using its built-in tools.

Statistical analysis

The specific electrical signals generated by biomarkers were analyzed using Origin 2021 (https://www.originlab.com/). For the comparison of expression levels of three biomarkers in prostate cancer patients and healthy specimens, t-tests were utilized. ROC curves were constructed to evaluate the accuracy of prostate cancer diagnosis and classification. Sensitivity was defined as the probability that a test result was positive when cancer was detected (true positive rate), specificity as the probability that a test result was negative when cancer was not detected (true negative rate), and accuracy as the overall probability that an individual was correctly classified. Significance analyses, construction of ROC curves, and AUC calculation were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.
