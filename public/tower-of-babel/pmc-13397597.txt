Assessing the preparedness of hospitals for road traffic accident in Central Africa: a hospital-based cross-sectional study in Cameroon and the South Kivu province in the Democratic Republic of Congo
Authors: Augustin Murhabazi Bashombwa, Ketina Hirma Tchio-Nighie, Etienne Guenou, Jerome Ateudjieu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRoad traffic accidents (RTA) are a public health concern worldwide, resulting in numerous medical emergencies. Well-prepared health systems and hospitals with sufficient human and material resources are essential for the management of road traffic injuries (RTI). The aim of this study was to assess preparedness of health facilities to provide adequate care to patients suffering from RTI.MethodsThis was a descriptive cross-sectional study targeting all hospitals located along the road axes most involved in RTAs in Cameroon (Yaoundé- Douala-Bafoussam axes) and in South Kivu province in Democratic Republic of Congo (DR Congo) (Uvira-Bukavu-Goma axes). Data were collected using a questionnaire administered face-to-face to hospital managers on the existence of an RTA management policy, human and material resources, infrastructure and staff training needs.ResultsA total of 53 hospitals were covered, including 30 (56.6%) in Cameroon and 23 (43.4%) in South Kivu - DR Congo. Of these 53 facilities, 46 (86.8%) were district hospitals. Twenty-nine hospitals (54.7%) had a RTA response plan, 38 (71.7%) had a strategy for managing indigent patients, 40 (97.6%) had a RTA communication plan, and 9 (17.0) had a staff training plan on RTA accident management. Regarding facility capacity, only 24 of the 53 hospitals surveyed (45.3%) met the minimum service requirements for managing RTAs cases. These essential services were disproportionately concentrated in higher-level facilities, being available in all national hospitals (100%) and in 40% of regional hospitals. Similarly, only seven hospitals (13.2%) met the minimum human resource requirements necessary for appropriate RTA care. This workforce capacity was more common in national hospitals, where half (50.0%) met these criteria.ConclusionHospitals are inadequately prepared to respond to road traffic accidents due to deficiencies in response planning and the insufficient availability of both material and human resources required for effective accident management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14743-4.

Background

Road traffic accidents (RTAs) are a major health problem worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), in 2021, RTAs caused almost 1.19 million deaths worldwide and around 20 to 50 million people suffered trauma and sequelae due to road traffic accidents [2]. Among young people aged 5 to 29, RTAs represent the leading cause of death and the 12th leading cause of death for all ages combined [2]. Despite accounting for less than 1% of all vehicles worldwide, developing countries account for over 90% of road traffic fatalities worldwide [2]. Nearly 2 in 10 road traffic fatalities worldwide occur in Africa [2]. In addition to having a higher rate of morbidity and mortality linked to RTAs in Africa, sub-Saharan African countries not only have the greatest disparities in healthcare but also some of the least developed healthcare delivery systems on the continent [3]. Additionally, the rapid increase in motorcycle use for rural and urban transport has led to more frequent incidents resulting in death and injury, particularly among young people [4–6]. In some Central African countries, such as Democratic Republic of Congo (DR Congo) and Cameroon, RTAs are the leading cause of trauma [7]. It has been shown that the number of people who die in RTAs in Cameroon is 35 times higher than that reported on similar roads in Western countries, and these deaths generally involve vulnerable road users (pedestrians, motorcycle drivers and their clients) [8].

National and international legislation has been developed to prevent RTAs [2, 9, 10]. Additionally, educational and enforcement measures have been implemented at international level with the same goal [2]. In Cameroon and the DR Congo, efforts have been made to enhance the prevention of road traffic accidents as well as the clinical care provided to injured patients. These efforts include constructing and upgrading healthcare facilities, providing essential medical equipment, and strengthening road safety and security measures. However, despite these efforts, hospital mortality rates from RTAs remain high [11–14]. This persistent burden may be attributed to resource shortages, inequitable distribution of available resources, and insufficient training of healthcare personnel. Additionally, significant implementation gaps continue to undermine the effectiveness of existing road safety and trauma care strategies. Road safety regulations including speed limits, drunk-driving laws, seat belt and helmet use, and driver licensing standards are inadequately enforced [2]. Concurrently, trauma care delivery faces structural constraints such as limited health budgets, maldistribution of human and material resources, and insufficient technical capacity to provide timely and comprehensive management of accident victims [15–21].

Studies conducted in several African countries have identified weaknesses in both the organization and delivery of trauma care, particularly concerning the availability of trained health personnel, the structure of prehospital services, and the quality of in-hospital management of injured patients [11, 22–24].

However, most of these studies have primarily focused on prehospital care or have been limited to single health facilities, thereby restricting the broader applicability of their findings.

To the best of our knowledge, no studies have yet been conducted on the preparedness of hospitals and their capacity to respond to road traffic accidents in either Cameroon or the DR Congo.

Assessing the preparedness of health facilities to respond to road traffic accidents is essential for generating evidence that can inform planning, anticipate resource needs, and guide efforts to strengthen the health system. This includes assessing the availability of a response plan, a communication plan, and a staff training plan, as well as the availability of human resources and essential services for managing RTAs within hospitals.

Therefore, this study was designed to assess the preparedness of health facilities and their capacity to manage RTIs along the most accident-prone roads in Cameroon and the South Kivu province of the DR Congo.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional, hospital-based study targeting hospitals on roads with highest road traffic accidents’ incidence in Cameroon and DR Congo. Data were collected between May 2024 and March 2025. All district, regional, and central hospitals located along the targeted roads were included. A pre-tested questionnaire was administered face-to-face to health facility managers on the level of preparedness of hospitals to respond to road traffic accidents.

Study setting

The study was carried out in two Central African countries: Cameroon and the DR Congo. Cameroon, a Central African country, had an estimated population of 29.8 million in 2025 [25]. Its road network spans approximately 121,873 km, of which 10,575.94 km are paved [26]. The most recent official estimate, dating from 2021, is that 2870 people were killed in road accidents [27–29]. In Cameroon, the study was carried out in the district, regional and central (referral) hospitals located along the Yaoundé-Bafoussam-Douala roads axes. The cities of Yaoundé (political capital), Douala (economic capital) and Bafoussam are respectively the capital cities of the Centre, Littoral and West regions of Cameroon. These roads were chosen because of their high incidence of traffic accidents in Cameroon.

The DR Congo is a Central African country with an estimated population of approximately 112,800 million in 2025 [30]. Its road network, one of the largest on the continent, spans about 153,209 km, of which roughly 3,000 km are paved [31]. This study was conducted in the South Kivu province of the DR Congo. The province of South Kivu is one of the 26 provinces of the DR Congo. It covers 3% of the country’s surface area, or 69,130 km². In 2017, South Kivu’s population was estimated at 8 million. Its road network covers 2,715 km, of which 20.6% is in good condition [32].

As in the country’s other provinces, road safety is still a problem, despite the existence of a number of regulations governing road traffic. In this province, the study was carried out in the district, regional and central hospitals located along the Uvira-Bukavu-Goma road axes, which are most affected by traffic accidents in South Kivu province. Population and sampling

The study population was made up of hospitals located on the roads most involved in road traffic accidents in Cameroon and the Democratic Republic of Congo. To be included, hospitals had to meet the following criteria: be located on the Yaoundé-Bafoussam-Douala highway in Cameroon or on the Uvira-Bukavu-Goma highway in South Kivu, DR Congo; be at least a district hospital or similar and offer health care to victims of road traffic accidents. Hospitals whose managers refused to participate in the study and those that were inaccessible geographically or for security reasons were excluded. All hospitals that respected the inclusion criteria were comprehensively included. All eligible hospitals located along the targeted roads were selected using an exhaustive sampling. In Cameroon and the Democratic Republic of Congo, the health system is organized as a pyramid. The first level is the health district, which includes the district hospital which is the biggest health facility at this level. The second level is the intermediate level, comprising regional (or provincial) hospitals, which serve as the largest healthcare facilities within their regions. The third level is the central level, consisting of national referral healthcare facilities [28, 33].

Data collection

Data collection tool

Data were collected using a face-to-face questionnaire (supplementary material) administered to health facility managers on the level of preparedness of hospitals to respond to road accidents. The questionnaire was specifically developed for this study by our research team and had not been previously published nor adapted from any existing tool. It was pre-tested among hospital and services managers at two hospitals in the city of Yaoundé that were not included in the study, prior to the start of data collection. This pretest was conducted to improve data quality, identify ambiguous questions, and correct errors in question phrasing. The main areas covered by the questionnaire included the general characteristics of hospitals, the response plan for RTAs, the availability of an RTA communication plan, and the provision of care for RTA victims. The availability of a response plan, including training for healthcare personnel, and the presence of a communication plan were among the key factors addressed in preparing hospitals for road traffic accidents. In this study, the communication plan was considered to be any structured document that defines all the actions, messages, targets, and timetable to be implemented for better communication about road traffic accidents. The questions used to assess hospitals’ capacity to respond to RTAs focused on the availability of services such as emergency departments, operating rooms, imaging facilities, blood banks, and ambulances and the availability of human resources, including nurse anesthetists or anesthesiologists, radiologists, general surgeons, trauma surgeons, and emergency physicians.

Data collection procedure

Upon obtaining all necessary authorizations, the data collection team, consisting of trained enumerators, proceeded to the healthcare facility to collect data. At each facility, the team approached the person in charge or their designated representative to explain the study objectives and data collection procedures. After obtaining informed consent, a member of the data collection team administered the questionnaire through face-to-face interviews with the respondents. All information provided by hospital or department managers on the availability of plans and resources was verified by a data collection team member through direct observation.

Data analysis

Data were collected on smartphones using the Open Data Kit (ODK) application and stored in a secure online database accessible only to the study investigators. Data were collected using the ODK, an open-source suite of software tools designed for data collection, management, and utilization in resource-limited settings. Data were submitted daily to a secure online database at each site. Subsequently, the data manager conducted daily reviews to identify any inconsistencies, which were then communicated to the field teams to improve data collection quality. Data were summarized in tables and figures as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and as means with standard deviations for continuous variables. The results were subsequently stratified by country and by category of health facility. The availability of minimum services was defined as having an emergency department with an on-call team; an operating room; an imaging department equipped with at least radiography, ultrasound, and CT scan for regional and national hospitals; access to a blood bank; and a functioning ambulance. This variable was coded as 1 when all five services were present and 0 when any of them was absent.

Similarly, the availability of minimum human resources was defined as the presence of a general surgeon, nurse anesthetist or anesthesiologist, and radiologist. This variable was coded as 1 when all three professionals were present and 0 when any of them was absent. Data analysis was done using Epi-info 7.2 version, Centre for Disease Control and Prevention Atlanta Georgia software [34].

Results

Presentation and coverage of hospitals

The study targeted 55 hospitals, 24 in the DR Congo and 31 in Cameroon. Of these 55 hospitals, 53 (96.4%) consented to participate, 30 (56.6%) from Cameroon and 23 (43.4%) from South Kivu, DR Congo. Twenty-eight hospitals (52.8%) belonged to the public sector, and 46 (86.8%) were district hospitals. Availability of road traffic accident response plan

Of the 53 hospitals covered by the study, 29 had a road traffic accident response plan, including 65.2% in South Kivu, DR Congo and 46.7% in Cameroon. Thirty-eight (71.7%) of the hospitals had a mechanism for handling indigent patients, including 73.3% of hospitals in Cameroon. Out of 53 hospitals, only 14 (26.4%) had a communication plan on road traffic accidents. Thirty hospitals (56.6%) had a sign indicating their presence on the road, including 16 (69.6%) in South Kivu in DR Congo.

Regarding the category of health facilities, we noted that 29 (54.7%) hospitals reported to have a RTA response plan. Of the 29 hospitals, all regional hospitals had one, whereas only 50.0% of national and health district hospitals had the RTA response plan. The RTA communication plan was available in one national hospital (50.0%) and 2 regional hospitals (40.0%). With regard to the availability of a training plan on RTAs, all national hospitals and 7 (15.2%) district hospitals had a training plan for teams on RTA management. Availability of infrastructure, material and human resources for the response to road traffic accidents

Overall, only 24 out of 53 hospitals (45.3%) had the minimum services (emergency department with on-call team; operating room; imaging department with at least radiography, ultrasound and CT scan for regional and national hospitals; access to blood bank, and operational ambulance) required to manage RTAs, of which 13 (43.3%) were located in Cameroon. Furthermore, only 7 hospitals (13.2%) had the minimum human resources (general surgeon, nurse or medical anesthetist, radiologist) required to manage RTAs, with 5 (16.7%) located in Cameroon.

All central-level hospitals had the minimum services necessary to manage RTAs, compared to only 20 district-level hospitals (43.5%). Additionally, one central-level hospital (50.0%) had the minimum human resources required to treat RTAs, compared to five district-level health facilities (10.9%).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to assess the level of preparedness of hospitals to respond to road traffic accidents on major roads in Cameroon and in the province of South Kivu in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DR Congo).

We note that of the 53 hospitals included in the study, 29 (54.7%) had a RTAs response plan. Regarding resources, we note that only 24 out of 53 hospitals (45.3%) had the minimum services required to manage road traffic accidents (RTAs). These services include an emergency department with an on-call team, an operating room, an imaging department equipped with at least radiography, ultrasound, and CT scan (for regional and national hospitals), access to a blood bank, and an operational ambulance.

Furthermore, only seven hospitals (13.2%) had the minimum required human resources (a general surgeon, an anesthetist, and a radiologist) to manage road traffic accidents (RTAs).

There was a predominance of central and regional hospitals in the availability of resources for treating victims of road traffic accidents.

Hospitals play a vital role in the response to road traffic accidents, particularly as they are able to treat the traumas associated with road traffic accidents [35].

The findings of this study show that only 54.7% of hospitals had a road traffic accident response plan, and only 26.4% had a road traffic accident communication plan. These results are considerably higher than those reported in a study conducted in Botswana, which documented the absence of formal trauma administrative functions within hospitals [36]. These discrepancies may be explained by differences in study scope and setting. The present study focused exclusively on RTIs and did not include facilities below the district hospital level, whereas the Botswana study examined trauma more broadly and included many lower-level facilities, which are less likely to have the technical capacity to develop and implement such response plans. The results indicate that, despite the high frequency of such accidents encountered by healthcare facilities, hospitals are not only inadequately prepared but also lack planned response and communication strategies in approximately half of all healthcare institutions. This deficiency could adversely affect resource management, coordination, and victim care, particularly during mass casualty incidents. To ensure effective preparedness and response to road traffic accidents, it is essential for hospital administrators, healthcare institution leaders, and their partners to develop and implement a comprehensive road traffic accident response plan.

Communication is an essential element in the response to road traffic accidents [37]. In the hospital context, communication helps to inform hospital users and the general public not only about the services offered by the hospital, but also about the danger of road traffic accidents and the need to prevent them [38]. In this study, we note that a communication plan on road traffic accidents was available in only 26.4% of hospitals, while 56.6% of hospitals had a sign indicating their presence on the road. This shows that some hospitals are communicating without no communication plan. It also indicates that more needs to be done for the planning and implementation of communication activities related to RTAs in and outside hospitals.

Findings from this study show that 17.0% of hospitals had a staff training plan on road traffic accident management. These results differ from those of studies conducted in Ghana and Ethiopia, where more than 70% of health facilities provided training to health personnel [39, 40]. This difference may be attributed, on one hand, to the fact that these studies were conducted in large hospitals, and on the other hand, to variations in how staff training was assessed. These results show that there are shortcomings in the capacity of hospitals to strengthen the skills of health personnel in the management of RTAs. In fact, the training plan was most available in the national level hospitals. This finding highlights disparities in human resource management between central and peripheral levels and underscores the need to strengthen RTA response through the implementation of continuous training programs for healthcare workers in facilities providing care to RTA victims in this resource-limited setting.

It is important that good health care services have good human and material resources for the care of RTA victims [41]. However, in the African context, hospitals are often unprepared to deal with accidents, notably due to the paucity of material, financial or specialized human resources [42].

The findings of this study reveal that only 45.3% of hospitals had the minimum services (These services include an emergency department with an on-call team, an operating room, an imaging department equipped with at least radiography, ultrasound, and CT scan for regional and national hospitals, access to a blood bank, and an operational ambulance) required to manage road traffic accidents (RTAs) and only 13.2% had the minimum required human resources (general surgeon, an anesthetist, and a radiologist) to manage road traffic accidents (RTAs). These results are consistent with those of a study conducted in Cameroon by Chiyom-Mefire, which found that no hospital possessed the recommended infrastructure for emergency and surgical care [43]. In contrast, these results are lower than those reported in Malawi, where human resources were more readily available [44]. This difference may be explained by variations in hospital sample size and inclusion criteria between our study and the one conducted in Malawi.

The findings highlight three critical concerns: firstly, only a small fraction of hospitals meet the minimum service requirements for managing trauma associated with road traffic accidents (RTAs). Secondly, a limited number of hospitals have the essential human resources needed to adequately care for RTA victims. Thirdly, there exists a substantial handicap in hospitals’ capabilities to deliver surgical treatment, resuscitation, and precise diagnosis of fractures and other injuries resulting from RTAs. Together, these findings raise concerns about hospitals’ ability to deliver quality care to accident victims. Healthcare institution managers must ensure that hospitals treating accident victims have the essential human resources needed for effective care. This can be achieved by enhancing healthcare personnel capabilities through continuing education, specialized initial training, or recruiting additional specialized staff.

Study limitations

This study may have certain weaknesses due to the use of the survey questionnaire, which could generate a certain number of biases, notably memory bias. To minimize these biases, an observation was made to ensure the veracity of the information provided by health facility managers, particularly with regard to services. Furthermore, the results of this study may have limited generalizability to all hospitals across Cameroon and the Democratic Republic of Congo, given that the research was confined to a distinct number of regions. Nevertheless, the regions incorporated in the study are characterized by a particularly high incidence of road traffic accidents, thereby increasing the applicability of the findings to the areas most impacted by this public health issue.

Conclusion

Overall, the level of preparedness of hospitals to respond to road traffic accidents was low, especially as most hospitals lacked the necessary resources to deal with road traffic accidents. Around 5 out of ten hospitals had a road traffic accident response plan, and fewer than 3 out of ten hospitals had an accident communication plan. Only 17% of hospitals had a road traffic accident training plan for their teams. Regarding the availability of resources for road traffic accident management, fewer than one in ten hospitals possessed the minimum services required to care for road traffic accident victims. At least four in ten hospitals had the minimum human resources necessary to manage these cases. Health authorities should prioritize the provision of both human and material resources, as well as the development of a comprehensive road traffic management policy for hospitals located along roads with high accident rates. This approach will enhance their preparedness and capacity to respond effectively to road traffic accidents. Additionally, further research should be conducted to assess the knowledge level of healthcare personnel regarding road traffic accident preparedness and response in hospitals situated along these roads.
