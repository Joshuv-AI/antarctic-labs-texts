Bee ovum-inspired hydrogel programs microenvironment remodeling for diabetic wound healing
Authors: Yanan Xue, Ying Lu, Yiran Lin, Junping Zhou, Kan Zhan, Cheng Chen, Huiling Zhang, Bailin Chen, Xue Cai, Qiuping Zhou, Lei Pan, Yi Sun, Sufan Wu, Zhiqiang Liu, Liqun Jin, Yuguo Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic diabetic wounds heal poorly due to a persistently imbalanced microenvironment involving inflammation and bacterial infection. Notably, oxidative stress, driven by mitochondrial damage, perpetuates inflammation and hinders repair. Existing therapeutic materials struggle to simultaneously address infection, inflammation, and oxidative stress through combined drug delivery and targeted activation of mitophagy. To tackle these intertwined challenges, we designed a bee ovum-inspired hydrogel (BOV) that mimics the parasitic wasp egg strategy, firm host adhesion and staged bioactive secretion, to programmatically remodel the wound microenvironment. The BOV consists of a borate-ester-crosslinked hyaluronic acid network providing robust wet adhesion and self-healing properties. It encapsulates gelatin-coated ZIF-8@Myricetin (Myr) nanoparticles and polyhexamethylene biguanide (PHMB), which are released sequentially in response to the wound's acidic, high-reactive oxygen species (ROS), and high-matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) microenvironment: PHMB first exerts antibacterial action to control infection, followed by Myricetin release to scavenge ROS and suppress inflammation. Beyond antioxidant effects, BOV further activates the SIRT1/FOXO3a/BNIP3 pathway to promote mitophagy, clearing damaged mitochondria and thereby mitigating oxidative stress at its source. In vivo, BOV reduced bacterial burden, alleviated inflammatory response, and enhanced collagen deposition, and re-epithelialization. This study translates a natural parasitic strategy into a programmable drug-delivery platform, offering a promising approach for refractory diabetic wound therapy through microenvironment-responsive sequential treatment and upstream mitochondrial homeostasis restoration.

Introduction

Chronic diabetic wounds are notoriously difficult to heal, a condition fundamentally driven by a vicious cycle of microenvironmental imbalance where pathological factors such as infection and sustained inflammation reinforce one another. Notably, oxidative stress, fueled by mitochondrial damage, is not merely a key pathological factor but also perpetuates inflammatory responses and directly impedes the repair process, creating a self-sustaining cycle that is exceptionally hard to break. The crux of the challenge is that existing therapeutic materials struggle to concurrently address infection, inflammation, and the root cause of oxidative stress. They typically lack both the capacity for combination drug delivery and the ability to target and activate cellular processes like mitophagy [[1], [2], [3]]. Consequently, the ideal therapeutic platform must not only possess suitable physical properties but, more critically, must intelligently sense and respond to the dynamic wound state, delivering stage-specific therapies while actively reprogramming cellular homeostasis to disrupt the vicious cycle at multiple levels [4,5].

Although the complex structures and functions in nature provide inspiration for advanced biomaterials, and designs inspired by octopus suckers or spider silk have yielded impressive mechanical or adhesive properties, such approaches often address only singular facets of this multifaceted problem [6,7]. In contrast, most existing biomimetic dressings fall short of this integrated goal. They either focus on mimicking a single biophysical property [8,9], or, although integrating multiple functions, fail to align closely with the dynamic progression of wound healing, particularly lacking the ability to actively intervene in and regulate the host cells' intrinsic repair programs [10]. This frequently leads to a misalignment between therapeutic action and physiological need, compromising outcomes [11,12]. Thus, the pivotal unmet need for a material system that can sense, execute sequenced actions, and reprogram homeostasis hinges on the integration of multiple capabilities, specifically tissue retention, robust adhesion, sequential secretion, and active modulation. The oviposition strategy of parasitic wasps provides a compelling blueprint for such an integrated system. It operates on an elegant logic of anchoring followed by stagewise manipulation. This involves precise physical anchoring and deep tissue deployment via the wasp's ovipositor, followed by timed secretion of bioactive factors such as venoms and polydnaviruses. Through this sequential strategy, parasitic wasps dynamically modulate the host microenvironment, toggling between immunosuppression and immunomodulation to ensure offspring survival [13,14]. We therefore hypothesize that translating this biological logic into a therapeutic material could systematically address the core challenges in diabetic wound management.

Inspired by this, we developed a Bee-Ovum-inspired Hydrogel (BOV). Its design centers on integrating macroscopic logic for spatiotemporal drug delivery with microscopic capability for activating cellular pathways (Scheme 1). The core biomimetic translation involves two key aspects: To realize the concept of a dynamic boronic ester-crosslinked network, inspired by the robust yet adaptable attachment of the parasitoid wasp egg, we selected hyaluronic acid (HA) as the foundational polymer. This choice was based on its excellent biocompatibility and ease of functional modification [15,16]. Specifically, dopamine (DA) and 3-aminophenylboronic acid (3-APBA) were grafted onto HA chains to construct HA-DA for intrinsic wet-tissue adhesion via catechol chemistry and HA-PBA for interfacial reinforcement via dynamic boronic ester bonding [[17], [18], [19]]. Through dynamic boronic ester crosslinking, these modules form an injectable, self-healing hydrogel matrix that also exhibits responsive degradation to the acidic and high-ROS wound microenvironment [20]. Furthermore, to mimic the staged secretion strategy, we engineered a cascaded drug delivery system. The antimicrobial agent polyhexamethylene biguanide hydrochloride (PHMB) is directly loaded for rapid release triggered by the acidic/high-ROS conditions of the early infection phase. Meanwhile, the antioxidant myricetin (Myr) is encapsulated in gelatin-coated ZIF-8 nanoparticles (GZ@M) for delayed release in response to the highly expressed matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) in the subsequent inflammatory phase [21,22]. Beyond this sequential sense and respond drug release, we further researched BOV to actively reprogram cellular homeostasis. Specifically, we investigated its potential to upregulate mitophagy via the SIRT1/FOXO3a/BNIP3 pathway, thereby enhancing clearance of damaged mitochondria to mitigate oxidative stress at its source. In vivo experiments confirmed that BOV significantly promoted wound healing in S. aureus-infected diabetic rats, as evidenced by accelerated bacterial clearance, attenuated inflammation, promoted ordered collagen deposition, and enhanced angiogenesis.

Biomimetic design and mechanism of the bee-ovum-inspired (BOV) hydrogel. (A) Schematic of the BOV hydrogel fabrication. (B) Biomimetic characteristics and application mechanism of BOV.

In summary, inspired by the colonization strategy of parasitoid wasp eggs, this study constructs a biomimetic therapeutic system that achieves closed-loop functionality encompassing microenvironment sensing, dynamic response, sequential drug delivery, and active cellular programming. Its innovativeness lies not only in enabling intelligent, temporal therapy, but more critically, in directly reprogramming endogenous repair processes. Mechanistically, the healing benefits are attributed to BOV's dual action: the programmed sequential delivery of antimicrobial and antioxidant agents first controls the external pathological microenvironment (infection, ROS), while the active induction of mitophagy via the SIRT1/FOXO3a/BNIP3 pathway resolves internal cellular dysfunction at its source. Together, this work provides a novel material design strategy that actively intervenes in the pathological cascade of chronic wounds, offering a systematic approach to break the cycle of dysregulated healing.

Result

Biomimetic design, synthesis, and structural validation

Parasitic wasps target hosts by detecting plant volatiles and secrete an adhesive fluid during egg deposition. Inspired by this dual strategy, we designed a dynamic hydrogel with robust wet adhesion and microenvironment-adaptive properties. The matrix was constructed by grafting 3-APBA and DA onto hyaluronic acid (HA) to form HA-PBA and HA-DA [23,24], which were then cross-linked via dynamic boronate ester bonds between PBA and catechol groups to create the HPD network (Fig. 1A); Successful synthesis was confirmed by 1H NMR and FT-IR, the latter showing a characteristic B–O–C band at 1153 cm−1 (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. 1). In this network, HA-PBA further enhances wet tissue adhesion and interfacial adhesion durability through reversible boronate ester interactions, building upon the intrinsic adhesiveness of HA-DA conferred by covalent and non-covalent interactions [25]. Subsequently, HPD1-HPD4 hydrogels were prepared by mixing HA-PBA (3% w/v) and HA-DA (2% w/v) solutions at volume ratios of 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, and 2:3, respectively, for systematic optimization. Mechanical and lap-shear tests showed that the fracture and adhesion strengths of the hydrogels increased with HA-DA content (Fig. 1C–G).

With increasing HA-DA content, the enhanced mechanical and adhesive performance of the HPD hydrogels was primarily associated with the greater catechol-group density, which promotes covalent interactions, such as Michael addition, and non-covalent interactions, including π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding. After eight consecutive adhesion-detachment cycles, HPD4 retained significantly higher adhesive strength than the non-PBA HADA hydrogel (Supplementary Fig. 2), indicating that the reversible PBA-catechol network contributes to interfacial adhesion durability under repeated mechanical loading [26]. This enhanced adhesion persistence may facilitate prolonged contact with the moist wound surface (Fig. 1I).

Furthermore, HPD4 maintained stable adhesion across diverse substrates, underwater, and under dynamic skin deformation, confirming its robust wet adhesion and surface compatibility (Fig. 1J, Supplementary Figs. 3–4). Swelling assays further supported its selection as the drug carrier. While HPD2 showed the highest swelling ratio (1761.32%), which risks premature drug burst release, HPD4 exhibited more moderate and controllable swelling due to its higher crosslink density (Fig. 1H), providing a suitable basis for sustained drug release.

Building on the previous bioinspired adhesive matrix, we further engineered a functional core that emulates the parasitic wasp egg. This smart core is designed to mimic the on-demand drug release of wasp eggs and their ability to switch between antimicrobial and immunomodulatory actions, enabling adaptive, proactive regulation of the local microenvironment.

Its foundation consists of ZIF-8 nanoparticles loaded with the antioxidant myricetin (ZIF-8@Myr, Z@M). Gelatin was subsequently employed for surface coating to obtain Gel/ZIF-8@Myr (GZ@M), thereby constructing a matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9)-responsive shell (Fig. 2A) [27]. Transmission electron microscopy confirmed that the resulting Z@M nanoparticles exhibited regular hexagonal morphologies with sizes of approximately 80–100 nm (Fig. 2B). The GZ@M particles increased in size to 100–120 nm with enhanced surface roughness (Supplementary Fig. 5), indicating that gelatin was successfully anchored onto the ZIF-8 surface via physical adsorption and weak covalent cross-linking, forming a distinct core-shell structure (Fig. 2E). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis revealed significantly enhanced C and N signals in GZ@M, further corroborating the gelatin modification (Fig. 2C–D, 2F-G). Zeta potential analysis further elucidated the evolution of surface properties: pristine ZIF-8 was positively charged (+14.93 mV), whereas loading with Myr reversed the surface charge to negative (−15.4 mV), evidencing successful drug encapsulation. Subsequent gelatin coating shifted the potential further to −20 mV, which revealed the interactions with biological surfaces (Fig. 2H). X-ray diffraction (XRD) indicated that the coating process preserved the crystalline structure of ZIF-8, with alterations largely confined to surface characteristics (Fig. 2I). FT-IR spectra displayed a new absorption band at 1716 cm−1 in Z@M and GZ@M, corresponding to the C=O stretch of Myr (Fig. 2K), further confirming the Myr successful loading. Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analysis indicated reduced surface area and pore volume in GZ@M due to pore blocking by gelatin, a change favorable for sustained and enzyme-triggered drug release (Fig. 2J; Supplementary Fig. 6). The dispersibility and stability of GZ@M were then evaluated. A clear Tyndall effect confirmed its excellent dispersion in aqueous solution (Fig. 2L). More importantly, GZ@M showed markedly enhanced stability over Z@M under physiological conditions. After 24 h, while Z@M had largely degraded (faint Tyndall signal), GZ@M maintained a strong signal, indicating sustained colloidal integrity. Building on its excellent dispersion and stability, we characterized the microenvironment-responsive release of Myr from GZ@M (Fig. 2M). At physiological pH 7.4, Myr release was minimal (41.75% at 48 h). Release was accelerated in the presence of MMP-9 (54.23%) and further under acidic conditions (pH 6.0). The highest cumulative release (83.53%) occurred under the combined stimulus of pH 6.0 and MMP-9, demonstrating that GZ@M preferentially releases its payload in the acidic, enzyme-rich microenvironment typical of inflamed wounds.

Intelligent responsiveness and biomimetic programmed release

Parasitic wasps exhibit a remarkable ability to dynamically adapt to host tissues: they sense tissue stiffness and finely adjust the curvature of their ovipositor for precise penetration, while rapidly repairing eggshell microcracks through conformational rearrangement of surface proteins. Inspired by this dual capability of shape adaptation and autonomous self-repair, based on optimized performance, HPD4 was selected as the matrix to construct the final multifunctional hydrogel, termed BOV. BOV was prepared via a one-pot method by co-incorporating the antimicrobial agent polyhexamethylene biguanide hydrochloride (PHMB) and GZ@M into the HA-DA/HA-PBA precursor solution, followed by cross-linking at room temperature. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that BOV possesses a uniform 3D porous network (pore size: 30-50 μm), conducive to nutrient exchange, cell migration, and drug diffusion (Fig. 3A). In oscillatory strain recovery tests, the material's storage modulus (G′) rapidly recovered after the cessation of high shear strain, corroborating its self-healing capability. Consequently, severed hydrogel fragments rapidly re-fused into an integral whole within minutes, demonstrating robust structural recovery akin to the biological prototype (Fig. 3B–D). Mechanistically, this self-healing behavior arises from the dynamic cross-linked network, which involves polymer chain diffusion, reversible boronic ester bonds, and hydrogen bonding interactions. Building on this dynamic network, the hydrogel also demonstrates excellent injectability, it can be smoothly extruded through fine needles (inner diameter 0.45 mm) to form customized shapes such as “ZJUT” (Supplementary Fig. 7). The network further confers shape-adaptive and migratory properties (Fig. 3E). Importantly, the degradation rate of BOV can be tuned by key wound signals like reactive oxygen species and acidic pH (Supplementary Fig. 8), thereby mimicking biological responsiveness to microenvironmental cues.

Similarly, parasitic wasps modulate anti-inflammatory factor release in response to host immune fluctuations, sustaining a hypoimmune microenvironment for larval development. Inspired by this on-demand release strategy, we evaluated the sequential drug release behavior of BOV under simulated diabetic wound conditions. Under varying pH and ROS conditions, the release profiles of PHMB revealed distinct patterns: at pH 6.0, the cumulative 12-h release reached 24.97%, significantly higher than 15.41% under physiological pH 7.4. In the presence of both pH 6.0 and 1 mM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), release was further accelerated, with a 12-h cumulative release of 55.14%, clearly attributable to the dual responsiveness of dynamic boronic ester bonds to acidic and high-ROS microenvironments (Fig. 3F). In contrast, Myr exhibited a different temporal release profile (Fig. 3G): during the first 12 h, Myr release was slow, but under a simulated late-inflammatory microenvironment (containing MMP-9, pH 6.0, and 1 mM H2O2), the 36-h cumulative release increased markedly to 72.29%. This on-demand release behavior originates from the design of GZ@M nanoparticles, whose gelatin shell is specifically cleaved by MMP-9. To further visualize the stimulus-responsive release behavior of BOV, Cy5-labeled PHMB and ICG-azithromycin, used as a fluorescent tracer for the GZ@M module, were monitored using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS). The IVIS results showed that PHMB release was significantly accelerated under acidic and H2O2 conditions (Supplementary Fig. 9), while the fluorescence signal of the ICG-azithromycin tracer associated with the GZ@M module increased substantially in the presence of acidic conditions, H2O2, and MMP-9 (Fig. 3I).

To elucidate the release mechanisms of PHMB and Myr, the release kinetics were analyzed [28]. The in vitro cumulative release profiles of both drugs were well fitted by the first-order, Ritger-Peppas, and Peppas-Sahlin models. Analysis of the Ritger-Peppas release exponents indicated that both PHMB and Myr exhibited anomalous transport. However, the lower n values of PHMB were close to the Fickian-diffusion threshold, suggesting a relatively greater contribution from molecular diffusion, whereas the higher n values of Myr indicated a greater contribution from polymer relaxation (Supplementary Figs. 10–11). Collectively, although both drugs exhibited anomalous transport, their release involved different relative contributions of diffusion and matrix relaxation. The rapid, early release of PHMB was predominantly associated with molecular diffusion through the hydrogel network, with a limited contribution from matrix relaxation, whereas the delayed release of Myr showed a greater dependence on polymer relaxation and degradation of the GZ@M gelatin shell. This difference in release behavior contributes to the designed cascaded, stage-specific drug delivery. The above results demonstrate that the BOV enables intelligent responsiveness to the diabetic wound microenvironment and spatio-temporally controlled drug delivery. The core advantage of this system lies in its bioinspired, cascaded drug release logic: in the early infection stage, high ROS and low pH at the wound site trigger dissociation of boronic ester bonds, driving rapid PHMB release for potent early-stage antibacterial action; in the later stages of inflammation, MMP-9 enzymatically degrades the gelatin shell of GZ@M, initiating on-demand Myr release to exert antioxidant and pro-regenerative functions. This temporal release strategy is highly similar to the adaptive strategy of parasitic bee eggs (Fig. 3H).

In vitro assessment of cytocompatibility and antimicrobial functions

The successful application of biomimetic materials requires excellent biological safety [29]. We systematically evaluated the cell compatibility. The results of live/dead cell staining and CCK-8 assay indicated that after co-culturing with RAW 264.7 macrophages for 3 days, the cell survival rates of each hydrogel group showed no significant difference from the control, and the cell morphology was relaxed and the cell number significantly increased, which proved that the material had no acute toxicity (Supplementary Figs. 12–13). Notably, quantitative CCK-8 data indicated that cell viability in the BOV group was consistently higher than in the HPD/PHMB and HPD groups throughout the culture period, suggesting the loaded active components may provide a mild proliferative benefit beyond mere biocompatibility. To further simulate wound repair, we assessed the effect on fibroblast migration using scratch assays. Both the composite BOV and the HPD/PHMB group significantly enhanced the migration of L929 cells compared to the control and the blank HPD group, with BOV showing the fastest scratch closure rate at 24 h (Supplementary Figs. 14–15). This preliminarily demonstrates that BOV not only coexists safely with cells but also actively fosters a microenvironment conducive to tissue repair. Additionally, the in vitro hemolysis experiments confirmed that the hemolysis rate of all the hydrogel groups was below the 5% safety threshold for biological safety. Among them, the BOV had the lowest rate, at only 0.81%, indicating good compatibility with blood components (Supplementary Fig. 16), providing a basic safety guarantee for its clinical application as a wound dressing.

The continuous high sugar environment in diabetic wounds is highly likely to cause bacterial infections [30]. Therefore, BOV must have the ability to quickly eliminate pathogens and establish local defense in the early stage of application. Comparative plate-counting assays revealed that BOV achieved the highest bacterial killing ratios of approximately 89% and 92% against E. coli and S. aureus, respectively. HPD/PHMB exhibited killing ratios of approximately 72% and 83%, whereas HPD/GZ@M exhibited moderate killing ratios of approximately 61% and 59%, respectively. In comparison, the blank HPD matrix showed only limited antibacterial activity, with killing ratios of approximately 34% against E. coli and 20% against S. aureus (Fig. 4A–C, D). These component-omission results identify PHMB as the predominant antibacterial component of BOV, while GZ@M provides an additional but comparatively weaker contribution to bacterial inhibition. This finding was visually corroborated by live/dead staining, which showed extensive bacterial death following BOV treatment (Fig. 4B), and by SEM observations, which revealed severe bacterial membrane disruption and leakage of intracellular contents (Fig. 4E). The primary mechanism involves the cationic agent PHMB, which electrostatically adsorbs to and disrupts bacterial membranes, paralleling the defensive secretion of parasitic wasp eggs [31].

Notably, BOV's therapeutic superiority lies in its combinatorial strategy, which surpasses the efficacy of either functional module alone. This is particularly evident against resilient bacterial biofilms. Crystal violet staining and Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) imaging revealed that although both HPD/PHMB and HPD/GZ@M partially reduced biofilm biomass, BOV produced the greatest inhibition of biofilm formation and the most pronounced disruption of mature biofilm architecture, with dead bacteria predominating (Fig. 4F–H) [32]. This enhanced efficacy stems from the coordinated and complementary functions of the different components within BOV: while PHMB acts as the primary bactericidal agent, the GZ@M module may provide supplementary anti-biofilm activity through the combined effects of Zn2+ release and Myr. This coordinated action allows BOV to effectively suppress bacterial survival and disrupt mature biofilms [33].

Assessment of antioxidant activity and mitochondrial protective effect

Oxidative stress represents one of the core pathological barriers impeding diabetic wound healing [34]. While the individual components possess known activities, whether their integration within our BOV hydrogel provides superior antioxidant and mitochondrial-protective effects remains a key question. To evaluate this, we first assessed the in vitro antioxidant capacity of the different formulations. As shown in Fig. 5A and B, HPD and HPD/PHMB exhibited moderate and comparable ABTS and DPPH radical-scavenging activities, indicating that PHMB provided little direct contribution to free-radical scavenging. In contrast, HPD/GZ@M and BOV showed the highest radical-scavenging activities among the tested formulations, supporting GZ@M as the principal antioxidant module of BOV. Intracellular ROS levels were subsequently evaluated using DCFH-DA fluorescence imaging and flow cytometry. The Control group exhibited the strongest DCFH-DA fluorescence, indicating substantial intracellular ROS accumulation. HPD treatment produced only a modest, statistically non-significant reduction in ROS fluorescence, whereas HPD/PHMB resulted in a small but significant decrease. In comparison, HPD/GZ@M markedly reduced intracellular ROS levels, and BOV exhibited the lowest fluorescence intensity among all groups (Fig. 5C–E). These findings further support the predominant contribution of the GZ@M module to intracellular ROS regulation.

We further investigated whether this superior ROS clearance translated to protection of subcellular organelles, specifically mitochondria, which are primary targets of oxidative stress. A decline in mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) is a key hallmark of such damage. Using JC-1 staining in RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 5F), we found that the Control group exhibited relatively weak red fluorescence and strong green fluorescence, consistent with mitochondrial depolarization. HPD and HPD/PHMB provided limited recovery, whereas HPD/GZ@M substantially increased JC-1 aggregate formation. BOV produced the most favorable fluorescence profile, characterized by the strongest red fluorescence and weakest green fluorescence among the tested formulations. Flow-cytometric analysis further confirmed these observations. The proportion of cells in the Q2 quadrant increased from 21.4% in the Control group to 42.2%, 51.7%, 75.1%, and 83.7% in the HPD, HPD/PHMB, HPD/GZ@M, and BOV groups, respectively (Fig. 5G). Thus, HPD and HPD/PHMB provided partial mitochondrial protection, whereas the markedly greater recovery observed in HPD/GZ@M identifies GZ@M as the principal mitochondrial-protective module. Consistently, quantitative analysis of the JC-1 aggregate-to-monomer fluorescence ratio showed only a modest, statistically non-significant increase following HPD treatment and a small but significant increase following HPD/PHMB treatment. In contrast, HPD/GZ@M markedly increased the ratio, whereas BOV exhibited the highest numerical value among all groups (Fig. 5H, Supplementary Figs. 17–18). These results indicate that GZ@M is the principal mitochondrial-protective module and that the complete BOV formulation provides the most favorable overall mitochondrial-polarization profile.

Collectively, the component-omission results demonstrate distinct but complementary functional contributions: PHMB primarily mediates antibacterial activity and provides little direct antioxidant activity, whereas GZ@M predominantly contributes to ROS scavenging and mitochondrial protection. By integrating these complementary modules within the HPD matrix, BOV achieved the most comprehensive overall profile of redox regulation and mitochondrial protection.

BOV promotes mitochondrial autophagy via the SIRT1/FOXO3a pathway

Based on the aforementioned in vitro findings of the BOV in removing reactive oxygen species and protecting mitochondrial function, this study aims to further elucidate its underlying molecular mechanism, particularly to explore whether it can go beyond the simple antioxidant effect and instead actively activate the cell's inherent defense and repair programs to restore cellular homeostasis. To clarify these deep-seated mechanisms, we conducted multi-level investigations ranging from transcriptomics to functional proteins. First, we analyzed the global impact of BOV treatment on the macrophage transcriptome in an LPS-induced inflammatory model. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that the gene expression profiles of the control group and the BOV-treated group were significantly separated, suggesting that BOV can systematically regulate the transcriptional program of cells (Fig. 6A). Volcano plot analysis further identified 606 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 6B), providing a foundation for mechanistic exploration. Through the Gene Ontology (GO)and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), we identified the key clues for regulating oxidative stress and cellular homeostasis. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis showed that upregulated DEGs were significantly enriched in biological processes such as DNA damage response, cell cycle regulation, and apoptosis progression, while also involving precise regulation of cytoskeleton remodeling, protein function, and signal transduction. This suggests that BOV may exert its functions by influencing core life activities of the cell (Fig. 6C). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that DEGs were significantly enriched in the FOXO signaling pathway (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, key genes associated with autophagy regulation (e.g., BNIP3), metabolism and stress resistance (IGF1), and deacetylation (SIRT1) were markedly upregulated in the BOV-treated group. Concurrently, genes related to the inflammatory NF-κB pathway were generally downregulated (Supplementary Fig. 19). This coordinated pattern of FOXO pathway activation and NF-κB pathway suppression suggests that the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects of BOV may be closely linked to its active regulation of cellular homeostasis, particularly mechanisms governing mitochondrial quality control.

Previous studies have reported that the SIRT1/FOXO3a axis serves as a pivotal pathway for regulating mitophagy and alleviating oxidative stress in models of diabetes and its complications, providing a strong rationale for our focus on this pathway [35]. Based on the transcriptomic clues identified above and prior research, we propose a central mechanistic hypothesis: BOV may synergistically activate SIRT1 and FOXO3a to form a potentially coordinated regulatory relationship, thereby potently driving the expression of its downstream mitophagy receptor, BNIP3. Upregulation of BNIP3 will promote the clearance of damaged mitochondria, thus mitigating oxidative stress at its source. Further TEM observations revealed that many damaged mitochondria accumulated in cells following LPS treatment, exhibiting pathological features such as matrix swelling and cristae dissolution (Fig. 6F). In contrast, the BOV-treated group displayed typical autophagic structures in which damaged mitochondria were enveloped by autophagosomes, providing ultrastructural features consistent with mitophagy. Western blot analysis showed that BOV treatment increased the relative protein levels of SIRT1, FOXO3a, and BNIP3. Notably, the FOXO-specific inhibitor JY-2 not only suppressed the expression of FOXO3a and its downstream target BNIP3, but also coordinately downregulated the upstream protein SIRT1 (Fig. 6G and H). This finding suggests that, in the context of BOV activation, SIRT1 and FOXO3a may form a functionally tightly coupled positive feedback regulatory module [36]. Previous studies have reported that activated FOXO3a can translocate into the nucleus, where it interacts with p53 to relieve p53-mediated transcriptional repression of SIRT1, thereby positively regulating SIRT1 expression [37]. Therefore, BOV may facilitate the clearance of damaged mitochondria through regulation of the SIRT1/FOXO3a/BNIP3 axis.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of infected wounds

The comprehensive in vitro experiments described above demonstrated the favorable performance of BOV in terms of biocompatibility, antibacterial activity, antioxidant capacity, and mitochondrial protection, preliminarily mimicking the ability of parasitic wasp eggs to colonize complex biological environments and modulate the local microenvironment.

To further validate the therapeutic efficacy of this biomimetic system under pathological conditions, we comprehensively evaluated its in vivo wound healing performance in a full-thickness skin defect model of S. aureus-infected diabetic rats [38]. To distinguish the respective in vivo contributions of the PHMB and GZ@M functional modules, the experiment groups included alongside the Control (3M™ Tegaderm), HPD, HPD/PHMB, HPD/GZ@M and complete BOV groups. Macroscopic images of the wound-healing process revealed that wounds treated with BOV exhibited a more favorable wound-closure trajectory than the other formulations, particularly during the early and intermediate stages of healing (Fig. 7B). Quantitative analysis of the residual wound area using ImageJ software further corroborated this advantage (Fig. 7C): By day 7, the wound closure rate in the BOV group reached 79.83%, which was numerically higher than those achieved with HPD/PHMB and HPD/GZ@M and significantly exceeded that of the Control group (35.87%). After 14 days of treatment, the wound closure rates in the control, HPD, HPD/PHMB, HPD/GZ@M, and BOV groups were approximately 70.36%, 83.57%, 88.44%, 92.14%, and 97.88%, respectively. Although the differences among the treatment groups became relatively modest at this later time point, BOV maintained the highest overall wound closure rate. These findings indicate that BOV primarily facilitates the early progression of healing, whereas its broader therapeutic advantage cannot be fully represented by the final wound closure rate alone.

To evaluate bacterial control in vivo, we performed bacterial isolation and culture using wound tissues. The results were consistent with the in vitro antibacterial experiments: the HPD and HPD/GZ@M groups still harbored large numbers of viable bacteria in the wounds, whereas the HPD/PHMB and BOV groups showed a significant reduction in colony counts (Fig. 7D and E). These results confirm that PHMB is the major contributor to early bacterial clearance in vivo. Importantly, however, the comparable reduction in bacterial burden observed in the HPD/PHMB and BOV groups suggests that the additional therapeutic advantage of BOV is not attributable solely to antibacterial activity, but is more clearly reflected in the subsequent quality of tissue regeneration and inflammatory regulation.

By day 14 post-treatment, we performed histological analysis of the newly regenerated skin tissue to evaluate the quality and structural maturity of tissue repair. H&E staining revealed that the control and HPD groups still exhibited residual scabs and substantial inflammatory cell infiltration, indicating persistent chronic inflammation and delayed healing (Fig. 7F). In contrast, the BOV group exhibited more complete re-epithelialization, better-developed granulation tissue, reduced inflammatory infiltration, and a tissue architecture more closely resembling that of normal skin. Compared with the HPD/PHMB and HPD/GZ@M groups, BOV displayed the most favorable overall histological regeneration profile.

Masson's trichrome staining further demonstrated that the BOV group had the most abundant and orderly collagen deposition, with a collagen volume fraction of 77.20%, in contrast to 35.16% in the Control group (Fig. 7G–J). Additionally, BOV treatment resulted in the smallest residual wound width and the greatest granulation tissue thickness among the tested groups (Fig. 7H and I). Collectively, these findings clarify that the principal advantage of BOV lies not merely in accelerating wound closure, but in improving re-epithelialization, granulation tissue formation, collagen organization, and the overall structural quality of regenerated tissue [39].

To further characterize the inflammatory microenvironment observed in the H&E-stained wound tissues on day 14, immunofluorescence analysis of CD86 and CD206 was performed. As shown in Fig. 8A, the Control group exhibited strong CD86 staining accompanied by relatively weak CD206 expression, indicating a persistent M1-associated pro-inflammatory profile in the wound tissue. In contrast, BOV treatment markedly reduced CD86 expression while enhancing CD206 expression, resulting in the highest CD206/CD86 fluorescence-intensity ratio among the tested groups (Fig. 8B). Immunohistochemical staining on day 14 further showed that IL-6 expression remained high in the Control group but decreased following hydrogel treatment, with the lowest level observed in the BOV group (Fig. 8C and D). Conversely, IL-10 expression was relatively low in the Control group and increased across the treatment groups, reaching the highest level following BOV treatment (Fig. 8C–E). The HPD/GZ@M group also exhibited beneficial regulation of inflammatory markers, supporting a contribution of the GZ@M module to inflammatory microenvironment regulation. When considered together with the in vitro antioxidant findings, these results suggest that GZ@M may contribute to the establishment of a more favorable regenerative microenvironment. However, the complete BOV formulation displayed the most favorable overall profile of reduced pro-inflammatory marker expression and enhanced pro-resolving marker expression. Collectively, these findings indicate that BOV alleviates the persistent pro-inflammatory microenvironment and promotes a shift from an M1-associated inflammatory profile toward an M2-associated pro-resolving profile, which may reflect the complementary effects of PHMB-mediated bacterial control and GZ@M-mediated microenvironmental regulation.

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the pro-regenerative effects of BOV, we performed RNA-seq analysis on wound tissues collected on day 5. Principal component analysis revealed a distinct divergence in the transcriptional profiles between the control and BOV groups (Fig. 9A). Volcano plot analysis identified 230 differentially expressed genes, including 111 upregulated and 119 downregulated genes (Fig. 9B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that the upregulated genes were significantly enriched in pathways associated with tissue repair, including the “complement and coagulation cascades,” “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway,” and “ECM-receptor interaction” (Fig. 9D). Heatmap analysis demonstrated that genes associated with cell survival, extracellular matrix synthesis, and tissue remodeling, including AKT3, ITGA4, FN1, VTN, and COL6A3, were markedly upregulated in the BOV group (Fig. 9C). GO analysis further confirmed that the differentially expressed genes were enriched in biological processes such as immune regulation and cell proliferation(Fig. 9E).

In summary, the in vivo results demonstrate that the therapeutic advantage of BOV over HPD/PHMB and HPD/GZ@M is not defined solely by differences in final wound closure. HPD/PHMB primarily provides effective bacterial control, whereas HPD/GZ@M shows a more favorable inflammatory-regulatory profile. When considered together with the in vitro antioxidant and mitochondrial-protection results, these findings suggest that GZ@M may contribute to the establishment of a more favorable regenerative microenvironment. By integrating these complementary functions, BOV promotes a more favorable early healing trajectory and achieves comprehensive improvements in re-epithelialization, granulation tissue formation, collagen organization, and inflammatory microenvironment regulation. Transcriptomic analysis further suggests that BOV may initiate early repair responses through regulation of the complement and coagulation cascades, while activating PI3K-Akt signaling and ECM-receptor interactions to support cell survival, proliferation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and angiogenesis-associated repair [40]. By recapitulating the colonization, defense, regulation, and repair strategies of parasitic wasp eggs, BOV establishes a coordinated antibacterial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and pro-regenerative therapeutic process, thereby promoting high-quality healing in infected diabetic wounds and demonstrating potential for further translational development.

Conclusion

In summary, this study addresses the core challenge of diabetic wound management, dynamic misalignment between therapeutic action and the pathological microenvironment, by translating the survival strategy of parasitic wasp eggs into a programmable material platform. Beyond mimicking singular physical properties, the BOV hydrogel comprehensively adopts the wasp's strategy: precise anchoring via wet tissue adhesion, microenvironment sensing, programmed resource utilization through spatiotemporally cascaded drug release, and active host reprogramming. BOV is built on a dynamic boronic ester network that provides injectability, self-healing, and dual pH/ROS responsiveness. This enables intelligent two-stage drug release: PHMB release during early infection, followed by delayed antioxidant/pro-regenerative (Myr) release triggered by MMP-9 in later inflammation. Beyond scavenging ROS, BOV actively upregulates mitophagy via the SIRT1/FOXO3a/BNIP3 pathway to clear damaged mitochondria, resolving oxidative stress at its source. In a diabetic rat model with S. aureus-infection, this coordinated strategy, sequencing drug delivery with active cellular programming, outperformed single-component approaches. It accelerated wound closure through early potent antibacterial action and promoted high-quality tissue regeneration, characterized by enhanced angiogenesis, organized collagen deposition, and reduced inflammation.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, while the rat model recapitulates key features of diabetic wound infection, further validation in large animals with comorbidities would strengthen translational relevance. Second, the long-term biosafety and metabolic fate of the hydrogel components require more extended in vivo evaluation. Finally, although we identified the involvement of the SIRT1/FOXO3a/BNIP3 axis, the detailed signaling crosstalk between material cues and mitochondrial quality control warrants deeper mechanistic investigation. Looking forward, future efforts may focus on (1) clinical translation, including scaling up production, optimizing sterilization protocols, and evaluating combinatory use with existing wound care modalities; (2) modular expansion, by exchanging the responsive motifs and therapeutic cargos to target other multi-stage pathological microenvironments, such as cancer stroma or degenerative tissues; and (3) mechanistic deepening, systematically mapping the material-triggered signaling networks to uncover new principles of biomaterial host dialogue. Through such continued development, bioinspired regulative materials like BOV may ultimately enable personalized, stage-adaptive therapies in regenerative medicine.

Synthesis and characterization of HA-DA and HA-PBA

HA-DA was synthesized as follows. First, 1.0 g of hyaluronic acid (HA) was dissolved in 100 mL of deionized water, and the pH was adjusted to 5.0. Next, 0.33 g of 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) were added, and the mixture was stirred for 2 h. Subsequently, 0.493 g of dopamine hydrochloride (DOA) was introduced, and the reaction was continued at room temperature for 4 h under nitrogen protection. The resulting solution was dialyzed against deionized water for 3 days and then lyophilized to yield HA-DA. HA-PBA was prepared as follows. Briefly, 1.0 g of HA and 0.6838 g of 3-aminophenylboronic acid (3-APBA) were dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water and 20 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to form solution A. Separately, 0.9526 g of EDC and 0.5855 g of NHS were dissolved in 30 mL of deionized water to form solution B. Solutions A and B were combined, and the pH was adjusted to 5.5 with 0.1 M HCl. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h, followed by dialysis for 3 days. Finally, the HA-PBA sample was lyophilized for 48 h. The chemical structures of the polymers were characterized by Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Thermo Fisher Nicolet iS10). The grafting rates of HA-PBA and HA-DA were determined from 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra (Bruker Avance II, 400 MHz).

Synthesis of gel/ZIF-8@Myr (GZ@M)

GZ@M was synthesized following a modified protocol. Briefly, 0.33 g of 2-methylimidazole and 20 μg of myricetin (Myr) were dissolved in 10 mL of methanol. Subsequently, 0.15 g of zinc nitrate hexahydrate [Zn(NO3)2·6H2O] was dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water and slowly added to the above solution under continuous stirring. The mixture was stirred at 37 °C for 2 h, then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min to collect the ZIF-8@Myr (Z@M) precipitate. The Z@M product was washed twice with methanol, then resuspended in 20 mL of 2% (w/v) gelatin (Gel) solution and stirred at 37 °C for 12 h. The resulting mixture was centrifuged again at 10,000 rpm for 15 min, and the precipitate was washed three times with deionized water. Finally, the product was lyophilized to obtain GZ@M nanocrystals. For comparison, ZIF-8 was synthesized using the same procedure but without the addition of Myr and Gel. Detailed methods for the synthesis of ZIF-8@Myr (GZ@M) and characterization of GZ@M are provided in the Supporting Information.

Fabrication of bee ovum–inspired gel (BOV)

HPD hydrogels were prepared by mixing 2% (w/v) HA-DA and 3% (w/v) HA-PBA solutions at volume ratios of 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, and 2:3, respectively, under continuous stirring at 37 °C. The mixtures were allowed to gel at room temperature to afford HPD1, HPD2, HPD3, and HPD4 hydrogels. For the fabrication of the bee ovum–inspired gel (BOV), PHMB and GZ@M were individually incorporated into the HA-DA and HA-PBA solutions, respectively, prior to mixing. The pre-loaded solutions were then combined at the specified volume ratios to obtain BOV.

Adhesion and compression testing

The adhesion strength of the hydrogels was measured in accordance with the ASTM F2255-05 standard using a universal testing machine (WO05200600015) at a crosshead speed of 50 mm/min under ambient conditions. Lap-shear tests were performed, and the adhesion strength was calculated as the maximum load divided by the adhesion area. For compression testing, cylindrical hydrogel samples (diameter: 12 mm, height: 4 mm) were prepared in polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) molds. Compression tests were conducted at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min, and the elastic modulus was determined from the initial slope of the stress–strain curve within the 0–10% strain range. The compressive strength was calculated as the maximum failure load divided by the original cross-sectional area.

Drug release of BOV

In this study, pH 6.0 was selected to represent the mildly acidic environment of infected chronic wounds. H2O2 at 1 mM was used as a standardized in vitro oxidative challenge condition rather than as a direct estimate of the absolute H2O2 concentration in wound exudate. MMP-9 at 1 nM was selected to model the elevated proteolytic activity of non-healing diabetic wounds. The PHMB release properties of the hydrogels were evaluated by loading the hydrogels into dialysis bags (molecular weight cutoff [MWCO] = 1000 Da) and immersing them in PBS (pH 7.4, pH 6.0, or pH 6.0 + 1 mM H2O2). Samples were placed in a shaker at 37 °C, and aliquots of release medium were collected at predetermined time intervals, with fresh medium replenished. The release efficiencies of PHMB was quantified using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer at wavelengths of 518 nm, respectively.

Fluorescent Labeling of PHMB: To label PHMB with Cy5, 1.0 mg of PHMB and 0.2 mg of Cy5-NHS (catalog #BD00953241, Bidepharm, China) were dissolved in 2 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, catalog #V900090, Sigma, Germany). The reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature for 24 h to synthesize Cy5-labeled PHMB. The product was then dialyzed against deionized (DI) water using a 2 kDa MWCO dialysis bag (Sigma, USA) for 3 days to remove unreacted Cy5-NHS and DMSO, with PHMB retained inside the dialysis bag throughout. For hydrogel preparation, 1 wt% of the Cy5-labeled PHMB was added to 2% (w/v) HA-DA, followed by mixing with 3% (w/v) HA-PBA to form the gel. The in vitro release of PHMB from BOV was monitored using the small animal living fluorescence imaging system (NEWTON FT-900). BOV samples were immersed in 24-well plates containing PBS at 37 °C, and fluorescence intensity was measured at predetermined time points, with fresh medium replenished.

The Myr release properties of the hydrogels were evaluated by loading the hydrogels into dialysis bags (molecular weight cutoff [MWCO] = 1000 Da) and immersing them in PBS (pH 7.4, pH 6.0, pH 6.0 + 1 mM H2O2, or pH 6.0 + 1 mM H2O2+1 nM MMP-9). Samples were placed in a shaker at 37 °C, and aliquots of release medium were collected at predetermined time intervals, with fresh medium replenished. The release efficiencies of Myr was quantified using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer at wavelengths of 236 nm, respectively.

Fluorescent Labeling of GZ@M: To track GZ@M release, azithromycin was labeled with indocyanine green (ICG-Azithromycin, catalog #XCTB0934, Xwcbio, China) as a fluorescent tracer to visualize the microenvironment-responsive behavior of the GZ@M module (ICG-azithromycin was used as a fluorescent tracer to visualize stimulus-responsive release from the GZ@M module. It was not used to quantify or model Myr release kinetics.). GZ@I nanoparticles loaded with ICG-Azithromycin were prepared using the same method as GZ@M. For hydrogel preparation, 1 wt% of GZ@I was added to 3% (w/v) HA-PBA, followed by mixing with 2% (w/v) HA-DA to form the gel. The in vitro release of ICG-Azithromycin from BOV was monitored using the small animal living fluorescence imaging system (NEWTON FT-900). BOV samples were immersed in 24-well plates containing PBS at 37 °C, and fluorescence intensity was measured at predetermined time points.

Antibacterial efficacy

Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus; A344885-0001) and Escherichia coli were used in this study. These bacteria were cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth at 37 °C with continuous stirring, and the concentration of each bacterial suspension was adjusted to 106 CFU/mL. The hydrogel antibacterial experiments were divided into five groups: (1) Bacteria + PBS (2) Bacteria + HPD, (3) Bacteria + HPD/PHMB, (4) Bacteria + HPD/GZ@M, and (5) Bacteria + BOV. After co-culturing the hydrogel and bacterial suspension for 12 h, the obtained bacterial suspensions were spread on an LB agar plate. Subsequently, the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 12 h to form observable colony units. The antibacterial effect was calculated by counting the number of colonies on the counting plate. The live/dead staining assay was conducted similarly. Bacterial suspensions co-cultured with hydrogels were stained using a Live/Dead Bacterial Staining Kit. Briefly, 1 mL of the bacterial suspension was stained with 1.5 μL of N, N-dimethylaniline N-oxide (DMAO) and 1.5 μL of propidium iodide (PI) at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. The samples were then washed twice with PBS, mounted onto slides, and fluorescence images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica STELLARIS 5). Detailed methods for the anti-biofilm experiments and other related procedures are provided in the Supporting Information.

ROS scavenging assay

Intracellular ROS levels in RAW 264.7 cells were evaluated using DCFH-DA fluorescence imaging and flow cytometry. Detailed experimental procedures are provided in the Supplementary Information.

JC-1 assay

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was measured using the fluorescent dye 5,5′,6,6′-tetrachloro-1,1′,3,3′-tetraethylbenzimidazolylcarbocyanine iodide (JC-1), which accumulates in mitochondria in a membrane potential–dependent manner. Red fluorescence indicates high membrane potential, whereas green fluorescence reflects a decrease in ΔΨm. Cells were analyzed using a JC-1 detection kit according to the manufacturer's instructions, and mitochondrial membrane potential was further quantified via flow cytometry.

RNA sequencing and analysis

RNA sequencing was performed for both RAW 264.7 cells and diabetic rat wound tissues by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Total RNA extraction, library preparation, and paired-end sequencing were conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using PE150 mode. Clean reads from RAW 264.7 cells and rat wound tissues were aligned to the Mus musculus and Rattus norvegicus reference genomes, respectively, using HISAT2. Gene expression levels were quantified using StringTie, and differential expression analysis was performed using Ballgown. Gene set enrichment analysis was conducted using MSigDB v7.3, with |NES| > 1, nominal p < 0.05, and FDR < 0.25 considered significant.

In vivo validation of the diabetic animal model

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Experimental Animal Management and Use Committee of the Animal Research Center, Zhejiang University of Technology (approval no.: MGS20251027151). Male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (body weight: 180–200 g) were used in this study. Diabetes was induced in SD rats via tail vein injection of streptozotocin (STZ) at a dose of 60 μg/g body weight. Rats with fasting blood glucose levels exceeding 16.7 mmol/L were defined as diabetic.
