Favorable outcome of engraftment of custom designed RADA16-I based hydrogels functionalized with SDF1 or IL4 mimicking peptides to injured or dystrophic muscles

ABSTRACT
Although muscle regeneration capacity is remarkable, under some conditions, such as extensive muscle damage or muscular dystrophies, it may be insufficient, leading to impaired muscle repair and deficits in its structure and function. Among the treatments considered for such conditions are those combining the use of biomaterials, cells, and the addition of selected factors. In the current study we for the first time evaluated potential in vivo effects of hydrogels designed by us, based on RADA16-I hydrogel and modified with IL4 or SDF1 mimicking peptides, as well as verified whether such effects depend on the environment of skeletal muscle, i.e. acutely injured or dystrophic, i.e. chronic one. Since induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and their derivatives are considered for dysfunctional skeletal muscle therapies, we injected muscles with hydrogels only or with hydrogels and myoblasts derived from human iPSCs (hiPSCs). We found that hydrogels functionalized with SDF1 or IL4 mimicking peptides were successfully engrafted in injured muscles, and that peptide presence had a significant, supporting effect on muscle regeneration manifested by improved treadmill outcome, as well as enhanced expression of myogenesis, angiogenesis, and neurogenesis markers. In the acute injury model, these effects were elevated after co-injection of hydrogels and hiPSC-derived DMD myoblasts. An increased expression of markers of myogenesis, neurogenesis, and angiogenesis was also observed in dystrophic muscles, but only after treatment with an SDF1 functionalized hydrogel. In such an environment, transplanted cells were rapidly removed, so their injection had no influence on observed effects. The muscle environment (acute injury versus dystrophic/chronic) was crucial for the final outcome of hydrogel transplantation, injected alone or with hiPSC-derived myoblasts. Introduction

Skeletal muscles are characterized by the ability to regenerate in response to injury, overload, or disease. Two main processes occur during muscle regeneration: (1) myolysis, characterized by rapid breakdown of muscle fiber integrity, necrosis, and infiltration of the injury site by inflammatory cells, and (2) reconstruction of muscle structure. The second one relies on the activation of satellite cells (SCs), skeletal muscle unipotent stem cells, re-entering the cell cycle and converting into myoblasts, which further differentiate into myocytes fusing with each other to form myotubes. These processes are regulated by numerous factors among which muscle regulatory factors (MRFs), that is, MYF5, MYOD, MYOGENIN, and MRF4 play a pivotal role. Regenerated myotubes grow and mature, forming functional muscle fibers, allowing restoration of muscle architecture and function1–3. Progression of muscle regeneration depends on growth factors and cytokines produced not only by muscle itself but also by inflammatory cells, fibroblasts, neural and endothelial cells present or appearing in this tissue. Although muscle regeneration capacity is remarkable, it may be insufficient under some conditions, such as muscular dystrophies or extensive muscle damage, leading to impaired muscle repair and deficits in its structure and function4,5. Muscular dystrophies, which belong to the group of muscle genetic diseases, are characterized by gradual weakening and degeneration of skeletal muscles due to mutations in genes encoding proteins essential for muscle structure and function, for example, DYSTROPHIN in Duchenne’s muscular dystrophy6,7. This impairment results in the permanent activation of SCs, leading to the exhaustion of their population, SC dysfunction, and excessive deposition of collagen and other extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, leading to fibrosis, and eventually muscle degeneration8,9. Limited muscle functionality, resulting from extensive deposition of fibrotic tissue, is also observed after volumetric muscle loss (VML), a significant muscle loss resulting, for example, from severe burns, accidents, or surgical procedures. Such a situation leads to incomplete muscle recovery and impairments in its structure and function10,11.

Among the potential treatments for the above-mentioned conditions are the use of biomaterials, cell and gene therapies, or their combination. Several examples of biomaterials, e.g., natural polymers such as collagen, fibrin, hyaluronic acid or laminin, as well as synthetic ones, such as polyethylene glycol, polycaprolactone, polylactic glycolic acid12, have already been tested or are being currently examined, for potential therapeutic application. Many of them mimic the native ECM microenvironment; however, their mechanical properties, such as stiffness, density, or degradation kinetics, may differ significantly. In the case of skeletal muscles, biomaterials are mostly tested as potential scaffolds enabling reconstruction of this tissue as well as cargo delivering cells or bioactive molecules, such as growth factors and cytokines. It was already shown that myogenic and non-myogenic cells can support skeletal muscle regeneration by different mechanisms12. Cells characterized by naïve or induced myogenic potential can participate in the formation of skeletal muscle myotubes/fibers during muscle regeneration, while non-myogenic ones, such as adipose-derived stromal cells or mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), could support regeneration through paracrine effects that affect, e.g., modulation of inflammatory response, promotion of angiogenesis or innervation. Previous studies on SC or myoblast transplantation indicated that such an approach is limited by the poor survival of transplanted cells, as well as difficulties in their efficient isolation and propagation in vitro13,14. For this reason, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) are currently considered the main potential source of myogenic cells for skeletal muscle transplantation and reconstruction. iPSCs have been shown to be efficiently induced to undergo myogenic differentiation, resulting in the generation of numerous functional myoblasts, for example, by a three-step protocol used in the current study15. Regulation of the activity of transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) in paraxial mesoderm cells, obtained from human iPSCs (hiPSCs) and cultured in gelatin-methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel may serve as an example of a strategy that combines the use of hiPSC derivatives together with factors affecting the muscle environment, as well as biomaterials16. Hydrogels are 3D cross-linked hydrophilic polymeric matrices with high water contents that can be obtained from natural, as well as synthetic components, or their combination17. Different types of hydrogels have already been successfully tested for regenerative approaches; for example, the self-assembled hydrogel RADA16-I functionalized with platelet-derived growth factor 2 (PDGF2) active sequence was shown to advance skin wound healing after biopsy punch18. In turn, the impact of the semi-synthetic GelMA hydrogel with incorporated black phosphorus nanosheets was evaluated for bone regeneration. Such a hydrogel exhibited antibacterial properties and supported both neurogenic and osteogenic activity. In vivo experiments aimed to evaluate this hydrogel effect on cranial defect reconstruction in the rat model confirmed a positive immunomodulatory effect and the promotion of bone formation and neural network reconstruction19. Hydrogels are also intensively tested as a tool supporting the reconstruction of injured or diseased skeletal muscles, including their revascularization and reinnervation. In the above-mentioned example, GelMA hydrogel and TGF-β inhibitor were used to obtain myotubes from hiPSC-derived paraxial mesoderm cells. Next, TGF-β-conjugated hydrogel was also used to locally control the differentiation of the fibroblasts that are responsible for the reconstruction of the ECM. The combination of both hydrogels allowed to obtain myofibers that exhibited high cell viability and spontaneous contraction layered with fibroblasts16. Using a model of severe muscle injury, i.e., surgical ablation of almost entire tibialis anterior (TA) muscle Fuoco et al. shown that the application of polyethyleneglycol and fibrinogen hydrogel (PEG-fibrinogen, PF) together with mesangioblasts expressing placental derived growth factor (PDGF) allows the formation of new artificial muscle approximately the same size as the ablated ones20. The reconstituted “artificial” muscle showed normal functionality and morphology characterized by peripherally nucleated fibers surrounded by well-formed basal lamina and a proper blood vessel network, which formation was promoted by PDGF. PF hydrogel together with alginate and mesangioblasts was also used for biofabrication of highly aligned, advanced artificial muscle substitutes that were successfully used to replace the ablated TA. The recovered muscle was characterized by proper architecture as well as showed neo-vascularization—a key feature for artificial tissue used for in vivo transplantation, and innervation leading to significant force production21. Indicated studies serve as examples of significant steps towards the generation of reconstructed, artificial, but functional muscle characterized by proper architecture and composition.

In the current study, we evaluated for the first time the in vivo effects of hydrogels designed by us as a tool that could improve skeletal muscle regeneration. These hydrogels, synthesized by us for the first time, were based on RADA16-I and modified with custom-designed peptides to mimic fragments of two myogenic regulators, i.e., interleukin 4 (IL4) or stromal-derived factor 1 (SDF1). Our previous studies showed that such functionalized hydrogels serve as a supportive environment for myoblasts derived from hiPSCs, enhancing their proliferation, migration, and differentiation. We showed that these hydrogels induced neither apoptosis in such myoblasts nor supported their unwanted differentiation toward adipocytes, chondrocytes, or osteocytes (Michalska et al., submitted). In the other study, we also evaluated their impact on selected cell populations essential for effective skeletal muscle regeneration, i.e., human myoblasts, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts22. Indicated cell populations reacted differently, but, importantly, we did not observe any harmful effects of our hydrogels. As an example, we found that human primary myoblasts cultured on hydrogel modified with the SDF1-X peptide were characterized by increased migration, while endothelial cells were characterized by decreased apoptosis. This indicates that hydrogels designed and tested by us could be successfully used for the treatment of dysfunctional skeletal muscle. As already mentioned, IL4 and SDF1, which were selected for modification of tested hydrogels, are known to be important factors for coordinated progression of skeletal muscle regeneration, as well as regulation of stem cell behavior23–26. IL4 promotes the incorporation of myoblasts into myotubes, allowing myotube growth and maturation in vitro and in vivo, and eventually muscle fiber formation27. SDF1 is a well-known factor that promotes stem and progenitor cell migration and mobilization28,29. When delivered in polyelectrolyte multilayer films, made of poly(L-lysine) and hyaluronan, it increased the mouse myoblast migration, and increased expression of myogenic markers, but decreased the fusion index29. IL4 and SDF1 modified biomaterials have also been shown to support the reconstruction of tissues other than skeletal muscles. For example, IL4 and SDF1 delivered in chitosan-coated gelatin hydrogel promoted tendon-to-bone healing by recruiting MSCs and modulating M2 macrophage polarization30. Additionally, injection of these hydrogels into a rat rotator cuff tear improved its reconstruction30. Furthermore, granular hydrogels bound to heparin-norbornene nanoparticles with covalently bound SDF1 were used to increase neural progenitor cell recruitment, maintenance and differentiation, as well as to promote angiogenesis in mouse models of stroke31. The potential enhancement of skeletal muscle regeneration by biomaterials/hydrogels modified with IL4 or SDF1 mimicking peptides has not been reported so far but, based on the results indicated above, appears promising. Furthermore, its potential therapeutic value is improved by the fact that the designed hydrogels were modified with peptides—fragments of IL4 or SDF1 proteins, not whole proteins, which limits the risk of rejection after transplantation32. In the current study, RADA-based hydrogels, modified with IL4 or SDF1 mimicking peptides, as well as unfunctionalized ones, were transplanted into injured or dystrophic mouse skeletal muscles to verify whether potential in vivo effects would depend on muscle environment, that is, acutely injured or dystrophic (chronic). Hydrogels were transplanted alone or with myoblasts derived from hiPSCs to assess whether such an approach will result in enhancement of the beneficial effects of hydrogel transplantation.

Materials and methods

Peptide synthesis, purification and gelling procedure

Peptides were synthesized using an automated peptide synthesizer (Activotec, Cambridge, UK) by standard solid phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) under heated conditions, as previously described33. TentaGel R RAM was used as solid support, and diisopropylcarbodiimide (DIC) and Oxyma Pure were used as coupling agents. Fmoc group was removed by 25% piperidine in dimethylformamide (DMF). For final deprotection, a mixture of trifloroacetic acid (TFA), phenol, water and triisopropylsilane (TIS) (ratio 88:5:5:2 v/v/v/v) was applied. The peptides were purified by a semi-preparative high-perfomance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system using a Jupiter Proteo (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) C12 column (4 µm, 90 Å). The purity and identity of the purified peptides were verified by HPLC and mass spectrometry (MS). The following gelation procedure was performed under sterile conditions. To ensure dissolution, the peptides were vortexed vigorously and subjected to an ultrasonic bath for 10 min. After 3 h of incubation at 37°C in the thermoblock, the pH was adjusted to 5 and again incubated for 3 h. The functionalized peptide (R1-SDF1 or R4-IL4), which is capable of forming a hydrogel independently, was used at a final concentration of 5 mg/ml. Next, hydrogels were mixed with nonmodified RADA16-I at a final concentration of 5 mg/ml (mixture ratio 1:1 v/v). This resulted in a total peptide concentration of 10 mg/ml, which corresponds to 1% (w/v) or 10 g/L.

Animals

The experiments were carried out with the approval of Warsaw Local Ethics Committee No. 1—agreement numbers 1377P1/2022 and 1568/2024. SCID NOD.CB17-Prkdcscid/J and SCID/mdx (B10ScSn.Cg-Prkdcscid Dmdmdx/J) mouse strains were used. The animals were acquired from Jackson Laboratory and maintained in the animal facility at the Faculty of Biology, University of Warsaw, on a 12 h/12 h (light/dark) cycle. Unrestricted supply of food and water was provided. All animal experiments were carried out in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and the EU Directive 2010/63/EU for animal experiments.

Cell lines, cell culture, and myogenic differentiation

Two commercially available human iPSC (hiPSC) cell lines were used in the experiments: hiPSC-DMD-EGFP (MONO-ALLELIC mEGFP-TAGGED DMD, Allen Institute for Cell Science), and hiPSC-LMNβ1-EGFP (MONO-ALLELIC mEGFP-TAGGED LMNβ1, Allen Institute for Cell Science), as previously described (Michalska et al., submitted). Briefly, hiPSCs were maintained on collagen I-coated cellware (354401, Corning) and then differentiated into myoblasts through the myogenic precursor stage, with skeletal muscle differentiation kit (SKM-KITM, Genea Biocells). Once myoblasts were generated, they were either immediately used for the experiments or cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen vapor. Hereinafter myoblasts obtained from hiPSC-DMD-EGFP are named MB-DMD while derived from hiPSC-LMNβ1-EGFP as MB-LMNβ1.

Muscle injury and transplantation

For the injury model, 2–3-month-old SCID mice were used. First, the animals were anesthetized by isoflurane inhalation and then the gastrocnemius muscle (right) was injected with 50 µl of 1.2% barium chloride (BaCl2) solution in sterile physiological saline. Each belly of the gastrocnemius muscle was injected in its central part with 25 µl of the indicated solution. The next day, the animals received 50 µl hydrogel or a hydrogel-saline solution (consisting of 40 µl of hydrogel and 10 µl of saline) with MB-DMD or MB-LMNβ1 into the injured muscle. The following hydrogels were used for the procedure: RADA16-I (unfunctionalized, hereinafter named R1), R1-SDF1-X, and R4-IL4-Y (hereafter named as R1-SDF1 and R4-IL4, respectively). In case of hydrogel and cell transplantation, either 5 × 105 MB-DMD or LMNβ1-MB were injected with 50 µl hydrogel-saline solution. The number of cells injected into muscles was established on the basis of available data (e.g.,33), as well as— primarily — on our previous experience with cell transplantation to regenerating muscles (e.g.,34,35). Before transplantation, cells were cultured on collagen-I covered flasks for 2 days and then rinsed with saline, trypsinized, counted, and centrifuged for 4 min at 1200 RPM. The supernatant was discharged and the cell pellet was suspended as indicated above. The experimental groups are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The transplantation was immediately followed by subcutaneous injection of the 25 µl of anti-inflammatory drug Metacam (5 mg/ml). The animals were injected with Metacam (5 mg/ml) once a day, for 3 days after transplantation. Uninjured (intact) animals and injured animals that did not receive any transplant (untreated) were considered controls. In another set of experiments 2–3 month old SCID/mdx mice received hydrogel transplantation alone or hydrogel-saline solution with MB-DMD or MB-LMNβ1, as described above. SCID/mdx muscles were not injured by BaCl2 injection. Animals that did not receive any transplant were considered controls (intact). The number of animals used per each experimental group is summarized in Supplementary Tables 1–3. The values obtained for each biological sample are shown as separate dots in graphs that present the results. However, the number of dots/values shown for particular analysis may differ from the number of animals summarized in Supplementary Tables 1–3. It happened when the animal or sample obtained from it was not used for a particular analysis or if the obtained value significantly misfitted the other values obtained for the experimental group, based on Grubbs’ test for outlier identification.

Functional test on a treadmill

A six-lane treadmill (Ugo basile) was used to perform the functional assessment of regeneration 28 days after transplantation. Exercise was carried out in two sessions: adaptation (2 min on a turned off treadmill, followed by 10 min at 5 m/min at 15% inclination) and experimental phase (2 min at 5 m/min, 2 min at 7 m/min, 2 min at 8 m/min, 2 min at 10 m/min, after which the speed increased 1 m/min every 2 min, until 20 m/min, at 15% inclination). When the mice stopped during the experiment, air puffs were activated to stimulate them to run. The experiment cut-off point was set at 5 s without running, despite receiving air puff stimulation. After the experiment, the animals were sacrificed (the animals were anesthetized with isoflurane using a dosing machine and sacrificed by cervical dislocation), body weight was assessed, and muscles were isolated. The weight of the animals is summarized in Supplementary Table 2 (for SCID mice) and Supplementary Table 3 (for SCID/mdx mice).

Muscle collection

Thirty minutes before muscle isolation, isopentane was cooled with liquid nitrogen until it was frozen. The right gastrocnemius muscles were then isolated, weighed, immediately frozen in isopentane, and then placed on dry ice and stored at − 80 °C. The frozen muscles were sectioned in a cryostat (Microm HM505N) to collect 8 µm sections. Contralateral gastrocnemius muscles, that is, those that did not receive treatment, were isolated, weighted, frozen, and stored similarly. The weight of both muscles is summarized in Supplementary Table 2 (for SCID mice) and Supplementary Table 3 (for SCID/mdx mice).

The separate dots shown in the graphs presenting the results of the histological analysis, described in detail below, reflect the value obtained for the animal, not separate sections. Measurement of muscle fiber area

Sections stained with HE were photographed using the Nikon eclipse TE200 microscope and the Nikon Digital Sight DS-U2 camera. Five photos of the field of view were taken for each gastrocnemius muscle. The photo analysis was performed using Image J software (NIS-Elements F 2.30). Each visible muscle fiber was manually outlined and its CSA was measured. All CSA values were used to visualize the distribution of muscle fiber size in muscles. The mean percentage of fibers belonging to each established size category was determined for each muscle group and subjected to statistical analysis.

Determination of the regenerating muscle fiber ratio

The number of fibers containing centrally located nuclei, corresponding to regenerating myofibers, was counted using a multi-point tool in Image J software (NIS-Elements F 2.30). Five photos of the fields of view were taken for each muscle. The values for each muscle were averaged and further subjected to statistical analysis.

Determination of the fibrosis ratio

Gastrocnemius muscle sections stained with Masson’s trichrome were photographed using the Nikon eclipse TE200 microscope and the Nikon Digital Sight DS-U2 camera. Five photos of the fields of view were taken for each muscle. The photo analysis was performed using Image J software (NIS-Elements F 2.30) software. The area of fibrosis was manually outlined and measured. The mean area of fibrosis was calculated in all muscles and subjected to statistical analysis.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from cell pellets using mirVana™ PARIS™ RNA and the Native Protein Purification Kit (AM1556, Thermo Fisher), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA concentration and contamination were determined using NanoDrop (Thermo Scientific). Reverse transcription was performed using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (K1622, Thermo Fisher), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, using 1 µg RNA for each reaction. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (4369016, Thermo Fisher) and TaqMan probes (Supplementary Table 4), according to previously published protocol34. GAPDH was used as a reference gene. Result analysis was performed with LightCycler® 96 Software 1.1 (Roche) and Graph Pad Prism 9. Data are shown as 2-ΔΔCT values.

Statistical analysis

At least values from three independent experiments were included in each analysis following the Grubbs’ test for outlier identification. Comparisons between experimental groups were performed using one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test, based on the distribution assumption (for normal distribution, ANOVA, if sampling was not from normal distribution—nonparametric alternative). In both cases, multiple comparisons were corrected using Tukey’s multiple comparison test, with a single pooled variance or Dunn’s multiple comparisons test, respectively. We have also checked whether the assumption of equal standard deviations was met, implementing an additional correction. The results presented in the figures are shown as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using the GraphPad Prism 9 software.

Results

Muscle structure after transplantation of hydrogels modified with IL4 or SDF1 mimicking peptides

In the initial experiments, muscles of SCID mice were injured with BaCl2 and after 24 h were transplanted with one of the following hydrogels: RADA16-I, that is, unfunctionalized control hydrogel, hereinafter called R1, or one of the functionalized hydrogels, that is, R4-IL4-Y or R1-SDF1-X, hereinafter called R4-IL4 or R1-SDF1, respectively. As a control, intact muscles or injured but untreated muscles were used. After 28 days we performed a functional muscle strength evaluation using a treadmill. It revealed that mice in which muscles were transplanted with R1-SDF1 hydrogel were characterized by the highest running distance and time (Fig. 1A). These results were significantly higher compared to injured but untreated muscles (Fig. 1A). Significant differences were also found between mice in which muscles were injected with control hydrogel and mice in which muscles were not treated (Fig. 1A). No significant differences were found between mice in which muscles were injected with different types of hydrogels (Fig. 1A).

After the treadmill test, the mice were sacrificed and weighted, and then the muscles were also isolated and weighted (Supplementary Table 2). Animal body weight was 23.27 to 29.94 g (Supplementary Table 2). No significant differences were found between all samples analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 1A–C). To assess whether hydrogel transplantation influenced the structure of regenerating muscles, we stained the histological sections obtained from them with HE and then CSA and the percentage of regenerating fibers, characterized by centrally located nuclei, was determined. We did not find any significant differences between all the muscle groups, i.e., acute injured muscles injected with different types of hydrogels (Fig. 1B–D). Next, we analyzed muscle sections stained with Masson’s trichrome to determine the level of fibrosis in all the indicated muscle types and again found no significant differences between them (Fig. 1E–F). Altogether, the results obtained indicated that reconstruction of the injured muscle in the presence of hydrogels proceeded properly and resulted in a better treadmill test outcome compared to untreated muscles.

Expression of myogenesis, angiogenesis, and neurogenesis markers in regenerating muscles transplanted with hydrogels modified with IL4 or SDF1 mimicking peptides

In the next experiments, we analyzed the expression of selected myogenesis, angiogenesis, and neurogenesis markers in the following SCID muscle groups: intact, untreated, transplanted with R1, R4-IL4 or R1-SDF1 to determine whether the presence of hydrogel influenced muscle reconstruction, including its innervation and vascularization. As myogenic markers we analyzed three types of myosin heavy chains, i.e. Myh3, which is expressed in embryonic and fetal but also in regenerating fibers, as well as Myh2 and Myh7, which are present in more mature fast and slow muscle fibers. In addition, we analyzed Myog, which is necessary for the differentiation of myogenic cells, Dmd, which encodes a structural protein that protects the integrity of the muscle fiber membrane, and finally Pax7, a marker of SCs, crucial cells for muscle regenerative potential. Among the angiogenesis markers analyzed by us there were Vwf, Cd31, Vegfa, and Flt1, while the neurogenesis markers included Achr, Musk, Lrp4, Dok7, and Rapsn.

The results indicated that muscles injected with R4-IL4 were characterized by significantly higher levels of Myh7, Dmd, and Pax7 expression compared to muscles injected with unfunctionalized hydrogel, as well as intact muscles (Fig. 2A). In the case of Dmd mRNA, a significant difference was also found between the muscles injected with R4-IL4 and the injured untreated ones (Fig. 2A). The level of Dmd expression was also significantly higher in muscles injected with the R1-SDF1 hydrogel compared to intact and untreated muscles and muscles injected with the unfunctionalized hydrogel (Fig. 2A). These muscles, i.e., transplanted with R1-SDF1, were also characterized by significantly higher levels of Myog mRNA, compared to untreated ones, as well as Myh7 mRNA, compared to intact muscles and muscles injected with control hydrogel (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, both muscles injected with functionalized hydrogels were characterized by an elevated level of Myh3 expression compared to intact muscles, and in case of R1-SDF1 treated muscles—also to untreated ones (Fig. 2A). Although the final conclusion needs to be confirmed by further protein analysis, the results obtained by us suggest that both groups of muscles injected with functionalized hydrogels, i.e., R1-SDF1 and R4-IL4, were characterized by enhanced levels of myogenesis markers. Also in the case of mRNAs encoding angiogenesis markers, we found a significantly higher level of them in muscles transplanted with functionalized hydrogels—Vwf compared to all other muscle groups (i.e., intact, untreated, R1) and Vegfa and Flt1 compared to intact and untreated muscles (Fig. 2B). The expression of neurogenesis markers was also significantly elevated in muscles injected with functionalized hydrogels. Significantly higher levels of Dok7 and Lrp4 expression were found in such muscles compared to intact and untreated ones (Fig. 2C). In the case of muscles treated with R4-IL4, the level of Dok7 mRNA was higher than in muscles injected with other hydrogels and Lrp4 mRNA was elevated in muscles treated with functionalized hydrogels compared to those injected with R1 hydrogel (Fig. 2C). All of the results indicate that transplantation of each functionalized hydrogel was beneficial for skeletal muscle reconstruction, as it supported expression of mRNAs encoding factors indispensable for myogenesis, angiogenesis, and innervation, i.e., processes that are crucial for regaining proper muscle functioning. Complex analysis of muscles transplanted with hydrogels and myoblasts derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells

In the next stage of our studies, muscles of SCID mice were injured with BaCl2 and then transplanted not only with hydrogels but also with myoblasts obtained from hiPSCs. Two lines of hiPSCs were used, i.e., hiPSC-DMD-GFP, synthesizing DYSTROPHIN linked to the GFP as well as hiPSC-LMNβ1-GFP, producing fluorescently tagged LAMINβ1. As a result, two types of myoblasts were generated—MB-DMD and MB-LMNβ1—hereinafter called DMD and LMNβ1, respectively. Before transplantation experiments we have assessed the viability of cells suspended in hydrogel-saline solution for 30 min, that is, the same time when during in vivo experiments the cells were injected into the muscles, kept in the incubator, and additionally for 2 h and 24 h. The mean value of the results obtained for a cell viability was 97% for 30 min incubation, 80% for 2 h, and 40% for 24 h. This is consistent with comprehensive chemical analysis that indicates that the structure of the hydrogel is appropriate for cells and shows that the hydrogels themselves are not detrimental for the cells suspended in them (Dierżyńska et al., in preparation). The drop in viability observed after 24 h probably resulted from the lack of nutrient delivery in hydrogel saline solution. This environment significantly differs from the in vivo condition to which hydrogel-transplanted cells are exposed at this time-point (24 h).

As before, during in vivo experiments intact muscles or injured but untreated muscles served as control. Again, 28 days after transplantation, we performed a treadmill test and found the highest running distance and time for mice whose muscles were injected with R1-SDF1 hydrogel and DMD cells (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Discussion

Exploring new approaches to improve skeletal muscle regeneration is critically important, particularly when inherent regenerative capacity is compromised, such as in the case of aging, major trauma, or the progression of diseases, for example, the muscular dystrophies mentioned above. Thus, the potential restoration of the structure and function of damaged skeletal muscle tissue with hydrogels emerges as a promising solution. Several hydrogel scaffolds and cell types have already been considered to support skeletal muscle reconstruction. Skeletal muscle regeneration is mostly investigated in limb muscles (e.g., tibialis anterior or gastrocnemius) as they are easily accessible and their response to injury is widely characterized, e.g.,38. For this reason, in the current study, we followed the regeneration of gastrocnemius that were transplanted for the first time with RADA16-I-based hydrogels, serving as an example of hydrogels formed by self-assembling peptides (SAP). RADA16-I hydrogels have already been tested for several applications, e.g., wound healing18,36, cartilage regeneration39, tendon repair40, or nerve regeneration41. As mentioned earlier, their regenerative function can be enhanced through the attachment of biologically active molecules to the scaffold structure, such as IL4 or SDF1 peptides, previously tested by us in vitro and complementary to the corresponding protein receptors22,42. We have already shown that such functionalized hydrogels (R1-SDF1 and R4-IL4, respectively) are nontoxic to human cells in vitro22. To date, a RADA16-I-based hydrogel has been evaluated for treating hindlimb ischemia only43. Therefore, our study is the first to reveal in vivo effects after transplantation of RADA-based hydrogels, serving as a potential tool for muscle reconstruction after acute or chronic injury. We also assessed whether the functionalization of RADA16-I-based hydrogels with precisely selected SDF-1 or IL-4 mimicking peptides has a noticeable influence on improving muscle reconstruction in both indicated injury models and whether the co-injection of cells with hydrogels to muscles changes the final in vivo outcome.

In the acute injury model, we observed an improved outcome of the treadmill test, manifested by increased time and distance, in mice which muscles were transplanted with SDF1 functionalized hydrogel, alone or with DMD cells. However, at the histological level, no differences in myofiber area or tissue fibrosis were observed between all analyzed groups of such injured muscles. Thus, such an effect has to be based on the more subtle changes occurring, e.g., at the molecular level. Indeed, both functionalized hydrogels, i.e., R1-SDF1 and R4-IL4, elevated expression of myogenesis markers in transplanted muscles, as evidenced by increased Dmd mRNA levels compared to all other muscle groups (i.e., intact, injured but untreated, injured and transplanted with an unmodified hydrogel). Furthermore, muscles injected with the SDF1 functionalized hydrogel were characterized by an increased level of MRF, that is, Myog expression, compared to untreated injured muscles while those injected with IL4 functionalized hydrogel – by elevated Pax7 expression compared to muscles that were intact or injured and injected with unmodified hydrogel. Effective skeletal muscle regeneration also requires coordinated processes of angiogenesis and neuromuscular junction (NMJ) formation44, so upregulation of transcripts involved in the mentioned processes observed in muscles injected with functionalized hydrogels was favorable outcome. Both functionalized hydrogels elevated the expression Vwf, Vegfa, and Flt1, which are responsible, for example, for proper vascularization at the site of injury by promoting the proliferation and migration of vascular endothelial cells or the transport of various proteins45–47. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the expression of two neurogenesis markers (Lrp4 and Dok7) was elevated in muscles treated with each functionalized hydrogels, especially with IL4 modified one, characterized by higher level of these markers also compared to muscles injected with SDF1 functionalized or unmodified hydrogels. LRP4 and DOK7 are the key components of the signaling cascade leading to the formation of NMJ, the synapse between motor neurones and skeletal muscle fibers critical for control of muscle contraction48–50. Therefore, the results obtained by us demonstrate for the first time that functionalized RADA16-I peptide hydrogels may improve innervation and vascularization in acute injured muscles, what could eventually result in better functional recovery of injured muscle, as suggested by the outcome of the treadmill test. The treadmill test has been used in numerous studies to assess skeletal muscle performance (e.g.,51–53), however it should be noted that it provides results that may reflect overall fitness, blood supply, or innervation, rather than individual muscle regeneration. For this reason, further verification of our observation requires other functional analysis regarding, e.g., isometric tetanic force, as well as analysis of proteins crucial for angiogenesis and neurogenesis in regenerating muscles. However, the results obtained remain in line with RADA16-I-based hydrogel effects observed in other reconstructed tissues, e.g., it was shown that such hydrogels improved cardiac function after acute myocardial infarction due to their pro-angiogenic and antifibrotic effects54. Recently, RADA16-I-based hydrogel functionalized with the SDF1 motif was also shown to promote repair of neural tissue after traumatic brain injury in rats55. To our knowledge, there were no data on in vivo effects of RADA16-I hydrogels functionalized using fragments or the whole IL4 protein.

In the current study, we also verified whether hiPSC-derived myoblasts could support the potential beneficial effects of hydrogel transplantation to injured skeletal muscles. We already know that SDF1 or IL4 functionalized hydrogels allowed proper proliferation, migration, and myogenic differentiation of hiPSC-derived myoblasts (Michalska et al., submitted). Moreover, we observed numerous changes in the expression of factors involved in angiogenesis, inflammatory mediation, cell adhesion, neurogenesis, and regulation of the myogenic programme in hiPSC-derived myoblasts cultured in vitro on the layer of functionalized hydrogels (Michalska et al., submitted). In the current study, we observed that muscle injected with IL4 or SDF1 functionalized hydrogels together with DMD myoblasts demonstrated even higher expression of transcripts involved in myogenesis and neurogenesis, such as: Myh3, Dmd, Lrp4, and Achr compared to muscles transplanted with functionalized hydrogels alone. Since ACHR serves as the essential receptor for the transmission of signals from motor neurones to muscle fibers resulting in muscle contraction56, elevated levels of all indicated markers suggest that co-injection of cells may enhance the beneficial effect of hydrogels on both myogenesis and neurogenesis. This effect is consistent with our previous study showing that DMD myoblasts underwent myogenic differentiation more effectively when cultured in the presence of hydrogels functionalized with SDF1 or IL4 peptides compared to collagen I or unmodified hydrogel. Interestingly, such an effect was not observed for the second tested iPSC line, i.e., LMNβ1 (Michalska et al., submitted). Regardless of the cell line type, in the current study we observed that both DMD or LMNβ1 myoblasts were found in acutely injured muscles transplanted with hydrogels only for 7 days and then their presence became limited. As mentioned before, we are currently extending the analysis regarding the indicated finding, as it was quite unexpected in terms of using the SCID mouse model. However, cells co-injected with hydrogels were present in regenerating muscles at least until day 7, indicating that they remained viable after suspension in hydrogels and transplantation into the muscles. Moreover, even their short-term presence was beneficial for muscle reconstruction, indicating that the co-injection of hydrogels with EVs derived from such cells could be considered and tested as cell-free therapy for dysfunctional skeletal muscles. Such an approach would limit some challenges related to therapies using cells, such as issues related to immune response, integration with host tissue, and long-term stability of the graft, but obviously requires additional complex studies.

Assessment of in vivo effects of functionalized hydrogel transplantation resulted in significantly different outcomes in the second muscle injury model used by us in the current study, i.e., chronic injury/dystrophic one. Muscular dystrophies are progressive muscle diseases among which Duchenne muscular dystrophy, characterized by the lack or deficit of DYSTROPHIN, structural muscular protein, presents the most severe phenotype. To this day, there is no cure for this disease and the available treatments aim to mitigate symptoms and improve patients’ quality of life. Due to the well-known role of SDF1 and IL4 as skeletal muscle regeneration support agents, we hypothesized that hydrogels modified with fragments of these molecules could improve the structure and functionality of dystrophic skeletal muscle. Recently, SDF1 was identified as a potential therapeutic target in Duchenne muscular dystrophy, as it was shown to play a crucial role in mediating muscle inflammation. Neurological deficits in mdx mice were reduced after prednisone treatment, which was accompanied by down-regulation of both CXCL12 and CXCR4 mRNA expression, indicating the potential role of the CXCL12 / CXCR4 pathway in Duchenne muscular dystrophy pathology57. However, it should be remembered that the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis plays a pivotal role in skeletal muscle formation during embryonic development58,59 and SDF1 was shown to be an important regulator of SC activation and the proper progression of skeletal muscle regeneration in vivo23. The supportive influence of IL4 on muscle regeneration is played by its known role as the factor involved in myoblast fusion, as well as by orchestrating the fate of FAPs and involvement in the removal of necrotic debris from injured tissue27,60,61. These findings were recently argued by Messing et al.61, who evaluated the effect of IL4 on skeletal muscle regeneration through STAT6 signaling and showed that the pathology observed in Duchenne muscular dystrophy does not depend on this pathway. This is consistent with our observation as noticeable effect of functionalized hydrogel transplantation in dystrophic muscles was observed by us only in the case of hydrogel modified with the SDF-1 mimicking peptide and manifested as an increase in myogenesis, angiogenesis, and neurogenesis markers. The cell co-injection effect was negligible as transplanted cells were detected in dystrophic muscles only on the day of injection and then their presence was rapidly and significantly limited. We are currently investigating the mechanisms underlying such an observation; however, it explains why in the model of dystrophic mice the differences between both approaches (transplantation of hydrogel alone vs. hydrogel and cells) were not actually noticeable. However, in chronic muscle injury/dystrophic mouse model the potential approach based on the SDF-1 functionalized hydrogel co-injected with EVs obtained from iPSC-derived myoblasts could be considered.

In summary, in the current study we evaluated, for the first time, in vivo effects of transplantation of RADA16-I-based hydrogels designed and functionalized by us with SDF1 or IL4 peptides in two models of muscle injury, i.e., acute and chronic/dystrophic one. We showed that hydrogel functionalization was beneficial for promoting factors crucial for key processes of muscle regeneration: myogenesis, angiogenesis, and neurogenesis. In the acute injury model, each functionalized hydrogels increased the expression of such markers (e.g., Myog, Pax7, Lrp4, Dok7, Vegfa, Vwf, Flt1) while in case of dystrophic model only transplantation of SDF1 functionalized hydrogel resulted in such noticeable result. In acute injury model, co-injection of DMD myoblasts additionally enhanced functionalized hydrogel beneficial effects on indicated marker expression while in the dystrophic environment, such an effect was not observed, probably due to rapid disappearance of injected cells. The findings presented in the current study indicate, for the first time, the regenerative utility of SDF1 and IL4-functionalized RADA16-I hydrogels in both acute injured and dystrophic muscles, indicating at the same time that the muscle environment (acute vs. chronic/dystrophic) was crucial for the final result of the hydrogel transplantation, significantly different in both muscle injury models.
