Prevalence and associated factors of mental health conditions among internally displaced persons living in a displacement camp in North Kivu, eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Esther Nabintu Kaboyi, Harry Kayembe, Anki Yambare, Lefilsoo Lambuku, Didier Bompangue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundForced displacement due to armed conflict is associated with a substantial burden of mental health disorders, particularly among internally displaced persons (IDPs) living in camps. In eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), ongoing insecurity and protracted displacement expose IDPs to repeated traumatic events and chronic stressors. However, data remain limited on the prevalence of common mental disorders and their associated factors among camp-based IDPs in North Kivu, especially when accounting for heterogeneous exposure profiles.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted between November and December 2025 among adult IDPs residing in the Nyanzale displacement camp, North Kivu. Anxiety, depression and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) were assessed using the GAD-7, PHQ-9 and PCL-5 scales, respectively. Exposure to potentially traumatic events before and during camp residence was analyzed using multiple correspondence analysis followed by hierarchical clustering to identify homogeneous exposure profiles. Multivariable logistic regression models were fitted separately for each mental health outcome.ResultsA total of 385 participants were included. Prevalences of moderate to severe anxiety, depression and probable PTSD were 56.2%, 43.6% and 47.3%, respectively. Exposure profiles characterized by persistent insecurity within the camp were strongly associated with increased odds of anxiety, depression and PTSD. Profiles marked by indirect exposure and non-sexual violence prior to displacement were also consistently associated with all three outcomes. Women and large household size were associated with higher odds of depression and PTSD, while previous residence in another displacement camp was associated with lower odds of all outcomes.ConclusionsMental health conditions are highly prevalent among IDPs living in camps in North Kivu and are shaped by both past traumatic exposure and ongoing camp-related stressors. These findings underscore the need for integrated mental health interventions that address insecurity and daily stressors in protracted humanitarian settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28005-9.

Background

Armed conflicts continue to represent one of the most significant drivers of humanitarian crises worldwide, with far-reaching consequences for civilian populations. Beyond large-scale destruction of infrastructure and disruption of health systems, conflicts generate chronic insecurity, loss of livelihoods and forced population displacement, all of which have profound implications for mental health [1–3].

The Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) is among the countries most severely affected by internal displacement globally. Ongoing armed violence and political instability have resulted in an estimated 6.8 million internally displaced persons (IDPs), with eastern provinces disproportionately affected [4, 5]. North Kivu province has experienced repeated waves of displacement linked to persistent insecurity and armed violence [5–7]. Displacement sites in this region are characterized by overcrowding, limited access to healthcare and basic services, food insecurity and continued exposure to violence, all of which may contribute to psychological distress among displaced populations [6–8].

IDPs living in camps are exposed not only to war-related traumatic events but also to chronic daily stressors associated with camp life, including insecurity, precarious living conditions and social disruption. Integrative psychosocial models suggest that psychological distress in conflict-affected populations results from the interaction between traumatic exposure and ongoing daily stressors related to displacement conditions [9, 10]. Depression, anxiety disorders and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) are among the most frequently reported mental health conditions in populations affected by armed conflict and forced displacement. A large meta-analysis estimated that 22.1% of individuals living in conflict settings experience depressive or anxiety disorders, while the prevalence of PTSD reaches approximately 26% [11].

Several studies conducted in conflict-affected and displaced populations in sub-Saharan Africa have documented high levels of psychological distress. In northern Uganda, prevalences of depression reached up to 67% and PTSD exceeded 50% among conflict-affected populations [12]. Studies conducted in Ethiopian IDP camps reported depressive symptom prevalences of 62.2%, strongly associated with cumulative exposure to violence [13]. Similar findings have been reported in Liberia and Mozambique among displaced populations [14, 15].

In the DRC, available studies have reported prevalences of depression ranging from 21% to 24%, anxiety from 18% to 20%, and PTSD from 12% to 15% in post-conflict settings [8]. However, data specifically focusing on internally displaced persons living in camps in North Kivu remain limited, despite the intensity and chronicity of insecurity in this region [7, 8, 16]. Despite a growing body of literature documenting the mental health consequences of armed conflict, important gaps persist. Most available studies focus on single mental health outcomes and do not adequately account for the heterogeneity of traumatic exposure profiles experienced before and during displacement [17, 18]. This fragmented approach limits the understanding of shared and context-specific determinants of psychological distress and constrains the development of integrated mental health and psychosocial support interventions.

Thus, the present study aimed to estimate the prevalence of anxiety, depression and PTSD among adult IDPs living in a displacement camp in North Kivu, eastern DRC, and to identify factors associated with these conditions. Using a profile-based approach to characterize heterogeneous exposure patterns, this study seeks to contribute evidence relevant to the development of more contextually adapted mental health interventions in protracted humanitarian settings.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted between November 20 and December 20, 2025, among internally displaced persons residing in a displacement camp in North Kivu Province, eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC). The study was carried out in the Nyanzale IDP camp, located in Rutshuru territory, an area that has experienced recurrent armed violence and repeated population displacement in recent years [5, 19, 20]. At the time of data collection, the camp hosted approximately 325 households, representing nearly 1,885 individuals, living under conditions characterized by limited access to adequate shelter, water, sanitation and healthcare, as well as persistent insecurity [5].

Study population

The target population consisted of internally displaced adults aged 18 years and older who had been residing in the Nyanzale camp at the time of the survey. Participants were eligible if they met all the following criteria: being classified as an internally displaced person, aged ≥ 18 years, and providing informed consent. Individuals presenting severe communication impairments that precluded meaningful interaction, as well as those who declined participation, were excluded from the study [11].

Data sources and collection

Due to the absence of a complete individual-level sampling frame and the organization of the camp around households, a one-stage cluster sampling design was employed, with the household defined as the primary sampling unit. This approach is commonly recommended in humanitarian and displacement settings where conventional random sampling is not feasible [9]. The required sample size was calculated using the single-proportion formula, adjusted for a design effect to account for cluster sampling. The parameters used were an expected prevalence of mental disorders of 22.1%, based on estimates from conflict-affected populations [10, 19], a 5% margin of error, a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), and a design effect of 1.2.

The minimum sample size obtained was 318 individuals. To enhance robustness, this figure was rounded up to 330 and further increased by 10% to account for potential non-response, yielding a target sample size of 363 adults. Assuming an average of three adults per household in displacement camps, the number of households required was estimated at 122. Households were selected using a systematic sampling procedure within the camp, following the random selection of an initial household. A complete list of all 325 households was obtained from camp management structures and used as the sampling frame. A sampling interval (k ≈ 3) was calculated by dividing the total number of households by the required number of households. The first household was selected randomly, and every third household was subsequently included until the desired sample size was reached.

This process resulted in the inclusion of 385 adults, ensuring that the planned sample size was achieved. The initial assumption of an average of three adults per household was used for sample size estimation; however, the observed number of adults per household during data collection was generally lower. Within each selected household, all eligible adults present at the time of the survey were invited to participate, and no additional within-household sampling was performed.

Data was collected using a structured questionnaire administered through individual interviews by trained community-based enumerators. The questionnaire covered socio-demographic characteristics, exposure to potentially traumatic events (PTEs) before and during camp residence, and mental health outcomes assessed using validated psychometric instruments. The survey tool was developed in French and translated into Swahili, the primary language spoken by participants, by bilingual translators familiar with the local context. A forward and backward translation procedure was applied to ensure linguistic and conceptual equivalence.

A pre-test was conducted among approximately 10% of the anticipated sample in a comparable population excluded from the final analysis, allowing for minor adjustments to wording and assessment of feasibility and acceptability.

Data collection was conducted remotely in collaboration with a local community-based organization, the Synergy of Refugees and Returnees for Integral Development (SRDI), which operates under UNHCR coordination and in partnership with international humanitarian organizations. Six enumerators and one supervisor received one day of training covering study objectives, questionnaire content and ethical considerations. The use of trained community enumerators was intended to enhance contextual understanding and reduce misinterpretation of sensitive items, while remote data collection was necessitated by security and logistical constraints limiting direct access to the site.

Measures

Socio-demographic variables

Collected socio-demographic variables included age, sex, marital status, level of education, household size, number of children, duration of stay in the current camp, history of residence in a previous camp, access to primary healthcare services and substance use [1].

Exposure to potentially traumatic events

Exposure to potentially traumatic events was assessed to capture cumulative experiences related to armed conflict and forced displacement, an approach widely used in humanitarian research where individuals are typically exposed to multiple stressors rather than isolated events [1, 11, 21]. Participants reported exposure to a range of traumatic and highly stressful experiences occurring both prior to arrival in the camp and during camp residence, including direct and indirect violence, losses, threats and conditions of deprivation.

Mental health outcomes

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a nine-item screening tool widely validated across cultural settings, including sub-Saharan Africa, and recommended for use in humanitarian contexts. Scores range from 0 to 27, with a threshold of ≥ 10 indicating a high probability of clinically significant depression [22].

Anxiety symptoms were evaluated using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), a validated seven-item instrument commonly used in community and low-resource settings [23].

Post-traumatic stress symptoms were measured using the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5), a validated instrument suitable for use in conflict-affected and humanitarian settings. A cut-off score of 31–33 was used to indicate a high probability of PTSD, in line with guidance from the National Center for PTSD [24].

Minor contextual adaptations were made to certain items to ensure cultural relevance in the displacement camp setting (e.g., replacing “watching television” with “listening to the radio”), without modifying the meaning, structure, or scoring of the standardized instruments [25, 26].

The internal consistency of the instruments was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The PHQ-9 showed acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.74), the PCL-5 showed good internal consistency (α = 0.84), and the GAD-7 showed moderate internal consistency (α = 0.62), which may reflect cultural variations in the expression of anxiety symptoms in this context.

The cut-off values used (≥ 10 for PHQ-9 and GAD-7, and ≥ 33 for PCL-5) were based on international recommendations and have been applied in studies conducted in similar settings [22–24].

Data analysis

Data were exported, cleaned and analyzed using R software. Descriptive analyses summarize socio-demographic characteristics, exposure to traumatic events and mental health outcomes using frequencies and percentages, as well as means and standard deviations (SD) for quantitative and categorical variables, respectively. Bivariate analyses were performed to explore associations between mental health outcomes and explanatory variables using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

To account for heterogeneity in exposure to potentially traumatic events, Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) was applied to PTEs occurring before camp arrival and during camp residence. MCA was used to reduce data dimensionality and to identify underlying structures of association among different forms of exposure. The distinction between exposures occurring before and during camp residence was based on retrospective self-report and should be interpreted with caution given the cross-sectional design of the study.

The number of retained dimensions was determined based on eigenvalues and inspection of the scree plot. The proportion of variance explained by the selected dimensions was examined to retain the most informative axes.

The stability of the factorial structure was assessed using a split-half procedure combined with Procrustes analysis and permutation testing. Hierarchical clustering on principal components (HCPC) was subsequently performed to identify homogeneous profiles of exposure. The optimal number of clusters was determined based on dendrogram interpretation and the gain in between-cluster inertia. Classes were characterized using v-tests, with absolute values ≥ 2 considered indicative of significant over- or under-representation of specific exposure modalities [27]. These exposure profiles were recoded as categorical variables for inclusion in regression models.

Multivariable logistic regression models were fitted for anxiety, depression and PTSD to identify associated factors. Variables included in the multivariable models were selected based on theoretical relevance and results from bivariate analyses.

Models included trauma exposure profiles derived from MCA, as well as socio-demographic variables. Results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and pseudo-R² measures. Multicollinearity between explanatory variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF).

Results

Table 1 presents the socio-demographic characteristics of the participants. A total of 385 internally displaced adults were included in the analysis. The study population was predominantly female (61.3%), with a mean age of 36 years. Most participants were aged 18–44 years (74.1%), living as a couple (79.2%), and had no formal education (54.3%). Households were generally large, with 77.1% of participants living in households of five or more members. More than half of respondents (54.8%) reported having previously lived in another displacement camp, and most had been residing in the current camp for seven to twelve months (77.1%).

Discussion

This study aimed to describe the socio-demographic characteristics, traumatic histories, and prevalence of mental health disorders among internally displaced persons living in a camp in North Kivu. The findings depict a predominantly female, young, and low-educated population exposed to multiple traumatic events before and after displacement, with relatively high levels of anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). These mental health outcomes were associated with profiles characterized by previous displacement to another camp, exposure to indirect and non-sexual violence before displacement, and persistent insecurity within the camp.

The study population was predominantly female, young, and poorly educated, a profile widely reported in protracted armed conflict settings, particularly in the DRC and across sub-Saharan Africa. The overrepresentation of women among internally displaced populations reflects gendered dynamics specific to conflict settings, where women and children constitute the majority of those fleeing insecurity [1, 3, 11]. Several mechanisms may explain this distribution. On the one hand, men are more directly exposed to armed combat, forced recruitment, arbitrary arrests, and targeted killings, resulting in excess male mortality in conflict zones [9]. On the other hand, displacement to camps more frequently involves women, children, and older persons, while some men remain in areas of origin to protect family property or engage directly or indirectly in hostilities [2].

Exposure to potentially traumatic events was extremely high in the study population, reflecting a context of chronic violence marked by prolonged armed conflict and repeated displacement. Almost all participants reported at least one traumatic event, confirming the magnitude of violence experienced by civilian populations in eastern DRC. Prior to arrival in the camp, reported events were predominantly directly related to armed conflict, including fighting near the home, destruction of property, exposure to extreme violence, and loss of relatives. Such cumulative exposure to severe events is well documented in armed conflict settings, where civilians are subjected to multiple and repeated traumas [12, 21, 28]. In addition, the high frequency of severe interpersonal violence, particularly sexual violence and attempted rape, aligns with patterns observed in humanitarian crises, which are often characterized by increased levels of gender-based violence [3, 29, 30]. These findings are especially concerning in North Kivu, where sexual violence has been described as systemic and persistent, including within displacement camps [16, 29].

The study further demonstrates that exposure to adversity does not end with displacement. Situations of insecurity and ongoing stress were reported during camp life, highlighting the continuity of risk after displacement. This observation is consistent with previous studies showing that displacement does not equate to safety, and that daily stressors related to insecurity, precarious living conditions, and limited access to essential resources are associated with the persistence or worsening of psychological distress [10, 13, 18, 31].

Beyond this overall high level of exposure, the multiple correspondence analysis combined with hierarchical clustering revealed marked heterogeneity in violence exposure trajectories among internally displaced persons. These findings suggest that traumatic experiences in prolonged conflict settings are not uniform but may be organized into differentiated exposure patterns, as reported in studies using exposure profiling approaches in humanitarian settings [30, 32]. Before camp arrival, the identification of a profile characterized by cumulative severe violence reflects trajectories marked by repeated exposure to direct violence, typically associated with armed confrontations, attacks against civilians, and forced displacement [8, 33] Such cumulative exposure has been repeatedly associated with severe mental health outcomes, particularly PTSD and anxiety–depressive comorbidity [15, 18, 25]. Conversely, the low-exposure pre-camp profile may reflect displacement trajectories more closely linked to diffuse or anticipated insecurity without systematic exposure to extreme violence, illustrating preventive displacement mechanisms reported in other contexts of protracted internal displacement [32].

During the camp stay, the emergence of profiles ranging from low exposure to cumulative severe exposure within the camp highlights the potential role of the post-displacement environment in the persistence or exacerbation of trauma. Chronic insecurity, precarious living conditions, and insufficient essential services represent prolonged stressors that may sustain or aggravate mental health symptoms, independently of prior trauma exposure [8]. These profiles may provide a useful analytical framework for understanding heterogeneous vulnerability patterns among internally displaced populations.

The high prevalence rates observed confirm the substantial burden of mental morbidity associated with forced displacement in contexts of persistent insecurity. These levels are comparable to, or higher than, those reported in other post-conflict African settings. In Uganda, studies among internally displaced persons have reported depression prevalence exceeding 60% and PTSD prevalence above 50% several years after the official end of armed conflict [12]. Similarly high proportions have been documented in Mozambique (Cabo Delgado), where over 40% of displaced persons present common mental disorders, and in Ethiopia, where nearly two-thirds of internally displaced individuals report significant depressive symptoms [13, 15]. In the DRC, a study conducted in the post-conflict Kasai province reported depression prevalence between 21% and 24%, anxiety between 18% and 20%, and PTSD between 12% and 15%, indicating that mental health burden remains substantial even outside zones of active conflict [8]. In comparison, the levels observed in the Nyanzale camp suggest particularly elevated levels of psychological distress in a context characterized by recent displacement and persistent insecurity.

Multivariable analysis highlighted the central role of living conditions and environmental insecurity in the occurrence of mental health disorders. Exposure profiles characterized by persistent insecurity within the camp emerged as major factors associated with anxiety, depression, and PTSD, even after adjustment for pre-displacement traumatic experiences. These findings support previous work suggesting that post-displacement stressors are strongly associated with psychological distress, sometimes beyond conflict-related trauma itself [9, 10]. Pre-displacement traumatic exposures, particularly those involving cumulative severe violence, were strongly associated with depression, suggesting a lasting impact of losses, social disruption, and prolonged violence on mood disorders.

This differentiation between mental health outcomes aligns with observations from Uganda, Afghanistan, and Ethiopia, where depression appears particularly sensitive to the accumulation and chronicity of adversities, while anxiety and PTSD are more reactive to current threats and persistent insecurity [12, 13, 18, 34]. Women appeared more vulnerable to depression and PTSD, a finding widely documented in forced displacement contexts. In eastern DRC, systematic exposure to gender-based violence combined with increased social and family role burdens in precarious environments may contribute to the disproportionate deterioration of displaced women’s mental health [6, 14, 16]. Larger household size was also associated with increased risk of depression, likely reflecting overcrowding, food insecurity, and economic pressure factors consistently identified as major correlates of psychological distress in displacement camps [10, 18, 35]. According to the ADAPT model, the inability to fulfill social and family roles constitutes a central disruption of psychosocial pillars, favoring the emergence of depressive symptoms [36].

Conversely, having previously lived in another displacement camp before Nyanzale appeared as a protective factor against mental health disorders. Although potentially counterintuitive, this finding may be interpreted considering several theoretical frameworks and empirical observations in humanitarian settings. Greater familiarity with humanitarian aid mechanisms, pre-existing social networks, and improved adaptive capacity to camp constraints may mitigate stress related to initial displacement [9, 10, 37]. However, selection bias, survivor bias, or unmeasured resilience factors cannot be excluded, and this interpretation should remain cautious.

Overall, these findings contribute to a growing body of evidence suggesting that mental health outcomes among internally displaced persons are associated with the interaction between past traumatic experiences and ongoing environmental stressors. In protracted crises such as those affecting North Kivu, mental health responses that fail to address daily insecurity and structural conditions within camps may have limited long-term effectiveness.

This study has several strengths. It was conducted in a conflict-affected and under-researched setting in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo and included a relatively large community-based sample of internally displaced persons. Standardized and widely used instruments (PHQ-9, GAD-7, and PCL-5) were employed to assess mental health outcomes. In addition, the use of multiple correspondence analysis combined with hierarchical clustering provided a person-centered approach that captured the heterogeneity of violence exposure trajectories and offered a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between traumatic experiences and mental health outcomes.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between traumatic exposures, camp living conditions, and mental health outcomes. In addition, the distinction between events occurring before displacement and during camp residence was based on retrospective self-report and should be interpreted with caution. Data were self-reported and may therefore be affected by recall and social desirability bias. Although widely used instruments (PHQ-9, GAD-7, and PCL-5) were employed, their cultural validity and the diagnostic performance of the cut-off values used may vary in this humanitarian setting. Anxiety, depression, and PTSD were analyzed separately despite their likely co-occurrence, and no formal adjustment was made for intra-household clustering; however, given the small number of adults per household, the impact on standard errors is likely to be limited. Some potentially important confounders, including socioeconomic status and cumulative trauma burden, were not explicitly measured, which may have resulted in residual confounding. Remote data collection may have limited direct observation of the study context and reduced the capture of non-verbal interactions. Finally, selection and survivor bias cannot be excluded, as the most severely affected individuals may not have survived the violence or reached the camp, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true burden of mental health disorders.

Conclusion

This study highlights the high burden of anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder among IDPs living in the Nyanzale camp in North Kivu, eastern DRC. More than half of participants presented moderate to severe anxiety symptoms, nearly half met criteria for probable PTSD, and more than two in five reported moderate to severe depressive symptoms. Profiles characterized by persistent insecurity within the camp and by indirect and non-sexual violence before displacement were consistently associated with all three mental health outcomes. In contrast, previous residence in another displacement camp was associated with lower odds of psychological distress, suggesting that adaptation or resilience mechanisms may play a role, although this finding should be interpreted with caution. These findings underscore the importance of addressing not only past traumatic experiences but also ongoing post-displacement stressors, including insecurity and poor living conditions within camps. In protracted humanitarian crises such as those affecting North Kivu, integrated mental health and psychosocial support interventions should be combined with efforts to improve security, access to essential services, and overall living conditions. Accounting for heterogeneous exposure profiles may help guide more targeted and contextually appropriate mental health responses.
