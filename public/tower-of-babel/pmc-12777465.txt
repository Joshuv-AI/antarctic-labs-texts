Development and validation of NTCP models for predicting acute esophagitis in carbon ion radiotherapy for thoracic malignancies
Authors: Yongqiang Li, Liwen Zhang, Yuqi Liu, Ningyi Ma, Kai-Liang Wu, Jingfang Mao, Jian Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute esophagitis (AE) is a common toxicity following carbon ion radiotherapy (CIRT) for thoracic malignancies (TM). Despite CIRT’s physical advantages, specific normal tissue complication probability (NTCP) models for predicting AE remain underdeveloped.PurposeThis study aims to develop NTCP models using clinical data to identify key risk factors for AE during CIRT, enabling personalized treatment optimization to reduce esophageal toxicity.Methods168 patients with TM (non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC): 95, tracheobronchial adenoid cystic carcinoma (TACC): 43, thoracic bone and soft tissue sarcomas (TBSTS): 30) treated with CIRT (59.5–80 Gy (RBE) in 16–23 fractions) were analyzed. Paradoxically, non-drinkers had 9-fold higher G2 risk (14.0% vs. 1.5%), while smoking showed protection (non-smokers vs. smokers, G2:1.2% vs. 17.1%). Background

Thoracic malignancies (TM), particularly lung and esophageal cancers, pose significant global health challenges [1]. The management of these tumors is complicated by their insidious onset, nonspecific symptoms, and frequent surgical ineligibility due to patient comorbidities or advanced disease at presentation. Radiotherapy has consequently emerged as a cornerstone of treatment [2], though its efficacy is limited by toxicity to adjacent structures, particularly radiation-induced esophagitis which may necessitate treatment interruption [3].

Particle radiotherapy, especially using pencil beam scanning (PBS) carbon-ion beam representing a cutting-edge treatment modality, offering superior dose conformity and enhanced biological effectiveness compared to conventional photon therapy [4, 5]. While CIRT-PBS demonstrates remarkable precision in TM, acute esophagitis (AE) is still a significant and common side effect of radiotherapy, particularly in patients with thoracic tumors, as it can lead to discomfort, swallowing difficulties, pain, and reduced quality of life [6]. It is a critical complication that may also interfere with the completion of the intended radiation therapy course, ultimately impacting the treatment efficacy. Radiation-induced esophagitis can range in severity from mild symptoms such as dysphagia and pain to severe conditions requiring hospitalization and medical intervention [7]. As the esophagus is a relatively radiosensitive organ, minimizing radiation exposure to this tissue is crucial for reducing the occurrence and severity of this side effect.

The development of effective dose constraints to limit esophageal radiation exposure is a priority for clinicians to optimize treatment plans and balance the need for tumor control with minimizing toxicity to surrounding normal tissues. Current understanding of AE risk factors primarily derives from photon and proton radiotherapy studies, identifying variables such as esophageal dose-volume parameters, concurrent chemotherapy, and patient comorbidities as significant predictors. Although photon and proton NTCP models for esophagitis are well-established, their application to CIRT faces three fundamental limitations: (1) RBE simplification: where photon models assume uniform biological effectiveness and proton models typically use constant RBE = 1.1, both failing to address the Linear Energy Transfer (LET)-dependent effects crucial for carbon ions; (2) dose-rate neglect: with existing models rarely considering the ultra-high dose-rate effects unique to CIRT delivery; and (3) anatomic specificity: as most models derive from photon IMRT cases exhibiting substantially different esophageal dose distributions compared to carbon ion Bragg peaks. While microdosimetry, fractionation interactions, and penumbra effects are fundamental to CIRT biology, the present study establishes a foundational NTCP model based on RBE-weighted dose calculated using the Local Effect Model (LEM), providing a crucial clinical tool and a necessary step toward future models that may explicitly incorporate these advanced physical and biological concepts. This study utilizes the LEM for RBE-weighted dose calculation, as implemented in our institution’s treatment planning system. Despite the advantages of CIRT, the variation in relative biological effect (RBE) [8] and the complexity of calculating biological effective doses (BED) have posed significant challenges in establishing dose constraints for normal tissues. Different carbon ion treatment devices and biological models used in clinical settings can lead to discrepancies in the estimation of RBE, making it difficult to create a standardized approach for normal tissue dose limits [9], resulting in a lack of consensus on esophageal dose threshold.

To address these challenges for providing an achievable methodology on determine unique dose limits for an individual planning system as our system, the present study aims to develop and validate a normal tissue complication probability (NTCP) model for predicting AE following thoracic CIRT. By identifying key dosimetric and clinical predictors of AE and analyzing their associations with toxicity outcomes, we seek to establish CIRT-specific dose-response relationships for the esophagus. A secondary objective is to propose evidence-based dose constraints derived from retrospective clinical data to guide esophageal sparing in thoracic carbon ion therapy. Our overarching goal is to facilitate personalized treatment planning strategies that minimize esophageal toxicity while preserving the therapeutic efficacy of CIRT. We hypothesize that NTCP models incorporating PBS-specific dosimetric parameters and clinical factors will enable accurate prediction of AE risk and contribute to the development of optimized, clinically actionable treatment protocols.

Methods

Patient population

This retrospective study included 168 patients with thoracic malignancies who received carbon ion radiotherapy (CIRT) at Shanghai Proton and Heavy Ion Center between 2015 and 2023. The cohort comprised patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (stage I-II, the American Joint Committee on Cancer staging 8th edition), tracheobronchial adenoid cystic carcinoma (TACC), and thoracic bone and soft tissue sarcomas (TBSTS). The CIRT dose unit Gy refers to the RBE-weighted dose, which was calculated using the LEM in the treatment planning system.

Patients were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) histologically confirmed thoracic tumors treated with curative-intent CIRT; (2) prescribed single-fraction dose between 3.0 and 4.0 Gy (RBE); (3) minimum follow-up of 3 months for acute toxicity assessment; (4) complete clinical and dosimetric data available; (5) measurable esophageal dose > 0 Gy (RBE); and (6) signed informed consent for data use. Exclusion criteria included: (1) prior thoracic radiotherapy; (2) receipt of concurrent chemotherapy; or (3) treatment with concurrent photon or proton therapy. This research had been approved by the institutional review boards of Shanghai proton and heavy ion center (SPHIC) (250624EXP-01) and all patients provided written informed consent before admission. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Acute esophagitis was graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 4.03. This grading system categorizes esophagitis severity into five grades, from Grade 1 (mild) to Grade 5 (death). Grade 1 esophagitis includes mild symptoms such as pain or dysphagia, while Grade 2 esophagitis involves more significant symptoms requiring medical intervention, and Grade 3 or higher involves severe symptoms requiring hospitalization or leading to treatment discontinuation.

CIRT treatment planning and delivery

Prior to treatment, all patients undergo multidisciplinary evaluation to confirm eligibility and provide informed consent. Patients are immobilized in supine/prone position using vacuum bags, with respiratory motion management determined by tumor displacement: For tumors with > 5 mm motion (assessed by 4D-computer tomography (CT)), respiratory gating/active breathing control (ABC)/compression was employed to maintain planning dose accuracy (aiming for < 5% deviation from planned vs. delivered dose, per institutional QA protocols and clinical goals [10, 11]). This ensures the planned dose distribution serves as a valid surrogate for actual esophageal exposure in AE analysis [10, 11]. Simulation CT (1–3 mm slices) encompasses the entire lungs plus 10 cm beyond the tumor, with 4D-CT performed for gating cases to define the respiratory window (typically end-exhalation). The gross tumor volume (GTV) was delineated based on contrast-enhanced thoracic CT/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG-PET/CT) imaging. For patients undergoing respiratory-gated radiotherapy, an internal gross tumor volume (iGTV) was generated by merging the GTVs from all respiratory phases. The clinical target volume (CTV) was typically created by expanding the GTV/iGTV with a uniform expansion of 0.6 cm for NSCLC, a 1.0–2.0 cm longitudinal and 0.5–1.0 cm radial margin for TACC and a 4.0 cm longitudinal and 1.5 cm radial margin for TBSTS. To account for treatment uncertainties, the planning target volume (PTV) was established by adding margins to the CTV: 0.3–0.5 cm laterally and 0.5–1.2 cm along the beam axis [12, 13].

Treatment planning and dose parameters

The treatment plans for all patients were generated using Syngo treatment planning system (TPS) with the Local Effect Model (LEM). Several key dosimetric parameters were extracted from the TPS for analysis, which were crucial for evaluating the risk of esophageal toxicity. These parameters included (1) Esophageal volume (the total volume of the esophagus receiving radiation), (2) Maximum dose (Dmax): The highest dose delivered to any point within the esophagus, (3) Mean dose (Dmean): The average dose delivered to the entire esophagus and (4) Dose-volume metrics. These metrics describe the percentage of the esophageal volume receiving a specific dose of radiation, such as V1Gy (RBE) to V81Gy (RBE) (the percentage of the esophageal volume receiving doses of 1 Gy (RBE) to 81 Gy (RBE) in 1 Gy (RBE) intervals); D0.1cc to D0.5cc (the maximum dose delivered to volumes of 0.1 cc and 0.5 cc, respectively; D1-10 cc (the maximum dose delivered to volumes between 1 cc and 10 cc); D15cc and D10% (the minimum dose to the hottest 15 cc and 10% of the esophageal volume, respectively). These dose parameters were selected based on their ability to provide a comprehensive understanding of the radiation dose distribution within the esophagus, which is crucial for evaluating the risk of AE.

Statistical analysis and NTCP modeling: univariate and multivariate logistic regression

To identify predictive factors associated with acute esophagitis, both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed. In univariate analysis, each dose parameter was independently assessed for its potential relationship with acute esophagitis, with statistical significance determined at a p-value of less than 0.05. The candidate predictors of acute esophagitis included dosimetric predictors of esophagus and clinical factors associated with quality-of-life and acute toxicity. The clinical factors included patient age (treated as a continuous variable), smoking history (1 = smoker, 0 = non-smoker), alcohol consumption (1 = drinker, 0 = non-drinker), and prior chemotherapy before radiotherapy (1 = yes, 0 = no). Continuous variables, such as age, were treated as continuous in the logistic regression analysis to preserve statistical power. Variables showing a potential association in the univariate analysis (p < 0.1) were entered into the multivariate logistic regression model. Model selection was performed using forward selection, and the final model was chosen based on the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), balancing model fit and complexity.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess the diagnostic performance of the predictive models, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each dosimetric parameter and multivariate model. Results

Patient characteristics and treatment details

From May 2015 to December 2023, a total of 168 eligible patients were included in the analysis. The cohort comprised 95 patients with stage I-II NSCLC, 43 with TACC, and 30 with TBSTS. A total of 87 cases developed AE of grade ≥ 1 (G1), indicating that esophageal toxicity was relatively common but generally mild. The patients who developed grade ≥ 2 (G2) esophagitis required medical intervention but did not experience life-threatening complications. No cases of grade ≥ 3 AE were observed. All patients were prescribed a dose range of 59.5–80 Gy (RBE) delivered in 16–23 fractions, with a single fraction dose of 3–4 Gy (RBE). In a small subset of patients, additional fractions were administered due to treatment interruptions (e.g., esophagitis, infectious pneumonia, or other clinical complications), resulting in cumulative doses exceeding 80 Gy (RBE) in select cases. Univariate analysis

Univariate logistic regression analyses were performed to assess potential associations between dosimetric parameters and the development of grade 1-2 AE, adjusted for clinical covariates (cancer type, gender, age, smoking status, alcohol status, and receipt of chemotherapy prior to CIRT). The evaluated dosimetric parameters included esophageal volume, maximum dose, mean dose, dose-volume parameters (V1Gy (RBE) to V81Gy (RBE)), and point doses (D0.1cc to D0.5cc, and D1cc to D10cc). As presented data in the univariate analysis column of Table 3, we found that the parameters with the statistically significant associations (p < 0.05) were V44Gy (RBE) (volume of esophagus receiving ≥ 44Gy (RBE)) for observed G1 and D9cc (dose to the hottest 9cc volume of the esophagus), age, and smoking status for G2 esophagitis. Other dose-volume parameters did not show a statistically significant association (p ≥ 0.05) in the univariate analysis.

Multivariate analysis

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of acute esophagitis, by assessing the independent predictive value of each variable when adjusted for others. Lyman-kutcher-burman (LKB) model and NTCP estimation

Based on the clinical data presented in Table 2, the best-fit dosimetric parameters for the LKB NTCP model predicting AE and its grade-specific endpoints within the entire cohort are summarized for the esophagus in Table 4. For G1 AE, the fitted LKB model yielded the following parameters: n = 0.23 (95% CI: 0.05–0.45), m = 0.81 (95% CI: 0.2–1.0), tolerance dose for TD50 = 62.8 Gy (RBE) (95% CI: 35.5–75.5 Gy (RBE)), and estimated NTCP = 54% (SD: 24%). In Table 4, the model demonstrated good predictive performance for G1 AE, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.792 (95% CI: 0.722–0.861, p < 0.001) and a Youden index of 0.51. The optimal cut-off points to limit G1 AE risk below 46.6% was a EUD of 58.2 Gy (RBE), corresponding to a sensitivity of 0.84 and 1–specificity of 0.33.

Internal validation of the multivariate and LKB-based NTCP models was performed using ROC curve analysis. Figure 3 illustrates the ROC curves for both NTCP models in predicting G1 and G2 AE in the esophagus. The multivariate model demonstrates excellent discriminative capability, with AUC values of 0.863 for G1 and 0.936 for G2 AE. Internal data validation was performed to assess the robustness and accuracy of the LKB-based NTCP model. It is important to note that this represents internal validation; the lack of an external validation cohort is a limitation of this work.

Discussion

Clinical significance of acute esophagitis in carbon ion radiotherapy

AE remains a prevalent dose-limiting toxicity in thoracic CIRT, even with the superior dose conformity achieved by PBS. In this cohort, over half of patients (51.8%) developed AE, predominantly Grade 1, while 8.9% experienced Grade 2 toxicity requiring clinical intervention. These findings highlight the importance of careful esophageal dose evaluation and the need for tailored constraints, particularly in anatomically challenging or high-risk cases. In our cohort, 51.8% (87/168) of patients developed acute esophagitis, with Grade 1 toxicity predominating. This high incidence underscores the imperative for rigorous esophageal dose monitoring even with advanced techniques like PBS CIRT. Notably, 8.9% of patients experienced Grade 2 toxicity, indicating clinically relevant complications that may compromise treatment tolerance, deteriorate quality of life, and impair nutritional status. Risk stratification by tumor type revealed the highest AE incidence in TACC, likely due to the proximity between tumor and esophagus. In contrast, early-stage NSCLC exhibited the lowest AE rates, probably benefiting from more favorable geometry and tissue separation, that is, may be related to its small lesion volume, absence of mediastinal lymph node metastasis (thus reducing radiation dose to the esophagus). Additionally, less frequent chemotherapy administration may also serve as a contributing factor. These observations underscore the value of histology- and location-specific planning approaches, with stricter esophageal sparing prioritized for large, centrally located or infiltrative lesions [19–22]. Sex- and age-specific differences also emerged. Male patients showed increased AE risk, potentially due to anatomical and dosimetric factors such as larger thoracic dimensions and higher mean esophageal doses. Similarly, younger patients had elevated AE incidence, possibly reflecting more aggressive treatment regimens and heightened mucosal radiosensitivity. These findings support incorporating patient demographic factors into personalized risk-adaptive planning [23]. Younger patients show increased radiation esophagitis incidence versus older individuals, driven by multifactorial biological and treatment differences. Enhanced mucosal proliferation and inflammatory responses heighten radiosensitivity in younger patients, while age-related fibrosis may confer partial radiotolerance in elders. Treatment intensity diverges significantly: younger patients typically receive aggressive regimens (higher doses, chemotherapy) for more extensive tumors, amplified by better performance status enabling intensified therapy. Concurrently, symptom underreporting in older patients with comorbidities may mask early toxicity. These findings necessitate age-adapted strategies, including stricter esophageal constraints for younger patients and personalized risk modeling accounting for age-dependent radiation responses. Further investigation should clarify underlying molecular pathways and validate age as an independent predictive factor [24]. Interestingly, AE rates were paradoxically higher among non-drinkers and non-smokers. This paradoxical 'protective effect' is likely due to strong confounding by tumor type. In our cohort, there was a significant statistical association between smoking history and tumor type (e.g., TACC patients, who had the highest AE risk, were predominantly never-smokers, while NSCLC patients often had a smoking history). Since TACC tumors are typically larger and located closer to the esophagus, tumor type itself is a strong risk factor for AE, which likely explains the apparent association with smoking status. While this could reflect unmeasured confounding—such as baseline mucosal vulnerability, comorbidities, or treatment selection bias—it highlights the complexity of interpreting behavioral risk factors in retrospective studies. Routine assessment of mucosal health and lifestyle-related comorbidities may help contextualize such findings in clinical practice [25]. Patients receiving CIRT without prior chemotherapy experienced higher AE risk. This may be related to case selection bias. Although patients with TACC demonstrated the highest incidence of AE (G1: 88.4%, G2: 20.9%), they rarely received chemotherapy. Conversely, early-stage NSCLC patients constituted the largest cohort (95) with the lowest incidence of AE (G1: 35.8%, G2: 1.1%) yet a higher proportion received chemotherapy than those with TACC. Overall, these results reinforce the need for individualized AE risk prediction and NTCP-guided dose constraint optimization in thoracic CIRT. By integrating clinical and dosimetric predictors, NTCP models can inform evidence-based planning decisions that balance tumor coverage with normal tissue preservation [26].

Identification of key predictive dosimetric parameters

By retrospectively analyzing the cohort of 168 CIRT patients, the LKB and multivariate NTCP models of the esophagus were established to predict the NTCP probabilities for two AE levels. The LKB model revealed volume effect parameters (n) approaching zero for both toxicity grades, indicating that the esophagus behaves as a serial organ with respect to radiation injury—where high-dose exposure to even small sub-volumes can result in clinically significant complications. Notably, the NTCP curve for G2 AE displayed a steeper slope than that for G1 AE (Fig. 1), reflecting a smaller “m” value and sharper dose-response relationship. The increase in TD₅₀ from 62.8 Gy (RBE) (G1) to 80.0 Gy (RBE) (G2) suggests a higher dose threshold is required to trigger more severe toxicity, consistent with the escalating nature of esophageal injury. The difference in the volume effect parameter 'n' between the Grade 1 and Grade 2 models is intriguing. It may suggest different underlying biological mechanisms for varying toxicity severities; milder Grade 1 reactions could be more related to mucosal surface effects (lower volume dependence), while Grade 2 reactions might involve deeper tissue layers with a different volumetric response. Alternatively, this discrepancy could be influenced by the smaller number of Grade 2 events, leading to greater uncertainty in the parameter estimate.

Multivariate modeling identified V44Gy (RBE) (volume receiving ≥ 44 Gy (RBE)) and D9cc (dose to the hottest 9 cc) as the most robust predictors for G1 and G2 esophagitis, respectively. These parameters, which characterize focal high-dose exposure, outperformed traditional metrics such as mean esophageal dose. This finding diverges from photon-based models that emphasize large-volume parameters, underscoring the distinctive dosimetric profile of carbon ions: high LET, increased RBE, and sharp dose fall-off make localized hotspots particularly impactful in normal tissue complication risk.

ROC analysis demonstrated excellent discriminative performance, with AUCs of 0.830 for V44Gy (RBE) and 0.936 for D9cc, supporting their clinical utility as dose constraints in treatment planning. Implementation of these parameters may allow for improved therapeutic ratio by minimizing esophageal toxicity without compromising target coverage. These results highlight the need for modality-specific dose-volume constraints in particle therapy. Although the pure dosimetric LKB model showed a slightly inferior AUC, the multivariate logistic regression model may be more clinically practical for implementation. It enables truly individualized risk assessment by integrating both dosimetric and patient-specific clinical factors, providing a more holistic basis for personalized planning decisions. Future prospective studies are needed to validate these thresholds in independent cohorts and to further elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying focal-dose sensitivity in esophageal tissue under high-LET exposure.

Our analysis revealed three key insights about model performance that we have now addressed: (1) The LKB model's inferior performance (AUC 0.764 vs. 0.936) primarily stems from its inability to accurately characterize steep dose-response relationships in small high-dose volumes (D5cc), as its sigmoidal shape underestimates toxicity risk at carbon ion's sharp Bragg peak margins (2) The 23% AUC improvement from incorporating clinical variables (age/smoking status) demonstrates that biological heterogeneity modifies radiation sensitivity beyond pure physical dose effects, particularly for grade ≥ 2 esophagitis where patient-specific factors; and (3) This advantage comes with the cost of requiring additional patient data collection, comparing logistic regression against traditional LKB approaches for clinical deployment.

Clinical implications of the NTCP model

The development and validation of our LKB NTCP model marks a significant advancement in personalized carbon-ion radiotherapy, demonstrating excellent predictive accuracy (AUC=0.79) for AE risk. Clinically, this model enables: 1) Enhanced risk assessment through quantitative evaluation of both dosimetric and clinical risk factors; 2) Personalized planning by providing evidence-based dose-volume constraints tailored to individual patient profiles (e.g., age, smoking history); and 3) Objective metrics for toxicity risk comparison during plan optimization. However, the implementation of these models in real-time clinical planning faces challenges, including computational integration into treatment planning systems and workflow considerations, which represent important areas for future development.

A primary limitation of this work is the lack of an independent, external validation cohort. While internal validation via bootstrapping and cross-validation was performed, the generalizability of our models must be confirmed in prospective, multi-institutional studies. Future priorities include developing Artificial Intelligence (AI)-powered real-time prediction tools and establishing universal NTCP thresholds through collaborative research, particularly crucial as carbon-ion therapy expands globally. These advancements will optimize therapeutic ratios by accounting for heavy-ion radiobiology while standardizing toxicity prevention protocols.

Future directions

To enhance the clinical utility of our NTCP model and dose constraints, five research priorities require investigation: 1) Multi-institutional prospective validation across diverse populations and treatment protocols, incorporating standardized data collection; 2) Integrated predictive models combining dosimetric parameters with biological markers (genetic polymorphisms, inflammatory cytokines) and clinical factors for superior risk stratification; 3) Systematic evaluation of late complications (esophageal ulcer, perforation, fistula, etc) to complement acute toxicity data; 4) Application of advanced machine learning to uncover non-linear variable relationships beyond conventional modeling; and 5) Development of clinic-ready decision support tools with real-time risk visualization for treatment planning systems. Implementing this agenda will advance personalized risk prediction in particle therapy and optimize therapeutic outcomes.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated a NTCP model for acute esophagitis in CIRT. Key dosimetric predictors—particularly V44Gy (RBE) for Grade 1; D9cc, age and smoking status for Grade 2 esophagitis—demonstrated strong predictive performance and clinical relevance. While demonstrating significant potential for personalized treatment planning, clinical implementation requires multicenter validation. Future refinements should incorporate additional clinical covariates and evaluate long-term toxicities to enhance predictive accuracy. Collectively, these findings establish a critical foundation for optimizing the therapeutic ratio of CIRT in TM.
