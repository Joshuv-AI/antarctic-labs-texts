Hidden Mould in the Air: A Practical Narrative Mini‐Review of Invasive Mould Infection Prevention for High‐Risk Patients and Clinicians
Authors: Angelika Bauer, Cornelia Lass‐Flörl, Stephan Steixner, Oliver Cornely, Ilana Reinhold, Laura Walti, International Society for Human and Animal Mycology (ISHAM) Working Group for Patient Representation and Advocacy in Invasive Fungal Infections
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAirborne mould spores, including those of the Aspergillus species, are ubiquitous both indoors and outdoors. While they are generally harmless, inhaled spores can cause severe infections, particularly invasive pulmonary aspergillosis, in patients with impaired immune defences. Allergic manifestations such as asthma arise by different mechanisms and are not covered. This narrative mini‐review summarises key evidence and guideline recommendations for healthcare teams and provides practical, patient‐friendly advice by highlighting clinical and infection‐prevention considerations. As there is no safe exposure threshold or minimum infectious dose for individual patients, prevention focuses on reducing avoidable exposure during high‐risk periods. These high‐risk periods typically refer to medical conditions such as prolonged neutropenia, the early post‐transplant period, and high‐dose corticosteroid therapy. Furthermore, prevention also focuses on optimising hospital environmental controls by implementing high‐efficiency particulate air filter filtration, protective rooms and construction dust containment, alongside antifungal prophylaxis when indicated. The most impactful measures at home are preventing and rapidly remediating visible mould and dampness, avoiding high‐spore activities such as composting, handling potting soil, and working with demolition or renovation dust, and maintaining simple hygiene practices that reduce dust reservoirs. This narrative mini‐review also summarises what the literature can and cannot provide. Although Aspergillus species account for most of the available evidence, the same exposure‐reduction principles apply to other environmentally acquired moulds, including Mucorales, Fusarium species and Scedosporium species.

Introduction

Airborne fungal spores (conidia) are ubiquitous, and humans inhale them daily. Whether this exposure progresses to infection is primarily determined by the competence of the host's immune system and pulmonary defence mechanisms rather than by measured spore counts at a single time point [1, 2]. For patients with haematological malignancy or those undergoing blood‐cell transplantation from donors, invasive mould infections—particularly invasive aspergillosis—remain an important and life‐threatening complication, associated with substantial morbidity and mortality [2, 3, 4, 5]. In this context, patients often seek practical advice on meaningful environmental risks and effective preventive measures. However, there is currently no validated minimum infectious dose or universally applicable safe indoor spore threshold for severely immunocompromised patients given substantial inter‐individual susceptibility and the methodological variability of environmental sampling [1, 2, 6, 7]. Therefore, prevention should prioritise interventions with the strongest evidence and guideline support. This narrative mini‐review provides a pragmatic, risk‐adapted framework for high‐risk patients and clinicians who care for them.

Identifying Relevant Literature

This manuscript was prepared as a narrative mini‐review of mould prevention strategies in patients at high‐risk for invasive mould infections. Its aim was to identify clinically practicable prevention measures for routine care. Given the heterogeneity of the available evidence and the predominance of studies addressing diagnostic rather than preventive aspects, a formal systematic review and meta‐analysis were not considered the most appropriate approach. Instead, a structured narrative synthesis was performed. The literature search was conducted in MEDLINE via PubMed and restricted to peer‐reviewed publications published between January 1999 and June 2026. Emphasis was placed on guidelines, consensus documents and original studies relevant to prevention strategies in high‐risk populations. Articles were selected based on relevance, methodological credibility and clinical applicability.

The aim was not to provide an exhaustive review, but a focused and clinically meaningful synthesis of the evidence most relevant to prevention strategies in high‐risk patients.

Overview of Evidence and Practical Recommendations

Pathogenic Properties of Mould Spores

Aspergillus species and other filamentous fungi produce large quantities of asexual spores in specialised reproductive structures (conidiophores). The most prevalent Aspergillus spp. are A. fumigatus, A. flavus and A. terreus , although prevalence depends on local epidemiology [8]. Conidia are typically a few micrometres in diameter (2–5 μm) and are easily aerosolised when fungal colonies are mechanically disturbed or when airflow is elevated. These conidia are then transported as bioaerosol particles through indoor and outdoor air. Due to their small size and low settling velocity, conidia can remain airborne for extended periods, disperse over long distances and can reach the lower respiratory tract upon inhalation. Additional features, such as hydrophobicity and surface charge, allow these spores good airborne transmission as well as tissue adherence [9]. These spores are present almost everywhere, including inside well‐maintained buildings, and they can survive under variable and extreme temperatures, pH conditions and nutrient conditions, including variable carbon and nitrogen concentrations [9, 10]. A healthy immune system usually clears inhaled spores without causing any clinical symptoms. However, in the context of impaired neutrophil function, T‐cell dysfunction, or disrupted airway, lack of immune defences can allow spores to germinate and invade organ tissue, particularly in the lungs [1, 2, 4, 5].

Aspergillus spp. are the most frequently reported filamentous fungi causing invasive disease. Other clinically important moulds include Mucorales, Fusarium spp. and Scedosporium/Lomentospora spp., which are ubiquitous in soil, water, and plant material and produce airborne spores that can lead to invasive infection, particularly in severely immunocompromised patients. Invasive infections caused by these moulds are often associated with poorer outcomes than those caused by Aspergillus spp., as several species have intrinsic resistance to antifungal agents commonly used for first‐line treatment or prophylaxis [11]. Additionally, non‐airborne routes of spore exposure may be particularly relevant for these non‐Aspergillus spp., with direct wound contamination and contact with contaminated textiles representing potential routes of exposure that can lead to invasive disease [11, 12].

Who Is at Increased Risk for Infection?

Individuals at greatest risk for invasive mould infection, particularly invasive aspergillosis, are those with impaired innate antifungal host defences. In healthy lungs, alveolar macrophages, dendritic cells and epithelial cells work together with recruited neutrophils to recognise and kill inhaled Aspergillus spores before they can germinate into tissue‐invasive hyphae [1, 2, 4, 5]. Risk increases when these cells are reduced, fail to reach the lung efficiently or have reduced killing capacity [2, 3, 4, 13], especially with defects in CARD9‐dependent signalling or reactive oxygen species production [14]. This may occur during intensive chemotherapy for acute leukaemia [2, 3, 4, 13]. Risk is also increased after receiving blood‐cell transplantation from donors, especially in the early post‐transplant period and when graft‐versus‐host disease (GVHD), a condition where the donor's immune cells attack the body's own tissues, necessitates intensified immunosuppression [2, 3, 4, 5].

Classification of early and late occurring infections differ between transplantation types and are not applied uniformly in the literature. After solid organ transplantation, invasive aspergillosis is conventionally dichotomised into early‐onset (≤ 3 months post‐solid organ transplant) or late‐onset (> 3 months post‐solid organ transplant) disease [4, 15]. After blood‐cell transplantation, early‐onset disease is defined as occurring before day 40 post‐transplantation and is closely associated with graft failure. Late‐onset disease occurs between day 40 and day 100, whereas very late‐onset disease occurs after day 100 [16]. A matched case–control study of 185 proven or probable cases of invasive aspergillosis in allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation patients in France revealed, that the median onset of disease was day 133 post‐transplantation, with 63% of cases being classified as very late‐onset disease [17]. Lung transplant recipients are especially vulnerable due to chronic T‐cell‐directed immunosuppression and potential post‐transplant airway abnormalities, such as impaired innervation, lymphatic drainage and mucociliary clearance [2, 4, 5, 18]. High dose or prolonged corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents (calcineurin inhibitors, antimetabolites), further impair macrophage and neutrophil function and blunt antifungal inflammatory responses [2, 3, 4, 5]. Unfortunately, there is no precise and universally accepted definition for high‐dose and prolonged corticosteroid therapy [4, 5]. Doses and duration depend on patient populations, underlying other risk factors and vary across studies [19]. In addition, primary immunodeficiencies that affect phagocyte function, such as chronic granulomatous disease, impair effective mould killing [1, 4, 5]. Finally, advanced structural lung diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis or pre‐existing cavities, predispose to airway colonisation and chronic forms of aspergillosis (e.g., chronic pulmonary aspergillosis or aspergilloma) [1, 4, 5].

In high‐risk settings, profoundly immunosuppressed patients are highly susceptible to invasive mould disease. Prevention focuses on controlling environmental exposures around these patients rather than achieving absolute reductions in airborne fungal counts. Because routine air and surface sampling are poorly reproducible and correlate weakly with infection risk, best practice prioritises engineering and procedural controls to minimise exposure during disturbances (e.g., construction/renovation, or handling contaminated materials) and prevent progression from transient airway deposition to germination and tissue invasion [2, 3, 6, 20, 21, 22].

Exposure and Dose: Why There Is no Simple Safe Number of Indoor Spore Thresholds

Airborne fungal concentrations show substantial temporal and spatial variability, influenced by seasonality, meteorological conditions, such as humidity and wind, building characteristics (e.g., ventilation/filtration performance, pressure differentials), and indoor activities that aerosolise dust, such as cleaning with a vacuum cleaner [7, 23, 24]. Although environmental sampling can quantify culturable fungi as colony‐forming units per cubic metre, these measurements are method‐dependent and capture only a part of the bioaerosol burden. Importantly, there is no reproducible, individual‐level infection threshold available [6, 7]. Current human data are not able to establish a minimum infectious dose, and the literature on the use of air filters in hospitals similarly emphasises the absence of consensus on any maximum allowable fungal air count that can be used clinically across settings [1, 2, 6, 7]. Consequently, regulatory or engineering target values, where used, are best interpreted as process and quality control indicators for environmental management, and not as a clinical assurance of safety for specific high‐risk patients [6, 7]. Airborne fungal spores at any concentration might represent a risk in severely immunocompromised individuals [20].

Where Do Mould Spores Come From?

Outdoors

Soil and decomposing organic substrates serve as major ecological reservoirs for Aspergillus spp. and other filamentous fungi and can contain dense fungal biomass with abundant conidia. Common examples for these decomposing organic substrates are compost heaps, household food‐waste containers, leaf litter, hay straw and mulch. When these materials are handled, turned, shaken or poured, mechanical disturbance releases spores and fragments into the air, creating high inoculum bioaerosols. Warm, moist conditions during decomposition further promote fungal growth and sporulation, increasing the burden available for aerosolisation. In practical terms, activities such as composting, potting, repotting plants, raking leaves or moving stored hay/straw can produce short but intense exposure peaks [23, 24, 25].

Indoors, at Home

Key indoor determinants of fungal bioaerosol burden are indoor humidity and the presence of organic reservoirs (e.g., indoor household food‐waste bins) that allow mould growth and sporulation. Persistent dampness due to leaks, condensation, or flooding, visible mould, and water‐damaged materials create microenvironments that support fungal proliferation and can increase the release of airborne conidia during routine airflow or cleaning. Although no study has demonstrated directly that reducing indoor humidity decreases the incidence of invasive aspergillosis, a small pilot study suggests a possible association between domestic mould exposure and invasive aspergillosis. Among 40 neutropenic patients, those who reported visible mould growth in their homes had a higher risk of developing invasive aspergillosis, with a risk ratio of 1.65 (95% CI 1.25–2.17). Measured indoor spore concentrations were not associated with invasive aspergillosis, suggesting that self‐reported mould exposure may be a more relevant indicator of domestic risk than ambient‐air spore counts [26]. A case report by Lavergne and colleagues describes a 66‐year‐old immunocompromised farmer who developed fatal invasive aspergillosis caused by an azole‐resistant A. fumigatus strain. Environmental sampling of the farmer's home and garden recovered azole‐resistant isolates that were genotypically indistinguishable from the clinical isolate, proving strong evidence that the household environment was a potential source of infection [27].

Dust acts as another reservoir by accumulating spores and fungal fragments, which can be re‐aerosolised by human activity, including dust‐disturbing cleaning activities, or by air currents, particularly in areas with poor ventilation or high humidity. In addition, indoor potted plants may contribute, as potting soil and compost‐based substrates can eventually support fungal growth. Also disturbing dry soil surfaces during watering, repotting, or removing dead plant material can generate short‐term spikes in airborne spore concentrations. Additionally, construction, renovation or demolition activities, independently of whether they take place within the building or in surrounding areas, can substantially increase indoor fungal aerosol burdens by disturbing contaminated materials and releasing accumulated spores into the air [23, 24, 25, 28]. Less common sources of mould spores are described in Section 3.6.2.

Hospitals and Clinics

Hospitals care for large numbers of immunocompromised patients and therefore rely on engineered environmental controls to reduce exposure to airborne fungal components. Highest risk exposures, including outbreaks, typically occurring during construction, renovation or demolition, when disturbed dust and building materials increase airborne fungal burden, especially when barriers or ventilation systems are inadequate [6, 7, 10]. Multiple investigations have shown that construction‐ or demolition‐related outbreaks of invasive aspergillosis are often hospital‐acquired and involve Aspergillus strains with the same genetic fingerprint. High‐resolution genotyping methods, such as microsatellite typing and short tandem repeat analysis, have identified genetically indistinguishable Aspergillus spp. isolates in patients and in corresponding ward air samples, indicating a single‐source, or a very small number of sources, in the hospital environment. In some outbreaks, hidden infrastructural reservoirs, such as service shafts, were implicated, and remediation measures including structural sealing were required to eliminate environmental contamination [29, 30, 31, 32]. Prospective air sampling studies further show that demolition work significantly elevates airborne Aspergillus spp. concentrations, particularly during warmer periods [33]. Many hospitals now limit exposure to fungal aerosols in high‐risk areas, such as oncology and transplant units, as discussed in the next chapter. In contrast, older facilities may lack structural and engineering controls and have patient‐operable windows. This allows higher indoor spore loads, especially near construction or demolition sites, and increases the risk of invasive mould disease in immunocompromised patients. In such settings, windows in at‐risk patient rooms should be closed or sealed [34].

Beyond construction‐ or demolition‐associated contamination, outdoor air represents an important potential source of indoor fungal exposure. This was shown by a 17‐month environmental survey of a major hospital in Kuwait. By sampling outdoor air, indoor air of medical and surgical wards as well as intensive care units, and hospital water, in total 6179 isolates of filamentous fungi representing 19 genera were recovered. Interestingly, itraconazole‐resistant A. fumigatus isolates carrying the TR34/L98H Cyp51A resistance mechanism were recovered from outdoor air, the hospital environment, and clinical isolates, supporting a possible epidemiological connection and suggesting that the environment may serve as a reservoir for resistant A. fumigatus . Mould recovery showed a seasonal pattern, with the highest isolation frequency found in October/November [35].

Prevention of Healthcare‐Associated Mould Infections in High‐Risk Patients

Protective Environment and Air Quality Standards

Guidelines for high‐risk haematology and transplant patients recommend single rooms with high‐efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filtration and positive pressure (protective environment), particularly during periods of profound immunosuppression [3, 6]. Observational data, outbreak reports and implementation studies consistently associate these measures with lower airborne mould counts and reduced healthcare‐associated invasive aspergillosis cases, especially during construction work [7, 20, 21].

Construction and Renovation Risk Management

Construction‐ and renovation‐related dust control is a cornerstone of mould infection prevention because these activities can aerosolise large amounts of particulate matter carrying fungal spores and disseminate it along airflow pathways. Core components of an effective programme include a formal risk assessment to match necessary risk mitigations, precautions and patient vulnerability. Other core components include effective dust control procedures such as cleaning and sealing of rooms, negative‐pressure differentials within work zones to contain contaminated air, controlled transport routes and traffic separation (including dedicated elevators/paths and covered waste removal) to limit spread beyond the site, and tight interdisciplinary coordination between clinical services, infection prevention, facilities/engineering, and contractors to ensure implementation, monitoring, and rapid escalation when breaches occur [6, 20]. Each of the supplementary measures is explicitly recommended in the guideline from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the Healthcare Infection Control Practices Advisory Committee, with most classified as category IB. This category indicates that a measure is strongly recommended for implementation and supported by certain experimental, clinical or epidemiologic studies, together with a strong theoretical rationale. However, none of these measures has been evaluated as an isolated intervention in a controlled trial. These supplementary measures include the use of sticky adhesive carpets at the exit of the work zone to collect dust and reduce carry‐over on shoes [6]. Also, removal of dusty cloths and change of shoe covers is recommended before leaving the work area [6, 34]. Finally, already at the beginning of any construction or renovation work, a multidisciplinary team is recommended to be formed, consisting of medical and construction professionals. This team must evaluate the level of risk mitigations, barrier precautions and must coordinate the project from its inception, maintain a summary of its activities, and regularly monitor the integrity of the barriers. Also, walk‐through inspections should be performed [6]. Dependent on the type of construction and amount of generated dust, demolition and construction activities are classified within different categories, also taking the patient risk group and level of required infection control activities into account [34].

A particularly concerning event that often triggers construction or renovation work is water leakage with in wall cavities, as the resulting damage may remain hidden and therefore go unnoticed until substantial damage has occurred. In addition to the general measures described above, specific steps apply once water intrusion is recognised. Affected areas should be identified promptly and wet materials dried rapidly. Regardless of whether the source is flooding or a leak from a point‐of‐use fixtures or a roof, wet absorbent structural material such as wallboard and carpeting should be removed if they cannot be easily and thoroughly cleaned and dried within 72 h. A moisture content of 20% or less on moisture metre readings can be used as guidance. Where repair requires opening the building fabric, the work should be handled as construction rather than as routine maintenance. When the space above a false ceiling is disrupted or an opening of a wall needs to be enlarged, the affected area should be contained using plastic sheeting or a prefabricated enclosure maintained under negative pressure, with air either filtered through a HEPA filter or exhausted directly to the outside [6]. Nonetheless, international guidance remains heterogeneous, as shown by a survey of 75 documents from 24 countries, which found that no single guideline covers all aspects of risk management and that many countries provide none at all [36].

Handling of Cardboard and Outer Packaging in High‐Risk Hospital Areas

A related and frequently asked question is the basis for the widespread local rule that cardboard boxes and outer packaging must not be opened in high‐risk areas such as intensive care units, operating rooms and transplant wards. To our knowledge, no study has directly linked the unpacking of cardboard boxes to invasive mould infections. Rather, this practice is based on the principle of reducing dust and particulate matter in areas where patients are particularly vulnerable to airborne fungal exposure. Cardboard is a cellulose‐based material that may accumulate dust and other particulate matter during storage and during transport under variable environmental conditions. Opening, tearing or crushing cardboard boxes can release these particles into the surrounding air. Avoiding this is therefore a precautionary measure consistent with general dust‐control practices at high‐risk wards [6].

Laminar Airflow: What It Can and Cannot Do

Earlier observational studies reported lower mould infection rates when laminar airflow (LAF) systems, which provide unidirectional, HEPA‐filtered airflow that reduces turbulence and particle recirculation, were used during periods of increased environmental risk such as hospital renovation or construction [21]. More recent cohort data suggest that targeted, short‐term LAF deployment may still lower risk in selected contexts, but the incremental benefit appears more variable and may be attenuated in settings with contemporary antifungal prophylaxis, improved baseline protective‐environment standards, and more robust construction containment practices [22, 37]. When LAF is used, its effectiveness depends on verified performance and strict preventive maintenance through airflow/pressure validation and regular checks of filter integrity. It should be embedded within a broader, multimodal prevention bundle rather than applied as a stand‐alone measure [6, 20].

Practical Guidance for High‐Risk Patients at Home and in Daily Life

Evidence‐based guidance for patients at high risk of invasive fungal infections on reducing environmental exposure in their homes and daily lives remains limited. Available recommendations are largely limited to observational studies and expert consensus, with substantial gaps in the evidence base. Nevertheless, practical considerations may help patients minimise potentially avoidable exposure to fungal spores in everyday environments. The following sections therefore summarise the available evidence and provide practical guidance, while highlighting areas where evidence remains insufficient. This summary was developed as part of the International Society for Human and Animal Mycology (ISHAM) working group for patient representation and advocacy (https://www.isham.org/working‐groups/patient‐advocacy/).

The Priorities

Clean‐up of visible mould should not be performed by patients themselves during high‐risk periods [23, 24, 25]. Bathroom black mould, such as Cladosporium species, on tile or grout rarely causes invasive disease [38], but any visible mould should be addressed to reduce overall spore load. For small, localised spots on non‐porous surfaces, a healthy household member should perform the cleaning by using fungicides, such as chlorine bleach with detergents [39]. Gloves, well‐fitting respirators and good ventilation are always recommended, and if possible porous materials having visible mould should be removed. Dampness should be prevented by fixing leaks quickly, maintaining adequate heating during colder seasons, and keeping indoor relative humidity below 60%, or lower in cold weather to limit condensation on cold surfaces [23, 24].

Evidence supports controlling indoor moisture with adequate ventilation and, where needed, dehumidification or air conditioning. Mechanical ventilation is suited to airtight or cold‐climate homes, while in hot‐humid climates, air conditioning or dehumidification should be preferred over introducing humid outdoor air [40, 41, 42]. Especially when relative humidity cannot be held below 60% by heating and ventilation alone [24], which is commonly the case in basement‐apartments, poorly insulated houses/flats or houses/flats with windows having one layer of glass, or homes with previous water damage, and hot‐humid climates, portable dehumidifiers are recommended for the duration of the high‐risk phase [24]. In a study of 290 Canadian homes, indoor relative humidity above 45% was positively associated with the presence of multiple indoor biocontaminants, including the fungal cell wall component 1,3‐beta‐D‐glucan, whereas the use of dehumidifier and frequent floor cleaning were inversely associated [43].

In tropical and subtropical settings, dehumidifiers and air conditioning are believed to be the most effective measurements to control indoor humidity and prevent indoor mould growth [44]. Where indoor humidity is already within the recommended range, the use of a dehumidifier is unlikely to provide additional benefit and is therefore not routinely recommended. Importantly, dehumidifiers do not address the underlying source of excess moisture, such as a water leak. Because they collect standing water, regular emptying and cleaning are essential, as described in Section 3.6.2. Notably, as no studies have evaluated whether the use of dehumidifiers reduces the incidence of invasive mould infections in high‐risk patients, these recommendations are based on indirect evidence from studies assessing fungal growth and indoor biocontaminant levels [43]. In practice, it is recommended to first purchase an inexpensive hygrometer to measure indoor relative humidity and determine whether a dehumidifier is necessary and likely to be beneficial. Operational tips (e.g., running exhaust fans for 20–30 min after showers or cooking, or opening windows for 5–10 min) are practice‐based rather than study‐proven. It has to be noted that moisture‐control recommendations are mostly based on general‐population and allergy‐oriented studies [40, 41, 42], assessing asthma, wheeze, rhinitis and other respiratory symptoms as outcomes, rather than invasive infection rates in severely immunocompromised populations. Nevertheless, as dampness permits fungal growth on building materials, leading to the release of conidia that can be subsequently inhaled, these recommendations are indirect also important for people being at high risk of invasive fungal disease [26, 27].

In general, window opening should be guided by outdoor spore levels. When outdoor fungal loads are elevated by construction or demolition, windows should be kept closed, even if this limits short‐term reductions in indoor relative humidity, as the net effect is increased indoor spore concentrations [4]. If mould growth is extensive or recurrent, the area should be avoided and professional remediation arranged when feasible [23, 24, 25].

High‐spore tasks such as composting, repotting plants, opening bags of potting soil, turning over leaf piles, handling rotting plant material, or spending time near demolition or construction dust should be avoided [23, 24, 25]. Kitchen food‐waste should not be kept inside the home longer than necessary and should be emptied daily. Mouldy food should be discarded in their entirety and visible mould should not be cut away to consume the rest [24]. Dust reservoirs in the home should be minimised preferably by regular damp wiping rather than methods that may aerosolise settled dust, such as vacuuming [18, 45].

In an experimental study testing 21 domestic and commercial vacuum cleaners, mould aerosol emission rates reached up to 6.5 × 106 mould cell equivalents per minute, while vacuum‐bag dust contained a mean of 2.1 × 107 mould cell equivalents per gram. The authors concluded that vacuum cleaning may generate a short‐term, high‐intensity bioaerosol exposure [46]. Whenever possible, vacuum cleaners with HEPA filters should be preferred [6].

Nevertheless, whenever possible, these tasks should be performed by another household member or caregiver. During periods of high risk, the patient should remain in a different part of the home while cleaning is undertaken [18, 45].

Current guidelines do not address agricultural dust but advise high‐risk patients to avoid environments with high airborne fungal spore levels [4]. During harvest, wind‐borne, spore‐laden dust may increase exposure and high‐risk patients should take reasonable precautions [47]. Based on our expert opinion, we recommend avoiding unnecessary outdoor activity during windy conditions near agricultural sites, and staying indoors with windows and doors closed. Where available, HEPA air filtration in primary living areas may reduce spore exposure. If outdoor exposure is unavoidable, well‐fitting particle‐filtering respirators (see next chapter) should be used. Where mould‐active antifungal prophylaxis is prescribed, compliance should be ensured [9].

Devices and Hidden Sources

Certain household devices and organic materials can trap moisture and create environments where mould can grow and release spores into the air, especially if they are not cleaned regularly and maintained properly. Examples of such devices include humidifiers, water chambers in devices such as evaporative units, and air‐conditioning systems, which are discussed in more detail below. If avoidance of these items is not feasible, the focus should be on careful cleaning, proper hygiene measures, and humidity control, and manufacturer‐recommended maintenance should be followed in accordance with the current risk phase [24].

Humidifiers, ‘Air Washers’ and Evaporative Units

These devices can become high‐burden microbial reservoirs because they contain standing water, where biofilms can form. They should be emptied daily, cleaned mechanically, and allowed to dry completely before reuse. Fresh water should be used as recommended by the manufacturer, and water stagnation should be prevented. During periods of maximal immunosuppression, non‐essential humidifiers should be avoided unless there is a specific medical indication and proper maintenance can be assured [24].

Air‐Conditioning Systems (Including Vehicle Air‐Conditioning Systems)

If regular service is not performed, fungal growth may occur on filters, cooling coils, or within condensate pans and drains. Filters should be replaced or cleaned at the intervals recommended by the manufacturer, and proper condensate drainage should be ensured to prevent chronic dampness [24].

Heat waves are becoming increasingly frequent, and electric fans are widely used in domestic settings to provide relief from heat. However, conventional fans neither filter air nor remove moisture from indoor air and therefore do not actively reduce airborne fungal spore concentrations. Instead, by increasing air movement, fans can promote the resuspension of settled dust and associated fungal particles from indoor surfaces, particularly at higher air velocities and turbulence intensities [48, 49]. To our knowledge, no controlled studies have specifically evaluated fan use as a risk factor for invasive mould infection. Nevertheless, given the potential for fan‐generated airflow to raise dust, reasonable precautions may be warranted for individuals at high risk of invasive mould infection. These include thorough and regular cleaning of fans to remove accumulated dust [24], positioning fans away from floors and visibly dusty surfaces, and avoiding direct airflow toward the face of high‐risk individuals at close range.

Organic Household Waste

Food waste and other organic material can support mould growth in household waste containers, particularly under warm and humid conditions. Waste should be disposed of promptly and stored in containers with tightly fitting lids. Household waste containers should be cleaned regularly, and indoor composting should be avoided during high‐risk periods. Ideally, another household member who is not immunocompromised should handle household food‐waste and clean these waste containers. Where visible mould is present or disposal has been delayed, handling of organic waste and cleaning of containers should be restricted to non‐immunocompromised members [24].

Cut Flowers, Dried Floral Arrangements and Decorative Plant Matter

As plant material degrades, it can increase fungal exposure. These items should be avoided in the home during high‐risk periods, especially in bedrooms or near sleeping areas. Walking across freshly cultivated fields should also be avoided, as fungal bioburden may be increased in these areas [24].

Potted Plants, Potting Soil and Compost

Soil is a significant indoor fungal reservoir. Repotting, mixing or disturbing dry soil can release spores into the air. During high‐risk phases, potted plants should be removed from main living and sleeping areas, and handling of soil or compost by the patient should be avoided [24].

Masks and Portable Air Cleaners: When They Make Sense

A well‐fitting particle‐filtering respirator, such as an FFP2, N95, KN95, KF94 or other nationally certified equivalent respirators, is recommended for patients at high risk. These respirators help to reduce the inhalation of fungal spores and are recommended when exposure cannot be avoided, for example when passing through construction or renovation sites [24]. However, clinical trials to support routine community mask use to prevent invasive aspergillosis are lacking. A randomised study investigated the use of these respirators in hospitalised high‐risk patients and failed to show association with lower rates of invasive fungal infections [50]. Guidelines generally frame masks as a potential adjunct for high‐exposure scenarios rather than a universal requirement [2, 3, 24].

Portable HEPA air cleaners may reduce indoor particle and spore burden [7, 23]. A recent clinical study suggests they can add benefit when combined with antifungal prophylaxis in selected high‐risk hospital populations [37]. For home use, evidence is less definitive, but a portable HEPA device can be considered when mould‐free housing cannot be guaranteed and after discussing priorities with the clinical team [23, 24]. For home portable HEPA filters, filter efficiency, clean air delivery rate matched to room size, and air‐exchange rate should be considered [51]. Filters must be replaced according to manufacturer specifications. Portable HEPA air cleaners sometimes have been used during hospital construction, such as in a neonatal intensive care unit undergoing renovation. Air sampling showed that renovation work in adjacent areas significantly raised Aspergillus spp. air concentration, and the use of a mobile HEPA filtration unit could significantly reduce these concentrations. However, no relationship was found between air spore concentration and nasopharyngeal colonisation in the neonates, and no invasive aspergillosis occurred [52].

In summary, the evidence base remains limited and heterogeneous (differences in patient populations, endpoints and device performance), so no universal recommendation can be made. Larger, well‐designed studies are needed to identify which patients benefit, which settings are most effective, and what level of exposure reduction is clinically meaningful, particularly for routine use in private homes.

Environmental Sampling and Spore Limits

Routine air sampling is not recommended for individual patient decision‐making because results vary widely over time and space, depend strongly on sampling methods, and no validated safe threshold values have been established that reliably predict individual risk. If visible mould is present in the home such as visible mould growth on a wall, this is considered actionable evidence of a humidity problem and a potential source of airborne spores. Therefore, air sampling is generally regarded as unnecessary to justify intervention. Instead, priority should be given to identification and elimination of the humidity source, removal or remediation of mould‐affected material using appropriate precautions, and minimisation of exposure until remediation is completed. This could include avoidance of the affected room, increased ventilation where safe, or consideration of temporary alternative accommodation in severe cases. Air sampling may be used for outbreak investigations, verification of construction controls or engineering quality assurance. However, clinical interpretation of results is limited, and a personal safe exposure limit is not defined [6, 7, 20]. Where a formal framework for microbiological air control is required, particularly in healthcare and other controlled environments, the European standard EN 17141:2020 provides a useful framework. This standard specifies among other aspects, principles for microbiological environmental monitoring, including the selection of air and surface sampling methods, sampling locations and frequencies. However, it does not define a universal numerical limit for airborne fungal concentrations applicable to hospital operating rooms [53, 54].

Practical Checklist for Clinicians Caring for High‐Risk Patients

The patient's current risk phase should be determined through integration of immune status and exposure history [55], taking into account the anticipated duration and depth of neutropenia, the interval since transplantation, the type and intensity of immunosuppression (including corticosteroid dose and duration), the presence and severity of GVHD, and relevant comorbidities such as underlying structural lung disease [2, 3, 4, 5]. Antifungal prevention should be tailored to the individual risk profile, with appropriate selection of antifungal prophylaxis, careful attention to drug–drug interactions, and therapeutic drug monitoring performed where indicated (e.g., azole trough concentrations), so that drug exposure is optimised and toxicity or breakthrough infection is minimised [13, 56]. Within inpatient high‐risk care areas, protective‐environment performance should be verified, with adequate HEPA filtration, positive‐pressure differentials, effective room sealing, and a documented schedule of preventive maintenance and verification (including filter‐integrity and airflow checks) maintained [3, 6]. A construction or renovation risk‐mitigation programme should be implemented, including formal risk classification, written controls for dust containment (e.g., barriers and negative‐pressure anterooms, where applicable), segregation of traffic routes, protection of ventilation systems, and ongoing monitoring with appropriate documentation [6, 20]. Structured, individualised patient counselling on the reduction of home and lifestyle exposure should be offered to patients. Precautions should be proportional to defined high‐risk phases, while overly restrictive measures should be avoided during lower risk periods in order to support adherence and quality of life [2, 23, 24]. Practical Checklist for Patients and Caregivers

It should be clarified with the clinical team whether an increased risk is currently present and how long enhanced precautions should be maintained [2, 3, 24]. Living areas (including bedrooms, living rooms and bathrooms) should be inspected regularly for dampness, water damage, condensation, musty odours or visible mould. If any of these are identified, the affected room should be avoided where possible, and timely professional assessment and remediation should be arranged, particularly during high‐risk phases [23, 24, 25]. High‐spore environments, including compost heaps, potting soil, gardening or soil turning, leaf litter, and dusty construction or renovation sites, should be avoided [23, 24, 25]. Dust‐generating household tasks, such as vacuuming, sweeping, changing litter or pet bedding, cleaning cages, clearing basements or attics should preferably be delegated. If such tasks cannot be avoided, dust generation should be minimised in accordance with clinical advice. For example, damp wiping should be used instead of dry sweeping [23, 24]. For unavoidable high‐dust exposure, a well‐fitting particulate respirator may be considered if recommended by the clinical team and if correct use can be ensured [2, 3, 24]. Conclusion

Complete avoidance of mould exposure is not feasible, and current evidence does not support a single airborne spore‐count threshold that can be interpreted as safe for an individual patient, given substantial temporal/spatial variability in measurements and host‐dependent susceptibility [1, 2, 6, 7]. Nevertheless, a multicomponent (layered) prevention approach, integrating clinical risk stratification, antifungal prophylaxis when indicated, protective‐environment engineering controls in hospital settings, rigorous dust containment during construction/renovation, and pragmatic exposure‐reduction measures in the home, is biologically and clinically plausible. It targets both host vulnerability and environmental inoculum pressure across the highest‐risk periods. This approach is consistent with guideline recommendations and supported primarily by observational data and implementation experience, with selected interventional evidence in specific high‐risk settings [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 13, 20, 21, 23, 37, 56].
