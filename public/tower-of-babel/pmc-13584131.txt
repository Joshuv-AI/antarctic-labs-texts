Dysbiosis-Associated Lysophosphatidic Acid Primes Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor–Induced Intestinal Barrier Dysfunction via the Lysophosphatidic Acid Receptor 2–Myosin Light Chain Kinase Axis
Authors: Lei Xiong, Jianshang Huang, Qiuyuan Liu, Hanqi Xia, Nina Zhu, Weizhen Xiang, Wentao Xu, Yunsheng Dong, Rumeng Zhu, Yiran Han, Abduxukur Rahman, Huiqing Xu, Weiwei Xie, Zehua Luo, Wei He, Jerrold R Turner, Yubao Zhou, Ruolei Hu, Hua Wang, Li Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsImmune checkpoint inhibitor therapy is frequently limited by severe gastrointestinal toxicity. Although the immune mechanisms driving late-stage, overt mucosal inflammation are increasingly recognized, the early microbiota–metabolic events that prime epithelial barrier dysfunction remain poorly defined.MethodsWe employed an integrated multiomics approach in a murine model of early immune checkpoint inhibitor–induced injury, with validation in a clinical cohort of patients with immune checkpoint inhibitor colitis. Mechanisms were investigated using in vitro receptor screening, genetically engineered mice with altered myosin light chain kinase 1 activity, and pharmacologic intervention with repurposed low-dose tacrolimus. Subcutaneous melanoma models were utilized to evaluate the relationship between barrier modulation and antitumor outcomes.ResultsEarly immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment induced a distinct dysbiotic state characterized by Pseudomonadota expansion, which was associated with the accumulation of bioactive lysophosphatidic acid—a metabolic signature also observed in human patients. Mechanistically, we identified lysophosphatidic acid receptor 2 as the key epithelial sensor that transduces this metabolic signal into pathological myosin light chain kinase 1 activation, driving the priming phase of barrier dysfunction. Genetic ablation of myosin light chain kinase 1 prevented this early barrier loss and was associated with improved tumor control and favorable regional immune alterations. Conversely, constitutive activation of myosin light chain kinase 1 exacerbated barrier disruption, which correlated with accelerated tumor progression and a systemic immunosuppressive shift. Furthermore, repurposing low-dose tacrolimus as a selective modulator to disrupt FKBP8-dependent myosin light chain kinase 1 recruitment effectively mitigated barrier injury without dampening antitumor responses.ConclusionsThe dysbiosis-associated lysophosphatidic acid–lysophosphatidic acid receptor 2–myosin light chain kinase axis acts as an early metabolic trigger for the priming phase of immune checkpoint inhibitor–induced barrier dysfunction. Modulating this upstream initiation step provides a temporal and mechanism-based strategy to uncouple intestinal toxicity from anti-tumor efficacy.Keywords: Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors, Intestinal Barrier, Lysophosphatidic Acid, Myosin Light Chain Kinase

Summary

This study demonstrates that gut dysbiosis-associated accumulation of lysophosphatidic acid primes early immune checkpoint inhibitor-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction via the lysophosphatidic acid receptor 2–myosin light chain kinase 1 axis. Targeting this initial barrier damage, rather than subsequent severe inflammation, provides a novel precision framework to improve cancer immunotherapy safety.

What You Need to Know

Background

Immune checkpoint inhibitors frequently cause severe gastrointestinal toxicity, traditionally managed with broad immunosuppressants that may risk compromising anti-tumor efficacy. The early, initiating metabolic events that trigger epithelial barrier dysfunction prior to overt inflammation are not fully understood.

Impact

Our findings suggest that immune checkpoint inhibitor–induced gut dysbiosis contributes to the accumulation of lysophosphatidic acid, which can activate the epithelial lysophosphatidic acid–myosin light chain kinase axis to initiate barrier leakage. Targeting this specific pathway—such as with low-dose tacrolimus—helps protect the intestinal barrier and shows potential to uncouple gastrointestinal toxicity from the systemic antitumor efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Future Directions

Further investigation is needed to explore the precise microbial or host enzymatic origins of luminal lysophosphatidic acid accumulation. Additionally, clinical trials are required to rigorously evaluate the safety and efficacy of targeted barrier-stabilizing therapies for managing immune-related adverse events in patients with cancer.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are highly effective in cancer therapy, but their clinical utility is frequently limited by immune-related adverse events, most notably gastrointestinal toxicity.1, 2, 3 Although the clinical presentation of this toxicity resembles autoimmune bowel diseases, its underlying etiology is distinct and not fully understood. Meanwhile, emerging research suggests that the gut microbiome plays an important role in modulating host immunity and shaping responses to ICI therapy.4, 5, 6 However, the specific dysbiosis-associated metabolic shifts that initiate early mucosal damage remain poorly defined.

Current strategies, adapted from inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) treatment, depend on systemic immunosuppressants (eg, corticosteroids, anti–tumor necrosis factor [TNF]α agents).7, 8, 9 Although effective against inflammation, these broad-acting agents carry the critical risk of suppressing antitumor immunity, creating a direct conflict between managing toxicity and preserving therapeutic efficacy. To understand this toxicity, we recently characterized a TNF-driven mechanism that mediates the later stages of ICI colitis.10 However, barrier dysfunction occurs before severe symptoms appear. Because the intestinal epithelium constantly interacts with local metabolites, early microbial shifts and their associated metabolic changes likely act as an initial trigger for barrier loss. Elucidating these initiating events is essential to block the disease progression at an early stage.

The intestinal epithelial barrier is centrally regulated by the apical junctional complex. Although the intestinal-specific myosin light chain kinase (MCLK) isoform (MLCK1) is known to drive barrier dysfunction,11, 12, 13, 14 its temporal dynamics require clarification. Our previous work established MLCK1 as a downstream effector of inflammatory cytokines (eg, TNF) during severe, established colitis.10 Yet, whether upstream metabolic signals engage MLCK1 to drive barrier loss remains unknown. We hypothesize that early metabolic triggering of MLCK1 creates the initial breach required for antigen influx and subsequent immune activation.

Here, using multiomics profiling of an ICI-induced injury model, we identified a specific dysbiotic state associated with the accumulation of bioactive lysophosphatidic acid (LPA). We demonstrate that LPA signaling through the epithelial lysophosphatidic acid receptor 2 (LPAR2) activates MLCK1, initiating barrier dysfunction. Furthermore, by repurposing low-dose tacrolimus, we propose a pharmacologic strategy to specifically inhibit the FKBP8-dependent recruitment of MLCK1, thereby restoring barrier integrity. Targeting this upstream initiation step offers a temporal- and mechanism-based strategy to prevent disease progression and uncouple early intestinal toxicity from antitumor efficacy, moving beyond generalized immunosuppression.

Results

Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor Treatment Initiates an Early Priming Phase of Barrier Dysfunction

To characterize how ICI-associated intestinal injury is initiated, we sought to explore the early events that precede severe, immune-driven colitis. We established a wild-type (WT) mouse model under standard specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions, evaluating the mice at 1, 2, 4, and 6 weeks of treatment. This unique setting allowed us to capture the early priming phase of the disease (Figure 1A). As anticipated in this mild microbial environment, ICI treatment did not induce severe macroscopic colitis or significant weight loss (Figure 1B); instead, it triggered an early state of intestinal dysfunction. This was evidenced by elevated clinical scores (Figure 1C) and increased fecal water content at weeks 4 and 6 (Figure 1D). Despite the significant barrier loss confirmed by fluorescein assay at week 6 (Figure 1E), the colon appeared macroscopically normal (Figure 1F). Histologic analysis further revealed only mild immune infiltration with intact crypt architecture (Figure 1G), which was corroborated by a modest but significant increase in mucosal CD8+ T cells and F4/80+ macrophages (Figure 1H and I). Notably, this early state of barrier loss and low-grade inflammation was pan-intestinal, as evidenced by the infiltration patterns of T cells and macrophages in the ileum, which mirrored the pathology observed in the colon (Figure 2A and B).

To investigate the molecular basis of barrier loss, we assessed the expression of tight junction components. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis revealed selective transcriptional dysregulation; although most barrier-associated transcripts (eg, Ocln) remained stable, Tjp1 messenger RNA (mRNA) was significantly downregulated in the colon at week 6 (Figure 3A), whereas ileal expression remained unaffected (Figure 3B). Despite these limited transcriptional shifts, the protein-level disruption was profound and widespread. In the colon, the protein abundance of both zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and occludin was significantly reduced (Figure 1J and K), a structural disassembly also observed in the ileum (Figure 3C–F). Concurrent with the breakdown of the leak pathway, the pore pathway was activated, evidenced by the upregulation of pore-forming claudins. Specifically, claudin-15 was increased in the ileum, whereas claudin-2 was elevated in the colon (Figure 3G–J).

Given that the structural disassembly involved the loss of ZO-1 and occludin—key components of the leak pathway—we investigated the MLCK signaling axis as a potential driver. Quantitative analysis revealed a concomitant upregulation of both MLCK1 and phosphorylated myosin light chain (pMLC) in the colonic epithelium (Figure 1L and M). Interestingly, this activation appeared to be localized to the colon, as changes in pMLC levels within the ileal epithelium did not reach statistical significance (Figure 3K and L). Collectively, these data indicate that early ICI treatment induces colonic MLCK activation and barrier dysfunction in our SPF model.

Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor–Mediated Tumor Suppression Coincides With Myosin Light Chain Kinase–Associated Barrier Loss and Mucosal Immune Activation

To evaluate the correlation between antitumor efficacy and gastrointestinal toxicity, we established a syngeneic B16 melanoma model treated with combined anti–cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) and anti–programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) antibodies (Figure 4A). ICI treatment led to significant reductions in both tumor weights (Figure 4B) and tumor volumes, as monitored by ultrasound (Figure 4C). However, this therapeutic benefit was accompanied by compromised intestinal integrity. Toxicity was evident through elevated fecal water content (Figure 4D) and the loss of mucosal barrier integrity, as confirmed by in vivo fluorescein tracer assays (Figure 4E). This functional decline correlated with substantial macroscopic tissue damage, notably colon shortening (Figure 4F). In association with this barrier defect, analysis of the colonic immune microenvironment revealed a polarized inflammatory profile, characterized by a significant upregulation of Ifng mRNA, whereas levels of Il1b and Il6 remained stable (Figure 4G). Corresponding to this transcriptomic shift, spectral flow cytometry demonstrated a significant increase in the abundance of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, natural killer T cells, and dendritic cells within the colonic lamina propria of the tumor + ICI group compared with controls (Figure 4H and I). We subsequently evaluated the structural integrity of the epithelial barrier; both immunofluorescence staining and Western blot analysis confirmed a significant reduction in ZO-1 and occludin protein levels in the colon following ICI treatment. Consistent with this barrier defect, we observed activation of the MLCK signaling axis, as reflected by the elevated levels of both MLCK1 and its phosphorylated substrate pMLC (Figure 4J and K). Collectively, these findings indicate that ICI therapy exerts a dual impact, suppressing tumor growth while inducing intestinal toxicity characterized by interferon gamma–dominant inflammation and MLCK-associated barrier loss.

Gut Dysbiosis-Associated Lysophosphatidic Acid Is Identified as a Conserved Pathogenic Mediator in Murine and Human Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor–Induced Injury

Although ICI therapy primarily drives systemic immune activation, the heterogeneity of intestinal toxicity suggests a pivotal role for the local microenvironment. We therefore integrated metagenomic and metabolomic profiling to determine whether ecosystem-level alterations contribute to the observed pathology. ICI treatment triggered profound gut dysbiosis, characterized by a significant reduction in microbial richness and alpha diversity (Figure 5A and B). This dysbiosis was further accompanied by a distinct change in bacterial composition, characterized by a relative decrease in Bacteroidota and an increase in pathobiont-associated Pseudomonadota (Figure 5C and D).

To identify the metabolic drivers of this intestinal barrier dysfunction, we performed untargeted fecal metabolomics. Principal component analysis revealed a clear separation between the ICI-treated and control groups (Figure 5E), with metabolic set enrichment analysis identifying glycerophospholipid metabolism as 1 of the top enriched pathways (Figure 5F). Given their established roles in barrier homeostasis, we focused on glycerophospholipid-derived species. Heatmap and quantitative abundance analyses identified an accumulation of specific bioactive lipids, notably LPA and lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), in ICI-treated mice (Figure 5G and H). Given that LPA acts as the direct ligand for epithelial receptors to trigger downstream signaling, we prioritized this specific metabolite for our subsequent mechanistic investigations. Spearman correlation analysis revealed a strong association between the altered microbiome and this metabolomic shift, revealing that fecal LPA abundance was negatively correlated with Bacteroidota and positively correlated with Pseudomonadota (Figure 5I).

Building upon this microbe-metabolite correlation, we sought to determine whether the dysbiotic microbiome possessed the genetic capacity to contribute to this luminal LPA pool. In our metagenomic data, we observed an enrichment of the plsB gene (EC 2.3.1.15), which encodes a key bacterial enzyme for de novo LPA biosynthesis, in the ICI-treated group (Figure 5J and K). Further analysis revealed that Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae—prevalent taxa within the expanded Pseudomonadota phylum—were enriched after ICI treatment (Figure 6A). Genomic databases confirmed that these pathobionts encode the plsB gene (Figure 6B). The abundance of both E coli and K pneumoniae showed a strong positive correlation with fecal LPA concentrations, reinforcing their potential functional role (Figure 6C). Overall, these findings indicate that the expansion of these particular pathobionts provides the dysbiotic microbiome with the genetic capacity to synthesize LPA de novo.

This lipid dysregulation was not restricted to the local intestinal microenvironment. Although our genomic data suggest the dysbiotic microbiome possesses the capacity for de novo luminal LPA synthesis, the accumulated LPC can also be converted into LPA within the host system (Figure 7A). Supporting a broader systemic impact of this metabolic shift, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay confirmed elevated serum LPA levels in the tumor + ICI group (Figure 7B).

To establish the clinical relevance of our murine findings, we extended our metabolic profiling to fecal samples obtained from our previously described clinical cohort of patients with ICI-associated colitis undergoing treatment with corticosteroids or vedolizumab. Consistent with our preclinical observations, fecal metabolomic profiling in this clinical cohort revealed a conserved dysregulation of glycerophospholipid metabolism. The concentration of fecal LPA and LPC declined following therapeutic intervention (Supplementary Dataset 1). These clinical data, reinforcing our murine models, identify these bioactive lipids as key pathogenic mediators and potential indicators of treatment response in ICI-associated barrier dysfunction.

Together, these cross-species findings identify the dysbiosis-associated accumulation of bioactive LPA as a hallmark metabolic signature of ICI-induced intestinal injury, providing a bridge between microbial shifts and epithelial pathology.

Lysophosphatidic Acid Triggers Barrier Dysfunction via the Lysophosphatidic Acid Receptor 2–Myosin Light Chain Kinase1 Signaling Axis in Intestinal Epithelial Cells

Following the identification of LPA as a candidate mediator, we sought to validate its direct pathogenicity using Caco-2 monolayers. Functional analysis revealed that LPA compromised barrier integrity, with the most pronounced reduction in transepithelial electrical resistance observed at 10 μM (Figure 8A). This impairment was further confirmed by a significant increase in paracellular permeability to 4-kDa fluorescein isothiocyanate–dextran (Figure 8B).

(C) LPA downregulates OCLN and TJP1 while upregulating MYLK mRNA expression in Caco-2 cells. Mechanistically, LPA treatment recapitulated the key features of the in vivo pathology; at the transcriptional level, LPA upregulated MYLK while suppressing OCLN and TJP1 mRNA (Figure 8C). Consistent with these shifts, immunofluorescence confirmed the structural loss of ZO-1 and occludin, accompanied by elevated levels of MLCK1 and pMLC (Figure 8D). Given that LPA exerts its biological functions through specialized G protein–coupled receptors, we performed a comprehensive expression screening to identify the specific epithelial sensor for this metabolic insult. Receptor expression profiling identified LPAR2 as the primary responding receptor, which was upregulated in a time-dependent manner, whereas other subtypes (LPAR1, LPAR3–6) remained stable (Figure 8E). Collectively, these data demonstrate that LPA initiates a pathogenic cascade by activating the LPAR2–MLCK1 axis, thereby establishing the molecular basis for the early barrier breach observed in vivo.

Myosin Light Chain Kinase Deficiency Alleviates Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor–Induced Intestinal Injury and Is Associated With Suppressed Tumor Progression

To determine whether MLCK is required for ICI-induced barrier loss, we utilized long MLCK-knockout (KO) mice. Functional assessment demonstrated that MLCK deficiency preserved barrier integrity, as evidenced by reduced fluorescein permeability compared with ICI-treated WT controls (Figure 9A). Consistent with maintained barrier function, KO mice retained a longer colon and preserved macroscopic tissue architecture (Figure 9B). Moreover, systemic inflammation was attenuated, evidenced by reduced spleen weights (Figure 9C). We next asked whether gut protection came at the cost of antitumor efficacy. MLCK deficiency did not compromise but rather correlated with improved tumor control. Tumor volume growth was suppressed (Figure 9D), and the final tumor burden was reduced, as validated by weight and imaging (Figure 9E).

Building on these protective phenotypes, we next asked how this modulation reshaped regional immune dynamics. Analysis of mesenteric lymph nodes (MLNs) showed a dampened regional inflammatory response in MLCK-KO mice. This was characterized by a reduction in infiltrating CD8+ T cells, neutrophils, and dendritic cells compared with controls (Figure 9F), suggesting an association between barrier protection and reduced immune hyperactivation in secondary lymphoid organs. In contrast to controls, MLCK-KO mice exhibited histologic protection, with reduced tissue damage and inflammatory infiltration (Figure 9G). Mechanistically, we confirmed that MLCK deficiency prevented actomyosin-dependent barrier loss. MLCK-KO mice exhibited reduced pMLC levels while preserving the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and occludin (Figure 9H). Furthermore, the expression of the pore-forming protein claudin-2 was reduced, aligning with the attenuated inflammatory state (Figure 10A). Collectively, these data demonstrate that targeting MLCK during the early phase mitigates immune-related intestinal toxicity alongside maintained tumor control in our experimental models.

Constitutive Activation of Myosin Light Chain Kinase Exacerbates Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor–Induced Intestinal Injury and Is Associated With Accelerated Tumor Progression

Having established that MLCK deficiency is protective, we next asked whether constitutive activation of MLCK (CA-MLCK) is sufficient to exacerbate this pathology. Following ICI treatment, CA-MLCK mice demonstrated higher intestinal permeability than WT controls in the fluorescein assay (Figure 11A). Despite comparable colon lengths between the groups (Figure 11B), CA-MLCK mice developed more severe histopathologic damage, characterized by increased inflammation and tissue injury upon ICI challenge (Figure 11C).

We next assessed the relationship between aggravated gut injury and antitumor efficacy. Constitutive MLCK activation correlated with accelerated tumor growth and a higher overall tumor burden (Figure 11D). Flow cytometry analysis of the spleen revealed a dysregulated systemic immune landscape in ICI-treated CA-MLCK mice, characterized by an expansion of F4/80+ macrophages and a reduction in CD4+Foxp3+ regulatory T cells (Figure 11E). Together, these data from the spleen suggest that severe gut barrier damage correlates with a systemic immune shift toward myeloid expansion. We propose that this myeloid-driven immunosuppressive state could override the potential antitumor advantages of regulatory T-cell reduction, fostering an environment conducive to tumor growth.

Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a reduction of ZO-1 and occludin in CA-MLCK mice, alongside elevated MLCK1 and pMLC levels (Figure 11F). Consistent with the exacerbated inflammatory environment driven by barrier loss, CA-MLCK mice exhibited elevated expression of the pore-forming protein claudin-2 (Figure 10B). Thus, these data indicate that MLCK-driven barrier dysfunction is sufficient to aggravate intestinal toxicity and correlates with compromised efficacy of immunotherapy.

Low-Dose Tacrolimus Restores Intestinal Barrier Function Without Compromising the Anti-Tumor Efficacy of Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors

Although our previous work established the structural basis of the FKBP8–MLCK1 interaction using the tool compound epicatechin,10 accelerating the clinical translation requires agents with established pharmacokinetic and safety profiles. Given its clinical familiarity and high affinity for FKBP proteins, we hypothesized that repurposing tacrolimus at subimmunosuppressive, low doses could effectively modulate MLCK1 recruitment. To explore this therapeutic implication, tumor-bearing mice receiving ICIs were cotreated with low-dose tacrolimus. At the effective dose of 0.03 mg/kg, tacrolimus alleviated ICI-induced weight loss (Figure 12A), improved barrier integrity (Figure 12B), and resolved diarrhea (Figure 12C). However, this protection exhibited a strict dose-dependency, revealing a limited therapeutic window. In contrast, a higher dose (0.1 mg/kg) was ineffective, suggesting that precise dosing is critical for uncoupling toxicity from efficacy. Furthermore, this optimal dose prevented macroscopic tissue damage, including colon shortening (Figure 12D). Crucially, low-dose tacrolimus protected the gut barrier while maintaining tumor control. Tumor growth (Figure 13A) and morphology assessed by ultrasound (Figure 13B) in the combination group were indistinguishable from the ICI-only controls. To explore the immunologic correlates of this preserved efficacy, we assessed systemic and regional immune responses. Profiling revealed that low-dose tacrolimus did not compromise the systemic activation of antitumor immunity; specifically, it maintained the ICI-driven expansion of splenic CD8+ T cells (Figure 13C and D). The functional capacity of these effector cells was also maintained. The production of key cytokines (TNF-α and interferon gamma) by both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells remained intact upon tacrolimus cotreatment (Figure 13E–J). This protected immunity extended regionally, as the augmented interferon gamma response by CD8+ T cells in the MLNs was maintained (Figure 13K). Collectively, our findings support a pharmacologic model wherein low-dose tacrolimus dissociates gut toxicity from therapeutic benefit; by modulating the MLCK1 pathway to restore barrier function, it avoids blunting the antitumor immune response in either systemic or regional secondary lymphoid compartments.

Discussion

Although ICIs have changed cancer treatment, their efficacy is frequently compromised by gastrointestinal toxicity.15,16 In this study, we identify a dysbiosis-associated metabolic shift characterized by LPA accumulation, which engages the LPAR2–MLCK1 pathway to disrupt epithelial barrier function. Through genetic and pharmacologic models, we demonstrate that MLCK1 inhibition stabilizes the intestinal barrier and alleviates tissue damage while maintaining tumor control. This work provides a proof-of-concept for a precision strategy to uncouple the therapeutic benefit from the adverse events.

Building upon our previously established MLCK1 framework,10 the present study defines an early priming window of ICI toxicity. The etiology of ICI-associated intestinal injury involves a disruption of host–microbiota metabolic crosstalk.17 Consistent with clinical profiling of patients with ICI colitis and conventional IBD,18,19 our data highlight the expansion of Pseudomonadota as a hallmark of mucosal disruption. However, although prior work has predominantly focused on the loss of protective commensals and short-chain fatty acids,20,21 our metabolomic profiling implicates a distinct pathogenic pathway: a dysregulated lipidomic signature characterized by the accumulation of bioactive LPA and its precursor LPC. Under homeostatic conditions, luminal LPA is tightly regulated by host-derived enzymes (eg, PLA2g1b).22 Our metagenomic analysis suggests that the expansion of Pseudomonadota equipped with de novo lipid synthesis genes (eg, plsB) may introduce an aberrant microbial contribution to the luminal LPA pool, consistent with microbial LPA production reported in other disease contexts.23,24 Conversely, the concurrent depletion of Bacteroidota may impair the gut’s capacity to metabolize these lipids. This creates a pathogenic feed-forward loop where bioactive lipid accumulation triggers barrier defects, which, in turn, exacerbate local dysbiosis. Importantly, our clinical cohort corroborated this concept: patients with ICI colitis exhibited similar lipid dysregulation, and their clinical remission coincided with a reduction in fecal LPA and LPC, implicating these lipids as active drivers of human disease rather than mere bystanders.

Despite these clear metabolic shifts, a discrepancy remains between the mild barrier loss observed in our SPF mice and the severe clinical inflammation seen in patients. We propose a 2-step temporal model to reconcile this. Our current study, utilizing the SPF model, indicates an early stage characterized by barrier dysfunction. Focusing on this early phase allowed us to uncover the initial microbiota–metabolite dysregulation, where luminal LPA activates the LPAR2–MLCK1 axis to cause early epithelial barrier disruption. Conversely, our prior publication utilized a wild-derived microbiota transfer model, which provides the late, severe phase.10 In that model, the initial metabolic barrier leak allows extensive microbial translocation, progressing to severe, TNF-driven clinical colitis. Therefore, comparing these 2 models demonstrates that our current SPF study defines the early metabolic trigger, whereas our previous wild-microbiome study characterized the later, immune-driven severe inflammation.

Mechanistically, the LPAR2–MLCK1 axis functions as a critical metabolic sensor linking microbial dysbiosis to epithelial pathology. This pathogenic model contrasts with previous reports recognizing LPAR2 signaling as a protective mediator for epithelial restitution following acute or radiation-induced injury.25,26 We propose that the biological outcome of LPAR2 activation is strictly context- and duration-dependent. Although transient LPAR2 signaling aids repair, the chronic accumulation of luminal LPA during ICI therapy turns a brief protective signal into continuous MLCK1 activation. This persistent activation leads to sustained phosphorylation of myosin light chain (MLC), driving contraction of the perijunctional actomyosin ring. This continuous mechanical tension, in turn, triggers the structural reorganization of tight junctions—characterized by the disruption of ZO-1 scaffolding and occludin internalization—ultimately resulting in increased paracellular permeability.27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35 Thus, targeting LPAR2 or MLCK1 offers a unique therapeutic window to intercept injury before broad immune cell infiltration occurs.

A central dilemma in managing immune-related adverse events is the risk of impairing antitumor immunity through the use of broad systemic immunosuppressants (eg, high-dose corticosteroids, anti-TNF agents).36,37 Our data suggest that MLCK1-driven barrier dysfunction may facilitate the systemic translocation of microbial antigens, potentially triggering immunosuppressive feedback mechanisms that constrain antitumor immunity. By genetically or pharmacologically stabilizing the epithelial interface, we successfully mitigated intestinal injury without compromising tumor control. Although our MLN and splenic data reflect regional and systemic immunity rather than the local tissue microenvironment, they demonstrate an association between barrier integrity and tumor control, providing biologic plausibility for gut–tumor crosstalk. Nevertheless, the local immune networks connecting the mucosal barrier to the tumor microenvironment are not fully understood, and future studies involving deeper tissue-level immune dissection are needed to clarify these pathways.

Based on these findings, we evaluated clinically available agents capable of modulating the LPAR2–MLCK1 pathway. In our previous work, we identified epicatechin as a structural inhibitor of the FKBP8–MLCK1 interaction. To accelerate clinical translation, we transitioned to tacrolimus, a United States Food and Drug Administration–approved agent recognized for its efficacy in steroid-refractory IBD.38 By utilizing a restricted, low-dose intragastric regimen (0.03 mg/kg), we advanced this approach to a translatable pharmacologic strategy. This low-dose paradigm is proposed to exploit the drug’s FKBP-binding capacity to disrupt MLCK1 recruitment and stabilize the barrier, potentially uncoupling intestinal toxicity from the systemic immunosuppression associated with standard tacrolimus dosing. Indeed, unlike broad immunosuppressants that compromise systemic antitumor immunity,39,40 our strategy preserves CD8+ T cell function and interferon gamma production. The superior tumor control observed in MLCK-KO mice was not fully recapitulated by low-dose tacrolimus, which maintained efficacy comparable to ICI monotherapy. This discrepancy likely reflects the fundamental distinction between constitutive genetic ablation and transient pharmacologic inhibition, as limitations in mucosal drug retention or target saturation may constrain maximal efficacy. Nevertheless, by achieving potent mucosal targeting with minimal systemic exposure, this pharmacologic approach offers a strategy to widen the therapeutic window of ICI therapy.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations of our study warrant further consideration. First, although our metagenomic analysis reveals that the enriched Pseudomonadota (eg, E coli and K pneumoniae) possess the genetic capacity (via the plsB gene) to synthesize LPA de novo, we acknowledge that definitive proof of the exact cellular origin requires further investigation. Without direct in vivo metabolic tracing, we cannot rule out the contribution of host-derived enzymes (such as PLA2g1b) operating together with the altered microbiota. Therefore, we propose that the dysbiotic microbiome contributes to luminal LPA accumulation and subsequent barrier dysfunction. Second, although we delineated the LPAR2-MLCK1 axis, intestinal barrier regulation is governed by a complex signaling network; thus, LPA may act synergistically with other ICI-upregulated factors, such as interferon gamma, to exacerbate mucosal damage. Our focus on a single signaling axis may not fully capture the multifactorial pathologic synergy involved in ICI-induced injury. Finally, we propose that the low-dose tacrolimus regimen targets the epithelial FKBP8–MLCK1 axis to uncouple toxicity from systemic immunosuppression. However, we acknowledge that this remains an inferential pharmacologic interpretation. Because the current study lacks direct in vivo measurements of local vs systemic drug exposure and specific mucosal target engagement, this selective epithelial model requires future validation. Furthermore, its clinical translation requires rigorous evaluation of human pharmacokinetics and the development of colon-targeted delivery systems to establish a safe therapeutic window.

In summary, our study identifies the dysbiosis–LPA–LPAR2–MLCK1 axis as the initiating mechanism during the early phase of ICI-associated intestinal toxicity. Our findings support treatments that prevent this initial barrier damage, rather than targeting the severe inflammation that follows. Ultimately, this work establishes a novel framework for improving the safety profile of cancer immunotherapy by targeting this early window through the precision modulation of the intestinal epithelial barrier.

Materials and Methods

Human Subjects

The human stool samples used for untargeted metabolomic profiling in this study were collected from a subset of the ICI-treated patients described in our recent publication.10 It is important to note that the study design and datasets are entirely distinct; the current study focuses on longitudinal comparisons (pre- and post-treatment fecal metabolomics) without the healthy control group, whereas our previous work focused on histologic evaluations of paraffin-embedded tissue biopsies.

Animals and Experimental Models

Mice and ethics

Female C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Gempharmatech Co, Ltd (Strain #N000013). Long MLCK-KO mice and Villin-CA-MLCK transgenic mice were generously provided by Professor Jerrold R. Turner (Brigham and Women’s Hospital). To maintain consistency, female mice were used uniformly across all experimental cohorts, including the WT, MLCK-KO, CA-MLCK, and tumor-bearing intervention studies. All mice were maintained under SPF conditions at Anhui Medical University. Importantly, all animal models, tumor-bearing experiments, and pharmacologic datasets (eg, tacrolimus treatments) presented in this study were generated from independent experimental cohorts, with no overlap with our previously published work.10 All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the protocols approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (Reference number: LLSC20200929).

Cell culture

Murine B16 melanoma cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, C11875500BT) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (VivaCell, C04001-500), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 15 mM HEPES. Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Experimental Design

Immune checkpoint inhibitor–induced intestinal injury model (nontumor)

To establish the intestinal toxicity time-course model, mice were treated intraperitoneally with a combination of anti–CTLA-4 (clone 9H10, BioXCell) and anti–PD-L1 (clone 10F.9G2TM, BioXCell) antibodies, or polyclonal Syrian hamster IgG control (BioXCell). Treatments were administered at a dose of 10 mg/kg per antibody every 3 days for durations of 1, 2, 4, or 6 weeks. Fecal samples were collected 24 hours prior to the end point for moisture analysis. At the indicated timepoints, mice were anesthetized for blood collection and subsequently euthanized to harvest tissues, including the small intestine, colon, spleen, and MLNs.

Syngeneic tumor model and immune checkpoint inhibitor efficacy

To evaluate the antitumor efficacy of ICIs, the flank region of the mice was depilated, followed by subcutaneous inoculation with 2 × 105 B16 melanoma cells. Mice were administered a combination of anti–CTLA-4 and anti–PD-L1 blocking antibodies via intraperitoneal injection. Each antibody was given at a dose of 10 mg/kg every 3 days. At the experimental end point, mice were euthanized, and peripheral blood and tissues were harvested for subsequent analysis.

Tacrolimus intervention study

To assess the barrier-protective effects of tacrolimus, mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 2 × 105 B16 melanoma cells on day 0. Tacrolimus (MedChemExpress, HY-13756) was administered by oral gavage at doses of 0.03 mg/kg or 0.1 mg/kg on the mornings of days 4, 7, 10, 13, and 16. In the afternoons of the same treatment days, mice received intraperitoneal injections of combined anti–CTLA-4 and anti–PD-L1 antibodies (10 mg/kg each). At the experimental end point, the mice were euthanized, and tissues including the spleen, MLN, and colon were collected for analysis.

Hematoxylin and Eosin

Staining paraffin-embedded tissue sections were incubated at 60 °C for 30 minutes and deparaffinized in 3 changes of xylene for 15 minutes each. Rehydration was performed using a graded ethanol series (100% for 2 minutes, followed by 95%, 80%, and 70% ethanol, and distilled water for 1 minutes each). The sections were stained with hematoxylin for 1 minute, rinsed under running tap water, and briefly differentiated in 1% acid alcohol. The slides were counterstained with eosin for 1 minute, then dehydrated through graded alcohols, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral medium for microscopic analysis.

Shotgun Metagenomic Sequencing

Raw sequencing data (Illumina HiSeq platform) were processed with fastp for quality control. Reads aligning to the host genome (GRCm39) were identified and removed using Bowtie2. De novo metagenomic assembly was carried out with MEGAHIT, utilizing a range of k-mer sizes (35–95). To maximize gene recovery, clean reads were mapped back to the assembled contigs; unmapped reads were subsequently pooled and subjected to a mixed assembly strategy using the same parameters.

Untargeted Metabolomics

Untargeted liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry analysis was performed on fecal samples (20 mg) using a Q Exactive HF-X Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at Metware Biotechnology Co, Ltd. Chromatographic separation was carried out on a Waters ACQUITY Premier HSS T3 Column using a mobile phase of 0.1% formic acid in water and acetonitrile under gradient conditions. Data acquisition was performed in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes using data-dependent acquisition. The mass range was set to m/z 75 to 1000 at a resolution of 35,000, and tandem mass spectrometry fragmentation was performed using stepped normalized collision energies (30, 40, and 50 eV).

Western Blot

Total proteins were extracted from intestinal tissues using radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein extracts were resolved by 7.5%–10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and electrotransferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Bio-Rad). After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 for 1 hour at room temperature, the membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following consecutive washes with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20, membranes were incubated with corresponding horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 hours at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system, and densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software. Protein expression levels were normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was isolated from Caco-2 cells and intestinal tissues using ES Science purification kits and quantified via a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer. Complementary DNA was synthesized from 500 ng of RNA using NovoScript SuperMix (Novoprotein), followed by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) using NovoStart SYBR SuperMix on a LightCycler 96 System (Roche). Relative expression was normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase using the the 2−ΔΔCt method. The qRT-PCR primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on 4-μm sections derived from tissues fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Following deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen unmasking was achieved by boiling in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 10 minutes. To eliminate autofluorescence, a photobleaching protocol was applied: sections were immersed in an alkaline peroxide mixture (4.8 mL 1 N NaOH combined with 30 mL 30% H2O2 and 164 mL phosphate-buffered saline) and exposed to high-intensity LED light. This bleaching step consisted of 2 consecutive 1-hour incubations using fresh solution. Nonspecific sites were blocked with 5% goat serum (37 °C, 30 minutes) prior to overnight incubation with primary antibodies (1:100) at 4 °C. Following 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Beyotime) nuclear counterstaining (15 minutes), sections were mounted with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant to minimize fluorescence quenching. Intestinal Permeability Assay

Intestinal permeability was determined by fluorescein tracer assay. After a 4-hour fast, mice were gavaged with 10 mg fluorescein sodium salt (Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.25 mL deionized water. Serum samples were obtained 4 hours later. Fluorescence was quantified on a BioTek Synergy HTX microplate reader (Agilent Technologies) using 485 nm excitation and 528 nm emission. Concentrations were interpolated from a standard curve.

Assessment of Fecal Water Content

Fecal samples were collected into preweighed tubes, and their wet weights were recorded. The samples were then dried uncapped at 90 °C for 48 hours in a forced-air oven to a constant weight. Dry weights were recorded, and fecal water content was calculated using the formula: [1 − (dry weight / wet weight)] × 100%.

Isolation and Ex Vivo Stimulation of Immune Cells

Spleens and MLNs were harvested into cold T-cell medium (RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum). After careful removal of extraneous adipose tissue from MLNs, tissues were dissociated through a 70-μm strainer using a syringe plunger, and cells were collected by rinsing. The resulting single-cell suspensions were used for downstream assays. All suspensions were centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 minutes at 4 °C. Splenic red blood cells were lysed with 1× RBC lysis buffer (room temperature). After filtering through a 40-μm strainer and a second centrifugation, cells from spleens and MLNs were resuspended in 500 μL of cell stimulation cocktail (phorbol myristate acetate 500 ng/mL, ionomycin 1 μg/mL, GolgiPlug 1×) and incubated for 4 hours at 37 °C in 5% CO2 prior to intracellular staining.

Flow Cytometry and Antibody Staining

Cells were first stained with Zombie Red Fixable Viability Dye (BioLegend; 20 minutes, room temperature) and washed with fluorescence-activated cell sorting buffer; they were then incubated with purified anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody (BioLegend; 20 minutes, room temperature) for Fc blockade prior to immunostaining. Surface staining was then performed using specific fluorophore-conjugated antibodies for 20 minutes at room temperature in the dark. For intracellular cytokine detection, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm Kit (BD Biosciences), whereas nuclear targets were processed using the True-Nuclear Transcription Factor Buffer Set (BioLegend). Following permeabilization, cells were stained with intracellular antibodies for 20 minutes at room temperature. Data were acquired on a NovoCyte Advanteon flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies).
