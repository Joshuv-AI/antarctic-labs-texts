Maximum smoothed likelihood method for the combination of multiple diagnostic tests, with application to the ROC estimation
Fangyong Zheng 1 , Pengfei Li 2 , and Tao Yu 3

Abstract
In medical diagnostics, leveraging multiple biomarkers can significantly improve classification accuracy compared to using a single biomarker. While existing methods based on exponential tilting or density ratio models have shown promise, their assumptions may be overly restrictive in practice. In this paper, we adopt a flexible semiparametric model that relates the density ratio of diseased to healthy subjects through an unknown monotone transformation of a linear combination of biomarkers. To enhance estimation efficiency, we propose a smoothed likelihood framework that exploits the smoothness in the underlying densities and transformation function. Building on the maximum smoothed likelihood methodology, we construct estimators for the model parameters and the associated probability density functions. We develop an effective computational algorithm for implementation, derive asymptotic properties of the proposed estimators, and establish procedures for estimating the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and the area under the curve (AUC). Through simulation studies and a real-data application, we demonstrate that the proposed method yields more accurate and efficient estimates than existing approaches.

Introduction

In medical diagnosis, multiple biomarkers are often collected for each subject. Each biomarker may carry partial and complementary information about the presence, severity, or progression of a disease. Relying on a single biomarker may lead to incomplete or inaccurate assessments, particularly when the disease is heterogeneous or affects multiple physiological pathways. Therefore, appropriately incorporating and combining multiple biomarkers has become increasingly important for improving diagnostic accuracy, enhancing risk stratification, and enabling more personalized medical decisions
(
18
;
17
)
. Effective combination strategies can exploit the joint distribution of biomarkers to construct powerful diagnostic tools, such as composite scores or multivariate ROC curves, that better distinguish between diseased and non-diseased individuals; see
11
and the references therein.

Proposing the appropriate combination methods, establishing effective numerical algorithms, and exploring the underline theoretical properties have been popular research topics in the statistical community for decades. Assuming that the biomarkers follow a multivariate normal distribution,
25
established the best linear combination approach. Based on the Neyman–Pearson lemma,
22
showed that the density ratio between the diseased and healthy subjects maximizes the true positive rate at any fixed false positive rate, making it the gold standard for combining multiple biomarkers; see also
10
;
4
. Methods for combining biomarkers, established on the density ratio model, are available in the literature.
24
considered an exponential tilting model, where they assumed that the log density ratio between the diseased and non-diseased populations is a combination of multiple diagnostic tests; specifically, the model is given by

\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\exp\left\{\alpha+\beta^{\tau}\xi(x)\right\},

(1)

where
\alpha
is a scalar parameter,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\beta
is a
p
dimensional parameter,
\xi(\cdot)
is a known
p\times 1
smooth vector function of
x
,
f(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
respectively denote the joint probability density functions (p.d.f.s) of the biomarker measurements from the diseased and healthy subjects.
3
observed that the exponential function form assumed by (
1
) could be restrictive and may not be well satisfied in practice; see Section
6
for examples. They proposed the semiparameteric model:

\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\phi(\beta^{\tau}\xi(x)),

(2)

where
\phi(\cdot)
is an unknown nondecreasing function, and an empirical likelihood based method for estimating the model parameters. More developments of methods for combining the biomarkers can be found in
21
,
2
,
20
,
16
,
15
, among others.

Model (
2
) is less restrictive than the exponential tilting model (
1
), and can yield more accurate estimation of
\beta
when the assumption underlying (
1
) is violated. Therefore, we adopt model (
2
) in this paper. While the empirical likelihood method proposed in
3
is compatible with this model, it does not exploit potential smoothness in the underlying densities
f(x)
and
g(x)
, or in the monotonic transformation function
\phi(\cdot)
. In many practical applications, however, these functions are indeed smooth, and failing to leverage this property may lead to efficiency loss and suboptimal estimation. To address this issue, we propose a method under model (
2
) that incorporates smoothness to enhance estimation accuracy of
f(\cdot)
,
g(\cdot)
, and
\beta
. Specifically, we adopt a smoothed likelihood approach, building on the maximum smoothed likelihood method introduced by
6
. This technique parallels maximum likelihood estimation in parametric settings but is designed for nonparametric density estimation, preserving many desirable properties such as consistency and efficiency. The smoothed likelihood method has proven effective in a variety of challenging statistical problems, including inverse convolution
8
, smooth monotone and unimodal density estimation
9
, estimation of hazard rates and event-time distributions
13
, density estimation in mixture models
19
;
28
, interval-censored data
14
, and two-sample problems with likelihood ratio ordering
27
. We build on these developments and incorporate the smoothed likelihood framework into our estimation procedure.

In this paper, assuming model (
2
), we propose the smoothed likelihood and, based on it, define estimators
\widehat{\beta}
,
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
, and
\widehat{f}(\cdot)
for the parameters
\beta
,
g(\cdot)
, and
f(\cdot)
, respectively. We also establish and implement an efficient computational algorithm for estimating these parameters. Using these estimators, we further estimate the ROC curve and AUC. We rigorously derive the asymptotic properties of our parameter estimators, as well as those of the ROC and AUC estimators. Through extensive simulation studies, we demonstrate that our method achieves superior estimation accuracy compared to competing approaches. Finally, we illustrate the practical utility of our method by applying it to a real-data example.

2
Maximum Smoothed Likelihood Estimation

Let
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
and
\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}\}
be the
d
-dimensional test results from diseased and healthy subjects, respectively. Assume that
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) as a random vector
X
, and
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}
are i.i.d. as
Y
. Denote by
F(x)
and
f(x)
the cumulative distribution function (c.d.f.) and p.d.f. of
X
, respectively; likewise, let
G(x)
and
g(x)
denote the c.d.f. and p.d.f. of
Y
. We assume the monotonic density ratio model as in equation (
2
). For presentational simplicity, and without loss of generality, we consider the following form:

\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\phi(\beta^{\tau}x),

(3)

where
\phi(\cdot)
is an unknown, nondecreasing function supported on
\mathbb{R}
, and thus
p=d
. To ensure the identifiability of the model, we assume that the first component of
\beta
equals 1. The log-likelihood up to a constant coeffient is

\widetilde{l}_{n,m}(f,g)=\frac{1}{n+m}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm log}\left\{f(X_{i})\right\}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\rm log}\left\{g(Y_{j})\right\}\right],

(4)

subject to (
3
), and

\displaystyle f(x)\geq 0,\quad g(x)\geq 0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)dx=1.

(5)

This log-likelihood, however, is unbounded, as we can make
f(X_{i})
arbitrarily large for each
X_{i}
. The smoothed likelihood approach can be borrowed to tackle this unbounded problem; see
27
;
28
;
19
, and the references therein. Specifically, let
N_{H}f(x)
be a nonlinear smoothing operator on a density
f(\cdot)
, given by

N_{H}f(x)=\exp\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{H}(u-x)\,\log f(u)\,du\right\},

where
K_{H}(x)=|H|^{-1}K(H^{-1}x)
. Here,
K(\cdot)
is a symmetric multivariate p.d.f., serving as the kernel function;
H
is a
d\times d
diagonal, positive definite bandwidth matrix. We replace
f
and
g
in the log-likelihood (
4
) respectively with
N_{H_{1}}f
and
N_{H_{2}}g
to obtain the smoothed log-likelihood:

l_{n,m}(f,g)=\frac{1}{n+m}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm log}\left\{N_{H_{1}}f(X_{i})\right\}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\rm log}\left\{N_{H_{2}}g(Y_{j})\right\}\right],

(6)

where
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are the bandwidth matrices,
H_{1},H_{2}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Let
\widetilde{f}_{n}(x)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{H_{1}}(x-X_{i})
,
\widetilde{g}_{m}(x)=m^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}K_{H_{2}}(x-Y_{j})
, and
\lambda=n/(n+m)
; for simplicity, we assume
\lambda\in(0,1)
and is a constant. The smoothed log-likelihood
l_{n,m}(f,g)
in (
6
) can be rewritten to be

l_{n,m}(f,g)=\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{f}_{n}(x){\rm log}\left\{f(x)\right\}dx+(1-\lambda)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{g}_{m}(x){\rm log}\left\{g(x)\right\}dx.

(7)

With the reparameterization:

\theta(\beta^{\tau}x)=\frac{\lambda f(x)}{\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)g(x)}=\frac{\lambda\phi(\beta^{\tau}x)}{\lambda\phi(\beta^{\tau}x)+(1-\lambda)},\quad\psi(x)=\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)g(x),

giving

f(x)=\frac{\theta(\beta^{\tau}x)\psi(x)}{\lambda}\,,\qquad g(x)=\frac{\left\{1-\theta(\beta^{\tau}x)\right\}\psi(x)}{1-\lambda},

(8)

we can write the smoothed log-likelihood function in (
7
) to be

l_{n,m}(f,g)=l_{n,m,1}(\theta,\beta)+l_{n,m,2}(\psi)+\text{constant},

where

\displaystyle l_{n,m,1}(\theta,\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lambda\widetilde{f}_{n}(x){\rm log}\,\theta(\beta^{\tau}x)dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1-\lambda)\widetilde{g}_{m}(x){\rm log}\,\{1-\theta(\beta^{\tau}x)\}dx

(9)

\displaystyle l_{n,m,2}(\psi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\lambda\widetilde{f}_{n}(x)+(1-\lambda)\widetilde{g}_{m}(x)\right\}{\rm log}\,\psi(x)dx.

Hereafter, we shall take
\theta(\cdot),\beta,\psi(\cdot)
as the parameters of interest; based on (
3
) and (
5
), our proposed estimators for
\theta(\cdot),\beta,\psi(\cdot)
are the maximizers of
l_{n,m,1}(\theta,\beta)
and
l_{n,m,2}(\psi)
, subject to

\displaystyle\psi(\cdot)\geq 0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(x)dx=1;\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(x)\theta(\beta^{\tau}x)dx=1\,;

(10)

\displaystyle\theta(\cdot)\in[0,1]\text{ and is non-decreasing}.

The estimation of
\psi(\cdot)
and
(\theta(\cdot),\beta)
can be carried out based on
l_{n,m,2}(\psi)
and
l_{n,m,1}(\theta,\beta)
separately. Referring to
7
, page 122, the maximizer of
l_{n,m,2}(\psi)
is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\psi}_{n,m}(x)=\lambda\widetilde{f}_{n}(x)+(1-\lambda)\widetilde{g}_{m}(x),

(11)

which serves as the estimator for
\psi(\cdot)
. The estimators for
\theta(\cdot),\beta
are defined to be

\displaystyle(\widehat{\theta}(\cdot),\widehat{\beta})=\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta,\beta\in\textbf{B}}l_{n,m,1}(\theta,\beta),

(12)

where
\Theta=\left\{\theta(\cdot):\theta(\cdot)\in[0,1]\text{ and is non-decreasing}\right\}
and
\textbf{B}=\{1\}\times\textbf{B}_{-1}
are the parameter spaces for
\theta(\cdot)
and
\beta
. The optimization problem (
12
) can be achieved by a profiling procedure as follows. For each
\beta\in\textbf{B}
, let

\displaystyle\theta_{\beta}(\cdot)=\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}l_{n,m,1}(\theta,\beta).

(13)

Theorem
1
below ensures that the optimization problem (
13
) is equivalent to a continuous weighted isotonic regression.

Theorem 1
.

Assume Condition
1
in the Appendix. For each
\beta\in\textbf{B}
, consider
\theta_{\beta}(\cdot)
defined in (
13
).

(a)

Equivalently,
\theta_{\beta}(\cdot)
is also the solution of a continuous weighted isotonic regression:

\displaystyle\theta_{\beta}(\cdot)=\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\frac{\lambda\widetilde{f}_{n}(x)}{\widehat{\psi}_{n,m}(x)}-\theta(\beta^{\tau}x)\right\}^{2}\widehat{\psi}_{n,m}(x)dx.

(14)

(b)

Consider
\widehat{\psi}_{n,m}(\cdot)
given by (
11
). We have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{\psi}_{n,m}(x)\theta_{\beta}(\beta^{\tau}x)dx=\lambda.

Part (a) of Theorem
1
illustrates that
\theta_{\beta}(\cdot)
is the solution of a continuous weighted isotonic regression
(
12
)
, and can be readily solved numerically; the details are given in Section
3
. Subsequently, the profile likelihood is given by

\displaystyle l^{*}(\beta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lambda\widetilde{f}_{n}(x){\rm log}\,\theta_{\beta}(\beta^{\tau}x)dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1-\lambda)\widetilde{g}_{m}(x){\rm log}\,\{1-\theta_{\beta}(\beta^{\tau}x)\}dx,

(15)

and thus

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}=\arg\max_{\beta\in\textbf{B}}l^{*}(\beta)\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\theta}(\cdot)=\theta_{\widehat{\beta}}(\cdot).

Based on Part(b) of Theorem
1
, we observe that
\widehat{\psi}(\cdot),\widehat{\theta}(\cdot),\widehat{\beta}
are the maximizers of
l_{m,n,1}(\theta,\beta)
,
l_{n,m,2}(\psi)
subject to (
10
). Consequently, the estimators for
f(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
can be obtained by

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(x)=\widehat{\theta}(\widehat{\beta}^{\tau}x)\widehat{\psi}(x)/\lambda,\qquad\widehat{g}(x)=\left\{1-\widehat{\theta}(\widehat{\beta}^{\tau}x)\right\}\widehat{\psi}(x)/(1-\lambda).

3
Computational Algorithm

We develop a numerical algorithm for computing the proposed estimators in Section
2
. The challenges lie in the fact that the objective functions in (
14
) and (
15
) are formed of
d
-dimensional integrals, which can be computationally intensive to evaluate directly. To avoid this multiple dimensional integration, we propose a Monte Carlo estimation procedure.

First of all, with data
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
,
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}
, we need the bandwidth
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
to compute
\widetilde{f}_{n}(x)
and
\widetilde{g}_{m}(x)
through

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{H_{1}}(x-X_{i})\,,\quad\widetilde{g}_{m}(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}K_{H_{2}}(x-Y_{j}).

We adopt the plug-in method of
26
to obtain
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
based on
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
and
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}
respectively.

We then generate
N
samples,
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N}
from
\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot)
, and
N
samples,
Z_{N+1},\ldots,Z_{2N}
from
\widetilde{g}_{m}(\cdot)
. Specifically, to generate a random variable
Z
from
\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot)
, we can use the following steps:

•

Step 1: Draw an
X
from
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
with equal probability.

•

Step 2: Draw a
U
from
K(\cdot)
.

•

Step 3: Let
Z=X+H_{1}U
.

We can check that given
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
,
Z\sim\widetilde{f}_{n}(x)
. In particular, the conditional c.d.f. of
Z
is given by

\displaystyle P(Z\leq x|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=P(X+H_{1}\cdot U\leq x|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}P\left(U\leq H_{1}^{-1}(x-X_{i})|X_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{K}(H_{1}^{-1}(x-X_{i})),

which is the corresponding conditional c.d.f. for
\widetilde{f}_{n}(x)
, where
\mathcal{K}(\cdot)
denotes the corresponding c.d.f. of
K(\cdot)
. Similarly, conditional on
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}
, we can generate
Z\sim\widetilde{g}_{m}(x)
. Subsequently, the objective function in (
14
) can be estimated by

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\frac{\lambda\widetilde{f}_{n}(x)}{\widehat{\psi}_{n,m}(x)}-\theta(\beta^{\tau}x)\right\}^{2}\widehat{\psi}_{n,m}(x)dx

(16)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\frac{\lambda\widetilde{f}_{n}(Z_{i})}{\widehat{\psi}_{n,m}(Z_{i})}-\theta(\beta^{\tau}Z_{i})\right\}^{2}+\frac{1}{N}(1-\lambda)\sum_{i=N+1}^{2N}\left\{\frac{\lambda\widetilde{f}_{n}(Z_{i})}{\widehat{\psi}_{n,m}(Z_{i})}-\theta(\beta^{\tau}Z_{i})\right\}^{2}.

To ensure the approximation accuracy, we can set
N
to be a large value; in particular, in our numerical studies, we have used
N=10,000
.
For each
\beta\in\text{B}
, finding
\theta\in\Theta
that maximizes (
16
) can be achieved by applying the standard pool adjacent violators (PAVA) algorithm
(
1
)
; the details are given in the following steps.

•

Step 1: sort
\left\{\beta^{\tau}Z_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{2N}
from smallest to largest, and denote

\displaystyle\beta^{\tau}Z_{(1)}\leq\ldots\leq\beta^{\tau}Z_{(2N)}.

•

Step 2: accordingly, the objective function (
16
) can be written to be

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\left\{\frac{\lambda\widetilde{f}_{n}(Z_{(i)})}{\widehat{\psi}_{n,m}(Z_{(i)})}-\theta(\beta^{\tau}Z_{(i)})\right\}^{2}w_{(i)},

(17)

where
w_{(i)}=\lambda
if
Z_{(i)}
is from
\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot)
, and
w_{(i)}=1-\lambda
, otherwise.

•

Step 3: the available PAVA algorithms, e.g.,
pava()
function in R, can be applied to solve (
17
) numerically.

Finally, the profile likelihood in (
15
) can be approximated by

\frac{1}{N}\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,{\rm log}\,\left\{\theta_{\beta}(\beta^{\tau}Z_{i})\right\}+\frac{1}{N}(1-\lambda)\sum_{i=N+1}^{2N}{\rm log}\,\left\{1-\theta_{\beta}(\beta^{\tau}Z_{i})\right\},

(18)

and can be readily solved by the
optim()
function in R.

Asymptotic Properties

In this section, we explore the asymptotic properties of our estimators. Let
F_{0}(\cdot)
,
f_{0}(\cdot)
,
G_{0}(\cdot)
,
g_{0}(\cdot)
,
\theta_{0}(\cdot)
, and
\beta_{0}
respectively denote their corresponding counter parts, and let

\displaystyle d(\theta_{1},\beta_{1};\theta_{2},\beta_{2})=\left[\int\left\{\theta_{1}(\beta_{1}^{\tau}x)-\theta_{2}(\beta_{2}^{\tau}x)\right\}^{2}\,d\Psi_{0}(x)\right]^{1/2},

where
\Psi_{0}(x)=\lambda F_{0}(x)+(1-\lambda)G_{0}(x)
. Furthermore, we denote

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}=\max_{H\in\{H_{1},H_{2}\};f\in\{f_{0},g_{0}\}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|K_{H}*f(x)-f(x)|dx,

where
K*f(x)
denotes the convolution of functions
K
and
f
:
K*f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x-y)f(y)dy
.
Based on the standard results in kernel smoothing, we have
\alpha_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. In particular, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
H=\text{diag}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})
, such that for each
i=1,\ldots,d
,
h_{i}\to 0
, and
K(\cdot)
satisfy Condition
1
in the Appendix. Moreover, let
f(x)
be a p.d.f. defined on
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, satisfying

\displaystyle\int|D^{\alpha}f(x)|dx<\infty,

where
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})^{\tau}
such that
\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{d}=2
, and
D^{\alpha}f=\frac{\partial^{\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}}f}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\int\left|K_{H}*f(x)-f(x)\right|dx=O\left\{\left(\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq d}h_{i}\right)^{2}\right\}.

We have the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Assume Conditions
1
–
6
in the Appendix. We have

(a)

d(\widehat{\theta},\widehat{\beta};\theta_{0},\beta_{0})=O_{p}(n^{-1/3})\vee O_{p}(\sqrt{\alpha_{n}}),

(b)

\widehat{\beta}-\beta_{0}=O_{p}(n^{-1/3})\vee O_{p}(\sqrt{\alpha_{n}})
.

Based on Theorem
2
, we are able to establish the convergence of
\widehat{f}(\cdot)
and
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
.

Corollary 1
.

Assume Conditions
1
–
7
in the Appendix. Let

\displaystyle\eta_{n}=\max\left\{|H_{1}|,\ |H_{2}|\right\},

where
|H|=h_{1}\cdots h_{d}
for
H=\text{diag}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})
. Assuming that
n\eta_{n}\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\int\left\{\widehat{f}(x)-f_{0}(x)\right\}^{2}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{p}\left(n^{-2/3}\right)\vee O_{p}\left(\alpha_{n}^{2}\right)\vee O_{p}\left(1/(n\eta_{n})\right)

\displaystyle\int\left\{\widehat{g}(x)-g_{0}(x)\right\}^{2}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{p}\left(n^{-2/3}\right)\vee O_{p}\left(\alpha_{n}^{2}\right)\vee O_{p}\left(1/(n\eta_{n})\right).

Remark 1
.

Consider the case where
H_{1}=H_{2}=\text{diag}(h,\ldots,h)
, and assume that the conditions of Lemma
1
are satisfied. Under this setup, the convergence rate in Theorem
2
becomes

O_{p}\left(n^{-1/3}\right)\vee O_{p}(\sqrt{\alpha_{n}})=O_{p}\left(n^{-1/3}\right)\vee O_{p}(h),

which simplifies to
O_{p}\left(n^{-1/3}\right)
if we choose
h=O_{p}\left(n^{-1/3}\right)
. This represents the best convergence rate achievable by our estimators based on Theorem
2
.

Similarly, for
d\geq 2
, the
l_{2}
convergence rate of
\widehat{f}(\cdot)
and
\widehat{g}(\cdot)
, based on Corollary
1
, is given by

\sqrt{O_{p}\left(n^{-2/3}\right)\vee O_{p}\left(\alpha_{n}^{2}\right)\vee O_{p}\left(1/(n\eta_{n})\right)}=O_{p}\left(n^{-1/3}\right)\vee O_{p}(h^{2})\vee O_{p}\left(1/\sqrt{nh^{d}}\right),

with the optimal rate being
O_{p}\left\{n^{-2/(4+d)}\right\}
, attained when
h=O_{p}\left\{n^{-1/(4+d)}\right\}
.

5
Estimation for ROC and AUC

The ROC curve and AUC have been popularly used statistical tools and measurements in many scientific areas, especially in the medical research. In the framework of Section
2
, the combined biomarkers are respectively
U=\beta^{\tau}X,\widetilde{U}=\beta^{\tau}Y
. Denote
F_{C}(u)=P\left(U\leq u\right)
and
G_{C}(u)=P\left(\widetilde{U}\leq u\right)
. The ROC curve and AUC are given by
23
:

\displaystyle{\rm ROC}_{C}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-F_{C}\left(G_{C}^{-1}(1-s)\right)

\displaystyle{\rm AUC}_{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}{\rm ROC}_{C}(s)ds,

for
s\in[0,1]
. Furthermore, when
\beta=\beta_{0}
, we denote
F_{C,0}(u)
,
G_{C,0}(u)
,
{\rm ROC}_{C,0}(s)
, and
{\rm AUC}_{C,0}
accordingly; in this case,
{\rm ROC}_{C,0}(s)
is the optimal ROC curve
(
10
;
22
)
. With our estimates of
\beta
,
f(\cdot)
, and
g(\cdot)
in Section
2
, we can estimate
F_{C}(\cdot)
and
G_{C}(\cdot)
by

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{C}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int I\left(\widehat{\beta}^{\tau}x\leq u\right)\widehat{f}(x)\,dx,

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{C}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int I\left(\widehat{\beta}^{\tau}x\leq u\right)\widehat{g}(x)\,dx.

As a consequence, the corresponding ROC curve estimate and the AUC estimate are given by

\displaystyle\widehat{{\rm ROC}}_{C}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\widehat{F}_{C}\left(\widehat{G}_{C}^{-1}(1-s)\right)\,\,\text{for each $s\in(0,1)$},

(19)

\displaystyle\widehat{{\rm AUC}}_{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\widehat{{\rm ROC}}_{C}(s)ds.

We observe that
\widehat{{\rm ROC}}_{C}(\cdot)
satisfies the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Assume Condition
1
in the Appendix. The ROC estimate given by (
19
) satisfies
\widehat{{\rm ROC}}_{C}(0)=0,\widehat{{\rm ROC}}_{C}(1)=1
, and
\widehat{{\rm ROC}}_{C}(s)
is a non-decreasing and concave function for
s\in[0,1]
.

The following theorem establishes the asymptotic properties of these estimates.

Theorem 3
.

Assume Conditions
1
–
6
in the Appendix. We have

(a)

\sup\limits_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\widehat{F}_{C}(u)-F_{C,0}(u)\right|=O_{p}(n^{-1/3})\vee O_{p}(\sqrt{\alpha_{n}})\,,

\sup\limits_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\widehat{G}_{C}(u)-G_{C,0}(u)\right|=O_{p}(n^{-1/3})\vee O_{p}(\sqrt{\alpha_{n}});

(b)

\sup\limits_{s\in(0,1)}\left|\widehat{\rm{ROC}}_{C}(s)-\rm{ROC}_{C,0}(s)\right|=O_{p}(n^{-1/3})\vee O_{p}(\sqrt{\alpha_{n}});

(c)

\widehat{\rm{AUC}}_{C}={\rm{AUC}}_{C,0}+O_{p}\left(n^{-1/3}\right)\vee O_{p}(\sqrt{\alpha_{n}}).

Based on Theorem
3
and the discussion in Remark
1
, the best convergence rate for all these estimators is
O_{p}\left(n^{-1/3}\right)
, attained when
h=O_{p}\left(n^{-1/3}\right)
.

6.1
Settings

In our numerical study, we compare our method (named “Our”) with three competitive methods: (1) the empirical likelihood based method by
3
(named “Empirical”); (2) the exponential tilting method by
24
(named “Exp Tilting”); (3) the method established on maximizing AUC by
21
(named “MH”).

We consider two simulation examples: Examples 1 and 2. For Example 1, we set
d=2
. Let
f_{1}(\cdot),f_{2}(\cdot)
, and
f_{3}(\cdot)
be the p.d.f.s of lognormal(0,1), lognormal(1,1), and lognormal(4,1), respectively. We simulate
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
to be i.i.d., with

\displaystyle X_{i}\sim f(x_{1},x_{2})=\rho f_{1}(x_{1})f_{1}(x_{2})+(1-\rho)f_{2}(x_{1})f_{3}(x_{2});

and simulate
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}
to be i.i.d. with

\displaystyle Y_{i}\sim g(x_{1},x_{2})=(1-\rho)f_{1}(x_{1})f_{1}(x_{2})+\rho f_{2}(x_{1})f_{3}(x_{2}),

where different values of
\rho
are considered in our numerical studies. Note that the p.d.f. of the
\text{lognormal}(\mu,\sigma)
distribution is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(\log x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}.

Thus,
U(x_{1},x_{2})\equiv\log\left\{\frac{f_{1}(x_{1})f_{1}(x_{2})}{f_{2}(x_{1})f_{3}(x_{2})}\right\}
is of the structure:

\displaystyle U(x_{1},x_{2})=\log\left\{\frac{f_{1}(x_{1})f_{1}(x_{2})}{f_{2}(x_{1})f_{3}(x_{2})}\right\}=\alpha+\beta_{1}\log x_{1}+\beta_{2}\log x_{2}.

(20)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\frac{f(x_{1},x_{2})}{g(x_{1},x_{2})}=\frac{\rho\exp(U)+(1-\rho)}{(1-\rho)\exp(U)+\rho}.

(21)

In practice,
f_{1}(x_{1})f_{1}(x_{2})
and
f_{2}(x_{1})f_{3}(x_{2})
may represent the joint p.d.f.s of the biomarkers for diseased and healthy subjects based on the ground truth, respectively. The quantity
1-\rho
represents the proportion of individuals in the identified diseased group who are actually healthy, and likewise, the proportion in the identified healthy group who are actually diseased. Thus,
\rho=1
corresponds to the ideal scenario in which all diseased and healthy subjects are correctly identified. In this case,
\log\left(f(x_{1},x_{2})/g(x_{1},x_{2})\right)=U(x_{1},x_{2})
, so the model assumptions for our method and all competing methods are fully satisfied. Similarly,
\rho\in(0.5,1)
corresponds to the scenario where a proportion
1-\rho
of subjects in both the diseased and healthy groups are misclassified. Based on (
20
) and (
21
), the model assumptions for the “Our”, “Empirical”, and “MH” methods are satisfied, whereas those for the “Exp Tilting” method are violated.

For Example 2, we consider
d=3
. Let
g_{1}(\cdot)\sim N(0,1)
,
g_{2}(\cdot)\sim\text{Gamma}(2.5,4)
,
g_{3}(\cdot)\sim N(1,1)
,
g_{4}(\cdot)\sim N(4.5,1)
,
g_{5}(\cdot)\sim\text{Gamma}(2,4)
. We simulate
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, and
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}
by

\displaystyle X_{i}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\rho g_{1}(x_{1})g_{1}(x_{2})g_{2}(x_{3})+(1-\rho)g_{3}(x_{1})g_{4}(x_{2})g_{5}(x_{3})

\displaystyle Y_{i}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(1-\rho)g_{1}(x_{1})g_{1}(x_{2})g_{2}(x_{3})+\rho g_{3}(x_{1})g_{4}(x_{2})g_{5}(x_{3}).

Similarly to Example 1,
g_{1}(x_{1})g_{1}(x_{2})g_{2}(x_{3})
and
g_{3}(x_{1})g_{4}(x_{2})g_{5}(x_{3})
may represent the joint p.d.f.s of the biomarkers for diseased and healthy subjects, respectively, and satisfy

\displaystyle U(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\log\left\{\frac{g_{1}(x_{1})g_{1}(x_{2})g_{2}(x_{3})}{g_{3}(x_{1})g_{4}(x_{2})g_{5}(x_{3})}\right\}=\alpha+\beta_{1}x_{1}+\beta_{2}x_{2}+\beta_{3}\log x_{3}.

The quantity
1-\rho
represents the proportion of individuals in the identified diseased group who are actually healthy, and likewise, the proportion in the identified healthy group who are actually diseased.

For both examples, we consider the combinations of setting:
\rho=1,0.9,0.8
;
n=m=300
and

In summary, our method outperforms the other approaches in terms of both ROC curve and AUC estimation accuracy, maintaining low bias and error even under moderate misclassification and smaller sample sizes. The Empirical and MH methods provide reasonable performance but are less robust to misclassification errors. The Exp Tilting method consistently exhibits the largest errors and bias, indicating it may be less suitable in settings with misclassified groups.

Application

Pancreatic cancer is among the most lethal forms of cancer, with a five-year survival rate of less than
10\%
once diagnosed. However, early detection significantly improves the chances of survival. Unfortunately, pancreatic cancer often remains asymptomatic until it has reached an advanced stage and metastasized. Therefore, a reliable diagnostic test capable of identifying pancreatic cancer at an early stage could be of great clinical value.

In
5
, the authors collected a series of urinary biomarkers from three groups of patients: healthy controls; patients with non-cancerous pancreatic conditions such as chronic pancreatitis; and patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. The sample sizes for these groups were 183, 143, and 83, respectively. The urinary proteins measured included LYVE1, REG1B, and TFF1. We proceed to combine the test results of LYVE1, REG1B, and TFF1. To this end, we adopt the following semiparametric monotonic density ratio model:

\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\phi\left(\beta_{1}(100\times\text{LYVE1})+\beta_{2}(\text{REG1B})+\beta_{3}(\text{TFF1})\right),

where multiplying LYVE1 by 100 serves to bring its scale in line with the other biomarkers.

We use the data collected in the same institution-Barts Pancreas Tissue Bank, London, UK (BRTB). We view the patients with non-cancerous pancreatic conditions as healthy people, thus the non-diseased group sample containing the Healthy control group plus the non-cancerous pancreatic group, i.e
n=83
,
m=326

Discussion

This paper introduces a maximum smoothed likelihood approach for combining multiple diagnostic tests under the semiparametric density ratio model defined in (
2
). The proposed method accommodates the flexibility and smoothness of nonparametric components, leading to improved accuracy in parameter estimation and enhanced robustness against model misspecification.

We develop an efficient algorithm, establish the asymptotic properties of the parameter estimators, and derive estimators for the ROC curve and AUC along with their theoretical guarantees. Through numerical studies, we demonstrate that the proposed method consistently yields more accurate and stable estimates for both the combination coefficients and ROC/AUC measures, particularly in settings with model misspecification.

Despite its strengths, the performance of the proposed method could benefit from improved bandwidth selection for kernel smoothing. Additionally, it may face computational challenges when the number of biomarkers
d
is large, and the current convergence rates may not be optimal. Future research could focus on automating bandwidth selection, enhancing scalability to high-dimensional settings, and establishing stronger theoretical guarantees.

Remark 2
.

With Condition

, we have

\displaystyle\int\left|I\left(\widetilde{\beta}_{1}^{\tau}z\leq u\right)-I\left(\widetilde{\beta}_{2}^{\tau}z\leq u\right)\right|\,dP(z)\leq C\left\|\widetilde{\beta}_{1}-\widetilde{\beta}_{2}\right\|_{1},

\widetilde{\beta}_{1}=(\beta_{1}^{T},\beta_{1,1}^{T})^{T}
,
\widetilde{\beta}_{2}=(\beta_{2}^{T},\beta_{2,1}^{T})^{T}

\beta_{1},\beta_{1,1},\beta_{2},\beta_{2,1}\in\textbf{B}

Proof.
This remark follows by:

\displaystyle\int\left|I\left(\widetilde{\beta}_{1}^{\tau}z\leq u\right)-I\left(\widetilde{\beta}_{2}^{\tau}z\leq u\right)\right|\,dP(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\int\left|I\left(\beta_{1}^{\tau}x\leq u-\beta_{1,1}^{\tau}t\right)-I\left(\beta_{2}^{\tau}x\leq u-\beta_{2,1}^{\tau}t\right)\right|\,dF_{0}(x)d\mathcal{K}(t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int\int\left|I\left(\beta_{1}^{\tau}x\leq u-\beta_{1,1}^{\tau}t\right)-I\left(\beta_{2}^{\tau}x\leq u-\beta_{1,1}^{\tau}t\right)\right|\,dF_{0}(x)d\mathcal{K}(t)

\displaystyle C\|\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}\|_{1}+C\|\beta_{1,1}-\beta_{2,1}\|_{1}=C\|\widetilde{\beta}_{1}-\widetilde{\beta}_{2}\|_{1}.
