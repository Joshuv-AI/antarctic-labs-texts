Letter to the editor: Cohort overlap and measurement timing considerations in a study of intraoperative T1 tilt and postoperative shoulder imbalance
Authors: Sharath Raj, Vijay Goni
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

I read the article by Kinoshita et al [1], “Can intraoperative T1 tilt predict postoperative shoulder imbalance in patients with Lenke 2 adolescent idiopathic scoliosis?: a retrospective study in Japan,” with interest, since a modifiable intraoperative predictor of shoulder imbalance would be genuinely useful in practice. A few points seem worth raising.

This study builds on the same group’s earlier finding that postoperative T1 tilt change predicts postoperative shoulder imbalance (PSI) [2], drawn from a related patient population at the same center. It would be helpful if the authors could clarify the extent of cohort overlap between the two studies, as this would allow readers to better judge to what degree the present findings represent an independent confirmation using intraoperative rather than postoperative T1 tilt as the exposure variable.

The subgroup correlation reported within the PSI group alone (intraoperative T1 tilt versus change in radiographic shoulder height [RSH], r=0.678) was based on only 12 patients. Given the inherent limitations of correlation estimates derived from such small samples [3], this finding would benefit from interpretation with appropriate caution.

Perhaps the most notable methodological consideration is that intraoperative T1 tilt was measured with the patient prone under anesthesia, whereas the outcome, RSH, was measured on standing postoperative films. Coronal alignment measured prone under anesthesia does not necessarily correspond to standing values in idiopathic scoliosis patients, given differences in muscle tone, spinal flexibility, and gravitational loading between the two positions [4]. Clarification from the authors regarding how this positional and physiological difference might affect the reliability of intraoperative T1 tilt as a predictor of a standing radiographic outcome would meaningfully enhance readers' interpretation of these findings and their clinical applicability.

The clinical practice of monitoring T1 tilt intraoperatively remains a reasonable, low-cost strategy. We believe clarification of the points raised above, particularly regarding the relationship between intraoperative and postoperative alignment, would further strengthen the study's clinical applicability and contribute to a valuable scientific discussion. We welcome the authors' response.
