Stable Robot Motions on Manifolds: Learning Lyapunov-Constrained Neural Manifold ODEs
David Boetius, Abdelrahman Abdelnaby, Ashok Kumar, Stefan Leue, Abdalla Swikir, Fares J. Abu-Dakka

Abstract
Learning stable dynamical systems from data is crucial for safe and reliable robot motion planning and control. However, extending stability guarantees to trajectories defined on Riemannian manifolds poses significant challenges due to the manifold’s geometric constraints. To address this, we propose a general framework for learning stable dynamical systems on Riemannian manifolds using neural ordinary differential equations. Our method guarantees stability by projecting the neural vector field evolving on the manifold so that it strictly satisfies the Lyapunov stability criterion, ensuring stability at every system state. By leveraging a flexible neural parameterisation for both the base vector field and the Lyapunov function, our framework can accurately represent complex trajectories while respecting manifold constraints by evolving solutions directly on the manifold. We provide an efficient training strategy for applying our framework and demonstrate its utility by solving Riemannian LASA datasets on the unit quaternion ( S 3 S^{3} ) and symmetric positive-definite matrix manifolds, as well as robotic motions evolving on ℝ 3 × S 3 \mathbb{R}^{3}\times S^{3} . We demonstrate the performance, scalability, and practical applicability of our approach through extensive simulations and by learning robot motions in a real-world experiment.

Stable Dynamical Systems on Riemannian Manifolds

Learning stable dynamical systems on Riemannian manifolds combines differential geometry, control theory, and machine learning challenges. Recent approaches
[
7
,
8
]
focus on learning diffeomorphic mappings to transform stable canonical dynamics into complex behaviours using NMODEs and Gaussian mixture models (GMMs).

RSDS
[
7
]
uses NMODEs to construct diffeomorphisms for Riemannian stable dynamical systems, providing theoretical stability guarantees through pullback operations. However, this method is costly, as it solves an NMODE at each time step, hindering real-time applications.
Additionally, a fundamental issue in
[
7
]
is that the equilibrium of the learned diffeomorphism is assumed to automatically correspond to the desired equilibrium point, without enforcing this constraint during training. While
[
7
]
ensures convergence to
some
equilibrium point, users have no control over the location of this equilibrium point.

In contrast, SDS-RM
[
8
]
employs GMMs to effectively learn diffeomorphisms. Compared to RSDS, SDS-RM ensures computational efficiency while guaranteeing convergence to a user-defined equilibrium point. Nevertheless, GMMs struggle with scalability in high-dimensional manifolds
[
27
]
and oftentimes fail to capture the complex nonlinear dynamics that neural networks handle well.

Alternative approaches
[
28
,
29
]
explore contraction theory on manifolds
[
28
]
and Riemannian motion policies
[
29
]
, but focus on tracking a reference trajectory rather than learning stable dynamics from demonstrations.

Positioning Our Approach

Our work addresses key limitations in current methods by merging the expressiveness of NODEs with stability guarantees on Riemannian manifolds.
Unlike diffeomorphism-based methods
[
7
,
8
]
, we learn stable vector fields on manifolds directly, avoiding expensive inverse mappings.
Our approach retains the computational benefits of NODEs while offering the geometric awareness crucial for robotic applications involving orientation, impedance, or other manifold-valued quantities.

III
Background

This section presents the mathematical foundations of Riemannian manifolds, Lyapunov stability, and numerical methods for solving ODEs on Riemannian manifolds.

III-A

Riemannian Manifolds

An
n
-dimensional
manifold

\mathcal{M}
is a topological space that locally resembles Euclidean space in the neighbourhood of each point
x\in\mathcal{M}
.
Each manifold is embedded in an Euclidean
ambient space

\mathbb{R}^{m}\supseteq\mathcal{M}
,
m\geq n
.
Each manifold is defined by a collection of charts
{(\varphi_{x}:U_{x}\to U_{x}^{\prime})}_{x\in A}
with index set
A
, where each
\varphi_{x}
is a homomorphism between the open sets
U_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
and
U_{x}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
A manifold is
smooth
if each
transition map

\varphi_{x}\circ\varphi_{y}^{-1}
is smooth for overlapping charts
x,y\in A
.
Each point
x\in\mathcal{M}
on a smooth manifold corresponds to an
n
-dimensional
tangent space

\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, which contains the velocities of curves passing through
x
.
The
tangent bundle

\mathcal{T}\mathcal{M}=\bigcup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
is the disjoint union of all tangent spaces.
The orthogonal projection
\operatorname{projt}_{x}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
maps vectors from the ambient space to the tangent space at
x
.

A
Riemannian manifold

(\mathcal{M},g)
is a smooth manifold equipped with a
Riemannian metric

g
, which assigns to each tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
a positive-definite
inner product

{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle}_{x}:\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
that varies smoothly with
x
.
The induced norm is
{\|u\|}_{x}=\sqrt{{\langle u,u\rangle}_{x}}
.
The Riemannian metric enables the definition of geometric concepts.
In particular, the
length
of a piecewise differentiable curve
\xi:[a,b]\to\mathcal{M}
is
\ell(\xi)=\int_{a}^{b}{\|\dot{\xi}(t)\|}_{\xi(t)}dt
, where
\dot{\xi}(t)=\frac{d\xi(s)}{ds}|_{s=t}
is the velocity of
\xi
.
The
geodesic

\gamma_{x,y}:[0,1]\to\mathcal{M}
is the shortest curve with
\gamma_{x,y}(0)=x
,
\gamma_{x,y}(1)=y
, and constant velocity
{\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|}_{\gamma(t)},\forall t\in[0,1]
.
The Riemannian
distance
between
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
is
d_{\mathcal{M}}(x,y)=\ell(\gamma_{x,y})
.
The
exponential map

\exp_{x}:\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
is defined as
\exp_{x}(u)=\gamma_{x}^{u}(1)
, where
\gamma_{x}^{u}
is the unique geodesic satisfying
\gamma_{x}^{u}(0)=x
and
\dot{\gamma}_{x}^{u}(0)=u
. Its inverse, the
logarithmic map

\log_{x}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
, exists locally and satisfies
\exp_{x}(\log_{x}(y))=y
for
y
sufficiently close to
x
.

Differential Equations on Manifolds

A
manifold ordinary differential equation (MODE)
is specified by a vector field
f:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{T}\mathcal{M}
such that
f(x)\in\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
for all
x\in\mathcal{M}
.
The solution of a MODE is the curve
\xi:[0,T]\to\mathcal{M}
that satisfies the initial value problem

\frac{d\xi(t)}{dt}=f(\xi(t))\in\mathcal{T}_{\xi(t)}\mathcal{M},\quad\xi(0)=x_{0},\quad\xi(t)\in\mathcal{M}.

(1)

If
f
is parameterised by a neural network,
Eq.

1
is a
neural manifold ordinary differential equation (NMODE)
.

Lie Derivative

The
Lie derivative
of a smooth function
F:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to the vector field
f:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{T}\mathcal{M}
provides a directional derivative

{\mathcal{L}_{f}\;\!\!F}(x)=\frac{d}{dt}\left.F(\xi_{x}(t))\right\rvert_{t=0}={\langle\nabla_{x}^{\mathcal{M}}F(x),f(x)\rangle}_{x}

where
\xi_{x}
is the solution of
Eq.

1
with
\xi_{x}(0)=x
and
\nabla_{x}^{\mathcal{M}}F(x)\in\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
is the Riemannian gradient of
F
at
x
.

and

26
]
for further examples of Riemannian manifolds.

Example 1
.

The set of unit quaternions
S^{3}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{4}\mid\|x\|_{2}=1\}
equipped with the Euclidean inner product
{\langle x,y\rangle}_{z}={x^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}}y
forms a three-dimensional Riemannian manifold embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. The exponential map
\exp_{x}:\mathcal{T}_{x}S^{3}\to S^{3}

\exp_{x}(u)=\cos(\|u\|)x+\sin(\|u\|)\frac{u}{\|u\|}
for

\exp_{x}(0)=x

, where
\nabla_{x}^{S^{3}}F(x)=\operatorname{projt}_{x}(\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{R}^{4}}F(x))=\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{R}^{4}}F(x)-x{\langle\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{R}^{4}}F(x),x\rangle}_{x}

III-B

Stability

In this paper, we are primarily concerned with exponential stability on the Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
.
Since some manifolds, such as
S^{3}
, do not permit global stability due to the Pointcaré-Hopf Theorem
[
30
]
, we consider quasi-global stability, which allows additional unstable equilibrium points.

Definition 1

(Quasi-Global Stability)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{T}\mathcal{M}
be a vector field.
A point
x_{e}
is an
equilibrium point
if
f(x_{e})=0
.
It is a
stable equilibrium point
if there exist
\alpha\geq 0,\beta>0
such that
d_{\mathcal{M}}(\xi(t),x_{e})\leq\beta e^{-\alpha t}d_{\mathcal{M}}(x_{0},x_{e})
for every
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}\setminus E
, where
\xi
is the solution of
Eq.

1
and
E\subset\mathcal{M}
is a finite set.
If
\alpha>0
,
x_{e}
is
\alpha
-exponentially stable
.

Theorem 1

(Lyapunov Function)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{T}\mathcal{M}
with equilibrium point
x_{e}
.
Let
V:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a continuously differentiable function with
V(x_{e})=0
and
V(x)>0
for
x\in\mathcal{M}\setminus E
, where
E\subset\mathcal{M}
is finite.
If there exist
\alpha\geq 0,c_{1},c_{2},p>0
, such that
{\mathcal{L}_{f}\;\!\!V}(x)\leq-\alpha V(x)
and
c_{1}d_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x_{e})^{p}\leq V(x)\leq c_{2}d_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x_{e})^{p}
for all
x\in\mathcal{M}
,
x_{e}
is a stable equilibrium point.
If
\alpha>0
,
x_{e}
is
\alpha
-exponentially stable.

We refer to
[
31
]
for a proof of
Theorem

1

III-C

Numerical Solutions of MODEs

For training NMODEs, we require a method for numerically solving MODEs.
A baseline approach for solving the MODE
Eq.

1
is the
tangent space method (TS)

[
3
]
.
TS constructs an approximate numerical solution
\tilde{\xi}_{0:N}
of
Eq.

1
by computing a numerical solution
\tilde{\zeta}_{0:N}
of the ODE

\frac{d\zeta(t)}{dt}=(\operatorname{projt}_{x_{r}}\circ f\circ\exp_{x_{r}})(\zeta(t)),\quad\zeta(0)=\log_{x_{r}}(x_{0}),

which evolves in the tangent space of a reference point
x_{r}\in\mathcal{M}
.
The numerical solution
\tilde{\zeta}
is computed using a standard technique for solving ODEs, such as the Euler method, or a Runge-Kutta method.
By projecting
\tilde{\zeta}
to the manifold, we obtain the numerical solution of
Eq.

1
as
\tilde{\xi}_{i}=\exp_{x_{r}}(\tilde{\zeta}_{i})\in\mathcal{M}
for each
i\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
.
In our setting, the equilibrium point
x_{e}
is a natural choice for the reference point
x_{r}
.

By evolving in only one tangent space, TS can introduce significant approximation error.
The
exp-step method (Exp)
reduces this approximation error by switching the reference point frequently.
The steps of the approximate solution
\tilde{\xi}_{0:N}
computed by Exp are
\tilde{\xi}_{0}=x_{0}
and
\tilde{\xi}_{i+1}=\exp_{\tilde{\xi}_{i}}(\hat{\zeta}_{i}^{\prime})
for
i\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
, where
\tilde{\zeta}_{i}^{\prime}
is the endpoint of a numerical solution of the ODE

\frac{d\zeta_{i}(t)}{dt}=(\operatorname{projt}_{\tilde{\xi}_{i}}\circ f\circ\exp_{\tilde{\xi}_{i}})(\zeta_{i}(t)),\quad\zeta_{i}(0)=0,

over the the time interval
t\in[(i-1)T/N,iT/N]
.

Finally, the
dynamic-chart method (DC)

[
26
]
leverages charts instead of tangent spaces to transfer Euclidean ODE solutions to the manifold.
Let
\varphi_{0}:U_{0}\to V_{0}
be a chart of
\mathcal{M}
with
x_{0}\in U_{0}
.
DC solves an ODE in
V_{0}
until the solution approaches the boundary of
V_{0}
.
Once this is the case, the method switches to an adjacent chart
\varphi_{1}:U_{1}\to V_{1}
and solves another ODE, switching the chart again when the solution of the ODE approaches the boundary of
V_{1}
.
The ODE that is solved for the chart
\varphi_{i}:U_{i}\to V_{i}
is

\frac{d\zeta_{i}(t)}{dt}=(D_{\varphi_{i}^{-1}(\zeta_{i}(t))}^{\mathcal{M}}\varphi_{i}\circ f\circ\varphi_{i}^{-1})(\zeta_{i}(t)),\quad\zeta_{i}(t)\in V_{i},

where
D_{x}^{\mathcal{M}}\varphi_{i}:\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the pushforward of
\varphi_{i}
with respect to
x
and the initial point
\zeta_{i}(0)
is the end point of the trajectory in the previous chart
\varphi_{i-1}
, respectively,
x_{0}
for
i=0
.
The steps of the overall numerical solution are
\tilde{\xi}_{0}=x_{0}
and
\tilde{\xi}_{i}=\varphi_{i}^{-1}(\tilde{\zeta}_{i}^{\prime\prime})
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
where
\tilde{\zeta}_{i}^{\prime\prime}
is the endpoint of the trajectory in the chart
\varphi_{i-1}
.
We refer to
[
26
]
for more details on DC.

IV-A

NMODE Architecture

Let
x_{e}
be the desired equilibrium point of the sNMODE to train.
Our approach is based on a neural base vector field
g:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{T}\mathcal{M}
and a positive definite neural Lyapunov function
V:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
.
For obtaining a well-defined NMODE, we also require that
\nabla_{x}^{\mathcal{M}}V(x)\neq 0
for every
x\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\{x_{e}\}
.
This is ensured by combining a convex neural network
[
32
]
with an invertible feature transformation
[
22
]
.

Base Vector Field

We first describe the architecture of our base vector field.
Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be any smooth neural network.
Our base vector field
g:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{T}\mathcal{M}
is

g(x)=\operatorname{projt}_{x}(h(x)-h(x_{e})).

(2)

This definition ensures
g(x)\in\mathcal{T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
and
g(x_{e})=0
, so that
x_{e}
is an equilibrium point of
g
.
When not enforcing
g(x_{e})=0
,
g
may learn an incorrect equilibrium point or may not even possess an equilibrium point.
The stability issue in
[
22
]
is failing to enforce
g(x_{e})=0
.
The implications of this are discussed in detail in
Section

IV-C
.

Lyapunov Function

We define the candidate Lyapunov function
V:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, similarly to
[
22
]
, as

V(x)=\sigma(C(F(x))-C(F(x_{e})))+\varepsilon d_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x_{e})^{2},

(3)

where
F:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}}
is a smooth invertible Lipschitz neural network
[
33
,
34
]
,
C:\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth convex neural network,
\varepsilon>0
, and
\sigma
is the smoothed ReLU function
[
22
]
.
Eq. (
3
) ensures
V(x_{e})=0
and
V(x)>0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{x_{e}\}
, while the invertibility of
F
and the convexity of
C
ensure that
\nabla_{x}^{\mathcal{M}}V(x)\neq 0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{x_{e}\}

[
22
]
.
Concretely, we use
F(x)={[H(x),x]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}}
, where
H:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{k^{\prime}}
is a smooth neural network and invertibility is ensured by carrying over the input
x
to the output.
Following
[
22
]
, we use an input convex neural network (ICNN)
[
32
]
for
C
.

Stable Vector Field

Given the base vector field
g
, the Lyapunov function
V
, and the desired exponential stability rate
\alpha\geq 0
, we can now define the vector field
f:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{T}\mathcal{M}
of our sNMODE as

\displaystyle f(x)=g(x)-\nabla_{x}^{\mathcal{M}}V(x)\frac{\operatorname{ReLU}{({\mathcal{L}_{g}\;\!\!V}(x)+\alpha V(x))}}{{\|\nabla_{x}^{\mathcal{M}}V(x)\|}_{x}^{2}},

(4)

where we use the convention

when
\nabla_{x}^{\mathcal{M}}V(x)=0
.
For isolated points,
\nabla_{x}^{\mathcal{M}}V(x)
can be undefined since
\nabla_{x}^{\mathcal{M}}d_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x_{e})
can be undefined on manifolds that only permit quasi-global stability, instead of global stability.
In this case, we let
f(x)=0
.

IV-D

Training Strategy

We present an efficient training strategy for fitting sNMODEs to a set of motion demonstrations.
Training
Eq.

4
using gradient descent requires computing a costly second derivative of
V
, since it requires computing the gradient of
\nabla_{x}^{\mathcal{M}}V(x)
.
In practice, we found training
Eq.

4
to require at least twice as much time as training
Eq.

2
, which does not require computing a second derivative.
To train more efficiently, we split the training into three stages, where the first stage has, in turn, two parts:

1.

fit the base vector field
g
to the demonstrations by

(a)

pretraining on estimated tangents and

(b)

fit
g
using multiple shooting
[
35
]
;

2.

train the Lyapunov function
V
to descend along the training trajectories and the solutions of
g
; and

3.

fine-tune the assembled stable vector field
f
on the demonstrations.

While the first and second stages find a strong initialisation of
g
and
V
, the third stage smoothes out kinks introduced by the stability projection.
In the first stage, we first pretrain on estimated tangent vectors and then leverage multiple shooting
[
35
]
to further speed up training.
In the following, we define each loss function for a single human demonstration
x_{0:N}\in\mathcal{M}^{N}
of the motion we want to learn.
In practice, we average each loss function over a small batch of demonstrations.

for

g(x_{i})
and
u_{i}

\mathcal{L}_{\text{tangents}}=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(g(x_{i})-u_{i})^{2}.

Next, multiple shooting divides the demonstration
x_{0:N}

where

and trains on all fragments in parallel.
Further, it adds a term to the loss function that penalises fragment solutions whose endpoint is not equal to the starting point of the next fragment.
We use a manifold-aware MSE loss together with the multiple shooting penalty

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{MS}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K\hat{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i=0}^{\hat{N}}d_{\mathcal{M}}(\tilde{\xi}_{i}^{(k)},x_{i}^{(k)})^{2}

\displaystyle{}+\frac{\lambda_{\text{MS}}}{K-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}d_{\mathcal{M}}(\tilde{\xi}_{\hat{N}}^{(k)},\tilde{\xi}_{0}^{(k+1)}),

During training, we linearly increase the shot length

Concretely, we train

VI
Conclusion

We propose sNMODE, a general and expressive framework based on neural manifold ODEs (NMODEs) for teaching robots complex motions from demonstrations.
By leveraging NMODEs, we enable robots to track data modalities that evolve on Riemannian manifolds, such as orientation.
Our theoretical analysis proves that sNMODEs are stable, thereby guaranteeing predictable, safe, and robust robot motions.
We provide an efficient training strategy for applying sNMODE in practice.
Our experimental results establish that sNMODE outperforms existing frameworks for learning stable dynamical systems on manifolds and demonstrate the practical applicability of our approach.
