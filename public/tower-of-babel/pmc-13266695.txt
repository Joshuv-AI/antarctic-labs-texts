Structure–Property Relationships for Linear, Extended, and Branched Alkane Ammonium Iodide Derivatives as Interface Passivators for Narrow-Bandgap Lead–Tin Perovskite Solar Cells
Authors: Lana M. Kessels, Nicolas Daub, Guus J. W. Aalbers, Laura Bellini, Willemijn H. M. Remmerswaal, Martijn M. Wienk, René A. J. Janssen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interface passivation is crucial to reduce nonradiative recombination losses at the perovskite-electron transport layer interface and enhance the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells. Various molecules are known to result in a gain in open-circuit voltage (V OC). However, this gain is often associated with increased instability. Here, we investigate the interface passivation of a Cs0.1FA0.6MA0.3Pb0.5Sn0.5I3 narrow-bandgap (1.26 eV) perovskite by ammonium iodide derivatives with multiple, primary, secondary, or tertiary ammonium iodide groups, connected via alkane linkers of different lengths to establish structure–property relationships. The impact of these passivators on interfacial recombination, charge extraction, and device stability is elucidated by tracking the quasi-Fermi level splitting (QFLS) of perovskite layers and perovskite/C60 bilayers, together with the V OC of complete solar cell devices over one month. All tested passivators reduce the nonradiative recombination losses at the perovskite/C60 interface, but the extent to which this translates into an improved photovoltaic performance strongly depends on the molecular structure. Short-chain primary diammonium passivators provide the most favorable balance between effective passivation and charge extraction, yielding QFLS values that closely match the device V OC. In contrast, extended and branched multiammonium passivators improve photovoltage stability but impede charge carrier extraction, leading to reduced fill factors (FF) and short-circuit current densities (J SC). Secondary ammonium terminal groups promote crystallite formation on the perovskite surface, which reduces the efficacy of passivation. Importantly, combining small with larger-sized passivators in a single layer enables simultaneous enhancement of QFLS, V OC, FF, and operational stability.

Introduction

Lead–tin halide perovskite solar cells have witnessed significant improvements and have reached a power conversion efficiency (PCE) exceeding 24%. Important progress has been achieved by introducing additives to the perovskite precursor solution to improve crystallization and reduce oxidation of tin, resulting in less nonradiative recombination in the perovskite. − In recent years, considerable attention has been paid to the interface between the perovskite absorber and the electron transport layer (ETL) in p–i–n devices, because the commonly used Buckminsterfullerene (C60) is known to result in large nonradiative recombination losses limiting the open-circuit voltage (V OC). Appropriate interface passivation can improve the PCE. For lead–tin halide perovskites, successful interface passivators include inorganic halide salts, bulky organic cations, − and linear organic molecules. − Inorganic halide salts provide a strong reduction in nonradiative recombination, while preserving charge extraction. However, these salts generally do not affect the surface energetics and are sometimes based on toxic metals such as cadmium. Bulky organic cations can form quasi-2D hydrophobic capping layers that retard degradation pathways, , but an excessive thickness can impede carrier extraction leading to s-shaped current density–voltage (J–V) characteristics and increased hysteresis. While several mechanisms explain hysteresis in the J–V characteristics, , the role of ion migration and accumulation in the perovskite layer and at the interfaces is considered to be a key element. − The dipole moment of linear mono- or diammonium ions helps tuning the surface polarity and reduces energetic offsets at the perovskite/ETL interface. For instance, ethane-1,2-diaminium iodide (EDAI2) has shown promise due to its simple structure and is widely used as bulk and interface passivator, providing high performance. ,,−

However, these smaller molecules seem to migrate or redistribute over time. Jokar et al. noticed that the best performance of an EDAI2-treated FASnI3 perovskite (FA is formamidinium) was achieved when the devices were stored over two months. This, so-called, slow passivation was linked to the relaxation of the strain in the crystal lattice and reduction of surface defects. Similarly, Hu et al. noticed that the interface passivator led to an increase in performance over the first days after which it stabilized. They hypothesized that the surface was better protected or that the interfacial defects were reduced, both limiting degradation. Fluctuations in the PCE over longer periods could present a significant challenge for upscaling, undermining long-term operational stability, and complicating the standardization of a manufacturing process. Understanding the origin of such PCE fluctuations and mitigating them is therefore necessary.

In this work, alkane-1,n-diaminium iodide (ADAI2) derivatives with different alkane linkers, with primary and secondary terminal ammonium groups, and with additional ammonium groups were prepared and tested to systematically investigate the effect of the linker length, and of the nature and number of ammonium sites on the device efficiency and stability. The V OC and the quasi-Fermi level splitting (QFLS) of the perovskite layer (i) and perovskite/C60 bilayers (i–n) were tracked over time for a month to gain insight into the ability of the ADAI2 derivatives to reduce nonradiative recombination losses and to monitor device stability. It is found thatindeedmost derivatives exhibit a stabilization phase for the QFLS of the perovskite/C60 bilayer and the V OC of the solar cell. Additionally, larger molecules showed increased stability under operational conditions. Photovoltaic performance and transient photovoltage (TPV) measurements revealed that linearly extended and branched molecules have a stronger tendency to reduce recombination losses, however, at the cost of decreased charge extraction. By combining the small EDAI2 molecule with the larger N 1,N 1-bis­(3-ammoniopropyl)­propane-1,3-diaminium iodide (BAP-PDAI4), a balance can be found between stability and efficiency.

Results and Discussion

A Cs0.1FA0.6MA0.3Pb0.5Sn0.5I3 (MA is methylammonium) narrow-bandgap (1.26 eV) perovskite was used as the photoactive layer. It was processed from a precursor solution containing 5 mol % SnF2 relative to SnI2. Furthermore, 2 mol % glycine hydrochloride (GlyHCl), and 2 mol % of guanidinium thiocyanate (GuaSCN), each relative to the combined PbI2 and SnI2 concentrations, were used as bulk additives. The perovskite layer was spin-coated using a one-step antisolvent method as discussed previously. Devices in a p–i–n configuration were fabricated using an indium tin oxide (ITO) bottom electrode, a poly­(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly­(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS) hole transport layer (HTL), a C60/bathocuproine (BCP) bilayer as ETL, and a silver top electrode. Detailed procedures can be found in the Experimental Section.

EDAI2 is known to increase the efficiency of perovskite solar cells but, due to conformational rearrangements, the effect is time-dependent. Ideally, the passivation effect is activated instantly and remains over a longer time period. In order to do so, we explored different molecular design strategies that enlarge the passivator to suppress such potential migration while retaining effective interfacial passivation. Four classes of ammonium iodides salts were assessed (Figure ). In the first class, the alkane chain of EDAI2 was extended to propane (PDAI2), butane (BDAI2), and hexane (HDAI2). In the second class, the ammonium group of EDAI2 was modified via one-sided or two-sided methylation, creating MEDAI2 and DMEDAI2 with secondary ammonium groups. The third class, comprised linearly extended derivatives of EDAI2 or PDAI2 by substituting them with a second ammonioalkyl chain, forming AE-EDAI3 and AP-PDAI3. In the fourth class, a third ammonioalkyl chain was attached to the central ammonium group to create branched derivatives BAE-EDAI4 and BAP-PDAI4. The linearly extended and branched derivatives have primary ammonium terminal groups and secondary and tertiary ammonium groups at the center. The ammonium salts were synthesized by protonating the corresponding amines with 57% aqueous hydroiodic acid and precipitated by adding acetonitrile and diethyl ether as described in the Experimental Section. To use the salts for passivation of the perovskite/C60 interface, they were dissolved in a 2:1 (v/v) mixture of 2-propanol (IPA) and chlorobenzene (CB) at 0.25 mg mL–1. We note that pure IPA cannot be used for this purpose because it partly dissolves the perovskite layer. To mitigate the adverse effect of IPA, mixtures of IPA and CB or toluene, which do not affect the perovskite, were tested in different ratios. We found that IPA/CB 2:1 (v/v) yielded the highest performance for EDAI2. This solvent mixture was subsequently used to deposit all ammonium salts. The concentration of 0.25 mg mL–1 was found to be optimal for EDAI2 and PDAI2 and was also used for the other salts, unless specified otherwise. After spin-coating the ammonium salts on the perovskite, the layers were thermally annealed at 100 °C prior to applying the C60/BCP ETL.

Passivation with Alkane-1,n-diaminium Iodides

Figure shows the J–V characteristics and external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra of a nonpassivated device and devices made with ADAI2 derivatives (with A = E, P, B, and H) as interface passivators. Interface passivation by EDAI2 and PDAI2 increased the V OC by 80 and 60 mV, similar to previous observations, and raised the PCE from 15.9% to 18.0% and 17.3%, respectively (Table S1, Figure S1). In contrast, BDAI2 reduced the V OC by 20 mV and the PCE did not exceed 16.3%. A small, 20 mV, increase in V OC was seen for HDAI2, but the device suffered from a lower short-circuit current-density (J SC), yielding a PCE of 15.0%. All interface treatments increased the hysteresis between reverse and forward J–V sweeps compared to the untreated device, most distinctly for HDAI2 (Table S1).

The inset in panel (b) shows the AM1.5G integrated short-circuit current density (J SC,EQE).

Because the ADAI2 derivatives show distinct effects on the photovoltaic parameters, the perovskite/ETL interface was examined in more detail. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) of perovskite films indicates that deposition of EDAI2 creates a small surface dipole and enhances the n-type character of the perovskite surface (Figure S2), in agreements with previous observations. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) confirmed the presence of the ammonium salts on the perovskite surface, as evidenced by an increased C–C/C–H contribution in the carbon 1s spectrum associated with the alkyl chain and an enhanced NH3 + signal in the nitrogen 1s region (Figures S3 and S4). The high-resolution photoelectron spectra of the N (1s), I (3d), Pb (4f), and Sn (3d) regions show small shifts in binding energies, attributed to charging (Figure S5). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed subtle differences in the surface morphology induced by the various derivates (Figure ). For the EDAI2- and HDAI2-treated perovskite samples, no discernible surface features were observed compared to the neat perovskite, suggesting the formation of thin and uniform layers. In contrast, PDAI2 treatment resulted in small additional features at the grain boundaries, while BDAI2 formed small crystallites, predominantly located at the grain boundaries. For BDAI2, approximately 3% of the perovskite surface was covered by cubic or rod-like crystallites with lateral dimensions between 100 and 300 nm. Such surface crystallites are often observed on top of lead-halide perovskites films and have commonly been associated with PbI2. − Their formation may be related to the use of IPA, which is known to result in decomposition of the perovskite and increasing the amount of PbI2. We found that the crystallites cannot be removed from the surface by washing with IPA:CB (2:1 v/v) which would be expected when they would correspond to BDAI2. Based on the previous assignments, we attribute these crystallites to PbI2. X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements confirmed the presence of PbI2 by a characteristic 2θ peak at 12.7°, but we note that the intensity of the peak of PbI2 was similar for all ADAI2 samples tested (Figure S6). Alternatively, the crystallites could be due to a low-dimensional perovskite phase such as identified by Pan et al. when using butane-1,4-diamine to chemically polish the perovskite surface. However, XRD did not reveal any signatures of low-dimensional perovskite phases for either of the ADAI2 derivatives (Figure S6), suggesting that, if present, their concentration lies below the detection limit.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of a neat perovskite film and perovskite films treated with EDAI2, PDAI2, BDAI2, and HDAI2. Scale bar is 2 μm.

To further elucidate the passivating effect of the ADAI2 derivatives, the QFLS was determined from absolute photoluminescence (APL) measurements on glass/perovskite (i) and glass/perovskite/C60 (i–n) layer stacks, with and without ADAI2 treatment of the perovskite top-surface (Figure a). For neat perovskite (i) layers, surface treatment with short-chain molecules resulted in a small reduction in QFLS. In particular, EDAI2-treatment yielded an average QFLS of 880 meV compared to 896 meV (89% of the radiative limit) for the neat perovskite, indicating a slightly increased nonradiative recombination. This decrease in QFLS can be caused by the induced lattice reconstruction upon binding of EDAI2.

(a) Quasi-Fermi level splitting (QFLS) of neat and ADAI2 (A = E, P, B, and H) passivated films (i) and (i–n) stacks and the average open-circuit voltage (V OC) directly after fabrication (t = 0 days). (b–f) Time evolution between t = 0 and 30 days of the QFLS and V OC for perovskites without interface passivation (b) and with interface passivation by EDAI2 (c), PDAI2 (d), BDAI2 (e), and HDAI2 (f). (g) Comparison between t = 0 and 30 days. In between measurements, cells were kept in the dark and in nitrogen atmosphere.

For PDAI2-, BDAI2-, and HDAI2-treatments, the QFLS progressively increased (887, 893, and 895 meV) to the QFLS of the neat perovskite, suggesting a lesser or a more benign interaction with the perovskite surface when the alkane length increases. Upon deposition of C60 (i–n), the EDAI2-, PDAI2-, and HDAI2-treated perovskites exhibited a QFLS loss of approximately 50 meV, which is substantially less than the 88 meV-loss found for the perovskite/C60 bilayer without interface passivation, pointing to an effective suppression of interfacial nonradiative recombination by the passivators. For BDAI2, the QFLS was reduced more with C60 on top (81 meV), similar to the loss observed for the neat perovskite. XPS (Figure S4) indicates a reduced surface coverage for BDAI2 compared to the other ADAI2 derivatives, which is consistent with the larger loss in QFLS for i–n stacks with BDAI2.

Half stacks and full devices were stored in the dark under inert nitrogen atmosphere (i.e., under nonoperational conditions) and the QFLS and V OC were periodically monitored over a period of 30 days (Figure b–f). The initial (fresh, t = 0 days) values and final (aged, t = 30 days) values are summarized in Figure g. For all samples, the i layers exhibited a gradual decrease in QFLS over time, indicating the formation of additional nonradiative recombination pathways under storage. Among the surface-treated films, PDAI2 and HDAI2 showed the smallest QFLS losses of 20 and 25 meV, respectively. In contrast, no significant loss in QFLS was observed for the i–n stacks. This suggests that no additional recombination-active defects are generated at the perovskite/C60 interface during storage or that the losses at this interface created by C60 overshadow any other losses that form over time.

All interface-passivated devices exhibited an increase in V OC during the first few days of storage, followed by a stabilization over the remainder of the measurements. Conversely, the reference device showed a continuous decrease in the V OC from an average of 0.77 to 0.70 V after 30 days (Table S2, Figure S7). For EDAI2 and PDAI2, the QFLS of the i–n stacks matched with the V OC of the devices, except in the first few days when the V OC was slightly less. The correspondence of the internal (QFLS) and external (V OC) photovoltage suggests small voltage losses at the interface, consistent with our previous observation that the QFLS of i–n and p–i–n stacks is very similar when applying EDAI2 or PDAI2. With BDAI2 and HDAI2, the V OC was significantly less than the QFLS. Warby et al. explained that such difference occurs when the nonradiative recombination in the contact region is fast compared to diffusion of carriers to the interface. On the other hand, Krückemeier et al. showed that fast recombination rates indeed lower the V OC but that slow extraction has little effect on the V OC and rather reduces J SC.

After 30 days of storage, the PCE of the reference device degraded from 15.9 to 13.7%, while devices treated with EDAI2 retained their initial performance with a slight increase in V OC from 0.84 to 0.86 V, enhancing the PCE from 18.0 to 18.2%. Although PDAI2-treated devices also exhibited an increased V OC after aging, a gradual decrease in J SC led to a substantially reduced PCE from 17.3 to 15.7%. For BDAI2, the PCE dropped from 16.3 to 15.4% after 30 days. The HDAI2-coated device demonstrated stable V OC and reduced nonradiative recombination losses over time, but a dramatic drop in PCE from 15.0 to 11.2% in reverse scans. This was accompanied by a reversal and increase in hysteresis index from +16.7 to −19.8%.

Passivation with Methylated Ethane-1,2-diaminium Iodides

Increasing the alkane chain length beyond EDAI2 did not result in a stable passivating effect, prompting for an alternative approach to prevent migration. First, we tested the effect of additional methyl groups positioned at the terminal ammonium groups of EDAI2, leading to secondary ammonium groups in MEDAI2 and DMEDAI2 (Figure ). The additional methyl group hinders incorporation of the ammonium group into the perovskite lattice at the top surface, potentially suppressing migration.

Photovoltaic parameters extracted from J–V and EQE measurements on freshly prepared samples (t = 0 days, Table S3, Figures S8 and S9) showed that the MEDAI2- and DMEDAI2-treated devices underperformed relative to the reference device, yielding PCEs of 15.1 and 13.9%, respectively, compared to a PCE of 15.7% for the untreated device. While these reductions mostly arise from modest changes across all photovoltaic parameters, the DMEDAI2-treated device showed a substantially lower V OC of 0.68 V compared to 0.74 V for the reference accompanied by a significantly larger hysteresis.

XRD of MEDAI2- and DMEDAI2-treated perovskite films did not show significant differences with that of a neat perovskite (Figure S10). Morphological analysis by SEM revealed that depositing these methylated derivatives on the perovskite surface results in the formation of crystallites with significantly high surface coverage (Figure a). MEDAI2 formed surface crystallites of 100–300 nm at a surface coverage of approximately 7%, extending beyond the grain boundaries to the grain faces. The DMEDAI2-treated films exhibited approximately 14% of surface coverage, displaying a broad size distribution ranging from 50 nm cubes to elongated rods up to 750 nm in length. Notably, these differences emerged despite using identical concentrations (by weight) in the IPA:CB solvent for spin-coating MEDAI2 and DMEDAI2. Apparently, secondary ammonium ions promote the formation of surface crystallites, possibly by weakening the binding affinity to the perovskite lattice compared to primary ammonium ions. Additional washing steps with an IPA:CB solvent mixture were ineffective in removing these crystallites, indicating the formation of stable surface phases.

(b) Quasi-Fermi level splitting (QFLS) of fresh (t = 0) glass/perovskite/passivator molecule stacks with (i–n) and without (i) C60 on top, and the average V OC obtained from the devices. (c) Comparison between t = 0 and 39 days.

Using MEDAI2, the QFLS of the perovskite layer (i) remained unchanged relative to the neat perovskite (Figure b). With C60 on top, the MEDAI2-treated perovskite showed a passivating effect and the nonradiative recombination losses reduced from ca. 90 meV for the neat perovskite to ca. 60 meV (Figure b), which is just slightly more than the ca. 50 meV-loss found for ADAI2 (A = E, P, and H). The evolution of V OC and QFLS for the i layer and the i–n stack was monitored over 39 days (Figure c). Just like for all ADAI2 derivatives, the use of MEDAI2 resulted in an initially increasing V OC (Figure S11). Subsequently, the V OC and QFLS of the i–n stack closely matched and remained constant, similar to EDAI2 and PDAI2 (Figure ). We ascribe these characteristics to the fact that the primary ammonium group of MEDAI2 can bind to the perovskite surface similar to EDAI2 and PDAI2. Nevertheless, with MEDAI2, the V OC maximum (0.79 V) and QFLS were less than those achieved with EDAI2 and PDAI2 (V OC of 0.85 and 0.84 V), indicating that methyl substitution reduces the effectiveness of surface defect passivation.

The DMEDAI2-treated devices exhibited rather stable V OC and QFLS values throughout the aging period (Figures c and S11). The QFLS of the i layer and the i–n stack are close to the QFLS of the nonpassivated reference, but the V OC of the device with DMEDAI2 was 80 meV less than that of the nonpassivated device. However, charge generation and extraction remained largely unaffected, as evidenced by the high J SC,EQE of 27.95 mA cm–2 obtained from EQE measurements, suggesting that enhanced recombination, rather than slow extraction is the cause.

In summary, introducing secondary ammonium groups reduces the passivating properties of EDAI2. Comparison of results obtained for MEDAI2, DMEDAI2, and BDAI2 also suggests a correlation between the appearance of surface crystallites and a loss in V OC although the underlying mechanism of such correlation is unknown.

Passivation with Linearly Extended and Branched Diammonium Iodides

Given the apparent importance of primary ammonium groups for effective interfacial passivation via anchoring to the perovskite surface, molecules with an increased number of ammonium functionalities were investigated. For this purpose, linearly extended and branched derivatives of EDAI2 and PDAI2 were synthesized, creating AE-EDAI3 and BAE-EDAI4, and AP-PDAI3 and BAP-PDAI4, respectively (Figure ). When using EDAI2 and PDAI2, a concentration of 0.25 mg mL–1 was employed. The same concentration was used for AE-EDAI3 and BAE-EDAI4, because lower concentrations did not result in significant changes in the device performance. However, for AP-PDAI3 and BAP-PDAI4, lower concentrations were required to obtain a higher PCE (Figure S12), which indicated charge extraction limitations due to a too-thick layer of a low mobility or insulating material. Consequently, concentrations of 0.15 and 0.10 mg mL–1 were used for AP-PDAI3 and BAP-PDAI4, respectively.

Devices treated with these linearly extended and branched ammonium salts showed a substantial rise in V OC, and increased the PCE from 15% (for the nonpassivated device) to 18% (Table S4, Figures S13 and S14). The highest increase in V OC to 0.87 V was observed for BAP-PDAI4. However, this gain was accompanied by a reduction in J SC and FF. Correspondingly, the J–V curve displayed a shallow slope in the low-voltage regime, consistent with a field-dependent charge extraction or a voltage-dependent extraction velocity.

The QFLS of the perovskite (i) layer and perovskite/C60 (i–n) stack passivated with the extended and branched derivatives was monitored over a period of approximately one month (Figure S15) and the results are summarized in Figure a (Results shown in Figures and for EDAI2 and PDAI2 are slightly different due to day-to-day variations in fabrication). Relative to a nonpassivated perovskite, all interface-passivated samples exhibited an enhanced QFLS for i–n stacks and an increased V OC in solar cells, indicating suppression of nonradiative recombination at the perovskite/C60 interface. In addition, for all interface-passivated perovskites, the QFLS of the i–n stacks and the V OC increased during the initial days of the storage, except for the branched BAE-EDAI4 derivative (Figure S15c), where the QFLS remained the same. Despite the constant QFLS for BAE-EDAI4, the passivating effect was substantially less with a concomitant loss in V OC. This behavior is similar to what was observed when using MEDAI2, albeit without the formation of the surface crystallites (Figure S16). For the linearly extended AE-EDAI3 passivator, the QFLS of the i and i–n stacks are similar and correspond to the V OC after a stabilization period of 10 days (Figure S15c). The PDAI2-derivatives, AP-PDAI3 and BAP-PDAI4, reached a stable QFLS and V OC of 870 m­(e)V within 1–2 days (Figure S15d–f).

(a) Quasi-Fermi level splitting (QFLS) and V OC of fresh and aged films and devices with (i–n) and without (i) C60. (b) Decay and (c) rise times obtained from transient photovoltage measurements (TPV). (d) Open-circuit voltage tracking of devices.

TPV measurements were performed to further elucidate charge carrier dynamics. The devices were illuminated under 1-Sun conditions, and a pulsed laser was used to perturb the steady-state photovoltage to obtain an external voltage deflection (Figure S17). The resulting transients were fitted with a biexponential function to extract the rise and decay times (see Experimental Section), corresponding to charge extraction and recombination processes, respectively. Under open-circuit conditions at 1-Sun illumination, mobile ions are homogeneously distributed throughout the material and are minimally screening the field and not expected to affect the rise and decay times. At these illumination intensities, also capacitive effects to the decay time are minimal because the TPV decay time is governed by the recombination time, not by capacitive effects. All interface-passivated devices exhibited longer decay times compared to the reference device (Figure b), indicating reduced nonradiative recombination at the perovskite/C60 interface in agreement with the QFLS measurements. The rise time also increased upon passivation (Figure c), suggesting slower charge extraction. This effect was most pronounced for the PDAI2 derivatives and correlated with the slightly reduced J SC observed for these devices. Slower charge extraction is commonly associated with the introduction of interfacial dipoles or insulating passivation layers that impose an additional energetic or physical barrier for carrier extraction. ,

Figure d reveals that the stability of the V OC under continuous illumination also improves with increasing molecular size, in agreement with the trend that can be noted for alkane-1,n-diaminium iodides with increasing alkane chain length (Figure S18). Notably, the three PDAI2 derivatives exhibited a similarly stable V OC over the tracking period, matching with enhanced stability of the QFLS. Maximum power point (MPP) measurements under operational conditions (25 °C, nitrogen atmosphere, and 1-Sun equivalent illumination) (Figure S19) revealed that despite the initial performance gains for all interface-passivated devices, they are less stable than the nonpassivated reference device. The PCE for the interface-passivated devices mainly dropped due to a decrease in J SC, most likely caused by the effect of mobile ions. Comparison with the much longer shelf life stability (Figure ) indicates that illumination is the main cause of performance loss.

Because the V OC tracking of the devices showed distinct differences between the various passivators, the QFLS of the i and i–n stacks was monitored over time (Figure a,b). Consistent with the V OC tracking, the EDAI2-treated perovskite (i) films showed a rapid decrease in QFLS of approximately 35 meV within 5 min. In contrast, the BAP-PDAI4-coated perovskite (i) film displayed a higher initial QFLS of ∼900 meV, which remained stable over the measurement period. With deposition of C60, the QFLS became more stable for all samples, but the EDAI2-treated film still showed a slight decrease, whereas the untreated reference and BAP-PDAI4-coated sample exhibited a modest increase of approximately 5 meV.

(c) Steady-state quasi-Fermi level splitting (QFLS) and V OC. (d) J–V characteristics of the devices using EDAI2, a bilayer of EDAI2 and BAP-PDAI4, and a mixture of the two.

To combine the favorable charge extraction characteristics associated with EDAI2 and the enhanced photovoltage stability provided by BAP-PDAI4, sequentially deposited and mixed passivation strategies were investigated. The photovoltaic parameters of solar cells processed with combinations of EDAI2 and BAP-PDAI4 as interface-passivator are shown in Figure S20. The incorporation of BAP-PDAI4 in the EDAI2 layer (either by sequential application or by mixing before application) resulted in a higher (∼900 meV) and more stable QFLS for the perovskite (i) films. For the bilayer, sequential addition of EDAI2 followed by BAP-PDAI4 enhanced the initial QFLS. This combination resulted in the highest QFLS for i–n stacks within the investigated set but showed a 10 meV decrease over time (Figure b). The V OC of devices fabricated with either sequential or simultaneous application of EDAI2 and BAP-PDAI4 (0.84–0.85 V) matched reasonably well with the initial QFLS of the i–n stack (Figure c). Compared to the neat film, the sequential deposition resulted in a reduced FF and pronounced hysteresis (Figure d and Table ). Applying a 1:1 (w/w) mixture of EDAI2 and BAP-PDAI4 increased the FF relative to the sequential approach, reduced the hysteresis, yielding a PCE of 18.3% with only 4.5% hysteresis, and gave improved stability in MPP-tracking compared to other passivators (Figure S19). Applying a 2:5 (w/w) mixture of EDAI2 and BAP-PDAI4 enhanced the QFLS stability, however, the slight decrease in FF and increase in J SC resulted in the same PCE of 18.3% in the reverse scan of the fresh sample but with a slightly higher hysteresis of 6.4%.

Conclusions

We systematically investigated how the molecular structure of linear, extended, and branched alkane ammonium iodide interface passivators affect interfacial recombination, charge extraction, and stability in narrow-bandgap lead–tin perovskite solar cells. Across all investigated interface-passivation strategies, a reduction of nonradiative recombination at the perovskite/C60 interface was found. The extent to which the interface passivation translates into device efficiency was found to depend strongly on the molecular structure. Monitoring the QFLS of i–n stacks and the V OC of devices over time revealed that most interface passivators exhibit a stabilization period of a few days in which the QFLS and V OC increase. This enhancement was slower for the EDAI2-derivatives compared to the PDAI2 analogs. Once stabilized, the QFLS and V OC remained constant for over 30 days for the passivated devices. In contrast, the nonpassivated reference devices showed a gradual decrease in V OC. Short-chain diammonium passivators such as EDAI2 and PDAI2 provided the most favorable balance between interface passivation and charge extraction, as inferred from the match between the QFLS of the i–n stacks and the V OC of the solar cells. Longer alkane linkers were less effective. Use of BDAI2 resulted in the formation of crystallites on the perovskite surface and reduced passivation, while HDAI2 gave rise to large hysteresis. Similar to BDAI2, the methylated MEDAI2 and DMEDAI2 derivatives led to the formation of crystallites on the perovskite surface and reduced the passivating effect compared to EDAI2. These results indicate that primary ammonium groups are key to the passivating effect. QFLS measurements and TPV measurements revealed that while extended and branched multiammonium passivators can provide superior photovoltage stability, they compromise charge transport resulting in lower FF and J SC. The smaller passivators such as EDAI2 facilitate rapid extraction but result in unstable photovoltage. Combining the complementary characteristics by mixing passivators, these limitations can be partly lifted, enabling simultaneous enhancement of QFLS, J SC, FF, and stability. While advantageous for the PCE, various interface-passivators were found to increase the hysteresis between reverse and forward scans pointing to a prominent role of mobile ions affecting charge extraction at the perovskite/C60 interface, which agrees with the reduced operational stability under continuous illumination compared to nonpassivated devices. These insights provide a framework for future interfacial engineering in narrow-bandgap perovskites and advance the development of stable, high-efficiency perovskite devices.

Materials

All materials were used as received without further purification and stored under nitrogen. Anhydrous tin­(II) iodide beads (SnI2, 99.99% trace metals basis), tin­(II) fluoride (SnF2, 99%), anhydrous cesium iodide beads (CsI, 99.999% trace metals basis), glycine hydrochloride (GlyHCl, ≥99% (HPLC)), anhydrous chlorobenzene (CB, 99.8%), anhydrous 2-propanol (IPA, 99.5%), and N 1-methylethane-1,2-diamine were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Anhydrous N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%) and anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, ≥99.9%) were purchased from Thermo Fisher. Formamidinium iodide (FAI, >99.99%), methylammonium iodide (MAI, >99.99%), ethane-1,2-diaminium iodide (EDAI2), propane1,3-diaminium iodide (PDAI2), butane-1,4-diaminium iodide (BDAI2), and hexane-1,6-diaminium iodide (HDAI2) were purchased from Greatcell Solar Materials. Lead­(II) iodide (PbI2, 99.99% trace metal basis), guanidine thiocyanate (GuaSCN, >99.0%), N 1,N 2-dimethylethane-1,2-diamine, N 1-(2-ammonioethyl)­ethane-1,2-diamine, N 1-(3-amoniopropyl)­propane-1,3-diamine, N 1,N 1-bis­(2-aminoethyl)­ethane-1,2-diamine, and N 1,N 1-bis­(3-aminopropyl)­propane-1,3-diamine were purchased from TCI Chemicals. Poly­(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly­(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS, PVP Al 4083) was purchased from Heraeus Clevios. Bathocuproine (BCP, >99.5%) was purchased from Lumtec and the Buckminsterfullerene (C60, 99.99%) from SES Research. Prepatterned indium tin oxide (ITO) on glass substrates were bought from Naranjo B.V. For the synthesized passivators NMR spectra were obtained on a Bruker 400 MHz spectrometer. 1H chemical shifts are reported in parts per million (ppm) relative to TMS, with the residual solvent peak used as an internal reference.

Synthesis of Interface Passivators

N 1-Methylethane-1,2-diaminium Iodide (MEDAI2)

N 1-Methylethane-1,2-diamine (0.59 mL, 6.75 mmol) was cooled to 0 °C and concentrated hydroiodic acid (2.8 mL, 57%, 21.25 mmol) was added slowly. After stirring overnight, acetonitrile (5 mL) was added, and the mixture was left in the fridge overnight. The white crystals were filtered and washed with cold diethyl ether. After drying in a vacuum oven overnight at room temperature, 160 mg (7%) of white crystals were obtained. 1H NMR (400 MHz, D2O) δ 3.45–3.42 (m, 4H), 2.83 (s, 3H).

N 1,N 2-Dimethylethane-1,2-diaminium Iodide (DMEDAI2)

N 1,N 2-Dimethylethane-1,2-diamine (0.53 mL, 5.67 mmol) was cooled to 0 °C and concentrated hydroiodic acid (2.0 mL, 57%, 15.14 mmol) was added slowly. After stirring overnight, acetonitrile (5 mL) was added, and the mixture was left in the fridge overnight. The white crystals were filtered and washed with cold diethyl ether. After drying in a vacuum oven overnight at room temperature, 94 mg (5%) of white crystals were obtained. 1H NMR (400 MHz, D2O) δ 3.47 (s, 4H), 2.82 (s, 6H).

N 1-(2-Ammonioethyl)­ethane-1,2-diaminium Iodide (AE-EDAI3)

N 1-(2-Ammonioethyl)­ethane-1,2-diamine (0.60 mL, 4.85 mmol) was cooled to 0 °C and concentrated hydroiodic acid (2.56 mL, 57%, 19.39 mmol) was added slowly. After stirring overnight, acetonitrile (5 mL) was added, and the mixture was left in the fridge overnight. The white crystals were filtered and washed with cold diethyl ether. After drying in a vacuum oven overnight at room temperature, 500 mg (21%) of white crystals were obtained. 1H NMR (400 MHz, D2O) δ 3.54–3.40 (m, 8H).

N 1-(3-Ammoniopropyl)­propane-1,3-diaminium Iodide (AP-PDAI3)

N 1-(3-Amoniopropyl)­propane-1,3-diamine (0.94 mL, 7.62 mmol) was cooled to 0 °C and concentrated hydroiodic acid (4 mL, 57%, 30.48 mmol) was added slowly. Afterward, ethanol (8 mL) was added and the mixture stirred overnight. The yellow/white solid was filtered and washed with cold acetonitrile. After drying in a vacuum oven overnight at room temperature, 1.08 g (27%) of a yellow powder was obtained. 1H NMR (400 MHz, D2O) δ 3.25–3.17 (m, 4H), 3.17–3.09 (m, 4H), 2.18–2.06 (m, 4H).

N 1,N 1-Bis­(2-ammonioethyl)­ethane-1,2-diaminium Iodide (BAE-EDAI4)

N 1,N 1-Bis­(2-aminoethyl)­ethane-1,2-diamine (0.21 mL, 1.4 mmol) was mixed with ethanol (1 mL). The mixture was cooled to 0 °C and concentrated hydroiodic acid (0.96 mL, 57%, 7.29 mmol) was added slowly. After stirring overnight, acetonitrile (5 mL) and diethyl ether (1 mL) were added, and the mixture was left in the fridge overnight. The white crystals were filtered and washed with cold diethyl ether. After drying in a vacuum oven overnight at room temperature, 103 mg (11%) of white crystals were obtained. 1H NMR (400 MHz, D2O) δ 3.16 (t, 6H, J = 6.67 Hz), 2.90 (t, 6H, J = 6.66 Hz).

N 1,N 1-Bis­(3-ammoniopropyl)­propane-1,3-diaminium Iodide (BAP-PDAI4)

N 1,N 1-Bis­(3-aminopropyl)­propane-1,3-diamine (0.53 mL, 2.66 mmol) was mixed with ethanol (2 mL). The mixture was cooled to 0 °C and concentrated hydroiodic acid (1.87 mL, 57%, 14.16 mmol) was added slowly. After stirring overnight, acetonitrile (5 mL) and diethyl ether (1 mL) were added, and the mixture was left in the fridge overnight. The yellow powder was filtered and washed with cold diethyl ether. After drying in a vacuum oven overnight at room temperature, 223 mg (12%) of yellow powder was obtained. 1H NMR (400 MHz, DMSO-d 6) δ 9.44 (s, 1H), 7.75 (s, 9H), 3.19 (br, 6H), 2.97–2.87 (m, 6H), 1.97–1.86 (m, 6H).

Solution Preparation

The perovskite precursor solution with a nominal composition of Cs0.1FA0.6MA0.3Pb0.5Sn0.5I3 was made by dissolving 0.9 M PbI2, 0.9 M SnI2, 0.18 M CsI, 1.08 M FAI, and 0.54 M MAI in DMF:DMSO (3:1, v/v). Additionally, 0.036 M GlyHCl, 0.045 M SnF2 and 0.036 M GuaSCN were added to the precursor solution. The solutions were stirred at 45 °C for 40 min after which they were allowed to cool to room temperature and filtered using a 0.22 μm PTFE syringe filter. All solutions were prepared in a nitrogen environment. The ammonium iodide salt interface passivators were first dissolved in IPA and stirred for approximately 2 h at 70 °C. Just before use, CB was added to reach a 2:1 IPA:CB volume ratio, and the solutions were stirred for another 10 min at 70 °C. The final concentrations were 0.10 mg mL–1 for BAP-PDAI4, 0.15 mg mL–1 for AP-PDAI3 and 0.25 mg mL–1 for EDAI2, PDAI2, BDAI2, HDAI2, MEDAI2, DMEDAI2, AE-EDAI3, and BAE-EDAI4. The solutions were allowed to cool to room temperature before being filtered using a 0.22 μm PTFE syringe filter.

Film Fabrication

The perovskite films were spin-coated on UV-ozone treated glass substrates using a volume of 200 μL precursor solution per sample. The spin-coating procedure consisted of a two-step acceleration. First, using an acceleration of 100 rpm s–1, the spin rate was increased to 1000 rpm at which the sample spun 10 s. Then, with an acceleration of 750 rpm s–1 the spin rate was increased to 4000 rpm at which the sample spun for 40 s. At 51 s after the start of the spinning, 400 μL of CB antisolvent was deposited on the film. The film was annealed at 65 °C for 10 min followed by 10 min at 100 °C. For interface passivation, 120 μL of the solution was dynamically spin-coated at a speed of 4000 rpm for 30 s. The layers were annealed for 5 min at 100 °C.

Device Fabrication

Patterned-ITO glass substrates were cleaned via sonication in acetone, scrubbing with a soapy water solution, sonication in the solution, flushed with demi-water, sonication in 2-propanol (IPA), and treated with UV-ozone for 30 min directly before use. The aqueous PEDOT:PSS dispersion was filtered using a 0.45 μm PVDF filter and 150 μL was spin-coated using a two-step acceleration procedure in air in which the substrate was first accelerated with 20,000 rpm s–1 to 500 rpm for 10 s followed by an acceleration of 20 000 rpm s–1 to 4000 rpm for 30 s. The films were annealed for 20 min at 140 °C before transferring them to an N2-filled glovebox where they had a second round of annealing of 30 min at 140 °C. After cooling to room temperature, the perovskite film and interface passivator were spin-coated in the glovebox. For the ETL, 20 nm C60 and 8 nm BCP were thermally evaporated with a rate of 0.2 Å s–1 under high vacuum (2–6 × 10–7 mbar). For the top electrode, Ag was thermally evaporated using an initial rate of 1 Å s–1 increasing to 3 Å s–1 to reach the target thickness of 100 nm. The overlap between the ITO and Ag contact yielded devices with an area of 0.9 or 0.16 cm2.

Film Characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained with a FEI Quanta 3D FEG microscope using an acceleration voltage of 5 keV electron beam and a secondary electron detector. A thin layer of gold was sputtered on the samples before imaging. The fraction of the surface covered by crystallites was determined using a Python image analysis on the SEM images. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was recorded using a Bruker 2D phaser in the range of 10°–40° with a step size of 0.05° and collection time of 0.5 s. Measurements in the range of 3°–10° were performed using a step size of 0.01° and a collection time of 0.2 s. For both ranges, a 0.6 mm slit and 0.5 mm antiscatter screen were employed. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed using a Thermo-Scientific K-Alpha with a 180° double-focusing hemispherical analyzer and a 128-channel detector. Monochromatic Al Kα (1486.6 eV) radiation was used, and the X-ray spot size was 400 μm. A survey spectrum was first measured for 12 scans (dwell time of 20 ms) with a pass energy of 200 eV. High-resolution scans (30 times, dwell time of 50 ms) of each element were conducted with a pass energy of 50 eV. The absolute photoluminescence (APL) was obtained by illuminating the sample with a 455 nm Thorlabs M455F3 fiber-coupled LED. The light passes through an in-fiber filter holder containing an Edmund Optics 550 nm short-pass filter before entering an integrating sphere (Avantes AvaSphere-30-REFL). The PL emission from the sample is collected through an optical fiber mounted to the side of the sphere and is passed through an in-fiber filter holder with an Edmund Optics 550 nm long-pass filter before it is collected by a spectrometer (Avantes AvaSpec-HSC1024X58TEC-EVO). The setup is calibrated using an Avantes halogen lamp with known spectral irradiance. Spectral photon fluxes ϕPL were obtained after a Jacobian transformation. Using the nonlinear least-squares fit method in MATLAB, The QFLS (ΔE F) was determined from the ϕPL. The relation between ΔE F and photon flux is defined as follows: ϕPL(E)=14π2ℏ3c2a(E)E2exp(E−ΔEFkBT)−1 where ℏ is the reduced Planck constant, c is the speed of light through vacuum, a(E) is the photon energy-dependent absorptivity, E is the photon energy, k B is the Boltzmann constant, and T is the temperature. The a(E) is assumed to be equal to 1 for photon energies sufficiently larger than the optical bandgap (E g). Each film was measured on three spots on the same film and on multiple films, the ΔE F values were averaged, and the standard deviation was determined.

Photovoltaic Performance Characterization

For the J–V measurements, a tungsten-halogen lamp was used with a UV filter (Schott GG385) and a daylight filter (Hoya LB120) to simulate the AM1.5G solar spectrum (100 mW cm–2). A shadow mask with 0.0676 and 0.1296 cm2 aperture sizes was used to define the illuminated active areas. J–V characteristics of the solar cells were measured with a Keithley 2400 SMU by sweeping the applied voltage from +1.0 to −0.5 V for a reverse scan or from −0.5 to +1.0 V for a forward scan in steps of 0.01 V at a scan rate of 0.25 V s–1. For the EQE measurement, the devices were contacted in a nitrogen-filled container. The light of a 50 W tungsten halogen lamp was chopped at 158 Hz before passing into a monochromator (Oriel, Cornerstone 130). A reference silicon detector was used to calibrate the current from the cell which was fed into a current preamplifier (Stanford Research, SR 570). The resulting voltage was measured using a lock-in amplifier (Stanford Research, SR 830). A green (Thorlabs, M530L3) LED was used as a light bias to generate approximately 1-Sun equivalent illumination intensity. By integrating the EQE with the AM1.5G spectrum, the J SC,EQE was obtained. We consider J SC,EQE the more accurate estimate. The J SC obtained from the J–V measurements is about 5% less than J SC,EQE, due to a known mismatch between the irradiance of the light source and the AM1.5G spectrum. Transient photovoltage (TPV) measurements were performed using a Continuum Minilite-II 10 Nd:YAG laser with a 355 nm wavelength output and a pulse rate of 15 Hz. The laser beam had an excitation fluence of around 955 nJ cm–2 and formed a circular spot diameter of 2 mm on the sample surface. The device was kept in a nitrogen-filled container with electrical contacts and 1-Sun equivalent light bias was applied using a 530 nm Thorlabs M530L3-C1 LED driven by a Thorlabs UpSeries LED driver. The change in photovoltage, induced by the laser pulse, was traced with a Tektronix TDS5052B 500 MHz digital phosphor oscilloscope. Open-circuit conditions were set by using a 1 MΩ load resistor on the oscilloscope. The obtained voltage deflection was fitted using a biexponential fit. ΔVext(t)=ΔVext,0[exp(−tτdecay)−exp(−tτrise)] Maximum power point tracking was performed on 4 nominally identical solar cells that were kept at 25 °C under a stream of N2 and illuminated at 1-Sun equivalent intensity with white-light LEDs (Automatic Research GmbH).
