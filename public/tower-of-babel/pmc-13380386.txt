Diabetes care through video consultations: Insights from people using insulin pump therapy and healthcare professionals—A longitudinal qualitative study
Authors: Anders Nikolai Ørsted Schultz, Leah Sejrup Christensen, Eithne Hayes Bauer, Kristian Kidholm, Georg Bollig, Frans Brandt, Karin Brochstedt Dieperink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo explore expectations and experiences with video consultations of people with type 1 diabetes using insulin pump therapy and healthcare professionals (HCPs).MethodsAn interpretive description approach was used in a longitudinal design. Semi‐structured interviews were conducted with people with diabetes and HCPs at two time points (T1 and T2). T1 interviews explored expectations prior to a randomised controlled trial. T2 interviews explored experiences with intervention group participants and HCPs. A reflexive thematic data analysis was employed.ResultsIn total, 33 interviews were conducted. Two themes for people with diabetes at T1 were: ‘Is video inferior?—The fear of missing something’ and ‘Allows me to stay in my everyday life’. At follow‐up interviews (T2): ‘It is fine for smaller issues—but not for larger issues’ and ‘Are video consultations worth it?’. HCPs' themes at T1 were: ‘Can video consultations increase empowerment?’ and ‘What can be done?—Something in‐between a phone and an in‐person consultation’. At follow‐up interviews (T2): ‘If carried out right, a lot can be done’ and ‘Obstacles for moving forward’.ConclusionsVideo consultations can potentially be a less disruptive form of consultation, allowing people with diabetes to stay in their everyday lives, especially for minor issues or minor treatment adjustments. Challenges, in terms of pre‐visit preparation, might outweigh the benefits. Adjustment of clinical work is required to address people with diabetes' preferences for video consultations, guidance on preparation and requirements for participants.

INTRODUCTION

Insulin is mandatory in the treatment of type 1 diabetes. One way of administering insulin is through insulin pumps, which are used by more than half of all people with type 1 diabetes. 1 Insulin pump therapy can improve blood glucose and reduce the risk of acute and long‐term complications of diabetes. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 However, insulin pump therapy is more complex than regular injection therapy, and guiding people with insulin pumps requires healthcare professionals (HCPs) with specialised training. 6 Larger treatment centres have lower complication rates and better glycaemic management. 6 However, centralising knowledge in larger centres can lead to longer transportation times for some people. One solution to overcome this challenge might be the use of remote consultations, such as video consultations.

Switching from in‐person to video consultations may seem straightforward, but several factors affect the transition. When implementing a new technology, different aspects must be considered to assess the value of the digital service prior to implementation. 7 One of these is the patient perspective. 7 Likewise, perceptions of the HCPs are equally important to deliver a successful telehealth service. 8 Thus, because of its multiple interacting components, such as the technology and users, which might be influenced by different contextual factors (culture, organisation and clinical setting), video consultations can be described as complex interventions. 9 Qualitative data can provide a broad picture and add to the understanding of experiences in a more nuanced way, leading to deeper insights into how complex interventions work, under which circumstances and for whom. 10 Longitudinal qualitative data can also reveal changes over time, 11 as initial expectations may differ from actual experiences.

Few qualitative studies have been conducted during the COVID‐19 pandemic exploring the perceptions of telehealth among people with diabetes or HCPs. 12 , 13 , 14 Studies regarding parents' perceptions of video consultations for children with diabetes found that saved transportation time, location, flexibility and increased empowerment were main advantages largely due to the increased number of contacts. 15 , 16 Barriers included poor internet connection and technical difficulties. However, studies on the perception of video consultations for diabetes management with insulin pump therapy among adults and HCPs and in a post‐pandemic context are lacking. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the expectations and experiences of adult people with type 1 diabetes using insulin pump therapy and their HCPs regarding video consultations.

METHODS

Design and epistemology

A longitudinal cross‐sectional explorative qualitative study was performed. 11 Interpretive description was chosen as the epistemological starting point, building on contextualism. 17 Interpretive description is a research approach designed for applied health sciences, where the researcher's pre‐existing knowledge is acknowledged as a source of applied practice and insight. 17 The clinical background of the researcher provides practical experience, which can add to the empirical and theoretical perspectives. Data analysis was guided by reflexive thematic analysis, 18 a theoretically flexible method allowing for a contextual lens. The study focused on generating broad themes by analysing participants' expectations and experiences within the context of diabetes care using video consultations. These themes evolved over the course of the study as both the participants and the researchers gained insights at two different time points.

Setting

In June 2021, a randomised controlled trial (RCT) evaluating the effectiveness of video consultations versus in‐person consultations for people with type 1 diabetes, using insulin pump therapy, began at the Hospital of Southern Denmark. 19 The results of the RCT are published elsewhere. 20 Although video consultations were used at the hospital before the trial, most HCPs had little to no experience with them, and people with diabetes at the outpatient clinic had not regularly been offered video consultations, relying mainly on in‐person or, when possible, telephone consultations.

Participants and data collection

Semi‐structured interviews were conducted with people with type 1 diabetes, using insulin pump therapy, as well as with the HCPs involved in their care. Data were collected at two different time points (T1 and T2) (Figure 1). Interview guides can be found in Appendix A. The interview guides were developed by the research team based on the study aims regarding telehealth and the experiences of people with diabetes. They were checked after the first couple of interviews, refined with no major changes, and all interviews were included in the analysis. We invited people to participate regardless of whether they were enrolled in or had declined to participate in the RCT study. Including both groups enabled exploration of experiences and reasons for non‐uptake of the video consultation model. Trial participants contributed insights into the practical use, benefits, and challenges of video consultations in everyday diabetes care, whereas people who declined provided perspectives on concerns, preferences and contextual factors that may limit engagement with such services. Sampling of both groups is consistent with qualitative work embedded in trials that seek to understand not only how an intervention works for users, but also why some eligible potential participants choose not to participate, which is important for real‐world implementation and equity in access. The inclusion criteria were adults (≥18) with type 1 diabetes who used insulin pump therapy for at least 6 months. Exclusion criteria were people unable to read and/or speak Danish.

Data collection flow of a study on video consultations in diabetes care: T1 = first round of interviews; T2 = second round of interviews.

Experiences at T2 were captured in end‐of‐study interviews with people from the intervention arm of the RCT after completing their 1‐year intervention period, during which they used video consultations as an alternative to standard in‐person consultations. Participants could be the same people as those interviewed at T1, but this was not a requirement. We employed a purposeful sampling recruitment strategy at T1, aiming for maximum variation, 21 keeping in mind the principle of information power. 22 Participant recruitment proved more challenging at T2, which might have been due to the considerably reduced number of eligible individuals, limited exclusively to those who had completed the 1‐year RCT intervention, compared to the broader outpatient clinic population available at T1. People with diabetes were notified by letter before their outpatient clinic appointment and invited to participate after their consultation.

All HCPs were interviewed before initiating the RCT (T1), and follow‐up interviews were conducted approximately two and a half years later (T2). We aimed to include all HCPs involved in the treatment of people using insulin pump therapy. At T2, no medical doctors had conducted video consultations, as they primarily see people with diabetes for annual check‐ups, which require in‐person consultations. Therefore, interviews were conducted with nurses and dietitians. All interviews were conducted at the outpatient clinic during working hours.

ANØS, who is a medical doctor and PhD student, conducted all T1 interviews and T2 interviews with HCPs. T2 interviews with RCT participants were performed by the LSC, a registered nurse with knowledge of diabetes care. The interviewers were guided by the GB and KBD, both of whom have extensive experience with qualitative research. None of the interviewers knew the people with type 1 diabetes prior to the interviews, but the ANØS had a professional relationship with the HCPs. This familiarity likely facilitated access and trust but could potentially have encouraged more positive or polite responses. This was mitigated through guidance from independent qualitative researchers (EHB and KBD) and by attention to critical or negative comments in the data, which were present and included in the analysis. All interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim. Coding was performed using NVivo version 14.

Analysis

Datasets were analysed separately: one analysis with the expectations and experiences of people with diabetes and another for HCPs. A recurrent cross‐sectional method was used. 11 Hence, data were analysed individually at each time point (T1 and T2), followed by a second analysis focusing on similarities and differences between the two time points. 23

Inductive reflexive thematic analysis, as described by Braun and Clarke, was employed, 18 looking for semantic and latent codes. The process included the following phases: (1–2) Familiarisation with the data and initial coding. (3–4) Searching for initial themes and developing and revising themes. (5–6) Defining, refining and naming themes and writing up the results. Phases 1–4 were performed by the ANØS and phases 4–6 by ANØS, in close collaboration and mutual discussion with the EHB. Phases 3–6 were discussed with KBD.

After completing the thematic analysis at each time point, the second analyses were conducted exploring changes over time. This part of the analysis was guided by framing questions, descriptive questions and analytic and interpretive questions, as described by Saldana. 23

Ethics

Written and verbal information were provided to all potential participants before interviews. Written consent was obtained from all participants. Participants were informed about their right to end their participation without any consequences. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. 24 The study received permission from the Regional Ethical Committee of Southern Denmark (S‐20200039G), the processing of personal data is approved by the Region of Southern Denmark and listed in the internal record (20/24459) cf. Art 20 of The European Union General Data Protection Regulation. Results are reported following the reporting guidelines for qualitative research by Braun & Clark. 25

Is video inferior?—The fear of missing something (T1)

Many participants expressed concerns that something might be overlooked in a video consultation compared to an in‐person consultation. They stated the importance of forming a connection during the consultation and believed that it required in‐person contact, as video consultations could not offer the same level of connection. Participants also expressed concerns that video consultations might be rushed. They expressed fear of being caught off‐guard in their daily life, not being mentally prepared, or missing out on unplanned activities, like an improvised consultation with a dietician. There seemed to be a general fear among the majority that video consultations were inferior to in‐person consultations.

Allows me to stay in my everyday life (T1)

The majority of people with type 1 diabetes describe saving time on transportation and how simply being able to have a consultation at work could provide more flexibility than usual. When necessary, this could allow for more frequent consultations. Not having to think about practical issues such as planning for transportation would also make participating in consultations easier. Furthermore, some participants found hospital consultations stressful because they felt uncomfortable when entering the hospital and believed that video consultations could alleviate this stress. These aspects could potentially allow people with diabetes to remain in their everyday lives, as video consultations were found to be less disruptive than in‐person consultations.

It is fine for smaller issues—But not for larger issues (T2)

In general, all participants found it easy to talk and form a connection with their HCPs on video. The participants described how knowing their HCP prior to the video consultation made it easy to adapt from in‐person consultations to video. The fear of inferiority, found in the T1 interviews, was not found in the T2 interviews; the participants showed a more nuanced view. Video consultations were seen as beneficial to use for planned follow‐ups regarding a specific topic, that is, regulating insulin regimen. The visual confirmation on video was reassuring and helped with the adjustment of insulin pump settings. However, in‐person consultations were preferred for more sensitive or complex problems, such as changing treatment strategy or personal problems. All participants agreed that although video consultations were generally positive, in‐person consultations were still necessary from time to time, depending on what needed to be discussed during the consultation.

Are video consultations worth it? (T2)

People with type 1 diabetes agreed broadly that there were clear benefits in using video consultations, in terms of transportation and flexibility, as anticipated after interviews at T1. However, they also expressed that the challenges might outweigh the benefits. The challenges mainly concerned preparation for the consultation. For some, having to upload their continuous blood glucose data as well as finding an appropriate location to carry out the video consultation, which was not always easy when working, proved challenging. Moreover, they expressed that although the saved transportation time was positive, it might not have been such a big issue after all, since the consultations were relatively infrequent (typically, 1–4 times a year). Some people found preparing for a video consultation was simply too much hassle compared to the perceived benefits.

Themes: HCPs

The two themes generated at T1 investigating expectations of video consultations were: ‘Can video consultations increase empowerment?’ and ‘What can be done?—Something in‐between a phone and an in‐person consultation’.

The two themes generated at T2 investigating experiences with video consultations were: ‘If carried out right a lot can be done’ and ‘Obstacles for moving forward’.

Can video consultations increase empowerment? (T1)

HCPs expressed a hope that video consultations would help empower people with diabetes. HCPs believed that some would feel safer during the video consultation, allowing them to gain more control over the conversation and speak up for themselves when needed. They also believed that video consultations would be more flexible, saving transportation time, thus allowing people with diabetes to have consultations more frequently when necessary. HCPs also suggested that switching to video might help empower people with diabetes in the time between consultations. Because people with diabetes using insulin pump therapy are encouraged to adjust their pumps themselves during the actual video consultations, the HCPs hoped they might gain a better understanding of their device, leading to more people adjusting their pumps by themselves between consultations.

What can be done?—Something in between a phone and an in‐person consultation (T1)

HCPs tried to grasp how they could make best use of video consultations, what could be done and how it might differ from what they knew from in‐person consultations or telephone consultations. The possibility of screen sharing, being able to share the blood glucose curves from the sensor, was considered a huge advantage compared to telephone consultations. Being able to see the insulin pump through the video when guiding adjustments was also considered an improvement compared to telephone consultations.

Limitations included a lack of physical examinations and a lack of equipment, that is, not being able to feel a person's pulse, or whether they are warm or cold. HCPs discussed how video consultations may affect their ability to build rapport. Knowing the person beforehand was seen as something that could facilitate this process. Video consultations were described as something in between an in‐person consultation and a telephone consultation, where the contact and relationship would be better than on the telephone, but not as good as having an in‐person consultation. Furthermore, the technical part of conducting video consultations was an issue of concern.

If carried out right, a lot can be done (T2)

At the follow‐up interviews, it was clear that the HCPs had gained a lot of experience, allowing them to describe and reflect upon this new form of consultation in more detail than in the T1 interviews. The HCPs felt that they had adjusted quickly to this new form of consultation, both in terms of technical issues and in relation to building rapport. The technical issues had not completely disappeared, but they had diminished. In terms of relationship building and contact, the HCPs noted that they felt more comfortable being ‘on the screen’, allowing them to focus their attention on the consultation itself.

Through experience, the HCPs adapted their practices, finding new and more specific ways of carrying out the video consultations, such as addressing confidentiality and privacy by asking who might be present. HCPs also gained a better understanding of the technical requirements, such as the importance of screen size when sharing blood glucose curves and ways to accommodate people who used their phones for video consultations. They started to rethink what and how video consultations could be used, expanding their views on video from simply being something in between a telephone or an in‐person consultation to something different that allowed for new ways of delivering care.

Obstacles for moving forward (T2)

HCPs acknowledged that video consultations are not for everyone, but also expressed that they were sometimes surprised by how difficult it actually was for some people to prepare for the video consultations. The main problems HCPs encountered were people not uploading sensor data or not having an appropriate location for the video consultation. Moreover, some people simply did not have the required hardware to conduct a video consultation.

HCPs had an expectation at T1 that by using video consultations, people with diabetes would learn how to adjust their pumps themselves, and maybe also to the extent that they would be able to do so in the time elapsing between consultations. At T2, these expectations were met to some extent; HCPs expressed that the majority of the people they cared for had become well acquainted with their insulin pumps, allowing them to adjust them by themselves during video consultations, but the video consultations did not actually lead to independent adjustments by people with diabetes between video consultations. HCPs speculated that this was not due to technical issues but because interpreting the blood glucose curves from the continuous glucose monitor was difficult, meaning that the people with diabetes were unsure about what adjustments to make by themselves.

Another obstacle was that HCPs had yet to establish a way to discuss consultation options, often forgetting to ask individuals about their preferred modality for next time.

Longitudinal analysis: From expectations to experiences

Looking across the themes generated for both people with diabetes and HCPs, there was a shift in how video consultations were perceived between the T1 and T2 interviews. The initial fear of inferiority compared to in‐person consultations shifted towards considering video consultations as practical, especially for smaller issues, as video consultations allowed people to remain in their everyday lives. In the beginning, the HCPs talked about possible empowerment, while still trying to grasp the concept of a video consultation, placing it somewhere between a telephone and an in‐person consultation. As they gained experience, they moved from having initial concerns to discovering that, even though there might be limitations, a lot could be done over video; however, they noticed the challenges in workflow and guidance on preparation.

DISCUSSION

Our study explored perceptions regarding video consultations of people with type 1 diabetes, using insulin pump therapy, and the HCPs involved in their treatment. While previous studies of telehealth have similar findings, this study is, to our knowledge, the first qualitative study focusing on video consultations for people using insulin pump therapy exclusively. A feasibility study of an online kidney school reported that technical issues did not fully disappear, although they diminished over time, allowing for more focus on content and opening the HCPs' understanding in terms of what can be done. 26 Similarly, a study from the United Kingdom examining communication strategies through video consultations for people with diabetes and other diseases found that adjustments were needed in the consultations, but that the process of making adjustments occurred quickly, mirroring our findings in shifts in HCPs' perceptions from T1 to T2. 27

Two qualitative studies from the United States examined people with diabetes and HCPs' experiences of telehealth (including both video and telephone) consultations during the COVID‐19 pandemic. 13 , 14 They found that telehealth was more convenient and flexible than in‐person consultations and found that people with diabetes valued the convenience of being able to remain in their home, at work, or another location. 13 , 14 The studies also highlighted that video consultations worked well for most people with diabetes but noted the need for in‐person visits for physical examinations and new referrals. 13 , 14 In common with our findings, video consultations appear most suitable for minor follow‐ups or specific issues but may be used in situations that are more complex. The full capacity of what can be addressed remotely has yet to be identified.

In our study, some people with diabetes questioned whether the benefits of video consultations outweighed the challenges they faced, such as finding an appropriate location for a video consultation and issues relating to uploading data. These challenges were echoed by the HCPs, who also noted that some people were more prepared than others. These challenges, especially data uploading, mental preparedness and participating from inappropriate locations, were found in the afore‐mentioned studies. 13 , 14 Hence, more focus should be given to guidance and requirements on what to expect from and how to prepare for video consultations. Furthermore, HCPs in our study expressed the need for a cultural shift in the way consultations were held to incorporate discussions on the different possibilities for consultation modalities for the next consultation. This reflects that daily work and organisation of outpatient clinics is rooted in the view of a consultation as an in‐person consultation, possibly hindering more widespread uptake of video consultations. Organisational issues, such as scheduling and possible delays, were also stated in the afore‐mentioned studies. 13 , 14 A Danish study regarding video consultation training and implementation found that adjusting workflows to HCPs' skills, knowledge and judgment improved the likelihood of successful implementation. 28 More focus should be placed on implementing clinical strategies to overcome organisational challenges for implementation, such as discussing consultation modality for follow‐up consultations and requirements for video consultations in diabetes care. Guidance for requirements could be inspired by the work of Forde et al., 29 in which they provide a flowchart, highlighting similar requirements as mentioned in this study, namely data uploading, access to video platforms, devices suitable for screen‐sharing sensor data and appropriate locations for video consultations. Giving more consideration to requirements for participation in video consultations may help identify suitable candidates and those for whom the challenges outweigh the benefits. This may help to achieve the potential for increasing the uptake of video consultations in diabetes care. A Danish survey found that approximately 40% of people with diabetes preferred virtual consultations (telephone or video) to in‐person and HCPs estimated that a quarter of the in‐person consultations could be converted. 30 Thus, a more systematic approach to addressing these challenges could allow many more people to benefit from video consultations.

Our study has several strengths and limitations. The longitudinal design of the study allows for the exploration of how the perception of video consultations changes over time. 11 The inclusion of both people with diabetes and HCPs allows for multiple perspectives and an in‐depth understanding of the use of video consultations. The use of interpretative description, a research approach developed specifically for the healthcare sciences, allows for actionable insights as guidance for clinical implications. Limitations include being a single‐centre study, which restricts the transferability of the findings. Additionally, the group of people with diabetes interviewed at T2 was relatively small and was less experienced with video consultations than the HCPs, resulting in lower information power within this group. Furthermore, no medical doctors participated in the T2 interviews. On the other hand, many of our findings are similar to the findings of other studies; thus, one might expect the findings to be transferable to similar settings.

Our rigorous use of reflexive thematic analysis has honed our understanding of what constitutes a video consultation, which evolved throughout the study, through the interview process and working with the generated data. Moving from considering video consultations as something in between telephone and in‐person towards recognising video consultations as a distinct method with unique possibilities and limitations for delivering care.

In conclusion, video consultations have the potential to be used as a less disruptive form of consultation, allowing people to stay in their everyday lives. However, challenges, in terms of preparation, might outweigh the benefits. Although the HCPs had initial concerns, they adjusted relatively quickly, allowing them to provide care in another manner. Guidance on requirements and on preparation for video consultations and adjustments in clinical workflows needs to be addressed.

Clinical implications

People with diabetes should be able to choose a consultation modality for minor issues or minor treatment adjustments. Clinical strategies for prompting conversations regarding consultation modality for follow‐up consultations and strategies to overcome organisational challenges for implementation need to be considered. Particular focus should be given to a systematic approach to requirements for participation to help people with access to video platforms and preparation for video consultations, especially for data uploading, mental preparedness, finding an appropriate location and selecting devices suitable for screen sharing sensor data.
