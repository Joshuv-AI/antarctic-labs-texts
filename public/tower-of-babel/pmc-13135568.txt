Real-world use of pharmacologic therapy and chronic blood transfusion in sickle cell disease, 2014–2021
Authors: Kangho Suh, Terri V Newman, Jingye Yang, Aaron Haubner, Melissa Anderson, Darrell J Triulzi, Enrico M Novelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSickle cell disease (SCD) care includes pharmacologic disease-modifying therapies (pDMTs) and chronic blood transfusion therapy (CBT). Using Optum Labs Data Warehouse (2014–2021), we identified individuals with SCD and classified yearly treatment as pDMT only, CBT only, combined pDMT + CBT, or no disease-modifying therapy. Among 4,100 patients, 26% used pDMT only, 3% received CBT only, 1% received both, and 70% received no DMT in a given year. pDMT uptake rose steadily while CBT remained low (3%–4%). In multinomial models (reference: no DMT), older age was associated with lower odds of pDMT and CBT, while prior vaso-occlusive crises and severe organ complications were associated with higher odds across categories. Men had higher odds of pDMT and combined therapy. These findings suggest treatment intensification in routine practice follows accumulating morbidity rather than preceding it, and that substantial implementation gaps persist, particularly among older adults and for CBT, which depends on specialized capacity. Expanding CBT capacity and improving pDMT uptake may narrow gaps and better align real-world care with guideline intent.Keywords: Sickle cell disease, Transfusion, Disease modifying treatment, Hydroxyurea

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is an inherited red blood cell disorder affecting approximately 100,000 individuals in the US [1]. It is characterized by recurrent acute complications and progressive end-organ damage driven by hemolysis, vaso-occlusion, and inflammation [2]. Potentially curative options such as hematopoietic stem cell transplantation and gene therapies remain constrained by donor availability, cost, and uncertain long-term durability, leaving most patients reliant on disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) to mitigate morbidity and improve survival [3, 4].

Within DMTs, two broad strategies are used: (1) pharmaceutical DMTs (pDMTs), hydroxyurea, L-glutamine, and crizanlizumab and (2) transfusion-based approaches including chronic blood transfusion therapy (CBT) via simple transfusion, manual exchange, or automated red-blood cell exchange (RBCx) [5–7]. Despite multiple approvals, real-world uptake of pDMTs has remained modest with up to 75% of eligible patients not on treatment, and the 2024 market withdrawal of voxelotor has further narrowed pharmacologic options [6].

Contemporary guidelines emphasize access to RBCx because of advantages such as more precise HbS% targeting and reduced iron accumulation relative to simple transfusions [5]. Yet transfusion is underutilized nationally, including outpatient simple transfusion and RBCx [8]. Barriers may include limited apheresis capacity, blood supply constraints, and care-delivery complexity [5, 7]. Pediatric surveillance work has documented increased hydroxyurea use while chronic transfusion remained relatively low [7].

Despite this context, real-world patterns of CBT utilization, and how they compare with, or complement pDMT use, remain incompletely characterized particularly among adults. Clarifying temporal trends and patient characteristics associated with CBT, pDMT, or their combined use could inform patient selection and health-system strategies to close utilization gaps. Thus, our objectives were to quantify yearly use of CBT, pDMTs, and combined therapy and identify factors associated with each group.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study to characterize yearly use of CBT and pDMTs and to evaluate factors associated with treatment patterns. We conducted this study using the Optum Labs Data Warehouse (OLDW). The OLDW contains de-identified retrospective administrative claims data, including medical and pharmacy claims and eligibility information, spanning January 1, 2014, through September 30, 2021. We identified individuals with SCD using a claims-based algorithm of ≥ 1 inpatient claim or ≥ 2 outpatient claims at least 30 days apart with a SCD diagnosis [9]. The index date was the earliest claim with a SCD diagnosis after meeting continuous enrollment criteria (≥ 6 months pre-index and 12 months post-index) to gather baseline characteristics and treatment use.

Based on their treatments received, patients were categorized into mutually exclusive groups of CBT, pDMT, pDMT and CBT combined (i.e. pDMT + CBT), and no treatment. CBT was defined as ≥ 6 transfusion or exchange-transfusion encounters within 12 months post-index, identified using procedural and diagnosis codes from a prior study, and acute blood transfusion (ABT) was defined as < 6 transfusions [7]. To reflect typical delivery of chronic transfusion, we restricted CBT to outpatient encounters. We defined pDMT use as ≥ 1 pharmacy fill for hydroxyurea, crizanlizumab, L-glutamine, or voxelotor.

We summarized baseline characteristics and compared groups using χ² or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables and t-tests for continuous variables. To assess factors associated with treatment group membership, we fit a multinomial logistic regression model including demographic, clinical, and healthcare utilization covariates. Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute). The study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board with consent exemption for de-identified data.

Results

We identified 4,100 individuals with SCD who met inclusion criteria. Of these, 26% (n = 1,069) used pDMT only, 3% (n = 119) received CBT alone, 1% (n = 42) received both, and 70% (n = 2,870) received no treatment (Table 1). Regarding disease burden, CBT-only and pDMT + CBT users had higher proportions of severe organ complications, particularly stroke, renal disease, and chronic pulmonary complications, than the pDMT-only and no-treatment groups. The pDMT + CBT group also had consistently higher proportions of acute and chronic systemic SCD complications. Consistent with this burden, baseline healthcare utilization was generally higher for pDMT + CBT. Throughout the study period, pDMT uptake increased steadily, ABT was flat to declining, and CBT rose slightly from 3% in 2014 to 4% in 2020 (Fig. 1).

In the multinomial model (reference: no treatment), older age was associated with lower use of both pDMT and CBT alone (Table 2). Compared with those < 18 years, individuals aged 31–54 and ≥ 55 had 38% (OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.50, 0.78) and 64% (OR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.27, 0.47) lower odds of pDMT-only, respectively. For CBT, there were 56% (OR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.25, 0.76) and 80% (OR = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.10, 0.40) lower odds in those age groups. Men had higher odds of pDMT-only (OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.11, 1.51) and combined pDMT + CBT (OR = 2.15, 95% CI: 1.13, 4.10) versus women. Prior VOCs were associated with higher odds of pDMT-only (OR = 2.81, 95% CI: 2.36, 3.36), CBT-only (OR = 2.67, 95% CI: 1.73, 4.13), and combined therapy (OR = 3.14, 95% CI: 1.48, 6.67). Severe organ complications (e.g., avascular necrosis, stroke, renal disease) were associated with higher CBT use (OR = 2.10, 95% CI: 1.26, 3.50) and combined therapy (OR = 3.12, 95% CI: 1.46, 6.66).

Discussion

We found pDMT use increased from 2014 to 2020, whereas CBT use remained low. Our analysis suggested that real-world selection of disease-modifying strategy was associated with disease severity as patients receiving CBT, alone or with pDMT, had the greatest prevalence of severe organ complications and acute/chronic systemic SCD complications. For CBT, profiles indicative of more severe disease (frequent VOCs, organ complications) aligned with scenarios in which transfusion support is commonly considered in practice, whereas lower uptake among older adults may reflect cumulative comorbidity, competing risks, and pragmatic barriers to initiating or sustaining CBT programs (e.g. venous access, visit intensity) [10, 11]. For pDMTs, the inverse age relationship could also reflect differential tolerability or perceived benefit, and competing priorities in multimorbidity [11, 12].

Our results showed a steady rise in pDMT use against flat lines for CBT and ABT. A likely driver was the study window’s gradual diffusion of newer agents in addition to the availability of hydroxyurea. Before the currently available gene therapies came to market, expert guidance urged clinicians to be more proactive in treatment before accumulating complications could render patients ineligible, an approach that implicitly favors maintaining disease control today to preserve access to tomorrow’s options [13]. This mirrors the “real option value” mechanism whereby expectations about near-term innovation increase the value of initiating or continuing a therapy that stabilizes health now so patients can survive to benefit from the next advancement [14]. Finally, CBT programs require sustained logistics and specialized resources, and reviews emphasize iron burden with simple transfusion versus the feasibility constraints of RBCx, and these factors keep CBT use low outside specialized settings [15, 16].

Our trends of treatment use showed similarities compared to a previous study in a pediatric population [7]. Tang et al. (2023) reported increasing hydroxyurea uptake across pediatric age groups with transfusion exposure that stabilized overall and, within the subset on chronic transfusion, a slight downward trend beginning in 2017 [7]. Our patient cohort showed similar trends but absolute levels differed, likely due to different patient populations in terms of age, geography, and calendar years. These findings suggest that pharmacotherapy has expanded gradually, whereas CBT programs remain constrained by logistical and resource barriers (e.g. travel distance and limited capacity). This interpretation aligns with national claims analyses showing low outpatient transfusion use and limited adoption of outpatient red-cell exchange [8].

At the patient level, two associations further contextualize our observed trends. Men were associated with higher odds of pDMT and combined therapy use. A possible explanation is that men with SCD accrue more end-organ morbidity (acute chest syndrome, cardiovascular and musculoskeletal complications) and undergo transfusion more frequently, and alongside higher cerebrovascular risk, these factors can prompt clinicians to escalate disease-modifying strategies [17, 18]. Second, opioid exposure, a pragmatic proxy for pain burden, was associated with higher odds of all treatment categories, reinforcing that treatment intensification in routine care tends to follow accumulating morbidity rather than precede it [19, 20].

In practice, simple transfusion and RBCx serve different purposes. Simple transfusion increases hemoglobin but also hematocrit and volume, which in turn increases risks such as hyperviscosity and transfusion-associated circulatory overload [21]. By contrast, RBCx removes HbS-containing cells while maintaining or lowering viscosity and volume, enabling tighter HbS% targets (commonly < 30%) and mitigating iron accumulation [22, 23]. Contemporary guidelines suggest automated RCE over simple or manual exchange for patients receiving chronic transfusion support, in part to better control HbS% and iron burden [5].

Beyond clinical considerations, economic and system-level factors likely influence modality choice and persistence. For example, published estimates suggest a typical 2-unit transfusion is approximately $1,231 per episode (2025 USD) when reimbursed by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services [24]. Conversely, pDMT encompasses a wide range of older, low-cost therapies (e.g. hydroxyurea, $880 annually in 2025 USD) and newer, high-cost agents (e.g. crizanlizumab and L-glutamine, around $148,000 and $45,000 annually, respectively, in 2025 USD) [25]. These cost differentials, together with payer coverage policies, prior authorization requirements, and patient out-of-pocket costs may contribute to the observed patterns of use and persistence in real-world care.

This study had several limitations. Our CBT definition (≥ 6 outpatient transfusion/exchange encounters per year) may miss inpatient-anchored chronic programs. Additionally, due to the nature of claims data we were unable to distinguish simple transfusion from RBCx and adjudicate indications (e.g. stroke prevention vs. pain reduction). As with any claims-based studies assessing compliance with drug treatment, we cannot confirm actual ingestion of medication. Also, our 2020–2021 estimates may have been affected by COVID-related supply and care disruptions. In addition, our data cannot directly measure operational barriers to CBT, such as limited availability of antigen-matched RBC units. These constraints, often compounded by alloimmunization and differences in blood group antigen frequencies between recipients and the broader donor pool, may delay transfusions and limit participation in chronic transfusion programs [26, 27]. Given our study design, which required 12 months of continuous enrollment post-index, we did not evaluate mortality during follow-up. Finally, although recently approved curative therapies may shift the treatment paradigm, we were unable to assess their uptake or impact because these approvals occurred after our study period.

In our national cohort of SCD patients that included pediatric and adult patients from 2014 to 2021, pDMT increased modestly while CBT use remained low and concentrated among patients with greater morbidity. Despite these gains, most patients were not on a DMT in a given year and older adults were least likely to receive disease-modifying care, underscoring persistent implementation gaps and resource constraints in routine practice. Future work should target barriers to uptake and persistence across payers and regions and link utilization to patient-centered outcomes to identify who benefits and when to escalate therapy.
