Predictors of treatment adherence in patients with type 2 diabetes: a cross-sectional study in Southern Iran based on Pender’s Health Promotion Model using structural equation modelling
Authors: Nahid Shahabi, Zahra Hosseini, Amin Ghanbarnejad, Teamur Aghamolaei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTreatment adherence in type 2 diabetes (T2D) is an important factor in optimal diabetes control and prevention of mortality. The present study aimed to determine the predictability of Pender’s Health Promotion Model (HPM) constructs in T2D treatment adherence behaviour.DesignThe present cross-sectional and analytical study was conducted from November 2022 to January 2023.SettingThe present study was conducted in Bandar Abbas, a city in Hormozgan Province, in the south of Iran.ParticipantsThe participants included 396 patients with T2D with medical records in the Hormoz Diabetes Clinic. Based on their record number, the participants were selected for inclusion in the study through a random systematic sampling.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe data collection instruments included a demographic questionnaire and a researcher-made questionnaire based on HPM constructs. The questionnaire was valid and reliable, achieving Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.609 to 0.798 across various constructs. The questionnaires were completed face to face. Pearson’s correlation test, path analysis and structural equation modelling were conducted using SPSS V.23, and STATA V.15.Study stageThis study was conducted before intervention (pre-results).ResultsAs the path analysis showed, perceived self-efficacy (β=0.23, p<0.001), treatment adherence experiences (β=0.26, p<0.001), immediate competing demands and preferences (β=−0.15, p<0.001) and commitment to plan of action (β=0.24, p<0.001) could significantly predict the treatment adherence behaviour. The results of indirect path analysis showed that the total effect of perceived benefits (β=0.24, p<0.001), perceived barriers (β=−0.14, p=0.002), perceived self-efficacy (β=0.32, p<0.001) on commitment to plan of action was statistically significant. Through the mediation of commitment to plan of action, they could predict the treatment adherence behaviour.ConclusionsIn light of the present findings, it can be concluded that the proposed model of T2D treatment adherence behaviour has an acceptable fit. Commitment to plan of action, treatment adherence experiences, perceived self-efficacy and immediate competing demands and preferences are the main predictors of T2D treatment adherence behaviour. It is recommended that educational interventions focus on these constructs.Trial registration numberThis study is registered on the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20211228053558N1).Keywords: diabetes mellitus, type 2; health education; medication adherence

Introduction

According to the International Diabetes Federation, 10.5% of the adult population (20–79 years) suffers from diabetes, and almost half are unaware that they have this condition. It is predicted that by 2045, the number of patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D) will reach 783 million. One out of every eight adults lives with diabetes.1 The prevalence of diabetes and the total cases of adult patients with diabetes in Iran are estimated at 9.5% and 5 450 300, respectively.2 T2D accounts for approximately 90% of all diabetes cases. It will increase significantly due to changes in lifestyle, leading to decreased physical activity and increased prevalence of obesity.3 T2D is related to a variety of modifiable risk factors (unhealthy diet, obesity, physical inactivity, smoking and alcohol consumption) and non-modifiable factors (age, genetic factors and demographic features).4 As a complex and chronic disease, T2D is associated with many complications, such as adult blindness, limb amputation and kidney and cardiovascular diseases.5,7 In addition to regular medication, a healthy lifestyle such as healthy eating, physical activity and psychosocial care is important in T2D.8

Treatment of chronic diseases such as diabetes usually involves a long-term treatment process. Although prescribed treatments and drugs are effective in treating chronic diseases, the positive effect is not complete due to poor treatment adherence.9 Treatment adherence can be defined as the extent to which a patient follows the doctor’s prescription for treating a specific disease.10 The existing evidence shows that treatment adherence is low among patients with T2D.11 12 Treatment adherence in patients with T2D has been reported in different countries, for example, 68% in the USA,13 34% in India,14 50% in Japan15 and 29% in Ethiopia.16 In Iran, research has proved the low adherence to diabetes treatment and reported this rate as 17%,17 31%18 and 40%19 in different studies.

In a number of studies, demographic factors such as gender, age, education level, employment, race,20 annual income and medical insurance21 and factors related to clinical diseases such as prior experience of the disease or the family history of T2D, the number of prescribed drugs, bleeding and high blood pressure22 have been reported as effective factors in adherence to the treatment of this disease. Since treatment adherence is a complex behaviour, health behaviour theories and models can help with a more accurate and complete exploration of the underlying factors.23 In the present study, Pender’s Health Promotion Model (HPM) was used to explore the predictors of adherence to T2D treatment. Researchers have used HPM to identify behavioural determinants of treatment adherence for diseases such as hypertension,24 breast cancer25 and heart disease.26

Theoretical framework

Pender’s HPM tries to explain healthy behaviour with a focus on the role of experience in shaping behaviour. This model enables health experts to explore the complex bio-psycho-social process that motivates people to adopt a health-promoting behaviour (T2D treatment adherence).27 HPM consists of three categories of factors: individual characteristics and experiences, behaviour-specific cognition and affect and behaviour outcome. Behaviour-specific cognition and affect have modifiable constructs, including the perceived benefits of behaviour, perceived barriers to behaviour, perceived self-efficacy, activity-related affect, interpersonal influences and situational influences. These factors can influence the performance of (or lack thereof) health-promoting behaviours, which compete with immediate competing demands and preferences. It is necessary to explore these constructs to assess their impact on behavioural outcomes.28

Related literature

The prevalence of T2D is related to the lifestyle.3 The prevalence of diabetes is increasing in Iran,29 and unhealthy lifestyles such as inactivity and unhealthy diets are growing in southern Iran,30 which can affect T2D treatment adherence in this region. Most previous studies explored the predictors of medication adherence,31 32 or only explored nutritional behaviours or physical activity of patients with T2D.33 34 Most studies lack a theoretical framework.35 36 Notably, none has used the HPM.37 38 In the present research, the predictors of diabetes treatment adherence were explored in a larger sample size than in previous studies based on Pender’s HPM. The scope and concepts of HPM related to individual, cognitive, affective and situational factors provided a more comprehensive understanding of factors affecting treatment adherence in these patients. Therefore, the present study aimed to explore the predictive power of each construct of Pender’s HPM in T2D treatment adherence.

Materials and methods

Design of study and participants

The present cross-sectional and analytical study was conducted from November 2022 to January 2023. It was set in Hormoz Clinic of Shahid Mohammadi hospital in Bandar Abbas, the capital city of Hormozgan Province, in the south of Iran.

Bandar Abbas is located at the 27.19 latitude and 56.28 longitude, and height of 9 m above sea level. This city has a population of 352 173, making it the biggest city in Hormozgan.

The research population included patients with T2D with health records in the Hormoz Diabetes Clinic.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design or conduct of this research.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were: willingness to participate in research, having a health record in Hormoz Diabetes Clinic as a patient with T2D, at least 1 year of diabetes treatment, residence in Bandar Abbas and consent to participate in the study. The exclusion criterion was incomplete questionnaires.

Sample of study

According to a similar study, the SD of the treatment adherence score was 33.1.39 With a 95% CI, the test power of 80% and effect size of 5d, the sample size was estimated at 344.

To avoid the possible attrition, 20% was added to the above sample size, which finally made it 412. The questionnaire was submitted to 412 patients. After removing 16 incomplete questionnaires, 396 questionnaires (response rate: 96.12%) were retained for the final analysis.

To select the sample from Hormoz Diabetes Clinic, the health records were retrieved and based on the record number, the patients were selected to be included in the study through a random systematic sampling. Patients with additional chronic diseases were included in the study. This inclusion allows for a more comprehensive understanding of treatment adherence and the factors influencing diabetes management in individuals facing multiple health challenges.

Data collection instrument

A researcher-made questionnaire, developed through a directed qualitative content analysis in a qualitative study40 was used as the measurement instrument.

The first part of the questionnaire was devoted to demographic information, which included age, gender, marital status, education level, employment, socioeconomic status (SES), history of diabetes, type of drug and smoking.

The second part of the questionnaire included the HPM constructs and behaviour-related experiences (online supplemental table 1).41 The questionnaire consisted of a total of 65 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from ‘strongly agree’ to ‘strongly disagree’ (with values assigned as: strongly agree=5, agree=4, no opinion=3, disagree=2, strongly disagree=1). Behaviour questions

Treatment adherence behaviour was measured using another set of five questions, also with a score range from 5 to 25.

To assess the instrument’s content validity, it was provided to 10 experts in health education and health promotion, internist and endocrinology specialists. Their comments were used to modify the questionnaire. To test the reliability of the instrument, the test-retest method was used. To this aim, the questionnaire was provided to 22 subjects meeting the same conditions as the main participants in 2 weeks. Next, each question in the first test was compared with the second test. If the correlation coefficient between the first and second tests was >0.7 in each section, the reliability was substantiated. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the questionnaire constructs were as follows: treatment adherence benefits 0.798, treatment adherence barriers 0.770, treatment adherence self-efficacy 0.686, treatment adherence-related affect 0.609, interpersonal influences 0.734, situational influences 0.741, immediate competing demands and preferences 0.764, commitment to plan of action 0.727, treatment adherence behaviour 0.739 and treatment adherence experiences 0.796.41

Data collection procedure

The data were collected from November 2022 to January 2023 in the morning and afternoon using a researcher-made questionnaire. The first author, who was trained, a native speaker and familiar with the data collection method, took charge of the questionnaire completion phase by visiting the Hormoz Diabetes Clinic. The questionnaires were completed face to face by patients with T2D. If the patient was unable to complete the questionnaire for illiteracy or poor eyesight, the researcher read the questions aloud in Persian or the local language. Completing each questionnaire took about 15 min. The researcher was present at the clinic to answer possible questions until the end of the questionnaire completion.

Data analysis procedure

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the interval data, including mean, SD, minimum and maximum scores. Frequency and percentage were used to describe non-interval variables. Pearson’s correlation test was used to assess the effects of independent variables, HPM constructs and behaviour-related experiences on treatment adherence. Path analysis and structural equation modelling (SEM) were run to test the relationships between HPM constructs and T2D treatment adherence behaviour. Several fit indices were used to test the hypothetical model. These indices included the χ² test of model fit, the goodness-of-fit index (GFI), the normed χ2 statistic (calculated by dividing χ² by df), the adjusted goodness-of-fit index (AGFI) and the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). Each of these indices provides unique insights into different features of model fitness, and we used them in combination for evaluation.42 IBM SPSS V.23 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA) and STATA V.15 were used for statistical analysis, and the significance level was set at 0.05.

Results

A total number of 396 patients with T2D completed the questionnaires. Most participants (39.9%) were in the age range of 45–59 years. Most participants were female (64.1%) and married (83.1%). About 24.2% held a university degree, 41.2% were housewives and 16.2% were employed. Half of the participants (56.8%) had a medium SES, and most (77.0%) lived in their own homes. Most participants (41.7%) had been diagnosed for over 10 years. Fifty-three per cent were consuming pills, and 68.2% had no history of smoking (table 1).

Pearson’s correlation test showed significant relationships between all HPM constructs and treatment adherence behaviour (online supplemental table 2). The correlation is negative for the constructs of perceived barriers, situational influences and immediate competing demands and preferences. Commitment to plan of action had the strongest positive correlation (0.624) with treatment adherence, indicating that stronger commitment leads to better adherence. Conversely, perceived barriers showed a negative correlation (−0.260), suggesting that higher perceived barriers are associated with lower adherence.

Model fitness

Table 3 shows the fit indices. GFI and AGFI values range from 0 to 1, with values >0.9 signifying a well-fitting model.42 For RMSEA, a good fit is indicated by a value <0.05, a moderate fit by a value close to 0.08 and bad model fit by a value >0.1. Although a good fit is indicated by a low χ2 statistic and a non-significant p value, these conditions are rarely met in practice; therefore, we chose to use normed χ2 values instead. A close fit is indicated by a normed χ2 value of 3 or less, but an appropriate model fit is indicated by a value <5. The modified model had sufficient fit. The GFI index was 0.96, AGFI was 0.92, CFI was 0.97, RMSEA was 0.05, χ2 (df) was 26.54 and χ2/df was 1.56 (table 3). These values indicate that the model adequately represents the data and supports the proposed relationships among constructs.

According to figure 1, the standardised estimates for the direct path between commitment to plan of action and immediate competing demands and preferences and behaviour were 0.36 and −0.21, respectively. Perceived benefits (0.17), perceived barriers (−0.15) and perceived self-efficacy (0.29) were also significantly correlated with commitment to plan of action. The direct paths between behaviour and perceived benefits, perceived self-efficacy and experiences were, respectively, 0.078, 0.21 and 0.26. The strongest two-way relationship was found between the related experiences and perceived benefits (0.46). The correlation between related experiences and interpersonal influences, perceived self-efficacy, affects, perceived barriers and situational influences were, respectively, 0.39, 0.38, 0.35, –0.21 and −0.2. There was a statistically significant association between perceived benefits and perceived self-efficacy and between perceived benefits and interpersonal influences, with coefficients of 0.39 and 0.36, respectively.

Discussion

The present study aimed to explore the predictors of T2D treatment adherence. The results of data analysis showed that all questionnaire constructs had a significant correlation with the behaviour of interest. The results of path analysis showed a direct relationship between related experiences, commitment to plan of action, perceived self-efficacy and immediate competing demands and preferences and the treatment adherence behaviour.

Commitment to plan of action was the strongest predictor of T2D treatment adherence behaviour. The results of path analysis showed that perceived benefits, perceived barriers and perceived self-efficacy had a significant relationship with commitment to plan of action. The concept of commitment to plan of action, as the name suggests, refers to the intention and identification of a planned strategy to implement health-promoting behaviours. Therefore, it is strongly associated with the behaviour of interest. A body of research used path analysis to investigate the predictive power of Pender’s HPM. They reported the relationship between other constructs and commitment to plan of action. There is also proof for the direct relationship between this construct and self-care behaviours in COVID-19 pandemic,43 students’ nutritional behaviour,27 nutritional factors in patients with T2D44 and women’s physical activity.45 Some other studies showed that commitment to plan of action predicts self-care behaviour in patients with a heart surgery26 and changes to the eating behaviour of overweight women.46 In the study by Haghi et al, commitment to plan of action was the strongest predictor of brushing behaviour in students.47 Shaahmadi et al reported a statistically significant relationship between stress management and this construct.48 It seems that due to the important role of this construct in adopting healthy behaviours, including treatment adherence, it is better to emphasise commitment to plan of action in interventions.

The results showed that behavioural experiences were directly correlated with treatment adherence behaviour. Individuals who had obtained a higher behaviour score had a higher score of related experiences. In addition, there were significant two-way relationships between related experiences and perceived benefits, interpersonal influences, perceived self-efficacy, related affects, perceived barriers and situational influences. Treatment experience is one parameter that can modify the effect on long-term treatment adherence.49 In other studies, experience has been less addressed as a predictor. Contrary to the present findings, in the USA, Tennant et al stated that treatment experience was a negative predictor of treatment adherence in patients with AIDS. These divergent findings can be attributed to different types of disease and demographic features of samples because the challenges faced by patients with HIV affect treatment adherence. The research population studied by Tennant et al consisted of rural residents with low SES.50 The present findings showed that people had different experiences during the long-term diabetes treatment. It is suggested that in future studies, researchers explore this variable separately so that they can accurately identify the challenges and effective factors in the formation of experiences. It is also suggested to use those who have positive experiences of treatment adherence as role models in interventions.

In the present study, the perceived self-efficacy construct was significantly correlated with the T2D treatment adherence behaviour. The path from perceived self-efficacy mediated by commitment to plan of action had a stronger overall effect than the direct effect of this construct. This finding showed that participants with higher perceived self-efficacy had higher treatment adherence. Perceived self-efficacy represents people’s belief in their ability to correctly perform diabetes treatment adherence behaviours, including medical treatment, healthy eating and physical activity. Perceived self-efficacy was directly related to commitment to plan of action in addition to treatment adherence behaviour, which is similar to other studies.27 43 45 In addition, researchers exploring non-diabetic cases also pointed out the predictive role of self-efficacy in behaviour, which is consistent with the present study. A similar study is the systematic review by Essery et al on treatment adherence behaviour in home physiotherapy.51 Another study is by Yue et al, which examines the relationship between self-efficacy and adherence to antihypertensive drugs.52 Yet another is the study by Khodavisi et al, which investigates the predictive role of self-efficacy in adherence to healthy eating behaviours.46 Tavor et al contended that self-efficacy mediates the relationship between depressive symptoms and treatment adherence in patients with diabetes and suggests that intervention to strengthen self-efficacy may reduce the negative effect of depression on treatment adherence.53 In some research, self-efficacy has not been a predictor of treatment adherence,26 54 which can be attributed to factors such as the different types of diseases studied and the smaller sample size compared with the present study. To improve people’s self-efficacy, methods such as role-play and stress reduction are suggested.

Immediate competing demands and preferences in the present cross-sectional study were the predictors of T2D treatment adherence behaviour. The results of path analysis showed a significant negative relationship between immediate competing demands and preferences and treatment adherence behaviour. Individuals who scored lower in immediate competing demands and preferences had higher treatment adherence. Contrary to the present study, Mehrabbeik et al reported that the direct relationship between this construct and breakfast consumption behaviour in students was not statistically significant; thus, this construct was removed from the final model.27 These different findings can be explained by the different demographic features of participants and the type of behaviour. Immediate competing demands and preferences refer to alternative behaviours over which patients with diabetes have little control due to environmental conditions. These conditions include work or family caregiving tasks, office meetings and the temptation to engage in activities such as watching TV or sleeping instead of adhering to treatment. A review introduced the immediate competing demands and preferences as one factor affecting nutritional behaviour of patients with diabetes.55 Also, in the study by Bashirian et al, unlike the present study, this construct was not a predictor of healthy behaviour,56 which can be attributed to the type of behaviour and participants. Therefore, the variables influencing this construct, such as family and social roles and people’s preferences of improving treatment adherence, should not be neglected. Efforts should be made to adjust these factors in the interventions.

In the present study, there was a significant bidirectional relationship between perceived benefits of treatment adherence behaviour and perceived self-efficacy. This association points to the importance of individual’s beliefs in the positive outcomes of T2D treatment adherence and their confidence in their ability to perform those actions effectively. Consistent with the present findings, Tavakoly Sany et al and Shahroodi et al also reported a bidirectional relationship between perceived benefits and perceived self-efficacy in physical activity behaviour.45 57 The relationship between the perceived benefits of behaviour and perceived self-efficacy is intertwined in health behaviour theories. This highlights the importance of individuals’ beliefs in the positive outcomes of behaviours and their confidence in successfully implementing those behaviours.

Another significant relationship found through path analysis in the present study is the bidirectional relationship between perceived benefits of behaviour and interpersonal influences. Thus, people’s perceptions of the benefits of treatment adherence are influenced by interpersonal factors, such as social support and norms. In other studies, in line with the present study, this two-way relationship has been reported.45 57 Pender’s HPM accounts for the interplay between perceived benefits and interpersonal influences, and shows how people’s perceptions of the benefits of adherence to T2D treatment are influenced by social factors. This interaction emphasises the importance of personal beliefs about the benefits of behaviours and external social influences in shaping T2D treatment adherence behaviour choices.

The other constructs of Pender’s HPM were significantly correlated with treatment adherence behaviour. However, as the path analysis showed, these constructs were not the predictors of treatment adherence behaviour. The results of several studies showed that other constructs of Pender’s HPM were also the predictors of healthy behaviour, including the related affects and perceived benefits in the study by Mohsenipoua et al26 or perceived benefits, perceived barriers, situational influences and interpersonal influences as the predictors of behaviour in the study by Gorbani et al.54 In a meta-analysis, Ho et al suggested that for an effective empowerment strategy, the use of emotions related to behaviour as well as interpersonal and situational influences can facilitate and increase health-promoting behaviours of patients with diabetes.58 Therefore, it is better to consider all the constructs of Pender’s HPM in interventions to improve treatment adherence behaviour.

Limitations

Since the questionnaires were completed face-to-face, participants may have given socially desirable answers rather than true feelings or behaviours. To make up for this, the researcher aimed to create a trusting and calm atmosphere during interviews. This common limitation in behavioural research should be dealt with caution in interpreting the findings. The study was conducted in one city, which can affect the generalisability of results to other regions or populations. Prospective research needs to use a more extensive geographic scope to increase external validity. The present research only emphasised urban patients with diabetes to exclude rural residents, which can affect the application of findings for people of different socioeconomic or environmental settings. A number of participants faced challenges such as illiteracy or low vision because of diabetes complications. Although attempts were made to collect unbiased data, such factors can affect the accuracy of responses and should be considered when evaluating the findings.

Conclusion

The present study explored the predictors of T2D treatment adherence behaviour. In light of the findings, it can be concluded that the proposed model of T2D treatment adherence behaviour has an acceptable fit. Commitment to plan of action, behaviour-related experiences, perceived self-efficacy and immediate competing demands and preferences are the predictors of T2D treatment adherence behaviour. It is suggested that educational interventions be made with an emphasis on these constructs. There is a need for workshops and meetings with an emphasis on the importance of commitment to treatment plans and use of practical tools to manage competing demands. These sessions should also address common barriers to adherence, with a focus on medication management and lifestyle changes. Moreover, follow-up sessions are needed to assess progress and create ongoing motivation, which can help maintain motivation and adherence through time.
