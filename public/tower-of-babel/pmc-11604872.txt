Knockout of onecut2 inhibits proliferation and promotes apoptosis of tumor cells through SKP2-mediated p53 acetylation in hepatocellular carcinoma

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is classified as a malignant neoplasm, with only 15% of newly diagnosed patients judged eligible for surgical excision, suggesting that liver transplantation is a potential curative approach [1]. However, the availability of donors is markedly limited, allowing only a minority of patients to undergo this surgical intervention [2]. For the treatment of advanced HCC, sorafenib has received approval from the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as a therapeutic agent. Nonetheless, its efficacy is compromised by the emergence of tumor resistance to the drug [3]. Therefore, it is crucial to explore alternative treatment modalities for patients with advanced HCC.

ONECUT2 (OC2) plays a crucial role in the development and function of various organs in the human body, such as the liver, pancreas, and brain [4]. OC2 is highly expressed in a variety of cancers, including prostate cancer [5], colorectal cancer [6], hepatocellular cancer [7], ovarian cancer [8], breast cancer [9], and lung cancer [10]. As a transcription factor, OC2 can extensively regulate the expression of proteins related to cell proliferation, migration, adhesion, and differentiation [4]. In aggressive prostate cancer variants, OC2 inhibited the transcriptional program of the androgen receptor and promoted tumor cell metastasis, and the small molecule CSRM617 was identified as an inhibitor of OC2 function [11]. In neuroendocrine prostate cancer, OC2, Smad3, and HIF-1α synergize with hypoxia signaling, inhibit androgen signaling, and promote tumor invasion and angiogenesis [5]. Moreover, our previous studies demonstrated that OC2 participates in the regulation of tumor angiogenesis by regulating the expression of VEGFA through the zinc finger protein ZKSCAN3 [8, 12]. However, the regulatory role and specific molecular mechanism of OC2 knockdown or knockout in the apoptosis of HCC cells remain unclear.

Apoptosis is a distinct biochemical and morphological process that regulates homeostasis within an organism [13]. The ultimate goal of anticancer therapy is to induce the apoptosis of tumor cells [14]. p53 is a powerful tumor suppressor that inhibits tumor growth in multiple ways [15]. As a transcription factor, p53 regulates the expression of target genes that can promote cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, DNA repair, etc [16]. The antitumor effect of p53 mainly involves activating the expression of p21 and p27 and preventing cells from entering the S phase, thereby promoting cell apoptosis [17]. S phase kinase-associated protein 2 (SKP2) is an important substrate recognition protein in the SCFSKP2 ubiquitin ligase complex that can target a variety of tumor suppressor protein substrates for ubiquitination degradation [18]. It has been reported that inhibiting SKP2 expression can promote p53-mediated apoptosis. This function of SKP2 is independent of ubiquitination and is due to SKP2 antagonizing the interaction between acetyltransferase p300 and p53, which inhibits p300-mediated p53 acetylation and transactivation [19, 20]. In addition, in breast cancer, SKP2 overexpression promotes cell proliferation and inhibits cell apoptosis by reducing p53-mediated PIG3 expression [21, 22]. Owing to the regulatory relationship between SKP2 and p53, SKP2 may be an important factor involved in the regulation of tumor cell apoptosis.

In this study, we explored the mechanism by which OC2 drives tumor growth and apoptosis in HCC cells and identified target genes involved in the response to OC2. Furthermore, we explored the role of OC2 in apoptosis, signaling pathways, and regulatory axes. We aimed to provide a basis for targeted tumor therapy based on OC2 and apoptosis.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and tissues

HepG2, SK-Hep1, HEK293T cells and HUVECs were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). The cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, VivalCell, Armenia) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution (Gibco) at 37 ℃ in an incubator with 5% CO2. The HCC samples and adjacent tissues were collected from HCC patients who underwent surgical resection at the Department of Hepatobiliary Surgery, the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University (Guangzhou, China).

Design of sgRNAs targeting the OC2 gene and vector construction

Two sgRNA sequences targeting exon 1 of OC2 were designed via the sgRNA design website (http://crispr.mit.edu/). The sequences of the sgRNAs are shown in Supplementary Tables 1 and were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Limited Company (Guangzhou, China). The sgRNA was ligated into the LentiCRISPRv2 plasmid via T4 ligase to construct the recombinant plasmid LentiCRISPR-sgRNA. The OC2 overexpression plasmid (pcDNA3.1(+)-OC2) was constructed by Wuhan Gene Create Biological Engineering Corporation (Wuhan, China). The OC2 CDS region in this plasmid has been codon optimized, and there is no sgRNA binding site.

Packaging and infection of lentivirus

HEK293T cells were cotransfected with LentiCRISPR-sgRNA, pSPAX2 and pMD2G plasmids using lip2000 (GK20005, GLPBIO). The lentivirus was produced in recombinant HEK293T cells. The lentivirus in the supernatant of the HEK293T cells was collected at 96 h and concentrated in PEG 8000 at 5000×g for 30 min at 4 ℃. SK-Hep1 cells were plated in 6-well plates and infected with 100 µL of lentivirus for 24 h. The cells were treated with 2 µg/mL puromycin (HY-K1057, MCE) for two weeks to attenuate noninfected cells. After selection with puromycin, the SK-Hep1 cells were cloned by limited dilution in 96-well plates. The monoclonal cells were harvested 14 days after transfer to 24-well plates and sequenced at Sangon Biotech (Guangzhou, China).

Western blot

The cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer containing PMSF, and the protein concentration was quantified via a BCA kit (E112-01, Vazyme). The proteins were separated by SDS‒PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore). The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies, followed by interaction with a secondary antibody (Cat: 7074; Cell Signaling Technology, USA). The primary antibodies used included an anti-OC2 antibody purchased from Proteintech (21916-1-AP). The anti-Ac-p53 (2525), anti-AKT (4685), anti-angiogenin (62224), anti-Bax (41162), anti-Bcl-2 (4223), anti-CD31 (77699), anti-FGF2 (46879), anti-GAPDH (5174), anti-MAPK (4695), anti-PARP (9532), anti-PDGF-B (3169), anti-p21 (2947), anti-p27 (3686), anti-p53 (2527), anti-p-AKT (4060), anti-p-MAPK (9101), anti-p-p53 (9284), anti-TGF-β1 (3709), and anti-VEGFA (50661) antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT‒PCR)

Fresh HCC and adjacent tissues and mouse xenograft tumor tissues from each treatment group were ground in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted via the RNA-Easy Isolation Reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). cDNA was prepared via reverse transcription using HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme). qRT‒PCR was performed using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) to amplify the target sequence. The relative mRNA expression levels were determined by the 2–ΔΔCt method and normalized to those of the control group. The primers used for qRT‒PCR are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay

Sections of liver tissue and xenograft tumor tissues were prepared at Servicebio (Wuhan, China). The sections were incubated with primary antibodies against OC2 (Proteintech, 21916-1-AP, 1:250), SKP2 (Cell Signaling Technology, 2652, 1:200), KI67 (Cell Signaling Technology, 12202, 1:400) and CD31 (Cell Signaling Technology, 77699, 1:200) at 4 °C overnight. The next day, peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology) were added, and the sections were incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The signal was visualized after 2 min of treatment with diaminobenzidine. Images were captured using an optical microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Cell viability

Cell viability was measured using a Cell Counting Kit 8 (HY-K0301, MCE). The cells were grown in 96-well plates, and 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well at 0 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 96 h. The absorbances of the 96-well plates at 450 nm were detected via a microplate absorbance reader (BioTek).

Colony formation assay

The cells were cultivated in 6-well plates at 2000 cells per well for approximately 7‒9 days. The positive clonal clusters grew to 50 cells or more. The cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h, stained with 1% crystal violet, and finally, colonies with 50 or more cells were counted via ImageJ.

Wound-healing assay

The HCC cells in each treatment group were seeded in 6-well plates at 3 × 105 cells per well. When the cell density reached 70-80%, the cells were scratched with 200 µL pipette tips. Images were taken at 0 h, 24 h, and 48 h using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The wound area was measured via ImageJ to calculate the migration rate.

Tube formation assay

The 96-well plate was coated with Matrigel (50 µL) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. HUVECs adjusted to 1 × 105 cells/mL in FBS-free DMEM were added to each well of a 96-well plate, and the culture supernatant (100 µL) of HCC cells was added. The plate was placed in a 37 ℃ incubator for 4 h. The tube formation of HUVECs was photographed under a microscope, and the numbers were determined via ImageJ.

ELISA assay

An AuthentiKine™ Human VEGF/FGF2 ELISA Kit (KE00216/KE00129, Proteintech) was used to detect human VEGFA and FGF2 concentrations in the supernatant of HCC cells. The primary antibodies used were anti-VEGFA and anti-FGF2, and the secondary antibodies used were HRP-conjugated goat anti-human monoclonal antibodies. The optical density (OD value) was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 630 nm with a CLARIOstar Microplate Reader (BMG LABTECH, Germany).

Apoptosis assay

Apoptotic cells were identified via an Annexin V-APC/7-AAD Apoptosis Kit (Elabscience, Cat: E-CK-A218). The HCC cells in each treatment group were digested with EDTA-free trypsin and adjusted to 2 × 105 cells in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. The cells were washed with PBS and treated according to the manual of the Annexin V-APC/7-AAD Apoptosis Kit. The cells were assayed with a flow cytometer (Attune NxT).

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

The cells (2 × 105) were seeded in a 12-well plate for 12 h. The dual-luciferase reporter assay system and OC2 overexpression plasmid were cotransfected into the cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA). After 48 h, the transfected cells with the luciferase reporter genes were lysed and then centrifuged for 3 min. Luciferase activity (firefly luciferase at a wavelength of 560 nm and Renilla luciferase at a wavelength of 465 nm in the supernatant) was measured with the substrates of the Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) with a CLARIOstar Microplate Reader (BMG LABTECH, Germany).

ChIP‒qPCR

The chromatin coimmunoprecipitation was performed via the BeyoChIP™ Enzymatic ChIP Assay Kit (Protein A/G Magnetic Beads) (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Following the manual’s instructions, the HCC cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde and lysed with cell lysis buffer and nuclear lysis buffer. The supernatant was subjected to ultrasonic treatment for 30 min at 4 °C at high power to obtain fragments of 200–500 bp in size, which were then immunoprecipitated from the protein‒DNA complex with anti-Flag antibodies. The protein‒DNA complex was combined with protein‒A‒agarose beads. The bound DNA fragments were isolated and purified via phenol‒chloroform-isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) and precipitated with ethanol and acrylamide. The purified DNA fragments were used for ChIP‒qPCR. The primers used for ChIP‒qPCR were listed in Supplemental Table 3.

Mouse xenograft model

Four-week-old BALB/c nude mice were treated humanely according to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Experimental Animals of Jinan University, and all animal assays were approved by the Experimental Animal Science Ethics and Welfare Committee of Jinan University. Monoclonal OC2 knockout cells (2 × 106 in 100 µL) with an equal volume of Matrigel were subcutaneously injected into the shoulders of the mice. The control group was injected with PBS and Matrigel. The volume of the tumors (length×width2)/2 was measured every 3 days. After 21 days, the mice were euthanized, and the xenograft tumors were harvested for IHC analysis and weight measurements.

TUNEL assay

The TUNEL assay was performed with a one-step TUNEL apoptosis assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sections were fixed with a fixative and permeabilized with Triton X-100. The slides were incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture at 37 °C for 1 h. The samples were re-stained with DAPI and analyzed via fluorescence microscopy.

Statistical analysis

All of the experiments were performed three times, and the data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 8. The results are shown as the means ± SDs. Statistical differences between groups were analyzed via one-way analysis of variance or Student’s t test.

Results

OC2 expression in HCC tissues

We collected tumor tissue and adjacent tissue from patients (n = 10) who underwent liver surgery and analyzed them via immunochemistry, qRT‒PCR and Western blotting. The results revealed that the expression level of OC2 in tumor tissues from 8 patients was significantly greater than that in adjacent tissues (Fig. 1A-C). Moreover, we analyzed four sets of RNA-seq data from HCC clinical samples in the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), including GSE87410, GSE105130, GSE124535 and GSE202853. We found that the OC2 expression levels in HCC tissues were significantly greater than those in normal tissues (Supplemental Fig. S1A). Through GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) analysis, we found that the relative expression level of OC2 in HCC tissues was significantly greater than that in normal tissues and that high expression of OC2 significantly reduced the overall survival (OS) of patients with HCC (Supplemental Fig. S1B-D).

OC2 knockdown with shRNA inhibits proliferation and promotes apoptosis in HCC cells

OC2 expression in HCC cells (SK-Hep1, PLC/PRF/5, Huh6 and HepG2) was detected via qRT‒PCR and Western blotting. The results revealed that the expression of OC2 was significantly greater in HepG2 and SK-Hep1 cells than in other HCC cells (PLC/PRF/5 and Huh6) and control cells (HEK293T cells and HUVECs) (Fig. 1D-E). Next, the expression of OC2 was inhibited by shRNA in HepG2 and SK-Hep1 cells. The results of qRT‒PCR and Western blotting revealed that the expression of OC2 was significantly downregulated by the shRNA in HepG2 and SK-Hep1 cells (Fig. 1F-G). The CCK-8 assay results showed that OC2 knockdown inhibited the proliferation of HepG2 and SK-Hep1 cells. The inhibition rates reached approximately 58% and 53%, respectively (Fig. 1H). The results of the colony formation assays also revealed that the proliferation of HepG2 and SK-Hep1 cells was significantly inhibited after OC2 knockdown (Fig. 1I). Furthermore, OC2 knockdown significantly downregulated the phosphorylation of AKT and MAPK and the expression of FGF2 in HepG2 and SK-Hep1 cells (Fig. 1J and S2). More importantly, we found that OC2 knockdown significantly increased the apoptosis of HepG2 and SK-Hep1 cells. The percentage of apoptotic cells reached 19.27% and 23.44%, respectively (Fig. 1K). Moreover, the expression of Bcl2 and PARP was downregulated, and that of Bax was upregulated (Fig. 1L-M).

Establishment of an OC2 knockout cell line via the CRISPR/Cas9 system

The sgRNA targeting OC2 exon 1 was designed according to Cas9 and the PAM (Fig. 2A and S3A). The OC2 gene of SK-Hep1 cells was knocked out via the CRISPR/Cas9 system. We screened and sequenced monoclonal cells and identified two OC2 knockout cell lines, B8 and B10 (Supplemental Fig. 3B-F). The results of the Western blot analysis revealed that the OC2 protein was not expressed in the B8 or B10 cells (Fig. 2B). The results of qRT‒PCR analysis revealed that OC2-mRNA expression was significantly decreased in the B8 and B10 cells (Fig. 2C).

OC2 knockout inhibited proliferation and promoted apoptosis in HCC cells

The proliferation of B8 and B10 cells with OC2 knockout was significantly inhibited. The inhibition rates were approximately 96.1% and 84.0%, respectively (Fig. 2D-E). The results of the qRT‒PCR and Western blot assays revealed that the phosphorylation levels of AKT and MAPK and the expression of FGF2 in B8 and B10 cells were significantly downregulated (Fig. 2F-G). Moreover, we observed that OC2 knockout decreased the expression of angiogenic factors and inhibited angiogenesis. The number of tubes formed of HUVECs treated with the culture supernatants of B8 and B10 cells were significantly decreased (Fig. 2H). Moreover, the expression of CD31, PDGFB, VEGFA, angiogenin, and TGF-β1 was significantly downregulated in B8 and B10 cells (Fig. 2I-J). The OC2 knockout B8 and B10 cells clearly exhibited high rates of apoptosis, approximately 29.79% and 30.514%, respectively (Fig. 2K). Moreover, the expression of Bcl2 and PARP was significantly downregulated, whereas the expression of Bax was significantly upregulated (Fig. 2L-M).

OC2 activated SKP2 transcription by binding to its promoter

Previous ChIP-Seq data from our laboratory showed that OC2 could bind to the promoters of 19 candidate genes [12]. The qRT‒PCR results revealed that the expression of DSP, SKP2, and ZKSCAN3 was significantly upregulated in both HepG2 and SK-Hep1 cells after OC2 was overexpressed (Fig. 3A-B). Additionally, the expression of DSP, FGD4, SKP2, SLC9A1, SSUH2, ZKSCAN3, and ZNF689 was significantly downregulated in B8 cells (Fig. 3C). These results showed that the expression levels of DSP, SKP2, and ZKSCAN3 were correlated with the expression of OC2, which indicated that they might be regulated by OC2. OC2 downregulated the expression of ZKSCAN3, which was previously shown in our laboratory to regulate the expression of VEGFA and tumor angiogenesis [22]. Furthermore, we analyzed the ability of OC2 to affect the promoter activities of DSP and SKP2 via dual-luciferase reporter assays. We found that OC2 significantly enhanced the promoter activity of SKP2 but not that of DSP (Fig. 3D). On the basis of the motif sequence of the OC2 transcription factor binding to DNA, the OC2 binding site of the SKP2 promoter sequence ranged from -193 bp to -203 bp (Fig. 3E). After mutation of the binding site in the promoter sequence, OC2 was no longer able to promote SKP2 promoter activity (Fig. 3F-G).

We analyzed clinical samples via Western blotting and found that the expression of SKP2 in tumor tissues was obviously greater than that in normal tissues (Fig. 3H-I). Correlation analysis between the expression of OC2 and SKP2 in the clinical samples revealed a positive correlation, with an R2 value of 0.8847 (Fig. 3J). Furthermore, the expression of SKP2 in various tumor tissues was analyzed via the GEPIA database (https://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/). The results of the bioinformatics analysis revealed that tumor tissues presented increased expression of SKP2 and that high expression of SKP2 could reduce the overall survival of patients with HCC (Fig. S4). The Western blot results also revealed that OC2 knockdown could downregulate the expression of SKP2 in HepG2 and SK-Hep1 cells (Fig. 3K), whereas OC2 overexpression could upregulate the expression of SKP2 (Fig. S5). Furthermore, the results of ChIP‒qPCR analysis revealed that OC2 overexpression significantly enriched the SKP2 promoter fragment in HepG2 and SK-Hep1 cells (Fig. 3L).

SKP2 knockdown inhibited proliferation and promoted apoptosis in HCC cells

We knocked down SKP2 with shRNA in HepG2 and SK-Hep1 cells. SKP2 knockdown obviously inhibited the proliferation and promoted the apoptosis of HepG2 and SK-hep1 cells (Fig. 4A-D). SKP2 knockdown significantly downregulated the expression of Bcl2 and PARP but upregulated the expression of Bax (Fig. 4E-F). To explore the regulatory role of SKP2 in apoptosis, we analyzed the expression of p21 and p27 via qRT‒PCR and Western blotting. SKP2 knockdown significantly upregulated the expression of p21 and p27, similar to OC2 knockdown and knockout (Fig. 4G-I and S6). These results indicate that OC2 may regulate the apoptosis of HCC cells via SKP2.

OC2 knockout promoted apoptosis by downregulating the expression of SKP2

To explore the regulatory relationship between OC2 and SKP2, OC2 and SKP2 were overexpressed in B8 cells. The results of the CCK-8 assay revealed that OC2 overexpression reversed the inhibition of proliferation mediated by OC2 knockout in B8 cells (Fig. 5A and S7). The results of the flow cytometry assay revealed that OC2 overexpression inhibited the apoptosis caused by OC2 knockout in B8 cells (Fig. 5B). Moreover, the expression of SKP2, PARP and Bcl2 increased, and the expression of the apoptotic factors Bax, p21 and p27 decreased significantly (Fig. 5C and S8). Similarly, SKP2 overexpression reversed the effects on proliferation and apoptosis; increased the expression of SKP2, PARP and Bcl2 and significantly decreased the expression of Bax, p21 and p27 significantly in B8 cells (Fig. 5D-G and S9). These results indicate that OC2 can indirectly regulate the expression of p21 and p27 via SKP2 and subsequently regulate the apoptosis of HCC cells.

OC2 knockout activated p53 acetylation via downregulation of SKP2 expression

Previous reports have demonstrated that SKP2 can competitively bind to p300 and inhibit p53 acetylation [19]. To explore the mechanism of apoptosis mediated by OC2 knockout, we further studied the relationship between the phosphorylation and acetylation of p53. The Western blot results revealed that neither the expression level nor the phosphorylation level of p53 was changed, but the acetylation level of p53 was significantly increased in B8 cells (Fig. 5H). The acetylation level of p53 was downregulated after SKP2 was overexpressed in B8 cells (Fig. 5H). To explore the regulatory relationship among OC2, SKP2 and p53, a dual-luciferase reporter vector was constructed with multiple p53 response elements inserted. The results showed that the fluorescence intensity obviously increased after OC2 and SKP2 were knocked down in SK-Hep1 cells. The fluorescence intensity increased 2.89-fold and 2.72-fold, respectively (Fig. 5I). Interestingly, we found that the acetylation level of p53 was downregulated in B8 cells overexpressing OC2 and SKP2 (Fig. 5J). Combined with previous reports, this result may be due to SKP2 inhibiting the interaction between p300 and p53. These results indicated that OC2 knockdown could regulate the acetylation of p53 via SKP2 and p300, promote the expression of p21 and p27, and thus promote apoptosis in HCC cells.

OC2 knockout inhibited tumor growth in vivo

To construct a mouse model, B8 and B10 knockout OC2 cells (2 × 106) were injected into the shoulder of nude mice (n = 5) (Fig. 6A). The results showed that tumor growth was significantly suppressed in OC2 knockout HCC cells. The inhibition rate of tumor growth was approximately 98.8% in B8 cells and 94.8% in B10 cells (Fig. 6B-D). The results of the Western blot analysis revealed that OC2 knockout downregulated the expression of p-AKT, p-MAPK, FGF2, CD31, PDGFB, VEGFA, angiogenin, TGF-β1, SKP2, Bcl2 and PARP and upregulated the expression of Bax, p21 and p27 (Fig. 6E-G). The results of the immunochemistry experiments revealed that the expression of Ki67 and CD31 was obviously lower in the B8 and B10 cells than in the control cells (Fig. 6H). The results of TUNEL assays revealed that there were many more apoptotic cells than in the control, which indicated that OC2 knockout could inhibit tumor growth and promote apoptosis in HCC.

Discussion

OC2 is upregulated in a variety of cancer cells and is involved in the angiogenesis and proliferation of tumor cells [23]. Previous studies of the OC2 gene have focused mainly on its influence on the early growth of the liver and the growth and differentiation process of retinal astrocytes and its mechanism [24]. Our study revealed that OC2 knockdown or knockout not only inhibited the proliferation of HCC cells but also promoted the apoptosis of HCC cells, which suggested that OC2 may promote the progression of HCC cells by inhibiting the apoptosis pathway. Therefore, studying the mechanism of action and potential molecular mechanism of OC2 in tumor apoptosis can provide a basis for improving therapeutic options for treating OC2 tumors.

Cell apoptosis is an essential aspect of the efficient functioning of an organism. The activation of p53 elicits various responses, including cell cycle arrest or apoptosis, thereby providing a critical barrier against tumor progression [25]. p53 can upregulate the expression levels of the downstream apoptosis-related proteins Bax and IGFBP3 and downregulate the expression level of Bcl2, thereby regulating cell apoptosis [26–28]. Our studies involving OC2 knockdown and knockout revealed a significant decrease in the expression of the antiapoptotic proteins PARP and Bcl2, alongside an upregulation of the proapoptotic protein Bax. These findings suggest a potential regulatory relationship between OC2 and p53. However, according to the ChIP-Seq data, there were no direct interactions between OC2 and the promoters of p53, PARP, Bcl2, and Bax, indicating that OC2 might regulate the expression of apoptotic factors in an indirect manner. The results of qRT‒PCR and dual-luciferase reporter assays revealed that SKP2 is a downstream regulator of OC2. SKP2 knockdown significantly induced apoptosis, and SKP2 overexpression restored OC2 knockout-induced apoptosis in HCC cells. These results suggest that OC2 knockout promotes tumor cell apoptosis via downregulation of SKP2 expression.

Posttranslational modifications of p53, such as phosphorylation and acetylation, play important roles in regulating the stability and activity of p53 [29]. p300 acts as an acetyltransferase that binds to the transcriptional activation domain of p53 and stimulates p53 transcriptional activity [19, 30]. In our study, SKP2 inhibited p53 acetylation by competing with p53 for binding to p300, thereby leading to p53 inactivation. Moreover, OC2 knockout or SKP2 knockdown enhanced the transcriptional activation of p53, suggesting that OC2 modulated the level of p53 acetylation via SKP2, thereby influencing apoptosis. Previous studies have shown that MDM2 is a negative regulator of p53, which binds to p300 and thereby inhibits the acetylation of p53 [30, 31]. Furthermore, MDM2 blocks the ability of p300 to stimulate sequence-specific DNA binding and p53 transcriptional activity [32]. Therefore, we believe that OC2 is a compelling target for inducing apoptosis in tumor cells because it acts on multiple apoptotic factors and signaling pathways. miRNAs such as miR-6086, short hairpin RNA (shRNA) gene editing, the small molecule CSRM617, and the signaling pathway inhibitors ERK1/2 and Smad can be used as candidate drugs to inhibit OC2 function for targeted tumor therapy [8, 11].

Tumor angiogenesis is a complex process involving the coordination of various angiogenic factors and signaling pathways [33, 34]. Our study revealed the targeting relationships of the transcription factor OC2 with ZKSCAN3 and VEGFA [11]. In addition to VEGFA, FGF2, PDGFB, TGF-β1 and angiogenin are also positively correlated with OC2, but their molecular mechanisms still need to be further explored. Furthermore, OC2 activates Smad3 to regulate the binding of HIF-1α to chromatin; thus, in addition to ZKSCAN3, OC2 may also regulate the expression of VEGFA through the HIF-1α pathway [35]. Overall, we suggest that OC2 is a compelling target for inducing complete inhibition of tumor angiogenesis and preventing drug resistance due to its effects on multiple angiogenic factors. Furthermore, we found that OC2 knockout downregulated the phosphorylation levels of AKT and MAPK and the expression levels of FGF2 in HCC cells, suggesting that OC2 may be involved in activating the AKT/MAPK signaling pathways, promoting tumor cell proliferation while inhibiting cell apoptosis. OC2 directly binds to the promoters of FGF2 and ACLY, transcriptionally upregulating their expression [36, 37]. Knockdown of FGF2 and ACLY inhibited OC2-mediated HCC metastasis, whereas their upregulation reversed the OC2 knockdown-induced suppression of HCC metastasis. Moreover, FGF2 upregulated OC2 expression through the FGFR1/ERK/ELK1 pathway, forming a positive feedback loop of FGF2-FGFR1-OC2 [37, 38].

In conclusion, on the one hand, OC2 promoted the transcriptional activation of SKP2, which competes with p53 for binding to p300, and downregulated the acetylation level of p53, thereby inhibiting the apoptosis of HCC cells; on the other hand, OC2 promoted the proliferation of HCC cells through the AKT/MAPK signaling pathways (Fig. 7). Our study reveals the mode of action of OC2 in regulating the apoptosis, growth, metastasis and angiogenesis of HCC cells and provides a theoretical basis for OC2 to become a clinical antitumor molecular targeted therapy.
