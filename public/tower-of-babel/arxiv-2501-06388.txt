Realizability-Preserving Discontinuous Galerkin Method for Spectral Two-Moment Radiation Transport in Special Relativity
Joseph Hunter, Eirik Endeve, M. Paul Laiu, Yulong Xing

Abstract
We present a realizability-preserving numerical method for solving a spectral two-moment model to simulate the transport of massless, neutral particles interacting with a steady background material moving with relativistic velocities. The model is obtained as the special relativistic limit of a four-momentum-conservative general relativistic two-moment model. Using a maximum-entropy closure, we solve for the Eulerian-frame energy and momentum. The proposed numerical method is designed to preserve moment realizability, which corresponds to moments defined by a nonnegative phase-space density. The realizability-preserving method is achieved with the following key components: (i) a discontinuous Galerkin (DG) phase-space discretization with specially constructed numerical fluxes in the spatial and energy dimensions; (ii) a strong stability-preserving implicit-explicit (IMEX) time-integration method; (iii) a realizability-preserving conserved to primitive moment solver; (iv) a realizability-preserving implicit collision solver; and (v) a realizability-enforcing limiter. Component (iii) is necessitated by the closure procedure, which closes higher order moments nonlinearly in terms of primitive moments. The nonlinear conserved to primitive and the implicit collision solves are formulated as fixed-point problems, which are solved with custom iterative solvers designed to preserve the realizability of each iterate. With a series of numerical tests, we demonstrate the accuracy and robustness of this DG-IMEX method.

Introduction

In this paper, we design and analyze a numerical method for solving a spectral two-moment model to simulate the transport of massless, neutral particles (e.g., photons or classical neutrinos) interacting with a steady (i.e., time independent) background material moving with relativistic velocities.
The proposed method uses the discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method for phase-space discretization and implicit-explicit (IMEX) time-stepping.
In particular, the fully discrete scheme is designed to maintain certain physical bounds for the evolved angular moments, generated from a nonnegative distribution function.
These bounds are respected numerically, through careful consideration of the phase-space and temporal discretizations, and the formulation of iterative nonlinear solvers, which are part of the solution process.

Neutral particle transport is an important aspect of modelling many relativistic astrophysical systems, including core-collapse supernovae (CCSNe)
[
1
]
, neutron star-neutron star or neutron star-black hole mergers
[
2

3

[
4

The study of transport processes in these systems is, in part, complicated by the need for kinetic models, seeking the distribution function
f(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{x},t)
— a phase-space density, which at time
t
gives the number of particles in an infinitesimal phase-space volume element centered about the phase-space coordinates
\{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{x}\}
.
Here,
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{x}
are momentum-space and position-space coordinates, respectively.
The evolution of
f
is governed by a kinetic equation, specifically the Boltzmann equation, which expresses a balance between phase-space advection and collisions.

Solving for
f
numerically, to study the aforementioned astrophysical systems in full dimensionality (six phase-space dimensions plus time), with high phase-space resolution, is computationally expensive, if not infeasible with present-day computational resources, although full-dimensional solvers, e.g., using Monte Carlo methods
[
5
,
6
]
, or methods based on spherical harmonics
[
7
]
, discrete ordinates
[

9

, finite-elements
[

, and finite-volumes
[
11

to discretize the angular dimensions of momentum space, have been proposed.
To reduce cost, a common approach is to solve for a few angular moments to capture the lowest-order directional features of the distribution.
To this end, spherical-polar momentum-space coordinates
\{\varepsilon,\vartheta,\varphi\}
are introduced, and
f
is integrated against angular basis functions (depending on momentum-space angles
\omega=\{\vartheta,\varphi\})
to obtain angular moments, which depend only on
\boldsymbol{x}
,

\varepsilon

In this paper, we consider a nonlinear two-moment model obtained as the special relativistic limit of the 3+1 general relativistic framework in
[

Due to the appearance of higher-order moments in the truncated equation hierarchy, obtained after taking moments of the Boltzmann equation, the model is not closed, and a closure procedure is required to express higher-order moments in terms of the lower-order evolved moments.
To close the model, we consider the maximum-entropy closure proposed by Minerbo
[
14
]
for Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics — the low-occupation number approximation to maximum-entropy closures of particle systems obeying Bose–Einstein or Fermi–Dirac statistics
[
15

Specifically, for our numerical experiments, we use an algebraic approximation to the Minerbo closure (e.g.,
[
16

The design of a numerical method to model the transport of particles interacting with a moving fluid is complicated by the need to choose appropriate coordinates for the momentum-space discretization.
Two obvious choices are Eulerian-frame and comoving-frame momentum coordinates (see, e.g.,
[
17
,
18
]
, for detailed discussions).
In this paper, as in, e.g.,
[
12
,
19
]
, we use comoving-frame momentum-space coordinates; that is, momentum coordinates with respect to an inertial frame instantaneously comoving with the fluid
[
20
]
.
The angular moments are thus functions of Eulerian-frame spacetime coordinates,
\{\boldsymbol{x},t\}
and the particle energy measured by a comoving observer,
\varepsilon
.
On the one hand, this choice simplifies the treatment of particle-fluid interactions (because material properties are more isotropic) and the moment closure procedure (because the distribution function is isotropic when particles are in equilibrium with matter).
On the other hand, the left-hand side of the moment equations, modeling phase-space advection, is made more complicated by the appearance of velocity-dependent terms (so-called observer corrections).

While the
primitive
variables of the two-moment model are components of a Lagrangian decomposition of the particle stress-energy tensor, we employ the four-momentum-conservative formulation of
[
12
,
13
]
, where the evolution equations evolve components of an Eulerian decomposition of the stress-energy tensor, and, when integrated over the particle energy dimension, express conservation of Eulerian-frame energy and three-momentum.
The Eulerian-frame (conserved) quantities can be expressed in terms of their comoving-frame (primitive) counterparts
[
13
,
1
]
to, e.g., evaluate closure relations and fluxes.
The mixture of Eulerian- and comoving-frame quantities necessitates a moment conversion process (similar to what is needed in relativistic magnetohydrodynamics
[
21
]
).
To this end, extending prior work on a similar model in the
\mathcal{O}{(v/c)}
limit
[
22
]
, we propose an iterative scheme to solve for comoving-frame quantities from the evolved Eulerian-frame quantities.

Several numerical methods for general relativistic two-moment (neutrino or photon) transport have been proposed to date.
These evolve either grey moments, where the energy dependence has been integrated out, or spectral moments, as we consider here.
For example, grey moment solvers have been developed for application to neutron star merger simulations

For application to CCSN, a general relativistic spectral two-moment solver was proposed by
[

, and in the context of conformally flat spacetimes by
[
29
,
30

, and for spherically symmetric spacetimes in
[

We also mention the general relativistic spectral two-moment solver proposed by
[

These schemes — largely based on finite-volume or finite-difference methods, in combination with explicit and implicit time stepping — have been coupled to hydrodynamics solvers and used extensively to model astrophysical phenomena.

Here, we apply the DG method (e.g.,

In contrast, finite-volume and finite-difference methods may have difficulties capturing this limit, unless, e.g., the particle mean free path is resolved by the spatial mesh, the numerical flux is modified in the diffusive regime

Resolving the mean free path is computationally inefficient for the applications we target.
Furthermore, with the modified flux, it is difficult to design a provably realizability-preserving method for the two-moment model, for which we take advantage of the flexibility offered by the DG method to more freely specify the numerical fluxes.
Similar to the finite-volume method, the DG method is locally conservative and is able to capture discontinuities.
There have been extensive studies of structure-preserving DG methods in recent years. These methods preserve exactly, at the discrete level, the continuum properties of the underlying physical models, such as positivity, maximum principle, and asymptotic limits. Some recent studies on structure-preserving DG methods can be found in
[
43
,
44
,

46
]
and the survey paper
[
47

.

To evolve the semi-discrete DG scheme, we use IMEX time-stepping methods
[
48
,
49
]
, where we treat the phase-space advection terms explicitly and the collision term implicitly.
We opt for IMEX methods because treating collisions explicitly results in a highly restrictive time-step condition for stability, while the explicit phase-space advection update is governed by a Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) time-step restriction depending on the speed of light, which is not too different from both the sound speed and flow velocity in relativistic systems.
The added cost of the implicit treatment of collisions is that a nonlinear system of equations (akin to a backward Euler step) must be solved multiple times per time step, depending on the number of implicit stages of the IMEX scheme.
We point out that collisions are local to each spatial point, which makes the implicit part embarrassingly parallel.

Under Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics, the particle distribution function
f
must be nonnegative, which leads to constraints that the moments must satisfy.
Specifically, the energy density must be positive, and the norm of the momentum density vector must be bounded by the energy density.
Moments satisfying these constraints are said to be realizable.
Solving the two-moment model numerically can result in nonrealizable moments, which is unphysical, makes the closure procedure ill-posed, and can result in code crashes.
Therefore it is desirable to design numerical methods which maintain moment realizability.
Some realizability-preserving two-moment schemes have been developed in
[
50
,
46
,
22
]
.

Our proposed method for the special relativistic two-moment model is designed to preserve moment realizability.
We use strong stability-preserving (SSP) IMEX schemes, which allow us to write the explicit update as a convex combination of forward Euler steps.
For the DG discretization, we propose numerical phase-space fluxes which, together with the SSP IMEX scheme, maintain moment realizability of the cell-averaged moments in the explicit update under a CFL-type time-step restriction.
After each stage of the IMEX scheme, the realizability-enforcing limiter proposed in
[
46
]
is applied to ensure realizability pointwise in each element.
As the moment closure procedure requires a conversion process from conserved to primitive moments, we formulate the conversion problem as a fixed-point problem analogous to the modified Richardson iteration, where each iteration preserves realizability.
Finally, each implicit update of the IMEX scheme can be formulated as a backward Euler step.
The resulting nonlinear system is an extension of the nonlinear system for the moment conversion process, and, with minor modifications, the iterative scheme for the implicit update is formulated to preserve realizability.
With each of these components, we prove that our proposed DG-IMEX scheme for the special relativistic two-moment model is realizability-preserving.

The method proposed here can be considered as an extension (to special relativity) of the realizability-preserving methods in
[
46
]
and
[
22
]
, which are, respectively, non-relativistic with focus on aspects of Fermi–Dirac statistics, or include special relativistic corrections to
\mathcal{O}(v/c)
.
An important distinction from
[
22
]
is that we here consider the special relativistic limit of the four-momentum-conservative general relativistic model from
[
13
]
, while
[
22
]
considered the
\mathcal{O}(v/c)
limit of the
number-conservative
general relativistic two-moment model (see, e.g., Section 4.7.3 in
[
1
]
).
While the relativistic four-momentum-conservative and number-conservative two-moment models are analytically equivalent at the continuum level, this is not the case for their respective
\mathcal{O}(v/c)
limits, which differ to
\mathcal{O}(v^{2}/c^{2})

[
22
]
.
Moreover, the two formulations may possess different characteristics of relevance to their respective discrete representations.
One benefit of the four-momentum-conservative model is that, in the absence of collisions, both the zeroth and first moment equations are in conservative form, while this is true only for the zeroth moment equation of the number-conservative model.
In
[
22
]
, the appearance of non-collisional source terms in the first moment equation introduced obstacles to proving the realizability-preserving property of the fully discrete multidimensional scheme (see
[
22
, Section 5.1.3]
).
The extension of the scheme proposed in
[
22
]
for the
\mathcal{O}(v/c)
number-conservative two-moment model to special relativity is also faced with these obstacles.
Since the realizability-preserving property is closely linked to numerical stability and conservation properties of two-moment solvers, one of the main objectives of this study is to consider this important issue in the context of the four-momentum-conservative two-moment model in special relativity.
The successful outcome in this context presents an important step towards robust methods for general relativistic two-moment models.
We also note that the numerical study of CCSNe in
[
51
]
was performed
without
velocity-dependent terms due to observed numerical instabilities when velocity dependence was included.
Although the origin of these instabilities is unknown to us, their link to velocity-dependent terms reveals additional challenges associated with their inclusion.

We consider a special relativistic two-moment model in Cartesian spatial coordinates, where the evolution of the spectral radiation four-momentum is governed by

(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f\,p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\,\frac{d\omega}{\varepsilon},

(2a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{\mu\nu\rho}

The fluid four-velocity, the four-velocity of a Lagrangian/comoving observer, has the
Eulerian decomposition

(5)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\nu}

\displaystyle=\delta^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\nu}+n^{\mu}n_{\nu},

\displaystyle h^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\nu}

\displaystyle=\delta^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\nu}+u^{\mu}u_{\nu},

\displaystyle\gamma^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\nu}n^{\nu}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\gamma^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\nu}u^{\nu}

\displaystyle h^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\nu}u^{\nu}

\displaystyle=0,\quad h^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\nu}p^{\nu}

\{\,\mathsf{E},\mathsf{F}^{i},\mathsf{S}^{ij}\,\}=4\pi\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\{\,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}^{i},\mathcal{S}^{ij}\,\}\,\varepsilon^{2}\,d\varepsilon.

(24)

We point out that the system in Eq. (
22
) is consistent with the following evolution equation for the Eulerian number density
[
13
]

\partial_{t}\mathcal{N}+\partial_{i}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{N}}^{i}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\partial_{\varepsilon}\big(\,\varepsilon^{2}\,\mathcal{T}^{\nu\rho}\,\partial_{\nu}u_{\rho}\,\big)=\chi\,\big(\mathcal{J}_{\mbox{\tiny Eq}}-\mathcal{J}\big)/\varepsilon,

(25)

where the Eulerian number density is given by

\mathcal{N}=W(\mathcal{E}-v^{i}\mathcal{F}_{i})/\varepsilon=(W\mathcal{J}+v^{i}\mathcal{H}_{i})/\varepsilon

(26)

and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{N}}^{i}=(\mathcal{H}^{i}+W\mathcal{J}v^{i})/\varepsilon
.
In the absence of collisions, Eq. (
25
) is a phase-space conservation law for the spectral number density.
Then, by further integrating Eq. (
25
) over particle energy, we obtain a conservation law for the Eulerian-frame number

\partial_{t}\mathsf{N}+\partial_{i}\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{N}}^{i}=0,

(27)

where the Eulerian-frame gray number density and number flux are defined as

\{\mathsf{N},\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{N}}^{i}\}=4\pi\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\{\mathcal{N},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{N}}^{i}\}\,\varepsilon^{2}d\varepsilon.

(28)

As elaborated in detail in
[
13
]
(see also Section 6.5.4 in
[
1
]
for the special relativistic case considered here), the analytical relationship between the system in Eq. (
22
) and Eq. (
25
), as can be inferred from Eq. (
26
), is nontrivial.
It involves exact cancellation of terms remaining after bringing
W/\varepsilon
and
Wv^{i}/\varepsilon
inside the phase-space divergences in Eq. (
22
).
When solving the system in Eq. (
22
) numerically, maintaining consistency with Eq. (
25
) at the discrete level for
simultaneous
four-momentum and number conservation is challenging, and, to our knowledge, remains an open problem.
Capturing this consistency is not a focus of this paper, but we monitor the number density in some of the test cases considered in Section
VI
.

Remark 1
.

As a simplification to make our analysis more tractable, we assume that the material background is steady; i.e., the fluid four-velocity (
u^{\mu}
), the emissivity (
\eta
), the absorption and scattering opacities (
\chi
and
\sigma
), and the equilibrium density (
\mathcal{J}_{\mbox{\tiny Eq}}
) are assumed to be independent of time.
Without these simplifying assumptions, the spectral two-moment model should be coupled to equations for relativistic hydrodynamics (e.g.,
[
52
]
), which is beyond the scope of this paper.
We discuss the relevance of our work to this more general case in Section
VII
.

II.2
Moment Closure

The two-moment model given by Eqs. (
22a
)-(
22b
) contains higher order moments, and is not closed.
To close the system, the higher-order moments are determined through a closure procedure.
Here, the pressure tensor
\mathcal{K}^{\mu\nu}
is obtained from the lower-order moments as (e.g.,
[
53
,
54
]
)

\mathcal{K}^{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\,\Big[\,(1-\mathsf{k})\,h^{\mu\nu}+(3\mathsf{k}-1)\,\widehat{\mathsf{h}}^{\mu}\widehat{\mathsf{h}}^{\nu}\,\Big]\,\mathcal{J},

(29)

where
\mathsf{k}
is the Eddington factor (in general a function of the flux factor
\mathsf{h}=\mathcal{H}/\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{J}
, where
\mathcal{H}=\sqrt{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}\mathcal{H}^{\mu}}
) and
\widehat{\mathsf{h}}^{\mu}=\mathcal{H}^{\mu}/\mathcal{H}
.
The pressure tensor in Eq. (
29
) satisfies the trace condition

\mathcal{K}^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\mu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}\,\mathcal{K}^{\mu\nu}=\mathcal{J}.

(30)

Moreover, since
\widehat{\mathsf{h}}_{\mu}\,\widehat{\mathsf{h}}_{\nu}\,\mathcal{K}^{\mu\nu}=\mathsf{k}\,\mathcal{J}
, the definition of
\mathcal{K}^{\mu\nu}
in Eq. (
18
) implies that the Eddington factor is given by

\mathsf{k}=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f\,(\widehat{\mathsf{h}}_{\mu}\ell^{\mu})^{2}\,d\omega}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}fd\omega}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathfrak{f}(\mu)\mu^{2}\,d\mu}{\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathfrak{f}(\mu)\,d\mu},

(31)

where we have defined

\mathfrak{f}(\mu)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(\mu,\varphi)\,d\varphi.

(32)

With this definition, the momentum-space coordinates in the co-moving frame have been aligned with
\widehat{\mathsf{h}}_{\mu}
so that
\widehat{\mathsf{h}}_{\mu}\ell^{\mu}=\cos\vartheta=\mu
.
Below, a functional form is imposed on
\mathfrak{f}(\mu)
, which allows the Eddington factor to be computed.

The energy derivatives in Eqs. (
22a
)-(
22b
) contain projections of the rank-three tensor
\mathcal{Q}^{\mu\nu\rho}
, which has the Lagrangian decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{\mu\nu\rho}/\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}\,u^{\mu}\,u^{\nu}\,u^{\rho}+\mathcal{H}^{\mu}\,u^{\nu}\,u^{\rho}+\mathcal{H}^{\nu}\,u^{\mu}\,u^{\rho}+\mathcal{H}^{\rho}\,u^{\mu}\,u^{\nu}

\displaystyle\hskip 12.0pt+\mathcal{K}^{\mu\nu}\,u^{\rho}+\mathcal{K}^{\mu\rho}\,u^{\nu}+\mathcal{K}^{\nu\rho}\,u^{\mu}+\mathcal{L}^{\mu\nu\rho},

(33)

where

\mathcal{L}^{\mu\nu\rho}=\frac{\varepsilon}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f\ell^{\mu}\ell^{\nu}\ell^{\rho}d\omega.

(34)

In analogy with Eq. (
29
), the rank-three (heat flux) tensor is written as (e.g.,
[
55
,
16
]
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\mu\nu\rho}=\frac{1}{2}\,\Big[\,

\displaystyle(\mathsf{h}-\mathsf{q})\,\big(\,\widehat{\mathsf{h}}^{\mu}\,h^{\nu\rho}+\widehat{\mathsf{h}}^{\nu}\,h^{\mu\rho}+\widehat{\mathsf{h}}^{\rho}\,h^{\mu\nu}\,\big)

\displaystyle+(5\mathsf{q}-3\mathsf{h})\,\widehat{\mathsf{h}}^{\mu}\,\widehat{\mathsf{h}}^{\nu}\,\widehat{\mathsf{h}}^{\rho}\,\Big]\,\mathcal{J},

(35)

where
\mathsf{q}
is the heat flux factor (also a function of
\mathsf{h}
and
\mathcal{J}
).
The heat flux tensor in Eq. (
35
) satisfies the trace conditions

\mathcal{L}^{\mu\nu}_{\hskip 8.0pt\nu}=\eta_{\nu\rho}\,\mathcal{L}^{\mu\nu\rho}=\mathcal{H}^{\mu}.

(36)

Then, since
\widehat{\mathsf{h}}_{\mu}\,\widehat{\mathsf{h}}_{\nu}\,\widehat{\mathsf{h}}_{\rho}\,\mathcal{L}^{\mu\nu\rho}=\mathsf{q}\,\mathcal{J}
, the definition in Eq. (
34
) gives the heat flux factor

\mathsf{q}=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f\,(\widehat{\mathsf{h}}_{\mu}\ell^{\mu})^{3}\,d\omega}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}fd\omega}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathfrak{f}(\mu)\mu^{3}\,d\mu}{\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathfrak{f}(\mu)\,d\mu}.

(37)

The moment model in Eq. (
22
) is closed when
\mathsf{k}
and
\mathsf{q}
are specified in terms of
\mathsf{h}
and
\mathcal{J}
.
We determine
\mathsf{k}
and
\mathsf{q}
using the maximum entropy closure (see, e.g.,
[
14
,
15
,
56
,
57
]
).
In this approach,
\mathsf{k}
and
\mathsf{q}
are determined by finding a distribution function
\mathfrak{f}_{\mbox{\tiny ME}}
that maximizes the entropy and recovers the lower order moments
\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{H}=\widehat{\mathsf{h}}_{\mu}\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
.
In the simple case of Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics, the maximum entropy distribution has the general form
[
14
]

\mathfrak{f}_{\mbox{\tiny ME}}(\mu)=\exp(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\mu).

(38)

Given
\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{H}
, direct integration of
\mathfrak{f}_{\mbox{\tiny ME}}
yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathfrak{f}_{\mbox{\tiny ME}}(\mu)\,d\mu=

\displaystyle e^{\alpha_{0}}\sinh(\alpha_{1})/\alpha_{1},

(39)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathfrak{f}_{\mbox{\tiny ME}}(\mu)\mu\,d\mu=

\displaystyle e^{\alpha_{0}}(\alpha_{1}\cosh(\alpha_{1})-\sinh(\alpha_{1}))/\alpha_{1}^{2},

(40)

which can be solved for
\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha_{1}
.
In particular, the known flux factor,
\mathsf{h}
, can be expressed in terms of the Langevin function on
\alpha_{1}

\mathsf{h}=\coth(\alpha_{1})-1/\alpha_{1}=\vcentcolon L(\alpha_{1}).

(41)

The coefficient
\alpha_{1}(\mathsf{h})=L^{-1}(\mathsf{h})
is then obtained by inverting the Langevin function.
Once
\alpha_{1}
is obtained,
\alpha_{0}
and
\mathfrak{f}_{\mbox{\tiny ME}}
are easily obtained.
Then, by directly integrating
\mathfrak{f}_{\mbox{\tiny ME}}
, we have

\mathsf{k}(\mathsf{h})=1-\frac{2\mathsf{h}}{\alpha_{1}(\mathsf{h})},\quad\mathsf{q}(\mathsf{h})=\coth(\alpha_{1}(\mathsf{h}))-\frac{3\mathsf{k}(\mathsf{h})}{\alpha_{1}(\mathsf{h})}.

(42)

This is the closure given by Minerbo.

Inverting the Langevin function requires numerical root finding, which can be costly.
In practice we use polynomial approximations to
\mathsf{k}
and
\mathsf{q}
in terms of
\mathsf{h}
.
This leads to the computationally more efficient algebraic expressions
[
15
,
16
]

\displaystyle\mathsf{k}(\mathsf{h})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}+\frac{2}{15}(3\mathsf{h}^{2}-\mathsf{h}^{3}+3\mathsf{h}^{4}),

(43)

\displaystyle\mathsf{q}(\mathsf{h})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{h}}{75}(45+10\mathsf{h}-12\mathsf{h}^{2}-12\mathsf{h}^{3}+38\mathsf{h}^{4}-12\mathsf{h}^{5}+18\mathsf{h}^{6}).

(44)

The algebraic expression for
\mathsf{k}
is accurate to within one percent, while the expression for
\mathsf{q}
is accurate to within three percent (e.g.,
[
22
]
).

II.3
Tetrad Formalism

In the following, it will sometimes be useful to appeal to the tetrad formalism (e.g.,
[
58
,
59
]
), which relates components of coordinate basis four-vectors and tensors (e.g.,
p^{\mu}
) to corresponding components in a local orthonormal frame comoving with the fluid (indices adorned with a ‘hat’; e.g.,
p^{\hat{\mu}}
)

p^{\mu}=\mathcal{L}^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{\mu}}\,p^{\hat{\mu}},\quad\text{where}\quad p^{\hat{\mu}}=\varepsilon\,\big(1,\ell^{\hat{\imath}}\big),

(45)

and where

\ell^{\hat{\mu}}=\big(\,0,\,\ell^{\hat{\imath}}\,\big)=\big(\,0,\,\cos\vartheta,\,\sin\vartheta\cos\varphi,\,\sin\vartheta\sin\varphi\,\big)

(46)

is a unit spatial four-vector with spatial components parallel to the particle three-momentum in the orthonormal comoving frame.

In the special relativistic case with Cartesian spatial coordinates considered here, the transformation
\mathcal{L}^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{\mu}}
from the orthonormal comoving basis to the coordinate basis is simply the Lorentz transformation

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{\mu}}=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{L}^{0}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{0}}&\mathcal{L}^{0}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{\imath}}\\
\mathcal{L}^{i}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{0}}&\mathcal{L}^{i}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{\imath}}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}W&WV_{\hat{\imath}}\\
WV^{i}&\delta^{i}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{\imath}}+(W-1)(V^{i}/V)(V_{\hat{\imath}}/V)\end{array}\right),

We follow
[
13
, Appendix B]
, where
V=\sqrt{v^{\mu}v_{\mu}}=\sqrt{v^{i}v_{i}}
and

V^{1}=v^{1}=V_{\hat{1}},\quad V^{2}=v^{2}=V_{\hat{2}},\quad V^{3}=v^{3}=V_{\hat{3}}

(51)

are three-velocity parameters in the Lorentz boost (not to be viewed here as components of four-vectors). This is expressed to be consistent with our general index convention, where unadorned indices are used to denote components of coordinate basis four-vectors while indices with a hat denote components of four-vectors expressed in an orthonormal basis comoving with the fluid.
The inverse transformation (obtained by replacing
V^{i}\to-V^{\hat{\imath}}
,
V_{\hat{\imath}}\to-V_{i}
, and
\delta^{i}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{\imath}}\to\delta^{\hat{\imath}}_{\hskip 4.0pti}
in Eq. (
II.3
)) is denoted
\mathcal{L}^{\hat{\mu}}_{\hskip 4.0pt\mu}
, and satisfies
\mathcal{L}^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{\mu}}\mathcal{L}^{\hat{\mu}}_{\hskip 4.0pt\nu}=\delta^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\nu}
.

Using Eq. (
45
) to write

p^{\mu}=\varepsilon\,(\mathcal{L}^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{0}}+\mathcal{L}^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{\imath}}\ell^{\hat{\imath}}),

(52)

a comparison with Eq. (
8
) shows that

u^{\mu}=\mathcal{L}^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{0}}=\mathcal{L}^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{\mu}}u^{\hat{\mu}}\quad\text{and}\quad\ell^{\mu}=\mathcal{L}^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{\imath}}\ell^{\hat{\imath}}=\mathcal{L}^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{\mu}}\ell^{\hat{\mu}}.

(53)

(Note that
u^{\hat{\mu}}=(1,0,0,0)
.)
Using Eq. (
53
), we can, as an example, write
\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
in Eq. (
18
) as

\mathcal{H}^{\mu}=\mathcal{L}^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{\mu}}\,\mathcal{H}^{\hat{\mu}},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{H}^{\hat{\mu}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f\,\ell^{\hat{\mu}}\,d\omega.

(54)

II.4
Moment Realizability

In this paper, we aim to design a realizability-preserving numerical method for the two-moment model in Eq. (
22
).
In the relativistic setting, multiple moment pairs are encountered; e.g., the Lagrangian moments
(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{H}_{\mu})^{\intercal}
and the Eulerian moments
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{\mu})^{\intercal}
.
To introduce the concept of moment realizability, we first define the set of non-negative distribution functions

\mathfrak{R}=\Big\{\,f~\big|~f\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f\,d\omega>0\,\Big\}.

(55)

(I.e., we avoid the trivial case where
f
is zero everywhere.)
The moments
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{M}}=(\mathscr{J},\mathscr{H}_{\mu})^{\intercal}
, defined by

\mathscr{J}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}g\,d\omega\quad\text{and}\quad\mathscr{H}_{\mu}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}g\,\mathscr{L}_{\mu}\,d\omega,

(56)

where
\mathscr{L}_{\mu}(\omega)
is a spacelike unit four-vector (
\mathscr{L}_{\mu}\mathscr{L}^{\mu}=1
), are said to be realizable if they arise from a distribution function
g\in\mathfrak{R}
, and we define the set of realizable moments
\mathcal{R}
as

\mathcal{R}=\big\{\,\boldsymbol{\mathscr{M}}=(\mathscr{J},\mathscr{H}_{\mu})^{\intercal}\,\,\rvert\,\,\mathscr{J}>0\text{ and }\gamma(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{M}})\geq 0\,\big\},

(57)

where
\gamma(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{M}})=\mathscr{J}-\sqrt{\mathscr{H}_{\mu}\mathscr{H}^{\mu}}
.
It is straightforward to verify that
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{M}}\in\mathcal{R}
if and only if there exists an underlying distribution
g
satisfying Eq. (
56
) that is in
\mathfrak{R}
.

From the definition of the realizable set in Eq. (
57
), the set of all realizable moments forms a convex cone.
As a consequence, we have the following lemma

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathscr{M}^{(a)}=(\mathscr{J}^{(a)},\mathscr{H}^{(a)}_{\mu})^{\intercal}
and
\mathscr{M}^{(b)}=(\mathscr{J}^{(b)},\mathscr{H}^{(b)}_{\mu})^{\intercal}
be realizable moments.
For
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}>0
, define
\mathscr{M}^{(c)}\vcentcolon=\theta_{1}\mathscr{M}^{(a)}+\theta_{2}\mathscr{M}^{(b)}
.
Then
\mathscr{M}^{(c)}\in\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathscr{M}^{(a)}\in\mathcal{R}
, there exists an
f^{(a)}\in\mathfrak{R}
such that

\mathscr{J}^{(a)}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f^{(a)}\,d\omega\quad\text{and}\quad\mathscr{H}^{(a)}_{\mu}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f^{(a)}\mathscr{L}^{\mu}\,d\omega.

There exists an analogous
f^{(b)}\in\mathfrak{R}
for
\mathscr{M}^{(b)}
.
Let
f^{(c)}=\theta_{1}f^{(a)}+\theta_{2}f^{(b)}
.
Then, since
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}>0
,
f^{(c)}\in\mathfrak{R}
, and thus
\mathscr{M}^{(c)}
is realizable as

\mathscr{J}^{(c)}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f^{(c)}\,d\omega\quad\text{and}\quad\mathscr{H}^{(c)}_{\mu}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f^{(c)}\mathscr{L}^{\mu}\,d\omega.

∎

Corollary 1
.

Let
\mathscr{M}\in\mathcal{R}
. Then, for
\theta>0
,
\theta\mathscr{M}\in\mathcal{R}
.

Remark 2
.

The definition of the realizable set in Eq. (
57
) is made with respect to the
generic
moment pair in Eq. (
56
).
In Propositions
1
-
4
we prove the realizability of
specific
moment pairs that appear in the analysis of our proposed numerical method.

Proposition 1
.

Let the moments
\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\hat{\mu}}
be defined as in Eqs. (
18
) and (
54
), respectively,
where
\varepsilon>0
,
f\in\mathfrak{R}
, and
\ell^{\hat{\mu}}
is defined in Eq. (
46
).
Then
(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{H}_{\hat{\mu}})^{\intercal}\in\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

Since
\varepsilon f\in\mathfrak{R}
it follows that
\mathcal{J}>0
.
Using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\hat{\mu}}\mathcal{H}^{\hat{\mu}}=\mathcal{H}_{\hat{\imath}}\mathcal{H}^{\hat{\imath}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{4\pi}\Big)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f\ell_{\hat{\imath}}d\omega\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f\ell^{\hat{\imath}}d\omega

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{4\pi}\Big)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}fd\omega\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f\ell_{\hat{\imath}}\ell^{\hat{\imath}}d\omega=\mathcal{J}^{2}.

Taking the square root on both sides completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 2
.

Let the angular moments
\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
be defined as in Eq. (
18
) with
\varepsilon f\in\mathfrak{R}
.
Then,
(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{H}_{\mu})^{\intercal}\in\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

Since
\varepsilon f\in\mathfrak{R}
, positivity of
\mathcal{J}
follows from Proposition
1
.
Lorentz invariance of the inner product and using Proposition
1
gives

\mathcal{H}^{2}=\mathcal{H}_{\mu}\mathcal{H}^{\mu}=\mathcal{L}^{\hat{\mu}}_{\hskip 4.0pt\mu}\,\mathcal{L}^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{\nu}}\,\mathcal{H}_{\hat{\mu}}\,\mathcal{H}^{\hat{\nu}}=\delta^{\hat{\mu}}_{\hskip 4.0pt\hat{\nu}}\,\mathcal{H}_{\hat{\mu}}\,\mathcal{H}^{\hat{\nu}}=\mathcal{H}_{\hat{\mu}}\,\mathcal{H}^{\hat{\mu}}\leq\mathcal{J}^{2}.

Taking the square root on both sides completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3
.

Let the angular moments
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}^{\mu}
be defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=n_{\mu}\,u_{\nu}\,\mathcal{T}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}E\,f\,d\omega

(58a)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{F}}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=-n_{\nu}\,h^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\rho}\,\mathcal{T}^{\nu\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}E\,f\,\ell^{\mu}\,d\omega,

(58b)

respectively, where
\mathcal{T}^{\mu\nu}
is given in Eq. (
2
) with
f\in\mathfrak{R}
, and
E>0
is defined in Eq. (
14
).
Then,
(\widehat{\mathcal{E}},\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mu})^{\intercal}\in\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

Since
E>0
, it follows that
E\,f\in\mathfrak{R}
, so that
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}>0
.
Using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{1}{4\pi}\Big)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}E\,f\,\ell_{\hat{\imath}}\,d\omega\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}E\,f\,\ell^{\hat{\imath}}\,d\omega

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{1}{4\pi}\Big)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}E\,f\,d\omega\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}E\,f\,\ell_{\hat{\imath}}\ell^{\hat{\imath}}\,d\omega=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{2}.

Taking the square root on both sides completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4
.

Let the angular moments
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\mu}
be defined as in Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=n_{\mu}n_{\nu}\mathcal{T}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(E^{2}/\varepsilon)\,f\,d\omega

(59a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=-\gamma^{\mu}_{\hskip 4.0pt\nu}n_{\rho}\mathcal{T}^{\nu\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(E^{2}/\varepsilon)\,f\,L^{\mu}\,d\omega,

(59b)

where
f\in\mathfrak{R}
and
E,\varepsilon>0
.
Then
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{\mu})^{\intercal}\in\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

Since
E,\varepsilon>0
and
f\in\mathfrak{R}
, it follows that
g\vcentcolon=(E^{2}/\varepsilon)f\in\mathfrak{R}
and
\mathcal{E}>0
.
Using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{2}=\mathcal{F}_{i}\mathcal{F}^{i}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{1}{4\pi}\Big)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}g\,L_{i}\,d\omega\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}g\,L^{i}\,d\omega

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{1}{4\pi}\Big)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}g\,d\omega\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}g\,L_{i}\,L^{i}\,d\omega=\mathcal{E}^{2}.

Taking the square root on both sides completes the proof.
∎

III
Numerical Method

In this section, we present the DG-IMEX method for the two-moment model in Eq. (
22
).
The semi-discretization of the two-moment model with the DG method is provided in Section
III.1
, while Section
III.2
details the integration of the semi-discrete DG scheme with IMEX time-stepping.

III.1
Discontinous Galerkin Phase-Space Discretization

To discretize the phase-space of Eq. (
22
), we divide the phase-space domain
\Omega=\Omega_{\varepsilon}\times\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}
into a disjoint union
\mathcal{T}
of open elements
\mathbf{K}=K_{\varepsilon}\times\mathbf{K}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, so that
\Omega=\cup_{\mathbf{K}\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbf{K}
.
Here,
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is the energy domain, and
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is the
d
-dimensional spatial domain.
We define the DG spatial and energy elements as

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\{\,\boldsymbol{x}:x^{i}\in K^{i}=(x^{i}_{\mbox{\tiny L}},x^{i}_{\mbox{\tiny H}})\,\,\text{ for }\,\,i=1,\ldots,d\,\}

(60)

\displaystyle K_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=(\varepsilon_{\mbox{\tiny L}},\varepsilon_{\mbox{\tiny H}}),

(61)

respectively.
We denote the volume of the DG element as

|\mathbf{K}|=\int_{\mathbf{K}}\varepsilon^{2}\,d\varepsilon\,d\boldsymbol{x},\quad\text{where}\quad d\boldsymbol{x}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}dx^{i}.

(62)

The length of individual spatial elements is given by
|K^{i}|=\int_{K^{i}}dx^{i}=x^{i}_{\mbox{\tiny H}}-x^{i}_{\mbox{\tiny L}}=\Delta x^{i}
.
In the energy dimension we let
|K_{\varepsilon}|=\int_{K^{\varepsilon}}\varepsilon^{2}d\varepsilon=\frac{1}{3}(\varepsilon_{\mbox{\tiny H}}^{3}-\varepsilon_{\mbox{\tiny L}}^{3})
and
\Delta\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\mbox{\tiny H}}-\varepsilon_{\mbox{\tiny L}}
.
We introduce the notation
\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}^{i}=K_{\varepsilon}\times\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{i}
, where
\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{i}=(\times_{j\neq i}K^{j})
, and
d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{i}=\prod_{j\neq i}dx^{j}
to define the surface elements orthogonal to the
i
th spatial direction.
We also define
|\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}^{i}|=|K_{\varepsilon}|\times(\prod_{j\neq i}|K^{j}|)
.
Finally we let
\boldsymbol{z}=(\varepsilon,\boldsymbol{x})
denote the phase-space coordinate, and define
d\boldsymbol{z}=d\varepsilon\,d\boldsymbol{x}
and
d\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}^{i}=d\varepsilon\,d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{i}
.

We let the approximation space for the DG method be

\mathbb{V}^{k}_{h}(\Omega)=\{\phi_{h}:\phi_{h}\rvert_{\mathbf{K}}\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}(\mathbf{K}),\,\forall\mathbf{K}\in\mathcal{T}\},

(63)

where, locally on
\mathbf{K}
,
\mathbb{Q}^{k}(\mathbf{K})
is the tensor product of one-dimensional polynomials of maximal degree
k
.

To write the two-moment model in Eq. (
22
) compactly, we define

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{E}\\
\mathcal{F}_{j}\end{bmatrix},

(64a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{i}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{F}^{i}\\
\mathcal{S}^{i}_{\hskip 4.0ptj}\end{bmatrix},

(64b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}-n_{\mu}\,\mathcal{Q}^{\mu\nu\rho}\,\partial_{\nu}u_{\rho}\\
\gamma_{j\mu}\,\mathcal{Q}^{\mu\nu\rho}\,\partial_{\nu}u_{\rho}\end{bmatrix},

(64c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}W\chi\,(\mathcal{J}_{\mbox{\tiny Eq}}-\mathcal{J})-\kappa\,v^{i}\mathcal{H}_{i}\\
-\kappa\,\mathcal{H}_{j}+Wv_{j}\,\chi\,(\mathcal{J}_{\mbox{\tiny Eq}}-\mathcal{J})\end{bmatrix}.

(64d)

Then, with the closure specified in Section
II.2
, Eq. (
22
) can be written as

\partial_{t}\mathbf{U}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}\big(\,\mathbf{F}^{i}(\mathbf{U})\,\big)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\partial_{\varepsilon}\big(\,\varepsilon^{3}\,\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{U})\,\big)=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{U}).

(65)

The semi-discrete DG problem for Eq. (
65
) is to find
\mathbf{U}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}^{k}_{h}(\Omega)
, where
\mathbf{U}_{h}
approximates
\mathbf{U}
, such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{K}}\partial_{t}\mathbf{U}_{h}\,\phi_{h}\,\varepsilon^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle\hskip 12.0pt+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Big\{\,\int_{\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}^{i}}\big[\,\widehat{\mathbf{F}}^{i}(\mathbf{U}_{h})\,\phi_{h}\rvert_{x^{i}_{\mbox{\tiny H}}}-\widehat{\mathbf{F}}^{i}(\mathbf{U}_{h})\phi_{h}\rvert_{x^{i}_{\mbox{\tiny L}}}\,\big]\varepsilon^{2}\,d\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}^{i}

\displaystyle\hskip 48.0pt-\int_{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{F}^{i}(\mathbf{U}_{h})\partial_{i}\phi_{h}\,\varepsilon^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{z}\,\Big\}

\displaystyle\hskip 12.0pt+\int_{\mathbf{K}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\big[\,\varepsilon^{3}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{U}_{h})\phi_{h}\rvert_{\varepsilon_{\mbox{\tiny H}}}-\varepsilon^{3}\widehat{\mathbf{F}}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{U}_{h})\phi_{h}\rvert_{\varepsilon_{\mbox{\tiny L}}}\,\big]\,d\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle\hskip 12.0pt-\int_{\mathbf{K}}\varepsilon^{3}\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{U}_{h})\partial_{\varepsilon}\phi_{h}\,d{\boldsymbol{z}}=\int_{\mathbf{K}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{U}_{h})\phi_{h}\varepsilon^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{z},

(66)

holds for all
\phi_{h}\in\mathbb{V}^{k}_{h}(\Omega)
and all
\mathbf{K}\in\mathcal{T}
.
In Eq. (
66
), the numerical fluxes,
\widehat{\mathbf{F}}^{i}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{F}}^{\varepsilon}
, approximate
\mathbf{F}^{i}
and
\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}
, respectively, on the surface of
\mathbf{K}
, and are given by Lax–Friedrichs-like fluxes.
The numerical flux in the
i
th spatial dimension is

\widehat{\mathbf{F}}^{i}\rvert_{x}=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\,(\mathbf{F}^{i}\rvert_{x^{-}}+\mathbf{F}^{i}\rvert_{x^{+}})-a^{i}\,(\mathbf{U}_{h}\rvert_{x^{+}}-\mathbf{U}_{h}\rvert_{x^{-}})\,\Big],

(67)

where
x^{\pm}=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}x\pm\delta
, and
a^{i}>0
is an estimate of the spectral radius of the flux Jacobian matrix
\partial\mathbf{F}^{i}/\partial\mathbf{U}
. In practice we always assume
a^{i}=1
.
To compute the fluxes
\mathbf{F}^{i}
, which requires closure evaluations, we first compute the
primitive moments

\mathbf{M}=(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{H}_{i})^{\intercal}
from the
conserved moments

\mathbf{U}
.
This procedure is described in detail in Section
IV.1
.

The numerical flux in the energy dimension is given by

\widehat{\mathbf{F}}^{\varepsilon}\rvert_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\,(\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}\rvert_{\varepsilon^{-}}+\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}\rvert_{\varepsilon^{+}})-a^{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\rvert_{\varepsilon^{+}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\rvert_{\varepsilon^{-}})\,\Big],

(68)

where
\varepsilon^{\pm}=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\varepsilon\pm\delta
,

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{j}\end{bmatrix}\vcentcolon=\begin{bmatrix}n_{\mu}u_{\nu}\mathcal{T}^{\mu\nu}\\
-\gamma_{j\mu}u_{\nu}\mathcal{T}^{\mu\nu}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}W\mathcal{J}+v^{i}\mathcal{H}_{i}\\
\mathcal{H}_{j}+W\mathcal{J}v_{j}\end{bmatrix},

(69)

and
a^{\varepsilon}\geq|q|
with the quantity
q
defined as

q\vcentcolon=\frac{p^{\nu}p^{\rho}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\partial_{\nu}u_{\rho}=k^{\nu}k^{\rho}\partial_{\nu}u_{\rho},

(70)

where
k^{\mu}=p^{\mu}/\varepsilon
.
To compute
\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}
, we again compute
\mathbf{M}=(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{H}_{i})^{\intercal}
from
\mathbf{U}
(discussed in Section
IV.1
).
The processes by which we evaluate
\partial_{\nu}u_{\rho}
and
a^{\varepsilon}
are discussed in the following paragraphs.

To compute
\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}
, we need to compute the derivatives of the four-velocity,
u_{\mu}
.
In this paper, as in
[
22
]
, we assume the four-velocity is independent of time, i.e.,
\partial_{t}u_{\mu}=0
.
Let
(u_{\mu})_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{k}(\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}})
denote the approximation of
u_{\mu}
.
We compute the derivatives,
(\partial_{i}u_{\mu})_{h}\in\mathbb{V}^{k}_{h}(\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}})
, by demanding that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{K}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}(\partial_{i}u_{\mu})_{h}\phi_{h}\,d\boldsymbol{x}=

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{i}}\big[\,\widehat{u}_{\mu}\,\phi_{h}\rvert_{x^{i}_{\mbox{\tiny H}}}-\widehat{u}_{\mu}\,\phi_{h}\rvert_{x^{i}_{\mbox{\tiny L}}}\,\big]\,d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{i}

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbf{K}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}(u_{\mu})_{h}\,\partial_{i}\phi_{h}\,d\boldsymbol{x},

(71)

holds for all
\phi_{h}\in\mathbb{V}^{k}_{h}(\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}})
, and where velocity on the element boundaries is approximated with the average

\widehat{u}_{\mu}(x^{i},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{i})=\frac{1}{2}\big[\,(u_{\mu})_{h}(x^{i,-},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{i})+(u_{\mu})_{h}(x^{i,+},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{i})\,\big].

(72)

For the numerical flux in Eq. (
68
), we require that
a^{\varepsilon}\geq|q|
.
Thus, we need an upper bound for
|q|
.
To this end, we first express
q
in terms of a contraction with a symmetric tensor
A_{\nu\rho}

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=k^{\nu}k^{\rho}\partial_{\nu}u_{\rho}

\displaystyle=k^{\nu}k^{\rho}\Big[\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\nu}u_{\rho}+\partial_{\rho}u_{\nu})+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\nu}u_{\rho}-\partial_{\rho}u_{\nu})\Big]

\displaystyle=k^{\nu}k^{\rho}A_{\nu\rho},

(73)

where
A_{\nu\rho}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\nu}u_{\rho}+\partial_{\rho}u_{\nu})
, since the contraction of the symmetric
k^{\nu}k^{\rho}
with the antisymmetric
\partial_{\nu}u_{\rho}-\partial_{\rho}u_{\nu}
vanishes.
Using an Eulerian decomposition of
A_{\nu\rho}
, we can derive an upper bound for
q
.
Next, we introduce the Eulerian decomposition

A_{\mu\nu}=An_{\mu}n_{\nu}+B_{\mu}n_{\nu}+B_{\nu}n_{\mu}+C_{\mu\nu},

(74)

where
n_{\mu}B^{\mu}=0
and
n_{\mu}C^{\mu\nu}=0=n_{\nu}C^{\mu\nu}
.
Using Eqs. (
13
) and (
74
), we have

q=\frac{E^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,\big(\,A-2L^{i}B_{i}+L^{i}L^{j}C_{ij}\,\big),

(75)

where the components of the Eulerian decomposition in Eq. (
74
) can be expressed in terms of derivatives of the three-velocity as

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=n_{\mu}n_{\nu}A^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}W,

(76)

\displaystyle B_{i}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{i\mu}n_{\nu}A^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\,\big(\,\partial_{t}(Wv_{i})+\partial_{i}(-W)\,\big),

(77)

\displaystyle C_{ij}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{i\mu}\gamma_{j\nu}A^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\big(\,\partial_{i}(Wv_{j})+\partial_{j}(Wv_{i})\,\big).

(78)

Then we bound
|q|
as

|q|\leq\frac{E^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,\big(\,|A|+2\sqrt{B_{i}B^{i}}+\rho(C_{ij})\,\big),

(79)

where the inequality follows from the triangle inequality, Cauchy–Schwarz, and using
L_{i}L^{i}=1
.
Here,
\rho(C_{ij})
denotes the spectral radius of
C_{ij}
.
Since
C_{ij}
is a symmetric
3\times 3
matrix, we compute its eigenvalues directly to determine
\rho(C_{ij})
.
The quantity
E/\varepsilon
depends on
\ell^{\mu}
(see Eq. (
14
)), which in practice is not known.
We have that

\displaystyle(E/\varepsilon)^{2}=(W+v^{\mu}\ell_{\mu})^{2}

\displaystyle=(W+Wv^{\hat{\imath}}\ell_{\hat{\imath}})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq W^{2}(1+v)^{2}=\frac{(1+v)}{(1-v)},

(80)

where
v=\sqrt{v_{i}v^{i}}
.
Hence

|q|\leq\Big(\frac{1+v}{1-v}\Big)\,\big(\,|A|+2\sqrt{B_{i}B^{i}}+\rho(C_{ij})\,\big)=\vcentcolon a_{\max}^{\varepsilon}.

(81)

In Eq. (
68
), we use this upper bound and set
a^{\varepsilon}\vcentcolon=a_{\max}^{\varepsilon}
.
We provide a discussion on the tightness of the upper bound
a_{\max}^{\varepsilon}
in Appendix
A
.

Remark 3
.

While we assume that the fluid four-velocity is independent of time in this paper, this assumption is not a fundamental limitation of the proposed method.
Specifically, the bound on
|q|
in Eq. (
81
) is still valid for the case when the four-velocity is time dependent.
Thus, extension of the proposed method will require an approximation of
\partial_{t}u_{\mu}
to be included in the numerical flux in Eq. (
68
).
This inclusion will increase
a_{\max}^{\varepsilon}
(and
a^{\varepsilon}
), and potentially decrease the time step needed to preserve realizability of the cell average, given later in Proposition

.

We use quadratures — constructed by tensorization of one-dimensional quadratures — to evaluate integrals over the multi-dimensional elements in Eq. (
66
).
We denote the
N
-point Legendre–Gauss (LG) quadrature on
K_{\varepsilon}
and
K^{i}
, respectively, by the points
S^{N}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{K})=\{\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{N}\}
and
S^{N}_{i}(\mathbf{K})=\{x^{i}_{1},\ldots,x^{i}_{N}\}
.
Then the set of local DG nodes in the element
\mathbf{K}
is denoted as

S_{\otimes}(\mathbf{K})=S^{N}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{K})\otimes\big(\bigotimes\nolimits_{i=1}^{d}S^{N}_{i}(\mathbf{K})\big).

(82)

Also, the analysis of the realizability-preserving property in Section
V
uses specific quadrature rules.
We denote the
M_{\varepsilon}
-point and
M_{x}
-point Legendre–Gauss–Lobatto (LGL) quadrature rules on the intervals
K_{\varepsilon}
and
K^{i}
, respectively, by the points
\hat{S}^{M_{\varepsilon}}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{K})=\{\varepsilon_{\mbox{\tiny L}}^{+}=\hat{\varepsilon}_{1},\hat{\varepsilon}_{2},\ldots,\hat{\varepsilon}_{M_{\varepsilon}}=\varepsilon_{\mbox{\tiny H}}^{-}\}
and
\hat{S}^{M_{x}}_{i}(\mathbf{K})=\{x_{\mbox{\tiny L}}^{i,+}=\hat{x}^{i}_{1},\hat{x}_{2}^{i},\ldots,\hat{x}^{i}_{M_{x}}=x_{\mbox{\tiny H}}^{i,-}\}
, with respective weights
\{\hat{w}^{\varepsilon}_{q}\}_{q=1}^{M_{\varepsilon}}
and
\{\hat{w}^{i}_{q}\}_{q=1}^{M_{x}}
.
Here
M_{\varepsilon}\geq\frac{k+5}{2}
so that the LGL quadrature rules integrate polynomials of degree
k+2
or less exactly, and
M_{x}\geq\frac{k+3}{2}
so that the LGL quadrature rules integrate polynomials of degree
k
or less exactly.
Exact integration is required for the realizability-preserving analysis.
In an element
\mathbf{K}
we define the auxiliary sets

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{\varepsilon,\otimes}(\mathbf{K})

\displaystyle=\hat{S}^{M_{\varepsilon}}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{K})\otimes\big(\bigotimes\nolimits_{i=1}^{d}S^{N}_{i}(\mathbf{K})\big),

(83a)

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{i,\otimes}(\mathbf{K})

\displaystyle=S^{N}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{K})\otimes\big(\bigotimes\nolimits_{j=1,j\neq i}^{d}S^{N}_{j}(\mathbf{K})\big)\otimes\hat{S}^{M_{x}}_{i}(\mathbf{K}).

(83b)

{\mathbf{K}}
is denoted as

\hat{S}_{\otimes}(\mathbf{K})=\hat{S}_{\varepsilon,\otimes}(\mathbf{K})\cup\big(\bigcup\nolimits_{i=1}^{d}\hat{S}_{i,\otimes}(\mathbf{K})\big),

(84)

and the union of the auxiliary sets and the local DG nodes is denoted as

\widetilde{S}_{\otimes}(\mathbf{K})=S_{\otimes}(\mathbf{K})\cup\hat{S}_{\otimes}(\mathbf{K}).

(85)

III.2
Time Integration

The semi-discrete DG problem in Eq. (
66
) can be rearranged into a system of ODEs of the form

\dot{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{u})+\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{u}),

(86)

where
\mathbf{u}=\{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{K}|}\int_{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{U}_{h}\phi_{h}\,d\boldsymbol{z}:\mathbf{K}\in\mathcal{T}\}
represents all the evolved degrees of freedom,
\mathbf{T}
is the transport operator corresponding to the terms in Eq. (
66
) arising from the spatial and energy derivatives, and
\mathbf{C}
is the collision operator corresponding to the right-hand side of Eq. (
66
).
We use IMEX Runge–Kutta (RK) methods to evolve the degrees of freedom forward in time, and integrate the transport operator explicitly, and the collision operator implicitly.
Diagonally implicit
s
-stage IMEX methods for the system of ODEs in Eq. (
86
) can be written generally as
[
49
]

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\tilde{a}_{ij}\,\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{u}^{(j)})+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{i}a_{ij}\,\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{u}^{(j)}),\quad i=1,\ldots,s

(87)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}\tilde{w}_{i}\,\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{u}^{(i)})+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}w_{i}\,\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{u}^{(i)}).

(88)

Here
\tilde{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{a}_{ij}\end{pmatrix}
and
A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{ij}\end{pmatrix}
are the
s\times s
stage coefficient matrices for the respective explicit and implicit parts of the IMEX scheme.
While the
s
-vectors
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}=(\tilde{w}_{i})
and
\mathbf{w}=(w_{i})
are the respective coefficients for the assembly stage of the explicit and implicit parts.
These matrices and vectors are subject to order conditions, which can be found in
[
49
, Section 2.1]
.

Importantly, for the purpose of proving the realizability-preserving property of our method, one hopes that the stage equations in Eq. (
87
) can be expressed in the Shu–Osher form

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{n}

(89)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}c_{ij}\mathbf{u}^{(ij)}+a_{ii}\,\Delta t\,\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{u}^{(i)}),\quad i=1,\ldots,s,

(90)

where the coefficients satisfy
c_{ij}\geq 0
and
\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}c_{ij}=1
, and
\mathbf{u}^{(ij)}
is given by the forward Euler step

\mathbf{u}^{(ij)}=\mathbf{u}^{(j)}+\hat{c}_{ij}\,\Delta t\,\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{u}^{(j)}),

(91)

where the parameters
\hat{c}_{ij}\geq 0
.
Details on determining
c_{ij}
and
\hat{c}_{ij}
for IMEX schemes can be found in
[
46
,
44
]
, and for explicit RK methods in
[
60
]
.
The Shu–Osher form of the stage equations in Eq. (
90
) allows us to express
\mathbf{u}^{(i)}
as a convex combination of forward Euler steps with step sizes
\hat{c}_{ij}\Delta t
.
Then to show the realizability-preserving property of the updates in Eq. (
90
), one only needs to consider a sequence of forward and backward Euler steps.
The other advantage of the Shu–Osher form is the derived time-step restriction.
If
\Delta t\leq\Delta t_{\mathrm{Ex}}
is the time-step restriction to maintain realizability of the cell average in the Forward Euler step, then, assuming the implicit solve in Eq. (
90
) does not impose a restriction on
\Delta t
, the time-step restriction to maintain realizability for the IMEX method is
\Delta t\leq\hat{c}\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{Ex}}
, where

\hat{c}=\min_{i,j}\frac{1}{\hat{c}_{ij}}.

(92)

It is then ideal for the IMEX method to have
\hat{c}
as close to
1
as possible.

In this paper, we consider
s
-stage IMEX methods that are diagonally implicit (
a_{ij}=0
for
j>i
) and globally stiffly accurate (GSA), i.e.,
\tilde{a}_{si}=\tilde{w}_{i}
and
a_{si}=w_{i}
, so that the assembly step in Eq. (
88
) can be omitted and
\mathbf{u}^{n+1}=\mathbf{u}^{(s)}
.
Specifically, we use the IMEX PD-ARS
[
46
]
scheme, whose Butcher tableau is

\begin{array}[]{c|c}\tilde{\mathbf{c}}&\tilde{A}\\
\hline\cr&\tilde{\mathbf{w}}\end{array}=\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}0&0&0&0\\
1&1&0&0\\
1&1/2&1/2&0\\
\hline\cr&1/2&1/2&0\end{array}\quad\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{c}&A\\
\hline\cr&\mathbf{w}\end{array}=\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}0&0&0&0\\
1&0&1&0\\
1&0&1/2&1/2\\
\hline\cr&0&1/2&1/2\end{array}

(93)

where the left table represents the coefficients for the explicit method, and the right table represents the coefficients for the implicit method.
The vectors
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}
and
\mathbf{c}
are used for the treatment of non-autonomous systems.
The IMEX PD-ARS scheme is formally only first order accurate, but performs well in the diffusion limit, and recovers the optimal second-order explicit RK scheme from
[
60
]
in the streaming limit (
\mathbf{C}=0
).
The Shu–Osher coefficients for the IMEX PD-ARS method are

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}c_{10}&\\
c_{20}&c_{21}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&\\
1/2&1/2\end{bmatrix}

(94a)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\hat{c}_{10}&\\
\hat{c}_{20}&\hat{c}_{21}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}.

(94b)

For problems with no collisions,
\mathbf{C}=0
, we set
a_{ii}=0
, and use explicit strong stability-preserving (SSP) RK methods.
Specifically, we use the second and third order SSP-RK methods from
[
60
]
(SSPRK
2
and SSPRK
3
, respectively).
The Shu–Osher coefficients for SSPRK
2
are identical to Eq. (
94
), while for the SSPRK
3
method they are

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}c_{10}&&\\
c_{20}&c_{21}&\\
c_{30}&c_{31}&c_{32}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&&\\
3/4&1/4&\\
1/3&0&2/3\end{bmatrix}

(95a)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\hat{c}_{10}&&\\
\hat{c}_{20}&\hat{c}_{21}&\\
\hat{c}_{30}&\hat{c}_{31}&\hat{c}_{32}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&&\\
0&1&\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.

(95b)

Note for each method we have
\hat{c}=1
.
As we will show in Section
V.5
that our implicit collision solver does not impose a restriction on the time step
\Delta t
, then our derived time step will be no different than if we were solving a collisionless problem with forward Euler.

IV
Iterative Solvers

The proposed DG-IMEX scheme requires two nonlinear solvers: (a) for the recovery of primitive moments
\mathbf{M}
from the conserved moments
\mathbf{U}
, and (b) for the implicit collision solver, which is formulated directly on the primitive moments.
Both solvers involve element-local data and are formulated in terms of point values, where input data is provided from pointwise evaluations of the polynomial representation; i.e.,
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}_{h}(\boldsymbol{z})
for some
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbf{K}
.
Both solvers also require the components of the three-velocity,
\boldsymbol{v}=(v^{1},v^{2},v^{3})^{\intercal}
, as input.

IV.1
Conserved to Primitive Moment Conversion

The recovery of the Lagrangian moments
\mathbf{M}=(\mathcal{J},\mathcal{H}_{i})^{\intercal}
from the evolved Eulerian moments
\mathbf{U}=(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{j})^{\intercal}
consists of two steps.
First, the “hat” moments
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}=(\widehat{\mathcal{E}},\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{j})^{\intercal}
, defined in Eq. (
58a
), are obtained from
\mathbf{U}
.
These moments are linearly related by

\mathcal{E}=W\widehat{\mathcal{E}}+v^{i}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{F}_{i}=\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{i}+Wv_{i}\widehat{\mathcal{E}},

(96)

which can be easily inverted to give

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}=W\big(\mathcal{E}-v^{k}\mathcal{F}_{k}\big)\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{i}=\mathcal{F}_{i}-W^{2}v_{i}\big(\mathcal{E}-v^{k}\mathcal{F}_{k}\big).

(97)

For use later, we write Eq. (
96
) as

\mathbf{U}=A(\boldsymbol{v})\,\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\quad\text{where}\quad A(\boldsymbol{v})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}W&\boldsymbol{v}^{\intercal}\\
W\boldsymbol{v}&I\end{array}\right),

(98)

where
I
is the
3\times 3
identity matrix.

Next, with
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
known, the Lagrangian moments
\mathbf{M}
are obtained through an iterative procedure.
The moments are nonlinearly related by

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}=W\mathcal{J}+v^{i}\mathcal{H}_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{j}=W\mathcal{H}_{j}+v^{i}\mathcal{K}_{ij}(\mathbf{M}).

(99)

To solve this system for the Lagrangian moments, we adopt the idea from
[
22
]
, which is based on Richardson iteration for linear systems, and formulate the following fixed-point problem

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{J}\\
\mathcal{H}_{j}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{J}\\
\mathcal{H}_{j}\end{array}\right)-\frac{\lambda}{W}\,\left(\begin{array}[]{c}(W\mathcal{J}+v^{i}\mathcal{H}_{i})-\widehat{\mathcal{E}}\\
(W\mathcal{H}_{j}+v^{i}\mathcal{K}_{ij})-\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{j}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}-\frac{\lambda}{W}\,\mathbf{F}_{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}}(\mathbf{M})\vcentcolon=\mathbf{H}_{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}}(\mathbf{M}),

(106)

where
\lambda/W
is a constant “step size”, and
\lambda
is taken to be

\lambda=\frac{1}{1+v},\qquad v=\sqrt{v_{i}v^{i}}.

(107)

The Lagrangian moments are then obtained with Picard iteration,

\mathbf{M}^{[k+1]}=\mathbf{H}_{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}}(\mathbf{M}^{[k]}),\quad k=0,1,\ldots,

(108)

with initial guess
\mathbf{M}^{[0]}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}/W
.
We consider the method to have converged when the residual satisfies

\|\mathbf{F}_{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}}(\mathbf{M}^{[k]})\|\leq\texttt{tol}_{\rm C2P},

(109)

where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm and
\texttt{tol}_{\rm C2P}
is a user-specified tolerance.

Remark 4
.

Convergence of the iterates generated by Eq. (
108
) to a unique solution is guaranteed for
v<0.221075
(see Appendix
B
), however, in our numerical tests we have not encountered a velocity for which the fixed-point iteration fails to converge when using
\lambda=(1+v)^{-1}
(see Figure
2
).
The convergence analysis requires that, when
\mathbf{M}^{[0]}\in\mathcal{R}
, each iterate
\mathbf{M}^{[k]}
remains realizable. This property is proved later in Lemma
6
.

The conserved to primitive moment conversion problem can also be solved by Newton’s method.
To this end, we write Eq. (
99
) as

\mathbf{F}_{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}}(\mathbf{M})=0,

(110)

The Newton update step is then given by

\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{M}^{[k]})\,\big(\mathbf{M}^{[k+1]}-\mathbf{M}^{[k]}\big)=-\mathbf{F}_{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}}(\mathbf{M}^{[k]}),\quad k=0,1,\ldots,

(111)

where
\mathbf{J}=[\partial\mathbf{F}_{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}}/\partial\mathbf{M}]
is the Jacobian matrix, and
\mathbf{M}^{[0]}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}/W
is used as the initial guess.
We also consider Newton’s method to have converged when Eq. (
109
) is satisfied.
Both the modified Richardson iteration method and Newton’s method have been implemented and are studied later in Section
VI.1
.

IV.2
Collision Solver

With collisional source terms, a nonlinear solve is required when performing the implicit update during time integration.
The element-local nodal implicit update of the IMEX scheme in Eq. (
90
) can be formulated as a backward Euler solve with step size
\Delta\tau
,

\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}^{(*)}+\Delta\tau\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{U}),

(112)

where
\mathbf{U}^{(*)}
represents the first (known) term on right-hand side of Eq. (
90
), and
\mathbf{U}
represents the unknowns to be solved for.
(For notational convenience we omit the superscript
(i)
on the unknowns.)
We formulate the implicit solve in terms of the primitive moments.
To this end, using the matrix
A
defined in Eq. (
98
), we can write the collision term as

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{U})=A(\boldsymbol{v})\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}}(\mathbf{M})\quad\text{where}\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}}(\mathbf{M})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\chi\,(\mathcal{J}_{\mbox{\tiny Eq}}-\mathcal{J})\\
-\kappa\,\mathcal{H}_{j}\end{array}\right),

(113)

so that multiplying Eq. (
112
) by
A^{-1}
on both sides leads to

\widehat{\mathbf{U}}(\mathbf{M})=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{(*)}+\Delta\tau\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}}(\mathbf{M}),

(114)

where
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{(*)}=A^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{(*)}=(\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{(*)},\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{j}^{(*)})^{\intercal}
.
Next, we formulate a fixed-point method to solve Eq. (
114
).
With
\mathbf{M}
known,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{U})
is evaluated and used to update the conserved moments in Eq. (
112
).

Similar to Eq. (
106
), and following
[
22
]
, we use the Richardson iteration idea to formulate the fixed-point problem

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}-\lambda\,\Lambda\,\big[\,\widehat{\mathbf{U}}(\mathbf{M})-\big(\,\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{(*)}+\Delta\tau\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}}(\mathbf{M})\,\big)\,\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}-\lambda\,\Lambda\,\mathbf{G}_{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}}(\mathbf{M})=\vcentcolon\mathbf{Q}_{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}}(\mathbf{M}),

(115)

where
\Lambda=\mbox{diag}(\mu_{\chi},\mu_{\kappa},\mu_{\kappa},\mu_{\kappa})
,
\mu_{\chi}=(W+\lambda\,\Delta\tau\,\chi)^{-1}
,
\mu_{\kappa}=(W+\lambda\,\Delta\tau\,\kappa)^{-1}
, and
\lambda\in(0,1]
is a step size parameter.
In this paper, for reasons detailed in Section
V.5
, we propose
\lambda=1/(1+v)
.

Remark 5
.

The fixed-point problem in Eq. (
115
) reduces to Eq. (
106
) when
\chi=\kappa=0
; i.e., when
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{U})=0
.

The primitive moments are then obtained through Picard iteration,

\mathbf{M}^{[k+1]}=\mathbf{Q}_{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}}(\mathbf{M}^{[k]}),\quad k=0,1,\ldots,

(116)

with initial guess
\mathbf{M}^{[0]}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{(*)}
.
Iterations continue until the residual satisfies

\|\mathbf{G}_{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}}(\mathbf{M}^{[k]})\|\leq\texttt{tol}_{\rm Coll},

(117)

where, as in Eq. (
109
), we use the Euclidean norm, and
\texttt{tol}_{\rm Coll}
is a user-specified tolerance.

V
Realizability-Preserving Property of the DG-IMEX Scheme

The realizability-preserving scheme is designed to preserve realizability of cell averages during time integration.
The realizability of cell averages is then leveraged to recover pointwise realizability within each element with the aid of a realizability-enforcing limiter, which is detailed below in Section
V.4
.

The cell average of the moments is defined as

\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{K}|}\int_{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{U}_{h}\,\varepsilon^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{z}.

(118)

Recall the evolved degrees of freedom are
\mathbf{u}=\{\frac{1}{|\mathbf{K}|}\int_{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{U}_{h}\phi_{h}\,\varepsilon^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{z}:\mathbf{K}\in\mathcal{T}\}
.
For
\phi_{h}=1
, the evolved degrees of freedom represent the cell averaged moments.
Using Eq. (
90
), the stage equation for the cell average of the moments is then

\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}^{(i)}=\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}c_{ij}\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}^{(ij)}+a_{ii}\,\Delta t\,\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}^{(i)}),\quad i=1,\ldots,s,

(119)

where

\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}^{(ij)}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}^{(j)}+\hat{c}_{ij}\,\Delta t\,\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{U}_{h}^{(j)})_{\mathbf{K}},

(120)

and (see Eq. (
66
))

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{U}_{h}^{(j)})_{\mathbf{K}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{K}|}\int_{\mathbf{K}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\big[\,\varepsilon^{3}\,\widehat{\mathbf{F}}^{\varepsilon}\rvert_{\varepsilon_{\mbox{\tiny H}}}-\varepsilon^{3}\,\widehat{\mathbf{F}}^{\varepsilon}\rvert_{\varepsilon_{\mbox{\tiny L}}}\,\big]\,d\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{K}|}\int_{\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}^{i}}\big[\,\widehat{\mathbf{F}}^{i}\rvert_{x^{i}_{\mbox{\tiny H}}}-\widehat{\mathbf{F}}^{i}\rvert_{x^{i}_{\mbox{\tiny L}}}\,\big]\,\varepsilon^{2}\,d\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}^{i}.

(121)

Eq. (
119
) can be separated into an explicit update and an implicit update.
We define the explicit update as

\mathbf{U}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{Ex}}=\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}c_{ij}\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}^{(ij)},

(122)

so that the implicit update is

\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}^{(i)}=\mathbf{U}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{Ex}}+a_{ii}\Delta t\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}^{(i)}),\quad i=1,\ldots,s.

(123)

V.1
Preparations for Analysis of Numerical Method

Our choice of numerical fluxes is motivated by the goal of designing a realizability-preserving numerical scheme for the two-moment model.
The following lemma is useful when proving that our choice of spatial numerical fluxes in Eq. (
67
) will preserve moment realizability under a CFL-type restriction on the time step.

Lemma 2
.

For a given
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, let
\mathbf{U}=(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{j})^{\intercal}
and
\mathbf{F}^{i}=(\mathcal{F}^{i},\mathcal{S}^{i}_{\hskip 4.0ptj})^{\intercal}
, as defined in Eq. (
64
), be moments of a distribution function
f\in\mathfrak{R}
.
Let
\alpha
be such that
0<\alpha^{-1}\leq 1
, then

\Phi^{i,\pm}(\mathbf{U})\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{U}\pm\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbf{F}^{i})\in\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

The first component of
\Phi^{i,\pm}(\mathbf{U})
is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{E}\pm\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{F}^{i})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}g\,d\omega,

where we have defined
g:=\frac{1}{2}(1\pm L^{i}/\alpha)f
, where
L^{i}
is given by Eq. (
15
).
Similarly, the second component of
\Phi^{i,\pm}(\mathbf{U})
is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{F}_{j}\pm\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{S}^{i}_{\hskip 4.0ptj})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}gL_{j}\,d\omega.

Since
|L^{i}|\leq 1
and
0<\alpha^{-1}\leq 1
,
g\in\mathfrak{R}
.
Using the same reasoning as in the proof of Proposition
4
, it follows that
\Phi^{i,\pm}(\mathbf{U})\in\mathcal{R}
.
∎

The following two lemmas will be useful when proving that our choice of numerical flux in the energy dimension in Eq. (
68
) will result in a realizability-preserving scheme under a CFL condition.

Lemma 3
.

Given
f\in\mathfrak{R}
, let
\mathbf{U}=(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{j})^{\intercal}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{E}},\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{j})
be as defined in Eq. (
64
) and (
69
), respectively. Then, for
\alpha\geq 0
such that
0\leq\alpha a^{\varepsilon}\leq W+v^{\mu}\ell_{\mu}=E/\varepsilon
(see Eq. (
14
)),

\mathbf{U}-\alpha a^{\varepsilon}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}
is realizable.

Proof.

The first component of
\mathbf{U}-\alpha a^{\varepsilon}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}
is

\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(E^{2}/\varepsilon)f\,d\omega-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\alpha a^{\varepsilon}Ef\,d\omega=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}g\,d\omega,

where we have defined
g\vcentcolon=(E^{2}/\varepsilon)(1-\alpha a^{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon/E))\,f
.
Since
\alpha a^{\varepsilon}\leq E/\varepsilon
,
g\in\mathfrak{R}
.
The second component of
\mathbf{U}-\alpha a^{\varepsilon}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}
is

\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(E^{2}/\varepsilon)fL_{j}\,d\omega-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\alpha a^{\varepsilon}EfL_{j}\,d\omega=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}gL_{j}\,d\omega.

It follows from arguments similar to Proposition
4
that
\mathbf{U}-\alpha a^{\varepsilon}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\in\mathcal{R}
.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Given
f\in\mathfrak{R}
, let
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}
and
\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}
be given by the definitions in Eqs. (
69
) and (
64
), respectively.
Let
a_{\max}^{\varepsilon}\geq|q|
, where
q
is defined in Eq. (
70
).
Then
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\pm\frac{1}{a_{\max}^{\varepsilon}}\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

The first component of
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\pm\frac{1}{a_{\max}^{\varepsilon}}\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}
is

\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}E\,f\,d\omega\pm\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{q}{a_{\max}^{\varepsilon}}\,E\,f\,d\omega=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}g\,d\omega,

where we have defined
g\vcentcolon=E\,(1\pm\frac{q}{a_{\max}^{\varepsilon}})\,f
.
Since
\frac{|q|}{a_{\max}^{\varepsilon}}\leq 1
,
g\in\mathfrak{R}
. The second component of
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\pm\frac{1}{a_{\max}^{\varepsilon}}\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}
is

\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}E\,f\,L_{j}\,d\omega\pm\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{q}{a_{\max}^{\varepsilon}}E\,f\,L_{j}\,d\omega=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}g\,L_{j}\,d\omega.

It follows that
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\pm\frac{1}{a_{\max}^{\varepsilon}}\mathbf{F}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{R}

∎

V.2
Conserved to Primitive Moment Conversion

In this section we prove that the Picard iteration defined in Eq. (
108
) for the moment conversion problem is realizability-preserving.
We need the conversion to be realizability-preserving so that the numerical fluxes are evaluated using moments of a distribution
f\in\mathfrak{R}
so that Lemmas
2
,
3
, and
4
hold.
The following two lemmas establish realizability-preserving conversion from conserved moments
\mathbf{U}
to primitive moments
\mathbf{M}
.

Lemma 5
.

Assume that
\mathbf{U}=(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{j})^{\intercal}
, defined in Proposition
4
, are realizable.
Let the three-velocity be
v^{\mu}=(0,v^{i})^{\intercal}
such that
0\leq v_{i}v^{i}<1
.
Then
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}=(\widehat{\mathcal{E}},\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{j})^{\intercal}
, defined in Proposition
3
, are realizable.

Proof.

To prove realizability of
(\widehat{\mathcal{E}},\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{j})^{\intercal}
, it is sufficient to show that
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}>0
and
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}\geq\widehat{\mathcal{F}}
, where
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}=\sqrt{\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mu}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}^{\mu}}
. Since
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{j})^{\intercal}
are realizable,
\mathcal{E}>0
and
\mathcal{E}\geq\mathcal{F}
, where
\mathcal{F}=\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{i}\mathcal{F}^{i}}
.
Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the left expression in Eq. (
97
), it is straightforward to show that
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}>0
;

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}=W(\mathcal{E}-v^{k}\mathcal{F}_{k})\geq W(\mathcal{E}-\sqrt{v_{i}v^{i}}\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{k}\mathcal{F}^{k}})>W(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{F})\geq 0.

To show that
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}\geq\widehat{\mathcal{F}}
, we note two useful equalities.
First, since
u^{\mu}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mu}=0
,
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{0}=-v^{i}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
.
Then, using the right expression in Eq. (
96
) gives

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{0}=Wv^{i}v_{i}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}-v^{i}\mathcal{F}_{i}.

Second, using the definition of the Lorentz factor, we have

1+W^{2}v_{i}v^{i}=1+\frac{1}{1-v_{i}v^{i}}v_{i}v^{i}=\frac{1}{1-v_{i}v^{i}}=W^{2}.

We then find

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-(\,Wv^{i}v_{i}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}-v^{i}\mathcal{F}_{i}\,)^{2}+(\mathcal{F}_{i}-Wv_{i}\widehat{\mathcal{E}})(\mathcal{F}^{i}-Wv^{i}\widehat{\mathcal{E}})

\displaystyle=-(v^{i}\mathcal{F}_{i})^{2}+\mathcal{F}^{2}+2W\widehat{\mathcal{E}}v^{i}\mathcal{F}_{i}(v_{i}v^{i}-1)

\displaystyle\hskip 12.0pt+W^{2}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{2}v_{i}v^{i}(1-v_{i}v^{i})

\displaystyle=-(v^{i}\mathcal{F}_{i})^{2}+\mathcal{F}^{2}-2\,\widehat{\mathcal{E}}\,v^{i}\mathcal{F}_{i}/W+\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{2}v_{i}v^{i}

\displaystyle\leq-(v^{i}\mathcal{F}_{i})^{2}+\mathcal{E}^{2}-2\,\widehat{\mathcal{E}}\,v^{i}\mathcal{F}_{i}/W+\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{2}v_{i}v^{i}

\displaystyle=(\,\mathcal{E}-v^{i}\mathcal{F}_{i}\,)\,(\,\mathcal{E}+v^{i}\mathcal{F}_{i}\,)\,-2\,\widehat{\mathcal{E}}v^{i}\mathcal{F}_{i}/W+\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{2}v_{i}v^{i}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}\,(\,\mathcal{E}-v^{i}\mathcal{F}_{i}\,)/W+\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{2}v_{i}v^{i}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{2}\,(\,1+W^{2}v_{i}v^{i}\,)/W^{2}=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{2}.

Taking a square root on both sides gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma 6
.

Let
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}=(\widehat{\mathcal{E}},\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{i})^{\intercal}
and
\mathbf{M}^{[k]}=(\mathcal{J}^{[k]},\mathcal{H}_{i}^{[k]})^{\intercal}
be realizable.
If
\lambda\leq(1+v)^{-1}
, then
\mathbf{M}^{[k+1]}=(\mathcal{J}^{[k+1]},\mathcal{H}_{i}^{[k+1]})^{\intercal}
, determined by the modified Richardson iteration scheme given by Eq. (
108
), is realizable.

Proof.

The first component of
\mathbf{M}^{[k+1]}
is

\mathcal{J}^{[k]}=\mathscr{J}^{[k]}+\frac{\lambda}{W}\widehat{\mathcal{E}},

where we have defined

\mathscr{J}^{[k]}=(1-\lambda)\mathcal{J}^{[k]}-\frac{\lambda}{W}v^{j}\mathcal{H}^{[k]}_{j}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}g^{[k]}\,d\omega,

and
g^{[k]}\vcentcolon=\big[\,(1-\lambda)-\frac{\lambda}{W}\,v^{j}\ell_{j}\,\big]\,\varepsilon f^{[k]}
.
Similarly, the second component of
\mathbf{M}^{[k+1]}
is

\mathcal{H}_{i}^{[k+1]}=\mathscr{H}_{i}^{[k]}+\frac{\lambda}{W}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{i},

where

\mathscr{H}_{i}^{[k]}=(1-\lambda)\mathcal{H}^{[k]}_{i}-\frac{\lambda}{W}v^{j}\mathcal{K}^{[k]}_{ij}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}g^{[k]}\,\ell_{i}\,d\omega.

Note that, since
\ell_{0}=-v^{i}\ell_{i}
, we also have

\mathscr{H}_{0}^{[k]}=-v^{i}\mathscr{H}_{i}^{[k]}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}g^{[k]}\,\ell_{0}\,d\omega.

We let
\mathscr{M}^{[k]}=(\mathscr{J}^{[k]},\mathscr{H}_{\mu}^{[k]})^{\intercal}
.
Since the first and second components of
\mathscr{M}^{[k]}
are expressed as moments arising from the same distribution function,
g^{[k]}
, then

\mathscr{M}^{[k]}\in\mathcal{R}
follows from Proposition
2
, provided
g^{[k]}\in\mathfrak{R}
.
By assumption
\varepsilon f^{[k]}\in\mathfrak{R}
, in order to obtain
g^{[k]}\in\mathfrak{R}
, we require

(1-\lambda)-\frac{\lambda}{W}v^{i}\ell_{i}\geq 0.

Since
v^{i}\ell_{i}=\ell^{0}=Wv_{\hat{\iota}}\ell^{\hat{\iota}}
,

-Wv\leq v^{i}\ell_{i}\leq Wv.

Then since
(1-\lambda)-\frac{\lambda}{W}v^{i}\ell_{i}\geq(1-\lambda)-\lambda v
, requiring the latter to be greater than
0
yields

\lambda\leq\frac{1}{1+v}.

Since
\mathscr{M}^{[k]},\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\in\mathcal{R}
, realizability of
\mathbf{M}^{[k+1]}=\mathscr{M}^{[k]}+\frac{\lambda}{W}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
follows from Lemma
1
.
∎

Remark 6
.

The mapping in Eq. (
108
) is formulated for an effective step size
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda/W
.
Given the result of Lemma
6
, the Picard iteration is realizability-preserving when
\lambda^{\prime}\leq\frac{1}{W(1+v)}
.

Following the realizability-preserving result in Lemma
6
, the convergence of the modified Richardson iteration scheme in Eq. (
108
) is proved in Appendix
B
under conditions detailed in Remark
4
, which concludes the realizability-preserving property analysis for the conserved to primitive moment conversion step.

V.4
Realizability-Enforcing Limiter

Proposition
5
guarantees the explicit update of the
i
th stage cell-average is realizable.
It does not guarantee that
\mathbf{U}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})^{(j)}\in\mathcal{R}
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\widetilde{S}_{\otimes}(\mathbf{K})
, as required by Condition 3 of Proposition
5
.
To ensure this requirement, we employ the following element-local realizability-enforcing limiter from
[
46
]
after each explicit update.

To enforce positivity of the zeroth moment,
\mathcal{E}
, we replace the polynomial
\mathcal{E}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})
with the limited polynomial

\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})=\theta_{1}\,\mathcal{E}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})+(1-\theta_{1})\,\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{K}},

(129)

where the limiter parameter
\theta_{1}
is given by

\theta_{1}=\min\left\{\left|\frac{m-\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{K}}}{m_{\widetilde{S}_{\otimes}}-\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{K}}}\right|,1\right\},

(130)

with

m=0\quad\text{and}\quad m_{\widetilde{S}_{\otimes}}=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\widetilde{S}_{\otimes}(\mathbf{K})}\mathcal{E}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}).

The next step enforces
\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{F}\geq 0
.
We define
\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{h}=(\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{h},\mathcal{F}_{h})^{\intercal}
.
If
\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{q}=\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{q})
lies outside of
\mathcal{R}
for any quadrature point
\boldsymbol{x}_{q}
, then
\boldsymbol{x}_{q}
is a troubled quadrature point.
For troubled quadrature points, there exists a line connecting the realizable cell average,
\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}
, and
\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{q}
that intersects the boundary of
\mathcal{R}
.
This line is parametrized by

\mathbf{s}_{q}(\psi)=\psi\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{q}+(1-\psi)\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}},\quad\psi\in[0,1].

(131)

The unique point where
\mathbf{s}_{q}(\psi)
intersects the boundary of
\mathcal{R}
is obtained by solving
\gamma(\mathbf{s}_{q}(\psi))=0
for
\psi
using the bisection algorithm.
(Recall that for
\mathbf{U}=(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{j})^{\intercal}
,
\gamma(\mathbf{U})=\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{F}
.)
We then replace
\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{h}
by

\overline{\overline{\mathbf{U}}}_{h}=\theta_{2}\,\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{h}+(1-\theta_{2})\,\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}},

(132)

where the parameter
\theta_{2}
is the smallest
\psi
obtained in element
\mathbf{K}
by considering all troubled quadrature points.
The limiter is conservative in the sense that it preserves the cell average; i.e.,
\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}=\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathbf{K}}=\overline{\overline{\mathbf{U}}}_{\mathbf{K}}
.

Lemma 11
.

Let

\mathbf{M}^{[k]}=(\mathcal{J}^{[k]},\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{[k]})^{\intercal}

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{2}

\displaystyle=(W\mathcal{J})^{2}\,\big(\,1+2s\mathsf{h}+s^{2}\mathsf{h}^{2}\,\big),

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}^{2}\,\big(\,W^{2}\mathsf{h}^{2}+2Wv^{\nu}\mathsf{k}_{\nu\mu}\widehat{\mathsf{h}}^{\mu}\mathsf{h}+v^{\nu}\mathsf{k}_{\nu}^{\hskip 4.0pt\mu}\mathsf{k}_{\mu\rho}v^{\rho}\,\big),

From the first expression of Eq. (
96
), using a bound similar to Eq. (
133
) for
v^{\mu}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mu}
, we have

\mathcal{E}=W\widehat{\mathcal{E}}+v^{\mu}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mu}\geq W\widehat{\mathcal{E}}-vW\widehat{\mathcal{F}}>W(\widehat{\mathcal{E}}-\widehat{\mathcal{F}})\geq 0.

(140)

Direct calculations with the expressions in Eq. (
96
) gives

\mathcal{E}^{2}-\mathcal{F}^{2}=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{2}-\widehat{\mathcal{F}}^{2}+(v^{\mu}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mu})^{2}\geq\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{2}-\widehat{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\geq 0.

(141)

This completes the proof.
∎

We can now prove Proposition
6
.

Proof of Proposition
6
.

Since the conditions of Proposition
5
hold,
\mathbf{U}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{Ex}}\in\mathcal{R}
.
The realizability-enforcing limiter is applied to
\mathbf{U}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{Ex}}
to ensure pointwise realizability.
Then, Lemma
11
can be repeatedly applied to Eq. (
123
), starting with
\mathbf{M}^{[0]}=\mathbf{U}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{Ex}}
, until the iterative solver converges to the Lagrangian moments which define
\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}^{(i)}
.
Thus it follows from Lemma
12
that
\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{R}
.
∎

V.6
Realizability-Preserving DG-IMEX Scheme

With the previously established lemmas and propositions in this section, we can finally prove the realizability-preserving property of our DG-IMEX scheme as stated in Theorem
7
.

Proof of Theorem
7
.

Using Proposition
5
with
i=1
and Proposition
6
we get
\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{R}
.
Applying the realizability-enforcing limiter gives
\mathbf{U}_{h}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{R}
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\widetilde{S}_{\otimes}(\mathbf{K})
.
Repeated application of these steps for
i=2,\ldots,s
gives
\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}^{(s)}\in\mathcal{R}
.
Then since
\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}^{(s)}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{K}}^{(n+1)}
, the proof is complete.
∎

VI.1
Moment Conversion Solver

The conserved to primitive moment conversion problem incurs the most cost in our realizability-preserving scheme.
Because of this, it is desirable to have an iterative scheme that converges quickly to the desired accuracy, and where the evaluation of each iterate is inexpensive.
Generally, Picard iteration is inexpensive per iterate but may converge slowly, while Newton iteration is more costly per iterate but with faster convergence.
Here, we compare the proposed Picard iteration solver with Newton’s method to solve Eq. (
99
).

To this end, similar to
[
22
]
, we fix a pair
(v,\mathsf{h}=\mathcal{H}/\mathcal{J})
, with
v\in[0,1)
and
\mathsf{h}\in[0,1]

100
realizable values of
\mathbf{M}
.
To generate the samples for a fixed
(v,\mathsf{h})

First, for randomly sampled
\vartheta\in[0,\pi]
and
\varphi\in[0,2\pi]
, the fluid three-velocity is given by

\displaystyle v^{1}

\displaystyle=v\sin\vartheta\cos\varphi,

(142a)

\displaystyle v^{2}

\displaystyle=v\sin\vartheta\sin\varphi,

(142b)

\displaystyle v^{3}

\displaystyle=v\cos\vartheta.

(142c)

To generate

We then set

This value of

Next, using this value of

\displaystyle=\mathsf{h}\sin\vartheta^{\prime}\cos\varphi^{\prime},

\displaystyle q^{\hat{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{h}\sin\vartheta^{\prime}\sin\varphi^{\prime},

VI.7

In this test, we consider a two-dimensional spatial domain,
\Omega_{x^{1}}\times\Omega_{x^{2}}=[-5,5]\times[-5,5]
, and model the propagation of free-streaming radiation through a spatially varying velocity field mimicking a vortex.
For the setup we follow
[
22
]
, which is an adaptation of the spherical-polar test in
[
16
]
to Cartesian coordinates.
We set the opacities as
\chi=\sigma=0
, and let the velocity field
\boldsymbol{v}=[v^{1},v^{2},0]^{\intercal}
be given by

\displaystyle v^{1}(x^{1},x^{2})

\displaystyle=-v_{\mathrm{max}}\,x^{2}\,\exp\big[\,(1-|\boldsymbol{x}|^{2})/2\,\big],

(158)

\displaystyle v^{2}(x^{1},x^{2})

\displaystyle=\hskip 8.0ptv_{\mathrm{max}}\,x^{1}\,\exp\big[\,(1-|\boldsymbol{x}|^{2})/2\,\big],

(159)

where
|\boldsymbol{x}|=\sqrt{(x^{1})^{2}+(x^{2})^{2}}
.
The energy domain is given by
\Omega_{\varepsilon}=[0,300]
.
In the energy dimension, elements have geometrically progressing spacing with
\Delta\varepsilon_{i+1}/\Delta\varepsilon_{i}=1.119237083677839
.
We discretize the spatial and energy domains with
48\times 48
and
32
elements, respectively.
The moments are initialized as

\mathcal{J}=1\times 10^{-8}
,
\mathcal{H}^{1}=(1-\delta)W\mathcal{J}
, and
\mathcal{H}^{2}=0
for all
(x^{1},x^{2})\in\Omega_{x^{1}}\times\Omega_{x^{2}}

\delta=10^{-8}

For the inner spatial boundary in the
x^{1}
-direction we set

\mathcal{J}(\varepsilon,x^{1}=-5,x^{2})=\varepsilon/\exp[(\varepsilon/3-3)+1]

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\varepsilon,x^{1}=-5,x^{2})=(1-\delta)W(x^{1}=-5,x^{2})\mathcal{J}(\varepsilon,x^{1}=-5,x^{2})

For the outer spatial boundary in the
x^{1}

In this test, the moments are evolved until a steady state is reached at

In the steady state, as radiation propagates through the vortex, we expect the spectra to be Doppler shifted towards lower (red shift) or higher (blue shift) energies, depending on the local direction and magnitude of the velocity.
When considering energy integrated quantities, we expect the Lagrangian number and energy densities to be affected by the velocity field due to Doppler shifts, while the Eulerian number and energy densities should be unaffected.
The purpose of this test is to investigate to what extent these aspects are captured by our proposed scheme.
Similar to the tests in

VII
Summary and Conclusions

We have proposed and analyzed a realizability-preserving numerical method for solving a spectral two-moment model to simulate the transport of massless, neutral particles interacting with a steady background material moving with relativistic velocities.
The model is obtained as the special relativistic limit of the four-momentum-conservative general relativistic model from
[
13
]
, which is formulated in terms of comoving-frame momentum-space coordinates to facilitate computational modeling of the material coupling.
The two-moment model solves for the Eulerian-frame energy and momentum, and is closed by expressing higher-order moments in terms of the evolved moments using the maximum-entropy closure proposed by Minerbo
[
14
]
.
The realizability-preserving method maintains algebraic bounds on the Eulerian-frame energy and momentum.

The proposed numerical method combines DG phase-space discretization with IMEX time-stepping.
The phase-space advection terms are treated explicitly, while the collision term is treated implicitly.
To preserve realizability of the evolved moments, the DG phase-space discretization is equipped with numerical fluxes, which allow us to derive a sufficient time-step restriction to preserve realizability of the cell-averaged moments.
For the IMEX scheme, the time-step restriction is no worse than the restriction imposed by the explicit Forward Euler scheme.
A realizability-enforcing limiter is used to recover pointwise realizability from the cell-averaged moments.

Because the closure procedure and the implicit collision solve are formulated in terms of primitive moments, the proposed method requires solving two respective nonlinear systems.
For both of these nonlinear systems, solution methods have been proposed in the form of fixed-point problems.
The fixed-point problems are formulated such that they preserve moment realizability of each iterate, subject to a mild step-size constraint.
For the primitive moment recovery problem we have proven convergence to a unique realizable moment pair for the algebraic approximation to the Minerbo closure for
v<0.221075
.
Although this (sufficient) convergence condition appears restrictive, it is likely not sharp since, numerically, we have not observed any convergence failures during primitive moment recovery for any
0\leq v<1
.
Additionally, in practice we have not observed any issues regarding convergence or maintaining realizability when using Newton’s method for primitive moment recovery.

The proposed algorithm has been implemented and tested against six benchmark problems.
We run two tests — Streaming Sine Wave and Gaussian Diffusion
1
D — with constant background velocities.
The results of these tests demonstrate the proposed method’s order of accuracy for smooth solutions and ability to perform well in the dynamic diffusion regime (e.g.,
[
20
]
), respectively.
Three tests — Streaming Doppler Shift, Transparent Shock, and Transparent Vortex — are run with spatially varying (smooth and discontinuous) background velocities.
The Streaming Doppler Shift and Transparent Vortex tests demonstrate the proposed method’s ability to capture special relativistic effects, such as Doppler shifted energy spectra, in one and two spatial dimensions, respectively.
The results of these tests also demonstrate improvements over the same tests for the
\mathcal{O}(v/c)
model in
[
22
]
.
The Transparent Shock test demonstrates the proposed method’s ability to capture solutions which develop discontinuities due to steep velocity gradients, as we see good agreement with reference solutions, even for large,
v\leq 0.5
, velocities.
The Shadow Casting test, which has zero velocity, demonstrates the proposed method’s ability to accurately capture radiation shadows.

Further work is needed to address challenges associated with extensions of the model considered here.
First, the collision operator considered in this paper is simplified and the background is fixed.
Extending the method to collision operators that include more complex particle interactions, such as inelastic scattering, and considering the dynamical response of the material should be done.
To this end, we would consider extending the nested fixed-point algorithm proposed in
[
22
]
to the relativistic case.
In this algorithm, the matter states are updated in an outer iteration layer, while the radiation moments are updated in an inner iteration layer with the matter states held fixed.
In this context, the iterative collision solver proposed in Section
IV.2
can be used straightforwardly in the inner layer.
Second, this paper addresses neutral particles obeying Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics, but we aim to apply the proposed method to model neutrino transport in nuclear astrophysics applications.
Neutrinos are fermions, which due to Pauli’s exclusion principle have an upper bound on the phase-space density and associated bounds on the moments.
Therefore, our analysis should be extended to moment closures based on Fermi–Dirac statistics.
Third, the model is special relativistic and formulated in Cartesian coordinates, so it is desirable to extend to general relativity and more general curvilinear spatial coordinates.
Finally, our method is not designed to conserve particles even though our model possesses this conservation law at the continuum level.
To model neutrinos, it is desirable to simultaneously conserve both number and energy (e.g.,
[
1
]
).
We believe the analysis and method proposed here can be helpful as a starting point to address these challenges in future work.
