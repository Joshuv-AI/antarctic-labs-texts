Clinical outcomes and prognostic factors in hematologic malignancy patients with probable CMV pneumonia
Authors: Özge Aydın Güçlü, Ezgi Demirdöğen, Fazıl Çağrı Hunutlu, Merve Nur Yıldız, Nilüfer Aylin Acet Öztürk, Aslı Görek Dilektaşlı, Esra Kazak, Vildan Gürsoy, Tuba Ersal, İmran Sağlık, Ahmet Ursavaş, Vildan Özkocaman, Gökhan Ocakoğlu, Halis Akalın, Fahir Özkalemkaş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCytomegalovirus (CMV) infection is an important cause of morbidity and mortality among immunocompromised patients, particularly those with hematologic malignancies. The present study aims to evaluate the clinical features, laboratory parameters, and treatment characteristics associated with 30-day and 90-day mortality in patients with hematologic malignancies diagnosed with probable CMV pneumonia.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study including 61 patients with hematologic malignancies who were diagnosed with probable CMV pneumonia between January 2018 and October 2023. Demographic data, underlying disease characteristics, laboratory parameters, CMV DNA levels in blood and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, co-infections, and antiviral treatment features were recorded. The primary outcomes were all-cause mortality at 30 and 90 days. Factors associated with mortality were evaluated using univariate analyses and multivariate time-dependent Cox proportional hazards regression models to account for potential immortal-time bias related to antiviral treatment duration.ResultsThe median age was 57 (19–84) years, and 65.6% of patients were male. All-cause mortality was 29.5% at 30 days and 62.2% at 90 days. In the time-dependent Cox regression analysis, longer antiviral therapy duration was significantly associated with lower 30-day mortality, with each additional treatment day reducing the risk by approximately 8.3% (HR 0.917; 95% CI 0.866–0.971; p = 0.003). A similar protective trend was observed for 90-day mortality, although the association did not reach statistical significance (HR 0.957; 95% CI 0.912–1.004; p = 0.074). Higher serum urea levels were independently associated with increased 30-day mortality, whereas no significant association was observed with 90-day mortality. Baseline CMV DNA levels and longitudinal viral load changes during treatment were not significantly associated with mortality.ConclusionPatients with hematologic malignancies and probable CMV pneumonia experience high early and mid-term mortality. A longer antiviral treatment duration was associated with improved short-term survival, even after adjustment for immortal-time bias, whereas baseline virologic burden was not associated with mortality. These findings suggest that treatment feasibility and host-related factors may have a greater prognostic impact than initial CMV viral load in this patient population.Keywords: CMV pneumonia, Hematologic malignancies, Antiviral therapy, Mortality, Prognostic factors

Introduction

Cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection is an important cause of morbidity and mortality among immunocompromised patients, particularly those with hematologic malignancies [1]. Although CMV reactivation is well documented in transplant recipients, its clinical significance in non-transplant hematologic patients remains less well defined [2]. Among the spectrum of CMV-related diseases, CMV pneumonia stands out as one of the most severe manifestations, often occurring in the context of profound immunosuppression and leading to high mortality rates [3].

In hematologic malignancies, disease-related immune dysfunction, intensive chemotherapy regimens, and immunosuppressive therapies such as corticosteroids and monoclonal antibodies contribute to increased susceptibility to CMV reactivation [4, 5]. Diagnosis of CMV pneumonia remains challenging due to overlapping clinical and radiological findings with other infectious and non-infectious pulmonary complications common in this population [6, 7].

CMV pneumonia is associated with a high mortality rate, typically 30% to 75%, according to current clinical data and published studies [3, 8, 9]. Significant pulmonary complications, extended hospital stays, and an elevated risk of death can result from CMV reactivation or primary infection in this susceptible group [10]. However, there remains a lack of comprehensive data on the prognostic determinants specific to non-transplant patients with hematologic malignancies and probable CMV pneumonia.

The present study aims to evaluate the clinical features, laboratory parameters, and treatment characteristics associated with 30-day and 90-day mortality in patients with hematologic malignancies diagnosed with probable CMV pneumonia.

Method

Subjects and study design

The retrospective cohort study enrolled patients with hematologic malignancies diagnosed with probable CMV pneumonia and treated with intravenous ganciclovir from January 2018 to October 2023. Patients with hematologic malignancies who showed clinical suspicion of pneumonia and radiological findings indicative of pneumonitis or interstitial involvement on thoracic computed tomography were included in the study. The study excluded patients with immune thrombocytopenic purpura, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, and Fanconi aplastic anemia. Patients who did not achieve complete remission within three months following the conclusion of first-line therapy were classified as refractory. Relapsed patients were those who showed evidence of disease relapse more than three months later. Figure 1 summarizes the study protocol.

Study flow chart

The initial symptoms, vital signs, and laboratory results for the cases were documented. Respiratory support requirements during follow-up, including oxygen therapy, non-invasive mechanical ventilation, and intubation, were recorded and analyzed. The time to treatment initiation, duration of antiviral therapy, length of hospital stays, and both 30-day and 90-day mortality rates were also documented.

At our center, routine CMV prophylaxis with valganciclovir or letermovir is not standard practice for this patient population, mainly because these agents are not routinely reimbursed for prophylactic use in our country. Instead, patients are followed with serial CMV viral load monitoring, and antiviral therapy is initiated preemptively for CMV reactivation when clinically indicated. In the present cohort, none of the patients had received valganciclovir or letermovir prophylaxis before the diagnosis of CMV disease.

The quantification process of CMV DNA viral loads

Patients with suspected CMV pneumonia were provided with plasma and bronchoalveolar lavage samples [11]. Bronchoscopy was performed using standard methods in accordance with international consensus recommendations [12]. The American Thoracic Society [13] clinical practice guidance states that BAL was performed with fiberoptic bronchoscopy in all patients with a preliminary diagnosis of CMV pneumonia and a platelet count of 20,000/mm3 or higher, without refractory hypoxemia. Informed consent was obtained for the bronchoalveolar lavage [11] procedure. The sample was promptly sent to the laboratory for microbiological analysis. Viral nucleic acid extraction and amplification were performed using the fully automated Abbott m2000 Molecular Analysis System (Abbott, Chicago, IL, USA). To convert the IU/mL values in plasma and BAL, they were multiplied by 1.55. The results are expressed in IU/mL. Plasma CMV viral loads were determined twice weekly.

The definition of probable CMV pneumonia

CMV pneumonia was classified according to the updated consensus definitions of the Transplant-Associated Virus Infections Forum. Since there are no standardized diagnostic criteria for probable CMV pneumonia in patients with hematologic malignancies outside the allogeneic HCT setting, we used a study-specific clinical definition. In this study, probable CMV pneumonia was defined as the presence of compatible respiratory symptoms and/or hypoxemia, new pulmonary infiltrates on thoracic imaging, and CMV DNA positivity above the predefined threshold in blood and/or BAL samples, when no more plausible alternative explanation could be identified clinically. The threshold of 500 IU/mL was chosen pragmatically to avoid including very low-level CMV DNA detections, which may reflect viral shedding or bystander reactivation rather than clinically meaningful disease [1, 13].

Follow-up

The primary outcomes included all-cause mortality at 30 days and 90 days. The clinic’s electronic medical records contained information on the dates of death and the patients’ vital status.

Statistical analysis

The data was analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28.0. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the distribution of continuous data. Categorical variables are presented as n (%), and continuous variables are presented as median (min-max) values or mean (standard deviation [SD]) according to their distribution. For comparisons between groups, Pearson’s χ2 test was used for categorical variables. Student’s t-test was used for continuous variables when data followed a normal distribution, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used when the distribution was non-normal. Variables with p < 0.25 in univariate analyses were included in the multivariate Cox regression model. To reduce potential immortal-time bias, antiviral therapy duration was additionally analyzed using a time-dependent Cox proportional hazards regression model. In this approach, antiviral treatment exposure was modeled as a time-varying covariate representing cumulative treatment duration. The treatment covariate was defined using the minimum of follow-up time and total antiviral treatment duration (min [t, total treatment duration]), ensuring that only treatment exposure accumulated up to a given time point contributed to the risk estimation. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Additionally, a repeated-measures ANOVA was used to assess changes in CMV DNA levels from baseline to post-treatment and to examine all-cause 30-day mortality among patients receiving antiviral therapy.

Results

Patient characteristics

The study comprised 61 patients with a median age of 57 (19–84) years. Of these, 65.6% (n = 40) were male. The underlying hematologic diagnoses were acute lymphoblastic leukemia in 13.1% (n = 8), acute myeloid leukemia in 14.8% (n = 9), non-Hodgkin lymphoma in 47.5% (n = 29), Hodgkin lymphoma in 8.2% (n = 5), multiple myeloma in 8.2% (n = 75), chronic lymphocytic leukemia in 4.9% (n = 3), and myelodysplastic syndrome in 3.3% (n = 2). At the time of CMV diagnosis, 59.0% (n = 36) of patients had active disease, whereas 41.9% (n = 25) were in remission. Hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) was performed in 39.3% (n = 24) of patients, with allogeneic HCT accounting for 26.2% (n = 16) and autologous HCT accounting for 13.1% (n = 8). Co-infection was observed in 42.6% (n = 26) of patients for bacterial infections and 14.8% (n = 9) for fungal infections (Table 1).

Independent variables affecting 30-day and 90-day mortality

To evaluate predictors of mortality while adjusting for immortal-time bias, multivariate time-dependent Cox regression models were constructed for 30-day and 90-day outcomes. The cumulative duration of antiviral therapy was incorporated as a time-dependent covariate, along with baseline serum urea levels.

In the 30-day mortality model, a longer cumulative duration of antiviral therapy was independently associated with a lower hazard of death (HR: 0.917; 95% CI: 0.866–0.971; p = 0.003). Additionally, higher baseline serum urea levels were independently associated with an increased hazard of 30-day mortality (HR: 1.012; 95% CI: 1.001–1.024; p = 0.030) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the clinical outcomes and prognostic factors influencing short- and long-term mortality in patients with hematologic malignancies who were diagnosed with probable CMV pneumonia. In this study, we analyzed 61 hematologic malignancy patients with CMV infection and found a significantly high all-cause mortality, with 30-day and 90-day death rates of 29.5% and 62.2%, respectively.

In the time-dependent Cox regression analysis, longer antiviral therapy duration was significantly associated with lower 30-day mortality, with each additional treatment day reducing the risk by 8.3%, even after accounting for immortal-time bias. Although a similar protective trend was observed for 90-day mortality, the association did not reach statistical significance. This link suggests that the biological benefits of long-term viral suppression may improve overall recovery outcomes. These findings are consistent with our previous analysis of immunocompetent patients with CMV pneumonia, in which delayed antiviral initiation and shorter treatment duration were significantly associated with increased in-hospital and 30-day mortality [14].

The association between elevated serum urea levels and increased mortality in patients with probable CMV pneumonia may reflect the impact of renal impairment on the adequacy of antiviral therapy. In the time-dependent Cox regression analysis, higher urea levels remained significantly associated with increased 30-day mortality, even after accounting for immortal-time bias, whereas no significant association was observed for 90-day mortality. Renal impairment commonly demands dose changes for ganciclovir or valganciclovir to avoid drug-related toxicity; nevertheless, such adjustments may result in suboptimal antiviral exposure [15, 16]. An inadequate dose may result in insufficient viral suppression, prolonged viral replication, and a delayed clinical response, all of which negatively impact outcomes [17]. Higher blood urea levels may serve not only as a sign of disease severity or comorbidity burden, but also as an indirect indicator of reduced treatment efficacy due to cautious dose decreases. This finding highlights the clinical challenge of balancing antiviral efficacy with renal safety in CMV pneumonia, particularly in critically ill or immunocompromised patients, and underscores the need for detailed therapeutic management and individualized dosing strategies to prevent under-treatment in patients with renal impairment.

CMV may contribute to pulmonary pathology through direct cytopathic effects, disruption of local immune defenses, and increased susceptibility to secondary infections [18–20]. In our cohort, co-infections were common, with bacterial pathogens detected in 42.6% of patients and fungal pathogens in 14.8%. These findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting that CMV infection can predispose immunocompromised individuals to opportunistic infections by impairing mucosal immunity and alveolar macrophage function [10, 21, 22]. Despite this co-infection rate, our study found no statistically significant association between bacterial or fungal co-infections and CMV-specific 30-day or in-hospital mortality. It is possible that the severity of the underlying CMV disease, host immune dysfunction, and timing of antiviral therapy have a more decisive influence on mortality than the additive burden of co-infection.

Although baseline CMV DNA levels in blood or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and viral load kinetics during treatment were not associated with mortality in our cohort, this finding warrants cautious interpretation. In patients with hematological malignancies and transplant recipients, prior studies have demonstrated heterogeneous and sometimes conflicting associations between CMV DNAemia and clinical outcomes [23, 24]. While several investigations have linked higher plasma CMV viral loads to increased mortality or CMV disease progression, others have failed to identify robust prognostic thresholds, particularly after adjusting for host-related factors such as the depth of immunosuppression, graft-versus-host disease, or co-infections [10, 13]. Similarly, although elevated CMV DNA levels in BAL fluid have been associated with CMV pneumonitis and pneumonia-attributable mortality in hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients, the lack of assay standardization, variability in sampling techniques, and the challenge of distinguishing true tissue-invasive disease from viral shedding limit the prognostic reliability of these findings [25, 26]. Repeated-measures analysis revealed a significant overall decline in CMV DNA viral load over the course of treatment; however, the pattern of decline was similar in survivors and non-survivors, with no significant interaction between treatment course and mortality status. This suggests that once antiviral therapy is initiated, virologic response per se may not be the main driver of outcome; instead, mortality appears to be determined by the severity of the underlying hematologic disease, organ dysfunction, and competing infectious or non-infectious complications. In particular, the lack of a validated and standardized method for CMV DNA quantification in BAL limits the reliability and comparability of pulmonary viral load measurements [11]. In profoundly immunocompromised patients, CMV DNA detection may reflect underlying immune dysfunction rather than the direct severity of CMV disease in the lungs.

Although hematopoietic cell transplantation was common in our cohort, allogeneic and autologous HCT were not consistently associated with early mortality in multivariable analysis. This may be due to the limited sample size and heterogeneity in transplant-related factors. Likewise, baseline clinical parameters, including fever, oxygen saturation, and inflammatory markers, did not differ significantly between survivors and non-survivors. These findings suggest that routine clinical measures at the time of CMV diagnosis may be insufficient for early risk stratification, underscoring the need for more comprehensive risk assessment.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Its retrospective, single-center design and relatively small sample size reduce the power to detect modest associations, particularly within subgroups such as HCT recipients. Second, antiviral therapy was not standardized, and detailed information regarding dose adjustments, drug-related toxicity, and treatment interruptions was not uniformly available. Although antiviral treatment duration was analyzed using a time-dependent Cox regression model to minimize immortal-time bias, residual confounding by indication cannot be fully excluded. Patients with better hematologic reserve, a more favorable response to chemotherapy, preserved organ function, or better overall clinical status may have been more likely to tolerate prolonged antiviral therapy. Therefore, the observed association between longer antiviral treatment duration and improved survival should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of causality.

In conclusion, among hematologic malignancy patients with CMV infection, we observed high early and late mortality rates. Prolonged antiviral therapy and preserved renal function were the key factors associated with improved survival, whereas baseline CMV DNA load and the trajectory of viral decline did not discriminate survivors from non-survivors. These findings suggest that host factors and the ability to maintain effective treatment may be more critical than virologic parameters alone in determining outcomes, highlighting the need for prospective studies to validate optimal treatment durations and risk-stratification strategies in this vulnerable patient population.
