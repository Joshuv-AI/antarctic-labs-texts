β1 Autoantibodies Stimulate T Cells to Become Cardiotoxic
Authors: Jop H. van Berlo, Esther Lutgens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

In recent years, the immune system has emerged as a central regulator in the pathogenesis of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. In particular, the role of the innate immune system, and specifically macrophages, has garnered a lot of attention that has elucidated important roles for macrophages in the homeostasis of cardiac metabolism and rhythm.1 Despite this recent interest in the innate immune system, it has long been known that the adaptive immune system can contribute to the development of heart failure. One example is autoimmune activation, resulting in the production of autoantibodies.2

However, the exact mechanisms driving the breakdown of self-tolerance in heart failure remain incompletely understood. A likely cause for the emergence of autoantibodies is exposure to intracellular proteins because of cardiomyocyte damage, which can result in immune activation, but why T and/or B cells evade central and peripheral checkpoints is not clear. A common feature of the emergence of autoantibodies is their escape from self-tolerance, which is typically prevented via autoantigen removal through several central and peripheral checkpoints.3 Proposed mechanisms include lack of expression of cardiac self-antigens in the thymus.4 Alternatively, molecular mimicry can contribute to the development of autoantibodies, such as in Chagas disease. Beyond these, there are other mechanisms for the emergence of autoantibodies through peripheral tolerance failure.5 Autoantibodies are fairly common in heart failure, although antigenic targets vary by disease etiology (ischemic vs nonischemic vs other causes).2 Regardless of the exact mechanism of autoantibody emergence, the frequency of autoantibodies varies widely in patients with heart failure of different etiologies, with detection in healthy subjects leading to questions regarding their pathogenicity.

Among the most compelling pathogenic drivers identified are autoantibodies that target the β1-adrenergic receptor in an agonistic manner in patients with idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy.6 First detected in dilated cardiomyopathy, these β1-adrenergic receptor autoantibodies (β1-AAs) have been identified across diverse heart failure etiologies. Direct evidence of their pathogenicity comes from animal models, where animals are first immunized, followed by serum transfer in a separate set of animals. The transfer of immunized serum resulted in cardiac dilation and dysfunction, proving that β1-AAs are sufficient and could be causal in heart failure development.6

In the current issue of JACC: Basic to Translational Science, Sun et al7 build on these findings by identifying β1-AAs in 16 out of 28 patients with heart failure from their hospital and found that this coincided with an increase in the CD4+CD28– T cell population. The loss of CD28 in CD4+ T cells has been associated with repeated antigenic stimulation (by autoantigens), and it can be observed in autoimmune diseases but also in vascular inflammation. Loss of CD28 costimulation renders these T cells less responsive to normal immune regulation, for example by regulatory T cells. CD4+CD28– T cells are also resistant to apoptosis, persist for long periods, and expand during chronic inflammation.8 Unlike classic CD4+CD28+ T helper cells, CD4+CD28– T cells acquire cytotoxic properties, thereby causing sustained tissue damage and chronic inflammation.9

Sun et al7 observed a parallel phenotype in murine models. Passively β1-AA immunized mice showed cardiac dilation and dysfunction, alongside CD4+ T cells infiltration to the myocardium. Next, the authors adoptively transferred the CD4+ T cells that emerged in response to β1-AA immunization in nude mice to directly assess their pathogenicity. Importantly, the authors included the transfer of CD4+ T cells derived from mice that were not previously immunized with β1-AA as a negative control. Remarkably, within 3 days after the adoptive transfer, recipient mice displayed cardiac dilation and dysfunction, with electrocardiographic evidence for conduction abnormalities. These effects were absent in the adoptive transfer of control CD4+ T cells. To further prove that β1-AA stimulation of CD4+ T cells directly affected CD28, the authors administered metoprolol to block β1-receptors on T cells (and other cells), which prevented the development of dysfunction, and it prevented the emergence of CD4+CD28– T cells, suggesting a direct effect of β1-receptors on the expression of CD28 by T cells. Furthermore, the authors assessed if inhibition of CD28 by itself was sufficient to turn CD4+ T cells into cytotoxic CD4+ T cells and become pathological. They isolated splenic CD4+ T cells and transduced them with CD28-targeted siRNAs that were then adoptively transferred. These CD4+CD28– T cells recapitulated the decline in cardiac function observed with β1-AA–induced CD4+CD28– T cells. However, CD4+CD28– T cells are a rather rare phenomenon in mice and are only observed in conditions of severe or acute immune activation. Their biology is considered to differ from their human counterparts.10 The observation that CD4+CD28+ T cells recapitulate the cardiac phenotype observed is interesting, but an in-depth characterization of these murine T cells, and comparison with their human counterpart in heart failure patients, is lacking. Finally, the authors ensured that the observed effects were specific to β1-adrenergic receptors. They isolated splenic T cells and stimulated them with β1, β2, and α adrenergic receptor agonists, where they found that only β1 receptor agonism induced CD28 downregulation, while blocking α or β2 adrenergic receptors also led to CD28 downregulation without effects on PD1 expression.

Together, these findings establish that CD4+ T cells are critical mediators in the pathological effects of β1-AA in the setting of heart failure development, expanding the paradigm of autoantibody-mediated injury well beyond the direct cardiomyocyte β-receptor stimulation and evasion of desensitization. The emergence of cytotoxic CD4+CD28– T cells is intriguing, as these are commonly found in autoimmune diseases. Future studies should delineate the intracellular signaling mechanisms that control if and how CD4+ T cells downregulate CD28 to clarify how adrenergic signaling reprograms CD4+ T helper cells toward a cytotoxic phenotype that drives chronic inflammation.

Translating these findings might yield therapeutic strategies that target the transition of CD4+CD28+ T cells to become cytotoxic CD4+CD28– T cells, which could potentially be more broadly applied than plasmapheresis, immunoadsorption, or B cell depletion for patients with β1-AAs.
