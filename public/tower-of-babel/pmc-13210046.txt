Design and psychometric evaluation of nurses’ social health tool
Authors: Leila Ghanbari-Afra, Zahra Tagharrobi, Zahra Sooki, Khadijeh Sharifi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Given the impact of nurses’ social health on the quality of care, its evaluation is essential. To evaluate social health, we need a specific and appropriate tool. The aim of this study was to design and psychometrically evaluate a social health tool for nurses.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This exploratory mixed-method study was conducted in 2024 in Kashan, Iran. The initial version of the tool was designed based on a qualitative study. Face and content validity were assessed using both quantitative and qualitative methods. The construct validity of the tool was assessed using exploratory factor analysis on 300 nurses. Reliability was assessed through using internal consistency, stability, and ceiling and floor effects. The Keyes Social Health Scale was used to assess convergent validity. Eigenvalues > 1 and scree plot were used to extract the factors. Data were analyzed in SPSS version 26 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) using exploratory factor analysis, Cronbach’s alpha, Pearson’s alpha, and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).RESULTS:The final form of the Nurses’ Social Health Questionnaire had 47 items. Exploratory factor analysis identified six factors that were able to explain 48.445% of the total variance of the tool score. Cronbach’s alpha and omega coefficients were 0.764 and 0.806, respectively. The ICC, standard error of measurement, and smallest detectable change coefficients were estimated to be 0.976, 3.767, and 5.379, respectively. The frequency of the minimum and maximum possible scores obtained by the tool was zero. There was no statistically significant relationship with the Keyes scale.CONCLUSION:The 47-item questionnaire was designed to be a valid and reliable tool for measuring the social health of nurses. The results of this study can be used for decision making and planning in the area of social health for nurses.

Introduction

Social health is defined as a coherent, complex and multidimensional concept.[1] Social health is the ability of individuals to interact effectively with others and society to establish appropriate personal relationships in fulfilling their social roles.[2] Social health is defined as an individual’s perception and experience of having good conditions, satisfaction with their environment, social interaction, and a sense of happiness.[1] In other words, social health is an assessment of how individuals live and function in society.[2] Experts have proposed several dimensions for this concept. Keyes proposes five dimensions of social integration, social contribution, social coherence, social actualization, and social acceptance for social health.[3] Larson proposes two main dimensions for social health: social adjustment (satisfaction with relationships and problems, playing social roles, and adapting to the environment) and social support (the extent of social relationships and satisfaction with them).[4] For Rafiey et al., the dimensions of social health include social interaction, social responsibility, conscientiousness, attitude toward society, empathy, family relationship, and social participation.[5]

Social health is essential for the general public and the employees of all institutions. It is even more important for some professions, such as nursing, which have broader relationships with patients and families from different cultures.

Given the nature of their profession, including personnel shortages,[6] multiple tasks, conflict and inadequate support, rotating shifts, physical problems, complexity of nursing services,[7] work/family conflict, and lifestyle disruption,[8] nurses are exposed to more physical, physiological, psychological and social stressors than other professions,[9] making their health, especially social health, vulnerable. In this regard, the results of a study conducted by Kim et al. in Korea showed that 3.6% of nurses enjoyed social health, 54.6% were potentially susceptible to social health disorders, and 41.7% were in the high-risk group.[10] The results of a study by Lewko et al., also showed that the lowest general health scores of nurses were associated with the social health dimensions.[11] Additionally, a study by Farahaninia et al. reported that Iranian nurses had an average level of social health.[12]

The suboptimal social health status of nurses has a wide range of consequences. These consequences include poor functioning in multiple roles, problems with professionalization of the field, fatigue, absenteeism, increased workload,[13] stress and anxiety,[14] job dissatisfaction,[15] decreased quality of life,[12] decreased self-efficacy, and decreased quantity and quality of services and care provided.[13] Although nurses do not achieve the desired level of social health, researchers have rarely addressed this important aspect of health in this group of healthcare providers.

The attention of researchers and nursing managers is needed to improve the social health of nurses. In this regard, before proposing any solution, it is necessary to first assess the current situation using specific and appropriate tools. The Keyes Social Health Scale has often been used to measure nurses’ social health in the few studies conducted in this area. This scale is designed to measure the attitudes of the general public. As mentioned above, to assess the current state of the social health of nurses, we need an appropriate, specific and comprehensive tool. In designing the items of the desired tool, it is essential to pay attention to the cultural infrastructure of society and the organizational culture prevailing in hospitals, and to consider all dimensions of social health in relation to the nature of the nursing profession. Considering these factors, the present study was conducted to design and psychometrically evaluate a social health tool for nurses.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study was conducted in 2024 in Kashan, Iran, using an exploratory mixed-method approach for the design and psychometric evaluation of a tool.

Study participants and sampling

In this study, 300 nurses were selected by stratified random sampling method.

Data collection tool and technique

Phase one: Design of a preliminary version of the tool

The four-step approach of Waltz et al.[16] was used to design the tool. In the first step, the concept to be measured and its constructs were identified through a qualitative study and using a conventional content analysis approach and conducting 21 semi-structured interviews with nurses.[17] In the second step, based on the definition obtained in the first step, the measurement objectives were identified in seven dimensions of social status, commitment, subjective health, hard work, life skills, humanism, and social participation [Figure 1]. In the third step, the initial draft of the tool was developed based on the theoretical definitions of each structure, according to the overall concept and the operational definitions of each dimension, utilizing the objective characteristics of each structure. In the fourth step, the written form of the items was modified based on the structures extracted from the qualitative study, and the scoring method and rules were determined.[16]

Theoretical framework of nurse’s social health and its factors

Phase two: Psychometric evaluation of the tool

This phase was implemented in the fallowing three steps:

First step: Examining face validity and content validity

To assess content validity using both qualitative and quantitative methods, the initial version of the questionnaire was distributed to 10 experts in nursing, sociology, psychology, and psychometrics.[18] To evaluate qualitative content validity, experts were consulted on various aspects, including comprehensibility, language usage, relevant literature, scoring criteria, key components of the concept under study, the overall structure of the tool, and the clarity and simplicity of the items. After presenting the experts’ opinions to the research team, necessary changes were made.

Content validity was assessed quantitatively by calculating the strict content validity ratio (CVR), the content validity index (CVI), and the modified kappa statistic. For each CVR strict item, the degree of necessity was assessed, and the CVI and modified kappa statistic were calculated based on the relevance criterion.[19] The Lawshe table,[19] the Waltz et al. and Bausell index,[16] and the Polit and Beck view[18] were used to evaluate the results of the CVR, CVI, and modified kappa statistics, respectively. The average S-CVI was also considered for the CVI of the entire tool.[19]

To assess the face validity qualitatively, the questionnaire items were read by the first author of the article to 10 nurses separately and they were asked to express their understanding of each item. In case of ambiguity, contradiction, or difficulty in understanding the items, the related questions and ambiguities were recorded and the items were revised by the research team if necessary. To test face validity quantitatively, the tool was administered to 10 nurses and they were asked to rate the significance of each item in measuring nurses’ social health on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not important at all, to 5 = very important). Then, an impact score was calculated for each item, and the items with an impact score >1.5 were retained for further analysis.[19]

Second step: Item analysis, assessment of construct validity (factor analysis and convergent validity), and ceiling and floor effects

Some researchers recommend 100–300 samples for psychometric studies of a tool.[20] Therefore, 300 samples were considered for this study. Inclusion criteria were employment in clinical nursing, at least 6 months of work experience, no known mental disorders (self-reported), and a university degree in nursing. Refusal to answer the questionnaire during its completion was also considered as the exclusion criterion. Sampling was conducted from all wards of hospitals affiliated with Kashan University of Medical Sciences, Iran, using stratified random sampling among eligible individuals from April 15, 2025 to May 15, 2024. From each ward, a number of samples were selected by simple random sampling in proportion to the number of samples required and the number of nurses working in that ward according to the inclusion criteria. Another person was randomly substituted if the selected sample was unavailable or uncooperative.

Data were collected using questionnaires. At the beginning of each shift, the first author of the article visited the wards, delivered the questionnaires to the samples, and collected them at the end of the shift. If the questionnaire was not completed, an agreement was made with the responsible nurse regarding the time she needed to deliver the completed questionnaire. Each nurse was assigned a specific code. Three tools were used to collect data:

1. Demographic information form

This tool includes personal information (age, gender, marital status, education, nationality) and occupational details (work history, position, dominant work shift, current ward), with its qualitative content validity confirmed by five faculty members.

2. The original version of the tool developed to measure the social health of nurses:

The original version of this tool included 70 items in 7 domains of social status (10 items), commitment (9 items), subjective health (6 items), hard work (10 items), life skills (15 items), humanism (11 items), and social participation (9 items). The items were scored on a 4-point Likert scale as never (0), rarely (1), sometimes (2), often (3), and always (4), with possible scores ranging from 0 to 280. Higher scores were associated with better social health.

3. Keys Social health Scale:

The scale has 33 items and 5 subscales of social integration, social contribution, social coherence, social actualization, and social acceptance. The items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5), with the social health score ranging from 33 to 165.[3]

After data collection, item analysis was first performed using the loop method,[18] inappropriate items were removed, and then exploratory factor analysis was performed. Maximum likelihood with varimax rotation was used to extract the data. The eigenvalue > 1 and the scree plot were used to extract the factors. The minimum factor loading was considered to be 0.403. For items with a common factor loading, the higher factor loading of the item and concept was considered.

The Keyes Social Health Scale was used to examine criterion-related validity to test the relationship between the tool and another criterion.[18] The ceiling and floor effects of the tool were examined, so that judgments about the presence of ceiling and floor effects were made based on the relative frequency of samples with the highest and lowest achievable scores.[21]

Third step: Reliability measurement (internal consistency and stability)

The methods of internal consistency and stability were used to assess the reliability of the final version of the developed tool. The internal consistency coefficient of the questionnaire and its subscales was examined on all samples by calculating Cronbach’s alpha and omega coefficients.

To test the stability of the questionnaire through the test-retest method, 10 nurses were randomly selected from the samples and asked to answer the questions of the questionnaire on two occasions, 1 week apart.[21] The intra-cluster correlation coefficient was calculated between the scores of the two occasions. Standard error of measurement (SEM) and smallest detectable change (SDC) were also estimated.[18]

Statistical analysis method

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 16. Quantitative variables were described using measures of dispersion and central tendency, and categorical variables were described using absolute and relative frequencies. The following methods were used to assess various aspects of validity and reliability: The CVI, modified kappa statistic, and CVR were used to assess quantitative content validity; the Impact Coefficient assessed quantitative face validity; the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) examined the correlation of scores from two assessments in the retest; the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test determined the suitability of the data for factor analysis; exploratory factor analysis assessed the construct validity of the instrument; and Cronbach’s alpha and omega coefficients tested the internal consistency of the instrument and its subscales. In addition, Pearson’s test or its alternative, the Spearman-Brown test, was used to assess the convergent validity of the instrument with the Keyes Social Health Scale. Finally, skewness and kurtosis indices were used to test the normality of the quantitative data, with a range of ± 2 considered indicative of normality. The ceiling and floor effects were also examined by calculating the relative frequency. The SEM was calculated according to formula 1, where SD is the sum of the observed scores in the two test and retest phases. The SDC was also estimated with 95% confidence using formula 2.[18] All analyses were performed at a significance level of less than 0.05.

Formula 1: SEM = SD√1-r

Formula 2: SDC = 1.96√(2*SEM)

Ethical considerations

Permission to conduct the research was obtained from the Research Council and Ethics Committee of Kashan University of Medical Sciences (No. 401017 and code IR.KAUMS.NUHEPM.REC.1401.019 dated May 16, 2022). Written informed consent was obtained from all nurses participating in the study. All participants were assured that their information would remain confidential, that the research results would not be published, and that they could withdraw from the study at any time.

Initial phase

The draft of the designed tool had 116 items with 7 dimensions including social status (15 items), commitment (15 items), subjective health (12 items), life skills (26 items), social participation (11 items), hard work (23 items), and humanism (14 items).

Psychometric phase

First step: Examining face validity and content validity

In the qualitative content validity step, 36 items were deleted and the appearance of some items was changed. For example, “I feel obligated to try to solve the problems and issues of my family members” was changed to “because of my job, I am sensitive to solving the problems and issues related to the health of my family members.” At the end of this stage, 80 items remained. Given the apparent changes in some items, the qualitative content validity was re-examined, resulting in the removal of three items and the addition of one. In the quantitative content validity assessment stage, the CVR Strict of all tool items except items 16, 28, 29, 32, 35, 68, 75, and 76 were > 0.62 (Lawshe number). Since the 8 above-mentioned items had similar items in the tool, they were removed at this stage at the discretion of the research team. The CVI and the calculated modified kappa statistic of all items except 28 and 35 were in the range of 0.8 to 1. The average S-CVI was also estimated to be 0.969. The quantitative measurement of face validity showed that the impact score of the items ranged from 2.1 to 5. No changes were made to the instrument during the qualitative face validity phase. In the first psychometric stage, an initial 70-item version was determined for the tool.

Second step: Item analysis, construct validity (factor analysis and convergent validity), and ceiling and floor effects

Analysis of the tool items was conducted using the loop method on 50 nurses from the original sample. The Cronbach’s alpha for the entire tool was calculated to be 0.967. Items 11, 14, and 16 had correlation coefficients less than 0.3, and items 2, 22, 25, 28, 30, 38, and 60 had correlation coefficients >0.7. However, since the alpha coefficient did not change significantly with the removal of any items, all items were retained [Table 2].

Based on convergent validity analysis on 50 samples, no statistically significant relationship was observed between the developed tool and the Keyes Social Health Scale. The relative frequencies of the minimum and maximum possible scores were both zero when measuring the floor and ceiling effects.

Third step: Reliability measurement

The internal consistency and Cronbach’ alpha and omega coefficients of the entire tool were 0.764 and 0.806, respectively. The omega coefficients of the six subscales of personal competencies, social status, social participation, ability to plan and identify educational needs, commitment, and spirituality were 0.923, 0.913, 0.898, 0.864, 0.822, and 0.887, respectively.

In the stability assessment section of the questionnaire, the ICC for test-retest scores of the entire tool was 0.976 (CI: 0.894–0.994, P = 0.001). The SEM was ± 3.767, and the minimum measurable change was calculated to be 5.379 with 0.95% confidence. Scoring was on a 5-point Likert scale, including never (0), rarely (1), sometimes (2), often (3), and always (4). The possible scores range from 0 to 188. Subscale scores were determined by calculating the mean score of the items in that subscale.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to develop and psychometrically evaluate a social health questionnaire for nurses in Iran. The designed 47-item tool demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability in the target population and can measure nurses’ social health scores ranging from 0 to 188. Nurses’ social health tool is divided into four levels that include low (0–47), low intermittent (47.1–94), high intermittent 94.1–141), and high (141.1–188).

The draft of the tool for nurses was designed based on Waltz’s 4-step approach[16] and based on the inductive method. The concept of social health and its constructs were identified in a qualitative study using a conventional content analysis approach.[17]

In the psychometric process, during the qualitative assessment of content validity, changes were made to the tool based on the opinions of experts from different and related fields. The CVR was >0.7, the CVI and modified kappa statistic of the items were >0.8, and the average of S-CVI was 0.971. The experts felt that it was necessary and important to measure content validity to ensure that all important and essential aspects of the desired concept were covered. The more knowledgeable the subjects selected at this stage, the more reliable the process of ensuring content validity would be. In addition, if the average S-CVI is > 0.9, the content validity of the entire tool is considered desirable.[22] Considering the desired values of content validity, it can be said that the designed tool has the necessary criteria to establish content validity. The absence of changes in the tool’s items during the qualitative face validity assessment confirms that the designed items are appropriate and that nurses have no difficulty understanding them. Polit states that the qualitative face validity study supports the ability of the target subjects to understand and comprehend the items of the tool.[18] Moreover, the results of calculating the impact score for all the tool’s items (>1.5) support the desired face validity of the tool.[19]

Using exploratory factor analysis, the construct validity results indicated that the tool was composed of 6 factors (individual competence, social status, social participation, ability to plan and identify educational needs, commitment, and spirituality). The extracted factors were able to explain 48.445% of the total variance of the tool score, with each factor contributing more than 5% to the overall variability. In line with the results of our study, Abachizadeh et al., extracted three factors (relationship with family, relationship with friends and relatives, and relationship with society) that explained 41.40% of the total variance in the Iranian Social Health Questionnaire score.[23] In Rafiey’s study, seven extracted factors (social interaction, social responsibility, conscientiousness, attitude toward society, empathy, family relationship, and social participation) explained 68.25% of the variance in the total score of the Iranian social health tool.[5] Rosário et al., showed that the different factors extracted from the Portuguese version of the Copenhagen Psychosocial Questionnaire in the areas of physical and mental health, job satisfaction and security, job support, work environment and work relationships were able to explain 60% of the variance in the total score of the tool.[24] Some experts argue that the construct validity of the tool is confirmed if the factor loading of the items is adequate and at least 40% of the total score variability is explained by the extracted factors,[19] with each identified factor explaining more than 5% of the total variability.[18] In addition, the variation in the factors extracted from the social health tools highlights the differences in the characteristics of the concept of social health among nurses compared to other populations.

The convergent validity analysis revealed no significant correlation between the scores of the tool developed for this study and the Keyes Social Health Scale. This finding may be influenced by the attitude-based nature of the Keys scale, which focuses on individuals’ perceptions of social health rather than directly measuring their social health status. This scale also deals with general adjustment and compatibility within civil society, while placing less emphasis on communication and the performance of social roles. However, an appropriate instrument must include items in the above areas in order to measure the social health of nurses. It is worth noting that since the nurses’ social health scale was designed to align with the professional culture and the cultural context of Iranian society, the lack of a significant correlation between the two questionnaires was not unexpected.

In this study, the tool demonstrated no ceiling or floor effect. This effect occurs when more than 5% of respondents receive the highest or lowest possible score. The presence of ceiling and floor effects is an indication of insufficient content validity.[21] In addition, Cronbach’s alpha and omega coefficients were calculated to be 0.764 and 0.806, respectively. Since the aforementioned coefficient is higher than 0.7,[19] it can be said that this tool has a desirable internal consistency. The test-retest correlation coefficient was estimated to be 0.976. Li (2015) considered an ICC of 0.8 or greater to indicate satisfactory reliability.[25] Therefore, according to the calculated figures, the stability coefficient is desirable. The SEM and the minimum measurable change were calculated to be ± 7.779 and 4.284, respectively. The small standard error supports the stability of the tool.[19] Considering the score range of the designed tool (0 to 188), the stability and repeatability of the tool can be regarded as desirable, thereby confirming its reliability from this perspective.

Limitations and recommendation

The main strength of the study lies in the use of stratified random sampling for all wards of hospitals affiliated to Kashan University of Medical Sciences and the 100% response rate. One of the limitations of the study is the use of the Keys Social Health Scale, which is an attitude-based scale with concurrent validity. Nurses may be inclined to answer the questions according to social desirability rather than their true feelings. Future research could revise this scale to serve as a tool for managers to evaluate the social health of nurses.

Conclusion

The 47-item questionnaire developed in this study can serve as a valid and reliable tool for health system managers to measure the social health of nurses, enabling them to make informed plans based on their understanding of current conditions in this area. The social health tool can also be used when designing health development training sessions. This tool can also assist clinical nurses in self-appraisal and identifying personal social health, thereby promoting both personal and professional development.
