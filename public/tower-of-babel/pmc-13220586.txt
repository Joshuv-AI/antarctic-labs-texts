“Self-burning is the only way to show how painful women's lives are”. A qualitative study of the social context of suicidal self-burning among Iraqi Kurdish women
Authors: Darya Rostam Ahmed, Jaafar O Ahmed, Karwan Kakabra Kakamad, Talar Jamal Rahim, Nashmeel Rasool HamahAmeen, Reinhard Heun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to understand suicidal self-burning among Iraqi Kurdish women in Iraq by exploring survivors’ motivations, cultural and social influences, community attitudes, and the structural conditions that sustain this phenomenon.MethodsA study was conducted in the Kurdistan region of Iraq between November 2024 and February 2025. Thirteen interviews were conducted with female suicidal self-burning survivors, and six focus groups were held with 36 family and community informants (i.e., religious leaders, community police, healthcare professionals, community members, and civil society representatives). These data were analysed thematically.ResultsSix interconnected themes were identified. (1) Context, meanings, and reasons for Iraqi Kurdish women’s suicidal self-burning: Suicidal self-burning most often occurred in private areas of the home. Survivors described the act as a cry for help and a means of communicating unbearable psychological pain, frequently arising from abuse within the family and community, including forced marriage. (2) Why Iraqi Kurdish women choose self-burning over other suicide methods: Survivors cited accessibility, cultural familiarity, and the symbolic power of fire as key reasons for selecting self-burning over other suicide methods. (3) Historical, cultural, religious, and social roots of Iraqi Kurdish women’s suicidal self-burning: Most informants said that religion, cultural traditions, generational narratives, and media coverage normalize female suicidal self-burning. (4) Community and family responses to Iraqi Kurdish women’s suicidal self-burning: Survivors and their families encountered mixed reactions, including emotional support, stigma, social exclusion, and religious condemnation. (5) Violations of human rights as social determinants of Iraqi Kurdish women’s suicidal self-burning: Survivors and informants identified violations of women’s human rights, particularly systemic inequalities at the disadvantage of women and male violence against women, as drivers of female suicidal self-burning. (6) Access to support and the prevention of Iraqi Kurdish women’s suicidal self-burning: Survivors reported substantial barriers to accessing mental health and social services while informants emphasised that prevention requires comprehensive educational, legal, and economic reforms and psychosocial interventions.ConclusionsSuicidal self-burning by Iraqi Kurdish women is an act of despair and a culturally shaped protest against structural injustices. The prevention of female suicidal self-burning requires multilayered strategies based on a human rights approach, including systemic (e.g. cultural, legal, policy) changes to eliminate female-male inequalities. Introduction

Suicide causes around 720,000 deaths annually and ranks as the third leading cause of mortality among people aged 15 to 29, with 73% of suicides occurring in low- and middle-income countries [1–3]. Suicidal self-burning is a tragic method of suicide that is particularly common in low-income countries [4–7]. Suicidal self-burning is particularly prevalent in the Middle East and in Central and South Asia, where the majority of individuals who engage in suicidal self-burning are women [8–14]. In the Kurdistan region of Iraq, suicidal self-burning accounts for about 36.7% of burn admissions. Suicidal self-burning has high lethality. In Iraq, more than 60% of women who engage in suicidal self-burning die from their injuries [4, 15]. Women who survive suicidal self-burning experience long-term physical and psychological adversities, including loss of work as well as financial and mental health problems [9]. The reasons for suicidal self-burning are influenced by intersecting structural, cultural and gender-norms factors. Documented drivers of women’s suicidal self-burning include child and forced marriage, physical and sexual violence, the code of honour /ownership of women, women’s forced economic dependency on men, and women’s lack of social and legal protection and recourse against violations of their human rights, including their right to self-determination, paid work, and safety [8, 15–17]. These conditions put women in a state of subordination and powerlessness [8, 15, 17, 18]. For some women, suicidal self-burning is a desperate way to protest and end their condition of subordination and powerlessness [17]. Limited access to mental health care and systemic services gaps further exacerbate women’s vulnerability to suicidal self-burning [16]. Furthermore, cultural narratives and the media contribute to the normalisation of self-burning, framing it as a relatively understandable female form of protest against injustice [8, 17, 19–21]. Against this backdrop, the present study aims to explore the cultural and social contexts of suicidal self-burning among Iraqi Kurdish women. It examines community perceptions, structural conditions, and power hierarchies at the disadvantage of Iraqi Kurdish women, with particular attention to the cultural framing of self-burning as an act communicating suffering and a local form of female protest against patriarchal systems..

Method

Study design and setting

This qualitative study used an exploratory approach to examine the social and cultural factors that contribute to suicidal self-burning among Kurdish women in Iraq. In-depth interviews were conducted with women who survived an act of suicidal self-burning (hence forward these interviews will be called Key Informant Interviews (KII)). Focus group discussions (FGDs) were also conducted with family and community informants to gather a diversity of perspectives on female suicidal self-burning. The research was carried out in the Kurdistan region of Iraq, specifically in the Sulaimaniyah governorate and the Soran district, from November 18, 2024, to February 7, 2025.

Procedures

The study team consisted of psychiatrists and clinical psychologists, all with experience working with suicidal self-burning cases. Before starting data collection, the study team received training in interview techniques, communication strategies, assessment tools, and ethical guidelines. The interviews were conducted in safe environments (e.g., in private rooms) to ensure comfort and confidentiality. The interviewers were also trained to monitor signs of distress and prepared to provide immediate psychosocial support or terminate the interview if it seemed necessary to protect the well-being of the participants, especially the suicidal self-burning survivors. All interviews were conducted in person and subsequently transcribed verbatim. The interviews were analysed using thematic analysis. Demographic data were tabulated to generate a detailed profile of the participants.

Sociodemographic information including age, sex, marital status, education, and employment status, was collected from suicidal self-burning survivors participants. Participants in FGD were categorised according to their roles such as (civil society organisations (CSOs), medial personnel, family member, community police, religious leaders, and healthcare professionals). The Key Informant Interviews (KII) with female survivors of suicidal self-burning focused on their personal experiences, drives, and what they believed influenced the self-burning act. FGDs were conducted with family and community informants to explore the social perceptions of suicidal self-burning as well as gender norms of suicidal self-burning and prevention strategies. Both the KII and the FGD followed guidelines to ensure neutrality, active listening, and complete data recording. The analysis of KII and FGD was guided by codes derived from the study objectives. Both data collection tools are provided in Additional Files 1 and 2.

Suicidal self-burning survivors were recruited from the Sulaimani Burn and Plastic Surgery Hospital. Recruitment occurred after they had achieved medical stability and were clinically able to participate. Only women who had been involved in suicidal self-burning were included. Women with accidental burns or without suicidal intent were excluded. Furthermore, six FGDs were conducted with a diversity of informants selected for their knowledge and participation in issues related to self-burning. Each FGD comprised a minimum of three and a maximum of eight participants representing a diversity of perspectives. This strategy allowed a manageable group size for in-depth discussion. Each interview lasted between 50 and 90 min.

Ethical considerations and informed consent

This study was approved by the Scientific and Human Subjects Committee of the Department of Psychology of Soran University, Soran, Iraq (Approval No. 5B) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All interviews were conducted using trauma-informed procedures (e.g., neutral and non-judgmental language, non-leading questions, supportive communication, and active monitoring of distress) to minimise harm. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Given the sensitivity of suicidal self-burning and local social dynamics, when contacting survivors at home or at bedside, we sought the courtesy permission to approach the survivor from adult male and female family members; however, participation was determined solely by the survivor’s voluntary consent. Confidentiality was stressed, and all audio files and deidentified materials were stored on encrypted drives with access restricted to the study team. All audio files and transcripts will be securely deleted after the publication of the study.

Participants

The study comprised 49 participants, including 13 women survivors of suicidal self-burning, the majority of whom were single and unemployed. The remaining 36 participants were family and community informants (approximately half female), including first- and second-degree relatives of survivors, as well as representatives of civil society organisations (CSOs) such as social activists and media personnel, healthcare professionals, community members, community police, and religious leaders. The ages of the informants ranged from 26 to 60 years. Detailed sociodemographic characteristics of suicidal self-burning are presented in Table 1.

Data analyses

The qualitative data from KII and the FGD were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, a flexible approach well suited to exploring shared meanings and sociocultural processes in health research [22, 23]. The analysis was primarily inductive, guided by the goals of the study and a human-rights-oriented, critical human rights-orientated perspective on gender norms, power, and structural inequalities, while addressing both semantic and latent meanings [23, 24]. The transcripts were read repeatedly for familiarisation, then coded line by line across the dataset, with similar segments coded consistently [22]. Codes were compared and clustered into candidate themes, which were iteratively reviewed, refined, and finally defined to ensure coherence and distinctiveness [22, 25]. Analytical rigour was supported through reflexive discussions, maintenance of an audit trail, and triangulation of survivor and family/community informant data [26]. Illustrative verbatim quotations are presented in the Results section as evidence for, and to contextualise each theme [22].

Results

Thematic framework and analysis

Six themes were identified. Each theme encompassed subthemes. The themes and subthemes are synthesised in Fig. 1. In what follows, each theme is described in detail with supporting quotes from informants.

Thematic framework of suicidal self-burning among Iraqi Kurdish women

Theme 1: Context, meanings, and reasons for Iraqi Kurdish women’s suicidal self-burning

Female survivors typically said that their suicidal self-burning act occurred in private areas within the home, such as the bathroom, kitchen or basement, where people have privacy and access to flammable materials. The timing of these incidents varied depending on the intent. Suicidal self-burning acts were more likely to occur when no one else is present, often early in the morning or late at night. The most commonly used material was kerosene because it is inexpensive and widely available in households. A police officer said: “Kerosene and lighters are more common because they will be found in every house in Kurdistan”.

What suicidal self-burning meant to its Iraqi Kurdish female survivors

What suicidal self-burning meant to its Iraqi Kurdish female survivors

For many Iraqi Kurdish female survivors, suicidal self-burning was a powerful and symbolic act that reflected their inner turmoil and unspoken struggles. One survivor expressed it as “showing deep personal pain in women’s hearts, shouting loudly for understanding women’s problems.” Other survivors described self-burning as a way of “shouting to be heard”, a symbolic act to draw attention to their suffering. A survivor said: “This is a symbol to show others the pain we live in every single minute”.

For some, self-burning was followed by a sense of deep regret and shame. A survivor said: “It’s a pain that will end, but people will talk about you forever”, highlighting the enduring stigma associated with such acts. Many simply described it as a “mistake,” an action driven by overwhelming emotions that they later wish they could undo. Another one described it as a cry for help: “Self-burning is a woman’s cry for help. It is a sign of deep psychological and emotional problems within women”. Reasons for Iraqi Kurdish women’s suicidal self-burning

Reasons for Iraqi Kurdish women’s suicidal self-burning

Iraqi Kurdish female survivors gave many reasons for their suicidal self-burning. Most of the reasons had to do with violations of women’s rights for self-determination, agency, safety from violence, and dignity. One survivor explained, “My family prevented me from making decisions about my own life.” Forced and/or child marriage was a major factor too. Having been the victim of physical and emotional abuse was another common reason. Survivors described dehumanisation: “We are not treated as human beings; they think we are just a machine’ and “Whatever women do, it’s related to honour.” Police officers confirmed that Iraqi Kurdish women endure higher rates of violence, One noted: “They think it is the only way to end their lives.” Mental health problems, including depression and schizophrenia, were triggered or intensified by abuse, the stigma and limited services to escape abuse and alleviate mental pain were considered other reasons. One survivor stated: “Mental health problems are ignored; we are called ‘psycho’ instead of getting help,” while another recounted, “Due to my condition, ‘schizophrenia’, I ran out of my medication for four days. During that time, I began to self-harm and unfortunately burnt myself.” Family and community informants recognised that this lack of care increased the vulnerability of women to suicidal self-burning. The enforced subordination of Iraqi Kurdish women to their husbands, fathers, brothers and in-laws, with no options for recourse, created a hostile family environment for women. A survivor explained: “My mother-in-law always caused me problems.” Community members highlighted the absence of family and community support: “Women have no or less support from family and community; whatever happens in the family, they blame women.” Structural and environmental factors also played a role. Health professionals observed that the fact that women are expected to take care of the home gave women easy access to kerosene and fire: “Women are more likely to have access to kerosene and fire because of their household duties.” Religious leaders trivialised Iraqi Kurdish women’s self-burning as a sign of their emotional sensitivity. For example, a religious leader proclaimed: “Women are more emotional and think less about the long-term consequences.” Finally, the media personnel was said to normalise the self-burning of Kurdish women in Iraq by portraying it as common and culturally accepted. One community member noted: “The extensive coverage of self-burning has become a cultural norm in Kurdistan.” A word cloud of female suicidal self-burning drivers is in Fig. 2.

Theme 2: Why Iraqi Kurdish women choose self-burning over other suicide methods

Iraqi Kurdish women identified several reasons for turning to self-burning rather than other methods of suicide. The reasons were easy access, symbolic power, cultural normalisation, and limited alternatives. Accessibility and familiarity were key. Fire, kerosene, and matches/lighter were available in almost every household, especially due to the daily kitchen responsibilities. A media personnel said: rn“It’s easier and does not require any planning; Women can quickly get kerosene and a match.” Similarly, a health professional emphasised, “Fire is always available, and women are familiar with it due to their household duties”. Symbolic expressions of pain were a reason reported by survivors of suicidal self-burning. They described fire as the ultimate symbol of suffering because fire hurts and leaves permanent marks that force others to witness their struggles. A survivor explained: “Nothing hurts more than fire and leaves a mark on the body. It is the only way to show how painful women’s lives are.” Another survivor recalled: “At that time, I thought they would finally listen to me, realise my problems, and start to care about me. But unfortunately, nothing changed.” The lack of alternatives also pushed women toward self-burning. Firearms and lethal drugs were described as difficult to access or use, while jumping from heights was rarely possible. A police officer dismissevely said: “Women do not know how to use guns or access other means.” Cultural influences were also said to normalise women’s choice to burn themselves out for suicide. Several informants said that traditional narratives from the media and community frame self-burning as an expected female response to distress. A social activist remarked: “This is becoming a culture, any woman who has a problem must commit suicide by self- burning if no one listens to her.” A survivor added: “All women in Kurdish culture use self-burning to end their lives; I think this is the way women should die.” Finally, some informants linked the choice of self-burning to psychological reasons. Although often planned, self-burning also occurred during moments of acute distress, they said. A health professional expressed the belief that self-burning is chosen because the act is “a sudden upheaval, not a planned and programmed one.” Other informants interpreted the self-burning of Kurdish Iraqi women as a way of intimidation or protest without the intention of dying. For example, a family member said: “Sometimes, her intention is not to commit suicide, but rather to scare others such as her family, her husband, or her parents.”

Theme 3: Historical, cultural, religious, and social roots of Iraqi Kurdish women’s suicidal self-burning

Most of the informants said that self-burning is deeply embedded in Kurdish society through historical, cultural, religious, and social traditions. They thought that religious interpretations, cultural traditions, protest narratives, intergenerational transmission, and the media generally converged to normalise suicidal self-burning.

Religious texts were described as expressing contradictory ideas regarding suicidal self-burning. On the one hand, Islamic teachings prohibit suicide. A religious leader said: “The Holy Quran says that there is a very clear prohibition of suicide, which is not permissible in any way” A health professional noted: “The religion itself is beautiful, but our people misuse it against women.” Cultural and historical traditions were also considered influential. Fire carries symbolic meaning in Kurdish mythology and in rituals such as those of Nowruz, when fire is associated with renewal, resistance, and honour. These narratives intersect with norms whereby women’s submission to men is required for male dignity and honour. A community member explained: “In our culture, if women face shameful problems and dishonour their families, they burn themselves.” Self-burning was also described as having historically been an act of protest against oppression: “In the past, it was used as a protest against oppression to show how Kurdish women face challenges and need freedom,” a community member said. Generational and family narratives were believed to normalise the act. Stories of women burning themselves were said to be passed down through generations, reinforcing its acceptance. One survivor shared: “We have always heard that if a woman lived a bitter life, she burnt herself to escape or punish those who hurt her.” The media personnel was said to amplify the normalisation of female suicidal self-burning. The frequent coverage of suicidal self-burning incidents, including on social media, were believed to reinforce modeling. A survivor said: “I saw a video on TikTok where people burnt themselves as a form of protest, and it gave me the idea that self-harm could be a way of dealing with the situation.”

Theme 4: Community and family responses to Iraqi Kurdish women’s suicidal self-burning

Despite Iraqi Kurdish society having views and attitudes that in many ways normalise suicidal self-burning, the response to a woman who engages in suicidal self-burning was said to be overwhelmingly negative. Informants said that women who engage in suicidal self-burning are considered dishonourable, shameful, weak, and sinful. Some survivors reported that their actions were dismissed as selfish or attention-seeking. As a result, suicidal self-burning survivors and their families often experienced harsh judgment, humiliation, and social isolation. Religious beliefs frame self-burning as moral and spiritual failure, which reinforces stigma. Families of Iraqi Kurdish women who died from self-burn suicide were negatively judged by their communities, which was said to lead many to conceal incidents or misrepresent them as accidents. Family reactions to female suicidal self-burning survivors varied. Some families initially provided sympathy and care. However, the initial caring reaction often changed to blame and criticism. Some families even sought to enable more self-burning behaviour by supplying tools such as lighters or kerosene. For suicidal self-burning survivors’ reintegration into society was difficult. Many faced extended stigma and were labelled foolish or weak, which deepened their isolation. In contrast, healthcare providers in burn hospitals were generally described as compassionate and respectful. Theme 5: Violations of human rights as social determinants of Iraqi Kurdish female suicidal self-burning

Both survivors and informants identified violations of human rights, including forced marriage, male violence against women, and systemic inequalities at the disadvantage of Iraqi Kurdish women as social determinants of Iraqi Kurdish female suicidal self-burning.

Female-male power asymmetries at the disadvantage of women and culture enabled male control over women were among the violations of women’s human rights believed to contribute to Iraqi Kurdish female suicidal self-burning. Female suicidal self-burning survivors talked about being treated as worthless in their family and community. They also described living in forced submission and rigid obedience to fathers, husbands, and/or brothers. A survivor described how shame was tied to her own identity: “Being female means everything is considered shameful, which directly affects our mental health.” Another explained: “Men think we are doing nothing, even with all the work we do.” The social preference for sons intensified these women’s feelings of worthlessness and inferiority to men. According to a diversity of informants, the burden of managing home and external work leaves many women overwhelmed: “If she works both outside and inside the home, this doubles her responsibilities and pressure,” a health professional said. Control by some men and abuse of women were described as widespread. Suicidal self-burning survivors said that they were treated like prisoners, denied freedom of movement, phone use, or social interaction: “My family treated me like a prisoner, they did not allow me to use a phone or go out.” Double standards for female and male behaviour were mentioned as oppressive and contributing to suicidal self-burning. “Whatever a boy does is not obvious, but a girl must follow traditions,” said a survivor. A community member put it this way: “Boys are free to do whatever they want to do; if they make mistakes, they might receive minimal punishments. But if the female makes the same mistake as the male, she will face violence from family members and be punished so severely.” Emotional neglect within marriage was described as a form of “emotional divorce”, leaving women in silent suffering. Forced marriage, child marriage, and female genital mutilation were also mentioned as contributing to women’s despair and suicidal self-burning. Violence from men family members against women was found to contribute to suicidal self-burning. Some survivors reported blaming themselves for the violence they experienced. One survivor explained, “Whatever happens in our family, they think it is our fault” Like many others, she described experiencing trauma, depression, and hopelessness, adding, “Domestic violence destroys women’s current and future mental health status; it leads women to think about suicide.” Violence forces compliance, said another survivor of suicidal self-burning: “The violence forces you to accept everything, even if you don’t agree with it, and ultimately leads to self-harm.” The cumulative impact of abuse and violence eroded the self-worth of suicidal self-burning women: “Violence directed at you makes you doubt yourself… when you cannot change the situation, you may feel that dying is the only option,” said one of them. Financial dependence further trapped women in the abusive family situation, a health professional noted: “Lack of financial support, being neglected by everyone, and experiencing physical abuse can push someone to the point of self-burn”. A police officer summed this reality in stark terms: “If there is no violence, there will be no self-burn.” Discrimination impacted women’s daily lives, limiting women’s education, paid work, access to financial resources, and freedom of movement. As one survivor put it: “We have no opportunity to move forward; we are in obedience to the men.” Another remarked: “Men can go out at night to anywhere they want, but women are not allowed to go out alone or with their friends.” Yet another survivor said: “Men spend money on things they want, but they do not spend money on their wives.” Discrimination left many survivors feeling powerless: “Discrimination makes us feel incomplete and powerless. This sense of powerlessness leads us to think of ourselves as weak and guilty,” a survivor said.

Theme 6: Access to support and the prevention of Iraqi Kurdish women’s suicidal self-burning

Suicidal self-burning survivors said that support services for women at risk of suicidal self-burning were largely inaccessible. Many had never heard of available help. One survivor said in frustration: “I have never heard anything. Where are the services for us?” Even when services existed, stigma, financial hardship, and restrictions imposed by men on women’s freedom of movement and assistance prevented access. One survivor explained: “Even if there are services, men will not allow women to benefit from them.” The need for psychological support was often viewed by survivors as shameful. Women’s shelters were said to have a negative reputation; they were described as places “for bad people”. Female suicidal survivors also faced bureaucratic obstacles, such as shelter policies that require a family member to accompany them to access shelter services, making violence prevention services impossible to access. A health professional said: “There is generally little support in the community. Families thought it was unnecessary to take the person to a therapist, and going to a psychiatrist is still considered a shame.”

Family and community informants said that the prevention of suicidal self-burning requires legal, educational, social, economic, cultural, and religious changes. Female suicidal self-burning survivors stressed the need for women to have equal dignity and rights as men: “Men must see us as human beings, understand women’s emotions, and treat women as equals.”

Family and community informants called for legal and policy reforms, including stricter punishment for men violence against women in the family, sanctions against forced and child marriage, and support for suicidal self-burning survivors. They also highlighted the need for more availability of free psychological counseling, accessible shelters for women to seek refuge from family violence, along with initiatives to facilitate women’s financial independence. A family member said, “Women should have their own salaries and be financially independent.”

Education about mental health and about female-male equality was considered critical. A media personnel recommended: “In schools, especially for girls aged 13–18, they should provide information on mental health and how to build a strong and confident personality.” The community informants also stressed the value of educational campaigns through television, religious institutions (Friday sermon), and social media: “Raising awareness through TV programmes could be an effective way to reach people of all generations.”

Family and community informants highlighted the importance of promoting women’s economic independence, job opportunities, and access to rehabilitation centres. It was also recommended that families with opportunities for education to support the equal status of women and men in the family: “Changing traditional family dynamics and rules would be very beneficial in reducing these issues within the community.” A social activities said.

Finally, cultural and religious reforms were considered necessary to prevent female suicidal self-burning. At the same time, some informants considered religion as a resource to counter female harmful cultural norms and practices. A community member said: “Religious awareness talks about the respect of the family and the position of women in this world and in heaven. Religion has set limits on all violations against women, saying that women should not be treated as commodities.” Religious leaders were considered by many to be essential allies for community educational campaigns to support of female-male social and economic equality. However, some informants viewed women’s suicidal self-burning as the result of “misunderstandings” caused by exposure to foreign media: “Preventing the presentation of all these foreign dubbed TV series… [which are] the basis of misunderstanding between men and women.”

Discussion

The present study aimed to examine the sociocultural context of suicidal self-burning among Iraqi women in the Kurdistan region of Iraq. Its findings indicated that in this region, suicidal self-burning is a response to systemic and brutal male oppression of women, as well the cultural normalization of female suicidal self-burning as a response to such oppression. Previous work in Iran, Afghanistan, and South Asia has linked female suicidal self-burning to local scripts of suicidal self-burning as female behaviour in response to despair over, for example, forced marriage, men physical and sexual violence against women in the family, and enforced economic dependence on men [1, 2, 4, 11, 13, 17]. This study’s findings added further depth and contextual richness to previous findings on suicidal self-burning by providing detailed examples of despair and protest expressed by female suicidal self-burning survivors, thereby highlighting the local intelligibility of female self-burning as a form of protest [27, 28]. The findings also generated evidence that illuminates the cultural link of suicidal self-burning with Kurdish stories of engaging in fire as a way of resistance and honour [5, 6]. In addition, the findings indicated the potential role of historical traditions and the media in the normalisation of female suicidal self-burning [15]. In this study, the stigma and moral condemnation of Iraqi Kurdish female suicidal self-burning was found to coexist with the stigma and condemnation of women’s suicidal self-burning survivors and their families. Finally, the findings provided additional evidence that Iraqi Kurdish women who engage in suicidal self-burning do so deliberately, as a result of and as a desperate protest against their unbearably oppressive life circumstances. Taken together, the findings of this study point to the importance of prevention strategies that go beyond individual treatment and address systemic female-male inequality. Systemic strategies include imposing sanctions against violations of the human rights of girls and women, including sanctions against male violence against female and forced marriage, and promoting female-male equality, for example, marriage choice and access to education, good paid work, financial resources, and leadership. By documenting the protest meanings and intent of Iraqi Kurdish female suicidal self-burning, this study contributes to rendering visible the systemic cultural, social, and economic determinants of female suicidal self-burning.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is its scope. To our knowledge, this is the first research to examine multiple facets of suicidal self-burning among Iraqi Kurdish women using survivors and diversity informants. Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. Recruiting female survivors of suicidal self-burning and obtaining informed consent was challenging due to stigma and the social sensitivity of self-burning in Kurdish culture. Consequently, the survivor sample was small and selective in unknown ways. Many potential participants declined the invitation to join the study, which constrained the breadth of the perspectives included. Furthermore, the research was conducted in a specific region of the Kurdistan region of Iraq, therefore, the findings may not be generalisable to Kurdish populations of other regions (e.g., Iran, Syria, or Turkey).

Recommendations

Future research on female suicidal self-burning should prioritise the development and testing of a female suicidal self-burning first-aid program tailored for the Iraqi Kurdish context. Future research could also include developing and testing an educational campaign to promote timely and safe help seeking by Iraqi Kurdish women at risk of suicidal self-burning. Research should also seek to develop and test programmes aimed at educating Iraqi Kurdish communities about women’s entitlement to equal rights.

Conclusion

Suicidal self-burning among Iraqi Kurdish women is a desperate protest response to the systemic discrimination, abuse, and violence that these women experience in their family and community, consistent with evidence on female suicidal self-burning in other parts of Central and South Asia [17]. Across regions, the prevention of suicidal female self-burning requires a human rights approach, including ensuring women’s self-determination in all life matters, and women having equal to men access to education, equitably paid work, healthcare, and leadership. Addressing the structural injustices experienced by women is essential to preventing female suicidal self-burning and to ensure dignity, autonomy, and equality for women in the Iraqi Kurdistan region and beyond.

By urging survivor-centred policing, legal redress, data systems, and non-discriminatory policies, the work supports SDG 16: Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions targets 16.1, 16.3, and 16.b. School and community awareness initiatives on mental health and gender norms contribute to SDG 4: Quality Education target 4.7 while measures to reduce stigma and remove structural barriers promote SDG 10: Reduced Inequalities Targets 10.2 and 10.3. Economic empowerment actions for women with cash assistance, jobs, and skills align with SDG 1: No Poverty targets 1.3 and 1.b and SDG 8: Decent Work and Economic Growth Target 8.5. Finally, the proposed interministerial task force and the CSO and faith-leader partnerships advance SDG 17: Partnerships for the Goals target 17.17.
