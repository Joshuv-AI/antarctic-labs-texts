Barriers and facilitators to providing grief counseling for family members after an unexpected patient death: a qualitative study of ICU nurses in China using the COM-B model
Authors: Weibin Zhang, Ying Qian, Li Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntensive Care Unit (ICU) nurses are frequently exposed to sudden patient deaths and are often the first professionals to interact with bereaved families. Providing grief counseling for family members in the context of unexpected death presents unique emotional and professional demands on ICU nurses, who are often the primary providers of bedside care and family support. Although grief support has received growing attention in pediatric and palliative nursing, the role of nurses in delivering grief counseling for family members following unexpected adult death in ICUs remains underexplored, particularly in the Chinese cultural context. In this study, the COM-B (Capability–Opportunity–Motivation–Behavior) model was used to examine factors affecting ICU nurses’ grief counseling behaviors toward family members.MethodsAn exploratory qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured interviews at a tertiary hospital in East China. Purposive sampling was used to recruit registered ICU nurses with at least a bachelor’s degree, one year of clinical experience, and prior experience in providing grief counseling for family members. Data were analyzed using directed content analysis guided by the COM-B framework with NVivo 11 software.ResultsSeventeen ICU nurses participated in this descriptive study. Directed content analysis identified three major themes and eleven subthemes affecting behaviors of grief counseling for family members: capability factors (inadequate grief-related knowledge, deficits in communication skills, and limited emotional self-regulation), opportunity (promotion of humanistic care initiatives, strong external support, sociocultural norms, and unit’s grief-support culture), and motivational factors (professional value, self-efficacy in grief counseling for family members, attitude toward and willingness to provide counseling, and empathy awareness and development). The elements interacted in complex ways, jointly shaping whether nurses were able, willing, and confident to provide grief counseling for family members after unexpected deaths.ConclusionsICU nurses’ grief counseling for family members after unexpected patient death is a complex behavioral process, shaped by internal competencies, external conditions, and cultural norms. Strengthening this practice likely requires targeted education to fill in knowledge and communication gaps, supportive organizational cultures that empower nurses, and strategies that enhance professional identity and motivation. Given the exploratory design and relatively small sample size, these findings must be interpreted with caution. Ultimately, these insights underscore the critical role of nurses in grief support and call for context-sensitive interventions to improve grief care in acute settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04707-8.Keywords: Grief counseling, ICU nurses, Unexpected death, COM-B model, Qualitative study, China, Bereavement support

Introduction

Nurses frequently witness sudden or unexpected patient deaths in healthcare settings such as emergency departments and Intensive Care Units (ICUs). These non-anticipated deaths tend to evoke intense grief reactions in bereaved family members, who may be unprepared for the emotional shock, particularly in ICUs, because the expectation of beneficial health outcome following ICU care is often higher [1]. Studies have shown that over 50% of bereaved family members who lose a loved one in the ICU—particularly following an unexpected death—experience acute grief responses, and a subset may develop complicated grief (CG), a clinical condition of dysfunctional mourning [2, 3]. Recent evidence underscores the seriousness of this problem: a systematic review reported that the prevalence of prolonged grief disorder among bereaved family members following unnatural or unexpected loss ranging from 9.8% to 21.5% across different populations [4], and it was found in a meta-analysis conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic that pooled prevalence rates of grief symptoms and disorders exceeded 30% [5]. These findings highlight the substantial risk of prolonged grief after unexpected deaths and reinforce the urgent need for supportive interventions in ICU settings.

ICU-based bereavement presents distinct challenges compared to other settings. A recent narrative review by Pattison et al. highlighted that ICU deaths are often rapid and medicalized, which intensifies the trauma for families and increases their risk for CG and post-traumatic stress symptoms [6]. The study identified four trajectories of dying in ICUs—rapid unexpected, rapid expected, chronic unexpected, and chronic expected—with the unexpected types contributing most to complicated bereavement. Moreover, factors such as poor communication with staff, inability to say goodbye, and witnessing invasive procedures (e.g., intubation) further exacerbate grief outcomes. Despite these known risks, support to bereavement for families of deceased patients in ICU settings remains fragmented and under-researched, particularly in adult care. The authors emphasized a critical gap in both evidence-based interventions and systemic bereavement follow-up, especially after deaths occurring in ICUs.

Timely grief counseling (GC) for bereaved family members can mitigate such adverse outcomes, but it remains underutilized in ICU settings of adults. Grief counseling for bereaved family members is defined as a structured and supportive process that helps bereaved individuals to cope with loss and re-establish emotional stability [7]. In this study, it refers to the supportive and communicative care provided by nurses to bereaved family members, rather than formal psychotherapy. Nurses are often the primary point of contact with patients’ families and clinical nurses tend to implicitly have the responsibility of providing grief support [8]. However, ICU nurses face unique challenges due to the sudden nature of patient deaths, time-sensitive clinical tasks, and the emotional volatility of bereaved families. These factors may create tension between professional responsibilities and humanistic care, making grief counseling for family members a complex behavioral decision rather than a standardized nursing action.

Nurses supporting bereaved families have been studied globally in the past [9, 10]. However, evidence on nurses’ grief counseling following unexpected patient deaths remains limited in China. Recent qualitative research has shown that Chinese ICU nurses face unique emotional and cultural challenges when providing end-of-life care, as family expectations and communication norms strongly affect how bereavement support is delivered [11]. Similarly, cultural beliefs surrounding death and emotional restraint often conflict with professional nursing values, making it difficult for nurses to openly address grief and provide adequate emotional support to family members [12]. This contrasts with many Western cultures, where individualism is more prominent and grief support may be framed more as a personal or professional service rather than a family-centered responsibility [13, 14]. These contextual factors suggest that ICU nurses’ grief counseling practices in China may be shaped by both individual and sociocultural influences, highlighting the need to explore the barriers and facilitators affecting this critical aspect of family support.

In the Chinese ICU context, grief counseling is not a formalized therapeutic intervention but rather a supportive practice embedded in everyday nursing care [15]. Unlike certified bereavement counseling, it is typically provided spontaneously by bedside nurses as they are often the first to encounter grieving families [16, 17]. In prior studies, we have noted that nurses are frequently the first and sometimes the only healthcare providers available to families in these moments, and families often rely on them for both information and comfort [18]. Such support emphasizes presence, attentive listening, and immediate communication rather than structured psychological techniques.

Furthermore, in most existing literature, nurses’ attitudes or training needs are explored, but the behavioral mechanisms that underlie whether grief counseling is initiated, sustained, or avoided are rarely examined [19]. To address these limitations, in the present study, the COM-B model (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation–Behavior) is used as a theoretical framework. According to the COM-B model, any behavior is the result of the dynamic interaction between an individual’s capabilities (knowledge, skills), opportunities (environmental and social factors), and motivations (reflective and automatic processes) [20]. The COM-B model has been widely used to investigate barriers and facilitators related to healthcare implementation and practice change. Its applications span a range of contexts, including exploring how hospital-based trainers implement antimicrobial stewardship education and training in UK hospital-based care [21], exploring the rehabilitation needs, preferences, barriers, and facilitators of sepsis survivors [22], and investigating barriers and facilitators for integrating pre-exposure prophylaxis into women’s health practices [23]. While alternative implementation frameworks exist, such as Normalisation Process Theory [24] and determinant-based models like the Theoretical Domains Framework [25], the COM-B model was chosen for its breadth and adaptability, allowing for the analysis of virtually any behavior across diverse contexts [26]. Furthermore, COM-B is embedded in the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW) framework [20], which enables researchers to systematically identify barriers and facilitators that affect behavior (C, O, M), and develop tailored interventions accordingly.

This exploratory qualitative study, guided by the COM-B model, aims to identify the barriers and facilitators affecting grief counseling behaviors among ICU nurses in China following unexpected patient deaths. Through qualitative inquiry, the study seeks to identify the interplay of internal and external factors shaping nurses’ decisions and actions, thereby generating initial insights and hypotheses to inform targeted strategies to support grief interventions in clinical practice.

Methods

Study design

A qualitative study using semi-structured interview with ICU nurses was conducted to examine factors affecting ICU nurses’ grief counseling behaviors. Data collection and analysis were guided by the COM-B model. The Consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) checklist were followed to ensure rigor and transparency [27].

Participants

The study was conducted in the Department of General Intensive Care Unit (ICU) at a tertiary general hospital in Zhejiang Province from February to May 2025. The department comprised four ICUs, each having the same function. A purposive sampling method was used to recruit clinical nurses. Research information was distributed by charge nurses to all the bedside nurses in their units. Nurses who expressed interest were invited to contact the research team by email. Eligibility screening was conducted by the researcher according to the following inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria: (1) being a registered nurse currently working in the ICU of the selected hospital; (2) holding a bachelor’s degree or higher in nursing; (3) having at least one year of clinical nursing experience; and (4) having prior experience in providing grief counseling in the ICU. Exclusion criteria: (1) nurses currently on maternity leave, sick leave, or extended leave, thus being unable to perform regular clinical duties; and (2) visiting nurses, nursing interns, or nurses in standardized residency training programs. Recruitment continued until data saturation was reached, when no new information or theme emerged from subsequent interviews.

Development of the interview guide

Before conducting the formal interviews, the researchers developed a preliminary interview guide based on the study objectives and a literature review, with reference to the COM-B model. In the COM-B model, Capability refers to an individual’s ability to perform a behavior, including both physical and psychological capacities; Opportunity includes all external factors (such as environmental and social conditions) that facilitate or enable the behavior; and Motivation involves the mental processes that energize and direct behavior [28].

In the context of this study, Capability refers to whether ICU nurses have the skills necessary to provide grief counseling to bereaved individuals experiencing unexpected death, as well as their ability to regulate their own emotions after encountering others’ negative emotions. Opportunity encompasses external factors that support or encourage ICU nurses to provide grief counseling in such circumstances. Motivation reflects the nurses’ willingness to engage in grief counseling for bereaved individuals facing unexpected loss.

The preliminary interview guide was reviewed and revised by two experts in the field of qualitative study. Additionally, two clinical nurses were invited to participate in pilot interviews. The guide was refined iteratively and finalized as follows:

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted at the time and locations mutually agreed upon with the participants from February to May 2025. Two researchers (one male and one female) conducted the interviews. Interview time and locations were arranged according to participants’ preferences. Interviews were conducted in private and comfortable settings, including a reserved café room in the hospital and a nurses’ duty room, to ensure privacy and comfort.

Before the commencement of the interviews, participants were fully informed of the study’s purpose, methods, and content. With their consent, all interviews were audio-recorded. Also, we emphasized that they could opt out of the interview at any moment when they experienced discomfort. In addition, researchers observed and documented non-verbal cues such as facial expressions and body language throughout the interviews, which were noted in interview memos. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in 24 h after each interview. Collected data included both interview content and participants’ demographic information.

The interviews lasted for 30 to 55 min, with an average duration of 38 min. All participants were audio recorded and interviewed deeply by two researchers, where one researcher conducted the interview and the other observed and recorded. A memorandum and a reflective journal were completed after each interview. Both researchers had training and expertise in qualitative research. No new theme emerged after the 16th and 17th interviews, indicating data saturation, and interview collection was therefore concluded.

Data analysis

In this study, data analysis and transcription checking were carried out by two female researchers. The interview audio recordings were transcribed, and field notes were compiled in 24 h of each interview by the primary researcher (the interviewer). A second researcher, who did not participate in the interviews, verified the accuracy of the transcriptions against the audio recording. All participants were then invited to review their transcripts and provide feedback or corrections to ensure the accuracy of their views. After participant confirmation, the primary researcher performed the initial coding using NVivo 11 software.

Data were analyzed using directed content analysis guided by the COM-B framework [29]. Unlike inductive thematic analysis, in directed content analysis, an existing theoretical model is used to structure the coding process. In this study, the COM-B domains (Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation) served as predetermined categories, in which relevant statements were coded.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval No. 2025 − 0321). Prior to the interviews, informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the researchers assured them of the confidentiality of all interview data by de-identifying and storing in a password-protected computer.

Rigor and reflexivity

Rigor in qualitative research aims to accurately represent participants’ experiences and perspectives [30]. In this study, rigor was maintained throughout the processes of sampling, data collection and data analysis. All three researchers had received training in qualitative research methods and had prior experience conducting interview-based studies. To ensure credibility, member checking was conducted by inviting participants to review and confirm the accuracy of their interview transcripts before analysis. Confirmability was enhanced through an audit trail that documented the research process, ensuring transparency and enabling external evaluation [30]. Transferability was supported by providing detailed descriptions of the study context, participant selection criteria, participant characteristics and the procedures used for data collection and analysis. The COM-B model provided a structured theoretical framework that contributed to the consistency of data interpretation. To minimize potential researcher bias, none of the researchers worked in the ICU where the participants were employed, and none had prior relationships with them.

Through directed content analysis, three major themes and eleven sub-themes were identified, corresponding to capability factors, opportunity factors, and motivational factors. These different elements interact in complex ways, jointly affecting whether nurses are able, willing, and actually engaged in providing grief counseling to bereaved individuals after unexpected deaths. The following sections present a detailed analysis of each theme and its underlying relationships.

Capability factors

In the context of unexpected death in clinical care, ICU nurses generally face multiple capability-related challenges when attempting to provide grief counseling. Interview data revealed that these challenges stem from a combination of factors, including insufficient knowledge, limited communication skills, and difficulties in emotional regulation. These intersecting issues act as barriers to effective grief counseling practices.

Inadequate grief-related knowledge

Although some nurses had received some form of training related to grief counseling, many reported limited retention and difficulty in applying the material—particularly in the emotionally charged circumstances following an unexpected death. In such situations, this knowledge gap often compromised their ability to respond effectively. One nurse noted, “When facing family members bereaved by an unexpected death, I feel unprepared. I can only offer some basic support—such as encouraging them to cry if they need to—but I don’t really know what else to do” (N2). Another explained, “I’ve attended lectures and even talks by grief experts, but I can’t recall much. In real-life situations, I feel that I can only empathize—I don’t have the language or skills to do more” (N6). Others also expressed concern that their limited knowledge might do more harm than good, with one nurse stating, “I’m not confident about my knowledge in grief support. Even the little I know—I fear that it might be wrong, which would just make things worse for the family” (N11). These narratives suggest that gaps in grief-related knowledge—particularly incompleteness, poor retention, and lack of confidence—undermine nurses’ coping strategies and weaken their professional confidence when supporting bereaved families.

Deficits in communication skills

In the high-pressure ICU environment, nurses have few opportunities for hands-on training in grief communication. The sudden nature of unexpected death further increases the intensity of these encounters, making effective communication even more challenging. One nurse reflected, “I’m introverted, so talking to grieving families feels like ‘social death’ for me. I freeze when they cry out things like ‘He was doing better yesterday—Why is he gone today?’ or ‘How am I supposed to live now?’” (N1). Another admitted, “I don’t even know how to comfort people in everyday life. When I see grieving families, I know that I should say or do something to prevent them from doing something extreme—but I just don’t know how” (N5). Others emphasized the professional demands of these situations, with one nurse stating, “Grief counseling is therapeutic communication. It’s not casual conversation. I lack the expertise, and that makes me feel awkward when facing bereaved family members” (N14). These interviews suggest that nurses’ communication challenges come not only from limited expression skills but also from personality traits, insufficient experience, and a lack of training in grief communication and emotional support skills. Such multifaceted capability gaps may hinder their ability to provide timely and appropriate emotional support in clinical practice.

Limited emotional self-regulation

Grief counseling often occurs in high-stress settings following unexpected death, where nurses must manage both the intense emotions of grieving families and their own psychological responses. Several participants described experiences of emotional overload, empathic distress, or avoidance, which hindered their ability to intervene effectively. One nurse recalled, “The ICU environment is already emotionally draining. I once comforted the family of a patient who died suddenly, but they unexpectedly blamed me. That made me really sad—I was afraid that I was falling into depression” (N3). Another admitted, “Whenever a patient dies—especially unexpectedly—I’m terrified of facing the family. Hearing them crying makes me want to cry too. I don’t even have the courage to approach them, let alone offer grief counseling” (N7). Others worried about the long-term effects on their professional functioning, with one stating, “I am concerned that I might not be able to adjust my own emotions after providing grief counseling to bereaved individuals, which could affect my daily nursing work. For example, my lack of focus might lead to a nursing error.” (N16). These reflections suggest that many nurses lack effective strategies for emotional regulation in the context of sudden death. Emotional distress not only reduces confidence and intention to communicate, but also interacts with knowledge gaps and limited communication skills—creating a dynamic and interrelated system of barriers to grief counseling.

Opportunity factors

In clinical practice, whether grief counseling after unexpected patient death is implemented by nurses is not solely dependent on their personal attitudes or capabilities, but it is strongly affected by structural and environmental conditions. External opportunity factors not only provide the necessary resources, but also offer psychological reassurance, acting as a critical bridge that translates internal motivation into actual behavior.

Promotion of humanistic care initiatives

Although sudden death is inherently unpredictable, hospital initiatives that promote humanistic care—such as narrative nursing, a reflective practice using storytelling and empathetic listening to help nurses to connect with patients and families, have provided nurses with practical tools to connect with bereaved families, and the use of encouragement cards has provided nurses with practical tools to connect with bereaved families. One nurse explained, “Our nursing department distributes encouragement cards to each unit. We can write messages to patients or their families, and I think that this is a great tool to support grief counseling for bereaved individuals” (N2). Another noted, “This year, our hospital started training us in narrative nursing. It’s actually an effective way to encourage emotional expression among the bereaved, and I think that it really helps in grief counseling” (N8). Such tools are particularly valuable for short-term communication following unexpected death, as they help to reduce emotional barriers and give nurses institutional support to engage in grief support—a crucial foundation for strengthening internal motivation.

Strong external support

Nurses generally emphasized that immediate grief counseling during acute grief episodes requires strong collaborative support. In practice, they often turned to colleagues, head nurses, or palliative care specialists for assistance. Such external support not only reduces the operational burden but also enhances their sense of confidence and self-efficacy. As one nurse explained, “It’s important to have someone who can help us when we run into problems. We often consult our Advanced Practice Nurses (APNs) or experienced palliative care nurses—They provide us with professional guidance, and we grow through these experiences” (N4). Another added, “Our head nurse takes bereavement cases very seriously. She fully supports us in offering grief counseling and reassures us that she’ll step in if needed. Her attitude really motivates us to act” (N13). The presence—or absence—of such support systems strongly affects whether nurses feel empowered to initiate grief interventions and is essential for sustaining such practices over time.

Influence of Chinese sociocultural norms

Beyond formal institutional support, nurses were also shaped by cultural values embedded in Chinese society—particularly the emphasis on compassion and emotional empathy, or “renqingwei” (human sentiment). In the context of sudden and unexpected loss, these cultural norms often prompt spontaneous acts of compassion. One nurse explained, “I’m not good with words, but watching the family in so much pain—I just can’t ignore it. I have to at least hand them a tissue or say something. Otherwise, I feel bad myself” (N1). Others described grief counseling less as a professional task and more as an ethically motivated act rooted in interpersonal care. As one noted, “I was the one who cared for him till the end. The family was crying—I felt that it was only right to walk over. We Chinese value emotional connection” (N14). Such culturally driven responses may compensate for the absence of formal protocols in some cases, serving as an important motivator that encourages nurses to take the first step toward grief intervention.

Influence of the unit’s grief-support culture

In ICU settings, when some nurses take the initiative to support bereaved families, it can create a guiding atmosphere that shapes the behavior of others in the unit. As one nurse described, “Sometimes I see colleagues offering tissues or gently comforting the family. At that moment, I feel inspired—They become role models for me” (N15). Another shared, “My preceptor is a kind and compassionate nurse. He stays with the bereaved during postmortem care and helps them to say goodbye to the patient. I admire him deeply and hope to be like that, too” (N16). Such examples help to cultivate a shared culture of emotional support, affecting the attitudes and practices of less experienced or less confident staff. In the absence of formal training, this peer-led environment becomes a powerful force guiding ordinary nurses to engage in grief care.

Motivation factors

Nurses’ motivation to provide grief counseling varies significantly among individuals. It is affected not only by professional identity and willingness to act, but also by perceived self-efficacy and emotional regulation. These motivational dynamics are multifaceted, involving personal attitudes, identity as caregivers, and the ability to manage internal states.

Sense of professional value

A nurse’s sense of professional identity directly affects whether they are willing to incorporate grief counseling into their responsibilities after an unexpected patient death. Some participants described the moment of profound encouragement from families, which reinforced their commitment to providing support. One explained, “Sometimes I only say a few comforting words, but it really touches me when the family says thank you. It makes me feel recognized as a nurse and more willing to help others to face the pain of loss” (N1). Another reflected, “I try to accompany families, not just with words but by helping to dress the deceased. When the family bowed to me in gratitude, it felt like my soul was deeply moved. I felt a duty to support them” (N11). In contrast, others felt uncertain or powerless due to role ambiguity. As one nurse shared, “I remember Trudeau’s epitaph. Sure. I’ll comfort grieving families, but I often feel powerless deep down, like ‘I’m not a psychologist—What help can my few words really offer?’” (N3). These accounts suggest that a strong sense of professional value can help to compensate for gaps in knowledge or skill, whereas a weaker sense may suppress the motivation to act.

Self-efficacy in grief counseling

Nurses’ belief in their ability to provide grief counseling played a crucial role in bridging the gap between “being able to” and “daring to” act, especially in the emotionally charged circumstance of unexpected deaths. Some participants expressed strong confidence, even when life-saving efforts failed. One reflected, “As an ICU nurse, I save lives. But I still want to help families waiting outside when that fails. I believe that I can do this” (N8). Another added, “I always say that I’m not capable, but really, it hurts when I see families who have spent so much and still lost their loved one. We all have families. I think that I can learn to help them with training” (N9). However, others doubted their communication skills. One nurse admitted, “I’m really bad at communication. It’s my weakness. Not everyone understands that my silence means kindness. I’m afraid of saying the wrong thing and being complained about (wry smile)” (N5). These accounts highlight how self-efficacy—whether strong or weak—directly shaped nurses’ willingness to step forward and engage in grief counseling.

Attitude toward and willingness to provide counseling

Attitudes strongly affected whether grief counseling after an unexpected death was enacted and whether it became a sustained practice. Some nurses expressed a proactive mindset, emphasizing that even limited efforts could make a difference. One explained, “We may not be as skilled as psychiatrists, but something is better than nothing. Doing something always brings results—This is why I’m willing to try” (N4). Another added, “It’s like a teacher helping a struggling student—You can’t just watch someone sink deeper into grief. I may not have much power, but I still want to extend my hand” (N10). In contrast, others adopted a more hesitant stance, citing professional boundaries and fear of potential disputes. As one nurse reflected, “I understand that it’s important, but grief counseling isn’t technically part of my job. If I don’t do it, I’m not wrong. Honestly, the more you say, the more likely you are to get into trouble—What if it leads to a medical dispute?” (N7). These divergent perspectives illustrate how positive attitudes can motivate engagement, while negative ones may reinforce avoidance.

Empathy awareness and development

Grief counseling after an unexpected death places nurses in emotionally vulnerable situations and highlights the importance of empathy. Several participants noted that such intense encounters also fostered the growth of their humanistic competencies. One explained, “Compared to general wards, we have fewer chances to talk to families. When we actively offer grief counseling, our communication and empathy skills improve” (N1). Another reflected, “Grief counseling is both helping others and helping ourselves. As we support bereaved families, we enhance our own understanding and communication skills” (N4). These positive experiences may reinforce both motivation and self-efficacy, creating a virtuous cycle that supports the practice of grief counseling over time.

Discussion

Strengthening training foundations to enhance practical competence in grief counseling

This exploratory study found that ICU nurse participants commonly reported deficiencies in grief-related theoretical knowledge, communication skills, and emotional regulation, particularly when engaging in grief counseling following unexpected patient deaths. These perceived challenges were often manifested as contributing to heightened anxiety and a sense of helplessness, which in turn could reduce their self-efficacy and initiative in conducting grief interventions.

A domestic survey by Zhang Ting et al. involving 447 nurses similarly indicated a widespread demand for grief counseling training due to knowledge gaps, revealing that existing training content and formats fail to adequately meet clinical needs [8]. International findings echo this concern. For example, in an Australian study of 159 patients who died within 72 h of admission, it was reported that 83.6% of deaths were unexpected, but only 27.0% of families received grief support after the death, and 47.8% had no record of grief or bereavement support either before or after the patient’s death, highlighting the inadequate provision of grief support in cases of unexpected death [31]. These findings mirror the study of Zhang Ting et al. in China. Collectively, both domestic and international evidence point to a consistent need: Bereaved families, particularly following unexpected death, frequently lack timely and adequate grief support.

The present study suggests that professional recognition of grief counseling alone is insufficient to translate into concrete action. There appears to be an urgent need to establish a systematic, context-specific, and practical training support system. For instance, Sima et al. reported that ICU nurses who received ELNEC (End-of-Life Nursing Education Consortium) training showed significantly better knowledge and skills in grief counseling compared to those in the control group [32].

Chinese scholars also recommend that nursing administrators should institutionalize grief counseling training by adopting diversified methods such as bedside teaching, scenario-based simulations, and narrative workshops [33, 34]. These approaches can help nurses to strengthen emotion recognition, communication strategies, and psychological endurance in realistic settings. Integrating training with clinical practice has been shown to significantly improve nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding grief interventions [35].Based on these concerns, in future training development, we could consider incorporating a “sudden-death response module” emphasizing acute calming and emotional regulation techniques. Such training could be explored as a means to help nurses stabilize themselves and effectively engage with the grief responses of bereaved families.

Activating intrinsic motivation to enhance nurses’ willingness to provide grief counseling

The emotional triggers and role internalization identified among our participants reflect the “automatic motivation” mechanism described in the COM-B model, while recognition developed through reflection and feedback aligns with the construction of “reflective motivation.” In this exploratory context, the findings suggest that ICU nurses’ willingness to engage in grief counseling varies significantly, affected by professional values, empathic capacity, prior experiences, emotional resilience, and perceived self-efficacy.

This variation was evident in the participant narratives. Some nurses were deeply moved by a simple “thank you” from a bereaved family member, which motivated them to continue offering support. However, others hesitated due to fears of “saying the wrong thing,” “facing complaints,” or “becoming emotionally overwhelmed.” [36]. For the nurses in this study, the regulatory role of intrinsic motivation is particularly critical.

These results emerging from our data are consistent with the study by Keh Kiong Ong et al., which reported that ICU nurses’ willingness to engage in grief counseling is shaped by their understanding of role boundaries, perceived organizational support, and personal emotion management strategies [37]. Our results further illuminate that nurses may face an internal tension between the desire to help and the fear of making mistakes in the context of “unexpected death”. The abruptness of unexpected patient death appears to intensify this emotional conflict, leaving nurses with little time to prepare psychologically or access appropriate resources for grief counseling.

Based on these challenges, practice interventions could consider systematically incorporating mechanisms to enhance intrinsic motivation—such as reinforcing a sense of achievement, providing timely positive feedback, and strengthening social recognition—to foster a positive psychological cycle. For example, after completing a grief counseling session, nurses can be encouraged to reflect on and document their experiences, building a sense of meaning through emotional processing and peer sharing. Additionally, appreciation letters from families or verbal recognition from head nurses could help reinforce the perceived value of their actions.

Existing studies have highlighted that creating a “psychological safety feedback platform” can enhance nurses’ willingness to express themselves and engage in humanistic care [38]. Consistent with this, the experiences shared in our study indicate that their behavioral consistency and professional identity are significantly reinforced when nurses shift from passively coping to actively responding. Therefore, exploring ways to embed “motivational activation” into both training and management systems could be valuable, potentially serving as a critical psychological resource for sustaining grief counseling behaviors.

Building multi-level collaborative support to optimize the implementation environment for grief counseling

In the aftermath of unexpected deaths, nurses often encounter emotionally charged family reactions that require rapid and coordinated team support [39]. Although grief counseling is recognized for enhancing psychological care for bereaved families, participants in this study reported encountering multiple barriers to its implementation. These included emotional overload, unclear procedures, and limited resources. Their narratives in some degree underscore the particular urgency for clear support systems and team collaboration in such high-stakes situations where emotional responses are intense and immediate. Organizational culture and interdisciplinary support play a key role in mitigating “secondary trauma” experienced by nursing personnel [39]. Consistent with this, nurses in our study described a greater willingness to initiate and sustain grief interventions when they felt supported by head nurses, palliative care specialists, or mental health professionals. Furthermore, their subjective evaluations of the intervention quality were reportedly higher in such environments.

It is therefore suggested that nursing administrators could consider establishing an integrated support framework comprising managerial guidance, team collaboration, and psychological backup. Specific strategies include institutionalizing grief intervention tools such as narrative nursing and standardized operating procedures (SOPs) for grief communication. As a holistic approach integrating emotional and physical care, narrative nursing emphasizes storytelling, reflective listening, and humanistic interaction to strengthen nurses’ empathy and communication with patients and families [40]. Such reflective practices can help nurses process patients’ and families’ emotional experiences more effectively, creating a foundation for more compassionate and meaningful grief support in ICU settings. In addition, create emotional support mechanisms led by head nurses, who play a central role in emotional regulation and responsiveness; and promote interdisciplinary collaboration by incorporating social workers, psychologists, and palliative care nurses into the care team.

Additionally, informal peer-support initiatives—such as “death cafés” and ICU emotional support groups—should be encouraged to create a psychologically safe space for open expression. Previous studies have shown that such interventions significantly reduce occupational burnout and emotional exhaustion among nurses [41, 42]. Consistent with these findings, these preliminary findings also indicate that stronger external support contributes to more stable and sustained grief counseling behaviors. This implies that support systems function not only as practical enablers but also as critical bridges that transform individual motivation into concrete action.

In the context of this study, external opportunities appear to act as essential connectors between “capability” and “motivation,” thereby facilitating the transformation of grief counseling from internal motivation to sustained professional behavior.

The subtle influence of Chinese sociocultural norms on nurses’ grief counseling behaviors

The qualitative data in our study revealed that, in the specific context explored, nurses’ decisions to engage in grief counseling following unexpected death appeared to be influenced not only by explicit factors such as capability, opportunity, and motivation, but also by the implicit impact of Chinese sociocultural values—particularly the concept of renqingwei (human sentiment or relational warmth) in clinical practice.

Many participants described how they would still offer tissues, speak comforting words, or quietly accompany grieving families even without formal institutional mandates. They frequently attributed these actions to a sense of emotional or moral obligation, expressed through phrases like “it just wouldn’t feel right if I do not do it,” “I couldn’t stand by and do nothing,” or “my conscience wouldn’t allow me to ignore it.” This form of “emotionally driven behavior” emerged repeatedly throughout the interviews as a key motivator for nurses’ actions.

Some nurses viewed grief counseling not as a professional duty but as a basic expression of humanity [43]. From their perspective, it was less about fulfilling a job task and more about embodying a naturally warm and caring role. As such, even when they felt inadequately trained or feared misinterpretation, a strong internal sense of moral responsibility compelled them to intervene and provide support.

However, this renqing-based motivation was also described as a potential source of emotional burden. Several nurse participants noted that their hesitation sometimes stemmed not from unwillingness, but from concerns about “not doing it well enough,” “saying something inappropriate,” or “becoming emotionally overwhelmed through deep empathy.” These reflections illustrate how the warmth of Chinese cultural values can be a double-edged sword—providing compassionate momentum on one hand while creating psychological strain on the other hand.

The experiences shared by participants suggest that the relational ethics embedded in Chinese culture subtly shape nurses’ behavioral choices in grief counseling after unexpected death [44]. On the one hand, such cultural norms help to compensate for institutional gaps and provide a flexible source of motivation. On the other hand, they highlight a potential consideration for nursing leadership to acknowledge this cultural context by both protecting the goodwill behind emotionally driven behaviors and establishing clear boundaries through policy and training. This dual approach could be important for helping to prevent emotional exhaustion and ensuring that compassion remains sustainable and appropriately guided in clinical practice.

Limitations and future research

This study also has several limitations. First, the sample was drawn from a single tertiary general hospital, and the results might have been affected by that institution’s specific cultural and organizational context, limiting generalizability. Nevertheless, the findings still provide meaningful reference value for similar clinical environments. Second, as participation was voluntary, the responses might reflect self-selection or expectancy bias and might not capture the full spectrum of attitudes present in the broader clinical workforce. Third, although the interview framework and thematic analysis were guided by the COM-B model, further theoretical depth and exploration of behavioral mechanisms are still needed. Finally, sociocultural norms and healthcare system structures may affect the transferability of the major findings of this study. For instance, in East Asian countries, including China, where emotional restraint is valued, nurses may be less likely to engage fully in grief counseling services, in contrast to those more individualistic and emotionally expressive societies in Western countries. Additionally, differences in staffing ratios, mental health support availability, and institutional prioritization of psychosocial services may also vary widely across regions and countries. The nature of the exploratory study and the relatively small sample size also limited our results.

In future studies, we should consider recruiting nurses from multiple regions, diverse healthcare departments (e.g., emergency departments), and various types of ICUs to enhance sample diversity and validity. Moreover, mixed-methods designs integrating qualitative and quantitative approaches may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the factors that influence grief counseling behaviors. Importantly, these findings should not be generalized to all bereavement contexts; and they are specifically situated in the setting of unexpected patient deaths, where emotional volatility and decision-making pressure are particularly pronounced.

Conclusions

This exploratory study offers insights into the initiation of grief counseling behaviors among ICU nurses in China after unexpected patient deaths likely driven by a dynamic interaction of three key dimensions: Capability, Motivation, and Opportunity, as conceptualized in the COM-B model. Compared with expected deaths, the abrupt and emotionally intense nature of unexpected loss was reported to present unique psychological and procedural challenges for nurses, often requiring nurses to act with limited preparation. Based on these findings, a potential direction for enhancing ICU grief support could involve establishing nurse-led support systems, complemented by the consultation and emotional supervision of licensed clinical psychologists. Such a collaborative framework would potentially strengthen nurses’ confidence and competence in grief counseling while maintaining a humanistic and sustainable model of bereavement support in critical care settings.
