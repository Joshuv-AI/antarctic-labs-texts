2026: A watershed year for RNA therapeutics—From breakthrough vaccines to chronic indications and beyond
Authors: Cesare Di Nitto, Paloma H. Giangrande
Published: 2026

Main text

The year 2026 is shaping up to be a pivotal watershed year for therapeutic RNA. The rapid progression of mRNA technology—once accelerated by emergency pandemic responses—has officially entered a mature, highly diversified phase. Recent milestones from commercial leaders like Moderna demonstrate that mRNA is no longer a niche or temporary platform, but a permanent pillar of modern pharmacology. As highlighted in recent press releases, Moderna’s 2026 late-stage successes in both infectious diseases (such as their combination flu/COVID and next-generation influenza vaccines) and oncology (notably intismeran,1 the individualized neoantigen therapy evaluated alongside pembrolizumab) underscore a crucial transition: linear mRNA can deliver robust, reproducible expression with meaningful clinical endpoints across diverse disease settings.

This momentum in mRNA-based oncology is spreading rapidly across distinct tumor types. In the same week as the Phase 3 readouts for intismeran,1 Brii Biosciences announced the dosing of the first participant with BRII-5395—its internally developed mRNA therapeutic vaccine for advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).2 Evaluated in combination with anti-PD-1 and anti-VEGF therapies, BRII-5395 underscores how therapeutic mRNA cancer vaccines are accelerating into clinic-stage development for high-burden solid tumors. Together, these regulatory and clinical developments validate that lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-formulated mRNA can achieve high translational efficacy and strong synergistic immunogenicity when paired with standard-of-care checkpoint inhibitors.

Individualized neoantigen therapy: Where mRNA has no equal

Intismeran autogene (formerly mRNA-4157/V940) originated at Moderna in 2017 and has been co-developed with Merck since 2018. The underlying science reads deceptively simple: transcribe the RNA via in vitro transcription (IVT), encapsulate it in LNPs, and administer it via intramuscular injection. The delivery logic mirrors the mechanism the world learned during the COVID-19 pandemic—yet the payload could not be more distinct.

Intismeran autogene encodes up to 34 patient-specific neoantigens within a single open reading frame. Translated as a continuous polypeptide chain, the construct relies on the cell’s native proteasomal and endosomal machinery to trim and process the polyepitope into MHC class I and II-presentable fragments, priming host T cells against the patient’s specific tumor architecture.

A sophisticated computational pipeline backs this final sequence. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) analyzes a patient’s tumor biopsy alongside a matched whole-blood germline control, effectively subtracting non-mutated host genetics to isolate somatic variants. Variant expression is quantified, the patient’s specific HLA haplotype is resolved, and machine-learning models rank candidate neoepitopes by predicted binding affinity and immunogenicity. The top 34 candidate epitopes are selected; the rest are discarded.

This platform provides four key clinical and manufacturing advantages over older modalities:•Rapid turnaround: a fully bespoke GMP construct can be synthesized within weeks rather than months.•Single-construct multiplexing: one mRNA strand concatenates dozens of neoepitopes utilizing optimized, non-immunogenic linkers.•Native in vivo processing: endogenous cellular machinery handles translation, proteasomal cleavage, and dual-class MHC presentation without synthetic engineering.•Repeatable dosing: unlike viral vectors, mRNA administration avoids neutralizing anti-vector immunity, enabling unhindered multi-dose regimens.

While peptide cocktails and viral vectors can achieve subsets of these features in isolation, none can simultaneously deliver week-scale individualized polyepitope manufacturing, dual-class MHC presentation, and unhindered repeated administration. This unique confluence explains why intismeran autogene relies on synthetic mRNA. Rather than acting as a prophylactic vaccine to prevent cancer onset, intismeran autogene functions as a personalized therapeutic strategy engineered to eliminate microscopic residual disease and prevent tumor recurrence.

Expanding the horizon: mRNA for chronic indications

Earlier this year in Molecular Therapy – Nucleic Acids, I reflected on the structural and functional convergence driving the RNA field forward, highlighting how optimization of LNP delivery, chemistry, and payloads is expanding RNA therapeutics far beyond transient protein replacement and infectious disease targets.3 A prime example of this evolution comes from recent work out of Thomas Thum’s group (Juchem et al.).4

Investigating Fabry disease—a monogenic lysosomal storage disorder leading to progressive, life-threatening cardiomyopathy—Juchem et al. demonstrated that nucleoside-modified GLA mRNA can effectively rescue cellular and functional Fabry cardiomyopathy phenotypes in human iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes.4 Cardiotoxicity and lysosomal substrate accumulation (such as Gb3) have historically been difficult to manage with conventional enzyme replacement therapies (ERT). The Thum lab’s work provides compelling proof-of-concept that nucleoside-modified mRNA delivered directly to targeted tissues can bypass host immunogenicity, achieve intracellular enzyme restoration, and halt chronic disease progression.4 This study exemplifies how mRNA platform technologies can address chronic genetic diseases previously considered beyond the reach of transient expression vectors.

Next-generation frontiers: Navigating the promises and growing pains of circular RNA

While linear mRNA continues to expand its clinical footprint across oncology and chronic conditions, the therapeutic RNA landscape is simultaneously being reshaped by circular RNA (circRNA) technologies. Inherently resistant to exonucleolytic degradation due to their closed-loop structure, circRNAs offer extended stability, prolonged translation half-lives, and lower intrinsic immunogenicity compared with linear counterparts.

This capability is enabling paradigm shifts in cellular engineering, particularly for in vivo CAR-T generation. Rather than relying on complex, expensive, and time-consuming ex vivo cell harvesting, genetic modification, and viral vector transduction, emerging platforms aim to reprogram immune cells directly inside the patient. RiboX Therapeutics recently provided a key clinical milestone for this modality with human investigator-initiated trial (IIT) data and subsequent U.S. FDA IND clearance for RXIM002—a targeted LNP-delivered circRNA encoding an anti-CD19 CAR.5 Early clinical readouts across severe B cell-mediated autoimmune conditions demonstrated deep B cell depletion and durable non-integrating CAR expression.5

However, the platform’s early clinical experience has also highlighted the complex safety considerations inherent to first-in-class modalities. RiboX disclosed a fatal adverse event in a systemic sclerosis patient with significant baseline comorbidities enrolled in an investigator-initiated trial.5 Such clinical setbacks are a sobering reminder that circular RNA platforms are still navigating the early safety and dosing optimization curves that linear mRNA spent over a decade refining. Much like early linear mRNA platforms encountered challenges with immunogenicity and LNP reactogenicity prior to nucleoside modification and advanced lipid formulation, circRNA simply requires time to catch up to mRNA’s technological maturity. As structural design, purified circularization methods, targeted LNP tropisms, and patient selection criteria continue to evolve, circRNA holds immense potential to mature into a safe, transformative toolkit for in vivo cell engineering.

Perspectives and outlook

The RNA field stands at an unprecedented inflection point in 2026. The clinical breakthroughs from Moderna1,6 and Brii Bio2 demonstrate that linear mRNA is a proven, rapidly expanding modality for cancer immunotherapies and prophylactic vaccines across varied oncologic indications. Concurrently, mechanistic advancements—such as the Thum lab’s work in Fabry disease3 —pave the way for chronic systemic therapies, while next-generation circular RNA platforms open up promising clinical avenues for in vivo cell engineering.4

As delivery vehicles, chemical modifications, and structural designs continue to refine one another, RNA therapeutics are transitioning from specialized single-use interventions to a foundational, versatile class of medicines for human disease.
