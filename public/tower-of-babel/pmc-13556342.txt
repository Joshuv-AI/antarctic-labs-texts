Viral kinetics of the Chikungunya virus ESCA-MAL lineage during the 2025 Guangdong outbreak
Authors: Yuwei He, Chuting Zeng, Shuyan Tan, Xin Zhang, Silin Xiang, Chumin Liang, Shanshan Gao, Baisheng Li, Jing Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•ECSA-MAL lineage of CHIKV shows robust replication and rapid clearance.•Peak venous viremia (median 2.3 × 107 copies/mL) occurs on day 1 of symptom onset.•Older age is significantly associated with higher peak viral loads.•Longitudinal monitoring of capillary blood reveals viral clearance by day 6.

Dear Editor,

Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), an alphavirus transmitted primarily by Aedes mosquitoes, has re-emerged as a significant global public health threat (Silva and Dermody, 2017; De Souza et al., 2025). By November 2025, Guangdong Province, China, had reported more than 20,000 cases of chikungunya, marking the largest outbreak in the region’s history (https://cdcp.gd.gov.cn/ywdt/zdzt/yfjkkyr/yqxx/). The evolutionary landscape of the chikungunya virus (CHIKV) is primarily defined by three phylogenetic genotypes: West African (WA), East/Central/South African (ECSA), and Asian (Volk et al., 2010). The ECSA genotype is characterized by high adaptive plasticity and further divided into distinct lineages. The Indian Ocean Lineage (ECSA-IOL) emerged from East Africa in 2004 and has since spread extensively across the Indian Ocean islands, the Indian subcontinent, Southeast Asia, and Europe (Phadungsombat et al., 2020; Kalyanasundram et al., 2024). The South American Lineage (ECSA-SAL) is primarily distributed in South America, particularly Brazil (De Oliveira Ribeiro et al., 2022). The Middle African Lineage (ECSA-MAL) is rooted in Central Africa and has recently been identified. Genomic surveillance revealed that the causative agent of the 2025 Guangdong outbreak was the ECSA-MAL lineage of chikungunya virus (Gong et al., 2025). Notably, this strain harbors the E1-A226V envelope protein mutation (Wang and Zhang, 2025), which historically facilitated the massive 2005–2006 Indian Ocean epidemic by enhancing replication efficiency in Aedes albopictus (Chen et al., 2021), the dominant vector in southern China.

While the vector competence of Aedes albopictus for the E1-A226V variant is well-documented (Tsetsarkin et al., 2007; Wu et al., 2013), high-resolution data regarding human-host viral kinetics for this specific lineage (ECSA-MAL) remain limited (Gong et al., 2025; Wang and Zhang, 2025). Characterizing the magnitude and duration of viremia is essential for two reasons: (1) it helps define the potential “infectious window” during which patients can transmit the virus to feeding mosquitoes, and (2) the impact of host factors—most notably age—on viral replication intensity and clearance rates remains poorly elucidated, which is essential to refine targeted public health interventions. In this study, we provide a comprehensive analysis of CHIKV viral kinetics using both longitudinal and cross-sectional datasets collected during the 2025 Guangdong outbreak, to address this critical knowledge gap and improve our understanding of its unique epidemic potential.

A total of 1183 patients with laboratory-confirmed CHIKV infection were included in this study, comprising 1146 patients from the cross-sectional venous blood cohort and 37 patients from the longitudinal capillary blood cohort. Baseline demographic analysis (Table 1) revealed no significant difference in age distribution between the two groups (Median age: 46.0 vs. 38.0 years, P = 0.493). The ECSA-MAL lineage was identified as the causative agent of the 2025 Guangdong outbreak through established genomic surveillance (Gong et al., 2025). Although individual sample sequencing was not performed in this study, all cases were locally acquired and had traceable epidemiological links to the outbreak epicenter, indicating uniform viral lineage across the outbreak (samples sources are detailed in Supplementary Table S1). This consistency is further supported by phylogenetic analysis of all publicly available sequences sampled from Guangdong between July and November 2025. We first analyzed paired capillary and venous samples collected simultaneously from the 37 longitudinal patients on the day of hospital admission. Qualitative analysis demonstrated 100% concordance in CHIKV RNA detection between the two sampling methods. While viral loads were significantly higher in venous blood, the diagnostic sensitivity remained equivalent. These results validate capillary blood as a highly reliable, minimally invasive surrogate for early-stage viral detection and provide the methodological basis for subsequent high-frequency longitudinal monitoring.

In this study, Day 1 was defined as the first day of symptom onset. In the longitudinal cohort (n = 37), only one sample was available on the first day, resulting a peak mean viral load recorded on Day 2 (3.57 × 105 copies/mL). Following this peak, viral loads plummeted rapidly, decreasing by 1.49 log10 units by Day 4 (Fig. 1A). However, the larger cross-sectional analysis of 1146 patients demonstrated that viral loads were significantly higher on Day 1 than on Day 2 (Fig. 1B). The longitudinal monitoring based on capillary blood revealed that viral loads typically fell below the limit of detection between Days 5 and 7, providing a high-resolution view of clearance trajectory at the individual level. In contrast, the cross-sectional data from venous blood samples in our study were limited to the early acute phase (Days 1–4) (Fig. 1C). Because this dataset only aggregates positive samples reported by medical facilities, it systematically excludes individuals who have already cleared the virus and transitioned to PCR negativity. Consequently, cross-sectional dataset tend to overestimate the duration of the infectious window. Given that repeated venipuncture for viral kinetic modeling is logistically and ethically impractical for outpatient and field settings, our longitudinal capillary data provide a more accurate estimation of the clearance rate of the ECSA-MAL lineage.

Age-stratified analyses of the venous samples revealed significantly higher and more persistent viral loads during the acute phase in patients aged ≥ 41 years. On days 2 and 3 after onset, middle-aged and elderly patients had median viral loads 6.5 to 12 times higher than those in younger age groups (P < 0.05), indicating a greater potential risk for transmission (Fig. 2A). Analysis of viral load kinetics in venous samples across different age groups (Fig. 2B) demonstrated a decreasing trend in viral load over time in all age groups, but with significant variations in viral clearance rates. Specifically, the 0–17 years group showed the most pronounced viral load reduction by day 4 post-symptom onset, reaching the lowest viral levels among all groups. In contrast, the 61+ years group exhibited the slowest viral clearance and sustained higher viral load levels throughout the observation period, suggesting impaired viral clearance capacity in elderly individuals. The clearance trends for the 18–40 years group and 41–60 years group were intermediate between these two extremes. In contrast to age, no significant gender-based differences in viral clearance were observed in these venous samples (Fig. 2C). Linear regression analysis revealed that neither the baseline viral load (P = 0.679) nor the rate of viral clearance over time (Days × Gender interaction: P = 0.495) differed significantly between males and females. Our study provides a high-resolution characterization of the human viral kinetics of the CHIKV ECSA-MAL lineage during the 2025 Guangdong outbreak. The integration of longitudinal and cross-sectional datasets revealed that the ECSA-MAL lineage undergoes rapid replication and clearance, with viral dynamics marked by a peak within the 24 hours post symptom onset and profiles that vary significantly by age. These findings have implications for defining the transmissibility window and optimizing diagnostic strategies in high-intensity outbreak settings.

Longitudinal capillary sampling revealed that the ECSA-MAL lineage induces high-magnitude viremia primarily within the first 72 hours (Days 1–3) post-symptom onset, followed by a rapid decline in viral load thereafter. This rapid attainment of peak viral loads is a hallmark of the ECSA lineage (Khongwichit et al., 2025), particularly for strains carrying the E1-A226V mutation, which has been shown to enhance replication efficiency in human fibroblasts and significantly increase midgut infectivity in Aedes albopictus (Tsetsarkin et al., 2007; Sokoloski et al., 2013). Our finding of an early, high-titer viremia plateau is consistent with the kinetic profiles of other highly pathogenic arboviruses. For instance, dengue virus (DENV) typically exhibits peak viremia within 1–3 days of fever onset (Nainggolan et al., 2023), and the ECSA-IOL lineage—another globally prevalent and emergent CHIKV variant—also exhibits robust early viral expansion and rapid peak titer formation (Khongwichit et al., 2025).

The high-resolution kinetics captured here are also critical for defining the putative human-to-mosquito transmission window. Previous studies indicate that the minimum infectious dose required to infect 50% (MID50) of Aedes albopictus with the E1-A226V variant ranges from 103 to 104 PFU/mL (Tsetsarkin et al., 2007). Previous studies have established a correlation between genomic RNA copy numbers and infectious titers (PFU) for alphaviruses during the acute phase (Sourisseau et al., 2007). The relatively high viral loads observed during the first three days post-symptom onset underscore a period of heightened transmission risk. However, as the correlation between infectious titers and genomic RNA can vary by lineage, the specific PFU-to-RNA relationship for the ECSA-MAL lineage warrants empirical validation through direct viral isolation and plaque assays in future studies.

We further evaluated the diagnostic utility of finger-prick capillary blood compared to traditional venous serum. Although venous serum remains the gold standard for diagnosis—with reported sensitivities often 10 to 100 times higher due to viral concentration and the absence of cellular interference—our findings suggest that capillary blood is a reliable surrogate during the acute phase. Within the first 4 days of illness, viral loads in capillary blood remain orders of magnitude above the detection limit. The near-non-invasive finger-prick sampling facilitates large-scale community surveillance by reducing reliance on professional phlebotomists and enabling rapid molecular screening without compromising diagnostic yield during peak viremia. However, due to the rapid decline of viral load in capillary blood after the acute phase systemic venous blood must be prioritized for patients presenting more than 5 days post-onset to minimize the risk of false-negative results.

Furthermore, we observed that age significantly modulate these kinetics. Older adults (41 years and above) exhibited markedly higher and more persistent viral loads, suggesting they may have more opportunities serve as transmission reservoirs. This age-dependent viral persistence is further underscored by the detailed viral load trends (Fig. 2C), where younger individuals demonstrate a significantly more rapid viral clearance, particularly by day 4, compared to older age groups, suggesting distinct age-related physiological responses impacting viral elimination. While higher viral loads in older adults may be partially attributed to underlying comorbidities or age-related impairment of innate antiviral immunity, the epidemiological consequence is a significantly expanded infectious window.

Our longitudinal analyses were based on finger-prick capillary blood sampling. Although this method is convenient, the sample volume is smaller than that of venous draws, which may influence the lower limit of viral quantification. However, the concordance between capillary longitudinal data and venous cross-sectional data supports the validity of this method. Furthermore, hospital-based cross-sectional datasets consist exclusively of PCR-positive cases, making them inherently susceptible to selection and survivorship biases. Consequently, population-level kinetic curves may be upwardly skewed during the late phase of infection, as individuals who have already cleared the virus (and thus test negative) are systematically excluded from the analysis.
