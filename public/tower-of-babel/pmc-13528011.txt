Trends in the burden of fire, heat, and hot substances in North Africa and the Middle East, 1990–2023: an analysis of the 2023 Global Burden of Disease Study
Authors: Peiyuan Tang, Runci Lu, Qian Chen, Ting Wen, Wenfeng Xiao, Yusheng Li, Haitao Long, Bangbao Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThermal injuries from fire, heat, and hot substances (FHHS) pose a significant public health challenge in North Africa and the Middle East (NAME). Comprehensive, long-term regional analyses, particularly those including the post-pandemic period, are limited. This study leverages Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 data to provide a comprehensive assessment of the FHHS burden in the NAME region from 1990 to 2023, analyzing the distinct effects of age, period, and cohort to inform targeted interventions.MethodsThis study utilized FHHS data from the GBD 2023 for the 21 countries and territories of the NAME region. We analyzed trends in prevalence, incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). The analysis employed joinpoint regression to identify temporal trend changes, decomposition analysis to isolate the effects of population growth, aging, and epidemiology, and an age–period–cohort model to delineate generational and age-specific risks.ResultsFrom 1990 to 2023, the age-standardized DALY rate for FHHS in NAME declined significantly (EAPC, − 2.6). Absolute prevalence increased in most countries, with increases exceeding 300% in Qatar and the United Arab Emirates. Joinpoint analysis identified an increase in ASIR during 2020–2023 (APC, 0.61%). Decomposition showed that population growth and ageing exerted upward pressure on absolute counts. For deaths, however, these positive demographic contributions were more than offset by favorable changes in age-specific epidemiological rates, resulting in a small net regional decline.ConclusionFrom 1990 to 2023, NAME achieved substantial reductions in age-standardized DALY and death rates from FHHS. Population growth and ageing increased pressure on absolute prevalence and incidence, whereas improved age-specific epidemiological rates outweighed these pressures for deaths. The recent change in ASIR and the increasing burden in older age groups warrant continued surveillance and targeted prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41182-026-01062-x.

Introduction

Thermal injuries from fire, heat, and hot substances (FHHS) represent a persistent and significant public health challenge, particularly within North Africa and the Middle East (NAME) [1]. These injuries are a major source of preventable mortality, long-term disability, and substantial economic burden, disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations such as young children, the elderly, and individuals in low-resource settings [2, 3]. Beyond the immediate physical trauma, burns often lead to devastating consequences including scarring, contractures, psychological distress, and social stigma, impacting quality of life and productivity for survivors and their families [4, 5]. Understanding the evolving burden of these injuries in this specific region is therefore critical for directing effective prevention strategies, trauma care resource allocation, and health policy development.

Earlier GBD analyses have established the global and regional burden of FHHS. James et al. described morbidity and mortality patterns using GBD 2017 estimates through 2017, whereas Huang et al. used GBD 2021 data through 2021 to examine global, regional, SDI-stratified and sex-specific patterns and to project future trends [6, 7]. These studies provide essential benchmarks, but they do not characterize changes through 2023 in NAME while jointly examining age–period–cohort patterns, decomposition of absolute changes, and country-level trajectory groups. To our knowledge, previous NAME-focused FHHS burden reports have not combined these three analytical components.

Using GBD 2023 estimates, we describe the FHHS burden in the 21 NAME countries and territories from 1990 to 2023, assess trends by age and sex, decompose changes in absolute burden, and describe heterogeneity in national trajectories. Including 2022–2023 provides the most recent GBD-based descriptive estimates for the region; these years are not interpreted as evidence of a specific post-pandemic or climate-related causal shift.

Methods

Sources

The GBD 2023 conducted a comprehensive analysis spanning 204 countries and territories. Methodological details of this study have been previously documented in published literature [8, 9], with all datasets publicly accessible through the official GBD data portals (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/). Adherence to the GATHER guidelines [10] ensures the transparency and reliability of health metric estimations. Approval for GBD 2023 was obtained from the University of Washington Institutional Review Board, with informed consent waived due to the exclusive use of anonymized data. This multinational collaboration systematically quantifies population health metrics—including prevalence, mortality, incidence, years lived with disability (YLDs), years of life lost (YLLs), and Disability-adjusted life years (DALYs)—stratified by geographical region, age group, and gender. This study utilized data from the GBD 2023 to investigate the epidemiology of FHHS in NAME and its constituent countries. Metrics such as prevalence, incidence, DALYs, and mortality were analyzed. All estimates incorporated 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) to reflect statistical precision. Epidemiological measures were standardized and expressed as annual rates per 100,000 population, enabling comparative assessments across geographic scales. The analysis focused on the NAME super-region and its national-level constituents to identify variations in disease burden and health outcomes associated with FHHS.

Estimation framework

The GBD study utilized an advanced Bayesian geospatial modeling framework to estimate the epidemiological burden of FHHS. Bayesian meta-regression algorithms with geospatial covariates reconcile heterogeneous data sources, including population health surveys, vital registration systems with nonspecific mortality coding, and radiographic-confirmed FHHS studies. This computational architecture systematically synthesizes demographic patterns, epidemiological relationships, and healthcare access indicators to generate age–sex–location specific estimates of FHHS incidence and prevalence. Prior to analysis, all input data underwent rigorous preprocessing involving outlier correction, uncertainty quantification, and cross-source harmonization to address methodological disparities across studies. Applied to the 2023 global health dataset, the model produced spatially resolved burden estimates through 1000 iterations of Monte Carlo simulations, ensuring probabilistic characterization of uncertainty. DALYs were computed by aggregating YLDs—weighted by FHHS-specific impairment severity from global health surveys—and YLLs derived from reference life expectancy tables. The analytical framework explicitly accounts for variations in diagnostic criteria and study designs through uncertainty propagation algorithms, thereby enabling cross-national comparability of mortality, incidence, and prevalence metrics. The estimates for NAME and its constituent countries analyzed in this study were generated using this comprehensive framework.

Joinpoint regression analysis

Sex-specific annual ASIRs for 1990–2023 were analyzed with Joinpoint Regression Program version 5.1.0 (National Cancer Institute). Rates were analyzed on the logarithmic scale. Standard errors were derived from the 95% uncertainty interval as (upper limit − lower limit)/(2 × 1.96). The program estimated APCs for individual segments and the AAPC for the full period. We used the default configuration for the 34 annual observations: candidate models with 0 to 5 joinpoints, a minimum of two observations between joinpoints and between a joinpoint and either end of the series, and 4499 Monte Carlo permutations for weighted permutation testing with Bonferroni adjustment. We report APCs and AAPCs with 95% confidence intervals and describe the post-2020 change as a temporal observation only [11].

Age–period–cohort model

Incidence counts and population denominators for 20 5-year age groups were arranged into 5-year periods and analyzed with the retained APC estimable-functions implementation. We estimated longitudinal age curves, period and cohort effects, net drift and local drifts. Because age, period and cohort are linearly dependent, their linear components cannot be uniquely identified; period and cohort results are therefore interpreted as estimable deviations and temporal associations, not as independent causal effects. Net drift summarizes the overall annual percentage change, whereas local drift describes age-specific annual percentage changes. This formulation makes the identification constraint explicit and avoids treating period or cohort patterns as uniquely identified effects [12].

Decomposition analysis

We used a symmetric three-factor, Das Gupta-type decomposition to partition the 1990–2023 change in absolute deaths, incidence, prevalence and DALYs into contributions from total population size, population age structure and age-specific epidemiological rates. Interaction terms were allocated symmetrically through the averaging terms of the decomposition formula. Positive and negative components indicate the direction of each contribution rather than causal effects. Numerical closure was checked for all four outcomes; residuals were zero.

Prediction

The retained BAPC workflow was implemented in R with the BAPC and INLA packages [13, 14]. It used observed FHHS counts and population denominators for 20 5-year age groups and sex-specific matrices, together with IHME reference population projections. An APCList was constructed with a generation factor of five. The BAPC model uses a Poisson likelihood with a log link; age, period and cohort effects follow second-order random walks and residual overdispersion is represented by an independent random effect. The retained default BAPC/INLA precision priors were log-gamma (1, 0.00005) for the random-walk effects and log-gamma (1, 0.005) for overdispersion. Age-standardized rates used the GBD standard population weights; second differences were not requested, and the posterior 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles from qapc() were reported as 95% credible intervals. Internal temporal validation used BAPC’s retrospective prediction setting (predict = list(npredict = 19, retro = TRUE)), which predicts held-out recent periods from earlier observed data. This is an internal retrospective assessment, not independent external validation; therefore, we do not claim superior predictive accuracy or report an uncalculated external validation metric.

Clustering analysis

Countries and territories were clustered using Z-standardized EAPCs for ASIR and age-standardized DALY rates. Euclidean distances were calculated and complete-linkage hierarchical clustering was applied to form relatively compact groups while limiting chaining. Mean silhouette widths were 0.454, 0.473, 0.481, 0.480 and 0.420 for two through six clusters, respectively. The three-cluster solution was selected as a parsimonious and epidemiologically interpretable grouping after considering the dendrogram and silhouette diagnostics; it should not be interpreted as a unique statistical optimum. Similar use of country-level trend indicators has been reported in other GBD-based analyses [15].

Results

Regional and national trends

From 1990 to 2023, the NAME region showed a consistent decline in the age-standardized burden of FHHS. The ASPR decreased from 871.5 to 641.0 per 100,000, with an EAPC of − 1.1 (95% CI − 1.17 to − 1.03) (Table 1). The ASIR also declined, from 83.3 to 62.9 per 100,000 population (EAPC: − 1.0; 95% CI − 1.06 to − 0.93) (Fig. 1). Larger reductions were observed for mortality and overall disease burden: the ASDR fell from 3.7 to 1.9 per 100,000, and the DALY rate declined from 221.2 to 104.1 per 100,000 (EAPC: − 2.6; 95% CI − 2.74 to − 2.47) (Supplementary Figures S1 and S2). National burdens remained heterogeneous. Iraq and Libya had among the highest 2023 rates, whereas Turkey and Kuwait had among the lowest death and DALY rates. Despite declining age-standardized rates, absolute prevalence cases increased in most countries, exceeding 300% in Qatar and the United Arab Emirates (Supplementary Figure S3). Joinpoint analysis

Joinpoint regression showed that the ASIR of FHHS declined for most of 1990–2023 but reversed in the most recent period (Fig. 2). For both sexes combined, the decline accelerated most strongly during 2010–2015 (APC: − 2.34%) before slowing during 2015–2020 and shifting to a significant increase in 2020–2023 (APC: 0.61%). Females showed a similar but more pronounced pattern, with the steepest decline during 2010–2015 (APC: − 2.83%) and the strongest rebound after 2020 (APC: 0.98%). Decomposition analysis

Decomposition analysis showed that changes in FHHS deaths, prevalence, incidence, and DALYs were shaped by competing demographic and epidemiological forces (Supplementary Figure S4). Population growth and ageing increased the number of deaths, but the favorable epidemiological component was larger, yielding a small net regional reduction in deaths (approximately 491 deaths) between 1990 and 2023 (Supplementary Figure S4A). Prevalence and incidence increased in absolute terms, chiefly because of population growth; ageing provided an additional positive contribution (Supplementary Figures S4B and S4C). DALYs declined overall because epidemiological improvement outweighed demographic pressures (Supplementary Figure S4D). Rising prevalence should be interpreted cautiously because prevalence is a stock measure influenced by incidence, duration and survival.

Clustering analysis

Hierarchical clustering based on EAPC values for ASIR and DALYs classified the 21 NAME countries and territories into three epidemiological trajectory groups (Supplementary Figure S5). The first cluster showed the most rapid declines in both incidence and DALYs, whereas the second showed moderate improvement, particularly in DALYs. The third cluster, including Afghanistan, Lebanon, Iraq, and Libya, showed stagnant or worsening trends. These groups summarize similarities in the two trend indicators and do not establish common causal determinants.

Predicted trends

Projections for 2024–2050 suggested divergent future trends in the FHHS burden. The ASPR (Fig. 4A) and incidence rate (Fig. 4B) were projected to increase, whereas the ASDR (Fig. 4C) and DALYs rate (Fig. 4D) were projected to continue declining. Discussion

Our analysis provides a long-term, NAME-focused description of FHHS burden from 1990 to 2023. The principal finding was a substantial decline in age-standardized death and DALY rates, accompanied by declining age-standardized prevalence and incidence rates over most of the study period. These improvements were not uniform across countries or outcomes. Absolute prevalence and incidence increased in many settings, and the recent ASIR pattern was less favorable, particularly among females. Earlier global GBD analyses by James et al. and Huang et al. also reported broad reductions in age-standardized FHHS burden [6, 7]. The present update does not establish a fundamentally different regional trajectory from those reports, because the GBD round, time window, geographic focus and analytical approaches differ. Rather, it extends the descriptive record through 2023 and adds a NAME-specific assessment of age–period–cohort patterns, demographic and epidemiological contributions to absolute change, and country-level trajectory groups.

The decomposition results help reconcile declining age-standardized rates with changing absolute burden. Population growth and ageing increased the number of people at risk and exerted positive pressure on the numbers of deaths, incidence cases and prevalent cases. For deaths, however, the favorable change in age-specific epidemiological rates was larger than the combined demographic contributions, yielding a small net regional reduction of approximately 491 deaths between 1990 and 2023. This distinction is important for public-health planning: falling rates do not remove the need to provide services for a growing population, whereas a rising absolute count should not be interpreted as evidence that every component of the underlying burden has worsened. In particular, prevalence is a stock measure influenced by incidence, duration and survival. Its increase may therefore reflect demographic change, longer duration, or survival after injury as well as incident risk. The ecological design cannot identify which country-specific prevention, care or social changes produced these patterns. Previous burn research has documented the substantial and heterogeneous burden of burns in low- and middle-income settings [16, 17]. Improvements in burn care may also influence survival and duration after injury, although such care pathways were not measured in the present analysis [18]. The importance of separating demographic change from changes in age-specific rates is consistent with prior decomposition work on population ageing and mortality [19]. Variation in adult burn outcomes across care settings further supports caution when extrapolating regional patterns to individual health systems [20].

The Joinpoint analysis identified a change from the long-term decline in ASIR to an increase during 2020–2023, with a more pronounced increase among females. This finding warrants surveillance, but it should not be treated as evidence that the COVID-19 pandemic caused the change. Reports from other settings described changes in traumatic injury presentations, burn admissions and care delivery during the pandemic period, but those observations cannot establish the explanation for the present regional pattern [21, 22]. The timing may be compatible with several explanations, including changes in exposure patterns, care seeking, reporting, access to services or the modeling of recent GBD estimates. None of these explanations was measured in the present analysis. The most recent estimates also deserve particular caution in settings with sparse primary data, where modeled information and covariate extrapolation may contribute more strongly to the final estimates. Individual-level studies and country-specific surveillance are needed to determine whether the observed inflection reflects a true change in FHHS incidence and, if so, which exposures or health-system conditions are involved.

Country-level heterogeneity was substantial. The clustering analysis grouped countries according to similar EAPC profiles for ASIR and DALY rates, not according to verified similarities in policy, health-system performance or risk exposure. Egypt, the United Arab Emirates and Kuwait were among the countries in the most rapidly improving group, whereas Afghanistan, Iraq, Libya and Lebanon were in the stagnant or worsening group. These patterns may reflect differences in socioeconomic conditions, prevention, fuel use, housing and workplace safety, access to acute and rehabilitative care, or data availability. They should be regarded as hypotheses for further investigation rather than established determinants. Broader evidence indicates that progress in health outcomes and injury control is shaped by interacting social, economic, health-system and prevention conditions, rather than by a single policy factor [23, 24]. Evidence production on burns is also uneven across low- and middle-income settings, which reinforces the need for cautious country comparisons [25]. In conflict-affected settings, interpretation is especially difficult. Displacement, disrupted registration and weak injury surveillance can affect data completeness and cause assignment. In addition, thermal injuries associated with armed conflict may be classified within the GBD framework under conflict and terrorism rather than FHHS. These factors may reduce comparability and could lead to underestimation of the broader thermal-injury burden in some countries.

The APC results add an age-specific perspective to these regional trends. Incidence rose from childhood to late adolescence and early adulthood before declining at older ages, while local drift was less favorable among older age groups. Older adults may have greater vulnerability to injury severity and prolonged recovery because of reduced mobility, multimorbidity and impaired healing capacity [26–30]. Nevertheless, the APC identification constraint means that linear age, period and cohort trends cannot be uniquely separated, and the ecological analysis cannot attribute the observed pattern to any single mechanism. The findings therefore support the need for age-responsive prevention and care planning, but the design of such programs should be guided by country-specific surveillance and individual-level research.

Several limitations should be considered. First, GBD estimates depend on heterogeneous primary data and modeling assumptions, especially where vital registration and injury-surveillance systems are limited. Conflict, displacement, incomplete registration and cause misclassification may affect estimates in some NAME settings, and conflict-related thermal injuries may be recorded outside the FHHS cause category. Second, this was an ecological analysis. It cannot support individual-level causal inference, and aggregate associations may not apply to individuals. Third, the APC model is subject to the well-known identification problem, so linear age, period and cohort effects cannot be uniquely distinguished. Fourth, the 2022–2023 estimates should be interpreted cautiously, particularly in locations where recent primary data are sparse. Finally, uncertainty intervals quantify uncertainty represented in the GBD estimates and model outputs, but do not substitute for a full sensitivity analysis or capture all long-term physical, psychological and social consequences of burns. Notwithstanding these limitations, the consistent decline in age-standardized fatal and disability burden, together with the demographic pressure on absolute service needs, provides a useful basis for targeted surveillance, prevention and care planning.

Conclusion

From 1990 to 2023, the NAME region substantially reduced its age-standardized DALY and death rates from thermal injuries. Population growth and ageing increased absolute prevalence and incidence, whereas favorable age-specific epidemiological changes outweighed demographic pressures for deaths, producing a small net regional decline. The recent increase in ASIR, particularly among females, and the less favorable trend among older age groups warrant continued surveillance and targeted prevention. These ecological, model-based estimates describe regional patterns and should not be interpreted as evidence of causal mechanisms.
