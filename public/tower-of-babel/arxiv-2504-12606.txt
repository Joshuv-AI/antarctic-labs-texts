Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce a novel method named Robo-SGG, i.e., Layout-Oriented Normalization and Restitution for Robust Scene Graph Generation. Compared to the existing SGG setting, the robust scene graph generation aims to perform inference on a diverse range of corrupted images, with the core challenge being the domain shift between the clean and corrupted images. Existing SGG methods suffer from degraded performance due to compromised visual features ( e.g. , corruption interference or occlusions). To obtain robust visual features, we exploit the layout information, which is domain-invariant, to enhance the efficacy of existing SGG methods on corrupted images. Specifically, we employ Instance Normalization (IN) to filter out the domain-specific feature and recover the unchangeable structural features ( i.e. , the positional and semantic relationships among objects) by the proposed Layout-Oriented Restitution. Additionally, we propose a Layout-Embedded Encoder (LEE) that augments the existing object and predicate encoders within the SGG framework, enriching the robust positional and semantic features of objects and predicates. Note that our proposed Robo-SGG module is designed as a plug-and-play component, which can be easily integrated into any baseline SGG model. Extensive experiments demonstrate that by integrating the state-of-the-art method into our proposed Robo-SGG, we achieve relative improvements of 5.6%, 8.0%, and 6.5% in mR@50 for PredCls, SGCls, and SGDet tasks on the VG-C dataset, respectively, and achieve new state-of-the-art performance in corruption scene graph generation benchmark (VG-C and GQA-C). We will release our source code and model.

1.
Introduction

Scene Graph Generation (SGG)
(
Xu et al. 2017
;
Yu et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2017
;
Yang et al. 2018
)
has been introduced to create a visually-grounded graph, wherein the nodes represent detected object instances and the edges encapsulate their pairwise relationships. The SGG model has been widely used in many vision tasks, such as autonomous driving
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
and robotic navigation
(
Werby et al. 2024
;
Singh et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
.
However, most existing studies in this field assume access to “clean” images, but in real-world scenarios, one encounters various types of impaired data (natural
(
Hendrycks and Dietterich 2019
)
, adversarial
(
Ito 2021
)
, etc.), with the most prevalent being corruptions emanating from natural phenomena such as noise, blur, various weather conditions, and digital distortions
(
Hendrycks and Dietterich 2019
)
, which significantly limits their practical applicability. This necessitates the robustness of SGG models to handle such severe conditions effectively.

Existing SGG methods
(
Dong et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2024a
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
leverage visual, textual, and external knowledge graph features to enhance model generalization via multimodal interaction. However, under the interference of corruption, visual features undergo domain shifts
(
Huang et al. 2023
)
, which erroneously guide the interaction among multimodal information, thereby significantly degrading the performance of SGG models. To enhance domain generalization capabilities under domain shifts, current robust models leverage strategies such as data augmentation
(
Hendrycks et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2022
)
, adversarial training
(
Herrmann et al. 2022
;
Kireev et al. 2022
;
Rusak et al. 2020
)
, and normalization
(
Zhou et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2020
)
to performance on unseen target domains with various types of corruption. However, these methods are predominantly object-centric and fail to enhance the robustness of structural features (
i.e.
, the positional and semantic relationships among objects), making them particularly unsuitable for SGG tasks that require comprehensive semantic relationship recognition.

To address this limitation, we propose to exploit the layout, which is domain-invariant information, to attain robust structural features of the image through the newly designed Layout-Oriented Normalization and Restitution Module (NRM).
Figure
1
(c) illustrates our main idea. The key challenge for NRM lies in effectively filtering out domain-specific features while simultaneously retaining domain-invariant structural features across different corruptions. We first employ Instance Normalization (IN) to eliminate domain-specific features. Subsequently, we propose a layout-aware attention mechanism to capture the image’s structure and re-establish the structural features based on the attention weights derived from the residual feature, representing the discrepancy between the original and normalized information.

Furthermore, we propose the Layout-Embedded Encoder (LEE) to obtain enhanced robust object and predicate features from individual objects and their interactions. Unlike the existing method, SHA
(
Dong et al. 2022
)
, which uses stacked self-attention and cross-attention layers to explore multi-modal fusion, our proposed LEE achieves more robust feature integration through the gated fusion of spatial and visual information within corruption interventions. Specifically, we enhance the object encoder for individual objects by embedding the coordinates of the object bounding boxes. For predicate (
i.e.
, object interactions), we similarly enhance the predicate encoder by embedding the coordinates of the object pair’s bounding boxes. Notably, a gate fusion mechanism dynamically integrates these embedded features with original visual features, thereby boosting the robustness of object and predicate features.

Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

(1)
We propose a novel approach, Robo-SGG, for the robust scene graph generation by designing a Layout-Oriented Normalization and Restitution Module (NRM) to attain a generalized domain-invariant structural feature.

(2)
We introduce a new Layout-Embedded Encoder (LEE) to embed the coordinates of objects and use gated fusion with visual features to enhance the generalizability of objects and predicates within corrupted images.

(3)
Experimental results demonstrate our proposed Robo-SGG is a plug-and-play method that can be incorporated into any current SGG baselines and show it can achieve relative improvements of 5.6%, 8.0%, and 6.5% in mR@50 for PredCls, SGCls, and SGDet tasks in the VG-C benchmarks, respectively.

;

)
. Current SGG methods predominantly addressed the bias in label distribution
(
Tang et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Sun et al. 2023
)
, incorporated external knowledge such as knowledge graphs
(
Zareian et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Qi et al. 2019b
;
Qi et al. 2020
;
Qi et al. 2025
;
Lv et al. 2023
)
, delved into the inherent semantic relationships between labels
(
Qi et al. 2019a
;
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
, and bolstered reasoning capabilities through causal reasoning
(
Tang et al. 2020
;
Sun et al. 2023
)
. At the same time, one-stage
(
Li et al. 2022
;
Im et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024b
)
and two-stage
(
Zellers et al. 2018
;

structures were proposed for various scenarios. With the emergence of Visual Language Models (VLM), VLM-based methods
(
Li et al. 2024b
;
Chen et al. 2024a
;

)
have further enhanced the capability of SGG in open-vocabulary settings. Furthermore, these methods expanded beyond static images to include video
(
Yang et al. 2023
;
Nag et al. 2023
;
Teng et al. 2021
)
and 3D scenes
(
Lv et al. 2024a
;

)
, significantly broadening its practical applications. These advancements enabled SGG to play a crucial role in various real-world scenarios, including autonomous driving

In contrast, our approach explicitly addressed this limitation by developing a robust SGG framework that could effectively handle image corruption, a common yet understudied challenge in real-world scenarios.

Robustness in Scene Understanding.
Most existing scene-understanding methods assume that the data obtained is always pristine and clear. However, in reality, input images are often subject to various degrees of damage. To address this, prior work focused on mitigating the impact of such damage, including multi-weather denoising
(
Lin et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024
)
and blur denoising
(
Garber and Tirer 2024
)
, but these approaches struggled to generalize across unseen or diverse types of corruption. Dan
et al.

(
Hendrycks and Dietterich 2019
)
benchmarked the robustness of classification models against prevalent visual corruptions by introducing ImageNet-C, which comprises 15 distinct types of corruption, each manifested in 5 varying severity levels. Within the context of corruption robustness, existing methods can be broadly classified into two primary categories: 1) Adapting to the corruption domain, such as transfer learning
(
Mirza et al. 2022
;
Jin et al. 2020
;
Tang et al. 2023
)
and test time adaptation
(
Schneider et al. 2020
;
Gao et al. 2023
;
Benz et al. 2021
)
. 2) Enhancing feature generalization, as seen in techniques like data augmentation
(
Mintun et al. 2021
;
Hendrycks et al. 2019
;
Yin et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2022
)
. Nevertheless, these studies predominantly focused on object-centric images, such as ImageNet
(
Deng et al. 2009
)
, and overlooked the effects of corruption on the interrelations among objects within the images. Recently, Zhang
et al.

(
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
introduced a novel synthetic VG-C benchmark for SGG, featuring 20 challenging image corruptions, and proposed a hierarchical knowledge-enhanced method for robust SGG. However, under conditions of severe visual impairment, the enhancement effect of hierarchical knowledge on the model was limited due to the inability to obtain generalizable visual features. In contrast, our method aimed to obtain robust features of the structural relationships between objects by utilizing layout-aware information, which is domain-invariant, to enhance the efficacy of existing SGG methods on corrupted images.

3.
Proposed Method

3.1.
Problem Definition

The task of scene graph generation aims to generate a structured representation graph
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathcal{O},\mathcal{E}\}
of the given image
\mathcal{I}
. Each nodes
o_{i}\in\mathcal{O}
in a scene graph represents an object with bounding boxes
b_{i}=\{x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}\}
and the corresponding categories
c_{i}
, while each edges
p_{i\rightarrow j}\in\mathcal{E}
correspond to the predicate categories between the object pair
(o_{i},o_{j})
. The output of the SGG method is a collection of visual triplets
\langle subject-predicate-object\rangle
, which can be formulated as
\{(o_{i},p_{i\rightarrow j},o_{j})|o_{i},o_{j}\in\mathcal{O},p_{i\rightarrow j}\in\mathcal{E}\}
.

3.2.
Standard SGG Framework

As illustrated in Figure
2
, our framework is built upon the conventional pipeline of standard SGG approaches. It’s worth noting that our Robo-SGG can be flexibly added to both one-stage
(
Li et al. 2022
;
Im et al. 2024
)
and two-stage
(
Tang et al. 2019
;
Yan et al. 2020
;
Yu et al. 2020
;
Zellers et al. 2018
)
SGG models, which generally contains five parts:
Backbone
,
Object Encoder
,
Object Decoder
,
Predicate Encoder
, and
Predicate Decoder
. The way our method is integrated into one-stage and two-stage models differs slightly, and these differences will be discussed in
Backbone
. Unless otherwise specified, we use the two-stage model as the example in the following description.

Backbone.
We use a CNN network (
e.g.
, ResNet-101
(
He et al. 2016
)
) as the backbone network
\phi_{f}
, which generates the multi-level feature maps
f_{map}
from the input image
\mathcal{I}
. Our method applies to feature maps at all levels. For simplicity, we illustrate our approach using only one layer of the feature map in this paper as follows:

(1)

f_{map}=\phi_{f}(\mathcal{I}),

where
f_{map}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}
,
C,H,W
represent the number of channels, height, and width, respectively. It’s worth noting that in the two-stage SGG model, the backbone exists within the proposal network, which additionally outputs
\mathcal{O}=\{o_{i}\}^{N}_{i=1}
, where
N
is the number of detected objects, and
o_{i}=(v_{i},b_{i},c_{i})
indicates that each object has a corresponding visual feature
v_{i}
, bounding box
b_{i}
and category
c_{i}
. In contrast, the one-stage SGG model utilizes predicted bounding boxes in our framework.

Object Encoder
is employed to embed the object feature
f_{i}
for subsequent modeling and prediction, which is computed as follows:

(2)

f_{i}=Enc^{obj}([v_{i},Emb^{lang}(c_{i})]),

where
i\in N
denote
i
-th object in image
\mathcal{I}
,
Enc^{obj}
denotes the object encoder, which can incorporate various feature encoding module (
e.g.
, BiLSTM
(
Zellers et al. 2018
)
, GNN
(
Chen et al. 2019
)
and Transformer
(
Im et al. 2024
)
). The operation
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes the concatenation of vectors, and
Emb^{lang}(\cdot)
is a function provided by a pre-trained language model (
i.e.
, Glove
(
Pennington et al. 2014
)
), tasked with extracting textual features from the object category
c_{i}
.

Object Decoder
is employed to predicted the final object label
c_{i}^{\prime}
from the object feature
f_{i}
. The following equation encapsulates this process:

(3)

c_{i}^{\prime}=\arg\max(\text{Softmax}(Dec^{obj}(f_{i}))),

where
Dec^{obj}
denotes the object decoder, typically implemented as a single fully connected layer.

Predicate Encoder
is designed to extract the predicate feature
f_{i\rightarrow j}^{P}
of the object pair (
o_{i}
,
o_{j}
), which is computed as follows:

(4)

f_{i\rightarrow j}=Enc^{pred}([f_{i},v_{i\rightarrow j},f_{j}]),

where
i,j\in N,i\neq j
, denote different object instances in image
\mathcal{I}
.
Enc^{pred}
denotes the predicate encoder, typically implemented as a single fully connected layer.
f_{i}
and
f_{j}
are the object features of
o_{i}
and
o_{j}
obtained in Eq.
2
, respectively, and
v_{i\rightarrow j}
denotes the Region of Interest (RoI) feature of the union bounding box that extracting from the feature map
f_{map}
obtained in Eq.
1
.

Predicate Decoder
is designed to predict the predicate label
p_{i\rightarrow j}
based on the predicate features
f_{i\rightarrow j}
of the object pair
(o_{i},o_{j})
, which is computed as follows:

(5)

p_{i\rightarrow j}=\arg\max(\text{Softmax}(Dec^{pred}(f_{i\rightarrow j}^{P}))),

where
i,j\in N,i\neq j
, denote different object instances in image
\mathcal{I}
.
Dec^{pred}(\cdot)
denotes the predicate decoder, typically implemented as a single fully connected layer.

3.3.
Layout-Oriented Normalization and Restitution Module (NRM)

As depicted in Figure
2
(a), we propose the Layout-Oriented Normalization and Restitution Module (NRM) to take the layout information (
i.e.
, all bounding box of the objects in image
\mathcal{I}
) and
f_{map}
from the backbone
\phi_{f}
as inputs, and outputs a robust feature map
f_{map}^{\prime}
which is invariable to any corruption.
Our key insight
is that various forms of corruption induce a covariate shift within the image domain
(
Huang et al. 2023
)
, with each type of corruption representing a distinct domain. Consequently, our motivation is to eliminate domain-specific disturbances and to extract domain-invariant features from
f_{map}
, utilizing layout information which is the domain-invariant factor. This process enables the encoding of a generalizable
f_{map}^{\prime}
that enhances the robustness of the scene graph generation model. We introduce NRM in two parts: Instance Normalization and Layout-aware Attention.

Instance Normalization.
To address the domain discrepancies in the
f_{map}
between clean and corrupted images, we employ Instance Normalization (IN)
(
Ulyanov 2016
)
to filter out the domain-specific feature from the feature map
f_{map}
motioned in Eq.
1
. Firstly, we calculate the mean and variance of all pixels in each image within the batch as follows:

(6)

\displaystyle\mu_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{HW}\sum_{l=1}^{W}\sum_{m=1}^{H}a_{ilm},

(7)

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{HW}\sum_{l=1}^{W}\sum_{m=1}^{H}(a_{ilm}-\mu_{i})^{2},

where
a_{ilm}
denote the
ilm
-th elements of the input
f_{map}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}
,
i
is the feature channel,
l
and
m
is the height and width of
f_{map}
respectively. Notably, unlike Batch Normalization (BN)
(
Li et al. 2016
)
, which calculates the mean and standard deviation across the entire batch, IN computes these statistics for each image. This approach can effectively eliminate the covariance shift caused by domain shift
(
Ulyanov 2016
)
.
Next, we use
\mu_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}^{2}
to obtain the normalized result for each channel in
f_{map}
:

(8)

b_{ijk}=\frac{a_{ijk}-\mu_{i}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\epsilon}},

where
a_{ijk}\in f_{map}
and
b_{ijk}\in\overline{f_{map}}
represent the
ijk
-th pixel values before and after IN, respectively.
We calculate the difference between
f_{map}
before and after IN as the residual feature
R
for subsequent operations:

(9)

R=f_{map}-\overline{f_{map}}.

Layout-aware Attention.
While IN effectively mitigates domain-specific features of
f_{map}
, it inherently constitutes a mathematical transformation that may inadvertently eliminate certain structural features pivotal for the SGG method. Therefore, We designed Layout-aware Attention, which utilizes the coordinates of all objects’ bounding boxes in an image to capture the positional and semantic relationships among objects. First, we calculate the centroid of each object’s bounding box
\left\{e_{i}=(x_{i_{c}},y_{i_{c}})|i\in 1,2...,N|e_{i}\in E\right\}
, where
N
denotes the number of objects in the image and
E
denotes the set of all bounding box’s centroids. Subsequently, we determine the Euclidean distance from each pixel to the centroid of each object and apply the Gaussian function to obtain the attention weights
A
as follows:

(10)

D_{i,j}=\text{Distance}(p_{i},e_{j}),

(11)

A=\exp(-\frac{D^{2}}{c^{2}}),

where
p_{i}
denotes the
i
-th pixel, and
i=1,2,...,H\times W
represents all the pixels in the image.
e_{j}\in E
represents the centroid of the
j
-th object’s bounding box and
j=1,2...,N
. We choose the L2-distance as the Distance function.
c
is a hyper-parameter that controls the RMS width of the Gaussian function. Finally, for numerical stability, we apply a normalizing across all pixels to compute the attention weights:

(12)

A=\frac{\exp(A_{n,m,k})}{\sum_{m=1}^{H}\sum_{k=1}^{W}\exp(A_{n,m,k})}.

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times H\times W}
. Therefore we propose to re-introduce the structural feature map
R^{+}
into the network by extracting it from the residual feature
R
by layout-aware attention weights
A
, defined as:

(13)

R^{+}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}\circ R,

where
A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times H\times W}
represents attention weights of pixels for the
i
-th object, and
R\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}
is the residual feature mentioned in Eq.
9
.
\circ
indicates element-wise multiplication.

In conclusion, by integrating the distilled structural feature
R^{+}
with the instance normalization feature
\overline{f_{map}}
, we formulate the output feature map of the NRM as follows:

(14)

f_{map}^{\prime}=\overline{f_{map}}+R^{+}.

Compared to the original feature map
f_{map}
, the output feature map
f_{map}^{\prime}
not only eliminates domain-specific disturbances but also preserves more structural representations.

3.4.
Layout-Embedded Encoder (LEE)

As depicted in Figure
2
(b), we propose the Layout-Embedded Encoder (LEE) to enhance the origin objects and predicates encoder described in Eq.
2
, by establishing interaction between the visual feature and coordinates of the bounding box. We introduce LEE in three parts: Object coordinates embedding, Predicate coordinates embedding, and Gate fusion.

Object coordinates embedding
aims to embed the bounding box coordinates
b_{i}=(x_{1},y_{2},x_{2},y_{2})
of the object
o_{i}
, which can be formulated as:

(15)

f_{i}^{C}=Emb^{obj}(b_{i}),

where the
Emb^{obj}(\cdot)
is an embedding module implemented as two fully connected layers and
f_{i}^{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the coordinate embedding of the
i
-th object.
d
is the hidden dimension of the SGG model.

Predicate coordinates embedding
aims to embed the coordinates of the object pair
(o_{i},o_{j})
, which capture the interaction
r_{i\rightarrow j}
between the objects, and formulate as:

(16)

r_{i\rightarrow j}=[b_{i},b_{j},e_{i}-e_{j},\left\|b_{i}-b_{j}\right\|_{2}],

(17)

f_{i\rightarrow j}^{C}=Emb^{pred}(r_{i\rightarrow j}),

where
i,j\in N,i\neq j
represent different objects in the image.
e_{i}
denotes the center point of the bounding box
b_{i}
, and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2}
refers to the L2-distance. The operation
[\cdot,\cdot]
signifies the concatenation of vectors. The
Emb^{pred}(\cdot)
is an embedding module implemented as two fully connected layers and
f_{i\rightarrow j}^{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the coordinate embedding of the object pair
(o_{i},o_{j})
.

Gate Fusion
is a fusion strategy for object and predicate features,
f_{i}
and
f_{i\rightarrow j}
, and their corresponding domain-invariant coordinate embedding,
f_{i}^{C}
and
f_{i\rightarrow j}^{C}
. This strategy dynamically adjusts the gate coefficient to mitigate the performance degradation caused by domain shifts affecting
f_{i}
and
f_{i\rightarrow j}
. The process for objects and predicates is similar and we illustrate using the object as an example. Initially, determining the extent of fusion with the coordinate embedding
f_{i}^{C}
, we compute the gate coefficient as follows:

(18)

z_{i}=\text{Sigmoid}(wf_{i}),

where
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
represents the learnable parameter, and
Sigmoid
is the activation function that maps the gate coefficient to a range between 0 and 1, indicating the proportion of
f_{i}^{C}
to be retained. Subsequently, we obtain the gate-fused object feature based on
z_{i}
:

(19)

f_{i}^{\prime}=(1-z_{i})\circ f_{i}^{C}+z_{i}\circ f_{i},

where
f_{i}^{\prime}
is the object feature outputted by Object LEE,
\circ
indicates element-wise multiplication. Similarly,
f_{i\rightarrow j}^{\prime}
is the predicate feature outputted by Predicate LEE, following a similar operation based on Eq.
18
and Eq.
19
.

3.5.
Training and Inference

Loss Function.
Following
(
Zellers et al. 2018
)
, we employ the standard scene graph generation loss function
\mathcal{L}_{SGG}
, which is the summation of cross-entropy for both predicted objects and predicates. More details are in the supplementary materials.

Integrating Robo-SGG into SGG model.
As a plug-in model, our Robo-SGG’s NRM component can be applied to the
f_{map}
derived from Eq. (
1
), while the LEE primarily influences the Object/Predicate Encoder in Eq.(
2
) and Eq.(
4
). During training, validation, and testing, our Robo-SGG can be easily inserted into the corresponding components.

4.1.
Experimental Settings

Datasets.
Visual Genome
(
Krishna et al. 2017
)
consists of 108,077 images with average annotations of 38 objects and 22 relationships per image. In this paper, we employ the most common split
(
Xu et al. 2017
)
, which incorporates the 150 most frequently occurring object categories and 50 predicate categories.
GQA
(
Hudson and Manning 2019
)
, an additional vision-language dataset, encompasses over 3.8 million relation annotations. For GQA, we adhere to the GQA200 split
(
Hudson and Manning 2019
)
, which incorporates the 200 most frequently occurring object categories and the 100 most prevalent predicate categories. To evaluate the robustness of our model, we adopt the approach delineated in
(
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
, employing 20 distinct types of corruption to design VG-C and GQA-C. For illustrative purposes, we categorize 20 types of corruption into five major classes, following the classification by
(
Hendrycks and Dietterich 2019
)
and
(
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
. These classes are: Noise (Gaussian Noise, Shot Noise, and Impulse Noise), Blur (Defocus Blur, Glass Blur, Motion Blur, and Zoom Blur), Weather1 (Snow, Frost, and Fog), Digital (Brightness, Contrast, Elastic, Pixelate, and JPEG Compression) and Weather2 (Sunlight Glare, Water Drop, Wildfire Smoke, Rain, and Dust). Please refer to our supplementary materials for more description and visualization of VG-C and GQA-C.

Metrics.
For evaluation, we conduct three popular SGG tasks: 1) Predicate Classification (PredCls), 2) Scene Graph Classification (SGCls), and 3) Scene Graph Detection (SGDet). To address the dataset’s pronounced long-tail distribution, we adopt the class-balanced metric of mean recall (mR@K) for the top K predictions. In our paper, K is set to 20, 50, and 100.

Compared Methods.
To demonstrate the effectiveness of our model-agnostic approach, we integrate our Robo-SGG into two categories of two-stage models: 1) Three baseline SGG models: MOTIFS
(
Zellers et al. 2018
)
, VCTree
(
Tang et al. 2019
)
, and VTransE
(
Tang et al. 2020
)
. 2) Recent state-of-the-art methods in the SGG task: DPL
(
Jeon et al. 2024
)
and HiKER-SGG
(
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
, a method specifically designed for robust SGG. Additionally, we include a one-stage model, EGTR
(
Im et al. 2024
)
, for comprehensive evaluation.

Implementation Details.
All experiments are designed to run on a single Tesla 32G V100 GPU, and we conduct them on a machine equipped with 8 V100 GPUs, using PyTorch
(
Paszke et al. 2019
)
as the framework. The hyper-parameter
c
, which controls the spread of the Gaussian function in Eq. (
11
), is set as 1.0. For the baseline SGG models (MOTIFS
(
Zellers et al. 2018
)
, VCTree
(
Tang et al. 2019
)
, and VTransE
(
Tang et al. 2020
)
), we conform to the configurations recommended in the Scene-Graph-Benchmark by
(
Tang et al. 2020
)
. For the recently published state-of-the-art methods (DPL
(
Jeon et al. 2024
)
, HiKER-SGG
(
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
, and EGTR
(
Im et al. 2024
)
), we strictly adhere to the official codebases and detailed implementation guidelines provided by the authors. More details are provided in the supplementary materials.

Effectiveness of Layout-Oriented Normalization and Restitution Module (NRM).
In Table
2
, we compare our NRM with the existing SNR method
(
Jin et al. 2020
)
, which uses learned channel attention for feature restitution. Unlike SNR, our proposed NRM incorporates a Layout-Aware Attention mechanism tailored to restore structural features essential for SGG. While SNR improves robustness under corruption, achieving gains of 2.2% and 2.0% in PredCls mR@100 and on MOTIFS
(
Zellers et al. 2018
)
and HiKER
(
Zhang et al. 2024b
)

(
Zellers et al. 2018
)
. In contrast, our NRM consistently enhances robustness across both baselines, with relative improvements of 4.3% and 3.8% in PredCls mR@100, demonstrating its superior ability to recover features critical for structural relationship recognition. Furthermore, in Figure
4
, we visualize the feature maps processed by SNR
(
Jin et al. 2020
)
and NRM under various types of corruption. It is observed that NRM achieves more robust feature maps, capturing better structural features of the scene under noise corruption and more effectively removing disturbances in scenarios of blur, weather, and digital corruption. This is attributed to our proposed Layout-aware Attention mechanism, which more effectively restitutes structural features pivotal for scene graph generation from the residual features after normalization.

Efficiency Comparison.
We compare the training time of different baseline models with our Robo-SGG. On MotifNet
(
Zellers et al. 2018
)
, the addition of LEE and NRM increases the time by 3 minutes and 20 minutes, respectively, accounting for 2.0% and 13.3% of the total training time (150 minutes). On VCTree
(
Tang et al. 2019
)
, the addition of LEE and NRM increases the time by 2 minutes and 13 minutes, respectively, accounting for 1.2% and 7.9% of the total training time (165 minutes), demonstrating that our Robo-SGG can enhance the robustness of SGG models at a relatively low cost. More details can be found in the supplementary material.

Impact of Corruption Severity.
As described in
(
Hendrycks and Dietterich 2019
)
, the corruption includes five levels, each indicating the degree of severity. We evaluate their impact on the mR@100 metric in the SGCls task, as shown in Figure
5
. Here, Severity = 0 represents clean images without corruption, while Severity = 1 to 5 corresponds to progressively increasing levels of corruption intensity. We observe that as the severity level increases, the performance of all methods declines; however, our Robo-SGG consistently outperforms the baseline. As the severity level rises, the relative improvement achieved by incorporating our Robo-SGG also increases. For example, the average relative gain of three baselines+Ours over baseline is 4.6% at severity level 1 and reaches 28.2% at severity level 5. This improvement can be attributed to: 1) our NRM utilizing domain-invariant layout information to maintain robust structural features across corruptions, unlike existing methods whose visual features suffer from domain shifts, and 2) our structural features correctly guide the interaction among multimodal information within corrupted images.

Comparasion with denosie method.
Existing approaches, such as DDPG
(
Garber and Tirer 2024
)
, have been developed to address the challenge of removing image corruptions, including motion blur. In our experiments, we adopted its pre-trained model to denoise motion-blurred images. These processed images were then used as input for the Scene Graph Generation (SGG) model. The experiments were carried out using a single Tesla 32G V100 GPU to ensure consistency in computational resources. As shown in Table
4
, the model achieved a 13.3% improvement when using images denoised by DDPG. However, this came at the cost of a 2394.3% increase in inference time due to the computational demands of denoising. In contrast, our method achieved a 46.7% performance improvement with only a 2.3% increase in time. This is because, while diffusion-based noise denoising produce visually appealing results, they tend to lose high-frequency information during the process, leading to overly smooth images that lack structural details
(
Whang et al. 2022
)
. In contrast, our NRM preserves structural features through Layout-aware Attention, resulting in better performance under different corruption.

5.
Conclusion

In this paper, we attributed the impact of corruption on the scene graph generation model to domain shift and presented a novel plug-in for robust scene graph generation, including Layout-Oriented Normalization and Restitution Module (NRM) for image feature map and Layout-Embedded Encoder (LEE) for object and predicate encoding. Designed as a plug-and-play component, our proposed Robo-SGG can be readily incorporated and has demonstrated its potential to enhance multiple baselines significantly. Extensive experiments on the VG-C and GQA-C benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness and state-of-the-art performance of our proposed method.
