Longitudinal data improves selection of drought-tolerant Eucalyptus germplasm
Authors: Guilherme B. Canal, Glêison A. dos Santos, Filipe M. Ferreira, Leonardo L. Bhering, Thales A. P. C. Nogueira, Camila F. Paixão, Saulo Chaves, Kaio Olimpio G. Dias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In Brazil, nearly half of the country’s eucalyptus plantations are located in regions where seasonal dry periods lasting three to five months bring monthly rainfall below 50 mm. Thus, the main goal of this study was to evaluate the potential benefits of incorporating multi-year and multi-environmental data, as well as to investigate the impact of modeling different covariance structures on the selection of drought-tolerant families, and thus compare the results with the standard analysis model framework used in forest breeding. For this purpose, we evaluated the diameter at breast height (DBH, cm) in 232 Eucalyptus families and six commercial clones, installed in different experimental trials distributed in three distinct locations in Brazil. Three measurements were performed at 18, 30, and 42 months after planting. Finally, we identified promising families for cultivation under drought conditions. Our results show that spatial row–column corrections improved model fit (as indicated by lower AIC values) in single-environment analyses and were therefore adopted for subsequent multi-environment and multi-age analyses. Among the selected best-fitting models, the heterogeneous compound symmetry covariance structure effectively accommodated the data complexity for predicting interaction effects, while diagonal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\otimes$$\end{document} first-order heterogeneous autoregressive structures were more suitable for modeling residual effects. In this context, the selected model exhibited significant variability among families in drought tolerance. Models that incorporated multi-year and multi-environmental data and adjustments in the variance-covariance structures for genetic and residual effects allowed for a better fit to the data, resulting in more consistent variance component estimates and increasing the accuracy of genetic and spatial interaction assessments. Furthermore, the selected families promise to continue regional breeding programs to develop drought-tolerant eucalyptus. Our findings underscore the potential of Brazilian eucalyptus germplasm for drought-tolerance improvement and highlight the importance of robust data integration in analyzing trials under abiotic stress.

Introduction

Eucalyptus sp. is the genus of trees most planted in the world1,2. It is commercially grown in 95 countries, totaling about 25 million hectares1,3. One factor that contributed to eucalypts’ success is the development of drought tolerance mechanisms during their evolution, which allowed them to perform better and survive under different degrees of water restriction4,5. However, climate change is leading to unpredictable water availability and scarcity, which severely reduce the growth rate of eucalypt plantations6,7. This impact on water availability is particularly worrying for Brazil, because the regions where eucalypt plantations are mostly concentrated (Southeastern and Northeastern regions, which hold 4.48 of the total 7.6 million ha8) already have a delicate water balance for the crop, with seasonal dry periods lasting three to five months, during which monthly rainfall below 50 mm9.

Recent studies documented an increased frequency of heatwaves and droughts throughout Brazil, confirming that the country is vulnerable to climate change effects10–13. Water limitation is likely the most challenging environmental factor, as it amplifies the damage caused by various biotic and abiotic stressors, such as pests, diseases and wildfires14. The increase in intensity and frequency of future droughts is expected to lead to higher mortality rates in planted eucalypt forests15, which will inevitably lead to severe financial losses.

Given their perennial nature and the large breeding timeline for a breeding cycle (15-30 years16,17), eucalypts are highly vulnerable to climate changes. Therefore, developing drought-tolerant varieties is crucial to ensuring the sustainability of eucalyptus production chain. Potential drought-tolerant germplasm has already been identified from different species, including E. urophylla, E. pellita, E. robusta, E. camaldulensis, E. tereticornis, E. brassiana and E. globulus9,18–21. These species can be used in breeding programs as a primary source of drought tolerance alleles to be complementarily combined via hybridization. Researchers also identified minor and major genes underlying drought-tolerance mechanisms, enabling molecular and genomic breeding approaches20,22,23. Pita-Barbosa et al.24 established a eucalyptus ideotype for drought tolerance, outlining key traits such as relative growth rate, root/shoot ratio, leaf area, photosynthetic capacity, abscisic acid (ABA) dynamics, and others. However, many of these traits are rarely measured routinely in forest breeding programs, with growth traits being prioritized. Furthermore, eucalypt trees may behave differently in response to drought depending on the environment7.

The differential responses of individual families to diverse environmental conditions are known as genotype-by-environment interaction (GEI)19,25,26. In this type of interaction, those showing crossover response between environments stand out due to their importance, as they alter the family ranking. The nature of the GEI can be assessed by decomposing its variability into two components: the first related to the shift in the scale of genetic values for the traits, and the second to the rank change, resulting in an imperfect genetic correlation between environments27,28. In essence, the GEI is due to the differential expression of genes triggered by environmental factors such as climate, soil composition, and availability of water and nutrients. The multifaceted nature of GEI is evidenced by genotype-dependency of gene expression29,30, and the fact that species have evolved and adapted to natural environments with chronic drought conditions often exhibit greater tolerance to drought stress compared to those that grow faster and come from consistently well-watered environments31,32. Therefore to mitigate risk in drought tolerance-related projects, considering GEI is crucial, encompassing both experimental design and data analysis stages. In addition, selection practices in eucalypt breeding have traditionally focused on final measurements, often neglecting the valuable data gathered from multiple assessments conducted during forestry inventories throughout the trial period. Utilizing longitudinal data can provide deeper insights into the effects of drought on tree growth33,34.

To model such multi-dimensional data (from multiple environments and years), linear mixed models are a compelling choice35–38. Compared to methods relying on ordinary least squares, linear mixed models are more adept at handling unbalanced datasets. This proves especially relevant in breeding for drought tolerance, where some candidates may not survive39. Moreover, these models offer the flexibility to incorporate complex covariance structures, capable of handling atypical growth variations due to drought. This sophistication in modeling enables more informative decision-making, addressing the limitations imposed by the rigid assumptions of simpler models38,40,41 that might not hold in trials that experienced drought. In light of the previously discussed, this study aims to: i) evaluate the contribution of spatial autocorrelation models to improve model’s fitting; ii) to explore the impacts of covariance structures modeling on the selection of drought-tolerant eucalyptus families; iii) assess the potential advantages of incorporating data from multiple years and multiple environments in the data analysis to select drought-tolerant families of eucalypt; and iv) discuss the more promising germplasm to be cultivated in drought conditions. We hypothesize that considering the longitudinal data and more complex covariance structures for random effects will improve the model’s suitability.

Methods

Plant material

The data set used in this study was originated from an experimental network of hybrid progeny trials (HPT). In total, we evaluated 232 Eucalyptus families (185 full-sib (FS) and 47 half-sib (HS) families) and six commercial clones (GG1980, GG1923, GG2673, I144, VM01, and AEC1528, used as checks). All families were obtained through controlled pollination. The full-sib families resulted from controlled crosses involving 30 known parents. Statistical analysis

We fitted a linear mixed model for single- and multi-environment trial analyses to predict genetic values at the family level. Variance components were estimated through residual maximum likelihood42. We used the algorithm implemented in the ASReml-R package43 to fit the statistical models. We performed all analyses in the R software environment44, Version 4.3.2,.

Variance components and genetic parameters of the multi-environment and multi-age model

According to the LRT, the family genetic effect, family-by-age interaction, and family-by-environment interaction were all significant. The triple interaction between family, environment, and age was not significant and was dropped from the model. To support decision-making in a forest breeding program, variance components and family genetic values were obtained using the M7 and M7.A models, which capture underlying genetic and spatial variations, without and with the incorporation of the A matrix, respectively. This approach enables family-level selection, guiding subsequent cloning and recombination processes. For Model M7, the accuracy estimate was 0.91 and 0.92 for Model M7.A. The genetic gain was slightly higher when we accounted for the pedigree information in the analyses (Table 3).

The selected models, M7 and M7.A, demonstrated the highest capacity for conducting analyses in imbalanced data set. These models offer the most efficient adjustments to data imbalances that, over time, may have led to larger differences between families across environments, particularly in the last measurements, amplifying the differences in variances between environments. In this way, the VCOV structure applied to these models showed greater efficiency in adjusting genetic and residual effects. Discussion

Initially, by single environment, we verified improved model fit (lower AIC) when we adopted the incorporation of spatial row–column correction in our study, particularly at later ages (Table 1). We believe this results may be associated with the increase in genetic variance over time (Table 1)51,52, driven by processes such as intensified intraspecific competition53, neighbor mortality, physiological differentiation, and microenvironmental adaptation. These factors enhance the spatial correlation structure, and its explicit modeling reduces confounding between genetic and environmental effects, thereby improving the accuracy of the estimates54,55.

Furthermore, we investigated the impact of integrating data from multiple years and environments to improve the selection efficiency of drought-tolerant eucalyptus families. To to achieve this goal, we selected the most accurate model after testing different heterogeneous variances for family-by-age and family-by-environment interactions, accounting for genetic relationships among families, and addressing the residual dependence, we achieved more reliable predictions of genetic effects. Additionally, when pedigree-based relatedness was incorporated into the models, changes in the ranking and selection of families were observed relative to the model without relationship information. This allowed the identification of promising inter-specific hybrid families for cultivation under drought conditions, and families with the potential to serve as parent donors in future stages of breeding programs targeting drought tolerance.

In our methodology, we aimed to improve the accuracy of the information, mainly variance components, genetic parameters and families performance, from the complex relationship between multiple ages and environments. Therefore, as expected the baseline model did not fit well, since it assumes that the variances and covariances between environments are uniform. This structure is considered overly simplistic for the actual experimental conditions, which limits its ability to capture the complexities of interactions in field trials38,41,56,57. Additionally, this approach use only on a reduced dataset, disregarding relevant information, which may induce biases in cases of non-random effects, such as mortality associated with genetic factors58. Consequently, we aimed to implement more realistic VCOV structures in models M7 and M7.A to more efficiently address the complexities arising from the differential phenotypic patterns of families under varying environmental conditions across locations50,59–62. This approach provides the analyses with a more realistic representation of the observed behavior in practice. Additionally, incorporating longitudinal data from different ages offers advantages when considering the age effect and the correlation between them63. Moreover, this approach increases the efficiency of the analysis by utilizing all available information, once the experiment is a progeny test wich presents the challenge of missing observations due to individual mortality (as shown in Figs. S1, S2 and S3 of the Supplementary Material), which is common in this trials58. This study observed, as reported by other authors38,50,58,60,63, that these models, with more realistic VCOV structures, proved to be more efficient than a baseline model in providing reliable estimates of genetic parameters.

The frequent presence of E. urophylla in the genetic composition of elite eucalyptus clones/families is likely attributable to its wood quality and evolutionary adaptation to extreme conditions, particularly drought tolerance64, which results from a complex interplay of physiological and genetic factors64. This species is efficient in regulating leaf gas exchanges, leading to enhanced long-term water use efficiency65. Eucalyptus camaldulensis was also frequently observed in the composition of selected families, which can be attributed to its ability to develop extensive root systems66, and the greater density of aquaporins67, lower leaf area index, and amphistomatic leaves that may result in reduced transpiration rates68. Both species belong to the subgenus Symphyomyrtus, being E. urophylla of the section Transversaria and E. camaldulensis of the section Exserta, which are among the most relevant groups for drought tolerance9,69.

Hybrids between Eucalyptus camaldulensis and Eucalyptus urophylla have demonstrated superior drought tolerance compared to their parent species70, which can be attributed to the aforementioned complementarity, as well as to genes that regulate the mechanism of calcium signalling in drought response71. In fact, hybridization can be explored to capitalize on growth traits of drought-susceptible species like E. grandis9,72. GG2759 and VM1 stood out as parents since 30% and 20% of their evaluated families were selected. GG2759 is a pure genotype of Eucalyptus urophylla and VM1, a commercial hybrid of Eucalyptus urophylla x Eucalyptus camaldulensis, which reinforces the importance of both species for drought tolerance.

The coincidence among the top 20 families selected by the baseline model and the best-fitted model was lower than 50%, indicating a low correspondence between the two approaches (Fig. 3). In turn, besides promoting greater gains (Table 3), the M7 and M7.A models also selected families with a higher proportion of surviving individuals (Supplementary Figs. S5, S6, S7 and S8). These outcomes directly impact breeding programs, emphasizing the importance of adopting more advanced analytical methods. Out of the 232 families, 27 were evaluated for physiological parameters related to drought tolerance by Cursi et al.73. Among these, three selected families (FS100 - (E. urophylla x E. brassiana), FS162 - [(E. grandis x E. brassiana) x E. pellita] x [(E. camaldulensis x E. grandis) x E. urophylla], and FS17 - [E. camaldulensis x (E. urophylla x E. camaldulensis)]) stood out for their higher performance in DBH, supported by their superior morphological and physiological parameters (diameter at breast height, total height, volume of wood (m3 ha-1), average annual volumetric increment (IMAvol, m3 ha-1 year-1), mortality, leaf water potential, specific area, dry mass, length and width of the leaf)73.

The prominence of Eucalyptus urophylla among the selected materials highlights the significant intraspecific variation of this species and the interspecific variation within the Eucalyptus genus. This underscores the importance of recurrent selection programs in Eucalyptus, aimed at achieving gains in pure species populations and their hybrids. Such strategies can improve genetic gains, as demonstrated in an empirical study, which reported up to a 30% gain in a single breeding cycle of clonal hybrids17. In addition, to increase the rate of genetic gain in forest breeding, it is important to consider several key factors, such as: (i) accounting for additional sources of variation, including genetic competition effects53,74; (ii) utilizing genomic information from DNA sequencing (e.g., genomic selection)75,76; and (iii) leveraging advanced technologies like high-throughput phenotyping77 and enviromics78–80.

Conclusions

Longitudinal data and complex covariance structures for random effects improve the model accuracy. The integration of data from multiple years and environments, coupled with the use of models incorporating advanced covariance structures and modeling of the spatial autocorrelation, enabled more precise variance component estimates and improved predictions of genetic values. Our results provide evidence that accounting for longitudinal data and evaluating more parameterized covariance structures enhances model fit to the data. This approach is especially critical for optimizing family selection processes under drought conditions. The contribution of E. urophylla to the genetic composition of the selected materials was remarkable, followed by E. camaldulensis and E. grandis. This highlights the efficiency of these species, particularly their hybrids, in combining physiological traits that confer drought tolerance.
