Abstract
Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO) provides a framework for decision-making under distributional uncertainty, yet its effectiveness can be compromised by outliers in the training data. This paper introduces a principled approach to simultaneously address both challenges. We focus on optimizing Wasserstein-1 DRO objectives for generalized linear models with convex Lipschitz loss functions, where an ϵ \epsilon -fraction of the training data is adversarially corrupted. Our primary contribution lies in a novel modeling framework that integrates robustness against training data contamination with robustness against distributional shifts, alongside an efficient algorithm inspired by robust statistics to solve the resulting optimization problem. We prove that our method achieves an estimation error of O ⁡ ( ϵ ) O(\sqrt{\epsilon}) for the true DRO objective value using only the contaminated data under the bounded covariance assumption. This work establishes the first rigorous guarantees, supported by efficient computation, for learning under the dual challenges of data contamination and distributional shifts.

Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO) is a general stochastic

on a worst-case distribution that is promised to lie
in an ambiguity set—a set of distributions close to the reference distribution,

models possible distributional shifts of the data.

A key desirable property of any outlier-robust DRO formulation

In this work, we initiate a systematic investigation of DRO in the

As mentioned in the preceding discussion, in sharp contrast to prior

1.1
Technical Overview

Our work makes two primary contributions.
At the conceptual level, we provide a novel formulation
of DRO in the presence of outliers in the training data that we argue
is appropriate in a number of settings.
At the technical level, we design the first computationally-efficient algorithm
with provable error guarantees for our outlier-robust DRO formulation.

Modeling DRO with Adversarial Training Data Contamination

We start by pointing out that effectively formulating DRO
in the presence of adversarial training data contamination is subtle.
Many prior approaches attempt to handle outliers in the training set
by encoding information about potential contamination directly
within the ambiguity set
[
NGS23a
]
,
sometimes alongside modifications to the loss function
[
Wan+24a
]
.
While such models can, in principle, provide guarantees if the true
data-generating distribution
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
remains within this augmented ambiguity
set centered around the contaminated empirical distribution,
they often suffer from various drawbacks.
As discussed in
Section

3.2
and further
elaborated in
Section

C.1
, these prior modeling strategies
can lead to guarantees that depend undesirably on data dimensionality
or may fail to recover standard robust estimation tasks
in the limit of no distributional shift (i.e., when
\rho\to 0
).
Moreover, as we argue in
Section

3.1
,
such formulations often implicitly treat training data outliers
in a way that is more aligned with handling outliers (or model misspecification)
in the
testing
distribution.

In sharp contrast, our modeling framework treats pre-decision (training data contamination)
and post-decision (distributional shift at test time) uncertainties separately, aligned
with
[
Bla+24a
]
. This separation allows for a more effective way to achieve robustness to both challenges simultaneously.
We focus on the setting where an
\epsilon
-fraction of the training data
is adversarially corrupted, and the goal is to find a model
that performs well on a test distribution
that may be shifted from the clean underlying data distribution
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
.

3
Data-contaminated DRO: Modeling Principles and Problem Formulation

We begin this section with an overview of our main modeling principles and a review of most closely related work, pointing out important differences. We then argue that under standard assumptions about the loss function and the reference (generating) distribution, we can reduce the considered problem to a convex optimization problem with an inexact data oracle.

3.1
Modeling Principles

The modeling principles follow similar considerations as
[
Bla+24a
]
. Let
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
denote the data generating distribution (also called the reference distribution): this is the distribution from which clean, uncorrupted training samples would be drawn if there were no data corruptions. The training samples passed to a learning algorithm might contain outliers, be contaminated, or otherwise have discrepancy with
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
; thus we let
{{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{train}}}
denote the contaminated distribution that generates the training data. A sample set of size
N
is then drawn from
{{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{train}}}
, leading to the empirical distribution
{\widehat{\mathcal{{\mathbb{P}}}}}={\widehat{\mathcal{{\mathbb{P}}}}}(N)
. The learner has access to
{\widehat{\mathcal{{\mathbb{P}}}}}(N)
, based on which it outputs a model parameterized by a vector
{\bm{w}}
. The model parameterized by
{\bm{w}}
is tested against an out-of-sample distribution (also known as testing distribution)
{{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{test}}}
, which may deviate from
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
. This process can be summarized as:

{\mathbb{P}}_{0}\xrightarrow{\text{contamination}}{{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{train}}}\xrightarrow{\text{sampling}}{\widehat{\mathcal{{\mathbb{P}}}}}(N)\xrightarrow{\text{train-to-test shift}}{{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{test}}}.

(2)

The model parameterized by
{\bm{w}}
might perform poorly on
{{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{test}}}
for various reasons:
(i) Overfitting/statistical error:
{\widehat{\mathcal{{\mathbb{P}}}}}(N)\neq{{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{test}}}
; (ii) Distributional shift:
{{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{test}}}\neq{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
; or (iii) Data contamination:
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}\neq{{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{train}}}
.
Errors due to (i) and (ii) arise in the post-decision stage, while the error in (iii) is incurred in the pre-decision stage.
Distributionally robust optimization (DRO) minimizes the loss with respect to distributions in some uncertainty (or “ambiguity”) set that is specified without inspecting the training data; as a consequence, it is best used to model post-decision errors.
In this work, we specifically focus on DRO’s advantage in guarding against distributional shifts. We gloss over the overfitting errors by drawing samples with a sufficiently large sample set size
N
and apply uniform convergence analysis to guarantee the performance of every model
{\bm{w}}
on
{{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{train}}}
.

Conclusion

This paper initiates a formal study of distributionally robust optimization with outliers in the training data, with the focus on rigorous error guarantees and polynomial-time algorithms. It provides the first polynomial-time algorithm guaranteeing error

Appendix A
Additional Related Work

In this section, we provide an overview of related work in DRO and outlier-robust stochastic optimization.

A.1
The Role of DRO Against Overfitting

Beyond its role in safeguarding against distributional shifts between training and testing environments, Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO) is also recognized for its capacity to mitigate overfitting and enhance model generalization. Specifically, DRO can also be used to ensure estimators from an empirical distribution
{\widehat{\mathcal{{\mathbb{P}}}}}(N)
generalize well to the true data-generating distribution
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
(i.e., assuming
{{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{test}}}={\mathbb{P}}_{0}
). A notable theoretical advantage is that generalization error bounds for DRO estimators can often be derived without explicit reliance on traditional measures of hypothesis class complexity, such as VC dimension or Rademacher complexity
[
SKE19a
, Section 4]
. This contrasts with standard Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM), where such complexity measures are typically central to the generalization analysis
[
SB14a
, Chapters 4, 6, 26]
. Consequently, DRO’s excess risk bounds are sometimes preferred due to these minimal assumptions or observed improvements in overfitting
[
Kuh+19a
, Example 2]
. By optimizing for worst-case performance within an ambiguity set centered on
{\widehat{\mathcal{{\mathbb{P}}}}}(N)
(and chosen to contain
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
), DRO inherently encourages solutions less sensitive to training data idiosyncrasies, promoting better out-of-sample performance (i.e., performance on testing distributions).

While this ability to control overfitting and provide strong generalization guarantees is an important feature of DRO, it is crucial to distinguish this role from its application to handling unknown, fixed distributional shifts—which is the main concern of the present work.
The practical tuning of the ambiguity set radius,
\rho
, often reflects this distinction. When the primary goal is to prevent overfitting and ensure convergence to the true underlying distribution
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
as more data becomes available, practitioners typically advocate for a radius
\rho
that shrinks with the sample size
N
(e.g.,
\rho\to 0
as
N\to+\infty
). For instance, specific rates like
\rho(N)=\tilde{\Theta}(N^{-1/2})
for Wasserstein ambiguity sets have been proposed to optimize this convergence while avoiding issues like the curse of dimensionality
[
CP18a
,
Gao23a
]
. This ensures that the DRO model eventually learns the data-generating distribution accurately, assuming it is well-specified within the model class. Conversely, when DRO is employed to ensure robustness against potential, persistent distributional shifts—whose magnitude is assumed to be independent of the sample size—the radius
\rho
is usually chosen as a constant. This constant reflects the anticipated level of mismatch between the training and deployment environments and is not diminished with more training data, as the shift itself is not expected to vanish
[
Bla+24a
]
. Our paper focuses on the latter scenario (constant
\rho
for distributional shifts), compounded by the challenge of training data contamination, rather than on the specific mechanisms by which DRO mitigates statistical overfitting through vanishing ambiguity sets.

The endeavor to achieve good generalization to
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}={{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{test}}}
faces additional hurdles when the training data
{\widehat{\mathcal{{\mathbb{P}}}}}(N)
itself stems from a contaminated source
{{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{train}}}
, rather than
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
. Some alternative frameworks, such as particular interpretations of Outlier-Robust Wasserstein DRO (OR-WDRO), might attempt to address this by ensuring
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
lies within an ambiguity set defined relative to
{{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{train}}}
(e.g.,
W_{p,\epsilon}({{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{train}}},{\mathbb{P}}_{0})\leq\rho
), while still aiming to evaluate performance on
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
. However, as further explored in
Section

3.2
and
Section

C.1
, applying such models to handle
training set contamination
within a DRO generalization context can prove insufficient. These approaches might not fully capitalize on the assumed structure or properties of
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
(if it is also the clean target distribution) and can yield guarantees with problematic aspects, such as dependence on data dimensionality. This highlights the necessity for a more direct and principled method for dealing with training data contamination—even if the end goal is classical generalization—further motivating the distinct modeling strategy for contamination that our work adopts.

A.2
Tractability of Wasserstein DRO

In this subsection, we survey key formulations and algorithmic approaches related to the tractability of Wasserstein DRO. This review provides context and evidence supporting the reasonableness of the structural assumptions on loss and cost functions adopted in our work, which enable the DRO problem to be reformulated as a tractable regularized risk minimization problem.

The tractability of solving the Wasserstein DRO problem,
\min_{{\bm{w}}}\sup_{{\mathbb{Q}}:W_{p}({\mathbb{Q}},{\widehat{\mathcal{{\mathbb{P}}}}}(N))\leq\rho}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\xi}}\sim{\mathbb{Q}}}[\ell({\bm{w}},{\bm{\xi}})]
, depends critically on the interplay between the loss function
\ell({\bm{w}},{\bm{\xi}})
, the order
p
of the Wasserstein distance, and the ground cost
c(\cdot,\cdot)
used to define
W_{p}
. For a fixed weight vector
{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the inner supremum, which represents the worst-case expectation, has several important characterizations. Key formulations, as discussed in works like
[
GK23a
]
and
[
GCK24a
, Corollary 2]
, include:

\displaystyle R_{S}({\bm{w}})

\displaystyle:=\sup_{W_{p}({\mathbb{Q}},{\widehat{\mathcal{{\mathbb{P}}}}}(N))\leq\rho}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\xi}}\sim{\mathbb{Q}}}[\ell({\bm{w}},{\bm{\xi}})],

\displaystyle R_{I}({\bm{w}})

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\lambda>0}\Bigl\{\lambda\rho^{p}+\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\xi}}_{0}\sim{\widehat{\mathcal{{\mathbb{P}}}}}(N)}\Bigl[\sup_{{\bm{\xi}}^{\prime}}\left(\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{\xi}}^{\prime})-\lambda c^{p}({\bm{\xi}}_{0},{\bm{\xi}}^{\prime})\right)\Bigr]\Bigr\},

\displaystyle R_{U}({\bm{w}})

\displaystyle:=\Bigl((\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\xi}}\sim{\widehat{\mathcal{{\mathbb{P}}}}}(N)}[\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{\xi}})])^{1/p}+\rho L_{{\widehat{\mathcal{{\mathbb{P}}}}}(N),\ell}({\bm{w}})\Bigr)^{p}.

The formulation
R_{I}
is a dual representation derived from Kantorovich duality, and the equality
R_{S}({\bm{w}})=R_{I}({\bm{w}})
holds under very mild conditions. The formulation
R_{U}
seeks to express the worst-case risk as a direct modification or regularization of the empirical risk, where
L_{{\widehat{\mathcal{{\mathbb{P}}}}}(N),\ell,c}({\bm{w}})
depends on the loss, samples, cost, and potentially
{\bm{w}}
itself
[
CLT24a
]
, and the equality
R_{S}({\bm{w}})=R_{U}({\bm{w}})
requires somewhat strong conditions
7
7

7

Early results for formulation
R_{U}
were only developed for Lipschitz generalized linear models with cost functions
c(({\bm{x}},y),({\bm{x}}^{\prime},y^{\prime}))
equal to either
\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\|_{r}+\chi(y-y^{\prime})
or
\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\|_{r}+|y-y^{\prime}|
for
r\in[1,+\infty]

[
SKE19a
]
. More recent results allow for broader combinations of loss functions under various assumptions and more general cost functions; see
[
CLT24a
, Table 1]
for a summary.
[
CLT24a
]

remains a strong condition.
.

While general forms of
R_{U}
might involve complex and data-dependent generalized Lipschitz terms

that are often not explicit, under even stronger conditions on the problem,

admits the following straightforward formulation:
\min_{{\bm{w}}}(\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\xi}}\sim{\widehat{\mathcal{{\mathbb{P}}}}}(N)}[\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{\xi}})]+\rho\psi({\bm{w}}))
. If
\ell
is convex in
{\bm{w}}
and the regularization term
\psi
is also convex, the overall DRO problem is generally considered tractable and can often be solved efficiently using first-order methods, even if nonsmooth, provided that subroutines like proximal operators are readily computable.

The dual formulation
R_{I}
, on the other hand, leads to solving a nested optimization problem. Tractability here often relies on a specific problem structure. For instance, if
\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{\xi}})
is convex in
{\bm{w}}
and concave in
{\bm{\xi}}
(or a maximum of such functions), the problem can be framed as a convex-concave saddle-point problem
[
GK23a
, Remark 9]
. However, the requirement for
\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{\xi}})
to be concave in
{\bm{\xi}}
is very restrictive and is not satisfied by most common machine learning loss functions (e.g., logistic loss or hinge loss), limiting the applicability of such direct minimax solution techniques.

Various specialized algorithms have been developed to address the min-max structure suggested by
R_{I}({\bm{w}})
even without global concavity in
{\bm{\xi}}
. For example,
[
BMZ22a
]
design algorithms for problems with twice differentiable loss functions
\ell
that are locally strongly convex in
{\bm{w}}
. Other research has explored specific loss functions. For instance,
[
LHS19a
]
studied logistic regression and
[
LCS20a
]
addressed
\ell_{2}
-regularized SVMs (both potentially under more general cost functions
c(\cdot,\cdot)
than studied in this paper, therefore the equivalence to a straightforward
R_{U}
reformulation is lost). This means that a unified algorithmic framework based on
R_{I}
that matches the breadth of loss functions (convex, Lipschitz, but not necessarily smooth or concave in
{\bm{\xi}}
) leading to the simpler
R_{U}
formulations considered in this paper is not readily available.

Considering these different avenues for tractability, Wasserstein DRO problems that can be reformulated as regularized risk minimization problems constitute a large and critically important class of solvable DRO models. This class notably includes
W_{1}
-DRO with general convex and Lipschitz loss functions combined with cost functions that yield a norm regularization on
{\bm{w}}
, a setting extensively analyzed by
[
SKE19a
]
and central to our work. The fact that these problems can often be solved using well-understood (though potentially nonsmooth) convex optimization techniques makes them particularly relevant for practical applications.

A.3
Robust Stochastic Optimization

An avenue for handling nonsmooth objective functions in optimization is through smoothing techniques, prominently featuring the Moreau envelope. For a given nonsmooth function
F({\bm{w}})
, its Moreau envelope is defined as
F_{\lambda}({\bm{w}})=\min_{{\bm{u}}}\{F({\bm{u}})+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{w}}\|^{2}_{2}\}
, which yields a smooth approximation to
F({\bm{w}})
. The work of
[
Jam+21a
]
explores robustly optimizing such smoothed objectives. However, this methodology presents significant challenges when considering its direct applicability to obtaining guarantees for the original (unregularized and nonsmooth) function
F({\bm{w}})
.

First, the theoretical guarantees provided in
[
Jam+21a
, Section 5]
are for a further modified objective, specifically
F_{\lambda}({\bm{w}})+(\mu/2)\|{\bm{w}}\|^{2}_{2}
, where an additional quadratic regularization (with strength
\mu/2>0
) is introduced on top of the Moreau smoothing. This combined objective,
F_{\lambda}({\bm{w}})+(\mu/2)\|{\bm{w}}\|^{2}_{2}
, benefits from
(1/\lambda)
-smoothness (inherited from
F_{\lambda}
) and, if
F
is convex,
(\mu+1/\lambda)
-strong convexity. These properties of smoothness and strong convexity are central to their convergence analysis. However, translating these convergence guarantees back to the original nonsmooth and unregularized function
F({\bm{w}})
appears nontrivial and was not provided in
[
Jam+21a
]
.

Second, certain assumptions within their framework appear to sidestep the core challenge of non-differentiability. Specifically, Assumption 3 in
[
Jam+21a
]
, which is crucial for their Algorithm 6 (ApproxMoreauMinimizer), posits access to a stochastic first-order oracle that returns “gradients” of the original, potentially nonsmooth, expected loss
F^{*}({\bm{w}})=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}},y)]
. If the function
F^{*}
is genuinely nonsmooth, its gradient will not exist at all points
{\bm{w}}
. Assuming the existence and accessibility of such gradients everywhere, especially around local optima
8
8

8

Although nonsmooth, mild conditions such as local Lipschitzness ensure that a function is almost everywhere differentiable. So by perturbing a little, one may guarantee access to the gradient with probability 1. However, if the function is nondifferentiable at optima, perturbation can give potentially large gradients that carry no information how close to a solution the algorithm may be, so in this sense nonsmoothness remains a concern.
, effectively diminishes the primary motivation for employing the Moreau envelope, which is designed specifically to create a smooth surrogate precisely where the original function lacks differentiability. This suggests that their proposed algorithm and its analysis might be implicitly tailored to nonsmooth functions of a specific and relatively benign structure where this assumption is not overly restrictive, rather than general nonsmooth convex functions.

Given these considerations,
[
Jam+21a
]
does not directly offer a solution for robustly optimizing the broad class of unregularized and nonsmooth generalized linear models considered in our work.

{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}

\infty

\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}}[\ell_{Y^{\prime}}({\bm{w}}\cdot{\bm{X}}^{\prime})]=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell_{Y}({\bm{w}}\cdot{\bm{X}})\mathbb{I}_{{\bm{X}}\notin A_{n}}]+\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell_{Y}({\bm{w}}\cdot\mathbf{z}_{n})\mathbb{I}_{{\bm{X}}\in A_{n}}]

Since
\ell_{Y}\geq 0

\int_{A_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{Y|{\bm{X}}}[\ell_{Y}(\|{\bm{w}}\|R_{n})]d{\mathbb{P}}_{0}^{\bm{X}}({\bm{x}}),

(5)

where we use the notation
\mathbb{E}_{Y|{\bm{X}}}
to denote the conditional expectation over
Y

{\bm{X}}
.
By Fatou’s lemma, it holds almost surely that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{Y|{\bm{X}}}\left[\frac{\ell_{Y}(R_{n}\|{\bm{w}}\|)}{(R_{n}\|{\bm{w}}\|)^{p+\iota}}\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{Y|{\bm{X}}}\left[\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\ell_{Y}(R_{n}\|{\bm{w}}\|)}{(R_{n}\|{\bm{w}}\|)^{p+\iota}}\right]=C.

Thus, for

\mathbb{E}_{Y|{\bm{X}}}[\ell_{Y}(R_{n}\|{\bm{w}}\|)]\geq\frac{C}{2}(R_{n}\|{\bm{w}}\|)^{p+\iota}.

Plugging it back into

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell_{Y}({\bm{w}}\cdot\mathbf{z}_{n})\mathbb{I}_{{\bm{X}}\in A_{n}}]\geq{\mathbb{P}}_{0}({\bm{X}}\in A_{n})\frac{C}{2}(\|{\bm{w}}\|R_{n})^{p+\iota}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\epsilon_{n}\frac{C}{2}(\|{\bm{w}}\|R_{n})^{p+\iota}=\frac{\rho^{p}}{(K_{A}+R_{n})^{p}+1}\frac{C}{2}(\|{\bm{w}}\|R_{n})^{p+\iota}.

As

\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}}[\ell_{Y^{\prime}}({\bm{w}}\cdot{\bm{X}}^{\prime})]\to\infty

∎

C.1

We begin by examining a canonical problem from robust statistics—robust mean estimation. Our objective here is to demonstrate how employing (

Example C.1

Lemma C.2

(Limit Behavior of OR-WDRO Formulation)

For any

\displaystyle\sup_{{\mathbb{Q}}:W_{p,\epsilon}({{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{train}}},{\mathbb{Q}})=0}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\xi}}\sim{\mathbb{Q}}}[\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{\xi}})]

(Limit Behavior of Tractable OR-WDRO Reformulation)

\lim_{\sigma\to\infty,\rho=0}\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{2}(\sigma,{\bm{z}}_{0}),\\

Based on the dual formulation in

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{2}(\sigma,{\bm{z}}_{0}),\\

The main text introduces the Distributionally Robust Outlier-aware Optimization (DORO) framework by

The Proposed Algorithm and Its (Lack of) Guarantee

From a computational standpoint,

We present a specialization of their method to the
\CVaR^{\alpha}
setting in Algorithm
4
.

Batch size
N_{batch}
; outlier fraction hyperparameter
\epsilon\in[0,1)

for

1

4.

In sum, while computationally appealing, the DORO algorithm should be regarded as a heuristic for DORO risk minimizer rather than a principled solution to its underlying optimization objective.

D({\mathbb{Q}}\|{\mathbb{P}})\leq\rho\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\xi}}\sim{\mathbb{Q}}}[\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{\xi}})].

The theoretical guarantees for the risk minimizer of DORO risk
[
Zha+21a
, Theorem 5]
are predicated on assumptions concerning the moments of

, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\xi}}\sim{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{\xi}})^{2k}]\leq\sigma_{2k}^{2k}

(e.g., bounded covariance or higher moments of

.

To illustrate the implications, consider the special case of robust mean estimation. We choose the quadratic loss
\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{\xi}})=\|{\bm{w}}-{\bm{\xi}}\|_{2}^{2}
and
\alpha=1
so that
\CVaR^{1}_{{\bm{\xi}}\sim{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{\xi}})]=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\xi}}\sim{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{\xi}})]
(intuitively because the smallest
\alpha
-subpopulation considered by CVaR becomes the whole population). DORO’s assumption on the
2k
-th moment of the loss value translates to a
4k
-th moment assumption on the data
{\bm{\xi}}
itself. For instance, if
k=1
, a bounded second moment of the loss
\ell(\hat{{\bm{w}}};{\bm{\xi}})
implies a bounded fourth moment for the data
{\bm{\xi}}
.

To precisely connect the error bounds on loss values (as provided in
C.4
) to errors in parameter estimates for the robust mean estimation task, we establish the following relationship:

Lemma C.5

(Loss Difference for Quadratic Loss)
.

Let the loss function be
\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{\xi}})=\|{\bm{w}}-{\bm{\xi}}\|_{2}^{2}
. Let
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
be a distribution for
{\bm{\xi}}
, and let
{\bm{w}}^{*}=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[{\bm{\xi}}]
be the true mean (the unique minimizer of
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{\xi}})]
). For any fixed vector
\hat{{\bm{w}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the difference in expected loss under
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
is given by:

\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell(\hat{{\bm{w}}};{\bm{\xi}})]-\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell({\bm{w}}^{*};{\bm{\xi}})]=\|\hat{{\bm{w}}}-{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

We expand the terms:

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell(\hat{{\bm{w}}};{\bm{\xi}})]-\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell({\bm{w}}^{*};{\bm{\xi}})]=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\|\hat{{\bm{w}}}-{\bm{\xi}}\|_{2}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\|{\bm{w}}^{*}-{\bm{\xi}}\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\|(\hat{{\bm{w}}}-{\bm{w}}^{*})+({\bm{w}}^{*}-{\bm{\xi}})\|_{2}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\|{\bm{w}}^{*}-{\bm{\xi}}\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\|\hat{{\bm{w}}}-{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+\|{\bm{w}}^{*}-{\bm{\xi}}\|_{2}^{2}+2\langle\hat{{\bm{w}}}-{\bm{w}}^{*},{\bm{w}}^{*}-{\bm{\xi}}\rangle]-\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\|{\bm{w}}^{*}-{\bm{\xi}}\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\|\hat{{\bm{w}}}-{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+2\langle\hat{{\bm{w}}}-{\bm{w}}^{*},\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[{\bm{w}}^{*}-{\bm{\xi}}]\rangle].

Since
{\bm{w}}^{*}=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[{\bm{\xi}}]
, the term
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[{\bm{w}}^{*}-{\bm{\xi}}]={\bm{w}}^{*}-\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[{\bm{\xi}}]={\bm{w}}^{*}-{\bm{w}}^{*}={\bm{0}}
.
Thus, the cross term
2\langle\hat{{\bm{w}}}-{\bm{w}}^{*},\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[{\bm{w}}^{*}-{\bm{\xi}}]\rangle
vanishes, and we are left with:

\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell(\hat{{\bm{w}}};{\bm{\xi}})]-\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell({\bm{w}}^{*};{\bm{\xi}})]=\|\hat{{\bm{w}}}-{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}.

∎

This lemma formally shows that an
O(\sqrt{\epsilon})
error in the expected loss value, as suggested by
C.4
(implying
\sigma_{2}\sqrt{\epsilon}
for the CVaR loss difference), directly translates to an
O(\epsilon^{1/4})
error in the parameter distance
\|\hat{{\bm{w}}}-{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2}
. Under a fourth moment assumption on the data (implied by
k=1
for quadratic loss), the robust statistics literature shows that an estimation error of
O(\epsilon^{3/4})
for
\|\hat{{\bm{w}}}-{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2}
is achievable; see, e.g.,
[
DKP20a
]
. This discrepancy highlights that DORO’s guarantees, stemming from loss value assumptions, may not align with or recover the standard (and often better) rates achieved in outlier-robust learning under comparable data moment conditions, especially when the distributional shift parameter
\rho
is effectively zero.

In summary, while DORO introduces a conceptual framework aligned with our modeling principles in
Section

3.1
for jointly addressing data contamination and distributional shifts, its applicability for provable robust learning remains limited. Crucially, their proposed computational algorithm lacks formal convergence guarantees and, as demonstrated, can fail even in prototypical cases such as robust mean estimation due to its heuristic filtering strategy. In addition, the theoretical results rely on unconventional assumptions on loss moments that can lead to suboptimal rates compared to those in established robust statistics. These issues underscore the challenges in designing algorithms that efficiently and provably optimize objectives combining outlier robustness with distributional robustness based on
f
-divergences.

Prepending covariates by 1 preserves bounded covariance assumption

Lemma C.6

(Prepending covariates by
1
preserves bounded covariance)
.

Let
\{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be independent samples from a distribution over
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
with mean
\tilde{\bm{\mu}}
and covariance
\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}
satisfying
\|\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\|_{\op}\leq\sigma^{2}
.
Define

by prepending a
1
to each
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}
.
Then
\{{\bm{x}}_{i}\}
can be viewed as i.i.d. samples drawn from a distribution with covariance
{\bm{\Sigma}}
satisfying
\|{\bm{\Sigma}}\|_{\op}\leq\sigma^{2}
.

Proof.

Each new vector
{\bm{x}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
can be written as

{\bm{x}}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}\end{bmatrix}.

The mean of the new distribution is

{\bm{\mu}}=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}_{i}]=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\tilde{\bm{\mu}}\end{bmatrix}.

The covariance matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}
is defined as
{\bm{\Sigma}}=\mathbb{E}\left[({\bm{x}}_{i}-{\bm{\mu}})({\bm{x}}_{i}-{\bm{\mu}})^{\top}\right]
.

Expanding
{\bm{x}}_{i}-{\bm{\mu}}
,

{\bm{x}}_{i}-{\bm{\mu}}=\begin{bmatrix}1-1\\
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}-\tilde{\bm{\mu}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}-\tilde{\bm{\mu}}\end{bmatrix}.

Hence, the covariance matrix becomes

{\bm{\Sigma}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}-\tilde{\bm{\mu}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{i}-\tilde{\bm{\mu}})^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\right]=\begin{bmatrix}0&{\bm{0}}^{\top}\\
{\bm{0}}&\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\end{bmatrix},

where
{\bm{0}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
is the zero vector.

Thus,
{\bm{\Sigma}}
is a block diagonal matrix with a zero entry in the top-left and
\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}
as the bottom-right block.

Since the eigenvalues of a block diagonal matrix are the eigenvalues of the diagonal blocks, the eigenvalues of
{\bm{\Sigma}}
are the eigenvalues of
\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}
together with an additional zero eigenvalue.

Therefore, the operator norm of
{\bm{\Sigma}}
satisfies

\|{\bm{\Sigma}}\|_{\op}=\max(\|\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\|_{\op},0)=\|\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\|_{\op}
.
Given that
\|\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\|_{\op}\leq\sigma^{2}
by assumption, we conclude

\|{\bm{\Sigma}}\|_{\op}\leq\sigma^{2}
.
∎

Plugging the sample size
N=O(\zeta W^{2}d\log(d)/\epsilon)
into the above error bound, the uniform convergence error term
O(\zeta W\sqrt{d/N})
becomes:

O(\zeta W\sqrt{d/N})=O\left(\zeta W\sqrt{\frac{d}{W^{2}d\log d/\epsilon}}\right)=O(\zeta\sqrt{\epsilon}/\log d)=O(\zeta\sqrt{\epsilon}),

which is dominated by the optimization error term
O(\sigma\zeta\sqrt{\epsilon}\|{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2})
.
∎

D.3
Further Details for SVM Example

This section provides additional details for the Support Vector Machine (SVM) case discussed in
Example

4.4
.
We consider a binary classification problem. Let the samples be
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{\pm 1\}
, drawn from a reference distribution
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
. The transportation cost for the Wasserstein distance is given by
c(({\bm{x}},y),({\bm{x}}^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=\chi(y-y^{\prime})+\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\|_{r}
, where
\chi(0)=0
and
\chi(z)=\infty
for
z\neq 0
(ensuring labels do not change during transport), and
\|\cdot\|_{r}
is the
\ell_{r}
-norm for covariates. The loss function is the hinge loss,
\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}},y)=(1-y{\bm{w}}\cdot{\bm{x}})_{+}
.

The
W_{1}
-DRO problem is

\min_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\max_{{\mathbb{P}}:W_{1}^{c}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{P}}_{0})\leq\rho}\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},y)\sim{\mathbb{P}}}[(1-y{\bm{w}}\cdot{\bm{x}})_{+}]\;.

As established in
Section

3.3
(specifically,
Equation

3
), and noting that the hinge loss
(1-z)_{+}
is
1
-Lipschitz (so
\zeta=1
), this problem is equivalent to:

\min_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},y)\sim{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[(1-y{\bm{w}}\cdot{\bm{x}})_{+}]+\rho\|{\bm{w}}\|_{s},

where
1/s+1/r=1
. For
Example

4.4
, we consider
r=2
, which implies
s=2
(Euclidean norms).

Assumption D.2

(Distributional Assumptions on Covariates)
.

We assume the following for the marginal distribution of covariates
{\bm{x}}
under
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
, similar to
[
Dia+19a
]
:

•

Bounded Second Moment:

\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[{\bm{x}}{\bm{x}}^{T}]\preceq\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}}_{d}
, where
{\bm{I}}_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix. This implies
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[({\bm{v}}\cdot{\bm{x}})^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
for any unit vector
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

•

Anti-concentration:
There exists a constant
B>0
and
\lambda>0
such that for all unit vectors
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that
\Pr_{({\bm{x}},y)\sim{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[|{\bm{v}}\cdot{\bm{x}}|\leq\lambda\sigma]\leq B\lambda
.

With
D.2
, we can show that restricting the weight vector
{\bm{w}}
to an
\ell_{2}
-ball of radius
1/(\lambda\sigma)
incurs at most an
O(\lambda)
additive error in the expected hinge loss. This is formalized in the following lemma.

Lemma D.3

(Clipping of
{\bm{w}}
for Hinge Loss)
.

Let
{\bm{w}}_{P}=\Proj_{{\mathcal{B}}(1/(\lambda\sigma))}({\bm{w}})=\min\{1,1/(\lambda\sigma\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2})\}{\bm{w}}
be the projection of
{\bm{w}}
onto the
\ell_{2}
-ball of radius
1/(\lambda\sigma)
. Then,

\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},y)\sim{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[(1-y({\bm{w}}_{P}\cdot{\bm{x}}))_{+}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},y)\sim{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[(1-y({\bm{w}}\cdot{\bm{x}}))_{+}]+2B\lambda.

Proof.

This proof adapts the argument from
[
Dia+19a
, Lemma E.9]
to the hinge loss.
Let
\ell(\hat{{\bm{w}}};{\bm{x}},y)=(1-y(\hat{{\bm{w}}}\cdot{\bm{x}}))_{+}
. Fix
{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If
\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}\leq 1/(\lambda\sigma)
, then
{\bm{w}}_{P}={\bm{w}}
, and the statement is trivial.
Assume
\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}>1/(\lambda\sigma)
, so
\|{\bm{w}}_{P}\|_{2}=1/(\lambda\sigma)
. Also,
{\bm{w}}_{P}=k{\bm{w}}
for
k=1/(\lambda\sigma\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2})<1
.

Define the event
E=\{({\bm{x}},y):|{\bm{x}}\cdot{\bm{w}}_{P}|>1\}
.
On
E
: If
y({\bm{w}}_{P}\cdot{\bm{x}})>1
, then
\ell({\bm{w}}_{P};{\bm{x}},y)=0
. Since
y({\bm{w}}\cdot{\bm{x}})=(1/k)y({\bm{w}}_{P}\cdot{\bm{x}})>(1/k)\cdot 1\geq 1
, it follows that
\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}},y)=0
. Thus,
\ell({\bm{w}}_{P};{\bm{x}},y)=\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}},y)
.
If
y({\bm{w}}_{P}\cdot{\bm{x}})<-1
, then
\ell({\bm{w}}_{P};{\bm{x}},y)=1-y({\bm{w}}_{P}\cdot{\bm{x}})
. Since
k<1
,
y({\bm{w}}_{P}\cdot{\bm{x}})=ky({\bm{w}}\cdot{\bm{x}})
. Since
y({\bm{w}}\cdot{\bm{x}})<0
,
y({\bm{w}}_{P}\cdot{\bm{x}})\geq y({\bm{w}}\cdot{\bm{x}})
. So
1-y({\bm{w}}_{P}\cdot{\bm{x}})\leq 1-y({\bm{w}}\cdot{\bm{x}})
. As both are positive,
\ell({\bm{w}}_{P};{\bm{x}},y)\leq\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}},y)
.
Thus, on
E
, we have
\ell({\bm{w}}_{P};{\bm{x}},y)\leq\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}},y)
.

On the complement event
E^{c}=\{({\bm{x}},y):|{\bm{x}}\cdot{\bm{w}}_{P}|\leq 1\}
:
We have
\ell({\bm{w}}_{P};{\bm{x}},y)=(1-y({\bm{w}}_{P}\cdot{\bm{x}}))_{+}
. Since
y\in\{\pm 1\}
and
|{\bm{x}}\cdot{\bm{w}}_{P}|\leq 1
,
y({\bm{w}}_{P}\cdot{\bm{x}})
is between
-1
and
1
.
So,
1-y({\bm{w}}_{P}\cdot{\bm{x}})
is between
0
and
2
. Thus,
\ell({\bm{w}}_{P};{\bm{x}},y)\leq 2
.
As
\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}},y)\geq 0
, on
E^{c}
we have
\ell({\bm{w}}_{P};{\bm{x}},y)\leq 2\leq 2+\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}},y)
.

We now bound
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}(E^{c})
. The condition
|{\bm{x}}\cdot{\bm{w}}_{P}|\leq 1
implies
|{\bm{x}}\cdot({\bm{w}}_{P}/\|{\bm{w}}_{P}\|_{2})|\leq 1/\|{\bm{w}}_{P}\|_{2}=\lambda\sigma
.
Let
{\bm{v}}={\bm{w}}_{P}/\|{\bm{w}}_{P}\|_{2}
. Then
\|{\bm{v}}\|_{2}=1
.
So,
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}(E^{c})={\mathbb{P}}_{0}(|{\bm{x}}\cdot{\bm{w}}_{P}|\leq 1)={\mathbb{P}}_{0}(|{\bm{x}}\cdot{\bm{v}}|\leq\lambda\sigma)
. By the anti-concentration assumption (
D.2
),
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}(E^{c})\leq B\lambda
.

Therefore, by the law of total expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\ell({\bm{w}}_{P};{\bm{x}},y)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\ell({\bm{w}}_{P};{\bm{x}},y)|E]{\mathbb{P}}_{0}(E)+\mathbb{E}[\ell({\bm{w}}_{P};{\bm{x}},y)|E^{c}]{\mathbb{P}}_{0}(E^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}},y)|E]{\mathbb{P}}_{0}(E)+\mathbb{E}[2+\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}},y)|E^{c}]{\mathbb{P}}_{0}(E^{c})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}},y)|E]{\mathbb{P}}_{0}(E)+2{\mathbb{P}}_{0}(E^{c})+\mathbb{E}[\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}},y)|E^{c}]{\mathbb{P}}_{0}(E^{c})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}},y)]+2{\mathbb{P}}_{0}(E^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}},y)]+2B\lambda.

∎

This lemma allows us to restrict the search space for
{\bm{w}}
to the ball
{\mathcal{B}}(1/(\lambda\sigma))
at the cost of an additive
O(\lambda)
term in the objective. The next fact provides a uniform convergence guarantee over this bounded set after removing a small fraction of data points with large norms.

Fact D.4

(Uniform Convergence for SVM, adapted from
[
Dia+19a
, Lemma C.3]
)
.

Let
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
be
N
i.i.d. samples from a distribution
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
satisfying
D.2
. Let
S=\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}):\|{\bm{x}}_{i}\|_{2}\leq 80\sigma\sqrt{d/\epsilon}\}
.
Then for a sample size
N=O(d\log(d/\lambda)/\epsilon)
, with probability at least
0.9
, it holds that
|S|\geq(1-\epsilon)N
and for all
{\bm{w}}\in{\mathcal{B}}((\lambda\sigma)^{-1})
,

\left|\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}},y)]\right|=O((1+\lambda^{-1})\sqrt{\epsilon}).

To obtain the
O(\epsilon^{1/4})
error bound mentioned in
Example

4.4
, we set the parameter
\lambda
from the anti-concentration assumption appropriately. The error from clipping
{\bm{w}}
(Lemma
D.3
) is
O(B\lambda)
. The uniform convergence error (Fact
D.4
), if
\lambda\ll 1
, is
O(\lambda^{-1}\sqrt{\epsilon})
.
Our main algorithm is applied to the set
S
, which can be seen as being
2\epsilon
-corrupted if the original data had
\epsilon
-corruptions and we discard an additional
\epsilon
fraction based on norm. The error from our main algorithm (
Proposition

4.1
) is
O(\sigma\zeta\sqrt{2\epsilon}W)=O(\sigma\sqrt{\epsilon}(\lambda\sigma)^{-1})=O(\lambda^{-1}\sqrt{\epsilon})
since
\zeta=1
for hinge loss. Total error from these sources is
O(B\lambda+\lambda^{-1}\sqrt{\epsilon})
.

We want to balance these errors.
To minimize this, we can set
\lambda=\lambda^{-1}\sqrt{\epsilon}
, which implies
\lambda^{2}=\sqrt{\epsilon}
, so
\lambda=\epsilon^{1/4}
.
Plugging this
\lambda
back, the error becomes
O(B\epsilon^{1/4})
.
This shows that an overall error of
O(\epsilon^{1/4})
in the objective function value is achievable viewing
B
as an absolute constant. The radius of the weight ball is
W=1/(\lambda\sigma)=1/(\sigma\epsilon^{1/4})
.

Appendix E
Tuning Step Sizes for Algorithm
1

The primary algorithm,
Algorithm

1
, and its theoretical guarantee in
Proposition

4.1
depend on the parameter
\gamma=\frac{\|{\bm{w}}_{0}-{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2}}{\zeta\sqrt{N}}
. Specifically,
\|{\bm{w}}_{0}-{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2}
(denoted by
D_{0}
hereafter) is the distance from the initial iterate to the optimal solution
{\bm{w}}^{*}
of the empirical stable objective, which is unknown in practice. This section details a practical and theoretically sound procedure for tuning
\gamma
by performing a geometric search for
D_{0}
. While a simple search across a predefined range is possible, we propose a more efficient method that allows for early stopping, avoiding unnecessary computation if the optimal parameter setting is found long before the search range is exhausted.

A Bounded Geometric Search with Early Stopping

We assume access to a loose upper bound
W_{0}
on
D_{0}=\|{\bm{w}}_{0}-{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2}
. This assumption provides a concrete upper bound on the noise level across the entire search, ensuring our stopping condition remains statistically meaningful.

We search for an estimate of
D_{0}
starting from a plausible minimum
D_{\min}=\Delta/\zeta
and generate a sequence of candidates
D_{j}=D_{\min}\cdot 2^{j}
for
j=0,1,2,\dots,J_{\max}
, where
J_{\max}=\lceil\log_{2}(W_{0}/D_{\min})\rceil
ensures the search space covers the full range up to
W_{0}
.

For each
j=0,1,\dots,J_{\max}
:

1.

Run
Algorithm

1
with
\gamma_{j}=D_{j}/(\zeta\sqrt{N})
to obtain a solution
\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(j)}
.

2.

Robustly estimate the true (population) objective value
F_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}(\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(j)})
using the available
\epsilon
-corrupted training data, which is discussed next. Let this noisy estimate be
\hat{F}^{(j)}
.

3.

Check for early stopping (
20
). If the condition is met, terminate the search.

After the search terminates, select the solution
\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(k)}
that corresponds to the minimum estimated value,
\min_{j}\hat{F}^{(j)}
, found among all runs performed.

Robust Estimation of Objective Function Value

For each candidate solution
\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(j)}
, we need to estimate the objective function value
F_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}(\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(j)})=\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},y)\sim{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell(\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(j)};{\bm{x}},y)]+\psi(\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(j)})
, where
\psi({\bm{w}})=\rho\zeta\|{\bm{w}}\|_{s}
. The regularization term
\psi(\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(j)})
can be computed directly. The challenge is to estimate the expected loss
\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},y)\sim{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell(\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(j)};{\bm{x}},y)]
using the
\epsilon
-corrupted training samples
\{({\bm{x}}_{i}^{c},y_{i}^{c})\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

We compute the loss values
L_{i}^{(j)}=\ell(\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(j)};{\bm{x}}_{i}^{c},y_{i}^{c})
for
i=1,\dots,N
. This set
\{L_{i}^{(j)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
constitutes an
\epsilon
-corrupted sample of the random variable
L^{(j)}({\bm{\xi}})=\ell(\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(j)};{\bm{\xi}})
, where
{\bm{\xi}}\sim{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
. We can robustly estimate the mean of
L^{(j)}({\bm{\xi}})
from these corrupted samples. Standard robust mean estimation techniques for 1-dimensional data (such as those based on truncated mean
[
DK23a
, Section 1.4.2]
or variants of
Algorithm

3
adapted to 1-dimensional settings) achieve an estimation error of
O(\sigma_{L^{(j)}}\sqrt{\epsilon})
; see
2.7
, where
\sigma_{L^{(j)}}
is the standard deviation of the clean loss values
L^{(j)}({\bm{\xi}})
.

To bound
\sigma_{L^{(j)}}
: The loss function
\ell({\bm{w}};{\bm{x}},y)=\phi({\bm{w}}\cdot{\bm{x}},y)
has
\phi(\cdot,y)
as
\zeta
-Lipschitz. The iterates
\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(j)}
are bounded. By
Corollary

D.1
,
\|\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(j)}\|_{2}=O(\|{\bm{w}}_{0}\|_{2}+\|{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2})
. By
3.4
, the arguments
{\bm{w}}\cdot{\bm{x}}
will have variance
\|{\bm{w}}\|_{2}^{2}\sigma^{2}
. The Lipschitz property of the loss
\ell_{y}
implies that the standard deviation
\sigma_{L^{(j)}}
of the loss values is
O(\zeta\|\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(j)}\|_{2}\sigma)
. Consequently, the error in estimating
\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},y)\sim{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}[\ell(\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(j)};{\bm{x}},y)]
is
E_{\text{eval}}=O(\zeta(\|{\bm{w}}_{0}\|_{2}+\|{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2})\sigma\sqrt{\epsilon})
.

Early Stopping Condition

At each step
k
, we keep track of the best (lowest) estimated value found so far,
\hat{F}_{\text{best}}=\min_{j<k}\hat{F}^{(j)}
. We terminate the search if the current estimate is significantly worse:

\text{Stop at step }k\text{ if }\hat{F}^{(k)}>\hat{F}_{\text{best}}+3E_{\text{ub}}\;,

(20)

where
E_{\text{ub}}
is the upper bound of
E_{\text{eval}}
that we know how to compute:
O(\zeta(\|{\bm{w}}_{0}\|_{2}+W_{0})\sigma\sqrt{\epsilon})
.

This stopping criterion has the following probabilistic guarantee: If the error bound
|\hat{F}^{(j)}-F^{(j)}|\leq E_{\text{eval}}
holds for all estimates (which occurs with high probability and the dependence on the failure probability is logarithmic), an observed increase of more than
2E_{\text{eval}}
guarantees that the true objective value has also increased. Specifically, if the condition triggers at step
k
, we have
F_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}^{(k)}>F_{{\mathbb{P}}_{0}}^{(j_{\text{best}})}
with high probability, where
j_{\text{best}}
is the index of the previous best run. This ensures we do not stop prematurely due to noise.

Overall Error Guarantee and Complexity

The geometric search ensures that for at least one candidate
D_{j}
, we have
D_{0}\leq D_{j}<2D_{0}
. For this
D_{j}
, running
Algorithm

1
with
\gamma_{j}=D_{j}/(\zeta\sqrt{N})
yields a solution
\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(j)}
. As shown in the analysis of the error term in
Proposition

4.1
when
\gamma
is mismatched (see
Equation

15
), the function value suboptimality
O(D_{0}\Delta)
becomes
O((D_{j}+D_{0}^{2}/D_{j})\Delta)
. Since
D_{j}\in[D_{0},2D_{0}]
, this is again
O(D_{0}\Delta)=O(\|{\bm{w}}_{0}-{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2}\sigma\zeta\sqrt{\epsilon})
.

If the true
D_{0}
is even smaller than
\Delta/\zeta
, our search does not try
D_{j}
values below
\Delta/\zeta
. For the first run
D_{\min}=\Delta/\zeta
, the proven error bound (see
Equation

15
) depends on
(D_{\min}+D_{0}^{2}/D_{\min})\Delta
and would be approximately
(\Delta/\zeta+D_{0}^{2}\zeta/\Delta)\Delta=\Delta^{2}/\zeta+D_{0}^{2}\zeta
. Since
D_{0}<\Delta/\zeta
,
D_{0}^{2}\zeta<(\Delta^{2}/\zeta^{2})\zeta=\Delta^{2}/\zeta
. So the error in this case is
O(\Delta^{2}/\zeta)=O(\sigma^{2}\zeta\epsilon)
.

The final excess risk bound for the selected solution
\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{T}^{(*)}
remains

O(D_{0}\Delta+E_{\text{eval}})=O(\zeta(\|{\bm{w}}_{0}\|_{2}+\|{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2})\sigma\sqrt{\epsilon}+\sigma^{2}\zeta\epsilon)\;.

Initializing at
{\bm{w}}_{0}={\bm{0}}
, this simplifies to
O(\zeta(\|{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2}+\sigma\sqrt{\epsilon})\sigma\sqrt{\epsilon})
.

The number of times
Algorithm

1
is executed is at most
O(\log(W_{0}\zeta/\Delta))
, adding this logarithmic factor to the total computational complexity. This procedure makes the algorithm adaptive to the unknown distance
\|{\bm{w}}_{0}-{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2}
, incurring total error
O(\zeta\sigma\sqrt{\epsilon}(\|{\bm{w}}^{*}\|_{2}+\sigma\sqrt{\epsilon}))
.
