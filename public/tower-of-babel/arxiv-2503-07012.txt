Tsubasa Sugeno, Takahiro Yokokura, Kazuya Yonekura

Abstract
In confining large N N theories with a θ \theta angle such as four-dimensional SU ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{SU}(N) pure Yang-Mills theory, there are multiple metastable vacua and it makes sense to consider the parameter region of “large θ \theta of order N N ” despite the fact that θ \theta is a 2 ​ π 2\pi -periodic parameter. We investigate this parameter region in the two-dimensional ℂ ​ ℙ N − 1 \mathbb{CP}^{N-1} model by computing the partition function on T 2 T^{2} . When θ / N \theta/N is of order 𝒪 ⁡ ( 0.1 ) \mathcal{O}(0.1) or less, we get perfectly sensible results for the vacuum energies and decay rates of metastable vacua. However, when θ / N \theta/N is of order 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathcal{O}(1) , we encounter a problem about saddle points that would give larger contributions to the partition function than the true vacuum. We discuss why it might not be straightforward to resolve this problem.

Introduction and Summary

The theta angle
\theta
in quantum field theories (QFTs) is an interesting parameter.
In particle physics and cosmology, the dependence of a theory on the theta angle is important for the potential energy of an axion or axion-like particles.
It is also interesting purely theoretically in QFTs because it appears only in nonperturbative phenomena (either instanton effects or strong coupling effects), and is related to topological properties of a theory.

Let us consider the case of four-dimensional (4d)
\mathrm{SU}(N)
gauge theories.
At weak coupling, we can calculate the
\theta
dependence of the vacuum energy by the dilute instanton gas approximation.
As a result, the vacuum energy
V(\theta)
as a function of
\theta
at the leading order of weak coupling expansion is given by
V(\theta)\propto(1-\cos\theta)
, where we have set the vacuum energy at
\theta=0
to be zero.
However, the situation is different at strong coupling.
To be concrete, let us focus on pure Yang-Mills theory. By large
N
argument, it was found that the vacuum energy should be of the form
V(\theta)=N^{2}\bar{V}(\theta/N)
, where
\bar{V}
is of order one in the large
N
limit
Witten:1980sp
;
Witten:1998uka
.

1

1

This difference in the behavior of the vacuum energy arises from the breakdown of the dilute instanton gas approximation at strong coupling, as pointed out by
(
Witten:1978bc
)
.

Moreover, even for finite
N
, an ’t Hooft anomaly was found that excludes the simple form
V(\theta)\propto(1-\cos\theta)
with the assumption of a unique gapped vacuum at each
\theta

Cordova:2019jnf
;
Cordova:2019uob
(see also
Gaiotto:2017yup
;
Kikuchi:2017pcp
).

A scenario that is consistent with both the large
N
argument and the ’t Hooft anomaly is to have multiple metastable vacua.

2

In some theories such as
{\cal N}=1

The
n

V_{n}(\theta)=N^{2}\bar{V}((\theta+2\pi n)/N)
. Including these metastable vacua, the vacuum energy has multi-branch structure in the sense that for a given
\theta
, there are multiple metastable vacua with energy
V_{n}(\theta)
. The
2\pi
-periodicity of
\theta
is realized by a nontrivial monodromy
V_{n}(\theta+2\pi)=V_{n+1}(\theta)
.
Although the theory as a whole has the
2\pi
-periodicity, each branch (i.e. fixed

) is not
2\pi
-periodic.
This structure has applications to cosmology. For instance, natural inflation
(
Freese:1990rb
)
uses an axion-like inflaton whose potential is often assumed to be
V(\theta)\propto(1-\cos\theta)
. However, in strongly coupled theories, it is possible to realize more general potentials of the form

(see e.g.
(

Recent numerical simulations for the case

.
In numerical simulations, however, it may not be easy (even if not impossible

) to study the regime of metastable vacua that is far from the true vacuum.
The true vacuum is given by
n
such that

.
For cosmological applications, we may want to know the shape of the potential for large values of
\theta

of order one, a general argument (see

V_{0}(\theta)=N^{2}\bar{V}(\theta/N)=\frac{1}{2}c\theta^{2}+{\cal O}(N^{-2})

For example, one qualitative question about the function

V_{n}(\theta)

\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}

There are previous studies of the vacuum energy in the large

\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}

The topological susceptibility (i.e. the constant

We calculate the partition function of the
\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}
model on
T^{2}
which contains the information of the vacuum energy including metastable vacua.

The partition function
Z(\theta)
on
T^{2}
turns out to have a decomposition

\displaystyle Z(\theta)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta}_{n}),\qquad\bar{\theta}_{n}=\frac{\theta+2\pi n}{N}.

(1.1)

The integer
n
in this sum corresponds to the label of metastable vacua which appeared above.
As far as
|\bar{\theta}_{n}|
is small (numerically of order
0.1
), we will get a sensible result
{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta}_{n})\sim\exp[-V_{n}(\theta){\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})]
where
V_{n}(\theta)=N^{2}\bar{V}(\bar{\theta}_{n})
and
{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})
is the volume of
T^{2}
. More precisely, there is also an imaginary part so that
V_{n}(\theta)
is replaced by
V_{n}(\theta)-\frac{{\mathsf{i}}}{2}\Gamma_{n}(\theta)
, where
\Gamma_{n}(\theta)
represents the vacuum decay rate of the
n
-th metastable vacuum. The result of
\bar{V}(\bar{\theta}_{n})
for small
|\bar{\theta}_{n}|
agrees with previous results in the literature, and the vacuum decay rate will also have a clear interpretation as the Schwinger effect.
For small
|\bar{\theta}_{n}|
, the absolute value
|{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta}_{n})|
is a monotonically decreasing function of
|\bar{\theta}_{n}|
and in particular
|{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta}_{n})|<|{\cal Z}(0)|
for
0<|\bar{\theta}_{n}|<{\cal O}(0.1)
.

However, when
|\bar{\theta}_{n}|
is somewhat large (numerically of order
1
), we encounter a puzzle:
if we follow the standard large
N
arguments such as the saddle point method, the result of computations is that the absolute value
|{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta}_{n})|
seems to exceed
|{\cal Z}(0)|
for somewhat large
|\bar{\theta}_{n}|\sim{\cal O}(1)
.
Recall that the true vacuum corresponds to
\bar{\theta}_{n}\sim 0
(or more precisely
-\pi/N\leq\bar{\theta}_{n}\leq\pi/N
). We expect that the true vacuum gives the most dominant contribution to the partition function in the large volume limit, and hence other contributions should not exceed the true vacuum contribution. Therefore, the above problem should be resolved in some way. We leave it a future work to find a resolution of the puzzle. In the present paper, we just present the puzzle and discuss why it might require something that is beyond the standard large
N
analysis.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we will explain the basic setup.
Section
3
gives a detailed review of the effective action in the large
N

\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}
model which is obtained after integrating out “matter fields”.
The
\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}
model is formulated by using an auxiliary

gauge field as well as an auxiliary scalar field

E
of the
\mathrm{U}(1)

D
and
E
. Although the effective action (including
E
) is known in the literature, we will try to be careful about the assumptions that are used to obtain the effective action.
In Section
4
, we will show our main results. We evaluate the path integral by using the saddle points of the effective action. This is a standard procedure in large
N

First, for small
\bar{\theta}_{n}
we will reproduce the known vacuum energy, and we will also compute the vacuum decay rate which is exponentially small for small
\bar{\theta}_{n}
.
We emphasize that the results for small
\bar{\theta}_{n}
have perfectly sensible physical interpretations.
Next we will show numerical results for the saddle point actions for all
\bar{\theta}_{n}
.
By using those results, in Section
5

2
Preliminaries

In this paper, we will perform the Euclidean path integral of the two-dimensional (2d)
\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}
sigma model in a large but finite volume torus
T^{2}
. (The size of the volume will be discussed later.)
In this section, we recall some basic facts about the
\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}
model and make some preparations for later sections.

2.1
The basic setup

The Euclidean action of the 2d
\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}
model with a
\theta
-term is given by

\displaystyle S_{\theta}[\phi,A,D]=\frac{N}{\bar{g}_{0}^{2}}\int{\rm d}^{2}x\ \left[(D_{\mu}\phi)^{\dagger}(D^{\mu}\phi)+D(\phi^{\dagger}\phi-1)\right]-{\mathsf{i}}\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}^{2}x\ \frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu},

(2.1)

where
\phi=(\phi^{1},\cdots,\phi^{N})
is an
N
-component complex scalar field,
\bar{g}_{0}
is a bare ’t Hooft coupling constant,
D_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}-{\mathsf{i}}A_{\mu}
is a covariant derivative with an auxiliary
\mathrm{U}(1)
gauge field
A_{\mu}
,
F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}
is the gauge field strength, and
D
is an auxiliary scalar field. We will also use differential form notations such as
A=A_{\mu}{\rm d}x^{\mu}
and
F={\rm d}A
.
The Euclidean path integral is

\displaystyle Z(\theta):=\int\mathcal{D}D\,\mathcal{D}A\,\mathcal{D}\phi^{\dagger}\,\mathcal{D}\phi\,e^{-S_{\theta}[\phi,A,D]}.

(2.2)

Unless otherwise stated, the path integral is performed on a flat
T^{2}
.

The 2d
\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}
model has instanton sectors whose topological charge is given by

\displaystyle m:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}^{2}x\ \frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

(2.3)

We can divide the path integral into different topological sectors as

\displaystyle Z(\theta)=\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{{\mathsf{i}}m\theta}Z_{m},\qquad Z_{m}:=\int_{m}\mathcal{D}D\,\mathcal{D}A\,\mathcal{D}\phi^{\dagger}\,\mathcal{D}\phi\,e^{-S_{\theta=0}[\phi,A,D]},

(2.4)

where the subscript
m
in the path integral for
Z_{m}
means that it is restricted to the fixed topological sector specified by
m
.

In a large volume, it will be convenient to rewrite the sum over instanton numbers as follows
(
Aguado:2010ex
)
.
Suppose that
Z_{m}
, as a function of
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, can be extended to a function
Z_{x}
of a real variable
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. (This point will be discussed more explicitly later.)
Then, the Poisson summation formula
\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\delta(x-m)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{2\pi{\mathsf{i}}nx}
gives

\displaystyle Z(\theta)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\,e^{{\mathsf{i}}(\theta+2\pi n)x}Z_{x}.

(2.5)

The integer
n
is a kind of the electromagnetic dual of the 1-form gauge field
A_{\mu}
in 2d.
3
3

3

The electromagnetic dual of a
p
-form gauge field in
d
-dimensions is a
(d-p-2)
-form. When
d=2
and
p=1
, the dual is a “
(-1)
-form” which is just described by a constant integer
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

The
n
will be interpreted as labeling metastable vacua, at least when the vacuum decay rate is small.
We define the vacuum energy
V_{n}(\theta)
and the vacuum decay rate
\Gamma_{n}(\theta)
for the vacuum labeled by
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
as

\displaystyle V_{n}(\theta)-{\mathsf{i}}\frac{\Gamma_{n}(\theta)}{2}:=-\lim_{{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})\to\infty}\frac{1}{{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})}\log\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\,e^{{\mathsf{i}}(\theta+2\pi n)x}Z_{x}\right).

(2.6)

where
{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})
is the volume of
T^{2}
.
We can recover the information of nonzero
n
from that of
n=0
because
\theta
and
n
appear in the combination
\theta+2\pi n
and hence

\displaystyle V_{n}(\theta)=V(\theta+2\pi n),\qquad\Gamma_{n}(\theta)=\Gamma(\theta+2\pi n),

(2.7)

where
V(\theta)=V_{0}(\theta)
and
\Gamma(\theta)=\Gamma_{0}(\theta)
.

2.2
Large
N

For large
N
analyses, it is convenient to define

\displaystyle\bar{\theta}=\frac{\theta}{N}.

(2.8)

More generally, we will put a bar over a quantity that is normalized to be of order one in the large
N
limit.

We first perform the path integral over
\phi
and
\phi^{\dagger}
.
Then we obtain the effective action for
A_{\mu}
and
D
as

\displaystyle S_{\text{eff}}[A,D]=N\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}[A,D],

(2.9)

where

\displaystyle\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}[A,D]=\log\Det(-D_{\mu}D^{\mu}+D)+\int{\rm d}^{2}x\left(-\frac{1}{\bar{g}_{0}^{2}}D\,-{\mathsf{i}}\frac{\bar{\theta}}{2\pi}\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}\right)

(2.10)

This form of the effective action and the standard large
N
argument suggest that the partition function is a function of
\bar{\theta}
as

\displaystyle Z(\theta)\sim\exp\left(-N\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}(\bar{\theta})\right),

(2.11)

where
\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}(\bar{\theta})
is a function of
\bar{\theta}
which is of order one in the sense of large
N
counting. The contribution (
2.11
) is expected to be dominated by the vacuum. However, we know that the theory has the
2\pi
-periodicity
\theta\sim\theta+2\pi
. This periodicity would be inconsistent if (
2.11
) were the complete answer, since the function
\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}(\bar{\theta})
is of order one and the
2\pi
shift of
\theta
corresponds to the
2\pi/N
shift of
\bar{\theta}=\theta/N
.

The resolution is as follows. Suppose that there are many (metastable) vacua labeled by an integer
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that the partition function is actually given by

\displaystyle Z(\theta)\sim\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\exp\left(-N\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}(\bar{\theta}_{n})\right),

(2.12)

where

\displaystyle\bar{\theta}_{n}:=\frac{\theta+2\pi n}{N}.

(2.13)

Then it has the
2\pi
-periodicity. This is a rather general structure of the
\theta
-dependence in large
N
field theories. (For a nice summary in the case of Yang-Mills, see Section 1 of
Witten:1998uka
.) For the
\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}
model, we have explicitly found this structure in (
2.5
) which is obtained after the Poisson summation.

Effective action

In this section, we study the effective action in the 2d
\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}
model. See
(
Riva:1980wq

;

3.1
Gauge field configurations

In a fixed topological sector labeled by
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we decompose the gauge field
A_{\mu}
as

\displaystyle A_{\mu}=A_{\mu}^{(m)}+A_{\mu}^{\rm flat}+A^{\prime}_{\mu}.

(3.1)

The meaning of each term on the right hand side is as follows.
The
A_{\mu}^{(m)}
is a topologically nontrivial gauge field configuration such that the field strength is constant,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}^{(m)}=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu}\left(\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}^{(m)}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}^{(m)}\right)=\frac{2\pi m}{{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})}.

(3.2)

More explicitly, let us represent a torus
T^{2}
(which we take to be rectangular for simplicity) by using a coordinate system
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with equivalence relations

\displaystyle x\sim x+L_{1},\qquad y\sim y+L_{2}

(3.3)

where
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are the length of the two sides of
T^{2}
. We have
{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})=L_{1}L_{2}
.
Then
A^{(m)}
may be taken as

\displaystyle A^{(m)}=A_{\mu}^{(m)}{\rm d}x^{\mu}=\frac{2\pi m}{L_{1}L_{2}}x{\rm d}y,

(3.4)

whose field strength 2-form is
F^{(m)}=(2\pi m/L_{1}L_{2}){\rm d}x\wedge{\rm d}y
. We remark that there must be a nontrivial transition function
h(y)
when we identify
x+L_{1}
and
x
because

\displaystyle-{\mathsf{i}}\left(A^{(m)}(x+L_{1},y)-A^{(m)}(x,y)\right)=h(y)^{-1}{\rm d}h(y),\qquad h(y)=\exp\left(-\frac{2\pi m{\mathsf{i}}y}{L_{2}}\right).

(3.5)

The
A_{\mu}^{\rm flat}
and
A^{\prime}_{\mu}
are topologically trivial gauge fields describing fluctuations around the
A_{\mu}^{(m)}
. The
A_{\mu}^{\rm flat}
is a flat connection of the form

\displaystyle A^{\rm flat}=2\pi\left(\frac{\alpha{\rm d}x}{L_{1}}+\frac{\beta{\rm d}y}{L_{2}}\right),

(3.6)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are constants.
Because of large gauge transformations
-{\mathsf{i}}A\to-{\mathsf{i}}A+g^{-1}{\rm d}g
by
\mathrm{U}(1)
-valued functions
g
, the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
have gauge equivalence relations

\displaystyle\alpha\sim\alpha+1,\qquad\beta\sim\beta+1.

(3.7)

The
A^{\prime}_{\mu}
contains other non-constant modes in the Fourier mode expansion of the (topologically trivial part of the) gauge field, or in other words the modes in
A^{\prime}_{\mu}
have nonzero momentum on
T^{2}
.

We remark that when
m\neq 0
, gauge field configurations with different values of
\alpha
and
\beta
are actually equivalent modulo translations on
T^{2}
(up to some changes of
A^{\prime}
due to the translations). For instance, let us consider a translation
x\to x+L_{1}a
by an arbitrary parameter
a
. Then

\displaystyle A^{(m)}(x+L_{1}a,y)=A^{(m)}(x,y)+\frac{2\pi ma}{L_{2}}{\rm d}y.

(3.8)

Thus, by choosing
a=-\beta/m
, we can cancel the part
2\pi\beta{\rm d}y/L_{2}
in
A^{\rm flat}
.
For a translation in the
y
-direction, we first perform gauge transformations by
g=\exp(2\pi m{\mathsf{i}}xy/L_{1}L_{2})
,

\displaystyle\tilde{A}^{(m)}=A^{(m)}+{\mathsf{i}}g^{-1}{\rm d}g=-\frac{2\pi m}{L_{1}L_{2}}y{\rm d}x.

(3.9)

Then we can repeat the same argument as in the case of translations in the
x
-direction.
4
4

4

One might think that if we perform a translation of
A^{(m)}=\frac{2\pi m}{L_{1}L_{2}}x{\rm d}y
directly in the
y
-direction as
y\to y+L_{2}b
, then we would get nothing because
{\rm d}(y+L_{2}b)={\rm d}y
. However, it is not straightforward to do translations in the
y
-direction in this gauge, because we have the transition function
h(y)
in (
3.5
) which is not invariant under translations in the
y
-direction.

3.2
Large
N
effective action

Let us study the effective action (
2.10
).
The first term in (
2.10
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\log\Det(-D_{\mu}D^{\mu}+D)=-\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t}\Tr e^{-t(-D_{\mu}D^{\mu}+D)}

(3.10)

where we introduced a cutoff
\varepsilon
.

The gauge field
A
is decomposed as (
3.1
).
Let us also decompose the auxiliary field
D
as

\displaystyle D=D_{0}+D^{\prime},

(3.11)

where
D_{0}
is a constant on
T^{2}
and
D^{\prime}
includes other non-constant modes in the Fourier mode expansion of
D
on
T^{2}
(i.e. they have nonzero momentum on
T^{2}
).
Then we define the effective action of
D_{0}
and
m
by

\displaystyle N\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}[m,D_{0}]=-\log\left(\int\mathcal{D}A^{\rm flat}\mathcal{D}A^{\prime}\mathcal{D}D^{\prime}e^{-N\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}[A,D]}\right).

(3.12)

For the time being, we focus on the case that
m\neq 0
.
We
assume
that the integral over non-constant modes
A^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
is dominated by saddle points on which

\displaystyle A^{\prime},D^{\prime}\sim{\cal O}(e^{-ML_{1}})+{\cal O}(e^{-ML_{2}}),

(3.13)

where
M=\sqrt{D_{0}}
is the mass of the field
\phi
, and
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are the lengths of the sides of
T^{2}
. Let us discuss the reasons for this assumption.

If we take the large volume limit of
T^{2}
, the spacetime is approximately the flat space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Then, it may be reasonable to assume that the path integral is dominated by saddle points that are Lorentz and translation invariant, such as (metastable) vacua. If so, the values of
A^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
in those saddle points should vanish in the large volume limit because
A^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
consist of modes with nonzero momentum.

Now let us consider their behavior in the case of large but finite
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
. If
A^{(m)}
were absent, translation invariance (i.e. momentum conservation) on
T^{2}
would imply that the action
\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}[A,D]
cannot have linear terms in
A^{\prime}
or
D^{\prime}
and hence is expanded as a function of
A^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}[A,D]={\cal O}(1)+{\cal O}(A^{\prime 2})+{\cal O}(D^{\prime 2})+{\cal O}(A^{\prime}D^{\prime})+\cdots

(3.14)

where the ellipses denote quartic and higher order terms in
A^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
. This would have
A^{\prime}=0,D^{\prime}=0
as a saddle point.

In reality, the translation invariance is violated by
A^{(m)}
. However, the violation of translation invariance by
A^{(m)}
only has the effect of changing
A^{\rm flat}
as discussed at the end of Section
3.1
. The flat connection
A^{\rm flat}
has only global effects on
T^{2}
because its field strength is zero,
{\rm d}A^{\rm flat}=0
. For it to be detected, a particle corresponding to the field
\phi
must travel around one of the topologically nontrivial cycles of
T^{2}
.
Then the quantum mechanical action of a
\phi
-particle going around the cycle of
T^{2}
in the
x
-direction has the exponentiated action (see Appendix
A
)

\displaystyle\exp\left(-M\int{\rm d}s\right)\sim\exp(-ML_{1}),

(3.15)

where
{\rm d}s
is the line element of the worldline of the
\phi
-particle and
M
is the mass.
Similarly, the exponentiated action of a
\phi
-particle going around the
y
-direction is given by
\exp(-ML_{2})
. This is the reason for the exponential suppression in (
3.13
). See Appendix
A.2
for more details.

At any rate, we assume the behavior (
3.13
) for the saddle point values of
A^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
, and we
neglect
them by taking
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
to be sufficiently large.
Then let us consider the path integral on the right-hand side of (
3.12
) around the saddle point
A^{\prime}\sim 0,D^{\prime}\sim 0
.
By the standard large
N
argument, the
1/N
can be regarded as a coupling constant in the perturbative expansion with respect to
A^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
, and hence we expect

\displaystyle\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}[m,D_{0}]=\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}[A=A_{\mu}^{(m)},D=D_{0}]+{\cal O}(1/N)+{\cal O}(e^{-ML_{1}})+{\cal O}(e^{-ML_{2}}),

(3.16)

where we have used the fact that when
A^{\prime}=0
and
D^{\prime}=0
, the effect of
A^{\rm flat}
can be eliminated by translation on
T^{2}
as discussed at the end of Section
3.1
and hence
A^{\rm flat}
does not affect the leading term of the effective action. (We must still perform the path integral over
A^{\rm flat}
, but it gives a constant overall factor of the partition function and is absorbed in the term
{\cal O}(1/N)
in (
3.16
).) We
assume
that the terms of order
{\cal O}(1/N)
as well as
{\cal O}(e^{-cL_{1}})+{\cal O}(e^{-cL_{2}})
are negligible for the purposes of the following analysis.

Now we need to calculate
\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}[A=A_{\mu}^{(m)},D=D_{0}]
.
For this purpose, we use the standard result about Landau levels.
We denote

\displaystyle E_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu}F^{(m)}_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{2\pi m}{{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})}.

(3.17)

(The minus sign here is introduced just for later convenience.)
We also denote

\displaystyle a_{\pm}=\frac{{\mathsf{i}}(D_{x}\pm{\mathsf{i}}D_{y})}{\sqrt{2|E_{0}|}},

(3.18)

where
E_{0}\neq 0
since we are considering the case
m\neq 0
.
The
a_{\pm}
are hermitian conjugates of each other and satisfy the commutation relation

\displaystyle[a_{-},a_{+}]=-\frac{{\mathsf{i}}}{|E_{0}|}[D_{x},D_{y}]=\frac{E_{0}}{|E_{0}|}.

(3.19)

Depending on the sign of
E_{0}
, we can regard one of
a_{\pm}
as an annihilation operator and the other as a creation operator. The numbers
N_{\pm}
of modes annihilated by
a_{\pm}
are determined by the Atiyah-Singer index theorem
5
5

5

The Dirac operator
{\mathsf{i}}\gamma^{\mu}D_{\mu}
and the chirality operator
\bar{\gamma}
in 2d are given by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{i}}\gamma^{\mu}D_{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}0&{\mathsf{i}}(D_{x}-{\mathsf{i}}D_{y})\\
{\mathsf{i}}(D_{x}+{\mathsf{i}}D_{y})&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\bar{\gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(3.20)

as

\displaystyle N_{+}-N_{-}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}x^{2}\,\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}=m=-\frac{1}{2\pi}{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})E_{0}.

(3.21)

One of
N_{\pm}
is zero because one of
a_{\pm}
is a creation operator which does not annihilate anything. Then the other one of
N_{\pm}
is given by
|m|
.

Because
-D_{\mu}D^{\mu}=|E_{0}|(a_{+}a_{-}+a_{-}a_{+})
, its spectrum is the same as that of a harmonic oscillator in quantum mechanics. Actually, there are
|m|
copies of harmonic oscillators because the lowest energy states have
|m|
-fold degeneracy. Thus we get

\displaystyle\Tr e^{-t(-D_{\mu}D^{\mu}+D_{0})}=|m|\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-t(2k+1)|E_{0}|-tD_{0}}=\frac{1}{4\pi}{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})\ \frac{|E_{0}|e^{-D_{0}t}}{\sinh|E_{0}|t}\,.

(3.22)

Then, the first term in (
2.10
) becomes

\displaystyle\log\Det(-D_{\mu}D_{\mu}+D_{0})=-\dfrac{1}{4\pi}{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t}\ \frac{E_{0}e^{-D_{0}t}}{\sinh E_{0}t}\,,

(3.23)

where we have used the fact that
E_{0}/\sinh(E_{0}t)
is an even function of
E_{0}
.

Let us denote the effective action
\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}[m,D_{0}]
defined in (
3.12
) also as
\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}[E_{0},D_{0},\bar{\theta}]
, where
E_{0}
and
m
are related by (
3.17
), and we have also made explicit the dependence on
\bar{\theta}
. From the above results, we get the effective action for
m\neq 0
given by

\displaystyle\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}[E_{0},D_{0},\bar{\theta}]

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{4\pi}{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(E_{0},D_{0},\bar{\theta})\,,

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(E_{0},D_{0},\bar{\theta})

\displaystyle:=-\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t}\frac{E_{0}e^{-D_{0}t}}{\sinh E_{0}t}+2{\mathsf{i}}\bar{\theta}E_{0}-\frac{4\pi}{\bar{g}_{0}^{2}}D_{0}\,.

(3.24)

More explicit forms will be calculated in Section
3.4
.

Let us recapitulate the
assumptions
used to arrive at the approximate expression (
3.24
) for
\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}[E_{0},D_{0},\bar{\theta}]
.

•

The values of
A^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
in the dominant saddle points are exponentially suppressed as (
3.13
) and their effects are negligible.

•

Higher order terms in the
1/N
expansion in (
3.16
) are negligible.

3.3
The partition function

Now the partition function is computed as follows. Recall the sum over instanton numbers in (
2.4
). For
m\neq 0
, the
e^{{\mathsf{i}}m\theta}Z_{m}
is given by
\hat{Z}[E_{0},\bar{\theta}]
define by

\displaystyle\hat{Z}[E_{0},\bar{\theta}]=\int{\rm d}D_{0}\,\exp\left(-N\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}[E_{0},D_{0},\bar{\theta}]\right).

(3.25)

We have computed the effective action only for
m\neq 0
. Thus, we rewrite (
2.4
) as

\displaystyle Z(\theta)=(Z_{0}-\hat{Z}[0])+\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\hat{Z}[E_{0},\bar{\theta}].

(3.26)

Now, one can see that the effective action (
3.24
) (neglecting subleading terms) has a straightforward continuation from integer
m
to real values,
m\to x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The
x
and
E_{0}
are related by

E_{0}=-{2\pi x/{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})}.

By using that continuation, we use the Poisson summation formula (
2.5
) to write the partition function as

\displaystyle Z(\theta)

\displaystyle=(Z_{0}-\hat{Z}[0])+\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\int{\rm d}x\,e^{2\pi{\mathsf{i}}nx}\hat{Z}[E_{0},\bar{\theta}]

\displaystyle=(Z_{0}-\hat{Z}[0])+\frac{{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})}{2\pi}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\int{\rm d}E_{0}{\rm d}D_{0}\,\exp\left(-N\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}[E_{0},D_{0},\bar{\theta}_{n}]\right),

(3.27)

where
\bar{\theta}_{n}=\bar{\theta}+2\pi n/N
was defined in (
2.13
).
Thus, we have reduced the path integral to the integral over just two variables
E_{0}
and
D_{0}
.

Let us briefly comment on
Z_{0}
and
\hat{Z}[0]
. The
Z_{0}
is (approximately) obtained by the following procedure. To get some intuition, we consider the case that
L_{1}=\beta=1/T
is interpreted as an inverse temperature and
L_{2}=L
is a spatial volume which we take to be much larger than
\beta
and the mass scale
M^{-1}=D_{0}^{-1/2}
of
\phi
. In this limit, only the holonomy
\mu=\int_{0}^{\beta}A_{x}{\rm d}x
in the Euclidean time direction
x
is important. The path integral of
\phi
gives

\displaystyle N\log\Det(-D_{\mu}D^{\mu}+D)=\beta LF(\mu),

(3.28)

where
F(\mu)
is the thermal free energy of
\phi
in the presence of the (imaginary) chemical potential
\mu
. At the leading order, the
Z_{0}
is given by

\displaystyle Z_{0}\sim\int{\rm d}\mu\,e^{-\beta LF(\mu)}\sim e^{-\beta LF(0)}.

(3.29)

The
\phi
has mass
M
and hence the thermal effects are exponentially suppressed by the Boltzmann factor
\exp(-\beta M)
(which is the same as the exponentiated action (
3.15
) of the
\phi
-particle).

On the other hand, the
\hat{Z}[0]
is computed by
\bar{S}_{\text{eff}}[E_{0},D_{0},\bar{\theta}]
in the limit
E_{0}\to 0
, which one can see to be the zero temperature effective action of the
\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}
model for
D_{0}
. Thus,
Z_{0}
and
\hat{Z}[0]
are different when the temperature is of order
M
or higher, but the difference goes to zero in the limit of large
L_{1}=\beta
and
L_{2}=L
.

Our concern in this paper is the
\bar{\theta}
dependence, and for that purpose the term
(Z_{0}-\hat{Z}[0])
is not important. We have also taken
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
to be large to justify the approximation used in (
3.24
). Therefore, at any rate, we neglect
(Z_{0}-\hat{Z}[0])
and then our formula for the partition function is

\displaystyle Z(\theta)\simeq\frac{{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})}{2\pi}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\int{\rm d}E_{0}{\rm d}D_{0}\,\exp\left(-\frac{N{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})}{4\pi}\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(E_{0},D_{0},\bar{\theta}_{n})\right).

(3.30)

The integration contours of
E_{0}
and
D_{0}
are as follows. It is clear that the
E_{0}
is integrated over
{\mathbb{R}}
. On the other hand, the
D
was originally introduced as a Lagrange multiplier to impose the condition

|\phi|^{2}=1
. To produce the delta functional
\delta(|\phi|^{2}-1)
by integrating over
D
using the action (
2.1
), its integration contour should be pure imaginary, and hence
D_{0}
is integrated over the imaginary axis
\sqrt{-1}\,{\mathbb{R}}
. We may slightly shift it to
\epsilon+\sqrt{-1}\,{\mathbb{R}}
for
\epsilon>0
to avoid the singular point
D_{0}=0
.

3.4
Calculations of the effective Lagrangian

The effective Lagrangian (
3.24
) can be calculated more explicitly. To perform renormalization, we first rewrite

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t}\frac{E_{0}e^{-tD_{0}}}{\sinh E_{0}t}=\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t}\left(\frac{E_{0}}{\sinh E_{0}t}-\frac{1}{t}\right)e^{-tD_{0}}+\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\frac{e^{-tD_{0}}}{t^{2}}

(3.31)

The first term on the right-hand side is finite in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
. On the other hand, the second term can be treated just as in the usual case of the
\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}
model without
E_{0}
on a flat space.
6
6

6

Notice that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\frac{e^{-tD_{0}}}{t^{2}}=\int\frac{{\rm d}^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t}e^{-t(p^{2}+D_{0})}=-\int\frac{{\rm d}^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}\log(p^{2}+D_{0}).

(3.32)

This is just the flat space one-loop determinant.

Because

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}D_{0}^{2}}\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\frac{e^{-tD_{0}}}{t^{2}}=\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}{\rm d}te^{-tD_{0}}=\frac{e^{-\varepsilon D_{0}}}{D_{0}}=\frac{1}{D_{0}}+{\cal O}(\varepsilon),

(3.33)

we have

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\frac{e^{-tD_{0}}}{t^{2}}=c_{0}+c_{1}D_{0}+D_{0}\log D_{0}+{\cal O}(\varepsilon),

(3.34)

where
c_{0}(=1/\varepsilon)
and
c_{1}(=\log\varepsilon+\cdots)
are constants which diverge in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
. The
c_{0}
is renormalized by the cosmological constant term and hence we neglect it. We renormalize
c_{1}
as

\displaystyle c_{1}+\frac{4\pi}{\bar{g}_{0}^{2}}:=-1-\log\Lambda^{2},

(3.35)

and take
\Lambda
to be fixed in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
. This
\Lambda
is the dynamical scale of the
\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}
model.
By using this renormalization in (
3.24
), the renormalized effective Lagrangian is given by

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(E_{0},D_{0},\bar{\theta})=-D_{0}\log(D_{0}/\Lambda^{2})+D_{0}+2{\mathsf{i}}\bar{\theta}E_{0}-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t}\left(\frac{E_{0}}{\sinh E_{0}t}-\frac{1}{t}\right)e^{-tD_{0}}\,.

(3.36)

The term
-D_{0}\log(D_{0}/\Lambda^{2})+D_{0}
is the usual one in the
\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}
model, and the rest of the terms gives the
E_{0}
-dependence.

Next we calculate the last term of (
3.36
) more explicitly. We define a function
F(z)
by

\displaystyle F(z)

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{z}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\sinh t}-\frac{1}{t}\right)\frac{e^{-zt}}{t}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{z}\log\Gamma\left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right)+\log\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)-1+\frac{1}{z}\log 2\pi,

(3.37)

where in the second equality we have used a formula (
B.1
) given in Appendix
B
.
By setting

\displaystyle z=\frac{D_{0}}{E_{0}},

(3.38)

the final result for the effective Lagrangian when
E_{0}>0
is given by

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(E_{0},D_{0},\bar{\theta})=-D_{0}\log\left(\frac{D_{0}}{\Lambda^{2}}\right)+D_{0}\left(1+\frac{2{\mathsf{i}}\bar{\theta}}{z}+F(z)\right)\qquad(E_{0}>0).

(3.39)

More explicit form is

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(E_{0},D_{0},\bar{\theta})=-2E_{0}\log\Gamma\left(\frac{D_{0}}{2E_{0}}+\frac{1}{2}\right)-D_{0}\log\left(\frac{2E_{0}}{\Lambda^{2}}\right)+E_{0}\log 2\pi+2{\mathsf{i}}\bar{\theta}E_{0}.

(3.40)

The formula for negative real
E_{0}
is given by replacing
E_{0}
by
|E_{0}|
.

The above formulas in terms of the gamma function somewhat make obscure the behavior in the region of small
E_{0}
. It is possible to expand the effective Lagrangian around
E_{0}=0
by using the Bernoulli numbers
B_{n}
which are defined by

\displaystyle\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{n}}{n!}x^{n}.

(3.41)

We have

\displaystyle\frac{x}{\sinh x}=2x\left(\frac{1}{e^{x}-1}-\frac{1}{e^{2x}-1}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2-2^{2n})B_{2n}}{(2n)!}x^{2n},

(3.42)

where we have used the fact that the left-hand side is an even function of
x
. Then (
3.36
) gives

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(E_{0},D_{0},\bar{\theta})=-D_{0}\left(\log\frac{D_{0}}{\Lambda^{2}}-1\right)+2{\mathsf{i}}\bar{\theta}E_{0}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2-2^{2n})}{2n(2n-1)}\,B_{2n}\,\frac{E_{0}^{2n}}{D_{0}^{2n-1}}\,.

(3.43)

The expansion with respect to
E_{0}
is only an asymptotic expansion. In fact, the original integral (
3.36
) can be seen as a Borel resummation of this asymptotic series by rewriting it as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t}\left(\frac{E_{0}}{\sinh E_{0}t}-\frac{1}{t}\right)e^{-tD_{0}}=E_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t}\left(\frac{1}{\sinh t}-\frac{1}{t}\right)e^{-t\frac{D_{0}}{E_{0}}}

(3.44)

where we have assumed
E_{0}>0
. The function
1/\sinh t
has poles at
\pi{\mathsf{i}}k
for
k=\pm 1,\pm 2,\cdots
. Thus the radius of convergence of the expansion of
1/\sinh t
is
\pi
, which implies that the expansion before the Borel resummation has zero radius of convergence.

We can check that the results of this section are consistent with the previous studies
(
Riva:1980wq
;
DAdda:1982lsk
;
Aguado:2010ex
;
Rossi:2016uce
)
.
The effective action (
3.24
) in the present paper is equal to eq. (3.4) in
(
Riva:1980wq
)
and eq. (3.5) in
(
DAdda:1982lsk
)
up to the term derived from the fermionic superpartners of the bosonic fields.
Eq. (32) in
(
Rossi:2016uce
)
is equal to our result (
3.40
) because
A
and
B
in
(
Rossi:2016uce
)
are interpreted as

and

respectively in our case.
In
(

)
, the instanton number

and the constant saddle point of the scalar field

correspond to

Thus our result is equivalent to eq. (41) in
(

by replacing

.

.

4
The theta angle and the partition function

Just for notational simplicity, from now on we denote
E_{0}
and
D_{0}
just as
E
and
D
,

\displaystyle E:=E_{0},\qquad D:=D_{0}.

(4.1)

Our purpose now is to compute the partition function (
3.30
). For this purpose, we need to evaluate the integral

\displaystyle{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta})=\int{\rm d}E{\rm d}D\,\exp\left(-\frac{N{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})}{4\pi}\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(E,D,\bar{\theta})\right),

(4.2)

where we remind the reader that
\bar{\theta}=\theta/N
.
Then we replace
\bar{\theta}
by
\bar{\theta}_{n}=\bar{\theta}+2\pi n/N
and sum over
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
to get (
3.30
).

4.1
The assumption about the saddle point method

For the purpose of computing
{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta})
defined by (
4.2
), we make a further
assumption
that it can be computed by the saddle point method. The saddle point method is a very common method in large
N
field theories, but actually there is a subtlety.

A precise framework for the saddle point method is Morse or Picard-Lefschetz theory (see Section 3 of
Witten:2010cx
for a detailed review of this theory). An important ingredient in this method is the ability to deform the integration contour freely so that the integral is represented by a sum of integrals over some integration contours (called Lefschetz thimbles) which pass through saddle points.

In more detail, let us extend the variables
E
and
D
to complex values,
E,D\in{\mathbb{C}}
. The
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(E,D,\bar{\theta})
is now regarded as a function of these complex variables.
Suppose that
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(E,D,\bar{\theta})
has (complex) saddle points
p_{a}
labeled by
a
,
p_{a}=(E_{a},D_{a})
. Let
{\cal J}_{a}
be the appropriate integration contour (Lefschetz thimble) which has the following properties; (i)
{\cal J}_{a}
passes through
p_{a}
, (ii) the real part
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(E,D,\bar{\theta})
increases as we go away from
p_{a}
along
{\cal J}_{a}
, and (iii) the imaginary part
\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(E,D,\bar{\theta})
is constant along
{\cal J}_{a}
. Let
{\cal C}
be the original integration contour. Suppose that
{\cal C}
can be deformed to (or is in the same “homology class” as) a sum

\displaystyle{\cal C}\to\sum_{a}{\mathfrak{n}}_{a}{\cal J}_{a},

(4.3)

where
{\mathfrak{n}}_{a}
are integers. Then we may want to replace the integral (
4.2
) over
{\cal C}
as

\displaystyle\int_{\cal C}{\rm d}E{\rm d}D\,\exp\left(-\frac{N{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})}{4\pi}\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(E,D,\bar{\theta})\right)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{~~{?}~~}\sum_{a}{\mathfrak{n}}_{a}\int_{{\cal J}_{a}}{\rm d}E{\rm d}D\,\exp\left(-\frac{N{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})}{4\pi}\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(E,D,\bar{\theta})\right).

(4.4)

The integral over each
{\cal J}_{a}
is dominated by the saddle point value of the integrand. This is the saddle point method.

This procedure is valid as far as the integrand is a holomorphic function of the integration variables
(E,D)
, because in that case we can use the Cauchy theorem to deform the integration contour without changing the value of the integral. However, as we have noticed in the paragraph containing (
3.43
), the
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(E,D,\bar{\theta})
is not analytic at
E=0
because the radius of convergence of the expansion around this point is zero. The integration contour
{\cal C}
(discussed at the end of Section
3.3
) passes through
E=0
. Therefore, it is not clear whether the decomposition such as (
4.4
) is possible.

In this paper, we neglect the above issue and simply
assume
the following:

•

The result for
{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta})
is given by a sum that appears on the right-hand side of (
4.4
), for some choice of integers
{\mathfrak{n}}_{a}
. (Any specific choice of
{\mathfrak{n}}_{a}
is not assumed.)

We will see that this assumption (with a certain choice of
{\mathfrak{n}}_{a}
) gives perfectly sensible results when
\bar{\theta}
is small (numerically of order
0.1
). However, when
\bar{\theta}
becomes larger (numerically of order
1
), then we will encounter a puzzling behavior. The puzzle will be that the values of
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(E_{a},D_{a},\bar{\theta})
become negative and hence give unphysically huge contributions. This is not about a single saddle point but about many saddle points, and the problem might not be solved no matter how we choose
{\mathfrak{n}}_{a}
(this point is subtle because there are infinitely many saddle points. We discuss more details later).

We leave the study of the resolution of the puzzle to future work. In the present work, we just give the results under the above assumptions.

4.2
Small
\bar{\theta}

Let us start from the case of small
\bar{\theta}
because this case has a very clear physical interpretation in terms of metastable vacua.
For small
\bar{\theta}
, we expect that small
E
gives the dominant saddle point, because when
\bar{\theta}=0
, the (true) vacuum is given by
E=0
.

For small
E
, the effective Lagrangian is expanded as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}(E,D,\bar{\theta})\simeq 2{\mathsf{i}}\bar{\theta}E-D\left(\log\frac{D}{\Lambda^{2}}-1\right)+\frac{1}{6}\frac{E^{2}}{D}.

(4.5)

Assuming that
\bar{\theta}
is small, the saddle point is given by

\displaystyle D\simeq\Lambda^{2}+6\Lambda^{2}\bar{\theta}^{2},\qquad E\simeq-6{\mathsf{i}}\Lambda^{2}\bar{\theta},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}|_{\rm saddle}\simeq\Lambda^{2}+6\Lambda^{2}\bar{\theta}^{2}.

(4.6)

Let us discuss the interpretation of each of them.

First, the leading term
\Lambda^{2}
in
D
is the standard result for the
\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}
model at
\bar{\theta}=0
. This gives the mass term of
\phi
. The result
D=\Lambda^{2}
for
\bar{\theta}=0
is slightly modified by
6\Lambda^{2}\bar{\theta}^{2}
for a small but finite
\bar{\theta}
.

Next, let us consider
E\simeq-6{\mathsf{i}}\Lambda\bar{\theta}
. This is (almost) pure imaginary, and it has the following natural interpretation. The
E
was defined by the gauge field strength
-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}
in Euclidean signature spacetime. We have to Wick-rotate it to obtain the corresponding Lorentz signature result. The Wick rotation is performed in the following way. The action contains the
\theta
term

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathsf{i}}\theta}{2\pi}\int E{\rm d}^{2}x.

(4.7)

We require that this form is preserved under Wick rotation.
7
7

7

We can write
\int E{\rm d}^{2}x
in terms of differential form notation as
-\int F
. This does not depend on the metric. It is possible to regard Wick rotation as a change of the metric
g_{\mu\nu}
(see
Kontsevich:2021dmb
for a systematic study of this point of view), so Wick rotation should not affect terms that are independent of the metric.

Let
x_{\rm E}
and
E_{\rm E}=E
be the Euclidean signature coordinates and the field strength, and let
x_{\rm L}
and
E_{\rm L}
be the Lorentz signature coordinates and the field strength, respectively. Then the requirement is that
E_{\rm E}\,{\rm d}^{2}x_{\rm E}=E_{\rm L}\,{\rm d}^{2}x_{\rm L}
. We have the standard relation
{\rm d}^{2}x_{\rm L}=-{\mathsf{i}}{\rm d}^{2}x_{\rm E}
, and hence

\displaystyle E_{\rm L}={\mathsf{i}}E\simeq 6\Lambda^{2}\bar{\theta}.

(4.8)

This is a real electric field. It is expected that such a constant electric field is generated in a 2d
\mathrm{U}(1)
gauge theory with a
\theta
-term
Coleman:1976uz
.

Finally, the result
\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}|_{\rm saddle}-\Lambda^{2}\simeq 6\Lambda^{2}\bar{\theta}^{2}
is interpreted in terms of the vacuum energy. From (
2.6
), the vacuum energy and the vacuum decay rate is given at the leading order of large
N
by

\displaystyle V(\theta)=\frac{N}{4\pi}\bar{V}(\bar{\theta}),\qquad\Gamma(\theta)=\frac{N}{4\pi}\bar{\Gamma}(\bar{\theta})

(4.9)

where

\displaystyle\bar{V}(\bar{\theta})-\frac{{\mathsf{i}}}{2}\bar{\Gamma}(\bar{\theta})=\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}|_{\rm saddle}.

(4.10)

Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\bar{V}(\bar{\theta})-\bar{V}(0)\simeq 6N\Lambda^{2}\bar{\theta}^{2},

(4.11)

where we have subtracted the term
\bar{V}(0)
which is independent of
\bar{\theta}
.
This is a natural result that the electric field
E_{\rm L}
creates energy.

In the above approximation, we could not see the imaginary part of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}|_{\rm saddle}
. We can determine the leading term of the small imaginary part as follows. We perform the above Wick rotation as
E=\exp[-{\mathsf{i}}(\pi/2-\epsilon)]E_{L}=(-{\mathsf{i}}+\epsilon)E_{\rm L}
, where
\epsilon>0
is an infinitesimal number which is introduced so that the Wick rotation angle is slightly less than
\pi/2
to avoid singularities in the following calculation.
8
8

8

This is analogous to the “Feynman
{\mathsf{i}}\epsilon
” in Feynman diagram computations. Our electric field
E
is analogous to the time component of momenta in Feynman diagrams.

Then, the part of the effective action that depends on
E
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle\supset-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t}\left(\frac{E}{\sinh Et}-\frac{1}{t}\right)e^{-tD}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t}\left(\frac{E_{\rm L}}{\sin(1+{\mathsf{i}}\epsilon)E_{\rm L}t}-\frac{1}{t}\right)e^{-tD}.

(4.12)

When
E_{\rm L}
is real (and
\epsilon
is infinitesimal), there are many poles on the integration contour of
t
, located at

\displaystyle t=\frac{\pi}{|E_{\rm L}|}k\qquad(k=1,2,\cdots).

(4.13)

Near these poles, we have

\displaystyle\frac{E_{\rm L}}{\sin(1+{\mathsf{i}}\epsilon)E_{\rm L}t}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(1+{\mathsf{i}}\epsilon)t-\pi k/|E_{\rm L}|}

\displaystyle={\rm P}\left(\frac{(-1)^{k}}{t-\pi k/|E_{\rm L}|}\right)-(-1)^{k}\pi{\mathsf{i}}\delta(t-\pi k/|E_{\rm L}|),

(4.14)

where
{\rm P}
means the principal value and we have used
(x+{\mathsf{i}}\epsilon)^{-1}={\rm P}(x^{-1})-\pi{\mathsf{i}}\delta(x)
. Therefore, for real
E_{\rm L}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\frac{|E_{\rm L}|}{k}\exp\left(-\frac{k\pi D}{|E_{\rm L}|}\right).

(4.15)

Therefore, when
|E_{\rm L}|\ll D
, the vacuum decay rate is given by

\displaystyle\bar{\Gamma}(\bar{\theta})\simeq 2|E_{L}|\exp\left(-\frac{\pi D}{|E_{\rm L}|}\right)\simeq 12\Lambda^{2}|\bar{\theta}|\exp\left(-\frac{\pi}{6|\bar{\theta}|}\right).

(4.16)

This is positive, as expected from the physical interpretation as the vacuum decay rate. In fact, if the Wick rotation were done in the wrong direction, then
\bar{\Gamma}(\bar{\theta})
would have been negative. The correct sign is a consistency check of our procedure for the Wick rotation discussed around (
4.7
).

The vacuum decay rate is exponentially small for small
\bar{\theta}
, and hence we can interpret the saddle point (
4.6
) as a sufficiently long-lived metastable vacuum.
The result (
4.16
) is interpreted as a consequence of the Schwinger effect; pairs of charged particles
\phi,\phi^{\dagger}
are created in the presence of the electric field
E_{\rm L}
. The vacuum decay proceeds via such a Schwinger pair creation. In fact, in Appendix
A.3
we give a different computation of the exponent
-\pi D/|E_{L}|
in (
4.16
) by using a particle picture of
\phi
. The computation there makes the interpretation as pair creations more explicit.

4.3
Analytic structure of the effective Lagrangian

For general values of
\bar{\theta}
, we perform the computation by using the result (
3.39
) or (
3.40
). If we regard
D
and
z=D/E
as independent variables, it is easy to solve the saddle point equation for
D
by using (
3.39
). The result for the value of
D
and the effective Lagrangian
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(z,\bar{\theta})
as a function of
z
is

\displaystyle D=D(z,\bar{\theta}):=\Lambda^{2}\exp\left(\frac{2{\mathsf{i}}\bar{\theta}}{z}+F(z)\right),

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(z,\bar{\theta})=D(z,\bar{\theta}),

(4.17)

where
F(z)
is given by (
3.37
),

\displaystyle F(z)=-\frac{2}{z}\log\Gamma\left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right)+\log\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)-1+\frac{1}{z}\log 2\pi.

(4.18)

Our remaining task is to find saddle points of
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(z,\bar{\theta})
. However, we must notice that this effective Lagrangian is not single valued for complex
z
. Moreover, the formula (
3.39
) is derived under the condition that
E>0
. Therefore, it is necessary to be more careful about the analytic structure.

Let us reexamine the case of small
\bar{\theta}
. The effective action was obtained for real
E
, but the actual saddle point for small
\bar{\theta}
was approximately pure imaginary. In particular, to get the correct vacuum decay rate, we have implicitly used the following analytic continuation. We start from some real value of
E
or
z=D/E
, say
z=A\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and then perform Wick rotation as

\displaystyle z=Ae^{{\mathsf{i}}\alpha},

(4.19)

where
\alpha
is increased from
0
to
\pi/2
.
The sign of
A
is taken to be the same as the sign of the saddle point value of
z_{\rm L}:=D/E_{\rm L}
.
For concreteness, we focus our attention to the case
\bar{\theta}>0
which gives
E_{\rm L}\simeq 6\Lambda^{2}\bar{\theta}>0
. Then we take the
A
in (
4.19
) to be positive. This means that we start the analytic continuation from positive
E
. Therefore we can use the formula (
3.39
). For
z>0
, the logarithm in (
4.18
) is evaluated by the principal value. Then we analytically continue
F(z)
along the path (
4.19
) for
A>0
.

Now we want to increase
\bar{\theta}
from small to large values. Let us distinguish several different types of saddle points as follows:

1.

The saddle point that is a continuation of the saddle point found in Section
4.2
from small to large values of
\bar{\theta}
.

2.

Saddle points that are not the above one, but can be still found by analytic continuation (
4.19
) in the range
0<\alpha<\pi
and
A>0
.

3.

Saddle points that can be still found by analytic continuation (
4.19
) in the range
-\pi<\alpha<0
and
A>0
.

4.

Saddle points obtained by more complicated analytic continuation.

5.

Saddle points that are obtained by analytic continuation from negative real
z
.

Let us comment on each of them.

The first type is the most natural one because, for small
\bar{\theta}
, it has perfectly sensible behavior as a metastable vacuum. For large
\bar{\theta}
, however, we will see that the interpretation as a metastable vacuum is no longer valid. We will also see that the
\alpha
in (
4.19
) must be continued to the range
\pi/2<\alpha<\pi
. For small
\bar{\theta}
it was
\alpha\simeq\pi/2
.

As we will see, there are also other saddle points. For the second type of saddle points, the analytic continuation is done in the same direction as the Wick rotation,
\alpha>0
. For the third type of saddle points, the analytic continuation is done in the opposite direction to the case of Wick rotation,
\alpha<0
. The saddle points of the third type (and the values of
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(z,\bar{\theta})
on them) are obtained from the saddle points of the first or second types by complex conjugation
z\to z^{*}
.

About the fourth type, a practical reason for distinguishing it from the first, second and third ones is as follows. The “LogGamma function”
\log\Gamma(x)
has a unique analytic continuation if we eliminate the negative real axis on the complex plane. The gamma function is basically a product of
(x+k)^{-1}
for
k=0,1,2,\cdots
, or more precisely,

\displaystyle\Gamma(x)^{-1}=e^{\gamma x}x\prod_{k\geq 1}\left(1+\frac{x}{k}\right)e^{-x/k},

(4.20)

where
\gamma
is the Euler’s constant.
For the function (
4.18
), if we go around some of the points of the form

\displaystyle z=-2k-1\qquad(k=0,1,2\cdots),

(4.21)

then the logarithm changes by
-2\pi\ell{\mathsf{i}}
for some
\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and hence
F(z)
changes as

\displaystyle F(z)\to F(z)-\frac{2}{z}\cdot 2\pi\ell{\mathsf{i}}.

(4.22)

By looking at (
4.17
), this shift of
F(z)
has the effect of replacing
\bar{\theta}
by
\bar{\theta}-2\pi\ell
,

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(z,\bar{\theta})\to\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}(z,\bar{\theta}-2\pi\ell).

(4.23)

Therefore, saddle points of the fourth type with the parameter
\bar{\theta}
can be found from saddle points of the first, second or third type for the parameter
\bar{\theta}-2\pi\ell
.
9
9

9

It is not yet clear whether the “
2\pi
-periodicity of
\bar{\theta}
” or in other words the “
2\pi N
-periodicity of
\theta
” as in (
4.23
) has a physical interpretation. At least it is consistent with the ’t Hooft anomaly of the
2\pi
-periodicity of
\theta

Cordova:2019jnf
;
Cordova:2019uob
(see also
Gaiotto:2017yup
;
Kikuchi:2017pcp
for early work), since
2\pi N
shift does not have any ’t Hooft anomaly.

The saddle points of the fifth type are obtained from others just by changing the signs of both
z
and
\bar{\theta}
. Therefore we will not explicitly show them in the following sections.

4.5
Approximate solutions for large
\bar{\theta}

In the large
\bar{\theta}

z

4.21
) and let us write

\displaystyle z=-(2k+1)+2{\mathsf{i}}\xi,

\xi
is small.
By analytic continuation (
4.19
) with
0<\alpha<\pi
(and
A>0
), we get

\displaystyle\log\Gamma\left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\Gamma\left(-k+{\mathsf{i}}\xi\right)\simeq\log\left(\frac{e^{-{\mathsf{i}}\pi k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{1}{{\mathsf{i}}\xi}\right)

\displaystyle=-\pi{\mathsf{i}}(k+1/2)-\log\xi-\log k!,

(4.29)

where we have used
\Gamma(x+k)=(x+k-1)(x+k-2)\cdots x\Gamma(x)

\xi

\bar{\theta}\xi

\frac{2{\mathsf{i}}\bar{\theta}}{z}+F(z)\simeq-\frac{{\mathsf{i}}\bar{\theta}}{k+1/2}\left(1+\frac{{\mathsf{i}}\xi}{k+1/2}\right)-\frac{1}{k+1/2}\left(\pi{\mathsf{i}}(k+1/2)+\log\xi+\log k!\right)\\
+\log(k+1/2)+{\mathsf{i}}\pi-1-\frac{1}{2k+1}\log 2\pi+{\cal O}(\xi).

\displaystyle\xi\simeq\frac{k+1/2}{\bar{\theta}}.

\displaystyle\frac{2{\mathsf{i}}\bar{\theta}}{z}+F(z)\simeq\frac{1}{k+1/2}\left(-{\mathsf{i}}\bar{\theta}+\log\bar{\theta}\right)+C,

5
A problem about saddle points with huge contributions

In the previous section, we have found that the real part of the effective Lagrangian normalized as
L(\bar{\theta})=(\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{eff}}|_{\rm saddle}-\Lambda^{2})/\Lambda^{2}
becomes negative for some values of
\bar{\theta}
. Now we discuss a puzzle about this behavior.

5.1
The puzzle

Modulo some overall factor that is irrelevant for our purposes, the partition function (
3.30
) is given by

\displaystyle Z(\theta)\propto\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta}_{n}),\qquad\bar{\theta}_{n}=\bar{\theta}+\frac{2\pi n}{N},

(5.1)

where
{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta})
is defined by (
4.2
). Because we sum over
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, the
\theta_{n}
takes various values, some of which are large.

We have also assumed that the
{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta})
can be computed by a sum of integrals over integration contours
{\cal J}_{a}
(Lefschetz thimbles) as in (
4.4
). Let
L_{a}(\bar{\theta})
be the value of
L(\bar{\theta})
at the saddle point labeled by
a
, and let
{\cal Z}^{\rm loop}_{a}
be the result of integration over
{\cal J}_{a}
(without the saddle point action
L_{a}(\bar{\theta})
). Then, modulo some irrelevant overall factor, we have

\displaystyle{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta})\propto\sum_{a}{\mathfrak{n}}_{a}{\cal Z}^{\rm loop}_{a}\exp\left(-\frac{N{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})\Lambda^{2}}{4\pi}L_{a}(\bar{\theta})\right),

(5.2)

where the integers
{\mathfrak{n}}_{a}
are not yet known. We remark that
{\cal J}_{a}
and
{\mathfrak{n}}_{a}
in general depend on
\bar{\theta}
, and may be written more precisely as
{\cal J}_{a}(\bar{\theta})
and
{\mathfrak{n}}_{a}(\bar{\theta})
. For notational simplicity, we omit writing the dependence on
\bar{\theta}
.

In our case, one representation of the label
a
consists of two integers
k,\ell
and a sign
o=\pm
as follows.
In Section
4.4
, we have discussed “rings” that appear in Figure
1
. Then the integer
k
specifies the
(k+1)
-th ring. For instance, Figure
2
is the result for
k=0
, and Figure
3
is the result for
k=1
(left figure) and
k=2
(right figure). The sign
o=\pm
specifies whether the analytic continuation (
4.19
) is done in the direction
0<\alpha<\pi

We have not determined the integers

). First let us consider the case that for

There are infinitely many saddle points and it is not possible to enumerate all of them. But we can study the cases that either

We have checked the behavior for large values of

For large and negative

In any case, as

5.2
Discussions

The resolution of the puzzle described above is not yet found. Here we would like to discuss some possibilities.

The validity of the saddle point method?

The precise treatment of the saddle point method (i.e. Morse or Picard-Lefschetz theory) assumes that the action can be extended to a holomorphic function of integration variables. As already mentioned in Section
4.1
, our effective action is not holomorphic at
E=0
and hence the validity of the saddle point method is questionable. We leave more detailed studies of this point for future work. If this is the resolution of the puzzle, our case gives an example in which the saddle point method is not applicable. Given the fact that the saddle point method is very commonly used in large
N
field theories, we may gain new insights about large
N
field theories from more studies of our example.

Nonzero momentum Fourier modes?

In the discussions around (
3.13
) as well as in Appendix
A.2
, we have discussed that the
A^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
consisting of nonzero momentum Fourier modes on
T^{2}
may have nonzero expectation values. The expectation values are exponentially small as
\exp(-\sqrt{D}L_{1}),~\exp(-\sqrt{D}L_{2})
where
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are the lengths of the sides of
T^{2}
. These exponentials are small as far as the real part
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\sqrt{D}
is positive. The equation (
4.17
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{D}{\Lambda^{2}}=L(\bar{\theta})+1.

(5.6)

From the results given in Figure
2
(and
3
and
4
), we see that
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\sqrt{D}
becomes negative when
\bar{\theta}
is increased.
In that region, our assumption of neglecting the expectation values of
A^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
may not be valid. However, we see from the figures that there are values of
\bar{\theta}
such that we have both
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}L(\bar{\theta})<0
and
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\sqrt{D}>0
. For instance, in Figure
2
,
\bar{\theta}\sim 1.5
is such a value.

Even when
\exp(-\sqrt{D}L_{1}),~\exp(-\sqrt{D}L_{2})
are negligible, we have also assumed that the saddle points at which
A^{\prime}\sim 0
and
D^{\prime}\sim 0
are the most dominant ones. Logically there is a possibility that these saddle points do not contribute to the path integral, and instead, some saddle points that are not translationally invariant may contribute. However, recall that at least when
\bar{\theta}_{n}
is small, we have a clear picture of metastable vacua for the saddle points with
A^{\prime}\sim 0
and
D^{\prime}\sim 0
.

Stokes phenomena with complicated analytic continuation?

As mentioned in Section
5.1
, the situation is complicated due to the existence of infinitely many saddle points. We have already argued that simple Stokes phenomena between saddle points
(0,0,+)
and
(k,\ell,o)
for small
k
and
|\ell|
do not resolve the problem even if they really happen. However, we cannot exclude the logical possibility that some saddle points that are obtained by complicated analytic continuation (i.e., large
|\ell|
and
k
) are relevant. We leave more explicit study of Stokes phenomena for future work.

Cancellation in the sum over
n
?

To obtain the final result for the partition function, we need to sum over all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
as in (
5.1
). Because of nonzero imaginary part
\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}L(\bar{\theta})
, the phase of
{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta})
is rapidly oscillating and there is a possibility that significant cancellation happens in the sum over
n
.

For simplicity, we neglect several issues discussed above, such as Stokes phenomena.
When
n
is changed to
n+1
, the exponent of the
{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta})
in (
5.2
) changes as

\displaystyle\frac{N{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})\Lambda^{2}}{4\pi}\left(L_{a}(\bar{\theta}_{n+1})-L_{a}(\bar{\theta}_{n})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})\Lambda^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial L_{a}}{\partial\bar{\theta}}(\bar{\theta}_{n})+\frac{\pi{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})\Lambda^{2}}{2N}\frac{\partial^{2}L_{a}}{\partial\bar{\theta}^{2}}(\bar{\theta}_{n})+{\cal O}(N^{-2}).

(5.7)

Let us focus on the real part.
We have assumed that
{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})\Lambda^{2}\gg 1
. If the first derivative
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(\partial L_{a}/\partial\bar{\theta})
is nonzero and of order one, the difference of the real part of the action between
n
and
n+1
is very large. Then the term with lower value of the real part of the action dominates. It is difficult to have a cancellation between
n
and
n+1
because the absolute values of
{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta}_{n})
and
{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta}_{n+1})
are very different (say
|{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta}_{n})|\gg|{\cal Z}(\bar{\theta}_{n+1})|
when
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(\partial L_{a}/\partial\bar{\theta})>0
).
On the other hand, when
\bar{\theta}_{n}
is such that
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(\partial L_{a}/\partial\bar{\theta})\sim 0
, the difference of the real part of the action is still large if
{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})\Lambda^{2}\gg N
and
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(\partial^{2}L_{a}/\partial\bar{\theta}^{2})\neq 0
. (A related point has been discussed in
Aguado:2010ex
in the case that
\bar{\theta}_{n}\sim 0
.)

We did not make any assumption about the ratio
{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})\Lambda^{2}/N
, so it is allowed to take a large
{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})
such that
{\rm Vol\,}(T^{2})\Lambda^{2}\gg N
.
In this limit, it seems difficult to have a cancellation in the sum over
n
. It would be interesting to study whether there is a loophole in this argument.

\displaystyle D^{\prime}=\frac{1}{L_{1}L_{2}}\sum_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}D_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}\exp\left[2\pi{\mathsf{i}}\left(\frac{\ell_{1}x}{L_{1}}+\frac{\ell_{2}y}{L_{2}}\right)\right].

(A.17)

The
A^{\prime}
is decomposed in the same way.
We can also consider more general topological classes of worldlines of the form

\displaystyle\{(k_{1}L_{1}\tau,k_{2}L_{2}\tau)~|~\tau\in[0,1]\},

(A.18)

where
(k_{1},k_{2})
are integers.
Then, the effective action contains terms proportional to

\displaystyle D_{(-mk_{2},mk_{1})}\exp\left[-\sqrt{D_{0}(k_{1}^{2}L_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}L_{2}^{2})}+2\pi{\mathsf{i}}(k_{1}\alpha+k_{2}\beta)\right],

(A.19)

and also similar terms for
A^{\prime}
.

Momentum conservation does not forbid quadratic and higher order terms in
A^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
as in (
3.14
) even if
A^{(m)}
is absent. For notational simplicity let us only consider
D^{\prime}
. Then the effective action contains terms of the schematic form

\bar{S}_{\rm eff}\supset cD_{(-mk_{2},mk_{1})}\exp\left(-\sqrt{D_{0}}L+2\pi{\mathsf{i}}{\cal A}\right)+\bar{c}D_{(mk_{2},-mk_{1})}\exp\left(-\sqrt{D_{0}}L-2\pi{\mathsf{i}}{\cal A}\right)\\
+dD_{(-mk_{2},mk_{1})}D_{(mk_{2},-mk_{1})}+\cdots

(A.20)

where
L=\sqrt{(k_{1}^{2}L_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}L_{2}^{2})}
,
{\cal A}=k_{1}\alpha+k_{2}\beta
, and
c
,
\bar{c}
and
d
are coefficients which are expected to be not exponentially suppressed. Notice that
(k_{1},k_{2})
and
(-k_{1},-k_{2})
give opposite momentum modes.
14
14

14

One can also interpret the particle worldline with
(-k_{1},-k_{2})
as the anti-particle worldline with
(k_{1},k_{2})
.

The saddle point is then given by

\displaystyle D_{(-mk_{2},mk_{1})}\sim-\frac{\bar{c}}{d}\exp\left(-\sqrt{D_{0}}L-2\pi{\mathsf{i}}{\cal A}\right),\qquad\bar{S}_{\rm eff}\supset-\frac{c\bar{c}}{d}\exp\left(-2\sqrt{D_{0}}L\right).

(A.21)

As far as
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\sqrt{D_{0}}>0
, they are exponentially suppressed for large
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
.

A.3
Schwinger effect

Here we give another derivation of the exponent of the vacuum decay rate (
4.16
) by using the particle picture. It is computed by using the standard thin wall approximation (see e.g.
Weinberg:1996kr
for a textbook account). In our case, the thin wall is given by a worldline of the charged particle. The derivation here has the advantage of giving an intuitive picture that the vacuum decay rate is indeed due to pair creations of charged particles, but the result is not as precise as the one given in Section
4.2
.
In the context of the
\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}
model, the same computation has been done in
Lawrence:2012ua
.

We work in Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
(which may be regarded as a large volume limit of
T^{2}
). Then we consider the worldline
{\mathsf{C}}
of a particle surrounding a bounded region
{\mathsf{D}}
. If the electric field
E_{\rm L}={\mathsf{i}}E
is positive, we take the orientation of
{\mathsf{C}}
to be clockwise, i.e.,
\partial{\mathsf{D}}=-{\mathsf{C}}
. It can also be regarded as the worldline of an anti-particle going in the counterclockwise direction
-{\mathsf{C}}
. The action of the particle is given by

\displaystyle-S_{\rm particle}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathsf{C}}M{\rm d}s+{\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathsf{C}}A

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathsf{C}}M{\rm d}s-{\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathsf{D}}F,

(A.22)

where
F={\rm d}A
is the field strength 2-form.

The fact that the particle worldline separates two different (metastable) vacua can be seen as follows. Suppose that the region outside of
{\mathsf{D}}
is in the
n
-th vacuum.
Recall that the
\theta
-angle appears in the combination
\theta+2\pi n
as shown in (
2.5
).
On the other hand, the term
-{\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathsf{D}}F
in
-S_{\rm particle}
means that the
\theta
-angle inside the region
{\mathsf{D}}
is effectively shifted by
-2\pi
,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{i}}\frac{(\theta+2\pi n)}{2\pi}\int F-{\mathsf{i}}\int_{\mathsf{D}}F={\mathsf{i}}\frac{(\theta+2\pi n-2\pi)}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathsf{D}}}F+{\mathsf{i}}\frac{(\theta+2\pi n)}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus{\mathsf{D}}}F.

(A.23)

Therefore, the region inside
{\mathsf{D}}
is in the
(n-1)
-th vacuum. Thus the worldline
{\mathsf{C}}
seperates the
n
-th vacuum and the
(n-1)
-th vacuum.

Let us compute the particle action in the thin wall approximation. As usual, we assume that the bubble solution is spherically symmetric so that the
{\mathsf{C}}
is
S^{1}
with radius
r
and the
{\mathsf{D}}
is the disk surrounded by
S^{1}
. Then, we get

\displaystyle-S_{\rm particle}=-2\pi rM+{\mathsf{i}}\pi r^{2}E=-2\pi r\sqrt{D}+\pi r^{2}E_{\rm L},

(A.24)

where we have used
F_{xy}=-E={\mathsf{i}}E_{\rm L}
and
M=\sqrt{D}
. Extremizing this action, we get

\displaystyle r=\frac{\sqrt{D}}{E_{\rm L}},\qquad-S_{\rm particle}=-\frac{\pi D}{E_{\rm L}}.

(A.25)

The vacuum decay rate is proportional to
\exp(-S_{\rm particle})
.
This action
-S_{\rm particle}=-\pi D/E_{\rm E}
is precisely the exponent of (
4.16
) for the case
E_{\rm L}>0
. When
E_{\rm L}<0
, we instead consider counterclockwise orientation of
{\mathsf{C}}
.

If we look at the worldline
{\mathsf{C}}
from the point of view of time evolution in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, it represents a pair creation of charged particles.
15
15

15

In Euclidean signature, the pair of charged particles is annihilated after the creation. In Lorentz signature, the charged particles go away from each other.
Thus it is indeed the Schwinger effect.

Appendix B
A formula for the gamma function

In this appendix we show the formula

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2\sinh t}-\frac{1}{2t}\right)\frac{e^{-2zt}}{t}{\rm d}t=\log\Gamma(z+1/2)-z\log z+z-\frac{1}{2}\log 2\pi.

(B.1)

Our starting point is the Binet’s first integral formula for the gamma function

\displaystyle\log\Gamma(z)=\left(z-\frac{1}{2}\right)\log z-z+\frac{1}{2}\log 2\pi+\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{t}+\frac{1}{e^{t}-1}\right)\frac{e^{-zt}}{t}{\rm d}t\,.

(B.2)

which hold for
\text{Re}[z]>0
(See Secction 12.3 in
(
Whittaker_Watson_2021
)
).
Note that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{t}+\frac{1}{e^{t}-1}\right)\frac{e^{-zt}}{t}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2\sinh(t/2)}-\frac{1}{t}\right)\frac{e^{-(z+1/2)t}}{t}{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e^{-t/2}}{t}-\frac{1}{t}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{e^{-zt}}{t}{\rm d}t.

(B.3)

Let us define a function
h(z)
by

\displaystyle h(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e^{-t/2}}{t}-\frac{1}{t}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{e^{-zt}}{t}{\rm d}t.

(B.4)

Its second derivative is easily calculated as

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}z^{2}}h(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{-t/2}-1+\frac{1}{2}t\right)e^{-zt}{\rm d}t=\frac{1}{z+1/2}-\frac{1}{z}+\frac{1}{2z^{2}}.

(B.5)

Therefore,

\displaystyle h(z)=(z+1/2)\log(z+1/2)-z\log z-\frac{1}{2}\log z+c^{\prime}_{0}+c^{\prime}_{1}z,

(B.6)

where
c^{\prime}_{0}
and
c^{\prime}_{1}
are constants. These constants are determined by comparing the behavior in the limit
z\to\infty
. The function
h(z)
goes to zero in the limit
z\to\infty
, and hence

\displaystyle h(z)=(z+1/2)\log\left(1+\frac{1}{2z}\right)-\frac{1}{2}.

(B.7)

By using it as well as
\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)
, the formula (
B.2
) gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2\sinh(t/2)}-\frac{1}{t}\right)\frac{e^{-(z+1/2)t}}{t}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\log\Gamma(z+1)-(z+1/2)\log(z+1/2)+(z+1/2)-\frac{1}{2}\log 2\pi.

(B.8)

This implies the desired formula (
B.1
).
