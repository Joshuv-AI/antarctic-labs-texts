Exploring the significant genetic diversity of Iranian isolates of Leishmania RNA virus 2 using whole genome sequence analysis
Authors: Reza Saberi, Homa Hajjaran, Mahdi Fakhar, Zahra Mirabedini, Mehdi Mohebali
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOur work presents the whole genome sequence and phylogenetic analysis of five Leishmania RNA virus 2 (LRV2) isolates obtained from patients with cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) in Iran.MethodsThe whole genome sequencing of LRV2 was performed using a primer walking approach. The resulting sequences were analyzed for genetic and haplotype diversity, highlighting their independent evolution and significant genetic divergence.ResultsThe whole genome sequence of the current LRV2 showed high genetic and haplotype diversity. The study also revealed the existence of three distinct clades of LRV2, with the LRV2 sequences infecting L. major, L. aethiopica, and sauroleishmania belonging to separate lineages. These lineages have seemingly evolved independently, as the geographic distribution of their flagellate hosts does not overlap with the Leishmania species. The divergence between these three clades is attributed to considerable antiquity, leading to genetic modifications within the viruses residing in them and resulting in structural differences in their genome.ConclusionsThese findings contribute to our understanding of the genetic diversity and evolution of LRVs, providing valuable insights into their role in Leishmania infections. Further investigations are needed to understand the significance of these polymorphic sites and their potential impact on viral characteristics and disease outcomes.

Introduction

Leishmania parasites causes a neglected disease known as leishmaniasis, which poses a threat to millions of people worldwide [1]. The disease presents in different forms, including cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL), mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (MCL) and visceral leishmaniasis (VL, also known as kala-azar) [2]. Leishmania RNA viruses (LRVs) are a unique type of virus from the family Totiviridae that circulate in different Leishmania genus (Kinetoplastea: Trypanosomatidae) [3, 4]. The phylogeny of LRVs is based on the genetic sequences of the virus. The LRVs are classified into two main groups: LRV1, which infects New World Leishmania species, and LRV2, which infects Old World Leishmania species [5, 6]. Additionally, LRV3 and LRV4 have been identified in the related genus Blechomonas spp., which are monoxenous trypanosomatids primarily parasitizing fleas [7]. LRVs are characterized by their small size and lack of an envelope [8]. The virions have an icosahedral symmetry (T = pseudo 2), with a viral capsid that is approximately 40 nm in diameter. Inside the capsid, there is a double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) that is about 5,280 nucleotides in length with two long, open reading frames on the positive strand encoding the capsid protein (CP; ORF2) and the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp; ORF3) [9]. LRVs have been shown to impact the biology and pathogenicity of the parasites [10], and there is evidence suggesting that they may influence the severity of leishmaniasis and the host’s immune response [11, 12]. In recent study, the influence of LRV2 on the expression of virulence factors (VFs) in Leishmania parasites and pro-inflammatory biomarkers were investigated. The findings of this study provide evidence that LRV2 may have a significant influence on the expression of virulence factors in Leishmania parasites and pro-inflammatory biomarkers in the human macrophage model [13].

The genetic diversity of LRV2 isolates has been identified through whole genome sequencing, highlighting the variability within this viral population. This diversity may play a role in the complex interactions between LRVs and their Leishmania hosts, impacting disease outcomes and host immune responses [11, 12]. The phylogeny of LRVs provides insights into the evolution and genetic variations and mutations that may be associated with drug resistance, virulence, and helps researchers understand its transmission dynamics and potential impact on leishmaniasis [14]. It can also enable comparisons with other LRVs isolates from different regions, shedding light on the evolutionary relationships and genetic diversity of these viruses [14]. In this study, we analyzed the sequence of whole genome and phylogenetic analysis of LRV2 isolated from CL patients in endemic foci of Iran.

Material and methods

Ethical statement

The current research was conducted with approval from the ethical principles and national regulations governing medical research (IR.MAZUMS.REC.1398.344). All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed written consent was obtained from adult participants and the parents of children, adhering to guidelines, and their responses were documented using a structured questionnaire.

Identification of Leishmania species by PCR–RFLP

The process of identifying Leishmania species through PCR-RFLP involves the preparation of Leishmania DNA using the Bioneer kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A specific set of primers, Forward (5' TCATGACTCTTGGCCTGGTAG 3') and Reverse (5' CTCTAGCGCACTTCATCGTAG 3'), is then used to amplify a fragment of approximately 1450-60 bp from the N-acetyl glucosamine-1-phosphate transferase (NAGT) gene in Leishmania parasites. The PCR products are then subjected to RFLP analysis using the Acetylcoenzyme A carboxylase 1 (ACC1) enzyme (Xmil) from Fermentas GmbH, Thermo Scientific, Germany [15].

RNA extraction and synthesis of cDNA

The total RNA from 1-5 × 106 promastigotes in the stationary phase was isolated following the instructions provided by the manufacturer (Favorgen RNA extraction kit, Favorgen Biotech). The isolated RNA was then suspended in 60 μL of nuclease-free water. The quantity of the extracted RNAs was determined using a NanoDrop spectrometer (Thermo Scientific Fisher, USA). Depending on the quantity of the extracted RNA, 1 to 2 μl were used for the synthesis of cDNA. The cDNA synthesis was carried out using a combination of oligo (dT) 18 and random hexamer primers in accordance with the instructions provided by the cDNA Synthesis Kit (YTA, Iran with Cat No: YT4500) [16].

Primer walking approach

To perform whole genome sequencing of the LRVs, ten pairs of primers were designed using Oligo 7 primer design software. These primers were designed based on the whole genome sequence available in the gene bank (GenBank Acc. No. MN418974 and MN418975). The specific details of the designed primers can be found in Table 2. The primers were then subjected to BLAST analysis against human and Leishmania sp. sequences to verify their specificity. PCR and whole genome sequencing

The PCR protocol for amplifying DNA using ten pairs of primers involved an initial step at 94 ºC for 5 min. This was followed by 35 cycles, each consisting of DNA denaturation at 94 ºC for 45 s, primers annealing at 59 ºC-60 ºC for 35 s, and strand elongation at 72 ºC for 1 min. To ensure complete synthesis of the DNA strands, a final extension was performed at 72 ºC for 5 min. The amplified product was visualized using a 1.5% agarose gel stained with SYBR safe gel stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and UV transilluminator. The PCR products were purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing in both forward and reverse directions. The sequencing was performed by ABI PrismTM 3730 Genetic Analyzer using a Genetic Analyzer ABI 3730 manufactured by Bioneer in Daejeon, South Korea.

Phylogenetic analysis

Whole genome sequences were aligned using BioEdit, version 7.0.5, and subsequently analyzed with the Mega x software. The phylogenetic trees for the LRVs datasets were generated using maximum likelihood (ML) methods and Tamura 3-parameter models. To evaluate the confidence of branches within each tree, bootstrap resampling analysis was performed with 1,000 replications [17]. Additionally, the Trichomonas vaginalis virus complete genome (NC 003824.1) was used as an out group to construct the phylogenetic tree. The Splits Tree was used for phylogenetic reconstruction between the LRV2 sequences in the current study and others LRV2 sequences using the NeighborNet method [18]. Nucleotide diversity were calculated using DnaSP. To calculate the phylogenetic networks between LRV1 and LRV2, dataset a comprised 19 LRV2 and 51 LRV1 were included. Phylogenetic networks were calculated using Network v4.6.1.5 (Bandelt et al. 1999; www.fluxus-engineering.com, Fluxus Technology Ltd., 2004). The alignment of polymorphic sites identified in the capsid and RdRp regions was utilized to generate sequence logos using the WebLogo tool (weblogo.berkeley.edu/logo.cgi). The open reading frames (ORFs) of the LRVs sequences were examined using NCBI Offender.

Results

Characterization of Leishmania species using NAGT-PCR–RFLP

The detection of Leishmania DNA involved amplifying a specific fragment of the NAGT gene, which was found to be approximately 1450-60 bp in length. This finding confirms the presence of the Leishmania genus. Based on the ACC1 enzyme digestion pattern all samples were positive for L. major.

Sequencing of the whole genome of LRV2

In this study, the whole genome of five LRV2 samples was sequenced using the primer walking technique. The designed primers were able to amplify specific regions of the virus’s complete genome. To assemble the complete genome of the LRV2 virus from the sequenced fragments, a combination of manual curation and bioinformatics software tools, including the CAP3 sequence assembly program, was used. Phylogenetic traits and haplotype variation among LRV isolates

Current sequences were 95-99% similar to the complete and partial sequences of LRV2 isolated from L. major in Iran, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan and Turkey. Interestingly, the similarity level between these sequences and other LRV2, which belong to the L. Ethiopian (KF757256), L. tarentolae (OR192167) and L. adleri (OR192166), ranged between 74 and 78%. This genetic difference is also evident in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2). In the phylogeny tree, the sequences are divided into two main clades, the first clade is related to LRV2 sequences and the second clade is related to LRV1 sequences. In the next step, the ORF finder reveled that sequences had in-frame arrangement of capsid (2145 nucleotides / 714 amino acids) and RdRp (2352 nucleotides / 783 amino acids) ORFs with a single stop codon between the frames (Table 3). In the capsid coding region (ORF2), a total of 110 variable (polymorphic) sites were identified, consisting of 67 singleton variable sites and 43 parsimony informative sites. It should be noted that the amino acid sequences did not exhibit insertions or deletions. These polymorphic changes indicated the presence of synonymous codons. Discussion

Studying the genetic diversity of LRVs provides crucial insights into their evolutionary history and can aid in strain classification, which is essential for understanding their impact on Leishmania infections. Furthermore, this knowledge supports the development of diagnostic tools and therapeutic interventions for leishmaniasis. By understanding the genetic diversity of LRVs, researchers can develop targeted approaches to diagnose and treat Leishmania infections, ultimately contributing to improved management and control of this significant global health issue [14]. In this study, the whole genome sequence and phylogenetic analysis of five isolates of LRV2 obtained from patients with CL in Iran were presented. The primer walking technique was used to sequence the virus’s complete genome.

Primer walking is a step-by-step approach to sequencing long DNA templates from end to end, which overcomes the inability of the Sanger chain. The designed primers were able to amplify specific regions of the virus’s complete genome, and RNA-sequencing was performed on PCR products [19]. This method involves dividing the long sequence into several consecutive short sequences, which are then sequenced using the Sanger chain termination method. It is essential to note that the primers and probes previously utilized for the quantification and detection of LRV1 in clinical samples exhibited nucleotide mismatches when compared to our sequence data set obtained in this study [20].

This finding highlights the importance of considering the potential effects of primer-template mismatches on the accuracy and specificity of PCR-based diagnostics. Mismatches between primers and templates can affect the stability of the primer-template complex, altering the original primer dynamics and potentially leading to errors in the detection and quantification of target sequences [21]. Therefore, it is crucial to carefully design and optimize PCR assays to ensure their performance and reliability in diagnostic applications.

In this study, ten pairs of primers were designed for the first time to sequence the whole genome of LRV2. These primers are powerful diagnostic tools that can be used in the future for screening large panels of clinical isolates, as well as for molecular phylogenetic studies. The primer walking technique used in this study provides valuable information about the variation and evolution of LRV2, contributing to our understanding of its role in Leishmania infections. It is important to note that the primers and probes previously utilized for the quantification and detection of LRV1 in clinical samples exhibited nucleotide mismatches when compared to our sequence data set. This finding highlights the importance of carefully designing and optimizing PCR assays to ensure their performance and reliability in diagnostic applications. The search results provide additional studies that have used similar techniques to sequence and analyze the genetic diversity of LRV1 and LRV2 in different Leishmania species and regions. These studies have contributed to our understanding of the evolution and co-evolution of LRVs and their Leishmania hosts, providing valuable insights into the dynamics of LRV infections and their implications in Leishmania infections.

The sequences were divided into two primary clusters, with one cluster containing LRV1 sequences and the other containing LRV2 sequences [5]. The genetic diversity between LRV1 and LRV2 was found to be remarkably high, as the two clades were distinctly separated due to their differences [22]. The LRV2 sequences were further classified into three distinct clades, with the closest sequences to those previously reported from Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan. [20, 23]. In the Tirera et al. study, the presence of 24 full-length coding sequences of LRV1 were reported, which significant genetic diversity among them were observed [23]. Furthermore, the mentioned study identified the occurrence of multiple LRV1 infections in three separate parasite isolates [23]. However, there has been a lack of comprehensive studies on the complete genomes of LRV2, and the existing evidence is derived from partial sequences [24–26]. Therefore, we examines the internal diversity of LRV2 for the first time, utilizing 19 whole genomes of LRV2 sequences from Uzbekistan (n = 14), Iran (n = 5), Turkmenistan (n = 1), Ethiopia (n = 2), Kenya (n = 1) and Senegal (n = 1). These LRV2 sequences were isolated from species of L. major, L. aethiopica, L. tarentolae and L. edleri were separated [5, 14, 25, 27]. The LRV2 sequences in this study demonstrate significant differences and are categorized into three distinct clades. These sequences are most closely related to whole genomes of LRV2 previously reported from Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan. However, sequences related to L. aethiopica and Sauroleishmania isolates were placed in two separate clades. The LRV2 viruses that infect L. aethiopica, L. tarentolae, and L. edleri belong to distinct lineages of LRV2. These findings provide support for the hypothesis that LRV2 is an ancient virus that has co-evolved alongside its parasitic host. These findings contribute to our understanding of the genetic diversity and evolution of LRVs, providing valuable insights into their role in Leishmania infections.

The study suggests that there is a higher level of diversity within the RdRp coding region compared to the capsid coding region. The results highlight the presence of polymorphisms within the examined nucleotide sequences, with variations observed in both the capsid and RdRp coding regions. The RdRp enzyme’s conservation mainly lies in its central region, which is associated with substrate uptake and catalytic activity. On the other hand, the capsid protein contains more functional fragments and thus has a higher proportion of conserved amino acid sequences [14]. This genetic diversity could potentially have implications for the function and evolution of the LRVs [30]. Further investigations are needed to understand the significance of these polymorphic sites and their potential impact on viral characteristics and disease outcomes.

Conclusion

Our data demonstrate that the genetic diversity and structural differences in the genome of LRV2 are due to considerable antiquity and genetic modifications within the viruses residing in them. The LRV2 sequences exhibit distinct differences and are classified into three clades, with the current sequences most closely related to the whole genome of LRV2 previously reported from Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan. The LRV2 viruses that infect L. aethiopica, L. tarentolae, and L. edleri belong to distinct lineages of LRV2, which have seemingly evolved independently. These findings contribute to our understanding of the genetic diversity and evolution of LRVs, providing valuable insights into their role in Leishmania infections. Further investigations are needed to understand the significance of these polymorphic sites and their potential impact on viral characteristics and disease outcomes.

Limitations

The limitations of this study include several key factors: sample size, diversity, and geographic and host specificity. The relatively small sample size of only five Leishmania RNA virus 2 (LRV2) isolates may not adequately represent the genetic diversity of LRV2 across various geographical regions or among different Leishmania species. Furthermore, it does not fully elucidate the functional implications of these genetic variations on viral pathogenicity or their interactions with Leishmania parasites. Future research should aim to address these limitations by incorporating a larger, more diverse sample set and investigating the functional roles of identified genetic variations.
