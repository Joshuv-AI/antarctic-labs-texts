Examining nurses’ awareness level and compliance between defined and required access levels to core functionalities of hospital information system : an observational and survey study
Authors: Farzad Salmanizadeh, Arefeh Ameri, Reza Khajouei, Leila Ahmadian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNurses constitute the largest number of hospital information system (HIS) users. Therefore, their awareness level and access to HIS functions based on their needs are particularly important. The present study aims to determine nurses’ access levels to HIS functions and examine the compliance between defined and required access levels to core functionalities of a comprehensive HIS.MethodsThis observational and survey study was conducted on nurses using the census method (n = 110) in two phases. In the first phase, nurses’ current access levels to core functionalities of the hospitalization management subsystem were identified in HIS. In the second phase, nurses’ awareness of defined access levels to HIS functions and compliance with their needs were investigated using a valid and reliable questionnaire (α = 0.90). The data were analyzed by descriptive and analytical statistics (t-test and one-way ANOVA).ResultsThe hospitalization management subsystem had 57 functions in 6 task groups. The information technology (IT) department enabled nurses to access 35 functions but did not allow them to access 22. 58.0% of the nurses were aware of those 35 functions they had access to, and 35.9% were aware of those 22 functions they needed access to. There was a significant correlation between nurses’ awareness of current and defined access levels (p < 0.0001), so the mean defined access levels were 23.42, greater than the mean level of nurses’ awareness of their current access to core functionalities.ConclusionUsers’ lack of awareness and access to HIS functions more or less than required could reduce user satisfaction, acceptance, and efficiency of optimal use of these systems. Therefore, hospital administrators and policymakers should determine users’ access levels based on their needs in accordance with the actual workflow and periodic evaluations. The method used in our study could help policymakers, managers, and staff at the hospital IT department accurately identify users’ needs for HIS functions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-12008-6.

Background

Hospital information system (HIS) is a comprehensive and integrated information system that provides the possibility of integrating, sending, and exchanging clinical and administrative information between wards at hospitals and other medical centers, increases the quality of care delivered and patient safety, and reduces care cost and time of access to patient records [1–3]. Despite HIS advantages, these systems may not be entirely accepted by their users and may fail to reach their predetermined purposes [4]. Various studies have reported new errors, low ease of use, and usability problems related to user interface quality as reasons for the low acceptance rate of HISs [5, 6]. Moreover, these studies have indicated that all functionality needs of users have not been taken into account in the design of these systems [7, 8]. These systems' quality, effectiveness, and efficiency depend on their proper design and considering users’ expectations and functionality needs. Moreover, the lack of familiarity of end users with HIS features and functionalities and their insufficient knowledge limit their ability to use these systems more effectively in their working environment [9]. Pursuing healthcare providers’ needs and their perception of HIS characteristics leads to user satisfaction and productivity, so this has become an important component in evaluations and research related to HISs [10]. Therefore, examining users’ viewpoints and satisfaction with the existing HIS functions is particularly important, identifying users’ functionality needs and implementing them in these systems [11]. Nurses constitute the most important and largest group of HIS end users and healthcare professionals at hospitals. They need frequent interaction with HISs due to the nature of their work. Nurses’ ability to effectively use HISs in clinical decisions is an important factor that could affect the quality of care. Thus, nurses’ attitudes and willingness to use HISs are considered important [12–16]. Despite extensive efforts to facilitate IT adoption and inform nurses of the benefits of integrating IT with healthcare, several studies have shown that nurses have not fully utilized such tools [17–20]. Lack of complete adoption of IT-based systems in clinical settings could lead to unwanted negative consequences such as anxiety, fear, desire to use traditional systems, dissatisfaction, fatigue, frustration, and adverse patient outcomes [21, 22]. Nurses’ IT adoption could be increased by examining their viewpoints towards these technologies, solving problems, and customizing them according to their practice [19, 23].

Studies have recently investigated the views and satisfaction of nurses and other users regarding using HISs by different methods such as usability evaluation, customer satisfaction index (CSI), technology acceptance model (TAM), Delone and McLean information systems success, computer system usability questionnaire (CSUQ), questionnaire for user interface satisfaction (QUIS) and usefulness, satisfaction and ease of use (USE) questionnaire [24–28]. Limited studies have evaluated the impact of information systems on nurses’ efficiency, considering usability, attitude, and experiences [29–32]. Also, access control (or access level), which refers to the level of access granted to users, is a crucial but often overlooked aspect of health information systems. This is particularly important in the healthcare industry, where sensitive information is frequently accessed [33]. A specific environment where access control is highly significant is healthcare [34, 35]. Confidentiality is a main security issue when it comes to patient clinical information. It is imperative to protect patient information from unauthorized access and misuse [35]. The introduction of health information systems (such as electronic medical records (EMRs) or hospital information systems (HISs)) within healthcare organizations aims to integrate heterogeneous patient information that is usually scattered over different locations [36, 37]. This is why the EMR is becoming an essential source of information and an important support tool for the healthcare professional. There is also an increasing need to access healthcare information at remote locations [35]. This and the distributed nature of the information stress the need for access control requirements to be taken seriously [38].

Access control is a part of the authorization process determining if users can access specific resources. It is one of the five security services outlined in the International Standards Organization (ISO) 7498–2 standard for Security Architecture [39] and is the foundation of information security [40]. However, human error can create issues regarding access control, as most users are not technical experts [41]. User access levels are permissions or rights granted to individuals or roles when interacting with a computer network. These access levels provide improved network security, controlled access to resources, assist in systematic system administration, maintain regulatory compliance, and simplify troubleshooting [42]. Security requirements have become more pressing with the rising number of security incidents in organizations and the increasing demand to ensure information confidentiality by controlling who or what is authorized to access information [43].

To the researchers' knowledge, nurses’ awareness level and access to HIS functions are among the aspects of HIS evaluation that have never been studied from the nurses’ viewpoint. Appropriate access to system functions according to users’ needs could support medical and nursing care service activities and education and research [44]. LE et al. showed that nurses’ attitudes and knowledge of HIS capabilities could influence the quality of computer medical records [45]. Therefore, the satisfaction level and compatibility of features embedded in HISs with nurses’ needs for their productivity is an important factor for hospital administrators, policymakers, and HIS providers [46]. Moreover, functions may have been embedded in HISs, but nurses may not have the necessary knowledge of their access to these functions, or their access may not be properly developed, so this issue could affect their system productivity. Therefore, the present study was conducted to determine nurses’ access levels to HIS functions and measure their awareness level of these access levels. This study examined the compliance between nurses’ defined and required access levels to core functionalities in a comprehensive HIS.

Methods

Design and setting

This observational and survey study was conducted in two phases in 2021–2022. In the first phase, nurses’ defined access levels were identified using an observational approach. In the second phase, nurses’ awareness level and compliance between defined and required access levels were measured using a survey method. SHAFA HIS, made by Tirajeh Rayaneh Company, is among Iran's most widely used HISs. SHAFA HIS is currently applied at more than 304 hospitals and 41 universities of medical sciences in Iran [47, 48]. According to the "Evaluation Indicators of Hospital Information Systems" instructions of the Ministry of Health of Iran [49], the software companies that produce HISs should meet specific standards in their products, and their HISs should have specific functions and features. Therefore, the HISs of reliable and limited software companies in Iran (such as Tirajeh Rayaneh Company, Payvand Dadeha Company, Rayavaran Company, etc.) [50] comply with these instructions, and only their products are licensed to be used in universities and medical sciences hospitals of the Ministry of Health of Iran. Due to the similarity of features and functions of SHAFA HIS with other HISs approved by the Ministry of Health of Iran at hospitals, the present research was carried out at Shafa Educational-Therapeutic Hospital in Kerman, one of the largest hospitals in southeast Iran. This hospital has 15 clinical-therapeutic wards and 13 outpatient and paraclinical wards, so more than 29,000 patients are admitted annually on average.

System

SHAFA HIS is a comprehensive system consisting of 13 subsystems (admission, hospitalization management, outpatient management, cash, laboratory, radiology, rehabilitation, emergency, etc.). Policymakers and IT department staff at Shafa Hospital applied each user’s access to subsystems and HIS functions based on the predetermined rules. If users needed a specific function, they referred to the IT department to receive the necessary access.

Survey sample size

Nurses constitute the majority of HIS users at Shafa Hospital. Due to the importance of nurses’ access to HIS functions in clinical wards, all nurses in these wards were included in the study using the census method. The inclusion criteria for nurses were having at least one year of working experience at the hospital and at least one year of working with HISs. The inclusion criteria for clinical wards were those wards in which the patients were hospitalized, and nurses needed daily interaction with HISs to perform their practice (n = 11).

Data collection

First phase: Examining current access levels to HIS functions

There are different subsystems in SHAFA HIS. Considering that nurses in clinical wards had the most interaction with the hospitalization management subsystem to perform daily tasks, the core functionalities in this subsystem were investigated. In this phase, one of the researchers went to the hospital’s IT department. Then, the core functionalities and defined access levels were examined in the hospitalization management subsystem with the cooperation of the relevant department staff and using an admin user and password that had access to all HIS functions and capabilities. Identified functions and defined access or lack of access by the IT department to each function were saved in an Excel file.

Second phase: Examining awareness level and compliance between defined and required access levels of nurses

In this phase, a questionnaire (Appendix A) was designed to evaluate nurses’ awareness of their current access levels and compliance with the defined access with the required access. This questionnaire was developed in four parts based on the identified functions in the first phase. In the first part, items were related to participants’ demographic-occupational characteristics such as gender, age, educational level, organizational position (nurse, supervisor), name of the clinical ward involved in activities, work experience in the current hospital, duration of familiarity with HIS and HIS use during a week. In the second part, the items were related to the access level to each core functionality of the hospitalization management subsystem (57 items). The nurses were asked to rate their awareness level of access to each core functionality by choosing “I have access”, “I do not know,” or “I do not have access”. In the third part, the nurses were asked to select one of these four options: “I do not need at all”, “I did not need it until now”, “I guess I need it,” or “I absolutely need it” to evaluate their need for having access to each core functionality. In the fourth part, there were three general items: user satisfaction with their current access levels, degree of compliance of the existing HIS functions with nurses’ current workflow, and degree of cooperation of the hospital IT department in determining the nurses’ required access levels. These three items were scored on a five-point Likert scale (very low to very high). The opinions of five health informatics specialists were examined to evaluate the questionnaire’s face and content validity. The questionnaire reliability was confirmed using Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.90). Based on the nurses ' morning or evening shifts, the data were collected by going to therapeutic wards in person within four months.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS 24.0. In the first phase, descriptive statistics, frequency, and percentage were employed to report the identified HIS functions. In the second phase, responses to questions about nurses’ awareness of their current access levels to functions were scored from 0 = I do not have access to 2 = I have access. The questions regarding the need to access functions were scored from 0 = I do not need it at all to 3 = I absolutely need it. The three 5-choice items in the third part were scored from 0 = very little to 4 = very much. Then, each item was analyzed using descriptive statistics, frequency, and percentage. The correlation between nurses’ awareness of their current access levels to functions and the access levels defined by the IT department was examined using a t-test. One-way ANOVA was employed to compare nurses’ awareness of their current access levels and task groups and investigate the correlation between nurses’ and task groups’ needs. Finally, the correlation between users’ awareness of functions and participants’ demographic-occupational characteristics was examined using an independent t-test and one-way ANOVA.

Results

First phase: Examining current access levels to HIS functions

In total, there were 57 core functions in the hospitalization management subsystem. As defined by the IT department, there was access to 35 functions and a lack of access to 22 cases for nurses. According to these functions in the hospitalization management subsystem, six tasks, including recorded services for patients (including ten functions), recorded requests for patients (including ten functions), patient admission in the ward (including 14 functions), patient medical record management (including 14 functions), statistics and reporting (including eight functions) and user account settings (including one function) were performed in therapeutic wards.

Second phase: Examining nurses’ awareness level and compliance between the defined and required access levels

Participants’ demographic-occupational information

In the present study, 92 nurses completed the questionnaires (response rate = 83%). Most of the participants were females (71.7%). Moreover, 54.3% were in the age group of 36–45 years old. Most participants had 11–15 years of work experience (30.4%). Some nurses (33.7%) had worked with HIS for 6–10 years, and some others (47.8%) used HIS for 11–15 h per week on average (Table 1). Nurses’ need to access the functions

Table 2 presents the compliance between nurses’ awareness and need to access task groups and defined access levels. There was a significant correlation between nurses’ awareness of current and defined access levels (p < 0.0001), so the mean defined access levels were 23.42, greater than the mean nurses’ awareness of their current access levels to core functionalities. In the task group of recorded services for patients, 54.9% of the nurses were aware of the functions they had access to, and 50.4% absolutely needed access to these functions. In this group, 45.1% of the nurses were aware of the functions they did not have access to, and 44.6% believed they would need access to this functional group. In the task group of recorded requests for patients, 64.8% were aware of the functions they had access to, and 58% absolutely needed access to these functions. In this group, 45.3% of the nurses did not know which functions they did not have access to, and 28% guessed they needed access. In the task group of patient admission in the ward, 61.3% were aware of the functions they had access to, and 59.4% absolutely needed access. In this group, 57.7% were aware of the functions they did not have access to, and 54.1% stated they absolutely needed access to them. In the task group of patient medical record management, most of the nurses were aware of access or lack of access to the functions of this group. In the statistics and reporting task group, contrary to the defined access levels, 44.0% of the nurses stated that they did not have access to the functions of this group, and 51.4% absolutely needed access to these functions. In this group, 38.0% of the nurses were unaware of the functions they did not have access to, and 32.3% guessed they needed access to these functions. In the task group of user account settings, 71.7% of the nurses were aware of their access level, and 79.3% stated that they absolutely needed access to the functions of this group (Table 3). More details related to nurses’ awareness of the defined access levels and the need to access core functionalities by functional groups are shown in Appendix B, e.g., the IT department did not define access for most of the functions of patient admission to the ward. However, nurses stated that they had access to these functions. Contrary to the lack of access defined by the IT department for the function of “Displaying daily statistics of the ward”, 96.7% of the nurses stated that they had access to the “statistics and reporting” function (Appendix B). Relationship between participants’ demographic-occupational information and awareness level of defined access levels and nurses’ need for functions

There was a significant correlation between nurses’ awareness of the defined access levels to core functionalities and therapeutic ward, duration of familiarity with HIS, and mean hours of using HIS per week (p < 0.05, so the awareness level of defined access levels among general ICU nurses was higher than that of CCU (p = 0.002, MD = 26.83), cardiac surgery ward (p = 0.02, MD = 22.33) and ophthalmic ward (p = 0.005, MD = 22.60) nurses. Moreover, the awareness level of defined access levels among reconstructive surgery ward nurses was higher than that of CCU (p = 0.007, MD = 24.83) and ophthalmic ward (p = 0.017, MD = 20.60) nurses. The mean awareness level of defined access levels among nurses familiar with HIS for 16–20 years was 16.56 greater than that of nurses familiar with HIS for 11–15 years (p = 0.024). Nurses who used HIS 16–20 h per week (p = 0.006, MD = 12.80) or 11–15 h per week (p = 0.004, MD = 11.87) on average, were more aware of their access levels than those who used HIS less than ten h a week on average. There was no significant correlation between the participants’ awareness level of defined access levels and other demographic-occupational variables (p > 0.05).

There was a significant correlation between nurses’ need to access core functionalities and the therapeutic ward (p < 0.0001), so that nurses’ need to access core functionalities in the ophthalmic ward was less than that in the orthopedic ward (p = 0.003, MD = 119.3), general ICU (p = 0.007, MD = 105.36), reconstructive surgery ward (p = 0.005, MD = 107.36), burn ward (p < 0.0001, MD = 135.10) and cardiac surgery ward (p = 0.01, MD = 78.70).

Discussion

The results indicated that nurses’ awareness of access or lack of access to the functions was low, and the hospital IT department did not provide the appropriate access to some functions based on the nurses’ needs. Users’ lack of awareness of the functions that could be used in the system could affect their optimal use of information systems and reduce individuals’ productivity. Saluvan et al. showed that service providers often do not have enough knowledge to use information systems effectively [51]. In line with our results, Pereira et al. found that half of the nurses did not have enough knowledge of documentation tools in an EHR, such as copy and paste, to increase documentation speed [52]. Lack of training could be among the reasons for nurses’ low knowledge of HIS functions and capabilities. Various studies have demonstrated that a lack of sufficient training regarding information system capabilities could cause nurses to be unaware of the systems [9, 53–55]. Sufficient training of healthcare providers enables them to become familiar with different HIS concepts and functions and better perform their daily tasks using these systems. Moreover, ongoing training, technical and educational support, technology literacy, and care providers’ informatics competency significantly contribute to the successful acceptance and implementation of HISs and users’ awareness of the capabilities of these systems in organizations [9, 56]. Technical support (or experts on call) and guidelines for working with HIS could directly and significantly affect usefulness, ease of use, and, ultimately, nurses’ satisfaction with HIS [57–59]. Therefore, continuous patient support and training should be provided to increase user satisfaction and productivity [46].

In the present study, examining the correlation between demographic-occupational variables and nurses’ awareness of access to core functionalities showed that users with more work experience and familiarity with HIS were more aware of the functions. The more the familiarity with HIS and the nurses’ work experience, the higher their productivity and satisfaction would be. The nurses’ awareness level in some wards, such as the ICU, was greater than that of nurses in other wards. This could be attributed to the greater experience of ICU nurses working with HIS due to the earlier implementation of HIS in this ward compared to other wards. Therefore, meetings should be held for nurses in all wards to determine the appropriate access levels to HIS functions by the hospital IT department.

The results revealed that nurses needed access to their required functions. Furthermore, some nurses had access to functions that they did not need, and the defined access levels were greater than the access levels that the nurses were aware of. Nurses’ lack of access to the required functions could reduce their work productivity with HIS. Accessing HIS capabilities and functions is among the main motivations for introducing information systems in healthcare settings [60]. Healthcare providers expect HIS to increase access to the required functions and enable them to perform daily tasks and deliver high-quality care [61, 62]. Moreover, lack of proper access to system functions makes users unable to benefit from system usefulness and ease of use and, eventually, causes them to be dissatisfied with the system and increases workload and risk of making errors [63]. Therefore, access to system functions should be considered based on nurses’ actual needs, not to increase their workload. Moreover, accessing functions that nurses do not need may lead to security problems, privacy breaches, and disclosure of patients’ information [64–66]. Nurses assumed they might need some functions in the future, indicating that users’ needs could change over time. Therefore, users’ needs for HIS functions should be periodically evaluated based on their current needs. Furthermore, customizing access to functions could effectively respond to users’ current and future needs [67, 68].

Nurses’ overall satisfaction with the access levels to HIS functions was moderate. In line with this result, other studies have reported nurses’ satisfaction with information systems at hospitals at a moderate level [14, 15, 69]. End users’ satisfaction with information systems could significantly affect the acceptance and use of these systems in organizations [70]. Low user satisfaction could increase resentment, frustration, and tension and lead to inefficiency in the optimal use of the system [71]. Various studies have indicated that increasing user satisfaction with information systems makes them use it more appropriately. User satisfaction with systems is necessary to achieve system goals [72–74]. Therefore, frequent system evaluations from nurses’ viewpoint and considering their needs could increase their satisfaction with the system.

In the present study, the survey rate and coordination between the IT department and nurses in different wards regarding access to HIS functions were low [63]. Nurses’ awareness of system usefulness, acceptance, and satisfaction could be enhanced if they participate in decision-making processes, implementing IT, and selecting and implementing HIS at hospitals [63]. Therefore, involving nurses and considering their needs should be considered by IT department staff in periodic evaluations.

The results revealed that the compliance of access to HIS functions with actual workflow was moderate from nurses’ viewpoint. In line with this finding, some studies have reported an inconsistency between access to information system functions and users’ actual workflow [75–79]. If access to functions does not match the actual workflow, it becomes more difficult to perform routine tasks by HIS, which increases nurses’ workload and fatigue and decreases user satisfaction [67, 75–80]. However, inconsistency between HIS functions and actual workflow may be due to nurses’ lack of awareness of HIS capabilities and training from their viewpoint.

In healthcare organizations with intra- and inter-organizational interactions, it is crucial to have authorization and access control mechanisms in place at the user and other levels that can accurately reflect these dynamic interactions. This can be accomplished by establishing structured and formal policies, models, and roles [81]. Access control models are frameworks that dictate how subjects such as users can access resources. They aim to control who can access what resources and to what extent. Various access control models have been developed to provide security solutions for multiple applications, including web and distributed [82]. These models include discretionary access control (DAC), mandatory access control (MAC), role-based access control (RBAC), task-based access control (TBAC), and attribute-based access control (ABAC) [35]. Each model uses different methods to control how subjects access resources. The models can be used exclusively or combined to achieve the security level required by the environment. The use of these models depends on the organization's business and security goals [35]. For example, the RBAC model can help apply access-level policies [83]. This model provides access to resources depending on the user's role in the organization. These features can accommodate organization-specific security policies, making them policy-neutral. RBAC is a flexible and easy-to-administer model that includes a special administrative role to manage all other roles, thereby supporting a hierarchical model [84, 85]. It also satisfies critical web application security requirements such as separation of duties and least privilege [86]. It is recommended to use models based on needs and security policies to ensure greater data security, confidentiality, and structured implementation of access levels in Iranian health organizations, particularly in HISs.

This research investigated users’ awareness level and compliance between defined and required access levels to core functionalities in a comprehensive HIS. Considering the importance of evaluating HISs in healthcare, studies have focused more on usability, satisfaction, factors influencing acceptance of HISs, and the advantages and challenges of these systems from the viewpoints of nurses and other users. The present work investigated nurses’ awareness level and access to HIS functions based on their needs and examined one of the possible factors in the acceptance of HIS by users. Future studies could investigate the effect of users’ awareness and access on adopting information systems.

Limitations

This study had three limitations. First, due to the large number of subsystems in HIS, only core functionalities related to the hospitalization management subsystem were investigated. Most nurses’ daily interactions and activities were associated with hospitalization management subsystem functions. Therefore, examining the core functionalities of this subsystem could give a general view of users’ current access levels to other subsystems. It is recommended that future studies examine users’ access levels to functions of other HIS subsystems. Second, given that defining user access to HIS functions varies in different settings, policies, and organizations, caution should be made when generalizing the results of this research to other settings. However, the method employed in this study could be used in different settings.

Implications of study

The method used in this study could help IT department staff, healthcare managers, and policymakers determine access to HIS functions according to users’ needs. Also, in Iran, software companies must comply with the minimum HIS design standards. Therefore, our tool can be expanded by extracting functions and features of other SHAFA HIS subsystems to measure the awareness and needs of other HIS users (such as physicians, employees, and managers). It is recommended that managers take measures such as holding continuous training courses about HIS capabilities, assessing users’ informatics competency, providing technical and educational support for users, and periodically evaluating users’ needs for access to HIS functions in accordance with actual workflow to increase users’ productivity and satisfaction with HIS. Policymakers can use access control models, such as the RBAC model, to implement security policies and ensure greater data security and confidentiality in Iranian health organizations.

Conclusion

The results showed that nurses’ awareness of their access or lack of access to core functionalities of HIS was low, and there was no compliance between users’ defined and required access levels to HIS functions. Moreover, access to HIS functions was not completely consistent with users’ actual workflow. Users did not have access to some functions while they absolutely needed them. Therefore, using customization capability and greater cooperation of IT department staff in organizations with users to adjust their access to the required functions could increase awareness and compliance between users’ needs and actual clinical workflow. Given that needs change over time, the access level of each user to HIS functions should be examined in different time periods. The method used in our study could help policymakers, managers, and staff at the hospital IT department accurately identify users’ needs for HIS functions.
