Prevalence of type 2 multimorbidities in COPD compared to asthma and relationship to blood eosinophilic counts
Authors: David M.G. Halpin, Jaspreet Kaur, Heath Heatley, Freya Tyrer, Cono Ariti, Rachel Pullen, Shelly Pathak, Victoria Carter, David Price
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType 2 (T2) inflammation is increasingly recognised in COPD, though its immunopathological profile may differ from asthma. T2 inflammatory diseases often coexist, but their prevalence in COPD patients without asthma remains underexplored. This study investigated the prevalence of T2 inflammatory multimorbidities in individuals with COPD (without asthma) and compared it with those in people with asthma. It also examined the relationship between these multimorbidities and blood eosinophil counts (BEC).MethodsUsing data from the Optimum Patient Care Research Database (OPCRD), we identified patients with COPD or asthma and assessed lifetime and adjusted prevalence of T2 comorbidities including allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis/eczema, nasal polyps, chronic rhinitis with or without polyps, eosinophilic oesophagitis and ulcerative colitis. Prevalence was stratified by BEC and adjusted for demographic and clinical variables.ResultsAmong 122 509 COPD and 1 154 094 asthma patients, T2 multimorbidities were present in both groups but significantly less common in COPD. Allergic rhinitis and nasal polyps were notably less prevalent in COPD, even at higher BEC levels. Prevalence of multimorbidities increased with BEC in both groups, with stronger associations observed for nasal polyps and ulcerative colitis. Eosinophilic oesophagitis was rare across both cohorts.ConclusionT2 multimorbidities are present in COPD and their prevalence increases with BEC, though they are generally less prevalent than in asthma, suggesting distinct immunopathology.

Introduction

Recently there has been much interest in the role of type 2 (T2) inflammation in patients with COPD [1]. The term type 2 inflammation came into use in the 1990s following the identification of the T-helper 2 (Th2) subpopulation of CD4+ Th cells [2]. T2 inflammation involves both the innate and adaptive immune systems and is characterised by activation of and increased numbers of Th2 and group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), and release of the so-called type 2 cytokines interleukin-4, -5 and -13 (IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13) [3]. Atopic T2 inflammation additionally involves a genetically determined predisposition to produce IgE antibodies in response to environmental allergens [4].

Overexpression of T2 inflammation is as a key feature of allergic diseases, including asthma, atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, chronic prurigo, chronic urticaria, chronic rhinitis with nasal polyps and eosinophilic oesophagitis [5–7]. Ulcerative colitis (UC) is also now considered a predominantly T2 disease [8]. Despite differences in their clinical features, these diseases share a T2 inflammatory signature with eosinophilia, eosinophilic tissue infiltration and/or high serum IgE levels.

A subgroup of patients with COPD have higher blood eosinophil counts (BEC) [9], and elevated sputum eosinophil counts have been reported in between 27% and 46% of stable COPD patients [1]. There is also evidence that total IgE levels are higher in some eosinophilic COPD patients [10] as well as evidence of increased T2 gene expression in bronchial epithelial cells [11, 12]. Elevated fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FENO) measurements, which are associated with T2 inflammation in asthma and are a surrogate marker of IL-13 activity [13], have been observed in a small subgroup of COPD patients [14], but the effects of tobacco smoking on FENO confounds their use as a reliable marker of T2 inflammation in COPD [15, 16].

There is, however, evidence that T2 inflammation in COPD is not identical to that seen in asthma [1, 17]. Moreover, whilst some biologic therapies that block the IL-5, IL-4/IL-13 and thymic stromal lymphopoietin pathways have shown efficacy in some patients with COPD and higher BEC, their effects are not as pronounced as in asthma [18].

T2 inflammatory diseases frequently coexist [19, 20]. People with atopy often develop dermatitis, allergic rhinitis and asthma in childhood. Atopic diseases may persist for years or resolve with increasing age, and their development is strongly influenced by genetic and environmental factors. Nonatopic T2 inflammatory diseases may reflect a multimorbid state with coexistent diseases sharing common intrinsic immunopathological mechanisms [21].

This study investigated the prevalence of T2 inflammatory multimorbidities in people with COPD and their relationship with BEC. Prevalence estimates were compared with asthma patients to identify possible differences in underlying immunopathology.

Methods

Data source

Data were sourced from the Optimum Patient Care Research Database (OPCRD; https://opcrd.co.uk), which contains anonymised routine, patient-level diagnostic, clinical and prescribing information, including key summary data from birth to last data entry, on over 24 million patients from over 1000 primary care centres across the UK (∼35% of the total UK population) [22]. To increase data reliability, practices included in the study were required to meet a predetermined data recording quality.

Patients

The study included all patients active in OPCRD aged between 5 and 95 years at the end of the study period (24 March 2025), with a COPD or asthma diagnosis (at least one diagnostic COPD or asthma Read or SNOMED code (Quality and Outcomes Framework defined)) at any time in their medical record. People with a history of cystic fibrosis and dual diagnosis with COPD and asthma were excluded from the study yielding two cohorts: COPD only and asthma only. People with COPD were further excluded if they did not have spirometry results confirming the presence of airflow limitation (i.e. forced expiratory volume in 1 s/forced vital capacity <0.7).

Diagnoses of allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis and eczema, chronic rhinitis with or without polyps, nasal polyps, eosinophilic oesophagitis and UC were identified using validated Read and SNOMED CT codes in OPCRD. Demographic data on age at diagnosis of COPD or asthma, age at the end of study period, sex, ethnicity, highest ever body mass index (BMI) and smoking history were extracted from OPCRD. The patient's highest ever recorded BEC at any point in their medical history was also extracted.

Data analysis

Demographic characteristics of people in the cohorts were compared using standardised mean differences and chi-squared tests. The lifetime prevalence of T2 inflammatory multimorbidities was calculated for each cohort by determining the number of people who had a diagnosis of a T2 inflammatory multimorbidity recorded in their medical records at any time before or during the study period. Adjusted prevalence was calculated using marginal standardisation, whereby model-based prevalence estimates for each disease group were averaged over the covariate distribution of the study population. Models were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, ethnicity, smoking status and blood eosinophil category.

The prevalence of T2 inflammatory multimorbidities was assessed in people with COPD and in people with asthma stratified by BEC. Prevalence ratios (PR) were estimated using the same regression models that included both asthma-only and COPD-only participants, with asthma-only specified as the reference group. Unadjusted PRs were estimated using the log-binomial models. Adjusted PR were estimated using a Poisson regression model with a log link, adjusting for age, sex, BMI, ethnicity, smoking status and blood eosinophil category.

PRs were used to compare the prevalence of T2 inflammatory multimorbidities in people with COPD and asthma stratified by BEC using people with BEC 0–100 cells·μL−1 as the reference group. Patients who did not have a recorded BEC were not included in these analyses. PR were also used to compare the prevalence of T2 inflammatory multimorbidities in people with COPD compared to people with asthma within each eosinophil stratum.

As expected, the mean age at diagnosis of asthma was significantly lower than the mean age at diagnosis of COPD. At the end of the study period the asthma cohort was significantly younger, with 75.8% of people with asthma aged under 50 years whilst 92.6% of people with COPD were aged 60 years or older. A significantly higher proportion of people with COPD were men (54.40% versus 47.73%, p<0.001). The mean BMI and its distribution were broadly similar between the COPD and asthma cohorts. Most people in both groups were of European descent, but there were more people of Asian, African and mixed ethnicity in the asthma group. The COPD group had significantly higher proportions of current and ex-smokers compared to the asthma group (p<0.001). The distribution of the highest recorded BEC was very similar in the COPD and asthma groups (figure 2), but the asthma group had a much higher rate of missing data (21.86% versus 1.34%; SMD=0.68). The demographic characteristics of patients with asthma with and without BEC are shown in supplementary Table S1. Patients without BEC available were younger, had a lower mean age of asthma diagnosis, were more likely to be female, had lower BMIs and were less likely to have smoked.

The lifetime and adjusted prevalence rates of the T2 inflammatory multimorbidities in people with COPD and asthma are shown in table 2. In the unadjusted analysis, the lifetime prevalence of allergic rhinitis was significantly lower in people with COPD compared to those with asthma only (PR=0.40, 95% CI: 0.37–0.43; p<0.001). Similarly, the unadjusted lifetime prevalence of atopic dermatitis and eczema was significantly lower in COPD (PR=0.67, 95% CI: 0.64–0.71; p<0.001). The unadjusted lifetime prevalence of nasal polyps was not significantly different in COPD and asthma (PR=1.13, 95% CI: 0.84–1.51; p=0.43), but chronic rhinitis (with or without nasal polyps) was more prevalent in COPD (PR=1.14, 95% CI: 1.01–1.28; p=0.03). The prevalence of eosinophilic oesophagitis was very low in both asthma and COPD populations. Among 1 154 094 people with asthma, 157 cases were identified (0.01%), whilst only two cases were recorded among 122 509 people with COPD (<0.01%). Given the extremely small number of events, adjusted prevalence estimates could not reliably be generated. The unadjusted lifetime prevalence of UC was significantly higher in COPD than in asthma (PR=1.85, 95% CI: 1.31–2.63; p<0.001).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge this is the first large real-world study reporting the prevalence of multimorbidities associated with T2 inflammation in very large community-based groups of people with COPD and asthma, and the first to relate the prevalence of these multimorbidities to BEC. We deliberately excluded patients with both asthma and COPD from this analysis as overlapping disease would confound interpretation of the COPD-specific prevalence of T2 inflammatory multimorbidities.

As expected, we found that allergic rhinitis and atopic dermatitis and eczema were the most common T2 multimorbidities in people with asthma, and they were also the most common T2 multimorbidities in people with COPD. When adjusted for age, the prevalence of allergic rhinitis and nasal polyps in people with COPD was less than half that in asthma; however, although the other T2 multimorbidities were also less common in people with COPD, the differences in prevalence rates were smaller.

The prevalence of rhinitis we observed in people with COPD was similar to that in the general population [23], but the prevalence of atopic dermatitis and eczema was considerably higher. Data from the European Community Respiratory Health Survey based on self-completed questionnaires suggested that rhinitis was more common in younger people with COPD than in the general population, but not in older people with COPD [24]. A population study, also based on self-reports, has also suggested that atopic dermatitis and eczema have an increased prevalence in COPD [25], but another found no association once comorbid asthma was taken into account [26]. In general population studies, the prevalence of allergic rhinitis decreases substantially with age [27]; however, this is not relevant to our analysis as we looked at the lifetime prevalence of T2 multimorbidities. We have not examined the relationship between multimorbidities and age or other demographic characteristics.

In our analysis of the relationship between BEC and T2 multimorbidities we used the highest BEC recorded for each patient. Many patients in OPCRD have had multiple BEC measurements over many years. Some may have been taken while on medications that suppress BEC, but in the UK there are few intercurrent illnesses such as parasitic infections that result in elevated BEC, and it has long been known that acute bacterial infections lead to lower BEC [28]. Individual BEC may have been affected by concomitant oral corticosteroid medication, but, as in previous studies [29, 30], we believe the highest recorded BEC is the best indicator of an eosinophilic propensity and T2 inflammatory status. The asthma group had a much higher rate of missing data, and those with missing data were younger and had a lower mean age of asthma diagnosis. These factors probably explain the lower likelihood of having a blood count either to assess their asthma or for any other reason.

We are not aware of any previous data on the incremental relationship between the prevalence of T2 inflammatory multimorbidities and BEC in people with asthma or COPD. One previous study reported that patients with asthma and BEC >400 cells·μL−1 had more comorbid rhinitis and eczema than those with lower BEC [31]. Our data show that in people with asthma, the prevalence of all T2 inflammatory multimorbidities increased with increasing BEC; however, the magnitude of the increase varied. The prevalence of nasal polyps, eosinophilic oesophagitis and UC showed a strong relationship to BEC, and the prevalences were over four times higher in people with BEC >500 cells·μL−1 compared to those with BEC ≤100 cells·μL−1. A similar pattern was seen in people with COPD with nasal polyps, showing a fivefold increase with increasing BEC. In contrast, although the prevalence of allergic rhinitis and atopic dermatitis and eczema increased in people with asthma and COPD with increasing BEC, the prevalence in people with BEC >500 cells·μL−1 was only around 50% higher than in those with BEC ≤100 cells·μL−1.

There is controversy about whether BEC can be used as a surrogate marker of airway eosinophilic inflammation in people with COPD, as some studies show a good correlation between BEC and airway eosinophilic inflammation [32] whilst others show that up to 30% of patients have discordance in their eosinophilia status in blood and in the airways [12]. Nevertheless, COPD patients with higher BEC have higher type 2 gene expression in the epithelium [11, 12, 17]. The differential relationship between BEC and T2 multimorbidities in COPD and asthma may reflect the differences between atopic and nonatopic forms of type 2 inflammation, in particular the involvement of IgE as well as eosinophils in the immunopathology of atopic conditions [4]; however, it has also been shown that there is little overlap between genes and pathways associated with BEC in asthma versus COPD [10].

Across all strata of BEC allergic rhinitis was significantly less prevalent in people with COPD than in people with asthma. Atopic dermatitis and eczema were also significantly less prevalent in people with COPD than in people with asthma at lower BEC, but at counts above >300 cells·μL−1 the difference was smaller, perhaps reflecting greater systemic T2 inflammation in people with COPD and higher BEC.

Eosinophilic oesophagitis is a less common chronic, immune-mediated disease characterised by eosinophilic inflammation and elevated levels of the T2 cytokines IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13, but not elevated IgE [33]. It has been described as the “fifth member” of the atopic march [34]. In our population it was less common in people with COPD compared to those with asthma irrespective of BEC, although overall it was rare. A previous analysis also showed that eosinophilic oesophagitis was rare in asthma [35], but we are unaware of previous studies reporting the prevalence in people with COPD.

The role of the T2 immune response in the pathogenesis of UC is controversial. Early reports suggested that there were elevated levels of T2 cytokines, but more recent studies have not consistently shown this. However, studies of untreated patients with UC have shown elevated IL-5, IL-13, IL-15 and IL-33 mRNA levels [36]. Our data show that UC was uncommon in both people with COPD and asthma, and the overall adjusted prevalence was similar in people with COPD and asthma. However, the prevalence in both diseases increased with increasing BEC. In people with BEC <400 cells·μL−1 the unadjusted prevalence of UC was higher in people with COPD than in asthma at similar BEC. This may reflect the fact that the COPD population was older, allowing more time to develop the disease.

In this analysis we do not have any information on the severity of the T2 multimorbidities nor their impact on patients’ health status. A previous study showed that people with asthma and a higher cumulative T2 multimorbidity burden were more likely to experience worse asthma outcomes [35] and multimorbidities are associated with worse health status [37]. It is not known whether T2 multimorbidities affect outcomes in COPD, but multimorbid rhinitis is associated with worse health status [38, 39].

Our study has several strengths including the large sample size of patients with physician-diagnosed asthma (n=1 154 094) and spirometrically confirmed COPD (n=122 509). OPCRD is a well-maintained database which is used frequently for observational research in COPD and asthma [35, 40–42], and it contains information which is collected prospectively and not subjected to recall bias. It also mostly contains data covering the lifespan of patients. BEC were available for 99% of people with COPD and nearly 80% of people with asthma. OPCRD includes data on people from wide geographically and socioeconomically diverse populations, and therefore we believe that the study results show the true relationship between COPD and asthma and T2 multimorbidities.

The limitations of our study include the fact that many mild cases of rhinitis and eczema are self-managed by patients (and parents) at home and thus may not be recorded in the EMR. Our data reflect diagnoses made in primary care and may reflect an over- or under-reporting of T2 multimorbidities. Individual patient records contain information collected for routine clinical use rather than for research purposes, and as a consequence there were some missing data and there may also be potential issues with accuracy of coding. The data reflect the demographics of people with COPD in the UK, and few people of non-European ethnicity were included. As in previous studies, we used the highest ever BEC recorded for patients but acknowledge that this may not be representative of the average BEC over a patients’ lifespan. The fact that 22% of people with asthma did not have a BEC and that these people were younger and had earlier onset asthma affects our ability to generalise about the relationship between BEC and T2 multimorbidities in people with asthma, particularly in younger people.

Conclusion

T2 multimorbidities are present in COPD and their prevalence increases with increasing BEC. The prevalence of atopic multimorbidities was lower than in people with asthma, even at higher BEC, but the prevalence of nonatopic multimorbidities was similar to that seen in asthma.
