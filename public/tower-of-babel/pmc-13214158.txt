STAT1 itaconation prevents macrophage cytolistic mtDNA -induced inflammation in wear particle-induced aseptic loosening
Authors: Yifan Yu, Taihe Liu, Chenhao Pan, Haopeng Sun, Zhipeng Chen, Haoxian Liu, Wingcheuk Ko, Siyuan Tan, Jiankai Luo, Shixun Li, Changchuan Li, Yue Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProsthetic wear particle-driven macrophage inflammation severely limits the long-term efficacy of total joint replacements by causing aseptic loosening. However, the specific mechanisms by which wear particles induce macrophage inflammation remain incompletely elucidated. Itaconic acid produced by the Krebs cycle is markedly up-regulated in TiPs-stimulated macrophages, which may modulate mitochondrial metabolism through protein itaconation or competitive inhibition. Here, using 4-OI, we demonstrate that itaconate functions as an endogenous metabolic regulator that suppresses SDH activity, thereby significantly inhibiting STING pathway activation. Moreover, 4-OI can alkylate STAT1, preventing its phosphorylation and relieving transcriptional repression of the mitochondrial transcription factor TFAM, which stabilizes mitochondrial homeostasis and attenuates macrophage inflammation. In a murine calvarial osteolysis model, 4-OI reversed bone destruction induced by TiPs and TFAM knockdown. Collectively, our findings establish itaconate as a critical endogenous metabolite that alleviates wear particle–mediated inflammation and osteolysis by reprogramming macrophage metabolism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02874-4.Keywords: Aseptic loosening, Osteolysis, Macrophage inflammatory, IRG1, 4-OI, Itaconylation

Introduction

Arthroplasty is the most effective method for treating end-stage bone and joint diseases, with over 5 million prostheses implanted globally each year [1]. However, studies report that aseptic loosening (AL)—now the leading cause of joint revision—significantly limits long-term success [2]. Mechanistically, prosthesis wear particles can activate macrophages, trigger inflammatory responses, and release cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) [3, 4], which further activate osteoclasts to promote bone resorption [5], ultimately leading toaseptic loosening. Therefore, in-depth investigation of wear particle-induced macrophage inflammation and the identification of the key regulatory factors could offer a novel therapeutic strategy for preventing the progression of aseptic loosening and prolonging the lifespan of prostheses.

Mitochondria have long been recognized as cellular powerhouses, where metabolites are oxidized through the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA cycle) and subsequently generate ATP via the electron transport chain [6]. However, recent studies reveal that mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming upon stimulation regulates macrophage immunophenotypes. In this process, mitochondrial products such as mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) serving as key signals for macrophage polarization and activation [7]. Specifically, leaked mtDNA activates the cytosolic cGAS-STING signaling pathway, perpetuating a self-amplifying inflammatory cascade [8]. Succinate dehydrogenase (SDH), an indispensable enzyme in the TCA cycle, catalyzes the conversion of succinate to fumarate to participate in the electron respiratory chain transport process [9]. Previous studies have shown that SDH modulates mtDNA release via mtROS production, indirectly regulating STING/TBK1-mediated inflammatory cytokine expression [10]. Simultaneously, SDH controls macrophage M1 polarization by altering succinate levels in the TCA cycle [11]. These findings collectively position SDH as a pivotal regulator of mitochondrial immunometabolism.

Immune responses are closely linked to mitochondrial metabolism. Under excessive stimulation, the compensatory increase of metabolites such as itaconate is a typical manifestation [12, 13]. Immune-responsive gene 1 (IRG1) encodes cis-aconitate decarboxylase, which catalyzes the decarboxylation of cis-aconitate in the mitochondria to produce itaconic acid (ITA) [14]. Evidence indicates that the induction of IRG1 is reliant upon the activation of the STING signaling pathway elicited by diverse stimuli [15, 16]. Itaconate exerts multifaceted anti-inflammatory effects through covalent protein modification (termed 'itaconylation') and metabolic reprogramming [17]. Previous studies reveal structural similarity between ITA and succinate, and demonstrate ITA's high binding efficacy to the succinate catalytic site on the α-subunit of SDH via electrostatic interactions [18]. This inhibition blocks the conversion of succinate to fumarate, thereby reducing mtROS generation and suppressing the HIF-1α pathway along with downstream pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β [19]. Concurrently, endogenous itaconate and 4-octyl itaconate (4-OI) have been demonstrated to alkylate cysteine residues on a variety of proteins, including KEAP1 [20], GAPDH [21], ALDOA [22], and NLRP3 [23]. This mode of alkylation, termed itaconation constitutes an essential component of its anti-inflammatory properties—for example, itaconation of KEAP1 activates Nrf2 and thereby suppresses the STING-driven inflammatory pathway [20]. Furthermore, ITA helps regulate the expression of mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) to mitigate mtDNA leakage [24]. Nevertheless, the specific role of IRG1/ITA in wear particle-mediated aseptic loosening of prosthetic joints remains unclear.

Here, we elucidate a previously unrecognized ITA-STAT1-TFAM axis that safeguards mitochondrial integrity during wear particle stimulation. We found that IRG1 up-regulation, induced upon TiPs stimulation, acts as a critical metabolic checkpoint that suppresses inflammatory osteolysis. Specifically, we demonstrated that itaconate, the product of IRG1, modulates macrophage metabolic reprogramming by inhibiting SDH activity, which significantly reduces STING pathway activation and M1 polarization, thereby attenuating TiPs-induced inflammation. Furthermore, we discovered that ITA can also affect the transcriptional expression of TFAM by alkylating STAT1, stabilizing mtDNA leakage. This metabolic-immune crosstalk disrupts the STING-NF-κB amplification loop, providing a unified explanation for itaconate’s dual anti-inflammatory and mitochondrial protective effects. Additionally, in vivo experiments have shown that 4-OI targeted injection can alleviate the osteolysis and inflammation of the mouse skull caused by TiPs and targeted knockdown of TFAM. In conclusion, our findings indicate that the IRG1/ITA represents a potential therapeutic target for preventing periprosthetic aseptic loosening.

Materials and methods

Clinical specimen

The synovial tissue samples were taken from the Department of Orthopedics, Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University. The interfacial membranes around the hip joint prosthesis were collected from 6 patients undergoing revision surgery for AL of the hip joint prosthesis, and the synovial tissues of the hip joint were collected from 6 patients with femoral head necrosis (FHN) undergoing primary total hip arthroplasty (THA). For the six patients undergoing revision THA for AL, from whom the interfacial membrane tissues were sourced in this study, a review of their primary surgical records confirmed that all failed prostheses were from the Smith & Nephew SYNERGY Hip series. The femoral stem of this system is a cementless, proximally plasma-sprayed porous titanium alloy stem, and the bearing couple in all cases was a zirconia ceramic femoral head against a highly cross-linked polyethylene liner. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University (SYSKY-2025–566-01), and all experiments were conducted with the consent of the patients.

Titanium particles

Sterile TiPs were prepared as previously described in our study [25]. TiPs solutions were purchased from Alfa Aesar (< 20 μm, W08A030) and further diluted with sterile water. TiPs with a size of 1.2 to 10 μm were filtered using Isopore membranes (pore sizes of 10 μm/1.2 μm, TCTP04700/R TTP04700, Millipore). The particle size primarily falls within the micrometer scale of 1–10 µm. Volume-based distribution analysis showed a D50 value of 3.5 µm, and the particles have an irregular, polygonal shape with an aspect ratio of approximately 1.8–2.6 and circularity ranging from 0.55 to 0.70. Subsequently, the particles were sterilized by high-pressure steam sterilization to eliminate endotoxins and other pathogenic microorganisms. Then the particles were weighed and diluted to 1mg/ml with PBS (YJ0014, YONG JIN). This concentration approximately corresponds to the operational standard of a TiPs suspension volume-to-cell number ratio of 100:1.

Preparation and application of 4-OI

4-OI (HY-112675, MedChemExpress) was centrifuged according to the manufacturer’s instructions and then dissolved in DMSO to prepare a stock solution at a concentration of 10 mM, which was stored at − 20 °C for later use. RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded into 6-well plates. When the cell density reached approximately 80%, 20 µL of the stock solution was added to 1 mL of culture medium to achieve a final working concentration of 4-OI at 200 µM. After incubation with the cells for 2 h, the intracellular and extracellular concentrations reached equilibrium, at which point subsequent cell manipulations could be performed.

Cell and culture

RAW264.7 cells (CL-0190, Procell) were routinely cultured in the special medium for RAW264.7 cells (TCM-G766, HyCyte). 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution had been pre-added. The culture conditions were 37° C and 5% CO₂. All in vitro experiments were conducted using 6-well/12-well cell culture plates (JET BIOFIL). Cells were seeded at a density of approximately 2 × 105 cells/cm2, reaching a confluency of about 70–80% prior to stimulation.

RNA sequencing

After 6 h of TiPs stimulation, macrophages were lysed with TRIzol™ reagent (15,596,018,Invitrogen). The collected total RNA was removed with rRNA to retain mRNA and ncRNA for constructing specific libraries and sequencing. After reverse transcription and PCR amplification detection of the processed RNA solution, sequencing was performed using Illumina NanoSeq 6000. Remove the original readings that contain adapters or are of low quality (Q value ≤ 20). The Reads were mapped to the mouse genome database (GRCm38), and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as FDR < 0.05 and log2FC > 1. We have deposited the raw RNA-seq data generated in this study to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) repository. The dataset has been assigned the accession number: PRJNA1372466.

Detection of SDH activity, itaconate and succinate

RAW264.7 was seeded in 6-well plates and cultured until the cell density was 80%. After the cells are treated, aspirate the supernatant, collect and lyse the cells. Measure the SDH activity in the lysates according to the instructions of the SDH Activity Assay Kit (S0530S, Beyotime) using spectrophotometry. Similarly, for the detection of intracellular itaconate and succinate, we applied the Amplex Red Succinate Assay Kit (S0529S, Beyotime) and the Itaconate ELISA Kit (HBDY-927922O1) for measurement.

Lentiviruses and stable cell lines’ establishment

Mouse TFAM overexpressing lentivirus (oe-TFAM) and knockdwown lentivirus (sh-TFAM/sh-STAT1) were designed and synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Corresponding empty vector (Mock) and scramble shRNA (sh-NC) constructs served as controls for overexpression and knockdown experiments, respectively.

For lentiviral transduction, RAW264.7 24-well plates were spread at a rate of 1 × 10^5 cells per well. Then, a medium containing 8 µg/mL Polybrene of lentivirus was added. Following 24 h of incubation, the virus-containing medium was replaced with standard growth medium. GFP expression was assessed by fluorescence microscopy 48 h post-transduction, and wells with transduction efficiency exceeding 70% were used for subsequent selection. Continuous screening with 2–5 µg/mL puromycin for 7 days; During this period, the medium should be changed every 2 to 3 days to retain the resistant clones. The expression of the target gene was verified by qPCR and WB, and the most effective knockdown lentivirus was selected for further experiments.

CHIP-qPCR

Chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed using a ChIP kit (P2080S, Beyotime). After collecting the cells, formaldehyde cross-linking was used to stabilize the interactions between proteins and DNA within the chromatin. Subsequently, ultrasonication was employed to lyse the cells, shearing the genomic DNA into fragments of 200–1000 bp in size. Specific antibodies against STAT1 (14,994, Cell Signaling) were then used to bind the target protein. Washing steps were conducted to remove non-specifically bound proteins and other contaminants, and the cross-links were reversed to dissociate the DNA fragments. Finally, the DNA was purified and analyzed by qPCR to measure TFAM binding.

For IF staining of intracellular dsDNA, we pre-treatted the cells with PicoGreen(12641ES01, YEASEN) for 2 h when cell confluence reached 50%, and the subsequent procedures were the same as those for cellular immunofluorescence. Since our goal was to investigate the spatial distribution of cytosolic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), specifically to determine if it originated from mtDNA leakage. While PicoGreen dye effectively stains all dsDNA, it cannot distinguish between nuclear DNA, cytosolic mtDNA, or DNA from other sources. Therefore, to specifically identify mtDNA and confirm its mitochondrial origin, we performed co-staining with the mitochondrial marker HSP60. This approach allowed us to determine whether the cytosolic dsDNA signals (PicoGreen-positive) were co-localized with or had leaked from the mitochondria (HSP60-positive areas).

In vivo experiments

The animals used in this research were sourced from the Animal Experiment Center of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval Number: SYSU-IACUC-2023–001033). Under the guiding principles of the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Sun Yat-sen University, this experiment selected 10-week-old C57BL/6 J male mice for animal experiments. Throughout the entire surgical procedure, every step was carried out strictly in accordance with the pre-set standards for animal surgery and welfare. The method for establishing a skull osteolysis model was described in our previous research. Specifically, we randomly divided 35 mice into 7 groups. The following are the grouping situations. SHAM group: 100 µL of PBS, TiPs group: 100 µL of PBS containing 5 mg of TiPs, 4-OI group: 100 µL of PBS containing 25 mg/kg 4-OI, 4-OI & TiPs group: 100 µL of PBS containing 25 mg/kg 4-OI and 5 mg of TiPs, TiPs & oe-TFAM group: 100 µL of PBS containing 5 mg of TiPs with oe-TFAM stably-knockdown lentiviral suspensions, TiPs & sh-TFAM group: 100 µL of PBS containing 5 mg of TiPs with sh-TFAM stably-knockdown lentiviral suspensions, TiPs & sh-TFAM & 4-OI group: 100 µL of PBS containing 5 mg of TiPs with sh-TFAM stably-knockdown lentiviral suspensions and 25 mg/kg 4-OI (for in vivo TFAM modulation, a lentiviral suspension carrying either a TFAM-targeting shRNA or a TFAM-overexpression construct was administered via direct calvarial injection into C57BL/6 J mice). A sagittal incision about 15 mm long was made along the midline of the skull, and gelatin sponges (0.5*0.5*0.3 cm3) were implanted for localized injections according to group assignments. No complications or mortalities occurred during the 14-day postoperative period. The mice were sacrificed under anesthesia, their skulls were isolated, all soft tissues were removed for subsequent analysis. Data analysis was conducted on the skulls of mice in each group, including bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume (BV), and total volume (TV). After micro-CT scanning, the skull was decalcified, dehydrated and embedded for histological analysis. Detect the levels of IL-6 and TNF-α to assess the inflammatory response.

H&E staining

The sectioned tissues were first heated in a constant temperature chamber at 37 °C for 1 h, then transferred to a 60 °C chamber and heated for an additional hour. Subsequently, the sections were sequentially washed twice with xylene and anhydrous ethanol for 10min each, followed by washes with 90% ethanol, 80% ethanol, 70% ethanol, 60% ethanol, and PBS for 3–5 min each. The processed samples were then stained with hematoxylin staining solution from an H&E staining kit (C0105S, Beyotime) for 10 min, rinsed briefly in distilled water to remove excess stain, and blued by rinsing under running tap water for approximately 10 min. After a quick rinse in distilled water (a few seconds), the sections were stained with eosin staining solution for 1 min. They were then dehydrated in 70% ethanol, cleared, and mounted. Finally, the sections were observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX73), and quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Statistics

Experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0) and displayed in the form of mean ± SEM. All values were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the T-test, while comparisons among mulTiPsle groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Total joint replacement is the most effective method for treating end-stage bone and joint diseases. However, the average lifespan of prostheses is currently only 15–20 years, with aseptic loosening being the primary cause of long-term failure [2]. Postoperative sliding friction and micromotion between prostheses and bone generates wear particles [31]. Macrophages are the primary cells that recognize wear particles and initiate inflammatory responses, producing pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α, which then activate osteoclasts and induce bone resorption [32]. Among various wear particles, TiPs have the strongest stimulatory effect [33], thus inhibiting TiPs-induced macrophage inflammation is crucial for delaying periprosthetic bone dissolution.

Wear particles stimulate macrophages by activating pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as TLRs and NLRs to mediate the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Among these, IL-1β, along with IL-6 and TNF-α, serves as a pivotal mediator in the inflammatory cascade initiated by TiPs. While IL-6 and TNF-α act as early and broad markers of inflammation, the elevated secretion of IL-1β holds particular pathophysiological significance in the context of particle-induced osteolysis. Its maturation and release are tightly controlled by a two-step mechanism involving a priming signal and an activation signal, the latter typically mediated by the NLRP3 inflammasome. The significant upregulation of IL-1β observed in our previous in vivo and in vitro models underscores that TiPs not only initiate the inflammatory priming step but also effectively drive the crucial activation step, thereby provoking a potent and sustained inflammatory response that is highly destructive to bone tissue [34–36]. Recently, we have focused on mitochondrial metabolism in macrophages, a process that not only provides energy but also regulates inflammation [37]. Within this context, SDH in the TCA cycle serves as a critical node. Changes in SDH activity influence the succinate/α-ketoglutarate ratio, thereby affecting ROS release and HIF1-α expression and driving metabolic reprogramming in macrophages [11]. Itaconate, an important derivative of the TCA cycle, structurally resembles succinate and acts as a competitive inhibitor of SDH, suppressing its activity [18, 38]. Furthermore, itaconate can mediate a novel post-translational modification—itaconation—by modifying cysteine residues of target proteins to regulate immune function, such as inhibiting SYK or activating TFEB [12, 39]. Thus, we consider IRG1/ITA can modulate macrophage mitochondrial metabolism and repress downstream inflammatory pathways to alleviate TiPs-induced osteolysis by inhibiting SDH activity.

In this study, comparison of synovial tissues from patients with FHN and AL of prosthetic joints revealed significantly elevated expression of inflammatory factors in the AL group, suggesting the presence of clinical inflammatory injury. Integrating the enrichment of IRG1 in RNA-seq analysis of TiPs-induced macrophages and the established association of IRG1 with various inflammatory diseases, we hypothesized that IRG1 plays a critical role in TiPs-induced macrophage inflammation. Further investigation demonstrated that 4-OI, on one hand, reduces HIF1-α expression by inhibiting SDH activity, and on the other hand, regulates the activation of inflammatory factors by suppressing the STING pathway and inhibiting macrophage polarization toward the M1.

Further exploring the STING pathway, we investigated the role of mtDNA, a key inflammatory agonist. Once leaked into the cytoplasm, mtDNA is recognized as a mitochondrial DAMP, activating downstream inflammatory cascades [40]. TFAM, a DNA‑binding protein that stabilizes mtDNA and initiates its replication, has been shown to be crucial for mtDNA integrity; its deficiency leads to mtDNA instability and promotes cytosolic leakage, thereby inducing stress responses [41, 42]. Based on our observation that TiPs induce mtDNA leakage in macrophages, we hypothesized that 4‑OI might alleviate this process by upregulating TFAM expression. Experimental results confirmed that 4‑OI treatment increased TFAM expression in macrophages and correspondingly reduced mtDNA leakage, thus validating our hypothesis.

High levels of intracellular itaconate can undergo Michael addition to important cysteine residues on proteins via its electrophilic α,β-unsaturated carboxylic structure, a post-translational modification also known as "itaconylation" [17]. Given these findings, we asked whether itaconylation also occurs during 4-OI–mediated regulation of mtDNA release. Using the Cistrome Data Browser, we found STAT1 binds the upstream promoter sequence of TFAM, inhibiting TFAM transcription. Following established methods [43], we used ITalk to detect itaconate alkylation in macrophages and, as expected, observed itaconylation of STAT1. Furthermore, we generated cells with targeted knockout of STAT1 cysteine residues to abolish itaconation. The results showed that the effects of 4-OI were attenuated. Thus, we confirmed at the cellular level the regulatory effect of 4-OI on the inflammatory response of macrophages stimulated by wear particles. To further verify these findings, we established a mouse calvarial osteolysis model. Consistent with our previous research methods, injections of 4-OI, oe-TFAM lentivirus all reversed TiPs-induced cranial bone resorption and inflammation.

However, there are still some limitations in our research. First, it remains unclear whether 4-OI, an itaconate derivative, is fully hydrolyzed by cellular esterases into itaconate and can entirely recapitulate the functions of endogenous itaconate [44]. Studies have suggested that 4-OI acts similarly to endogenous itaconate [45, 46] and can further enhance the LPS-induced elevation of intracellular itaconate levels in macrophages [21]. While isotopic tracing would be required to definitively quantify the hydrolysis kinetics of 4-OI in our cellular model, its primary anti-inflammatory mechanism is attributed to its electrophilic property, which is shared with itaconate. Therefore, the observed suppression of macrophage inflammation and STING pathway activation aligns with the established biological role of the itaconate pathway, irrespective of whether 4-OI acts as a direct electrophile or a prodrug. Future studies using stable isotope-labeled 4-OI will help clarify its metabolic fate in the context of particle-induced inflammation. Second, while we preliminarily demonstrated the role of TFAM in regulating TiPs-induced inflammation and osteolysis by injecting TFAM-knockdown lentivirus into mouse calvariae, this approach lacks cellular specificity. Employing macrophage-specific conditional TFAM knockout models in future studies will provide more definitive evidence for the cell-autonomous function of TFAM in vivo and further elucidate its cell-type-specific mechanisms.

Although this study primarily focuses on deciphering the immunometabolic reprogramming of macrophages in response to TiPs, we recognize that periprosthetic osteolysis is a multicellular process involving osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and fibroblasts. While the present study does not directly investigate the effects of 4-OI on these mesenchymal cells, potential roles can be inferred based on existing literature and our discovered mechanisms. For instance, itaconate and its derivatives have been reported to exert antioxidant and osteoblast-protective effects via activation of the Nrf2 pathway [47]. In an inflammatory milieu, 4-OI may mitigate TNF-α or particle-induced osteoblast apoptosis through this mechanism, thereby potentially promoting bone formation. Furthermore, given that itaconate is a competitive inhibitor of succinate dehydrogenase, it may suppress osteoclast differentiation and function by altering cellular aerobic glycolysis, which warrants future in-depth investigation [48]. The formation of the periprosthetic fibrous membrane is primarily driven by fibroblasts. Evidence suggests that itaconate can inhibit the pro-fibrotic response and inflammatory cytokine production in fibroblasts [49]. Consequently, 4-OI might suppress aberrant tissue fibrosis under inflammatory conditions by modulating fibroblast activity, thereby creating a more favorable microenvironment for maintaining bone homeostasis. A limitation of this study is the lack of direct validation of the effects of 4-OI on these cell types in multicellular co-culture systems or in vivo models. The itaconation of STAT1 in macrophages we discovered may represent only one component of the complex network through which itaconate regulates osteolysis. Future studies will focus on elucidating these cellular interactions within more sophisticated co-culture systems and evaluating the comprehensive impact of 4-OI on overall bone homeostasis in animal models.

Finally, this study selected micrometer-sized TiPs as the model primarily based on the following considerations. On the one hand, within the Smith & Nephew SYNERGY prosthesis system under investigation, TiPs are the main bioactive component of its wear debris, which can be phagocytosed by macrophages and directly trigger inflammation. On the other hand, the early inflammatory and osteolytic signaling pathways activated by different metal wear particles are highly conserved, with the core mechanism relying on the physical activation following particle phagocytosis rather than on chemical differences. Therefore, pure TiPs are widely employed as a standard model for studying such common biological responses. Furthermore, the use of high-purity commercial TiPs helps to eliminate interference from trace elements present in alloys, thereby allowing a clearer elucidation of the function of the target metabolic‑immunological axis (IRG1-STAT1-TFAM). It should be noted that although clinical wear debris consists of a multi-scale mixture, this study focused on TiPs in the 1–10 µm range, as this size is most prone to inducing "frustrated phagocytosis"—a key mechanism driving chronic inflammation and osteolysis [50, 51]. We recognize that nanoparticles may exhibit distinct bioactivity and distribution profiles. Future studies comparing the effects of particles of different sizes will help further clarify size-specific impacts and the translational significance of our findings.

In summary, our study demonstrates that the IRG1/ITA alleviates TiPs-induced inflammation in macrophages through a dual mechanism. First, itaconate modulates SDH activity within the mitochondrial TCA cycle, thereby restraining inflammatory activation. Second, itaconate promotes TFAM expression via STAT1 itaconylation, which stabilizes mtDNA and reduces its cytosolic leakage, thus mitigating mitochondrial stress and downstream inflammatory responses. These findings reveal a coordinated immunometabolic reprogramming in particle-stimulated macrophages and suggest potential new avenues for preventing and treating aseptic loosening of artificial joints.
