Thyroid nodule treated by herbal food composite formulation: a case report
Authors: Yi You, Hui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThyroid nodules are a prevalent endocrine disorder with a global prevalence estimated to range from 5% to 67%. Conventional treatments, including surveillance, levothyroxine suppression therapy, and surgical resection, are often limited by side effects, invasiveness, and variable efficacy. To date, documented instances of spontaneous regression are exceedingly rare.Case presentationIn this case report, we present a 48-year-old Chinese Asian woman with bilateral thyroid nodules who experienced significant nodule regression after a 3.5-month intervention with herbal food composite formulation. The formula, comprising Aronia melanocarpa, Daucus carota, Apium graveolens, Citrus paradisi, and Litchi chinensis, was consumed as juice every other day post-lunch. The patient's ultrasound examination showed multiple mixed echogenic nodules in both thyroid lobes, with the largest nodule measuring 20.7 × 10.4 mm. After treatment, the nodules significantly decreased in size, and a lymph node in the left neck disappeared.ConclusionsThis case raises the possibility that the herbal food composite formulation may have potential value to be explored as an auxiliary therapy for thyroid nodules, warranting further investigation in larger patient populations.

Background

Thyroid nodules are a common clinical finding, with a global prevalence that varies depending on the detection method and study population, estimated to range from 5% to 67% [1]. In China, with the increasing popularity of routine health check-ups and the widespread use of high-resolution ultrasound, the detection rate of thyroid nodules is steadily rising. Relevant survey data show that the occurrence of thyroid nodules detected by high-resolution ultrasound in adults has increased from 19% to 68% [2]. Conventional management strategies include surveillance, levothyroxine suppression therapy, and surgical resection. However, these approaches are often limited by side effects, invasiveness, and variable efficacy [1]. To our knowledge, documented cases of spontaneous regression of thyroid nodules are scarce.

The World Health Organization (WHO) acknowledged the importance of prioritizing quality of life in situations, where standard healthcare services are unavailable, urging nations to delve into the realm of traditional medicine [3]. Among traditional medicines, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) was first involved in the International Classification of Diseases published by WHO. The therapeutic approaches in TCM, such as acupuncture and the use of herbal formulas, have been validated for their effectiveness [4]. Increasing bioactive compounds derived from herbs have been identified to be effective, such as artemisinin [5]. Ingredients such as Aronia melanocarpa and Daucus carota have demonstrated antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects in preclinical studies [6]. These properties are particularly relevant to thyroid nodules, where oxidative stress and chronic inflammation are thought to play key roles in pathogenesis [7].

Here, we report that an herbal food composite formulation comprising Aronia melanocarpa was associated with the regression of bilateral thyroid nodules in a 48-year-old female. To the best of our knowledge, this appears to be a preliminary observation of potential therapeutic benefit from a food-based formula for thyroid nodules. The intervention, comprising Aronia melanocarpa (fruit), Daucus carota (tuber root), Apium graveolens (stem and leaf), Citrus paradisi (flesh), and Litchi chinensis (flesh), was associated with significant nodule regression and resolution of cervical lymphadenopathy. The findings suggest a potential role for dietary interventions in the management of thyroid nodules, warranting further investigation.

Case report

A 48-year-old Chinese Asian woman presented with a 10-year history of thyroid nodules diagnosed during routine physical examinations, with persistent stability under follow-up monitoring. Despite regular ultrasound monitoring, no significant regression of the nodules had been observed. The patient had worked in marketing at a large company for over 20 years and had a habit of drinking alcohol, but not excessively. It was noted that her mother had a history of breast cancer. During a routine thyroid follow-up visit in September 2024, the patient expressed dissatisfaction with the lack of improvement in her nodule status and opted for dietary intervention instead of surgical treatment.

Initial diagnosis

The medical examination at baseline (November 18, 2019) confirmed euthyroid status (TSH 0.39 mIU L−1, FT4 46.15 µg mL−1, FT3 1.16 ng mL−1) and autoimmune markers (Tpo-Ab 4.70 IU mL−1, Tg-Ab < 1.00 IU mL−1) were all normal and kept stable in the examinations in the next 5 years. Grayscale and colour-Doppler ultrasound revealed normal thyroid size and echotexture; the capsule was intact. Multiple hypoechoic and isoechoic nodules were present in both lobes, the largest measuring 14 × 7 mm in the right lobe and 13 × 6 mm in the left lobe. All nodules showed well-defined margins and no abnormal intra-nodular vascularity on CDFI (Fig. 1). On September 5, 2024, the patient underwent an ultrasound examination of the thyroid nodules at Longhua Hospital Affiliated to the Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. According to the ultrasound report, the size and shape of both thyroid lobes were normal, with uniform echogenicity. Color Doppler flow imaging showed no abnormalities, but multiple mixed echogenic nodules were present within both thyroid lobes. The largest nodule on the right side measured 16.3 × 10 mm, and the largest nodule on the left side measured 20.7 × 10.4 mm. The internal echogenicity was non-uniform, with some areas showing calcification. According to the TI-RADS (Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System) classification, these nodules were categorized as Class III (Fig. 2, left). No abnormal findings were noted in other systems, such as cardiovascular, respiratory, and gastrointestinal. There were no diagnostic challenges: the patient had no language barriers, and ultrasound costs were covered by routine health insurance. Prognostically, TI-RADS III classification indicated a favorable benign course, consistent with her 10-year stable nodule history.

Intervention

Over the past 10 years, the patient had been troubled by the lack of improvement in the follow-up results of the thyroid nodules. After learning about herbal food composite formulation, she refused surgical treatment and opted for dietary intervention. The ingredients were homogenized and consumed as juice every other day post-lunch for 3.5 months, with no concomitant pharmacotherapy or lifestyle modifications. No adverse gastrointestinal or allergic reactions were reported during treatment.

Follow-up and outcomes

Three and a half months later, the patient returned to the same hospital for a follow-up. The result showed unchanged euthyroid status with negative Tpo-Ab and Tg-Ab, stable adequate iodine excretion, no weight change (−0.2 kg) and no new medication, stress or dietary confounders. Surprisingly, the thyroid nodules had significantly decreased in size. Notably, the nodule on the right side reduced from 16.3 × 10 mm to 8 × 5 mm, which was a significant improvement. The largest nodule on the left side measured 18 × 11 mm, slightly smaller than the previous 20.7 × 10.4 mm, and no calcification was observed. In addition, a 16 × 7 mm lymph node in the left neck was no longer detectable, indicating a possible resolution of inflammation (Fig. 2, right). After this examination, the patient discontinued the regimen and planned to resume during the spring and summer, with scheduled annual ultrasonographic monitoring.

Conclusions and discussion

The national epidemiological survey project [8] on thyroid diseases, iodine nutrition, and diabetes in China found that the prevalence of thyroid nodules (diameter greater than 0.5 cm) in adults (> 18 years) is 20.43%. As one of the most common clinical findings of the endocrine system, approximately 5% of thyroid nodules are malignant, and 10–30% are indeterminate or suspicious for malignancy [9]. The epidemiological characteristics of thyroid nodules exhibit significant geographical differences, gender preferences, and age dependence. Due to the regulatory effects of estrogen on thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) receptors and angiogenesis, the incidence in females is approximately 2-4 times higher than in males, with a peak age range of 40–60 years [10]. In addition, in high-iodine regions such as the coastal areas of China, the prevalence is even higher (approximately 35–40%) [11]. Current treatment options include observation and follow-up, levothyroxine (LT4) suppression therapy, radioactive iodine (131I) ablation, and surgical resection. While levothyroxine therapy is widely used, its effectiveness in reducing nodule size is limited, and it may lead to subclinical hyperthyroidism and bone loss [12]. Radioactive iodine therapy is effective for functional nodules, but it may induce hypothyroidism in 20–30% of cases [13]. Surgery remains the definitive treatment for malignant or compressive nodules, but the incidence of postoperative complications (such as hypocalcemia and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury) is 5–10% [14].

The formulation of this herbal food composite may align with TCM approaches to addressing endocrine gland dysfunction, a framework that focuses on restoring balanced regulatory function of the body's hormonal systems. Notably, this composite contains several amine-related metabolites with documented bioactivity, including feruloylputrescine, dopamine, spermidine, and nicotinamide. It is hypothesized that these compounds could influence key pathways in thyroid gland pathophysiology: dopamine may modulate hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid signaling; spermidine and nicotinamide might help mitigate oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α); and feruloylputrescine could potentially reduce local tissue inflammation. This hypothetical mechanism, while offering a biologically plausible explanation for the observed effects, requires further verification in experimental models and clinical studies.

In the present case, the regression of nodules and resolution of lymphadenopathy may be associated with the bioactive components in the herbal formula. Aronia melanocarpa is rich in polyphenols, which exhibit potent antioxidant activity by scavenging free radicals implicated in thyroid nodule pathogenesis [15]. Daucus carota contains falcarinol, a polyacetylene shown to suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6 and TNF-α) in vitro [16]. Apium graveolens provides feruloylputrescine and trace dopamine, and its extracts have been shown to inhibit COX-1/2 and TNF-α/IL-6 release [17]. Citrus paradisi supplies spermidine and nicotinamide that induce autophagy and activate NAD⁺-SIRT1 signaling [18]. Litchi chinensis pulp contributes nicotinamide and low polyphenols that scavenge ROS and suppress NF-κB [19]. While these properties offer a plausible biological rationale, they remain speculative in the context of this single case and require confirmation through further study.

This single case report has several inherent limitations that necessitate extreme caution in interpreting the findings. First, as a single case, the findings are not generalizable. Second, and most critically, a causal relationship between the herbal food intervention and the observed nodule regression cannot be definitively established. The reduction in size could plausibly be attributed to natural variability in nodule size over time, differences in ultrasound measurement techniques or interpretation between the two timepoints, or even spontaneous fluctuation, which, while rare, cannot be ruled out. Third, the short intervention period (3.5 months) and absence of long-term follow-up preclude conclusions about sustained efficacy. Finally, the proposed mechanisms of action are speculative and based on extrapolation from preclinical studies, not on data derived from this patient.

In conclusion, these preliminary observational findings highlight the potential therapeutic promise of multicomponent herbal extracts, with multi-target properties, including antioxidant and immunomodulatory effects, as adjunctive agents to conventional regimens for benign thyroid lesions. However, this observation must be interpreted with the utmost caution, and further rigorous, well-designed clinical studies are essential to validate these preliminary findings, elucidate the underlying mechanisms of action, and assess their applicability in larger and more diverse patient populations.
