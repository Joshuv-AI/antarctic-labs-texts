Risk factors for mucormycosis among diabetic individuals, Bathinda, Punjab: a case-control study
Authors: Gopi Kumbha, Michi Monya, Sushma Choudhary, Gagandeep Singh Grover, Usha Goyal, Rakesh Kakkar, Vaibhav Saini, Vikasdeep Gupta, Anuradha Raj, Soumya Swaroop Sahoo, Sivanantham Krishnamoorthi, Tanzin Dikid

Background

Mucormycosis is a rare but severe fungal infection caused by a group of fungi called mucormycetes and primarily affects immunocompromised individuals or those with underlying conditions [1]. The disease gained significant attention during the COVID-19 pandemic, as it emerged as a severe complication in some instances compared to COVID-19 itself, alongside other opportunistic fungal infections [2]. The global incidence of mucormycosis ranges from 0.0005 to 1.7 cases per million population. In India, the estimated prevalence is 140 cases per million population, approximately 80 times higher than that in developed nations, equating to 0.14 cases per 1,000 individuals [1, 3]. Despite the availability of standard treatment, mucormycosis is associated with a high mortality rate, approximately 50%, which increases to over 90% in cases of disseminated disease [4].

Mucorales fungi are ubiquitous and thermotolerant, with infection occurring via ingestion of contaminated food, inhalation of spores, or direct inoculation through disrupted skin or wounds [5]. These fungi are commonly found in decaying organic matter, indoor and outdoor dust. Air conditioning systems, particularly air filters, have also been identified as potential sources of exposure contributing to mucormycosis infections [6–9].

The primary predisposing factors for mucormycosis include diabetes mellitus (DM), haematological malignancies, other types of cancer, solid organ or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, intravenous drug use, prolonged neutropenia, corticosteroid use, trauma, iron overload, neonatal prematurity, advanced age, and malnutrition [1]. Among these, diabetes mellitus is notably linked to a significant proportion of mucormycosis cases. Specifically, in COVID-19-associated mucormycosis, pre-existing or underlying diabetes has been reported in 62–80% of cases, with poor glycaemic control observed in 41–97% of the affected individuals [10, 11].

In November 2023, the Media Scanning and Verification Cell of the Integrated Disease Surveillance Program (IDSP), Delhi, issued a media alert reporting an unexpected surge in mucormycosis hospital admissions at AIIMS Bathinda, Punjab. Following this alert, a detailed investigation was conducted, including a descriptive epidemiological analysis of mucormycosis cases reported at AIIMS Bathinda from January to November 2023. In our descriptive analysis, diabetes was present in 70% (28/40) of mucormycosis cases. Furthermore, 50% of the cases reported additional stress events, such as hospitalisations, surgeries, dental procedures, or trauma, within the preceding six weeks of illness onset. AIIMS Bathinda, a tertiary care health facility providing treatment for mucormycosis, experienced a notable surge in mucormycosis case presentations following increased referrals from neighbouring states, including Chandigarh, Rajasthan, and Haryana. Historical data from 2022 to 2023 for this tertiary care facility show the observed numbers did not exceed the established threshold (mean ± 2 standard deviations). The role of stress-related events as a risk factor for mucormycosis among non-COVID-19 cases is still not yet well established. To address this, we proposed an investigation to evaluate the association between stress events and the occurrence of mucormycosis in individuals with underlying diabetes.

Methods

Study design and setting

An unmatched case-control study was conducted at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), Bathinda, Punjab, while case diagnosis spanned January 2023 to June 2024, recruitment and data collection occurred from July to October 2024. AIIMS Bathinda serves as a major referral centre for both urban and rural populations in the Malwa region of Punjab, providing comprehensive medical care. The hospital provides a treatment option for mucormycosis patients. The study population included patients with more than one year of diabetes attending this tertiary care hospital, which caters to a diverse patient population (Punjab, Rajasthan, Haryana).

A case was defined as a diabetic individual diagnosed with mucormycosis by clinical presentation, microbiological confirmation (KOH/Cacoflour white microscopy), and imaging (CT scan) at AIIMS Bathinda between January 2023 and June 2024. Controls were diabetics attending the endocrinology outpatient department (OPD) of the same hospital who did not have mucormycosis between July and October 2024. Autoimmune disorders were not used as exclusion criteria, and patients with underlying autoimmune diseases were retained in the study population. Stress events were defined as hospitalisations, surgeries, dental procedures, or trauma within the six weeks before symptom onset in cases and within the six weeks before enrolment in controls. Hospitalisation: Hospital admission with any condition for at least 24 h, Surgeries: Any procedure involving the administration of local or general anaesthesia, Dental procedures: Any procedure involving a breach in the buccal mucosa and Trauma: Any physical injury (abrasions, lacerations) involving a violation of the dermis.

The sample size was calculated for a case–control study, assuming a 50% prevalence of stress events among cases (based on a descriptive study conducted in Bathinda in November 2023). Since published evidence specifically reporting the prevalence of acute stress events (trauma, surgery, or hospitalization) among diabetic controls was limited, a prevalence estimate of 25% was assumed for the control group. This assumption was supported by evidence indicating increased hospitalization burden among diabetic patients. Tomlin et al. [12] reported that approximately 22% of diabetic patients required hospitalization annually. A 3:1 control-to-case ratio was used to achieve 80% power at a 95% confidence level. However, the final enrolment included 102 controls and 36 cases (approximately 2.8:1) based on the feasibility and availability of participants during the study period. Case selection: A list of mucormycosis cases admitted to the ENT department at AIIMS Bathinda between January 2023 and June 2024 was obtained. Diabetic cases were identified using medical records, and those who provided informed consent were included in the study. Control selection: Controls were selected from diabetic individuals attending the endocrinology outpatient department (OPD) at AIIMS Bathinda in July and October 2024. Convenience sampling was used to select the controls. After thorough controls were enrolled following case identification, both groups were drawn from the same source population at AIIMS Bathinda. The key exposures were assessed within a defined recall period of six weeks, thereby minimising potential temporal or survival bias.

Data collection

The selected cases were contacted using contact details available in medical records. Informed consent and appointments were obtained prior to the interviews. Cases were interviewed in person, and controls were interviewed in person at the endocrinology outpatient department (OPD).

A semi-structured questionnaire was used to collect information on sociodemographic characteristics, clinical (for cases), and potential risk factors, including pre-existing comorbidities, recent stress events, COVID-19 infection, environmental exposures, and health-seeking behaviour. The questionnaire was developed by the research team based on the study objectives and relevant literature. It was pre-tested prior to data collection to ensure clarity, relevance and appropriateness. The English version of the questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.

To ensure data quality and reliability, all interviewers underwent training on the study objectives, questionnaire content and standardised interviewing procedure. Interviews were conducted using a uniform protocol to minimise interviewer bias. The investigators regularly supervised and cross-checked completed questionnaires to ensure completeness and consistency. Data were collected using the Epi-collect5 (version 3.0.3) tool for questionnaire administration. The tool included in-built validation checks to minimise data entry errors. In addition, periodic data review and cross-validation were performed to identify and correct inconsistencies or missing information, thereby ensuring overall data quality.

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. Additionally, the STROBE checklist is provided as Supplementary File 2.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using Epi Info (V 2.6.0). Continuous variables were summarised as median (range) and categorical variables as frequencies and proportions.

Categorical variables were defined such as age (18–30, 31–45, 46–60, and > 61 years), sex (male/female), education (illiterate/literate), occupation (farmer, construction worker, homemaker, others), place of residence (rural/urban), body mass index (BMI) (≤ 18.5, 18.5–22.9, 23–24.9, and ≥ 25 kg/m²), BG prasad scale was used for socioeconomic status (upper, upper middle, middle, lower middle, lower); comorbidity (diabetes alone, diabetes with one comorbidity, diabetes with ≥ 2 comorbidities); with a derived variable for soil and dust exposure (yes/no) and exposure variables (e.g., cattle handling, earthen pot use, stress events) categorized as yes/no.

Univariate analysis was performed to assess the associations between exposure factors and mucormycosis. Crude odds ratios (cORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for potential risk factors. Multivariate logistic regression identified independent mucormycosis predictors. In addition to the categorical BMI analysis, sensitivity analysis was performed by treating BMI as a continuous variable in the regression models to assess the robustness of the observed associations.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the National Centre for Disease Control Ethics Review Committee (NCDC-ERC), New Delhi, India (Approval No. NCDC/NERC/2024/Research Protocol/003) on July 31, 2024. Participant consent: Written informed consent was obtained from all participants included in the study. Participants were volunteers and informed about the study objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits before enrolment. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/ or national research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study adhered to the ethical guidelines, ensuring participant confidentiality and voluntary participation.

Results

A total of 36 individuals were diagnosed with Mucormycosis between January 2023 and June 2024, with 102 after screening identified for comparison. The sociodemographic characteristics of both groups were analysed. The median age (interquartile range, IQR) was 54 years (45–61 years) among cases and 52 years (43–61 years) among controls. Males accounted for cases compared to controls (81% vs. 55%), and 75% of cases and 63% of controls have resided in rural areas. Regarding occupation, 39% of the cases were farmers, while the most common occupation among controls was homemakers (39%). The Body Mass Index (BMI) of 18.6–22.9 kg/m2 was observed in 44% of cases and 20% of controls, overweight (23–24.9 kg/m2) 28% vs. 18%, and obesity (≥ 25 kg/m2) 8% vs. 60%. Diabetes without any other comorbidity was present in 61% of cases and 51% of controls. A prior diagnosis of COVID-19 was reported in 11% of both cases and controls (Table 1).

On univariate analysis, the presence of acute stress events (OR = 4.0; 95%CI = 1.6–9.6), hospitalization (OR = 4.5; 95%CI = 1.5–13.2), dental procedures (OR = 5.3; 95%CI = 1.2–23.5), and using an earthen pot for drinking water storage (OR = 5.4; 95%CI = 2.3–12.2) were significant risk factors for the disease. Moreover, treating drinking water (reverse osmosis/boiling) before consumption (OR = 0.45; 95%CI = 0.2–0.99) was found to be protective. Those involved in cattle handling activities like handling cow dung, cattle fodder, bedding (cattle housing), and composting were at risk (OR = 6.2; 95%CI = 2.6–14.9). The use of an air conditioner at home and health-seeking preference for quacks and traditional healers were protective (Table 4).

Discussion

This study identified that individuals diagnosed with mucormycosis were more likely to be male, experience stress events, and engage in occupations involving cattle handling and the use of earthen pots for drinking water storage, which were significant risk factors for illness. Clinical presentations included headache, facial swelling, and vision disturbances, with rhino-orbital-cerebral involvement consistent with that documented in the literature [11]. In a study by John M T et al., the most common presentations of mucormycosis were Rhino-orbital (17/41) and rhino-orbito-cerebral (11/27), which were reported [11] . Similarly, ROC mucormycosis was the predominant presentation in our study. Additionally, our study highlights the role of earthen pot usage in rural diabetics as an underexplored risk factor. These findings suggest that, in addition to established clinical patterns, environmental and behavioural exposures may play an important role in disease occurrence in this population.

In the current study, findings are likely due to cultural norms that predominantly involve men in soil-related occupations, such as agriculture and construction, which increase their exposure to Mucorales spores. This male predilection may be attributed to higher occupational risks in these work settings, resulting in greater environmental exposure to fungal pathogens [13]. The study found occupational exposure as a significant risk factor for mucormycosis among individuals with diabetes. Occupations involving direct contact with soil, manure, or organic matter, such as farming, labouring, or cattle handling, substantially increase the odds of infection. Soil and organic materials are natural reservoirs of Mucorales spores, which thrive in decomposing organic matter. Diabetic individuals in these occupations will likely encounter these spores regularly, which poses a unique risk due to their impaired immune defences [14].

In the current study, the Rhino-Orbital-Cerebral (ROC) form of the disease was the most common type among our cases, which may be due to inhalational exposure, proximity to nasal sinuses in diabetic ketoacidosis, consistent with findings from other studies [15, 16]. This type of mucormycosis is most commonly seen in patients with diabetes mellitus [4, 17–20]. The presence of comorbidities other than diabetes was not found to be associated with mucormycosis. Uncontrolled diabetes has been documented as a risk for mucormycosis [4, 15, 21, 22]. Infection is considered rare in patients with well-controlled blood glucose levels [22]. Consistently, diabetes self-care practices, including regular health center visits for diabetes check-ups and daily diabetic foot care, were found to be significantly protective against the development of mucormycosis in the current study.

Diabetes mellitus is the most commonly reported risk factor, especially when poorly controlled, as hyperglycaemia and acidosis create a favourable environment for fungal growth and impair neutrophil function [23]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, a surge in mucormycosis cases was observed, particularly among patients with COVID-19-associated comorbidities, such as diabetes and corticosteroid use, highlighting the synergistic effect of multiple risk factors. Thus, the cumulative burden of co-morbidities significantly elevates the risk of developing mucormycosis [24].

Although underweight individuals showed a higher crude risk of mucormycosis, this association was not statistically significant after adjustment in multivariate analysis. Similarly, obesity was not identified as an independent risk factor in our study. Notably, the control group showed a disproportionately high prevalence of obesity, likely reflecting sampling bias rather than a protective effect. Underweight or overweight individuals often exhibit malnutrition, which weakens the immune system and impairs the body’s ability to combat infections, including fungal pathogens like Mucorales. The published literature suggests that both under- and overweight states impair immunity. According to the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), individuals with compromised immunity, such as those with uncontrolled diabetes or malnutrition, are more susceptible to fungal infections. Mucormycosis thrives in immunocompromised environments, and insufficient nutritional reserves in underweight or overweight individuals can exacerbate vulnerability, facilitating its growth and spread. Therefore, maintaining a healthy BMI is crucial in reducing the risk of mucormycosis, especially in high-risk populations [25].

In particular, activities such as cattle handling, including cleaning cattle sheds, accumulating cow dung for manure, or managing fodder, may facilitate higher fungal spore exposure. Organic materials such as cow dung and decaying bedding are ideal growth substrates for Mucorales. This proximity to fungal habitats, combined with the immune compromise inherent in diabetes, heightens the risk of inhaling or coming into contact with fungal spores, potentially leading to infection. Soil acts as a natural reservoir for mucorales, and the fungi can spread through inhalation and via ingestion of contaminated food or water. Earthen pots, commonly used for storage, create a conducive environment for fungal growth due to their porous nature and ability to retain moisture, which supports the survival and proliferation of fungal spores. Ingested spores may colonise the gastrointestinal tract, particularly in immunocompromised individuals, such as those with diabetes [14].

The current study also highlights that stressor events such as surgery, trauma, dental procedures, or hospitalisations markedly increase the risk of mucormycosis in diabetic patients. These events exacerbate the already compromised immune state of individuals with diabetes by inducing systemic stress, impairing wound healing, or causing tissue damage. For instance, surgical wounds or trauma can act as portals of entry for fungal pathogens, while prolonged hospitalisations may expose individuals to healthcare-associated fungal infections. Breach of the skin or mucosal barrier due to puncture wounds, including surgical wounds and injection drug use, has been identified as a risk factor for mucormycosis [24, 25]. The potential routes of mucorales spore transmission correspond with these acute conditions, reinforcing the possibility of an association [2].

Additionally, diabetes itself is associated with microvascular complications, delayed wound healing, and reduced phagocytic activity, which further amplifies the risk [26]. Stressor events act as a catalyst, tipping the balance in favour of fungal invasion and disease progression. Recognising the role of such events is critical in identifying high-risk individuals and implementing preventive strategies during perioperative care and post-trauma management [27].

Limitations

This study has several limitations, first there is a potential for recall bias due to the long interval between mucormycosis diagnosis and data collection, which may affect the reporting of stress-related events. Second, selection bias may have occurred, as cases were diagnosed using clinical, microbiological, and imaging (CT scan) criteria, whereas controls were not subjected to imaging, potentially allowing for undetected subclinical mucormycosis. Additionally, the use of convenience sampling for controls and recruitment from a different source population than cases may have introduced selection bias. The restriction of the study to diabetic individuals limited the ability to assess other risk factors and reduced generalizability. The relatively small sample size further limits the generalizability of the findings. Finally, the absence of environmental sampling limits the ability to establish direct links between occupational or household exposures and mucormycosis risk.

Conclusion

The study found that recent acute stress events were associated with higher odds of mucormycosis among individuals with diabetes. Associations were also observed with hospitalisation, dental procedures, and certain occupational exposures, including cattle handling, as well as the use of earthen pots for water storage. Preventive measures such as promoting safe handling practices during cattle handling and improving hygienic water storage may be considered, particularly among high-risk populations. Increasing clinicians’ awareness of potential risk factors and strengthening health education in rural settings may also be beneficial. Future prospective studies, including environmental assessments, are needed to better understand these associations.
