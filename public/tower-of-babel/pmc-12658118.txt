CT-based intratumoral heterogeneity quantification fusing deep learning radiomics for predicting lymph node metastasis in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma: a multicenter study
Authors: Min Hu, Xingyu Hu, Wenxiang Chen, Chengcheng Xia, Weirong Yao, Minjing Zuo, Ze Lin, Libin Deng, Zhaoqiu Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis multicenter study aims to quantified CT-based intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH) and provide a novel valuable dimension for deep learning radiomics in predicting lymph node metastases (LNM) in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). Data from 787 early-stage LUAD patients who underwent surgery were retrospectively analyzed across three centers. By unsupervised clustering CT tumor 2D subregions, the ITHscore was generated. A fusion predictive model incorporating the deep learning features, Radscore and ITHscore was developed and validated. ITHscore was an important predictor achieved AUCs of 0.813, 0.807, 0.718 in test sets, respectively. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis of the test dataset D revealed the epithelial-mesenchymal transition pathway as the top enriched gene signature (NES = 1.71, p < 0.001) in the high ITHscore group. The fusion model demonstrated the best prediction performance, with AUCs of 0.832, 0.884, and 0.815 in test sets. Correlation and interpretability analyses confirmed that these three feature sets from different dimensions have a good synergistic effect in capturing imaging phenotypes. The ITHscore can be used as a novel imaging biomarker for predicting LNM in early-stage LUAD. Introduction

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the frequent and intricate subtype of Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)1,2. Lymph node metastasis (LNM) often indicates poor disease prognosis3. Even after extensive pretreatment scans, including routine PET scans, brain imaging, and selective assessment of invasive lymph nodes, over 10% of patients with T1aN0 still have occult lymph node metastasis detected during surgery4,5. Assessing LNM in early-stage LUAD preoperatively is essential for accurate staging, determination of surgical scope and prognosis evaluation6.

The advantage of radiomics is to obtain the high-throughput image features extracted from radiographic images noninvasively7. Image features have become important predictors to characterize lung cancer8–10. In recent years, more studies have combined deep learning (DL) with radiomics to form a new innovative model, which makes it possible to explore image predictors from multiple modalities to improve the prediction ability of models11–13.

However, the main challenge of deep learning and radiomics comes from the mining and fusion of multimodal information in images14. Radiomics features extracted from subregions provide new insights to this problem15–17. Some studies18,19 generated quantifiable Intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH) based on subregion radiomics features, demonstrating good predictive value. The studies of Li et al.20 and Zhang et al.21 demonstrated the important role of 2D radiomics features in revealing ITH of NSCLC. Therefore, it occurred to us that quantifying ITH based on subregional 2D radiomics features may provide another promising image feature.

In this study, we generated a quantifiable ITHscore based on the CT subregional 2D radiomics features of early lung adenocarcinoma, then fused the ITHscore, radiomics features and DL features to develop a predictive model. The aim of this study is to confirm ITHscore can be used a novel imaging biomarker, which can improve the precision and reliability of deep learning radiomics fusion model.

Materials and methods

Study sample

This retrospective multicenter study was approved by the ethics committees of Jiangxi Provincial People’s Hospital and Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, with patient consent waived. Public database data were exempt from consent requirements. We confirm that all methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study dataset included early-stage LUAD patients who underwent radical surgery and systematic lymphadenectomy at Jiangxi Provincial People’s Hospital (center 1) between January 2018 and December 2023, and at Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (center 2) between January 2020 and December 2022. Additionally, the R01 dataset from The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) was utilized22. Inclusion criteria were: (a) pathologically confirmed primary invasive LUAD (≤ 4 cm) without distant metastasis; (b) complete preoperative CT imaging data; (c) no prior chemotherapy or radiotherapy; and (d) lymphadenectomy results confirming lymph node metastasis status. Exclusion criteria were: (a) history of prior malignancies; (b) presence of non-LUAD components; (c) multiple nodules; (d) incomplete clinicopathologic information; and (e) poor-quality CT scans (Fig. 1). N0 patients were defined as those without LNM, while N + patients had LNM.

Patient exclusion process for each dataset.

CT image preprocessing

CT acquisition parameters are provided in the Supplementary Material. Firstly, B-spline interpolation was used to resample the voxels to 1 × 1 × 1.25 mm³. The tumor region of interest (ROI) was semi-automatically delineated on the resampled images by a single radiologist (4 years of experience) who was blinded to pathological reports, using 3D-Slicer (v5.4.0).

To standardize intensity, HU values within the ROI were clipped to the range − 700 to 400 and linearly mapped to 0–255. For each patient, to simultaneously retain the most representative tumor features and suppress irrelevant background, only the three axial slices with the largest ROI were chosen. These slices were then tightly cropped to a 64 × 64 pixel patch centered on the tumor centroid, deliberately reducing image size so that downstream processing demands less memory and lower computational overhead without sacrificing diagnostic information. These patches were resized to 256 × 256 pixels via bilinear interpolation, center-cropped to 224 × 224, and then normalized to a scale from 0 to 1. Finally, the images were inputted into the deep learning architecture for feature extraction. A flowchart of the overall pipeline is shown in Fig. 2.

The workflow of this study. The process began with the input of CT images, which were first subjected to image preprocessing. This was followed by feature extraction, encompassing deep learning features (red), ITH features (blue), and Radiomics features (yellow). The extracted features were then screened to retain only the most representative and relevant ones. Subsequently, multiple predictive models were constructed using the XGBoost classifier. The performance of each model was comprehensively evaluated to determine their effectiveness and reliability. Feature generation and dimensionality reduction

For Radiomics features, 107 three-dimensional features were extracted from the entire tumor region using PyRadiomics (version 3.0.1; https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io)23, following the guidelines of the Imaging Biomarker Standardization Initiative24. Intra-and inter-observer reproducibility of Radiomics features was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), and features with an ICC below 0.75 were considered of poor agreement and therefore excluded25.

ITH features were extracted by clustering the intratumoral subregions (Fig. 2). To objectively extract subregions within the tumor, simple linear iterative clustering (SLIC) was applied to the ROI of each CT slice. Details can be found in the Supplementary. A total of 101 two-dimensional features were extracted from the intratumoral subregions using PyRadiomics, Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) were employed to cluster each subregion. The optimal number of clusters for each feature was determined unbiasedly using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with clustering ranging from 1 to 5. The number of clusters for each patient was then used as an ITH feature vector to measure ITH.

Three axial slices of the maximum diameter of the tumor were input into five ImageNet pre-trained networks (ResNet18, ResNet34, ResNet50, DenseNet121, Inception V3). After removing the original classification head, the output of the global average pooling layer was extracted as a feature vector for each slice. Subsequently, an XGBoost classifier was trained on these features for LNM classification, and the prediction performance of the five networks was compared. The network with the highest performance was ultimately retained as the official feature extractor. To better characterize the tumor, two DL feature sets were generated by taking the mean value (Mean_value) and the maximum value (Max_value). By comparing with the DL feature set of the maximum slice (Max_slice), the representative and robust DL feature set were selected for subsequent research.

Feature dimension reduction involved removing low-variance features (< 75%), comparing features with t-tests/Mann-Whitney U tests, recursive feature elimination, and eliminating highly correlated features (VIF > 5). 6 Radiomic features, 8 ITH features, and 10 DL features were ultimately screened out, respectively.

Development of predictive models

Three single-modality models were established using Radiomics features (Radiomics model), ITH features (ITH model), Deep learning features (Deep_R model). The output probability of Radiomics model and ITH model were used to generate Radscore and ITHscore, respectively. These two scores were used to build Radscore-ITHscore model and combined with DL features to develop a fusion model. Additionally, two hybrid models—Deep-Radscore and Deep-ITHscore—were constructed by respectively concatenating deep-learning features with Radscore and ITHscore. In constructing the Radiomics model, the performance of five different machine learning algorithms (Support vector machine, Decision tree, Random forest, k-Nearest Neighbors, and XGBoost.) was compared, ultimately selecting the XGBoost library (version 2.0.2) in the Python environment for building these seven predictive models. All models were tuned by grid search with five-fold cross-validation; optimal settings are in Supplementary Table S1. The analysis codes are available in a GitHub repository (https://github.com/ZqWu25/DLR_CTITH_LNMLung).

Correlation and model interpretation

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

To verify the biological significance of the produced ITHscore, GSEA26 was conducted on the dataset D. The optimal cut-off value of the ITHscore was determined by the maximum Youden’s J statistic (0.430) of the ITH model, resulting in a binary ITH index (high or low). Tumors were first categorized into high and low ITH groups based on the ITH index, and differentially expressed genes between these groups were identified using the R package DESeq227. The differentially expressed genes were then ranked based on log2 fold change, and GSEA was performed using the R package clusterProfiler28. Gene sets with a p-value less than 0.05 from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB)29 were considered significantly enriched.

Correlation and model interpretation

A heatmap was employed to visually illustrate the relationship between Radscore, ITHscore, DL features, and lymph node status. The Pearson correlation coefficient was applied to assess the correlations between Radscore, ITHscore, and 10 DL features. Furthermore, the contribution of each feature to the fusion model’s predictions was evaluated using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis30. SHAP quantifies the predictive importance of each feature at the individual patient level, thereby enhancing the interpretability of the model’s predictions.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables and categorical variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and frequencies with percentages, respectively. Model performance for predicting LNM was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy were calculated based on the cutoff value identified by the maximum Youden’s J statistic. The Delong test was used to compare the AUCs. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.0; R Foundation) and Python (version 3.10) following the methods outlined by Hu and Chen. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 indicates statistical significance.

Results

Patient clinical characteristics

A total of 524 patients were included from the center 1, which was stratified randomly divided in an 8:2 ratio into dataset A (training set, n = 418, median age: 61 years, IQR: 53.0–67.0 years) and dataset B (internal test set, n = 106, median age: 59 years, IQR: 53.0–65.0 years). The dataset C from center 2 (n = 200, median age: 60 years, IQR: 53.0–66.0 years) and the dataset D (n = 63, median age: 69 years, IQR: 62.5–76.5 years) were used as external test sets. The dataset D, sourced from TCIA, lacked certain clinical data. A total of 130 patients had LNM, including 69 in dataset A, 18 in dataset B, 34 in the dataset C, and 9 in the dataset D (Table 1).

Underlying DL architecture selection

The specific results of the models built from features extracted by the five network architectures are shown in Table 2. The experimental results indicate that ResNet18 performed optimally on the dataset B, with an AUC value reaching 0.836, which is higher than that of other models (ResNet34: 0.754, ResNet50: 0.747, DenseNet121: 0.816, Inception V3: 0.782). In addition, ResNet18 also demonstrated strong generalization ability on the dataset C and the dataset D, with AUC values of 0.766 and 0.793 respectively. Although ResNet34 and DenseNet121 achieved slightly higher AUC values on the dataset C (0.811 and 0.791 respectively), their performance on the dataset D was poor (AUC values of 0.665 and 0.708 respectively). ResNet18 provided more reliable and stable results across all datasets. Considering the balanced performance and generalization ability of the models, we ultimately selected ResNet18 as the backbone network for DL feature extraction.

DL feature fusion method selection

We conducted a comparative analysis of the performance of the features obtained by the three methods, Max_slide, Mean_value, and Max_value, on four datasets (see Table 3). The experimental results show that the Mean_value method performs stably on the dataset B (0.833) and the independent external validation set dataset C with a relatively large data volume (0.834), indicating its strong generalization ability. Although its performance on the dataset D is slightly lower (0.747), its overall performance is still better than that of other models. Considering the performance of each dataset comprehensively, we choose the Mean_value method as the final solution to support the reliability and generalization ability of subsequent research.

Model performance evaluation

The predictive performance of the Radiomics models developed using five machine-learning algorithms is summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Among these classifiers, XGBoost demonstrated the best overall performance and was selected as the optimal algorithm for all subsequent model development. As can be seen from the ROC curves in Fig. 3, the ITH model exhibited slightly higher prediction performance than the Radiomics model across the dataset B, dataset C, and dataset D, with AUCs of 0.813, 0.807, and 0.718 compared to 0.812, 0.803, and 0.713, respectively. The Deep_R model built using the Mean_value method achieved AUCs of 0.833, 0.834 and 0.747. Deep-Radscore model yielded AUCs of 0.822, 0.869 and 0.767 across datasets B, C and D, whereas Deep-ITHscore model achieved 0.831, 0.860 and 0.809, respectively. All hybrid models outperformed the single-modality Radiomics and ITH models. The fusion model outperformed all other models, achieved AUCs of 0.832, 0.884, and 0.815. Specifically, in the dataset C, its AUC of 0.884 was significantly higher than the ITH model’s AUC of 0.807 (p = 0.02). Other performance metrics, including the 95% CI of AUC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV and accuracy, are presented in Supplementary Table S3. Correlation and model interpretation

Patients with high Radcore and high ITHscore tend to have LNM, and DL features also show an association with lymph node status (Fig. 5a). As shown in Fig. 5b, the correlations between Radscore, ITHscore and 10 DL features were weak, with all absolute correlation coefficients being less than 0.70. The range of absolute correlation coefficients between DL features and ITHscore (0.10–0.53) is lower than that between DL features and Radscore (0.24–0.62). Discussion

In this comprehensive study, we developed a quantifiable ITHscore based on 2D radiomics features of subregions of CT images, which demonstrated performance in predicting LNM in early-stage LUAD. The ITHscore can enhance the characterization ability of Radiomics model. The model fusing ITHscore with Radscore and DL features achieved the best prediction performance in the two independent external test sets, with an AUC of 0.884 in the dataset C and 0.815 in the dataset D. This demonstrates that ITHscore can effectively complement both DL features and Radscore. Our ITHscore can be used as a novel imaging biomarker to characterize the ITH of early LUAD, and provide promising image features in another dimension.

Studies had shown that ITH plays a key role in the occurrence, development and treatment response of early-stage LUAD31. To characterize the overall spatial and subregional spatial heterogeneity, we clustered subregions by SLIC on every CT slices of the tumor region20, and then extracted the 2D radiomics features21 of each subregion to generate a quantifiable ITHscore. Compared to habitat radiomics, these studies32,33 pay more attention to the sub-region properties, but do not consider the global distribution of sub-regions. In our GSEA of the dataset D to verify the biological meaning of ITH score8, EMT was upregulated in the group of patients with high ITH index and was most strongly correlated with high ITH index. Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is closely related to tumor invasion, metastasis, and immune escape34,35, which can affect tumor microenvironment and microstructure and increase ITH36,37. Simply put, ITH-related biological processes are proved to be correlated with ITHscore.

From CT images, DL networks can extract intricate, category-specific DL features to provide additional image features11–13,38. DL networks can simplifies the process of image preprocessing, but they need to deal with the challenges of small samples and image quality39,40. DL networks with complex architectures41,42 or those based on 3D images43,44 require larger datasets to effectively learn complex spatial features, and insufficient data may lead to suboptimal performance or overfitting. Pai et al.45 found that 2D images can improve the development of deep-learning-based imaging biomarkers in specific medical tasks, especially in limited dataset-size scenarios. Therefore, we extracted DL features from the image with the largest tumor ROI and compared the prediction performance of several lightweight algorithms, including ResNet18, ResNet34, ResNet50, DenseNet121, and Inception V3, to select the best-performing algorithm. This strategy effectively mitigated the uncertainties of modeling. The results show ResNet18 showed the best performance.

It’s essential to note that the DL features were extracted from the cropped images containing the largest tumor ROI and may still not fully represent the entire tumor. We compared feature sets based on mean values, maximum values, and maximum slice features to identify the best-performing one for modeling46. The results show the DL features by Mean_value can capture more comprehensive image information, and facilitate better decision making. Moreover, directly interpolating a small 64 × 64 pixel cropped image up to 256 × 256 can cause the loss of a great deal of feature information that is useful for model prediction. Although studies have shown that deep learning models perform better when given whole-image inputs47, early-stage pulmonary nodules are tiny, and feeding the entire 512 × 512×L volume consumes large amounts of memory. In addition, 95% of the voxels are background that can distract a pre-trained network and degrade its ability to capture lesion texture, thereby lowering performance. Cropping the 64 × 64 lesion region first and then enlarging it to 256 × 256 saves computation while preserving critical details, increases the model’s sensitivity to the texture of early nodules, and minimizes background interference42,48.

Although deep networks can produce thousands of features, the limited size of medical datasets makes such high-dimensional representations prone to over-fitting. Careful selection of 10 highly informative DL and 6 radiomic variables not only improves generalisation but also preserves the complementary information required for effective fusion49. Zhang et al.50 screened 10 DL features in characterizing the non-invasive precision treatment of NSCLC patients, and verified that traditional radiomics features and DL features have complementary effects, which is similar to our results.

In our study, both Radscore and ITHscore had strong gain effects on DL features in the two independent test datasets, indicating the good robustness and generalization ability of our fusion model. These may be attributed to the shallow network depth and relatively simple network structure of ResNet18 that we selected. To enhance the interpretability16 of the fusion model, we performed heatmaps, correlation and interpretability analyses for the features from different dimensions. Figure 5 visually shows the association of Radscore, ITHscore, and DL features with LNM. Correlation analysis shows that the DL features are weakly correlated with Radscore and ITHscore, indicating that DL features and ITHscore are complementary rather than redundant. SHAP30 which is a highly practical machine learning interpretation tool, shows that these features have good synergistic effect. Consequently, our fusing strategy yielded superior predictive performance.

This study has certain limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis, there may be inherent data bias despite external validation. Prospective multicenter studies with large cohorts should be performed to validate the generalizability and clinical applicability of this model in the future. Second, manual tumor delineation by different readers may affect the stability of radiomics features. Accurate automated tumor segmentation can not only improve the efficiency of quantitative imaging analysis, but also ensure stability and consistency during feature extraction, but study in this field needs to be developed. Third, models consistently underperform on external test sets compared with the local training data, possibly reflecting ethnic or geographic heterogeneity; future work should investigate federated-learning frameworks to enhance external validity. Finally, although our study sheds new light on the quantification of tumor heterogeneity, there is still no standardized method to assess imaging intratumoral heterogeneity. The biological interpretability of radiomics remains a major challenge, and further studies are needed to reveal the potential link between radiomics and genomics/ proteomics.

The quantifiable ITHscore generated based on CT subregion 2D radiomics features is an independent predictor of LNM in early-stage LUAD, providing another perspective for calculating quantifiable ITH. The multimodal DL radiomics model, which integrates DL features, ITHscore and Radscore, demonstrates excellent predictive performance for LNM in early-stage LUAD. The work also demonstrated that there is a good synergistic effect between DL features, radiomics features, and ITHscore. In summary, our study brings the personalized treatment strategy for early-stage LUAD closer to realization.
