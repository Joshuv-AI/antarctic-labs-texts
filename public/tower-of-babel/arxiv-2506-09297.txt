A thorough study of Riemannian Newton’s Method
Caio O. da Silva, Yuri A. Aoto, Felipe F. G. S. Costa, Márcio F. da Silva

Abstract
This work presents a thorough numerical study of Riemannian Newton’s Method (RNM) for optimization problems, with a focus on the Grassmannian and on the Stiefel manifold. We compare the Riemannian formulation of Newton’s Method with its classical Euclidean counterpart based on Lagrange multipliers by applying both approaches to the important and challenging Hartree–Fock energy minimization problem from Quantum Chemistry. Experiments on a dataset of 125 molecules show that the Riemannian approaches achieve higher convergence rates, require fewer iterations, and exhibit greater robustness to the choice of initial guess. In this work we also analyze the numerical issues that arise from using Newton’s Method on the total manifold when the cost function is defined on the quotient manifold. We investigate the performance of a modified RNM in which we ignore the small eigenvalues of the Hessian and the results indicate that this modified method is stable and performs on par with the RNM on the quotient manifold. Keywords Newton’s Method ⋅ \cdot Lagrange multipliers ⋅ \cdot Grassmannian ⋅ \cdot Stiefel manifold ⋅ \cdot Hartree–Fock ⋅ \cdot Riemannian Optimization

Riemannian Newton’s Method

In this section we will define what the Riemannian Newton’s Method (RNM) is.
We will assume the reader is familiar with Differential Geometry, but, since the Riemannian Optimization literature diverges a little from the traditional Differential Geometry literature in the definition of some objects, we will provide such definitions and some reminders in what follows.
For more details, see
[
11
,
4
]

From now on,
(X,\left\langle\cdot,\,\cdot\right\rangle_{X})
is assumed to be a Riemannian manifold, with
\left\langle\cdot,\,\cdot\right\rangle_{X}
being the Riemannian metric,
T_{x}X
is the tangent space of
X
at
x\in X
, and

TX\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{x\in X}\mathinner{\left\{x\right\}}\times T_{x}X

(2.1)

X

For convenience, we will often omit
X
from the metric
\left\langle\cdot,\,\cdot\right\rangle_{X}
and we will refer only to

Definition 2.1
.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}X\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function defined on a Riemannian manifold
X
.
The
Riemannian gradient
of
f
is the vector field
\riemgrad{f}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}X\to TX
uniquely defined by the following identity:

Df(x)(v)=\left\langle v,\,\riemgrad{f}(x)\right\rangle

(2.2)

for all
(x,v)\in TX

Df(x)(v)
denotes the
directional derivative
of
f
at
x
in the direction

.

Recall that a Riemannian metric
\left\langle\cdot,\,\cdot\right\rangle_{X}
always induces a connection in
X
known as
Levi-Civita connection
.
We will denote such connection by
\nabla^{X}
.
A connection allows us to differentiate vector fields and if we apply this to the Riemannian gradient, we obtain the concept of a Riemannian Hessian.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}X\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function defined on a Riemannian manifold
(X,\left\langle\cdot,\,\cdot\right\rangle_{X})
and let
\nabla^{X}
be the associated Levi-Civita connection.
The
Riemannian Hessian
of
f

x\in X
is the operator
\riemhess{f}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T_{x}X\to T_{x}X
defined as:

\riemhess{f}(x)(v)=\nabla_{v}^{X}\riemgrad{f}.

(2.3)

It is worth noting that the Riemannian gradient and Hessian coincide with the usual Euclidean gradient and Hessian one learns in Calculus when
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
However, this is not true if
X\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
because the geometry of
X
should be taken into account.
Often
X
is a set of matrices satisfying some constraints and in these cases the Riemannian gradient and Hessian are computed by projecting the Euclidean gradient and Hessian onto the tangent space of
X
.
For more details, see
[
1
,
4
]
.

Observation 2.3
.

From now on
I\subset\mathbb{R}
will be used to denote an interval.
The interval can be open or closed depending on the context.
Notice that in both cases it can be seen as a manifold, but, if it is a closed interval, then it is a manifold with boundary.
In both cases the concepts below are well-defined.

So far we have two important concepts in optimization:
the gradient of a function

However, we still need to know how we can move from one point to another because the Newton’s Method is iterative.
To do this, we need geodesics,

Thus, we need to describe a straight line in terms of derivatives because that is what Differential Geometry is about.
Based on the fact that straight lines in the Euclidean space are curves whose second derivative is zero, we will see how to translate this to the Riemannian setting.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a Riemannian manifold

Definition 2.5

Let

The

\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\differential{t}}{(aY+bZ)}=a\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\differential{t}}{Y}+b\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\differential{t}}{Z}

2.

\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\differential{t}}{(gZ)}=g^{\prime}Z+g\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\differential{t}}{Z}

\mathinner{\left(\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\differential{t}}{(U\circ\gamma)}\right)}(t)=\nabla_{\gamma^{\prime}(t)}^{X}U

Definition 2.6

A smooth curve on a Riemannian manifold

\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\differential{t}}\gamma^{\prime}(t)=0

To conclude, we need to define the Riemannian exponential, which allows us to move from one point to another on a manifold.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
X
be a Riemannian manifold and

\mathcal{U}\coloneqq\mathinner{\left\{(x,v)\in TX\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\text{$\gamma_{v}$ is defined on an interval containing $[0,1]$}\right\}}.

(2.5)

The

\exp^{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{U}\to X

(2.6)

We will usually denote

Putting all these concepts together, we can define the main algorithm we are interested in the present work.

Algorithm 2.9

(RNM)
.

Let
X
be a Riemannian manifold and
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}X\to\mathbb{R}
a cost function.
Given a starting point
x_{0}\in X
, proceed as follows:

1.

Compute
\riemgrad{f}(x_{k})
and
\riemhess{f}(x_{k})
;

2.

Solve

\riemhess{f}(x_{k})(v_{k})=-\riemgrad{f}(x_{k})

3.

Update the point according to

.

At the end of this procedure, one obtains a sequence
\mathinner{\left\{x_{k}\in X\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}
that, under certain hypothesis (see

16
]

2.1
Stiefel manifold

Given a symmetric positive-definite matrix
S\in\mathrm{Mat}(d\times d)
and
N\in\mathbb{N}

\mathrm{St}(d,N)\coloneqq\mathinner{\left\{C\in\mathrm{Mat}(d\times N)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C^{\top}SC=\mathrm{Id}_{N}\right\}}.

(2.7)

It is worth pointing out that

\mathrm{Id}_{d}

To implement the RNM in the Stiefel manifold we need to know the explicit formulas for some objects.
Starting with the tangent space to
\mathrm{St}(d,N)

T_{C}\mathrm{St}(d,N)=\mathinner{\left\{\mu\in\mathrm{Mat}(d\times N)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C^{\top}S\mu+\mu^{\top}SC=0_{N}\right\}}.

The Riemannian metric we should consider in this generalized Stiefel manifold is:

\left\langle\mu,\,\mu^{\prime}\right\rangle=\tr(\mu^{\top}S\mu'),

(2.9)

Now, defining

\mathrm{sym}\!\mathinner{\left(M\right)}\coloneqq\frac{M+M^{\top}}{2},\quad\mathrm{skew}\!\mathinner{\left(M\right)}\coloneqq\frac{M-M^{\top}}{2},

\mathrm{proj}_{C}^{\ST}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathrm{Mat}(d\times N)\to T_{C}\mathrm{St}(d,N)

\mathrm{proj}_{C}^{\ST}(M)=M-C\mathrm{sym}\!\mathinner{\left(C^{\top}SM\right)}=(\mathrm{Id}_{d}-CC^{\top}S)M+C\mathrm{skew}\!\mathinner{\left(C^{\top}SM\right)}.

(2.11)

The Riemannian gradient of a smooth cost function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathrm{St}(d,N)\to\mathbb{R}
is given by:

\gradSt{f}(C)=\mathrm{proj}_{C}^{\ST}\mathinner{\left(\mathbf{G}_{\overline{f}}(C)\right)},

(2.12)

being
\overline{f}
an extension of

to
\mathrm{Mat}(d\times N)
and
\mathbf{G}_{\overline{f}}(C)
the usual Euclidean gradient of
\overline{f}
.
The (Riemannian) Hessian of

is given by:

\hessSt{f}(C)(\mu)=\mathrm{proj}_{C}^{\ST}\mathinner{\left(S^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{\overline{f}}(C)(\mu)-\mu\,\mathrm{sym}\!\mathinner{\left({C}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{\overline{f}}(C)\right)}\right)},

(2.13)

being
\mathbf{H}_{\overline{f}}(C)

\overline{f}

We would like to make some comments regarding this formula for the Hessian.
First, since
\mathbf{H}_{\overline{f}}(C)\in\mathrm{Mat}(dN\times dN)

To fix this we need to (1) vectorize

(2) calculate

(3) unvectorize the resulting vector, obtaining a matrix in

(4) left-multiply by

Another problem is that to solve Newton’s Equation (

The reader can take a look at Section

To conclude, the Riemannian exponential is:

\mathrm{Id}_{N}&&C^{\top}S\mu\end{bmatrix}\right)}\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{Id}_{N}\\

2.3
Newton’s Method with Lagrange multipliers

The traditional approach to tackle constrained optimization problems is using Lagrange multipliers combined with the Euclidean Newton’s Method.
However, this is just a particular case of the Riemannian version.
To see that, suppose we have the following optimization problem:

\text{minimize}\;\overline{f}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\;\text{subject to}\;c(x)=0,

(2.23)

being
0\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
a regular value of the function
c\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
that encodes the constraints.
According to the Lagrange Multipliers Theorem (
[
13
, Theorem 12.1]
), we can equivalently look for saddle points of the following Lagrangian:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}&\to\mathbb{R}\\
(x,\varepsilon)&\mapsto\overline{f}(x)-\varepsilon^{\top}c(x).\end{split}

(2.24)

So, considering the manifold
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
and the cost function
\mathcal{L}
, we can see that, if we use Newton’s Method to find the saddle points of
\mathcal{L}
, then the Lagrange multipliers approach is just a particular case of the Riemannian Newton’s Method.
7
7

7

It is possible to use other algorithms to look for saddle points, but the most common is Newton’s Method.

To distinguish from the Riemannian version, though, we will call this approach Newton’s Method with Lagrange multipliers (NMLM).

Moving to our particular case, the manifold we are interested in is
X=\mathrm{Mat}(d\times N)\times\mathrm{Mat}(N\times N)
and the cost function is the following Lagrangian:

\mathcal{L}(C,\,\varepsilon)=\overline{f}(C)-\tr(\trnp{\eps}\qty(\trnp{C}SC - \id_N)).

(2.25)

Notice that the matrix
{C}^{\top}SC-\mathrm{Id}_{N}
is symmetric and, consequently,
\varepsilon
should also be.
This is useful to know because we need less parameters to optimize,
but obtaining Equations (
2.28
) and (
2.29
) below
is more complicated if we ignore the entries above or below the diagonal of
\varepsilon
.
It would also be possible to deduce the formulas assuming explicitly that
\varepsilon
is symmetric, but then the constraints itself would form a manifold.
We could use even more Lagrange multipliers to impose the symmetry of
\varepsilon
, but we observed experimentally that, if we start with
\varepsilon
symmetric,
it remains symmetric in all iterations.
We could not prove this fact, though, and we opted not to impose any constraint on
\varepsilon
because the formulas are still easy to write down and because in practice
\varepsilon
remains symmetric anyway.

As already mentioned, in this case the Riemannian and Euclidean gradient and Hessian coincide, and they are given by:

\mathbf{G}_{\mathcal{L}}(C,\,\varepsilon)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{G}_{\overline{f}}(C)-2SC\varepsilon\\
-({C}^{\top}SC-\mathrm{Id}_{N})\end{bmatrix}

(2.26)

and

\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{L}}(C,\,\varepsilon)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}_{\overline{f}}(C)-\mathbf{H}_{c}(C)&-\mathbf{J}_{{C}^{\top}SC}(C)\\
-{(\mathbf{J}_{{C}^{\top}SC}(C))}^{\top}&0_{N^{2}}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(2.27)

being
\mathbf{H}_{c}(C)
the Hessian of the constraint
\tr(\trnp{\eps}\qty(\trnp{C}SC - \id_N))
and
\mathbf{J}_{{C}^{\top}SC}(C)
the Jacobian of the function
C\mapsto{C}^{\top}SC
.
Note that in the block
\mathbf{H}_{\overline{f}}(C)-\mathbf{H}_{c}(C)
we are considering only the second-order partial derivatives of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
C
while in the block
-\mathbf{J}_{{C}^{\top}SC}
we have the mixed second-order partial derivatives with respect to
C
and
\varepsilon
.
The block made of zeros is the second-order partial derivatives of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
\varepsilon
.
Observe also that in Equation (
2.26
) we have a matrix instead of a vector, which means that to solve Newton’s Equation (
3.1
) one has to vectorize the gradient before plugging it into a solver such as
numpy.linalg.solve
.
Let us now see how to construct the Hessian.

Ignoring
\mathbf{H}_{\overline{f}}(C)
because it depends on the specific problem one is trying to solve, we have:

\mathbf{H}_{c}(C)=(\varepsilon+{\varepsilon}^{\top})\otimes S,

(2.28)

being
\otimes
the Kronecker product, and:

\mathbf{J}_{{C}^{\top}SC}=\mathrm{Id}_{N}\otimes{C}^{\top}S+\begin{bmatrix}{C}^{\top}S\otimes e_{1}&\dots&{C}^{\top}S\otimes e_{N}\end{bmatrix}.

(2.29)

Moreover, the exponential in this case is quite easy because it is just a sum of vectors:

\exp_{(C,\,\varepsilon)}^{\Euc}(v,w)=(C+v,\;\varepsilon+w).

(2.30)

For more details, see
[
15
]
.

To conclude, observe that, if
(C^{*},\,\varepsilon^{*})
is a saddle point of
\mathcal{L}
,
then
\varepsilon^{*}
can be obtained in terms of
C^{*}
.
Indeed, since
\mathbf{G}_{\mathcal{L}}(C^{*},\,\varepsilon^{*})=0
, we know from Equation (
2.26
) that
\mathbf{G}_{\overline{f}}(C^{*})-2SC^{*}\varepsilon^{*}=0
and, consequently,
2(C^{*})^{\top}SC^{*}\varepsilon^{*}=(C^{*})^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{\overline{f}}(C^{*})
.
However, using Equation (
2.26
) again we know that
(C^{*})^{\top}SC^{*}=\mathrm{Id}_{N}
, which gives us the following identity:

\varepsilon^{*}=\frac{1}{2}(C^{*})^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{\overline{f}}(C^{*}).

(2.31)

3
Implementing the Riemannian Hessian

Recall that in Newton’s Method we need to find
v\in T_{x}X
such that

\riemhess{f}(x)(v)=-\riemgrad{f}(x).

(3.1)

We will call this equation
Newton’s Equation
throughout this paper.
If we are planning to solve this equation using a package such as
NumPy
, we need to address two issues:
(1) the Riemannian Hessian for the Stiefel manifold (Equation (
2.13
)) and for the Grassmannian (Equation (
2.21
)) are not just multiplying the tangent vector on the left, which is the format assumed by most solvers;
(2) the solution
v
obtained does not necessarily satisfy the constraint
v\in T_{x}X
.
In what follows, we will provide two detailed implementations of the Hessian in the Stiefel manifold that fix both issues.
However, as it will become clear, both approaches can be adapted to other manifolds as well.

3.1
Intrinsic Hessian

Given an orthonormal basis
\mathinner{\left\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\right\}}
for a vector space
V
endowed with inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\,\cdot\right\rangle
, we can write any vector
v\in V
as:

v=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\langle v,\,v_{i}\right\rangle v_{i}.

(3.2)

We also know that any operator
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V\to V
can be represented as a matrix as follows:

[T]_{ij}=\left\langle v_{i},\,T(v_{j})\right\rangle,

(3.3)

being
i,j=1,\ldots,d
.
Consequently, if
(X,\left\langle\cdot,\,\cdot\right\rangle)
is a Riemannian manifold and
\mathinner{\left\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\right\}}
is an orthonormal basis for
T_{x}X
, the matrix representation of
\riemhess{f}(x)
is:

[\riemhess{f}(x)]_{ij}=\left\langle v_{i},\,\riemhess{f}(x)(v_{j})\right\rangle,

(3.4)

being
i,j=1,\ldots,d
.

It is straightforward to implement Equation (
3.4
), but it is not very efficient because we have to iterate over the basis and compute
\riemhess{f}(C)(v_{j})
for all
j=1,\ldots,d
, which can be costly.
The advantage of this implementation is that we do not need extra dimensions and terms as we do in the other one we will see in Subsection
3.2
.
However, this implementation is intrinsic to the manifold, which allows us to analyze the spectrum of the Hessian (see Section
6
).
Moreover, it is easy to check that the implementation is correct because of the following identity:

[\riemhess{f}(x)]_{ii}=\left\langle v_{i},\,\riemhess{f}(x)(v_{i})\right\rangle=\dfrac{\dif{{}^{2}}}{\dif{t^{2}}}f(\gamma_{v_{i}}(0)),

(3.5)

being
\gamma_{v_{i}}(t)
the geodesic of
X
starting at
x
in the direction
v_{i}
.
To verify this, we computed the right-hand side using finite differences with a step size of
10^{-4}
and confirmed that the result satisfies Equation (
3.5
) within a tolerance of
10^{-5}
.
Since both implementations of Newton’s Method (the one presented in this subsection and the one in the next subsection) produce identical results (within the numerical precision of floating-point operations), it indicates that our implementations are indeed correct.

Another advantage of this implementation of the Hessian is that we can disregard small or negative eigenvalues.
Consider the case in which the (symmetric)
\riemhess{f}(x)
operator has
d
orthonormal eigenvectors
u_{i}
with corresponding eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
such that
\lambda_{i}>\delta>0
, being
\delta>0
a chosen cutoff threshold.
Then, the solution to Newton’s Equation can be approximated as:

v=-\sum_{\lambda_{j}>\delta}\frac{\left\langle\riemgrad{f}(x),\,u_{j}\right\rangle}{\lambda_{j}}u_{j}.

(3.6)

We will refer to this method as the
modified Riemannian Newton’s Method
(mRNM) and we will see in Section
6
that the choice of
\delta
has a significant impact in the performance of Newton’s Method on the Stiefel manifold.

Before we move forward to explain how we compute the Hessian for the Stiefel manifold and the Grassmannian, let us adapt Equation (
3.4
) to the case in which the basis is indexed by two indices.
Let
\mathcal{B}\coloneqq\mathinner{\left\{v_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}i\in J,\,j\in J^{\prime}\right\}}
be a basis for a subspace of
\mathrm{Mat}(d\times N)
.
Then, to compute the Hessian, we need to order this basis.
There are two main conventions for this:
running first over
i
(rows) and then over
j
(columns) or vice-versa.
We will adopt the convention of running first over the rows in this paper.
By doing this, we can compute the matrix representation of the Hessian as follows:

[\riemhess{f}(x)]_{(i+j\cdot|J|,\,k+l\cdot|J|)}=\left\langle v_{ij},\,\riemhess{f}(x)(v_{kl})\right\rangle,

(3.7)

being
|J|
the cardinality of the set
J
.
One important observation to the reader interested in implementing this is that
[\riemhess{f}(x)]\in\mathrm{Mat}(|J||J^{\prime}|\times|J||J^{\prime}|)
, which means that the solution to Newton’s Equation (
3.1
) and the gradient should be written in the basis
\mathcal{B}
and not in the canonical basis of
\mathrm{Mat}(d\times N)
.

Moving to the Stiefel manifold and to the Grassmannian, we know that (see
[
9
]
) any vector
\mu\in T_{C}\mathrm{St}(d,N)
can be written as:

\mu=C\nu+\eta,

(3.8)

being
\nu\in\mathrm{Mat}(N\times N)
a skew-symmetric matrix and
\eta\in T_{C}\mathrm{Gr}(N,d)
.
Moreover, any
\eta\in T_{C}\mathrm{Gr}(N,d)
can be written as:

\eta=C_{\perp}\xi,

(3.9)

being
\xi\in\mathrm{Mat}(d-N\times N)
and
C_{\perp}\in\mathrm{Mat}(d\times d-N)
such that
\begin{bmatrix}C&C_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{St}(d,d)
.
Now, if we consider the canonical basis of
\mathrm{Mat}(d-N\times N)
formed by the matrices
E_{kl}
such that all entries are
0
except
(k,l)
, which is
1
, we obtain an orthonormal basis for
T_{C}\mathrm{Gr}(N,d)
:

\mathcal{B}^{\Gr}\coloneqq\mathinner{\left\{C_{\perp}E_{kl}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}k=1,\ldots,d-N,\,l=1,\ldots,N\right\}}.

(3.10)

Ordering this basis as mentioned above, we can easily compute the Hessian intrinsic to the Grassmannian.
For the Stiefel manifold, on the other hand, we have that

\mathcal{B}^{\ST}\coloneqq\mathinner{\left\{C\mathrm{skew}\!\mathinner{\left(\sqrt{2}E_{ij}\right)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}i=2,\ldots,N,\,j=1,\ldots,i-1\right\}}\cup\mathcal{B}^{\Gr}

(3.11)

is an orthonormal basis for
T_{C}\mathrm{St}(d,N)
.
Ordering this basis by running first over
C\mathrm{skew}\!\mathinner{\left(E_{ij}\right)}
and then over
\mathcal{B}^{\Gr}
, we can compute the Hessian intrinsic to the Stiefel manifold as well.

Before moving to the extrinsic implementation, let us make two remarks:
(a) in order to compute
C_{\perp}
, one can initialize this matrix randomly and then use the Gram–Schmidt algorithm to obtain its columns.
Observe that we need to initialize the Gram–Schmidt algorithm with the columns of
C
, though, because in the end we want to obtain
\begin{bmatrix}C&C_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{St}(d,d)
.
Moreover, we also need to use the inner product defined in Equation (
2.9
) to take the matrix
S
into account.
(b) In practice, even though the solution to Newton’s Equation (
3.1
) is written in terms of an intrinsic basis, the elements of the basis are usually written in terms of the canonical basis of an underlying Euclidean space.
As an example, we have
N(d-N)
elements in the basis of the Grassmannian,
\mathcal{B}^{\Gr}
, but each element in this basis,
C_{\perp}E_{kl}
, lives in
\mathrm{Mat}(d\times N)
.
So, when we compute the tangent vector obtained from solving Newton’s Equation, it will also live in
\mathrm{Mat}(d\times N)
.
Indeed, if each
\eta_{kl}\in\mathbb{R}
represents the coefficient associated to
C_{\perp}E_{kl}
, then

\eta=\sum_{k=1}^{d-N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\eta_{kl}C_{\perp}E_{kl}\in\mathrm{Mat}(d\times N).

(3.12)

3.2
Extrinsic Hessian

The extrinsic implementation is motivated by the case in which we do not have an explicit basis for the tangent space and by a faster and direct construction of the Hessian.
Observe that, even if we do not have an explicit basis for the tangent space of a manifold
X
, the manifold itself is usually a submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or a quotient of a submanifold (see
[
4
]
).
Consequently, very often the tangent space can be described as
T_{x}X=\mathinner{\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}h(x)v=0_{k}\right\}}
, being
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathrm{Mat}(k\times n)
a function of
x
.
Moreover, it is usually not hard to obtain a formula for the Hessian of a function defined on
X
that can be applied to any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
instead of only
v\in T_{x}X
(see Equations (
2.13
) and (
2.21
), for example).
In this case we can see
T_{x}X
as a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and use the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to construct the (extended version of the) Hessian.
8
8

8

This should not be confused with the Hessian of the extension
\overline{f}
.

Explicitly:

[\riemhess{f}(x)]_{ij}=\left\langle e_{i},\,\riemhess{f}(x)(e_{j})\right\rangle.

(3.13)

However, in this representation the solution to Newton’s Equation (
3.1
) may not be in
T_{x}X
.
To fix this issue, we can force the solution to be in
T_{x}X
by augmenting the Hessian and the gradient as follows:

\begin{bmatrix}[\riemhess{f}(x)]\\
h(x)\end{bmatrix}v=-\begin{bmatrix}[\riemgrad{f}(x)]\\
0_{k}\end{bmatrix}.

(3.14)

This augmented version of Newton’s Equation is also straightforward to solve using a solver such as
numpy.linalg.solve
once we know the matrix representation of the Hessian and who
h
is.
Let us now show how this can be done for the Stiefel manifold.

The first observation we have to make is that the tangent space in this case is a subspace of
\mathrm{Mat}(d\times N)
.
Consequently, we will adopt the same convention used in the previous subsection, i.e., the Hessian will be computed according to Equation (
3.7
).
By doing this, observe that we need to vectorize Equation (
3.14
), which means we actually want to solve

\begin{bmatrix}[\hessSt{f}(C)]\\
h(C)\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(\mu\right)}=-\mathrm{vec}\begin{bmatrix}[\gradSt{f}(C)]\\
0_{N}\end{bmatrix},

(3.15)

being
\mathrm{vec}
the
vectorization
operation defined as:

M\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}M_{11}&\ldots&M_{1n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
M_{m1}&\ldots&M_{mn}\end{bmatrix}\leadsto\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(M\right)}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}M_{11}\\
\vdots\\
M_{m1}\\
M_{12}\\
\vdots\\
M_{mn}\end{bmatrix}.

(3.16)

Now that we know which equation we have to solve and how to solve it, let us start figuring out who
h
is so we can plug it in Equation (
3.15
).

Recalling that

T_{C}\mathrm{St}(d,N)=\mathinner{\left\{\mu\in\mathrm{Mat}(d\times N)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C^{\top}S\mu+\mu^{\top}SC=0_{N}\right\}},

(3.17)

we can vectorize the constraint
\mu
to find
h
.
Using the very handy identity
\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(ABC\right)}=(C^{\top}\otimes A)\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(B\right)}
, we have:
9
9

9

Again,
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(C^{\top}S\mu+\mu^{\top}SC\right)}&=\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(C^{\top}S\mu\right)}+\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(\mu^{\top}SC\right)}\\
&=\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(C^{\top}S\mu\mathrm{Id}_{N}\right)}+\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(\mathrm{Id}_{d}\mu^{\top}SC\right)}\\
&=(\mathrm{Id}_{N}\otimes C^{\top}S)\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(\mu\right)}+(C^{\top}S\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{d})\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(\mu^{\top}\right)}.\end{split}

(3.18)

The first term is already as we want, but the second we need to develop further.
Observe that when we multiply a matrix
M
and a vector
v
and we swap two rows of the vector, say
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
, the result of this product is the same as if we had swapped the
i
-th and
j
-th columns of
M
instead of the rows of
v
.
Consequently, since
\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(\mu^{\top}\right)}
is just a permutation of the rows of
\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(\mu\right)}
, we can permute the columns of
(C^{\top}S\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{d})
instead.
Denoting this operation as
\mathrm{perm}
, we have that:

h(C)=\mathrm{Id}_{N}\otimes C^{\top}S+\mathrm{perm}\!\mathinner{\left(C^{\top}S\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{d}\right)}.

(3.19)

To implement the equation above, instead of accessing the columns of
C^{\top}S\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{d}
in the order
[0,1,2,\ldots]
, we access it according to
[i+j\cdot d]
, running first over
j=0,\ldots,N-1
and then over
i=0,\ldots,d-1
.

Now let us move to the Hessian.
Recall that in the Stiefel manifold the Hessian is given by:

\hessSt{f}(C)(\mu)=\mathrm{proj}_{C}^{\ST}\mathinner{\left(S^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{\overline{f}}(C)(\mu)-\mu\,\mathrm{sym}\!\mathinner{\left({C}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{\overline{f}}(C)\right)}\right)},

(3.20)

being

\mathrm{proj}_{C}^{\ST}(M)=M-C\mathrm{sym}\!\mathinner{\left(C^{\top}SM\right)}=M-C\frac{C^{\top}SM+M^{\top}SC}{2}.

(3.21)

Vectorizing the projection using the same ideas presented above, we obtain the following:

\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\left(\mathrm{Id}_{N}\otimes(2\mathrm{Id}_{d}-CC^{\top}S)-\mathrm{perm}\!\mathinner{\left(C^{\top}S\otimes C\right)}\right)}\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(M\right)}.

(3.22)

Moving to what is inside parenthesis in Equation (
3.20
), we need to handle the mismatch in the dimensions of
S^{-1}\in\mathrm{Mat}(d\times d)
,
\mathbf{H}_{\overline{f}}(C)\in\mathrm{Mat}(dN\times dN)
and
\mu\in\mathrm{Mat}(d\times N)
.
To fix this issue, we need to interpret
\mathbf{H}_{\overline{f}}(C)(\mu)
as the product
\mathbf{H}_{\overline{f}}(C)\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(\mu\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
and then, in order to multiply by
S^{-1}
, we need to unvectorize this vector to
\mathrm{unvec}\!\mathinner{\left(\mathbf{H}_{\overline{f}}(C)\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(\mu\right)},\,d\times N\right)}
.
Now that we fixed this issue, we can vectorize everything inside parenthesis, obtaining:

\mathinner{\left((\mathrm{Id}_{N}\otimes S^{-1})\mathbf{H}_{\overline{f}}(C)-\mathrm{sym}\!\mathinner{\left(C^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{\overline{f}}(C)\right)}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{d}\right)}\mathrm{vec}\!\mathinner{\left(\mu\right)}.

(3.23)

Putting everything together, the final matrix representation of the Hessian for the Stiefel manifold is:

[\hessSt{f}(C)]=\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\left(\mathrm{Id}_{N}\otimes(2\mathrm{Id}_{d}-C{C}^{\top}S)-\mathrm{perm}\!\mathinner{\left({C}^{\top}S\otimes C\right)}\right)}\\
\cdot\mathinner{\left((\mathrm{Id}_{N}\otimes S^{-1})\mathbf{H}_{\overline{f}}(C)-\mathrm{sym}\!\mathinner{\left({C}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{\overline{f}}(C)\right)}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{d}\right)}.

(3.24)

Moreover,

[\hessSt{f}(C)]_{(i+j\cdot d,\,k+l\cdot d)}=\left\langle E_{ij},\,\hessSt{f}(C)(E_{kl})\right\rangle,

(3.25)

showing that the vectorization procedure described above is equivalent to computing the Hessian in the canonical basis of the underlying Euclidean space.
However, it is (arguably) easier to compute the full matrix representation directly than to compute entrywise.

The Hessian for the Grassmannian can be computed analogously.
In this case we have:

h(C)=\mathinner{\left(\mathrm{Id}_{N}\otimes C^{\top}S\right)}

(3.26)

and the Hessian is:

[\hessGr{f}(C)]=\mathinner{\left(\mathrm{Id}_{N}\otimes(\mathrm{Id}_{d}-CC^{\top}S)S^{-1}\right)}\mathbf{H}_{\overline{f}}(C)-(\mathbf{G}_{\overline{f}}(C))^{\top}C\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{d}.

(3.27)

4
Some important remarks

The Grassmannian encodes two constraints:

C^{\top}SC=\mathrm{Id}_{N}
and the equivalence relation given by
C\sim C^{\prime}
if, and only if,
C=C^{\prime}M
for some
M\in\mathrm{O}(N)
.
The first constraint is easy to encode using Lagrange multipliers (see Subsection
2.3
).
However, to the best of our knowledge, it is unclear whether the equivalence relation can also be encoded using Lagrange multipliers.
Consequently, a more appropriate comparison to Newton’s Method with Lagrange multipliers is the version of Newton’s Method defined on the Stiefel manifold, rather than on the Grassmannian.

In practice the situation is more complicated because the cost function to be optimized (including our cost function) may be defined in the Grassmannian (or any quotient manifold) and lifting this function to the Stiefel manifold (or to the total space, in general) leads to numerical issues (see
[
4
, Chapter 9]
).
While it might seem reasonable to consider Lagrange multipliers as an alternative, as we will show in Section
6
, this approach is outperformed by the Riemannian methods.

Having said that, it is not always evident that an optimization problem is defined on a quotient manifold (see
[
3
]
).
Moreover, it is usually easier to compute the Riemannian gradient, Hessian, and exponential map on submanifolds of a Euclidean space than on quotient manifolds.
With this in mind, in Subsection
3.1
we showed how to adapt the well known technique of truncating the spectrum of the Hessian to the Riemannian setting and in Section
6
we show that this technique mitigates the numerical issues associated with the Hessian and that it leads to good empirical performance.

From an implementation standpoint, both Riemannian methods are very similar.
In fact, as discussed in (
[
4
, Chapter 9]
), the gradient of a cost function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}X\to\mathbb{R}
and its lift
\overline{f}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\overline{X}\to\mathbb{R}
are the same.
Moreover, the Hessians are also very similar.
In the total space it is given by:

\mathrm{Hess}^{\overline{X}}{\overline{f}}(x)(v)=\mathrm{proj}_{x}^{\overline{X}}\mathinner{\left(\mathrm{proj}_{x}^{\overline{X}}\mathinner{\left(\mathbf{H}_{\overline{\overline{f}}}(x)(v)\right)}+\mathrm{proj}_{x}^{\overline{X}}(v)\mathbf{G}_{\overline{\overline{f}}}(x)\right)},

(4.1)

while in the quotient it is given by:

\mathrm{Hess}^{X}{f}(x)(v)=\mathrm{proj}_{x}^{X}\mathinner{\left(\mathrm{proj}_{x}^{\overline{X}}\mathinner{\left(\mathbf{H}_{\overline{\overline{f}}}(x)(v)\right)}+\mathrm{proj}_{x}^{\overline{X}}(v)\mathbf{G}_{\overline{\overline{f}}}(x)\right)}.

(4.2)

As one can see, the only difference is the outer projection.
One may interpret the fact that the Hessians are not equivalent due to the fact that, in general,
\mathrm{grad}^{\overline{X}}\overline{f}(x)\neq\mathrm{grad}^{\overline{X}}\overline{f}(y)
even when
x\sim y
.
10
10

10

Both gradients project to
\mathrm{grad}^{X}f([x])
, though.

Consequently, since the Hessian on the total space measures variations of the gradient in directions
v\in T_{x}\overline{X}=T_{[x]}X\oplus V_{x}
, it may not vanish when acting on vectors orthogonal to
T_{[x]}X
.

It is also worth mentioning that we cannot, in general, lift geodesics from a quotient manifold to the total space in such a way that, given
[x]\in X
,
v\in T_{[x]}X
and a geodesic
\gamma_{v}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T\to X
emanating from
([x],v)
, we have:

\mathrm{exp}^{X}([x],v)=[\mathrm{exp}^{\overline{X}}(x,v)].

(4.3)

However, in practice the quotient structure of
X
is usually obtained via a Lie group action (satisfying some requirements, see
[
4
, Theorem 9.18]
) and in this case the result holds (see
[
7
]
).
Since this is the case for the Grassmannian, with the Lie group being
\mathrm{O}(N)
, Equation (
4.3
) holds.

Overall, despite the fact that the Riemannian Newton’s Method on a quotient manifold and on its respective total space are not equivalent, in practice they can behave quite similarly, as shown in Section
6
when the spectrum of the Hessian on the Stiefel manifold is truncated.

5
Hartree–Fock

The application we are interested in this work is the
Hartree–Fock Method
, which we will define as a constrained optimization problem that aims to solve an approximation of the Schrödinger equation.
To describe this problem mathematically, let a
Slater determinant
be a wave function (i.e., an element of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
, being
N\in\mathbb{N}
the number of electrons in the system) that can be written as:

\begin{split}(\phi_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\phi_{N})(\mathbf{r}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{r}_{N})&\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\det\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{1}(\mathbf{r}_{1})&\ldots&\phi_{N}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\phi_{1}(\mathbf{r}_{N})&\ldots&\phi_{N}(\mathbf{r}_{N})\end{bmatrix}\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)\phi_{\sigma(1)}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\ldots\phi_{\sigma(N)}(\mathbf{r}_{N}),\end{split}

(5.1)

being
\phi_{i}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.
Denoting by
\nabla_{\mathbf{r}_{i}}^{2}
the Laplacian that considers only the derivatives with respect to
\mathbf{r}_{i}=(r_{1i},r_{2i},r_{3i})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we define the
electronic Hamiltonian
as:

\Hel\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathinner{\left(-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{r}_{i}}^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{Z_{j}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{R}_{j}\right\rvert}}+\sum_{j>i}^{N}\frac{1}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{r}_{j}\right\rvert}}\right)},

(5.2)

being
M
the number of nuclei in the system and
Z_{j}
the atomic number of the
j
-th nucleus (after we fix an order for the nuclei).
It is important to mention that
\mathbf{R}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is assumed to be fixed
11
11

11

This is known as the
Born–Oppenheimer approximation
.
and that
\Hel
is defined on (a subspace of)
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
.
Now, fixing a finite set
\mathscr{B}\coloneqq\quantity{\psi_1, \ldots, \psi_d}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
such that

S_{ij}\coloneqq\left\langle\psi_{i},\,\psi_{j}\right\rangle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{r})\psi_{j}(\mathbf{r})\differential\mathbf{r},

(5.3)

we define Hartree–Fock as the following constrained optimization problem:

minimize

\displaystyle\left\langle\phi,\,\Hel(\phi)\right\rangle,

(5.4)

subject to

\displaystyle\phi=\phi_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\phi_{N},

(5.5)

\displaystyle\phi_{j}\coloneqq c_{1j}\psi_{1}+\ldots+c_{dj}\psi_{d},

(5.6)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}c_{11}&\ldots&c_{1N}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{d1}&\ldots&c_{dN}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{Gr}(N,d)\,.

(5.7)

Observe that, since
\mathscr{B}
is fixed, this problem is indeed defined in the Grassmannian.
We will write down the cost function in terms of
c_{ij}
in a moment, but before that let us explain the physical meaning behind this problem.
The cost function we are minimizing represents the electronic energy of a system with
2N
electrons (a molecule, for example).
We are considering only systems with
2N
electrons instead of
N
because in this case we can treat both spins of an electron equally and, consequently, use the same Slater determinant for both spins.
The constraint serves two purposes:
it ensures that the wave function remains orthonormal and it encodes the fact that state spaces in Quantum Mechanics are defined as the projective space of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
.
See
[
15
]
for the details of this method, including the unrestricted version in which the spins can differ.
Let us now describe this minimization problem explicitly so we can compute the derivatives of the cost function.

Let
\oneh(\mathbf{r})
represent the
one-electron operator
, which is defined as follows:

\oneh(\mathbf{r})\psi(\mathbf{r})=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}\psi(\mathbf{r})-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{Z_{j}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R}_{j}\right\rvert}}\psi(\mathbf{r}).

(5.8)

The motivation behind this operator is that
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
represents an electron, the first term measures the kinetic energy of this electron and the second term measures the electric potential energy of the electron with respect to all nuclei of a given system.
Exploiting the basis set
\mathscr{B}
, we can define the following matrices:

h_{ij}\coloneqq\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{r})\oneh(\mathbf{r})\psi_{j}(\mathbf{r})\differential\mathbf{r},

(5.9)

which represents the one-electron operator,

g_{ijkl}\coloneqq\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\psi_{j}(\mathbf{r}_{2})\frac{1}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{r}_{2}\right\rvert}}\psi_{k}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\psi_{l}(\mathbf{r}_{2})\differential\mathbf{r}_{1}\differential\mathbf{r}_{2},

(5.10)

which represents a two-electron operator that measures the Coulomb interaction between two electrons, and

F_{ij}\coloneqq h_{ij}+\sum_{k,l=1}^{d}\sum_{\nu=1}^{N}c_{k\nu}c_{l\nu}\quantity(2g_{ikjl} - g_{ijkl}),

(5.11)

which is known as the
Fock matrix
.
Using all these matrices, we can rewrite the Hartree–Fock problem as follows:

minimize

\displaystyle f(C)\coloneqq\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}c_{i\mu}c_{j\mu}(h_{ij}+F_{ij}),

(5.12)

subject to

\displaystyle C\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}c_{11}&\ldots&c_{1N}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{d1}&\ldots&c_{dN}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{Gr}(N,d).

(5.13)

See
[
17
]
for more details about the method from a Chemistry perspective.

To conclude, we need to compute the Euclidean gradient and Hessian of the (lift/extension of the) function
f
, which we will denote as
\overline{f}
.
12
12

12

Observe that in this case we can use the exact same expression for
\overline{f}
and
f
, but to keep the notation compatible with the previous sections we will use
\overline{f}
.

The Euclidean gradient of
\overline{f}
is given by:

\mathbf{G}_{\overline{f}}(C)=2FC,

(5.14)

and the entries of the Euclidean Hessian,
\mathbf{H}_{\overline{f}}
, are given by:

\partialderivative{}{x_{ki}}{x_{lj}}\overline{f}\mathinner{\left(C\right)}=2\quantity(F_{kl} \delta_{ij} + \sum_{r,s=1}^d c_{ri} c_{sj}
(2g_{krls} - g_{kslr} - g_{klrs})),

(5.15)

being
\delta_{ij}
the Kronecker delta function.
We could not find a direct computation for the matrix
\mathbf{H}_{\overline{f}}
, which means we construct it iteratively using the equation above and the convention adopted in Equation (
3.7
) for the indices.
See
[
15
, Chapter 4]
for more details.
That is it, we have everything we need to implement the algorithms described in the previous sections to solve the Hartree–Fock problem.

6.2
Spectrum

The discussion in Subsection
6.1
on the water molecule shows that RNM-Gr and mRNM-St are very similar because the spectra of the Hessians on the Grassmannian and the Stiefel manifold, excluding the smallest
\dim{\ST}-\dim{\Gr}
eigenvalues and their corresponding eigenvectors, are numerically close.
Since examining the individual spectrum of each molecule in the dataset is impractical, we extend this analysis to the full dataset using the root mean squared difference between the eigenvalues of both Hessians:

D\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{1}{\dim{\Gr}}\sum_{i=1}^{\dim{\Gr}}\left(\lambda_{i}^{\Gr}-\lambda_{i}^{\ST}\right)^{2}}\,.

(6.1)

We assume the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}^{\Gr}\}_{i=1}^{\dim{\Gr}}
and
\{\lambda_{i}^{\ST}\}_{i=1}^{\dim{\ST}}
are in descending order, as in Table
1
, and we use only the first
\dim{\Gr}
eigenvalues of
\hessSt{f}(C)
to compute
D
.

Figure
2
presents the values of
D
for all molecules in the dataset.
All eigenvalues were computed at the initial guess
C_{0}
.
The results show that
D
is small for the entire dataset:
no value exceeds
0.08
and 75% fall below
0.02
, indicating that the spectra of both Hessians are numerically close.
In addition to plotting the values of
D
, we use colors and markers to convey more information about how the two methods compare for each molecule.
In general, molecules with low values of
D
exhibit very similar convergence patterns for RNM-Gr and mRNM-St.
Both methods tend to converge to the same critical point and very often in the same number of iterations.
In some cases, however, RNM-Gr requires one extra iteration (highlighted in yellow).
Conversely, calculations where one method fails to converge or converges to a different critical point tend to exhibit larger values of
D
.
This is consistent with the fact that a large
D
indicates the solutions to Equation (
3.6
) on the two manifolds are not the same, leading to different optimization steps.

Some outliers are worth mentioning.
For instance, the H
2
CO molecule has a small
D\approx 0.01
, yet mRNM-St and RNM-Gr converge to different critical points.
In contrast, the NH molecule exhibits the largest
D
in the dataset, but both methods converge to the same critical point, with RNM-Gr requiring two extra iterations.
While a detailed analysis of each case is beyond the scope of this work, the results suggest that a closer inspection of the Hessians spectra may help clarify the behavior of the outliers.

Ablating

The Riemannian Newton’s Method on the Stiefel manifold (RNM-St) never converges.
This motivated us to modify the method by truncating the spectrum of the Hessian.
Specifically, we discard eigenvalues smaller than a threshold
\delta
when solving Newton’s Equation (
3.1
) (see Section
3
for details).
By default, we use
\delta=10^{-8}
since it is numerically close to zero and is sufficient to ensure the Hessian is positive-definite.
However, we noticed that increasing this value may lead some molecules that previously did not converge to converge.
We therefore tested the impact of changing
\delta
in the performance of mRNM-St over the entire dataset.

Table
4
shows that increasing
\delta
actually reduces the number of converged cases.
However, the average number of iterations
among converged cases
decreases as
\delta
increases.
This happens because, for small values of
\delta
, some molecules require many iterations to converge, which increases the average.
These are exactly the molecules that fail to converge for larger values of
\delta
, and thus the cases that still converge at those values, do so in fewer iterations.
This indicates that the more difficult cases (those requiring many iterations) depend on the small eigenvalues.
If those are excluded by the threshold, convergence fails.
Only in a few cases (2 molecules) increasing
\delta
results in convergence where it previously failed, but even in those cases the optimization failed after further increasing
\delta
.
These results suggest that using a small
\delta
is a reasonable starting point and, if convergence is not achieved, then increasing it may help in some cases.
Moreover, the results did not change for
\delta
between
10^{-8}
and
10^{-3}
.

6.5
Performance profile for the number of iterations

Although the previous subsections already indicate that the Riemannian methods outperform the classical Newton’s Method with Lagrange multipliers, we also carried a
performance profile
analysis, which is a standard practice in the literature (see
[
8
]
for details).

Recall that performance profiles compute a cumulative distribution of best results.
For a given
\tau\geq 1
,
\rho(\tau)
is defined as the proportion of cases in which a method’s performance is better than
\tau
times the best case.
Naturally, the notion of “best” depends on the metric under consideration.
In this subsection, we use the number of iterations required for convergence, but other metrics will be considered later.

Formally, let
p
index the 125 molecules in the dataset.
For each method
s
, we define:

\rho_{s}(\tau)\coloneqq\frac{\text{number of molecules $p$ with $r_{p,s}\leq\tau$}}{\text{number of molecules in the dataset}},

(6.2)

being
r_{p,s}
the performance ratio, defined as:

r_{p,s}\coloneqq\frac{\text{number of iterations for molecule $p$ and method $s$}}{\text{minimum number of iterations among all $s$ for $p$ fixed}}.

(6.3)

For example, for
s=
RNM-Gr, the molecule H
2
converges in 2 iterations, which is the best case.
There are 20 molecules that converge in 3 iterations, 71 in 4 iterations, and so on.
In all these cases RNM-Gr is the best method (or it ties with mRNM-St).
Therefore:
13
13

13

We omit the remaining terms for brevity.

\rho_{\text{RNM-Gr}}(\tau)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{125}=0.008,&\text{if $\tau\in[1,\frac{3}{2})$},\\
\frac{1+20}{125}=0.168,&\text{if $\tau\in[\frac{3}{2},\frac{4}{2})$},\\
\frac{1+20+71}{125}=0.736,&\text{if $\tau\in[\frac{4}{2},\frac{5}{2})$}.\end{cases}

(6.4)

Observe that, the faster
\rho_{s}(\tau)
increases, the better the method, since more cases converge in fewer iterations.
Furthermore, as we increase
\tau
,
\rho_{s}(\tau)
eventually reaches the proportion of molecules for which the method

converged.

slightly higher than

for large

We also observe that increasing
\delta
reduces the overall performance of mRNM-St for large
\tau
.
Finally, we can see that once again NMLM is outperformed by the Riemannian methods:

6.6
Characterizing the convergence neighborhood

The statistical results and the performance profile discussed in the previous sections show a clear advantage for the Riemannian methods.
However, these results strongly depend on the choice of initial guess.
To decouple convergence performance from the initial guess, we will make use of the fact that there exists a convergence neighborhood around a critical point such that, if the starting point lies within this region, Newton’s Method is guaranteed to converge to that critical point (see
[
10
,
18
]
).
Explicitly describing such neighborhoods is very difficult, especially when the domain of the cost function is a high-dimensional Riemannian manifold.
Therefore, we take a numerical approach to estimate and compare the convergence neighborhoods of the methods under study.
This allows us to determine which method has the largest convergence region, which in turn suggests greater robustness, since convergence does not necessarily require starting close to the solution.

We now describe the approach in a general form, as it may be applied in other settings.
Let
(X,\left\langle\cdot,\,\cdot\right\rangle)
be a complete Riemannian manifold, let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}X\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth cost function, and let
x_{*}
be a critical point of
f
, meaning
\riemgrad{f}(x_{*})=0
.
Consider an iterative algorithm
A
that, given
x_{0}\in X
, produces a sequence
\{x_{k}\in X\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}k\in\mathbb{N}\}
which
may
converge to
x_{*}
.
For a fixed small
\varepsilon>0
, we define the number of iterations for
A
to converge to
x_{*}
as the smallest
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\riemgrad{f}(x_{n})\right\rVert}<\varepsilon
.
Note that this is consistent with the convergence criterion used previously, where
\varepsilon=10^{-8}
.
Taking all of this into account, we aim to describe the following set:

V_{x_{*}}\coloneqq\left\{x\in X\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\begin{array}[]{c}\text{$x=\exp_{x_{*}}(v)$ for some $v\in T_{x_{*}}X$ and $A$ converges to $x_{*}$}\\
\text{if it starts at $\exp_{x_{*}}(tv)$ for $t\in[0,1]$}\end{array}\right\}.

(6.5)

This set
V_{x_{*}}
can be interpreted as the largest star-shaped set centered at
x_{*}
such that, if the algorithm
A
starts at any
x\in V_{x_{*}}
, it converges to
x_{*}
.
While the true convergence region may not be star-shaped, in this work we will focus on this simplified region.
We also examine how the number of iterations
n
varies as a function of
x
.
To estimate this region numerically, we consider an orthonormal basis
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
for
T_{x_{*}}X
, and define
\{v_{j}\}\coloneqq\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\cup\{-u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
as the set of directions we will move along to obtain
V_{x_{*}}
.
Once we have
v_{j}
, we consider several values of
t>0
and calculate
x=\exp_{x_{*}}(tv_{j})
to use as the initial guess of algorithm
A
.

4
depicts the convergence neighborhood for the water molecule.

14

7
Conclusions and final remarks

This work presented a thorough numerical study of the Riemannian Newton’s Method, with two main goals:
to compare its performance against the method of Lagrange multipliers, and to better understand the convergence issues that arise when the objective function is defined on a quotient manifold.

We carried out the experiments on the Hartree–Fock optimization problem from Quantum Chemistry, which can be formulated on both the Stiefel manifold and the Grassmannian.
Computations were performed on 125 molecules from the G2/97 dataset.
To evaluate the performance of the methods, we considered two metrics:
the percentage of cases that successfully converged and the number of iterations required to converge.
All methods started from the same initial guess.
We also examined the convergence behavior by numerically estimating the convergence radii of each method.
The results show that the Riemannian methods outperform the Lagrange multipliers method across all evaluation criteria.

Although it is well known that lifting a cost function from a quotient manifold to the total space can lead to numerical issues (since the function is invariant under equivalence classes), we showed that these issues can be mitigated by solving Newton’s Equation in the subspace spanned by the Hessian’s eigenvectors with eigenvalues above a small threshold.
This is relevant because it is often easier to formulate and implement Newton’s Method on a total space than on a quotient manifold.
Moreover, the modified Newton’s Method on the Stiefel manifold not only fixes the convergence problem, but it also slightly outperforms Newton’s Method on the Grassmannian.
