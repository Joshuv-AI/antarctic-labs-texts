Introduction

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) has a global prevalence of approximately 0.1% to 1.4%, with a predilection for males aged 20–40 years [1], [2]. Traditionally, AS was considered a disease confined to the musculoskeletal system. However, pleuropulmonary involvement has been increasingly recognized. A previous study reported pleuropulmonary involvement in 28 out of 2080 patients with ankylosing spondylitis (1.3%). Among these 28 patients, the most common abnormality was upper lobe fibrobullous lesions [3]. Costovertebral joint ankylosis causing limitation of chest expansion has been reported as the most prevalent feature of thoracic manifestation of AS. AS-associated lung injury (ASLI) not only impairs respiratory function but also increases the risk of severe complications such as infection and fibrosis, significantly reducing patients' quality of life [4]. Moreover, ASLI is often overlooked by clinicians and misdiagnosed as pulmonary infectious diseases, such as pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) and pulmonary aspergillosis.

Here, we report two cases of male patients with bilateral upper lung destruction. They were both initially misdiagnosed as pulmonary TB with destructed lungs and received empirical anti-TB treatment. TB was subsequently ruled out for these patients, and they were diagnosed with ASLI. We hope to provide a reference for clinicians in diagnosing ASLI.

Case description

Case 1

A 58-year-old man was admitted on 28 October 2024 with a six-month cough and one-week anorexia. One month before admission, chest CT scan at local hospital suggested the possibility of pulmonary tuberculosis in both lungs (Fig. 1). But sputum for Acid-fast bacilli (AFB), GeneXpert MTB/RIF, TB-DNA and culture were all negative. He received empirical anti-tuberculosis treatment with HRE (isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol) regimen. One week prior to admission, he started to have poor appetite with no nausea and vomiting. He reported that he had AS for more than 20 years but never received regular and typical treatment. On admission: body temperature 36.7℃, pulse 122 beats per minute, respiratory rate 20 breaths per minute, blood pressure 108/64 mmHg, air room oxygen saturation (SpO2) 99%; height 165 cm, weight 42 kg, body mass index (BMI) 15.43 kg/m2. The patient had poor nutritional status, clear consciousness. There was kyphosis of the cervical spine and flattened lordosis of the lumbar spine; spinal movement was limited in all directions (Fig. 2). Breath sounds in the upper lobes of both lungs were diminished, and no dry or moist rales or pleural friction rub were heard. Laboratory tests showed increased C-reactive protein (CRP) 83.62mg/L (Reference: 0-6 mg/L) and Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 120mm/h (Reference: 2-20.9 mm/h) with normal liver and kidney function. Two consecutive bacterial cultures of sputum showed the growth of Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA). He received two weeks’antibiotic for PA. Followed sputum tests for fungi and mycobacteria were all negative. According to the typical CT scan and negative sputum result, he was diagnosed with pulmonary injury caused by ankylosing spondylitis. The anti-TB treatment was stopped. He was referred to rheumatology department and was also followed in pulmonary medicine department.

Chest CT scan showed multiple irregular patchy, nodular, and ground-glass opacities in both lungs. Cavitation formation is noted in the apical and posterior segments of the upper lobes of both lungs, with visible draining bronchus signs. Additionally, thoracic kyphosis deformity, extensive fusion of the zygapophyseal joints, and bamboo spine changes of the vertebral bodies are observed.

Typical body posture of patients with ankylosing spondylitis: global spinal stiffness, cervical kyphosis, and forward head posture.

Case 2

A 37-year-old male was admitted on August 13, 2025, with the chief complaints of “productive cough for 11 days and hemoptysis for 1 day”. Past medical history showed that he had suffered of severe hemoptysis and received bronchial artery embolization in 2015 and in 2020 respectively. He also received empirical anti-TB treatment 5 years ago. He was diagnosed with AS and chronic hepatitis B 20 years ago and has been intermittently treated with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. Family history reported that his father and uncle have a history of AS. On admission, body check showed restricted cervical spine mobility (Fig. 3). Chest CT revealed multiple bilateral pulmonary lesions, bronchiectasis, cavitation in the right upper lobe, and atelectasis or partial destruction of the bilateral upper lobes. Moreover, partial fusion of the thoracic intervertebral facet joints, consistent with ankylosing spondylitis (Fig. 4). Sputum for Acid-fast bacilli smear, molecular TB test and TB culture were all negative. Sputum for Aspergillus nucleic acid test was positive. His white blood cell count(WBC) slightly increased to10.16×10^9/L (Reference:3.5-9.5×10^9/L). ESR was 109 mm/h and CRP was 94.050 mg/L. Human leukocyte antigen HLA-B27 was positive. Pulmonary TB was ruled out. He was diagnosed as chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA) and ASLI. He received oral voriconazole for CPA and was followed up in pulmonary medicine department. He was also managed by a rheumatologist and started on standard treatment for AS.

Body check showed restricted cervical spine mobility.

Chest CT scan showed multiple bilateral pulmonary lesions, bronchiectasis, cavitation in the right upper lobe, and atelectasis or partial destruction of the bilateral upper lobes; Minimal small calcifications in the left lung; Incidental finding: Partial fusion of the thoracic intervertebral facet joints, consistent with ankylosing spondylitis.

Discussion

Our report described two cases of AS presenting with bilateral upper-lung structural destruction, both of which were initially misdiagnosed as pulmonary TB. These cases underscore the diagnostic challenge in distinguishing chronic pulmonary sequelae of AS from infectious cavitary lung diseases, particularly in TB-endemic regions.

Radiographic assessment might confirm cervical ankylosis in most of the advanced-stage patients with AS. However, in patients with structural destruction of the bilateral upper lobes, imaging-based confirmation of ASLI is often challenging. A separate analysis of 1,028 Chinese patients with ankylosing spondylitis (AS) in Taiwan demonstrated that only 22 individuals were diagnosed with apical lung fibrosis [5]. Apical fibrosis, cavity lesion, and architectural distortion in upper lobes might be remarkably similar to the sequelae of chronic granulomatous infections like TB or fungal disease [6].

The initial misdiagnosis as TB in our cases highlights a critical clinical pitfall. This diagnostic bias can lead to delayed recognition of non-infectious causes. Notably, ASLI are highly susceptible to secondary colonization and infection by organisms like Mycobacterium tuberculosis, non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), and Aspergillus species. This superinfection further obscures the underlying diagnosis, creating a cycle where treatment targets only the superinfection while the primary inflammatory process of AS remains unaddressed. In a study by Rosenow et al. involving 26 patients with upper lobe fibrobullous disease, superinfection was documented in seven cases. Aspergillus fumigatus was the most frequently isolated pathogen, followed by various non-TB mycobacterial species including M. kansasii, M.avium, M. fortuitum, and M. scrofulaceum [7]. Clinically, repeatedly negative TB tests in the face of persistent upper lobe cavities should prompt consideration of alternative diagnoses. And a positive culture for NTM or Aspergillus does not exclude underlying ASLI, it may indicate superinfection of a pre-existing abnormal lung architecture. When sputum tests are repeatedly microbiological negative, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) or CT-guided lung aspiration might be considered to definitively exclude Mycobacterium tuberculosis, NTM, and fungal infections before establishing a diagnosis of exclusion. Differentiating primary ASLI from infectious lung damage remains challenging. The bilateral upper-lobe distribution, chronic progression in long-standing AS, and repeatedly negative microbiology support ASLI as the primary etiology, even when secondary colonization occurs. We thus recommend that a multidisciplinary approach integrating radiology, rheumatology and pulmonology perspectives is essential when evaluating upper-lung destruction in patients with AS.

Conclusions

In conclusion, when evaluating AS patients with upper-lobe fibrocavitary lesions, it is crucial to differentiate between primary ASLI and secondary infection. Maintaining vigilance for superinfection is key to preventing misdiagnosis and guiding appropriate therapy. Future studies are warranted to elucidate the pathogenesis of ASLI and to explore targeted management strategies.
