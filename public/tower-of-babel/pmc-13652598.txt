Morphological and Topographical Characteristics of Parietal Foramen in the Adult Human Skull Bones of North Indian Population and Its Clinical Correlations – A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Shubhangi Yadav, Rajat S. Das, Anshu Mishra, Durgesh Singh, Parmatma P. Mishra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionParietal foramen (PF) is an inconstant small opening situated between the middle and posterior thirds of the parietal bone, close to the sagittal border, serving to protect the parietal emissary vein.ObjectiveThe aim is to study the incidence and morphology of the PF in a cross-section of the adult North Indian population.Materials and MethodsThis cross-sectional study was performed between March 2025 and July 2025 on 60 adult human skulls available in the Department of Anatomy at AIIMS, Raebareli, RDASMC, Ayodhya, and MSDASMC, Bahraich, Uttar Pradesh, India. Each skull was examined for the presence of PF. Measurements included the distance between the PF and the midline of the sagittal suture (SS), as well as the distance of the foramen from lambda. Statistical analysis was performed to evaluate the findings.ResultsThe PF was absent in 12 skulls (20%), present on the SS in one skull (1.66%), unilateral in 10 skulls (16.6%), bilateral in 36 skulls (60%), and triple in one skull (1.66%). The distance between PF and lambda ranged from 2 to 4 cm in 99% of skulls, with an average distance of 31.9 ± 5.15 mm. The distance of PF from the SS (midline) varied between 2 and 6 mm in 80% of the cases. The average diameter of PF was found to be approximately 0.82 mm, ranging from 0.3 to 1.5 mm.ConclusionThis descriptive study provides valuable insights into the variations of PF, which is crucial for neurosurgeons in modifying surgical procedures to prevent injury to PF-emerging structures. Understanding the frequency and location of parietal foramina is crucial for comprehending the relationship between sinuses and extracranial veins, as well as for analyzing scalp injuries.

Introduction

The English word “skull” is derived from “skulle” meaning head, while the word “cranium” originates from the Greek root “κρaνίον” (Kranion). 1 The skull is commonly categorized into two main parts: the neurocranium and the viscerocranium.

The neurocranium or cranial vault encloses the brain, cranial meninges, and cerebrospinal fluid, while viscerocranium or the facial skeleton hangs down from the front of the neurocranium and houses the organs of special sense. Parietal bone is the bone present in neurocranium and forms most of the cranial roof and side of the skull. Parietal foramen (PF) is an inconstant small opening located between the middle and posterior thirds of the parietal bone, near the sagittal border. 2 The region between the two parietal foramina is termed as “obelion,” named after the Greek symbol obelos (%), representing these foramina on each side of the sagittal suture (SS). These foramina likely persist from the lateral most aspects of the primitive parietal notch. 3

The occurrence of parietal foramina ranges from 50 to 80% across different populations. 4 5 In newborns, they are situated approximately 2 cm in front of the Lambda, while in adults, they are approximately 2 to 5 cm anterior to inion. This means that the PF shifts further posteriorly in adults. 3 6 7

According to Testut and Latarjet, the number of parietal foramina is variable, sometimes there is only one or may be three, two on one side and one on the opposite side, can also be four, two on either side. 8

Each foramen transmits an emissary vein (EV), an occasional branch of occipital artery, and an anastomotic vessel connecting middle meningeal artery and branches of the superficial temporal artery. 9 10

The parietal EVs usually carry blood from extracranial to intracranial regions, but their valveless nature allows flow reversal with increased intracranial pressure (e.g., Valsalva maneuver). During hyperthermia, blood flow shifts from superficial to deep veins, while hypothermia may reduce or reverse this flow. 11 In the upright posture, EVs act as an important venous drainage pathway. They may also contribute to thermoregulation of the head and spinal cord via the vertebral venous plexus. 12

The fourth layer of the scalp is composed of loose areolar connective tissue and is termed the “danger area” because it provides a pathway for the parietal EV, which connect the superficial veins of the scalp to the superior sagittal sinus. This link could potentially facilitate the spread of infection from the scalp to the dural venous sinuses. 11 Infection involving the diploic, meningeal, and cerebral veins, which are linked to the EVs, can lead to complications such as cranial vault osteomyelitis, meningitis, and cerebral abscesses. 6 Additionally, these EVs serve as a mechanism to regulate intracranial pressure, sometimes enlarging in the presence of a space-occupying lesion.

In case of the parietal craniotomy, the blood vessels emerging from PF, most notably parietal EVs, can cause significant bleeding leading to severe complications. Because these veins communicate directly with the superior sagittal sinus, and are firmly embedded within the calvarial bone, bleeding from them is often difficult to control. Failure to achieve adequate hemostasis can result in complications such as epidural hematoma, venous air embolism, superior sagittal sinus thrombosis, venous congestion with cerebral edema or infarction, postoperative infection, and delayed neurological deficits. 6 13 It is crucial to accurately identify the emissary foramina to gain insight into the regional blood vessels and differentiate them from other structures. 14 15

Therefore, the knowledge of location, number, and size of the PF is crucial for the neurosurgeons to avoid complications during and after surgery. A clear knowledge gap exists due to the limited data on the incidence, morphology, and precise topographical relationship of the PF with anatomical landmarks such as the SS and lambda in the adult North Indian population.

The aim of this research work is to study the morphological characteristics of the PF in a cross-section of the adult North Indian population with the objective of documenting incidence, morphology, and topography of the foramen in relation to SS and lambda.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This cross-sectional study was performed on 60 macerated adult dry skulls (100 parietal bones) of unknown sex, available in the Department of Anatomy at AIIMS, Raebareli, RDASMC-Ayodhya, and MSDASMC, Bahraich, Uttar Pradesh, India, from March 2025 to July 2025.The study was conducted after obtaining ethical clearance from the Institutional Ethics Committee.

Sample Size

The sample size of 60 skulls was determined by the availability of specimens meeting the inclusion criteria in the participating institutions. Comparable morphometric studies have employed similar sample sizes, which are considered sufficient for documenting anatomical variations of cranial foramina. As this was a descriptive study, formal sample size calculation was not required. The selected sample was deemed adequate to provide a representative overview of the morphological and topographical variations of the PF in the adult North Indian population.

Statistical Analysis

The collected data were analyzed statistically. The frequency and percentage of skulls with different PF presentations (absent, unilateral, bilateral, and triple) were computed. Descriptive statistics, such as the mean, standard deviation, and range, were calculated for the distances between the PF and the midline, as well as the distances from the lambda. An independent t -test was performed to compare the distance of parietal foramina from the SS on the right and left sides. The result showed no statistically significant difference between the two sides ( t = −0.68, p = 0.50). Since the p -value is greater than 0.05, the difference in mean distances between the right and left sides is not statistically significant.

Discussion

Understanding the PF is crucial in neurosurgery because during operations, there is a risk of rupturing EVs. 8 16 This can lead to postoperative epidural hematomas. 13 EVs, lacking valves, permit blood flow in both directions. Under normal conditions, blood flow through the EVs is typically sluggish. They assist in blood drainage and help reduce intracranial tension in cases of elevated intracranial pressure. 17 However, their valveless nature also poses a risk of transmitting infections from the scalp to the cranial cavity, potentially causing complications such as osteomyelitis, meningitis, or brain abscesses, as noted by Boyd. Without the EVs the risk of scalp injuries and diseases would potentially be reduced to half. 7 13 These factors highlight the significance of PF anatomy. The consistency of any specific variant within a particular race emphasizes the necessity for population-specific data regarding the anatomical parameters of the PF, as highlighted by Berry and Berry. 9

In the present study, the incidence of PF was found in approximately 80% of skulls, which is similar to the findings of authors who worked on skulls of Indian origin, as reported by Murlimanju at 87.9% and by Fating at 72.5%. 18 19 The incidence of PF in the present study is significantly higher compared with studies by authors from British and United States populations, who reported the incidence of PF to be around 60%. 5 7 This difference could be attributed to racial and geographical variations ( Table 3 ).

In our study, 60% of the skulls presented with bilateral foramen. This finding is similar to what was reported by Murlimanju, who found the presence of bilateral foramen in 55.2% of cases, and by Collipal et al, who observed it in 58.97% of skulls. Le Double suggested that parietal foramina are often absent among individuals of White descent compared with those of other races. 18 20 21

The presence of the PF, either unilaterally or bilaterally on the skull, shows a wide range of variation. The presence of the PF on the SS, which we found in 3.32% of skulls, including one skull with triple foramen with one of the foramina on the SS, has also been reported in a few studies. 22 23 24 Some other authors have also reported multiple parietal foramina ( Table 3 ). 23 24

The topographic location of PF is important from surgical, radiological, and academic points of view. 25 According to Currarino, the PF is reported to be located approximately 2 cm anterior to the lambda in new born and 2 to 5 cm anterior in adults. 3 In the study by Murlimanju on South Indian skulls, it was noted that the majority of foramina were situated approximately 4 to 8 mm from the SS. 18 In the present study, we found that the position of the PF in reference to the SS ranged between 2 and 6 mm with an average of approximately 4 mm, which is slightly less than the results reported by other authors. The average distance of the PF from the lambda in our study was 31.9 ± 5.15 mm, which is very similar to the findings of other researchers ( Table 4 ).

Halagatti and Sagar noted that understanding the frequency and location of parietal foramina is crucial for comprehending the relationship between sinuses and extracranial veins, as well as for analyzing scalp injuries and surgical procedures. 26

Yoshioka et al examined 40 adult parietal bones and found that each foramen transmitted an anastomotic vessel connecting the middle meningeal artery to extracranial arteries. 5 This anastomosis was believed to have implications in various clinical conditions. However, in our study using dried skulls, we were unable to examine the vessels and their anastomoses at the PF.

This study has reported the findings regarding the distance of the foramen from the SS and the lambda. Such information was previously unavailable in the North Indian sample, making these results particularly novel.

The parietal bone develops through intramembranous ossification, starting around the 7th to 8th week of fetal life. Ossification spreads outward from the primary center near the parietal eminence in a sunburst pattern. However, certain regions along the SS, particularly near the obelion, ossify more slowly. This intentional delay in ossification allows the EVs to establish their pathways through the bone, forming the parietal foramina.

While the formation of parietal foramina is a normal anatomical feature, variations in their size, number, or persistence can occur. These anomalies may result from differences in ossification patterns or developmental factors. 8 27 28 29 In our study, we also observed the presence of a PF along the SS in one skull.

The average diameter of the PF observed in the present study closely corresponds with the findings reported by Berge and Bergman (0.67 mm) in the United States and Singh and Raibagkar (0.91 mm) in the Indian population. 30 31 Mann et al and Naidoo et al found incidences of enlarged parietal foramina of 0.7 and 3.4%, respectively in Japanese and South African populations. 24 32 Boyd noted that parietal foramina tend to be larger in Australian and New Zealand individuals compared with other ethnic groups. 7 However, our current study did not observe any notably enlarged parietal foramina.

These are likely the result of incomplete ossification of the parietal bones and are generally not linked with a markedly dilated EV. 7 Various studies have linked enlarged parietal foramina with clinical conditions such as cerebral venous and cortical abnormalities, perineural tumors, and skull fractures. 33 34 It is understood that enlarged parietal foramina can have a genetic basis and follow an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. 35 Although individuals may not show symptoms in many instances, these conditions have been linked to various signs such as nausea, having multiple bony growths, intellectual disability, cleft palate, recurring headaches, straight sinus hypoplasia, dilated vein, and abnormalities in other structures of brain. 35 Conversely, it is proposed that small parietal foramina do not significantly affect blood flow.

Conclusion

We observed the considerable variability of the PF; being absent in one-fifth of the skulls, while bilateral presence is found in 60% skulls, followed by unilateral and rare multiple foramina. On an average, the foramen was located close to the lambda, most frequently within 31.9 ± 5.15 mm, and lay only a few millimeters lateral to the SS.

It correlates well with earlier works that have contributed knowledge in various populations. The observed parametric differences may be due to racial factors. The variation across the races can be attributed to the ethnic differences of expression of genetic factors determining the ossification. This supposition may be verified through future research.

The anatomical knowledge of these reported variations would guide to prevent any inadvertent injury to EVs and in reducing the risk of bleeding or intracranial complications during neurosurgical procedures involving the parietal region, including burr-hole placement, craniotomies, and approaches near the superior sagittal sinus.

Limitations

The present study has specific limitations, such as the lack of examination into potential differences between males and females. Additionally, conducting in vivo studies of the foramen could be achieved using radiographic films and computed tomography scans. A larger study, maybe a confirmatory one, is required.
