A global mantle xenolith bulk-rock and paired-mineral dataset
Authors: Ben Qin, Tao Sun, Litong Li, Dongya Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This data descriptor presents a globally compiled dataset of major element concentrations for mantle peridotite and pyroxenite xenoliths. The dataset includes whole-rock analyses, constituent mineral compositions, manually collated sample ages, and pressure-temperature estimates predicted using machine learning thermobarometry models. Mantle xenoliths—fragments of lithospheric mantle transported to the surface by kimberlitic and basaltic magmas—serve as direct samples for investigating mantle evolution and heterogeneity. The initial whole-rock and minerals geochemical data were obtained from GEOROC (Geochemistry of Rocks of the Oceans and Continents) database. Following data cleaning and processing, samples were matched by comparing identifiers, references, and coordinates across mineral-specific (clinopyroxene, orthopyroxene, olivine, spinel, and garnet) databases. The results were supplemented by manual verification to ensure accurate grouping of minerals based on their common xenolith origins. This produces a globally consistent mineral assemblage dataset, comprising 19,999 rows of mantle xenolith data from approximately 547 locations. This dataset offers a comprehensive geochemical foundation for mantle-related studies.

Background & Summary

In the era of data-driven Earth science, the analysis of multi-dimensional, integrated large-scale geochemical datasets is pivotal to unraveling deep Earth processes, such as mantle dynamics and crust-mantle interaction1. The lithospheric mantle, i.e., the mantle portion of both the subcontinental lithosphere and the oceanic lithosphere (distinct from the convecting mantle), is the rigid uppermost layer of the mantle. As a critical interface between the Earth’s crust and the underlying asthenospheric mantle, it plays a key role in linking surface and deep Earth processes. It exerts profound effects on Earth’s internal dynamics (e.g., mantle convection and plate tectonics) and long-term evolution of surface environment (e.g., regulation of topography)2–7. Therefore, studying the chemical and mineralogical compositions of lithospheric mantle is not only essential for deciphering Earth’s internal structure and dynamics but also provides key insights into the material cycling and energy exchange processes between the deep interior and surface.

The lithospheric mantle is predominantly composed of peridotite, which are considered as residues following varying degrees of melting8–11. Subjected to long-term tectonic activity, mantle plume processes, and melt/fluid metasomatisms12–17, the lithospheric mantle has undergone modifications of physicochemical properties, resulting in significant compositional and structural heterogeneity. Mantle xenoliths are commonly regarded as fragments of the lithospheric mantle, which are captured by kimberlites, basalts, or other mantle-derived magmas and subsequently transported to the Earth’s surface (Fig. 1). Although pyroxenites are volumetrically subordinate in the lithospheric mantle (~5%), mounting evidence highlights their critical role in shaping mantle heterogeneity, particularly as one of the key sources of mantle-derived magmatism18. Therefore, mantle peridotite and pyroxenite xenoliths serve as direct samples for studying mantle compositional heterogeneity19,20.Fig. 1Conceptual diagram illustrating the process by which mantle xenoliths are captured by eruptive rocks. Currently, our understanding of the global mantle based on mantle xenolith data remains constrained by both the insufficient volume and multidimensional characteristics of available datasets. For example, elemental ratios of clinopyroxene in mantle xenoliths (e.g., Ca/Al and Mg# values) are widely employed to identify metasomatic processes (lithologies generated by reaction of infiltrating melts with the ambient lithospheric peridotite). This approach proves effective at a local scale with limited datasets (typically fewer than 1,000 geochemical analyses), however, its accuracy diminishes significantly when applied to larger, global-scale clinopyroxene datasets (exceeding 10,000 records)21. Furthermore, reliable estimates of equilibrated pressure and temperature of the global lithospheric mantle require extensive mineral-pair data from mantle xenoliths, as the fundamental principle of thermobarometry models is based on element partitioning equilibrium between coexisting minerals22. However, the scarcity of such paired mineral data at a global scale impedes our understanding of the lithospheric mantle. In response to the above two constraints, we have made a preliminary attempt to evaluate and predict mantle metasomatism, as well as map global lithospheric mantle pressure‐temperature conditions, using machine learning methods based on the mineral compositions of mantle xenoliths21,22. Therefore, a comprehensive dataset of global mantle xenoliths is urgently required to facilitate the excavation of additional information about the lithospheric mantle.

The GEOROC database (Geochemistry of Rocks of the Oceans and Continents) offers a fundamental resource for global geochemistry research on different ultramafic rocks23,24. However, only a subset of ultramafic rock data within this database is from mantle xenoliths, which must be selected and merged from the global ultramafic rock geochemistry dataset. Additionally, this database lacks detailed information on the mineral compositions of mantle xenoliths as well as the pyroxenite xenoliths, which is essential to understanding the mantle compositions. In addition, the utility of these raw datasets depends on rigorous preprocessing to address inherent data quality issues—a critical prerequisite for any robust geochemical big data study. Key steps include data cleaning to correct numerical inaccuracies, mislabeled information, and inconsistent symbols21,25,26. Furthermore, the dataset requires enhancement through expert-curated labels and supplementary information extracted from the original publications. This is especially true for the eruption age of the host rocks that transport mantle xenoliths to the surface, which provides an important temporal constraint on the mantle conditions at the time of xenolith entrainment. Consequently, accurate age data of host rocks are indispensable for unlocking the full utility of mantle xenolith datasets yet, such critical information is frequently absent from the original data compilation. Therefore, a high-quality, research-ready dataset of the global mantle xenoliths is developed in this study. This dataset integrates major element compositions of key minerals (clinopyroxene, orthopyroxene, olivine, spinel, garnet) and bulk compositions of peridotite and pyroxenite xenoliths (Fig. 2), complemented by eruption ages of host rocks and pressure-temperature conditions of lithospheric mantle. This multi-dimensional resource facilitates robust cross-comparison and sophisticated machine learning approaches. Methods

We retrieved the most recent precompiled datasets for the mantle-derived ultramafic rocks from the Geochemistry of Rocks of the Oceans and Continents (GEOROC) database via the Digital Geochemistry Infrastructure (DIGIS) at https://georoc.eu. The datasets, which include dunite, harzburgite, lherzolite, orthopyroxenite, peridotite, pyroxenite, and wehrlite, had not been updated as of the submission date. Each original dataset is associated with a Digital Object Identifier (DOI) to ensure traceability27–33.

After integrating all relevant rock datasets into a unified compilation of mantle-derived xenolith, samples were systematically extracted for further processing. Based on a manual screening of the literature, orogenic mantle massifs and abyssal peridotites were excluded from the analysis because they commonly record evolutionary histories that differ from xenoliths and, in practice, are rarely reported with paired mineral analyses, with most records providing only bulk-rock compositions data. Given the scarcity and uneven distribution of trace element data, we used major elements as the primary indicator of xenolith composition due to their comprehensive coverage. Additionally, including trace-element data would introduce substantial sparsity, meaning a large proportion of missing values, because trace-element coverage is highly uneven and generally scarce. Trace-element analyses represent only a subset of samples with major-element measurements, which would hinder robust matching between whole-rock and mineral datasets. In accordance with this rationale, data for elements other than major elements were excluded.

During the data cleaning stage, major element data were verified and corrected against original publications through multiple rounds of expert manual review. This process systematically addressed data issues, including wrong xenolith labels, column misalignments, special characters, entry errors, and significant anomalies.

The eruption ages of the host volcanic rocks (which carried the mantle xenoliths to the surface) and the major-element compositions of minerals (clinopyroxene, orthopyroxene, olivine, garnet, and spinel) within those xenoliths were compiled from their original publications. To resolve conflicting eruption age data for the same sample, two rules were applied. First, ages derived from isotopic analyses were prioritized over those inferred from stratigraphic correlations. Second, recognizing that age discrepancies often reflect methodological and interpretational refinements, the values from the more recent literature were adopted. For samples whose eruption ages were not documented in the literature that provided their composition data, we consulted other publications focusing on the same locality to obtain the age information.

Therefore, we have established a comprehensive thematic database of major element geochemistry for mantle xenoliths by the steps described above (Fig. 3). This integrated dataset includes the eruption ages of the xenoliths, the eruptive rock type and the bulk rock major element composition of the host ultramafic rocks, and detailed major element data for the constituent minerals within the xenoliths, with all components meticulously cross-referenced in a one-to-one correspondence.Fig. 3The data processing work flow.

The data processing work flow.

Clinopyroxene major element compositions record physicochemical evolution processes and are widely used to identify mantle metasomatism. Here, we apply a XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting) machine learning classification model built by Qin et al. (2022), trained on petrographic and geochemical data21, with an accuracy of 95% to assess the possibility of metasomatism information record in clinopyroxene geochemistry (10.5281/zenodo.6466993).

The pressure and temperature data of xenolith and mineral were obtained by XGBoost machine learning thermobarometers built by Qin et al.22. These machine learning thermobarometers were trained based on mineral data from high‐pressure and high‐temperature experiments (10.5281/zenodo.11054662). The machine learning barometers for clinopyroxene and orthopyroxene yielded test R² values of 0.71 and 0.74, respectively. The corresponding thermometers achieved test R² values of 0.82 for clinopyroxene and 0.76 for orthopyroxene.

In summary, starting from DOI-linked GEOROC/DIGIS precompiled source files, we produced a research-ready mantle xenolith dataset by (1) extracting mantle xenolith records from the global ultramafic compilations and excluding non-xenolith ultramafic datasets (e.g., orogenic mantle massifs and abyssal peridotites), (2) conducting expert manual checks to correct mislabeled entries, column misalignments, special characters, entry errors, duplicates, and obvious numerical anomalies through cross-checking against the original publications, (3) reconstructing xenolith-level coexisting mineral linkages by building an identifier-based structure (XID and mineral-specific IDs recorded in the Dictionary sheet) and compiling a xenolith-centred mineral assemblage dataset that is not explicitly provided as such in GEOROC, (4) manually compiling and harmonizing host-rock eruption ages from the source literature using explicit conflict-resolution rules and recording them as Min/Max/Mean eruption-age fields in the Xenolith sheet, and (5) adding derived fields not provided as a unified product in GEOROC, including Petrography and Metasomatism Probability in the clinopyroxene table (from a published XGBoost classifier) and equilibrium pressure–temperature estimates in the clinopyroxene and orthopyroxene tables (from published machine-learning thermobarometers).

Data Records

The dataset is available at the Zenodo repository34. This dataset comprises eight sheets named: Dictionary, Xenolith, Cpx, Opx, Ol, Grt, Spl, and Reference. The Dictionary sheet serves as a central index that connects xenolith samples to their respective mineralogical data through a system of unique identifiers (Fig. 4). The XID is a unique identifier assigned to each xenolith, which links directly to its detailed information in the Xenolith sheet. The Citation corresponds to the reference number in the Reference sheet. The Sample Name is the original sample name from the source literature. The Cpx_ID, Opx_ID, Ol_ID, Grt_ID, and Spl_ID are unique identifiers for each mineral, which correspond to their respective data in the corresponding sheets.Fig. 4Overview of the xenolith dataset structure.

Overview of the xenolith dataset structure.

The Xenolith sheet contains detailed contextual (e.g., sample name, location, rock type, age etc), petrographic, and geochemical information for each xenolith sample. The XID serves as the primary key that uniquely identifies each xenolith entry and corresponds directly to the same identifier in the Dictionary sheet. The Tectonic Setting field specifies the broad geodynamic context in which the xenolith was formed and erupted (e.g., continental rift, craton, subduction zone). The Location field describes the general geographic origin or collection site name. The Location Comment offers supplementary notes or clarifications regarding the sampling location. Precise spatial data is defined by the Latitude Min, Latitude Max, Longitude Min, and Longitude Max fields, which together specify the geographical coordinates and their uncertainty bounds, often defining a sampling area rather than a single point. The Xenolith Rock Type classifies the bulk xenolith rock according to standard petrological nomenclature based on its mineralogy and composition (e.g., lherzolite, harzburgite, wehrlite, and pyroxenite). Temporal constraints on the eruption of the volcanic host rock are provided by the Max Eruptive Age, Min Eruptive Age, and Mean Age fields, representing the maximum, minimum, and mean estimates for the eruption age (typically in millions of years), which may reflect analytical uncertainty or a range of published ages. Finally, the Eruptive Rock field identifies the volcanic rock type that carried the xenolith to the surface (e.g., basalt, kimberlite). Major element data columns (SiO2, TiO2, Al2O3, etc.) report the whole-rock geochemical composition in weight percent oxides.

The sheets for Cpx, Opx, Ol, Grt, and Spl primarily contain mineral ID links that correspond to the xenoliths listed in the Dictionary sheet, along with major element data columns (e.g., SiO2, TiO2, Al2O3, etc.). An exception is the Cpx sheet, which, as described in the Methods section, includes additional columns for Petrography—indicating secondary minerals observed within the xenolith—and Metasomatism Probability, representing the probability of metasomatism influence on the xenolith as determined by a machine-learning classification model. Additionally, both the Cpx and Opx sheets contain Pressure and Temperature fields, which reflect the equilibrium pressure–temperature conditions in the mantle derived from mineral major element compositions using machine learning thermobarometers.

This xenolith dataset is publicly available on the Zenodo repository, can be accessed via 10.5281/zenodo.18993479, and is provided in Microsoft Excel format for maximum accessibility.

Data Overview

This mantle xenoliths database synthesizes data from 489 publications spanning 1991 to 2023, encompassing 547 distinct sample locations where the number of analyses per site ranges from 1 to 100 (Fig. 5). It contains a total of 5,553 individual xenolith entries. Corresponding mineralogical data are extensive, comprising 2,800 clinopyroxene (cpx), 3,413 orthopyroxene (opx), 4,421 olivine (ol), 595 garnet (grt), and 3,217 spinel (spl) analyses.Fig. 5Global distribution and sample counts in the same location of the xenolith dataset.

The xenolith whole-rock major element compositions show SiO2 contents average 44.05 wt% (Standard Deviation = 2.93) (Fig. 6(a–j)). The xenolith whole-rock MgO contents are notably high, averaging 39.73 wt% (SD = 8.03). The xenolith whole-rock FeOT content averages 8.12 wt% (SD = 1.83), resulting in calculated Mg# values (molar MgO/(MgO + FeOT)) typically ranging from 85–92. Al2O3 concentrations average 2.59 wt% (SD = 2.70). The age distribution of host rocks spans a broad temporal range from Archean to recent (Fig. 6k), with mean eruption ages ranging from 2725 Ma to 0.005 Ma (median = 8.25 Ma). Approximately 75% of the samples have mean ages less than 39.4 Ma, indicating a strong bias toward younger geological periods, particularly Cenozoic volcanic events.

Technical Validation

During the data collection phase, verification against the original literature confirmed that 99% of the bulk composition data of mantle xenoliths are accurate, with the corresponding mineral composition data achieving 100% verification. For age data of the host volcanic rocks, priority was given to direct isotopic results. Where such precise ages were unavailable, the maximum and minimum age bounds were estimated based on the eruption periods provided in the literature. Therefore, the age data of host rocks in the dataset represent the best available information.

During the data processing stage, a comprehensive cleaning procedure was implemented to ensure the accuracy and consistency of the data. This involved the removal of all duplicate entries, followed by the systematic correction of issues including incorrect sample labels, column misalignments, special characters, entry errors, and significant anomalies. Finally, the major element compositions of the xenoliths reveals compositional characteristics of lithospheric mantle. The SiO2 contents of peridotite xenoliths yield a restricted range (average = 44.05 wt%, SD = 2.93), varying from approximately 35 to 50 wt%, which is consistent with their ultramafic nature. Moreover, most peridotite xenoliths are characterized by high MgO contents (often exceeding 37.71 wt% of the primitive mantle)35, aligning with their origins as refractory mantle residues after partial melting. This is further corroborated by the elevated MgO contents of their constituent minerals; for instance, MgO contents of Ol (median: 49.28 wt%) and Opx (median: 33.52 wt%) are even more than those of the depleted mantle (Ol: 48.59 wt%; Opx: 30.55 wt%), respectively36. In addition, compared with peridotite, pyroxenites exhibit relatively higher SiO2 and lower MgO contents (Fig. 6a,g), which is consistent with the source characteristics of pyroxenite-derived melts that are higher in silicon but poorer in magnesium37.
