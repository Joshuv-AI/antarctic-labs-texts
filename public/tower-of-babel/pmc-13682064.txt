Asymmetric metaphyseal cones versus medial modular block augment with central cone for medial tibial defects
Authors: Yi Ren, Chloe E. H. Scott, Nick D. Clement, Shuqiao Xie, Pankaj Pankaj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsManaging large medial tibial bone defects during total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains technically demanding, particularly in elderly patients. This study quantitatively compares two reconstructive strategies: ‘bridging’ the defect using an asymmetric metaphyseal cone versus ‘anatomical reconstruction’ employing a symmetrical cone with a metallic block augment, and assesses their sensitivity to osteoporotic bone quality.MethodsA finite element model of a tibia with a standardized medial defect (Anderson Orthopaedic Research Institute (AORI) Type 2a) was adapted to simulate both configurations under physiological loading cycles in healthy and severe osteoporotic bone. Cumulative interface micromotion was quantified using a coordinate-based post-processing algorithm that decouples normal separation from tangential shear. A parametric sweep of the tangential contact clearance evaluated near-surface biological interaction zones without additional simulations.ResultsIn healthy bone, both strategies demonstrated adequate stability with micromotions predominantly below 50 μm. In osteoporotic bone, the asymmetric cone exhibited a loss of stability, characterized by intermittent lift-off during walking and excessive tangential shear during stair descending. This instability induced a toggle effect at the distal stem. The augmented symmetrical cone maintained stable fixation regardless of bone quality. Expanding the tangential contact clearance revealed considerable unrecognized shear risk surrounding the asymmetric cone, while the augmented construct remained insensitive to this parameter in the high-risk range (> 100 μm).ConclusionAsymmetric cones are viable in good bone stock but exhibit instability in osteoporosis due to the cantilever effect. Anatomical reconstruction with a modular augment and central cone provides a predictable, load-sharing structural column, making it the preferred strategy for large medial defects in osteoporotic patients.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(10):1274–1287.

Introduction

Medial proximal tibial bone loss poses distinct biomechanical challenges due to the medial condyle’s primary role in weightbearing and load transfer.1 Such patterns of medial bone loss are often encountered in revision total knee arthroplasty (TKA) due to aseptic loosening with osteolysis or tibial component subsidence. Similar defects are also found in complex primary TKA when cysts or medial tibial plateau fractures cause medial proximal tibial bone loss. While lateral plateau fractures are more frequently observed, medial fractures involving subchondral insufficiency are also encountered in frail elderly patients.2 In elderly patients in whom tibial plateau fractures involve severe comminution, substantial articular depression, or pre-existing osteoarthritis, acute TKA has emerged as a reliable alternative to internal fixation, facilitating immediate mobilization.3-5 Regardless of the aetiology, the surgical management of extensive uncontained bone defects (Anderson Orthopaedic Research Institute (AORI) Type 2a) is a key factor in TKA implant survival.6

Metaphyseal cones have proved effective in reconstructing these defects by providing immediate structural support and facilitating biological fixation.7,8 When managing large uncontained medial defects, surgeons typically face a choice between two reconstructive strategies, each requiring a different approach to bone preparation. The first strategy uses an asymmetric cone to bridge the defect, filling the void but leaving some cone unsupported by bone peripherally while relying on the remaining central host bone for stability. The second involves anatomical reconstruction using a symmetrical cone combined with a tibial augment, which requires resecting the compromised bone into a standardized step-cut geometry to create a stable platform.

Bone quality strongly influences the initial stability of cementless implants. In osteoporotic bone, the structural capacity to resist tipping moments and interface micromotion is markedly reduced.9 In a connected series of finite element (FE) studies, Ren et al10-12 established stability thresholds for asymmetric metaphyseal cones in lateral and medial tibial plateau fractures and in osteoporotic metaphyseal defects. FE comparisons of cone and augment constructs have been reported.13 The present analysis resolves interface micromotion and bone strain as cumulative, path-dependent quantities across the full physiological gait cycle, rather than at peak or static load.14 It is hypothesized that the cantilever action of an asymmetric cone may produce greater micromotion and bone overload in osteoporotic bone than the direct columnar support of an augment.

This study aims to compare the biomechanical performance of asymmetric cones versus augmented symmetrical cones for managing large medial tibial defects. By simulating physiological loading conditions in both healthy and severe osteoporotic bone models, we seek to quantify cumulative interface micromotion and cancellous bone strain, informing implant selection in elderly patients.

Methods

Finite element model construction and defect simulation

An established 3D FE model of a synthetic medium-sized tibia served as the base geometry.10,11,15,16 In this model, the cortical shell and cancellous core were represented as the distinct geometrical regions of the synthetic tibia rather than being derived from CT attenuation thresholds. The proximal tibia was resected 9 mm distal to the lateral plateau perpendicular to the mechanical axis to simulate a standard primary TKA cut (Figure 1a). To represent a clinical scenario requiring modular reconstruction, a large, uncontained medial tibial bone defect (AORI Type 2a) was introduced. This defect was modelled with a standardized step-cut geometry (L-shaped in the coronal plane) with a depth of 10 mm, corresponding to the dimensions of a standard 10 mm tibial augment (Figure 1a). This configuration simulates the extensive bone resection required to prepare a stable platform for anatomical reconstruction, representing a worst-case scenario of bone loss. The FE mesh was generated using ten-node quadratic tetrahedral elements (C3D10), with localized refinement applied at the bone-implant interfaces. Mesh convergence analysis confirmed that further refinement beyond the selected density resulted in less than 1% variation in the relative displacement between paired nodes at the bone-implant interface. The nominal element edge length was 1 to 3 mm, and all analyses were solved in Abaqus/Standard (Dassault Systèmes, France) using an implicit, quasi-static formulation without mass scaling.

Implant configurations and surgical strategies

Two surgical reconstruction strategies were simulated to address this standardized 10 mm step-cut defect using a universal tibial baseplate (Size 5, 5521-B-500; Stryker Orthopaedics, USA) and a short, cemented stem (5560-S-109, 9 mm diameter × 50 mm length) (Figure 1a). Baseplate coverage was 89% with a maximum overhang of 1 mm. The first strategy, designated as the defect-bridging group, used an asymmetric metaphyseal cone (Size C, 5549-A-231) to bridge the void. In this configuration, the asymmetric lobe spanned the step-cut defect, relying principally on the remaining host bone for fixation without additional modular augmentation. The second strategy, the anatomical reconstruction group, used a symmetrical metaphyseal cone (Size B, 5549-A-120) combined with a 10 mm thick augment (Size 5, 5546-A-501). This configuration completely refilled the resected step-cut volume, creating a direct column of structural support from the augment to the cone and stem. A cement mantle varying from 1 to 5 mm in thickness was modelled to fill the interstitial spaces, with bonded contact defined at cement–implant interfaces to represent fully cured bone cement.

Material properties and interface mechanics

All biological tissues and implant materials were modelled as homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic (Table I).17-20 The FE model used frictional contact definitions for the cone-bone interface, assigning friction coefficients of 0.35 to the titanium surface and 1.01 to the porous coating.21-23 To represent the porous structure, a simplified volumetric layer with a lower elastic modulus was used. The cement layer served as a barrier between the cone and adjacent implant components. Both the cement-implant and cement-bone interfaces were modelled as fully bonded (tie) constraints, representing the stability of cured bone cement. To evaluate the influence of bone quality, two bone conditions were simulated. A ‘severe osteoporosis’ model was developed to represent the compromised bone stock typical of elderly patients, wherein the Young’s moduli of cortical and cancellous bone were reduced to 40% and 28% of the healthy values, respectively. These site-specific reductions follow a staged scheme that reflects the disproportionately greater loss of trabecular density and connectivity relative to cortical thinning in osteoporotic bone.12,20,24

Loading conditions and biomechanical analysis

The models were subjected to four physiological loading scenarios (Figure 1b): standing up (SU), knee bending (KB), walking (WA), and stair descending (SD). These activities were selected to span a range of mechanical demands, with force and moment components in lateral (X), superior (Y), and anterior (Z) directions derived from standardized telemetric implant measurements for a 75 kg individual.25 All loads were applied to a reference point kinematically coupled to all nodes on the superior surface of the baseplate, while the distal tibia was fully constrained at the section 200 mm away from the baseplate. Each of the four activities was applied as a single normalized loading cycle rather than as a set of discrete load cases, with the force and moment histories ramped incrementally; the abscissa of the loading curves (Figure 1b) therefore represents normalized cycle time (0% to 100%) rather than absolute duration, consistent with the quasi-static formulation.

Standard FE outputs can underestimate cumulative micromotion during complex, reciprocating gait cycles due to path-dependence. Therefore, a coordinate-based post-processing algorithm, the Solver-Decoupled Kinematic Framework,14 was employed to quantify implant stability. This method tracked the trajectory of paired nodes at the bone-implant interface at each time increment to compute the true scalar motion path, ensuring that the cumulative history of motion was preserved even during intermittent contact separation. This approach decoupled interfacial motion into normal (separation/lift-off) and tangential (sliding/shear) components relative to the local surface curvature. This distinction allowed precise differentiation between failure modes driven by instability (lift-off) and those driven by friction and wear (sliding). Implant stability was assessed on the tangential component against the threshold for osseointegration (150 μm), with values below 50 μm considered optimal for long-term stability.26,27 As cumulative tangential motion is never smaller than the tangential motion amplitude during contact, a cumulative value below a threshold implies that the amplitude also remained below it, whereas a value above a threshold does not by itself indicate exceedance. Normal micromotion was evaluated by its distribution across the interface rather than against these thresholds.

The coordinate-based algorithm defines tangential contact clearance (ϵt) as a kinematic threshold that governs the geometrical envelope within which tangential motion is accumulated. Regions where the normal distance between paired nodes falls within ϵt are included in the tangential motion calculation, regardless of the instantaneous solver contact state. This parameter can be interpreted as a near-surface biological interaction zone: areas that the solver classifies as separated but where biological processes such as early tissue formation or gap-bridging may still subject the interface to shear. Because the clearance operates purely on the archived displacement field, its value can be varied in post-processing without re-running the finite element simulation. The baseline analysis used ϵt = 0 μm (strict solver contact only). To evaluate the sensitivity of the tangential micromotion assessment to this parameter, a parametric sweep was performed on the two osteoporotic configurations exhibiting the highest micromotion (Asy-SD-OP and Aug-SD-OP), with ϵt varied from 0 to 100 μm (0, 5, 10, 50, and 100 μm). The normal clearance was maintained at zero throughout to preserve the strict characterization of macroscopic separation.

Bone mechanical response was evaluated by quantifying the volume of cancellous bone exceeding physiological yield limits (> 0.5% in tension and < −-0.7% in compression).24,28

Results

Implant stability and interface micromotion

The temporal evolution of the bone-coating contact area varied across bone qualities and surgical strategies (Figure 2). In all configurations, osteoporotic models exhibited higher contact area percentages compared with healthy bone models. However, the magnitude of this increase differed between strategies: the augmented symmetrical cone exhibited a more moderate increase and maintained temporal stability throughout the loading cycles. In contrast, the asymmetric cone demonstrated marked fluctuations in the osteoporotic environment. Specifically, during the phase of the walking cycle with lower axial forces (Figure 1b), a pronounced reduction in contact area was observed for the asymmetric cone (decreasing from approximately 40% to 20%). This indicates that intermittent implant lift-off driven by the unbalanced cantilever moment is a source of temporal instability.

This instability was accompanied by increased interface micromotion, with spatial distributions that varied by activity type and motion component (Figure 3 and Figure 4). In static and low-demand activities (SU and KB), both strategies maintained adequate stability with micromotions in both components predominantly distributed below 50 μm. However, under high-demand dynamic loading, the asymmetric cone in osteoporotic bone exhibited activity-specific failure mechanisms. During walking (WA), instability was driven largely by normal separation (lift-off). While peak tangential micromotion remained below the absolute threshold for osseointegration (< 150 μm) across all configurations, the asymmetric cone in osteoporotic bone exhibited a marked loss of stability. High-magnitude micromotion extended across the unsupported medial rim, considerably reducing the safety margin compared with the healthy baseline. This pattern correlated with the contact area loss, confirming that the cantilever bending moment caused the implant to separate from the bone surface. Conversely, the SD activity exhibited a motion pattern dominated by tangential shear. While the peak cumulative micromotion magnitude in SD was comparable to WA (91 μm tangential vs 97 μm normal), the distribution was spatially concentrated at load-bearing regions rather than the broad separation seen in WA. In contrast, the augmented symmetrical cone mitigated normal separation across all configurations. In healthy bone, normal micromotion remained below the 20 μm reporting threshold across all activities. In osteoporotic bone, modest normal micromotion was observed under high-demand loading (WA and SD), although the proportion of interface area exceeding 20 μm remained appreciably lower than that of the asymmetric cone. Tangential micromotion in the augmented construct followed a similar pattern, with well-controlled values in healthy bone and moderate increases under osteoporotic high-demand loading (Figure 4).

Cancellous bone strains and overload

The mechanical consequences of instability were evaluated through the temporal evolution of cancellous bone strains (Figure 5). The volume of bone exceeding physiological yield limits (defined as > 0.5% in tension and < -0.7% in compression) fluctuated in phase with the gait cycles, with distinct peaks corresponding to the maximum force and moment phases defined in the loading protocols (Figure 1b). While walking induced widespread interface separation, the SD activity imposed the most severe structural demand. At the critical timepoint of maximum overload during SD, the asymmetric cone resulted in an overloaded bone volume of 4.2%, compared with 3.5% for the augmented symmetrical cone. This represents a relative increase of approximately 20% in overloaded bone volume when using the bridging strategy in osteoporotic bone.

To further investigate the failure mechanism, strain distributions were analyzed at the critical instant of maximum bone overload for each configuration (Figure 6). This breakdown revealed configuration-dependent failure modes. In the compromised Asy-OP models, total overload was predominantly driven by bone volumes exceeding the tensile threshold. This dominance of tensile overload aligns with the lift-off mechanism, indicating that the rocking of the unsupported implant generates high traction forces on the underlying bone. Spatial strain contours (Figure 7 and Figure 8; Supplementary Figures S1 and S2) revealed different distribution patterns. Primary strain concentrations were located at the proximal cone base, specifically at the medial fulcrum and lateral periphery. Secondary strain concentrations were evident at the distal stem. The morphology of these distal strains was also activity-dependent. While the proximal concentration remained consistent, the strain distribution at the distal stem varied noticeably between WA and SD (Figure 8 and Supplementary Figure S2). The distal strain distribution shifted spatially in response to the changing resultant force vectors specific to the activity’s peak loading phase, consistent with a distal toggle mechanism. In contrast, the augmented symmetrical cone exhibited a more uniform strain distribution, where the metallic augment acted as a structural column to share the proximal load, thereby mitigating both the proximal strain concentrations and the secondary strain concentration at the distal stem.

Sensitivity to tangential contact clearance

The influence of the tangential contact clearance ϵt on cumulative tangential micromotion was evaluated for the stair descending activity in osteoporotic bone, where the baseline analysis identified the highest tangential shear demands (Figure 9). For the asymmetric cone (Asy-SD-OP), increasing ϵt from 0 to 5 μm produced a marked increase in the proportion of interface area exceeding 30 μm, as regions in near-contact that were excluded from the baseline assessment were brought into the tracking envelope. Further increases from 5 to 10 μm continued to expand the affected area, with the proportion of interface exceeding 100 μm also increasing. Beyond 10 μm, the results approached a plateau, with only marginal changes observed between ϵt = 50 μm and ϵt = 100 μm. This convergence indicates that the majority of near-surface regions contributing to tangential shear risk are concentrated within approximately 10 μm of the contact boundary. For the augmented symmetrical cone (Aug-SD-OP), a similar expansion of the tracked area was observed with increasing ϵt, but no interface area exceeded 100 μm at any clearance value. The sensitivity analysis therefore confirms that the asymmetric cone harbours a considerably larger near-surface zone of elevated micromotion that is not captured by standard contact-only assessment. The augmented construct, by contrast, remained insensitive to this parameter in the high-risk range, consistent with its stable load transfer mechanism.

Discussion

This study quantitatively evaluated two surgical strategies for managing large medial tibial defects, bridging with an asymmetric cone versus anatomical reconstruction with an augment and a central symmetrical cone, across different bone qualities. While both strategies provided adequate initial stability in healthy bone, their performance diverged markedly in the presence of osteoporosis. Our results indicate that relying on an asymmetric cone to bridge a large medial step-cut defect is sensitive to bone quality degradation. Although peak tangential micromotion remained within the theoretical limits for bone ingrowth (< 150 μm), the safety margin was reduced compared with the augmented construct. The asymmetric configuration exhibited greater susceptibility to cantilever-induced destabilization, whereas the use of a modular augment to anatomically refill the defect created a stable structural column, mitigating these risks regardless of bone quality.

Mechanisms of instability: the cantilever effect

The biomechanical divergence between the two strategies arises from the load transfer mechanism relative to host bone stiffness. The asymmetric cone functions as a cantilever beam when the underlying bone support is absent. In healthy bone, the high elastic modulus of the host trabecular bone provides sufficient clamping force at the porous interface to counteract the bending moment caused by physiological loads.29,30 However, the reduced stiffness of osteoporotic bone permits greater elastic deformation and settling of the host bone surface against the implant. While this settling initially increases the contact area (as observed in our results), it fails to provide rigid support against the tipping moment generated during dynamic activities. This reduction in support creates a disproportionate mechanical response in the asymmetric cone, consistent with the amplification of instability previously reported across progressively reduced bone-quality grades.12 This structural vulnerability manifests as a ‘rocking’ motion, as evidenced by the pronounced loss of contact area and the pronounced normal separation (lift-off) observed in our coordinate-based micromotion analysis. Whereas previous FE studies used scalar equivalent micromotion,10,16,31 our coordinate-based analysis decoupled normal and tangential motion components. This revealed activity-dependent failure modes: while walking induced instability primarily through normal separation (lift-off) due to the cantilever bending moment, stair descending imposed critical tangential shear demands on the load-bearing interface. This distinction is clinically relevant, as interface separation inhibits biological ingrowth, whereas excessive tangential shear risks abrasion and fibrous tissue formation.26

This decomposition also invites a reappraisal of the widely cited micromotion threshold for osseointegration. The classical criterion of 150 μm was derived from resultant measurements that do not distinguish between normal and tangential components. From a mechanistic standpoint, however, these components represent distinct biological consequences. Normal separation eliminates contact pressure and therefore removes the mechanical stimulus required for bone formation;32,33 even small magnitudes of sustained lift-off preclude osseointegration regardless of the tangential state. Tangential motion, by contrast, occurs exclusively during contact and reflects the transmission of shear forces across the interface. Moderate tangential shear under compressive load may in fact provide the mechanical stimulus associated with bone remodelling, whereas excessive tangential sliding drives abrasion and fibrous encapsulation.26,34 Consequently, the applicability of a single scalar threshold to both components is questionable. While normal micromotion serves as a direct indicator of mechanical instability, the biological consequence of tangential micromotion depends additionally on the concurrent contact pressure, an aspect not captured by kinematic analysis alone. The present findings suggest that normal separation should be minimized towards zero as a prerequisite for ingrowth. The cumulative normal metric and its spatial distribution across the interface (Figure 3 and Figure 4) capture both the magnitude and the spatial extent of repeated separation events, providing a more comprehensive characterization of fixation stability than a single peak value. Tangential micromotion, in turn, requires evaluation within the context of the local loading environment.

Proximal-distal mechanical coupling

The results also demonstrated mechanical coupling between proximal defect management and distal stem kinematics. The ‘rocking’ of the proximal asymmetric cone acts as a toggle, shifting the fulcrum and forcing the distal stem tip to lever against the medullary canal. This creates activity-dependent strain concentrations at the distal stem, specifically shifting between walking and stair descending. This ‘toggle effect’ provides a biomechanical explanation for the clinical incidence of stem tip pain, which has been reported in up to 14% of revision TKA patients and is often associated with unstable metaphyseal fixation.35,36 By reconstructing the medial column with an augment, the construct converts the bending moment into direct compressive load transfer, thereby mitigating the toggle effect and reducing excessive strain at the distal stem.

Comparison with previous studies and loading protocols

These findings contrast with the investigation by Ren et al11 of asymmetric cones in medial tibial plateau fractures. In that study, asymmetric cones demonstrated adequate stability for medial uncontained defects, even with unsupported area ratios reaching 52%. However, that investigation modelled healthy bone under monotonic loading. The contrast in results highlights that the ‘bridging’ strategy, which is viable for healthy medial defects, cannot be extrapolated to osteoporotic bone, where the mechanical demands are considerably higher. The combination of higher medial loads and compromised bone quality in the current study exceeded the mechanical limits of the asymmetric design. These observations are consistent with earlier FE analyses of augmented revision-TKA constructs, which identified the modular augment as an effective means of restoring load transfer across uncontained metaphyseal defects.37,38 Furthermore, previous biomechanical evaluations predominantly used simplified static loading. While effective for assessing peak stress, monotonic loading fails to capture the dynamic fluctuations of the gait cycle. In this study, the inclusion of the full gait cycle revealed that the critical ‘lift-off’ event for the asymmetric cone occurred during the swing phase, a period of low axial force that is not captured by the peak-load analyses used in earlier studies.39

Clinical implications

For the orthopaedic surgeon, these findings support a stratified treatment approach based on preoperative bone quality assessment. In patients with healthy bone stock, the asymmetric cone remains a viable, bone-preserving option that simplifies the surgical technique. However, in elderly patients with osteoporosis, an increasingly common clinical scenario, the efficiency of bridging the defect comes at the cost of reduced mechanical stability. In osteoporotic cases, where bone stock is compromised, the ‘reconstruction’ strategy, using a symmetrical cone with a modular augment, may offer a more predictable mechanical environment, preventing the pathological lift-off and tensile strains that predispose implants to early failure.40 Furthermore, because periprosthetic bone quality continues to decline with age, a cone that is adequately supported at the time of implantation may face a progressively greater risk of late instability as the surrounding bone deteriorates, reinforcing the value of preoperative bone-quality assessment, for which quantitative CT measures such as Hounsfield-unit analysis are increasingly available.41

The parametric sensitivity analysis on the tangential contact clearance further reinforces this distinction. By expanding the kinematic tracking envelope beyond strict solver contact, regions that standard analyses classify as safely separated were shown to harbour considerable tangential shear for the asymmetric cone. This finding is clinically relevant because the immediate postoperative environment is not a rigid metal-on-bone interface but rather a dynamic biological zone where fibrous tissue, haematoma, and early bone formation span micro-gaps at the implant surface. The clearance sweep provides a conservative estimate of the tangential motion these tissues would experience, and the rapid convergence of the results beyond ϵt = 10 μm suggests that this assessment is insensitive to the precise extent of biological gap-bridging. That the augmented construct remained stable across the full range of ϵt values confirms its mechanical advantage is not contingent on idealized contact conditions, but is preserved even when the effective interaction zone is considerably broadened.

Limitations include the modelling of bone as an isotropic linear elastic material, which does not capture post-yield plastic deformation. However, as the primary focus was on initial stability (micromotion) rather than long-term remodelling, this is standard practice in comparative FE studies.42,43 Although cortical bone is in reality anisotropic, an isotropic representation was applied identically to both constructs, so that differences in fixation could be attributed to the reconstruction strategy rather than to direction-dependent material behaviour. Additionally, only one specific defect geometry (AORI Type 2a step-cut) was simulated; defects with different morphologies or containment levels may exhibit different stability thresholds. Likewise, a single implant system and a neutrally (mechanically) aligned tibia without pre-existing axial deformity were simulated; personalized alignment strategies or a pre-existing varus deformity would alter the medial load distribution, and a medializing deformity in particular would be expected to aggravate rather than relieve the cantilever loading of an asymmetric cone, whereas the columnar load transfer of the augmented construct would be expected to remain advantageous. The clearance sensitivity analysis operates on the archived displacement field from a single simulation and does not recalculate force equilibrium at the expanded contact boundary. The resulting tangential motion estimates therefore represent a conservative kinematic bound, as true biological bridging would introduce additional stiffness that constrains relative displacement. Surrounding soft tissues and the extensor mechanism were not modelled explicitly; their net stabilizing contribution is partly embedded in the telemetric joint loads applied as boundary conditions, although the local constraint provided by the joint capsule and collateral ligaments was not represented.25 Finally, this study is purely computational and lacks direct experimental validation using cadaveric or synthetic bone models. While the absolute magnitudes of micromotion and strain should be interpreted with caution, the relative biomechanical differences observed between the two surgical strategies remain informative for guiding clinical decision-making.

In conclusion, the findings indicate that both asymmetric bridging and anatomical reconstruction strategies are viable options for managing large medial tibial defects, as peak tangential micromotions for both configurations remained below the critical threshold for osseointegration (< 150 μm), even in osteoporotic bone. However, the mechanical impact of osteoporosis proved to be strategy-dependent. The asymmetric cone exhibited heightened sensitivity to bone quality degradation, manifesting as intermittent lift-off and distal stem toggling that markedly eroded the safety margin for fixation. Parametric analysis of the tangential contact clearance further revealed that the near-surface zone surrounding the asymmetric cone harbours appreciable unrecognized tangential shear risk that is not captured by standard contact-only assessment. In contrast, the augmented symmetrical cone demonstrated consistent stability regardless of host bone quality. For defects amenable to medial block augmentation, the augmented strategy offers a more predictable mechanical environment with superior resistance to the instabilities observed in compromised bone stock. The asymmetric cone remains a feasible choice in healthy bone, but the augmented construct is the more reliable option in the presence of osteoporosis.
