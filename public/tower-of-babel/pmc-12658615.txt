Cannabis Use and Risk of Chronic Rhinosinusitis and Sinus Surgery
Authors: Austin J. Lee, Michael W. Liu, David C. Kaelber, Mohamad R. Chaaban
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveWhile cannabis' link to asthma is well‐studied, its impact on CRS is less clear. This study explores the association between cannabis use and rates of new‐onset chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS), chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP), and functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS) rates.MethodsThe TriNetX Analytics Research Network was queried for adults ≥ 18 years old, stratified into cannabis user and non‐user cohorts based on electronic health record data from January 2012 to December 2019. Separate cohorts of patients with pre‐existing CRS—with and without cannabis use—were analyzed to evaluate associations with FESS. Primary outcomes were relative risks of new‐onset CRS and CRSwNP encounter diagnosis and FESS 1, 2, and 5 years after initial cannabis use diagnosis.ResultsAfter 1:1 propensity score matching, cohorts analyzing CRS and CRSwNP included 73,091 patients each. Cannabis use was associated with reduced risk of unspecified CRS at 1 year (aRR = 0.87, 95% CI 0.80–0.95), 2 years (aRR = 0.84, 95% CI 0.78–0.90), and 5 years (aRR = 0.83, 95% CI 0.78–0.87). There was no difference in risk of CRSwNP at any timepoints. For FESS outcomes, matched cohorts included 5591 patients with pre‐existing CRS; cannabis users had lower risk at 1 year (aRR = 0.67, 95% CI 0.47–0.96), 2 years (aRR = 0.64, 95% CI 0.46–0.88), and 5 years (aRR = 0.69, 95% CI 0.52–0.91).ConclusionsPatients with cannabis use demonstrated significantly reduced risks in new‐onset diagnoses of CRS and FESS compared to non‐users. Further studies are warranted to examine the cause of this relationship.Level of Evidence4.

Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) is a common inflammatory condition of the paranasal sinuses with an estimated prevalence of up to 12.5% in the US population [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. CRS is defined as symptomatic inflammation of the sinonasal tract lasting 12 weeks or longer, with diagnosis requiring the presence of at least two of four key symptoms (nasal obstruction, facial pain, pressure, fullness, or congestion, mucopurulent drainage, and hyposmia) with objective evidence of inflammation, documented through nasal endoscopy or through CT imaging showing mucosal changes in the sinuses [6, 7]. It significantly impacts quality of life and poses substantial economic burdens on healthcare systems [8, 9, 10, 11]. Risk factors for CRS, including tobacco smoking and air pollution, are well documented, while its relationship with other inflammatory conditions like asthma and allergic rhinitis is increasingly recognized [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. However, while much attention is given to these factors, the potential impact of cannabis use on CRS development remains underexplored.

Cannabis is one of the most widely used recreational substances in the United States, with one study estimating 17.7 million daily or near‐daily cannabis users in 2022 [17]. Its popularity continues to grow, partly due to increasing legalization for both medicinal and recreational use [18, 19]. The primary mode of cannabis consumption is through inhalation, which directly affects the upper airway and respiratory tract [20, 21, 22]. Cannabis use has thus been shown to affect asthma, an inflammatory airway condition closely related to CRS due to overlapping epithelial types and patterns of inflammation [23]. Goodwin et al. found current asthma was more common among individuals who reported cannabis use in the past 30 days compared to those who did not, with the risk of asthma significantly higher in individuals using cannabis 20–30 days per month [24]. Moreover, a study by Winhusen et al. found that regular cannabis use, with or without concurrent tobacco use, was linked to higher asthma risk, with an adjusted odds ratio of 1.50 [25]. This association is further reflected in treatment patterns, as a longitudinal study by Bramness and von Soest found current cannabis users had higher odds of filling prescriptions for asthma medications [26].

Despite strong evidence demonstrating the association between asthma and cannabis consumption, there is limited research on how cannabis use affects the upper airway. In the only study to examine cannabis exposure in human nasal epithelium, Rouabhia et al. reported that cannabis smoke condensate negatively affected epithelial cellular behaviors, including growth, adhesion, and migration, suggesting a potential mechanism for the development of sinonasal disorders [27]. However, that study utilized an in vitro model, limiting its direct applicability to clinical outcomes such as a confirmed diagnosis of CRS. Additionally, a review of unintended cannabis side effects across otolaryngology only included studies reporting associations with sinusitis and allergic rhinitis, without mentioning CRS [28]. These studies also did not specifically mention chronic rhinosinusitis, nor define it per the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) definition. To date, only one study has specifically examined the impact of cannabis use on CRS, though it focused on a population with pre‐existing CRS [29].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association of cannabis use with the development of new‐onset CRS, CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP), and subsequent functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS) in the United States population, as well as the first to utilize a national electronic health records (EHR) database to conduct this research. Given the rising prevalence of both cannabis use and CRS, our findings may enhance clinicians' ability to counsel patients on potential changes related to sinonasal and airway inflammation associated with cannabis consumption.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

The US Collaborative Network within the TriNetX Analytics platform consists of aggregated and de‐identified EHR data of more than 100 million patients in 69 health care organizations. This retrospective study is exempt from informed consent. The data reviewed is a secondary analysis of existing data, does not involve intervention or interaction with human subjects, and is de‐identified per the de‐identification standard defined in Section §164.514(a) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule. The process by which the data is de‐identified is attested to through a formal determination by a qualified expert as defined in Section §164.514(b)(1) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule. This formal determination by a qualified expert was refreshed in December 2020. This study was also approved as exempt human subject research by the Cleveland Clinic IRB. We performed a retrospective cohort study by querying the TriNetX Analytics platform using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10), Current Procedural Terminology (CPT), and EHR‐reported demographics to define our cohorts, matched variables, and outcomes. This article was written in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study Population

All patients included in this study were 18 years or older. Patients were divided into two cohorts: a cannabis user cohort and a nonuser cohort. Using methodology previously outlined by TrinetX studies identifying cannabis use [30, 31], patients in the cannabis user cohort had the following terms in their EHR between January 2012 and December 2019: Cannabis‐related disorder (ICD‐10 F12), Cannabinoids, natural (CPT 80349), Cannabidiol (RxNorm 2045371) and a recorded outpatient hospital clinic visit (Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System, HCPCS: G0463). The nonuser control cohort consisted of patients with no record of cannabis use before or up to 5 years after a recorded outpatient hospital clinic visit. To accurately evaluate the association between cannabis consumption and FESS, two separate cohorts of patients with existing CRS were constructed: one with cannabis use and the other without cannabis use.

Patients with a history of CRS, CRSwNP, or FESS prior to the index event (date of cannabis use diagnosis and/or outpatient hospital clinic visit) were excluded from the study. All analyses were completed in April 2025. The summary of patient selection, propensity score matching (PSM), and cohort comparison is illustrated in Figure 1.

Outcomes of Interest

The primary outcomes of interest were new encounter diagnoses of unspecified CRS (ICD‐10 J32), CRSwNP (ICD‐10J33), and FESS (CPT 31253, 31254, 31255, 31256, 31257, 31259, 31267, 31276, 31287, 31288). These associations were analyzed within 1, 2, and 5 years of the index event.

Propensity Score Matching

Demographic data including age at diagnosis, race, ethnicity, and comorbidities were collected for each cohort. Patients in the experimental cohorts and control cohort were propensity‐score matched by age, sex, race, and comorbidities, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes, gastro‐esophageal disease (GERD), vasomotor and allergic rhinitis, overweight and obesity, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, and tobacco use. Statistical Analysis

All statistics were performed using the TriNetX built‐in advanced analytics tools for comparison of cohorts. Adjusted relative risks (aRR) between the experimental and control cohorts were calculated with a 95% confidence interval (CI). The platform compares baseline and matched patient characteristics with chi‐squared tests for categorical variables and independent sample t‐tests for continuous variables.

Results

At the time of analysis, there were a total of 73,091 patients in the cannabis user cohort and 2,452,201 patients in the non‐user cohort. The incidence of a new‐encounter ICD‐10 diagnosis of unspecified CRS within 1 year was 1.28% for the cannabis user group and 1.21% for the non‐user cohort. When increasing the follow‐up period to within 2 years, the incidence of a new‐encounter ICD‐10 diagnosis of unspecified CRS was 2.04% for the cannabis user group and 1.99% for the non‐user cohort. At 5 years, the incidence of a new‐encounter ICD‐10 diagnosis of unspecified CRS was 3.82% for cannabis users and 3.77% for the non‐user cohort.

For analysis of rates of FESS, there were a total of 5598 adult patients diagnosed with pre‐existing CRS and concurrent cannabis use and 171,209 with pre‐existing CRS and no cannabis use. The incidence of FESS within 1 year was 0.92% for cannabis users and 1.64% for the non‐user cohort. At 2 years, the incidence of FESS was 1.06% for cannabis users and 1.90% for the non‐user cohort. At 5 years, the incidence of FESS was 1.47% for cannabis users and 2.32% for the non‐user cohort. The incidences of new‐encounter ICD‐10 encounter diagnosis of CRS, CRSwNP, and FESS within 1, 2, and 5 years are reported in Tables 3 and 4.

Relative Risks of New‐Onset Unspecified CRS and CRSwNP

After 1:1 propensity score‐matching, each cohort contained 73,078 patients. The adjusted relative risk of a new‐onset ICD‐10 diagnosis of CRS within 1 year was significantly reduced in the cannabis user cohort compared to the non‐user cohort (aRR = 0.87, 95% CI 0.80–0.95). This protective association persisted within 2 years (aRR = 0.84, 95% CI 0.78–0.90). Finally, at 5 years, the adjusted relative risk of a new‐onset CRS diagnosis in cannabis users was significantly lower (aRR = 0.83, 95% CI 0.78–0.87) than that of the non‐user cohort (Table 3).

There were no significant differences in rates of new‐onset ICD‐10 diagnosis of CRSwNP between cannabis users and non‐users at any timepoints.

Relative Risks of Functional Endoscopic Sinus Surgery

After 1:1 propensity matching, each cohort consisted of 5591 patients. The adjusted relative risk of undergoing FESS within 1 year of the index event was significantly lower in the cannabis user cohort than in the non‐user cohort (aRR = 0.67, 95% CI 0.47–0.96). This association was again observed at 2 years (aRR = 0.64, 95% CI 0.46–0.88) and 5 years (aRR = 0.69, 95% CI 0.52–0.91) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study is the first to leverage a very large aggregated, de‐identified EHR platform to examine the association between cannabis use and new‐onset CRS, CRSwNP, and FESS. Previous studies have successfully employed this database to investigate associations between various risk factors and CRS incidence and management [32, 33, 34, 35]. We observed that cannabis users had a lower risk of new‐onset CRS diagnoses compared to non‐users. Among patients with existing CRS, cannabis users also had a lower risk of requiring FESS than non‐users. Given that FESS is typically reserved for patients who have failed appropriate medical therapy—including saline irrigation, nasal steroids, antibiotics, and oral steroids [7]—these findings suggest that cannabis use is associated with a reduced likelihood of surgical intervention in patients with CRS. This contrasts with the association established between cannabis use and asthma. The predominance of inhaled cannabis use may partly explain this discrepancy; inhalation has been associated with lower airway inflammation and higher asthma risk [36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. The apparent protective association between cannabis consumption and CRS observed in this study may be confounded by access to care, health‐seeking behaviors, or the anti‐inflammatory effects of cannabinoids themselves, which might offset the pro‐inflammatory impact of smoke in certain CRS endotypes. However, further research is warranted to provide a definitive mechanism for the two different impacts observed with cannabis use in asthma and CRS.

Only one study exists that specifically investigates the link between cannabis use and CRS diagnosis. Awad et al. demonstrated that patients who smoked tobacco and cannabis experienced significantly more severe CRS compared to those who smoked only tobacco, as measured by the SNOT‐20, Lund‐Mackay CT score, and Lund‐Kennedy endoscopy score [27]. However, in our analysis of comparable patient cohorts, we observed contrasting findings—patients with concurrent cannabis use and tobacco dependence had a lower risk of new‐onset CRS compared to those with tobacco dependence alone (Tables S1 and S2). One possible explanation lies in the distinct clinical questions addressed: Awad et al. examined disease severity in patients with established CRS, while our study evaluated the incidence of newly diagnosed CRS. In addition, regional differences in cannabis use may influence outcomes; the Awad study was conducted in Canada, where legalization and cultural norms may lead to more frequent or heavier use compared to the US cohort in our study. Furthermore, variations in the underlying inflammatory endotype of CRS, particularly the balance between eosinophilic and neutrophilic phenotypes, may affect how cannabis exposure shapes disease expression.

These divergent findings are further contextualized by mixed evidence from population‐based studies. In support of our findings, a cross‐sectional study by Orozco et al. using data from the 2013–2014 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey found that regular cannabis users reported lower rates of sinonasal symptoms, including nasal congestion, sinus pain, and olfactory changes—hallmark symptoms of CRS—compared to non‐users [41]. While the study relied on self‐reported symptoms and did not confirm CRS diagnoses, it aligns with the possibility that cannabis use may attenuate symptom burden. In contrast, an earlier analysis of over 29,000 individuals from the 2005–2007 National Surveys on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH) reported a modest association between marijuana use and increased odds of self‐reported sinusitis (OR = 1.23; 95% CI 0.99–1.28) [42]. However, this study did not differentiate between acute and chronic sinusitis, nor did it rely on physician‐confirmed diagnoses, limiting its relevance to chronic disease pathophysiology.

Together, these findings highlight the challenge of interpreting self‐reported data across heterogeneous populations and support the need for prospective, phenotype‐specific studies. Furthermore, one possible explanation for our observed reduction in CRS incidence among cannabis users may relate to behavioral factors—cannabis use could alter symptom perception or healthcare‐seeking behavior, leading to fewer diagnoses independent of the actual inflammatory burden.

Orozco et al. reported that cannabis use was associated with a decrease in patient‐reported sinonasal symptoms, a finding that may be a reflection of the analgesic effects of cannabis use [41]. Cannabinoids, particularly THC, have been shown to provide small, although not insignificant, improvements in pain relief for chronic conditions, especially when inhaled [43, 44]. These effects may potentially mask the sinonasal symptoms of CRS, leading to fewer healthcare visits and subsequent diagnoses. This hypothesis aligns with previous research suggesting that cannabis use can alter pain perception and reduce the use of pain medications [45, 46, 47]. Additionally, previous studies have shown that cannabis use is underreported, both to physicians and within the EHR. An anonymous online survey study on cannabis use and attitude within the Adult Allergy and Asthma network found that only 40.9% of physicians inquired about cannabis use, while only 37.5% of patient users desired discussion of cannabis use [48]. Another cross‐sectional survey study among 5000 randomly selected patients found the prevalence of patient‐reported medical cannabis use was 38.8% while the prevalence of EHR‐documented cannabis use was just 4.8%, indicating that most cannabis use is not documented within medical records [49]. Whether this is due to the persistent stigma surrounding cannabis use or the federally illicit nature of the substance, this may cause users to be less likely to seek medical attention, especially for mild to moderate symptoms that characterize conditions like CRS. This behavior could explain the observed protective association without necessarily implying a biological reduction in sinus inflammation and highlights the importance of inquiring about cannabis use in physician‐patient interactions.

In addition to these effects on sinonasal symptoms, recent studies have also shown that cannabinoids modulate immune responses by activating endogenous cannabinoid‐1 and cannabinoid‐2 (CB1 and CB2) receptors, reducing pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α and influencing immune cell recruitment pathways [50, 51, 52]. It is thus plausible that cannabis exerts local or systemic immunomodulatory effects via CB1 and CB2 activation that may reduce sinonasal inflammation or delay disease progression. Though these findings stem primarily from preclinical and animal models, these findings provide biological pathways through which cannabis use may lead to the observed results in our study.

While our study did not directly assess these pathways, future investigations should aim to bridge this translational gap. Prospective studies incorporating objective assessments of sinonasal inflammation, like nasal endoscopy, imaging, and biomarker profiling, paired with detailed cannabis use characterization could help distinguish between true biological effects and behavioral factors. Furthermore, evaluating how different modes of cannabis consumption (e.g., inhaled vs. ingested) influence upper airway inflammation may reveal additional insights. Collectively, these directions offer promising opportunities to clarify cannabis' potential role in modulating sinonasal disease.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. While the large sample size provides substantial statistical power, the inherent limitations of EHR‐based research, including incomplete documentation and variability in coding practices, must be considered when interpreting these findings. The primary limitation is the reliance on physician‐entered ICD‐10 codes in the EHR to identify cannabis use, which may exclude undocumented recreational use, underestimate true exposure, and fail to specify the method of consumption. Because cannabis use frequency, route, and intensity are not reliably encoded and documentation is incomplete, we modeled exposure as binary, similar to prior TriNetX cannabis studies [30, 31, 53]. Additionally, the use of ICD‐10 codes to classify CRS diagnoses introduces the potential for misclassification or miscoding, which may contribute to measurement bias and confounding. Second, we were unable to use billing codes capturing a larger patient population (e.g., CPT codes 99213 and 99214) during the time period studied (January 2012–December 2019) due to TriNetX platform constraints preventing propensity‐score matching in large cohorts. We used the HCPCS G0463 code, which, although representing a smaller subset of available records, has been validated in prior studies and allowed for effective cohort balancing within the 2012–2019 study period [31, 54]. To assess potential bias introduced by this limitation, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using both “Visit: Ambulatory” and CPT codes 99213/99214, separately, limited to December 2019 during which cohort sizes were sufficiently reduced to permit matching. Results from this analysis were consistent with our primary findings, supporting our approach. Third, underlying conditions known to increase the risk for severe CRS and surgical intervention, such as aspirin‐exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD) and allergic fungal rhinosinusitis, do not have unique ICD‐10 identifiers and as such could not be reliably excluded, representing a potential source of residual confounding. Fourth, the incidence of CRSwNP in this study is based on ICD‐10 codes for nasal polyps rather than endoscopic or surgical findings, likely resulting in an underrepresentation of both the CRSwNP population and the strength of their associated relationships, as highlighted by a similar TriNetX study examining CRSwNP [55]. Finally, given the observational and database‐driven nature of this analysis, the results demonstrate association but cannot establish causality.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that cannabis use is associated with a reduced incidence of new‐onset CRS and a lower need for FESS in patients already diagnosed with CRS. While these results are intriguing, further prospective studies are needed to confirm this relationship and its mechanistic basis.
