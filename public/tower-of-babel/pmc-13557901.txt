Clinical value of blood circulating tumor RNA (ctRNA) in liquid biopsy of lung cancer: a narrative review
Authors: Lei Zhu, Longjun Xian, Lu Zhou, Min Yi, Jili Li, Yi Liu, Shuxiao Ma, Lunxu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveLung cancer remains a major global health challenge, with substantial incidence rates and the highest cancer-related mortality. Liquid biopsy based on circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has been widely used in lung cancer diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment guidance. However, its sensitivity remains limited due to the low abundance of ctDNA in circulation. Circulating tumor RNA (ctRNA), released actively or passively by tumor cells and carrying tumor-specific alterations, offers a promising complementary biomarker in lung cancer liquid biopsy. This review summarizes current ctRNA research in lung cancer and highlights the potential of ctRNA approaches to improve diagnostic and monitoring performance.MethodsWe performed a narrative review by systematically searching the PubMed and Google Scholar databases for identifying significant literatures related to clinical research for ctRNA in lung cancer.Key Content and FindingsThis review summarizes the biological resources, including plasma, exosome, and platelet, and liquid biopsy techniques, such as quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), digital droplet PCR (ddPCR), nCounter, as well as next-generation sequencing (NGS) for ctRNA detection in lung cancer. In addition, we discuss the current clinical studies of ctRNA in diagnosis, prognosis, and monitoring, as well as its integration with ctDNA in liquid biopsy of lung cancer. Finally, we highlight the current limitations and further perspective of ctRNA in clinical applications.ConclusionsctRNA represents a powerful and increasingly essential component of next-generation liquid biopsy. Its integration with ctDNA enhances molecular profiling and supports more precise diagnosis, dynamic disease monitoring, and adaptation of targeted therapies in lung cancer. Continued methodological advancements will further accelerate its clinical translation.Keywords: Circulating tumor RNA (ctRNA), liquid biopsy, lung cancer

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most prevalent malignancies and leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 2.2 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths annually (1). Despite advances in screening and therapy, overall prognosis of lung cancer remains poor, particularly for patients diagnosed at advanced stages (2). Early diagnosis, dynamic treatment monitoring, and timely detection of resistance mutations are central to effective lung cancer management, as they directly inform therapeutic decision-making, guide prognostic assessment, and enable real-time adaptation of treatment strategies (3-5).

Traditional diagnostic approaches, including tissue biopsy and imaging, provide limited insight into the molecular complexity of lung cancer and/or are unsuitable for continuous monitoring (6). The emergence of liquid biopsy offers a non-invasive alternative that enables real-time assessment of tumor-derived material in blood or other body fluids (7,8). Among the various analytes, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has been the most extensively investigated. Released from apoptotic and necrotic tumor cells, ctDNA provides a comprehensive snapshot of the tumor’s genomic landscape and has proven valuable for guiding mutation detection, treatment selection, minimal residual disease (MRD) assessment, and resistance monitoring (9-12). Hence, ctDNA analysis has significantly advanced molecular profiling and monitoring in lung cancer and become a cornerstone of liquid biopsy-based precision oncology. However, the fraction of ctDNA released into the bloodstream is often extremely low and may fall below the detection threshold of current technologies, leading to false-negative results, particularly in patients with early-stage disease or minimal tumor burden (10-14). These limitations underscore the urgent need for complementary biomarkers that can enhance detection sensitivity and provide additional layers of biological information beyond the genomic level.

Cell-free RNA (cfRNA), also known as extracellular RNAs, refers to RNA molecules that are present in body fluids, such as blood, urine, or cerebrospinal fluid (15,16). It has been demonstrated that cfRNAs are derived from a variety of cellular activities, including passively-released by apoptotic or necrotic cells, as well as active processes like cellular secretion or vesicle release in the form of exosomes and microvesicles (16,17). Furthermore, cfRNA can also associate with lipoprotein complexes or RNA-binding proteins, which protect it from rapid degradation by plasma RNases (18). cfRNA encompasses a diverse population of RNA molecules, including messenger RNA (mRNA), transfer RNA (tRNA), ribosomal RNA (rRNA), and regulatory non-coding RNA, such as microRNA (miRNA), tRNA-derived small RNA (tsRNA), long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), circular RNA (circRNA), and other fragmented RNAs (19-22). Once released into bloodstream, cfRNA can be detected and analyzed to gain insights into cellular and molecular processes occurring in the body.

In cancer patients, a proportion of cfRNA is shed by tumor cells, carrying tumor-associated genetic and transcriptional information. This tumor-derived RNA in circulating systems, known as circulating tumor RNA (ctRNA), including tumor-derived mRNA and non-coding RNAs, provides a valuable molecular window into the active transcriptional landscape of the tumor and holds promise for use in noninvasive cancer diagnostics and monitoring (21-23). Previous studies on ctRNA have mainly focused on changes in expression levels of cfRNA species in cancer patients; however, such expression-based markers often lack tumor specificity, as similar alterations may occur in non-malignant conditions. Increasing evidence suggests that cfRNA in cancer patients contains tumor-derived, mutation-bearing transcripts released from tumor cells, including RNAs harboring oncogenic mutations or fusion transcripts. In this review, we use the term ctRNA to specifically refer to tumor-derived, mutation-bearing RNA circulating in the bloodstream. Like ctDNA, ctRNAs represent tumor-specific circulating biomarkers that can capture the genetic alterations of tumor cells. Importantly, beyond reflecting tumor genomic features, tumor-derived ctRNA may provide additional information on tumor transcriptional activity and the functional state of viable tumor cells (24,25). These mutation-containing ctRNA molecules could also serve as a functional complement to ctDNA, potentially improving diagnostic sensitivity, guiding targeted therapy, and enabling dynamic monitoring of treatment response and resistance (25). Integrating ctRNA analysis with ctDNA profiling may therefore provide a more comprehensive and precise molecular portrait of lung cancer. In this review, we summarize recent advances in mutation-bearing ctRNA researches in liquid biopsy of lung cancer, focusing on its biological resource, detection technologies, clinical applications, and future perspectives in precision oncology. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-0525/rc).

Methods

In this narrative review, PubMed and Google Scholar databases were searched between the date of database inception and December 10, 2025, to identify relevant studies investigating the biological characteristics, detection strategies, and clinical applications of ctRNA-based liquid biopsy in lung cancer. The search strategy was developed using keywords, including “non-small cell lung cancer”, “liquid biopsy”, “cell-free RNA”, and “circulating tumor RNA”. Only articles published in English were considered. Original research articles, clinical studies, and translational studies focusing on the detection, characterization, and clinical relevance of circulating RNA in lung cancer were included.

Due to substantial heterogeneity among the included studies regarding patient populations, RNA sources, detection platforms, molecular targets, and clinical endpoints, quantitative meta-analysis was not considered appropriate. Therefore, a narrative synthesis approach was adopted to summarize current evidence, compare different ctRNA detection strategies, and discuss the potential clinical applications of ctRNA-based liquid biopsy in lung cancer. History of ctRNA

As early as 1948, Mandel and Métais made the first observation of extracellular RNA in human plasma, providing the earliest evidence that RNA can exist in the circulation (26). Given the limited understanding of DNA as the genetic material and the poorly defined biological role of RNA at the time, research on circulating RNA was largely neglected in decades following this pioneering discovery. Only a few studies during this period explored the presence and concentration of RNA in plasma (27). In the mid-1980s, a novel RNA-proteolipid complex was identified in the sera of cancer patients but not in healthy individuals, suggesting a strong association of cfRNA with malignant diseases (28). Subsequent studies demonstrated that circulating levels of RNA-proteolipid complex fell after effective treatment and rose again 10 months prior to the conventional clinical diagnosis of relapse, highlighting its potential as a biomarker for cancer diagnosis and early relapse monitoring (29). Beyond the bloodstream, cfRNA was also identified in the urine of cancer patients, and their concentrations were found to vary with disease stage, indicating their potential utility as noninvasive biomarkers for cancer detection and monitoring (30,31). Despite these early findings, technical limitations and an incomplete understanding of tumor biology restricted cfRNA research at that time, with little exploration of tumor-specific cfRNA in liquid biopsy.

Advances in tumor biology have revealed that numerous oncogenes and cancer-associated transcripts are aberrantly upregulated in malignant cells (32), raising the possibility that these tumor-derived mRNAs might also be dysregulated in circulation. Motivated by this hypothesis and inspired by the successful detection of extracellular tumor RNA in plasma and serum through amplification-based methods, researchers began to investigate whether similar molecular approaches could enable the detection of tumor-associated mRNA in blood. In 1999, Kopreski et al. employed reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) amplification to successfully detect tyrosinase mRNA in the plasma of melanoma patients (33). Tyrosinase transcripts were detected in most patients but absent in healthy controls, establishing a foundation for using RT-PCR based methods to identify tumor-associated RNA in circulation (33). Lately, Fleischhacker et al. reported that combined detection of hnRNP-B1 and Her2/neu mRNA in serum enabled the identification of all patients with malignant lung tumors, highlighting their potential as complementary biomarkers for lung cancer (34). Apart from cell-free mRNA, the discovery of diverse cell-free non-coding RNAs, including miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs, that has profoundly expanded the landscape of liquid biopsy, showing great potential in cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment monitoring (19-21). In previous studies, these upregulated cfRNA species detected in the blood of cancer patients were broadly referred to as ctRNA (16,35,36). However, many of these RNA molecules are also expressed in non-tumor tissues or circulating cells and therefore lack true tumor specificity, making the use of the term “ctRNA” imprecise. In a more rigorous sense, ctRNA should be defined as RNA transcripts derived from tumor-specific genomic alterations, such as mutations, fusions, or aberrant splice variants, that are actively and solely transcribed in tumor cells and subsequently released into the circulation. In this review, we emphasize tumor-derived RNA transcripts that harbor cancer-specific genetic mutations, referred to as ctRNA, which may serve as more precise and informative biomarkers for lung cancer diagnosis, treatment guidance, and disease monitoring.

Detection methods for ctRNA

As with ctDNA, ctRNA contains somatic mutations, gene fusions, and other alterations derived from tumor cells (37-40), allowing its use as tumor-specific biomarker in noninvasive molecular diagnostics. However, unlike ctDNA, ctRNA exists as RNA molecules that require an additional reverse transcription step to generate complementary DNA (cDNA) prior to analysis. Once converted, ctRNA-derived cDNA can be analyzed using various analytical platforms, including quantitative PCR (qPCR), digital droplet PCR (ddPCR), nCounter, and next-generation sequencing (NGS) (Figure 1). Each platform in ctRNA detection presents unique advantages and limitations regarding sensitivity, specificity, throughput, and clinical applicability, and has been extensively investigated in lung cancer liquid biopsy.

qPCR

qPCR represents one of the earliest and most widely applied methods for RNA detection due to its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and rapid turnaround time (41). The method involves reverse transcription of RNA into cDNA followed by amplification using target-specific primers, allowing precise quantification of transcripts. It is particularly suited for identifying known fusion transcripts or hotspot mutations with predefined primer sets. qPCR assays have been employed to detect ctRNA in lung cancer patients, including gene fusions, such as EML4-ALK, ROS1, and RET, as well as mutations in EGFR (24,42-45) (Table 2). Despite its accessibility, qPCR is limited by relatively low sensitivity for low-abundance transcripts and its inability to identify novel or unexpected alterations (Table 3).

ddPCR

ddPCR has emerged as a highly sensitive and precise method for quantifying DNA and RNA molecules, particularly when targets are present at extremely low abundance (55). As for ctRNA detection, cDNA is synthesized from ctRNA reverse transcription, and then partitioned into tens of thousands of nanoliter-sized droplets, each serving as an independent PCR micro-reaction (56). By counting the fraction of positive droplets, absolute quantification of target RNA molecules is achieved without reliance on standard curves, enabling detection of rare transcripts and low-frequency mutations (56). In lung cancer, ddPCR has been successfully applied to detect EGFR mutant transcripts, ALK fusion, and other oncogenic markers in plasma and exosome (48-51,54,57) (Table 2). The method offers superior sensitivity and reproducibility compared with conventional qPCR, making it particularly suitable for monitoring MRD, early recurrence, and therapeutic response. However, ddPCR is also generally limited to a small number of predefined targets and requires careful optimization of assay conditions to avoid false positives and maintain accuracy (Table 3).

NGS

Unlike qPCR and ddPCR methods, NGS provides the most comprehensive platform for ctRNA analysis, enabling simultaneous detection of multiple mutation types, including point mutations, insertions/deletions, gene fusions, and alternative splicing events in a single assay (25,46,47,50,52,53,58). NGS of plasma or exosomal RNA has successfully revealed EGFR mutations, ALK and ROS1 fusion transcripts in lung cancer (Table 2).

NGS assays can be broadly classified into amplicon-based (25,46,47) and hybrid-capture-based approaches (37). Amplicon-based NGS relies on target-specific primer amplification and is generally efficient for detecting predefined fusion transcripts or known breakpoint regions. This strategy requires relatively low RNA input, has a rapid workflow, and can achieve high sensitivity for recurrent and clinically actionable fusion variants. However, its detection capacity is restricted by primer design, and it may miss rare fusion partners, novel rearrangements, or atypical breakpoint configurations. In contrast, hybrid-capture-based NGS uses probe-based enrichment across broader genomic or transcriptomic regions and may provide more comprehensive coverage for complex or previously uncharacterized fusion events. Despite these advantages, hybrid-capture-based NGS ctRNA analysis faces technical challenges, including the low abundance and fragmentation of circulating RNA, contamination from abundant background transcripts, and complex bioinformatic requirements for accurate mutation calling. Optimized library preparation protocols, RNA enrichment strategies, and stringent data analysis pipelines are therefore essential to maximize sensitivity and specificity (Table 3).

nCounter

The nCounter system uses color-coded molecular barcodes that hybridize directly to RNA targets from tissue samples without requiring reverse transcription or amplification, thereby minimizing bias introduced by enzymatic reactions and preserving the original transcript abundance (59,60). This digital counting approach enables multiplexed quantification of dozens to hundreds of target transcripts simultaneously, making it particularly suitable for profiling gene fusions, splice variants, and expression signatures (60) (Table 3). However, the number of RNA transcripts in liquid biopsy samples is expected to be low. Hence, Giménez-Capitán et al. performed a 30-cycle preamplification of cDNAs retrotranscribed from cfRNA with a custom primer panels and then successfully detected ALK, ROS1, RET fusions, and the MET∆ex14 splicing variant by using nCounter (48) (Table 2).

Overall, methods that rely on predefined primers or probes, such as qPCR, ddPCR, and hybridization-based assays, are primarily limited to detecting known fusion variants and may therefore miss rare or novel fusion partners and atypical breakpoint configurations, particularly in molecularly heterogeneous ALK, ROS1, and RET rearrangements.

Blood as a primary source for ctRNA detection

Of all biological fluids, the bloodstream represents the most extensively explored and clinically feasible source for liquid biopsy in human diseases, including lung cancer, owing to various specific advantages. First, blood circulates throughout the entire body, serving as a central reservoir that collects tumor-derived materials released from both primary and metastatic lesions, thus providing a more comprehensive and systemic molecular snapshot of tumor dynamics (61). Second, blood sampling is minimally invasive, standardized, and clinically feasible across diverse patient populations, unlike other fluids that may require site-specific or invasive collection procedures (62-64). Third, the ability to perform serial blood collections at different time points enables real-time monitoring of treatment response and disease progression, making the bloodstream the most practical and informative source for liquid biopsy (64).

Within the bloodstream, distinct subcomponents, including plasma, exosomes, and platelets (Figure 1), that serve as critical carriers of ctRNA (24,54) (Table 2). Derived from apoptotic or necrotic tumor cells, active secretion through extracellular vesicles, or tumor-educated platelets (TEPs), ctRNA collectively capture the dynamic molecular features of tumor progression, underscoring their value in liquid biopsy.

Plasma as a source of ctRNA

Among the various blood-derived components, plasma represents the most abundant and intensively investigated source of ctRNA (65). Despite the intrinsic instability of RNA molecules, plasma ctRNA is often protected within vesicles, protein complexes, or lipoprotein particles, which enhances its stability and detectability (18). Plasma-based ctRNA analysis offers substantial practical advantages, as plasma can be easily obtained from routine blood draws and enables direct RNA extraction without additional complex processing, providing a simple, rapid, and scalable approach for both clinical diagnostics and research applications (66).

In lung cancer, numerous studies have demonstrated that plasma-derived ctRNA reflects tumor-specific molecular alterations, including gene mutations and fusions (Table 2). The EML4-ALK fusion, present in approximately 3–7% of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), defines a distinct molecular subtype highly sensitive to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) (67). In 2015, to determine the sensitivity and specificity of EML4-ALK fusion transcripts detection in plasma, Nilsson et al. analyzed 32 NSCLC patients, 14 of whom harbored EML4-ALK rearrangements (24). The EML4-ALK fusion transcript was detected in plasma from three fusion-positive patients but in none of the fusion-negative ones, corresponding to 21% sensitivity and 100% specificity. Another study by Heeke et al. reported that among 30 patients with EML4-ALK rearrangements confirmed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and/or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) at baseline, qPCR detected EML4-ALK fusion transcripts in 10 patients’ plasma, yielding a sensitivity of 33.33% (45). None of the 24 ALK fusion-negative patients tested positive, demonstrating 100% specificity for plasma cfRNA-based ALK fusion detection. RET and ROS1 rearrangements occur in approximately 3% of NSCLC cases (57). Although relatively rare, their detection is clinically crucial, as patients harboring these fusions can benefit from targeted therapeutics that specifically inhibit the aberrant kinase activity that results from the onco-protein (57). In 2018, Mellert et al. developed a plasma RNA-based assay capable of simultaneously detecting eight distinct fusion variants each for RET and ROS1, covering approximately 99% and 88% of fusion-positive NSCLC cases, respectively (57).

In addition to gene fusions, single-nucleotide variants in EGFR and KRAS represent key driver alterations and therapeutic targets in NSCLC, guiding the use of tyrosine kinase inhibitors and other targeted therapies. Nesselbush et al. demonstrated that plasma-derived RNA enables the detection of EGFR and KRAS variants, highlighting its potential utility for noninvasive genotyping and real-time molecular monitoring in lung cancer (37). Together, these studies highlight the growing potential of plasma ctRNA analysis as a minimally invasive tool for molecular profiling, therapeutic monitoring, and resistance tracking in lung cancer.

Exosomes as carriers of ctRNA

Exosomes are small (30–150 nm) extracellular vesicles secreted by virtually all cell types, including tumor cells, and are enriched with RNA, DNA, proteins, and lipids (17). Tumor-derived exosomes encapsulate ctRNA, providing protection from degradation by extracellular RNases and thereby enhancing the stability and detectability of these molecules in circulation. Hence, exosomes have emerged as critical carriers of ctRNA in the bloodstream. Specifically isolating exosomes from plasma enables enrichment of tumor-derived RNA, improving tumor specificity and detection sensitivity, particularly for low-abundance ctRNAs or high-precision molecular analyses.

In lung cancer, several studies have demonstrated that exosomal ctRNAs can serve as biomarkers for cancer diagnosis and monitoring (Table 2). Both Brinkmann et al. and Sánchez-Herrero et al. demonstrated that exosome-derived RNA enables detection of ALK fusions in NSCLC patients, with ALK fusion transcripts identified in ~50% of cases, highlighting the consistent utility of extracellular vesicles for noninvasive fusion RNA detection (42,54). Collectively, exosome-mediated ctRNA represents a robust and functionally informative component of liquid biopsy in lung cancer, complementing plasma-derived cfRNA analyses.

Platelets as carriers of ctRNA

Platelets, traditionally recognized for their role in hemostasis, have recently been identified as important carriers of ctRNA in the bloodstream (68,69). TEPs arise when platelets interact with tumor cells or are influenced by tumor-derived signals, resulting in the uptake of tumor-specific RNA, including both mRNA and non-coding RNAs (69). These RNA molecules are also protected within the platelet cytoplasm from extracellular RNases, providing a stable and enriched source of tumor-derived transcripts (70). Studies in lung cancer have demonstrated that TEP RNA profiles reflect tumor-specific gene expression patterns, including oncogenic mutations and splice variants, which can be used for early detection, prognosis prediction, and monitoring of therapeutic response. Furthermore, the high RNA content and protective intracellular environment of platelets make them particularly valuable for detecting low-abundance ctRNA that might be missed in cell-free plasma analyses.

Nilsson et al. isolated platelet RNA from 67 NSCLC patients, including 34 with EML4-ALK rearranged tumors, and detected the fusion in 22 of these cases but in none of the ALK fusion-negative patients or 21 healthy donors, demonstrating 65% sensitivity and 100% specificity (24). In 2019, Meng et al. applied RNA-based ddPCR to detect mutant KRAS on platelet RNA, and identified KRAS mutant-positive droplets in 3 out of 9 samples (71). Collectively, platelet-based ctRNA represents a complementary and functionally informative component of liquid biopsy, expanding the sensitivity and precision of molecular tumor profiling.

Comparative evaluation of sources and detection methods for ctRNA analytical performance

The analytical performance of ctRNA detection is strongly influenced by both the biological source from which ctRNA is isolated and the analytical platform employed. Several studies have systematically investigated how different ctRNA sources and analytical platforms affect detection efficiency, sensitivity, and clinical interpretability in lung cancer (Table 2). Among these, ctRNA derived from the EML4-ALK fusion transcript has been the most extensively studied, serving as a representative model for evaluating ctRNA detection performance across diverse biological sources and analytical methods (Table 2). The following section summarizes comparative studies assessing various ctRNA sources and detection techniques, with a particular focus on their relative analytical performance and practical applicability in EML4-ALK detection.

Comparison of ctRNA sources

To explore whether platelets could serve as a biomarker source for EML4-ALK fusion detection, Nilsson et al. employed qPCR to analyze platelet-derived RNA from 34 EML4-ALK-positive NSCLC patients and compared the results with plasma RNA from 14 EML4-ALK-positive patients (24). The study reported a sensitivity of 65% for detecting EML4-ALK rearrangements in platelets, whereas plasma RNA showed only 21% sensitivity (24). These findings suggest that platelet-derived RNA provides a more reliable source than plasma for detecting EML4-ALK fusion transcripts. Subsequently, Giménez-Capitán et al. applied qPCR to detect EML4-ALK fusions in plasma from 30 NSCLC patients with known EML4-ALK-positive tumors, identifying fusion transcripts in 10 cases, corresponding to a sensitivity of 33.3% (48). In addition, Mezquita et al. analyzed exosomal RNA isolated from plasma samples of 8 EML4-ALK-positive NSCLC patients at baseline or disease progression and detected EML4-ALK fusion transcripts in 5 cases (63%), while another study reported a detection sensitivity of approximately 50% (43). Comparison of qPCR-based analyses across different biological sources indicates substantial variability in the sensitivity of EML4-ALK detection among plasma, platelet, and exosome-derived cfRNA. Platelet-derived RNA demonstrated the highest detection sensitivity, followed by exosomal RNA. However, given the limited sample sizes in these studies, further large-scale investigations are required to validate these findings.

Comparison of detection methods

In addition to the biological source of ctRNA, different analytical platforms exhibit distinct capabilities that influence the efficiency and sensitivity of EML4-ALK detection. Comparative studies using plasma-derived RNA clearly illustrate these methodological differences (Table 2). For example, Mellert et al. employed ddPCR to analyze plasma from 15 EML4-ALK-positive NSCLC patients and detected EML4-ALK fusion transcripts in 10 cases, yielding a sensitivity of 66.7% (49). In contrast, Nilsson et al., using qPCR, identified EML4-ALK transcripts in only 3 of 14 ALK fusion-positive plasma samples, corresponding to a markedly lower sensitivity of 21% (24). Using a multiplex amplicon-based PCR followed by NGS, Hasegawa et al. detected EML4-ALK in 3 of 5 ALK fusion-positive plasma samples (60% sensitivity), further underscoring the impact of assay design on analytical performance (46).

Notably, even within the same technological category, assay performance varies across studies. Giménez-Capitán et al., also applying qPCR, detected EML4-ALK transcript in 8 of 14 ALK fusion-positive patients’ plasma (57.1% sensitivity), more than double the sensitivity reported by Nilsson et al. (24,48). These findings collectively demonstrate that both the choice of analytical platform and specific assay implementation substantially influence ctRNA detection outcomes. Collectively, the combination of optimal ctRNA sources and appropriate analytical platforms determines the success of ctRNA-based liquid biopsy.

Accordingly, cross-study sensitivity estimates should be interpreted cautiously, as they may not reflect the intrinsic performance of a given methodology. In addition to biological source, RNA abundance, and sample quality, assay-specific primer or probe design and the spectrum of fusion variants covered by each assay can substantially influence detection sensitivity.

Clinical applications of ctRNA in lung cancer

ctRNA, capturing genomic alterations from transcriptionally active genes, has emerged as a valuable biomarker in lung cancer. Growing evidence supports its utility across multiple clinical contexts, including molecular diagnosis and prognosis, monitoring treatment response and recurrence, as well as resistance detection. Recent evidence also indicates that ctRNA provides complementary information to ctDNA, enhancing the molecular resolution of liquid biopsy and increasing the sensitivity for detecting clinically actionable alterations.

Molecular diagnosis of actionable drivers

Accurate molecular diagnosis that identifies clinically actionable genomic alterations directly guides the choice of targeted therapies in personalized lung cancer management. Actionable alterations in lung cancer include oncogenic fusions, such as EML4-ALK, ROS1, and RET, which are therapeutically addressed with approved TKIs including alectinib, brigatinib, lorlatinib (ALK inhibitors); crizotinib, entrectinib (ROS1 inhibitors); and selpercatinib or pralsetinib (RET inhibitors) (72). Transcript-defined events such as MET exon 14 skipping are also readily captured at the RNA level and can be targeted with capmatinib or tepotinib (73). Beyond fusions and splice variants, activating point mutations, including EGFR and KRAS, which guide the use of matched inhibitors such as osimertinib for EGFR-mutant disease or sotorasib and adagrasib for KRAS G12C (74).

Blood-ctRNA-based molecular diagnosis enables the identification of clinically actionable genomic alterations that are transcribed at high levels in a non-invasive way (Table 2). Multiple studies have shown that plasma-, platelet-, and exosome-derived RNA can reveal these targetable genomic alterations. However, current ctRNA-based identification of therapeutically actionable genomic alterations has been evaluated predominantly in advanced-stage disease, and its utility for early detection remains largely unexplored. Establishing the performance of ctRNA in early-stage lung cancer is particularly important, as timely identification of actionable drivers could expand opportunities for curative-intent interventions and earlier initiation of targeted therapies.

Prognostic assessment

Beyond its diagnostic potential, ctRNA also serves as a valuable indicator for prognostic assessment in lung cancer. In the BRIGHTSTAR clinical trial by Heeke et al., they retrospectively analyzed baseline plasma samples from 27 patients and identified EML4-ALK fusion transcripts in 11 individuals (25). They further associated the detection of EML4-ALK fusion transcripts in plasma with clinical outcome, revealing that detection of ALK fusion transcripts at baseline was associated with significantly worse progression-free survival (PFS). Besides, they also found that ctRNA-based detection highlighted numerically worse PFS than detection by ctDNA (25). Besides, Nilsson et al. further demonstrated that crizotinib-treated patients with EML4-ALK-positive platelets had poorer outcomes and significantly shorter PFS than those with platelet-negative tests (68). Together, these studies suggest that ctRNA provides an additional layer of molecular information, allowing more precise prognostic evaluation in lung cancer.

Monitoring treatment response and recurrence

Because ctRNA reflects active gene expression from viable tumor cells, its dynamic changes can provide real-time insights into treatment efficacy, offering unique advantages for therapeutic monitoring. Declines in ctRNA levels following targeted therapy or chemotherapy often correlate with radiographic and clinical response, whereas rising ctRNA levels can precede radiographic progression. Hasegawa et al. performed the cfRNA-based assay at multiple time points to monitor disease status during treatment (46). They found that in one case with EZR-ROS1 fusion was detected in cfRNA when the patient exhibited progressive disease (PD) to first-line treatment with crizotinib. After 3 months of second-line chemotherapy, when the patient exhibited partial response (PR), the EZR-ROS1 fusion gene was not detected with the RNA-based assay. Similarly, in another two patients with the CD74-ROS1 and KIF5B-RET fusions, respectively, whose fusion was detected in cfRNA were detected before treatment initiation and were negative after 3 months of treatment when the patients achieved PR.

Conversely, re-elevation of ctRNA levels may precede radiologic relapse, enabling earlier detection of disease recurrence. A representative case reported by Nilsson et al. illustrates the potential of platelet-ctRNA for early detection of emerging resistance and recurrence (24). In a 37-year-old female never-smoker with EML4-ALK v1 positive NSCLC, the fusion transcript was detectable in both tumor tissue and platelets at baseline. Following initiation of crizotinib treatment, the platelet-derived EML4-ALK signal became undetectable, which is consistent with treatment response. During a temporary treatment interruption, the fusion transcript reappeared in platelets but again disappeared upon resumption of therapy. Notably, EML4-ALK reemerged in platelets, while radiographic progression on PET-CT was not evident until two months later, highlighting the potential of ctRNA to signal acquired resistance and relapse prior to imaging-based detection. Collectively, these findings underscore ctRNA as a dynamic biomarker capable of complementing conventional imaging analysis for continuous disease surveillance and early detection of recurrence in lung cancer.

Integration with ctDNA

Obviously, ctRNA and ctDNA represent two complementary analytes in liquid biopsy, both carrying tumor-derived genetic information that reflects the molecular characteristics of cancer. Hence, integration ctRNA with ctDNA, can further enhance the molecular resolution of lung cancer monitoring. Several studies have reported that combined analyses of ctRNA and ctDNA could improve the detection sensitivity of oncogenic mutations and gene fusions in lung cancer patients. For example, Hasegawa et al. compared the sensitivity of the cfRNA/cfDNA-based assays with 9 plasma samples collected from 9 lung cancer patients who were chemotherapy-naïve or diagnosed with PD (46). Their results showed that only 3 (33%) fusions were detected with the cfDNA-based assay, while 7 (78%) were detected with the cfRNA-based assay. Heeke et al. analyzed ctDNA and ctRNA to detect ALK fusion from plasma of 28 lung cancer patients at baseline (25). Their results showed that the rearrangement was detected by both ctRNA and ctDNA in 8 samples, 4 fusions were detected in ctDNA only, whereas 3 were detected in ctRNA only. These evidences suggested that incorporation of ctRNA into ctDNA could improve the diagnostic sensitivity of genomic alteration.

Conclusions

Currently, ctRNA derived from plasma, exosomes, and platelets has shown broad clinical utility across multiple facets of precision oncology, including the detection of actionable drivers, prognostic assessment, therapeutic response monitoring, and resistance surveillance. Despite increasing evidence supporting the potential of ctRNA in liquid biopsy-guided cancer management, ctRNA research is still in its early phase, and considerable limitations and challenges still need to be addressed.

Existing studies are restricted to small cohorts, which undermines the reliability and generalizability of ctRNA findings. Moreover, existing tsRNA studies lack standardized experimental procedures, from sample collection to data analysis, resulting in substantial variability in results and undermining the reproducibility of ctRNA-based diagnostic and monitoring applications. The limited sample size and the lack of standardized sample processing protocols pose a major barrier to translating ctRNA research into clinically actionable tools and underscores the urgent need for larger and more diverse cohorts alongside with standardized methodologies.

Current ctRNA research has largely centered on lung cancer, where abundant actionable mutations enable ctRNA-based detection to complement ctDNA and improve the sensitivity and accuracy of therapeutic target identification. In contrast, ctRNA studies targeting therapeutically relevant alterations in other malignancies remain scarce. Through enhanced sensitivity for therapeutically actionable genomic alterations, ctRNA analysis provides a rationale for extending mutation-targeted detection to additional cancer types and advancing precision treatment selection.

Beyond its role in detecting actionable mutations, ctRNA recognized as a dynamic molecular mirror of tumor genomic variations, opening avenues for broader clinical applications. First, owing to its ability to capture tumor-specific transcriptional activity, even at early disease stages, ctRNA is emerging as a promising analyte for non-invasive early cancer detection. Moreover, the use of ctRNA for MRD assessment represents a promising but largely undeveloped area that may offer earlier and more biologically informative indications of residual disease. Finally, integrated ctRNA with ctDNA profiling may enhance detection sensitivity, particularly in early-stage disease and postoperative MRD settings where ctDNA abundance is low and the addition of ctRNA could substantially improve assay performance.
