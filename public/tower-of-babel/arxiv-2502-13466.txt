Subdifferential determination of a primal lower regular function on a Banach space
M. Ivanov, M. Konstantinov, N. Zlateva

Abstract
We generalize to Banach space Thibault-Zagrodny Theorem that if f f and g g are primal lower regular functions and ∂ f = ∂ g \partial f=\partial g locally, then f f and g g locally differ by a constant. Our method consists in reduction to slopes considerations. 2020 MSC : 49J52, 47J22 Key words : primal lower regular function, slope, determination of a function



1
Introduction

The primal lower regular functions are introduced by R. Poliquin in

[
18
]
under the name primal lower nice functions, for a modern account see
[
21
, Chapter 11]
. They are a useful abstraction of the framework for non-convex
optimization proposed by R.T. Rockafellar, see e.g.
[
20
]
, that is, the compositions of
convex lower semicontinuous and twice smooth functions. The latter framework is
really apt for the purpose, however doing calculus directly with those so called

convexly composite
functions, e.g.
[
21
, 11.1.2]
is cumbersome, because of having to account for two
maps. In this respect primal lower regular functions come in handy.

In
[
18
]
R. Poliquin shows that primal lower regular functions share many properties with the convex
functions, including a local version of Moreau-Rockafellar integrability theorem. Namely,
if the subdifferentials of two primal lower regular functions coincide in a neighbourhood of some point,
then in a neighbourhood of that point the functions differ by a constant. In
[
18
]
it was proved
in finite dimensions and later in
[
22
]
it was extended to Hilbert space setting, see also
[
21
, p.1113]
. These proofs, however, rely
on the special properties of Moreau-Yousida regularization which allow transferring the subdifferential
equality to the regularized functions, when the norm is Hilbertian. This does not work in a general Banach space.

As the title suggests,
this work is devoted to extending this result to a general Banach space setting.

The problem of extending the subdifferential determination of primal lower regular functions to Banach spaces is surprisingly tough.
One of the reasons being that the domain of a primal lower regular function could potentially be quite irregular: only approximate convexity
can be perhaps claimed
[
15
]
and even that is not fully established.

The new method we employ here is based on
slopes
, see e.g.
[
13
]
and the references therein, and
it works, because it does not require linear (or convex) structure, so the irregularity of the domain is not such an issue.

Let us recall the definition of a primal lower regular function.

Definition 1

(cf.
[
21
]
, Definition 11.1)
.

Let
(E,\|\cdot\|)
be a Banach space. Let the function
f:E\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be proper and lower semicontinuous. We say that
f
is
primal lower regular at

\bar{x}\in E
with coefficient
c>0
and radius
\delta>0
, and write

\bar{x}\in\mathrm{plr}_{c,\delta}f,

if

f(y)\geq f(x)+p(y-x)-c(1+\|p\|)\|y-x\|^{2},

(1)

for all
x,y\in B^{\circ}(\bar{x};\delta)
, and all
p\in\partial f(x)
.

The function
f
is called
primal lower regular
if

\bigcup_{c,\delta>0}\mathrm{plr}_{c,\delta}f=E.

We will now formulate our main result.

Theorem 2
.

Let
(E,\|\cdot\|)
be a Banach space. Let the functions
f,g:E\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be proper and lower semicontinuous. Let

\bar{x}\in\mathrm{plr}_{c,\delta}f\cap\mathrm{plr}_{c,\delta}g\cap\mathrm{dom}\,f,

for some
c,\delta>0
. Let

\partial f(x)=\partial g(x),\quad\forall x\in B^{\circ}(\bar{x},\delta).

(2)

Then there is a constant
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that

f(x)=g(x)+a,\quad\forall x\in B^{\circ}(\bar{x},\hat{\delta}),

(3)

where

\hat{\delta}:=\min\left\{\frac{\delta}{2},\frac{1}{18c}\right\}.

(4)

Here
\partial
denotes the Clarke-Rockafellar subdifferential, see Section
3
for a brief review of the latter.

This work is organized as follows. In Section
2
we develop the slope techniques we need. In Section
3
we recall what we use from the theory of Clarke-Rockafellar subdifferential and retrieve how subdifferential inclusions imply slopes inequalities. Finally, in Section
4
we prove the main result.

2
Slope regularity

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space. Functions like
g:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
are assumed proper and lower semicontinuous.

For a function
g:X\to\mathbb{R}
and a convex set
U\subset X
define

\mathrm{Lip}\,g(U):=\sup\left\{\frac{|g(y)-g(x)|}{d(y,x)}:\ x\neq y\in U\right\},

and for
x\in X

\mathrm{Lip}\,g(x):=\inf_{\delta>0}\mathrm{Lip}\,g(B(x;\delta)).

Estimating the Lipschitz constant of the kind of functions will be using, is straightforward.

Lemma 3
.

Let
x_{0}\in X
, and

g(x):=\varphi(d(x,x_{0})),

where
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a convex, increasing smooth function such that
\varphi(0){=}0
. Then

\mathrm{Lip}\,g(x)\leq\varphi^{\prime}(d(x,x_{0})),\quad\forall x\in X.

Proof.

Fix a
x\in X
and a
\delta>d(x,x_{0})
.

We have that
\varphi^{\prime}
is positive and increasing. Therefore,

\mathrm{Lip}\,\varphi([0,\delta])=\varphi^{\prime}(\delta).

So, if
x,y\in B(x_{0};\delta)
, that is,
d(x,x_{0}),d(y,x_{0})\in[0,\delta]
, then

|g(y)-g(x)|\leq\varphi^{\prime}(\delta)|d(y,x_{0})-d(x,x_{0})|\leq\varphi^{\prime}(\delta)d(y,x),

from the triangle inequality. So,

\mathrm{Lip}\,g(B(x_{0};\delta))\leq\varphi^{\prime}(\delta).

But
\mathrm{Lip}\,g(x)\leq\mathrm{Lip}\,g(B(x_{0};\delta))
and
\displaystyle\inf_{\delta>d(x,x_{0})}\varphi^{\prime}(\delta)=\varphi^{\prime}(d(x,x_{0}))
, because
\varphi
is smooth.
∎

The
local slope
of a function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
at
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f
is

|\nabla f|(x)=\limsup_{y\to x\atop y\neq x}\frac{[f(x)-f(y)]^{+}}{d(x,y)},

where

[t]^{+}:=\max\{0,t\}=(t+|t|)/2
. Therefore,
|\nabla f|(x)=0
if
x
is a local minimum to
f
, and if not

|\nabla f|(x)=\limsup_{y\to x\atop y\neq x}\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{d(x,y)}.

We explicit that
|\nabla f|(x)=0
at the isolated points of
\mathrm{dom}\,f
, where the limit above is undefined.
This notion was introduced by De Giorgi, Marino and Tosques
[
8
]
and used thereafter for study of concepts as descent curves (e.g.
[
7
]
), error bounds (e.g.
[
2
]
), subdifferential calculus and metric regularity (e.g. the monograph
[
9
]
and references therein).
For determination of functions, local slopes recently appear as an useful tool, see e.g.
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
13
]
, as well as, in the convex case subgradients of minimal norm, see
[
16
]
, and global slopes, see
[
23
]
.

We may consider
|\nabla f|
as a function from
\mathrm{dom}\,f
to
[0,\infty]
. Moreover,

|\nabla(rf)|(x)=r|\nabla f|(x),\quad\forall r\geq 0,\ \forall x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f.

In some sense, not a strict one, the local slope
|\nabla f|(x)
can be regarded as an one-sided, or
unilateral
, analogy of
\mathrm{Lip}\,f(x)
. Formally,
|\nabla f|(x)
is an unilaterisation of
\displaystyle\limsup_{y\to x}|f(x)-f(y)|/d(x,y)
. What we will be using is the following relationship.

Lemma 4
.

Let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
be proper and lower semicontinuous and let
g:X\to\mathbb{R}
be locally Lipschitz. If
f+g
has a local minimum at
x_{0}
then

|\nabla f|(x_{0})\leq\mathrm{Lip}\,g(x_{0}).

(5)

Proof.

We can assume without loss of generality that
f(x_{0})=g(x_{0})=0
. So, there is
\varepsilon>0
such that

f(x)+g(x)\geq 0,\quad\forall x\in B(x_{0};\varepsilon).

Then for each
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon)

f(x)\geq-g(x)\geq-\mathrm{Lip}\,g(B(x_{0};\delta))d(x,x_{0}),\quad\forall x\in B(x_{0};\delta).

By definition

|\nabla f|(x_{0})\leq\mathrm{Lip}\,g(B(x_{0};\delta)),

and taking infimum over
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon)
, we get (
5
).
∎

Later on we will be using the following simple observation.

Lemma 5
.

Let
f
be proper and lower semicontinuous and let
x_{0}\in\mathrm{dom}\,f
. Consider the sublevel set

Y:=\{x\in X:\ f(x)\leq f(x_{0})\},

and the restriction of
f
to it

\overline{f}:=f\upharpoonright Y.

Then

|\nabla f|(x)=|\nabla\overline{f}|(x),\quad\forall x\in Y.

(6)

Proof.

The definition of
|\nabla f|(x)
can be rewritten as

|\nabla f|(x)=\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\sup\left\{\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{d(x,y)}:\ y\in B(x;\varepsilon),\ f(y)\leq f(x)\right\}.

Obviously, if
x\in Y
, then
f(y)\leq f(x)
implies
y\in Y
as well, and this gives (
6
).
∎

Recall the famous Ekeland Variational Principle, eg.

[
17
, p.45]
, or
[
21
, p.198]
.

Theorem 6

(Ekeland)
.

If
f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is proper, lower semicontinuous, and bounded below, and
x_{0}\in\mathrm{dom}\,f
, then for each
\lambda>0
there exists
x_{\lambda}
such that

\lambda d(x_{\lambda},x_{0})\leq f(x_{0})-f(x_{\lambda}),

f(x)+\lambda d(x_{\lambda},x)>f(x_{\lambda}),\quad\forall x\neq x_{\lambda}.

Lemma 7
.

Let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
be a proper and lower semicontinuous function, and let
g:X\to\mathbb{R}
be locally Lipschitz. If
f+g
is bounded below, then for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists
x_{\varepsilon}
such that

(f+g)(x_{\varepsilon})\leq\inf(f+g)+\varepsilon,

(7)

and

|\nabla f|(x_{\varepsilon})\leq\mathrm{Lip}\,g(x_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon.

(8)

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and pick a
x_{0}
such that

(f+g)(x_{0})\leq\inf(f+g)+\varepsilon.

By Ekeland’s Variational Principle – Theorem
6
– applied for
(f+g)
(instead of
f
), and
\varepsilon
(instead of
\lambda
), there is
x_{\varepsilon}
which satisfies

\varepsilon d(x_{\varepsilon},x_{0})\leq(f+g)(x_{0})-(f+g)(x_{\varepsilon}),

(9)

(f+g)(x)+\varepsilon d(x_{\varepsilon},x)>(f+g)(x_{\varepsilon}),\quad\forall x\neq x_{\varepsilon}.

(10)

Now, (
9
) implies
(f+g)(x_{\varepsilon})\leq(f+g)(x_{0})
, and we get (
7
).

On the other hand, (
10
) means that the function

x\to f(x)+(g(x)+\varepsilon d(x_{\varepsilon},x))

attains its minimum at
x_{\varepsilon}
. Obviously,
\mathrm{Lip}\,(g(\cdot)+\varepsilon d(\cdot,x_{\varepsilon}))(x_{\varepsilon})\leq\mathrm{Lip}\,g(x_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon
, so Lemma
4
gives (
8
).
∎

Definition 8
.

The function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is
regularly sloped of order 2 with coefficient

c>0
whenever for each
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|
,

f(y)\geq f(x)-|\nabla f|(x)d(y,x)-c(1+|\nabla f|(x))d^{2}(y,x),\quad\forall y\in X.

(11)

Theorem 9
.

Let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be regularly sloped of order
2
with coefficient
c>0
. Then for each
\bar{x}
with

0<|\nabla f|(\bar{x})<\infty

there is a sequence
x_{n}\to\bar{x}
such that

|\nabla f|(\bar{x})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(\bar{x})-f(x_{n})}{d(\bar{x},x_{n})},

(12)

and

|\nabla f|(x_{n})-|\nabla f|(\bar{x})<2c(|\nabla f|(\bar{x})+2)d(\bar{x},x_{n}),\quad\forall n>1/|\nabla f|(\bar{x}).

(13)

Proof.

Fix
\bar{x}\in\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|
such that

r:=|\nabla f|(\overline{x})>0
.
To shorten the annotations, we can assume without loss of generality that

f(\bar{x})=0
.
So, (
11
) gives

f(x)\geq-rd(x,\bar{x})-c(r+1)d^{2}(x,\bar{x}),\quad\forall x\in X.

(14)

Fix an arbitrary
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n>1/r
, thus

r_{n}:=r-1/n>0.

(15)

Consider the function

g_{n}(x):=r_{n}d(x,\bar{x})+c(r+2)d^{2}(x,\bar{x}).

Lemma
3
gives that

\mathrm{Lip}\,{g_{n}}(x)\leq r_{n}+2c(r+2)d(x,\bar{x}),\quad\forall x\in X.

(16)

Note that the function
(f+g_{n})
is bounded below. Indeed, from (
14
) it follows that

f(x)+g_{n}(x)\geq-\frac{d(x,\bar{x})}{n}+cd^{2}(x,\bar{x})\geq\min_{t\geq 0}\left(ct^{2}-\frac{t}{n}\right).

It is not possible for the function
(f+g_{n})
to have a local minimum at
\bar{x}
, for otherwise Lemma
4
and (
16
) would give
r=|\nabla f|(\bar{x})\leq r_{n}<r
, contradiction. So,
(f+g_{n})(\bar{x})=0>\inf(f+g_{n})
.

Choose an
\varepsilon_{n}>0
such that

\inf(f+g_{n})+\varepsilon_{n}<0,

(17)

and

\varepsilon_{n}<\frac{1}{n}.

(18)

From Lemma
7
there is
x_{n}
such that

(f+g_{n})(x_{n})\leq\inf(f+g_{n})+\varepsilon_{n},

(19)

and

|\nabla f|(x_{n})\leq\mathrm{Lip}\,{g_{n}}(x_{n})+\varepsilon_{n}.

(20)

From (
19
) and (
17
) it follows that
(f+g_{n})(x_{n})<0
, so
f(x_{n})<-g_{n}(x_{n})\leq-r_{n}d(x_{n},\bar{x})
. Thus,

\frac{f(\bar{x})-f(x_{n})}{d(\bar{x},x_{n})}=-\frac{f(x_{n})}{d(\bar{x},x_{n})}>r_{n}.

(21)

Using again
(f+g_{n})(x_{n})<0
together with (
14
), we estimate
0>f(x_{n})+g_{n}(x_{n})\geq-d(x_{n},\bar{x})/n+cd^{2}(x_{n},\bar{x})
, so

0>d(x_{n},\bar{x})(cd(x_{n},\bar{x})-1/n).

Clearly, this implies

d(x_{n},\bar{x})<\frac{1}{cn}.

(22)

From (
20
) and (
16
) we have that
|\nabla f|(x_{n})-|\nabla f|(\bar{x})\leq(r_{n}+2c(r+2)d(x_{n},\bar{x})+\varepsilon_{n})-r=(r_{n}-r+\varepsilon_{n})+2c(r+2)d(x_{n},\bar{x})
. From (
15
) and (
18
) it follows that
(r_{n}-r+\varepsilon_{n})=\varepsilon_{n}-1/n<0
. Thus,

|\nabla f|(x_{n})-|\nabla f|(\overline{x})<2c(r+2)d(x_{n},\bar{x}).

(23)

Having constructed the sequence
(x_{n})_{n>1/r}
, we claim that it satisfies the conclusion of the theorem. Indeed, (
22
) means that
x_{n}\to\bar{x}
, as
n\to\infty
, and (
21
) plus
r_{n}\to r
, as
n\to\infty
, gives (
12
). Finally, (
23
) immediately implies (
13
).
∎

Lemma 10
.

If
f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is lower semicontinuous and regularly sloped of order
2
with coefficient
c>0
, then the function

x\to|\nabla f|(x)

is lower semicontinuous.

Proof.

Fix
x\in X
and let
x_{n}\to x
as
n\to\infty
. We have to show that

|\nabla f|(x)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}|\nabla f|(x_{n}).

If
|\nabla f|(x_{n})\to\infty
this holds, so let
r\in\mathbb{R}
be such that

r>\liminf_{n\to\infty}|\nabla f|(x_{n}).

There is a subsequence
x_{n_{k}}\to x
as
k\to\infty
, such that
|\nabla f|(x_{n_{k}})<r
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. From (
11
) it follows that

f(y)\geq f(x_{n_{k}})-rd(y,x_{n_{k}})-c(1+r)d^{2}(y,x_{n_{k}})

for any fixed
y\in X
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Passing to limit as
k\to\infty
and taking into account that
f
is lower semicontinuous, we get

f(y)\geq f(x)-rd(y,x)-c(1+r)d^{2}(y,x),

so, by definition
r\geq|\nabla f|(x)
.
∎

Lemma 11
.

If
f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is lower semicontinuous and regularly sloped of order
2
with coefficient
c>0
, and the sequence
x_{n}\to x
is such that the sequence
(|\nabla f|(x_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}f(x_{n})=f(x).

(24)

Proof.

Let
r>0
be such that
|\nabla f|(x_{n})<r
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. From (
11
) it follows that

f(x)\geq f(x_{n})-rd(x,x_{n})-c(r+1)d^{2}(x,x_{n}),\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
d(x,x_{n})\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
, we have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}f(x_{n})\leq f(x),

and (
24
) follows from the lower semicontinuity of
f
.
∎

The following estimate is crucial for our approach.

Lemma 12
.

Let
a_{n}\geq 0
and
b_{n}>0
be such that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}<\infty,

(25)

and for some
c>0

b_{n+1}-b_{n}<2c(2+b_{n})a_{n},\quad\forall n\geq 0.

(26)

Then the sequence
(b_{n})_{0}^{\infty}
is bounded.

Proof.

Set

s:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}
.
Let

A:=\{0\}\cup\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\ b_{n}\leq 1\}
.

Fix an arbitrary
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus A
. Let

k:=\max\{i\in A:\ i<n\}
.
This means that
b_{k}\leq b:=\max\{b_{0},1\}
and
b_{i}>1
, for all
i
such that
k+1\leq i\leq n
. So, we derive from (
26
) that

\frac{b_{i+1}}{b_{i}}<1+2c\left(1+\frac{2}{b_{i}}\right)a_{i}<1+6ca_{i},\quad\text{for all }i\text{ such that }k+1\leq i\leq n-1.

Since
\ln(1+t)\leq t
for
t\geq 0
, we have

\ln b_{i+1}-\ln b_{i}<6ca_{i},\quad\text{for all }i\text{ such that }k+1\leq i\leq n-1.

Summing these, we get

\ln b_{n}-\ln b_{k+1}<6c\sum_{i=k+1}^{n-1}a_{i}<6cs.

Therefore,

b_{n}<b_{k+1}e^{6cs}.

But because
b_{k}\leq b
, (
26
) gives
b_{k+1}\leq b+2c(2+b)a_{k}<b+2c(2+b)s
, so

b_{n}<(b+2c(2+b)s)e^{6cs},\quad\forall n\in N\text{ such that }\ b_{n}>1.

∎

We will prove here a variant of Long Orbit Empty Value (LOEV) method, see
[
12
,
14
]
. It concerns multivalued maps
S:X\rightrightarrows X
, where
(X,d)
is a complete metric space. As usual,
\mathrm{dom}\,S:=\{x\in X:\ S(x)\neq\varnothing\}
.

A
S
-
orbit
is called any finite or infinite sequence
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}\ldots
such that
x_{i+1}\in S(x_{i})
for all
i
’s with a successor. We call
x_{0}

the starting point
of the orbit. We differentiate between
finite
and
infinite
orbits. It stands to reason that for a finite orbit
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
, with
x_{i+1}\in S(x_{i})
, for all
i
such that
0\leq i\leq n-1
, the last point
x_{n}
would be called
the end point
of the orbit. Obviously, each finite orbit
(x_{i})_{0}^{n}
is of
finite length

\left|(x_{i})_{0}^{n}\right|:=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}d(x_{i+1},x_{i}),

if
n\geq 1
. The length of an orbit consisting of one point is, of course, zero.

Considering infinite orbits,
(x_{i})_{0}^{\infty}
with
x_{n+1}\in S(x_{n})
for all
n\geq 0
is called orbit of
finite length
if

\left|(x_{i})_{0}^{\infty}\right|:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d(x_{n+1},x_{n})<\infty,

and, respectively, of
infinite length
if the above series is divergent.

Since
X
is complete, each infinite orbit of finite length is convergent as a sequence, that is, there is
x_{\infty}\in X
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}d(x_{n},x_{\infty})=0.

We call
x_{\infty}
the
end point
of the orbit
(x_{i})_{0}^{\infty}
.

Definition 13
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space and let
S:X\rightrightarrows X
be a multivalued map. We say that
S
satisfies property
(\ast)
if

x\not\in S(x),\quad\forall x\in X,

(27)

and for any infinite
S
-orbit
(x_{n})_{0}^{\infty}
of finite length with end point
x_{\infty}
, and any
y\in S(x_{\infty})
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

y\in S(x_{n}),\quad\forall n>N.

Theorem 14
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space. Let
S:X\rightrightarrows X
satisfy property
(\ast)
and let
x_{0}\in X
be fixed.

Then at least one of the following two holds:

(a)

There is a
S
-orbit of finite length starting at
x_{0}
and ending outside of
\mathrm{dom}\,S
.

(b)

There is a
S
-orbit of infinite length starting at
x_{0}
.

Proof.

We construct the desired
S
-orbit by induction, starting at
x_{0}
.

If
x_{n}
is already chosen, then:

If
S(x_{n})=\varnothing
then
(a)
is fulfilled and we are done.

If, on the other hand,
S(x_{n})\neq\varnothing
, let

\nu_{n}:=\sup\{d(y,x_{n}):\ y\in S(x_{n})\}>0.

(28)

Take
x_{n+1}\in S(x_{n})
such that

d(x_{n+1},x_{n})>\min\{1,\nu_{n}/2\}.

(29)

Assume that we obtain in this way an infinite orbit
(x_{n})_{0}^{\infty}
with
x_{n+1}\in S(x_{n})
,
\forall n\geq 0
.

If
(x_{n})_{0}^{\infty}
is of infinite length then
(b)
is fulfilled and we are done.

If
(x_{n})_{0}^{\infty}
is of finite length, then let
x_{\infty}
be the end point of
(x_{n})_{0}^{\infty}
, i.e.

\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}=x_{\infty}.

Assume that there is some
y\in S(x_{\infty})
. We know from
(\ast)
property, see (
27
), that
y\neq x_{\infty}
, and
y\in S(x_{n})
for all but finitely many
n
’s. Then (
28
) gives
\nu_{n}\geq d(y,x_{n})\to d(y,x_{\infty})>0
and (

\liminf_{n\to\infty}d(x_{n+1},x_{n})>0

\left|(x_{n})_{0}^{\infty}\right|=\infty

(a)
holds.
∎

Lemma 15

(see
[
13
]
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be complete metric space. Let
f,g:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be lower semicontinuous. Let
f
also be proper and bounded below. Then

\inf_{\mathrm{dom}\,f}(f-g)=\inf_{\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|}(f-g).

(30)

In
[
13
, Lemma 3.8.]
the equality (
30
) is proved for the global slope, which dominates the local slope, so the formula there is stronger, but we do not need it in full.

Definition 16
.

Let
(X,d)
be complete metric space. Let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be proper, lower semicontinuous and bounded below.

We say that
\bar{x}
is the
sharp minimum
of
f
, if it is the minimum:

f(\bar{x})=\min f,

and, moreover,

0<\inf_{x\neq\bar{x}}|\nabla f|(x).

(31)

If
f
has a sharp minimum, then
f
cannot have any other local minima, because
|\nabla f|(x)=0
at each local minimum
x
. It is easy to check that the sharp minimum is
strong
, that is, each minimising sequence converges to it, but a strong minimum is not necessarily sharp.

Proposition 17
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space and let
f,g:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be proper and lower semicontinuous functions. Let
f
be regularly sloped of order
2
with coefficient
c>0
and have sharp minimum at
\bar{x}
. If

|\nabla g|(x)<|\nabla f|(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|\setminus\{\bar{x}\},

(32)

and

g(y)\geq g(x)-|\nabla f|(x)d(y,x)-c(|\nabla f|(x)+1)d^{2}(y,x),

(33)

for all
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|
and all
y\in X
,
then

\min_{\mathrm{dom}\,f}(f-g)=f(\bar{x})-g(\bar{x}).

(34)

Proof.

If
\bar{x}\not\in\mathrm{dom}\,g
then the right hand side of (
34
) is
-\infty
, this is the minimum, and (
34
) is trivial.

So, let
g(\bar{x})<\infty
and let us assume for simplicity – and without loss of generality – that

f(\bar{x})=g(\bar{x})=0.

To verify (
34
) we need to prove that
f\geq g
. But from Lemma
15
we know that it is enough to show that

f(x)\geq g(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|.

Therefore, fix an arbitrary
x_{0}\in X
such that
f(x_{0})<\infty
. In what follows we will show that
f(x_{0})\geq g(x_{0})
.

Since
\bar{x}
is a sharp minimum, from (
31
) there is
\varepsilon>0
such that

|\nabla f|(x)>\varepsilon,\quad\forall x\in\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|(x)\setminus\{\bar{x}\}.

(35)

For any
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|
such that
x\neq\bar{x}
, define the following subsets of
\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|
:

S_{1}(x):=\{y\in\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|:\ f(y)-g(y)<f(x)-g(x)\}

(36)

(note that because of (
32
),
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla g|\subset\mathrm{dom}\,g
, so
f(x)-g(x)
is well defined);

S_{2}(x):=\{y\in\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|:\ f(y)<f(x)-\varepsilon d(y,x)\};

(37)

S_{3}(x):=\{y:\ |\nabla f|(y)<|\nabla f|(x)+2c(2+|\nabla f|(x))d(y,x)\},

(38)

and

S(x):=S_{1}(x)\cap S_{2}(x)\cap S_{3}(x).

In this way we define a multivalued map
S:X\rightrightarrows X
. We will study its properties.

First, the way it is defined ensures that
\mathrm{dom}\,S\subset\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|\setminus\{\bar{x}\}
. We claim that in fact

\mathrm{dom}\,S=\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|\setminus\{\bar{x}\}.

(39)

Indeed, fix
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|
such that
x\neq\bar{x}
, so

r:=|\nabla f|(x)\in(\varepsilon,\infty),

see (
35
). From (
32
) we have
|\nabla g|(x)<r
, so there is
\delta>0
such that
r-\delta>0
and

g(y)\geq g(x)-(r-\delta)d(y,x),\quad\forall y\in B(x;\delta).

From Theorem
9
there is a sequence
x_{n}\to x
satisfying (
12
) and (
13
). Now, (
13
) simply means that
x_{n}\in S_{3}(x)
for all large
n
’s. From (
12
) we get on the one hand that,
f(x)-f(x_{n})>\varepsilon d(x,x_{n})
, because
|\nabla f|(x)>\varepsilon
. Therefore,
x_{n}\in S_{2}(x)
for all large
n
’s. On the other hand,
x_{n}\in B(x;\delta)
for all large
n
’s, so using the above inequality for
g
and again (
12
) we get

\frac{f(x)-f(x_{n})}{d(x,x_{n})}>r-\delta\geq\frac{g(x)-g(x_{n})}{d(x,x_{n})}\Rightarrow f(x_{n})-g(x_{n})<f(x)-g(x),

for all
n
large enough. This means that
x_{n}\in S_{1}(x)
for all large
n
’s. So,
x_{n}\in S(x)
for all but finitely many
n
’s and, therefore,
S(x)\neq\varnothing
and (
39
) is verified.

Next, each
S
-orbit starting at
x_{0}
is of finite length. Indeed, if
(x_{n})_{0}^{\infty}
is a
S
-orbit, it is also a
S_{2}
-orbit, so iterating on (

\sum_{i=0}^{n}d(x_{i+1},x_{i})<\varepsilon^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(f(x_{i})-f(x_{i+1}))=\varepsilon^{-1}(f(x_{0})-f(x_{n+1})),

and, since

, we get

|(x_{n})_{0}^{\infty}|\leq f(x_{0})/\varepsilon.

(40)

The same estimate is valid, of course, for the lengths of the finite
S
-orbits starting at
x_{0}
(but we do not need it).

Let
(x_{n})_{0}^{\infty}
be an arbitrary infinite
S
-orbit starting at
x_{0}
. We already know that it is of finite length, so it has an end point, say
x_{\infty}
. We claim that

\lim_{n\to\infty}(f(x_{n}),g(x_{n}))=(f(x_{\infty}),g(x_{\infty})).

(41)

Indeed, set
a_{n}:=d(x_{n+1},x_{n})
and
b_{n}:=|\nabla f|(x_{n})
. We know that
a_{n}
’s satisfy (
25
) and because
(x_{n})_{0}^{\infty}
is a
S_{3}
-orbit, (
38
) translates to (
26
). From Lemma
12
we get that the sequence
(|\nabla f|(x_{n}))_{0}^{\infty}
is bounded and then Lemma
11
ensures that
f(x_{n})\to f(x_{\infty})
. We repeat the proof of Lemma
11
for
g
using (
33
). Thus (
41
) is verified.

Now we can show that
S
has property
(\ast)
. Because of the strict inequality,
S_{1}
satisfies (
27
), thus so does
S
.

Let
(x_{n})_{0}^{\infty}
be an infinite
S
-orbit of finite length ending at
x_{\infty}
and let
y\in S(x_{\infty})
. Because
y\in S_{1}(x_{\infty})
, we have by (

) that
f(y)-g(y)<f(x_{\infty})-g(x_{\infty})
, then (
41
) gives that
f(y)-g(y)<f(x_{n})-g(x_{n})
for all large
n
’s. That is, eventually

.

In a similar fashion, since
y\in S_{2}(x_{\infty})
, we have
f(y)<f(x_{\infty})-\varepsilon d(y,x_{\infty})
, see (
37
). Since the function

is lower semicontinuous and with positive value at

, we have

for all but finitely many

Since

, we have by (

|\nabla f|(y)<|\nabla f|(x_{\infty})+2c(2+|\nabla f|(x_{\infty}))d(y,x_{\infty}).

From Lemma

we have that the function

is lower semicontinuous, thus the function

eventually.

Property

of

14
. From (

only (a) is possible.

If a finite

-orbit starting at
x_{0}
, say

, ends outside of

, then because

maps

into
\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|
, necessarily

. Since

, from (

. Iterating on (

), we get

.

Finally, let
(x_{n})_{0}^{\infty}
be an infinite

-orbit of finite length starting at

and ending at

with

gives that

. Since

Theorem 18
.

Let
(X,d)

f

2
with coefficient

and have sharp minimum at
\bar{x}
. If

|\nabla g|(x)\leq|\nabla f|(x),\quad\forall x\in X,

(42)

and (

f(x)\geq(f(\bar{x})-g(\bar{x}))+g(x),\quad\forall x\in X.

(43)

Since

is the minimum of

, we have
|\nabla f|(\bar{x})=0
. From (
42

|\nabla g|(\bar{x})=0

. This means that the right hand side of (

Since

For

3
Clarke-Rockafellar subdifferential

To the end of the article we work in a Banach space denoted by
(E,\|\cdot\|)
with closed unit ball
B_{E}
and dual
E^{*}
.

Provided
f:E\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is lower semicontinuous at
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f
, the Clarke-Rockafellar directional derivative,
e.g.
[
3
, p.97]
, at the point
x
at direction
h\in E
is:

f^{\circ}(x;h):=\sup_{\varepsilon>0}f^{\circ}_{\varepsilon}(x;h),\text{ where }

(45)

f^{\circ}_{\varepsilon}(x;h):=\limsup_{y\to_{f}x,t\searrow 0}\frac{\inf f(y+t(h+\varepsilon B_{X}))-f(y)}{t}.

The Clarke-Rockafellar subdifferential of
f
at
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f
is defined as:

\partial f(x):=\{p\in E^{*}:\ p(h)\leq f^{\circ}(x;h),\ \forall h\in E\}.

(46)

Of course,
\partial f(x)=\varnothing
if
f(x)=+\infty
.
It can be shown that
\mathrm{dom}\,\partial f=\{x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f:\ f^{\circ}(x;h)>-\infty,\ \forall h\in E\}=\{x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f:\ \exists c>0:\ f^{\circ}(x;h)\geq-c\|h\|,\ \forall h\in E\}
, and that on the latter set the function
h\to f^{\circ}(x;h)
is sublinear, thus convex, and lower semicontinuous function from
E
to
(-\infty,\infty]
. It is clear that
\partial f(x)
is convex and
w^{*}
closed subset of
E^{*}
.

Note that if
f
is
\lambda
-Lipschitz around
x
, then
\inf f(y+t(h+\varepsilon B_{E}))\geq f(y+th)-\lambda t\varepsilon
, so for a Lipschitz function (
45
) reduces to the much simpler Clarke’s formula:

f^{\circ}(x;h)=\limsup_{y\to_{f}x,t\searrow 0}\frac{f(y+th)-f(y)}{t}.

We consider also the Fréchet subdifferential of
f
at
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f
:

p\in\partial_{F}f(x)\iff\liminf_{\|h\|\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)-p(h)}{\|h\|}\geq 0,

otherwise
\partial_{F}(x)=\varnothing
.
It is easy to check that
\partial_{F}\subset\partial
, see e.g.
[
21
, Proposition 4.8(a)]
.

The function
f:E\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is called
F
-regular
at
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f
if

\partial_{F}f(x)=\partial f(x),

and it is called
F
-regular
if it is so at any point
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f
.

The basic example of
F
-regular functions are the convex continuous functions.

We will use the following Sum Theorem.

Theorem 19

(Sum Theorem)
.

Let
f:E\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be proper, lower semicontinuous and
F
-regular function. Let
h:E\to\mathbb{R}
be locally Lipschitz and
F
-regular function. Then
f+h
is
F
-regular, and

\partial(f+h)(x)=\partial f(x)+\partial h(x),\quad\forall x\in E.

(47)

Proof.

First, let
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f
. From
[
21
, Theorem 2.98]
we have that

\partial(f+h)(x)\subset\partial f(x)+\partial h(x).

To get the opposite inclusion, we use that

\partial f(x)+\partial h(x)=\partial_{F}f(x)+\partial_{F}h(x)

by
F
-regularity of both functions, and that

\partial_{F}f(x)+\partial_{F}h(x)\subset\partial_{F}(f+h)(x),

see e.g.
[
21
, Proposition 4.11]
.
Hence,

\partial f(x)+\partial h(x)\subset\partial_{F}(f+h)(x)\subset\partial(f+h)(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f.

If
x\not\in\mathrm{dom}\,f
, then
x\not\in\mathrm{dom}\,(f+h)
either, hence
\partial(f+h)(x)=\partial f(x)=\varnothing
. Then
\partial f(x)+\partial h(x)=\varnothing
by definition, and (
47
) is established.
∎

F
-regularity provides a concise condition allowing the transfer of subdifferential inclusions to slope inequalities.

Proposition 20
.

Let
f:E\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be proper, lower semicontinuous and
F
-regular. Then

\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|=\mathrm{dom}\,\partial f,

(48)

and

|\nabla f|(x)=\min\{\|p\|:\ p\in\partial f(x)\},\quad\forall x\in\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|.

(49)

Proof.

Note that, because the intersection of
\partial f(x)
with a dual ball is
w^{*}
-compact, and the dual norm is
w^{*}
upper semicontinuous, the minimum in the right hand side of (
49
) is really attained.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|
and let
r>|\nabla f|(x_{0})
be arbitrary. By definition, the function

u(x):=f(x)+r\|x-x_{0}\|

attains a local minimum at
x_{0}
, so
0\in\partial u(x_{0})
. By Sum Theorem – Theorem
19
– there are
p\in\partial f(x_{0})
and
q\in\partial r\|\cdot-x_{0}\|(x_{0})
such that
\|q\|\leq r
, and
p+q=0
. Clearly,
\|p\|\leq r
. Thus we get

\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|\subset\mathrm{dom}\,\partial f,

(50)

and, taking infimum over
r>|\nabla f|(x_{0})
we obtain that

|\nabla f|(x_{0})\geq\|p\|\geq\min\{\|p\|:\ p\in\partial f(x_{0})\}.

(51)

Note that for these we did not use the
F
-regularity of
f
.

Let now
x_{0}\in\mathrm{dom}\,\partial f
and let
p_{0}\in\partial f(x_{0})
be such that

\|p_{0}\|=\min\{\|p\|:\ p\in\partial f(x_{0})\}.

Since
f
is
F
-regular,
p_{0}\in\partial_{F}f(x_{0})
, that is,

f(x)-f(x_{0})\geq p_{0}(x-x_{0})+\alpha(x)\|x-x_{0}\|,

where
\alpha(x)\to 0
, as
x\to x_{0}
. So,
f(x)-f(x_{0})\geq-(\|p_{0}\|-\alpha(x))\|x-x_{0}\|
, and

\liminf_{x\to x_{0}}\frac{f(x)-f(x_{0})}{\|x-x_{0}\|}\geq-\|p_{0}\|,

meaning that

|\nabla f|(x_{0})\leq\|p_{0}\|.

(52)

So,
x_{0}\in\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|
and, since
x_{0}\in\mathrm{dom}\,\partial f
was arbitrary, we have
\mathrm{dom}\,\partial f\subset\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|
, which together with (
50
) gives (
48
). On the other hand, (
51
) and (
52
) give (
49
).
∎

4
Primal lower regular functions

We start with some preliminary results concerning primal lower regular functions defined on a Banach space
(E,\|\cdot\|)
.

First, observe that if
\bar{x}\in\mathrm{plr}_{c,\delta}f
, then
f
is
F
-regular on
B^{\circ}(\bar{x};\delta)
, see
[
21
, Thorem 11.16(a)]
.

Lemma 21
.

If
\bar{x}\in\mathrm{plr}_{c,\delta}f
for some
c,\delta>0
, and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then

\bar{x}\in\mathrm{plr}_{c,\delta}(\alpha f).

Proof.

Let
x,y\in B^{\circ}(\bar{x};\delta)
and
p\in\partial f(x)
be arbitrary. Multiplying (
1
) by
\alpha>0
, and using that
p_{1}:=\alpha p\in\partial(\alpha f)(x)
, we get

\displaystyle\alpha f(y)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\alpha f(x)+p_{1}(y-x)-c(\|p_{1}\|+\alpha)\|y-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\alpha f(x)+p_{1}(y-x)-c(\|p_{1}\|+1)\|y-x\|^{2}.

Because
\partial(\alpha f)=\alpha\partial f
, we are done.
∎

Lemma 22
.

Let
f:E\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper and lower semicontinuous function, and let
h:E\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex and continuous function.

If

\bar{x}\in\mathrm{plr}_{c,\delta}f\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \mathrm{Lip}\,h(B^{\circ}(\bar{x};\delta))=L,

then

\bar{x}\in\mathrm{plr}_{c(L+1),\delta}(f+h).

Proof.

Denote
u:=f+h
and fix arbitrary
x\in B^{\circ}(\bar{x};\delta)
and
\xi\in\partial u(x)
.

From the Sum Theorem – Theorem
19
– we have that
\xi=p+q
where
p\in\partial f(x)
and
q\in\partial h(x)
. The latter means

h(y)\geq h(x)+\langle q,y-x\rangle,\;\forall y\in X.

Adding this and (
1
) together yields

u(y)\geq u(x)+\langle\xi,y-x\rangle-c\left(\|p\|+1\right)\|y-x\|^{2},\quad\forall y\in B^{\circ}(\bar{x};\delta).

(53)

Note that, since
\|p\|=\|\xi-q\|\leq\|\xi\|+\|q\|
,

\|p\|+1\leq\|\xi\|+\|q\|+1\leq(\|q\|+1)(\|\xi\|+1),

and because
\|q\|\leq L
, the Lipshitz constant of
h
around
\bar{x}
, (
53
) gives

u(y)\geq u(x)+\langle\xi,y-x\rangle-c(L+1)(\|\xi\|+1)\|y-x\|^{2},\quad\forall y\in B^{\circ}(\bar{x};\delta).

∎

Since
\|\xi\|<1
and
\|p\|<1
, we have

q(\bar{x}-x)=(\xi+p)(\bar{x}-x)-r(\bar{x}-x)\geq 2\|\bar{x}-x\|.

By definition and using that
\|q\|\leq 2+\|r\|=6
,

\displaystyle f(\bar{x})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle f(x)+q(\bar{x}-x)-c(\|q\|+1)\|\bar{x}-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle f(x)+2\|\bar{x}-x\|-7c\|\bar{x}-x\|^{2}.

But, again by definition, and using that
\|p\|<1
,

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle f(\bar{x})+p(x-\bar{x})-c(\|p\|+1)\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle f(\bar{x})-\|x-\bar{x}\|-2c\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2}.

Now, putting the above two together, we get

0>\|x-\bar{x}\|-9c\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2},

meaning that
\|x-\bar{x}\|>1/9c\geq\delta^{\prime}
, see (
57
), contradiction.

Since the function

h(x)=-p(x)+4\|x-\bar{x}\|

is convex and globally Lipschitz with constant less or equal to
\|p\|+4<5
, Lemma
22
immediately gives that
\bar{x}\in\mathrm{plr}_{c^{\prime},\delta}f_{1}
with
c^{\prime}
given by (
57
). Since
\delta^{\prime}\leq\delta
, we are done.

Moreover, for any
x\in B^{\circ}(\bar{x};\delta^{\prime})
it holds that

f(x)\geq f(\bar{x})+p(x-\bar{x})-c(\|p\|+1)\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2}.

Hence, having in mind that
\|p\|<1
,

f(x)-p(x)\geq f(\bar{x})-p(\bar{x})-2c\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2},

f(x)-p(x)+4\|x-\bar{x}\|\geq f(\bar{x})-p(\bar{x})+4\|x-\bar{x}\|-2c\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2},

f_{1}(x)\geq f_{1}(\bar{x})+2\|x-\bar{x}\|(2-c\|x-\bar{x}\|).

Since
2c\|x-\bar{x}\|<1
for
x\in B^{\circ}(\bar{x};\delta^{\prime})
, we have per force that
f_{1}(\bar{x})\leq\inf f_{1}(B^{\circ}(\bar{x};\delta^{\prime}))
.
∎

Lemma 24
.

Let the functions
f,g:E\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be proper and lower semicontinuous, and let for some
c,\delta>0
,

\bar{x}\in\mathrm{plr}_{c,\delta}f\cap\mathrm{plr}_{c,\delta}g\cap\mathrm{dom}\,\partial f.

(59)

Let

\partial f(x)\subset\partial g(x),\quad\forall x\in B^{\circ}(\bar{x};\delta).

(60)

Then

f(x)\geq g(x)+(f(\bar{x})-g(\bar{x})),\quad\forall x\in B^{\circ}(\bar{x};\delta^{\prime}),

(61)

where
\delta^{\prime}
is given by (
57
).

Proof.

First, let us note that from (
59
) and (
60
) it follows that
\bar{x}\in\mathrm{dom}\,g
, so the right hand side of (
61
) is well defined.

We can assume without loss of generality that
\bar{x}=0
and
f(\bar{x})=g(\bar{x})=0
.

Fix an arbitrary
x_{0}\in\delta^{\prime}B^{\circ}_{E}
. We need to show that
f(x_{0})\geq g(x_{0})
, so we can assume without loss of generality that
f(x_{0})<\infty
.

Let
\nu\in(0,1)
be such that

\nu+\|x_{0}\|<\delta^{\prime}.

From Proposition
20
it follows that
0\in\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|
. Pick
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that

|\nabla(\alpha f)|(0)<\min\{1,\nu/\delta^{\prime}\},\ \inf(\alpha f)(\delta^{\prime}B_{E})>-\nu,\ (\alpha f)(x_{0})<\nu.

(62)

Note that by definition
f
is bounded below on
\delta^{\prime}B_{E}
, so such choice is possible. From Lemma
21
and (
59
) we have that

0\in\mathrm{plr}_{c,\delta}(\alpha f)\cap\mathrm{plr}_{c,\delta}(\alpha g).

(63)

From (
62
) and Proposition
20
, see (
49
), it follows that there is a
p\in\partial(\alpha f)(0)
such that

\|p\|<\min\{1,\nu/\delta^{\prime}\}.

(64)

In particular, (
55
) holds. Therefore, Lemma
23
gives that

0\in\mathrm{plr}_{c^{\prime},\delta^{\prime}}f_{1},

where
f_{1}(x)=\alpha f(x)+h(x)
,
h(x)=-p(x)+4\|x\|
, and
c^{\prime}=6c
, see (
57
). Moreover,
\inf f_{1}(\delta^{\prime}B_{E}^{\circ})\geq 0
and

|\nabla f_{1}|(x)\geq 1,\quad\forall x\in\delta^{\prime}B_{E}^{\circ}\setminus\{0\}.

(65)

Define

g_{1}(x):=\alpha g(x)+h(x).

Using (
59
), (
60
) and Lemma
23
, we get

0\in\mathrm{plr}_{c^{\prime},\delta^{\prime}}g_{1}.

(66)

Since
\partial(\alpha f)=\alpha\partial f
, and
\partial(\alpha g)=\alpha\partial g
from (
60
) it holds that
\partial(\alpha f)\subset\partial(\alpha g)
. Now the Sum Theorem – Theorem
19
– gives that

\partial f_{1}(x)=\partial\alpha f(x)+\partial h(x)\subset\partial\alpha g(x)+\partial h(x)=\partial g_{1}(x),\quad\forall x\in\delta B_{E}^{\circ}.

From Proposition
20
it follows that

|\nabla f_{1}|(x)\geq|\nabla g_{1}|(x),\quad\forall x\in\delta B_{E}^{\circ}.

(67)

Set

Y:=\{x\in\delta^{\prime}B_{E}^{\circ}:f_{1}(x)\leq f_{1}(x_{0})\}.

Since
0
is the minimum of
f_{1}
on
\delta^{\prime}B_{E}^{\circ}
, we have that

0\in Y.

(68)

We claim that

Y\subset(\nu+\|x_{0}\|)B_{E}.

Indeed, if
x
is such that

\nu+\|x_{0}\|<\|x\|<\delta^{\prime},

then, see (
62
) and (
64
),

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha f(x)-p(x)+4\|x\|

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle-\nu-\frac{\nu}{\delta^{\prime}}\delta^{\prime}+4(\nu+\|x_{0}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\nu+4\|x_{0}\|,

while
f_{1}(x_{0})<\nu+\frac{\nu}{\delta^{\prime}}\delta^{\prime}+4\|x_{0}\|=2\nu+4\|x_{0}\|
, so
x\not\in Y
.

This means that
Y
is closed, because
f_{1}
is lower semicontinuous. So, with respect to the canonical metric induced by
\|\cdot\|
,
Y
is a complete metric space. In addition, Lemma
5
gives that for

\overline{f}_{1}:=f_{1}\upharpoonright Y,

we have

|\nabla\overline{f}_{1}|(x)=|\nabla f_{1}|(x),\quad\forall x\in Y.

(69)

Set

\overline{g}_{1}:=g_{1}\upharpoonright Y.

Obviously,
|\nabla\overline{g}_{1}|(x)\leq|\nabla g_{1}|(x)
, for all
x\in Y
, so (
67
) gives

|\nabla\overline{f}_{1}|(x)\geq|\nabla\overline{g}_{1}|(x),\quad\forall x\in Y.

From this and (
66
) we have that for all
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla\overline{f}_{1}|\subset\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla\overline{g}_{1}|
, and all
y\in Y
we have

\displaystyle\overline{g}_{1}(y)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\overline{g}_{1}(x)-|\nabla\overline{g}_{1}|(x)\|y-x\|-c(|\nabla\overline{g}_{1}|(x)+1)\|y-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\overline{g}_{1}(x)-|\nabla\overline{f}_{1}|(x)\|y-x\|-c(|\nabla\overline{f}_{1}|(x)+1)\|y-x\|^{2}

Finally, since
0
is the minimum of
\overline{f}_{1}
on
Y
, it is sharp by (
65
) and (
69
), and we can apply Theorem
18
to conclude that
\overline{f}_{1}\geq\overline{g}_{1}
on
Y
. Since
x_{0}\in Y
, this means
\alpha f(x_{0})+p(x_{0})+4\|x_{0}\|\geq\alpha g(x_{0})+p(x_{0})+4\|x_{0}\|
, therefore
f(x_{0})\geq g(x_{0})
and we are done.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We again assume that
\bar{x}=0
.

From (
4
) and (
57
) it is clear that
\hat{\delta}=\delta^{\prime}/2
.

For any fixed
x\in\hat{\delta}B^{\circ}_{E}
such that
|\nabla f|(x)<\infty
(and there are such
x
’s because of the graphical density of
\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|
in
\mathrm{dom}\,f
, see e.g.
[
13
, Lemma 3.7.]
), Lemma
24
applied to
f
and
g
and then to
g
and
f
on
B^{\circ}(x;\hat{\delta})
shows that:

f(y)=g(y)+(f(x)-g(x)),\quad\forall y\in B^{\circ}(x;\hat{\delta}).

But
0\in B^{\circ}(x;\hat{\delta})
, so

f(0)=g(0)+(f(x)-g(x))\iff f(x)-g(x)=f(0)-g(0).

That is, for
a:=f(0)-g(0)
we have

f(y)=g(y)+a,\quad\forall y\in B^{\circ}(x;\hat{\delta}),\ \forall x\in\hat{\delta}B^{\circ}_{E}\cap\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|.

But because
\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|
is dense in
\mathrm{dom}\,f
and
0\in\mathrm{dom}\,f

\hat{\delta}B^{\circ}_{E}\subset\left(\hat{\delta}B^{\circ}_{E}\cap\mathrm{dom}\,|\nabla f|\right)+\hat{\delta}B^{\circ}_{E}.

The proof is then complete. ∎
