Inhibition of the QPCT–PDIA4 axis rescues ΔF508 and N1303K CFTR in cystic fibrosis
Authors: Le Sun, Larry Rodriguez, Sandra Pankow, Jolene Diedrich, Ke Qin, Jianan Zhang, Xu Wu, Peng Wu, John R. Yates
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a genetic disorder caused by CFTR mutations, most commonly ΔF508, leading to defective ion transport and multisystem pathology. Small-molecule modulators partially restore mutant CFTR function, but therapeutic efficacy remains limited, particularly for N1303K mutation refractory to current treatments. Here, we show that inhibition of the glutaminyl-peptide cyclotransferase (QPCT)-dependent pathway rescues both the surface expression and functional activity of ΔF508 CFTR. Integrated molecular and physiological analyses identify protein disulfide-isomerase A4 (PDIA4) as a key mediator of this process through a pyroglutamate (pGlu)-dependent association with misfolded ΔF508 CFTR. QPCT-dependent pGlu modification promotes the association of PDIA4 with mutant CFTR within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) quality control machinery, whereas inhibition of QPCT disrupts this interaction, relieving ER retention and enabling a fraction of ΔF508 CFTR to reach the cell surface. Furthermore, inhibition of QPCT also restores the function of the N1303K CFTR mutant, indicating a broader relevance of this pathway in regulating CFTR proteostasis. These findings provide evidence for an ER quality control mechanism governing mutant CFTR fate and suggest potential therapeutic strategies for CFTR mutations that are unresponsive to existing modulators.

Introduction

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a life-threatening autosomal recessive disorder that primarily impacts the respiratory, digestive, and reproductive systems1. It occurs worldwide across diverse populations, although it has historically been most frequently diagnosed in individuals of European ancestry, with an estimated incidence of ~1 in 2500 live births in this group2. This disease is caused by mutations in the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene, which encodes a transmembrane protein that traffics through the Golgi via the conventional exocytic pathway and undergoes complex glycosylation3. The mature, fully glycosylated form (band C) localizes to the apical membrane of epithelial cells, functioning as a chloride and bicarbonate channel that regulates salt and water balance across epithelial surfaces4,5.

CF-causing mutations in CFTR disrupt channel function, leading to characteristic features of cystic fibrosis, including wheezing, shortness of breath, and recurrent lung infections6. There are more than 2,000 identified mutations in CFTR gene, but not all cause CF7. Disease-causing mutations are classified into six classes (Class I–VI) based on their effects on CFTR protein function8. Class II mutations result in misfolded proteins that are retained and degraded in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), preventing their trafficking to the cell surface. Representative mutations within this class include ΔF508, N1303K and G85E. The ΔF508 mutation is caused by a three-nucleotide deletion resulting in the loss of phenylalanine at position 508. This deletion is present on at least one allele in 90% of CF patients and accounts for ~70% of cases worldwide9. Misfolded ΔF508 CFTR is recognized by the ER quality control machinery and targeted for degradation via the ER-associated degradation (ERAD) pathway and the ubiquitin–proteasome system. As a result, the immature, core-glycosylated form (band B) is trapped on the ER, leading to reduced CFTR expression at the cell surface and impaired chloride and bicarbonate transport10. In addition to these folding and trafficking defects, ΔF508 CFTR also exhibits impaired channel gating. The N1303K mutation, located within the second nucleotide-binding domain (NBD2) essential for ATP binding and hydrolysis, causes a severe folding and assembly defect11. Although a small proportion of N1303K CFTR reaches the plasma membrane, the residual channels display markedly impaired gating and minimal chloride conductance, rendering this variant highly refractory to existing CFTR modulators.

Despite recent therapeutic advances, no single treatment fully corrects the functional defects caused by Class II mutations. Combination CFTR modulators such as Orkambi (VX-809 + VX-770), Symdeko (VX-661 + VX-770), and Trikafta (VX-445 + VX-661 + VX-770), approved by the FDA between 2015 and 2019, partially restore CFTR function in patients carrying the ΔF508 mutation12–14. Trikafta therapy has led to substantial improvements in lung function and overall clinical outcomes in individuals with cystic fibrosis15. However, their efficacy toward other Class II variants, including N1303K, remains minimal. N1303K impairs both CFTR processing and channel gating and exhibits limited responsiveness to currently available correctors or potentiators when used individually, indicating a need for combination therapeutic strategies16. Furthermore, these modulators also have side effects, including pulmonary issues, abdominal pain and nausea, which may reduce patient compliance as they will be treated with these life-saving drugs for a lifetime17. Consequently, these limitations underscore the need for therapies that more effectively address a broader range of CFTR mutations.

Previously, we used an affinity-purification mass spectrometry (AP-MS) platform for deep interactome analysis (CoPIT) to demonstrate that the ΔF508 CFTR mutation acquires disease-specific interactions that disrupt the CFTR protein interaction network18. We found that targeted modulation of several disease-specific protein interactions, such as those involving TRIM21, PABPC1 and YBX1, facilitate the functional rescue of ΔF508 CFTR in primary cystic fibrosis bronchial epithelial cells, thereby offering alternative therapeutic strategies for the treatment of cystic fibrosis. One of these interactors is Glutaminyl-peptide cyclotransferase (QPCT), also known as glutaminyl cyclase (QC), a zinc-dependent enzyme responsible for the biosynthesis of pyroglutamyl peptides19,20. QPCT catalyzes the intramolecular cyclization of N-terminal glutamine and glutamic acid residues into pyroglutamate (pGlu), a post-translational modification observed in many proteins and various neuroendocrine peptides such as Neurotensin (NT), Pyrokinins (PK), and Amyloid-beta (Aβ) peptides21–23. This modification is critical for maintaining the structural stability and functional bioactivity of these peptides, thereby influencing their physiological roles and efficacy24,25. SEN177 is a selective and potent QPCT inhibitor that suppresses the catalytic activity of the enzyme, thereby preventing N-terminal pyroglutamate (pGlu) formation26. Given that QPCT-mediated pyroglutamate formation facilitates the generation and stabilization of amyloid plaques, which constitute a pathological hallmark of neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s and Huntington’s disease, SEN177 has garnered considerable attention for its therapeutic potential to treat Alzheimer’s disease27. By inhibiting QPCT, SEN177 may attenuate the accumulation of neurotoxic amyloid-related peptides, offering a potential mechanistic approach to mitigate disease progression28.

In this study, we uncover a previously unrecognized molecular mechanism that restores ΔF508 CFTR trafficking by targeting mutation-specific interactions in cystic fibrosis. We identify PDIA4, an endoplasmic reticulum chaperone, as a regulator of ΔF508 CFTR quality control through a pyroglutamate (pGlu)-dependent interaction controlled by QPCT. QPCT-mediated pGlu formation at the N terminus of PDIA4 facilitates its interaction with misfolded ΔF508 CFTR within the ER quality control network. Inhibition of QPCT disrupts this interaction, thereby reducing ER retention of the mutant protein and allowing a fraction of ΔF508 CFTR to escape ER quality control and reach the cell surface. Remarkably, this mechanism also improves the processing defect of the N1303K mutant, indicating a broader applicability across CFTR variants with folding defects. Collectively, our findings define a mechanism by which modulation of a pGlu-dependent proteostasis interaction can partially restore mutant CFTR function.

Results

Inhibiting QPCT restores ΔF508 CFTR plasma membrane expression and function in CFBE41o- cells

Previous work has suggested that altering several disease-specific protein interactions promoted the surface expression of ΔF508 CFTR and restored its function18. To assess the potential of QPCT inhibition in rescuing the ΔF508 CFTR defect, we treated CFBE41o- cells expressing ΔF508 CFTR with a QPCT inhibitor SEN177 and monitored CFTR maturation. Dose- and time-dependent analyses showed that 10 μM SEN177 was necessary for efficient rescue, with a marked effect evident after 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). SEN177 increased the cell surface abundance of complex-glycosylated ΔF508 CFTR (band C) In CFBE41o- cells expressing ΔF508 CFTR. A 24-h treatment at 10 μM resulted in ~60% increase in mature CFTR, consistent with enhanced CFTR maturation and surface expression, with no detectable effect on WT CFTR (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. 1C). To determine whether these glycosylated CFTR were successfully localized to the plasma membrane, we evaluated CFTR cell surface expression using immunofluorescence staining. Confocal microscopy analysis confirmed that SEN177 enhanced ΔF508 CFTR protein expression at the cell surface, resulting in a more WT-like localization pattern (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 1D). This result indicated that immature, core-glycosylated CFTR translocates from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi apparatus, where it undergoes complex glycosylation to form mature CFTR, before trafficking to the plasma membrane. The same findings were recapitulated when we performed RNA interference (RNAi) using short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) to knock down QPCT expression. Our results demonstrated that reducing QPCT levels led to an increased ratio of band B to band C of ΔF508 CFTR, which is a prerequisite for the successful rescue of CFTR mutations, while no significant changes were observed in WT CFTR (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 1E). Furthermore, a significant amount of ∆F508 CFTR, which was located only in the ER of control cells, reached the cell surface when QPCT was knocked down (Fig. 1D). However, a critical question remains as to whether the immature, core-glycosylated ΔF508 CFTR proteins that reach the cell surface via unconventional trafficking are functionally competent. To address this, we measured CFTR mediated Cl– current after ER-to-Golgi blockade in Air-liquid interface (ALI) cultures of CFBE41o- cells (Supplementary Fig. 1F)29. Epithelial cells exhibited CFTR-mediated ion transport that was modestly enhanced following 24 h of SEN177 treatment (Fig. 1E, F). CFTR activity was assessed under stimulated conditions: following blockade of epithelial sodium channels with amiloride, forskolin was applied to elevate intracellular cAMP and activate CFTR, resulting in a significant change in short-circuit current (Isc) and transepithelial conductance (G)30,31. Genistein, a CFTR potentiator, was subsequently added to further enhance channel gating. These responses were more pronounced in SEN177 pre-treated ALI cultures compared to untreated controls. Notably, both current and conductance change were markedly abolished upon application of the CFTR-specific inhibitor CFTRinh-17232. These findings indicated the presence of spontaneous CFTR activity in SEN177 pre-treated cultures (Supplementary Fig. 1G, H). Knockdown of QPCT consistently enhanced forskolin-stimulated ΔF508 CFTR channel activity at the apical plasma membrane in CFBE41o− epithelia, and ΔF508 CFTR channel activity was abolished by CFTRinh-172 treatment (Fig. 1G, H and Supplementary Fig. 1I, J). Our results showed that QPCT inhibition improved the channel function of ΔF508 CFTR, suggesting that QPCT, as a modulator of interactions, might be a promising route to correction of the ΔF508 CFTR defect.

PDIA4 mediates QPCT-dependent regulation of ΔF508 CFTR to the plasma membrane

To determine whether SEN177 modulates the interaction between ΔF508 CFTR and QPCT, Co-immunoprecipitation assays were conducted in CFBE41o- cells stably expressing ΔF508 CFTR. QPCT was selectively enriched in CFTR IP samples, but absent in IgG controls, confirming the specificity of the interaction. Notably, treatment with SEN177 did not affect the association between ΔF508 CFTR and QPCT (Supplementary Fig. 2A). These results suggested that QPCT might not directly mediate the regulation of core-glycosylated ΔF508 CFTR to the plasma membrane.

We next sought to investigate the indirect mechanisms by which QPCT might contribute to CFTR processing. QPCT catalyzes the conversion of N-terminal glutamine residues into pyroglutamate, a modification that can affect the stability and function of peptides33. An in-silico prediction of mature human protein sequences with potential to form N-terminal pyroglutamate was conducted under the assumption that signal peptide cleavage was the primary mechanism for exposing non-Met N-terminal amino acids34. This analysis identified ~600 candidates with predicted glutamine or glutamate residues at their N-terminus. Among these candidates, protein disulfide isomerase family A member 4 (PDIA4) was significantly enriched in the ΔF508 CFTR mutation-specific interactome but not in the WT CFTR interactome under the same experimental conditions, suggesting preferential interaction with the misfolded ΔF508 variant (Supplementary Fig. 2B). PDIA4 functions as both an oxidoreductase and a chaperone, facilitating the formation, breakage, and rearrangement of disulfide bonds to ensure proper protein conformation35,36. It is an integral component of the ER quality control machinery and contributes to proteostasis under conditions that challenge protein folding37. Under ER stress, misfolded proteins such as ΔF508 CFTR accumulate and engage ER quality control pathways, including interactions with chaperones such as PDIA438. While these mechanisms are generally protective, they can also promote the retention of misfolded proteins within ER39. In this context, we hypothesized that PDIA4 may act as a downstream regulator mediating the QPCT-dependent control of ΔF508 CFTR processing.

To understand the distinct functions of PDIA4 in regulating ΔF508 CFTR, we first performed Co-immunoprecipitation experiments in CFBE41o- cells overexpressing ΔF508 CFTR. The results showed that an anti-CFTR antibody could enrich the endogenous PDIA4 protein, supporting the existence of a specific interaction between these two proteins (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, immunofluorescence demonstrated partial co-localization of ΔF508 CFTR and PDIA4 within the ER network distributed throughout the cytoplasm, suggesting a close spatial relationship (Fig. 2B). AlphaFold-based structural prediction suggested a potential interaction between PDIA4 and ΔF508 CFTR40. However, this prediction should be interpreted with caution, as it did not account for the established topology of CFTR, in which PDIA4 resides in the ER lumen while the nucleotide-binding domains (NBDs) are cytosolic. Therefore, this model was not intended to depict a biologically resolved interaction interface but rather to serve as a conceptual illustration. Consistent with this, the model was not used to infer direct physical interactions or binding interfaces. Next, we investigated the effect of SEN177 treatment on the interaction between ΔF508 CFTR and PDIA4. Co-immunoprecipitation and immunofluorescence analyses consistently demonstrated that SEN177 treatment for 24 h significantly weakened the physical association between the two proteins, indicating that SEN177 disrupted their molecular interaction (Fig. 2C, D). To substantiate this finding, we further silenced QPCT expression using shRNA in CFBE41o− cells. Consistent with the pharmacological inhibition, QPCT knockdown also markedly reduced the association between ΔF508 CFTR and PDIA4, confirming that this interaction was regulated by QPCT (Fig. 2E, F). These results suggested that the ΔF508 CFTR–PDIA4 interaction contributed to ER retention of misfolded ΔF508 CFTR, and that disruption of this interaction allowed a fraction of the mutant protein to escape ER quality control and reach the cell surface.

N-terminal pGlu modification of PDIA4 is required for QPCT-mediated rescue of ΔF508 CFTR to the plasma membrane

Proteins in the protein disulfide isomerase (PDI) family are predominantly expressed in the ER, although some family members have been detected in other subcellular compartments41. Due to their localization, all members of this family contain a short NH₂-terminal signal peptide, which is typically 15–30 amino acids in length and is cleaved upon entry into the ER42. Using SignalP 6.0, the signal peptide cleavage site of PDIA4 was predicted to be between positions 24 and 25, with a probability score of 0.9997 (Fig. 3A)43. To experimentally validate this prediction, we overexpressed PDIA4 in CFBE41o- cells using two distinct plasmids, one encoding PDIA4 with a C-terminal FLAG tag and the other with an N-terminal FLAG tag. While FLAG signal was detected only in the C-terminally tagged construct, PDIA4 protein was detected in both constructs, indicating that the N-terminally tagged PDIA4 undergoes N-terminal cleavage (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Subsequently, we overexpressed both a PDIA4 N-terminal deletion mutant (residues 2–24) and the full-length PDIA4, each bearing a C-terminal FLAG tag. Co-localization with the ER was observed only in the full-length group, indicating that the N-terminal region of PDIA4 mediated the targeting of nascent PDIA4 to the ER (Supplementary Fig. 4B). When the N-terminal signal peptide of PDIA4 is removed, the first exposed amino acid is glutamic acid (E), a known substrate for QPCT-mediated cyclization. Based on this observation, we hypothesized that PDIA4 might be a potential substrate of QPCT. To test this hypothesis, we overexpressed PDIA4 with a C-terminal FLAG tag in CFBE41o- cells. At 24 h post-transfection, PDIA4 was enriched using anti-FLAG beads, and the samples were subjected to mass spectrometry to assess the presence of an N-terminal pyroglutamate (pGlu) modification. Mass spectrometric analysis revealed the presence of a pGlu modification at the N-terminus of PDIA4, and the intensity of this modified peptide was decreased by both SEN177 treatment and QPCT knockdown (Fig. 3B, C). To confirm the chemical identity of the observed mass shift, we synthesized a peptide corresponding to the N-terminal sequence of PDIA4 containing a pyroglutamate residue. MS/MS and co-elution analyses were then performed to compare the synthetic peptide with its endogenous counterpart. The MS/MS spectra of the two peptides were highly consistent, and both peptides co-eluted under identical chromatographic conditions, confirming the presence of an N-terminal pGlu modification on PDIA4 (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B).

To further confirm the presence of this modification in vivo and enable its detection, we generated a specific pGlu antibody. The antibody’s specificity was validated by dot blot analysis, which showed selective recognition of a pGlu-modified α-synuclein peptide serving as a positive control44 (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Using this antibody, we detected pGlu modified PDIA4, consistent with N-terminal modification. Notably, treatment with SEN177 or QPCT knockdown both significantly reduced the level of this modification (Fig. 3D, E). These results further supported our belief that QPCT enzymatically regulated the N-terminal pGlu modification of PDIA4. To evaluate whether this modification affected the interaction between PDIA4 and ΔF508 CFTR, we generated a site-directed mutant in which glutamic acid at position 25 was substituted with alanine (E25A). Co-immunoprecipitation analysis showed that the E25A mutation markedly reduced the interaction between PDIA4 and ΔF508 CFTR (Fig. 3F). This weakened association was further supported by immunofluorescence imaging, which revealed reduced colocalization between the E25A mutant and ΔF508 CFTR (Supplementary Fig. 5D). Notably, despite the diminished interaction, immunofluorescence microscopy revealed increased plasma membrane localization of ΔF508 CFTR (Fig. 3G). These results supported a model in which the pGlu-dependent PDIA4–CFTR interaction contributed to ER retention of misfolded CFTR, and disruption of this interaction permitted partial escape from ER quality control and increased CFTR levels at the cell surface.

QPCT catalyzes the formation of pGlu25‑PDIA4 in vitro

The N-terminal pyroglutamate (pGlu) modification is a post-translational modification formed from a glutaminyl residue by the action of glutaminyl cyclase. All members of the PDI family possess a short N-terminal signal sequence (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, we observed a modification at the N-terminus of PDIA4 following signal peptide cleavage, and this modification appeared to be regulated by QPCT. Next, we employed mass spectrometry to assess the formation of the pyroglutamate (pGlu) product. A synthetic PDIA4 peptide (residues 25–40) was used, and the E25A mutant peptide served as a negative control. No pGlu-form product was detected from the E25A peptide regardless of QPCT enzyme addition, validating the specificity and reliability of our detection system (Supplementary Fig. 6A). Further mass spectrometric analysis showed that incubation of the wild-type PDIA4 peptide (residues 25–40) alone for up to 60 min failed to yield spontaneous pGlu formation, indicating that non-enzymatic cyclization didn’t occur under the assay conditions. In contrast, when the fragment was incubated with recombinant QPCT, pGlu-modified PDIA4 (25–40) was detectable as early as 10 min. Full conversion into the pGlu-modified form was observed after 60 min. Importantly, co-incubation with the QPCT inhibitor SEN177 effectively abolished pGlu formation, confirming that the modification was QPCT-dependent (Fig. 4D). These results identified PDIA4 (25–40) as a substrate of QPCT. This finding was consistent with QPCT’s known enzymatic activity toward other physiological substrates, such as Gln79–α-synuclein (Supplementary Fig. 6B), further supporting PDIA4-dervied peptides as substrates for QPCT44.

Then we performed validation experiments using recombinant full-length human PDIA4 fused to a C-terminal His tag, as well as a truncated PDIA4 construct lacking the endogenous signal peptide while retaining Glu25, in a simplified system. When QPCT was co-incubated with PDIA4 for 1 h under in vitro conditions, pGlu modification was readily detected on the truncation PDIA4 lacking the endogenous signal peptide, whereas no pGlu signal was observed on the full-length WT protein. Inclusion of QPCT-specific inhibitor SEN177 in the co-incubation reaction markedly reduced pGlu levels, confirming QPCT dependency (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, mutation of Glu25, the predicted N-terminal pyroglutamylation site of PDIA4, to alanine (E25A) in truncation constructs of PDIA4 lacking the N-terminal signal peptide (ΔSP-PDIA4) completely abolished pGlu formation, demonstrating that this residue is essential for QPCT-catalyzed modification of PDIA4 (Supplementary Fig. 6C). These collective results supported the conclusion that PDIA4 serves as a QPCT substrate.

Discussion

This study revealed a previously unrecognized role for the QPCT–PDIA4 axis in regulating ER quality control of ΔF508 CFTR, the most common disease-causing mutation in cystic fibrosis (CF). QPCT inhibition increased cell surface levels of ΔF508 CFTR and improved ion transport activity in well-differentiated three-dimensional airway epithelial cultures, suggesting that disrupting the ΔF508 CFTR–PDIA4 interaction alleviates ER retention and permits partial escape of misfolded CFTR.

Mechanistically, our results support a model in which QPCT-dependent pyroglutamate (pGlu) modification promotes the association of PDIA4 with misfolded CFTR within the ER quality-control machinery, thereby favoring its retention. Consistent with this model, disruption of the pGlu-dependent interaction reduces ER retention without markedly increasing steady-state levels of mature CFTR. Co-immunoprecipitation and biochemical analyses further support a pGlu-dependent association between PDIA4 and ΔF508 CFTR, indicating that the QPCT–PDIA4 axis acts as a regulatory node influencing the fate of misfolded CFTR. This framework aligns with studies showing that PDIA4 preferentially interacts with CFTR variants that remain responsive to corrector treatment, suggesting engagement at a relatively late stage of CFTR biogenesis. In this context, PDIA4 may associate with partially folded CFTR intermediates to modulate the balance between ER retention and forward trafficking48. Given the topology of CFTR and PDIA4, such regulations likely occur within the ER lumen, for example through transient engagement with luminal loops during folding or through cooperation with ER-resident chaperones, including calnexin/calreticulin and other protein disulfide isomerases. Consistent with this view, ER chaperone systems such as calnexin have been shown to regulate late-stage CFTR assembly and pharmacological responsiveness in a variant-dependent manner, highlighting the broader role of proteostasis networks in shaping CFTR rescue49.

Building on these insights, we next examined whether this pathway extends beyond ΔF508 CFTR. Trikafta (VX-445 + VX-661 + VX-770) is one of the few approved therapies for individuals with the N1303K mutation, particularly in compound heterozygous cases (e.g., ΔF508/N1303K)50. However, treatment options for N1303K remain extremely limited. Prior studies indicated that Trikafta confers modest benefit in compound heterozygotes but shows minimal efficacy in N1303K homozygous or single-mutant contexts, underscoring the need for mutation-specific therapeutic strategies51. In this context, we found that QPCT inhibition increased cell surface expression and functional activity of N1303K in CFF-16HBEge cells. These results suggest that the QPCT–PDIA4 axis extends beyond ΔF508 and functions as a regulatory component of ER quality control across CFTR variants with folding defects. More broadly, modulation of this pathway reduces retention of misfolded CFTR and is associated with improved surface availability and functional of additional CFTR variants.

To assess selectivity, we examined the effect of QPCT inhibition on WT CFTR. In contrast to the pronounced rescue observed for ΔF508 and N1303K variants, QPCT inhibition had minimal impact on WT CFTR expression. These findings indicated that the QPCT–PDIA4 axis preferentially regulates folding-defective CFTR variants. Extending this analysis to the NBD1 mutant A455E revealed a similar, albeit more modest, enhancement of CFTR maturation, supporting a model in which the QPCT–PDIA4 axis modulates proteostasis across variants with differing folding defects (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Although current CFTR modulators, correctors and potentiators have transformed cystic fibrosis therapy, their efficacy remains limited by mutation specificity and incomplete functional restoration52. QPCT inhibition operates through a complementary mechanism by modulating post-translational modifications and proteostasis pathways, thereby promoting stability and membrane localization of CFTR variants refractory to existing modulators. Consistent with this, co-treatment with the QPCT inhibitor SEN177 with VX-445 (Elexacaftor) and VX-661 (Tezacaftor) resulted in higher increase in CFTR maturation compared to either treatment alone, and this effect was observed for both ΔF508 CFTR and N1303K variants. These findings suggest that targeting QPCT could alleviate additional proteostatic constraints and enhance responsiveness to current modulators. Future studies will be required to evaluate the long-term effects of QPCT inhibition, particularly under conditions of chronic inflammation and infection that may influence ER stress responses and proteostasis53–55. QPCT inhibitors have primarily been explored in neurodegenerative diseases, where inhibition of pyroglutamate formation reduces amyloid pathology56. Although SEN177 is a preclinical tool compound and has not been evaluated for clinical use, these findings support that potential of QPCT inhibition, alone or in combination with existing modulators, as a complementary therapeutic strategy for cystic fibrosis57.

Several limitations should be noted. CFTR functional rescue was primarily assessed using the Ussing chamber measurements, and additional orthogonal approaches will be required to further validate these findings. Broader evaluation using multiple QPCT inhibitors, including compounds such as PQ912 (varoglutamstat), will be important to confirm the robustness and specificity of the observed effects. Furthermore, the mechanistic basis of this pathway remains incompletely defined, particularly how PDIA4 modification is regulated, how PDIA4 engages mutant CFTR, and whether this mechanism extends to other misfolded CFTR variants or secretory pathway clients. Addressing these questions will be critical for establishing the QPCT–PDIA4 axis as a broadly applicable strategy for modulating CFTR proteostasis.

In summary, our findings identify QPCT as a modulator of CFTR proteostasis through a pGlu-dependent PDIA4 interaction within the ER quality-control machinery. Inhibition of the QPCT–PDIA4 axis improved processing and function of both ΔF508 and N1303K CFTR, revealing a shared mechanism of rescue. This work expands our understanding of CFTR biosynthetic regulation and highlights modulation of ER retention as a strategy to partially restore mutant CFTR function.

Methods

Ethical

Primary bronchial epithelial cells from patients with cystic fibrosis were obtained from the University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) under protocols approved by the UAB Institutional Review Board (IRB Protocol No. 080625002). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible institutional review board and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection and study involvement.

Cell lines and cell culture

Human cystic fibrosis bronchial epithelial cells (CFBE41o − ) carrying the ΔF508 CFTR mutation and HBE41o− cells harboring a WT CFTR allele were provided by J. Clancy (University of Alabama Birmingham)58. CFF-16HBEge cell lines expressing the N1303K mutation were obtained from Cystic Fibrosis Foundation Therapeutics59. Cells were cultured in Advanced Minimum Essential medium (Gibco, 12492013), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, A5256801), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122), and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco, 35050061) in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were dissociated with TrypLE Express (Gibco, 12605010) and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min before seeding. Medium was changed three times weekly. Primary bronchial epithelial cells from 3 different patients homozygous for the F508del genetic mutation were obtained from the University of Alabama (UAB) and cultured in complete BEGM medium (Lonza, NC9202780) at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for up to three passages, starting with passage 0. HEK293T expressing A455E CFTR were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Gibco, 11965092) which contains 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were treated with 10 μM SEN177 (MedChemExpress, HY-136780) or vehicle control (DMSO) for 24 h. Cultures were functionally analyzed in Ussing chamber or lysed/processed and assayed for CFTR protein levels.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Co-immunoprecipitation was performed using standard protocols18. ~1 × 10⁸ cells were lysed on ice for 20 min in lysis buffer containing 0.5% Igepal CA-630, 50 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 250 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, protease inhibitors, and PhosSTOP (Roche, 04906845001). Lysates were sonicated in a 55 kHz water bath for 3 min and clarified by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was pre-cleared with Sepharose CL-4B beads (Cytiva, 17-0150-01) for 2 h at 4 °C, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with CFTR-specific antibodies (clone 3G11) covalently bound to Protein G-Sepharose (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 101242). Immunoprecipitants were washed three times with lysis buffer and two times with lysis buffer containing no detergent. Bound proteins were eluted twice with SDS loading buffer at 50 °C for 20 min and separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis.

Western blot (WB)

The separated proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane and blotted with appropriate primary and secondary antibodies. The following primary antibodies were used: mouse monoclonal antibodies against CFTR (American Type Culture Collection, Cat# HB-11947, clone 24-1, 1:500), rabbit monoclonal antibodies against GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 2118, clone 14C10, 1:5,000), rabbit polyclonal antibodies against QPCT (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# PA593165, 1:1,000), rabbit polyclonal antibodies against PDIA4 (Proteintech, Cat# 14712-1-AP, 1:1,000), mouse monoclonal antibodies against FLAG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# MA1-91878, clone FG4R, 1:2,000). HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat# 715-035-150, 1:5,000) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat# 711-035-152, 1:5,000) secondary antibodies were used for immunoblotting detection. Signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantification was performed using ImageJ (Fiji), and statistical analysis was conducted with Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). For experiments with variable loading controls, CFTR band C/B ratios were normalized to the corresponding GAPDH signal and subsequently normalized to the DMSO control condition within each experiment. When loading controls were consistent across samples, C/B ratios were presented without additional GAPDH normalization. Uncropped and unprocessed scans of all relevant blots were provided in the Source Data file and referenced in the corresponding figure legends.

Ussing chamber measurements

~1 × 10⁵ cells were seeded onto the apical side of transwell inserts (Corning Life Sciences). Cells were maintained in growth medium (PneumaCult™-Ex Plus Medium, 05040) for 2–3 days at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator to allow attachment and initial proliferation. After this period, the medium from both the apical and basolateral chambers was removed, and 100 μL of differentiation medium (PneumaCult™-ALI Medium, 05001) was added to the apical side, while 500 μL of differential medium was added to the basolateral side. Cells were further incubated for 2–3 days under submerged conditions, after which the apical medium was removed to establish an air–liquid interface (ALI)60. Transepithelial resistance (RT) of the ALI cultures was measured with a Millicell ERS2 Voltohmmeter (Millipore) and was between 200 and 2,700 Ω cm−2. Cells were treated with SEN177 in fresh media for 1 day, using DMSO as vehicle control. Polarized cultures were mounted in EasyMount Ussing chambers (Physiological Instruments), bathed bilaterally with Krebs–Ringer bicarbonate solution (140 Mm Na+, 119.8 mM Cl−, 25 mM HCO3−, 2.8 mM K+, 2.4 mM HPO42−, 0.4 mM PO43−, 1.2 mM Mg+, 1.2 mM Ca+, 5 mM glucose) and the solution saturated with 95% O2, 5% CO2. The epithelial sodium channel was blocked with amiloride (Sigma-Aldrich, A7410). CFTR was stimulated by the addition of forskolin (Sigma-Aldrich, S2449) and genistein (Sigma-Aldrich, G6649) to the apical side of the chamber followed by CFTRinh-172 (MedChemExpress, HY-16671) to isolate the CFTR-specific, apical Cl− current. Measurements were carried out at 37 °C and short circuit current (Isc) was recorded and analyzed with Acquire and Analyze 2.0 (Physiological Instruments). The ΔIsc was calculated as the difference between the maximum Isc value reached upon Forskolin/Genistein stimulation and the Isc value obtained upon addition of the CFTRinh-172. For Ussing chamber experiments, measurements were obtained from primary HBE cultures derived from three independent donors. Multiple recordings were collected for each donor and averaged to obtain a single value per donor prior to statistical analysis (n = 3 biological replicates).

Plasmid construction

Human QPCT shRNA plasmid was constructed by VectorBuilder (Chicago, IL, USA). The shRNA construct was obtained as a custom-designed reagent, and detailed sequence information is available from the manufacturer. To obtain full-length PDIA4 and a C-terminal truncation variant, PCR amplification was performed using appropriate primers and a template containing the PDIA4 sequence. The amplified fragments were then subcloned into the pLenti-P2A-Puro lentiviral gene expression vector between the EcoRI and XhoI restriction sites.

Site-directed mutagenesis

Single point mutations (E25A in PDIA4 and A455E in the CFTR coding sequence) were introduced using PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis. Mutagenic primers containing the desired nucleotide change were manually designed. PCR was carried out with high-fidelity DNA polymerase, followed by DpnI digestion to remove the methylated parental plasmid. The resulting product was transformed into E. coli competent cells, and positive clones were identified by Sanger sequencing.

Lentiviral-mediated knockdown and overexpress of target proteins

Lentiviral-mediated knockdown and overexpression of target proteins were performed using standard protocols61. Lentiviral particles carrying shRNA or cDNA constructs targeting the proteins of interest were generated in HEK293T cells. After 48 h, the culture medium containing lentiviral particles was collected and clarified by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 5 min at room temperature. CFBE41o- cells or primary bronchial epithelial cells were infected with the lentiviral particles for 24 h, followed by selection with puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich, P7255) prior to downstream assays.

Expression and purification of recombinant PDIA4

To generate recombinant PDIA4 proteins carrying a C-terminal His tag, the coding sequences of human PDIA4 for wild-type (WT), truncation (excluding the endogenous signal peptide), and E25A mutant were subcloned into the pET-SUMO vector (Addgene, 111560). The SUMO fusion tag was included to enhance protein solubility and allow removal via Ulp1-mediated cleavage, yielding PDIA4 with a native N-terminus. The plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells, and protein expression was induced with 1 mM IPTG. After 2 h of incubation at 37 °C, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 10 min and stored at −80 °C overnight prior to purification. All purification steps were performed on ice or at 4 °C. Frozen cell pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) and 100 mM NaCl and disrupted by sonication. After centrifugation, the supernatant was incubated with HisPur™ Ni-NTA Resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 88221) overnight. The column was washed twice with buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl) to remove nonspecific proteins, and the bound protein was eluted with imidazole-containing buffer. The SUMO tag was cleaved by Ulp1 protease (Yeasen, 20410ES60), and the mixture was subjected to a second Ni–NTA purification to separate the tag-free PDIA4-His protein. Finally, the purified protein was concentrated, dialyzed into storage buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl), and stored at −80 °C for subsequent use.

Affinity purification-mass spectrometry (AP-MS)

Cells were harvested and lysed in 1% NP-40 buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% protease inhibitor cocktail, 1% NP-40) at 4 °C. Cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 14,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were incubated with anti-FLAG M2 affinity resin (Sigma-Aldrich, M8823) at 4 °C for 4 h with gentle rotation. After incubation, beads were washed extensively with 1% NP-40 buffer to remove nonspecific proteins. The FLAG beads were resuspended in 8 M urea (Sigma-Aldrich, U5128) in 100 mM TEAB (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 90114) and then incubated with 5 mM TCEP (Sigma-Aldrich, C4706) at room temperature for 30 min followed by 10 mM IAA (Sigma-Aldrich, I1149) with vortex at room temperature for 15 min in the dark. 100 mM TEAB was added to reduce the urea concentration to 2 M. Then 1 mM CaCl2 and 1 μg trypsin (Promega, V5113) were added for overnight digestion at 37 ˚C. The resulting peptides were acidified with 5% formic acid, desalted, and saved for further mass spectrometry analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Samples were loaded onto EvoTips (Evosep) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol and were analyzed by liquid chromatography tandem mass-spectrometry using an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an Evosep One system (Evosep). Peptides were separated on a C18 analytical column (100 μm × 25 cm, 5 μm tip) packed with 1.7 μm BEH beads (Waters) using a gradient of buffer A (0.1% formic acid in water) and buffer B (90% acetonitrile). Liquid chromatography separation was performed using an SPD method (15 samples per day). The voltage applied to nanoelectron spray ionization (nESI) source was 2.5 kV and the source temperature was 275 °C. Peptide spectra were acquired in the data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. Briefly, Full MS1 scans were collected in the Orbitrap at resolution 120,000. The cycle time was set to 3 s, and within these 3 s, the most abundant ions per scan were selected for collision-induced dissociation MS2 in Orbitrap. Monoisotopic precursor selection was enabled, and dynamic exclusion was used with an exclusion duration of 10 s.

MS data analysis

Protein and peptide identification was done with Integrated Proteomics Pipeline (IP2, Bruker Scientific LLC). MS data were searched using the ProLuCID algorithm against a sequence database of UniProt SwissProt human reviewed proteome appended with the sequences of common contaminant proteins, and with the reverse sequences as decoy. MS/MS spectra were searched using the following parameters: static modification, carbamidomethylation ( + 57.02146 Da, C); dynamic modifications, pyroglutamate formation ( − 17.02 Da on N-terminal Q or −18.01 Da on N-terminal E) and oxidation ( + 15.995 Da, M); precursor mass tolerance, 10 ppm; fragment ion mass tolerance, 0.6 Da; enzyme specificity, semi-trypsin (K/R) with up to two missed cleavages; and a minimum peptide length of four residues. Protein identification required that at least 1 peptide was identified per protein, and the data was filtered to a peptide and protein false discovery rate (FDR) less than 0.01 using the target-decoy method. Protein identification was required at least one unique peptide. Peptide quantification was performed using label-free quantification in MaxQuant software (version 2.1.0.0). The search parameters were consistent with those used in the IP2 analysis. First-search and main-search peptide mass tolerances were set to 20 ppm and 4.5 ppm, respectively. The MS/MS match tolerance was set to 0.5 Da for ITMS and 20 ppm for FTMS. The “Match between runs” option was enabled, with a match time window of 2 min. The false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled using a decoy database and was maintained below 1%.

Dot blot

Samples were directly spotted onto PVDF membranes and air-dried at room temperature. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h to prevent non-specific binding, followed by incubation with the appropriate primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Detection was carried out using standard Western blotting procedures, including incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and chemiluminescent substrate development.

Immunofluorescence

CFBE41o− cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, washed three times with PBS, and then incubated with 5.0 µg/mL WGA-Alexa Fluor 594 solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# W11262, 1:200) for 10 min at 37 °C. Cells were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, blocked with 3% BSA in 1 × PBS for 1 h at room temperature, and incubated with the following antibodies at 4 °C overnight: mouse monoclonal anti-CFTR (Millipore, Cat# 05-583, clone M3A7, 1:200), rabbit polyclonal anti-PDIA4 (Enzo Life Sciences, Cat# ADI-SPS-720, 1:200). After washing, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG and/or goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A-11001 and Cat# A-11008, respectively; 1:500) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen, Cat# D1306). Photographs of cells mounted in ProLong Gold antifade reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# P36930) were taken with a laser scanning confocal microscope LSM 710 (Zeiss).

pGlu antibody generation

The hapten was activated using EDC/NHS (1:1.2) in a water/DMSO (1:1) solution at a concentration of 1 mg/100 µL. After 15 min at room temperature, the activated hapten was added to BSA or KLH in PBS (1 mg protein per 1 mg hapten). The reaction mixture was rotated at room temperature for 2 h and then purified by dialysis. The conjugation ratio ( ~ 20) was confirmed by MALDI-TOF (Bruker). The resulting conjugate was used for immunization. Mice were immunized intraperitoneally with 30–50 µg of KLH-conjugated antigen mixed with Sigma Adjuvant System. Injections were administered in weeks 0 and 3, with an optional third dose in week 6. Blood was collected at weeks 4 and 7 for ELISA to assess antibody titters62. BSA-conjugated antigen ( ≥ 2 mg) was used for initial ELISA screening of hybridomas, and linker-BSA ( ≥ 0.5 mg) was used to exclude non-specific clones. The resulting antiserum was affinity-purified and characterized as antigen specificity. The affinity-purified in-house anti-pGlu monoclonal antibody was used at a dilution of 1:1,000 for immunoblotting analyses.

Enzymatic activity assay

The kinetics of QPCT-catalyzed pGlu-PDIA4 (25-40) formation were measured using a continuously coupled spectrophotometric assay. The reaction was carried out in 50 mM Tris/HCl buffer at pH 8.0, employing glutamate dehydrogenase as an auxiliary enzyme to monitor the release of glutamate63. Kinetic parameters, including Km and Vmax, were derived by fitting the experimental data to the Michaelis–Menten equation via nonlinear regression analysis in accordance with established methodologies64.

Detection of pyroglutamate (pGlu) product formation

The analysis was performed on an LC-MS consisting of a Waters I-Class LC and a Waters SQD2 single-quadrupole MS (Waters Corporation) with a Waters Cortecs C18 column (1.6 um, 2.1 × 55 mm) (Waters Corporation) using a 5–99% B gradient lasting 2.5 min (A: 0.1% aqueous formic acid, B: 0.06% formic acid in acetonitrile, 0.8 mL/min flow rate, 35 °C column temperature). Product purity was quantified using combined UV absorbance signals at 220 nm and 260 nm, and product identity was confirmed by mass spectrometry.

AlphaFold2 prediction of PDIA4 and ΔF508 CFTR interaction

The potential interaction between PDIA4 and ΔF508 CFTR was predicted using AlphaFold2 in multimer mode40. The structures of PDIA4 and ΔF508 CFTR were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB IDs: 3IDV and 8EJ1, respectively). The corresponding UniProt identifiers are P13667 (PDIA4) and P13569 (CFTR). AlphaFold-Multimer was run with default settings, and the top-ranked model was selected as the most accurate model. The predicted complex was visualized using PyMOL version 2.5 (Schrödinger, LLC), and interface residues were identified based on a distance cutoff of 5 Å. Model confidence was evaluated using predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT), interface predicted TM-score (ipTM) generated by AlphaFold65,66.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10 or Microsoft Office Excel. Data were presented as mean values with error bars representing the standard error of the mean (SEM), calculated from independent biological replicates, with the sample size (n) specified in the figure legends unless otherwise noted. For microscopy-based analyses, fluorescence intensity and/or co-localization were quantified using ImageJ, as indicated in each figure. Co-localization was assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
