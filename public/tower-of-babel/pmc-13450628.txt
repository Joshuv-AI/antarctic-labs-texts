Repeated Spontaneous Transurethral Passage of Urinary Fungal Masses Associated with Candida tropicalis Candiduria in a Diabetic Patient with Multiple Urinary Calculi: A Case Report
Authors: Shaowei Niu, Ting Li, Fengjun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeRepeated spontaneous transurethral passage of visible urinary fungal masses containing Candida organisms is rare. We report a case associated with Candida tropicalis (C. tropicalis) candiduria in a patient with poorly controlled diabetes mellitus and multiple urinary calculi.CaseA 55-year-old man with poorly controlled diabetes mellitus, urinary calculi, urinary retention, bilateral hydronephrosis, and recent cystolitholapaxy presented with dysuria, pyuria, and repeated passage of yellow-white soft masses during urination. Cystoscopy and computed tomography showed intravesical lesions accompanied by bladder inflammation. Histopathological examination of the expelled masses demonstrated abundant Candida organisms within necroinflammatory debris, whereas urine culture yielded fluconazole-susceptible C. tropicalis. Passage of the fungal masses, dysuria, and pyuria persisted during the early course of fluconazole treatment. After adjunctive caspofungin was added together with intensified glycemic control, urinary drainage, daily bladder irrigation, and local perineal care, the frequency and volume of the expelled masses decreased, and the urinary symptoms and pyuria improved.ConclusionVisible urinary fungal masses in patients with candiduria should undergo histopathological examination, while urine and, when feasible, the expelled material should be submitted for fungal culture and species identification. Management of complicated candiduria should combine appropriate antifungal therapy with urinary drainage, glycemic control, bladder irrigation, and other source-control measures.

Introduction

Fungal urinary tract infections are uncommon but clinically important, particularly in patients with diabetes mellitus, urinary stasis, urinary tract instrumentation, urinary calculi, antimicrobial exposure, or structural urinary tract abnormalities.1–3 Candida species are the predominant fungal pathogens associated with urinary tract infections and may form organized intraluminal aggregates commonly described as fungal masses, fungal balls, or fungal bezoars.1–5 These aggregates may consist of fungal elements, necrotic tissue, inflammatory debris, mucus, and urinary sediment and can lead to persistent infection or urinary tract obstruction.3–5

Most reported urinary fungal balls present as obstructive intraluminal lesions identified by imaging, endoscopy, or surgery.4–13 Although spontaneous passage of fungal material in the urine has occasionally been reported, repeated transurethral passage of multiple grossly visible fungal masses during micturition has been only sporadically described.14,15 Here, we report a patient with diabetes mellitus, urinary retention, multiple urinary calculi, and bilateral upper urinary tract dilatation who repeatedly passed yellow-white urinary fungal masses. Histopathological examination of the expelled material demonstrated abundant Candida organisms within inflammatory and necrotic debris, whereas urine culture yielded Candida tropicalis (C. tropicalis).

Case Presentation

A 55-year-old man from Sichuan Province, China, was admitted on April 14, 2026, with urinary frequency and urgency lasting for more than two months. His symptoms had worsened during the preceding ten days and included dysuria, pyuria, lower abdominal discomfort, and repeated passage of yellow-white soft masses during urination. He reported passing peanut-sized soft material approximately seven to eight times per day. His medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension treated with amlodipine, bilateral renal stone surgery more than seven years previously, and cystolitholapaxy for bladder calculi on February 25, 2026. Before referral, abdominal ultrasonography showed a right renal cyst, bilateral renal calculi, bilateral upper ureteral dilatation, bilateral hydronephrosis, bladder calculi, urinary retention with a postvoid residual urine volume of 334 mL, and prostatic enlargement. He denied fever, gross hematuria, tuberculosis, or the use of immunosuppressive therapy.

On admission, his temperature was 36.2°C, heart rate was 96 beats/min, respiratory rate was 21 breaths/min, and blood pressure was 123/83 mmHg. Abdominal and external genital examinations revealed no remarkable abnormalities. Glycemic control was poor, with an HbA1c (glycated hemoglobin) level of 9.08%. The procalcitonin level was 0.057 ng/mL. On April 21, the serum creatinine level was 81.1 μmol/L, and the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was 86.5 mL/min/1.73 m2.

Urinalysis showed persistent pyuria and hematuria. On April 23, the urine white blood cell count was 6020/μL, the red blood cell count was 446/μL, urinary protein was 2+, occult blood was 3+, and leukocyte esterase was 500 Leu/μL. Cystoscopy performed before referral on April 13 showed multiple soft, mobile white lesions within the bladder, accompanied by redness and swelling of the bladder wall. The expelled masses were submitted for histopathological examination, fluorescence staining, and direct microscopic examination. Hematoxylin and eosin staining demonstrated abundant Candida organisms embedded within inflammatory and necrotic debris and amorphous material, findings consistent with a urinary fungal mass (Figure 3). Fluorescence staining and direct microscopy of the expelled material further demonstrated numerous fungal structures (Figure 4). A clean-catch midstream urine specimen collected on April 15 yielded C. tropicalis when the culture result became available on April 19. Antifungal susceptibility testing of the urine isolate showed minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) of ≤0.5 μg/mL for fluconazole and ≤0.12 μg/mL for caspofungin. Intravenous fluconazole at 200 mg once daily was initiated on April 20 after the urine culture and susceptibility results became available. Dysuria, pyuria, and passage of the fungal masses persisted during the early treatment period; therefore, intravenous caspofungin at 50 mg once daily was added on April 23. Additional management included intensified glycemic control, urinary drainage with an indwelling catheter, catheter care, daily bladder irrigation, and local perineal care. The frequency and volume of the expelled masses subsequently decreased, and the lower urinary tract symptoms improved. Repeat urinalysis on April 26 showed a decrease in the urine white blood cell count to 500/μL. The patient continued to show clinical improvement during hospitalization while receiving antifungal therapy and source-control measures.

Discussion

The clinical importance of this case lies not merely in the presence of candiduria but in the repeated spontaneous transurethral passage of multiple grossly visible urinary fungal masses. Most previously reported urinary fungal balls have presented as obstructive intraluminal lesions detected by imaging, endoscopy, or surgery, often in association with urinary retention, hydronephrosis, anuria, renal dysfunction, emphysematous cystitis, or bladder rupture.4,6–13 In contrast, the expelled material in the present case provided direct specimens for histopathological examination, fluorescence staining, and direct microscopy. These examinations demonstrated abundant Candida organisms within inflammatory and necrotic debris, while urine culture yielded C. tropicalis. This presentation broadens the recognizable clinical spectrum of urinary fungal mass disease and highlights the importance of examining yellow-white urinary material rather than assuming that it represents calculi, mucus, blood clots, or nonspecific necrotic debris.

Several published cases of urinary fungal balls have focused on urinary obstruction, emphysematous cystitis, renal failure, anuria, urinary retention, or bladder rupture.6–13 Previous bladder fungal balls were generally detected as intravesical masses and frequently required endoscopic or surgical management.7,8,10 In contrast, the distinctive feature of the present case was the repeated spontaneous passage of multiple grossly visible yellow-white masses during micturition. Although spontaneous passage of fungal material has occasionally been reported, repeated passage of numerous visible masses appears to be uncommon.14,15

The histopathological finding of abundant Candida organisms embedded within inflammatory and necrotic debris is consistent with an organized urinary fungal aggregate rather than planktonic candiduria alone. Urinary fungal balls have been described as conglomerates of fungal elements, host cellular debris, inflammatory material, mucus, and urinary sediment.3–5 Several factors present in this patient—including poor glycemic control, urinary retention, multiple urinary calculi, recent urinary tract instrumentation, bladder inflammation, and bilateral upper urinary tract dilatation—are recognized conditions associated with candiduria, fungal persistence, and urinary fungal ball formation.1–5 The biofilm-forming capacity of C. tropicalis may also have contributed to fungal adherence and persistence.16 We speculate that urinary stasis, intravesical inflammation, and mechanical disturbance during micturition facilitated the formation and subsequent detachment of the aggregates.

The limited early clinical response to fluconazole despite in vitro susceptibility should be interpreted cautiously. Fluconazole remains the preferred treatment for urinary tract infection caused by susceptible Candida species because it achieves high urinary concentrations.1,2 However, organized fungal aggregates may impede drug penetration and may exhibit some characteristics of biofilm-associated infection. C. tropicalis is capable of biofilm formation, and biofilm-associated cells may be less susceptible to antifungal agents than planktonic cells evaluated by routine susceptibility testing.16 Nevertheless, because C. tropicalis was isolated from urine rather than directly from the expelled masses, the role of species-specific biofilm formation in this patient remains inferential.

Clinical improvement occurred after caspofungin was added to fluconazole together with multiple source-control measures. This temporal association should not be interpreted as evidence that caspofungin alone was responsible for the response, because echinocandins achieve low urinary concentrations and are not routinely recommended as first-line therapy for candiduria.1 Limited clinical reports have suggested a possible role for caspofungin in selected complicated urinary infections caused by Candida species, including non-albicans species.17 In the present patient, improvement most likely reflected the combined effects of fluconazole, adjunctive caspofungin, reduction of the fungal burden, urinary drainage, daily bladder irrigation, glycemic control, and local care. The relative contribution of each intervention cannot be determined.

This report has several limitations. First, the expelled masses were not submitted for fungal culture or molecular species identification. Although histopathology demonstrated abundant Candida organisms within the masses and urine culture yielded C. tropicalis, direct species-level attribution of the masses to C. tropicalis could not be established. Second, long-term post-treatment follow-up was unavailable at the time of manuscript preparation; therefore, durable microbiological clearance, radiological resolution, and recurrence could not be assessed. Third, because several therapeutic and source-control interventions were implemented concurrently, the contribution of each component could not be separated. Nevertheless, this case highlights an unusual but recognizable presentation of urinary fungal mass disease and emphasizes the importance of combining antifungal treatment with urinary drainage, glycemic control, and other source-control measures in complicated candiduria.

Conclusion

Repeated spontaneous transurethral passage of urinary fungal masses associated with C. tropicalis candiduria is a rare but clinically distinctive manifestation of urinary fungal aggregate disease. In patients with diabetes mellitus, urinary calculi, urinary stasis, pyuria, and candiduria, yellow-white soft urinary masses should be submitted for histopathological examination, fungal staining, and direct microscopy; when feasible, the expelled material should also undergo fungal culture and species identification. Management should be individualized and combine appropriate antifungal therapy with glycemic control, urinary drainage, bladder irrigation, and other source-control measures.
