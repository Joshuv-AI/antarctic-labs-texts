1.
Counting points on algebraic varieties over finite fields

For an algebraic variety
X
over a finite field
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
there exist non-negative integers
m,k
and algebraic numbers
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}\in\overline{{\mathbb{Q}}}
such that numbers of points on
X
over all field extensions are given by

(1)

\#X({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{s}})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}^{s}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{j}^{s}\quad\text{ for all }\quad s\geq 1.

We will refer to
\alpha_{i}
’s and
\beta_{j}
’s as the
Frobenius roots
of
X
. Their existence was proved by Bernard Dwork circa 1960 using
p
-adic methods. The natural building blocks in Dwork’s approach were hypersurfaces. We are going to elaborate explicit
p
-adic formulas for Frobenius roots of hypersurfaces and explore related algebraic constructions.

1.1.
Affine and projective algebraic varieties

An
affine algebraic variety

X
over a field
k
is a common zero locus of a collection of multivariate polynomials
f_{1}({\bf x}),\ldots,f_{m}({\bf x})\in k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. Sets of points of
X
over any field extension
K\supseteq k
are defined as

X(K)=\{{\bf x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in K^{n}:f_{1}({\bf x})=\ldots=f_{m}({\bf x})=0\}.

If there are no equations (
m=0
) we denote
X=\mathbb{A}^{n}
, the
n
-dimensional
affine space
. In this case one simply has
\mathbb{A}^{n}(K)=K^{n}
.

More general algebraic varieties are glued of affine pieces. A straightforward example of such a glueing is given by projective spaces and projective varieties. The
n
-dimensional
projective space

\mathbb{P}^{n}
is the variety of lines through the origin in
\mathbb{A}^{n+1}
. Its sets of points are given by

\mathbb{P}^{n}(K)=(K^{n+1}\setminus\{{\bf 0}\})/K^{\times}=\{(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\in K^{n+1}:\exists i\quad x_{i}\neq 0\}/\sim,

where
\sim
denotes the homothety equivalence
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\sim(cx_{0},\ldots,cx_{n})
for all
c\in K^{\times}
. The respective equivalence class is denoted by
[x_{0}:\ldots:x_{n}]
. These brackets are called the homogeneous coordinates on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. The
n
-dimensional projective space can be covered by
n+1
affine spaces

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}=\cup_{i=0}^{n}U_{i},\qquad U_{i}=\{[x_{0}:\ldots:x_{n}]:x_{i}\neq 0\}

\displaystyle\cong\mathbb{A}^{n},

\displaystyle[x_{0}:\ldots:x_{n}]

\displaystyle\mapsto(\frac{x_{0}}{x_{i}},\ldots,\frac{x_{i-1}}{x_{i}},\frac{x_{i+1}}{x_{i}},\ldots,\frac{x_{n}}{x_{i}}).

A
projective variety

X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a common zero locus of a collection of homogeneous polynomials
F_{1}({\bf x}),\ldots,F_{m}({\bf x})\in k[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
. It is a union
X=\cup_{i=0}^{n}X_{i}
of affine varieties
X_{i}=X\cap U_{i}
. Here
X_{i}
can be identified with the zero locus of polynomials in
n
variables given by

f_{j}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})=F_{j}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{i-1},1,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})
for
j=1,\ldots,m
.

Let
a,b,c\in k
. The projective cubic curve

x_{0}x_{1}^{2}=x_{2}^{3}+ax_{0}x_{2}^{2}+bx_{0}^{2}x_{2}+cx_{0}^{3}

is given in the affine sheet
U_{0}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
by the equation

in coordinates
(x,y)=(\frac{x_{2}}{x_{0}},\frac{x_{1}}{x_{0}})
. Check that the only point on this curve in

is

. Look up the definition of non-singular algebraic varieties and check that this curve is non-singular whenever the polynomial
x^{3}+ax^{2}+bx+c
has no multiple roots. If is the case, this curve is called an elliptic curve.

Non-singular cubic curves with a specified point
O
defined over
k
are called
elliptic curves
over
k
. When the characteristic of
k
is not equal to
2
or
3
such a curve can be always given by an equation as above in a suitably chosen system of coordinates.

1.2.
Weil conjectures

Let
p
be a prime number and
X
be an algebraic variety over the finite field with
p
elements
k={\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.
The natural generating series for the numbers of points
\#X({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{s}})
turns out to be

\mathcal{Z}_{X}(T)=\exp\left(\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\#X({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{s}})\frac{T^{s}}{s}\right)\in{\mathbb{Q}}\llbracket T\rrbracket.

This formal series is called the
local zeta function
of
X
. The naturality of the above definition of a zeta function is explained by the following fact.

Theorem 2

(Dwork,
[
13
]
)
.

The local zeta function of an algebraic variety
1
1

1

In this theorem algebraic varieties are assumed to be of
finite type
, which means they can be covered by finite number of affine pieces. It is a standard assumption in the definition of varieties.

X
over
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
is rational:

\mathcal{Z}_{X}(T)\in{\mathbb{Q}}(T).

Writing this rational function as
\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1-\beta_{j}T)/\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1-\alpha_{i}T)
we obtain expression (
1
) for the numbers of points on
X
over the extensions of
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.

The statement of Theorem
2
was the first one on the list of properties of local zeta functions which were conjectured by André Weil in 1940s. The rest of Weil’s conjectures concerns non-singular projective varieties
X
. If
X
is such a variety of dimension
n
then

\mathcal{Z}_{X}(T)=\prod_{i=0}^{2n}P_{i}(T)^{(-1)^{i-1}}=\frac{P_{1}(T)\ldots P_{2n-1}(T)}{P_{0}(T)\ldots P_{2n}(T)}

where
P_{i}\in 1+T{\mathbb{Z}}[T]
are integral polynomials with the following properties:

(RH)

P_{0}(T)=1-T
,
P_{2n}(T)=1-p^{n}T
and for
1\leq i\leq 2n-1
polynomials
P_{i}(T)
factor over
{\mathbb{C}}
as
P_{i}(T)=\prod_{j=1}^{\beta_{i}}(1-\alpha_{i,j}T)
with
\alpha_{i,j}\in\overline{{\mathbb{Q}}}
satisfying
|\alpha_{ij}|=p^{i/2}
.

(FE)

\mathcal{Z}_{X}(\frac{1}{p^{n}T})=\pm(p^{n/2}T)^{\chi(X)}\mathcal{Z}_{X}(T)
where
\chi(X)
is the Euler characteristic of
X
; in particular, for every
\alpha_{i,j}
we have that
p^{n}/\alpha_{i,j}
is a reciprocal root of
P_{2n-i}(T)
.

(TOP)

If
X
can be lifted to a smooth variety defined over a number field then
\deg P_{i}(T)
equals to the
i
th Betti number of the topological space of complex points of this variety.

Everything which was said above also holds true for varieties over finite fields
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
where
q
is a power of
p
, and one should substitute
p
with
q
throughout the statement of the Weil conjectures. We restrict to the case of
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
for brevity. These conjectures were proved in 1960s and 1970s leading to the creation of
p
-adic and étale cohomology theories, with contributions by Bernard Dwork, Alexander Grothendieck, Michael Artin, Pierre Deligne and many other mathematicians.

Exercise 4
.

Consider a non-singular projective conic (that is, a curve of degree two)
C\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
. Assuming
C
has a point over
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
, show that its zeta function is given by
\frac{1}{(1-T)(1-pT)}
.

For this purpose you may fill the details in the following argument over a field
K={\mathbb{F}}_{p^{s}}
: every line through our specified point (defined over
k={\mathbb{F}}_{p}
) will have one other point of intersection with the conic; the line is defined over
K
if and only if this other point is defined over
K
; hence
\#C(K)=\#{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(K)=p^{s}+1
.

Computation of zeta functions of cubic curves is more sophisticated. The zeta function of an elliptic curve
E\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
defined over
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
is given by

(2)

\mathcal{Z}_{E}(T)=\frac{1-a_{p}T+pT^{2}}{(1-T)(1-pT)},

where the number
a_{p}=p+1-\#E({\mathbb{F}}_{p})\in{\mathbb{Z}}
is called the
Frobenius trace
of
E
. Helmut Hasse proved that
|a_{p}|\leq 2\sqrt{p}
. Note that this implies that the discriminant of the quadratic polynomial in the denominator is
\leq 0
. If the discriminant could be
0
we would have
a_{p}=2\sqrt{p}\notin{\mathbb{Z}}
. Hence the two reciprocal roots are complex conjugate and have absolute value
\sqrt{p}
in agreement with (RH).
The Sato–Tate conjecture is a statement about the distribution of numbers
a_{p}/(2\sqrt{p})
in the interval
[-1,1]
when
p
varies and
E
is an elliptic curve without complex multiplication defined over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. This conjecture was proved by Laurent Clozel, Michael Harris, Nicholas Shepherd-Barron, and Richard Taylor circa 2008 under mild assumptions. The proof was completed by Thomas Barnet-Lamb, David Geraghty, Harris, and Taylor in 2011. Several generalizations to other algebraic varieties are open. We wanted to mention this to motivate our interest in the knowledge of Frobenius roots.

1.3.
The role of hypersurfaces in proving rationality of local zeta functions

Dwork’s proof of Theorem
2
used
p
-adic analysis. The building blocks of this proof are algebraic varieties given by one equation. Such varieties are called
hypersurfaces
.

Proposition 5
.

It suffices to prove Theorem
2
for affine hypersurfaces.

Proof.

An algebraic variety can be covered by a finite number of affine pieces whose intersections are also affine.
2
2

2

An algebraic variety
X
is of finite type if it can be covered by a finite number of affine pieces. Under an additional assumption that
X
is separated, this covering can be refined to satisfy the property that intersections are also affine. Here we give a proof for separated varieties. Without this assumption one needs to proceed in two steps. First, we deduce rationality of zeta functions for separated varieties. Second, we note that intersections of affine varieties are separated and apply the inclusion-exclusion argument using separated varieties instead of hypersurfaces.
If
X=\cup_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}
is such a covering then the inclusion-exclusion principle reduces the count of points on
X
to the count of points on its affine pieces:

(3)

\#(\cup_{i=1}^{m}X_{i})({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{s}})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}(-1)^{k-1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\leq\ldots\leq i_{k}\leq m}\#(X_{i_{1}}\cap\ldots\cap X_{i_{k}})({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{s}}).

Now let
X
be an affine variety of common zeroes of polynomials
f_{1}({\bf x}),\ldots,f_{m}({\bf x})\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. For a polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
we denote the hypersurface of its zeroes by

X_{f}=\{{\bf x}:f({\bf x})=0\}.

Then
X=X_{f_{1}}\cap X_{f_{2}}\cap\ldots\cap X_{f_{m}}
. We also note that the union of hypersurfaces is again a hypersurface:
X_{f_{1}}\cup X_{f_{2}}\cup\ldots\cup X_{f_{m}}=X_{f_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot f_{m}}
. Let us apply inclusion-exclusion formula (
3
) with
X_{i}=X_{f_{i}}
. Since in the left-hand side of (
3
) we have a hypersurface, this formula allows to do induction on the number
m
of equations. When
m=2
one has

\#(X_{f_{1}\cdot f_{2}})({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{s}})=\#X_{f_{1}}({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{s}})+\#X_{f_{1}}({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{s}})-\#(X_{f_{1}}\cap X_{f_{2}})({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{s}}),

which expresses the zeta function of an intersection of two hypersurfaces as a ratio of zeta functions of single hypersurfaces. Similarly, formula (
3
) expresses zeta function of an intersection of
m
hypersurfaces (the last term in the right-hand side) as a ratio of zeta functions of varieties given by less than
m
equations.
∎

In the above proof one can also see that having an effective method of computing Frobenius roots for hypersurfaces would be sufficient, as Frobenius roots of general varieties occur among the roots of their affine pieces. Of course some cancellations would happen in the multiplication and division of zeta functions of pieces.

1.4.
Weil cohomology and de Rham cohomology

André Weil suggested that his conjectures would follow from the existence of a suitable cohomology theory for varieties over finite fields, similar to the usual (singular) cohomology for complex varieties. For a variety
X
over
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
there is the
p
th power Frobenius map on the set
X(\overline{{\mathbb{F}}}_{p})
of its points defined over all extensions of the ground field. Here
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}}_{p}
denotes the algebraic closure of
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
. The Frobenius map is expressed locally as raising coordinates of points to the
p
th power:
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto(x_{1}^{p},\ldots,x_{n}^{p})
. Since
{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{s}}\subset\overline{{\mathbb{F}}}_{p}
can be identified as the set of solutions to the eqiation
x^{p^{s}}=x
, we see that
X({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{s}})\subset X(\overline{{\mathbb{F}}}_{p})
is the set of fixed points of the
s
th power of the Frobenius map for every
s\geq 1
. In algebraic topology the number of fixed points of a continuous map can be worked out using the Lefschetz fixed-point theorem, given as an alternating sum of traces of the induced map on its cohomology groups. Weil anticipated existence of a cohomology theory which would asoociate to a variety over a finite field vector spaces over a field
F
of characteristic zero (cohomology groups) along with a canonical linear map on them, traces of powers of which would count points over the extensions of the ground field. Such a map would be also called the Frobenius map, and we called the
\alpha_{i}
’s and
\beta_{j}
’s in formula (
1
) the Frobenius roots referring to this hypothetical Frobenius map. One could also call them the Frobenius eigenvalues, or simply the reciprocal roots of the zeta function.

As was mentioned in §
1.2
, such cohomology theories were constructed in subsequent decades with coefficients in
F={\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
,
\ell\neq p
(
\ell
-adic or étale cohomology)
3
3

3

There is also étale cohomology with
\ell=p
.
and
F={\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
(
p
-adic or rigid cohomology).

The advantage of
p
-adic cohomology theory is that it allows to determine Frobenius maps explicitly. For non-singular affine and projective varieties
X
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
for almost all primes
p
the
p
-adic cohomology groups (of the reduction of
X
modulo
p
) are isomorphic to
H^{i}_{dR}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
, where
H^{i}_{dR}
is the
i
th algebraic de Rham cohomology group. Roughly, the de Rham cohomology groups of an affine variety are given by

H^{i}_{dR}(X,{\mathbb{Q}})=\frac{\text{ closed differential $i$-forms on $X$}}{\text{ exact differential $i$-forms on $X$}}

with
0\leq i\leq\dim X
. Thus the Frobenius map is a
p
-adic operation on equivalence classes of differential forms which is responsible for counting points on
X
over all
{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{s}}
. In §
3
we will construct such an operation explicitly in the case of affine hypersurfaces.

Congruences

2.1.
Warming up with
p
-adic numbers

Lemma 6
.

Let
a,b\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
s\geq 1
. If
a\equiv b({\rm mod}\ p^{s})
then
a^{p}\equiv b^{p}({\rm mod}\ p^{s+1})
.

Proof.

Let us write
a=b+cp^{s}
and raise to the
p
th power:

a^{p}=(b+cp^{s})^{p}=b^{p}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\binom{p}{i}b^{p-i}c^{i}p^{si}\equiv b^{p}({\rm mod}\ p^{s+1})

because for
i=1
we have
\binom{p}{1}p^{s}=p^{1+s}
and terms with higher
i
are divisible by
p^{is}
with
is\geq 2s\geq s+1
.
∎

For
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, let us start with the known congruence
a^{p}\equiv a({\rm mod}\ p)
and raise it to
p
th power multiple times. The above lemma yields

a^{p^{s}}\equiv a^{p^{s-1}}({\rm mod}\ p^{s})\text{ for }s\geq 1.

It follows that the sequence
a^{p^{s}}
has a limit in the ring of
p
-adic integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
.

2.2.
Lifting Hasse–Witt matrices

Our main object is a multivariable Laurent polynomial

f({\bf x})\in R[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]

with coefficients in a commutative characteristic zero ring
R
. About this ring we will assume that

\cap_{s\geq 1}\;p^{s}R=\{0\}.

In particular, this defines the
p
-adic topology on
R
(two elements are close when their difference belongs to
p^{s}R
for a high
s
) and one can embed
R
into its
p
-adic completion

\widehat{R}=\underset{\leftarrow}{\lim}\;R/p^{s}R.

The
Newton polytope
of
f({\bf x})
is the convex hull in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of the set of exponent vectors of monomials occuring in
f({\bf x})
. It is denoted by
\Delta=\Delta(f)
. We shall use the notation
{\bf x}^{{\bf u}}=x_{1}^{u_{1}}\ldots x_{n}^{u_{n}}
for
{\bf u}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. One can then write
f=\sum_{{\bf u}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}f_{{\bf u}}{\bf x}^{\bf u}
with coefficients
a_{\bf u}\in R
. The support
{\rm supp}(f)=\{{\bf u}:f_{\bf u}\neq 0\}
is a finite subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, and the Newton polytope
\Delta\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is precisely the convex hull of
{\rm supp}(f)
.

Let
\Delta^{\circ}
denote the interior of
\Delta
in the Euclidean topology and

\Delta^{\circ}_{\mathbb{Z}}=\Delta^{\circ}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}

be the set of internal integral points of the Newton polytope.

Example 9
.

Here we see the Newton polytope
\Delta
of the two variable
Laurent polynomial
f({\bf x})=x_{1}+x_{2}+\frac{1}{x_{1}x_{2}}
. Any other polynomial of the form

\displaystyle f({\bf x})=f_{(1,0)}\,x_{1}+f_{(0,1)}\,x_{2}

\displaystyle+f_{(-1,-1)}\,\frac{1}{x_{1}x_{2}}+f_{(0,0)}

with non-zero coefficients

f_{(1,0)},\;f_{(0,1)},\;f_{(-1,-1)}\neq 0

will have same Newton polytope
\Delta
. The set of internal integral points of this polytope is
\Delta^{\circ}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}=\{{\bf 0}\}
.

Assume that the number of internal integral points
g=\#\Delta^{\circ}_{\mathbb{Z}}
is non-zero. We then define a sequence of
g\times g
matrices
\{\beta_{m};m\geq 1\}
with entries in
R
whose rows and columns are indexed by the elements of
\Delta^{\circ}_{\mathbb{Z}}
:

(\beta_{m})_{{\bf u},{\bf v}\in\Delta^{\circ}_{\mathbb{Z}}}=\text{ coefficient of }{\bf x}^{{\bf v}m-{\bf u}}\text{ in }f({\bf x})^{m-1}.

By convention,
\beta_{1}
is the identity matrix. We are going to describe congruences satisfied by these matrices.

A
Frobenius endomorphism

\sigma:R\to R
is a ring endomorphism such that

\sigma(r)\equiv r^{p}\;({\rm mod}\ pR)\quad\text{ for every }r\in R.

In other words
\sigma
lifts the
p
th power endomorphism on the ring
R/pR
, and therefore it is also called a
Frobenius lift
. For example, on
R={\mathbb{Z}}
the identity
\sigma=id
is a Frobenius endomorphism. On the ring of polynomials
R={\mathbb{Z}}[t]
one can take
\sigma:r(t)\to r(t^{p})
.

In the following theorem Frobenius endomorphisms will be applied entry-wise to
g\times g
matrices with entries in
R
.

Theorem 10

(
[
23
]
)
.

For every Frobenius endomorphism
\sigma:R\to R
one has

\beta_{p^{s}}\equiv\beta_{p}\cdot\sigma(\beta_{p})\cdot\ldots\cdot\sigma^{s-1}(\beta_{p})\quad({\rm mod}\ p).

Assuming that
\det(\beta_{p})
is invertible modulo
p
, one has congruenecs

\beta_{p^{s+1}}\cdot\sigma(\beta_{p^{s}})^{-1}\equiv\beta_{p^{s}}\cdot\sigma(\beta_{p^{s-1}})^{-1}\quad({\rm mod}\ p^{s})

for every
s\geq 1
.

Some remarks are needed to the statement of this theorem. In the first congruence
({\rm mod}\ p)
should be read as
({\rm mod}\ pR^{g\times g})
, where
R^{g\times g}
is the ring of
g\times g
matrices with entries in
R
. Next, we would like to note the following.

Remark 11
.

An element
r\in R
is invertible in the
p
-adic completion ring
\widehat{R}\supseteq R
if and only if its image in
R/pR
is invertible. The implication
\Rightarrow
is clear. For the inverse implication
\Leftarrow
we observe that if
r,u,w\in R
are such that
ru=1+pw
then the multiplicative inverse of
r
in
\widehat{R}
is given by
r^{-1}=u(1+pw)^{-1}=u\sum_{m\geq 0}(-1)^{m}p^{m}w^{m}
.

If
\det(\beta_{p})
is invertible in
R/pR
then all
\det(\beta_{p^{s}})
are also invertible in
R/pR
because the first congruence implies that
\det(\beta_{p^{s}})\equiv\det(\beta_{p})^{1+p+\ldots+p^{s-1}}({\rm mod}\ p)

is invertible modulo

is an invertible element of

for our hypersurface

2.4.

Take

For

The reader may find a similar limit computation in

2.5.
Formal expansions of rational functions

Let
f({\bf x})=\sum f_{\bf u}{\bf x}^{\bf u}\in R[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
and
\Delta\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be its Newton polytope. There are different ways to expand rational functions
h({\bf x})/f({\bf x})^{m}
with
h({\bf x})\in R[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
and
m\geq 1
into formal Laurent series. Such expansions will be useful for us in the constructions in the following sections.

Let us describe an expansion procedure with respect to vertices of
\Delta
. Pick a vertex
{\bf b}\in\Delta
and assume that
f_{\bf b}\in R^{\times}
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{h({\bf x})}{f({\bf x})^{m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{h({\bf x})}{f_{{\bf b}}^{m}{\bf x}^{m{\bf b}}(1+\ell({\bf x}))^{m}}=\frac{h({\bf x}){\bf x}^{-m{\bf b}}}{f_{{\bf b}}^{m}}\sum_{s\geq 0}\binom{-m}{s}\ell({\bf x})^{s}=\sum_{{\bf v}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}c_{{\bf v}}{\bf x}^{{\bf v}}.

Here
\binom{-m}{s}=\frac{-m\cdot(-m-1)\cdot\ldots\cdot(-m-(s-1))}{s!}=(-1)^{s}\binom{s+m-1}{m-1}
are integers.

In this computation
\ell({\bf x})
is the Laurent polynomial
\ell({\bf x})=f_{{\bf b}}^{-1}{\bf x}^{-{\bf b}}f({\bf x})-1
supported in the cone
C(\Delta-{\bf b})
. Since
\ell({\bf x})
has no constant term, only finitely many summands
\ell({\bf x})^{s}
will contribute to each monomial
{\bf x}^{\bf v}
, and hence the above series is well-defined.

For later we would like to remark that if
supp(h)\subset m\Delta
then the formal expansion
\sum_{{\bf v}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}c_{{\bf v}}{\bf x}^{{\bf v}}
is itself supported in the cone
C(\Delta-{\bf b})
.

Examples of such formal expansions will be given in the next §.

2.6.
Gauss congruences

Let
f({\bf x}),h({\bf x})\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
,
\Delta
is the Newton polytope of
f({\bf x})
and
{\rm supp}(h)\subseteq\Delta
. We expand the ratio of these functions at one of the vertices of
\Delta
as was explained in §

(6)

\frac{h({\bf x})}{f({\bf x})}=\sum_{{\bf v}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}c_{\bf v}{\bf x}^{\bf v}.

Theorem 16
.

(Gauss’ congruences,
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
) If the only integral points in
\Delta
are its vertices then for every odd prime

which does not divide any of the coefficients of

the coefficients in the expansion (

We will prove this fact in §

. These congruences are named after Gauss due to the basic one-variable example

As our second example, let us expand at
{\bf 0}
the rational function

. We find that

(8)

\mathscr{C}_{p}\left((m-1)!\frac{{\bf x}^{\bf u}}{f({\bf x})^{m}}\right)=\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{p^{r}}{r!}\frac{(m-1)!}{(\lceil m/p\rceil-1)!}(\lceil m/p\rceil+r-1)!\frac{Q_{r}({\bf x})}{f^{\sigma}({\bf x})^{r+\lceil m/p\rceil}},

where the
Q_{r}({\bf x})=\mathscr{C}_{p}(G({\bf x})^{r}{\bf x}^{{\bf u}}f({\bf x})^{p\lceil m/p\rceil-m})
are
Laurent polynomials in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
with support in

(\lceil m/p\rceil+r)\Delta
and coefficients in
R
. Since the
p
-adic valuations of
p^{r}/r!
grow infinitely, the right-hand sum is a
p
-adically convergent sum of elements of
\Omega_{f^{\sigma}}
, and hence it belongs to
\widehat{\Omega}_{f^{\sigma}}
.
∎

From now on it is convenient to
assume that
R
is
p
-adically complete
. The
R
-linear operation

(9)

\mathscr{C}_{p}:\widehat{\Omega}_{f}\to\widehat{\Omega}_{f^{\sigma}},\quad\sum c_{\bf v}{\bf x}^{\bf v}\mapsto\sum c_{p{\bf v}}{\bf x}^{\bf v}

is called the
p
-adic Cartier operation
. We shall now list its properties.

Theorem 19
.

(i)

The operation
\mathscr{C}_{p}:\widehat{\Omega}_{f}\to\widehat{\Omega}_{f^{\sigma}}
is independent of the choice of vertex
{\bf b}\in\Delta
with respect to which the formal expansion in (
9
) is done.

(ii)

For any open set
\mu\subseteq\Delta
the image of
\widehat{\Omega}_{f}(\mu)
lies in
\widehat{\Omega}_{f^{\sigma}}(\mu)
.

(iii)

For
1\leq i\leq n
we have

\mathscr{C}_{p}\circ x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}=p\,x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\circ\mathscr{C}_{p}.

In particular,
\mathscr{C}_{p}(d\widehat{\Omega}_{f})\subset d\widehat{\Omega}_{f^{\sigma}}
and the Cartier operation descends to the homological quotients

\mathscr{C}_{p}:\widehat{\Omega}_{f}/d\widehat{\Omega}_{f}\to\widehat{\Omega}_{f^{\sigma}}/d\widehat{\Omega}_{f^{\sigma}}.

(iv)

If
R={\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
and
\sigma=id
then

(p^{s}-1)\;{\rm Trace}(\,\mathscr{C}_{p}^{s}\,|\,\widehat{\Omega}_{f}\,)=\#({\mathbb{T}}^{n}\setminus X_{f})({\mathbb{F}}_{p}^{s})

for every
s\geq 1
.

Sketch of proof.

Part (i) follows from formula (
8
) which actually describes the Cartier map without appealing to the formal expansion procedure. Part (ii) can be also deduced from this formula, because our definition of finite topology in §
3.1
implies that when
{\bf u}inm\mu
then
{\rm supp}({\mathbb{Q}}_{r})\subset(\lceil m/p\rceil+r)\mu
. The reader can find a detailed explanation of this in
[
5
, Prop 3.4]
. Part (iii) can be easily checked on formal expansions.

Part (iv) is proved in
[
5
, Thm A.1]
. There are obvious relations among the generators
(m-1)!\frac{{\bf x}^{\bf u}}{f({\bf x})^{m}}
of the module
\Omega_{f}
. We consider its resolution in which such generators become free and lift the Cartier action to such free modules in
[
5
, Thm A.5]
. These free modules with the Cartier action turn out to be the
exponential modules
introduced by Dwork, and the desired computation of the trace on them is precisely the
Dwork trace formula
(
[
5
, Prop A.8]
). We remark that the traces here are well defined because modulo any power of

the image of
\mathscr{C}_{p}
is of finite rank, and the same property holds true for the lift of this operation to the exponential module.
∎

3.3.
Formally exact forms

In §
3.2
we defined the Cartier operation thorough its action on formal expansions with respect to a vertex
{\bf b}\in\Delta

Lemma 20

A series

(ii)

Here criterion (i) holds in general over rings of characteristic 0, while for (ii) to be true one needs

to be a

-algebra, so that all other primes are invertible in

to be

-algebra.

Proof.

We leave this proof as an exercise.
∎

For a subset

which is

in the finite topology of §

indexed by the set

\beta_{m}(\mu)_{{\bf u},{\bf v}\in\mu_{\mathbb{Z}}}=\text{ coefficient of }{\bf x}^{m{\bf v}-{\bf u}}\text{ in }f({\bf x})^{m-1}.

The matrix

We shall now state the key result of our paper

Theorem 21
.

(
[
5
, Thm 4.3]
) Assume that

and

is
p
-adically complete. If the Hasse–Witt condition holds for an

It is clear from Lemma

(and even maps it to

\mathscr{C}_{p}\left(\frac{{\bf x}^{\bf u}}{f({\bf x})}\right)=\sum_{{\bf v}\in\mu_{\mathbb{Z}}}\lambda_{{\bf u},{\bf v}}\frac{{\bf x}^{\bf v}}{f^{\sigma}({\bf x})}\quad({\rm mod}\ p\,d\Omega_{\rm formal})

The proof of Theorem
21
will be given in §
3.4
. It will exploit the
p
-adic contraction property of the Cartier operator stated in the following proposition.

Proposition 22
.

One has

\mathscr{C}_{p}(\Omega_{f}(\mu))\subset\Omega_{f^{\sigma}}^{(1)}(\mu)+p\,\widehat{\Omega}_{f^{\sigma}}(\mu).

For any
{\bf u}\in\mu_{\mathbb{Z}}
we have

\mathscr{C}_{p}\left(\frac{{\bf x}^{\bf u}}{f({\bf x})}\right)=\sum_{{\bf v}\in\mu_{\mathbb{Z}}}HW_{{\bf u},{\bf v}}\frac{{\bf x}^{\bf v}}{f^{\sigma}({\bf x})}\quad({\rm mod}\ p\widehat{\Omega}_{f^{\sigma}}(\mu)).

Proof.

We analyze formula (
8

p^{r}/r!

for all

Note that this proposition shows that under the conditions of Theorem

Suppose that

.

Let

-modules and

Proof of Theorem

.

We apply Proposition
25

and

for all

. For

3.5.
Proof of Gauss congruences

We are now ready to prove Theorem
16
. Let us recall its formulation. We assume that
f({\bf x})\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
has a Newton polytope
\Delta
whose only integral points are vertices. Then for any Laurent polynomial
h({\bf x})
with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Z}}
and
{\rm supp}(h)\subset\Delta
the coefficients of any formal expansion

\frac{h({\bf x})}{f({\bf x})}=\sum_{{\bf v}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}c_{\bf v}{\bf x}^{{\bf v}}

satisfy congruences
c_{{\bf v}}\equiv c_{{\bf v}/p}({\rm mod}\ p^{\mathrm{ord}_{p}({\bf v})})
for every prime
p
which doesn’t divide any of the coefficients of
f({\bf x})
.

The desired congruence is equivalent to the fact that
\omega=h({\bf x})/f({\bf x})
satisfies

\mathscr{C}_{p}\omega-\omega\,\in p\,d\Omega_{\rm formal},

where
d\Omega_{\rm formal}
is the module of formal derivatives introduces in §
3.3
. It clearly suffices to prove this claim for
\omega={\bf x}^{{\bf u}}/f({\bf x})
where
{\bf u}
is a vertex of
\Delta
. Consider

\mu=\Delta\setminus\text{union of all faces which do not contain }{\bf u}.

This set is
open
in the topology of §
3.1
and its only integral point is
{\bf u}
, that is we have
\mu_{\mathbb{Z}}=\{{\bf u}\}
. The
1\times 1
Hasse–Witt matrix for this
\mu
is

HW(\mu)=\text{ coefficient of }{\bf x}^{(p-1){\bf u}}\text{ in }f({\bf x})^{p-1}=f_{{\bf u}}^{p-1}\equiv 1({\rm mod}\ p).

Here
f_{{\bf u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
is the coefficients at
{\bf x}^{\bf u}
in
f({\bf x})
and the congruence holds because
p\nmid f_{{\bf u}}
by our assumption on
p
. Hence the Hasse–Witt condition holds for
\mu
over
R={\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
and by Theorem
21
applied with the trivial Frobenius lift
\sigma=id
there exists a unique
\lambda\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
such that

(11)

\mathscr{C}_{p}\omega=\lambda\,\omega\;({\rm mod}\ p\,d\Omega_{\rm formal}).

By Theorem
19
the Cartier operation, and hence this
p
-adic number
\lambda
, is independent of the choice of vertex of
\Delta
at which the formal expansion is done. Hence we can use expansion at
{\bf u}
to determine
\lambda
. For this expansion we have
c_{{\bf 0}}=f_{{\bf u}}^{-1}\neq 0
, and therefore comparing the constant terms on the two sides of (
11
) we find that
\lambda=1
as desired.

4.
Periods

In this lecture we will focus on computation of the Cartier action modulo formal derivatives which were introduced in §
3.3
. The setup is like in §
3.1
-
3.3
. We work with a Laurent polynomial
f({\bf x})\in R[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
with coefficients in a characteristic 0 ring
R
such that
\cap_{s\geq 1}p^{s}R=\{0\}
. We also assume that
R
is
p
-adically complete.

The Newton polytope of
f({\bf x})
is denoted by
\Delta\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Let
\mu\subseteq\Delta
be an open set in the sense of §
3.1
. Recall that, if the Hasse–Witt condition holds for
\mu
, Theorem
21
implies existence of a unique matrix
\Lambda(\mu)=(\lambda_{{\bf u},{\bf v}})_{{\bf u},{\bf v}\in\mu_{\mathbb{Z}}}
with entries in
R
such that

(12)

\mathscr{C}_{p}\left(\frac{{\bf x}^{{\bf u}}}{f({\bf x})}\right)=\sum_{{\bf v}\in\mu_{\mathbb{Z}}}\lambda_{{\bf u},{\bf v}}\,\frac{{\bf x}^{{\bf v}}}{f^{\sigma}({\bf x})}\quad({\rm mod}\ p\,d\Omega_{\rm formal})

for every
{\bf u}\in\mu_{\mathbb{Z}}
. We are going to exploit divisibility properties of coefficients of elements of
d\Omega_{\rm formal}
to compute the Cartier entries
\lambda_{{\bf u},{\bf v}}\in R
. We first explain a straightforward approach to this question, which is a version of Nick Katz’s
Internal reconstruction of unit-root F-crystals via expansion coefficients
in
[
18
]
. Later we will move to more sophisticated methods using period maps.

4.1.
p
-adic interpolation of Cartier matrices via expansion coefficients

In Section
2.5
we explained the procedure of formal expansion of rational functions with respect to a vertex
{\bf b}
of
\Delta
. We have expansions

\omega=\sum_{{\bf u}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}c_{\bf u}(\omega){\bf x}^{\bf u},\qquad\omega\in\Omega_{f}.

Proposition 26
.

For any vector
{\bf w}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
one can consider the sequence of vectors
A_{s}\in R^{\#\mu_{\mathbb{Z}}}
,
s\geq 1
with entries

(A_{s})_{{\bf u}\in\mu_{\mathbb{Z}}}=c_{p^{s}{\bf w}}\left(\frac{{\bf x}^{{\bf u}}}{f({\bf x})}\right).

Then

A_{s}\equiv\Lambda(\mu)\,\sigma(A_{s-1})\quad({\rm mod}\ p^{s}).

Proof.

Let us take the expansion coefficient at
p^{s-1}{\bf w}
on both sides of (
12
). By Lemma
20
this coefficient is divisible by
p^{s-1}
on

, and therefore we obtain

c_{p^{s}{\bf w}}\left(\frac{{\bf x}^{{\bf u}}}{f({\bf x})}\right)\equiv\sum_{{\bf v}\in\mu_{\mathbb{Z}}}\lambda_{{\bf u},{\bf v}}\,c_{p^{s-1}{\bf w}}\left(\frac{{\bf x}^{{\bf v}}}{f^{\sigma}({\bf x})}\right)\;({\rm mod}\ p^{s}).

This congruence is precisely our claim.
∎

Usually one can determine
\Lambda(\mu)
from congruences as in Proposition
26
taken for several exponent vectors
{\bf w}
.

4.2.
Period maps

Definition 27
.

A
period map
with values in an
R
-module
S
is a homomorphism of
R
-modules
\mathcal{P}:\Omega_{f}\to S
which vanishes on
d\Omega_{f}
.
Values of a period map are called
periods
.

The classical notion of periods for algebraic varieties was introduced by Alexander Grothendieck. Since elements of
d\Omega_{f}
correspond to exact differential
n
-forms on
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}\setminus X_{f}
, there are classical period maps of integration along topological n-cycles
Y\subset({\mathbb{T}}^{n}\setminus X_{f})({\mathbb{C}})
:

\mathcal{P}_{\emph{Y}}:\omega\to\int_{\emph{Y}}\omega.

Here we identify elements of
\Omega_{f}
with differential forms on the complement
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}\setminus X_{f}
as was explained in §
3.1
, that is
\frac{h({\bf x})}{f({\bf x})^{m}}\mapsto\frac{h({\bf x})}{f({\bf x})^{m}}\frac{x_{1}}{x_{1}}\ldots\frac{x_{n}}{x_{n}}
. Elements of
d\Omega_{f}
correspond to exact forms, and therefore
\mathcal{P}_{Y}(d\Omega_{f})=0
. In general such maps take values in
{\mathbb{C}}
, or in analytic functions in case of families of hypersurfaces, and we do not expect them to behave well with respect to the Cartier operation. Our goal will be to construct Cartier-invariant period maps. We start with an example.

Example 28
.

Take
f({\bf x})=1-tg({\bf x})
with
g({\bf x})\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
. Assume that
{\bf 0}\in\Delta
(not necessarily a vertex or an internal point). We can work with any ring
R
such that
{\mathbb{Z}}[t]\subset R\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
. Consider the formal expansion of rational functions at
{\bf 0}
:

\frac{h({\bf x})}{f({\bf x})^{m}}=h({\bf x})\sum_{s\geq 0}\binom{s+m-1}{m-1}t^{s}g({\bf x})^{s}=\sum_{{\bf u}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}c_{{\bf u}}(t){\bf x}^{\bf u}.

This expansion is convergent
t
-adically, and the coefficients
c_{\bf u}(t)
belong to the ring of formal power series
S={\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
. Then

\mathcal{P}_{{\bf 0}}:\omega\mapsto c_{{\bf 0}}(\omega)

is a period map with values in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
which

•

vanishes on
d\Omega_{\rm formal}
,

•

is
\mathscr{C}_{p}
-invariant.

The first property is clear because the constant terms of logarithmic derivatives
x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\nu
are zero. The second property follows from the fact that the Cartier operation acts on formal expansions at
{\bf 0}
is the same way as on expansions at vertices of the Newton polytope. That is, we have
\mathscr{C}_{p}:\sum_{\bf u}c_{\bf u}(t){\bf x}^{\bf u}\mapsto\sum_{\bf u}c_{p{\bf u}}(t){\bf x}^{\bf u}
. The reader who is not convinced may find a discussion of this later point in
[
6
, §2]
.

We note that the period map in the above example is of the kind of earlier mentioned integration maps. This is integration along the
n
-cycle
Y=S^{1}\times\cdots\times S^{1}
, if we divide the result by
(2\pi i)^{n}
, or the residue map at
{\bf 0}
. One can write this fact as
\mathcal{P}_{\bf 0}=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{n}}\mathcal{P}_{S^{1}\times\cdots\times S^{1}}
.

Now we can revisit §
2.4
. We prove the following fact.

Theorem 29
.

Take
f({\bf x})=1-tg({\bf x})
with
g({\bf x})\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
. Assume that the Newton polytope
\Delta
has only one interior integral point. Assume further that this interior point is the origin, that is
\Delta^{\circ}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}=\{{\bf 0}\}
. For
i\geq 0
we denote by
c_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
the constant term of
g({\bf x})^{i}
. Consider the generating series

\gamma(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_{i}t^{i}=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{n}}\oint\ldots\oint\frac{1}{1-tg({\bf x})}\frac{dx_{1}}{x_{1}}\ldots\frac{dx_{n}}{x_{n}}

and denote its truncations by
\gamma_{m}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}c_{i}t^{i}
. Then for any odd prime number
p
one has

\frac{\gamma(t)}{\gamma(t^{p})}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t,\gamma_{p}(t)^{-1}]\,\widehat{\;}.

Proof.

Consider
\mu=\Delta^{\circ}
. The respective Hasse-Witt matrix (of rank 1 in this case) is given by
HW(t)=\text{ constant term of }(1-tg({\bf x}))^{p-1}=\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{p-1}{i}c_{i}t^{i}
. Since
HW(t)\equiv\gamma_{p}(t)({\rm mod}\ p)
, it follows that
HW(t)
is invertible in the ring
R={\mathbb{Z}}[t,\gamma_{p}(t)^{-1}]\,\widehat{\;}
. Let
\sigma:R\to R
be the Frobenius lift
\sigma(r(t))=r(t^{p})
. By Theorem
21
there exists
\lambda\in R
such that

(13)

\mathscr{C}_{p}\frac{1}{f({\bf x})}=\lambda\frac{1}{f^{\sigma}({\bf x})}({\rm mod}\ p\,d\Omega_{\rm formal}).

We apply to this congruence the period map
\mathcal{P}_{{\bf 0}}
defined in Example
28
. Note that
\mathcal{P}_{\bf 0}(1/f({\bf x}))=\gamma(t)
. Using the two properties of the map
\mathcal{P}_{{\bf 0}}
we get

\gamma(t)=\lambda\,\gamma(t^{p}),

from which it follows that
\lambda=\gamma(t)/\gamma(t^{p})
. Since
\lambda\in R
, this proves our claim.
∎

Our next goal will be to prove an explicit
p
-adic approximation to
\gamma(t)/\gamma(t^{p})
by ratios of truncations
\gamma_{p^{s}}(t)/\gamma_{p^{s-1}}(t^{p})
. For that we should evidence that truncations are periods. In fact, they will be periods modulo
p^{s}
which we shall now introduce.

4.3.
Periods modulo
p^{s}

Definition 30
.

Let
S
be an
R
-module such that
\cap_{s}p^{s}S=\{0\}
. An
R
-linear map
\rho:\Omega_{f}\to S
such that
\rho(d\Omega_{f})\subset p^{s}S
is called a period map modulo
p^{s}
.

Example 31
.

Let
\omega\mapsto\sum_{{\bf u}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}c_{{\bf u}}(\omega){\bf x}^{\bf u}
be either one of the procedures of formal expansion introduced earlier. It can be either an expansion at a vertex
{\bf b}\in\Delta
as in §
2.5
or an expansion at
{\bf 0}\in\Delta
as in Example
28
. We take
S=R
and
S={\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
in these cases respectively. Then for any exponent vector
{\bf w}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
the map

\omega\mapsto c_{p^{s}{\bf w}}(\omega)

is a period map modulo
p^{s}
with the following properties:

•

c_{p^{s}{\bf w}}\left(d\Omega_{\rm formal}\right)\subset p^{s}S
,

•

c_{p^{s}{\bf w}}=c_{p^{s-1}{\bf w}}\circ\mathscr{C}_{p}
.

Example 32
.

Let
\omega\mapsto\sum_{{\bf u}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}c_{{\bf u}}(\omega){\bf x}^{\bf u}
be either one of the procedures of formal expansion introduced earlier with coefficients in
S=R
or
S={\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket\supset R
. Let
\ell({\bf x})\in S[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
be a Laurent polynomial. Then

\rho_{s,\ell}:\omega\mapsto c_{{\bf 0}}\left(\ell({\bf x})^{p^{s}}\omega\right)

is a period map modulo
p^{s}
with the following properties:

•

\rho_{s,\ell}\left(d\Omega_{\rm formal}\right)\subset p^{s}S
,

•

\rho_{s,\ell}\equiv\rho_{s-1,\ell^{\sigma}}\circ\mathscr{C}_{p}\;({\rm mod}\ p^{s})
.

The reason that the first property holds is that

\ell({\bf x})^{p^{s}}x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\nu\equiv x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(\ell({\bf x})^{p^{s}}\nu\right)({\rm mod}\ p^{s}).

We than take
c_{{\bf 0}}
and obtain that
\rho_{s,\ell}(x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\nu)\in p^{s}S
.
Proof of the second property is left an an exercise for the reader.

The fact that expansion coefficients are period maps (Example
31
) was actually used in the proof of Proposition
26
. Example
32
provides a generalisation, which we shall now use to prove Dwork’s congruences announced in §
2.4
.

Exercise 33

. For that, check that

and apply these period maps to the identity (

).

Now we are in a position to prove the following generalisation of Theorem
10
.

Theorem 34
.

Let
\mu\subseteq\Delta
be an
open
set. We consider the sequence of square matrices

\beta_{m}(\mu)_{{\bf u},{\bf v}\in\mu_{\mathbb{Z}}}=\text{ coefficient of }{\bf x}^{m{\bf v}-{\bf u}}\text{ in }f({\bf x})^{m-1}.

Suppose that the Hasse–Witt condition holds for
\mu)
, that is matrix
\beta_{p}(\mu)=HW(\mu)
is invertible, and let
\Lambda(\mu)
be the Cartier matrix (
12
) on the quotient of
\Omega_{f}(\mu)
by the submodule of formal derivatives. Then for every
s\geq 1
we have

\beta_{mp^{s}}(\mu)\equiv\Lambda(\mu)\sigma(\beta_{mp^{s-1}}(\mu))\;({\rm mod}\ p^{s}).

In particular, when
m=1
,

\Lambda(\mu)\equiv\beta_{p^{s}}(\mu)\circ\sigma(\beta_{p^{s-1}}(\mu))^{-1}\;({\rm mod}\ p^{s}).

Proof.

Denote
\omega_{\bf u}={\bf x}^{\bf u}/f({\bf x})
for
{\bf u}\in\mu_{\mathbb{Z}}
. Matrix
\Lambda(\mu)=(\lambda_{{\bf u},{\bf v}})
satisfies

\mathscr{C}_{p}(\omega_{\bf u})\equiv\sum_{{\bf v}\in\mu_{\mathbb{Z}}}\lambda_{{\bf u},{\bf v}}\omega_{\bf v}\;({\rm mod}\ pd\Omega_{\rm formal}).

For
{\bf w}\in\mu_{\mathbb{Z}}
let
\ell({\bf x})=f({\bf x})^{m}/{\bf x}^{m{\bf w}}
. We apply to the above congruence the period map
\rho_{s-1,\ell^{\sigma}}
defined in Example

\rho_{s,\ell}(\omega_{\bf u})\equiv\sum_{{\bf v}\in\mu_{\mathbb{Z}}}\lambda_{{\bf u},{\bf v}}\,\rho_{s-1,\ell^{\sigma}}(\omega^{\sigma}_{{\bf v}})\;({\rm mod}\ p^{s}).

It remains to notice that

\rho_{s,\ell}(\omega_{\bf u})=c_{{\bf 0}}\left(f({\bf x})^{p^{s}m-1}{\bf x}^{{\bf u}-p^{s}m{\bf w}}\right)=\beta_{p^{s}m}(\mu)_{{\bf u},{\bf w}}.

∎

5.
Beyond the unit root part

So far we developed a method which gives those Frobenius roots which are
p
-adic units. In the last lecture we will introduce
higher Hasse-Witt conditions
and explain a far going generalization of the previous results. These methods will allow to construct Cartier matrices on (
p
-adic completions of) the whole de Rham cohomology modules. We will also discuss applications and related phenomena of
supercongruences
.

The setup is like in §
3.1
-
3.2
. We work with a Laurent polynomial
f({\bf x})=\sum_{{\bf u}}f_{{\bf u}}{\bf x}^{\bf u}\in R[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
with coefficients in a characteristic 0 ring
R
such that
\cap_{s\geq 1}p^{s}R=\{0\}
. We also assume that
R
is
p
-adically complete and equipped with a Frobenius lift
\sigma:R\to R
.

5.1.
Higher formal derivatives

In §
2.5
and §
3.3
we defined an embedding of
\Omega_{f}
into the space of formal power series

\Omega_{\rm formal}=\{\sum_{{\bf u}\in C(\Delta-{\bf b})\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}a_{\bf u}{\bf x}^{\bf u}\;|\;a_{\bf u}\in R\}

by the procedure of formal expansion of rational functions at a vertex
{\bf b}\in\Delta
. It is assumed that
f_{{\bf b}}\in R^{\times}
.

Definition 35
.

The submodule of
k
-th formal derivatives

d^{k}\Omega_{\rm formal}\subset\Omega_{\rm formal}

is the
R
-module generated by elements of the form
\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{i_{k}}\nu
, where
\theta_{i}=x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}
,
\nu\in\Omega_{\rm formal}
and
1\leq i_{j}\leq n
for each index

.

We have the following generalisation of Lemma
20
:

Lemma 36
.

A series
\nu=\sum_{{\bf u}}a_{\bf u}{\bf x}^{\bf u}\in\Omega_{\rm formal}
is a
k
th formal derivative if and only if one of the following equivalent conditions holds

(i)

a_{\bf u}\in g.c.d.(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})^{k}R
for each
{\bf u}
,

(ii)

\mathscr{C}_{p}^{s}(\nu)\in p^{ks}\Omega_{\rm formal}
for each
s\geq 1
.

Proof.

The proof is left as an exercise.∎

5.2.
Higher Hasse–Witt conditions

Let
\mu\subset\Delta
be an
open
set in the finite topology introduced in §
3.1
. There is a natural filtration on
\Omega_{f}(\mu)
by the order of pole along the zero locus of
f({\bf x})
:

\Omega_{f}^{(k)}(\mu)={\rm Span}_{R}\left((k-1)!\frac{{\bf x}^{{\bf u}}}{f({\bf x})^{k}}\right)_{{\bf u}\in(k\mu)_{\mathbb{Z}}},\quad k\geq 1.

On de Rham cohomology this filtration corresponds to the Hodge filtration, see Remark
17
.
The main result of
[
7
]
describes a splitting filtration in arithmetic terms:

Theorem 37
.

Let
R
be
p
-adically complete,
\mu\subseteq\Delta
be an
open
set and
1\leq k<p
. If the
k
th Hasse–Witt condition
holds for
\mu
, then there is a direct sum decomposition of
R
-modules

\widehat{\Omega}_{f}(\mu)=\Omega_{f}^{(k)}(\mu)\oplus\mathcal{F}_{k},

where

\mathcal{F}_{k}=\widehat{\Omega}_{f}(\mu)\cap d^{k}\Omega_{formal}=\{\omega\in\widehat{\Omega}_{f}(\mu):\mathscr{C}_{p}^{s}(\omega)\in p^{ks}\widehat{\Omega}_{f^{\sigma^{s}}}(\mu)\quad\forall s\geq 1\}

is the submodule of
k
th formal derivatives. Secondly, one has

\mathscr{C}_{p}(\Omega_{f}(\mu))\subset\Omega^{(k)}_{f^{\sigma}}(\mu)+p^{k}\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\sigma}.

This theorem will be proved in the next section. Let us recall formula (
8
) which describes explicitly the Cartier action
\mathscr{C}_{p}:\widehat{\Omega}_{f}\to\widehat{\Omega}_{f^{\sigma}}
:

(14)

\mathscr{C}_{p}\left((m-1)!\frac{{\bf x}^{\bf u}}{f({\bf x})^{m}}\right)=\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{p^{r}}{r!}\frac{(m-1)!}{(\lceil m/p\rceil-1)!}(\lceil m/p\rceil+r-1)!\frac{Q_{r}({\bf x})}{f^{\sigma}({\bf x})^{r+\lceil m/p\rceil}},

where the
Q_{r}({\bf x})=\mathscr{C}_{p}(G({\bf x})^{r}{\bf x}^{{\bf u}}f({\bf x})^{p\lceil m/p\rceil-m})
are
Laurent polynomials in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
with support in
(\lceil m/p\rceil+r)\Delta
and coefficients in
R
.
Polynomial
G({\bf x})
was defined as
(f^{\sigma}({\bf x}^{p})-f({\bf x})^{p})/p
, it is supported in
p\Delta
and has coefficients in
R
. We observe that formula (
14
) implies that

\mathscr{C}_{p}(\Omega_{f}(\mu))\subset\Omega_{f^{\sigma}}^{(k)}(\mu)+p^{k}\widehat{\Omega}_{f^{\sigma}}(\mu).

The
k
th Hasse-Witt condition means that the image of the Cartier operator modulo
p^{k}
has maximal possible rank. We shall now explain how to verify this condition in practice.

Definition 38
.

Assume that
1\leq k<p
. Denote

F^{(k)}({\bf x})=f({\bf x})^{p-k}\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}\left(f^{\sigma}({\bf x}^{p})-f({\bf x})^{p}\right)^{r}f^{\sigma}({\bf x}^{p})^{k-1-r}.

The
k
-th Hasse-Witt matrix
HW^{(k)}
is the matrix indexed by the set
(k\Delta)_{\mathbb{Z}}=(k\Delta)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
with entries given by

HW^{(k)}_{{\bf u},{\bf v}}=\mbox{coefficient of ${\bf x}^{p{\bf v}-{\bf u}}$ in }F^{(k)}({\bf x}).

For an
open
set
\mu\subseteq\Delta
we denote by
HW^{(k)}(\mu)
the submatrix indexed by
(k\mu)_{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Exercise 39
.

Check that

HW^{(k)}_{{\bf u},{\bf v}}\equiv\text{coefficient of ${\bf x}^{p{\bf v}-{\bf u}}$ in }\frac{f^{\sigma}({\bf x}^{p})^{k}}{f({\bf x})^{k}}({\rm mod}\ p^{k}),

where on the right one has to consider the Laurent series expansion in order to determine the coefficient.

Lemma 40
.

Let
\mu\subset\Delta
be an
open
set.

(i)

For
{\bf u}\in(k\mu)_{\mathbb{Z}}
one has

\mathscr{C}_{p}\left(\frac{{\bf x}^{\bf u}}{f({\bf x})^{k}}\right)\equiv\sum_{{\bf v}\in(k\mu)_{\mathbb{Z}}}HW^{(k)}_{{\bf u},{\bf v}}\frac{{\bf x}^{\bf v}}{f^{\sigma}({\bf x})^{k}}\quad({\rm mod}\ p^{k}\,\widehat{\Omega}_{f^{\sigma}}(\mu))

(ii)

Let us denote
m_{\ell}=\#(\ell\mu)_{\mathbb{Z}}
for
\ell\geq 1
and

L(k,\mu)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}(\ell-1)(m_{\ell}-m_{\ell-1}).

Then
\det\left(HW^{(k)}(\mu)\right)\in p^{L(k,\mu)}R
.

Proof.

(i) follows from formula (
14
). We leave the check as an exercise.
For (ii) we choose a basis
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{m_{k}}
in the free
R
-module
\Omega^{(k)}_{f}(\mu)
so that
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{m_{\ell}}
is a basis in
\Omega^{(\ell)}_{f}(\mu)
for every
\ell\leq k
. This is called an
extended basis
. Under our standard assumption that there is a vertex
{\bf b}
in
\Delta
such that the coefficient of
f
at
{\bf x}^{\bf b}
is in
R^{\times}
, one may check that

\frac{{\bf x}^{{\bf u}}}{f({\bf x})^{\ell}},{\bf u}\in(\ell\mu)_{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus((\ell-1)\mu)_{\mathbb{Z}},\quad 1\leq\ell\leq k

is an extended basis in
\Omega_{f}^{(k)}(\mu)
.

We write the action of the Cartier operator on
\Omega_{f}^{(k)}(\mu)
modulo
p^{k}
with respect to an extended basis. That is, we choose some
C_{ij}\in R
such that
\mathscr{C}_{p}(\omega_{i})=\sum_{j}C_{ij}\omega_{j}^{\sigma}({\rm mod}\ p^{k})
. Formula (
14
) shows that
\mathscr{C}_{p}
maps
\Omega_{f}^{(k)}(\mu)
to
\sum_{l=1}^{k}p^{l-1}\Omega_{f^{\sigma}}^{(l)}(\mu)
, and hence

is divisible by

when

m_{l-1}<j\leq m_{\ell}
. We conclude that

.

Let
\tilde{\omega}_{{\bf u}}={\bf x}^{{\bf u}}/f({\bf x})^{k}
,
{\bf u}\in(k\mu)_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
be the monomial basis in
\Omega_{f}^{(k)}(\mu)
. From part (i) we know that

describes the action of the Cartier operator modulo

in this monomial basis. Let
A
be the transition matrix, that is
\tilde{\omega}_{{\bf u}}=\sum_{i}A_{{\bf u},i}\omega_{i}

It follows that
\det(A)^{-1}\det(HW^{(k)}(\mu))\det(A)^{\sigma}\in p^{L(k,\mu)}R
. Since

is
p
-adically complete, invertibility

\det(HW^{(k)}(\mu))\in p^{L(k,\mu)}R.

∎

Definition 41
.

We say that the

th Hasse-Witt condition holds for an

\det(HW^{(\ell)}(\mu))\in p^{L(\ell,\mu)}R^{\times},\quad 1\leq\ell\leq k.

In
[

it is explained that this condition means maximality of the Cartier image modulo

.

\det(C_{k-1})\in R^{\times}
,
which concludes the proof of invertibility of (
19
) when
i=1
.
For general

we use the same argument with
f
substituted by
f^{\sigma^{i-1}}
.

We checked that the assumptions of Proposition
25
are satisfied.
From parts (i) and (iv) of that proposition we conclude that
M_{0}=N_{0}\oplus U_{0}
, where

\displaystyle U_{0}

\displaystyle=\{\omega\in M_{0}|\phi_{s}\circ\ldots\circ\phi_{1}(\omega)\in p^{s}M_{s}\text{ for all }s\geq 1\}

\displaystyle=\{\omega\in\mathcal{F}_{k-1}|\mathscr{C}_{p}^{s}(\omega)\in p^{ks}\mathcal{F}_{k-1}^{\sigma^{s}}\text{ for all }s\geq 1\}=\mathcal{F}_{k}.

We proved that
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\cong(\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\cap\Omega_{f}^{(k)})\oplus\mathcal{F}_{k}
.
Using the argument from the beginning of the induction step, the first claim of our theorem follows.

Our second claim follows from (
15
)
and the decomposition that we already proved:

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{p}\left(\Omega_{f}\right)\subset\Omega_{f^{\sigma}}^{(k)}+p^{k}\widehat{\Omega}_{f^{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{f^{\sigma}}^{(k)}+p^{k}\left(\Omega_{f^{\sigma}}^{(k)}+\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\sigma}\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega_{f^{\sigma}}^{(k)}+p^{k}\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\sigma}.

This finishes the proof of the theorem.

for

5.5.
Supercongruences and excellent Frobenius lifts

Let us start with a simple example, in which one can explicitly compute the action of
\mathscr{C}_{p}
modulo
\mathcal{F}_{2}
using the method of the previous section. This example will help us to illustrate the phenomenon in the name of this section.

Example 42
.

Let
f({\bf x})=(1-x_{1})(1-x_{2})-tx_{1}x_{2}
and

R=\text{$p$-adic completion of }\;{\mathbb{Z}}[t,1/t].

Frobenius lifts
\sigma:R\to R
are given by
t\mapsto t^{\sigma}\in t^{p}+pR
. In
[
7
, §6]
we show that for any Frobenius lift one has

(22)

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{p}\left(\frac{1}{f({\bf x})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f^{\sigma}({\bf x})}({\rm mod}\ p\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\sigma}),

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{p}\left(\frac{1}{f({\bf x})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f^{\sigma}({\bf x})}+\log\left(\frac{t^{\sigma}}{t^{p}}\right)\left(\theta\frac{1}{f({\bf x})}\right)^{\sigma}({\rm mod}\ p^{2}\mathcal{F}_{2}^{\sigma}),

where
\theta=t\frac{d}{dt}
. We see that in the special case
t^{\sigma}=t^{p}
one has

(23)

\mathscr{C}_{p}\left(\frac{1}{f({\bf x})}\right)=\frac{1}{f^{\sigma}({\bf x})}\quad({\rm mod}\ p\mathcal{F}_{2}^{\sigma}).

For this special Frobenius lift
1/f({\bf x})
becomes an ‘eigenvector’ of the Cartier operator modulo
\mathcal{F}_{2}
, while in general it is only an ’eigenvector’ modulo
\mathcal{F}_{1}
. Existence of such lifts was observed by Bernard Dwork who called them
excellent Frobenius lifts
.

In the above example, let us expand
1/f
at the vertex
{\bf 0}\in\Delta
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{f({\bf x})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{(tx_{1}x_{2})^{m}}{(1-x_{1})^{m+1}(1-x_{2})^{m+1}}=\sum_{m,u_{1},u_{2}\geq 0}t^{m}\binom{u_{1}}{m}\binom{u_{2}}{m}x_{1}^{u_{1}}x_{2}^{u_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf u}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}}c_{{\bf u}}(t)\,{\bf x}^{\bf u},\qquad c_{{\bf u}}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{\min(u_{1},u_{2})}\binom{u_{1}}{m}\binom{u_{2}}{m}t^{m}

The first line in (
22
) means that for any Frobenius lift
\sigma:R\to R

c_{u_{1}p^{s},u_{2}p^{s}}(t)\equiv c_{u_{1}p^{s-1},u_{2}p^{s-1}}(t^{\sigma})\quad({\rm mod}\ p^{s}),\quad s\geq 1.

However for the excellent Frobenius lift
t^{\sigma}=t^{p}
we have (
23
) and the modulus improves:

c_{u_{1}p^{s},u_{2}p^{s}}(t)\equiv c_{u_{1}p^{s-1},u_{2}p^{s-1}}(t^{p})\quad({\rm mod}\ p^{2s}),\quad s\geq 1.

This is an example of
supercongruences
.

Note that values
t=\pm 1
are fixed by our excellent Frobenius lift
t\mapsto t^{p}
. For example, for
t=1
we have supercongruences for the expansion coefficients of

\frac{1}{1-x_{1}-x_{2}}=\sum_{{\bf u}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}}\binom{u_{1}+u_{2}}{u_{1}}\,{\bf x}^{\bf u}.

Namely, the binomial coefficients satisfy

\binom{(u_{1}+u_{2})p^{s}}{u_{1}p^{s}}\equiv\binom{(u_{1}+u_{2})p^{s-1}}{u_{1}p^{s-1}}({\rm mod}\ p^{2s}).

This supercongruence is ‘twice stronger’ than the Gauss’ congruence in §
2.6
.

5.8.

Let us look at the situation when the coefficients of

Suppose that
\Omega_{f}(\mu)/d\Omega_{f}

Let

\theta

Proposition 49
.

The Cartier matrix
\Lambda
satisfies the differential equation

(28)

\theta(\Lambda)=N\Lambda-\Lambda\widetilde{N}.

Proof.

Note that
\mathscr{C}_{p}
and
\theta
commute as operations on
\widehat{\Omega}_{f}
and
\widehat{\Omega}_{f^{\sigma}}

\theta\circ\mathscr{C}_{p}=\mathscr{C}_{p}\circ\theta.

Perhaps the easiest way to check that they commute is by looking on the Cartier action on formal expansions. Let us apply
\theta
to the equation (

\theta(\mathscr{C}_{p}\,\omega_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\theta(\lambda_{i,j})\omega_{j}^{\sigma}+\lambda_{i,j}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\widetilde{N}_{j,k}\omega_{k}^{\sigma}\right)\quad({\rm mod}\ d\widehat{\Omega}_{f^{\sigma}}).

On the other hand, the Cartier operation is
R
-linear and therefore

\mathscr{C}_{p}(\theta\omega_{i})=\mathscr{C}_{p}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}N_{i,j}\omega_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}N_{i,j}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j,k}\omega_{k}^{\sigma}\quad({\rm mod}\ d\Omega_{f^{\sigma}}).

Since
\mathscr{C}_{p}
and
\theta
commute, the two above expressions are equal and we obtain that
\theta(\Lambda)+\Lambda\widetilde{N}=N\Lambda
. This is precisely our claim.
∎

5.9.
Integrality of mirror maps and instanton numbers

Mirror symmetry
is a relationship between Calabi–Yau manifolds. Two such manifolds may be very different geometrically but are nevertheless equivalent when employed as ‘extra dimensions’ to describe interaction of particles in string theory. In
[
10
]
physicists Candelas, de la Ossa, Green and Parkes made a sensation: they predicted solutions to very hard problems of enumerative geometry by doing miraculous computations with solutions of a differential equation ’on the other side of the mirror’. We will briefly recall their discovery.

Candelas, de la Ossa, Green and Parkes considered the differential operator

(31)

L=\theta^{4}-5^{5}t\left(\theta+\tfrac{1}{5}\right)\left(\theta+\tfrac{2}{5}\right)\left(\theta+\tfrac{3}{5}\right)\left(\theta+\tfrac{4}{5}\right)

where
\theta=t\tfrac{d}{dt}
. The differential equation

has a singular point at

with maximally unipotent local monodromy. There is a unique (up to multiplication by a constant) solution which is analytic analytic at
t=0

There is also a unique solution of the form

y_{1}(t)=F_{0}(t)\log(t)+F_{1}(t)\quad\text{ with }F_{1}(t)\in t{\mathbb{Q}}\llbracket t\rrbracket.

Here
F_{1}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(5n)!}{n!^{5}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{5k}\frac{5}{j}\right)t^{k}
does not have integral coefficients in contrast to

, but the authors of
[
10
]

:

This observation was proved by by B.-H.Lian and S.-T.Yau in 1996.
