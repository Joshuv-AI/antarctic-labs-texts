RIMOXCLAMIN's Therapeutic Impact in a Larger Brazilian Hansen's Disease Population
Authors: Marco Andrey Cipriani Frade, Gustavo Sartori Albertino, Natália Aparecida de Paula, Fabiana Aparecida Correa Cinto, Fernanda Cruz Perecin, Andrezza Westin, Mariane de Moraes Monteiro, Wilson Marques Junior, Helena Barbosa Lugão, Filipe Rocha Lima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRIMOXCLAMIN, a new antibiotic regimen for Hansen's disease (HD), combines rifampicin, moxifloxacin, clarithromycin, and minocycline; the WHO-MDT combines rifampicin, dapsone, and clofazimine. To overcome limitations of WHO-MDT, we evaluated both regimens in a 2-arm cohort of multibacillary patients.MethodsTwo-arm observational cohort included 162 newly diagnosed multibacillary cases (103 RIMOXCLAMIN and 59 WHO-MDT) evaluated between 2015 and 2025. Patients were assessed at least quarterly (baseline and months 3, 6, and 12) for neurological and cutaneous findings, adverse events, tactile sensitivity, and physical disability grade (PDG).ResultsNeurological symptoms were frequent regardless of lesion count (≤5 vs > 5; P > .05). RIMOXCLAMIN demonstrated a greater reduction in nerve thickening than WHO-MDT (P < .0001) and increased PDG-0 from 18% to 82% (P < .0001), compared with 22%–53% in the WHO-MDT group (P = .0006). RIMOXCLAMIN reduced PDG-2 by 67% at month 6 (P = .002) and 83% at discharge (P < .0001), whereas WHO-MDT showed no change at month 6 and a 50% reduction at discharge. At month 3, neurological symptoms were greater in the RIMOXCLAMIN group than in the WHO-MDT, with no intragroup differences. WHO-MDT showed higher adverse events, including severe anemia (requiring dapsone discontinuation in 33.9% [20/59]). RIMOXCLAMIN adverse events were mild, requiring regimen adjustments in 19.4% (20/103) without additional medications. A general limitation is that the groups are not fully socioeconomically comparable.ConclusionsRIMOXCLAMIN was safe and clinically advantageous as a first-line HD regimen, offering faster neurological recovery, disability reversal, and minimal adverse event. These findings support incorporating neurological parameters of skin sensory changes, as lesion count alone does not appear to be a reliable marker of the clinical and neurological severity.Keywords: Hansen’s disease, leprosy, multidrug therapy, RIMOXCLAMIN, tactile sensitivity, treatment

BACKGROUND

Hansen's disease (HD) is a chronic infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium leprae and Mycobacterium lepromatosis, with a spectrum of clinical manifestations primarily affecting peripheral nerves and skin, leading to sensory loss, muscle weakness, and deformities [1, 2]. Neurological impairment remains central to HD morbidity, and its early detection is critical for timely diagnosis and prevention of sequelae [3–8].

Since 1982, the World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended WHO-multidrug therapy (MDT) for all patients, combining rifampicin, dapsone (DDS), and clofazimine [9]. Despite its therapeutic efficacy in HD and its role in reducing bacillary transmission, WHO-MDT poses significant limitations: DDS is associated with severe toxicity, including hemolytic anemia, methemoglobinemia, and hypersensitivity syndrome, while clofazimine can cause stigmatizing skin discoloration and ichthyosis [4, 10–13], adverse events (AEs) which compromise adherence and quality of life, highlighting the need for safer regimens. Brazil's M. leprae Resistance Surveillance Plan identified drug-resistance mutations in 2.5% (61/2463) of samples analyzed between 2018 and 2022, with resistance rates of 0.9% in new multibacillary (MB) cases and 12.3% in relapse cases [14]. In patients with a high bacillary load, the risk of selecting resistant strains is elevated [15]. WHO data from 2021 show that 20% of 51 countries reported retreatment following MDT relapse, with higher rates in Brazil (37%) and India (16%) [16]. Delayed diagnosis in MB disease is associated with worse neurological outcomes during treatment, underscoring the need for regimens that promote neurological recovery while maintaining safety.

Alternative regimens remain rarely used worldwide. ROM (Rifampicin, Ofloxacin, Minocycline) regimen is employed in selected cases [17], while the recently proposed RIMOXCLAMIN regimen (RIfampicin, MOXifloxacin, CLArithromycin, MINocycline) was designed to maximize bactericidal activity while minimizing severe AEs [17–23]. Each drug in RIMOXCLAMIN has documented efficacy against HD, with moxifloxacin exhibiting superior bactericidal activity, clarithromycin and minocycline demonstrating potent synergistic effects, and both being associated with a lower severe adverse event compared with DDS and clofazimine [17–23]. The initial pilot study in Brazil included MB patients treated with RIMOXCLAMIN and WHO-MDT, showing faster neurological recovery, improved sensory outcomes, and fewer AEs with new scheme [3].

The present study expands the number of patients treated with RIMOXCLAMIN and WHO-MDT, aiming to confirm the safety profile of RIMOXCLAMIN in a larger cohort and compare neurological outcomes between regimens to determine whether RIMOXCLAMIN shows improved efficacy compared with WHO-MDT.

METHODS

Study Design and Participants

A 2-arm observational cohort study with consecutive enrollment was conducted between 2015 and 2025 at the National Referral Center for Sanitary Dermatology and HD, Clinical Hospital of the Ribeirão Preto Medical School – University of São Paulo (HCFMRP-USP), including 162 new HD cases from both the public and private sectors.

Case Definition and Classification

Diagnosis was based on clinical evaluation by leprologists, in accordance with Brazilian Ministry of Health and WHO guidelines, using the recommended cardinal signs [1, 2, 6, 7]: (1) skin lesions with definite loss of sensation; (2) thickening or tenderness of peripheral nerves with sensory and/or motor impairment; and (3) positive slit-skin smear (SSS) for acid-fast bacilli. Supplementary diagnostic tools included Semmes–Weinstein monofilament (SWM) testing, peripheral nerve ultrasonography (USG-PN), electroneuromyography (ENMG), anti-Phenolic glycolipid-I (aPGL-I) enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), M. leprae specific repetitive element (RLEP) – quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), and SSS microscopy [6, 7, 24–27]. Patients were classified according to the adapted Madrid (1953) and Indian Association of Leprologists (1982) systems, and WHO operational criteria into paucibacillary (PB) and MB forms. Borderline/MB forms were defined as previously described for patients with multiple neurological abnormalities and hypopigmented skin lesions [6, 7].

Clinical and Neurological Evaluation

Patients underwent at least quarterly follow-up, including assessment of neurological symptoms, muscle strength (Medical Research Council scale [MRC]), pain intensity (Numerical Rating Scale 0–10), nerve tenderness, paresthesia/dysesthesia, SWM testing (7 points per hand, 11 points per foot), Doppler nerve USG, ENMG, and physical disability grade (PDG) according to WHO criteria: PDG-0 = no disability, PDG-1 = loss of protective sensation in hands/feet/eyes and/or muscle weakness without visible deformity, PDG-2 = visible deformity/damage of hands/feet/eyes (eg, ulcers, clawing, lagophthalmos) [4, 24, 26].

Supplementary Laboratory Tests

PGL-I serology was performed as described previously [25], with ELISA IgM index values >1.0 considered positive. RLEP-qPCR detection of M. leprae DNA was performed with cycle threshold (Ct) <45.0 considered positive [25, 27]. SSS microscopy from 4 standard sites (2 earlobes, 1 elbow, and 1 knee or skin lesion) was performed for the detection of acid-fast bacilli using Ziehl–Neelsen stain [24, 25].

Imaging and Electrophysiology

USG-PN evaluated cross-sectional area (CSA), ΔCSA (asymmetry), ΔTpT (proximal-distal focality), nerve vascularity (Doppler), and nerve echogenicity of the median, ulnar, fibular, and tibial nerves [28–35]. ENMG followed the Tomaselli et al. [33] protocol, assessing motor and sensory nerve conduction to evaluate asymmetric peripheral polyneuropathy of the mosaic axonal and demyelinating pattern [34, 35]. Multiple mononeuropathy was defined by combined abnormalities on USG-PN and/or ENMG.

Therapeutic Regimen

According to Frade et al. [3], both groups received 12 monthly doses of rifampicin 600 mg. RIMOXCLAMIN comprised moxifloxacin 400 mg and clarithromycin 500 mg daily during the first 2 months and then monthly moxifloxacin and daily clarithromycin plus minocycline 100 mg from the third month onward. Adjustments to moxifloxacin dosing were based on safety signals by the European Medicines Agency [36] and prior evidence of its bactericidal activity [23]. The WHO-MDT regimen for MB forms consisted of a 12-month course with standard dosing: rifampicin 600 mg once monthly and clofazimine 300 mg once monthly (supervised) plus DDS 100 mg and clofazimine 50 mg daily [9].

Details on supplementary laboratory tests, statistical analyses, enrollment and allocation, AEs, HD reactions and neuritis (definitions and management), and relapse were allocated to Supplementary 3.

RESULTS

Clinical and Neurological Outcomes

In the RIMOXCLAMIN group, males accounted for 34.9%, compared to 54.2% in the WHO-MDT group. The median age (interquartile range [IQR]) was 55 (44–68) years for RIMOXCLAMIN and 59 (43–66) years for WHO-MDT (Table 1). The borderline (B) clinical form was the most common, accounting for 81.5% in the RIMOXCLAMIN group and 76.3% in the WHO-MDT group (P = .37). Although no statistically significant differences were found in the socioeconomic analysis, the occupational profile indicates that the groups differ in socioeconomic composition. Occupation was the only socioeconomic factor analyzed, with homemakers comprising 2.9% (2/103) of the RIMOXCLAMIN group and 13.6% (8/59; P = .01) of the WHO-MDT group (Table 1).

Baseline case classification showed that new cases accounted for 78.6% of the RIMOXCLAMIN group and 59.3% of the WHO-MDT group (P = .008). Median time (IQR) from symptom onset to diagnosis was 36 (12–60) months for RIMOXCLAMIN and 24 (12–36) months for WHO-MDT (P = .02).

A binomial logistic regression model was used to evaluate the influence of sex, age, and symptom duration on the type of treatment received. The model demonstrated poor predictive power and fit (χ2 = 10.2; P = .017; McFadden's R2 = 0.048; area under the curve = 0.65), indicating no significant association with symptom duration (odds ratio [OR] = 0.99; 95% CI = .98–1.00; P = .095), age (OR = 0.99; 95% CI = .97–1.02; P = .66), or sex (OR = 2.11; 95% CI = 1.08–4.12; P = .028) (Supplementary 2), despite the differences observed in the univariate analyses (Table 1).

At baseline, we examined whether lesion count (≤5 vs >5) was associated with neurological involvement within each treatment arm (Table 2). This cross-sectional analysis was not designed to compare arms or timepoints; comparisons between arms and longitudinal analyses are presented in subsequent sections (eg, Figure 1; Tables 4–6). In the RIMOXCLAMIN group, 66% of patients had ≤5 skin lesions and 34% had >5 lesions, while in the WHO-MDT group, the proportions were 79.6% and 20.4%, respectively (P = .06). In both groups, when stratified by the number of skin lesions (≤5 or >5, respectively), high frequencies of neurological symptoms were observed regardless of lesion count, including numbness/tingling/cramps (RIMOXCLAMIN: 97.1% vs 94.3%; WHO-MDT: 100% both), weakness (52.9% vs 65.7%; 59.6% vs 66.7%), burning/pricking/stinging sensations (79.4% vs 80.0%; 70.2% vs 66.7%), nerve thickening (79.4% vs 82.9%; 95.7% vs 91.7%), and pain on nerve palpation (58.8% vs 62.9%; 68.1% vs 41.7%). The median (IQR) number of points with altered sensation was 4.0 (1.0–10.25) versus 5.0 (1.5–12.5) in the RIMOXCLAMIN group and 13.0 (8.0–18.0) versus 15.5 (4.3–21.0) in the WHO-MDT group. The median (IQR) number of affected nerves was 4.5 (3.0–6.0) versus 5.0 (3.5–6.0) and 6.0 (4.0–7.0) versus 6.0 (4.0–8.0), respectively. No statistically significant differences were observed in any comparison (Table 2).

In the WHO-MDT group, upper limb involvement ranged from 15.3% to 72.9%, again highest in the ulnar nerve (72.9%, 59.3%), followed by the median (32.2% bilaterally) and radial (15.3% bilaterally) nerves. Lower limb abnormalities were more frequent, with the fibular (84.7%, 89.8%) and tibial (79.7%, 74.6%) nerves most affected.

At diagnosis, no significant differences were observed between the RIMOXCLAMIN and WHO-MDT groups regarding nerve thickening (P = .095), nerve pain (P = .81), or PDG-0 status (P = .58). In the RIMOXCLAMIN group, the proportion of patients with ≥1 thickened nerve decreased from 80.6% to 21.4% at 12 months (P < .0001), and the proportion with ≥1 painful nerve decreased from 60.2% to 5.8% (P < .0001). Notably, the proportion of patients with PDG-0 increased from 18.4% to 82% (P < .0001), while the proportions with PDG-1 and PDG-2 decreased from 58.3% to 14.6% (P < .0001) and from 23.3% to 3.9% (P < .0001), respectively (Table 4).

In the WHO-MDT group, the prevalence of thickened nerves decreased from 94.9% to 44.1% at 12 months (P < .0001), and the prevalence of painful nerves decreased from 62.7% to 22.0% (P < .0001). The proportion of patients with PDG-0 increased from 22.0% to 53% (P = .0006), PDG-1 decreased from 67.8% to 42.4% (P = .005), and PDG-2 decreased from 10.2% to 5.1% (P = .49). The most pronounced intergroup differences were observed at 3 (P < .0001), 6 (P = .0008), and 12 (P = .0014) months for thickened nerves and at 6 (P = .02) and 12 (P < .0001) months for PDG-0.

A significantly greater reduction in neurological symptoms was observed with RIMOXCLAMIN at 3 months compared with that in the WHO-MDT group: numbness (94.2% to 82.5%, P = .009% vs 96.6% to 91.5%, P = .25), tingling (80.6% to 63.1%, P = .005% vs 94.9% to 89.8%, P = .29), cramps (63.1% to 38.8%, P = .0005% vs 91.5% to 79.7%, P = .06), pain (79.6% to 66%, P = .01% vs 69.5% to 66.1%, P = .55), and weakness (57.3% to 38.8%, P = .008 vs 61.0% to 62.7%, P = .84) (Table 5).

Regarding pain scale scores during follow-up, the majority of patients in the RIMOXCLAMIN group had a baseline pain score of ≥3 (85.4%). The first significant reduction occurred at 3 months (62.2%; P < .0001), followed by a decrease to 32.1% at 6 months (months 3–6, P < .0001) and 11.6% at 12 months (months 6–12, P = .0013). In the WHO-MDT group, the baseline prevalence of pain ≥3 was 96.6%, with a significant reduction observed only at 6 months (55.9%; months 3–6, P = .0009) and 44.1% at 12 months (months 6–12, P = .05). Intergroup comparisons revealed significant differences at 3 months (P = .001), 6 months (P = .01), and 12 months (P < .0001), with the RIMOXCLAMIN group demonstrating a consistently higher proportion of patients (88.3%) with pain scores between 0 and 2 compared to the WHO-MDT group (55.9%) (Table 6). Pain scores were recorded at each timepoint.

Molecular and Serological Biomarkers and Neurophysiological Evaluation

In the RIMOXCLAMIN group, 28.4% of patients had at least one positive laboratory test (RLEP-qPCR, aPGL-I, or SSS bacilloscopy), compared with 29.1% in the WHO-MDT group. RLEP-qPCR positivity was 18.1% in the RIMOXCLAMIN group and 16.4% in the WHO-MDT group, while aPGL-I was positive in 13.9% (index median = 0.4; IQR = 0.2–0.8; P = .008) and 20.0% (index median = 0.6; IQR = 0.3–0.9), respectively. Bacilloscopy was positive in 20.0% of RIMOXCLAMIN patients and 14.3% of WHO-MDT patients (Table 7).

Sensory Recovery and Improvement

In the RIMOXCLAMIN group, the prevalence of normal hand sensitivity increased from 93.2% at baseline to 96.2% at discharge (P < .0001); for the feet, it rose from 72.9% to 96.1% (P < .0001). In the WHO-MDT group, hand sensitivity increased from 77.7% to 84.0% (P = .007); for the feet, from 69.3% to 79.7% (P < .0001). Between-group analysis demonstrated significant differences favoring RIMOXCLAMIN at all time points for the feet (P < .0001) and at baseline, 3 months, and discharge for the hands (P < .0001) (Table 8).

DISCUSSION

Our study expands on the findings of the RIMOXCLAMIN pilot trial [3], now evaluating a larger and more diverse cohort while maintaining consecutive enrollment, prespecified longitudinal endpoints, standardized instruments (SWM, MRC, and PDG), prospective AE monitoring with objective thresholds, and standardized nerve ultrasound (Doppler) and ENMG at predefined visits. The longer median symptom duration before diagnosis in the RIMOXCLAMIN group underscores the persistent challenge of delayed diagnosis in HD [6, 7].

Consistent with the pilot study [3], analysis of the results confirms that operational classification based on skin lesion count fails to accurately reflect the degree of neural involvement [7, 37]. Both treatment groups exhibited a high prevalence of neurological symptoms, comparable numbers of points with altered sensation, and similar numbers of affected nerves, regardless of whether lesion counts were ≤5 or >5. These data reinforce prior calls for the revision of classification systems to integrate more sensitive and specific neurological parameters, including the number of affected nerves, points of altered sensation, and PDG [7, 38].

The pattern of nerve involvement at baseline mirrors previous observations [3], with lower limb nerves, particularly the fibular and tibial, more frequently affected than upper limb nerves [35]. Ulnar nerve involvement remained the most common in the upper limbs, confirming its high susceptibility in HD [39]. This pattern supports the continued comprehensive lower limb nerve assessment at each follow-up.

The findings highlight the superior and more rapid recovery profile with RIMOXCLAMIN. While both regimens reduced nerve thickening and pain, improvements emerged earlier and were sustained longer in the RIMOXCLAMIN group, with functional gains reflected in greater increases in PDG-0 and reductions in PDG-1 and PDG-2, representing a substantial improvement in quality of life for patients. Reinforcing this, the marked and persistent decline in PDG-2 observed exclusively with RIMOXCLAMIN supports its ability to reverse established disability, a finding of major clinical relevance [38]. RIMOXCLAMIN accelerated neurological recovery by month 3 versus delayed WHO-MDT responses. Ancillary test results again confirmed the higher diagnostic yield of USG-PN and ENMG over laboratory tests in this predominantly B HD cohort [4], underscoring their role in both baseline assessment and monitoring. Follow-up sensory assessment demonstrated objective and quantifiable sensory recovery, particularly in the feet, with greater and earlier gains in the RIMOXCLAMIN group. This supports the incorporation of SWM testing into standard monitoring protocols [7, 40].

The lower serological and bacilloscopy positivity observed in the current cohort, comprising 81.5% and 17.5% borderline and pure neural cases in the RIMOXCLAMIN group and 76.3% and 18.0% in the WHO-MDT group, respectively, likely reflects the predominance of these clinical forms. Consistent with this, previous study by Lima et al. [25] reported serological and bacilloscopy positivity rates of 45.1% and 24.1%, respectively, in a cohort with 69% borderline and 5.6% pure neural patients.

Adverse event analysis demonstrated a significantly more favorable safety profile for RIMOXCLAMIN. In the WHO-MDT group, AEs were markedly more frequent and clinically significant, often requiring treatment modifications. Notably, severe outcomes such as anemia requiring DDS substitution, along with instances of clofazimine hypersensitivity and DDS-related seizures, necessitated substantial regimen changes. Conversely, RIMOXCLAMIN-associated AEs, including nausea/vomiting, bitter taste, and hyperpigmentation, were consistently mild and self-limiting. Importantly, no patient in this arm experienced severe complications, treatment discontinuation, or anemia. These events were largely managed by adjusting the dosage of concurrent medications within the RIMOXCLAMIN protocol.

The RIMOXCLAMIN regimen permitted practical flexibility for dose adjustments or single-agent substitutions when clinically indicated, without adding new drugs, while maintaining the bactericidal backbone [4, 10–13]. This inherent adaptability, coupled with its superior functional recovery outcomes, underscores RIMOXCLAMIN's distinct advantage over the current standard regimen, improving patient adherence and safety [17, 18, 23].

RIMOXCLAMIN's drugs are widely used against rapidly growing mycobacteria, nontuberculous mycobacteria, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex [41]. RIMOXCLAMIN combines multiple bactericidal drug classes, enhancing therapeutic potential, whereas rifampicin is the only drug with bactericidal activity against M. leprae in WHO-MDT [42]. We do not recommend extrapolating these results to other mycobacterial diseases; in HD, this combination is supported by its bactericidal profile and contribution to neurological recovery [43].

Thus, clinical improvement in the study patients was clearly evident upon completion of the 12-dose regimen, highlighting that complete neurological recovery is unattainable after just 6 months. Regarding limitations, the study lacked patient follow-up after treatment discontinuation, preventing the monitoring of high bacterial loads and positive results on specific laboratory tests.

Limitations

Differences at baseline are consistent with nonrandomized, availability-based allocation; we prespecified a within-arm longitudinal analysis across multiple neurological endpoints; tested covariates demonstrated no significant association among socioeconomic or sociodemographic factors and key outcomes. However, the impact of other socioeconomic variables on AEs warrants assessment in future studies.

Baseline imbalances, including a greater number of points with altered tactile sensation in the WHO-MDT arm, reflect allocation based on availability and consecutive enrollment in a nonrandomized setting. Consequently, our primary inferences rely on within-arm trajectories and a range of neurological outcomes (PDG, neuritis, pain, and nerve palpation findings), rather than a single baseline metric.

Long-Term Outcomes and Relapse

This analysis is limited to on-treatment (0–12 months) outcomes. Treatment failure and relapse generally require ≥5 years of follow-up and were outside the scope of the present study, especially given the large proportion of patients treated in 2022–2024. Although AEs may influence adherence, our dataset was not designed to capture adherence patterns beyond AE-driven regimen changes and documentation of supervised monthly dosing.

While multicenter studies are desirable to enhance external validity, the consistency and magnitude of the present findings provide guidance for clinical decision-making in comparable settings. Additionally, future analyses should incorporate logistic regression models and Kaplan–Meier curves.

It is important to note that the drugs used in the RIMOXCLAMIN regimen were obtained either privately or through donations from the patients themselves due to their cost. Because this is a neglected disease, we did not receive financial support from the Ministry of Health for this research.

Safety and Interactions

As with clofazimine, given the interaction profiles of moxifloxacin and clarithromycin (including QT prolongation), use was supervised by a specialist, with written guidance provided to patients and pharmacists.

CONCLUSION

The expanded dataset presented here solidifies RIMOXCLAMIN's position as a safe, effective, and clinically beneficial regimen for HD. Its superior and more rapid neurological recovery, ability to reverse disability, and minimal AEs support its potential as a future standard of care.

Based on our findings, we recommend the following: (1) Initiating national and international dialogues to evaluate the feasibility of implementing RIMOXCLAMIN as a first-line regimen, prioritizing safety and the recovery of sensory function. (2) Conducting multicenter trials encompassing tuberculoid and lepromatous forms and monitoring therapeutic response in MB patients with positive SSS microscopy using qPCR for DNA detection or reverse transcriptase-PCR for RNA viability markers. (3) Performing controlled, head-to-head studies in closely matched patient cohorts to definitively assess the comparative therapeutic efficacy of RIMOXCLAMIN versus WHO-MDT. These real-world data warrant investigation in controlled settings and justify multicenter, controlled trials to confirm effectiveness and optimize implementation.
