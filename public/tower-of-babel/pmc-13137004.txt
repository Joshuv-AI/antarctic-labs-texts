Timing and Trends of Periprosthetic Joint Infections Following Over 1,500,000 Primary and Revision Total Knee and Hip Arthroplasty Cases
Authors: Maxwell T Trudeau, Eric Wang, Olivia Schaffer, Vinay Aggarwal, Joshua C Rozell, Ran Schwarzkopf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) following primary and revision total knee arthroplasty (rTKA) and revision total hip arthroplasty (rTHA) have been studied, but a contemporary large-scale analysis of incidence has not been performed. This study assessed PJI incidence and trends for TKA, THA, rTKA, and rTHA using a contemporary database.MethodsEpic Cosmos database was retrospectively queried (2015-2023). Current procedureal terminology codes identified patients: 27447 (TKA), 27130 (THA), 27486 (rTKA one component), 27487 (rTKA both components), 27134 (rTHA both components), 27137 (rTHA acetabular component), and 27138 (rTHA femoral component). PJI rates were identified using International Classification of Diseases 9/10 codes and analyzed using linear regression.ResultsA total of 1,658,742 cases were identified: 962,967 TKA, 600,225 THA, 59,212 rTKA, and 36,338 rTHA. PJI rates for TKA were 0.25% (30 days) and 1.19% (1 year). PJI rates for THA were 0.46% (30 days) and 1.28% (1 year). One-year PJI rates from 2015 to 2023 decreased 17% for TKA and 40% for THA (P = .001). The 1-year PJI rate for rTKA (all types) was 6.51%, differing between one-component (9.34%) and both components (4.68%) (P = .001). The 1-year PJI rate for rTHA (all types) was 8.78%, differing between both-components (8.79%), acetabular component (8.62%), and femoral component (8.88%) (P = .01). One-year PJI rates were not correlated with year of surgery for rTKA or rTHA (P = .43, P = .08).ConclusionsSignificant PJI reduction after primary TKA and THA was observed over the last decade, but infection rates remained stable in revision cohorts. Further investigations are required to assess underlying reasons for observed PJI rate reductions following primary arthroplasty.Keywords: Periprosthetic joint infection, Total knee arthroplasty, Total hip arthroplasty, Total joint arthroplasty, Revision total joint arthroplasty, Cosmos

Introduction

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) occurs in approximately 0.5-2.4% of primary total knee arthroplasties (TKAs) and total hip arthroplasties (THAs), respectively, with rates rising sharply to as high as 10% in revision procedures (rTKA and rTHA). Recent registry data from the American Joint Replacement Registry show that infection remains the most common indication for revision arthroplasty, accounting for 33.1% of revision TKAs and 21.4% of revision THAs between 2012 and 2023 [1]. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes the prevalence approximates 2% at 10 years post-implantation for both joints, and the frequency is expected to increase proportionally to the rising volume of arthroplasty procedures [2]. Key risk factors for infection include male sex, younger age, diabetes, obesity, rheumatoid arthritis, poor nutritional status, prior joint infection, and perioperative factors such as prolonged operative time and blood transfusion [[2], [3], [4]]. PJI is associated with substantial morbidity, including extended hospitalizations, high rates of disability, decreased quality of life, and increased mortality compared to noninfected arthroplasties [5]. The economic burden is profound, with annual direct costs in the United States projected to exceed $1.85 billion by 2030, driven by high per-case management costs and increasing procedure volume [6].

Commonly adopted protocols to improve the rates of PJI in both primary and revision TKA and THA have focused on evidence-based perioperative strategies. These include preoperative Staphylococcus aureus screening with targeted nasal and skin decolonization, optimization of patient comorbidities, and standardized antiseptic skin preparation protocols, as well as timely administration of prophylactic antibiotics—most commonly cefazolin within 60 minutes before incision, as recommended by the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons [5,7]. Intraoperative measures such as meticulous surgical technique, environmental controls, and judicious use of antiseptic lavage (eg, dilute povidone-iodine) have also been adopted, though the benefit of some modalities (eg, vancomycin powder, antimicrobial drapes) and extended prophylactic postoperative antibiotics remains under investigation [5]. Lastly, the use of tranexamic acid has also played a substantial role in reducing perioperative and postoperative bleeding and transfusion requirements, and the adoption of aspirin for deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis has further reduced the risk of additional bleeding associated with traditional anticoagulants [[8], [9], [10]].

Considering these advancements and paradigm changes, a more contemporary analysis of the incidence and trends of PJI is warranted. This study aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of PJI incidence and trends across primary and revision TKA and THA utilizing a large-scale analysis. It was hypothesized that PJI after primary and revision TKA and THA would demonstrate a decreased incidence across the study time frame.

Material and methods

The Epic Cosmos database was used to perform a retrospective analysis. Epic Cosmos is a large, cloud-based healthcare data platform that aggregates deidentified patient records from participating health systems using the Epic electronic health record system. It enables large-scale data analysis, research, and artificial inteligence development by providing access to over 300 million patient records across approximately 800 hospitals and 10,000 clinics [11]. In accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, institutional review board approval was not needed as all patient data were deidentified. The following current procedural terminology (CPT) codes were used to identify patients from 2015-2023 for inclusion: 27130 (THA), 27447 (TKA), 27134 (rTHA both components), 27137 (rTHA acetabular component), 27138 (rTHA femoral component), 27486 (rTKA one component), and 27487 (rTKA both components). The primary outcome variable, PJI, was identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9/10 codes (T84.51, infection and inflammatory reaction due to internal right hip prosthesis; T84.52, infection and inflammatory reaction due to internal left hip prosthesis; T84.53, infection and inflammatory reaction due to internal right knee prosthesis; T84.54, infection and inflammatory reaction due to internal left knee prosthesis; T84.59, infection and inflammatory reaction due to other internal joint prosthesis). All rTHA and rTKA cases with a previous diagnosis of PJI prior to the revision procedure, identified using the previously described ICD-9/10 codes, were excluded. Patient demographic variables including age, legal sex, race, and additional comorbidities were also collected.

Statistical analysis

All database queries and statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.0.2, R foundation, 2022). Statistical significance was defined as P < .05. PJI rates between various procedures were compared using chi-squared analysis. PJI timing was assessed, and year-by-year trends were analyzed using linear regression.

Results

Demographics

From 2015 to 2023, a total of 1,658,742 cases were identified from the Epic Cosmos database with 962,967 TKA, 600,225 THA, 59,212 rTKA, and 36,338 rTHA.

The prevalence of female patients in the cohort for TKA and THA were 60.2% and 52.9%, respectively, and there were 51.7% and 57.4% in the cohort for rTKA and rTHA, respectively. In all cohorts, over 70% of patients were between 50 and 80 years of age. Across all 4 procedures, most patients were White (85.6, 87.3, 70.3, and 89.1% for TKA, THA, rTKA, and rTHA) or Black (10.1, 10.1, 12.7, and 8.8% for TKA, THA, rTKA, and rTHA). The prevalence of obesity among varying procedures was 49.4, 38.1, 44.3, and 33.0% in TKA, THA, rTKA, and rTHA, respectively.

Primary TKA/THA PJI rates and trends

From 2015 to 2023, 962,967 TKA and 600,225 THA cases were identified. The associated PJI rates for TKA were 0.25% for 30 days, 0.69% for 90 days, and 1.19% for 1 year. The associated PJI rates for THA were 0.46% for 30 days, 0.90% for 90 days, and 1.28% for 1 year (Table 1). The majority of PJIs occurred within 90 days for both TKA (57.9%) and THA (71%). The 1-year PJI rate for TKA from 2015-2023 progressed as follows: 1.44, 1.58, 1.42, 1.44, 1.34, 1.27, 1.25, 1.24, and 1.23%; there was a 17% reduction in TKA PJI between 2015 and 2023 (P = .001). The 1-year PJI rate for THA from 2015-2023 progressed as follows: 1.36, 1.39, 1.24, 1.25, 1.13, 1.02, 1.01, 0.95, and 0.97%; there was a 40% reduction in THA PJI between 2015 and 2023 (Fig. 1, P < .001).

Revision TKA/THA PJI rates and trends

From 2015-2023, 59,212 rTKA and 36,338 rTHA cases were identified. The associated PJI rates for rTKA (all types) were 3.26% for 30 days, 4.83% for 90 days, and 6.51% for 1 year. The 1-year PJI rate differed between rTKA one component (27486) at 9.34% and rTKA both components (27487) at 4.68% (P < .001). The associated PJI rates for rTHA (all types) were 4.80% for 30 days, 6.86% for 90 days, and 8.78% for 1 year (Table 2). The 1-year PJI rate differed between rTHA both components (27134) at 8.79%, rTHA acetabular component (27137) at 8.62%, and rTHA femoral component (27138) at 8.88% (P = .01). The 1-year PJI rate between 2015 and 2023 was not correlated with the year of surgery and did not show a significant change in either rTKA (all types) or rTHA (all types) (Fig. 2, P = .43, P = .08, respectively).

Discussion

The major findings of this study demonstrate a significant reduction in PJI rates following primary TKA and THA over the last decade, with 1-year PJI rates declining by 17% for TKA and 40% for THA between 2015 and 2023. In contrast, PJI rates following rTKA and rTHA remain substantially higher and did not show significant improvement over the same period. The majority of PJIs occurred within 90 days postoperatively for both primary and revision procedures.

When compared to current literature, the observed 1-year PJI rates for primary TKA (1.19%) and THA (1.28%) in this study are consistent with large population-based cohort studies and national registry data, which report cumulative incidences ranging from 0.5%-2.4% for primary procedures [3,4,[12], [13], [14]]. Recent studies have shown that most PJIs are diagnosed within the first 2 years postoperatively, with a steady but modest decline in incidence over time for primary TKA and THA [15]. For example, Karlsen et al. reported a 1-year reoperation rate for PJI after primary THA of 1.2%, with a corresponding decline in surgical site infection and PJI rates between 2013 and 2022 [13]. Similarly, Chiu et al. demonstrated a national reduction in 2-year septic revision rates for TKA, including high-risk patients, suggesting that contemporary preventive strategies may be effective on a population level [15]. However, other studies, such as Murphy et al. and Kurtz et al. have found only marginal or no significant changes in PJI rates over time, despite widespread adoption of evidence-based prevention protocols [14,16]. The present study’s finding of a marked reduction in PJI rates for primary procedures may reflect improved perioperative optimization, infection control measures, and patient selection, but also highlights ongoing variability in reported trends across different datasets.

In contrast, revision TKA and THA are associated with markedly higher PJI rates, with the present study reporting 1-year rates of 6.51% for rTKA and 8.78% for rTHA, and no significant reduction in rates over the past decade. These findings are in line with broader literature, which consistently demonstrates that revision procedures carry a substantially increased risk of PJI, often exceeding 5-10% depending on patient factors and procedure complexity [3,17]. Perni et al. found that the risk of developing PJI after a revision hip or knee replacement was approximately 4 times greater than primary arthroplasty [17]. The lack of improvement in PJI rates for revision procedures, as observed in both the cosmos study and other large database analyses, underscores the persistent challenges in infection prevention and management in this high risk population [3]. However, it must be noted that a major limitation of the Cosmos database is the reliance on proper documentation of PJI, while this analysis attempted to exclude revision cases completed for PJI, it is possible that PJI was not listed as a diagnosis in these patient charts and exclusion criteria may fail to eliminate these cases. This would result in an inflated associated PJI rate calculated for revision cases.

The observed differences in 1-year PJI rates between rTKA one component (9.34%) and rTKA both components (4.68%) likely reflect differences in patient selection, surgical complexity, and underlying risk factors. One-component revisions may be performed in patients with higher comorbidity burden, leading to an increased risk of subsequent PJI and readmission for infection [18,19]. Additionally, less extensive debridement and retention of potentially colonized components in patients with undiagnosed subclinical infection may contribute to higher PJI rates compared to more comprehensive two-component exchanges [18]. For rTHA, while a statistically significant difference was found (P = .01), the absolute differences in 1-year PJI rates across both component (8.79%), acetabular (8.62%), and femoral (8.88%) revisions were small and suggest that the risk of infection is high regardless of which component is revised, likely due to the shared presence of patient-related risk factors and the technical challenges inherent to rTHA [5,20].

Limitations of this study include the retrospective design and reliance of administrative coding within the Epic Cosmos database, which may be subject to miscoding and misclassification of procedures and outcomes. Although the database aggregates a large volume of deidentified patient records, variability in coding practices and completeness of data across participating instructions may introduce bias. CPT code 27132 for conversion THA was not included in this analysis, as well as the 27488 (removal of knee prosthesis) and 27091 (removal of hip prosthesis), which may result in a decreased calculated PJI rate. Furthermore, when analyzing PJI based on CPT codes, we attempted to exclude cases with previous PJI. However, if the presence of prior PJI was not properly documented in the patient’s chart, it was not possible to ensure identification of exclusively index PJI occurring after these procedures. For THA, there is no single head-liner exchange code so the revisions get diluted among 3 codes (27138, 27137, 27134). Additionally, the study does not account for potential confounding factors such as perioperative antibiotic protocols, surgical technique, or patient-level risk stratification. As with other large database studies, the generalizability of these findings may be limited by the absence of granular clinical detail and the potential for residual confounding. Epic Cosmos database itself also introduces a potential selection and referral bias, as it is predominantly used by large healthcare systems and academic centers. Further prospective studies and registry-based analyses are warranted to elucidate the mechanisms underlying observed trends and to identify effective strategies for further reducing PJI, particularly in revision cases.

Conclusions

This large-scale analysis demonstrates a significant reduction in PJI rates following primary TKA and THA over the past decade, aligning with recent data showing modest but meaningful declines in PJI. However, PJI rates after revision procedures remain substantially higher and have not shown similar improvement, underscoring the persistent complexity and risk associated with revision arthroplasty. These findings highlight the need for continued innovation and targeted interventions to further address the ongoing burden of PJI.
