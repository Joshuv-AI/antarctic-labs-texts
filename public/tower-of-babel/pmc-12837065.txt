Evaluating the effect of systemic hypertension on retinal, optic nerve head microvasculature and field of vision using optical coherence tomography angiography and visual field analyzer
Authors: Salma Maged Mohammed Mohammed El-Maghrabi, Rafik Mohamed Fouad El Ghazawy, Hisham Mohamed Khairy Abdel Dayem, Al Hussein Aly Mohamed Swelem, Passant Sayed Saif
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the effect of systemic hypertension on retinal and peripapillary vascular density and visual field (VF) deviation.MethodsThis prospective observational case-control study included 136 eyes, 68 eyes of 34 patients with hypertension (HTN), and 68 eyes of 34 healthy controls. HTN patients: group 1: ≤5 years HTN duration was less than 5years and group 2: HTN > 5 years. All participants were assessed using OCTA quantitative (vessel density (VD), foveal avascular zone (FAZ) area, superficial capillary plexus (SCP), deep capillary plexus (DCP), choriocapillaris (CC), radial peripapillary capillaries (RPC)) and 24 − 2 glaucoma hemifield tests. The results were recorded and analyzed.ResultsMacula OCTA revealed a reduction in foveal and total VD in the SCP and DCP in HTN groups 1 and 2. There was a reduction in the inferior CC layer in the HTN group2, enlarged FAZ area and perimeter were demonstrated with reduced circularity. in DCP in HTN. Humphrey visual field (HFA) testing in HTN group 1 and 2. revealed that visual field defects occurred more often in group 2 patients with longer HTN duration than in group 1 and controls.ConclusionThe association between the duration of systemic hypertension and the diminution in vessel density, VF deviation, and defects justifies the verification of longitudinal studies. OCTA and visual field examinations are tools for monitoring systemic hypertension.Keywords: Hypertension, Optical coherence tomography angiography, Visual field analyzer

Introduction

Systemic hypertension is a growing global public health concern, with an associated increased risk of coronary heart disease, stroke, and end-stage renal disease. It involves anatomical and functional changes in microcirculation throughout the body, including microvascular rarefaction and reduced circulatory efficiency, contributing to organ dysfunction [1].

The human eye provides a unique window for direct observation of the in vivo microvasculature using noninvasive optical techniques. Owing to anatomical and physiological similarities, retinal microvasculature has been used as a biomarker for systemic vascular disorders [1].

Moreover, the continuous improvement in retinal optical coherence tomography (OCT) and the development of OCT angiography (OCTA), a device based on the motion contrast of blood cells that provides depth-resolved and high-resolution images of retinal and choroidal vessels in seconds without the need for intravenous contrast agents such as fluorescein [2], has enabled documentation of hypertensive retinopathy (HR) stages in development. Narrowing of retinal arteries, arteriovenous crossings, the formation of retinal microaneurysms, fundus hemorrhages, hard exudates, and cotton-wool spots, or a combination of these signs may occur in hypertensive retinopathy [3].

Swept-source OCTA (SS-OCTA), an instruments system with longer center wavelength (1050 nm), has led to better visualization of the choroid [1]. and can easily assess retinal blood flow based on various retinal and choroidal microvascular parameters, such as vessel density (VD), choriocapillaris flow area, foveal avascular zone (FAZ) area, and optic nerve head (ONH) capillary density [3, 4].

Visual field assessment using a Humphrey field analyzer (HFA) is the “gold” standard for assessing visual field loss [5] and can evaluate the progression of functional complications of systemic diseases.

This study aimed to assess the effect of systemic hypertension on the macula, retinal, and peripapillary vascular density and glaucoma hemifield of vision testing using Optical Coherence Tomography Angiography and 24 − 2 Visual Field Analyzer.

We hypothesized that systemic hypertension, particularly of longer duration, is associated with significant reductions in OCTA-derived vessel density across the superficial capillary plexus (SCP), deep capillary plexus (DCP), choriocapillaris (CC), and radial peripapillary capillaries (RPC), as well as enlargement of FAZ parameters. Furthermore, we anticipated that these structural changes would correlate with measurable alterations in visual field indices.

The primary objective of this study was to quantitatively assess the impact of systemic hypertension and its duration on macular and peripapillary vascular density and FAZ parameters using swept-source OCTA, and to evaluate the functional consequences on visual field indices.

Understanding the microvascular consequences of systemic hypertension in the retina and optic nerve head is of clinical relevance for early detection and monitoring of hypertensive end-organ damage. Demonstrating structure–function correlations between OCTA findings and visual field indices may support the role of OCTA as a noninvasive biomarker in the management of systemic hypertension and its ocular complications.

Patients and methods

This prospective observational case-control study was conducted on participants recruited from the ophthalmology outpatient clinic of the Misr University for Science and Technology University Hospital, Giza, Egypt. This study included 136 eyes from 68 participants. 68 eyes of 34 HTN patients and 68 eyes of 34 healthy subjects. Patients with HTN were divided into two groups: group 1,32 eyes of 16 patients for less than 5 years HTN duration and group 2,36 eyes of 18 patients with HTN duration > 5 years. (the 5-year cutoff was based on previous epidemiological studies [1, 3] where duration > 5 years was associated with more advanced vascular remodeling and end-organ effects). Data was collected from July 2023 to May 2025. The secondary outcome measure was the statistical correlation of patient data, hypertension data, and treatment factors.

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Ain Shams University, Cairo, Egypt (approval # FMASU MS 363/2023). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The inclusion criteria for cases were an age of 40 years or more and an established diagnosis of primary hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg) based on the World Health Organization (WHO) included in the study [6]. The inclusion criteria for the controls were healthy normotensive subjects aged ≥ 40 years.

The exclusion criteria were the presence of other ocular and/or retinal diseases (age-related macular degeneration, retinal vascular occlusion, uveitis, and glaucoma), history of systemic disease that could affect retinal microcirculation, history of vitreoretinal surgery, and media opacities that may compromise the image.

Age, sex, and medical history were recorded for each participant. The duration of HTN and the number and type of antihypertensive medications were also noted. Each participant underwent comprehensive ophthalmic examination: uncorrected (UCVA), best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) tested with logMAR visual acuity chart; anterior and posterior segment examination; SS-OCT and OCTA with SS-OCT, DRI OCT Triton (Topcon, Tokyo, Japan); visual field examination using Humphery field analyzer (HFA) (Carl Zeiss Meditec) model (1.5.3.714) with Swedish Interactive Threshold Algorithm SITA- Standard 24 − 2.

Hypertensive retinopathy grading was performed using the Wong and Mitchell classification (2004) [7] into Grades 1–3, based on fundus examination.

Blood pressure was measured using a standardized mercury sphygmomanometer.

OCT analysis, a linear B-scan centered on the fovea was performed in all participants. The central macular thickness (CMT) and choroidal subfoveal thickness (CST) were measured. CMT was defined as the distance between the internal limiting membrane (ILM) and Bruch’s membrane (BM) at the fovea, and CST was defined as the distance between the BM and the sclerochoroidal junction below the fovea.

OCTA imaging was performed in the macular region with both 3 × 3 mm and 4.5 × 4.5 mm OCTA scans were used because the 3 mm scan provides higher resolution for central macular microvasculature and FAZ analysis, while the 4.5 mm scan captures a broader parafoveal and perifoveal area, allowing assessment of more extensive vascular changes. Including both enhances reliability and completeness of microvascular evaluation. SS-OCT Triton uses a 1,050-nm wavelength and has a scanning speed of 100,000 A-scans/s.

OCTA images were evaluated using the automatic layer segmentation software IMAGEnet provided by the device manufacturer. Image quality was assessed for all OCTA scans; poor quality images defined as scans with a signal strength index (SSI) < 35 were excluded from the analysis. The segmentation boundaries used by the Topcon IMAGEnet software (SCP: ILM to 15.6 μm below IPL; DCP: 15.6–70.2 μm below IPL; CC: BM to 20.8 μm below BM). These are device-standard default settings, widely used in published studies [3, 4] (the layer-based segmentation could be more accurate and the axial length and refractive status (all patients were phakic) were considered to reduce inter-subject variability.)

We defined the choriocapillaris (CC) as the slab between 0 μm at the BM and 20,8 μm under the BM. The superficial capillary plexus (SCP) included the slab between 0 μm at the internal limiting membrane (ILM) and 15,6 μm under the inner plexiform layer (IPL). The deep capillary plexus (DCP) was the slab with upper and lower boundaries 15,6 μm and 70,2 μm below the IPL, respectively.

Vessel density (VD) (expressed as %) and foveal avascular zone (FAZ) parameters, including (FAZ area) (expressed in mm²), (FAZ perimeter) (expressed in mm), and (FAZ circularity) were measured in each participant. VD was provided by the IMAGEnet software of the device. VD is inferred from the decorrelation motion contrast signal provided by OCTA. The instrument automatically applied an early treatment diabetic retinopathy study (ETDRS) grid overlaying an external ring of 2,5 mm in diameter and a central area of 1 mm in diameter. The external ring comprised the superior, inferior, nasal, and temporal quadrants. Parafoveal VD was the mean value of the four quadrants of the external ring. The total VD was the average of the five ETDRS subfields.

OCTA imaging was performed on the peripapillary region using a scan of 4,5 mm × 4,5 mm centered on the optic disc. The radial peripapillary capillary (RPC) network was automatically separated using the OCT instrument software IMAGEnet, extending from the internal limiting membrane (ILM) to the posterior boundary of the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL). Image quality was assessed for all OCTA scans; poor quality images defined as scans with a signal strength index (SSI) < 35 were excluded from the analysis.

The capillary density in the RPC network was quantitatively assessed as the percentage of the measurement area occupied by vessels. The software automatically fitted the inner circle to the optic disc according to the ETDRS grid. The four areas of the RPC are superior, inferior, nasal, and temporal quadrants, dividing the center of the optic disc were automatically displayed. The mean RPC value was calculated as the average of the values obtained for the four RPC areas.

All participants underwent visual field examination using a Humphrey field analyzer (HFA) (Carl Zeiss Meditec) model (1.5.3.714) with the Swedish Interactive Threshold Algorithm SITA-Standard 24 − 2. Appropriate near refraction was used. Only reliable visual field tests and OCTA data were entered into the master table. Unreliable HFA tests that recorded false-positive or false-negative rates > 33% or fixation losses > 33%) were repeated.

Recorded data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences, version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). Quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation and ranges when their distribution was parametric (normal), while non-normally distributed variables (non-parametric data) were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). Qualitative variables were presented as numbers and percentages. Data were explored for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests.

Correlations between continuous variables (e.g., OCTA parameters, HR grades, visual field indices) were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation test (for ordinal or non-normally distributed data) or Pearson’s correlation test (for normally distributed continuous variables), as appropriate. Correlation strength was reported as correlation coefficient (r or ρ) with corresponding p-values.”

Discussion

This study evaluated the effect of systemic hypertension on retinal and peripapillary microvasculature and visual field function using swept-source optical coherence tomography angiography (SS-OCTA) and Humphrey visual field analysis. Our results demonstrate significant alterations in microvascular parameters, particularly in relation to the duration of hypertension, with functional repercussions evident on visual field indices. In the following, we discuss our findings in the context of retinal and choroidal vascular physiology and pathology, and compare them with previous literature.

Superficial capillary plexus (SCP)

The SCP is located within the nerve fiber and ganglion cell layers and is nourished by central retinal artery branches. It possesses robust autoregulatory capacity mediated by local metabolic and myogenic mechanisms, independent of autonomic innervation [7, 8]. Despite this autoregulation, our results showed a significant reduction in both foveal and total SCP vessel density (VD) in hypertensive patients, especially with longer disease duration.

Chronic hypertension leads to endothelial dysfunction, increased vascular tone, and eventual capillary dropout or rarefaction 2. Multiple OCTA studies have confirmed SCP rarefaction in hypertensive cohorts, including hospital-based and community samples [3, 9]. Population-based OCTA normative work shows that VD varies with age, sex, and refractive status [10], underscoring the need for covariate adjustment (as applied here with SE). A 2021 JAHA study further linked lower retinal capillary density in hypertension to adverse cardiac remodeling, suggesting SCP metrics may reflect systemic microvascular burden [11].

Not all studies report uniform SCP involvement. Some found minimal SCP change when hypertension was well controlled or cohorts were younger [8, 9]. Methodologic factors—including OCTA model, scan size, segmentation, and thresholding—contribute to heterogeneity, and should be considered when comparing results across studies.

Deep capillary plexus (DCP)

The DCP lies in the inner nuclear layer, a metabolically demanding region with relatively lower autoregulatory reserve than the SCP [10, 12]. It is particularly susceptible to hypoxic injury. In our cohort, DCP VD was significantly reduced in parafoveal and total regions in hypertensive eyes, more prominently with longer hypertension duration.

Several independent studies support predominant DCP vulnerability in hypertension. Peng et al. (2020) [13] and Xu et al. (2021) [1] reported parafoveal DCP loss, while a recent TVST analysis in referable diabetic retinopathy showed that concomitant hypertension exacerbates DCP ischemia and vision loss; importantly, geometric perfusion deficit (GPD) and vessel length density (VLD) outperformed simple VD for detecting DCP injury [14]. These findings align with the concept that DCP, given its location and oxygen demand, is an early locus of hypertensive microvascular compromise.

Discrepancies across reports likely reflect differences in hypertension duration and control, comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, dyslipidemia), and image quality. A prospective study in hypertensive patients with uncontrolled dyslipidemia found accentuated macular microvascular impairment on OCTA, supporting a cumulative vascular risk effect [15].

Foveal avascular zone (FAZ)

The FAZ is an avascular region dependent on the surrounding capillary network. Enlargement of the FAZ area and perimeter, and reduced circularity, are markers of perifoveal ischemia. In our study, FAZ parameters were significantly abnormal in hypertensive patients, especially in the DCP layer.

FAZ enlargement in hypertension has been documented in several cohorts [16–19]. However, findings are not universal; Remolí Sargues et al.2 reported no FAZ difference, potentially due to segmentation approaches and younger, well-controlled subjects. Given the sensitivity of FAZ metrics to scaling and segmentation, studies increasingly recommend reporting both area and morphologic descriptors (perimeter, acircularity) to improve robustness.

Choriocapillaris (CC)

The CC supplies the outer retina and photoreceptors and is partially regulated by autonomic input rather than local autoregulation [20]. It has high flow but is vulnerable to systemic hemodynamic changes. In our study, inferior CC VD was significantly reduced in patients with longer hypertension duration.

SS-OCTA analyses focusing on CC flow deficits (CFD) have shown greater CFD area and density in hypertensive individuals, particularly with poor blood pressure control, and correlations with renal parameters [19, 20]. Recent work in renal hypertension similarly demonstrated reduced retinal and conjunctival vascular density on OCTA [21], reinforcing the systemic nature of choroidal microvascular compromise. Differences across studies may reflect wavelength (SS vs. SD), CC slab definition, and compensation for signal attenuation; harmonized CC analytics are essential for cross-study comparability.

Radial peripapillary capillaries (RPC)

The RPC network, located around the optic disc, nourishes the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) and is critical for maintaining visual function. It has limited collateral circulation and is susceptible to ischemic injury in systemic vascular disease [15, 22]. We observed a significant reduction in superior RPC VD in hypertensive patients, particularly in those with longer disease duration.

Large-sample studies confirm that RPC density is sensitive to vascular and pressure-related stressors and varies by device and algorithm. A multicenter comparison found poor absolute agreement in RPC metrics across four OCTA devices, underscoring the need to interpret between-study differences cautiously [23, 24]. In metabolic disease, hypertension superimposed on type 2 diabetes was associated with further peripapillary VD loss [25]. Together, these data support our observed superior-quadrant susceptibility and the structure–function correspondence with inferior field loss.

Visual field changes and antihypertensive medications

Visual field indices (MD, PSD, VFI) worsened with longer hypertension duration and higher hypertensive retinopathy grade. Regression analysis including age, spherical equivalent (as a surrogate for axial length), and antihypertensive medication type revealed a modest trend toward better VF indices in patients receiving calcium channel blockers (CCBs), although statistical significance was not reached.

The link between microvascular rarefaction and functional loss is supported by longitudinal glaucoma cohorts in which faster macular and peripapillary VD loss predicts subsequent VF decline [26]. Pharmacologically, CCBs have been proposed to enhance ocular perfusion [26, 27]. Although evidence specific to systemic hypertension without glaucoma remains limited, our trend parallels these mechanistic insights and merits testing in larger, medication-stratified cohorts.

Confounding by spherical equivalent (SE)

A significant difference in spherical equivalent (SE) was observed among the three groups. Since SE reflects axial length and retinal magnification, it may influence OCTA-derived vessel density and FAZ measurements. Eyes with greater hyperopia generally have shorter axial lengths, potentially leading to an apparent increase in vessel density due to image scaling, whereas myopic eyes may show reduced densities. To account for this, SE was incorporated into our regression models as a covariate, minimizing its confounding effect. Additionally, all participants were phakic, which reduced variability related to pseudophakia and optical aberrations. Nevertheless, residual confounding by refractive status cannot be entirely excluded; direct axial length and axial length–corrected scaling (e.g., Littmann/Bennett corrections) should be incorporated in future work, alongside device-specific magnification factors and normative databases.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

Strengths of this study include the use of SS-OCTA with high-resolution layer-specific analysis, inclusion of functional visual field testing, and stratification by hypertension duration. Limitations include the cross-sectional design, modest sample size—particularly for grade 3 hypertensive retinopathy—possible segmentation errors, and lack of full adjustment for systemic comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, dyslipidemia) and lifestyle factors such as smoking, which is a known confounder for choroidal vasculature [8]. The potential influence of different antihypertensive drug classes was not fully controlled.

Future research should employ longitudinal designs, larger samples, multimodal imaging, and stratification by medication type to better elucidate the relationship between systemic hypertension, retinal microvasculature, and visual function.

Conclusion

There was an association between duration of systemic hypertension and diminution in retinal vessel density accompanied with abnormal FAZ parameters in the form of increased area and perimeter; and decreased circularity index especially in the DCP.

An association between development of visual field defects and longer duration of systemic hypertension was demonstrated, suggesting that there is a correlation between OCTA retinal vascular parameters in different layers with visual field parameters.

Both OCTA and visual field examination could be used as a potential tool for the follow up of systemic hypertension end- organ damage. Nonetheless, larger and prospective studies are needed to verify these findings.
