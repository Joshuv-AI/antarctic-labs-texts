Resolution of the Borel–Kolmogorov Paradox via the Maximum Entropy Principle
Raphaël Trésor Mykola Lukashchuk

Abstract
This paper presents a rigorous resolution of the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox using the Maximum Entropy Principle. We construct a metric-based framework for Bayesian inference that uniquely extends conditional probability to events of null measure. The results unify classical Bayes’ rules and provide a robust foundation for Bayesian inference in metric spaces.



1
Introduction

Conditional probability of an event
B
given an event
A
is classically defined as

\mathbb{P}\left(B\mid A\right)\vcentcolon=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(B\cap A\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)}\,,

(1.1)

provided that
\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)>0

[
Kolmogorov 1956
, Chapter 1.4]
.
This formulation constitutes the core of Bayes’ rule, also known as Bayesian conditionalization, which updates a prior probability measure to reflect new information.
When an event
A
is known to occur almost surely, Bayes’ rule prescribes replacing the prior with the conditional probability measure defined above.

However, in many practical and theoretical contexts, the event
A
of interest has null measure under the prior, rendering the classical definition (
1.1
) inapplicable.
A canonical example arises when
A
corresponds to the observation of a continuous random variable
Y
taking a specific value
\hat{y}
.

Attempts to generalize Bayes’ rule to the case
\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)=0
have yielded either multiple and conflicting definitions or concluded that such an extension is inherently ill-posed.
This leads to the well-known Borel–Kolmogorov paradox, which highlights the ambiguity and inconsistency in extending conditional probability to events of measure zero.

Given that the primary utility of conditional probability lies in its role within Bayes’ rule, the existence of multiple Bayes posteriors for the same pair
(\mathbb{P},A)
raises significant concerns about the coherence of Bayesian inference, as highlighted by
Meehan and Zhang 2021
,
Rescorla 2015
,
Arnold and Roberston 2003
,
Bordley 2015
.

In this work, we seek a principled and mathematically rigorous framework that yields a unique conditional probability formula for each probabilistic model.
Departing from purely measure-theoretic approaches, we incorporate geometric considerations, following the insights of
Borel 1909
and
Gyenis et al. 2017
, who argue that intuitive resolutions to the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox are best understood through the joint lens of geometric and probabilistic structures rather than probabilistic only.

We construct a metric-based extension of Bayes’ rule, resolving the paradox via continuity arguments. Specifically, we demonstrate that topology enables the definition of a sequence
(\mathbb{P}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

converging to a unique limiting measure, denoted
\mathbb{P}(.\mid A)
regardless of whether
\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)=0
.
This sequence is derived from the Maximum Entropy Principle (MaxEnt), originally introduced by
Jaynes 1957
in the context of statistical mechanics and now widely applied across disciplines including physics, economics, and information theory
Ben-Naim 2019
,
Chandrasekaran and Shah 2017
,
Wu 1997
,
Gull and Skilling 1984
,
Baldwin 2009
,
Clarke 2007
.

MaxEnt provides a natural framework for extending conditional probability.
Indeed,
Williams 1980
then later
Giffin and Caticha 2007
proved that classical conditional probability (
1.1
) can be recovered as a special case of MaxEnt (see Section
2.1
).

Building on the convex duality framework of
Borwein and Lewis 1992
(see Section
2.2
), we make explicit the sequence
(\mathbb{P}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of MaxEnt problems and define its limit as the Bayes’ posterior (see Section
3
).
Subject to mild regularity conditions, we prove that our definition is coherent and applicable to both null and non-null events (see Section
3.2
).
We prove that in metric spaces, our definition is the unique extension of the conditional probability (
1.1
) to the case
\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)=0
(see Section
3.3
).

Our extension depends on the underlying metric.
This dependence explains the multiplicity of Bayes’ posterior for the same couple (
probabilistic model
,
conditionalization set
) and clarifies the role of geometry in resolving ambiguities (see Section
4
). To our knowledge, this geometric approach provides a mathematically rigorous framework for resolving the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox while addressing practical modeling needs.

Beyond its theoretical contributions, our framework offers a robust and intuitive methodology for Bayesian inference in models enriched with metrizable topologies (see Section
5
).
Our main contributions are as follows:

1.

We derive conditional probability formulas from a single unifying principle—the Maximum Entropy Principle—yielding a coherent generalization of Bayes’ rule (Section
3
).

2.

We provide a geometric resolution of the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox (Section
4
).

3.

We demonstrate that our approach enables consistent and principled Bayesian inference in any probabilistic model endowed with a metrizable topology (Section
5
), thereby offering a rigorous solution to a longstanding modeling challenge.

2
Preliminary

2.1
Maximum Entropy Principle

To avoid ambiguity, we begin by clarifying our usage of Bayes’ rule and the Maximum Entropy Principle (MaxEnt).
We then explain how the classical definition of conditional probability (
1.1
) arises from MaxEnt.

Both Bayes’ rule and MaxEnt are procedures that update a prior probability measure — representing initial knowledge — based on new information, yielding a posterior measure.
In MaxEnt, the update is performed by selecting the posterior measure that minimizes the relative entropy (also known as the Kullback-Leibler divergence) subject to constraints imposed by the new information.

We define the relative entropy as follows:

Definition 1

(Relative Entropy or Kullback Leibler divergence)
.

Consider a
\sigma
-finite measure
\nu
on a measurable space
(E,\mathcal{E})
.
For a probability measure
\mu
absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu
(i.e.,
\mu\ll\nu
) the entropy of
\mu
relative to
\nu
is defined by

\text{H}_{\nu}\left[\mu\right]\vcentcolon=\int_{E}\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{}\mu}{\operatorname{d}\!{}\nu}(x)\ln\left(\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{}\mu}{\operatorname{d}\!{}\nu}(x)\right)\operatorname{d}\!{}\nu(x)\,.

(2.1)

where
\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}
denotes the Radon-Nikodym derivative.
If
\mu
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu
(i.e.,
\mu\not\ll\nu
), we set

\text{H}_{\nu}\left[\mu\right]\vcentcolon=+\infty\,.

(2.2)

When the evaluated measure is absolutely continuous with respect to the reference measure, the relative entropy is always a positive value or null:

Proposition 1

(
Cover and Thomas 2001
Theorem 2.6.3)
.

Consider two probability measure
\nu
and
\mu
on a measurable space
(E,\mathcal{E})
. If
\mu\ll\nu
, then

\text{H}_{\nu}\left[\mu\right]\geqslant 0\,,

(2.3)

with equality if and only if
\mu=\nu
.

Unlike conditional probability, Bayes’ rule and MaxEnt are not mathematical objects per se, but rather inference procedures.
Due to their widespread use beyond mathematics, the definition of MaxEnt often lacks the precision required for rigorous comparison with Bayes’ rule (see
[
Uffink 1996
]
).
In this work, we adopt a specific formulation of MaxEnt based on linear constraints:

Definition 2

(MaxEnt with Linear Constraints)
.

Consider a prior probability
\nu
measure on a measurable space
(E,\mathcal{E})
.
On the space of measures that measure
\mathcal{E}
, consider a constrained set defined as follows:

I\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 3.8889pt\hskip-3.8889pt\hbox{$=$}\hskip-3.8889pt\hskip-2.7431pt\raisebox{4.66875pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\Delta$}}\hskip-2.7431pt\hskip 3.8889pt}}\left\{\mu:\int_{E}f_{i}(x)\operatorname{d}\!{}\mu(x)=a_{i}\,,\;\textit{ for }i=1\ldots n\right\}\,,

(2.4)

where each
f_{i}
is a measurable real-valued function,
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Then the MaxEnt problem
(\nu,I)
consists of finding

\inf_{\mu\in I}\text{H}_{\nu}\left[\mu\right]\,.

(2.5)

The problem is said to be well-defined if the infimum (
2.5
) is attained by a unique measure.
If the MaxEnt problem
(\nu,I)
is well-defined, the MaxEnt refers to the procedure that returns this unique measure.

Williams 1980
showed that the classical conditional probability (
1.1
) can be derived from this MaxEnt formulation.
Given a probability measure
\mathbb{P}
with
\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)>0
, and the constraint set

\displaystyle I=\left\{\mathbb{Q}\,:\,\mathbb{Q}(A)=1\right\}=\left\{\mathbb{Q}:\int_{E}1\,\operatorname{d}\!{}\mathbb{Q}(x)=1\,,\;\int_{E}\mathds{1}_{A}(x)\,\operatorname{d}\!{}\mathbb{Q}(x)\right\}\,,

(2.6)

the solution to the MaxEnt problem
(\mathbb{P},I)
is the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}\left(.\mid A\right)
(
1.1
) (see Appendix
A
for a modern version of Williams’s proof).
Thus, where conditional probability is unambiguous, Bayes’ rule is a special case of MaxEnt, making MaxEnt a natural framework for extending conditional probability and unifying Bayesian inference.

However, Williams’s proof does not extend directly to the case
\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)=0
.
If we attempt to constrain the search space to
\{\mathbb{Q}\;:\;\mathbb{Q}(A)=1\}
when
\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)=0
, then by definition (
2.2
), all such measures have infinite relative entropy with respect to
\mathbb{P}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\text{H}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathbb{Q}\right]=\infty\,,\quad\forall\,\mathbb{Q}\,\text{ such that }\;\mathbb{Q}(A)=1\,.

(2.7)

To address this, we will extend Williams’s approach to topological spaces.
In such spaces, the constraint
\mathbb{Q}(A)=1
can be replaced by the more precise condition
\text{Supp}(\mathbb{Q})\subset A
.
The support of a measure is defined as follows:

Definition 3

(Support)
.

Consider a topological space
(E,\mathcal{T})
measured by the Borel
\sigma
-algebra. Then the support of a measure
\mu
is defined by

\displaystyle\text{Supp}(\mu)\vcentcolon=E/\bigcup\left\{U\,:\;U\in\mathcal{T}\text{ and }\mu(U)=0\right\}\,.

(2.8)

2.2
Convex Optimization

Our extension of conditional probability requires explicit solutions to MaxEnt problems.
This necessitates tools from the convex optimization. In particular, we rely on results by
Borwein and Lewis 1992
, which provide general conditions for the existence and expression of solutions of convex optimization problems.
Notably,
Borwein and Lewis 1991
applied their duality framework to a class of problems structurally analogous to MaxEnt problems, enabling us to present the following result as a direct application of their theory.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(E,\mathcal{E})
be a measurable space equipped with a
\sigma
-finite measure
\nu
.
Let
a_{i}\in L_{\nu}^{\infty}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and let
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Consider the following pair of primal and dual optimization problems:

\displaystyle\text{(ME)}\quad\left.\begin{array}[]{ll}\inf&\int_{E}f(x)\ln\left(f(x)\right)\operatorname{d}\!{}\nu(x)\,,\\
&\\
\textit{subject to}&\int_{E}a_{i}(x)f(x)\operatorname{d}\!{}\nu(x)=b_{i}\,,\quad i=1,\ldots,n\,,\\
&\\
&f\geqslant 0\textit{,}\quad f\in L_{\nu}^{1}\textit{.}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\text{(DME)}\quad\left.\begin{array}[]{ll}\max&\vec{\lambda}^{T}b-\int_{E}\exp\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}a_{i}(x)\right)\operatorname{d}\!{}\nu(x)\,,\\
&\\
\textit{subject to}&\vec{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,.\end{array}\right.

Suppose that there exists a feasible
f
for
(\text{ME})
such that
f>0

\nu
-almost everywhere and
\int_{E}f(x)\ln\left(f(x)\right)\operatorname{d}\!{}\nu(x)<\infty
.
Then:

1.

The optimal values of
(\text{ME})
and
(\text{DME})
coincide, and both the infimum in
(\text{ME})
and supremum in
(\text{DME})
are attained.

2.

The unique minimizer
f^{\star}
of
(\text{ME})
is given by

f^{\star}(x)=\exp\left(-1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{\lambda}_{i}a_{i}(x)\right)\,,

(2.17)

where
\overline{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the optimal dual variable of
(\text{DME})
.

Proof.

The first statement follows from
Borwein and Lewis 1991
, applied to the measured space
(E,\mathcal{E},\nu)
and the strictly convex function
x\ln x
on
[0,\infty[
.
The second statement follows from Theorem 4.8. in the same reference, using the fact that
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x\ln x}{x}=\infty
.
∎

3
Bayes’ Rule

In this section, we present a method for deriving a Bayesian posterior on a separable metric space using the Maximum Entropy Principle (MaxEnt). Our approach is broadly applicable and relies solely on the metric structure of standard Borel spaces, thereby providing a general framework for Bayesian inference across a wide class of probabilistic models.

We begin in Section
3.1
with a conceptual overview of the method, highlighting the intuition behind our definition.
In Section
3.2
, we establish that the MaxEnt-based Bayesian map is well-defined (existence and uniqueness).
Section
3.3
demonstrates that this map coincides with the classical Bayes rule when the conditioning event has positive measure.
Section
3.4
verifies that our definition satisfies the properties expected of Bayesian inference in probabilistic modeling, particularly in scenarios involving the observation of a random variable.
Additionally, Section
3.4
provides an explicit formula for the Bayesian map when the prior is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure and the topology is Euclidean.
This yields the canonical formula (
3.1
), thereby establishing a rigorous mathematical connection between the classical conditional probability (
1.1
) and the canonical Bayesian formula (
3.1
) commonly used in practice.

3.1
Solution Sketch

We illustrate our method using a canonical example in which the conditioning event corresponds to the observation of a Lebesgue-continuous random variable.
Consider a pair of random variables
X\in\mathbb{R}^{p},Y\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, with a joint prior measure
p(x,y)\operatorname{d}\!{x}\operatorname{d}\!{y}
, where
\operatorname{d}\!{x}\operatorname{d}\!{y}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{p+q}
.
The canonical Bayes rule for models absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure defines the marginal posterior for the random variable
A
as:

p(x\mid\hat{y})\operatorname{d}\!{x}\vcentcolon=\frac{p(x,\hat{y})}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}p(x,\hat{y})\operatorname{d}\!{x}}\operatorname{d}\!{x}\,,

(3.1)

where
\hat{y}
is the observed value of
Y
.

Our goal is to derive equation (
3.1
) from the MaxEnt framework by constraining the posterior measure to be supported on the set
\mathbb{R}\times\{\hat{y}\}
.
However, the MaxEnt optimization problem on
\mathbb{R}\times\{\hat{y}\}
is not well-defined, because
\mathbb{R}\times\{\hat{y}\}
has zero Lebesgue measure and MaxEnt requires the posterior to be absolutely continuous with respect to the prior (Section
2.1
).

To derive (
3.1
), we relax the linear constraint
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathds{1}_{A}\right]=1
(see (
A.2c
) of Theorem
6
) with the following constraint:
To circumvent this issue, we relax the constraint
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{R}\times\{\hat{y}\}}\right]=1
(see condition (
A.2c
) in Theorem
6
) by imposing the following approximate constraint:

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\lVert Y-\hat{y}\rVert^{2}\right]\leqslant\sigma^{2}\,,

(3.2)

where
\mu
denotes the posterior measure and
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is the Euclidean norm.
We build a sequence
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of solutions to MaxEnt problems subject to constraint (
3.2
), with
\sigma_{n}\to 0
.

By assertion (
3.3
), the marginal distribution of
Y
under
\mu_{n}
converges to a Dirac measure at
\hat{y}
:

\left(\mathbb{E}_{(X_{n},Y_{n})\sim\mu_{n}}\left[\lVert Y_{n}-\hat{y}\rVert^{2}\right]\,\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\,0\right)\implies\left(Y_{n}\,\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\textit{weakly}}\,\delta_{\hat{y}}\right)\,.

(3.3)

Our method consequently reproduces the constraint
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathbb{R}\times\{\hat{y}\}\right]=1
through an asymptotic behaviour.

3.2
Defining Bayesian Map on Metric Space

In this section, we define a Bayesian inference map derived from the Maximum Entropy Principle.
To adapt MaxEnt to Bayesian inference, we must carefully select linear constraints that ensure the posterior measure is supported on the conditioning set
A
, as is expected from Bayes’ rule.

We begin with a characterization of support inclusion via a linear constraint:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A
be a closed subset of a measurable metric space
(E,d,\mathcal{B}(E))
and let
\mu
be a Radon probability measure.
Then:

\displaystyle\int_{E}d^{2}(x,A)\operatorname{d}\!{\mu(x)}=0\iff\text{Supp}(\mu)\subset A\,.

(3.4)

Proof.

Suppose
\int_{E}d^{2}(x,A)\operatorname{d}\!{\mu(x)}=0
.
As
x\mapsto d^{2}(x,A)\leqslant 0
is continuous and
E
is equipped by the Borel
sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(E)
, the definition of the integral enables us to state:

\displaystyle\int_{E}d^{2}(x,A)\operatorname{d}\!{\mu}(x)=0\iff\forall U\text{ open subset such that }\inf_{x\in U}d^{2}(x,A)>0\,,\quad\text{then}\;\mu(U)=0\,.

(3.5)

Therefore, the points
x
such that
d^{2}(x,A)>0
are included in
E/\text{Supp}(\nu)
, and as
A
is closed we conclude
A^{\complement}\subset E/\text{Supp}(\nu)
.

The other implication follows from the fact that for any open set
U\subset A^{\complement}
, we have
U\subset E/\text{Supp}(\nu)
, so
\mu(U)=0
.
Due to the outer regularity of Radon measure, we extend the previous fact to any Borel set
B
included in
A^{\complement}
by

\displaystyle\mu(B)=\inf\left\{\mu(U\cap A^{\complement})\;:\;U\text{ open set such that }B\subset U\right\}=0\,.

(3.6)

∎

The equivalence (
3.4
) allows us to enforce support inclusion through a linear constraint.
However, this formulation fails when
\nu(A)=0
, as MaxEnt requires the posterior to be absolutely continuous with respect to the prior.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
\mu
is a solution of a well-defined MaxEnt problem (Definition
2
), then
\mu\ll\nu
.

Proof.

Consider a set
A
of
\nu
-null measure. Suppose that there exists
\mu^{\star}
solution of a well-defined MaxEnt problem
(\nu,I)
such that
\mu^{\star}(A)=1
. Then
\mu^{\star}\not\ll\nu
, so
\text{H}_{\nu}\left[\mu^{\star}\right]=\infty
. So any
\mu
such that
\mu\not\ll\nu
is also a solution of the same MaxEnt problem
(\nu,I)
, which implies that
(\nu,I)
is not well-defined, contradicting our hypothesis.
∎

Thus, constraint (
3.4
) cannot be used to obtain a posterior supported on
A
when
\nu(A)=0
.
To address this, we introduce a relaxed constraint that enables convergence to a posterior supported on
A
via a limiting process.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
A
be a closed set on a standard Borel space
(E,d)
.
Let
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of Radon probability measures that weakly convergent to
\mu
.
Then,

\displaystyle\forall R>0\,,\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{E}d_{R}^{2}(x,A)\operatorname{d}\!{\mu_{n}(x)}=0\iff\text{Supp}(\mu)\subset A\,,

(3.7)

where
d_{R}=\min(d,R)
.

Proof.

Consider
R>0
. We begin by proving a first implication, suppose

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{E}d_{R}^{2}(x,A)\operatorname{d}\!{\mu_{n}(x)}=0\,.

(3.8)

As
x\mapsto d^{2}(x,A)\geqslant 0
is continuous and
E
is measured by the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(E)
, by definition of the integral

\displaystyle\int_{E}d_{R}^{2}(x,A)\operatorname{d}\!{\mu}(x)=0\iff\forall U\text{ open set such that }\inf_{x\in U}d_{R}^{2}(x,U)>0\;\text{ then }\;\mu(U)=0\,.

(3.9)

Consider
U
open subset such that
\inf_{x\in U}d^{2}(x,A)>0
, then

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}_{R}(x,A)}{\inf_{x\in U}d^{2}(x,A)}\geqslant 1\quad\forall x\in U\,.

(3.10)

From (
3.10
), we can find a convergent upper bound on
\mu_{n}(U)
as follows:

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(U)

\displaystyle=\int_{U}1\operatorname{d}\!{\mu_{n}}(x)

(3.11)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{U}\left(\frac{d^{2}_{R}(x,A)}{\inf_{x\in U}d^{2}(x,A)}\right)\operatorname{d}\!{\mu_{n}}(x)\quad\text{due to \eqref{eq:100}}

(3.12)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\int_{E}d^{2}_{R}(x,A)\operatorname{d}\!{\mu_{n}}(x)}{\inf_{x\in U}d^{2}(x,A)}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\quad\text{due to hypothesis \eqref{eq:101}}\,.

(3.13)

By definition of weak convergence
\liminf\mu_{n}(U)\geqslant\mu(U)
, so
\mu(U)=0
.
We can conclude that
\displaystyle{\text{Supp}}(\mu)\subset A
.

We now prove the other implication.

Suppose
\text{Supp}(\mu)\subset A
, using the same argument that for the proof of Lemma
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{E}d_{R}^{2}(x,A)\operatorname{d}\!{\mu(x)}=0\,.

(3.14)

As
d^{2}_{R}
is a continuous and bounded function, by definition the definition weak convergence:

\displaystyle\int_{E}d_{R}^{2}(x,A)\operatorname{d}\!{\mu(x)}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{E}d_{R}^{2}(x,A)\operatorname{d}\!{\mu_{n}(x)}\,,

(3.15)

which combined with (
3.14
) conclude the proof.
∎

This result motivates the following definition of a Bayesian posterior via MaxEnt:

Definition 4

(MaxEnt Posterior)
.

Let
R>0
.
Let
(E,d,\mathcal{B}(E))
be a standard Borel space (see
Kechris 1995
[Definition 12.5]) measured by
\nu
a prior Radon probability measure.
Let
A\subset Supp(\nu)
be a closed subset.
Suppose that for all
\sigma>0
, the MaxEnt problem:

\inf_{\mu}\Big\{\text{H}_{\nu}\left[\mu\right]\;:\quad\int_{E}\operatorname{d}\!{\mu(x)}=1\,,\;\int_{E}d_{R}(x,A)^{2}\operatorname{d}\!{\mu(x)}\leqslant\sigma^{2}\Big\}

(3.16)

is well-defined with solution
\mu_{\sigma}
.
If
\mu_{\sigma}
converges weakly when
\sigma
converges to
0
, we define the limit as the MaxEnt posterior of
(\nu,A)
denoted
\nu(.\mid A)
.

We now establish that this definition yields a well-defined posterior measure, with the structural properties required for a coherent extension of Bayes’ rule:

•

Independence from the
R
Truncation
: The posterior is independent of the truncation parameter
R>0
, as the convergence occurs at the boundary of
A
.

•

Normalization
: If the MaxEnt posterior
\nu(\cdot\mid A)
exists, it is a probability measure satisfying
\nu(A\mid A)=1
.

•

Invariance under Measure-Preserving Isometries
: Since the framework is defined on standard Borel spaces, the MaxEnt posterior is invariant under transformations that preserve both the metric and the measure.

We begin by verifying that the MaxEnt problem (
3.16
) is well-defined and expresses its closed form.

Theorem 2
.

Let
R>0
.
Let
(E,d,\mathcal{B}(E))
be a standard Borel space measured by a Radon measure
\nu
.
Let
A\subset Supp(\nu)

Then the MaxEnt problem (
3.16
) has a unique solution of the form

\operatorname{d}\!{\mu_{a(\sigma)}(x)}\vcentcolon=\frac{\exp\left(-a(\sigma)d_{R}(x,A)^{2}\right)}{\int_{E}\exp\left(-a(\sigma)d_{R}(x,A)^{2}\right)d\nu(x)}\operatorname{d}\!{\nu(x)}\,.

(3.17)

We refer to (
3.17
) as the pre-MaxEnt posterior of
(\nu,A)
.
Moreover, if we assume
\nu(E/A)>0
, the limit behaviour of
\mu_{a(\sigma)}
is characterised by the relation:
a(\sigma)\xrightarrow{}\infty
when
\sigma\to 0
.

The problem (
3.16
) falls under Theorem

3.16
) is equivalent to the following optimization problem over the measurable function

on

:

\displaystyle\inf

\displaystyle\int_{E}f(x)\ln\left(f(x)\right)d\nu(x)

(3.18a)

(ME)

subject to

\displaystyle\int_{E}d_{R}(x,A)^{2}f(x)d\nu(x)=\sigma^{2},\;\;\int_{E}f(x)d\nu(x)=1\textit{,}

(3.18b)

\displaystyle f>0\textit{,}\quad f\in L_{\nu}^{1}\textit{,}

(3.18c)

Due to Lemma
B.1
, the function
a\longmapsto\int_{E}d_{R}(x,A)^{2}\operatorname{d}\!{}\mu_{a}(x)
converges to
0
when
a\to\infty
.
As the previous function is also continuous, for
\sigma
small enough, (
3.18
) is a feasible optimization problem.

So according to Theorem
1
, (
3.16
) is well-defined and has a unique solution of the form (
3.17
).

Let us denote the set of points at a distance less that
\sqrt{\eta}
of
A
by

A^{\eta}\vcentcolon=\left\{x\;\;\Big|\;\;d^{2}(x,A)\leqslant\eta\right\}\,.

(3.19)

We now prove that
a(\sigma_{n})\to\infty
when
\sigma_{n}\to 0
.
Consider a sequence
\sigma_{n}

The MaxEnt posterior is invariant under measure-preserving isometry.

We have established that Definition

However, its existence is not guaranteed in general.
We conclude this section by providing a sufficient condition for the existence of the MaxEnt posterior.
This condition is satisfied in all known applications of Bayesian Inference appearing in the literature.

For a probability measures defined on a standard Borel spaces, the weak convergence topology is metrized by the Lèvy-Prokhorov metric

\displaystyle d_{\text{L-P}}(\mu,\nu)=\inf\left\{\varepsilon>0\mid\nu(C)\leqslant\mu(C^{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon\,,\;\mu(C)\leqslant\nu(C^{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon\,,\quad\forall C\text{ measurable}\right\}\,,

as

.
Since the space of probability measures on the Borel

4
Resolution of the Borel-Kolmogorov Paradox

The Borel–Kolmogorov paradox reveals a fundamental inconsistency in the definition of conditional probability for events of null measure.
In various probabilistic models, multiple posterior distributions have been proposed for the same pair consisting of a prior and a conditionalization set.
The resulting ambiguity in selecting the correct posterior formulation has led to what is commonly referred to as the Borel-Kolmogorov paradox.
In this section, we examine the origin of this non-uniqueness and propose a resolution grounded in geometric considerations.

4.1
A Problem of Unicity

In his foundational work,
Borel 1909
highlighted the difficulty of defining probability in geometric contexts.
Specifically, he noted that the probability of a subset of lower dimension than the ambient space is not directly implied by the axioms of probability theory as formalized by
Kolmogorov 1956
[Section 1.1].
For example, in Euclidean spaces equipped with the Lebesgue measure, lower-dimensional subsets typically have measure zero.
Nevertheless, certain problems require computing the probability of a point in a three-dimensional volume, conditioned on its belonging to a two-dimensional surface — or analogously, a point on a surface conditioned on its presence in a one-dimensional curve.

In such cases, the intuitive principle of proportionality, which underlies the classical definition of conditional probability (
1.1
), fails to apply.

Borel 1909
illustrated this issue through the example of conditionalization on a meridian of a uniformly measured sphere.
He demonstrated that a uniform measure on the sphere does not necessarily imply a uniform measure on a conditional great circle.
While Borel’s reasoning is coherent and insightful, it remains case-specific and lacks a general mathematical formalization.

Kolmogorov 1956
subsequently concluded that conditional probabilities for null events cannot be defined within the standard axiomatic framework.
He proposed that to define a posterior distribution on a meridian, the meridian should be treated as an atomic element of a
\sigma
-algebra that partitions the sphere.
This idea was later generalized through the formalism of conditional expectation, as developed and clarified by
Gyenis et al. 2017
.

Although conditional expectation provides a rigorous method for defining conditional probabilities on null sets, it does not resolve the paradox.
As shown in Section
C.2
, this approach permits an infinite number of valid posterior distributions for the same conditioning set.

Alternatively,
Jaynes 2003
[15.7] and
Bungert and Wacker 2022
[2 and 4.2] proposed defining conditional probabilities for null events as limits of conditional probabilities for non-null events (see Section
C.1
).
However, the outcome of such limit procedures depends on how the approximating non-null events are chosen, and neither work offers a principled method for selecting these approximations.
Consequently, the problem of identifying a unique posterior measure remains unresolved.

The key to understanding the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox lies in noting that, within measure theory, sets of null measure are indistinguishable.
However, any extension of the classical conditional probability formula (
1.1
) to null events is expected to differentiate between such sets.
Consequently, a definition of conditional probability based solely on measure-theoretic principles cannot satisfy this expectation.

To address this limitation, an additional mathematical structure must be introduced.
In this work, we incorporate topology to distinguish between null sets.
Topology provides an intuitive framework for differentiating between non-homeomorphic sets, thereby enabling a more refined treatment of conditional probabilities.
As we will demonstrate, topological considerations offer a coherent resolution to the intuitive challenges posed by the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox.

4.2
Geometrical Resolution

In the following, we present a topological resolution of the paradox.
We show that on the sphere, once a prior measure and a distance function are fixed, Definition
4
yields a unique MaxEnt posterior.
However, if the prior and conditionalization set are held constant while the distance function is varied, the resulting posterior measures differ.
This observation supports our claim that Definition
4
resolves the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox by attributing the multiplicity of posterior distributions to the choice of non-isometric metrics in the initial model.

5
Advantages of a MaxEnt approach to Bayes’ rule

While several generalizations of Bayes’ rule rely exclusively on the Kolmogorov axioms (see
[
Klenke 2020
,
Gyenis et al. 2017
]
, see Appendix
C
), our approach integrates topological and optimization-theoretic structures.
This raises a natural question: why invoke multiple mathematical frameworks to address a problem that appears to be purely probabilistic?

In Section
5.1
, we address intuitive concerns surrounding the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox, demonstrating that our resolution aligns with common expectations about conditional probability.
In Section
5.2
, we argue that our formulation is better suited for rigorous and interpretable probabilistic modeling.
Finally, in Section
5.3
, we discuss the axiomatic implications of our framework.

5.1
Interpretation of the Borel Kolmogorov Paradox

The MaxEnt posteriors derived in Propositions
6
and
7
highlight the necessity of treating the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox as a geometric-probabilistic problem, rather than a purely probabilistic one, as originally suggested by
Borel 1909
.

Under the geometric model
(\mathbb{S}^{2},d_{g},\nu)
of the embedded Riemannian manifold sphere measured by the uniform measure defined in Section
4.2.1
, all great circles are topologically equivalent.
Consequently, for any pair of great circles, there exists a measure-preserving isometry of
(\mathbb{S}^{2},d_{g})
that maps one to the other. As shown in Proposition
4
, this symmetry implies that the conditional probabilities defined via Definition
4
must be identical across all great circles.

In contrast, under the map-projection metric
\widetilde{d}
(
4.7
), the equator and meridians are not topologically equivalent. There exists no homeomorphism mapping one to the other.
Thus, differing conditional probabilities for these sets are not only plausible but expected.

Some authors, such as
Bungert and Wacker 2022
, argue that a correct definition of conditional probability should yield a uniform posterior on any great circle of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
This intuition, however, is not probabilistic but topological as argued by
Gyenis et al. 2017
.
It originates from perceiving the sphere as an embedded submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
equipped with the Euclidean topology—i.e., as
(\mathbb{S}^{2},d_{g})
.
Proposition
6
confirms this intuition, while Proposition
7
refutes the claim that uniformity is universally correct.

The non-uniform posterior derived in Proposition
7
becomes intuitive when
(\mathbb{S}^{2},\widetilde{d},\nu)
is projected onto a Euclidean space via a measure-preserving isometry.
As illustrated in Figure
1(b)
, this projection recovers the expected proportionality intuition, showing that the perceived paradox arises from the difficulty of forming intuition in non-Euclidean spaces.

Therefore, the argument of
Bungert and Wacker 2022
for a unique posterior based solely on proportionality fails.
Both the uniform and non-uniform posteriors are valid, and their differences reflect distinct topological choices.
Unlike Definition
6
of
Gyenis et al. 2017
that cannot discriminate between the posteriors allowed by the probabilistic model
(\mathbb{S}^{2},\nu)
(see Appendix
C.2
), our Definition
4
yields a unique and principled posterior for each model
(\mathbb{S}^{2},d,\nu)
.
Crucially, this resolution demonstrates that the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox dissolves when viewed through the lens of the triplet: prior measure, metric, and conditionalization set.

5.2
A Probabilistic Modeling perspective on Bayes’ Rule

To appreciate the modeling advantages of our approach, one must view probability theory not merely as a mathematical discipline but as a tool for representing phenomena.

From a purely mathematical standpoint, the conditional expectation framework (see Appendix
C.2
) provides a general and rigorous extension of conditional probability.
In cases where
\mathbb{P}(A)=0
, the existence of multiple valid conditional probabilities is not paradoxical—it is a direct consequence of the Kolmogorov axioms.

However, in modeling contexts, this multiplicity becomes problematic.
Definition
6
requires the selection of a sub-
\sigma
-algebra and a specific form for the conditional expectation.
These choices are arbitrary and lack interpretability from a modeling perspective.
When applying Bayes’ rule to different events within the same model, it is unclear how to ensure coherence among these choices.

Our approach resolves this issue by replacing event-specific arbitrariness with a global metric structure.
Definition
4
enforces topological coherence across all conditional probabilities derived from the model.

In contrast,
Bungert and Wacker 2022
addresses the ambiguity by prescribing a single posterior formula.
While this eliminates modeling uncertainty, it restricts expressivity and lacks justification for the chosen formula.
Their approach is less general than both Definition
6
and our Definition
4
.

In summary, our framework supports coherent and interpretable modeling while preserving flexibility. It allows the modeler to guide inference using topological intuition, rather than arbitrary choices.

5.3
Axiomatic implication

Our framework offers a new perspective on the axiomatic foundations of Bayes’ rule.
The classical formula (
1.1
) satisfies Cox’s axioms (see
[
Cox 2001
,
Knuth 2002
]
), which justify its use in probabilistic reasoning.
However, the widely used formula for Lebesgue-continuous random variables (
3.1
) does not follow from Cox’s axioms, despite being applied in the same way.

By unifying both formulas under the MaxEnt principle, our work provides a principled justification for their common usage.
In this light, the MaxEnt principle (see
[
Shore and Johnson 1980
]
) can be viewed as a generalization of Bayesian inference.

Appendix C
Other Generalizations of Bayes’ Rule

Beyond the framework developed in this paper, the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox has been approached through two different methodologies.
This appendix presents these alternative approaches to extending the classical definition of conditional probability (
1.1
) to events of null measure.
We explain why these methods fail to satisfy two essential criteria for a rigorous extension: (i) a mathematically precise definition with a clear modeling context, and (ii) uniqueness of the posterior distribution for each conditioning set within a given probabilistic model.

C.1
Limit Process on Conditional Probability

Jaynes 2003
and
Bungert and Wacker 2022
propose defining the conditional probability of a null event as the limit of conditional probabilities over approximating events of positive measure:

\displaystyle\nu(B\mid A)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\nu(B^{\varepsilon}\cap A^{\varepsilon})}{\nu(A^{\varepsilon})}\,,

(C.1)

where each
C^{\varepsilon}
is an enlargement of the set
C
satisfying

\displaystyle\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}C^{\varepsilon}=C\,\text{ for all }C\text{ measurable}\,,

(C.2)

and
\nu(A^{\varepsilon})>0
for all
\varepsilon>0
.

The resulting conditional probability (
C.1
) depends on the choice of the approximating enlargement (
C.2
).
However, neither
Jaynes 2003
nor
Bungert and Wacker 2022
provide a principled method for selecting these approximations, rendering their definition incomplete and incapable of resolving the paradox.

In light of our resolution of the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox (see Section
4
), we interpret the various posterior distributions obtained by Jaynes, Bungert, and Wacker as arising from different metric choices.
Their constructions implicitly rely on a topology, as the approximating sets
\{C^{\varepsilon}\mid C\text{ measurable, }\varepsilon>0\}
are typically defined via metric neighbourhoods (cf. (
3.19
)).
Changing the metric alters the approximating sequence and, consequently, the limiting posterior. While this interpretation aligns with our framework, it is not made explicit in their work, which lacks a formal topological structure.

C.2
Conditional Probability via Conditional Expectation

To extend conditional probability rigorously, several authors—including
Kolmogorov 1956
,
Klenke 2020
,
Gyenis et al. 2017
, and
Easwaran 2019
—embed it within the broader framework of conditional expectation.
This approach is justified by the identity

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathds{1}_{B}\mid A\right]=\mathbb{P}\left(B\mid A\right)\text{(see \cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{klenke_conditional_2020}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}, Definition 8.9]} for instance)}\,,

(C.3)

which allows conditional probability to be interpreted as a special case of conditional expectation when
\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)>0
.

Gyenis et al. 2017
demonstrate that, for a fixed pair
(\mathbb{P},A)
with
\mathbb{P}(A)=0
, the conditional expectation framework yields infinitely many valid posterior distributions.
We briefly outline their construction and explain why it fails to resolve the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox.

Definition 5

(Conditional Expectation)
.

Let
(E,\mathcal{E},\nu)
be a probabilistic space and
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{E}
a sub-
\sigma
-algebra.
A conditional expectation with respect to
\mathcal{A}
is a map

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[.|\mathcal{A}\right]\,:\;L^{1}_{\nu}(E,\mathcal{E})\longmapsto L^{1}_{\nu}(E,\mathcal{A})\,,

(C.4)

satisfying for all
f\in L^{1}_{\nu}(E,\mathcal{E})
:

(i)

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[f|\mathcal{A}\right]
is
\mathcal{A}
-measurable,

(ii)

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[f\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[f|\mathcal{A}\right]\right]\,
.

The function
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[f|\mathcal{A}\right]
is defined up to
\nu
-null sets.

Gyenis et al. 2017
define the conditional probability formulas for Bayesian Inference as follows:

Definition 6

(Conditional Probability via Conditional Expectation)
.

On a measurable space
(E,\mathcal{E})
, consider a prior
\nu
and a measurable event
A
.
Choose a
\sigma

\mathcal{A}
such that
A
is an atomic event,
then choose one
\mathcal{A}
-conditional expectation map
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[.|\mathcal{A}\right]
.
Finally, consider the unique probability measure
\mu_{A}
on
(E,\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle\mu_{A}(A)=1\,,\;\mu_{A}(A^{\complement})=0\,.

(C.5)

The
(\nu,A,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[.|\mathcal{A}\right])
-conditional probability is the unique probability measure

(E,\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[f\right]=\int_{E}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[f|\mathcal{A}\right]\operatorname{d}\!{\mu_{A}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{A}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[f|\mathcal{A}\right]\right]\,.

(C.6)

A
(\nu,A)
-conditional probability is then unique up to the choices

This definition depends on the arbitrary choices of the sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
and of the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[.|\mathcal{A}\right]
.
Purely based on probability theory, the derivations of

are correct, rigorous, and applicable to any probability space
(E,\mathcal{E},\nu)
on any measurable event
A
.
However, their interpretation is flawed.

Definition
6
requires an arbitrary choice of the form of the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[f|\mathcal{A}\right]
on
\nu
-null measurable sets for all
f\in L^{1}_{\nu}(E,\mathcal{E})
.
When
\nu(A)=0
, this choice directly determines the form of the posterior.
As a result, any posterior distribution is valid under Definition

Consequently, both posteriors (
4.5
), (
4.9

4.3
),(

In summary, while Definition

offers a rigorous and general formulation of conditional probability within probability theory, it does not resolve the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox.
It confirms that probability theory alone cannot provide a unique and principled extension of Bayes’ rule to events of null measure.
