Application of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid cytomorphology in diagnosing Nocardia otitidiscaviarum: a case report
Authors: Yuyi Lai, Fuxian Zhou, Haibin Wang, Xiao He, Qiongli Zhang, Yuli Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNocardiosis is a rare infectious disease, which is frequently underdiagnosed because of the distinct bacterial shape of the causative agent and its developmental history. The morphological analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid can facilitate the detection of pathogenic bacteria by observing cellular–bacterial interactions.Case presentationAn immunocompetent 67-year-old male patient of Asian ethnicity developed a cough without apparent cause 20 days earlier. We initially discovered mycobacteria that appeared to be Nocardia in the cytomorphological examination of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, which was later determined to be Nocardia otitidiscaviarum through metagenomic next-generation sequencing and microbiological cultures. The patient was eventually diagnosed with Nocardia pneumonitis after other testing and clinical signs were considered. After anti-infective treatment, the patient improved and was discharged.ConclusionSeveral diagnostic approaches were used in this case, and the importance of cell morphology as an early screening method was emphasized for suspected Nocardia infection.

Introduction

Nocardia, a filamentous actinomycete of the Corynebacterium order [1], is a Gram-positive, aerobic bacteria that exhibits weak acid-fast staining properties. It is ubiquitous in soil, decaying plants, and diverse organic substrates. To date, over 120 bacterial species have been recognized in this genus [2]. Nocardia is primarily considered an opportunistic pathogen; however, Nocardia infections affect both immunocompromised and immunocompetent individuals, with approximately one-third of the cases reported in immunocompetent individuals [3]. Depending on the transmission mode, Nocardia infections can manifest diversely, including pulmonary, cutaneous, and neurological diseases [4]. Nocardia asteroids, Nocardia brasiliensis, Nocardia farcinica, Nocardia nova, and Nocardia otitidiscaviarum are commonly associated with lung infections [5]. This case report presents a rare instance of Nocardia pneumonia caused by Nocardia otitidiscaviarum infection.

Case report

Approximately 20 days earlier, a 67-year-old man of Asian ethnicity developed a cough without sputum production or shortness of breath. Subsequently, he experienced a low-grade fever for 2 days, but the specific temperature was unknown. The patient reported no substantial medical history and denied any allergies. Upon admission, his vital signs were within normal limits. Blood tests and sputum samples were collected for pathogen examination on day 2 of hospitalization. The key hematological indices included a white blood cell count of 4.1 × 109/L, neutrophils at 72.2%, and lymphocytes at 17.9%. Biochemical markers indicated a C-reactive protein level of 3.2 mg/L and a 630 µg/L d-dimer. Microscopic sputum analysis did not detect acid-fast bacilli, and the culture results were negative for pathogenic bacteria.

On day 4 of hospitalization, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected for a comprehensive examination. The cytological BALF analysis results were suggestive of Nocardia infection. Subsequently, the metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) test detected Nocardia otitidiscaviarum (110,724) and Cryptococcus neoformans (18). On day 5 of hospitalization, further assessments confirmed a positive BALF galactomannan test and a negative cryptococcal antigen test. Taken together, the patient was diagnosed with Nocardia pneumonia, specifically caused by Nocardia otitidiscaviarum. Consequently, antimicrobial therapy, consisting of the oral administration of two tablets of compounded amiodarone three times daily for 7 days, was initiated.

On day 12 of hospitalization, the patient’s cough improved considerably, with fewer coughing episodes and no sputum. His body temperature was normal, and the patient reported no chest tightness or breathing difficulties. Discussion

The primary means for identifying Nocardia involve traditional pathogen identification methods, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis, and mNGS gene sequencing. However, traditional pathogen identification poses challenges because inconsistent staining can overlook Nocardia. Weak acid-fast staining is used only for symptoms suggestive of Nocardia infection. Additionally, slow Nocardia growth, along with the difficulty in isolating, results in a low isolation rate. The aerobic cultivation of visible colonies at 37 °C typically requires 2–7 days [6]. In some instances, it may require 4–6 weeks [7]. The colonies are chalky colored and embedded in agar; however, the rapid proliferation of dominant bacteria can obscure the target colony, leading to missed diagnoses. Furthermore, immunosuppressants can prolong the growth cycle of Nocardia [8], making cultivation more challenging. Of note, the presence of antibiotic resistance and diabetes can worsen the prognosis of patients with this infection [9, 10], emphasizing the need for accurate and timely diagnosis.

Gene sequencing is the most accurate approach for the species-level identification of Nocardia. Researchers have evaluated the involvement of various genes, including 16S rRNA, secA1, hsp65, gyrA, and rpoB [11], and the molecular identification of Nocardia through 16S DNA sequencing is regarded as the gold standard [12]. However, polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based detection necessitates designing specific primers or probes, which numerous laboratories have yet to undertake.

mNGS, also termed high-throughput sequencing technology, offers the advantages of high efficiency and wide coverage. Nevertheless, mNGS exhibits a significantly higher false-positive detection rate for pathogenic bacteria compared with conventional detection methods [13], and the cost of mNGS remains high, causing challenges in achieving widespread adoption.

The morphological analysis of BALF facilitates identifying diverse cell types, classifying nucleated cells, discriminating parasites and crystalline species, and identifying bacteria or fungi, among other tangible components [14]. The morphological analysis of Nocardia can determine the numerical count, proportional shifts, and structural hallmarks of various cell types and facilitate detecting pathogenic bacteria by observing cellular–bacterial interactions. However, Nocardia staining often fails to distinguish the bacteria from the surrounding background cells. Furthermore, morphological BALF analysis necessitates a concentrated specimen because a concentrated and enriched smear substantially enhances the likelihood of detecting pathogenic bacteria.

Conclusion

We recommend combining morphological BALF analysis with Gram staining for microscopic examination to improve Nocardia pneumonia detection. Weak acid-fast staining should be used to confirm findings suggestive of Nocardia infections. In positive cases, clinicians should extend the culture time, observe chalky coloration, monitor colony growth, and isolate or purify the bacteria promptly. Where possible, mNGS should be conducted to shorten the detection time. The morphological analysis of BALF is simple, fast, and effective for initial screening and reduces missed and misdiagnosis.
