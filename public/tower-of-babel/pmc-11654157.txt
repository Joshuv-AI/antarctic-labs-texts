Liver abscess and splenic infarction due to Yersinia pseudotuberculosis bloodstream infection: a case report
Authors: Yixun Wang, Yanni Xiang, Chao Lei, Xiaxia Zheng, Wen Wu, ZhaoHui Zhang, Xingguang Qu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYersinia pseudotuberculosis is an emerging zoonotic intestinal pathogen primarily transmitted through contaminated food and water. Infections caused by Yersinia pseudotuberculosis are typically self-limiting, often presenting as gastroenteritis or “pseudoappendicitis,” which is characterized by fever and abdominal pain. Although bloodstream infections with Yersinia pseudotuberculosis are rare, they can lead to multiple distant sites of infection, including abscesses in the liver, spleen, and kidneys, as well as in the hip and knee joints, particularly in individuals with underlying immunodeficiency.Case presentationWe report the case of a 40-year-old male patient who was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) with intermittent fever lasting for five days, followed by exacerbation of abdominal pain and distension one day after ingesting contaminated food. Upon admission, the patient exhibited severe hypotension, which rapidly progressed to signs of liver and kidney failure, ultimately leading to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS). Notably, abdominal computed tomography (CT) revealed evidence of a liver abscess and splenic infarction. The treatment regimen included fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, vasopressors, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), and plasma exchange. Blood cultures along with metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) confirmed an infection caused by Yersinia pseudotuberculosis. After 17 days in the ICU, the patient was transferred to the infectious disease department for an additional 14 days of treatment before being discharged. Three months post-discharge, follow-up visits indicated that the patient was in good health.ConclusionsTo the best of our knowledge, this case represents a rare instance of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), liver abscess, and splenic infarction resulting from Yersinia pseudotuberculosis infection. The identification of the pathogen was facilitated by blood culture and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of blood samples. Background

Yersinia pseudotuberculosis (Y. pseudotuberculosis) is a Gram-negative, facultatively anaerobic coccobacillus that has a diverse range of animal hosts, including both domestic and wild mammals as well as birds [1]. This bacterium represents an emerging zoonotic enteric pathogen primarily transmitted through contaminated food and water. Notably, Y. pseudotuberculosis can persist in refrigeration environments where contamination occurs, leading to its colloquial designation as “refrigerator disease” [2]. Infections caused by Y. pseudotuberculosis are typically self-limiting and often manifest as gastroenteritis or “pseudoappendicitis,” which is characterized by fever and abdominal pain. Systemic infections, including sepsis attributed to Y. pseudotuberculosis, are rare and generally occur in individuals with pre-existing conditions such as cirrhosis, diabetes mellitus, organ transplants, severe anemia, malignancies, or HIV infection. Although bloodstream infections with Y. pseudotuberculosis are uncommon, they can lead to multiple distant sites of infection—including abscesses in the liver, spleen, or kidneys—often occurring in patients with underlying immunodeficiency.

Case presentation

A 40-year-old man was admitted to the emergency department of Yichang Central People’s Hospital (Yichang, China) due to complaints of fever and abdominal pain. Five days prior to presenting at the emergency department, he endured persistent high fever, abdominal pain, and lethargy after consuming some half-cooked foods from the refrigerator. He received intravenous treatment with levofloxacin 2 days before admission. The patient denied recent travel, contact with sick individuals, animal exposure, or water-related activities. He worked in waste recycling and had no known history of diabetes, hepatitis, tuberculosis, or other immunocompromising conditions. His body temperature was 38.5 °C, blood pressure was 80/50 mmHg, heart rate was 120 beats/min with no murmurs, respiratory rate was 28 breaths/min, and he had a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 13. Abdominal examination disclosed generalized muscle rigidity, tenderness in the left upper quadrant without rebound tenderness, mild tenderness at McBurney’s point, no hepatosplenomegaly, and slightly hyperactive bowel sounds. Laboratory tests revealed an elevated white blood cell count (16.34 × 10⁹/L), decreased hemoglobin (61 g/L), a normal platelet count of 152 × 10⁹/L, elevated procalcitonin (9.6 ng/ml), elevated interleukin-6 (3918 pg/ml), elevated C-reactive protein (325 mg/L), elevated serum lactate (6.8 mmol/L), elevated alanine aminotransferase (156 U/L), and an elevated aspartate aminotransferase (177 U/L). Both total and direct bilirubin levels were mildly elevated. Serum creatinine was 101 mmol/L, random blood glucose was 19.6 mmol/L, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was 8.43%.

The patient was initially diagnosed with septic shock secondary to abdominal infection in the intensive care unit (ICU). Due to severe hypotension, the patient was started on norepinephrine and vasopressin to maintain a mean arterial pressure (MAP) of ≥ 65 mmHg. Subsequently, intravenous treatment with piperacillin-tazobactam (administered at a dose of 4.5 g every 8 h) was promptly commenced, accompanied by the implementation of the Sepsis Bundle protocol as per the established guidelines [3]. On the 4th day of hospitalization, the results of metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), with the sample labeled as No.UGPE4W5S and processed by Hangzhou Matridx Biotechnology Co., Ltd. in Hangzhou, China, indicated the presence of Y. pseudotuberculosis and Human betaherpesvirus 6B. Notably, no sequence number corresponding to other pathogens were detected. The mNGS analysis identified Y. pseudotuberculosis with a sequence number of 7 and a relative abundance of 17.08%, and the quality index was determined to be 10633.27, which exceeded that of 92.16% of comparable samples. This pathogen was not tested for virulence factors and antibiotic resistance genes were not detected. On the 5th day, two sets of blood culture tests yielded positive results at 25, 28.1, 35.1, and 53.7 h after sample collection using the Fully automated BACT/ALERT-3D blood culture system (bioMérieux, Marcy l’Etoile, France). The pathogen isolated was identified as Y. pseudotuberculosis by using the automatic microbial mass spectrometry detection system (VITEK MS, Kratos Analytical Limited, United Kingdom). Laboratory personnel then proceeded to draw the culture fluid for smear staining. Under microscopic examination, Gram-negative rod bacteria with a thick morphology were observed. The culture medium was subsequently transferred to solid media, specifically columbia blood medium and chocolate medium, and incubated in a CO₂ incubator (6% CO2)maintained at 35 °C for a period of 24 ~ 48 h. At the end of this incubation period, the growth of smooth, moist small colonies was noted (Fig. 1). Antibiotic susceptibility testing, by using the minimum inhibitory concentration method, demonstrated that Y. pseudotuberculosis exhibited sensitivity to ampicillin, ampicillin/sulbactam, cefotiam, ceftazidime, cefmetazole, meropenem, gentamicin, and levofloxacin. Based on the comprehensive findings of the blood culture results and the mNGS report, a multidisciplinary team discussion concluded that the patient’s condition was attributable to Y. pseudotuberculosis infection. Consequently, the decision was made to defer treatment for Human herpesvirus 6B for the time being. In accordance with the recommendations provided in the Sanford Guide to Antimicrobial Therapy and considering the patient’s glomerular filtration rate, the antibiotic regimen was adjusted to include gentamicin (administered at a dose of 160,000 U once daily) and levofloxacin in sodium chloride (administered at a volume of 100 ml once daily). Given the severity of the septic shock, it was determined that continued administration of meropenem (at a dose of 1.0 g every 8 h) was warranted.

Owing to the rapid deterioration of renal function and the presence of progressive oliguria, the patient commenced continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) with citrate anticoagulation on the second day of hospitalization. After a course of treatment, as the renal function improved and the urine output increased, CRRT was discontinued on the tenth day. Coagulation abnormalities and elevated blood ammonia levels were indicative of acute liver injury. Plasma exchange therapy, utilizing 2000 ml of fresh frozen plasma, was initiated on the fifth day in the ICU. Following seven rounds of plasma exchanges, the liver function returned to normal. Brain, chest, and contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scans (Fig. 2: A, B) disclosed no brain abnormalities, bilateral lung infiltrates accompanied by mild pleural effusion, multiple hypodense liver lesions suggestive of infection, splenic infarction, retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy, and a small amount of pelvic fluid. Tests for hepatitis A, B, C, E, and autoimmune hepatitis yielded negative results, thereby ruling out liver diseases as the cause of the acute liver injury. Point-of-care abdominal ultrasonography revealed no evidence of intrahepatic bile duct dilatation. All tests for HIV infection, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and extractable nuclear antigen (ENA) yielded negative results, and the stool culture also tested negative.

Subsequent to the implementation of combined antibiotic therapy, shock management strategies, glycemic control measures, intermittent red blood cell transfusions, and organ support interventions, the patient’s body temperature normalized on the 8th day. The patient’s blood pressure stabilized, with vasopressin being discontinued on the 6th day and norepinephrine on the 12th day. Blood cultures obtained on the 7th, 12th, and 18th days were negative. Meropenem was administered for a duration of 6 days, while gentamicin and levofloxacin were continued for 8 days. Abdominal CT scans conducted on the 12th, 19th, and 27th days demonstrated a significant resolution of the hepatic lesions, with no further progression of the splenic infarction (Fig. 2: C, D, E).

After spending 17 days in the ICU, the patient was transferred to the infectious diseases unit for an additional 13 days of further treatment and was subsequently discharged after reaching a stable condition. During the 3-month outpatient follow-up, the patient reported no significant discomfort. A follow-up abdominal CT scan (Fig. 1: F) exhibited a marked reduction in the liver lesions, while the splenic infarction remained stable without any further enlargement. Regular telephone follow-up did not reveal any signs of recurrence. The imaging and laboratory findings during the course of treatment are presented in Table 1; Fig. 2.

Discussion and conclusions

We report a case of an adult patient who developed septic shock, acute kidney injury, acute liver injury, liver abscess, and splenic infarction due to Y. pseudotuberculosis bacteremia. The most common manifestations of Y. pseudotuberculosis infection are gastroenteritis and “pseudoappendicitis,” characterized by fever and abdominal pain [3, 4]. Generally, Y. pseudotuberculosis infection is a self-limiting disease that often does not require antimicrobial treatment [4]. Severe cases usually occur in patients with underlying conditions, including liver cirrhosis, diabetes, organ transplantation, severe anemia, malignancy, and HIV infection [5]. In 2021, Hashimoto et al. [6] reported a case of severe sepsis in an adult with normal immune function caused by Y. pseudotuberculosis, and the bacterium was identified by serotyping and the determination of virulence factors. In this case, it was a very severe systemic infection which indicates that this strain is highly virulent. There is a broad variation in the pathogenicity of different Y. pseudotuberculosis strains and therefor, it is important to study the virulence genes present in the strain. However, neither our mNGS assay nor the microbiology laboratory of our hospital tested Yersinia pseudotuberculosis for virulence factors, which is the liminations in this case.

In this patient, who had no previous medical history of HIV infection, cancer, immune disease, or liver disease, we speculate that the main risk factor may have been undiagnosed diabetes before admission. Regarding the route of infection, upon careful inquiry, the patient’s family reported the habit of using a microwave to reheat food stored in the refrigerator. Despite the patient’s negative stool culture result, we hypothesized that undercooked or contaminated food was the probable origin of the infection.In another case report, the authors also hold the view that the bloodstream infection of Y.pseudotuberculosis originates from intestinal infection, mainly due to the consumption of contaminated food or water [6].

In terms of treatment, the patient received multiple antibiotics and timely interventions with plasma exchange and CRRT. Without a doubt, plasma exchange is the first-line treatment for acute liver failure [7]. After seven plasma exchange sessions, the patient’s liver function returned to normal. Moreover, plasma exchange plays an essential role in treating severe sepsis by clearing inflammatory mediators, correcting coagulopathy, and restoring internal environment stability [8]. The patient’s renal failure was primarily attributed to septic shock, and after 9 days of bedside CRRT, her urine output and serum creatinine levels returned to normal.

In most cases of Y. pseudotuberculosis infection, hepatic involvement is uncommon. Potentially due to limitations in imaging techniques, most cases of liver abscess reported in early literature were diagnosed postmortem during autopsy [9]. Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT ruled out hepatocellular carcinoma and hepatic hemangioma, leading to the consideration of a hepatic abscess due to bacteremia. Y. pseudotuberculosis is not a common pathogen for hepatic abscess, but diabetes is an independent risk factor for liver abscess [10]. For severe adult cases of Y. pseudotuberculosis infection, a combination of ceftriaxone (2 g, once daily) and gentamicin (5 mg/kg) is recommended for at least three weeks [11]. In our case, due to severe septic shock early on, after consulting with infectious disease experts (microbiologists), we primarily used gentamicin, levofloxacin, and meropenem. Although gentamicin has potential nephrotoxicity, we adjusted the dosage on the basis of the patient’s glomerular filtration rate. After antibiotic treatment, the patient’s body temperature gradually returned to normal, and infection indicators such as white blood cell count, procalcitonin and IL-6 showed a significant downward trend. It is worth noting that in this case, the level of Interleukin-6 in the patient increased rapidly on the day of admission and reached its peak on the second day. After active anti-infective treatment, it began to decline significantly on the third day. The changing process of IL-6 was in line with the progress of the patient’s condition. Studies have demonstrated that the level of IL-6 is associated with the severity of bloodstream infections [12]. Persistently high concentrations of IL-6 usually indicate a poor prognosis, and dynamically observing its level is also helpful for understanding the progress of infectious diseases and the response to treatment [13]. Multiple follow-up abdominal CT scans revealed that the hepatic infection focus was controlled without liquefaction or abscess formation, avoiding the need for drainage or surgical intervention.

To the best of our knowledge, there have been no clinical case reports of splenic infarction caused by Y. pseudotuberculosis infection. In our case, the patient’s ECG, echocardiography, and laboratory tests excluded embolic splenic infarction caused by infective endocarditis and atrial fibrillation. It has been reported that splenic infarction is often closely related to decreased splenic artery perfusion, hypercoagulable state, complicated abdominal diseases (inflammatory or malignant) and sepsis [14]. We speculate that the splenic infarction in this patient may be related to septic shock, hypotension, and decreased splenic artery blood flow. The dangerous complications of splenic infarction include the formation of pseudocysts, abscesses, hemorrhage, splenic rupture, and aneurysms [15]. Fortunately, none of these complications occurred in our patient.

The detection of pathogens with a low sequence number by blood mNGS brings certain challenges to clinical diagnosis [16], as it is difficult to determine whether these pathogens with a low sequence number are truly pathogenic. The main reasons for the low sequence number detected by mNGS are as follows [17–20]: during the sample processing, problems such as nucleic acid loss and contamination may occur, leading to a reduction in the number of detected pathogen sequences; secondly, impurities and cell fragments in the blood specimen may interfere with the detection, resulting in the appearance of a low sequence number; thirdly, the characteristics of low-abundance pathogens themselves may also lead to the detection of a low sequence number by mNGS; fourthly, the use of antibiotics can also reduce the number of pathogens in the blood, resulting in a low sequence number. The mNGS report of this case showed only 7 sequence number of Y. pseudotuberculosis, which is generally considered a low sequence number. However, considering that the result was obtained from a peripheral blood specimen and the relative abundance of the bacterial sequence reached 17.08%, it indicates that the pathogen sequence accounted for a considerable proportion among all the identified microbial sequences. In addition, the quality indicators were significantly higher than those of similar samples, thus supporting the reliability of the nucleic acid sequence counts obtained from this specimen [21]. The patient in this case had been administered broad-spectrum antibiotics for several days before the mNGS test, which might also be an important reason for the low sequence number [22]. Therefore, when interpreting the blood mNGS report, one cannot solely rely on the sequence number in the sequencing result. For the situation of a low pathogenic sequence number, it is more necessary to make a judgment based on comprehensive information such as the patient’s clinical symptoms, medical history, and physical signs. Considering that Y. pseudotuberculosis was cultured in all four blood - culture specimens of this patient, we comprehensively determined that the mNGS test result was also reliable.

In conclusion, we herein report a case of septic shock, liver abscess, splenic infarction and MODS caused by Y. pseudotuberculosis infection. The combined application of blood mNGS and blood culture can maximize the detection of pathogenic microorganisms in bloodstream infections [17]. This report will help to raise awareness among clinicians that Y. pseudotuberculosis bacteremia should be included in the differential diagnosis when patients exhibit fever and abdominalgia after consuming undercooked food from the refrigerator.
