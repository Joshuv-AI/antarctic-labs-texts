Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) versus metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-associated steatotic liver disease (Met-ALD), do the differences matter in clinical practice?
Authors: Christopher D. Byrne
Published: 2026

The consequences of steatotic liver disease (SLD) are a considerable current and future burden for global health (1,2). Not only does SLD increase the risk of major adverse liver outcomes (MALOs) such as cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), but SLD forms part of a multi-system disease that also increases the risk of extrahepatic disease outcomes (3-5). Many of these extrahepatic disease outcomes contribute a considerable healthcare major burden, with SLD increasing risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and obesity related cancers (3-5).

Increasing evidence suggests that alcoholic SLD is strongly linked with metabolic syndrome (MetS) traits (6) and in 2023 a multi-society Delphi consensus statement redefined SLD to distinguish between metabolic dysfunction-associated SLD (MASLD) and metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-associated SLD (Met-ALD) (7). Within this classification, MASLD comprises liver steatosis and ≥1 MetS trait, together with alcohol consumption <210 g and <140 g per week for men and women, respectively (7). In contrast, a diagnosis of Met-ALD comprises the presence of liver steatosis with ≥1 MetS trait and alcohol consumption between 210 and 450 g per week for men, and 140 and 350 g per week for women (7).

In 2010, in two important studies from the UK, one in men in Scotland (8), and the other in women in England (9), investigated the relationship between modest alcohol consumption and overweight/obesity and risk of incident liver cirrhosis. In the Million Women Study in England, Liu and colleagues showed risk of liver cirrhosis increased by 28% for every 5-unit alcohol increase in women with a body mass index (BMI) >22.5 kg/m2 (9). The absolute risk of cirrhosis was 1/1,000 women over 5 years and 1,811 women had a 1st hospital admission or died from cirrhosis (9). The investigators established that hospital admissions or deaths from liver cirrhosis could be attributed to alcohol consumption in 42% of affected individuals, and 17% of hospital admissions or death from liver cirrhosis could be attributed to excess body weight (9). Importantly, in this study, the combination of obesity and modest alcohol consumption (≥150 g per week) was associated with about a five-fold increase in incidence of cirrhosis compared to women with a similar BMI who consumed <70 g of alcohol/week (9). The 2nd of these studies in Scottish men (n=9,559) investigated the relationship between baseline BMI and self-reported alcohol consumption on liver-related morbidity and mortality after 29 years median follow-up (8). Similar to Liu and colleagues’ results (9), BMI and alcohol consumption were both strongly and independently associated with mortality from liver disease (8). However, in this study in men, overweight/obesity, and alcohol consumption of 15 units or more each week (approximately 150 g), had a synergistic effect to increase the risk of liver-related morbidity and death (8). Thus, in the context of a diagnosis of Met-ALD and the 2023 diagnostic criteria, these two UK studies from over 15 years ago emphasise an important interaction between overweight and obesity as a metabolic risk factor together with modest alcohol consumption, to amplify the risk of incident MALOs.

In support of the notion that metabolic risk factors interact with modest alcohol to amply adverse disease outcomes; in 2025, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 studies involving 17.6 million patients showed a significant increase in liver-related events and composite cardiovascular outcomes across all SLD subtypes (10). Additionally, patients with Met-ALD had a higher risk of liver-related events compared to patients with MASLD) (10). Similarly, in 2025, Celsa and colleagues undertook a systematic review/meta-analysis including 24 studies, involving 11,575,558 individuals (11). In total, 9,801,312 had MASLD and 1,774,246 had Met-ALD. Individuals with Met-ALD had increased risk of liver-related events [hazard ratio (HR) =1.62; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.16, 2.25; P=0.0086], HCC (HR =1.33; 95% CI: 1.00, 1.77; P=0.048), and extrahepatic cancers (HR =1.03; 95% CI: 1.01, 1.06; P<0.0001) compared with those with MASLD (11). For cardiovascular events, extrahepatic cancer deaths and all-cause mortality, there were no significant differences between Met-ALD and MASLD (11). Thus, the evidence from these two meta-analyses, and the evidence from the UK over 15 years ago discussed above, suggest that modest alcohol consumption combined with overweight/obesity (as a key MetS trait), interact to increases risk of adverse liver-related outcomes.

Although the evidence suggests that modest alcohol consumption may further increase the risk of MALO in Met-ALD compared to MASLD; it is important to recognise that for both SLDs, there can be very variable severity of metabolic dysfunction (manifest by the number of MetS traits). Increasing evidence is now suggesting that the heterogeneity of metabolic dysfunction is a very important contributor to the risk of disease/death, not only for MALO, but also for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), CKD, extrahepatic cancers, and all-cause mortality (12,13). Recently, we have studied this issue using data from the UK Biobank prospective cohort (12,13). SLD, defined by a hepatic steatosis index (HSI) >36 (14), was identified amongst the 502,381 UK Biobank participants. MetS traits and MetS (greater than or equal to 3 traits) were identified using the MASLD and Met-ALD criteria (12,13). Cox regression was used to estimate adjusted HRs and 95% CIs of MASLD or Met-ALD plus 1 to 5 MetS traits with incident MALO, MACE, obesity related cancers and 5-/10-year incidence rates, versus the reference group, which was no SLD/MetS traits (13). In summary, current evidence suggests that for MASLD and for Met-ALD, the risk of incident MALO increases according to the number of individual MetS traits that are present. For overweight/obesity (amongst easily measurable MetS traits), the evidence shows that modest alcohol consumption and overweight/obesity synergistically increase the risk of liver-related morbidity and mortality. Although the introduction of Met-ALD as a distinct clinical entity is challenging in clinical practice, this diagnostic category introduces opportunities for research to understand the various aspects of natural history, risk stratification, pharmacological management, and designing of clinical trials (15). Such studies need to focus not just on liver disease outcomes but also outcomes involving other organ systems beyond the liver. The latter is an example of an exciting new area for research in this field. For example, recently it has been shown that both MASLD and Met-ALD are associated with Preserved Ratio Impaired Spirometry (PRISm) (16). PRISm is a distinct pattern of lung function impairment, and this novel finding suggests a link between metabolic dysfunction in the liver and non-obstructive pulmonary impairment, further illustrating the need for holistic approaches to care in MASLD and Met-ALD.
