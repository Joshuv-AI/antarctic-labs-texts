A PAC-Bayesian Analysis of Channel-Induced Degradation in Edge Inference
Yangshuo He and Guanding Yu, Jingge Zhu

Abstract
In the emerging paradigm of edge inference, neural networks (NNs) are partitioned across distributed edge devices that collaboratively perform inference via wireless transmission. However, standard NNs are generally trained in a noiseless environment, creating a mismatch with the noisy channels during edge deployment. In this paper, we address this issue by characterizing the channel-induced performance deterioration as a generalization error against unseen channels. We introduce an augmented NN model that incorporates channel statistics directly into the weight space, allowing us to derive PAC-Bayesian generalization bounds that explicitly quantify the impact of wireless distortion. We further provide closed-form expressions for practical channels to demonstrate the tractability of these bounds. Inspired by the theoretical results, we propose a channel-aware training algorithm that minimizes a surrogate objective based on the derived bound. Simulations show that the proposed algorithm can effectively improve inference accuracy by leveraging channel statistics, without end-to-end re-training.



I
Introduction

The emerging demand for deploying Artificial Intelligence (AI) applications in edge devices has motivated the integration of deep learning and wireless communications, termed edge AI
[
1
]
.
A key application in this area is edge inference, specifically via edge co-inference architecture
[
2
]
. In this setting, a neural network (NN) is split and deployed across separate edge devices, where the transmitter processes initial NN layers and sends extracted features over wireless channels to the receiver
[
3
]
.
The deployed NNs in edge inference are generally pre-trained and optimized for specific tasks. However, these baseline models are typically trained in idealized, channel-free conditions, without considering future edge deployment.
During actual edge inference, the feature transmission between edge devices is corrupted by the noisy wireless channel. This inherent mismatch between the noiseless training environment and the noisy edge deployment inevitably results in inference performance degradation.

To address this challenge, existing efforts primarily rely on communication-inference co-design frameworks. These works focus on either resource allocation strategies
[
3
,
4
]
or optimized feature transmission mechanisms
[
5
]
.
Alternative approaches are based on the end-to-end training framework
[
6
,
7
]
which incorporates wireless channels directly into the NN to optimize edge inference accuracy.
While effective, they generally require novel NN architectures and model re-training for varying channel conditions.
Despite these practical advancements, theoretical analysis in edge inference remains limited. Recent studies utilize the information bottleneck framework
[
8
]
or information-theoretic bounds
[
9
]
to evaluate the trade-off between inference accuracy and channel distortion.
However, they premise the analysis on end-to-end learning, where the NN is jointly trained with channel distributions. Thus, these results cannot explicitly characterize the performance degradation due to the mismatch between noiseless training and noisy inference.

In this paper, we consider the edge inference scenario where an NN is trained in a traditional channel-free framework and subsequently deployed in a wireless edge network. We formulate the performance deterioration caused by this mismatch as a wireless generalization error.
Unlike the traditional generalization regarding unseen data samples, we extend this concept to characterize the model’s robustness against unseen channel conditions.
To facilitate theoretical analysis, we introduce an augmented NN model that explicitly integrates channel statistics into the weight space. Specifically, we model the wireless channel as an additional stochastic layer in the NN, and the layer’s weights are random variables determined by channel statistics. Leveraging the PAC-Bayesian framework
[
10
,
11
]
, we derive high-probability bounds that explicitly quantify the edge inference performance degradation caused by the channel-free training mismatch. We further specialize these bounds in various practical wireless environments, facilitating closed-form bounds characterized by channel statistics (e.g., outage probability and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)). Finally, we propose a channel-aware training algorithm designed to minimize a tractable surrogate of the derived bound. By incorporating channel statistics in the training objective, the proposed algorithm improves the edge inference performance against channel-induced distortions, without requiring instantaneous channel realizations.

II
Problem Formulation

The primary focus of this work is to derive a theoretical upper bound on the generalization error of NNs deployed in wireless edge systems. Unlike traditional deep learning, where training and inference occur under identical noiseless conditions, wireless edge deployment introduces a distribution shift caused by channels that are not necessarily present in the training stage.
To formalize this “clean” training and noisy inference mismatch, we first introduce an augmented NN model that characterizes wireless channels as stochastic weights. Then, we establish a PAC-Bayesian framework for the weight mismatch, enabling us to quantify the performance degradation caused by the channel distortion.

II-A

System Model and Augmented Network

Let
\tilde{W}=(\tilde{W}^{(1)},\tilde{W}^{(2)},\cdots,\tilde{W}^{(L)})\in\tilde{\mathcal{W}}
be the weights of a baseline
L
-layer NN. We consider an instance space
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, which is the product of a feature space
\mathcal{X}
and a label space
\mathcal{Y}
. A training set
S=(Z_{1},Z_{2},\cdots,Z_{n})
consists of
n
i.i.d. samples
Z_{i}\in\mathcal{Z}
drawn from a data distribution
P_{Z}
. We denote the distribution of the training set
S
as
P_{S}=P_{Z}^{n}
.
The learning algorithm is formalized as a stochastic mapping from training data
S
to weights
\tilde{W}
, characterized by the conditional distribution
P_{\tilde{W}|S}
over
\tilde{\mathcal{W}}
.

When an NN task is deployed in wireless systems, the NN is generally split into two parts that communicate through the wireless channel. Due to channel impairments, the hidden layer features transmitted by the NN on the transmitter may be distorted, causing errors during the subsequent inference in NN on the receiver.
These channel-induced distortions on the features can be captured by a fully connected (FC) layer. Therefore, we introduce an additional layer to characterize the NN deployed in wireless environments, as shown in Figure
1
.

This structural equivalence allows us to integrate the channel statistics directly into the weight space.
Let
l_{0}
denote the additional layer, the output of this layer can be formulated as
f^{(l_{0})}=M^{(l_{0})}f^{(l_{0}-1)}+B^{(l_{0})}
,
where
f^{(l_{0}-1)},f^{(l_{0})}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the input and output features, respectively. The weights of this layer
W^{(l_{0})}=(M^{(l_{0})},B^{(l_{0})})
consist of a weight matrix
M^{(l_{0})}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and a bias vector
B^{(l_{0})}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. These stochastic weights are determined by the specific channel conditions.
While this formulation represents an affine transformation, it serves as a versatile abstraction capable of modeling various wireless environments. In this framework, even non-linear operations in digital channels including quantization and coding can be encapsulated into the effective stochastic weights. Detailed examples are discussed in Section
IV-A
.

By incorporating the additional FC layer, we define an augmented NN consisting of
L+1
-layer weights
W=(W^{(1)},W^{(2)},\cdots,W^{(L+1)})\in\mathcal{W}
, where the non-learnable weights on the
l_{0}
-th layer can represent the wireless channels and the rest of the layers
\tilde{W}=(W^{(l)})_{l\neq l_{0}}
correspond to the learnable weights of the baseline
L
-layer NN. In the rest of this article, we will focus on the
L+1
-layer augmented NN.

II-B

Probabilistic framework and Wireless Generalization Error

Let
W
and
W^{\prime}
denote the weights of the augmented NN in the absence and presence of wireless channel, respectively. We utilize
W
to characterize the weights during the noiseless training phase, while
W^{\prime}
to represent the weights during edge inference.
Therefore, in the training phase, the
l_{0}
-th layer weights are
W^{(l_{0})}=(\mathbf{I}_{d},\mathbf{0})
, indicating no channel perturbation on the hidden layer feature. As this layer is independent of other layers, the learning algorithm
P_{W|S}
in channel-free training can be defined as

\begin{split}P_{W|S}(W|S)&\coloneqq P_{\tilde{W}|S}\left(\tilde{W}|S\right)\delta_{\left(\mathbf{I}_{d},\mathbf{0}\right)}\left(W^{(l_{0})}\right),\end{split}

(1)

where
\tilde{W}=(W^{(l)})_{l\neq l_{0}}
denotes the weights of the baseline
L
-layer NN and
\delta_{\left(\mathbf{I}_{d},\mathbf{0}\right)}
is the Dirac measure centered at the noiseless weights
(\mathbf{I}_{d},\mathbf{0})
.

When the NN is deployed in a wireless system for edge inference, the learnable weights
\tilde{W}
are frozen and inherited directly from the training phase, implying
{W^{\prime}}^{(l)}={W}^{(l)}
for
l\neq l_{0}
. However, channel distortions are introduced in the
l_{0}
-th layer. Thus, the
l_{0}
-th layer weights
{W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}
are determined by the wireless channel characterization, independent of the other layers.
Therefore, we can formulate the channel effect as a probabilistic transition
P_{{W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{W}^{(l_{0})}}
over
\mathcal{W}^{(l_{0})}
.
Combining with the deterministic preservation of other layers, the conditional distribution
P_{W^{\prime}|W}
over
\mathcal{W}
can be defined as

\begin{split}P_{W^{\prime}|W}\left(W^{\prime}|W\right)\coloneqq&P_{{W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{W}^{(l_{0})}}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)\\
&\cdot\prod_{l\neq l_{0}}\delta_{{W}^{(l)}}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l)}\right).\end{split}

(2)

The weights
W^{\prime}
are distributed according to this distribution in the inference phase rather than the learning algorithm in training phase.
Given weights
W
trained in a channel-free condition, the weights
W^{\prime}
in the wireless edge inference are independent of the training set
S
. This implies a Markov chain:
S\to W\to W^{\prime}
.
Therefore, the joint distribution
P_{W^{\prime}W|S}
over joint space
\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{W}
is given by

P_{W^{\prime}W|S}\left(W^{\prime},W|S\right)\coloneqq P_{W^{\prime}|W}\left(W^{\prime}|W\right)P_{W|S}\left(W|S\right).

(3)

Both distributions
P_{W|S}
and
P_{W^{\prime}|S}
are the marginal distributions over
\mathcal{W}
induced by this joint distribution
P_{W^{\prime}W|S}
.

To evaluate the wireless generalization error against channel noise, we quantify the performance degradation of an NN when shifting from a channel-free training environment to a practical noisy inference phase in a wireless system. We consider a loss function
l:\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
. For specific weights
w
, the inference quality is measured by the population risk on the sample space
L(w)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{P_{Z}}[l(w,Z)]
.
However, since the data distribution
P_{Z}
is generally unknown, the population risk
L(w)
is unobservable. Instead, the learning algorithm computes the average of the loss function on the observable training set, named empirical risk
\hat{L}(w,S)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}l(w,Z_{i})
.

In the PAC-Bayesian framework, the NN selects a class of weights according to a distribution, rather than selecting specific weights randomly.
To measure the performance of a given weight distribution
P
, we adopt the expected population risk over the randomness of weights, denoted by
L(P)=\int_{\mathcal{W}}L(w)P(dw)
. Accordingly, the expected empirical risk is denoted by
\hat{L}_{S}(P)=\int_{\mathcal{W}}\hat{L}(w,S)P(dw)
. Based on these definitions, we define the performance degradation induced by the mismatch between channel-free training conditions and noisy wireless channel as wireless generalization error:

\Delta\coloneqq L\left(P_{W^{\prime}|S}\right)-\hat{L}_{S}\left(P_{W|S}\right).

(4)

This difference implicitly evaluates the distribution shift caused by the wireless channel
P_{W^{\prime}|W}.

Next, we will derive PAC-Bayesian bounds for this difference, which characterizes the inherent robustness against wireless channel perturbations.

III
Main Results

In this section, we derive PAC-Bayesian bounds for the wireless generalization error defined in (
4
).
It connects the population risk
L(P_{W^{\prime}|S})
in the noisy inference phase to the empirical risk
\hat{L}_{S}(P_{W|S})
observed during the “clean” training phase. We begin by establishing a general theorem for any data-independent prior. Subsequently, we investigate the optimal structure of the prior distribution and derive the tightest form of channel-aware bound.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that the loss function
l(w,z)
is
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian under
P_{Z}
for all
w\in\mathcal{W}
and is
K
-Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{W}
for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, then, for all data-independent prior distribution
Q_{W^{\prime}W}
over
\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{W}
and
k>0
, w.p. at least
1-\epsilon
over
S
, we have

\begin{split}\Delta&\leq\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{2n}+\frac{D(P_{W^{\prime}W|S}\|Q_{W^{\prime}W})-\log\epsilon}{k}\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{k}\log\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}W}}\left[e^{kKd_{\mathcal{W}}\left(W^{\prime},W\right)}\right],\end{split}

(5)

where the distance metric
d_{\mathcal{W}}
on the weight space is defined as the sum of layer-wise distances
d_{\mathcal{W}}(W^{\prime},W)=\sum_{l=1}^{L+1}d^{(l)}({W^{\prime}}^{(l)},{W}^{(l)})
, and
d^{(l)}
is a metric appropriate for the
l
-th layer weights.

Proof.

The following lemmas are used to prove the theorem.

Lemma 1

(Donsker-Varadhan variational representation)
.

For any two probability measures
\rho
and
\pi
defined on a measurable space
(\mathcal{X},\Sigma)
where
\Sigma
is a
\sigma
-algebra on the set
\mathcal{X}
. Let
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function such that
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi}\left[e^{f(X)}\right]<\infty
, then

D(\rho\|\pi)=\sup_{f}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\rho}\left[f(X)\right]-\log\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi}\left[e^{f(X)}\right]\right\}.

(6)

Lemma 2

(Markov’s inequality)
.

If
X
is a non-negative random variable and
\delta\in(0,1)
, then

\Pr\left\{X\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{\delta}\right\}\geq 1-\delta.

(7)

By applying the Lemma
1
with
\rho=P_{W^{\prime}W|S}
,
\pi=Q_{W^{\prime}W}
, and
f=k(L(w^{\prime})-\hat{L}(w,S))
, we have

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}_{P_{W^{\prime}W|S}}\left[k\left(L\left(W^{\prime}\right)-\hat{L}(W,S)\right)\right]\\
&\leq\log\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}W}}\left[e^{k\left(L\left(W^{\prime}\right)-\hat{L}(W,S)\right)}\right]+D(P_{W^{\prime}W|S}\|Q_{W^{\prime}W}).\end{split}

(8)

The LHS can be simplified as
k\Delta
. To upper bound the first term on the RHS, we utilize Lemma
2
with
X=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}W}}[e^{k(L(W^{\prime})-\hat{L}(W,S))}]
and take logarithm: w.p. at least
1-\epsilon
over
S
, we have

\begin{split}&\log\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}W}}\left[e^{k\left(L\left(W^{\prime}\right)-\hat{L}(W,S)\right)}\right]\\
&\leq\log\mathbb{E}_{P_{S}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}W}}\left[e^{k\left(L\left(W^{\prime}\right)-\hat{L}(W,S)\right)}\right]-\log\epsilon\\
&\overset{(a)}{=}\log\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}W}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{S}}\left[e^{k\left(L\left(W^{\prime}\right)-\hat{L}(W,S)\right)}\right]-\log\epsilon,\end{split}

(9)

where (a) is because
Q_{W^{\prime}W}
is data-independent, thus we can swap the expectations by the Fubini’s theorem.
We further decompose the exponent as
L(W^{\prime})-\hat{L}(W,S)=(L(W^{\prime})-L(W))+(L(W)-\hat{L}(W,S))
, and we have

\begin{split}&\log\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}W}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{S}}\left[e^{k\left(L\left(W^{\prime}\right)-\hat{L}(W,S)\right)}\right]\\
&=\log\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}W}}\left[e^{k(L(W^{\prime})-L(W))}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{P_{S}}\left[e^{k(L(W)-\hat{L}(W,S))}\right]\right]\\
&\overset{(a)}{\leq}\log\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}W}}\left[e^{k(L(W^{\prime})-L(W))}\cdot e^{\frac{k^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2n}}\right]\\
&=\log\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}W}}\left[e^{k(L(W^{\prime})-L(W))}\right]+\frac{k^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2n}\\
&\overset{(b)}{\leq}\log\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}W}}\left[e^{kKd_{\mathcal{W}}(W^{\prime},W)}\right]+\frac{k^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2n},\end{split}

(10)

where (a) follows from the
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian property of the loss function and (b) follows from the
K
-Lipschitz continuity assumption. Substituting (
10
) back into (
9
) and rearranging the terms concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem
1
provides a general bound that holds for any data-independent prior distribution. In traditional PAC-Bayesian framework, this prior is generally fixed as a generic distribution. However, in the edge inference scenario, utilizing the wireless channel statistics allows us to design an optimal prior structure which yields the tightest form of this bound.

Corollary 1
.

Assume that the loss function
l(w,z)
is
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian under
P_{Z}
for all
w\in\mathcal{W}
and is
K
-Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{W}
for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.
Let
P_{ch}
denote the joint distribution of the weights in the channel layer, i.e.,
P_{ch}\coloneqq P_{{W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{W}^{(l_{0})}}\delta_{(\mathbf{I}_{d},\mathbf{0})}
.
The optimal prior
Q_{W^{\prime}W}
takes the following form:

\begin{split}Q_{W^{\prime}W}\left(W^{\prime},W\right)&=Q_{ch}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)Q_{\tilde{W}}\left(\tilde{W}\right)\\
&\quad\cdot\prod_{l\neq l_{0}}\delta_{{W}^{(l)}}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l)}\right),\end{split}

(11)

where
Q_{ch}
is the Gibbs distribution defined by

\begin{split}Q_{ch}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)&\propto P_{ch}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)\\
&\quad\cdot e^{-kKd^{(l_{0})}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)},\end{split}

(12)

and
Q_{\tilde{W}}
is some data-independent prior distribution over
\tilde{\mathcal{W}}
. Using the above
Q_{W^{\prime}W}
, (
5
) becomes

\begin{split}\Delta&\leq\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{2n}+\frac{D\left(P_{\tilde{W}|S}\|Q_{\tilde{W}}\right)-\log\epsilon}{k}\\
&\quad+K\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{P_{ch}}\left[d^{(l_{0})}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)\right]}_{\Omega\text{ (Channel Penalty)}},\end{split}

(13)

where the channel penalty
\Omega
quantifies the expected distortion introduced by the noisy channel.

Proof.

We construct the prior
Q_{W^{\prime}W}
to minimize the generalization bound in Theorem
1
. The optimization involves two step:
First, the KL divergence
D(P_{W^{\prime}W|S}\|Q_{W^{\prime}W})
in (
5
) needs to be finite. This indicates that the prior
Q_{W^{\prime}W}
covers the support of
P_{W^{\prime}W|S}
. As defined in (
3
),
P_{W^{\prime}W|S}
can be factorized into

\begin{split}P_{W^{\prime}W|S}\left(W^{\prime},W|S\right)&=P_{ch}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)P_{\tilde{W}|S}\left(\tilde{W}|S\right)\\
&\quad\cdot\prod_{l\neq l_{0}}\delta_{{W}^{(l)}}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l)}\right).\end{split}

(14)

Thus, any prior
Q_{W^{\prime}W}
assigns zero probability mass to the set
\{{W^{\prime}}^{(l)}={W^{\prime}}^{(l)}\}
for
l\neq l_{0}
will result in an infinite KL divergence. Therefore, the optimal prior must adopt the same structure as
P_{W^{\prime}W|S}
:

\begin{split}Q_{W^{\prime}W}\left(W^{\prime},W|S\right)&=Q_{ch}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)Q_{\tilde{W}}\left(\tilde{W}\right)\\
&\quad\cdot\prod_{l\neq l_{0}}\delta_{{W}^{(l)}}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l)}\right),\end{split}

(15)

where
Q_{\tilde{W}}
is any data-independent distribution for the baselines weights
\tilde{W}=(W^{(l)})_{l\neq l_{0}}
.
We substitute this prior structure into the general bound from Theorem
1
. The KL divergence becomes

\begin{split}&D(P_{W^{\prime}W|S}\|Q_{W^{\prime}W})\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{P_{W^{\prime}W|S}\left(W^{\prime},W|S\right)}{Q_{W^{\prime}W}\left(W^{\prime},W\right)}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(\frac{P_{ch}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)P_{\tilde{W}|S}\left(\tilde{W}|S\right)}{Q_{ch}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)Q_{\tilde{W}}\left(\tilde{W}\right)}\right.\right.\\
&\quad\cdot\left.\left.\frac{\prod_{l\neq l_{0}}\delta_{{W}^{(l)}}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l)}\right)}{\prod_{l\neq l_{0}}\delta_{{W}^{(l)}}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l)}\right)}\right)\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{P_{ch}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)P_{\tilde{W}|S}\left(\tilde{W}|S\right)}{Q_{ch}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)Q_{\tilde{W}}\left(\tilde{W}\right)}\right]\\
&=D\left(P_{\tilde{W}|S}\|Q_{\tilde{W}}\right)+D\left(P_{ch}\|Q_{ch}\right),\end{split}

(16)

and the logarithmic term becomes

\begin{split}&\log\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}W}}\left[e^{kKd_{\mathcal{W}}\left(W^{\prime},W\right)}\right]\\
&=\log\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}W}}\left[e^{kK\sum_{l=1}^{L+1}d^{(l)}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l)},{W}^{(l)}\right)}\right]\\
&=\log\mathbb{E}_{Q_{ch}}\left[e^{kKd^{(l_{0})}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)}\right].\end{split}

(17)

Therefore, for all data-independent prior
Q_{\tilde{W}}
and
k>0
, w.p. at least
1-\epsilon
, we have

\begin{split}\Delta&\leq\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{2n}+\frac{D(P_{\tilde{W}|S}\|Q_{\tilde{W}})-\log\epsilon}{k}\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{k}\left(\log\mathbb{E}_{Q_{ch}}\left[e^{kKd^{(l_{0})}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)}\right]+D(P_{ch}\|Q_{ch})\right).\\
\end{split}

(18)

Second, we minimize the last term on the RHS w.r.t. the distribution
Q_{ch}
. Let
\mathcal{L}(Q_{ch})
denote the terms in (
18
) dependent on the channel layer:

\mathcal{L}(Q_{ch})=\log\mathbb{E}_{Q_{ch}}\left[e^{kKd^{(l_{0})}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)}\right]+D(P_{ch}\|Q_{ch}).

(19)

By the Donsker-Varadhan variational representation in Lemma
1
,
\mathcal{L}(Q_{ch})
achieves minimum if and only if
Q_{ch}
follows the Gibbs distribution defined as

\begin{split}Q^{*}_{ch}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)&=\frac{1}{Z}P_{ch}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)\\
&\quad\cdot e^{-kKd^{(l_{0})}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)},\end{split}

(20)

where
Z=\mathbb{E}_{P_{ch}}[e^{-kKd^{(l_{0})}({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})})}]
is the normalizer. Evaluating
\mathcal{L}(Q_{ch})
at the optimal distribution
Q^{*}_{ch}
gives

\begin{split}&\inf_{Q_{ch}}\left\{\mathcal{L}(Q_{ch})\right\}=kK\mathbb{E}_{P_{ch}}\left[d^{(l_{0})}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)\right].\end{split}

(21)

Substituting this minimal value back into (
18
) yields the bound in (
13
).
∎

By utilizing the optimal channel-aware prior, the generalization bound can be decomposed into two components: the traditional PAC-Bayesian bound for a channel-free model, and an additive channel penalty term quantifying the cost induced by channel interference.

Remark 1
.

Theorem
1
provides a general bound for all data-independent prior
Q_{W^{\prime}W}
, whereas Corollary
1
yields the tightest result via the optimal channel-aware prior, although making the expression less tractable in many cases. In practice, computing the channel penalty
\Omega
requires us to evaluate an expectation, which is feasible for simple models but often intractable for complex fading channels with spatial correlation. Since numerical approximations (e.g., Monte Carlo) lack theoretical guarantees, Theorem
1
remains essential for deriving analytic bounds using simple priors when
\Omega
is intractable, as we will show in Example
2
later.

IV
Applications in Wireless Channels

In this section, we evaluate the derived bounds in two scenarios: the Binary Erasure Channel (BEC) and the Rayleigh fading channel. We first formalize the transition probability
P_{{W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{W}^{(l_{0})}}
for each scenario. Subsequently, we derive closed-form generalization bounds utilizing Corollary
1
for the first scenario and Theorem
1
for the latter.

IV-A

Wireless Channel Characterizations

BEC
:
In the digital transmission scenario, we assume the usage of channel codes. Specifically, the hidden layer features
f^{(l_{0}-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are encoded and transmitted as packets. Therefore, we adopt the BEC model to characterize whether feature elements are either correctly received or lost due to channel outages.
If an outage occurs, the receiver cannot recover the code, treating the received element as zero. Otherwise, the feature element is perfectly recovered. This is mathematically equivalent to applying a diagonal mask matrix to the feature vector. The distorted feature
\hat{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be formulated as
\hat{f}=Uf^{(l_{0}-1)}
,
where
U=\text{diag}(U_{1},\cdots,U_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. The diagonal components
U_{i}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables with parameter
1-p_{o}
, denoted as
U_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\text{Bernoulli}(1-p_{o})
, where
p_{o}
is the outage probability. Therefore, the effective weights of the
l_{0}
-th layer
{W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}=({M^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})})
are given by
{M^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}=U
and
{B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}=\mathbf{0}
.
We omit the zero bias in this case for convenience. Then, the channel transition probability
P_{{W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{W}^{(l_{0})}}
is defined element-wise as

\begin{split}&\Pr\left({M^{\prime}}_{ii}^{(l_{0})}=1|{M}^{(l_{0})}=\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)=1-p_{o},\\
&\Pr\left({M^{\prime}}_{ii}^{(l_{0})}=0|{M}^{(l_{0})}=\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)=p_{o}.\end{split}

(22)

Rayleigh Channel
:
In the analog transmission scenario, the hidden layer features are directly mapped to channel symbols. We adopt the quasi-static Rayleigh fading model, where the channel gain changes slowly and remains static during the transmission of the hidden layer feature.
We assume orthogonal parallel sub-channels, e.g., OFDM, ensuring no interference between feature elements.
To align with the real-valued framework established in Section
II
, we adopt the real-valued isomorphism for complex signals and assume
d
is even.
Let
f^{(l_{0}-1)}
be normalized to unit power, the received feature is given by
\hat{f}=W_{eq}(\sqrt{P}Hf^{(l_{0}-1)}+N)
,
where
W_{eq}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the equalization weight and
P>0
is the transmit power. The channel gain matrix
H=\text{diag}(\tilde{H}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{H}_{d/2})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is block-diagonal, where each block
\tilde{H}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
represents the real-valued equivalent of the complex sub-channel gain
H_{i}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
.
The channel noise vector
N=(\tilde{N}_{1},\cdots\tilde{N}_{d/2})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
consists of
d/2
sub-vectors, where each
\tilde{N}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
corresponds to the complex noise
N_{i}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
.
Utilizing a zero-forcing equalizer
W_{eq}=H^{-1}/\sqrt{P}
, the effective weights
{W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}=({M^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})})
are given by

{M^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}=\mathbf{I}_{d}\quad\text{and}\quad{B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{P}}H^{-1}N.

(23)

Generalization Bounds

Example 1
.

Consider the case where
P_{W^{\prime}|W}
follows the BEC model defined in (
22
). We utilize Frobenius norm as the weights distance
d^{(l_{0})}
. Under the same assumptions of Corollary
1
, for all data-independent prior
Q_{\tilde{W}}
and
k>0
, w.p. at least
1-\epsilon
, we have

\begin{split}\Delta&\leq\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{2n}+\frac{D\left(P_{\tilde{W}|S}\|Q_{\tilde{W}}\right)-\log\epsilon}{k}\\
&\quad+K\sum_{r=1}^{d}\binom{d}{r}p_{o}^{r}(1-p_{o})^{d-r}\sqrt{r}.\end{split}

(24)

Proof.

We compute the channel penalty by evaluating the expected distance. Since
P_{ch}
is defined as follows:

\begin{split}&P_{ch}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)\\
&=P_{{W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{W}^{(l_{0})}}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)\delta_{\mathbf{I}_{d}}\left({M}^{(l_{0})}\right),\end{split}

(25)

we have,

\begin{split}\Omega&=\mathbb{E}_{P_{ch}}\left[d^{(l_{0})}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{P_{ch}}\left[\left\|{M^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}-{M}^{(l_{0})}\right\|_{F}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{M^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}-{M}^{(l_{0})}\right\|_{F}\Big|{M}^{(l_{0})}=\mathbf{I}_{d}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|U-\mathbf{I}_{d}\right\|_{F}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}(U_{i}-1)^{2}}\right]\\
&\overset{(a)}{=}\sum_{r=1}^{d}\binom{d}{r}p_{o}^{r}(1-p_{o})^{d-r}\sqrt{r},\end{split}

(26)

where (a) is because
R=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(U_{i}-1)^{2}
follows a Binomial distribution
\text{Bin}(d,p_{o})
. Substituting this into (
13
) completes the proof.
∎

Example 2
.

Consider the case where the conditional distribution
P_{W^{\prime}|W}
is determined by the Rayleigh channel characterized in (
23
). Unlike the BEC scenario, the channel penalty
\Omega
in this case depends on the inverse channel gain
H^{-1}
. Consequently, the upper bound in Corollary
1
becomes analytically intractable. To obtain a closed-form bound, we utilize Theorem
1
and construct the prior distribution as
Q_{W^{\prime}W}=Q_{W}Q_{W^{\prime}|W}
, where

Q_{W}(W)=Q_{\tilde{W}}\left(\tilde{W}\right)\delta_{(\mathbf{I}_{d},\mathbf{0)}}\left(W^{(l_{0})}\right),

(27)

and

\begin{split}Q_{W^{\prime}|W}\left(W^{\prime}|W\right)&=\delta_{M^{(l_{0})}}\left({M^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}\right)Q_{B}\left({B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{B}^{(l_{0})}\right)\\
&\quad\cdot\prod_{l\neq l_{0}}\delta_{{W}^{(l)}}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l)}\right).\end{split}

(28)

Here,
Q_{B}
is the conditional distribution over the
l_{0}
-th layer bias vector, defined as the product of independent bi-variate isotropic Laplace distributions over the
d/2
sub-channels:

Q_{B}\left({B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{B}^{(l_{0})}\right)=C\prod_{i=1}^{d/2}e^{-\lambda\left\|{B^{\prime}}_{i}^{(l_{0})}-{B}_{i}^{(l_{0})}\right\|_{2}},

(29)

for some parameter
\lambda>0
. Here,
C
is the normalizer constant, and
{B^{\prime}}_{i}^{(l_{0})},{B}_{i}^{(l_{0})}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
represent the sub-vector corresponding to the
i
-th sub-channel.
It can be shown that with the optimal choice of
\lambda=kK+4\sqrt{\gamma}/\pi
, under the same assumptions in Theorem
1
, for all data-independent prior
Q_{\tilde{W}}
and
k>0
, w.p. at least
1-\epsilon
, we have

\begin{split}\Delta&\leq\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{2n}+\frac{D\left(P_{\tilde{W}|S}\|Q_{\tilde{W}}\right)-\log\epsilon}{k}+\frac{d\pi K}{4\sqrt{\gamma}}+\frac{d}{2k}\log\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}.\\
\end{split}

(30)

Proof.

We first evaluate the expectation term. Let the distance metric on the
l_{0}
-th layer weights
d^{(l_{0})}
be the sum of the distance of the weight matrix
d_{M}^{(l_{0})}
and the distance of the bias vector
d_{B}^{(l_{0})}
.
Under the definition of
Q_{W^{\prime}|W}
in (
28
), the weights satisfy
{W^{\prime}}^{(l)}={W}^{(l)}
for all
l\neq l_{0}
, and
{M^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}={M}^{(l_{0})}
.
Consequently, the total weight distance can be simplified to the distance between the bias vectors of the
l_{0}
-th layer. To align with the
d/2
sub-channels assumed in Section
IV
-A, we define the distance metric on the
l_{0}
-th layer bias as the sum of
\ell_{2}
norms of the sub-channels:

d_{B}^{(l_{0})}\left({B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},B^{(l_{0})}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{d/2}\left\|{B^{\prime}}_{i}^{(l_{0})}-{B}_{i}^{(l_{0})}\right\|_{2}.

(31)

Let
V_{i}={B^{\prime}}_{i}^{(l_{0})}-{B}_{i}^{(l_{0})}
and
V=(V_{1},V_{2},\cdots,V_{d/2})
, then the expectation in Theorem
1
can be formulated as follows:

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}W}}\left[e^{kKd_{\mathcal{W}}\left(W^{\prime},W\right)}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}|W}}\left[e^{kK\sum_{l=1}^{L+1}d^{(l)}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l)},W^{(l)}\right)}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{B}}\left[e^{kKd_{B}^{(l_{0})}\left({B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},B^{(l_{0})}\right)}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{kKd_{B}^{(l_{0})}\left({B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},B^{(l_{0})}\right)}\big|B^{(l_{0})}\right]\\
&\overset{(a)}{=}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W}}\left[\int C\prod_{i=1}^{d/2}e^{-\lambda\left\|V_{i}\right\|_{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{d/2}e^{kK\left\|V_{i}\right\|_{2}}dV\right]\\
&\overset{(b)}{=}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{d/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}C_{i}e^{-\left(\lambda-kK\right)\|V_{i}\|_{2}}dV_{i}\right)\right],\end{split}

(32)

where (a) is from substituting the definitions (
29
) and (
31
), and (b) follows from the independence of sub-channels.
The normalizer
C_{i}
satisfies
C=C_{i}^{d/2}

and is given by

C_{i}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-\lambda\|V_{i}\|}dV_{i}\right)^{-1}=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\pi}.

(33)

Utilizing polar coordinates, the integral in (
32
) can be computed as

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}C_{i}e^{-\left(\lambda-kK\right)\|V_{i}\|_{2}}dV_{i}\\
&=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\int_{0}^{\infty}re^{-\left(\lambda-kK\right)r}dr\\
&=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\pi}\cdot 2\pi\cdot\frac{1}{(\lambda-kK)^{2}}\\
&=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-kK}\right)^{2}.\end{split}

(34)

This integral converges only if
\lambda>kK
. Substituting this result into (
32
), we obtain

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}W}}\left[e^{kKd_{\mathcal{W}}\left(W^{\prime},W\right)}\right]&=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{d/2}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-kK}\right)^{2}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W}}\left[\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-kK}\right)^{d}\right]\\
&=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-kK}\right)^{d}.\end{split}

(35)

Taking the logarithm gives

\log\mathbb{E}_{Q_{W^{\prime}W}}\left[e^{kKd_{\mathcal{W}}\left(W^{\prime},W\right)}\right]=d\log\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-kK}\right).

(36)

Next, we compute the divergence term in the upper bound. Based on the definition in (
3
), the joint distribution
P_{W^{\prime}W|S}
can be factorized as

\begin{split}&P_{W^{\prime}W|S}\left(W^{\prime},W|S\right)\\
&=P_{W|S}\left(W|S\right)P_{W^{\prime}|W}\left(W^{\prime}|W\right)\\
&=P_{W|S}\left(W|S\right)\prod_{l\neq l_{0}}\delta_{{W}^{(l)}}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l)}\right)\\
&\quad\cdot P_{M}\left({M^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{M}^{(l_{0})}\right)P_{B}\left({B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{B}^{(l_{0})}\right),\end{split}

(37)

where
P_{M}
and
P_{B}
denote the conditional distribution over the
l_{0}
-th layer weight matrix and bias vector. Therefore, the KL divergence can be decomposed as

\begin{split}&D\left(P_{W^{\prime}W|S}\|Q_{W^{\prime}W}\right)=D\left(P_{W|S}\|Q_{W}\right)\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{w\sim P_{W|S}}\left[D\left(P_{W^{\prime}|W=w}\|Q_{W^{\prime}|W=w}\right)\right].\end{split}

(38)

For the first term, since both distributions
P_{W|S}
and
Q_{W}
contains Dirac measures at
W^{(l_{0})}=(\mathbf{I}_{d},\mathbf{0})
, we have

D\left(P_{W|S}\|Q_{W}\right)=D\left(P_{\tilde{W}|S}\|Q_{\tilde{W}}\right).

(39)

The second term is the expectation over
P_{W|S}
. Since
P_{W|S}
fixes
W^{(l_{0})}=(\mathbf{I}_{d},\mathbf{0})
, the expectation simply evaluates the KL divergence on a fixed point. Furthermore, both
P_{W^{\prime}|W}
and
Q_{W^{\prime}|W}
share the identical deterministic structures, i.e.,
{W^{\prime}}^{(l)}={W}^{(l)}=\mathbf{I}_{d}
for
l\neq l_{0}
, and
{M^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}={M}^{(l_{0})}
. Therefore, the Dirac measures cancel out in the log likelihood ratio, and the expectation of divergence reduces to

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}_{w\sim P_{W|S}}\left[D\left(P_{W^{\prime}|W=w}\|Q_{W^{\prime}|W=w}\right)\right]\\
&=D\left(P_{{W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{W}^{(l_{0})}=(\mathbf{I}_{d},\mathbf{0})}\|Q_{{W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{W}^{(l_{0})}=(\mathbf{I}_{d},\mathbf{0})}\right)\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(\frac{P_{M}\left({M^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{M}^{(l_{0})}\right)P_{B}\left({B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{B}^{(l_{0})}\right)}{\delta_{M^{(l_{0})}}\left({M^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}\right)Q_{B}\left({B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{B}^{(l_{0})}\right)}\right.\right.\\
&\quad\cdot\left.\left.\frac{\prod_{l\neq l_{0}}\delta_{{W}^{(l)}}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l)}\right)}{\prod_{l\neq l_{0}}\delta_{{W}^{(l)}}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l)}\right)}\right)\bigg|{M}^{(l_{0})}=\mathbf{I}_{d},{B}^{(l_{0})}=\mathbf{0}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{P_{B}\left({B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{B}^{(l_{0})}\right)}{Q_{B}\left({B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{B}^{(l_{0})}\right)}\bigg|{B}^{(l_{0})}=\mathbf{0}\right]\\
&=D\left(P_{B}\left({B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{B}^{(l_{0})}=\mathbf{0}\right)\big\|Q_{B}\left({B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{B}^{(l_{0})}=\mathbf{0}\right)\right)\\
&=-H\left(P_{B|\mathbf{0}}\right)+CE\left(P_{B|\mathbf{0}},Q_{B|\mathbf{0}}\right),\end{split}

(40)

where
H(\cdot)
is the entropy, and
CE(\cdot,\cdot)
is the cross entropy.
We denote
P_{B|\mathbf{0}}=P_{{B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{B}^{(l_{0})}=\mathbf{0}}
and
Q_{B|\mathbf{0}}=Q_{{B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{B}^{(l_{0})}=\mathbf{0}}
.
Based on the channel characteristic in the Rayleigh channel (
23
),
P_{B|\mathbf{0}}
is essentially the distribution of the random variable
{B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}=H^{-1}N/\sqrt{P}
. Given the block-diagonal structure of
H
, each sub-vector in
{B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}
is given by
{B^{\prime}}_{i}^{(l_{0})}=\tilde{H}_{i}^{-1}\tilde{N}_{i}/\sqrt{P}
, corresponding to the
i
-th sub-channel.
Specifically,
{B^{\prime}}_{i}^{(l_{0})}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the real-valued isomorphism of the complex scalar
H_{i}^{-1}N_{i}/\sqrt{P}
, where
N_{i}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
and
H_{i}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
.
Since the sub-channels are i.i.d., the entropy
H(P_{B|\mathbf{0}})
can be decomposed into the sum of the entropies of the individual components:

H\left(P_{B|\mathbf{0}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{d/2}H\left({B^{\prime}}_{i}^{(l_{0})}\right).

(41)

Let
T=H_{i}^{-1}N_{i}/\sqrt{P}\in\mathbb{C}
, using the isomorphism between
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the entropy
H({B^{\prime}}_{i}^{(l_{0})})
is equivalent to
H(T)
. Therefore, we have

H\left(P_{B|\mathbf{0}}\right)=\frac{d}{2}H(T).

(42)

Since
T
is the ratio of complex Gaussian variables, its probability distribution function (PDF) is given by

f(t)=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\pi\left(\alpha^{2}+|t|^{2}\right)^{2}},

(43)

where
\alpha^{2}=\sigma_{0}^{2}/P
.
The entropy is computed as

\begin{split}H(T)&=-\mathbb{E}\left[\log f(T)\right]\\
&=-\log\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}}{\pi P}+2\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}}{P}+|T|^{2}\right)\right].\end{split}

(44)

We utilize the polar coordinates to compute the expectation
I=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(\alpha^{2}+|T|^{2}\right)\right]
. Let
r=|t|
be the radius, the expectation is given by

\begin{split}I&=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\pi\left(\alpha^{2}+r^{2}\right)^{2}}\log\left(\alpha^{2}+r^{2}\right)rdrd\theta\\
&=2\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{r\log\left(\alpha^{2}+r^{2}\right)}{\left(\alpha^{2}+r^{2}\right)^{2}}dr\\
&\overset{(a)}{=}\alpha^{2}\int_{\alpha^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{\log u}{u^{2}}du\\
&=\alpha^{2}\left(-\frac{\log\alpha^{2}+1}{u}\right)_{\alpha^{2}}^{\infty}\\
&=\alpha^{2}\left(0-\frac{\log\alpha^{2}+1}{\alpha^{2}}\right)\\
&=\log\alpha^{2}+1,\end{split}

(45)

where (a) is from substitution
u=\alpha^{2}+r^{2}
. Substitute
I
into the entropy
H(T)
gives

H(T)=-\log\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}}{\pi P}+2\log\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}}{P}+2=\log\left(\pi e^{2}\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}}{P}\right).

(46)

Therefore, entropy
H(P_{B|\mathbf{0}})
is

H\left(P_{B|\mathbf{0}}\right)=\frac{d}{2}\left(\log\left(\frac{\pi e^{2}}{\gamma}\right)\right),

(47)

where
\gamma=P/\sigma_{0}^{2}
is the average SNR.
The cross entropy between
P_{B|\mathbf{0}}
and
Q_{B|\mathbf{0}}
can be derived as the following:

\begin{split}&CE\left(P_{B|\mathbf{0}},Q_{B|\mathbf{0}}\right)\\
&=-\mathbb{E}_{P_{B|\mathbf{0}}}\left[\log Q_{B|\mathbf{0}}\left({B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}|{B}^{(l_{0})}=\mathbf{0}\right)\right]\\
&=-\mathbb{E}_{P_{B|\mathbf{0}}}\left[\log\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d/2}C_{i}e^{-\lambda\left\|{B^{\prime}}_{i}^{(l_{0})}-\mathbf{0}\right\|_{2}}\right)\right]\\
&=\lambda\mathbb{E}_{P_{B|\mathbf{0}}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d/2}\left\|{B^{\prime}}_{i}^{(l_{0})}\right\|_{2}\right]-\frac{d}{2}\log C_{i}\\
&\overset{(a)}{=}\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{d/2}\mathbb{E}_{P_{B|\mathbf{0}}}\left[\left\|{B^{\prime}}_{i}^{(l_{0})}\right\|_{2}\right]-\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\pi}\right)\\
&\overset{(b)}{=}\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{d/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{N_{i}}{\sqrt{P}H_{i}}\right|\right]-\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\pi}\right)\\
&\overset{(c)}{=}\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{P}}\sum_{i=1}^{d/2}\mathbb{E}\left[|N_{i}|\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{|H_{i}|}\right]-\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\pi}\right),\end{split}

(48)

where (a) follows from the normalizer
C_{i}
in (
33
), (b) is from the real-valued isomorphism of
{B^{\prime}}_{i}^{(l_{0})}
, and (c) follows from the independence between noise
N_{i}
and channel gain
H_{i}
.
Since both
|N_{i}|
and
|H_{i}|
follow the Rayleigh distribution, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[|N_{i}|\right]=\frac{\sigma_{0}\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{|H_{i}|}\right]=\sqrt{\pi}.

(49)

Therefore, the cross entropy is

\begin{split}CE\left(P_{B|\mathbf{0}},Q_{B|\mathbf{0}}\right)&=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{P}}\cdot\frac{d}{2}\cdot\frac{\pi\sigma_{0}}{2}-\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\pi}\right)\\
&=\frac{d}{2}\left(\frac{\pi\lambda}{2\sqrt{\gamma}}-\log\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\pi}\right)\right).\end{split}

(50)

Combining the entropy and cross-entropy, the KL divergence becomes

\begin{split}&D\left(P_{W^{\prime}W|S}\|Q_{W^{\prime}W}\right)=D\left(P_{\tilde{W}|S}\|Q_{\tilde{W}}\right)\\
&+\frac{d}{2}\left(\frac{\pi\lambda}{2\sqrt{\gamma}}+\log\left(\frac{2\gamma}{\lambda^{2}}\right)-2\right)\end{split}

(51)

Substituting the expectation and divergence terms into Theorem
1
gives the following:

\begin{split}&\Delta\leq\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{2n}+\frac{D\left(P_{\tilde{W}|S}\|Q_{\tilde{W}}\right)-\log\epsilon}{k}\\
&+\frac{d}{2k}\left(\frac{\pi\lambda}{2\sqrt{\gamma}}+\log\frac{2\gamma}{\left(\lambda-kK\right)^{2}}-2\right).\end{split}

(52)

We observe that the final term on the RHS can be minimized w.r.t.
\lambda
. Let
\mathcal{L}(\lambda)
denote this term, defined as

\mathcal{L}(\lambda)=\frac{\pi\lambda}{2\sqrt{\gamma}}+\log\frac{2\gamma}{\left(\lambda-kK\right)^{2}}-2.

(53)

We can find the optimal
\lambda
by taking the derivative of
\mathcal{L}(\lambda)
:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\lambda)}{d\lambda}=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\gamma}}-\frac{2}{\lambda-kK}.

(54)

Setting the derivative to zero yields the optimal
\lambda
:

\lambda^{*}=kK+\frac{4\sqrt{\gamma}}{\pi}.

(55)

Substituting
\lambda^{*}
into (
52
) completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2
.

Consider the simplified case of a scalar Rayleigh fading channel characterized by channel gain
H_{0}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
and noise
N_{0}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
(i.e.,
d=2
). Since the channel penalty is tractable in this case, we apply Corollary
1
to obtain:

\begin{split}\Delta\leq\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{2n}+\frac{D\left(P_{\tilde{W}|S}\|Q_{\tilde{W}}\right)-\log\epsilon}{k}+\frac{\pi K}{2\sqrt{\gamma}}.\end{split}

(56)

Proof.

The channel penalty can be computed as follows:

\begin{split}\Omega&=\mathbb{E}_{P_{ch}}\left[d^{(l_{0})}\left({W^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})},{W}^{(l_{0})}\right)\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{P_{ch}}\left[\left\|{M^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}-M^{(l_{0})}\right\|_{op}+\left\|{B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}-B^{(l_{0})}\right\|_{2}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{M^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}-\mathbf{I}_{d}\right\|_{op}+\left\|{B^{\prime}}^{(l_{0})}-\mathbf{0}\right\|_{2}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathbf{I}_{d}-\mathbf{I}_{d}\right\|_{op}+\left|\frac{N_{0}}{\sqrt{P}H_{0}}-0\right|\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{N_{0}}{\sqrt{P}H_{0}}\right|\right]\\
&\overset{(a)}{=}\frac{1}{\sqrt{P}}\mathbb{E}[|N_{0}|]\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{|H_{0}|}\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{P}}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{0}\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\cdot\sqrt{\pi}\\
&=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\gamma}},\end{split}

(57)

where (a) is because that the channel gain is independent of noise. Substituting this into (
13
) completes the proof.
∎

In the high-SNR regime, the bound reduces to the traditional channel-free PAC-Bayesian bound.
The comparison of (
30
) and (
56
) illustrates the trade-off between a tighter bound for simplified channels, versus a general bound incurring an additional logarithmic term for complex fading channels.

V
Channel-aware Training Algorithm

Inspired by the aforementioned theoretical analysis, we propose a channel-aware training algorithm to enhance edge inference performance. Instead of minimizing the exact theoretical bound, we formulate a tractable surrogate objective function.
Specifically, we utilize the tightest form of generalization bound derived in Corollary
1
.
By integrating channel statistics such as outage probability and average SNR into the optimization objective (via channel penalty
\Omega
), we explicitly mitigate the mismatch between noiseless training and noisy edge deployment.
This enhances NN’s inherent robustness against channel perturbations, without requiring novel architecture designs or instantaneous channel information.

Since we assume a channel-free training environment where the
l_{0}
-th layer weights are independent, the optimization is restricted to the learnable weights
\tilde{W}
. Therefore, the primary goal is to find a posterior distribution
P_{\tilde{W}|S}
that minimizes the wireless generalization error.
However, directly minimizing the theoretical bound in (
13
) is difficult due to the global Lipschitz constant
K\coloneqq\sup_{w\in\mathcal{W},z\in\mathcal{Z}}\|\nabla_{w}l(w,z)\|_{2}
. Computing this supremum over the entire weight and data space is computationally intractable for NNs. Alternatively, we approximate
K
using the tractable local gradient norm of the empirical risk, denoted as
\hat{K}(\tilde{W})=\|\nabla_{W}\hat{L}_{S}(P_{W|S})\|_{2}
.
By utilizing this surrogate, neglecting the constant terms, and introducing a scaling factor
\eta_{1}>0
for effective optimization as in
[
12
]
, we formulate the objective function as the following

\mathcal{J}\left(P_{\tilde{W}|S}\right)=\hat{L}_{S}\left(P_{W|S}\right)+\frac{\eta_{1}}{k}D\left(P_{\tilde{W}|S}\|Q_{\tilde{W}}\right)+\hat{K}\left(\tilde{W}\right)\Omega.

(58)

To enable tractable optimization of (
58
), we employ the variational approximation in
[
13
]
where the posterior
P_{\tilde{W}|S}
is modeled as a diagonal Gaussian distribution over
\tilde{\mathcal{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Specifically, weights are sampled as
\tilde{W}=\mu+\sigma\odot\epsilon
, where
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)
and
\mu,\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are learnable mean and deviation, respectively. We parametrize the deviation element-wise as
\sigma=\log(1+e^{\rho})
to ensure non-negativity. For the data-independent prior, we select
Q_{\tilde{W}}
as a centered isotropic Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{p}\mathbf{I}_{p})
, where
\sigma_{p}
represents the initial weight variance. This gives a closed-form expression for the KL divergence. Consequently, the objective
\mathcal{J}(P_{\tilde{W}|S})
is differentiable w.r.t.
\mu
and
\rho
, allowing efficient optimization via stochastic gradient descent algorithm.

Simulation

To validate our theoretical results and the proposed channel-aware training algorithm, we evaluate the generalization performance on image classification tasks. Specifically, we utilize a 4-layer fully connected network (FCN) and a 4-layer convolutional neural network (CNN) on the MNIST dataset, and a 9-layer CNN on CIFAR-10. To simulate the edge inference system, we partition the NNs into two parts. For FCN-4 and CNN-4, the first two layers are deployed at the transmitter, while the remaining two layers are at the receiver, corresponding to
l_{0}=3
in the augmented model. For CNN-9, the split point is set after the fourth layer.
We consider four channel environments: the BEC with outage probability 0.1 and 0.8, and the simplified Rayleigh channel in Remark
2
at an average SNR of 0 dB and -5 dB. In the simulation, we set
\epsilon=0.025
,
\sigma=1
, and
k=\sqrt{n}
.

We summarize the results in Table
I
, which presents a comparison between the traditional empirical risk minimization (ERM) and the proposed channel-aware training algorithm. We evaluate the expected population risk in wireless environment for the 0-1 loss (i.e., accuracy). For the proposed algorithm, we calculate the decomposition of the generalization bound into empirical risk
\hat{L}_{S}
, channel penalty
\hat{K}\Omega
, and the KL divergence term
D(P_{\tilde{W}|S}\|Q_{\tilde{W}})/k
.
While the bounds are loose in some cases, they are valid upper bounds for the population risks in wireless edge inference.
The proposed method achieves lower population risks compared to the ERM baseline across various networks and channel conditions.
The results demonstrate that the proposed algorithm can effectively mitigate the mismatch between noiseless training and noisy deployment.
By utilizing the channel statistics, the channel-aware algorithm improves the edge inference accuracy.
Furthermore, the bound decomposition reveals the mechanism of our algorithm. The algorithm balances the trade-off among the empirical risk, channel penalty, and the KL divergence, via penalizing both sensitivity to the channel-induced distortion and complexity of the model space.
This optimization tightens the generalization bound, ensuring a better inference performance when deployed in a noisy wireless channel.
