Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate meromorphic solutions in ℂ m \mathbb{C}^{m} of the nonlinear differential equation f n ​ ∂ u ( f ) ​ g n ​ ∂ u ( g ) = 1 , \displaystyle f^{n}\partial_{u}(f)g^{n}\partial_{u}(g)=1, where ∂ u ( f ) = ∑ j = 1 m u j ​ ∂ j ( f ) \partial_{u}(f)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}\partial_{j}(f) and ∑ j = 1 m u j ≠ 0 \sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}\neq 0 . Our results extend those of Yang and Hua [ 34 ] to the framework of several complex variables. Moreover, we establish new uniqueness theorems that further generalize their conclusions to higher dimensions. As an application, explicit solutions of certain nonlinear partial differential equations in several variables are derived, and their physical interpretations are summarized in tabular form.

1.
Introduction and Historical Background

Solving nonlinear differential equations remains one of the most challenging and stimulating problems in mathematical analysis. Among the earliest contributions to this field, Yang and Hua
[
34
]
were the first to investigate the nonlinear differential equation

f^{n}f^{(1)}g^{n}g^{(1)}=1,

(1.1)

and to determine possible functional forms for the solutions

and
g

Historical Results

In 1997, Yang and Hua

Let

f^{n}f^{(1)}g^{n}g^{(1)}=1

For

Analytic Sets

•

A closed subset

Multiplicities of Zeros

•

For a holomorphic function

\partial^{I}f(z)=\frac{\partial^{|I|}f(z)}{\partial z_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\partial z_{m}^{i_{m}}},\qquad|I|=\sum_{j=1}^{m}i_{j}.

The

\mu_{f}^{0}(a)

\mu_{f}^{0}(a)=\min\{i:P_{i}(z-a)\not\equiv 0\}.

Meromorphic Functions and Divisors

•

If
f
is meromorphic on
G

such that
hf=g
on
G
, and
g
,
h
are coprime at each point.
The

of

\mu_{f}^{c}=\begin{cases}\mu_{g-ch}^{0},&c\in\mathbb{C},\\[6.0pt]
\mu_{h}^{0},&c=\infty.\end{cases}

•

The

of

is

\nu=\mu_{f}=\mu_{f}^{0}-\mu_{f}^{\infty}
,
and its support is

\operatorname{supp}(\nu)=\overline{\{z\in G:\nu(z)\neq 0\}}.

Counting and Valence Functions

•

For
A=\operatorname{supp}(\nu)
and

, the

is

n_{\nu}(t)=t^{-2(m-1)}\int_{A[t]}\nu\,\upsilon_{m}^{m-1}.

•

The

is

N_{\nu}(r)=N_{\nu}(r,r_{0})=\int_{r_{0}}^{r}n_{\nu}(t)\frac{dt}{t},\qquad(r\geq r_{0}).

•

For a meromorphic function

n_{\mu_{f}^{a}}(t)=\begin{cases}n(t,a;f),&a\in\mathbb{C},\\
n(t,f),&a=\infty,\end{cases}\quad N_{\mu_{f}^{a}}(r)=\begin{cases}N(r,a;f),&a\in\mathbb{C},\\

•

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define the

:

\mu_{f,k}^{a}(z)=\min\{\mu_{f}^{a}(z),k\}.

Then

n_{k}(t,a;f),\quad N_{k}(r,a;f)

denote the corresponding truncated counting functions, and for

:

\overline{n}(t,a;f)=n_{1}(t,a;f),\qquad\overline{N}(r,a;f)=N_{1}(r,a;f).

Sharing of Values and Proximity Functions

•

Let
f
,
g
, and
a
be meromorphic functions on
\mathbb{C}^{m}
.
Then one can find three pairs of entire functions

(f_{1},f_{2})
,
(g_{1},g_{2})
and
(a_{1},a_{2})
,
where each pair is coprime at every point in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, such that

f=\frac{f_{2}}{f_{1}},\qquad g=\frac{g_{2}}{g_{1}},\qquad a=\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}}.

•

We say that
f
and
g

share the value

by counting multiplicities (CM)
if

\mu_{a_{1}f_{2}-a_{2}f_{1}}^{0}=\mu_{a_{1}g_{2}-a_{2}g_{1}}^{0}\quad(a\not\equiv\infty),

and

\mu_{f_{1}}^{0}=\mu_{g_{1}}^{0}\quad(a=\infty).

•

A property is said to hold
generically
if it holds except on an analytic subset of strictly smaller dimension.
For instance, if
f
is a meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, then there exist holomorphic functions
g
and
h
such that

\dim\big(g^{-1}(\{0\})\cap h^{-1}(\{0\})\big)\leq m-2,\qquad f=\frac{g}{h}.

Consequently, the common zero factor of
g
and
h
can be cancelled
generically
over its support sets (see
[
14
, pp. 103]
).

•

With the help of the positive logarithm function, we define the
proximity function
of
f
by

m(r,f)=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{m}\langle r\rangle}\log^{+}|f|\,\sigma_{m}\geq 0.

•

The
characteristic function
of
f
is then defined as

T(r,f)=m(r,f)+N(r,f)
.

•

We further define

m(r,a;f)=\begin{cases}m(r,f),&a=\infty,\\[6.0pt]
m\!\left(r,\dfrac{1}{f-a}\right),&a\in\mathbb{C}.\end{cases}

•

The
First Main Theorem of Nevanlinna Theory
in this setting states that, for
a\in\mathbb{C}
,

m(r,a;f)+N(r,a;f)=T(r,f)+O(1),

where
O(1)
denotes a bounded function for sufficiently large
r
.

2.
Solutions of
f^{n}\partial_{u}(f)g^{n}\partial_{u}(g)=1

The differential equation

f^{n}\,\partial_{u}(f)\,g^{n}\,\partial_{u}(g)=1

can be regarded as a natural extension, to several complex variables, of classical nonlinear differential equations involving a function and its derivative in one variable, such as

f^{n}f^{\prime}=1,\qquad f^{n}f^{\prime}g^{n}g^{\prime}=1,\qquad\text{or more generally }f^{p}(f^{\prime})^{q}=c.

Equations of this type have been extensively studied in connection with the growth, uniqueness, and invariance properties of meromorphic functions. For instance, the equation
f^{n}f^{\prime}=1
in one variable implies that
f
is of exponential type, namely

f(z)=c_{1}e^{cz},

where
c_{1},c\neq 0
.

In several variables, the directional derivative
\partial_{u}(f)
serves as the analogue of the ordinary derivative, leading to more intricate interactions among variables. The above equation links
f
and
g
through their powers and directional derivatives, imposing strong structural constraints on possible solutions.

Our objective is to determine all non-constant entire or meromorphic pairs
(f,g)
satisfying this identity. The following theorems describe the general form of these solutions.

.

Let
f:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}
and
g:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}
be two non-constant entire functions and let
n\geq 1
be an integer. If

(f^{n}\partial_{u}f)(g^{n}\partial_{u}g)\equiv 1,

then
f=\exp(\alpha)
and
g=\exp(\beta)
, where
\alpha
and
\beta
are non-constant entire functions in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
such that
\partial_{u}(\alpha)=c
,
\partial_{u}(\beta)=-c
and
c^{2}e^{(n+1)(\alpha+\beta)}=-1
.

Proof.

We start from the given differential equation

f^{n}\partial_{u}(f)\,g^{n}\partial_{u}(g)\equiv 1.

(2.1)

Step 1. Preliminary setup.

Define

I=I_{f}\cup I_{g},\qquad S=\bigcup_{a\in\{0,\infty\}}\big(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{f}^{a}\big)_{s}\cup\big(\operatorname{supp}\mu_{g}^{a}\big)_{s},

where
A_{s}
denotes the set of singular points of the analytic set
A
.
Note that
\dim(I\cup S)\leq m-2
. Choose
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}-(I\cup S)
.

If possible, suppose
\mu_{f}^{0}(z_{0})=l>0
. Then by (
2.1
), we have
\mu_{g}^{\infty}(z_{0})=k>0
.
Since
z_{0}\notin S
, there exists a holomorphic coordinate system

(U;\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m})

around
z_{0}
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}-(I\cup S)
such that

U\cap\operatorname{supp}\mu_{g}^{\infty}=\{z\in U\mid\varphi_{1}(z)=0\},\qquad\varphi_{j}(z_{0})=0,\quad j=1,\ldots,m

(see Lemma 2.3 in
[
20
]
). So biholomorphic coordinate transformation
z_{j}=z_{j}(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m})
,
j=1,\ldots,m
near

z_{0}=z(0)=(z_{1}(0),\ldots,z_{m}(0))
. So we can write

f=\varphi_{1}^{l}\hat{f}(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}),\qquad g=\varphi_{1}^{-k}\hat{g}(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}),

where
\hat{f}
and
\hat{g}
are holomorphic and non-vanishing along
\operatorname{supp}\mu_{g}^{\infty}
.

Step 2. Computing directional derivatives.

For
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we have

\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_{i}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\varphi_{j}}\frac{\partial\varphi_{j}}{\partial z_{i}}=l\varphi_{1}^{l-1}\hat{f}\frac{\partial\varphi_{1}}{\partial z_{i}}+\varphi_{1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\varphi_{j}}\frac{\partial\varphi_{j}}{\partial z_{i}}.

Hence,

\partial_{u}(f)=l\varphi_{1}^{l-1}\hat{f}\partial_{u}(\varphi_{1})+\varphi_{1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\varphi_{j}}\partial_{u}(\varphi_{j}).

(2.2)

Similarly,

\partial_{u}(g)=-\frac{k}{\varphi_{1}^{k+1}}\hat{g}\partial_{u}(\varphi_{1})+\frac{1}{\varphi_{1}^{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\partial\hat{g}}{\partial\varphi_{j}}\partial_{u}(\varphi_{j}).

(2.3)

Step 3. Expanding the main identity.

We obtain

\displaystyle f^{n}\partial_{u}(f)

\displaystyle=l\varphi_{1}^{(n+1)l-1}\hat{f}^{n+1}\partial_{u}(\varphi_{1})+\varphi_{1}^{(n+1)l}\hat{f}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\varphi_{j}}\partial_{u}(\varphi_{j}),

(2.4)

\displaystyle g^{n}\partial_{u}(g)

\displaystyle=-\frac{k}{\varphi_{1}^{(n+1)k+1}}\hat{g}^{n+1}\partial_{u}(\varphi_{1})+\frac{1}{\varphi_{1}^{(n+1)k}}\hat{g}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\partial\hat{g}}{\partial\varphi_{j}}\partial_{u}(\varphi_{j}).

(2.5)

From (
2.1
) and (
2.4
)-(
2.5
), it follows that
(n+1)(l-k)=2
, a contradiction.
Hence
l=\mu_{f}^{0}(z_{0})=0
, so
f\neq 0
. Similarly,
g\neq 0
. Again from (
2.3
), we get

\parallel N(r,\partial_{u}(g))\leq N(r,g)+\overline{N}(r,g).

Similarly

\parallel N(r,\partial_{u}(f))\leq N(r,f)+\overline{N}(r,f).

Step 4. Setting up the auxiliary function.

Let

h=\frac{1}{fg}.

Then
h
is an entire function. We consider two cases.

Case 1:

h
is non-constant.

Since
f\neq 0
, from (
2.1
), we see that
\mu^{\infty}_{g^{n}\partial_{u}g}\leq\mu_{\partial_{u}f}^{0}
holds over
\text{supp}\;\mu^{\infty}_{f}
generically and so (

) gives

(n+1)\mu_{g}^{\infty}(z_{0})+\mu_{g,1}^{\infty}(z_{0})\leq\mu_{\partial_{u}f}^{0}(z_{0})
.

\parallel\;(n+1)N(r,g)+\overline{N}(r,g)\leq N(r,0;\partial_{u}(f))

\displaystyle+(n+1)m(r,h)+O(1).

Lemma 3.6

Let

be a non-constant meromorphic function and let

(i)

\parallel\;(n+1)T(r,g)\leq T(r,G)+N(r,0;g)-N(r,0;\partial_{u}(g))+o(T(r,g))
,

(ii)

\parallel\;N(r,0;\partial_{u}(g)\bigr|g\neq 0)\leq\overline{N}(r,0;g)+\overline{N}(r,g)+o(T(r,g))
,

(iii)

\parallel\;N(r,0;\partial_{u}(g))\leq N(r,0;g)+\overline{N}(r,g)+o(T(r,g))

Proof.

(i)
. Using the first main theorem and Lemma
2.1
, we get

\displaystyle\parallel\;(n+1)T(r,g)=T(r,g^{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m(r,0,g^{n+1})+N(r,0;g^{n+1})+O(1)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m\left(r,G/g^{n+1}\right)+m(r,0;G)+N(r,0;f^{n+1})+O(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m(r,0;G)+N(r,0,g^{n+1})+o(T(r,g))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T(r,G)-N(r,0;G)+N(r,0;g^{n+1})+o(T(r,g))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle T(r,G)+N(r,0;g)-N(r,0;\partial_{u}(g))+o(T(r,g)).

(ii)
. Using the first main theorem and Lemma
2.1
, we get

\displaystyle\parallel\;N(r,0;\partial_{u}(g)\bigr|g\neq 0)\leq N\left(r,g/\partial_{u}(g)\right)\leq T\left(r,g/\partial_{u}(g)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T\left(r,\partial_{u}(g)/g\right)+O(1)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle N\left(r,\partial_{u}(g)/g\right)+o(T(r,g)),

i.e.,

\displaystyle\parallel\;N(r,0;\partial_{u}(g)\bigr|g\neq 0)\leq N(r,H)+o(T(r,g)),

(3.22)

where

We now prove

. Define

,
where

denotes the set of singular points of the analytic set
A
. Take

.

First we assume that

. Then from (
2.3
), we obtain

\parallel\;N(r,\partial_{u}(g))\leq N(r,g)+\overline{N}(r,g)

and

H=\frac{\partial_{u}(g)}{g}=-\frac{l}{\varphi_{1}}\partial_{u}(\varphi_{1})+\frac{1}{\hat{g}}\sideset{}{{}_{j=1}^{m}}{\sum}\frac{\partial\hat{g}}{\partial\varphi_{j}}\partial_{u}(\varphi_{j}).

Next we assume that

\displaystyle H=\frac{\partial_{u}(g)}{g}=\frac{l}{\varphi_{1}}\partial_{u}(\varphi_{1})+\frac{1}{\hat{g}}\sideset{}{{}_{j=1}^{m}}{\sum}\frac{\partial\hat{g}}{\partial\varphi_{j}}\partial_{u}(\varphi_{j}),

which means that

Therefore

\parallel N\left(r,\partial_{u}(g)/g\right)\leq\overline{N}(r,0;g)+\overline{N}(r,g)

\parallel\;N(r,0;\partial_{u}(g)\bigr|g\neq 0)\leq\overline{N}(r,0;g)+\overline{N}(r,g)+o(T(r,g)).

(iii)

\displaystyle\parallel\;m(r,0,g)\leq m(r,0,\partial_{u}(g))+m\left(r,\partial_{u}(g)/g\right)=m(r,0;\partial_{u}(g))+o(T(r,g)),

\displaystyle\parallel\;N(r,0;\partial_{u}(g))

\displaystyle T(r,\partial_{u}(g))-T(r,g)+N(r,0,g)+o(T(r,g))

\displaystyle N(r,\partial_{u}(g))+m(r,g)-T(r,g)+N(r,0;g)+o(T(r,g))

does not satisfy the nonlinear condition

\partial_{u}(\beta)\partial_{u}(\gamma)=(n+1)(\partial_{u}(\gamma))^{2}+\partial_{u}^{2}(\gamma)

for all
z_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
. Hence, in this case there exists no entire solution of exponential type. This example demonstrates that any quadratic dependence of
\beta
on the direction variable
u
destroys the exponential-type entire solution structure.

Example 4.8
.

Let
\dim(\mathbb{C}^{m})=2
,
u=(1,1)
and take a mixed linear?periodic function

\beta(z_{1},z_{2})=a(z_{1}+z_{2})+d\sin(z_{1}+2z_{2})+c,

where
a,d,c\in\mathbb{C}
. Then

\partial_{u}(\beta)=\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial z_{1}}+\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial z_{2}}=2a+d\cos(z_{1}+2z_{2})(1+2)=2a+3d\cos(z_{1}+2z_{2}),

which is a non-constant oscillatory function. Substituting this into the compatibility condition

\partial_{u}(\beta)\partial_{u}(\gamma)=(n+1)(\partial_{u}(\gamma))^{2}+\partial_{u}^{2}(\gamma),

we obtain a differential equation involving
\gamma
. If we let
\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})=\Gamma(s)
where
s=z_{1}+2z_{2}
, then

\partial_{u}(\gamma)=\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial z_{1}}+\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial z_{2}}=3\Gamma^{\prime}(s).

The above compatibility condition then becomes the Riccati-type equation as follows

(2a+3d\cos s)G(s)=(n+1)\,3G(s)^{2}+G^{\prime}(s).

In general, this equation does not admit an entire zero-free solution
G(s)
unless
d=0
, in which case
\beta
becomes linear and Theorem 4.2 applies. Therefore, for
d\neq 0
, there is no entire exponential-type solution of (
4.1
). This example further confirms that the linearity of
\beta
in the direction of
u
is essential for the existence of entire exponential solutions.

Remark 4.1
.

Examples
4.7
and
4.8
emphasize the necessity of the linearity condition on
\beta
.
When
\beta
involves nonlinear or oscillatory terms (such as quadratic or trigonometric components) along the direction of differentiation
\partial_{u}
,
the functional relation

\partial_{u}(\beta)\partial_{u}(\gamma)=(n+1)(\partial_{u}(\gamma))^{2}+\partial_{u}^{2}(\gamma)

fails to possess zero-free entire solutions
\gamma
.
Hence, entire exponential-type solutions arise only when
\beta
is a polynomial of degree one in the variables along
u
,
fully confirming the restriction imposed in Theorem
4.2
.
