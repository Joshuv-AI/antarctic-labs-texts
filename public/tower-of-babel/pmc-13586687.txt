Data-driven upper limb sensorimotor recovery prediction in patients with stroke: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Siddharth Savadia N, John M Solomon, Ramana Kumar Vinjamuri, Aparna R Pai, Arvind Prabhu, Senthil Kumaran D
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stroke is a major cause of long-term disability globally, with upper limb sensorimotor deficits being among the most debilitating consequences. Accurate prediction of recovery trajectories can help clinicians individualise rehabilitation planning and optimize resource allocation, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where access to high-cost neurophysiological or imaging tools is limited. This protocol describes a prospective cohort study designed to develop and validate both conventional statistical and machine learning–based models for predicting upper limb sensorimotor recovery and quality of life at three and six months post-stroke. One hundred first-ever stroke survivors will be recruited and assessed using a standardised measurement tool consisting of demographic details and motor, sensory, and quality-of-life domains and complemented by 3D kinematic and video-based movement analysis. Statistical and machine learning algorithms (multivariable regression, logistic regression, random forest, support vector machine, and deep neural networks) will be trained and validated to forecast outcomes. This protocol aims to establish an accessible and scalable prediction framework to guide post-stroke rehabilitation strategies.•Development and validation of a multimodal, data-driven model for upper limb sensorimotor recovery prediction.•Multidomain and 3D kinematic variables are combined for comprehensive prognostication.•This provides a scalable, low-cost approach suitable for diverse rehabilitation settings.

Background

Stroke is the leading cause of disability worldwide and ranks as the second most common cause of death, making it a major public health issue worldwide. The reported prevalence of stroke was 1240.26 cases per 100,000 population in 2019 [1]. In low- and middle-income countries, the burden of stroke is high, accounting for 70% of deaths and nearly 87% of stroke-related impairments [2]. In those coming from low- or low- to middle-income countries such as India, stroke causes early mortality and disability [3].

Approximately 85% of post-stroke patients experience upper limb motor impairments, affecting individuals’ ability to perform activities of daily living and return to work post-stroke [4,5]. Between 5% and 20% of patients recover completely and are able to fully restore their upper extremity functions. In remaining survivors, upper extremity impairments contribute to the development of disabilities and restrict participation in daily activities [6], thus reducing quality of life. Post-stroke recovery extends beyond six months, progressing from hyperacute to chronic stages through spontaneous recovery and motor learning [7]. Upper limb recovery is slower [8], with 55–75% of survivors showing residual deficits at six months [9,10]. Hence early prediction is essential to guide treatment planning and outcomes.

Prediction models are statistical tools that support clinical decision-making by estimating patient-specific outcomes at predefined time points [11,12]. Existing prediction models combine demographic, clinical, neurophysiological, neuroimaging, and biomechanical predictors, such as age, sex, motor-evoked potentials, baseline motor impairment, lesion location, and sensory-evoked potentials, to generate individualised prognostic models and guide rehabilitation planning [13]. For example, resting state functional connectivity has been used to predict motricity index at three months post-stroke [14], whereas the PREP-2 model combines clinical, neurophysiological and imaging-based predictors to estimate upper limb functional outcome at three months [15]. While these models have advanced stroke recovery prediction, they differ considerably in predictor selection, outcome measure and validation approaches, and rely on specialised investigation that may limit their routine clinical implication, particularly in resource-constrained settings like in LMICs.

Machine learning (ML) algorithms excel at analysing nonlinear relationships and handling complex, high-dimensional datasets such as kinematic variables, enabling the identification of complex patterns [13]. Kinematic analysis provides an objective assessment of upper limb movement quality and has shown potential to improve prediction when combined with clinical measures [16,17]. More recently, markerless video-based movement analysis has emerged as a promising low-cost approach for extracting movement characteristics [18]. Despite the multifactorial nature of stroke recovery, most prediction models fail to account for predictors across multiple domains, with few studies integrating clinical, sensory, cognitive, psychological, and kinematic measures into a single predictive framework.

This prospective cohort study aims to develop and internally validate prediction models for upper limb sensorimotor recovery and quality of life after stroke by integrating demographic, clinical, cognitive, three-dimensional kinematic, and video-derived movement measures. The proposed protocol combines conventional statistical and machine learning approaches within a unified prediction framework, enabling comparison of their predictive performance using prespecified predictors and outcomes. By incorporating accessible multidomain assessments, this study seeks to develop clinically feasible prediction models that may support individualised rehabilitation planning, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.

Description of the protocol

This protocol has been developed in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis (TRIPOD+AI) guidelines.

Study design

This is a single-centre prospective cohort in which variables will be assessed at four different time points. Independent variables will be evaluated within 7 days post-stroke and at approximately 5-week post-stroke, whereas dependent variables will be assessed at the 12th (3 month) and 26th (6 month) weeks post-stroke. The prediction models will be developed according to the timing of predictor measurements. Models based on predictors collected within 7 days post-stroke will represent early prediction models, whereas models incorporating additional predictors collected at approximately 5 weeks post-stroke, including 3D kinematic and video-derived variables, will represent updated prediction models.

Study setting

The study will take place at Kasturba Hospital, Manipal, India. The screening of participants will be performed at the Stroke Unit, Department of Neurology, Kasturba Hospital, Manipal. Assessment of different variables will be conducted at the Physiotherapy Department of Kasturba Hospital and the Neuro-Motor Control Unit, Kasturba Hospital, Manipal, India.

Participants

Stroke survivors admitted to the stroke unit, Department of Neurology, Kasturba Hospital, with the presence of a first ischemic or hemorrhagic unilateral stroke and aged between 18 and 80 years of any sex will be eligible for the study. Patients admitted to the hospital seven days after stroke, who are unable to follow three-step commands and cannot comprehend, those who have undergone surgical procedures related to the brain, with any existing impairment affecting the upper extremity, and with any other musculoskeletal conditions, significant medical problems or comorbidities, or uncooperative patients, which might restrict the kinematic analysis, will be excluded from the study.

Sample size

The sample size for this prospective cohort study was estimated using a correlation-based approach. A 95% confidence level was considered, and on the basis of previous studies, an expected correlation of 0.41 between key predictors and outcomes was assumed [16]. Different levels of precision were explored using allowable margins of error of 0.10, 0.12, and 0.15, which resulted in initial sample size estimates of 69, 48, and 31, respectively. Among these, a sample size of 69, corresponding to an allowable margin of error of 0.10 around the anticipated correlation coefficient, was selected to provide the highest precision among the three evaluated scenarios.

Since the study includes multiple predictor variables that may be interrelated, the initial sample size was adjusted to account for potential intercorrelation among the predictors. Assuming an intercorrelation of 0.10, an inflation factor of 1/(1–0.10) was applied, increasing the required sample size from 69 to 77 participants. Finally, considering a possible 30% loss to follow-up over the six-month study period, the sample size was increased to 100 participants.

To align the prediction modelling strategy with the available sample, 10 candidate predictors have been prespecified a priori based on clinical relevance and evidence from previous stroke recovery prediction studies, which is broadly consistent with traditional recommendations regarding the ratio of sample size to predictor variables in prediction model development [19]. The primary prediction models will focus on the Fugl–Meyer Assessment (FMA), for which both continuous and binary prediction models will be developed, whereas sensory function and quality-of-life outcomes will be evaluated as separate secondary prediction models.

Study registration

This study is registered at “Kasturba Medical College and Kasturba Hospital IEC” (Reg. No. IEC1 377/2024) and Clinical Trials Registry – India (Reg. No. CTRI/2025/02/079927)

Variables

Independent variables (predictors)

Independent variables were prespecified a priori on the basis of clinical relevance and prior stroke recovery literature.

Demographic details and baseline characteristics

The demographic details recommended by the Stroke Recovery and Rehabilitation Roundtable play crucial roles in enhancing stroke prediction models [17]. These details cover age, sex, ethnicity, education, limb dominance, medical history, stroke type and site, side effects, post-stroke duration, premorbid functions and walking status, active hand movement at stroke onset, independence in walking at onset, and information on whether stroke treatment included thrombolysis, reperfusion therapy, or medication. To ensure clear and consistent operationalisation, each variable will be predefined as continuous, binary, or categorical, with explicit response options and coding specified prior to data collection and model development.

National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS)

The tool is best known for measuring stroke severity, and it also has good predictive value for predicting patient outcomes. The baseline NIHSS score at admission is strongly correlated with the functional outcomes of stroke patients [20]. The scale measures neurological deficits and stroke severity in a quantitative manner following stroke. It consists of 15 items that assess impairment, such as consciousness, the ability to respond to commands, visual and motor function and language skills. The total score ranges from 0 to 42, with higher scores indicating greater neurological impairment and greater stroke severity, whereas lower scores indicate less severe neurological impairment [21].

Montreal Cognitive Assessment scale (MoCA)

This tool is widely used to detect mild cognitive impairment in stroke patients. It includes 16 items and 11 categories to evaluate various cognitive components. A score of less than 26 out of 30 indicates mild cognitive impairment [7]. This scale assesses domains such as visuospatial, naming, memory, attention, language, abstraction, and orientation.

The motricity index

This is a test of manual muscle strength that includes tests for both the upper and lower extremities. This test has a total score of 100, indicating normal motor function. The upper extremity tests include tests of the shoulder, elbow and grip. The lower extremity tests consisted of hip, knee and ankle movements [22]. There are two categories for scoring grip and scoring movements other than grip. It ranges from 0 to 33, where 0 indicates no movement and 33 indicates normal power.

Shoulder Abduction and Finger Extension (SAFE) score

The SAFE score is widely used after a stroke to predict upper limb functional recovery. It is popular and reliable because it can be performed at the bedside without any special equipment. The strength of the upper limb shoulder abductors and finger extensors was assessed via the Medical Research Council grading system. The total score is calculated by summing the MRC score of shoulder abduction and finger extension, which results in a total score of 10 [23].

Grip and pinch strength

The grip strength will be assessed using a Jamar hand dynamometer. The participants will be asked to sit in a chair with their arms in a neutral position, with elbows at 90 degrees of flexion and forearms neutrally positioned. If needed, the elbow will be supported on the table. The participants will be instructed to squeeze the dynamometer with the maximum force for a duration of 5 to 10 s. The same trial will be repeated three times, and the average score will be noted [17]. To assess pinch strength, participants will be in the same position and will be asked to press the pinch meter with maximum force using their thumb and index finger. They will complete three trials, and the average of these three will be recorded [17] (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).Fig. 1Pinch meter.Fig. 2Dynamometer.

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)

It is a two-dimensional scale developed to detect depression and anxiety in patients with physical illnesses. The 14 items are divided into two 7-item subscales: anxiety (HADS-A), which targets generalized anxiety, and depression (HADS-D), which focuses on anhedonia. The participants rated each item on a 4-point scale from 0 (absent) to 3 (extreme). The total score can reach 42, with each subscale contributing up to 21 points. Scores are calculated by summing the responses within each subscale or across the entire scale. Higher scores reflect greater levels of anxiety or depression.

Kinematic analysis of the 3D upper limb functional task

The G4 Polhemus is a wireless electromagnetic system (G4TM Polhemus, Vermont, USA) that measures the kinematic parameters of the upper limb. This system includes five sensors (frequency rate of 120 Hz) providing six degrees-of-freedom (6-DOF) position and orientation data with sub-millimetre positional accuracy, and for the transmission of data, one radio frequency USB module is used. Sensors will be placed in the sternal area, acromion process, upper arm and forearm (Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7). The near and far target locations will then be established on a table positioned in front of the participant. The far target will be defined as the point corresponding to the participant's distal wrist crease during maximal upper-limb extension, whereas the near target will be positioned at two-thirds of the far-target distance. A cone with a diameter of 6 cm will be used as a target [24] (Fig. 8). The computer and the electronic unit of the motion analysis system will subsequently be switched on, and the PiMgR software will be initialized for data acquisition and kinematic analysis. Prior to each testing session, the electromagnetic field source will be positioned at a fixed location, and sensor signal integrity will be verified before data collection to minimise interference-related error. A fifth sensor will then be attached over the metacarpophalangeal region and calibrated for both the near and far target positions. Following calibration, distance between the sternal and acromion sensors, distance from the acromion sensor to the olecranon process, distance from the upper-arm sensor to the olecranon process, distance from the forearm sensor to the ulnar styloid process, and distance from the ulnar styloid process to the metacarpophalangeal sensor will be measured to facilitate kinematic modelling. Sensors will be securely attached to these anatomical landmarks using standardised procedures to minimise movement artefact. Upon completion of the system setup, participants will receive clear instructions in the language they understand. They will be asked to lift their affected arm when they hear a beep and move it forward at their own pace, as if they are reaching for a cone. If possible, participants should grasp the cone and bring it closer to their mouth. If they cannot grasp the cone, they should touch it and then move their hand toward their mouth. The participants will be asked to perform the task three times on the unaffected side, followed by performing the same task 15 times on the paretic side at a self-paced speed. Kinematic parameters.

Raw kinematic data will be visually inspected and processed offline. High-frequency noise will be reduced using a low-pass Butterworth filter, with the cut-off frequency determined following pilot testing and residual analysis. Smoothing will be applied where necessary to preserve movement characteristics while minimising measurement noise. Artefacts arising from transient signal disturbance, excessive movement, or electromagnetic interference will be identified through visual inspection. Trials showing substantial artefact will be excluded or repeated. Brief periods of missing data will be interpolated where appropriate, whereas trials with extensive signal loss will be discarded from analysis.

Video-based kinematic analysis of the upper limb functional task

The reach-to-grasp-to-mouth task of the affected upper limb will be video recorded using an Apple iPhone 13 smartphone camera. Video recording will be captured in the sagittal plane. We will be using the Apple iPhone 13 smartphone camera for video analysis with a recording parameter of 60 fps at 1080p resolution. The mobile device will be positioned 2.5 to 3.5 m laterally from the participant at a perpendicular angle. Ensuring coverage of the upper limb and the trunk. Recording will be performed in proper lights with an illumination level of approximately 300 lux, measured at the participant’s position using a lux meter, no high contrast in the background or shadows. The smartphone will be mounted on a tripod stand to promote stable footage and avoid unwanted vibrations. To evaluate the validity of the video-based approach, MediaPipe-derived kinematic variables will be compared with corresponding variables obtained from the Polhemus G4 system, which will serve as the reference standard. Agreement between methods will be assessed using Bland-Altman analysis.

The video recordings will be processed to derive the predefined kinematic parameters specified in Table 1. Following data collection, the video files will be transferred from the Apple iPhone 13 to the principal investigator’s laptop and subsequently to a password-protected institutional laptop for secure storage. After successful transfer and verification, the files will be deleted from the iPhone and the temporary storage on the principal investigator’s laptop. Cloud synchronization will be disabled, and the video files will not be uploaded to any third-party cloud storage. Each video file will be labelled using a unique study identification code without directly identifying information. As participants’ faces may be visible in the original recordings, the faces will be masked before video analysis. Access to the original video files will be restricted to the principal investigator and the corresponding author. Video recording and its use for the purposes of this research were included in the ethical approval process, and the informed consent form contains a specific clause regarding video recording. The video files will be retained securely in accordance with institutional data-retention policies and securely deleted after the required retention period.

The parameters of the G4 Polhemus and video-based kinematic analysis will undergo preprocessing, including quality checks, handling of impulsive values, and normalisation or scaling as needed. Feature extraction from video data will be performed.

Based on clinical relevance and evidence from previous stroke recovery prediction studies, the following candidate predictors have been prespecified a priori for model development: age, NIHSS score, SAFE score, MoCA score, grip strength, pinch strength, HADS score, and kinematic parameters, including elbow extension, total displacement, and total movement time [13,16].

Primary dependent variable (outcome)

The dependent variables, which constitute the primary outcome measures, will be assessed at three and six months post-stroke.

Fugl Meyer assessment upper extremity (FMA-UE)

The FMA-UE will be intended to evaluate the motor function of the paretic upper limb. This scale evaluates upper limb sensorimotor recovery. It includes 33 items that evaluate reflex activity, coordination, hand function, voluntary movement, and wrist function. Each item is scored on a 3-point scale from 0 to 2, with a maximum total score of 66.

Secondary dependent variable (outcomes)

The dependent variables, which constitute the secondary outcome measures, will be assessed at three and six months post-stroke.

Erasmus MC modification of the Nottingham sensory assessment (Em-NSA)

This scale will be used to assess the somatosensory function of the paretic limb. It is a performance-based clinical assessment that measures light touch, pressure, pinprick, proprioception, and stereognosis. All sensations will be assessed in the anatomical sites of the paretic upper limb. The items are scored from 0 to 2, where 0 indicates the absence of sensation, 1 indicates impaired, and 2 indicates normal.

EuroQol 5-dimension questionnaire (EQ-5D)

This scale measures health-related quality of life in stroke patients across five areas: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each area is evaluated using a 5-level scale. Participants will also rate their overall health with a visual analogue scale (EQ-VAS), which ranges from 0 (worst imaginable health) to 100 (best imaginable health). The EQ-5D will be provided in the participant’s preferred language, such as English or Kannada.

Study procedure

The study will follow a structured, stepwise process. Patients will be screened every day from the hospital for eligibility criteria to be included in the study by the principal investigator. Each potential participant will receive a Participant Information Sheet (PIS) in their preferred language (English or Kannada), outlining the purpose, procedures, potential risks, and confidentiality measures of the study. Prior to inclusion, the investigator will verbally explain all aspects of the research, address any questions, and obtain written informed consent from willing participants.

Following recruitment, the baseline assessment (T0) will be conducted within seven days post-stroke while participants are hospitalized. This baseline evaluation will document demographic and clinical details, including age, sex, stroke type, lesion site, comorbidities, premorbid function, limb dominance, walking status, and rehabilitation details. The standardised clinical and psychological assessments conducted at baseline will include the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) to determine stroke severity, the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) for assessing cognitive function, the motricity index (upper limb), the shoulder abduction and finger extension (SAFE) score, and the grip and pinch strengths, measured using a Jamar dynamometer and a pinch meter, will be assessed for strength, and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) for assessing emotional well-being. During hospitalization, participants will receive routine physiotherapy care as part of the hospital’s standard stroke management protocol. This treatment is not part of the research intervention but rather routine clinical management delivered by the hospital physiotherapy team.

On or before five weeks post-stroke (T1), participants will be reassessed during their scheduled neurology outpatient visit. At this stage, participants will undergo 3D kinematic analysis and 2D video-based movement recording to objectively capture upper limb motor performance. For 3D motion analysis, the Polhemus G4 wireless electromagnetic tracking system will be used. Simultaneously, 2D video recording will be conducted using an iPhone 13 smartphone camera (1080p resolution, 60 fps) positioned approximately 2.5 m from the participant. These 2D recordings will be analysed using MediaPipe software to extract kinematic parameters as mentioned in Table 1.

Follow-up assessments will be conducted at three months (T2) and six months (T3) post-stroke for dependent variables, which will coincide with the participants’ neurology follow-ups. These sessions track longitudinal improvements in sensorimotor function, movement quality, and quality of life. Assessments at these stages will include the FMA-UE for motor recovery, the Erasmus MC Modification of the Nottingham Sensory Assessment (Em-NSA) for sensory function, and an EQ-5D questionnaire for health-related quality of life (Figs. 9 and 10).Fig. 9Timepoints and the assessments.Fig. 10Flowchart of the study procedure.

Bias

Measurement (observer) bias will be reduced through assessor blinding and standardised protocols. Two investigators—each a doctoral scholar trained in neurological physiotherapy—will independently collect data: one responsible for recording independent (predictor) variables and the other for dependent (outcome) variables. Both investigators will undergo calibration sessions before data collection to standardise scoring methods for all assessment tools, including the NIHSS, FMA-UE, Em-NSA, and HADS.

Statistical analysis

Appropriate statistical methods will be used to analyse the data. Descriptive statistics will be performed to summarise participant characteristics. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test will be used to assess the normality of continuous variables.

Handling of missing data

The extent, distribution, and patterns of missing data will be examined prior to model development by quantifying the degree of missingness for each variable and assessing whether it is likely to be completely random or systematically related to observed characteristics. Comparisons between participants with complete and incomplete data will be conducted to inform handling strategies. The primary approach for handling missing data will be multiple imputation, which is consistent with current recommendations for prediction model development. Imputation will incorporate all the candidate predictors and outcomes associated with missingness. A sensitivity analysis will be conducted by comparing model performance metrics obtained from the multiply imputed dataset with those from a complete-case analysis.

Conventional statistical prediction models

Prediction outcomes

The primary prediction models will be developed for upper limb motor recovery measured using the Upper Extremity Fugl–Meyer Assessment (FMA-UE). Separate prediction models will be developed for both the continuous FMA-UE score and the binary FMA-UE outcome at 3 months and 6 months post-stroke. For the binary models, participants will be classified as having a poor outcome (FMA-UE ≤ 25) or not poor outcome (FMA-UE > 25) based on the Upper Extremity Fugl–Meyer Assessment [25].

Secondary prediction models will be developed separately for sensory recovery measured using the Erasmus MC modification of the Nottingham Sensory Assessment (Em-NSA) and quality of life (QoL). These secondary outcomes will be modelled independently and will not be incorporated into the primary FMA-UE prediction models.

Data preprocessing

Prior to model development, data quality will be assessed by examining missing values, outliers, data distribution, and inconsistencies. Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation as described in Section 9.1. Continuous predictors will be standardised where appropriate, and categorical variables will be appropriately coded before model development.

Candidate predictors

Prediction models will be developed using the 10 prespecified candidate predictors described in Section 4, including age, NIHSS score, SAFE score, MoCA score, grip strength, pinch strength, HADS score, elbow extension, total displacement, and total movement time. These predictors were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and evidence from previous stroke recovery prediction studies. No additional data-driven predictor selection or dimensionality reduction techniques will be performed.

Model development

Separate prediction models will be developed for each outcome and prediction time point. Early models will use predictors collected within 7 days post-stroke, whereas updated prediction models will additionally incorporate predictors measured at approximately 5 weeks post-stroke. For continuous FMA-UE outcomes, penalized multivariable linear regression using Elastic Net regularization will be developed. For binary FMA-UE outcomes, penalized multivariable logistic regression using Elastic Net regularization will be developed. Secondary prediction outcomes will be analysed using statistical models appropriate to the scale and distribution of each outcome.

Internal validation

Internal validation will be performed using bootstrap resampling to estimate optimism-corrected model performance. When multiple comparable datasets are available, internal–external validation will additionally be undertaken using a leave-one-dataset-out approach, whereby the model will be developed on all but one dataset and evaluated on the remaining dataset, with this process repeated across datasets.

Model performance evaluation

For continuous prediction models, model performance will be evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²), root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), calibration intercept, and calibration slope. For binary prediction models, model performance will be evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), classification accuracy, Brier score, calibration intercept, and calibration slope.

Estimation of uncertainty and reporting

Regression coefficients, model performance measures, and calibration statistics will be presented with 95% confidence intervals obtained using bootstrap resampling where appropriate. Model development, validation, and reporting will follow the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) recommendations, and the risk of bias will be assessed using the Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool (PROBAST).

Machine learning–based prediction models

Prediction outcomes

Machine learning models will be developed for the same primary and secondary outcomes described for the conventional statistical models. Separate machine learning models will be developed for the continuous and binary FMA-UE outcomes at 3 months and 6 months post-stroke, as well as for the secondary outcomes of sensory recovery (Em-NSA) and quality of life.

Data preprocessing

Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation. All preprocessing procedures, including imputation, scaling, and normalization where appropriate, will be performed within the cross-validation folds to prevent data leakage.

Candidate predictors

The same 10 prespecified candidate predictors used in the conventional statistical prediction models will be used for all machine learning models to ensure direct comparability between modelling approaches. No additional feature selection or dimensionality reduction techniques will be performed.

Machine learning model development

Machine learning models will be developed using regularized, relatively low-complexity algorithms. For continuous FMA-UE prediction, Elastic Net regression and Support Vector Regression (SVR) with a linear kernel will be developed. For binary FMA-UE prediction, Elastic Net logistic regression and Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifiers with a linear kernel will be developed. Separate models will be developed for early prediction using predictors collected within 7 days post-stroke and updated prediction using predictors collected on or before 5 weeks post-stroke.

Hyperparameter tuning

Hyperparameters will be optimized within training data using repeated 10-fold cross-validation. For Elastic Net models, the regularization parameter (λ) and mixing parameter (α) will be optimized, whereas for Support Vector Machine models, the cost parameter (C) will be optimized using a grid search approach within the cross-validation framework.

Internal validation

Internal validation will be performed using repeated 10-fold cross-validation to assess model stability and predictive performance. When multiple comparable datasets are available, internal–external validation will additionally be undertaken by developing the model on all but one dataset and evaluating its performance on the held-out dataset, with this process repeated across datasets.

Model performance evaluation

For continuous prediction models, performance will be evaluated using R², RMSE, MAE, calibration intercept, and calibration slope. For binary models, performance will be evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration and classification performance using AUROC, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, classification accuracy, Brier score, calibration intercept, and calibration slope.

Estimation of uncertainty and model comparison

Model performance measures, regression coefficients, feature importance estimates, and calibration statistics will be reported with 95% confidence intervals obtained using bootstrap resampling where appropriate. The performance of machine learning models will be compared with the corresponding conventional statistical prediction models using the same evaluation metrics without assuming the superiority of either modelling approach. Given the exploratory nature of the machine learning analyses and the anticipated modest sample size, findings will be interpreted cautiously with emphasis on transparency, clinical plausibility, and reproducibility. Independent external validation in a separate cohort will be required before any developed prediction model can be recommended for routine clinical use.

All statistical analyses and machine learning model development will be performed using R (version 4.6 or later) or Python (version 3.14 or later). Machine learning analyses will primarily be implemented using the scikit-learn library (version 1.9 or later) in Python. When analyses are conducted in R, the rpart package will be used for decision tree–based modelling where appropriate.

Protocol validation

All the outcome measures included in this protocol are widely used and validated in stroke rehabilitation research, supporting their reliability and suitability for longitudinal assessment. The assessment battery was designed to be feasible within routine inpatient and outpatient clinical workflows and can be completed within a single session at each time point without adverse events.

Prior to participant recruitment, assessors will go under structured training and calibration sessions to standardise administration and scoring procedures across all measurement tools. To reduce interobserver variability, standardised operating procedures will be followed for clinical assessments and kinematic data collection.

The 3D kinematic assessment protocol is adapted from previously published methodologies utilizing the Polhemus G4 electromagnetic motion capture system, which has demonstrated reliable measurement of upper limb movement parameters in individuals with stroke. The 2D video-based recording protocol is standardised with respect to the camera placement, frame rate, and lighting conditions to ensure the consistent extraction of movement features.

Together, these procedures support the feasibility, reproducibility, and methodological robustness of the proposed protocol, enabling replication across diverse clinical and research settings.

Limitations

Attrition during the six months of follow-up may lead to an incomplete gathering of data, adding a type of bias due to attrition; this will be mitigated through structured participant retention strategies such as reminders, flexible follow-up strategies such as tele- or video conferencing interviews, as well as engaging caregivers, and proper statistical analysis of missing data using multiple imputation techniques and sensitivity analyses. Single-centre recruitment of participants may lead to a lack of generalizability of the results to a larger population of stroke patients; this will be mitigated through thorough documentation of participant characteristics and stroke rehabilitation details, ensuring standardised assessment protocols to increase replicability, and future multicentre studies. Additionally, the small number of participants may hamper the external validity of machine learning algorithms, particularly in terms of creating a new prediction model; this will be mitigated through appropriate feature engineering and the use of regularization in machine learning algorithms.
