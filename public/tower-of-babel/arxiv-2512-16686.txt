Prescribed L p L_{p} curvature problem for convex capillary hypersurface
Xinqun Mei, Guofang Wang, Liangjun Weng

Abstract
. We study the prescribed L p L_{p} curvature problem for convex capillary hypersurfaces in the Euclidean half-space. By reducing the problem to finding a convex solution of a Hessian quotient–type equation with a Robin boundary condition on a spherical cap, we establish the existence and uniqueness of smooth admissible (in fact, strictly convex) solutions. As applications, we solve the capillary L p L_{p} Christoffel–Minkowski problem in the smooth category, and we also obtain corresponding results for the prescribed L p L_{p} curvature problem and the related eigenvalue problem for convex capillary hypersurfaces in the Euclidean half-space.

1.
Introduction

The study of prescribed curvature problems lies at the core of modern convex geometry and geometric analysis. Over the past decades, a wide array of tools—ranging from the theory of fully nonlinear elliptic equations to geometric curvature flows—has been brought to bear on classical questions such as the Minkowski problem, the Christoffel–Minkowski problem, and their various
L_{p}
extensions. These developments have revealed a striking unifying principle: many geometric inequalities, variational structures, and rigidity phenomena can be encoded in nonlinear curvature quantities, whose analytic behavior captures surprisingly refined geometric information. In contrast to the extensively studied closed setting, curvature prescription for hypersurfaces with boundary, particularly in capillary geometries, remains far less understood. The presence of a supporting hyperplane and a prescribed Young’s contact angle creates boundary interactions that resist direct application of techniques successful in the closed case. This intrinsic boundary coupling introduces substantial analytic difficulties and necessitates the development of genuinely new ideas and techniques.

A
capillary hypersurface
in the Euclidean half-space
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}

(n\geq 2)
is a properly embedded, smooth, compact hypersurface
\Sigma\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
with nonempty boundary
\partial\Sigma\subset\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
, whose boundary meets the supporting hyperplane at a constant contact angle
\theta\in(0,\pi)
. That is,

\displaystyle\langle\nu,e\rangle=-\cos\theta,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ along }\penalty\ \partial\Sigma,

where
\nu
and
e=-E_{n+1}
are the unit outward normal of
\Sigma\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
and
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
respectively. A basic example of a capillary hypersurface is the unit spherical cap

\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\coloneqq\left\{\xi\in\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}\mid\penalty\ |\xi-\cos\theta e|=1\right\}.

Let
\kappa(X)\coloneqq(\kappa_{1},\cdots,\kappa_{n})
denote the principal curvatures of
\Sigma
at
X\in\Sigma
, and let
\sigma_{k}(\kappa(X))
denote the
k
-th mean curvature of
\Sigma
, see Section
2
for details.

In this paper, we study the prescribed
L_{p}
curvature problem for convex capillary hypersurfaces in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
. More precisely,

Given a smooth function
f
on the spherical cap
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, can one find a strictly convex capillary hypersurface
\Sigma\in\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
such that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{n-l}(\kappa(X))}{\sigma_{n-k}(\kappa(X))}=f^{-1}(\tilde{\nu}(X))\langle X,\nu(X)\rangle^{1-p}\penalty\ ?

Here
0\leq l<k\leq n
, and

\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}(X)\coloneqq\nu(X)+\cos\theta e,

is the
capillary Gauss map
of
\Sigma
, which is a diffeomorphism from
\Sigma
to
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
if
\Sigma
is strictly convex. See
[
41
, Section 2]
for more discussion on
\tilde{\nu}
.

Before proceeding, we briefly recall some progress on the prescribed
L_{p}
curvature problem in the closed setting. Let

When

In
[
47
]
, we studied the prescribed Gauss-Kronecker curvature problem for convex capillary hypersurfaces in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
, which we refer to as the capillary Minkowski problem. A necessary and sufficient condition for the solution to the capillary Minkowski problem was obtained therein. We
[
44

further investigated the prescribed Weingarten curvature problem for convex capillary hypersurfaces, i.e., the problem of finding a strictly convex capillary hypersurface that satisfies

(1.5)

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(\kappa(X))=f^{-1}(\tilde{\nu}(X)).

Under the condition that
f
is a capillary even function on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, we obtained the existence of a strictly convex, capillary even hypersurface solving Eq. (
1.5
). A function
f\colon\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
capillary even
on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, if for any point
\xi\coloneqq(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n},\xi_{n+1})\in\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
,

f(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n},\xi_{n+1})=f(-\xi_{1},\cdots,-\xi_{n},\xi_{n+1}).

We say that a capillary hypersurface is capillary even if its support function is a capillary even function on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
. On the other hand, the capillary Minkowski problem can also be viewed as a prescribed surface area measure problem for the capillary hypersurface, see
[
47
, Section 1]
. Subsequently, in
[
46

L_{p}
-Minkowski problem for capillary convex bodies with

, which seeks to find a capillary convex body with a prescribed capillary
L_{p}
-surface area measure in the Euclidean half-space. This problem corresponds to the solvability of the following Monge-Ampère equation with a Robin boundary value condition:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\vskip 5.69054pt\displaystyle\det(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)&=&fh^{p-1},&\quad\hbox{ in }\mathcal{C}_{\theta},\\
\nabla_{\mu}h&=&\cot\theta h,&\quad\hbox{ on }\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta},\end{array}\right.

where
\nabla h
and
\nabla^{2}h
are the gradient and the Hessian of
h
on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
with respect to the standard spherical metric
\sigma
on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}

\mu
is the unit outward normal of
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\subset\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
.
We solved (
1
) for the case
p=1
and

using an elliptic approach in
[
47
]
and
[
46
]
respectively. Later,
[

]
and
[

studied the case

Motivated by these results for closed convex hypersurfaces and the capillary
L_{p}
Minkowski problem, it is natural for us to further investigate the prescribed
L_{p}
curvature problem for convex capillary hypersurfaces. From

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\vskip 5.69054pt\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k}(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)}{\sigma_{l}(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)}&=&fh^{p-1}&\quad\hbox{ in }\mathcal{C}_{\theta},\\
\nabla_{\mu}h&=&\cot\theta h,&\quad\hbox{ on }\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}.\end{array}\right.

We say that a function
h\in C^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
is (strictly) convex if

W\coloneqq\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma

is positive (definite) semi-definite. A function

is called admissible if it is
k
-convex in the sense of Definition
2.3
. When

, the problem reduces to the classical closed case via a reflection argument; hence, in what follows, we focus on the general case
\theta\neq\frac{\pi}{2}
.

Our first main result establishes the existence and uniqueness of an admissible solution to (
1
) for the case
p\geq k-l+1
. Moreover, under additional conditions on the function
f
, the admissible solution can be shown to be strictly convex. We state the theorem precisely below.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
0\leq l<k\leq n
and
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then, for any positive, smooth function
f
on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, the following results hold.

(1)

If
p>k-l+1
, then there exists a unique, smooth, admissible solution
h
solving Eq. (
1
).

(2)

If
p=k-l+1
, then there exists a positive, smooth and admissible solution
h
and a positive constant
\tau
solving

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\vskip 5.69054pt\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k}(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)}{\sigma_{l}(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)}&=&\tau fh^{k-l},&\quad\hbox{in }\mathcal{C}_{\theta},\\
\nabla_{\mu}h&=&\cot\theta h,&\quad\hbox{ on }\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}.\end{array}\right.

Uniqueness holds up to a dilation.

Furthermore, when
k\leq n-1
, assume that
f
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\vskip 5.69054pt\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f^{-\frac{1}{p+k-l-1}}+f^{-\frac{1}{p+k-l-1}}\sigma&\geq&0,\penalty\ \penalty\ {\text{in}}\penalty\ \mathcal{C}_{\theta},\\
\nabla_{\mu}f+\cot\theta(p+k-l-1)f&\geq&0,\penalty\ \penalty\ {\text{on}}\penalty\ \partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}.\end{array}\right.

Then the admissible solutions in (1) and (2) are strictly convex.

We conclude the introduction by outlining the main ideas of the proofs.
To establish existence in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, we employ the continuity method and the topological degree method, respectively. A key ingredient in both approaches is the derivation of a priori estimates for solutions to Eq. (
1
). When
p>k-l+1
, the
C^{0}
estimate of admissible solutions follows directly from the maximum principle.
However, for
k\leq n-1
, the possible loss of convexity prevents the
C^{0}
bound from implying a
C^{1}
estimate
(see, e.g.,
[
44
, Lemma 3.3]
).
Another major difficulty arises in obtaining global
C^{2}
estimates, particularly in controlling the double normal derivative of
h
at the boundary.
For convex
h
, the problematic terms

-2(1+\beta\bar{d})F^{ii}W_{ii}\bar{d}_{ii}
in (
3.73
) can be bounded by
C\widehat{f}
.
We adopt the techniques of Ma-Qiu
[
39
]
(see also the similar idea used in
[
37
,
8
]
)
and introduce suitable auxiliary functions to overcome these challenges.

When
p=k-l+1
, the dilation invariance of Eq. (
1
) results in the lack of estimates for its solution. To address this problem, we adopt the approach outlined in
[
18
, Section 3]
and
[
24
, Section 4]
. The key step is to establish a logarithmic gradient estimate for the positive, admissible solution to Eq. (
1
), given by

(1.18)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\log h\|_{C^{0}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})}\leq C.

Compared with the classical case, the Robin boundary condition of
h
introduces additional challenges in deriving the above estimate (
1.18
). To achieve this objective, we introduce a new test function (see Lemma
3.4
),

\displaystyle\omega\coloneqq\log h-\cot\theta\tilde{\ell},

where the positive function
\tilde{\ell}
is defined in (
3.30
). Through a detailed and rigorous analysis, we ultimately establish the desired estimate as given in (
1.18
).

For the case
1<p<k-l+1
, the crucial step is to show positive lower and upper bounds for
h
under the capillary even assumption. In contrast to the capillary
L_{p}
Minkowski problem considered in
[
46
]
, the special structure of Eq. (
1
) with
l=0
and evenness assumption on
h
implies a positive lower and upper bounds for the solution, but this argument fails for the cases
0\leq l<k\leq n-1
and
1\leq l<k\leq n
. We adopt an alternative and unified approach to complete this step for all
0\leq l<k\leq n
. We briefly sketch the proof below. First,
we establish a quantitative dependence relationship between the maximal eigenvalue of
W=\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma
(i.e., the maximal principal radii of
\Sigma
),
\|h\|_{C^{0}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})}
,
\max\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h
and
\min\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h
(see (
3.77
)) :

(1.19)

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}|\nabla^{2}h|\leq C\left[1+\|h\|_{C^{0}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})}\left(1+(\min\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h)^{a-1}\right)+(\max\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h)^{2-\gamma}(\min\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h)^{\gamma-1}\right].

The estimate (
1.19
) is one of the important innovations of this paper. On the other hand, from the maximum principle, we get

(1.20)

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h\leq C,\quad{\rm{and}}\quad\max\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h\geq c.

and the geometric Lemma
2.7
for strictly convex capillary hypersurfaces implies

(1.21)

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h\leq C\sup\limits_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\lambda_{x,\Sigma}\cdot\min\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h,

where
\lambda_{x,\Sigma}
is the maximal eigenvalue of the symmetric matrix
W
at
x
. By combining (
1.19
), (
1.20
) and (
1.21
), we obtain the positive lower and upper bounds for the support function
h
and
C^{2}
estimate simultaneously. Finally, we note that when
1<p<k-l+1
, an angle constraint is required, and this constraint is only intended to obtain a weighted gradient estimate (
3.44
), which plays a crucial role in establishing (
1.19
), see (
3.66
) and (
3.67
). Nevertheless, we believe that the estimate (
3.44
) and Theorem
1.2
hold for at least the range
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we first recall several well-known properties of the elementary symmetric functions. We then review certain geometric aspects of capillary convex bodies, and further introduce the notion of the capillary
k
-th
p
-area measure for capillary convex bodies.

2.1.
Elementary symmetric functions

In this subsection, we recall the definition and some basic properties of the elementary symmetric polynomial functions.

Definition 2.1
.

For
k=1,2,\ldots,n,
the
k
-th elementary symmetric function
\sigma_{k}
is defined by

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(\lambda)=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\lambda_{i_{2}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{k}},\qquad\text{for}\quad\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We use the convention that
\sigma_{0}=1
and
\sigma_{k}=0
for
k>n
. Let
H_{k}(\lambda)
be the normalization of

\sigma_{k}(\lambda)
given by

\displaystyle H_{k}(\lambda)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sigma_{k}(\lambda).

Denote
\sigma_{k}(\lambda\left|i\right.)
the symmetric
function with
\lambda_{i}=0
and
\sigma_{k}(\lambda\left|ij\right.)
the symmetric function with
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}=0
. Recall that the Gårding cone is defined as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}\coloneqq\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\sigma_{i}(\lambda)>0,\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall 1\leq i\leq k\right\}.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
A=\{A_{ij}\}
be an
n\times n
symmetric matrix, for
k=1,2,\cdots,n
, we define

(2.1)

2.2.
Geometric properties of capillary convex bodies

In this subsection, we briefly review some properties of the capillary convex bodies and for more details concerning the geometry of capillary convex bodies, we refer the reader to
[
47
, Section 2]
and
[
41
, Section 2]
.

We call
\widehat{\Sigma}
a
capillary convex body
if
\widehat{\Sigma}
is a compact closed domain in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
enclosed by a convex capillary hypersurface
\Sigma
and
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
, and denote
\widehat{\partial\Sigma}\coloneqq\partial(\widehat{\Sigma})\setminus\Sigma\subset\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
. Denote
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
as the set of all capillary convex bodies in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
, and
\mathcal{K}^{\circ}_{\theta}
as the family of capillary convex bodies for which the origin is an interior point of their flat boundary. Let
\widehat{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
and
\nu
be the unit outward normal of
\Sigma
in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
. The capillary Gauss map of
\Sigma
, which is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{v}\coloneqq\nu+\cos\theta e,

is a diffeomorphism for
\Sigma
to
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
. See
[
41
, Lemma 2.2]
. It is clear that for
\Sigma=\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
,
\tilde{\nu}
is the identity map, and its support function is given by

\displaystyle\ell(\xi)\coloneqq\langle\xi,\xi-\cos\theta e\rangle=\sin^{2}\theta+\cos\theta\langle\xi,e\rangle.

The support function of
\Sigma
is given by

\displaystyle h(X)=\langle X,\nu(X)\rangle.

By the parametrization of
\tilde{\nu}^{-1}
, we can view
h
as a function on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
by

(2.2)

\displaystyle h(\xi)=\langle X(\xi),\nu(X(\xi))\rangle=\langle\tilde{\nu}^{-1}(\xi),\xi-\cos\theta e\rangle,

and satisfies

(2.3)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}h=\cot\theta h,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ on }\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}.

cf.
[
41
, Lemma 2.4]
. We still call
h
in (
2.2
) the support function of
\Sigma
(or of
\widehat{\Sigma}
), which is only defined on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
.

By viewing
\widehat{\Sigma}\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
as a closed convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, we recall several notions of principal radii used in
[
53
, Section 2.2]
and
[
45
, Section 2]
. These principal radii will play a crucial role in controlling the geometry of
\Sigma
, in particular in obtaining uniform diameter estimates. The inner radius of
\widehat{\Sigma}
, which is defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{-}(\widehat{\Sigma})\coloneqq\sup\left\{\rho>0\mid B_{\rho}^{+}(x_{0})\subset\widehat{\Sigma}\penalty\ \text{for\penalty\ some\penalty\ }x_{0}\in\partial\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}\right\},

and the outer radius of
\widehat{\Sigma}
is defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma})\coloneqq\inf\left\{\rho>0\mid\widehat{\Sigma}\subset B_{\rho}^{+}(x_{0})\penalty\ \text{for\penalty\ some\penalty\ }x_{0}\in\partial\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}\right\}

where
B^{+}_{\rho}(x_{0})\coloneqq B_{\rho}(x_{0})\cap\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
and
B_{\rho}(x_{0})
is an open ball of radius
\rho
centered at
x_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The notion of the capillary inner radius of
\widehat{\Sigma}\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{-}(\widehat{\Sigma},\theta)\coloneqq\sup\left\{r>0\penalty\ \mid\penalty\ \widehat{\mathcal{C}_{r,\theta}(x_{0})}\subset\widehat{\Sigma}\text{ for some }x_{0}\in\partial\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}\right\},

and
the capillary outer radius of
\widehat{\Sigma}
as

\displaystyle\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma},\theta)\coloneqq\inf\left\{r>0\penalty\ \mid\penalty\ \widehat{\Sigma}\subset\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{r,\theta}(x_{0})}\text{ for some }x_{0}\in\partial\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}\right\},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{r,\theta}(x_{0})\coloneqq\left\{x\in\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}\penalty\ \mid\penalty\ |x-(x_{0}+r\cos\theta e)|=r\right\}

is the spherical cap centered at
x_{0}+r\cos\theta e
with radius
r>0
.

The following proposition was shown in
[
45
, Proposition 2.3]
(or
[
53
, Proposition 2.4 and its proof]
), which shows that for capillary convex bodies, the inner (outer) radius and the capillary inner (outer) radius can be mutually controlled when
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\widehat{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
and
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then there hold

\displaystyle(1-\cos\theta)\rho_{-}(\widehat{\Sigma},\theta)\leq\rho_{-}(\widehat{\Sigma})\leq\sin\theta\rho_{-}(\widehat{\Sigma},\theta),

and

\displaystyle(1-\cos\theta)\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma},\theta)\leq\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma})\leq\sin\theta\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma},\theta).

Finally, we introduce an important geometric lemma, which plays a crucial role in establishing the a priori estimates for the solution to (
1
) when
1<p<k-l+1
. This geometric lemma was originally obtained by Chou-Wang for smooth, closed, strictly convex hypersurfaces, as shown in
[
11
, Lemma 2.2]
, see also
[
2
, Lemma 15.11]
. We obtain an analogous result for strictly convex capillary hypersurfaces in
[
44
, Lemma 2.3]
.

Lemma 2.7

(
[
44
]
)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a strictly convex capillary hypersurface in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
and
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
.
Then there exists a dimensional constant

such that

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma},\theta)^{2}}{\rho_{-}(\widehat{\Sigma},\theta)}\leq C\sup\limits_{x\in\Sigma}\lambda_{x,\Sigma},

where
\lambda_{x,\Sigma}
is the maximal principal radii of

at the point

.

2.3.
The capillary
k
-th
p
-area measure

In this subsection, we introduce the concept of capillary
k
-th
{p}
-surface area measure for a capillary convex body

, which serves as an extension of the capillary surface area measure introduced in
[
46
, Definition 2.2]

[
46
, Section 2]
, we introduced the capillary surface area measure via the first variation of the volume of a capillary convex body under Firey

[

, we introduce the concept of the capillary

-th

k
-th quermassintegral of capillary convex bodies under the perturbation of Firey’s

-sum.

For
\varepsilon>0
, and
K,L\in\mathcal{K}^{\circ}_{\theta}
, we denote
K_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq K+_{p}\varepsilon L
as the Firey
p
-sum of convex bodies
K
and
L
. From
[

, we see that

is also a capillary convex body in

, and its support function

is given by

\displaystyle h_{K_{\varepsilon}}=\left[h_{K}^{p}(\cdot)+\varepsilon h_{L}^{p}(\cdot)\right]^{\frac{1}{p}},

where
h_{K},h_{L}\in C^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
are the support functions of the convex bodies
K
and
L
, respectively.
For
p\geq 1
and
1\leq k\leq n
, the capillary mixed
p
-quermassintegral of
K
and

is defined by

(2.5)

\displaystyle\frac{n+1-k}{p}\mathcal{V}_{p,k}(K,L)=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\mathcal{V}_{k,\theta}(K_{\varepsilon})-\mathcal{V}_{k,\theta}(K)}{\varepsilon}.

Here,
\mathcal{V}_{k,\theta}(K)
denotes the
k
-th quermassintegral (cf.
[
55
]
and
[
41
, Lemma 2.14]
),
given by

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{k,\theta}(K)=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{K}H_{n-k}(W[h_{K}])d\sigma,

where
W[h_{K}]\coloneqq\nabla^{2}h_{K}+h_{K}\sigma
.
Next, we establish the following integral formula for the capillary mixed
p
-quermassintegral.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
K,L\in\mathcal{K}^{\circ}_{\theta}
and
1\leq k\leq n
. The capillary mixed
p
-quermassintegral defined in (

) behaves as the following integral representation

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{p,k}(K,L)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{L}^{p}h_{K}^{1-p}dS_{K,k},

where
dS_{K,k}=H_{n-k}(W[h_{K}])d\sigma
.

Proof.

Let
K,L\in\mathcal{K}^{\circ}_{\theta}
, we set
K_{\varepsilon}=K+_{p}\varepsilon L
for
\varepsilon\geq 0
.
From (
2.4
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}h_{K_{\varepsilon}}=\frac{1}{p}h_{L}^{p}h_{K}^{1-p}.

Together with (
2.5
) and (
2.6
), we derive

(2.8)

\displaystyle\frac{n+1-k}{p}\mathcal{V}_{p,k}(K,L)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}\mathcal{V}_{k,\theta}(K_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p(n+1)}\left[\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{L}^{p}h_{K}^{1-p}H_{n-k}(W[h_{K}])d\sigma\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{K}H_{n-k-1}^{ij}(W[h_{K}])\left((h_{L}^{p}h_{K}^{1-p})_{ij}+h_{L}^{p}h_{K}^{1-p}\sigma_{ij}\right)d\sigma\right].

It is readily verified that
\nabla_{\mu}(h_{L}^{p}h_{K}^{1-p})=\cot\theta h_{L}^{p}h_{K}^{1-p}
on
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
. From
[
2
, Lemma 18.30]
, we know that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{i}\left(H_{n-k-1}^{ij}(W[h_{K}])\right)=0
. Using
[
41
, Lemma 2.7]
and integrating by parts twice, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{K}H_{n-k-1}^{ij}(W[h_{K}])\left((h_{L}^{p}h_{K}^{1-p})_{ij}+h_{L}^{p}h_{K}^{1-p}\sigma_{ij}\right)d\sigma=(n-k)\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{L}^{p}h_{K}^{1-p}H_{n-k}(W[h_{K}])d\sigma.

Together with (
2.8
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{p,k}(K,L)=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{L}^{p}h_{k}^{1-p}H_{n-k}(W[h_{K}])d\sigma,

which complete the proof of (

).
∎

\displaystyle dS_{\widehat{\Sigma},k,p}^{c}\coloneqq\ell^{p}h_{\widehat{\Sigma}}^{1-p}\,dS_{\widehat{\Sigma},k},

and call it the

of the capillary convex body

In particular, when

introduced in

To conclude this subsection, we formulate the
capillary
L_{p}
Christoffel-Minkowski problem
. The goal is to identify a capillary convex body
\widehat{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{K}^{\circ}_{\theta}
whose capillary
k
-th

-area measure is prescribed on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
.

The capillary
L_{p}
Christoffel-Minkowski problem:

Let
1\leq k\leq n
. Given a positive function

, does there exist a capillary convex body
\widehat{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{K}^{\circ}_{\theta}
such that

\displaystyle dS_{\widehat{\Sigma},k,p}^{c}=f\ell^{p}d\sigma\penalty\ ?

From
[
41
, Proposition 2.6]
, there exists a one-to-one correspondence between a strictly convex capillary hypersurface and a strictly convex function

satisfying the Robin boundary condition (
2.3
).
By applying arguments similar to those in

, the capillary

More precisely, this corresponds to solving the following Hessian equation with Robin boundary value condition:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\vskip 2.84526ptH_{n-k}(\nabla^{2}h+h\,\sigma)&=&f\,h^{\,p-1},&\quad\text{in }\mathcal{C}_{\theta},\\
\nabla_{\mu}h&=&\cot\theta\,h,&\quad\text{on }\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}.\end{array}\right.

In this section, we establish a priori estimates for any solution to Eq. (
1
), and we state the main theorem as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
0\leq l<k\leq n
. For any positive smooth function
f
on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, the following results hold :

(1)

If

and

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h\geq c,

and for any

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|h\|_{C^{3,\alpha}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})}\leq C,

where the constants

depend only on

and

.

(2)

If
1<p<k-l+1
and

, or if
p=1
and
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, suppose that

) and (

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{h}\|_{C^{3,\alpha}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})}\leq C^{\prime},

\displaystyle F(W)\coloneqq\left(\frac{\sigma_{k}(W)}{\sigma_{l}(W)}\right)^{\frac{1}{k-l}}=f^{\frac{1}{k-l}}h^{\frac{p-1}{k-l}}=\widehat{f}h^{a},

Lemma 3.4

\displaystyle\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{u^{2}}(\xi_{0})>N.

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}(A)\coloneqq\left(\frac{\sigma_{k}(\widehat{A})}{\sigma_{l}(\widehat{A})}\right)^{\frac{1}{k-l}}=f^{\frac{1}{k-l}}(\ell u)^{\frac{p-1}{k-l}}\coloneqq u^{a}g

around
\xi_{0}

is diagonal at
\xi_{0}

(3.50)

\displaystyle 0=\nabla_{i}\log P=\frac{2u_{s}u_{si}}{|\nabla u|^{2}}-\frac{\gamma u_{i}}{u},

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}^{ij}(\log P)_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}^{ij}\left(\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}_{ij}}{|\nabla u|^{2}}-\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}_{i}|\nabla u|^{2}_{i}}{|\nabla u|^{4}}-\frac{\gamma u_{ij}}{u}+\frac{\gamma u_{i}u_{j}}{u^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{2\widetilde{F}^{ij}u_{s}u_{sij}+2\widetilde{F}^{ij}u_{si}u_{sj}}{|\nabla u|^{2}}-\frac{\gamma\widetilde{F}^{ij}u_{ij}}{u}+(\gamma-\gamma^{2})\frac{\widetilde{F}^{ij}u_{i}u_{j}}{u^{2}}.

Taking the first derivatives of Eq. (

When

[

and Mei–Wang–Weng
[

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
0\leq l<k\leq n-1
and
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})

and

(4.1)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f^{-\frac{1}{p+k-l-1}}+f^{-\frac{1}{p+k-l-1}}\sigma\geq 0,\quad{\text{in}}\quad\mathcal{C}_{\theta},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}f+\cot\theta(p+k-l-1)f\geq 0,\quad{\text{on}}\quad\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}.

Let

be a positive admissible solution to Eq. (
1

W
that is positive semi-definite, then we have
W
is positive definite, i.e.,

is strictly convex.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction and assume that there exists a positive integer

\sigma_{r}(W)>0

for all
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, and for some point

(4.3)

\displaystyle\phi(W)(\xi_{0})\coloneqq\sigma_{r+1}(W)(\xi_{0})=0.

Next, according to the position of
\xi_{0}

Case 1

[

and condition (

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{1}^{p}fd\sigma\right)^{\frac{k}{k+1}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{2}^{p}fd\sigma\right)^{\frac{1}{k+1}}.

Using the Hölder inequality, we derive that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{2}h_{1}^{p-1}fd\sigma\leq\left(\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{2}^{p}fd\sigma\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{1}^{p}fd\sigma\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}.

Together with (
5.13
) and the fact that
1<p<k+1
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{1}^{p}fd\sigma\leq\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{2}^{p}fd\sigma.

A Similar argument yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{2}^{p}fd\sigma\leq\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{1}^{p}fd\sigma.

Then the equality in (
5.13
) holds. By
[
41
, Theorem 1.1]
, we obtain

h_{1}=ah_{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\langle\cdot,E_{i}\rangle,\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ in }\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathcal{C}_{\theta}

for some constants
a,a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,\cdots,n
. Combining with Eq. (
5.12
), we deduce that
h_{1}\equiv h_{2}
.

∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
0\leq l<k\leq n
and
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Suppose that
p>k-l+1
and
h
is a positive admissible solution to Eq. (
1
), the corresponding linearized operator is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{h}z\coloneqq F^{ij}(z_{ij}+z\delta_{ij})-\frac{p-1}{k-l}h^{a-1}\widehat{f}z,\quad z\in\mathcal{A}^{4,\gamma},

where
\mathcal{A}^{4,\gamma}\coloneqq\{z\in C^{4,\gamma}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta}):\nabla_{\mu}z=\cot\theta z\}
. Then the operator
\mathcal{L}_{h}
is invertible.

Proof.

For any function
z\in\mathcal{A}^{4,\gamma}
that satisfies
\mathcal{L}_{h}z=0
, we prove that
z\equiv 0
. Consider the function

\displaystyle\tilde{z}\coloneqq\frac{z}{h},

we can check that
\nabla_{\mu}\tilde{z}=0
on
\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
and

(5.14)

\displaystyle 0=\mathcal{L}_{h}z=\left(1-\frac{k-l}{p-1}\right)h^{a}\widehat{f}\tilde{z}+hF^{ij}\tilde{z}_{ij}+2F^{ij}\tilde{z}_{i}h_{j}.

Suppose that
\widetilde{z}
attains its maximum value at some
\xi_{0}
, at
\xi_{0}
, we have

(5.15)

\displaystyle\nabla\tilde{z}=0,\quad{\rm{and}}\quad\nabla^{2}\tilde{z}\leq 0.

Combining (
5.14
) and (
5.15
), we derive

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{p-1}{k-l}\right)h^{a}\widehat{f}\tilde{z}\geq 0,

which implies
\max\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\tilde{z}=0
. Similarly, we can deduce that
\min\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\tilde{z}=0
. Hence, we conclude that
z\equiv 0
on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
.
∎

With the preceding preparatory work completed, we now proceed to complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

According to the range of
p
, we divide the proof into the following two cases.

Case 1

p>k-l+1
. We use the method of continuity to establish the existence part.
Consider the following one-parameter family of equations

(5.16)

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k}(W)}{\sigma_{l}(W)}=h^{p-1}f_{t},

where

(5.17)

\displaystyle f_{t}\coloneqq\left[(1-t)\left(\frac{\binom{n}{l}}{\binom{n}{k}}\ell^{p-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{p+k-l-1}}+tf^{-\frac{1}{p+k-l-1}}\right]^{-(p+k-l-1)}.

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\{t|0\leq t\leq 1,\text{and}\penalty\ \eqref{t-equ}\penalty\ \text{has\penalty\ a\penalty\ positive},\text{admissible\penalty\ solution}\penalty\ h_{t}\in\mathcal{A}^{4,\gamma}\}.

Since
h=\ell
is a positive, admissible solution to Eq. (
5.16
) with
f_{0}=\frac{\binom{n}{k}}{\binom{n}{l}}\ell^{1-p}
,
\mathcal{I}
is a non-empty set. The openness of
\mathcal{I}
follows from Lemma
5.5
and the implicit function theorem. The closeness follows from Theorem
3.1
. Therefore, we conclude that
\mathcal{I}=[0,1]
and thus obtain the existence of an admissible solution to Eq. (
1
). The uniqueness follows from Theorem
5.1
.

Next, we prove the strict convexity of the admissible solution under the assumption that
f
satisfies condition (
1.1
). Denote
\tilde{\lambda}=\frac{p-1}{p+k-l-1}
, when
p>1
, we have

\tilde{\lambda}\in(0,1)
. In the following, we prove

(5.18)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\ell^{\tilde{\lambda}}+\ell^{\tilde{\lambda}}\sigma>0,\quad\forall\xi\in\mathcal{C}_{\theta}.

Fixed a point
\xi\in\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, we can choose an orthonormal tangential frame
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
around
\xi
, such that at
\xi
,
e_{i}\in T_{\xi}M(1\leq i\leq n-1)
is the tangential vector of level set
M\coloneqq\{\eta\in\mathcal{C}_{\theta}:\ell(\eta)=\ell(\xi)\}
,
e_{n}
is the unit outward co-normal of
M\subset\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, then we have
\ell_{i}(\xi)=0,
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
, and
\ell_{n}=\cos\theta\langle e_{n},e\rangle\coloneqq\cos\theta\sin\widehat{\theta}
, which implies

(5.19)

\displaystyle\ell(\xi)=\sin^{2}\theta+\cos\theta\langle\xi,e\rangle=1-\cos\theta\cos\widehat{\theta}\geq\sin^{2}\widehat{\theta}.

Combining (
5.19
), we obtain

\displaystyle(\ell^{\tilde{\lambda}})_{ij}+\ell^{\tilde{\lambda}}\delta_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{\lambda}\ell^{\tilde{\lambda}-1}+(1-\tilde{\lambda})\ell^{\tilde{\lambda}}\right)\delta_{ij}+\tilde{\lambda}(\tilde{\lambda}-1)\ell^{\tilde{\lambda}-2}\ell_{i}\ell_{j}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}\ell^{\tilde{\lambda}-2}\left(\ell+(\tilde{\lambda}-1))\ell_{i}\ell_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}\ell^{\tilde{\lambda}-2}\left(\sin^{2}\widehat{\theta}+(\tilde{\lambda}-1)\cos^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\widehat{\theta}\right)\delta_{ij}>0,

we conclude that (
5.18
) holds, and it implies that
f_{t}
satisfies condition (
4.1
). On the other hand, we can check that
f_{t}
satisfies the boundary condition (
4.2
).

When
t=0
,
h=\ell
is a strictly convex solution to Eq. (
5.16
). Suppose that there exists some
t_{0}\in(0,1]
such that
h_{t}
is strictly convex for
0\leq t<t_{0}
, and
h_{t_{0}}
is not strictly convex. However, by Theorem
4.1
, we know that
h_{t_{0}}
must be strictly convex. This leads to a contradiction. Therefore, no such
t_{0}\in[0,1]
exists, and we conclude that the solution
h=h_{1}
is strictly convex.

Case 2

p=k-l+1
.
We adapt the approximation technique in
[
18
, Section 3]
and
[
24
, Section 4]
to complete the proof.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, from
Case 1
, there exists a unique positive, smooth and admissible function
h_{\varepsilon}
satisfying the following equation

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k}(W)}{\sigma_{l}(W)}=h^{k-l+\varepsilon}f.

Set
\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{h_{\varepsilon}}{\min\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{\varepsilon}}
, then
\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}
satisfies the following equation

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k}(W)}{\sigma_{l}(W)}=f(\min\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{\varepsilon})^{\varepsilon}h^{k-l+\varepsilon}.

By Theorem
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\|\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{4,\alpha}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})}\leq C,

where the constant
C
depends on
n,k,l,f
, but is independent of
\varepsilon
. Hence, there exists a subsequence
\{\varepsilon_{j}\}
with
\varepsilon_{j}\rightarrow 0
as
j\rightarrow\infty
, such that
\tilde{h}_{\varepsilon_{j}}\rightarrow\tilde{h}
in
C^{4}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
. Lemma
3.2
implies
(\min\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}h_{\varepsilon_{j}})^{\varepsilon_{j}}\rightarrow\tau
for some positive constant
\tau
satisfying

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\cos\theta)^{p+l-k-1}}{\max\limits_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}f\cdot(\sin\theta)^{2(p-1)}}\leq\tau\leq\frac{(\sin\theta)^{2(p+l-k-1)}}{\min\limits_{C_{\theta}}f\cdot(1-\cos\theta)^{p-1}},

Therefore,
\tilde{h}
is a positive, smooth and admissible function satisfying

(5.20)

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k}(W)}{\sigma_{l}(W)}=\tau fh^{k-l},\quad\text{on}\penalty\ \mathcal{C}_{\theta}.

When
k\leq n-1
and
f^{-\frac{1}{2(k-l)}}
is convex, we can construct a sequence of function
\{f_{s}\}_{s\in(0,1)}
that converges to
f
in
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
as
s\rightarrow 1
. Moreover, for each
s\in(0,1)
,
f_{s}^{-\frac{1}{2(k-l)}}
is strictly convex and
f_{s}
satisfies the boundary condition in (
1.1
). In fact, we can choose

\displaystyle f_{s}\coloneqq\left[(1-s)\left(\frac{\binom{n}{l}}{\binom{n}{k}}\ell^{k-l}\right)^{\frac{1}{2(k-l)}}+sf^{-\frac{1}{2(k-l)}}\right]^{-2(k-l)}.

For each
f_{s}
, the above argument implies there exists a pair
(h_{s},\tau_{s})
satisfies the following equation

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k}(W)}{\sigma_{l}(W)}=\tau_{s}f_{s}h^{k-l}.

Using Theorem
3.1
again, we conclude that there exists a subsequence of
\{s_{i}\}
, such that
\tau_{s_{i}}\rightarrow\tau
and
h_{s_{i}}\rightarrow h
in
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
as
s_{i}\rightarrow 1
. The pair
(\tau,h)
satisfies Eq. (
5.20
).

The strict convexity of the solution follows from Theorem
4.1
. As for the uniqueness of the positive constant
\tau
and the strictly convex solution
h
(up to a dilation) to Eq. (
2
), one can follow a similar argument as outlined in
[
31
, Section 5]
, we omit it here for conciseness. Hence, we complete the proof.
∎

5.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this subsection, we proceed to the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
When
1<p<k+1-l
, within the class of capillary even functions, we are unable to prove that the kernel of the corresponding linearization operator of Eq. (
1
) is trivial for
t\in(0,1]
. However, we can prove that the kernel of the linearized operator to Eq. (
5.16
) at
t=0,h=\ell
is trivial. We will use the degree method to establish the existence part of Theorem
1.2
. Before proceeding, we introduce some notations.

For
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and integer
l\geq 0
, for any
\xi\in\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, we set
\widehat{\xi}\coloneqq(-\xi_{1},\cdots,-\xi_{n},\xi_{n+1})
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{l,\epsilon}\coloneqq\left\{h\in C^{l,\epsilon}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta}):h(\xi)=h(\widehat{\xi}),\penalty\ \forall\xi\in\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\penalty\ {\text{and}}\penalty\ \nabla_{\mu}h=\cot\theta h,\penalty\ {\text{on}}\penalty\ \partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\right\}.

Now define a subset of
\mathcal{B}^{l,\epsilon}
given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\coloneqq\left\{h\in\mathcal{B}^{l+2,\epsilon}:\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma>0,\penalty\ {\rm{and}}\penalty\ \|h\|_{C^{l+2,\epsilon}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})}<C\right\},

where
C
is a uniform positive constant to be determined later.

Consider the map
G(\cdot,t):\mathcal{B}^{l+2,\epsilon}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}^{l,\epsilon}
given by

\displaystyle G(h,t)\coloneqq\frac{\sigma_{k}(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)}{\sigma_{l}(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)}-h^{p-1}f_{t},

where
f_{t}
is given by (
5.17
). From (
5.18
), for any
0\leq t\leq 1
, we see that
f_{t}
satisfies condition (
4.1
) and (
4.2
).

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
p\in[1,k-l+1)
and
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. The kernel of the linearized operator for the equation
G(h,0)=0
at
h=\ell
is trivial in
\mathcal{B}^{l+2,\epsilon}
.

Proof.

The linearized operator of
G
at
h_{0}=\ell
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\varphi=a_{0}\left[(\Delta\varphi+n\varphi)-(p-1)b_{0}\ell^{-1}\varphi\right],\quad\text{for\penalty\ any}\penalty\ \varphi\in\mathcal{B}^{l+2,\epsilon},

where
a_{0},b_{0}
is a positive constant and
b_{0}>1
. If
\varphi\in\text{Ker}(\mathcal{L})\cap\mathcal{B}^{l+2,\epsilon}
, which implies

(5.21)

\displaystyle\Delta\varphi+n\varphi-(p-1)b_{0}\ell^{-1}\varphi=0.

Multiplying both sides of (
5.21
) by
\ell
and integrating over
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\left[n-(p-1)b_{0}\right]\varphi d\sigma=0.

Since
p<k+1-l
, we further get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\varphi d\sigma=0.

By taking
f=\varphi
and
f_{1}=\cdots=f_{n}=\ell
in
[
41
, Theorem 3.2]
, it follows that

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\varphi d\sigma\right)^{2}\geq\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\varphi(\Delta\varphi+n\varphi)d\sigma\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\ell d\sigma,

which implies

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\varphi(\Delta\varphi+n\varphi)d\sigma=0.

On the other hand, multiplying (
5.21
) by
\varphi
and integrating over
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, we obtain

(5.22)

\displaystyle(p-1)b_{0}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\ell^{-1}\varphi^{2}d\sigma=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}(\Delta\varphi+n\varphi)\varphi d\sigma=0.

When
p>1
, from (
5.22
), we conclude that
\varphi\equiv 0
on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
. When
p=1
, the corresponding linearized operator
\mathcal{L}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\varphi=a_{0}(\Delta\varphi+n\varphi),

following the same argument in
[
44
, Section 4]
, we can also deduce that
\varphi\equiv 0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

First, we
Claim
that if
C
is sufficiently large, then
G(h,t)=0
has no solution on the boundary of
\mathcal{B}
. If not, there exists a function
h\in\partial\mathcal{B}
, i.e.,
\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma\geq 0
or
\|h\|_{C^{4,\epsilon}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})}=C
, satisfying

(5.23)

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k}(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)}{\sigma_{l}(\nabla^{2}h+h\sigma)}=h^{p-1}f_{t}.

By Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
4.1
, this reaches a contradiction. Thus, the
Claim
is true. Then by
[
34
, Proposition 2.2]
, we have

(5.24)

\displaystyle\text{deg}\left(G(\cdot,0),\mathcal{B},0\right)=\text{deg}(G(\cdot,1),\mathcal{B},0).

From
[
13
, Theorem 1.6]
, we know that
h_{0}=\ell
is the unique capillary even solution to
G(h,0)=0
for
p\geq 1
. By Lemma
5.6
, we see the linearized operator of
G(h,0)=0
at
h=\ell
is invertible, it follows that (cf.
[
34
, Proposition 2.3, Proposition 2.4]
and
[
33
, Theorem 1.1]
),

(5.25)

\displaystyle\text{deg}(G(\cdot,0),\mathcal{B},0)=\text{deg}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{B},0)=\pm 1.

Combining (
5.24
) and (
5.25
), we conclude that
\text{deg}(G(\cdot,1),\mathcal{B},0)\neq 0
, which implies Eq.
G(\cdot,1)=0
(i.e., Eq. (
5.23
) at
t=1
) has at least one solution in
\mathcal{B}
and when

, the uniqueness follows from Theorem
5.4
. We complete the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
