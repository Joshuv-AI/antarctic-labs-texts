A cross-sectional study of nutrition policy, food quality and hygiene in Pune city school canteens: a call for policy reinforcement and quality improvement
Authors: Hema Matela, Surabhi Singh Yadav, Akhila Nair, Purva Sharma, Kavitha Menon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe school food environment plays a significant role in children’s nutritional well-being. The widespread accessibility and availability of unhealthy foods in school canteens demand urgent attention. The study aimed to evaluate the school nutrition policies implemented in private secondary schools in Pune city, to examine the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of food handlers in ensuring the delivery of safe and nutritious meals to schoolchildren, and to compare the availability of food high in fat, salt, and sugar (HFSS) in school canteens across various cities in India.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted to collect data from 32 private secondary schools in Pune city regarding the implementation of any school nutrition policy and types of food offered through school canteens. All available food handlers (n = 64) were recruited for the study.ResultsOnly 42% of the food handlers were aware of Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) guidelines; about 54.7% were able to discern nutritious foods from the canteen menus and half the school canteens had poor hygiene status. Most of the food handlers lacked formal training in food safety. Approximately 40.6% of schools partially adhered to FSSAI nutrition guidelines; none implemented a nutrition and health-promoting curriculum and 18.75% of schools featured menus with ≥ 80% healthy foods. The prevalence of high fat, sugar, and salt foods in the school canteens aligned with findings from other Indian cities.ConclusionThe study findings underscore training of food handlers, compliance with food safety and nutrition guidelines, regulatory supervision, and community engagement to improve the nutritional quality and food safety in school canteens.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25666-w.Keywords: HFSS, School canteen policy, Food safety and hygiene, Food handlers, Nutrition policy

Background

Individuals under 18 years constitute fifty percent of the South Asian population. South Asia, with a population of 350 million adolescents, is the largest adolescent demographic internationally [1]. Worldwide, childhood obesity is rising alarmingly and is one of the foremost public health challenges of the twenty-first century, attaining epidemic levels [2]. Recently, the necessity for prompt measures to curb the obesity rates in the South Asian region has been emphasized [3]. Further, in India, 4.8% of adolescents aged 10 and 19 are classified as overweight [4]. Nonetheless, the rising obesity trends are evident in countries such as India, where undernutrition was previously the predominant nutritional problem among children [5]. The nutrition transition significantly contributes to obesity, as traditional diets are supplanted by highly processed and calorie-dense foods [6].

Adolescent dietary practices are significant determinants of health consequences throughout adulthood [7]. Schools play a significant role in promoting children’s health by offering nutritious food options in school canteens, which are integral to the overall school food environment. Thus, School canteens play a vital role in ensuring the nutritional well-being of schoolchildren. However, evidence indicates a preponderance of fried foods, unhealthy snacks, and fast and junk foods on the menus of South Asian school canteens [8]. Several studies demonstrated the association between excessive consumption of high-fat, salt, and sugar (HFSS) foods and the prevalence of overweight and obesity in schoolchildren [9, 10]. Childhood obesity predominantly persists into adulthood and leads to premature onset of non-communicable diseases such as diabetes and cardiovascular diseases. Therefore, the prevention of childhood obesity requires a high priority [11].

The school food environment, both within and outside, shapes the eating behaviour of schoolchildren [12]. Hence, school food environments are promoted as a key intervention setting to improve diet quality. The World Health Organization has extensively advocated for school nutrition policies that limit the accessibility of unhealthy foods and beverages [13, 14]. Existing evidence favours the implementation of school-based policies to reduce the availability of HFSS foods [15], enhance the nutritional quality of foods and beverages available at school [16], thus improving the nutritional outcomes of the school children (i.e., children’s BMI and BAZ) [17]. The scope (single component or multi-component) of the school nutrition policy varies across the globe, from the mere implementation of nutrition awareness initiatives to the formal incorporation of nutrition education into the school curriculum, and from restricting unhealthy foods in the school canteens to making provision for healthy food choices.

In light of the broader consequences of children’s unhealthy eating practices, globally extensive promotion of the Nutrition Friendly School Initiative (NFSI) was advocated. At the same time the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) established Indian guidelines in 2015 [18]. The FSSAI guidelines underscore three key domains, i.e., food safety, promotion of healthy foods, and restriction on food marketing and advertising within and adjacent to school premises. Additionally, they delineate five key components: a formal written school nutrition policy; nutrition awareness; and capacity building within the school community; nutrition and healthpromoting school curriculum, a supportive school environment; and supportive school nutrition and health services [18, 19]. All Food Business Operators (FBOs) in schools are required to register under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, and maintain hygiene and sanitation standards in accordance with the Act [20]. Additionally, the FSSAI guidelines recommended serving safe and nutritious foods in accordance with the established color coding (red, yellow, and green categories) and the reformulation of the HFSS foods to improve their nutritional content. Thus, the implementation, consistent monitoring, and evaluation of the school food policies in India would promote a nutritious school food environment, foster healthy eating behaviours among schoolchildren, and eventually improve the physical growth and academic performance [21, 22]. Regardless of the nature of the policy (mandatory or voluntary), the implementation responsibility lies with the school. Many studies in India suggest inadequate implementation of the school food policies [23, 24]. In the given scenario, the present study aimed to examine the existing school nutrition policies in the private secondary schools under Pune Municipal Corporation (PMC), Pune city to understand the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of the food handlers regarding the provision of safe and nutritious foods for school children.

Methods

Study design, study setting, and population

The present cross-sectional study was conducted in private secondary schools under Pune Municipal Corporation (PMC) in Pune City, Maharashtra, India. The data was collected between February 2020 and April 2020. As per the inclusion exclusion criteria at school level, the private secondary schools with on-campus cafeteria facilities (where mandatory daily on-site food preparation and serving occurred, along with/without the sale of commercially available pre-packaged foods and beverages) were included in the study. The government schools (as there were no canteen facilities available), private primary schools, and private secondary schools under Pimpri Chinchwad Municipal Corporation (PCMC) were excluded, as per the study objectives. At participants level, the study participants were the school canteen staff (canteen owners, cooks, food handlers, and assistants) who were engaged in food preparation or service. All food handlers who were available and provided consent to participate in the study were included in the study. The participants were selected based on the inclusion criteria, i.e., the participant must have worked in the school canteen for at least one month, be willing to provide information, and have been involved with food preparation, service, and handling. The participants unwilling to provide the requisite study information and who declined to give written informed consent were excluded from the study.

Sample size

The school was considered a fundamental unit for sample size computation. Sample size estimation was performed based on the reported prevalence (86%) of unhealthy snack availability in the school canteen in the adjacent city of Pune (Mumbai) [25]. A detailed sample size estimation for a finite population is presented (Supplementary file 1). Based on the sample size estimation, a total of 117 schools was required to achieve the 95% confidence interval and the power of 0.8, or 80%. In addition, the sample size was adjusted for a 20% non-response rate, based on our previous experience, taking the total to 140 schools. In an attempt to achieve the sample size of 117 at the minimum, we approached 138 for conducting the study. However, further enrolment was curtailed with the lockdown of schools due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Continuing the study post-covid could bring bias to the estimates, so we closed the recruitment. During a short timeframe of 3 months, we collected information from 32 schools. More schools could have been approached till we met the sample size of 117, however, this was not feasible in the COVID-19 scenario.

Schools were randomly selected from the list of 316 private secondary schools in the PMC area. A total 138 schools were contacted, of which eleven schools were recognized as duplicates due to their implementation of the double shift system; five schools were either permanently closed or non-functional; sixty-eight schools were devoid of canteen facilities; seventeen declined to participate in the study; and five schools did not respond to the request for the invitation to the study. Consequently, data was collected from 32 private secondary schools in PMC area. In tandem, all available food handlers from these schools (n = 64), directly engaged in food preparation and serving were enrolled and interviewed for the study, and all of them consented to be a part of the study (Fig. 1).

Data collection tools

The data collection tools comprised a questionnaire (Supplementary file 2) to elicit information from food handlers on age, gender, working experience, and trainings attended related to safe and hygienic food handling conducted by FSSAI.

A structured pre-tested questionnaire developed based on the FSSAI guidelines for schools (interviewer-administered) was used to collect data on food handlers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding the quality of foods served in the school canteens along with food sanitation, hygiene, and safety guidelines. Data was collected from food handlers directly engaged in the food service department, such as canteen proprietors, food handlers, and assistants. Knowledge-related questions included awareness regarding FSSAI guidelines and the ability to distinguish between nutritious and non-nutritious foods available in the school canteens. Attitude-related questions included the necessity for government policies to ensure the nutrition and safety of canteen foods, the availability of nutritious foods in school canteens, the rationale for the presence or absence of nutritious foods, food handlers’ perceptions regarding foods served in the school canteens, and the willingness to substitute unhealthy foods with healthier alternatives. Practice-related questions included verification of the product expiry dates, the duration of food service post-preparation, information on reheating of foods served in school canteens, the storage practices for beverages, the materials used for food service in the canteens, and the management of leftovers within the school canteens. The scores were derived by summing up the correct answers and the categories based on the percent of correct responses.

A 10-point scorecard was developed to evaluate the food safety and hygiene conditions of the school canteen, adhering to the FSSAI’s guidelines on food safety, hygiene, and sanitation for food available in school canteens [18]. The checklist evaluated multiple factors, including safe handling of cooked foods, personal cleanliness, health status of food handlers, hygiene, and the maintenance of food preparation and service facilities (Supplementary file 2). The hygiene scores from the scorecard were aggregated based on the responses obtained from the food handlers, and FSSAI food safety and hygiene ratings were computed. Based on the percentage of scores, the facilities were categorized as very good (81%−100%), good (61%−80%), fair (41%−60%), needs improvement (21%−40%), and needing urgent improvement (≤ 20%) [8].

Additionally, a canteen-foods questionnaire was used to collect information regarding types of food offered in the school canteens, the best-selling food items (sales data reported by the food handlers), and the rationale for food preferences amongst school children. The total number of food items on the school menu card was divided by the number of healthy food items on the school canteen menu to determine the percentage share of healthy foods. Furthermore, the presence of any nutrition guidelines and information on the implementation status of key components of the FSSAI guidelines was gathered [18].

Post data collection, the FSSAI classification was used to categorize food items as healthy and unhealthy (HFSS) [18]. The nutritional quality of foods served in school canteens was assessed using HFSS cut-offs given by the FSSAI (high fat: > 17.5 g of fat per 100 g, low fat: ≤ 3 g of fat per 100 g, high sugar: > 22.5 g of total sugars per 100 g, low sugar: ≤ 5 g of total sugars per 100 g, high salt: > 1.5 g of salt per 100 g or 0.6 g sodium, low salt: ≤ 0.3 g of salt per 100 g or 0.1 g sodium). The fat, salt, and sugar levels of non-packaged canteen-prepared food items were estimated using standardized recipes provided by the food handlers, or, in cases of recipe modifications, the actual recipes were consulted. The nutritional information on the labels of packaged items was utilized.

Analysis

The acquired data was entered into a Microsoft EXCEL worksheet, cleaned and validated prior to analysis. The data analysis was performed using STATA 14 software. Descriptive statistics (frequency and percentage) were used to summarize the data regarding age, gender, and experience of food handlers, trainings, types of foods available in the school canteens; and other categorical variables. The knowledge, attitudes, and practice-related variables; data on the execution of nutrition guidelines in the schools; and sanitation and hygiene were presented as frequencies and percentages. Fisher's exact test was performed to explore the association between awareness of FSSAI guidelines and key KAP variables. Similarly, the status of any nutrition policy implementation and its adopted components was quantified as percentages.

Ethics approval

The research proposal was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Symbiosis International (Deemed University), (Letter No: SIU/IEC/66). All participants provided written informed consent prior to their enrollment in the study.

Results

Demographic information and characteristics of study participants

The mean ± SD age of the food handlers was 36.9 ± 9.7 years. Most of the food handlers (71.9%) were aged between 31 and 40 years, while the rest were 30 years or less (28.1%). Food handlers were predominantly male (64.1%); about three-fourths had no formal training on food service, safety, sanitation, and hygiene, and 37.5% of food handlers had > 10 years of working experience in school canteens (Table 1).

Food handler’s KAP regarding nutrition guidelines and delivering healthy and safe foods through school canteens

Attitude- and perception-related parameters were analyzed (Table 2). Seventy-three percent of the food handlers believed that a mandatory government policy should be implemented to guarantee the safety and nutritional quality of foods served in the school canteen (Table 2). Also, the majority of the food handlers (n = 42, 65.6%) expressed that schools should provide nutritious meals via school canteens. Among the food handlers advocating for nutritious foods in the school canteens (n = 42, 65.6%), half (52.4%) reported that this would inculcate good eating habits in children, and 31.0% mentioned that schools/school canteens are responsible for the student’s health. Similarly, those food handlers who reported that school canteens are not necessary to serve nutritious foods (n = 21, 32.8%) reported that only parents are responsible for inculcating healthy eating habits in children (38.1%). Also, they stated that the sale of nutritious foods in the school canteens is unprofitable (52.4%). Overall perception of food handlers indicated that a significant majority (85%) believed the foods served in the canteens of private secondary schools were nutritious. Further, about 73.4% of the food handlers were willing to replace unhealthy foods with healthy alternatives.

Further, practice-related parameters of food handlers showed that nearly all (98.4%) food items sold or ingredients bought for food preparation in the school canteens were examined for expiry dates. Most foods were served within three hours of their preparation, and half of the food handlers reported that foods were reheated before serving in the school canteens. Cold beverages such as fruit juices, sugar-sweetened beverages, and cordials were stored in the refrigerator (46.9%); half of the school canteens used a mix of disposable plastic and stainless-steel tableware items for food service, whereas 9.4% used solely newspaper and disposable plastic materials for food service. The food leftovers were used differently in the school canteens: 11% were preserved until the following day, 8% were discarded, and 23.4% were repurposed for sale on the same day. All school canteens underwent daily cleaning and implemented regular pest control measures, with 23% conducting the same monthly, 52.5% every four months, and 25% every six months.

The study investigated the implementation of any nutritional guidelines in the private secondary schools of Pune city (Table 4). The research revealed that 40.6% of the schools implemented FSSAI guidelines, and 50% established school-specific guidelines. Additionally, it was observed that certain schools adopted select components of the FSSAI guidelines. In Pune city, 62.5% of the private secondary schools had a written school nutrition policy. Similarly, 65.6% of schools participated in enhancing nutrition awareness and capacity building within the school community; 59.3% focused on creating a supportive school environment (physical activity promotion, supervising school children during lunch); and 68.8% of the schools offered supportive school nutrition and health services. Foods offered in school canteens

An analysis was conducted on the foods offered in school canteens, the most popular foods among students, the most selling food items in school canteens, and potential factors influencing children’s food preferences (Table 5). The average number of food items per canteen menu that were high-fat was 5.06 ± 2.34, high-sugar 13.85 ± 8.98, and high-salt 15.73 ± 12.79. Merely 18.8% of the school’s canteens offered ≥ 80% of the healthy food options on their menus. Regarding popular foods amongst school children, 15.6% of the food handlers reported having a fixed meal system in their canteens. Among the unhealthy food options, fast foods were the most popular among children (60.9%), followed by fried snacks (39.1%), sugar-sweetened beverages (10.9%), and desserts (4.7%). Approximately,10.9% identified healthy food items as the most popular, while 3.13% cited fruits. According to food handlers, local snacks such as vada pav, misal pav, bhaji pav, etc., constituted the highest/most selling food items (32.8%), followed by pizza (18.8%), pasta (10.9%), and burgers (3.1%). Food handlers shared that taste is the primary factor influencing school children’s food preferences (75.0%), followed by portion size (39.1%), cost effectiveness (7.8%), food attractiveness (4.69%), convenience (4.7%), which pertains to ease of consumption while on the go, and personal choice (1.6%).

Additionally, 77.78% were inclined to replace unhealthy foods with healthier options. Conversely, a smaller percentage of handlers knowledgeable of the guidelines believed that nutritious foods should be supplied in school canteens (59.26%). Regarding practice, all handlers who were informed verified that product’s expiration date prior to usage.

To summarize, the majority of the food handlers lacked formal training in food safety, sanitation, and hygiene. About 85% of the food handlers believed the foods served in the canteens of private secondary schools were nutritious; this overestimation can be attributed to poor nutrition knowledge amongst food handlers, as only half of the food handlers (54.7%) could accurately recognize the healthy/nutritional foods from their canteen menu. It highlights the need for robust nutrition training for food handlers. Half the school canteens had poor hygiene status; about 40.6% schools followed FSSAI nutrition guidelines; no school incorporated a nutrition and health-promoting curriculum. The availability of healthy food options was limited in most of the school canteens; fast foods were the most popular among school children; taste dominated the food preferences; and the prevalence of high-fat, high-sugar, and high-salt foods was consistent with the findings from the other Indian cities. Also, the awareness of FSSAI guidelines was significantly higher among food handlers who attended food handler training.

Discussion

The present study aimed to explore the existing school nutrition policies of private secondary schools in Pune City, to assess the KAP of the food handlers regarding the provision of safe and nutritious foods for the schoolchildren, to examine the hygiene status of school canteens, and to examine the existing school nutrition policies.

The current study is the first to assess the FSSAI nutrition guidelines or any school nutrition policy existing in private secondary schools and the hygiene status of the school canteens of Pune city. The study findings showed inadequate formal training in food safety, sanitation, and hygiene for food handlers; insufficient awareness of FSSAI nutrition guidelines and the concept of nutritious foods; and poor hygiene status of school canteens. Furthermore, a few private secondary schools implemented school nutrition policies; nevertheless, they missed a few components, and none integrated health and nutrition into their curriculum. School children predominantly favored fast foods, with taste being the primary factor influencing their food choices, and the prevalence of HFSS foods mirrored trends observed in other Indian cities. The findings emphasize the key areas for intervention to enhance the food environment of school canteens, with ramifications for public health and policy.

The study demonstrates a significant gap in food handlers’ knowledge of FSSAI guidelines, which leads to their incorrect perception of the healthiness of existing school canteen food and which hinders their ability to modify the menu (i.e., reformulating the recipes or replacing unhealthy items with the healthy alternatives). The school canteens are mandated to have the FSSAI license to ensure compliance with food safety standards and to assure the quality and safety of food served to the students. Additionally, FSSAI promotes nutritional guidelines, regular health, and safety training for canteen staff to serve healthy or nutritious foods in school canteens. The current study showed that food handlers had limited knowledge about healthy and unhealthy foods. Lack of training may contribute to the food handlers’ poor knowledge and practices, but they emphasized the necessity of a government policy to ensure that canteen foods are safe and nutritious. Although positive attitudes were observed among food handlers, actual practices, including the use of unsafe materials for food service and reheating foods, underscore the discrepancy between knowledge and behaviour.

Further, the poor hygiene status of half the school canteens raises concern regarding food safety and potential risk for foodborne illnesses. An Indian study had emphasized the provision of clean drinking water and safe and hygienic school canteens [24]. The earlier school canteens have been branded as the most cited source of foodborne ailments, and stressed upon the need for and benefits of food safety and personal hygiene training for food handlers in school canteens [26]. To enhance the existing scenario, it is essential to implement robust training programmes for food handlers that educate them on nutritional guidelines and food safety practices, thereby improving the food quality and service in school canteens. Previously, training for canteen managers was considered as the facilitators for the school’s nutrition policy compliance [27].

A significant finding of the study was the partial implementation of the FSSAI nutritional guidelines in the school canteens. Positively, all the schools were following at least one component of FSSAI nutritional guidelines. A previous study from India reported the absence of clear health and nutrition policies in urban schools [28]. Similarly, an Indian study from Mumbai observed that none of the schools studied followed all components of the FSSAI nutritional guidelines [29]. Empirical global studies examined school adherence to school nutrition policies and found a low level of compliance among schools [30]. Even after implementing the school food policy, only 29% of schools had a menu offering ≥ 50% of healthier foods, whereas 10% had menus offering < 25% of healthy items [31]. However, the evaluation of the reintroduction of school food standards between 2001 and 2003 (ensuring food quality, food diversity, and restrictions in HFSS foods) in the UK reported a significant impact on diet and nutrition extending school premises, benefiting students from diverse socio-economic backgrounds [32]. Similarly in Australia, the implementation of school nutrition policy significantly improved the school menu [33]. The absence of a nutrition and health promoting curriculum in all surveyed school mandates policy reforms to integrate nutrition education within school curricula. However, a lack of mandate to the schools to modify the curricula may be the major barrier to integrating the nutrition component as advised in the policy.

The global and Indian contexts indicate that the availability [34] and accessibility of HFSS foods in school canteens significantly influence the increased intake of HFSS foods during school hours [35, 36]. School canteens often provide energy-dense, nutrient-poor foods, including french fries (90.4%), pizza (79.5%), and cakes (69.2%) [30]. The prevalence of HFSS foods in school canteen menus aligns with data from other Indian cities. Comparative analyses with studies conducted in Mumbai, Delhi, and Kolkata reported similar trends, signifying a national challenge. A study in Mumbai found a consistent availability of unhealthy snacks (samosa/vada pav/pav bhaji) in school canteens [25]. Likewise, a separate study in Mumbai reported that all food items in private schools were high in fat (100%), with 46.20% containing high sugar and 7.7% with high salt [29]. In government schools, all the food items were high in sugar (100%), 71.40% had elevated salt levels, and 14.3% were classified as high fat [29]. In Delhi, all schools (100%) had food categorized as red on the menu; 33% of the schools additionally offered food items from the yellow category [23]. Furthermore, 83% of the schools included the nutritious foods (green category) in their canteen menu. Similarly, in Kolkata, unhealthy food items dominated over healthy options in canteen offerings [30]. Similarly, in Brazil, more than 60% of the foods available and purchased were found to have low nutritional value, compared to only 11% with high nutritional value [37]. The growing demand for fast food and fried snacks, mostly driven by taste and portion size, and the profitability of selling HFSS raise concern due to the rising incidence of childhood obesity and related metabolic disorders. Addressing these preferences necessitates strategic interventions beyond the policy that limits the sale of HFSS foods in school canteens, which includes effective behavioural change initiatives, inclusion of attractive healthier food options, nudging, pricing strategies, student involvement to improve awareness, and modifications in school food environments to facilitate nutritious food choices [38].

Although the current study provides insightful results, it acknowledges a few limitations. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences regarding the effect of nutrition policy implementation on the availability of HFSS food in school canteens. A smaller sample size of food handlers might seem to restrict the use of inferential statistics, subgroup analysis, and the generalizability of the study beyond the selected schools in PMC area. However, the inclusion of all accessible food handlers from the 32 school canteens enhances the validity and reliability of the findings. The incorporation of qualitative perspectives would have enriched the understanding of school children’s food preferences and the challenges the students encounter due to the limited availability of healthy food options in the school canteens. Notwithstanding the limitations, the study has highlighted the critical gaps in school nutrition policies and the necessity for programmes and policies to improve them.

Conclusion

The findings underscore the necessity for implementing comprehensive measures to enhance the nutritional quality and safety of foods served in the private secondary schools under PMC of Pune city, Maharashtra, India. Although the FSSAI guidelines exist to guarantee nutritional quality and food safety in school canteens, their effective implementation continues to face significant challenges. Training of food handlers (mandatory certification, training using skill-based modules, and performance audits on a regular basis); compliance with the FSSAI food safety and nutritional guidelines (menu vetting, implementing standard operating procedures of FSSAI, maintaining daily logs for food sourcing, preparation, and storage temperatures); and enhancing the nutritional literacy of school children (through curriculum integration, interactive nutrition education activities, and constitution of health clubs) to foster informed food choices should be emphasized. Together with the FSSAI-recommended initiatives, regulatory supervision (implementation of school-level monitoring, the FSSAI inspections, and the incident reporting system) and community involvement (parent participation, local sourcing of foods, and awareness campaigns) promote healthy eating habits among school children and safeguard their long-term health.
