Network based associations between peer rejection and adolescent depression: evidence from general population and clinical samples
Authors: Chunyan Lu, Duanwei Wang, Yu Cao, Yufei Hu, Ying Yang, Huijing Sun, Xiuling Tian, Linghua Kong, Kangcheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeer rejection is a significant risk factor for adolescent depression, but most studies have examined their associations treating them as unitary constructs. Considering their conceptional complexity, our study employs network analysis to examine the associations between different types of peer rejection and specific depressive symptoms at the dimension level and their changes from the general population to clinical samples.MethodsA total of 23,995 adolescents from general population (primary dataset, 11,577 males, age = 13.90 ± 2.19) were recruited through online advertisements. The network of peer rejection and depressive dimensions was first constructed in this sample, followed by comparisons across age- and sex-stratified subsamples. In addition, separate network analyses were performed in clinically depressed adolescents (n = 610, 155 males, age = 14.86 ± 1.96) and a subsample of high-risk adolescents from the general population (n = 7,523, 3,416 males, age = 14.53 ± 2.09), and subsequently a network comparison test was performed between them.ResultsThe edge between ignore and anhedonia exhibited the highest bridge expected influence in the primary dataset, the clinical depression and high-risk adolescent groups. Adolescents in different age and sex groups showed distinct patterns of specific edges within the dimension-level networks. Clinically depressed adolescents exhibited significantly stronger network connections than high-risk adolescents, particularly the edges between insult and interpersonal problems and between ignore and anhedonia.ConclusionsIgnore and anhedonia were crucial bridges connecting peer rejection to adolescent depression, and the connection was stronger in clinically depressed adolescents. These findings may provide a theoretical basis for guiding future research on interventions targeting peer relationships in adolescent depression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04731-y.

Introduction

Depression is a debilitating disorder and its prevalence among adolescents has risen significantly in recent years [1]. It not only impairs cognitive performance and social interactions but also elevates the risk of suicide [2, 3]. Adolescent depression is highly heterogeneous, with individuals differing in syndrome profiles and treatment responses [4, 5]. Thus, relying on a single or total score derived from self-report questionnaires may obscure our understanding on its risk factors and hinder the development of effective treatment [6]. A growing body of research has started to focus on specific symptoms and elucidate their unique risk factors [7, 8]. Given the increasing prevalence and serious negative impact of depression in adolescents, identifying risk factors are critically important for implementing early prevention strategies. Among these, negative social risk factors, particularly adverse interpersonal experiences, such as peer rejection, have been shown to significantly influence on the development of adolescent depression [9].

Adolescence is considered a key period of social development, during which individuals are more sensitive to peer rejection and more likely to experience it than adults [10, 11]. Peer rejection refers to a persistent experience of being disliked, excluded, or not accepted by peers within a social group [12]. Rejection is not always intentional, nor does it necessarily constitute bullying [13]. Lev-Wiesel et al. [14] identified four dimensions of peer rejection: physical attacks and bossiness, insult, ignore, and accusation. The effects of peer rejection vary across individuals, highlighting the importance of considering its multiple dimensions [14]. Adolescents who experience peer rejection often show adverse emotional and behavioral outcomes, including academic difficulties, reduced prosocial behavior, and lowered self-esteem [15, 16]. These difficulties, in turn, were associated with increased risk of mood disorders, especially among adolescents lacking adequate social support [17]. According to the temporal need–threat model, peer rejection threatens adolescents’ basic psychological needs, including belonging, control, and meaningful existence, and may be associated with negative outcomes such as depression, helplessness, and social alienation [18]. Empirical evidence suggests that adolescents with depressive disorder experiencing peer rejection exhibit heightened emotional reactivity and deficits in emotion regulation, increasing the likelihood of depression exacerbation or persistence [19]. Importantly, the psychological meaning and emotional impact of peer rejection may vary depending on broader motivational and normative contexts, suggesting that its associations with depression may not be uniform across individuals [20, 21]. However, most existing theoretical frameworks emphasize a relatively general effect of peer rejection on basic psychological needs and depressive outcomes. Previous research has primarily examined peer rejection as a whole concept in relation to depression in adolescent populations [22, 23]. Recent studies have begun to investigate the specific dimensions of peer rejection, particularly in clinical sample of depressed adolescents. For instance, Wang et al. included the four dimensions of peer rejection in a network analysis of various environmental risk factors and depressive domains in adolescents patients [24]. The authors found being ignored emerged as a significant dimension that associated with depressive dimensions, including anhedonia, negative self-esteem and ineffectiveness. More studies are warranted to systematically investigate the associations between specific dimensions of peer rejection and depression.

In general, prior research on specific types of peer rejection and depressive symptoms has focused on clinically depressed adolescents, relied on relatively small samples, and did not consider different adolescence stages [17, 24, 25]. Due to the inclusion of small and clinical samples, it remains largely unknown whether the identified associations could be generalized to adolescents in the general population. Such a knowledge gap would hinder the identification of high-risk individuals and obstruct the implementation of interventions before the onset or worsening of depression. Moreover, adolescence is not a unitary period; it is commonly divided into early adolescence (between 10 and 13) and older adolescence (i.e., middle to late adolescence, from 13 to 18) [26, 27]. Research has shown that individuals in older adolescence are at a higher risk for depression [28]. In addition, these adolescents exhibit stronger physiological emotional responses to emotional stimuli than early adolescents [29]. Such increased emotional reactivity may lead those at older adolescence to adopt distinct coping strategies when confronted with peer rejection, potentially affecting the severity of their emotional problems. It is therefore crucial to clarify whether the association between peer rejection and depression vary across adolescence stages. Taken together, these considerations highlight the need for studies involving a large sample of adolescents from general population to examine the association between peer rejection and depression and elucidate how the associations may vary across different adolescence stages.

Furthermore, given that mental health exists on a continuum, a critical issue arises concerning subthreshold depression - the intermediate state between full mental health and clinical depression. Adolescents presenting with depression without meeting the clinical diagnostic criteria are commonly defined as having subthreshold depression or being at high risk for depression [30, 31]. These adolescents also experience the negative impact of depressive states, which can lead to long-term adverse consequences [32], including increased health, behavioral, and academic risks [33]. Song et al. [34] found that high-risk individuals show impaired short-term responses to positive stimuli and poor maintenance of positive affect in daily life [34]. Peer rejection, as a form of negative social feedback, has been shown to undermine positive affect maintenance and emotional flexibility [35]. Currently, the relationship between specific types of peer rejection and depression within adolescents with subthreshold depression remains unclear, and the potential differences between subthreshold and clinical depression are not yet understood at the dimension level.

The present study aimed to comprehensively delineate the associations between specific types of peer rejection and individual depressive dimensions using network analysis [36, 37], with the goal of identifying specific bridge nodes and key connections linking peer rejection to depression dimensions. Network analysis applied to cross-sectional data could provide the representation of statistical associations among these dimensions, but does not permit causal or temporal inferences about dynamic changes within individuals. Bridge expected influence can be interpreted as an index of statistical connectivity across domains within the estimated network, rather than as direct evidence of causal pathways. Recent studies have demonstrated its utility in adolescent depression research, including analyses of peer related risk factors and links between childhood adversity and specific depressive symptoms [38]. Specifically, in this study, we applied network analysis to three adolescent samples stratified by graded levels of depression severity: (1) a large, population-based adolescent cohort (primary dataset); (2) adolescents with clinically diagnosed depression (dataset 2); and (3) adolescents with subthreshold depression from the primary dataset (dataset 3). We firstly estimated the primary network, identified bridge nodes and key edges, and performed network comparisons across subsamples stratified by age and sex. Then, we constructed networks within adolescents with clinical and subthreshold depression separately. These networks were subsequently compared to determine whether peer rejection - related dimension-level networks differ between subthreshold and clinical depression.

Methods

Participants

The primary dataset included a total of 23,995 adolescents from the general population (dataset 1, 11,577 males; age = 13.90 ± 2.19 years, age range = 10–18 years; see Table 1). Participants were recruited via online advertisements between June and October 2021 from seven provinces in China (Shandong, Shanxi, Tianjin, Shanxi, Henan, Gansu, and Shanghai), including students from grades 3 to 12. Of the 24,689 initially recruited respondents, questionnaires with missing data, careless or random responses, or extreme values were excluded, yielding a finial valid sample of 23,995 adolescents. Within this dataset, adolescents with a total score ≥ 13 on the Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI) were classified as having subthreshold depression (dataset 3: N = 7,523; 3,416 males; age = 14.53 ± 2.09 years; see Table S1).

The study was approved by the ethics committees of Shandong Normal University and Shandong Provincial Mental Health Center. Written or electronic informed consent was obtained from participants and/or their legal guardians prior to participation.

Measurements

Peer rejection

Peer rejection was assessed using the Social Peer Rejection (SPR) scale [14]. The scale consists of 21 items describing different forms of peer rejection and includes four dimensions: (1) physical attacks and bossiness, including vandalism of possessions; (2) insult, such as humiliation and name-calling; (3) ignore, including exclusion from social activities or being sent away from gatherings; and (4) accusation, such as being blamed for things not done or having negative rumors. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“no harm”) to 5 (“very severe harm”), with higher scores indicating a greater perceived psychological impact of peer rejection. In the present study, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α of 0.98 for the primary dataset, 0.92 for the clinically depressive dataset.

Adolescent depression

Adolescent depression were assessed using the CDI scale [41]. The CDI scale consists of 27 items covering five domains: negative self-esteem, negative mood, interpersonal problems, ineffectiveness and anhedonia. Each item provides three response options scored from 0 to 2, and participants selecting the statement that best described their feelings. Total CDI scores range from 0 to 54, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive depression. Consistent with prior research, participants with a CDI total score ≥ 13 were considered as exhibiting subthreshold depression [42, 43]. In the primary dataset, 7,523 adolescents (3,416 males; age = 14.53 ± 2.09 years, see Table S1) were identified as a high-risk group (dataset 3). In the present study, the CDI demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in the primary dataset (Cronbach’s α = 0.87) and clinically depressive dataset (Cronbach’s α = 0.88).

Statistical analysis

All analyses and visualizations were conducted using R (4.4.2). In the primary dataset, a weighted undirected network of peer rejection and adolescent depression was first constructed using Spearman partial correlations within a Gaussian Graphical Model (GGM) framework. To obtain a sparse and stable network structure, the graphical least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) combined with the extended Bayesian information criterion (EBIC) was applied to determine the optimal tuning parameter [44]. Spearman correlations were used as a rank-based approximation suitable for ordinal data and robust to non-normality. This approach is widely adopted in psychological network analysis to estimate GGM-based conditional associations from Likert-type data [45–47]. In the estimated network, each node represented a dimension of peer rejection (4 nodes) or depression (5 nodes). In cross-sectional between-person network data, the estimated edges represent group-level conditional associations among variables and can be used to describe the structure of symptom co-occurrence.

The R packages “qgraph” and “networktools” were used for network estimation, visualization and computation of centrality indices. Expected influence was used as the primary centrality measure, as it accounts for both positive and negative edge weights. To identify nodes linking peer rejection and adolescent depression clusters, bridge expected influence was calculated [48], which quantifies a node’s connectivity to nodes in other clusters by summing cross-cluster edge weights while retaining their sign. This bridge measure has been proposed as an index to identify nodes that are highly connected across communities, which may provide informative reference for understanding potential associations between different symptom clusters or disorders [49]. Higher expected influence values indicate greater importance of a node within or across clusters [50].

Network accuracy and stability were evaluated using the R package “bootnet”. Nonparametric bootstrapping (1,000 iterations) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was performed to estimate the accuracy of the edge-weights. Centrality stability was examined using the case-dropping bootstrap procedures (1,000 iterations) to evaluate the centrality stability coefficient. Values above 0.25 indicated acceptable stability, with values exceeding 0.50 considered good stability [51].

To examine age-related differences in the network structure across developmental stages, participants in the primary dataset were divided into two subgroups using the age of 13 years as the cutoff: early adolescent and older adolescent groups (Table S2). The Network Comparison Test (NCT), a permutation-based method, was used to compare the network structure, global strength, node centrality, and edge weights between the two age subgroups [50, 52]. The NCT was applied with 1,000 permutations, and multiple comparisons were performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Similarly, network comparisons were also conducted between male and female adolescents in the primary dataset.

To explore potential network differences between adolescents with subthreshold and clinical depression, the NCT framework was applied to dataset 2 and 3. We also conducted two supplementary analyses to assess the robustness of observed network differences and to evaluate the potential influence of demographic factors and sample size. First, a demographically matched subsample (N = 3,000, 762 males; age = 14.85 ± 0.80 years) was selected from the dataset 3 using stratified random sampling by sex and age to match the dataset 2. Second, a demographically and sample size matched subsample (N = 610, 155 males; age = 14.87 ± 2.02 years) was selected from the dataset 3 using same methods to match the dataset 2. The network structure invariance test and the global strength invariance test were re-evaluated for both two supplementary analyses.

Results

Sample characteristics

In the primary dataset, females scored significantly higher than males on the CDI total score, three depressive dimensions, and the SPR total score and all its subscales (Table 1). With respect to age, early adolescents scored lower on CDI total score and all five depressive dimensions. Early adolescents did not differ from older adolescents on the SPR total score, but perceived higher levels of physical attacks and bossiness and insult, and a lower level of ignore (Table S2). Compared to those with CDI < 13 in the primary dataset, the high-risk group reported markedly higher levels of perceived peer rejection across all four sub-dimensions (ps < 0.001). Clinically depressed adolescents scored significantly higher than the high-risk group on the CDI and SPR total scores, as well as all of their subscales (ps < 0.001). The SPR and CDI were positively correlated in the primary dataset (r = 0.20, p < 0.001), the high-risk group (r = 0.13, p < 0.001) and the clinical depression group (r = 0.44, p < 0.001).

Network in adolescents from the general population

Figure 1A presented the estimated network of peer rejection and adolescent depressive domains in the primary dataset. In this network, 28 of 36 possible edges (77.78%) had nonzero weights, and 5 edges (13.89%) were negative (Table S3). Within the peer rejection cluster, the strongest connection was observed between physical attacks and bossiness and insult (weight = 0.517), followed by the connection between insult and accusation (weight = 0.360). Within the depressive domains, negative mood and anhedonia were most strongly connected (weight = 0.305), followed by the connection between negative self-esteem and negative mood (weight = 0.272). Between peer rejection and depressive domains, the connection between ignore and negative self-esteem showed the largest weight among cross-cluster edges (weight = 0.045), followed by the connection between ignore and anhedonia (weight = 0.035) (Table S3). Regarding network centrality, Fig. 1B presented the centrality indices of expected influence for each node. Anhedonia was the most central node, followed by physical attacks and bossiness and insult. The most prominent bridge node was ignore, which showed the highest bridge expected influence and serves as the key bridge connecting the peer rejection and depressive domains. Anhedonia also showed relatively high bridge expected influence (Fig. 1C).

Network comparisons between early and older adolescents

Figure 2A and B displayed the estimated networks for the two age groups. Older adolescents (strength = 3.945) exhibited higher global network strength than early adolescents (strength = 3.729; test statistic S = 0.216, p = 0.001). The maximum edge weight difference test also identified the largest discrepancy in edge strength between the two networks (test statistic M = 0.054, p = 0.010, Figure S3). Older adolescents showed significantly higher network expected influence in negative self-esteem, negative mood, and anhedonia than early adolescents (Fig. 2C). The edge between insult and negative self-esteem was stronger in early than in older adolescents, while the edges between insult and anhedonia, and between physical attacks and bossiness and ineffectiveness were stronger in older adolescents than in early adolescents (Fig. 2D, Table S4). Due to the sex difference between two age groups, we performed a supplementary analysis matching sex proportion and found same network differences, indicating the robustness of the results.

Network comparisons between female and male adolescents

We compared network models between male and female participants in the primary dataset, and the estimated networks were shown in Fig. 3A and B, respectively. The Network Comparison Test revealed that the global network strength was higher in females than in males (males = 3.876, females = 3.961, test statistic S = 0.085, p = 0.002; Figure S4). The maximum edge weight difference test identified the largest discrepancy in edge strength between the two networks (test statistic M = 0.04, p = 0.020; Figure S4). Females showed significantly higher network expected influence in accusation and negative self-esteem, while males exhibited significantly higher network expected influence in interpersonal problems (Fig. 3C). The edge between negative mood and interpersonal problems was stronger in males than in females (Fig. 3D, Table S5). No difference was found for bridges connecting the peer rejection and depressive domains.

Network comparisons between adolescents with subthreshold and clinical depression

The networks for adolescents with clinical and subthreshold depression were shown in Fig. 4A and B. Clinically depressed adolescents exhibited higher global network strength than high-risk adolescents (high-risk adolescents = 2.767, clinically depressed adolescents = 4.029, test statistic S = 1.262, p = 0.001). The maximum edge weight difference test also identified the largest discrepancy in edge strength between the two networks (test statistic M = 0.328, p = 0.001; Figure S5). The high-risk adolescents showed higher expected influence in physical attacks and bossiness, interpersonal problems, and ineffectiveness, whereas the clinically depressed adolescents showed higher expected influence in negative self-esteem, negative mood, and anhedonia (Fig. 4C). The edges between insult and interpersonal problems (p = 0.031, test statistic E = 0.043), and between ignore and anhedonia (p = 0.048, test statistic E = 0.055) were significantly stronger in the clinically depressed adolescents than in the high-risk adolescents (Fig. 4D, Table S6).

Two supplementary analyses indicated same global network strength differences. Regarding edges, the connections between insult and interpersonal problems (N = 3,000, p = 0.126, test statistic E = 0.032; N = 610, p = 0.255, Test statistic E = 0.043), and between ignore and anhedonia (N = 3,000, p = 0.057, test statistic E = 0.058; N = 610, p = 0.255, test statistic E = 0.068) were still stronger in the clinically depressed adolescents than in the high-risk adolescents, although the statistical evidence was reduced and did not consistently reach significance across supplementary analyses. Overall, these findings suggested a similar direction of effects, indicating mixed and weakened statistical support for specific edge differences after matching and sample adjustment procedures.

Discussion

The present study used network analysis to reveal the associations between peer rejection and adolescent depression in adolescents at the dimension level, and explore how these associations vary across developmental stages and between clinical and nonclinical populations. A major strength of this study was its large sample size, which enhanced statistical power and improves the robustness and generalizability of the network models [53], while also allowing for examination of age-related differences in the network. In addition, our study provided novel insights into group differences in dimensional network structures between high-risk and clinically depressed adolescents, particularly in terms of specific edge connections. Across the general population, high risk adolescents and clinically depressed adolescents, ignore and anhedonia consistently served as key bridge nodes connecting peer rejection and depression, exhibiting the strongest cross domain associations. Network comparisons suggested that adolescents in different age and sex groups showed distinct patterns of specific edges within the dimension-level networks. In addition, connections among dimensions were stronger in adolescents with clinical depression than in high-risk adolescents, particularly the connections between insult and interpersonal problems and between ignore and anhedonia.

In the network of the general adolescent population, ignore and anhedonia showed relatively high bridge expected influence, indicating their potential role in connecting the peer rejection and depression clusters, which is consistent with previous studies [46–48]. For example, Luo et al. [54] identified “frequently feeling alone” as a core bridge node connecting to the negative mood system within the loneliness–rumination–depression network. Conceptually, ignore is closely related to “frequently feeling alone”, as both reflect disruptions in social connectedness [55]. Unlike active forms of peer rejection, such as insult and accusation, ignore is characterized by its passivity, subtlety, and persistent nature. This covert form of peer rejection may erode the quality of peer relationships, increase feelings of loneliness, and further exacerbate depression [56]. Anhedonia manifests as a reduced capacity to experience pleasure, decreased motivation, and reduced anticipation of positive future experiences. The association between ignore and anhedonia suggests that being ignored by peers may be related to depressive processes involving reduced sensitivity to positive experiences. Converging longitudinal neuroimaging studies support the finding, showing that adolescents exposed to social stressors, such as being ignored, exhibit reduced reactivity in reward-related brain regions, particularly the ventral striatum, which is closely associated with increases in anhedonia [57]. In addition, our study identified a relatively prominent association between ignore and negative self-esteem. As a form of relational rejection, being ignored directly threatens adolescents’ sense of belonging and self-worth [58], making them more susceptible to negative moods and exacerbating depressive experiences [59].

Consistent with prior research [60], our findings further revealed that clinically depressed adolescents exhibited higher global network strength, indicating a denser network, than high-risk adolescents. One possible explanation is that adolescents with clinical depression are more sensitive to peer rejection, exhibit stronger aversive and avoidance responses [22], and experience more intense experiences of social pain, which may contribute to differences in overall connectivity within the network. This pattern is also consistent with the rejection sensitivity framework, which suggests that individuals who anxiously expect rejection tend to show heightened sensitivity to interpersonal cues of rejection and stronger emotional and behavioral reactivity in social contexts [61]. Among clinically depressed adolescents, the association between insult and interpersonal problems, as well as the connection between ignore and anhedonia, is stronger than in high-risk nonclinical adolescents. Depression severity and social dysfunction in high-risk adolescents fall between those of healthy adolescents and adolescents with clinical depression [30, 62]. When experiencing insults or being ignored, high-risk adolescents may experience transient negative affect; however, they have not yet generalized these experiences into a pervasive sense of interpersonal failure. Consequently, our results suggested that these findings may provide hypothesis-generating information for future prevention research in high-risk adolescents of depression.

This study revealed that the network structure in older adolescence is more densely connected than in early adolescence. As adolescence progresses, individuals develop greater self-awareness and become increasingly concerned with peer evaluation and social comparison [63, 64]. This heightened sensitivity amplifies vulnerability to failure, rejection, and negative feedback, thereby increasing negative affect and declines in self-efficacy [29, 65]. Network analyses showed that the edge between insult and negative self-esteem was stronger in early adolescence. During early adolescence, youth are in Erikson’s stage of industry versus inferiority, and experiences of verbal or nonverbal peer insults are likely to be internalized, directly undermining self-worth and self-esteem [66, 67]. In mid-to-late adolescence, the edges between insult and anhedonia, and between physical attacks and bossiness and ineffectiveness, were stronger. At this stage, maturing reward systems increase sensitivity to social feedback, so repeated insults may reduce pleasure responsiveness, leading to anhedonia [68]. Aggressive behaviors also become more socially strategic and linked to control and reduced self-efficacy, strengthening their associations with bossiness and ineffectiveness.

The current study has several limitations. First, due to its cross-sectional design and focus on the dimensional level, this study cannot establish causal relationships or temporal sequences between peer rejection and adolescent depression, and may have overlooked more fine-grained associations at the item level. Future longitudinal and item-level studies are needed to further clarify these relationships. Second, this study relied on self-report questionnaires for both peer rejection and depression, without validation from peer nominations or teacher reports. This may introduce common method bias due to participants reporting both their social experiences and symptoms themselves. Third, although the present study examined associations between peer rejection and depressive dimensions in relatively large samples of adolescents from both general population and clinical settings, it did not include other relevant cultural differences across China and other psychological factors such as rumination, loneliness, or anxiety symptoms. These unmeasured variables may contribute to shared underlying processes that influence cross-cluster associations in the network, and therefore the interpretation of specific edges should remain cautious. Future research incorporating a broader range of cognitive, emotional, and comorbid symptoms would help to better clarify the specificity of these associations. Fourth, in the comparison between the clinical and high-risk groups, there is potential circularity in defining the high-risk group, as participants were classified based on CDI cutoff scores while the same measure was also used in the network analysis. The classification further relied solely on self-report data without clinical interviews, multi-informant reports, or biological indicators, which may limit the precision of group definition. In addition, the clinical and high-risk groups were recruited from different sources, which may introduce non-equivalence in sampling context and other unmeasured confounding factors. Future research should adopt multi-method assessments to improve classification validity and better validate group differences. Finally, we acknowledge that some key edge differences were attenuated in supplementary matched analyses, suggesting that these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

Using large samples of adolescents from general population and clinical settings, this study applied network analysis to demonstrate that ignore and anhedonia served as bridge nodes associating peer rejection and adolescent depression. Their relatively close association in the network suggested that these bridge nodes may be of interest in future intervention research. Network comparisons further revealed differences in dimensional network structures across distinct adolescence stages and clinical statuses. Specifically, clinically depressed adolescents exhibited stronger network connections than high-risk adolescents, particularly the edge between ignore and anhedonia, suggesting stronger interconnections between dimensions of peer rejection and adolescent depression in the clinical group. Collectively, these findings spanning adolescents from general population and clinical samples highlight the potential relevance of central and bridge nodes for future prevention research on adolescent depression.
