Privacy-Preserving Cohort Analytics for Personalized Health Platforms: A Differentially Private Framework with Stochastic Risk Modeling
Richik Chakraborty, Lawrence Liu, Syed Hasnain

Abstract
Personalized health analytics increasingly rely on population benchmarks to provide contextual insights such as ”How do I compare to others like me?” However, cohort-based aggregation of health data introduces nontrivial privacy risks, particularly in interactive and longitudinal digital platforms. Existing privacy frameworks such as k k -anonymity and differential privacy provide essential but largely static guarantees that do not fully capture the cumulative, distributional, and tail-dominated nature of re-identification risk in deployed systems. In this work, we present a privacy-preserving cohort analytics framework that combines deterministic cohort constraints, differential privacy mechanisms, and synthetic baseline generation to enable personalized population comparisons while maintaining strong privacy protections. We further introduce a stochastic risk modeling approach that treats re-identification risk as a random variable evolving over time, enabling distributional evaluation through Monte Carlo simulation. Adapting quantitative risk measures from financial mathematics, we define Privacy Loss at Risk (P-VaR) to characterize worst-case privacy outcomes under realistic cohort dynamics and adversary assumptions. We validate our framework through system-level analysis and simulation experiments, demonstrating how privacy-utility tradeoffs can be operationalized for digital health platforms. Our results suggest that stochastic risk modeling complements formal privacy guarantees by providing interpretable, decision-relevant metrics for platform designers, regulators, and clinical informatics stakeholders.



1
Introduction

Digital health platforms increasingly seek to provide personalized insights grounded in population-level context
43
;
33
. Comparisons such as “individual versus cohort” or “percentile relative to peers” have become central to user engagement and clinical relevance across wellness, chronic disease management, and preventive care applications
7
;
45
. These features, however, require aggregating sensitive health data across groups defined by demographic, clinical, or behavioral attributes.

This creates a fundamental tension between personalization and privacy. While population benchmarks enhance interpretability and motivation
26
, they also expose latent information about cohort composition. Historical incidents, such as the unintended disclosure of sensitive locations through aggregated fitness data
18
, demonstrate that even aggregate statistics can enable re-identification when combined with auxiliary information
35
.

Unlike static data releases, modern health platforms are interactive and longitudinal. Users may issue repeated queries over time, cohorts may evolve as users join or leave, and adversaries may incrementally refine their background knowledge
12
. In such settings, privacy risk is not a fixed quantity but an evolving process shaped by system dynamics and usage patterns.

Existing privacy frameworks provide critical safeguards.
K
-anonymity
42
enforces minimum indistinguishability thresholds, while differential privacy (DP)
10
offers formal worst-case guarantees against re-identification. However, these approaches are typically evaluated in static or worst-case terms. They do not directly address how privacy risk accumulates over time, how likely extreme outcomes are under realistic conditions, or how platform designers should balance privacy and utility in practice.

1.1
Contributions

In this work, we argue that privacy risk in deployed health analytics systems should be treated as a stochastic, tail-risk–dominated quantity rather than solely as a static compliance constraint. Our contributions are:

1.

A system architecture for privacy-preserving cohort analytics that integrates deterministic safeguards, differential privacy mechanisms, and synthetic baseline generation.

2.

A stochastic risk modeling framework that quantifies re-identification risk as a distribution evolving over time.

3.

Introduction of Privacy Loss at Risk (P-VaR), a tail-risk measure adapted from quantitative finance to characterize extreme privacy outcomes.

4.

Monte Carlo simulation experiments demonstrating privacy-utility tradeoffs under realistic system dynamics.

5.

Design guidelines for health platform developers seeking to operationalize privacy risk management.

2
Related Work and Background

2.1
Privacy-Preserving Health Data Analytics

Privacy protection in health data has been extensively studied, with approaches ranging from de-identification and
k
-anonymity to cryptographic methods and differential privacy
15
;
5
.

K
-anonymity and extensions.

K
-anonymity
42
aims to ensure that each individual record is indistinguishable from at least
k-1
others along selected quasi-identifiers. Extensions such as
\ell
-diversity
30
and
t
-closeness
28
address composition attacks and attribute disclosure. While conceptually simple, these methods are vulnerable to sophisticated linkage attacks
35
and often degrade utility in high-dimensional settings
2
.

Differential privacy.
Differential privacy (DP)
10
;
12
provides a mathematically rigorous framework that bounds the influence of any single individual on released statistics. For a randomized mechanism
\mathcal{M}
,
\varepsilon
-differential privacy requires:

\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D)\in S]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D^{\prime})\in S]

(1)

for all neighboring datasets
D
and
D^{\prime}
differing in one record, and all outcome sets
S
. DP has been applied to health data analyses including count queries
17
, genomic data
19
, and clinical registries
8
. However, practical deployment remains limited due to challenges in parameter selection
27
, interpretability, and cumulative privacy loss under repeated queries
11
.

Advanced mechanisms.
Recent work has explored local differential privacy
9
, personalized privacy
20
, and privacy amplification through subsampling
3
. Federated learning approaches
22
enable collaborative model training while keeping data decentralized, though they introduce new attack surfaces
32
.

2.2
Cohort-Based Benchmarking in Health Platforms

Cohort-based benchmarking is widely used in consumer and clinical health tools
41
;
29
, yet implementation details are often opaque. Platforms like Apple Health, Google Fit, and clinical registries provide varying degrees of population comparison, but transparent descriptions of deployed cohort analytics systems with formal privacy evaluation are rare in the literature
38
.

2.3
Stochastic Privacy and Risk Modeling

The treatment of privacy as a random variable has precedent in several contexts. The Pufferfish framework
25
generalizes DP by allowing specification of secrets and discrimination pairs. Concentrated differential privacy
13
and Rényi differential privacy
34
provide tighter composition bounds by tracking privacy loss distributions. However, these frameworks focus primarily on worst-case guarantees rather than operational risk distributions.

In parallel, risk-based approaches to privacy have been explored in the context of disclosure risk assessment
40
;
31
and privacy impact assessments
46
. Our work bridges these traditions by adapting tail-risk measures from quantitative finance to characterize privacy loss distributions in deployed systems.

2.4
Limitations of Static Privacy Metrics

Both
k
-anonymity and differential privacy are typically evaluated using static metrics: minimum cohort size or a fixed privacy budget
\varepsilon
. While these guarantees are necessary, they are insufficient to characterize operational risk in interactive systems
24
. In particular, they do not describe the distribution or likelihood of extreme privacy outcomes, nor do they capture how risk evolves as cohorts change and queries accumulate.

System Overview: Privacy-Preserving Cohort Analytics

3.1
Design Goals

The proposed framework is guided by four design principles:

Personalized Utility:
Provide meaningful population context for individual users without compromising privacy.

Strong Privacy Guarantees:
Prevent exposure of individual-level data and limit re-identification risk through formal mechanisms.

Interpretability:
Ensure that privacy mechanisms and outputs are understandable to users, clinicians, and regulatory stakeholders.

Scalable Deployment:
Support low-latency serving and cost-efficient computation for millions of users.

Architecture Overview

presents the system architecture, comprising four main components:

2.

Aggregation Layer:
Computes privacy-preserving statistics over cohorts.

3.

Synthetic Baseline Generator:
Provides reference distributions for small cohorts.

4.

Serving API:
Delivers personalized benchmarks to client applications.

3.3
Cohort Assignment Engine

Users are assigned to one or more cohorts based on coarse-grained demographic and clinical attributes (e.g., age range, sex, condition category). The cohort taxonomy is designed to balance clinical relevance with privacy:

•

Age ranges:
5-year bins (e.g., 25-29, 30-34)

•

Biological sex:
Male, Female, Other/Prefer not to say

•

Condition categories:
High-level groupings (e.g., cardiovascular, metabolic) rather than specific diagnoses

•

Geographic region:
Country or large metro area only

Multiple cohort membership allows for flexible benchmarking while reducing reliance on narrowly defined groups
30
. Cohort definitions are intentionally constrained to resist auxiliary information attacks where adversaries combine platform outputs with external knowledge
35
.

3.4
Aggregation and Serving Architecture

Aggregate statistics are computed using a batch-oriented analytics layer (e.g., columnar data warehouse such as BigQuery or Snowflake) and served through a low-latency datastore (e.g., Redis or DynamoDB). Only aggregate outputs—like percentiles, means, or summary statistics—are exposed via APIs. Individual-level data are never returned to client applications.

Approximate quantile computation.
We use approximate quantile algorithms
16
to reduce sensitivity to outliers and enable efficient aggregation over large datasets. These algorithms provide
(1\pm\delta)
-approximate quantiles with sublinear space complexity.

Minimum cohort size.
Aggregates are computed only for cohorts exceeding a minimum size threshold
k_{\min}
. We evaluate
k_{\min}\in\{50,100,200\}
based on re-identification risk and clinical utility considerations.

3.5
Differential Privacy Mechanisms

We apply the Laplace mechanism
10
to aggregate outputs:

\tilde{f}(D)=f(D)+\text{Lap}\left(\frac{\Delta f}{\varepsilon}\right)

(2)

where
f(D)
is the true statistic,
\Delta f
is the global sensitivity, and
\varepsilon
is the privacy budget. For count queries,
\Delta f=1
; for percentiles, we bound contribution through winsorization or clipping
14
.

Privacy budget allocation.
We employ a hierarchical budget allocation strategy:

•

Per-cohort budget:
\varepsilon_{\text{cohort}}=0.1
per day

•

Per-user query budget:
\varepsilon_{\text{user}}=1.0
per month

•

Global platform budget:
\varepsilon_{\text{global}}=10.0
per year

Advanced composition theorems
23
are used to track cumulative privacy loss across queries.

3.6
Synthetic Baseline Generator

For cohorts that do not meet minimum size requirements, the system provides synthetic baselines derived from external research norms
6
;
44
or population-level priors. The synthetic baseline generator:

1.

Identifies the nearest cohort with sufficient size

2.

Adjusts for known demographic differences using published epidemiological data

3.

Generates a reference distribution with explicit uncertainty intervals

These baselines are explicitly labeled as synthetic and presented with visual indicators of uncertainty. Synthetic outputs are treated as reference distributions rather than precise estimates, following best practices in clinical decision support
39
.

4
Privacy Guarantees and Threat Model

4.1
Threat Model

We consider an external adversary with the following capabilities:

Access to released statistics:
The adversary can query the platform API and observe aggregate outputs over time.

Bounded auxiliary information:
The adversary possesses background knowledge about some individuals (e.g., approximate age, location, health conditions) but not complete records.

Computational resources:
The adversary can perform inference attacks and probabilistic reasoning but is computationally bounded (no breaking of cryptographic primitives).

Honest-but-curious platform:
The platform is assumed to correctly implement declared privacy mechanisms but may be subject to inference attacks. We do not consider insider abuse, side-channel attacks, or compromised infrastructure.

This threat model aligns with realistic adversaries in health data contexts
15
;
5
while remaining tractable for formal analysis.

4.2
Deterministic Safeguards

The system enforces several deterministic controls as a first line of defense:

•

Minimum cohort size:

k\geq k_{\min}
for all released statistics

•

Attribute suppression:
Rare attribute combinations are filtered

•

Query rate limiting:
Maximum queries per user per time period

•

Result caching:
Repeated identical queries return cached results to prevent timing attacks

These controls prevent trivial re-identification attacks
42
and complement formal DP guarantees.

4.3
Differential Privacy Mechanisms

Differential privacy serves as a mathematical floor on privacy loss. The platform implements:

•

Per-query DP:
Each aggregate output satisfies
\varepsilon
-DP

•

Composition tracking:
Cumulative privacy loss is tracked across queries using advanced composition
23

•

Budget exhaustion:
Users who exhaust their privacy budget receive only synthetic baselines

In this framework, DP provides worst-case guarantees, while stochastic risk modeling (Section
5
) addresses realized operational risk under typical conditions.

5
Stochastic Risk Modeling

5.1
Motivation

In deployed systems, privacy risk evolves over time as:

•

Cohort composition changes due to user churn and growth

•

Query patterns shift based on platform features and user behavior

•

Adversary knowledge accumulates through observation and inference

We model re-identification risk as a random variable rather than a fixed bound, enabling distributional evaluation through simulation.

5.2
Privacy Loss as a Stochastic Process

Let
L_{i,t}
denote the privacy loss for individual
i
at time
t
, defined as:

L_{i,t}=\log\frac{\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ identifies }i\mid\mathcal{O}_{t},K]}{\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ identifies }i\mid K]}

(3)

where
\mathcal{O}_{t}
represents observations (released aggregates) up to time
t
,
K
is the adversary’s background knowledge, and
\mathcal{A}
is the adversary’s inference algorithm.

Cohort size dynamics.
We model cohort size
N_{t}
as a stochastic process reflecting user growth, churn, and filtering:

N_{t+1}=N_{t}+\text{Poisson}(\lambda_{\text{join}})-\text{Binomial}(N_{t},p_{\text{churn}})

(4)

Attribute entropy.
The entropy of cohort attributes captures the degree of heterogeneity:

H(C_{t})=-\sum_{j}p_{j}\log p_{j}

(5)

where
p_{j}
is the frequency of attribute value
j
in cohort
C_{t}
. Higher entropy corresponds to lower re-identification risk
30
.

Query accumulation.
Privacy loss accumulates across queries according to DP composition bounds:

L_{\text{total}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(n\varepsilon,n\varepsilon^{2}\right)

(6)

for
n
queries under approximate DP composition
11
.

5.3
Privacy Loss at Risk (P-VaR)

To summarize distributional risk, we introduce
Privacy Loss at Risk
(P-VaR), defined analogously to Value at Risk (VaR) in quantitative finance
21
:

\text{P-VaR}_{\alpha}=\inf\{l:\Pr(L>l)\leq 1-\alpha\}

(7)

P-VaR answers: with confidence level
\alpha
, how large can privacy loss become under realistic system dynamics?

For example, P-VaR
0.95
represents the 95th percentile of privacy loss—only 5% of scenarios result in higher loss. This provides an interpretable, decision-relevant metric for risk management.

Conditional tail risk.
We further consider Conditional P-VaR (CP-VaR), the expected privacy loss given that loss exceeds P-VaR:

\text{CP-VaR}_{\alpha}=\mathbb{E}[L\mid L>\text{P-VaR}_{\alpha}]

(8)

This characterizes extreme scenarios beyond the VaR threshold
37
.

Utility of Cohort Benchmarks

Percentile stability.
The variance of reported percentile rankings under DP noise:

\sigma^{2}_{\text{rank}}=\text{Var}(\text{rank}(\tilde{x})-\text{rank}(x))

The Spearman correlation between true and noisy rankings across cohort members.

The probability that DP noise changes a user’s percentile by more than 10 percentage points.

7
Design Implications for Health Platforms

The stochastic risk framework enables platform designers to select privacy parameters based on explicit risk tolerance:

Risk appetite specification.
Organizations can define acceptable P-VaR thresholds (e.g., P-VaR
{}_{0.95}<2.0
) aligned with governance policies.

Dynamic parameter adjustment.
Privacy budgets and cohort size thresholds can be adjusted in response to observed risk metrics.

Regulatory communication.
Distributional risk metrics provide interpretable evidence for privacy impact assessments and regulatory submissions.

User transparency.
Uncertainty intervals around synthetic baselines and population benchmarks can be derived from simulation distributions.

8
Discussion

8.1
Limitations

Modeling assumptions.
The proposed stochastic models are abstractions that rely on assumptions about adversary knowledge, query patterns, and cohort dynamics. While sensitivity analyses suggest qualitative robustness, real-world deployments may exhibit unmodeled behaviors.

8.2
Adversary capabilities.

Our threat model assumes bounded auxiliary information. We formalize the adversary’s knowledge state as:

K=\{(i,a_{i},p_{i}):i\in\mathcal{I}_{K}\}

(10)

where
\mathcal{I}_{K}
is the set of individuals known to the adversary,
a_{i}
are known attributes, and
p_{i}
is the adversary’s confidence in the association. We model
|\mathcal{I}_{K}|/|C|
(the fraction of known individuals) as a key uncertainty parameter.

Attack scenarios:
We consider three attack classes:

1.

Membership inference
: Determining whether an individual belongs to a cohort based on aggregate statistics

2.

Attribute inference
: Inferring sensitive attributes of known cohort members from population statistics

3.

Reconstruction attacks
: Recovering individual-level values from repeated noisy queries

Adversary resources:
We assume computational polynomial-time adversaries who can:

•

Issue up to
Q_{\max}
queries per time period

•

Maintain state across queries (linkage attacks)

•

Access public datasets for auxiliary information

•

Perform Bayesian inference over observed outputs

Out of scope:
Insider threats, side-channel attacks (timing, traffic analysis), compromised infrastructure, and attacks requiring subexponential computation.

Adversary inference model:
Following
25
, we model the adversary’s belief update as:

\displaystyle\Pr[i\in C\mid\mathcal{O}_{t},K]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pr[\mathcal{O}_{t}\mid i\in C,K]\Pr[i\in C\mid K]}{\Pr[\mathcal{O}_{t}\mid K]}

(11)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{t}\Pr[o_{j}\mid i\in C,K,\mathcal{O}_{<j}]\cdot\pi_{i}}{\sum_{i^{\prime}}\prod_{j=1}^{t}\Pr[o_{j}\mid i^{\prime}\in C,K,\mathcal{O}_{<j}]\cdot\pi_{i^{\prime}}}

(12)

where
\pi_{i}
is the prior probability and
o_{j}
are individual observations. This Bayesian framework captures both initial uncertainty and evidence accumulation.

Composition complexity.
Tracking privacy loss across heterogeneous queries and multiple cohorts requires careful accounting. We implement a hierarchical composition framework:

Sequential composition:
For
n
queries with privacy parameters
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
, basic composition
10
gives:

\varepsilon_{\text{total}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}

(13)

Advanced composition:
Using the moments accountant
1
or tight composition
23
, for
n
identical
\varepsilon
-DP queries:

\varepsilon_{\text{total}}\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{2n\ln(1/\delta)}+n\varepsilon(e^{\varepsilon}-1)

(14)

for failure probability
\delta
. This provides tighter bounds for large
n
.

Parallel composition:
When queries operate on disjoint data partitions (different cohorts), privacy loss does not accumulate:

\varepsilon_{\text{parallel}}=\max_{i}\varepsilon_{i}

(15)

Multi-cohort accounting:
A user may appear in multiple overlapping cohorts. We conservatively track:

\varepsilon_{\text{user},t}=\sum_{C:\text{user}\in C}\sum_{q\in Q_{C}(t)}\varepsilon_{q}

(16)

where
Q_{C}(t)
is the set of queries on cohort
C
up to time
t
.

Budget allocation strategy:
We implement a three-tier budget system:

•

Per-query
:
\varepsilon_{q}=0.01
(prevents single-query leakage)

•

Per-cohort-day
:
\varepsilon_{C,d}=0.10
(limits daily exposure)

•

Per-user-month
:
\varepsilon_{u,m}=1.0
(bounds individual cumulative loss)

When any budget is exhausted, the system switches to synthetic baselines for affected queries. This multi-level approach balances responsiveness with long-term privacy protection.

Analysis:
Pure k-anonymity provides intuitive protection but lacks formal worst-case bounds. Pure DP offers strong theoretical guarantees but struggles with interpretability and utility at strong privacy levels (
\varepsilon<0.1

The synthetic baseline fallback ensures universal coverage without compromising privacy when cohorts are too small.

Rényi DP offers marginally better P-VaR (1.58 vs 1.63) due to tighter composition tracking, but at the cost of reduced interpretability. Local DP eliminates the trusted curator but requires much higher privacy budgets to achieve comparable utility, resulting in weaker practical protection (P-VaR = 3.87).

8.4
Ethical and Regulatory Considerations

Transparency.
Users should be informed when receiving synthetic baselines and understand the uncertainty in population comparisons. Visual indicators and explanatory text are essential for informed consent
4
.

Regulatory alignment.
P-VaR metrics can support compliance with privacy regulations (GDPR, HIPAA) by demonstrating quantitative risk management. However, legal interpretation of “adequate protection” remains context-dependent.

Misuse prevention.
While no system can eliminate misuse, risk-aware design enables informed governance and accountability. Regular audits and algorithmic impact assessments should accompany deployment
36
.

8.5
Future Directions

Adaptive privacy budgets.
Machine learning models could predict optimal budget allocation based on real-time risk estimates and utility feedback.

Multi-stakeholder optimization.
Extending the framework to balance privacy interests across users, platform operators, and third-party researchers.

Causal privacy metrics.
Incorporating causal inference to assess whether privacy interventions truly reduce re-identification risk or merely obfuscate information.

9
Conclusion

Personalized cohort analytics are likely to remain a core feature of digital health platforms. We argue that privacy in such systems should be treated as a dynamic risk management problem rather than a static compliance exercise. By combining formal privacy guarantees with stochastic risk modeling, this work provides a practical framework for evaluating and managing privacy-utility tradeoffs in deployed health analytics systems.

The introduction of Privacy Loss at Risk (P-VaR) provides an interpretable, decision-relevant metric that complements differential privacy guarantees. Our simulation experiments demonstrate that operational privacy risk is shaped by system dynamics, query patterns, and adversary knowledge in ways that static metrics do not fully capture.

We hope this framework will inform the design of next-generation health platforms that balance personalization with robust privacy protections, ultimately supporting both individual autonomy and population health goals.
