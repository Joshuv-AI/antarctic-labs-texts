Reimagining invasive weeds through a preliminary antibacterial and phytochemical evaluation of Ipomoea purpurea (L.) Roth floral and seed ethereal extracts
Authors: David Arregui-Almeida, Amanda Cevallos-Vallejo, Maria Fernanda Yauri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Plant specialized metabolites have emerged as key targets in natural products research for their broad bioactivities and potential in novel drug discovery, such as antibiotics. Regrettably, the reliance on vegetal sources often raises ecological concerns, prompting a shift toward alternative species with no conservation constraints—among them, the invasive weed Ipomoea purpurea (L.) Roth. With limited studies on I. purpurea non-polar metabolites and easy access to its floral and seed material, this study preliminarily explored the antibacterial activity of its ethereal extracts using agar diffusion and microdilution assays, complemented by phytochemically screening their metabolites through conventional methods (i.e., qualitative tests, TLC) and GC-MS molecular networking. Flower and seed extracts showed distinct antibacterial activity against model Gram-negative (E. coli ATCC 25922) and Gram-positive (S. aureus ATCC 25923) reference strains, with the seed extract notably inhibiting S. aureus (MIC = 193 mg/mL). Both shared key phytochemicals, namely coumarins, phytosterols, and terpenoids. Molecular networking showed alkanes, fatty acids and derivatives, and ethers as dominant metabolites in both extracts, with the seed extract exhibiting a higher haloalkanes, phytosterols, and terpenoid count. Six and eight known antibacterial specialized metabolites were identified in the flower and seed extract, respectively. These findings highlight the phytochemical potential of invasive species like I. purpurea as alternative sources of bioactive compounds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30596-z.

Introduction

Over the years, the imposing need of new drugs has become a research focus, as several conventional ones have become ineffective1. This is particularly the case with antibiotics, given the modern medical challenge posed by antimicrobial resistance2. Fortunately, new antibiotics with better biocompatibility and bioavailability3,4 can be isolated (i.e. pure molecules) or designed (synthetic or semisynthetic analogues) from bioactive natural products (NPs)5.

Among these NPs, plant specialized metabolites (SMs) have drawn notable interest, especially those found in medicinal plants6. Regrettably, an important number of these plants require a conservation-oriented sustainable use6, as they hold an endangered or threatened conservation status. For instance, of the 50,000 to 80,000 medicinal flowering species used worldwide, around 15,000 are facing extinction7. These concerns create substantial bottlenecks in phytochemical research, particularly at securing sufficient material for extract and compound screening6—and even more so during the isolation of specific target molecules, which often requires large quantities. For threatened or endangered species, such collection volumes are prohibited, despite the potential for discovering biologically active compounds6.

Given this restrictive scenario, there is a clear and compelling need to explore alternative plant sources for research—preferably those with no conservation constraints. This is precisely where invasive weeds warrant consideration, as they may offer relief from the abovementioned bottlenecks hindering phytochemical research.

Ipomoea purpurea (L.) Roth (hereby only referred as I. purpurea), also known as common morning glory, is a continuous bloomer twining vine native to South and Mesoamerica8,9, now widespread worldwide in light of its high phenotypic plasticity and genetic adaptability9. Readily recognized by its funnel-shaped purple flowers, I. purpurea is predominantly known for being an invasive species8,10. As a prolific weed, infestations in agronomic, horticultural and nursery crops are particularly troublesome11,12. Its rapid growth and climbing nature usually lead to intense competition for light and water resources13, ultimately suffocating productive crops.

Interestingly, these survival traits can be traced back to I. purpurea’s NPs, including specialized metabolites (SMs). In plants, all NPs have been structurally “optimized” by evolution to serve different biological functions14, such as aiding in environmental adaptation15, regulating competitive interactions14,16 and providing defense mechanisms14,17. Given I. purpurea’s notable adaptive and competitive traits, it is consequential to regard this weed as a potential source for SMs, of diverse biological activities.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, I. purpurea’s SMs research is scarce and mainly devoted to polar SMs. Most reports have been limited to flavonoid content (i.e., flavones, anthocyanins, antocyanindins) in flowers10,18, N-acyltyramines in epigeal plant sections, resin glycosides from seed/areal parts18, alkaloids from seeds (contradictory results)19, among others. In contrast, mildly polar (i.e., coumarins) and non-polar SMs (i.e., terpenoids or oxygen-bearing terpenes, phytosterols) have not been addressed, in despite of the well-documented bioactivity potential of these phytochemical classes20–22. Hence, this reveals an underexplored niche, within NPs research on I. purpurea, that should be pursued.

Although modern phytochemical research usually follows a bioactivity-guided fractionation to isolate SMs5,23,24, this approach usually underestimates the possibility of re-isolating already known active compounds5,14,25. To remedy this issue, identification of known SMs by means of spectroscopic techniques (i.e., dereplication)4 is highly recommended prior isolation5,14,25; especially when the discovery of new NPs is a primary objective.

Among the various dereplication strategies, mass spectrometry (MS)-based molecular networking stands out by offering a graphical representation of the chemical space occupied by compounds detected in MS data26. Its primary graphical output is a node-based network, where each node corresponds to a molecule’s MS spectrum and edges reflect spectral similarities. As a result, clustering emerges from shared fragmentation patterns, enabling the recognition of structurally related compounds. Furthermore, spectral comparison against reference libraries enables the tentative identification of each molecule, contributing to a more complete depiction of the chemical space. In the case of I. purpurea, this approach—though well-established—has not been previously utilized for phytochemical profiling, to the best of the authors’ understanding.

In light of the previous remarks and considering I. purpurea’s continuous bloomer and prolific nature can facilitate access to sufficient biological material, the present study sought to conduct a preliminary antibacterial activity evaluation on I. purpurea’s flowers and seeds non-polar, ethereal extracts. In addition, phytochemical screening was performed through conventional techniques and molecular networking, as a dereplication strategy applied for the first time in I. purpurea extracts; focused on examining SMs with documented antibacterial activity as isolated or individual commercial compounds. Ultimately, by these means, I. purpurea’s stance as a mere invasive and problematic weed is aimed at being revisited.

Results and discussion

Preliminary antibacterial activity assays

Test microorganisms

In this initial exploration of the antibacterial properties of I. purpurea’s ethereal flower and seed extracts—an area previously unaddressed in the literature—it is methodologically sound to evaluate their bioactivity against standard reference strains that are widely recognized as representatives of Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial pathogens: Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus27,28.

Accordingly, the strains E. coli ATCC 25922 and S. aureus ATCC 25923 were selected and their identity successfully validated, prior to the bioassays. In brief, E. coli ATCC 25922 showed positive growth on eosin methylene blue agar (EMB), displaying round dark colonies with a green metallic sheen; and key Enterobacteriaceae biochemical tests results in accordance with literature29 (Supplementary Information, Figure S1). Similarly, S. aureus ATCC 25923 showed a positive growth on mannitol salt agar (MS), exhibiting round yellow colonies, as well as positive results for the catalase and slide-coagulase tests. The oxidase test results were found to be negative30 (Supplementary Information, Figure S2). It is worth emphasizing that the purpose of these tests is to verify the identity of reference strains prior to any bioactivity assessment, thereby ensuring scientific rigor. As outlined in the World Health Organization’s Good Practices for Pharmaceutical Microbiology Laboratories Annex 2, appropriate purity and biochemical tests should be conducted when handling reference strains31.

On preliminary assessment, after comparing the extracts inhibitory zone diameters with those of the positive controls, it is evident that Et2O-F and Et2O-S exert slight antibacterial activity against E. coli, in the same manner as Et2O-F against S. aureus. When closely observed, the inhibition zones under discussion appear faint and small (Supplementary Information, Figure S3-5). Such an appearance is probably a direct result of the non-polar nature of Et2O-F and Et2O-S, leading to insufficient diffusion on the test agar and thereby partial inhibitory activity. According to literature, this effect is often encountered when testing other lipophilic substances such as essential oils32. In addition, small inhibitory zones can also occur when antibacterial compounds in extracts are present in such low concentrations that they do not exert inhibitory effects33,34.

Contrary to the abovementioned remarks, Et2O-S displayed considerable bioactivity against S. aureus. Featuring a notable diameter and distinctive light-yellow hue (Supplementary Information, Figure S6), the Et2O-S inhibitory zone did not exhibit any tetrazolium reduction to pink formazan after the 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) solution was sprayed on the test plates. Accordingly, this result suggested no sign of dehydrogenase production33, and thus the absence of metabolically active bacterial cells35. To verify the suggested bioactivity against S. aureus, Et2O-S underwent further assessment via microdilution.

Broth microdilution assay for Et2O-S against S. aureus

The results for the antibacterial activity of Et2O-S against S. aureus, evaluated through broth microdilution, are displayed in Fig. 1.

Fig. 1Broth microdilution assay results for Et2O-S dilutions against S. aureus, following incubation with 2.5 mg/mL TTC solution, and corresponding well contents cultivation on Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA).

Broth microdilution assay results for Et2O-S dilutions against S. aureus, following incubation with 2.5 mg/mL TTC solution, and corresponding well contents cultivation on Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA).

As shown above, the majority of extract-containing wells turned various shades of pink following the addition of TTC. The color intensity gradually decreased as the concentration of Et2O-S increased from 1 mg/mL to 193 mg/mL. At 193 mg/mL, triplicate wells exhibited no signs of TTC reduction to pink formazan derivatives and thus, no indication of active bacterial cells. When cultivated on MHA, all wells’ contents lead to colonies growth after incubation, hence suggesting cells retained viability after exposure to the 193 mg/mL dilution. Taken together, these results imply that Et2O-S possesses the ability to inhibit S. aureus without leading to cellular death (i.e., bacteriostatic effect), at a concentration of 193 mg/mL; that being fundamentally, its MIC.

Similar results have been reported for other Ipomoea species, particularly those of the “morning glory class”, in a wide variety of solvents. To name but a few, coumarins, phytosterols and terpenes (i.e., hydrocarbons derived from isoprene units) were screened in petroleum ether and chloroform extracts from Ipomoea obscura (L.) Ker Gawl. seeds37. Likewise, phytosterols were found in Ipomoea nil (L.) Roth seed ethanolic extract38, as well as in Ipomoea pauciflora M. Martens & Galeotti floral hydroalcoholic extract; along with coumarins39.

Furthermore, and as anticipated, the presence of these non-polar phytochemicals is entirely dependent on the chosen solvent for extract preparation. Diethyl ether, having a polarity index of 2.840, allows non-polar metabolites like terpenoids and phytosterols, as well as mildly polar ones, namely coumarins, to be partitioned to the organic phase during extraction. The latter scenario has been found for furanocoumarins, which can be extracted by petroleum ether, diethyl ether and/or chloroform41–43.

Under UV400 and UV254 (Fig. 2A1-2 & Fig. 2B1-2), both extracts showed fluorescent and dark-zone bands, respectively; which strongly suggest the presence of SMs containing UV-absorbing chromophores44, including highly conjugated π-systems (e.g., aromatics, polyenes, α, β-unsaturated carbonyl derivatives). In both extracts, red and blue/cyan, fluorescent bands can be distinguished; these being more numerous and exhibiting greater fluorescence intensity in the Et2O-F TLC profile.

Normally, red emitting bands can be usually attributed to the presence of chlorophyl and/or porphyrin derivatives45. Conversely, blue emitting bands commonly indicate the presence of either phenolics, alkaloids, and terpenoids45. Within phenolics, coumarins are notable for exhibiting blue, fluorescent hues45,46, particularly when they possess extended conjugation. That is the case of furanocoumarins, which are cyan-green emitters46. Within the Ipomoea genus, coumarins like umbelliferone and scopoletin have been identified47. Similarly, some alkaloids outstand as blue-emitting SMs; namely, quinolines, quinolones, isoquinolines, ergolines and guanidinopyridines can be found46. Among these, ergolines have been commonly identified in seeds across the Ipomoea genus, although with some controversy in I. purpurea due to botanical misidentification48.

With the vanillin-H2SO4 reagent application, most bands in both extracts’ profiles acquired violet, yellow, red and brown hues (Fig. 2A3 & Fig. 2B3). Only the Et2O-F profile displayed a blue-colored band (Fig. 2A3). According to literature, violet and yellow probably correspond to monoterpenes, steroids and/or carotenoids49. Conversely, red and brown bands may be indicative of metabolites whose double bond is either adjacent or distant from an aromatic ring, respectively41. Furthermore, blue-colored bands might indicate the presence of cardenolide aglycones49 or terpenes50. In the Ipomoea genus, monoterpenes have been found in several species, particularly in leaves and flower essential oils51. Similarly, steroids like phytosterols have been identified in aerial parts, root tubers, and seeds52. Concerning cardenolides, they have not been reported in the Convolvulaceae family53.

Through the use of the SnCl4 reagent, chromatographic bands in both extracts developed yellow and red visible hues (Fig. 2A3.1 & Fig. 2B3.1), which showed blue fluorescence under UV400 (Fig. 2A3.2 & Fig. 2B3.2). Only the Et2O-F exhibited a pink visible band that showed an orange, fluorescent response under UV400 (Fig. 2A3.1 & Fig. 2B3.2). Accordingly, these observations are expected from the staining of bands that contain triterpenes, sterols or steroids54–56. Moreover, visible band colors showing red hues are said to indicate metabolites with the triterpenoid skeleton57; probably this being the case of the pink and red bands in the Et2O-F profile and dark-red bands in Et2O-S profile.

Finally, only a few bands in both extracts showed yellow hues after the KOH reagent application and heating (Fig. 2A4.1 & Fig. 2B4.1). These bands were readily identified in Et2O-F, while on the Et2O-S plate they appeared faintly. Under UV400, these bands and several others showed blue fluorescence. As per literature, this is a common staining behavior of coumarins and/or their corresponding glycosides58–60.

GC-MS analysis: metabolite spectral annotation and molecular networking

Et2O-F and Et2O-F spectral annotation

The GC-MS analysis, Et2O-F revealed a total of 93 chromatographic features (i.e., peaks), while Et2O-S showed a total of 91 features. Corresponding annotation results are summarized in Fig. 3:

Fig. 3Distribution overview of Et2O-F and Et2O annotated and unannotated metabolites, incorporating corresponding cosine score classification for annotated metabolites.

Distribution overview of Et2O-F and Et2O annotated and unannotated metabolites, incorporating corresponding cosine score classification for annotated metabolites.

From the data presented above, a high success rate of metabolite annotation is readily identified, as more than two-thirds of the metabolites analyzed in Et2O-F and Et2O-S were annotated (i.e., 71 metabolites − 76.34% and 61 metabolites – 67.78%, respectively).

Out of these metabolites, nearly two-thirds exhibited a spectral similarity cosine score of at least 0.85 (i.e., 54 metabolites − 76.06% and 42 metabolites − 68.85%, respectively), thus suggesting a highly reliable putative identification. According to literature, annotations showing a cosine score between 0.85 and 1.00 depict a high spectral similarity with reference spectra61. Even though these criteria are not applicable to the remaining metabolites with scores greater than 0.70 but under 0.85, annotations above 0.7 can still be useful while exploring metabolites structural resemblance and grouping into metabolites classes.

Conversely, around 20–30% of the analyzed metabolites in Et2O-F and Et2O-S did not match any reference spectra in the GNPS spectral public libraries (i.e., 22 metabolites, 23.94% and 29 metabolites, 31.15%; respectively). Typically, these results arise due to the absence of matching reference spectrum in the spectral database, either because of its limited spectra coverage62 or the encouraging prospect of a novel metabolite yet to be identified.

Et2O-F molecular networking

Following an initial assessment of the molecular networking results, Fig. 4 illustrates the main phytochemical subclasses found in Et2O-F (See Et2O-F molecular networking results, Supplementary Information, Table S1 and Figures S7-8).

Fig. 4Metabolite subclass distribution among Et2O-F metabolites.

Metabolite subclass distribution among Et2O-F metabolites.

Overall, the most prevalent subclasses among the Et2O-F annotated metabolites include alkanes, alkenes, ethers, fatty acids and corresponding derivatives (i.e., esters, amides), as well as aliphatic ketones. In flower petals, alkanes are abundantly found in cuticular waxes, sharing in common a linear long-chain aliphatic structure63. In a similar fashion, long-chain fatty acids and derivatives, as well as long-chain, aliphatic derivatives also constitute cuticular waxes; particularly bearing oxygen functionalities like alcohol, aldehyde, ester and ketone64.

Concerning the annotated long-chain ethers, aliphatic amides and haloalkanes, they might also be considered part of the cuticle composition; however, this remains uncertain due to the scarce research on petal cuticular waxes65. To exemplify this, while aliphatic amides have been identified in Convolvulaceous species using GC-MS, they have not yet been documented in I. purpurea66. Similarly, no reports related to the Ipomoea genus have been provided for haloalkanes, particularly iodoalkanes. Still, they have been found to be constituents of fruit cuticular waxes67.

Additional subclasses, namely terpenoids, phenolic compounds and phytosterols can be also traced back to cuticular waxes68,69, although terpenoids are mostly found comprising floral volatile organic compounds (VOCs)70. Similarly, phytosterols are not secluded to cuticular waves; in turn they exist in all plant cell membranes71, including those of a flower. Apart from the latter subclasses, tocopherols are also accumulated in floral tissues to act as antioxidants, particularly α-tocopherol72.

Regarding the remaining subclasses, overall information on their individual annotated metabolites biosynthesis and role in plants is definitely limited.

Et2O-S molecular networking

After an initial inspection of the molecular networking results, Fig. 5 presents the main phytochemical subclasses identified in Et2O-S (See Et2O-S molecular networking results, Supplementary Information, Table S2 and Figures S9-10).

Fig. 5Metabolite subclass distribution among Et2O-S metabolites.

Metabolite subclass distribution among Et2O-S metabolites.

The most frequent phytochemical subclasses in Et2O-S encompass annotated alkanes, fatty acids and derivatives (i.e., esters, amides) and ethers. According to literature, fatty acids are predominant in seeds from most members of the Convolvulaceae family, including those from the ornamental species of the Ipomoea genus66. Similarly, to Et2O-S, tocopherols are also commonly found among seed metabolites, as they ensure their viability73 and limit lipid content oxidation74.

Although comparable to Et2O-F at first, the Et2O-S metabolite subclass distribution includes a higher count of annotated metabolites belonging to the haloalkanes, phytosterols, and terpenoids subclasses. As expected, the last two subclasses notably contribute to the unsaponifiable matter in seed fats75. Notably, among terpenoids, β-amyrin-type triterpenoids are the most frequently found in seeds75.

Among small-membered subclasses that contribute to the seeds’ fat constituents (i.e., alkenes, aliphatic and fatty alcohols, aliphatic esters), the alkaloid and lignans subclasses are particularly noteworthy.

Depending on the specific type of alkaloid, these metabolites have been reported in several members of the Convolvulaceae family18. As previously described, special care must be taken when discussing ergoline alkaloids in I. purpurea, due to contradictory reports19.

Regarding the lignans subclass, several reports have been made among Convolvulaceae species, particularly regarding furofuran-type lignans, as well as lignanolides and lignanamides. When examining Ipomoea species, lignanolides have been identified in the seeds of Ipomoea cairica (L.)76.

Annotation of antibacterial SMs present in Et2O-F and Et2O-S

Before moving forward, it is key to note that antibacterial activity in plant extracts may originate in two distinct scenarios. The simplest approach assumes that one or a few specific metabolites can be accounted for the observed extract bioactivity. Nonetheless, given that extracts consist of a mixture of different metabolites, their overall bioactivity may depend on how these metabolites interact —whether through synergy, additive effects, or antagonism77.

In light of these statements, the examination of annotated Et2O-F and Et2O-S antibacterial metabolites will deviate from focusing on the most abundant components. Instead, it will consider all metabolites with documented antibacterial effects as isolated or individual commercial compounds. Conversely, unannotated metabolites and those lacking prior bioactivity assessments will not be included for obvious reasons.

Et2O-F antibacterial SMs molecular networking

A total of six SMs were identified amidst the Et2O-F molecular network, as displayed in Fig. 6.

Fig. 6Et2O-F molecular network highlighting SMs with documented antibacterial activity.

Et2O-F molecular network highlighting SMs with documented antibacterial activity.

Belonging to the terpenoid subclass and to the same molecular network cluster, two metabolites were annotated: 3,7-dimethyloct-6-enyl acetate (1, cosine = 0.83) and 3,7,11,15-tetramethylhexadeca-2,6,10,14-tetraen-1-ol (3, cosine = 0.94). 1, also known as citronellyl acetate, has shown antibacterial activity against the same susceptible bacterial strains tested in this study, E. coli ATCC 25922 (MIC = 7.5 mg/mL) and S. aureus ATCC 25923 (30 mg/mL). Also, it possesses bioactivity against the non-pathogenic Bacillus subtilis ATCC 6633 (15 mg/mL)78.

Concerning 3, the lack of isomer-specific details in its GNPS public library spectral match complicates the process of tracing its documented antibacterial activity. Among its isomers, (2E,6E,10E)−3,7,11,15-tetramethylhexadeca-2,6,10,14-tetraen-1-ol, also known as geranyl geraniol, is the most prevalent in plants, serving as a biosynthetic intermediate for tocopherols, phytol, and chlorophyll79. Particularly, geranyl geraniol has shown activity against S. aureus FDA209P (methicillin-susceptible) effectively inhibiting its growth at 1.25 µg/mL and exhibiting a bactericidal effect in between 10 and 80 µg/mL (No significant differences)80. Additional bioactivity has been reported against Mycobacterium tuberculosis ATCC 27294 (MIC = 1.56 µg/mL), E. coli ATCC 25922 (MIC > 32 µg/mL) and S. aureus ATCC 25923 (MIC > 32 µg/mL)81.

Pertaining to the same cluster as 1 and 3 and belonging to the fatty amides subclass, (Z)-docos-13-enamide (2, cosine = 0.94) was annotated. This fatty acid derivative has documented activity against E. coli ATCC 25922 (MIC = 10.03 ± 0.03 µg/mL) and S. aureus ATCC 25923 (MIC = 8.60 ± 0.40 µg/mL), in addition to the susceptible strain Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 13883 (MIC = 7.90 ± 0.10 µg/mL) and B. subtilis NRRL B-543 (MIC = 8.10 ± 0.17 µg/mL)82.

In the phytosterols subclass, isofucosterol (4, cosine = 0.75) was annotated, which has demonstrated antibacterial activity against the susceptible strains Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, E. coli ATCC 25922, S. aureus ATCC 25923 and clinical-isolated Methicillin Resistant S. aureus (MRSA) (in all cases, MIC > 256 µg/mL)83.

Finally, belonging to a three-membered cluster and exhibiting aromatic structural moieties, α-tocopherol (5, cosine = 0.79) and 7-methoxy-4-methylcoumarin (6, cosine = 0.73) were annotated. While V lacks significant antibacterial activity (MIC ≥ 1024 µg/mL) according to literature84,85, research also indicates it may enhance bacterial sensitivity when used alongside specific antibiotics. For instance, 5 has proven successful in rendering the opportunistic Burkholderia cenocepacia more sensitive to norfloxacin. Specifically, this effect arises from 5’s binding to the extracellular bacterial lipocalin protein BcnA, which B. cenocepacia produces to capture bactericidal antibiotics as part of its extracellular antimicrobial resistance mechanism86.

Regarding 6, it has demonstrated antibacterial activity approaching that of Ciprofloxacin, particularly when tested against the Gram-negatives E. coli (MIC50 = 12.76 ± 0.22 µg/mL) and Shigella sonnei (MIC50 = 13.47 ± 0.02 µg/mL), in addition to the Gram-positives S. aureus (MIC50 = 11.36 ± 0.20 µg/mL) and B. subtilis (MIC50 = 11.76 ± 0.11 µg/mL)87.

Et2O-S antibacterial SMs molecular networking

As shown in Fig. 7, eight SMs were identified within the Et2O-S molecular network.

Fig. 7Et2O-S molecular network highlighting SMs with documented antibacterial activity.

Et2O-S molecular network highlighting SMs with documented antibacterial activity.

On the whole, three out of the eight SMs were previously discussed in the Et2O-F molecular network, namely 3 (Cosine = 0.92), 4 (Cosine = 0.87) and 5 (Cosine = 0.78). As for the remaining ones, the mostly belong to the phytosterol subclass, followed by the terpenoid and hydrocarbon subclasses.

Within the largest cluster of the Et2O-S molecular network, the following phytosterols, including 4, were annotated: β-sitosterol (7, cosine = 0.77), campesterol (8, cosine = 0.78) and stigmasterol (11, cosine = 0.79). 7 antibacterial activity has been widely reported in literature, for both Gram-positives and Gram-negatives88–91. For instance, when testing clinical isolates, 7 has shown bioactivity against S. aureus (MIC = 25 µg/mL), B. subtilis (MIC = 12.5 µg/mL), K. pneumoniae (MIC = 25 µg/mL), Streptococcus pyogenes (MIC = 12.5 µg/mL) and Shigella dysenteriae (MIC = 12.5 µg/mL)89. Comparable results have been found when evaluated against the following isolates: MRSA, B. subtilis, S. pyogenes, S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa (in all former cases, MIC = 5 µg/mL), K. pneumoniae and Salmonella typhi (in the last two cases, MIC = 10 µg/mL)92. Furthermore, when tested reference strains, similar results have been found for P. aeruginosa (PAO1, MIC = 50 µg/mL; PA14, MIC = 25 µg/mL)88 and S. aureus ATCC 2592393. Documented bioactivity has also been displayed through inhibitory zones against E. coli ATCC 25922, S. aureus ATCC 25923 and K. pneumoniae ATCC 1003194.

Similarly, 8 antibacterial activity has been assessed against widely occurring pathogenic susceptible strains including E. coli ATCC 10536, S. aureus ATCC 6538, P. aeruginosa ATCC 15442 and K. pneumoniae ATCC 10031 (in all five cases, MIC = 512 µg/mL or 1.280 mM)95. Research has additionally confirmed the antibacterial activity of isolates: MRSA, B. subtilis, S. pyogenes, S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, E. coli, P. aeruginosa and S. typhi (in all cases, MIC = 5 µg/mL)92. Interestingly, 8 has also shown bioactivity against lesser-studied bacterial species, such as Streptococcus mutans, an oral bacterium known for its role in tooth decay. Accordingly, 8 antibacterial properties have been examined in S. mutans ATCC 0046 (MIC = 512 µg/mL or 1.280 mM) reference strain95, S. mutans ATCC 25175 (MIC = 500 µg/mL), along with multiple field isolates obtained from patients (MIC ≤ 500 µg/mL)96.

11 has also proven to be bioactive against the oral-based bacterial strains S. mutans (MIC = 625 µg/mL) and Streptococcus sanguinis (MIC = 312.5 µg/mL)97, in addition to other clinical isolates: Enterococcus faecalis (312.5 µg/mL)97, E. coli (MIC = 25 µg/mL), Pseudomonas fluorescens (MIC = 25 µg/mL), Streptococcus faecalis (MIC = 12.5 µg/mL), S. aureus (MIC = 12.5 µg/mL) and MRSA (MIC = 6.25 µg/mL)98.

Among the terpenoids present in the Et2O-S molecular framework, and alongside 3, propan-2-yl tetradecanoate (9, cosine = 0.90) was annotated. Also referred to as isopropyl myristate, 9 has documented bioactivity, particularly against P. aeruginosa99.

Finally, pertaining to the hydrocarbon subclass, hexacosane (10, cosine = 0.95) was annotated. 10 has shown bioactivity against reference strains, namely E. coli ATCC 25922, S. aureus ATCC 25923 and K. pneumoniae ATCC 1003194.

Integrating molecular networking and spectral annotation results with the antibacterial activity of Et2O-F and Et2O-S

To effectively integrate the molecular networking and spectral annotation results with the preliminary antibacterial findings, it is first necessary to consider the general mechanisms by which secondary metabolites act on bacterial cells. Only then, with this framework in place, the specific bioactivities of Et2O-F and Et2O-S against E. coli and S. aureus can be meaningfully assessed.

Antibacterial mechanistic insights on the annotated metabolites in Et2O-F and Et2O-S extracts

As revealed by the Et2O-F and Et2O-S molecular networks showcasing annotated SMs with documented antibacterial activity (See Figs. 6 and 7), the predominant phytochemical subclasses associated with this specific bioactivity include phytosterols and terpenoids. While it may seem intuitive that compounds from the same phytochemical subclass act through similar antibacterial mechanisms, this notion oversimplifies the complexity of bioactivity: structurally related molecules can surprisingly diverge in function, while unrelated ones may exhibit comparable effects100. Beyond their main targets—and adding yet another layer of complexity—some antibacterial mechanisms involve secondary or off-primary target effects101.

Broadly speaking, there are five principal modes by which antibacterial molecules exert their effects on bacteria: inhibition of cell wall biosynthesis, inhibition of protein synthesis, inhibition of nucleic acids synthesis, disruption of membrane integrity and disruption of biosynthetic pathways102,103. On this basis, when considering phytosterols collectively, studies indicate that they can disrupt the bacterial membrane integrity by inhibiting cell surface proteins and inducing alterations in membrane composition104. This is exemplified, for instance, by β-sitosterol—one of the most studied phytosterols and annotated in Et2O-S—has been reported to exert antibacterial effects through disruption of key biosynthetic pathways and inhibition of cell wall biosynthesis.

Molecular docking studies have shown β-sitosterol inhibits dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR)105, a key enzyme for maintaining bacterial growth106, as well as the metabolism-related S. aureus pyruvate carboxylase107. Using a similar in silico approach, it has been shown that β-sitosterol can inhibit MurA and SrtA, two enzymes involved in peptidoglycan biosynthesis and, consequently, bacterial cell wall formation108. This last mode of action also applies for campesterol and stigmasterol, other phytosterols annotated in Et2O-S. These two modes of action have also been proposed respectively for campesterol and stigmasterol, two additional phytosterols annotated in Et2O-S. Specifically, campesterol has been reported to inhibit S. aureus pyruvate carboxylase107, while stigmasterol targets MurA and PBP97—enzymes involved in peptidoglycan biosynthesis108.

With regards to terpenoids, even though their precise mode of action remains unclear, it has been documented that they interfere with two key metabolic and survival processes: oxygen uptake and oxidative phosphorylation109. The first constraint is particularly critical for aerobic bacteria, whereas the second is broadly relevant, as it underpins bacterial cellular respiration. This last process is critical for bacteria, as ATP is primarily generated through oxidative phosphorylation—such is the case for E. coli110 and S. aureus111 (during aerobic growth). Additionally, terpenoids have been shown to disrupt the lipid fraction of bacterial membranes, leading to altered permeability and leakage of intracellular contents112. Such is the case for geranyl geraniol, putatively annotated in Et2O-F, which has been reported to compromise S. aureus membrane integrity80. Additional mechanisms attributed to oxygenated terpene derivatives include inhibition of protein synthesis and disruption of DNA replication113.

Assessment of differential antibacterial activity between Et2O-F and Et2O-S

To initiate a tentative rationale for the observed differences in antibacterial activity between Et2O-F and Et2O-S, two key factors should be considered: differential phytochemical composition and the selected bacterial model strains under testing.

Regarding the first factor, given the previously discussed antibacterial activities and proposed mechanisms of action of several phytosterols present in Et2O-S, this compositional difference between Et2O-F and Et2O-S may underline the observed variation in antibacterial activity—minimal in Et2O-F and considerable in Et2O-S. Nevertheless, further conclusions cannot be drawn without venturing into speculation unsupported by experimental evidence. As previously emphasized, the bioactivity of plant extracts involves multifaceted interactions and cannot be reduced to the influence of a few major constituents. Assessing the potential synergistic, antagonistic, or additive effects between phytosterols and other phytochemical classes, especially those containing SMs with known antibacterial properties (e.g., terpenoids), remains essential for accurate interpretation. Nonetheless, this level of analysis exceeds the scope of the present work.

Shifting focus to the second factor, the selection of bacterial model strains for evaluating the antibacterial activity of plant extracts introduces interpretive complexity, as their cellular architecture influences how specialized metabolites interact with specific targets. Fortunately, this complexity is more manageable, as most strains fall into two well-conserved physiological categories: Gram-negative and Gram-positive. Gram-negative bacteria feature a tripartite cell envelope: an outer lipopolysaccharide membrane, a thin peptidoglycan cell wall, and an inner membrane114. Gram-positive bacteria, by contrast, lack the outer membrane114 and have a thicker peptidoglycan cell wall with lipophilic ends of lipoteichoic acid115. Due to the presence of an outer membrane, Gram-negative bacteria possess an additional protective barrier that limits interactions with external agents (e.g., SMs) and their potential permeation. As a result, Gram-negative bacteria are generally more resistant to antibacterial compounds, whereas Gram-positive bacteria tend to be more susceptible—a pattern frequently observed with bioactive compounds from plant extracts114.

These observations may initially explain the slight antibacterial activity of Et2O-F and the more pronounced activity of Et2O-S, both of which were effective only against S. aureus (Gram-positive) and not E. coli (Gram-negative). Furthermore, given that both extracts are rich in non-polar, lipophilic secondary metabolites (SMs), it is plausible that these compounds interact more effectively with lipophilic domains of the Gram-positive’s cell walls, potentially facilitating the five previously described antibacterial mechanisms of action. Nonetheless, as addressed, this proposal also remains a theoretical rationale that requires experimental validation before it can be substantiated.

Future perspectives on Et2O-F and Et2O-S molecular networks and related specialized metabolites

As illustrated in the molecular networks, many SMs remained unannotated, which —as previously discussed— can be attributed to the lack of matching reference spectra in current databases. Nonetheless, this lack of spectral annotation could also suggest the existence of yet-to-be-isolated and structurally elucidated SMs.

Fortunately, molecular networking offers valuable advantages for the preliminary phytochemical exploration of extracts SMs, aside from the dereplication of known SMs. By grouping mass spectra with similar fragmentation patterns116, molecular networks allow annotated metabolites to guide the initial identification of phytochemical classes/subclasses for unannotated ones. Furthermore, similarities in fragmentation patterns can hint at the structural motifs of unannotated metabolites, aiding subsequent structural characterization of isolated compounds.

Future research on I. purpurea’s unannotated SMs may benefit from these insights to ease structural elucidation. Moreover, a bioactivity-guided molecular networking strategy could be implemented to prioritize the isolation of unknown SMs with promising bioactivity, thereby reducing the consumption of extraction solvents. This approach would also allow spectroscopic characterization to be streamlined to a limited set of target molecules, optimizing the use of analytical instruments (e.g., NMR spectrometers, mass spectrometers) and their associated consumables (e.g., deuterated solvents, mass spectrometry-grade solvents).

Methods

Chemicals and reagents

Diethyl ether (Et2O) was obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), HPLC grade ethyl acetate (AcOEt) and GC-MS grade hexane (n-Hex) were purchased from Fisher Scientific (New Jersey, United States). All organic solvents met American Chemical Society (ACS) specifications and were used without further purification.

Phytochemical screening and TLC plates visualization were performed using analytical grade solvents and reagents, from various commercial sources. Phytochemical screening reagents (i.e., Dragendorff, Wagner, Mayer, (CH3COO)2Pb, FeCl3) were prepared according to Lide & Haynes117and Sun et al118.. TLC vanillin-sulfuric acid spray reagent, potassium hydroxide (KOH) and tin (IV) chloride (SnCl4) dipping solutions followed Jork et al. preparation119.

For the antibacterial activity assays, microbial growth media (i.e., agar plates, broths and semisolids) were purchased from Becton, Dickinson and Company (BD, Sparks, Maryland, United States), while antibiotic disks (i.e., ampicillin and azithromycin) were obtained from Oxoid (Hampshire, England). Gibco Penicillin-Streptomycin (6000 µg/mL − 10,000 µg/mL) solution in 0.85% saline was ordered from Invitrogen, Thermo Scientific (Grand Island, New York, United States). 2,3,5-triphenyl-tetrazolium chloride (TTC) was obtained from Fisher Scientific (New Jersey, United States) and Triton X-100 from BDH Chemicals (Poole, England). Tween 80 was purchased from Mallinckrodt (Paris, Kentucky, United States).

Plant material

I. purpurea flowers and dry seeds were collected daily through January-March 2024, from a wild specimen found at a private cropland, in Guayllabamba, Quito, Ecuador (00°05’7.7’’ S 78°19’28.5’’ W). Authorization for the collection and transport of the specimens was granted by Ecuador’s designated authority, the Ministry of the Environment, Water and Ecological Transition, under permits MAATE-ARSFC-2023-0319 (collection) and MAATE-ARSFC-2023-0319, Tracking N°. 01999 (transport).

Flowers were dried under pressure, at room temperature, and then stored in silica gel under further use. Dried seeds were stored in a similar manner. A voucher specimen (QCA249676, Supplementary Information, Figure S11) was identified by Professor Álvaro Pérez and deposited in the QCA Herbarium, Facultad de Ciencias Exactas y Naturales, Pontificia Universidad Católica del Ecuador (Quito, Ecuador).

Extraction

Vegetal material was finely ground with an electric grinder (Hamilton Beach, 80350R, China) and then extracted with Et2O (10 mL/g). Extraction was assisted by ultrasonication (40 kHz), carried out in an ultrasonic bath (Bransonic M3800H, Mexico). After an 8 h period, ethereal extracts were microfiltered (cellulose acetate microfilter, 0.45 μm, MCA grade, chm, Barcelona, Spain) and concentrated to dryness under rotary evaporation (20 °C, 400 mmHg). Crude extracts were stored in aliquots, in sterile vials, at −20 °C, until further use.

Preliminary antibacterial activity assays

Test microorganisms

Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 bacterial strains, stored as stock cultures at −20 °C, were reactivated by inoculation on brain heart infusion broth (BHI). After growth at 37 °C, E. coli was cultured on EMB agar, while S. aureus was initially cultured on blood agar, and then on MS agar. Both EMB and MS plates were kept at 4 °C until further use. Simultaneously, each bacterial identity was verified through conventional biochemical testing. For E. coli, Triple Sugar Iron (TSI), citrate, Motility Indol Lysine (MIL), urea, methyl red, Sulfide Indol Motility (SIM), and phenylalanine tests were performed. Instead, S. aureus was identified through catalase, slide-coagulase and oxidase tests.

Antibacterial susceptibility test: agar diffusion spot assay

Crude extracts’ antibacterial activity was initially assessed through agar diffusion spot assay, following the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100 (2024) guidelines. This assay was repeated with three biological replicates.

Initially, given the flower extract semi-solid consistency, it was initially dissolved with pure DMSO up to 334 mg/mL to make handling more convenient. Conversely, as the seed extract exhibited an oily nature, dissolution in DMSO was unnecessary.

After the sample’s preparation, each extract was tested separately. In brief, 3 µL of the extract was directly added to Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA) plates lawn cultures (i.e., 0.5 McFarland standard bacterial inoculum, OD600 = 0.616–0.632, ~ 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL). Ampicillin (10 µg) and azithromycin (15 µg) antibiotic disks were used as positive controls for E. coli and S. aureus, respectively. Pure Et2O was used as an extraction solvent control while testing the flower and seed extracts. In turn, pure was exclusively used as a redissolution solvent control during the flower extract test. Plates were incubated for 18 h at 37 °C, under aerobic conditions. For better inhibition zone visualization, plates were sprayed with an aqueous 2.5 mg/mL TTC solution, containing 300 µL of Triton X-100. Afterwards, they were incubated for two additional hours. All inhibition zone diameters were recorded to the nearest millimeter including the extracts’ spot diameter.

Broth microdilution assay

Based on the results of the agar diffusion spot assay, extracts that showed bacterial inhibition were selected for Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) determination; adapting the broth microdilution procedures described by Thielmann et al.120 and Mansouri et al.121. This whole assay was repeated with three biological replicates.

To provide a brief overview, the crude extract was mixed with Tween 80, in a 5:1 extract/Tween 80 proportion. Then, a series of two-fold dilutions were prepared using MHB supplemented with 0.15% (m/v) MHA (MHB-0.15%MHB). For the inoculum, a 1,000,000-fold bacterial suspension was prepared by diluting a 0.5 McFarland standard bacterial inoculum (OD600 = 0.616–0.632, ~ 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL) with sterile MHB-0.15%MHA. Then, in a 96-well plate, 50 µL of bacterial inoculum, along with 20 µL of the extract’s dilutions were added to each well, of the first nine columns. Remaining wells were used for the positive, negative and sterility controls.

For the positive control, 20 µL of bacterial inoculum and 50 µL of Penicillin-Streptomycin (6000 µg/mL-10,000 µg/mL) solution was used. The negative control was prepared in a similar manner, replacing the antibiotic by 20 µL of 5:1 sterile MHB-0.15%MHB/Tween 80 mixture. Conversely, the sterility control contained 20 µL of uninoculated MHB-0.15%MHA and 50 µL 5:1 sterile MHB-0.15%MHB/Tween 80 mixture.

Plates were incubated for 18 h at 37 °C, under aerobic conditions. Afterwards, 20 µL of the 2,5 mg/mL TTC solution, used in Sect. 3.7.2., was added to each well. Then, plates were incubated for two more hours. The MIC was determined by the extracts’ lowest concentration at which wells showed no bacterial growth and thus, no TTC reduction to pink formazan. Finally, all well contents were cultivated on MHA plates under similar incubation conditions to verify MIC-related well contents growth reactivation.

The potential reduction of the added TTC solution by the extract was ruled out using a TTC reduction control, which carried out in the same experimental conditions previously described. Supporting results and full procedural details are available in the Supplementary Information, Figure S12 and explanatory text accompanying Figure S12.

Preliminary phytochemical screening

Qualitative phytochemical testing

Prior to the testing, crude extracts were redissolved in Et2O. A 10 µL sample of ethereal extract and a blank of pure Et2O was used in each phytochemical test. Phytochemical classes under assessment and corresponding tests included: alkaloids (Dragendorff, Wagner, Mayer), coumarins (alkaline), flavonoids (alkaline and lead acetate), phytosterols (Salkowski and Libermann-Buchard), saponins (foam), tannins (FeCl3) and terpenoids (H2SO4). These were adapted from previous works118,122–124 and carried out as described in the Supplementary Information, Table S3.

Thin layer chromatography (TLC)

Crude extracts were separated by TLC, using 5.6 × 6 cm aluminum plates with fluorescent indicator (Silica gel 60, F254 indicator, Supelco, Germany). Each plate contained six individual application spots. Floral extracts were separated under isocratic elution, using a n-Hex-AcOEt (4.75:1) mobile phase, whereas seed extracts required step elution. Initially, separation was carried-out with 3 mL n-Hex mobile phase. Then, the same plate was re-chromatographed twice, after the consecutive addition of 300 µL of AcOEt.

After separation, dried plates were initially visualized under UV254 and UV400. Then, plates were cut so that every separated sample spot would be allocated in an individual plate strip. Each was visualized through staining with vanillin-sulfuric acid spray, or after dipping in KOH, SnCl4 solutions. Strips under vanillin-sulfuric acid, KOH and SnCl4 visualization required heating for staining to take place.

Gas chromatography – mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis

GC-MS samples were prepared by dissolving each crude extract in GC-MS grade n-Hexane. Afterwards, they were microfiltered (0.2 μm pore size, Titan3™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States) and injected on a gas chromatograph (Nexis GC-2030, Shimadzu, Japan) coupled to a single quadrupole mass detector (GCMS-QP2020 NX, Shimadzu, Japan). The GC-MS was equipped with an SH-I-5MS column (Shimadzu, Japan). GC-MS grade n-Hexane was used as a solvent blank.

Samples (3 µL) were injected in split mode (split ratio: 10), at 50 µL/s rate while the injector temperature was set to 250 °C. Elution was carried out with Helium 5.0 (1.01 mL/min, 53.9 kPa). Initial and final oven temperatures were adjusted, respectively, to 50 °C (1 min) and 300 °C (5 min). The corresponding heating rate was set to 5 °C/min. The mass detector interphase and ion source temperature were fixed, correspondingly, to 250 °C. The ion source operated on electron impact mode (70 eV) and the mass analyzer, at a 10–450 Da mass scan range, with a 1666 Da/s scan speed.

GC-MS data processing, metabolites spectral annotation and molecular networking

Et2O-F, Et2O-S and solvent blank (n-Hexane) vendor chromatographic-mass spectra files (.qgd) were initially converted to open format files (.mzML), with OpenChrom Lablicate Edition (McLafferty) (RRID: SCR_026227, Version 1.5.0, Lablicate GmbH, Germany). These were uploaded to MassIVE FTP server (RRID: SCR_013665, massive.ucsd.edu) using WinSCP (RRID: SCR_026226, Version 6.3.4.) (See MassIVE dataset identifiers, Supplementary Information, Table S4).

Using the Global Natural Product Social Molecular Networking (GNPS, RRID: SCR_019012, Version 1.3.16) GC-MS data analysis environment, Et2O-F and Et2O-S raw spectral data were deconvoluted by the MS-Hub algorithm processing workflow. Resulting deconvoluted spectra and quantification files (.mgf and csv., respectively) were submitted to the Library Search/Molecular Networking GC workflow. Search parameters were set as follows: a Fragment Ion Mass Tolerance of ± 1 Da, 6 Min Matched Peaks for spectral annotation (i.e. workflow’s default) and a Score threshold of 0.7 (i.e. cosine score). Furthermore, under advanced search options, the Top Hits Per Spectrum parameter was set to 10, to increase the probability of achieving a correct annotation, as suggested by the workflow´s tutorial documentation125. Additional processing options were kept as default. Upon completion of the Library Search/Molecular Networking workflow, Cytoscape (RRID: SCR_003032, Version 3.10.2) was used to visualize the resulting molecular network files (.graphml).

Given that the GC-MS MS-Hub deconvolution workflow does not support peak selection under user-specified parameters, a detected and blank-subtracted peak list was generated with the MestReNova suite (Version 14.2.0–26256.0, Mestrelab Research S.L., Spain) to ensure consistency in retaining or removing nodes (i.e., chromatographic features representation) from the Et2O-F and Et2O-S molecular networks. In brief, peaks were detected using the Automatic Peak Detection tool, disabling the detection of Wide Peaks, and setting a Minimum Area Threshold of 0.04% and a Sensitivity level of 50. Following a thorough side-by-side comparison of retention times and mass fragmentation spectra, all peaks arising from chromatographic features found in the solvent blank were manually removed from each extract’s molecular networks (See Overlay comparison of GC-MS chromatograms for solvent blank, flower and seed extracts, Supplementary Information, Figures S13-14). Similarly, peaks showing an area below the given threshold were also removed.

As part of the networks’ data curation process, IUPAC names and InChI identifiers for the remaining nodes were verified and/or added using the online databases PubChem (RRID: SCR_004284) and NIST Chemistry WebBook (RRID: SCR_026225). Additionally, a chemical taxonomical classification was assigned using the ClassyFire web-based application (RRID: SCR_026224, Version 1.0).

For the molecular network layout, node sizes were assigned proportionally to summed peak intensity associated with each node, offering a visual representation of relative abundance. Node shapes were designated based on their cosine scores, while their fill colors reflected their chemical taxonomical classifications. Moreover, the color intensity of all nodes’ edges was dependent on their corresponding edge score values (i.e., similarity between nodes’ mass spectra). All nodes and edges legends were created with the Legend Creator Cytoscape App (V1.1.7).

Finally, nodes corresponding to specialized metabolites whose antibacterial activity had been previously evaluated as isolated compounds, and whose reports have been described in literature, were highlighted from all annotated metabolites.
