The burden of suicide by paraquat poisoning in Taiwan (1987–2017): trends, geographic variations, and demographic characteristics
Authors: Hwei Yuen Chang, Chao-Ying Tu, Chien-Yu Lin, Chia-Yueh Hsu, Tzung-Hai Yen, David Gunnell, Michael Eddleston, Shu-Sen Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPesticide self-poisoning accounts for 14%–20% of global suicides. Paraquat, a highly lethal pesticide, is involved in a considerable number of suicides in some countries. This study investigated the burden, trends, geographic variations, and demographic characteristics of pesticide and paraquat suicides in Taiwan.MethodsSuicide data (1987, 1992, 1997, and 2002–2017) were obtained from Taiwan’s cause-of-death mortality data files. Trends and geographic variations in pesticide and paraquat suicides were examined using graphs and maps. Logistic regression was used to investigate the differences in the characteristics of pesticide versus non-pesticide suicides and paraquat versus non-paraquat pesticide suicides.ResultsAge-standardised pesticide suicide rates decreased by 74%, from 7.7 to 2.0 per 100,000, in 1987–2017. Paraquat was the leading agent involved in pesticide suicides, accounting for 59.9% (2,268/3,786) of pesticide suicides where the pesticide agents involved were recorded on the death certificate and at least 4.0% (2,268/56,361) of overall suicides in Taiwan. The percentage of paraquat suicides in overall suicides varied markedly across cities/counties (0.4%–15.6%). Pesticide suicides were more likely to be male, older people aged 65 + years, and from rural areas than non-pesticide suicides. By contrast, paraquat suicides tended to be younger than non-paraquat pesticide suicides, with no difference in sex distributions in the two groups.ConclusionsThe ban on paraquat implemented in 2018–2020 would have a significant impact on pesticide suicides and overall suicides, particularly in high-risk areas and demographic groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-29134-x.Keywords: Paraquat, Pesticide suicide, Pesticide ban, Suicide prevention, Taiwan

Background

Pesticide self-poisoning is a significant public health issue worldwide, accounting for 14%–20% of global suicides, or 110,000–168,000 deaths, annually [1]. Past research suggested marked variations in pesticide suicide rates over time [2, 3], across different regions within a country [4–6], and across individuals of various demographic and socioeconomic characteristics [7]. Globally, the majority of pesticide suicides occur in the rural areas of countries in South East Asia and the Western Pacific region [1, 8]. Pesticide self-poisoning is also among the most commonly used methods for suicide in Taiwan, with pesticides being the leading agents involved in poisoning-related suicides [9].

Pesticides commonly used in suicide vary markedly in case fatality [10]. Paraquat, a commonly used herbicide, is among the most lethal pesticides involved in self-poisonings [11], with a case-fatality ratio exceeding 70% in some studies [12, 13]. Paraquat was found to be the most frequently ingested pesticide in South Korea and Taiwan, accounting for 35.5% and 34.7% of pesticide poisonings in previous hospital-based studies in South Korea (2008) and Taiwan (2012–2019), respectively [4, 14]. However, few previous studies investigated the epidemiology of paraquat suicides based on population-based nationwide samples.

Product-specific analyses of the characteristics of people who died by pesticide self-poisoning could inform prevention strategies targeted at regulating certain highly hazardous pesticides (HHPs), such as paraquat, and regulating or banning HHPs was recommended by the World Health Organization and Food and Agriculture Organization as among the most effective suicide prevention strategies [15, 16]. In South Korea, for instance, the age-standardised rate of pesticide suicide fell annually by 28.2% between 2011 and 2014 following the ban on paraquat in 2011–2012 [2]. A systematic review of 27 studies from 16 countries showed that national bans on HHPs commonly used in self-poisonings appeared to be effective in reducing pesticide-specific and overall suicide rates, whilst the evidence for an effect of restricting HHPs sales to authorised users was less consistent [17].

In Taiwan, previous studies showed that suicide by pesticide poisoning decreased markedly (67%) between 1987 and 2010 [18], and pesticide suicide rates were greater in rural areas with a higher percentage of agricultural workers [19] and in males and older people [18] than their counterparts. The reduction in pesticide suicide rates in 1987–2010 was thought to be linked to the decrease in the agricultural population, but not to the bans of a number of pesticides during this period [18], as these pesticide bans did not include major pesticides found to be involved in pesticide suicides, such as paraquat [20]. Paraquat (24% soluble concentrate) was later banned in Taiwan in 2018–2020 in two stages—the import and production of paraquat were first banned in February 2018, and the sale and use of paraquat were completely banned in February 2020. However, there have been no previous national studies on the epidemiology of paraquat suicide, including its trends and geographic variations and the characteristics of people who died by paraquat self-poisoning, prior to the nationwide paraquat ban in Taiwan.

Before implementing a pesticide ban aimed at reducing suicides, it is essential to identify the leading agent contributing to pesticide suicides and its burden. Additionally, the pre-ban burden of paraquat suicides across different sociodemographic groups and geographical areas is the basis for estimating the impact of a paraquat ban on suicide prevention. This study aimed to examine the burden of suicides by pesticide and paraquat poisoning before the paraquat ban (2018–2020) in Taiwan. Specifically, we investigated the contribution of paraquat self-poisoning to pesticide suicides in Taiwan, as well as the trends, geographic variations, and characteristics of people who died by pesticide self-poisoning, in particular paraquat self-poisoning, in 1987–2017.

Methods

Data

We extracted suicide data for people aged 15 years or over from Taiwan’s cause-of-death data files, coded using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), for years 1987, 1992, 1997, and 2002–2017. The ICD-10 includes a specific 3-digit code (i.e., ICD-10 X68) for suicide by pesticide poisoning. By contrast, in the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9), suicides by pesticide poisoning were grouped with suicides using other solid or liquid substances under the 3-digit ICD-9 code E950. Taiwan officially started to use the ICD-10 in 2009, allowing the identification of pesticide suicides. To identify suicide by pesticide poisoning before 2009, we obtained ICD-10 coded cause-of-death data files for 1987, 1992, 1997, and 2002–2008 from the Ministry of Health and Welfare; these data were specially created for the preparation of the transition from the ICD-9 to the ICD-10; a previous study showed that the change in ICD revisions had little impact on the total number of suicide [21].

In this study, suicides were identified using the following ICD-10 codes: X60–X84 (suicide), Y10–Y34 (undetermined death), W75–W76 and W83–W84 (accident by suffocation), and X48 (accident by pesticide poisoning). A previous study from Taiwan showed that many of the deaths in the above alternative cause-of-death categories (i.e., undetermined death, accident by suffocation, and accident by pesticide poisoning) were likely to be misclassified suicides [22]. For the rest of the paper, the term ‘suicide’ referred to both certified suicides and possible suicides (undetermined deaths, accidental suffocation, or accidental pesticide poisoning) combined. Suicides by pesticide poisoning (‘pesticide suicides’) were identified using ICD-10 68 (suicide), Y18 (undetermined death), and X48 (accident); all other suicides were classified as ‘non-pesticide suicides’.

The following variables were extracted from the cause-of-death data files: sex, age, date of death, and the city/county of registered residence. Age was grouped into four categories (15–24, 25–44, 45–64, and 65 + years). Individuals aged 15–24 years generally represent those who have recently entered the labour market, those aged 25–44 and 45–64 years correspond to individuals in the early and late stages of career development, respectively, and those aged 65 years and over are mostly retired. Based on the date of death, the season variable was created with the following categories–spring (March through May), summer (June through August), autumn (September through November), and winter (December through February). Geographic analyses were conducted at the city/county level, which corresponds to conventional administrative boundaries in Taiwan. More detailed geographic information was not available for pesticide suicide data. Taiwan’s 23 cities/counties were grouped according to the tertiles of the percentage of agricultural workers in the working population—as an indicator of rurality and ease of access to pesticides—resulting in three categories: urban (low percentage; 0.19%–1.16%), semi-rural (medium percentage; 1.21%–7.66%), and rural (high percentage; 9.31%–26.94%). Data for the percentage of agricultural workers were from the 2000 census.

Among pesticide suicides, we extracted information on the pesticides involved from the original free text in the cause-of-death field on the death certificate. We grouped the pesticides according to the classification used by Taiwan’s Council of Agriculture, which used a matrix classifying each pesticide component based on the purpose of use (e.g., herbicide, insecticide, fungicide, rodenticide, etc.) and the chemical structure (e.g., organophosphate, carbamate, etc.) of the pesticides. An 11-category variable was created for the classification of pesticides commonly involved in suicides in Taiwan (Table S1 and Table S2); they included four groups of herbicides (paraquat, glyphosate, other herbicides, unspecified herbicides), four groups of insecticides (organophosphate, carbamate, other insecticides, unspecified insecticides), rodenticides, other pesticides, and ‘unspecified pesticides’ (i.e., the specific pesticides involved were not recorded on the death certificate although pesticide poisoning was certified as the cause of death). The ‘unspecified pesticides’ group was not included in the main analysis concerning the number and percentage of suicides involving different categories of pesticides. Detailed data for the cause-of-death free text on the death certificate were only available for the years 1987, 1992, 1997, and 2002–2012. By contrast, in 2013–2017, detailed data on the pesticide agents involved in suicide were not provided by the Ministry of Health and Welfare, although the number of paraquat pesticides and their percentage in all suicides could be calculated based on data provided by the Ministry of Health and Welfare, which compiled the data for the number of suicides by paraquat poisoning by year, sex, age (in 5-year bands), and city/county as part of its surveillance system to support suicide prevention strategies and monitor trends in paraquat suicide.

Population data were extracted from the Demographic Fact Books (1987–2017) published by the Ministry of the Interior, Taiwan.

Statistical analysis

We calculated the age-standardised rates of pesticide suicide in each year using the World Health Organization (WHO) 2000 world standard population. The percentage of paraquat suicides (i.e., pesticide suicides by paraquat poisoning) in all pesticide suicides with known pesticide agents was calculated by year in 1987, 1992, 1997, 2002–2012, and its trend was examined graphically. The annual number and percentage of pesticide suicides using other categories of pesticides were also calculated.

We calculated the number and percentage of pesticide suicides and paraquat suicides, overall and by sex, age group, and city/county with different levels of the percentage of agricultural workers in 2002–2017. Maps generated with ArcGIS Version 10.4 were used to examine the geographic variations in the rate and percentage (in all suicides) of pesticide suicides and paraquat suicides across the 23 cities/counties in Taiwan. We used Spearman’s correlation coefficients to investigate the correlation between the rates of pesticide and paraquat suicides and the percentage of agricultural workers in the working population across cities/counties.

We used chi-square tests to compare the characteristics between pesticide and non-pesticide suicides, as well as between pesticide suicides involving paraquat (‘paraquat suicides’) and those involving other non-paraquat pesticides (‘non-paraquat pesticide suicides’). We did not include suicides by self-poisoning using unspecified pesticides (n = 4927) and unspecified herbicides (n = 192) in the categories of either paraquat suicides or non-paraquat pesticide suicides due to a lack of information on the specific pesticide agents involved to make such a distinction. Logistic regression analyses were used to investigate the association of demographic characteristics, season, and geographic area (grouped according to the percentage of agricultural workers) with pesticide suicide (versus non-pesticide suicide [reference group]) or paraquat suicide (versus non-paraquat pesticide suicide [reference group]). In subsequent multivariable analyses, we simultaneously adjusted for sex, age group, season, and geographic area to investigate whether these factors were independently associated with pesticide suicide or paraquat suicide.

We conducted sensitivity analyses to examine the potential impact of suicide misclassification on the results by including data for certified suicides only (ICD-10 X60–X84) and excluding deaths of undetermined intent, accidental suffocation, or accidental pesticide poisoning.

The comparison between paraquat and non-paraquat pesticide suicides was restricted to the years (1987, 1992, 1997, and 2002–2012) when detailed information about the pesticide involved in suicides was available; suicides using unspecified pesticides or unspecified herbicides were excluded from the analysis.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding two cities/counties (i.e., Taichung City and Taichung County) from the comparison due to the high percentage of unspecified pesticide suicides (85.0% and 76.7%, respectively), indicating relatively low data quality in the two cities/counties.

Because suicides using unspecified pesticides accounted for a substantial proportion (56.5%) of overall pesticide suicides, we conducted supplementary analyses to examine whether the city’s or county’s percentage of suicides using unspecified pesticides was associated with the proportion of paraquat suicides among specified pesticide suicides and the rate of paraquat suicides in the city/country, indicating differential certification practices in recording paraquat as the causative agent across cities/counties. Scatter plots and Spearman’s correlation coefficients were used to examine the association between the percentage of unspecified pesticide suicides and (1) the proportion of paraquat suicides among specified pesticide suicides and (2) the age-standardised paraquat suicide rate across cities/counties.

All analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 19. We report p-values and 95% confidence intervals; a p-value < 0.05 was used as a conventional reference point for evaluating the strength of evidence.

Results

Trends and burdens of pesticide suicide and paraquat suicide

Age-standardised pesticide suicide rates decreased markedly from 7.7 per 100,000 in 1987 to 2.0 per 100,000 in 2017, i.e., a 74% reduction, while the percentage of paraquat suicides among pesticide suicides remained stable (Fig. 1). This suggests that the decline was due to factors affecting all pesticides (e.g., decreased accessibility as the farming population decreased) rather than factors specific to paraquat.

Organophosphates and carbamates were the second and third most common categories of pesticides involved in pesticide suicides. The percentage of organophosphates decreased from 24.0% in 1987 to 11.1% in 2012, while the percentage of carbamates increased from 0.3% to 13.5% during the period (Table 1). Overall, organophosphates accounted for 19.8% (748/3,786) of pesticide suicides with known pesticide agents, followed by carbamates (8.1%; n = 305), other/unspecified herbicides (5.1%; n = 193), other/unspecified insecticides (4.2%; n = 158), and glyphosate (2.3%; n = 86). Pesticide suicides involving other categories of pesticides were less common.

The percentage of pesticide suicides using unspecified pesticides was shown in Table S2; they were the largest group in each year, although its percentage decreased from 65.8% in 1987 to 40.2% in 2012, suggesting improved data quality and completeness over time. Supplementary analyses showed no statistical evidence for a correlation between the percentage of unspecified pesticide suicides and the proportion of paraquat suicides among specified pesticide suicides (Spearman’s correlation coefficients = -0.064, p = 0.77) or the age-standardised paraquat suicide rate (Spearman’s correlation coefficients = -0.364, p = 0.088) (Figure S1), indicating no differential practices of recording paraquat as the causative agent involved in pesticide suicides across cities/counties.

Among a total of 70,039 suicides, there were 9,006 pesticide suicides and 2,869 paraquat suicides in Taiwan between 2002 and 2017 (Table 2). Pesticide and paraquat suicides accounted for 12.9% and 4.1% of total suicides (2002–2017), respectively. The percentage of pesticide and paraquat suicides in all suicides was higher in males (versus females) and highest in the older group aged 65 + years and rural areas, where pesticide poisoning accounted for one in four suicides (24.4%) and paraquat poisoning accounted for one in twelve suicides (8.1%), respectively. The sensitivity analysis using certified suicides only showed similar results (Table S3).

The geographical distribution of pesticide suicides and paraquat suicides

Figure 2 shows the maps of the age-standardised rates and percentages of pesticide and paraquat suicides across 23 cities/counties in Taiwan in 2002–2017. Detailed data for each city/county were presented in Table S4. For both pesticide and paraquat suicides, the highest rates and percentages were found in counties in Central and East Taiwan, whilst the lowest rates and percentages were found in major cities across Taiwan (Fig. 2). There were 23-fold and 62-fold variations in pesticide and paraquat suicide rates across cities/counties, respectively (ranged 0.35–7.95 per 100,000 for pesticide suicide and 0.06–3.84 per 100,000 for paraquat suicide). The percentages of pesticide suicides in all suicides ranged 2.4%–32.6% and those for paraquat suicides ranged 0.4%–15.6% across cities/counties (Table S4). There was a strong positive association of the percentages of agricultural workers with the rates of pesticide suicides (Spearman’s correlation coefficients = 0.76, p < 0.001) and paraquat suicides (Spearman’s correlation coefficients = 0.68, p < 0.001), respectively.

Table 3 shows the comparison of characteristics between pesticide and non-pesticide suicides. The characteristics of paraquat suicides and non-paraquat pesticide suicides were generally similar, except that paraquat suicides were younger compared to non-paraquat pesticide suicides (aOR = 2.43 [95% CI 1.71–3.44] for 15–24-year-olds and 2.00 [95% CI 1.66–2.40] for 25–44-year-olds; Table 4). There was no difference in the distributions of sex or season between the two groups, but paraquat suicides were marginally less likely to be from rural areas (aOR = 0.84 [95%CI 0.71–1.01] versus urban areas). The sensitivity analysis using certified suicides only showed similar results (Table S6). Additionally, in the sensitivity analysis that excluded two cities/counties (Taichung City and Taichung County) with relatively low data quality (indicated by a high proportion of pesticide suicides lacking the information on the pesticide agents involved [Table S4]), the results were similar. However, the lower odds of paraquat suicides occurring in rural areas, compared to non-paraquat pesticide suicides, became more pronounced (aOR = 0.81 [95% CI 0.68–0.97]) (Table S7).

Discussion

The annual age-standardised pesticide suicide rates decreased by 74% from 7.7 to 2.0 per 100,000 in 1987–2017. In years when detailed data were available (1987, 1992, 1997, and 2002–2012), paraquat was the leading agent involved in pesticide suicides, accounting for approximately 60% of pesticide suicides for which the information on the specific pesticide agents involved was available. It was estimated that paraquat accounted for 4.0%–9.3% of all suicides in Taiwan. Geographically, the percentage of paraquat suicides in all suicides varied markedly (0.4%–15.6%) across cities/counties. Compared to non-pesticide suicides, pesticide suicides were more likely to be male, from the older group aged 65 + years, and from rural areas. By contrast, paraquat suicides were younger than non-paraquat pesticide suicides, with no difference in the distributions of sex and season in the two groups.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including its use of data from the cause-of-death mortality data files that allow a comprehensive and countrywide assessment of suicides by pesticide self-poisoning. Access to detailed information on the pesticides used in self-poisoning, as recorded on the death certificates, enabled us to investigate the categories of pesticides commonly involved in pesticide suicides. However, there are several limitations of the study. First, this study employed an ecological design and primarily descriptive analyses to examine long-term temporal patterns using annual data, rather than applying more advanced methods, such as regression modelling, or utilising finer-grained data, such as monthly records, which were not available. Therefore, causal inferences regarding the relationship between pesticide availability and suicide cannot be drawn from the findings. Second, suicide data were obtained from cause-of-death mortality files derived from death certificates, which are not designed for detailed analysis of the specific pesticides involved in suicide. Under Taiwanese law, any suspected unnatural death (e.g., accident, suicide, or homicide) must be referred to the prosecutor’s office for a medico-legal investigation. However, autopsies and toxicological examinations are not routinely conducted and are typically reserved for cases with uncertain causes of death or suspected homicide. In suicide cases, the cause of death is generally determined based on interviews with family members, evidence collected at the scene, and relevant documentation, such as medical records, when available. In addition, Taiwan’s cause-of-death data files are coded using ICD-10, which does not allow the recording of detailed information on the pesticides involved. Consequently, there is a lack of systematic data collection regarding the specific pesticides used in pesticide-related suicides. A substantial proportion of the agents involved in pesticide suicides were unspecified during the study period, particularly in the earlier years. Although the high lethality of paraquat poisoning may reduce the likelihood of underreporting compared with other pesticides, the lack of comprehensive autopsy and toxicological examinations means that some suicides recorded as involving unspecified pesticides may in fact have involved paraquat. Conversely, it is possible that paraquat, which is better known to the public in Taiwan as a pesticide used in self-poisoning than other pesticides, may have been more likely to be reported than other pesticides. However, although the proportion of unspecified pesticides decreased over time, the percentage of paraquat suicides amongst those deaths that involved a specified pesticide remained essentially unchanged during the study period. In addition, the lack of statistical association between the percentage of unspecified pesticide suicides and the proportion of paraquat suicides in specified pesticide suicides across cities and counties suggests that the practice of recording paraquat as the causative agent involved in pesticide suicides is similar across geographical areas. Future studies based on comprehensive information about the pesticides involved, such as those based on forensic toxicology records [23], would allow a more detailed investigation of specific pesticides used in self-poisoning. Third, our ability to assess annual trends is constrained by the availability of pesticide suicide data only in selected years before 2002, and by the lack of available paraquat suicide data from 2012 to 2017, which limits evaluation of paraquat’s contribution to total pesticide suicides. Fourth, we categorised Taiwan’s administrative areas into tertiles based on the percentage of agricultural workers as a proxy for rurality. While this allows for within-Taiwan comparisons, caution is warranted when making cross-territory comparisons. Fifth, official mortality statistics often underestimate suicide incidence [24]. Although cause‑of‑death coding for injuries and poisonings in Taiwan has high accuracy [25], some deaths officially recorded as undetermined intent or accidental may in fact be suicides [22]. Therefore, our primary analyses also included deaths of undetermined intent, accidental pesticide poisoning, and accidental suffocation [22]. Sixth, the study examined the epidemiology of pesticide suicides in Taiwan in 1987–2017 only. The impact of the 2018–2020 paraquat ban was not assessed in this study and has been reported elsewhere [26]. Lastly, the analysis was completed after paraquat had already been banned in Taiwan, which would limit its impact on domestic regulatory policies. However, we believe the findings remain highly valuable for informing pesticide regulation in other countries, with the potential to prevent deaths from self-poisoning with paraquat and other highly hazardous pesticides globally.

Comparison with previous studies

Our study showed a 74% decrease in pesticide suicide rates in Taiwan over three decades (1987–2017), whereas overall suicide increased by 15% (age-standardised rate of certified suicide increased from 10.9 to 12.5 per 100,000) during the same period [27]. Changes in trends in pesticide suicide rates were found to be associated with certain sociodemographic and pesticide accessibility (e.g., the percentage of agricultural population) factors [2, 18, 28]. Additionally, pesticide suicides decreased after restricting access to highly hazardous pesticides in several countries [17]. A previous study indicated that the reduction in pesticide suicides in Taiwan from 1987 to 2010 was mainly associated with the decrease in the agricultural population, which had easier access to pesticides than their counterpart [18]. During this period, bans were imposed on several pesticides, including selected high-strength formulations of organophosphates and carbamates, but not on the leading pesticide involved in suicide (i.e., paraquat). Bans implemented during this period were not found to be associated with a reduction in pesticide suicides. By contrast, studies from other countries showed a reduction in pesticide suicide following bans on high-lethality pesticides commonly used in self-poisoning. In South Korea, pesticide suicide rates declined markedly following the 2011–2012 paraquat ban [2, 28, 29]. Deaths from pesticide poisoning were on the rise in Japan before 1986 [30] but showed a marked 92% reduction from 1986 to 2019, following its policy to de-register high-concentration paraquat products [31]. In low- and middle-income Asian countries, such as Sri Lanka, India, Bangladesh, and China, where pesticide suicide constituted a larger proportion of total suicides, deaths from pesticide poisoning have declined markedly over the last 10–20 years. Evidence suggests that bans on highly hazardous pesticides contributed to reductions in both pesticide and overall suicides [32–35]. The WHO now considers pesticide regulation to be the most effective approach to suicide prevention in countries and areas where pesticides are an important means of suicide [15].

Paraquat emerged as the predominant agent involved in pesticide suicides in Taiwan over the study period, accounting for approximately 60% of all pesticide suicides involving a known pesticide agent; the corresponding figure was 26% if pesticide suicides where the pesticide agents involved were not specified were included and assumed to be not paraquat, thus resulting in a possible underestimation. Earlier epidemiologic data (1985–1993) from the Taiwan National Poison Center indicated that paraquat was responsible for 54.4% of poison-related fatalities [36]. In a later study based on suicide data for 2006–2008 in Taiwan, paraquat was the leading pesticide involved in pesticide suicides, accounting for 28.5% (= 471/1651) of all cases [20]. Similar to Taiwan, paraquat is the main product responsible for pesticide poisoning deaths in some countries. Paraquat accounts for 16.3% of poisoning deaths in Australia [7], approximately one-third of fatal poisonings involving known pesticides in Sri Lanka [10], where paraquat has been banned since 2008–2011, and 71.2% of pesticide poisoning deaths in South Korea in 2011 [37], prior to the complete ban of paraquat in 2012. In these countries, prevention strategies targeting paraquat, e.g., a national ban, would contribute to a decrease in paraquat suicides, and pesticide suicides as a whole.

We found a marked geographical difference in paraquat suicide rates within Taiwan, with the highest rate (3.84 per 100,000) being 62 times greater than the lowest rate (0.06 per 100,000) across cities/counties. In addition, there was a notable disparity in the proportion of paraquat suicides among all suicides between urban areas and rural areas, ranging from 0.4% to 15.6%. Similar findings regarding the urban-rural disparity in pesticide suicide rates have been reported in South Korea [38] and China [5, 39]. The high pesticide suicide rates in rural areas could be associated with easy access to pesticides, lower quality of medical service, an ageing population (with physical vulnerability and a tendency to use immediately available lethal means), social isolation, socioeconomic disadvantages, and attitudes towards seeking help for mental illnesses [38, 40–43]. The high correlation between the percentage of agricultural workers and the pesticide suicide rate shown in this study suggested that the accessibility of pesticides influenced pesticide suicide rates. Similarly, previous research reported an association between the percentage of the agriculture workforce and regional pesticide suicide rates even after controlling for socioeconomic disadvantage indicators [19].

Males and older people had a greater burden of pesticide suicides and paraquat suicides compared to their counterparts. The sex- and age-specific patterns of pesticide suicides in Taiwan were similar to those observed in South Korea [38], Australia [7], and Germany [44], where males and older people constituted the populations most affected. By contrast, individuals who died by pesticide self-poisoning on the Greek island of Crete consisted mainly of middle-aged males [45]. In China, pesticide suicide was previously found to be most prevalent among young females [46]; however, there was a more pronounced decline in pesticide suicides among females than males over the last two decades [5, 47, 48]. Compared to low-and middle-income countries, industrialised countries such as Taiwan have an ageing rural population. The higher likelihood of older people dying from pesticide self-poisoning (compared to their younger counterparts) in this study, relative to other suicide methods, could be attributable to the higher accessibility to pesticides and case fatality ratio of pesticide poisoning among older people living in rural areas. The higher male-to-female ratio in pesticide suicides, compared to non-pesticide suicides, is consistent with our previous findings [14]. In addition to the propensity of males to choose methods of higher lethality [49], the male predominance of pesticide self-poisoning in Taiwan could also be attributable to males’ higher agricultural participation than their female counterparts, which led to higher accessibility to pesticides [50].

Our study found no difference in the distributions of sex and season between paraquat suicide and non-paraquat pesticide suicide. However, individuals who died by paraquat self-poisoning tended to be younger than those who died from non-paraquat pesticide self-poisoning. Similarly, a recent study from Taiwan found that, among patients who presented to hospitals with pesticide self-poisoning, those who ingested paraquat were younger and had a higher education level than those ingesting other pesticides [14]. By contrast, a recent hospital-based study from Malaysia did not find an association of sex and age with the types of pesticide involved in pesticide self-poisonings [51]. Our finding of similar sociodemographic profiles, except for the age pattern, in paraquat suicides and non-paraquat pesticide suicides suggested that most people who died by pesticide self-poisoning might turn to use pesticides for self-harm because of easy access to pesticides. The high case fatality ratio of paraquat poisoning, even with small ingested volumes, which is common among young people with low suicidal intent [52], could explain the relative preponderance of young individuals in paraquat suicides compared to non-paraquat pesticide suicides. Another possible explanation for the younger age of paraquat suicides is that younger people’s choice of pesticides for self-poisoning is more likely to be influenced by the ‘cognitive availability’ —that is, their awareness or knowledge of it as a means of suicide [53]. As paraquat has been the most commonly used pesticide in self-poisoning and received more media coverage than other pesticides in Taiwan, it is likely to be more prominent in the minds of younger people when considering suicide methods. The higher odds of paraquat suicide compared to non-paraquat pesticide suicide in urban areas may also be explained by the greater ‘cognitive availability’ of paraquat relative to other pesticides among people living in these areas, as urban residents might be more aware of paraquat ingestion as a means for suicide than the ingestion of other pesticides. However, the hypothesis needs support from future studies that investigate the reasons why individuals who self-poison using pesticides choose specific pesticide agents.

Conclusions

Paraquat was the leading pesticide involved in suicides in Taiwan, accounting for 59.9% of pesticide suicides where the specific pesticide agents involved could be identified. Paraquat accounted for at least 4.0% of all suicides in the pre-ban period. The percentage of paraquat suicides among all suicides showed marked geographical variations and accounted for a significant proportion (approximately 16%) of all suicides in counties with the highest proportion of agricultural population. The burden of paraquat suicides was higher among males, individuals aged 65 + years, and rural areas than their counterparts. The findings indicated the potential of the paraquat ban as an effective approach to preventing suicide, particularly in high-risk areas and demographic groups.
