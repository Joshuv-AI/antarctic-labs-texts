Lifetime Management in Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation: Alignment, Coronary Protection and Redo Strategies are No Longer Optional
Authors: Ignacio Jesús Amat Santos
Published: 2026

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has undergone a remarkable evolution over the past two decades. Initially developed for inoperable and high-risk patients, it is now routinely performed across the entire surgical risk spectrum and increasingly in younger patients with longer life expectancy.1,2 As the treated population changes, so too must our procedural mindset. The success of TAVI should no longer be measured solely by procedural safety or short-term valve performance, but by how well the initial procedure preserves future therapeutic options. In this context, lifetime management has emerged as the central paradigm in contemporary TAVI practice.3,4

The three manuscripts presented in this special collection address key components of this evolving paradigm: commissural and coronary alignment, coronary obstruction prevention through electrosurgical leaflet modification and redo TAVI strategies.5–7 Although these topics may appear technical and procedural, they are in fact deeply interconnected and represent three pillars of lifetime TAVI management. Together, they underscore a fundamental shift in the field – from procedural success to long-term planning. However, despite the growing body of evidence and the increasing awareness of these issues, a significant number of operators continue to perform TAVI without adequately considering commissural alignment, future coronary access or the feasibility of redo TAVI. In the contemporary era, such an approach is difficult to justify and may ultimately compromise patient outcomes. Implanting a transcatheter valve without considering lifetime management is no longer simply a technical omission; it represents a failure to provide optimal contemporary care (Figure 1).

Commissural and Coronary Alignment: Planning for Future Coronary Access

In this collection, Fernandez-Cordón and Amat-Santos highlight how commissural alignment has emerged as a critical concept in TAVI, particularly in younger patients who are likely to require future coronary angiography or reintervention.5 Historically, the orientation of transcatheter valves during implantation was largely random, in contrast to surgical valve replacement, where commissural alignment is achieved under direct visualisation. However, growing evidence suggests that misalignment between native commissures and prosthetic commissural posts may have important clinical implications, particularly regarding coronary re-access and possibly valve haemodynamics and durability.8–11

More recently, our group described the concept of coronary alignment, which focuses on positioning the prosthetic commissural posts away from the coronary ostia to minimise the risk of coronary overlap and facilitate future coronary cannulation.8 This distinction is important because optimal commissural alignment does not always guarantee optimal coronary alignment, particularly in patients with asymmetric cusps or eccentric coronary ostia. Bicuspid valve disease represents an increasingly important example of this challenge because, as younger patients are treated with TAVI, its prevalence within the treated population continues to rise. In such anatomies, prioritising coronary alignment may be more relevant than achieving perfect commissural alignment.

Beyond coronary access, commissural misalignment has been associated in experimental and observational studies with altered flow dynamics in the aortic root, increased leaflet stress and potentially higher transvalvular gradients and aortic regurgitation. However, despite these mechanistic observations, the clinical impact of commissural alignment on long-term valve durability remains uncertain and long-term outcome data are still limited.8–10 This uncertainty should not lead to inaction, but rather to a more thoughtful and individualised procedural strategy.

From a practical standpoint, alignment should be viewed not merely as a technical refinement but as a strategy to preserve coronary access and facilitate future interventions. Importantly, alignment is influenced not only by implantation technique but also by device design, frame height, cell geometry and implantation depth. Thus, achieving alignment requires a comprehensive understanding of both anatomy and device-specific behaviour, further highlighting the importance of meticulous pre-procedural planning. In patients with a long life expectancy, failure to consider alignment is becoming increasingly difficult to justify in light of current evidence.

Coronary Protection and the BASILICA Technique: From Bailout to Preventive Strategy

Coronary obstruction remains one of the most feared complications of TAVI because, although rare, it is associated with extremely high mortality.12,13 The risk is particularly relevant in valve-in-valve and redo TAVI procedures, where displaced leaflets from the initial prosthesis may obstruct the coronary ostia after implantation of the second valve. These scenarios are becoming more common as TAVI expands to younger populations and redo procedures increase.

Historically, chimney or snorkel stenting has been used as a bailout technique to preserve coronary flow. However, this approach carries significant long-term concerns, including stent thrombosis, restenosis and challenges in future coronary re-access. In this context, the BASILICA technique – or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to Prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery Obstruction – has emerged as an effective preventive strategy.12 By intentionally lacerating the leaflet at risk of obstructing the coronary ostium, BASILICA allows the leaflet to splay open during valve deployment, preserving coronary flow.

In this collection, Gill and Khan explain how the development and increasing adoption of BASILICA reflect an important conceptual shift in TAVI: the move toward preventive structural intervention guided by detailed anatomical assessment.6 Pre-procedural computed tomography plays a central role in identifying patients at high risk of coronary obstruction, using parameters such as coronary ostial height, leaflet length, virtual valve-to-coronary distance and sinotubular junction dimensions.12–14 These measurements allow operators to predict the risk of coronary obstruction and plan preventive strategies accordingly.

However, BASILICA is a technically demanding procedure that requires specialised equipment, operator expertise and careful patient selection. Its increasing use raises broader questions about procedural complexity, operator training and the potential need for referral to specialised centres for complex structural interventions. Ultimately, the need for BASILICA reinforces the concept that the greatest risk in TAVI may not occur during the first procedure, but during subsequent interventions, particularly if the index procedure was performed without adequate planning.

Redo TAVI: the First Valve Is a Lifetime Decision

Fabris et al. discuss in this collection how redo TAVI – or TAV-in-TAV – is rapidly becoming more common.7 Although redo TAVI has been shown to be feasible and often safe, it presents unique technical challenges, particularly regarding coronary access, coronary obstruction, device sizing and valve positioning.15,16 These challenges underscore a critical concept: the first valve implanted determines the feasibility and safety of future interventions.

One of the key concepts in redo TAVI is the neoskirt, which is formed by the combination of the new valve skirt and the displaced leaflets of the original prosthesis. The height of the neoskirt relative to the coronary ostia and the sinotubular junction plays a central role in determining the risk of coronary obstruction and the feasibility of coronary re-access. Implantation depth of the second valve is, therefore, a crucial determinant of both haemodynamics and coronary accessibility.15

Device selection also plays a major role. Valve frame design, leaflet position (intra-annular versus supra-annular), radial force and cell geometry all influence the risk of coronary obstruction and the feasibility of future coronary cannulation. Pre-procedural CT imaging is essential for redo TAVI planning, allowing accurate measurement of the internal dimensions of the initial prosthesis and assessment of the relationship between the valve frame, coronary ostia and sinotubular junction.15,16

These considerations highlight the importance of detailed anatomical planning not only before redo TAVI, but also before the index procedure. Indeed, when implanting the first valve, operators should already be considering the potential need for a second valve in the future. Failure to do so may transform a feasible future procedure into an impossible one.

The Big Picture: the Era of Lifetime TAVI Planning

Taken together, the topics addressed in these three manuscripts illustrate a broader transformation in TAVI practice. TAVI is no longer a single procedure performed to treat aortic stenosis; it is the beginning of a lifelong management strategy for patients with structural heart disease.3,4

This shift has several important implications. First, pre-procedural imaging has become central to procedural planning, not only to determine valve size and access route, but also to evaluate coronary anatomy, commissural orientation, sinotubular junction dimensions and the feasibility of future coronary access and redo procedures. Second, device selection must be individualised based not only on annular dimensions and calcification but also on future coronary access and valve-in-valve considerations. Third, procedural techniques such as commissural alignment and BASILICA are no longer niche techniques reserved for rare cases, but essential tools in the armamentarium of modern structural heart operators.

Despite this, many TAVI procedures are still performed without systematic commissural alignment, without assessing future coronary access and without considering redo TAVI feasibility. In the current era, this approach is increasingly difficult to defend. When treating younger patients with long life expectancy, failing to consider lifetime management strategies may expose patients to preventable complications in the future, including coronary obstruction, impossible coronary access or technically unfeasible redo procedures. As such, lifetime management should not be considered an advanced concept reserved for specialised centres, but rather a fundamental principle of modern TAVI practice.

At the same time, these advances increase procedural complexity and require a higher level ofexpertise, imaging integration and multidisciplinary collaboration. The modern TAVI team must include not only interventional cardiologists but also imaging specialists, cardiac surgeons and structural heart experts capable of managing complex redo procedures and coronary protection strategies. Long-term data, standardised strategies and continued technological innovation will be essential to support this new paradigm.

Conclusion

The field of TAVI is undergoing a paradigm shift. In the early years, the primary goal was to perform a safe procedure and achieve immediate haemodynamic success. Today, the goal is broader and more complex: to ensure that the initial procedure preserves future treatment options, maintains coronary access and allows safe and effective redo interventions when needed.

Commissural and coronary alignment, coronary protection techniques, such as BASILICA, and careful planning for redo TAVI are not isolated technical considerations; they are interconnected components of a comprehensive lifetime management strategy. The three manuscripts presented in this issue highlight these critical aspects and emphasise that the success of TAVI should be measured not only by what happens in the catheterisation laboratory today, but by what remains possible for the patient tomorrow.

In the contemporary TAVI era, the most important part of the procedure may not be the valve we implant today, but the options we preserve for the future.
