Application of Machine Learning Models to Predict Tigecycline Exposure in Critically Ill Patients Using Different Limited Sampling Strategies
Authors: Anran Dai, Hao Liu, Bo Yang, Feiyue Zheng, Yan Guan, Jieqiong Liu, Jianping Zhu, Gang Liang, Yi Yang, Lingyan Yu, Yuhua Zhao, Zhenwei Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe high variability in the pharmacokinetics of tigecycline may impact therapeutic efficacy, particularly in critically ill patients. This study introduces machine learning (ML) methods, aiming to clarify their effectiveness in individualized drug exposure prediction and determine the optimal modeling strategy.MethodsThe least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) method was used to screen the covariates. Four ML models, random forest, extreme gradient boosting, multilayer perceptron, and long short-term memory network (LSTM), were trained and tested using 751 concentrations from 98 patients in the pharmacokinetic dataset to predict the area under the steady-state plasma concentration-time curve within 24 hours (AUC0-24h) of tigecycline. Furthermore, on the internal test set, we evaluated the accuracy of the published tigecycline Population Pharmacokinetics (PopPK) models.ResultsSeven covariates were screened out by LASSO method, including total bilirubin, creatinine clearance, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, body mass index, serum creatinine, age, and prothrombin time. Internal validation showed that, among the four ML models, the LSTM model performed the best, with the combination of covariates and the plasma concentration feature “C0 + C0.5 + C6” exhibited the optimal predictive performance (R2: 0.887, RMSE: 2.496 mg·h/L, F20: 75%). In comparison, the model combining covariates with C0 was the most concise and better suited to practical computational conditions; its LSTM model was comparable to some PopPK models in terms of overall trend fitting and systematic bias control (R2: 0.818, RMSE: 3.164 mg·h/L, F20 = 60%).ConclusionThe LSTM model established in this study enables an accurate estimate of tigecycline AUC0-24h. Three-point sampling provided the lowest prediction error, whereas C0 alone reduced the sampling burden. These findings provide an effective modeling reference for tigecycline therapeutic drug monitoring.Keywords: tigecycline, machine learning, trough concentration, area under curve, precision medicine

Introduction

Tigecycline, as a broad-spectrum glycylcycline antibiotic, holds significant importance in treating complicated intra-abdominal infections, skin and soft tissue infections, community-acquired pneumonia, and infections caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria.1–3 Its clinical efficacy and safety are closely dependent on the drug’s exposure levels within the body.4 However, critically ill patients often present with complex pathophysiological alterations, including organ dysfunction, hemodynamic instability, and abnormal fluid distribution. These factors lead to significant inter-individual variability in pharmacokinetic profiles, making it challenging for conventional dosing regimens to achieve optimal exposure targets.5,6 Therefore, although the 2020 international antimicrobial therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) paper do not include tigecycline among agents recommended for routine monitoring,7 it is still being experimentally applied in specific populations (such as those with liver dysfunction or in the intensive care unit (ICU)), and cases of insufficient exposure at the standard dose have been identified.8,9 Consequently, monitoring and evaluating tigecycline plasma concentrations in critically ill patients holds significant clinical importance.

The area under the steady-state plasma concentration-time curve within 24 hours (AUC0-24h) directly reflects the exposure level of tigecycline. Using this metric, the ratio of AUC0-24h to the minimum inhibitory concentration (AUC0-24h/MIC) can be calculated, which is closely associated with antimicrobial efficacy.10 Exposure-response analyses from Phase III studies have identified site-specific AUC0-24h/MIC cut-off values associated with optimal outcomes: complex skin and soft tissue infections (cSSTI) ≥ 17.9, complex intra-abdominal infections (cIAI) ≥ 6.96, and hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) ≥ 4.5. In addition to efficacy, higher exposure levels are also associated with hepatotoxicity: a cohort study in the ICU, the steady-state minimum concentration (Cmin) of 474.8 ng/mL is the best predictor of tigecycline-induced hepatotoxicity;11 A recent study showed that in patients with severe infections, trough concentration (Cmin), peak concentration (Cmax), concentration at 6 hours post-dose (C6), and AUC0-24h were all significantly associated with an increased risk of hepatotoxicity; specifically, the optimal cutoff values for Cmin and C6 were 0.535 and 0.690 mg/L, respectively.12 Therefore, both the efficacy and toxicity of tigecycline appear to be exposure-related. However, estimating AUC0-24h requires collecting multiple plasma samples within a single dosing interval, which may be invasive and unfeasible in clinical practice. In the past, tigecycline exposure studies were predominantly based on traditional population pharmacokinetics (PopPK) models.13 These models are mostly based on data from intensive sampling points and employ nonlinear mixed-effects modeling to evaluate drug exposure levels. However, the accuracy of this approach is highly dependent on the comprehensiveness and reliability of model parameters, which are often constrained by sampling frequency and analytical limitations, making practical implementation challenging. Furthermore, substantial inter-individual variability further increases prediction uncertainty. These limitations have prompted researchers to seek more precise and efficient methodologies.

In recent years, machine learning (ML) methods have seen increasingly widespread application in the field of pharmaceutical modeling, particularly providing new supportive pathways for antimicrobial therapy in critically ill patients.14 Through data-driven approaches, they can capture complex nonlinear relationships, automatically screen high-dimensional features, and perform interaction modeling. Compared to traditional PopPK models that focus more on pharmacokinetic parameters and related covariates, ML models are more inclined to fully leverage available data to enhance predictive performance. Existing research has applied ML to predict plasma concentrations for various drugs, including antipsychotics, antiepileptics, antibiotics, antiviral drugs and immunosuppressants.15–20 Introducing ML into tigecycline AUC0-24h prediction holds promise to overcome the limitations of traditional PopPK model-based methods, enhancing prediction accuracy and robustness under sparse data conditions and thereby providing more reliable support for personalized dosing.

This study aims to develop and evaluate the performance of ML methods in predicting tigecycline AUC0-24h. The specific objectives include exploring the optimal algorithm strategy of the ML model, and analyzing the impact of different numbers of therapeutic drug monitoring points (eg, 1, 2, or 3 sampling points) on model performance.

Method

Study Design, Ethics Approval and Study Cohort

The study workflow is illustrated in Figure 1. The present study is a retrospective secondary analysis of pharmacokinetic data from an earlier prospective observational cohort. The secondary analysis was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Protocol No. 2025–1098), with a waiver of informed consent for the retrospective analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patient data has been de-identified to ensure patient privacy.

Research design flowchart.

The original prospective observational cohort evaluated tigecycline pharmacokinetics in intensive care unit (ICU) patients between July 2019 and July 2023. Its protocol was approved by the same ethics committee (Protocol No. KEYAN20190108-9). Tigecycline was administered as intermittent intravenous infusions over 30 minutes every 12 hours. The dosage of tigecycline is determined by physicians based on clinical evaluation. In every patient, a blood sample was drawn immediately before the seventh administration (C0; presumed pharmacokinetic steady state). In addition, samples were drawn at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 12 h postdose. Plasma samples were obtained after centrifugation and analyzed using a validated liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method with a lower limit of quantification of 0.01 μg/mL.

Patient Inclusion and Data Collection

Eligibility was defined by the original study criteria (Supplementary Table S1). For eligible patients, we collected baseline information of patients, including demographic variables (age, sex, body mass index (BMI)), infection characteristics (site, pathogens), daily dose, clinical severity scores (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA)), and laboratory parameters. Laboratory measurements included serum creatinine (SCr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine clearance (CrCl), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), γ-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), indirect bilirubin (IBIL), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), and prothrombin time (PT). The CrCl was calculated using the Cockcroft-Gault equation, based on actual body weight.21 The dosing and sampling information, as well as corresponding tigecycline concentrations, were also collected. For each patient, the AUC0-12h is calculated using the linear trapezoidal rule in combination with all observed concentration values. When a midpoint value is missing, the calculation is performed using the trapezoid formed by the two adjacent observed concentration values within the extended time interval. AUC0-24h was obtained by doubling the observed AUC0-12h.22

Data Preprocessing

For the ML analyses, the patient was the unit of analysis. Each patient contributed one patient-level record comprising clinical covariates and tigecycline concentrations measured at the prespecified sampling times as predictor features, together with one corresponding AUC0-24h value as the outcome. Patients were randomly divided at the patient level into a training set (n = 78) and an independent test set (n = 20) at an 8:2 ratio. A fixed random seed of 42 was used to ensure reproducibility. No patient contributed data to both sets, and the test set was not used for feature selection, model fitting, or hyperparameter selection. Missing predictor values were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE).23 Implemented using scikit-learn 1.6.1 with Bayesian ridge regression as the conditional imputation model. During the chained-equation procedure, the available continuous clinical, laboratory, dosing, and pharmacokinetic predictor variables were used to estimate each incomplete variable, whereas AUC0-24h was excluded from the imputation predictors. To prevent information leakage, the imputation procedure was fitted exclusively using the training set and subsequently applied to the test set without refitting or incorporating information from the test data. Twenty imputation runs were performed. For each originally missing predictor value, the mean of the values obtained across the 20 imputations was used to construct a single completed dataset for subsequent feature selection and ML model development.

Preliminary identification of outliers was based on the 1.5-interquartile range (IQR) criterion. Data points falling outside the upper and lower limits of this criterion were further reviewed independently by two medical staff members to jointly confirm whether they were true outliers. Physiologically plausible outliers or those attributable to critical illness were retained, while other extreme outliers were excluded. Continuous predictors were standardized using scaling parameters derived exclusively from the training set and applied unchanged to the test set. Categorical variables were encoded according to variable type, with binary variables coded as 0/1, ordinal variables retaining their natural order, and nominal variables, when present, represented using one-hot encoding. Five plasma drug concentration characteristic combinations, reflecting practical tigecycline sampling strategies, were evaluated: (i) trough concentration (C0); (ii) C0 + concentration at 0.5 h (C0.5); (iii) C0 + C6; (iv) C0.5 + C6; and (v) C0 + C0.5 + C6. These feature combinations were overlapping rather than mutually exclusive and were evaluated independently using the same patient-level training and test partitions; individual concentration features could therefore appear in more than one sampling strategy.

Covariate Selection

To identify the most predictive variables and reduce model complexity, L1-regularized least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used for feature selection. Ten-fold cross-validation was performed exclusively within the training set to determine the regularization strength parameter (α). The primary analysis used the α value corresponding to the minimum cross-validated mean squared error (α_min = 0.7500), which retained seven covariates with non-zero coefficients. For comparison, the one-standard-error (1-SE) rule was also evaluated; the largest α value with a cross-validated mean squared error within one standard error of the minimum was α_1SE = 2.2592 and retained three covariates. The minimum-MSE solution was retained for subsequent ML model development.

Machine Learning Models Establishment

We evaluated four ML algorithms for predicting tigecycline AUC0-24h: Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), and Long Short-Term Memory network (LSTM). Among these, RF and XGB are tree-based ensemble methods capable of modeling nonlinear relationships and interactions among predictors, whereas MLP and LSTM are neural-network approaches capable of capturing complex nonlinear patterns. All models were trained and evaluated using the same data partitioning and preprocessing framework.

For RF and XGBoost, hyperparameters were selected by exhaustive grid search with 10-fold cross-validation performed exclusively within the training set, using mean squared error as the optimization criterion. For MLP and LSTM, the network architectures and training settings were predefined and applied consistently across all five concentration-feature combinations; no separate validation subset, data-driven hyperparameter search, or early stopping was used. Both neural networks were optimized using the Adam optimizer with mean squared error loss and trained for 10 epochs using full-batch optimization. Fixed random seeds were used throughout the analysis to ensure reproducibility. The LSTM architecture used in the primary analyses consisted of three stacked recurrent layers with 256 hidden units per layer and contained 1,325,313 trainable parameters. To assess the influence of network capacity, an additional capacity-ablation analysis was performed for the C0-based LSTM model by varying the number of hidden units (8, 16, 32, 64, 128, and 256) and recurrent layers (1, 2, and 3), while keeping all other preprocessing, training, and evaluation procedures unchanged. Detailed model settings, hyperparameter search ranges, and final selected values are provided in Supplementary Table S2, and the capacity-ablation results are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

All ML analyses were conducted using Python 3.9.23 with scikit-learn 1.6.1, XGBoost 2.1.4, PyTorch 1.13.1, SHAP 0.49.1, NumPy 1.26.4, pandas 2.3.2, SciPy 1.13.1, and statsmodels 0.14.5. And additional statistical analyses and figure preparation were conducted using R 4.2.2 and GraphPad Prism 8.3.0.

Literature Search of PopPK Models of Tigecycline

Using the PubMed and Web of Science databases, a comprehensive literature search was conducted for published PopPK models of tigecycline as of April 2025. If the study population received intravenous infusion of tigecycline at a center other than ours, and a population pharmacokinetic analysis was performed and reported, the model was included. We considered the consistency between the model development population and our dataset, as well as the technical feasibility of implementing the model. The models were excluded for the following reasons: (1) The study population comprised non-ICU patients; (2) The study was conducted only among patients receiving continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT); (3) The model included significant covariates that extended beyond the scope of the data collected in this study.

Multi-Models Evaluation

Model performance was rigorously evaluated using a multi-tiered assessment framework that integrates statistical robustness with clinical interpretability. Fundamental predictive accuracy was quantified by the coefficient of determination (R2), RMSE, measuring the proportion of explained variance, and the absolute magnitude of prediction errors, respectively. Clinical acceptability was assessed via F20 and F30, the percentages of predictions falling within ±20% and ±30% of observed values, which are recognized as clinically acceptable precision benchmarks in therapeutic drug monitoring and personalized dose adjustment. Additionally, the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) provides a continuous quantitative reference for clinical error levels. Furthermore, to detect potential systematic bias, median prediction error (MDPE) was computed to evaluate directional bias (ie, consistent over- or under-prediction). All metrics were calculated separately on the training and testing sets. Additionally, using the test dataset, the accuracy of the published PopPK model was quantified and compared with the ML model. Briefly, the PopPK model was implemented in NONMEM (v7.5.0, ICON, Ellicott City, MD, United States), and Bayesian estimation with maximum a posteriori based on trough concentrations was conducted to obtain individual pharmacokinetic parameters. The AUC0-24h was calculated with the following equation: AUC0-24h = Daily dose/CL.

Uncertainty in model performance was assessed using patient-level non-parametric bootstrap resampling with 1,000 replicates. In each replicate, 20 patients were sampled with replacement from the internal test set, and R2, RMSE, MAPE, MDPE, F20, and F30 were recalculated. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distributions were used to derive 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The same bootstrap procedure was applied to the ML and PopPK models.

Statistical Analysis

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median and IQR. Categorical variables were expressed as n (%).

Evaluation and Comparison of Models

Covariates were combined with the five plasma concentration feature sets and entered into the four ML models. Goodness-of-fit plots for the training and testing sets are shown in Figure 4, and detailed test-set performance estimates with bootstrap-derived 95% CIs are presented in Table 2, and the corresponding training-set estimates are presented in Supplementary Table S4. Across the five sampling strategies, the LSTM model showed the highest point-estimate R2 among the four algorithms. The “C0 + C0.5 + C6” LSTM model yielded the highest point-estimate R2 and the lowest RMSE in the internal test set (R2 = 0.887 (95% CI, 0.693–0.954); RMSE = 2.496 mg·h/L (95% CI, 1.65–3.38 mg·h/L)). The “C0 + C0.5” LSTM model showed similar point-estimate performance (R2 = 0.885 (95% CI, 0.687–0.953); RMSE = 2.518 mg·h/L (95% CI, 1.67–3.45 mg·h/L)). When only C0 was used as the concentration feature, the LSTM model achieved an R2 of 0.818 (95% CI, 0.527–0.914), an RMSE of 3.164 mg·h/L (95% CI, 2.11–4.28 mg·h/L), and an F30 of 90.0% (95% CI, 68.3–98.8%).

Fourteen published PopPK models of tigecycline were screened (Supplementary Table S5), of which four were retained for evaluation in the present cohort (Table 3).4,24–26 Their predictive performance in the internal test cohort is summarized in Table 4. Discussion

This exploratory study compared four ML algorithms and five limited sampling strategies for estimating the AUC0-24h of tigecycline in critically ill patients. The LSTM achieved the highest R2 on the internal test set for each concentration feature set. Among these, the model combining covariates with plasma concentration features “C0 + C0.5 + C6” exhibited the lowest RMSE. Meanwhile, the model combining covariates with C0 was the most concise and better suited for practical computational conditions; its LSTM model was comparable to some PopPK models in terms of overall trend fitting and systematic bias control. This study provides optimal algorithms and feature selection strategies for accurately predicting the AUC0-24h of tigecycline.

This outcome aligns with the operational mechanism of LSTM models,27 which are derived from artificial neural networks - a technology already successfully applied in pharmacokinetic modeling.28,29 LSTM is particularly suited for processing time-series data, and the variation of drug concentrations inherently constitutes a temporal process. A core strength of LSTM lies in directly capturing dynamic sequential patterns. Unlike traditional ML models (such as XGB) that rely on manually constructed features like “previous time point concentration” or “dosage interval”, LSTM automatically learns temporal dependencies between concentrations through its memory chain structure. This enables precise simulation of cumulative effects after drug administration or overall trends under irregular sampling.30 In elderly epilepsy patients, a model developed by Soeorg H et al demonstrated superior performance over traditional methods for sodium valproate concentration prediction.17 Similarly, the LSTM-ANN model proposed by Khusial R et al proved effective in predicting olanzapine concentrations, successfully learning from sparsely sampled data and achieving results comparable to NONMEM models.31 The XGB model, which performed well in multiple studies,19,32,33 showed inferior results to the LSTM model in this research. This suggests that, in this study, LSTM is better regarded as an efficient nonlinear estimator for extracting information from a limited amount of sample data.

TDM is commonly recommended as a guideline for personalized antibiotic dose optimization.7,34,35 The balance between efficacy and toxicity of tigecycline is also influenced by drug exposure levels and patient characteristics.11 In clinical practice, however, the frequency of TDM sampling is often limited due to practical and ethical constraints, particularly in critically ill patients. AUC0-24h serves as a more comprehensive and reliable indicator of tigecycline exposure, yet it remains challenging to predict accurately without support from a population pharmacokinetic model. This study developed a predictive model for tigecycline AUC0-24h based on limited sampling data. By entering the necessary features, physicians can obtain a relatively accurate estimate of AUC0-24h, which they can then correlate with the AUC0-24h/MIC targets specific to the site of infection and the identified pathogen.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to systematically investigate the impact of incorporating plasma concentration data from different time points on the predictive accuracy of tigecycline AUC0-24h. The results indicate that the “C0 + C0.5 + C6” combination demonstrated optimal performance on both the training and testing sets, an expected outcome given that this combination captures trough concentration (C0), peak concentration (C0.5), and mid-concentration (C6) during metabolism, providing the most comprehensive information. Secondly, the dual-concentration combination “C0 + C0.5” performed comparably to the triple-concentration combination in the testing set (R2 = 0.885 vs 0.887). The single‑feature C0 and the combination “C0 + C6” ranked next, with R2 values of 0.818 and 0.819 and RMSE values of 3.164 mg·h/L and 3.157 mg·h/L, respectively, suggesting that the inclusion of C6 did not substantially enhance predictive performance. Finally, the combination “C0.5 + C6”, lacking C0, exhibited a significant decline in predictive performance in the testing set, with MAPE rising to 22.6%, highlighting the critical role of the C0 feature. Notably, C0 corresponds precisely to the most commonly used sampling time point in clinical practice.

Both PopPK models and ML models are implementation methods of model-informed precision dosing (MIPD). It is generally recognized that both have their strengths and limitations: PopPK models offer strong pharmacological interpretability but are relatively constrained in handling nonlinearities and high-dimensional interactions, whereas ML models excel at capturing complex temporal relationships and data patterns, although they often exhibit weaker interpretability and require higher data quality and larger sample sizes.36 In the present study, the LSTM model incorporating baseline characteristics and a single C0 concentration achieved favorable point-estimate performance while requiring only one plasma concentration measurement. Its performance was comparable to that of several evaluated PopPK models in the internal test cohort. However, the bootstrap confidence intervals for the LSTM and the better-performing PopPK models overlapped substantially, and the PopPK models were originally developed in populations that differed from the present cohort. Therefore, the current analysis does not establish superiority of the LSTM model over PopPK approaches, but rather suggests that ML-based limited-sampling models may provide a complementary strategy for tigecycline exposure estimation. Multicenter external validation and head-to-head evaluation in independent cohorts are required to determine the robustness, generalizability, and relative performance of these approaches.

Previous pharmacokinetic studies have identified several key covariates, such as TBIL,8,37–39 CrCl,40,41 SCr,4,39 BMI,39 and age.4,26 This study validated the importance of some of these variables. Additionally, we incorporated SOFA score and PT into the final model. To our knowledge, this is the first time these two indicators have been integrated into the model, yet their clinical rationale is clear. The SOFA score comprehensively reflects the functional status and perfusion of drug-eliminating organs (liver, kidneys) by quantifying the severity of multiple organ dysfunction. This finding effectively explains the significant interindividual variability in tigecycline pharmacokinetics among critically ill patients, thereby serving as a key predictor of plasma drug concentrations. Another commonly used score in the ICU, the APACHE II, is also considered a valuable covariate due to its integration of key organ function and overall disease severity information, it has been incorporated into tigecycline pharmacokinetic models in studies by Luo et al.26 From a mechanistic perspective, tigecycline is primarily eliminated via biliary excretion, and its clearance rate is therefore highly dependent on hepatic function status. PT, as a sensitive indicator of liver function, often prolongs with reduced hepatic synthetic capacity (commonly seen in liver injury, cirrhosis, severe infection, or malnutrition), thus indirectly reflecting changes in drug metabolism. Additionally, abnormal PT may indicate coagulation dysfunction, and the pathophysiological state of such patients typically involves greater pharmacokinetic variability.42

This study also has several limitations. First, the ML model was developed using a single-center dataset, potentially limiting its generalizability to other populations with differing demographics and clinical practices. Second, due to the lack of suitable external datasets, external validation could not be performed, nor could a direct performance comparison with the published PopPK model be conducted on independent cohorts. Third, actual sampling points may deviate from the theoretical ones, potentially causing some decline in model performance. However, we opted for broad tolerance intervals for sampling times as this aligns with real-world clinical practice. A previous study has incorporated variables related to theoretical sampling time bias, and the results indicate that this variable has a negligible impact on concentration estimates.20 In addition, AUC0-24h was obtained by doubling the observed AUC0-12h. Although this is a common method, it is based on the assumption of a perfectly symmetric steady-state dose profile. In reality, there may be potential biases.

Conclusion

The LSTM algorithm developed in this study can estimate individual tigecycline AUC0-24h based on trough concentrations. The single-trough (C0) strategy is recommended as the pragmatic final strategy because it relies solely on the most routinely available sample, whereas the best-performing C0 + C0.5 + C6 strategy provides only marginal gains at the cost of additional sampling. Given that only a pharmacokinetic surrogate endpoint was assessed and that validation was internal, the model should be considered an exploratory pharmacokinetic prediction tool. External validation, and studies linking predicted exposure to validated therapeutic targets and patient outcomes, are required before the model can support routine tigecycline therapeutic drug monitoring or model-guided dose optimization.
