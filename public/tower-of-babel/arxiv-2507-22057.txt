MetaLab: Few-Shot Game Changer for Image Recognition
Chaofei Qi, Zhitai Liu, Jianbin Qiu

Abstract
Difficult few-shot image recognition has significant application prospects, yet remaining the substantial technical gaps with the conventional large-scale image recognition. In this paper, we have proposed an efficient original method for few-shot image recognition, called CIELab-Guided Coherent Meta-Learning (MetaLab). Structurally, our MetaLab comprises two collaborative neural networks: LabNet, which can perform domain transformation for the CIELab color space and extract rich grouped features, and coherent LabGNN, which can facilitate mutual learning between lightness graph and color graph. For sufficient certification, we have implemented extensive comparative studies on four coarse-grained benchmarks, four fine-grained benchmarks, and four cross-domain few-shot benchmarks. Specifically, our method can achieve high accuracy, robust performance, and effective generalization capability with one-shot sample per class. Overall, all experiments have demonstrated that our MetaLab can approach 99% ↑ ⁣ ↓ \uparrow\downarrow accuracy, reaching the human recognition ceiling with little visual deviation. Our codes are at https://github.com/ChaofeiQI/MetaLab .

Related Work

According to the relationship between the meta-encoder and meta-classifier, meta-learning methods are reclassified into the coherent and non-coherent categories. Moreover, we analyze the human binocular perception mechanism.

2.1
Coherent Meta-Learning

Classical coherent meta-learning
[
11
,
16
]
aims to concurrently focus on both the feature extractor and classifier, in order to construct a network architecture with enhanced generalization capabilities. Training meta-feature extractor and meta-classifier simultaneously remains the main strategy for few-shot learning
[
48
]
. Many meta-learning methods
[
42
,
30
,
27
]
employ this framework, including weak collaboration approaches such as meta-transfer fine-tuning
[
44
]
or meta-classifier parameter optimization
[
10
,
24
]
, as well as strong collaboration methods like feature reconstruction
[
51
,
53
]
, and those based on GNNs
[
21
,
57
]
. The primary challenge is to build a robust feature extractor and develop an improved classification mechanism that enables effective collaboration between the encoder and classifier.

2.2
Non-Coherent Meta-Learning

Rapid advancement in machine learning and few-shot learning has promoted pretrained few-shot models
[
51
,
15
]
accessible for feature extractions, emphasizing feature integrations and meta-classifier patterns.
This fosters advanced few-shot image recognition, pushing the domain’s boundaries.
Leveraging the pre-trained models allows for the immediate attainment of robust feature extraction capabilities
[
31
,
2
,
5
,
9
,
56
]
.
Additionally, the incorporation of statistical methodologies
[
59
]
or multimodal approaches
[
62
]
significantly improves the capacity to capture intra-class similarities and inter-class differences.
However, there are two main challenges:
1. Related approaches rely on the training capability of pre-trained models. When pre-trained models are unavailable, alternative networks must be employed for re-training.
2. Multimodal fusion related methods produce a significant amount of redundant features, making it challenging to effectively extract primary features from them.

2.3
Human Binocular Perception

Humans excel at few-shot image recognition by employing binocular perception to discern both macro-level commonalities and micro-level differences between images.
Research
[
45
]
indicates that human vision is highly sensitive to color, meaning that color and lightness features offer actually most intuitive visual cues.
While classic RGB encoding on three primary colors offers some certain advantages
[
6
]
, it would not be universally suitable for all visual scenes.
By strategically learning those color and brightness features of images
[
1
,
4
]
, it is also possible to acquire essential edge information and detailed features within regions of interest.

Inspired by this insight, we boldly explored a new meta-learning-MetaLab, to tackle the few-shot image recognition problem. Rather than focusing on constructing networks to extract deep abstract features for capturing inter-class differences, we convert images into the CIELab color space for grouped feature extraction of the lightness and color channels. Moreover, we establish relationship learning based on the lightness and color graphs. This effectively extracts primary features and reasonably filters out redundant features, closely simulating the human meta-learning mechanism.

Methodology

In this section, we model few-shot problem and provide a detailed explanation of our LabNet, based on Lab feature encoding, and LabGNN, which operates based on message passing for graph edges update and node classification.

3.1
Few-Shot Task Formalization

{D^{{\rm{supp}}}}
and query set
{D^{{\rm{qry}}}}
.
At various stages, both of them are randomly sampled from the training, validation, and novel sets of benchmarks, respectively. In specific subtasks, we often utilize scenario training, specifically the
{\rm{K}}
-way-
{\rm{N}}
-shot strategy, which is composed of the non-overlapping support sample pairs
{T^{{\rm{supp}}}}=\{({x_{i}},{y_{i}})\}_{i=1}^{{\rm{N}}\times{\rm{K}}}
and query instance pairs
{T^{{\rm{qry}}}}=\{({x_{i}},{y_{i}})\}_{i={\rm{N}}\times{\rm{K}}+1}^{{\rm{K}}\times({\rm{N+Q})}}

Here,
{\rm{K}}
represents total category number per scenario training episode,
{\rm{N}}
denotes the sample number drawn from each category in
{D^{{\rm{supp}}}}
, and
{\rm{Q}}
indicates the instance number drawn from each category in
{D^{{\rm{qry}}}}
.
In general, increasing the
{\rm{Q}}
is more apt to enhance model’s generalization and robustness.

LAB-Guided Graph Network

In

3

Let

3.3.1
Graph Construction

The nodes (vertices) and edges of these two sub-graphs are initialized through two-tiered feature embeddings.
For the sake of analysis, we assume the
{\mathop{\rm K}\nolimits}=5
,
{\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits}=1
,
{\mathop{\rm Q}\nolimits}=1
, and consequently
{\mathop{\rm K(N+Q)}}=10
images per scenario.

Node Initialization

LabGNN comprises parallel brightness and color subgraphs, each containing
{\mathop{\rm K(N+Q)}}
nodes per task.
Here, we directly define the corresponding brightness and color feature embeddings of
{{\mathop{\rm E}\nolimits}^{Ls}}
as the initial node values for the two subgraphs (zero generation), as follows:

V_{0}^{L}=\{E_{1}^{Ls}\}^{B},V_{0}^{C}=\{E_{2}^{Ls}\}^{B}

(5)

Edge Initialization

Graph edges directly represent the relationships between nodes, or more precisely, their similarity. Nodes with stronger relationships tend to have more similar categorical feature attributes.
As illustrated in
Figure

5
, we take three graph nodes as examples, with penultimate feature embeddings. They can be separated into two sub-branches: light and color.
We compute the node similarities within the branches via light and color similarities, and construct two square similarity matrices with one on diagonals.
Let
{F_{LS}}
and
{F_{CS}}
represent the operations of light similarity and color similarity, respectively, both using the L1 metric by default. The light and color edges satisfy:

E_{0}^{L}=F_{LS}(\{E_{1}^{Pe}\}^{B}),E_{0}^{C}=F_{CS}(\{E_{2}^{Pe}\}^{B})

(6)

3.3.2
Message Passing

The graph parameters update between nodes and edges are accomplished through message passing.
Besides, we have initialized the nodes and edges of two subgraphs based on embedded features, which serve as the initial values for first message passing.
Once message passing follows:
\to E_{\hat{g}}^{L}\to V_{\hat{g}}^{C}\to E_{\hat{g}}^{C}\to V_{\hat{g}}^{L}
, where
\hat{g}
is current graph transfer algebra, and
\hat{g}\in[1,g]
. For convenience, let
T=\rm K\times(N+Q)
.

Light Graph Edge

Given light nodes, light edges quantify the similarity between embedded lightness features.
To ensure completeness, the
\hat{g}
-th generation edges update relies on the current light nodes and the (
\hat{g}
-1)-th generation’s edges.
Specifically, first calculate the similarity between the current light graph nodes, then multiply it by the edge matrix of the previous generation.
Related calculation process is as follows:

\{V_{\hat{g}}^{L},E_{\hat{g}-1}^{L}\}\to E_{\hat{g}}^{L},E_{\hat{g}}^{L}=\{\{e_{\hat{g},ij}^{L}\}_{i=1,j=1}^{T,T}\}^{B}

e_{\hat{g},ij}^{L}=F_{LS}(v_{\hat{g},i}^{L})\cdot e_{\hat{g}-1,ij}^{L}

(7)

Here,
e_{\hat{g},ij}^{L}
denotes the edge between two light nodes,
v_{\hat{g},i}^{L}
denotes the
i
-th light nodes, and
i
and
j
are node indices.

Color Graph Node

Color graph nodes encapsulate rich color dimension attributes and detailed image information.
To distinguish color node differences, we consider both the (
\hat{g}
-1)-th generation’s nodes and the similarity of light nodes.
To execute the goal, we introduce color layering, denoted as
{F_{CL}}
, as illustrated in
Figure

6

The cross-domain fusion between the light edges and color nodes enables the formation of more accurate color nodes that carry lightness information.
The computation of color nodes proceeds as follows:

\{E_{\hat{g}}^{L},V_{\hat{g}-1}^{C}\}\to V_{\hat{g}}^{L},V_{\hat{g}}^{C}=\{\{v_{\hat{g},i}^{C}\}_{i=1}^{T}\}^{B}

v_{\hat{g},i}^{C}=F_{CL}(e_{\hat{g},ij}^{L},v_{\hat{g}-1,i}^{C})

(8)

Here,
v_{\hat{g},i}^{C}
denotes the
i
-th color nodes,
e_{\hat{g},ij}^{L}
denotes the edge between two light nodes, and
i
and
j
are node indices.

Color Graph Edge

Color edges quantify the similarity of node within the color dimension, constituting an essential metric for assessing relationships among color nodes.
To ensure color completeness, color edges update based on color node and the edges from the preceding generation.
We compute the similarity between color nodes, then multiply this by the edge matrix of the (
\hat{g}
-1)-th generation:

\{V_{\hat{g}}^{C},E_{\hat{g}-1}^{C}\}\to E_{\hat{g}}^{C},E_{\hat{g}}^{C}=\{\{e_{{\hat{g}},ij}^{C}\}_{i=1,j=1}^{T,T}\}^{B}

e_{\hat{g},ij}^{C}=F_{CS}(v_{\hat{g},i}^{C})\cdot e_{\hat{g}-1,ij}^{C}

(9)

Here,
e_{\hat{g},ij}^{C}
denotes the edge between two color nodes,
v_{\hat{g},i}^{C}
denotes the
i
-th color nodes, and
i
and
j
are node indices.

Light Graph Node

Light nodes features generally outshine the color nodes, more effectively capturing the inter-class distinctions and intra-class similarities.
For new light nodes, we integrate color node similarities to rectify the absence of color dimension features in light nodes.
This detailed process is accomplished via our Light Gradient operation, denoted as
{F_{LG}}
, as depicted in
Figure

6

Light graph nodes update as follows:

\{E_{\hat{g}}^{C},V_{\hat{g}-1}^{L}\}\to V_{\hat{g}}^{L},V_{\hat{g}}^{L}=\{\{v_{\hat{g},i}^{L}\}_{i=1}^{T}\}^{B}

v_{\hat{g},i}^{L}=F_{LG}(e_{\hat{g},ij}^{C},v_{\hat{g}-1,i}^{L})

(10)

Here,
v_{\hat{g},i}^{L}
denotes the
i
-th light nodes,
e_{\hat{g},ij}^{C}
denotes the edge between two color nodes, and
i
and
j
are node indices.

H

of LabNet

We conducted this ablation study on

In summary, we sight: 1) Best accuracy is achieved when H = 96; 2) As H decreases, model’s convergence speed slows down and it becomes less stable; 3) With H smaller, the 95% confidence interval widens, indicating reduced robustness.

Coherent Embedding Dimension(CED)

The CED of LabNet directly determines actual embedded feature dimension of graph nodes in LabGNN.
To illustrate this, we conducted related analysis on
tiered
-ImageNet with Q = 15.
As illustrated in
Figure

8(b)

In summary, we can discern: 1) Optimal performance is achieved when CED is 128; 2) With CED = 96, a suboptimal accuracy is obtained, but the convergence speed is notably slower; 3) For smaller CED, its robustness decreases, and actual accuracy is reduced.

Graph Generation(GG) of LabGNN

GG dictates the message-passing iterations within subgraphs in LabGNN, thereby determining the meta-learning depth among graph nodes.
To investigate its impact on MetaLab, we conducted particular analysis on
meta
-iNat with Q = 15.
As depicted in
Figure

8(c)
, we illustrate the phased training process for GG = 15 and present the corresponding validation curves for four distinct GG settings.
As GG equals 8, 10, 12, and 15, actual test accuracies are 61.14 ± 0.44, 84.65 ± 0.30, 96.80 ± 0.12, and 99.34 ± 0.05, respectively.
In a nutshell, we discover: 1) Optimal performance is achieved when GG is set to 15; 2) As GG decreases, the convergence slows down, resulting in lower accuracy; 3) Additionally, smaller GG leads to wider 95% confidence interval, indicating poorer robustness.

5
Conclusion

To fully tackle few-shot image recognition, in this paper, we propose the efficient CIELab-Guided Coherent Meta-Learning (MetaLab). Structurally, our MetaLab consists of two collaborative neural networks: LabNet and LabGNN. The former aims to perform color space conversion, channel separation, and group feature extraction. Subsequently, the latter seeks to treat grouped features as nodes of lightness and color graphs, and obtains primary inter-class and intra-class attributes with color layering and light gradient.
For sufficient certification, we conduct the comparative experiments on four coarse-grained and four fine-grained benchmarks, and the cross-domain experiments on four additional datasets.
Overall, all the studies demonstrate that our MetaLab exhibits SoTA accuracy, strong robustness, and sound generalization, reaching the limits of human visual system.
