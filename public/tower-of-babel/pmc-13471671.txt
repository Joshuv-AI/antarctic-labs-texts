Development and validity evidence for the probabilistic reasoning test in undergraduate medical students
Authors: David Drummond, Dulanjalee Kariyawasam, Héloïse Torchin, François-Xavier Mauvais, Romain Berthaud, Naim Ouldali, Michael Levy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScript Concordance Tests (SCT) are widely used to assess clinical reasoning in medical education, but their use in high-stakes examinations raises concerns related to ambiguity, reliance on expert panels, and limited acceptability among students. To address these issues, we developed the Probabilistic Reasoning Test (PRT), a simplified format designed to assess normative probabilistic reasoning within clinical scenarios using a single predetermined correct answer and a three-point response scale.MethodsWe conducted a study including fifth-year medical students, pediatric residents, and pediatricians. Participants completed matched triads of SCT, PRT, and multiple-choice questions (MCQs) based on identical clinical scenarios. Student Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) scores were collected. Outcomes included concordance between SCT and PRT responses, internal consistency, relations with other assessment modalities, score interpretation across experience levels, and participant acceptability. ResultsA total of 575 participants were analyzed (538 students, 19 residents, 18 pediatricians). Concordance between SCT and PRT responses was high (agreement 91.4%, weighted κ = 0.91). Background

Clinical reasoning represents a fundamental competency in medical practice, enabling healthcare professionals to synthesize complex clinical data, formulate differential diagnoses, and implement appropriate management strategies [1].

Script Concordance Tests (SCT) were developed to evaluate this competency in situations characterized by clinical uncertainty [2]. Grounded in cognitive psychology and script theory, SCT operate on the principle that clinical decision-making involves multiple intermediate judgments that can be systematically evaluated against expert consensus [3]. Script theory proposes that clinical reasoning is supported by “illness scripts”- structured networks of pre-stored knowledge that integrate signs, symptoms, contextual features, and management expectations, and are progressively refined through clinical experience [4]. Because illness scripts are individual and exposure-dependent, SCT scoring uses the aggregated responses of an expert panel as its reference standard, with the dispersion of expert responses interpreted as a legitimate reflection of the variability of expert scripts in genuinely uncertain situations.

The validity and utility of SCT in assessing clinical reasoning have been well-established through extensive research [5] but using these tests in high stakes exams determining students’ eligibility for specialty training programs can raise some concerns. First, these exams usually target foundational knowledge in domains where consensus is strong and management is guided either by established evidence-based protocols or by expert consensus statements. Unlike residents or practising physicians, for whom SCT can be used to foster reflection on different possible management strategies in situations of genuine uncertainty due to gaps in the evidence, students are not expected to face such scenarios [6, 7]. In undergraduate assessment contexts, the expected level of reasoning is therefore not primarily the management of epistemic uncertainty, but the correct updating of diagnostic or therapeutic likelihoods based on new clinical information. As a result, when used with undergraduate learners, SCT items tend to function as measures of probabilistic reasoning - evaluating how students adjust the likelihood of a diagnostic or therapeutic hypothesis when new information is introduced - rather than as assessments of reasoning in situations of true clinical uncertainty [8, 9]. When probabilistic updating is guided by well-established evidence or consensus, a normative direction of change can reasonably be expected, supporting the use of a single correct response.

Second, SCT scoring requires large expert panels, typically ≥ 10 members [10], which are logistically difficult to assemble [11]. Moreover, the only study comparing expert judgments with evidence-based likelihood ratios reported significant discrepancies in 73% of cases, highlighting the limitations of expert consensus as a gold standard when probabilistic reasoning is the primary construct of interest [12]. A system based on a single predetermined correct answer, defined by question authors or through consensus prior to test administration, and aligned with the intended learning outcomes of undergraduate medical training, could therefore be more appropriate while also being easier to implement, grade, and explain [13, 14].

Finally, the response scale itself presents challenges. Although SCT were initially designed with seven anchors and later reduced to five, the five-point Likert scale still introduces ambiguity [15]. Distinguishing between “positive” (+ 1) and “very positive” (+ 2) impacts, for instance, remains subjective and inconsistent across both experts and students, particularly when fine-grained distinctions in likelihood change are not explicitly taught or expected at the undergraduate level [16].

For all these reasons, we developed the Probabilistic Reasoning Test (PRT) as an alternative format for undergraduate assessment. The PRT preserves the clinical scenario format of SCT but uses a simplified three-point scale (− 1, 0, + 1) corresponding to the expected direction of probability change: decrease, no substantial change, or increase. The PRT is grounded in normative probabilistic reasoning: in this framework, new clinical information should update the probability of a diagnostic or therapeutic hypothesis in a direction that can be predicted from established evidence, such as likelihood-ratio data when available, clinical guidelines, or consensus-based undergraduate reference materials [8, 9]. When the targeted reasoning task is restricted to such normative updates - as is appropriate for undergraduate learners assessed against curriculum-defined objectives - a single predetermined correct answer can be defined a priori without relying on expert-response dispersion. The PRT is therefore conceptually distinct from SCT in its intended construct: rather than capturing the variability of expert scripts under genuine clinical uncertainty, it operationalizes the expected direction of probabilistic updating at the end of undergraduate training.

The aim of this study was to assess the validity evidence supporting the intended interpretation and use of PRT scores as indicators of normative probabilistic reasoning within clinical scenarios among medical students approaching specialty selection. We considered validity within Messick’s unified framework, in which validity refers to the degree to which evidence and theory support the intended interpretation and use of assessment scores. Accordingly, we interpreted the evidence collected in this study in relation to complementary sources of validity evidence, including content, response process, internal structure, relations to other variables, and consequences [17]. We hypothesized that PRT responses would show high directional concordance with SCT responses at the level of probabilistic updating, while relying on a conceptually distinct scoring logic, and would demonstrate comparable reliability and higher acceptability ratings.

Test preparation

The assessment comprised 20 matched triads, each focusing on identical clinical content (e.g., hematuria in infants). Each triad included one SCT, one PRT, and one multiple-choice question (MCQ) to enable direct comparison across assessment modalities while controlling for content effects. The 20 clinical scenarios were developed by the seven authors involved in item construction (DD, DK, HT, FXM, RB, NO, ML), who are medical educators and pediatric clinicians/researchers with expertise across several pediatric-related fields, including pulmonology, endocrinology, neonatology, physiology, infectious diseases, intensive care, and general pediatrics. Scenarios were selected to reflect core diagnostic and therapeutic situations from the official French undergraduate medical curriculum. Each author drafted two to four matched triads according to his or her area of expertise. For each scenario, three matched items were developed to address the same clinical content: one SCT, one PRT, and one MCQ. The SCT and PRT items shared the same clinical stem and additional information, whereas the MCQ assessed related factual knowledge within the same clinical domain. All triads were then reviewed by the full author panel for clinical accuracy, alignment with undergraduate learning objectives, consistency across the three item formats, and clarity of wording. An illustrative example of a matched SCT/PRT/MCQ triad is provided in Supplemental Table 1.

SCT were developed following established recommendations [15, 18, 19], presenting clinical scenarios with new information rated on a five-point Likert scale (-2 to + 2). PRT items were constructed by adapting each corresponding SCT item to a three-point response scale (-1, 0, + 1). For each PRT item, the predetermined correct answer was defined a priori according to the expected direction of probability change, based on established clinical evidence, consensus recommendations, or undergraduate reference materials. MCQs assessed factual knowledge using standard 5-option multiple-choice format.

Participants completed the 60 questions (20 SCT, 20 PRT, and 20 MCQs) presented in randomized order through an online platform within a maximum duration of 120 min, and then completed an acceptability questionnaire. Time-stamped responses were recorded to ensure test integrity.

PRT scoring awarded 1 point for correct responses among the three options, 0 otherwise. For SCT scoring, an independent expert panel of 14 pediatricians completed the SCT items, and scoring keys were derived from their aggregated responses using the standard SCT scoring method. Specifically, 1 point was awarded for the most popular expert response, with proportional scoring for other responses according to the following formula: number of experts choosing student’s response/number of experts choosing modal response. MCQ scoring followed French national examination standards, which differ from the all-or-nothing approach used in some other systems. Each MCQ comprises five independent true/false statements; 1 point is awarded if all are correctly classified, 0.5 if one is misclassified, 0.2 if two are misclassified, and 0 if three or more are misclassified.

All assessments were normalized to 20-point scales for direct statistical comparison. Student OSCE scores (0–20 points) were collected as an additional validity measure recognizing that OSCEs assess a broader set of clinical competencies than probabilistic reasoning alone.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the concordance between responses to PRT (-1, 0, or + 1) and SCT (-2, -1, 0, + 1, or + 2) for matched questions. This endpoint was intended to examine whether both formats elicited similar directional response patterns when applied to identical undergraduate clinical reasoning tasks and was considered evidence related to relations with other variables.

The secondary endpoints were:

(i) Internal consistency of the PRT, assessed by Cronbach's alpha coefficient, complemented by the Standard Error of Measurement (SEM) to estimate measurement precision, and by Ferguson’s delta (δ) to assess score discrimination. This endpoint contributed to internal-structure evidence.

Content evidence was addressed during item development, as described in the Test preparation section. Direct response-process evidence was not collected, as no cognitive interviews, think-aloud procedures, or response-time analyses were conducted.

Statistical analysis

We first described overall performance in each test (PRT, SCT, MCQ, OSCE) using descriptive statistics. For the comparison of SCT and PRT responses among students, SCT responses on the five-point Likert scale (− 2/−1/0/+1/+2) were mapped a priori onto the three-point PRT scale (− 1/0/+1). This mapping reflected a deliberate conceptual choice: the PRT was designed to assess the direction of probabilistic update - decrease, no substantial change, or increase -whereas the SCT additionally captures the perceived intensity of this update through its five-point scale. The comparison therefore focused on directional concordance rather than full equivalence between the original response formats. Concordance was quantified by the exact agreement rate, with 95% binomial confidence intervals, and by a weighted kappa coefficient. Weighted kappa was used to account for chance agreement and partial agreement between adjacent categories, although it cannot fully eliminate the structural effect of collapsing five categories into three.

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The Standard Error of Measurement (SEM = SD × √(1 - α)) was reported as a complementary precision metric to express measurement error in the test’s native units (points out of 20). Formal equivalence testing (TOST, 90% CI, margin ± 0.10) was performed on Cronbach’s alpha only, as SEM is mathematically derived from this coefficient.

To explore associations between the different types of tests, we computed a Pearson correlation matrix including PRT, SCT, MCQs, and OSCEs as measures addressing related but distinct aspects of clinical competence.

Between-group comparisons (students, residents, senior pediatricians) were conducted using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey post-hoc tests if significant.

For the satisfaction survey (Likert 1–5), responses to PRT vs. SCT were compared for each question and group using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Finally, the question addressing preferred modality for national exams, SCT vs. PRT, was analyzed by exact binomial tests within each group and a global chi-square test for independence between experience level and preferred choice.

All analyses were performed using R software, version 4.4.3.

Results

A total of 611 responses were collected: 571 from medical students among 849 invited students, corresponding to a response rate of 67.3%, 21 from pediatric residents, and 19 from senior pediatricians. After applying exclusion criteria, 575 participants were retained for analysis (538 students, 19 residents, 18 senior pediatricians; Supplemental Fig. 1). Among senior pediatricians, subspecialties were pulmonology (n = 3), neurology (n = 3), pediatric intensive care (n = 3), cardiology (n = 2), immunology (n = 2), nephrology (n = 2), general pediatrics (n = 2), and endocrinology (n = 1). Among students, OSCE scores were available and successfully matched for 521 participants.

Descriptive statistics for student performance across the four assessment modalities are summarized in Supplemental Table 3. Mean scores were highest for the PRT (15.8 ± 1.5), followed by the SCT (13.7 ± 1.5), OSCE (13.8 ± 2.2), and MCQ (11.3 ± 2.2). Score distributions are shown in Supplemental Fig. 2, illustrating a narrower dispersion and right-shifted peak for PRT relative to the other formats.

Regarding the primary outcome, concordance between PRT and SCT responses among students was high, with an overall agreement of 91.4% (95% CI, 91.1–91.7) and a weighted kappa coefficient of 0.91, which indicates very high agreement. Most items showed strong alignment between the two scoring modalities, with only a minority falling below the 85% agreement threshold. The Sankey diagram (Fig. 1) illustrates the mapping of SCT categories onto the three-point PRT scale, showing that the majority of responses in the extreme or intermediate SCT categories converged toward their expected PRT counterparts, consistent with similar patterns of probabilistic updating across formats, with limited dispersion across adjacent categories. Because this analysis was based on the predefined mapping of SCT responses onto the three PRT directional categories, these results should be interpreted as directional concordance rather than equivalence between the original SCT and PRT response scales.

SCT: Script Concordance Test

For reliability, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.63 (95% CI [0.58–0.68]) for the PRT and 0.62 (95% CI [0.56–0.66]) for the SCT, with a difference of 0.02 (90% CI [− 0.03; 0.06]), indicating statistical equivalence between both tests. Measurement precision was comparable, with Standard Errors of Measurement (SEM) of 0.89 points (95% CI [0.86–0.92]) for the PRT and 0.95 points (95% CI [0.93–0.96]) for the SCT. Score discrimination was excellent for both instruments, as reflected by Ferguson’s delta values of 0.97 (95% CI [0.97–0.98]) for the PRT and 1.00 (95% CI [1.00–1.00]) for the SCT.

With respect to relations with other assessment modalities, correlation analyses are presented in Fig. 2. PRT scores showed strong positive correlation with SCT scores and moderate correlation with MCQ scores. Correlations with OSCE performance were weaker, both for the pediatric OSCE station and for the overall OSCE score. A similar pattern was observed for SCT scores, which correlated moderately with MCQs and weakly with OSCE outcomes. All correlations were statistically significant (p < 0.05).

OSCE - ped: Objective Structured Clinical Examinations – pediatric station. OSCE - total: Objective Structured Clinical Examinations – total scores (5 stations)

With respect to support for intended score interpretation across levels of clinical experience, mean PRT scores were similar across groups: 15.8 ± 1.5 for students, 16.0 ± 0.9 for residents, and 15.5 ± 1.6 for senior pediatricians (p = 0.67) (Supplemental Fig. 3). Corresponding SCT scores were 13.6 ± 1.5, 14.6 ± 1.1, and 14.2 ± 1.8, with a significant difference between students and residents (p = 0.02). Mean MCQ scores were similar across groups: 11.3 ± 2.2 for students, 11.1 ± 0.9 for residents, and 11.4 ± 2.0 for senior pediatricians (p = 0.90). In a post-hoc, exploratory analysis restricting senior pediatricians’ scores to items corresponding to their subspecialty, mean values increased to 16.5 ± 2.3 for PRT and 15.4 ± 2.2 for SCT, whereas MCQ scores remained comparable (10.9 ± 6.1) (Supplemental Fig. 4). In this adjusted analysis, differences reached statistical significance only for SCT when comparing students and residents, and students and senior pediatricians.

Finally, regarding acceptability, satisfaction ratings consistently favored the PRT over the SCT across almost all items (Supplemental Fig. 5). Students, residents, and senior pediatricians perceived the PRT as offering a more appropriate response scale (with only three options: “−1, 0, + 1”) and a clearer scoring modality (with a single correct answer). Among residents and senior pediatricians, PRT responses were also judged easier to explain to students in the event of score interpretation or challenge. When asked to state their preferred modality for the French national fifth-year examination, 90% of participants favored the PRT over the SCT, independently of clinical experience level. (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this study, PRT responses showed high item-level concordance with SCT responses when applied to identical clinical stems, and PRT scores were strongly correlated with SCT scores. Internal consistency was comparable between PRT and SCT, although modest for both formats. PRT scores showed moderate correlations with MCQ scores and weak correlations with OSCE scores. Unlike SCT, PRT scores did not significantly differentiate students, residents, and senior pediatricians. Finally, the PRT received higher acceptability ratings than the SCT across participant groups.

Interpreted within Messick’s framework, these findings provide several sources of validity evidence, although not all sources were addressed to the same extent. Content evidence was supported by curriculum-aligned item development and multidisciplinary author review. Internal-structure evidence was provided by reliability and score-distribution analyses. Relations-to-other-variables evidence included item-level PRT–SCT concordance, total-score correlations with SCT, MCQ, and OSCE scores, and comparisons across levels of clinical experience. Acceptability findings provided preliminary consequences evidence. Direct response-process evidence was not collected, as no cognitive interviews, think-aloud procedures, or response-time analyses were conducted. Therefore, the high item-level PRT–SCT concordance should be interpreted as indirect evidence that the two formats elicited similar directional response patterns under matched scenarios, not as direct response-process evidence or as standalone evidence of construct validity.

The strong association between SCT and PRT was expected, given that both formats used identical clinical stems and targeted probabilistic updating in undergraduate clinical scenarios. However, this association should be interpreted cautiously. The concordance analysis relied on a deliberate mapping of the five-point SCT scale onto the three-point PRT scale, which mechanically reduced the number of disagreement categories. Although weighted kappa accounts for chance agreement and partial agreement between adjacent categories, it does not fully eliminate the structural effect of collapsing categories. Concordance results should therefore be interpreted as evidence that the two formats elicited a consistent direction of probabilistic update, not as evidence that they are fully interchangeable. Accordingly, the high PRT-SCT concordance supports the hypothesis that, in this undergraduate context, both formats elicit similar directional response patterns despite relying on different scoring logics.

The internal consistency was modest, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.63 for PRT and 0.62 for SCT. Previous studies have generally reported higher values, usually between 0.7 and 0.9 [5]. However, lower coefficients are not uncommon and may be explained by the heterogeneity of the expert panel or of the clinical domains being tested [18]. This is particularly relevant in our study, as the expert group included pediatricians from diverse subspecialties - such as endocrinology, pulmonology, cardiology, intensive care, nephrology and neurology - and the items covered a wide range of pediatric domains. Such variability in expertise and content likely contributed to the moderate reliability observed, consistent with the well-established phenomenon of context specificity in clinical reasoning, whereby performance on one case provides only limited prediction of performance on another, even within the same domain [20]. This is also reflected in other pediatric studies, where Cronbach’s alpha has ranged from 0.49 to 0.77 [21–23], and is compatible with assessments targeting multifaceted reasoning processes rather than a single unidimensional construct.

Correlations between PRT and MCQs were moderate and very similar to those observed between SCT and MCQs (r = 0.56 and r = 0.54). This is higher than usually reported, as most studies describe only weak associations with knowledge-based tests [24–26]. The persistence of a moderate correlation in our cohort may be explained by the design of our study, in which MCQs, SCT, and PRT were built on the same themes in triads, likely reinforcing their overlap at the level of content rather than construct. The weaker correlations with OSCE scores warrant a balanced theoretical interpretation. On the one hand, this pattern is compatible with discriminant validity within Messick’s framework: the PRT and SCT target probabilistic updating from clinical vignettes, whereas the OSCE assesses integrated bedside performance, including history-taking, physical examination, communication, and procedural skills, across multiple disciplines and using scenarios that did not overlap with the PRT items. Under these conditions, only weak-to-moderate correlations would be theoretically expected; a strong correlation could, conversely, raise concerns about construct conflation. On the other hand, weak correlations with an external clinical-performance indicator also constitute a limitation for any broader claim about the clinical relevance of the PRT. They support a narrow construct interpretation but do not, by themselves, demonstrate that PRT scores translate into meaningful differences in clinical performance. Our claims are therefore restricted to the construct the PRT was designed to assess and do not extend to overall clinical competence, for which predictive-validity studies will be required.

While SCT scores were significantly higher in senior pediatricians and residents compared to students, reflecting the expected gradient of clinical reasoning development with experience, this difference was not statistically significant with the PRT. This finding should be interpreted in light of the PRT’s intended construct and use. The PRT was explicitly designed to minimize ambiguity by converging toward a single correct answer, thereby prioritizing normative probabilistic updating over the expression of multiple, experience-dependent reasoning strategies, which may have reduced its sensitivity to subtle variations in reasoning strategies across levels of expertise, as in other studies that compared five-point to three-point scoring methods [27, 28]. The fact that PRT scores were overall higher than SCT scores, combined with the small size of the resident and physician groups relative to the student cohort, also likely reduced statistical power to detect between-group differences in PRT scores. Finally, the heterogeneity of the senior pediatrician group - composed of subspecialists with varying degrees of familiarity with the tested domains- may have further contributed to the absence of a clear gradient across experience levels. In the post-hoc analysis restricted to items corresponding to each physician’s subspecialty, mean scores increased markedly, supporting the interpretation that lower performance in unrelated domains, rather than intrinsic limitations of the PRT format, may explain the lack of discrimination observed with the PRT.

PRT items were rated much more positively than SCT across medical students, residents, and senior pediatricians: participants found PRT clearer, less ambiguous, easier to explain, and more appropriate for a high-stakes national exam. These findings speak to the implications of adopting assessment formats that favor transparency and interpretability in summative contexts. Student acceptance of SCT has been consistently limited in some countries. In a French multicenter cohort of 551 sixth-year students, 86% judged SCT harder than MCQs and 73% felt they lacked the knowledge to answer them [24] ; another survey found predominantly negative views from students (58%) and nearly half of teachers (47%), with frequent complaints about ambiguity and variability among expert responses [29]. Similar perceptions have been observed outside France, where students preferred MCQs and labeled SCT “too subjective” or “too open to interpretation” [26]. We interpret the acceptability findings as one component of consequences evidence within Messick’s framework: they speak to candidate acceptance, ease of explanation in the event of score challenges, and alignment between learners’ perceptions and the stated purpose of the assessment, rather than to construct validity. Stated preference for the PRT may reflect, at least in part, its simpler scoring format and clearer response options, and does not by itself establish that the PRT is a better instrument. It does, however, support its feasibility and perceived acceptability for summative use, which are important considerations in a potential high-stakes summative assessment context.

This study has several strengths, including the direct comparison of PRT, SCT, and MCQ using matched triads of identical scenarios, and the inclusion of participants across different experience levels (students, residents, and pediatricians). It also has several limitations. First, generalizability is restricted to a single specialty and institutional setting. Second, the marked group-size imbalance, with 538 students, 19 residents, and 18 senior pediatricians, underpowered between-group comparisons; the absence of significant PRT differences across experience levels should not be interpreted as evidence of absence of such differences. In addition, both the SCT reference panel and the senior-clinician group were limited in size and heterogeneous in pediatric subspecialty, which may have constrained the reliability estimates and complicated the interpretation of score comparisons across experience levels. Third, direct response-process evidence, such as cognitive interviews, think-aloud procedures, or response-time analyses, was not collected; the PRT-SCT concordance reported here is best classified as indirect evidence under relations to other variables. Fourth, no predictive validity evidence against performance at the national examination or in subsequent clinical practice was assessed. Fifth, the reliance on author-defined predetermined correct answers, even after multidisciplinary author-panel review, remains a potential source of scoring bias and would benefit from independent external review in any future operational deployment. Finally, our 20-item, multi-domain instrument was designed as a head-to-head pragmatic comparator; the observed Cronbach’s alpha values, although equivalent between formats, remain below thresholds commonly recommended for high-stakes individual decisions.

Conclusions

The PRT showed strong directional concordance with the SCT, comparable but modest internal consistency, and higher acceptability among participants when applied to undergraduate clinical reasoning tasks. However, PRT scores did not significantly discriminate between students, residents, and senior pediatricians, limiting its usefulness for interpretations requiring differentiation across levels of clinical experience. These findings support the PRT as a feasible and acceptable candidate format for assessing normative probabilistic reasoning in undergraduate medical education, but not as a replacement for SCT in contexts where differentiation of expertise is required.
