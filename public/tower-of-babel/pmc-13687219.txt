Integrated transcriptomic analysis identifies an AKT1-associated metabolic-inflammatory signature in osteoporosis
Authors: Chenglong Bao, Ronggui Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoporosis is a significant global health challenge, and low bone mineral density (Low-BMD) is a major clinical feature and risk indicator of osteoporosis. Fatty acid metabolism has been increasingly implicated in bone remodeling and inflammatory regulation, but the transcriptomic links between fatty acid metabolism and immune-inflammatory alterations in Low-BMD remain unclear. We integrated three GEO datasets, including 60 High-BMD controls and 59 Low-BMD subjects. Differential expression analysis, immune infiltration analysis, NMF clustering, WGCNA, pseudotime analysis, and machine learning were performed to identify fatty acid metabolism-related genes associated with Low-BMD. AKT1 knockdown was used to evaluate its potential role in regulating inflammatory mediators. Eighteen fatty acid metabolism-related differentially expressed genes were identified between High-BMD and Low-BMD samples and were enriched in inflammatory, immune, and osteoclast-related pathways. NMF clustering revealed two Low-BMD molecular subtypes with distinct metabolic and immune-inflammatory features. Machine learning identified an AKT1-containing diagnostic signature with good predictive performance, and AKT1 was positively correlated with inflammatory response scores and inflammatory markers. Quantitative Real-time PCR confirmed Akt1 knockdown, and ELISA showed reduced IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β secretion in MC3T3-E1 cells. AKT1 may serve as a fatty acid metabolism-related candidate gene associated with immune-inflammatory remodeling and inflammatory mediator regulation in Low-BMD. These findings suggest a potential AKT1-associated metabolic-inflammatory regulatory relationship and provide candidate biomarkers for future validation in osteoporosis-related bone loss.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10238-026-02263-8.

Introduction

Osteoporosis represents a major global health challenge, affecting over 200 million individuals worldwide, with approximately one-third of women aged over 50 years experiencing osteoporotic fractures, resulting in 8.9 million fractures annually [1]. This metabolic bone disorder is characterized by decreased bone mass, deteriorated microarchitecture, and increased fracture susceptibility, particularly affecting postmenopausal women and the elderly population [2]. Low bone mineral density (Low-BMD) is a key clinical feature and risk indicator of osteoporosis, and understanding the molecular alterations associated with Low-BMD may provide insights into osteoporosis-related bone loss [3]. Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, the molecular mechanisms linking metabolic dysregulation, inflammation, and impaired bone remodeling remain incompletely understood [4, 5].

Recent advances in understanding bone metabolism have revealed the complex interplay between metabolic regulation and skeletal homeostasis, with emerging evidence highlighting fatty acid metabolism as a critical mediator [6, 7]. Fatty acids, beyond their canonical function in energy metabolism, are increasingly recognized as key regulators of bone remodeling and skeletal homeostasis through their roles in cell membrane signaling, inflammatory mediation, and calcium homeostasis [8, 9]. Specifically, alterations in fatty acid profiles may influence bone remodeling through PPARγ-dependent pathways and RANK/RANKL signaling cascades, directly modulating both osteoblast differentiation and osteoclastogenesis [8, 10]. The differential effects of omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids establish a complex regulatory network, where their metabolites serve as crucial signaling molecules in the bone microenvironment [11].

A hallmark of osteoporosis progression is the dynamic pattern of immune cell infiltration, including T cells, B cells, macrophages, and neutrophils into bone tissue [12, 13]. This infiltrative process, significantly modulated by fatty acid-derived metabolites, influences bone metabolism through altered cytokine profiles and immune-mediated regulation of osteoclast differentiation, thereby establishing a complex network between fatty acid metabolism, immune responses, and bone homeostasis [14–16]. Emerging evidence suggests that fatty acid metabolism plays a pivotal role in shaping the inflammatory microenvironment in osteoporosis, where inflammatory cytokines and immune cell activation enhance bone resorption and reduce bone formation [14, 15].

Osteoporosis-related bone loss has been increasingly linked to immune-inflammatory alterations within the bone microenvironment [12, 17]. Inflammation acts as a cornerstone of bone loss, where the infiltration of immune cells and the subsequent overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6) create a state of metabolic stress that disrupts the balance of bone remodelling [18]. This inflammatory process is significantly modulated by fatty acid-derived metabolites, which enhance osteoclast-mediated bone resorption while simultaneously suppressing osteoblast-mediated bone formation [6, 19, 20]. Emerging evidence suggests that metabolic dysregulation may contribute to sustained inflammatory responses, yet the precise drivers connecting fatty acid metabolism to this immune remodeling remain poorly defined [5].

Recent studies have increasingly combined bulk transcriptomics, single-cell transcriptomics, machine learning, and experimental validation to identify disease-associated diagnostic markers and candidate regulatory genes in complex diseases [21–23]. These integrative strategies provide a useful framework for prioritizing biologically relevant genes from high-dimensional transcriptomic datasets.

Our study aims to elucidate the role of fatty acid metabolism in osteoporosis progression and its interaction with immune responses, potentially identifying novel therapeutic targets and biomarkers for early disease detection and intervention. Through comprehensive transcriptomic analysis and advanced computational approaches, explore transcriptomic associations among fatty acid metabolism, inflammatory immune remodeling, and Low-BMD-related bone alterations.

Materials and methods

Data collection and processing

We collected data from three Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets: GSE2208, GSE56815, and GSE7429 [24–26]. These datasets were selected based on their relevance to osteoporosis and their common microarray platform (GPL96), which facilitated integration and comparison. The combined dataset comprised 119 samples, including 60 High-BMD controls and 59 Low-BMD subjects. Detailed information on the included datasets, including sample source, platform, BMD grouping strategy, sample size, menopausal status, available clinical covariates, and exclusion criteria reported in the original GEO records or corresponding publications, is summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Raw expression data from each dataset were downloaded and processed using the R programming environment (version 4.1.0). Data preprocessing involved several steps to ensure data quality and comparability across samples and datasets. First, probes were mapped to their corresponding gene symbols using the annotation information provided by the GPL96 platform. For genes with multiple probes, we retained the probe with the highest mean expression value across all samples to avoid redundancy. The expression data were then log2-transformed and quantile normalized to ensure comparability across samples and datasets.

To address potential batch effects arising from the integration of multiple datasets, we employed the ComBat function from the sva package (version 3.40.0) in R [27]. This empirical Bayes approach adjusts for known batch effects while preserving biological variation of interest. The effectiveness of batch effect removal was assessed through principal component analysis (PCA) visualization before and after batch correction.

Differential expression analysis

Following data preprocessing and integration, we performed differential expression analysis to identify genes with significantly altered expression between Low-BMD subjects and High-BMD controls. This analysis was conducted using the limma package (version 3.50.0) in R [28, 29], which employs linear models and empirical Bayes methods to assess differential expression in microarray data.

The limma model was fitted with BMD group status as the primary variable of interest. We applied the empirical Bayes method to moderate the standard errors of the estimated log-fold changes. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using a threshold of adjusted p-value < 0.05 (FDR correction method). The p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate.

Functional enrichment analysis

To gain insights into the biological processes and pathways potentially affected in Low-BMD, we performed functional enrichment analysis on the identified DEGs. We utilized the clusterProfiler package (version 4.0.5) in R to conduct both Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses [30].

For GO enrichment analysis, we examined three aspects: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). The analysis was performed using the enrichGO function from clusterProfiler, with the following parameters: ont = "BP", "CC", or "MF", pAdjustMethod = "BH", pvalueCutoff = 0.05, and qvalueCutoff = 0.2.

KEGG pathway analysis was conducted using the enrichKEGG function from the same package. We set the parameters as follows: organism = "hsa" (for Homo sapiens), pAdjustMethod = "BH", pvalueCutoff = 0.05, and qvalueCutoff = 0.2. Fatty acid metabolism-related genes were collected from KEGG, Hallmark, Reactome, and WikiPathway gene sets, and duplicate genes were removed before intersection with DEGs.

Immune microenvironment analysis

To characterize the immune microenvironment in Low-BMD and High-BMD samples, we employed multiple computational methods to estimate immune cell composition from the RNA-seq data. First, we applied CIBERSORT (Cell-type Identification By Estimating Relative Subsets Of RNA Transcripts) algorithm with the LM22 signature matrix, which defines 22 immune cell subtypes [31]. The algorithm was run with 1,000 permutations to calculate p-values for the deconvolution.

To complement the CIBERSORT results, we employed xCell, a gene signature-based method for cell type enrichment analysis. The xCell package (version 1.1.0) in R was applied to our normalized gene expression data with default parameters to generate enrichment scores for each cell type.

Furthermore, we performed single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) to quantify immune-related pathway activities. We used a set of 29 immune-related gene signatures derived from He et al. (2018) study. Notably, to estimate inflammation-related transcriptional activity, the Hallmark Inflammatory Response gene set was retrieved from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). The overall inflammatory response score for each sample was quantified using the ssGSEA algorithm to evaluate the global inflammatory response score and its correlation with key metabolic regulators. The ssGSEA analysis was conducted using the GSVA package (version 1.40.1) in R, with method = "ssgsea", kcdf = "Gaussian", and abs.ranking = TRUE parameters.

Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) clustering

To identify molecular subtypes of Low-BMD based on fatty acid metabolism-related gene expression patterns, we employed Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) clustering using the NMF package (version 0.23.0) in R [32, 33]. We selected 18 fatty acid metabolism-related genes that showed significant differential expression between Low-BMD and High-BMD controls (P-value < 0.05) as input features.

Metabolic pathway analysis

To investigate metabolic alterations in Low-BMD and its identified subtypes, we employed Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) and ssGSEA. Hallmark metabolic, inflammatory, and stress-related gene sets were obtained from MSigDB and scored using GSVA and ssGSEA. We utilized the GSVA package (version 1.40.1) in R to perform both analyses. For ssGSEA analysis, we modified the method parameter to "ssgsea" while maintaining other parameters. Both analyses were performed to compare High-BMD controls with Low-BMD patients, and to analyze differences between NMF-derived subtypes. Significantly altered metabolic pathways were identified using limma with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

To identify co-expression modules associated with BMD status and to explore the co-expression context of AKT1, we performed WGCNA using the WGCNA package (version 1.70–3) in R [34]. We performed WGCNA using the genome-wide expression matrix after preprocessing and batch correction. Genes with low expression or low variance were filtered before network construction, and the remaining genes were used as input for co-expression module detection.

The soft-thresholding power was selected based on the scale-free topology criterion. Modules were identified using dynamic tree cutting, and module eigengenes were correlated with BMD group status. Because AKT1 was assigned to the brown module, the top 200 genes in this module were selected for KEGG enrichment analysis.

Pseudotime analysis

To infer Low-BMD-associated transcriptional ordering, we performed pseudotime analysis using all 21,655 genes from the expression matrix. Sample ordering was determined using principal component analysis (PCA) of High-BMD controls and Low-BMD samples with different NMF subtypes. A 3-knot b-spline was fitted to the first two principal components, and each sample was assigned a location on the spline by minimizing Euclidean distance. The distance along the b-spline was used to calculate a pseudotime score, scaled from 0.0 to 10.0. Hierarchical clustering with average linkage was performed using the dendextend package (version 1.9.0) to produce a dendrogram of between-sample relationships.

Machine learning analysis

To identify robust diagnostic genes associated with Low-BMD, we applied multiple machine learning algorithms to the 18 fatty acid metabolism-related differentially expressed genes identified above. Feature selection was performed using Bagged Trees, Bayesian model, Random Forest, Learning Vector Quantization (LVQ), LASSO regression, and Boruta algorithms. For Bagged Trees, Bayesian, and Random Forest models, cross-validation was used to evaluate model accuracy with different numbers of variables. LVQ, LASSO, and Boruta were further used to assess feature importance and selection stability.

Genes selected by different algorithms were integrated using an UpSet plot to identify consensus candidate genes. The overlapping genes were then used to construct an elastic net prediction model using the glmnet package in R. The elastic net model was fitted with Low-BMD status as the outcome variable, and model tuning parameters were selected by cross-validation.

The diagnostic performance of the final model was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves in the training set, test set, and GSE2208 validation dataset. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify model discrimination. Calibration analysis was performed to assess the agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test was used to evaluate model fit. A nomogram was constructed based on selected diagnostic features, and decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to estimate the potential clinical net benefit of the prediction model.

Cell culture and transfection

Mouse MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured in α-MEM (Minimum Essential Medium Alpha) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. For AKT1 silencing, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting AKT1 (si-AKT1) and a negative control siRNA (si-NC) were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions when cells reached 60–70% confluence. Cells and culture supernatants were collected 48 h post-transfection for qPCR and ELISA analyses.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from transfected cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). The concentration and purity of RNA were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using a PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). Quantitative PCR was conducted on an ABI StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) using SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara, Japan). The mRNA expression level of Akt1 was measured to confirm the knockdown efficiency of si-AKT1. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method, with Gapdh used as the internal control.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

After siRNA transfection, the culture supernatants of MC3T3-E1 cells were collected 48 h after transfection and centrifuged to remove cell debris. The secretion levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β in the culture supernatants were measured using mouse-specific ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader, and cytokine concentrations were calculated according to the standard curves generated for each assay. The results were normalized to the corresponding experimental groups and expressed as cytokine secretion levels.

Statistical analysis of experimental data

Statistical analysis for the in vitro experiments was performed using R software (version 4.1.0). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between the si-NC and si-AKT1 groups were evaluated using the unpaired Student’s t-test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001).

Differential expression analysis identified multiple dysregulated genes in Low-BMD samples compared with High-BMD controls (Fig. 1c). By intersecting differentially expressed genes with fatty acid metabolism-related genes, we identified 18 fatty acid metabolism-related differentially expressed genes, which showed different expression patterns between the two groups (Fig. 1d).

These results suggest that fatty acid metabolism-related genes may be involved in Low-BMD-associated alterations through inflammatory and osteoclast-related pathways.

GSVA analysis further showed significant differences in inflammation-related pathways, including IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling, TNFα signaling via NF-κB, and reactive oxygen species pathway (Fig. 2c). Cell type-specific enrichment analysis revealed differential enrichment of osteoblasts, HSCs, preadipocytes, and melanocytes between High-BMD and Low-BMD samples (Fig. 2d, f). ssGSEA-based immune enrichment analysis further showed decreased Tfh cells and aDCs, together with increased pDCs and iDCs, in Low-BMD samples (Fig. 2e, g).

These findings further support a potential link between fatty acid metabolism-related transcriptional changes and immune-inflammatory remodeling in Low-BMD.

Discussion

In this study, we integrated human transcriptomic datasets to investigate fatty acid metabolism-related molecular alterations associated with Low-BMD. By combining differential expression analysis, immune profiling, molecular subtyping, WGCNA, pseudotime inference, machine learning, and experimental validation, we identified an AKT1-associated metabolic-inflammatory signature linked to Low-BMD. Because the analyzed datasets were stratified by High-BMD and Low-BMD status, these findings should be interpreted as Low-BMD-associated molecular alterations with potential relevance to osteoporosis-related bone loss, rather than definitive mechanisms of clinically diagnosed osteoporosis.

Fatty acid metabolism has increasingly been recognized as an important regulator of skeletal homeostasis [5–7]. Beyond serving as an energy source, fatty acids participate in membrane remodeling, mitochondrial β-oxidation, lipid mediator production, oxidative stress regulation, and osteoblast–osteoclast coupling [9, 35]. Previous studies have shown that lipid metabolic disturbances can affect bone remodeling by modulating osteoblast differentiation, osteoclastogenesis, RANKL-related signaling, PPARγ-dependent lineage allocation, and inflammatory cytokine production [10, 11]. Consistent with this biological framework, our pathway-level findings suggest that fatty acid metabolism-related transcriptional changes in Low-BMD are closely associated with inflammatory response, TNF/IL-17 signaling, oxidative stress, and osteoclast-related pathways. These observations support the hypothesis that fatty acid metabolism may contribute to Low-BMD-associated bone remodeling not as an isolated metabolic event, but as part of a broader metabolic-inflammatory regulatory program [5, 6].

Immune-inflammatory remodeling provides another important context for interpreting fatty acid metabolism-related alterations in Low-BMD. Bone remodeling is tightly regulated by immune cells and cytokine networks, and immune-derived signals can influence osteoclast differentiation, osteoblast activity, and bone resorption [36–38]. In this study, the differences in monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, Treg-related signatures, HLA-related signatures, and immune checkpoint-related features suggest that Low-BMD may be accompanied by remodeling of the immune microenvironment. In particular, changes in monocyte-, macrophage-, and dendritic cell-related signatures may reflect alterations in the monocyte–macrophage/dendritic cell axis, which is closely connected with antigen presentation, inflammatory mediator regulation, and osteoclast-related immune regulation [39–42]. The altered expression of representative immune genes, including HLA-DPB1, IDO1, PDCD1, and GZMB, further suggests that Low-BMD-associated immune remodeling may involve antigen presentation, metabolic immune regulation, checkpoint-related immune modulation, and cytotoxic effector activity. Importantly, the immune differences between fatty acid metabolism-related molecular subtypes indicate that the NMF classification is not merely a statistical separation, but may reflect distinct metabolic-inflammatory states within Low-BMD samples. These findings support a potential link between fatty acid metabolism-related transcriptional heterogeneity and immune microenvironmental remodeling, although experimental validation is required to confirm the inferred immune cell changes.

Pseudotime analysis provided an inferred framework to explore how fatty acid metabolism-related genes and immune-inflammatory genes vary across Low-BMD-associated transcriptional states [43]. In this study, genes such as AKT1, ALOX5, IL17RA, MFNG, MGAT4B, and GNB2 showed pseudotime-dependent expression patterns, suggesting coordinated changes among metabolic regulation, inflammatory signaling, and cell communication programs. In particular, ALOX5 is involved in lipid mediator metabolism, IL17RA reflects IL-17-related inflammatory signaling, MGAT4B is associated with N-glycosylation-dependent regulation of membrane proteins, GNB2 participates in G protein-mediated signal transduction, and MFNG is related to Notch signaling regulation [44]. These findings suggest that Low-BMD-associated transcriptional transition may involve broader metabolic-inflammatory and receptor-signaling programs rather than fatty acid metabolism alone. However, because pseudotime analysis was performed on cross-sectional transcriptomic data, it should be interpreted as an inferred ordering of transcriptional states rather than direct evidence of longitudinal disease progression or causality.

The fatty acid metabolism-related molecular subtypes and diagnostic signature identified in this study may provide a useful framework for understanding the heterogeneity of Low-BMD [33]. The NMF-derived subtypes showed distinct metabolic and immune-inflammatory characteristics, suggesting that Low-BMD may comprise transcriptionally heterogeneous states rather than a single uniform molecular phenotype. In parallel, machine learning analysis identified a fatty acid metabolism-related gene signature with potential value for distinguishing Low-BMD from High-BMD samples [45]. These findings suggest that metabolic-inflammatory features may contribute to molecular stratification and biomarker discovery in Low-BMD. However, the clinical translation of these findings remains preliminary. The molecular subtypes should not yet be interpreted as established clinical subgroups, and the prediction model requires validation in larger independent cohorts with standardized expression platforms, detailed clinical information, fracture outcomes, and longitudinal BMD measurements [46–48].

Although AKT1 did not show the highest correlation coefficient with the inflammatory response score among all 18 fatty acid metabolism-related genes, it was repeatedly identified across multiple analytical layers, including differential expression analysis, molecular subtype analysis, metabolic-inflammatory pathway analysis, pseudotime analysis, machine learning, WGCNA, inflammatory correlation analysis, and siRNA-based qPCR validation. Therefore, AKT1 was prioritized as an integrated fatty acid metabolism-related candidate node associated with inflammatory mediator regulation in Low-BMD.

AKT1 emerged as a key candidate gene connecting fatty acid metabolism-related transcriptional changes with inflammatory mediator regulation. AKT1 is a central component of the PI3K/AKT pathway and has been implicated in cell survival, metabolism, inflammatory signaling, and the balance between osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation [49]. In bone biology, PI3K/AKT-related signaling has been implicated in osteoblast survival and differentiation, osteoclast activity, and the maintenance of bone remodeling balance [49, 50]. From a metabolic perspective, AKT signaling can regulate lipid biosynthetic programs through downstream mTORC1/SREBP-related pathways, providing a plausible connection between AKT1 activity and lipid metabolic transcriptional regulation [51–53]. In the present study, AKT1 was retained as an important fatty acid metabolism-related candidate gene and showed positive associations with inflammatory response scores and inflammatory markers, including IL17RA, IL6R, and TNF. Moreover, qPCR confirmed reduced Akt1 mRNA expression after si-AKT1 transfection, and ELISA showed that AKT1 knockdown reduced the secretion levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β in MC3T3-E1 cells. These findings support a potential role of AKT1 in maintaining pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion in osteoblast-lineage cells. However, because lipid uptake, fatty acid oxidation, lipid accumulation, lipidomics, and metabolic flux were not directly measured, our findings should be interpreted as evidence for an AKT1-associated metabolic-inflammatory axis rather than direct proof that AKT1 reprograms fatty acid metabolism.

An important consideration of this study is that the bioinformatics analyses were based on human transcriptomic datasets, whereas the experimental validation was performed in mouse MC3T3-E1 cells. AKT1/Akt1 is a conserved component of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, and AKT family proteins are broadly involved in cellular survival, metabolism, proliferation, and inflammatory regulation across mammalian systems [54]. Therefore, MC3T3-E1 cells provide a useful preliminary osteoblast-lineage model to test whether Akt1 knockdown affects inflammatory mediator expression. MC3T3-E1 cells were selected because they are a well-established murine pre-osteoblast model widely used for studying osteoblast differentiation, extracellular matrix maturation, and mineralization [55]. Although human osteoblast-like cell lines such as hFOB1.19 and MG-63 could provide additional validation, each has specific limitations. hFOB1.19 is a conditionally immortalized human fetal osteoblast cell line with temperature-sensitive culture requirements, whereas MG-63 is an osteosarcoma-derived osteoblast-like cell line and may not fully represent normal osteoblast biology [56, 57]. Thus, the use of MC3T3-E1 cells is appropriate for preliminary functional validation, but species- and cell-type-specific differences cannot be excluded. Future studies should validate the AKT1–inflammatory mediator axis in human osteoblast-like cells, primary human osteoblasts, bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells, or patient-derived bone-related samples [58].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all transcriptomic analyses were based on public microarray datasets with limited clinical information, and the High-BMD/Low-BMD grouping strategy limits direct inference regarding clinically diagnosed osteoporosis. Second, immune infiltration analyses were inferred from bulk transcriptomic profiles; therefore, the observed immune cell signatures should be considered computational estimates rather than direct measurements of immune cell composition [31]. Third, pseudotime analysis was performed using cross-sectional samples and should be interpreted as an inferred ordering of transcriptional states rather than longitudinal disease progression [59]. Fourth, although the fatty acid metabolism-related prediction model showed potential discriminative ability, its clinical utility remains preliminary and requires validation in larger independent cohorts with standardized platforms, fracture outcomes, and longitudinal BMD measurements [47]. Finally, although AKT1 knockdown reduced the secretion levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, lipid uptake, fatty acid oxidation, lipid accumulation, lipidomics, osteoblast differentiation, mineralization, and osteoclastogenesis were not directly assessed. Similar integrative studies in musculoskeletal disorders have emphasized the importance of combining computational screening with experimental validation to clarify the biological relevance of candidate regulatory axes [60]. Therefore, further studies integrating single-cell sequencing, lipidomics, metabolic flux assays, human osteoblast-lineage models, and functional bone cell experiments are needed to clarify the mechanistic role of the AKT1-associated metabolic-inflammatory axis in osteoporosis-related bone loss.

In conclusion, this study identifies AKT1 as a fatty acid metabolism-related candidate gene associated with inflammatory mediator regulation in Low-BMD. The integrated bioinformatics analyses suggest that fatty acid metabolism-related transcriptional alterations are linked to immune-inflammatory remodeling, osteoclast-related pathways, and molecular heterogeneity in Low-BMD samples. Experimental AKT1 knockdown further supports its potential role in regulating pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, as reflected by reduced IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β levels. These findings provide a candidate AKT1-associated metabolic-inflammatory axis for future mechanistic validation and may contribute to biomarker development for osteoporosis-related bone loss.
