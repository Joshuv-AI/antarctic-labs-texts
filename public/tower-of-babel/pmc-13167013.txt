Evaluation of postoperative stereotactic ablative radiotherapy vs. conventional fractionation in head and neck sarcomas
Authors: Alper Kahvecioglu, Mervenur Bay, Mustafa Cengiz, Sezin Yuce Sari, Oguz Kuscu, Ibrahim Gullu, Gozde Yazici
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of conventional fractionated radiotherapy (CFRT) and stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR) in the postoperative management of head and neck sarcomas.Materials and methodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 40 patients with resected head and neck sarcomas who had received postoperative radiotherapy with either CFRT (n = 30) or SABR (n = 10). The median radiation dose was 35 Gy (range: 24–40 Gy in 3–5 fractions) for SABR and 62 Gy (range: 60–70 Gy in 30–35 fractions) for CFRT.ResultsMedian follow-up was 60 months. Five-year overall survival (OS), local recurrence-free survival (LRFS), and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) were comparable between the CFRT and SABR groups. In multivariate analysis, R2 resection was independently associated with worse OS (p = 0.02) and DMFS (p = 0.02). Both R2 resection (p = 0.009) and tumor size ≥ 5 cm (p = 0.01) were identified as independent predictors of poorer LRFS. The overall incidence of grade ≥ 3 late toxicity was 10%, with no significant difference between SABR and CFRT (p = 0.25). Notably, three of the four severe toxicity events occurred in patients with tumors involving the orbit.ConclusionsCFRT remains the established postoperative standard for head and neck sarcomas. Our findings suggest that SABR may also be considered for carefully selected patients, although its use requires caution in anatomically sensitive locations such as the orbit. Further prospective studies are needed to confirm its safety and to better define patient selection criteria.

Introduction

Head and neck sarcomas pose significant therapeutic challenges due to their complex anatomy and proximity to critical structures, making their management more demanding than that of sarcomas in the extremities or trunk [1]. Surgical resection with clear margins remains the mainstay of treatment and the addition of perioperative radiotherapy (RT) has been shown to reduce local recurrence rates [2]. However, due to their rarity, head and neck sarcomas are rarely included in randomized clinical trials. Most available data are extrapolated from studies on extremity and trunk sarcomas, which suggest that both neoadjuvant and adjuvant RT are viable strategies, each with distinct benefits and limitations [3, 4]. Neoadjuvant RT allows smaller treatment volumes and lower doses, potentially reducing long-term toxicities such as fibrosis, but is associated with a higher risk of postoperative wound complications [5–7].

Although the efficacy of RT is well established, the optimal fractionation schedule, whether preoperative or postoperative, remains uncertain. Conventionally fractionated RT (CFRT), typically delivered at 1.8 to 2 Gy per fraction, has long been considered the standard. However, the relative radioresistance of sarcomas has led to growing interest in hypofractionated regimens, such as stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR), which delivers higher doses per fraction over fewer sessions [8]. While the use of preoperative hypofractionated RT is increasing in clinical practice, its role in the postoperative setting, especially for head and neck sarcomas, remains underexplored [9]. This study aims to compare the clinical outcomes and toxicity profiles of postoperative CFRT and SABR in patients with head and neck sarcomas.

Materials and methods

A retrospective evaluation was conducted on the medical records of head and neck sarcoma patients treated with RT in our department between 2000 and 2023, utilizing data from both the institutional electronic information system and individual patient files. Patients who had received neoadjuvant or definitive RT, those with initially distant metastasis, cases lacking pathological confirmation of tumors, and patients with insufficient follow-up data were excluded from the analysis. After these exclusions, 40 patients remained eligible for inclusion in the study. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board.

Staging was conducted according to the guidelines of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition [10]. All patients presented with resectable tumors at diagnosis and underwent upfront wide local excision, with reconstruction performed as clinically indicated. The extent of resection was determined through a comprehensive review of surgical notes, pathology reports, and postoperative radiological findings. Resection status was categorized as follows: R0 indicated clear surgical margins, R1 signified microscopic residual tumor at the margin, and R2 was defined as gross residual disease remaining after surgery. Adjuvant RT was initiated following a minimum postoperative interval of four weeks to allow for sufficient postoperative wound healing. The choice of RT fractionation, either CFRT or SABR was determined at the discretion of the treating physician. Patients who received fraction doses of less than 3 Gy were classified into the CFRT group, while those receiving fraction doses of 6 Gy or higher were included in the SABR group. Notably, none of the patients in this cohort received moderately hypofractionated regimens (3–5 Gy per fraction) and none underwent elective nodal irradiation. Furthermore, none of the patients received targeted therapy or immunotherapy as part of their initial treatment. The decision to administer adjuvant chemotherapy was individualized and left to the discretion of the treating medical oncologist, based on the specific clinical characteristics of each patient.

After completing RT, patients were followed up with regular radiological assessments and physical exams conducted at varying intervals. Toxicity data were gathered from patient records, the institutional electronic information system, and phone interviews with patients. Treatment-related toxicities were evaluated according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0.

Statistical analysis was conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), including descriptive statistics, overall survival (OS), local recurrence-free survival (LRFS), and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS). The variables between the CFRT and SABR groups were compared using independent samples t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, or chi-square test, depending on whether they were numerical or categorical, and based on their distribution characteristics, including normality. The follow-up duration was calculated from the first day of RT, with time-to-event data recorded from the start of RT until the last follow-up, death, or recurrence, whichever occurred first. OS was defined as the time from RT initiation to death from any cause, while LRFS and DMFS were defined as the time from RT initiation to either local recurrence or distant metastasis, respectively, or death. The local control rate was determined by calculating the proportion of patients who remained free of local recurrence throughout the follow-up period relative to the total number of patients. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all tests.

Results

The baseline characteristics of patients, tumors, and treatments are summarized in Table 1 and Figure 1. All patients underwent surgical resection prior to RT with R0, R1, and R2 resection rates of 25%, 43%, and 32%, respectively. The median SABR dose was 35 Gy (range: 24–40 Gy), delivered over three to five fractions, while the median CFRT dose was 62 Gy (range: 60–70 Gy), delivered over 30 to 35 fractions. Baseline characteristics between the SABR and CFRT groups were generally similar, with the exception of tumor origin. Bone sarcomas were significantly more common in the SABR group than in the CFRT group (40% vs. 23%, p = 0.03). Although the median biologically effective dose calculated using an α/β ratio of 3 Gy (BED3Gy) value (116.7 Gy vs. 106.7 Gy, p = 0.09) and R2 resection rate (50% vs. 27%, p = 0.24) were higher in the SABR group, these differences were not statistically significant.

The median follow-up duration was 60 months (range: 4–250 months). During this period, 10 patients (25%) developed local recurrence, and 8 patients (20%) developed distant metastases. Regional recurrence was observed in 3 patients (7.5%), with tumor histologies comprising one case of angiosarcoma and two cases of synovial sarcoma. A total of 15 deaths were observed during the follow-up period.

The 5-year OS, LRFS and DMFS rates were 71%, 66% and 66%, respectively. Univariate analysis results, assessing the impact of various factors on survival, are presented in Table 2. Patients ≥ 45 years, those with T3–4 tumors, those who underwent R2 resections, and those with tumors ≥ 5 cm in size had significantly lower 5-year OS, LRFS, and DMFS compared to patients with more favorable characteristics. Although SABR showed numerically higher 5-year OS (80% vs. 67%, p = 0.57), LRFS (80% vs. 60%, p = 0.33), and DMFS (80% vs. 60%, p = 0.48) compared to CFRT, none of these differences reached statistical significance.

In multivariate analysis, R2 resection emerged as the only independent negative prognostic factor for OS (HR = 4.09, 95% CI: 1.2–13.8, p = 0.02]. For LRFS, both tumor size ≥ 5 cm (HR = 6.9, 95% CI: 1.4–33.6, p = 0.01) and R2 resection (HR = 4.8, 95% CI: 1.4–15.6, p = 0.009) were identified as significant independent negative prognostic factors. For DMFS, R2 resection was identified as the only independent negative prognostic factor (HR = 3.5, 95% CI: 1.2–10.5, p = 0.02).

Acute toxicity rates were not analyzed due to incomplete medical records. The overall incidence of grade ≥ 3 late toxicity was 10% (n = 4). Of these, one patient with orbital leiomyosarcoma developed grade 4 retinopathy following an R2 resection and postoperative SABR (40 Gy in 5 fractions). Another patient with an orbital hemangiopericytoma originating from the lacrimal gland experienced grade 3 xerophthalmia after R1 resection and CFRT (64 Gy in 32 fractions). A third patient, also with hemangiopericytoma of the lacrimal gland, developed grade 3 xerophthalmia after R2 resection and CFRT (60 Gy in 30 fractions). Additionally, one patient with maxillary sinus chondrosarcoma developed grade 3 brain necrosis following R1 resection and SABR (35 Gy in 5 fractions). The incidence of grade ≥ 3 late toxicity was 20% for the SABR group and 7.5% for the CFRT group, with no statistically significant difference between the two (p = 0.25).

Discussion

This study indicates that both CFRT and SABR achieve comparable survival rates in the postoperative management of head and neck sarcomas. However, although not statistically significant, severe late toxicity was numerically higher in the SABR group. Additionally, prognostic factors such as larger tumor size and incomplete resection were found to significantly influence patient outcomes.

Guiding treatment for head and neck sarcomas is particularly challenging due to the rarity of these tumors and the difficulty of conducting randomized trials, with most evidence coming from retrospective studies or research on sarcomas in other regions like the extremities or trunk. While wide local excision with clear margins is critical, achieving this is often difficult in head and neck sarcomas due to the complex anatomy and proximity to vital structures, which frequently leads to positive margins. This challenge contributes to the higher local recurrence rates seen in head and neck sarcomas compared to extremity sarcomas [11]. Adjuvant RT plays a crucial role in cases where radical surgery with clear margins cannot be achieved, as retrospective studies have shown that it significantly improves local control rates [2, 12, 13]. Although adjuvant RT is primarily used to enhance local control without significantly influencing survival in most sarcomas, a National Cancer Database (NCDB) analysis of 1,142 head and neck sarcoma patients also revealed a significant OS benefit from adjuvant RT in cases with R1 or R2 resections [14]. This is largely due to local recurrences in the head and neck region being the primary cause of mortality in most patients, underscoring the importance of local control as a critical prognostic factor for survival. In our study, only 25% of patients achieved a R0 resection, with incomplete resection emerging as the sole negative prognostic factor for OS, aligning with findings reported in the literature. Despite the high rate of incomplete resections, local recurrence occurred in only 10 patients (25%), highlighting the important role of adjuvant RT in optimizing postoperative outcomes in this patient population.

The role of RT in the multidisciplinary management of sarcomas is well established through numerous large-scale studies, yet research continues to refine the optimal timing and techniques for RT delivery. Neoadjuvant RT, while offering the potential to improve resectability by shrinking tumors, carries a higher risk of postoperative wound complications compared to postoperative RT. Additionally, tumor progression during the course of preoperative RT may compromise the feasibility of achieving successful surgical resection. Although higher postoperative wound complication rates are particularly evident in extremity sarcomas, O’Sullivan et al. [3] reported a 20% incidence of major wound complications in head and neck sarcomas treated with preoperative RT. While this is lower than the 35% observed in extremity sarcomas, it remains a notable concern [5]. In our institutional approach, surgical resection is prioritized for all patients with initially resectable head and neck sarcomas, with postoperative RT employed as needed, based on the considerations outlined above.

A review of the literature on RT techniques reveals that hypofractionated regimens are more commonly employed in preoperative RT protocols [15, 16]. However, sarcomas’ inherent radioresistance, further exacerbated by hypoxia in the tumor microenvironment during the postoperative period, suggests that hypofractionated RT regimens in the postoperative setting may provide greater benefits. Given the α/β ratio for sarcomas, which ranges from 2 to 6 Gy, higher fraction doses could potentially enhance tumor control and improve therapeutic outcomes [17, 18]. Additionally, hypofractionated regimens shorten the overall treatment duration, improving patient adherence while reducing the financial burden and time toxicity associated with extended therapy [19]. However, in areas like the head and neck, where critical structures are present, increasing the fraction dose may heighten the risk of late toxicity, as late-responding tissues are more sensitive to fraction size than to the overall radiation dose. Although few in number, retrospective studies in the literature have reported favorable outcomes with the use of postoperative moderately hypofractionated RT regimens for various types of sarcomas [20–22]. To the best of our knowledge, no data are currently available in the literature regarding the postoperative application of SABR for head and neck sarcomas. In this study, despite the SABR group exhibiting a nearly twofold higher R2 resection rate compared to the CFRT group, albeit not statistically significant, comparable survival outcomes were observed in both groups. These findings raise the hypothesis that the unique radiobiological properties of SABR might help mitigate the poor prognosis typically associated with R2 resections, identified in our results as the sole independent negative prognostic factor for OS. The comparable rates of grade 3 or higher late toxicity between the SABR and CFRT groups provide further encouragement for the potential of SABR in clinical practice. However, although not statistically significant, the incidence of grade 3 or higher late toxicity was numerically higher in the SABR group. Notably, three of the four patients who experienced grade 3 or higher late toxicity had tumors located in the orbit. This observation suggests that the primary contributing factor to the observed toxicities in these cases was the challenging anatomical location of the tumor rather than the RT technique employed.

Although this study is the first to establish in the literature that both SABR and CFRT are effective postoperative RT options for patients with head and neck sarcomas, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations. First, the SABR cohort consisted of only 10 patients, which is very small and severely limits the statistical power of the analyses. With such small numbers, statistical findings may be misleading, and the apparent trends observed for variables such as tumor location, T stage, and BED in Table 1 should therefore be interpreted with caution. The retrospective design of the study inherently introduces selection bias, as treatment decisions were made at the discretion of the treating physician based on individual patient characteristics. Moreover, late toxicities were recorded retrospectively and may thus be incomplete or biased, while acute toxicity data were not available, precluding a comprehensive evaluation of treatment safety. While the variables between the CFRT and SABR groups were generally well balanced, the limited sample size may have prevented certain differences, such as the rate of R2 resections, from reaching statistical significance, thereby impacting group homogeneity. Finally, the inclusion of both bone and soft tissue sarcomas contributes to histological heterogeneity, which may reduce the generalizability of our findings, although this was largely unavoidable given the rarity of head and neck sarcomas.

Conclusions

Conventional fractionated radiotherapy remains to be the established postoperative standard for head and neck sarcomas. Our findings suggest that SABR may be considered in carefully selected patients, but its use warrants caution in anatomically sensitive sites such as the orbit. Given the very small SABR cohort and the retrospective design, prospective studies are needed to validate safety, clarify comparative effectiveness, and better define patient selection.
