Cardiometabolic associations of SGLT-2 inhibitors on epicardial adiposity and left ventricular function in type 2 diabetes mellitus with coronary artery disease: insights from Malaysian EpiCAD study
Authors: Wan Ahmad Syazani Mohamed, Hasniza Zaman Huri, Ahmad Syadi Mahmood Zuhdi, Samshol Sukahri, Khairul Nisa’ Ishak, Mohd Nasiruddin Mansor, Han Yin Lim, Nurakmal Baharum, Azrul Fahmi Jaharudin, Nur Ain Zahidah Zainudin, Aswir Abd Rashed, Norizan Rosli, Mohd Fairulnizal Md Noh, Amira Hajirah Abd Jamil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) is an emerging cardiometabolic risk factor in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). While SGLT-2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i) offer cardiovascular benefits, their specific effects on EAT and left ventricular (LV) remodeling and function in Asian populations remain understudied. This study investigated the relationship between SGLT-2i on cardiometabolic parameters, EAT thickness and LV function in Malaysian patients with T2DM and coronary artery disease (CAD). A total of 360 patients with T2DM and CAD were divided into non-SGLT-2i and SGLT-2i groups. Anthropometric, cardiometabolic, and echocardiographic measurements were analyzed at baseline and post-6 months for 302 patients who competed the study (n = 151 per group). Both groups showed improvements in glycemic control (fasting blood glucose − 1.7 vs. − 2.6 mmol/L; HbA1c − 0.3% vs. − 0.6%; all p < 0.001). Broader cardiometabolic benefits were observed predominantly among SGLT-2i group, including greater reductions in body mass index (– 0.7 vs. − 0.3 kg/m2) and serum lipid concentrations (all p < 0.001), a marked decrease in EAT thickness (–1 .5 mm vs. + 0.6 mm, p < 0.001), and concomitant improvements in left ventricular structure, with reductions in LV mass (– 20 vs. + 7.8 g) and increased LV ejection fraction (+ 4.6% vs. − 1.0%) (all p ≤ 0.01). Within the SGLT-2i group, EAT reduction was associated with lower body weight and BMI (p < 0.05) but was independent of glycemic changes. Reductions in body weight and BMI were associated with improvements in LV diameter and mass (p < 0.05) and although not directly mediated by changes in EAT. Beyond glycemic control, SGLT-2i was associated with reduced EAT thickness and improved LV remodeling and function in Malaysian T2DM patients with CAD. The reduction in EAT thickness specifically was associated with lower body weight and BMI. These findings provide Asian population–specific evidence with distinct cardiometabolic and adiposity traits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53191-2.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a progressive metabolic disorder with cardiovascular disease (CVD) remain as the leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally. Over 75% of diabetes cases and CVD-related deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries1 with Malaysia having the highest prevalence of T2DM in Southeast Asia for the last decade2, imposing significant economic burden. Indeed, genetic predisposition plays a key role in cardiac complications among T2DM patients, with race and ethnicity influencing pathophysiology and metabolic characteristics3. Asians exhibit reduced β-cell function with significant decline in insulin sensitivity and metabolic vulnerabilities associated with more pronounced visceral adiposity, predisposing them to a higher risk for developing T2DM lower body mass index (BMI)4,5.

Emerging evidence indicates that excess epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), a specialized visceral fat deposit surrounding the heart contributes to the pathogenesis of atherosclerotic CVD among T2DM patients. Its anatomical proximity to the myocardium allows EAT-secreted adipokines and pro-inflammatory cytokines, including leptin, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) to directly influence both cardiac and coronary function, contributing to myocardial inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and atherosclerosis6,7. Normal EAT thickness ranges between 3.0 to 5.0mm8,9, but varies across ethnicities; 2.2 –4.9 mm in White populations, 1.3–3.7 mm in Black populations10,11 and generally lower in Asians (1.1 –1.5 mm in Koreans, 2.6 mm in Indians)12,13. We have previously reported that healthy Malaysians have thicker EAT of 3.7 mm, exceeding other Asian cohorts14,15. Evidently, EAT accumulation is more pronounced in patients with T2DM16 and is consistently associated with adverse left ventricular remodeling17, impaired cardiac function18,19, supporting EAT as a clinically relevant marker of cardiometabolic risk among this high-risk population.

Given its rapid metabolism, organ-specific fat characteristics, and clinical measurability, EAT is now regarded as a modifiable risk factor and a promising therapeutic target. Sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i), an emerging class of oral antidiabetic agents, have demonstrated distinct cardiovascular benefits via its ability to modulate lipid metabolism and visceral adiposity. Beyond their primary glucose-lowering effects via glycosuria, SGLT-2i exert multifaceted cardiometabolic benefits that are independent of immediate hemodynamic changes20,21. By promoting urinary glucose excretion, SGLT-2i shift substrate utilization from glucose towards lipids, accompanied by increased lipolysis, hepatic ketogenesis, and mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation22–26. This metabolic adaptation contributes to decreased total adipose mass and reduced ectopic lipid deposition23. Reductions in EAT have been reported with SGLT-2i in many clinical studies, even with modest or non-significant changes in body weight and/or BMI27–30. Notably, these changes often show inconsistent correlation with glycemic control and HbA1c reduction, underscoring a substantial glycemia-independent component to SGLT-2i lipid-remodeling actions.

Collectively, metabolic adaptations, improvement in lipid distribution and body weight appear to contribute to the EAT-lowering effects of SGLT-2i, though the association remains inconsistent. The complex and multifaceted role of EAT, along with its response to SGLT-2i, has yet to be fully characterized, particularly in populations higher predisposition to EAT accumulation across ethnicities. In the Malaysian cohort, unique demographic, genetic, and cardiometabolic factors may influence these effects. Therefore, the present study aims to investigate the cardiometabolic effects of SGLT-2i on EAT thickness and its associations with left ventricular (LV) functions, among T2DM patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) in a Malaysian clinical setting.

Methods

Study design and setting

The study is a single-center, prospective observational cohort study conducted at the cardiovascular ward and outpatient clinic of the University Malaya Medical Center (UMMC), Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Supplementary Table 1). The study received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of UMMC (MREC ID No: 2020117-9213) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment. The study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (identifier: NCT06128096) and Malaysia’s National Medical Research Register (NMRR ID-22-02966-ENM).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

A total of 415 patients were screened between June 2022 and August 2024 (Fig. 1). After screening process, eligible patients were recruited and divided into the SGLT-2i group (those who received SGLT-2i upon admission to the ward or attended the outpatient clinic) and the non-SGLT-2i group (those who received other oral antidiabetic drugs (OADs) apart from SGLT-2i). The SGLT-2i prescribed in this study were dapagliflozin 10 mg once daily (OD) or empagliflozin (10 mg OD and 25 mg OD). Principal inclusion criteria were Malaysian patients aged ≥ 18 years old, diagnosed with T2DM (and not any other type of diabetes), on stable-dose OAD pharmacotherapy for at least 3 months, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) > 30 ml/min/1.73 m2, and an established diagnosis of CAD. Participants were excluded if they were contraindicated for SGLT-2i (history of diabetic ketoacidosis, repeated urinary tract infections, CKD stage 4 or 5), or were taking any drugs that could cause drug-drug interactions with SGLT-2i (phenytoin, phenobarbital, rifampicin, and ritonavir). Throughout the 6-month study period, diet/exercise therapy, other medications, and combination antihyperglycemic agents were maintained without changes from baseline.

Anthropometric and cardiometabolic assessment

Anthropometric measurements (weight, height, body mass index, waist circumference, hip circumference, and waist-to-hip ratio) and blood pressure were recorded. Venous blood samples were collected from the antecubital vein following a 12-h fast at baseline and after 6 months follow up. Biochemical analyzes included fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), insulin and lipid profile comprising total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL). The definitions of cardiometabolic parameters followed the Malaysian Clinical Practice Guidelines on the Management of Obesity, Second Edition, 2023, and the Malaysian Clinical Practice Guidelines on the Management of Type Two Diabetes Mellitus, Sixth Edition, 2020. Based on these guidelines, FBG of ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or random blood glucose of ≥ 11.1 mmol/L is a diagnostic value for T2DM, overweight was defined as a BMI of 23 kg/m2 or greater, while obesity is defined as a BMI of 27.5 kg/m2 or greater.

Echocardiographic measurement

A comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography was performed using Philips Xcelera R3.3 software (Best, The Netherlands). Patients were positioned in the left lateral decubitus position to obtain optimal measurements in the apical two- and four-chambers view, as well as long- and short-axis view. Parasternal and two-dimensional echocardiography and Doppler flow imaging were captured from standard parasternal and apical positions. All measurements were conducted by an appointed sonographer who was blinded of the clinical and quantitative analysis data.

EAT thickness.

EAT appears as an echo-free space between the visceral layer of the pericardium and the outer layer of the myocardium. In the parasternal long-axis view, intraventricular septum and aortic root were used as a reference point. EAT thickness was measured at the thickest portion of the hypoechoic region between the epicardium and parietal pericardium. Using the aortic annulus and right ventricular free wall as the landmarks, measurements were aligned with the midline of the ultrasound beam and recorded at end-systole. To ensure reproducibility, values were averaged across three consecutive cardiac cycles for all subsequent analyzes (Fig. 2).

Systolic and diastolic functions of the left ventricle.

Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was calculated using the modified Simpson’s biplanar method, and the value were expressed as percentage. The same method was also applied to measure LV end-diastolic and end-systolic volume, and expressed as millilitre (ml). The LV diameter (measured in centimetre) was measured in the apical four-chamber view. To calculate LV mass (LVM), the measurements were done by using M-mode with the formula:1.04 × ((EDD + PWT + IVST)3 - EDD3) × 0.8 + 0.6, whereby EDD stands for LV end-diastolic diameter, PWT denotes LV posterior wall thickness and IVST represents interventricular septal thickness. For the LVM index (LVMI), the calculation is as follows: LVM is divided by the patient’s body surface area (BSA), which is calculated as: BSA = 0.007184 × Weight (kg)0.425 × Height (cm)0.725. Doppler ultrasonography measured early (E) and late (A) diastolic filling velocity (E/A ratio) to solidify diastolic function.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyzes were performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range (IQR)), and categorical data as count percentages. Multivariable linear regression was employed to assess the independent association between SGLT-2i therapy and changes in EAT thickness. This allowed for adjustment of multiple continuous and categorical covariates, and provided the calculation of mean-based effect sizes (β coefficients). Comparisons between the SGLT-2i and non-SGLT-2i groups were performed by independent sample t-test and Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous data. For categorical data, the comparison between SGLT-2i and non-SGLT-2i group were performed using Chi-Square test and Fisher’s exact test. The relationship between EAT thickness changes and cardiometabolic parameters and LV functions was examined using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. To account for the risk of Type I error due to multiple comparisons across secondary cardiometabolic and echocardiographic outcomes, a Bonferroni correction was applied, and the adjusted significance thresholds were used for interpretation. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant for all analyzes.

Results

Baseline characteristics

At the end of enrolment, 360 patients were recruited, comprising 180 SGLT-2i patients and 180 non-SGLT-2i patients. Of these, 302 patients (151 SGLT-2i patients, 151 non-SGLT-2 patients) completed the follow-up (Fig. 1). Malay ethnicity and male patients were more represented in the study, accounting for 35.4% and 70.2% of the participants, respectively (Table 1). Baseline characteristics were generally well-balanced, although the SGLT-2i group exhibited higher rates of heart failure and baseline differences in LVEF, LVM, and LVMI, alongside varying use of diuretics, metformin, and statins. These variations were anticipated in our cohort31–33, as they align with the 2023 Malaysia Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Management of Heart Failure (5th Edition), which recommend SGLT-2i as second-line therapy to enhance cardiovascular outcomes and reduce hospitalizations in patients with adverse cardiac profiles.

Changes in cardiometabolic parameters, EAT thickness and LV functions

While both groups achieved significant glycemic improvement, SGLT-2i treatment was associated with further reductions in lipid biomarkers and body compositions (p < 0.001). EAT thickness decreased significantly with SGLT-2i, showing a 29% reduction (− 1.5 mm), whereas the non-SGLT-2i group exhibited a 13% increase (+ 0.6 mm) from baseline (Fig. 3). Over the 6-month observation period, SGLT-2 inhibition was also associated with significant improvements in LVEF (4.6 ± 1.8%, p < 0.001), as well as reductions in LVM (− 20 g, p < 0.001) and LVMI (− 8.6 ± 37.2, p = 0.011) (Table 2).

SGLT-2i treatment showed a significant association with the change in EAT thickness (β coefficients = − 0.637; p < 0.001) (Table 3). These findings support the observation that SGLT-2i is consistently linked to EAT thickness alterations within this prospective cohort study.

Cardiometabolic effects of SGLT-2i towards EAT thickness and its associations with LV functions

The significant glycemic and pleiotropic effects observed over 6 months in both were analyzed for their associations with changes in EAT thickness (Table 4). Notably, changes in glycemic parameters did not correlate with EAT thickness. Reductions in weight and BMI secondary to SGLT-2 inhibition were significantly associated with decreased EAT thickness (p < 0.05). Further analysis within the SGLT-2i group showed that both weight and BMI were significantly associated with LVEDD (weight: 95% CI 0.006–0.332, p = 0.037; BMI: 95% CI 0.012–0.337, p = 0.031), LVESD (weight: 95% CI 0.025–0.349, p = 0.021; BMI: 95% CI 0.012–0.337, p = 0.044), and LVM (weight: 95% CI 0.049–0.394, p = 0.011; BMI: 95% CI 0.039–0.385, p = 0.015) (Table 5). Although the correlations were weak to moderate, these findings suggest that weight and BMI were significantly associated with the changes in LV diameter and mass within the SGLT-2i group, independently of changes in EAT thickness.

Discussion

The current findings provide new insights into the cardiometabolic benefits of SGLT-2i among Malaysian T2DM patients with CAD. The observed effects on EAT thickness and LV function are consistent with the growing evidence linking SGLT-2i use to metabolic effects beyond glycemic control. Notably, SGLT-2i-associated reductions in weight and BMI, are correlated to both reduction in epicardial adiposity and improvements in LV structure and function. These results underscore the broader metabolic advantages of SGLT-2i, highlighting their relevance in comprehensive cardiovascular risk management.

EAT expansion in T2DM is driven by metabolic dysregulation17–19, as chronic hyperglycemia activates lipogenic transcription factors that promote de novo lipogenesis and triglyceride accumulation34. This pathological condition is compounded by impaired adipogenic differentiation favoring hypertrophy35, with consequent inflammation and extracellular matrix remodeling exacerbating lipid accumulation and dysfunction21,36. SGLT-2i-induced glycosuria has been shown to induce a metabolic shift towards fatty acid and ketone utilization37,38, creating a transient fasting-mimetic state that enhances systemic and myocardial fat utilization26,39. Consistent with this, the current study suggests that weight and BMI reduction, rather than direct glycemic improvement, is associated with changes in EAT thickness among SGLT-2i–treated patients.

Numerous large-scale, imaging-based studies have consistently reported a strong correlation between EAT thickness/volume and obesity indices with clinical studies increasingly support a direct effect of SGLT-2i on epicardial adiposity12,35,40. Clinical studies providing mechanistic insight in patients with T2DM and stable CAD demonstrate that dapagliflozin reduces EAT thickness by nearly 20% within 4 weeks and selectively suppresses EAT glucose uptake, consistent with depot-specific metabolic effects41. Longer-term interventions with canagliflozin and dapagliflozin show reductions in EAT volume over 6 months that are more closely associated with concurrent reductions in body weight29,42.

Beyond its role as a lipid storage depot, EAT is increasingly recognized as an active immunometabolic organ that modulates vascular and myocardial remodeling in CAD43. EAT shares a direct microcirculation with the myocardium, facilitating the paracrine secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, adipokines, and free fatty acids that drive endothelial dysfunction, atherosclerosis, myocardial inflammation and interstitial fibrosis18,44. In dyslipidemic states, lipid-immune interplay contributes to vascular and myocardial injury associated with fibrotic remodeling in CAD45,46, with systemic markers of myeloid activation correlating with subclinical atherosclerosis47. The reduction in EAT thickness observed with SGLT-2i in our cohort likely reflects broader modulation of this cardiometabolic axis, with improvements in lipid profiles potentially attenuating the underlying inflammatory milieu. Supporting this, human mechanistic data demonstrate that SGLT-2i downregulates inflammatory pathways including nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B (NF-κB) and Janus kinase/signal transducers and activators of transcription (JAK/STAT), reduces immune cell infiltration, and promotes lipid remodeling towards less oxidative and anti-ferroptotic profiles within EAT, suggesting a phenotypic shift towards a more cardioprotective adipose tissue secretome48. Although the absence of biomarkers such as interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), IL-6, TNF-α, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β)[49] limits confirmation of reduced inflammatory burden, the observed changes support the role of SGLT-2i as modulators of the lipoinflammatory axis beyond glucose lowering.

While EAT reduction appears to correlate with decreased LV mass and improved LVEF, the causal nature of this relationship remains unclear. Observational studies have linked increased EAT volumes with greater LV mass, impaired myocardial strain, and diastolic dysfunction50; however, these relationships remain correlational, and direct causality has not been established. The improvements observed with SGLT-2i are likely multifactorial, arising from hemodynamic unloading, natriuresis, afterload reduction, and myocardial metabolic adaptations, in addition to EAT-mediated effects51. Similarly, EAT may contribute to LV remodeling through immunometabolic mechanism, impairing LV relaxation and filling independently of changes in ventricular mass52. Circulating markers such as IL-6, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) reflect activation shared inflammatory pathways, which contribute to both vascular injury and adverse myocardial remodeling associated with increased EAT53–55. This is particularly relevant in the context of T2DM, where early cardiovascular injury is increasingly recognized as a continuum involving vascular, metabolic, and myocardial dysfunction, where integrated markers such as arterial stiffness, intima-media thickness, and subclinical atherosclerosis burden provide a more comprehensive assessment than isolated cardiac parameters alone56,57. Validating structural improvements against measures of vascular adaptation would provide further mechanistic insight into the effects of SGLT-2i on EAT and LV remodeling.

The clinical utility of EAT thickness may extend beyond its role as a mechanistic marker to serve as a complementary tool in cardiovascular risk stratification for T2DM. Diabetes-specific algorithms, such as SCORE2-Diabetes, have demonstrated a superior ability to capture vascular subclinical damage, correlating significantly with markers of arterial stiffness and carotid atherosclerosis56. EAT represents a distinct intermediate cardiometabolic phenotype, reflecting localized interaction. Its integration into these diabetes-specific models could enhance identification of high-risk individuals and provide a more granular understanding of subclinical cardiometabolic burden in the Malaysian T2DM population. Notably, the cardiometabolic benefits observed should be interpreted within the broader framework of residual risk. Even in the presence of improved glycaemic control and reduced adiposity, patients with T2DM and CAD remain at high risk due to persistent inflammatory activation55. The absence of direct measures of vascular function and inflammatory phenotype in our study limits the ability to determine whether SGLT-2i modifies these underlying key pathophysiological drivers. Accordingly, while SGLT-2i is associated with favorable changes in key cardiometabolic parameters, it should be viewed as one component of a comprehensive strategy to mitigate, rather than entirely normalize, the complex cardiovascular risk profile in this population. Furthermore, within a broader cardiovascular prevention framework, risk stratification should also incorporate sex-specific determinants across the life course, taking into consideration female physiological and hormonal changes, which are increasingly recognized as important contributors to cardiovascular risks58.

The strengths of this study include its prospective, observational cohort design. This design reflects the real-world heterogeneity of T2DM with CAD in the Malaysian clinical setting. The current study provides a better understanding of T2DM patients, who are often presented with co-existing illnesses and polypharmacy. While propensity score matching was not employed in the main analysis due to the good balance in baseline characteristics between the SGLT-2i and non-SGLT-2i groups, it may be considered for other analyzes or data subsets with uneven or imbalanced grouping. Additionally, this study is the first in Malaysia to highlight the metabolic effects of SGLT-2i along with its extensive benefits beyond the antidiabetic effects.

This study has several limitations. The non-randomized observational design may introduce treatment allocation bias and residual confounding. Notably, the SGLT-2i group exhibited a higher prevalence of heart failure and lower baseline LVEF, reflecting Malaysian clinical practice guidelines that prioritize SGLT-2i for high-risk cardiac patients. Although multivariable linear regression was applied to adjust for these baseline differences, unmeasured confounding (e.g., duration of diabetes, severity of CAD, revascularization status, and inflammatory markers) cannot be excluded. Therefore, findings should be interpreted as clinical associations, rather than causal relationships. In addition, a larger cohort with longer follow-up, such as 12–24 months is also warranted to capture long-term LV structural and functional effects. Variation in SGLT-2i dosing and concomitant medications may also have influenced outcomes. While cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (cMRI) remains the gold standard for quantifying LV function and epicardial adiposity, echocardiography was selected for its accessibility, cost-effectiveness, and widespread use in the Malaysian healthcare setting, particularly in an upper-middle-income nation. The absence of Tissue Doppler Imaging (e’ wave) precluded calculation of the load-independent E/e’ ratio, limiting diastolic function assessment and potentially underestimating its prevalence. Future studies incorporating additional indices, including E/e’ ratio and Myocardial Performance Index, may provide a more robust and comprehensive evaluation of global ventricular function59.

Conclusion

Our findings provide an exploratory yet comprehensive characterization of the cardiometabolic benefits of SGLT-2i that extend beyond glycemic control. The decrease in EAT with of SGLT-2i is associated with reductions in body weight and BMI, which may reflect cardiometabolic adaptations related to modulation of the lipoinflammatory axis, supporting EAT as a modifiable therapeutic target. These findings underscore the potential of SGLT-2i as an effective therapeutic strategy for improving cardiometabolic health among Malaysian patients with T2DM and CAD.
