Development of production methodologies for scFv-Fc conjugated critical reagents to support CAR-T clinical programs
Authors: Ellen O’Connor, Eric Lachacz, Jared Delmar, Bradley D’souza, Samuel Edwards, Christopher Griffin, Rosie Lin, Neil Mody, Alicia Salas, Natalia Schoch-Lopez, Mitali Shah, Ai Shih, Dewald van Dyk, Yueming Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionRoutine conjugation protocols are typically used by bioanalytical laboratories for production of their assay critical reagents. Novel molecules can pose unique challenges to the production of high-quality conjugated critical reagents required for clinical bioanalytical assays. Using routine conjugation protocols, we observed gross instability of conjugated-drug surrogate material for use in antidrug antibody (ADA) assays in clinical autologous Chimeric Antigen Receptor (CAR)-T cell programs, thus halting assay development.MethodsWe highlight our approach in developing process conditions for CAR-T supporting conjugated critical reagents, where recombinant CAR scFv-hFc is the surrogate drug material to be conjugated. We show that when routine platform process approaches are not appropriate to produce conjugated novel molecule critical reagents, in-silico modeling can be used to determine conditions imparting repeatable generation of stable reagents. Implementing this modeling is advantageous as it decreases laborious efforts to develop stable novel critical reagent production methodologies.ConclusionAs a result of these studies herein, optimal stable conjugated critical reagents were produced which enabled successful development and validation of clinical CAR-T ADA assay. This work provides a framework that can be applied to the production of other bioanalytical assay critical reagents when platform conjugation approaches fail.KEYWORDS: Biopharmaceutical, ligand-binding assay, conjugated critical reagent, CAR-T, scFv

Introduction

CAR-T background

Chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy is an emerging technology for cancer treatment. As shown in Supplemental Figure S1, this technology consists of identification of the variable sequence of an antibody specific to a tumor antigen. This single chain variable fragment (scFv) is then engineered into a patient’s T cells as a CAR, with an extracellular targeting domain and intracellular signaling domains. It is the scFv-based ectodomain of the CAR that is expressed on the T cell surface that enables the T cell to bind to specific antigens present on cancer cells. The application of this technology has resulted in increased remission rates compared to standard of care treatments for leukemias and lymphomas [1]. CAR-T therapies directed at tumor antigens CD19, CD20, CD22, CD38, and BCMA have also been described [2–9]. Approved CD19-directed CAR-T therapeutics to treat B-cell malignancies include Kymriah (tisagenlecleucel), Breyanzi (lisocabtagene maraleucel), and Tecartus (brexucabtagene autoleucel). Approved BCMA-directed CAR-T therapies to treat multiple myeloma include Abecma (idecabtagene vicleucel) and Carvykti (ciltacabtagene autoleucel) [10,11].

CAR-T critical reagents

As “living drugs,” characterization is of high importance in CAR-T clinical programs. Cellular kinetic (CK) clinical endpoints help us understand how these drugs are performing in the patient. Clinical immunogenicity endpoints help us understand how a patient’s immune response may impact the CK, efficacy, and safety. To this end, high-quality bioanalytical methods with high performing critical reagents are important for accurate clinical CK and immunogenicity assessments [12–15]. However, CAR-Ts pose unique challenges in critical reagent strategy and selection in bioanalytical assays.

Regulatory bodies recommend the assessment of CAR-T humoral immunogenicity, which is typically done through antidrug antibody (ADA) assays [16,17]. Unlike traditional biotherapeutics, use of the drug itself (drug substance or reference material) as the basis for critical reagents to demonstrate ADA drug binding is impractical for CAR-Ts. This is because each autologous CAR-T drug received by each patient is different, as each patient’s cells which form the basis of the therapy will vary. Thus, drug surrogates are utilized for critical reagents in ADA assays for autologous CAR-T.

Using surrogate reagents to mimic the immunogenicity of a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) has several challenges. One such challenge is patient variability. The quality and composition of the starting T-cell product can vary widely between individuals depending on their disease stage and prior treatment, affecting T-cell expansion and the final CAR-T cell product. Additionally, some patients may have preexisting antibodies that can cross-react with the CAR construct’s components, including the scFv. Finally, each patient’s immune system is unique, the expansion, persistence, and exhaustion of CAR-T cells can vary considerably from patient to patient [18–21].

Both cell-based and protein-based surrogates have been employed in ADA assays. In the case of Kymriah, a novel flow-based ADA with CAR transduced immortalized T cells as a surrogate for the drug were used [22]. While whole cells to detect ADA is advantageous because the provided cellular context may be closer to the reality in the patient, cell-based assays are operationally challenging at the scale of clinical trials. However, as the ADA-risk primarily lies in the scFv-based ectodomain of the CAR molecule, a ligand-based assay approach with a protein CAR ectodomain can be employed [16]. Soluble CAR constructs consisting of scFv, linker, and hinge domains are the critical reagents useful for LBAs (depicted in Supplemental Figure S1) [16]. This LBA strategy has been successfully used by several programs. For example, scFv-based surrogate conjugates were used in a traditional ADA-bridging assay to detect ADA of zevor-cel, a commercial CAR-T treatment [23].

To progress CAR-T programs, it is essential that production processes for the scFv and it’s conjugated critical reagents are controlled, repeatable, and result in stable molecules. The stability of the scFv is essential to support both pre-clinical and clinical studies. For example, the design of the scFv molecule is screened in the pre-clinical design stage. Screening activities include determining target affinity, specificity, and off-target binding risks [24].

Additionally, scFv reagents are vital tools for analyzing CAR-T clinical results because they measure CAR-T cell presence and activity in patient samples. scFv reagents can be used during clinical manufacturing to verify that the CAR is being expressed correctly on the T-cells before they are infused into the patient. scFv–Fc reagents are also used in flow cytometry to detect and quantify circulating CAR-T cells in a patient’s blood over time [25].

When used in the bioanalysis for ADA detection, the stability of the scFv and the scFv-conjugated critical reagent are also key, as the quality and consistency of the ADA data lies in the quality and consistency of the critical reagent. Poor quality critical reagents, such as those with aggregates, can introduce significant variability and affect the accuracy of ligand binding assays (LBAs). Aggregates and other particles can sequester the active reagent, leading to a reduced effective concentration and causing a variety of quantitative issues, including reduced binding affinity and inaccurate concentration measurements [26]. In one study on assay interference, individual protein aggregates were shown to sequester up to 10,000 individual enzyme molecules, reducing the number of functional binding sites on the microplate and shifting the calibration curve. This resulted in a lower signal response for a given analyte concentration, leading to an overestimation of the analyte concentration in test samples [27].

Formulation conditions impact the stability of conjugates. Much has been published about scFv conjugations in the literature as the conjugates are frequently needed for a variety of biopharmaceutical reasons. Although conjugation protocols differ in process, conjugated scFvs are frequently formulated in PBS. For example, an scFv used for photodynamic cancer therapy was conjugated and formulated in PBS [28]. PEGylated scFvs used in diagnostic testing for Alzheimer’s Disease or Cancer were conjugated and formulated in PBS [29–31]. scFvs have also been conjugated with radionuclides to target Cancer cells [32,33]. Besides application to detect or treat Cancer, conjugated scFvs have also been used as Covid-19 detection reagents. scFv conjugation to magnetic beads was performed and formulated in PBS [34]. Although conjugated scFvs are frequently formulated in PBS and PBS is a default formulation condition of many conjugation kits, this platform approach does not always result in stable critical reagents. Engineered proteins can experience instability when stored in Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS), particularly under stress conditions like freeze–thaw cycles. Instability can manifest as aggregation, fragmentation, or conformational changes. Freezing protein in PBS can cause the dibasic sodium phosphate to crystallize, leading to a significant drop in the buffer pH by as much as two units. This pH shift can destabilize the protein, causing it to aggregate upon thawing [35] Additionally, free radicals and other oxidants can be generated in the buffer over time, especially during long-term storage or in the presence of certain impurities. This can also lead to protein degradation or aggregation [36].

The manufacturing process is complex for scFv-hFc critical reagents, as each scFv-hFc must be developed for each CAR-T program individually. The complexity of creating an scFv-hFc for each drug stems from the unique amino acid sequence of the variable regions for each new target, which can cause varying stability and expression levels. Efforts are underway across the industry to ease this challenge. One approach used to ease the challenge is the standardization of expression platforms (optimizing vectors, expression systems, and cultures) [37,38]. Companies such as Antibody Design Labs offer standardized vectors that can accelerate the cloning and expression of scFv-hFc fusions. These vectors are designed for high yield and quick transient expression in common cell lines like HEK293 which have been genetically modified to increase protein production in serum-free culture [39,40].

Another strategy to ease the manufacturing challenge is to enhance structural predictability using computational design and framework libraries. For example, in silico modeling can predict the stability and affinity of scFv-hFc molecules and simulate their interactions with target antigens [41]. This allows researchers to engineer more stable and effective molecules upfront, reducing the need for extensive trial-and-error experimentation. Additionally, using stable antibody frameworks to construct new scFv fragments can improve the predictability of biophysical properties, ensuring that the final scFv-hFc molecule is both stable and functional [42].

Industrial efforts are also underway to develop a universal reagent that can monitor ADA with different CAR T cells [43]. For example, Adapter-mediated targeting is emerging, which uses “universal CAR” systems that decouple the antigen-recognition domain (the scFv) from the T-cell’s signaling domain. The CAR T-cell itself is nonspecific, and an intermediate “adapter” molecule – a bispecific antibody – is used to bridge the universal CAR to the specific tumor antigen. The system would then lend itself to universal ADA monitoring as an assay can be developed to detect ADAs against the universal CAR itself. ADAs against the specific adapter molecule could also be monitored, a less variable process compared to monitoring ADAs against many different CARs. Another approach to developing a universal reagent is using generic bridging assays. For instance, an assay might capture the ADA using an antibody against the human Fc region and use a fluorescent-labeled version of the therapeutic to detect the complex. This can shorten the development time compared to creating fully customized assays for each scFv.

In this work we describe an optimized process to produce scFv critical reagents. Biotinylated and ruthenylated scFv-hFc critical reagents were needed to support clinical CAR-T studies, specifically ADA assays. The initial surrogate conjugated products, without optimization, precipitated under platform conjugation and PBS formulation conditions. In silico modeling of the molecule along with range finding studies allowed for the development of a stable formulation for the reagents for use in clinical bioanalytical assays. This provides a framework where surrogate drug-based critical reagents stability challenges need to be overcome. This formulation condition was successfully applied to scFv-hFc critical reagents for other clinical programs.

Materials & methods

Quality assurance (QA)/quality control (QC) statement

Quality assurance of the worked described in this study consisted of the performance of these procedures according to AstraZeneca’s global lab standard (GLS). GLS consists of oversight by the Quality system to ensure training of staff, systems to document data/records, audits/inspections, controls of processes, sustainable science, and supplier management. Some assay development and validation work carried out externally at vendor labs followed those vendors SOPs. These vendor labs routinely support clinical bioanalytical validation work with dedicated quality control and quality assurance programs. The product quality of conjugated materials, QC, was determined by testing protein concentration, binding activity, purity, and stability according to methods described herein.

Production of scFv-hFc conjugates

Production of scFv-hFc

Construction of the scFv-hFc proceeded as follows. The light and heavy chain variable domains of scFv-Fc were derived from a fully human antibody. The molecular design of the fusion protein includes a scFv portion in which the VL and VH domains were fused through a (G4S)4-linker, followed by a IgG4P hinge and a human IgG1 Fc-backbone. To facilitate cloning, a 1500 bp gene fragment covering the full gene plus mammalian expression vector flanking overhangs was ordered (Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc, Coralville, Iowa, USA). Using Gibson assembly, the scFv-Fc gene fragment was subsequently cloned into an expression vector that was linearized with high fidelity BsrG1 and NotI restriction enzymes. 0.7 µl of the Gibson assembly reaction mixture was transformed into TOP10 chemically competent E. coli cells. Bacterial transformants were plated on LB + 100 ug/ml carbenicillin containing agar medium and allowed to grow overnight at 37°C. The next day single colonies were picked to inoculate 5 ml 2XYT +100 µg/ml carbenicillin containing liquid media, and the cultures were incubated overnight at 37°C. The next day constructs were recovered from individual bacterial cultures and the miniprep pDNA was submitted for Sanger sequencing to assess the sequence integrity of the inserts and the vector/insert junctions prior to transfection and expression.

Transient expression and purification proceeded as followed. scFv-hFc stock plasmid DNA was transformed into TOP10 chemically competent cells, plated on LB + carbenicillin, and grown overnight at 37°C. Two single colonies were picked and grown in 100 mL 2XYT/LB + carbenicillin liquid media overnight. DNA was prepared from each flask using QIAGEN® Plasmid Plus Maxi Kit high yield protocol (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) and submitted to Azenta for whole-plasmid sequencing to confirm expected sequences. Transient expression of sequence confirmed scFv-hFc DNA was completed in duplicate using 293x cells. Reagent A and B mixes were created. Reagent A was comprised of 200 µg scFv-Fc DNA +10 mL Optimem (VWR, West Chester, PA, USA). Reagent B was comprised of 300 µL of 293Fectin (VWR, West Chester, PA, USA) added to 10 mL of Optimem. Reagent A and B were first incubated separately for 5 minutes, followed by combining both reagents for 20-minute incubation at room temperature. The entire mixture volume was added to 300 mL of 293x cells at 1.1 x106 cells/mL density after incubation. Cells were grown at 37°C, 5% CO2, 150 rpm for 10 days including the following steps: Day 3: 11.2 mL F-009 + 648 uL F-010 nutrient feeds added and culture volume increased to 450 mL using Gibco FreeStyle 293 medium (VWR, West Chester, PA, USA), Day 6: 11.2 mL F-009 + 648 µL F-010 nutrient feeds added and culture volume increased to 600 mL using FreeStyle 293 medium, Day 10: Cells harvested by centrifugation at 3000 ×g for 5 mins and followed by 0.2 µm filtration. During expression, 1 mL of cell culture from day 3, 6, and 10 was analyzed on a Vi-Cell XR instrument to confirm > 96% cell viability on each day. Final expression titer was estimated to be 441 mg/L by Agilent 1260 HPLC Protein A column. To purify the scFv-hFc, the harvest product was captured by protein A affinity chromatography (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). The purified eluate was formulated in 1 X PBS, pH 7.2 (VWR, West Chester, PA, USA) and analyzed by HPSEC to confirm the monomer purity was greater than 95% and visual analysis to confirm the absence of visible particles.

Platform conjugation, purification, and formulation conditions

Platform scFv-hFc conjugations were performed according to the following methodology. As part of preclinical development scFv-hFc constructs of the parent antibodies are produced. Such surrogate constructs are used to provide information on (but not limited to) target affinity, specificity, and off-target risk assessments. The scFv-hFc was dialyzed in a 30 kDa Slide-A-Lyzer Cassettes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) into 1 X PBS, pH 7.4 buffer. After buffer exchange, the material was adjusted to 2 mg/mL. For both ruthenylation or biotinylation, the protein was reacted at a 1:10 ratio, respectively, with MSD GOLD SULFO-TAG NHS-Ester (Meso Scale Discovery, Rockville, MD, USA) or EZ Link Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Each reaction proceeded for 1 hour at room temperature with mixing. Each conjugated material was purified by application to a pre-packed 2.6 × 60 cm Superdex 200 column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) equilibrated in 1 × PBS, pH 7.2 for size exclusion chromatography (SEC). Using an Akta Avant controlled with Unicorn 7.0 software (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). The first peak, containing the conjugate was collected. For the platform formulation, the conjugate was washed with 1 × PBS, pH 7.2 and concentrated to 2 mg/mL using a 30 kDa Vivaspin filter (VWR West Chester, PA, USA). For the developed formulation, the conjugate was dialyzed in a Slide-A-Lyzer cassette in 25 mM histidine, 150 mM sodium chloride, pH 5.5. The conjugate was then concentrated to 0.5 mg/mL using a Vivaspin filter. Chemicals including Gibco 1 × PBS pH 7.2, Gibco 1 × PBS pH 7.4, JT Baker L-Histidine, JT Baker L-Histidine HCl, JT Baker NaCl, and lab consumables including single use sterile luer lock syringes and BD Precision Glide needles were purchased from VWR (West Chester, PA, USA).

Process development of optimized conjugation, purification, and formulation conditions

Range finding experiments were performed according to the described procedures in 2.2.2 with the following modifications. Biotinylated and ruthenylated scFv-hFc conjugates were prepared at 1:10 molar ratios. Upon purification by Superdex 200, materials were divided, buffer exchanged by dialysis, and concentrated by Vivaspin filtration into different conditions: phosphate pH 7.2 (0.5 and 1 mg/mL), histidine pH 6.5 (0.5 and 1 mg/mL), and histidine pH 5.5 (0.5 and 1 mg/mL). After formulation was complete, while protecting from light, materials were subjected to three cycles of freeze–thaw (−80°C) and held at 2–8°C for 4 days as that simulated shipping and the maximum amount of freeze thaw cycles and hold times the critical reagents would be exposed to at the testing location. Samples were analyzed for visual appearance and HPSEC purity.

Production of scFv-hFc critical reagents using the developed process

Preparation of the scFv-hFc biotinylated and ruthenylated conjugates according to the developed process proceeded as follows. The scFv-hFc was dialyzed in a 30 kDa Slide-A-Lyzer Cassettes into 1 × PBS, pH 7.4. The material was adjusted to 2 mg/mL prior to reaction at a 1:10 ratio with either MSD GOLD SULFO-TAG NHS-Ester or EZ Link Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotin. Each reaction proceeded for 1 hour at room temperature with mixing. Materials were then purified over Superdex 200 resin. The conjugates were buffer exchanged, using Slide-A-Lyzer cassettes, into 25 mM histidine, 150 mM sodium chloride, pH 5.5 prior to concentration by Vivaspin filtration to 0.5 mg/mL.

Analysis of scFv-hFc conjugates

Protein concentration

Quantification of the scFv-hFc and its biotinylated products were determined using the NanoDropTM 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Quantification of the scFv-hFc and its ruthenylated scFv-hFcs were performed using the Pierce 660 nm Protein Assay Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

HPSEC purity

Biotinylated and ruthenylated scFv-hFc materials were analyzed using a TSKgel G2000SWXL column (7.8 mm × 300 mm, 5 μm) (TOSOH TSKgel G3000SWXL, Minato, TKY, Japan) on a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Conjugates were processed using a running solution of 0.1 M disodium phosphate containing 0.1 M sodium sulfate, pH 6.8, at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Proteins were detected using UV absorbance at 280 nm. Results were calculated as the percent area of the monomer peak versus all other peaks. Peaks eluting prior to the monomer peak were calculated as aggregate; high molecular weight or dimer. Peaks eluting after the monomer peak were calculated as fragment.

Subvisible particle analysis

Subvisible particles found in the biotinylated and ruthenylated scFv-hFc conjugates were analyzed using the MFI 5200 system with MVSS software (Protein Simple, San Jose, CA). The system was flushed and primed with RODI water. To confirm system suitability, size and count verifications were performed. To confirm the size, a 5 µm particle suspension was prepared by adding 1 drop of 5 µm polystyrene beads size calibration standard (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to 10 mL of water. The 10 µm particle suspension was prepared by adding 2 drops of 10 µm polystyrene beads size calibration standard to 15 mL of water. The size verification solutions were lightly vortexed prior to the separate additions of 0.4 mL of the 5 and 10 µm suspensions. To confirm the count verification, the 1 mL of water was applied to the system to obtain background particle levels. 1 mL of the 5 µm polystyrene bead suspension was then applied to confirm counts between 2,430 and 3,630 particles/mL. For sample analysis, 1 mL of material was then applied and analyzed. Prior to each verification or analysis, the system was flushed with water.

Fourier transformed infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) analysis of visible particles

Visible particles were collected by 0.2 µm Al2O3 filtration for FT-IR analysis. A vacuum filtration setup was assembled. A water-resistant insert, with a 5 mm radius circular hole, was placed under the Anodisc 25” 0.02 µm 25 mm Al2O3 filter (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington MA, USA), to focus the particles on the center of the filter. Both sides of the water-resistant insert and filters were rinsed with USP water before being placed atop the filter funnel. The filter was again rinsed with USP water prior to sample filtration. After filtration of the biotinylated and ruthenylated scFv-hFc conjugates, filters containing the particles were rinsed with USP water then dried under filtration vacuum. FT-IR analysis was performed with the Spectrum 100 spectrometer, Spotlight 400 FT-IR Imagining system, and Spectrum Image Software (Perkin-Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Each Al2O3 filter was placed atop an adherent slide and placed on the instrument stage. Visible reflectance mode was used to identify particles for imaging and transmittance mode was used for IR scanning. Sample spectra were obtained using point mode. Each sample was scanned 10–50 times against a background at 2 cm−1 resolution in transmittance mode unless specified otherwise. Atmospheric correction was used to subtract background H2O and CO2. Filtration units used in the particle analysis were purchased from VWR (West Chester, PA, USA).

Appearance analysis

Prior to testing the scFv-hFc biotinylated and ruthenylated conjugates for appearance, reference standard solutions were aliquoted in 2 R/3ccL vials (West Pharmaceutical Services, West Whiteland Township, PA, USA) and sealed with 13 mm stoppers (Cozzoli, Somerset, NJ, USA). References for turbidity included water, 3, 6, 18, 30, 60, 120, 200, 1000, 2000, 4000, and 7500 NTU Hach-Lange StablCal Stabilized Formazin Standards (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). References for color included 7 standards each of yellow, brown, and yellow-brown solutions (MilliporeSigma, Burlington MA USA) ranging from nearly colorless to intense color. Standards were inverted 10 times and allowed to stand for 2–3 minutes prior to assessment at 3000–3400 lux units. Samples for analysis were aliquoted into 2 R/3ccvials, repeatedly inverted, and allowed to sit for several minutes. Samples were analyzed using an Apollo II Liquid Viewer (Adelphi Manufacturing Company Ltd., Haywards Heath, UK) and a Lux meter (VWR, West Chester, PA, USA). The color and clarity were then assessed and recorded.

Reversed phase liquid chromatography couple to mass spectrometry (RPLC-MS)

Mass spectrometry linked reversed phase liquid chromatography (RPLC – MS) analysis of non-reduced, deglycosylated intact protein was performed to confirm the label incorporation of the scFv-hFc biotinylated and ruthenylated conjugates. Samples were deglycosylated with 1:1 Promega PNGaseF with water and kept at 37°C overnight. A Xevo G2-XS mass spectrometer combined with an ultra-performance LC system (Waters Corporation, Milford, Massachusetts, USA) was used. Chromatographic separation was performed by using an Acquity UPLC BEH C4 column (Waters Corporation, Milford, Massachusetts, USA) of size 2.1 × 50 mm, with 1.7 µm particle size, 300 Å pore size. A linear gradient from 5% to 80% B in 2 minutes was used with mobile phase A of 0.1% formic acid and 0.01% trifluoroacetic acid in water and mobile phase B of 0.1% formic acid and 0.01% trifluoroacetic acid in acetonitrile. Molecular mass was determined from the deconvoluted mass spectra using Byos (Protein Metrics, Cupertine, California, USA).

In silico modeling of scFv-hFc conjugates

Modeling of structure-based solubility profiles

CamSol modeling for calculating solubility was performed according to Sormanni and Vendruscolo [44]. 3D structures were generated for the Fv region using AlphaFold 2 (DeepMind, London, England, UK). Surface Patch analysis and protein properties were calculated using Molecular Operating Environment (Chemical Computing Group, Montreal, QC, CA).

Modeling of spatial aggregation propensity

Spatial Aggregation Propensity (SAP) calculations were performed according to methods described by Chennamsetty et al. [45]. Briefly, the solvent accessible surface area (SASA) of side chain atoms of exposed residues within a radius of 5 Å was divided by the total SASA of side chain atoms of the fully exposed residue. To obtain the SAP for each atom, this fraction was then multiplied by the residue hydrophobicity assigned according to Black and Mould [46] in which glycine is assigned a score of zero. Residues less hydrophobic than glycine are assigned a negative value, whereas residues more hydrophobic than glycine are positive. The SAP for each residue was determined by averaging the constituent atomic SAP values. The CHARMM36m force field software was used for calculations [47].

Modeling of spatial charge map

Spatial Charge Map (SCM) calculations were performed according to methods utilized by Agrawal [48]. Briefly, the SCM profile was determined by analyzing the atoms comprising the exposed residues in the 3D structure of the scFv. A residue was classified as solvent exposed if the total of its side chain atoms was ≥10 Å. The partial charge of atoms comprising each exposed residue was calculated and mapped at pH 5.5. The CHARMM36m force field software was used for calculations [47].

Support vector machine modeling

SVM analysis was performed according to Chennamsetty et al. [45]. Briefly, using Python in the sklearn package [49], machine learning models were trained with a database of features of 40,000 individual antibody residues, enabling the predictions of local Γ23 values. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were then performed at pH 5.5 on CHARMM36m [47] force field and GROMACS 5.0.5 [50] using parameters from Cloutier [51–56].

ADA bridging assay for scFv-hFc conjugates

Generally, samples and controls were tested in the ADA bridging assay according to the following procedure. Each sample (control or test sample) was diluted (either 20-fold or 40-fold) with a master mix containing a mixture of the ruthenylated and biotinylated scFv-hFc reagents at optimal concentrations determined during development. Samples were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 1 hour ±10 minutes with shaking at 600 rpm. After incubation, 50 µl of the solution was added per well (in duplicate) to streptavidin-coated 96-well plates (Meso Scale Discovery, Rockville, MD, USA) that had been prepared by blocking and washing with an assay buffer. After sealing, the plate was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 50–70 minutes at 600 rpm. After incubation for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark with gentle mixing, plates were washed four times with assay buffer. The application of 150 µl per well of 2X MSD Read Buffer (Meso Scale Discovery, Rockville, MD, USA) enabled quantification on the Sector 6000 Imager (Meso Scale Discovery, Rockville, MD, USA). ECL values were recorded for each sample. ECL values were converted to S:B values by dividing the mean of sample signal values by the mean negative control (NC) that was measured on the same plate.

The ADA bridging methods were validated to characterize assay attributes as described in health authority guidance documents, Astra Zeneca internal SOPs, and bioanalytical vendor SOPs. Broadly, the assay sensitivity, precision, cut point, selectivity, specificity, sample stability, and robustness were established. Assay performance was determined with pooled normal Human Serum (pNHS) as NC and pNHS spiked with monoclonal anti-CAR antibodies (as surrogate ADA). Varying serum spiked levels of anti-CAR antibodies for low positive control (LPC), middle positive control (MPC), and high positive control (HPC) materials were used in the validations. For cut point determination, S:B of over 300 total measurements consisting of at least 50 treatment-naïve patient serum samples (obtained with consent following the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki) were used. Outliers were identified per lab SOPs for removal and calculation of the ADA screening cut point for a 5% False Positive Rate (FPR) (eg. Tukey Box Blot analysis, best linear unbiased predictor, log transformation of data, etc.). Finally, stability and robustness assessment included, room temperature sample stability, refrigerator sample stability, freeze thaw sample stability, and intentionally stressing the min/max assay incubation times.

Results

Conjugation of scFv-hFc using platform conditions

Critical reagents were needed to enable a CAR-T therapy clinical development. In accordance with regulatory guidelines, critical reagents were generated to evaluate cellular kinetics and immunogenicity. For immunogenicity analysis, scFv-hFc produced for early cross reactivity studies provided a convenient surrogate for the ectodomain of the CAR-T cell therapy. Biotinylated scFv-hFc and ruthenylated scFv-hFc reagents were required for use in a standard bridging LBA to detect ADA response against the scFv ectodomain which included hinge, linker, and antigen-binding domains.

Consistent with the conjugation practices of other protein biotherapeutic molecules, conjugated scFv-hFc material was prepared according to materials and methods section 2.2.2. Briefly, purified scFv-hFc was reacted at a 1:10 ratio in 1 × PBS, pH 7.4 with either EZ Link Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotin or MSD GOLD SULFO-TAG NHS-Ester. The conjugated products were then purified by SEC and formulated in 1 × PBS, pH 7.2 Biotinylated and ruthenylated scFv-hFc materials were of expected color, clarity, and purity (measuring greater than 95% by HPSEC). However, upon transport and/or single freeze-thaw, visible particles were observed in both the ruthenlyated and biotinylated materials, as shown in Figure 1. The phenomenon was observed in various challenge ratios and across laboratories (data not shown). Given the challenges posed by gross critical reagent instability to ADA assays, development of the ADA method was paused. Furthermore, the scFv-hFc material is a surrogate of our drug, as opposed to the well characterized Drug Substance/Drug Product material that is often used in ADA assay reagents. This distinction and the observed reagent instability prompted further investigation to enable ADA assay development.

The particle containing materials were further analyzed. As shown in Figure 1, a HPSEC analysis was performed to detect the presence of dimers or high molecular weight aggregated species measuring less than 0.1 µm. Compared to the initial value of 100% monomer, the biotinylated scFv-hFc material increased slightly in dimer to 0.7%. On the other hand, the ruthenylated scFv-hFc materials had significant increases in dimer and high molecular weight species. The HPSEC value of the initial material was 96.2%. After freeze-thaw, the material had 33% aggregates roughly evenly split amongst dimer and high molecular weight species.

Since aggregated materials were not observed in the biotinylated materials by HPSEC, an effort to analyze larger subvisible particles, up to 25 µm, was made for both scFv-hFc conjugates. As shown in Figure 1, Micro Flow Imaging (MFI) was used to study the subvisible particles. Samples of each conjugate were analyzed and the total number of ≥1, ≥2, ≥5, ≥10, and ≥25 µm particles was recorded (duplicate experiments for each size analysis). Overall, the biotinylated scFv-hFc contained less subvisible particles than the ruthenylated scFv-hFc. This trend was observed across the range of screened particle sizes. For example, the total number of all particles greater than or equal to 1 µm in a 1 mL samples was, on average, 456 for biotinylated scFv-hFc versus 5733 particles for ruthenylated scFv-hFc. A similar difference was also observed when analyzing particles greater than or equal to 25 µm in 1 mL samples. On average, there were 26 particles for biotinylated scFv-hFc versus 745 particles for ruthenylated scFv-hFc.

As HPSEC purity analysis revealed aggregates, less than 0.1 µm in size, consisting of less than 1% of total purity and decreased levels of subvisible particles observed in the biotinylated scFv-hFc, larger visible particle analysis was conducted. As shown in Figure 1, 50 µl of both conjugates were analyzed by FT-IR (50 scans each), to observe their appearance and compositions. Visible reflectance mode was used to identify particles for imaging and IR scanning within the focusing filtration region of the Al2O3 filters. Scanning in transmittance mode and comparing with the library illustrated that 96% of the analyzed particles were proteinaceous. In the ruthenylated sample, 98% of the analyzed particles were comprised of protein. As shown in Figure 1, the visible particle sizes were compared to a 100 µm standard and photographed. As expected, biotinylated scFv-hFc visible particles appeared white and those of ruthenylated scFv-hFc appeared yellow.

In-silico modeling of formulation conditions

In-silico modeling was employed to develop acceptable scFv-hFc conjugate formulation conditions while simultaneously decreasing time and laboratory resources. First, the solubility of the parental scFv containing the hinge, linker, VL and VH domains, was studied using CamSol modeling. The CamSol method consists of analyzing physicochemical characteristics of a protein’s constituent amino acids. This analysis enables the generation of 3D structures using AlphaFold 2. Then, surface patch analysis and protein properties can be calculated using Molecular Operating Environment (MOE). CamSol has shown strong correlations between the calculated scores and experimental solubility measurements [44]. The overall solubility score of the parental scFv molecule was −0.54, indicative of low solubility and a propensity to aggregate.

Structural models of the parental scFv and the Fv were next generated, using MOE, to analyze the impact of changing formulation conditions on the component hydrophobic and charge patches. Modeled conditions included pH ranging from pH 5.5 to 7 and salt concentrations ranging from no salt to 0.3 M sodium chloride. Figure 2 shows the results for the differing formulation conditions. For the parental scFv, as pH was decreased to 5.5 and the sodium chloride was increased to 0.3 M, the percentage of hydrophobic patches was not significantly impacted, with a slight decrease from 17.2% to 17.0%. A larger charge impact was observed, as negative patches were decreased, and positive patches were increased resulting in an increase of net charge from 7.5 to 21.9. Supplemental Figure S2 shows similar trends in hydrophobicity and charge were calculated for the Fv domain. As pH was decreased to 5.5 and the sodium chloride was increased to 0.3 M, the percentage of hydrophobic patches were decreased from 5.8% to 4.9%. An impact to net charge was also observed, increasing from 7.2 to 8.6. The hydrophobic, negative, and positive patch differences for both the parental scFv and the Fv are shown in the structural models for the most hydrophobic and least charged condition, pH 7.2 and no salt, as well as the least hydrophobic and most charged condition, pH 5.5 and 0.3 M salt. Visible changes between the two formulation conditions can be seen in the number and location of patches within the both the parental scFv and Fv. Based on these models, the aggregate and particle formation was predicted to be decreased for the scFv in the pH 5.5 with salt formulation condition.

As MOE predicted that pH 5.5 was the most favorable pH to decrease aggregation and particle formation, spatial aggregation propensity (SAP) and spatial charge maps (SCM) were calculated at pH 5.5. These maps of the Fv are shown in Figure 3. SAP identifies hot spots for aggregation based on the dynamic exposure of spatially adjacent hydrophobic amino acids. In this method, the surface area of the side-chain atoms of exposed residues are calculated and multiplied by a relative residue hydrophobicity. The SAP for each residue is therefore determined by averaging the constituent atomic SAP values. The SAP map for the Fv revealed a mostly hydrophilic molecule with some hydrophobic regions. SCM is a commonly used structure-based method for quantifying charge patches on the surface of proteins. SCM calculations were determined by analyzing the atoms comprising the exposed residues in the 3D structure of the Fv. The partial charge of atoms comprising each exposed residue was calculated and mapped at pH 5.5. The resulting SCM map of the Fv showed that the majority of exposed residues were electropositive. In addition, excipient interactions were studied to identify excipients that would further contribute to the stability of the molecule at pH 5.5. These excipient interactions were calculated by the support vector machine (SVM) model. Proline, sucrose, sorbitol, trehalose showed some hydrophobic interactions with the scFv. In comparison, arginine-HCl and sodium chloride showed interactions at the charged regions, with sodium chloride being the most impactful excipient.

Experimental range finding of scFv-hFc formulation conditions

Formulation stability range finding experiments were performed to experimentally confirm that the parental scFv and Fv modeling outcomes would extend to the conjugated scFv-hFcs. According to the model, it was predicted that low pH condition which induced the strongest positive charge combined with the inclusion of sodium chloride excipient to prevent molecular interactions, would enable the stability of the scFv conjugates. To experimentally test these conditions, the scFv-hFc was separately reacted with EZ Link Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotin or MSD GOLD SULFO-TAG NHS-Ester at a 1:10 molar ratio. After incubation, the materials were purified by SEC and then formulated into buffers with sodium chloride ranging from 150 to 300 mM and pH ranging from 5.5 (histidine buffer) to 7.5 (phosphate buffer). Histidine and phosphate buffers were selected as they are the most frequently used buffers in industry to buffer at the desired pH. In addition, the protein concentration of the formulation was also examined ranging from 0.5 to 2 mg/mL. After formulation, to simulate critical reagent storage and use conditions, materials were subjected to 3 cycles of freeze-thaw and then incubation at 2–8°C for 4 days. Visual inspection and HPSEC analysis were then performed on the samples. Particles were not observed in materials with decreased protein concentration, 150 mM or 300 mM sodium chloride, and at decreased pH. The HPSEC results for the 3 × 3full factorial experiments performed at 150 mM sodium chloride are shown in Figure 4. Based on 95% confidence intervals, Ruthenylated scFv-hFc products showed a wide range of monomer purities. Monomer purity was greater than 95% at pH 5.5 and 0.5 mg/mL concentration. Although all biotinylated scFv-hFc products had monomer purities greater than 95%, the highest levels were also observed at pH 5.5 and 0.5 mg/mL. These results are consistent with the modeling predictions that lower pH and increased sodium chloride concentration maximized the net positive charge contributing to molecular repulsion. The decreased protein concentration also aided to prevent molecular interactions. These conditions likely prevented aggregate and larger particle formation.

An additional set of experiments was performed to determine if the aggregation phenomenon was reversible. Freeze–thaw visible particle containing biotinylated and ruthenylated scFv-hFc conjugates were buffer exchanged into the 20 mM histidine, 150 mM sodium chloride, pH 5.5, 0.5 mg/mL formulation condition. Visible particles remained in both experiments, indicating the aggregation event was not reversible for either conjugate.

Scale-up of scFv-hFc developed formulation conditions

Based on the results of the modeling and confirmatory experimental studies, the 20 mM histidine, 150 mM sodium chloride, pH 5.5 at 0.5 mg/mL formulation condition was selected for production of the scFv-hFc critical reagents. Prior to scaling the productions to 50 mg, a set of small-scale reactions were performed to confirm the reaction labeling ratio. Biotinylation and ruthenylation reactions were both performed at labeling ratios of 1:3, 1:6, 1:10, and 1:12 and formulated into the developed buffer. A use test of antibody spiked serum with the different ratios indicated the conjugates generated with the 1:10 ratio yielded the best sensitivity (data not shown). Conjugation reactions generated with the 1:10 ratio and the 20 mM histidine, 150 mM sodium chloride, pH 5.5 at 0.5 mg/mL formulation condition were scaled to 50 mg. Figure 4 shows the product quality assessments of the scaled reagents which were subjected to freeze-thaw. Visual appearance testing confirmed that both materials were devoid of visible particles, indicating turbidity of 3 NTU or less. Additionally, the HPSEC monomer purities of the biotinylated and ruthenylated scFv-hFcs were 99.1% and 98.9% monomer, respectively. Liquid chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry was also performed on the freeze–thaw conjugates to confirm label incorporation ratio. The deconvoluted mass profiles showed that the biotinylated scFv-hFc had an average incorporation of 2.2 biotin labels per molecule. The ruthenylated scFv-hFc had an average incorporation of 3.4 labels per molecule.

ADA assay using scFv-hFc conjugated reagents

Initial development work for the ADA bridging assay was performed with the optimized critical reagents. A monoclonal anti-CAR antibody was used for preparing ADA positive control serum samples in this development work. Assay conditions such and critical reagent concentrations, minimum required dilutions of sample, and incubation times. These development parameters were selected to enable adequate sensitivity for ADA ( < 100 ng/mL) and appropriate signal background of serum-negative controls (data not shown). Briefly, the method entails dilution of serum samples into a master mix of the biotinylated and ruthenylated scFv-Fc, incubation, capture of bridged complexes on a streptavidin plate, and reading of ECL signal on a MSD plate reader (see methods for more details). Sample result signals (S) were normalized per plate as S/B ratios, with background (B) measured from pooled negative control serum.

The developed method with the optimized conjugated scFv-hFc reagents was then assessed in a formal method validation in accordance with FDA/EMA regulatory guidelines [57]. Assay precision, cut point determination, sensitivity, selectivity, specificity, sample stability, and robustness were assessed. As shown in Figure 5, Negative control (pNHS), low positive control (LPC), medium positive control (MPC), and high positive controls (HPC), showed acceptable inter-assay precision over the course of the validation ( < 20% CV). The controls also gave increasing ECL and S/B values as ADA concentration increased. This expected behavior of the assay (increasing signal with increasing ADA concentration) and high analytical inter-assay precision indicates the optimized critical reagent are performing as expected, in a consistent manner over multiple runs, which is desirable for ADA critical reagents.

The S/B assay signals from 300 sample measurements of a 50 individual treatment-naïve diseased patient panel was analyzed. Visual inspection of these data showed, that the assay signal is clustered around a 1.00 S/B ratio as calculated from the pNHS NC with reasonable biological variability. From these data, only two analytical outliers were identified, and no biological outliers were identified. From these data, a screening cutpoint for ADA positivity (target 5% FPR) was determined to be 1.18 S/B. With this cutpoint, 10/10 selected individual samples spiked with ADA at LPC and HPC levels correctly identified as ADA positive. Altogether, these data demonstrate that the optimized conjugated critical reagents give assays results that are expected from individual samples without excessive gross outliers, namely that samples will correctly classify during an immunogenicity study.

Finally, the assay from these optimized conjugated critical reagents was tested for stability and robustness. For sample stability, the NC, LPC, and HPC samples were subjected to stress conditions of, room temperature (24 hours), 2–8 C (24 hours), and freeze thaw cycles. All PC stability samples in each condition tested positive and fell in rank order with acceptable CVs. All but one of the NC stability samples tested negative at the room temperature condition, which still met our target acceptance criteria (2/3 NCs classifying as negative). In robustness tests, the minimum and the maximum incubation times of samples with the critical reagents still resulted in correctly classifying of the controls in rank order. These data show our conjugated critical reagents still correctly classify the samples within the typical variations of sample handling and assay procedures.

Discussion

The development and production of conjugated critical reagents for ADA assays are central to enabling reliable bioanalysis assays across increasingly diverse biologic therapeutics. Conjugated critical reagents of the drug material itself are often employed in some bioanalysis assays, with ADA bridging LBAs being an example for clinical immunogenicity assessment. Some therapeutics pose significant challenges for conjugated drug critical reagent preparation, where standard “platform” conjugation procedures employed by bioanalysis laboratories may adversely impact critical reagent stability and by extension assay performance.

In the case of conjugated drug critical reagent preparation for clinical ADA assays of autologous CAR-Ts the challenges were two-fold. First, the nature of the drug itself as a cell requires careful selection of drug surrogate that is relevant to the risk of ADA formation. In the cases herein, recombinant scFv-hFc material produced for early cross reactivity studies provides a convenient source for surrogate material most relevant to ADA risk, namely the ectodomain of the CAR protein (scFv, linker, and hinge). Second, this research grade material is not as well characterized and formulated as compared to designated Drug Substance/Drug Product material that is often used in routine conjugated drug critical reagent preparation.

The downstream effects of the above points are that if problems are encountered with the conjugated critical reagents from this material, it leaves the bioanalytical scientist at a disadvantage to develop a usable ADA assay. Indeed, gross instability was observed with our conjugated critical reagents using routine methods with appearance of visible precipitates during ADA assay development (Figure 1E,F). Initial attempts in overcoming this observed instability by varying the conjugation challenge ratio and preparing in different laboratories were unsuccessful. Due to challenges posed by grossly instable critical reagents, ADA assay development was paused.

This work focused on scFv-hFc conjugates supporting CAR-T programs and demonstrated how a model informed process development strategy, outlined in Supplemental Figure 3, can derisk formulation and scaleup. In silico assessments, combining CamSol solubility predictions and structure-based charge modeling across pH and excipient conditions indicated that the parental scFv and derived Fv possess low solubility and aggregation propensity, especially near neutral pH with limited ionic strength. Literature further predicted that NHS-ester conjugation at exposed amines, with either neutral EZ Link Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotin or negatively charged MSD GOLD SULFO-TAG NHS-Ester, would reduce the net positive charge and thereby decrease solubility versus the unconjugated material [58]. Guided by these insights, formulation conditions maximizing net molecular charge separation were identified to minimize self-association, then confirmed experimentally by rangefinding studies. Upon scaleup and freeze–thaw challenge (three cycles), both biotinylated and ruthenylated reagents remained high purity by HPSEC with acceptable appearance, and these outcomes were reproduced across two independent program conjugates, supporting the robustness of the approach.

A notable observation was the label-dependent stability behavior under stress. While the initial PBS formulations showed no aggregates by HPSEC or visible particles for either conjugate, freeze-thaw and accelerated conditions revealed divergent product quality. The neutral biotin label was associated with minimal HPSEC-detectable aggregates and few subvisible particles but increased visible particle formation, consistent with enhanced particle growth via attractive interactions among positive surface patches once neutral moieties replace charged amines. Conversely, the negatively charged GOLD SULFO-TAG NHS-Ester produced aggregates, subvisible, and visible particles, suggesting that although the net negative contribution may impart electrostatic repulsion that moderates rapid large particle formation, it does not eliminate association and nucleation pathways that generate multiscale particulate species. These observations align with prior reports for platform antibodies that highlight charge modulating conjugations as key determinants of aggregation kinetics and particle size distributions.

Importantly, the modeling indicated PBS is suboptimal for conjugated scFv-hFc stability, with improved behavior expected at lower pH and higher ionic strength, where net charge distributions and screened interactions reduce self-association. The empirical data support that the parental scFv-hFc in PBS was near the solubility edge, and conjugation further pushed the system toward instability via decreased positive charge density. Together, these findings emphasize that label chemistry, conjugation site context, buffer pH, and ionic strength jointly shape the colloidal stability landscape for scFv-hFc critical reagents.

The optimized conjugated critical reagents were supplied for clinical ADA assay development. During assay development, the material performed adequately with no reports of precipitation or instability across multiple bioanalytical laboratories. In validation it was demonstrated that the ADA assay from these optimized critical reagents performed acceptably, showing performance characteristics consistent with typical ADA assays (Figure 5). Furthermore, this workflow was also employed for a clinical ADA assay in second CAR-T program where the solubility score of the conjugated scFv-hFc material indicated a propensity to aggregate with routine conjugation protocols. Here, the optimized conjugated material was provided proactively, potentially saving time in ADA assay development. This material also provided for an ADA assay that performed acceptably in validation.

Beyond downstream ADA reagent supply, the results have upstream implications for CAR scFv design and screening. Incorporating solubility and oligomerization risk modeling during preclinical construct selection can reduce later formulation burdens by selecting sequences with favorable colloidal profiles. Explicitly modeling the impact of intended labels on isoelectric point and charge patch distribution provides a line-of-sight to bioanalysis, enabling proactive mitigation (e.g., buffer selection, excipient strategies, or alternative label/linker chemistries). Finally, expanding the dataset to additional scFv-hFc molecules will clarify the predictive range and limits of the modeling framework and refine label-specific formulation rules that generalize beyond the two programs studied.

Conclusion

Some biotherapeutics can pose unique challenges in critical reagent preparation for assays in drug development. This study shows that a model-guided process development approach can reliably produce high-purity, stable-conjugated critical reagents for ADA assays, even when routine platform conditions are suboptimal. Charge aware structural modeling predicted reduced solubility after amine-directed NHS-ester conjugation and correctly pointed to lower pH, higher salt formulations to mitigate self-association, findings that were confirmed experimentally and scaled successfully. Label chemistry matters: neutral biotin favored visible particle formation with limited soluble aggregates, while negatively charged GOLD SULFO-TAG NHS-Ester produced aggregates across size classes, highlighting the need to integrate label-dependent charge effects into formulation selection. These insights support broader biopharmaceutical pipelines by enabling fit-for-purpose reagent design and advocate for early sequence and label impact modeling during development to improve downstream manufacturability and bioanalytical reliability.
