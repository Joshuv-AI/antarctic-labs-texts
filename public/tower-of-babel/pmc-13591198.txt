Unlocking the Hepatoprotective Potential of Urolithin A in High‐Fat Diet‐Induced MASLD: Integrated Role of Oxidative Stress, Inflammation, FXR/PPAR‐α, and Fibrotic Pathway
Authors: Hamad Alsaykhan, Amany Mohamed Shalaby, Walaa Arafa Keshk, Shahd Saad El‐dien Kamel, Sulaiman Mohammed Alnasser, Mohammed Alorini, Fatima A Jaber, Mohamed Ali Alabiad, Amr Mohamed Younes, Hanim Magdy Abdelnour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT“Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease” (MASLD) is marked by hepatic steatosis, oxidative stress, inflammation, and fibrosis. Urolithin A (Uro A), a metabolite of ellagitannins, exhibits anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and metabolic regulatory activities. This work examined the preventive benefits of Uro A against high‐fat diet (HFD)–induced MASLD in rats and elucidated the underlying mechanisms. Four experimental groups of rats were established: control, Uro A, HFD, and HFD‐Uro A. MASLD was induced by 12 weeks of HFD feeding. Uro A (2.5 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) was administered four times weekly. Metabolic indices, lipid profile, liver transaminases, oxidative stress markers, inflammatory mediators, fibrogenic markers, and hepatic farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and peroxisomal proliferator‐activated receptor (PPAR)‐α gene expression were evaluated. Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses were performed. HFD significantly increased body weight, adiposity, hepatic index, transaminases, dyslipidemia, oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokines, and fibrotic markers, while downregulating FXR and PPAR‐α expression. Histopathology revealed steatosis, inflammatory infiltration, and increased expression of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and Bax. Uro A markedly ameliorated metabolic, biochemical, and molecular alterations, with evident histological improvement. These findings demonstrate that Uro A exerts hepatoprotective effects against HFD‐induced MASLD through integrated modulation of oxidative stress, inflammation, fibrosis, and FXR/PPAR‐α pathway, bolstering its prospects as a multi‐target therapeutic option for MASLD.Keywords: histopathology, inflammatory biomarkers, MASLD, oxidative stress, Urolithin A

Introduction

Obesity and its problems are consistently associated with long‐term unhealthy dietary habits and a sedentary lifestyle. These effects result from impairment of intestinal barrier integrity, alteration of the gut microbiome and the gut‐liver axis, with consequent impaired metabolism, ectopic fat deposition, adipose tissue dysfunction, disruptions in glucose and lipid homeostasis, and lipotoxicity. The resulting metabolic stress triggers inflammation and oxidative stress, ultimately promoting the emergence of insulin resistance [1, 2].

One of the most common metabolic liver disorders worldwide is non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), now known as “metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease” (MASLD). It is marked by hepatic fat deposition greater than 5% of liver weight along with either diabetes mellitus, obesity, or overweight, or other metabolic dysfunctions [3]. The condition varies in severity from uncomplicated steatosis to non‐alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), currently termed “metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis” (MASH), and liver fibrosis, which can advance to cirrhosis, with MASH being the most severe and progressive form [4].

Its pathogenesis remains incompletely elucidated. However, interactions among unhealthy lifestyle factors, such as chronic nutrient overload, genetic and epigenetic susceptibility, gut microbiome dysbiosis, insulin resistance, and uncontrolled lipolysis with unregulated fatty acid trafficking, are strongly implicated in its etiology. Collectively, they lead to hepatic fatty acid influx exceeding their oxidation or export, resulting in lipid accumulation within hepatocytes [5, 6].

Intrahepatic accumulation of lipid or its toxic metabolites leads to hepatic lipotoxicity, a central mechanism in MASLD progression. Lipotoxicity enhances oxidative stress, inflammation, and mitochondrial injury, with excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and impaired electron transport chain, resulting in further mitochondrial dysfunction and establishing a vicious cycle [7]. Additionally, steatotic or injured hepatic cells secrete extracellular vesicles and/or soluble factors that exacerbate the inflammatory and oxidative responses and can cause progressive fibrosis by stimulating hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) to generate more extracellular matrix. Thus, metabolic overload, coupled with the activation of inflammatory, fibrogenic, and apoptotic pathways in hepatocytes, aids in the development of MASH [8].

Impaired antioxidant mechanisms and increased oxidative stress are implicated as the central driver of NASH. Oxidative stress triggers macromolecular damage, mitochondrial and lysosomal dysfunction, inflammation, and hepatocellular cell death. The antioxidant response is mainly controlled by nuclear factor E2‐related factor 2 (Nrf2), which also modulates lipid metabolism genes, prevents inflammation, and is necessary for mitochondrial integrity. Consequently, modification of the antioxidant response represents a compelling target for preventing NASH [9]. The gut–liver axis links the intestinal barrier integrity, gut microbiome, and hepatic metabolic reprogramming through two main pathways: the hepatic portal vein and bile acids. It has been reported to induce metabolic dysfunction, which is a critical factor in NAFLD progression. Prior research has demonstrated that the liver is negatively affected by disrupted bile acid balance, changes in the gut microbiome, and compromised intestinal barrier integrity, leading to increased influx of intestinal microbes and their byproducts to the liver [10].

Transcription factors, including farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and peroxisomal proliferator‐activated receptor (PPAR)‐α, serve as mediators of the gut–liver axis, converting extracellular signals, such as nutrients and microbiota metabolites, into transcriptional responses that modulate genes associated with inflammation and metabolism [2]. The PPAR‐α transcription factor, a nuclear receptor, is predominantly expressed in hepatocytes, where it is essential for enhancing energy production by activating energy‐generating pathways, including fatty acid oxidation [11].

The liver, colon, and kidney are the primary sites of FXR expression, a ligand‐activated nuclear receptor that engages with bile. It orchestrates multiple metabolic pathways, including modulation of the intestinal barrier, bile acid synthesis, insulin sensitivity, energy homeostasis, glucose homeostasis, and lipid metabolism. Intestinal dysbiosis with altered bile acid types and composition, particularly secondary bile acids, disrupts FXR signaling, thereby promoting hepatocellular injury, apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress. FXR agonists have demonstrated efficacy in ameliorating liver inflammation, steatosis, and fibrosis, whereas dual FXR/PPAR agonists are being studied for their therapeutic efficacy [2, 10, 12].

Despite intensive research efforts, lifestyle modifications and pharmacological interventions have demonstrated limited therapeutic efficacy due to the complex multifactorial pathogenesis of NASH, suboptimal patient adherence, and drug‐related adverse effects [13]. Identifying bioactive compounds capable of simultaneously modulating the interconnected pathways of lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, inflammation, fibrosis, and cell death represents a critical research priority in shielding against MASH. Owing to the reported roles of dietary supplements as probiotics and polyphenols in normalizing the gut microbiome and intestinal barrier function, targeted nutritional interventions may help protect against MASLD [10].

Urolithins are naturally occurring polyphenols generated by the gut microbiota's metabolism of ellagic acid and foods abundant in ellagitannins, including almonds, pomegranates, and berries. The most bioactive metabolite of ellagitannins, urolithin A (Uro A), has been documented to possess numerous beneficial activities, including anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, lipid‐metabolism‐modulating, anti‐fibrotic, mitochondrial‐quality‐improving, and anti‐carcinogenic [14].

The protective efficacy of Uro A against MASLD and its underlying mechanisms remains elusive. In the present investigation, we generated a MASLD rat model using a high‐fat diet (HFD) and employed biochemical, molecular, and histological analysis to investigate the integrated roles of redox balance, inflammation, PPAR signaling, FXR, and the transforming growth factor‐beta (TGF‐β)/SMAD3 fibrotic pathway in HFD‐induced MASH. This work also illustrated the potential protective impact of Uro A against MASLD progression and clarified the underlying mechanisms.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

Sigma Aldrich (MO, USA) supplied Uro A powder (purity ≥ 97%, Cat. No. SML1791) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Cat. No. D2650). Uro A was dissolved at 25 mg/mL in DMSO and diluted to 0.25 mg/mL in PBS (pH 7.4). The maximum DMSO concentration in any therapy was 1%.

Experimental Design and Treatment Protocol

Forty adult male rats, weighing 130–150 g at the beginning of the experiment, were maintained in a setting with a regulated room temperature of 18°C–22°C, a relative humidity of 50%–60%, and a 12‐h light‐dark cycle. The animal investigations followed the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo trials) guidelines. All experimental techniques and procedures complied with the National Institutes of Health standards for the care and use of laboratory animals (NIH Publications No. 8023, revised 1978) and received approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Tanta University (Approval code: 36264PR1310189/2/26). Prior to the experimental procedures, the animals underwent an acclimatization period of 1 week, during which they had unrestricted access to a standard pellet diet; they were then randomly assigned to four groups (n = 10 each). Group I (control group) rats were maintained on a standard laboratory diet and received vehicle (1% DMSO/PBS) intraperitoneally (i.p) for 12 weeks. Group II (Uro A group) rats fed a standard diet and treated with Uro A at a dose of 2.5 mg/kg i.p, four times per week for 12 weeks of the experiment [15]. Group III (HFD group) comprised rats fed HFD (standard chow supplemented with 10% lard and 2% cholesterol) for 12 weeks [16]. Group IV (HFD‐Uro A group) consisted of rats fed HFD concomitantly with Uro A, as in previous groups, for 12 weeks.

Food intake was monitored to calculate calorie intake in all groups of rats. The body weight was measured weekly. A digital scale was used to assess body weight, while a regular ruler was used to measure body length from the nasal bone to the anus with the head extended. The body mass index (BMI) and Lee index were used to assess obesity, where: BMI = Body weight (g)/naso‐anal length (cm2) [17, 18].

Sample Collection

Twelve weeks after the induction, the overnight‐fasted rats were weighed, anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 2% pentobarbital (2 mL/kg), and then sacrificed. Blood was collected and centrifuged to obtain serum, which was then stored at −80°C until analysis. The liver from each animal was rapidly removed, weighed, and dissected into sections over ice. The first section of the fresh liver tissue from each rat was immersed in neutral buffered formalin (10%) for histopathological evaluation. Additionally, the second one was placed in liquid nitrogen and then transferred to a −80°C for storage. The third one was homogenized on ice with 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), then centrifuged at 11,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was stored at −80°C until analysis. Liver tissue protein level was assessed as previously described by [19]. Furthermore, retroperitoneal adipose tissue was collected and weighed to calculate the adiposity index [18].

Biochemical Study

Assessment of Hepatic Function and Lipid Profile

Hepatic function was evaluated by measuring serum levels of albumin, alanine transaminase (ALT), and aspartate transaminase (AST) using commercial kits (Biodiagnostic Inc., Egypt). High‐density lipoprotein (HDL), triglycerides (TG), and total cholesterol (TC) were assessed employing commercial kits (Biomerieux, Egypt). Friedewald's formula was employed to estimate low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) level, expressed as LDL = TC ‐ HDL ‐ TG/5 [20]. The levels of very‐low‐density lipoproteins (VLDL) were assessed. utilizing Wilson et al. equation, VLDL = TG/5 [21].

Assessment of Redox Status, Inflammation, and Fibrosis Signals

Oxidative stress was evaluated by assessing hepatic malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, an indicator of lipid oxidation, whereas hepatic antioxidant status was determined by assessing superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity using commercial kits (Bio‐diagnostic Co., Giza, Egypt). Inflammatory marker interleukin (IL)−6 levels and fibrogenic upstream signal, TGF‐β in hepatic tissue were measured using commercial ELISA kits (My BioSource, CA, USA). Hepatic hydroxyproline content (marker for fibrosis) was measured as previously described by Medugorac [22].

Molecular Study

Assessment of FXR, PPAR‐α, and SMAD‐3 Gene Expression Using Real‐time Polymerase Chain Reaction

Following the manufacturer's instructions, frozen hepatic tissues were processed, and total RNA was isolated using the Qiagen RNeasy Total RNA isolation kit (Qiagen, Hiden, Germany). The NanoDrop spectrophotometer was then used to evaluate the extracted RNA's concentration and purity. The SuperScript III First‐Strand Synthesis System for RT‐PCR kit (Life Technologies) was used to synthesize cDNA. Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Life Technologies) was used to conduct PCR experiments. Rat GAPDH served as an internal reference for SMAD‐3, whereas FXR and PPAR‐α mRNA transcripts were evaluated in relation to the housekeeping gene β‐actin. Sequence‐specific primers were designed in Table 1 according to [23, 24, 25]. The enzyme was activated by cycling at 95°C for 12 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95°C for 30 s, 60°C for 60 s, and 72°C for 60 s. The 2 − ΔΔCT comparative technique was used to determine target gene expression [26].

Histological Study

Light Microscopic Study

Liver samples were preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin solution and subsequently processed into paraffin blocks. The paraffin slices, with a thickness of 4 µm, were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological evaluation [27]. Liver tissue was also processed to form semithin sections and then stained with toluidine blue.

NAFLD activity was assessed in H&E‐stained liver sections by examining 20 randomly selected high‐power fields per section. The NAFLD Activity Score (NAS) was derived from three histological parameters: steatosis, lobular inflammation, and hepatocellular ballooning. Steatosis was scored from 0 to 3 according to the percentage of affected hepatocytes (< 5%, 5%–33%, 34%–66%, and > 67%, respectively). Lobular inflammation was graded from 0 to 3 based on the number of inflammatory foci per 200× field (none, < 2, 2–4, and > 4 foci, respectively). Ballooning was scored from 0 to 2 according to the absence, presence of a few, or presence of numerous ballooned hepatocytes. The three component scores were then summed to obtain the total NAS (0–8) [28].

The percentage area occupied by intracellular lipid vacuoles was quantified in toluidine blue‐stained semithin sections. Twenty randomly selected microscopic fields were analyzed for each animal using image analysis software, and the area occupied by lipid vacuoles was expressed as a percentage of the total analyzed tissue area.

Immunohistochemistry

Five‐micrometre sections from the paraffin blocks utilized for histological assessment were affixed to silanized slides, deparaffinized, and subsequently rehydrated in ethanol. Antigen retrieval was conducted using a pH 6.0 citrate buffer, and endogenous peroxidase activity was minimized with 0.3% H2O2 in methanol. Primary antibodies; glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) (SC‐58,766, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), tumor Necrosis Factor Alpha (TNF‐α) (ab6671, Abcam), and Bax (SC‐7480, Santa Biotechnology, INC) were incubated overnight at 4°C in a humidified chamber, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies. The reaction was seen using 3,3‐Diaminobenzidine. Counterstaining was performed with Mayer's hematoxylin [29, 30]. Slides were captured with a Nikon Ci‐L light microscope. The images were examined on a computer equipped with an Intel Core i7 processor utilizing Video Test Morphology software (Russia), which incorporates a specialized technique for measuring area %.

Statistical Analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SD. The statistical significance between experimental groups was evaluated using one‐way ANOVA and Tukey's post hoc test. Probability values below 0.05 were significant.

Results

Effect of HFD and Uro A on Body Weight, Hepatic Index, BMI, and Adiposity Index

Body weight, adiposity index, BMI, and hepatic index increased significantly (p < 0.001) in the HFD group versus the control group. Meanwhile, the co‐administration of Uro A and HFD in group IV protected against the increase in the previous parameters relative to the HFD group (p < 0.001, Table 2, Figure 1A). There was no significant difference in the daily food intake among the studied groups (p > 0.05). Meanwhile, caloric intake was significantly increased (p < 0.05) in the HFD groups versus control group with no significant difference was reported between HFD and HFD‐ Uro A groups (Figure 1B,C).

Effect of HFD and Uro A on Liver Function and Lipid Profile

Feeding rats with HFD (group III) significantly increased (p < 0.001) ALT and AST activity in addition to TG, TC, VLDL, and LDL. Also, a concomitant reduction in HDL and albumin levels was reported compared with the control group. Meanwhile, the co‐administration of Uro A and HFD markedly improved liver function parameters and the lipid profile (p < 0.001, Table 2).

Effect of HFD and Uro A on Redox Status, Fibrogenesis

Feeding rats with HFD (group III) significantly increased (p < 0.001) hepatic tissue MDA levels, IL‐6, hydroxyproline, and TGF‐β levels, and SMAD‐3 expression levels (markers of fibrogenesis and inflammation), while it significantly decreased (p < 0.001) tissue SOD activity compared to control. Meanwhile, the co‐administration of Uro A in group IV markedly ameliorated the redox, inflammatory, and fibrogenic status compared with HFD group, as marked herein by a remarkable reduction (p < 0.001) in the levels of MDA, IL6, hydroxyproline, TGF‐β, and SMAD‐3 expression, with a concomitant elevation (p < 0.001) in SOD activity (Table 2 and Figure 1D).

Effect of HFD and Uro A on FXR and PPAR‐α Expression in the Liver

Feeding rats with HFD resulted in a marked decrease (p < 0.001) in FXR and PPAR‐α expression compared with control group. Co‐administration of Uro A markedly enhanced FXR (p = 0.004) and PPAR‐α (p = 0.011) expression in HFD‐Uro A group compared with the HFD group (group III) (Figure 1E,F).

Discussion

The liver is essential for controlling energy balance, although it is vulnerable to the consequences of excessive food consumption. MASLD, a significant global public health issue, highlights the complex association between hepatic steatosis and metabolic dysfunction [31]. Excess nutrient intake and unhealthy dietary habits, such as HFD induce abdominal obesity, systemic oxidative stress, inflammation, intestinal barrier disruption, and gut dysbiosis. Furthermore, it is associated with an exaggerated hepatic flux of free fatty acids (FFA) and impaired hepatic export, with consequent deleterious effects, including altered liver metabolism, lipotoxicity, inflammation, and oxidative stress, culminating in hepatocellular damage and MASLD [32].

Despite the increasing prevalence of MASLD, its pathogenesis and the approved therapeutic strategies remain unknown. Likewise, MASLD is often not clinically detected until the liver is severely injured or complications arise. Thus, research is aiming to effectively prevent or manage this condition. Uro A is a polyphenol metabolite derived from ellagitannin‐rich food by the action of gut microbiota with anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, anti‐fibrotic effects, gut‐liver axis supportive effect, and mitochondria and autophagy enhancing effects [33]. Consequently, the multi‐target potential, cost‐effectiveness, and natural origin of Uro A provide it a compelling contender, particularly as an adjuvant therapy for neurodegenerative disorders, cardiovascular diseases, muscular dysfunctions, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and hepatic conditions [33].

However, its role in the underlying mechanism of HFD‐induced MASLD remains poorly understood. To address this knowledge gap, we established an HFD‐induced MASLD rat model that mimics unhealthy dietary habits to evaluate the potential integrated roles of oxidative stress, inflammation, TGFβ/SMAD3, PPAR‐α, and FXR, and to highlight the possible effect of Uro A. In the current research, histological, morphometric, immunohistochemical, and biochemical assessments verified the valuable effects of Uro A, including improvements in BMI, adiposity index, liver index, lipid profile, maintenance of the redox status, and reductions in inflammation and fibrosis, indicating its beneficial adjuvant role in shielding against MASH.

Results obtained herein demonstrated a significant increase in body weight that was obviously noticed from the fourth to the 12th week of HFD feeding. Moreover, BMI and adiposity index increased significantly upon HFD feeding compared with controls. Additionally, the lipid profile was impaired, as evidenced by increases in pro‐atherogenic TG and LDL‐C and a decrease in anti‐atherogenic HDL‐C. The obtained results were in congruence with Rosnah et al. [34] and Kwarteng and Laing [35], HFD‐induced weight gains and the impairment in lipid profile reported here probably may be due to the increased accumulation of abdominal fat due to increased caloric intake, marked here by adiposity index, for the HFD group, which was proven to be associated with metabolic stress, dyslipidemia, oxidative stress, and ectopic fat deposition as steatosis [36].

Moreover, hypertrophy, hyperplasia, and adipocyte phenotype changes, with consequent immune cell recruitment, prompt activation of inflammatory signaling pathways and IL6 production. Excess cytokines exacerbate insulin resistance, promote lipolysis, affect appetite and satiety, and alter glucose and lipid metabolism, inflammation, and immune function [37].

Meanwhile, the co‐administration of Uro A to the HFD group protected them from the excess weight gain, visceral fat deposition, and dyslipidemia. Xia et al. [38] reported that Uro A boosted energy consumption by improving adipose tissue thermogenesis both in vivo and in vitro, and suggested that it may be through adipose tissue triiodothyronine action and lipolytic enzymes upregulation. Additionally, Manigandan and Yun [39], found that treatment with Uro A significantly increased thermogenesis, lipolysis, fatty acid oxidation, and white adipocyte browning via stimulation of the β3‐adrenergic receptor. Meanwhile, lipogenesis was downregulated in white adipocytes. Abdulrahman et al. [40] reported that Uro A modified gut microbiota, improved lipid metabolism, decreased visceral adipose tissue mass, and BW gain by increasing metabolic energy expenditure and reducing dietary fat absorption in the HFD model.

In the present study, HFD feeding significantly impaired liver function, indicating hepatocellular damage. Meanwhile, it was improved with Uro A co‐administration. The obtained outcomes match with those published by Xia et al. [38] and can be explained by decrements in FFA liver flux and, consequently, lipotoxicity.

Although multiple diagnostic approaches are available, liver biopsy continues to be the gold standard for the diagnosis and grading of liver disease. Histopathological evaluation is essential for assessing the extent of hepatic injury and the course of MASH [41]. In our study, we identified several histopathological changes, including hepatic steatosis and inflammatory cell infiltration.

Dysregulation of fatty acid and bile acid metabolism is closely associated with oxidative stress and inflammation, both are essential agents in the MASLD development. Oxidative stress, in particular, has been identified as a major driver of disease progression. Antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, constitute a primary defense against oxidative damage [42]. Lipotoxicity further exacerbates cellular injury by inducing oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and inflammation, while also activating pro‐apoptotic pathways that culminate in cellular damage and death. Damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) are released as a result of this activity, which then triggers the nuclear factor kappa‐B (NF‐κB) signaling pathway, maintaining sterile inflammation and encouraging the generation of proinflammatory cytokines [43].

Through cell stress pathways, damaged hepatocytes trigger Kupffer cells, which in turn stimulate inflammation through Toll‐like receptor signaling, TNF‐α, and NF‐κB. Additionally, a number of mediators secreted by injured hepatocytes and activated macrophages, such as ROS, lipid peroxidation products, and TGF‐β1, cause HSCs activation and their subsequent transformation into myo‐fibroblasts that secrete extracellular matrix proteins like collagen I and fibronectin, resulting in liver fibrosis and a loss of the equilibrium between extracellular matrix generation and breakdown [41, 44]. This could account for the upregulation of HSCs marker “GFAP” observed in our study.

Upon attachment of TGF‐β to its receptor, the TGF‐β type I receptor phosphorylates and activates the mediator in non‐SMAD and SMAD circuits, causing various biological consequences, including angiogenesis, cell survival, and proliferation. Additionally, in the liver, it promotes the expression of genes controlling fibrogenesis, lipid accumulation, and lipogenesis, while suppressing those involved in β‐oxidation [45].

HFD feeding was associated with significant impairment in hepatic tissue redox state, as evidenced by reduced SOD activity and elevated MDA levels. The findings correspond with Abdulrahman et al. [15] and Zhang et al. [42]. Co‐administration of Uro A to HFD‐fed rats improved the redox state. The results obtained are in line with earlier findings [15].

Chen et al. [46] discovered that Uro A therapy ameliorated d‐gal‐induced hepato‐renal injury by improving the redox state and decreasing the level of inflammatory cytokines. Moreover, hepatic IL6, TGF‐β levels, hydroxyproline level, SMAD‐3 expression, and TNF‐α immune expression were increased in HFD‐fed animals, indicating a heightened inflammatory, oxidative, and fibrogenic state. The findings were consistent with Ishiyama et al. [47] and Yadav et al. [41].

Meanwhile, Uro A co‐administration decreased IL6 and TGF‐β levels, hydroxyproline level, SMAD‐3expression, and TNF‐α immune expression, underscoring the protective potential of Uro A in modulating liver fibrosis and inflammation. The reduced generation of inflammatory cytokines during Uro A co‐administration can be attributed to its capacity to block NF‐κB pathway stimulation, limit NADPH oxidase and inducible nitric oxide synthase activity, and decrease ROS formation [48].

It was reported that Uro A reduced hepatic triglyceride and lipogenic gene expression, hepatic and adipose inflammation, oxidative and ER stress, and upregulated beta‐oxidation genes in the HFD model [49]. Additionally, Hasheminezhad et al. [50] suggested that Uro A prevented barrier destruction caused by inflammation.

Alterations in hepatic lipid metabolism represent key mechanisms underlying the development of steatosis. A vital cellular energy sensor and regulator, AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) modulates several pathways linked to MASLD. These include enhancing insulin sensitivity, suppression of hepatic lipogenesis, promotion of fatty acid oxidation, and reducing inflammation by downregulating transcription factors like PPAR‐α and sterol regulatory element‐binding protein‐1 (SREBP‐1) [41, 51].

PPAR‐α is crucial for enhancing oxidation of fatty acids, reducing VLDL production, increasing HDL production, increasing lipoprotein lipase activity, and decreasing inflammation and oxidative stress. In clinical practice, PPAR‐α agonists have been utilized as medications that decrease cholesterol [52, 53].

In the current work, HFD feeding significantly downregulated PPAR‐α expression. Consistent with our findings, Shi et al. [54] found that fibroblast growth factor overexpression and PPARα activation ameliorate HFD–induced hepatic steatosis. Increased production of inflammatory mediators in HFD directly damages hepatocytes, inhibits PPAR‐α expression, and activates the NF‐κB pathway, resulting in elevated levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and perpetuating a detrimental cycle [55].

Meanwhile, co‐administration of Uro A resulted in a marked rise in PPAR‐α expression, suggesting enhanced fatty acid oxidation and a consequent decrease in lipid accumulation. These findings are in agreement with previous reports of Hashimoto et al. [56] and Abdulrahman et al. [15]. Furthermore, Kang et al. [57] demonstrated that Uro A treatment elevated levels of phosphorylated AMPK, thereby enhancing fatty acid oxidation and markedly inhibiting the production of adipogenic proteins in both adipocytes and hepatoma cells.

Growing data indicate a strong correlation between NAFLD and bile acid metabolism abnormalities, implying the potential of targeting bile acid signaling for MAFLD therapy. FXR, a bile acid‐activated nuclear receptor. It exhibits favorable effects on cholesterol and bile acid homeostasis, hepatic gluconeogenesis, fatty acid metabolism, inflammation, and fibrosis. Moreover, it modulates intestinal barrier integrity and microbiota homeostasis [58, 59].

Previous researchers reported that nuclear receptor dysfunction, as in FXR, contributes to liver inflammation and the development of MASLD. Moreover, it could modulate hepatic lipid metabolism by suppressing stearoyl‐CoA desaturase and SREBP‐1c, while stimulating AMPK [55, 60]. In the present research, HFD feeding significantly downregulates FXR expression. Meanwhile, Uro A co‐administration significantly improved their expression. Kuang et al. [61] reported that hyodeoxycholic acid, a gut microbiota‐modified bile acid, administration to the HFD model significantly enhances lipid catabolism via activation of PPAR‐α signaling, which consequently increases hepatic FXR expression.

In the same line, Zhang et al. [62] found that the high expression of hepatic zinc finger and BTB domain‐containing 18 mitigated insulin resistance and hepatic steatosis, by activating FXR‐mediated fatty acid oxidation in an HFD model. Chen et al. [63] discovered that diosgenin downregulated genes related to hepatic denovo lipogenesis and increased the expression of FXR and small heterodimer partner (SHP). Moreover, it promoted PPAR‐α expression in an HFD model. Yan et al. [64] found that FXR, Nrf2, and SHP expression were downregulated in an HFD model, while curcumin, a natural polyphenol compound, administration restored this pathway. However, to our knowledge, no investigation has shown the effect of Uro A on hepatic FXR expression in an HFD model.

The present study has several limitations, including a limited sample size, which may constrain the generalizability of the findings to the wider community. Additionally, further investigations incorporating microbiome profiling and assessment of intestinal permeability are necessary to clarify the processes by which Uro A affects the gut flora.

Conclusion

Uro A exerts hepatoprotective effects against HFD‐induced MASLD through integrated modulation of oxidative stress, inflammation, fibrosis, and the FXR/PPAR‐α pathway, supporting its potential as a multi‐target treatment option for MASLD.
