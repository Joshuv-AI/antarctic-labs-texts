Psychometric properties of the Turkish version of the Parkinson’s disease quality of life-7 (PDQoL-7) in older adults
Authors: Aslı Demirtaş, Dilek İşcan, Filiz Kardiyen, Selcan Suiçmez, İlke Keser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParkinson’s disease (PD) is a common neurodegenerative condition in older adults and is associated with substantial impairment in quality of life. In geriatric populations, brief, and reliable disease-specific instruments are particularly important for reducing cognitive and physical burden during assessment. This study aimed to adapt the Parkinson’s Disease Quality of Life-7 (PDQoL-7) into Turkish and to evaluate its validity and reliability in older adults with PD.MethodsThis methodological study included 129 individuals aged 65–90 years with PD who attended a neurology outpatient clinic between November 2023 and February 2025. The translation and cross-cultural adaptation process followed internationally accepted guidelines. Psychometric evaluation included internal consistency assessed by Cronbach’s alpha, test-retest reliability assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient, construct validity examined through inter-item correlations and confirmatory factor analysis, and concurrent validity evaluated by correlations with the Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire-39 (PDQ-39) and the Short Form-36 (SF-36).ResultsThe Turkish PDQoL-7 demonstrated excellent test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.970; item ICCs = 0.863–0.947; p < 0.001) and internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.928; item-deleted range: 0.913–0.921). Inter-item correlations (0.549–0.724) supported item homogeneity and a unidimensional structure. Confirmatory factor analysis confirmed a one-factor model with strong loadings (0.76–0.90) and good model fit (χ²(14) = 20.47, p = 0.116; CFI = 0.984; TLI = 0.977; RMSEA = 0.060; SRMR = 0.034). Strong correlations were found with PDQ-39 (r = 0.916) and SF-36 general health (r= -0.809).ConclusionThe Turkish version of the PDQoL-7 is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing quality of life in older adults with PD. Its brief structure and strong psychometric properties support its use in geriatric clinical practice and research settings.Clinical trial numberClinicalTrials.gov, NCT06230926, registered on 19 January 2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07458-y.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder caused by dopaminergic dysfunction in the basal ganglia [1]. It is characterized by both motor (e.g., resting tremor, bradykinesia, postural instability, rigidity) and non-motor (e.g., cognitive impairment, depression, anxiety, dysautonomia, sleep disturbances) symptoms [2]. The characteristic degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra disrupts basal ganglia function and leads to motor disorders such as bradykinesia, tremor, rigidity, and postural instability; these impair functional independence and the ability to perform daily activities [3, 4]. However, neuropsychological dysfunction-including cognitive decline, emotional disturbances such as depression, psychosis, and mood swings, as well as fatigue and sleep disorders-which become particularly evident in the advanced stages of the disease, underscores the significant impact of these impairments on patient well-being [3, 5–7]. Given its progressive nature and high prevalence in older adults, PD leads to substantial functional decline and significantly impairs patients’ quality of life (QoL) [8].

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines health-related quality of life (HRQoL) as an individual’s subjective perception of their overall well-being, encompassing their social, physical, mental, and emotional functioning as shaped by their health status [7]. Therefore, in the assessment of HRQoL, patients should be evaluated in a multidimensional manner using a biopsychosocial approach, taking into account physical, psychological, environmental, and social relationship factors [9]. HRQoL not only considers physical health but also accounts for how the disease and treatment affect the patient’s ability to lead a fulfilling life in daily life, including expectations, concerns, goals, and standards [7]. In healthcare research, HRQoL-which reflects patients’ perceptions of their health and functioning-is frequently assessed, especially in chronic and progressive conditions like PD [10]. In PD, both motor and non-motor symptoms contribute to a decline in HRQoL; however, it has been shown that non-motor symptoms-such as depression, anxiety, cognitive decline, and fatigue-have a particularly strong impact, especially in older adults [2]. Therefore, evaluating QoL is essential for guiding clinical decision-making, treatment planning, and long-term care in geriatric populations with PD.

Several generic HRQoL instruments are used in PD, including the Short Form-36 (SF-36) [11], Nottingham Health Profile (NHP) [12], Sickness Impact Profile (SIP) [13], EuroQol-5 Dimension (EQ-5D) [14], and Quality of Life in Neurological Disorders (Neuro-QoL) [15]. Also, to address this, disease-specific tools such as the Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire-39 (PDQ-39) [16], the Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire-8 (PDQ-8) [17], Parkinson’s Disease Quality of Life Scale (PDQL) [18], and Parkinson’s Impact Scale (PIMS) [19] have been developed. Although these tools provide valuable information, many are relatively long and may pose practical challenges in routine clinical settings, particularly among older adults who may experience cognitive, sensory, or physical limitations. The PDQ-39 and its shorter version, the PDQ-8, are among the scales frequently used in the literature to assess disease-specific QoL in patients with PD [17, 20, 21]. Because the PDQ-8 is shorter, it takes less time to complete; however, its validity and reliability are lower than those of the PDQ-39 [22–24]. Although the PDQ-8 is a brief disease-specific assessment tool for PD patients, it has certain limitations. These include the ambiguity of some items and the insufficient number of items addressing non-motor symptoms [20, 22]. Additionally, its focus on assessing the patient’s condition and events over the past month is another drawback [25]. Considering that non-motor symptoms can affect HRQoL and that cognitive problems may be present, these disadvantages have necessitated the development of new scales that are short, easy to administer, and capable of providing a detailed assessment. Therefore, there is a need for a practical, reliable, and easy-to-administer tool that covers the full range of symptoms experienced by PD patients, imposes no cognitive burden or fatigue, has a short recall period, and is clear and comprehensive.

The Parkinson’s Disease Quality of Life-7 (PDQoL-7) questionnaire, developed by Stathis and Papadopoulos in 2022, meets this need. PDQoL-7 is a brief, patient-reported tool designed to assess QoL in individuals with PD [25]. It consists of 7 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating worse QoL. Its brevity, ease of administration, and disease-specific focus make it particularly suitable for use in geriatric clinical practice and research. To assess the impact of PD across the biopsychosocial domains (physical, mental, and social), a cross-cultural adaptation of the updated PDQoL-7, a valid PD-specific HRQoL questionnaire, is required.

However, to date, a Turkish version of the PDQoL-7 with established psychometric properties has not been available. Given the growing older population with PD and the need for efficient QoL assessment tools in geriatric care, cross-cultural adaptation and validation of the PDQoL-7 are warranted. Therefore, this study aimed to adapt the PDQoL-7 into Turkish and to test the hypothesis that its original single-factor structure would be confirmed, with satisfactory validity and reliability, in individuals diagnosed with PD.

Methods

Study design and participants

This observational, cross-sectional study was conducted in collaboration with Gazi University Faculty of Health Sciences and Niğde Ömer Halisdemir University. Data were collected from patients diagnosed with PD who visited the Neurology Outpatient Clinic of Niğde Ömer Halisdemir University Training and Research Hospital between November 5, 2023, and February 5, 2025. Inclusion criteria were adults aged 65 years and older with a confirmed diagnosis of PD by a neurologist, and native Turkish language proficiency. Cognitive status was assessed clinically by a neurologist using orientation to person, place, and time; individuals with evident cognitive impairment were excluded from the study. Individuals with dementia, significant hearing or visual impairments, or who were unable to read or write sufficiently to comprehend and complete the questionnaires independently were excluded from the study. Only patients who were clinically stable and receiving routine medical treatment were included. No changes in medical treatment occurred during the two-week test–retest interval, and all participants had been on stable medication regimens for at least three months prior to the study.

Ethics approval

The aim and methodology of the study were explained in detail to all participants prior to data collection, and only those who voluntarily agreed to participate were included. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Gazi University (approval number: E-77082166-604.01.02-739070), and all procedures were carried out in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. In addition, the necessary institutional permissions were secured, and formal consent was obtained from the original developers of the PDQoL-7 questionnaire for its translation and adaptation into Turkish.

Data collection

All assessments were conducted via face-to-face interviews. Demographic and disease-related data, including age, height, weight, gender, marital status, education level, dominant hand, assistive device use, and disease duration, were recorded. The BMI was calculated as weight (kg) /height2 (m²) [26]. Clinical evaluation and disease staging were performed using the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) [27] and the Modified Hoehn and Yahr Scale (H&Y) [28]. To assess the psychometric properties of the PDQoL-7, patients were administered the PDQoL-7 [25], the PDQ-39 [29], and the SF-36 [11].

The UPDRS is a valid and reliable scale frequently used to assess impairments and disabilities in PD [27]. The scale consists of four subsections with 42 items and assesses patients’ mental status, behavior, and mood (4 items), activities of daily living (13 items), motor function (14 items), and complications of dopaminergic therapy (11 items). It consists of 199 points, including 16 for mental state, 52 for activities of daily living, 108 for motor functioning, and 23 for treatment complications. Higher scores indicate worsening of the patient’s clinical condition. The Turkish version was validated by Akbostancı, Balaban, and Atbaşoğlu [30]. The modified H&Y scale was used to stage PD, with seven stages corresponding to increasing disease progression [28].

The PDQoL-7, developed by Stathis and Papadopoulos in 2022, is a self-reported, disease-specific instrument designed to assess the QoL in individuals with PD [25]. It comprises seven items rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (always), with total scores ranging from 0 to 28. Higher scores reflect a greater negative impact on QoL. The PDQoL-7 questionnaire was also re-administered to the same participants after a two-week interval to evaluate test-retest reliability.

The PDQ-39, developed by Peto et al., is one of the most widely used disease-specific health-related QoL instruments [29]. It includes 39 questions in eight domains: mobility, activities of daily living, emotional well-being, stigma, social support, cognition, communication, and bodily discomfort. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Kayapınar Bilge et al. [31].

The SF-36 consists of 36 items across eight subdomains evaluating both positive and negative aspects of health status [11]. Each subscale is scored between 0 and 100, with higher scores indicating better health. The Turkish adaptation and validation of the SF-36 were conducted by Koçyiğit et al. [32].

Translation and cultural adaptation process

The translation and cultural adaptation of the PDQoL-7 were conducted in accordance with internationally accepted guidelines and COSMIN recommendations. First, the original English version was independently translated into Turkish by four bilingual experts. The four translated versions were compared and synthesized into a single preliminary Turkish version. This version was then reviewed by two linguists to ensure linguistic and conceptual equivalence. Subsequently, the synthesized Turkish version was back-translated into English by two independent bilingual translators who were not involved in the initial translation process and were blinded to the original version. The back-translated versions were compared with the original scale, and a consensus report was generated. This report was further evaluated by the same linguists to ensure consistency and accuracy.

An expert review process was conducted to finalize the pre-final version of the scale, including the instructions, response format, and administration procedures. To assess comprehensibility and cultural relevance, a pilot study (cognitive debriefing) was conducted with 10 individuals diagnosed with PD who were not included in the main study. Participants were asked to evaluate the clarity and understandability of each item. Feedback indicated that the items were generally clear and comprehensible, with no major difficulties reported, including among elderly participants. Minor wording adjustments were made where necessary. Following these procedures, the final Turkish version of the PDQoL-7 was established for full implementation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and STATA 64. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), including the Satorra–Bentler correction, was conducted in STATA 64. Descriptive statistics were calculated for each variable. Continuous variables (such as age, PD diagnosis duration, and education) are presented as means ± standard deviations. Categorical variables (such as gender, education level, and H&Y stage) are reported as frequencies and percentages. Floor and ceiling effects were evaluated by calculating the percentages of participants who obtained the lowest (0) and highest (28) possible total scores. A threshold of 15% was used to indicate significant floor or ceiling effects [33]. In addition, floor and ceiling effects were low, and no marked asymmetry was observed in the distribution of the summed scores. Therefore, the total and subscale scores were treated as approximately continuous in the correlation analyses. Although the PDQoL-7 consists of Likert-type ordinal items, each item had five response categories and showed sufficient variability. In line with common practice in psychometric research, the items were treated as approximately continuous for the purposes of inter-item correlation analysis and confirmatory factor analysis.

To assess construct validity, inter-item correlations were examined using Pearson’s correlation coefficients; values between 0.55 and 0.85 were considered indicative of adequate item homogeneity. To evaluate the factor structure of the PDQol-7. The CFA was conducted using the maximum likelihood estimation method with Satorra–Bentler corrections. Sample size planning was addressed at the study design stage. The final sample of 129 participants exceeded the protocol-based minimum target of 5–10 participants per item and also met COSMIN recommendations for factor analysis in PROM studies. Model fit was assessed using the following indices: Satorra–Bentler χ²,Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR).

The following cut-off criteria were employed to evaluate model fit: CFI and TLI values greater than 0.95,RMSEA values less than 0.08, and SRMR values less than 0.05 [34]. Concurrent validity was examined by calculating Pearson’s correlation coefficients between the total PDQoL-7 score and the scores of related measures, including the total PDQ-39 score and the PDQ-39 subscale scores, as well as the SF-36 subscale scores. The strength of correlations was interpreted as follows: 0.80–1.00, very strong; 0.60–0.80, strong; 0.40–0.60, moderate; 0.20–0.40, weak; and < 0.20, negligible [35].

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the total scale, and Cronbach’s alpha if an item was deleted was also examined. A Cronbach’s alpha value ≥ 0.70 was considered acceptable, and ≥ 0.90 was considered indicative of excellent internal consistency [36]. Test–retest reliability was assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), calculated based on a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement, for both the total score and individual items. ICC values were interpreted as follows: < 0.5 = poor, 0.5–0.75 = moderate, 0.75–0.90 = good, and > 0.90 = excellent reliability. A 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated for all ICC estimates [37]. In addition, the standard error of measurement (SEM) and the minimal detectable change at the 95% confidence level (MDC95) were calculated to provide information on measurement error and clinical interpretability.SEM was calculated as SD × √(1 − ICC), and MDC95 was calculated as SEM × 1.96 × √2. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical data

Demographic characteristics and clinical data of the patients were analyzed. A total of 129 individuals diagnosed with PD were included in the study. The mean age of PD patients was 72.93 ± 6.88 years (min: 65, max: 90). 61.2% of patients were male, and 85.3% had a disease staging of HYE 3 or lower. Floor and ceiling effects were evaluated based on total scale scores. In the initial assessment, floor effects (score = 0) were observed in 3.1% of participants, while ceiling effects (score = 28) were observed in 0.8%. In the retest assessment, floor effects were observed in 1.6% of participants, and ceiling effects in 3.1%. As all values were below the 15% threshold, no significant floor or ceiling effects were identified. The results regarding other data were analyzed in detail in Table 1.

Validity

Construct validity

The construct validity of the PDQoL-7 was evaluated through inter-item correlation analysis. The correlation coefficients between items ranged from 0.549 (Item 1–7) to 0.724 (Item 5–6), which falls within the accepted range of 0.55 to 0.85. These results indicate sufficient item homogeneity and support the unidimensional structure of the scale, suggesting that all items reflect the same underlying construct-QoL in PD (Table 2).

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA)

To determine the theoretical structure of the questionnaire and its suitability for the sample. The CFA was conducted. The factor loadings ranged from 0.76 to 0.90 and were statistically significant (p-values < 0.001). The result supports a one-factor structure. As indicated by various fit indices, the model demonstrated an acceptable to excellent fit with the data. The Satorra–Bentler scaled chi-square value was 20.47 with 14 degrees of freedom (p = 0.116), indicating good model fit. Furthermore, the CFI and TLI were 0.984 and 0.977, respectively, indicating excellent fit (both > 0.97). The RMSEA was 0.060, falling within the acceptable range of 0.05–0.08. The SRMR was 0.034, reflecting a very good fit (SRMR < 0.05). Overall, the model fit indices support the unidimensionality of the PDQoL-7 scale and confirm that the scale adequately represents the latent construct it is intended to measure (Table 3).

Reliability

Internal consistency (Cronbach’s Alpha)

The Cronbach’s alpha value of the Turkish version of the PDQoL-7 scale is 0.928, indicating excellent internal consistency. When each item of the PDQoL-7 was removed individually, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient values ranged from 0.913 to 0.921. These findings suggest that all items contribute positively to the questionnaire’s internal consistency (Table 2).

Test–retest reliability (Intraclass Correlation Coefficient – ICC)

Test–retest reliability of the PDQoL-7 was evaluated using the ICC for both the total score and each individual item under clinically stable conditions, with participants stable for at least 3 months prior to reassessment. The total score yielded an ICC of 0.970 (95% CI: 0.957–0.979, p < 0.001), indicating excellent temporal stability of the scale. Item-level ICC values ranged from 0.863 to 0.947. According to widely accepted thresholds, three items demonstrated excellent reliability (ICC > 0.90), while four items (Items 3, 5, 6, and 7) exhibited good reliability (ICC between 0.75 and 0.90). All ICCs were statistically significant (p < 0.001), and the confidence intervals were narrow, reflecting high precision in the estimates. For the total PDQoL-7 score, the SEM was 1.33, and the MDC95 was 3.69. These findings indicate that a change of approximately 3.7 points or more may be considered a real change beyond measurement error at the individual level. Overall, these results indicate that the Turkish version of the PDQoL-7 demonstrates strong test-retest reliability at both the total score and item levels, while the SEM and MDC95 values provide additional evidence of measurement error and clinical interpretability (Table 5).

Discussion

The use of culturally adapted and linguistically validated self-reported assessment tools enhances the comparability of international research and facilitates the collection of reliable data across diverse populations [38]. In this study, the Turkish version of the PDQoL-7 demonstrated excellent psychometric properties, including high internal consistency, excellent test–retest reliability, and strong evidence of construct and concurrent validity in an older adult population with PD.

To assess the internal consistency of the Turkish version of the PDQoL-7, item-total correlation coefficients ranged from 0.768 to 0.839. In contrast, the item-total correlations in the original version ranged from 0.29 to 0.63 [25]. These findings suggest that the Turkish version shows higher internal consistency. Additionally, no significant changes in the scale’s overall reliability were observed upon removal of individual items, supporting the retention of all items in the Turkish version. To date, apart from the original version, no other studies have examined the validity and reliability of the PDQoL-7 in different languages or cultural contexts. This study, therefore, represents the first cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric evaluation of the scale in a non-English-speaking population. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient obtained in this study was 0.928, indicating excellent internal consistency. However, such a high alpha value in a brief scale should be interpreted with some caution, as a degree of item overlap cannot be entirely excluded. At the same time, the content of the PDQoL-7 items covers clinically related but distinct domains, suggesting that the high alpha is more likely to reflect the close coherence of a brief unidimensional instrument than clear redundancy among items.

In comparison, the original version reported a total Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.804 [25]. According to Bland and Altman (1997), a Cronbach’s alpha above 0.70 is generally considered to be indicative of acceptable internal consistency [36]. As in the original version, the Turkish PDQoL-7 demonstrates robust internal consistency.

It is essential for a scale to demonstrate both validity and reliability. Accordingly, the test–retest reliability of the Turkish version of the PDQoL-7 was assessed using ICCs for the total score and individual items. Since test–retest reliability was not examined in the original PDQoL-7, direct comparisons with other language versions were not possible. In the present study, the ICC for the total score was calculated as 0.970, indicating excellent reliability. Item-level ICCs ranged from 0.863 to 0.947, reflecting good to excellent reliability. These findings suggest that the Turkish version of the PDQoL-7 has a very high temporal stability [37]. Nevertheless, the very high reliability observed in this study suggests that the Turkish PDQoL-7 provides consistent and stable measurements over time, which is especially valuable in geriatric populations where repeated assessments are common. In addition, the SEM and MDC95 values further support the scale’s clinical interpretability by indicating the magnitude of change required to exceed measurement error at the individual level.

The convergent validity findings also support the psychometric soundness of the scale. As expected, the PDQoL-7 showed a strong positive correlation with the PDQ-39 (r = 0.916) and a strong negative correlation with the general health subscale of the SF-36 (r = − 0.809). These results indicate that higher PDQoL-7 scores are associated with poorer perceived health and support the scale’s ability to reflect disease-related quality-of-life impairment. Although the strong correlation with the PDQ-39 supports convergent validity, it may also reflect substantial overlap between the two instruments. Clinically, the main value of the PDQoL-7 lies in its brevity and ease of administration. It may save time and reduce respondent burden, particularly in older adults during routine outpatient follow-up or in settings where longer questionnaires are less feasible. In this context, the PDQoL-7 may be preferred when a brief disease-specific quality-of-life assessment is needed, while longer instruments may remain useful for more comprehensive evaluations. Similar findings from previous studies further support the scale’s convergent validity [25].

The findings of the present study regarding the reliability and validity of the Turkish version of the PDQoL-7 are largely consistent with previous literature and affirm its utility as a robust measure of quality of life in individuals with PD. The CFA revealed a unidimensional structure, with all items loading on a single latent construct. Model fit indices (CFI = 0.984, TLI = 0.977, RMSEA = 0.060, SRMR = 0.034) indicated good to excellent model fit, supporting the theoretical framework proposed in the original version [25].

This study offers several notable strengths. It provides a comprehensive psychometric evaluation of the Turkish version of the PDQoL-7, including assessments of internal consistency, test–retest reliability, construct validity through confirmatory factor analysis, and concurrent validity using established instruments. The inclusion of an exclusively older adult sample enhances the relevance of the findings to geriatric clinical practice. Furthermore, the absence of floor and ceiling effects suggests that the scale can capture a wide range of quality-of-life experiences among individuals with PD. Importantly, this study represents the first cross-cultural adaptation and validation of the PDQoL-7 in a non-English-speaking population.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. Most participants were in Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) stage 3 or below, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to individuals with more advanced PD. In addition, the cross-sectional design precluded evaluation of the PDQoL-7’s responsiveness to changes in clinical status over time. The study was conducted in a single center, and the sample may not fully reflect the cultural and clinical diversity of the broader PD population. Furthermore, cognitive status was assessed clinically rather than with a standardized objective measure, which may limit the precision of cognitive evaluation. Additionally, as with all self-reported measures, the results may be influenced by social desirability bias. Future longitudinal and multicenter studies including patients across different disease stages and more diverse populations are needed to further evaluate the responsiveness and generalizability of the PDQoL-7.

Conclusion

The Turkish version of the PDQoL-7 demonstrated excellent psychometric properties, including high internal consistency, strong test–retest reliability, and robust construct and concurrent validity. A unidimensional structure was supported by inter-item correlations and confirmatory factor analysis, with model fit indices indicating good overall fit. Strong positive correlations with the PDQ-39 and significant negative correlations with relevant SF-36 subdomains further confirmed the scale’s ability to capture disease-specific aspects of quality of life in individuals with PD.

Given its brevity, clarity, and ease of administration, the PDQoL-7 appears to be a practical and informative instrument for assessing QoL in older adults with PD. Its main clinical value lies in providing a brief and feasible assessment with lower respondent burden, particularly in geriatric settings, while its strong psychometric properties support its use in routine practice and research among Turkish-speaking populations.
