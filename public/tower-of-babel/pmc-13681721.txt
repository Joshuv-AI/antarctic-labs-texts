Pneumorrhachis is a rare radiological finding characterized by the presence of air within the spinal canal, in either the epidural or intradural spaces. The majority of reported cases occur in adults and result from traumatic or iatrogenic mechanisms.1 Common causes include blunt or penetrating trauma, spinal or neuraxial procedures such as epidural anesthesia or lumbar puncture, and postoperative complications.1 In contrast, spontaneous pneumorrhachis occurs in the absence of trauma or procedural intervention and is rare, especially in pediatric and adolescent populations. Spontaneous cases are typically associated with conditions or maneuvers that acutely increase intrathoracic pressure, such as forceful vomiting, intense coughing, asthma exacerbations, Valsalva maneuvers, strenuous exercise, or drug use.2–4 Elevated intrathoracic pressure can lead to alveolar rupture with air dissecting into the mediastinum, subsequently tracking through cervical fascial planes and neural foramina into the posterior epidural space, which offers the “path of least resistance.”5–9

Pneumorrhachis is typically discovered incidentally on CT imaging performed for suspected pneumomediastinum or subcutaneous emphysema.10 Pneumorrhachis is frequently asymptomatic; however, neurological symptoms such as pain, paresthesia, or motor weakness are rare and typically occur in the context of traumatic or iatrogenic etiologies.10–12 Management involves treating the underlying cause such as perforated viscus, infection, or barotrauma,10 and for neurologically asymptomatic patients, conservative management and supportive care typically result in spontaneous resolution on serial imaging in the majority of patients.1 However, the clinical course remains anecdotal, and no standardized management guidelines exist.

Here, we describe a case of spontaneous pneumorrhachis spanning the cervical/thoracic spine with extensive pneumomediastinum and subcutaneous emphysema in a previously healthy adolescent female who presented with intractable vomiting secondary to streptococcal pharyngitis. In addition, we present a systematic review of the literature on spontaneous pneumorrhachis in the adolescent, describe common clinical and radiographic features, explore potential risk factors, and propose a clinical management algorithm for this patient population.

Illustrative Case

A 16-year-old, otherwise healthy female presented to the emergency department with 1 day of bilateral facial swelling and 3 days of intractable vomiting and fever secondary to strep pharyngitis, for which she was being treated with oral amoxicillin. The patient’s vomiting episodes were forceful and repetitive, consistent with a Valsalva-type mechanism contributing to elevated intrathoracic pressure. Subjectively, both the patient and her mother denied any evidence of neurological complaints. Physical examination revealed notable crepitus of the bilateral neck and face, and neurological examination revealed no focal deficits. On presentation, vital signs were within normal limits, including a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, heart rate of 82 beats per minute, and oxygen saturation of 100% on room air. Laboratory studies were notable for leukocytosis (white blood cell count 21.7 × 10³/µL), reflecting her underlying infection, and mild electrolyte abnormalities consistent with recent vomiting, including hypokalemia (3.4 mmol/L) and hypochloremia (96 mmol/L). Hemoglobin and hematocrit were elevated (17.0 g/dL and 49.4%, respectively), likely due to dehydration in the setting of repeated emesis.

Chest radiographic imaging demonstrated significant pneumomediastinum continuing to extensive subcutaneous emphysema of the anterior chest wall extending superiorly into the supraclavicular regions, neck, and shoulders bilaterally (Fig. 1). CT with contrast was performed to rule out Boerhaave syndrome. No esophageal tear was identified; however, CT demonstrated pneumomediastinum, subcutaneous air, and the presence of air within the posterior spinal epidural space spanning from C7 through T8 and with extension to the extraforaminal space at several levels (Fig. 2). MRI was not obtained given the absence of neurological deficits.

Conservative management with fluid resuscitation and antiemetics was pursued for symptom management, and observation of the pneumorrhachis given no neurological deficits. On hospital day 2, a chest radiograph was obtained and demonstrated improvement of the pneumomediastinum and subcutaneous emphysema. After progressive clinical improvement, the patient was discharged on hospital day 4. Advanced imaging was planned for the 6-week follow-up; however, the patient was lost to follow-up despite multiple attempts to contact her by both medicine and neurosurgical services.

Discussion

Observations

Spontaneous pneumorrhachis occurs without any direct injury or procedural trigger and is rare, especially in the adolescent age group. It has been associated with conditions that elevate intrathoracic pressure, such as asthma exacerbations,13,14 respiratory tract infections,13,15–19 forceful vomiting,13,20–23 and strenuous exercise,24,25 or, in some cases, has no clearly identifiable cause.4,20,26,27 Although spontaneous pneumorrhachis is most often benign and self-limited, its radiological appearance frequently prompts consultation with the neurosurgery team due to concern for potential spinal cord compression, infection, or other structural pathology. However, no formal guidelines exist for the management of pneumorrhachis, and decision-making is largely based on clinical judgment and radiological interpretation.

We conducted a literature review using the PubMed and Scopus databases, focusing on adolescents aged 10–19 years, in accordance with PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines (Fig. 3). We used the search terms (“pneumorrhachis” OR “pneumatorrhachis” OR “spinal canal pneumatosis” OR “epidural pneumatosis” OR “intradural pneumatosis”) AND (“pediatric” OR “child” OR “children” OR “adolescent” OR “teenager”). From 85 unique articles identified, we excluded those with traumatic or iatrogenic causes, non–English-language publications, and papers that did not describe new cases. Of the remaining 46 articles describing spontaneous pneumorrhachis, 34 articles included patients between 10 and 19 years of age, accounting for a total of 38 adolescents (Table 1).4,13–45 Study selection and data extraction were reviewed by two authors, with high agreement (94%), and discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

We explored common predisposing features present across adolescents with spontaneous pneumorrhachis. Of the 38 adolescent cases, 34 (89.5%) were male and 4 (10.5%) were female, suggesting a strong male predominance, with a mean age of 16 years. The male predominance observed may reflect behavioral and physiological factors. Adolescent males may be more likely to engage in activities associated with sudden increases in intrathoracic pressure, such as strenuous exertion or inhalational drug use. Additionally, differences in respiratory mechanics or force generation during coughing or vomiting may contribute, although the underlying drivers remain unclear. Most patients were previously healthy, although several had comorbidities such as asthma,13,14,20,22,28,31,32,36,37,40 type 1 diabetes,26 or a history of vaping or cannabis use.21,23,44 Precipitating factors were identified in most cases, with the most frequent triggers being forceful vomiting, upper respiratory infections including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) or respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and asthma exacerbation or coughing fits (Table 1). A few cases were due to strenuous exertion or exercise.24,25,33,41 A small subset of cases were due to inhaled recreational substances, including ecstasy, tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), ketamine, and nitrous oxide.22,23,44

Various symptoms and physical examination findings were frequently encountered in adolescents with spontaneous pneumorrhachis. Dyspnea and shortness of breath were the most common physical examination findings (17 cases, 45%); however, chest pain (13 cases, 34%), neck or facial swelling (8 cases, 21%), and crepitus or subcutaneous emphysema (15 cases, 39%) were also commonly observed (Table 1). Crepitus and/or subcutaneous emphysema were key clinical findings that prompted further imaging. Despite the presence of air in the spinal canal, the majority of cases were clinically benign. Only 1 case reported neurological symptoms, which included lower extremity neuropathic pain and hyperreflexia.43

Common radiographic features are present within adolescents who develop spontaneous pneumorrhachis. Mediastinal involvement was present in all cases and frequently accompanied by subcutaneous emphysema. The posterior epidural space was involved in most cases. Among cases in which spinal levels were specified, epidural air most commonly accumulated in the cervical and upper thoracic regions, with only a few cases identifying air in the lumbar region.13,45 In many cases, including our patient, air traversed through the neural foramina, consistent with the pathophysiological cascade described by the Macklin effect. The Macklin effect involves alveolar rupture secondary to increased intrathoracic pressure, with air dissecting along the bronchovascular sheaths into the mediastinum.5,6 From there, air may follow fascial planes in the neck and enter the epidural space through the neural foramina,7,8 particularly favoring the posterior spinal canal, which offers less anatomical resistance than the anterior compartment due to its looser areolar tissue and lack of dense vascular structures.9

Management was conservative in 37 of 38 cases (97%), consisting of oxygen therapy, bronchodilators, analgesics, and observation. One patient who presented with an acute exacerbation of ulcerative colitis and was found to have pneumorrhachis on preoperative imaging underwent a colectomy.39 No adolescents underwent surgical decompression or laminectomy. Whereas most adolescent cases of spontaneous pneumorrhachis followed a benign clinical course, 2 patients experienced fatal outcomes. Showkat et al. reported on a patient with a history of B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) who developed respiratory arrest resulting in death.43 Sriganesh et al. described a patient with underlying neurological pathology awaiting endovascular intervention who developed acute respiratory distress syndrome, leading to rapid deterioration and death.18 In both cases, death was attributed to complications of underlying disease rather than to pneumorrhachis. The remaining patients (36 cases, 95%) recovered fully without neurological sequelae after conservative management. While only 8 studies reported hospital length of stay, the duration ranged from 2 to 9 days. Length of stay appeared to correlate with underlying etiology and comorbidities, with most patients discharged within 5 days.

Few studies reported follow-up intervals after discharge or specified whether repeat imaging was obtained. One study described ­follow-up at 6 months, which demonstrated no abnormalities.19 For the other studies that disclosed interval imaging, follow-up imaging was typically performed within 1–2 months and consistently demonstrated complete radiographic resolution in clinically stable patients. Reported modalities included CT at 2 months and chest radiography or ultrasound at 1 month.24,28,36,39

Based on the findings from our systematic review, we propose a clinical framework to guide the evaluation and management of spontaneous pneumorrhachis in the adolescent population (Fig. 4). In adolescents presenting with neck or chest crepitus, swelling, or respiratory symptoms following forceful vomiting, coughing, or infection, initial imaging with chest radiography and CT should be obtained to assess for pneumomediastinum and pneumorrhachis. If air is identified in the spinal canal, a thorough neurological examination is indicated and neurosurgical consultation is warranted. In patients with focal neurological deficits such as motor weakness, paresthesia, or hyperreflexia, MRI should be obtained to evaluate for cord compression, infection, or other contributory structural pathology. In neurologically intact patients, particularly when air is localized to the posterior epidural space, conservative management is appropriate. Supportive care with supplemental oxygen, analgesics, antiemetics, and close observation is typically sufficient. Patients should be monitored for red-flag symptoms including worsening neck or facial swelling, progressive crepitus, or the emergence of any neurological signs. Repeat imaging should be considered in cases of clinical worsening or atypical radiographic features. Concerning new radiographic features such as anterior epidural air or extensive foraminal tracking should prompt neurosurgical reevaluation. Patients who remain clinically stable may be discharged on symptom improvement, with outpatient MRI and neurosurgical follow-up in 4–6 weeks to confirm pneumorrhachis resolution, consistent with the timelines reported in the literature.

Proposed clinical management algorithm for spontaneous pneumorrhachis in adolescents.

While spontaneous pneumorrhachis generally follows a benign course, its alarming radiographic appearance and rare but serious complications underscore the need for a systematic approach guided by symptomology and imaging in emergency settings. Although developed for adolescent cases, our pneumorrhachis management framework may extend to adult patients, in whom spontaneous pneumorrhachis similarly demonstrates a benign natural history with favorable outcomes under conservative treatment.1

Lessons

Spontaneous pneumorrhachis is a rare but increasingly recognized entity, particularly in adolescents presenting with forceful vomiting, respiratory infections, or other causes of elevated intrathoracic pressure. Although its radiographic appearance can raise significant concern, the condition is typically benign and self-limiting, with conservative management proving effective in nearly all reported cases. However, vigilance should remain high as rare cases have resulted in neurological compromise or death, underscoring the need for careful clinical assessment. Our systematic review and resulting management framework offer a structured, stepwise approach to evaluation and management. Clinical features such as crepitus, neck/facial swelling, subcutaneous emphysema, or chest symptoms should raise early suspicion and prompt appropriate imaging to identify air in the mediastinum and/or epidural space. Patients without neurological deficits can be safely managed expectantly. Those with neurological deficits should receive urgent MRI and neurosurgical evaluation to rule out cord compression, infection, or other complications. This case adds to the limited body of literature on adolescent spontaneous pneumorrhachis and proposes guidelines to aid in triage and management, emphasizing the role of symptomatology, air distribution, and multidisciplinary evaluation in guiding decision-making.
