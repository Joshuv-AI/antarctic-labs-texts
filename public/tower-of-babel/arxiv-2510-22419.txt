Beyond Isotonization: Scalable Non-Crossing Quantile Estimation via Neural Networks for Student Growth Percentiles
Kaihua Chang

Abstract
Student Growth Percentiles (SGPs), widely adopted across U.S. state assessment systems, employ independent quantile regression followed by post-hoc correction using an isotonic projection method ( isotonize=TRUE in the SGP R package) to address quantile crossing. We demonstrate this approach contains a fundamental methodological inconsistency: interpolation between independently-estimated, potentially crossed quantiles requires monotonicity, yet the post-hoc correction alters estimates in ways that may violate the quantile property P ⁡ ( Y ≤ Q ^ τ ​ ( Y | X ) ∣ X ) = τ P(Y\leq\hat{Q}_{\tau}(Y|X)\mid X)=\tau . We term this the interpolation paradox . While theoretically sound constrained joint quantile regression (CJQR) eliminates crossing by enforcing non-crossing constraints during optimization, we analyze its computational complexity (often scaling poorly, e.g., 𝒪 ⁡ ( ( q ​ n ) 3 ) \mathcal{O}((qn)^{3}) for standard LP solvers) rendering it intractable for large-scale educational data ( n > 100,000 n>100{,}000 ). We examine the SGP package’s switch to the Frisch-Newton interior point method ( rq.method.for.large.n="fn" ) for large N N , noting that while efficient for independent QR, it doesn’t resolve the joint problem’s complexity or the paradox. We propose neural network-based multi-quantile regression (NNQR) with shared hidden layers as a practical alternative. Leveraging the convexity of the composite pinball loss, SGD-based optimization used in NN training can reliably approach the global optimum, offering scalability ( O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) ) and implicitly reducing crossing. Our empirical analysis shows independent QR yields crossing, while both CJQR and NNQR enforce monotonicity. NNQR emerges as a viable, scalable alternative for operational SGP systems, aligning theoretical validity with computational feasibility.



1
Introduction

Student Growth Percentiles (SGPs), introduced by
Betebenner 2009
, have become a predominant methodology for quantifying academic progress in U.S. educational accountability systems. The approach estimates conditional quantiles of current achievement given prior achievement histories, enabling normative comparisons of student growth trajectories relative to academic peers. Central to the SGP methodology is quantile regression (QR)
(
Koenker and Bassett Jr 1978
;
Koenker and Hallock 2001
)
, chosen for its scale-transformation invariance and ability to characterize conditional distributions without restrictive parametric assumptions.

However, standard SGP implementations face a well-documented challenge endemic to independent estimation of multiple quantiles: quantile crossing
(
Koenker 2005
;
Dette and Volgushev 2008
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2010
)
. When QR models are fitted independently for each percentile
\tau\in\{0.01,0.02,\ldots,0.99\}
, nothing guarantees the fundamental monotonicity requirement—that
\hat{Q}_{\tau_{1}}(y|x)\leq\hat{Q}_{\tau_{2}}(y|x)
must hold for all
x
whenever
\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}
. Crossed quantiles (
\hat{Q}_{\tau_{1}}>\hat{Q}_{\tau_{2}}
) produce logically inconsistent and mathematically invalid conditional distribution functions, yet the core SGP calculation involves interpolating between these potentially crossed estimates to assign a student’s final percentile rank.

The widely used
SGP
R package
(
Betebenner et al. 2024
)
addresses crossing pragmatically through its
isotonize=TRUE
parameter (defaulting to true). This triggers a post-hoc correction based on the methods of
Dette and Volgushev 2008
, described in the documentation as involving “simultaneous inversion and isotonization.” This is essentially an isotonic projection or rearrangement algorithm that modifies the initial
\hat{Q}_{\tau}
estimates to enforce monotonicity. While computationally necessary for interpolation, this correction masks rather than resolves the underlying structural problem. As demonstrated theoretically by
Bondell et al. 2010
regarding similar post-hoc adjustments, such procedures typically violate the defining quantile property (
P(Y\leq\hat{Q}_{\tau}(Y|X)\mid X)=\tau
) derived from the pinball loss minimization. We term the SGP workflow—independent estimation allowing crossing, followed by interpolation that requires non-crossing (achieved via a correction that potentially invalidates the estimates’ quantile interpretation)—the
interpolation paradox
.

Theoretically rigorous alternatives exist. Constrained joint quantile regression (CJQR) formulates the estimation of all desired quantiles as a single, large optimization problem with explicit non-crossing inequality constraints incorporated directly
(
Bondell et al. 2010
;
Liu and Wu 2009
)
. However, as we demonstrate through complexity analysis, formulating CJQR as a linear program (LP) results in computational requirements (e.g.,
O((qn)^{3})
or worse for standard solvers) that scale intractably with sample size (
n
) and the number of quantiles (
q
), rendering it infeasible for typical state assessment systems involving millions of student records.

Even the independent QR estimation within the
SGP
package reveals scalability concerns. The code switches the underlying
quantreg::rq
solver method from the default Barrodale-Roberts simplex algorithm (
rq.method="br"
) to the Frisch-Newton interior point method (
rq.method.for.large.n="fn"
) when the sample size
n
exceeds 300,000. While both
"br"
and
"fn"
are designed to find the
global optimum
for the
convex
single-quantile problem, the switch highlights the computational strain even before considering joint constraints, motivating the search for more scalable approaches
(
Portnoy and Koenker 1997
)
.

Recent advances suggest a path forward through modern machine learning techniques. Multi-output neural network architectures trained on a composite quantile loss function offer a practical approach. By utilizing shared hidden layers, these models implicitly encourage monotonicity through representation learning and parameter tying, effectively reducing quantile crossing while maintaining linear scalability (
O(n)
)
(
Cannon 2018
)
. Furthermore, the optimization landscape of the composite pinball loss allows gradient-based methods like Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), commonly used for training neural networks, to effectively approximate the global optimum.

This paper aims to:

1.

Formally introduce the mathematical programming framework for single and constrained joint multi-quantile regression.

2.

Critically analyze the methodological inconsistency inherent in the SGP approach of independent estimation plus post-hoc isotonic projection, considering the specific implementation choices within the SGP package.

3.

Discuss the optimization geometry of quantile regression and why SGD-based methods offer a viable path to globally optimal solutions for large datasets.

4.

Conduct an empirical comparison using a sample dataset, evaluating:

•

Independent QR (representing the pre-correction SGP estimates).

•

Constrained Joint QR implemented via linear programming.

•

A Neural Network QR approach.

5.

Discuss the implications for SGP methodology and suggest directions for robust conditional quantile estimation.

2
Mathematical Framework and the Interpolation Paradox

2.1
Quantile Regression: The Forward Problem

Let
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
denote paired observations where
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
represents prior achievement covariates and
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
current achievement for student
i
. The
\tau
-th conditional quantile function
Q_{Y}(\tau|X)
satisfies

P(Y\leq Q_{Y}(\tau|X)|X)=\tau\quad\forall\tau\in(0,1).

(1)

Under a linear specification,
Q_{Y}(\tau|X)=X^{\top}\beta(\tau)
, where
\beta(\tau)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the quantile-specific coefficient vector.
Koenker and Bassett Jr 1978
introduced quantile regression as the solution to

\hat{\beta}(\tau)=\arg\min_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{\tau}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)

(2)

where
\rho_{\tau}(u)=u(\tau-\mathbb{I}_{u<0})
is the convex but non-differentiable pinball loss function (
\mathbb{I}
is the indicator function).

This minimization can be reformulated as a Linear Programming (LP) problem:

\min_{\beta,u^{+},u^{-}}\left(\tau\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{+}+(1-\tau)\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{-}\right)

(3)

subject to

\displaystyle Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta

\displaystyle=u_{i}^{+}-u_{i}^{-}\quad\forall i

(4)

\displaystyle u_{i}^{+},u_{i}^{-}

\displaystyle\geq 0\quad\forall i

(5)

where
u_{i}^{+}
and
u_{i}^{-}
represent positive and negative deviations respectively. This LP formulation transforms the non-smooth optimization into a problem solvable by efficient algorithms like the simplex method or interior point methods
(
Koenker 2005
;
Portnoy and Koenker 1997
)
.

2.2
Independent Quantile Regression: The Standard SGP Approach

The SGP methodology requires estimation of
q=99
quantiles corresponding to percentiles
\tau_{j}\in\{0.01,0.02,\ldots,0.99\}
. In the standard implementation
(
Betebenner et al. 2024
)
, each quantile
\tau_{j}
is estimated independently:

\hat{\beta}(\tau_{j})=\arg\min_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{\tau_{j}}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)\quad j=1,\ldots,q

(6)

This approach incurs
O(q\cdot C_{single})
computational cost, where
C_{single}
is the complexity of single-quantile estimation. For typical quantile regression solvers:

•

Simplex-based methods (e.g., Barrodale-Roberts):
C_{single}=O(n^{2}p)
to
O(n^{3})

•

Interior point methods (e.g., Frisch-Newton):
C_{single}=O(n^{1.5}p^{2})
to
O(np^{3})

The SGP package employs adaptive solver selection based on sample size. For
n\leq 300{,}000
, it uses the Barrodale-Roberts simplex algorithm (
rq.method="br"
), switching to the Frisch-Newton interior point method (
rq.method.for.large.n="fn"
) for larger datasets
(
Gondzio 2012
)
.

2.3
The Interpolation Paradox

The crux of the SGP methodology lies in assigning each student’s growth percentile rank through linear interpolation between adjacent quantile estimates. Given a student’s outcome
y^{*}
and covariates
x^{*}
, the SGP computation involves finding consecutive quantiles
\tau_{k},\tau_{k+1}
such that:

\hat{Q}_{\tau_{k}}(y|x^{*})\leq y^{*}\leq\hat{Q}_{\tau_{k+1}}(y|x^{*})

(7)

The final percentile is then computed via linear interpolation between
\tau_{k}
and
\tau_{k+1}
.

This procedure fundamentally requires monotonicity:
\hat{Q}_{\tau_{1}}(y|x)\leq\hat{Q}_{\tau_{2}}(y|x)
whenever
\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}
. However, independent estimation provides no such guarantee. When quantile crossing occurs (
\hat{Q}_{\tau_{k}}(y|x^{*})>\hat{Q}_{\tau_{k+1}}(y|x^{*})
), the interpolation becomes mathematically undefined.

The SGP package addresses this through its
isotonize=TRUE
parameter, implementing the isotonic projection method of
Dette and Volgushev 2008
. This post-hoc correction modifies the crossed quantile estimates to enforce monotonicity. However, this creates what we term the
interpolation paradox
:

1.

The independently estimated
\hat{Q}_{\tau_{j}}
minimize the pinball loss and satisfy the quantile property
P(Y\leq\hat{Q}_{\tau_{j}}(Y|X)|X)=\tau_{j}
asymptotically.

2.

The isotonic projection modifies these estimates to enforce monotonicity, producing adjusted estimates
\tilde{Q}_{\tau_{j}}
.

3.

The adjusted estimates
\tilde{Q}_{\tau_{j}}
generally no longer minimize the pinball loss and may violate the defining quantile property
(
Neocleous and Portnoy 2008
)
.

4.

The SGP calculation proceeds using the adjusted estimates, interpolating between values that may no longer represent valid conditional quantiles.

This methodological inconsistency undermines the theoretical foundation of the SGP approach. While pragmatically necessary for computation, the isotonic correction transforms statistically principled quantile estimates into values that merely preserve ordering without maintaining their probabilistic interpretation.

2.4
Constrained Joint Quantile Regression (CJQR)

A theoretically rigorous solution formulates the estimation of all
q
quantiles as a single optimization problem with explicit non-crossing constraints:

\min_{\{\beta(\tau_{j})\}_{j=1}^{q}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{\tau_{j}}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta(\tau_{j}))

(8)

subject to

X_{i}^{\top}\beta(\tau_{j})\leq X_{i}^{\top}\beta(\tau_{j+1})\quad\forall i=1,\ldots,n;\quad j=1,\ldots,q-1

(9)

This constrained joint quantile regression (CJQR) guarantees non-crossing by construction
(
Bondell et al. 2010
;
Liu and Wu 2009
)
. The resulting LP formulation involves:

•

Variables:
qp
coefficients plus
2nq
auxiliary variables

•

Constraints:
nq
equality constraints plus
n(q-1)
non-crossing inequalities

The computational complexity becomes prohibitive for large-scale problems. Standard interior point methods scale as
O((qn)^{3})
in the worst case, making CJQR computationally intractable for typical SGP applications with
n>100{,}000
students and
q=99
quantiles.

3
Optimization Landscape and Neural Network Solutions

3.1
Convexity of the Composite Pinball Loss

The composite pinball loss for multiple quantiles:

L_{composite}(\beta)=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{\tau_{j}}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{j})

(10)

inherits convexity from the individual pinball losses. This convexity ensures that any local minimum is also a global minimum, providing theoretical justification for gradient-based optimization methods
(
Koenker 2005
)
.

However, the joint optimization presents significant challenges distinct from single-quantile estimation. The loss landscape, while convex, exhibits a complex ”bowl-like” structure formed by the summation of multiple V-shaped pinball losses. This geometry creates several practical difficulties:

1.

Flat regions
: The non-differentiability at zero for each component creates extensive flat regions where gradients provide limited directional information.

2.

Sharp valleys
: The intersection of multiple pinball losses creates narrow valleys that require careful navigation to avoid overshooting.

3.

Ill-conditioning
: The condition number of the Hessian approximation can be extremely large, particularly when quantiles are closely spaced.

3.2
Implementation Challenges for Joint Optimization

The practical implementation of neural network quantile regression requires careful consideration of several factors that distinguish it from standard regression tasks:

3.2.1
Optimization Strategy

While mini-batch SGD is standard for neural network training, joint quantile regression benefits significantly from full-batch optimization when computationally feasible. The author’s successful implementation utilized:

•

Full-batch training
: Processing all samples simultaneously provides more stable gradient estimates, crucial for navigating the non-smooth loss landscape.

•

L-BFGS optimizer
: This quasi-Newton method, closer to traditional statistical optimization in R’s
quantreg
, leverages second-order information to handle the ill-conditioned optimization landscape more effectively than first-order methods.

3.2.2
Learning Rate Scheduling

The complex loss landscape necessitates sophisticated learning rate strategies:

•

OneCycle scheduling
: Gradually increasing then decreasing the learning rate helps escape shallow local minima while ensuring convergence.

•

Warmup periods
: Starting with very small learning rates prevents early instability when gradients may be poorly estimated.

•

Adaptive decay
: Monitoring loss plateau patterns to trigger learning rate reductions.

3.2.3
Training Duration and Early Stopping

Joint quantile regression typically requires substantially longer training than standard regression:

•

Extended epochs
: Convergence often requires 10,000+ epochs, far exceeding typical neural network training.

•

Early stopping criteria
: Balancing computational cost with convergence quality requires sophisticated stopping rules based on:

–

Loss plateau detection over extended windows (e.g., 500+ epochs)

–

Monitoring quantile crossing frequency

–

Validation set performance on held-out data

•

Patience parameters
: Allowing temporary loss increases to escape local optima.

3.3
Neural Network Quantile Regression (NNQR)

Multi-output neural networks provide a scalable approach to joint quantile estimation. The architecture comprises:

•

Input layer:
p
features

•

Hidden layers: Shared representation learning

•

Output layer:
q
nodes, one per quantile

The key innovation lies in the shared hidden layers, which implicitly couple the quantile estimates through common feature representations. This architectural choice encourages monotonicity without explicit constraints, as the network learns representations that naturally preserve ordering across quantiles
(
Cannon 2018
;
Taylor 2000
)
.

Training minimizes the composite pinball loss:

L_{NNQR}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{q}\rho_{\tau_{j}}(Y_{i}-f_{\theta,j}(X_{i}))

(11)

where
f_{\theta,j}
denotes the network output for quantile
\tau_{j}
with parameters
\theta
.

5
Discussion and Implications

5.1
Methodological Implications

Our analysis reveals a fundamental tension in current SGP methodology between computational tractability and theoretical validity. The standard approach of independent estimation followed by isotonic correction achieves computational feasibility at the cost of statistical rigor. While the isotonized estimates enable interpolation, they no longer represent valid conditional quantiles in the probabilistic sense defined by the pinball loss minimization.

This interpolation paradox has direct implications for the interpretation of SGP scores. When a student receives an SGP of, say, 65, this value ostensibly means the student performed better than 65% of their academic peers. However, if this score derives from interpolation between isotonically adjusted quantile estimates, its precise probabilistic interpretation becomes unclear. The adjusted quantiles may not satisfy
P(Y\leq\tilde{Q}_{\tau}(Y|X)|X)=\tau
, undermining the normative interpretation central to SGP’s policy applications.

5.2
Practical Implementation Considerations

The successful implementation of NNQR for joint quantile estimation requires careful attention to optimization details that differ substantially from standard neural network practice:

1.

Optimization Algorithm
: L-BFGS or other quasi-Newton methods outperform first-order methods (SGD, Adam) for this problem due to better handling of the ill-conditioned, non-smooth loss landscape.

2.

Batch Size
: Full-batch training provides more stable convergence than mini-batches, though this limits scalability for extremely large datasets.

3.

Training Duration
: Expect 10-100× longer training than standard regression tasks. Budget computational resources accordingly.

4.

Hyperparameter Tuning
: Critical parameters include:

•

Initial learning rate (typically 0.001-0.01)

•

Learning rate schedule (OneCycle or cosine annealing recommended)

•

Early stopping patience (500+ epochs recommended)

•

Network architecture (shallow networks often suffice)

5.3
Practical Recommendations

For operational SGP systems, we recommend:

1.

Transparency
: Acknowledge the limitations of isotonic correction in technical documentation and policy communications.

2.

Validation
: Implement diagnostic checks for quantile crossing frequency and magnitude to assess the severity of the issue in specific contexts.

3.

Alternative Methods
: Consider NNQR as a scalable alternative that maintains better alignment between computational implementation and theoretical foundations, but recognize the implementation complexity.

4.

Hybrid Approaches
: For critical high-stakes decisions, use CJQR on stratified subsamples to validate NNQR or isotonized estimates.

5.4
Future Directions

Several avenues warrant further investigation:

•

Theoretical Analysis
: Characterize the bias introduced by isotonic projection in finite samples and develop correction factors.

•

Smoothing Methods
: Explore smoothed quantile regression approaches that naturally discourage crossing
(
Fernandes et al. 2021
;
He et al. 2023
)
.

•

Optimization Improvements
: Develop specialized optimizers for the joint quantile regression problem that better handle the unique loss landscape.

•

Scale Studies
: Conduct large-scale empirical comparisons using actual state assessment data to quantify the practical impact of different approaches.

6
Conclusion

The widespread adoption of Student Growth Percentiles in educational accountability systems necessitates careful scrutiny of the underlying statistical methodology. Our analysis reveals a fundamental inconsistency—the interpolation paradox—in the standard approach of independent quantile estimation followed by isotonic correction. While this pragmatic solution enables computation at scale, it compromises the theoretical validity of the resulting growth percentile scores.

Constrained joint quantile regression offers theoretical rigor but fails to scale to operational requirements. Neural network quantile regression emerges as a promising middle ground, providing implicit monotonicity enforcement through architectural design while maintaining the linear scalability essential for state-level implementations. However, successful implementation requires careful attention to optimization details, including full-batch training, quasi-Newton optimizers, and extended training periods.

As educational policy increasingly relies on sophisticated statistical models, ensuring alignment between theoretical foundations and computational implementations becomes crucial. The methodological issues identified here extend beyond technical concerns to affect the validity of high-stakes decisions based on SGP scores. Moving forward, the field must balance computational feasibility with statistical rigor to maintain the integrity of educational accountability systems.

The choice is not merely technical but fundamentally about the trade-offs we accept in quantifying student growth. Do we prioritize computational efficiency at the potential cost of statistical validity? Or do we invest in more sophisticated methods that better preserve the probabilistic interpretation essential to normative comparisons? As states continue to refine their accountability systems, these questions demand careful consideration from both statistical and policy perspectives.
