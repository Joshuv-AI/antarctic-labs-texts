Application of project-based and simulation-based approaches in medical curriculum
Authors: E Kldiashvili, S Al-Rustum, M Zarnadze, D Goderdzishvili, M Tvildiani, A Mamiseishvili
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluates the implementation of project-based learning (PrBL) and simulation-based learning (SBL) within the undergraduate medical curriculum to support the development of research and academic writing skills. A total of 247 students participated: 179 in the PrBL component and 68 in the SBL component using the Anatomage table. The study aimed to examine whether these pedagogical approaches could enhance academic performance and promote originality in student work. Students engaged in PrBL demonstrated higher average academic performance scores (82.5) compared to those completing traditional literature review-based assignments (66.5). Similarly, the use of the Anatomage table in SBL activities resulted in higher academic scores (33.0) compared to paper-based case preparation (26.6), and was associated with lower similarity percentages, suggesting improved originality. These findings were observed in two separate semester cohorts and reflect cross-sectional group comparisons rather than longitudinal pre-post outcomes. While the results support the educational value of PrBL and SBL, the study’s design limits causal attribution. Differences in student cohorts, non-randomised group allocation, and potential novelty effects may have influenced performance outcomes. Nonetheless, the findings underscore the potential of research-oriented PrBL and advanced simulation technologies such as the Anatomage table to enhance learning outcomes when carefully integrated into medical training. Future studies employing randomised designs and longitudinal follow-up are warranted to confirm these effects and guide best practice implementation.Keywords: Undergraduate research, Medical education, Research integration, Medical curriculum, Skill development, Project-based learning, Anatomage table

Introduction

Research skills are fundamental in the medical field, as healthcare professionals must be lifelong learners capable of evaluating and analyzing data and understanding the scientific principles underlying medical processes. Whether or not a medical school graduate pursues a research career, they must be able to critically evaluate information and design and execute their own research studies [1]. The World Federation for Medical Education—WFME [2] has established standards to evaluate research competence in medical students, highlighting the importance of research training in medical education. Similarly, the Association for Medical Education in Europe (AMEE) emphasizes the need for active student participation in research activities to enhance their understanding of research methodology. Therefore, medical education programs must incorporate training and activities that foster the development of research and science communication skills among students. Research skills are crucial for medical students, not only for their academic success but also for their future professional practice. Practicing evidence-based medicine (EBM) requires medical professionals to acquire, critically evaluate, and apply the most reliable research evidence in their clinical work [1]. Conducting research projects during medical training equips students with essential skills needed for EBM. The project-based as well as simulation-based learning can facilitate the effective performance of the research activities specific to medical curriculum.

Project-based learning (PrBL) and simulation-based learning (SBL) are two pedagogical approaches that have gained significant traction in medical education due to their ability to foster active learning, collaboration, and the development of higher-order cognitive and clinical skills [3, 4]. PrBL, rooted in constructuvist learning theory, engages students in authentic problem-solving process that mirror real-world professional tasks [5]. Numerous studies have demonstrated its positive impact on students’ knowledge retention, critical thinking, and self-directed learning capabilities [6–10]. Simulation-based learning, on the other hand, emphasizes experiential learning through the use of technology-enhanced environments that replicate clinical scenarios [11–14]. SBL has been shown to improve clinical reasoning, diagnostic accuracy, communication skills, and teamwork in a safe and controlled setting [15–17]. The integration of high-fidelity simulation platforms – such as Anatomage Table, virtual patients, or hybrid simulators – into preclinical and clinical training is increasingly seen as essential for patient safety and effective skills transfer [18, 19].

Despite this growing body of evidence, the combined use of PrBL and SBL specifically to foster research and scientific communication skills remains underexplored. While prior research has highlighted the impact PrBL on research confidence and methodology understanding [20], fewer studies have addressed how simulation tools can enhance not only clinical competence but also the formulation of research questions and interpretation of data.

While project-based and simulation-based learning have been widely studied in medical education, existing literature tends to examine these approaches in isolation—focusing either on their contribution to clinical competency or their role in improving knowledge retention and skill application. However, fewer studies have explored how these pedagogical methods can be systematically leveraged to enhance students’ research capabilities, scientific reasoning, and academic writing—competencies increasingly recognized as core outcomes in modern medical education. Moreover, the potential of simulation platforms such as the Anatomage Table has primarily been evaluated in the context of anatomical education and clinical diagnostics, with limited attention to their use as tools for research-based inquiry and case-based academic writing. Addressing this gap, the present study investigates how integrating project-based learning and simulation-based case analysis can support the development of research and science communication skills in undergraduate medical students within a competency-based curriculum framework.

Materials and methods

This study involved 247 undergraduate medical students enrolled in a single-step medical curriculum at Petre Shotadze Tbilisi Medical Academy. It examined the effectiveness of two pedagogical strategies – project based learning (PrBL) and simulation based learning (SBL), in enhancing research competence and academic performance.

Project based learning (PrBL) implementation

A total of 179 students participated in the PrBL component. These students were in their twelfth semester and had already completed foundational courses in “Academic Writing”, “Critical Thinking”, “Introduction to Medical Research”, and “Biostatistics and Scientific Reporting”. A bank of virtual cases was preloaded and thematically categorized (e.g., trauma, oncology, cardiology). Students selected a unique case from a shared list and were instructed to analyse clinical findings, from differential diagnoses, and prepare an academic case report. Guidance on case analysis, diagnostic frameworks, and report structure was provided during orientation sessions. Students were given 14 days to prepare their reports.

Study design and sampling rationale

This study employed a quasi-experimental, cross-sectional design using naturally occurring cohorts within the institutional medical curriculum. The total PrBL sample consisted of 179 students enrolled in a required teaching course. These students were not randomly assigned to groups. Instead, the division into literature review-based assessment (n = 71) and project-based learning (n = 108) occurred organically based on faculty mentor availability, scheduling logistics, and student topic preferences. The allocation process mirrored real-world educational constraints rather than being designed for experimental control.

While randomisation would have minimized allocation bias, it was not feasible due to institutional and pedagogical limitations- -specifically, the need to preserve mentorship continuity and curricular alignment. We acknowledge that this introduces a potential for sampling bias, particularly if more motivated or research-inclined students opted into the project-based pathway. To mitigate this, we ensured that all participants had similar prior academic exposure, including standardized training in “Academic Writing,” “Critical Thinking,” and “Introduction to Medical Research,” and that mentorship was equitably distributed.

The sample size of 179 students was deemed statistically adequate to detect meaningful differences in academic performance (power > 0.8 at α = 0.05), especially when analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics. However, unequal group sizes may influence the variability in measured outcomes and are noted as a limitation in interpretation.

In the simulation-based learning (SBL) component, the use of the Anatomage table was intentionally added as a distinct intervention to explore the impact of virtual anatomy-based case learning versus conventional paper-based analysis. These 68 students were in a different semester cohort and had no prior experience with the Anatomage tool, allowing for a clean comparative contrast within their learning phase. Attribution of performance outcomes, however, must be interpreted with caution, as the combined analysis of multiple educational innovations (PrBL and SBL) in a non-randomised design inherently limits causal inferences. Future studies using controlled randomised designs are needed to strengthen attribution claims and rule out residual confounding.

Although this study did not employ a randomized controlled design, the allocation of students into pedagogical streams reflected pragmatic constraints and pedagogical priorities within the real-world curriculum. Group assignments were determined by factors such as semester scheduling, faculty mentor availability, and alignment with existing coursework, rather than experimental manipulation. While this introduces the potential for selection bias and limits the attribution of causality, these design choices enabled the preservation of mentorship continuity and curricular coherence—critical considerations in applied educational settings. The study thus represents a trade-off between methodological control and ecological validity, aiming to generate practice-relevant insights under authentic institutional conditions. Future studies employing randomised or matched-cohort designs are warranted to further validate and isolate the effects observed.

Although no formal pre-test/post-test models was applied, comparisons were made between students who completed conventional assignments and those who engaged in PrBL/SBL interventions. This allowed the identification of learning impacts through comparative cross-sectional analysis – particularly differences in academic scores and originality.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize group characteristics, academic performance scores, and similarity percentages. Mean, median, and standard deviation values were calculated. Independent samples t-tests and Mann–Whitney U tests were applied to assess differences between PrBL vs literature-review groups and SBL vs traditional paper-based case groups. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used to determine statistical relevance.

Results – project-based learning (PrBL)

Students who participated in the project-based learning (PrBL) track demonstrated significantly higher academic performance compared to those who completed conventional literature-review assignments. The average academic score in the PrBL group was 82.5, compared to 66.5 in the literature-review group (p < 0.01). The median scores were similarly improved, rising from 63.75 (literature review) to 85.0 (PrBL).

In addition to improved grades, student work in the PrBL group exhibited higher originality. The average similarity score (as measured by Turnitin) decreased from 12.62% in the literature-review group to 4.17% in the PrBL group (p < 0.01), indicating a reduction in potential text overlap and enhanced academic independence. The standard deviation of similarity scores also narrowed, suggesting more consistently original outputs across the cohort.

These findings (Table 2) support the potential of PrBL to promote deeper engagement with academic research tasks, enhance structured scientific writing, and encourage greater independence in evidence use and argumentation.

Results – simulation-based learning (SBL)

Among students who engaged in simulation-based learning (SBL) using the Anatomage Table, academic performance was significantly improved compared to those completing paper-based case assignments. The average academic score rose from 26.6 (paper-based) to 33.0 (SBL) (p < 0.001), with a corresponding median increase from 25.5 to 34.0.

Students in the SBL group also demonstrated significantly greater originality in their case reports, with average similarity scores dropping from 14.92% (paper-based) to 6.21% (Anatomage-based) (p < 0.001). The median similarity score fell from 18% to 6.5%, further highlighting this improvement.

These results (Table 3) suggest that immersive digital simulation using clinical imaging data can not only support clinical case understanding but also improve the quality and originality of students’ written work.

It should be noted that while both PrBL and SBL interventions were associated with improved academic outcomes and reduced similarity scores within their respective groups, no direct comparison between the two modalities is implied, as the cohorts differed in terms of academic level, curricular exposure, and instructional context. The findings are therefore best interpreted as parallel exploratory analyses rather than comparative evaluations.

Discussion

Project-based learning (PrBL)

The findings indicate that students participating in the project-based learning (PrBL) component demonstrated improved academic performance and greater originality in their work compared to those completing traditional literature-review-based assignments. These results align with existing literature highlighting the benefits of PrBL in fostering critical thinking, knowledge integration, and student motivation [3, 4, 6–8, 10]. In particular, structured project tasks—such as formulating a research question, conducting analysis, and producing an academic paper—appear to support the development of skills fundamental to scientific inquiry and academic writing.

While the higher performance and lower similarity scores in the PrBL group suggest meaningful learning gains, it is important to interpret these as exploratory outcomes rather than evidence of causal effect. Allocation to PrBL or literature-review assignments was non-random and influenced by curricular logistics, which may have introduced selection bias. Moreover, variability in scores within the PrBL group may reflect differences in topic complexity or student engagement levels, rather than instructional impact alone [21, 22]. Nonetheless, these results support ongoing calls for embedding research-oriented learning activities within undergraduate medical education [23, 24].

Simulation-based learning (SBL)

Students who completed simulation-based case reports using the Anatomage Table also demonstrated higher academic scores and lower similarity percentages compared to peers completing paper-based case analyses. These results echo previous findings that high-fidelity simulation can enhance cognitive engagement, contextual understanding, and decision-making skills [11, 12, 15–17]. In this study, students benefited from a more interactive and visual learning process, which may have fostered deeper case analysis and improved the originality of report content.

However, as with the PBL component, these outcomes should be interpreted with caution. The simulation and paper-based case groups belonged to different cohorts and had differing prior experiences, making direct attribution of performance differences to the Anatomage intervention methodologically inappropriate. Moreover, as the Anatomage Table was introduced for the first time, improvements may partially reflect novelty effects—an observation consistent with motivational shifts reported in studies evaluating educational technologies [25–27].

Nevertheless, these exploratory findings suggest that simulation-based tools may hold underutilized potential in supporting not only anatomical understanding but also science communication and structured case analysis [18, 24, 28–30].

Educational implications and future directions

While both PrBL and SBL interventions yielded positive academic and originality-related outcomes, it is critical not to generalize these findings beyond their intended exploratory scope. The interventions were implemented in different student groups at different academic stages, targeting distinct learning objectives—scientific inquiry in the case of PrBL and clinical case reporting in the case of SBL. Consequently, no direct comparison or pedagogical superiority claim is warranted. The improvements observed likely reflect context-specific benefits rather than generalizable effects of one methodology over another.

In addition, the enhanced performance across both intervention groups may partially reflect increased student engagement, perceived innovation, or the motivational boost often associated with novel instructional approaches (e.g., Hawthorne effect). While curricular exposure and faculty mentorship were kept as consistent as possible, unmeasured confounding variables such as learning style preferences, topic complexity, or peer collaboration may have contributed to outcome variability.

These limitations underscore the need for future research employing randomized controlled designs, matched cohorts, and longitudinal follow-up to evaluate sustained impacts of these pedagogical strategies. Comparative studies that integrate implementation science frameworks could also help clarify how and when PrBL and SBL approaches most effectively support core educational outcomes in research competence and clinical reasoning.

While the two interventions—PrBL and SBL—were implemented with different cohorts, timelines, and learning objectives, the study does not aim to compare or claim additive effects. Instead, both components were evaluated independently, with a focus on capturing context-specific outcomes rather than drawing generalizable conclusions. Presenting them within a single exploratory study allows for a broader pedagogical reflection on how varied active learning formats might contribute to academic performance and originality in undergraduate settings. The inclusion of both approaches reflects a shared institutional effort to diversify learning experiences and explore innovation in parallel rather than in competition. Thus, while causal conclusions cannot be drawn, this study provides initial insight into the potential value of combining research-oriented project-based learning and simulation-enhanced case analysis to strengthen academic performance and originality in undergraduate medical education. These exploratory findings may help guide the refinement of competency-based curricula that integrate scientific inquiry and applied reasoning as foundational elements of professional development [1, 2].

Conclusion

The demand for innovative educational approaches in medical schools is growing, as traditional methods often fall short in engaging students and enhancing their academic performance. Research skills are essential for medical students, not only for their academic success but also for their future professional practice. Incorporating project-based learning (PrBL) and advanced simulation tools into the medical curriculum offers promising ways to address these needs. Our study highlights the potential of project-based learning (PrBL) to significantly enhance academic performance and research skills in medical students. Integrating PrBL into the medical curriculum provides valuable hands-on experience, better preparing students for future academic and professional challenges. Further research should explore long-term outcomes and refine PrBL implementation strategies. Additionally, the use of simulation-based learning, such as the Anatomage table, can effectively facilitate the development of research and science communication skills among medical students. The Anatomage table aids in a better understanding of medical problems and helps in formulating research questions. However, further research is necessary to develop best practice models for the application of simulation-based learning in enhancing research and science communication skills among medical students.

Limitations

This study was conducted within a real-world educational setting and was designed to explore the integration of project-based and simulation-based pedagogies in a medical curriculum. As such, several limitations must be acknowledged—some of which reflect methodological constraints, while others represent pragmatic trade-offs inherent to curriculum-integrated research.

First, the use of a non-randomised, cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences regarding the effect of either intervention. Group allocation was influenced by scheduling logistics, mentor availability, and student topic preferences, which may have introduced sampling bias—particularly if more motivated or research-inclined students disproportionately participated in the project-based or simulation-supported activities. While prior academic exposure and mentorship guidelines were standardized across groups, unmeasured variables such as learning style, intrinsic motivation, or familiarity with digital tools may have influenced the observed outcomes.

Second, the interventions were implemented in distinct student cohorts, each operating at different stages of the curriculum. This design preserved curricular alignment and ensured pedagogical relevance but also limited comparability across groups. Consequently, no direct claims regarding the superiority of PBL or SBL should be inferred from this study.

Third, while topic diversity was intentionally built into both the project and simulation assignments to promote individualized exploration, this heterogeneity may have introduced variation in task complexity and scope. Despite the use of shared assessment rubrics and common instructional guidelines, variations in topic depth, data availability, or case difficulty could have influenced academic performance and originality outcomes. Future studies may benefit from stratified topic clustering or controlled topic assignment to enhance internal consistency.

Additionally, the relatively small sample size in the simulation-based component and the single-institution context may limit generalizability to other educational environments. However, the use of multiple consecutive semester cohorts and alignment with core curriculum competencies strengthens the ecological validity of the findings.

Finally, the potential influence of novelty effects—particularly in the introduction of the Anatomage Table and structured project design—cannot be ruled out. It is possible that perceived innovativeness or increased faculty attention influenced student effort or engagement. While such effects are common in pedagogical innovation studies, they reinforce the need for longitudinal evaluations that measure sustained learning gains over time.

In summary, while the study offers promising exploratory evidence, it also highlights the need for rigorously controlled, multi-institutional research designs that examine the impact of integrated pedagogical models on research competence, academic performance, and professional readiness in medical education.
