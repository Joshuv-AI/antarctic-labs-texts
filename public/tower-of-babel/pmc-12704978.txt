Pheochromocytoma: A rare cause of abdominal pain identified in a family medicine outpatient department setting
Authors: SRK Teja, Nisarg Aravindan, Athulya V. Ajith, Aakash Sachdeva, Joen Mathew, Santosh Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAbdominal pain is a frequent complaint in primary care, but rarely due to pheochromocytoma. A 23-year-old male farmer presented with right lumbar pain for 3 months, unintentional weight loss of 10 kg, and a nonproductive cough for 1 month. Initial evaluation revealed anemia and right-sided pleural effusion. Abdominal ultrasonography and contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated a large heterogeneous right suprarenal mass with retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy and pleural deposits, suggestive of malignant pheochromocytoma. The patient was referred to endocrinology for further management. This case highlights the importance of family physicians in recognizing red flags such as chronic abdominal pain, weight loss, and respiratory symptoms, and ensuring early referral. Strengthening family medicine services can facilitate timely diagnosis of serious illnesses, reduce complications, and improve patient outcomes.

Introduction

Family medicine importance

The family medicine practitioner can address a wide range of health issues, either through direct care or through referral to other specialties, thereby maximizing the cost-effectiveness of the healthcare system.[1] Some common symptoms are seen in the primary care setting, with abdominal pain being one such complaint with multiple etiologies that often pose diagnostic and therapeutic dilemmas for primary care practitioners. The vague symptomatology and difficult correlation to specific organ pathology obscure clinical findings that lead to incorrect diagnoses. Although most presentations of abdominal pain are benign, a significant number of patients have life-threatening conditions that require a meticulous approach to management to prevent morbidity and mortality. The skill of assessing patients presenting with abdominal pain is fundamental for all primary care doctors.[2] The delayed diagnosis and treatment would lead to serious consequences like lifetime morbidity and even early mortality. Hence, this case report focuses on the aspects of family medicine and its role in diagnosing and managing a case of abdominal pain.

Case Description

The case history was taken with due informed consent from the patient.

Socio-demographic details

The patient was a 23-year-old male who was a resident of Bijnor, UP, India.

Occupation

He is a farmer by occupation and completed secondary education.

Family composition

He belongs to a four-member family and lives with his father, mother, and young brother. His sister got married 2 years ago. The per capita income is 2,500 rupees per month, belonging to class 3 of the Modified BG Prasad Classification of socio-economic status.

Chief complaints

Pain in the right lumbar region of the abdomen for 3 months, cough of nonproductive type with difficulty breathing for 1 month, and unintentional weight loss of 10 kg in 2 months.

HOPI

A 23-year-old man presented to the family medicine outpatient department with abdominal pain present over the right lumbar region for 3 months, insidious in onset, gradually progressive, dull aching type, which was nonradiating. The pain was only partially relieved with analgesics without any relation to food intake or bowel movement.

He reported a cough for 1 month of a nonproductive type associated with shortness of breath of MMRC grade 3, which worsened on lying down on the right side. Unintentional weight loss of 10 kg was reported in 3 months with normal appetite and bowel habits. He denied episodes of headaches, lower extremity swelling, palpitations, chest pain, tremors, or sweating.

The patient had visited the local hospital in Bijnor four times with a history of abdominal pain, where he was treated with antispasmodics and antibiotics for 2 months before coming to the Family Medicine Department, AIIMS Rishikesh.

History

The patient had no past medical history of significance.

Family history

The patient had no significant family history.

Personal history

Normal bowel and bladder habits, sleeps 7–8 h/day, mixed diet, normal appetite, no history of blood transfusions, no addictions.

Use of community resources and impact on family

He visited a local private clinic multiple times, having been given symptomatic care for 2 months without due investigations, leading to a delay in the diagnosis. There was also a lack of a Medical Officer at his local Health and Wellness Centre/Primary Health Centre, due to which he made repeated visits to the private clinic.

Vitals

On presentation, the patient was conscious and oriented. He was afebrile with a blood pressure of 94/60 mmHg, a heart rate of 99/min, a respiratory rate of 22 breaths/min, a body mass index of 14.9 kg/m2, and an ECOG score of 1 out of 5.

GPE

A general physical examination revealed severe pallor present. Other findings were unremarkable. Jugular vein engorgement and peripheral edema were not found.

Systemic examination

Lung examination revealed decreased air entry with a dull note on percussion in the right lung. Abdominal examination revealed tenderness in the right lumbar region, which was restricting his movements. There was no obvious abnormality in the central nervous system or central venous system examination.

Management

The patient underwent the following investigations as prescribed by the family medicine physician [Figure 1], where the complete blood count revealed the following: haemoglobin 10.3 g/dL, RBC 3.84 106/uL, hematocrit 33.4%, and platelets 693,000/mm3. Liver function test results were serum total protein 6.3 gm/dL with serum albumin 3.3 g/dL and serum globulin 3.0 g/dL. Kidney function test results were blood urea was 14.0 mg/dL, and serum creatinine was 0.54 mg/dL. Imaging was also done, where the X-ray of the chest revealed gross pleural effusion on the right side of the lung [Figure 2]. Ultrasonography (USG) abdomen revealed a large heterogeneous mass lesion measuring 16 cm × 13 cm in the retroperitoneal/right suprarenal region [Figure 3].

Following the significant findings of the chest X-ray and USG abdomen, he was advised to undergo a contrast-enhanced computed tomography abdomen, which revealed a heterogeneously enhancing mass lesion in the right suprarenal region with extensions, retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy, and pleural deposits—suggesting malignant transformation of pheochromocytoma [Figure 4]. The patient was then referred to the endocrinology department, given the suspected pheochromocytoma.

Discussion

The case was evaluated as per the abdominal pain symptoms, with the routine investigations being done as per the standard protocols.[3] Pain in the abdomen can occur due to inflammation, infections, trauma, autoimmunity, functional causes, malignancy, and iatrogenic causes, to name a few. Pheochromocytomas and paragangliomas are catecholamine-producing tumors derived from the sympathetic or parasympathetic nervous system. These tumors may arise sporadically or be inherited as features of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN 2), von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) disease, or several other pheochromocytoma-associated syndromes.

The clinical presentation is variable, ranging from an adrenal incidentaloma to a hypertensive crisis with associated cerebrovascular or cardiac complications, with 2–8 million people being affected every year. Its clinical presentation is so variable that pheochromocytoma has been termed “the great masquerader.” Among the presenting manifestations, episodes of palpitation, headache, and profuse sweating are typical, and these manifestations constitute a classic triad. However, a pheochromocytoma can be asymptomatic for years, and some tumors grow to a considerable size before patients note symptoms.[4] It is now quite evident that higher out-of-pocket health spending exacerbates poverty. Corruption, knowledge, education, and information can affect health beliefs, perceptions of health and illness, health-seeking behavior, and adherence to therapy, which impacts proper demand for and adherence to health services and leads to poor outcomes in patients.[5]

Health promotion and specific protection by primary prevention have failed, though the mass per abdomen has little chance of being stopped from occurring, as it has a strong genetic component as well. Early diagnosis and treatment by secondary prevention is the most significant level of failure, as this step would ensure that the mass would be detected early and managed in a much more efficient manner without causing much discomfort to the patient, albeit being more cost-effective as well. The health system that the patient was seeking care from failed to detect the disease early on, which led to the worsening of his health status.

Implications of Case Report

This case report highlights the key role of the family medicine physician by correctly identifying the red flags and symptoms, as was done in this case, due to the worsening abdominal pain, significant weight loss, and shortness of breath, and imaging findings, which led to the patient being referred to endocrinology for suspected pheochromocytoma. Pain abdomen should not be neglected, as it can be due to a serious illness as described earlier.

Conclusion

A comprehensive and patient-centric care approach forms the tenets of family medicine practice. By implementing a robust network of family medicine practices, early diagnosis and treatment of serious conditions can be done at low costs and prevent further complications.
