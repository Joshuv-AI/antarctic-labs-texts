The Psyche Light Elements Investigation
Authors: Thomas H Prettyman, David W Mittlefehldt, Erik I Asphaug, Richard P Binzel, Samuel W Courville, Linda T Elkins-Tanton, David J Lawrence, Simone Marchi, José M G Merayo, Timothy J McCoy, Benjamin P Weiss
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLight elements, such as C, S, Si, O, C, and H, are thought to be present in Earth’s liquid-Fe outer core. These elements lower melting temperatures, thereby allowing the core to remain in liquid state at high pressure and influencing magnetic and geodynamic processes. However, the identity and abundance of the light elements in the cores of terrestrial planets and how they were delivered to these cores is not well known. The NASA Psyche mission will travel to and explore (16) Psyche, which may be the metal-rich core of a differentiated planetesimal exposed by collisional stripping. If so, the Psyche mission could provide a direct assessment of the light element content of an asteroidal core, allowing comparisons to the inferred composition of planetary cores and the parent bodies of the magmatic iron group meteorites. In particular, Earth’s high-pressure core formed gradually (over ∼100 Myr), in a multistage process, under increasingly oxidizing conditions, whereas the cores of planetesimals formed quickly (within 10 Myr) at low pressure, likely in chemical equilibrium with their mantles. The trace element systematics and mineral composition of magmatic iron meteorites indicate the presence of C, P, and S in planetesimal cores prior to solidification. Such elements would have played a role in core dynamics, including dynamo generation. Their low solubility combined with the immiscibility of their mineral precipitates would have resulted in their separation from Fe upon crystallization and their eruption onto the surface of a stripped core (via ferrovolcanism). The Psyche spacecraft will detect their elemental, mineral, and magnetic signatures with the payload instruments, which include a Gamma Ray and Neutron Spectrometer, a Multispectral Imager, and a Magnetometer. Additional constraints on interior composition and processes influenced by light elements will be provided by Psyche’s gravity and geomorphology investigations. We provide a brief introduction to the topic of light elements along with prospects for (16) Psyche. While we emphasize core formation processes, we also consider other possibilities for the origin and evolution of this metal-rich body.Keywords: Planetary cores, Asteroid (16) Psyche, NASA Psyche mission, Planetary cores, Light elements, Iron meteorites, Ferrovolcanism, Metal-rich chondrite meteorites

Introduction

The NASA Psyche spacecraft is on its way to explore the main-belt asteroid (16) Psyche (Elkins-Tanton et al. 2020, 2022; Dibb et al. 2024). (16) Psyche is thought to be metal-rich, perhaps the stripped core of a differentiated protoplanet, a layered asteroid on which the surface was enriched in metal by some process, or an undifferentiated body consisting of primitive, metal-rich materials similar in composition to CB chondrites (Bierson et al. 2025). The Psyche spacecraft will acquire remote sensing data from close-proximity orbits. The observations will unravel many aspects of (16) Psyche’s origins and evolution, and – if (16) Psyche is a core – provide a deeper understanding of processes that occurred in the cores of terrestrial planets and their building blocks.

The Psyche mission’s gravity investigation will determine the asteroid’s density and interior structure (Zuber et al. 2022). The Multispectral Imager will determine (16) Psyche’s shape and morphology and place constraints on the relative surface age and mineralogy (Jaumann et al. 2022; Marchi et al. 2022; Dibb et al. 2023; Bell et al. 2025). The Gamma-Ray and Neutron Spectrometer (GRNS) will determine the elemental composition of (16) Psyche’s regolith (Lawrence et al. 2025). And, the Magnetometer will constrain core formation processes by searching for a remanent magnetic field from an ancient dynamo (Weiss et al. 2023).

The Light Elements Working Group directly supports one of the primary mission objectives, Psyche Mission Objective C:

“Determine whether small metal bodies incorporate the same light elements as are expected in the Earth’s high-pressure core.”

The objectives of the Working Group overlap with and complement other aspects of the Psyche mission’s investigation, including the “Is (16) Psyche a Core?” and “Oxidation-Reduction” Working Groups (Elkins-Tanton et al. 2022; McCoy et al. 2022). The Light Elements Working Group will provide a forum for the Psyche team and members of the planetary and Earth sciences community to discuss these topics. Goals of the working group include anticipation of light element measurement scenarios based on plausible hypotheses for origins and formation prior to the arrival of the spacecraft at (16) Psyche, and the discussion, analysis, and interpretation of light element data acquired during orbital operations.

The possibility that (16) Psyche is the residual metallic core of a differentiated planetesimal may lead to new insights into the interiors of the terrestrial planets. As Earth’s deep interior is not accessible, our understanding of the evolution and current state of Earth’s core is inferred from seismology, the geochemistry of the crust and mantle, cosmochemical constraints, knowledge of geologic processes supported by high pressure experiments (Dziewonski and Anderson 1981; Stevenson 1981; Fischer and McDonough 2025), as well as the study of the geomagnetic field over large time scales based on paleomagnetism (Smirnov et al. 2011; Zhou et al. 2022).

Earth’s core is thought to consist of a liquid outer layer that is gradually freezing on top of a solid Fe-Ni-rich inner core. The latent heat released by this process drives mass convection in the outer core, which in turn sustains Earth’s dynamo magnetic field. The outer core is a liquid iron alloy containing nickel and light elements, such as H, C, O, Si, P, S, and K (e.g., Hirose et al. 2021). The presence of light elements influences the thermodynamic properties of the fluid, lowering the melting temperature and allowing the outer core to remain in liquid state at high pressure (Stevenson 1981).

The protracted, multi-stage process of the formation of Earth’s high-pressure core (tens to 100 Myr) contrasts with the likely path of smaller planetesimal cores, many of which would have formed more quickly (within a few Myr) and at low pressure (e.g., Bizzarro et al. 2005; Wood et al. 2006). The formation of Earth’s core occurred continuously with accretion, with a portion of the metal derived from the infall of differentiated planetesimals (Anderson and Hanks 1972; Poirier 1994; Wood et al. 2006). Accreted metal equilibrated with a silicate-rich magma ocean on Earth, delivering siderophile elements and light elements to the core (Wade and Wood 2005; Wood et al. 2006). In the earliest stages, conditions in Earth’s core would have resembled the low-pressure conditions expected during the growth of planetesimals. As interior pressures increased, crystallization of perovskite at the base of the magma ocean led to increased oxygen fugacity for metal-silicate equilibrium between the core and the mantle (Wade and Wood 2005). This resulted in oxidation of accreted iron, a transition to the addition of iron sulfide to the core, and ultimately the cessation of core growth (e.g., Newsom and Sims 1991; Wade and Wood 2005). Delivery of light elements to the Earth’s core occurred in a multistage process with oxygen fugacity, pressure and temperature all increasing with time.

The high-pressure conditions during Earth’s core formation contrast with planetesimal formation, which occurred at low pressure. Core-mantle partition coefficients derived from low pressure experiments, relevant to the formation of planetesimals, are much higher for many siderophile elements than inferred for Earth, which has an excess of siderophile elements in its mantle (e.g., Wood et al. 2006). Wood et al. (2006) argue that the excess indicates that the Earth’s core could not have formed under conditions of chemical equilibrium, with constant pressure, temperature and oxygen fugacity, whereas core formation on protoplanets, like (4) Vesta, may well have occurred under those conditions (e.g., Toplis et al. 2013). Consequently, the distribution of light elements in planetesimal cores is expected to differ from that of Earth’s core and other terrestrial planets (Fig. 1).

Differences between the light element content of Earth, which is thought to include S, Si, O, C, and H, and the cores of magmatic planetesimals, known to include S, O, C, and P from the iron meteorites, are a result of many factors, including accreted materials, the complexity of core-mantle evolution, and the pressure, temperature, and redox conditions during core growth. The Psyche mission provides an opportunity to directly examine the relict core of a planetesimal stripped of its outer silicate layers by collisions. If (16) Psyche is a core, then the planned observations will enable inferences of the role light elements played in the evolution of this small, metal-rich body. Observations of light elements and their associated minerals within metallic phases on the surface will provide constraints on the light element content of (16) Psyche’s ancient, molten core. Additional constraints on present bulk composition will be provided by gravimetry. The detection of remanent magnetism from an ancient dynamo would provide additional clues to the role of light elements in core dynamics. Geomorphology will provide information on processes like volcanism that are controlled by light element content. The inferred pattern of light elements within (16) Psyche’s ancient, molten core can be compared with that inferred for the cores of terrestrial planets, including Earth, as well as the parent bodies of the magmatic iron meteorites.

If (16) Psyche is not a stripped core, then the distribution of light elements would likely be different. For example, if (16) Psyche is a layered, differentiated asteroid, then the metal enrichment might have occurred via ferrovolcanism (Johnson et al. 2020; Courville et al. 2025). The metal-rich magmas could have been Fe-Ni-S cotectic liquids and could have a predictable S content (e.g., Kitakaze et al. 2016). Should (16) Psyche prove to be a metal-rich primitive body, the measurement of light elements could constrain the sulfide and graphite contents on the surface.

This paper provides a brief introduction to light elements in Earth’s core, including a description of metal-silicate partitioning at high pressure and constraints on core composition (Sect. 2). Core formation on protoplanets differentiated by igneous processes is supported by meteorite studies (Sect. 3) and inferences about (4) Vesta by Dawn (Sect. 4). These sections support the Psyche mission’s top-level objective to determine if small metal bodies include the same light elements as Earth’s core, as well as providing background needed to understand possible paths for Psyche’s origins and evolution and insights into what can be learned about small bodies from spacecraft remote sensing data. Prospects for observations at (16) Psyche and their interpretation are presented in Sect. 5. This work supplements several prior publications that assess possibilities for (16) Psyche’s origins and evolution.

Light Elements in Earth’s Core

That Earth’s interior is denser than its crust was inferred by gravimetry over 250 years ago (e.g., Mills 2022). The modern view of Earth’s interior was informed primarily by the measurement of seismic waves (e.g., Lehmann 1936; Brush 1980; cf., Donini et al. 2019). These observations revealed the layered structure of the Earth, including the presence of a solid iron-rich inner core surrounded by a liquid outer core containing iron alloyed with nickel and light elements. Geochemical evidence for the core, and constraints on the partitioning of light elements between the mantle and the core, comes from the relative abundances of many siderophile and chalcophile elements in bulk silicate Earth, which are suppressed relative to lithophile elements when controlled for volatility (McDonough 2003). A combination of geological and cosmochemical constraints is needed to constrain the composition of the core (Fischer and McDonough 2025) (Fig. 1).

The core makes up 32% of Earth’s mass and 16% of its volume (e.g., Hirose et al. 2021; Fischer and McDonough 2025). The outer core makes up approximately 96 vol.% of the total core (Hirose et al. 2021). Extreme conditions are found within the core, with estimated temperatures and pressures of 4300 K and 136 GPa, respectively at the core-mantle boundary and 6300 K and 229 GPa at the boundary between the inner and outer core (Dehant et al. 2022).

Birch (1952) noted that the density of the Earth’s liquid outer core calculated from seismic data was slightly lower than expected for pure Fe at the temperatures and pressures of the deep interior; he posited that this was caused by light elements being contained within the molten Fe. Current estimates are that the liquid outer core density is reduced by 6–8%, and the solid inner core density is reduced by 4–5%, compared to those of pure Fe (e.g., Hirose et al. 2021). The question of which light elements are in the core continues to be an immensely active area of research, especially in the experimental determination of metal-silicate partitioning at high temperature and pressure. The literature on the topic is too vast to summarize here; we will focus on recent results and how they can inform us of the Earth’s core composition. Commonly considered light element candidates are H, C, O, Si and S (e.g., Poirier 1994), to which we will add P.

Beyond the simple compositional dependency inherent in Eq. 2, parameterizations of partitioning results show a dependency on the bulk composition of the molten silicate and molten metal. The most comprehensive treatment of this for O, S and Si is that of Chidester et al. (2022), who combined their results with the existing body of experimental partitioning data to parameterize partition coefficients for these light elements, the lithophile elements Mg, Al, and Ca, and the radioactive lithophile elements K, U and Th. An important feature of their parameterization is that they model the molten silicate using a multicomponent regular solution model. Other studies typically model the molten silicate using the commonly used non-bridging oxygens per tetrahedral cation (nbo/t) formalism (e.g., Ikuta et al. 2024). Chidester et al. (2022) noted that at mid-mantle pressures where metal-silicate equilibration might have occurred, network forming cations (Si, Al) transition from tetrahedral to octahedral structures in the melt which is not accounted for using the nbo/t parameter. Chidester et al. (2022) found that their parameterizations for partition coefficients gave good fits to the body of experimental data, and that excluding O, simple exchange reactions (Eq. 1) were for the most part sufficient to model element partitioning.

Element partitioning can have a pressure dependency that can reverse partitioning trends established at lower pressures. This has been demonstrated for P in which increasing siderophile behavior with increasing temperature at modest pressures reverses at pressures greater than ∼15 GPa (Ikuta et al. 2024); these authors found the coefficient for pressure dependency at <15 GPa was strongly positive, while above that pressure it was strongly negative. This demonstrates the complexity of light element partitioning and hints at the uncertainties that may arise when experimental data are used to estimate the composition of planetary cores.

Hydrogen becomes significantly siderophile at higher temperatures and pressures. For example, Tagawa et al. (2021) found that for experiments run between 30 and 60 GPa pressure and 3100 and 4600 K, H partitioning can be modeled as an exchange reaction (Eq. 1) yielding DHmetal/silicate≥29. These authors parameterized their results with respect to pressure and temperature but not specifically with respect to composition or melt structures. Using a range of literature estimates for fixed P and T of core formation and bulk silicate Earth water content, they estimated a range of H contents of the core of 0.32 to 0.61 wt% (Tagawa et al. 2021).

Considerable experimental work has been done on C partitioning between molten silicate and metal, but recent work has suggested that much of the earlier studies might not be germane to the core forming process. Grewal et al. (2021) found that the partitioning of C between molten silicate and metal is dependent on the C concentration of the system. Many of the previous experimental studies were done using graphite capsules and therefore the system was graphite-saturated; graphite-saturation is not thought to have pertained during core formation from a terrestrial magma ocean (Grewal et al. 2021). The experiments were done at modest pressure (3 GPa), a temperature of 1973 K and reducing conditions (2.86 to 6.67 log units below the iron-wüstite buffer). Under these conditions, C is strongly siderophile, with DCmetal/silicate of 840-8040 (Grewal et al. 2021). Thus, one can expect that C was quantitatively partitioned into the molten metallic phase during core formation.

The difficulty in using partition coefficients to model the light element inventory of the core lies in the little-constrained nature of the physical models. It is likely that core formation was an ongoing process during accretion of the Earth. At some point during accretion the proto-Earth’s interior became hot enough for significant melting of the silicate and metallic systems and core formation began. As additional planetesimals were accreted, they too separated metal from silicate, growing the core. As the Earth grew, the temperatures and pressures at which planetesimals segregated generally increased; the light element contents of metallic material added to the core would have varied with time even if the accreted planetesimals were identical in composition.

Chidester et al. (2022) modeled core compositions for several accretion scenarios with the baseline case assuming the molten metal could be modeled as having equilibrated with molten silicate at a fixed temperature and pressure; this is often assumed to be equivalent to the average temperature and pressure of metal-silicate equilibration. Continuous growth models of both constant and varying compositions and assuming complete and partial equilibration were tested. Their modeling shows that wide ranges in light element contents can result, depending on the model assumptions. For example, they find a range of Si in the core of ∼1-8 wt% for all the models tested, but this range can be narrowed to ∼2-4 wt% by requiring the model to also reproduce the estimated Si content of the mantle. Similarly, their models that match the Fe content of the mantle and the mass of the core result in ≤1 wt% O in the core. None of the models of Chidester et al. (2022) result in significant S in the core; they found ≤0.2 wt%. However, their formalism treats S in the silicate melt as S0, whereas it can have valences between S2− and S6+ depending on the fO2; additional partitioning experiments for S are required to determine the robustness of the S partitioning model (Chidester et al. 2022).

Cores of Differentiated Protoplanets

While no meteorites have been identified as originating from (16) Psyche, iron meteorites provide key clues to understanding the role of light elements in core formation in planetesimals in the early solar system. Iron meteorites present abundant evidence for light elements having been components of asteroidal cores; this evidence is in the form of minor and accessory minerals included within them (Buchwald et al. 1977). Troilite (FeS) nodules are a ubiquitous and obvious feature of irons. Typically less abundant but still ubiquitous is schreibersite ((Fe,Ni)3P). Carbon phases – graphite, cohenite ((Fe,Ni)3C), and haxonite ((Fe,Ni)23C8) – are not as common and are present in only a few iron meteorite groups. Chromite (FeCr2O3) is an accessory phase in many iron meteorites and daubréelite (FeCr2S4) is common in some groups. Silicon is present in some highly-reduced iron meteorites, sometimes forming perryite ((Ni,Fe)8(Si,P)3). Together, these attest to S, P, C, O, and Si having been components of the metallic melts which formed the iron meteorites.

The classification of iron meteorites is based on their chemical composition, originally using their Ni, Ga and Ge contents as developed through a series of papers by the Wasson group at UCLA (e.g., Wasson 1967), but now including other elements. The current classification system recognizes 13 distinct groups (Goldstein et al. 2009, with the exception that IIICD irons are not included within the IAB complex), where a group is defined based on there being five or more meteorites having compositional characteristics that allow them to be distinguished from other irons. However, compositional and isotopic evidence indicates the IIAB and IIG irons are likely from the same planetesimal core (Wasson and Choe 2009; Anand et al. 2024). Not all iron meteorites can be grouped and of the 1279 iron meteorites with known compositions, roughly 12.5% cannot be associated with defined groups; they are classified as ungrouped irons. In addition, the compositions of the metal phase of stony-iron meteorites can be compared to the iron meteorites and inferences can be drawn regarding their origin. There are three such groups: main-group pallasites; Eagle Station pallasites; and mesosiderites.

Most iron meteorite groups display negative linear trends on log(E) vs. log(Ni) or log(Au) plots where E is a refractory siderophile element such as Re, Os or Ir; the IIIAB irons are an example (Fig. 2). For those groups with many members, variations in refractory siderophile element contents are large, a factor of 1600 for IIIAB irons and 5000 for IIAB irons. These trends are consistent with fractional crystallization of metallic cores in which iron meteorites with high Ir and low Au contents represent early formed solids and those with low Ir and high Au contents late-forming solids (Scott 1972); there are 11 of these groups (counting IIAB and IIG separately) which are referred to as magmatic irons. The compositional characteristics of the metal in the two pallasite groups and the mesosiderites indicate that they also were likely derived from magmatic cores. Main-group pallasites and mesosiderites have metal compositions similar to those of IIIAB irons, but there is no conclusive evidence that they were formed in a single core (e.g., Hassanzadeh et al. 1990; Wasson and Choi 2003; Chabot and Zhang 2022). Similarly, there are compositional similarities between IIIF irons and Eagle Station pallasites, but it seems unlikely that they were formed on a single parent object (Hilton et al. 2020).

Iron meteorite group IIE shows a limited range in refractory siderophile element contents and there is no anti-correlation between these elements and Ni or Au (Fig. 2). The IAB irons do have a wide range in refractory siderophile element contents (Ir by a factor of 1000) but their correlations with Ni or Au are more diffuse than those of the IIIAB irons. The IAB and IIE irons contain abundant silicate inclusions (e.g., Goldstein et al. 2009). These two groups are not thought to have originated in magmatic cores. They are sometimes called “non-magmatic,” although they clearly formed from metallic melts (Maurel et al. 2020).

The ungrouped irons and pallasites present a problem when attempting to established how they were formed. Scott (1979) suggested that ungrouped irons represent probably at least 50 distinct parent objects; including the grouped irons and stony irons, the total would be >65. However, Scott (1979) knew of only 69 ungrouped irons, while currently there are 160; the number of distinct parent objects represented by ungrouped irons is undoubtedly much higher than 50, on the order of 115. Scott (1979) also noted that compositional trends among all ungrouped irons mimicked trends observed within individual magmatic iron meteorite groups and suggested that the ungrouped irons most likely had similar evolutionary histories; they were formed in fractionally crystallized cores. However, it is difficult to be confident of the petrogenetic history of one-off meteorites with such simple mineralogy. We can be confident that we have samples of 14 planetesimal cores, and an unknown, but probably large, number more are plausibly represented by the ungrouped irons and pallasites (e.g., Goldstein et al. 2009).

As noted in relation to Fig. 2, the light element contents of molten Fe,Ni affect the partitioning of trace siderophile elements between solid and liquid Fe,Ni (e.g., Jones and Drake 1983). This was ascribed to the light element as interacting with other elements in metallic systems (Jones and Malvin 1990). The latter study assumed that the light element either repelled or attracted other elements in the metallic liquid, the result being that those other elements either partitioned more strongly into solid metal or had greater solubility in the liquid metal.

Carbon and P also affect siderophile element partitioning in metallic systems (e.g., Chabot et al. 2017). In many cases, the effects of these elements are in the same direction as S (see Chabot et al. 2017, Fig. 3) and modeling assuming S only will slightly overestimate the core S content. Chabot and Zhang (2022) and Zhang et al. (2024) have modeled trace siderophile element trends for all magmatic iron groups to derive core P and S contents. They assumed that C was not significant, which is a safe assumption given that C < 0.2 wt% in iron meteorites (see Grewal and Asimow 2023) and calculated core compositions are similarly low (Buchwald et al. 1977; Hirschmann et al. 2021). The model results show that S contents in planetesimal cores varied from ∼0 to 15 wt% (Fig. 1).

Warren (2011) summarized the data on isotopic anomalies in the Earth, Moon, Mars, chondrites, achondrites and the lithic components of stony irons and demonstrated that these materials fall into one of two clusters, the carbonaceous (CC, so-called because it contains all carbonaceous chondrite groups) and the noncarbonaceous (NC). These two clusters were posited to represent separate reservoirs in the early Solar System, which were suggested to be the outer Solar System for the carbonaceous cluster, and the inner Solar System for the noncarbonaceous cluster. Warren (2011) further showed that main-group pallasites and mesosiderites are in the noncarbonaceous cluster, while the Eagle-Station pallasites are in the carbonaceous cluster. Kruijer et al. (2017) used Mo isotopic systematics to show that seven iron meteorite groups (including both non-magmatic groups) are members of the noncarbonaceous cluster; and five are members of the carbonaceous cluster. Kruijer et al. (2017) did not analyze samples of IIG irons, but Anand et al. (2024) have shown that these are members of the noncarbonaceous cluster. Thus, differentiation and core formation occurred in planetesimals of the carbonaceous and noncarbonaceous clusters, and if Warren’s hypothesis is correct, in the inner and outer Solar System. The models of iron meteorite cores show that those from the carbonaceous cluster tend to have lower S contents than do those of the noncarbonaceous cluster (Fig. 1).

(4) Vesta – a Case Study

The NASA Psyche mission derives much of its scientific heritage from the NASA Dawn mission, which explored the two largest bodies in the main asteroid belt, (4) Vesta and (1) Ceres (Russell and Raymond 2011). These protoplanets have survived largely intact, providing a window into the earliest epoch of the Solar System. (4) Vesta is a differentiated, igneous body (520 km diameter), with a basaltic crust, ultramafic mantle, and core. Prior to Dawn, (4) Vesta was thought to be the source of the howardite, eucrite, and diogenite (HED) meteorites. Data acquired by Dawn at (4) Vesta strengthened the connection between (4) Vesta and these achondrites (McSween et al. 2013). Examining interpretations of (4) Vesta derived from Dawn mission data can give us a perspective on what might be learned from the Psyche mission, including through evaluation of the light element data.

The composition of (4) Vesta’s surface as revealed by Dawn is like that of howardite, a brecciated mixture of basaltic eucrites, cumulate eucrites, and diogenites, representative of crustal materials. A portion of howardites – the so called “regolithic howardites” – contain H-bearing, carbonaceous chondrite inclusions (Cartwright et al. 2013), likely delivered from the infall of carbonaceous impactors to (4) Vesta’s basaltic surface (McCord et al. 2012).

The detection of OH-bearing phases and H by Dawn’s Visible to Infrared mapping spectrometer (VIR) and the Gamma Ray and Neutron Detector (GRaND), in association with low albedo regions on (4) Vesta, supports this hypothesis (De Sanctis et al. 2012; Prettyman et al. 2012). Exogenic carbonaceous chondrite materials are a minor component of (4) Vesta’s regolith, less than about 5 wt.% based on the analysis of GRaND data (Mittlefehldt and Prettyman 2025). Much of the observed carbonaceous material may have been delivered by a small number of impacts (Reddy et al. 2012).

This assessment is supported by meteorite studies. The most common exogenous component found in HED polymict breccias is CM chondrite clasts (Zolensky et al. 1996), and similarly, excess siderophile and volatile element contents of howardites are best matched by CM chondrites (Chou et al. 1976). Regolithic howardites are thought to represent ancient, well-mixed regolith (Warren et al. 2009). Using the Ni, Ir and Au contents from a literature database (Mittlefehldt 2015) as a basis, regolithic howardites (Warren et al. 2009; Mittlefehldt et al. 2013) average ∼4 wt% CM material. If HEDs are indeed from (4) Vesta, then this is an estimate for the vestan regolith.

Although large impact basins like Rheasilvia and Veneneia are present on (4) Vesta, evidence for endogenous olivine is lacking (Le Corre et al. 2015). This suggests a crust-mantle boundary deeper than the floors of these craters, with diogenitic plutonic rocks dominating the lower crust sampled by impacts (e.g., Barrat et al. 2010; Toplis et al. 2013; Vaci et al. 2021). A pure olivine mantle may not be present at depths expected if (4) Vesta formed a magma ocean (Ermakov et al. 2014). However, laboratory spectroscopic measurements of olivine-bearing and olivine-free diogenites indicate that 70:30 orthopyroxene:olivine mixtures might be difficult to resolve by the Dawn VIR instrument (Beck et al. 2013).

Dawn’s gravity investigation revealed a central mass concentration, consistent with an Fe,Ni core, with a radius between 107 and 113 km (Russell et al. 2012), making up about 8% of (4) Vesta’s volume and 17% of the mass (Ermakov et al. 2014). The presence of a core along with geochemical constraints from meteorite studies and remote sensing data by Dawn, confirms that (4) Vesta underwent igneous differentiation from a chondritic source. However, recent modeling of Dawn geophysical data indicates that the core is much smaller, ∼50 km in radius, and has a lower density and smaller density contrast with the mantle than previously modeled; the core might be a metal-sulfide-olivine mixture (Park et al. 2025).

Efficient melting of accreted chondritic material on small bodies resulted from the presence of short-lived radioisotopes, like 26Al and 60Fe, present in the early solar system (McCoy et al. 2006; Zuber et al. 2011; Formisano et al. 2013). The details of the differentiation process, including the silicate melt fraction required for segregation of metal and the style of differentiation (e.g., formation of a magma ocean vs. limited partial melting and fractional crystallization), have been explored through the lens of the HED meteorites and geochemical modeling (e.g., Stolper 1977; Righter and Drake 1996, 1997; Ruzicka et al. 1997; Warren 1997; Mandler and Elkins-Tanton 2013).

Toplis et al. (2013) used mass balance constraints and thermodynamic modeling combined with data from HED meteorites and Dawn to constrain the bulk composition of (4) Vesta. Their analysis considered 12 chondritic compositions and found that (4) Vesta’s bulk composition was best matched by a Na-depleted H-chondrite with the possible addition of a CM-like component. The crustal composition was taken to be that of the primitive, main group eucrite Juvinas, which was assumed to be in equilibrium with the olivine and pyroxene rich mantle (Fig. 3). Core and mantle composition was determined using an iterative mass balance procedure, resulting in estimates of the mass of metal (Fe,Ni) and sulfide (Fe,Ni-S) consistent with gravity constraints from Dawn (Fig. 1). A 60:40 mixture of olivine to pyroxene was estimated for the mantle, with 9% porosity required to match density constraints from gravimetry, possibly resulting from fracturing of the interior by the Rheasilvia impact (Park et al. 2014).

Fu et al. (2012) reported evidence that (4) Vesta formed an advecting metallic core with a magnetic dynamo, dissipating within about 6 Myr following the formation of the solar system. Remanent magnetism in Allan Hills A81001, a eucrite meteorite with an 40Ar-39Ar plateau age (cooling age) of 3.69 Ga, suggests a surface magnetic field of <2 μT was present at that time. The field strength is sufficient to withstand irradiation by solar wind, perhaps explaining limited weathering by solar wind ions. While the meteorite could not have been magnetized directly by the dynamo, the study provides evidence that a dynamo was once active in (4) Vesta’s early history. An ancient dynamo on (4) Vesta is supported by thermophysical modeling by Formisano et al. (2016), who argued that an active core dynamo field with intensity up to 2600 μT could have persisted for 150-500 Myr with surface fields similar to that predicted by Fu et al. (2012). Dynamos may have been active on other small differentiated protoplanets, including the residual core of asteroid (16) Psyche following collisional stripping.

Prospects for Psyche

A strong case can be made that (4) Vesta is the parent body of most meteorites in the HED clan (McSween et al. 2013). This argument is based on telescopic observations of (4) Vesta, analyses of Dawn mission data, and meteorite studies. To make more definitive statements about the identity of the HED parent body, acquiring representative samples from Vesta may be required. The likely valid HED-Vesta connection supports many of the detailed and very specific geochemical inferences described in the foregoing section. On the other hand, no such connection exists for asteroid (16) Psyche. However, it’s metallic character and other properties inferred from telescopic observations provide some hints as to what (16) Psyche might be (Elkins-Tanton et al. 2022; Bierson et al. 2025).

In this section, we briefly review the available observations of (16) Psyche and provide a partial list of hypotheses for (16) Psyche’s origins and evolution. Observations planned for the Psyche spacecraft are described, focusing on light element signatures and how they would constrain aspects of (16) Psyche’s magmatic evolution, if indeed (16) Psyche is a stripped core. If large portions of Psyche’s surface are metallic, then minerals bearing light elements that precipitated from the melt may be present and could be detected by instruments on board the Psyche spacecraft. We note that an important outcome of the Psyche mission would be the identification of meteorites that best represent (16) Psyche’s composition, such that the detailed information provided by meteorite studies can be brought to bear on the analysis of Psyche mission data.

Summary of Current Observations

Asteroid (16) Psyche is in the main asteroid belt (2.92 AU semi-major axis, 0.134 eccentricity, and 3.097° inclination, retrieved from the IAU Minor Planet Center on 15-Feb-2025). (16) Psyche’s irregular shape is roughly an oblate spheroid, with ellipsoidal dimensions of 278 × 238 × 171 km, with uncertainties of a few km (Shepard et al. 2021). The asteroid has a rotational period of about 4.2 hours with the spin axis tipped towards the plane of the ecliptic. Such high obliquity suggests (16) Psyche experienced at least one giant impact. It is difficult if not impossible to remove the mantle of a differentiated protoplanet to produce a body the size of (16) Psyche in a single collision (Bottke et al. 2005). Asphaug et al. (2006) argue that hit and run collisions during the pairwise accretion of planetesimals may have been relatively common. These collisions would have stripped protoplanets of their outer silicate layers, resulting in the exposure of some of their cores while asteroids were still accreting (Yang et al. 2007).

Earth based remote sensing observations of (16) Psyche reveal a body that is likely metal rich, with a density that is higher than silicate minerals, but much lower than iron (e.g., Elkins-Tanton et al. 2020; Cantillo et al. 2021; Elkins-Tanton et al. 2022). The asteroid’s high radar albedo suggests the presence of Fe,Ni metal on or near the surface (e.g., Ostro et al. 1985; Shepard et al. 2017). Reflectance spectra bear similarities to iron meteorites; and analyses of infrared reflectance spectra suggest high metal content (e.g., 82.5% metal per Cantillo et al. 2021). Data from the Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA) show that (16) Psyche’s surface is heterogeneous, with some regions rich in metal and others rich in silicates (Cambioni et al. 2022). Rotationally-resolved spectra in the mid-infrared lack spectral features and is consistent with that expected for a metal-rich body (Arredondo et al. 2024). A metal-rich surface is also supported by thermal infrared interferometric observations (Matter et al. 2013).

The most recent published measurement of (16) Psyche’s density is 4172 ± 145 kg/m3 from high-precision astronomy (Farnocchia et al. 2024). This can be compared to the bulk density of (4) Vesta of 3459 kg/m3 reported by Konopliv et al. (2014), which is understood to be a largely intact differentiated protoplanet. Like (4) Vesta, the surface of (16) Psyche is thought to include exogenic contamination based on observations of the 3 μm spectral region, which suggest the presence of OH- and water-bearing mineral phases (Takir et al. 2016). Jarmak et al. (2024) used James Webb Space Telescope data spanning 1.1-6.63 μm to estimate the hydrogen abundance, which was found to be in the 250-400 μg/g range, similar to that found on (4) Vesta (Prettyman et al. 2012).

Possible Origins and Evolution of (16) Psyche

Several hypotheses have been suggested to explain the foregoing observations. (16) Psyche may be the stripped core of a differentiated protoplanet like (4) Vesta (e.g., Burbine et al. 2024), as proposed by the Psyche mission (Elkins-Tanton et al. 2020); this is the homogeneous high-metal, high-porosity asteroid model of Bierson et al. (2025). This explanation has fallen in and out of favor, given improvements in density estimates and analyses of remote sensing data (e.g., Viikinkoski et al. 2018; Shepard et al. 2021). However, if (16) Psyche were a residual core, then materials enriched in sulfur may have been erupted onto the surface by ferrovolcanism (Johnson et al. 2020; Courville et al. 2025). This scenario involves outside-in cooling of the core which is expected for asteroid-sized bodies (Williams 2009), which may have been covered by a thin mantle. The formation of iron-nickel dendrites as the core cooled (Haack and Scott 1992) would produce pockets of melt, increasingly enriched in sulfur. It is hypothesized that excess pressures would result in the intrusion of the dense, sulfur-rich metallic magma through dykes into the mantle and/or eruption onto the surface (Johnson et al. 2020).

The fluid likely to be intruding or erupting would be an immiscible liquid that forms after fractional solidification begins in an Fe-Ni-S system. This immiscible liquid is stoichiometrically nearly identical to the mineral troilite (Jones and Drake 1983), though it would carry other light elements that are incompatible with iron-nickel metallic liquid (e.g., Corgne et al. 2008). The most likely ferrovolcanism, therefore, would not actually be metallic but rather sulfide-rich (Bercovici et al. 2022).

Curiously, this cooling scenario could also apply to a largely intact, differentiated silicate body that accreted primitive materials with high metal content, with a bulk composition like that of the enstatite or CB/CH chondrite meteorites; this is the internally layered asteroid model of Bierson et al. (2025). As suggested by Shepard et al. (2021), radar bright regions would correspond to local ferrovolcanic eruptions/intrusions through the silicate mantle and crust. As with Mercury, the silicate layers of such a planetesimal would be relatively thin compared to the core.

Alternatively, Viikinkoski et al. (2018) suggested (16) Psyche may be a mesosiderite-like asteroid, with a pyroxene rich outer layer. This hypothesis is genetically related to the possibility of stripped core in that the proposed collisional stripping could have removed the outer layers of a differentiated protoplanet, followed by the accretion of core and silicate materials. Kim and Hirabayashi (2022) modeled (16) Psyche as differentiated body with an outer silicate layer and core, providing constraints on minimum core size (up to 40% of the asteroid’s volume). They argue that ferrovolcanic surface eruptions could be a source of metal-rich surface materials. However, the lack of FeO spectral features for (16) Psyche make a mesosiderite-like material on its surface unlikely (Elkins-Tanton et al. 2020).

The observed, coarse heterogeneity of the surface does not seem to fit the hypothesis that (16) Psyche is an undifferentiated body that accreted primitive, metal rich material (e.g., Elkins-Tanton et al. 2022); this is the homogeneous silicate-rich asteroid model of Bierson et al. (2025). If this model pertains, the bulk density could be consistent with that of the CB chondrites (average grain density 5650 kg m−3), with added porosity (e.g., Macke et al. 2011; Dibb et al. 2023). The CB chondrite meteorites have higher, but variable, concentrations of metal compared to all other chondrite groups (25-80 vol.%; Weisberg et al. 2001; Krot et al. 2002, 2024) and the existence of these primitive meteorites suggests metal-rich planetesimals formed in the early solar system, possibly in close proximity to the Sun, with implications for the formation of the inner planets (Weisberg et al. 2001). Whether the parent body of the CB chondrite meteorites formed by direct condensation from the solar nebula or from the ejecta plume of a giant impact is unknown (Krot et al. 2005; Weyrauch et al. 2019), but recent work favors the latter (e.g., Krot et al. 2024).

Inferring Psyche’s Sulfur Content Based on Current Observations

Assuming (16) Psyche is a residual core, it is possible to predict the range of bulk composition and porosity of the body (Elkins-Tanton et al. 2020, 2022). In this study, we inferred bulk properties, including the concentration of sulfur, using additional information from reflectance spectroscopy and meteorite studies.

We fit a model of core-mantle composition to the density of (16) Psyche reported by Farnocchia et al. (2024). The asteroid’s bulk density was modeled as a mixture of void space (porosity) and solid consisting of Fe,Ni metal, with variable Ni content, troilite (FeS), and olivine, with variable amounts of forsterite and fayalite (Mg- and Fe-rich end members). Because the model has multiple parameters and there is only one observational constraint (density), the analysis can only describe the range of model parameters in the absence of other information.

However, some additional information is available from analyses of spectral reflectance observations in the visible to near infrared (0.35 to 2.5 μm) (Cantillo et al. 2021), which provide constraints on (16) Psyche’s regolith composition. They found 82.5 wt.% metal, 7 wt.% low Fe pyroxene, and 10.5 wt.% carbonaceous chondrite by mass best fit the reflectance data. Their best fit to the density data resulted in a macroporosity of 35%, assuming (16) Psyche’s bulk composition was identical to the surface.

Our analysis takes a slightly different approach. We assume that the carbonaceous chondrites contaminate the near surface and are not a significant bulk component, analogous to (4) Vesta (e.g., De Sanctis et al. 2012; McCord et al. 2012; Prettyman et al. 2012). However, the reflectance data along with meteorite studies can aid in coarsely constraining the four volume fractions (porosity, metal, troilite, and silicate) in our core-mantle model.

In our analysis, we assume that the ratio of silicates to metal determined by reflectance spectroscopy is the same as the bulk and that the density range for spanned by olivine end members forsterite and fayalite is representative of the bulk silicate composition. Our ingoing assumption for the concentration of sulfur in the solid bulk material is about 6 wt.%, with the range of that inferred for the magmatic irons (Fig. 1). This would imply that much of the sulfur in (16) Psyche’s liquid core was retained. Finally, we assumed initially that the macroporosity is on the order of 10% to represent a core that was not substantially broken apart by collisional processes. The foregoing assumptions were used to construct a prior probability distribution for the model parameters.

We used Bayesian inference with Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to sample model parameters that are consistent with (16) Psyche’s density and uncertainty. The inferred parameters are shown in Fig. 4 and details of the inversion can be found in the Appendix. The results depend on the model assumptions (core-mantle composition) as well as the foregoing assumptions about (16) Psyche’s bulk composition; however, the process illustrates the potential range of compositional parameters and their correlations, given what is currently known about (16) Psyche.

The density inversion results in a porosity (void volume fraction) of 0.31−0.06+0.05, consistent with the value reported by (Cantillo et al. 2021), but larger than the upper bound of 0.2 discussed by Elkins-Tanton et al. (2020, 2022). Porosity is characteristic of the internal structure of small bodies, with coherent bodies having low porosity and bodies with high porosity consisting of rubble/aggregate held together loosely by gravity (e.g., Britt et al. 2002). Asteroids with greater than 30% porosity are likely rubble piles. Moreover, cold compaction in metal-rich rubble piles is thought to be minimal, leading to relatively high porosities (Zhang et al. 2022). As such, (16) Psyche may not be an intact core but instead may be re-accreted collisional debris. The abundance of Fe,Ni metal in the solid portion of the asteroid ranges from 55 wt.% to 76 wt.% (16th to 84th percentile), with a median of 66 wt.%. Such high metal fractions are consistent with core material, but are also consistent with CB chondrites once the 25-80 vol.% metal is converted to a wt.% basis.

The values of sulfur in the form of troilite in the solid asteroid material sampled by MCMC ranged from 4.2 wt.% to 11 wt.% (16th to 84th percentiles) with the median of 7.2 wt.% taken to be a representative value (Figs. 1 and 7). This is consistent with our ingoing assumptions (prior) and shows that (16) Psyche’s density is consistent with models with sulfur in the range expected for the parent cores of the magmatic iron meteorites. Few analyses of S or modal calculations of troilite are available for CB chondrites and the data plotted in Fig. 1 are based on duplicate analyses of a single meteorite which have different metal and troilite contents (Jarosewich 1990, 2006). Limited data indicate that CB chondrites are depleted in volatile elements (see for example Krot et al. 2002),and likely contain much less S than we calculate for (16) Psyche. The data shown in Fig. 1 likely are higher in S than a truly representative CB chondrite would have. On a primitive, undifferentiated body, we would expect the S to be effectively uniformly distributed across the surface.

It would be difficult to distinguish the residual core scenario from that of an undifferentiated, metal-rich body based on density alone. The CB chondrite meteorites have 25-80 vol.% metal and minor amounts of troilite (Weisberg et al. 2001; Krot et al. 2002, 2024). Silicates are often in the form of coarse olivine-rich chondrules. The same mineral components were used in our mixing model. To fit the density data (Fig. 4 trend), an undifferentiated, CB-chondrite-like body would also require significant macroporosity.

Spacecraft Observations

Spacecraft observations will resolve much of the uncertainty in Earth-based remote sensing data. The Gravity Investigation will accurately determine the mass of (16) Psyche and provide constraints on the interior density structure (Zuber et al. 2022). The Multispectral Imager will reveal the detailed shape and geomorphology of the surface (Jaumann et al. 2022). Crater morphology will be a key observable of the Psyche mission (Marchi et al. 2022), as bulk composition and porosity will influence the size, shape, and distribution of ejecta. For instance, the presence of light elements may alter the mechanical properties of the target, thus cratering efficiency and morphology. Crater counting will be used to determine the relative chronology of surface units. Geomorphological evidence for magmatic volcanism is abundant on all of the terrestrial planets; although (4) Vesta’s volcanic past is primarily informed by geochemical signatures. Perhaps (16) Psyche will reveal distinct morphologies associated with ferrovolcanism, such as vents, floodplains, domes, or dykes. If so, this would provide evidence that light elements, likely sulfur, played a role in (16) Psyche’s evolution.

Psyche’s Multispectral Imager includes seven narrow band filters spanning the visible to near infrared (0.4 to 1.1 μm) (Dibb et al. 2023; Bell et al. 2025). The filters provide information on the slope/shape of the reflectance spectrum (e.g., sensitive to the presence of Fe,Ni metal) and absorption features characteristic of specific minerals, including Fe-bearing silicates and sulfides. Laboratory studies of meteorites show that diagnostic sulfide minerals, like troilite (FeS), which is expected if (16) Psyche is a core, and oldhamite (CaS), which is not commonly found in iron meteorites, are detectable by the imager. Detection limits for sulfur-bearing minerals are expected to be on order of 10 wt.% (Elkins-Tanton et al. 2022). Enstatite chondrites contain oldhamite (Keil 1968), which may be present if (16) Psyche accreted primitive materials formed in a highly reducing environment (McCoy et al. 2022). Studies of the mineralogy of iron meteorites show that sulfur and phosphorus were present in the molten cores of protoplanets, with sulfur expected to be the primary light element. As such, phosphides may be present on (16) Psyche’s surface; however, their abundance is expected to be much lower than that of the sulfides. Additional work is needed to determine if iron-bearing phosphides like schreibersite (Fe3P) found in meteorites could be detected by the imager.

The Psyche mission’s Gamma-Ray and Neutron Spectrometer (GRNS) includes a high resolution gamma-ray spectrometer (GRS) capable of resolving characteristic gamma rays produced by the decay of radioactive elements and excitation of nuclei by cosmic ray interactions (Lawrence et al. 2025). The GRNS will measure and map the elemental composition of (16) Psyche’s surface on coarse spatial scales (on the order of 10s of km, compared to about 10 m expected for the Multispectral Imager). The instrument is sensitive to composition in the outermost few decimeters of the surface, including the abundance of radioelements, major elements in silicate minerals, Fe, Ni, and compositional macroparameters such as the thermal neutron absorption cross section and effective atomic mass of the materials that make up the regolith. The GRS is similar to the GRS flown on the MESSENGER mission to Mercury (Goldsten et al. 2007).

Sulfur was found to be abundant in surface materials of Mercury (up to 4 wt.%) as determined by X-Ray Spectroscopy (Nittler et al. 2011). The MESSENGER GRS detected gamma rays produced by neutron inelastic scattering and radiative neutron capture with 32S (Evans et al. 2012). The former produced a doppler broadened peak at 2230 keV in a complex spectral region with gamma rays from reactions with H, Al, and the decay of 214Bi (238U decay chain) and 24Na (multiple reaction channels). At Mercury, spectral fitting was required to determine the intensity of the 2230 keV peak separately from the other gamma rays in the region. The analysis of a low altitude spectrum acquired at an effective altitude of 0.4 Mercury body radii (1.88 steradians) and accumulated for 11.5 days resulted in about 10% relative uncertainty (precision only) in the intensity of the 2230 keV peak. This demonstrates the feasibility of the detection of S using high resolution gamma ray spectroscopy.

Using MESSENGER GRNS-like backgrounds and energy resolution sensitivity (e.g., Evans et al. 2017), along with conservative estimates of the cosmic-ray environment and a measurement altitude of 1-Psyche radii, it was determined that the GRS would quantify Psyche’s S abundance at a relative precision of 20% if it were present with an abundance of 3 wt.% (Lawrence et al. 2025). Given that the Psyche GRS energy resolution performance is over a factor of two better than MESSENGER (Lawrence et al. 2024, 2025) and the expected range of orbit altitudes is ∼0.5 to ∼0.8 Psyche radii, we expect to achieve a S abundance sensitivity of less than 3 wt.%. Thus, quantification of S if present at the 4 wt.% level (about 10 wt.% troilite) is expected and some level of mapping may be possible, assuming that the current GRS performance remains consistent and the goals for proximity and accumulation time for the Psyche spacecraft’s low altitude orbit are met. This orbit is currently planned to have an average altitude of about 0.65 body radii, close to that achieved by MESSENGER at Mercury and much lower than the altitudes sampled by Dawn at (4) Vesta (Evans et al. 2012; Prettyman et al. 2019; Polanskey et al. 2025). This orbit would enable mapping on coarse spatial scales (up to 200 km2 areas) (Lawrence et al. 2025).

Lawrence et al. (2025) reported sensitivities for other light elements using gamma ray spectroscopy, including K and Si (with 200 mg/g and 2.5 wt.% detection limits when mapped on 200 km2 areas). The average concentrations of carbon and phosphorus may be measurable over the entire surface sample by the orbit if they were present in concentrations greater than 1 wt.%.

The detection of sulfur in abundances higher than that found in chondritic meteorites accompanied by other observations indicating (16) Psyche is a core (Elkins-Tanton et al. 2020, 2022), would provide strong indication that sulfur was the primary light element in (16) Psyche’s core, similar to that expected for the parent bodies of the magmatic iron meteorites. High sulfur concentrations may also be observed if (16) Psyche were accreted from primitive materials formed in a reducing environment, like the enstatite chondrites with 2-5.6 wt.% S (Mason 1966; Lodders and Fegley 1998). The droplets from which metal grains found in CB chondrites formed may have contained up to 3 wt.% S (Srinivasan et al. 2017).

The Psyche mission’s Magnetometer investigation will determine if (16) Psyche has a remanent magnetic field produced by an ancient dynamo (Weiss et al. 2023). Attributes of the magnetic field as measured from orbit, including the magnetic moment, provide clues to the strength and duration of a dynamo, which in turn depend on the concentration of light elements present in the core. Detection of a magnetic field of about 6 nT in the lowest altitude mapping orbit would be consistent with this scenario. On the other hand, the lack of a remanent field would be indeterminate with respect to whether Psyche formed from a core since there are many reasons a core might not have generated a strong and long dynamo field. The interpretation of orbital data is made further ambiguous if (16) Psyche was substantially disrupted by collisions that could have reset magnetization due to shock heating. Finally, ancient ferrovolcanism may have left a magnetic signature that could be detected by Psyche’s Magnetometer (Courville et al. 2025).

Light Elements in Metal-Rich Regions

Science requirements for the mission include determining the abundance of light elements in regions of Psyche’s surface that appear in the metal phase and comparing the results to their inferred abundance in Earth’s core. If metallic regions on the scale of the areas mappable by GRNS, about 200 km2 are exposed on the surface of (16) Psyche, then the GRNS and Multispectral Imager could determine the composition and mineralogy of light-element precipitates in the metal phase. Regions larger than a few 10s of meters would be accessible to the Multispectral Imager. These observations would constrain the light element content of the ancient, molten core. In meteorites, such precipitates are in the form of nodules, most commonly of troilite (Fig. 5), but in some meteorite groups, troilite-graphite or graphite (Buchwald et al. 1977). Metallic regions smaller than the GRNS footprint could be analyzed using spatial unmixing guided by higher resolution mineralogy data acquired by the imager. This approach is model based and adds uncertainty but has been demonstrated on other missions (e.g., Prettyman et al. 2004; Hagerty et al. 2006).

During crystallization of metallic cores, the S and P contents of the melt phase will build up as Fe,Ni crystallizes. If the initial P content was low, the system will reach the Fe,Ni-FeS cotectic and metal plus troilite will crystallize. However, with sufficient P in the melt, the melt could enter a two-phase melt field in which immiscible S-rich, P-poor and P-rich, S-poor liquids are present (Jones and Drake 1983). Liquid immiscibility has been suggested to have occurred during crystallization of the IIAB (Wasson et al. 2007) and IIIAB (Ulff-Møller 1998) iron meteorite cores based on trace siderophile element distributions. However, in the case of the IIIAB irons, more recent geochemical modeling indicates that liquid immiscibility might not be necessary to explain the trace siderophile element distributions (Chabot and Zhang 2022; see Fig. 2, this study). Experimental studies show that multiple immiscible melts should be expected in the low pressure environments of asteroidal cores that contain S, C and O; one will be C-rich but low in S and O and the other will be S-rich, have high O but low C (Corgne et al. 2008).

If (16) Psyche is a largely intact core, the near surface of the asteroid could expose metallic regions containing crystalline products of one or more of the immiscible melts. Jones and Drake (1983) ran one experiment in the Fe-Ni-S-P system to estimate the effects of Ni on the boundaries of the two-liquid immiscibility field. The S-rich liquid contained 28.9 wt% S but only 0.16 wt% P, while the P-rich liquid contained 3.0 wt% S and 5.7 wt% P. It is not possible to make firm predictions for compositions of possible crystalline immiscible liquids on (16) Psyche, but using the experimental results as a guide, the S content might be in the 10-20 wt% range while the P content could be a few wt%. It is difficult to predict what might be found on the surface of the asteroid. If crystallization occurred at the core-mantle boundary or if the liquids erupted onto the surface (Bercovici et al. 2019), then high concentrations of S and P might be distributed on scales that can be sensed by the instruments. Absent experiments in more complex systems, predictions as to the number of immiscible melts that would form if C, O, P and S were all present in significant amounts cannot be made.

Summary

Light elements play an important role in the dynamics of planetary cores, including Earth’s core and the ancient cores of differentiated protoplanets. The addition of light elements to molten Fe,Ni metal, changes the properties of the fluid, lowering melting temperatures. This contributes to the formation and persistence of magnetic dynamos. Despite advances in seismometry and high-pressure experimental methods, including diamond anvil and shock wave experiments, the light element content of Earth’s liquid outer core is still uncertain. What is known is that light elements account for roughly 10 wt.% of the outer core, with S, Si, and O as likely primary light elements with the possibility of smaller amounts of C and H.

The protracted, and complex evolution of Earth’s active core contrasts with that of protoplanets. (4) Vesta and the parent bodies of the magmatic iron meteorites formed quickly (within about 10 Myr), with light elements partitioned into the core in chemical equilibrium with their mantles. The mineralogy of magmatic iron meteorites shows that C, O, S, and P were present in the cores of protoplanets, with S as the dominant light element. Analyses of trace element systematics show that the molten cores represented by these meteorites would have contained a wide range of S (approaching 20 wt.% S in some cases). As these cores cooled, light elements would have been enriched in an increasingly buoyant melt, potentially resulting in ferrovolcanism, causing the intrusion of S-rich magmas into the mantles of intact protoplanets or the eruption of these materials onto the surface of residual molten cores stripped of their outer silicate layers by collisions.

Constraints from the NASA Dawn mission and studies of meteorites originating from (4) Vesta, show that the smallest terrestrial planet is a mostly intact, fully differentiated world with a Fe,Ni core containing S, an ultramafic mantle, and basaltic crust. Moreover, studies of remanent magnetism in meteorites suggest (4) Vesta sustained an ancient dynamo. Our knowledge of asteroid (16) Psyche is limited. Earth based observations constrain the asteroid’s surface composition, mass, shape, and volume. These observations suggest an asteroid rich in metal, but with a lower bulk density than expected for a planetary core. This leaves open many possible pathways for (16) Psyche’s origins and development. The presence of metal implies formation in a reducing environment. (16) Psyche may be a homogeneous body accreted from primitive, metal-rich materials, a residual core exposed by collisions, or a fully intact, differentiated body with a large core. As Dawn did with (4) Vesta, the Psyche mission will enable a detailed investigation of the geology of (16) Psyche.

The Psyche spacecraft’s instruments are capable of characterizing sulfide mineralogy, determining the concentration of sulfur on (16) Psyche’s surface, and measuring remanent magnetic fields if a dynamo was active. (16) Psyche’s gravity investigation will provide constraints on interior structure and composition, placing limits on the thickness of silicate/sulfide layers and porosity. High resolution imagery will enable investigation of surface morphology, which may reveal evidence for ferrovolcanism.

The data acquired by the Psyche mission will enable further studies of the partitioning and segregation of light elements into and from the metal phase during accretion, core formation, and subsequent cooling. If (16) Psyche formed a core, then the Psyche mission will enable a first, direct investigation of core formation processes on a protoplanet, which will have implications for understanding the evolution of Earth’s early core. The Psyche mission’s Light Elements investigation will provide context for studies of iron meteorites and insights into the inner workings of the terrestrial planets.

Appendix

We used Bayesian inference with Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to sample from the posterior distribution of the parameters of a density model of (16) Psyche. This approach samples parameters that are consistent with (16) Psyche’s density given the observational uncertainties and prior information from reflectance spectroscopy, meteorite studies, and astrometric observations. The model was fitted to a recently reported value for (16) Psyche’s density 4172 ± 145 kg/m3 determined by high-precision astrometry (Farnocchia et al. 2024).

The density model considered a mixture of void (porosity), metal, troilite (FeS), and silicates. The metal fraction was represented by an Fe,Ni alloy free of impurities, with Ni allowed to vary up to 30 wt.%, consistent with the range for iron meteorites. The silicate component was modeled as olivine, with a linear mixture of forsterite (Mg) and fayalite (Fe) end members. This gives 6 parameters, including 4 for the metal, non-metal, and void fractions, and an additional 2 for the weight fraction of Ni in metal and the weight fraction of forsterite in olivine. Component densities were assumed to be 7870, 8900, 4600, 3220, and 4390 kg/m3 for iron metal, nickel metal, troilite, forsterite, and fayalite, respectively. Because porosity, metal, and non-metal fractions must sum to 1, the model effectively has 5 parameters, which were compared to a single observation. Since this system is underdetermined, a precise set of parameters that matches (16) Psyche’s density cannot be found. However, prior information is available to guide estimates of (16) Psyche’s bulk composition.

Cantillo et al. (2021) constrained (16) Psyche’s regolith composition using visible to near infrared spectra (0.35-2.5 μm), finding a best fit for 82.5% metal, 7% low Fe pyroxene, and 10.5% carbonaceous chondrite by mass. Their density inversion estimated a porosity of 35%, assuming the asteroid’s bulk composition was the same as the surface. In this study, we assumed that carbonaceous materials on (16) Psyche are accreted asteroid debris that contaminate the near surface and do not make up an appreciable portion of the bulk. Our ad hoc model includes only core material and silicates modeled as olivine, a mixture of forsterite and fayalite end members. Our initial guess at the silicate to metal fraction was taken to be the same as pyroxene to metal ratio found by Cantillo et al. (2021). Baer et al. (2011) estimated the porosity of 26 asteroids based on astrometric observations. They found that asteroids with radii below 300 km have a wide range of bulk porosities, ranging from about 5% to 60%, with a reported value for (16) Psyche of 10%. For our inversion, we started with the assumption that (16) Psyche’s porosity was 10% to represent a core that was exposed while it was still partially molten vs. a reaccreted rubble pile formed by the disruption of a solid body, which would be expected to have high porosity. We assumed the volume fraction of troilite was 0.2. Given the assumed porosity and silicate to metal ratio, this corresponds to roughly 6 wt.% S, within the range found in magmatic meteorites shown in Fig. 1.

MCMC methods

The differential evolution Markov Chain Monte Carlo (DE-MC) algorithm of Nelson et al. (2014) was used to draw samples given the log likelihood of the posterior distribution (Ter Braak 2006). The DE-MC algorithm operates on an ensemble of walkers. Jumps are determined by fixed multiples of differences between randomly selected parameter vectors, and the acceptance probability is determined using the Metropolis ratio, ensuring detailed balance with respect to the posterior distribution. The posterior distribution is proportional to the product of the likelihood and the probability density function for the prior. A Dirichlet distribution was used as the prior for the volume mixing fractions, from which the initial population of walkers was selected. The uncertainty in the density observation was modeled as a normal distribution with the quoted 1-sigma uncertainty. Solutions with parameter values outside their allowable range [0,1] were excluded, and the sum of the core/mantle material volume fractions was constrained to 1. The Markov Chain included 1000 walkers and sampled 20,000 steps, with an acceptance fraction of 25%. Convergence of the Markov Chain was assessed using batch means, with the autocorrelation time of 37(10), allowing most of the 20 million samples to be used following burn in. The reconstructed density was 4231 ± 143 kg/m3, consistent with the observational density constraint.
