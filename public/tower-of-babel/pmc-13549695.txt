Incidence of Pathogens and Evaluation of their Antibacterial Sensitivity among Iraqi cases with Inflammatory Acne
Authors: Fadhil Abbas Abdulrasool, Faten Al-Azzawi, Wisam Majeed Kattoof, Rabab Naeem Alag
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acne vulgaris (AV) is one of the most common skin diseases. It is caused by several types of bacterial genera that are resident in the human skin. P. acne in particular is one of the most important causes because it is found in the pilosebaceous units. This study aimed to isolate and identify the common bacteria that cause AV and test the antibiotic susceptibility.Materials:This study ran from April to October 2024. Samples of the study were taken from inflammatory papular and pustular acne lesions from 50 dermatology patients (22 males and 28 females) aged between 18 and 26 years.Results:Staph aureus was the most prevalent bacteria with (29) 43.28% and Staph epidermidis the least common with (3) 4.47%. Sometimes patients had both P. acne and Staphylococcal aureus (12 samples, 17.91%). Both species showed high sensitivity to levofloxacin in the top of the list and the strongest resistance to ampicillin on the other side.

Introduction

Acne vulgaris (AV) is a multifactorial chronic inflammatory skin disorder; it is the most common skin disease that affects the pilosebaceous follicles; it accounts 85% of adolescent and young adults (1E). It appears with clinical polymorphic lesions, which consist of non-inflammatory (open and close comedones) and inflammatory lesions (papules, pustules, and nodules). In untreated cases, scarring appears (1 IR). The pathogenesis of AV includes four major factors; they are seborrhea, hyperproliferation of the infundibulum, abnormality of the flora, and inflammation.[12] Although AV is not an infectious disease, previous studies reported that several microorganisms are implicated in the pathogenesis of the disease. P. acne, Staph. aureus, Malassezia furfur, and Micrococcus spp are reported.[3]

Propionibacterium acnes (P. acnes) is a microbe of considerable significance within the scientific community and is a type of bacteria that is Gram-positive, meaning it retains a purple stain in the Gram staining technique. It is also non-spore-forming, indicating that it does not produce specialised structures for survival in unfavourable conditions. Furthermore, it is an anaerobic bacillus, which means it thrives in environments with low or no oxygen.[4] These bacteria primarily reside in the sebaceous region, which refers to the areas of the body that contain sebaceous glands, such as the skin. Predominantly localised within sebaceous regions of the skin, P. acnes is well established in its role in instigating inflammatory lesions in acne vulgaris. It displays lipase activity, catalysing the conversion of triglycerides into free fatty acids.[567] The presence of free fatty acids promotes the aggregation and establishment of P. acnes bacteria, leading to an inflammatory response. This response is further potentiated through complement activation mediated by anti-Propionibacterium antibodies.[8]

The administration of antibiotics in the treatment of AV effectively hinders the proliferation of P. acnes bacteria and/or their synthesis of pro-inflammatory substances. Historically, P. acnes exhibited susceptibility to various antimicrobial agents, including clindamycin, erythromycin, and tetracyclins, until the late 1970s.[6]

Antibiotic modalities, encompassing both topical and systemic routes, have been integral to AV management for decades. However, reports of P. acnes strains demonstrating resistance to several antibiotic classes, notably aminoglycosides, mupirocin, and metronidazole, underscore evolving challenges in treatment paradigms.[7]

Material and Methods

This study was conducted during the period from April to October 2023. It was approved by the ethical committee in the medical college/university of Misan. Samples were collected from 50 patients (22 males and 28 females) attending to the dermatology and venereology department in Al-sader teaching hospital, Misan. Inclusion criteria: Any young adult aged between 18 and 25 years complaining for inflammatory AV without any associated other dermatological problems such as seborrhiec dermatitis, rosacea, and drug-induced acne. Pregnant women, breastfeeding, patients with polycystic ovary syndrome or hyperandrogenism, and those who receive systemic or topical antibiotics for one last month were excluded from the study. Samples of the study were taken from inflammatory popular and pustular acne lesions. Patients were diagnosed with AV by history taking and physical examination.

Bacteriological study

Sterile needles, disposable sterile cotton swabs, and transport media were used for this purpose. The site of the lesion was first wiped with 70% alcohol and then with 0.5% iodine; the contents of the pustules were collected after being ruptured with a sterile needle. Samples were immediately transported to the microbiology laboratory by using cotton swabs with Amies transport media (Merk, Darmstadt, Germany).[9]

All samples were cultured on blood agar media (two plates for each sample) and subjected to both aerobic and anaerobic growth. For aerobic growth, samples were incubated at 37°C overnight for primary isolation, while for anaerobic growth, specimens reach the lab. The process occurred in less than an hour in the transport tube, where it was incubated for 5 days at 20–25 centigrade degree under anaerobic conditions (mechanical exclusion of oxygen: anaerobic incubator), which was performed by using AnaeroGen; Oxoid Ltd, Basingstoke, UK (2); after that, the growth was examined.

Pure individual colonies from both aerobic and anaerobic growth were identified by morphological and standard biochemical tests (Api20) (BioMerieux).[7]

Antibiotic susceptibility

Antibiotic susceptibility was performed by the disc diffusion method; commercially available discs for (Ampicillin, Erythromycin, Ciprofloxacin, Tetracycline, Clindamycin, Azithromycin, Rifampicin, Levofloxacin, Doxycycline, Trimethoprim/Sulfamethoxazole, Amoxicillin/Clavulanic acid) antibiotics were used (antibiotic discs; Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) according to the CLSI 2022 guidelines. The standardised Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method was performed on Mueller-Hinton agar (Himedia). Inoculates were adjusted to a 0.5 MacFarland standard from an aerobic and anaerobic pure culture on blood agar. Mueller-Hinton agar plates were inoculated and incubated at 37°C for 24 h aerobic and for 5 to 7 days anaerobically.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis in the present study was performed by using SPSS program, and the results were expressed as a percentage (%).

Discussion

AV presents a multifaceted challenge, involving chronic inflammation in pilosebaceous units primarily found in seborrheic areas like the face, neck, chest, and upper back. The primary reason, Cutibacterium acne, has historically been targeted with antibiotics, both topical and systemic, to alleviate symptoms. However, the efficacy of this approach is increasingly hampered by the emergence of antibiotic-resistant strains.

Despite antibiotics like clindamycin, macrolides, and tetracyclines traditionally being effective against Cutibacterium acne, their overuse has led to a rise in resistant strains globally. This resistance not only compromises treatment outcomes but also heightens the risk of recurrent outbreaks. Moreover, recent studies have identified other microorganisms, such as Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis, which exhibit varying responses to antibiotics, further complicating treatment decisions.[101112]

Previously, Cutibacterium acne was regarded the most causative anaerobic microbe in the pathogeneses of AV exacerbations; in the present study, from a total of 50 AV patients, other organisms had been identified, like staphylococcal aures, (29) Cutibbacterium acne (23), and staphylococcal epidermidis (3), with great differences to antibiotic response, and this reflected on the successfulness of therapy. Therefore, before prescribing any oral antibiotics for acne, history of topical and systemic antibiotic usage must be taken.

The most affected age group prone to have AV was 17–28 years, with predominantly female gender in the present study, and this is consistent with other previous studies.[9]

In respect to the types of bacterial species, in the present report, three types were found with different percentages, mainly staphylococcal aureus 29, then cutibacterium acne 23, and last staphylococcal epidermidis 3 mostly mixed with cutibacterium acne, in contrast to the study conducted by Iranians that showed P. acne 41%, then staphylococcal epidermidis 34%, and the least staphylococcal aureus 5% from samples collected from the face.

An Egyptian study showed the most common AV bacteria was mainly staphylococcal epidermidis (44%), followed by P. acne (35%) and then staph. Aereus (11%). Loena Sari et al. also report the most common aerobic bacteria in AV lesion was staphylococcal epidermidis (47.5%), then staph. aeruis (8.7%), while anaerobic bacteria was P. acne (21.2%), and the most common mixed growth was P. acne and staphylococcal epidermidis (13.7%). Variations in geographical regions may influence the bacteria implicated in AV, thus accounting for differences in bacterial distribution among acne cases. Current research has pointed to the idea that C. acnes and S. epidermidis have a symbiotic relationship. Both bacteria exist on the normal flora of the skin, and a disrupt in balance of these bacteria on the skin can result in acne or other bacterial infections.[13] Whether this is a root causality, just opportunistic and a side effect, or a more complex pathological duality between C. acnes and this particular Staphylococcus species is not known.[14] Antibiotics are commonly employed in treating acne, serving either as bactericidal agents or as anti-inflammatory agents. However, the widespread use of antibiotics has led to the emergence of resistant strains of Propionibacterium acnes (P. acnes), a key bacterium involved in acne pathology.

An interesting paper from Iran in 2008 shows that P. acnes (Cutibacterium acne), the most common microbe isolate taken from AV patients, was sensitive to Azithromycin (98.3%), Doxycycline (98.3%), and tetracycline (93.1%) and the least sensitive to cotrimazole (69%) and clindamycin (56.9%). About 31% of the P. acnes (Cutibacterium acne) was resistant at least to one antibiotic. In the present study regarding the P. acnes (Cutibacterium acne), the sensitivity to the following drugs in ascending order was to Azithromycin (52.17%), Erythromycin (56.52%), Trimethoprim/Sulfamethoxazole (56.52%), Doxycycline (69.56%), clindamycin (73.915), and tetracycline (82.6%); this change in the sensitivity can explain by the revolution of antibiotics with time. However, on the other hand, our results showed that Levofloxacin and Ciprofloxacin were the most sensitive treatments in acne for both. Both of them were (96.55%) and P. acnes, 95.65% and 86.95%, respectively.[15]

According to an Egyptian study regarding the antibiotic susceptibility patterns of P. acnes (Cutibacterium acne) patients with AV, the results showed high sensitivity rates of levofloxacin (80%) than doxycycline (51.4%) and highly resistant to the following drugs in descending order: clindamycin (85.7%), erythromycin (82.9%), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (80%), and tetracycline (62.9%).[1617] In comparison with the present results, there is a similar finding but with a higher rate of sensitivity regarding levofloxacin (95.65%) and doxycycline (69.56%) and a lower rate of resistance regarding clindamycin (26%), erythromycin (43.47%), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (43.47%), and tetracycline (17.39%).

The reason for the difference in the in vitro antibiotic susceptibility patterns of P. acnes among different countries is not clear, although it may be contributed to the different antibiotic prescribing habits and⁄or different ethnicities of the patients.[181920]

Although the exact cause of P. acnes resistance to antibiotics is not fully understood, it can be related to the inability to obtain in vivo, in the sebaceous follicle of acne patients, a concentration of antibiotic greater than the minimum inhibitory concentration.[21] P. acnes resistant to antibiotics may be also related to poor treatment compliance, and easy access to therapeutic agents without medical supervision.

Prolonged use of antibiotics is also one of the factors that have contributed to the development of antibiotic resistance.[2223]

However, further studies are recommended to assess the in vivo efficacy of this antibiotic in treatment of inflammatory acne to prove its validity and to exclude the possibility of emergence of cross-resistance among different types antibiotics.

Conclusion

Overall, the susceptibility patterns of P. acnes in vitro varied among Iraqi individuals with AV in relation to routinely administered antibiotics (in our results, the antibiotic susceptibility/sensitivity to Cutibacterium acnes isolate was different/changed from previous studies, with high sensitivity to levofloxacllin, ciprofloxacillin, and tetracyclin and more resistance to ampicillin, amoxilin/clavulinic acid, and azithromycin), with high sensitivity of Cutibacterium acnes to more than one drug, levofloxacllin, ciprofloxacillin, and tetracyclin and high resistance to ampicillin and amoxilin/clavulinic acid and in between to azithromycin, erythromycin, and trim-sulfa. Regarding bacteria isolate in AV, the two most common are Staphylococcus aureus and Cutibacterium acnes and the least Staphylococcus epidermidis, which mostly existed in mixed growth with Cutibacterium acnes. Therefore, more research is needed to determine the effectiveness of these antibiotics in treating acne in real-life situations. We suggest that this study should be extended to include a broader sample of acne patients from various regions in Iraq to evaluate the clinical effectiveness of antibiotics that are susceptible to P. acnes in vitro, to give us the better strategy in the treatment protocol of AV to decrease the failure rate and bad complications of this disease. Last, we recommend to survey large study groups with different ages.
