Insights of nurses regarding healthcare waste management: a cross-sectional, district-level study in hospitals selected from a peripheral region of Sri Lanka
Authors: T. Thaslim Musthak, N. T. Bhavantha Dias, K. A. Ayomi Dilhari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImproper management of healthcare waste poses significant health and environmental hazards. Nurses handle healthcare waste during their day-to-day service, yet data on awareness and practices regarding this matter are limited. This study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding healthcare waste management (HWM) among nurses working in selected hospitals in the Trincomalee district.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional, questionnaire-based study was carried out among 209 nurses employed in the Trincomalee district. The ‘good’ and ‘poor’ levels of knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding HWM were assessed by a predetermined scoring method. The associations of the level of knowledge, attitudes, and practices with the basic demographics of the participants were analysed separately at the 0.05 significance level.ResultsNearly 87% of the nurses were able to correctly define HWM. Only 32.5% had attended training programmes on HWM. A strong association was observed between participation in training programmes and increased knowledge of HWM (Pearson chi-square test; p = 0.011). Almost 64% of the participants demonstrated a strong willingness to adhere to safe waste-disposal protocols, however only nearly half of the nurses (49.8%) demonstrated good practices related to the use of personal protective equipment. Around 55% of respondents further showed satisfactory waste disposal techniques. However, no significant associations were detected between any of the reported demographics and the levels of practices (p > 0.05). In the overall analysis, respondents with good knowledge (55.1%), positive attitudes (70.8%), and good practices (57.9%) constituted the majority of the sample compared to those with poor knowledge, negative attitudes, or inadequate practices.ConclusionsA substantial proportion of nurses possess good knowledge, positive attitudes, and reasonable practices regarding HWM. Training sessions have had a significant impact on enhancing knowledge and attitudes of nurses toward HWM. Therefore, continuous education and training are important to improve compliance with environmental and health standards.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13807-1.

Background

Healthcare waste management (HWM) is the process of collecting, transporting, treating, and disposing of the waste generated from a wide range of activities in healthcare facilities, including diagnostic procedures, surgery, and patient care [1]. Healthcare facilities produce two main types of waste: non-hazardous “general trash” and hazardous “healthcare risk waste”. The latter accounts for 10% to 25% of the waste, including sharps, human body parts, blood, chemical wastes, and pharmaceuticals [2, 3]. Healthcare waste is produced primarily by hospitals, laboratories, mortuaries, and other places where health-related activities are performed. Improper disposal of blood-tinted sharps makes it easier for different viruses (i.e., hepatitis B and C, HIV) to spread [4] and infect healthcare professionals, patients, people in support services, visitors to facilities, waste collectors and pickers, and the general public [5].

Poor handling and improper disposal of healthcare waste can have detrimental effects not only on human health but also on the environment by contaminating water and soil, leading to pollution [6]. At the global scale, efficient management of healthcare waste is still a significant issue in developing nations such as Sri Lanka [7]. Colombo, the most urbanised district of Sri Lanka, has the highest rate of healthcare waste generation (26.8%), whereas all the other districts have different non-zero rates, demanding strict monitoring of HWM [8, 9].

Improving the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAPs) of healthcare professionals is one of the effective approaches in HWM and it includes training, continuous education, and the provision of adequate resources. Despite the existing guidelines, several studies have shown that there is a strong need to improve the practices of nurses towards healthcare waste in Sri Lanka to efficiently handle the clinical waste crisis in hospitals. Previous studies conducted at some other local settings have revealed that the majority of hospitals staff show unsatisfactory HWM practices, despite the availability of sufficient facilities. These studies identified several shortcomings in sustainable waste management, including inadequate segregation, financial constraints, weak infrastructure, and limited awareness [10, 11].

Sri Lanka has made good progress in improving health indicators overall; however, remote regions such as the Eastern Province of the country face a unique set of health challenges compared with other regions. The region was once heavily affected by protracted conflicts, and the rural setting of the province often creates more challenges [12]. The prolonged periods of conflict have disrupted infrastructure development for decades and strained the public health services. These challenges have contributed to persistent disparities in healthcare quality and waste management systems in the region. Predominant rural setting and geographical distance from the capital have worsen the inadequate access to up-to-date waste treatment facilities [13].

In resource-limited settings like peripheral regions of Sri Lanka, improper handling of medical waste poses significant risks of infection and environmental contamination. Nurses, being at the forefront of patient care, are one of the primary generators and segregators of healthcare waste. Thus assessing their KAP is fundamental to a safe healthcare system. Gaps in the understanding of nurses regarding waste identification, segregation protocols (such as the national colour code system), and safe disposal can lead to a breakdown in the waste management chain. The present cross-sectional study aimed to fill an important knowledge gap by providing insights into the HWM realities faced by nurses in a peripheral disrict in Sri Lanka, thereby establishing a necessary baseline for developing targeted, evidence-based training and policy interventions to enhance both occupational and public safety.

Materials and methods

Study design and sampling

This study was a questionnaire-based, multi-centred, cross-sectional study. It was carried out at selected three hospitals in Trincomalee district, Sri Lanka: Trincomalee General Hospital, Mutur Base Hospital, and Thoppur Divisional Hospital, from April to July 2023. These hospitals differ in bed capacity, staff strength (including number of nurses employed), and services provided representing tertiary, secondary, and primary healthcare facility levels, respectively. The study population included nursing officers who were employed in hospitals in the Trincomalee district. The study aimed to recruit 242 participants, representing approximately one-third of the total nursing population (659 ÷ 3 ≈ 220), plus an additional 10% to account for non-respondents (= 22). However, the study could only recruit 209 nursing officers via convenience sampling. The sample included both male and female registered nursing officers who were actively occupied in their duties at the time of this study. Nursing officers who were unavailable (due to long-term leave or any other reason) during the data collection period, nursing trainees, and other healthcare workers were excluded from the study.

Data collection

A structured, self-administered questionnaire was developed and pre-tested by the investigators (supplementary 1). The questionnaire items were developed by the review of published studies related to the KAP on hospital waste management among healthcare professionals, including nurses [14, 15]. The content validity of the initial draft was subsequently reviewed by a senior scientist specialized in public health research and a nursing officer who was not part of the study population. The questionnaire was used to obtain data on KAP related to HWM (which were the dependent variables) and the independent variables including age, gender, educational level, work experience, residential area, and participation in training programmes. The self-administered questionnaire was distributed to the hospital nurses in the selected hospitals via ward matrons under the supervision of the investigators. Nurses were informed about the ongoing study through their social media groups. The sample cohort was provided with an adequate period of three months to complete the questionnaire. All the participants had the freedom to either voluntarily accept or repel their participation in the study. Names and other sensitive information were not requested from the participants. They were provided with information sheets in their preferred language (Sinhala/Tamil/English), and informed written consent which was signed by the participant was obtained individually. Further, this study involved multiple visits to the selected hospitals over a 3-month period to facilitate data collection and observation.

Scoring of the responses

The assignment technique was used to determine the HWM-related KAP score by explicitly judging each question. Each item assessing knowledge, attitudes, and practices was scored as ‘1’ for a correct response and ‘0’ for an incorrect response, and the total score was obtained by summing these individual item scores. The knowledge section had a maximum score of 40 points, whereas the attitudes and practices sections had maximum scores of 20 and 42 points, respectively. Individuals whose score was greater than the mean score were considered to have “good” knowledge/attitudes/practices. Conversely, “poor” knowledge/attitudes/practices were considered if any individual had a score less than the mean score for the respective item.

Data analysis

Descriptive data were presented as frequencies and percentages. The data were analysed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS), version 22. Pearson chi-square test was performed to determine the statistical significance between the percentage of nursing officers with good and poor KAP. The association of KAP score with demographics of participants was analysed by the chi-square test for independence. All the analyses were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Socio-demographic statistics of the participants

Among all the nursing officers working in the selected hospitals in Trincomalee district, the majority (63.2%) presented from Trincomalee General Hospital, 68 (32.5%) were from Muthur Base Hospital, and 9 (4.3%) were from Thoppur Divisional Hospital. Out of all the participants, approximately 93% were females, and males constituted a minority. The mean age of the participants in the study group was 31.75 ± 6.46 years. The majority were Sinhalese, considering the race of the respondents. Most of the nursing officers were diploma holders, and only around 4% of the nursing officers were university graduates. Approximately two third had never attended a training programme related to HWM, and of those who had attended training sessions, 29.2% had attended 1 or 2 training sessions, and the others had attended 3 or more training sessions. In addition, most of the participants had less than 8 years of working experience in the healthcare sector (Table 1).

Knowledge regarding healthcare waste management

Among all the nursing officers, a major percentage of nurses were able to correctly define the HWM. Nurses’ knowledge of HWM was assessed under four subcategories: waste type, handling, segregation, and waste disposal.

Under the “types of healthcare waste” category, more than 50% of nurses identified pathological waste, sharps waste, pharmaceutical waste, cytotoxic waste, radioactive waste, and non-hazardous or general waste as major types of healthcare waste. With respect to the “handling of healthcare waste”, more than 70% of the nurses reported that personal protective equipment (PPE) should be worn when healthcare waste is handled. Furthermore, more than 70% of nurses responded that sharps-inflicted injuries and infections (HIV/AIDS, hepatitis B and C) are associated with healthcare waste. Nearly 76% of the nurses reported that poor HWM is a risk to health workers (Table 2).

With respect to the “segregation of waste”, the majority were aware of the bin colour codes for different waste streams. Of them, more than 80% knew that glass waste is collected into red bins, whereas more than 55% were conscious of other bin colour codes for different waste categories, such as infectious waste (yellow), general waste (black), and biodegradable waste (green). Under the “disposal of waste”, nearly 83% of nurses identified that the disposal of untreated healthcare waste in can lead to contamination of surface and ground water, whereas majority knew that inadequate incineration results in various environmental issues (Table 2).

Attitudes towards healthcare waste management

Attitudes of the nurses regarding HWM were assessed under two subcategories: perceptions of the importance of proper waste management and willingness to follow HWM guidelines.

Under the “perception of importance” category, more than 75% of the nurses believed that strict implementation of guidelines is necessary for proper HWM, whereas a similar percentage believed that nurses should personally adhere to proper waste management practices during their routine work. Around one third of nurses believed that segregating medical waste ensures safe management of hospital waste. However, over 90% of the participants indicated that workload and associated pressure often lead to shortcuts being taken in HWM (Table 3).

Regarding “willingness to follow HWM guidelines”, substantial proportion of the nurses were keen on following guidelines and protocols for waste management daily. Interestingly, no significant difference (p = 0.189) was observed between those who agreed and disagreed with the statement: “It is good to get feedback and evaluations related to waste management practices to identify areas for improvement”. However, the majority of participants believed that nurses should be provided with continuous education and training to stay up to date with best practices on proper HWM (Table 3).

Practices in dealing with healthcare waste management

Practices of the nurses regarding HWM were assessed under four subcategories: use of PPE, collection and storage, disposal methods, and adherence to standard operating procedures/practices. The findings revealed that a simple majority of the respondents agreed that adequate quantities of PPE were available in their workplace, whereas around 56% believed that PPE was being disposed safely and appropriately after use. Considering the subcategory “collection and storage” of waste, only nearly half of the participants reported that their workplace had sufficient resources (such as bins, bags, and containers) for managing healthcare waste. However, marginal proportion of the nurses indicated that waste segregation practices were regularly monitored at their workplaces, and approximately 56% reported that the number of staff employed to handle waste was insufficient (Table 4).

Furthermore, half of the respondents mentioned that liquid waste in their workplace has been collected together with other waste. Scoring results of the knowledge, attitudes, and practices

The mean was used as the cut-off, since the median and mode values were comparable. Moreover, the distribution of the scores approximated normality. The mean overall knowledge score of the participants was 28.80 ± 11.71. Therefore, individuals with a score of ≥ 28.80 were considered to have “good” knowledge, whereas “poor” knowledge was considered when an individual had a score of less than 28.80 of the total score. The mean overall attitudes score of the participants was 10.89 ± 5.10. Therefore, individuals with a score of ≥ 10.89 were considered to have “positive” attitudes, whereas “negative” attitudes were considered when an individual had a score of less than 10.89 of the total score. The mean score for the overall practices of the participants was 24.22 ± 12.16. Therefore, individuals with a score of ≥ 24.22 were considered to have “good” practices, whereas “poor” practices were considered when an individual had a score of less than 24.22 of the total score. There were notable differences in the number of nurses with “good/ positive” versus “poor/ negative” KAP in the overall assessment and across each subcategory (Table 5).

Factors affecting the level of knowledge, attitudes, and practices

There was a statistically significant association (p = 0.011) between participation in training sessions and the level of knowledge of waste management. However, no association was observed between other selected factors (gender, education level, residential area, and work experience) and the level of knowledge. Furthermore, a significant association (p = 0.011) was observed between the level of attitudes and education level. Nevertheless, there were no statistically significant associations with any of the tested demographics with the level of nurses’ practices in HWM (Table 6).

Discussion

Nurses are custodians of proper HWM in hospitals, and their role in preventing spread of infection and disease through clinical waste is undeniable [16]. Approximately 45% of the nurses interviewed in this study had a good level of overall knowledge of HWM, indicating that a substantial proportion of the cohort possessed a sound understanding of the basic concepts associated with the management of healthcare waste. Strengths and weaknesses for improvement were revealed by further breaking down knowledge levels in particular HWM domains. This study further established that most of the nursing officers had good knowledge of the segregation of different waste types. A recent Sri Lankan study has reported that most of the subject rural hospitals from Ampara district were equipped with adequate physical facilities for waste segregation, despite their effective usage being unsatisfactory. The study suggests more financial allocations and recruiting officers dedicated for HWM [10]. According to another study, nurses and laboratory staff in Allahabad, India had a better understanding of waste colour coding and segregation compared to doctors [17]. However, a study conducted in Iraq revealed a low average level of knowledge among healthcare workers in relation to medical waste management. The study proposes that the lack of strict regulations and training would be the underlining cause for the negative observation [18].

In the present study, the positive outlook of a substantial portion of the nursing cohort reflected a genuine commitment to understanding the importance of and willingness to adhere to established protocols. Similar to the findings of the present investigation, it has been reported previously that a significant proportion of nurses demonstrated positive attitudes toward HWM in India. The study has suggested providing comprehensive training focused on areas such as waste segregation, importance of following strict guidelines, and reporting waste-based injuries is essential to improve the attitudes on proper waste management [19]. The findings of the present study indicated that the majority of nurses demonstrate good practices towards the handling of healthcare waste. A similar study done in a designated hospital, Colombo, Sri Lanka has reported a similar observation, the nurses had the best HWM practices among all the hospital staff. In the same study, it has been noted that adhering to good practices on waste management by the staff was outstanding in in-patient units (such as intensive care unit, clinical wards, etc.) compared to out-patient divisions [20]. Another study conducted in several government hospitals in Colombo district highlighted poor waste management practices in healthcare facilities, which recommended the establishing management committees and HWM plans for each hospital to mitigate the waste handling challenges [21].

It is essential to encourage the use of PPE to shield healthcare workers from potential hazards associated with waste, in order to confirm effective HWM. Adequate collection and storage facilities are necessary to prevent cross-contamination and ensure the safe handling of waste [22]. Proper disposal methods, such as incineration or other appropriate treatment methods minimise the environmental and public health risks. Adherence to strict standard operating procedures is also required to comply with established guidelines and protocols [23].

The present study revealed significant links between participation in training sessions and HWM knowledge as well as the academic backgrounds of nurses and their attitudes toward HWM. Numerous prior studies from international settings have consistently highlighted the positive impact of training on HWM knowledge, which is a reliable predictor of improved knowledge levels among healthcare workers [24, 25]. Although nurses possess greater knowledge of HWM, translating this knowledge into practice may not be easily achievable. The absence of a significant association between practices and training sessions is perhaps due to the poor curriculum of training programmes, which mainly focuses on theory, instead of hands-on experiences. The hands-on practical sessions should be incorporated along with the theory lessons or discussions [26, 27]. The absence of a significant association between practices and training sessions further implies that HWC practices are perhaps more shaped by institutional elements rather than demographic attributes (including participation to training sessions). Moreover, evidence suggests that investment in comprehensive training initiatives as well as regular monitoring and supervision are essential to guarantee that healthcare professionals are adequately prepared to manage healthcare waste properly [28].

Limitations

The study relied on a self-administered questionnaire, which may be subject to social desirability bias and inaccurate self-assessment. Since the study was conducted in a single peripheral district using convenient sampling, the findings are not generalisable to all nurses in the district, or nurses working in other regions of Sri Lanka. Urban hospitals usually benefit from better access to technology and facilities. Therefore, differences in institutional resource availability should also be considered when interpreting the results. Moreover, the study did not differentiate between the type/ level of hospital in the analysis of data from selected three hospitals, which may further affect the final outcome. Different levels of hospitals acquire varying levels of resources (both physical and human) and administrative procedures. Tertiary hospitals are generally better equipped with trained staff, infrastructure, and formalized waste management systems, whereas primary-level hospitals often face limitations in manpower and facilities. Recognizing these institutional differences is important for interpreting the findings. Additionally, the present study used score mean in statistical analysis. The mean is a relative, internally derived measure commonly used in KAP studies, and it does not rely on an external or predetermined benchmark. Consequently, the categorization does not reflect variations against an absolute standard of performance. This limitation should be considered when comparing the current findings with studies conducted in different other contexts.

Recommendations

It is recommended to develop and enforce localised HWM guidelines that are adaptable to the resources and challenges of peripheral healthcare settings (such as waste management plans of local governments). Regular training sessions on healthcare waste management should be introduced to bridge knowledge-practice gaps and update nurses on evolving standards and guidelines. Future research should include hospitals from both urban and other rural areas across Sri Lanka to enable comparative analysis and improve the external validity of findings. Combination of self-reported data with direct observation of waste management practices would provide a more accurate picture of real-world issues.

Conclusions

A substantial proportion of nurses from Trincomalee district, Sri Lanka, who participated in the study displayed good knowledge, positive attitudes, and reasonably good practices regarding HWM. Participation in training sessions significantly influenced knowledge levels, whereas the educational background of nurses significantly impacted their attitudes toward healthcare management. Further interventions and continuous education with proper practical exposure may enhance practices and bridge existing gaps in HWM among nurses.
