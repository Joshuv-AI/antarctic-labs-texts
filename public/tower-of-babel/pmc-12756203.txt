Mapping the landscape of predictive biomarkers for immune checkpoint inhibitors a bibliometric analysis
Authors: Xiaodong Wang, Jing He, Gouping Ding, Yixuan Tang, Qianqian Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis large-scale bibliometric analysis maps the global research landscape of predictive biomarkers for immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) from 2011 to 2025. Leveraging 9,075 publications from the Web of Science Core Collection, we used co-citation, co-authorship, and keyword co-occurrence analyses to quantify publication dynamics, collaborative networks, and conceptual evolution. China produced the most publications (1,923, a country-level count reflecting multi-national co-authorship), while the United States led in influence as reflected by high-impact institutions (e.g., MD Anderson Cancer Center) and prolific authors (e.g., Kurzrock R, H-index 116). The strongest international collaboration was between the USA and China (276 co-authored publications). Thematic evolution revealed a paradigm shift from reliance on single biomarkers (e.g., PD-L1, tumor mutational burden [TMB]) toward integrated multi-omics signatures that incorporate tumor microenvironment features and advanced computational approaches. Keyword analysis highlighted artificial intelligence (n = 640), radiomics, and liquid biopsy as emerging frontiers. Notably, gastroesophageal junction cancers exhibited the strongest citation burst (strength = 11.53), highlighting unresolved tumor-specific controversies such as the predictive validity of PD-L1 in this setting. However, significant translational barriers remain: lack of biomarker assay standardization, high analytical variability (e.g., differing PD-L1 immunohistochemistry clones and inconsistent TMB cutoff thresholds), and insufficient clinical validation. This study provides an evidence-based overview to guide future research toward multi-omics integration, prospective validation, and cross-disciplinary collaboration, thereby advancing precision immuno-oncology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04137-z.Keywords: Predictive biomarkers, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, Tumor mutational burden, Artificial intelligence, Gastroesophageal junction cancer, Precision immuno-oncology

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed cancer therapy, producing durable remissions in some patients. However, only a minority of patients derive long-term benefit, and many experience primary or acquired resistance, underscoring the need for predictive biomarkers to guide therapy selection [1–3]. Predictive biomarkers can help identify those most likely to respond to ICIs while sparing others from unnecessary toxicity [4].

Several biomarkers are used or proposed to inform ICI therapy [5]. Tumor expression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) by immunohistochemistry is an established predictor in certain cancers (e.g., non-small cell lung cancer, NSCLC), and high PD-L1 levels have been associated with improved outcomes on PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in clinical trials [4, 6]. Similarly, mismatch repair deficiency or high microsatellite instability (dMMR/MSI-H) and high tumor mutational burden (TMB) have been linked to better ICI efficacy, leading to biomarker-guided regulatory approvals [4, 7].

Despite their promise, current ICI biomarkers face significant translational barriers. Analytical variability is a major challenge: for example, PD-L1 assay results can differ widely depending on the antibody clone, scoring method, and tumor sampling site, leading to inconsistent patient stratification [6, 8]. For example, in NSCLC a high PD-L1 tumor proportion score predicted benefit from pembrolizumab in one study but not in another using a different ICI [4] underscoring inconsistent predictive value. Likewise, there is no universal standard for “TMB-high”; thresholds vary by sequencing platform and cancer type, and a cutoff like 10 mutations/Mb may not predict response in all tumors [9, 10]. Lack of assay harmonization and tumor heterogeneity further limit the reproducibility and generalizability of biomarker findings. These issues highlight the need for standardized, validated assays and tumor-specific calibration of biomarker cutoffs in order to translate biomarkers into routine practice.

Beyond these validated markers, many other potential biomarkers have been explored but remain unvalidated. For instance, high densities of intratumoral B cells within tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) have been proposed to predict favorable ICI response [11]. Likewise, gut microbiome composition has been correlated with differences in ICI outcomes [3]. While these findings suggest intriguing biological contributors to immunotherapy efficacy, they are largely observational. Such factors are still under investigation and have not yet achieved the status of predictive biomarkers [3]. Distinguishing which of these emerging biomarkers reflect causal, validated mechanisms versus correlative associations is an ongoing challenge in the field.

Reflecting a paradigm shift in biomarker research, current efforts are moving toward multi-omics and artificial intelligence (AI)-driven strategies to improve predictive power [12, 13]. Multi-omics approaches integrate genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and other data to capture the complex determinants of immunotherapy response, often using machine learning models to identify composite biomarker signatures. Early studies indicate that combining features from multiple modalities can improve prediction of ICI outcomes compared to single markers [4]. However, these advanced strategies remain largely investigational and require extensive validation in prospective trials.

Given the rapidly evolving, interdisciplinary nature of this field, a systematic overview of the research landscape is needed. In this study, we conducted a bibliometric analysis to map the landscape of predictive biomarker research for ICIs. Our aim was to quantify publication trends, identify influential studies and emerging themes, and highlight knowledge gaps. This bibliometric approach provides insights into the evolution of biomarker research and may inform future directions in precision immunotherapy.

We further confined results to English-language publications classified as original research articles or review articles. Visualization and statistical analysis

All bibliometric analyses were performed using established software tools. We utilized R [14, 15] (version 4.5.1) for data handling and statistical computation, in conjunction with the Bibliometrix R package [16] (v5.0.0) for specific bibliometric calculations. Key publication metrics (such as annual publication counts, citation counts, and keyword frequencies) were computed and visualized using VOSviewer [17, 18] (1.6.20). VOSviewer’s built-in clustering algorithms were used to generate co-authorship networks and keyword co-occurrence maps, where nodes represent items (e.g., keywords or authors) and edges represent collaborative or co-occurrence relationships. These network visualizations allowed identification of major research clusters and thematic areas in the field. Co-authorship and co-occurrence analyses formed the core of our methodology, enabling us to examine collaboration patterns across countries, institutions, and individual authors.

We also employed CiteSpace [19, 20] (6.4.R1) for advanced trend analysis. CiteSpace was used to perform co-citation analysis of references and to detect citation bursts for keywords, which are indicators of emerging topics gaining rapid attention. The time slicing in CiteSpace was set from 2011 to 2025 (1-year per slice), aligning with our study period. This temporal parameter allowed us to track the evolution of research interests and highlight periods of rapid development within the field. Notably, the citation burst analysis in CiteSpace identified which keywords showed significantly increased citation frequency over short intervals, thereby pinpointing recent hotspots in ICI biomarker research. All visualizations (network maps, overlays, and burst timelines) were generated using the aforementioned software tools. We emphasize that these software packages (R, VOSviewer, and CiteSpace) were used for data analysis and visualization in this study (they are established tools and were not outcomes or products of our work).

Results

Research profile

Based on the comprehensive literature search and screening process (Fig. 1), we identified a total of 9,075 eligible publications on predictive biomarkers for ICIs spanning 2011 to 2025. Bibliometric analysis of the dataset (Supplementary Fig. 1) further characterizes the landscape: in total, the 9,075 publications encompassed 9,973 unique author keywords and cited 239,202 references. The mean number of authors per article was approximately 9–10, underscoring the collaborative nature of this field. On average, each paper has accrued about 34 citations, and the average cited half-life of publications in this domain is 3.2 years, indicating that references tend to cite studies from roughly the past three years.

Analysis of national publication counts

Analysis of publication output by country (Supplementary Fig. 2B) highlights the leading national contributors to this research field. China is the top contributor with 1,923 publications, followed by the United States with 1,029 publications. Other major contributing countries include Japan (700), Italy (684), Germany (567), Spain (532), France (513), the United Kingdom (220), South Africa (148), and Australia (135). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2A, a world map color-coded by publication density, China is clearly visible as the most prolific country, consistent with its leading publication count (1,923 articles). The United States is the only other country in this top tier, whereas the remaining nations are depicted in lighter shades corresponding to their lower output.

Beyond publication counts, we examined international collaboration relationships among countries (Supplementary Fig. 3). China’s leading role is also evident in these networks: the most frequent bilateral collaboration is between China and the United States, with 276 co-authored publications. This is followed by substantial collaborative ties between the United States and Italy (250 co-publications) and between the United States and United Kingdom (210). To further map the global collaboration structure, we performed a co-authorship network analysis of countries. Figure 2A depicts the network of 65 countries/regions with ≥ 5 publications (covering 64% of all 101 countries in the dataset), grouped into seven distinct clusters of collaborating nations. In this network, each node represents a country (node size proportional to its publication volume) and each link indicates collaboration strength (with color/width denoting the frequency of co-authorship). The largest cluster (shown in red in Fig. 2A) comprises 15 countries, reflecting a highly interconnected group of prolific contributors. The temporal overlay in Fig. 2B adds a time dimension: node colors correspond to the mean publication year of each country’s papers. This reveals, for example, that the United States and its close collaborators had a concentration of publication activity around early 2021, whereas countries like India and Saudi Arabia show more intense engagement in the most recent years of the study period.

Analysis of institution publications

To investigate the contributions of different institutions to ICI biomarker research, we analyzed global publication output across organizations. This domain currently involves approximately 8,661 contributing institutions worldwide. Supplementary Fig. 4 displays the top 20 institutions by publication volume, with each bar now labeled by its exact publication count for clarity. Chinese institutions dominate this elite group (12 of the top 20), followed by the United States (7 institutions) and France (1). The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center (USA) leads with 287 publications, whereas Mayo Clinic (USA) ranks 20th with just over 100.

Inter-institutional collaboration patterns were further examined via co-authorship network analysis. In contrast, more recent years have seen a surge in output and collaborations by Chinese institutions. This evolving trend, visualized by the color gradient in Fig. 3B, underscores the rising prominence of China in the ICI biomarker field even as U.S. institutions maintain a foundational presence.

Research hotspot analysis

Most cited publications

Citation analysis reveals several seminal contributions that have shaped the predictive biomarker landscape for ICIs. Table 3 lists the top 10 most-cited papers in this field, each accumulating over 1,690 citations (ranging from 1,692 to 3,452). The highest-cited work is Jiang et al. (Nat Med 2018; 3,452 citations), which established transcriptomic signatures of T cell dysfunction and exclusion as predictors of immunotherapy response [24]. Notably, tumor mutational burden (TMB) emerges as a central theme across five of the top ten publications. For example, Samstein et al. (Nat Genet 2019; 2,822 citations) validated TMB as a pan-cancer predictor of survival after immunotherapy [25], and Chalmers et al. (Genome Med 2017; 2,538 citations) characterized the genomic landscape of TMB across 100,000 cancer genomes [26]. Marabelle et al.’s KEYNOTE-158 trial (Lancet Oncol 2020; 1,692 citations) provided prospective clinical evidence confirming TMB’s utility as a biomarker in pembrolizumab-treated solid tumors [27]. Additionally, Goodman et al. (Mol Cancer Ther 2017; 1,708 citations) and Chan et al. (Ann Oncol 2019; 1,906 citations) reported influential biomarker development studies that further solidified TMB’s role as a cornerstone of ICI response prediction [28, 29].

Their clinical and conceptual impact has accelerated the integration of biomarker-driven approaches into oncology practice, guiding patient selection and inspiring new therapeutic strategies for precision immuno-oncology.

Analysis of citation bursts

Supplementary Fig. 6 presents a timeline of the top 25 references with the strongest citation bursts detected from 2011 to 2025. In bibliometric terms, a citation burst is defined as a period in which a publication’s citation rate increases sharply compared to its prior baseline (e.g., more than double its usual rate) and remains elevated for at least two years [34]. In Supplementary Fig. 6, the blue line represents the observation window (2011–2025), and the red bars indicate the specific time intervals during which each reference experienced a citation burst. Notably, the highest burst in our analysis was observed for the global cancer statistics report by Sung et al. (CA Cancer J Clin 2021) [35], which exhibited an exceptional burst strength of 190.65. This paper entered a burst phase around 2023 that continues through 2025, reflecting its profound and sustained impact as a foundational epidemiological resource on worldwide cancer incidence and mortality. The surging citations of Sung et al.’s study underscore how frequently it is being cited to contextualize current cancer research (including ICI biomarker studies), indicating its broad influence across oncology literature. Several other publications are currently in active burst phases, highlighting emerging focal points in the ICI biomarker field. For instance, Marabelle et al. (Lancet Oncol 2020; burst strength 64.85) [27] is experiencing an ongoing burst (through 2025) driven by its pivotal finding that TMB can predict outcomes to pembrolizumab across diverse advanced tumors. The prominence of this burst reflects a major trend toward adopting tumor-agnostic biomarkers like TMB in clinical decision-making and trial design. Another active burst is seen with Tawbi et al. (N Engl J Med 2022) [36], which reported significantly improved progression-free survival with the combination of relatlimab (anti–LAG-3) and nivolumab versus nivolumab alone in advanced melanoma. The intense citation of this trial, representing a practice-changing advance in 2022, signals growing interest in next-generation immune checkpoints (such as LAG-3) and combination immunotherapies beyond the PD-1/PD-L1 axis. Together, these ongoing bursts illustrate how the field is rapidly coalescing around new evidence and innovations—from validating novel biomarkers to expanding therapeutic strategies—that are shaping the future trajectory of precision immuno-oncology.

Frequency, clustering, and temporal trends of keywords

Our bibliometric analysis identified 15,990 unique author keywords in the corpus. After semantic consolidation of synonyms and closely related terms for clarity, 656 keywords with ≥ 20 occurrences were retained for in-depth analysis. These keywords form a co-occurrence network visualized in Fig. 4A, where each node represents a keyword (node size proportional to its frequency) and links indicate co-occurrences between keywords (link thickness reflecting co-occurrence strength). Using VOSviewer’s modularity-based clustering algorithm, we partitioned the network into eight distinct thematic clusters, which reveal the major conceptual domains of the field. Specifically, Cluster 1 (212 keywords) encompasses fundamental immunological mechanisms and tumor biology (e.g., immune checkpoint, tumor microenvironment, apoptosis, hypoxia, immune evasion), highlighting the field’s roots in cancer immunobiology. Cluster 2 (160 keywords) revolves around therapeutic strategies and clinical contexts (e.g., neoadjuvant therapy, specific agents like pembrolizumab and bevacizumab, combinations like platinum-based chemotherapy, and treatment settings such as first-line treatment), reflecting the clinical translation of immune checkpoint inhibitors across regimens. Cluster 3 (135 keywords) centers on treatment outcomes, toxicity, and biomarker discovery (e.g., efficacy, survival, adverse events, immune-related adverse events, biomarkers), underscoring the emphasis on clinical benefit and safety monitoring in ICI research. Smaller clusters capture more specialized themes: Cluster 4 (80 keywords) highlights specific ICI agents and resistance mechanisms (e.g., anti-PD-1, nivolumab, acquired resistance), indicating focused interest in overcoming therapeutic resistance; Cluster 5 (51 keywords) is defined by genomic instability markers and innovative biomarker approaches (tumor mutational burden, microsatellite instability [MSI], liquid biopsy, circulating tumor DNA [ctDNA]), reflecting efforts to integrate genomic and liquid biopsy biomarkers; Cluster 6 (10 keywords) groups topics specific to head and neck cancers, pointing to tumor-specific investigative niches; Cluster 7 (6 keywords) involves translational and methodological aspects (e.g., clinical trial design, validation cohorts), emphasizing the bridge from research to clinic; and Cluster 8 (2 keywords) contains a very narrow focus on a particular therapy (ipilimumab combinations), representing a minor niche. Together, these clusters map the conceptual landscape of predictive biomarker research in ICIs, ranging from basic immunology to clinical application.

The temporal co-occurrence overlay (Fig. 4B) further elucidates the evolution of these themes over time. In this network visualization, node colors correspond to the average publication year of papers using that keyword (with cooler colors like purple indicating earlier usage and warmer colors like yellow indicating more recent emergence). This temporal mapping reveals a clear shift in research focus across the last decade. Early-stage ICI research (keywords colored purple/blue, mostly from 2011–2015) was predominantly anchored in foundational mechanisms and safety, exemplified by frequent early keywords such as PD-1 blockade, CTLA-4 blockade, and safety. These reflect the initial emphasis on understanding checkpoint biology and managing immune-related toxicities when ICIs were first introduced. In contrast, the more recent literature (keywords colored green to yellow, 2018–2025) shows a pronounced turn toward advanced diagnostics, outcome prediction, and novel technologies. Keywords that have surged in recent years include machine learning, deep learning, radiomics, artificial intelligence, prognosis, and survival, indicating the field’s rapid embrace of computational approaches and complex data integration. This chronological progression underscores a paradigm shift from relying on single, mechanism-focused biomarkers to leveraging multi-dimensional data and in silico methods to predict ICI response. Notably, many terms related to Cluster 5 (genomic and blood-based biomarkers) and computational tools now appear as yellow nodes, signifying how emerging technologies have become integral to the current research front.

The overall frequency distribution of top keywords (Fig. 4C) confirms the dominance of core concepts while also highlighting the rise of new methodologies. “Biomarkers” itself is the most frequent keyword in the dataset (n = 2545 occurrences), reflecting the central goal of the field to discover and validate predictive biomarkers. Other foundational terms also rank highly by occurrence—for instance, “immune checkpoint” (n = 1144) and “tumor microenvironment” (n = 882)—underscoring that immunological context and tumor contexture are ubiquitous considerations in ICI studies. Classic biomarker terms like “PD-L1” and “tumor mutational burden (TMB)” also appear among the frequently occurring keywords, affirming their ongoing relevance as benchmark predictive markers. At the same time, the prominence of newer concepts such as “machine learning” (n = 640) within the top keywords—alongside related terms like “radiomics” and “deep learning” that show lower absolute frequencies but very high recent usage—signals the field’s swiftly expanding integration of artificial intelligence and image analysis. The high frequency of these data-driven keywords, coupled with their recency (as seen in Fig. 4B’s color overlay), illustrates a convergence of immuno-oncology with computational science. In aggregate, the keyword frequency and co-occurrence patterns depict a dynamic research landscape: one deeply rooted in immunobiology and clinical oncology basics, yet increasingly augmented by cutting-edge technologies aimed at personalizing ICI therapy.

In addition to frequency and co-occurrence analyses, we examined citation bursts among keywords to identify emerging “hotspots” of research interest over time. Citation burst detection (performed with CiteSpace) highlights keywords that experienced a sudden and significant increase in citation frequency within a given period. Figure 4D displays the top 12 keywords with the strongest citation bursts (each burst lasting ≥ 1 year) and the timeline of when these bursts occurred. This analysis provides a forward-looking indicator of research trends, as burst keywords often correspond to new frontiers or intensified investigation. Notably, “gastroesophageal junction” emerged with the highest burst strength (11.53) among all keywords and is the only term still in an active burst phase as of 2023–2025. This indicates that gastroesophageal junction cancers—and their associated biomarker challenges—have become a focal point of recent attention, likely spurred by ongoing debates over PD-L1’s predictive value and the pressing need for reliable biomarkers in this tumor context. Several other keywords showed strong citation bursts in earlier years, often aligning with the introduction of influential concepts or pivotal clinical trials. For example, terms related to novel biomarkers or landmark studies (e.g., “tumor mutational burden” around 2018 following its validation in trials, or “microbiome” surges in the late 2010s with increasing interest in gut microbiota) exhibited transient bursts, reflecting periods of intense investigation that have since stabilized. The presence of these bursty keywords underlines how the field responds to new evidence and controversies—when a promising biomarker or finding arises, it rapidly becomes a concentrated topic of citations and discussion. As of 2025, the gastroesophageal junction remains a hotspot (active burst) in keyword analysis, suggesting that future research will continue to focus on resolving biomarker questions in upper gastrointestinal cancers. In summary, integrating the keyword frequency, clustering, temporal trend, and burst analyses provides a cohesive picture: the predictive biomarker landscape for ICIs is grounded in established immunological concepts while continually branching into new thematic clusters, with research priorities visibly shifting over time and certain emerging topics (like gastroesophageal biomarkers) sparking particularly intense recent interest.

Discussion

This first large-scale bibliometric analysis (n = 9075 publications) provides a comprehensive map of the evolving landscape of predictive biomarkers for ICIs, revealing distinct patterns in knowledge production, conceptual focus, and translational challenges. Geographically, China emerged as the leading contributor by publication volume followed by the United States. However, qualitative indicators paint a nuanced picture: influential work disproportionately originates from the USA and Europe. For example, US-based institutions like MD Anderson Cancer Center led in high-impact output, and authors such as Razelle Kurzrock attained exceptional influence (H-index 116). International collaboration networks mirror this dynamic, with U.S.–China partnerships being the most frequent (276 instances) and rising contributions from countries like India and Saudi Arabia. Notably, a gap persists between sheer productivity and transformative innovation—China’s quantitative dominance has not yet translated into equivalent clinical breakthroughs, whereas many seminal trials and biomarker discoveries have come from Western groups. This suggests that enhancing research quality, translational focus, and international collaboration should be a priority even for prolific regions.

Scientifically, the field has undergone a clear paradigm shift. Foundational biomarkers such as PD-L1 immunohistochemistry, tumor mutational burden (TMB), and microsatellite instability (MSI) remain central pillars of ICI research and clinical decision-making. Our analysis confirms their continued prominence—for instance, high TMB has been validated as a pan-cancer predictor of ICI benefit in pivotal studies (e.g. Samstein et al. 2019 and the KEYNOTE-158 trial) [25, 27]. Likewise, features of the tumor microenvironment have gained recognition as key determinants of response, exemplified by the presence of B cells and tertiary lymphoid structures correlating with improved outcomes in melanoma patients [31]. Nevertheless, reliance on any single biomarker has proven limiting, driving a transition toward multi-dimensional predictive signatures. Correspondingly, keyword trends demonstrate a decisive turn toward advanced computational methodologies: terms like “machine learning,” “deep learning,” and “radiomics” exhibit strong recent upticks. This transformative wave signals broad consensus that robust prediction of ICI responses demands capturing the intricate, multi-omic interplay within the tumor–immune ecosystem, rather than one-dimensional metrics.

A major barrier to biomarker development is the inconsistency of assay methodologies across studies. For example, different PD-L1 immunohistochemical assays (distinguished by antibody clones and scoring criteria) can yield non-equivalent results, complicating cross-trial comparisons [37]. Harmonization studies show that some diagnostic PD-L1 clones (e.g. 22C3, 28-8, SP263) produce broadly similar staining, whereas others like SP142 label significantly fewer tumor cells as positive [38]. Similarly, panel-based TMB assays often lack standardization. TMB values and “high TMB” cut-off thresholds vary by sequencing platform, gene panel size, and bioinformatic pipeline [39]. For instance, one FDA-approved assay defines TMB-high as ≥ 10 mutations per megabase, yet an institutional panel (MSK-IMPACT) used ≥ 13.8 mutations/Mb in its cohort [39]. These discrepancies underscore how assay variability undermines comparability and validation of putative biomarkers across different labs and clinical trials. Ongoing efforts are therefore focused on technical harmonization—from aligning PD-L1 IHC scoring (e.g. companion diagnostic vs laboratory-developed tests) to establishing universal TMB calibration standards—as prerequisites for reliable biomarker implementation.

It is also critical to distinguish between hypothesized biomarkers supported by preliminary or preclinical evidence and those with empirical validation in patients. The gut microbiome exemplifies a promising yet still speculative biomarker: multiple preclinical studies suggest that commensal bacteria can modulate anti-tumor immunity and thereby influence ICI efficacy [40]. However, microbiome composition has not yet yielded a clinically actionable predictor of response, in part due to inter-patient variability and the need for mechanistic clarity in humans. By contrast, certain immune contexture features have moved into the realm of validated biomarkers. Notably, the presence of B-cell–rich tertiary lymphoid structures in the tumor microenvironment has been causally linked to better ICI outcomes, as demonstrated in melanoma where patients with intratumoral TLS showed superior response rates [31]. These examples illustrate that while novel factors (e.g. microbiome, tumor metabolites, neoantigen quality) are actively being explored, only a subset have crossed the threshold of robust clinical evidence. Disentangling speculative mechanistic links from validated predictors in the literature is essential to guide future research investment.

Our analysis further reveals that biomarker research trends differ markedly by tumor type, sometimes misaligned with clinical needs or regulatory approvals. Publications on non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) comprise the largest segment of this field, which aligns with NSCLC being an early success story for ICIs (multiple FDA approvals for anti–PD-1/PD-L1 therapies since 2015) and a high-prevalence cancer [41, 42]. Melanoma—the first malignancy to demonstrate durable ICI responses—also represents a major focus, with extensive work on predictive factors like PD-L1, TMB, and immune gene signatures [43, 44]. Urothelial carcinoma (bladder cancer) has a more moderate research presence, reflecting its status as one of the initial ICI indications (e.g. atezolizumab approval in 2016) [45] but a smaller patient population than lung or breast cancers. In contrast, gastrointestinal (GI) cancers appear underrepresented in biomarker research despite their high global incidence and unmet need. For example, gastroesophageal junction tumors are a current “hot spot” of interest—as evidenced by the strongest recent keyword citation burst in our data (burst strength = 11.53)—yet clinical progress has lagged. A case in point is the ongoing controversy over PD-L1 as a predictive biomarker in upper GI malignancies. Some trials in advanced gastric and esophageal cancers have shown improved outcomes with ICI in PD-L1 positive subgroups, but overall results have been inconsistent and highly cutoff-dependent [46]. A 2022 meta-analysis of 17 trials found that while PD-L1 status correlates with benefit to some degree, its predictive value in advanced gastroesophageal cancer varies across studies and thresholds [46], leading to debate on its utility in this setting. This mismatch between research emphasis and clinical impact—particularly the lag in GI cancers, where biomarker development has not kept pace with disease burden—highlights the importance of directing future efforts toward tumor types and contexts with the greatest unmet need.

Finally, despite rapid advances, substantial translational hurdles remain before multi-omics and AI-driven biomarkers can fulfill their promise in routine practice. Computational models integrating pathology, genomics, and other data are increasingly sophisticated, but their clinical deployment faces challenges in algorithm transparency, reproducibility, and generalizability across diverse patient populations. Rigorous multi-center validation and regulatory-grade evidence will be required to ensure that AI-generated biomarker signatures truly improve patient outcomes. Moreover, the sheer breadth of this interdisciplinary field—spanning basic immunology, genomics, bioinformatics, and clinical trials—has created a fragmented knowledge ecosystem. Bridging these silos will require concerted collaboration among oncologists, pathologists, immunologists, and data scientists, as well as the establishment of standardized frameworks for assay performance and data sharing. By prioritizing standardization and cross-disciplinary integration alongside continued prospective validation, the field can more efficiently translate emerging biomarker insights into clinically actionable tools.

This study has several limitations inherent to the bibliometric approach. First, our reliance on the Web of Science Core Collection may introduce selection bias, as it excludes non-indexed publications such as preprints, conference proceedings, patents, and some regional journals. Consequently, certain contributions—for example, industry-led studies or findings reported in local venues—could be underrepresented. Second, citation-based metrics used in our analysis (e.g. total citations, H-index, burst strength) reflect academic impact but do not directly equate to clinical utility or quality of evidence. Highly cited papers may include exploratory research that has yet to be validated in practice. Third, our keyword analysis, while refined through manual curation and clustering, remains susceptible to terminology heterogeneity. Variations in wording (e.g. “PD-1 blockade” vs “anti-PD-1 therapy”) and evolving nomenclature could affect the grouping of related concepts. Finally, we limited our dataset to English-language publications, omitting work in other languages. This language restriction may overlook region-specific research insights (for instance, studies from East Asia published in local journals). These constraints mean that our findings, though robust, represent an incomplete snapshot of the global activity in this field. Future bibliometric and systematic analyses incorporating broader data sources and multilingual search strategies would be valuable to complement our results and mitigate these gaps.

Conclusion

This bibliometric analysis quantitatively maps the global research landscape of predictive biomarkers for immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) from 2011 through 2025, encompassing 9,075 publications. It reveals a field characterized by China’s leading research output and the United States’ prominent influence. China contributed the largest share of publications, whereas the United States, with slightly fewer, exhibited the greatest citation impact. Significant international collaboration is evident: the most frequent co-authorship partnership is between the USA and China, underscoring strong bilateral ties. Other major countries (e.g., Italy, Japan, Germany) also feature prominently, and research output from emerging economies has accelerated in recent years.

Conceptually, research themes have shifted markedly over time: early studies emphasized individual immune checkpoints and single biomarkers (such as PD-L1 expression or tumor mutational burden), whereas later work embraced a broader multi-modal paradigm. Recent publications increasingly integrate tumor microenvironment features, genomic instability indicators, and other multi-omics data into composite predictive signatures. Correspondingly, there has been a surge in keywords related to artificial intelligence, machine learning, radiomics, and liquid biopsy—signaling the growing prominence of computational multi-omics approaches. This evolution underscores the recognition that predicting ICI response requires capturing complex, multifaceted biological interactions rather than any single parameter.

Despite these advances, translating predictive biomarkers into clinical practice remains challenging. The analysis highlights persistent issues such as the lack of standardized assays, variability in results across platforms, and insufficient validation across diverse patient populations. For instance, the ongoing debate over PD-L1’s predictive value in gastroesophageal junction cancers—currently a prominent topic in the literature—illustrates the difficulties of validating biomarkers across different tumor contexts. Overall, this comprehensive mapping depicts a rapidly evolving and globally collaborative field that is increasingly data-driven, yet still grappling with unresolved translational hurdles in bringing biomarker innovations into routine clinical care.
