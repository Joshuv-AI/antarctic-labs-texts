Analysis of factors affecting the academic performance of university students using machine learning
Authors: Yuri Reina Marín, Lenin Quiñones Huatangari, Judith Nathaly Alva Tuesta, Omer Cruz Caro, Jorge Luis Maicelo Guevara, Einstein Sánchez Bardales, River Chávez Santos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Understanding the determinants of university students’ academic performance has become a strategic priority for higher education institutions, especially in contexts marked by social, economic, and academic diversity. However, performance assessment remains a challenge due to the complexity of the educational process and the nonlinear nature of learning behaviors. A machine learning-based prediction model was developed using primary data from 386 university students. The performance of nine educational data mining (EDM) algorithms, including XGBoost, Random Forest, artificial neural networks (ANNs), Support Vector Machines, Decision Trees, Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression, AdaBoost, and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), was evaluated using demographic, socioeconomic, academic, social and family, health and wellness, infrastructure and services, and time management and extracurricular activity factors. The results reveal that machine learning is an effective tool for representing nonlinear relationships between academic performance and its determinants, allowing for accurate prediction of academic outcomes and explaining the individual contribution of each variable through sensitivity and interpretability analyses. Despite differences in their predictive accuracy, all algorithms effectively modeled educational dynamics. In particular, those models that integrate multiple student dimensions demonstrated better generalization capabilities. It is concluded that, to achieve an accurate assessment of academic performance in diverse university environments, it is essential to consider influencing factors in machine learning-based predictive models.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28870-1.

Introduction

Education is foundational to individual and social development and underpins sustainable progress and cultural continuity1,2. As a result, education has become a global priority for governments, communities, and families3; countries have implemented diverse policies and allocated substantial resources to improve quality4, A central component of these efforts is educational evaluation, where student academic performance is a key metric5. However, measuring academic performance is complex due to the interaction of multiple factors, including socioeconomic status, institutional quality, family environment, peer influence, teaching effectiveness, classroom conditions, and cultural context6. For example, Subasi et al.7 and Doyumgaç et al.8 examined students’ perceptions of online distance education using the photovoice methodology during the COVID-19 pandemic. Scientific and rigorous evaluation of academic performance is essential for students, schools, and the education system; however, their findings have limitations in objectivity and evaluative rigour, which makes it difficult to extrapolate to non-emerging educational contexts9.

The school success of students in these institutions is a matter of great concern and interest to researchers, parents, institutions, and governments in every nation10. Educational institutions must monitor the academic performance of their students and take appropriate improvement measures11. Teachers should monitor student performance in higher education institutions to set precise learjing objectives and foster a culture of continuous improvement12. When assessing the performance of an educational institution, a number of elements are taken into account. Higher education institutions seek to improve their standing in university rankings while prioritizing instructional Quality. One important consideration in the rankings of higher education institutions is the academic performance of the students13. Educators can gain insight into the obstacles students encounter in achieving superior academic performance, and educators can improve students’ skills and implement corrective actions to improve poor student performance14.

Consequently, research is geared toward the application of performance prediction techniques, highlighting the high potential offered by data mining methods. EDM is a methodology adopted to extract meaningful knowledge and patterns from academic or demographic databases, but it excludes psychosocial, health, well-being, and infrastructure factors2,15. EDM is a specific branch of data mining that focuses on analyzing educational datasets to identify patterns related to teaching and learning processes16, it serves as an effective tool for revealing latent structures in educational data, evaluating student performance, and improving learning outcomes17; the EDM allows for the processing of large amounts of student-related information to extract meaningful insights that support educational improvement18.

Early identification of underperforming students, provision of timely support, and improvement in the quality of educational processes are achieved through the use of quantitative tools and academic performance assessments1. Data mining was used in the educational system, and EDM was created to uncover the intricate relationship between academic performance and the variables that affect it19,20, data mining in the higher education system could help all stakeholders in the educational process: students, teachers, administration, support staff, and the social community21; by analyzing large data sets, EDM identifies patterns and factors that influence student success22, thus facilitating proactive educational strategies23. The application of various machine learning algorithms in EDM has shown promising results in accurately predicting academic outcomes24,25.

EDM has been applied in diverse areas, such as e-learning analytics26, pedagogical support27, educational data clustering28, and student performance prediction29, this study focuses on student performance forecasts because we want to know the primary academic, social, and personal elements that influence students’ academic achievement30,31; EDM is a well-established field that focuses on applying data mining techniques, such as classification or clustering, to educational data to better understand the learning process and student achievement32,33. This is achieved by seeking knowledge that is interpretable and understandable by humans34, classification techniques are specifically used to analyze educational data to forecast students’ academic success; the outcomes enable the improvement of institutional performance and the development of a more productive learning environment34.

EDM has been used to examine emotional factors related to academic performance and to track student behavior throughout the learning and teaching processes35–38. According to Upadhyay et al.38, research on EDM can be classified into five main areas: relationship identification, pattern discovery, data filtering for expert analysis, clustering, and predictive model development. Among these, the most widely applied approach is the prediction of academic outcomes32,39–41. In this context, Thai-Nghe et al.42 introduced a novel recommendation system designed to evaluate academic performance through the analysis of educational data. They developed a customized multiple linear regression model to predict student performance at American universities43. However, to evaluate students’ academic development, Polyzou & Karypis44 developed a distributed linear model based on past grades.Bao et al.45 used binary logistic regression to forecast student academic achievement; yet, academic performance is hard to describe and forecast with conventional statistical and mathematical techniques. In EDM, machine learning has been widely utilized to forecast and evaluate academic achievement.

To generate skilled pupils who perform well on the job, academic success is crucial46,47. To understand students’ behavior and abilities, research on their performance can produce better predictions in the future48. Classification accuracy quantifies the proportion of correctly predicted labels. Academic success is essential to producing competent students who do well in the workplace49. In this study, the target (label) variable is the previous semester’s GPA. In addition, validation techniques such as cross-validation were applied to the datasets50,51 and the decision tree52,53. They contain various types of student-related information that can influence their performance. Incorporating cross-validation into a shared data collection can help evaluate the results of statistical calculations.

However, despite the significant advances reported by previous studies, important limitations remain. Most works rely on secondary datasets with limited variables, focus mainly on academic factors without considering psychosocial or well-being aspects, evaluate only a single algorithm, and rarely include interpretability analyses. These limitations restrict their applicability for evidence-based decision-making in higher education. The present study aims to identify the elements that affect students’ academic performance and to apply the findings to improve various university programs. This study fills this gap by using a large primary dataset of university students from diverse academic programs, integrating academic, socioeconomic, and well-being factors. It systematically compares several machine learning algorithms, including XGBoost, Random Forest, and Artificial Neural Networks, and incorporates sensitivity and SHAP analyses to enhance transparency and explainability. By doing so, the study not only improves predictive accuracy but also provides actionable insights for personalized interventions and strategic decision-making in higher education institutions.

Academic performance assessment and machine learning

One important measure of the caliber of education is the academic achievement of the students54. There are significant concerns about the prospects for educational institutions, such as whether the student is on track to pass or not, what kind of grades they will get, and therefore whether they will drop out of school55,56. A system that could forecast dropout rates as well as the challenges students encounter in earning good marks would be advantageous to both students and educational institutions57–59. EDM techniques are capable of performing these tasks60,61.

Tools for machine learning offer comprehensive data on the variables influencing academic achievement54, they help identify at-risk students early and allow for targeted interventions to improve educational quality62; the characterization of educational behavior is the central content of the evaluation of academic performance1, given the complexity of the factors involved and the limitations of conventional statistical approaches, machine learning has become the preferred method for analyzing and predicting academic performance.

Evaluation of academic performance

Predicting student performance and the underlying causes of their learning challenges is a crucial task that benefits both students and educational institutions56. Academic performance evaluation is also crucial for modifying curricula and pedagogical strategies and for assessing how well classroom activities and programs are working. The process of collecting data from multiple sources that precisely describes how well students meet learning objectives to enhance instructional strategy is called assessment (Fig. 1)1; predicting student behavior and learning outcomes is considered one of the most important tasks in the field of EDM56.

Grade point average (GPA) is a commonly used indicator for measuring academic performance. To model and interpret student behavior, researchers have used statistical and regression techniques to predict performance based on historical academic records; numerous studies have explored the use of data mining and machine learning methods to predict student outcomes63. For example, Mueen et al.64 applied classification algorithms such as Naïve Bayes (NB), Neural Networks (NN), and Decision Trees (DTS) in courses with Learning Management Systems (LMS), finding that NB was the most accurate; in similar research by Amrieh et al.34, demographic characteristics, academic background, and parent and student involvement were incorporated, testing methods such as RNA, NB, DTS, and ensemble techniques such as Bagging, Boosting, and Random Forest, with RNA and Boosting being the most effective.

Meanwhile, Kaur et al.65 explored the classification of students as qualified or not, highlighting the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) as the most efficient algorithm; Guo et al.66 proposed a pre-alert system based on deep neural networks to predict the risk of course failure, whose architecture outperformed NB, MLP, and SVM; Saa67 examined how personal and social variables relate to academic performance and concluded that the CART algorithm achieved the best classification, albeit with a modest accuracy rate of 40%.

Singh et al.68 used a multiple regression analysis model to capture the complex relationship between student academic performance and influencing factors, such as learning facilities, communication skills, and parental guidance, and to predict student performance; However, this application of statistical models shows low power to capture nonlinear and high-dimensional relationships, reducing the predictive power of their results. Recently, EDM has been developed to evaluate and predict educational performance by addressing more complex educational phenomena. Predicting academic performance is one of the most important components of EDM69. In addition, machine learning is a widely used technology for predicting academic performance. EDM has adopted 4 of the 19 machine learning methods to assess student academic performance1.

Yaacob et al.13 supervised data mining tools, such as k-nearest neighbor, decision tree, logistic regression model, and Naïve Bayes, to find important information and forecast student performance; as demonstrated by the accuracy, precision indicator, ROC curve, and outcomes, Naïve Bayes outperformed the other categorization methods. Tomasevic et al.70 employed algorithms including logistic regression, artificial neural networks (ANN), support vector machines (SVM), decision trees, Naïve Bayes, and k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) to forecast student achievement based on factors like prior academic performance, demographic data, and student engagement levels; their findings revealed that incorporating data on engagement and previous performance into ANN models resulted in lower prediction accuracy in both classification and regression tasks. In contrast, demographic factors had minimal influence on prediction accuracy71. Similarly, Yang & Li72 used a backpropagation neural network (BP-NN) to develop a matrix of student attributes, which allowed them to estimate indicators such as course grades and learning aptitude; Malini & Kalpana73 applied ANN, along with ensemble methods such as Boosting and Bagging, to predict academic performance based on student grades, socioeconomic context, and school-related variables. Their study highlighted the important role of economic status in shaping academic outcomes. It is worth noting that the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) classifier achieved an accuracy of 72%, while the Bagging and MultiBoost methods achieved 88% and 86%, respectively, demonstrating that models incorporating economic factors achieved higher predictive performance.

Khan et al.74 implemented random forest classifiers, artificial neural networks, and Naïve Bayes to predict students’ academic outcomes, taking into account variables such as perceived teaching effectiveness, grading leniency, previous academic delays, student aptitude, and subject mastery; the models achieved overall accuracy above 80%75; in another study, Widyahastuti & Tjhin76 investigated student demographics utilizing decision tree, rule-based, and naïve bayes techniques to enhance student performance and aptitude through active participation. For weaker pupils, performance forecasts helped them understand the level of difficulty of the course.

Amrieh et al.34 evaluated students’ academic performance and attitudes using Artificial Neural Networks, Boosting, Bagging, Naïve Bayesian, Random Forest, and Decision Tree. According to the study, the accuracy of the proposed model that made use of attitude features increased by as much as 22.1%, when evaluating recently arriving pupils, the accuracy rate was 80%. Additionally, the authors used Fuzzy Soft Set Classification (FSSC) to achieve high accuracy results, enabling them to identify at-risk students early in their education; therefore, the study found that higher education can minimize the number of students who fail to graduate on time or drop out by providing appropriate treatment and strategic program design.

Other studies have focused on specific aspects of academic performance. A comparative study of EDM techniques showed that data preprocessing and algorithm tuning are crucial in predicting failure in programming courses, with SVM being the most effective.Asif et al.77 used academic information from the four years of study, achieving with NB an accuracy of 83.65% in predicting the students’ final performance. Finally, a studyKostopoulos et al.78 demonstrated the usefulness of semi-supervised learning (SSL) methods such as Self-Training, Co-Training, and Tri-Training. The latter, using a C4.5 decision tree as the base classifier, obtained the best results, even surpassing the supervised model with the same classifier trained on the entire dataset.

Machine learning

This technique shows great potential for modeling complex, nonlinear relationships between inputs and outputs, its applications span a wide range of disciplines in engineering and science. In the present study, several well-established machine learning methods were used, including ANNs and SVMs, decision trees, and gradient-based algorithms, to evaluate and interpret students’ academic performance.

Advances in artificial intelligence and computer science have led to the widespread application of machine learning techniques in the fields of engineering79–83; these methods have also gained importance in EDM, especially for predicting student academic performance. For example, decision tree algorithms have been used to identify relationships between academic outcomes and their underlying factors, enabling predictive models based on educational datasets29.Bunkar et al.84 used the Naïve Bayes classifier to estimate student academic performance, whileArsad et al.85 implemented neural networks to predict final grades using first-year academic records; similarly, Hamsa et al.86 introduced a hybrid approach that integrates fuzzy genetic algorithms with decision trees to improve performance prediction.Injadat et al.87 further contributed to this field by developing a high-precision bagging ensemble model to predict student success.

Zhang et al.88 evaluated undergraduate academic performance at a Chinese engineering faculty using tree-based machine learning models, to improve the accuracy of predictions, Talamás-Carvajal & Ceballos89 presented a stacking ensemble technique that detects dropout risks with high accuracy, random forest classifiers are designed to forecast college students’ academic and professional success90.Vidhya & Vadivu91 proposed a two-tier classification framework for organizing and interpreting student-related data. In a related effort; Keser & Aghalarova92 designed a hybrid ensemble algorithm aimed at predicting future academic achievements; Bansal et al.93 introduced an automated system for estimating student performance, relying on deep learning techniques and historical academic records.

Overall, machine learning algorithms have become integral to EDM, attracting increasing interest in the field of educational evaluation. As novel machine learning methods continue to emerge and are applied to EDM, the overall performance and analytical capability of the field have improved substantially. Nevertheless, the diversity in algorithmic structures, application contexts, and data requirements underscores the importance of critically assessing each method’s strengths and limitations; leveraging a range of machine learning techniques is essential for gaining deeper insights into educational processes, modeling student behavior, and ultimately improving the quality of education.

Support vector machine (SVM)

It is a supervised algorithm developed to model nonlinear relationships under the principle of structural risk minimization94. Its main advantage lies in its formulation as a quadratic optimization problem with a unique solution, which guarantees optimal overall results, especially when working with small, high-dimensional samples. SVM transforms the input space into a high-dimensional feature space using kernel functions, and then searches for an optimal decision boundary between classes95. In educational contexts, Tomasevic et al.70 used SVMs to predict student performance based on demographic and academic engagement data, demonstrating an accuracy of over 80%. Comparative studies have also highlighted their effectiveness in predicting academic failure67.

Experience has shown that support vector machines are capable of generalization in a variety of applications, including pattern recognition, nonlinear regression, and so forth. Artificial neural networks (ANN)

It is a subset of machine learning and the core of deep learning algorithms98, which are inspired by the human brain and mimic the way biological neurons transmit signals to each other99. These networks learn by adjusting connection weights using algorithms such as backpropagation, thereby reducing the error between the predicted value and the actual value. In the educational field, their ability to model nonlinear relationships has been widely demonstrated. Guo et al.66 proposed an early warning system based on deep neural networks, outperforming other traditional algorithms; similarly, Malini & Kalpana73 reported an accuracy of 88% when using an ANN to predict academic performance based on social and school factors.

Figure 2 illustrates the structure of an artificial neural network (ANN), the network begins with an input layer, which receives external data, and ends with an output layer, responsible for delivering the final results; between these two layers are one or more hidden layers, which are characteristic of most neural networks; each layer is composed of individual units, commonly referred to as neurons or perceptrons, which are interconnected with nodes from the previous and subsequent layers. Neural networks are usually fully connected, meaning that each node in a given layer is linked to all nodes in adjacent layers. These connections are assigned specific weight values, which determine the degree of influence that the output of one unit has on the activation of the next unit1.

Decision tree

It is a machine learning technique that organizes decisions and possible outcomes in a hierarchical tree-like structure100; each node represents an attribute, branches indicate possible values, and leaves represent categories or output values, common algorithms include ID3, C4.5, and CART; its simplicity and interpretability make it ideal for educational contexts. Mueen et al.64 found that the Decision Tree performed competitively against algorithms such as Naïve Bayes or ANN in virtual environments. Furthermore; Saa67 reported that the CART classifier was the best performer in predicting low-performing college students.

The main objective of a decision tree is to build a predictive model that assigns input variables to an objective result; decision trees are classified into two main types: classification trees, which deal with categorical objective variables, and regression trees, which are designed for continuous results (usually with real values)1. Figure 3 shows the structure of a decision tree; there are several specific algorithms that fall under this method, including Iterative Dichotomiser 3 (ID3), Classification and Regression Tree (CART), and Chi-square Automatic Interaction Detector (CHAID), among others101,102.

Ensemble learning

Ensemble learning combines multiple base models, called “weak learners,” to build a more accurate and reliable predictive model103. There are three main strategies: Bagging (such as Random Forest), Boosting (such as AdaBoost and XGBoost), and Stacking; bagging works through a parallel learning approach, in which homogeneous weak learners are trained independently and their results are aggregated using a defined averaging method. In contrast, boosting follows a sequential learning framework, in which each new weak learner is trained to correct the errors of its predecessor, and the results are combined using a specific adaptive strategy; in this study, ensemble methods such as AdaBoost, XGBoost, GradientBoost, and Random Forest were used to evaluate students’ academic performance1.

A machine learning technique known as ensemble learning combines several learned models (commonly referred to as “weak learners”) to create a more robust model (Fig. 4). A weak learner typically exhibits high variance or bias, resulting in poor standalone performance. Amrieh et al.34 applied Bagging and Boosting to an educational dataset and obtained substantial improvements in accuracy compared to individual classifiers. Talamás-Carvajal & Ceballos89 implemented a stacking model to identify students at risk of dropping out, achieving high levels of accuracy.

Hyperparameters for each machine learning model were tuned using a grid search nested within a 10-fold cross-validation on the training set. Candidate values for each algorithm (e.g., number of trees and maximum depth in Random Forest and XGBoost, kernel type and C/ε in SVM, hidden layers and learning rate in ANN) were predefined according to prior literature and pilot tests. The optimal configuration was selected by minimizing the average root mean squared error (RMSE) across folds. This procedure ensures reproducibility and avoids information leakage.

Machine learning-based prediction of academic performance

Main Idea

This research established a theoretical framework grounded in machine learning to evaluate the effectiveness of various algorithms and examine the factors that influence academic performance; using machine learning, the study modeled the complex relationships between academic outcomes and their determinants, enabling the construction of a predictive model, this approach facilitates the identification and analysis of educational patterns and student behavior based on relevant data, providing a powerful means of anticipating academic performance. The proposed model consists of three key components: data collection, representation of academic performance, and performance prediction.

It should be noted that, for the methodological development of this work, we followed the sequence proposed by Zhao et al.1, who proposed a structured approach that includes the collection and preprocessing of educational data, the identification of influencing factors, the selection and training of machine learning algorithms, and the subsequent validation and analysis of the predictive model; this sequence allowed for a systematic application of machine learning to the study of academic performance, considering both its nonlinear nature and the need to evaluate multiple explanatory variables.

Data generation

To establish ML(X), known information about the students and their educational level (training samples) is required. This study collected data from university students enrolled in the 2025-I academic semester. Students from the second cycle of university education onwards were included in the study. The survey technique used was administered virtually using Google Forms and sent to students’ institutional emails in May by the university’s Information Technology Office.

All procedures performed in this study involving human participants strictly adhered to the institutional ethical standards and the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Before data collection, the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, the anonymity of responses, and the exclusive academic use of the information were thoroughly explained to all participants. All students who agreed to take part provided written informed consent before completing the survey, thereby ensuring respect for their autonomy and confidentiality.

Predicting academic performance

Once the ML(X) machine learning model was trained and validated, it was applied to estimate the academic performance of university students at the Toribio Rodríguez de Mendoza National University in Amazonas. The objective of this stage was to anticipate performance patterns based on previously identified individual, academic, social, and economic factors. Predicting performance not only allowed for estimating expected outcomes but also for exploring the sensitivity of each explanatory variable in the model. This evaluation offers an analytical tool that contributes to the design of more focused educational strategies and evidence-based pedagogical decision-making. Likewise, the performance of the selected machine learning algorithms was analyzed to identify which offered the greatest accuracy and generalization capacity in the specific educational context of the population studied.

EDM procedure machine learning-based

In this study, a machine learning methodology was applied to model the complex and nonlinear relationship between student academic performance and the factors that determine it, using primary data collected at the National University Toribio Rodriguez of Mendoza of Amazonas. From this information, a training dataset was constructed that incorporates both academic performance and previously operationalized explanatory variables; these data allowed for the generation of a predictive mapping function using machine learning algorithms, aimed at estimating the performance of new students with similar characteristics.

The methodological procedure adopted was based on the sequence proposed by Zhao et al. (2023), adapted to the specific characteristics of our study population; this sequence included the collection of educational data, the selection and transformation of variables, the training of the model with different machine learning algorithms, and the subsequent evaluation of its predictive performance; (Fig. 5) illustrates the workflow implemented in this process, which allowed for the systematic integration of data analytics into educational assessment.

Results

Dataset

The dataset used in this study comprised information collected from 386 university students enrolled in the 2025-I academic semester. It included the previous semester’s GPA as the target variable for assessing academic performance, along with a wide range of associated factors such as demographic characteristics, socioeconomic conditions, academic aspects, social and family environment, health and well-being, institutional infrastructure and services, and students’ time management and extracurricular activities.

Figure 6 presents the statistical distribution of the main study variables. As shown, the sample is balanced in terms of gender (52% male and 48% female), and the distribution of the previous semester’s GPA approximates a normal curve centered around 14 points, indicating an overall satisfactory academic performance. The boxplots reveal no substantial difference in academic averages between genders; however, students from High-Performance Schools (COAR) exhibit higher averages compared to those from public or private institutions, suggesting that prior educational background influences university achievement.

To understand and verify the performance of the machine learning model, the three hundred and eighty-six (386) students were divided into two groups. One group consisted of three hundred and fifty-six (356) students, used for training and predicting academic performance. The other group included thirty (30) students, which were used exclusively to evaluate the generalization and validation capability of the machine learning model. Overall, this analysis shows that, while traditional academic indicators remain strong predictors, incorporating non-academic variables enriches the predictive model and provides a more comprehensive understanding of student performance determinants, forcing institutions to design academic support policies complemented with socio-emotional and well-being programs, such as guidance for managing academic load, nutritional awareness campaigns, and mentoring systems to support students with more vulnerable profiles.

Discussions

The study’s findings reaffirm that university students’ academic performance responds to a complex combination of interrelated factors, with prior academic variables standing out as key predictors. Specifically, the previous semester’s GPA and the number of courses passed showed a significant positive correlation with current performance, a result consistent with previous research that has highlighted the importance of academic history as one of the main determinants of student success13,73,74. This observation also coincides with the findings of Guo et al.66, who identified that grade history can serve as a reliable indicator of the risk of underachievement or dropping out. In addition to these academic indicators, our results show that non-academic factors such as sleep quality, family support, and time management, although with lower weights, also exert an influence on academic performance. This finding underscores the relevance of adopting a comprehensive perspective that integrates psychosocial and environmental dimensions into predictive models.

Machine learning-based predictive models, especially XGBoost and AdaBoost, demonstrated a high degree of accuracy, robustness, and generalizability in estimating students’ final grades; this result corroborates what was reported by Amrieh et al.34 and Bakhshinategh et al.69, who show that ensemble learning methods outperform individual classifiers by reducing overfitting and capturing nonlinear relationships between variables; however, they failed to include robust cross-validation processes, compromising the reliability and generalization of the findings. In the context of the study, the agreement between actual and predicted grades suggests that the applied approach is not only statistically sound but can be practically applied to educational management and evidence-based pedagogical decision-making54,61.

Sensitivity analysis and interpretation using SHAP values provided a deeper understanding of the individual impact of each variable on the prediction. These tools allow us not only to estimate which students might be underperforming but also to understand the reasons behind that prediction, making the model an explanatory rather than merely descriptive tool87,92. As prior work has noted, Khan & Ghosh36, one of the historical limitations of data-driven education has been the low interpretability of models; however, the present study contributes to overcoming this barrier by integrating transparent and understandable interpretations for educational stakeholders.

As for non-academic factors, variables such as sleep quality, family support, stress, and time management, while having a moderate impact, also influence academic performance. This finding is consistent with studies that highlight the importance of psychosocial and well-being factors in the educational process; however, these variables were not fully integrated into the academic data, resulting in a partial explanation and limitations in consistent predictive models applicable to educational management35,111. On the other hand, the exclusion of some factors has prevented a holistic understanding of academic performance, as it reduces the strictly cognitive dimensions1,2. In contexts of high academic demands, the mental and emotional health of the student can make significant differences in their ability to learn and perform in a sustained manner7,8. Therefore, it is suggested that university support programs should not be limited to strengthening cognitive skills, but should also offer socioemotional support, especially to students with fragile academic backgrounds.

The decision tree is used as an explanatory tool to identify the main factors that influence academic performance, providing a hierarchical and understandable representation of the decisions that lead to a high or low academic classification (Fig. 4). The root node of the tree indicates that the commute time to university (≤ 5.5) is the first segmentation criterion, suggesting that a shorter commute time is strongly associated with higher academic performance, possibly due to the reduction in stress or lost time, as proposed by Roslan et al.105 and Zhao et al.112; in the left branch, the number of courses passed (≤ 6.0) is positioned as a key variable, coinciding with previous studies that highlight the immediate academic trajectory as a relevant predictor13,73; on the right branch, lower maternal occupations, along with a higher number of grade repetitions, are associated with lower performance, which is consistent with evidence on the influence of cultural capital and family educational stability35,107. Finally, variables such as eating habits reinforce the need to incorporate well-being factors into predictive models, as proposed by Masnan et al.111; this tree, in addition to its predictive accuracy, provides interpretive clarity about student trajectories, serving as a basis for more informed and focused academic decision-making.

From a methodological perspective, this study was based on a data mining strategy adapted from the framework proposed by Zhao et al.1, which comprises a rigorous sequence of educational data collection, processing, training, and validation. This approach allowed for the modeling of nonlinear and high-dimensional relationships, an aspect that is difficult to address using traditional statistical methods such as multiple linear regression. Although this method provides initial advances, it has low explanatory power given the complexity of the interactions between multiple factors, which limits the accuracy and validity of predictions42,68. The choice of algorithms, such as decision trees, artificial neural networks, and ensemble models, responded to their proven effectiveness in similar contexts64,90, and allowed comparing their performances using correlation metrics, mean square error, and sensitivity analysis.

The sample consisted of 386 university students enrolled in the 2025-I semester, which represents a significant coverage of the university’s student population. Although the sampling was not probabilistic, the diversity of majors, educational levels, and socioeconomic conditions included in the study ensures adequate variability for training machine learning models104,105. Furthermore, the use of anonymous surveys administered by institutional means guaranteed a high response rate and allowed for the collection of reliable self-reported data, a valid methodological strategy in educational prediction studies31,77. However, the lack of incorporation of longitudinal variables is recognized as a limitation, so future research is recommended to analyze complete academic trajectories and allow for modeling changes over time.

The results not only provide empirical evidence on the determinants of academic and socio-emotional performance but also reaffirm the usefulness of machine learning techniques as effective tools for analysis, prediction, and interpretation. These techniques allow us to address the complexity of the educational phenomenon, characterized by nonlinear relationships, interactions between variables, and high dimensionality, which exceeds the explanatory capacities of traditional statistical models37,69. In particular, the implementation of models such as XGBoost, Random Forest, or artificial neural networks facilitates the early identification of at-risk students, contributing to timely and personalized intervention87,90. However, it is emphasized that the predictive value of these algorithms must be complemented with a comprehensive pedagogical understanding of the educational environment, which considers the social, family, and institutional contexts in which students develop8,54. As they point out33, , EDM is most effective when combined with theoretical frameworks and student-centered educational strategies, transforming data into concrete improvement actions. Consequently, the ethical and contextualized use of machine learning not only enhances the quality of teaching but also promotes more equitable, informed education centered on students’ real-life trajectories.

Conclusions

The study shows that university academic performance is primarily determined by prior academic factors, such as the previous semester’s GPA, the number of courses passed, and academic ranking. These factors showed significant positive correlations with final grades, supporting the hypothesis that recent academic trajectory is a robust indicator of future performance. It was also found that students with a history of low performance, a higher number of failed courses, and sleep difficulties tend to obtain lower GPAs, reinforcing the importance of considering both cognitive and well-being variables in performance analysis.

The implementation of machine learning models, particularly XGBoost and AdaBoost, enabled the development of highly accurate and robust predictive systems that overcome the limitations of statistical models. These models not only accurately estimated final grades but also offered explanatory tools, such as sensitivity and variable importance analyses, that allow for understanding how each factor impacts the prediction. This explanatory capacity provides added value for decision-making in the educational setting, facilitating timely and targeted interventions for students at academic risk.

Regarding non-academic factors, although their weight was lower in the predictive models, variables such as stress, family support, health status, and time management were also observed to influence student performance. This suggests that an educational approach focused exclusively on quantitative performance may be insufficient. This reaffirms the need to adopt a comprehensive approach that considers the psychosocial and emotional dimensions of students, strengthening mentoring, psychological counseling, and socioemotional support programs within higher education institutions.

This study has certain limitations; it is a cross-sectional analysis that does not incorporate longitudinal information on the evolution of academic performance over time. Furthermore, non-probability sampling and data collection through self-reported surveys may lead to response bias or limitations in the generalizability of the results. Therefore, it is recommended that future research adopt longitudinal designs that allow for modeling complete academic trajectories and dynamic changes over time. Furthermore, it would be desirable to incorporate institutional and teacher variables for a more holistic understanding of the educational environment.

The findings also offer important implications for the design of educational policies and procedures in universities. It is recommended to incorporate early warning systems machine learning-based algorithms to identify at-risk students and provide personalized interventions. Curricular plans should also include spaces for emotional empowerment, time management, and comprehensive well-being, beyond a purely academic focus. Institutions must foster a culture of data analysis and evidence-based decision-making, integrating technological tools that allow for anticipating problems and optimizing student support. In this sense, educational analytics should not be an end in itself, but rather a means to promote a more equitable, personalized education focused on the comprehensive development of university students.

From a theoretical standpoint, this study contributes to the growing literature on the intersection between educational data mining, learning analytics, and student success by demonstrating how advanced machine learning algorithms can complement traditional statistical approaches. It highlights the relevance of integrating cognitive, behavioral, and well-being variables into predictive frameworks, reinforcing the multidimensional nature of academic performance.

From a practical perspective, the results underscore the importance of implementing data-driven strategies in higher education institutions. Universities can translate these findings into concrete interventions such as adaptive learning systems, personalized mentoring programs, and early risk detection platforms. This practical dimension not only enhances institutional efficiency but also promotes equity by ensuring that vulnerable students receive the timely support they need to succeed academically.
