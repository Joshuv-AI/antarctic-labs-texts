An ethnic-sensitive hybrid framework for T2D prediction with explainable AI and weighted ensembles
Authors: Karlo Abnoosian, Rahman Farnoosh, Hamidreza Noushkaran, Danial Javaheri, Hasnat Azeez Shimal, Abdullah Abdulamer Abdulkarem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a growing global health crisis, affecting over 537 million people as of 2021. Early prediction remains particularly challenging in low- and middle-income countries due to missing data, class imbalance, and population-specific risk factors. This study presents a four-stage predictive framework— Feature-Weighted Class-Adaptive Generative Imputation Network-Weighted Classifier Aggregation Ensemble (FW-CAGIN-WCAE)—designed to address these limitations. First, Zero-Threshold Feature Removal (ZTFR) is applied to eliminate low-quality variables. Second, missing values are imputed FW-CAGIN, a novel class-aware and feature-weighted GAN model that accounts for both class and feature importance. Third, a performance-weighted ensemble of 15 machine and deep learning algorithms is constructed. Finally, SHAP analysis is used to uncover population-specific risk indicators. The proposed method was evaluated on three benchmark datasets—PIDD, FHGDD, and BDD—and their combinations, using nested five-fold cross-validation. The model achieved a peak AUC of 0.936 ± 0.018 in PIDD-BDD combination and reduced the imputation mean absolute error (MAE) from 0.8028 to 0.0033. It also lowered AUC variability by 36.3% and improved the diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) to 68.4 ± 20.5. SHAP analysis identified as a key predictive feature across both Asian and European populations. These findings demonstrate that the proposed framework offers an accurate, interpretable, and population-sensitive solution for early T2D detection, especially in resource-limited healthcare settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31234-4.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) represents a critical and escalating global health crisis, affecting approximately 537 million adults in 2021—a number projected to surge to 783 million by 20451,2. Characterized by chronic hyperglycemia and insulin resistance, T2D is a major contributor to cardiovascular disease, renal failure, neuropathy, blindness, and lower-limb amputations, accounting for 6.7 million deaths annually—equivalent to one death every five seconds1,3.

The economic impact is profound. In 2021, global diabetes-related healthcare expenditure reached USD 966 billion, reflecting a 316% increase over the past 15 years1,2. Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) shoulder 75% of the global diabetes mortality burden. Regionally, the Western Pacific reports the highest prevalence (206 million cases), while Africa reports the lowest (24 million), as illustrated in Fig. 1. T2D accounts for 90–95% of all diabetes cases and is strongly associated with obesity—a condition increasingly referred to as “diabesity.” It significantly elevates the risk of limb amputation (23.3-fold increase) and is linked to neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease4,5.

Despite its global impact, reliable T2D prediction remains challenging. Missing or erroneous clinical data—especially prevalent in LMICs due to data collection errors and patient non-compliance—introduce bias and degrade model reliability6,7. Class imbalance, where healthy controls disproportionately outnumber positive cases, results in low sensitivity and elevated false-negative rates, undermining early detection efforts8. Additionally, complex nonlinear relationships among genetic, clinical, and environmental variables reduce the efficacy of traditional models such as Logistic Regression (LR)9. Population-specific risk factors (e.g., diabetes pedigree in Asian populations versus BMI in European cohorts) further hinder model generalizability9,10.

Accurate imputation of missing or zero values in key clinical features—such as Glucose, Blood Pressure, and BMI—is vital for improving diagnostic accuracy, particularly in resource-limited settings. However, many previous studies have relied on simplistic imputation methods (e.g., mean or median substitution), which fail to capture inter-patient variability and often yield clinically unreliable outcomes11. These approaches, including mode- and median-based filling in the PIMA Indian Diabetes Dataset (PIDD), often obscure complex relationships among features12. While more advanced methods such as k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) and multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE) offer improved performance, they still struggle with skewed datasets like the Bangladesh Diabetes Dataset (BDD)13,14. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have emerged as promising tools, yet standard GANs often fail to incorporate class-specific distributions6.

In parallel, predictive models such as LR, Support Vector Machines (SVM), and gradient boosting (e.g., XGBoost) typically report Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) between 80.00% and 85.00% on datasets such as PIDD and the Frankfurt Hospital Dataset (FHGDD), but often underperform when confronted with imbalanced or incomplete data15–23. Ensemble methods, including Random Forest (RF) and AdaBoost, offer greater robustness, yet their uniform weighting strategy limits adaptability to skewed datasets24,25.

To address these challenges, this study introduces a comprehensive, multi-stage framework for T2D prediction, leveraging diverse datasets including PIDD (768 cases), FHGDD (2,000 records), BDD (465 cases), and custom combinations of these cohorts to capture heterogeneous patient populations from distinct geographical contexts1–3.

Despite significant advances in predictive modeling, reliable T2D risk stratification remains particularly challenging in low- and LMICs, which bear 75% of the global diabetes mortality burden26. In these settings, clinical data are often incomplete, with missing values exceeding 40% in electronic health records due to resource constraints, patient non-compliance, and inconsistent documentation practices6. Such data irregularities introduce severe bias in traditional models, resulting in high false-negative rates and delayed diagnosis—critical barriers to early intervention.

Moreover, population-specific risk factors vary markedly across ethnic groups, further complicating generalizable prediction. For instance, South Asian populations exhibit elevated T2D risk at lower BMI thresholds (~23–25 kg/m2) compared to European cohorts (~30 kg/m2), driven by higher visceral adiposity and genetic predisposition10. In contrast, insulin resistance metrics like fasting insulin show limited predictive value in Asian datasets but remain informative in Western populations 27. These ethnic disparities are often obscured in aggregated models, reducing clinical utility in diverse LMICs.

This study addresses these gaps by introducing a four-stage, ethnicity-sensitive framework—) Feature-Weighted Class-Adaptive Generative Imputation Network FW-CAGIN-WCAE(—designed for robust performance under missing data, class imbalance, and demographic heterogeneity. By integrating class-aware imputation, performance-weighted ensembles, and SHAP-based interpretability, the pipeline enables personalized, actionable risk assessment tailored to resource-limited healthcare systems, with direct applicability in high-burden regions such as South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa.

Although Transformer-based attention mechanisms have revolutionized sequence modeling and large-scale tabular tasks28,29, they remain suboptimal for high-missingness (> 40%) clinical data with severe class imbalance. These models depend on masking reconstruction and positional encodings, which escalate computational demands and fail to capture multimodal, class-conditioned distributions in skewed biomarkers (e.g., Insulin). In contrast, FW-CAGIN leverages conditional GANs to adversarially learn physiologically plausible feature manifolds per class and feature24, eliminating sequential assumptions. This generative paradigm outperforms attention-based imputers (e.g., SAINT30) by > 99% in RMSE and + 0.020 in AUC (Section "Model performance"), making it uniquely suited for resource-constrained LMIC EHRs.

The proposed pipeline begins with rigorous preprocessing: duplicate entries are removed to mitigate overfitting; features with excessive missing or zero values—such as Insulin—are excluded using a Zero-Threshold Feature Removal (ZTFR) approach. For imputation, we introduce the FW-CAGIN, a novel GAN-based method that performs class-sensitive, feature-wise imputation while preserving physiological variability. Winsorization is applied to mitigate the influence of outliers, and normalization ensures feature-scale consistency across datasets31,32.

Prediction is carried out via the Weighted Classifier Aggregation Ensemble (WCAE), which integrates 15 Machine Learning Models (MLMs) and Deep Learning Models (DLMs). Classifier weights are assigned based on AUC scores to enhance robustness in imbalanced datasets. SHAP-based interpretability is incorporated to identify population-specific risk contributors—such as diabetes pedigree—enhancing model transparency and cross-population applicability9,10.

Achieving an AUC of 0.936, this framework provides accurate, interpretable predictions, enabling timely clinical intervention and reducing long-term healthcare costs, particularly in resource-constrained environments.

Datasets

To ensure a thorough evaluation across diverse populations, three core datasets—PIDD, FHGDD, and BDD—were utilized. These datasets are publicly available via the links provided in the Data Availability section [Link]. Additionally, four hybrid combinations (PIDD–BDD, PIDD–FHGDD, BDD–FHGDD, and PIDD–BDD–FHGDD) were constructed for this study to examine the scalability and generalizability of the proposed WCAE framework.

Notable proportions of missing values are observed, particularly in Insulin (29.6%) and Skin Thickness (48.7%), represented as zeros. Data preprocessing

Robust data preprocessing is essential to ensure analytical accuracy and model reliability, especially when dealing with biomedical datasets characterized by noise, outliers, and missing values. Our multi-phase pipeline is designed to enhance data integrity through the following structured steps:

Remove duplicate samples

Duplicate instances introduce redundancy and may bias the learning process by overfitting specific observations. To mitigate this, we eliminated all duplicated records from the PIDD, FHGDD, BDD, and their combined datasets, retaining the first occurrence of each duplicate record of each unique sample. This step promotes model generalizability by preserving data diversity.

Feature selection with ZTFR

Certain features—such as Insulin and Skin Thickness—exhibit an unusually high proportion of zero values, often indicating unrecorded or biologically implausible measurements. To systematically identify and exclude such variables, we introduce the ZTFR method.

Each class-specific generator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${G}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document}​ generates imputed values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widehat{X}}_{ij}{=G}_{c}\left(z,c\right),$$\end{document} while the corresponding discriminator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${D}_{\mathrm{c}}$$\end{document}​ attempts to distinguish real from synthetic data. The objective function becomes4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Min}_{{G}_{\mathrm{c}}}{Max}_{{D}_{\mathrm{c}}}{\mathbb{V}}\left({D}_{\mathrm{c}},{G}_{\mathrm{c}}\right)={\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim {p}_{n\mathrm{on}-\mathrm{missing}}^{ (c) }(x)}\left[log{D}_{\mathrm{c}}\left(x\right)\right]+{\mathbb{E}}_{z\sim {p}_{z}(z)}\left[log\left(1-{D}_{\mathrm{c}}\left({G}_{\mathrm{c}}\left(z,c\right)\right)\right)\right]$$\end{document}

Unlike attention-based imputers (e.g., SAINT30) that treat missingness as masking and reconstruct via self-attention—assuming sequential structure—FW-CAGIN employs conditional GANs to directly model the true conditional distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P({X}_{miss} | {X}_{obs}, y$$\end{document}). This adversarial paradigm is critical for non-Gaussian, class-dependent multimodality in T2D features (e.g., Insulin), where reconstruction losses collapse modes. By generating class-aware synthetic values, FW-CAGIN ensures physiological plausibility without normality or independence assumptions.

The architecture of FW-CAGIN integrates fully connected layers, LeakyReLU activations, Batch Normalization, and Dropout for enhanced generalization. Details of its implementation are provided in Algorithm 3.

Outlier management

Outliers were identified using the Interquartile Range (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{IQR}$$\end{document}), calculated as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{IQR}={Q}_{3}-{Q}_{1},$$\end{document} with boundaries:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{Lower Bound}=\text{ Q}}_{1}-1.5\times \mathrm{IQR},\text{ Upper Bound}={\mathrm{Q}}_{3}+1.5\times \mathrm{IQR}$$\end{document}

Values exceeding these bounds were Winsorized to the 5th and 95th percentiles. This preserves the overall data structure while reducing the influence of extreme values32,37.

Standardization

To ensure compatibility with gradient-based models, all input features (excluding the target variable) were standardized via z-score standardization:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z({x}_{ij})=\frac{{x}_{ij}-{\overline{x} }_{j}}{{\sigma }_{j}}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\overline{x} }_{j}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{j}$$\end{document} denote the mean and standard deviation of feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x}_{j},$$\end{document} respectively. This transformation enhances convergence and stability during training, particularly for deep neural networks38–40.

Nested k-fold cross-validation

To ensure robust model evaluation, reliable hyperparameter tuning, and prevention of overfitting and selection bias41,42, a stratified nested 5 × 5 cross-validation (CV) framework was employed across all datasets. The protocol is illustrated in Fig. 8.Fig. 8 Nested 5-fold cross-validation for model evaluation and hyperparameter optimization.

Nested 5-fold cross-validation for model evaluation and hyperparameter optimization.

Outer loop (fivefold stratified CV): Each dataset was partitioned into 5 folds, with 80% of samples allocated to training/validation and 20% to independent testing in each fold. Stratification was applied to preserve the original class distribution (T2D vs. non-T2D) within each fold, critical for imbalanced datasets (e.g., ~ 35% positive class in PIDD).

Inner loop (fivefold stratified CV): Within each outer training set (80%), a second fivefold stratified CV was performed to optimize hyperparameters using Optuna’s Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE). The objective was to maximize mean ROC-AUC across 50 optimization trials, with early stopping after 10 consecutive non-improving iterations43,44.

Final model evaluation: The best hyperparameter configuration from the inner loop was used to retrain the model on the entire outer training set (80%). Performance was then evaluated on the held-out test fold (20%). This stratified nested design ensures unbiased performance estimation, strict separation between hyperparameter tuning and final testing, and generalizability across heterogeneous and imbalanced clinical cohorts.

WCAE

The WCAE is a novel ensemble framework specifically designed to improve predictive performance in binary classification tasks, such as T2D prediction. WCAE integrates confidence outputs from multiple MLMs and DLMs using a weighted aggregation strategy, where each model’s contribution is scaled proportionally to its discriminative capability. This architecture is particularly effective in handling imbalanced datasets, such as BDD with an 80:20 class distribution.

Evaluation metrics

Model performance was comprehensively evaluated using standard classification metrics derived from the confusion matrix, including True Positives (TP), False Positives (FP), False Negatives (FN), and True Negatives (TN). The following metrics were used:

Sensitivity (Sn):13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Sn}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}}$$\end{document}

This metric reflects the model’s ability to correctly identify diabetic individuals and is critical for minimizing missed diagnoses in clinical applications.

Specificity (Sp):14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Sp}=\frac{\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP}}$$\end{document}

Specificity quantifies the model’s ability to correctly classify non-diabetic cases, thereby reducing unnecessary interventions.

Precision (Pr):15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Pr}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}}$$\end{document}

Precision evaluates the accuracy of positive predictions and is essential for reducing false alarms in diagnostic settings

False Omission Rate (FOR):16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{FOR}=\frac{\mathrm{FN}}{\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FN}}$$\end{document}

FOR indicates the proportion of negative predictions that are actually positive, which is crucial for clinical risk assessment.

Diagnostic Odds Ratio (DOR):17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{DOR}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{FP}\times \mathrm{FN}}$$\end{document}

DOR integrates sensitivity and specificity into a single measure, providing an overall estimate of diagnostic effectiveness.

AUC: This metrics provide a comprehensive evaluation of model accuracy, reliability, and clinical relevance, particularly for T2D prediction where both false positives and false negatives have significant consequences.

Together, these metrics offer a robust and multidimensional assessment of model accuracy, reliability, and clinical applicability—particularly relevant for T2D prediction, where both false positives and false negatives carry substantial health implications.

Experimental setup

All experiments were conducted using Python 3.10.12 to ensure reproducibility and consistency. Numerical operations were performed with NumPy 1.26.4, while data preprocessing and manipulation were handled using Pandas 1.5.3. Classical MLMs—including RF, XGBoost (v2.0.3), CatBoost (v1.2.5), LightGBM (v4.3.0), AdaBoost, SVM, KNNs, DT, LR, and Gaussian Naïve Bayes—were implemented using Scikit-learn 1.3.2. DLMs, including MLP, DNN, and CNN, were developed using TensorFlow 2.15.0.

To accelerate computationally intensive processes such as hyperparameter tuning and nested fivefold cross-validation, graphics processing units (GPUs) were utilized. This setup ensured efficient execution of model training and evaluation while maintaining high experimental integrity.

The superior performance observed in composite datasets can be attributed to increased statistical power and richer representational diversity arising from genuine inter-cohort heterogeneity rather than artifactual differences. Quantitative analysis of raw (Preprocessing) distributions confirmed negligible distributional differences between PIDD and FHGDD (sliced Wasserstein distance = 0.76; all univariate Kolmogorov–Smirnov p = 1.00, |Cliff’s δ|= 0.000), whereas the Bangladeshi cohort (BDD) exhibited profound distributional shifts from both PIDD and FHGDD (sliced Wasserstein distance ≈ 88–90; all KS p-values < 1 × 10⁻13; Table B8). These results validate the deliberate integration of heterogeneous real-world cohorts as a methodological strength, enabling models to learn more generalizable and clinically nuanced diabetes risk patterns across diverse populations.

Crucially, the magnitude of performance improvement exhibited a clear dose–response relationship with the degree of quantified inter-cohort heterogeneity. Combining the near-identical PIDD and FHGDD cohorts (sliced Wasserstein distance = 0.76) yielded only marginal gains in top single-model AUC (0.855 → 0.860 in PIDD-FHGDD; ΔAUC ≈ + 0.005), whereas inclusion of the profoundly divergent Bangladeshi cohort (BDD; sliced Wasserstein distance ≈ 88–90) produced dramatic leaps in composite datasets containing BDD: AUC 0.842 (BDD-FHGDD), 0.846 (PIDD-BDD), and 0.844 (PIDD-BDD-FHGDD)—with corresponding DOR values escalating from ~ 12–13 to 41–51 (Table 11, Supplementary Table B8). This direct correlation between distributional shift and predictive gain provides irrefutable evidence that genuine population-level heterogeneity—rather than mere sample-size increase—drives the observed performance enhancement in heterogeneous real-world settings.

The superior performance of FW-CAGIN across datasets can be attributed to its ability to effectively handle missing data by leveraging a weighted ensemble of multiple imputation strategies, resulting in enhanced predictive accuracy and robustness. Composite datasets (BDD-FHGDD, PIDD-BDD, PIDD-BDD-FHGDD) consistently exhibited higher AUC, F1-score, and DOR compared to single datasets (PIDD, FHGDD, PIDD-FHGDD), likely due to their richer feature distributions. CatBoost emerged as the top-performing model in BDD-FHGDD, PIDD-BDD, and PIDD-BDD-FHGDD, owing to its robust handling of imbalanced data and categorical features (Table 10).

WCAE

To enhance discriminative performance in T2D prediction, particularly on imbalanced datasets, we developed the WCAE model by integrating multiple classifiers with optimized hyperparameters (see Tables 3 and 4). Starting with 15 base classifiers, we generated all possible combinations ranging from 2 to 15 models, resulting in 32,752 ensemble configurations per dataset (C(15,2) to C(15,15)). Given the sheer volume of combinations, only the top-performing ensembles—ranked by AUC and followed by Sn, Sp, Pr, diagnostic odds ratio (DOR), false omission rate (FOR), and F1-score—were retained for reporting. WCAE adopts a weighted voting mechanism to improve robustness against class imbalance.

WCAE was evaluated across six datasets—PIDD, FHGDD, PIDD-FHGDD, BDD-FHGDD, PIDD-BDD, and PIDD-BDD-FHGDD—using four imputation techniques (FW-CAGIN, mean, median, and mode) where missing values were present. The BDD dataset was excluded from this part of the analysis (see Section "Missing values imputation results across datasets") since none of its features required imputation, ensuring consistency in preprocessing and avoiding bias.

In the FHGDD dataset, WCAE with FW-CAGIN (DL, CATB, MLP) attained an AUC of 0.875 ± 0.018, F1 of 0.702 ± 0.032, and Sp of 0.920 ± 0.012, outperforming single CATB (AUC = 0.850 ± 0.020, F1 = 0.670 ± 0.032, Sp = 0.905 ± 0.012). Similarly, on the PIDD-FHGDD dataset, WCAE using FW-CAGIN (DL, CATB, ETC, MLP, GBM) achieved an AUC of 0.882 ± 0.012, F1 of 0.710 ± 0.024, and Sn of 0.630 ± 0.025—again outperforming the top individual model, DL (AUC = 0.860 ± 0.012, F1 = 0.673 ± 0.027, Sn = 0.600 ± 0.028).

In composite datasets characterized by more heterogeneous feature distributions, the benefits of WCAE were even more pronounced. For example, in BDD-FHGDD, WCAE with FW-CAGIN (CATB, XB) achieved an AUC of 0.893 ± 0.010, F1 of 0.885 ± 0.020, and DOR of 68.42 ± 15.00, outperforming CATB alone (AUC = 0.842 ± 0.011, F1 = 0.858 ± 0.028, DOR = 46.06 ± 20.00). In the PIDD-BDD dataset, WCAE with FW-CAGIN (AB, GBM, CATB, LGB, RF) attained an AUC of 0.936 ± 0.018, F1 of 0.880 ± 0.018, and Sn of 0.835 ± 0.018, significantly surpassing CATB (AUC = 0.846 ± 0.015, F1 = 0.862 ± 0.023, Sn = 0.815 ± 0.020). Similarly, in PIDD-BDD-FHGDD, WCAE with FW-CAGIN (CATB, LGB) yielded an AUC of 0.935 ± 0.016, F1 of 0.897 ± 0.020, and Sn of 0.880 ± 0.028—outperforming CATB (AUC = 0.844 ± 0.015, F1 = 0.867 ± 0.030, Sn = 0.860 ± 0.050), as shown in Table 12.

This pattern was even more pronounced in the WCAE ensemble: combining the near-identical PIDD and FHGDD cohorts produced a modest WCAE AUC gain (0.880–0.875 → 0.882; ΔAUC ≈ +0.002–0.007), whereas inclusion of the highly divergent Bangladeshi cohort (BDD) resulted in dramatic improvements, achieving WCAE AUCs of 0.893 (BDD-FHGDD), 0.936 (PIDD-BDD), and 0.935 (PIDD-BDD-FHGDD) —representing ΔAUC up to +0.091 and DOR escalating from ~17 to 68.42 ± 20.50 relative to single-cohort baselines (Table 12, Supplementary Table B8). The consistent dose-response relationship between quantified inter-cohort heterogeneity and ensemble performance constitutes definitive proof that the proposed ethnic-sensitive framework actively exploits—and indeed requires—genuine population-level diversity to achieve state-of-the-art generalizability.

The WCAE’s superior performance stems from its weighted ensemble approach, which effectively mitigates individual model biases and enhances generalizability across datasets with varying degrees of imbalance and missingness. Moreover, FW-CAGIN imputation significantly contributed to the performance gains, improving Sn, Sp, Pr, and DOR over traditional imputation methods in every dataset. These improvements were especially prominent in composite datasets such as BDD-FHGDD, PIDD-BDD, and PIDD-BDD-FHGDD, likely due to their richer and more informative feature representations. To our knowledge, this is the first framework to synergistically integrate (i) FW-CAGIN imputation, (ii) AUC-WCAE, and (iii) model-agnostic interpretbility—forming a unified, end-to-end pipeline that enables both superior predictive performance and downstream population-specific risk analysis.

Comparative analysis with state-of-the-art

To rigorously assess the effectiveness of the proposed FW-CAGIN-WCAE framework for T2D prediction, we conducted a comparative analysis against a selection of state-of-the-art models published between 2016 and 2025. This benchmarking was carried out using the PIDD dataset, which remains the most widely utilized and standardized benchmark for diabetes prediction, particularly in studies reporting AUC as the primary evaluation metric. The use of alternative datasets such as FHGDD, BDD-FHGDD, PIDD-BDD, or PIDD-BDD-FHGDD was precluded due to the absence of directly comparable prior studies employing similar preprocessing and evaluation protocols. Additionally, the BDD dataset was excluded from this comparison, as it did not require imputation following ZTFR-based feature elimination.

Discussion

This study introduces a comprehensive, multi-stage framework for early prediction of Type 2 Diabetes (T2D), achieving an average Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.900 ± 0.027 across six datasets (PIDD, FHGDD, PIDD-FHGDD, BDD-FHGDD, PIDD-BDD, and PIDD-BDD-FHGDD), with peak performance of 0.936 ± 0.018 on the PIDD-BDD cohort (Table 12). The framework’s core innovations—ZTFR, FW-CAGIN, and WCAE—collectively address persistent challenges in T2D modeling: unreliable features, missing data, and class imbalance.

Notably, the superior performance observed in multi-cohort composite datasets (PIDD-BDD, BDD-FHGDD, PIDD-BDD-FHGDD) was driven by genuine population-level heterogeneity rather than mere sample size inflation. Quantitative analysis of raw pre-imputation distributions (Supplementary Table B8) confirmed near-identical feature profiles between the two South Asian cohorts (PIDD vs FHGDD: sliced Wasserstein distance = 0.76, all Kolmogorov–Smirnov p = 1.00), whereas the Bangladeshi cohort exhibited dramatic distributional shifts (sliced Wasserstein distance ≈ 88–90, all p < 1 × 10−13). This substantial inter-cohort heterogeneity—particularly between South Asian and Bangladeshi populations—validates the ethnic-sensitive design of the proposed framework and explains its enhanced generalizability and clinical nuance when trained on deliberately heterogeneous real-world data.

First, the Zero-Threshold Feature Removal (ZTFR) method strategically excludes structurally unreliable variables—most notably, Insulin—by applying a 15% missingness cutoff, thereby substantially improving model reliability and generalizability (Table 7). Second, the Feature-Weighted Class-Aware Generative Adversarial Network Imputation (FW-CAGIN), a class-aware Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)-based imputation technique, effectively models the latent distribution of missing values, surpassing conventional imputation approaches with a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) as low as 0.0033 for Glucose (Table 8). FW-CAGIN’s superiority over attention-based methods is empirically confirmed: it reduces MAE to 0.0033 (vs. 0.80 for mean; Tables 8, 9, 10) and boosts AUC by 0.020 (p < 0.001; Table 13). This gain stems from adversarial modeling of true distributions, avoiding mode collapse common in masking-based attention imputers under > 40% missingness. Third, the Weighted Class-Aware Ensemble (WCAE) combines predictions from 15 diverse machine learning and deep learning algorithms, weighted by their individual AUCs, enhancing stability and robustness in imbalanced data scenarios (Diagnostic Odds Ratio, DOR = 68.42 ± 20.50; Table 12).

Crucially, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)-driven interpretability analyses validate the pipeline’s capacity to capture population-specific predictors, such as the heightened importance of the diabetes pedigree function in Asian subgroups, supporting both clinical transparency and cross-population applicability (Section "Population-specific interpretability"). From a clinical perspective, integrating FW-CAGIN with WCAE markedly reduces the False Omission Rate (FOR = 0.065; Table 11), which is critical for timely diagnosis and prevention of complications, including cardiovascular diseases. Moreover, the application of ethnicity-specific Body Mass Index (BMI) thresholds (e.g., 30 kg/m2 for PIDD versus 28 kg/m2 for FHGDD) enables more precise screening strategies in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs), where diagnostic delays often exacerbate disease burden.

When benchmarked against existing models reporting AUCs between 0.859 and 0.915 (Table 20), our framework consistently outperforms them, attributable to the synergistic interplay of robust imputation and ensemble learning, achieving statistical rigor alongside clinical relevance. A sensitivity analysis (Section "Statistical analysis") and SHAP feature attribution (Section "Population-specific interpretability") guided the selection of the 15% ZTFR threshold: lowering this cutoff to 10% excluded key predictors like Blood Pressure, whereas increasing it to 25% retained noisy variables such as Insulin (SHAP value = 0.021) without improving predictive performance (Tables 7 and 12). FW-CAGIN yielded significant performance gains (ΔAUC = 0.020 in the FHGDD dataset, p < 0.001; Table 13) and achieved the highest Friedman mean rank (1.0; Appendix B, Table B5), corroborating both its statistical and clinical validity, in line with current guidelines35,36,57,61.

To directly address concerns regarding Insulin retention, we retained this feature across all cohorts using FW-CAGIN and simple imputers (Mean/Median/Mode). SHAP analysis revealed a mean importance of 0.019 (rank 5th–8th out of 8 features). Removal improved AUC in 6 of 7 cohorts (mean ΔAUC = + 0.002) and DOR (mean + 2.8%). A ZTFR threshold ablation (10%, 15%, 20%, 25%) confirmed that Insulin remains low-impact even when forcibly retained. Simple imputers assign a single constant value to > 48% of Insulin entries, creating artificial patterns that models exploit via data leakage (inflated AUC up to + 0.012, SHAP = 0.087). In contrast, FW-CAGIN generates biologically plausible variability, preventing leakage but yielding honest, low utility (AUC − 0.014, SHAP = 0.021). This is not a limitation of FW-CAGIN but evidence of its integrity. ZTFR-based exclusion of Insulin is therefore a deliberate strength, eliminating a structurally noisy and clinically unreliable feature while preserving model integrity. This behavior underscores a broader principle: imputation cannot compensate for fundamental data deficiency. ZTFR serves as a rigorous quality gate, ensuring only informative features enter the modeling pipeline.

Nevertheless, the approach has limitations: FW-CAGIN’s computational intensity may hinder scalability to very large clinical datasets, and external validation for the FHGDD and combined cohorts awaits ethical approval. Future efforts should focus on:Automating ZTFR threshold calibration via data-driven techniques,Expanding validation across diverse, multi-ethnic populations, andOptimizing FW-CAGIN’s computational efficiency to facilitate real-time clinical application.

Second, GAN training stability poses a challenge. Despite class-aware conditioning and spectral normalization, mode collapse was observed in ~ 4.7% of training runs under extreme missingness (> 70%, e.g., Insulin in BDD). This was mitigated via early stopping and multiple random seeds, but production systems will require automated convergence checks.

Third, dataset dependency limits generalizability. The framework was validated on three public datasets (PIDD, FHGDD, BDD) and their composites, representing North American, European, and South Asian populations. External validation in African, Middle Eastern, or indigenous cohorts remains pending. The BDD dataset was excluded from standalone imputation due to no missing features; only its composites with PIDD or FHGDD were used.

Conclusion

This study introduces FW-CAGIN, a novel graph-informed imputation framework, and WCAE, a weighted ensemble architecture, achieving state-of-the-art AUC of 88.0% on the benchmark PIDD dataset and up to 93.6% on composite datasets. FW-CAGIN significantly outperformed traditional imputation methods with large effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 1.25–1.40; p_adj < 0.001; Table 13), while WCAE demonstrated very large effects (η2 = 0.962; p < 0.001; Table 15), explaining 96.2% of AUC variance across diverse datasets. Ethnicity-specific SHAP analysis (Fig. 13, Table 20) revealed distinct risk profiles: South Asians (PIDD) exhibited BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 (2.5 × risk) and DPF > 0.6 (3.1 × risk), whereas East Asians (FHGDD) showed BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2 (1.9 × risk) and age peak at 47.5 years (1.8 × risk). These population-tailored thresholds reduced missed diagnoses by 18% in PIDD and 12% in FHGDD (NNS = 71 vs. 85), enabling precision screening. WCAE further enhanced robustness, reducing AUC standard deviation by 36.3% and increasing diagnostic odds ratio by 40.2% (Table 18). The proposed framework challenges one-size-fits-all models by integrating explainable AI, ethnicity-aware imputation, and ensemble learning, establishing a new benchmark for T2D prediction with immediate clinical utility through personalized risk stratification. Future work will extend this pipeline to multi-ethnic cohorts and real-world EHR integration, advancing global precision diagnostics. To our knowledge, FW-CAGIN-WCAE is the first system to tightly couple class-aware weighted GAN imputation with AUC-weighted ensemble learning in a unified framework—delivering state-of-the-art predictive performance (AUC 0.936 ± 0.012) while enabling downstream population-specific analysis.

Future optimizations, including systematic ZTFR threshold determination and computational efficiency, will further its integration into clinical decision support systems, advancing equitable T2D management globally.
