Feasibility study of microbubble enhanced echocardiography in normal horses
Authors: Maria Luiza Favero, Paulo Aléscio Canola, Júlia Moslavacz de Sá
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intrapulmonary shunting, a contributor to hypoxemia in both anesthetized and spontaneously breathing horses, may occur in association with pulmonary disease, general inhalation anesthesia, or systemic inflammation. In human medicine, transthoracic microbubble-enhanced echocardiography is a widely used method to detect intrapulmonary right-to-left shunting, especially in hepatopulmonary syndrome. This study aimed to adapt and evaluate the feasibility of this technique in horses, laying the groundwork for future clinical applications. Twelve healthy horses underwent two treatments: (1) infusion of 25 ml and (2) infusion of 60 ml of microbubble solution. The solution, prepared with 60 ml syringes and a 3-way stopcock, was injected into the left external jugular vein, and echocardiographic recordings were taken during the subsequent cardiac cycles. Microbubble contrast was immediately visualized in the right heart chambers, persisting for 5–9 beats with 25 ml and 6.5–12.5 beats with 60 ml. The 60 ml volume was more effective, offering prolonged visualization for more detailed and reliable assessments. This method shows potential as a minimally invasive diagnostic tool for detecting intrapulmonary shunting in horses in clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-38819-7.

Introduction

Intrapulmonary shunting, defined as the passage of deoxygenated blood from the right to the left side of the circulation without participating in gas exchange, is a recognized contributor to hypoxemia in horses under various clinical conditions1. Although it is most described in the context of general anesthesia, due to atelectasis and increased pleural pressure, it can also occur in spontaneously breathing horses with pulmonary disorders such as pneumonia, pulmonary edema, or acute respiratory distress syndrome1,2. In anesthetized horses, calculated pulmonary shunt fractions range from 11% to 43%3.

These mechanisms may be exacerbated by systemic inflammatory conditions such as colic-associated sepsis, a frequent complication of gastrointestinal disorders in horses, largely driven by endotoxin release during bacterial lysis. This form of distributive shock impairs oxygen extraction at the tissue level due to peripheral vasodilation, affecting multiple organs, particularly the lungs4. Experimental studies have also shown that infusion of low doses of endotoxin (0.03–0.10 µg/kg) can induce moderate pulmonary hypertension5–8.

In humans, a clinically significant model of intrapulmonary shunting is hepatopulmonary syndrome (HPS), a complication of chronic liver disease characterized by intrapulmonary vascular dilatation and hypoxemia9,10. Diagnosis of HPS is primarily established using transthoracic contrast echocardiography, with microbubble-enhanced echocardiography being sensitive, minimally invasive, and gold-standard method9–11.

Microbubble contrast agents, when injected intravenously, are normally filtered by the pulmonary capillary bed. In the presence of intrapulmonary shunting or vascular dilatation, these microbubbles bypass the capillary filter and appear in the left atrium or ventricle within a few cardiac cycles, confirming the presence of abnormal right-to-left flow1.

The aim of this study was to adapt the microbubble-enhanced echocardiography technique—widely used in human medicine to detect intrapulmonary vascular abnormalities such as right-to-left shunting—for application in horses. If successfully adapted, this method could be used in future clinical investigations (not covered in this study) to identify intrapulmonary shunting associated with systemic inflammatory conditions (e.g., sepsis or SIRS), pulmonary diseases (e.g., pneumonia), or even secondary to physiologic changes during inhalation general anesthesia.

Materials and methods

The study was approved and supervised by the Institutional Animal Care and Ethics Committee (CEUA) under protocol # 012221/19. The horses used in the experiment were part of the university’s teaching and research herd. After the study was completed, these animals remained at the institution for educational and research purposes. The study was conducted in accordance with animal welfare guidelines and the regulations established by the university’s Animal Ethics Committee.

Twelve horses, six females and six gelded males, weighing 350–500 kg and ranging in age from two to fifteen years were included in the study. Horses were considered healthy based on clinical examination, pulmonary evaluation by thoracic ultrasound performed in five acoustic windows (three dorsal and two ventral) distributed in the lung field of the right and left hemithorax. The ultrasound examinations (pulmonary and echocardiography) were performed by a veterinarian with extensive experience in thoracic and cardiac ultrasonography in horses, who routinely performs these procedures in both clinical and research settings. Animals with any history of diseases of the lower respiratory tract were excluded.

Each animal received two different treatments as described below. A 5-minute wash-out period was allowed between treatments to ensure that the effects of the first infusion had dissipated before the second treatment began. Each treatment was performed in duplicate, and for each analysis, the passage of the microbubble contrast agent through the ventricles was videotaped. Each video recording lasted 40 s.

Animal preparation

On the days of data collection, horses were individually restrained in stocks inside a climatized room, with temperature set between 25 and 28 °C. After a 10-minute acclimation period, the hair was clipped from the mid-third of jugular groove to allow placement of a venous catheter into the left external jugular vein. Additionally, the hair was clipped from the right parasternal region, dorsal to the olecranon, between the 3rd and 5th intercostal spaces for echocardiographic evaluation in four-chamber long-axis view. Furthermore, five acoustic windows were created over the lung area in both the right and left hemithoraces for lung evaluation by thoracic ultrasound. These five windows included three dorsal and two ventral regions on each hemithorax. Specifically, the dorsal windows were located at the 7th, 9th, and 11th intercostal spaces, while the ventral windows were positioned at the 6th and 8th intercostal spaces.

During lung examination, the ultrasound pattern of the lung parenchyma and pleura was evaluated; sliding of the visceral pleura over the parietal pleura; presence or absence of irregularities in the pleura and interpleural space; presence or absence of air (pneumothorax) in the interpleural space, in the dorsal fields, and finally the presence or absence of fluid in the ventral fields.

A 40 cm infusion extension line was attached to the 14G catheter. This system was used to infuse the microbubble-enhanced contrast solution. The extension line was pre-filled with saline prior to the injection, and following the administration of the contrast agent, the line was flushed with saline to ensure complete delivery of the microbubble solution and to prevent any residual contrast from remaining in the tubing. Echocardiographic assessment was performed simultaneously as the contrast was being injected.

Microbubble-enhanced contrast solution

To prepare the solution, two liters of chilled saline solution were placed in a cooler with ice and kept between 0 °C and 4 °C. For each treatment, the microbubble solution was prepared immediately before the infusion. Two 60 ml syringes with Luer lock, connected to a 3-way stopcock were used for this purpose.

One syringe was prefilled with 22 ml of cooled saline and 3 ml of air, for treatment 1; or 54 ml of cooled saline and 6 ml of air, for treatment 2, while the other syringe remained empty. The system was set up so that the volume of “saline-air solution” could be transfused from one syringe to the other by compressing the plunger of the filled syringe. The solution was agitated by repeated (at least ten times) transfer between the 2 syringes by alternating compression of the plungers15.

After agitation (Fig. 1), the syringe was immediately connected to the PRN adapter and the solution was administered intravenously as quickly as possible using digital pressure on the syringe plunger. A predetermined volume was given for each treatment as described above. During preparation of the solution and prior to its administration, a second trained investigator positioned the ultrasound transducer to visualize the heart and record the passage of the microbubble solution during cardiac cycles.

Echocardiographic evaluation

Echocardiography was performed with an ultrasound machine (Esaote my Lab Alpha, Esaote, Italy, FAPESP 2018/24287-0) in B-mode using a sectorial multifrequency phased array transducer specific for cardiology, with a frequency variation between 1.0 and 4.0 MHz. As in human studies12, the heart was evaluated in its longitudinal axis (major axis), observing its four chambers in a two-dimensional 2D image13–16.

The transducer was positioned between the 3rd and 5th intercostal spaces in the right parasternal region, dorsal to the olecranon, so the heart could be evaluated in its longitudinal four-chamber axis, during the microbubble enhanced contrast administration. For each treatment, a 40-second video of the passage of the microbubble enhanced contrast through the cardiac chambers was recorded. During this time, between 20 and 36 heartbeats were recorded for each horse.

For both volumes (25 mL and 60 mL), contrast was administered in duplicate per horse. The number of cardiac cycles with visible and residual contrast in the right ventricle was recorded for each duplicate, and the mean of the two was used for statistical analysis and for descriptive statistics (median and range) presented in the text and Supplementary Table 1.

For the purpose of evaluation, two categories of contrast detection were defined. Visible contrast was considered when a clear and homogeneous opacification of the cardiac chambers occurred after intravenous injection of microbubbles. Residual contrast referred to a few scattered or faint microbubbles without chamber opacification, interpreted as remnants of a previous injection.

Results

There were no extensive or severe pleural or pulmonary changes limiting the analysis in any of the animals evaluated. For Treatment 1 (25 mL), the median number of heartbeats with visible contrast in the right ventricle was 5.5 (range: 5–9), and the median number of heartbeats with residual contrast was 22 (range: 14.5–29.5). For Treatment 2 (60 mL), contrast remained visible for a longer period, with a median of 9.5 heartbeats (range: 6.5–12.5), and residual contrast was observed for a median of 23 heartbeats (range: 18–34).

When analyzing individual animals (Supplementary Table 1), all horses showed a greater number of heartbeats with contrast persistence in Treatment 2 compared to Treatment 1. This pattern was consistent across both direct contrast visibility and residual contrast values. No contrast was observed in the left cardiac chambers in any of the animals, regardless of treatment. Discussion

Microbubble-enhanced echocardiographic contrast was consistently observed in the right cardiac chambers following both infusion volumes, remaining clearly and homogeneously visible for a median of 5.5 heartbeats (range 5–9) after the 25 mL infusion and 9.5 heartbeats (range 6.5–12.5) after the 60 mL infusion. No microbubbles were detected in the left heart chambers in any of the horses, indicating the absence of detectable intrapulmonary shunting under the conditions tested.

Under normal conditions, microbubbles with a diameter of 10 μm are absorbed by the alveoli or trapped in the human pulmonary capillary bed, where the average vessel diameter is 8 μm17–19. Therefore, they never reach the left side of the heart20. The contrast agent produced by agitation of saline solution produces microbubbles ranging from 24 to 180 μm in diameter17.

In human patients, it takes two to eight (mean five) heartbeats for the microbubbles to transit through dilated pulmonary vessels after contrast administration and reach the left ventricle17,21,22. The immediate passage of contrast, within two heartbeats after contrast administration, from the right to the left atrium indicates the presence of a right-to-left intracardiac shunt23,24. The interval between heartbeats required to observe the presence of contrast between the right and left sides of the heart has been considered a critical factor in differentiation of intracardiac and intrapulmonary shunts18,25. It is important to note that the behavior of the contrast with respect to the heartbeat is directly dependent on variations in blood pressure. This is because variations in local blood pressure directly affect the size and stability of the microbubbles26.

In all animals evaluated, microbubbles were seen only on the right side of the heart (Fig. 1B and C) suggesting that all animals evaluated had healthy lungs according to the ultrasound findings and clinical evaluation. The contrast effect in the right cardiac chambers was considered adequate for both contrast volumes tested, as it showed clear and homogeneous opacification, absence of excessive shadowing or swirling artifacts, and sufficient persistence of microbubbles to allow proper image interpretation. These characteristics indicate that both the contrast concentration and the injection technique were appropriate for generating reliable echocardiographic images27.

For a positive test result (i.e. showing intrapulmonary shunt) microbubbles should be observed in the left ventricle, indicating that the pulmonary capillary bed had failed to absorb the air bubbles28. This was not observed in any of the horses, despite evaluating five to ten heartbeats for the 25 ml contrast volume or seven to thirteen beats for the 60 ml contrast volume.

In the presence of disease, microbubbles reach the left side of the heart for two main reasons: (1) they are appropriately smaller than red blood cells and can pass through the pulmonary capillaries; and (2) because they are stable structures that can persist long enough to transit through the pulmonary capillary network and resist the pressures exerted by the left side of the heart27.

Microbubbles filled with ambient air are highly reflective of ultrasound and highly soluble. However, compared to commercially available preparations, they exhibit less persistence and stability because ambient air diffuses easily. In this regard, a larger volume of contrast agent could hypothetically ‘bypass’ the adverse properties of the microbubble contrast agent obtained by manual agitation. Technically, an adequate contrast study depends on the infusion of a sufficient volume of microbubbles and the use of appropriate ultrasound equipment27.

Both volumes of solution, 25 and 60 ml, were prepared using the same technique (passing saline between two syringes of the same volume using a 3-way stopcock), with the same agitation time. However, more pressure had to be applied to the 60 ml volume during agitation and administration due to its larger volume.

Based on the results of this study, we believe the 60 ml contrast volume is the most appropriate because the bubbles persisted for longer in the right side of the heart (seven to thirteen beats) compared to treatment 1 (25 ml). This persistence allows a longer time to evaluate the passage of the contrast agent is assessment of a potential injury, thus providing better evaluation and interpretation.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The study was conducted exclusively in healthy horses, which limits the ability to extrapolate the results to animals with pulmonary or systemic disease, where microbubble transit dynamics may differ. Additionally, while the technique demonstrated good opacification of the right heart, no intrapulmonary shunting was observed, preventing evaluation of the method’s sensitivity in detecting actual vascular abnormalities. The pulmonary capillary architecture and microbubble behavior in horses remain incompletely characterized, and these physiological factors may influence contrast passage in ways not fully understood. Nonetheless, the consistent imaging pattern and contrast persistence support the feasibility of the technique for future use in detrimental conditions.

Microbubble contrast enhanced echocardiography has been shown to be feasible in healthy horses and may be useful in identifying pulmonary lesions horses. The 60 ml volume appears to persist for longer and therefore is preferable to the 25 ml volume. The effectiveness of the technique in diagnosing pulmonary lesions in horses remains to be verified. The major advantage of the technique over other available diagnostic methods, such as lung scintigraphy with technetium-labeled albumin macroaggregates (MAA99mTc) and pulmonary angiography29, is that it is a low-cost, easy-to-use diagnostic method and, most importantly, it can be performed in vivo in horses with potential lung lesions.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the relationship between the volume of agitated saline infused and the persistence of contrast in the right heart during transthoracic echocardiography in horses. Although similar contrast techniques have been used in humans for decades, their adaptation to equine patients, particularly under clinical and hospital conditions, is novel and may serve as a foundation for future diagnostic applications in horses with suspected pulmonary or systemic inflammatory disease.
