Autonomous RCM-less endoscope control: integrating force-based pivoting with deep learning visual servoing
Authors: Carlos Fontúrbel, Ana Cisnal, Diego Benavides-Cobos, Juan Carlos Fraile-Marinero, Javier Pérez-Turiel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Robotic endoscope holders improve visual stability, yet enforcing a fixed remote center of motion (RCM) often induces lateral interaction forces due to fulcrum drift. This study proposes an autonomous guidance framework combining deep learning–based visual servoing with force-based pivoting to track surgical instruments while minimizing interaction forces, without a fixed geometric RCM. To test this, a UR3e manipulator with an integrated force/torque sensor controlled a laparoscope in a pelvitrainer. Instruments were localized using a fine-tuned YOLOv11n on 3,695 annotated frames. The detected tools’ centroids drove a hysteresis-based visual servoing law with jerk-limited planning, while an admittance-based controller generated angular motion from measured forces. Performance was assessed in continuous tracking and step-response tasks, comparing fixed-RCM with the proposed pivoting approach. The localization model achieved 0.91–0.93 precision, 0.83–0.90 mAP, and 28 FPS on a CPU. Visual servoing re-centered targets in 98.8% of transitions (median recovery: 2.60 s). Force-based pivoting significantly outperformed fixed-RCM control, reducing median interaction forces from 2.35 to 0.19 N. Integrating CNN-driven servoing with force-based pivoting enables autonomous guidance that preserves visual stability while substantially reducing interaction forces. By eliminating fixed RCM assumptions, this framework offers a safer, more adaptive alternative for robotic camera assistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54550-9.

Introduction

Minimally Invasive Surgery (MIS) has become the standard approach for a wide range of procedures, owing to its well-established clinical benefits, including reduced postoperative pain and faster patient recovery1. Robot-assisted laparoscopic surgery (RALS) has augmented surgical dexterity by increasing precision, filtering physiological tremors and reducing surgeon fatigue through optimized ergonomics2. Within this context, robotic endoscope holders (REHs) have emerged as auxiliary systems dedicated to automatic camera guidance, aiming to improve image stability and relieve assistants from highly demanding and error-prone tasks3–5.

Despite these technical advantages, the clinical superiority of robotic over manual laparoscopy remains unclear due to conflicting results regarding postoperative recovery and pain1,6, often attributed to the loss of haptic feedback and the rigid mechanical interaction with the abdominal wall6. Excessive lateral forces at the incision have been associated with increased mechanical stress, which may contribute to postoperative pain, compromise pneumoperitoneum, and increase the risk of port-site complications. During manual laparoscopy, surgeons intuitively mitigate such trauma using haptic feedback, although non-negligible forces (typically up to 3 N) are still exerted at the trocar7. Achieving improved atraumatic interaction remains a key challenge for robotic systems.

To address this issue, most commercial laparoscopic robotic platforms rely on the Remote Center of Motion (RCM) principle, which assumes a fixed entry point through the trocar8–11. While accurate RCM alignment can theoretically ensure zero lateral forces at the incision12, defining the pivot geometrically is inherently problematic13. The effective fulcrum often lies within the thickness of the abdominal wall rather than at a well-defined surface location14. While mechanical ventilation induces mild volumetric fluctuations of the abdominal cavity15, the fulcrum misalignment arises primarily from initial setup inaccuracies and local tissue deformation within the abdominal wall during tool manipulation. As a result, even an initially well-aligned RCM is subject to inevitable drift as the procedure advances, introducing uncontrolled mechanical stress at the trocar. To minimize these effects, systems such as the da Vinci require complex preoperative setup and docking procedures to enforce a rigid geometric constraint that cannot adapt to these dynamic changes16,17.

Adaptive RCM approaches have been proposed to mitigate these limitations, but they often depend on real-time estimation of the trocar position and additional cameras or sensors that may clutter the surgical scenario13,18–21. Moreover, reliance on geometric tracking makes these systems vulnerable to inaccuracies caused by visual occlusions or sensor drift. To overcome these issues, we previously introduced a force-based pivoting control strategy22 which eliminates the need for a predefined geometric RCM. Following similar principles than23,24 by continuously adjusting endoscope orientation based on measured interaction forces, the effective fulcrum self-aligns with the incision, reducing lateral loads while naturally adapting to tissue deformation and motion.

In parallel, REHs traditionally have relied on additional user interfaces (e.g. joystick, gaze, voice, head gesture)25. However, these interfaces may increase surgeon cognitive load or necessitate external markers, imposing additional hardware and line-of-sight constraints within the operating room26. In contrast, advances in deep learning–based approaches enable robust, markerless instrument detection under intraoperative conditions, supporting autonomous visual servoing27,28.

Despite these individual advancements in vision and control, existing systems remain fragmented; they either rely on rigid kinematic assumptions potentially compromising tissue safety or prolonging recovery, or depend on control interfaces that disrupt clinical workflow. To address this, the primary contribution of this work is the development and validation of the first fully autonomous, RCM-less endoscope control framework that actively bridges this gap. In this study, we integrate and evaluate an adaptive force-based pivoting strategy with convolutional neural network (CNN)-driven visual servoing to achieve autonomous REH guidance that prioritizes both visual stability and atraumatic interaction. Instrument positions are detected in real time using a lightweight YOLOv11n model29, while a force-compliant controller minimizes interaction forces during camera motion.

Results

Instrument localization model performance

The fine-tuned YOLOv11n achieved reliable instrument detection on the test set (Table 1). In single-tool trials, the grey tool achieved a precision of 0.79, whereas in dual-tool scenarios precision exceeded 0.90 for both instruments. Furthermore, the model demonstrated absolute specificity against the biomimetic background, yielding zero false positive detections across the standalone set of 102 background-only images.

To further evaluate robustness under clinically relevant visual artifacts, a synthetic sensitivity analysis was conducted across the test set by applying progressive contrast reduction (α) and gain shifts (β) to simulate smoke and lens fogging. Under mild degradation (α = 0.8, β = 10), performance remained comparable to baseline (recall 86.8%, precision 92.0%). Under moderate degradation (α = 0.5, β = 30), recall decreased to 70.9%, while precision increased to 94.5%. Under severe simulated smoke (α = 0.3, β = 50), recall dropped markedly to 18.9%, indicating severe perception loss, whereas precision peaked at 98.9%. Notably, no false positives were observed on background-only frames across all degradation levels. This behavior ensures that under degraded visibility the system transitions to perception loss rather than producing erroneous detections.

From a control perspective, image-level detection rates indicated high signal availability for visual servoing. In single-tool trials, the correct detection rate was 92.35% (grey tool only), with 0% false positive detections for the black tool. In dual-tool scenarios, simultaneous detection of both instruments was dominant (76.28%), while single-tool detections occurred less frequently (7.65% grey only and 14.54% black only). Instances of no detection were rare (1.53%), indicating a low perception-loss rate that minimizes tracking interruptions.

Running on a standard CPU, the model achieved a median inference time of 37 ms per frame (≈ 28 FPS), providing sufficient temporal redundancy for the visual servoing control loop and ensuring robust operation despite occasional detection dropouts.

Visual servoing dynamics

Each experimental run had a mean duration of 178.8 s, with target updates occurring at a mean interval of 8.13 ± 0.11 s, comprising a sequence of 22 displacements per test. The controller successfully re-centered the instruments in 98.8% of target transitions, with a median recovery time of 2.60 s. The recovery-time distribution (Fig. 1a) is positively skewed, indicating rapid convergence in most cases, with occasional delays associated with aggressive operator maneuvers or transient tracking loss.

Spatial analysis of steady-state behavior (Fig. 1b) revealed a stable low-amplitude limit-cycle pattern near the target and transition zones. This indicates that the controller maintains a dynamic equilibrium at the central deadband (Zone A). Excursions into the outer violation zone occurred only transiently, indicating rapid correction of large deviations and consistent maintenance of the tools within the camera FoV.

Step-response analysis

Figure 3 illustrates the dynamic response of the proposed system to a step disturbance mimicking a sudden loss of visual centering. At t = 0 (Fig. 3a), the target is detected in the violation zone (Zone C), triggering a corrective camera motion that centers the target within 2.44 s (Fig. 3b). The corresponding evolution of visual error (Fig. 3c) shows a rapid and monotonic decay.

The Cartesian velocity profile (Fig. 3d) demonstrates smooth and well-regulated motion during recovery. Rather than exhibiting a bang–bang response, the camera accelerates progressively to its saturation velocity and decelerates without overshoot; the controller anticipates the entry into Zone A and ramps down velocity to achieve a critically damped response.

Notably, interaction forces remain low throughout the transient response (Fig. 3e). Although a brief peak force (Fp= 1.17 N) is observed during the initial acceleration phase, its magnitude remains well below clinically relevant thresholds. The angular velocity profile (Fig. 3f) exhibits smooth rotational dynamics, indicating coordinated translational-rotational behavior during pivoting. Once the target enters Zone A and the camera tip stops, the pivoting motion dissipates residual forces, which rapidly decay to negligible levels, demonstrating the effective mitigation of mechanical stress at the incision site.

Discussion

This study addresses a central yet often underestimated challenge in robotic laparoscopic surgery: achieving stable visualization while preserving atraumatic interaction at the trocar site. While recent advances have focused heavily on improving tracking algorithms5,27,28, the physical interaction between robotic instruments and the abdominal wall remains a critical and often overlooked determinant of postoperative recovery6. Our results demonstrate that integrating a force-based pivoting strategy with deep learning-based visual servoing effectively overcomes key limitations of fixed-RCM approaches, enabling autonomous endoscope guidance that adapts to the deformable nature of the abdominal wall.

The proposed tracking framework was designed to meet the latency and precision requirements of the visual servoing control loop without relying on high-performance computing infrastructure. Prior vision models for laparoscopic tool tracking have largely been developed for offline analysis, often favouring architectural complexity over real-time performance. For example, recurrent architectures such as MTRCNet31 achieve high precision (89.1%) but operate at low inference speeds (~ 3.3 FPS), even on high-end GPUs, while segmentation-based pipelines report moderate precision (72.45%) and limited update rates (9 Hz)5. Building on our prior findings showing limited generalization of CPU-based hourglass models under domain shifts (dropping to ~ 43% accuracy)27, and noting that early YOLO-based approaches achieved high inference rates (48.9 FPS) but insufficient precision for safe camera control (72.26%)32, our task-specific implementation of the YOLOv11n architecture overcomes these historical trade-offs. Supported by recent cadaveric evidence of robust YOLOv11m-seg feature extraction (F1-score 0.94; 36 FPS)33, the proposed system achieves high precision (89.4%) at ~ 28 FPS on a standard CPU, enabling reliable real-time visual servoing.

Additionally, the perception module exhibits fail-safe behavior against contrast-degrading conditions such as smoke or lens fogging, which induce non-linear distortions and degrade image quality34. Under these conditions, the model maintains high precision while recall decreased, avoiding erroneous localization. This asymmetric degradation is desirable for visual servoing, as false positives may induce unintended camera motion, whereas false negatives result only in temporary perception loss. While recent approaches address such variability through advanced feature extraction strategies, such as spectral-based pooling paradigms35 and contrast-aware attention mechanisms36, these methods often rely on increased model complexity and hardware acceleration.

The definition of the visual reference point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{s}$$\end{document} is also relevant. Equal weighting of instrument centroids was adopted as a methodological simplification for evaluation, although this does not fully reflect the asymmetric roles of surgical tools in clinical practice. Importantly, the proposed framework inherently supports dynamic weight allocation, enabling prioritization of the active instrument and adaptation to task-specific requirements.

The integration of force-based pivoting with visual servoing proved robust despite intermittent perception loss. Although transient tracking interruptions were observed, the combination of high-level visual servoing and low-level jerk-limited motion planning ensured smooth camera behavior and high recovery success. Step-response analysis confirmed that large visual errors were corrected rapidly, without overshoot and without compromising safety. These results indicate that autonomous camera guidance can be both responsive and atraumatic.

Interaction forces under fixed-RCM control are strongly influenced by the patient-specific viscoelastic properties of the abdominal wall, which cannot be adequately captured by a static geometric constraint13. In our experiments, the fixed-RCM control produced sustained lateral forces, whereas the proposed force-based pivoting continuously adapted the effective fulcrum to tissue deformation, significantly reducing interaction forces (median 0.19 N vs. 2.35 N), and effectively decoupling robotic motion from abdominal wall resistance.

From a clinical perspective, this approach effectively bridges the gap between the tissue-preservation benefits of manual laparoscopy and the operational stability of robotic assistance. While manual surgery currently tends to offer superior postoperative recovery compared to rigid robotic systems1,6 due to the surgeon’s intuitive compliance, it remains subject to human limitations, including physiological tremor, fatigue, and cumulative interaction forces (typically 0–3 N)7. The proposed system replicates the compliant nature of a manual assistant by regulating interaction forces at negligible levels. Furthermore, by automating camera guidance, the system provides stable visualization and eliminates the need for manual camera operation, which has been associated with ergonomic strain during laparoscopic assistance37, improving ergonomic conditions and reducing the assistant’s workload.

From translational and clinical adoption, the proposed framework benefits from minimal hardware requirements. The system operates on a standard CPU and relies solely on the F/T sensor already integrated into the robot end-effector and the conventional laparoscopic camera routinely used in clinical practice, without the need for external cameras, markers, or additional sensing infrastructure. In contrast to approaches that require continuous surgeon input25 or rely on external vision systems prone to occlusions26, the proposed method enables fully autonomous camera guidance while preserving robustness and simplicity. Although brief control interruptions were observed, these are expected to be further mitigated in clinical practice through sensorimotor co-adaptation, whereby surgeons naturally adjust instrument handling to maintain favorable visual conditions.

Regarding safety, the proposed layered design provides an intrinsic safety mechanism that remains effective during transient perception failures or external disturbances. When visual feedback is temporarily unavailable (e.g., due to occlusions exceeding 0.5 s), the jerk-limited planner ensures smooth deceleration to a controlled stop. In parallel, the force-based pivoting controller operates independently of the visual loop and remains continuously active, ensuring that the robot always yields to external interaction forces arising from physiological motion, trocar friction, or even, accidental contact22. At the low-level control layer, both velocity and acceleration are explicitly bound. In the event of force–torque sensor disconnection or failure, the system automatically transitions to a safe locked state, preventing uncontrolled motion. Overall, this behavior is consistent with the principles of intrinsic safety and graceful degradation required for medical electrical equipment outlined in IEC 60601-1.

A major limitation must be acknowledged: validation was performed in a pelvitrainer, which does not fully reproduce physiological motion or patient-specific variability in tissue compliance properties. Nevertheless, this setup represents a conservative scenario inherently favorable to fixed-RCM strategies, as in vivo dynamics would further exacerbate fulcrum displacement, and therefore, interaction forces, which the pivoting control has been shown to mitigate22. Additionally, interaction forces were measured at the robot end-effector (F/T sensor) rather than directly at the trocar site. While direct measurement would provide a more accurate assessment of tissue interaction, it would require custom-instrumented hardware, limiting clinical applicability. Therefore, the measured force is used as a proxy, enabling consistent comparison across control strategies. Furthermore, although the impact of contrast-degrading artifacts has been synthetically evaluated, instrument detection may still be affected by other physical intraoperative artifacts such as active bleeding or solid occlusions.

Future work will focus on cadaveric and in vivo studies to assess performance under these broader realistic surgical conditions. Furthermore, managing pivoting control during tissue-tool interaction remains an open challenge. Some approaches aim to decouple tissue-abdominal forces, although these typically require patient-specific modelling38. In this context, Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)39 offer a potential framework to capture the non-linear dynamics of the abdominal wall in real-time settings. Additionally, multi-modal approaches such as imitation learning could integrate force and visual feedback to estimate tissue deformation and interaction dynamics40, replicating optimal pivoting behaviour.

In conclusion, this work challenges the long-standing reliance on fixed geometric constraints in robotic laparoscopy by presenting an autonomous REH control framework. The proposed system combines deep learning-based visual servoing with force-based pivoting to achieve stable visualization while minimizing interaction forces. By abandoning the assumption of a fixed geometric remote center of motion, the proposed approach adapts naturally to the deformable and dynamic nature of the abdominal wall, addressing a key limitation of conventional robotic camera assistance. Experimental results demonstrate that the system maintains robust visual centering while significantly reducing interaction forces (median 0.19 N vs. 2.35 N), without increasing hardware complexity or disrupting surgical workflow. By aligning autonomous camera guidance with fundamental surgical principles of tissue preservation, the proposed framework advances robotic endoscope holders toward safer, less traumatic, and clinically compatible behavior.

Methods

Hardware, experimental setup and vision database

Experiments were conducted using a UR3e robotic manipulator (Universal Robots) equipped with an integrated force/torque (F/T) sensor and a laparoscopic vision system (HOPKINS 0°, 640 × 480 px, 25 FPS). The setup utilized a SZABO-BERCI-SACKIER pelvitrainer (Karl Storz) to emulate a minimally invasive surgical environment (Fig. 4a), where two standard laparoscopic instruments (Clickline grasper and 33510 UO Forceps; Karl Storz) were manipulated.

The system was implemented within a Robot Operating System (ROS) framework and executed on a standard CPU-based workstation (Intel® Core™ i5-14500, 2.60 GHz, 32 GB RAM) running Ubuntu 18.04.

Experiments were performed in a visually challenging environment using a heterogeneous biomimetic background, overlaid with a grid (Fig. 4b), which was deliberately excluded from model training and validation to assess generalization. Model development relied on a dataset of 3695 laparoscopic frames acquired under three background configurations (silicone phantom, blue, and white) covering single-tool, dual-tool, and background-only scenarios, and randomly split into 76% training and 24% validation sets. This variability, combined with class-specific annotation, was intended to promote morphology-driven instrument detection rather than background-dependent feature learning. For evaluation, an independent test set was constructed from experimental recordings with the biomimetic background, comprising 690 images: 102 background-only, 196 grey-tool-only, and 392 dual-tool frames.

Autonomous robotic endoscope control framework

The proposed REC system integrates real-time visual tracking, visual servoing and force-compliant control into a unified closed-loop architecture (Fig. 4d). The objective is to maintain the surgical instruments centered within the camera field of view (FoV) while minimizing interaction forces at the trocar site.

Yolov11n and visual servoing

Instrument localization is performed using a CNN based on a lightweight YOLOv11n architecture, initialized with pretrained COCO weights41 and fine-tuned on our dataset. The lightweight architecture was selected to enable real-time inference on standard CPU-based systems. The fine-tuning procedure and training hyperparameters are provided in Supplementary Table S1 online. Experimental protocol

Two complementary experiments were conducted to evaluate system performance: (1) continuous tracking experiments to assess steady-state behavior during sustained operator interaction, and (2) a step-response analysis to characterize the system’s transient response under worst-case initial error conditions.

Tool-localization performance

The model performance was evaluated using precision, recall, and mean average precision (mAP), with true positives defined at an Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold of 0.7. To assess the perception module’s robustness under specific visual artifacts, a synthetic sensitivity analysis was conducted across the test set by applying progressive contrast reduction (α) and gain shifts (β) to simulate smoke and fogging. To assess signal availability for robot control, image-level detection rates were computed for each class (grey and black tool), their joint detection, and complete miss events, normalized by the total number of images. Additionally, detection persistence was evaluated to quantify the percentage of time a specific detection state was maintained continuously for more than 0.5 s. These were computed on the validation set using a confidence threshold of 0.4, which was later used for real-time experiments. This value was set to filter out low-confidence artifacts while maintaining a high detection rate for continuous and robust tracking.

Continuous tracking

Continuous tracking experiments were designed to evaluate camera stability and force interaction under controlled yet challenging conditions using both fixed-RCM and the proposed force-based pivoting control. Surgical instruments were displaced across a predefined 3 × 3 grid uniformly spaced at 50 mm intervals within the workspace (Fig. 4b), providing repeatable target locations for visual servoing assessment. To minimize operator-dependent bias and sensorimotor learning effects, all experiments were performed by a novice operator without prior laparoscopic experience.

Three experimental trials were conducted in a single-tool configuration and one in a dual-tool configuration (a representative demonstration of the system’s operation in both scenarios is provided in SI Video 1). In single-tool trials, one instrument was actively manipulated toward successive grid targets, whereas in the dual-tool condition, two instruments were moved simultaneously in opposing directions to increase task complexity. Target grid updates occurred every 8 s, with transitions restricted to orthogonally adjacent grid points. Identical target sequences were used for both control strategies to ensure comparable experimental conditions.

To assess robustness under varying kinematic constraints, instruments were inserted through different trocar ports for each test, while the camera port remained fixed to simulate a standard umbilical insertion (Fig. 4a). The camera height was calibrated such that lateral grid points lay near the limits of the camera FoV, intentionally imposing worst-case target conditions. For each configuration, matched trials were performed using both fixed-RCM and force-based pivoting control under equivalent geometric conditions, enabling direct comparison between control approaches.

In the fixed-RCM experiments, the Remote Center of Motion (RCM) was manually aligned with the incision point. For the pivoting trials, the feedforward plane height was explicitly configured to match the vertical baseline of the corresponding fixed-RCM test.

The experimental layout and port distribution are illustrated in Fig. 4b-c. In all the experiments, the robotic endoscope holder was positioned at the central umbilical port (port 9), while one or two laparoscopic instruments were inserted through peripheral ports, depending on the specific scenario. For the three single-tool experiments (Tests 1–3), the instruments were inserted through ports 7, 1, and 2, resulting in calibrated heights (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Z}^{B}$$\end{document}) of 129 mm, 130 mm, and 133 mm, respectively. For the dual-tool experiment (Test 4), the instruments were inserted through ports 8 and 6, with a calibrated height of 131 mm.

System performance was evaluated along three dimensions: (i) perception reliability, quantified by sustained tracking interruptions; (ii) visual servoing performance, assessed through convergence success, recovery time, and steady-state spatial distribution of the reference point; and (iii) physical interaction, quantified through forces measured at the end-effector as a proxy for trocar interaction forces. Statistical comparisons between control strategies were assessed using a two-sided Mann-Whitney U test.

Step-response analysis

A step-response experiment was designed to characterize the dynamic behavior of the controller under worst-case operating conditions. The instruments were initially placed at the edge of the camera FoV, corresponding to a maximal tracking error. This scenario reproduces clinically relevant situations, such as tool re-acquisition following temporally visual tracking loss or the entry of a new instrument into the surgical field. System performance was evaluated by the temporal evolution of the visual error, kinematic profile, and the measured interaction forces.
