Cell-type specific early perception of nine phytohormones revealed by single-nucleus transcriptomics in Arabidopsis
Authors: Zhijian Liu, Zhuowen Li, Yuzhuo Wang, Yanping Long, Hongming Zhao, Yuwei Qin, Xinlong Zhu, Hongwei Guo, Kai Jiang, Jixian Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlant development and stress responses are coordinated through phytohormone-mediated gene networks, yet resolving their spatiotemporal crosstalk remains challenging. Here, we generate a single-nucleus transcriptomic atlas of Arabidopsis seedlings, capturing early (0.5 h, 3 h) responses to 9 hormones across ~500,000 nuclei, including auxin, cytokinin, ABA, gibberellin, strigolactone, brassinosteroid, ethylene, JA, and SA. Within this window, most hormones showed rapid and cell-type-specific responses, whereas JA, SA, ABA showed more sustained and convergent responses by 3 h. Co-directional transcriptomic overlap was strongest at 0.5 h, while the JA-, SA-, and ABA-related responses showed the highest overlap at 3 h. Pathway-level analysis indicated asymmetric relationships among JA, SA, and ABA across biosynthetic and catabolic layers. Spatially, transcriptomic response overlap separated shoots from other tissues, with guard cells as shoot-side outliers showing weak JA–SA–ABA overlap. We further identified an SA-induced guard-cell-specific MYB60-centered module linked to ABA-associated stomatal regulators, suggesting a circuit that may fine-tune stomatal dynamics. Together, this atlas provides a high-resolution view of phytohormone response dynamics and interactions.Subject terms: Plant hormones, Transcriptomics, Plant signalling

Introduction

Plant growth, development, and stress adaptation are orchestrated by a complex network of phytohormones1–4. Classic hormones such as auxin, cytokinin, gibberellin, abscisic acid, ethylene, brassinosteroid, jasmonic acid, strigolactone and salicylic acid collectively regulate every aspect of a plant’s life cycle—from embryogenesis and organogenesis to defense against pathogens and abiotic stress5–12. Despite their broad systemic roles, hormonal functions are often highly context-dependent and even cell-type specific13,14. Different cell types can interpret the same hormonal cue in distinct ways. For example, abscisic acid induces stomatal closure in plant leaves15,16, and inhibits the lateral root formation in the root17. Auxin and cytokinin exhibit a mutually inhibitory interaction that spatially delineates protoxylem cell fate in the root, demonstrating how hormonal output is confined to specific cell types18. The localized regulation of hormones is influenced by several factors, including variations in hormone concentration distribution and differentiation of hormone signaling reception systems among cell types19. Meanwhile, the signaling pathways of different phytohormones are interconnected, and different hormones function synergistically or antagonistically, and at some condition, one hormone can induce or inhibit the production of the other2,6,20–24. This hormone–hormone crosstalk enables plants to fine-tune their growth and stress responses25. For example, evidence indicates that a subset of genes is co-responsive to both brassinosteroids and auxin, and is indispensable for cellular expansion26. By contrast, Auxin and cytokinin often have inverse effects on organ growth and patterning, and in the root meristem, they create an inhibitory feedback loop that sharpens developmental boundaries18. Together, such findings from diverse hormone pairs highlight a regulatory network of hormonal synergy and antagonism, whereby plant outputs emerge from the integration of multiple hormonal signals.

Parsing crosstalk among phytohormone pathways is difficult because each pathway branches into cell‑type‑specific modules that integrate both developmental programs and stress cues. Pioneering whole‑seedling microarray work in Arabidopsis found that seven canonical hormones share only a handful of early transcriptional targets, despite controlling similar physiological processes27. Such limited overlap is at least in part from the cellular averaging inherent to bulk tissues: cell identity and developmental stage profoundly constrain transcriptional outputs to a given stimulus19. For example, ethylene promotes the auxin-mediated growth inhibition effect in the root epidermis elongation zone, thereby inhibiting primary root elongation, while it inhibits the auxin-mediated effect during lateral root formation28,29. Besides, time-series ABA transcriptome and TF-binding analyses30 have shown that ABA-responsive transcriptional programs continue to evolve over several hours after treatment, rather than being fully captured by a single early time point. Recent genetically encoded hormone biosensors31,32, including SALICYLIC ACID SENSOR1 for SA and GIBBERELLIN PERCEPTION SENSOR2 for GA, further illustrate the value of resolving hormone dynamics at cellular resolution. These studies suggested that hormone responses are highly spatiotemporally dynamic, providing plants with the flexibility to balance growth and defense19. Thus, mapping hormone responses and their intersections at tissue and single-cell resolution is essential for uncovering latent regulatory nodes.

Single-cell and single-nucleus RNA sequencing allow for the detection of cell-to-cell variability in gene expression and are more sensitive and suitable for studying genes with tissue-specific responses to single or multiple hormones. Recently, several studies have been conducted to explore the transcriptomic landscape of plant hormones at the cell-type or single-cell level33–38. These studies unraveled cell type-specific response to auxin, brassinosteroid, jasmonic acid, abscisic acid and cytokinin, and respectively identified novel responsive genes and potential transcriptional regulators, suggesting a great potential of single-cell sequencing in phytohormone studies. Despite this, current single-cell transcriptome analysis across different hormones faces several limitations. One major challenge is the standardization of experimental protocols and data analysis pipelines, as different studies may employ varying methods and technologies, making it difficult to directly compare results across studies39. The currently available hormone-related single-cell datasets of hormones were all generated from protoplast, which could lead to unexpected transcriptomic changes and time lag during the protoplast preparation and impact the interpretation of true phytohormone response. Besides, there is still a lack of single-cell transcriptomic datasets of phytohormones from the whole seedlings. Taken together restrict our comprehensive understanding of the interactions between different hormones at the single-cell level.

Compared with protoplast-based single-cell approaches, snRNA-seq avoids protoplasts at the cost of reduced gene detection in individual cells, thereby minimizing stress-induced transcriptional artifacts40,41. Moreover, because nascent transcripts are enriched in the nucleus relative to whole-cell42, snRNA-seq is particularly well suited to capturing the earliest phases of transcriptional responses. In this work, we utilized snRNA-seq to capture the early transcriptional responses (0.5 and 3 h) of Arabidopsis seedlings to 9 major phytohormones alongside a mock treatment, generating in total 40 libraries including 2 replicates for each treatment. Our work provides a comprehensive dataset with high cell type resolution at single-cell level to compare the divergently and convergently regulated genes by multiple hormones.

Results and discussion

Generation of a comprehensive transcriptome atlas to uncover early perception of nine phytohormones by snRNA-seq

To construct a comprehensive spatiotemporal transcriptome atlas for the early response of plant hormones in Arabidopsis, we collected the 7-day-old wild-type seedlings treated exogenously with nine phytohormones for durations of 0.5 and 3 h and corresponding mocks as control to prepare snRNA-seq libraries (Fig. 1A). These two time points were chosen by following the Goda/AtGenExpress/Nemhauser hormone-response benchmark27,43, in which 7-day-old Arabidopsis seedlings were profiled at 30, 60, and 180 min after hormone treatment. Thus, 0.5 h and 3 h bracket an established early hormone-response window, allowing us to compare the initial and later phases of early transcriptional responses. To facilitate the comparisons across different hormones, we adopt a standardized liquid treatment strategy for all hormones as Nemhauser et al.27, transferring seedlings to liquid 1/2 MS medium supplemented with either hormone triggers or mock solution. We applied these hormone triggers at concentrations previously established for liquid treatment as follow, 5 μM indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) for auxin (AUX)38, 5 μM BA for cytokinin (CK)44, 100 μM S-ABA for abscisic acid (ABA)45, 100 μM GA3 for gibberellin (GA)46, 5 μM GR24 (4DO) for strigolactone (SL)47, 1 μM brassinolide(BL) for brassinosteroid (BR)48, 100 μM -Aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid hydrochloride(ACC) for ethylene (ET)49, 100 μM Methyl Jasmonate (MeJA) for jasmonic acid (JA)50, and 500 μM salicylic acid (SA) for SA, with 1‰ DMSO serving as the mock (Supplementary Data 1, Fig. 1A). We conducted qRT-PCR using the marker genes previously reported for each hormone response and confirmed the expected responses during the initial treatment period (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Data 2). Then we generated 40 snRNA-seq libraries comprising two biological replicates for each of ten treatment groups (nine hormone treatments and one mock) at two timepoints (0.5 h post-treatment [hpt] and 3 hpt) by 10x Genomics Chromium system as we previously described51 (Fig. 1B). We collected hormone-responsive genes to confirm the responses induced by hormone treatments and observed significant responses as expected in both time points in our snRNA-seq data (Fig. 1C, The references supporting these marker genes are provided in Supplementary Data 3).

After the quality control, we recovered 498,376 nuclei in total, covering 30,366 genes, with a median of 563 gene counts and 671 UMI detected per nucleus (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Data 4). Following dimensionality reduction and data integration, we resolved 28 cell clusters (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Next, we identified specific expressed genes for each cluster (Supplementary Data 5) and compared them with published single-cell Arabidopsis root and shoot scRNA-seq datasets52,53 for annotation. Based on the Jaccard similarity metric between our data and a merged well-established single-cell root and shoot datasets, most clusters were assigned to known cell types based on the highest overlap in cluster-specific expressed genes (Supplementary Fig. 3D). However, cluster 9, cluster 14, cluster 19 and cluster 21 exhibited the characteristics of multiple cell types (Supplementary Fig. 3D). Therefore, we further subdivided these clusters and rule out overclustering possibility using the sc-SHC method54 (Supplementary Fig. 4), resulting in a total of 35 cell clusters, with 31 clusters that annotated as known cell types (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. 3E). The proportions of samples from various treatments remain relatively consistent among the remaining clusters, which suggests the well-integrated dataset (Supplementary Fig. 3B, C). The annotation results were further validated with the published cell type-specific marker genes (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplementary Data 6). We successfully distinguished most shoot and root-specific cell types and found that the vascular tissue is mixed, which is consistent with previous research indicating that vascular cells from both shoots and roots have a tendency to cluster together53. All these results showed that we have assigned cell-type identities for most clusters and got a single-nucleus atlas for hormone response.

Distinct early response trends are observed across hormone treatments

To investigate the early temporal and spatial responses of nine hormones at the single-cell level while mitigating potential confounding effects from liquid immersion treatments, we identified hormone-induced differentially expressed genes (DEGs) at each time point by comparing each hormone treatment to the corresponding mock-treated control subjected to the same treatment duration (Fig. 2A, B, Supplementary Data 7). Notably, most hormones triggered stronger transcriptional changes in aboveground tissues than in roots, except for one root epidermal cluster (Cluster 2) and one root cortex cluster (Cluster 8). Between the two snRNA-seq time points, AUX, CK, GA, SL, BR, and ET generally showed more DEGs at 0.5 hpt than at 3 hpt, whereas JA, SA, and ABA showed broader DEG responses at 3 hpt (Fig. 2C). Thus, the 0.5 h and 3 h snRNA-seq snapshots revealed two broad early response trends: an early-biased pattern for several growth-related hormones and a more sustained or later-increasing pattern for JA, SA, and ABA. To further characterize these later and broader JA, SA, and ABA responses, we performed gene ontology (GO) enrichment analyses focusing on hormone biosynthesis pathways (Fig. 2D). BR treatment caused particularly broad down-regulation of hormone-biosynthesis-associated genes. This pattern is consistent with the well-established negative feedback regulation55,56 of BR homeostasis: exogenous brassinolide or BR-excess conditions repress BR biosynthetic genes such as DWF4. Thus, the broad repression observed here likely reflects BR-associated transcriptional rebalancing of hormone-biosynthesis programs. Remarkably, upregulated genes following JA, SA, and ABA treatments remain significantly enriched in their respective biosynthetic processes at both 0.5 h and 3 h in almost all cell types (Fig. 2D), especially for JA and ABA, indicating sustained biosynthetic pathway engagement over this time window at whole seedling level. In contrast, for most other hormones, we did not detect significant enrichment of upregulated genes in their own biosynthetic pathways or only detected such enrichment at the earliest time point (0.5 hpt) (Fig. 2D). These results indicate sustained hormone biosynthesis pathway-associated transcriptional activity for JA, SA, and ABA over the sampled early time window.

Previous studies6,57,58 have shown that phytohormone outputs are strongly influenced by treatment conditions, including hormone concentration and response temporal dynamics. To test the treatment condition effect, we re-analyzed the low-dose seedling microarray dataset generated by Goda et al.43 (AtGenExpress dataset) (Supplementary Fig. 6). In this analysis, low-dose ABA (10 μM) showed a temporal pattern broadly consistent with our data, with substantially more DEGs detected at 3 hpt than at earlier time points. By contrast, low-dose JA (10 μM) showed a different trajectory, with the response increasing from 0.5 hpt to 1 hpt but returning toward the 0.5 hpt level by 3 hpt. These results also support that temporal profiles are condition-dependent, with hormone concentration likely being an important contributing factor.

To assess whether the two snRNA-seq time points reflected broader response kinetics, we performed a qRT-PCR time course for representative hormone-responsive marker genes in root and shoot tissues at 0.5, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hpt, using time-matched DMSO-treated samples as controls (Supplementary Data 2, Supplementary Data 8). These experiments showed that hormone responses are more complex than could be inferred from the first 3 h alone (Supplementary Fig. 7). For example, AUX/GH3.3, GA/GA20ox, BR/DWF4, and ET/WOX11 exhibited strong early induction responses that gradually diminished after 3 h, particularly in the roots. CK/ARR5 and SL/BRC1 showed very weak signals overall, but their expression levels peaked 12 h after treatment. Furthermore, even for JA, SA, and ABA, the temporal patterns were not uniform when additional time points were included. For instance, in JA/JAZ1, the signal in the root continued to rise over time, while in the shoot, it peaked at 3 h and began to decline—a pattern consistent with that of ABA/ABI5 in the root. For SA/PR1, qPCR results indicate that the signal began to rise at 6 h after treatment. Thus, while the snRNA-seq data reveal distinct early response patterns between 0.5 h and 3 h, the qRT-PCR time course indicates that these patterns become more complex at later stages, potentially reflecting hormone-specific differences in uptake, transport, and physiological response kinetics. Thus, dense and extended time-course approaches or direct live-biosensor analyses are needed to fully resolve hormone-response kinetics.

To evaluate how much such heterogeneity is obscured by sample-level averaging, we aggregated all cells per sample (pseudo‑bulk) and re‑called DEGs after discarding cluster identities. We found that over 30% of the cell-type DEGs were undetected at the sample (bulk) level (Supplementary Fig. 8, Supplementary Data 9). Notably, at 3 hpt, the bulk analysis captured fewer than 50% of cluster-level DEGs for ET, BR, SL and GA, indicating pronounced spatial divergence and strong sample masking for these hormones at later time points compared with the 0.5 hpt (Supplementary Fig. 8). To further assess the functional relevance of these masked responses, we performed GO enrichment analysis on the single-cell-only DEGs. We found that these genes uncovered cell-type-associated processes that were not detected in the pseudo-bulk DEG enrichment analysis. For instance, in mesophyll cells, chloroplast-related and photosynthesis-associated pathways were enriched specifically in the single-cell-only DEG set, but not in the corresponding pseudo-bulk DEG set (Supplementary Fig. 9). Thus, sample-level averaging obscures not only individual DEGs, but also biological programs, highlighting the importance of single-cell resolution for resolving hormone-responsive pathways in specific cell types. In addition, by intersecting hormone-responsive DEGs with publicly available target sets for key transcription factors in core hormone signaling pathways, we identified a subset of single-cell-only DEGs that are more likely to be under direct regulation by these hormone-pathway TFs, thereby prioritizing candidate direct targets for future mechanistic analysis (Supplementary Fig. 10, Supplementary Data 10). We next examined the degree of overlap in DEGs among different clusters for each hormone using a hypergeometric test denoted as S-MOD score59. From this analysis, two distinct temporal patterns emerged based on how extensively the induced DEGs were shared across clusters (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. 11, 12). Several growth-related hormones, represented by auxin, showed the greatest DEG overlap as early as 0.5 hpt, and this enrichment was concentrated in shoot-derived clusters (epidermis, mesophyll), whereas root clusters shared far fewer early DEGs in this study. In contrast, ABA, SA, and JA showed modest overlap at 0.5 hpt but broader DEG sharing across many shoot and root clusters by 3 hpt (Fig. 2E). Thus, within our two-time-point sampling window, different hormones displayed distinct early response trends: some showed an early-biased and more spatially restricted pattern, whereas ABA, SA, and JA showed a more sustained and broadly cell-type-shared transcriptional response by 3 hpt. One possible explanation is the widespread induction of endogenous hormone-biosynthetic genes in these pathways. Alternatively, these defense- or stress-related hormones may activate a broader transcriptional program with less pronounced cell-type specificity, leading to more global responses across tissues.

Transcriptomic overlap among hormone responses is cell-type and time-dependent

Having defined the dynamics of individual hormones, we next explored how transcriptional response overlaps among hormone treatments varied across cell types and time points. Here, we use the overlap between DEG sets induced by two different single-hormone treatments to represent the transcriptional response overlap. This includes both same-direction overlap, in which the same genes are upregulated or downregulated by two hormone treatments, and opposite-direction overlap, in which genes are upregulated by one hormone treatment but downregulated by another. To assess these response-overlap patterns, we examined all hormone pairwise comparisons of hormone-induced DEGs, distinguishing between upregulated and downregulated sets at 0.5 h vs. 3 h post-treatment, across the 36 single-nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq) clusters identified in our atlas. One cluster (Cluster 27) was excluded due to its unknown cell type identity and the near-complete absence of DEGs, resulting in 35 clusters for analysis (Figs. 1B, 2A, B).

For each of the nuclei clusters, we also used the S-MOD score to quantify the significance of overlap for every comparison (2 DEG direction × 9 hormones × 2 duration times), thereby deriving a 35 × 36 matrix of transcriptomic response-overlap signatures. Unsupervised clustering of these signatures resolved seven major response-overlap groups (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Data 11). We found that the first three groups, which exhibited the strongest overall hormone-hormone DEG overlap degree, were almost exclusively shoot‑derived clusters, with guard cells being the only consistent exception among shoot types (Fig. 3B). Other groups were dominated by root or less tissue‑defined clusters, whereas the seventh group, containing highly proliferative cells from both shoots and roots, showed the weakest hormone-response overlap (Fig. 3B). These patterns underscore that transcriptomic overlap among hormone responses is cell-type and time dependent.

Having described the cell‑type interaction landscape, we next asked which hormone pairs regulate the genes in the same direction (co‑directional overlap) and which pairs act in opposite directions (opposite‑direction overlap). For each pair we computed, across the 35 clusters, the S-MOD statistic for (i) jointly up‑ or down‑regulated genes and (ii) genes up‑regulated by one treatment but down‑regulated by the other, and summarized each category by the cluster‑median score (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Data 11). Two clear patterns emerged. First, co‑directional overlaps were strongest within the same time point, and especially prominent at 0.5 hpt. At 3 hpt the overall signal weakened, yet defense‑related pairs such as ABA–SA and ABA–JA rose to first and fifth place, respectively, by jointly repressing a large gene set (Fig. 3C, D). Second, the highest opposite‑direction scores typically spanned the two time points, reflecting a temporal rewiring of transcriptional networks (Fig. 3C, D). ET, SA, ABA and JA are classic defense hormones that frequently converge yet can counteract one another60–62. In our data, five of the top ten contrasts involved early ethylene (ET, 0.5 h) versus later ABA, SA or JA (3 h), hinting that an initial ET burst may dampen, delay or redirect subsequent defense programs (Fig. 3D). This shift is consistent with the well‑established view that defense hormones can display both synergy and antagonism depending on timing and context63.

JA-, SA-, and ABA-related pathways show strong but asymmetric cross-regulation

Under our treatment conditions, the overlap in genes influenced by JA, SA, and ABA was higher than that observed for other hormone pairs (Fig. 4A). For example, approximately 50% of ABA-responsive genes were also responsive to SA at 3 hpt (Fig. 4B). We therefore asked whether this high global overlap reflected symmetric activation of the two core hormone-metabolism pathways. We collected genes directly involved in hormone biosynthetic, and catabolic or inactivation pathways for JA, SA, and ABA based on previous reviews64–66 and calculated the average fold change of each set following exogenous hormone treatments relative to mock controls (Fig. 4C, D, Supplementary Data 12). This pathway-level analysis revealed that interactions among JA, SA, and ABA are directional and depend on the pathway layer. Among the three hormone branches, the JA-associated pathway displayed the strongest cross-responsiveness: both SA and ABA treatments increased JA biosynthetic, and catabolic signatures though with some distinctions (Fig. 4C, D). For example, the effect of exogenous SA treatment on JA pathways was relatively weaker than that of ABA, especially at 0.5 hpt (Fig. 4C, D). Although about half of SA-responsive genes also responded to ABA at 3 hpt (Fig. 4B), pathway-level analysis revealed that exogenous ABA treatment upregulated SA synthesis-related genes and detected only a weak response to ABA in SA-related genes within the synthesis pathway (Fig. 4C). However, upon including genes related to degradation/inactivation, we found that SA degradation/inactivation-related genes were also upregulated (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, genes related to ABA degradation/inactivation were sharply downregulated 3 h after exogenous SA treatment (Fig. 4D), which may partially contribute to the high proportion of DEG overlap between ABA and SA. These results also suggested that the pathway-level co-regulation between hormones is not symmetrical.

Taking advantage of the single-cell resolution of snRNA‑seq, we further examined pathway-level responses across vascular tissues and other non‑vascular cell types in roots and shoots (Fig. 4C, D). These analyses revealed spatiotemporal heterogeneity in hormone pathway cross-regulation. For example, 3 h after treatment with JA, genes associated with ABA degradation/inactivation were downregulated in the vascular system but upregulated in the shoot and root. For the biosynthetic pathway, the opposite occurred: genes associated with ABA synthesis were upregulated in the vascular system but downregulated in the shoot and root. These findings support that the patterns of hormonal cross-regulation are cell-type-dependent.

Defense hormone interactions are known to be highly dependent on treatment conditions, including exogenous hormone concentration57. We therefore further evaluated our observations in the context of published hormone-treatment transcriptome datasets using the same pathway-layer framework applied to our own data (Supplementary Fig. 13). Specifically, we compared our results with the low-dose Arabidopsis seedling dataset from Goda et al./AtGenExpress43 and the T87 cultured-cell dataset from Okamoto et al.67, both of which include separate exogenous treatments with ABA, JA, and SA. For each dataset, we also quantified the responses of JA-, SA-, and ABA-associated biosynthetic and catabolic/inactivation gene sets (Supplementary Fig. 13). This comparison showed that direct activation of the corresponding hormone-associated pathways was generally conserved across datasets, whereas cross-hormone responses were more dependent on treatment conditions. Unlike the relatively consistent patterns of ABA-to-SA interaction across different datasets, in the low-dose AtGenExpress seedling dataset, JA–SA cross-responses were weak at the pathway level, with little evidence for mutual induction between JA- and SA-associated genes (Supplementary Fig. 13B). By contrast, in the Okamoto T87 dataset, JA treatment induced SA biosynthetic and catabolic/inactivation signatures at later time points, consistent with the JA-to-SA trend observed in our dataset (Supplementary Fig. 13C). Conversely, the SA-to-JA response was much stronger in our dataset than in the Okamoto dataset (Supplementary Fig. 13C), which may at least partly reflect the higher SA concentration used in our study than in Okamoto et al. (500 μM versus 300 μM). These results agree with previous findings57 that JA–SA interactions are highly concentration dependent.

To more directly test combinatorial crosstalk among JA, SA, and ABA, defined here as non-additive transcriptional responses under simultaneous hormone treatments, we further performed bulk RNA-seq under single, pairwise, and triple hormone combinations at 0.5 hpt and 3 hpt (Supplementary Fig. 14A). We then fitted a full factorial model to quantify the deviation of multi-treatment responses from the additive expectation based on the corresponding single-hormone effects, and found that hundreds of genes with significant non-additive effects were detected at both time points (Supplementary Fig. 14B, Supplementary Data 13), demonstrating widespread non-additive transcriptional responses among these three hormones. The pairwise interactions were asymmetric in strength, with ABA × SA and SA × JA affecting larger gene sets than ABA×JA (Supplementary Fig. 14B). GO enrichment analysis further showed that for the pairwise interaction terms, hormone-related categories were significantly enriched, including not only the expected pathways corresponding to the two hormones in each pair, but also categories associated with the third hormone pathway (Supplementary Fig. 15A). For example, ABA×SA interaction genes were also enriched for JA-related terms. Moreover, genes unique to the three-way interaction term, ABA × SA × JA, showed a marked temporal shift: they were predominantly upregulated at 0.5 hpt and enriched for defense- and stress-related processes, but became predominantly downregulated by 3 hpt (Supplementary Fig. 14C, Supplementary Fig. 15B).

Based on snRNA-seq analysis, despite these clear patterns of co-directional DEG overlap at the whole-tissue level and especially in shoot, a closer inspection in guard cells reveals a more nuanced picture. Among the three hormone combinations ABA, SA, and JA, regardless of the treatment duration, the degree of overlap in hormone-induced DEGs in guard cells is always the lowest among the shoots. (Fig. 5A). Together, these results indicate that the apparent JA–SA–ABA crosstalk pattern is concentration sensitive, pathway specific, tissue dependent and asymmetric.

Single-cell analysis pinpoints a MYB60‑linked node in SA–ABA guard‑cell Interplay

Our single‑cell atlas shows that guard cells display the weakest co‑directional overlaps among ABA, SA and JA compared with other shoot clusters in our dataset (Figs. 3A, 5A), and hormone responsiveness in guard cells was largely confined to these three hormones, with only minor transcriptional changes detected for the others (Fig. 2A, B). This suggests that the JA-SA-ABA hormone crosstalk regulatory network in guard cells may differ significantly from that in other shoot cell types.

By building shoot transcription‑factor (TF) networks for each treatment based on integrating TF‑motif enrichment with co‑expression links68 (Supplementary Data 14), we found that a MYB60‑centered network stood out: it was specifically detected under SA treatment, but not under the other hormones nor in non‑guard‑cell clusters (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Data 14). MYB60 is a guard‑cell‑specific TF required for stomatal opening69. We next quantified MYB60 regulon activity using AUCell, and found that the activity was specifically elevated in SA-treated guard cells (Fig. 5C). Consistent with this pattern, MYB60 expression itself showed strong guard-cell specificity in our dataset (Fig. 5D) and MYB60 was only upregulated in the SA-treated guard-cell (Fig. 5E, Supplementary Data 14). The SA‑linked MYB60 module includes multiple genes previously implicated in stomatal movement, such as IQD2170, WRKY7071, MPK1272, BLUS173, TGG174, LHCB375, FOCL176 (Fig. 5B). Seven of these genes have been reported in ABA‑regulated stomatal movement, yet in our dataset, they are co‑induced by SA and strongly connected to MYB60, hinting that SA may engage a branch of the ABA program via MYB60.

SA is usually regarded as the hormone that induces stomatal closure, and MYB60 is thought to promote stomatal opening69. In contrast, our atlas shows that MYB60 is selectively up‑regulated by SA in guard cells. The SA‑induced MYB60 hub is connected to multiple genes previously characterized in ABA‑directed closure. This paradox points to a more nuanced role for MYB60: after the initial wave of SA-induced stomatal closure, the upregulation of MYB60 may serve as a delayed, negative feedback mechanism. By co-opting and modulating components of the ABA signaling pathway, MYB60 could prevent excessive or prolonged stomatal closure, thereby balancing the need for defense with the need for gas exchange. Such a braking mechanism may contribute to the SA pretreatment’s ability to partially reverse ABA‑mediated or mitigate drought‑induced stomatal closure77,78. Our network thus nominates MYB60 as a selective SA hub that can tap into ABA‑associated components to fine‑tune stomatal dynamics in a cell‑type‑specific manner. To test this prediction, we examined stomatal aperture dynamics in the myb60 T-DNA insertion mutant SALK_148646C under DMSO or SA treatment followed by recovery. This line was selected because it has been reported79 to show impaired stomatal opening, and heterologous overexpression of the AtMYB60 ortholog in the mutant background can rescue the stomatal phenotype, supporting its relevance for studying MYB60-mediated stomatal regulation. Two independent experimental repeats showed consistent results (Fig. 5F–H, Supplementary Fig. 16, Supplementary Data 15). In WT, SA treatment caused a transient reduction in stomatal aperture after 3 h, followed by significant reopening after 24 h of recovery. And the myb60 mutant also showed reduced stomatal aperture after 3 h of either DMSO or SA treatment (Fig. 5F, G). However, whereas DMSO-treated myb60 stomata significantly reopened by 24 h of recovery, SA-treated myb60 stomata did not show a significant difference in aperture between the 3 h treatment time point and the 24 h recovery time point. Substantial reopening in SA-treated myb60 was observed only at the later 48 h recovery time point (Fig. 5F, G). We next generated snRNA-seq data from myb60 mutant leaves under DMSO and SA treatment and identified guard cells in the mutant dataset (Fig. 5I, J, Supplementary Fig. 17). We then examined expression of seven ABA-related guard-cell regulatory genes within the SA-induced MYB60 regulon defined from the wild-type single-cell data. The SA-induced fold changes of all seven genes were attenuated in myb60 guard cells compared with Col-0 guard cells (Fig. 5K, L), suggesting that MYB60 contributes to the activation of an SA-responsive guard-cell regulatory program. Together, these results support the network-based prediction that MYB60 promotes timely stomatal reopening or recovery after SA-associated stomatal closure, potentially through regulation of ABA-related guard-cell genes.

In summary, our single-nucleus atlas of ~0.5 million Arabidopsis nuclei charts how nine phytohormones rapidly and differentially remodel transcription at cell-type resolution. It captures the dominant, tissue-wide reprogramming driven by ABA, JA and SA; an early synergistic to late antagonistic shift in hormone crosstalk; and a guard-cell-specific SA/MYB60 module that repurposes ABA components to fine-tune stomatal behavior. Together, these insights reveal phytohormone-sharing scaffolds and customized circuits.

Methods

Chemical information

Nine hormone treatments and their respective concentrations were selected based on representative published studies, as summarized in Supplementary Data 1. MeJA was first dissolved in ethanol to prepare a 5000× stock solution. ACC was dissolved in water to prepare a 1000× stock. The remaining hormone compounds were dissolved in DMSO at a 1000× concentration. All stock solutions were subsequently diluted with liquid 1/2 MS medium to achieve the final working concentrations. For MeJA and ACC treatments, 0.1% (v/v) DMSO was added to the final solution to ensure consistency with other treatments for parameter control.

Plant material and chemical treatment

Arabidopsis Columbia-0 (Col-0) seedlings used in this study were grown vertically on 1/2 MS plates at 22 °C under long-day conditions (16 h light/8 h dark). After 7 days, seedlings were harvested and separated into 40 portions, representing two biological replicates, two treatment time points and ten different treatment conditions. Nine portions were immersed in liquid 1/2 MS medium containing the specific concentration of hormone (Supplementary Data 1), while one portion was immersed in liquid 1/2 MS medium containing 0.1% (v/v) DMSO as a mock control. After treatment for 0.5 or 3 h, seedlings were briefly surface-dried with paper, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further processing. For the myb60 mutant snRNA-seq experiment, myb60 mutant (SALK_148646C) seedlings were grown under the same conditions described above. SALK_148646C was selected because this line has been reported79 to show impaired stomatal opening, and this phenotype can be rescued by heterologous overexpression of the AtMYB60 ortholog in the mutant background. After 7 days, myb60 seedlings were immersed in liquid 1/2 MS medium containing 500 μM SA or 0.1% (v/v) DMSO for 3 h. For this mutant snRNA-seq experiment, one portion was prepared for each treatment. For the JA/SA/ABA combinatorial treatment experiment, Col-0 seedlings were grown under the same conditions described above. Seven-day-old seedlings were treated with mock, single-hormone treatments, pairwise hormone combinations, or the triple hormone combination, including ABA, JA, SA, ABA + JA, ABA + SA, JA + SA, and ABA + JA + SA. For combined treatments, hormones were added simultaneously to liquid 1/2 MS medium at the same final concentrations used for the corresponding single-hormone treatments.

RT-qPCR validation

To confirm the effectiveness of hormone treatments, one well-established hormone-response marker gene was selected for each treatment group and validated using qRT-PCR. Total RNA was extracted from the frozen seedlings using the RNAprep Pure Plant Plus kit (#DP441, TIANGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For cDNA synthesis, total RNA was reverse-transcribed using Hifair® Ⅲ 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix with a mix of random primers/Oligo (dT)18 primers (#11141ES60, Yeasen). All qRT-PCR reactions were performed with a LightCycler 96 (Roche) system using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (#Q711-02, Vazyme). The primers used were listed in Supplementary Data 2. ACT2 or PP2A served as internal reference genes for normalization of gene expression levels.

Stomatal aperture measurements

One-week-old Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings were used for stomatal aperture assays. Seedlings were immersed in 1/2 MS liquid medium supplemented with either 500 µM SA or an equal volume of DMSO as the vehicle control for 3 h. Immediately after treatment, a subset of leaves was sampled and imaged under a microscope to record initial stomatal states. The remaining seedlings were transferred to fresh 1/2 MS liquid medium for recovery, and leaves were harvested for microscopic observation at 24 h and 48 h post-transfer. Stomatal dimensions were quantified using ImageJ software (version Insert Version, 1.52a). The spatial scale was calibrated using the “Set Scale” function under the “Analyze” menu based on the pixel length of the scale bar. Individual stomatal regions of interest (ROIs) were saved and managed using the ROI Manager tool. The aperture length and width of each stoma were measured using the straight-line selection tool. The stomatal aperture ratio was calculated as the width-to-length ratio.

snRNA-seq libraries construction

For each treatment group of Arabidopsis, we isolated nuclei and constructed 40 snRNA-seq libraries following our previously published protocol51. Sequencing was carried out on the Illumina NovaSeq 5000 platform using PE150 reads. Each library yielded approximately 40 Gb of raw sequencing data, providing sufficient depth for comprehensive transcriptomic profiling at single-nucleus resolution. For the single-cell dataset of the myb60 mutant, libraries were constructed using the BGI DNBelab C4 platform.

Data analysis

10X Raw base‑call files were demultiplexed and processed with Cell Ranger v6.0. Reads were aligned to the Arabidopsis thaliana TAIR10 reference genome, using the Araport11 gene annotation80. Nuclei were retained if they contained 400–3000 UMIs, 350–2000 detected genes, and <5% of UMIs mapped to mitochondrial and chloroplastic genes. Putative doublets were removed separately for each library with scDblFinder81. For the mutant dataset, preprocessing was performed using dnbc4tools, and the reads were aligned to the same reference genome. Expression counts were normalized per library with the SCTransform v2 method82. To integrate the 40 libraries, we applied Harmony83 on the top 3000 variable features to correct for batch and treatment effects, and exported the harmonized principal components to Scanpy84. A 30‑nearest‑neighbor graph was constructed and a two‑dimensional UMAP85 was computed with PAGA‑initialization86, preserving global topology. Clustering used the Leiden87 algorithm with a resolution of 1.3; whenever sub‑clusters within a predefined cell type were required, we re‑ran “scanpy.tl.leiden” with the “restrict_to” argument to confine the partitioning. Cell‑type‑specific marker genes were identified with Cellex88. Genes scoring a specificity (τ) > 0.95 were identified as cluster‑specific markers and served for biological annotation.

DEGs were identified following the pseudobulk strategy benchmarked by Squair et al.89. For every hormone × time point × cell type, raw counts from the two biological replicates were summed, yielding one pseudobulk profile per condition. Pseudobulk matrices were analyzed with edgeR90. Genes with an absolute log2-fold-change ≥1 and an FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Pairwise overlap between DEG sets was quantified with the S‑MOD statistic59, a hypergeometric-based, –log10-transformed metric.

TF regulatory networks were inferred with the MiniEx algorithm68. Genome-wide TF binding predictions for promoter regions were taken from the MiniEx reference dataset, and network filtering/trimming thresholds were matched to those of Ferrari et al.68

For bulk RNA-seq analysis, raw RNA-seq reads from the JA/SA/ABA single, pairwise, and triple-treatment samples were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT291. Gene expression levels were quantified using StringTie92 and Ballgown93. The resulting gene-level expression matrix was used for downstream differential expression and interaction analysis with edgeR90. For the combinatorial treatment experiment, 0.5 hpt and 3 hpt samples were analyzed separately using a full-factorial model: Y∼ABA + JA + SA + ABA:JA + ABA:SA + JA:SA + ABA:JA:SA. In this model, ABA, JA, and SA were encoded as binary variables indicating the presence or absence of each hormone. Pairwise and three-way interaction terms were used to identify non-additive transcriptional responses under combined hormone treatments. Genes were considered significant for each model term when the FDR-adjusted P<0.05 and the absolute value of the corresponding log2-scale coefficient was >2.

Functional enrichment of DEG lists used GO annotations retrieved from TAIR10 and was performed with gseapy94. Hierarchical clustering of overlap matrices employed SciPy95.
