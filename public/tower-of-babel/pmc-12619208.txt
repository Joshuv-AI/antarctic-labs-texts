Application of an individualized nomogram in the first-trimester for predicting cleft lip and palate
Authors: Xiaocen Yang, Shu Huang, Qihong Li, Lixuan Chen, Xiaoli Chen, Yihuang Hong, Biao Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFetal cleft lip and palate (CLP) is among the most common congenital craniofacial anomalies, posing significant physical and psychological burdens on affected families and contributing to increased societal and healthcare costs. We aimed to develop an individualized nomogram model incorporating fetal nuchal translucency thickness (NT), crown-rump length (CRL), and facial profile markers for predicting CLP in the first trimester of pregnancy.MethodsSingleton pregnancies undergoing first-trimester screening (11+0 to 13+6 weeks) were enrolled. Fetal NT, CRL, and ultrasonographic facial markers, including the frontomaxillary facial angle (FMFA) and inferior facial angle (IFA), were measured. Feature selection was performed using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator algorithm, followed by multivariable analysis to establish a nomogram model. The model’s diagnostic performance was assessed through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and precision-recall (PR) curves, calculating the area under the ROC curve (AUC) and area under the PR curve (AUPRC). Calibration curve analysis and decision curve analysis (DCA) were used to evaluate model calibration and clinical utility. Performance was compared with individual ultrasonographic facial markers.ResultsA total of 764 fetuses were included, comprising 732 normal and 32 CLP cases. The nomogram demonstrated excellent predictive performance with an AUC of 0.847 (95% CI: 0.751-0.944) and AUPRC of 0.689. The model achieved an accuracy of 0.977, F1-score of 0.640, sensitivity of 0.482, specificity of 0.999, positive predictive value of 0.955, and negative predictive value of 0.978. Compared to FMFA (AUC = 0.757), IFA (AUC = 0.708), NT (AUC = 0.714), and CRL (AUC = 0.592), the nomogram demonstrated significantly improved diagnostic performance (both P < 0.05). DCA confirmed the net clinical benefit of the model, and the calibration curve demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed outcomes.ConclusionThe nomogram model integrating NT ≥ 2.5 mm, FMFA, CRL, and IFA demonstrates superior diagnostic performance for the first-trimester predicting of CLP compared with individual markers. Given its reliance on routinely measured parameters and ease of use, this model offers a practical, non-invasive, and efficient tool that may facilitate early detection of CLP in the first trimester of pregnancy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08274-9.Keywords: Cleft lip and palate, Nomogram, Facial profile, Ultrasonography, First trimester

Introduction

Facial malformations, such as cleft lip and palate (CLP) and micrognathia, are among the most common fetal surface anomalies. They pose substantial physical and psychological burdens on affected families and contribute to increased societal and healthcare costs [1, 2]. Early detection of these malformations allows for timely counseling, genetic testing, and intervention, which can significantly improve pregnancy outcomes and provide parents with critical information for decision-making [2, 3]. While many fetal structural malformations are detectable via ultrasound in the second trimester, identifying facial anomalies in early pregnancy remains a challenge. Non-invasive and reliable markers for early detection of facial developmental issues are thus of great clinical value.

Ultrasound remains the most widely used imaging modality for screening fetal facial malformations [4, 5]. Advances in ultrasound technology have made it possible to assess some craniofacial structures during the first trimester of pregnancy [6]. The frontomaxillary facial angle (FMFA) and inferior facial angle (IFA) have recently emerged as promising markers for detecting facial abnormalities early in pregnancy [6, 7]. These angular measurements are easy to obtain via routine ultrasound and could provide a cost-effective, non-invasive alternative for early screening. Importantly, a recent study by Liao et al. [6] demonstrated that these ultrasound markers have satisfactory intra- and inter-observer agreements, as well as reproducibility. Although both measurements have been individually studied in relation to fetal congenital malformations [6–8], their combined diagnostic performance, particularly for CLP, has not been thoroughly evaluated. Moreover, most existing studies have focused on the second and third trimesters, with limited evidence available for the first trimester.

In recent years, nomograms have attracted increasing attention in the fields of disease diagnosis and prognostic evaluation due to their ability to visually represent complex predictive models and provide individualized risk assessments [9, 10]. By integrating multiple measurable parameters, nomograms enable clinicians to estimate the likelihood of specific fetal conditions in a quantitative and intuitive manner. In particular, ultrasonographic fetal facial markers, such as the FMFA and IFA, are critical indicators of early fetal facial development. Sun et al. [10] recently developed a nomogram incorporating fetal nuchal translucency thickness (NT) along with several ultrasonographic facial profile markers, demonstrating good performance in screening for trisomy 21. However, to date, no nomogram models have been specifically designed for the detection of CLP during early pregnancy.

To address this gap, the present study aimed to develop an individualized nomogram that combines fetal NT with ultrasonographic facial markers (FMFA and IFA) to detect CLP during early pregnancy. By comparing these prenatal measurements with postnatal diagnostic outcomes, we sought to determine whether the proposed nomogram could serve as a more reliable, non-invasive, and accessible tool for early predicting in routine clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and participant selection

This retrospective study enrolled pregnant women at Chenggong Hospital, Xiamen University, China, between Between January 2018 and October 2019. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) both parents of the fetus were Chinese; (2) women had a singleton pregnancy with a gestational age between 11 weeks and 13 + 6 weeks; (3) the pregnancy outcome could be followed up through the Maternal and Child Health Care system; and (4) women were willing to undergo prenatal ultrasound evaluations, with clear images obtained. The exclusion criteria were: (1) cases lost to follow-up; (2) fetal facial malformations other than CLP; and (3) blurred images that hindered clear visualization of fetal facial structures. A detailed flow chart of the study is provided in Fig. 1. Fetuses with either normal measurements or a diagnosis of CLP, a major type of fetal facial malformation, were selectively included.

Flowchart of the study

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chenggong Hospital, Xiamen University (approval number: 73JYY2025179759). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Ultrasound equipment and examinations

In this study, a SIEMENS Acuson S2000 and a GE Voluson E8 color ultrasound system were utilized. Both systems were equipped with a 2-dimensional convex probe, operating at a frequency of 2–6 MHz. All ultrasound examinations were performed following the guidelines set by the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology (ISUOG) [11]. Briefly, eligible pregnant women were placed in a supine position on the examination table. Fetal facial profile measurements, including the FMFA and IFA, were obtained via transabdominal ultrasound. The transabdominal approach was primarily used for the evaluation of congenital anomalies, with a transvaginal approach employed as a supplementary method when further clarification was required. Pregnancy outcomes were followed up through the Maternal and Child Health Care system. All measurements for each case were independently performed by two licensed sonographers, each with over five years of prenatal ultrasound experience. When discrepancies occurred, a third senior sonographer was consulted, and the final measurement was determined by consensus among all three experts.

Definition of the markers

The FMFA is defined as the angle between the extension line along the superior edge of the palate and the tangent line of the frontal bone on the mid-sagittal plane, with the front end of the palate serving as the apex [12]. The IFA is defined as the angle between the line orthogonal to the vertical part of the forehead at the level of the synostosis of the nasal bone and the line joining the tip of the mentum to the most anterior point of the more protruding lip [13]. Detailed protocols for ultrasound measurement of these two fetal facial profile markers have been well described in previous studies [6, 7] and are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Additionally, the crown-rump length (CRL) and NT were measured. The CRL is the length from the top of the head to the bottom of the buttocks, while the NT thickness refers to the maximal perpendicular measurement (in millimeters) of the sonolucent subcutaneous fluid-filled space between the fetal skin and the soft tissue overlying the cervical spine on a mid-sagittal plane. We recorded NT values ≥ 2.5 mm, as increased NT thickness may be associated with a high risk of fetal structural abnormalities, including craniofacial anomalies.

Nomogram model development

A total of six candidate predictors were evaluated for model development: maternal age, fetal CRL, fetal NT, NT ≥ 2.5 mm, and two facial ultrasonographic markers (FMFA and IFA). Feature selection was performed using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) algorithm with 10-fold cross-validation. The LASSO method improves model interpretability and mitigates overfitting by shrinking the coefficients of less informative variables to zero, thereby retaining only the most relevant predictors for model development, even in studies with modest sample sizes [10]. Following this, multivariable analysis was applied to the variables selected by LASSO, and those with P < 0.05 were retained for model development. A nomogram was then constructed based on these selected predictors using a logistic regression model.

The diagnostic performance of the nomogram was evaluated by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, and F1-score. The F1-score is a commonly used evaluation metric for assessing the performance of binary classification models. It combines the model’s precision and recall, providing a comprehensive measure of overall performance. In addition, the area under the precision recall curve (AUPRC) was calculated. While the AUC assesses overall discriminative ability, the AUPRC emphasizes performance on the positive class, making it particularly informative for imbalanced datasets. Model calibration was assessed using calibration plots and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, with a P value > 0.05 indicating adequate agreement between predicted and observed outcomes. In addition, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of the nomogram across a range of threshold probabilities.

The diagnostic performance of the nomogram was further compared with that of individual ultrasonographic markers (FMFA, IFA, CRL, and NT) using the DeLong test for comparison of AUCs [14]. The optimal thresholds (cut-off values) for predicting CLP using the nomogram model and individual ultrasonographic markers (FMFA, IFA, CRL, and NT) were determined based on Youden’s index.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on their distribution. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using the independent t-test or one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables that followed a normal distribution, and the Mann-Whitney U test for those that were not normally distributed. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS (version 26.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and R (version 4.2.1, R Foundation). A two-sided P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant enrollments

Pregnant women who underwent first-trimester ultrasound screening were enrolled in this study. A total of 764 fetuses meeting the inclusion criteria were included in the final analysis, comprising 732 in the normal group and 32 in the CLP group (Fig. 1). Pregnancy outcomes were followed up through the Maternal and Child Health Care System. Satisfactory ultrasound images were obtained for all enrolled cases, with a nearly 100% success rate in measuring both facial markers.

The median maternal age was comparable between the two groups (31.00 vs. 32.00, P = 0.217), as was the median CRL (60.20 vs. 62.50, P = 0.077). However, significant differences were observed in fetal measurements: the CLP group showed higher median NT values (2.20 vs. 1.65, P < 0.001) and a greater proportion of cases with NT ≥ 2.5 mm (40.6% vs. 4.8%). Both FMFA (99.00 vs. 83.00) and IFA (88.00 vs. 78.00) were significantly elevated in the CLP group (both P < 0.001), indicating marked ultrasonographic facial markers differences between the two groups. Correlations between CRL and ultrasonographic facial markers

As shown in Fig. 2, Pearson correlation analysis revealed a strong negative correlation between CRL and FMFA (r = −0.826, P < 0.001), and a weak but significant positive correlation between CRL and IFA (r = 0.223, P < 0.001).

Performance of individual ultrasonographic markers (FMFA, IFA, CRL, and NT)

The diagnostic performance of individual ultrasonographic markers for predicting CLP is summarized in Table 2. Corresponding AUCs are shown in Fig. 3, and the AUPRCs are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2. Among the four evaluated markers, FMFA demonstrated the highest overall discriminative performance, with an AUC of 0.757 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.644–0.870) and a Youden index of 0.522 at a cut-off value of 99°. Although FMFA had a relatively modest sensitivity (0.531), it showed extremely high specificity (0.990) and a high NPV (0.980), yielding an overall accuracy of 0.971. The IFA showed a sensitivity of 0.969, suggesting strong ability to detect CLP cases; however, its specificity was limited (0.492), resulting in a lower accuracy (0.512) and PPV (0.077), despite an AUC of 0.708 (95% CI: 0.652–0.764). For CRL, the diagnostic performance was limited, with an AUC of 0.592 (95% CI: 0.496–0.688), sensitivity and specificity of 0.469 and 0.712, respectively, and an overall accuracy of 0.702. The NT exhibited moderate diagnostic accuracy with an AUC of 0.714 (95% CI: 0.606–0.823). At a cut-off of 2.4 mm, it achieved 0.500 sensitivity, 0.895 specificity, 0.172 PPV, and 0.976 NPV, yielding an accuracy of 0.878.

Feature selection in nomogram model

Feature selection was performed using the LASSO algorithm (Fig. 4), which identified five key predictors: fetal CRL, fetal NT, NT ≥ 2.5 mm, FMFA, and IFA. Subsequently, multivariable analysis was conducted on these variables. Fetal NT was excluded due to its lack of statistical significance (P = 0.495). As a result, four variables were incorporated into the final nomogram model.

Performance of the nomogram model

The nomogram model demonstrated superior diagnostic performance for predicting CLP, with an AUC of 0.847 (95% CI: 0.751–0.944) and an AUPRC of 0.689. At the optimal cut-off value of 0.06, the model achieved an overall accuracy of 0.977, F1-score of 0.640, sensitivity of 0.482, specificity of 0.999, PPV of 0.955, and NPV of 0.978. Compared with individual ultrasonographic markers, including FMFA, IFA, CRL, and NT, the nomogram exhibited significantly better discriminative power and predictive performance (both P < 0.05).

Evaluation of nomogram model performance using DCA and calibration curve

DCA demonstrated that the nomogram model provided a superior net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities, indicating its potential utility in early prenatal decision-making (Fig. 6A). The calibration curve revealed a strong agreement between the predicted probabilities and actual observations, reflecting good model calibration. Furthermore, the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test yielded P values greater than 0.05, suggesting no significant deviation between predicted and observed outcomes, and supporting the reliability of the model’s predictions (Fig. 6B). Overall, these results suggest that the integration of the nomogram model enhances both risk stratification and diagnostic discrimination for CLP during early pregnancy.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a nomogram model incorporating key markers, including NT ≥ 2.5 mm, FMFA, fetal CRL, and IFA, for early predicting of CLP in the first trimester of pregnancy. The model demonstrated superior diagnostic performance compared with individual markers, with an AUC of 0.847 (95% CI: 0.751–0.944), high specificity (0.999), and excellent overall accuracy (0.977). These findings suggest that the nomogram model has substantial potential to improve early prenatal identification of CLP.

Notably, while FMFA and NT showed relatively high AUC values (0.757 and 0.714, respectively), their sensitivity and specificity varied, with FMFA demonstrating high specificity (0.990) but limited sensitivity (0.531). In contrast, IFA exhibited high sensitivity (0.969) but low specificity (0.492), leading to limited clinical utility when used alone. The nomogram model, by integrating these complementary markers, effectively balanced sensitivity and specificity, and yielded higher precision-recall performance (AUPRC = 0.689), which is particularly valuable in datasets with class imbalance. Furthermore, DCA confirmed the clinical net benefit of the nomogram across a wide range of decision thresholds, emphasizing its real-world applicability. The calibration curve and non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow test also support the model’s robustness and accurate prediction capabilities.

Our findings are in line with previous studies suggesting that combination models or machine learning-assisted frameworks outperform single-marker approaches in fetal anomaly screening [10]. Sun et al. [10] recently developed a nomogram model incorporating fetal NT and a series of ultrasonographic facial profile markers, achieving AUCs exceeding 0.95 across different datasets. Their findings demonstrated the strong predictive performance of such models for detecting trisomy 21 in the first trimester, outperforming traditional models based solely on fetal NT and maternal age. The inclusion of facial profile parameters, such as FMFA and IFA, highlights the growing relevance of fetal craniofacial biometrics in first-trimester anomaly detection. This integrated model could potentially serve as a decision-support tool in routine prenatal ultrasound evaluations.

Fetal CLP is one of the most common congenital craniofacial malformations, with significant implications for feeding, speech, hearing, facial development, and long-term psychosocial well-being [15]. Globally, the incidence of CLP is estimated at approximately 1 in 500 live births [16]. Early detection of CLP during the first trimester provides critical opportunities for timely parental counseling, referral to multidisciplinary teams, and consideration of additional diagnostic testing, especially when syndromic anomalies are suspected. Conventional mid-trimester anomaly scans often identify CLP; however, first-trimester screening offers a valuable window for earlier risk stratification and clinical decision-making. Enhancing detection at this stage may allow families to achieve earlier psychological preparedness and enable optimization of prenatal care planning [17].

Traditionally, CLP diagnosis relies on multisection two-dimensional and three-dimensional ultrasound in the second trimester. However, this approach has limitations: facial structures begin developing in the first trimester, multiplanar scanning is time-consuming, and the complexity of fetal facial anatomy demands experienced operators, which may affect screening efficiency and accuracy [18]. Although recent studies have introduced various ultrasound scanning techniques and dedicated software to improve the detection rate of CLP [18–21], their widespread implementation may be limited due to high costs, the steep learning curve, and the need for specialized expertise in interpreting complex fetal facial anatomy.

In contrast, the nomogram model developed in this study offers a practical and accessible predicting tool for CLP in the first trimester. It is based on standard ultrasonographic parameters, namely NT ≥ 2.5 mm, FMFA, fetal CRL, and IFA, which are routinely assessed during early pregnancy without the need for advanced imaging equipment or proprietary software. This ensures that the model can be easily integrated into routine obstetric ultrasound examinations without additional costs or significant operator burden. Furthermore, the graphical representation of the nomogram allows for intuitive risk estimation, facilitating informed clinical decision-making even in primary care or low-resource settings.

However, several limitations should be noted. First, the relatively small sample size and single-center design may affect generalizability. External validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is warranted to confirm the model’s reliability. Second, fetuses with poor-quality or ineligible ultrasound images were excluded from the analysis. This exclusion may have introduced selection bias and potentially limited the generalizability of our findings. Third, our study focused exclusively on the detection of CLP. Other facial anomalies, such as those detectable by fetal eye ultrasound examination, may also play an important role in first-trimester predicting and should be considered in future research [22].

Conclusions

In conclusion, the proposed nomogram model demonstrates promising diagnostic value for early predicting of CLP and may facilitate risk stratification and timely referral in prenatal care pathways. Future research should focus on model generalization, automation of marker measurement, and integration into clinical workflows.
