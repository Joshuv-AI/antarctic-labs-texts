Intelligent hybrid optimization of sustainable machining parameters for Inconel 718 using ANN driven evolutionary and swarm algorithms
Authors: Jasgurpreet Singh Chohan, Rajat Yadav, Kumel K Nagori, T Ramachandran, Swetarani Biswal, Ripendeep Singh, Pardeep Singh Bains, Rakesh Kumar, Abhijit Bhowmik, Yalew Tamene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study investigates the sustainable machining performance of Inconel 718 under different lubrication environments, including dry, minimum quantity lubrication (MQL), Nano-MQL, and cryogenic CO₂ conditions. Experiments were designed using a Taguchi L16 orthogonal array to evaluate the influence of cutting speed, feed rate, and lubrication strategy on cutting force, tool wear, surface roughness, and temperature. An artificial neural network (ANN) model was developed to predict machining responses, and hybrid optimization frameworks combining ANN with Genetic Algorithm (GA) and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) were implemented for multi-objective optimization. The ANN model demonstrated high prediction accuracy (R² > 0.97). Among the optimization approaches, the ANN–GA model achieved superior performance with a success rate of 86.7%, while ANN–PSO exhibited faster convergence. Cryogenic CO₂ machining significantly improved performance, reducing key responses by up to 43% compared to dry machining. The proposed hybrid framework provides an efficient and sustainable approach for optimizing machining parameters of Inconel 718, contributing to improved machining performance and environmentally responsible manufacturing.Keywords: Inconel 718, Sustainable machining, Artificial Neural Network, Genetic Algorithm, Particle Swarm Optimization, Hybrid optimizationSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Nickel-based superalloys are critically important in high-performance engineering sectors because they exhibit outstanding strength, excellent resistance to high-temperature deterioration, and superior corrosion resistance in harsh operating conditions. Within this class of materials, Inconel 718 stands out as one of the most extensively utilized alloys in aerospace, nuclear, and energy industries, primarily due to its remarkable creep resistance and ability to retain structural integrity at elevated temperatures. Machining Inconel 718 remains highly challenging, even with its favorable properties, due to several intrinsic material characteristics. These include its low ability to dissipate heat, significant tendency to undergo strain hardening during deformation, and a high affinity for reacting with tool materials, all of which adversely affect the machining process. Together, these attributes contribute to rapid tool deterioration, elevated machining forces, and a decline in the quality of the machined surface1–4. In machining Inconel 718, the heat generated at the tool–workpiece interface tends to accumulate rather than dissipate effectively, owing to the material’s poor thermal conductivity. This results in significant localized temperature buildup, which in turn accelerates tool wear and degradation. Moreover, the occurrence of built-up edge (BUE) along with strong adhesive interactions at the interface further compromises machining efficiency and surface integrity. Such challenges highlight the need for employing advanced lubrication and cooling approaches that can improve machinability without compromising environmental sustainability5–8. Conventional flood cooling methods can effectively reduce cutting temperatures; however, their extensive use of cutting fluids introduces significant environmental burdens and potential health risks for operators, particularly due to challenges in fluid management and safe disposal.

To mitigate the shortcomings of conventional machining environments, eco-friendly lubrication approaches such as minimum quantity lubrication (MQL), nano-enhanced MQL (Nano-MQL), and cryogenic cooling have attracted significant research interest. MQL involves the application of a very small quantity of biodegradable lubricant in combination with compressed air, which helps in lowering environmental impact while enhancing lubrication efficiency at the cutting interface9–11. The incorporation of nanoparticles to the base fluid further improves thermal transport properties and tribological behavior through mechanisms such as rolling, mending, and polishing actions12–14. In contrast, cryogenic cooling methods, particularly those utilizing CO₂ or liquid nitrogen, are highly effective in reducing cutting zone temperature and minimizing tool wear by enabling rapid heat removal without producing harmful residues15–17. However, despite these developments, determining the optimal combination of machining parameters and lubrication strategies remains challenging due to the complex and nonlinear interactions among process variables, making it problematic to simultaneously attain superior machining performance and sustainability.

Optimizing machining conditions plays a crucial role in improving overall process performance by minimizing cutting forces, reducing tool wear, enhancing surface integrity, and controlling heat generation during machining operations. Conventional optimization approaches such as response surface methodology (RSM) and Taguchi techniques often struggle to effectively address problems involving multiple objectives and strong nonlinear interactions18. As a result, advanced metaheuristic methods like Genetic Algorithm (GA), Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II), Teaching–Learning-Based Optimization (TLBO), and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) have gained significant attention in machining optimization studies19–22. While these techniques are capable of delivering efficient solutions for complex multi-objective scenarios, their effectiveness is frequently limited by dependence on empirical or regression-based models, which may not adequately represent intricate nonlinear behaviors inherent in machining processes.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI)-driven modeling strategies, particularly Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), have increased significant prominence for predicting machining responses, because of their strong competence to apprehend complex nonlinear relationships between input parameters and output responses based on experimental datasets23–25. These models have been extensively employed in machining applications to accurately estimate critical performance measures for example surface roughness, cutting forces and tool wear. However, ANN models, when used independently, are limited in their ability to perform optimization tasks, while metaheuristic algorithms alone lack the predictive intelligence required for reliable response estimation. To address these limitations, hybrid methodologies that integrate ANN with optimization techniques have been developed as an effective solution. In such approaches, ANN acts as a surrogate predictive model, whereas optimization algorithms such as GA and PSO are applied to determine the optimal set of machining parameters26–28. The ANN–GA hybrid model is known for its strong global search capability, while the ANN–PSO framework offers faster convergence due to its memory-based search mechanism. Consequently, these hybrid models significantly enhance both optimization efficiency and predictive accuracy by reducing computational effort and improving the fidelity of the results.

Although hybrid artificial intelligence-driven optimization methods have recently gained attention, their implementation in the sustainable machining of Inconel 718 under advanced lubrication conditions remains relatively underexplored. A significant portion of the existing literature tends to address optimization methodologies and lubrication techniques as separate domains, lacking a unified framework that combines intelligent modeling with sustainable machining practices. In addition, there is a noticeable shortage of comparative investigations focusing on hybrid ANN-integrated models alongside advanced evolutionary algorithms. To address these gaps, the present work proposes a robust hybrid optimization framework that combines ANN with GA and PSO techniques for sustainable machining of Inconel 718 under diverse lubrication environments, including dry cutting, MQL, Nano-MQL, and cryogenic CO₂ conditions. The experimental plan is structured using the Taguchi design methodology, while the impact of machining parameters on critical performance measures is evaluated through main effect plot analysis. The developed ANN model is further utilized as a predictive engine within the optimization scheme, and the effectiveness of ANN–GA and ANN–PSO approaches is benchmarked against conventional GA and PSO methods. The key contribution of this study lies in synergistically integrating artificial intelligence with advanced optimization strategies to establish a sustainable and high-performance machining approach for difficult-to-machine superalloys. The findings are anticipated to support the advancement of intelligent manufacturing systems by enhancing machining efficiency, minimizing environmental burden, and improving decision-making processes in contemporary machining operations.

Materials and methods

Despite these advantages, the material is difficult to machine because of its poor thermal conductivity, severe strain hardening tendency, and strong chemical affinity with cutting tools. These characteristics lead to high cutting forces, rapid tool wear, and deterioration of surface quality, making it an appropriate material for evaluating sustainable machining strategies. The selection of machining parameters, specifically cutting speed and feed rate, was performed using a systematic approach to ensure both industrial relevance and experimental reliability. The parameter ranges were initially identified based on recommendations provided by the cutting tool manufacturer, ensuring safe operating conditions for the TiAlN-coated carbide insert. These ranges were further refined using insights from existing literature on the machining of Inconel 718, which helped in identifying practically relevant and commonly adopted parameter windows. In addition, preliminary trial experiments were conducted under the current experimental setup to validate the selected parameter levels. These pilot trials ensured stable cutting conditions, minimized the risk of excessive tool wear or tool failure, and eliminated the possibility of process instability such as chatter or thermal damage.

For the machining trials, a TiAlN-coated carbide insert of type APMT 1135-PDTR, deposited using the Physical Vapor Deposition (PVD) technique, was employed. The tool was selected due to its superior hardness, oxidation resistance, and ability to withstand elevated temperatures encountered during machining of superalloys. The insert was mounted on a compatible tool holder and securely clamped to reduce vibration and ensure consistent machining performance. The use of a TiAlN coating enhances tool life by reducing adhesion and wear, thereby making it suitable for machining difficult-to-cut materials such as Inconel 718 under various lubrication environments.

Experimental setup and design

The machining trials were carried out on a three-axis CNC vertical milling machine under controlled laboratory conditions to ensure consistency and repeatability of the experimental results. The experimental setup was equipped with a Kistler piezoelectric dynamometer for accurate measurement of cutting forces, a thermal infrared imaging system for monitoring cutting temperature, a portable surface roughness tester for evaluating surface quality, and an optical microscope for examining tool wear characteristics. To systematically investigate the influence of process parameters with a reduced number of experimental runs, a Taguchi L16 orthogonal array was adopted. This design enables efficient evaluation of multiple factors and their interactions while maintaining experimental economy. The primary input parameters considered in this study were cutting speed (Vc), feed rate (f), and lubrication/cooling medium (LM), whereas the output responses included cutting force (Fr), tool wear (VB), surface roughness (Ra), and cutting temperature (T). The Taguchi L16 orthogonal array was specifically selected because it efficiently accommodates three input parameters at four levels each, while significantly reducing the number of experimental trials compared to a full factorial design. In the present case, a full factorial approach would require 4³ = 64 experiments, whereas the L16 design reduces this to only 16 runs, thereby minimizing experimental time, cost, and resource utilization. Additionally, the Taguchi design ensures a balanced and statistically robust experimental framework, enabling reliable estimation of the main effects of input parameters with adequate accuracy.

The selection of machining parameters and their respective levels was carried out based on a combination of tool manufacturer recommendations, extensive literature review, and preliminary trial experiments. The manufacturer guidelines ensured that the chosen cutting conditions remained within the safe operational limits of the TiAlN-coated carbide insert, thereby preventing premature tool failure. Insights from previous studies on machining of Inconel 718 were used to identify practically relevant parameter ranges, while pilot experiments were conducted to fine-tune these levels under the current experimental setup. This combined approach ensured stable machining conditions, reliable data acquisition, and avoidance of excessive tool wear or process instability. Each experimental run was conducted three times under identical conditions to improve the reliability and accuracy of the results, and the average values were used for further analysis. Lubrication and cooling strategies

To evaluate machining performance from a sustainability perspective, four lubrication and cooling environments were considered: dry machining, MQL, nano-enhanced MQL (Nano-MQL), and cryogenic cooling using CO₂. Special emphasis was placed on the scientific selection of the base oil used in MQL and Nano-MQL systems. Sunflower oil was selected as the base lubricant due to its excellent biodegradability, non-toxicity, and superior lubricity compared to conventional mineral oils17. In the MQL setup, sunflower oil was atomized with compressed air and delivered directly to the cutting zone through a nozzle at a flow rate of 3.5 ml/min and an air pressure of 5 bar, ensuring effective penetration into the tool–workpiece interface with minimal fluid consumption.

The selection of sunflower oil was further justified based on its favorable physicochemical properties, which make it highly suitable for MQL applications. It possesses a relatively high viscosity (≈ 35–45 mm²/s at 40 °C), enabling the formation of a stable lubricating film, and a high flash point (> 300 °C), ensuring safe operation under elevated cutting temperatures without premature evaporation. Additionally, its good thermal stability supports its performance under severe machining conditions11. A key advantage of sunflower oil lies in its chemical composition, particularly its high content of unsaturated fatty acids (such as oleic and linoleic acids), which exhibit strong polarity and adsorption capability on metallic surfaces, forming a protective boundary film that significantly reduces friction and tool wear3.

For Nano-MQL, alumina nanoparticles of approximately 20–30 nm size were uniformly dispersed within the sunflower oil to enhance both thermal conductivity and tribological behavior. The step-wise nanofluid preparation process, including magnetic stirring and ultrasonication, is illustrated in Fig. 2. The inclusion of nanoparticles improves lubrication through rolling, mending, and polishing mechanisms, while also enhancing heat dissipation from the cutting zone14. In contrast, dry machining was performed without any lubrication and used as a baseline condition. Cryogenic cooling was implemented using high-pressure CO₂, which undergoes rapid expansion at the nozzle due to the Joule–Thomson effect, forming CO₂ snow that absorbs significant heat during sublimation. This approach provides efficient cooling without generating harmful residues, reinforcing its suitability as an environmentally sustainable machining strategy6.

Measurement of machining responses

Machining performance was evaluated through precise measurement of four key response variables: cutting force, tool wear, surface roughness, and cutting temperature. A dedicated and well-calibrated measurement setup was employed to ensure accuracy, repeatability, and reliability of the recorded data.

Cutting forces were measured using a Kistler piezoelectric dynamometer mounted rigidly on the machine table beneath the workpiece. The dynamometer was connected to a charge amplifier and data acquisition system, enabling real-time recording of force components along three orthogonal directions. Prior to experimentation, the system was calibrated to minimize signal drift and noise. The resultant cutting force was computed from the measured components and used for further analysis.

Tool wear was assessed in terms of flank wear (VB) using a high-resolution optical microscope. After each experimental run, the cutting insert was carefully removed, cleaned, and examined under the microscope. Multiple measurements were taken along the wear land, and the average value was reported to improve measurement consistency. This approach ensured reliable quantification of tool degradation under varying machining conditions.

Surface roughness was measured using a portable surface roughness tester (Perthometer), which was calibrated prior to measurement following standard procedures. Measurements were taken over a sampling length of 5.6 mm at multiple locations on the machined surface to account for surface variability. The arithmetic mean roughness (Ra) was considered as the representative parameter for surface quality evaluation.

Cutting temperature was recorded using a non-contact thermal infrared (IR) camera strategically positioned to capture the tool–workpiece interaction zone. The camera was configured with appropriate emissivity settings corresponding to the workpiece material to ensure accurate temperature acquisition. It operated within a temperature range of − 20 °C to 600 °C with an accuracy of ± 2 °C. Thermal images were continuously recorded during machining, and peak temperature values were extracted using dedicated analysis software.

To ensure the reliability and repeatability of the measurements, each experiment was conducted three times under identical conditions, and the average values were considered for analysis. Additionally, the entire measurement setup was arranged to minimize external disturbances such as vibration and environmental fluctuations. The use of precise instrumentation and systematic measurement procedures ensured high-quality data acquisition, forming a robust basis for subsequent ANN modeling and optimization studies.

Development of ANN model

The machining of Inconel 718 is governed by highly complex and nonlinear relationships between process parameters and lubrication environments, which are often beyond the predictive capability of traditional regression-based approaches. To overcome this challenge, an ANN was implemented as a predictive tool to model the relationship among input machining variables and the resulting output responses. ANN is well suited for such problems, as it can effectively capture nonlinear interactions directly from experimental data without the need for explicit mathematical expressions24–26. In this work, a feed-forward backpropagation network architecture was employed. The constructed model is organized into three fundamental components: an input layer for receiving data, a hidden layer for processing and feature extraction, and an output layer responsible for generating the final results. The input layer includes three neurons representing Vc, f, and LM, while the output layer consists of four neurons corresponding to Fr, VB, Ra, and T. Figure 3 illustrates the architecture of the ANN model, highlighting how input variables are processed through the hidden layer to generate the predicted outputs. The size of the hidden layer, specifically the number of neurons it contains, significantly influences both the predictive performance of the model and its ability to generalize effectively to unseen data. Therefore, the number of neurons in the hidden layer was determined using an iterative trial-based procedure, where various network configurations were tested to achieve a balanced model that avoids both underfitting and overfitting. The final selection was based on achieving stable convergence during training along with reliable prediction performance across different datasets.

Architecture of the ANN for prediction of machining responses.

Prior to model training, the experimental data were subjected to a normalization process, whereby all input and output variables were scaled within a range of 0 to 1. This normalization process allows all parameters to influence the model on a comparable scale, thereby improving numerical stability and accelerating the convergence of the learning algorithm. The experimental dataset obtained from the machining experiments was carefully partitioned into three distinct subsets: one used to train the model, another employed to optimize and adjust its parameters, and a final subset reserved for evaluating its performance on previously unseen data. Such a data-splitting approach is essential for minimizing overfitting and improving the reliability of the model. The ANN model was developed using the Levenberg–Marquardt backpropagation technique, recognized for its fast-learning capability and strong performance in handling highly nonlinear regression tasks. Throughout the training phase, the weights and biases of the network were continuously adjusted to reduce the discrepancy between predicted and actual outputs. This optimization process involves forward propagation of inputs to generate predictions, followed by backward propagation of the associated errors to refine the network parameters.

The ANN model can be mathematically interpreted as a nonlinear functional relationship that maps the machining input variables to the corresponding output responses. In the network architecture, neurons located in both the hidden and output layers produce their responses by applying activation functions—typically the sigmoid or hyperbolic tangent functions. These functions introduce nonlinearity into the system, enabling the model to effectively learn and represent intricate relationships within the data. The neuron output is determined by the weighted summation of input signals combined with an associated bias term, enabling the network to approximate intricate interactions among variables. During the training process, the effectiveness of the model was evaluated by employing key statistical indicators, including mean squared error (MSE), root mean square error (RMSE), and the coefficient of determination (R²). A comprehensive discussion of these performance metrics and comparative analysis is shown in the Results and Discussion section. The trained ANN model ultimately acts as a surrogate predictive framework, which is further coupled with optimization techniques like GA and PSO. This hybrid approach facilitates efficient multi-objective optimization by substituting time-intensive experimental trials with rapid and reliable ANN-based predictions.

Hybrid ANN–GA optimization framework

To enable effective multi-objective optimization of machining parameters during the milling of Inconel 718, a hybrid framework combining ANN and GA was established. In this methodology, the trained ANN model functions as a surrogate evaluator, while GA is utilized as a powerful global search technique to identify optimal combinations of process parameters. This integrated approach addresses the shortcomings of individual optimization techniques by leveraging the strong predictive capability of ANN alongside the efficient exploration characteristics of evolutionary algorithms30,31. The Genetic Algorithm is a search and optimization technique that operates on a set of candidate solutions, drawing its principles from evolutionary processes such as selection, reproduction, and heredity. The approach operates on a population of possible solutions, with each member representing a distinct combination of machining variables, including cutting speed, feed rate, and the applied lubrication environment. These variables are encoded into chromosomes, generally in real-valued format, to support continuous parameter optimization. The execution of the ANN–GA framework starts with the random generation of an initial population within the specified bounds of the process parameters. Each chromosome is then assessed using the ANN model, which predicts the associated machining responses. Based on these predicted outputs, a fitness value is assigned to each individual according to a defined multi-objective criterion. The complete procedure of the ANN–GA hybrid strategy, demonstrating the interaction between the ANN-based prediction module and the GA optimization cycle, is shown in Fig. 4.

Flowchart of the hybrid ANN–GA optimization framework.

In this work, the fitness of each candidate solution was determined using a combined objective function (COF), which consolidates multiple machining responses into a single representative metric. This function was developed by first normalizing each response and then assigning suitable weights based on their relative significance, thereby ensuring a balanced consideration of all performance measures. The COF is essentially expressed as a weighted aggregation of normalized outputs, allowing simultaneous optimization of multiple criteria. After computing the fitness values, the Genetic Algorithm proceeds with evolutionary operations, namely selection, crossover, and mutation, to progressively improve the solution quality. The selection stage identifies superior individuals according to their fitness, and in this study, tournament selection was adopted due to its efficiency and ability to preserve diversity within the population. The selected parent individuals are subsequently combined through a crossover operation, in which segments of their encoded characteristics are interchanged to generate offspring. The degree to which this recombination occurs is controlled by a specified crossover probability parameter. Mutation is subsequently introduced to incorporate random perturbations in the population, which helps avoid premature convergence and promotes broader exploration of the search domain. The mutation rate is carefully controlled to strike an appropriate balance between exploration and exploitation. The offspring generated through these operations are again evaluated using the ANN model, and a new population is formed based on fitness-driven replacement criteria. The process of assessment followed by refinement continues in successive iterations until a predefined termination criterion is satisfied, such as reaching the specified generation limit or attaining stability in the fitness metrics. Ultimately, the ANN–GA framework yields an optimal set of machining parameters corresponding to improved performance outcomes. It is important to note that GA control parameters—such as population size, crossover rate, mutation rate, and the number of generations—significantly influence optimization efficiency. Hybrid ANN–PSO optimization framework

To enhance both the convergence characteristics and overall effectiveness of the optimization process, a hybrid approach integrating ANN with PSO was adopted. Similar to the ANN–GA methodology, the trained ANN model functions as a surrogate evaluator, while PSO is employed as the optimization mechanism to regulate the optimal machining parameters for milling Inconel 718. This ANN–PSO framework effectively integrates the strong nonlinear modeling capability of ANN with the rapid convergence and straightforward implementation of swarm intelligence techniques32,33.

PSO is a probabilistic, population-driven search algorithm that mimics the coordinated motion seen in flocks of birds and schools of fish. In this approach, every potential solution is treated as a particle navigating the solution domain with an associated position and velocity. The position of each particle corresponds to a particular set of machining parameters, whereas its velocity governs the trajectory and magnitude of its movement within the search space. The optimization procedure begins with the random initialization of a swarm of particles within predefined parameter limits. Each particle is then evaluated using the ANN model, which predicts the corresponding machining responses. These predicted outputs are used to compute a fitness value through a combined objective function, thereby enabling multi-objective optimization in a scalarized form.

The trajectory of each particle is influenced by two key principles: its own historically optimal position, known as the pbest, and the most optimal solution attained collectively by the swarm, referred to as the gbest. The velocity of each particle is iteratively updated by considering its current velocity, the deviation from its pbest, and the distance from the gbest. This mechanism allows the algorithm to effectively balance global exploration with local exploitation of promising regions. The interaction between ANN-based prediction and PSO-driven search is depicted in Fig. 5, illustrating the workflow of the hybrid ANN–PSO framework. From a mathematical standpoint, PSO functions by iteratively modifying both the velocity and spatial position of every particle within the search space. The velocity is adjusted based on three key influences: the particle’s previous motion (inertia), its own experience (cognitive component), and the information shared by neighboring particles or the swarm (social component). The inertia component maintains the particle’s existing trajectory, whereas the cognitive factor pulls it toward its personal best position, and the social factor guides it in the direction of the globally optimal solution identified by the swarm. The revised velocity is subsequently applied to determine the next position, enabling a continuous and guided exploration of the search domain. The inertia weight is a key parameter that governs the balance of the search process: larger values facilitate extensive exploration of the solution space, while smaller values enhance the algorithm’s ability to refine and converge on optimal solutions. To maintain a balance between these behaviors, the inertia weight is typically reduced linearly over successive iterations. The cognitive and social coefficients regulate the influence of individual and collective experiences and are chosen based on established guidelines in the literature. The efficacy of the PSO algorithm is highly reliant on its control parameters, including swarm size, inertia weight, cognitive and social coefficients, and the maximum number of iterations. These parameters were carefully selected through preliminary tuning and insights from prior studies to ensure stable convergence and to avoid premature stagnation. By employing ANN as a surrogate predictor, the need for repeated experimental trials is eliminated, thereby considerably reducing computational time and effort. Moreover, PSO achieves relatively faster convergence than many evolutionary algorithms due to its memory-based search strategy and the involvement of fewer control parameters. Its straightforward implementation, coupled with a strong capability for global exploration, makes PSO highly suitable for tackling complex multi-objective optimization problems in machining. The integration of ANN with PSO thus enables precise prediction of machining responses along with efficient identification of optimal process parameters. As a result, the proposed integrated approach facilitates the development of smart and eco-conscious machining strategies, especially for difficult-to-machine materials like Inconel 718.

Results and discussion

Effect of cutting parameters on responses

Effect on cutting force

Cutting force is widely recognized as a fundamental parameter for assessing machinability, since it strongly influences tool wear, power consumption, and the resulting surface integrity when machining Inconel 71834. The effect of varying machining parameters and different lubrication conditions on the cutting force was analyzed through main effect plot, as illustrated in Fig. 6. The findings indicate a direct relationship between feed rate and cutting force, where higher feed rates lead to increased force. This behavior can be scientifically explained by the increase in uncut chip thickness at higher feed rates, which enlarges the shear plane area during machining. As a result, a greater amount of material undergoes plastic deformation simultaneously, requiring higher shear energy and leading to increased cutting forces. Furthermore, Inconel 718 exhibits a strong strain hardening tendency, whereby the material ahead of the cutting tool becomes progressively stronger and more resistant during deformation. This strain hardening effect increases the shear strength of the material in the primary deformation zone, thereby further elevating the resistance to shearing and resulting in higher cutting forces. Together, these factors contribute to elevated opposition against the cutting tool during machining, particularly for difficult-to-machine superalloys like Inconel 718.

Influence of cutting parameters and lubrication conditions on cutting force.

On the other hand, cutting force shows only a marginal change with variations in cutting speed, indicating a comparatively weaker influence of this parameter. At reduced cutting velocities, the material exhibits increased resistance to plastic deformation owing to its higher effective strength, which consequently results in elevated cutting forces. As the cutting speed increases, the cutting force shows a modest reduction, mainly due to the softening of the workpiece material caused by elevated temperatures. At increased cutting speeds, the rise in temperature softens Inconel 718 by lowering its shear strength, which facilitates smoother material removal. After reaching a threshold, the cutting force tends to level off or exhibit a slight rise, primarily due to progressive tool degradation and the escalation of friction between the tool and the chip interface. The magnitude and variation of cutting force are strongly governed by the applied lubrication and cooling environments. Among the evaluated machining conditions, the dry setup exhibits the maximum cutting force, primarily because the lack of lubrication intensifies friction and promotes strong adhesive interactions between the cutting tool and the workpiece surface. The use of MQL significantly lowers cutting forces by generating a fine lubricating film at the tool–workpiece interface, thereby minimizing frictional resistance. However, its performance diminishes at higher temperatures, primarily because the lubricant droplets struggle to effectively reach and infiltrate the cutting interface. A further decrease in cutting force is achieved under Nano-MQL conditions, owing to the incorporation of nanoparticles that enhance lubrication through mechanisms such as rolling, mending, and surface smoothing. These nanoparticles function like microscopic ball bearings, reducing direct contact and consequently lowering frictional resistance.

The minimum cutting force is obtained under cryogenic CO₂ cooling, which can be attributed to its superior cooling efficiency. The rapid expansion of CO₂ results in substantial heat absorption during phase transformation, significantly lowering the temperature in the cutting zone35. This reduction in temperature diminishes adhesion and softening effects, thereby facilitating smoother chip formation and lowering cutting resistance. Additionally, the generation of CO₂ snow aids in efficient chip evacuation and reduces the tool–chip contact length, further contributing to the decrease in cutting force. The combined influence of machining parameters and lubrication strategies clearly demonstrates that optimized cutting conditions can substantially reduce cutting forces, leading to improved machining performance and extended tool life. These observations are in agreement with earlier studies on nickel-based superalloys, where feed rate has been identified as the most influential factor affecting cutting force, followed by lubrication conditions and cutting speed36. Overall, the findings emphasize that the adoption of advanced lubrication approaches, particularly cryogenic cooling, plays a vital role in minimizing cutting forces during the machining of Inconel 718, thereby enhancing both process efficiency and sustainability.

Effect on tool wear

Tool degradation serves as a critical parameter in machining operations, significantly affecting the lifespan of the cutting tool, the accuracy of the produced dimensions, and the overall finish of the machined surface37. Machining Inconel 718 is inherently difficult because of its exceptional strength, tendency to undergo severe strain hardening during cutting, and high chemical reactivity with tool materials, which collectively accelerate tool wear and degradation. Figure 7 illustrates the main effects plots, highlighting how variations in machining parameters and different lubrication environments influence flank wear behavior. The results clearly indicate that tool wear increases markedly with rising cutting speed. This can be attributed to the fact that higher cutting speeds significantly increase the strain rate in the primary and secondary deformation zones, resulting in greater plastic deformation energy. A large portion of this energy is converted into heat, thereby elevating the temperature at the tool–chip interface. Moreover, at higher cutting speeds, the contact time between the tool and chip is reduced, which limits heat dissipation and leads to heat accumulation near the cutting edge. In addition, intensified frictional interactions at the tool–chip interface further contributes to heat generation. The combined effect of increased heat generation and restricted heat dissipation accelerates thermally driven wear mechanisms such as diffusion, oxidation, and adhesion, ultimately resulting in higher tool wear. Additionally, increasing the cutting speed promotes stronger material adhesion at the tool–workpiece interface, facilitating the development of a built-up edge (BUE), which can lead to tool edge chipping and deterioration of surface quality38.

Influence of cutting parameters and lubrication conditions on tool wear.

The role of lubrication and cooling conditions in controlling tool wear is highly pronounced. In dry machining, the lack of any lubrication leads to intense friction and excessive heat generation, which significantly accelerates tool degradation. In these circumstances, adhesive and abrasive wear become the primary modes of material degradation, intensifying due to the high temperature and pressure present at the tool–workpiece interface. Employing MQL alleviates this effect by creating a fine lubricating film that minimizes friction and protects the tool surface. However, its cooling capability is limited, especially at higher temperatures, which restricts its overall effectiveness. Consequently, although MQL performs better than dry machining, the extent of wear reduction remains moderate. A more noticeable improvement is achieved with Nano-MQL, where nanoparticles are dispersed within the base lubricant. These nanoparticles enhance heat dissipation due to improved thermal conductivity and contribute to superior lubrication through mechanisms such as rolling, mending, and surface smoothing. By reducing direct contact between interacting surfaces, they effectively lower friction and wear. Additionally, the nanoparticles can occupy surface irregularities, resulting in smoother contact and reduced abrasive action. The most substantial reduction in tool wear is achieved under cryogenic CO₂ cooling conditions. The high cooling efficiency of CO₂ significantly decreases the temperature in the machining zone, thereby limiting thermally driven wear mechanisms such as diffusion and oxidation. Lower temperatures also reduce adhesion at the tool–workpiece interface, preventing the formation of built-up edge35. Moreover, improved chip fragmentation and efficient chip removal under cryogenic conditions reduce the duration of tool–chip contact, which further contributes to minimizing wear.

Effect on surface roughness

Surface finish serves as a critical indicator of machining effectiveness, significantly influencing the operational behavior, durability under cyclic loading, and frictional as well as wear properties of manufactured parts. Producing a superior surface finish while machining Inconel 718 remains highly challenging, primarily because of its exceptional mechanical strength, significant work-hardening characteristics, and its inherent propensity to adhere to the cutting tool39. Figure 8 presents the main effects plots, highlighting how variations in machining parameters and lubrication strategies affect the resulting surface roughness. Among all the considered factors, the feed rate emerges as the most influential parameter affecting the quality of the surface finish. A higher feed rate significantly deteriorates the surface finish, as it produces more pronounced and deeper tool feed impressions along the machined surface. An increase in feed rate leads to a thicker undeformed chip layer, thereby intensifying the prominence of tool marks and causing greater surface roughness. Additionally, increasing the feed rate intensifies cutting forces and induces greater vibrations, which collectively lead to a deterioration in surface finish. Surface roughness shows only a minor sensitivity to variations in cutting speed. At reduced cutting speeds, the machined surface quality generally deteriorates, primarily because of intensified adhesion between the workpiece and the tool, which promotes the development of a BUE on the cutting interface. The periodic breakaway of the adhered material leads to the formation of surface inconsistencies during machining. An increase in cutting speed generally leads to a reduction in surface roughness, primarily because elevated temperatures soften the work material, facilitating more uniform chip formation and minimizing the tendency for built-up edge development. Nevertheless, when the cutting speed exceeds an optimal threshold, the surface finish can slightly degrade, which is mainly attributed to intensified tool wear and thermal fluctuations at the tool–workpiece interface40.

Influence of cutting parameters and lubrication conditions on surface roughness.

The machining environment, particularly the presence or absence of lubrication and cooling, plays a critical role in determining surface finish. Under dry cutting conditions, the lack of any cooling or lubricating medium intensifies frictional interaction and promotes adhesion between the tool and workpiece, while also causing a significant rise in interface temperature. These combined effects ultimately result in a comparatively rougher machined surface. These conditions promote unstable chip formation and significant surface damage. The introduction of MQL improves the surface finish to some extent by providing a thin lubricating layer that reduces friction and enhances chip stability. However, its limited cooling capability restricts its performance under high-temperature conditions, and thus the improvement in surface quality remains moderate compared to more advanced techniques. A further enhancement in surface finish is observed with Nano-MQL, where the addition of nanoparticles significantly improves tribological behavior. These particles facilitate rolling and polishing actions at the interface, resulting in smoother surfaces. Additionally, their mending effect helps fill micro-level surface irregularities, thereby reducing roughness. The most superior surface quality is obtained under cryogenic CO₂ cooling conditions. The intense cooling effect substantially lowers the cutting temperature, which minimizes thermal distortion and adhesion at the interface. This reduction in temperature suppresses built-up edge formation and promotes stable chip flow, leading to improved surface integrity. Furthermore, efficient chip evacuation under cryogenic conditions reduces the chances of surface damage caused by redeposited material. Overall, cryogenic cooling provides the most favorable environment for achieving minimal surface roughness, followed by Nano-MQL and conventional MQL36. These observations highlight the importance of effective lubrication and thermal control in obtaining high-quality surfaces during the machining of Inconel 718.

Effect on cutting temperature

Cutting temperature plays a decisive role in machining performance, particularly for hard-to-cut alloys like Inconel 718. The substantial heat generated during the process can significantly influence tool durability, surface integrity, and overall operational efficiency. Due to its inherently poor thermal conductivity, Inconel 718 restricts heat dissipation, causing thermal concentration at the tool–workpiece interface. This localized heat buildup leads to higher cutting temperatures and promotes rapid tool wear41. The influence of machining parameters and lubrication environments on cutting temperature is illustrated through the main effects plots in Fig. 9. The results indicate that cutting temperature increases markedly with an increase in cutting speed. At higher cutting speeds, intensified friction and severe plastic deformation in both primary and secondary deformation zones contribute to substantial heat generation. Additionally, the reduced time available for heat dissipation at elevated speeds leads to heat accumulation near the cutting edge, thereby raising the temperature. This effect is further intensified by the poor thermal conductivity of Inconel 718, which limits the efficient transfer of heat away from the cutting zone.

Influence of cutting parameters and lubrication conditions on cutting temperature.

Although feed rate does affect the cutting temperature, its influence is not as dominant as that of cutting speed. An increase in feed rate results in a higher material removal rate and greater chip thickness, which intensifies the interaction between the tool and chip. This heightened interaction leads to increased frictional resistance and, consequently, greater heat generation. As a result, the cutting temperature rises. Additionally, elevated feed rates expand the contact region between the cutting tool and the workpiece, further amplifying thermal accumulation in the cutting zone42. Notably, the role of lubrication and cooling conditions becomes especially significant in governing the cutting temperature. In dry machining, the absence of any cooling or lubrication results in maximum temperature buildup due to severe friction and heat accumulation at the cutting interface. Without an effective heat dissipation mechanism, thermal concentration becomes significant, negatively affecting both tool performance and machining quality. The use of MQL provides a moderate reduction in temperature by forming a thin lubricating film that reduces friction at the interface. However, the limited quantity of lubricant restricts its cooling capacity, making it less effective in controlling the high temperatures generated during machining of Inconel 718. An improvement is observed with Nano-MQL, where the inclusion of nanoparticles enhances the thermal conductivity of the lubricant, leading to better heat dissipation and improved lubrication. The nanoparticles contribute to reduced friction and facilitate more efficient heat transfer. Nevertheless, factors such as particle agglomeration and dispersion stability can limit its effectiveness under extreme cutting conditions. The most effective temperature reduction is achieved under cryogenic CO₂ cooling. This is primarily due to the Joule–Thomson effect, where high-pressure CO₂ expands rapidly upon release, causing a sharp drop in temperature and the formation of CO₂ snow. The phase transformation absorbs a substantial amount of heat through sublimation, significantly lowering the temperature in the cutting zone. In addition, CO₂ is chemically inert, which prevents oxidation and contamination, making it an environmentally sustainable cooling medium. Efficient heat extraction under cryogenic conditions not only reduces cutting temperature but also enhances tool life and surface integrity by minimizing thermal damage and adhesion. Furthermore, improved chip breakability and reduced tool–chip contact length under such conditions contribute to lowering the overall thermal load35. These observations underscore the importance of advanced cooling strategies in the sustainable machining of Inconel 718, where effective thermal management is essential for improving machining performance and prolonging tool life.

Contribution to sustainable manufacturing

The present study contributes to sustainable manufacturing by integrating environmentally benign machining strategies with intelligent modeling and optimization techniques to improve both ecological and operational performance. The proposed framework systematically addresses sustainability through reduction of resource consumption, enhancement of machining efficiency, and minimization of environmental impact, as illustrated in Fig. 14.

AI-based hybrid optimization framework for sustainable machining of Inconel 718.

From an environmental standpoint, the implementation of MQL and Nano-MQL significantly reduces the consumption of cutting fluids compared to conventional flood cooling methods. This reduction minimizes fluid waste, lowers disposal requirements, and mitigates potential health hazards associated with prolonged exposure to conventional coolants. Furthermore, the adoption of cryogenic CO₂ cooling eliminates the use of liquid lubricants entirely, thereby preventing harmful chemical residues and contributing to a cleaner machining environment. The sublimation-based cooling mechanism of CO₂ ensures that no secondary waste is generated, reinforcing its suitability as an eco-friendly cooling technique.

In terms of process sustainability, the optimized machining parameters obtained in this study lead to a noticeable reduction in cutting forces and cutting temperature, particularly under Nano-MQL and cryogenic CO₂ conditions. Lower cutting forces directly reduce energy consumption during machining, while effective thermal control minimizes tool wear and extends tool life. This not only reduces the frequency of tool replacement but also decreases material waste and associated production costs. Consequently, the process efficiency is significantly enhanced while maintaining high machining performance.

A key contribution of this work lies in the integration of ANN with evolutionary and swarm-based optimization techniques such as GA and PSO. The ANN model serves as a surrogate predictive tool, enabling accurate estimation of machining responses without relying on repeated physical experimentation. This substantially reduces material usage, machine time, and energy consumption. The hybrid ANN–GA and ANN–PSO frameworks further improve optimization efficiency by identifying optimal machining conditions with fewer iterations, thereby reducing computational and experimental effort.

Conclusion

This study examined the machining performance of Inconel 718 under different lubrication and cooling environments, including dry machining, MQL, Nano-MQL, and cryogenic CO₂ conditions. The experimental results indicate that machining parameters significantly influence performance responses. Among these, feed rate was observed to have a dominant effect on cutting force and surface roughness, while cutting speed showed a stronger influence on cutting temperature and tool wear. The findings also indicate that the application of advanced lubrication strategies, particularly cryogenic CO₂, can improve machining performance compared to dry conditions under the investigated parameter range.

The developed ANN model demonstrated strong predictive capability, with coefficient of determination (R²) values exceeding 0.97 for all response parameters. The low values of MSE and RMSE further indicate that the model predictions are in close agreement with experimental observations. These results suggest that the ANN model is suitable for capturing the nonlinear relationships between machining parameters and output responses within the studied domain.

The optimization results show that hybrid approaches (ANN–GA and ANN–PSO) provide improved solution quality compared to standalone GA and PSO under the current experimental framework. The ANN–GA approach yielded the lowest combined objective function value among the tested methods, while ANN–PSO exhibited relatively faster convergence. Experimental validation confirmed that the optimized parameter sets produce results that are consistent with model predictions, with errors remaining within an acceptable range of approximately 3–4%.

Overall, the study demonstrates that integrating ANN with evolutionary and swarm-based optimization techniques can be an effective approach for analyzing and optimizing machining parameters of Inconel 718 under controlled conditions. The results provide useful insights into the influence of lubrication strategies and process parameters; however, their applicability is limited to the investigated range of conditions. Further studies incorporating a wider range of machining parameters, real-time monitoring, and industrial-scale validation may help to extend the applicability of the proposed approach.
