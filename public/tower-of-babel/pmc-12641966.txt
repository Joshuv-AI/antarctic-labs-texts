Recurrence risk prediction in resected stage I-III melanoma utilizing circulating tumor DNA
Authors: Mengke Zhao, Lianjun Zhao, Yueling Yang, Fufeng Wang, Jiani Yin, Xiaoying Wu, Yu Ren, Xinyu Su, Yirong Wu, Luqiao Li, Rong Huang, Kelin Zheng, JiaYu Wang, Zhengyun Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdjuvant therapies are now considered standard care for high-risk melanoma patients. It is urgent to identify reliable biomarkers to select patients at high risk of relapse, who will derive more benefit from adjuvant therapy.MethodsTargeted next-generation sequencing using a 437 cancer-related gene panel was performed on primary tumor samples from 55 patients in stage I-III melanoma and postsurgical plasma samples from 46 patients. Association analysis of clinico-genomic characteristics with disease-free survival was conducted.ResultsThe median DFS was 39.2 months (rangement: 1.4–49.7 months). Patients receiving anti-PD-1 adjuvant therapy had a longer DFS than those receiving adjuvant interferon therapy (mDFS, NA vs. 21.3 months, HR 0.36 [95% CI: 0.13–1.03], P = 0.047). No significant correlation of DFS with driver mutations in BRAF, NRAS and KIT was observed. Chromosomal instability score (CIS) was identified as an independent predictive factor of DFS following adjustment for clinical and genetic factors (HR 4.06 [95% CI: 1.28–12.89], P = 0.017), with an inferior DFS observed in patients with high CIS compared with the CIS-low patients (mDFS, 14.3 vs. 49.7 months, HR 3.90 [95%CI: 1.40–11.00], P = 0.005). In addition, patients with a maxVAF > 1% in the postsurgical ctDNA had a worse DFS compared with those with a maxVAF ≤1% (mDFS, 11.0 vs. 49.7 months, HR 3.70 [95% CI: 1.20–11.00], P = 0.012). Finally, CIS and postsurgical ctDNA status were considered as independent factors for recurrence risk in stage I-III melanoma.ConclusionIdentification of clinical and genetic biomarkers for recurrence risk prediction in resected melanoma may aid in clinical decision-making for early disease monitoring and optimal design of therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15093-w.

Introduction

Melanoma is a highly malignant neoplasm that arises from melanocytes. Invasion is an important feature of melanoma, which leads to a high risk of metastatic spread and poor prognosis. In patients who underwent surgical resection, the five-year survival rates of stage II and III for were 44.0% and 38.4%, respectively, which were markedly worse compared with a 94.1% survival rate in stage I patients [1]. Despite multimodal curative treatment, these patients may still have minimal residual disease (MRD) after surgery, and ultimately develop recurrence [2]. Thus, biomarkers that might predict recurrence of resected melanoma are urgently needed, particularly for stage II-III patients.

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has emerged as a surrogate measure for minimal tumor burden and serves as a reliable predictive and prognostic marker in both resected and advanced melanoma [3, 4]. In stage III melanoma, ctDNA released at baseline and post-operation were associated with inferior relapse-free survival (RFS) and distant metastasis free survival (DMFS) [5]. In stage II/III melanoma, detectable ctDNA after surgery related to decreased disease-free interval (DFI), DMFS, five-year overall survival rate and overall survival [3]. However, stage I patients were included in few studies, and more relevant data and clinical practice are demanded to uncover the prognostic or predictive implications of ctDNA for patients with stage I-III melanoma. In addition, the advent of high throughput sequencing and its widespread availability in clinical practice in recent years has led to the discovery of a vast number of prognostic and predictive genomic biomarkers in melanoma. For example, chromosomal instability has been proposed to be a potential driver for tumor progression and metastasis and recognized as an important prognostic feature in melanoma, as well as in other solid tumors such as ovarian cancer and prostate cancer [6, 7]. Extensive research has also been conducted to identify clinicopathological features that could risk stratify the survival outcomes in melanoma [8–10]. Clinical factors such as thickness and anatomical site of the primary tumor, extent of ulceration, age of diagnosis, and whether nodal metastases were clinically occult or apparent are associated with prognosis in localized melanoma [11]. However, the combined prognostic and predictive value of these clinical and genetic features remained to be explored.

In this study, using a 437-cancer-gene panel for ctDNA detection by the NGS method, we sought to determine whether ctDNA monitoring could forecast disease recurrence and survival outcome and explore additional biomarkers of recurrence risk in stage I -III melanoma patients receiving curative intent surgery.

Materials and methods

Patient enrollment

The research involved 55 individuals diagnosed with stage I-III melanoma who underwent radical surgery at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, affiliated with Nanjing University. The Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital approved this research. All clinical information was recorded by the resident physicians in accordance with the normative requirements.

Sample collection

Surgical tumor tissues and post-surgical plasma samples were gathered for mutational profiling. The genomic information was derived in all cases from the primary and not the lymph node metastases. We handled plasma specimens within two hours after blood was drawn. Simultaneously, in order to identify germline mutations and mutations from clonal hematopoiesis, we gathered white blood cells from the buffy coat as standard controls. Genetic analysis was performed at Nanjing Geneseeq Technology Inc., China, which is certified by Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA), College of American Pathologists (CAP), and ISO 15,189, following the reviewed protocols.

Library preparation and sequencing

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) was extracted from plasma by QIAmp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen). Genomic DNA were extracted from surgical tumor specimens and leukocytes was isolated using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen). The quality of the purified genomic DNA was evaluated with a Nanodrop2000 by Thermo Fisher Scientific. Each DNA sample was measured using a Qubit 3.0 and the dsDNA HS Assay Kit from Life Technologies, adhering to the instructions provided by the manufacturer.

We used KAPA Hyper Prep Kit (Roche) as modified to prepare sequencing libraries. In brief, for tumor tissue and normal control samples, 1–2 µg of genomic DNA, which was sheared into ~ 350 bp fragments using Covaris M220 instrument (Covaris), underwent end-repairing, A-tailing and ligation with indexed sequencing adapters, followed by size selection using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter). Libraries for the plasma samples were prepared by fragmenting up to 50 ng of cfDNA with a modified protocol using endrepair, A-tailing, and ligation with a custom adapter containing a molecular index (UMI), then performed PCR amplification with demultiplexing index primers and recovered the cfDNA libraries with Agencourt AMPure XP beads.

Distinctive indexed libraries were merged in particular proportions, accumulating to micrograms of input (2 µg). Adding Human cot-1 DNA (Life Technologies) and xGen Universal blocking oligos (Integrated DNA Technologies) as blocking reagents. Tailored xGen Lockdown probes (Integrated DNA Technologies) aimed at 437 genes were employed for hybridization enrichment. The capture process utilized Dynabeads M-270 (Life Technologies) and the xGen Lockdown hybridization and wash kit (Integrated DNA Technologies) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Libraries that were captured were subjected to on-bead PCR amplification in KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (KAPA Biosystems), following purified with Agencourt AMPure XP beads.

The library amount was measured by qPCR with KAPA Library Quantification Kit (KAPA Biosystems), and the length of the library was detected by the Bioanalyzer2100 (Agilent Technologies). After the library enrichment, we sequenced the library on HiSeq4000 next-gen sequencing platform (Illumina) by the manufacture instructions.

Mutation calling

For quality assessment of FASTQ files, Trimmomatic [12] was employed to discard low-quality bases at the start or end (with leading/trailing low quality below 30) as well as N bases. The filtered reads were then mapped to the reference human genome (hg19) via the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner [13]. Post local realignment around known indels and base quality recalibration with the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK 3.4.0), removing PCR duplicates by Picard (Broad Institute, MA, USA).

We used VarScan2 to call single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and indels from the samples, using a cutoff of 0.01 (0.001) variant allele frequency for tissue (cfDNA) samples. Call SNVs and indels were annotated using ANNOVAR [14] to provide variant information such as variant type and dbSNP ID; clinical annotation information; and Protein Impact prediction using SIFT [15] and PolyPhen [16]. Germline mutations were excluded based on comparison to whole blood matched controls. CNVkit [17] was used to analyze the CNVs from the samples, and call gain (depth ratio >2.0 for tissue or 1.6 for cfDNA) and loss (depth ratio < 0.6). We defined the chromosomal instability score (CIS) as the average percent of the genome found to be aberrant copy number (log2 depth ratio more than 0.2 or less than − 0.2) on all of the autosomal chromosomes, weighted for number of chromosomes that were analyzed. A stepwise approach was used to determine the optimal cutoff value. Specifically, we calculated the hazard ratios (HR) at different cutoff values for CIS and selected the optimal cutoff by comparing the HRs and the widths of their 95% confidence intervals. Additionally, we employed Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis to compare disease-free survival (DFS) between high and low-risk groups at this cutoff value, thereby validating its effectiveness.

Statistical analysis

Overall survival, DFS were measured by the standard radiological criteria. DFS was considered as the duration from surgical date to date of radiological relapse or death. We used Fisher’s exact test for the comparison of proportions among cohorts. Survival data were analyzed by the Kaplan-Meier plots and by the log-rank test for comparison. Both univariate and multivariate analyses were done to evaluate the relationship between various factors and the prognosis (Cox proportional hazards model). All statistical analysis was done using R software version 4.2.2 (a two-sided p < 0.05 is considered significant).

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study cohort

This study included 57 patients with stage I-III melanoma who underwent radical surgery at our institute. Two patients who were lost to follow-up were excluded from the analysis. A schematic flowchart of this study is shown in Fig. 1, with clinical characteristics of 55 patients summarized in Table 1. The median age at diagnosis was 59 years (range: 17–79 years), and 56.4% (31/55) were males. 63.6% (35/55) of the patients presented with stage III disease, and the remaining 3.6% (2/55) and 32.7%(18/55) patients had stage I and II diseases, respectively. The two major histological subtypes were 67.3% (37/55) acral melanoma and 30.9% (17/55) cutaneous melanoma. And one patient’s onset is metastatic lymph nodes. The majority (> 80%) of patients had Breslow thickness of more than 2 mm, and were previously untreated. 65.5% (36/55) of patients had ulceration and 60.0% (33/55) had lymph node metastasis. All patients had received adjuvant therapy, including commonly with interferon (45.5%, 25/55) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (43.6%, 24/55). The clinical characteristics in cutaneous melanoma and acral melanoma patients are showed in Supplementary Table S1. No differences in clinical characteristics other than adjuvant therapy regimens were observed between the acral melanoma and cutaneous melanoma subgroups. 64.7%(11/17) of patients in the cutaneous subgroup received anti-PD-1 therapy, while more patients (56.8%, 21/37) in acral group received interferon therapy (P = 0.03).

Univariate analysis revealed an association of DFS with the types of adjuvant therapy administered. Patients who had received anti-PD-1 therapy had a longer DFS than those received interferon (mDFS, NA vs. 21.3 months, HR 0.36 [95% CI: 0.13–1.03], P = 0.047) (Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Fig. S1D). In addition, several other clinical characteristics, including over 60 years of age (mDFS, 22.8 months vs. NA, HR 2.40 [95% CI: 0.86–6.72], P = 0.087), the acral subtype (mDFS, 22.8 months vs. NA, HR 3.08 [95% CI: 0.88–10.80], P = 0.064) and ulceration of the primary tumor (mDFS, 22.8 months vs. NA, HR 3.07 [95% CI: 0.88–10.70], P = 0.066) showed trends towards increased risk of recurrence (Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Fig. S1A-S1C). The multivariable hazard ratio (HR) for PD-1 compared to interferon is greater than 1 (Supplementary Table S2), reversing the protective effect observed in the univariate analysis. This change may be attributed to the fact that the multivariable analysis accounted for additional confounding factors that were not controlled for in the univariate analysis. None of the clinical variables remained associated with DFS in multivariate COX analysis.

Association of recurrence risk with genetic alterations

Genetic profiling was performed in 52 cases with available primary tumor samples. BRAF was the most frequently mutated gene (15/52, 28.8%), with a higher frequency in cutaneous melanoma (10/17, 58.8%). NRAS and CCND1 alterations were detected in 23.1% (12/52) and 17.3% (9/52) of patients, respectively. Mutations in KIT, TERT and NF1 were detected in 15.4% (8/52) of patients. The common driver genes in melanoma, such as BRAF, NRAS and KIT, were mutually exclusive (Fig. 2B). No significant correlation of DFS with common driver mutations in BRAF, NRAS and KIT was found (Supplementary Fig.S2). Patients with CCND1 amplification (mDFS, 15.2 vs. 49.7 months, HR 2.74 [95% CI: 0.93–8.04], P = 0.056; Fig. 3A) or FGF19 amplification (mDFS, 14.2 vs. 49.7 months, HR 2.58 [95% CI: 0.81–8.26], P = 0.096; Fig. 3B) exhibited a trend towards inferior DFS compared with wild-type. At the pathway level, patients with alterations in cell cycle pathway genes tended to have poor DFS (mDFS, 22.8 vs. 49.7 months, HR 2.73 [95% CI: 0.73–5.60], P = 0.170, Fig. 3C and D).

We also explored the association of chromosomal instability as measured by chromosomal instability score (CIS) with recurrence risk. An increase in the risk of recurrence was observed as CIS value increased (Fig. 4A). At a CIS cutoff of 0.3, we observed a significant reduction of DFS in patients with high CIS compared with CIS-low patients (mDFS, 14.3 vs. 49.7 months, HR 3.90 [95%CI: 1.40–11.00], P = 0.005; Fig. 4B). Subgroup analysis showed similar results on CIS in acral melanoma (mDFS, 14.3 vs. 39.2 months, HR 3.41 [95% CI: 1.04–11.21], P = 0.032; Fig. 4C) and cutaneous melanoma (mDFS, 12.5 months vs. NA, HR 7.43 [95% CI: 0.66–83.93], P = 0.059; Fig. 4D).

CtDNA in prediction of melanoma recurrence risk

Postsurgical plasma samples (median time post-surgery, two weeks) were collected from 46 patients in the study. Of those patients, 31 had acral melanoma and the remaining patients had cutaneous melanoma. 26.1% (12/46) of patients had detectable ctDNA. No difference in the detection rate of postsurgical ctDNA was found comparing acral melanoma and cutaneous melanoma (22.6% vs. 35.7%, Supplementary Fig. S3A). Furthermore, no association was found between other baseline clinicopathological characteristics and ctDNA detection rate (Supplementary Fig. S3B).

Next, we divided the patients into ctDNA-positive and -negative subgroups according to the ctDNA status in postsurgical plasma. The ctDNA-negative group had a trend towards higher DFS than the ctDNA-positive group (mDFS, 15.6 vs. 49.7 months, HR 2.10 [95% CI: 0.73–5.90], P = 0.160; Fig. 5B). In addition, we assessed the association of ctDNA maximum variant allele frequency (maxVAF), which is indicative of the metabolic burden of the tumor [18], with recurrence risk. We found that recurrence risk was strongly correlated with maxVAF levels (Fig. 5A). In particular, patients with a maxVAF greater than 1% had worse DFS compared with those with maxVAF less than or equal to 1% (mDFS, 11.0 vs. 49.7 months, HR 3.70 [95% CI: 1.20–11.00], P = 0.012; Fig. 5C). Multivariate analyses adjusted by histological subtype confirmed that postsurgical MRD positive (ctDNA maxVAF is greater than 1%) was an independent predictor of DFS (HR 3.73 [95% CI: 1.22–11.36], P = 0.021; Table 3).

Discussion

Recent therapeutic advances in melanoma in the adjuvant setting highlight the need to develop reliable biomarkers for selecting patients at high risk of relapse, who might benefit from early monitoring of disease progression and/or a more intensive therapeutic regimen. In this retrospective study, next-generation sequencing on primary tumor samples and postoperative plasma revealed that CIS at baseline and ctDNA status following surgery were independent predictors of disease recurrence in patients with stage I-III melanoma.

Previously, interferon played a significantly important role in the treatment of melanoma patients, and many clinical trials demonstrated its ability to reduce disease recurrence and death in high-risk patients. While interferon showed promise, its efficacy was limited, the overall response rates were modest, and many patients experienced significant side effects, leading to ongoing debates about its clinical utility. Therefore, currently, interferon is less commonly used as a front-line treatment for melanoma, but it remains an option for certain patients, particularly in adjuvant settings. Clinical benefit from adjuvant immunotherapy in patients with resected melanoma has been demonstrated in several trials [19–21]. Multiple immune checkpoint inhibitors, including ipilimumab, nivolumab, and pembrolizumab, have been approved by the FDA for use in the adjuvant setting of melanoma [22]. Similarly, results from the present study also found that patients receiving anti-PD-1 adjuvant therapy showed prolonged DFS than those receiving interferon therapy.

The molecular landscape of primary tissues as depicted by next-generation sequencing in the current study was consistent with previous reports [23, 24]. However, it is worth noting that the predictive value of common driver mutations has been controversial. Aleksandra Gos et al. has reported that patients harbouring BRAF or NRAS mutations showed markedly shorter DFS when compared with wild-type melanoma patients [25]. Meanwhile, based on a meta-analyses study, it has been shown that BRAF mutated melanoma had a comparable effect on DFS to non-BRAF in stage I-III melanoma [26]. In our study, we found no association of the driver gene mutations with recurrence risk, which could have been attributed to receiving adjuvant therapy. On the other hand, patients with CCND1 or FGF19 amplification showed a trend towards higher recurrence risk in our cohort. And it has been reported that CCND1 copy number alteration might be associated with Breslow thickness in invasive acral melanoma [27] and the inactivation of cell cycle was common in many tumors [28]. However, the P-values were not significant in our multivariate analyses. We should avoid asserting that these amplifications have significant clinical relevance due to the p-value being greater than 0.05. Instead, it is recommended to view these results as preliminary findings that require more samples and further research to validate their actual impact.

More importantly, our study identified CIS as a promising predictor of recurrence risk. This corresponds to previous clinical studies, which demonstrated that the change in CIS score can serve as an early predictor of therapeutic response to chemotherapy and immunotherapy of many cancer types [29]. In our study, acral melanomas exhibited a higher prevalence of CIS compared to cutaneous melanomas (with a lower P-value), it would be important to explore whether CIS serves as a stronger marker for progression risk in acral melanomas than in cutaneous ones. The mechanisms of genomic instability in melanoma can be sophisticated and multi-factorial. Chromosomal alterations, including deletions, duplications and rearrangements, in melanoma may derive from telomere biology, oncogene-induced replication stress and UV-induced DNA damage, which is the primary cause of melanoma [30]. As a prevalent feature in melanoma, our findings further emphasize the need for CIS estimation in the early stage. We confirmed that CIS is an independent predictive factor for DFS. However, given that acral melanomas have been shown to exhibit a higher prevalence of CIS compared to cutaneous melanomas, it would be important to explore whether CIS serves as a stronger marker for progression risk in acral melanomas than in cutaneous ones.

CtDNA has been recognized as a blood-based biomarker for solid tumors with the characteristics of being minimally invasive, simple, relatively affordable and sustainable. In advanced melanoma patients, ctDNA levels of BRAF, NRAS or TERT was associated with progression-free survival [31, 32]. In early-stage melanoma, we showed that postsurgical ctDNA status may serve as an independent predictor of DFS. We also examined the cumulative potential of these biomarkers to provide optimal risk stratification of recurrence in melanoma.

There are several limitations in the current study, including single-center retrospective study design and relatively small sample size. Thus, future studies with a large sample size are needed to verify our results. In addition, plasma samples at only one time point were available for the correlation analysis with recurrent risk. Collection of plasma samples during post-operative care remains the most convenient and feasible option given the retrospective nature of the study and challenges in long-term clinical follow-up. Baseline and longitudinal plasma samples should be collected to better monitor minimal residual disease, guide treatment, and predict clinical outcome. Besides, cutaneous melanomas were primarily treated with anti-PD-1 therapy, while acral melanomas were mainly treated with interferon. This indicates that not all patients received standardized first-line postoperative treatment, which represents a significant limitation. In spite of these limitations, our research represents a proof-of-concept study for future references. In summary, we investigated the correlation of clinicopathological characteristics, genomic alterations, and postsurgical ctDNA status with the recurrence risk in patients with stages I-III melanoma after surgery. CIS and postsurgical ctDNA status were considered as independent factors for recurrence risk in stage I-III melanoma, and can be used to guide adjuvant therapy. Therefore, our findings may help clinical decision-making for optimal therapeutic in melanoma patients at high recurrence risk and provide insights that will advance future studies.
