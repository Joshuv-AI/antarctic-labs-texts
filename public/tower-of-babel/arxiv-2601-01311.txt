Concave Certificates: Geometric Framework for Distributionally Robust Risk and Complexity Analysis
Hong T.M. Chu

Abstract
Distributionally Robust (DR) optimization aims to certify worst-case risk within a Wasserstein uncertainty set. Current certifications typically rely either on global Lipschitz bounds, which are often conservative, or on local gradient information, which provides only a first-order approximation. This paper introduces a novel geometric framework based on the least concave majorants of the growth rate function. Our proposed concave certificate establishes a tight bound of DR risk that remains applicable to non-Lipschitz and non-differentiable losses. We extend this framework to complexity analysis, introducing a deterministic bound that complements standard statistical generalization bound. Furthermore, we utilize this certificate to bound the gap between adversarial and empirical Rademacher complexity, demonstrating that dependencies on input diameter, network width, and depth can be eliminated. For practical application in deep learning, we introduce the adversarial score as a tractable relaxation of the concave certificate that enables efficient and layer-wise analysis of neural networks. We validate our theoretical results in various numerical experiments on classification and regression tasks on real-world data.



Keywords:
Distributionally Robust Optimization, Generalization Bound, Rademacher Complexity

Introduction

Given feature data
X
and label
Y
, we seek a parameterized network
f_{\theta}
to model their relationship
Y\approx f_{\theta}(X)
. This is typically formulated as minimizing the expected loss

\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathbb{P}_{\rm true}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)],

(1)

where
Z=(X,Y)
,
\Theta
is the set of feasible parameters and
\bm{l}
is the loss function. The true data distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\rm true}
in (
1
) is often unknown and approximated by the empirical distribution
\mathbb{P}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{\chi}_{\{Z^{(i)}\}}
, which can lead to over-fitting. To mitigate this issue, the robust counterpart of (
1
) aims to minimize the worst-case loss within a neighborhood of
\mathbb{P}_{N}
by solving

\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\sup_{\mathbb{P}:\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}_{N})\leq\epsilon}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)],

(2)

where
\mathcal{D}
is a discrepancy on the probability space
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
. In this work, we focus on the Wasserstein discrepancy (Definition
3
), which intuitively represents the minimum cost to transport the mass of
\mathbb{P}
to that of
\mathbb{P}_{N}
. The inner supremum problem is referred to as the distributionally robust (DR) risk:

\text{(DR risk)}\quad\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)=\sup_{\mathbb{P}:\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}_{N})\leq\epsilon}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)].

(3)

Distributionally Robust Risk.
Essentially, the DR risk (
3
) quantifies the sensitivity of the loss value under distributional shifts bounded by a budget
\delta
. In general, computing
\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)
is intractable. To bypass this, two primary approaches are used: the
Lipschitz certificate
and the
gradient certificate
. The Lipschitz certificate estimates an upper bound of
\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)
. For instance, if
\bm{l}
is
L_{\theta}
-Lipschitz then
\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]+L_{\theta}\epsilon
, and this bound is known to be tight for linear hypothesis
(
16
,
11
,
10
,
1
,
13
,
14
)
. We refer reader to a recent survey by
41
on how Lipschitz calculus can be used to study robustness. Estimating the global Lipschitz constant
L_{\theta}
for deep networks often reduces to a layer-wise estimation of Lipschitz constants
(
35
,
32
,
24
)
. This raises fundamental questions: why do functions like the entropy loss or the square-root loss
(
8
)
exhibit robustness despite being non-Lipschitz? Furthermore, even though the
4\times
Sigmoid, Tanh and ReLU activations share a Lipschitz modulus of 1, why do they not possess identical robustness properties? Alternatively, the other approach of gradient certificate
(
5
,
15
,
3
)
approximates
\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)
using first-order information as
\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)\approx\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]+\epsilon\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N}}[\left\|\nabla_{x}\bm{l}(Z;\theta)\right\|^{q}]\right)^{1/q}
where
1/p+1/q=1
. However, this first-order estimation is asymptotic and holds only as the budget
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. Moreover, it requires
\bm{l}
to be differentiable and does not provide a true upper bound of
\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)
.

Generalization Capability.
To understand the model’s generalization capability in this robust setting, we utilize the notion of Rademacher complexity
(
6
,
23
)
.
For a class of loss functions
\mathcal{L}:=\{z\mapsto\bm{l}(z;\theta)\mid\theta\in\Theta\}
, the empirical Rademacher Complexity (RC) measures the richness of
\mathcal{L}
by its ability to correlate with random noise on the sample
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
:

\text{(RC)}\quad\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L})=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\bm{l}(Z^{(i)};\theta)\right],

(4)

where
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{N})
are independent Rademacher variables taking values in
\{-1,+1\}
with equal probability. Intuitively, if RC is large, then
\mathcal{L}
has the capacity to fit arbitrary noise
\sigma
, leading to overfitting. Conversely, a small RC indicates that
\mathcal{L}
learns meaningful patterns. Standard results by
7
,
23
show that
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L})
directly bounds the generalization gap:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\rm true}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]+c\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L})+C\!(\delta)\!,

(5)

where
C(\delta)
is a confidence term. Therefore, a small RC also implies a tight generalization bound. In the context of DRO, we focus on the class of worst-case loss functions
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\epsilon}:=\{z\mapsto\tilde{\bm{l}}_{\epsilon}(z;\theta)=\sup_{z^{\prime}:d(z^{\prime},z)\leq\epsilon}\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta)\mid\theta\in\Theta\}
, which leads to the definition of Adversarial Rademacher Complexity (ARC)
(
22
,
38
,
2
,
36
)
:

\text{(ARC)}\,\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\epsilon})=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\tilde{\bm{l}}_{\epsilon}(Z^{(i)};\theta)\right].

(6)

Deriving tight bounds for ARC is significantly more challenging than for RC because the inner supremum operator destroys structural properties that are typically exploited in traditional complexity analysis. For linear hypotheses,
22
,
38
establish that the gap between ARC and RC scales linearly with the weight norm
\left\|\theta\right\|
, which serves as the Lipschitz constant. For deep neural networks, however,
2
and
36
show these bounds grow not just with weights, but also with the network’s depth, width, and data diameter
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
. This predicted surge is counter-intuitive when compared to the DR risk
\mathcal{R}_{p}
mentioned earlier: the adversarial risk of a feedforward network is fundamentally controlled by its Lipschitz constant, suggesting that the actual complexity should not blow up simply because a network becomes wider or deeper.

Main Contributions.
We summarize our main contributions and organize our paper as follows.

•

We introduce a novel framework to estimate the distributionally robust risk
\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)
. This geometric framework establishes an elegant bound showing that the robust-empirical risk gap
\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)-\hat{\mathcal{R}}
stays between the average of the
least star-shaped majorants

\operatorname{lb}_{p}(\epsilon)
and the
least concave majorant

\operatorname{cc}_{p}(\epsilon)
of loss’s growth functions (Theorem
1
). Notably, we do not require the loss
\bm{l}
to be convex/differentiable/Lipschitz, or the cost
d
to be a metric, or the domain
\mathcal{Z}
to be bounded. Our analysis reveals how the DR risk
\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)
evolves as the exponent
p
changes (Corollary
1
) and provides exact conditions for determining whether
\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)
is finite (Corollary
2
, Example
1
).

•

We extend this framework to analyze robust generalization by introducing
concave complexity

\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon)
, defined as the supremum of the least concave majorant of the maximal rates across the loss class
\mathcal{L}
. This metric allows us to derive a deterministic generalization bound (Proposition
1
) that complements the standard statistical bound (
5
). Analogous to standard Rademacher complexity, we prove that our proposed concave complexity exhibits several calculus properties, including monotonicity, subadditivity, invariance under convex hull, and a contraction lemma.

•

By leveraging these properties, we prove that the adversarial-empirical Rademacher complexity gap is controlled by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon)
(Theorem
2
) and establish a corresponding result for the adversarial-empirical concave complexity gap (Theorem
3
). Specifically, our framework recovers existing complexity bounds for linear classifiers (Example
3
) while providing significantly tighter bounds for MLPs (Example
5
) compared to previous works. We demonstrate that many components in existing bounds (such as input diameter, network width, and depth) can be removed entirely, and the loss class can be tailored to obtain dimension-free bound.

•

To facilitate practical implementation in deep learning when
d
is the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we introduce the adversarial score
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
as a relaxation of the concave certificate
\operatorname{cc}_{p}
. This enables the robustness of a deep network to be evaluated layer-by-layer through a composition rule (Lemma
3
). We provide explicit calculations of
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
for common activation and loss functions in both classification (Proposition
4
) and regression (Proposition
5
) tasks. Notably, we show that our framework is capable of explaining and distinguishing the robustness properties of loss functions that are non-Lipschitz or non-differentiable (Figure
3
).

•

We validate our theoretical results through two experiments using real-world datasets. In the regression task (Section
4.1
), we numerically demonstrate that our adversarial score is strictly tighter and more informative than traditional Lipschitz and gradient-based certificates. In the classification task (Section
4.2
), we verify that the adversarial-empirical Rademacher gap for tailored loss class is dimension-free. We conclude our paper in Section
5
.

In this work, we are interested in the smallest concave/star-shaped upper bound of a non-negative univariate function on

(

18

Definition 1

(least concave majorant)
.

Given

\mathcal{C}_{f}(t)\coloneqq\operatorname{inf}\left\{H(t)\mid H(t)\geq f(t),\,H\text{ is concave}\right\}.

Definition 2

(least star-shaped majorant)
.

Given
f\colon[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, define the least star-shaped majorant

\mathcal{S}_{f}(t)\coloneqq\operatorname{inf}\left\{H(t)\!\mid\!H(t)\!\geq\!f(t),\,H\text{ is star-shaped}\right\}\!.

It is worth noting that the definitions of least majorant are valid, since the infimum of a collection of functions is equivalent to the intersection of their hypographs; thus, concavity or star-shapedness is induced immediately. The following lemma follows directly from the definitions and

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that

.

•

\mathcal{S}_{f}(t)\leq\mathcal{C}_{f}(t)

.

•

\mathcal{C}_{f}(t)\leq\inf_{a,b\in\mathbb{R}}\{at+b\mid au+b\geq f(u)\forall u\geq 0\}

•

If

If

If

3
Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we establish the formal connection between the growth rate of a loss function and its distributional sensitivity, demonstrating that this sensitivity can be effectively certified through univariate rate functions and providing a framework that remains applicable to non-Lipschitz and non-differentiable losses. Based on the concave certificate in Theorem
1
and its extension to robust generalization bound, we show how the geometric properties of a model directly control its performance under distributional shifts.

3.1
Distributional Robustness Certificates via Least Majorants

We begin by formally defining key quantities that measure how the loss changes in response to localized perturbations of the data.

Definition 4

(growth rate functions)
.

Given Notation
1
, we define the
individual
rate
\Delta_{\theta}
of the loss
\bm{l}
at
z
as

\Delta_{\theta}(z,t)\triangleq\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta)-\bm{l}(z;\theta)\mid d(z^{\prime},z)\leq t\right\},

(7)

for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}
and
t\geq 0
. Recall that
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
is finite, we define the (empirical)
maximal
rate as

\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(t)=\max\limits_{\hat{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\Delta_{\theta}(\hat{z},t),

(8)

for any
t\geq 0
.

Intuitively, the rate
\Delta_{\theta}(z,t)
(
7
) measures the maximal increase in loss when we perturb a single data point
z
within a radius of
t
. In fact, (function)
\Delta_{\theta}(z,t)
is a generalization of the (scalar) growth rate notion proposed in
14
. Besides, the adversarial loss
\tilde{\bm{l}}(\hat{z};\theta)
(
6
)
(
22
,
38
,
36
)
is equal to
\Delta_{\theta}(\hat{z},t)+\bm{l}(\hat{z};\theta)
. It is worth noting that both
\Delta_{\theta}(\hat{z},\cdot)
and
\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(\cdot)
are non-decreasing. By shifting the focus from the complex loss function
\bm{l}
to these univariate rate functions, our first theoretical result proposes to bound the robust-empirical risk gap through the geometric construction of least concave and star-shaped majorants.

Theorem 1

(Distributional Robustness Certificates via Least Majorants)
.

Given Notation
1
, define the least concave majorant
\mathcal{C}_{f}
, the least star-shaped majorant
\mathcal{S}_{f}
, the individual rate
\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},t)
, and maximal rate
\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(t)
as in Definitions
1
,
2
, and
4
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
,

\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)-\hat{\mathcal{R}}\geq\operatorname{lb}_{p}(\epsilon)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}s^{(i)}(\epsilon),\end{array}

(9)

where
s^{(i)}(\epsilon)=\mathcal{S}_{f^{(i)}}(\epsilon^{p})
with
f^{(i)}\colon t\mapsto\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},t^{1/p})
if
p<\infty
, and
s^{(i)}(\epsilon)=\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},\epsilon)
if
p=\infty
. In addition,

\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)-\hat{\mathcal{R}}\leq\operatorname{cc}_{p}(\epsilon),

(10)

where
\operatorname{cc}_{p}(\epsilon)=\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}(\epsilon^{p})
with
f^{\max}\colon t\mapsto\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(t^{1/p})
if
p<\infty
, and
\operatorname{cc}_{p}(\epsilon)=\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\epsilon^{+}}\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(t)
if
p=\infty
.

Sketch of Proof.
We first derive the lower bound
\operatorname{lb}_{p}(\epsilon)
by constructing a discrete perturbation
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
such that
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\mathbb{P}_{N})\leq\epsilon
and show that the risk gap
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]-\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}_{N}}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]\geq\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\sup_{u\in[\epsilon^{p},\infty)}\left\{\frac{\epsilon^{p}\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},u^{1/p})}{u}\right\}
, which is equal to
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\mathcal{S}_{f^{(i)}}(\epsilon^{p})
according to Lemma
1
. We then derive the upper bound by rewriting the risk gap as
\int_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\left(\bm{l}(\tilde{z};\theta)-\bm{l}({z};\theta)\right)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\pi}(\tilde{z},z)
where
\tilde{\pi}
is the optimal coupling between
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{N}
. Note that the loss change
\bm{l}
is upper bounded by the maximal rate
\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}
, which is in turn upper bounded by its least concave majorant
\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}
. Since
\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}
is concave, we can apply Jensen’s inequality to move the majorant outside the integral
\int_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{N}}
to get the desired conclusion. The full proof of Theorem
1
is given in Appendix
A.1
.
\square

of the maximal growth rate of the loss function (Theorem

\bm{l}
to be convex/differentiable/Lipschitz, or the cost function

to be a metric, or the domain
\mathcal{Z}
to be bounded. We illustrate Theorem

in a special case where

Corollary 1

(
p
-dynamic of
\mathcal{R}_{p}
)
.

Take
1\leq p\leq p_{2}\leq\infty
, it is known that
p
-Wasserstein uncertainty set is larger than the
p_{2}
-Wasserstein uncertainty set (Lemma
2
). Consequently,
\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)\geq\mathcal{R}_{p_{2}}(\epsilon)
. Our proposed geometric bounds preserve this ordering as well. That is,

•

\operatorname{lb}_{1}(\epsilon)\geq\operatorname{lb}_{p}(\epsilon)\geq\operatorname{lb}_{p_{2}}(\epsilon)\geq\operatorname{lb}_{\infty}(\epsilon)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},\epsilon)
, and

•

\operatorname{cc}_{1}(\epsilon)\geq\operatorname{cc}_{p}(\epsilon)\geq\operatorname{cc}_{p_{2}}(\epsilon)\geq\operatorname{cc}_{\infty}(\epsilon)\geq\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(\epsilon)
.

Proof.
By Lemma
1
,
\mathcal{S}_{f^{(i)}}(\epsilon^{p})=\sup_{u\geq\epsilon^{p}}\frac{\epsilon^{p}f^{(i)}(u)}{u}=\sup_{t\geq\epsilon}\frac{\epsilon^{p}\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},t)}{t^{p}}
. For any fixed
t\geq\epsilon
, the function
p\mapsto(\frac{\epsilon}{t})^{p}
is non-increasing since
\frac{\epsilon}{t}\leq 1
. Therefore,
\frac{\epsilon\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},t)}{t}\geq\frac{\epsilon^{p}\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},t)}{t^{p}}\geq\frac{\epsilon^{p_{2}}\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},t)}{t^{p_{2}}}
. Taking supremum on
t\in[\epsilon,\infty)
, we have the first conclusion. Next, let
\mathcal{C}_{f}
and
\mathcal{C}_{f_{2}}
be the least concave majorants of
f\colon t\mapsto\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(t^{1/p})
and
f_{2}\colon t\mapsto\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(t^{1/p_{2}})
, respectively. By Lemma
5
and Lemma
6
,
\mathcal{C}_{f}(t^{p/p_{2}})
is concave. In addition,
\mathcal{C}_{f}(t^{p/p_{2}})\geq\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(t^{p/p_{2}\times 1/p})=\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(t^{1/p_{2}})
. Thus,
\mathcal{C}_{f}(t^{p/p_{2}})\geq\mathcal{C}_{f_{2}}(t)
. Choose
t=\epsilon^{p_{2}}
, one has
\mathcal{C}_{f}(\epsilon^{p})\geq\mathcal{C}_{f_{2}}(\epsilon^{p_{2}})
. Therefore,
\operatorname{cc}_{p}\geq\operatorname{cc}_{p_{2}}
. Finally, since
\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}
is non-decreasing,
\operatorname{cc}_{\infty}(\epsilon)=\lim_{t\rightarrow\epsilon^{+}}\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(t)\geq\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(\epsilon)
.
\square

Beyond characterizing the magnitude of the robust gap, our analysis allows us to identify the conditions under which the distributionally robust loss remains finite: either the robustness certificate is finite across the entire domain, or it diverges everywhere. Existing literature typically addresses the finiteness of the robust risk through the lens of strong duality
(
39
,
40
)
or equilibrium theory
(
31
)
.

Corollary 2

(Finiteness of
\mathcal{R}_{p}
)
.

Given
1\leq p<\infty
, then exactly one of the following two cases must occur.

•

\operatorname{lb}_{p}(\epsilon)=\operatorname{cc}_{p}(\epsilon)=\infty
for any
\epsilon>0
.

•

\operatorname{lb}_{p}(\epsilon)<\infty
and
\operatorname{cc}_{p}(\epsilon)<\infty
for any
\epsilon>0
.

Proof.
Suppose that
\operatorname{lb}_{p}(\epsilon)=\infty
for any
\epsilon>0
. Since
\operatorname{lb}_{p}(\epsilon)\leq\operatorname{cc}_{p}(\epsilon)
, it implies that
\operatorname{cc}_{p}(\epsilon)=\infty
for any
\epsilon>0
. Suppose otherwise that
\operatorname{lb}_{p}(\epsilon)=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\mathcal{S}_{f^{(i)}}(\tilde{\epsilon}^{p})<\infty
for some
\tilde{\epsilon}>0
. Since
f^{\max}=\max_{i}f^{(i)}
, this implies
\mathcal{S}_{f^{\max}}(\tilde{\epsilon}^{p})<\infty
. As
\mathcal{S}_{f^{\max}}(t)
is star-shaped,
\frac{\mathcal{S}_{f^{\max}}(t)}{t}
is non-increasing on
(0,\infty)
. Then
\frac{\mathcal{S}_{f^{\max}}(t)}{t}\leq\frac{\mathcal{S}_{f^{\max}}(\tilde{\epsilon}^{p})}{\tilde{\epsilon}^{p}}=\tilde{a}<\infty
and thus
f^{\max}(t)\leq\mathcal{S}_{f^{\max}}(t)\leq\tilde{a}t
for any
t\geq\tilde{\epsilon}^{p}
. Note that
f^{\max}
is non-decreasing on
[0,\infty)
, it follows that
f^{\max}(t)\leq\tilde{a}t+f^{\max}(\tilde{\epsilon}^{p})
for any
t\geq 0
. That is to say
f^{\max}
is upper bounded by an affine (concave) function, thus
\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}(t)<\infty
for any
t\geq 0
.
\square

Corollary
1
and Corollary
2
demonstrate that our proposed bounds not only track how the exponent

(as in the

, we illustrate this transition and demonstrate the superiority of our approach over traditional convexity, differentiability or Lipschitz certificates in the following example.

Suppose that the loss function

3.2
Generalization Bounds and Concave Complexity

In this section, we focus on another topic in statistical learning which aims to understand how models capture patterns in unseen data. Recall that the standard statistical generalization bound (
5
) is given by

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\rm true}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]+c\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L})+C\!(\delta)\!,

where
C(\delta)
is a confidence term,
c
is a constant depending on the boundness of loss
\bm{l}
, and
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L})
is the empirical Rademacher complexity (
4
) of the loss class
\mathcal{L}
. We propose an alternative deterministic version of this bound based on the above concave certificate framework.

Proposition 1

(Deterministic Generalization Bound via Concave Complexity)
.

Given Notation
1
and let
\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(t)
be the maximal rate of
\bm{l}(\cdot;\theta)
. Let
\mathcal{L}:=\{z\mapsto\bm{l}(z;\theta)\mid\theta\in\Theta\}
be the class of loss functions. We define the concave complexity of
\mathcal{L}
as

\operatorname{(CC)}\quad\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon)\triangleq\sup\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{C}_{\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}}(\epsilon),

(11)

where
\mathcal{C}_{\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}}
is the least concave majorant of
\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}
. If
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{P}_{\rm true},\mathbb{P}_{N})\leq\epsilon
for some
p\in[1,\infty]
then

\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\rm true}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]\leq\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon).

(12)

Notably, in the setting of Example
1
, equality holds for
p=\alpha=1
.

This deterministic generalization bound (
12
) complements existing statistical bounds (
5
) by shifting the focus from sample size
N
to the transport budget
\epsilon
. While statistical bounds suggest that more samples lead to tighter results, our deterministic bound shows that higher confidence in the distribution (smaller
\epsilon
) directly tightens the bound. Unlike
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L})
and
C(\delta)
, the proposed bound
\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon)
absorbs the complexity and the confidence term into a single geometric metric.

3.2.1
Calculus and Algebraic Properties

Interestingly, the concave complexity
\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}
satisfies several properties analogous to Rademacher complexity
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}
.

Proposition 2
.

Given
0<\epsilon<\epsilon^{\prime}<\infty
,
a,b>0
and
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
, then the following statements hold.

\operatorname{(a)}

\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon)\leq\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon^{\prime})
and
\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime})\leq\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon)+\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon^{\prime})
.

\operatorname{(b)}

\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon)\leq\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\epsilon)
and
\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(c\cdot\mathcal{L}+b,\epsilon)=c\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon)
.

\operatorname{(c)}

\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{L}),\epsilon)=\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon)
(invariant under convex hull).

Proof.
(a) For any
\theta
, one has that
\mathcal{C}_{\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}}
is concave, non-decreasing (by Lemma
6
) and
\mathcal{C}_{\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}}(t)\geq\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}(0)\geq\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(0)\geq 0
. Thus, it is subadditive. (b) As the maximal rate of
c\cdot\bm{l}+b
is
c\cdot\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}
and
\mathcal{C}_{c\cdot\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}}=c\mathcal{C}_{\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}}
.

(c) Let
\bar{\bm{l}}=\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}\bm{l}_{j}\in\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{L})
where
\sum\alpha_{j}=1,\alpha_{j}\geq 0
. Then the individual rate function of
\bar{\bm{l}}
is given by
\Delta_{\bar{\bm{l}}}(\hat{z},t)=\sup\limits_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\sum\limits_{j}\alpha_{j}(\bm{l}_{j}(z^{\prime})-\bm{l}_{j}(\hat{z}))
. Since
\sup\sum_{j}A_{j}\leq\sum_{j}\sup A_{j}
,
\Delta_{\bar{\bm{l}}}(\hat{z},t)\leq\sum\limits_{j}\alpha_{j}\Delta_{\bm{l}_{j}}(\hat{z},t)
.
By Lemma
1
, for any
\hat{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}
,

\mathcal{C}_{\Delta_{\bar{\bm{l}}}(\hat{z},\cdot)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\sum\limits_{j}\alpha_{j}\Delta_{\bm{l}_{j}}(\hat{z},\cdot)}\leq\sum\limits_{j}\alpha_{j}\mathcal{C}_{\Delta_{\bm{l}_{j}}(\hat{z},\cdot)}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\Delta_{\bm{l}_{j}}^{\max}(\cdot)}.

Therefore,
\mathcal{C}_{\Delta_{\bar{\bm{l}}}^{\max}}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\Delta_{\bm{l}_{j}}^{\max}}
. Take the supremum over all
\bar{\bm{l}}\in\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{L})
, we have
\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{L}),\epsilon)\leq\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon)
. By part (b), one also has
\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{L}),\epsilon)\geq\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon)
.

\square

To this end, we illustrate a contraction lemma, which mirrors the Ledoux-Talagrand Lemma
(
25
)
, allowing for the analysis of composite functions.

Proposition 3

(Contraction Lemma)
.

Let the concave complexity
\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon)
be defined as in (
11
) where
\bm{l}=\ell\circ F_{\theta}
. Let
\mathcal{F}^{\pm}:=\{z\mapsto\pm F_{\theta}(z)\mid\theta\in\Theta\}
. If
\ell\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
\operatorname{Lip}_{\ell}
-Lipschitz then

\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon)\leq\operatorname{Lip}_{\ell}\times\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{F}^{\pm},\epsilon).

(13)

Proof.
If
F_{\theta}(z^{\prime})\geq F_{\theta}(\hat{z})
then
\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta)-\bm{l}(\hat{z};\theta)=\ell\circ F_{\theta}(z^{\prime})-\ell\circ F_{\theta}(\hat{z})\leq\operatorname{Lip}_{\ell}\times(F_{\theta}(z^{\prime})-F_{\theta}(\hat{z}))\leq\operatorname{Lip}_{\ell}\times\Delta_{f_{\theta}}(\hat{z},t)
. Otherwise,
\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta)-\bm{l}(\hat{z};\theta)\leq\operatorname{Lip}_{\ell}\times\Delta_{-f_{\theta}}(\hat{z},t)
. Therefore

\Delta_{\theta}(\hat{z},t)\leq\operatorname{Lip}_{\ell}\times\max\{\Delta_{f_{\theta}}(\hat{z},t),\Delta_{-f_{\theta}}(\hat{z},t)\}.

Taking maximum over all (finite) samples
\hat{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}
, one has
\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(t)\leq\operatorname{Lip}_{\ell}\times\max\{\Delta_{f_{\theta}}^{\max}(t),\Delta_{-f_{\theta}}^{\max}(t)\}
. By Lemma
1
,

\mathcal{C}_{\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}}\leq\operatorname{Lip}_{\ell}\times\max\{\mathcal{C}_{\Delta_{f_{\theta}}^{\max}},\mathcal{C}_{\Delta_{-f_{\theta}}^{\max}}\}.

The proof is completed by taking supremum over all
\theta\in\Theta
.
\square

By applying the contraction lemma, we can prove that linear classifiers possess generalization bounds controlled by the norm of its regressors directly.

Example 2
.

Consider a linear classifier
\bm{l}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\times\{-1,1\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by
\bm{l}(z;\theta)=\ell(y\left\langle\theta,x\right\rangle)
and the cost function
d(z^{\prime},z)=\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|+\infty\left\lvert y^{\prime}-y\right\rvert
. If
\ell
is
\operatorname{Lip}_{\ell}
-Lipschitz then for any
\epsilon\geq 0
,

\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon)\leq\operatorname{Lip}_{\ell}\times\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\left\|\theta\right\|_{*}\times\epsilon.

Specifically, if
\Theta=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\left\|\theta\right\|_{*}\leq c\}
then the concave complexity
\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon)\leq c\epsilon\operatorname{Lip}_{\ell}
remains independent of the dimension
n
and holds even for unbounded or non-differentiable
\bm{l}
.

3.2.2
Adversarial Complexity Gaps

We now extend our framework to compare the standard loss class
\mathcal{L}
with the class of worst-case losses
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\epsilon}=\{\tilde{\bm{l}}_{\epsilon}(\cdot;\theta)\mid\theta\in\Theta\}
where

\tilde{\bm{l}}_{\epsilon}(z;\theta)=\sup_{z^{\prime}:d(z^{\prime},z)\leq\epsilon}\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta).

Recall that the empirical Rademacher Complexity (RC) (
4
) is given by

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L})=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\bm{l}(Z^{(i)};\theta)\right]
and Adversarial Rademacher Complexity (ARC)
(
22
,
38
,
2
,
36
)
is given by (
6
)
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\epsilon})=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\tilde{\bm{l}}_{\epsilon}(Z^{(i)};\theta)\right]
. The gap between ARC and RC eventually measures how much more complex the learning problem becomes under adversarial conditions. Existing analysis often focuses on exploiting the structure of
\mathcal{L}
(such as linearity or boundedness) to estimate this gap. In this work, we propose a novel approach by using the concave complexity tool described above to simplify this calculation. The proof can be found in Appendix
A.2
.

Theorem 2

(ARC-RC Gap)
.

Let
\operatorname{R-Gap}\coloneqq\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\epsilon})-\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L})
, then

\left\lvert\operatorname{R-Gap}\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(\epsilon)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon).

(14)

Intuitively, Theorem
2
suggests that the ARC-RC gap can be reduced to a simple supremum program over univariate functions
\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(\epsilon)
, which is computationally efficient. In the following example, we show that Theorem
2
not only recovers several concrete existing results but also offers a broader view on whether ARC-RC gap is dimension-free. We also present a similar result for multi-layer perceptrons in the later Example
5
.

Example 3

(ARC-RC gap for linear classifier)
.

Under the setting of binary linear classifier Example
2
where
d(z^{\prime},z)=\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|+\infty\left\lvert y^{\prime}-y\right\rvert
, one has

\left\lvert\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\epsilon})-\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L})\right\rvert\leq\frac{\epsilon\operatorname{Lip}_{\ell}}{\sqrt{N}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\left\|\theta\right\|_{*}.

(15)

Specifically, if
\Theta=\Theta_{*}\coloneqq\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\left\|\theta\right\|_{*}\leq c\}
then
\left\lvert\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\epsilon})-\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L})\right\rvert\leq\frac{\epsilon\operatorname{Lip}_{\ell}}{\sqrt{N}}c
, which holds even for unbounded/non-monotone/non-differentiable
\bm{l}
or unbounded
\mathcal{Z}
.

From Example
3
, we recover several existing results without requiring loss
\bm{l}
or domain
\mathcal{Z}
to be bounded.

\operatorname{(a)}

In
38
, authors consider the
\infty
-adversarial attack (i.e.,
d(z^{\prime},z)=\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|_{r=\infty}+\infty\left\lvert y^{\prime}-y\right\rvert
) and show that if
\Theta=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\left\|\theta\right\|_{s}\leq W\}
then
\operatorname{R-Gap}\leq\frac{\epsilon\operatorname{Lip}_{\ell}}{\sqrt{N}}Wn^{1-1/s}
. We can obtain this bound by noting in Example
3
that
\Theta\subseteq\Theta_{*}=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\left\|\theta\right\|_{1}\leq c=Wn^{1-1/s}\}
.

\operatorname{(b)}

In
2
, authors consider arbitrary attack
d(z^{\prime},z)=\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|_{r}+\infty\left\lvert y^{\prime}-y\right\rvert
and show that if
\Theta=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\left\|\theta\right\|_{s}\leq W\}
then
\operatorname{R-Gap}\leq\frac{\epsilon\operatorname{Lip}_{\ell}}{\sqrt{N}}W\max\{n^{1-1/r-1/s},1\}
. We can obtain this bound by noting in Example
3
that
\Theta\subseteq\Theta_{*}=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\left\|\theta\right\|_{1/(1-1/r)}\leq c=W\max\{n^{1-1/r-1/s},1\}\}
. Therefore, ARC-RC gap is dimension-free if
1/r+1/s\geq 1
.

We conclude this section by deriving a similar gap for our proposed concave complexity, where the proof is given in Appendix
A.3
.

Theorem 3

(ACC-CC Gap)
.

Let
\operatorname{C-Gap}\coloneqq\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\epsilon},t)-\hat{\mathfrak{C}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L},t)
, then

\operatorname{C-Gap}\leq\sup_{\theta\in\Theta,z\in\mathcal{Z}}\Delta_{\theta}(z,\epsilon).

(16)

Note that unlike the ARC-RC gap, this ACC-CC gap does not necessarily shrink as
N
increases. This bound introduces a fixed and intrinsic complexity to the loss class, governed solely by the maximum rate of change of the loss function across the entire feature space
\mathcal{Z}
instead of just
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
.

3.3
Adversarial Score for Deep Neural Networks

Despite the theoretical tightness of the concave certificates discussed in Section
3.1
and Section
3.2
, calculating the least concave majorant
\mathcal{C}_{\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}}
exactly for a complex network is often intractable. To bridge the gap between these powerful theoretical bounds and the practical requirements of deep learning, we introduce in this section a relaxation of the concave certificate
\operatorname{cc}_{1}=\mathcal{C}_{\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}}
within Euclidean space. The following assumption is adopted throughout this section.

Assumption 1
.

The data space is given as

\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the space of features and
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the space of labels. The cost function
d
is given by

d(z^{\prime},z)=\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|_{r}+\kappa\left\|y^{\prime}-y\right\|_{1},

where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{r}
is
r
-norm defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
r\in[1,\infty]
and
\kappa\in(0,\infty)\cup\{\infty\}
.

We now define the
adversarial score

\mathcal{A}
as a relaxation of the least concave majorant
\mathcal{C}
, which also allows us certificate vector-to-vector map. Moreover, this definition enables the composite rule for scores established in Lemma
3
.

Definition 5

(adversarial score)
.

Given Assumption
1
and
f\colon\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}}
, then
F
is called an adversarial score of
f
if
F
is
non-decreasing concave
and for any
t\geq 0
,

•

\sup_{x^{\prime},x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{\left\|f(x^{\prime})-f(x)\right\|_{r}\colon\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|_{r}\leq t\right\}\leq F\left(t\right)
if
\tilde{n}>1
, or

•

3.3.2
Robustness Certificates for Classification

Consider a (multi-class) classification problem where the feature space
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the label space is a subset of the standard
m
-simplex
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\Delta^{m}\coloneqq\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid\sum_{j=1}^{m}y_{j}=1,y\geq 0\right\}
. This formulation represents a classification problem with
m
clusters. The optimization goal is to fit a network
f_{\theta}\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
so that the final state
f_{\theta}(x)
(or some intermediate state) can be used to predict the corresponding label
y
. To this end, one often considers the loss function given by

\bm{l}(x,y;\theta)=\left\langle y,f_{\theta}(x)\right\rangle.

(17)

The following proposition shows that the adversarial score of the classification model (
17
) can be calculated directly from the adversarial score
F_{\theta}
of the network
f_{\theta}
as studied in Lemma
3
.

Proposition 4

(Adversarial Score in Classification)
.

Given Assumption
1
where
d(z^{\prime},z)=\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|_{r}+\kappa\left\|y^{\prime}-y\right\|_{1}
and a network
f_{\theta}\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
, define the loss function by
\bm{l}(x,y;\theta)=\left\langle y,f_{\theta}(x)\right\rangle
. Suppose that
F_{\theta}
is an adversarial score of
f_{\theta}
(Definition
5
).

\operatorname{(a)}

If
\kappa=\infty
, then
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(t)\coloneqq F_{\theta}(t)
is an adversarial score of
\bm{l}
.

\operatorname{(b)}

If
\kappa\in(0,\infty)
and there exists
M\in(0,\infty)
such that
\left\|f_{\theta}(x)\right\|_{\infty}\leq M
for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, then
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(t)\coloneqq\sup_{\tau\in[0,t]}\left\{F_{\theta}(t-\tau)+M\kappa^{-1}\tau\right\}
is an adversarial score of
\bm{l}
.

Proposition
4
proves that the classification model with loss
\bm{l}(x,y;\theta)=\left\langle y,f_{\theta}(x)\right\rangle
is always robust with respect to its feature
x
, and robust with respect to its label if the output
f_{\theta}(x)
is bounded by
M
. This covers several practical scenarios:

•

f_{\theta}
is continuous and
\mathcal{X}
is compact (such as images’ pixels or waves’ signals). Then,

.

•

The last layer is the softmax layer. Then,
M=1
.

In the following example, we apply Proposition

Example 5

Given Assumption

3.3.3
Robustness Certificates for Regression

Consider the regression problem where the feature space
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the label space is a subset of the real numbers
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. The regression task aims to fit a network
f_{\theta}\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
so that its output
f_{\theta}(x)
can be used to predict the corresponding label
y
. To this end, one often considers the loss function given by

\bm{l}(x,y;\theta)=\gamma\left(\left\lvert y-f_{\theta}(x)\right\rvert\right),

(20)

where
\gamma\colon[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a given univariate function. Examples of linear regression loss functions (possibly be non-differentiable, non-convex, or non-Lipschitz) are listed in Example

. One can observe that
\Gamma(t)<Lt
in many scenarios. This provides evidence of the competitiveness and versatility of our certificate compared to traditional Lipschitz or differentiability certificates.

(Adversarial Score in Regression)

1
where
d(z^{\prime},z)=\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|_{r}+\kappa\left\|y^{\prime}-y\right\|_{1}

\bm{l}

In summary, we have established explicit methods for calculating the adversarial scores of deep networks by composing the scores of individual layers with those of the final loss function. The versatility of this framework allows for tight and finite robustness certificates even when components are non-Lipschitz or non-differentiable. As shown in Proposition

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a novel framework based on concave certificates to analyze the robustness and complexity of learning models. By deriving certificates from the maximal rate of the loss function, we established a tight bound on the distributionally robust risk that remains applicable to non-Lipschitz and non-differentiable losses. We extended this framework to analyze robust generalization and effectively removed the dimension dependence commonly found in existing statistical results. Furthermore, we introduced the adversarial score as a relaxation to facilitate the layer-wise analysis of deep neural networks. Numerical validations across classification and regression tasks confirm that these certificates are strictly tighter and more informative than traditional Lipschitz or gradient-based approaches, offering a precise tool for certifying performance under distributional shifts.

. Therefore,

\textstyle\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)\geq\eqref{eq:opt-0}\geq\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}_{N}}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},\epsilon).

Case 2.
Suppose that
p\in[1,\infty)
. Then

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{W}_{p}\left(\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\mathbb{P}_{N}\right)&\leq\left(\int_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}}d^{p}(\tilde{z},z)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\pi}(\tilde{z},z)\right)^{1/p}\\
&=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\eta_{i}d^{p}\left(\tilde{Z}^{(i)},Z^{(i)}\right)\right)^{1/p}.\end{array}

Hence the following optimization problem yields a lower bound of
\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)=\sup_{\mathbb{P}:\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}_{N})\leq\epsilon}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]
.

\begin{array}[]{lll}\sup&\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}_{N}}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]+\!\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\eta_{i}\left(\!\bm{l}(\tilde{Z}^{(i)};\theta)-\bm{l}(Z^{(i)};\theta)\!\right)\\
&\text{such that }\eta_{i}\in[0,1],\,\tilde{Z}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{Z},\,i=1,\dots,N,\\
&\text{and }\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\eta_{i}d^{p}\left(\tilde{Z}^{(i)},Z^{(i)}\right)\right)\leq\epsilon^{p}.\end{array}

(23)

Let
t_{i}=\frac{\epsilon}{\eta_{i}^{1/p}}
, or equivalently,
\eta_{i}=\epsilon^{p}/t_{i}^{p}
. Then
t_{i}\in[\epsilon,\infty)
implies
\eta_{i}\in(0,1]
, and
\eqref{eq:opt-1}\geq\eqref{eq:opt-2}
where

\begin{array}[]{lll}\sup&\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}_{N}}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]\!\!+\!\!\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\frac{\epsilon^{p}}{t_{i}^{p}}\left(\!\bm{l}(\tilde{Z}^{(i)};\theta)-\bm{l}(Z^{(i)};\theta)\!\right)\\
&\text{such that }t_{i}\in[\epsilon,\infty),\,\tilde{Z}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{Z},\,i=1,\dots,N\\
&\text{and }\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\frac{\epsilon^{p}}{t_{i}^{p}}d^{p}\left(\tilde{Z}^{(i)},Z^{(i)}\right)\leq\epsilon^{p}.\end{array}

(24)

Let
\rho
be an arbitrary positive scalar. For every
i=1,\dots,N
, by definition of the individual rate
\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},\epsilon)
(
7
), there exists
\tilde{Z}_{\rho}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{Z}
such that
d\left(\tilde{Z}_{\rho}^{(i)},Z^{(i)}\right)\leq t_{i}
and

\bm{l}(\tilde{Z}_{\rho}^{(i)};\theta)-\bm{l}(Z^{(i)};\theta)\geq\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},t_{i})-\rho.

This implies that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\frac{\epsilon^{p}}{t_{i}^{p}}d^{p}\left(\tilde{Z}_{t_{i},\rho}^{(i)},Z^{(i)}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\frac{\epsilon^{p}}{t_{i}^{p}}t_{i}^{p}=\epsilon^{p}
. Therefore,
\left\{t_{i}\in[\epsilon,\infty),\tilde{Z}^{(i)}=\tilde{Z}_{t_{i},\rho}^{(i)}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
is a feasible solution of (
24
) and hence,

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\eqref{eq:opt-2}\\
\geq&\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}_{N}}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]+\!\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\sup\limits_{t_{i}\in[\epsilon,\infty)}\!\left\{\frac{\epsilon^{p}\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},t_{i})}{t_{i}^{p}}-\frac{\epsilon^{p}}{t_{i}^{p}}\rho\right\}\\
\geq&\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}_{N}}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}s^{(i)}(\epsilon)-\rho,\end{array}

where
s^{(i)}(\epsilon)=\sup_{t_{i}\in[\epsilon,\infty)}\left\{\frac{\epsilon^{p}\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},t_{i})}{t_{i}^{p}}\right\}=\sup_{u\in[\epsilon^{p},\infty)}\left\{\frac{\epsilon^{p}\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},u^{1/p})}{u}\right\}
. By Lemma
1
,
s^{(i)}(\epsilon)=\mathcal{S}_{f^{(i)}}(\epsilon^{p})
. This holds true for every
\rho>0
. Therefore,

\textstyle\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)\!\geq\!\eqref{eq:opt-1}\!\geq\!\eqref{eq:opt-2}\!\geq\!\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}_{N}}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]+\!\!\!\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\!\mu_{i}\mathcal{S}_{f^{(i)}}(\!\epsilon^{p}).

Upper Bound (Case 1).
Suppose that
p\in[1,\infty)
.

By the definition of the maximal rate
\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}
in (
8
), we have that

\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z},\hat{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\left\{\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta)-\bm{l}(\hat{z};\theta)\mid d(z^{\prime},\hat{z})\leq t\right\}=\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(t)
for any

. Since
\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}(t)
is the least concave majorant of
\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(t^{1/p})
, we have that
\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(t^{1/p})\leq\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}(t)
and thus

\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z},\hat{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\left\{\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta)-\bm{l}(\hat{z};\theta)\!\mid\!d^{p}(z^{\prime},\hat{z})\leq t\right\}\leq\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}(t).

(25)

This implies that for any
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z},\hat{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}
,

\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta)-\bm{l}(\hat{z};\theta)\leq\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}(d^{p}(z^{\prime},\hat{z})).

(26)

Let
\rho>0
be an arbitrary scalar. For any
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
such that
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\mathbb{P}_{N})\leq\epsilon
, by the definition of
\mathcal{W}_{p}
, there exists
\tilde{\pi}\in\Pi(\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\mathbb{P}_{N})
such that

\textstyle\left(\int_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{N}}d^{p}(\tilde{z},z)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\pi}(\tilde{z},z)\right)^{1/p}\leq\epsilon+\rho.

(27)

Note that
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\bm{l}(\tilde{z};\theta)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(\tilde{z})=\int_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\bm{l}(\tilde{z};\theta)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\pi}(\tilde{z},z)
and

.

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\quad\int\nolimits_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\left(\bm{l}(\tilde{z};\theta)-\bm{l}({z};\theta)\right)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\pi}(\tilde{z},z)\\
&\leq\int\nolimits_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}\left(d^{p}(\tilde{z},z)\right)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\pi}(\tilde{z},z)\\
&\leq\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}\left(\int\nolimits_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{N}}d^{p}(\tilde{z},z)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\pi}(\tilde{z},z)\right)\\
&\leq\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}\left((\epsilon+\rho)^{p}\right),\end{array}

where the first inequality follows from (
26
); the second equality follows from the fact that the least concave majorant
\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}
is concave; the last inequality follows from (
27
) and the fact that
\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}
is non-decreasing (Lemma
6
). This means that for any
\rho>0
and any
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
such that
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\mathbb{P}_{N})\leq\epsilon
, we have shown that
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}[\bm{l}(Z;\theta)]\leq\hat{\mathcal{R}}+\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}\left((\epsilon+\rho)^{p}\right)
. Thus

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{R}_{p}(\epsilon)&\leq\hat{\mathcal{R}}+\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}\left((\epsilon+\rho)^{p}\right).\end{array}

Since
\mathcal{C}_{f^{\max}}
is concave on
(0,\infty)
, it is also continuous of
(0,\infty)
and by letting
\rho\rightarrow 0
, we have the desired conclusion.

Upper Bound (Case 2).
Suppose that
p=\infty
.

Let
\rho>0
be an arbitrary scalar. For any
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})

\mathcal{W}_{\infty}(\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\mathbb{P}_{N})\leq\epsilon

\mathcal{W}_{\infty}

\tilde{a}\leq\epsilon+\rho\text{ and }\tilde{\pi}(\tilde{A})=0.

(28)

where the last inequality follows from (

A.2
Proof of ARC-RC Gap Theorem
2

Proof.

Since
\tilde{\bm{l}}_{\epsilon}(Z^{(i)};\theta)=\bm{l}(Z^{(i)};\theta)+\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},\epsilon)
and
\sup(A+B)\leq\sup A+\sup B
,

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\epsilon})=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[\sup\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\tilde{\bm{l}}(Z^{(i)};\theta)\right)\right]\\
=&\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[\sup\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\left(\bm{l}(Z^{(i)};\theta)+\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},\epsilon)\right)\right)\right]\\
\leq&\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L})+\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[\sup\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},\epsilon)\right)\right]\\
=&\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L})+\Upsilon.\end{array}

Note that the second term
\Upsilon
is the RC of the class of rate functions
Z\mapsto\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},\epsilon)
. In addition, each rate function is dominant by
0\leq\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},\epsilon)\leq\Delta_{\theta}^{max}(\epsilon)
for all
i=1,\dots,N
. As a consequence of the contraction principle in
21
and
25
, we have

\begin{array}[]{c}\Upsilon\leq\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[\sup\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(\epsilon)\right)\right].\end{array}

Using the fact that
\sup cA\leq\left\lvert c\right\rvert\sup A
, one arrives

\begin{array}[]{c}\sup\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(\epsilon)\right)\leq\sup\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\left(\!\frac{1}{N}\Delta_{\theta}^{max}(\epsilon)\!\right)\!\left\lvert\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\right\rvert\!.\end{array}

By Khintchine’s inequality
(
19
)
, one has
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\right\rvert\right]\leq\sqrt{N}
and thus

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\epsilon})\leq\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(\epsilon).

(29)

Similarly, as
\sup(A+B)\geq\sup A+\inf B=\sup A-\sup(-B)
and
\sigma\sim-\sigma
,

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\epsilon})=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[\sup\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\tilde{\bm{l}}(Z^{(i)};\theta)\right)\right]\\
=&\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[\sup\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\left(\bm{l}(Z^{(i)};\theta)+\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},\epsilon)\right)\right)\right]\\
\geq&\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L})-\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[\sup\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}-\sigma_{i}\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},\epsilon)\right)\right]\\
=&\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L})-\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[\sup\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}\Delta_{\theta}(Z^{(i)},\epsilon)\right)\right].\end{array}

Therefore,

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\epsilon})\geq\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{Z}_{N}}(\mathcal{L})-\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\Delta_{\theta}^{\max}(\epsilon).

(30)

From (
29
) and (
30
), we have the desired conclusion.
\square

A.4
Proof of Classification Proposition
4

Proof.
To show that
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
is a adversarial score, we need to verify that
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
is concave and for any
t\geq 0
,

\sup\limits_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z},\hat{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\!\!\!\!\!\left\{\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta)-\bm{l}(\hat{z};\theta)\mid d(z^{\prime},\hat{z})\leq t\right\}\leq\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(t).

Note that
y\in\epsilon^{m}
is a probability vector, thus
\left\|y\right\|_{s}\leq 1
for any
s\in[1,\infty]
.

(a)

Since
\kappa=\infty
,
d(z^{\prime},z)\leq t
if and only if
y^{\prime}=y
and
\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|_{r}\leq t
. In that case, we have
\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta)-\bm{l}(\hat{z};\theta)=\left\langle y,f_{\theta}(x^{\prime})-f_{\theta}(x)\right\rangle\leq\left\|y\right\|_{1/(1-1/r)}\left\|f_{\theta}(x^{\prime})-f_{\theta}(x)\right\|_{r}\leq F_{\theta}(\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|_{r})\leq F_{\theta}(t)
. As
F_{\theta}
is an adversarial score of
f_{\theta}
, it is concave, and therefore
F_{\theta}
is a adversarial score of
\bm{l}
.

(b)

We can decompose the loss difference into two components by
\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta)-\bm{l}(\hat{z};\theta)=\left\langle y^{\prime},f_{\theta}(x^{\prime})-f_{\theta}(x)\right\rangle+\left\langle y^{\prime}-y,f_{\theta}(x)\right\rangle
. For any
z,z^{\prime}
such that
d(z^{\prime},z)\leq t
, it is equivalent to
\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|_{r}\leq t-\kappa\left\|y^{\prime}-y\right\|_{1}=t-\tau
where
\tau\coloneqq\kappa\left\|y^{\prime}-y\right\|_{1}\in[0,t]
. Thus the first component
\left\langle y^{\prime},f_{\theta}(x^{\prime})-f_{\theta}(x)\right\rangle\leq\left\|y^{\prime}\right\|_{1/(1-1/r)}\left\|f_{\theta}(x^{\prime})-f_{\theta}(x)\right\|_{r}\leq F_{\theta}(\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|_{r})\leq F_{\theta}(t-\tau)
and the second component
\left\langle y^{\prime}-y,f_{\theta}(x)\right\rangle\leq\left\|y^{\prime}-y\right\|_{1}\left\|f_{\theta}(x)\right\|_{\infty}\leq M\left\|y^{\prime}-y\right\|_{1}=M\kappa^{-1}\tau
. Hence,

\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta)-\bm{l}(\hat{z};\theta)\leq F_{\theta}(t-\tau)+M\kappa^{-1}\tau.

Therefore,
\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta)-\bm{l}(\hat{z};\theta)\leq\sup_{\tau\in[0,t]}\left\{F_{\theta}(t-\tau)+M\kappa^{-1}\tau\right\}=\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(t)
wherever
d(z^{\prime},z)=\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|_{r}+\kappa\left\|y^{\prime}-y\right\|_{1}\leq t
. Since
F_{\theta}(t)
is concave,
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(t)
is also concave (see Lemma
5
), and
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(t)
is a adversarial score of
\bm{l}
.

\square

A.5
Proof of Regression Proposition
5

Proof.
We need to verify that
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
is concave and for any
t\geq 0
, that is,

\sup\limits_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z},\hat{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{N}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\left\{\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta)-\bm{l}(\hat{z};\theta)\mid d(z^{\prime},\hat{z})\leq t\right\}\leq\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(t).

Let
u^{\prime}={y^{\prime}-f_{\theta}(x^{\prime})}
and
u={y-f_{\theta}(x)}
, then
\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta)-\bm{l}(\hat{z};\theta)=\gamma\left(\left\lvert u^{\prime}\right\rvert\right)-\gamma\left(\left\lvert u\right\rvert\right)
. Since
\Gamma
is an adversarial score of
\gamma
, one has
\gamma\left(\left\lvert u^{\prime}\right\rvert\right)-\gamma\left(\left\lvert u\right\rvert\right)\leq\Gamma(\left\lvert\left\lvert u^{\prime}\right\rvert-\left\lvert u\right\rvert\right\rvert)\leq\Gamma(\left\lvert u^{\prime}-u\right\rvert)
.

(a)

Since
\kappa=\infty
,
d(z^{\prime},z)\leq t
if and only if
y^{\prime}=y
and
\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|_{r}\leq t
. In that case, we have
\left\lvert u^{\prime}-u\right\rvert=\left\lvert f_{\theta}(x^{\prime})-f_{\theta}(x)\right\rvert\leq F_{\theta}(\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|_{r})\leq F_{\theta}(t)
and thus
\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta)-\bm{l}(\hat{z};\theta)\leq\Gamma(\left\lvert u^{\prime}-u\right\rvert)\leq\Gamma(F_{\theta}(t))
. As both
\Gamma
and
F_{\theta}
are non-decreasingly concave,
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}=\Gamma\circ F_{\theta}
is also concave and therefore it is a adversarial score of
\bm{l}
at
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
.

(b)

For any
z,z^{\prime}
such that
d(z^{\prime},z)\leq t
, it is equivalent to
\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|_{r}\leq t-\kappa\left\|y^{\prime}-y\right\|_{1}=t-\tau
where
\tau\coloneqq\kappa\left\|y^{\prime}-y\right\|_{1}\in[0,t]
. Hence,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\left\lvert u^{\prime}-u\right\rvert&\leq\left\lvert f_{\theta}(x^{\prime})-f_{\theta}(x)\right\rvert+\left\lvert y^{\prime}-y\right\rvert\\
&\leq F_{\theta}(t-\tau)+\kappa^{-1}\tau.\end{array}

Therefore,
\bm{l}(z^{\prime};\theta)-\bm{l}(\hat{z};\theta)\leq\Gamma(\left\lvert u^{\prime}-u\right\rvert)\leq\Gamma\left(\sup_{\tau\in[0,t]}\left\{F_{\theta}(t-\tau)+\kappa^{-1}\tau\right\}\right)=\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(t)
wherever
d(z^{\prime},z)=\left\|x^{\prime}-x\right\|_{r}+\kappa\left\|y^{\prime}-y\right\|_{1}\leq t
. By Lemma
5
,
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(t)
is concave and thus

\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(t)
is a adversarial score of
\bm{l}
at
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
.

\square

B.1

Lemma 4

(Three-slope lemma)
.

(
29
)

Let
\Gamma:I\to\mathbb{R}
be a univariate function defined on an interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.
The function
\Gamma
is concave if and only if for any
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\in I
such that
t_{1}<t_{2}<t_{3}
,

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\Gamma(t_{2})-\Gamma(t_{1})}{t_{2}-t_{1}}\geq\frac{\Gamma(t_{3})-\Gamma(t_{1})}{t_{3}-t_{1}}\geq\frac{\Gamma(t_{3})-\Gamma(t_{2})}{t_{3}-t_{2}}.\end{array}

Lemma 5
.

Suppose that
\varphi,\varphi_{2}\colon[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
are non-decreasingly concave.

•

\varphi\circ\varphi_{2}
is also non-decreasingly concave.

•

\phi(t)\coloneqq\sup_{\tau\in[0,t]}\left\{\varphi(t-\tau)+c\tau\right\}
is non-decreasingly concave for any
c>0
.

Proof.
The first item follows
30
. To prove the second item, fix arbitrary
0<t_{1}\leq t_{2}<\infty
and
\eta\in[0,1]
. One has
\phi(t_{1})=\sup_{\tau\in[0,t_{1}]}\left\{\varphi(t_{1}-\tau)+c\tau\right\}\leq\sup_{\tau\in[0,t_{2}]}\left\{\varphi(t_{1}-\tau)+c\tau\right\}\leq\sup_{\tau\in[0,t_{2}]}\left\{\varphi(t_{2}-\tau)+c\tau\right\}=\phi(t_{2})
since
\varphi
is non-decreasing, thus
\phi
is non-decreasing. In addition,

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\eta\phi(t_{1})+(1-\eta)\phi(t_{2})\\
=&\eta\sup_{\tau_{1}\in[0,t_{1}]}\left\{\varphi(t_{1}-\tau_{1})+c\tau_{1}\right\}\\
&+(1-\eta)\sup_{\tau_{2}\in[0,t_{2}]}\left\{\varphi(t_{2}-\tau_{2})+c\tau_{2}\right\}\\
=&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sup\limits_{\tau_{1}\in[0,t_{1}],\tau_{2}\in[0,t_{2}]}\!\!\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\eta\left(\varphi(t_{1}-\tau_{1})+c\tau_{1}\right)\\
+(1-\eta)\left(\varphi(t_{2}-\tau_{2})+c\tau_{2}\right)\end{array}\right\}\\
\leq&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sup\limits_{\tau_{1}\in[0,t_{1}],\tau_{2}\in[0,t_{2}]}\!\!\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\varphi\left(\eta(t_{1}-\tau_{1})+(1-\eta)(t_{2}-\tau_{2})\right)\\
+c\left(\eta\tau_{1}+(1-\eta)\tau_{2}\right)\end{array}\right\}\\
\leq&\sup\limits_{\tau\in[0,\eta t_{1}+(1-\eta)t_{2}]}\left\{\varphi(\eta t_{1}+(1-\eta)t_{2}-\tau)+c\tau\right\}\\
=&\phi(\eta t_{1}+(1-\eta)t_{2}).\end{array}

Here the first inequality follows from the concavity of
\varphi
, and the second inequality follows from letting
\tau=\eta\tau_{1}+(1-\eta)\tau_{2}
. Thus
\phi
is concave.

B.2
Univariate least concave majorant

Lemma 6
.

Suppose that
\gamma\colon[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is non-decreasing. Let
\Gamma
be the least concave majorant (Definition
1
) of
\gamma
. Furthermore, define the rate function

\Delta_{\gamma}(t)\coloneqq\sup_{s\geq 0}\{\gamma(s+t)-\gamma(s)\},

and let
G_{\gamma}
be the least concave majorant of
\Delta_{\gamma}
. Then the following properties hold.

\operatorname{(a)}

The least concave majorant
\Gamma
of
\gamma
is also non-decreasing on
[0,\infty)
.

\operatorname{(b)}

If
\gamma
is
L
-Lipschitz, then
\gamma(t)-\gamma(0)\leq\Delta_{\gamma}(t)\leq G_{\gamma}(t)\leq Lt
for any
t\geq 0
.

\operatorname{(c)}

If
\gamma
is concave, then
\Delta_{\gamma}(t)=G_{\gamma}(t)=\gamma(t)-\gamma(0)
for any
t\geq 0
.

\operatorname{(d)}

If
\Delta_{\gamma}
is concave, then
\Delta_{\gamma}(t)=G_{\gamma}(t)
for any
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

\operatorname{(a)}

Suppose that
\Gamma
is
not
non-decreasing on
[0,\infty)
. That is to say, there exists
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}<\infty
such that
\Gamma(t_{1})>\Gamma(t_{2})
. Since
\Gamma
is concave, by Lemma
4
, for any
t_{3}>t_{2}
one has

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\Gamma(t_{3})-\Gamma(t_{1})}{t_{3}-t_{1}}\leq\frac{\Gamma(t_{2})-\Gamma(t_{1})}{t_{2}-t_{1}}<0.\end{array}

Let
a\coloneqq\frac{\Gamma(t_{2})-\Gamma(t_{1})}{t_{2}-t_{1}}
and
b\coloneqq-at_{1}+\Gamma(t_{1})
. Then
a<0
and
\Gamma(t_{3})\leq at_{3}+b
for any
t_{3}>t_{2}
. This implies that
\lim_{t_{3}\rightarrow+\infty}\Gamma(t_{3})\leq-\infty
, which contradicts the fact that
\Gamma(t)\geq\gamma(t)\geq 0
.

\operatorname{(b)}

The first inequality holds by choosing
s=0
. For any
s,t\geq 0
, we have
\gamma(s+t)-\gamma(s)\leq L|(s+t)-s|=Lt
. Taking the supremum over
s\geq 0
, we obtain the second inequality. By definition of least concave majorant,
\Delta_{\gamma}(t)\leq G_{\gamma}(t)
. Finally, since
h(t)=Lt
is concave and
\Delta_{\gamma}\leq h
, the lowest concave upper bound
G_{\gamma}
must satisfy
G_{\gamma}(t)\leq Lt
.

\operatorname{(c)}

If
\gamma
is concave, the function
s\mapsto\gamma(s+t)-\gamma(s)
is non-increasing in
s
for any fixed
t\geq 0
. Therefore, the supremum defining
\Delta_{\gamma}
is achieved at
s=0
, yielding
\Delta_{\gamma}(t)=\gamma(t)-\gamma(0)
, thus
\Delta_{\gamma}
is also concave and
G_{\gamma}(t)=\Delta_{\gamma}(t)=\gamma(t)-\gamma(0)
.

\operatorname{(d)}

If
\Delta_{\gamma}
is already concave, then it is the least concave majorant of itself.

\square
