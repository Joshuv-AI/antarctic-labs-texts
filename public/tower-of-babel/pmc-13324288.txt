A semantic bit-plane based three-layer encryption framework for secure medical images
Authors: Harshini R, Karthika Veeramani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSecure transmission and storage of medical images is critical as patient information is sensitive and the trend toward digital healthcare, telemedicine and cloud based medical systems is growing rapidly. To address the increased challenges of security and privacy, a novel three layer medical image encryption algorithm is proposed providing a high level of security as well as providing lossless reconstruction for accurate diagnosis when using medical images. This algorithm decomposes the medical image according to bit-planes, into three semantic layers that contain structural, detail, and fine information, making it possible for medical images to be differentiated by level of protection based on the sensitivity of the information contained therein. A separate encryption process is applied for each of the three semantic layers, each using a different cryptographic key created from a single master key generated from an SHA-256 based key derivation process. This will eliminate key exchange overhead and improve the sensitivity of the keys being used. The key dependent substitution, block level diffusion, keystream encryption, and pixel chaining operations used for encrypting the layers provide confusion and diffusion within the system while maintaining the efficiency of computation. The three semantic layers that were previously encrypted are combined and processed with a global plaintext dependent diffusion operation, providing resistance to statistical and differential attacks. The results were evaluated experimentally across several imaging modalities demonstrating strong performance achieving an average entropy of 7.9998 indicating high randomness and uniform pixel distribution, near zero correlation confirming effective removal of spatial relationships between adjacent pixels, an average Number of Pixel Change Rate (NPCR) of 99.61 percent showing the percentage of pixels that change significantly when a single pixel in the original image is modified, and Unified Average Changing Intensity (UACI) of 33.47 percent indicating the average intensity difference between the original and encrypted images, and infinite Peak Signal to Noise Ratio (PSNR) confirming perfect lossless reconstruction.Keywords: Medical image security, Multi-layer encryption, Bit-plane analysis, Cryptographic diffusion, Multimodal medical imagingSubject terms: Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Digital health care and telemedicine have experienced tremendous growth and the use of medical imaging technologies like X-ray, ECG, ultrasound, and MRI in the diagnosis and clinical decision-making processes is becoming widespread1. As such, most of these images contain a lot of private patient information and are typically exchanged over hospital networks, cloud architectures, and remote consultation sites. While the use of digital imaging has improved accessibility and efficiency, it has also increased the concern regarding patient privacy, confidentiality of data, and unauthorized access to data2. Therefore, secure storage and exchange of medical images is now viewed as an imperative requirement in any modern healthcare system3. Traditional encryption techniques that were originally created for text-based data do not effectively secure medical images due to the sheer amount of data involved, the degree of redundancy of the image, and the strong spatial correlation between pixels4. In addition, there is a requirement that medical images used for diagnostic purposes can be losslessly recovered5.

To solve these issues, this paper proposes a new three-layered medical image encryption methodology that utilizes bit-plane decomposition and semantic information separation to encrypt the original image into three distinct layers. Each layer of the encrypted image is separated into coarse, detailed, and fine layers depending on the visual and diagnostic significance of the image. The proposal is to encrypt each layer of the original image separately while applying a unique and independently generated key on each layer. This adaptive design protects critical image structures against unauthorized access or use of their information while enabling accurate reconstruction of these images and is applicable across multiple imaging modalities including X-ray, ECG, ultrasound, and MRI.

Related works

In past research on medical image security, researchers addressed the high redundancy and pixel correlation associated with medical images through both classical cryptography methods and chaos-based methods of encryption. Fan et al. used a combination of DWT and DCT to create a hybrid watermark framework. They successfully improved the watermark’s performance against both signal processing attacks and geometric attacks but at the expense of increased computational costs due to the use of a deep learning feature extractor based on Inception-v36. To develop fast permutation/diffusion encryption for IoMT applications, a 4D memristive hyperchaotic map was created. However, its validation was limited to software simulations7. Other work involved integration of chaos with DNA diffusion and ECC to improve the management of keys and the strength of diffusions, however, the majority of these algorithms were based primarily on spatial domain processing techniques8. Hyperbolic sine systems provided an increased level of controllability to Pseudo-Random Sequences nevertheless, the only evaluation of this system was with grayscale images and basic statistical evaluations9. Combination of bit-plane slicing with both chaotic and hash functions provided improvements in the diffusion of images. However, it also significantly increased the algorithm’s complexity10. Combination of a modified logistic/exponential chaotic map along with multi-level matrix scrambling improved the resistance of the images to differential attacks at the cost of introducing additional multi-stage processing overheads11. Hybrid chaotic -DNA-hash encryption schemes offered very good possibility of utilization within IoT, although they were primarily assessed against gray datasets12. Quantum-chaotic encryption frameworks provided randomization through increasingly chaotic keys, but were limited as they could only rely on simulated quantum key generation to produce keys13. Hyperchaotic schemes, developed using combinations of computational automata and Gold-sequenced S-boxes, provided excellent randomness however, the complexity of these schemes became excessive due to their structural complexity14.

A number of studies attempted to improve accessibility via the application of selective and structural encryption schemes as a means to reduce the computational burden, as well as enhance scalability. Multi-image stack encryptions provided the opportunity to perform multiple encryptions at once against a large medical dataset, however, they severely complicated independent access to specific images within that dataset15. Dual-image encryptions, utilizing dynamic manipulations based on DNA operations, produced a greater level of diffusion within the encryption, however, those operations resulted in a higher overall computational burden16. An encryption scheme to encrypt medical images using 1D inverse cosine chaotic map together with Josephus intra-block scrambling and adaptable diffusion produced a fast execution time with good statistical security17. In this regard, there may be structural dependencies created by the one-dimensional chaotic sequences and limited robustness to randomness. An encryption framework using refined sine-derived 1D chaotic maps as seen in telemedicine and SHA-256 based key generation also could yield high NPCR and UACI18. However, traditional methods of entropy measurements might not fully measure localized random shortfalls which require improvements in security methods for evaluation. Resource-constrained selective encryption schemes offered protection to only the Region of Interest (ROI), while utilizing chaotic maps to create the encrypted image, resulting in a relatively low computational burden, however, this resulted in a greater risk of leaking background structural information19. The combination of zigzag traversal with 2D chaotic diffusion provided a more robust ROI encrypted image, however, this method relied upon the accuracy of the initial extraction of the region20. Multi-level encryptions utilized chaotic processing combined with watermarking to develop a more efficient method of encrypting stereoscopic medical images, although they primarily utilized gray datasets and did not validate in real-time21. Segmentation-based encryption of images, using rubik’s cube-style scrambling and secret sharing, provided greater robustness and protection from copyright infringement, however, it greatly increased the complexity due to the use of an increased number of processing elements22.

While fixed-time synchronization (FTS) of Clifford-valued neural networks can give high-level theoretical security guarantees, they have been validated primarily through numerical simulations23. Neural encryption using FTS has thus far been impractical to implement because of its high computational complexity and absence of true-world validation. Deep learning-based ROI segmentations combined with chaos-based encryption provided good confidentiality and copyright protection, but their dependence on model robustness was a new concern24. Generative adversarial network (GAN) assisted encryption provides a level of unpredictability for the keys by using DCGAN-generated decoy images however, this solution requires pre-trained models and GPU resources25. Deep learning with cloud-based encryption and color-space scrambling allows for both grayscale and color medical images to be encrypted26. End-to-End neural encryption that uses trained generator neural networks enables the elimination of classical cryptographic operations, but it does require significant compute and memory resources27. Despite eliminating traditional cryptographic primitives, neural encryption frameworks demand substantial computational resources, limiting scalability in real-time systems.

Proposed semantic three-layer encryption framework

The paper proposes a semantic-based technique for the encryption of medical images. The strength of the encryption is determined by the inherent value of the image for the purpose of diagnosis. Unlike other techniques that apply uniform encryption to all the pixels in a medical image, the technique proposed in the paper considers the entire medical image as a single entity and divides it into segments based on the semantic value. Then a separate layer-specific encryption technique is employed to encrypt the pixels in the segment. This strategy helps to maintain strong protection of severely diagnostic segments of an image while avoiding the unnecessary computational burden of encrypting those parts that are not as critical within any individual image. The framework is capable of full reversibility and guarantees that the original medical image can be recreated precisely upon decrypting.

Overall framework architecture

The proposed framework follows a structured end-to-end encryption pipeline that converts input medical images into separate secure encrypted images following multiple stages of controlled processing. This process begins with bit-plane decomposition of the grayscale image and is followed by identifying semantic meaning in each bit-plane for classification purposes into one of three layers based on degree of importance. Each semantic layer is independently encrypted using a distinct encryption method and key derived independently from each other. This modular method allows for strong layer separation and encryption strength can be adjusted according to the degree of importance associated with each semantic layer.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, once the encrypted semantic layers are created, they are combined to create a single intermediate encrypted medical image. Global diffusion is used to create strong inter-pixel dependencies throughout the entire image which creates a significant avalanche effect and eliminates statistically meaningful patterns. The final result is an encrypted medical image with high visual randomness and resiliency against statistical, differential or cryptographic attack.

Bit-plane representation of medical images

In a grayscale medical image the intensity of each pixel is represented by an 8-bit number that ranges from 0–255. Bit-plane decomposition is the process of taking this 8-bit number and decomposing it into 8 individual layers or planes of binary data which are the bit planes BP0-BP7. Each plane is a separate layer of binary data corresponding to a different bit position in the 8-bit representation of pixel intensity. BP0 represents the least significant bit from all of the pixels in the image while BP7 represents the most significant bit. Bit plane decomposition separates out the bits of pixel intensity from an 8-bit binary number using bitwise operations that isolate each of the bit positions from all of the pixels in the grayscale medical image.

Each bit-plane contributes differently to the image content, the highest order bit-planes BP6 and BP7 contain much of the structural/anatomical information necessary to identify boundaries of organs and major patterns of intensity that physicians rely upon when diagnosing patients. The midrange bit-planes contain edge and texture data for providing fine detail of the tissues, while the lowest order bit-planes are primarily used to represent noise and minor differences in intensity and contribute very little to the overall visual perception. The foundation of bit-plane decomposition as an effective mechanism for adaptive encryption is due to the non-uniform nature of diagnostic information which exists throughout the various bit-planes as represented in Fig. 2. By treating each plane as an independent entity with respect to its clinical importance, we are able to apply more robust mechanisms of protection to the planes that are structurally significant and apply less intense processing to the planes that are not as clinically significant, leading us to have both a high level of security and computational efficiency overall.

Semantic layer formation

The proposed framework forms semantic layers by grouping bit-planes according to their diagnostic importance and visual contribution. After decomposing an 8-bit grayscale medical image into BP0–BP7, the bit-planes are combined into three layers: a coarse layer composed of bit-planes BP7 and BP6, a detail layer formed from bit-planes BP5–BP3, and a fine layer consisting of bit-planes BP2–BP0. The semantic grouping of bit-planes also has some practical implications in the clinical interpretation of medical images. In a grayscale medical image, the most significant bit-planes tend to hold the major structures in the image and the intensity transitions, which are of prime importance in the clinical interpretation of the image. The coarse semantic layer of the bit-planes primarily contains the structural components of the image, which have a greater contribution to the total energy of the image than the other semantic layers of the bit-planes. The intermediate semantic layer of the bit-planes primarily contains the texture variations and refinements of the structures in the image. The fine semantic layer of the bit-planes captures the small intensity variations in the image. Therefore, the major structural characteristics that the clinician relies on to interpret a medical image are preserved in the more significant bit-planes, even when the lower-level semantic layers are compromised.

A better understanding of this hierarchical classification can be obtained by examining the types of features present in different medical imaging modalities as illustrated in Fig. 3. In X-ray images, the coarse layer contains major skeletal structures and outer bone margins that are used to detect fractures and deformities. The intermediate layer captures soft-tissue gradients and edge transitions between bones and surrounding tissues. The fine layer contains small contrast variations and imaging noise introduced during acquisition. In ultrasound imaging, the coarse layer maintains the overall shape of organs and the borders between organs. The intermediate layer contains speckled images of tissue and the saturation of the contrast found in the two tissue types. The fine layer contains the high frequency noise associated with ultrasound imaging. In MRI imaging, the coarse layer contains large anatomical structures such as the brain and the outer margin of these structures. The intermediate layer contains the differences in texture and the subtle differences in the boundaries of the various tissue types. The fine layer contains the small variations in intensity caused by imaging noise and image production. In ECG images, which represent waveform signals rather than anatomical structures, the coarse layer preserves the dominant waveform morphology including the P-wave, QRS complex, and T-wave. The intermediate layer represents finer waveform variations and slope transitions, while the fine layer contains small fluctuations or noise in the recorded signal.

Key generation and key management

The encryption method proposed in this study uses a deterministic hierarchy for key management based on one master key. As depicted in Fig. 4, a SHA-256 hash function is employed to derive four independent keys from the master key: KL, KL, KL and a global key KG. Using dynamic S-boxes, keystreams and diffusion to generate the coarse level of the image, the key KL enables strong encryption of the coarse layer. The key KL provides moderate encryption of the detail layer using random substitutions and diffusion. Key KL provides lightweight keystream based encryption of the fine layer. Once all three layers are encrypted and merged, a strong inter-pixel relationship across the entire cipher image is established through a final diffusion stage using the global key KG.

SHA-256 based key derivation from a single master key.

To derive layer specific keys, the master key is converted into a fixed-length binary representation before it is hashed using the SHA-256 algorithm. The result of the SHA-256 hashing algorithm is a 256-bit hash value. The resulting hash value is then divided into a series of non-overlapping segments in order to independently create subkeys by assigning different parts of the hash value to keys. The deterministic nature of this process removes the need to send multiple encryption keys, reduces the requirement for storing the keys, and removes correlations between keys used during encryption at various stages. Therefore, each semantic layer is independently cryptographically isolated while still using a simple and secure key management system. The properties of the SHA-256 hash function provide a great extent of key sensitivity and security so that even a one bit change in the master key creates completely different derived keys, thereby isolating encryption stages and preventing cross-layer compromise as well as permitting efficient and practical management of the keys.

Coarse layer encryption

The coarse layer contains most of the clinically significant medical information in an image including major anatomical structures, intensity patterns, and boundaries for making a diagnosis. Therefore, any leakage of information from this layer could expose sensitive information about patients. For this reason, the proposed framework implements the highest level of encryption on this layer. Protecting this layer is particularly important in medical applications because it directly corresponds to the visual structures that clinicians rely on for diagnosis and interpretation. As illustrated in Fig. 5 the encryption process begins by using dynamic AES-based S-box substitution to create non-linearity and eliminate any direct connection between the original pixel value and its encrypted counterpart. Once substituted, the image is divided into small blocks. Then, a unique round key based on the layer key is used to XOR each block to ensure that encryption is not uniform spatially across the image. This block-by-block processing helps disperse the local structure of the image, making it difficult for an attacker to exploit local intensity patterns.

Coarse layer encryption flow.

Multiple diffusion processes are then applied to the image in order to disperse pixel changes throughout the entire coarse layer. Row-wise and column-wise XOR diffusion spread changes from the original location to neighboring pixels horizontally and vertically. Additionally, pixel-chained diffusion propagates changes sequentially throughout the image. Finally, a SHA-256-based pseudorandom keystream is generated from the layer key and XORed with the diffused image to provide an additional layer of randomness and eliminate residual statistical patterns. As a result, even small changes in the plaintext or key lead to significant and unpredictable changes in the encrypted coarse layer. These combined operations ensure that structural information in the coarse layer is thoroughly masked while maintaining reversibility for accurate medical reconstruction. Nonlinearity

Nonlinearity is defined as the minimum Hamming distance between the S-box Boolean functions and all possible affine functions. It is an important criterion in assessing the strength of an S-box against linear cryptanalysis. A high value of non-linearity implies a low probability of an adversary successfully approximating the S-box using linear equations. The theoretical value of non-linearity for an S-box of 8-bit strength is 112, which is also the value of the standard AES S-box. It may be noted that the dynamically generated S-box proposed in the paper also results in a high value of non-linearity equal to 112.

Strict avalanche criterion (SAC)

The Strict Avalanche Criterion assesses the impact of a single-bit flip in the input of the S-box on the output bits of the S-box. Ideally, a single-bit flip in the input should cause a flip in all output bits with a probability of 0.5. This ensures a high degree of uncertainty in the assessment of the impact of a single-bit flip in the input of the S-box on the output bits of the S-box. It may be noted that the proposed S-box results in a high value of Strict Avalanche Criterion equal to 0.5048, which is very close to the ideal value 0.5.

Bit independence criterion (BIC) nonlinearity

The Bit Independence Criterion is a test that determines if two output bits change independently if changes are made to input bits. The BIC Nonlinearity test calculates the nonlinearity of Boolean functions that are created by taking a combination of two output bits. Higher BIC nonlinearity values indicate a better ability to resist both differential and linear cryptanalysis. The ideal nonlinearity value for an optimal 8-bit S-box is 112. The result shows that the nonlinearity is 110.6, which is a very good result for bit independence.

Bit independence criterion (BIC) – SAC

BIC–SAC is a test that determines if two output bits change independently if a single input bit is changed. The ideal result should be close to 0.5. The result shows that the output is acceptable for use as a secure substitution box since it has acceptable independence characteristics.

Linear approximation probability (LAP)

Linear Approximation Probability is a test that determines the highest probability that a linear relationship exists between input bits and output bits of an S-box. The result shows that a lower value is desirable since it is more difficult to linearly approximate an S-box that has a lower value. For an 8-bit S-box, the optimal LAP value is 0.0625, which is also achieved by the AES S-box. The proposed S-box achieves this optimal value, indicating strong resistance to linear attacks.

Differential approximation probability (DAP)

Differential Approximation Probability represents the maximum probability that a specific input difference results in a particular output difference. Lower DAP values imply stronger resistance to differential cryptanalysis, which exploits predictable input-output differences. The ideal value for an 8-bit S-box is 0.015625. The proposed dynamic S-box achieves this optimal value, indicating strong security against differential attacks.

Detail layer encryption

The detail layer generates intermediate diagnostics, such as texture patterns, edge data, and the varying anatomical features of tissues, which offers visual clarity for diagnosis, but is less important than structural data in the coarse layer. While leaks from this layer don’t show most of the larger anatomical structures, they do provide useful contextual data to an attacker. Therefore, the proposed framework uses a moderate level of encryption to achieve a security and efficiency balance. As seen in Algorithm 2, the encryption process begins with a key-dependent random substitution of pixels via an S-box and introduces non-linearity to prevent direct relationships between original and substituted pixel values. After this, the substituted image is sectioned into blocks, and each block is XORed by a key derived from the layer key to provide a higher level of spatial variability in the encryption process, as well as disrupting local texture patterns.

Detail Layer Encryption.

After substitution and block processing, pixel diffusion operations occur to distribute overall pixel changes within a layer. Row-wise XOR diffusion spreads intensity changes horizontally across adjacent pixels, while circular column shifting redistributes pixel positional data vertically. Both methods serve to disrupt residual structural continuity amongst the original pixels. Collectively, these methods reduce statistical correlations and mask texture information but don’t produce significant computational overhead. The final encrypted output of this process is the protected detail layer, which preserves reversibility while ensuring adequate privacy protection for intermediate diagnostic features. Fine layer encryption

The fine layer of an image is made up of the lower order bit-planes that represent mainly small changes in intensity variations and noise in the medical images. Although difficult to see visually and having little value for interpreting an image diagnostically, not protecting these bit-planes can reveal low-level pixel data and statistical properties that are useful for an attacker when trying to recreate the image. The proposed system utilizes a lightweight encryption method as shown in Fig. 7 to protect the data while minimizing computational cost.

Fine layer encryption flow.

Complex substitution and block diffusion operations are not required for this layer due to its limited diagnostic significance. Therefore, the fine layer is encrypted using a pseudorandom keystream, created using SHA-256, and XORed directly to the fine layer pixels. The use of this method results in sufficient randomness in the images to eliminate the residual statistical patterns in them, preventing the leakage of low-level intensity information. This lightweight design ensures that the computational overhead of encrypting the fine layer remains low while still maintaining adequate protection of all pixel information. Using XOR-based keystream encryption is a fast method of encrypting pixel data and is easily reversible, thus providing an adequate level of security relative to the cost of processing the fine layer. Layer recombination and global diffusion

After individual encryption, the coarse, detail, and fine layers are recombined to form a single intermediate cipher image. A reversible XOR operation combines the three layers and guarantees that all layers have contributed equally to the encrypted result while preserving the ability to perfectly reconstruct each layer during decryption. By merging the layers in this manner, the structural, textural, and fine intensity information becomes interdependent, preventing an attacker from being able to separate or analyze the individual semantic parts of an image. Although XOR operations are used in the diffusion and recombination stages due to their computational efficiency, the overall encryption process is not purely linear. Each semantic layer goes through a nonlinear substitution phase prior to recombination using a dynamically generated AES-based S-box, as well as through both block-level transformations and key-dependent diffusion operations. These nonlinear components, introduce confusion to the encryption pipeline, which prevents the entire encryption process from being interpreted as a basic linear XOR transformation. Even if an adversary obtains plaintext–ciphertext pairs, the proposed encryption framework maintains strong resistance to known-plaintext attacks, making it highly difficult to derive linear relationships despite the use of XOR-based diffusion operations.

Following recombination, a global diffusion process is applied after the individual layers have been recombined to enhance security. This process distributes modification to each pixel throughout the entire cipher image. Each pixel is modified sequentially according to its previous pixel value and key KG, and creates an extremely strong association between pixels when viewed as a cipher image. This process also ensures that a small change to the image or key creates a large change in the output image, making it difficult for an attacker to perform statistical, differential, or chosen plaintext attacks. Since the operations carried out during the diffusion process are simple, the processing costs are low and increase proportionally with the resolution of the images. Therefore, the chances of layer cancellation and other types of attacks exploiting XOR recombination, are extremely low. Decryption process

The decryption process is structured to be the exact inverse of the encryption framework. Its purpose is to completely recover the original unmodified medical image with no form of data loss. This stage begins with the application of inverse global diffusion to the final encrypted image using the global key KG. Since the global diffusion stage during encryption creates strong inter-pixel dependency through the sequential modification of every pixel as a function of its neighboring pixel values using the global key, it is necessary to reverse this stage to eliminate the chain links between all pixels in order to obtain an intermediate image where there is no longer any relationship between individual pixel values. Once the intermediate image no longer exhibits the effect of diffusion, it can be separated back into the three encrypted semantic layers through inverse XOR recombination. This process accurately retrieves the coarse, detail, and fine encrypted layers from the intermediate image without any data loss.

Each of the three semantic layers is decrypted independently by applying the inverse of all operations performed during the respective semantic layer’s encryption stage, but in reverse order. Therefore, the decryption of the coarse layer begins with the use of the layer key to perform inverse keystream XOR, and then continues by reversing pixel-chained diffusion, followed by row-wise diffusion, and finally reversing the block-level XOR operations. Inverse dynamic S-box substitution is then performed. The detail layer is reconstructed by reversing the block-wise XOR operations performed on it, restoring the original values.

Decryption Process.

Directional diffusion processes are also reversed, along with the random S-box substitutions applied during encryption. The lightweight keystream-based encryption implemented for the fine layer allows simple decryption through the application of inverse keystream XOR using the corresponding layer key. After successfully decrypting all three semantic layers, they are recombined to reconstruct the original medical image. Inverse operations are used throughout the framework, and hence the decryption is lossless, meaning that it is capable of producing the original image without any distortion or loss of information. Lossless decryption is very important in medical images, as any changes in pixel value may affect diagnosis. The entire description of the decryption method that is used is outlined in Algorithm 5.

Security analysis

In addition to statistical evaluation metrics like NPCR, UACI, entropy, and correlation coefficient, it is necessary to evaluate the proposed encryption framework’s cryptographic strength. A secure image encryption algorithm should have a sufficiently large key space to provide resistance to brute-force attacks and high key sensitivity to guarantee that even a small change in the secret key will result in a totally different ciphertext. Therefore, two significant security characteristics of the proposed scheme are analyzed in this section: the effective key space and the sensitivity of the encryption process to small variations from a master key.

Key space analysis

An image encryption system requires a sufficiently large key space to make standard attacks, such as brute-force attacks, computationally infeasible. The key space represents the total number of possible secret keys that can be used for encryption or decryption. In the proposed encryption scheme, the secret key mainly consists of the master key, which is expanded using a SHA-256 based key derivation function (KDF) to generate 256-bit layer-specific keys used in the encryption process. Since the cryptographic strength of the system is determined by the 256-bit output of the KDF, the total key space can be expressed as possible keys. This key space is substantially larger than the commonly accepted minimum security level of for most modern cryptography systems. In order to put this key space into a better perspective, if an attacker is able to test keys per second, which is beyond the capabilities of most supercomputers, it is clear that it would take approximately years to test the entire key space. It is thus evident that the proposed encryption framework has a very large key space and is highly resistant to brute-force attacks.

Key sensitivity analysis

A robust encryption algorithm must be highly key sensitive, meaning a small alteration in the secret key must produce a completely different ciphertext. This was examined by conducting two independent experiments in encryption, where two master keys, very similar in nature, were used. In the first experiment, the original master key “PatientID_StudyID_PrivateSecret” was used to derive a new key using a key derivation function based on the SHA-256 hash algorithm. This new derived key was denoted by Key1. A plain image was encrypted using the new derived key, and a ciphertext image C1 was produced. In the second experiment, the original master key was first converted to hexadecimal and a single bit was flipped to produce a new master key. This slight alteration in the master key produced a new derived key, denoted by Key2. This new derived key was used to encrypt the same plain image, producing a second ciphertext image C2. Due to the high sensitivity and robustness of the SHA-256 based key derivation function and the multi-layered encryption framework, a small alteration in the master key produced completely different encryption parameters and ciphertext images. As a result, C2 is completely different from C1, thus validating the avalanche effect of the proposed scheme. When both encrypted images are decrypted using the original key Key1, only correct key decryption results in a correct reconstruction of the original image; otherwise, as shown in Fig. 9, decryption using a wrong key fails to retrieve meaningful information from the images.

Since the diffusion stage of the encryption creates a sequential dependency between pixels, when a localized pixel is corrupted in the cipher image, the disturbance propagates to neighboring pixels during the inverse diffusion process, producing distributed intensity variations in the decrypted image. Conclusion and future work

The proposed approach presents a novel three-layered encryption framework that uses semantic awareness to ensure that images are securely and efficiently protected based on their importance for making diagnoses. The proposed method divides an image into separate bit-plane semantic layers and uses a unique encryption method and independent cryptographic key for each layer, to offer layers with coarse, detail, and fine information protected according to importance while still providing for exact reversibility. The use of dynamic substitution, multi-stage diffusion, and plaintext-dependent global diffusion to provide additional randomness adds further strength to the confusion and diffusion properties of the encryption methods used and enhances the resistance to the statistical and differential attack. To validate the performance of the proposed framework via multiple experimental evaluations, different types of medical imaging data were evaluated, including images from X-ray, ultrasound, MRI, and ECG modalities. The empirical results demonstrate that the proposed framework provides strong performance at all four of the expected cryptographic performance benchmarks, information entropy values near 8, negligible correlation coefficients approaching 0, high NPCR and UACI values near their theoretical limits, and perfect reconstruction with lossless decryption. These results demonstrate that the proposed framework has excellent scalability as well as independence from imaging modality and practical applicability for securely storing and transmitting sensitive medical imaging data in the next generation telemedicine systems and cloud-based healthcare applications.

Future work will aim to extend the proposed framework’s capabilities beyond grayscale medical images and also add more types of medical images including digital pathology slides and dermatological images typically represented as color images. The proposed framework can be extended to such images by applying the semantic bit-plane decomposition and layer-wise encryption process independently to each color channel or by operating in alternative color spaces. Future work will also explore incorporating compression techniques into the framework so that the framework can meet DICOM requirements; because healthcare providers must use compression when they store and transmit large amounts of medical image files so they do not overwhelm their storage systems and reduce the speed and efficiency of the healthcare provider. Since the proposed encryption framework operates on pixel intensity values, the encryption stage can be incorporated either after decompression or within secure storage pipelines. Future research will investigate compression-aware encryption strategies and direct integration with DICOM-based medical imaging systems to further improve the practicality of the framework in real clinical deployments.
