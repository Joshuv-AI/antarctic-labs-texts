Concurrent validity and test reliability of the deep learning markerless motion capture system during the overhead squat
Authors: Kyungun Bae, Seyun Lee, Se-Young Bak, Hyo Sang Kim, Yuncheol Ha, Joshua H. You
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Marker-based optical motion capture systems have been used as a cardinal vehicle to probe and understand the underpinning mechanism of human posture and movement, but it is time-consuming for complex and delicate data acquisition and analysis, labor-intensive with highly trained operators. To mitigate such inherent issues, we developed an accurate and usable (5-min data collection and processing) deep-learning-based 3-Dimensional markerless motion capture system called “Ergo”, designed for use in ecological digital healthcare environments. We investigated the concurrent validity and the test–retest reliability of the Ergo system measurement’s whole body joint kinematics (time series joint angles and peak joint angles) data by comparing it with a standard marker-based motion capture system recorded during an overhead squat movement. The Ergo system demonstrated excellent agreement for time series joint angles (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{2}$$\end{document}R2 = 0.88–0.99) and for peak joint angles (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ICC_{2,1}$$\end{document}ICC2,1 = 0.75–1.0) when compared with the gold standard marker-based motion capture system. Additionally, we observed high test-retest reliability (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ICC_{3,1}$$\end{document}ICC3,1 = 0.92–0.99). In conclusion, the deep learning-based markerless Ergo motion capture system considerably shows comparable performance with the Gold Standard marker-based motion capture system measurements in the concurrent accuracy, reliability, thereby making it a highly accessible choice for diverse universal users and ecological industries or environments.

Introduction

Precise quantitative assessment of posture and movement holds pivotal significance in establishing ergonomic or sports prevention, clinical diagnosis, prognosis, and intervention strategies for a myriad of musculoskeletal and neurological conditions such as industry or sports-related injuries, strokes, parkinsonism, and cerebral palsy, respectively1–7. Conventional posture and movement assessment have primarily encompassed clinical8,9 and instrumentation-based measures10,11. The former predominantly captures a crude and visual assessment of the activity domain of abnormal static posture and dynamic movement including squatting, lifting, throwing, and walking regardless of the extent of impairments. Nonetheless, even standardized clinical measures may have inherent limitations associated with the ceiling effect and subjective interpretations, potentially jeopardizing internal validity and reliability within and among the experimental tester(s)12. The latter marker-based motion capture (MoCap) systems provide considerably noteworthy results in the biomechanical impairment domain of posture and movement control as defined in the WHO’s International Classification of Functioning (ICF) model for accurate clinical decision-making13.

Contemporary marker-based MoCap systems including 3D Qualisys (Qualisys, Gothenburg, Sweden) or the Vicon system (Vicon, Oxford, UK) using infrared camera have been widely utilized to assess normal and pathological posture and movement14–16. The marker-based MoCap system is considered a gold standard measure, but it poses inherent issues with movement artifacts17, marker confusion, occlusion error due to the interposition of opaque objects between the marker and the cameras, or markers falling outside the camera’s field of view18. Moreover, a higher level of operational skills, time-consuming process such as calibration, proficiency in marker attachment, post-processing for missing markers and errors, as well as patient cooperation are constraints in the current marker-based MoCap systems19,20.

In contrast, the markerless Kinect system (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA) using RGB depth camera is a promising new technology, demonstrating excellent usability and gaining widespread acceptance for alternative motion analysis for gross movement21–23. However, it has been challenged for clinical use due to questionable accuracy stemming from self-occlusion-whereby only the nearest depth value is reported by the RGB depth camera sensor, ignoring hidden body parts24. Additionally, there are limitations of environmental factors such as a camera distance, lighting, and clothing, as depth images are acquired via infrared light, making them less reliable in broader testing environments and sunlit conditions25,26. Correspondingly, a clear need exists to devise an accurate, reliable, and usable system with easier learning and faster data collection and processing.

To mitigate the shortcomings of the current systems, we have developed the accurate and reliable 3D markerless MoCap system (“Ergo” NAVER Inc., Seongnam, Republic of Korea) using the deep-learning (DL) algorithms. Specifically, we have used advanced deep learning algorithms including YoloX27, HRNet28 with DARK29, TCFormer30 models and algebraic triangulation31 to accurately and automatically extract 3-dimensional joint kinematics from captured human motion images. As such, it renders more accurate and reliable kinematic data with resilient to any signal drops and strong generalization in environment as well as greater performance efficiency from faster and simpler data acquisition and process time than the gold standard marker-based systems31,32.

Nevertheless, the validity and test–retest reliability of the DL-based 3D Ergo MoCap system remain unknown. Therefore, the research question of the study was whether a DL-based markerless motion-capture system would demonstrate comparable validity and reliability to the gold-standard marker-based system during the ecological overhead squat movement test33 in non-symptomatic adults. The specific purpose of the study was to establish the acceptable validity and reliability of the DL-based 3D markerless Ergo MoCap system for the precise quantitative assessment of posture and movement by analyzing time-series joint angle and peak joint angle data of the shoulder, hip, and knee joints compared to the gold standard kinematic measurements. In general practice and application, our Ergo system can be used across multiple fields, such as clinical settings, rehabilitation, sports biomechanics, and ergonomics, to improve patient care, enhance athletic performance, or create safer workplaces. Its ability to provide real-time feedback, customize interventions, and track progress across various environments makes it an invaluable resource in these fields.

Methods

Study design

The present study was a cross-sectional experimental design, which was a part of ongoing research and development projects. Data screening and completeness were conducted by two investigators (K.B, S.L).

Participants

A total of 71 participants (mean age 34.2 ± 4.2 years, 25 females, 46 males) out of 4189 employees (1942 females) at NAVER Corporation, one of the ten biggest businesses in South Korea, volunteered. The participants were selected based on the following specific criteria. Inclusion criteria entailed the ability to accurately perform overhead squats three consecutive times, males and females aged 18–65 with no prior history of cardiovascular or neuromusculoskeletal disease. Exclusion criteria involved any history of medical or health conditions that could influence the overhead squat, as well as visual, cognitive, and vestibular balance disorders. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yonsei University. The participants provided an informed consent to participate in this study prior to participation. Informed consent obtained to publish the information/image(s) in an online open-access publication.

Movement task

The experimental procedure was consistently implemented to validate the Ergo system throughout the entire process of the overhead squat, a common workplace activity involving tasks like lifting and carrying heavy objects and a clinical injury assessment tool that evaluates all angles of the human body, including both upper and lower extremities33,34. Participants were asked to successfully perform three trials of overhead squats after practice trials. Each trial was separated by 15 min of passive rest. For the overhead squat, subjects adopted a standardized feet position35 with a 120 cm wooden dowel raising it overhead and executing a squat to the maximum range. Specifically, the marker-based Qualisys system was used to determine the validity of the Ergo system by means of comparing the bilateral shoulder, hip, and knee joint angular data during the overhead squat. The data were simultaneously obtained from both measurement systems.

Hardware setup

For the “gold standard” marker-based Qualisys system, eight-infrared cameras (MIQUS M3, Qualisys, Gothenburg, Sweden) were used to determine the joint kinematics. The participant was required to wear tight-fitting clothes and 36 reflective markers according to the modified Helen Hayes marker set protocol36. The reflexive markers (Fig. 1a) were applied by a trained investigator on the following anatomical landmarks: the clavicle-acromion joint, humerus lateral epicondyle, radius styloid process, anterior superior iliac spine, greater trochanter, lateral side of the knee joint center, superior surface of the patella, tibial tuberosity, lateral malleolus, heel and between the distal ends of the 2nd and 3rd metatarsals of each leg. Prior to the data acquisition, the calibration of the eight-infrared cameras was successfully completed with a mean residual error of < 0.2 mm37. The kinematic data were acquired at a sampling rate of 100 Hz and the collected raw data were processed with labeling by Qualisys Track Manager (v2019.3, Qualysis, Gothenburg, Sweden) and exported to Visual 3D (v6, C-Motion Inc, Germantown, USA).

For the DL-based 3D markerless Ergo MoCap system, eighteen synchronized RGB video cameras (FLIR BFS-PGE-31S4C) were attached on two slave computers controlled by one master computer. The RGB images can be obtained at a frequency of up to 30 Hz. Initially, object detection involved the process of detecting a specific object in an image with the YoloX27 model, which was purported to configure and detect movement images associated with human activities and the estimation of crowds through human bounding boxes on each slave computer. The object tracking was implemented to maintain temporal consistency of detected bounding boxes using the customized algorithm to maximize Intersection over Union (IoU) values by comparing the bounding box candidates of the current frame with the results of the previous frame. 2D human pose estimation was processed on the cropped images using the detected bounding boxes, 2D human positions were extracted using HRNet28 with DARK29 and TCFormer30 models. These models have been trained on a dataset comprising 200,000 images with 250,000 annotated data38. Accurate data aggregation created by neural network and data classification processed by distributed slave computers connected to vision cameras without data transfer time on the master computer. Additionally, the 2D positions are not only compact but also effective compared to image features. So this helps reduce to data execution time for data transfer and computing 3D position. After that, aggregated 2D positions on the master computer are used to calculate 3D positions. 3D pose estimation was processed by algebraic triangulation31 which took little computing cost and it shows similar results compared to the learnable triangulation method. Finally, kinematics joint angle data and outputs were rendered as the 3D joint positions were converted to 3D joint angles based on the definitions provided in Table 2. These joint angles serve as measures for maximum and minimum joint angles, as well as the range-of-motion (ROM) which were provided as example videos in the supplementary material. The outputs of our system consisted of 133 2D and 3D joint positions per frame, adhering to the COCO-Wholebody38 dataset format. These key points comprise body, hands, and face, with 23, 21, and 68 key points, respectively. Body key points consist of the nose, eyes, ears, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles, toes, and heels, as shown in Fig. 1b. The Ergo system demonstrated a mean time of 5 min for data processing without the need for marker layout and skeletal solving39.

Data analysis

We transformed the 3D joint rotation data from the Qualisys coordinate frame to the same reference coordinate frame in the Ergo system software to synchronize the kinematic data from both systems. The coordinate frame was synchronously aligned and established based on the International Society of Biomechanics (ISB) standards: Y is vertical, X is perpendicular to Y, and Z is orthogonal to Y and X40,41. The joint angles from the Ergo systems were first upsampled from 30 to 100 Hz and then the joint angles between the two systems were synchronized using a cross-correlation-based shift synchronization technique. This technique allows for the synchronization of signals without subjective assessment42. A fourth order, zero phase shift, lowpass Butterworth filter with a cut-off frequency of 20 Hz was used43. Angular kinematic data included the “Time-series joint angle” and “Peak joint angle” recorded during the overhead squat trials stored for further statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical data are expressed as means and standard deviations. Note that first, the large COCO 2017 dataset was used for training the deep learning algorithms. Second, 71 participants were initially recruited as test data for the deep learning algorithms. Third, 31 participants out of 71 participants were randomly selected by a computer random number generation method as independent test dataset to improve the quality of research design for validation of the Ergo system. Therefore, there is no issue with regard to the validation process. the concurrent validity and reliability data were analyzed using 178,200 image data recorded from randomly selected 31 participants out of 71 participants, whereas we used the large dataset of COCO 2017 for the training data, which did not contribute to the overfitting to evaluation environment. The concurrent validity of the Ergo system for time-series joint angle was computed by linear regression analysis and the root mean squared error (RMSE)43. The concurrent validity of the peak joint angle was determined by the intraclass correlation coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ICC_{2,1}$$\end{document}ICC2,1)44. Additionally, Bland–Altman analysis was used to determine the agreement between the two system’s values with the potential bias and variability within 95% limits of agreement (LoA) level. The test–retest reliability of the two trial measurements obtained from the Qualisys and Ergo systems was determined by using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ICC_{3,1}$$\end{document}ICC3,1, and ratio coefficient of variation (CV), respectively23. ICC was performed using SPSS 23.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). CV, RMSE, and linear regression were analyzed using Python, and Bland–Altman analysis was performed using MedCalc software (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium). Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The present study established that the concurrent validity and reliability of the Ergo system were excellent, demonstrating that the DL-based Ergo kinematics measurement achieved outstanding results on the overhead squat movement when compared with the gold standard marker-based Qualisys system, supporting that our Ergo system provides excellent accuracy and consistency during an ecological overhead squat movement.

Concurrent validity data for the time-series joint angles between the Ergo and Qualisys systems revealed relatively excellent correlations, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{2}$$\end{document}R2 = 0.88–0.99 and close kinematic time series trajectories (RMSE = 2.33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document}∘–6.25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document}∘), corroborating the kinematic measurement accuracy. This finding was consistent with earlier markerless MoCap validity studies in the time series joint angular data acquired from boxing45, squat46, gait47,48 and throwing48 movement tasks. Lahkar et al.45 examined the concurrent validity of a DL 3D markerless system in shoulder-elbow-wrist joint angular data during boxing and reported poor to good accuracy, ranging from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{2}$$\end{document}R2 = 0.27 to 0.99. Similarly, Musha et al.46 showed relatively high concurrent validity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{2}$$\end{document}R2 = 0.86–0.94) in hip-knee-ankle joint angle data obtained from the DL based MoCap measurement when compared with the VICON system during of the squat test. Additional RMSEs confirmed shoulder, hip, and knee time series joint angles (RMSE = 5 to 5.5 degrees) during gait analysis recorded from the multiple vision-based DL 3D MoCap system47, which was compatible with our Ergo system. Nakano et al.48 demonstrated unavoidable inherent noise-associated errors of time serial joint angles in the vision-based DL 3D MoCap with the application of the supervised DL model from manually labeled data recorded from gait and throwing tasks. To resolve such inherent shortcomings, we applied the robust confidence levels of 2D and 3D key points during the aggregation of 2D multi-view features for 3D key points. For example, the 3D key points are achieved by triangulating 2D predictions in each camera view, where 2D key points that fell within the scope of the low confidence level were automatically discarded and processed through a weighted sum method31.

As with the time-series joint angle data, our concurrent validity data for the peak joint angles between the Ergo and Qualisys system rendered excellent agreement (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ICC_{2,1}$$\end{document}ICC2,1 = 0.76–1.0). Lee et al.49 reported a excellent accuracy of a DL-based markerless MoCap using “OpenPose” system in the peak joint angle in the hip flexion (ICC = 0.90) and in the knee joint (ICC = 0.93) when compared with the gold standard marker-based kinematic measurement of the squat movement. We confirmed a relatively much high agreement in the hip flexion (ICC = 0.99) and the knee joint(ICC = 1.0) than that of the “OpenPose” system49. Such considerable outstanding results may arise from the fact that While OpenPose’s 2D model, with its low-resolution outputs, results in imprecise keypoint estimates, we utilized a more precise 2D model, HRNet with DARK, designed to preserve low-level features that provide detailed information about objects, making it more suitable and effective region guidance for object localization38. This model achieved a mean average precision (mAP) of 0.661 on COCO WholeBody benchmarks, surpassing OpenPose’s 0.563 mAP38. As for 3D pose estimation, our multi-view approach, employing algebraic triangulation as a baseline, exhibited a mean per joint position error (MPJPE) of 19.2 mm on Human 3.6 M benchmarks. In fact, our Ergo system showed a superior accuracy of 15.78% compared to RANSAC utilized by OpenPose50, which registered a 22.8 mm MPJPE31,51, thereby rendering a more accurate estimate than the OpenPose model.

Bland-Altman analysis showed that the mean systematic bias was below 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document}∘ for most joint angles with the exception of the shoulder flexion (LoA < ± 10.71\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document}∘) during overhead squat. The Bland–Altman plots reveal significant deviations in shoulder joint angles compared to other tested joints, suggesting that the shoulder is more prone to discrepancies between the marker-based and markerless motion capture systems. There are several potential reasons for these observed differences. First, dynamic shoulder positioning between the marker-based and markerless systems may result in discrepancies, especially for joints with more complex movements like the shoulder52. During this overhead squat task, the shoulder moves a large range of flexion motion (165\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document}∘), often combined with abduction or external rotation, accounting for such discrepancies between systems. Second, soft tissue movement between reflective markers, called soft tissue artifact (STA), induces differences. The long axis of a limb segment, such as the shoulder, is especially affected53. In occupational biomechanics, previous studies have indicated that a lower or upper LoA of 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ }$$\end{document}∘ is generally considered acceptable for most practical applications54,55. Therefore, while the shoulder joint deviations indicate some limitations in the system’s precision, the overall error margin remains within a range deemed acceptable for applied biomechanics, particularly in occupational settings.

Test-retest reliability data for the peak joint angles between the Ergo measurements produced excellent relative agreement (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ICC_{3,1}$$\end{document}ICC3,1 = 0.92–0.99) and low coefficient of variation (CV < 10%), which was comparable or better than other commercialized Kinect-based markerless systems reported in the current literature56. For example, the ICC values of upper limb motions recorded from the Kinetic system ranged from 0.86 to 0.99, and CV values (< 18%) during catching movement56. It is conceivable that such excellent consistency in joint angle measurements has stemmed from the fact that our DL-based markerless Ergo system is capable of predicting occluded landmarks based on learned spatial configurations of adjacent joints, such as estimating hand positions from visible shoulder and elbow joints enabling robust generalization even in challenging testing conditions32. During the 3D joint pose estimation process, some joint keypoints may be occluded in certain views, resulting in low confidence due to insufficient visual cues for accurate estimation. In contrast, other views may detect these joints with high confidence due to the presence of richer visual features. To address this, our system discards 2D keypoints with low confidence and applies a weighted average based on the confidence values31 when aggregating detected multi-view 2D keypoints. This 3D joint pose estimation process makes the system more stable and robust against signal drops caused by body occlusions, compared to marker-based methods or Kinect32.

The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged for a more comprehensive understanding of the findings. A primary limitation lies in the sensitivity of the markerless motion capture system, specifically the Ergo system, to a range of participant, environmental factors and various movement tasks that have not yet been rigorously examined, considering that the existing marker-based systems57. Participant characteristics, including age, sex, ethnicity, health status, and anatomical deformities, could potentially impact the system’s ability to accurately capture motion. For example, differences in body morphology across age groups or the presence of physical deformities may affect the system’s ability to track joint movements with high precision. Similarly, environmental conditions, such as the camera distance and participants, lighting quality, and even the type of clothing worn, could introduce variability in the data58,59. Moreover, variations in movement tasks could further influence the system’s consistency in tracking motion, leading to potential discrepancies in the data48. Future research should focus on systematically testing the influences of these variables on the validity and reliability of the system. Controlled experiments that explore how different movement tasks, diverse participant demographics, and variable environmental setups affect the system’s accuracy will be crucial. This would enable the refinement of the Ergo system, ensuring its robustness across a wider range of real-world scenarios and participant profiles, thereby enhancing its applicability in both clinical and research settings. By addressing these limitations, future studies can provide stronger evidence regarding the generalizability and reliability of markerless motion capture technologies.

Conclusion

The present study established the concurrent validity, reliability of the DL-based 3D markerless Ergo MoCap system in detecting multi-dimensional joint kinematics, which showed comparable performance with traditional marker-based motion capture systems during the ecological overhead squat movement test. The Ergo system demonstrated accuracy and reliability in this specific task, suggesting its potential utility in clinical, rehabilitation, sports biomechanics, and ergonomic fields. As the field of DL-based markerless motion capture advances, the Ergo system has the potential to contribute to designing movement assessment, effective or sustainable prevention, and interventional programs using markerless MoCap.
