Real‐World Clinical Evaluation of S2,3PSA Test in a Prostate Biopsy Cohort With PSA Level of 4–10 ng/mL
Authors: Jun Furumido, Sho Nonoyama, Chika Hoshi, Shogo Aizawa, Nariaki Ozaki, Takuto Morita, Akihiro Yamashita, Issei Tsunemi, Ryo Kato, Kanta Hori, Naohiko Shimoda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundProstate‐specific antigen (PSA) is a widely used prostate cancer marker; however, its use is limited by its low specificity. Novel biomarkers are being explored to avoid unnecessary biopsies. A new prostate cancer marker, S2,3PSA, became eligible for insurance coverage in February 2024.Material and MethodsA total of 144 patients underwent S2,3PSA measurement prior to prostate biopsy at Asahikawa Kosei Hospital and Teine Keijinkai Hospital. PI‐RADS score on MRI, PSA level, prostate volume (PV), and Gleason score were retrieved from medical records.ResultsThe median age of the patients was 69 years, the median PSA level was 5.79 ng/mL, and prebiopsy MRI PI‐RADS scores were 2 or less (43 patients), 3 (31 patients), 4 or greater (64 patients), and without MRI (6 patients). The median PV was 43 cm3. The median S2,3PSA was 41.85%. Of the cases examined, 55 (38%) exhibited prostate cancer and 36 (25%) were classified as significant cancer (SC, defined as Gleason Group 2 or higher). S2,3PSA and S2,3PSAD levels were significantly higher in the cancer group than the non‐cancer group, and in the SC group than in the no‐SC group. The AUC was 0.609 and 0.688 in the cancer group, and 0.646 and 0.703 in the SC group. Of the cases with MRI PI‐RADS 2 or lower, two cases (4.6%) were identified as SC, and these cases exhibited elevated S2,3PSA and S2,3PSAD.ConclusionThe AUC observed in this study was lower than that reported in previous studies. The efficacy of S2,3PSA may be enhanced when used in combination with MRI. Prostate biopsy may be unnecessary in cases where an MRI scan is negative and the S2,3PSA or S2,3PSAD levels are low.Keywords: prostate biopsy; prostate cancer; S2,3PSA

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the second most common malignant tumor in men in the world and the sixth most common cause of cancer death [1]. Prostate‐specific antigen (PSA) level is a widely utilized biomarker for prostate cancer [2, 3]. However, the clinical utility of PSA testing is limited due to its low cancer specificity, which can potentially result in unnecessary biopsies, overdiagnosis, and overtreatment [4, 5, 6, 7]. The prostate cancer positivity rate in patients with PSA levels ranging from 4 to 10 ng/mL is reported to be about 30% [8, 9].

To address these limitations, the Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI‐RADS) and new biomarkers, such as the Prostate Health Index (phi), have been developed, enabling more accurate prebiopsy assessment of prostate cancer risk [10, 11, 12, 13]. A group from Hirosaki University identified a novel biomarker, S2,3PSA, which enhances the specificity of prostate cancer detection by distinguishing cancer‐associated PSA from non‐cancer‐associated PSA based on abnormally glycosylated free PSA [14, 15, 16]. A previous study that analyzed a PSA range of 4–10 ng/mL reported that the diagnostic performance of S2,3PSA (area under the curve [AUC]: 0.727) was superior to that of total PSA (AUC: 0.517) and the free PSA/total PSA ratio (AUC: 0.679) [16]. Considering these results, S2,3PSA testing received insurance approval in Japan on February 1, 2024. Presently, S2,3PSA is a commercially available novel prostate cancer marker in Japan, similar to phi.

We conducted a retrospective study at Asahikawa Kosei General Hospital and Teine Keijinkai Hospital to examine cases in which S2,3PSA was measured under insurance coverage for patients with PSA values ranging from 4 to 10 ng/mL.

Material and Methods

The study was conducted at Asahikawa Kosei General Hospital or Teine Keijinkai Hospital in Hokkaido. These two hospitals are among the regional hospitals in Hokkaido that perform a high number of prostate biopsies and prostate cancer treatments. The present study included patients who underwent prostate biopsy at two hospitals and had their S2,3PSA measured prebiopsy. The following data were collected: age, prebiopsy MRI (PI‐RADS score), PSA, and prostate volume (measured by MRI or ultrasound at the time of biopsy). Since the S2,3PSA test is covered by insurance for patients with PSA levels ranging from 4 to 10 ng/mL, patients with PSA levels outside this range were excluded, resulting in a total of 144 cases for analysis. Prostate biopsies were performed transrectally, and 12 samples were systematically collected. If a positive area was found on MRI, that area was visualized with ultrasound, and a targeted biopsy (1 or 2 samples) was performed. Significant cancer (SC) was defined as Gleason grade group 2 or higher, as indicated by a Gleason score of 3 + 4 = 7 or higher. PSAD (ng/mL/cm3) was determined as PSA divided by Prostate Volume (cm3) [17], and S2,3PSAD (%/cm3) was determined as S2,3PSA (%) divided by Prostate Volume (cm3). S2,3PSA measurement was outsourced to SLR Inc. and performed with a μTASWako i50 device (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan).

Most MRI scans were performed without the use of contrast agents. All MRI examinations and PI‐RADS version 2 assessments were performed by radiologists who were blinded to the patients' S2,3PSA values. The grade groups of prostate biopsy specimens were assessed by pathologists at each hospital who were also blinded to the patient's S2,3PSA values.

Statistical analysis was conducted using JMP Student Edition 18. Mann–Whitney U test, ANOVA test, and χ 2 test were used for comparisons between groups. The AUC was measured using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. A p value less than 0.05 was defined as statistically significant. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards as laid out by the Ethics Committees at each participating hospital (Asahikawa Kosei Hospital No. 2023059, Teine Keijinkai Hospital No. 2‐024052‐00).

Result

The patient cohort in two hospitals is shown in Table 1. S2,3PSA levels were significantly higher in the cancer group (46.4%) than in the non‐cancer group (41.7%) (p = 0.0134) (Figure 1A). At a reference cutoff value of 38%, the sensitivity was 69%, and the specificity was 22%. When the S2,3PSA reference value was set to 50%, the sensitivity was 42%, and the specificity was 83%. Thus, the optimal cut‐off value was determined as 47.7%, which resulted in a sensitivity of 51% and a specificity of 78%. The AUC was 0.609 (Supporting Information S2: Table 1A). S2,3PSAD levels were significantly higher in the cancer group (1.36%/cm3) than the non‐cancer group (0.95%/cm3) (p < 0.0001) (Figure 1B). The reference value for S2,3PSAD remains uncertain, however, studies have demonstrated that, at the median value of 0.96%/cm3, the sensitivity and specificity levels were 65% and 58%, respectively. The optimization of the cut‐off to 1.27%/cm3 enhanced diagnostic performance, with a sensitivity of 53% and a specificity of 79%. The AUC was 0.688 (Supporting Information S2: Table 1B). When sensitivity was fixed at 80%, S2,3PSA exhibited a specificity of 21%, whereas S2,3PSAD specificity increased to 44%. For PSA and PSAD, the AUCs were 0.622 and 0.717, respectively, with fixed sensitivity at 80% yielding specificities of 36% and 45% (Supporting Information S2: Table 1A,B).

Grade Group 2 or higher was defined as SC. The group with SC had significantly higher S2,3PSA levels than those without SC (no‐SC group) (48.3% vs. 41.8%, p = 0.0023) (Figure 2A). At a cutoff value of 38%, the sensitivity was 72%, and the specificity was 38%. When the S2,3PSA cutoff was set at 50%, the sensitivity and specificity were 50% and 81%, respectively. The optimization of the cut‐off to 47.8% yielded a sensitivity of 58% and a specificity of 76%. The AUC was 0.646 (Supporting Information S2: Table 2A). S2,3PSAD levels were significantly higher in the SC group (1.48%/cm3) than the no‐SC group (0.98%/cm3) (p < 0.0001) (Figure 2B). At the median value of 0.96%/cm3, sensitivity was 67%, and specificity was 55%. The optimization of the cutoff to 0.81 resulted in 86% sensitivity and 46% specificity. The AUC was 0.703 (Supporting Information S2: Table 2A). When sensitivity was fixed at 80%, S2,3PSA exhibited a specificity of 29%, whereas S2,3PSAD specificity increased to 47%. For PSA and PSAD, the AUCs were 0.586 and 0.708, respectively, with fixed 80% sensitivity yielding specificities of 30% and 41% (Supporting Information S2: Table 2). Moreover, an S2,3PSAD cutoff of 1.0%/cm3 significantly stratified patients regarding both cancer (p = 0.0048) and SC (p = 0.0262) (Supporting Information S2: Table 3).

The present study examined the association between biopsy grade group and S2,3PSA and S2,3PSAD. As the grade group increased, both S2,3PSA and S2,3PSAD values increased (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2A,B).

In addition, we performed an analysis combining the results with MRI. We identified a positive correlation between elevated PIDAS scores and an increased propensity for the development of cancer. Furthermore, increases in S2,3PSA and S2,3PSAD values were observed (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2C–E). Furthermore, at PIDAS scores of 2 or below, the probability of detecting cancer was 16% (7/43), but the incidence of SC was low (2/43 = 4.6%). These cases exhibited elevated values for S2,3PSA (≥ 50) and S2,3PSAD (≥ 2.0) (Figure 3).

Discussion

Insurance approval for S2,3PSA was granted in Japan on February 1, 2024, designating it as a novel prostate cancer marker for commercial use. S2,3PSA has been demonstrated to be unaffected by pharmaceutical agents that influence PSA, such as Dutasteride [18], making it suitable for routine clinical use.

A previous study by Yoneyama et al. [16] reported an AUC for S2,3PSA in prostate cancer detection at PSA 4–10 ng/mL of 0.7266, while the AUC for S2,3PSAD was 0.7428. For high‐grade prostate cancer detection, the AUC was 0.7484 for S2,3PSA and 0.7704 for S2,3PSAD. Utilizing the MRI‐fusion biopsy approach, the AUC was 0.6830 for S2,3PSA in prostate cancer detection, and it was 0.7808 for S2,3PSAD. For high‐grade prostate cancer detection, the AUC was 0.6817 for S2,3PSA, and it was 0.7806 for S2,3PSAD. S2,3PSAD exhibited a high AUC. A study by Miura et al. [18] reported that using MRI‐fusion biopsy resulted in a higher AUC for S2,3PSA in prostate cancer detection (0.776–0.837), showing slightly higher AUC values than this study. Tokunaga et al. [19] reported that the AUC for S2,3PSA was 0.737 and for S2,3PSAD was 0.757 in prostate cancer detection. They also reported that the AUC was 0.799 for S2,3PSA and 0.810 for S2,3PSAD using MRI‐fusion biopsy.

In this study, PSAD exhibited high AUC and specificity. Miura et al. [18] also reported similar trends, with PSAD AUC values of 0.7572 for prostate cancer detection and 0.7444 for high‐grade prostate cancer detection, both higher than the AUC for S2,3PSA.

A combination of S2,3PSA with MRI has been reported to improve sensitivity and specificity. The incorporation of S2,3PSA testing into cases classified with PI‐RADS Score 3 or 4 and above has been reported to improve the detection rates for both biopsy‐negative and biopsy‐positive cases [16].

The median S2,3PSAD level was 0.96%/cm3 in this study, approximately 1.0%/cm3. Yoneyama and colleagues reported 1.03%/cm3 in a cancer‐free group and 1.88%/cm3 in a cancer group. S2,3PSAD levels were positively correlated with PI‐RADS scores. Incorporation of S2,3PSAD, with a cutoff value of ≥ 0.8750%/cm3, in cases classified as PI‐RADS Score ≥ 3 or ≥ 4, has improved prostate cancer detection rates in previous study [16]. In this study, an S2,3PSAD cutoff of 1.0%/cm3 significantly stratified patients regarding both cancer (p = 0.0048) and SC (p = 0.0262), suggesting that this threshold may be a simple and practical clinical cutoff.

Several previous studies reported that prostate biopsy may be unnecessary if MRI shows PI‐RADS Score 2 or lower [19, 20]. A Cochrane systematic review that included 18 studies demonstrated a high negative predictive value (91%) for detecting high‐grade prostate cancer in addition to a low risk (9%) of missing high‐grade prostate cancer in biopsy‐naïve men with negative MRI [21]. In this study, the risk of SC was 4.6% when MRI was negative. We included elevated S2,3PSA (≥ 50%) and S2,3PSAD (≥ 2.0%/cm3) cases, suggesting biopsy is necessary in patients with high S2,3PSA and S2,3PSAD.

This study had several limitations. First, MRI‐targeted biopsy, which is a significant component of comprehensive cancer research, was not performed. In particular, MRI‐positive lesions may not be always detectable through ultrasound imaging. This may explain the lower AUC compared with previous studies. Second, the study did not utilize MRI contrast agents, which may have further impacted the results. Third, this is a review of a limited number of cases in a specific region, Hokkaido, Japan.

Overall, although the present study could not demonstrate additional value of S2,3PSA over S2,3PSAD beyond that reported in previous studies for the detection of prostate cancer and SC when combined with MRI, lesions with PI‐RADS scores of 3 or higher were associated with an increased likelihood of SC, suggesting the necessity of prostate biopsy. Even when MRI shows PI‐RADS scores of 2 or lower, SC cannot be excluded in patients with elevated S2,3PSA or S2,3PSAD, and biopsy should therefore be considered. On the other hand, biopsy avoidance may be a viable option in cases with PI‐RADS scores of 2 or lower in combination with low S2,3PSA and S2,3PSAD levels. Further large‐scale studies are required to validate these findings.

Conclusion

Elevated S2,3PSA or S2,3PSAD levels in case with MRI score of PI‐RADS score of 2 or lower were associated with the presence of SC. Thus, prostate biopsy may not be necessary in case with PI‐RADS 2 or lower and low S2,3PSA or S2,3PSAD. In this cohort of patients, S2,3PSA did not perform better than PSAD, and further study is necessary to determine the utility of combining this testing with MRI.
