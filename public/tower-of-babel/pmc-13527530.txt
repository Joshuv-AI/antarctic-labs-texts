Clinical and Financial Outcomes With Minimally Invasive Excision With Epidermal Autografting and Poly‐Lactic Acid Skin Substitute in Adult Mid‐Deep Dermal Burns
Authors: Tony Zhao, Kaitlyn Malek, Anjay Khandelwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMid‐deep dermal burns have traditionally been treated with nonoperative management and split‐thickness skin grafting, but minimally invasive excision with epidermal autografting and poly‐lactic acid skin substitute (MEP) has offered an alternative approach. Comparative outcomes have remained incompletely defined. We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adult patients with mid‐deep dermal burns treated with MEP compared with a propensity score‐matched standard‐of‐care (SOC) cohort. The primary outcome was achievement of ≥ 90% wound healing by postoperative day 10, with secondary outcomes including length of stay, complications and hospital financial outcomes. Among 39 pairs analysed, MEP‐treated patients were significantly more likely to achieve early wound healing compared with SOC (82.1% vs. 45.5%, p = 0.001) and experienced fewer complications, without an increase in length of stay. Although MEP was associated with higher upfront hospital costs, reimbursement rates were comparable. MEP was associated with superior early wound healing and fewer complications without prolonging hospitalisation.

Introduction

Burn injuries continue to pose a significant global health challenge in both patient outcomes and socioeconomic burden. In the United States, approximately half a million patients receive medical treatment for nonfatal burns [1]. These burn admissions represent 88.5 admissions per million lives per year [2]. Factors such as length of stay (LOS), ventilator usage or total body surface area (TBSA) affected can all significantly increase healthcare costs for both patients and medical facilities. Therefore, the substantial resource burden underscores the importance of developing burn care interventions that continually improve patient outcomes while reducing subsequent costs.

Treating mid‐to‐deep dermal burns involved consideration for achieving rapid wound closure while minimising scarring and long‐term functional or aesthetic adverse effects. Traditionally, this relied on split‐thickness skin grafts (STSG), but this approach posed concerns including pain, scarring, depigmentation or limited healthy donor tissue in larger burns [3, 4, 5]. Ultimately, these limitations drove demand in finding alternative therapeutic approaches that enhanced healing while reducing complications, infections and cost.

Advances in burn care have focused on minimally invasive techniques. In 2018, the FDA approved epidermal autografting, utilising techniques of autologous skin cell suspensions (ASCS), for wound closure in burn patients. ASCS functions to enable rapid regeneration and re‐epithelialisation by creating a spray‐on solution containing harvested live keratinocytes, melanocytes, fibroblasts and Langerhans cells directly to the wound site [6]. ASCS has proven superior clinical and economic benefits compared to STSG, such as reduction in donor skin harvesting requirements, enhanced re‐epithelialisation and decreased follow‐up reconstructive procedures [7]. Additionally, ASCS has reduced hospital LOS, which has been a known driver for cost increases [8]. A similar advancement includes synthetic skin substitutes, such as poly‐lactic acid‐based matrices. This mechanism involves providing a biocompatible scaffold to the wound site for dermal regeneration and wound healing.

While these advancements have been adopted within clinical practice, it is necessary to continually evaluate clinical and cost data to determine their efficacy versus the previous standard of care (SOC). This study evaluates the outcomes of minimally invasive excision with epidermal autografting and poly‐lactic acid skin substitute (MEP) compared to SOC, focusing on length of stay (LOS), sedations and ≥ 90% healing by Day 10. Ultimately, we aim to provide insight into the potential of MEP to enhance burn management by evaluating various patient outcome variables and actual hospital cost data.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective cohort study evaluated adult patients admitted for burn injuries between 2022 and 2023 treated with MEP for mid‐deep dermal burns. Patients younger than 18 years of age, those lacking documented total body surface area (TBSA), patients undergoing traditional tangential excision, those with full‐thickness burns and patients treated at another facility were excluded. The control group was a propensity matched cohort managed non‐operatively from 2017 to 2020, prior to the utilisation of MEP for mid‐deep dermal burns.

Prior to 2021, adult patients with mid‐deep dermal burns were treated either with a long‐acting silver based antimicrobial dressing, daily dressing with antibiotic ointment and petroleum gauze or with antibiotic ointment combined with collagenase and petroleum gauze. In 2021, the two burn surgeons started utilising MEP for adult mid‐deep dermal burns and developed a practice consensus/guideline which included (1) adult patients with greater than 5% TBSA mid‐deep dermal burns, (2) mid‐deep dermal burns involving > 50% of the face, (3) mid‐deep dermal burns resulting in painful dressing changes that were felt to require multiple sedations, (4) situations where MEP was felt to shorten length‐of‐stay due to social challenges (e.g., lack of ability to perform dressing changes at home) and (5) situations where MEP was felt to represent a viable alternative to traditional excision and split‐thickness skin grafting. Burn depth was assessed clinically by one of two attending burn surgeons, each with more than 10 years of experience in burn care. Classification as a mid‐deep dermal burn was based on the attending surgeon's clinical examination; objective diagnostic technologies, including laser doppler imaging, were not routinely used.

This retrospective study qualified for exemption from Institutional Review Board oversight. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional requirements for retrospective research.

Operative Procedure

Upon admission, all patients were initially managed with an antimicrobial ointment and petroleum‐based gauze in accordance with traditional standard‐of‐care protocols. Patients subsequently underwent MEP in the operating room under general anaesthesia. Minimally invasive excision (dermabrasion) was performed using a TPX microdrill (Stryker, Portage, MI) equipped with either a 7 or 12‐mm dermabrader head and operated at rotational speeds between 25 000 and 40 000 RPM. Excision was continued until punctate bleeding was achieved.

Epidermal autograft was harvested from an area immediately adjacent to the burn using a standard dermatome at a depth of 6–8/1000th of an inch. Skin harvest volume was calculated at approximately 6 cm2 for every 480 cm2 of wound surface area requiring coverage, consistent with manufacturer recommendations permitting an expansion ratio of up to 80:1. Prepared epidermal autografts utilising the ReCell (Avita Medical, Valencia, CA) were then sprayed uniformly over the excised wound bed and immediately covered with a poly‐lactic acid skin substitute (SupraThel, PolyMedics Innovations GmbH, Denkendorf, Germany). A contact layer dressing (Rylon, BioMed Sciences, Allentown, PA) or antimicrobial barrier dressing (Acticoat Flex, Smith and Nephew, Watford, England) was applied based on anticipated wound drainage.

Donor sites, which were typically adjacent to the burn area, were managed in a similar fashion. Petroleum‐based gauze was placed over the contact layer dressing, followed by an outer dry sterile gauze layer. Dressings were generally changed one to two times prior to the planned final dressing change, which occurred on postoperative day 10. At that time, the wound bed was inspected to assess final MEP outcomes. Moderate to deep sedation was typically administered during the final dressing change to ensure patient comfort and procedural efficiency.

Study Variables and Outcomes

Demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, sex, total body surface area (TBSA), anatomical burn location and mechanism of injury. Anatomical burn locations were categorised as head and face, torso or trunk, upper extremities and lower extremities. Mechanism of injury was categorised as thermal, scald, chemical, electrical or other. For this study, the thermal category was defined specifically as flame and non‐electrical flash burns. Scald burns were classified and analysed separately and were not included in the thermal category. The single injury categorised as electrical represented an electrical flash burn without passage of electrical current through body tissues. Patients with true electrical injuries involving current passage through the body were excluded.

The primary outcome was achievement of ≥ 90% wound healing by postoperative day 10. Secondary outcomes included length of stay, number of procedures, number of sedations, presence of at least one infection or complication and hospital financial outcomes, including adjusted charges and adjusted payments. Length of stay normalised to TBSA (LOS/TBSA) was calculated and reported descriptively to contextualise hospitalisation duration relative to burn size.

Propensity Score Matching

To minimise selection bias and create comparable groups, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using a 1:1 nearest‐neighbour algorithm with a calliper of 0.13, corresponding to 0.2 times the pooled standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score [9]. Covariates included in the matching process were age, TBSA and mechanism of injury. Common support was assessed by comparing the distribution of propensity scores across groups, and matching quality was evaluated using standardised mean differences (SMDs). After matching, all SMDs were below 0.1, indicating adequate covariate balance between treatment and control groups.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted in both the unmatched and propensity score‐matched cohorts. The unmatched cohort was used to describe baseline characteristics and overall population trends, whereas the matched cohort was used for adjusted comparisons between MEP and SOC groups.

Continuous variables were summarised as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables.

Associations between covariates and outcomes were evaluated using regression models appropriate to each outcome type. Negative binomial regression was used for over dispersed count outcomes, including length of stay, number of sedations and number of procedures. Binary logistic regression was applied to dichotomous outcomes, including achievement of ≥ 90% wound healing by postoperative day 10 and presence of infection or complication. Quantile regression at τ = 0.50 was used to analyse financial outcomes, including adjusted charges and adjusted payments, to account for the skewed distribution of cost data. Multivariable models incorporated treatment groups and prespecified covariates selected a priori based on clinical relevance, including age and TBSA. Mechanism of injury was not included in outcome regression models due to its use in matching, high correlation with burn severity and the risk of collinearity in the matched cohort.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.0). Statistical significance was assessed at a Type I error rate of α = 0.05.

Primary Outcome: Early Wound Healing

In the propensity score–matched cohort, patients treated with MEP were significantly more likely to achieve ≥ 90% wound healing by postoperative day 10 compared with SOC (82.1% vs. 45.5%, p = 0.001), corresponding to an absolute increase of 36.6% (Table 3).

On multivariable logistic regression adjusting for age and TBSA, MEP treatment was independently associated with increased odds of early wound healing (OR 5.50, 95% CI: 1.89–16.01; p = 0.002; Table 4). Neither TBSA nor age was independently associated with early healing.

Secondary Outcomes

Length of Stay

In the unmatched cohort, MEP‐treated patients had a longer median hospital length of stay compared with SOC (4.0 vs. 2.0 days; p < 0.001), reflecting larger burn size and increased procedural intervention (Table 2). In the propensity score–matched cohort, median length of stay remained higher in the MEP group (4.0 [IQR 3.0–6.0] vs. 2.0 [IQR 1.0–5.0] days; p = 0.008; Table 3). However, in multivariable negative binomial regression adjusting for age and TBSA, MEP treatment was not independently associated with length of stay (IRR 1.24, 95% CI: 0.86–1.79; p = 0.24), whereas TBSA was a strong independent predictor of prolonged hospitalisation (IRR 1.09 per 1% increase; p < 0.001; Table 5).

Number of Procedures and Sedations

MEP‐treated patients underwent a greater number of procedures than SOC patients in both unmatched and matched analyses. In the matched cohort, the median number of procedures was higher in the MEP group (1.0 [IQR 1.0–1.0] vs. 0.0 [IQR 0.0–0.0]; p < 0.001; Table 3). On multivariable negative binomial regression, MEP treatment was independently associated with a higher procedure count (IRR 9.19, 95% CI: 3.65–23.15; p < 0.001; Table 5).

Similarly, MEP‐treated patients required more sedations. In the matched cohort, the median number of sedations was 1.0 (IQR 1.0–1.5) in the MEP group compared with 0.0 (IQR 0.0–0.5) in the SOC group (p < 0.001; Table 3). In adjusted analysis, both MEP treatment (IRR 1.83, 95% CI: 1.04–3.22; p = 0.037) and TBSA (IRR 1.20 per 1% increase; p < 0.001) were independently associated with increased sedation requirements (Table 5).

Infections and Complications

For the purposes of this study, infection was defined as either patients requiring systemic antibiotics after presentation (thereby excluding patients who came in with concern for infection) or those requiring a change in the topical antimicrobial therapy, for example, from bacitracin to mupirocin or silver sulfadiazine. In the matched cohort, no patients in the MEP group experienced a wound infection or complication, whereas 23.1% of SOC patients had at least one infection or complication (p = 0.002; Table 3). Due to the absence of events in the treatment group, adjusted regression modelling for complications was not performed.

Financial Outcomes

In the matched cohort, patients treated with MEP incurred higher adjusted hospital costs and payments compared with SOC (Table 3). Median adjusted costs were $84 982 (IQR $68 383–$113 615) in the MEP group versus $15 542 (IQR $10 864–$35 055) in the SOC group and median adjusted payments were $37 932 (IQR $21 962–$59 582) versus $7798 (IQR $1185–$10 252), respectively. These differences reflect baseline imbalances in burn size and procedural intensity prior to matching.

In the matched cohort, MEP‐treated patients continued to demonstrate higher adjusted costs and payments compared with SOC (both p < 0.001; Table 3). However, reimbursement rates did not differ significantly between groups (median 55.3% vs. 29.7%; p = 0.28), indicating comparable reimbursement efficiency despite higher absolute expenditures.

On quantile regression at the median, treatment with MEP was independently associated with increased adjusted costs (β = $63 566, 95% CI: $52 988–$74 144; p < 0.001) and adjusted payments (β = $29 833, 95% CI: $25 654–$34 012; p < 0.001), after adjusting for age and TBSA (Table 6).

Discussion

This study evaluated the clinical and financial outcomes of minimally invasive excision with epidermal autografting and poly‐lactic acid skin substitute (MEP) compared with standard‐of‐care (SOC) in adult patients with mid‐deep dermal burns. After accounting for baseline differences through propensity score matching, MEP was associated with significantly improved early wound healing and fewer complications, with comparable hospitalisation duration despite greater procedural intensity.

MEP‐treated patients were substantially more likely to achieve ≥ 90% wound healing by postoperative day 10 compared with SOC, and no infections or complications were observed in the treatment group. These findings suggest that MEP may enhance both the speed and quality of wound closure in this population. The combination of epidermal autografting with a poly‐lactic acid scaffold may support rapid epithelialisation by providing both viable autologous cells and a biocompatible dermal matrix, which may reduce susceptibility to wound‐related complications.

Naturally, MEP is associated with a higher number of procedures; however, patients treated with MEP did require a greater number of sedations. Although MEP‐treated patients underwent more procedures and required more sedations, these findings appear to reflect the procedural demands of the technique rather than postoperative morbidity. This reflects the intrinsic association to the procedure rather than procedural morbidity. Importantly, increased sedation requirements were not associated with prolonged length of stay or higher complication rates. After adjustment for age and burn size, MEP was not independently associated with increased length of stay, indicating that improved healing outcomes were achieved without extending hospitalisation. In this context, sedation should be viewed as a therapeutic adjunct and enhanced quality of care that facilitates wound management, patient comfort and efficiency rather than an adverse outcome. In addition, it is reflective of institutional practice variability. MEP was not independently associated with increased length of stay, indicating that improved healing outcomes were achieved without extending hospitalisation. Length of stay was influenced by the time to surgery in the MEP group, which over the course of time has decreased due to enhanced operating room access designed to facilitate the flow of patients treated utilising MEP.

From a financial perspective, MEP was associated with higher adjusted hospital costs and payments in both matched and adjusted analyses. These increased expenditures likely reflect the upfront costs of advanced biologic materials and operative resource utilisation. However, reimbursement rates did not differ significantly between treatment strategies, suggesting comparable reimbursement efficiency. From a value‐based care standpoint, higher initial costs may be offset by clinically meaningful benefits, including faster wound closure and the absence of complications, which have the potential to reduce downstream resource utilisation and the need for secondary interventions. Importantly, the financial analysis in this study was limited to hospitalisation costs and payments and did not capture downstream resource utilisation post‐discharge.

In burn care, management of delayed wound healing, secondary reconstruction (including laser therapy) and treatment of complications are significant drivers of downstream cost. Therefore, higher upfront expenditures associated with MEP should be interpreted within the context of higher front‐end investment that may alter the subsequent trajectory of care rather than an assessment of long‐term cost‐effectiveness.

Consistent with prior literature, total body surface area (TBSA) was a strong independent predictor of both length of stay and hospital cost, underscoring burn size as a primary driver of resource use. Notably, MEP demonstrated favourable healing outcomes despite larger burns in the treatment population prior to matching, supporting its potential utility in more severe injuries.

This study has limitations. Because dressings were not changed daily, the first planned formal wound assessment occurred at the postoperative day 10 dressing takedown. Therefore, the precise time to re‐epithelialisation could not be determined, and differences in epithelialisation rates across anatomical regions could not be reliably evaluated. This limitation may be particularly relevant for areas such as the face and scalp, where the greater density of hair follicles and other adnexal structures may contribute to different healing patterns. Accordingly, the study was limited to the consistently documented endpoint of achievement of at least 90% wound healing by postoperative day 10. In addition, the retrospective, single‐centre design may limit generalisability and remains subject to residual confounding despite propensity score matching. The financial analysis was also limited to hospitalisation costs and payments and did not capture post‐discharge resource utilisation, subsequent reconstructive procedures or long‐term cost‐effectiveness.

Conclusion

Minimally invasive excision with epidermal autografting and poly‐lactic acid skin substitute was associated with improved early wound healing and fewer complications compared with standard of care in adult patients with mid‐deep dermal burns. These clinical benefits were achieved without prolonging hospitalisation, despite increased procedural requirements. Although MEP was associated with higher upfront hospital costs, reimbursement efficiency was comparable and the observed improvements in healing suggest potential downstream value. By concentrating resources early in the course of care, MEP may represent a value enhancing strategy in burn management—prioritising effective wound closure and reducing the biological risk while offering the potential to reshape downstream clinical end economic outcomes. Further prospective, multicentre studies are warranted to evaluate long‐term clinical outcomes, cost‐effectiveness and durability of benefit.
