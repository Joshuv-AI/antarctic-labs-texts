Resolving memorization in empirical diffusion model for manifold data in high-dimensional spaces
Yang Lyu †, Tan Minh Nguyen §, Yuchun Qian ‡, Xin T. Tong *

Abstract
Diffusion models are popular tools for generating new data samples, using a forward process that adds noise to data and a reverse process to denoise and produce samples. However, when the data distribution consists of n n points, empirical diffusion models tend to reproduce existing data points—a phenomenon known as the memorization effect. Current literature often addresses this with complex machine learning techniques. This work shows that the memorization issue can be solved simply by applying an inertia update at the end of the empirical diffusion simulation. Our inertial diffusion model requires only the empirical score function and no additional training. We demonstrate that the distribution of samples from this model approximates the true data distribution on a C 2 C^{2} manifold of dimension d d , within a Wasserstein-1 distance of O ⁡ ( n − 2 d + 4 ) O(n^{-\frac{2}{d+4}}) . This bound significantly shrinks the Wasserstein distance between the population and empirical distributions, confirming that the inertial diffusion model produces new, diverse samples. Remarkably, this estimate is independent of the ambient space dimension, as no further training is needed. Our analysis leverages the fact that the inertial diffusion samples resemble Gaussian kernel density estimations on the manifold, revealing a novel connection between diffusion models and manifold learning.

Introduction

Generative models are computational mechanisms trained to produce new synthetic data points from an unknown distribution where a limited amounts of training data is readily available.
They have been implemented in various scenarios for the generation of images, audio and texts.
Well known members of generative models include
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), and normalizing flows. Diffusion model (DM), also known as the denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM)
[
17
]
, is a new addition to the family. It has gained great popularity due to its capability in generating high quality samples
[
8
]
and model reproducibility
[
41
]
.

Mathematically, the idea behind DM can be explained using simple stochastic analysis
[
35
]
. Consider an Ornstein–Unlenbeck (OU) process starting from
X_{0}\sim p_{0}
, with

dX_{t}=-X_{t}dt+\sqrt{2}dW_{t},

(1)

where
W_{t}
is a
D
-dimensional Brownian motion.
The Fokker Plank equation (FPE) describes the distribution of
X_{t}
:
\tfrac{d}{dt}p_{t}=\nabla\cdot(xp_{t})+\Delta p_{t}
. Due to the linearity of the OU process, we can write

p_{t}(x)=\int K_{\sigma_{t}}(x,\alpha_{t}y)p_{0}(dy),

(2)

using
\alpha_{t}=e^{-t},\sigma^{2}_{t}=1-e^{-2t}
along with Gaussian kernel

K_{\sigma}(x,y)=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-D/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x-y\|^{2}\right).

The reversed distribution
q_{t}=p_{T-t}
follows

\frac{d}{dt}q_{t}=-\nabla\cdot(xq_{t})-\Delta q_{t}=\nabla\cdot(-(x+\nabla\log q_{t})q_{t})=\nabla\cdot(-(x+2\nabla\log q_{t})q_{t})+\Delta q_{t}.

Now consider an ordinary differential equation (ODE)

\frac{d}{dt}Y_{t}=Y_{t}+\nabla\log p_{T-t}(Y_{t}).

(3)

or a stochastic differential equation (SDE)

d\widetilde{Y}_{t}=(\widetilde{Y}_{t}+2\nabla\log p_{T-t}(\widetilde{Y}_{t}))dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t},

(4)

where
B_{t}
is another
D
-dimensional Brownian
Suppose
Y_{0},\widetilde{Y}_{0}\sim q_{0}=p_{T}
, using continuity equation and FPE, it is straight forward to check that the distributions of
Y_{t}
and
\widetilde{Y}_{t}
follow
q_{t}=p_{T-t}
. In particular, if we simulate (
3
) or (
4
),
Y_{T}
and
\widetilde{Y}_{T}
will be samples from
p_{0}
. For the simplicity of exposition, we will focus on the ODE version when discussion DM. We remark that most of our results also apply to the SDE version.

1.1
Empirical DM and memorization

One significant challenge confronting all generative models is
memorization
, where the generated data closely resembles—or occasionally replicates exactly—a training data point. Memorization not only undermines the goal of generating truly “new" data points but can also lead to legal concerns related to plagiarism or privacy violations. Several studies
[
30
,
23
,
24
]
attribute this phenomena to the empirical DM, which can be viewed a full capacity limit of deep neural network (NN) trained DM used in practice.

Empirical DM can be easily obtained by replacing
p_{0}
with its empirical estimate in the DM derivation.
In practice, we cannot access the population distribution
p_{0}
directly. Rather, we have
n
i.i.d. samples
X_{(i)}\sim p_{0}
. We denote their empirical distribution as

\hat{p}_{0}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{(i)}},

where
\delta_{x}
stands for the Dirac measure at point
x
. Replacing
p_{0}
with
\hat{p}_{0}
in (
2
), the empirical DM distribution is given by

\hat{p}_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}K_{\sigma_{t}}(x,\alpha_{t}X_{(j)}).

(5)

One can consider the DM ODE (
3
) with the empirical score, i.e.

\frac{d}{dt}Z_{t}=Z_{t}+\nabla\log\hat{p}_{T-t}(Z_{t}).

(6)

It is not difficult to show that if
Z_{0}
is generated from
\hat{p}_{T}
, the distribution of
Z_{t}
will be the same as
\hat{p}_{T-t}
. In particular, the distribution of the final outcome
Z_{T}
will follow
\hat{p}_{0}
, which is the training data distribution.
While mathematically this is elegant, it also means
Z_{T}
does not generate a new data point, but rather, it will be one of the training data point.
This is often referred to as the
memorization
phenomena.

In practice,
Z_{0}
is usually generated from an approximation of
\hat{p}_{T}
when
T
is very large. Due to the OU setup, this distribution is often conveniently as
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{D})
. But this modification does not resolve much of the memorization issue. See Lemma
4.10
for a detailed discussion.

Empirical diffusion models (DM) are not typically implemented directly in practice. Instead, it is more common to train an DNN to approximate the score function. When the DNN reaches its full capacity, the empirical score can be viewed as the global minimizer of denoising score matching
[
23
,
24
]
. In this context, memorization within the empirical DM can be interpreted as a fundamental cause. Therefore, addressing memorization in empirical DM is of critical importance.

1.2
Generalization with inertia update

Since the discovery of memorization phenomena, there has been growing interest in finding ways to resolve it. One line of works focus on the fact that DM with neural network implementation does not show memorization when the data size is rich. The main goal of this line is showing that if the empirical score
\nabla\log\hat{p}_{T-t}
is replaced by an appropriate neural network architecture approximation, memorization will not take effect
[
27
,
36
,
1
]
. Another line of works focus on modifying the empirical DM score function so
Z_{T}
will not be any of the existing data point. Some examples include
regularization schemes
[
2
]

and Wasserstein proximal operator
[
40
]
.

By looking at these existing works, one may think that empirical score by itself is not enough to generate bonafide new data samples. One of the main goal of this work is to debunk this fallacy.

We consider a simple adjustment to the empirical DM process (
6
) that consists of two step. In the first step, we generate the empirical DM process (
6
) up to time
T-h^{2}
. In the second step we update

\widehat{Z}_{T}=\alpha_{h^{2}}^{-1}(Z_{T-h^{2}}+\sigma^{2}_{h^{2}}\nabla\log\hat{p}_{T-h^{2}}(Z_{T-h^{2}})).

(7)

Here
h
is a small tuning parameter, which plays the role similar to the bandwidth in kernel based learning methods. We will refer (
7
) as an
inertia
update, since it only uses the empirical score at time
T-h^{2}
to generate an update for the final time period
[T-h^{2},T]
. We will also refer the data generation process as
inertia diffusion model
(IDM) in subsequent discussion. Its pseudo code can be found in Algorithm
1
. We name it IDM as its trajectory is similar to running the DM ODE (
3
) in the terminal time period
[T-h^{2},T]
with a fixed direction
\nabla\log\hat{p}_{T-t}(Z_{T-t})
. A simple illustrate of the IDM procedure with
S^{1}
being the latent manifold can be found in Figure
1
. It is clear that inertia update pushes the samples onto the manifold and interpolates between the training data points.

Comparing with other existing methods, the implementation of IDM requires only the empirical score function and no further training. It involves no requirement of DNN architecture specification or training modification like regularization. In particular, analysis of IDM does not require the assumption that the training loss optimization–which is often nonconvex–can be solved.

On the other hand, the training-free IDM does not leverage amortization like other DNN-based diffusion models. Specifically, generating a new data sample requires processing the entire training dataset, which can be computationally expensive in practice. To improve efficiency, a more practical implementation of IDM would involve replacing the empirical score
\hat{p}_{t}
with an accurate approximation
\tilde{p}_{t}
, achieved using standard DNNs or other machine learning architectures. The primary advantage of IDM is that it eliminates concerns about the DM memorizing training data at full capacity.

To rigorously illustrate that IDM’s capability on generalization, we consider the popular manifold data assumption, which has widely been seen as the reason why many machine learning methods can beat the curse of dimensionality. The detailed definition can be found in Section 2.

Theorem 1.1
.

[Informal]
Suppose
p_{0}
is a regular
C^{2}
density supported on a
C^{2}

d
-dimensional compact manifold
\mathcal{M}
. Suppose
X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(n)}
are i.i.d. samples from
p_{0}
. Choose bandwidth
h=n^{-\frac{1}{d+4}}
, let
\hat{p}_{\text{IDM}}
be the output distribution of Algorithm
1
with
Z_{0}\sim\hat{p}_{T}
. Then with high probability

W_{1}(\hat{p}_{\text{IDM}},p_{0})\leq\widetilde{O}(h^{2}).

Here
W_{1}
is the Wasserstein-1 distance, and
\widetilde{O}
hides some polynomials of
\log n,d
and information of
\mathcal{M}
. But it is independent of
D
.

To see why Theorem
1.1
indicates IDM generates a new sample from
p_{0}
, we recall that with high probability
W_{1}(\hat{p}_{0},p_{0})=\widetilde{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{d}})

[
13
]
, which we will referred to as the
memorization
learning rate. So if
d\geq 5
, then
W_{1}(\hat{p}_{\text{IDM}},p_{0})\ll W_{1}(\hat{p}_{0},p_{0})
, which indicates IDM has significantly better generalization capability than pure memorization.

Another important remark is that the upper bound in Theorem
1.1
is completely independent of
D
and the sample size does need to surpass
D
. This is different from other existing DM generalization analysis.

Finally, our work has a close connection with manifold learning literature. In particular 1) we can show the inertia update have high probability "projecting" points onto
\mathcal{M}
approximately; 2) we can also show
W_{1}(\hat{p}_{\text{IDM}},\hat{p}_{\text{KDE}})=\widetilde{O}(h^{2})
where
\hat{p}_{\text{KDE}}
is the kernel density estimation (KDE) on
\mathcal{M}
with Gaussian kernel bandwidth
h
. We delay the detailed discussion to Section
2
.

1.3
Literature review

Since the invention of DM
[
17
,
35
]
, it has gained great interest in the community. There have been many works aiming to understand its performance
[
6
]
or designing modifications for various purposes
[
15
]
. It is unlikely that we can cover every line of this fast growing literature, and we will only discuss works that are relevant to the memorization phenomena.

One line of works focus on statistical learning rate of DM for the estimation of the data distribution
p_{0}
. Earlier works like
[
6
]
indicates that the accuracy of DM depends on the accuracy of the population score
\nabla\log p_{t}
estimation.
[
27
]
indicates the indicates the learning rate of deep neural network (DNN) based DM is
\widetilde{O}(C_{D}n^{-\frac{s+1}{2s+D}})
in
W_{1}
distance, where
s
is the smoothness of the density
p_{0}
and
C_{D}
is a constant that depends on
D
. While this rate is minimax optimal, it is also subject to the curse of ambient dimension
D
, which can scale up to millions for practical problems.

To explain why DM in practice can have good performance despite the curse of dimension, several works have focused on the popular manifold hypothesis
[
11
]
, where the data is assumed to lie on a
d
-dimensional manifold
\mathcal{M}
with smoothness
\beta
.
d
is assumed to be significantly smaller than
D
and often viewed as a
O(1)
constant. Recently,

[
36
]
shows that with appropriate DNN structure, DM can achieve
\widetilde{O}(D^{s+d/2}\max\{n^{-\frac{s+1}{2s+d}},n^{-\frac{\beta}{2s+d}}\})
rate. Later
[
1
]
improves estimate to
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{D}n^{-\frac{s+1}{2s+d}})
but the manifold smoothness
\beta
needs to be sufficiently high and the sample size
n\gtrsim D
. Both these rates are better than the memorization rate
W_{1}(\hat{p}_{0},p_{0})=\widetilde{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{d}})

[
13
]
, so they indicate DM can generate new data under the manifold hypothesis.
Meanwhile,
both these works leverage on a specific DNN structure to approximate the population score (see equation (15) in
[
1
]
), and this structure need to be configured according to the training data. In other words, implementation such DNN structure can be impractical.
Besides manifold hypothesis, one can also leverage the locality of the distribution to resolve memorization and improve estimation accuracy
[
19
,
14
]
. But this is a different framework and the results are difficult to compare with ours.

Another line of works focus on modifying the empirical DM score function so
Z_{T}
will not be any of the existing data point. Some examples include
regularization schemes
[
2
]
, Wasserstein proximal operator
[
40
]
, and local manifold dimension fitting
[
31
]

. While these works demonstrate convincing empirical evidence, there has been no rigorous analysis showing they are better estimator of
p_{0}
than pure memorization.

One very interesting connection this paper create is the link between DM and manifold learning. In particular, as one important step, we show that IDM will generate samples that are approximately following the kernel density estimator (KDE) on
\mathcal{M}
. Manifold KDE is known to have
O(n^{-\frac{2}{d+4}})
rate for
C^{2}
manifold and
p_{0}

[
28
]
. But to implement it, one needs information of
\mathcal{M}
, e.g. a proper parameterization on
\mathcal{M}
. In particular, manifold KDE does not provide a direct way to generate new samples from
p_{0}
. On the other hand, there is also a big and constantly growing literature on inference of manifold
[
32
,
3
,
7
,
34
,
20
]
. Many of these methods consider estimation of the manifold Laplace-Beltrami operator
[
18
]
or a linear interpolation matrix. The implementation of these methods often require processing pairwise distance between training data points, which is often
O(n^{2})
computational complexity. This can be prohibitive in applications with large sample size.

Manifold kernel density estimation, which also relies on approximating and estimating
\Delta_{\mathcal{M}}
but directly provides distribution estimation on manifold data, often incorporates curvature corrections to estimate distributions more accurately
[
29
,
16
,
21
]
.
[
10
]
proves a curvature-corrected KDE attains the minimax optimal Wasserstein rate in intrinsic dimension
d

[
26
]
.
[
9
]
employs a sampler supported on the convex hull of the data points akin to ours but targets manifold reconstruction and relies on a graph constructions.
[
33
]
proposed an empirical score based barycentric prior that forces generated points into barycenters of subsets of data points, while empirically successful, lack theoretical guarantees.
Moreover, as mentioned above, these methods have at least
O(n^{2})
computational complexity, and some require knowledge of
d
or oracle access the the manifold.

1.4
Our contribution

This paper has proposed and analyzed the IDM approach.
We showed rigorously that IDM avoid the memorization issue. Moreover, comparing with the existing literature, IDM has the following advantage:

1.

IDM is a simple adaptation of the existing empirical DM. It uses only the empirical score function. It does not require any additional training procedure or configuration. As a consequence, its learning rate under the manifold assumption,
\widetilde{O}_{d}(n^{-\frac{2}{4+d}})
is independent of ambient dimension
D
. Also this rate can be reached with theoretical guarantee, while others methods’ learning rate can only be achieved if the non-convex optimization problem related to model training is perfectly solved.
Finally, understanding of IDM does not rely on any specific machine learning architecture, so it will not be subject to the fast update cycle of machine learning literature.

2.

Comparing with the distribution learning literature, our case corresponds to the
s=\beta=2
case, and our exponent,
n^{-\frac{2}{4+d}}
, matches the one from
[
36
]
. Admittedly, when the manifold and density smoothness are higher than
2
,
[
1
]
has an improved exponent
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{D}n^{-\frac{s+1}{2s+d}})
. This is because IDM matches the performance of a Gaussian KDE, and Gaussian kernel cannot leverage smoothness beyond second order
[
37
]
. On the other hand, if we consider
s=2
while letting
\beta
to be sufficiently large, in order for
\sqrt{D}n^{-\frac{3}{4+d}}\leq n^{-\frac{2}{4+d}}
, one would need
n\geq D^{4+d}
, which is often a requirement cannot be met even with moderate dimension settings like
D=10^{3}
and
d=1
. Moreover, even if there are very high level of smoothness, estimates in
[
1
]
require
n\geq D
, which can be a prohibitive condition for very large scale models. In comparison, IDM does not have such constraint.

3.

Under the manifold hypothesis, IDM is closely related to the manifold KDE, a density estimation tool in the manifold learning literature. This reveals an interesting connection between diffusion models and manifold learning. Such connection has not been discussed in the literature.

4.

Comparing to manifold KDE, implementation of IDM does not require knowledge of the underlying manifold.
While manifold learning methods like graphical Laplacian can be used to infer the manifold, the computational cost usually scale like
O(n^{2})
. Computational cost IDM is only
O(n)
.

5.

As a side product, we show the IDM update is a Naradaya–Watson estimator, and it is a generalized version of manifold projection for perturbed data points. This can be an interesting finding for other purposes.

Notation and convention

Throughout the paper, we assume the data lies on a
d
-dimensional compact Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. We treat
d
and all constants related to
\mathcal{M}
as order
1
constants. We keep track of all quantity relate to the ambient space dimension
D
and the sample size
n
, which both can diverge to
\infty
with different speed. If a quantity that does not depend on
D
nor
n
, we refer to it as a
constant
.

We say an event happens with high probability if it happens with probability
1-n^{-k}
. Here
k
is fixed constant that is independent of
D
and
n
.

We say
f(D,n)\lesssim g(D,n)
or
f(D,n)=O(g(D,n))
if there is a constant
C
that does not depend on
D
or
n
so that
f(D,n)\leq Cg(D,n)
. But this constant may depend on configuration of
\mathcal{M}
. We write
f(D,n)=\widetilde{O}(g(D,n))
, if there is a constant
C
that does not depend on
D
or
n
so that
f(D,n)\leq C(k\log n)^{\alpha}g(D,n)
for certain
\alpha>0
.

A chart of a manifold
\mathcal{M}
is a pair
(U,\phi)
where
U\subset\mathcal{M}
is an open set, and
\phi:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a homeomorphism, i.e.,
\phi
is bijective and both
\phi
and
\phi^{-1}
are continuous.
We say that a manifold
\mathcal{M}
is
C^{2}
if for every
y\in\mathcal{M}
, there exists an open set
U_{y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a one-to-one map
\Phi_{y}:U_{y}\to\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
such that
\Phi_{y}(0)=y
and
\Phi_{y}
is
C^{2}
-smooth. In this case,
\mathcal{M}
can be locally represented as the image of a
C^{2}
function.
Given a point
p\in\mathcal{M}
, the exponential map
\exp_{p}:T_{p}\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
is defined by mapping a tangent vector
v\in T_{p}\mathcal{M}
to the point reached at time
t=1
along the unique geodesic starting at

.
We say that
\mu
has a
C^{2}
density, if its density
p
is
C^{2}
, i.e., for every chart
(U,\phi)
of
\mathcal{M}
, the function
p\circ\phi^{-1}:\phi(U)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\text{ is a }C^{2}\text{ function. }

2
Method and analysis

2.1
Derivation of IDM

There has been no clear definition or metric deciding if a given generative model is truly generative.
Different existing works use different metrics
[
41
,
40
,
2
]
.

The manifold hypothesis
[
11
]
is a common setup used in the literature to explain why machine learning models avoid the curse of dimension in practice. It assumes the data points lie on a latent low dimensional sub-manifold:

Assumption 2.1
.

The following hold

1.

\mathcal{M}
is a compact
C^{2}

d
-dimensional Riemannian manifold without boundary.

2.

\mathcal{M}
is locally isometrically embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. In particular, there are some constant
C
so that

|\|x-y\|-\text{dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,y)|\leq C\text{dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,y)^{3}.

3.

The data distribution
p_{0}
is supported on
\mathcal{M}
with a
C^{2}
density
p_{0}(x)
.

4.

The density is bounded from both sides
p_{\min}\leq p_{0}(x)\leq p_{\max}
.

5.

The data points
X_{(i)}
are i.i.d. samples from
p_{0}
.

A similar version of this assumption can be found in
[
1
]
.

Given the manifold data assumption, intuitively, a truly generative model should produce outcomes that are 1) Close to the manifold
\mathcal{M}
, since
\mathcal{M}
captures latent features within data. 2) Different from existing data points, otherwise it is purely memorization. Each of two criteria is easy to fulfill by itself.
\hat{p}_{0}
will automatically be supported on
\mathcal{M}
, but it is pure memorization. Intuitively, we can consider perturbing each data point with Gaussian noise
N(0,\delta I_{D})
so the samples are "new" (This distribution is close to
\hat{p}_{\delta}
). But most of these samples will be of distance
\sqrt{D\delta}
from
\mathcal{M}
, which can be significant deviation if
D
is large.

Given these observations, it is natural to consider a two step procedure: We first generate random samples different from
X_{[n]}
, say using
\hat{p}_{\delta}
, then push these points onto
\mathcal{M}
. Then intuitively both basic criteria above will satisfy. In the data driven setting, we do not know the exact location and shape of
\mathcal{M}
, so finding the push map is the main challenge. However, we note that the DM dynamics will automatically push
p_{t}
, which is a distribution off
\mathcal{M}
, onto
\mathcal{M}
. So it is natural to ask how does it achieve this.

Given a manifold
\mathcal{M}
, we say an
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
is uniquely normal projected (UNP), if
\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}(x)\in\mathcal{M}
is the unique minimizer of
\|x-y\|
with
y\in\mathcal{M}
.
It is well known that the
\tau
neighborhoood
\mathcal{M}^{\tau}=\{x:\text{dist}(x,\mathcal{M})\leq\tau\}
has all point being UNP if
\tau
is below some threshold
[
22
]
. The threshold
\tau_{0}
is often referred as the reach of
\mathcal{M}
.
Given these concept, we have the following characterization of the population score function, of which the asymptotic version appeared in
[
24
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose Assumption
2.1
holds and
x\in\mathcal{M}^{\tau}
with a sufficiently small
\tau
, there is a threshold
t_{0}
such that if
h^{2}<t_{0}

\nabla\log p_{h^{2}}(x)=\frac{\Pi_{\alpha_{h^{2}}\mathcal{M}}(x)-x}{\sigma_{h^{2}}^{2}}+O(1+dist(x,M)^{3/2}/h^{2}+\sqrt{d\log(1/h)}/h).

The proof of Proposition
2.1
is allocated in Section
4.7
. Given Proposition
2.1
, we see that the population inertial map

F_{\sigma_{h^{2}}}(x)=\alpha_{h^{2}}^{-1}(x+\sigma^{2}_{h^{2}}\nabla\log p_{h^{2}}(x))

(8)

is a
\widetilde{O}(\delta)
approximation of
\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}(\alpha_{\delta}x)
and
\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}(x)
. From this, it is natural to consider using the empirical version of inertia map as a projection to the manifold. This will give us the inertia diffusion model.

2.2
Inertia update as manifold projection

Next, we investigate how well does the empirical inertia update inherit the projection property. Note that the empirical score can be explicitly written as

\nabla_{x}\log\hat{p}_{h^{2}}(x)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{x}K_{\sigma_{h^{2}}}(\alpha_{h^{2}}X_{(i)},x)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{\sigma_{h^{2}}}(\alpha_{h^{2}}X_{(i)},x)}=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{h^{2}}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(x)\left(x-\alpha_{h^{2}}X_{(i)}\right),

where
w_{i}(x)=\frac{K_{\sigma_{h^{2}}}(\alpha_{h^{2}}X_{(i)},x)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}K_{\sigma_{h^{2}}}(\alpha_{h^{2}}X_{(i)},x)}
. To continue, given a data set
X=\{X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(n)}\}
and a bandwidth
\sigma
, we define the Nadaraya–Watson estimator (NWE):

F_{X,\sigma}(z):=\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}K_{\sigma}(z,X_{(i)})X_{(i)}}{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}K_{\sigma}(z,X_{(i)})}.

(9)

Such estimator is commonly used in kernel based nonparametric estimation.

Note that samples from
\hat{p}_{h^{2}}
can be written
z=\alpha_{h^{2}}X_{(i)}+\sigma_{h^{2}}\xi
with some data point
X_{(i)}
and a Gaussian random vector
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{D})
. Its image with the empirical inertia update is given by

\alpha^{-1}_{h^{2}}(z+\sigma_{h^{2}}^{2}\nabla\log\hat{p}_{h^{2}}(z))=\alpha^{-1}_{h^{2}}F_{\alpha_{h^{2}}X,\sigma_{h^{2}}}(\alpha_{h^{2}}X_{(i)}+\sigma_{h^{2}}\xi)=F_{X,\sigma^{\prime}}(X_{(i)}+\sigma^{\prime}\xi),

with
\sigma^{\prime}=\frac{\sigma_{h^{2}}}{\alpha_{h^{2}}}=h(1+O(h^{2}))
.
If we denote the projection of
\xi
on tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{X_{(i)}}\mathcal{M}
as
\xi_{\mathcal{T}}
, and the exponential map on
\mathcal{M}
as
\exp_{X_{(i)}}(v)
with any element
v\in\mathcal{T}_{X_{(i)}}
. The following theorem characterize the difference between the exponential map and IDM update.

Theorem 2.2
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, let
\sigma\asymp n^{-\frac{4}{d+4}}
. Fix
x\in\mathcal{M}
and
r>0
. Let
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
with decomposition
\xi=\xi_{\mathcal{T}}+\xi_{\bot}
on
T_{x}\mathcal{M}\oplus N_{x}\mathcal{M}
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|F_{X,\sigma}(x+\sigma\xi)-\exp_{x}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}})\right\|\lesssim(d(k+1)\log n\,\sigma)^{2}\right)\geq 1-n^{-k}.

(10)

In particular,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|F_{X,\sigma}(x+\sigma\xi_{\bot})-x\right\|\lesssim(d(k+1)\log n\,\sigma)^{2}\right)\geq 1-n^{-k}.

(11)

The proof is allocated in the end of Section
4.4
.
In view of this theorem, the NW estimator effectively truncates the noisy components in the normal bundle, and move along the geodesic of
\mathcal{M}
along the tangent space component.

From (
11
), we see
F_{X,\sigma}
can be viewed as a high probability projection of points outside of the manifold onto the manifold. This is an interesting phenomena, since the typical distance from
x+\sigma\xi_{\bot}
to
\mathcal{M}
is
O(\sqrt{D}\sigma)
. When
D
is very large,
\sqrt{D}\sigma
can be larger than the "reach" of the manifold, so one cannot easily use standard differential geometric tools to establish this result.

2.3
IDM as manifold Kernel density estimation

Kernel density estimation (KDE) is a classical method for non parametric density estimation. Given
n
data points
\{X_{(i)}\}_{i\in[n]}
, the
d
-dimensional KDE density with Gaussian bandwidth
\sigma
is given by

\hat{p}_{\text{KDE}}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}k_{\sigma}(x,X_{(i)}),\quad k_{\sigma}(x,y)=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-d/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x-y\|^{2}\right).

Note that we use
k_{\sigma}
to denote the
d
-dimensional Gaussian kernel, which is different from the
D
-dimensional Gaussian kernel
K_{\sigma}
by a factor of
(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-(D-d)/2}
.

When the manifold
\mathcal{M}
and its dimension
d
are known to us,
\hat{p}_{\text{KDE}}(x)
defines a density for
x\in\mathcal{M}
. It is well known that
\hat{p}_{\text{KDE}}(x)
is an
O(n^{-\frac{2}{d+4}})
approximation. The
L_{2}
error, i.e. mean square error, is analyzed by
[
28
]
. We can improve it to
L_{\infty}
error:

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose Assumption
2.1
holds and
X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(n)}
are i.i.d. samples from
p_{0}
.
If we choose
\sigma\asymp n^{-\frac{1}{d+4}}
in the KDE estimator, with high probability
1-n^{-k}

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\|\hat{p}_{\text{KDE}}(x)-p_{0}(x)\|=O(n^{-\frac{2}{d+4}}\sqrt{(d+k)\log n}).

The proof is allocated in Section
4.4
.
Notably, the
O(n^{-\frac{2}{d+4}})
rate is known as the minimax
C^{2}
-density estimation rate under our smoothness assumption
[
37
]
. Using optimal transport inequality, Proposition 7.10 of
[
38
]
, we have

W_{1}(\hat{p}_{\text{KDE}}(x),p_{0}(x))\lesssim\|\hat{p}_{\text{KDE}}(x)-p_{0}(x)\|_{L_{\infty}}=\widetilde{O}(n^{-\frac{2}{d+4}})

Note also in our context, this indicates that
\hat{p}_{\text{KDE}}(x)
is also generative.

However, in order to use
\hat{p}_{\text{KDE}}(x)
, we need to know information of
\mathcal{M}
, which is often difficult to derive in the practical data driven setting. In view of Theorem
2.2
, we see that samples from
\hat{p}_{\text{IDM}}
can be roughly seen as
\exp_{X_{U}}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}})
, where
U
is an index randomly selected in
[n]
by the uniform distribution and
\xi_{\mathcal{T}}
is a standard
d
-dimensional Gaussian random vectors in the tangent space.

When
\mathcal{M}
is flat, i.e. a
d
dimensional subspace, it’s easy to see that
\exp_{X_{U}}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}})=X_{U}+\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}
has exactly the same distribution as
\hat{p}_{\text{KDE}}
. For general
\mathcal{M}
, the two distributions are off by
\widetilde{O}(\sigma^{2})
in
W_{1}
distance:

Proposition 2.4

(Expectation Representation of Manifold KDE Integral)
.

For sufficiently small
\sigma
, given any
X=[X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(n)}]
and Lip-1
f

\left|\int_{\mathcal{M}}\hat{p}_{\mathrm{KDE}}(x)f(x)\,dV(x)-\mathbb{E}_{U,\xi_{\mathcal{T}}}[f\left(\exp_{X_{U}}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}})\right)]\right|\leq(2(d+4)\log(2\pi/\sigma))^{\frac{3}{2}}\sigma^{2}.

The proof is allocated in Section
4.5
.

2.4
Conclusion

Combining Theorem
2.2
, Propositions
2.4
and
2.3
, it is quite straight forward to obtain the detailed version of
Theorem
1.1

Theorem 2.5
.

Under Assumption
2.1
. Choose bandwidth
h=n^{-\frac{1}{d+4}}
, let
\hat{p}_{\text{IDM}}
be the output distribution of Algorithm
1
with
Z_{0}\sim\hat{p}_{T}
, or
Z_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{D})
with
T>C(\log(Dnk))
.
There is a threshold
n_{0}
independent of
D
. If
n>n_{0}
,
then with high probability
1-n^{-k}

W_{1}(\hat{p}_{\text{IDM}},p_{0})\leq O(d^{2}k^{2}n^{-\frac{2}{d+4}}).

Here
W_{1}
is the Wasserstein-1 distance, and
O
hides some constants that depend on
\mathcal{M}
.

The proof is allocated in Section
4.6
.

4
Proofs of the theoretical results

We provide the proofs for the results in this section.

4.1
NWE truncates normal perturbation

Our first step is showing that NWE will effectively truncate perturbation in the normal tangent bundle.

Lemma 4.1
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, let
X_{(1)},\dots,X_{(n)}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}p_{0}
.
For all measurable
\displaystyle A\subseteq\mathcal{M}
,
\mu(A)=\int_{A}p_{0}(y)\,dV(y)
.
Fix any point
x\in\mathcal{M}
and radius
r>0
, define

I_{X,r}=\left\{i\in[n]:\|X_{(i)}-x\|\leq r\right\}\text{, and }p^{\prime}:=p_{0}(X\in B_{\mathcal{M}}(x,r))\asymp r^{d}.

There is some universal constant
c_{0}>0
, so that if

\log n\leq c_{0}np^{\prime}
, then for any fixed
k>0
, there exists
N_{0}=N_{0}(k)
such that for all
n\geq N_{0}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\lvert I_{X,r}\rvert-np^{\prime}\right|\leq\sqrt{2np^{\prime}(1-p^{\prime})k\log n}+\frac{2}{3}k\log n\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{k}}.

In particular, if
np^{\prime}\gg\log n
, then

\left|\lvert I_{X,r}\rvert-np^{\prime}\right|\leq\sqrt{2np^{\prime}k\log n},\quad\text{with probability at least }1-\frac{2}{n^{k}}.

Proof.

Note that

\lvert I_{X,r}\rvert=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{X_{(i)}\in B_{\mathcal{M}}(x,r)},\quad\text{where }\mathbf{1}_{X_{(i)}\in B_{\mathcal{M}}(x,r)}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathrm{Bern}(p^{\prime}).

Applying Bernstein’s inequality for Bernoulli sums, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\lvert I_{X,r}\rvert-np^{\prime}\right|\leq\sqrt{2np^{\prime}(1-p^{\prime})k\log n}+\frac{2}{3}k\log n\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{k}}.

Finally, when
np^{\prime}\gg\log n
, the first term dominates:

\sqrt{2np^{\prime}k\log n}\geq\frac{2}{3}k\log n,

so the bound simplifies to

\left|\lvert I_{X,r}\rvert-np^{\prime}\right|\leq 2\sqrt{2np^{\prime}k\log n}.

∎

Corollary 4.1.1
.

Under condition of Lemma
4.1
, if
np^{\prime}\gg 1
and
n
is sufficiently large,
with probability
\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{k}}
,
\lvert I_{X,r}\rvert\asymp np^{\prime}
.

Corollary 4.1.2
.

Under condition of Lemma
4.1
, there is a
\sigma_{0}>0
, if
\sigma<\sigma_{0}
and
n\sigma^{d}\gg 1
, then with probability
1-\sigma^{k}
,
\inf_{y\in\mathcal{M}}|I_{y,\sigma}|\geq 1.

Proof.

We pick a
\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma
-net of
\mathcal{M}
, which consists of point
\{y_{j},j\in[M_{\sigma}]\}
. The cardinality
|M_{\sigma}|\leq 2^{d}\sigma^{-d}|\mathcal{M}|
by Proposition 3 of
[
1
]
, where
|\mathcal{M}|
is the volume of the manifold. Then we apply Lemma
4.1
,
|I_{y_{j},\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma}|>1
with probability
1-n^{-(k+1)}
. So

\min_{j}\{|I_{y_{j},\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma}|>1\}
has probability
1-n^{-k}
. Finally we note that for any
y
, take
y_{j}
being the closest point in the net to it,
I_{y_{j},\frac{1}{2}\sigma}\subset I_{y,\sigma}
.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Given any fixed realization
X=[X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(n)}]
and
x\in\mathcal{M}
and
r>0
. Suppose

\inf_{y\in\mathcal{M}}|I_{y,\sigma}|\geq 1,

and
n,\sigma^{-1}
are sufficiently large.
Let
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
with decomposition
\xi=\xi_{\mathcal{T}}+\xi_{\bot}
on
T_{x}\mathcal{M}\oplus N_{x}\mathcal{M}
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|F_{X,\sigma}(x+\sigma\xi)-F_{X,\sigma}(\exp_{x}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}))\right\|\lesssim(d(k+1)\log n\,\sigma)^{2}|X\right)\geq 1-n^{-k}.

In particular,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|F_{X,\sigma}(x+\sigma\xi_{\bot})-F_{X,\sigma}(x)\right\|\lesssim(d(k+1)\log n\,\sigma)^{2}|X\right)\geq 1-n^{-k}.

Proof.

Step 1: A Pythagorean approximation.

We start by showing for
r=\sqrt{2d(k+1)\log n}\sigma,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\big|\|x+\sigma\xi

\displaystyle-X_{(i)}\|^{2}-\|x+\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}-X_{(i)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle-\|\sigma\xi_{\bot}\|^{2}\big|\lesssim\sqrt{k\log n}\,\sigma r^{2},\ \forall i\in I_{x,r}|X\bigg)\geq 1-n^{-k}.

(12)

We decompose

\displaystyle\Delta_{1,i}:

\displaystyle=\|x+\sigma\xi-X_{(i)}\|^{2}-\|x+\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}-X_{(i)}\|^{2}-\|\sigma\xi_{\bot}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\sigma\langle x+\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}-X_{(i)},\xi_{\bot}\rangle=2\sigma\langle x-X_{(i)},\xi_{\bot}\rangle.

(13)

Since
i\in I_{X,r}
,
\exists v_{i}\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}
with
\|v_{i}\|\leq 2r
so that,

X_{(i)}=\exp_{x}(v_{i})=x+v_{i}+O(\|v_{i}\|^{2}).

This indicates,

\langle x-X_{(i)},\xi_{\bot}\rangle=\langle x+v_{i}-\exp_{x}(v_{i}),\xi_{\bot}\rangle.

Note that

\|x+v_{i}-\exp_{x}(v_{i})\|=\mathcal{O}(\|v_{i}\|^{2})=\mathcal{O}(r^{2}).

Also,
\langle x+v_{i}-\exp_{x}(v_{i}),\xi_{\bot}\rangle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\|P_{\bot}(x+v_{i}-\exp_{x}(v_{i}))\|^{2})
. Here,
P_{\bot}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to N_{x}\mathcal{M}
is the orthogonal projection onto
N_{x}\mathcal{M}
.

Let
t_{i}:=\|P_{\bot}(x+v_{i}-\exp_{x}(v_{i}))\|=\mathcal{O}(r^{2})
,
q_{i}:=\langle x+v_{i}-\exp_{x}(v_{i}),\xi_{\bot}\rangle
, standard Gaussian tail inequality says

\mathbb{P}(|q_{i}|>z|X)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2t_{i}^{2}}\right).

Apply union bound for the event
\mathcal{A}=\{\max_{i\in I_{X,r}}|q_{i}|>z\}
, then for some constant
C

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}\,|\,X\right)\leq 2|I_{X,r}|\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{Cr^{2}}\right).

To have

2n\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2Cr^{4}}\right)\leq n^{-k},

It suffices to have
z\gtrsim r^{2}\sqrt{(k+1)\log n}.
Using (
13
), we have (
12
).

Step 2: Show
\|\exp_{x}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}})-X_{(i)}\|^{2}-\|x+\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}-X_{(i)}\|^{2}=\widetilde{O}_{p}(\sigma^{3})
for
i\in I_{X,r}
.

Denote the event
\mathcal{B}=\{\|\xi_{\mathcal{T}}\|\geq\sqrt{d(k+1)\log n}\}
. By Gaussian tail probability, we know
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{B}|X)\leq n^{-k}
. Note under
\mathcal{B}^{c}
, for
i\in I_{x,r}

\displaystyle|\Delta_{2,i}|

\displaystyle:=|\|\exp_{x}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}})-X_{(i)}\|^{2}-\|x+\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}-X_{(i)}\|^{2}|

\displaystyle\leq(\|\exp_{x}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}})-X_{(i)}\|+\|x+\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}-X_{(i)}\|)\|\exp_{x}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}})-x-\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}\|

\displaystyle\leq 4Cr\|\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}\|^{2}\leq 4C\sigma^{3}(d(k+1)\log n)^{3/2}.

Step 3: Showing
F_{X,\sigma}(x+\sigma\xi)=F_{X,\sigma}(\exp_{x}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}))+\widetilde{O}_{p}(\sigma^{2})
.

Denote
x^{\prime}=\exp_{x}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}})
, we note that if we let
z_{i}=\Delta_{1,i}+\Delta_{2,i}

\displaystyle k_{\sigma}(x+\sigma\xi,X_{(i)})

\displaystyle=\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x+\sigma\xi-X_{(i)}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x^{\prime}-X_{(i)}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|\xi_{\bot}\|^{2})\exp(-\frac{z_{i}}{2\sigma^{2}})

\displaystyle=\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\|\xi_{\bot}\|^{2})\exp(-\frac{z_{i}}{2\sigma^{2}})k_{\sigma}(x^{\prime},X_{(i)}).

Therefore we have

\displaystyle F_{X,\sigma}(x^{\prime})=\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}k_{\sigma}(x^{\prime},X_{(i)})X_{(i)}}{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}k_{\sigma}(x^{\prime},X_{(i)})}=\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}X_{(i)}}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}},

and

\displaystyle F_{X,\sigma}(x+\sigma\xi)=\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}k_{\sigma}(x^{\prime},X_{(i)})\exp(-\frac{z_{i}}{2\sigma^{2}})X_{(i)}}{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}k_{\sigma}(x^{\prime},X_{(i)})\exp(-\frac{z_{i}}{2\sigma^{2}})}=\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i}X_{(i)}}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i}},

where
w_{i}:=k_{\sigma}(x^{\prime},X_{(i)})
and
u_{i}:=\exp(-\frac{z_{i}}{2\sigma^{2}})
.
So

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i}X_{(i)}}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i}}-\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}X_{(i)}}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i}(X_{(i)}-x^{\prime})}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i}}-\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}(X_{(i)}-x^{\prime})}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}(X_{(i)}-x^{\prime})}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i}}-\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}(X_{(i)}-x^{\prime})}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}}\right\|+\left\|\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}(u_{i}-1)(X_{(i)}-x^{\prime})}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i}}\right\|,

Note that if
i\notin I_{x^{\prime},r}
with
r=\sqrt{2d(k+1)\log n}\sigma

w_{i}=\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x^{\prime}-X_{(i)}\|^{2})\leq\exp(-(k+1)\log n)\leq n^{-k-1}.

So

\sum_{i\notin I_{x^{\prime},r}}w_{i}\leq n^{-k},\quad\sum_{i\notin I_{x^{\prime},r}}w_{i}u_{i}\leq n^{-k},\quad\left\|\sum_{i\notin I_{x^{\prime},r}}w_{i}(X_{(i)}-x^{\prime})\right\|\leq n^{-k}\text{diam}(\mathcal{M}).

Note that for all
i\in I_{x,r}
, under
\mathcal{A}^{c}\cap\mathcal{B}^{c}
,

\max\{|u_{i}-1|,\frac{|u_{i}-1|}{u_{i}}\}\leq\frac{|z_{i}|}{2\sigma^{2}}\leq C\sigma(d(k+1)\log n)^{3/2}<1/2,

where we use the exponential ratio bound:
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
with small enough norm, we have:

\left|e^{-x}-1\right|\leq|x|.

Meanwhile by our assumption of
X
,

\sum_{i}w_{i}\geq\sum_{i\in I_{x^{\prime},\sigma}}w_{i}\geq\sum_{i\in I_{x^{\prime},\sigma}}\exp(-1/2)\geq e^{-1/2}.

Meanwhile under
\mathcal{A}^{c}\cap\mathcal{B}^{c}
,
I_{x^{\prime},\sigma}\subset I_{x,r}
,
\sum_{i}u_{i}w_{i}\geq\sum_{i\in I_{x^{\prime},\sigma}}\exp(-1/2)u_{i}\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{-1/2}
.

We bound each term using

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}(X_{(i)}-x^{\prime})}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i}}-\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}(X_{(i)}-x^{\prime})}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}(X_{(i)}-x^{\prime})\|(\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i}|u_{i}^{-1}-1|)}{(\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i})(\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}(X_{(i)}-x^{\prime})\|(\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i})\sigma(d(k+1)\log n)^{3/2}}{(\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i})(\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\|\sum_{i\in I_{x^{\prime},r}}w_{i}(X_{(i)}-x^{\prime})\|+\text{diam}(\mathcal{M})n^{-k})}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}}\sigma(d(k+1)\log n)^{3/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(r+C_{d}\text{diam}(\mathcal{M})n^{-k}\right)\sigma(d(k+1)\log n)^{3/2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2}(d(k+1)\log n)^{2}.

Similarly

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}(u_{i}-1)(X_{(i)}-x)}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{\sum_{i\in I_{x,r}}w_{i}(u_{i}-1)(X_{(i)}-x)}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i}}\right\|+\left\|\frac{\sum_{i\in I^{c}_{x,r}}w_{i}(u_{i}-1)(X_{(i)}-x)}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq r\left|\frac{\sum_{i\in I_{x,r}}w_{i}(u_{i}-1)}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i}}\right|+\frac{n^{-k}\text{diam}(\mathcal{M})}{\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}u_{i}}\leq 2e\sigma^{2}((k+1)\log n)^{2}.

Finally, note that
\xi_{\mathcal{T}}
and
\xi_{\bot}
are independent. So all the discussion above holds if we add in the condition that
\xi_{\mathcal{T}}=0
.
∎

4.2
Concentration for KE on
\mathcal{M}

We will refer to functions of form

\widehat{f}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{\sigma}(x,X(i))f(X(i))

a kernelised estimator (KE) for function
f
. Notably, the NWE (
9
) is the ratio of KE for
f(x)=x
and
f(x)\equiv 1
, and the manifold KDE is the KE for
f(x)\equiv 1
.

Our second step is studying that KE on the manifold. We note that
[
28
]
has already proved the convergence of manifold KDE in
L_{2}
norm.

Lemma 4.3
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, suppose
X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(n)}
are i.i.d. samples from
p_{0}
. Denote
K(r)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}e^{-r^{2}/2}
so that
k_{\sigma}(x,X_{(i)})=\frac{1}{\sigma^{d}}K\left(\frac{\|x-X_{(i)}\|}{\sigma}\right)
.
Given a
C^{2}
function
f
,
let the kernelized regressor at a point
x\in\mathcal{M}
with bandwidth
\sigma>0
can be written as:

\widehat{f}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}k_{\sigma}(x,X_{(i)})f(X_{(i)}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d}}K\left(\frac{\|x-X_{(i)}\|}{\sigma}\right)f(X_{(i)}),

where
\|x-y\|
is the distance in the
\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
Then the pointwise bias

b(x):=\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]-p_{0}(x)f(x)

satisfies
b(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\Delta(f(x)p_{0}(x))+o(\sigma^{2})=O(\sigma^{2})
.

Proof.

Denote
q(x)=p_{0}(x)f(x)
.
We analyze the pointwise bias of the kernel density estimator:

\text{Bias}(x):=\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]-q(x)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d}}K\left(\frac{\|x-y\|}{\sigma}\right)[q(y)-q(x)]\,dV(y).

Split the integral into a local and a tail region:

\displaystyle\text{Bias}(x)=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int_{B_{\sigma\gamma}(x)}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d}}K\left(\frac{\|x-y\|}{\sigma}\right)[q(y)-q(x)]dV(y)}_{\text{Bias}_{\text{local}}(x)}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{M}\setminus B_{\sigma\gamma}(x)}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d}}K\left(\frac{\|x-y\|}{\sigma}\right)[q(y)-q(x)]dV(y)}_{\text{Bias}_{\text{tail}}(x)},

where
\gamma
will be set later.

Step 1: tail Term.

We estimate the tail bias term directly using the exponential decay of the Gaussian kernel and the compactness of
\mathcal{M}
.
From the definition:

\text{Bias}_{\text{tail}}(x):=\int_{\mathcal{M}\setminus B_{\sigma\gamma}(x)}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d}}K\left(\frac{\|x-y\|}{\sigma}\right)[q(y)-q(x)]\,dV(y).

Since
q
is
C^{2}
on compact
\mathcal{M}
, it is uniformly bounded, so there exists
C_{1}>0
such that:

|q(y)-q(x)|\leq C_{1}\quad\text{for all }y\in\mathcal{M}.

For
y\in\mathcal{M}\setminus B_{\sigma\gamma}(x)
, we have
\|x-y\|\geq\sigma\gamma
, so the Gaussian kernel satisfies:

K\left(\frac{\|x-y\|}{\sigma}\right)\leq(2\pi)^{-d/2}\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\right).

Thus, the entire integrand is uniformly bounded on the integration domain:

\left|\frac{1}{\sigma^{d}}K\left(\frac{\|x-y\|}{\sigma}\right)[q(y)-q(x)]\right|\leq\frac{C_{2}}{\sigma^{d}}\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\right).

Hence,

|\text{Bias}_{\text{tail}}(x)|\leq\frac{C_{2}}{\sigma^{d}}\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\right)\cdot\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{M}).

Now set
\gamma=R\sqrt{\log(1/\sigma)}
, and then:

\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\right)=\sigma^{R^{2}/2},

so we have:

|\text{Bias}_{\text{tail}}(x)|\leq C_{3}\cdot\sigma^{-d}\cdot\sigma^{R^{2}/2}=C_{3}\cdot\sigma^{R^{2}/2-d}.

Choosing
R^{2}>2d+4
, we obtain:

|\text{Bias}_{\text{tail}}(x)|=O(\sigma^{3}),

which shows that the tail contribution is negligible compared to the leading order bias.

Step 2: local Term.

In geodesic normal coordinates centered at
x
, set

y=\exp_{x}(\sigma u),\quad u\in T_{x}\mathcal{M},\quad\|u\|\leq\gamma,

then:

r(u):=\frac{1}{\sigma}(\|x-y\|-\sigma\|u\|)\leq C\sigma^{2}\gamma^{3},\quad dV(x)=\sigma^{d}\sqrt{\det g_{ij}(\sigma u)}\,du.

Use the expansion with
x_{u}
being a point along
\exp_{x}(\sigma s),s\in[0,u]

[
4
]
,

q(\exp_{x}(\sigma u))=q(x)+\sigma\nabla q(x)\cdot u+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}u^{\top}\nabla^{2}q(x_{u})u+O(\sigma^{3}).

Here
\nabla q(x)
is the
Riemannian gradient
of
q
at
x
, i.e., the unique vector in
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
such that for all
v\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}
,
\langle\nabla q(x),v\rangle=D_{v}q(x),
where
D_{v}q(x)
is the directional derivative of
q
at
x
along
v
and
\nabla^{2}q(x_{u})
is the
Riemannian Hessian
of
q
at the point
x_{u}
, defined via the Levi-Civita connection that

\nabla^{2}q(x_{u})(v,w)=\langle\nabla_{v}\nabla q,w\rangle\big|_{x_{u}},\text{ for }v,w\in T_{x_{u}}\mathcal{M}.

From the expansion of the metric tensor in normal coordinates (see page 91 of
[
5
]
):

g_{ij}(\sigma u)=\delta_{ij}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{3}\sum_{k,l}R_{ikjl}(x)u^{k}u^{l},

where
R_{ikjl}(x)
is the Riemann curvature tensor at point
x
in normal coordinates.
Applying the determinant identity
\sqrt{\det(I+A)}=1+\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}(A)+O(\|A\|^{2})
, we find:

\operatorname{tr}\left(-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{3}R_{ikjl}u^{k}u^{l}\right)=-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{3}\operatorname{Ric}_{ij}(x)u^{i}u^{j}=-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{3}\operatorname{Ric}_{x}(u,u).

Therefore:

\sqrt{\det g_{ij}(\sigma u)}=1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{6}\operatorname{Ric}_{x}(u,u)+O(\sigma^{3})=1+O(\sigma^{2}).

Also, since:

|K(\|u\|+r(u))-K(\|u\|)|\leq C|r(u)|\leq C\sigma^{2}\gamma^{3},

we obtain:

\displaystyle\text{Bias}_{\text{local}}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\|u\|\leq\gamma}K(\|u\|)\left[\sigma\nabla q(x)\cdot u+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}u^{\top}\nabla^{2}q(x_{u})u\right]du.+O(\sigma^{3})

By symmetry of the Gaussian kernel:

\int_{\|u\|\leq\gamma}K(\|u\|)u\,du=0,\quad\int_{\|u\|\leq\gamma}K(\|u\|)u_{i}u_{j}\,du=\mu_{2}(\gamma)\delta_{ij},

where

\mu_{2}(\gamma):=\int_{\|u\|\leq\gamma}K(\|u\|)u_{1}^{2}\,du.

Since:

\mu_{2}(\gamma)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(\|u\|)u_{1}^{2}\,du=\mu_{2},

since
\|\nabla^{2}q(x_{u})-\nabla^{2}q(x)\|=o(1)
, we have:

\text{Bias}_{\text{local}}(x)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\mu_{2}\Delta q(x)+o(\sigma^{2}).

Combining both terms, we obtain:

\text{Bias}(x):=\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]-q(x)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta q(x)+o(\sigma^{2}).

∎

Lemma 4.4
.

With the same assumption as lemma
4.3
, then there exists a constant
C_{v}>0
such that the pointwise variance of
\hat{f}(x)
satisfy:

\mathrm{Var}[\hat{f}_{KDE}(x)]\leq\frac{C_{v}}{n\sigma^{d}}.

Proof.

Since

\hat{f}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{\sigma}(x,X_{(i)})f(X_{(i)}),\quad\text{where }k_{\sigma}(x,X_{(i)})=\frac{1}{\sigma^{d}}K\left(\frac{\|x-X_{(i)}\|}{\sigma}\right).

Then

\mathrm{Var}[\hat{f}(x)]=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Var}[k_{\sigma}(x,X_{(1)})f(X_{(1)})]\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[k_{\sigma}^{2}(x,X_{(1)}){f^{2}(X_{(1)})}].

We compute

\mathbb{E}[k_{\sigma}^{2}(x,X_{(1)})f^{2}(X_{(1)})]=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{d}}K\left(\frac{\|x-y\|}{\sigma}\right)\right)^{2}f^{2}(y)p_{0}(y)\,dV(y).

Split the integral into two parts:

\int_{\mathcal{M}}=\int_{B_{\mathcal{M}}(x,R\sigma)}+\int_{\mathcal{M}\setminus B_{\mathcal{M}}(x,R\sigma)}=:I_{\text{local}}+I_{\text{tail}}.

For the tail region, Gaussian tail decay gives

K\left(\frac{\|x-y\|}{\sigma}\right)^{2}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\leq e^{-R^{2}},

and that
|f^{2}p_{0}(x)|\leq C_{1}
on
\mathcal{M}
, so

I_{\text{tail}}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\sigma^{2d}}e^{-R^{2}}\cdot\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{M})=O(\sigma^{-2d}e^{-R^{2}}).

Choosing
R^{2}=(d+c)\log(1/\sigma)\Rightarrow e^{-R^{2}}=\sigma^{d+c}
, we have
I_{\text{tail}}=O(\sigma^{-d-c}).

Now for the local region, use the exponential map
x=\exp_{y}(\sigma z)
, with
z\in B(0,R)\subset T_{y}\mathcal{M}
. Then:

r(z):=\frac{1}{\sigma}(\|x-y\|-\sigma\|z\|)\leq C\sigma^{2}R^{3},\quad dV(x)=\sigma^{d}\sqrt{\det g_{ij}(\sigma z)}\,dz.

and use the expansion,

|\sqrt{\det g_{ij}(\sigma z)}-1|\leq C\sigma^{2},

as well as:

|K(\|z\|+r(z))-K(\|z\|)|\leq C|r(z)|\leq C\sigma^{2}R^{3}.

Hence,

\int_{B_{\mathcal{M}}(y,R\sigma)}K^{2}\left(\frac{\|x-y\|}{\sigma}\right)dV(x)=\sigma^{d}\int_{B(0,R)}K^{2}(\|z\|)(1+O(\sigma^{2}))\,dz=\sigma^{d}\omega_{d}+O(\sigma^{d+2}),

where
\omega_{d}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K^{2}(\|z\|)dz
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[k_{\sigma}^{2}(x,X_{(1)})]\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\sigma^{2d}}(\sigma^{d}\omega_{d}+O(\sigma^{d+2}))=\frac{C_{1}\omega_{d}}{\sigma^{d}}+O(\sigma^{-d-2}).

Therefore, we obtain

\mathrm{Var}[\hat{f}(x)]\leq\frac{C_{v}}{n\sigma^{d}},\quad\text{with }C_{v}:=C_{1}\omega_{d}.

∎

|\int_{\mathcal{M}}\hat{p}_{\mathrm{KDE}}(x)f(x)\,dV(x)-\mathbb{E}_{U,\xi_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[f\left(\exp_{X_{U}}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}})\right)\right|\leq(2(d+4)\log(2\pi/\sigma))^{\frac{3}{2}}\sigma^{2}.

Proof.

Pick any Lip-1
f
. Without loss of generality, we assume
f(x_{0})=0
for some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
. Then we have
f(x)\leq\text{diam}(\mathcal{M})
. We start by

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\hat{p}_{\text{KDE}}(x)f(x)\,dV(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d}}K\left(\frac{\|x-X_{(i)}\|}{\sigma}\right)f(x)\,dV(x)=:\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}.

We split the integral into a local and a tail region:

I_{i}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d}}K\left(\frac{\|x-X_{(i)}\|}{\sigma}\right)f(x)\,dV(x)=I_{\text{local}}+I_{\text{tail}},

where
I_{\text{local}}
integrates over
B_{\sigma\gamma}(X_{(i)})\subset\mathcal{M}
, a radius
\sigma\gamma
neighborhood around
X_{(i)}
in
\mathcal{M}
, and
I_{\text{tail
}}
is the integral over the complement. Here
\gamma
is chosen as
\sqrt{2(d+4)|\log(2\pi/\sigma)|}

Using the exponential decay of the Gaussian kernel and compactness of
\mathcal{M}
, and the Lipschitz-ness of
f
,
the tail term satisfies:

|I_{\text{tail}}|\leq C\cdot(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{-d}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\right)=O(\sigma^{3}).

We consider the integral:

I_{\text{local}}:=\int_{B_{\sigma\gamma}(X_{(i)})\cap\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d}}K\left(\frac{\|x-X_{(i)}\|^{2}}{\sigma}\right)f(x)\,dV(x),

and restrict to a small neighborhood
B_{\sigma\gamma}(X_{(i)})
around
X_{(i)}
, where the exponential map is a diffeomorphism. We change variables to geodesic normal coordinates centered at
X_{(i)}
, writing:

x=\exp_{X_{(i)}}(\sigma u),\quad u\in T_{X_{(i)}}\mathcal{M}\cong\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Under this map, we have:

r(u):=\frac{1}{\sigma}(\|x-X_{(i)}\|-\sigma\|u\|)\leq C\sigma^{2}\gamma^{3},\quad dV(x)=\sigma^{d}\sqrt{\det g_{ij}(\sigma u)}\,du.

Hence, the integral becomes:

I_{\text{local}}=\int_{\|u\|\leq\gamma}K(\|u\|+r(u))f(\exp_{X_{(i)}}(\sigma u))\sqrt{\det g_{ij}(\sigma u)}\,du.

We use the expansion:

|\sqrt{\det g_{ij}(\sigma u)}-1|\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{6}\operatorname{Ric}_{X_{(i)}}(u,u)+o(\sigma^{2})\leq C\sigma^{2},

as well as

|K(\|u\|+r(u))-K(\|u\|)|\leq C|r(u)|\leq C\sigma^{2}\gamma^{3}.

So we can conclude that

\left|I_{i}-\int_{\|u\|\leq\gamma}K(\|u\|)f(\exp_{X_{(i)}}(\sigma u))\,du\right|\leq C\sigma^{2}\gamma^{3}.

(14)

On the other hand,

\mathbb{E}_{U,\xi_{\mathcal{T}}}[f(\exp_{X_{U}}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}))]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{\mathcal{T}}}[f(\exp_{X_{(i)}}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}))],

since
U\sim\mathrm{Unif}([n])
and
\xi_{\mathcal{T}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
is independent of
U
. Each expectation over
\xi_{\mathcal{T}}
is equivalent to

\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{\mathcal{T}}}[f(\exp_{X_{(i)}}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}))]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\exp_{X_{(i)}}(\sigma u))K(\|u\|)du.

Compare with (
14
), we can reach our conclusion when we note by
\chi^{2}_{d}
tail bound,

\displaystyle\int_{u:\|u\|\geq\gamma}f(\exp_{X_{(i)}}(\sigma u))K(\|u\|)du

\displaystyle\leq\text{diam}(\mathcal{M})\int_{u:\|u\|\geq\gamma}K(\|u\|)du

\displaystyle\leq\text{diam}(\mathcal{M})\gamma^{2}\exp(-\gamma^{2}/2)\leq C\sigma^{2}\gamma^{3}.

Thus, the KDE integral and the Gaussian expectation differ only by
O(\sigma^{2}\gamma^{3})
, and we may write:

\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{\mathcal{T}}}[f(\exp_{X_{(i)}}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}))]=I_{i}+O(\sigma^{2}\gamma^{3}).

Taking expectation over
U\sim\mathrm{Unif}([n])
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{U,\xi_{\mathcal{T}}}[f(\exp_{X_{U}}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}))]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{\mathcal{T}}}[f(\exp_{X_{(i)}}(\sigma\xi_{\mathcal{T}}))]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}+O(\sigma^{2}\gamma^{3}),

establishing the desired correspondence and completing the proof.

∎

Lemma 4.10
.

If we start a diffusion process from
Z_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{D})
with
T=k(\log n+\log D+\log(\text{diam}(\mathcal{M})))
. Then it can be coupled with a diffusion process from
Z^{\prime}_{0}\sim\hat{p}_{T}
exactly, i.e.
Z_{t}=Z^{\prime}_{t},t\in[0,T]
, with probability
1-n^{-k}
.

Proof.

We first note that by convexity of KL divergence,

\displaystyle\text{KL}(\hat{p}_{T}\|\mathcal{N}(0,I_{D}))

\displaystyle=\text{KL}(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{T}X_{i},\sigma_{T}^{2}I_{D})\|\mathcal{N}(0,I_{D}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{KL}(\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{T}X_{i},\sigma_{T}^{2}I_{D})\|\mathcal{N}(0,I_{D})).

Meanwhile,

\displaystyle\text{KL}(N(\alpha_{T}X_{i},\sigma_{T}^{2}I_{D})\|N(0,I_{D}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha^{2}_{T}\|X_{i}\|^{2}+D(\sigma_{T}^{2}-1-\log\sigma_{T}^{2}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}(e^{-2T}\text{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}+De^{-2T}).

So choosing
T=k(\log n+\log D+\log(\text{diam}(\mathcal{M}))
, by Pinsker inequality

\text{TV}(\hat{p}_{T},N(0,I_{D}))\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\text{KL}(\hat{p}_{T}\|\mathcal{N}(0,I_{D}))}\leq n^{-k}.

Let

and

be two samples from
\hat{p}_{T}
and
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{D})
with the optimal coupling. Then
{\mathbb{P}}(Z_{0}\neq Z^{\prime}_{0})\leq n^{-k}
. Moreover, their DM trajectory will be exactly the same, i.e.
{\mathbb{P}}(\widehat{Z}_{t}\neq\widehat{Z}^{\prime}_{t})\leq n^{-k}
.
∎

Proof of Proposition

.

Now, we calculate the gradient of

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{t}(x)}\nabla p_{t}(x),

\displaystyle-\nabla p_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\frac{x-\alpha_{t}y}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-\alpha_{t}y\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)p_{0}(dy).
