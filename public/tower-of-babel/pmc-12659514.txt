Improving employer and employee well-being in small and medium-sized enterprises in Flanders: a realist evaluation protocol of the FitBonus program
Authors: Fleur Van De Wielle, Robin Vandecasteele, Vera Verbestel, Maïté Verloigne, Benedicte Deforche, Rik Crutzen, Els Clays
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh levels of absenteeism, presenteeism, and productivity loss are significant issues, especially for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), which form a large part of the workforce in Flanders. The FitBonus program, commissioned by the Flemish Government, aims to improve the physical, mental, and social well-being of employers and employees in SMEs. This protocol outlines the methodology for a realist evaluation of the FitBonus program to understand how, why, for whom and under what circumstances the program enhances overall well-being and perceived workability among employers and employees in SMEs in Flanders.MethodsThe evaluation is conducted in three phases: Phase 1 involves developing an Initial Program Theory (IPT) through stakeholder consultations, program documentation review, and literature review. Phase 2 validates the IPT using a mix of quantitative data from online surveys and qualitative data from interviews and focus groups. Phase 3 refines the IPT based on the findings, resulting in a detailed theory and practical recommendations.DiscussionThis realist evaluation protocol aims to elucidate the mechanisms and contextual factors influencing the FitBonus program’s potential success in improving physical, mental, and social well-being, as well as perceived workability. The insights gained from this evaluation will offer actionable recommendations for optimizing the program’s implementation and effectiveness. Additionally, by enhancing the understanding of how well-being interventions work in various organizational contexts, this study can inform the development of future workplace well-being programs, leading to more targeted and effective interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24091-3.Keywords: Research design, Realist evaluation, Realist evaluation protocol, FitBonus program, CMO-configurations, Mixed-methods, Workplace wellbeing, Employers, Employees, Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs)

Background

High levels of absenteeism, presenteeism, and productivity loss are significant issues that many organizations face, leading to substantial economic costs [1–5]. Absenteeism alone costs employers in the European Union billions of euros annually, while presenteeism exacerbates productivity losses as employees remain physically present but mentally disengaged [1, 2, 5].

Therefore, effective interventions that promote employee well-being, which encompasses physical, mental and social dimensions [4], are essential. Interventions aimed at cultivating a healthy lifestyle among employees – including promoting physical activity, balanced nutrition, and robust mental health and social support programs– have consistently shown to enhance workability and productivity and overall employee well-being [6–14].

The workplace serves as an ideal setting for implementing well-being interventions due to the significant amount of time employees spend there, making it feasible to target large groups efficiently [4]. Interventions that target various dimensions of well-being (e.g.: physical, mental and social), have been shown to be more effective in enhancing overall well-being compared to interventions that only target one dimension of well-being, as they address the multifaceted nature of well-being more comprehensively [10, 15]. Moreover, interventions that encompass the entire organization, addressing both individual and organizational factors, tend to yield superior long-term benefits [16]. By embedding health and well-being initiatives within the organizational structure, sustained support and engagement across all workforce levels can be aimed for.

Workplace interventions are often evaluated with a primary focus on effectiveness, considering randomized controlled trials (RCTs) as the gold standard for this purpose (e.g. [12, 17]). While these studies frequently demonstrate positive intervention outcomes, they fall short in elucidating the underlying mechanisms that contribute to their effectiveness and thus fail to capture the interventions’ complexity [10, 11, 18–22]. Therefore, it is essential to adopt evaluation methods that not only assess effectiveness but also uncover the contributing factors and contextual elements that determine the success of an intervention [20, 23, 24]. One approach that receives considerable attention in this regard is the realist evaluation approach. The primary aim of a realist evaluation, an approach developed by Pawson and Tilley in the 1990s, is to address the question “what works for whom in what circumstances and how?” [20, 24], in contrast to merely asking “what works?“, which is characteristic of a traditional randomized controlled trial design ([20, 25]). The realist evaluation approach allows for gaining insight into the ‘black box’ of real-life workplace interventions [20, 26]. A central assumption within a realist evaluation is that a given intervention, e.g. in the workplace, does not always work for everyone across all contexts. Interventions “work” by empowering participants to make different choices regarding, for example, their lifestyle [23]. However, for participants to make and sustain different choices, a change in their reasoning and/or available resources is required [23, 27]. This change in participant’s reasoning in response to the resources offered by the intervention is what enables an intervention to work. Within the realist evaluation approach, this is defined as a “mechanism” [23, 24, 27, 28]. Moreover, interventions “work” in different ways for different participants, as different mechanisms can be triggered depending on the context in which the intervention is implemented. The context can be defined as features of the conditions in which interventions are introduced that shape participant’s reasoning in response to the intervention resources [24, 29]. Certain contextual factors may facilitate the activation of specific mechanisms, while others may prevent them from being triggered. Consequently, there is a constant interaction between contexts and mechanisms, influencing the intervention’s outcomes. In a realist evaluation, the interplay between context, mechanisms, and outcomes of an intervention is captured within CMO-configurations. CMO-configurations are hypotheses about how different components of an intervention are supposed to work and what the expected results are [24]. The CMO-configurations formulated for an intervention are aggregated in a program theory within the framework of a realist evaluation. Developing and reporting this program theory allows for a comprehensive understanding of the intervention under evaluation, clarifying how the intervention is expected to function and its potential impacts [22]. To better account for the degree to which individuals engage with an intervention, the traditional CMO-framework was extended in this study by adding a fourth element: Uptake. This results in what is referred to as CUMO-configurations later in this manuscript. The inclusion of uptake serves to explicitly capture the extent to which participants choose to engage with the intervention, which can vary considerably across individuals.

Setting

The Department of Labour and Social Economy of the Flemish Government has commissioned the Belgian company “Golazo Energy” to develop and implement a well-being-promoting program called “FitBonus”. According to the latest assessment conducted by the Foundation for Innovation and Labour in 2023, the workability rate of jobs in Flanders was 51.8% [30]. The workability rate refers to the extent to which jobs meet criteria for being manageable and sustainable for workers, including factors such as work-related stress, work-life balance, learning opportunities and job satisfaction. Through this initiative, the government aims to enhance the well-being of employers and employees of small and medium-sized organizations (SMEs) in Flanders, thereby creating a resilient workforce and improving perceived workability. Workplace well-being interventions are particularly important for SMEs, as a significant portion of the working population is employed in these organizations. In Flanders, 713,076 of the 714,083 enterprises are SMEs, employing a substantial portion of the workforce [31, 32]. Due to their limited financial and time resources, individual SMEs often struggle to develop and implement health promotion initiatives on their own [33–35]. To address this challenge, the government has commissioned the development of the FitBonus program as a comprehensive intervention that can be implemented across multiple SMEs. This approach helps to ensure that SMEs benefit from well-being initiatives without each organization needing to individually invest in and develop such programs. Moreover, SMEs are more susceptible to productivity losses due to their employees’ exposure to mental health risks [33]. However, research on this specific type of organizations is limited. The FitBonus well-being program addresses both physical, mental and social aspects of well-being, based on the idea that well-being is a holistic concept [4] and that multidimensional interventions are more effective than unidimensional interventions [10, 11].

The program

The FitBonus program exists of various program components: kick-off event, workshops, Q&A sessions, Energy Scan, sports events, podcasts, diary, emails, and an online platform/app. A detailed overview of the program and a full description of the program components can be found in Fig. 1 and Additional file 1, Appendix 1 respectively. The FitBonus program has a total duration of nine months and is structured into three distinct well-being dimensions: physical, mental and social well-being. Within each dimension, the program focuses on three topics relevant to the respective type of well-being. Specifically, the physical well-being dimension emphasizes physical activity, sleep and nutrition, while the mental well-being dimension addresses concentration, stress management, and resilience. Lastly, the social well-being dimension focuses on trust, connectedness, and coping with differences within the organization.

Overview of the FitBonus program

FitBonus mainly focuses on the individual level and is based on “subject empowerment”, as the central concepts underpinning the program are inspiration, reflection, and education. Subject empowerment is often utilized in healthcare settings and aims at increasing the capacity of subjects to think critically and make autonomous, informed decisions [36]. Following this approach, FitBonus is primarily designed to inspire individuals about the topics covered in the program, encouraging them to reflect on these topics, make informed decisions and effect positive changes in relation to them, thereby improving well-being and workability at the individual level.

In addition to its individual focus, the FitBonus program also addresses the organizational level, aiming to positively influence the well-being climate within organizations and encourage systematic investment in the domains of physical, mental, and social well-being. An organizational well-being climate can be defined as “employee perceptions of coworker relationships, policies, and social norms that support optimizing well-being” ([34], p. 1). From the perspective of Complex Adaptive Systems (CAS), individual actions within an organization are interconnected, meaning that changes in one individual can influence the broader system [37, 38]. Therefore, by improving individual well-being, the program can create positive ripple effects that enhance the overall organizational climate. To facilitate this, various components of the program were designed to be conducted in groups or within the organizational setting (e.g., with colleagues), and employers received guidance on how to optimally implement FitBonus in the workplace. Additionally, enrollment in the FitBonus program was exclusively facilitated through employers or department heads, ensuring that decisions regarding participation in the program were consistently made at the organizational level.

The FitBonus program will be implemented and evaluated twice. The second edition of the program will be implemented with the same overarching design, objectives, and core content across both periods. However, adjustments to the program in the second addition might be in place based on the experiences from the implementation and evaluation of the first edition.

Study population

Recruitment of participants for both editions of FitBonus was carried out by Golazo Energy through various communication channels, including campaigns on social media and LinkedIn pages of the Flemish Government, the Department of Work and Social Economy, and Golazo Energy itself. Additionally, Golazo Energy’s own media channels, such as newsletters, were utilized to reach potential participants. A FitBonus website was launched where interested individuals could find all necessary information and register for participation in the program.

Registration was open to all SMEs in Flanders, with participants required to sign a participation agreement upon registration, stating their organization’s consent for participation in the program. Golazo Energy was responsible for the final selection of participants, first ensuring that all participants were indeed employed in an SME, in accordance with the legal requirements of SME status, and that they were between the ages of 18 and 65. Potential participants who did not meet these inclusion criteria were excluded. Subsequently, participant selection followed a “first come, first served” principle, with attention to ensuring balanced representation of participants across all provinces in Flanders, proportional to the size of each province.

Participants were selected as part of the program. Across both editions, a total of 3500 individuals were selected to participate in the FitBonus program, with 2500 selected for the first edition and 1000 for the second edition. The difference in participant numbers between the two editions stems from logistical considerations. The number of participants was determined by the Department of Labor and Social Economy, aiming to reach as many individuals within the target group as possible, considering available resources and budget needed to execute a guidance program addressing the central aspects of physical, mental and social well-being.

The first edition of the FitBonus program received significant interest, resulting in more registrations than the available resources could accommodate. Consequently, individuals not selected for the first edition were placed on a waiting list for the second edition and served as a comparison group for the first edition. In the second edition of the program, no comparison group will be present.

Methods

Aims

The overall aim of this paper is to describe the protocol of the evaluation of the FitBonus program using a realist evaluation approach. The primary objective of conducting the realist evaluation of FitBonus is to understand which components of the program are effective, for whom, how, and under which circumstances. In essence, this evaluation aims to explore how specific contextual variables activate particular mechanisms and, subsequently, result in specific outcomes within the scope of the FitBonus program.

Procedure

To conduct the realist evaluation of the FitBonus program, a three-phase procedure was developed (Fig. 2). The first phase involved the development of an initial program theory (IPT). The second phase involved the validation of this theory through both data collection (phase 2a) and data analysis (phase 2b). The final phase involved theory refinement based on data interpretation.

Overview of the evaluation procedure for the realist evaluation of the FitBonus program. CUMO = Context-Uptake-Mechanism-Outcome

Although these phases are presented sequentially for clarity and structure, it is important to emphasize that realist evaluation is inherently a cyclic and iterative process. Data collection, analysis, and theorizing are interwoven, and new insights may prompt a return to earlier stages or even additional data collection. This phased representation aligns with the realist logic of inquiry, as it helps clarify the steps taken from theory development to theory refinement [24].

Figure 2 shows the different phases of the realist evaluation, each of which will be elaborated upon in the following sections.

Phase 1: Initial program theory development

The first phase of the realist evaluation of the FitBonus program involved the development of an initial program theory (IPT). This theory is an initial conceptual model that includes hypotheses (i.e. CMO-configurations) about how the program may operate, in which contexts the program should work, which mechanisms should be triggered, and which outcomes should be achieved [24, 39].

A first preliminary version of the IPT was developed by the research center of the Department of Labor and Social Economics of the Flemish Government. The preliminary IPT was then further developed and refined by the researchers of this evaluation study. The refinement of the IPT was informed by three primary sources: a) interaction with key stakeholders, b) analysis of program documentation and c) analysis of the literature [22, 40]. Firstly, stakeholders (i.e., the developer of the preliminary IPT and the program developer and implementer) were involved in this process. Informal interaction with the Department of Labor and Social Economics took place to gain a deeper understanding of the IPT prototype. This involved communication with the coordinators of the project within the Department, primarily through email correspondence and online meetings. As a result, key constructs of the IPT prototype, such as the targeted outcomes (physical, mental, and social well-being; workability; and organizational well-being climate), and the communication of change agents as a mechanism (see “Initial program theory of the FitBonus program at organizational level”) were retained in the final and refined IPT. Subsequently, stakeholder interaction with Golazo Energy, the developer and implementer of the FitBonus program, further enhanced insight into and understanding of the program. This interaction occurred primarily through email correspondence, online meetings, and face-to-face meetings with the project coordinator within Golazo Energy. In parallel, a comprehensive analysis of program documentation was conducted to deepen understanding of the program. This analysis facilitated the identification of potential mechanisms and contextual variables, which were incorporated into the final IPT. Furthermore, a thorough literature review was undertaken to complement the stakeholder interaction and document analysis, providing additional theoretical insights and evidence to support the IPT configurations. This literature review involved searching academic databases such as PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar using relevant keywords and phrases. The specific keywords and phrases used included “workplace wellness interventions”, “employee well-being”, “physical well-being initiatives in the workplace”, “mental well-being initiatives in the workplace”, “social well-being at work”, “small and medium-sized enterprises health initiatives”, “holistic employee well-being programs”, and “workability improvement programs”. Studies were selected based on their relevance, publication date (< 20 years), and the quality and robustness of their methodologies. Robustness and relevance were assessed based on general academic standards, the expertise within the research team and citation frequency of the sources.

Since the FitBonus program is expected to produce outcomes at both the individual and organizational levels, two separate yet interconnected program theories were developed: one focused on individual participants, and one on the broader organizational context. While integration into a single model was considered, the distinction between levels allows us to (a) reflect the dual-target nature of the intervention, (b) account for uptake both as individual agency and as embedded in organizational structures and (c) explore under which circumstances individual-level changes might, or might not, translate into organizational-level outcomes. The procedure described above was followed for the development of both IPTs.

Initial program theory of the FitBonus program at individual level (Fig. 3)

Initial program theory of the FitBonus program at individual level

Since FitBonus emphasizes “subject empowerment” [36], the program theory at the individual level expects enhancements in the knowledge, motivation, and skills (M1) related to the topics covered in the program, which are key determinants of behavior [41]. The program theory further anticipates that enhancing these determinants will result in positive changes in lifestyle behaviors (M2) [42–44], subsequently increasing the physical and mental well-being of participants (O1, O2).

Secondly, it is anticipated that the FitBonus program will trigger a sense of community (M3) among colleagues, since various components of the program are designed to be implemented in groups or within the organizational setting. According to the program theory, this sense of community directly results in improved social well-being (O3) and indirectly contributes to enhancements in all types of well-being (O1, O2, O3) through increased social capital (M4). Social capital in the workplace is a broad concept, but in this study we focus on the cognitive elements of employees’ individual perceptions of trust and reciprocity within their team or department—both horizontally among colleagues and vertically between staff and management [45–47]. Finally, an improvement in well-being is expected to lead to an enhancement in perceived workability (O4) [7, 48, 49].

The finalized IPT of the evaluation of the FitBonus program consists of CUMO-configurations instead of CMO-configurations [50]. The additional ‘U’ in this configuration stands for ‘Uptake’ of the program by individual participants. It should be noted that whenever “uptake” is mentioned in this manuscript, it always refers to uptake at the individual level and never at the organizational level. Program uptake was included in the configurations as the FitBonus program allows each participant to decide the extent to which they want to engage in the program, meaning the implementation depends on the personal initiative of participants within organizations to engage in the program. Consequently, it is expected that program uptake will influence both the emergence of knowledge, motivation and skills (M1) and the emergence of a sense of community (M3) within the organization. Furthermore, this relationship between uptake and these mechanisms is likely to also be influenced by contextual factors, suggesting that the effectiveness of the triggered mechanisms, and ultimately the program outcomes, may vary depending on the specific contexts in which the program is implemented. For instance, a supportive organizational culture may enhance the uptake of program activities, thereby increasing the likelihood of positive outcomes, whereas a lack of such support could impede the program’s success.

Finally, this program theory predicts that contextual variables, both at the individual (C1) and at the organizational level (C2), will influence how the FitBonus program operates. Research shows that socio-economic and demographic characteristics (e.g.: SES, age, gender) affect health behaviors and outcomes [51–55]. Additionally, organizational characteristics, including structural (e.g.: size) and cultural factors (e.g.: organizational support), play a crucial role in shaping the effectiveness of workplace interventions [56–58].

Initial program theory of the FitBonus program at organizational level (Fig. 4)

Initial program theory of the FitBonus program at organizational level

In the program theory at the organizational level, communication behavior of change agents (M5) is highlighted as a mechanism through which the FitBonus program may influence the organizational well-being climate. Change agents can be defined as “individuals who make a deliberate effort to improve a system’s functioning” ([59], p.1), and in the case of FitBonus, this role may be taken on by participating employees who actively engage with the program and influence their peers.

More specifically, this mechanism refers to the use of communicative strategies such as sharing personal experiences, offering informal encouragement, initiating peer networks, and celebrating small successes [60], as well as autonomy-supportive communication and reflective listening [59]. These forms of communication can function as social resources that, depending on the organizational context, shift how others think about and engage with well-being practices.

It is expected that such communication can influence the well-being climate in the organization, which can be defined as “employee perceptions of coworker relationships, policies, and social norms that support optimizing well-being” [34]. Although evidence is still relatively scarce, research shows that offering well-being training to employees who act as change agents is an effective method to bring about changes in the entire organization [60, 61]. This aligns with the principles of CAS, which suggest that individual actions can influence broader organizational dynamics, leading to systemic change [37]. Additionally, an indirect influence of communication from change agents on the well-being climate in the organization is expected through an enhanced sense of community (M3). By offering a program within a group of change agents in the organization, a participatory process can be initiated, and a sense of community can be fostered, which is an important element in driving change in organizations [62]. Shared experiences within a group can further ensure that the change becomes embedded in the organization and thus becomes part of the climate [63].

Similar to the IPT at individual level, it is expected that the level of program uptake will influence the extent to which change agents exhibit communication behavior towards change recipients. Additionally, both individual (C1) and organizational (C2) contextual factors are taken into account in this program theory.

Based on these IPT’s, data collection tools were developed and used in phase 2 of the realist evaluation.

Phase 2: Theory validation

The second phase of the realist evaluation of the FitBonus program involves theory validation, divided into two subphases: (a) data collection and (b) data analysis. Both processes will be used to assess the validity and accuracy of the IPT. This phase is ongoing at the time of writing this protocol paper.

Phase 2a: Data collection for IPT testing

Data were collected to gain insight in both the context, mechanisms, and outcome variables included in the IPT. In accordance with internationally recognized quality standards and guidelines for realist evaluation, a combination of both qualitative and quantitative research methods was used [22].

Quantitative data collection

Two online self-report questionnaires were developed, in co-creation between the research team and Golazo Energy, to investigate several core concepts of the FitBonus IPT at the individual level. Specifically, they assess the outcomes at the individual level (O1, O2, O3, O4), cultural organizational characteristics (C2b), lifestyle behaviors (M2) and program uptake (see Table 1). Information on socio-economic and demographic characteristics (C1), as well as structural organizational characteristics (C2b), is also provided by these questionnaires, supplemented with data from Golazo Energy databases. The first questionnaire, distributed before (pre) and after (post) the program implementation, captures baseline and post-intervention data. In the first edition of the program, this questionnaire was administered to both the intervention and the comparison group; in the second edition, only an intervention group was included. These data serve to evaluate changes over time in the targeted outcome variables (e.g. self-reported stress, energy levels, sleep quality, connectedness, or work ability) and allow for comparative analysis between groups. The second, intermittent questionnaire, was distributed to the intervention group at the end of each well-being dimension (i.e., physical, mental, and social). These surveys aimed to capture intermediate feedback on participant engagement, perceived relevance, and the implementation of specific program elements, thereby informing interpretation of uptake and potential mechanisms of change (Table 1). The questionnaires used for this research are available in Additional file 1, Appendix 2.

Qualitative data collection

To assess knowledge, motivation and skills (M1), sense of community within the organization (M3), social capital within the organization (M4), and communication behavior of change agents (M5), interviews (a) will be conducted with participants of the FitBonus program. Meanwhile, to assess whether the FitBonus program influenced the well-being climate of their organization (O5) and thus had outcomes at the organizational level, focus groups (b) are conducted with employers and/or HR managers of the participating organizations. Moreover, both the interviews and the focus groups are used to further elaborate on the quantitatively surveyed constructs of the IPT’s. The interview guides for both the interviews and focus groups can be found in Additional file 1, Appendix 2. In accordance with the realist evaluation approach, the topic guides were theory-driven and based on the IPT’s, ensuring that the questions are aligned with the realist framework to explore the context, uptake, mechanisms and outcomes in relation to the FitBonus program [22]. Simultaneously, the topic guides remained sufficiently flexible to accommodate unanticipated insights or experiences introduced by participants.

Interviews and focus groups will take place shortly after the end of each FitBonus edition and will be conducted by the first authors (FVDW and RV). All participants of the FitBonus program and employers or HR-managers of participating organizations will receive an email inviting them to respectively participate in an interview or focus group regarding their experiences with the FitBonus program. The goal is to conduct an average of 15 in-depth interviews and three focus groups (n per focus group = 6–10), or until saturation of meaningful information is achieved. Regarding participant selection for the interviews and focus groups, variation in context (i.e., age, sex, education, sector, etc.) is aimed for during the recruitment process. For the interviews with FitBonus participants, the aim is to include both active participants with high program uptake as well as participants who dropped out or had minimal uptake. The aim of the interviews with dropouts is to gain insight into why some participants did not actively participate in the program. This information will be used to optimize the FitBonus program (i.e., in between editions and after the second edition) and formulate recommendations for future well-being-promoting programs. Employers or HR managers included in the focus groups may be participants of the program themselves, but this is not a requirement. They may simply have enrolled members of their organization in FitBonus without participating themselves.

The interviews and focus groups will take place face-to-face or online, depending on what works best for the participants, and are conducted in a semi-structured manner [70]. Adjustments to the interview guide will be made iteratively based on generated themes and insights during the interviews that are not yet incorporated in the interview guide but are deemed relevant. The interview guides were developed based on the information needed to test the IPT’s of the FitBonus program. However, certain questions are inspired by existing questionnaires to map certain constructs from the program theories (i.e., to assess social capital [71] and organizational well-being climate [34]). These questions were not directly copied from the existing questionnaires but were adapted into open-ended questions to facilitate in-depth qualitative exploration.

2b: Data analysis for IPT testing

In this phase of the evaluation, the data collected in phase 2a will be analyzed to test CUMO-configurations.

In line with the convergent mixed methods design, quantitative and qualitative data are analyzed independently during the same phase of the research [72–74]. Subsequently, the results of both analyses will be integrated during the interpretation phase to comprehensively evaluate how, for whom and under which circumstances the FitBonus program works.

Quantitative data will primarily be used to assess the program’s effectiveness at the individual level, focusing on outcomes related to physical, mental, and social well-being (O1, O2, O3), as well as perceived workability (O4). To achieve this, pre-post intervention data from both the intervention and comparison groups will be analyzed using linear mixed models as the primary analysis method. Additionally, secondary analyses will be conducted to explore the influence of lifestyle behaviors (M2) and program uptake on these outcomes. Moderation analyses will be performed to explore how contextual factors such as socio-economic and demographic characteristics (C1) and cultural and organizational characteristics (C2) may influence the activation of mechanisms and the resulting program outcomes. These analyses are used as a complementary tool within the realist evaluation framework, helping to examine under which contextual conditions the program appears to work differently for different groups.

Simultaneously, qualitative data will be analyzed to identify the contextual factors and mechanisms influencing the outcomes at both the individual and organizational level and to understand how these various program constructs are interconnected. Moreover, qualitative data will be utilized for the assessment of program effectiveness at organizational level – specifically, its effect on organizational well-being climate (O5). For the analysis of the qualitative data, the audio records of the interviews and focus groups will first be transcribed. These transcriptions will serve as the foundation for coding the interviews and focus groups. The coding process will involve at least two researchers, who will engage in iterative discussion, with each other and the broader research team, to develop a shared understanding of how the data relate to the initial program theories. Differences in interpretation will be discussed to deepen understanding of the underlying mechanisms and contextual influences, and to refine the program theories accordingly.

As concerns the method for analysis, a combination of thematic analysis and an approach put forward by Jackson and Kolla [75] will be followed. Initially, a thematic analysis will be conducted to identify and categorize codes within the data into context (C), uptake (U), mechanisms (M), and outcomes (O). Following this, the data will be further examined to empirically generate CMO-configurations, often referred to as “strings” [75]. These strings can take the form of dyadic (CM/MO), triadic (CMO), or more complex configurations (e.g., CUMO) found within the narratives. Both deductive and inductive coding approaches will be utilized. During coding, focus will be placed on identifying CUMO strings generated from constructs of the IPT, while simultaneously allowing for the discovery of CMO strings composed of new contexts, mechanisms, and outcomes not yet included in the IPT.

Phase 3: Theory refinement and development of lessons learnt

Building upon the findings from phase 2, the initial program theories for the FitBonus program will be refined and adjusted to comprehensively include the relationships among context variables, uptake, mechanisms, and outcome variables at play within the FitBonus program. As such, finalized program theories will be developed that determines what has worked, for whom, and under what circumstances. The iterative process, which will be ongoing throughout the study, is anticipated to persist during this stage [24]. If unexpected or unintended results arise during data interpretation and theory refinement, additional data analysis and potentially further data collection will take place to address these ambiguities. This approach ensures a thorough understanding and refinement of the program theories. Furthermore, recommendations for future well-being programs will be developed.

Ethical considerations

For both the quantitative and the qualitative data, participants were requested to provide their written informed consent, thereby approving the utilization of their personal data in this evaluation study. All data will be pseudonymized to ensure the privacy of the participants. Ethical approval for data collection and analysis has been obtained from the Ethical Committee of the University Hospital Ghent (reference numbers: ONZ-2023-0297 and ONZ-2024-0011).

Discussion

This paper describes the protocol for evaluating the FitBonus program. When evaluating real-life well-being programs like FitBonus, it is essential to select an appropriate evaluation methodology that comprehensively captures the program’s complexity and multilevel objectives. By focusing on context-uptake-mechanism-outcome (CUMO) configurations, realist evaluation offers a robust approach to gaining an in-depth understanding of how and why the FitBonus program works, for whom it works, and under which circumstances it is effective [20, 24, 26].

This study transparently presents and elucidates the proposed program theories in an early stage. This transparency ensures full understanding of the nature of the program being evaluated, including its components, target audience, and intended outcomes. This detailed description aligns with the best practices in realist evaluation, facilitating a clearer understanding of how the program is expected to function and its potential impacts [22]. Given the scarcity of protocol papers in realist evaluation, the transparency of this paper can contribute to and inspire the operationalization of future realist evaluation methodologies.

Realist evaluation contrasts with traditional RCTs by focusing on the context in which a program operates and the underlying mechanisms driving change [18–22]. Unlike RCTs, which may be rigid and less adaptable to dynamic organizational settings, realist evaluation allows for adjustments based on participant feedback and changing circumstances. RCTs’ controlled conditions may limit their applicability in real-world policy and practice, where nuanced understanding of program effectiveness in diverse contexts is crucial [19, 22]. While policymakers often seek the “hard” evidence that RCTs provide, it is important to recognize that the controlled conditions of RCTs may not accurately reflect real-world settings, meaning the observed effectiveness may not hold when interventions are broadly implemented [76]. Realist evaluation, on the other hand, offers valuable insights into which interventions are likely to be successful in specific contexts, resulting in a more efficient use of resources. By understanding the conditions under which programs work, policymakers can make more informed decisions that are likely to lead to sustained and meaningful outcomes in diverse, real-world environments.

Although realist evaluation primarily aims to explain how and why interventions work in specific contexts, the first edition of the FitBonus program also includes a comparison group. This design choice does not replace the realist logic of inquiry but offers a pragmatic way to explore variations in outcomes between groups. These variations can inform the refinement of program theories and support a deeper understanding of where, for whom, and under what conditions the program appears to work. Although this approach departs from more traditional realist designs, it complements the theory-driven analysis rather than replacing it, and can enhance the explanatory depth of the evaluation. While we acknowledge the limitations of non-randomized designs [77, 78], this methodological choice provides a useful opportunity to support and challenge emerging CUMO configurations in a comparative framework.

The focus on SMEs, a sector often overlooked in existing research, despite a significant proportion of the workforce being employed in these organizations, is another key aspect of this study [34, 35]. SMEs often have limited resources for well-being initiatives, unlike larger corporations that typically have more financial means and infrastructure to implement extensive programs for individual well-being. By directing attention to SMEs, this research contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of workplace well-being across diverse organizational contexts.

Moreover, the inclusion of the ‘uptake’ element in the CMO-configurations represents a methodological innovation within realist evaluation that enhances the comprehensiveness and relevance of program evaluations [50]. While implementation processes have been extensively studied in various evaluation methodologies, the explicit incorporation of uptake into the realist evaluation approach is relatively novel. Although uptake is often implicitly treated as part of the context, we deliberately treat it as a separate explanatory condition to better account for the variability in individual engagement within a voluntary and flexible intervention. This addition underscores the importance of not only identifying mechanisms and contextual factors influencing program outcomes but also understanding how the program is adopted and utilized in real-world settings. By integrating uptake into the evaluation framework, this study contributes novel insights into implementation dynamics within the context of realist evaluation.

However, there is a consideration that will need to be taken into account when interpreting the results of this study. It is likely that primarily motivated individuals and those already engaged with well-being initiatives will choose to participate in the surveys, interviews, and focus groups, which may lead to a selection bias. This could result in a sample that does not accurately represent the well-being and perceived workability of the broader participant population. Nevertheless, efforts will be made to clarify that participation in the questionnaires, interviews, and focus groups is always valuable, regardless of satisfaction, level of engagement, or motivation.

In this study, a mix of previously used instruments [64–68] and newly developed instruments was used. Newly developed instruments were included for practical reasons, as the questionnaires were developed in collaboration with Golazo Energy and these instruments were already part of the company’s operational framework. Additionally, psychometric analyses will be conducted to evaluate the reliability and validity of these newly developed instruments, ensuring they meet the necessary standards for future research applications.

Conclusions

This study outlines the protocol for a realist evaluation of the FitBonus program. By employing realist evaluation, the aim is to gain a profound understanding of how, why, and under which circumstances the FitBonus program can be effective in promoting physical, mental, and social well-being, as well as perceived workability among employers and employees in SMEs in Flanders. By gaining insight into the uptake, the context and the mechanisms, not only can be explained why the program is successful (or not), but recommendations can also be made for potential adjustments to enhance its effectiveness. This realist evaluation can contribute to the growing understanding of well-being interventions and can provide valuable guidance for promoting well-being and workability in SMEs.
