School autonomy with accountability as a cross-national policy model: diverse adoptions, practices and impacts
Authors: Antoni Verger, Lluís Parcerisa, Marcel Pagès, Marjolein Camphuijsen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis special issue examines the policy model of School Autonomy with Accountability (SAWA) and its diverse configurations . The focus is on understanding how SAWA reforms shape governance practices and influence educational systems in different educational settings. Drawing on a range of theoretical perspectives, the issue explores how political factors, administrative traditions, and local professional frameworks shape the adoption and evolution of these policies. SAWA reforms promote school autonomy in decision-making while holding schools accountable for performance outcomes through various instruments such as standardized assessments, school improvement projects, and accountability arrangements. However, these policies often create tensions between autonomy and accountability, and their implementation produces varied outcomes depending on local contexts and institutions. The issue also highlights the growing role of data use in educational decision-making and examines the challenges associated with policy enactment at the school level. Taking stock of these discussions, in this introduction to the special issue, we delve into the complexities of SAWA reforms and their implications for educational equity, innovation, and governance. We also highlight the need for further research into the dynamic interaction between the formulation of SAWA policies and their local implementation, particularly in underexamined national contexts.

Introduction: conceptualizing SAWA as a policy model

Periods of sustained policy stability are rare in the educational sector. Education reforms are continuously debated and enacted to adapt educational systems to evolving economic, societal, and technological demands. Two central pillars – school autonomy and accountability – have emerged as cornerstones in both the discourse and practice of modern education reforms. These pillars aim to provide governments with a coherent governance framework, which is expected to make educational systems more flexible, enabling them to respond more effectively to external pressures and societal needs while aiming to improve their overall efficiency (Ball et al., 2017; Bush, 2013; Keddie, 2014; Verger et al., 2019).

The reform model of School Autonomy with Accountability (SAWA) emphasizes empowering schools to make decisions across various domains while holding them accountable for their outcomes. The main instruments and policies that configure the SAWA model include the definition of learning standards, large-scale learning assessments, school improvement projects, accountability contracts, and managerialism in school leadership. Nonetheless, a policy model is more than a collection or the sum of individual instruments; it functions as an interconnected system where the configuration of one component influences the others and vice versa (Weyland, 2004). Positioned at a meso-level, policy models represent an intermediate level of abstraction; they are more concrete than broad policy paradigms but broader than specific interventions or programmes. Furthermore, beyond their theoretical underpinnings, policy models carry a prescriptive nature, aiming to normalize certain policy objectives (von Gliszczynski & Leisering, 2015). In the specific case of SAWA, the model shifts the focus of education systems from an input-based orientation towards performance-oriented goals (Fontdevila & Verger, 2024). This transition aims not only to ensure the achievement of specific learning outcomes, but also to introduce a form of regulatory governance that equips the state with new tools andinstruments to oversee education systems and regulate schools actors’ behaviour from a distance .

To a great extent inspired by New Public Management (NPM) principles, SAWA seeks to overcome bureaucratic governance structures by decentralizing educational provision, promoting outcomes-based management, and positioning the state more as an evaluator rather than a direct provider of education. Despite its managerial origins, many aspects of the SAWA model have been well-received by several educational stakeholders, professional organizations and policy-makers largely because they align with certain professional teacher expectations and encourage schools to tailor their practices to the needs of students, local contexts, and school philosophies. However, several SAWA reforms have raised large criticisms among teachers and educators, especially when the increased autonomy comes with heightened monitoring and consequential forms of assessment.

To a great extent, accountability serves as a counterbalance to the greater autonomy granted to schools. Nonetheless, the relationship between autonomy and accountability is multidirectional and very often generates tensions and contradictions. In this sense, accountability and supervision do not simply respond to increased autonomy; rather, accountability frameworks require certain levels of organizational autonomy to function effectively. Indeed, for accountability pressures to drive improvement, schools must have the autonomy to make meaningful decisions in pedagogical, managerial, and organizational domains. The rationale is straightforward: it would be unjust to hold schools accountable for results if they lack the necessary decision-making capacity to address their specific challenges. Needless to say, over time, SAWA analysts have come to recognize the limits of school autonomy when it comes to triggering school improvement. More than autonomy alone, what many schools, particularly underperforming ones and those operating in disadvantaged contexts, need is more external support, stronger networks, and increased collaboration (Quilabert et al., 2024).

A related key objective of the SAWA model is to raise awareness among school stakeholders about their performance and encourage them to respond to student attainment data in various ways – such as using performance data to inform instructional improvements or evaluate teacher efficacy. In essence, under this policy model, schools are expected to engage in cycles of performance assessment, data-driven decision-making, innovation, and continuous improvement, governed through contractual relationships with public authorities. Authorities encourage schools to make informed decisions about pedagogical and organizational arrangements based on performance data from standardized tests. However, as we develop below, the actual use of this data often depends on the extent to which teachers and principals perceive national assessments as fair, valid, and actionable.

In this special issue, we offer a cross-fertilization of theoretical frameworks and methodological strategies to analyse global education reforms. Specifically, we delve into the expansion and adaptation of SAWA policies in education, examining them through multiple lenses and across diverse regulatory regimes. One first area of focus is the variety of SAWA configurations, where political contingencies, public administration traditions, and professional teacher frameworks shape both the regulation and evolution of these policies. The way SAWA is received, endorsed, or resisted by key stakeholders highlights the dynamic interactions that drive policy adaptation, while the emergence of specific policy tools reflects ongoing recalibration and modification in response to changing local needs and political junctures, among other domestic contingencies.

The special issue also focuses on the practical effects of SAWA policies, analysing the organizational and instructional changes that schools and teachers adopt to improve their performance. In particular, we consider how performance data is used in practice and what factors enhance or impede its role in guiding instructional improvements. Furthermore, the volume explores the various trade-offs, paradoxes, and tensions that SAWA policies generate within the educational sphere. Schools and educators often navigate conflicting demands as they adjust their organizational and pedagogical practices in response to multiple reform mandates, which under SAWA models tend to grow exponentially. These adaptations are further mediated by the unique contexts in which schools operate. As several contributions to the special issue highlight, professional settings, policy environments, and local school cultures all influence how SAWA principles are interpreted and enacted on the ground.

This introduction is structured as follows. First, we reflect on the varied policy trajectories and configurations of SAWA reforms at the national level, with an emphasis on the key role of instrumentation and retooling dynamics. Second, we present the SAWA theory of change and discuss its impacts, highlighting the gap between policy expectations and the situated evidence from the field. This is followed by a reflection on the effects of SAWA and the emerging tensions during the implementation of these policies at the local level. Finally, we conclude by underscoring the main theoretical, methodological, and empirical contributions of the publications included in this special issue.

National encounters with SAWA: policy trajectories and configurations

Over the last two decades, countries from diverse regions with different public administrative traditions and varying degrees of economic development have embraced SAWA policies in education. It is also a policy approach that receives bipartisan support since both left- and right-leaning governments are adopting them, although not necessarily for the same reasons (Teltemann & Jude, 2019; Verger et al., 2019). The model has been embraced at the international level, but this has resulted in highly diverse policy frameworks, not to say the model has penetrated the field of practice differently depending on previous institutions and policy frameworks.

A recent Eurydice report, Equity in School Education in Europe (2020), highlights the significant diversity of school governance policy frameworks across Europe, in which school accountability and autonomy can be looser and tighter (see also Green & Mostafa, 2013; Högberg & Lindgren, 2021). These differences are closely tied not only to the specific goals pursued through the adoption of SAWA policies, but also to administrative traditions, school governance systems, and teacher regulation regimes that shape educational systems (Parcerisa et al., 2022).

In general, these variations manifest in different school assessment and accountability systems, which vary in their intensity and level of articulation. Eurydice (2020) classifies them into three broad categories: highly articulated, moderately articulated, and less developed. First, highly articulated accountability systems are characterized by external assessments, publication of results, and the use of data for evaluating schools. Secondly, moderately articulated accountability systems involve some external assessments, primarily for diagnostic purposes rather than evaluation, with results generally not published. Thirdly, less developed accountability systems feature no external assessments or only limited, sample-based ones; results are not published, and school evaluations are not based on standardized tests.

Beyond the differences in levels of autonomy and accountability across educational systems, this volume seeks to move beyond a descriptive approach and focus on the conditions that enable different SAWA configurations to emerge, their enactment at the school level, and their impacts across various dimensions. To do so, we delve into the concrete instrumentation, calibration and (re)tooling dynamics of the SAWA model, which is an emerging research topic. Countries are constantly revisiting their school governance policies in an attempt to connect to ongoing innovations in school management and assessments, but also to address the increasing criticism triggered by the most controversial SAWA instruments. Student growth measures, school rankings, value-added models, and pay-for-performance are contentious instruments that have lost centrality in many SAWA regimes, in part due to the feedback effect coming from implementation issues,teacher protests and/or expert recommendations.

To a great extent, assessment policies have evolved from a focus on learning outcomes mainly measured with standardized tests towards more complex systems, where several aspects of the education production process are also included through instruments such as class observation, e-portfolios, teachers’ self-appraisal, peer evaluation, and student and parent surveys (Skedsmo, 2011). In this special issue, we have aimed to understand SAWA as a live and constantly evolving policy model, as well as the reasons behind this continuous transformation, including the role of political contingencies, feedback loops, and instrument constituencies.

Previous research has demonstrated that the implementation of accountability ideas in education is highly dependent on local institutions and, more specifically, on the pre-existing forms of evaluation and control in each region (Maroy & Pons, 2019). The contrast between Anglo-American and French-speaking countries is particularly illustrative of these dynamics. In Anglo-American countries, accountability policy is closely linked to the marketization of public services and the adoption of high-stakes approaches. In contrast, as documented by Dumay et al. (2013), many French-speaking educational systems have taken a different path, often embracing a neo-statist form of accountability that avoids market-based strategies, favouring instead vertical accountability mechanisms that reinforce bureaucratic administration.

These findings are echoed by Högberg and Lindgren (2021), who argue that outcomes-based management and accountability are implemented quite differently across regions and sub-regions. Specifically, they observe that Anglo-American countries, along with most Nordic and Eastern European countries, operate under a thick regime of accountability, characterized by an intensive use of performance data. In contrast, Southern European countries typically employ a thin regime of outcomes-based accountability, where the use of accountability tools is more limited and dynamics of policy decoupling are prevalent (Dumay et al., 2013).

From this research, it derives that the instrumentation of SAWA at the state and sub-national levels is the result of continuous processes of bricolage and translation through which policy-makers assemble global scripts within their country’s policy realities and institutional legacies (Maroy & Pons, 2019). Several papers in the volume emphasize this perspective, although they do so by adopting different theoretical perspectives. Drawing on a political sociology approach to policy instruments and policy borrowing literature, the paper written by Steiner-Khamsi, Jóhannesdóttir and Magnúsdóttir (2024) explores policymakers’ ideational selectivity in the adoption of SAWA reforms in Iceland. To do so, the authors combine interviews with key actors with a systematic analysis of policy documents that allows them to unpack the SAWA theory of change and identify three reform waves in which different instruments were privileged. The investigation by Steiner-Khamsi, Jóhannesdóttir, and Magnúsdóttir (2024) makes a novel contribution to existing research by highlighting the key role of time in policy transfer processes. Additionally, this study contributes to three areas of inquiry: global SAWA reform, the analysis of policy processes in Iceland, and comparative education policy research. Furthermore, the analysis of this underexplored case sheds light on actors’ selectivities and bricolage dynamics in their engagement with the SAWA policy script, revealing a gradual dilution of the accountability component within the SAWA framework.

Next, based on a comparative analysis of Denmark and England, Milner and Ydesen (2024) investigate how SAWA reforms have developed in two contexts with different administrative traditions and governance arrangements. Drawing on documentary analysis and semi-structured interviews with policymakers, school leaders, and teachers, and following an abductive approach that cross-fertilizes critical realism with assemblage theory, this study analyzes the evolution and trajectory of test-based accountability instruments in the two countries. This critical realist assemblage framework, inspired by Martyn and Galvin (2022) work, enables the authors to examine how different social entities interact to produce specific accountability outcomes within the educational systems examined. Interestingly, their paper shows that despite differences in how these systems are structured, both Denmark’s and England’s accountability regimes have been highly influenced by the OECD-driven SAWA framework. The study concludes that these regimes persist due to resilient epistemic governance logics, making substantial change unlikely despite localized resistances or policy adjustments. The hybrid theoretical framework used by Milner and Ydesen (2024) in this study makes a unique contribution to comparative education policy studies and becomes a valuable tool for understanding multiscalar dynamics in policy formation processes.

Esper (2024) explores the policy trajectory of SAWA reforms in Colombia through a historical lens, examining how SAWA instruments have evolved over time and highlighting the drivers influencing their adoption and continuous transformation. Theoretically, the study integrates historical institutionalism with a political sociology of policy instruments approach to analyse the interplay between political elites and teachers’ unions in shaping these policies. Using a robust qualitative case study approach, the research draws on semi-structured interviews with 48 key informants and secondary document analysis to provide comprehensive insights into the reforms and changes within Colombia’s education sector over recent decades. The study reveals that the SAWA reforms in this Andean country did not follow a linear trajectory; instead, they experienced a paradigm shift characterized by both subtle and significant institutional changes. It highlights the critical role of ideas and ‘power-distributional tensions’ among key actors during the adoption of SAWA reforms (p. 11), showing how these tensions can provoke contentious dynamics between government initiatives and stakeholders such as teachers’ unions. Unconsulted reforms, in particular, can lead to conflicts and resistance. Ultimately, Esper’s (2024) study sheds light on the complexities of policy transfer and adaptation, as well as the evolving nature of educational governance in a Latin American country like Colombia.

SAWA in practice: from policy expectations to evidence from the field

As argued in this special issue, SAWA policies have a strong presence in global education debates and are being constantly debated, adjusted, and implemented in countries with variegated levels of economic development and different administrative traditions. SAWA policies are designed to promote diverse policy goals, including educational innovation, enhancing schools’ quality and effectiveness, and strengthening equity and collaboration within schools (see Levatino, Parcerisa, et al., 2024). While these policies may prioritize different objectives depending on specific contexts, some common assumptions guide how SAWA is expected to generate positive outcomes in schools.

Firstly, positive changes in school practices are assumed to be most effective when the performance pressures of accountability systems are combined with school autonomy. Autonomy enables schools to develop locally adapted, context-sensitive pedagogies and is critical for addressing performance issues. Pedagogical autonomy allows schools to customize their curricula according to their educational mission and adjust teaching practices to suit their specific contexts (cf. Ball et al., 2011). This flexibility makes schools more responsive to the needs and preferences of students and parents, helping them bypass the bureaucratic constraints of centralized governance and foster pedagogical innovation.

Secondly, performance-based accountability, as a central pillar of SAWA reforms, is intended not only to inform school improvement efforts but also to motivate them. Incentive structures and corrective measures – both material and symbolic – play a crucial role in accountability systems and in encouraging teachers to make decisions based on performance data, fostering efficacy, and directing attention towards improving student outcomes. These efforts often materialize in the form of a school improvement plan, which outlines key educational challenges and priority areas for intervention, along with specific activities and tasks to achieve those goals.

Thirdly, managerial autonomy expands principals’ decision-making authority, particularly in staffing policies such as recruitment, evaluation, and professional development. This autonomy is assumed to help build teacher cohesion and align teachers’ work with the school’s educational philosophy, methodology, and mission. Paradoxically, despite the widespread popularity and diffusion of the SAWA model and the high expectations surrounding it, existing research reveals inconclusive and often contradictory findings regarding its implementation and outcomes. Several studies highlight that SAWA policies can have positive effects on learning outcomes (Patrinos et al., 2015), the alignment of schools’ education with learning standards (Mintrop & Trujillo, 2007), and the promotion of organizational changes that may foster pedagogical innovation (Greany, 2018). Other studies, however, show how these policies do not result in the expected effects and can even trigger undesired side-effects of a different nature (Werler & Færevaag, 2017). Especially when performance pressures are significant, SAWA is conducive to the intensification of teacher- and test-centred pedagogies and the adoption of non-inclusive educational practices (Au, 2011). In these cases, the SAWA balance shifts in favour of accountability, and teachers perceive a reduction in their pedagogical autonomy (Brass & Holloway, 2021).

The existing literature often distinguishes the effects of autonomy based on the decision-making domain: pedagogical or managerial. Evidence gathered from the European context suggests that educational systems with higher levels of pedagogical autonomy tend to be more effective and equitable. In contrast, systems with greater managerial autonomy but less developed pedagogical autonomy do not exhibit a similar relationship with school performance (European Commission, 2018; Eurydice, 2020). A study using PISA data concludes that when pedagogical autonomy translates into greater teacher involvement in decision-making processes, it is associated with higher student performance. «We find that although school governance arrangements vary substantially across countries, increases in teacher decision-making responsibilities are consistently and positively related to student achievement in math, reading, and science, all else equal. Furthermore, controlling for school principals’ leadership style does not fundamentally change the positive and significant relationships between teacher decision making and student achievement, suggesting that the impact of teachers may be independent of school principals’ attitudes and actions» (Luschei & Jeong, 2021, p. 1)

However, several authors question whether greater school autonomy alone benefits educational systems, both in terms of educational outcomes and equity. School autonomy is highly context-sensitive, and depending on the social and institutional environment in which it is deployed, it may produce different – and even opposing – effects (Hanushek et al., 2013). Numerous studies point to the contingent and contradictory nature of school governance policies, whose effects are context-dependent and non-generalizable (Buerger et al., 2023; Ko et al., 2016; Schütz et al., 2007). The effectiveness of autonomy policies largely depends on the training level, organizational capacity, and professional development opportunities available to teachers. It is also influenced by the social and economic context in which schools operate, particularly in systems marked by social inequality. In segmented educational systems, school autonomy can hinder the equitable distribution of learning opportunities (Keddie, 2015) and affect the ability of families to have an equal voice in the school environment (Galiani et al., 2008).

Educational decentralization and school autonomy processes are particularly problematic when they interact with market forces (see Figure 3) and with high-stakes accountability measures. In contexts with freedom of school choice and/or where school league tables based on external assessment results are published, autonomy policies create opportunities for competitive and exclusionary practices, further intensifying the segmentation of the education system, stigmatizing certain schools, and excluding particular students (Ferrer-Esteban, 2015). Consequently, high levels of autonomy, without sufficient support structures for schools, can widen disparities – both in terms of their educational projects and the quality of education they offer – reinforcing the hierarchy among schools and harming educational equity (Altrichter et al., 2014). In such contexts, schools, shielded by their autonomy, often compete for higher positions by developing pedagogical, organizational, and communicative strategies aimed at attracting more advantaged students (Maroy & Van Zanten, 2009; Zancajo, 2019).

Specifically, SAWA policies can reinforce a reciprocal selection cycle between certain types of schools and specific family profiles. On one hand, schools with autonomy, an advantaged student population, and strong average academic results may act strategically to attract students from higher social backgrounds. On the other hand, families with greater social and cultural capital – and thus better networks and access to information about schools – may strategically choose schools with higher academic achievement and an advantaged student body. This dynamic can result in a progressively polarized school system (Allen & Vignoles, 2007). While autonomy policies offer many potential benefits, they can also produce adverse effects that undermine equal treatment and the fair distribution of educational opportunities.

Quantitative studies indicate that the relationship between accountability, managerial school autonomy, and student performance is statistically weak. This finding has recently led the OECD, – previously an advocate of school rankingsbased on PISA data – to adopt a more cautious and nuanced stance when promoting these policies (Torres, 2021). The European Commission conducted its own analysis of data from later PISA editions and found that the association between autonomy, accountability and performance is even weaker in European countries. The Commission concludes that ‘none of the accountability indicators available in PISA 2015 (i.e. mandatory tests, publicly posted achievement data, achievement data tracked over time) is significantly correlated with the shares of top performers or low achievers’. Furthermore, it notes: ‘This holds true even when these indicators are interacted with measures of school autonomy’ (European Commission, 2018, p. 102).

Many of the desired and undesired effects of accountability systems stem from how school performance data is processed and used. For this reason, achieving a clear understanding and effective use of data by various actors and at different levels – families, teachers, and school leadership at the classroom and school levels, educational inspectors at the school level, and administrators and researchers at the system level – becomes a key challenge for policymakers. Two basic conditions can be identified to ensure that data is used appropriately. First, stakeholders must perceive the educational data deriving from large scale assessments and other sources as useful, actionable and valid resources for making educational decisions. Second, data is only effective if stakeholders have access to it, along with the necessary resources – such as training and time – to process the information and implement the necessary actions. Despite the widespread use of large-scale external assessments, there is a paradoxical underutilization of the resulting finding and limited institutionalization of their use for formative purposes in numerous educational systems (Prøitz et al., 2017; Terhart, 2013). According to UNESCO (2019), the underutilization of data is as unfortunate as its indiscriminate use: ‘Given the significant resources required to administer external assessments, the effective use of the resulting data is crucial to ensure that this exercise is not rendered futile or perceived as a waste of public resources’ (p. 12).

SAWA effects: new territories, impact dimensions and practices

Most existing research on the implementation of SAWA has been conducted in Anglo-American countries, where these policies are typically high stakes, exert significant performance pressure on schools and interact with the dynamics of marketization and school choice in complex ways (Keddie, 2014; Sahlberg, 2006; Levatino, Verger, et al., 2024). In these countries, alongside implementation issues, both overt and subtle forms of resistance to SAWA and, in particular, to performance-based governance instruments, have been extensively documented (Hardy, 2015; Williams & Tolbert, 2021). However, our understanding of the dynamics and outcomes produced by SAWA instruments within other administrative regimes remains limited.

This special issue presents new empirical evidence from research conducted in under-explored contexts, including countries with more bureaucratic administrative traditions, often found in Francophone and Southern European countries, and those with equity-oriented public administrations, characteristic of Nordic European countries. Several papers included in this special issue develop a better understanding of how SAWA approaches work under different conditions and analyse their effects on different organizational and pedagogical dimensionsas well as on individual-level outcomes.

Órdenes and Ulloa’s (2024) research explores how extrinsic incentives influence teachers’ intrinsic motivation within Chile’s professional career ladder. Their analysis is grounded in motivational theory, particularly the ‘contingent performance’ model elaborated by Harackiewicz and Sansone (2000), which examines how extrinsic incentives can either support or undermine intrinsic motivation depending on factors like perceived competence and autonomy. Methodologically, their investigation follows qualitative interviews with 24 teachers across five schools to gather insights into their motivational patterns and perceptions of the career ladder evaluation . This paper makes a relevant contribution to the literature on policy implementation in education by shedding ‘light on the motivational pattern that emerges from teachers’ experience with the policy design’. This research unpacks how teachers enact policy by connecting their ideas about the intentions of new governance instruments with their intrinsic motivation. The paper highlights the key role of teachers’ trust in accountability models and suggests that the ’[p]ervasive evaluative threat, lack of trust in the evaluation process, and the lack of competence cues from the evaluative judgments seem to have crowded out intrinsic motives associated with the work’ of those teachers positioned as sceptics concerning the career ladder policy (p. 14).

Based on research conducted in Sweden, Carl-Henrik Adolfsson examines ‘new’ modes of local school governance, focusing on the strategies and activities developed by Local Education Authorities (LEAs) to manage and regain control over schools amidst growing education re-centralization in Sweden. Drawing on neo-institutional theory and the concept of ‘loosely coupled’ systems (Weick, 1976), this study analyzes how LEAs navigate the emerging tensions between school autonomy, accountability, and control in a shifting educational governance landscape. The paper identifies four management strategies used by LEAs to manage schools. First, data use practices to monitor and control schools through various symbolic systems such as guidelines and local school improvement plans. Second, the standardization of institutional quality assurance routines and processes, through which LEAs ‘gain stronger control over the schools’ quality assurance work’ (Adolfsson, 2024, p. 94). Third, regular quality dialogues within LEAs, which are integrated into the previous management strategies and reinforced by symbolic systems. Such a management strategy is conceived as a hidden mechanism of control and accountability through data-intensive practices. The fourth and final strategy focuses on promoting professional development for school principals, aimed at fostering a shared understanding, skills, and language related to leadership and school improvement. In this strategy, relational systems play a crucial role in transmitting cognitive elements by identifying and disseminating role models and best practices. Indeed, Adolfsson’s study provides new insights into how LEAs are adapting to national reforms, while also making a theoretical and empirical contribution to curriculum theory. It sheds light on how to understand and analyse local school governance.

Varying school responses: tensions of SAWA at the local level

There is a limited understanding of how and under what circumstances teachers and principals use performance data, including the results of standardized tests, to promote processes of school improvement, or under what conditions they can take advantage of school autonomy frameworks to enhance pedagogic innovation. The implementation of these policies is particularly complex due to their broad, multi-dimensional nature, comprising areas from teaching practices to governance structures. School reforms such as those promoting SAWA often face challenges in implementation, resulting in both intended and unintended outcomes due to resistance, misinterpretation, or capacity issues (e.g. see Bertrand & Marsh, 2015; Landri, 2021; Oplatka, 2017). Moreover, the interaction between different policy instruments can overlap, conflict, or complement one another, with success heavily reliant on how schools perceive and integrate them into their specific contexts (Quilabert et al., 2024).

Thus, a related area of inquiry that deserves further research and that we wish to draw attention to in this special issue is related to the inherent tensions and paradoxes that SAWA policies can generate in educational systems, and how these tensions are lived and negotiated at different governance levels. As numerous scholars warn, despite the policy discourse emphasizing the importance of autonomy in the governance of education, more and more schools feel increasingly constrained by government requirements, among which PBA stands out (Bush, 2013)

Beyond performance and effectiveness, ongoing school reforms often aim to address equity, inclusion, and innovation. However, these goals do not always align easily with one another. By simultaneously combining seemingly contradictory ideas – such as innovation and standardization, excellence and equity, autonomy and control, networks and competition, and well-being and effectiveness – the SAWA model may create significant tensions for policy implementers and enactors. Schools may handle these tensions differently depending on their material conditions, educational capacities, market positioning, and organizational culture. Educators’ ideas and preferences are also crucial for understanding how schools interpret the improvement mandate and balance performance expectations with the increasing emphasis on student inclusion and well-being.

Several papers in this special issue offer empirical insights into how school actors interpret and negotiate overlapping SAWA policy prerogatives and societal expectations, as well as the outcomes of these interactions. Mentini (2024)’s paper examines how schools in Italy navigate the dual pressures of accountability and innovation. Specifically, her study focuses on the National Evaluation System (SNV), which integrates both internal and external accountability mechanisms designed to hold schools accountable for student outcomes while promoting pedagogical innovation. Theoretically, the study draws on sensemaking and institutional filtering theories to explore how school actors interpret, adapt, and implement external policy demands in diverse local contexts. Mentini finds that schools respond to the SAWA policy framework in varied ways, influenced by factors such as socioeconomic context, staff cohesion, and teachers’ attitudes towards accountability. Some schools manage to align innovation with accountability, while others experience tensions between these mandates, often prioritizing external performance pressures over meaningful pedagogical changes. Mentini’s research provides a critical understanding of how educational reforms are enacted in practice, highlighting the complexity of balancing accountability with innovation in different institutional contexts.

Quilabert (2024) examines the increasing pressure on schools and teachers in Catalonia (Spain) to adopt innovative teaching practices, with recent policy developments positioning innovation as a key pillar of school improvement. Using a policy enactment perspective and sense-making theories, the study explores how teachers and principals interpret and implement innovation mandates in their daily practice. One key finding is that while many school actors engage with the innovation mandate, translating this engagement into practical changes is challenging. This difficulty stems from differing interpretations of what innovation means – whether it is a means to improve academic outcomes or an end in itself. The broad and ambiguous nature of the innovation policy allows schools to adapt it to their specific contexts, but there is a notable tension between policy expectations and actual practices.

The study reveals that while most schools had implemented innovative projects, these were often externally driven, fragmented, and standardized, rather than organically developed. Schools in lower socioeconomic status (SES) communities faced greater challenges in fostering bottom-up innovation due to material constraints such as limited funding and high teacher turnover. Quilabert’s paper contributes to understanding the gap between policy rhetoric and school-level realities, especially highlighting the unintended consequences of top-down innovation policies. His findings call for reflection on how policymakers can better support meaningful, context-specific innovation, particularly in resource-constrained environments, and offer valuable insights into the complexities of education reform and policy implementation in SAWA environments.

To conclude

The adoption of SAWA is reshaping educational governance styles and altering responsibilities and power dynamics between schools, educational stakeholders, and governments in both the global North and South. In this special issue, we examine diverse SAWA configurations and provide empirical accounts of the policy trajectory and effects of school governance reforms across various contexts. Specifically, we explore the impact of these policies on organizational and educational practices at both the school and individual levels in countries with different educational governance approaches. As highlighted, this special issue underscores the need for further research beyond early-adopter countries or NPM marketizers, in order to develop a more nuanced understanding of the rationales, drivers and impacts of SAWA reforms in underexplored national settings. That said, this special issue makes at least three key contributions to existing research on school reforms.

Firstly, several studies included in this special issue illuminate new and fruitful avenues of research by comparing SAWA reform trajectories across different countries, while cross-fertilizing theoretical approaches and heuristic tools – including those coming from the political sociology of policy instruments, historical institutionalism, critical realism, and literature on policy assemblages (see Esper, 2024; Milner & Ydesen, 2024; Steiner-Khamsi et al., 2024). These papers provide fresh analytical lenses to examine the global-local policy nexus, making relevant contributions to the literature on policy transfer and adoption. They underscore the key role of time in policy processes (see Steiner-Khamsi et al., 2024) and explore the interplay of various social entities in shaping specific policy configurations. The insights from small-n and single-case studies contribute evidence that challenges the often-assumed policy convergence thesis. Despite the international spread of SAWA reforms and their endorsement by key international organizations, we show through novel comparative, multiscalar, and historical accounts that the rationales behind the adoption of SAWA reforms – and their subsequent trajectories – vary due to a wide range of factors such as differing administrative traditions, professional regulatory frameworks, and involvement of various stakeholders and coalitions in education reform debates. These papers highlight the dynamic and contested nature of policy adoption, illustrating the influence of policymakers’ selectivities and the mediating role of instrument constituencies in the adoption and recontextualization of policy models.

Secondly, the special issue emphasizes the need to further investigate the complex relationship between policy design and enactment processes. It encourages opening new lines of empirical inquiry to explore the interaction between the formulation and design of new governance instruments – and their extrinsic incentive structures – and the intrinsic motivation of professionals socialized under certain regulatory regimes (see Órdenes and Ulloa, 2024). This raises new challenges in understanding the motivational mechanisms that drive teachers’ behaviours and practices in achieving performance goals. Methodologically, the contributions in this issue highlight the need for innovative research designs to address the limitations of studies on sensitive topics like work motivation and professional engagement. Combining survey experiments with other research techniques may help mitigate social desirability bias, particularly when analysing sensitive issues such as the emergence of opportunistic behaviour under performance pressure, or the crowding-out effects of incentive schemes on teacher motivation (see Levatino, Parcerisa, et al., 2024)

Thirdly, this special issue introduces new analytical approaches to studying the enactment of various policy mandates, such as those arising from the SAWA reform package, in a context where governments increasingly accumulate policy instruments to respond to rapidly changing societal demands. Several papers (see Quilabert et al., 2024; Mentini, 2024) shed light on the complexities schools face in diverse contexts as they attempt to align the mandates of accountability, autonomy, and innovation. These studies highlight the key role of meso-level variables – such as shared beliefs, staff cohesion, and school socioeconomic composition – in shaping different types of responses and degrees of coherence or incoherence between policy mandates. The investigations also offer valuable insights into the additional challenges encountered by disadvantaged schools during the implementation of school governance reforms, with severe implications for educational equity and inclusion. Overall, the papers in this special issue provide a detailed account of the dialectical and mutually constitutive dynamics between cultural-ideational factors and socio-material components that influence the policy enactment processes of SAWA policies.

In short, despite the global spread and acceptance of SAWA reforms among international fora and policymakers, the evidence on their outcomes remains inconclusive in several areas, which contributes to these reforms being continually revisited. This uncertainty can be partly explained by variations in policy design configurations and the multiple forms of policy enactment. Beyond the factors already influencing SAWA reform implementation widely covered in this volume – such as actors’ filtering and meaning-making processes, socioeconomic and micropolitical contexts, and institutional cultures – new challenges are rapidly emerging with technological change. Policy concepts such as autonomy and accountability are continually reshaped by developments that enable administrations, schools, and other actors to capture, manage, and use larger amounts of data and improvement techniques within schools. While the technical capacities for datafication are expanding rapidly, school capacity may not be growing at the same pace, potentially clashing with institutional limits and educational beliefs. This can generate new tensions and contradictions for educators and schools, with direct implications for educational practices and organizational arrangements. Further research should explore the limits, tensions, and potential of SAWA reforms in such a changing scenario, while also considering policy alternatives that could foster more meaningful and horizontal accountability relations between schools, and ways to embed schools’ autonomous decisions within solid and stable support structures.

She currently works as an assistant professor at the Department of Educational and Family Studies at the Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam. Her research focus is on the political sociology of education, global education policy, policy enactment, and the effects of public policy on social and educational inequalities.

Antoni Verger is Professor of Sociology at the Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona and research fellow at the Catalan Institution for Research and Advanced Studies (ICREA).
