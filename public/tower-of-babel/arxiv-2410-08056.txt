Generalized Cesàro operators in the disc algebra and in Hardy spaces Thanks: Mathematics Subject Classification 2020: Primary 46E15; Secondary 47B38. Thanks: Article accepted for publication in Advances in Operator Theory.
Angela A. Albanese, José Bonet and Werner J. Ricker

Abstract
. Generalized Cesàro operators C t C_{t} , for t ∈ [ 0 , 1 ) t\in[0,1) , are investigated when they act on the disc algebra A ⁡ ( 𝔻 ) A({\mathbb{D}}) and on the Hardy spaces H p H^{p} , for 1 ≤ p ≤ ∞ 1\leq p\leq\infty . We study the continuity, compactness, spectrum and point spectrum of C t C_{t} as well as their linear dynamics and mean ergodicity on these spaces.

1.
Introduction and Preliminaries

The (discrete) generalized Cesàro operators
C_{t}
, for
t\in[0,1]
, were first investigated by Rhaly,
[
18
,
19
]
. The action of
C_{t}
from the sequence space
\omega:={\mathbb{C}}^{{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
into itself, with
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}:=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, is given by

(1.1)

C_{t}x:=\left(\frac{t^{n}x_{0}+t^{n-1}x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n}}{n+1}\right)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}},\quad x=(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\in\omega.

For
t=0
and with
\varphi:=(\frac{1}{n+1})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
note that
C_{0}
is the diagonal operator

(1.2)

D_{\varphi}x:=\left(\frac{x_{n}}{n+1}\right)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}},\quad x=(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\in\omega,

and, for
t=1
, that
C_{1}
is the classical Cesàro averaging operator

(1.3)

C_{1}x:=\left(\frac{x_{0}+x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n}}{n+1}\right)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}},\quad x=(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\in\omega.

The behaviour of
C_{t}
on certain sequence spaces is well understood; see

[
1
,
8
,
22
]
, and the references therein.

By
H({\mathbb{D}})
we denote the space of all holomorphic functions on
{\mathbb{D}}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,|z|<1\}
, which is
equipped with the topology
\tau_{c}
of uniform convergence on the compact subsets of
{\mathbb{D}}
. According to
[
15
, §27.3(3)]
the space
H({\mathbb{D}})
is a Fréchet-Montel space. A family of norms generating
\tau_{c}
is given, for each
0<r<1
, by

(1.4)

q_{r}(f):=\sup_{|z|\leq r}|f(z)|,\quad f\in H({\mathbb{D}}).

We identify a function
f\in H({\mathbb{D}})
with its sequence of Taylor coefficients
\hat{f}:=(\hat{f}(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
(i.e.,
\hat{f}(n):=\frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}
, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
), so that
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{f}(n)z^{n}
, for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
.
The operator
C_{t}\colon H({\mathbb{D}})\to H({\mathbb{D}})
, for
t\in[0,1)
, given by
(C_{t}f)(0):=f(0)
and

(1.5)

(C_{t}f)(z):=\frac{1}{z}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{f(\xi)}{1-t\xi}\,d\xi,\ z\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\},

for every
f\in H({\mathbb{D}})
, was investigated in
[
2
]
. For
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{f}(n)z^{n}
in
H({\mathbb{D}})
, we also have that

(1.6)

\displaystyle(C_{t}f)(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{t^{n}\hat{f}(0)+t^{n-1}\hat{f}(1)+\ldots+\hat{f}(n)}{n+1}\right)z^{n}.

Note that the coefficients in (
1.6
) are as in (
1.1
).

The discrete generalized Cesàro operator acting in
\omega
(cf. (
1.1
)) is denoted by

C_{t}^{\omega}
, whereas the notation
C_{t}
will be used for the operator (
1.5
) acting in
H({\mathbb{D}})
. Note that
C_{0}^{\omega}=D_{\varphi}
(see (
1.2
)). Moreover, for
t=0
observe that the operator
(C_{0}f)(z)=\frac{1}{z}\int_{0}^{z}f(\xi)\,d\xi
for
z\not=0
and
(C_{0}f)(0)=f(0)
is the traditional Hardy operator in
H({\mathbb{D}})
.

The generalized Cesàro operators
C_{t}
, for
t\in[0,1]
, are investigated in
[
2
]
, where they act on the Fréchet space
H({\mathbb{D}})
and on the weighted Banach spaces
H_{v}^{\infty}
and
H_{v}^{0}
endowed with with their sup-norms. The operator
C_{t}
is actually continuous on
H({\mathbb{D}})
,
[
2
, Proposition 2.1]
. In this article
we study the operators
C_{t}
when they act on the disc algebra
A({\mathbb{D}})
and on the Hardy spaces
H^{p}
over
{\mathbb{D}}
, for
1\leq p\leq\infty
.

Section 2 establishes the continuity and compactness of
C_{t}
acting on the spaces
A({\mathbb{D}})
and
H^{\infty}
and determines their spectrum. It is also shown that each
C_{t}
, for
t\in[0,1)
, is power bounded and uniformly mean ergodic in these spaces but, it fails to be supercyclic. In Section 3 the continuity, compactness and spectrum of
C_{t}
are investigated when they act in the Hardy spaces
H^{p}
for
1\leq p<\infty
. Estimates for the operator norm
\|C_{t}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}
are also provided. To establish the compactness of
C_{t}
in
H^{p}
it is necessary to invoke properties of the forward and backward shift operators acting in
H^{p}
as well as properties of certain Volterra type operators. An important point is that each
C_{t}
, for
t\in(0,1)
, belongs to the class of Volterra operators being considered. It is also established that
C_{t}
maps
H^{\infty}
into
A({\mathbb{D}})
. The spectrum and point spectrum of
C_{t}
(acting on
H^{p}
) are completely determined. As in the case when
C_{t}
acts in
A({\mathbb{D}})
or
H^{\infty}
, it turns out that
C_{t}
is again power bounded and uniformly mean ergodic when acting in
H^{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
, but fails to be supercyclic.

Let us briefly recall the definition of the spaces involved; see
[
10
,
24
]
for more details.
The space
H^{\infty}
is a Banach space when it is endowed with the norm

(1.7)

\|f\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{D}}}|f(z)|,\quad f\in H^{\infty}.

The disc algebra
A({\mathbb{D}})
consists of all holomorphic functions on
{\mathbb{D}}

that extend to a continuous function on the closure
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
of
{\mathbb{D}}
. That is,
f\in A({\mathbb{D}})
if and only if there exists
\tilde{f}\in C(\overline{{\mathbb{D}}})
, necessarily unique, such that
f(z)=\tilde{f}(z)
for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
. In particular,
f\in H^{\infty}
.
Endowed with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
the space
A({\mathbb{D}})
is a commutative Banach algebra with respect to multiplication of functions. It is routine to verify that
A({\mathbb{D}})
is a closed subalgebra of
H^{\infty}
.

For
0<p<\infty
the Hardy space
H^{p}
consists of all functions
f\in H({\mathbb{D}})
satisfying

(1.8)

\|f\|_{p}:=\sup_{0\leq r<1}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|^{p}\,d\theta\right)^{1/p}<\infty.

For
p=\infty
, a function
f\in H({\mathbb{D}})
belongs to
H^{\infty}
if

(1.9)

\sup_{0\leq r<1}\left(\sup_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]}|f(re^{i\theta})|\right)<\infty.

Of course, the expression in (
1.9
) equals
\|f\|_{\infty}
; see (
1.7
).
With this quasi-norm,
H^{p}
is a metrizable, complete, topological vector space. For
1\leq p<\infty
, it turns out that
\|\cdot\|_{p}
is actually a norm for which
H^{p}
is a Banach space.

We are mainly interested in the setting when
1\leq p<\infty
. In this case, for all
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty
, it turns out that
H^{q}\subseteq H^{p}
with a continuous inclusion. Moreover, the norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}
is increasing with
p
.
Given
1\leq p<\infty
and
f\in H^{p}
, there is a standard notation for the terms in (
1.8
), namely

M_{p}(r,f):=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|^{p}\,d\theta\right)^{1/p},\quad r\in[0,1),

which increase with
0\leq r\uparrow 1
. Accordingly, for every
r\in[0,1)
, we have

(1.10)

M_{p}(r,f)\leq\|f\|_{p}=\sup_{0\leq r<1}M_{p}(r,f)=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}M_{p}(r,f).

The same is true for
p=\infty
if we define

M_{\infty}(r,f):=\sup_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]}|f(re^{i\theta})|,\quad r\in[0,1).

It is known, for each
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, that the evaluation functional
\delta_{z}\colon f\mapsto f(z)
, for
f\in H^{p}
, is continuous, that is,
\delta_{z}\in(H^{p})^{\prime}
; see the Lemma on p.36 of
[
10
]
for
1\leq p<\infty
. For
p=\infty
, fix
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
and let
r:=|z|
. Then the inequality

|\delta_{z}(f)|=|f(z)|\leq M_{\infty}(r,f)\leq\|f\|_{\infty},\quad f\in H^{\infty},

shows that
\delta_{z}\in(H^{\infty})^{\prime}
.

We end this section by recalling a few definitions and some notation concerning Fréchet spaces (always locally convex) and operators between them. For further details about functional analysis and operator theory relevant to this paper see, for example,
[
11
,
15
,
16
,
21
]
.

Given Fréchet spaces
X,Y
, denote by
{\mathcal{L}}(X,Y)
the space of all linear operators from
X
into
Y
which are continuous. If
X=Y
, then we simply write
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
for
{\mathcal{L}}(X,X)
. If both
X
,
Y
are Banach spaces then, for the operator norm
\|T\|_{X\to Y}:=\sup_{\|x\|_{X}\leq 1}\|Tx\|_{Y}
, with
T\in{\mathcal{L}}(X,Y)
, the space
{\mathcal{L}}(X,Y)
is a Banach space.
Equipped with the topology of pointwise convergence on a Fréchet space
X
(i.e., the strong operator topology
\tau_{s}
) the quasi-complete locally convex Hausdorff space
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
is denoted by
{\mathcal{L}}_{s}(X)
. The range
T(X):=\{Tx:\ x\in X\}
of
T\in{\mathcal{L}}(X)
is also denoted by
{\rm Im}(T)
. Moreover,
\mathop{\rm Ker}(T):=\{x\in X:\ Tx=0\}
.

Let
X
be a Fréchet space.
The identity operator on
X
is written as
I
. The
transpose operator
of
T\in{\mathcal{L}}(X)
is denoted by
T^{\prime}
; it acts from the topological dual space
X^{\prime}:={\mathcal{L}}(X,{\mathbb{C}})
of
X
into itself. Denote by
X^{\prime}_{\sigma}
(resp., by
X^{\prime}_{\beta}
) the space
X^{\prime}
equipped with the weak* topology
\sigma(X^{\prime},X)
(resp., with the strong dual topology
\beta(X^{\prime},X)
). It is known that
X^{\prime}_{\sigma}
is quasicomplete, that
T^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{L}}(X^{\prime}_{\sigma})
and also that
T^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{L}}(X_{\beta}^{\prime})
,
[
16
, p.134]
. The bi-transpose operator
(T^{\prime})^{\prime}
of
T
is denoted simply by
T^{\prime\prime}
and belongs to
{\mathcal{L}}((X^{\prime}_{\beta})^{\prime}_{\beta})
. In the event that
X
is a Banach space, both
X^{\prime}_{\beta}
(denoted simply by
X^{\prime}
) and
(X^{\prime}_{\beta})^{\prime}_{\beta}
(denoted simply by
X^{\prime\prime}
) are again Banach spaces. The dual norm in
X^{\prime}
is given
\|x^{\prime}\|:=\sup_{\|x\|\leq 1}|\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle|
, for
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
.

The following result,
[
1
, Theorem 6.4]
, which is stated below for Banach spaces, will be needed in the sequel. Given
\delta>0
, we introduce the notation
B(0,\delta):=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\ |z|<\delta\}
and
\overline{B(0,\delta)}
for its closure in
{\mathbb{C}}
. Denote the unit circle in
{\mathbb{C}}
by
\mathbb{T}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\ |z|=1\}
. Of course,
B(0,1)={\mathbb{D}}
and
\mathbb{T}
is the boundary of
{\mathbb{D}}
. For the definition of power bounded and uniformly mean ergodic operators see Section 2.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach space and let
T\in{\mathcal{L}}(X)
be a compact operator such that
\sigma(T;X)\subseteq\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
and
\sigma(T;X)\cap{\mathbb{T}}=\{1\}

and which satisfies
\mathop{\rm Ker}(I-T)\cap{\rm Im}(I-T)=\{0\}
. Then
T
is power bounded and uniformly mean ergodic.

Remark 1.2
.

The conditions in Theorem
1.1
concerning
\sigma(T;X)
are equivalent to

1\in\sigma(T;X)
and
\sigma(T;X)\setminus\{1\}\subseteq\overline{B(0,\delta)}
, for some
\delta\in(0,1)
.

2.
Continuity, Compactness and Spectrum of
C_{t}
in
H^{\infty}
and in
A({\mathbb{D}})

For
t=1
it is clear that

C_{1}
fails to act from
H^{\infty}
into itself; consider
C_{1}\mathbbm{1}
, where
\mathbbm{1}
is the constant function
z\mapsto 1
for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
. For a study of
C_{1}
acting on
H^{\infty}
see
[
9
]

and, for
C_{t}
acting on
H^{\infty}
, for
t\in[0,1)
, see
[
2
, Proposition 2.3]
.

The following result establishes that
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(A({\mathbb{D}}))
and identifies its operator norm precisely.

Proposition 2.1
.

For each
t\in[0,1)
the operator
C_{t}\colon A({\mathbb{D}})\to A({\mathbb{D}})
is continuous. Moreover, the operator norms are given by
\|C_{0}\|_{A({\mathbb{D}})\to A({\mathbb{D}})}=1
and

\|C_{t}\|_{A({\mathbb{D}})\to A({\mathbb{D}})}=\frac{-\log(1-t)}{t},\quad t\in(0,1).

Proof.

Fix
t\in[0,1)
. We first show that
C_{t}(A({\mathbb{D}}))\subseteq A({\mathbb{D}})
. Recall, for
f\in H({\mathbb{D}})
, that we have
(C_{t}f)(0)=f(0)
and, via the parametrization
\xi:=sz
, for
s\in[0,1]
, that

(2.1)

(C_{t}f)(z)=\frac{1}{z}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{f(\xi)}{1-t\xi}\,d\xi=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{f(sz)}{1-stz}\,ds,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\}.

Moreover, if
f\in H^{\infty}
then
C_{t}f\in H^{\infty}
,
[
2
, Proposition 2.3]
. Accordingly, for a fixed function
f\in A({\mathbb{D}})\subseteq H^{\infty}
, it follows that
C_{t}f\in H^{\infty}
. The claim is that
C_{t}f
is the restriction to
{\mathbb{D}}
of an element of
C(\overline{{\mathbb{D}}})
. Indeed, let
\tilde{f}\in C(\overline{{\mathbb{D}}})
agree with
f
on
{\mathbb{D}}
. Then it is routine to verify that the function
z\mapsto\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\tilde{f}(sz)}{1-stz}\,ds
, for
z\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
, is continuous on
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
and agrees with
C_{t}f
on
{\mathbb{D}}
. Accordingly,
C_{t}f\in A({\mathbb{D}})
.

Since
C_{t}(A({\mathbb{D}}))\subseteq A({\mathbb{D}})
, for
t\in[0,1)
, and
A({\mathbb{D}})
is a Banach space, by a closed graph argument we can conclude that
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(A({\mathbb{D}}))
. Indeed, let
f_{n}\to 0
in
A({\mathbb{D}})
and
C_{t}f_{n}\to g
in
A({\mathbb{D}})
for
n\to\infty
. Since
A({\mathbb{D}})\subseteq H({\mathbb{D}})
continuously, also
f_{n}\to 0
in
H({\mathbb{D}})
and
C_{t}f_{n}\to g
in
H({\mathbb{D}})
for
n\to\infty
. The continuity of
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H({\mathbb{D}}))
yields that
C_{t}f_{n}\to 0
in
H({\mathbb{D}})
for
n\to\infty
and so
g=0
.

We now compute the norm of
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(A({\mathbb{D}}))
for
t\in[0,1)
.

Consider first
t=0
.
Let
f\in A({\mathbb{D}})
be fixed. Then
f\in H^{\infty}
and so, by
[
2
, Proposition 2.3]
, we can conclude that

\|C_{0}f\|_{\infty}\leq\|f\|_{\infty}
.
Since
A({\mathbb{D}})
is a closed subspace of
H^{\infty}
,
this implies that
\|C_{0}\|_{A({\mathbb{D}})\to A({\mathbb{D}})}\leq 1
. On the other hand, the function
h_{0}(z):=1
, for
z\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
, belongs to
A({\mathbb{D}})
and satisfies both
\|h_{0}\|_{\infty}=1
and
C_{0}h_{0}=h_{0}
. It follows that
\|C_{0}\|_{A({\mathbb{D}})\to A({\mathbb{D}})}=1
.

Now let
t\in(0,1)
. Again by
[
2
, Proposition 2.3]
, for each
f\in A({\mathbb{D}})\subseteq H^{\infty}
we have that

\|C_{t}f\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{-\log(1-t)}{t}\|f\|_{\infty},

which implies that
\|C_{t}\|_{A({\mathbb{D}})\to A({\mathbb{D}})}\leq\frac{-\log(1-t)}{t}
. But,
\|C_{t}h_{0}\|_{\infty}=\frac{-\log(1-t)}{t}
and so
\|C_{t}\|_{A({\mathbb{D}})\to A({\mathbb{D}})}=\frac{-\log(1-t)}{t}
.
∎

Recall that a linear map
T\colon X\to Y
, with
X
and
Y
Banach spaces, is called
compact
if
T(B_{X})
is a relatively compact set in
Y
, where
B_{X}
denotes the closed unit ball of
X
. It is routine to show that necessarily
T\in{\mathcal{L}}(X,Y)
.
For the spectral theory for compact operators see
[
11
,
14
,
21
]
, for example.

The proof of the next result, which establishes the compactness of
C_{t}
on both
A({\mathbb{D}})
and
H^{\infty}
, proceeds along the lines of the proof of
[
2
, Proposition 2.7]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For each
t\in[0,1)
, both of the operators
C_{t}\colon H^{\infty}\to H^{\infty}
and
C_{t}\colon A({\mathbb{D}})\to A({\mathbb{D}})
are compact.

Proof.

Fix
t\in[0,1)
. Since
A({\mathbb{D}})
is a closed subspace of
H^{\infty}
and
C_{t}(A({\mathbb{D}}))\subseteq A({\mathbb{D}})
(cf. Proposition
2.1
),
it suffices to show that
C_{t}\colon H^{\infty}\to H^{\infty}
is compact.

First we establish the following Claim:

(
\star
)

Let a sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset H^{\infty}
satisfy
\|f_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
f_{n}\to 0
in
(H({\mathbb{D}}),\tau_{c})
for
n\to\infty
. Then
C_{t}f_{n}\to 0
in
H^{\infty}
.

To prove the Claim, let
(f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset H^{\infty}
be a sequence as in (
\star
).
Fix
\varepsilon>0
and select
\delta\in(0,\beta)
, where
\beta:=\min\{1,\frac{\varepsilon(1-t)}{2}\}
. Since
\overline{B(0,1-\delta)}
is a compact subset of
{\mathbb{D}}
, there exists
n_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

\max_{|\xi|\leq 1-\delta}|f_{n}(\xi)|<\delta,\quad n\geq n_{0}.

Recall that
(C_{t}f_{n})(0)=f_{n}(0)
for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Therefore,
(C_{t}f_{n})(0)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. For
z\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\}
we have, via (
2.1
), that

|(C_{t}f_{n})(z)|=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{f_{n}(sz)}{1-stz}ds\right|\leq\int_{0}^{1-\delta}\frac{|f_{n}(sz)|}{|1-stz|}ds+\int_{1-\delta}^{1}\frac{|f_{n}(sz)|}{|1-stz|}ds.

Denote the first (resp., second) summand in the right-side of the previous inequality by
(A_{n})
(resp., by
(B_{n})
).
Since
|1-stz|\geq 1-st|z|\geq\max\{1-s,1-t,1-|z|\}
, for all
s,t\in[0,1)
and
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, it follows, for every
n\geq n_{0}
, that
\int_{0}^{1-\delta}|f_{n}(sz)|\,ds\leq(1-\delta)\max_{|\xi|\leq(1-\delta)}|f_{n}(\xi)|
(as
|sz|\leq(1-\delta)
for all
s\in[0,1-\delta]
) and hence, that

(A_{n})\leq\frac{(1-\delta)}{1-t}\max_{|\xi|\leq 1-\delta}|f_{n}(\xi)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

On the other hand, for every
n\geq n_{0}
, observe that

(B_{n})=\int_{1-\delta}^{1}\frac{|f_{n}(sz)|}{|1-stz|}\,ds\leq\int_{1-\delta}^{1}\frac{\|f_{n}\|_{\infty}}{1-t}\,ds\leq\frac{\delta}{1-t}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

It follows that
\|C_{t}f_{n}\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
for every
n\geq n_{0}
. That is,
C_{t}f_{n}\to 0
in
H^{\infty}
for
n\to\infty
and so (
\star
) is proved.

Concerning the compactness of
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{\infty})
, let
(f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset H^{\infty}
be any bounded sequence. There is no loss of generality in assuming that
\|f_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. To establish the compactness of
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{\infty})
we need to show that
(C_{t}f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
has a convergent subsequence in
H^{\infty}
. This follows routinely from the fact that
(f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is also bounded in the Fréchet-Montel space
H({\mathbb{D}})
, as
H^{\infty}\subseteq H({\mathbb{D}})
continuously, together with condition (
\star
).
∎

Given a Fréchet space
X
and
T\in{\mathcal{L}}(X)
, the resolvent set
\rho(T;X)
of
T
consists of all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that the inverse operator
R(\lambda,T):=(\lambda I-T)^{-1}
exists in
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
. The set
\sigma(T;X):={\mathbb{C}}\setminus\rho(T;X)
is called the
spectrum
of
T
. The
point spectrum

\sigma_{pt}(T;X)
of
T
consists of all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
(also called an eigenvalue of
T
) such that
(\lambda I-T)
is not injective. In the event that
X
is a Banach space, the residual spectrum
\sigma_{r}(T;X)
(resp. continuous spectrum
\sigma_{c}(T;X)
) of
T
consists of all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\lambda I-T
is injective and
{\rm Im}(\lambda I-T)
is not dense (resp. is proper and dense) in
X
. This provides the pairwise disjoint decomposition

\sigma(T;X)=\sigma_{pt}(T;X)\cup\sigma_{r}(T;X)\cup\sigma_{c}(T;X).

Proposition 2.3
.

For each
t\in[0,1)
the spectra of
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{\infty})
and of
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(A({\mathbb{D}}))
are given by

(2.2)

\sigma_{pt}(C_{t};H^{\infty})=\sigma_{pt}(C_{t};A({\mathbb{D}}))=\left\{\frac{1}{m+1}\,:m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\right\},

and

(2.3)

\sigma(C_{t};H^{\infty})=\sigma(C_{t};A({\mathbb{D}}))=\left\{\frac{1}{m+1}\,:m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\right\}\cup\{0\}.

Proof.

Let
t\in[0,1)
be fixed.
By
[
8
, Lemma 3.6]
the point spectrum of the operator
C_{t}^{\omega}\in{\mathcal{L}}(\omega)
is given by
\sigma_{pt}(C_{t}^{\omega};\omega)=\{\frac{1}{m+1}\,:\,m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
and, for each
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, the corresponding eigenspace
\mathop{\rm Ker}(\frac{1}{m+1}I-C_{t}^{\omega})
is 1-dimensional and is generated by an eigenvector
x^{[m]}=(x_{n}^{[m]})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\in\ell^{1}
.
Since
A({\mathbb{D}})\subseteq H^{\infty}\subseteq H({\mathbb{D}})
with continuous inclusions and the map
\Phi\colon H({\mathbb{D}})\to\omega
given by
f\mapsto(\hat{f}(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
, is a continuous embedding (see
Section 1 of
[
2
]
), we have that
\sigma_{pt}(C_{t};A({\mathbb{D}}))\subseteq\sigma_{pt}(C_{t};H^{\infty})\subseteq\{\frac{1}{m+1}\,:\,m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
. Indeed, let
f\in H({\mathbb{D}})\setminus\{0\}
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
satisfy
C_{t}f=\lambda f
. Then
\lambda f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widehat{(\lambda f)}(n)z^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda\hat{f}(n)z^{n}
and, by (
1.6
), we have that
(C_{t}f)(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(C_{t}^{\omega}\hat{f})_{n}z^{n}
. It follows that
C_{t}^{\omega}\hat{f}=\lambda\hat{f}
in
\omega
with
\hat{f}\not=0
and so
\lambda\in\sigma_{pt}(C_{t}^{\omega};\omega)=\{\frac{1}{m+1}\ :\ m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
.

To conclude the proof, it remains to show that
\{\frac{1}{m+1}\,:\,m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}\subseteq\sigma_{pt}(C_{t};A({\mathbb{D}}))
. To establish this recall, for each
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, that the eigenvector
x^{[m]}
corresponding to
\frac{1}{m+1}
belongs to
\ell^{1}
and hence, the function
g_{m}(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^{[m]}_{n}z^{n}
belongs to
A({\mathbb{D}})
. Then (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) imply, for each
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, that

(2.4)

(C_{t}g_{m})(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(C_{t}^{\omega}x^{[m]})_{n}z^{n}=\frac{1}{m+1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^{[m]}_{n}z^{n}=\frac{1}{m+1}g_{m}(z).

Thus
g_{m}
is an eigenvector of
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(A({\mathbb{D}}))
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\frac{1}{m+1}
.

Finally,
\sigma(C_{t};A({\mathbb{D}}))=\sigma(C_{t};H^{\infty})=\{\frac{1}{m+1}\,:\,m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}\cup\{0\}
follows from the fact that
C_{t}
is a compact operator on both
A({\mathbb{D}})
and
H^{\infty}
.
∎

An operator
T\in{\mathcal{L}}(X)
, with
X
a Banach space, is called
power bounded
if
\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\|T^{n}\|_{X\to X}<\infty
. Given
T\in{\mathcal{L}}(X)
, define its Cesàro means by

T_{[n]}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}T^{m},\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Then
T
is said to be
mean ergodic
(resp.,
uniformly mean ergodic
) if
(T_{[n]})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a convergent sequence in
{\mathcal{L}}_{s}(X)
(resp., a convergent sequence for the operator norm in
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
). It is routine to check that
\frac{T^{n}}{n}=T_{[n]}-\frac{n-1}{n}T_{[n-1]}
, for
n\geq 2
, and hence,
\tau_{s}
-
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{T^{n}}{n}=0
whenever
T
is mean ergodic. An operator
T\in{\mathcal{L}}(X)
is said to be
supercyclic
if, for some
z\in X
, the projective orbit
\{\lambda T^{n}z\ :\ \lambda\in{\mathbb{C}},\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
is dense in
X
. Since the closure of the linear span of a projective orbit is separable, whenever any supercyclic operator in
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
exists, then
X
is necessarily separable.

For the linear dynamics of
T
we refer to
[
5
,
13
]
and for mean ergodic operators to
[
17
]
, for example.

As a consequence of
the previous proposition, combined with Theorem
1.1
, we have the following result.

Proposition 2.4
.

For each
t\in[0,1)
both of the operators
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{\infty})
and
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(A({\mathbb{D}}))
are power bounded, uniformly mean ergodic but, fail to be supercyclic.

Proof.

Fix
t\in[0,1)
. Since
H^{\infty}
is non-separable,
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{\infty})
cannot be supercyclic.

The operator
C_{t}
is compact on both
H^{\infty}
and on
A({\mathbb{D}})
(cf. Proposition
2.2
). Therefore, the compact transpose operators
C_{t}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{L}}((H^{\infty})^{\prime})
and
C^{\prime}_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}((A({\mathbb{D}}))^{\prime})
have the same non-zero eigenvalues as
C_{t}
(see, e.g.,
[
11
, Theorem 9.10-2(2)]
). In view of Proposition
2.3
it follows that
\sigma_{pt}(C_{t}^{\prime};(H^{\infty})^{\prime})=\sigma_{pt}(C_{t}^{\prime};(A({\mathbb{D}}))^{\prime})=\{\frac{1}{m+1}\,:\,m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
. We can apply
[
5
, Proposition 1.26]
to conclude that
C_{t}
is not supercyclic on the separable space
A({\mathbb{D}})
.

By Proposition
2.3
and its proof (as
x^{[0]}=(t^{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
) we have that
\mathop{\rm Ker}(I-C_{t})={\rm span}\{h_{t}\}
, with
h_{t}(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}z^{n}=\frac{1}{1-tz}
, for
z\in B(0,\frac{1}{t})
(with
\frac{1}{t}>1
). Note that
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}\subseteq B(0,\frac{1}{t})
. The function
h_{t}\in A({\mathbb{D}})
is the eigenvector denoted by
g_{0}
in the proof of Proposition
2.3
corresponding to the eigenvalue
1
. On the other hand,
{\rm Im}(I-C_{t})
is a closed subspace of
H^{\infty}
(resp., of
A({\mathbb{D}})
), as
C_{t}
is compact in
H^{\infty}
(resp., in
A({\mathbb{D}})
)). Also,
{\rm Im}(I-C_{t})\subseteq\{g\in H^{\infty}\,:\,g(0)=0\}
(resp.,
{\rm Im}(I-C_{t})\subseteq\{g\in A({\mathbb{D}})\,:\,g(0)=0\}
), because
(C_{t}f)(0)=f(0)
for each
f\in H^{\infty}
(resp., for each
f\in A({\mathbb{D}})
). Moreover,
[
11
, Theorem 9.10.1]
implies that
{\rm codim}\,{\rm Im}(I-C_{t})={\rm dim}\mathop{\rm Ker}(I-C_{t})=1
regarded as subspaces of
H^{\infty}
(resp., of
A({\mathbb{D}})
). Accordingly, both
{\rm Im}(I-C_{t})
and
\{g\in H^{\infty}\,:\,g(0)=0\}=\mathop{\rm Ker}(\delta_{0})
are hyperplanes of
H^{\infty}
(resp., of
A({\mathbb{D}})
). It follows that necessarily
{\rm Im}(I-C_{t})=\{g\in H^{\infty}\,:\,g(0)=0\}
(resp.,
{\rm Im}(I-C_{t})=\{g\in A({\mathbb{D}})\,:\,g(0)=0\}
).

Let
u\in{\rm Im}(I-C_{t})\cap\mathop{\rm Ker}(I-C_{t})
. Then
u(0)=0
and there exists
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
u=\lambda h_{t}
. This yields that
0=u(0)=\lambda h_{t}(0)=\lambda
. Hence,
u=0
. So,
{\rm Im}(I-C_{t})\cap\mathop{\rm Ker}(I-C_{t})=\{0\}
.

Proposition
2.3
implies that
1\in\sigma(C_{t};H^{\infty})=\sigma(C_{t};A({\mathbb{D}}))=\{\frac{1}{m+1}\,;\,m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}\cup\{0\}
. Consequently, for
\delta=\frac{1}{2}
, all the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
(see also Remark
1.2
) are satisfied. So, we can conclude that
C_{t}
is power bounded and uniformly mean ergodic both on
H^{\infty}
and on
A({\mathbb{D}})
.
∎

3.
Continuity, Compactness and Spectrum of
C_{t}
acting in
H^{p}

In this section we investigate various properties of the operators
C_{t}
when they act in the family of Hardy spaces
H^{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
, as well as some related Volterra type operators. We recall a known fact which follows easily from Jensen’s inequality for
{\mathbb{R}}
-valued functions,
[
20
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
h\colon[0,1]\to{\mathbb{C}}
be an integrable function. For each
p\geq 1
we have

\left|\int_{0}^{1}h(s)\,ds\right|^{p}\leq\int_{0}^{1}|h(s)|^{p}ds.

It follows from
[
4
, Theorem 1]
, by substituting for
g
there the particular function
g(z)=z
, for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, that the Hardy operator
C_{0}
is bounded in
H^{p}
for all
1\leq p<\infty
. We include a simple and direct proof of this fact based on Lemma
3.1
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. The Hardy operator
C_{0}\colon H^{p}\to H^{p}
is continuous with operator norm
\|C_{0}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}=1
.

Proof.

Recall from (
2.1
) that
the Hardy operator
C_{0}\colon H({\mathbb{D}})\to H({\mathbb{D}})
is given by
(C_{0}f)(0)=f(0)
for
z=0
and, for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\}
, by

(3.1)

(C_{0}f)(z)=\frac{1}{z}\int_{0}^{z}f(\xi)\,d\xi=\int_{0}^{1}f(sz)\,ds.

For
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
fixed,
s\mapsto f(sz)
is continuous on
[0,1]
and hence, integrable.
For every
f\in H^{p}
and
r\in[0,1)
, Lemma
3.1
and Fubini’s theorem imply that

\displaystyle M_{p}(r,C_{0}f)^{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|(C_{0}f)(re^{i\theta})|^{p}\,d\theta=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|\int_{0}^{1}f(sre^{i\theta})\,ds\right|^{p}d\theta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{1}|f(sre^{i\theta})|^{p}\,ds\right)\,d\theta=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(sre^{i\theta})|^{p}\,d\theta\right)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}M_{p}(sr,f)^{p}ds\leq M_{p}(r,f)^{p}\leq\|f\|^{p}_{p}.

This implies that
C_{0}f\in H^{p}
and
\|C_{0}f\|_{p}\leq\|f\|_{p}
for every
f\in H^{p}
. So,
C_{0}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
and
\|C_{0}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}\leq 1
. Since
h_{0}:=\mathbbm{1}\in H^{p}
with
\|h_{0}\|_{p}=\|\mathbbm{1}\|_{p}=1
and
C_{0}h_{0}=\mathbbm{1}=h_{0}
, it follows that
\|C_{0}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}=1
.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. For each
t\in(0,1)
the operator
C_{t}\colon H^{p}\to H^{p}
is continuous and its operator norm satisfies

(3.2)

1\leq\|C_{t}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}\leq\frac{1}{1-t},\quad t\in(0,1).

Actually, for
p=1
, it is the case that

(3.3)

\|C_{t}\|_{H^{1}\to H^{1}}\leq\frac{-\log(1-t)}{t},

and for
1<p<\infty,
it is the case that

(3.4)

\|C_{t}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}\leq\left[\frac{1}{t(p-1)}\left(\frac{1}{(1-t)^{p-1}}-1\right)\right]^{1/p}.

Proof.

Fix
p\geq 1
and
t\in(0,1)
. The function
h_{t}(z)=\frac{1}{1-tz}
, for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, belongs to
A({\mathbb{D}})\subseteq H^{\infty}
(see the proof of Proposition
2.4
) and satisfies
\|h_{t}\|_{\infty}=\frac{1}{1-t}
, because
|h_{t}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{1-t}
and
\lim_{z\to 1^{-}}h_{t}(z)=\frac{1}{1-t}
. Since
h_{t}\in H^{\infty}
, the multiplication operator
M_{t}\colon H^{p}\to H^{p}
defined by

M_{t}f:=h_{t}f,\quad f\in H^{p},

is continuous with
\|M_{t}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}\leq\|h_{t}\|_{\infty}=\frac{1}{1-t}
, as is well known (and routine to verify).

Since
C_{t}=C_{0}\circ M_{t}
(cf. (
2.1
) and (
3.1
)), it follows from Proposition
3.2
that
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
and that
\|C_{t}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}\leq\|C_{0}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}\cdot\|M_{t}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}=\frac{1}{1-t}
.

On the other hand, the function
h_{t}
belongs to
H^{p}
and satisfies
C_{t}h_{t}=h_{t}
. This yields that
1\leq\|C_{t}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}
. Hence, (
3.2
) has been established.

Concerning (
3.3
) let
p=1
. Then, for every
f\in H^{1}
and
r\in[0,1)
, by (
1.10
) and Fubini’s theorem we obtain

\displaystyle M_{1}(r,C_{t}f)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{f(sre^{i\theta})}{1-srte^{i\theta}}ds\right|d\theta\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|f(sre^{i\theta})|}{1-ts}ds\right)d\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{1-ts}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(sre^{i\theta})|d\theta\right)ds=\left(\frac{-\log(1-t)}{t}\right)M_{1}(r,f)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{-\log(1-t)}{t}\right)\|f\|_{1}.

Again via (
1.10
) it follows that
\|C_{t}f\|_{1}\leq\left(\frac{-\log(1-t)}{t}\right)\|f\|_{1}
, for every
f\in H^{1}
. Hence,
\|C_{t}\|_{H^{1}\to H^{1}}\leq\frac{-\log(1-t)}{t}
which is (
3.3
).

Let
1<p<\infty
. Then, for every
f\in H^{p}
and
r\in[0,1)
, by Fubini’s theorem and Lemma
3.1
(because
s\mapsto\frac{f(sz)}{1-stz}
is integrable over
[0,1]
for each
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
) we obtain via (
1.10
) that

\displaystyle M_{p}(r,C_{t}f)^{p}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{f(sre^{i\theta})}{1-srte^{i\theta}}ds\right|^{p}d\theta\leq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{(1-ts)^{p}}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(sre^{i\theta})|^{p}d\theta\right)ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{(1-ts)^{p}}M_{p}(rs,f)^{p}ds\leq\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{(1-ts)^{p}}ds\right)\|f\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t(p-1)}\left(\frac{1}{(1-t)^{p-1}}-1\right)\|f\|_{p}^{p}.

It follows that

\|C_{t}\|_{p}\leq\left[\frac{1}{t(p-1)}\left(\frac{1}{(1-t)^{p-1}}-1\right)\right]^{1/p}\|f\|_{p},\quad f\in H^{p},

from which (
3.4
) follows.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

For each
1\leq p<\infty
and
t\in(0,1)
the estimate of
\|C_{t}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}
given in (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) is better than the upper estimate given in (
3.2
). Indeed, for
p=1
we have
\frac{-\log(1-t)}{t}<\frac{1}{1-t}
for every
t\in(0,1)
, as was shown in
[
2
, Example 2.2]
. For a fixed
1<p<\infty
, observe that

(3.5)

\left[\frac{1}{t(p-1)}\left(\frac{1}{(1-t)^{p-1}}-1\right)\right]^{1/p}<\frac{1}{1-t},\quad t\in(0,1),

if and only if

\frac{1}{t(p-1)}\left(\frac{1}{(1-t)^{p-1}}-1\right)<\frac{1}{(1-t)^{p}},\quad t\in(0,1).

Therefore, the inequality (
3.5
) is satisfied if and only if

\frac{1-(1-t)^{p-1}}{t(p-1)}\cdot\frac{1}{(1-t)^{p-1}}<\frac{1}{(1-t)^{p}},\quad t\in(0,1),

that is, if and only if

(3.6)

\frac{1-(1-t)^{p-1}}{t(p-1)}<\frac{1}{1-t},\quad t\in(0,1).

To show the validity of (
3.6
) it suffices to establish, for each
\alpha>0
, that

(3.7)

\frac{1-(1-t)^{\alpha}}{\alpha t}<\frac{1}{1-t},\quad t\in(0,1).

So, fix
\alpha\geq 0
and define
\gamma(t)=[1-(1-t)^{\alpha}](1-t)-\alpha t
, for
t\in[0,1]
. The function
\gamma
is continuous in
[0,1]
and differentiable in
(0,1)
. Furthermore,
\gamma(0)=0
and
\gamma(1)=-\alpha<0
. On the other hand, for each
t\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\alpha(1-t)^{\alpha-1}(1-t)-[1-(1-t)^{\alpha}]-\alpha

\displaystyle=\alpha(1-t)^{\alpha}-1+(1-t)^{\alpha}-\alpha

\displaystyle=(\alpha+1)[(1-t)^{\alpha}-1].

Since
\alpha>0
and
0<(1-t)^{\alpha}<1
for
t\in(0,1)
, it follows that
(1-t)^{\alpha}-1<0
for
t\in(0,1)
. Accordingly,
\gamma^{\prime}(t)<0
for every
t\in(0,1)
. This means that the function
\gamma
is decreasing in
[0,1]
and hence,
\gamma(t)<\gamma(0)=0
for every
t\in(0,1)
. So, we can conclude that

\gamma(t)=[1-(1-t)^{\alpha}](1-t)-\alpha t<0,\quad t\in(0,1),

that is,

[1-(1-t)^{\alpha}](1-t)<\alpha t,\quad t\in(0,1).

The previous inequality implies that (
3.7
) is valid. This completes the proof that (
3.6
) is valid. Hence, also (
3.5
) is valid.

In order to establish the compactness of the operators
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
we need to introduce some additional operators and preliminaries.

For each
1\leq p\leq\infty
, define

H_{0}^{p}:=\{f\in H^{p}\,:\,f(0)=0\}.

Accordingly,
H_{0}^{p}=\mathop{\rm Ker}(\delta_{0})
is a closed 1-codimensional subspace of
H^{p}
. Moreover,
A_{0}({\mathbb{D}}):=\{f\in A({\mathbb{D}}):\,f(0)=0\}
is a closed 1-codimensional subspace of
A({\mathbb{D}})
.

Consider now the operator
S\colon H({\mathbb{D}})\to H({\mathbb{D}})
defined by

(Sf)(z):=zf(z),\quad f\in H({\mathbb{D}}),\ z\in{\mathbb{D}},

which belongs to
{\mathcal{L}}(H({\mathbb{D}}))
and is called
the
forward shift
. The following Lemma
3.5
concerning the forward and backward shift operators is certainly known. Observe that the operator
S^{-1}
in the proof of Lemma
3.5
below is the restriction to the closed subspace
H_{0}^{p}
of
H^{p}
of the
backward shift
operator given by
(Bf)(z):=(f(z)-f(0))/z
, for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
. This operator and its invariant subspaces on Hardy spaces have been thoroughly investigated in
[
6
]
. We present a formulation of Lemma
3.5
which is useful for our purposes; a proof is included for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Then
S\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
with
\|S\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}=1
and
S(H^{p})=H^{p}_{0}
. Moreover, the operator
S
is injective with the inverse operator
S^{-1}\colon S(H^{p})\to H^{p}
continuous and satisfying
\|S^{-1}\|_{S(H^{p})\to H^{p}}=1
.

The operator
S\in{\mathcal{L}}(A({\mathbb{D}}))
with operator norm
\|S\|_{A({\mathbb{D}})\to A({\mathbb{D}})}=1
and range
S(A({\mathbb{D}}))=A_{0}({\mathbb{D}})
. Moreover,
S
is injective with the inverse operator
S^{-1}\colon S(A({\mathbb{D}}))\to A({\mathbb{D}})
continuous and satisfying
\|S^{-1}\|_{S(A({\mathbb{D}}))\to A({\mathbb{D}})}=1
.

Proof.

Consider first the case
1\leq p<\infty
. Given
f\in H^{p}
and
r\in[0,1)
we have

M_{p}(r,Sf)^{p}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|re^{i\theta}f(re^{i\theta})|^{p}\,d\theta\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|^{p}\,d\theta=M_{p}(r,f)^{p}.

Accordingly,
\|Sf\|_{p}\leq\|f\|_{p}
, which implies that
S\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
and
\|S\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}\leq 1
. Moreover, for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, observe that

\|z^{n}\|_{p}=\sup_{0\leq r<1}M_{p}(r,z^{n})=\sup_{0\leq r<1}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|(re^{i\theta})^{n}|^{p}\,d\theta\right)^{1/p}=\sup_{0\leq r<1}r^{n}=1

and that
S(z^{n})=z^{n+1}
, from which we can conclude that
\|S\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}=1
.

Clearly,
S
is injective and satisfies
S(H^{p})\subseteq H^{p}_{0}
.

To show that
S(H^{p})=H_{0}^{p}
and that the inverse operator
S^{-1}\colon S(H^{p})\to H^{p}
is continuous, we proceed as follows.

Given
g\in H^{p}_{0}
, let
f(z):=\frac{g(z)}{z}=\frac{g(z)-g(0)}{z-0}
, for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
. Clearly
f\in H({\mathbb{D}})
as
z=0
is a removable singularity of
f
by setting
f(0):=g^{\prime}(0)
. On the other hand, for every
r\in(0,1)
, we have

M_{p}(r,f)^{p}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|^{p}\,d\theta=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{|g(re^{i\theta})|^{p}}{r^{p}}\,d\theta=\frac{1}{r^{p}}M_{p}(r,g)^{p}

It follows from (
1.10
)
that
f\in H^{p}
with
g=Sf
and
\|f\|_{p}\leq\|g\|_{p}
. Hence,
S(H^{p})=H_{0}^{p}
(in particular
S(H^{p})
is closed in
H^{p}
) and the inverse operator
S^{-1}\colon S(H^{p})\to H^{p}
is continuous. Moreover,
\|S^{-1}\|_{S(H^{p})\to H^{p}}=1
because
\|S^{-1}g\|_{p}=\|f\|_{p}\leq\|g\|_{p}
and the function
g_{0}(z):=z
, for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, belongs to
H^{p}_{0}
and satisfies
\|g_{0}\|_{p}=1
with
S^{-1}g_{0}=\mathbbm{1}
.

The case
p=\infty
follows along the same lines. We only observe that

\|Sf\|_{\infty}=\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{D}}}|zf(z)|\leq\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{D}}}|f(z)|=\|f\|_{\infty},\quad f\in H^{\infty},

and that
\|S\mathbbm{1}\|_{\infty}=\|g_{0}\|_{\infty}=1
. So,
S\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{\infty})
and
\|S\|_{H^{\infty}\to H^{\infty}}=1
.

Moreover, if
g\in H^{\infty}_{0}
, then the function
f(z):=\frac{g(z)}{z}
, for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\}
, and
f(0):=g^{\prime}(0)
belongs to
H^{\infty}
(see above) and satisfies, for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
r\in(\frac{k}{k+1},1)
, the inequality

M_{\infty}(r,f)=\max_{|z|=r}\left|\frac{g(z)}{z}\right|\leq\frac{k+1}{k}M_{\infty}(r,g).

It follows that
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{k+1}{k}\|g\|_{\infty}
for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. This implies that
f\in H^{\infty}
with
g=Sf
and
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq\|g\|_{\infty}
. Now proceed as for
H^{p}
with
1\leq p<\infty
.

Concerning
A({\mathbb{D}})
, note that it is a closed, invariant subspace of
S\colon H^{\infty}\to H^{\infty}
and so
S\in{\mathcal{L}}(A({\mathbb{D}}))
with
\|S\|_{A({\mathbb{D}})\to A({\mathbb{D}})}\leq 1
. Since
\mathbbm{1}\in A({\mathbb{D}})
satisfies
\|S\mathbbm{1}\|_{\infty}=\|g_{0}\|_{\infty}=1
, it follows that
\|S\|_{A({\mathbb{D}})\to A({\mathbb{D}})}=1
, Clearly
S(A({\mathbb{D}}))\subseteq A_{0}({\mathbb{D}})
. As for
H^{\infty}
it can be verified that
S(A({\mathbb{D}}))=A_{0}({\mathbb{D}})
, that
S
is injective and that
\|S^{-1}\|_{S(A({\mathbb{D}}))\to A({\mathbb{D}})}=1
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

It follows from
\|S\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}=1
, for each
1\leq p\leq\infty
, that the operator
S\colon H^{p}\to H^{p}_{0}
also satisfies
\|S\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}_{0}}=1
.

We now investigate a further class of operators.
For a fixed
g\in H({\mathbb{D}})
, let us consider the operators
V_{g}\colon H({\mathbb{D}})\to H({\mathbb{D}})
and
T_{g}\colon H({\mathbb{D}})\to H({\mathbb{D}})
defined by

(3.8)

(V_{g}f)(0):=f(0),\ (V_{g}f)(z):=\frac{1}{z}\int_{0}^{z}f(\xi)g^{\prime}(\xi)\,d\xi,\quad f\in H({\mathbb{D}}),\ z\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\},

and

(3.9)

(T_{g}f)(z):=\int_{0}^{z}f(\xi)g^{\prime}(\xi)\,d\xi,\quad f\in H({\mathbb{D}}),\ z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

Note that
(T_{g}f)(0)=0
for each
f\in H({\mathbb{D}})
. The operators
V_{g}
and
T_{g}
are called
Volterra-type
operators. Both operators
V_{g}
and
T_{g}
act continuously in
H({\mathbb{D}})
.
They have been investigated on different spaces of holomorphic functions by many authors. We refer to
[
7
,
23
]
and the references therein.

The important connection to this paper is that the generalized Cesàro operators
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H({\mathbb{D}}))
, for
t\in(0,1)
, are Volterra type operators of the kind
V_{g}
for suitable functions
g
. Indeed, fix
t\in(0,1)
. Given
f\in H({\mathbb{D}})
, by (
1.5
) we have that
(C_{t}f)(0)=f(0)
and

\displaystyle(C_{t}f)(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{f(\xi)}{1-t\xi}\,d\xi=\frac{1}{z}\int_{0}^{z}f(\xi)\frac{1}{1-t\xi}\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z}\int_{0}^{z}f(\xi)\left(\frac{-\log(1-t\xi)}{t}\right)^{\prime}\,d\xi,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\},

where the function

(3.10)

g_{t}(z):=\frac{-\log(1-tz)}{t}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n+1}z^{n}

is holomorphic on
B(0,1/t)
with
1/t>1
. Since
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}\subseteq B(0,1/t)
, we can conclude that
g_{t}\in A({\mathbb{D}})\subseteq H({\mathbb{D}})
. Accordingly,

(3.11)

C_{t}f=V_{g_{t}}f,\quad f\in H({\mathbb{D}}).

Topological properties such as continuity and (weak) compactness of the operators
V_{g}
and
T_{g}
, for
g\in H({\mathbb{D}})
, when acting in
H^{p}
are related to each other as the following result shows.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
g\in H({\mathbb{D}})
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Then
V_{g}\colon H^{p}\to H^{p}
is continuous (resp. compact, resp. weakly compact) if and only if
T_{g}\colon H^{p}\to H^{p}
is continuous (resp. compact, resp. weakly compact). In the case of continuity we have
\|T_{g}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}=\|V_{g}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}
.

Proof.

Fix
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Assume first that
V_{g}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
. By Lemma
3.5
the forward shift operator
S\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
and so also
T_{g}=S\circ V_{g}
belongs to
{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
. Conversely, suppose that
T_{g}\colon H^{p}\to H^{p}
is continuous. Clearly,
T_{g}(H^{p})\subseteq H^{p}_{0}
and
T_{g}\colon H^{p}\to H^{p}_{0}
is also continuous. Since the inverse operator
S^{-1}\colon H^{p}_{0}\to H^{p}
exists and is continuous, the operator
V_{g}=S^{-1}\circ T_{g}
belongs to
{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
. The proof for the compactness (resp. weak compactness) follows along the same lines.

From the fact that
\|S\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}_{0}}=\|S^{-1}\|_{H^{p}_{0}\to H^{p}}=1
(cf. Lemma
3.5
and Remark
3.6
), together with
T_{g}=S\circ V_{g}
and
V_{g}=S^{-1}\circ T_{g}
, it follows that
\|T_{g}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}=\|V_{g}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}
.
∎

The following definitions play an important role; see
[
24
]
for more details.
The space
BMOA
consists of all functions
f\in H^{2}
such that

|f(0)|+\sup_{a\in{\mathbb{D}}}\|f\circ\phi_{a}-f(a)\|_{2}<\infty,

where
\phi_{a}
, for
a\in{\mathbb{D}}
, is the family of Möbius automorphisms of
{\mathbb{D}}
given by
\phi_{a}(z):=\frac{z-a}{1-\overline{a}z}
, for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
. The space
VMOA
consists of all functions
f\in BMOA
satisfying

\lim_{|a|\to 1}\|f\circ\phi_{a}-f(a)\|_{2}=0.

The space
VMOA
is the closure of the polynomials in
BMOA
,
[
12
, Theorem 5.5]
. In particular,
H^{\infty}\subseteq BMOA
and
A({\mathbb{D}})\subseteq VMOA
,
[
12
, Theorem 5.5 and Remark 5.2]
.

The following result collects together various facts concerning the operators
T_{g}
when they act in the
H^{p}
-spaces.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
.

(i)

The operator
T_{g}\colon H^{p}\to H^{p}
is compact if and only if
g\in VMOA
. In particular, if
g\in A({\mathbb{D}})
, then both
T_{g}\colon H^{p}\to H^{p}
and
V_{g}=S^{-1}\circ T_{g}\colon H^{p}\to H^{p}
are compact.

(ii)

Let
t\in(0,1)
. The generalized Cesàro operator
C_{t}\colon H^{p}\to H^{p}
is compact.
Hence, also the operator
S_{t}:=T_{g_{t}}=S\circ C_{t}\colon H^{p}\to H^{p}
is compact, where
g_{t}
is given by (
3.10
).

For part (i) of Theorem
3.8
we refer to
[
4
]
and, for all
p>0
, to
[
3
]
; see also
[
23
, Corollary 4.2]
. Part (ii) of Theorem
3.8
follows from part (i) after recalling (see (
3.11
)), for each
t\in[0,1)
, that
C_{t}=V_{g_{t}}
with
g_{t}\in A({\mathbb{D}})
.

Compactness criteria for the operators
T_{g}
on both
H^{\infty}
and on
A({\mathbb{D}})
are formulated in the following result.

Theorem 3.9
.

(i)
Let
g\in H({\mathbb{D}})
and
T_{g}\colon H^{\infty}\to H^{\infty}
be weakly compact. Then
g\in A({\mathbb{D}})
.

(ii)
For
g\in H({\mathbb{D}})
the following statements are equivalent.

(a)

T_{g}\colon H^{\infty}\to H^{\infty}
is compact.

(b)

T_{g}\colon A({\mathbb{D}})\to A({\mathbb{D}})
is compact.

(c)

T_{g}\colon H^{\infty}\to A({\mathbb{D}})
is compact.

(d)

T_{g}\colon A({\mathbb{D}})\to H^{\infty}
is compact.

If either one of
(a)–(d)
holds, then necessarily
g\in A({\mathbb{D}})
.

We point out that parts (i) and (ii) of Theorem
3.9
are, respectively, Theorem 1.4 and Theorem 1.7 in
[
7
]
. The statement in part (ii) that
g\in A({\mathbb{D}})
follows from part (i).

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
t\in[0,1)
. Then
C_{t}(H^{\infty})\subseteq A({\mathbb{D}})
.

Proof.

Fix
t\in[0,1)
. By Proposition
2.2
the operator
C_{t}=V_{g_{t}}\colon H^{\infty}\to H^{\infty}
is compact and hence, by Lemma
3.7
the operator
T_{g_{t}}\colon H^{\infty}\to H^{\infty}
is compact, where
g_{t}
is given in (
3.10
). So, Theorem
3.9
(ii) ensures that the operator
T_{g_{t}}\colon A({\mathbb{D}})\to A({\mathbb{D}})
is also compact and satisfies
T_{g_{t}}(H^{\infty})\subseteq A({\mathbb{D}})
. Since
(T_{g_{t}}f)(0)=0
, for every
f\in H({\mathbb{D}})
, actually
T_{g_{t}}(A({\mathbb{D}}))\subseteq A_{0}({\mathbb{D}})
. Moreover, Lemma
3.5
shows that
S^{-1}\colon A_{0}({\mathbb{D}})\to A({\mathbb{D}})
continuously. Hence,
C_{t}=V_{g_{t}}=S^{-1}\circ T_{g_{t}}\colon A({\mathbb{D}})\to A({\mathbb{D}})
is compact and
C_{t}(H^{\infty})=V_{g_{t}}(H^{\infty})=S^{-1}(T_{g_{t}}(H^{\infty}))\subseteq A({\mathbb{D}})
.
∎

We are now able to calculate the spectrum of the generalized Cesàro operator
C_{t}
and of the operator
S_{t}=S\circ C_{t}
, for
t\in[0,1)
, when they act in
H^{p}
.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. For each
t\in[0,1)
the spectra of
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
are given by

(3.12)

\sigma_{pt}(C_{t};H^{p})=\left\{\frac{1}{m+1}\,:m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\right\},

and

(3.13)

\sigma(C_{t};H^{p})=\left\{\frac{1}{m+1}\,:m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\right\}\cup\{0\}.

Proof.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and
t\in[0,1)
be fixed.
Recall, by
[
2
, Proposition 3.7]
, that the point spectrum of
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H({\mathbb{D}}))
is given by
\sigma_{pt}(C_{t};H({\mathbb{D}}))=\{\frac{1}{m+1}\,:\,m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
. Since
A({\mathbb{D}})\subseteq H^{p}\subseteq H({\mathbb{D}})
with continuous inclusions, we obtain that
\sigma_{pt}(C_{t};A({\mathbb{D}}))\subseteq\sigma_{pt}(C_{t};H^{p})\subseteq\sigma_{pt}(C_{t};H({\mathbb{D}}))
. Then Proposition
2.3
implies the validity of (
3.12
).

Finally, (
3.13
) follows from the fact that
C_{t}
is a compact operator on
H^{p}
; see Theorem
3.8
(ii).
∎

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
.
For each
t\in[0,1)
the spectra of
S_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
are given by

(3.14)

\sigma_{pt}(S_{t};H^{p})=\emptyset;\ \sigma_{r}(S_{t};H^{p})=\{0\};\ \sigma_{c}(S_{t};H^{p})=\emptyset.

In particular,
\sigma(S_{t};H^{p})=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and
t\in(0,1)
be given. For a fixed
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
the equation

\lambda f-S_{t}f=0
yields, after differentiation, that
\lambda f^{\prime}(z)=\frac{f(z)}{1-tz}
for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, which has the solutions

f(z)=A(1-tz)^{-1/(t\lambda)}
for constants
A\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Moreover,
S_{t}f=\lambda f
together with
S_{t}(H^{p})\subseteq H^{p}_{0}
implies that
f(0)=0
, that is,
A=0
and hence,
f\equiv 0
.

For
t=0
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
we consider the solutions of
\lambda f-S_{0}f=0
, for
f\in H^{p}
. From the definition of
S_{0}
this equation reduces to
\int_{0}^{z}f(\xi)d\xi=\lambda f(z)
. Differentiating yields
f^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{\lambda}f(z)
and so
f(z)=Be^{z/\lambda}
for some
B\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Since
(S_{0}h)(0)=0
for every
h\in H({\mathbb{D}})
, it follows that
B=0
. Hence,
f\equiv 0
is the only solution.

Finally, for
\lambda=0
the equation
S_{t}f=0
implies
f\equiv 0
as both
S\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
and
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
are injective.

So, we have established that
\sigma_{pt}(S_{t};H^{p})=\emptyset
.

Since the operator
S_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
is compact (cf. Theorem
3.8
(ii)), it follows that
\sigma(S_{t};H^{p})=\sigma_{pt}(S_{t};H^{p})\cup\{0\}=\{0\}
. Moreover,
S_{t}
is injective and
S_{t}(H^{p})=(S\circ C_{t})(H^{p})\subseteq S(H^{p})\subseteq H_{0}^{p}
show that
S_{t}(H^{p})
is not dense in
H^{p}
. Hence,
0\in\sigma_{r}(S_{t};H^{p})
. The proof of (
3.14
) is thereby complete.
∎

Our final result establishes certain linear dynamic features of
C_{t}
and
S_{t}
.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. For each
t\in[0,1)
both of the operators
C_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
and
S_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
are power bounded, uniformly mean ergodic and fail to be supercyclic.

Proof.

Fix
t\in[0,1)
. The operator
C_{t}
is compact in
H^{p}
by Theorem
3.8
(ii). Therefore, the transpose operator
C_{t}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{L}}((H^{p})^{\prime})
, which is also compact, has the same non-zero eigenvalues as
C_{t}
; see
[
11
, Theorem 9.10-2(2)]
. In view of Proposition
3.11
it follows that
\sigma_{pt}(C_{t}^{\prime};(H^{p})^{\prime})=\{\frac{1}{m+1}\,:\,m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
. So, we can apply
[
5
, Proposition 1.26]
to conclude that
C_{t}
is not supercyclic on
H^{p}
.

Arguing as in the proof of Proposition
2.4

one shows that
{\rm Im}(I-C_{t})\cap\mathop{\rm Ker}(I-C_{t})=\{0\}
. On the other hand,
Proposition
3.11
implies that
1\in\sigma(C_{t};H^{p})=\{\frac{1}{m+1}\,;\,m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}\cup\{0\}
. Consequently, for
\delta=\frac{1}{2}
, all the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
are satisfied; see also Remark
1.2
. So, we can conclude that
C_{t}
is power bounded and uniformly mean ergodic on
H^{p}
.

The operator
S_{t}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
is also power bounded and uniformly mean ergodic. Indeed, Proposition
3.12
implies that its spectral radius
r(S_{t};H^{p})=0
.
On the other hand, it is known that also
r(S_{t};H^{p})=\lim_{n\to\infty}(\|S_{t}^{n}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}})^{1/n}
; see pp.234-235 of
[
21
]
for the unital Banach algebra
\mathcal{A}:={\mathcal{L}}(H^{p})
. Accordingly,
\|S_{t}^{n}\|_{H^{p}\to H^{p}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
and so
S_{t}
is surely power bounded.

Given
f\in H^{p}
, its projective orbit satisfies

\{\lambda S_{t}^{n}f:\ \lambda\in{\mathbb{C}},\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}\subseteq{\rm span}(\{f\})\cup\{\lambda S_{t}^{n}f:\ \lambda\in{\mathbb{C}},\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}.

But,
S_{t}^{n}(H^{p})\subseteq H^{p}_{0}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and so

(3.15)

\{\lambda S_{t}^{n}f:\ \lambda\in{\mathbb{C}},\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}\subseteq{\rm span}(\{f\})\cup H^{p}_{0}.

Since both
{\rm span}(\{f\})
and
H^{p}_{0}
are proper closed subsets of
H^{p}
, also their union is a proper closed subset of
H^{p}
. It follows from (
3.15
) that the projective orbit of
f
cannot be dense in
H^{p}
. Since
f\in H^{p}
is arbitrary, we can conclude that
S_{t}
is not supercyclic.
∎
