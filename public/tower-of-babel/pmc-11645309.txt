Examining the impact of obstructive sleep apnea on cognitive function in severe COPD
Authors: Kristina Kock Hansen, Anders Løkke, Hanne Irene Jensen, Eline Kirstine Gantzhorn, Ingeborg Farver-Vestergaard, Ole Hilberg
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to examine the association between obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and cognitive function in patients with severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).MethodsPatients with severe COPD, who had not previously been diagnosed with OSA, were recruited between January 2021 and January 2023. To evaluate cognitive function, the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), a continuous reaction time test (CRT) and a driving simulator were used. OSA was identified using a CardioRespiratory Monitor (CRM). Cognitive tests were repeated for patients who were identified with and received treatment for OSA, to determine whether treatment improved cognitive function.ResultsIn total, 80 patients participated in the study and 50 patients (63%) were diagnosed with OSA, with or without nocturnal desaturation (ND), and six patients (8%) with ND only. Thirty-two patients (40%) had mild OSA and 18 (23%) had moderate/severe OSA. We found no statistically significant difference in the prevalence of cognitive impairment (CI) between patients with and without OSA and/or ND. However, a multiple regression analysis showed that patients with moderate/severe OSA had a significant lower CRT-index, indicating a decrease in attention and reaction time, compared to patients with mild OSA.ConclusionIn patients with COPD and OSA, the degree of CI may depend on the severity of OSA. The 23% prevalence of moderate/severe OSA emphasizes the importance of OSA screening in severe COPD.The study was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov in March 2020, with the identification number NCT04458038.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11325-024-03228-5.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is characterized by upper airway obstruction, complete or partial, leading to reduced or absent breathing during sleep [1]. OSA causes serious metabolic and cardiovascular problems. Sleep fragmentation, nocturnal intermittent hypoxemia and a change in respiratory effort are all symptoms of OSA. Higher age, male sex, obesity, smoking and excessive alcohol consumption are risk factors associated with OSA [2].

OSA is a prevalent comorbidity in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [3] and more frequent in COPD than in in the general population. A recent review by Lurie et al. [4] shows that the prevalence of OSA in the general population is approximately 5% for men and 3% for women. In a study by Soler et al. [5], sleep apnea was found in 65.9% of patients with moderate to severe COPD. However, in the review by Lurie et al. [4] they found a prevalence ranging from 2.9 to 65.9%, indicating a substantial degree of study variation.

Overlap syndrome is the term used to describe OSA in patients with COPD. Hypoxemia and systemic inflammation are two effects shared by COPD and OSA that can lead to cardiovascular disease and other comorbidities [6, 7]. Compared to individuals with COPD or OSA alone, patients with overlap syndrome have more severe nocturnal desaturation (ND) as well as daytime hypoxemia and hypercapnia [8]. Nocturnal hypoxemia is found to be a powerful predictor of sudden cardiac death [9].

Both COPD and OSA are associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment (CI) [10]. CI in patients with COPD is associated with reduced physical activity and decreased adherence to inhalation therapy [11]. In not yet published data from our research team, we found that four out of ten patients with severe or very severe COPD had CI based on Montreal Cognitive Assessment score (MoCA). However, prevalence estimates of CI in patients with COPD are inconsistent across studies, ranging from 10.4 to 77% [12, 13]. Furthermore, some studies state that untreated OSA is associated with daytime sleepiness, cognitive decline and is a possible predictor for developing Alzheimer disease [14, 15]. Additionally, nocturnal hypoxemia is strongly associated with dementia [16]. To our knowledge, only two studies [17, 18] have examined cognitive function in patients with overlap syndrome. These studies show that CI is more prevalent in patients with overlap syndrome compared to COPD or OSA alone. Some studies [19, 20] find improvements in cognitive function after continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) treatment. However, a study by Henke et al. [21] find no differences in cognitive function after treatment. These studies only examine patients with OSA and not overlap syndrome [19–21].

Therefore, the aim of our present study was to examine if there was an association between OSA and cognitive function in patients with severe COPD. Furthermore, we wanted to study if patients diagnosed with symptomatic OSA, with or without ND, showed an improvement in cognitive function after minimum four months of treatment with CPAP or nocturnal oxygen therapy. Our hypotheses were that overlap syndrome was associated with CI and that cognitive function would improve with treatment.

Methods

Between January 2021 and January 2023, patients with severe COPD were enrolled from the Outpatient Clinic of Respiratory Diseases at a Danish University Hospital. This study is part of a larger study that also included a control group of non-COPD individuals, used for comparison of cognitive function [22]. However, in the present study, this control group is excluded due to the study’s aim.

Study population

Patients with severe or very severe COPD (forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) < 50% of expected), as defined by the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) 2019 classification [23], were eligible to participate in the study. A valid driver’s license, the age range of 40 to 75 and the absence of exacerbations within the previous three weeks were additional requirements for eligibility.

Patients were excluded from the study if they met one of the following criteria: (1) were physically unable to operate a driving simulator; (2) needed oxygen therapy; (3) had a history of obstructive sleep apnea; (4) consumed a large amount of alcohol (> 21 units per week for men; >14 units per week for women); (5) had uncorrected vision or hearing loss; (6) had serious uncontrolled comorbidities; (7) did not read or understand Danish. Prior to data collection, all subjects provided written informed consent.

Data collection

KKH, who had prior training utilizing the cognitive evaluation battery used in the current study, collected the data. It involved the completion of questionnaires, clinical and cognitive assessments with a total duration of 2½−3 h. In order to reduce the impact of fatigue on cognitive assessment results, we conducted the cognitive tests initially, followed by the clinical evaluation and questionnaires.

Cognitive assessment

A series of neuropsychological tests were used to evaluate cognitive abilities. Patients with an overall MoCA score < 26 were considered cases with CI, while patients who received an overall MoCA score ≥ 26 were considered as cases without CI. In the research by Nasreddine et al. [24] they identified the cutoff score of 26, as the point that achieved the optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity. The following five selected MoCA domains were evaluated using the domain definitions by Nasreddine et al. [24]: executive functioning, attention, language, visuospatial ability and short-term memory using delayed recall. A 20-minute driving simulator test was conducted [25]. It was used to assess perception, attention and reaction abilities [26]. Patients also completed the Continuous Reaction Time (CRT) test, which measures sustained attention and attention stability, including reaction time and stability in reaction time [27]. A CRT-index > 1.9 is considered normal [27].

Questionnaires and clinical assessment

Patients completed the COPD Assessment Test (CAT) [28], the modified Medical Research Council breathlessness scale (mMRC) [29], the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) [30] and provided information about their age, sex, height, weight and smoking history. Medical records were used to gather data on patients´ exacerbation history and comorbidities, including Charlson Comorbidity Index Score. Blood pressure, pulse, exercise capacity (measured by the 6-minute walk test (6MWT)) [31], arterial oxygen saturation (SaO2), arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2), arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), forced vital capacity (FVC) (L), FVC% predicted, FEV1 (L), FEV1% predicted and FEV1/FVC were all measured.

Sleep apnea

Polysomnography (PSG) is the gold standard for diagnosing OSA. However, in-hospital PSG is not performed under typical sleep conditions, and a full PSG involves high costs in both equipment, health professionals and patient time [32]. Resources that were unavailable in this project. Therefore, to diagnose OSA, we used the CardioRespiratory Monitor (CRM), NOX T3™ (ResMed, Denmark), which serve as the standard diagnostic tool for OSA in Outpatient Sleep Clinics across Europe. During sleep, the CRM measures breathing effort, nasal airflow, pulse, oxygen saturation, snoring and body position. The CRM was provided to the patients, along with guidance on its usage at home that same night, with a requirement to return it the following day. First author KKH uploaded the sleep data. To avoid including “wake time” before the patient fell asleep, sleep recordings were reviewed and manually assessed to ensure a more accurate analysis focused solely on actual sleep. This adjustment was deemed necessary, as PSG was not available. The sleep apnea specialist, co-author EKG, personally reviewed and analyzed all the sleep measurements.

The following sleep data were registered: Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI), Oxygen Desaturation Index (ODI), percentage of sleep with oxygen saturation under 90% (T90), average saturation (%), and minimum saturation (%). AHI is number of respiratory arrests (flow < 10%) ≥ 10 s per hour and number of flow reduction (≥ 30%) ≥ 10 s escorted by desaturation ≥ 3%. Mild OSA is defined as an AHI of 5 to 14 per hour, moderate OSA as an AHI of 15 to 29 per hour and severe OSA as an AHI ≥ 30 per hour [33]. Patients were prescribed treatment only if they had mild OSA accompanied by symptoms such as headache, daytime fatigue, snoring, difficulty concentrating, nocturia or poor quality of sleep [34]. Patients with moderate and severe OSA were offered treatment regardless. ODI is the average number of desaturation episodes per hour. Patients with T90 > 30% were registered as having ND [35].

OSA treatment and repeated measurement

Patients diagnosed with symptomatic OSA/ND with AHI ≥ 5 were offered treatment in the hospital´s Outpatient Sleep Clinic. Standard treatment for patients with OSA is CPAP [36]. Patients with ND, who did not cooperate with the CPAP, were switched to nocturnal oxygen therapy as single treatment. After minimum four months of treatment, patients with overlap syndrome were invited to a repeated cognitive function test, with the purpose of evaluating the effect of the OSA treatment on patients´ cognitive functions.

Statistics

Descriptive analyses were conducted, and continuous data were checked for normal distribution.

Unpaired t-tests were used to compare normally distributed outcomes. Mann Whitney U-tests were used for ordinal and not normally distributed continuous data. The Chi square test was used for categorical data. A multiple regression analysis was performed to examine the association between cognitive function and the severity of OSA, adjusting for covariates associated with cognitive function (age, sex, years of education and Charlson Comorbidity Index) [37–40]. Paired t-tests for continuous and McNemar for categorical paired data were used to examine differences in cognitive function in patients diagnosed with OSA before versus after OSA treatment. Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for exploring differences in non-normally distributed data. Independent variables that were not normally distributed were transformed into logarithmic data to gain normality. All comparisons were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as a level of significance of 5%. Estimates were presented with a 95% confidence interval A sample size calculation was conducted and is detailed in the study by Hansen et al. (2023) [22]. Stata version 17.0 was used for the statistical analysis.

Results

Patient characteristics

Study flowchart is illustrated in Fig. 1. The study included 80 patients with COPD. We found a high prevalence of patients with OSA with and without ND in this study. Fifty patients were diagnosed with symptomatic OSA (63%) and six patients (8%) with ND only. Out of the 50 patients with OSA, 32 (40%) had mild OSA, while 18 (23%) had moderate to severe OSA. Patients with ND alone, with mild or moderate to severe OSA (with or without ND) as well as no OSA or ND differed on some physical and sleep apnea variables (Table 1). Patients with OSA had higher Body Mass Index (BMI) compared to patients with ND alone or no OSA or ND. Out of the 56 patients diagnosed with OSA/ND, 36 patients were offered and accepted treatment with CPAP/nocturnal oxygen therapy and 20 patients refused. Eight patients did not continue the treatment because of difficulties cooperating with the CPAP mask or change in health status. After minimum four months of treatment, 28 patients were still in treatment. Twenty patients with overlap syndrome participated in cognitive testing both before and after OSA treatment.

Cognitive function and overlap syndrome

We found no statistically significant difference in the prevalence of CI between patients with and without OSA/ND (Table 2). Patients with OSA, with or without ND, scored statistically significant higher in the specific cognitive domain of executive functioning, compared to patients without OSA or ND. We found no significant differences in CRT-index and driving outcomes between the two groups. However, when we looked at the two OSA severity groups, we found that patients with moderate/severe OSA, with or without ND, had a significant decrease in CRT-index (indicating a reduced stability in reaction time) compared to patients with mild OSA after adjustment (Table 3). Changes in cognitive function after treatment

Twenty individuals with overlap syndrome were examined after four months of treatment, and we found an improvement, based on the distributions of the medians, in the specific cognitive domain short-term memory in patients who completed Test 1 and 2 (Table 4). When calculated as means, the results were 3.25 for Test 1 and 3.85 for Test 2, making the significant difference between the two results more apparent. Discussion

In our present study, we found a 63% prevalence of OSA in a group of patients with severe and very severe COPD. Eighteen patients (23%) were diagnosed with moderate to severe OSA. Compared to patients with COPD alone, we did not find any associations between OSA and cognitive function in patients with severe COPD. However, patients with moderate to severe OSA exhibited poorer attention and reaction time compared to those with mild OSA.

Prevalence of OSA

According to a review by Shawon et al. [3], patients with COPD appear to have a wide range of OSA prevalence, from 2.9 to 65.9%; placing the 63% prevalence of our study in the relatively high end. However, according to a review by Lurie et al. [4], prospective studies are needed to assess prevalence in specific populations such as in patients with GOLD 2–4. In a study by Soler et al. [5], 44 patients with moderate to severe COPD had full PSG, and 65.9% of these individuals were diagnosed with OSA. Our study, which doubled the sample size of Soler et al.‘s study and concentrated on patients in GOLD 3 and 4, contributes to the indicated knowledge gap.

The importance of severity of OSA on cognitive function

Few studies [17, 18] have examined cognitive function in patients with overlap syndrome. A Chinese study by Wang et al. [17] states that CI is significantly more common in patients with overlap syndrome compared to COPD alone, and specifically severe OSA in overlap syndrome is associated with CI. Similar to our study their findings were also based on MoCA score. However, while we included patients in the OSA group in our study with an AHI ≥ 5, the Chinese study defined OSA as having an AHI ≥ 15 per hour. As we saw in both studies, moderate and severe OSA is an explaining factor to CI and therefore using AHI ≥ 15 may result in a higher prevalence of CI. Furthermore, Wang et al. used a cut-off value at 23 instead of 26, as we used in our study. Using 26 as a cut-off value may have underestimated our results compared to the study by Wang et al. [17].

The impact of sleep disruption and hypoxemia on cognitive function

Another explanation for the difference in attention and reaction time between the two groups of OSA severity could be difference in severity of sleep fragmentation and nocturnal hypoxemia; symptoms of OSA, which have both been related to daytime sleepiness and CI [14]. Nocturnal COPD symptoms such as cough, dyspnea and sputum are also associated with poor quality of sleep [41]. Furthermore, patients with COPD have hypoxemia, during daytime and nocturnal, which may have an effect on cognition [12, 42]. It is still unclear, if it is disruption in sleep, daytime or nocturnal hypoxemia in patients with COPD that leads to CI in patients with OSA. However, it is generally agreed that sleep fragmentation is the primary cause of daytime sleepiness, as shown by lower results in sustained attention tests and driving simulations [14].

Treatment length and compliance

Based on findings from other studies [19, 20], we expected that gold standard treatment of OSA with CPAP would lead to an improvement in cognitive function and we did find an improvement in the specific domain short-term memory. However, it is possible that we did not observe additional improvements in cognitive functioning because of factors related to the duration of treatment and adherence. Nevertheless, prior to repeating the cognitive test, patients received treatment for minimum four months. According to other studies, CPAP therapy improves objective sleepiness (which is linked to CI) even after just 1–14 days [43]. Therefore, we think that if there had been further associations, we would have seen it after four months. Furthermore, the patients´ adherence was registered automatically in an electronically system, and we could see that a week before repeating the cognitive test, 15 out of 18 patients (83%) treated with CPAP had an adherence > 70% (using CPAP > 4 h). Therefore, in our view, adherence did not appear to be a contributing factor to the absence of further improvements in cognitive function in our study. However, despite limited improvement it is still highly relevant to treat overlap syndrome. In patients with overlap syndrome, CPAP therapy is associated with better outcomes, including functionality and mortality [6, 44].

Strength and limitations

The results of this study is based on data from a large test battery, which included comprehensive cognitive assessment and sleep data, which is a strength. Furthermore, it is a strength that the majority of patients (20 out of 28) who continued treatment for > 4 months were examined twice with various cognitive tests, and that the treated patients demonstrated a high level of adherence to the treatment. Likewise, the first author (KKH), who carried out all examinations, maintained, and evaluated the data, which increased the study’s internal validity. However, conducting the examinations and analyses may also be considered a limitation and could potentially have introduced information bias.

The sample size of our study may have been a limitation. We did not find significant differences in cognitive function between patients with and without OSA as expected. Small sample sizes increases the risk of type 1 and type 2 errors. Therefore, we may have erroneously rejected the null hypothesis of no difference even though there actually was a difference. Conversely, the difference we observed in attention and reaction time between patients with mild OSA compared to those with moderate/severe OSA may have been due to a type 1 error. The sample size calculation was based on a comparison between patients and controls in the overall study [22]. With a larger sample size, it is likely that a difference in cognitive function would have been observed. However, it is a difficult patient group to include due to their severe COPD symptoms, and our sample size of 80 patients is relatively large compared to similar studies.

Out of 177 eligible patients, 97 did not want to participate or did not meet the inclusion criteria after further examination. The impact of COPD symptoms on physical functioning, lack of motivation and too frequent hospital visits were cited as reasons for non-participation. There were no significant differences in mean age and mean FEV1 between patients who participated and those who declined participation. However, it remains unclear, whether patients who declined were cognitively superior or inferior compared to included patients. Based on this, there is a potential risk of selection bias, which can limit the generalisability of our findings. Another limitation is that the driving simulator was inoperable during the second test (Test 2), preventing nine patients from participating in the driving simulator assessment at that time. Consequently, comparisons of driving simulator results before and after treatment are not included in Table 4 and Table S1.Furthermore, as several factors may influence the development of CI, there remains the possibility of residual confounding.

Conclusion

In present study, we showed that patients with moderate to severe OSA as a comorbid condition in COPD seemed to be at greater risk of CI – specifically affecting the cognitive domains of attention and reaction time. Over time, these findings may be related to a decline in general health and further deterioration of quality of life. Every fifth patient with severe COPD were diagnosed with moderate to severe OSA in our study. The magnitude of this prevalence highlights the need for an increased focus on OSA as an important component in the treatment and care of patients with severe COPD.
