Alpha-lipoic acid targets KLF7 expression to inhibit cervical cancer progression
Authors: Yi Mao, Hongtao Li, Gang Xu, Jiazhen Tian, Yuechan Chen, Zhiwei Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt is unclear what part KLF7 plays in cervical cancer. In this study, immunohistochemical and bioinformatics analyses reveal that KLF7 expression is lower in normal cervical tissues than in cervical cancer tissues, and the high level of KLF7 transcripts in cervical cancer tissues is negatively correlated with patients’ overall and disease-free survival. In addition, KLF7 overexpression facilitates the proliferation, migration, and invasion of cervical cells, reduces PFKL expression, and increases the expressions of KLF4, Nanog, OCT4, CD44, SOX2, and ACADL. Additionally, knocking out the Exon 2 of KLF7 in HeLa cells results in a decrease in the total expression of KLF7 but an increase in the nuclear expression of KLF7, an increase in the capacity for proliferation, migration, invasion, and oncogenicity, and an increase in the density and ridge density of mitochondria. Consistent with these findings, RNA-seq analysis shows that knocking out the Exon 2 of KLF7 facilitates the expression of gene sets associated with cancer compared with that in wild-type HeLa cells. Moreover, the administration of alpha-lipoic acid (ALA) leads to a reduction in KLF7 expression in cells and tumor tissues, a suppression of the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HeLa and SiHa cells, and an increase in the carcinogenic potential of HeLa cells, while KLF7 overexpression shows the opposite effect on the expressions of ACADL and PFKL in HeLa and SiHa cells. In conclusion, KLF7 promotes the development of cervical cancer, and ALA can downregulate KLF7 expression and play a positive role in cervical cancer treatment. Keywords: cervical cancer, Krüppel-like factor 7, alpha-lipoic acid, reprogramming of energy metabolism

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer among females and is associated with high morbidity and mortality worldwide [ 1, 2]. There were approximately 604,127 newly reported cases of cervical cancer, and more than 342,000 deaths resulted from cervical cancer globally in 2020. Approximately 18% of the cases and 17% of the deaths were from China [3]. Surgery and radiation therapy are effective methods for the clinical treatment of cervical cancer; however, more treatment methods and drugs are still needed to improve the cure rate of patients to date [4].

The development of new treatment methods or drugs requires understanding the molecular mechanisms of cervical cancer development as much as possible. Infection with human papilloma virus (HPV) is a necessary condition for the occurrence of cervical cancer [ 5– 7]. However, HPV infection does not always lead to cervical cancer [8]. The alteration of gene transcription patterns in cervical epithelial cells is the real reason for the development of cervical cancer, in which transcription factors play key roles [9]. In addition, increased metabolic capacity and metabolic pattern reprogramming in cancer cells are important hallmarks of carcinogenesis [10].

Krüppel-like factor 7 (KLF7) is a ubiquitously expressed zinc-finger transcription factor that regulates the development of the nervous system, adipose tissue, and type 2 diabetes, as well as the maintenance of stem cells [11]. In addition, KLF7 acts as an oncogene, and its expression is upregulated in various cancer tissues, including liver cancer, lung cancer, gastric cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, glioma, and ovarian cancer [ 12, 13]. Recent studies have suggested that KLF7 potentially plays a crucial role in regulating the cellular inclination toward glucose and fatty acid metabolism [14].

Tumor cells undergo metabolic reprogramming as a means of adapting to hypoxia and nutritional shortages. The regulation of glucose and fatty acid metabolism by KLF7 might contribute to metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells. In addition, the biological function of KLF7 in cervical cancer development is not clear. In this study, the role of KLF7 in the development of cervical cancer was investigated, and the results revealed that KLF7 is an oncogene of cervical cancer and that alpha-lipoic acid (ALA) inhibits cervical cancer development at least by inhibiting KLF7 transcription.

Materials and Methods

Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis

IHC analysis was performed on 50 diagnosed cervical tissue samples provided by the First Affiliated Hospital of Shihezi University, including 16 cervical squamous cell carcinoma tissue samples, 12 cervical adenocarcinoma tissue samples, and 22 normal cervical tissue samples. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Shihezi University School of Medicine (approval number: KY2021-032-01), and all patients provided informed consents and received no therapy before surgery. IHC was performed according to previously established protocols [15]. Briefly, paraffin sections were subjected to antigen retrieval and then incubated with anti-KLF7 antibodies, followed by incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies. KLF7 expression in cervical cancer tissues was independently evaluated by two pathologists who were blinded to the clinical status of the patients. If the two pathologists disagreed on the immunostaining results, a third pathologist was consulted.

Cell line construction and validation

The two KLF7-knockout HeLa cell lines (KO cells), D4 and E11, were constructed by deleting the nucleotides from the 30th to 105th in Exon 2 of KLF7 via CRISPR/Cas9 technology by UBIGENE Company (Guangzhou, China) with two designed gRNAs (R1: 5′-TCCTCCGGGGCTCCGTCTGTAGG-3′ and R2: 5′-CGGAAGCTTTCCTCAATGCACGG-3′). By using the PCR method and sequencing, the genomes of wild-type (WT) and KO (D4 and E11) cells, along with the primers ( Table 1), were utilized to identify the knockout of the 30‒105th nucleotides in Exon 2 of KLF7 in HeLa cells. In addition, the knockout efficiency of KLF7 in the KO cells was analyzed via real-time PCR and western blot analysis.

Cell culture

HeLa and SiHa cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, #C04001; Viva Cell Biosciences, Shanghai, China). Before functional research, all the cells were treated with the TransSafe TM Mycoplasma Elimination Reagent (TransMyco Plus) Kit (FM421; TransGen, Beijing, China) to remove mycoplasma contamination, and only the cells in the logarithmic growth phase were used for the experiments [16]. The experimental groups were randomized before transfection or treatment with alpha-lipoic acid (#A835604; Macklin, Shanghai, China).

Western blot analysis

The cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (#R0010; Solarbio, Beijing, China). The cell lysates were separated on 5%–12% SDS‒polyacrylamide gels and transferred to polyvinylidenedifluoride membranes. Following an overnight treatment at 4°C with the appropriate antibodies, which included the following: KLF7 antibody (1:1000, rabbit pAb #abs140715; Absin, Shanghai, China), ACADL antibody (1:1000, rabbit mAB #17526-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), LaminB1 antibody (1:20000, rabbit mAB #12987-1-AP; Proteintech), PFKL antibody (1:1000, mouse mAB #sc-393713; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, USA), ACTB antibody (1:1000, mouse mAB #TA-09; ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China), and GAPDH (1:1500, mouse mAB # TA-08; ZSGB-BIO), the membranes were incubated with HRP labeled goat anti mouse IgG (1:10000, #ZB-2305; ZSGB-BIO) or goat anti rabbit IgG (1:10000, #ZB-2301; ZSGB-BIO) at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, a BeyoECL Plus kit (#P0018S; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used for detection. The original western blot images are shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

Real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated via TRIzol™ Reagent (#15596026; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA quality was assessed by denaturing formaldehyde agarose gel electrophoresis. Reverse transcription was performed via the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+gDNA wiper, #R312; Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Real-time PCR was used to detect gene expression via the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (#Q711; Vazyme) on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA) with the primers listed in Table 2. GAPDH was used as an internal control.

RNA-seq

The cells were collected via TRIzol™ Reagent (#15596026; Invitrogen) for RNA-seq analysis. The RNA-seq and subsequent data analysis were performed by Novogene Company (Beijing, China).

Plasmids

The pCDNA3.1- KLF7 plasmid was constructed by inserting the full-length CDS of human KLF7 (reference sequence: NM_003709.4) into the polyclonal site of the pCDNA3.1 plasmid. The KLF7 promoter luciferase reporter plasmids pGL4.10- KLF7(–2475, +16), pGL4.10- KLF7(–1479, +16), pGL4.10- KLF7(–945, +16) and pGL4.10- KLF7(–2475, –208) were the luciferase reporter plasmids of pGL4.10 (Promega, Madison, USA) containing the human KLF7 promoter (nucleotides –2475 to +16, –1479 to +16, –945 to +16, and –2475 to –208; relative to the start site of NM_003709.4). pGL4.10- KLF7 (–838, +118) was the luciferase reporter plasmid of pGL4.10 (Promega) containing the human KLF7 promoter (nucleotides –838 to +118; relative to the start site of NM_001270942.1). pGL4.10- KLF7 (–1177, –74) was the luciferase reporter plasmid of pGL4.10 (Promega) containing the human KLF7 promoter (nucleotides –1177 to –74; relative to the start site of NM_001270943.2).

Luciferase reporter assay

HeLa cells were seeded into 24-well plates and transfected with a given firefly luciferase reporter plasmid and pRL-TK ( Table 3). Following 48 h of transfection, ALA was introduced into the culture medium. The cells were harvested and lysed for the luciferase reporter experiment after 48 h of ALA treatment. The Dual-luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) was used to detect relative luciferase activity.

Xenograft tumor-formation assay

Thirty female BALB/C nude mice (SKBEX Biology Technology, Zhengzhou, China) aged 4 and 5 weeks were used in this study. A total of 1 × 10 6 cells were subcutaneously injected into the armpit or back of each mouse. For ALA treatment, mice bearing established xenografts were randomly divided into two groups, with six mice in each group. Throughout the experiment, the investigators were blinded to the allocation of the groups. They were intraperitoneally injected every other day for two weeks with ALA (60 mg/kg, body weight) or the corresponding solvent. The tumor volume was measured and calculated via the following formula: V = length × width 2 × π/6 [17]. At the end of the experiments, the mice were sacrificed after anesthesia, and the tumors were excised, weighed, and paraffin-embedded. Three-micron-thick sections were cut and subjected to IHC staining. This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Shihezi University School of Medicine (Approval number: A2023-069-01).

Flow cytometric analysis

The fixed cells were stained with a Cell Cycle Staining Kit (#S012; MultiSciences, Hangzhou, China), flow cytometric analysis was performed via a BD FACSAriaTM III flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, USA), and the data were analyzed via FlowJo software.

Transwell assay

The 200 μL of serum-free medium was added to the upper transwell chamber with 3 × 10 4 cells, and 600 μL of culture medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower transwell chamber. After incubation at 37°C for 40 h, the cells were fixed in methanol and subsequently stained with crystal violet. Finally, under a light microscope, the number of migrated cells in five randomly selected fields was determined. There were three replicates in each group.

Cell proliferation assay

The cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a concentration of 3,000 cells/well. The cell viability was determined using a CCK-8 kit (Biosharp, Hefei, China) at the designated time points, including 24, 48, 72, 96 and 120 h. In addition, the cells were cultivated for 10 d at a concentration of 1000 cells per well in 6-well plates to assess their capacity to generate clones. The clone was fixed with methanol, stained with crystal violet, and photographed for counting the number of colonies.

Transmission electron microscopy

The cells in the logarithmic growth phase were transferred into 1.5-mL centrifuge tubes and fixed overnight with 3% glutaraldehyde in a 4°C refrigerator. After sequential rinsing with PBS, osmotic acid fixation, dehydration with ethanol and acetone, embedding, polymerization, blocking, sectioning and staining, the intracellular ultrastructure was observed with a transmission electron microscope (H7650; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Fluorescence microscopy

The cells that had reached the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates. After 24 h of incubation or 72 h of ALA treatment, the cells were incubated with MitoTracker Green (#C1048; Beyotime) for 15 min, rinsed with PBS three times, and then incubated with Hoechst 33342 staining solution (#C1022; Beyotime) for 30 min. Before observation and imaging with an inverted fluorescence microscope (IX73; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), 1.5 mL of serum-free medium was added to each well.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed via the R software system (R version 4.3.1) according to the methods of Chen et al. [18]. Briefly, the normality of the data was examined via the Shapiro-Wilk test. The Wilcoxon rank sum test or two-tailed Student’s t test was used to analyze the differences between the two groups. The Dunnett t test was used to analyze differences between several groups with a fixed control group. Kaplan-Meier analysis and the log-rank test were used for survival estimates. The differences were considered significant at P​< 0.05 unless otherwise indicated.

Results

Correlation analysis between KLF7 expression and the prognosis of cervical cancer patients

The data retrieved from the databases of GEO, TCGA, and GTEx were used to study the KLF7 expression patterns in cervical cancer tissues. The results revealed that the transcript level of KLF7 in normal cervical tissues was lower than that in cervical cancer tissues ( P≤ 0.05; Figure 1A). Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) showed that high expression of KLF7 in cervical cancer tissues was negatively correlated with overall and disease-free survival ( P​< 0.05; Figure 1B). In addition, IHC analysis of cervical cancer samples revealed that KLF7 was expressed in both normal cervical tissues and cervical cancer tissues; however, the KLF7 expression level in cervical cancer tissues was significantly greater than that in normal cervical tissues ( Figure 1C,D).

KLF7 overexpression facilitates the proliferation, migration, and invasion of SiHa and HeLa cells

To study the effect of KLF7 on the biological behavior of cervical cancer cells, KLF7 was overexpressed in SiHa and HeLa cells via transfection with the pCDNA3.1- KLF7 plasmid ( Figure 2A). Real-time PCR analysis revealed that after transfection for 72 h, the KLF7 transcription level was significantly greater in the cells transfected with pCDNA3.1- KLF7 than in those transfected with the empty vector (EV) ( Figure 2B). In addition, western blot analysis revealed that the total expression and nuclear expression of KLF7 were significantly greater in the cells transfected with pcDNA3.1- KLF7 than in those transfected with EV ( P​< 0.05; Figure 2C,D).

The results of the CCK-8 assay revealed that after 120 h of cultivation, the proliferation ability of HeLa and SiHa cells transfected with pcDNA3.1- KLF7 was significantly greater than that of those transfected with EV ( P​< 0.01; Figure 2E). In addition, after transfection for 10 days, the colony formation efficiency of the cells transfected with pCDNA3.1- KLF7 was significantly greater than that of those transfected with EV ( P​< 0.01; Figure 2F). Compared with the EV group, the Transwell assay revealed that KLF7 overexpression significantly facilitated the migration and invasion of SiHa and HeLa cells ( P​< 0.001; Figure 2G,H).

KLF7 overexpression regulates the expressions of stemness and metabolism marker genes in SiHa and HeLa cells

After transfection for 72 h, the cells were collected to study gene expression. In HeLa cells, real-time PCR showed that, compared with that in the EV group, KLF7 overexpression significantly increased the transcription levels of KLF4, Nanog, OCT4, CD44, SOX2, and ACADL and suppressed PFKL expression ( P​< 0.05; Figure 3A). In SiHa cells, real-time PCR revealed that the transcripts of KLF4, CD133, Nanog, CD44, SOX2, and ACADL were significantly increased and that PFKL transcripts were significantly downregulated ( P​< 0.05; Figure 3B). In addition, western blot analysis showed that, in SiHa and HeLa cells, the expression levels of ACADL and PFKL in the cells of the KLF7-overexpression group were significantly greater and lower than those in the EV group, respectively ( P​< 0.05; Figure 3C,D).

Effect of KLF7 knockout on the proliferation, migration, and morphology of HeLa cells

To further reveal the function of KLF7 in HeLa cells, two KLF7-knockout cell lines, D4 and E11, were constructed by deleting the 30th–105th nucleotides of Exon 2 of KLF7 via CRISPR/Cas9 technology. PCR analysis revealed a 76 bp deletion of the Exon 2 of KLF7 in D4 and E11 cells ( Figure 4A,B). Real-time PCR and western blot analysis revealed that the total expression of KLF7 in the KO cells was significantly lower than that in the WT cells ( Figure 4C,D). However, western blot analysis revealed that the nuclear protein expression of KLF7 in the KO cells was greater than that in the WT cells ( Figure 4E).

CCK-8 and colony formation assays showed that the proliferation ability of the KO cells was significantly greater than that of the WT cells ( P​< 0.001; Figure 4F,G). Flow cytometry revealed that the proportion of G0/G1 phase in KO cells was significantly greater than that of WT cells ( P​< 0.05; Figure 4H). Effect of KLF7 knockout on the transcriptome expression patterns of HeLa cells

The impact of deleting the 30th–105th nucleotides of KLF7’s Exon 2 on the expression patterns in HeLa cells was investigated via RNA-seq. Compared with those in WT cells, 2241 genes were upregulated, and 2240 genes were downregulated in KO cells ( Figure 5A). Notably, the expression of KLF7 was downregulated in the KO cells, and the expressions of CD44 and KLF4 were upregulated in the KO cells compared with those in the WT cells. ( Figure 5B).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the KO and WT cells were enriched in biological processes such as multicellular organism development and anatomical structure development, cellular components such as the Golgi stack and extracellular region, and molecular functions such as growth factor activity, molecular function regulator and DNA-binding transcription factor activity ( Figure 5C).

Effect of KLF7 knockout on the tumorigenic ability of HeLa cells in vivo

The HeLa cell lines of WT, D4, and E11 were subcutaneously transplanted into the mice ( Figure 6A). The tumor size was measured every 5 days, and the results revealed that the tumor formation ability of D4 and E11 cells was significantly greater than that of WT cells ( Figure 6B,C). The volume and weight of tumors formed by D4 and E11 cells were significantly greater than those of tumors formed by WT cells after 15 days ( Figure 6D,E). The IHC analysis showed that the nuclear expression level of KLF7 in the tumor tissues formed by the KO cells was greater than that in the tumor tissues formed by the WT cells ( Figure 6F,G).

ALA inhibits KLF7 expression in HeLa and SiHa cells

The results of the luciferase reporter assay revealed a significant decrease in the activity of the KLF7 promoter in HeLa cells in response to the addition of ALA to the medium compared with that in the control group ( P​< 0.05; Figure 7A). Real-time PCR analysis revealed a significant decrease in KLF7 expression in HeLa and SiHa cells after the administration of ALA compared with the control group ( P​< 0.05; Figure 7B). Western blot analysis revealed that both the total expression and nuclear expression of KLF7 were significantly downregulated in the cervical cells treated with ALA ( P​< 0.05; Figure 7C,D). In addition, western blot analysis revealed that, in contrast to the effect of KLF7 overexpression, the administration of ALA notably decreased the expression level of ACADL and increased PFKL expression in HeLa and SiHa cells ( P​< 0.05; Figure 7E,F).

ALA inhibits the proliferation and tumorigenicity of HeLa and SiHa cells

The results of the CCK-8 assay revealed that the proliferation ability of HeLa cells was significantly reduced when they were exposed to ALA at concentrations greater than 200 μM for 72 h ( Figure 8A). Fluorescence microscopy revealed that, following 200 μM ALA treatment, the density of mitochondria in the HeLa or SiHa cells was lower than that in the control cells ( Figure 8C). Transmission electron microscopy revealed that megamitochondria appeared following ALA treatment ( Figure 8D).

Discussion

A crucial step in the fight against cancer is understanding the basic molecular processes that contribute to its onset. Previous reports have indicated that KLF7 is an oncogene that is expressed at low levels in normal tissues but is upregulated in cancer tissues, including lung cancer, gastric cancer, glioma, ovarian cancer, squamous cell carcinoma, pancreatic cancer, and liver cancer [ 12, 13]. It may be a potential target for broad-spectrum cancer treatment drugs. However, the roles of KLF7 in cervical cancer remain uncertain.

In the present study, an investigation of the expression patterns of KLF7 in cervical cancer tissues from publicly accessible databases and pathological cervical cancer tissues revealed that significant upregulation of KLF7 expression was detected in cervical cancer tissues and that increased KLF7 expression in cancer tissues was poorly correlated with patient survival, implying that KLF7 plays a role in cervical cancer development.

Cancer is a pathological condition characterized by aberrant cellular proliferation, invasion, and migration [19]. Notably, within cancer tissues, cancer stem cells exhibit the highest degree of malignancy, and the expression levels of CD44, CD133, Nanog, SOX2, KLF4, and OCT4 are recognized as biomarkers of cancer stem cells [20]. The present study revealed that overexpression of KLF7 resulted in increased proliferation, invasion, and migration in SiHa and HeLa cells, as well as increased expression levels of CD44, CD133, Nanog, SOX2, KLF4, and OCT4. These findings further suggest that KLF7 is a key regulator of the progression of cervical cancer.

KLF7-knockout HeLa cells were generated through targeted disruption of Exon 2 of the KLF7 gene via CRISPR/Cas9 technology. The overall expression of KLF7 was lower in KO cells than in WT cells, whereas the nuclear expression of KLF7 was considerably greater in KO cells than in WT cells. Moreover, the KO cells exhibited considerably greater proliferation, invasion, and migratory capabilities than the WT cells. Additionally, the KO cells had a better capacity to produce tumors in vivo than the WT cells. These data suggested that the nuclear expression, rather than the overall expression, of KLF7 might promote cervical cancer development.

Vigorous energy metabolism ability guarantees the malignancy of cancer. Although glucose is the primary fuel for most tumor cells, recent studies have shown the presence of increased fatty acid metabolism in malignant cells [21]. KLF7 has been shown to influence alterations in glucose and fatty acid metabolism patterns in cardiomyocytes [14]. RNA-seq revealed that the gene sets related to cancer development were upregulated in the KO cells. In addition, the densities of the mitochondrial and mitochondrial cristae were greater in the KO cells than in the WT cells. Additionally, KLF7 overexpression in SiHa and HeLa cells led to a decrease in PFKL expression and an increase in ACADL expression. These results suggest that KLF7 may play a role in reprogramming energy metabolism in cervical cancer cells.

ALA, a coenzyme of pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDC), inhibits aerobic glycolysis and fatty acid metabolism in cancer cells and has been used for cancer treatment [22]. In this study, the results revealed that in HeLa and SiHa cells, ALA inhibited the expressions of KLF7 and ACADL while facilitating the expression of PFKL. Furthermore, ALA treatment reduced the proliferative capacity of SiHa and HeLa cells. Additionally, after ALA treatment, the size and mass of the tumors created by HeLa cells under the skin decreased, accompanied by a decrease in KLF7 expression in the tumor tissue. These results were opposite to the effect of KLF7 overexpression on HeLa and SiHa cells. Given the dynamic function of KLF7 in the conversion of cellular glucose and fatty acid metabolism [23], downregulation of KLF7 expression might be another mechanism by which ALA inhibits cancer development. These findings might provide a theoretical basis for the application of ALA in the treatment of cervical cancer.

As a transcription factor, KLF7 mainly plays a role in the nucleus and regulates the transcription of target genes by binding to DNA sequences, such as the CACCC motif, in chromosomes [13]. Therefore, the mechanism by which the KLF7 nucleoprotein affects tumor formation may involve mainly the formation of tumor-specific gene transcription patterns. Notably, the KLF7-knockout cells generated via CRISPR/CAS9 technology in the present investigation did not successfully reduce KLF7 nucleoprotein expression. Hence, it is imperative to employ alternative methodologies to investigate the impact of suppressing KLF7 expression in cervical cancer cells. In addition, the therapeutic validation of ALA for the treatment of cervical cancer was not established in clinical practice in this study. The impact of KLF7 on cellular metabolism has not been investigated via metabolic methods, and the mechanism by which KLF7 regulates the expressions of ACADL and PFKL has not been elucidated. In subsequent investigations, our research team will further focus on these factors to improve the finding of this study.

In conclusion, the present study revealed that KLF7 plays a cancer-promoting role in the progression of cervical cancer. ALA could downregulate KLF7 expression and play a positive role in the treatment of cervical cancer ( Figure 8J). The present study established a theoretical framework for the utilization of ALA as a therapeutic intervention for cervical cancer through targeting KLF7 expression.
