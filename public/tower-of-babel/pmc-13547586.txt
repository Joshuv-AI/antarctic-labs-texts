Oncogenic Mutations and Tumor Microenvironment Alterations in Diffuse Large B‐Cell Lymphoma With Bulky Disease
Authors: Yu‐Qing Wang, Yue Wang, Zhi‐Shan Du, Xin‐Yun Huang, Yan Dong, Shuang Tian, Di Fu, Shu Cheng, Li Wang, Peng‐Peng Xu, Sheng‐Jin Fan, Shu‐Ye Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundBulky disease represents a clinically aggressive subset of diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) associated with adverse clinical outcomes. The aim of this study was to investigate the influence of oncogenic mutations and tumor microenvironment alterations on bulky disease in DLBCL.MethodsWe analyzed a cohort of 939 patients with newly diagnosed DLBCL. Using DNA (n = 934) and RNA (n = 524) sequencing, we compared oncogenic mutations and tumor microenvironment (TME) alterations based on tumor diameter, with cutoff values at 5.0 cm and 10.0 cm. Further stratification by mutations in key genes (CD58, STAT6, EBF1) correlated with tumor diameter revealed distinct transcriptomic and immunologic profiles. Subsequent single‐cell RNA sequencing, guided by these mutational signatures, resolved the cellular heterogeneity within the TME.ResultsIntegrative analysis revealed that tumor diameter correlated with increased incidence of mutations in CD58, STAT6, and EBF1; adverse genetic subtypes such as EZB‐like MYC+ and TP53 Mut; activation of oncogenic pathways (JAK/STAT, BCR, PI3K, and MYC); and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Notably, immune checkpoint molecules varied across the bulky stages, with CTLA‐4, TIGIT, ICOS, and CD28 expression inversely correlated with tumor diameter, while CD70 and 4‐1BBL expression positively correlated. Single‐cell RNA sequencing further revealed mutation‐specific tumor microenvironment insights. CD58‐mutated tumor exhibited a profoundly immune‐deserted microenvironment dominated by malignant B cells with minimal immune infiltration, whereas STAT6‐mutated tumor was associated with increased fibroblasts and CD4 + T cells, particularly regulatory T cells (Treg) and Th1‐like cells; EBF1‐mutated tumor was characterized by increased proportions of malignant B cells.ConclusionsCollectively, our findings highlight the biological complexity of bulky disease, identifying candidate molecular targets and providing a biological framework for future therapeutic hypothesis generation in this clinically aggressive subset of DLBCL.Keywords: bulky disease, diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma, genetic mutation, immune checkpoint, immunotherapy, lymphoma microenvironment

Background

Diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL), the most common subtype of non‐Hodgkin lymphoma, comprises a heterogeneous group of lymphoid neoplasms with distinct morphological, genetic, and clinical features [1, 2, 3]. Despite significant advances in treatment strategies, including the application of rituximab in combination with cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (R‐CHOP), a substantial proportion of patients experience relapse or refractory disease, highlighting the need for better risk stratification and personalized therapeutic approaches [4]. Efforts have been made to reveal the prognostic implications of clinical features [5, 6], genetic [7, 8], and tumor microenvironment alterations in DLBCL [9, 10, 11]. Among the diverse clinical features, bulky disease, defined as a large tumor mass [12, 13], is a clinically relevant feature and is associated with inferior prognosis in newly diagnosed [14], relapsed or refractory [15], and even patients after chimeric antigen receptor T‐cell (CAR‐T) therapy in DLBCL [16]. Notably, no universal consensus exists regarding the optimal cutoff for defining bulky disease, with thresholds ranging from 5.0 cm to 10.0 cm across clinical trials [14, 17, 18]. For instance, the MabThera International group study observed a linear correlation between maximum tumor diameter and prognosis, and ultimately used 10 cm as the cutoff value [14]. S1001 and Southwest Oncology Group 014 trials also defined bulky disease as more than 10.0 cm [17, 18], while studies conducted at MD Anderson Cancer Center [19] and the RICOVER‐60 trial [20] adopted 5.0 cm and 7.5 cm, respectively. These variations indicate the lack of a standardized definition of bulky disease. In addition, previous studies have largely focused on its clinical and prognostic relevance; the underlying biological mechanisms of bulky disease remain poorly understood in DLBCL.

DLBCL exhibits profound genetic heterogeneity, with distinct oncogenic mutations driving divergent signaling pathways and clinical behaviors. Notable achievements have been made in revealing this heterogeneity, with genetic subtypes defined based on patterns of co‐occurring genetic alterations [8, 21, 22]. The LymphGen and consensus cluster (C0–C5) algorithm categorizes comparable molecular features into distinct genetic subtypes [8, 21], including the MCD/C5, BN2/C1, and EZB/C3 clusters, while also identifying less overlapping subtypes such as ST2/C4, N1, and TP53‐associated/C2 clusters. Shen et al. further developed the LymphPlex algorithm [7], incorporating mutational data from 35 genes and rearrangements of BCL2, BCL6, and MYC, to classify DLBCL into 7 genetic subtypes, namely MCD‐like, BN2‐like, N1‐like, EZB‐like MYC+, EZB‐like MYC−, ST2‐like, and TP53Mut, offering practical utility in informing subtype‐specific therapeutic strategies [7, 8, 21]. Critically, these genetic alterations do not act in isolation; specific oncogenic mutations actively remodel the tumor microenvironment (TME), shaping immune cell infiltration patterns, cytokine milieu, and immune evasion strategies in subtype‐specific manners [3]. Beyond genetic profiling, large‐scale transcriptomic [10] and digital pathology data [9] have been utilized to identify four distinct lymphoma microenvironment (LME) subtypes in DLBCL, including germinal center‐like (GC‐like), mesenchymal (MS), inflammatory (IN), and depleted (DP) subtypes. The significant disparity in treatment response and prognosis among these subtypes directly underlies the substantial clinical heterogeneity characteristic of DLBCL. Collectively, tumor‐intrinsic genetic features and tumor‐extrinsic immune context are deeply intertwined determinants of disease outcome, and bulky disease, as a macroscopic manifestation of tumor burden, likely reflects the convergence of accumulated oncogenic alterations and progressive immune escape. Accordingly, simultaneous analysis of oncogenic mutations, signaling pathway activation, and microenvironment alterations across the spectrum of tumor size is essential to decode the biological underpinnings of bulky disease in DLBCL and to identify rational targets for future intervention.

In this study, we analyzed a cohort of 939 DLBCL patients to systematically investigate the clinical and biological relevance according to tumor diameter‐based staging, including oncogenic mutations and tumor microenvironment alterations. Integrative analyses revealed molecular and immunosuppressive features of bulky disease, providing insights for improved risk stratification and personalized targeted therapeutic strategies in DLBCL.

Methods

Patient Selection

Patients were recruited from Ruijin Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, and the study enrolled patients between May 2013 and December 2021. A total of 939 patients with newly diagnosed DLBCL were included. Patients with primary central nervous system lymphoma, PMBCL, primary cutaneous lymphoma, and intravascular lymphoma were excluded. Histological diagnoses were reviewed and confirmed by experienced pathologists according to the 2016 World Health Organization classification [23]. Survival analyses were performed in a subset of 673 patients receiving R‐CHOP‐based immunochemotherapy. DNA and RNA sequencing were performed on tumor samples from 934 and 524 patients, respectively, to evaluate genetic aberrations, perform gene set enrichment analysis, and assess immune cell activity scores. Primary tumor size was determined by measuring the maximal diameter of the largest tumor lesion. Patients who underwent surgical resection of the primary lesion were excluded. Tumor measurements were obtained via radiological imaging, including computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography‐computed tomography (PET‐CT), or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [24]. Approval for this study was obtained from the Ruijin Hospital Ethics Committee, Shanghai JiaoTong University School of Medicine, and informed consent was obtained from all patients in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

DNA Sequencing

Oncogenic mutations of 55 genes closely related to DLBCL were analyzed in 934 patients, including 125 cases by whole‐exome sequencing (WES), 167 cases by whole‐genome sequencing (WGS), and 642 cases by targeted sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted from frozen tumor tissue or from formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue. Genomic DNA extracted from FFPE samples using a GeneRead DNA FFPE Tissue Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions was fragmented to approximately 200 bp using a focused ultrasonicator prior to library construction. DNA quantity and integrity were subsequently assessed using a NanoDrop 8000 UV–Vis spectrometer, Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer, and 2200 TapeStation Instrument.

Sequence Alignments and Variant Calling

Raw sequencing data quality was evaluated for all sequencing platforms using FastQC software (version 1.11.4). For targeted sequencing, paired‐end reads were mapped to the Human Reference Genome GRCh37 using the Burrows‐Wheeler Aligner (BWA, version 0.5.9‐tpx). Sequencing depth varied by approach: Capture‐based targeted sequencing achieved a median depth of ~300×, while amplicon‐based targeted sequencing achieved a substantially greater median depth of ~2000×. Adapter trimming and quality filtering of raw reads were performed with Trimmomatic (version 3.6), with removal of 3′‐end adapter sequences, low‐quality bases (Phred quality score < 25), and short reads below 35 bp in length. Processed reads were subsequently handled using SAMtools [25] (v0.1.18) and Picard (v1.93) for BAM file processing and duplicate marking, followed by local realignment, base quality score recalibration, and variant calling with the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK, v4.1.4.0). Coding region variants were functionally annotated using ANNOVAR [26].

Variant filtering was performed in a stepwise manner. An initial quality filter required a minimum read depth of 10× and a variant allele frequency (VAF) of at least 5%. Variants identified in a panel of 42 matched peripheral blood samples from our prior work [27] were subsequently excluded as putative germline or technical artifacts. From the remaining calls, non‐synonymous single nucleotide variants (SNVs), insertions/deletions (indels), and splice‐site alterations were retained if they satisfied at least one of the following criteria: (1) previously reported as confirmed somatic mutations in our group's published studies [27, 28]; (2) recognized recurrent hotspot mutations in DLBCL, such as MYD88 p.L265P; (3) classified as Tier I or Tier II variants per the evidence‐based somatic variant classification guidelines; or (4) absent from the 1000 Genomes Project (1KG) database, present at a population frequency below 1% in 1KG, or present at a frequency below 5% in 1KG but recurrently observed (n > 5) in hematological malignancies within the COSMIC database (v77). Conversely, variants were excluded if they were classified as Tier IV per the aforementioned guidelines, or if they were confirmed as germline variants through Sanger sequencing of paired peripheral blood DNA. Variants at critical VAF thresholds were orthogonally validated by Sanger sequencing or digital PCR prior to inclusion in downstream analyses.

LymphPlex Algorithm for Genetic Subtyping

The LymphPlex algorithm was applied to paraffin samples with information on mutation status of 35 genes and rearrangements of three genes (BCL2, BCL6, and MYC) by FISH according to the classification tool [7].

RNA Sequencing

RNA sequencing was performed on frozen tumor tissues from 524 patients. RNA quantity was assessed on a NanoDrop instrument (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, Delaware), and the integrity of RNA was qualified on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). The paired‐end reads were pseudo‐aligned to the GRCh38 transcriptome (Ensembl version 106) and quantified using Kallisto (version 0.46.0) [29]. R package “sva” was used to remove the batch effect.

LME Categories

LME subtypes were identified using the LME categories based on the tool as previously described [10]. Gene expression profiles in 524 patients were analyzed to define the diversity in the composition and functionality of the microenvironment.

Bioinformatics Analyses

Pathway activity was assessed via ssGSEA with the R package “GSVA”, based on pre‐defined gene sets from Louis M. Staudt et al. (https://lymphochip.nih.gov/signaturedb/) and Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB) [30]. GSEA results were presented using GSEA v4.0.1 software and MSigDB [30] using normalized count data. Pathways were considered statistically significant when the p‐value was < 0.05, and the false discovery rate was < 0.25. The activity scores of tumor immune cell recruitment were revealed by the xCell [31] method using TPM data.

Single‐Cell RNA Sequencing

Single‐cell RNA sequencing was performed as previously described [32]. Dissociated tissues were subjected to FACS to enrich for viable hCD45+ immune cells. Libraries were constructed using the 10 × Chromium Single Cell platform and sequenced on either the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) or the BGI MGISEQ‐2000 platform (Shenzhen, China) following manufacturer guidelines. Raw sequencing data were processed through the Cell Ranger software pipeline (version 6.0.1) [33] for barcode demultiplexing, alignment to the GRCh38 reference genome, and feature‐barcode matrix generation. Subsequent analysis was conducted in the Seurat [34] R package (version 5.1.0). The DoubletFinder [35] R package (version 2.0.3) was employed to detect and remove potential doublets. Following quality control, the filtered unique molecular identifier (UMI) count matrices were normalized, scaled, and subjected to variable feature selection and linear dimensionality reduction using standard Seurat workflows [34].

Single‐Cell Subsets Identification and Annotation

We performed two rounds of clustering to identify the cell type of each cluster. Initial clustering identified five cell types, including B cells, T/NK cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and myeloid cells using canonical cell type markers. The second round of clustering was performed within each major cell type to identify subclusters.

InferCNV Analysis

B cells were isolated for CNV inference against a reference of T/NK and myeloid cells using the R package inferCNV (v1.20.0). The analysis was performed based on the raw counts matrix, and the resultant CNV score per cell was quantified as the quadratic sum across all genomic regions evaluated by the tool.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R v4.2.1 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). Associations between categorical variables were assessed with the Chi‐square or Fisher's exact test, while group comparisons of continuous variables were performed with the Kruskal–Wallis test. Correlations between continuous variables were quantified using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. PFS was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of disease progression or relapse, or the date of death or last follow‐up. OS was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of death or last follow‐up. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier curve and the log‐rank test. Univariate hazard ratios were derived from Cox proportional hazards models.

Results

Clinical and Prognostic Significance of DLBCL With Bulky Disease

Defining Bulky Disease Stages Based on Treatment Response and Survival Profiles

A total of 939 patients were included in the analysis, comprising 528 (56.2%) cases with a maximum tumor diameter of less than 5.0 cm, 194 (20.7%) cases from 5.0 cm to less than 7.5 cm, 100 (10.6%) cases from 7.5 cm to less than 10.0 cm, and 117 (12.5%) patients with diameters of 10.0 cm or greater. In the context of first‐line immunochemotherapy (R‐CHOP‐based, n = 673), significant differences in progression‐free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were observed between patients with tumor diameters less than 5.0 cm and those measuring from 5.0 cm to less than 7.5 cm (PFS, p < 0.001; OS, p < 0.001), as well as between those with diameters from 7.5 cm to less than 10.0 cm and those with diameters of 10.0 cm or greater (PFS, p = 0.005; OS, p = 0.026). However, no significant survival differences were observed between patients with tumor diameters of 5.0 cm to less than 7.5 cm and 7.5 cm to less than 10.0 cm (Figure S1A,B). Similarly, complete response rate (CRR) and objective response rate (ORR) did not differ significantly between these two groups (Figure S1C). Based on the response and survival profiles, cutoff values for tumor diameter were defined as 5.0 cm and 10.0 cm for subsequent analyses. Accordingly, tumors with a maximum diameter less than 5.0 cm were classified as non‐bulky, those greater than or equal to 5.0 cm but less than 10.0 cm as intermediate‐bulky, and those greater than or equal to 10.0 cm as high‐bulky. Notably, survival outcomes varied significantly among the three subgroups, with both PFS and OS progressively reduced from non‐bulky to high‐bulky (PFS, p < 0.001, Figure 1A; OS, p < 0.001, Figure 1B). Similarly, CRR and ORR differed significantly among the three subgroups (CRR, p < 0.001; ORR, p < 0.001, Figure 1C).

Clinical Characteristics and Prognostic Implications

The clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. Patients classified as high‐bulky were significantly associated with advanced Ann Arbor stage (p < 0.001), multiple extranodal involvement (ENI, p < 0.001), elevated serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, p < 0.001), poor performance status (p < 0.001), intermediate/high‐ or high‐risk International Prognostic Index (IPI) scores (p < 0.001), and double/triple‐hit (DH/TH) lymphoma (p = 0.001) compared to those in non‐bulky and intermediate‐bulky. In contrast, no significant differences were observed in double‐expressor (DE) lymphoma (p = 0.060) or HANS classification (p = 0.177). Furthermore, both univariate (Figure 1D,E) and multivariate analyses (Table 2) confirmed intermediate‐bulky and high‐bulky as independent factors of inferior prognosis (intermediate‐bulky, PFS, p < 0.001, OS, p < 0.001; high‐bulky, PFS, p < 0.001, OS, p < 0.001).

Oncogenic Mutations and Genetic Subtypes in DLBCL With Bulky Disease

Oncogenic Mutations

Oncogenic mutations of 55 genes closely related to DLBCL were analyzed in 934 patients. As shown in Figure 2A, patients with high‐bulky exhibited significantly increased mutation frequencies in CD58, EBF1, and STAT6 compared to those in non‐bulky and intermediate‐bulky. Additionally, mutations in BTG2, CD79B, CREBBP, DTX1, GNA13, TBL1XR1, TP53, and ZNF608 were notably more prevalent among patients with tumors measuring ≥ 5.0 cm in diameter than in those with tumors < 5.0 cm (Figure S2A). These findings suggest a correlation between increasing tumor diameter and the accumulation of oncogenic alterations in DLBCL.

Asterisks mark all bivariate correlations that are significant. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001. (D) Genetic subtypes related to bulky groups. Further analysis revealed distinct concurrent mutation patterns involving CD58, EBF1, and STAT6 genes, whose mutation frequencies increased in parallel with tumor diameter. CD58 mutations frequently co‐occurred with STAT6 alterations, along with concurrent mutations in BTG1, EP300, HIST1H1E, MPEG1, PIM1, SOCS1, TP53, and ZFP36L1. Interestingly, mutations in CD58 and TP53 demonstrated mutual exclusivity (Figure 2B), consistent with previous reports in DLBCL [36]. STAT6 mutations exhibited a significant association with other mutations, such as B2M, BTG2, CIITA, CREBBP, EZH2, FAS, GNA13, IRF8, KMT2D, MYC, SOCS1, and TNFAIP3 (Figure 2B). The concurrent mutations of CD58 and STAT6 could represent a dual mechanism of immune evasion in DLBCL. Furthermore, EBF1 mutations displayed the most extensive co‐mutation network, involving ARID1A, ATM, B2M, BCL6, BTG1, CARD11, CD70, CREBBP, DDX3X, DTX1, DUSP2, IRF4, KMT2C, MTOR, NOTCH1, SGK1, SOCS1, STAT3, TNFRSF14, TSC2, ZFP36L1, and ZNF608 (Figure 2C).

Genetic Subtypes

Among the 934 patients with DNA sequencing data, 539 were classified into genetic subtypes by the LymphPlex algorithm, with the remaining 395 patients as the NOS subtype. Specifically, BN2‐like subtype was predominantly observed in patients with non‐bulky compared to those with intermediate‐bulky or high‐bulky (non‐bulky vs. intermediate‐bulky vs. high‐bulky: 23.7% vs. 20.9% vs. 8.7%), Conversely, TP53 Mut subtype was most prevalent in patients with intermediate‐bulky and high‐bulky (non‐bulky vs. intermediate‐bulky vs. high‐bulky: 23.7% vs. 36.1% vs. 33.3%). EZB‐like MYC+ subtype was markedly enriched in high‐bulky patients (non‐bulky vs. intermediate‐bulky vs. high‐bulky: 1.1% vs. 2.1% vs. 8.7%) (Figure 2D). Further analysis demonstrated that both TP53 Mut and EZB‐like MYC+ subtypes were significantly associated with tumor diameter ≥ 5.0 cm (Figure S2B), reinforcing their relevance to aggressive disease phenotypes. To evaluate the prognostic impact of bulky disease in the context of genetic heterogeneity, both univariate and multivariate models were constructed between bulky disease and genetic subtypes, with non‐bulky and NOS subtypes set as references. Both intermediate‐ and high‐bulky were associated with inferior prognosis, independent of the genetic subtypes (Figure S2C,D; Figure 2E,F). Together, our findings reveal that bulky tumors correlate with distinct genetic subtypes, particularly highlighting the enrichment of CD58, STAT6, EBF1, TP53 mutations, and MYC‐driven features in more advanced stages. Nevertheless, the prognostic impact of bulky disease persists independent of genetic subtype, underscoring the importance of integrating tumor size into risk stratification models for DLBCL.

Oncogenic Signaling Pathways in DLBCL With Bulky Disease

Pathway Alterations

To elucidate the biological characteristics of bulky disease in DLBCL, we performed transcriptomic analyses using RNA sequencing data from 524 patients, including 267 patients in non‐bulky, 184 in intermediate‐bulky, and 73 in high‐bulky. As shown in Figure 3A, a clear trend of progressive activation of oncogenic and immune‐related pathways was observed with increasing tumor diameter, including upregulation of JAK and JAK2 signaling, B‐cell receptor (BCR) signaling, PI3K signaling, and MYC oncoprotein‐related signatures. Additionally, gene signatures related to cell proliferation and the glycolytic pathway were significantly enriched with increasing tumor diameter, suggesting a metabolic reprogramming characteristic of bulky disease. Of note, genes induced by OCT2 and IRF4 exhibited a positive correlation with bulky disease, highlighting their potential role in driving DLBCL progression.

Pathway Enrichment of Genetic Mutations

To further delineate the functional impact of specific genetic mutations enriched in bulky disease, we conducted gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) across major mutations. Each mutation exhibited a distinct pattern of pathway activation, reflecting their potential mechanistic contributions to tumor growth and immune escape. Cytokine‐cytokine receptor interaction, chemokine signaling pathway, and negative regulation of T cells and immune response were upregulated in the CD58‐mutated group (Figure 3B), likely reflecting an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment facilitating immune evasion. In contrast, the EBF1 mutation exhibited enrichment in oxidative phosphorylation, DNA replication, DNA damage response pathway, and cell cycle checkpoints, indicating a hyperproliferative state with increased genomic instability (Figure 3C). The STAT6 mutation was characterized by both immune modulation and stromal remodeling. Enriched pathways included T helper 2 cell differentiation, macrophage differentiation, interleukin 4 production, and negative regulation of T cell activation. Concurrently, oncogenic pathways like JAK–STAT, NOTCH, MAPK, and TGF‐β were also upregulated (Figure 3D).

Tumor Microenvironment Alterations in DLBCL With Bulky Disease

The Landscape of Immuno‐Cell Infiltration

To elucidate the role of immune cells in bulky disease, we evaluated the activity scores of immune cell recruitment using the xCell (27) method based on RNA sequencing data. A significant inverse correlation was observed between tumor diameter and the scores of multiple immune cell types, including CD4+ T cells, central memory (Tcm) and effector memory (Tem) CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, naïve CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, megakaryocytes, natural killer T (NKT) cells, neutrophils, and both classical and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (cDCs and pDCs). These findings indicate a progressive depletion of both adaptive and innate immune cell components with increasing tumor diameter. In contrast, pro‐B cells and mast cells exhibited a positive correlation with bulky disease (Figure 4A). Additionally, we assessed the relationship between bulky disease and LME subtypes (8): 74 cases (14.1%) GC, 170 cases (32.4%) MS, 134 cases (25.6%) IN, and 146 cases (27.9%) DP subtype. Interestingly, the GC subtype was negatively associated with bulky disease (p = 0.038), whereas the DP subtype, characterized by an immune‐depleted phenotype, showed a positive correlation (p = 0.006) (Figure 4B; Figure S3A). Moreover, multivariate regression analysis confirmed that both intermediate‐bulky and high‐bulky remained independent adverse prognostic factors, irrespective of LME subtypes (Figure 4C,D; Figure S3B,C). We also explored the expression of immune checkpoint molecules across bulky stages. The expression of CTLA‐4, TIGIT, ICOS, and CD28 declined progressively with increasing tumor diameter, whereas CD70 and 4‐1BBL expression showed a positive correlation (Figure 4E). These findings suggest a shift toward an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in bulky disease, which may have implications for immunotherapeutic strategies in DLBCL.

Relation of Oncogenic Mutations With Immuno‐Cell Infiltration

To further dissect the interplay between genetic mutations and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in bulky disease, single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) was performed on tumor samples from 4 patients, each harboring a specific mutation: One with a CD58 mutation, one with a STAT6 mutation, one with an EBF1 mutation, and 1 without any of these genes (non‐mutant, NM).

Based on gene expression profiles, cells were categorized into five primary clusters: B cells, T/NK cells, myeloid cells, endothelial cells (ECs), and fibroblasts (Figure 5A,B). Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) demonstrated the distribution of these five clusters across the different mutation backgrounds (Figure 5C). Myeloid cell heterogeneity was deconvoluted into six transcriptomically defined subclusters: cDC1, cDC2, pDCs, mature DCs, monocytes, and macrophages (Figure 5D), each exhibiting established canonical marker signatures (Figure 5E). Discussion

In this study, we added biological implications of bulky disease in DLBCL through integrated analysis of clinical data, genetic mutations, transcriptomic profiles, and tumor microenvironment alterations. With significant enrichment of adverse clinical features such as advanced Ann Arbor stage, elevated LDH, high‐risk IPI scores, and DH/TH, bulky disease is an independent poor prognostic factor in DLBCL.

Specific oncogenic mutations have been increasingly recognized as critical drivers of DLBCL progression [4, 27]. In addition to TP53 [36], MYC‐altered [37] DLBCL, genomic alterations in CD58, STAT6 and EBF1 were positively correlated with increasing tumor diameter.

CD58 is a ligand of the CD2 receptor expressed on T cells and NK cells [38]. Loss‐of‐function mutations in CD58 may impair immune surveillance by attenuating CD2‐mediated costimulation [39]. Our data revealed that CD58‐mutated tumors exhibited upregulation of pathways associated with negative regulation of T cell activity and immune suppression, along with a profoundly immune‐deserted microenvironment dominated by malignant B cells and minimal immune infiltration. The DP subtype, which notably lacked environmental components [10], was significantly enriched in increasing tumor diameter, reinforcing the concept of an immune‐deserted phenotype of bulky disease. Interestingly, a hallmark of bulky disease was the presence of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, characterized by decreased infiltration of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and cDC cells. These findings support the notion that CD58 mutations contribute to an immunosuppressive phenotype in bulky disease, potentially facilitating immune escape and tumor progression.

Alternatively, STAT6 mutations were associated with enrichment of oncogenic signaling pathways, including JAK/STAT and PI3K, both of which are associated with bulky disease in our study. STAT6 is known to be phosphorylated by JAK1/JAK3 or JAK2 upon cytokine stimulation, regulating immune and metabolic gene expression [40]. Consistent with previous reports linking STAT6 to fibroblast proliferation [41] and the GC‐like subtype characterized by CD4+ T‐cell abundance [10], our sc‐RNA‐seq analysis revealed increased fibroblasts and CD4 + T cells in STAT6‐mutated DLBCL, supporting its role in immune tumor microenvironment reprogramming. Given the association between STAT6 mutations and multiple pathways, STAT6 may serve as a modulator of oncogenic signaling, contributing to tumor progression and immune resistance in bulky disease of DLBCL. Notably, the frequent co‐occurrence of CD58 and STAT6 mutations observed in our cohort raises the possibility of a complementary dual mechanism of immune evasion. These two alterations may therefore be co‐selected during lymphomagenesis to cooperatively establish an immune‐deserted microenvironment characteristic of bulky disease, though direct functional validation of their cooperative interaction warrants future investigation.

EBF1 is a key transcription factor essential for B‐cell specification and early development. It regulates a broad array of genes critical for B‐cell commitment, including PAX5, CD79A, and IL7R [42, 43], and directly binds to regulatory regions of the Myc locus, thereby modulating pro‐B‐cell proliferation [44, 45]. Our analysis demonstrated increased pro‐B‐cells with bulky disease, implying that dysregulated B‐cell development, potentially driven by EBF1 alterations, may contribute to the formation and progression of bulky disease.

Ruxolitinib, a selective JAK1/2 inhibitor, demonstrates anti‐tumor effects in Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and primary mediastinal B‐cell lymphoma (PMBCL) [46]. Clinical studies also suggest that STAT6‐driven tumors may be responsive to JAK–STAT inhibition [47], making ruxolitinib a treatment option, particularly in light of the JAK/JAK2 pathway enrichment. Similarly, Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK) and PI3K inhibitors exhibit efficacy in disrupting BCR [48, 49] and PI3K [50] signaling, respectively, and can also be applied. Notably, bulky disease has been associated with reduced efficacy of immunotherapy [51]. A recent study showed that CD58 mutations predicted poor response to CAR T‐cell therapy [52]. CD58 may enhance PD‐L1‐driven immune suppression via CD2‐mediated costimulation [39]. Direct activation of CD58‐CD2 costimulatory signaling in combination with anti‐PD‐L1 antibodies or IDO inhibitor sensitized CD58‐deficient DLBCL to CAR T‐cell therapy [8]. Together, these results support pathway‐guided treatment strategies for patients with bulky disease, particularly those harboring corresponding genetic alterations.

Expression of CTLA‐4 and TIGIT was inversely correlated with tumor diameter, implying that their respective inhibitors may hold promise in combination therapies. Conversely, increased expression of 4‐1BBL and CD70 in intermediate‐ and high‐bulky disease suggests vulnerability to immune agonists or targeted CAR strategies. Supporting this, a prior study demonstrated that CD70‐specific CAR‐NK cells incorporating 4‐1BB costimulatory domains outperformed both non‐transduced NK cells and conventional CD19‐CAR‐NK cells in eliminating CD19‐negative B‐cell lymphoma cells [53], highlighting a promising therapeutic avenue for managing advanced bulky disease.

While the molecular features of bulky disease identified in our study provide a biological rationale for exploring targeted therapeutic strategies, the translation of these findings into clinical benefit may be substantially complicated by factors inherent to macroscopic lesions. Bulky diseases present unique pharmacological and biological challenges that are not captured by pathway enrichment analyses alone. First, bulky diseases are characterized by elevated interstitial fluid pressure, aberrant vasculature, and dense stromal content, all of which impair drug penetration and reduce effective intratumoral concentrations of targeted agents, potentially limiting the efficacy of targeted drugs despite their on‐target activity in vitro [54, 55]. Second, the hypoxic core that develops within bulky diseases can activate hypoxia‐inducible factor (HIF)‐dependent survival pathways that bypass targeted inhibition [56] and is well recognized to impair T cell trafficking, effector function, and persistence, thereby undermining the efficacy of both immune checkpoint blockade and CAR‐T cell therapy [57, 58]. Third, our transcriptomic data demonstrating enrichment of glycolytic signatures in bulky diseases suggest that metabolic reprogramming within the microenvironment may competitively deplete glucose and essential amino acids, further impairing immune cell function and reducing the durability of immunotherapeutic responses [59, 60]. Finally, the greater clonal diversity expected within bulky diseases increases the probability of harboring pre‐existing resistant subclones or of generating acquired resistance through clonal selection under therapeutic pressure [61]. Taken together, these considerations highlight that the rational selection of therapeutic strategies for bulky diseases should integrate not only molecular pathway profiles but also the unique biological challenges, and dedicated clinical and preclinical studies specifically designed for bulky diseases are therefore warranted to establish evidence‐based treatment approaches.

It is also noteworthy that a proportion of patients in our cohort were classified as NOS, reflecting a recognized limitation of current mutation‐based classification frameworks. Our findings suggest that integrating tumor size‐based stratification with existing genetic classification systems may offer complementary biological insight into this heterogeneous population and potentially support the refinement of current classification frameworks by providing additional biological context for NOS cases.

In summary, our integrative analysis provides novel insights into the biological characteristics of bulky disease in DLBCL. Tumor diameter reflects not only disease burden but also distinct genetic, transcriptomic, and immunological phenotypes. We propose that future prospective studies incorporating both diameter‐based and molecular stratification could help establish a more biologically informed definition of bulky disease that better captures its underlying pathological heterogeneity. Furthermore, the candidate therapeutic strategies tailored to specific molecular and immune profiles provide future precision medicine approaches for patients with bulky disease in DLBCL.
