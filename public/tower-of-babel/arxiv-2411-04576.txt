Abstract
. Large Language Models (LLMs) have been increasingly adopted by professionals for work tasks. However, using LLMs also introduces compliance risks relating to privacy, ethics, and regulations. This study investigated the compliance risks professionals perceive with LLM use and their risk mitigation strategies. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 24 law, healthcare, and academia professionals. Results showed that the main compliance concerns centered around potential exposure to sensitive customer/patient information through LLMs. To address risks, professionals reported proactively inputting distorted data to preserve privacy. However, full compliance proved challenging, given the complex interactions between user inputs, LLM behaviors, and regulations. This research provides valuable insights into designing LLMs with built-in privacy and risk controls to support professionals’ evaluation and adoption of emerging AI technologies while meeting compliance obligations.



1.
Introduction

The rapid development of Large Language Models (LLMs) has drastically changed how professionals (e.g., lawyers, creative writers, doctors, financial analyst and professors) work. “Professionals” are individuals who possess specialized knowledge and experience in a particular field. They are typically entrusted with sensitive information such as employer’s or client’s interests as part of their work
(
Colwill 2009
)
. These professionals use their professional knowledge to help people solve practical problems, and LLMs have become an integral part of their toolchains in their professional work
(
Garneau et al. 2021
)
. LLMs offer significant benefits to professionals, including increased efficiency, accuracy, and access to information. However, the use of LLMs also presents
compliance risks
that professionals have to cope with in their unique professional contexts.

Compliance risks refer to the potential legal, ethical, and reputational risks that professionals may face when conducting their professional work
(
Fanto 2021
;
Weidinger et al. 2021
)
. These risks can arise from a variety of sources, including data privacy laws
(
Klymenko et al. 2022
)
, ethical guidelines
(
Treviño et al. 1999
)
, and professional standards
(
Fanto 2021
)
. For example, lawyers may face compliance risks when using LLMs to draft legal documents since the LLMs may leak the private information of customers
(
Cheong et al. 2024
)
, while doctors may face compliance risks when using LLMs to diagnose patients since the private physical data of their patients may also be leaked
(
Qiu et al. 2023
)
. The compliance risks strongly influence the attitudes and practical strategies of professionals toward introducing LLMs to their work. Although professionals could greatly benefit from introducing LLMs to their work to improve efficiency
(
Thirunavukarasu et al. 2023
)
, there is little research investigating how compliance risks may influence their attitudes towards LLMs. Our study aims to explore the compliance risks that professionals perceive when using LLMs in their work, and to identify strategies for mitigating these risks. Specifically, we seek to answer the following research questions:

•

RQ1
: What compliance risks do professionals perceive in using LLMs to complete their work?

•

RQ2
: How do professionals mitigate compliance risks?

•

RQ3
: What challenges do professionals face when mitigating compliance risks?

We conducted semi-structured interviews with 24 professionals from data-intensive and sensitive industries, including lawyers, creative writers, journalists, doctors, auditors, and professors. The study aimed to gain insights into professionals’ perceptions of compliance risks and their mitigation strategies while using LLMs to complete professional work tasks. Our research concentrated on how professionals in different industries utilize LLMs in their workflow and their attitudes, considerations, and experiences around privacy, security, and sensitive data. We also explored how they ensure compliance with industry norms, company regulations, and professional ethics in cooperating with LLMs in work tasks. Finally, we analyzed the challenges, resources, and actions they undertake to minimize compliance risks in their work.

We found that not all professionals are well-trained in the compliance risks associated with using LLMs, despite some having previous compliance training. Their professional ethics and morals play a role in their compliance awareness, responsibility for sensitive data, and the uncertainty of consequences, all of which affect their subconscious when considering compliance risks of using LLMs. Professionals faced more external inhibitors such as ambiguity in policies and organizational climate than motivators when it came to compliance work. This led many of them to intentionally misrepresent their work or limit the use of LLMs as primary mitigation measures.
However, professionals require more resources to assess risk hazards in their risk mitigation work, especially with the lack of LLMs and profession-specific compliance guidance. The absence of guidance hampers their ability to conduct risk mitigation measures.

This study also uncovered that professionals need up-to-date knowledge about model-as-service compliance. This need calls for more focused education and training regarding the use of LLMs in professional contexts. The evolving nature of LLMs can be challenging for professionals to understand and control without clear guidance. The mismatch between rigid compliance protocols and the flexible LLM environment leads to high learning and implementation costs for professionals.
Additionally, the absence of LLM-specific guidelines and professional codes leaves compliance open to interpretation and inconsistency. These factors pose significant challenges for professionals, making it difficult for them to comply with model-as-service requirements.
This research could provide insights into the perceived compliance risks associated with LLMs and identify best practices for mitigating these risks in professional contexts.

Sensitive information can be categorized into three distinct areas: business, confidential, and personal information
(
Ware 1967
)
. Leaking such information is potentially devastating for both organizations and individuals. Under the GDPR regulations,
Personally Identifiable Information
(PII) encompasses data such as biometric and financial records, passports, and other identifiers.
Numerous studies studied various types of personal data, as specified under the GDPR. This line of research covers financial data
(
Tsoukas et al. 2021
)
, health records
(
Andalibi et al. 2017
)
, and voice recordings
(
Gómez Ortega et al. 2023
)
. The Privacy Paradox – the discrepancy between users’ privacy concerns and their actual online behavior, is another area of substantial research
(
Gerber et al. 2018
;
Willems et al. 2022
)
. Some studies have found that incentives can increase willingness to share sensitive data
(
Rudnicka et al. 2019
)
, while others have explored the economics of privacy from both consumer and corporate perspectives
(
Li and Li 2023
)
.
Within an organizational setting, confidential and commercial information is typically intended for internal use only. Such data might include meeting minutes or the terms of transactions
(
Evfimievski et al. 2002
;
Kahn et al. 2005
)
. Certain types of information, often labeled as ‘top secret’ or ‘secret’, carry higher levels of sensitivity and are strictly controlled
(
Ware 1967
)
. Industry perspectives on the handling of this data have been partially studied, focusing on various stakeholders such as IT professionals, developers, and end-users
(
Evripidou et al. 2023
;
Mink et al. 2023
;
Chan et al. 2005
)
.
Although the risks of information leakage are well-understood, particularly in the end-users or AI practitioners, there is a lack of research on groups such as professionals. Even professionals who have been trained in ethics may have a limited understanding of how these emerging technologies could compromise data privacy and security.
Our study aims to explore how professionals perceive and act upon their compliance concerns when using LLM-driven tools for completing work tasks. We seek to understand the risk definition, mitigation measures, and challenges professionals faced in terms of compliance risks, with the ultimate goal of developing industry-specific compliance-adhering mechanisms for generative AIs for professional users.

2.3.
Privacy and Security Perceptions in Large Language Models

Understanding people’s privacy perceptions of technologies is crucial in the design and implementation of LLMs. Research in this area has shown that users have varying degrees of comfort and expectations when it comes to how their data is used and protected by technological systems
(
Danezis et al. 2015
)
. Although some attention has been given to LLMs, a significant gap remains in understanding users’ privacy perceptions, specifically in a professional context.
With the rapid proliferation of LLMs in various industries, their utility in handling complex tasks across personal and business contexts has become undeniable. Yet, this advancement is not without security, privacy, and ethical challenges, including data security
(
Wei et al. 2023a
)
, information accuracy
(
Kinoshita and Shiramatsu 2022
)
, potential privacy breaches
(
Zanella-Béguelin et al. 2020
;
Yang et al. 2023
)
, disinformation
(
Botha and Pieterse 2020
)
, and copyright issues
(
Qu et al. 2023
)
. A significant concern stems from LLMs’ training data. These models, especially those with larger capacities, can inadvertently ‘memorize’ and disclose training data details
(
Brown et al. 2022
;
Kim et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2023
)
. This risk escalates due to the models’ complexity, extensive parameters, and their exposure to vast datasets, enhancing their generalization capabilities
(
Yang et al. 2023
;
Peris et al. 2023
)
. Yang et al. identified instances where LLMs overgeneralize, thereby leaking training data details, further compounded by the public availability of many LLMs
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
. Researchers have tried to mitigate the negative influence of such problems through prompt tuning
(
Li et al. 2023
)
.

Although there is a wealth of work about LLM’s usable privacy and security, there is a lack of exploration into its compliance risks, particularly in professionals’ work, which involves intensive customer data.
Our study delves into how this gap can be bridged, probing social norms and policy frameworks tailored for LLMs in professional business use contexts. We focus on professional ethics education, information protection, and enhancing privacy safeguards for business users within the LLM sphere. By comparing insights from professionals’ feedback and their definitions of compliance work, this study aims to uncover nuanced findings on AI-centred regulations and the effects of professionalism.

Compliance risk, defined as the potential for legal or regulatory sanctions against organizations and individuals

, and novel risk assessment measures
(

(
Rakha 2023

. This can involve ensuring that content adheres to cultural norms and state regulations

These compliance frameworks highlight that the risks professionals face are not only about adhering to laws but also maintaining professional reputation. This dual concern is particularly evident in industries where creative output and regulatory standards must be balanced. Regarding the differences between LLMs and other AI-based tools, it’s important to recognize that LLMs often operate with more extensive datasets and sophisticated algorithms, which can introduce unique compliance risks. Unlike traditional AI tools, LLMs have the potential to inadvertently disclose sensitive information, necessitating a deeper understanding and careful management of these risks.

3.
Method: Semi-structured Interviews

The focus of the current research is on professionals’ experiences with compliance work in their job tasks. To ensure consistency, we used a semi-structured approach during our interviews. This also allowed us to ask follow-up questions when necessary to gain deeper insights. We clarified to the interviewees that different industries and organizations may have varying definitions of what constitutes compliance work. Therefore, we explained that ‘compliance behavior’ refers to actions that align with relevant laws, rules, and standards. The definition of compliance depends on the professional’s understanding of their specific industry and organization. This approach allowed us to gain more meaningful answers from the interviewees. We interviewed a total of 24 professionals working in data-intensive and sensitive industries, including lawyers, creative writers, doctors, and professors.

3.1.
Pilot Study

Our study initially employed the Draw Exercises task, inspired by previous research
(
Zeng et al. 2017
;
Kang et al. 2015
)
. We asked professionals to illustrate their perceived data flow of LLMs and then discuss their understanding of LLMs, workflow, and data flow perceptions related to privacy, security and compliance concerns with researchers (can see in
Appendix C
). However, this approach revealed significant limitations. Many participants demonstrated limited familiarity with LLMs or their applications, such as ChatGPT
1

ChatGPT: https://chat.openai.com/
or the Auto-Summary function in Otter.ai
2
2

2

Otter.ai: https://otter.ai/
. Their representations often reflected general views of AI tools or other systems, with little variability specific to LLMs. We analysed the outputs, discussed and identified several potential reasons for this limitation. Participants may not fully understand the specific capabilities and limitations of LLMs compared to previous AI-based tools. Additionally, they may be unaware of the specific regulations and guidelines that apply to LLMs. The rapid evolution of LLM technology may have also outpaced general professional awareness. Given these challenges and considering the increasing prevalence of new LLMs alongside growing concerns about data sensitivity and security in text-based tasks, we realized a change in methodology was necessary. We pivoted to a more direct interview approach, focusing on participants’ self-reflected compliance risks and mitigation strategies. This method allowed us to explore these topics in greater depth, even with participants who had limited prior knowledge of LLMs, providing more insightful and relevant data for our study.

3.2.
Procedure

We conducted a qualitative semi-structured interview with 24 professionals to gain insights into their perceptions of compliance risks and their mitigation actions while using LLMs to complete work tasks, inspired by Board’s work
(
Board 2014
)
. We started each interview by asking participants to recall their general experiences of using LLMs and work tasks, as well as the laws and regulations they needed to comply with in their work, the rules and regulations of their industry, the organization’s work processes and rules and regulations, and the requirements of professional ethics.
During the interview process, we asked professionals about the following:

(1)
Their industry-specific practices and experiences in incorporating LLMs into their workflow and the type of work tasks they performed.

(2)
Whether there were considerations, attitudes, and reasons related to privacy, security, and sensitive data during this process. If so, what threats may arise from it?

(3)
Whether they had considered compliance with standard workflows, company regulations, industry norms, and professional ethics during this period. If so, what compliance threats may arise, as well as the motivators and obstacles to compliance;

(4)
The actions, resources, and challenges faced in reducing compliance risks in their work;

(5)
The support needed when using these LLMs tools.
We analyzed their responses to understand the definition and perception of compliance risks driven by scenarios used by professionals, practices, strategies, reasons for mitigating risks, and challenges and needs faced during this process. The entire interviewing process typically lasted around 45-60 minutes.

3.3.
Participants

In the current research, we define ‘professionals’ as individuals who possess specialized skills that they rely on for their livelihood. These individuals possess specific knowledge and skills that are necessary to fulfill a particular role within their respective industries. Most professionals adhere to strict codes of conduct, and they carry ethical and moral obligations. We recruited participants through word-of-mouth. Participants will qualify if they fit the definition of profession and have spent 3+ years in their jobs. We further selected a diverse participants based on their demographics (education, training, industry, etc.). Prior experiences with LLMs were not required as we provide a training session before the study. Participants were not compensated because as professionals, many were not allowed to take such compensation in our region.
To build on previous studies on privacy or security within sector practitioners
(
Jin et al. 2021
;
Sarkar 2023
;
Boenisch et al. 2021
)
, we conducted a study that included 24 sector professionals. These professionals were chosen from eight pivotal sectors such as Commerce and Law and were a mix of 12 females and 12 males (can see in
Table 1
). The participants had diverse academic backgrounds, including chemistry, sociology, nursing, computing, communications, and finance. These participants all have experience using LLMs (i.e. Kimi, Wenxinyiyan or POE platform)in their work. Before the interview began, we provided participants with an introduction to the basic concepts of LLMs, input usage, and the expected output effects and content, to ensure a uniform level of understanding among the interviewees regarding generative AI tools. Despite being non-native English speakers, we used Chinese as the medium for interview execution.

3.4.
Data Collection and Analysis

All the interviews are recorded using a voice recorder and then saved as audio data. The interviews are conducted in Chinese and transcribed by the first author. After the first author completed the data collection, all original interview transcripts and their translations were cross-checked and confirmed by the second and third authors. All three authors possess an English proficiency level of at least IELTS 6.5 and have completed higher education courses and academic degrees at universities where English is the medium of instruction.
In terms of protecting subjects’ information, all data were processed using our university-approved softwares followed by researchers’ manual correction to ensure accuracy, then stored in a password-protected, university-owned computer. All personal information was removed.

For data analysis, we used open coding in two steps, inspired by Merriam and Grenier
(
Merriam and Grenier 2019
)
. In the first step, we coded the transcripts line-by-line. Examples of such codes include “delete real name” and “damage personal reputation.” In the second step, we synthesized the codes obtained from the first phase to extract higher-level themes. Examples of these higher-level categories include “misrepresentation of truthful information,” “perceptions about negative impacts,” and “lack of awareness.” The coding process was iterative and discussed between two coders to ensure consistency in themes. Two researchers coded 20% of the data at sentence-level to generate initial codebook, then independently coded the rest. They regularly discussed their codes to ensure consistency and added codes to the codebook as needed. Upon completion, they organized the codes into themes.

3.5.
Study Limitations

This study has limitations that need to be addressed. A group of 24 sector professionals from eight industries was intentionally selected to focus on how privacy and security practices manifest in a particular group that frequently uses AI tools for handling sensitive information. A limitation was found concerning a subset of participants (n=4) who had not received prior professional ethics training, which may have led to a bias in their perspectives on data protection. However, this bias was instrumental in revealing differences between established ethical norms and emerging AI practices. The research team aims to contribute to developing security and privacy norms in data-dependent industries while acknowledging these limitations.

4.
Findings

In this section, we explore the perceptions of compliance risk when professionals integrating LLMs into their workflow.
We also discuss the risk mitigation strategies affected by these perceptions and the factors that motivate or inhibit their compliance. Lastly, we present methods and sources of information that professionals use to mitigate risks and overcome the challenges that they encounter.

4.1.
RQ1. What Compliance Risks do Professionals Perceive in Using LLMs to Complete Their Work?

We first report the compliance risks professionals perceive in using LLMs, which are mainly transferred from their past experiences with AI-based tools, gained from their past professional training experiences, shaped by moral dilemmas, out of responsibilities, and intertwined with uncertainty.

4.1.1.
Perceptions of compliance risks transferred from experiences with past AI-based tools

We found that some professionals(N=5) are not aware that using generative AIs can have compliance effects. One of the main reasons is that they think it is like common intelligent services, just tools that support work, such as speech-to-text software or AI-specific audio for meetings (e.g., content recording and transcription features in Zoom, Teams, or Tencent meeting). The interviewed professionals believe that as long as they actively constrain their data-sharing behavior and do not distribute the data arbitrarily, it is difficult to violate compliance regulations. For example, a business lawyer (P15) listed some smart applications (such as speech transcription applications and summarization) and hardware (such as voice recorders) that are commonly used in their work within the industry when asked about their practices using LLMs, e.g., “
We often use voice recorders or mobile phones to record the conversations with the parties involved, which greatly facilitates our documentation work. We also use AI tools like iFlytek for transcription and automatic analysis to extract key points and summaries from the dialogues. (…) Now, GPT is also being used for the same purpose.
” P2 also stated that there is almost no difference between the LLMs and the AI technologies she uses in her daily work, and the use of these software tools is also tacitly approved by the industry, and everyone uses them, e.g., “
We often use Grammarly to check the grammar and automatically edit papers. If this application does not comply, is it necessary to retract most papers?
”

One possible reason why the interviewed professionals may not perceive the compliance risks of using LLMs differently from those of using previous AI-based tools is that they may not be fully aware of the differences between LLMs and previous tools that they are familiar with. For example, they may not understand the specific capabilities and limitations of LLMs compared to previous AI-based tools, or they may not be aware of the specific regulations and guidelines that apply to LLMs. As a result, they may not be able to fully assess the compliance risks associated with using LLMs, and may, therefore, not perceive these risks as being significantly different from those associated with previous AI-based tools.

4.1.2.
Perceptions of unwritten regulations based on previous training experiences

We found that most of the interviewed professionals (N=8) were aware of certain compliance risks due to their previous training experiences, including profession-related training, job onboarding, artificial intelligence-related explanations, and other learning channels. This training knowledge makes these professionals aware of the vulnerabilities that their use of LLMs may cause, as well as the foreseeable adverse consequences, which aligns with prior work about compliance with regulation and policy
(
Tahaei et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. [n. d.]
)
. Some professionals listed several regulatory requirements to which they had to comply, and this knowledge was learned from previous training experiences. Through previous training experiences, professionals may gain a deeper understanding of compliance within their area of expertise and the ability to identify and address complex compliance issues.

For example, a financial analyst (P20) who has easy access to a large amount of commercial transaction data in China listed
anti-corruption
and
anti-commercial bribery
as important components of compliance management in Chinese companies. He is so cautious about the use of data involved in his work because of the potential penalties for non-compliance.

“
There’s also stuff like studying financial law in my major courses. Besides the ‘laws and regulations’ content we studied when getting our securities qualifications in China, these compliance issues specific to the region will also be covered in additional training at the company, such as the ‘PRC Anti-Unfair Competition Law’ or the ‘Anti-Corruption Convention’. For example, facing a hefty fine from the China Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC), the company’s management team may face criminal penalties and ultimately cause reputational damage to the company to the point where no one wants to do business with them.
”

For some professionals, the training has likely given them a good understanding of the compliance risks associated with using LLMs in their work, and can potentially help them to identify potential compliance issues.

However, not all professionals are well trained in terms of compliance risks of using LLMs in their professional work.
For some professionals, their previous training experiences may not have adequately prepared them to identify and mitigate compliance risks associated with using LLMs. For example, P15 expressed,

“
The training we received mainly explains what large language models are, how to use them, mainly for searching, I think. so far, I understand that there is no uniform industry regulations or processes involved, and most places (companies) should not have this thing, right? Anyway, no one cares about how I use it, and the use of it depends on the individual’s choice.’
’

For professionals like P15, the training they received mainly explained what LLMs are and how to use them, but did not cover any industry regulations or processes. This suggests that these professionals may not be fully aware of the compliance risks associated with using LLMs, and may, therefore, not perceive these risks as being significant.

4.1.3.
Perceptions of ethical issues in moral dilemma and responsibility

Some professionals (N=2) mention that using LLMs to complete tasks is in a gray area, lacking corresponding constraint rules and regulatory measures, which leads to a greater need to rely on
personal morality
. Unlike the clearly defined ethical standards that professionals in certain fields should follow, including work ethics, industry rules and standards, normative work processes, and other aspects of professional ethics, or explicit legal provisions as external constraints, the realization of morality when using LLMs in professional contexts relies more on professionals’
self-discipline
and
internal self-restraint
. Lawyers (P15) who have received popularization campaigns on LLMs and usage in their work organizations because of the lack of clear rules and regulations, which depends more on the individual’s personality cultivation and attitude. As she said,

“
For example, which data should be used and which should not, not to the point of being prescribed to this level of detail. without these specific guidelines, then how it is used to get the job done is my freedom. Some people don’t really care about this kind of thing when they use it, but there are some rigid people who refuse to use this tool altogether just because it might leak secrets. It has to do with their personal character and etiquette.
”

Some professionals (N=6) who are aware of compliance risks also expressed that triggering awareness of risks is due to being exposed to a large amount of sensitive information in their work. When they are in contact with such sensitive data, they realize the significant responsibility they need to bear. These responsibilities come from a variety of sources. As P16 said, “
If these leaks out, it won’t be as simple as just accepting the punishment. I need to be responsible for this client.
”
Others believe in the value of data, which has led them to recognize the importance of risk sharing, with compliance being a key means to achieve effective risk sharing. “
I feel that if I don’t follow the rules and regulations they have formulated, something will go wrong when the time comes, and who will be responsible
.” – P18.

4.1.4.
Perceptions of unknown and unanticipated consequences

Unknown consequences refer to those risks that are difficult to predict due to rapid changes in technology, laws, and societal standards, which may include new security threats, legal and compliance requirements, and other unidentified risks that affect professionals’ cognition and knowledge,
(
Buiten 2019
)
.
With the rapid development and expanding application of LLMs, new privacy and security challenges may arise. Existing laws, regulations, and knowledge frameworks may not fully cover or adapt to these rapid changes. Although professional following current best practices may face unforeseeable and knowable risks, as they may not be aware of the latest updates in compliance risks.

For example, P18 mentioned that due to the novelty of the LLMs technology, as of the interview time, there has not yet been a guiding data audit workflow method established within their work handbook, which may lead to audit negligence and erroneous audit reports, ultimately posing audit risks or unknowable risks of violation to both the auditors and the audited parties in the future.

“
This is just what we can audit within this timeframe, whether it complies with regulations within the audited party’s knowledge. If something is beyond the audited party’s knowledge, they won’t know, we won’t know, nobody in the industry will know, and there won’t be any authoritative basis. So maybe we’ll only find out in the future that something was wrong or had problems.
”

4.2.
RQ2. How Do Professionals Mitigate Compliance Risks in Their Workflows?

As part of our research on perceived compliance risks and resulting behavioral changes in professionals (RQ2),
our findings revealed that some professionals proactively mitigate compliance risks by intentionally misrepresenting their work or limiting the use of LLMs. Others seek external help by consulting the Terms of Service or reaching out to appropriate individuals. We also identified three factors that motivate professionals to ensure compliance, including maintaining professionalism, protecting their reputation, and avoiding punitive consequences. Conversely, uncertainty in compliance work, myths of modern AI, and the organizational climate were identified as inhibiting factors. The work-style costs and trade-offs play the role of a balance, with professionals making compliance decisions based on different situations and values.

4.2.1.
Risk Mitigation Practices

Proactive mitigation through intentional misrepresentation and limited prompting LLMs

Most professionals (N=14) prefer to use intentional misrepresentations to obscure the real information in their work and reduce truthful information disclosure, such as using
pseudonyms
(N=5) instead of real names due to the perceived sensitivity level of information and disclosure-induced risks, or intertwining fictional with factual elements (N=4), because the workload of data editing is small before prompting the LLMs and it is their own controllable way to be compliant.
For example, a researcher who uses AI tools to reorganize the language of academic writing mentioned, “
I usually delete some of the key content of the original text before using it. I definitely can’t let it (LLMs) know about keywords, experimental data results, or important arguments, otherwise what if other users find out?
” and P13 thought “
Because it’s fast, and it’s the easiest and most convenient way to use.
” and P16 also expressed “
I can’t believe that this thing won’t leak to anyone else, so I can only control the source of information and make sure that there will be no problems from my own side
”

There are other professionals (N=5) who consider limited use of prompts to reduce in-depth prediction or creation of inputs, i.e., coarse-grained processing of information, such as search queries and extensive original modifications, because they are afraid that too many or detailed prompting instructions will reveal an individual’s true intentions and easily allow LLMs to figure out important confidential information or easily predict or guess the key information based on the context of the inputs, ultimately violating the disclosure requirements and leading to compliance hazards.
For example, P2 noted, “
Our industry is very small, and I am worried that there will be too much hint information, one is that the original data will be easily searched by peers, and the other is that not-so-new conclusions will be seen as well.
”

Seeking external compliance information

We also found that some professionals (N=3) actively sought relevant colleagues or legal information; the main reasons are to predict risks and share compliance risks. Some professionals examine relevant legal provisions in order to predict risk points in advance and take preemptive mitigation measures.
For example, a law graduate (P16) in the relevant industry explains, “I use various software to check the client agreement to see where our data will be used, how and who can see it, whether they can protect this information and other rules, and it is their responsibility (the service provider) to go through the customer agreement before using the software, and if something goes wrong or the promise is violated. (…) For example, a WeChat mini-program will provide personal information to third parties to access, so I will consider whether to use it or how to use it”
Other professionals (N=2) believe that there are legal grounds and use data resources in accordance with regulatory procedures, even if there are unknown threats, individuals will not bear full responsibility.
One way to share compliance risks is through the terms of service of LLMs, where they will read the terms of service and feature statements before use, to hold them accountable in the future in case of data leakage or violations, preventing individuals from bearing all consequences. For example, professionals (P24) who commonly use AI tools that are recognized within the industry for auditing also stated that “
if they (LLM-service provider) fail to uphold their promise to protect data and end up leaking our data elsewhere, the responsibility is not solely ours to bear.
”

4.2.2.
Attitude Towards Compliance

Maintaining Professionalism

Some professionals talked about maintaining good professionalism as a motivation to consider compliance efforts when using LLMs to get the job done, include: Adhere to industry regulation (N=6), uphold professional ethics and duties (N=3), and ensure work quality (N=2). For example, while a lecturer used generative AI to create her teaching slides, compliance issues may inadvertently arise as part of fulfilling her responsibilities as a teacher, explained
“It can generate a lot of things, but the content still needs me to carefully review it, because there are many places where the logic is not clear, or the content has issues. As a teacher, I also need to ensure the accuracy and originality of my teaching content. (…) For example, there can’t be any bloody violence content displayed in it, or content that is not suitable for students to watch. There are also some teachers’ teaching courseware that is their own original work, with copyright ownership, and this also needs my additional review and citation. It’s also about being responsible for my work and responsible for the students.”

Other professionals have also expressed that this is related to individuals maintaining professional habits, that is, no matter how convenient and powerful LLMs services are, they will adhere to professional ethics and never disclose work information in unfamiliar applications.As P23 said
“It’s my habit to check my work equipment and network, and to refuse to use software that is not permitted by the company or specified by the client to process my work content, no matter how awesome that software is.”

Protecting Reputations

We found that some professionals (N=14) engage in compliance because they are concerned about protecting the reputation of the individual or the organization they work for. If compliance issues are not fully considered when using an LLM assistant, there may be legal consequences, such as violating professional standards or regulations on data privacy. This can have a negative impact on an individual or an organization, damaging their reputation with customers and society. As P19 said,
“Customers may not know that their data is being used in GPT and may be held accountable.”
Expert work inherently requires a high level of integrity. If there is a problem with the way LLM assistants are used, it may call into question the reliability and professionalism of an individual or organization in the legal work. Any adverse effects resulting from the use of LLMs could significantly damage the reputation of individuals or organizations in this regard. As P14 said
“After all, GPT is not a substitute for real lawyers for legal advice, and if you use the wrong information and evidence they provide, you will be questioned by your clients and ridiculed by your peers.”

Avoiding punitive consequences

We found that existing professionals engage in compliance behaviour in order to avoid punitive consequences. If there are irregularities in the work, you may face punitive consequences such as fines (N=8), prosecution (N=2), or license revocation (N=9). Some professionals indicated that compliance risks could be violated if compliance risks were not properly assessed and controlled when using LLM assistants. This can trigger legal consequences, such as fines or criminal prosecution. As P21 mentioned “Under the terms of the work contract, they can sue us. ” For professionals, their license is the most important asset. Any serious violation may result in a license revocation. This can have far-reaching consequences for individuals, such as the loss of their careers and the difficulty of continuing to work in the industry. As P23
“If there are any problems that are audited, the most serious revocation of the practice is the revocation of the practice, I’m going to be fired, and then announced, and the whole industry knows about it. and I may have to change careers and start all over again.”
Losing a license or facing penalties can not only affect an individual’s reputation, but can also lead to serious damage to jobs and livelihoods. This is a consequence that cannot be ignored for any professional. Therefore, considering the various disciplinary legal consequences that may be faced, professionals attach importance to conducting sufficient compliance assessment when using LLM assistants to avoid related risks to the greatest extent. This is one of the important reasons why they are cautious.

4.2.3.
Compliance Inhibitors

Uncertainty in compliance work

Most professionals (N=15) indicate that the main reason for not carrying out compliance work is that the current laws, regulations, and professional ethical standards do not clearly specify the standards and compliance requirements for the use of emerging LLMs collaboration technologies. In an environment where the current legal and regulatory system is unclear, professionals find it difficult to conduct a clear and comprehensive compliance risk assessment of LLM usage.
Some argued that the limitations of LLMs’ services themselves are unclear, making it difficult to determine their scope and restrictions, and it is uncertain how to use them in accordance with workflow or data usage standards. Just like mentioned in P18,
“We were only trained on how to use it (LLMs) for some basic tasks, but we don’t understand its principles specifically, what can be used, what cannot be used, and how to use them, none of it is explained.”

Various laws and regulations do not clearly specify requirements for new technologies, bringing implicit uncertainty to compliance judgments. For example, personal professional ethics rules may struggle to immediately adapt to new situations, bringing subjectivity to judgment standards. As P20 stated,
“Even though annual updates to exams are just general regulations of the system, they are different from this type of model service. It is unknown if the previous mosaic theory can be applied.” Additionally, different institutions may have varying requirements and standards for the use of LLM, increasing complexity in judgment. Just as P8 mentioned, “Our company currently requires that the code cannot be placed in gpt, but there are no temporary restrictions on other content.”

Myths of Modern AI

Some professionals (N=2) hold false beliefs about the security of their data while using LLMs. Such misunderstandings are further fueled by trust in brand well-know tech companies like OpenAI and Google. Some are aware of the “black box" nature LLMs but mistakenly think it absolves them of legal responsibility. As P9 observed,
“I only use snippets; nobody knows if I directly copy it. Only my computer knows.”

Organizational Climate

Some professionals (N=4) discussed how they driven by the behavior of their surrounding colleagues to use LLMs to improve work efficiency, and overlook compliance considerations. As P9 said, “
I didn’t expect to use this, but I saw that my colleagues around me were using it, and more and more, everyone said yes, and it was indeed better than our usual writing, and I didn’t pay special attention to what the process or regulations were not in line with, so I used it too.
”

4.2.4.
Convenient Work-style Costs and Trade-offs

Integrating LLMs into professional work processes brings both opportunities and challenges regarding regulatory compliance. To gain comprehensive insight, we examined key dimensions professionals must balance when using these tools (N=5). Productivity gains are an important benefit but must be weighed against privacy and security risks. LLMs can streamline workflows but also introduce vulnerabilities if sensitive data is exposed without safeguards. professionals noted speed alone should not compromise critical information. More robust tools are needed to proactively identify privacy and security issues when conducting non-compliance work. For example, P13 remarked,
“I will stop using it if it compromises critical information.”

Data security is another critical factor, but excessive precautions can overburden users and clash with efficient workstyles. Strong protections increase complexity, slowing processes. At the same time, basic due diligence prevents noncompliance. The feedback from P7
“As a tool, it definitely improves my work efficiency. Because I’m really not good at writing. But if I need to spend more time to repeatedly filter the unusable information, I will think that the time spent is not worth it, and this will change from reducing the burden to increasing the burden. This tool has no meaning of existence, it defeats the purpose.”
navigating this efficiency-compliance tradeoff demands guidance on impacts to workflows and priorities.

Fostering innovation also drives compliance considerations. While creative data applications may yield solutions, policies like plagiarism prevention must be upheld. professionals expressed uncertainty around legal obligations in novel scenarios. As P10 remarked,
“Maybe I don’t ask questions in a good way, so its answers are very monotonous.(…) If I add as more information, then it won’t give away more information, I’m not sure if this is legal or not.(…) Like other AI writing fiction tools, I don’t dare to use them, I don’t know how many of those segments have been used. in the end, I may be spammed by netizens saying that the work is plagiarized. the platform might also deduct my money.”
Metrics and alerts clarifying parameters could support boundary-pushing work that still conforms with regulations.

4.3.
RQ3. What Challenges Do Professionals Face when Mitigating Compliance Risks in Workflows?

In integrating LLMs into the professionals’ workflow,we discovered that professionals encountered several significant challenges in their attempts to mitigate compliance risks. Specifically, they lacked a comprehensive understanding of the entire data flow process, which led to confusion in compliance work. Additionally, a lack of education and training made it difficult to raise awareness about compliance, and the absence of LLM- and professional-specific compliance guidance hindered their ability to perform their compliance duties effectively.

4.3.1.
Lack of comprehensive understanding of the entire data flow process

Model-as-service are complex and professionals found it difficult to know what, why and how to conduct safety measures to mitigate compliance risks (N=13).
First, they lack a comprehensive understanding of the entire data flow process, which leads to confusion in compliance work. Due to not being clear about the source and operation process of the data, they find it difficult to identify and address potential compliance risks. As P13 said
“The whole process is invisible, and I don’t know where the input will go or who will see it.”

Additionally, the complexity of models as a service poses challenges for them. Professionals struggle to understand how models work and how results are produced, as well as not knowing which security measures should be taken to effectively reduce compliance risks. This uncertainty increases their work difficulty. For example, content creator (P10) who attempted to use LLMs to write some snippets said
“They say it’s text generated, but will the generated results be the same as others, and can I use it directly? I don’t know about any of this.”

4.3.2.
Lack of model-as-service compliance education and training

We found that in the process of integrating language models into professional workflows, there was a lack of compliance knowledge training for professionals in trying to mitigate compliance risks (N=7). Specifically, professionals have outdated or insufficient knowledge of such new technologies. It’s hard for them to keep up with the industry’s rapidly iterating knowledge updates just by asking IT staff or reviewing internal guidelines. For example, P15 said
“I learned about this AI from a company training seminar, and besides asking the company’s technical staff or compliance department, we had to check the news on the internet, like a lawyer used to generate fake information for a lawsuit or something, to understand the dangers of this model, such as false information, data leakage and so on. (…) .It seems that in addition to knowing this, there is also something that specifically says and privacy and security regulations.”
It is difficult for professionals to proactively access new industry cases, best practices, and changes in regulatory requirements. This also makes it difficult for them to identify unknown compliance pitfalls that language models may introduce.

4.3.3.
Lack of LLM- and profession-specific compliance guidance

We also found that the lack of support for specific compliance guidelines for language model technology and professional work content is also a challenge for professionals to implement compliance mitigation measures. On the one hand, it is difficult for professionals to cope with the new requirements brought about by language models with their previous experience (N=11). As P19 said
“For example, we have requirements that you can’t use Baidu cloud drive to store data, and you can’t upload meeting records to tencent meetings that are not corporate accounts because of the potential for data leakage. for me, I at least know that I can’t use the uploading feature that there’s just a clear behavioral red line. but the gpt kind of, puts that you don’t know how to control the process, you’re just conversationally communicating, rather than having that kind of a button to click on.”
At the same time, the complexity of the language model itself, such as its operation as an open service and the lack of clear interaction rules, also increases the difficulty of control.
On the other hand, strict standard compliance processes are not flexible enough to cope with the iterative nature of technologies such as language models. This brings high costs to professionals in learning and mastering new knowledge points and implementing compliance work. As P24 who work as IT auditor confuse about LLMs audit process and methodology, said
“Let’s say we have ITCG (IT general control) for system auditing, testing system access, change process, or other control environment and physcial security that is somewhat related to this. But if their employees are using chatgpt, which is not an official software or putting in-house system to complete the work, it’s also difficult for us to follow the established audit process to complete the access compliance audit.”

5.
Discussion

In summary, for
RQ1
, we found that although many professionals have some knowledge of compliance risks base on previously training experience, not all are well-trained in the compliance risks of using LLMs. Professional ethics and morals, the sense of responsibility for sensitive data and uncertainty of consequence subconsciously influence professionals’ awareness of compliance.
For
RQ2
, we found that professionals faced more external inhibitors than motivators for compliance work; thus, many professionals conducted mitigation measures by intentionally misrepresenting their work or limiting the use of LLMs.
For
RQ3
, we found existing resources that professionals utilized in their risk mitigation work were relying too much on external news to assessing risk hazards. The lack of LLMs- and professionals-specific compliance guidance hampering their ability to conduct risk mitigation measures.
This paper examines the compliance risks associated with LLMs in professional practices. Our recommendations primarily address the impact of LLMs on professional workflows across various industries and their implications for legal regulations. We synthesize approaches to enhance professionals’ awareness, motivation, and capability to mitigate compliance risks when integrating LLMs into their workflows. Our focus remains on compliance-specific recommendations, aiming to provide pragmatic guidance for managing the regulatory challenges posed by LLM adoption in professional settings.

5.1.
Enhancing professionals’ awareness of compliance risks

In this study, we discovered that while some professionals have some knowledge of compliance risks due to previous training, not all of them are well-trained in the compliance risks associated with using LLMs. This lack of training leads to intentional misrepresentation of work or limited use of LLMs as a mitigation measure. There is a need for professionals to have up-to-date knowledge about model-as-service compliance. This need calls for more focused education and training regarding the use of LLMs in professional contexts. We propose two potential methods to enhance professionals’ awareness of compliance risks below.

Conducting compliance training for different professionals.

Generative AI models can produce inaccurate or incomplete results, negatively impacting financial statements and medical diagnoses. To mitigate these risks, professionals could be aware of compliance threats that may arise from incorporating LLMs into their own work. Companies could establish practical and effective compliance training programs that are updated with the latest regulations and laws
(
Sekerka 2009
)
. A key takeaway is to tailor the training scope to the target audience’s specific needs.

Cultivating compliance culture within the organizational climate.

A strong compliance culture is crucial for any organization to operate legally and ethically, especially in the workplace
(
Sekerka 2009
)
. Promote and improve compliance culture by setting the tone from the top leadership, selecting compliance champions, and establishing a hotline for confidential reporting of any violations or ethical dilemmas. Encouraging a speak-up culture demonstrates the organization’s commitment to addressing compliance breaches promptly and fairly.

5.2.
Eliciting professionals’ motivation of proactive compliance mitigation

Professionals’ professional ethics and morals have been found to affect their compliance awareness, responsibility for sensitive data, and their uncertainty of consequences, which all impact their subconscious. However, professionals face more external inhibitors like ambiguity in policies and organizational climate than motivators when it comes to compliance work. To encourage proactive risk mitigation, design transparency and accountability should be followed to foster intrinsic motivation through ethical values
(
Atmadja et al. 2019
)
. Base on pro-social design consideration for motivate practitioners to addressing AI-exacerbated threats
(
Lee et al. 2024
)
, we suggest a potential method to
motivate professionals’ compliance response through designing transparency, accountability, and user-centricity to encourage prosocial behavior
.

Previous studies have shown that social factors largely influence developers’ security and privacy practices
(
Xiao et al. 2014
)
, and incorporating prosocial design could help improve them
(
Wu et al. 2022
)
. While integrating LLMs into work tasks, professionals often balance the convenience of their work style with the workload of compliance. Pro-social design could educate professionals on how ethical principles like fairness, privacy, and consent align with organizational or business values to improve their career reputation. This approach frames compliance as a means of advancing prosocial goals like establishing feedback channels for professionals and technical and legal teams to openly discuss compliance issues. Such a design could incentivize accountable behavior and foster a shared sense of responsibility. To ensure that professionals’ needs, values, and trade-offs are addressed in compliance methods, we suggest conducting ethical reviews that involve other professionals from the same industry or community. These reviews present opportunities to learn from peers through communities of practice, thereby improving professionals’ security and privacy practices
(
Whitaker et al. 2003
)
.

5.3.
Empowering professional’s ability to conduct risk mitigation strategies

As we found in RQ3, professionals require more resources to conduct effective risk mitigation measures, especially with the need for LLMs and professionals-specific compliance guidance. The absence of guidance hampers their ability to assess risk hazards and to comply with model-as-service requirements. Detailed role-based procedures and automation tools are needed to empower effective risk assessment and mitigation
(
Zakaria et al. [n. d.]
;
Kim et al. 2016
)
. By reducing workload through the above scaffolding and automation, professionals can dedicate more efforts to issues needing human judgments for continual improvement in risk governance. We envision two potential methods to empower professionals’ ability of conducting risk mitigation strategies:

Scaffolding support through protocols, checklists, and internal resources can boost capabilities.

Previous studies have demonstrated that a well-designed scaffolding plan can establish a systematic workflow that adheres to regulations and standards
(
Zakaria et al. [n. d.]
)
. The scaffolding can be customized according to industry-specific model documentation templates and audit programs, effectively structuring processes for professional compliance work protocol
(
Kim et al. 2016
)
. It can also provide role-specific standard operating procedures, control matrices, and compliance checklists as reference material to ensure a consistent and compliant approach in the workplace.

Leveraging compliance automation and tooling to reduce workload while ensuring rigor.

Implementing compliance automation and tooling can reduce the manual effort required for repetitive compliance tasks such as documentation, testing, and monitoring model drift over time
(
Bedi et al. 2020
)
. This will ensure consistency and accuracy in applying controls while minimizing human errors
(
Dzuranin and Mălăescu 2016
)
. We can leverage model monitoring tools that continuously track performance metrics and detect anomalies. Furthermore, we can utilize testing frameworks that automatically execute professional-specific checklists on model versions. We can streamline the process by integrating documentation generation tools that extract and update metadata at each stage.

Providing fine-tune model for professionals.

An important strategy to decrease the perceived compliance risk is to fine-tune the LLMs for professionals from different areas, which could identify the sensitive information from each viewer and conduct preprocessing before inputting them into the AI model for inference. Fine-tuning LLMs can leverage the abundant professional knowledge and be supported by expert organizations. Techniques like few-shot learning also ensure good performance with limited input data. This strategy could greatly filter out the sensitive information from users’ input. Considering the cost of fine-tuning LLMs, any strategy is to conduct a data-make operation concerning the input from users, which could also strongly alleviate professional users’ anxiety about data security.

5.4.
Limitations and Future Work

Our study’s primary limitation is the lack of an objective “ground truth” for compliance risks associated with LLMs use in professional contexts. We relied on participants’ subjectivity perceptions, which may not accurately reflect actual risks. Consequently, we cannot draw definitive conclusions about real compliance risks, only report on professionals’ attitudes and awareness. Future research should address this by combining technical analysis of LLMs, comprehensive legal and regulatory assessments, and objective knowledge tests for professionals. This multi-faceted approach would allow for a more accurate evaluation of the gap between perceived and actual risks. Additionally, longitudinal studies could track how these risks evolve with advancing LLM technology.

6.
Conclusion

Professionals have increasingly adopted Large Language Models (LLMs) for their daily work tasks, however, compliance issues continue to be significant concerns for not only professionals but also corporates.
This research aims to investigate the compliance risks professionals perceive with LLM use and their risk mitigation strategies. Through an interview-based study with 24 professionals from law, healthcare, and academia domains, we identified several main compliance risks perceived by professionals, such as compliance risks transferred from experiences with past AI-based tools, unwritten regulations based on previous training experiences and ethical issues in moral dilemma and responsibility. We found that professionals often encounter significant challenges due to external factors that hinder their ability to carry out their duties effectively. As a result, many professionals resort to tactics such as misrepresenting their work or limiting the prompts of LLMs to mitigate risks. The lack of LLMs- and professional-specific compliance guidance further exacerbates the problem. Our findings suggest that there is a strong need for more comprehensive and tailored solutions that can support professionals in mitigating compliance risks when integrating LLMs into their workflow.
