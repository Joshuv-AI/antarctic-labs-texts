Functionalized MoS2 nanosheets as high-affinity nanocarriers for gemcitabine: a molecular simulation study
Authors: Fatemeh Najafi, Farzaneh Farzad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were employed to investigate the adsorption mechanisms of the chemotherapeutic drug gemcitabine (GEM) on pristine and functionalized molybdenum disulfide (MoS₂) nanosheets. The functionalization of MoS₂ was achieved by incorporating polyethyleneimine (PEI), polyethylene glycol (PEG), and cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CPG) groups, enhancing the material’s drug adsorption capacity. Our simulations reveal that the MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG system exhibits a significantly higher affinity for GEM compared to pristine MoS₂, with interaction energies reaching −236 kJ/mol, driven primarily by van der Waals (vdW) interactions and electrostatic forces. Metadynamics simulations further confirm that the global minimum free energy for the functionalized system is approximately −237 kJ/mol, compared to −199 kJ/mol for pristine MoS₂. Radial distribution function (RDF) analysis and density profiles highlight stronger drug-substrate interactions and reduced solvent exposure in the functionalized system, which also exhibits lower drug mobility, suggesting more stable adsorption. These findings are supported by experimental data, demonstrating that functionalized MoS₂ nanosheets offer a promising platform for enhancing drug delivery systems. This work underscores the importance of surface functionalization in optimizing drug adsorption and stability, offering valuable insights for the development of more efficient nanomaterials in targeted drug delivery applications.

Introduction

Cancer remains a significant threat to human health, with standard treatments like chemotherapy and radiotherapy offering limited efficacy and often severe side effects. While these methods have been widely used in clinical settings, they frequently cause extensive damage to the body. They are associated with high recurrence rates, failing to fully address the demands of advanced biomedical therapies1,2.

Gemcitabine (GEM) is a well-established and widely utilized chemotherapeutic agent that has gained significant attention due to its efficacy against specific tumor types, particularly pancreatic cancer and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)3,4. Early studies using both mouse and human cell lines, as well as solid tumor models in mice, demonstrated that gemcitabine effectively inhibits the growth of various tumors, especially pancreatic and NSCLC. It is relatively well-tolerated by patients and is associated with fewer severe side effects compared to traditional chemotherapy agents such as cisplatin and doxorubicin3,4.

Promising clinical outcomes in the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, breast cancer, ovarian cancer, and bladder cancer significantly contributed to the approval of gemcitabine by the U.S. food and drug administration (FDA) in 1996 and the European medicines agency (EMA)5–11.

Over the past few decades, a diverse range of nanomaterials with varying dimensions have been developed and are increasingly employed in promising biomedical applications. These include zero-dimensional (0D) nanoparticles and quantum dots, one-dimensional (1D) nanotubes and nanofibers, two-dimensional (2D) nanosheets, and three-dimensional (3D) nanostructured scaffolds. These materials have shown remarkable potential in critical fields such as targeted drug delivery, advanced cell imaging, and innovative tumor therapy strategies12–17.

A novel class of two-dimensional (2D) nanomaterials known as transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) has recently attracted significant attention and demonstrated immense potential across various fields, including biomedicine18–20. These materials are characterized by the general formula MX₂, where M denotes a transition metal, and X represents a chalcogen. Structurally, TMDCs consist of a single monolayer of transition metal atoms sandwiched between two layers of chalcogen atoms in an X-M-X configuration. The transition metal (M) typically belongs to groups IV, V, or VI of the periodic table, while the chalcogen (X) is commonly sulfur (S), selenium (Se), or tellurium (Te). Within each layer, the atoms are held together by strong covalent bonds, whereas adjacent layers interact through weak van der Waals (vdW) forces, enabling exfoliation into ultrathin nanosheets with unique electronic, optical, and mechanical properties21.

TMDC nanosheets exhibit substantial promise for biomedical applications, including drug delivery, tumor imaging, and therapeutic interventions, owing to their high surface area, abundant reactive sites, and ease of functionalization. Their large specific surface area and numerous active sites facilitate chemical modifications, enabling the attachment of biomolecules such as proteins, nucleic acids, or targeting ligands. This functionalization enhances the biocompatibility of TMDC nanosheets and enables precise, targeted therapeutic effects22.

In recent years, numerous research groups have explored the potential of TMDC nanosheets, including molybdenum disulfide (MoS₂), tungsten disulfide (WS₂), and bismuth selenide (Bi₂Se₃), for diverse applications such as biosensing, imaging, drug delivery, and photothermal therapy. These investigations have demonstrated promising results in both in vitro and in vivo studies, highlighting the versatility and efficacy of these materials in biomedical and technological contexts23–25.

Among TMDC nanosheets, MoS₂ and WS₂ stand out due to their exceptional physical and chemical properties, such as high electrical conductivity, large surface area, and tunable bandgaps. These characteristics render them highly suitable for a broad spectrum of applications, including transistors, catalysis, and biomedicine26,27. Their unique properties, combined with their ability to be functionalized for specific purposes, underscore their potential as advanced materials in both industrial and medical fields.

As is well established, graphene and graphene oxide are extensively utilized as drug carriers for delivering various small molecules through π-π stacking and hydrophobic interactions. However, compared to graphene, and molybdenum disulfide, nanosheets demonstrate significantly higher drug-loading efficiency and a superior adsorption coefficient at 808 nm, making them particularly suitable as photothermal agents for cancer therapy. These properties enable MoS₂ nanosheets to effectively convert near-infrared (NIR) light into thermal energy, facilitating targeted tumor ablation23,25.

Liu et al. demonstrated the potential of MoS₂ nanosheets as a dual-functional platform, combining chemotherapy and photothermal therapy for the delivery of anticancer drug molecules. Their studies revealed significant synergistic effects in both in vitro and in vivo models, highlighting the enhanced therapeutic efficacy of this approach25. This dual-modality strategy not only improves drug delivery precision but also reduces systemic toxicity, offering a promising avenue for advanced cancer treatment25.

Recent advancements in the chemical synthesis and functional modification of transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) have spurred extensive research into their biomedical applications. Several comprehensive reviews have been published, detailing the synthesis, modification, and biomedical potential of TMD materials. For instance, Chen et al. highlighted the use of two-dimensional (2D) TMD nanosheets in cancer therapy and bioimaging, while Chimene et al. explored the biomedical applications of TMDs and transition metal oxides (TMOs), emphasizing their versatility in therapeutic and diagnostic contexts1,28,29.

In a notable study, Xiaofei Zhou and colleagues30 demonstrated that the functionalization of molybdenum disulfide (MoS₂) nanosheets with polyethylene glycol (PEG), polyethyleneimine (PEI), and cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) groups significantly enhanced their solubility and efficacy as drug delivery systems (DDS) in cancer cells. These modified MoS₂ nanosheets were shown to improve the bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy of poorly soluble drugs, underscoring their potential as advanced nanocarriers. Despite these significant advancements and the valuable insights gained, considerable opportunities remain to further explore and optimize the biomedical applications of TMDs, paving the way for innovative solutions in nanomedicine1.

Despite numerous experimental studies investigating the use of molybdenum disulfide (both pristine and modified) in drug delivery systems30–32, as well as extensive research on gemcitabine as a therapeutic agent5,33,34, the molecular interactions between GEM and MoS₂ remain inadequately explored in theoretical studies (Fig. 1). A comprehensive understanding of these interactions is essential to enhance the performance and efficiency of drug delivery systems (DDSs). Methods

MD simulation

Molecular dynamics simulation

Four molecular dynamics (MD) simulation systems were designed to evaluate the efficacy of functionalized molybdenum disulfide (MoS₂) nanosheets as two-dimensional drug carriers for the anticancer agent gemcitabine. The systems included:

A pristine MoS₂ nanosheet with four gemcitabine molecules.

A PEG/PEI-functionalized MoS₂ nanosheet with four gemcitabine molecules.

A CPG-functionalized MoS₂ nanosheet with four gemcitabine molecules.

A MoS₂ nanosheet functionalized with PEG/PEI/CPG and four gemcitabine molecules.

All systems were solvated in a TIP3P water model within a periodic cubic box (11 × 11 × 11 nm3). To maintain structural integrity, MoS₂ nanosheet atoms were position-restrained during simulations35. The functionalization scheme used in our simulations was designed based on the experimental work by Zhou et al.30, which demonstrated that PEG, PEI, and CPG groups can be effectively immobilized on MoS₂ nanosheets through covalent bonding (Fig. 3). Following their experimentally verified configuration, we modeled the systems as follows:Fig. 3The initial snapshots of the pristine molybdenum disulfide (a), MoS2/PEG-PEI (b), MoS2/CPG (c), and MoS2/CPG-PEI-PEG (d).

The CPG group and the PEG chain were each covalently attached to opposite sides of the MoS₂ nanosheets. The PEG group was covalently attached to the CPG strand via a stable N–C bond. The PEI chain was covalently attached to the remaining 2 sides of the MoS₂ nanosheet via a terminal amine bond. Both PEG and PEI chains were made with four monomer units (Fig. 4). The chain length was carefully chosen to match the dimensions of the nanosheet (11 × 11 × 11 nm3) and to ensure a realistic and balanced molecular configuration that avoids steric hindrance or excessive crowding while maintaining sufficient structural flexibility.Fig. 4The final snapshots of the pristine molybdenum disulfide (a), MoS2/PEG-PEI (b), MoS2/CPG (c), and MoS2/CPG-PEI-PEG (d).

In brief, all three functional groups—PEG, PEI, and CPG—were covalently attached to the MoS₂ nanosheet according to the experimental model of Zhou et al.30. The choice of chain length and attachment configuration was made to ensure structural stability, compatibility with the nanosheet surface, and alignment with experimental observations.

Simulations were performed using GROMACS 2019.2, with the CHARMM36 force field applied to GEM, PEG, PEI, and CPG. GEM topology files were generated via the SwissParam server, while MoS₂ parameters were adopted from Liu et al.36. The Nose–Hoover thermostat and Parrinello–Rahman barostat maintained system temperature at 310 K and pressure at 1 bar using isotropic coupling37.

The energy minimization of systems using the steepest descent algorithm was done. Then, all of the systems were equilibrated at an average temperature of 310 K and an average pressure of 1 bar throughout the entire simulation process. The equilibration was performed for 300 ps using the NVT and NPT ensembles, respectively. The temperature and pressure were maintained constant using V-rescale and Berendsen algorithms, respectively. During equilibration, the usage of the NVT and NPT ensembles allowed systems to achieve thermal and pressure equilibrations. The V-rescale thermostat is provided efficient temperature control without impacting molecular dynamics. The Berendsen algorithm for pressure coupling ensured gradual equilibration without causing abrupt structural perturbations. All of the MD simulations were run for 105 ns under periodic boundary conditions. To keep all bonds in their equilibrium position in these systems, the LINCS algorithm was used. To evaluate long-range electrostatic (Ele) interactions, the particle-mesh Ewald (PME) method was employed. Visual molecular dynamics (VMD) was used to analyze the movement path of molecules35.

Well-tempered metadynamics simulations

The well-tempered metadynamics (WT-MetaD) technique, developed by Parrinello et al., was employed to identify the free energy surface with respect to the collective variable (CV), which is the center-of-mass (COM) distance of a single gemcitabine (GEM) molecule from the MoS₂ surface. The metadynamics simulations are carried out using the PLUMED plugin version 2.5.239,40 which is integrated into the GROMACS software (version 2019.2) package. The initial Gaussian height of the well-tempered metadynamics algorithm is 1.0 kJ mol−1 with a width of 0.25 Å. Additionally, a bias factor of 15 is employed, and deposition occurs every 500-time steps. It should be emphasized that the metadynamics simulations are carried out for 105 ns across all systems investigated. Generally, the metadynamics simulations provide a reliability estimate of the free energy landscape for the pristine MoS₂ and functionalized MoS₂ systems. The study focused on comparing the free energy landscapes of both MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG and pristine MoS₂ systems at 310 K. The free energy changes are presented as a function of the distance between the center of mass of MoS₂ and GEM38. Additionally, it is important to note that, despite multiple GEM molecules being included in the system, metadynamics was applied to one representative GEM molecule at a time. This approach was chosen to focus on the interaction between a single GEM molecule and the MoS₂ surface, ensuring a clearer understanding of the adsorption behavior and free energy profile for drug-nanosheet interactions.

Result and discussion

MD simulation

This research analyzed the efficiency of functionalized MoS2 nanosheets in drug adsorption through molecular dynamics simulation. Zhou et al. demonstrated that PEG and CpG functionalization of MoS₂ greatly improves its solubility and biomolecule delivery capabilities, which directly inspired our attachment geometry and supports the covalent PEG–CPG linkage30. Liu et al. showed that PEGylated MoS₂ boosts drug loading and therapeutic outcomes in combined photothermal and chemotherapy, aligning with our observations of enhanced drug interactions25. Also, Han et al. revealed that CpG-functionalized MoS₂ not only binds nucleic acid but also amplifies biological activity, reinforcing our findings on strong π–π interactions26. Furtheremore, Yin et al. demonstrated MoS₂’s ability to efficiently deliver drugs in a photothermal-triggered manner, further validating our conclusion that MoS₂ can be an effective drug carrier when properly engineered23. Lastly, Kou et al. highlighted PEG’s role in enhancing MoS₂’s stability and drug loading, which mirrors our findings on improved GEM adsorption and reduced solvent exposure32.

The primary novelty of this study lies in the development of a functionalized MoS₂-based drug delivery system that combines PEG, PEI, and CPG groups to achieve enhanced drug adsorption and targeted release properties. While previous studies have explored MoS₂ for drug delivery, our work differentiates itself in several key aspects.

First, the co-functionalization of MoS₂ with PEG, PEI, and CPG groups is a unique strategy that not only improves the dispersion and stability of MoS₂ in aqueous solutions but also enhances its interaction with hydrophilic drug molecules like gemcitabine. Unlike other approaches that focus on functionalizing MoS₂ with a single group, our combined functionalization significantly improves the drug loading capacity, while also providing a controlled release mechanism. This dual functionality is critical for overcoming challenges related to non-specific adsorption and drug leakage, common issues in many drug delivery systems30,31.

Second, our study introduces a detailed mechanistic investigation into the interactions between the functionalized MoS₂ surface and gemcitabine, which is rarely addressed in previous literature. By using molecular dynamics simulations, we provide a comprehensive understanding of how the functional groups (PEG, PEI, and CPG) influence the adsorption behavior, reducing water molecule interaction and enhancing the overall drug delivery efficiency. This level of detail in simulating the nanosheet-drug interactions is a significant contribution to the field25,26,32.

The basic step in molecular dynamics (MD) simulations involves evaluating structural stability and confirming system equilibration, typically determined by analyzing root mean square deviation (RMSD) trajectories. Interaction energy

Understanding the interaction energies between drug molecules and MoS₂ substrates is crucial for unraveling the adsorption mechanisms that govern drug delivery systems35. These energy profiles provide insights into the molecular forces—such as van der Waals (vdW) interactions, π-π stacking, and electrostatic forces—that dictate drug-substrate binding. Analyzing these interactions allows for the optimization of nanomaterial design to achieve enhanced drug delivery efficiency and stability35,38.

Using the gmx energy module in Gromacs software, we quantified the vdW interaction energies between drug molecules and nanostructures, with results illustrated in (Fig. 6). The MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG system with gemcitabine exhibits the strongest interactions, with vdW and electrostatic energy values of −162 and −74 kJ/mol, respectively, resulting in a total interaction energy of −236 kJ/mol (Table 2). The high absolute values of these energies indicate robust binding between the substrate and Gem. The increasingly negative energy of the system with enhanced adsorption demonstrates the stabilizing effect of functional groups. This observation aligns with prior studies35,41,42 that highlight the importance of vdW forces in biomolecule adsorption on MoS₂, confirming that vdW interactions are the primary driver of drug adsorption in these systems.

Functionalization of the substrate significantly enhances both vdW and electrostatic energies, with vdW interactions dominating. For instance, drug molecules preferentially bind to PEI-PEG functional groups via hydrogen bonding and to CPG through π-π stacking interactions. These specific interactions not only enhance adsorption efficiency but also contribute to the overall stability of the drug-substrate complex. The combined presence of PEG, PEI, and CPG functional groups makes MoS₂ substrates highly effective for drug adsorption, outperforming systems with fewer or no functional groups. This synergistic effect is further supported by the RDF analysis (Fig. 8), where the MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG system exhibits the highest peak, indicating the greatest proximity and thus strongest interaction between the drug molecules and the substrate.

As illustrated in Fig. 6, the MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG system exhibits the most significant drop in vdW energy as GEM approaches the substrate, indicating a stronger, more stable interaction. This system reaches a lower energy state compared to MoS₂/CPG, MoS₂/PEI-PEG, and pristine MoS₂, confirming the synergistic effect of the combined functional groups. The initial sharp decrease in vdW energy for MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG around 20,000 ps suggests rapid and favorable adsorption. These results are consistent with the lower MSD values observed for this system, indicating reduced drug mobility due to stronger binding.

These findings validate the spontaneous adsorption of drug molecules onto aqueous MoS₂ surfaces, with drug adsorption driving a reduction in vdW energy. Systems with lower Lennard–Jones (LJ) potentials are more efficient at facilitating cellular drug uptake, further highlighting the importance of functional group engineering. The energy values confirm that drug molecules achieve high stability on functionalized MoS₂ nanostructures, making them ideal for drug delivery systems.

Our analyses indicate that the superior adsorption in the co-functionalized system arises from a synergistic—or cooperative—effect among the different functional groups rather than from a simple increase in binding-site density. Although the PEG, PEI, and CPG groups are spatially distinct, their combined presence modulates the physicochemical environment of the MoS₂ surface in a complementary way, producing a cooperative enhancement in GEM binding through multiple short-range interactions: The CPG moiety provides aromatic bases that engage in π–π stacking with the aromatic ring of gemcitabine, while the nearby PEG and PEI chains generate a hydrogen-bond-rich microenvironment that stabilizes the adsorbed drug at the nanosheet–solvent interface. Even though GEM does not simultaneously contact all three functional groups, the local electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding fields created by the neighboring groups reinforce one another, leading to stronger overall binding.

The interaction-energy analysis shows the most negative van der Waals and electrostatic energies for the co-functionalized system, confirming enhanced non-covalent stabilization.

The spatial distribution of the three functional groups creates an energetically favorable adsorption pocket in which π–π stacking with CPG is complemented by hydrogen bonding and electrostatic stabilization from nearby PEG and PEI chains. This cooperative environment effectively reduces desorption tendencies and enhances the persistence of GEM on the functionalized surface. The results of this study have significant implications for the design of nanomaterials for medical applications. Functionalized substrates, particularly MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG, exhibit stronger interaction energies and better drug stability compared to non-functionalized substrates. This demonstrates the critical role of functional group engineering in optimizing drug adsorption and delivery. The triple-functionalized MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG system outperforms other configurations, offering higher adsorption efficiency and stability. This makes it a promising candidate for targeted drug delivery applications. By carefully selecting and combining functional groups (e.g., PEG, PEI, CPG), researchers can design nanomaterials with tailored adsorption properties and stability. The spontaneous adsorption of drug molecules onto functionalized MoS₂ surfaces, driven by vdW and electrostatic interactions, ensures stable binding and efficient drug delivery. This is particularly important for applications requiring controlled and sustained release of therapeutic agents.

In summary, van der Waals (vdW) interactions are the primary driver of drug adsorption on MoS₂ substrates, complemented by electrostatic contributions. The MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG system, with its triple functionalization, exhibits the strongest interactions and highest adsorption efficiency. These findings provide a foundation for designing next-generation nanomaterials with tailored properties for targeted drug delivery and other biomedical applications. By optimizing functional group interactions and leveraging the unique properties of MoS₂, we can develop advanced drug delivery systems that improve therapeutic outcomes and patient care.

Analyzing the Mean Square Displacement (MSD) plot reveals differences in the diffusive behavior of the four systems; MoS2 exhibits the highest MSD, indicative of unrestricted diffusion, while the addition of PEI-PEG and CPG sequentially reduces the MSD, suggesting a constrained diffusion regime, and the MoS2/CPG-PEI-PEG system displays the lowest MSD, which points towards highly restricted or localized movement likely due to increased drug-carrier interaction. Notably, drug molecules exhibit high selectivity toward specific functional groups when adsorbed onto the nanosheets. Compared to other systems, the MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG system exhibits a lower self-diffusion coefficient, as evidenced by the reduced slope of its MSD curve (Fig. 7a). This indicates restricted mobility of drug molecules, attributed to their strong adsorption onto the adsorbent surface. Specifically, drug molecules interact with the CPG functional group through π-π interactions, while hydrogen bonds form with the PEI and PEG functional groups. These interactions limit the drug’s ability to diffuse freely.

Conversely, the pristine MoS₂ system displays significantly higher diffusion coefficients (Di) and greater error margins compared to functionalized systems. This behavior is likely due to weak interactions between the drug molecules and the unmodified nano surface, resulting in less controlled and more variable diffusion dynamics. Additionally, the smooth, fluctuation-free MSD curves suggest that the drug molecule systems converge and stabilize, reaching a steady state during the adsorption process on the substrate39. The validity of the molecular dynamics (MD) simulation results is strongly supported by the excellent agreement between our findings and experimental data39. Functionalization with groups such as CPG, PEI, and PEG increases the substrate’s hydrophilicity, thereby improving its ability to attract and adsorb drug molecules. This synergy between simulation and experimental insights underscores the potential of tailored surface modifications to optimize drug delivery systems.

Furthermore, Xiaofei’s group demonstrated that functionalizing MoS₂ with CPG groups significantly enhances the drug adsorption capacity of the substrate compared to both pristine MoS₂ and MoS₂ functionalized with PEI and PEG30. The MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG system exhibits the lowest diffusion, indicative of strong drug-substrate interactions and stable adsorption. These findings underscore the importance of carefully balancing surface functionalization to optimize drug loading in drug delivery applications.

The RDF profiles for the four systems are presented in (Fig. 8). A common feature across all systems is the presence of a primary peak within a distance of approximately 1 nm from the MoS₂ surface. However, the intensity of these peaks varies significantly, indicating differing interaction strengths.

The RDF peak heights, indicative of the strength of drug-substrate interaction, follow the order: MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG > MoS₂/CPG > MoS₂/PEI-PEG > MoS₂. This trend reveals that the MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG system exhibits the most pronounced interaction with GEM, suggesting a synergistic effect from the combined functional groups. Specifically, the high peak in MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG indicates a higher probability of finding GEM molecules in proximity to the nanosheet surface compared to the other systems. The MoS₂/CPG substrate also shows a stronger peak relative to MoS₂/PEI-PEG and pristine MoS₂, suggesting more robust intermolecular interactions, potentially driven by π-π stacking between GEM and the CPG functional group. In contrast, the pristine MoS₂ system displays the lowest peak, consistent with weaker, non-specific interactions.

Furthermore, the RDF analysis reveals that the highest peaks for drug molecules occur at distances ranging from 0.8 to 1.5 nm from the substrate surface, indicating the spatial distribution of drug molecules relative to the substrate (Fig. 8). These findings strongly align with the MSD results, where MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG displayed the lowest diffusion coefficient, suggesting restricted mobility due to strong adsorption. The RDF data supports this by showing that GEM molecules are more likely to be found in close proximity to the MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG surface.

Overall, the RDF analysis offers critical insights into the nature and strength of drug-substrate interactions within the four systems. These findings are consistent with the MSD results and previous studies26,30, further validating the reliability of the observations and underscoring the role of surface functionalization in modulating drug adsorption behavior.

A drug’s interaction with its solvent environment is directly influenced by its exposure level to surrounding conditions46. The solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) quantifies this exposure geometrically, providing a critical link between molecular surface accessibility and the system’s free energy47,48. While some models assume a linear relationship between SASA and free energy46, exact computational determination remains challenging due to SASA’s non-additive pairwise interactions49,50.

SASA is calculated using the Lee and Richards algorithm51,52, which models the solvent-accessible surface as a sphere of radius R around each atom. Here, the solvent molecule’s center traces the atom’s surface, ensuring no overlap with neighboring particles.

The MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG nanocomposite shows superior hydrogen bonding capabilities due to the synergistic effect of both CPG and PEI-PEG functionalization. The MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG system, with its superior hydrogen bonding network, presents the most promising architecture for efficient gemcitabine delivery applications.

Figure 13 illustrates the free energy surface (FES) landscape for both systems: (a) pristine MoS₂ and (b) MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG. The global minimum free energy for the MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG system at 310 K is approximately -237 kJ/mol, while for the pristine MoS₂ system, it is around -199 kJ/mol. This difference indicates that the MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG system exhibits a more negative free energy level, which corresponds to stronger interactions between GEM and the functionalized surface compared to the pristine MoS₂.

The enhanced FES in the MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG system is attributed to the presence of functional groups, particularly CPG and PEI-PEG. These groups enhance interactions with GEM, leading to a more negative free energy state. These findings align with the interaction energy values presented in Table 2 and Fig. 6, reinforcing the conclusion that functionalization promotes stronger binding.

As the GEM molecule approaches the water-substrate interface, it encounters relative energy barriers, as shown in Fig. 13. For the MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG system, these barriers are observed at approximately −183 kJ/mol at a distance of ~ 6 nm and −202 kJ/mol at ~ 7.8 nm. The most significant barrier occurs at ~ 1.4 nm with an energy of −237 kJ/mol. Notably, as GEM approaches the MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG surface and surpasses the initial barrier, a significant decrease in free energy is observed, indicating a transition to a more stable adsorbed state.

This progressive decrease in free energy stabilizes the system, highlighting the role of functional groups in enhancing adsorption efficiency. These results align with recent findings reported by Shahabi et al. and T. Walsh and colleagues35,41, who also observed that surface modifications can significantly impact the adsorption behavior of drug molecules.

The MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG system has indeed been shown to be an excellent choice for maximizing the adsorption of gemcitabine due to the strong interactions between the functional groups and the drug molecule. However, whether gemcitabine will desorb once the correct location is reached depends on the specific conditions and mechanisms at play, which we have considered in our simulations.

Gemcitabine adsorption to the MoS₂ surface is primarily driven by electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals forces, enhanced by the functional groups (PEG, PEI, and CPG). Once gemcitabine is adsorbed, its desorption is expected to occur under specific conditions, such as changes in pH, ionic strength, or the presence of competing molecules. For instance, in a biological setting, such as within the tumor microenvironment, factors like local pH shifts or the presence of specific enzymes or biomolecules could facilitate desorption and subsequent release of the drug31.

In our simulations, we observed that the functionalized MoS₂ nanosheet holds gemcitabine relatively tightly under neutral conditions, which is ideal for drug delivery purposes. However, the design of the functionalized MoS₂ surface ensures that gemcitabine can desorb in response to the appropriate external stimuli, such as changes in pH or the presence of other molecules that compete for adsorption sites. This controlled desorption mechanism could be crucial for achieving targeted drug release at the desired location, such as in the tumor site, where acidic conditions could trigger the release of the drug54,55.

In summary, while the MoS₂/CPG-PEI-PEG system provides strong adsorption of gemcitabine, the system is designed to allow for controlled desorption when the appropriate conditions are met, facilitating the targeted delivery and release of the drug at the intended site.

Radial distribution function (RDF) analysis and density profiles further confirm the enhanced drug-substrate interactions and reduced solvent exposure in the functionalized system.
