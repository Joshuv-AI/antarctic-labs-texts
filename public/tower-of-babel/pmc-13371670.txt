Mpox knowledge, attitudes toward Mpox vaccine and hesitancy for Mpox vaccination among teachers in Kenya
Authors: Patrick M. Mutua, Michael M. Gicheru, Joshua Mutiso, Erick K. Serem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSixteen African countries, including Kenya, reported Mpox cases over a two-month period, prompting the Africa Centers for Disease Control and Prevention to declare the outbreak a Public Health Emergency of Continental Security on 13th August 2024. The World Health Organization issued guidance for Mpox prevention in schools, where teachers are expected to support infection prevention. This study assessed teachers’ knowledge of Mpox and attitudes toward Mpox vaccination, given these factors that may influence teachers’ confidence in promoting preventive behaviours and vaccination awareness among learners.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted among 1,139 teachers in Kenya between 21st November 2024 and 17th February 2025. Structured questionnaires assessed Mpox knowledge (12 items) and attitudes toward Mpox vaccination (6 items). The use of mean scores to define adequate versus inadequate knowledge and attitude was justified by approximate normal distribution of the data. Descriptive statistics and logistic regression analyses were performed.ResultsThe mean Mpox knowledge score was 8.2 out of 12 (68.3%), and the mean attitude score for Mpox vaccine was 3.7 out of 6 (61.7%), indicating moderate knowledge and attitudes. Over 34% of respondents were unaware of reported Mpox cases in Kenya. Vaccine hesitancy was 49.8%, with 62% of the hesitating respondents citing concerns about potential side effects. Teachers with certificate and diploma qualifications were significantly more likely to accept vaccination.ConclusionsTeachers demonstrated moderate knowledge and attitudes but high Mpox vaccine hesitancy. Implementation of focused strategies, including structured risk communication and integration of Mpox education into professional development are essential to enhance vaccine uptake, reinforce positive attitudes and strengthen teachers’ capacity to protect learners in line with WHO guidance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27882-4.

Introduction

Mpox, formerly known as Monkeypox, is a zoonotic disease caused by the Mpox virus, belonging to the genus Orthopoxvirus and family Poxviridae ([20, 35]. The virus was first isolated in monkeys in 1958, and the first human case was reported in the Congo in 1970 [10]. Central Africa, particularly the Congo, remains the epicenter of infection [19].

Mpox continues to re-emerge as an important global public health concern as demonstrated by the multi-country outbreak reported in 2022. During this outbreak, more than 87,000 laboratory-confirmed cases and over 140 deaths were reported across more than 110 countries, with the majority occurring in Europe and the Americas [32]. Although global case numbers declined in 2023 leading to the lifting of the Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC), transmission persisted in Africa where the disease has historically been endemic. The African continent experienced a steady increase in reported Mpox cases, rising from approximately 8,000 cases in 2022 to about 15,000 cases in 2023, and reaching more than 18,000 suspected and confirmed cases by August 2024 [2]. The majority of these cases were reported from Central and West African countries, including the Democratic Republic of Congo and Nigeria, where zoonotic transmission, limited surveillance capacity and constrained access to diagnostics and vaccines continue to challenge outbreak control [3, 33]. The growing burden and geographic spread of Mpox across the continent prompted the Africa Centers for Disease Control and Prevention to declare a Public Health Emergency of Continental Concern on 13th August 2024 after the disease had been reported in at least 16 African countries. Within this regional context, Kenya reported its first confirmed Mpox case on 31st July 2024, and by October 2024, a total of 12 confirmed cases had been documented in the country [30]. These developments highlight the increasing risk of Mpox transmission in East Africa and underscore the need for strengthened public health preparedness and community awareness in countries such as Kenya. The increasing burden of Mpox cases in African countries highlights persistent continental vulnerability and underscores the public health importance of targeted interventions across the continent. It also highlights broader global health inequities in outbreak prioritization, whereby international attention and resources often diminish once transmission declines in high-income settings despite ongoing transmission in endemic regions [1]. In this context, community-based risk communication and health literacy become critical components of outbreak preparedness and response.

While Mpox is primarily zoonotic, increasing human-to-human transmission has been reported via close contact, sexual intercourse, respiratory droplets, and vertical transmission from mother to child [7, 12]. Vaccination remains the most viable preventive strategy [9], with smallpox vaccines conferring 35–85% cross-protection [11, 15]. The Africa CDC is collaborating with the European Commission and Bavarian Nordic to secure over 215,000 doses of the MVA-BN vaccine for the continent [14], though rising cases may require careful allocation per WHO SAGE guidelines [34].

Teachers were selected for this study because they represent a strategic population for both infectious disease risk and public health intervention. On one hand, teachers work in densely populated school environments that facilitate the spread of communicable diseases. On the other hand, they are widely distributed across the country and interact daily with large numbers of learners positioning them as effective agents for disseminating public health information and supporting early detection and response to outbreaks.

In Kenya, for example, the basic education school community comprises approximately 222,000 teachers working in 32,594 primary schools and 128,000 instructors teaching in 10,482 secondary schools [27]. Additionally, school communities include an estimated 10.4 million learners aged 6–13 years in primary schools and 3.9 million students aged 13–18 years in secondary institutions [27]. This extensive network means that teachers are present in virtually all regions of the country, allowing them to reach large segments of the population through school-based health interventions.

Additionally, the Kenyan basic education learning ecosystem is vulnerable to Mpox outbreaks. This vulnerability arises not only because learners fall within age groups that can facilitate transmission, but also due to structural challenges within many schools. Numerous institutions experience congestion, inadequate water supply, and insufficient sanitation infrastructure while others are located far from medical facilities [29]. Furthermore, an estimated 70% of students commute to school from their homes, increasing the likelihood of introducing Mpox infections from surrounding communities into school environments [29].

Given these conditions, teachers have a unique role compared to other community groups. They are well positioned to detect early symptoms among learners, facilitate isolation where necessary, and refer suspected cases to health facilities in line with WHO school-based guidance [36]. Despite this critical role, data on Kenyan teachers’ Mpox knowledge and attitudes toward vaccination remain scarce. Establishing these baseline measures is essential for designing targeted educational interventions and vaccination programs that strengthen teachers’ capacity to protect learners and help curb potential outbreaks in school settings.

Beyond their institutional role, teachers’ own health beliefs and preventive behaviours may influence how effectively they communicate public health messages to learners and school communities. Previous public health research has shown that individuals who demonstrate confidence in vaccination are generally more likely to recommend vaccines and participate in health promotion activities [17]. Although teachers do not necessarily need to be vaccinated in order to educate learners about disease prevention, negative attitudes toward vaccination or personal vaccine hesitancy may reduce the confidence, consistency, and credibility with which preventive messages are delivered. Conversely, teachers who possess adequate knowledge and positive attitudes toward vaccination may be better positioned to support evidence-based health education, encourage preventive practices, and contribute to a culture of trust toward public health interventions within schools. Assessing teachers’ own willingness to accept Mpox vaccination is therefore important not only from an occupational health perspective, but also because it may indirectly influence school-based outbreak preparedness and learner protection.

Materials and methods

Study setting and design

This was a cross-sectional study to examine knowledge of Mpox and attitudes toward Mpox vaccine as well as identify factors associated with Mpox vaccination hesitancy among teachers in Kenya. The study was conducted from 21st November 2024 to 17th February 2025. The TSC Sub County directors, who are in charge of teacher management in the 300 sub counties in Kenya, were first taken through the study design and objectives by the authors. The study objectives were then explained to the participants via virtual meetings that were conducted by the TSC Sub County Directors at the beginning of the study period. This study was approved by Pwani University Institutional Scientific and Ethics Review Committee and assigned approval number ISERC/PU-STAFF/005/2024.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study sampled and included registered teachers employed by the TSC and working in primary and secondary schools in Kenya at the time of the study. Furthermore, only teachers who read and agreed to provide informed consent and submitted responses to all questionnaire items within the required time were included in the final study sample.

Sample size

There are approximately 350,000 teachers employed by the Teachers Service Commission (TSC) and working in more than 32,000 public schools across Kenya [27]. The minimum sample size for this study was initially estimated using the Raosoft online sample size calculator [25], applying a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a conservative response distribution of 50%, which maximizes sample size when the true prevalence is unknown. Under these assumptions, the minimum required sample size for a simple random sample was 384 respondents. Consistent with recommendations for large-scale population surveys using stratified sampling, a design effect of 2.0 was applied [8]. This adjustment increased the minimum required sample size to 768 respondents. To further account for potential non-response and incomplete questionnaires, the sample size was adjusted using an anticipated non-response rate of 25%, which is commonly observed in online survey-based studies among professionals [16, 23]. The adjusted sample size was therefore calculated as:

To enhance statistical power for multivariable logistic regression analyses, as well as ensure adequate representation across Kenya’s geographically and demographically diverse counties, the target sample size was further increased to approximately 1,500 respondents. A stratified random sampling technique was used to ensure national representativeness. Kenya’s 47 counties served as the primary sampling strata, while sub-counties within each county constituted secondary strata. The sampling frame was derived from the official Teachers Service Commission database, which provides the distribution of teachers by county and sub-county. The number of respondents selected from each sub-county was allocated proportionally to the number of teachers employed in that sub-county relative to the total national teacher population. Within each sub-county, teachers were randomly invited to participate through links provided by the respective TSC Sub-County Directors. The survey link was distributed electronically using Google Forms to facilitate nationwide participation. This proportional stratified approach ensured inclusion of teachers from diverse geographic and institutional contexts, including urban, peri-urban and rural schools thereby improving the external validity and national representativeness of the study findings.

Data collection and analysis

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire that had been previously developed and validated [37]. Minor modifications were made to ensure contextual relevance. This included the addition of items capturing respondents’ sub-county of work and their awareness of Mpox cases reported in Kenya at the time of data collection. The adapted questionnaire underwent content validation by the authors and reliability testing using a test–retest method among 185 randomly selected teachers in Makueni County, Kenya. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha which yielded a coefficient of 0.82, indicating good internal consistency. Test–retest reliability analysis produced an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.79 demonstrating acceptable stability of the instrument over time. The final questionnaire consisted of four sections. The first section collected socio-demographic information, including age, sex, years of teaching experience, type of school, awareness of Mpox cases in Kenya and sources of Mpox-related information. The second section comprised 12 items assessing knowledge of Mpox, including transmission routes, symptoms, prevention measures and vaccination. Each knowledge item was scored 1 for a correct response and 0 for an incorrect or “don’t know” response yielding a total possible knowledge score ranging from 0 to 12. The third section contained six Likert-scale items assessing attitudes toward the Mpox vaccine, with responses ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5). These items were summed to generate a composite attitude score ranging from 6 to 30, with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes toward Mpox vaccination. The fourth section included ten potential factors influencing vaccine hesitancy, from which participants selected those that applied to them.

Prior to inferential analysis, the distribution of knowledge and attitude scores was assessed for normality using the Shapiro - Wilk test. The results indicated that both knowledge scores (W = 0.97, p = 0.08) and attitude scores (W = 0.98, p = 0.11) did not significantly deviate from normality. Given the approximately normal distribution of these variables, mean scores were used as cut-off points to categorize respondents into two groups: adequate versus inadequate knowledge and positive versus negative attitudes toward the Mpox vaccine. To enhance interpretability, these cut-offs were further interpreted using Bloom’s Taxonomy for non-health professionals in which scores of 80–100% are considered “good,” 60–79% as “moderate,” and below 60% as “poor” [5]. Applying this framework, respondents in this study who scored below 80% were classified as having moderate knowledge and attitudes, providing a structured justification for the reported classifications. The use of the mean as a threshold has also been applied in similar Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP) studies where validated cut-off points are unavailable and the score distribution approximates normality [22].

Data collection

Data were collected electronically using Google Forms, which were distributed through Teachers Service Commission (TSC) Sub-County Directors across all 47 counties in Kenya. Completed responses were coded and exported into Microsoft Excel before being imported into IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 for analysis. Data cleaning and consistency checks were conducted prior to statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize respondent characteristics and study variables. Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Because the knowledge and attitude variables were subsequently categorized into binary outcomes, chi-square tests were used to examine bivariate associations between categorical independent variables (such as sex, type of school, and years of teaching experience) and the dichotomized knowledge and attitude levels. To obtain adjusted estimates and control for potential confounding variables, binary logistic regression analyses were performed. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and a p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

(A) Demographic characteristics of the respondents

A total of 1,549 participants submitted their responses; however, 410 questionnaires were excluded because they were incomplete, and, therefore, 1,139 questionnaires constituted the final sample representing a response rate of 75.9% of the estimated sample size. The respondents included 672 males and 467 females accounting for 59% and 41% of the respondents, respectively. The median age group of the respondents was 35–44 years. More than 40% of the sampled teachers had a certificate or diploma, 41% had a degree certificate while 18.8% had postgraduate qualifications. Primary and secondary school teachers comprised 59.35% and 40.65% of the respondents, respectively. More than 71% of the participants were from day schools. (B) Sources of Mpox information

As shown in Fig. 1, more than 73% of the participants acquired Mpox information from digital portals and social media such as WhatsApp and Facebook. More than one-fifth of the respondents obtained Mpox information from traditional media including television, newspapers and radio. Approximately 2.19% and 2.6% of the participants obtained Mpox information from communications with colleagues and friends and from other sources, respectively. Only 1.23% of the sampled teachers obtained Mpox information from the employer (Fig. 1). At the time of the interviews, more than 34% (388/1139) of the respondents were not aware of the Mpox cases reported in the country.

(C) Respondents’ knowledge of Mpox

A total of 12 questions were used to assess respondents’ knowledge of Mpox. The mean Mpox knowledge score was 8.2 (SD = 1.6), equivalent to 68.3%, therefore, an adequate Mpox knowledge score was considered for values between 8.2 and 12. An estimated 86% and 72% of the respondents correctly answered that Mpox was an infectious disease and was caused by a virus. More than 71% of the participants correctly responded that people are susceptible to Mpox. However, more than 52% of the respondents incorrectly reported that Mpox has a cure. Regarding symptoms, 29% and 25% of the participants were not aware that Mpox can cause fatigue and fever, respectively. Additionally, 37% and 27% of the respondents incorrectly indicated that Mpox does not cause swollen lymph nodes and headache, respectively. An estimated 39% of the sampled teachers were not aware that Mpox causes back or muscle aches. Table 2 shows summary of the responses obtained in each of the 12 questions.

(D) Respondents’ attitudes toward the Mpox vaccine

Six questions were used to determine respondents’ attitudes toward the Mpox vaccine. An attitude score was derived by calculating the number of appropriate responses to the 6 questions and a mean attitude score of 3.7 (SD = 0.72), translated to an average attitude percentage score of 62% was obtained. An adequate attitude score was considered for values between 3.7 and 6. More than 81% of the participants indicated that it is important to be vaccinated against Mpox. However, when asked “would you be willing to get vaccinated against Mpox”, the percentage dropped to 50.22% of the participants who would agree to be vaccinated. The respondents had inadequate attitude scores of 3.3 and 3.0 for vaccine safety and for willingness to get vaccinated, respectively. Additionally, an inadequate attitude score of 3.2 for the willingness of participants to be vaccinated if the Mpox vaccine was provided for free was reported. (E) Association between groups and their level of Mpox knowledge and attitudes toward Mpox vaccination

A chi-square test was applied to determine the statistical associations between background characteristics (age, gender, marital status, level of education and type of school a teacher taught) and their level of Mpox knowledge as well as their attitudes toward Mpox vaccination. There was no significant (P ˃ 0.05) association between any of the respondents’ background characteristics and their level of Mpox knowledge or their attitudes toward vaccination. In order to evaluate the correlation between Mpox knowledge and attitude towards Mpox vaccination, a Phi test was conducted and a value of 0.077 (P = 0.05) was obtained.

(F) Demographic characteristics of participants and their willingness to receive Mpox vaccine

The acceptance group represented more than 50% (572/1139) of the respondents whereas 49.78% of the participants (567/1139) composed the hesitant group. A chi-square test was used to determine the differences in the distribution of vaccination hesitancy among characteristics which included age, gender, level of education, marital status, type of school, category of school and experience. The results revealed that only the level of education was significantly (P = 0.03) different between Mpox vaccine acceptance and hesitancy. The rates for vaccine acceptance and vaccine hesitancy for certificate/diploma participants were 54.8% and 45.17%, respectively. For undergraduates, the acceptance and hesitation rates were 46.48% and 53.51%, whereas postgraduate vaccine acceptance and hesitant rates were 48.6% and 51.4% respectively (Table 4). A binary logistic regression analysis examining factors influencing Mpox vaccine acceptance (1 = vaccine acceptance, 0 = vaccine hesitancy) showed that teachers holding certificate or diploma qualifications were 2.32 times more likely to accept the Mpox vaccine compared with teachers holding other qualifications (OR = 2.32; 95% CI: 1.29–4.15; P = 0.01). (G) Mpox hesitancy factors among the participants

On the basis of the WHO 3Cs (convenience, confidence, complacency) vaccine hesitancy model [31], participants were provided with a list of 10 hesitancy factors to select from. As shown in Fig. 2, more than 62% of the respondents hesitated to receive the Mpox vaccine because of they are concerned about the potential side effects. More than 18% and 16% of the participants would hesitate Mpox vaccination because of a failure to receive professional advice and because they were concerned about vaccine effectiveness, respectively. Approximately, 2.9% of the participants would hesitate to be vaccinated because they had been vaccinated against smallpox whereas more than 1.4% of the participants would hesitate to receive vaccination because they believed that Mpox was not a serious infection. Discussion

This study is the first in Kenya to assess Mpox knowledge, attitudes, and vaccine acceptance among teachers, providing important insights for school-based public health interventions. Overall, teachers demonstrated moderate knowledge (68.3%) and attitude scores (62%) toward Mpox vaccination, with significant gaps in understanding treatment, vaccine availability, and symptom recognition. While more than 86% and 72% of respondents knew that Mpox is infectious and caused by a virus, respectively, only 47.9% were aware that there is no cure, and over 58% were unaware of the existence of a safe vaccine. Knowledge gaps were also evident regarding prodromal symptoms such as swollen lymph nodes, muscle aches and skin rash. These deficiencies may limit teachers’ ability to identify, isolate, and support affected learners, as recommended in the WHO interim guidance for schools [36].

When contextualized with studies involving non-health professional populations in Africa, Kenyan teachers’ Mpox knowledge and attitudes appear relatively higher. For instance, only 28.9% of Nigerian non-health professional respondents demonstrated adequate Mpox knowledge and positive perceptions of the vaccine [4], while 42.8% of Algerian university students in health and life sciences had sufficient knowledge [21]. In the Congo, over 17% of respondents, including both health and non-health professionals, lacked Mpox knowledge despite a vaccine acceptance rate of 61% [24]. These comparisons suggest that, even within non-health professional populations, knowledge and vaccine attitudes vary widely; nonetheless, Kenyan teachers show comparatively higher knowledge and moderately positive attitudes, indicating their potential as key agents for school-based Mpox prevention and vaccination campaigns.

A central finding of this study is the attitude-behavior gap. Although 81% of teachers agreed that Mpox vaccination is important, only just over 50% reported willingness to be personally vaccinated. This discrepancy aligns with contemporary behavioural theories of vaccine uptake, which emphasize the role of perceived risks and benefits, barriers, self-efficacy and cues to action in influencing health behavior [26]. In this study, perceived barriers, including concerns about vaccine side effects (62%) and lack of professional advice (18%), likely reduced personal uptake despite recognition of vaccination importance. Smaller proportions cited beliefs that Mpox would not become endemic in Kenya. These findings highlight the importance of addressing perceived barriers and providing clear, professional guidance to increase confidence, motivate uptake, and narrow the gap between recognizing the importance of vaccination and actual willingness to be vaccinated.

The observed discrepancy between recognizing the importance of Mpox vaccination and personal willingness to be vaccinated has broader implications for school-based health promotion. Teachers are often regarded by learners and parents as trusted sources of information and behavioural role models within school communities. While vaccination of teachers is not a prerequisite for teaching learners about disease prevention, teachers who express uncertainty or hesitancy toward vaccination may be less likely to confidently reinforce public health recommendations related to vaccination and outbreak control. In contrast, positive vaccine attitudes among teachers may strengthen the credibility and effectiveness of school-based health communication. These findings therefore support the importance of improving both Mpox knowledge and vaccine confidence among teachers as part of comprehensive school health preparedness strategies.

Education level was a significant predictor of vaccine acceptance, with teachers holding certificate or diploma qualifications being 2.32 times more likely to accept the Mpox vaccine compared with those holding other qualifications. Most certificate/diploma teachers were primary school educators, working in congested schools with limited sanitation and large populations of commuting students, factors that increase the risk of infectious disease transmission [27, 29]. Leveraging this group’s higher acceptance rates could strengthen vaccination campaigns if coupled with targeted educational interventions to address knowledge gaps and concerns regarding safety and efficacy.

Sources of information influenced both knowledge and attitudes. Over 73% of participants reported receiving Mpox information via digital platforms such as WhatsApp and Facebook, whereas only 1.23% received information from the Teachers Service Commission. Traditional media reached more than 20% of respondents. Given the documented potential for misinformation on digital platforms [6, 13], there is an urgent need for collaboration between the TSC and healthcare providers to provide accurate, professional guidance. Evidence suggests that professional, authoritative communication reduces vaccine hesitancy and improves uptake, particularly when accompanied by clear messaging on vaccine type, indications, dosage, efficacy, and safety [17, 28].

Prior smallpox vaccination was cited by a small proportion of older respondents as a reason for hesitancy, reflecting waning immunity and the global discontinuation of smallpox vaccination since 1981 [18]. Educational campaigns should clarify that previous smallpox vaccination confers limited protection against Mpox and that vaccination is recommended irrespective of age or prior immunization history.

Conclusions

This study highlights critical gaps in Mpox knowledge and attitudes toward the Mpox vaccine among teachers in Kenya which may impede the successful implementation of vaccination programs targeting school communities. Despite moderate overall knowledge and attitudes, over 49% of participants expressed vaccine hesitancy, reflecting a significant barrier to achieving high vaccine coverage. Notably, knowledge gaps were most pronounced regarding Mpox treatment, the existence of a safe vaccine, and the recognition of prodromal symptoms, while an attitude-behavior gap revealed that many teachers acknowledged the importance of vaccination but were hesitant to be vaccinated themselves.

The findings have important implications for the Teachers Service Commission and the Ministry of Health. The TSC, in collaboration with healthcare providers, should prioritize the dissemination of professional and reliable Mpox information to teachers, focusing on vaccine safety, anticipated side effects, efficacy and symptom recognition. Tailored educational interventions targeting certificate and diploma teachers, particularly in primary schools where the risk of outbreaks is higher due to school congestion, could strengthen school-based vaccination campaigns and enhance teachers’ roles as agents of health promotion in their communities.

At the policy level, this study underscores the need for integrated school-based public health strategies that combine professional education, active communication, and vaccination delivery, ensuring that teachers are well informed and confident in advocating for Mpox prevention. Furthermore, the study highlights the value of longitudinal research to monitor changes in teachers’ knowledge, attitudes, and vaccine hesitancy over time as the Mpox outbreak evolves in Kenya. Such research would provide evidence to refine interventions, assess the impact of educational programs, and inform broader public health policy for emerging infectious diseases in school settings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the data were collected at a specific point in time, reflecting teachers’ knowledge and attitudes toward Mpox during the survey period. Consequently, the results may not capture subsequent changes in awareness, perceptions, or policy developments that have occurred since data collection. As Mpox-related information and public health responses continue to evolve, current levels of knowledge and attitudes could differ from those observed in this study.

Second, the study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias, potentially influencing participants’ responses. Additionally, the use of TSC Sub County Directors to disseminate the Google forms may have introduced organizational social desirability bias, whereby respondents provided answers they perceived to be expected by their superiors or the TSC rather than their true beliefs or behaviours.

Third, the study utilized online data collection methods, which may have limited participation among teachers in areas with poor internet connectivity. Internet coverage in Kenya varies considerably between urban and rural areas, and some teachers may have faced difficulties accessing the survey due to limited network availability, low bandwidth or intermittent power supply. This digital divide could have introduced selection bias, as respondents with reliable internet access may differ in knowledge, attitudes, or access to public health information compared with those in under-connected areas.

Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into Mpox knowledge, attitudes, and vaccine acceptance among teachers in Kenya, serving as a baseline for future research and informing school-based public health interventions.
