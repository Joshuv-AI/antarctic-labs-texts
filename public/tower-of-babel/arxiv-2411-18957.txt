Bayesian Cluster Weighted Gaussian Models
Panagiotis Papastamoulis, Konstantinos Perrakis

Abstract
We introduce a novel class of Bayesian mixtures for normal linear regression models which incorporates a further Gaussian random component for the distribution of the predictor variables. The proposed cluster-weighted model aims to encompass potential heterogeneity in the distribution of the response variable as well as in the multivariate distribution of the covariates for detecting signals relevant to the underlying latent structure. Of particular interest are potential signals originating from: (i) the linear predictor structures of the regression models and (ii) the covariance structures of the covariates. We model these two components using a lasso shrinkage prior for the regression coefficients and a graphical-lasso shrinkage prior for the covariance matrices. A fully Bayesian approach is followed for estimating the number of clusters, by treating the number of mixture components as random and implementing a trans-dimensional telescoping sampler. Alternative Bayesian approaches based on overfitting mixture models or using information criteria to select the number of components are also considered. The proposed method is compared against EM type implementation, mixtures of regressions and mixtures of experts. The method is illustrated using a set of simulation studies and a biomedical dataset.

Introduction

Assume that the observed data consists of independent pairs
\{(y_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top})^{\top},i=1,\ldots,n\}
, where
n
denotes the sample size. Each observation consists of a continuous response variable (denoted as
y_{i}
) which could be linearly affected by a (subset) of
p
covariates, denoted as
\mathbf{x}_{i}=(x_{i1},\ldots,x_{ip})^{\top}
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
. We consider the situation where the population exhibits heterogeneity; i.e., it consists of an unknown number of latent groups (or clusters), where each cluster exhibits distinct patterns in the underlying (linear) relation of the response (
y
) to the covariates (
\mathbf{x}
). The goal then, is to infer the number of hidden clusters as well as their underlying distributional characteristics. Under a model-based clustering point of view
(
Banfield and Raftery 1993
;
Bensmail et al. 1997
;
Fraley and Raftery 2002
;
McNicholas 2016
;
Grün 2019
)
, mixtures of linear regression models
(
DeSarbo and Cron 1988
)
or mixtures of generalized linear models
(
Jansen 1993
;
Wedel and DeSarbo 1995
;
Gaffney and Smyth 1999
)
(as briefly reviewed below) are widely used for this purpose.

Typically, in a regression setting, the covariates are considered fixed. However, this is not always a realistic assumption.
Random covariates can occur in any application where it is not possible to control their values.
Hosmer 1974
estimated a mixture of two regressions, motivated by an example where female and male halibuts can be grouped by examining the relationship between their length (response variable) and age (covariate). Commercial catches are used for this purpose and in such a setting it is difficult to control the age of each fish
(
Hennig 2000
)
.
Perrakis et al. 2023
are motivated by latent disease sub-types in biomedicine, where subtype-speciﬁc covariance patterns can help to reveal heterogeneity in the response (cancer type). According to
Punzo and Ingrassia 2015
, the main benefit when considering random covariates in a mixture of regressions is that they affect the clustering structure, a property which is known as
assignment dependence

(
Hennig 2000
)
.

The literature on mixtures of regressions, considering fixed covariates, is vast
(
Wedel 2002
;
Leisch 2004
;
Grün and Leisch 2007
;
Grün and Leisch 2008
;
Yao et al. 2014
;
Punzo and Ingrassia 2015
;
Papastamoulis 2016
;
Papastamoulis 2023
)
. Within a maximum likelihood framework, estimation is performed using the Expectation-Maximization algorithm
(
Dempster et al. 1977
)
or variants
(
McLachlan and Krishnan 2008
)
. Bayesian approaches are also available
(
Viele and Tong 2002
;
Hurn et al. 2003
;
Shi et al. 2005
;
Lee et al. 2016
;
Papastamoulis 2023
;
Im et al. 2023
)
. Penalized mixtures of regressions are considered in
Khalili and Jiahua 2007
;
Khalili and Lin 2013
;
Städler et al. 2010
;
Chamroukhi 2016b
under a frequentist framework, while
Cozzini et al. 2014
offer a Bayesian treatment. Other models include semi-parametric
(
Hunter and Young 2012
;
Huang and Yao 2012
;
Berrettini et al. 2023
)
or non-parametric
(
Huang et al. 2013
)
mixtures of regressions.

A broader class of mixture models where mixing probabilities are modeled as functions of the covariates are the so-called mixtures of experts
(
Nguyen and Chamroukhi 2018
;
Gormley and Frühwirth-Schnatter 2019
)
. The origins of such models can be traced in the machine learning community
(
Jacobs et al. 1991
)
, with various applications found in speech recognition
(
Peng et al. 1996
)
, election studies
(
Gormley and Murphy 2008
)
, social networks
(
Gormley and Murphy 2010
)
, time-series data analysis
(
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2012
)
.
Nguyen and McLachlan 2016
and
Chamroukhi 2016a
present mixtures of experts with Laplace and
t
distributed errors, respectively.
Murphy and Murphy 2020
introduced a parsimonious family of Gaussian mixtures of experts, which is also available as an R package
(
Murphy and Murphy 2023
)
.

Mixture models which treat covariates as random are referred to as cluster-weighted models (CWMs)
(
Gershenfeld 1997
;
Ingrassia et al. 2012
;
Ingrassia et al. 2015b
;
Gallaugher et al. 2022
;
Oh and Seo 2023
)
. In CWMs, the number of parameters increases dramatically with
p
due to estimation of covariance matrices within each cluster, thus, it is crucial to enforce sparsity by introducing parsimonious representations
(
Subedi et al. 2013
;
Punzo and Ingrassia 2015
;
Dang et al. 2017
)
of the model.
Perrakis et al. 2023
proposed a joint mixture model with random covariates under a Bayesian framework, using shrinkage priors and an Expectation-Conditional Maximization algorithm
(
Meng and Rubin 1993
)
for sparse estimation.
The approach is novel within the CWM family, but not without limitations as it is: (i) restricted to point estimation, (ii) dependent on tuning procedures for penalty parametes and (iii) designed for models with a fixed number of components.

In this work we present a new Bayesian Gaussian Cluster Weighted Model (BGCWM) approach. The proposed model allows direct uncertainty quantification for all related aspects of latent structure. Specifically, the proposed model treats the number of mixture components as unknown by using the flexible machinery of generalized mixtures of finite mixture models of
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2021
(see
McCullagh and Yang 2008
;
Miller and Harrison 2018
for additional information). Furthermore, the model incorporates shrinkage of (i) the regression coefficients via the Bayesian lasso prior
(
Park and Casella 2008
)
and (ii) the covariance structure of the covariates via the Bayesian graphical lasso
(
Wang 2012
)
, using half-Cauchy hyperpriors
(
Polson and Scott 2012
)
for the corresponding penalties, thus, avoiding potential tuning problems. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling is based on an augmented Gibbs algorithm where all the full conditional distributions of the parameters are available and only one additional Metropolis-Hastings step for a single parameter is required when the number of clusters is unknown.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section
2.1
defines the likelihood of the model. The prior distributions are presented in Section
2.2
, starting with the prior assumptions for the mixture components and number of clusters and followed by the corresponding prior assumptions related to the parameters of the mixture of the linear regressions and the parameters of the covariate distributions. Posterior inference based on MCMC sampling is discussed in Section
3
; the full conditional distributions (conditional on the number of mixture components) are described in Section
3.1
and the implementation of telescoping sampling for updating the number of mixture components is presented in Section
3.2
. Identifiability issues and variable selection are discussed in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
, respectively. In Section
4
we consider simulation studies and comparisons to other methods as well as an application to a real dataset. A synopsis and future research considerations are provided in Section
5
.

For the response variable, we assume a normal linear regression model within each cluster

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{352000}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle Y$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{352000}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle Y$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{307966}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle Y$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{307964}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle Y$}}}}}}=(\alpha_{k},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k},\sigma^{2}_{k})^{\top}

the error variance. Thus, conditional on

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left\{p(y_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{352000}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle Y$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{352000}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle Y$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{307966}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle Y$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{307964}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle Y$}}}}}},\mathbf{x}_{i})p(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{389220}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle X$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{389220}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle X$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{347926}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle X$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{347927}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle X$}}}}}})\right\}^{z_{ik}}

for
i=1,\ldots,n
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})^{\top}
and

. We also denote by

independently for

. So, the

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\pi},K)

(2)

2
), considering the latent group allocations as missing data. Then the
complete likelihood
can be expressed as

p(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Z}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\pi})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\Big\{\pi_{k}f_{\mathrm{N}_{1}}(y_{i}\mid\alpha_{k}+\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k},\sigma^{2}_{k})f_{\mathrm{N}_{p}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})\Big\}^{z_{ik}},

where the dimensionality of
\mathbf{Z}
is

.

2.2
Prior distributions

In this section we describe the proposed prior specifications of the BGCW model. We begin with the prior distribution of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
and the different approaches for handling an unknown number of
K
components. Following this, we present the priors assigned to the parameters of the linear regression component and to the parameters of the multivariate predictor component of the proposed mixture model.

2.2.1
The prior over the mixing weights and approaches for unknown
K

For the conditional distribution of mixing proportions (given the number of components
K
), we make the usual assumption of a Dirichlet distribution; i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\pi}|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},K

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{K}),

(4)

where
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{K})
, with
\gamma_{k}>0
for all
k=1,\ldots,K
.
Usually, each component in a finite mixture model represents a cluster. However, this is not always the case. Another school of thought makes a clear distinction between the number of components in a mixture (that is,
K
) and the number of clusters, which corresponds to the number of non-empty components; i.e., components with at least one observation assigned to them. Below we review alternative approaches that we consider in our experimental results.
In brief, when the number of components is considered fixed we set
\gamma_{k}=1
, for
k=1,\ldots,K
(as is typical; see e.g.,
Marin et al. 2005
and Approach 1 below). In the case of an unspecified number of components, we can estimate overfitting mixture models under a sparse Dirichlet prior (see Approach 2 below) or consider the more general approach where
K
is random and fit a generalized mixture of finite mixture models (see Approach 3 below).

Approach 1: Fixed number of components

The simplest approach we could follow is to assume that the number of mixture components is fixed and each component represents a cluster. Then, we estimate models, separately, for a range of possible values
K\in\{1,\ldots,K_{\mathrm{max}}\}
, where
K_{\mathrm{max}}
denotes a fixed constant. The number of components (or clusters) can then be selected according to information criteria, such as the Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC)
(
Akaike 1974
)
, the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)
(
Schwarz 1978
)
or the Integrated Completed Likelihood (ICL)
(
Biernacki et al. 2000
)
criterion. Although this is not a fully Bayesian technique, we will consider it in our illustrations as a baseline approach due to its popularity within the model-based clustering community; see e.g., the
flexmix

(
Gruen and Leisch 2023
;
Leisch 2004
;
Grün and Leisch 2007
;
Grün and Leisch 2008
)
and
mclust

(
Scrucca et al. 2016
)
packages in R. In this case, the Dirichlet concentration parameters
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{K}
in Eq. (
4
) are all set equal to
1
, a choice leading to a uniform distribution defined in the set
\{\pi_{k},k=1\ldots,K:\pi_{k}>0,\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}=1\}
.

Approach 2: Overfitting mixture models

An overfitting mixture model is one where the number mixture components
K
is larger that the “true” one, the latter one representing the actual number of clusters in the data.
Rousseau and Mengersen 2011
showed that the posterior distribution of an overfitted mixture model, asymptotically, will empty the extra components after placing a sparse Dirichlet prior distribution in the mixing proportions. Various implementations of this framework can be found in
Van Havre et al. 2015
;
Papastamoulis 2018
;
Papastamoulis 2020
;
Papastamoulis 2023
. Under this setup, the number of mixture components is fixed to a constant value
K=K_{\mathrm{max}}
which serves as an upper bound on the number of clusters. Then, inference for the number of clusters is based on the number of non-empty mixture components, i.e. components with at least one observation allocated to them, across the MCMC run. In the experiments presented below we consider
K_{\mathrm{max}}=20
with the Dirichlet concentration parameters
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{K}
in Eq. (
4
) all set equal to
10^{-3}
.

Approach 3: Generalized mixtures of finite mixtures

Ultimately, the general framework of mixture models with a prior on the number of components is considered. We follow the approach in
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2021
, modeling the data as a dynamic mixture of finite mixtures and estimating the posterior distribution using the telescoping sampler. The flexibility offered by this approach lies mainly on the fact that it explicitly samples the number of (unknown) mixture components, requiring only a relatively trivial MCMC step (unlike other approaches such as the reversible jump MCMC sampler
(
Richardson and Green 1997
)
, for instance).
In this method, a translated Beta-Negative Binomial
\mathrm{BNB}(a_{\lambda},a_{\pi},b_{\pi})
prior is placed on
K-1
, such that the corresponding probability mass function on the number of components is given by

p(K)=\frac{\Gamma(a_{\lambda}+K-1)B(a_{\lambda}+a_{\pi},K-1+b_{\pi})}{\Gamma(a_{\lambda})\Gamma(K)B(a_{\pi},b_{\pi})}.

(5)

Following
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2021
, we set
a_{\lambda}=1,a_{\pi}=4,b_{\pi}=3
, which results in a weakly informative prior with fat tails for the number of clusters. From Eqs. (
2
) and (
5
), the joint marginal distribution of the data
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
is represented as a countably infinite mixture of finite mixtures with
K
components

p(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}|\{{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{K},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{K}\}_{K=1}^{\infty})=\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}p(K)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k,K}p(y_{i}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k,K}^{Y},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})p({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k,K}^{X}),

where
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{K}:=(\pi_{1,K},\ldots,\pi_{K,K})
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{K}:=(\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1,K}^{Y}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1,K}^{X}\},\ldots,\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{K,K}^{Y}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{K,K}^{X}\})
, for
K\in\mathbb{N}
.
Finally, following the
dynamic mixture of finite mixtures
approach
(
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2021
)
, we set the concentration parameters in Eq. (
4
) as

\gamma_{1}=\ldots=\gamma_{K}=\frac{\gamma}{K},

(6)

placing Snedecor’s F distribution as a hyper-prior distribution on
\gamma

\gamma\sim\mathrm{F}(\nu_{l},\nu_{r}),

(7)

and setting
\nu_{\ell}=6
and
\nu_{r}=3
.

2.2.2
The priors for the linear regression mixture component

The linear regression component-specific parameters are assumed to be independent conditional on
K
. Specifically, we consider the following prior design for subgroups indexed by
k=1,\ldots,K
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}|K

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}),

(8)

\displaystyle\beta_{kj}\mid\sigma^{2}_{k},\lambda_{k},K

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{DE}\Bigg(0,\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\sigma_{k}}\Bigg),

(9)

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}^{2}|K

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{IG}(a,b),

(10)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}|K

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{half-Cauchy}(0,1),

(11)

for
j=1\ldots,p
, where
\mathrm{DE}(\chi,\rho)
in (
9
) denotes the double exponential distribution with location
\chi
and rate
\rho
, and
\mathrm{IG}(\chi,\rho)
in (
10
) the inverse-gamma distribution with shape
\chi
and rate
\rho
. The prior variance for the intercepts can be set arbitrarily large (e.g.,
\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}=10^{3}
) in a typical non-informative fashion and, similarly, the scale and rate parameters of the inverse-gamma prior can be set equal to a small value (e.g.,
a=b=10^{-3}
).

The double-exponential prior for the regression coefficients is according to the Bayesian lasso formulation of
Park and Casella 2008
; specifically, we have that

p(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}\mid\sigma^{2}_{k},\lambda_{k},K)=\prod_{j=1}^{p}p(\beta_{kj}\mid\sigma^{2}_{k},\lambda_{k})=\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{p}\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2\sigma_{k}}\exp\Bigg(-\lambda_{k}\displaystyle\frac{|\beta_{kj}|}{\sigma_{k}}\Bigg).

(12)

As discussed in
Park and Casella 2008
the prior in Eq. (
12
) can be written as a scale mixture of normal distributions with an exponential mixing density, leading to an augmented “global-local” shrinkage-prior representation of the following form

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}\mid\sigma^{2}_{k},\tau^{2}_{k1},\ldots,\tau^{2}_{kp},K)

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{N}_{p}\big(\mathbf{0}_{p},\sigma^{2}_{k}\mathbf{T}_{k}\big),\mbox{~with~}\mathbf{T}_{k}=\mathrm{diag}(\tau^{2}_{k1},\ldots,\tau^{2}_{kp}),

(13)

\displaystyle\tau^{2}_{kj}|K

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda_{k}^{2}/2),~\mbox{for}~j=1,\ldots,p.

(14)

In Equation (
14
), the general notation
\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)
refers to the exponential distribution with rate
\lambda>0
. The above augmentation facilitates the use of Gibbs sampling used in the proposed MCMC algorithm presented in Appendix A.

The prior formulation for the regression part of the model is completed by the assignment of a half-Cauchy prior on the group-specific penalty parameters
\lambda_{k}
, specifically, we have that

p(\lambda_{k}|K)=\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi(1+\lambda_{k}^{2})}.

(15)

The use of half-Cauchy priors for scale parameters of this type is justified in the relevant work of
Polson and Scott 2012
; namely, based on the argument that the prior density does not vanish at the origin (as it would be the case with an inverse-gamma prior). As previously, for the purposes of the Gibbs sampler we will make use of an augmented representation of the prior in (
15
), formulating it as follows

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}|\delta_{k},K

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{half-Normal}(0,\delta_{k}^{-1}),

(16)

\displaystyle\delta_{k}|K

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(1/2,1/2),

(17)

so that
p(\lambda_{k}|\delta_{k},K)~\propto~\delta_{k}^{1/2}\exp\big(-\delta_{k}\lambda_{k}^{2}/2\big)
and
p(\delta_{k}|K)~\propto~\delta_{k}^{1/2-1}\exp(-\delta_{k}/2)
.

2.2.3
The priors for the Gaussian covariate mixture component

For the second component of the model that takes into account the distribution of covariates we begin by specifying multivariate normal priors for the group-specific means conditional on the group-specific covariances, so that

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k},K\sim\mathrm{N}_{p}(\mathbf{m}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}),

(18)

indepentently for
k=1,\ldots,K
. Above, without loss of generality, we can assume that
\mathbf{m}_{0}=\mathbf{0}_{p}
. For the group-specific precision matrices
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}=\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}
, we adopt the Bayesian graphical lasso approach
(
Wang 2012
)
which entails using independent double-exponential distributions for the off-diagonal terms and independent exponential distributions for the elements of the main diagonal; specifically, we have that

\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}:p(\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}\mid\psi_{k},K)\propto\bigg[\prod_{j=1}^{p}f_{\mathrm{Exp}}(\omega_{kjj}\mid\psi_{k}/2)\prod_{j<l,l=2}^{p}f_{\mathrm{DE}}(\omega_{kjl}\mid 0,\psi_{k})\bigg]\mathbbm{1}_{(\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}\in M^{+})},

(19)

where
\psi_{k}>0
is the corresponding graphical lasso penalty,
\mathbbm{1}_{(\cdot)}
is the indicator function and
M^{+}
is the space of positive definite matrices. Above,
f_{\mathrm{Exp}}(\cdot\mid q)
and
f_{\mathrm{DE}}(\cdot\mid 0,q)
denote the respective densities of the exponential distribution with rate
q
and of the zero-mean double exponential distribution with rate
q
. Here again, in order to facilitate posterior simulation via Gibbs sampling, instead of Eq. (
19
), one can use the following expansion

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}\mid\psi_{k},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k},K)

\displaystyle~\propto~\bigg[\prod_{j=1}^{p}f_{\mathrm{Exp}}(\omega_{kjj}\mid\psi_{k}/2)\prod_{j<l,l=2}^{p}f_{\mathrm{N}_{1}}(\omega_{kjl}\mid 0,\phi_{kjl})\bigg]\mathbbm{1}_{(\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}\in M^{+})},

(20)

\displaystyle\phi_{kjl}|K

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\psi_{k}^{2}/2),

(21)

for
j<l
and
l=2,\ldots,p
.
Finally, the prior formulation for this part of the model is completed via the specification of a gamma hyper-prior for
\psi_{k}
; namely,

\psi_{k}|K\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(r,s),

(22)

independent fof
k=1,\ldots,K
, with default values for shape and rate being
r=1
and
s=0.01
, respectively
(
Wang 2012
)
.

3
Posterior inference

3.1
MCMC sampling for fixed number of components

Naturally, the resulting posterior distribution is intractable. However, under the prior specifications in Section
2.2
and, especially, by making use of the augmentation schemes therein, it is possible to fully derive all the full conditional distributions required for Gibbs sampling.

Specifically, the full conditional distribution of the latent indicators
z_{ik}
is a multinomial distribution with success probabilities
p_{ik}~\propto~f_{\mathrm{N}_{1}}(y_{i}\mid\alpha_{k}+\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k},\sigma^{2}_{k})f_{\mathrm{N}_{p}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})\pi_{k}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
k=1,\ldots,K
. For the linear regression component of the mixture we have
\alpha_{k}\mid\cdot\sim\mathrm{N}\big(n_{k}^{-1}w_{k}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k})^{\top}\mathbf{z}_{k},n_{k}^{-1}w_{k}\sigma_{k}^{2}\big)
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}\mid\cdot\sim\mathrm{N}_{p}\big(\mathbf{A}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{k}(\mathbf{y}-\alpha_{k}\mathbf{1}_{n},\sigma_{k}^{2}\mathbf{A}_{k}^{-1})\big)
and
\sigma_{k}^{2}\mid\cdot\sim\mathrm{IG}\big(a+\frac{1}{2}(n_{k}+p),b+\frac{1}{2}[\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{k}\mathbf{e}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{T}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}]\big)
, where
n_{k}=\sum_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{(z_{ik}=1)}
,
\mathbf{z}_{k}=(z_{1k},\ldots,z_{nk})^{\top}
,
\mathbf{Z}_{k}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{z}_{k})
,
w_{k}=\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}/(\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}+n_{k}^{-1}\sigma_{k}^{2})
,
\mathbf{A}_{k}=(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{k}\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{T}_{k}^{-1})
and

\mathbf{e}_{k}=\mathbf{y}-\alpha_{k}\mathbf{1}_{n}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}
. Furthermore, from the augmentation schemes presented in Eqs. (
13
), (
14
), (
16
) and (
17
) we obtain that
1/\tau^{2}_{kj}\mid\cdot\sim\mathrm{InvGaussian}\big(\sigma_{k}\lambda_{k}\beta_{kj}^{-1},\lambda_{k}^{2}\big)
, for
j=1,\ldots,p
, as well as
\lambda_{k}^{2}\mid\cdot\sim\mathrm{Gamma}\big(p+0.5,0.5(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\tau_{kj}^{2}+\delta_{k})\big)
and, finally,
\delta_{k}\mid\cdot\sim\mathrm{Gamma}\big(1,0.5(\lambda_{k}^{2}+1)\big)
.

Continuing, for the multivariate Gaussian component of the mixture model we initially get that
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}\mid\cdot\sim\mathrm{N}_{p}\big((n_{k}+1)^{-1}(\mathbf{m}_{0}+\sum_{i}z_{ik}\mathbf{x}_{i}),(n_{k}+1)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}\big)
. Sampling
\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}
from their full conditionals directly, based on the priors in (
20
) and (
21
), is not possible; however, as shown in
Wang 2012
it is possible to use a block Gibbs sampling scheme, based on reparametrizations, which produces row/column-wise updates of
\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}
. The scheme is described and discussed in detail in
Wang 2012
; here we will provide a brief description. First for each row and column
j=1,\ldots,p
the following partitions are defined

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}^{(j)}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,\smallsetminus j)}&\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,j)}\\
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,j)^{\top}}&\omega_{kjj}\end{bmatrix},~\mathbf{S}_{k}^{(j)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{S}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,\smallsetminus j)}&\mathbf{s}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,j)}\\
\mathbf{s}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,j)^{\top}}&s_{kjj}\end{bmatrix}~,\mathbf{\Phi}_{k}^{(j)}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,\smallsetminus j)}&\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,j)}\\
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,j)^{\top}}&0\end{bmatrix},

(23)

where for a
n\times n
symmetric matrix
\mathbf{Q}
,
\mathbf{Q}^{(\smallsetminus i,\smallsetminus i)}
is the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
matrix without the
i
-th row/column of
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{q}^{(\smallsetminus i,i)}
is the
i
-th column of
\mathbf{Q}
without element
q_{ii}
. In addition, we have
\mathbf{S}_{k}=\sum_{i}z_{ik}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})^{\top}+(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}-\mathbf{m}_{0})(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}-\mathbf{m}_{0})^{\top}
, while
\mathbf{\Phi}_{k}
is a symmetric matrix with zeros in the main diagonal and the elements of
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}
filling the upper diagonal entries. Having defined the above partitions we sample
\eta_{k1}\mid\cdot\sim\mathrm{Gamma}\big(0.5(n_{k}+1),0.5(s_{kjj}+\psi_{k})\big)
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{k2}\mid\cdot\sim\mathrm{N}_{p-1}\big(-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{s}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,j)},\mathbf{C}\big)
, where
\mathbf{C}=\big((s_{kjj}+\psi_{k})\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,\smallsetminus j)^{-1}}+\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,j)})^{-1}\big)
, and then set
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,j)}=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{k2}
and
\omega_{kjj}=\eta_{k1}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{k2}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,\smallsetminus j)^{-1}}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{k2}
for
j=1,\ldots,p
. Subsequently, for
j<l~(l=2,\ldots,p),
we draw
u_{kjl}\sim\mathrm{InvGaussian}\big(\omega_{kjl}^{-1}\psi_{k},\psi_{k}^{2}\big)
and set
\phi_{kjl}=u_{kjl}^{-1}
.
The graphical lasso penalty is updated from its corresponding full conditional, which is
\psi_{k}\mid\cdot\sim\mathrm{Gamma}\big(r+0.5p(p+1),s+0.5\|\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}\|_{1}\big)
, where
\|\cdot\|_{1}
denotes the
\ell_{1}
norm.

Finally, the full conditional distribution of mixing proportions is simply
\boldsymbol{\pi}|({\boldsymbol{z}},K,\gamma)\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\gamma_{1}+n_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{K}+n_{K})
.

3.2
Sampling an unknown number of components

In the case of an unknown number of components, the MCMC sampler should also update
K
. Under the prior in Equation (
5
), the full conditional distribution of the number of components is given by
(
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2021
)

p(K|{\boldsymbol{z}},\gamma_{K})~\propto~p(K)\frac{K!}{(K-K_{+})!}\frac{\Gamma(\gamma_{K}K)}{\Gamma(n+\gamma_{K}K)\Gamma(\gamma_{K})^{K_{+}}}\prod_{j:n_{j}>0}\Gamma(n_{j}+\gamma_{K}),

(24)

for
K=K_{+},K_{+}+1,\ldots
where
K_{+}
denotes the number of non-empty components (clusters) induced by
{\boldsymbol{z}}
.
The hyperparameter
\gamma
is updated using a Metropolis-Hastings step in order to sample from the corresponding conditional distribution, that is,

p(\gamma|{\boldsymbol{z}},K,\nu_{\ell},\nu_{r})~\propto~p(\gamma)\frac{\gamma^{K_{+}}\Gamma(\gamma)}{\Gamma(n+\gamma)}\prod_{k=1}^{K_{+}}\frac{n_{k}+\gamma/K}{\Gamma(1+\gamma/K)},

(25)

as described in
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2021
.
The main steps of the proposed telescoping sampler are summarized in the form of a pseudocode in Section A of the Appendix.

3.3
Identifiability issues

Mixtures of distributions impose various identifiability concerns, including
trivial
and
generic
identifiability
(
Frühwirth-Schnatter 2006
)
. The term trivial identifiability refers to problems related to the invariance of the likelihood with respect to permutations of the parameters; i.e., the label-switching problem
(
Redner and Walker 1984
)
. This can also include cases with empty components and the presence of components with identical parameters
(
Grün and Leisch 2008
)
. We deal with label switching applying the Equivalence Classes Representatives (ECR) algorithm
(
Papastamoulis and Iliopoulos 2010
;
Papastamoulis 2016
)
in order to post-process the generated MCMC samples and derive meaningful estimates of the marginal posterior distributions. The ECR algorithm uses the generated allocation variables of the mixture model and
by default
acts to the occupied mixture components in the MCMC output, conditional on the number of components.

Generic identifiability refers to the question of what can be estimated consistently. This issue has been addressed in
Hennig 2000
for mixtures of Gaussian linear regressions with fixed or random covariates. In the case of random covariates, the main result requires a check of a coverage condition in order to ensure generic identifiability; specifically, that the distribution of the covariates does not assign positive probability to any
(p-1)
-dimensional hyper-plane (see Theorem 3.2 in
Hennig 2000
), where
p
denotes the number of covariates. In general, caution is needed when checking the validity of this condition in cases where discrete covariates are considered. However, in our setup, this condition is satisfied as the conditional distribution of covariates within each cluster is a
p
-dimensional multivariate normal distributions.

3.4
Variable selection

Following
Papastamoulis and Ntzoufras 2022
, we assess variable selection ex-post by estimating simultaneous credible regions of the regression coefficients. We apply this procedure cluster-wise, after post-processing the retained MCMC sample in order to undo label-switching. In case where a zero is not contained in the simultaneous credible region for at least one cluster, the corresponding variable is deemed as “significant”. On the other hand, if a zero is contained in the simultaneous credible region for all clusters, the corresponding variable is deemed as “non-significant”. This scheme is not a strictly Bayesian variable selection approach; however, it is straightforward to apply within our model and leads to reliable results according to the simulation studies presented next.

We denote by
I_{k}=I_{k1}\times\cdots\times I_{kp}
the (estimated)
100(1-\alpha)\%
simultaneous credible region of
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}=(\beta_{k1},\ldots,\beta_{kp})
, for cluster
k=1,\ldots,K
. We them define the binary vector
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}_{k}=(\hat{\xi}_{k1},\ldots,\hat{\xi}_{kp})
, where

\hat{\xi}_{kj}=\begin{cases}1,&\mbox{if}\quad 0\notin I_{kj}\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

The subset
S_{k}=\{j=1,\ldots,p:\hat{\xi}_{kj}=1\}
contains the variables deemed as significant for cluster
k
. Finally, define the binary vector
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}=(\hat{\xi}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\xi}_{p})
where

\hat{\xi}_{j}=1-\prod_{k=1}^{K}(1-\hat{\xi}_{kj})

(26)

for
j=1,\ldots,p
. The subset
S=\{j=1,\ldots,p:\hat{\xi}_{j}=1\}
contains the variables deemed as significant, to at least one cluster.

Applications

In this section we consider applications of the proposed methodology, initially, detailing technical aspects in Section
4.1
, followed by simulation studies and comparisons to other existing related methods in Section
4.2
. A real-world data application is presented in Section
4.3
.

4.1
Technical details

Initialization

The MCMC sampler for the BGCWM model as well as the EM algorithm in
Perrakis et al. 2023
(denoted as RJM in what follows) are initialized by the same procedure. For this purpose, the initial values of latent allocation variables are set equal to the classification obtained by estimating a finite mixture of normal linear regression models as implemented in the
flexmix
package
(
Leisch 2004
;
Grün and Leisch 2007
;
Grün and Leisch 2008
)
.

Specifically, for the case of fixed number of components, the BGCWM sampler was initialized by setting the allocation variables to the resulting single best clustering arising by running
flexmix
under 30 randomly chosen starting values. For the case of the overfitting mixture model or the telescoping sampler, we repeated the same initialization procedure for 10 randomly selected numbers of components in the set
6\leqslant K\leqslant 15
. The final starting value is chosen as the one which corresponds to the highest log-posterior density
p(K,\gamma,\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k},\pi_{k},\sigma^{2}_{k},\lambda_{k},\psi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}|{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{X}})
(up to a normalizing constant) after an additional single iteration of the telescoping sampler.

MCMC details and post-processing

In our simulations in Section
4.2
, the total number of MCMC iterations was set equal to
T=11000
. The first
1000
iterations were discarded as burn-in period. Every 10th iteration was retained for inference. A larger number of MCMC iterations was considered in the real data application of Section
4.3
. The retained MCMC samples were post-processed according to the Equivalence Classes Representatives (ECR) algorithm
(
Papastamoulis and Iliopoulos 2010
;
Papastamoulis 2016
)
in order to deal with possible label switching issues. We have also used the same method in order to suitably permute the final point estimates of the EM algorithm, and make direct comparisons against the true values as well as the between methods.

4.2
Simulations

We generated synthetic datasets considering that the number of clusters varies in the set
K\in\{2,3,4\}
, with the number of explanatory variables varying in the set

. In order to incorporate various cluster sizes, the mixing proportions were set equal to
\pi_{k}~\propto~k
for

\beta_{kj}

5
Discussion

In this study we proposed a novel Bayesian mixture of normal linear regression models with random Gaussian covariates. The introduced model exploits shrinkage prior distributions both in the coefficients of the linear predictor within each cluster via the Bayesian lasso prior
(
Park and Casella 2008
)
, as well as the covariance of the random covariates via the Bayesian graphical lasso
(
Wang 2012
)
. We note that the proposed model is modular in nature, in the sense that different shrinkage methods can be applied as future extentions and variations; for instance, a combination of horseshoe priors for the regression coefficients
(
Carvalho et al. 2010
)
and the covariances of the predictors
(
Li et al. 2019
)
.

The flexibility provided by generalized mixtures of finite mixtures allows us to treat the number of mixture components as random and draw fully Bayesian inference using the telescoping sampler of
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2021
. We also discussed alternative approaches such as the overfitting mixture models
(
Rousseau and Mengersen 2011
)
as well as estimating separate models for each distinct value of the number of components and selecting one according to information criteria.

Naturally, our model is invariant to label switching, therefore posterior inference conditional on a given value of number of clusters is meaningful only after the MCMC output is suitably post-processed. The Equivalence Classes Representatives (ECR) algorithm
(
Papastamoulis and Iliopoulos 2010
;
Papastamoulis 2016
)
was used for this purpose. After this step, we followed the approach of
Papastamoulis and Ntzoufras 2022
in order to select relevant variables within each cluster, by computing simultaneous credible intervals. Evidently, one could fully address the issue of variable selection, for example by exploiting stochastic search variable selection
(
George and McCulloch 1995
;
Ntzoufras et al. 2000
;
Dellaportas et al. 2002
)
approaches. We plan to explore this issue in the future.

In its current form the proposed model is designed for continuous covariates. Therefore, an interesting further direction of future research relates to mixed sets of data tpyes. Obviously, it would be quite challenging to efficiently describe covariance patterns between discrete and continuous data in such settings. In addition, the generalization to other types of response variables, e.g. categorical or count responses as considered in
Ingrassia et al. 2015a
;
Punzo and Ingrassia 2015
and/or multivariate responses, as considered in
Murphy and Murphy 2020
, is another interesting future direction worth of exploration.

Finally, one could also consider the question of improving the mixing of the MCMC sampling. For instance, by considering parallel tempering schemes such as Metropolis-coupled MCMC.

R

https://github.com/mqbssppe/BGCWM
, together with scripts that exemplify its usage in simulated as well as our real datasets. The authors are also planning to release the code in the form of an
R
package.

A
Steps of the telescoping sampler

The steps of the telescoping sampler for the introduced BGCWM model is summarized in the form of a pseudo-code below, where
T
denotes the total number of MCMC iterations.

Algorithm:
Telescoping sampler for the BGCWM model

Iterate the following steps for
t=1,\ldots,T
:

0.

Obtain a set of starting values
\mathbf{z}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
and for all component-specific parameters and hyper-parameters.

1.

For
i=1,\ldots,n
, draw
\mathbf{z}_{i}\sim\mathrm{Multinomial}(1,p_{i1},\ldots,p_{iK})
with success probabilities

p_{ik}=\frac{\pi_{k}f_{\mathrm{N}_{1}}(y_{i}\mid\alpha_{k}+\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k},\sigma^{2}_{k})f_{\mathrm{N}_{p}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})}{\sum_{l=1}^{K}\pi_{l}f_{\mathrm{N}_{1}}(y_{i}\mid\alpha_{l}+\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{l},\sigma^{2}_{l})f_{\mathrm{N}_{p}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{l},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{l})}.

and permute the labels
\{1,\ldots,K\}
so that the first
K_{+}
mixture components are not empty, where
K_{+}
denotes the number of non-empty components.

2.

For steps (2.i)–(2.x) apply for
k=1,\ldots,K
:

2.i

Set
n_{k}=\sum_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{(z_{ik}=1)}
,
\mathbf{z}_{k}=(z_{1k},\ldots,z_{nk})^{\top}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{k}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{z}_{k})
.

2.ii

Draw
\alpha_{k}\sim\mathrm{N}\big(n_{k}^{-1}w_{k}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k})^{\top}\mathbf{z}_{k},n_{k}^{-1}w_{k}\sigma_{k}^{2}\big)
, where
w_{k}=\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}/(\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}+n_{k}^{-1}\sigma_{k}^{2})
.

2.iii

Draw
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}\sim\mathrm{N}_{p}\big(\mathbf{A}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{k}(\mathbf{y}-\alpha_{k}\mathbf{1}_{n},\sigma_{k}^{2}\mathbf{A}_{k}^{-1})\big)
, where
\mathbf{A}_{k}=(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{k}\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{T}_{k}^{-1})
and
\mathbf{T}_{k}=\mathrm{diag}(\tau_{k1}^{2},\ldots,\tau_{kp}^{2})
.

2.iv

Draw
\sigma_{k}^{2}\sim\mathrm{IG}\big(a+\frac{1}{2}(n_{k}+p),b+\frac{1}{2}[\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{k}\mathbf{e}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{T}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}]\big)
, where
\mathbf{e}_{k}=(\mathbf{y}-\alpha_{k}\mathbf{1}_{n}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k})
.

2.v

For
j=1,\ldots p
, draw
t_{kj}\sim\mathrm{InvGaussian}\big(\sigma_{k}\lambda_{k}\beta_{kj}^{-1},\lambda_{k}^{2}\big)
and set
\tau_{kj}^{2}=t_{kj}^{-1}
.

2.vi

Draw
\ell_{k}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}\big(p+0.5,0.5(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\tau_{kj}^{2}+\delta_{k})\big)
and set
\lambda_{k}=\sqrt{\ell_{k}}
.

2.vii

Draw
\delta_{k}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}\big(1,0.5(\lambda_{k}^{2}+1)\big)
.

2.h

Draw
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}\sim\mathrm{N}_{p}\big((n_{k}+1)^{-1}(\mathbf{m}_{0}+\sum_{i}z_{ik}\mathbf{x}_{i}),(n_{k}+1)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}\big)
.

2.ix

Set
\mathbf{S}_{k}=\sum_{i}z_{ik}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})^{\top}+(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}-\mathbf{m}_{0})(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}-\mathbf{m}_{0})^{\top}
.

2.x

For
j=1,\ldots,p
:

2.x.i

Form the partitioned matrices
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k}^{(j)},\boldsymbol{S}_{k}^{(j)},\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{k}^{(j)}
as defined in Eq. (
23
).

2.x.ii

Draw
\eta_{k1}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}\big(0.5(n_{k}+1),0.5(s_{kjj}+\psi_{k})\big)
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{k2}\sim\mathrm{N}_{p-1}\big(-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{s}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,j)},\mathbf{C}\big)
, where
\mathbf{C}=\big((s_{kjj}+\psi_{k})\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,\smallsetminus j)^{-1}}+\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,j)})^{-1}\big)
.

2.x.iii

Set
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,j)}=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{k2}
and
\omega_{kjj}=\eta_{k1}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{k2}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}^{(\smallsetminus j,\smallsetminus j)^{-1}}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{k2}
.

3.

For
j<l~(l=2,\ldots,p),
draw
u_{kjl}\sim\mathrm{InvGaussian}\big(\omega_{kjl}^{-1}\psi_{k},\psi_{k}^{2}\big)
, set
\phi_{kjl}=u_{kjl}^{-1}
.

4.

Draw
\psi_{k}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}\big(r+0.5p(p+1),s+0.5\|\mathbf{\Omega}_{k}\|_{1}\big)
.

5.

Draw
\boldsymbol{\pi}|({\boldsymbol{z}},\gamma,K)\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\gamma_{1}+n_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{K}+n_{K})
, where
\gamma_{k}=\gamma/K
,
k=1,\ldots,K
.

6.

Draw
K|({\boldsymbol{z}},\gamma)
from Eq. (
24
)

7.

Draw
\gamma|({\boldsymbol{z}},K,\nu_{\ell},\nu_{r})
from Eq. (
25
).

Fixed parameters:
\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}=10^{3},a=b=10^{-2},\mathbf{m}_{0}=\mathbf{0}_{p},r=1,s=10^{-2}
,
\nu_{\ell}=6
,
\nu_{r}=3
,
a_{\lambda}=1
,
a_{\pi}=4
,
b_{\pi}=3
.

B
Simulation details and further results

We generated synthetic data assuming that the real number of clusters is equal to
K=2,3,4
. The sample size is set equal to
n=500
and
n=1000
. In order to incorporate various cluster sizes, we considered that
\pi_{k}\propto k
,
k=1,\ldots,K
, that is, the weight of cluster
k
is proportional to
k
. The number of covariates (excluding the constant term) is set equal to
p=9
or
p=18
. The parameters related to the linear regression mixture components (constant terms, regression coefficients and error variances) were generated according to

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,10^{2})

\displaystyle\beta_{kj}

\displaystyle\sim p_{0}1_{\{0\}}+(1-p_{0})\mathcal{N}(0,3^{2})

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{k}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{E}(1)

independent for
k=1,\ldots,K
and
j=1,\ldots,p
, where
1_{\{0\}}
denotes a degenerate distribution at
0
. In our presented examples we considered cases with
p_{0}=2/3
and
p_{0}=1/3
.
The covariates were generated according to the following 4 scenarios.

1.

Uncorrelated homogeneous covariates across clusters.

The covariates within each cluster are independent with unit variance, i.e.
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}=\mathrm{{\boldsymbol{I}}}_{p}
, for all
k=1,\ldots,K
. In addition,
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}={\boldsymbol{0}}_{p}
where
{\boldsymbol{0}}_{p}=(0,\ldots,0)^{\top}
denotes the zero
p
-dimensional vector. In this case, the covariates
{\boldsymbol{X}}
and latent allocation variables
{\boldsymbol{Z}}
are independent, therefore
{\boldsymbol{X}}
are indistinguishable among clusters.

2.

Correlated homogeneous covariates across clusters.

The covariates within each cluster have common variance, i.e.
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}={\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
, for all
k=1,\ldots,K
. For each simulated dataset, the matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
was generated by a Wishart distribution:
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\sim\mathcal{W}_{p}({\boldsymbol{V}},20)
. A Toeplitz structure was considered for the positive definite scale matrix
{\boldsymbol{V}}
, that is,
{\boldsymbol{V}}_{ij}=(p-|i-j|)/p
, for
i,j=1,\ldots,p
. Furthermore, we set
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}={\boldsymbol{0}}_{p}
. As in Scenario 1,
{\boldsymbol{X}}
are indistinguishable among clusters.

3.

Uncorrelated heterogeneous covariates across clusters.

The covariates within each cluster are independent with unit variance, i.e.
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}=\mathrm{{\boldsymbol{I}}}_{p}
, for all
k=1,\ldots,K
. In addition,
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}={\boldsymbol{0}}_{p}
. The mean for cluster
k
is set equal to

\mu_{kj}=\tilde{\mu}_{\rho_{k}j}

where
\rho=(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{K})
denotes a random permutation of
\{1,\ldots,K\}
and

\tilde{\mu}_{kj}=\begin{cases}0,&\quad k=1\\
2\sin\left\{(j-1)k\pi/p\right\},&\quad k=2\\
2\cos\left\{(j-1)k\pi/p\right\},&\quad k=3\quad(\mbox{if $3\leqslant K\leqslant 4$})\\
4\sin^{2}\left\{(j-1)k\pi/p\right\}-4\cos^{2}\left\{(j-1)k\pi/p\right\},&\quad k=4\quad(\mbox{if $K=4$})\\
\end{cases}

for
j=1,\ldots,p
.

4.

Correlated heterogeneous covariates across clusters.

The covariates were generated using a mixture of Gaussian factor analyzers
(
Papastamoulis 2018
;
Papastamoulis 2020
)
, where each cluster exhibits distinct covariance pattern
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}
and means (see Scenario 3 above) for
k=1,\ldots,K
.

For each case, a unique set of parameters
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
,
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
,
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
,
\Sigma
,
\sigma^{2}
was generated as described above.

Variable selection

Let
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p})
denote the binary vector with
\xi_{j}=1
if and only
\beta_{kj}\neq 0
for at least one cluster
k
,
k=1,\ldots,K
, for each variable
j=1,\ldots,p
. We assess variable selection accuracy by reporting the number of falsely selected or falsely not selected variables with respect to the ground truth, that is,

||\hat{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}||=\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\hat{\xi}_{j}-\xi_{j}|,

where the vector
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}
is defined in Equation (
26
). For each scenario, we have generated multiple datasets with
K=2
or
K=3
clusters. The results are summarized in Figure
B.6
where we are also comparing the proposed method against
MoEClust

(
Murphy and Murphy 2020
;
Murphy and Murphy 2023
)
and the lasso implementation in
RJM
. Overall, we conclude that our method is remarkably better in all scenarios.
