Is MOND necessarily nonlinear?
Mordehai Milgrom

Abstract
The iconic, deep-MOND-limit (DML) relation between acceleration and mass, a ∼ ( M ​ 𝒜 0 ) 1 / 2 / r a\sim(M\mathcal{A}_{0})^{1/2}/r , implies that, in MOND, accelerations cannot be linear in the mass distribution ( 𝒜 0 ≡ G ​ a 0 \mathcal{A}_{0}\equiv Ga_{0} is the DML constant, and a 0 a_{0} the MOND acceleration). This leads to important idiosyncracies of MOND, such as a breakdown of the strong equivalence principle, and the resulting “external-field effect” (EFE). I show that the DML axioms are, in themselves, consistent with a, possibly unique, nonrelativistic, action-based, linear formulation of the DML: For a test particle in the gravitational field of a mass distribution ρ ⁡ ( 𝐫 ) \rho({\bf r}) , the equation of motion is d 4 ​ 𝐫 / d ​ t 4 = ∇ → ​ ψ d^{4}{\bf r}/dt^{4}=\vec{\nabla}\psi , where ψ ( 𝐫 ) = − 𝒜 0 2 ∫ ρ ⁡ ( 𝐫 ′ ) ​ d 3 ​ 𝐫 ′ | 𝐫 − 𝐫 ′ | 2 \psi({\bf r})=-\frac{\mathcal{A}_{0}}{2}\int\frac{\rho({\bf r}^{\prime})d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}}{|{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|^{2}} . This model suffers from important drawbacks, which may make it unacceptable as a basis for a full-fledged MOND theory, which may imply that the answer to the title question is “yes.” The model is unique among MOND theories propounded to date not only in being linear – hence not exhibiting an EFE, for example – but in constituting a modification of both Newtonian inertia and Newtonian gravity. This linear and time-local model inspires and begets several one-parameter families of models. One family employs nonlinear, time-nonlocal kinetic terms, but still linear gravitational-field equations. Other families generalize the DMLs of AQUAL and QUMOND, modifying gravity as well as inertia. All families employ fractional time derivatives and possibly fractional Laplacians. While, at present, I cannot base some acceptable MOND theory on these models – for example, I cannot offer a sensible umbrella theory that interpolates between these DML models and Newtonian dynamics – they are useful in elucidating various fundamental aspects of MOND. For example, they help to understand which predictions follow from only the basic tenets of MOND – so-called primary predictions – and which are secondary, i.e., theory dependent. For example, they demonstrate how the EFE can be strongly theory dependent, not only in its strength but in its very nature. The models may also help show the way to a wider class of MOND theories. Finally, I describe another family of models that are all linear, but non-MONDian, having Newtonian dynamics at one end and the linear DML model at the other.

[

Newtonian gravitational dynamics is linear, since the acceleration at position

is a linear functional of the mass density,
\rho

\frac{d^{2}{\bf r}}{dt^{2}}={\bf g}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}({\bf r})=\int d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\rho({\bf r}^{\prime})\vec{\mathcal{G}}({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime});~~~~~\vec{\mathcal{G}}({\bf r})\equiv-G\frac{{\bf r}}{|{\bf r}|^{3}}.

(1)

In contradistinction, the emblematic deep-MOND relation between the mass distribution that sources gravity, and the acceleration it imparts to bodies,
a\approx(a_{0}g_{\scriptscriptstyle N})^{1/2}
– where

is the Newtonian gravitational acceleration that scales as
M/r

However, a linear relation between accelerations and
\rho

More generally, in the context of nonrelativistic gravity, which I concentrate on here, strict linearity can be taken to mean that the trajectory,
{\bf r}(t)

(\vec{\mathcal{O}}{\bf r})(t)=({\vec{\mathcal{C}}}\rho)({\bf r}),

(2)

\{{\bf r}\}
to give another vector function of time, (
\vec{\mathcal{O}}{\bf r})(t)

{\vec{\mathcal{C}}}
is a vector, linear operator acting on the field
\rho
.

When

\{{\bf r}\}
is the full trajectory, not the local value of
{\bf r}
, and a finite number of its time derivatives, at time

The particle’s mass is assumed not to appear in the EoM so as to ensure the universality of free fall (independence of the trajectory on the mass of the test particle).

We ask whether there is a DML theory that is linear in the more general sense of Eq. (
2

Note that special relativity is not linear even in this more general sense. Its EoM for a particle is of the form

\frac{md(\gamma{\bf v})}{dt}\equiv m\overset{\text{\scriptsize$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mu}\left(\frac{{\bf v}}{c}\right){\bf a}={\bf F}.

(3)

Here,
{\bf F}

\overset{\text{\scriptsize$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mu}

\overset{\text{\scriptsize$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mu}\left(\frac{{\bf v}}{c}\right)\equiv\gamma(1+\gamma^{2}\frac{{\bf v}\otimes{\bf v}}{c^{2}}).

(4)

In the Newtonian limit,
c\rightarrow\infty

\overset{\text{\scriptsize$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mu}
tend to the unit matrix.
For example, for a charge in an electromagnetic field,
{\bf F}
is the Lorentz force, which is linear in the sources (charges and currents).
But the left-hand side is not linear in the trajectory.

Special relativity is, nonetheless, ‘‘quasilinear’’ (to use the standard mathematical nomenclature) in that the left-hand side of the EoM is linear in the highest derivative of the trajectory (the second in this case) and the nonlinearity enters only through the lower derivatives (the first in this case).

\mathcal{E}[{\bf v},{\bf a},{\bf r}^{(3)},...,{\bf r}^{(n-1)}]+\mathcal{F}[{\bf v},{\bf a},{\bf r}^{(3)},...,{\bf r}^{(n-1)}]{\bf r}^{(n)}=({\vec{\mathcal{C}}}\rho)({\bf r})

(5)

\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial{\bf r}^{(k)}\partial{\bf r}^{(k)}}\cdot{\bf r}^{(2k)}.

(6)

We use such a time-local EoM – one that involves a finite number of derivatives – to propagate a trajectory in time from some initial conditions. We know all the lower-than-highest derivatives at any given time step, and we use the equation to calculate the highest one, which then does become a linear functional of the mass distribution. However, MOND theories that modify Newtonian inertia are, generically, time nonlocal. This is implied by a “theorem” I proved in Ref.

This frees us from a crucial requirement in the above theorem, and may allow construction of theories that are local and thus are much more wieldy.

II
Narrow path to linear deep-MOND

The generic nonlinearity of MOND enters through the DML. So, I start by considering whether it is possible to write a linear theory for the pure DML. Thus, consider the form that the linear equation (
2
) has to take, assuming only the MOND basic tenets that concern the DML. Namely, we require that only
G
and
a_{0}
appear in the equations, and that they only appear in the combination
\mathcal{A}_{0}=Ga_{0}
-- a requirement that is equivalent to requiring scale invariance.
4
4

4

This was shown, e.g., in Ref.
[
12
]
, and it requires that we normalize all degrees of freedom so that their scaling dimension agrees with their
[m][\ell][t]
dimensions – which is always possible by multiplying them by powers of
a_{0}
and
G
. For example, The Newtonian gravitational potential, which scales as
MG/r
, transform under scaling as
\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\rightarrow\lambda^{-1}\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
which does not agree with its dimensions being
[\ell]^{2}[t]^{-2}
. We should then work with
\bar{\phi}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}=a_{0}\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
, which does have matching dimensions.
In addition we assume that the action/EoM does not have explicit time dependence – otherwise, we would have to introduce an additional constant with the dimensions of time. We also assume, as a matter of course, translation and rotation invariance.

It is easy to see that under these requirements, linearity is not possible to implement in theories that can be described as pure modified gravity.
In (nonrelativistic) modified-gravity MOND theories of the general class discussed in Ref.
[
13
]
(AQUAL
[
14
]
, QUMOND
[
15
]
, and TRIMOND
[
16

{\bf a}=-\vec{\nabla}\phi
, but the DML potential is determined from
\rho({\bf r})
by some modified DML prescription, not by the Poisson equation. Whatever the prescription is, dimensional arguments show that
\vec{\nabla}\phi
cannot be a linear functional of
\rho
, but that it has to scale as
\rho^{1/2}
: To see this, note that, in such theories, the left-hand side of the EoM (i.e.,
{\bf a}
) has dimensions
[\ell][t]^{-2}
. The right-hand side is constructed from
\mathcal{A}_{0}
(of dimensions
[m]^{-1}[\ell]^{4}[t]^{-4}
),
\rho
(of dimensions
[m][\ell]^{-3}
), and
{\bf r}
. If we do not allow explicit time dependence, the only possibility to combine these to get the right dimensions must scale as
(\mathcal{A}_{0}\rho r)^{1/2}
, which scales as
(a_{0}GM/r^{2})^{1/2}\sim(a_{0}a_{\scriptscriptstyle N})^{1/2}
. Hence, nonlinearity follows inevitably.
5
5

5

These expressions are, of course, not algebraic, local relations between the DML acceleration
\vec{\nabla}\phi
and the density; they only give us the dimensional scaling of the former with the latter. For example, in AQUAL,
\vec{\nabla}\phi
is determined from the field equation
\vec{\nabla}\cdot(|\vec{\nabla}\phi|\vec{\nabla}\phi)\propto\mathcal{A}_{0}\rho
, and in QUMOND it is determined from
\Delta\phi\propto a_{0}^{1/2}\vec{\nabla}\cdot(|\vec{\nabla}\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle N}|^{-1/2}\vec{\nabla}\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle N})
, where
\vec{\nabla}\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
is determined from
\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{\nabla}\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle N}\propto G\rho
.

Thus, if we want to seek linear, DML EoMs, we must seek them among so-called modified-inertia theories, those that modify the inertial term and possibly also the gravitational one.

In Ref.
[
10
]
, I showed that a MOND theory (hinging only on
a_{0}
and
G
) derivable from a Lagrangian, that modifies the kinetic Lagrangian, and that has time-translation, space-translation, rotation, and Galilei invariance, and that has the correct MOND and Newtonian limits, must be time-nonlocal. Namely, its Lagrangian cannot be a function of only a finite number of time derivative, or even a limit of a series of such Lagrangians.
However, with all its nonlocality, the Lagrangian of such a theory does have become local, of course, in the Newtonian limit, achieved formally by taking the limit
a_{0}\rightarrow 0
. Likewise, it is possible that such a theory also becomes local in the very deep-MOND limit (DML), achieved formally in the limit
a_{0}\rightarrow\infty
and
G\rightarrow 0
, while
\mathcal{A}_{0}\equiv a_{0}G
remains constant
[
12
]
.
Indeed, the DML model I shall construct is local and modifies inertia (as well as gravity). So the theorem will tell us that it does not have a local umbrella theory that interpolates it with Newtonian dynamics. If there is one it will be nonlocal.

Thus, we want to construct a DML EoM for the trajectory
{\bf r}(t)
of a test particle of mass
m
under the gravitational influence of a (bounded) mass distribution
\rho({\bf r})
.
Among the system attributes and constants only
\rho
,
m
, and
\mathcal{A}_{0}
have mass dimensions (
m^{-1}
in
\mathcal{A}_{0}
). Since we want to retain the universality of free fall,
m
cannot appear.
So, Eq. (
2

(\vec{\mathcal{O}}{\bf r})(t)=\mathcal{A}_{0}({\vec{\mathcal{G}}}\rho)({\bf r}),

(7)

where
{\vec{\mathcal{G}}}
is a linear, vector operator with only time and length dimensions, and
\mathcal{A}_{0}
not appearing anywhere else.
If we apply the model to stationary density distributions, and we do not have dimensioned constants to use in the construction,
{\vec{\mathcal{G}}}
must not have time dimensions.
Then, the right-hand side, and hence the left-hand one, has time dimensions
[t]^{-4}
(from
\mathcal{A}_{0}

Since we want to maintain the linearity of the left-hand side in
{\bf r}
, it must have dimensions of
[\ell][t]^{-4}

{\vec{\mathcal{G}}}
is dimensionless.

Time-translation invariance, and the absence at our disposal of a constant with the dimensions of time, dictate that

\vec{\mathcal{O}}{\bf r}\propto d^{4}/dt^{4}
.
If we further impose translational and rotational invariance, a simple choice of
{\vec{\mathcal{G}}}
gives the EoM

\frac{d^{4}{\bf r}}{dt^{4}}=\mathcal{A}_{0}\int d^{3}r^{\prime}\frac{\rho({\bf r}^{\prime})({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})}{|{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|^{4}}=\vec{\nabla}\psi({\bf r}),~~~~~~~~\psi({\bf r})=-\frac{\mathcal{A}_{0}}{2}\int d^{3}r^{\prime}\frac{\rho({\bf r}^{\prime})}{|{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|^{2}}.

(8)

This is the result arrived at, and discussed briefly in Ref.
[
12
]

But it would be interesting to explore this issue further.

The sole appearance of
\mathcal{A}_{0}
ensures that for any physical trajectory

While this follows straightforwardly from the specific form of the EoM, this result holds in any gravitational theory, where the central mass determines the dynamics, and which involves only
\mathcal{A}_{0}

For circular trajectories, it follows that the circular speed,
V_{c}

M

V_{c}\propto(M\mathcal{A}_{0})^{1/4}

The normalization of the right-hand side of the EoM is chosen according to the standard normalization of

For a system of point masses,
m_{p}

{\bf r}_{p}(t)
– which I will consider hereafter – the EoM (

\frac{d^{4}{\bf r}_{p}}{dt^{4}}=\mathcal{A}_{0}\sum_{q\not=p}\frac{m_{q}({\bf r}_{p}-{\bf r}_{q})}{|{\bf r}_{p}-{\bf r}_{q}|^{4}}=\vec{\nabla}\psi({\bf r}_{p}),

(9)

\psi({\bf r})=-\frac{\mathcal{A}_{0}}{2}\sum_{q\not=p}\frac{m_{q}}{|{\bf r}-{\bf r}_{q}|^{2}}.

(10)

This model can be derived from the action
I\propto\int dt~L({\bf r}_{p},\ddot{\bf r}_{p})
, with the Lagrangian

L=\sum_{p}\frac{1}{2}m_{p}\ddot{\bf r}_{p}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{A}_{0}\sum_{q<p}\frac{m_{p}m_{q}}{|{\bf r}_{p}-{\bf r}_{q}|^{2}}.

(11)

In the formulation above,
\psi
is only an auxiliary field; it is not treated as a system degree of freedom, with its own “free” term in the Lagrangian.
As is done in going from the Newtonian expression of the potential in terms of the density to the Poissonian formulation of Newtonian dynamics, here too, we can introduce
\psi
as a gravitational degree of freedom and start from the Lagrangian (for the continuum description)

L=\int d^{3}r\{\mathcal{A}_{0}\rho[\frac{1}{2}(\ddot{\bf r})^{2}-\psi]+\mathcal{F}(\psi)\}.

(12)

\mathcal{F}(\psi)
is a functional of
\psi

\psi
has dimensions of acceleration squared, and its scaling dimension must be
-2
(namely, under scaling
\psi\rightarrow\lambda^{-2}\psi
). Varying over the matter position degree of freedom, we get

\frac{d^{4}{\bf r}_{p}}{dt^{4}}=\vec{\nabla}\psi({\bf r}_{p}),

(13)

8

We want to take
\mathcal{F}

In the present context we also want
\mathcal{F}

\psi

\mathcal{F}(\psi)

\psi^{2}/r

\psi

\mathcal{F}

\psi\rightarrow\psi+{\rm const.}

The above very restricted form, forced on us by the DML basic tenets, and the symmetry requirements, involves a combined modification of Newtonian gravity (since the Newtonian gravitational potential does not play any role), and of Newtonian inertia (as Newton’s second law does not apply).

It is the fact that
\mathcal{A}_{0}
has mixed length and time dimensions that forces us to modify both the inertial and the gravitational terms in order to get a theory that is both linear and scale-invariant.

If we introduce, instead, a new length constant,
\ell_{0}
, for example, we can construct a scale-invariant, linear EoM by modifying only the gravitational part. This is done, e.g., in Ref.
[
17
]
, where one replaces
\vec{\mathcal{G}}
in the right-hand side of the Newtonian EoM (

\vec{\mathcal{G}}=-G{\bf r}/r^{2}\ell_{0}
, or, equivalently, replace the Poisson equation for the gravitational potential by one involving a fractional Laplacian:
\Delta\phi\propto\rho~\Rightarrow~\ell_{0}\Delta^{[3/2]}\phi\propto\rho
.
Such models predict asymptotically flat rotation curves – which follows only from the scale invariance. However, they predict the wrong mass asymptotic speed relation; namely, they predict
V^{2}_{\infty}\propto MG/\ell_{0}
, instead of the observed
V^{4}_{\infty}\propto M
. To repair this, and reproduce the prediction of MOND, the author of Ref.
[
17
]
and others who attempt to reproduce MOND by introducing a scale length, make the ansatz that
\ell_{0}
is not a constant of the theory, but instead
\ell_{0}\propto r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}=(MG/a_{0})^{1/2}
, where
r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
is the MOND radius of the total mass of the body in question. I think that this is unacceptable if only because “the total mass” is not defined since every system (e.g., a galaxy) is part of larger and larger systems. Which “total mass” is then to enter the gravitational field at some specific point in the Universe?

Similarly, introducing a frequency constant
\omega_{0}
enables us to construct a linear, scale-invariant theory by modifying only the inertial term.

III
Some generic predictions of the linear DML model and comparison with other MOND theories

Here I consider the linear, DML model arrived at in Sec.
II
in some more detail. I discuss some of its consequences, distinguishing primary DML predictions from secondary ones, and comparing with predictions of other MOND theories. Some MOND predictions concern the transition from the DML to the Newtonian domain – such as the onset of the mass discrepancies always occurring at accelerations around
a_{0}
. Clearly, such predictions are beyond the power of this model, which is a strict DML one, without transition to the Newtonian regime.
Predictions that can be studied in the model concern, e.g., asymptotic behavior of rotation curves, the mass-asymptotic-speed relation, the relation between the velocity dispersion in a pressure-supported system and the system’s total mass, the center-of-mass motion of a composite system, and whether it is dictated by the kinematics of that center, or is affected by the kinematics of the constituents, and the workings of an external-field effect (EFE).

Note that the general, two-parameter scaling of the DML holds here:
If
{\bf r}_{p}(t)
is a system history for masses
m_{p}
, then
\lambda{\bf r}_{p}(t/\kappa)
[with velocities
(\lambda/\kappa){\bf v}_{p}(t/\kappa)
], is a system history, for masses
(\lambda/\kappa)^{4}m_{p}
for any
\lambda,\kappa>0
. For continuum systems, if
\rho({\bf r},t)
,
{\bf v}({\bf r},t)
is a solution, so is
\lambda\kappa^{-4}\rho({\bf r}/\lambda,t/\kappa)
,
(\lambda/\kappa){\bf v}({\bf r}/\lambda,t/\kappa)
. (This is the scaling that, when applied to
[\ell,~t,~m]

\mathcal{A}_{0}
.)

III.1
Center-of-mass motion

Consider a system of point masses,
m_{p}

{\bf R}_{s}

|{\bf R}_{s}|\gg|{\bf r}_{p}|

s

\frac{d^{4}{\bf r}_{p}}{dt^{4}}=\mathcal{A}_{0}\sum_{q\not=p}\frac{m_{q}({\bf r}_{p}-{\bf r}_{q})}{|{\bf r}_{p}-{\bf r}_{q}|^{4}}+\mathcal{A}_{0}\sum_{s}\frac{\mathcal{M}_{s}({\bf r}_{p}-{\bf R}_{s})}{|{\bf r}_{p}-{\bf R}_{s}|^{4}}.

(19)

Multiplying by

, summing, and replacing the denominator in the second term by
|\bar{\bf R}-{\bf R}_{s}|^{4}
(justified because
|{\bf r}_{p}|,~|\bar{\bf R}|\ll|{\bf R}_{s}|)
, the sum over the first term drops, and we get in the limit

\frac{d^{4}\bar{\bf R}}{dt^{4}}=\mathcal{A}_{0}\sum_{s}\frac{\mathcal{M}_{s}(\bar{\bf R}-{\bf R}_{s})}{|\bar{\bf R}-{\bf R}_{s}|^{4}},

(20)

which justifies the above statement. This, of course is the result of the linearity of the model.

It follows that in a system of well-separated (many-body) subsystems – subsystems much smaller than their separations – we can represent the subsystems by point masses (irrespective of their internal dynamics) if we only want to describe the center-of-mass motions of the subsystems.
We can then write the virial relation for only the relative motions.

Since
\bar{\bf R}=\sum_{p}m_{p}{\bf r}_{p}
, satisfies
d^{4}\bar{\bf R}/dt^{4}=0
for an isolated system, we identify the conserved momentum as

\bar{\bf P}=\pm Md^{3}\bar{\bf R}/dt^{3}.

(21)

This follows also from the general Ostrogradsky, Hamiltonian formalism for higher-derivative theories (
[
26
]
, and see Sec.
III.6
below), where it follows formally that the particle momentum should be identified with
-md^{3}{\bf r}/dt^{3}
.

Multiplying the EoM (
9

\dot{\bf r}_{p}
shows that for a particle in static field
\psi({\bf r})
, the particle attribute
E_{p}=E_{Kp}+m_{p}\psi({\bf r}_{p})
is conserved, with the particle kinetic energy

E_{K}\equiv m[\frac{({\bf a})^{2}}{2}-\dot{\bf a}\cdot{\bf v}]

(22)

(
{\bf v}=\dot{\bf r}
is the velocity, and
{\bf a}=\ddot{\bf r}
is the acceleration).
For an isolated many-body system, the total energy
\sum_{p}[E_{Kp}+m_{p}\psi({\bf r}_{p})]
is conserved.
This definition of the energy also follows from the Hamiltonian formalism.

III.2
Virial relation

Take the scalar product of Eq. (

III.3
Spherical systems

Since
\psi
, which determines particle dynamics, is not the Newtonian
1/r
potential, there are no useful shell theorems to facilitate its determination for spherical configurations.
\vec{\nabla}\psi
does not vanish inside a spherical hollow, and its value outside a spherical mass is not the same as that of a point mass.

For example, for a thin, homogeneous, spherical shell of mass
M
and radius
R
we have (inside and outside)

\psi(r)=-\frac{M\mathcal{A}_{0}}{4Rr}\ln\left|\frac{r+R}{r-R}\right|.

(27)

So,
\psi(r)
depends on
R
for any
r

r\gg R

\psi\rightarrow-M\mathcal{A}_{0}/2r^{2}

We also see that
\psi
(but not
\vec{\nabla}\psi

IV
).

III.4
Rotation curves

Asymptotic flatness and
V_{\infty}^{4}\propto Ma_{0}
(equality for our normalization) follow from the DML basic tenets and are predicted in the model.
More generally, the expression for the rotational speed on circular orbits in the midplane of an axisymmetric and plane-reflection symmetric mass distribution (such as an idealized disk galaxy) is

V^{4}(r)=r^{3}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}.

(28)

This result differs in important ways from the corresponding results in both the modified-gravity and the modified-inertia formulations considered to date. For example, the orbital acceleration,
a=V^{2}/r
, is not a function of the Newtonian acceleration,
g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
, as is the case in pure modified-inertia theories
[
10
]
, where we have
a=(a_{0}g_{\scriptscriptstyle N})^{1/2}
in the DML. And, this result differs from that in a large class of modified-gravity theories, in not having shell theorems.

III.5
The two-body problem

Consider two bodies much smaller than their separation (“point masses”).
As a result of linearity, the two-body problem is reducible to that of a single body in a central field: If
{\bf a}_{12}={\bf a}_{1}-{\bf a}_{2}
is the relative acceleration of the two bodies of masses
m_{1},~m_{2}
, then
\mu\ddot{\bf a}_{12}=m_{1}m_{2}({\bf r}_{1}-{\bf r}_{2})/|{\bf r}_{1}-{\bf r}_{2}|^{4}
, where
\mu=m_{1}m_{2}/(m_{1}+m_{2})
.

If the two bodies are on circular trajectories around each other, their velocity difference can be shown to be given by

V^{4}_{12}=M\mathcal{A}_{0},

(29)

and is independent of the individual masses.
11
11

11

This also follows from the dynamics of the equivalent single-body system, with the expression for the reduced mass being
(m_{1}m_{2})/M
, as in Newtonian dynamics.

This contrasts with the analogous results in modified-gravity theories, such as AQUAL and QUMOND, TRIMOND, and, in fact, the general class of modified gravity theories described in Ref.
[
13
]
:

V^{4}_{12}=\frac{4}{9}\left(\frac{1-\mu_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle 3/2}-\mu_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle 3/2}}{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}\right)^{2}M\mathcal{A}_{0},

(30)

where
\mu_{i}=m_{i}/M
are the fractional masses. This result also differs from that for a class of modified-inertia theories
[
24
]
:

V^{4}_{12}=(\mu_{1}^{1/2}+\mu_{2}^{1/2})^{2}M\mathcal{A}_{0}.

(31)

This demonstrates, once more, that the coefficient of

in the expression for

III.6
Hamiltonian formulation and the Ostrogradsky instability

Ostrogradsky
[
25
]
propounded a Hamiltonian formalism for higher-derivative theories, such as our DML model, which can be very useful in analyzing such theories. A recent detailed account of the formalism is discussed in Ref.
[
26
]
.
For a fourth-order theory, such as the DML model derived from the Lagrangian (
11
), one defines two canonical coordinates and momenta for each particle,
p
on a trajectory
{\bf r}_{p}(t)

[
26
]
(I am omitting the particle index
p
for clarity):

{\bf X}_{1}={\bf r},~~~~~{\bf X}_{2}=\dot{\bf r},~~~~~{\bf P}_{1}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\bf r}}-\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\ddot{\bf r}}\right),~~~~~{\bf P}_{2}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\ddot{\bf r}}.

(32)

In our case

{\bf P}_{1}=-m\dot{\bf a},~~~~~~{\bf P}_{2}=m{\bf a}.

(33)

The Hamiltonian is then defined as

H=\sum_{s=1}^{2}{\bf P}_{s}\cdot{\bf r}^{(s)}-L,

(34)

which can be expressed in terms of the canonical variables as (reinstating the particle index)

H=\psi({\bf X}_{p1})+\sum_{p}\frac{{\bf P}_{p2}^{2}}{2m_{p}}+{\bf P}_{p1}\cdot{\bf X}_{p2}.

(35)

The Hamilton equations then hold

-\frac{\partial H}{\partial{\bf X}_{ps}}=\dot{\bf P}_{ps},~~~~~~\frac{\partial H}{\partial{\bf P}_{ps}}=\dot{\bf X}_{ps},~~~~~s=1,2,

(36)

and encapsulate both the equations of motion and the definition of the canonical variables.

The total
{\bf P}_{1}=\sum_{p}{\bf P}_{p1}
is what we identified in Sec.
III.1
as the conserved, center-of-mass momentum.
As usual, it follows from Hamilton’s equations that
H
is conserved, i.e.,
\dot{H}=0
.

It is well documented (e.g., Ref.
[
26
]
) that such higher-order, local theories suffer from instabilities, owing to the fact that their Hamiltonian is not bounded from below: the last term in expression (
35
) can be negative and arbitrarily large. The theory may then have exploding solutions.

This is a serious fault in a model. However, the model is, in any event, not an acceptable description of nature, since it does not have a sensible continuation to the Newtonian regime. Such a continuation may render the model as only a limit – not achieved in nature – of a nonlocal (and nonlinear) theory, that, in itself, does not suffer from the Ostrogradsky instability.

IV
Limitations and deficiencies of the linear DML model

At present, the linear DML model suffers from some deficiencies that make it unacceptable as a basis for a full fledged MOND theory. However, this model, and its generalizations to nonlinear DML models, presented below in Sec.
V
(and the model families described in Sec.
VI
, in which the linear model is not a member) are quite useful in elucidating some fundamental issues concerning MOND.
Besides the stability issues discussed in Sec.
III.6
, it has the following two drawbacks.

One drawback is the lack of an extension that has a Newtonian limit.
To understand why it is difficult to find an umbrella theory that has both Newtonian dynamics and the above DML model as limits, we note the following: The theorem I proved in Ref.
[
10
]
stands in the way of achieving this with a local theory – i.e., using only derivatives of natural order all through the interpolation. More specifically, in the present case, we would have to interpolate between a
|{\bf r}^{(2)}|^{2}
DML particle kinetic Lagrangian to a
|{\bf r}^{(1)}|^{2}
Newtonian kinetic term. Doing this with only natural derivatives appearing, requires, perforce, introducing a constant with the dimensions of time. This is not permitted by our basic axioms, and, indeed, departs from the assumptions underlying the above-mentioned proof (which posits that
a_{0}
is the only new constant).

One may try, alternatively, to interpolate between the two kinetic Lagrangians, by changing smoothly the order of the time derivative in the kinetic Lagrangian, from
2
to
1
, going through a sequence of fractional time derivatives, and, thus constructing a nonlocal umbrella theory. This is allowed by the above-mentioned proof. In Sec.
VII
, I describe such a sequence, but for reasons explained there, I do not know how to implement, in a sensible way, a continuous interpolation based on such a sequence.

The other potential issue with the linear DML model is the poorer convergence properties, of the expressions for
\psi
, than those of the Newtonian potential.
We saw in Eq. (
27
) that
\psi
diverges logarithmically near a thin mass shell of finite surface density
\Sigma
, and radius
R
:
\psi(r)\approx\mathcal{A}_{0}\pi\Sigma\ln\epsilon
, where
\epsilon=|r/R-1|
. This is also the behavior on the symmetry axis, at a distance
d
from a thin disk of radius
R
and surface density
\Sigma
, where
\psi(d)=\mathcal{A}_{0}\pi\Sigma\ln(d/\sqrt{R^{2}+d^{2}})
.
This is similar to the divergence in Newtonian gravity near an infinitely thin mass wire.
More generally, because of the extra factor of the radius difference in the denominator of expression (
10
) for
\psi
(compared with the Newtonian potential), the discontinuities in
\psi
near discontinuities in the mass distribution are more severe than those of the Newtonian potential.

This makes the model inapplicable to some idealized mass distributions, since it leads to unacceptable phenomenology in such cases. For example, in a spherical system, where there is a finite jump in density at some radius
R
, the dominant behavior
of
\psi(r)
near
R
is
\propto|r-R|\ln|r-R|
, so
\vec{\nabla}\psi
diverges there, logarithmically.
As in Newtonian dynamics, discontinuities of this kind become more severe in thin-disk geometries. For example,
\psi
diverges everywhere on an infinitely thin disk of finite surface density. For a disk of finite thickness, the thickness enters as a cutoff in the logarithm.

The DML models described below in Secs.
V
and
VI
, have better convergence properties, some even better than those of the Newtonian potential.

V.1.2
Two masses on circular orbits

VIII
Summary – some lessons learned

All nonrelativistic MOND theories, or models, that were studied to date modify either only the gravitational sector (i.e., the Poisson equation), or only inertia (i.e., the free particle action). The models presented here show that it may be productive, or even necessary, to modify both. This is expected on general grounds if we take the path of modified inertia, because the Einstein-Hilbert action, which reduces to the Poisson action in the nonrelativistic limit, may be thought of as the inertial actions of the gravitational field.

Another lesson that pertains to theories that modify Newtonian inertia is that since the inertia term in the particle EoM is no longer the acceleration, such theories do not predict an acceleration field, as is the case in Newtonian dynamics and MOND modified gravity theories. In pure modified-inertia theories, such as those discussed in Ref.
[
24
]
, the gravitational field does enter as an acceleration (the gradient of the potential), but the EoM equates it with some more involved functional of the trajectory than the acceleration itself (this is also true in special relativity, which modified Newtonian inertia).

This is crucial in the context of galactic dynamics, where, by and large, we do not even measure accelerations directly, let alone higher time derivative, or full trajectories.
18
18

18

Except in special cases such as for ideal circular orbits in an axisymmetric field, where the trajectory is fully specified by the measure orbital radius, and the velocity at one point.
At best, we combine measured velocities and radii to determine, or estimate, accelerations of certain test particles, at certain positions, and compare them with the calculated gravitational accelerations.
For example, in using rotation curves of disk galaxies we measure the accelerations of test particles on circular orbits, in the plane of the disk.
If a modified-inertia theory underlies the dynamics, this procedure can lead us astray, because the predicted kinematic acceleration at a given position depends not only on the position, but also on more details of the trajectory of the test particles used to map the field.

The models we studied here demonstrate that in theories that modify both inertia and gravity, even gravity itself does not enter as an acceleration field
in the DML
, which makes the acceleration itself an even less relevant quantity in the DML.

This may sound paradoxical in MOND, which is widely advertised as hinging on accelerations. MOND certainly does hinge on accelerations, inasmuch as we are speaking of transitional Newtonian-to-DML phenomena, where
a_{0}
itself plays the crucial role. However, the constant that is relevant in the DML – “the second MOND constant”,
\mathcal{A}_{0}
, which is perhaps the more fundamental of the two (see discussion in Ref.
[
32
]
) – is not an acceleration, and there is no preferred acceleration scale in the DML. The often-encountered DML relation
g\approx(g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}a_{0})^{1/2}
should be read as
g\approx(M\mathcal{A}_{0})^{1/2}/R
. The quantity
MG/R^{2}
, which we call the Newtonian gravitational acceleration, because it is that in the Newtonian regime, is undefined and has no meaning in a DML-governed world, as the above pure-DML models demonstrate.
19
19

19

Sometimes, we find it convenient to use our acquaintance with the Newtonian regime, and the knowledge of its constant
G
, to express DML results in therm of the familiar – but DML irrelevant –
g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
, by introducing the constant
\mathcal{A}_{0}/G
, which we call
a_{0}
.

We do, as much as we can, express results in terms of accelerations, because accelerations are what we can measure at present in galactic dynamics, even if acceleration is not the most directly relevant quantity.

Another important lesson, learned already to an extent, e.g., from studies of some pure modified-inertia models
[
24
]
, concerns the EFE.
In Newtonian dynamics, the internal dynamics of a small system, falling freely in the gravitational field of a large mother system, are oblivious to this external motion (barring some tidal effects if the external field is not constant across the subsystem). In MOND, with its generic nonlinearity, the internal dynamics can be greatly affected by the external free fall
[
1
,
14
]
. In the modified-gravity studies to date, this effect depends in a rather concrete manner on the momentary acceleration of the external motion. In some of the modified-inertia models discussed in Ref.
[
24
]
the effect depends also on the frequencies of the external and internal motions.

The DML models presented in Secs.
V
and
VI
exhibit an even larger variety of EFE workings. For example, the linear theory we started with predicts no EFE. In other models of all the DML the families, the EFE may depend on the external orbit in more complicated ways. In these models, accelerations do not even appear as fundamental orbital attributes. What enters, and may determine the workings of the EFE, are quantities with the dimensions of some fractional time derivative of the trajectory, which is not even a local attribute, but depends on the full trajectory. To recapitulate,
the EFE may have nothing to do with internal and external accelerations. In the DML there is no special role to accelerations.

Finally, an important, but perhaps little known, fact is that some primary DML predictions of MOND make use not only of the DML axioms, but also rely crucially on the relatively fast transition to the Newtonian regime as we cross
a_{0}
from low to high accelerations. This reliance on the vicinity of Newtonian behaviors to the DML is evident e.g., in the derivations in Refs.
[
12
,
33
]
. A case in point that I use to explain the issue is the
M-\sigma
relation, derived, e.g., in Ref.
[
12
]
, which states that with an appropriately defined system velocity dispersion,
\sigma
, and representative mass,
M
, the quantity
\chi=\sigma^{4}/M\mathcal{A}_{0}
is of order unity for all isolated, stationary (time-independent) DML systems.

Such would have been the case had the characteristic radius,
R
, of any DML system been equal to its MOND radius:
R=r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\equiv(MG/a_{0})^{1/2}
, and if the systems satisfied a Newtonian virial relation for some
\sigma
:
\sigma^{2}\approx MG/R
. Neither is the case, however, since DML systems have, by definition,
R\gg r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
, and yet this argument for the approximate universality of
\chi
is not fortuitous, as I now show.

The DML axioms – scale invariance, and the sole appearance of
a_{0}
and
G
– imply that within any two-parameter family of similar, time-independent mass distributions,
\rho({\bf r})=a\bar{\rho}({\bf r}/b)
, for any
a,b>0
,
\chi
takes the same value for any definition of
\sigma
and
M
consistent with the scaling. This follows from the scaling relations discussed in the beginning of Sec.
III
(taking there
a=\lambda\kappa^{-4}
and
b=\lambda
, and imposing time independence). For example, all DML systems with exponential density,
\rho(r)=\rho_{0}{\rm e}^{-r/h}
, have the same value of
\chi
, if
\sigma
is the velocity dispersion at
5h
and
M
is the mass contained within
2h
, irrespective of
\rho_{0}
and
h
. But, if
\sigma
and
M
are defined arbitrarily, the value of
\chi
may depend strongly on the exact density distribution.
However, we are after a universal, DML correlation for which
\chi
is of order unity irrespective of system type; for this
\sigma
and
M
have to be chosen discriminately.

Consider a two-parameter family of isolated, self-gravitating systems as described above, and take
M
to be the total mass. Choose a definition of a characteristic mean velocity dispersion,
\sigma
, and a characteristic radius,
R
, satisfying the following two requirements: (a) Newtonian dynamics would justify the virial relation
\sigma^{2}\approx GM/R
, between
\sigma
and
R
; (b) The ratio
R/r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
is a faithful measure of the mean acceleration in the system in the MOND sense; namely, for
R\gg r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
, DML dynamics apply, and for
R\ll r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
, Newtonian dynamics do.
The requirement from a MOND theory that Newtonian behavior lies near the DML behavior is crucial, as it implies that members of the family that lie on the
R=r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
line in the
M-R
plane, live approximately in the two worlds at once:
They have approximately the universal, DML
\chi
value of their family, and they also satisfy approximately the Newtonian virial relation, yielding
\chi\sim 1
.

All this is relevant in the present context. Because the DML models discussed in Secs.
V
and
VI
do not have a proper Newtonian continuation, it is not clear that they predict a distribution of
\chi
values around unity. Indeed, the coefficient in the
M-\sigma
relation (
26
) that we derived for the linear, DML model, is not guaranteed to be of order unity for all systems, and it is not clear that the definitions of
\sigma
and of the characteristic radius used in the derivation satisfy the above two conditions. If they, indeed, do not, we shall learn from it that the proximity of the Newtonian limit to the DML is, indeed, necessary to make even pure DML primary predictions.

Appendix A
Fractional derivatives and their manipulation

In this paper, I employ derivatives of fractional order, both space derivatives, appearing here as fractional Laplacians, and fractional time derivatives.
There is much literature on the subject. Some more recent detailed discussions can be found in Refs.

[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
17
]
.

There are many ways to define fractional derivatives (of non-natural order). Since derivation of any natural order is an algebraic manipulation in Fourier space – multiplication by the appropriate power of the Fourier variable – a natural way to define fractional derivatives, which I use in this paper, is to define them by their action in Fourier space.
I now describe the definition I use and some manipulations and formulas involving such derivatives.

A.1
Fractional Laplacian

If
\hat{\psi}({\bf k})
is the Fourier transform of
\psi({\bf r})
; namely,

\psi({\bf r})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int d^{3}k~\hat{\psi}({\bf k}){\rm e}^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}},

(89)

then, the Fourier transform of
\Delta\psi({\bf r})
is
-|{\bf k}|^{2}\hat{\psi}({\bf k})
. We would then tentatively want to define a generalization to a fractional degree
\alpha
, designated
\Delta^{[\alpha]}
, such that the Fourier transform,
\widehat{\Delta^{[\alpha]}\psi}({\bf k})
scales as
(-|{\bf k}|^{2})^{\alpha}\hat{\psi}({\bf k})=(-1)^{\alpha}|{\bf k}|^{2\alpha}
. This would indeed coincide with the transform of
\Delta^{n}\psi({\bf r})
for natural
\alpha=n
.
However, I also require that if
\psi
is a real function, so is
\Delta^{[\alpha]}\psi
. Its transform thus has to satisfy
\widehat{\Delta^{[\alpha]}\psi}(-{\bf k})=\widehat{\Delta^{[\alpha]}\psi}^{*}({\bf k})
.
Writing
(-|{\bf k}|^{2})^{\alpha}
as
{\rm e}^{i\xi}|{\bf k}|^{2\alpha}
, with
\xi
some phase that does not depend on
{\bf k}
, the reality requirement constrains the phase factor to be real; i.e., either
1
or
-1
. I also require that
\Delta^{[1]}=\Delta
; so, the phase factor has to be taken as -1.
I thus define

\Delta^{[\alpha]}\psi({\bf r})=-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int d^{3}k~|{\bf k}|^{2\alpha}\hat{\psi}({\bf k}){\rm e}^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}}d^{3}k.

(90)

This definition is in line with those discussed, e.g., in Refs.
[
21
,
17
]
, arrived at based on other justifications. Expression (
90
) equals what is defined in those references as
-(-\Delta)^{\alpha}
.

Note that
\Delta^{[n]}=(-1)^{n-1}\Delta^{n}
; so, even for natural degrees it coincides with the standard derivatives only for odd
n
, but has the opposite sign for even
n
. Note also that
\Delta^{[\alpha]}c=0
, for a constant
c
, for any
\alpha>0
.

I now derive some results that are used in the body of the paper.

A.1.1
Variation of the free
\psi
Lagrangian

In Secs.
V
and
VII
, we needed the variation of
\int d^{3}r~(\Delta^{[\alpha]}\psi)^{2}
, appearing in the free
\psi
Lagrangian, to first order in a small change
\delta\psi
in
\psi
.
Use the definition (
90
) above to write

\int d^{3}r~(\Delta^{[\alpha]}\psi)^{2}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{6}}\int d^{3}r\int\int d^{3}k~d^{3}k^{\prime}~(|k||k^{\prime}|)^{2\alpha}\hat{\psi}({\bf k}){\rm e}^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}}\hat{\psi}({\bf k}^{\prime}){\rm e}^{i{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf r}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int d^{3}k~|k|^{4\alpha}\hat{\psi}({\bf k})\hat{\psi}(-{\bf k}),

(91)

where the
{\bf r}
integration was performed to give
(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{3}({\bf k}+{\bf k}^{\prime})
.
Thus,

\delta\int d^{3}r~(\Delta^{[\alpha]}\psi)^{2}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int d^{3}k~|k|^{4\alpha}[\hat{\psi}({\bf k})\delta\hat{\psi}(-{\bf k})+\delta\hat{\psi}({\bf k})\hat{\psi}(-{\bf k})]=\frac{2}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int d^{3}k~|k|^{4\alpha}\hat{\psi}({\bf k})\delta\hat{\psi}(-{\bf k}),

(92)

since the two terms in the integrand contribute equally.
Comparing with

\int d^{3}r~(\Delta^{[2\alpha]}\psi)\delta\psi=-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{6}}\int d^{3}r\int\int d^{3}k~d^{3}k^{\prime}~|k|^{4\alpha}\hat{\psi}({\bf k}){\rm e}^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}}\delta\hat{\psi}({\bf k}^{\prime}){\rm e}^{i{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf r}}=-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int d^{3}k~|k|^{4\alpha}\hat{\psi}({\bf k})\delta\hat{\psi}(-{\bf k}),

(93)

we see that

\delta\int d^{3}r~(\Delta^{[\alpha]}\psi)^{2}=-2\int d^{3}r~(\Delta^{[2\alpha]}\psi)\delta\psi.

(94)

A.1.2
The free
\psi
Lagrangian for
\alpha=1/2
equals the free Poisson Lagrangian

In Sec.
VII
, we needed the result that for
\alpha=1/2
, the free
\psi
Lagrangian is the same as the Poisson Lagrangian. Write

\int d^{3}r~(\Delta^{[1/2]}\psi)^{2}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{6}}\int d^{3}r\int\int d^{3}k~d^{3}k^{\prime}~(|k||k^{\prime}|)\hat{\psi}({\bf k}){\rm e}^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}}\hat{\psi}({\bf k}^{\prime}){\rm e}^{i{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf r}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int d^{3}k~|k|^{2}\hat{\psi}({\bf k})\hat{\psi}(-{\bf k}),

(95)

and compare with

\int d^{3}r~(\vec{\nabla}\psi)^{2}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{6}}\int d^{3}r\int\int d^{3}k~d^{3}k^{\prime}~i{\bf k}\cdot i{\bf k}^{\prime}\hat{\psi}({\bf k}){\rm e}^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}}\hat{\psi}({\bf k}^{\prime}){\rm e}^{i{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf r}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int d^{3}k~|k|^{2}\hat{\psi}({\bf k})\hat{\psi}(-{\bf k}).

(96)

So the two expressions are equal.

A.1.3
Green’s function of the fractional Laplacian

We seek
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}({\bf r})
such that

\Delta^{[\alpha]}\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}({\bf r})=\delta^{3}({\bf r})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int d^{3}k~{\rm e}^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}}.

(97)

Comparing with the definition in Eq. (
90
) we see that the Fourier transform of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}
is

\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha}({\bf k})=-|k|^{-2\alpha}.

(98)

Using tables for inverse Fourier transforms, we get

\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}({\bf r})=-\frac{\Gamma(3/2-\alpha)}{4^{\alpha}\pi^{3/2}\Gamma(\alpha)}|{\bf r}|^{-(3-2\alpha)},

(99)

where
\Gamma
is the Gamma function. For the Laplacian itself,
\alpha=1
, we get the standard
\mathcal{G}_{1}=-1/4\pi|{\bf r}|
. For our linear DML model (
\alpha=1/2
), we get
\mathcal{G}_{1/2}=-1/2\pi^{2}|{\bf r}|^{2}
.

A.2
Fractional time derivatives

Decompose the trajectory
{\bf r}(t)
into its Fourier components

{\bf r}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega~\hat{\bf r}(\omega)e^{i\omega t}

(100)

[since
{\bf r}(t)
is real,
\hat{\bf r}(-\omega)=\hat{\bf r}^{*}(\omega)
].
For natural values of the order
n
, we have

{\bf r}^{(n)}\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega~(i\omega)^{n}\hat{\bf r}(\omega)e^{i\omega t}.

(101)

One then takes this to be the definition of the time derivative of any order,
\eta
.
But, we need to specify the definition of
(i\omega)^{\eta}
, which depends on the choice of the logarithm branch in the complex plane. Write

u(\omega)\equiv(i\omega)^{\eta}=e^{\eta\ln(i\omega)}=e^{\eta[\ln{|\omega|}+i\frac{\pi}{2}s(\omega)+2i\pi k]},

(102)

where
s(\omega)
is the sign of
\omega
(
s=0
for
\omega=0
), and
with the integers
k
corresponding to the branches of the logarithm.
We want to define the
\eta
derivative of a real function so that it is also real. This requires
that
u(-\omega)=u^{*}(\omega)
, leading to the choice
k=0

u(\omega)=e^{\eta[\ln{|\omega|}+i\frac{\pi}{2}s(\omega)]}=|\omega|^{\eta}e^{i\eta\frac{\pi}{2}s(\omega)},

(103)

with
|\omega|^{\eta}
the real, non-negative value.
We thus define

{\bf r}^{(\eta)}\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega~|\omega|^{\eta}e^{i[\omega t+\eta\frac{\pi}{2}s(\omega)]}\hat{\bf r}(\omega).

(104)

With this definition we also have the usual relation
d{\bf r}^{(\eta)}/dt={\bf r}^{(\eta+1)}
, and, more generally,
[{\bf r}^{(\eta)}]^{(\zeta)}={\bf r}^{(\eta+\zeta)}
.

A.2.1
Variation of the particle action

Using definition (
104
), the particle action defined in Sec.
VII
is

I_{\scriptscriptstyle P}=\frac{1}{2}\int dt~[{\bf r}^{(\eta)}(t)]^{2}=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int dt\int d\omega\int d\omega^{\prime}|\omega|^{\eta}|\omega^{\prime}|^{\eta}\hat{\bf r}(\omega)\hat{\bf r}(\omega^{\prime}){\rm e}^{i\{(\omega+\omega^{\prime})t+\eta\frac{\pi}{2}[s(\omega)+s(\omega^{\prime})]\}}.

(105)

The time integration yields
2\pi\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})
; so, we have

I_{\scriptscriptstyle P}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\omega~|\omega|^{2\eta}\hat{\bf r}(\omega)\hat{\bf r}(-\omega).

(106)

Varying over
{\bf r}
we have

\delta I_{\scriptscriptstyle P}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\omega~|\omega|^{2\eta}[\hat{\bf r}(\omega)\delta\hat{\bf r}(-\omega)+\hat{\bf r}(-\omega)\delta\hat{\bf r}(\omega)]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\omega~|\omega|^{2\eta}\hat{\bf r}(\omega)\delta\hat{\bf r}(-\omega),

(107)

which equals

\delta I_{\scriptscriptstyle P}=\int dt~{\bf r}^{[2\eta]}\cdot\delta{\bf r}(t),

(108)

where

{\bf r}^{[2\eta]}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega~|\omega|^{2\eta}\hat{\bf r}(\omega)e^{i\omega t}.

(109)

(Everywhere,
|\omega|^{2\eta}
is the real non-negative value.)
