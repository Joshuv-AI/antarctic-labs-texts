Customizable Fully Structured Vector Trajectory Beams in the Focal Plane via a Single‐Layer Metasurface
Authors: Zhibang Tan, Zebin Chen, Zepeng Chen, Yantao Zheng, Min Ouyang, Wei Hu, Qing Wang, Lijun Wu, Qian Shou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAs a class of structured light exhibiting unique spatial configurations, trajectory beams (TBs) feature the characteristic parameters of the light field, also referred to as the degrees of freedom (DoFs) of the light field, distributed along predefined trajectories. However, achieving TBs in which multiple DoFs are completely decoupled remains a significant challenge. Here, we demonstrate the generation of customizable fully structured vector trajectory beams (VTBs) in the focal plane using a single‐layer metasurface designed via computer‐generated holography. The controllable DoFs encompass the trajectory shape and the distributions of amplitude, phase, and polarization along the predefined trajectory. The generated TBs surpass existing methods in the number of controllable DoFs. More significantly, by shifting the key principle of the design from angular‐variant topological phases to spatial trajectory, they surpass conventional vortex beams based on the prior principle, including their more advanced derivatives. The demonstrated ability to independently control multiple DoFs holds great promise in enhancing the channel capacity in optical communication.Keywords: degrees of freedom, metasurfaces, Poincaré sphere, trajectory beams, vector vortex beams

Introduction

By modulating degrees of freedom (DoFs) including amplitude, phase, and polarization, either singly or jointly, complex structured light fields with distinct physical properties can be generated. Representative examples such as vortex beams with helical phases [1], non‐diffracting Bessel beams [2], and vector beams with spatially dependent polarization [3], have revealed numerous novel physical phenomena. These fields are of great theoretical and practical significance in optical communication [4, 5], particle manipulation [6, 7, 8], optical sensing [9, 10], and computational imaging [11, 12].

In response to the increasingly complex control requirements in modern optics, metasurfaces with their sub‐wavelength scale control advantages have provided a more flexible and compact platform, leading to significant progress in the past decade [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. As a focal point in light field manipulation, vortex beams have garnered significant attention due to their distinctive helical phase wavefronts and their ability to carry orbital angular momentum (OAM) [18, 19, 20]. In recent years, researchers have incorporated additional DoFs into vortex beams—such as amplitude and polarization—to generate advanced beam structures. These include generalized vortex beams that transcend traditional circular shape constraints [21], perfect vortex beams in which the topological charge is decoupled from the ring radius [22, 23], and vector vortex beams (VVBs) characterized by spatially non‐uniform polarization distributions [24, 25, 26]. Subsequent research has further dedicated efforts to the decoupled manipulation of multiple DoFs to construct even more complex structured light fields, with a typical example being generalized perfect vector vortex beams (GPVVBs), which simultaneously integrate the characteristics of the three aforementioned beam types [27, 28, 29, 30]. Although research on vortex beams has become relatively systematic and extensive, the core design principle of such beams fundamentally relies on an azimuthally varying phase gradient. Consequently, most existing studies still face significant limitations. On one hand, optical parameters such as phase inherently vary along the azimuthal direction independently of any predefined trajectory, and this decoupling between phase evolution and spatial paths severely restricts potential applications in specific scenarios. On the other hand, the DoFs in most existing beams remain constrained; the beam morphology is generally confined to circular or elliptical shapes, and the polarization ellipticity typically exhibits a uniform distribution.

Distinct from vortex beams, a class of structured light centering on the trajectory as the core manipulation object can effectively surmount the mentioned limitations [31, 32, 33, 34, 35]. Its core feature is that the amplitude, phase, and polarization of the light field vary along a predefined trajectory. We define these fields as trajectory beams (TBs). Recently, researchers have demonstrated the generation of beams with polarization varying along arbitrary spatial trajectories [36, 37]. However, for such TBs, the decoupling and independent control among multiple DoFs—including the trajectory shape and the distributions of amplitude, phase, and polarization along the trajectory—remain a research direction that urgently demands in‐depth exploration.

To address the mentioned challenges, this paper proposes customizable fully structured vector trajectory beams (VTBs) in the focal plane that can be directly generated via a single‐layer metasurface (Figure 1). Compared with the existing related studies (see Supporting Information S1: Table S1 in Supplementary Section 1 for details), our work has significant advantages, as follows: (1) It overcomes the current limited number of independently controllable DoFs, achieving full command over all spatial DoFs in the focal plane—including its shape and the distributions of amplitude, phase, and polarization; (2) moreover, the arbitrary control of polarization has been achieved, including ellipticity and orientation angle; and (3) crucially, all the control DoFs change along the trajectory, and this characteristic is beyond the reach of traditional beams constructed based on azimuthally varying vortex phases. This work enables the construction of complex highly adapted structured light fields tailored to specific application scenarios, holding significant theoretical importance and practical value in frontier domains such as precise particle manipulation, multi‐dimensional optical encryption, and customized optical communication.

Under the prerequisite of a fixed incident polarization state (i.e., Θ and Φ remain constant), the parametric design of the dual‐channel complex amplitudes based on Equations (4), (5), (6), (7) enables independent and precise tuning of each DoF of the light field. Notably, whether these physical properties share common base components or not, they are completely decoupled in logic. Because the dual‐channel amplitude factors AL and AR are mutually independent, the trajectory amplitude A(ξ) and the weighted amplitude ratio ρ are fundamentally decoupled. Accordingly, while keeping the ratio ρ constant, scaling AL and AR simultaneously adjusts the intensity distribution along the trajectory; conversely, by actively regulating AL, one can vary ρ (to modulate the ellipticity) while keeping the total amplitude A(ξ) constant. Furthermore, the global phase and polarization orientation angle, dictated by the topological charges lL,lR of the dual channels, are also decoupled. The global phase φ(ξ) is determined by the sum of the dual‐channel topological charges (i.e., the generalized phase order lG), whereas the polarization orientation is explicitly controlled by their difference (i.e., the generalized polarization order lP). This independent mathematical mapping ensures that by regulating the parameters of the dual‐channel complex amplitudes, fully structured and customizable VTBs can be achieved in the focal plane.

The Design of Metasurfaces

The realization of customizable fully structured VTBs demands that the metasurface be capable of independently manipulating the complex amplitudes of a pair of orthogonal polarization channels. The design of the metasurface is illustrated in Figure 2. We select anisotropic TiO2 nanopillars exhibiting birefringence on a square SiO2 substrate with a lattice constant of P=340 nm as a fundamental meta‐atom (Figure 2a) These structures possess a high refractive index and low loss across the visible spectrum [43]. Figure 2b displays the meta‐molecule structure composed of four meta‐atoms, where nanopillars A and B, fabricated from TiO2, are arranged alternately on the SiO2 substrate. This structure is based on composite phase modulation and double‐atom interference mechanism, and can achieve the control of dual‐channel complex amplitude information [44, 45]. Numerical simulations were performed to investigate the transmission characteristics of meta‐atoms with a fixed height of H=600 nm under normal incidence of a plane wave at λ=532 nm, utilizing the finite‐difference time‐domain (FDTD) method. In the simulations, periodic boundary conditions were applied along the x and y directions, whereas perfectly matched layers were imposed along the z direction. The simulated transmission coefficients and phase shifts are plotted as functions of the varying length and width of the nanopillars in Figure 2c,d,f,g. It is evident that by selecting appropriate combinations of lengths and widths, a full phase coverage of [0,2π] can be achieved. Figure 2e presents the transmittances and phase shifts of the 10 selected nanopillars. As the transmittances are close to 1, and the phase difference approaches π, each nanopillar functions as a highly efficient half‐wave plate. The detailed dimensions (lengths and widths) of these 10 nanopillars are listed in Supporting Information S1: Table S2 of the Supplementary Section 3. Based on these meta‐atoms, a metasurface with an aperture size of 44 μm × 44 μm was designed, comprising an 128×128 array of nanopillars with a target focal length of 80 μm. Regarding the numerical setup, an auto‐non‐uniform meshing algorithm was employed with the mesh accuracy set to 1. Based on the Rayleigh criterion, the theoretical trajectory width is calculated to be wt=0.61λ/NA=1.22 μm.

Results and Discussion

Dual‐Channel Customizable Fully Structured TBs in the Focal Plane

To validate independent control over dual‐channel complex amplitudes, we implemented dual‐channel customizable fully structured TBs in the focal plane via a single‐layer metasurface (Figure 3). Following the theoretical design, the calculated amplitude and phase distributions required for the metasurface are illustrated in Supporting Information S1: Figure S1, with the corresponding spatial maps of the phase shifts and rotation angles for nanopillars A and B across the metasurface plane presented in Supporting Information S1: Figure S2 (see Supporting Information S1: Supplementary Section 4 for details). As shown in Figure 3a,b, when the metasurface is illuminated with LCP light and RCP light respectively, different effects will be observed in the focal plane. The corresponding results are presented in Figure 3c,d. In the LCP channel, a circular trajectory with a gradually increasing amplitude gradient is generated (the first column in Figure 3c). In the RCP channel, a hexagonal trajectory with alternating bright and dark amplitude distributions is presented (the first column in Figure 3d). This indicates that our method is capable of independently controlling the trajectory shapes of the LCP and RCP channels, as well as the amplitude variations along the preset trajectories. It is easily observed that the trajectory width is close to the theoretical value of 1.3 μm. The second column displays S3, indicating that the spin state of the beam is reversed after modulation by the metasurface; this reflects the high purity of the channels and the high polarization conversion efficiency. The third column shows the preset topological structures in the phase distributions, where lL=4,lR=6. This indicates that the phases of the LCP and RCP channels can be precisely and independently controlled. The excellent agreement between FDTD simulations and the numerical calculations validates the proposed method.

In addition, the designed metasurface exhibits the expected broadband response characteristics. Supporting Information S1: Figure S3 of the Supplementary Section 5 illustrates the intensity and phase distributions of the TBs at various wavelengths, specifically 480, 532, 580, and 630 nm. At the corresponding focal planes of z=88,80,70,and66 μm, the in‐plane intensity patterns and phase orders maintain high similarity, demonstrating that the metasurface remains effective for generating TBs across a wide range of visible wavelengths. Furthermore, considering potential fabrication errors in actual processing, we calculated the focal‐plane field distributions when the nanopillar lengths and widths undergo deviations of ±10, ±20,±30,±40, and ±50 nm. As shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S4 of the Supplementary Section 6, we numerically analyzed the focal‐plane field distributions under varying fabrication errors. The simulation results indicate that the amplitude and phase distributions of both channels maintain high fidelity for dimensional deviations within 30 nm. Although noticeable stray spots appear when the deviation reaches 50 nm, current fabrication techniques can generally be controlled within approximately 10 nm [46], further confirming the reliability of our design.

Customizable Fully Structured VTBs in the Focal Plane

The preceding section validated the metasurface’s capability for the independent manipulation of dual‐channel amplitude and phase. Therefore, by designing the metasurface to generate beams with identical trajectory shapes under both LCP and RCP illumination, the SoP distribution along the predefined trajectory can be tailored via the amplitude ratio and phase difference between these two orthogonal components.

In order to describe the characteristics of TBs, we compared and analyzed them with traditional Laguerre–Gaussian VVBs and GPVVBs that have the same phase order (lG) and polarization order (lP) as illustrated in Figure 4a. Conventional VVBs are typically restricted to circular trajectories. Specifically, for a high‐order VVB, the polarization orientation varies linearly only with the azimuthal coordinate (Figure 4a1), while a hybrid‐order VVB encompasses complex radial–azimuthal variations in both ellipticity and orientation (Figure 4a4). To overcome the limitation of fixed geometric profiles, high‐order and hybrid‐order GPVVBs have been introduced to enable customizable trajectory shapes, such as the “S”‐shaped GPVVBs demonstrated here (Figure 4a2,a5). However, the evolution of their light field parameters remains constrained by their intrinsic azimuthal dependence, leading to a mismatch between the polarization distribution and the trajectory shape. Furthermore, for both VVBs and GPVVBs, the presence of a central intensity null arising from phase singularities remains unavoidable (Figure 4a1,a2,a4,a5). In contrast, the VTBs proposed in this work overcome the limitations. For both high‐order (Figure 4a3) and hybrid‐order (Figure 4a6) configurations, the polarization distribution of VTBs evolves exclusively along the predefined trajectory, being solely determined by generalized polarization order (lP). Finally, we demonstrate the normalized intensity distributions of an “S”‐shaped VTB after passing through analyzers oriented in various directions. The theoretical predictions show excellent agreement with the results obtained from FDTD simulations (Figure 4b).

Beyond the trajectory‐dependent evolution of field parameters, the core advantage of the proposed VTBs lies in the decoupling of multiple DoFs, specifically evidenced by the generalized phase order (lG) and the generalized polarization order (lP). Under illumination with a fixed SoP (Θ,Φ)=π2,0, we analyzed VTBs with identical generalized polarization orders (lP) but different generalized phase orders (lG), as well as those with different generalized polarization orders (lP) but identical generalized phase orders (lG). Figure 5 presents the results obtained from theoretical calculations and FDTD simulations, showing the intensity distributions under analyzers oriented at different angles (0,π/4,π/2,3π/4), along with the extracted phase distributions for the LCP and RCP components, as well as the global phase distributions. Comparing Figure 5a lG=3,lP=−1 and Figure 5b lG=4,lP=−1, it is evident that VTBs can carry different global phases along the predefined trajectory while maintaining the same SoPs. Conversely, a comparison of Figure 5b lG=4,lP=−1 and Figure 5c lG=4,lP=−2 reveals that VTBs with identical global phases can exhibit distinct polarization distributions along the predefined trajectory. These findings further confirm the complete decoupling of the polarization and phase orders of VTBs designed by the proposed method. Although consistent with results reported [24], our work distinguishes itself by extending the concept to arbitrary shapes, with a specific focus on TBs rather than azimuthally varying vortex beams.

Finally, to validate the capability of the proposed method in generating customizable fully structured VTBs under specific incident polarization light, we designed a single‐layer metasurface capable of generating a VTB with a distinctive spatial structure under x‐polarized illumination. Specifically, the trajectory shape is designed as a “lips”‐shaped. The amplitude distribution is characterized by a dimmer upper half and a brighter lower half while carrying a global phase of the third order. The polarization distribution evolves along two distinct paths on PS(0,0). Figure 7a illustrates the polarization design concept: starting from point Aπ2,0 on PS(0,0), the state first completes one full rotation along the path C1 returning to point A, and subsequently completes another rotation along the path C2 back to point A. The specific polarization evolution along these two paths is depicted in Figure 7b. Figure 7d presents the results for intensity and phase. The intensity distribution exhibits the “lips”‐shaped trajectory with the designed “dim‐top and bright‐bottom” contrast. By extracting the phase distributions of the LCP and RCP components of the total field, a value of lL=2,lR=4 is obtained, indicating a global vortex phase of the third order lG=3. The polarization distribution is displayed in Figure 7e; as intended, the SoPs along the trajectory follow the evolution along the two paths C1 and C2 on the PS(0,0), with specific Stokes parameter distributions provided. To further demonstrate the flexibility of our method in generating arbitrary beams, Figure 7f,g display another VTB with the same amplitude distributions as those in Figure 7d,e, but with different phase orders lG=4 and polarization distributions. Specifically, the evolution of their SoPs along the trajectory completes four full cycles around path C1 on the PS(0,0). Furthermore, the results obtained from the FDTD method demonstrate excellent agreement with the theoretical calculations. This agreement confirms that our method successfully achieves independent control over the four key DoFs—trajectory shape and the distributions of amplitude, phase, and polarization along the predefined trajectory—enabling the generation of customizable fully structured VTBs in the focal plane via a single‐layer metasurface.

This capability significantly extends the DoFs and flexibility in polarization control compared to traditional schemes. Such dynamic and independent control offers a new dimension for constructing customized optical fields, demonstrating broad potential applications in fields such as dynamic encryption and secure communication.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have systematically proposed and demonstrated a method for generating TBs via a single‐layer all‐dielectric metasurface. By integrating computer‐generated holography with composite phase modulation and a diatomic interference mechanism, on‐demand customizable fully structured VTBs in the focal plane—including the trajectory shape and the distributions of amplitude, phase, and polarization along the predefined trajectory—can be achieved. Compared with previous studies, this method not only overcomes the restriction that the vortex phase must strictly vary along the azimuthal direction but also significantly enhances the DoFs for independent control. As a proof of concept, we first demonstrated the generation of dual‐channel customizable fully structured TBs in the focal plane via a single‐layer metasurface, where the trajectory shape and the distributions of intensity and phase for each channel were independently controlled. Furthermore, via the coherent superposition of two orthogonal channels, we realized a “lips”‐shaped fully structured VTB with completely decoupled spatial structural parameters under specific incident polarization illumination. This successfully validates the independent control over the trajectory shape and the distributions of amplitude, phase, and polarization along the predefined trajectory. This research provides an efficient and flexible technical pathway for the advanced tailoring of complex structured light fields. This work unlocks significant potential for advanced optical applications, such as enhancing information capacity and transmission speed in optical communications [19, 29, 30, 47, 48, 49, 50], and facilitating the exploration of light–matter interaction mechanisms in particle manipulation by leveraging multiple DoFs that vary along the trajectory.
