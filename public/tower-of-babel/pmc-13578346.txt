Metabotropic signaling downstream of GABAA receptors suppresses neuroinflammation in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Wenjing Lu, Lirong Zhang, Xi Chen, Xiaoxi Ren, Fenqin Xue, Feilong Zhang, Jing Sun, Wei Wang, Haixia Huang, Jianliang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder, in which persistent neuroinflammation has been recognized as a major pathological component. Thus, strategies aimed at suppressing neuroinflammation are particularly important for developing disease-modifying therapies in PD. In this study, we examined the role of gamma-aminobutyric acid Type A Receptors (GABAARs) in regulating microglial activation and neuroinflammation, and their implications for neuroprotection in a lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced PD model. We confirmed that stimulation of GABAARs markedly inhibited neuroinflammation and consequently attenuated related neurotoxicity both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistic investigations revealed that metabotropic signaling cascades downstream of GABAARs in microglia were involved, including intracellular calcium release and the mobilization of phosphatases, which dephosphorylated IκBα and restricted nuclear translocation of p65, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects. Moreover, pharmacological and immunoprecipitation assays showed that phospholipase C (PLC) physically interacted with GABAARs and mediated calcium release from intracellular stores. These metabotropic signaling mechanisms underlie the suppressive action of GABAARs on LPS-induced neuroinflammation. Given the central role of neuroinflammation in PD pathogenesis and other neurodegenerative conditions, this study provides new insights into the therapeutic potential of targeting GABAARs to combat neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration in PD.Subject terms: Diseases, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder worldwide, affecting ~1% of individuals over the age of 601. PD is characterized by the selective loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra (SN) and the presence of Lewy bodies within the remaining neurons. Although the molecular mechanisms underlying PD progression are highly complex, persistent neuroinflammation has been recognized as a key pathological component of nearly all neurodegenerative diseases2. This is particularly relevant in PD, as the SN contains a relatively high density of microglia3,4. Importantly, restricting neuroinflammation through pharmacological or other interventions may hold great potential for preventing the spread and progression of PD pathology5,6.

Across commonly used PD models, including LPS, MPTP, rotenone, and α-synuclein paradigms, microglial activation is consistently observed as part of the induced neuroinflammatory response, accompanied by elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine signaling, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-65,7–9. Accordingly, biological agents targeting cytokine signaling pathways have been developed, including anti-TNF antibodies, recombinant IL-1 receptor antagonists, anti-IL-6R antibodies, and antibodies targeting Th17-associated pathways. While clinically impactful in inflammatory disease, these agents have important limitations, including elevated infection risk resulting from immune pathway suppression, rather than selective modulation alone10. Furthermore, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) in PD patients have not delivered meaningful clinical benefit, which may be explained by the still-incomplete understanding of the molecular and cellular mechanisms driving PD pathogenesis11,12. Therefore, identifying effective strategies to curb persistent neuroinflammation remains particularly important for developing disease-modifying therapies for PD.

Accumulating evidence indicates that the GABAergic system plays a crucial inhibitory role in the immune system13,14. GABA is the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter of the central nervous system (CNS), synthesized from glutamate via the enzyme glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD)2. Reduced GAD expression or activity lowers endogenous GABA levels, which not only impairs inhibitory neurotransmission but also exacerbates inflammatory responses15,16. Notably, in PD patients, the activity of GAD is reduced in the caudate nucleus (CN) and substantia nigra (SN), and the density of GABAARs is decreased in both cortical and subcortical brain regions17. Moreover, treatment of PD patients with gabapentin (which enhances GABA synthesis and release), muscimol (a competitive GABAAR agonist), or zolpidem (a GABAAR allosteric agonist) has been reported to improve Parkinsonian symptoms18–20. Experimental studies have also shown that microglial activation is negatively regulated by GABAergic neurotransmission21, and that diazepam can suppress inflammation22 and modulate immune responses23 via GABAARs. However, the precise mechanisms by which GABAARs inhibit inflammation and their implications for the neuroprotection of dopaminergic neurons in PD remain largely unclear.

GABAARs are pentameric complexes composed of membrane-spanning subunits, each featuring a large extracellular N-terminal domain, four transmembrane (TM) domains, and a substantial intracellular loop between TM3 and TM424. GABAA receptors, as the primary hyperpolarizing influence in the CNS, play a crucial role in counterbalancing glutamate-mediated excitatory transmission. Consequently, numerous studies have explored the functional significance of GABAA receptors ion conduction pathways25,26. Beyond their classical ionotropic function as chloride-permeable channels, GABAARs also participate in metabotropic signaling, influencing various physiological and pathological processes. Recent studies indicate that GABAAR activation can also regulate several metabotropic pathways. For example, it has been shown to suppress apoptosis via the Bax/Bcl-2 pathway, inhibit autophagy through the AMPK-mTOR pathway27, and promote antioxidant responses by downregulating Nrf2 expression28. Additionally, GABAAR activation can attenuate inflammatory responses by downregulating key mediators such as IFN‑γ and NF‑κB and inhibiting the release of IL‑6 and TNF‑α2. Collectively, GABAAR activation may critically regulate disease procession through mechanisms such as immunosuppression and antioxidation, although the underlying metabolic signaling pathways remain largely unclear.

In this study, we confirmed that stimulation of GABAARs plays a prominent role in suppressing neuroinflammation in a PD model, thereby alleviating associated neurotoxicity both in vitro and in vivo. This effect involves metabotropic signaling cascades downstream of GABAARs in microglia, including intracellular calcium release and activation of phosphatases, which dephosphorylate IκBα and limit the nuclear translocation of p65, ultimately exerting anti-inflammatory effects. Furthermore, pharmacological and co-immunoprecipitation assays revealed that phospholipase C (PLC) physically interacts with GABAARs and mediates calcium release from intracellular stores. Collectively, these findings provide new insights into the GABAAR-targeted therapeutic potential in combating neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration in PD.

Results

GABA attenuated LPS-induced neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration both in vivo and in vitro

It is well established that LPS stimulates microglial activation and triggers inflammatory responses, making intranigral LPS injection a widely used alternative PD model5. To further investigate whether GABAergic agents can prevent neuroinflammation in PD mice29, LPS-challenged mice were orally administered GABA according to the schedule shown in Supplementary Fig. 1a. Immunofluorescence (IF) staining revealed significant activation of Iba1+ microglia ipsilateral to the injection site in the substantia nigra (SN) at day 9 post-LPS injection (Fig. 1a), whereas no obvious activation was observed in the striatum (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Notably, GABA administration markedly ameliorated LPS-induced microglial activation in the SN, while GABA alone showed no significant effect on microglial activation compared with PBS group (Fig. 1a). To further validate the neuroinflammatory response induced by LPS, mRNA levels of TNF-α, IL-6, iNOS, and COX2 in midbrain were assessed by qPCR. LPS significantly upregulated these pro-inflammatory mRNA compared to PBS-injected controls, and GABA treatment attenuated this increase (Supplementary Fig. 1c–f). Consistently, ELISA measurements showed that LPS elevated TNF-α and IL-6 protein levels in the midbrain, which were significantly suppressed by GABA administration (Fig. 1b, c); Meanwhile, GABA alone exerted no obvious effect on the production of inflammatory cytokines compared with the PBS group (Fig. 1b, c). To further investigate whether GABA exerts neuroprotective effects against LPS challenge, dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra (SN) and nerve fibers in the striatum were assessed by tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) immunohistochemistry. As shown in Fig. 1d, LPS-challenged mice exhibited substantial loss of TH+ cells in the SN and striatal nerve fibers compared to PBS-injected controls, whereas GABA administration markedly restored their integrity. Consistently, TH protein levels in both the SN and striatum were significantly reduced by LPS but rescued by GABA treatment (Fig. 1e). Furthermore, on day 8 post-LPS injection, motor functions were evaluated using the rotarod test (motor coordination), pole test (motor balance), and wire hang test (muscle strength). LPS-intoxicated mice displayed severe motor deficits, including reduced latency on the rotarod, prolonged time in the pole test, and decreased hanging time on the wire. GABA treatment significantly improved these motor performances (Fig. 1f–h). Notably, GABA alone did not affect motor behaviors relative to the PBS group (Fig. 1f–h). Collectively, these results demonstrated that systemic administration of GABA mitigated LPS-induced neuroinflammation, dopaminergic neurodegeneration, and associated motor deficits.

To corroborate the in vivo findings, the effects of GABA were further investigated in vitro. Microglia were pretreated with GABA prior to LPS stimulation, and ELISA results showed that LPS (1 μg/mL) significantly increased TNF-α and IL-6 secretion compared to controls, which was dose-dependently suppressed by GABA (Fig. 1i, j). Similarly, GABA pretreatment blocked LPS-induced elevations of TNF-α and IL-6 (Supplementary Fig. 1g, h) and reduced the upregulation of iNOS and COX2 in BV2 microglia (Fig. 1k). Furthermore, to determine whether GABA protects neurons from microglia-mediated inflammation, primary neurons were exposed to conditioned medium (CM) from LPS-stimulated primary microglia. GABA pretreatment conferred neuroprotection in a dose-dependent manner, as indicated by LDH release (Fig. 1l) and MTT colorimetric assay (Fig. 1m). Consistently, GABA also prevented viability loss in SH-SY5Y cells exposed to pro-inflammatory cytokines from LPS-stimulated BV2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 1i, j). Together, these results indicated that GABA attenuates microglial inflammatory responses and associated neurotoxicity, with 100 μmol/L chosen as the working concentration for subsequent in vitro experiments.

GABAARs mediated protective effects against neuroinflammation

GABA primarily exerts its effects via the ionotropic receptors, GABAARs and the metabotropic receptors, GABABRs30. To ensure selective activation of GABA receptors in suppressing neuroinflammation, the GABAAR antagonists picrotoxin (PTX) and bicuculline (BIC), as well as the GABABR antagonists CGP54626 or CGP36742, were administered prior to GABA treatment. It indicated that PTX, BIC, and CGP blocked the ability of GABA to reduce LPS-induced microglial activation in the substantia nigra (Fig. 2a) and elevated inflammatory cytokine levels in the midbrain (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Meanwhile, immunohistochemical staining showed that PTX, BIC, and CGP blocked the protective effect of GABA against LPS-induced loss of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)-positive cells in the substantia nigra and degeneration of striatal nerve fibers (Fig. 2b). Consistently, immunoblotting demonstrated that GABA reversed the LPS-induced decrease in TH protein levels in both regions, an effect that was also prevented by pretreatment with PTX, BIC, or CGP (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). In addition, PTX, BIC, and CGP blocked the beneficial effect of GABA on motor impairments in LPS-challenged mice, as demonstrated by the rotarod, pole, and wire hang tests (Fig. 2d–f).

To further support the in vivo observations, we next examined the effects of GABA in vitro. The ability of GABA to suppress the secretion of TNF-α and IL-6 from LPS-stimulated primary microglia was reversed by PTX, BIC, and CGP (Fig. 2g, h). Consistently, pretreatment with PTX, BIC, and CGP also attenuated the neuroprotective effect of GABA against the toxicity of conditioned medium from activated primary microglia on primary neurons (Fig. 2i, j). Similarly, in BV2 microglial cells, PTX, BIC, and CGP blocked the anti-inflammatory effect of GABA (Supplementary Fig. 2e, f) and attenuated the beneficial effect of GABA against the neurotoxicity of conditioned medium from stimulated BV2 cells to SH-SY5Y cells (Supplementary Fig. 2g, h). Furthermore, the ability of GABA to limit the upregulation of the inflammation-associated proteins iNOS and COX-2 in LPS-stimulated BV2 cells was attenuated in the presence of PTX, BIC, and CGP (Fig. 2k). Together, these data suggested that both types of GABA receptors contribute to the anti-inflammatory effects of GABA, and more interestingly, even GABAARs, which are ligand-gated ion channels, appear to mediate anti-inflammatory responses.

To further verify the involvement of GABAARs in the anti-inflammatory effect, the GABAAR-specific agonist muscimol was applied. Pretreatment with muscimol suppressed the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6) from LPS-stimulated primary microglia in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3a, b). Similarly, muscimol reduced TNF-α and IL-6 secretion (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b) and downregulated the inflammation-associated proteins iNOS and COX2 (Fig. 3c) in LPS-stimulated BV2 cells. Notably, all these effects were abolished by the GABAAR antagonists PTX and BIC. Furthermore, muscimol conferred a protective effect on primary neurons against the neurotoxicity induced by conditioned medium from LPS-stimulated primary microglia, an effect that was blocked by GABAAR antagonists (Fig. 3d, e). Consistently, a similar anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective action of GABAARs was observed in BV2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). Taken together, these findings demonstrated that GABAARs played a protective role in suppressing neuroinflammation and thereby alleviating its associated neurotoxicity to neuronal cells.

To further validate the anti-inflammatory effects of GABAARs in an LPS-induced PD mouse model, LPS-challenged mice were intraperitoneally administered with PTX (1 mg/kg) or BIC (1 mg/kg), followed by daily treatment with muscimol (1 mg/kg), as outlined in Supplementary Fig. 1a. Behavioral tests were performed on day 8 after LPS injection, followed by biochemical analyses of brain tissues. Muscimol attenuated LPS-induced microglial activation ipsilateral to the substantia nigra injection site (Fig. 3f), reduced the elevated mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-6, iNOS, and COX) (Supplementary Fig. 3e–h), and reversed the increased inflammatory cytokine levels in the midbrain (Supplementary Fig. 3i–j); notably, these effects were abolished by pretreatment with the GABAAR antagonists PTX or BIC. Immunohistochemical staining further revealed that muscimol restored the loss of TH+ cells in the substantia nigra and the degeneration of striatal nerve fibers induced by LPS (Fig. 3g). Consistently, immunoblotting showed that muscimol rescued the LPS-induced reduction of TH protein levels in both the substantia nigra and striatum, which was prevented by PTX or BIC administration (Fig. 3h). In addition, muscimol significantly improved motor impairments in LPS-challenged mice, as demonstrated by the rotarod, pole, and wire hang tests, whereas these benefits were mitigated by GABAAR antagonists (Fig. 3i–k). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that GABAAR-specific agonists exerted potent anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects in LPS-induced PD mouse models.

Activation of GABAARs exerted anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the nuclear translocation of NF-κB

Nuclear factor κB (NF-κB/p65) is a key mediator of LPS-induced glial inflammatory responses, during which NF-κB translocate from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, driving the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines and proteins31. To investigate whether GABAAR activation regulates the NF-κB inflammatory signaling pathway, we examined the nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity of NF-κB in LPS-challenged microglia following muscimol treatment. Primary microglia (Fig. 4a) and BV2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 4a) were pretreated with muscimol prior to LPS stimulation, and it showed that muscimol pretreatment inhibited LPS-induced nuclear localization of p65, an effect that was reversed by PTX. We further confirmed this by immunoblotting in BV2 cells. LPS stimulation markedly increased nuclear p65 levels while reducing cytoplasmic p65; muscimol pretreatment significantly counteracted this nuclear translocation, and the effect was abolished by the GABAAR antagonist PTX (Fig. 4b). Consistently, in vivo p65 staining of LPS-challenged mice revealed that the elevated number of nuclear p65+/Iba1+ cells per region of interest (ROI) and the increased nuclear/cytoplasmic ratio of p65 in Iba+ cells in the substantia nigra were reduced by muscimol, and these effects were reversed by PTX and BIC (Supplementary Fig. 4b), suggesting that GABAAR activation attenuated LPS-induced nuclear NF-κB signaling. Furthermore, western blot analysis demonstrated that muscimol administration rescued LPS-induced nuclear translocation of p65 in mouse midbrains, an effect inhibited by GABAAR antagonists (Fig. 4c). Taken together, these findings indicate that GABAAR stimulation blocks LPS-induced nuclear translocation of the NF-κB p65 subunit.

Previous studies have shown that LPS treatment increases p65 phosphorylation, thereby enhancing NF-κB transcriptional activity32. Here, we measured p65 phosphorylation and found that muscimol pretreatment attenuated LPS-induced p-p65 levels in both BV2 cells (Fig. 4d) and mouse midbrains (Fig. 4e), effects that were reversed by PTX. We further assessed NF-κB transcriptional activity using a cis-acting reporter assay in BV2 cells, which revealed that muscimol significantly reduced LPS-induced luciferase activity, an effect blocked by PTX (Fig. 4f). These results indicated that GABAAR activation suppressed LPS-induced NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity in microglia, thereby inhibiting microglial activation.

Stimulation of GABAARs mobilize phosphatases to dephosphorylate IκBα

IκBα retains NF-κB in an inactive state within the cytoplasm and serves as a key upstream regulator of NF-κB activation. Upon LPS stimulation, IκBα undergoes phosphorylation and subsequent proteasomal degradation, thereby permitting NF-κB dimers to translocate into the nucleus33. To determine whether IκBα phosphorylation contributes to the protective effect of muscimol against neuroinflammation, we examined its phosphorylation status in BV2 cells by western blot analysis. The results showed that LPS-induced elevation of phosphorylated IκBα (p-IκBα) and reduction of total IκBα were both mitigated by muscimol in a concentration-dependent manner. Moreover, these effects of muscimol were abolished by PTX pretreatment (Fig. 5a, b).

The phosphorylation status of IκBα is regulated by the balance between protein kinases and phosphatases34, such that dephosphorylation of IκBα by protein phosphatase 1 (PP1)35, protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A)36, or the calcium-dependent phosphatase calcineurin (CN; PP2B)37 prevents its proteasomal degradation and thereby suppresses NF-κB activation. First, we assessed the effects of muscimol (10 μmol/L) on CN, PP2A, and PP1 activities in BV2 cells. As shown in Fig. 5c–e, muscimol treatment markedly increased the activity of all three phosphatases. To examine whether the muscimol-induced reduction in p-IκBα involves these phosphatases, we applied FK506 (1 μmol/L) to inhibit CN, as well as low-dose (0.03 μmol/L) or high-dose (0.5 μmol/L) okadaic acid (OA) to selectively inhibit PP2A or PP1, respectively, in BV2 cells. The levels of p-IκBα and total IκBα were then assessed. As shown in Fig. 5f–g, inhibition of these phosphatases significantly diminished the ability of muscimol to suppress p-IκBα and to restore total IκBα levels following LPS stimulation. These findings suggest that activation of GABAARs might recruit phosphatases (CN, PP2A, and PP1) to dephosphorylate IκBα, thereby stabilizing it in the cytoplasm and restricting NF-κB activation.

Next, the role of one particular phosphatase, calcineurin, was further validated in LPS-challenged mice. Specifically, the suppressive effect of muscimol on IκBα phosphorylation and degradation in response to LPS stimulation was significantly diminished by FK506, a pharmacological inhibitor of calcineurin (Fig. 5h–i). Moreover, the inhibitory effect of muscimol on LPS-induced IκBα phosphorylation and degradation was abolished by the GABAAR antagonists PTX or BIC in the midbrain (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). Consistently, the muscimol-mediated reduction of nuclear p65 in the substantia nigra was reversed by FK506 (Fig. 5j). Both the decreased number of nuclear p65+/Iba1+ cells per ROI and the lowered nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio of p65 in microglia within the substantia nigra were also abolished by FK506 (Supplementary Fig. 5c). In line with these findings, the muscimol-induced suppression of p-p65 levels was also attenuated in the presence of FK506 (Fig. 5k). Pathological analysis further demonstrated that the muscimol-dependent inhibition of microgliosis, including reduced microglial proliferation (Fig. 5l) and decreased expression of proinflammatory cytokines such as iNOS and COX2 (qPCR, Supplementary Fig. 5d, e) as well as TNF-α and IL-6 (qPCR, Supplementary Fig. 5f, g; ELISA, Supplementary Fig. 5h, i), was effectively blocked by FK506 in midbrain. Immunohistochemical examination revealed that the protective effect of muscimol against DA neuron loss in the SNpc and dendritic degeneration in the striatum ipsilateral to the LPS injection was markedly diminished following FK506 pretreatment (Fig. 5m). These observations were further supported by western blot analysis showing that muscimol’s protective effect on TH protein levels was mitigated by FK506 (Supplementary Fig. 5j). At the behavioral level, the beneficial effects of muscimol on motor coordination deficits and reduced grip strength induced by LPS were significantly alleviated by FK506 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 5k–m). Notably, in LPS-challenged mice, FK506 treatment alone did not show significant effects on microglial activation (Supplementary Fig. 6a), pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c), dopaminergic neuron loss (Supplementary Fig. 6d, e), and motor function (Supplementary Fig. 6f–h) compared with the LPS group. Taken together, these findings indicate that the phosphatase, calcineurin, played a critical role in GABAAR-mediated anti-inflammation and neuroprotection actions.

GABAARs triggered Ca2+ release from intracellular stores by activating PLC

Next, we sought to investigate the functional relationship between GABAARs and the phosphatases calcineurin, PP2A, and PP1. Previous studies have suggested that these phosphatases can be activated directly or indirectly by elevated cytoplasmic Ca2+/calmodulin (Ca2+/CaM) levels38–40. To test this, we monitored intracellular Ca2+ dynamics using the Ca2+ indicator Fluo-4 AM by live-cell imaging, with ATP serving as a positive control for intracellular Ca2+ mobilization41. Our results showed that muscimol induced a robust increase in intracellular Ca2+ in both primary microglia (Fig. 6a) and BV2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Notably, this Ca2+ elevation was significantly abolished when muscimol was applied in the presence of PTX (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 7b).

Elevated intracellular calcium concentrations are primarily mediated by two mechanisms: release from intracellular stores such as the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-a process involving phospholipase C (PLC)-and extracellular Ca2+ influx.38. Interestingly, the muscimol-induced rise in intracellular Ca2+ was completely abolished when ER Ca2+ stores were depleted with thapsigargin (TG), a non-competitive inhibitor of Ca2+ ATPase, in both primary microglia (Fig. 6c) and BV2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Extracellularly, when cells were exposed to a Ca2+-free buffer, muscimol still elicited a significant intracellular Ca2+ increase in primary microglia (Fig. 6d) and BV2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 7d), indicating that muscimol primarily triggered Ca2+ release from the ER, consistent with classical PLC-dependent signaling. To further validate this mechanism, live-cell imaging with Fluo-4AM revealed that pretreatment with the PLC inhibitor U73122 effectively abolished muscimol-induced intracellular Ca2+ elevation in both primary microglia (Fig. 6e) and BV2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 7e). Consistently, the inhibitory effects of muscimol on LPS-induced p-IκBα upregulation and IκBα degradation were reversed by TG or U73122 in BV2 cells (Fig. 6f). In agreement with these findings, the muscimol-mediated suppression of p65 nuclear translocation was also diminished in the presence of TG or U73122 in both BV2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 7f) and primary microglia (Supplementary Fig. 7g). Taken together, these results suggested that GABAAR activation evoked Ca2+ release from ER stores via PLC signaling, thereby preventing NF-κB nuclear translocation.

To further substantiate the link between PLC activation and GABAARs, we employed an indirect approach by transfecting WSS-1 cells stably expressing GABAAR subunits with DsRed-E2GFP-tagged PH domain of PLCδ (DsRed-E2GFP-PHPLCδ). This fusion protein consists of DsRed, which provides stable red fluorescence for tracking PLC localization, and E2GFP, a Cl--sensitive EGFP mutant that enables real-time monitoring of chloride flux in living cells (Supplementary Fig. 7h). Confocal live-cell imaging revealed that muscimol treatment reduced E2GFP fluorescence intensity, indicating Cl- influx through activated GABAAR channels, an effect abolished by PTX (Fig. 6g, h). In parallel, muscimol treatment for 5 min significantly decreased the membrane-to-cytoplasm fluorescence ratio of DsRed (Fm/Fc) compared with untreated controls (Fig. 6g, i), suggesting that endogenous PLC activation and PIP2 depletion induced partial translocation of membrane-bound PHPLCδ (Fm) into the cytoplasm (Fc). Importantly, this muscimol-induced reduction in DsRed Fm/Fc ratio was reversed by PTX pretreatment (Fig. 6g, i). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that GABAAR activation exerted a metabotropic effect via the PLC/Ca2+/phosphatase signaling cascade, thereby promoting dephosphorylation of p-IκBα and attenuating NF-κB activation.

GABAARs physically interacted with PLCδ and primed its activation

We next sought to elucidate the molecular mechanism by which GABAARs trigger PLCδ activation. Since GABAARs are ligand-gated ion channels permeable to Cl-, and PLCδ is known to be autoinhibited by its X/Y linker—which contains a high density of negatively charged residues and is electrostatically repelled from the phospholipid membrane upon PLC recruitment to the PIP2-enriched membrane42—we initially hypothesized that Cl- influx through GABAARs might alter this repulsive electrostatic force, thereby relieving PLCδ autoinhibition (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Unexpectedly, muscimol-induced reductions in the Fm/Fc ratio of red fluorescence intensity (Supplementary Fig. 8b) and elevations in intracellular Ca2+ mobilization (Supplementary Fig. 8c) were not mitigated under extracellular Cl- depletion. These findings suggested that PLCδ activation downstream of GABAAR stimulation was independent of Cl- influx through the receptor channels.

Beyond classical ionotropic function, GABAARs can also engage in metabotropic signaling, influencing diverse cellular events2,41,43. So, here GABAARs might physically interact with PLC, and the conformational change triggered by GABAAR activation could displace X/Y linker from the catalytic site, leading to PLCδ activation. GABAARs are assembled from various subunit combinations, with the α1β2γ2 configuration being the most prevalent in the brain; other abundant assemblies include α2β3γ2 and α3β3γ224. Structurally, PLCδ isoforms share similarity with PRIP1 (PLC-related but catalytically inactive protein 1), which binds primarily to GABAAR β subunits and, to a lesser extent, γ2 subunits, but not to α1–6, δ, or ρ1 subunits44. We first confirmed the expression of the GABAAR-β2/3 subunit in the membrane fractions of our experimental model cells, BV2 cells and primary microglia (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Based on this, we initially examined whether PLCδ associates with the β2/3 subunits of GABAARs. Confocal microscopy revealed colocalization of PLCδ (green) with the β2 subunit of GABAARs (red) in cells (Fig. 7a). Consistently, co-immunoprecipitation assays in WSS-1 cells (Fig. 7b) and BV2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 8e) co-transfected with PLCδ-HA and GABAAR-β2-myc confirmed that PLCδ associated with the β2 subunit of GABAARs.

To further dissect these protein–protein interactions, GST-pulldown assays were carried out. It is well established that the intracellular loop between M3 and M4 of GABAAR subunits mediates their interactions with intracellular proteins. Therefore, purified GST-fusion proteins containing the intracellular TM3–4 loop of the GABAAR-β2 subunit (GST-β2) (Fig. 7c) were incubated with lysates of WSS-1 cells transfected with PLCδ-HA. The results revealed a clear association between PLCδ and the TM3–4 loop of the GABAAR-β2 subunit (Fig. 7d). To map the domains of PLCδ required for this interaction, we generated a series of constructs: full-length PLCδ tagged with HA at the C terminus, along with four truncated variants lacking the PH domain (ΔPH), EF-hands domain (ΔEF), X-Y linker (ΔXY), or C2 domain (ΔC2) (Fig. 7e). These constructs were expressed in WSS-1 cells, and cell lysates were incubated with purified GST-β2. The pulldown assays demonstrated that deletion of either the PH domain or the EF-hands domain markedly reduced the binding of PLCδ to the GABAAR-β2 subunit (Fig. 7f), indicating that the PH and EF-hands domains were primarily responsible for mediating this interaction.

To further investigate the impact of GABAAR activation on its interaction with PLCδ, WSS-1 cells were pretreated with muscimol and subjected to co-immunoprecipitation assays. The results showed that, compared with the untreated group, muscimol markedly reduced the association between PLCδ and the β2 subunit. This reduction was reversed by the GABAAR antagonists PTX and BIC (Fig. 7g), but not by the PLC inhibitor U73122 (Fig. 7h). Previous studies have demonstrated that agonist binding to GABAARs induces an anticlockwise motion in the extracellular domain, which is transmitted to the transmembrane and intracellular domains45. Consistent with this model, our data suggested that the conformational transition of GABAARs from the closed to open state might trigger PLCδ activation while concomitantly weakening its association with the receptor.

To further assess the critical role of PLCδ in GABAAR-mediated suppression of neuroinflammation, short hairpin RNA (shRNA) was employed to knock down PLCδ in primary microglia (Supplementary Fig. 8f). In the scrambled control group, muscimol treatment induced a marked elevation of intracellular Ca2+, whereas this response was obviously abolished in the PLCδ knockdown group (Fig. 8a). Similarly, muscimol pretreatment effectively restricted LPS-induced nuclear translocation of p65, an effect that was largely lost upon PLCδ silencing (Fig. 8b). Moreover, the muscimol-dependent downregulation of proinflammatory cytokine production, including TNF-α (Fig. 8c) and IL-6 (Fig. 8d), as measured by ELISA, was almost completely abolished, once the PLCδ expression was interfered. Taken together, these findings supported that PLCδ played a central role in GABAARs exerting anti-inflammatory effects.

Discussion

In this study, we identified a previously unrecognized mechanism by which GABAAR activation attenuates neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration, ultimately improving motor behavior in an LPS-induced Parkinson’s disease model. Specifically, we demonstrated that stimulation of GABAARs triggers PLC-dependent intracellular calcium release in microglia, which in turn activates phosphatases that dephosphorylate IκBα. This process effectively limits NF-κB nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity, thereby protecting neurons from LPS-induced inflammatory damage (Supplementary Fig. 9). Considering the central role of neuroinflammation in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases, GABAAR-mediated anti-inflammatory signaling may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for Parkinson’s disease (PD).

Persistent neuroinflammation is a major pathological component of PD. Although GABA treatment has been shown to attenuate inflammatory responses in murine microglia and TNF-α-challenged monocytes46,47, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Consistent with these findings, we demonstrated that GABA acts through GABAAR to suppress microglial activation and proinflammatory cytokine production in an LPS-induced PD model, thereby rescuing dopaminergic neuron loss and motor dysfunction. Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of GABAAR abolished both the anti-neuroinflammatory and neuroprotective effects of GABA.

Activation of GABAARs suppressed NF-κB (p65) nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity, thereby reducing the production of proinflammatory cytokines and proteins, and this inhibitory effect was abolished by the GABAAR antagonists PTX and BIC. In the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway, the central event is the stimulus-induced phosphorylation and subsequent degradation of IκBα, which sequesters NF-κB in the cytoplasm under steady-state conditions41, thus downregulation of p-IκBα level is considered a means of limiting NF-κB activation48. We found that muscimol, a specific GABAAR agonist, inhibited LPS-induced phosphorylation and degradation of IκBα, thereby attenuating NF-κB nuclear translocation and transcriptional activation. This is consistent with previous findings showing that dephosphorylation of IκBα suppresses the production of proinflammatory cytokines and proteins. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that PP1 and PP2A can dephosphorylate IκBα and regulate NF-κB signaling49–51, while activation of calcineurin (PP2B) by insulin-like growth factor-1 in astrocytes dephosphorylated IκBα at Ser32, thereby attenuating p65 nuclear translocation37. In line with these reports, our data suggest that PP1, PP2A, and PP2B are all involved in IκBα dephosphorylation downstream of GABAAR activation, providing new insight into the role of phosphatases in GABAergic regulation of NF-κB signaling.

Generally, intracellular Ca2+ elevation can arise from two main sources: release from intracellular stores or influx from the extracellular space via various ion channels38. In immature neurons, GABAAR activation has been reported to depolarize the membrane potential and thereby induce Ca2+ influx through voltage-gated calcium channels52. However, this mechanism was ruled out in our study on microglia, as muscimol still evoked Ca2+ mobilization even under Ca2+-free extracellular conditions. Instead, the Ca2+ increase in microglia following GABAAR activation was derived from thapsigargin (TG)-sensitive intracellular stores, since muscimol-induced Ca2+ mobilization was abolished in the presence of TG, an inhibitor that depletes intracellular Ca2+ stores. Moreover, this intracellular Ca2+ elevation appears to represent a pivotal event for subsequent phosphatase activation in microglia.

It is well established that the release of Ca2+ from intracellular stores such as the endoplasmic reticulum requires the involvement of phospholipase C (PLC). PLC is a core signal transduction enzyme that hydrolyzes membrane phospholipids into key second messengers, thereby transducing extracellular signals into various intracellular physiological responses. PLC hydrolyzes phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) at the plasma membrane, generating inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG). IP3 subsequently diffuses to the ER, where it binds to IP3 receptors (IP3Rs) and triggers Ca2+ release from the ER lumen53. In our study, we found that PLCδ participated in GABAAR-induced intracellular Ca2+ elevation and phosphatase activation, consistent with previous biochemical evidence demonstrating a direct interaction between GABAAR-β subunits and PLCδ54. Furthermore, our data revealed that PLCδ interacts with the GABAAR-β2 subunit through its PH/EF domains, while the intracellular TM3–4 loop of the β2 subunit mediates the association with PLCδ. Notably, the PH/EF domain of PLCδ not only serves as the binding interface for the β2 subunit but also exhibits high affinity for membrane phospholipids such as PIP2. This dual functionality likely stabilizes PLCδ in an autoinhibited conformation at the plasma membrane under resting conditions, where concurrent binding to the β2 subunit and membrane lipids restricts access of PIP2 to the catalytic site55. Nevertheless, given the diversity of GABAAR subunits expressed in microglia, and considering that PLCδ is only one among the 13 known human PLC isoforms56, the precise and potentially complex interactions between GABAAR subunits and distinct PLC isoforms remain to be fully elucidated.

The XY domain of PLCδ has long been recognized as the primary catalytic site for PIP2 cleavage; however, recent studies have revealed that the highly dynamic X/Y linker region functions as an allosteric regulatory switch by modulating access to the catalytic site53. Consistent with this notion, binding of the PH domain of PLCβ2 to membrane-anchored Rac induces electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged X/Y linker and the membrane, thereby displacing the linker from the catalytic site and relieving autoinhibition57. Based on this, we hypothesized that GABAAR activation–induced conformational changes in the β2 subunit may alter its interaction with the PH/EF domains of PLCδ, subsequently displacing the X/Y linker from the catalytic site and resulting in PLCδ activation. Indeed, the data showed that PLCδ can directly interact with the β2 subunit of GABAARs; upon stimulation with the GABAAR-specific agonist muscimol, we also observed a reduced association between PLCδ and GABAARs. This interaction appears to serve as a crucial on/off switch by which GABAAR agonists exert their anti-neuroinflammatory effects. Pharmacological inhibition of PLC with the selective inhibitor U73122 abolished LPS-induced NF-κB activation in microglia, as demonstrated by our results. Moreover, targeted genetic knockdown of PLCδ recapitulated this effect, suppressing both LPS-mediated NF-κB signaling and the subsequent release of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6. These findings collectively establish PLCδ as a critical intermediary in the GABAAR-mediated suppression of neuroinflammation.

On the other hand, given that PRIP1 primarily binds to GABAAR β subunits and shares a structural similarity with PLCδ44, it remains possible that conformational changes in the β2 subunit upon GABAAR activation may increase its affinity for PRIP1 while reducing its affinity for PLCδ, thereby promoting PLCδ dissociation from GABAARs. This possibility warrants further investigation.

In summary, our study uncovered a novel signaling pathway through which ionotropic GABAAR activation attenuates neuroinflammation, thereby providing new molecular targets for therapeutic intervention in PD. These findings enriched the theory that GABAergic system played an important role in mitigating inflammatory response in immune system, so that improved the performances in neurodegenerative diseases pathology. Characterization of its underlying mechanism had revealed a metabotropic-type signaling downstream of GABAARs, in addition to their canonical ionotropic signaling as chloride channels. Because the abnormal immune system and neuroinflammation are predominantly involved in the pathological process of PD, the present study represents one step toward the development of GABAAR-related anti-inflammation as a promising therapeutic measure against neurodegeneration in Parkinson’s disease (PD).

Methods

Chemicals and reagents

Lipopolysaccharides (LPS, L2630), GABA (A2129), and muscimol hydrobromide (G109) were purchased from sigma-Aldrich. Picrotoxin (PTX, HY-101391), bicuculline (BIC, HY-N0219), FK506 (HY-K0202), MG132 (HY-13259), thapsigargin (TG, HY-13433), U73122 (HY-13419), okadaic acid (OA, HY-N6785), muscimol (HY-N2313) were obtained from MedChemExpress (MCE). CGP54626 (CGP, 20121-1) was acquired from cayman Chemical.

Mice

Male C57BL/6J mice (8–12 weeks old) were supplied from the Laboratory Animal Center of Capital Medical University (Beijing, China). Mice were housed under standard conditions (20–22 °C, 12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water for 7 days prior to experiments. All the procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Capital Medical University (approval No. AEEI-2022-197) and conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Every effort was made to minimize adverse effects on the mice.

Cell culture and treatment

The BV2 microglial cells (1101MOU-PUMC000063, Cell Center of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, China), the SH-SY5Y cell line (CL0278, Fenghuis Biotechnology, China), and WSS-1 cell lines (CL0694, Fenghuis Biotechnology, China) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, C11995500BT, GIBCO, VA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10270106, GIBCO, VA, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (p1400, Solarbio, China) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

Primary mouse microglia were isolated from the cortices of newborn C57BL/6J mice (postnatal days 1–2) as previously described58. Briefly, cortical tissues were digested with 0.125% (w/v) trypsin supplemented with DNase I (0.1 mg/mL) for 20 min at 37 °C, and were seeded onto dishes coated with 0.1% poly-L-lysine (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA). Mixed glial cultures were maintained in DMEM/F12 (GIBCO, VA, USA) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin for 7–10 days. Microglia were then separated from astrocytes via treatment with 0.05% trypsin and plated on poly-L-lysine-coated dishes at appropriate densities for subsequent experiments.

Primary cortical neurons were prepared from C57BL/6J mice (embryonic days 14–16) as previously described59. In brief, cortical tissues were dissociated with 0.25% trypsin containing Dnase Ⅰ (0.2 mg/mL) for 15 min at 37 °C, and plated on poly-L-lysine-coated dishes. The neurons were incubated in DMEM supplemented with 5% FBS, 5% horse serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin mixture for 4–6 h, then switched to Neurobasal medium (GIBCO, 21103049) supplemented with L-glutamine (0.5 mM) and B27 (GIBCO) for 7–10 days prior to experimentation.

Induction and treatment of LPS-associated PD models

The mice were deeply anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg; Bela-Pharm) and xylazine (10 mg/kg; Bela-Pharm), then either LPS (2 μg/μL, 0.5 μL, infused at 0.1 μL/min) or PBS (0.5 μL) was unilaterally injected into the substantia nigra (SN) as described5,29. Stereotaxic coordinates for the right SN were: −3.1 mm AP, 1.2 mm ML, −5.1 mm DV from bregma. The needle was retained in place for 5 min before slow withdrawal. GABA (200 mg/kg, oral gavage) or muscimol (1 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection) was administrated 1 h before LPS injection, and PTX (1 mg/kg, i.p.), BIC (1 mg/kg, i.p.), CGP36742 (30 mg/kg, i.p.) or FK506 (5 mg/kg, i.p.) were given 0.5 h before LPS injection. The same dose was administered daily for the subsequent 8 days unless otherwise indicated (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Mice were anesthetized and intracardially perfused with 0.9% saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M PBS (PFA/PBS). Brains were removed, post-fixed in 4% PFA/PBS overnight, cryoprotected in 10–30% sucrose, embedded in OCT (SAKURA, Japan), and sectioned at a thickness of 40 or 10 μm.

For immunohistochemical staining, sections were permeabilized in 0.3% Triton X-100/PBS for 60 min and treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for 10 min to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. After PBS washes, sections were blocked with 10% goat serum in 0.3% TX-100/PBS for 60 min and incubated with a primary antibody against tyrosine hydroxylase (TH, 1:8000, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) overnight at 4 °C. Sections were then incubated with streptavidin-HRP (ZSGB-BIO, PV-9002) for 20 min and developed with DAB substrate (ZSGB-BIO, ZLI-9019).

For immunofluorescence staining in Supplementary Figs. 4b, 5a, sections were treated as follows: (1) permeabilization in 0.3% TX-100/PBS for 60 min; (2) endogenous peroxidase inhibition with 3% H2O2 for 10 min; (3) blocking with QuickBlock™ Blocking Buffer (Beyotime, P0260) for 60 min; (4) incubation with rabbit anti-NFκB/p65 (1:300, 10745-1-AP, Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C; (5) incubation with the HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:300, Jackson, 111-035-003) for 50 min; (6) tyramide signal amplification using FITC-conjugated tyramide (1:500, G1222, Servicebio) for 10 min; (7) microwave treatment was performed to strip primary antibodies; (8) incubation with rabbit anti-Iba-1 (1:500, 019-19741, Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Japan). (9) incubation with IRDy594-conjugated secondary antibody (1:400, LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA) for 50 min.

For the other immunofluorescence staining, sections or cells on coverslips were blocked with 10% goat serum and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: anti-TH (1:8000, Sigma, St.louis, MO, USA), anti-Iba-1 (1:500, 019-19741, Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Japan), anti-NFκB/p65 (1:300, 10745-1-AP, Proteintech). After PBS washes, samples were incubated with secondary antibodies: rabbit IRDy594, mouse IRDy488 or rabbit IRDy488 (1:400, LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA). Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Tokyo, Japan).

Cytokine analysis by ELISA

Supernatants from primary mouse microglia and BV2 cells were collected after in vitro stimulation. Midbrain tissues were homogenized in PBS and centrifuged with 5000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to collect supernatants. TNF-α and IL-6 levels were measured using mouse TNF-α ELISA kits (EM02, Bio-kits) and mouse IL-6 ELISA kits (EM05, Bio-kits) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from midbrain tissues using the RNAsimple Total RNA Kit (DP419, TIANGEN, China) and reverse-transcribed with HiScript III RT SuperMix (R323-01, Vazyme, China). qPCR amplification of TNF-α, IL-6, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and cyclooxygenase 2 (COX2) were performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme, China) on CFX96TM Real-Time System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA, USA). The primer pairs were as follows: GAPDH: forward (F) 5ʹ-GCCTTCCGTGTTCCTACC-3ʹ, reverse (R) 5ʹ-CCTCAGTGTAGCCCAAGATG-3ʹ; TNF-αː (F) 5ʹ-GGCACTCCCCCAAAAGATGG-3ʹ, (R) 5ʹ-TTGCTACGACGTGGGCTACAG-3ʹ; IL-6: (F) 5ʹ-ACCTGTCTATACCACTTCA-3ʹ, (R) 5ʹ-GCATCATCGTTGTTCATAC-3ʹ; iNOS: (F) 5ʹ-CATGCTACTGGAGGTGGGTG-3ʹ, (R) 5ʹ-CAGGATGTTGTAGCGCTGTG-3ʹ; COX2: (F) 5ʹ-ACATTGAAGACCAGGAGTAC-3ʹ, (R) 5ʹ-TGTCTGGTGAATGACTCAAC-3ʹ. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Tissues or cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (C-1053, APPLYGEN, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (P002, NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China). Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were harvested as total protein29. Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were isolated using the nucleus extraction kit (SN0020, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Protein concentrations were determined with BCA protein assay kit (23227, Thermo Fisher, MA, USA). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF or NC membranes, and blocked with 5% not-fat dried milk in TBST for 1 h60. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-TH (1:1000, T1298, Sigma-Aldrich), anti-β-Actin (1:5000, Beijing GuanXingYu, Beijing, China), anti-iNOS (1:1000, 13120, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-COX2 (1:1000, 12282, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-NF-κB/p65 (1:1000, 8242, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-Lamin-B1 (1:2000, 66095-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-p-p65 (1:1000, 3033, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-p-IκBα (1:600, bsm-52169R, Bioss), anti-IκBα (1:1000, 9242, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-HA (1:5000, 51064-2-AP, Proteintech), anti-myc (1:2000, 16286-1-AP, Proteintech). After TBST washes, membranes were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 1.5 h: HRP-conjugated mouse secondary antibody, HRP-conjugated rabbit secondary antibody, mouse IRDy800 or rabbit IRDy680 (1:10,000, LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA). Signals were detected using FluorChem FC3 (Proteinsimple, USA) or Odyssey Infrared Imaging system (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA).

Measurement of phosphatase activities

The enzymatic activities of Calcineurin (CN/PP2B), Protein Phosphatase 2A (PP2A), and Protein Phosphatase 1 (PP1) were determined using specific commercial colorimetric assay kits (Calcineurin: HL50042.1; PP2A: HL50042.3; PP1: HL50313.1; Shanghai Haling Biological Technology, China), following the manufacturers’ protocols. All assays are based on a common principle: the respective phosphatase dephosphorylates its highly specific substrate (RII phosphopeptide for CaN, a synthetic phosphopeptide for PP2A, and glycogen phosphorylase a for PP1), releasing inorganic phosphate. The free phosphate is then quantified by the malachite green dye reaction, with absorbance measured at 660 nm. All measured activities were normalized to the total protein concentration of the cell lysates and are expressed as a percentage relative to the control group.

Behavioral tests

Motor function was assessed using three behavioral tests before sacrifice. The mice were exposed to behavioral tests in a randomized order to avoid biases. The experimenters were blinded to treatment groups for all behavioral studies, and mice were acclimatized to the laboratory 30 min before tests began. For rotarod test, mice were placed on a rotating rod accelerating from 4 to 40 rpm over 5 min61. Each mouse received the tests for 3 times within an interval of 1 h and the mean latency was recorded for analysis. The mice were trained 2 sessions (6–5-min trials) in 2 days before formal test to acclimate them to the rotarod apparatus62. For pole test, mice were placed atop a vertical pole (50 cm height, 3 cm diameter). The latency of mice to climb from the top of the pole to the bottom was recorded for 3 times, and average values were computed for analysis. The mice received 2 training sessions in 2 days before formal test. 3 trials were performed with an interval of 1 h. For wire hang test, mice were placed on the back of standard wire cage lid, ensuring them grip the wires and turn upside down. The latency of mice falling off the wire grid was measured, and average values were computed from 2 trials62. Trials were stopped when the mice remained on the lid for 5 min.

Conditional medium (CM) test, MTT and LDH assays

Primary mouse microglia or BV-2 cells were pretreated with PTX, BIC or CGP for 30 min, followed by GABA or MUS for 30 min, and LPS for 12 h. CM was collected and applied to primary mouse neurons or SH-SY5Y cells. After 24 h, cell viability and cytotoxicity were assessed using lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and assays3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-Diphenyltetrazolium Bromide (MTT) colorimetric assay(C0017, Beyotime). For LDH, 120 μL supernatant was incubated with 60 μL working solution for 30 min, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm. For MTT, cells were incubated with MTT (5 mg/mL) for 4 h, followed by DMSO addition to dissolve formazan crystals. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm.

Luciferase assay

BV2 cells were co-transfected with pNF-κB-Luc and pCMV-renilla (10:1) using PEI and seeded into 96-well plates. After 24 h, cells were treated with PTX (10 min), muscimol (30 min), and LPS (6 h). Luciferase activity was measured using Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (11402ES60, YEASEN). The pNF-κB-Luc was NF-κB activity reporter plasmid, which contains firefly luciferase (luc) gene and multiple copies of the NF-κB consensus sequence fused to a TATA-like promoter region from the HSV-TK promoter. The pCMV-renilla was served as control plasmid, which contains renilla reniformis luciferase gene under the control of CMV promoter.

Intracellular Ca2+ imaging

Cells were incubated with 2 μM Fluo-4 AM (S1060, Beyotime, China) for 30 min at 37 °C in recording buffer (10 mM HEPES PH 7.35, 156 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 1.25 mM KH2PO4, 10 mM D-glucose, 2 mM CaCl2)54. After washing, cells were treated with muscimol in absence or presence of PTX, TG, U73122, Ca2+, or Cl-. Fluorescence was recorded at 488 nm using an Olympus IX83 microscope and analyzed with ImageJ.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Cells were co-transfected with PLCδ-HA and GABAAR-β2-myc using PEI. After 24 h, cells were treated with muscimol in absence or presence of PTX. The cells were lysed with lysis buffer on ice for 30 min, then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were incubated with 2 μg anti-myc (60003-2-Ig, proteintech) or anti-HA (66006-2-Ig, proteintech) antibody overnight at 4 °C, then precipitated with protein A/G magnetic beads (HY-K0202, MedChemExpress) for 4–6 h at 4 °C. Precipitates were washed and western blot was performed as previous described. The primary antibodies were as follows: anti-HA (1:5000, 51064-2-AP, proteintech) and anti-myc (1:1000, 16286-1-AP, proteintech).

GST-pull-down assay

The intracellular loop between TM3-TM4 of GABAAR-β2 subunit was cloned into pGEX-4T-1 vector for GST fusion protein expression59,63. The plasmid was transformed into Escherichia coli strain BL21 (CB105-01, TIANGEN) and amplificated to exponential phase (OD600 = 0.6–0.8). IPTG (0.1 mM) was added into bacterial fluid for 4–6 h at 24 °C for expression of GST- β2. The bacteria were collected by centrifuging at 5000 × g, 4 °C for 8 min, then were ultrasonicated for 5 min at 350 W 2 s/2 s in 0.1 M PBS containing 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.01 mg/mL Dnase Ⅰ, 0.25 mg/mL lysozyme, and 0.1 mM PMSF. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g, 4 °C for 60 min and supernatants were harvested. The glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (17075601, cytiva) were added into supernatants rotating for 8–16 h at 4 °C. After washing 5 times with PBS, the purified GST-β2 was obtained. Protein concentrations were determined by the Coomassie blue assay.

The plasmids containing PLCδ-HA, PLCδ-ΔPH-HA, PLCδ-ΔEF-HA, PLCδ-ΔXY-HA, or PLCδ-ΔC2-HA were transfected into WSS-1 cells, and the lysates were incubated with purified GST-β2 for 16 h at 4 °C. After washing with lysate buffer for 5 times, the glutathione Sepharose 4B beads were mixed with 1× loading buffer for 10 min at 95 °C, and resolved using SDS-PAGE. The NC membranes transfected proteins were incubated with anti-HA antibody (1:5000, 51064-2-AP, Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated rabbit secondary antibody for 1.5 h and detected with FluorChem FC3 (Proteinsimple, USA).

Fluorescent imaging of DsRed-E2GFP-PHPLCδ

WSS-1 cells were transfected with DsRed-E2GFP-PHPLCδ for 24 h and incubated with 2 μM Calcein Blue AM (65-0855, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at 37 °C in recording buffer (10 mM HEPES PH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 5 mM D-glucose, 2 mM CaCl2). Cells were administrated with muscimol in absence or presence of PTX or Cl-. Calcein blue, E2GFP, or DsRed emission was collected at 405, 488, or 594 nm respectively, and images were captured every 30 s using confocal microscope (Leica Micro systems, Tokyo, Japan).

AAV-MG1.2 transduction

AAV-MG1.2 (AAV9-derived) was used to deliver siRNA in primary microglia. The tandem sequences used were as follows: scrambled 1–2 (GAGGACATCCGTCGGTCAATA-GGCGTAGATATCCCTATGACT) and PLCδ-KD 1–2 (GCAGGAATCTCGCAAGGTCAT-GACTTTATCGGCCAGAGTACT), combined with some skeleton and complementary sequence. Scrambled and PLCδ-KD sequences were packaged into AAV using HEK293 cells64. After 96 h, cells were collected and the virus particles were purified using Universal virus concentration kit (C2901M, Beyotime). The primary microglia were transduced with AAV-MG1.2 for 2 days. Then the cultured medium was changed, and the microglia were used after 3 days.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as the means ± standard error of mean (SEM) at least three independent experiments. Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA using GraphPad Prism software (version 6; GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA). The values were considered to be statistically significant at p <0.05.
