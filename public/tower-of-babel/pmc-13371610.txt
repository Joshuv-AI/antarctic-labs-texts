Early postoperative treprostinil in for severe pulmonary arterial hypertension in a high-risk pediatric patient with complete atrioventricular septal defect: a case report
Authors: Sha Zhao, Zhong-yuan Lu, Pantao Zhou, Li Xiong, Xiao-Bin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPerioperative management of pediatric patients with congenital heart disease (CHD) and severe pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) remains challenging because of limited evidence-based guidelines.Case presentationWe present the case of a 14-year-old female with a complete atrioventricular septal defect (Rastelli type A) and a single atrium with a biventricular connection. The preoperative pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) was 6.3 Wood units after oxygen inhalation. Following a multidisciplinary evaluation, fenestrated patch closure was performed to reduce the right ventricular afterload. With standard perioperative management in place, early intravenous Treprostinil was initiated and carefully titrated. Thereafter, the patient maintained hemodynamic stability, was successfully extubated, and was discharged in stable condition.ConclusionsEarly intravenous treprostinil, combined with individualized surgical protection, may help maintain hemodynamic stability and support recovery in high-risk pediatric patients with CHD-associated PAH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05954-y.

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common congenital malformation worldwide [1, 2]. In patients with unrepaired shunt lesions, longstanding left-to-right shunting may lead to progressive pulmonary vascular remodeling, increasing pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) and the development of severe pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) [3, 4]. Complete atrioventricular septal defect (CAVSD) is one such lesion in which delayed diagnosis or repair may result in advanced pulmonary vascular disease [5, 6]. In this setting, pulmonary arterial pressure may become near-systemic or even supra-systemic, substantially increasing the perioperative risks of PAH crisis, acute right ventricular failure, and low cardiac output syndrome [7, 8]. Perioperative management of severe CHD-associated PAH remains challenging.

Current perioperative pharmacological approaches for PAH include endothelin receptor antagonists (ERAs), phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (PDE5i), soluble guanylate cyclase stimulators, inhaled nitric oxide (iNO), milrinone, and prostacyclin analogs [9, 10]. However, some agents have delayed onset, while others require specialized delivery systems or careful titration in unstable patients [11, 12].

Treprostinil, a prostacyclin analog available in multiple formulations, has pharmacologic features that may be advantageous in high-risk perioperative settings. However, robust evidence for early postoperative treprostinil administration in pediatric patients with CAVSD or other high-risk CHD-associated PAH remains limited [13]. Such patients often have significant hemodynamic fragility and limited right ventricular reserves. This makes precise dosing, optimal route selection, and timing crucial.

We present a pediatric case of CAVSD with severe PAH. This patient underwent protective surgical repair followed by early postoperative intravenous Treprostinil as part of comprehensive perioperative management. We aim to describe the clinical decision-making process, hemodynamic changes, and perioperative management experience in this case.

Case presentation

A 14-year-old girl (50 kg), without documented clinical features of Down syndrome, presented with a 5-year history of exertional dyspnea and was referred for surgical evaluation after outpatient transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) suggested a CAVSD. On admission, her blood pressure was 84/64 mmHg and SpO₂ was 74% on room air. She was alert but had cyanotic lips and generalized cyanosis without differential saturation. Cardiac auscultation revealed a systolic blowing murmur at the apex, without peripheral edema. Arterial blood gas analysis showed severe hypoxemia, with a PaO₂ of 41 mmHg. Electrocardiography demonstrated sinus rhythm with right axis deviation and right ventricular hypertrophy. Preoperative TTE demonstrated a Rastelli type A CAVSD with a single atrium, absent interatrial septum, and a 15-mm inlet ventricular septal defect, together with a common atrioventricular valve and moderate regurgitation. Bidirectional shunting with predominant left-to-right flow was present across both the atrial- and ventricular-level defects, and the pulmonary artery was dilated (Fig. 1A and Fig. 1B). Chest computed tomography (CT) showed dilatation of the main pulmonary artery, prominent bilateral pulmonary vasculature, and multiple faint perivascular small patchy opacities in both lungs (Fig. 2A and Fig. 2B). Right heart catheterization confirmed severe pulmonary vascular disease with near-systemic pulmonary arterial pressure. The mean pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) was 70 mmHg, pulmonary vascular resistance was 13.5 Wood units, and the pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio (Qp/Qs) was 0.93 before oxygen testing. After administration of 100% oxygen, PVR decreased to 6.3 Wood units and Qp/Qs increased to 2.91. Given that the post-oxygen PVR remained above 6 Wood units, direct complete closure was considered high risk. Therefore, the “treat–repair–treat” strategy was adopted, including preoperative bosentan in addition to supportive medical therapy [14].

On September 16, 2023, under general anesthesia and cardiopulmonary bypass, the patient underwent CAVSD repair, pericardial window creation, and epicardial pacing lead placement. A small atrial septal fenestration was intentionally retained to reduce right ventricular afterload. After surgery, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU). On ICU admission, Swan–Ganz catheter monitoring showed a pulmonary artery pressure of 98/61 mmHg and a systemic blood pressure of 100/62 mmHg, indicating near-systemic postoperative pulmonary arterial pressure. Despite deep sedation, mechanical ventilation, and vasoactive/inotropic support, pulmonary artery pressure remained near systemic, consistent with persistent severe postoperative pulmonary arterial hypertension and a high risk of PAH crisis. Immediate postoperative TTE confirmed overall defect closure and a small residual atrial-level fenestration with right-to-left shunting. Representative bedside transthoracic echocardiographic clips obtained in the ICU are provided as Additional files 1–4.

After confirming adequate systemic perfusion and a mean arterial pressure higher than pulmonary artery pressure, intravenous treprostinil was initiated at 5 ng/kg/min and gradually titrated to 10 ng/kg/min at 2 h, 15 ng/kg/min at 6 h, and 20 ng/kg/min at 10 h under close hemodynamic monitoring (Fig. 3). The patient remained on milrinone at 0.6 µg/kg/min with vasoactive support as needed, while treprostinil was maintained at 20 ng/kg/min. Planned extubation was initially postponed because of pulmonary artery pressure fluctuations during sedation lightening. After withdrawal of sedation and analgesia except for dexmedetomidine, she was successfully extubated to high-flow nasal oxygen, after which pulmonary artery pressure remained consistently below mean arterial pressure (Fig. 2) (Table 1).

The patient was transferred from the ICU to the ward on September 20, 2023, and oral bosentan 5 mg once daily was subsequently initiated. Before discharge, SpO₂ was 90%. Pre-discharge TTE showed right ventricular hypertrophy, mild left atrial enlargement, a residual 6-mm atrial septal defect with right-to-left shunting, dilated pulmonary arteries, an estimated pulmonary artery pressure of 70 mmHg, mild-to-moderate eccentric mitral regurgitation, mild tricuspid and aortic regurgitation, and preserved left ventricular systolic function. Postoperative computed tomography angiography showed marked right ventricular wall thickening, particularly in the right ventricular outflow tract, with RVOT narrowing, persistent pulmonary hypertension, residual endocardial cushion defects, and multiple faint pulmonary opacities (Fig. 2C and Fig. 2D). This RVOT narrowing was considered likely related to postoperative right ventricular hypertrophy and remodeling in the setting of persistent severe pulmonary hypertension.

At the 1-year follow-up, the patient had returned to school. Repeat TTE showed persistent right ventricular hypertrophy, an 8-mm residual atrial-level shunt with bidirectional flow, moderate-to-severe eccentric mitral regurgitation, mild eccentric tricuspid, pulmonary, and aortic regurgitation, and an estimated right ventricular systolic pressure of > 50 mmHg based on the tricuspid regurgitation jet velocity (Fig. 1C and Fig. 1D).

Discussion

CAVSD (Rastelli type A) with a single atrium and survival into adolescence is unusual [15]. Markedly elevated PVR limits surgical timing and increases perioperative mortality. In this case, a 14-year-old with CAVSD and single atrium had a preoperative PVR of 13.5 WU. This PVR reduced to 6.3 WU with oxygen administration but was still in the high-risk range. Fenestrated patch closure combined with early postoperative IV treprostinil in this settings suggests that individualized surgical strategies coupled with refined perioperative management may help selected high-risk patients undergo intervention beyond the traditional “no-go” threshold [16–18]. When PVR is at least 5 WU but partially reversible, guidelines recommend a protective shunt rather than complete closure [19, 20]. Ivy et al. used 6 WU as their high-risk threshold and emphasized cautious decision-making when PVR remained near this level despite partial reversibility [21].

In this case, preoperative PVR decreased from 13.5 to 6.3 WU after oxygen, but remained within the high-risk range. Therefore, fenestrated patch closure with a small atrial fenestration was selected to provide decompression of the right heart should right-sided pressure rise acutely. This “treat-repair-treat” approach-preoperative bosentan therapy, protective repair, and postoperative targeted therapy (IV treprostinil plus milrinone) - may have contributed to early postoperative stabilization and was associated with a favorable short-term postoperative course. The approach illustrates the synergy between surgical and pharmacologic management in a multidisciplinary setting for high-risk CHD with PAH. This strategy has been reported to reduce early postoperative complications in PAH patients with ASD and allows transcatheter closure if the pulmonary vascular status improves [22, 23]. However, in our patient, postoperative pulmonary artery pressure remained near systemic despite conventional support, prompting consideration of further escalation of pulmonary vasodilator therapy.

Perioperative management of PAH in pediatric patients with CHD commonly includes inhaled nitric oxide and milrinone. Because inhaled nitric oxide was not available in our center, this patient was initially managed with deep sedation, mechanical ventilation, vasoactive support, and milrinone before IV Treprostinil was cautiously introduced. Both act rapidly and can be titrated, but iNO requires costly specialized equipment, acts only on ventilated lung units, and may cause rebound PAH upon abrupt withdrawal. Milrinone improves right ventricular function but is less effective when PAP approaches systemic levels. Other targeted agents, such as oral ERAs (e.g., bosentan) and PDE-5i (e.g., sildenafil), have delayed onset and postoperative administration constraints. Furthermore, IV sildenafil is unavailable in mainland China [20]. Short-acting prostacyclin analogs (e.g., iloprost and epoprostenol) require continuous infusion with frequent adjustments, which may lead to unstable plasma levels, rebound risk, and a higher infection risk from prolonged central venous access [24].

However, treprostinil addresses several of these limitations. Its longer half-life (approximately 4 h) allows for more stable plasma concentrations and a lower rebound risk. By activating IP receptors, it induces pulmonary vasodilation, reduces PVR and right ventricular afterload, improves cardiac output, and has antiproliferative and anti-inflammatory effects [24]. Multiple administration routes enable perioperative IV titration, with the possibility of later transition to subcutaneous, inhaled, or oral therapy. Published clinical reports show that early IV treprostinil with accelerated titration in high-risk postoperative PAH patients may lower PAP, support right ventricular function, and facilitate safe weaning [25]. Gradual protocols typically start at 1.25–2.5 ng/kg/min with stepwise increases, whereas accelerated rescue regimens may start at 5 ng/kg/min and reach 20 ng/kg/min within hours under invasive hemodynamic monitoring [26]. Treprostinil is often combined with PDE-5i, ERAs, and inotropes for synergistic PVR reduction and right ventricular support [25, 27]. In our patient, the timing of treprostinil initiation was guided not only by persistently near-systemic PAP, but also by the need to create a safer hemodynamic window for sedation reduction and extubation.

In this case, early postoperative treprostinil was introduced when conventional perioperative measures alone were considered insufficient to address persistently elevated pulmonary artery pressure and the anticipated risk of deterioration during sedation weaning and extubation. Its use was temporally associated with maintenance of hemodynamic stability and subsequent clinical recovery. However, because this is a single case report and multiple perioperative interventions were applied concurrently, it is not possible to determine with certainty what the patient’s course would have been without treprostinil or to isolate its specific contribution from the overall multimodal management strategy. At one-year follow-up, reassessment was limited to echocardiography, and repeat cardiac catheterization was not performed. Therefore, the residual severity of pulmonary hypertension, the need for escalation of PAH-targeted therapy, and the hemodynamic significance of the residual mitral regurgitation could not be fully defined invasively [28].

Conclusions

In high-risk pediatric patients with CAVSD and severe pulmonary arterial hypertension, perioperative management may benefit from individualized surgical protection, multidisciplinary coordination, and careful multimodal cardiopulmonary support. In this case, early postoperative IV treprostinil was used as part of a comprehensive management strategy and may represent a feasible adjunctive rescue option when severe postoperative PAH persists despite initial supportive measures. However, this single case does not establish the independent efficacy of treprostinil. Further multicenter studies are needed to clarify the optimal initiation, titration, and combination strategies for treprostinil in pediatric perioperative CHD-associated PAH and to better define its role in pediatric perioperative CHD-associated PAH and support more standardized clinical practice.
