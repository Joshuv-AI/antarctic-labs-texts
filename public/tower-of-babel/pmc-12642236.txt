Hospitalizations and inpatient resource consumption of patients suffering from chronic disease – Past trend and forecast for Switzerland
Authors: Daria Bukanova-Berend, Thomas Campbell-James, Justus Vogel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic diseases are an increasing burden for Swiss healthcare. Demographic change aggravates the problem, with an ageing, multi-morbid population increasing the need for care while also depleting the healthcare workforce. Originally developed to assess the structural quality of and access to primary care, Potentially Avoidable Hospitalizations (PAHs) are an OECD-metric employed as an indicator of inpatient burden for various chronic diseases. So far, evidence on future PAH-burden remains limited.Aims(1) To assess the evolution of PAHs and associated resources (bed capacities, inpatient healthcare expenditures) in Switzerland for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, congestive heart failure (CHF), hypertension and diabetes between 2012 and 2022, (2) to forecast PAHs until 2032, based on expected demographic change and (3) to explore methodologically how changes in primary care physician (PCP)-supply and countermeasures might influence future PAH-development.MethodsWe identified PAHs in retrospective routine hospital data and derived numbers of occupied hospital beds, based on cases’ length of stay, and inpatient healthcare expenditures, based on diagnosis-related group payments. We utilized population forecasts for Switzerland to extrapolate PAH-volume in the base scenario. Additionally, we predicted via linear regression how reduced ageing-induced PCP-availability might affect PAHs. Finally, we explored the potential impact of countermeasures, optimal Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy (GDMT)-implementation, for CHF and COPD.ResultsThe number of PAHs across all five chronic diseases increased from 30.1 to 40.2 thousand cases between 2012 and 2019 (+ 34%) and is projected to rise to 54.9 thousand cases until 2032 (+ 37%). We project PAH-volume to increase by an additional 11% by 2032 due to changes in PCP-supply. By 2032, optimal GDMT-implementation has the potential to reduce CHF- and COPD-related PAH-volumes by 31% (95% CI: 22.23–37.60%) and 14% (95% CI: 2.17–26.02%), respectively.ConclusionsOur study assesses cumulative PAH-trends and is the first to forecast future burden in Switzerland. A strong rise in PAHs and associated inpatient resources was registered for the last decade, with accelerating growth expected throughout the next. A depleting healthcare workforce may exacerbate burden. Optimal GDMT-implementation could potentially curb increases in PAHs. However, methods to reach optimal implementation require further research and policy efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13687-5.

Introduction

Accounting for 79.4% of total healthcare costs, almost a third of Switzerland’s population suffers from at least one chronic disease [1, 2], placing significant pressure on the Swiss health system. Further, the number of people aged 65 years and older is forecasted to increase by 32% over the next ten years [3]. Said ageing of the population exacerbates the need for care for chronic diseases as these tend to emerge and worsen with age. Moreover, ageing also affects the supply of healthcare services, as it depletes the (healthcare) labour force. This, in turn, might increase the number of hospitalizations subsequent to suboptimal outpatient care [4, 5]. Chronic disease patients require long-term medical supervision and treatment. In Switzerland, chronic disease management is typically provided by primary care physicians (PCPs). Specialists and inpatient treatment are usually involved only in case of exacerbations - acute worsening of chronic conditions.

The prevalence of chronic diseases in Switzerland has been increasing steadily, particularly as the population ages. According to data from the Swiss Monitoring System of Addiction and Noncommunicable diseases (MonAM), the prevalence for cardiovascular diseases, diabetes and respiratory diseases – such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic bronchitis, and emphysema – has risen over time [6]. In Switzerland, recent scientific publications have primarily focused on assessing the health burden posed by chronic diseases [7–10]. They reported a rising number of hospitalizations due to asthma, COPD, hypertension, and congestive heart failure (CHF) for the period between 1998 and 2018. Furthermore, recent research has investigated factors associated with the evolution of hospitalization trends, highlighting a negative association between the number of hospitalizations and access to primary care services [4, 5]. The effectiveness of interventions in alleviating clinical, epidemiological and financial burdens of chronic diseases remains an ongoing focus of research efforts. An aim of such interventions, optimal implementation of Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy (GDMT), which in itself is undergoing increased investigation, has been shown to effectively reduce the risk of hospitalization in both CHF and COPD [11–13].

Given the current state of research, our study is the first to quantify both health and inpatient financial burden associated with the hospitalizations of chronically ill patients. Further, in addition to the retrospective analysis, it is the first to forecast how this burden is likely to develop due to ageing of the Swiss population and its healthcare workforce. Furthermore, it integrates findings on optimal GDMT-implementation, allowing us to explore its potential impact on alleviating pressure on the Swiss health system. In line with the abovementioned aims, we address the following research questions:

To address our research questions and quantify the burden of chronic diseases for the hospital sector, we utilize avoidable hospital admissions, often referred to as Potentially Avoidable Hospitalizations (PAHs). This metric has been defined by the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) and commonly serves as an indicator of the availability, accessibility, and quality of primary care services [5, 14]. In recent studies, PAHs are employed to assess the burden of chronic diseases, particularly in relation to the supply of primary care services [7–9]. Additionally, this metric is utilized to compare the performance of outpatient care both across countries and across administrative divisions within one country, such as the cantons in Switzerland [14–16].

To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to comprehensively measure the health and inpatient financial burden of PAHs across all chronic conditions defined by the OECD. While prior research has focused on individual diseases, our results provide a holistic view of five chronic conditions, offering valuable insights to policymakers and healthcare providers.

Methods

Data

PAH definition and data source

We adopted the definition of PAHs from the OECD from 2022 for asthma, COPD, CHF, hypertension, and diabetes [17]. The main inclusion criteria were specific principal diagnoses, described as diagnosis codes as per definition in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10-WHO [18]. The main exclusion criteria were: (1) patients aged 15 and younger which we extended to 19 and younger to only include adults (age bands in our data set are in 5-year steps, e.g., 10–14, 15–19, 20–24, etc.), (2) inpatient death, (3) admission due to transfer-in from another acute care institution, (4) obstetric hospitalization, (5) same day/day only cases, (6) selected ICD-codes listed as secondary diagnosis (only relevant for asthma and COPD), and (7) selected cardiac procedures for the indications CHF, and hypertension (see full list of inclusion (principal diagnoses) and exclusion criteria (secondary diagnoses) in the Table 1 in the supplementary material).

To identify PAHs, we utilized retrospective routine hospital data as part of the Medical Statistics dataset, which we obtained from the Swiss Federal Statistical Office (FSO). Our sample covers the period from 2012 to 2022 and included descriptive case level data such as sex, age group (5-year bands), residence (Swiss or non-Swiss residents), area of residence (at the level of MedStat regions which are larger than zip-code areas but smaller than cantons), inpatient length of stay in days, and Swiss Diagnosis Related Group (DRG) codes. In addition, per case, the principal diagnosis and up to 49 secondary diagnoses (comorbidities) were provided, along with one main treatment and up to 99 additional treatments, described via procedure codes as defined in the Swiss Classification of Operations and Procedures (CHOP) [19].

Apart from the exclusion criteria listed above, the OECD defines 141 cardiac procedures using ICD-9-CM procedure codes as exclusion criteria for CHF and hypertension (see Table 2 in the supplementary material). Of these, 36 cardiac procedures had direct equivalents in both the ICD-9-CM and CHOP catalogues (e.g., “Operations on papillary muscle” is coded as 3531 in ICD-9-CM and Z35.31 in the CHOP). The remaining 105 ICD-9-CM codes were manually matched with CHOP-equivalents, based on the procedure descriptions. To ensure accuracy, we accounted for annual updates in the Swiss CHOP catalogues and used all editions issued between 2012 and 2022 to maximize accuracy of our matching.

Previous publications did not exclude cases involving cardiac procedures [15, 16] or relied on broader exclusion criteria, such as excluding all CHOP codes from entire subchapters (e.g., any CHOP codes starting with 35, 36 or 37) [9]. Since the OECD continuously refines their PAH definition, these discrepancies may partially occur from variations between different versions of the OECD definition guidelines. With our detailed matching approach, we aimed to follow the most current OECD definition closely. The final list of matched CHOP-codes is provided in Table 3 in the supplementary material.

Inpatient healthcare expenditures

To calculate the inpatient healthcare expenditures associated with PAHs, we utilized the Swiss DRG catalogues issued between 2012 and 2022, which we obtained from the website of the SwissDRG AG, responsible for DRG-calculation at system level [20]. In the DRG system, each case is assigned a relative cost weight, which is adjusted if a case’s length of inpatient stay is (1) below or (2) above a certain threshold, (3) if the patient was admitted as a transfer from another hospital or (4) discharged as a transfer to another hospital. Such adjustments then yield the effective cost weight relevant for billing [21]. The Swiss DRG catalogues hold all information to calculate effective cost weights, namely the relative cost weight per DRG, the first day of cost-weight reduction, the first day of cost weight increase, the volume of daily reductions or increases, and the volume of reductions for transfers. Note that we did not consider cost weight reductions for patients that were admitted as transfers from other hospitals (transfer-ins) as we excluded these cases as per the OECD exclusion criteria for PAHs (cf. above).

To calculate the flat rate payment of a DRG, the effective cost weight is multiplied by individual hospital base rates, representing the price for a DRG with cost weight 1.0 [21]. As we were not able to identify individual hospitals in our dataset, we used cantonal reference tariffs, i.e., the base rate a canton pays for hospitals not part of the cantonal hospital list, as proxies [21]. We multiplied a patient’s effective cost weight with the reference tariff of the canton in which the patient resided.

To this end, we manually collected reference tariffs for each canton for the years 2012 to 2022 (for sources see Table 4 in the supplementary material). Twelve cantons (Appenzell Innerrhoden, Bern, Geneva, Nidwalden, Obwalden, St. Gallen, Schaffhausen, Solothurn, Schwyz, Vaud, Zug, and Zurich) publish reference tariffs for university hospitals and non-university hospitals separately. The remaining cantons apply the same reference tariffs regardless of the hospital type. We collected reference tariffs accordingly. Since our dataset does not specify whether a hospital is a university hospital or not, we calculated a range for inpatient healthcare expenditures, depicting the two extremes if (1) all cases had been treated at non-university hospitals, and (2) all cases had been treated at university hospitals in cantons where this distinction applied. Where reference tariffs for a specific year were unavailable, we substituted them with tariffs from the most recent available year. Here, we assumed that if no tariff was published, the most recent was still valid. The list of reference tariffs is available in Table 5 in the supplementary material.

Population dataset for base year and demographic change

For the two base years of our PAH-projection, 2019 and 2022, we employed the FSO population dataset “Demographic balance by age and canton” [3]. It includes the number of inhabitants by age (provided as a continuous variable), sex, and canton. To match the age variable in the Medical Statistics dataset, we converted the continuous variable into 5-year age bands.

Additionally, we utilized FSO population forecasts for the demographic base scenario of our PAH projection. The FSO outlines three development scenarios covering the period from 2020 to 2050 [22]. For our study, we relied on the “Reference scenario AR-00-2020” for the period 2023 to 2032. This reference scenario is based on population trends observed in recent decades. The dataset includes the number of inhabitants by age group (same 5-year age bands as in the Medical Statistic dataset), sex and canton.

To determine the density of PCPs for our analysis, we utilized a dataset on working physicians from the Swiss Medical Association (FMH). This dataset includes the number of working general practitioners and outpatient internists, which we define as PCPs [23]. Our sample included anonymized data at physician level, including sex, age, age group (5-year age bands), and canton of service provision, covering the period from 2012 to 2022.

Modelling approach

Past trend

To answer our research questions, we calculated the number of PAHs per chronic disease for each year between 2012 and 2022. To this end, we applied the inclusion and exclusion criteria for each chronic condition, described above and in the supplementary material (see Tables 1, 2 and 3 in the supplementary material).

Scenario extensions

In addition to the base scenario, we developed two exploratory extension scenarios. First, we modelled the influence of the change in demographic structure on the quantity, or supply, of healthcare services and how this affects the number of PAHs. Second, we explored potential effects of countermeasures on the number of PAHs, namely enhanced adherence to clinical practice guidelines resulting in optimal GDMT-implementation by PCPs treating patients suffering from COPD or CHF.

With the second exploratory scenario extension, we accounted for the fact that according to the OECD definition [17], PAHs are potentially avoidable given high-quality outpatient care structures. Beyond assessment of the efficacy of individual therapies, the effects of optimal, cross-therapy implementation of GDMT on clinical, epidemiological and health economic measures of outcome are undergoing increased investigation and interest [27–32]. Here, we thus applied optimal GDMT-implementation as a surrogate for quality in ambulatory care. By reducing variations in treatment, optimized GDMT-implementation improves clinical outcomes – including improved survival, and reduction of hospitalizations and complications [28–32]. However, GDMT-implementation in both CHF and COPD is suboptimal [33–39]. For this scenario extension, we leveraged literature findings from studies quantifying levels and effects of GDMT in CHF and COPD [11–13]. We considered only these two indications, because (1) they represent the major share of PAHs in Switzerland, (2) there is ample evidence for the potential of GDMT for these indications, (3) guideline complexity and heterogeneity are considerably high for diabetes and hypertension [40, 41], and finally (4) analysis of the underlying data in this project indicates that asthma has a succinctly different patient demographic compared to all other indications.

Extension 1: Supply-side scenario

To predict how a change in the demographic structure of PCPs might affect the number of PAHs, we employed a linear regression. We trained our prediction model based on past PAH-trends. We selected linear regression as the past PAH-trend is linear, thus rendering a satisfactory fit (see Table 5 in the supplementary material).

We applied the number of PCP full-time equivalents (FTEs) per 100 000 inhabitants of the age groups 20–49 and > 50 per canton and canton dummies as features to predict the number of PAHs per canton. For FTE-rate calculation, we assumed full-time work for PCPs aged 65 years and under, 75% for those aged 65 to 69 years, 50% for those aged 70 to 79 years and 20% for those aged 80 years and above.

We trained our model using data from 2012 to 2018, comprising 26 cantonal PAH rates per 100 000 inhabitants per year as observations of the dependent variable, i.e., n = 208. Additionally, we used 2019 as a holdout set for model evaluation. As the model demonstrated adequate performance for our purposes, we used it to project PAHs on a cantonal level for 2019 (base year) and 2023 to 2032 (future years). The model evaluation results can be found in Table 6 in the supplementary material.

Extension 2: Optimal GDMT-implementation

To inform an exploratory scenario analysis on optimal adherence to clinical practice guidelines among PCPs, we searched the literature for studies investigating the effectiveness of optimal GDMT-implementation and levels of guideline adherence for COPD and CHF. Search terms applied to PubMed for CHF and COPD included “heart failure” OR “heart failure with reduced ejection fraction” OR “heart failure with preserved ejection fraction” OR “HFrEF” OR “HFpEF” OR “Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease” OR “COPD” AND “hospitalization” AND/OR “admission” AND/OR “Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy” AND/OR “GDMT” AND/OR “treatment recommendations” AND/OR “guideline adherence” AND/OR “guideline compliance” AND/OR “medication adherence” AND/OR “MARS-5” (The Medication Adherence Report Scale) AND/OR “GOLD2” AND/OR ”GOLD guidelines” AND/OR “GOLD adherence”. In addition, we identified Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs) with relevance to hospital admissions and GDMT via review of clinical practice guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) [42] and the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease [43]. We considered studies published between January 2015 and July 2024. For our projection model, we focused on selecting studies that assessed the impact of GDMT on hospital admissions, as this was the primary endpoint of our projections.

For our scenario analysis for patients suffering from CHF, we employed a five-step approach: (1) we categorized CHF-patients into heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and heart failure with mildly reduced or preserved ejection fraction (HFmrEF or HFpEF), based on recent Swiss cohort data [44]; (2) we identified the share of CHF-patients treated in compliance with the ESC’s clinical practice guidelines [42]; (3) we determined the proportion of adherent patients who were hospitalized despite adherence, while assuming the rest of the hospitalized patients belong to the non-adherent group; (4) we projected how a gradual optimisation of GDMT-implementation among the non-adherent patient groups may impact the number of PAHs; (5) we then aggregated results to demonstrate the overall effect in CHF.

With respect to step (1), Norhammar et al. [44] assessed contemporary prevalence of heart failure in 11 countries, including Switzerland, and reported 48%, 13% and 40% of HFrEF, HFpEF, and HFmrEF cases respectively (rounded shares). The study utilized the same ICD-10 codes as we did to describe hospitalized patients suffering from CHF. Based on this, we assumed the same prevalence of heart failure with ejection fraction categories in our sample, resulting in 8,561 patients with HFrEF, 2,261 with HFmrEF, and 7,125 with HFpEF for the base year 2019.

With respect to step (2), Kerwagen et al. [45] found that 54.8% of PCPs in Germany prescribed GDMT-compliant medication to patients with HFrEF in 2023. Although the implementation of GDMT in HFpEF and HFmrEF is generally lower compared to HFrEF [42, 46], we adopted a conservative approach and assumed guideline adherence for HFmrEF and HFpEF to match levels reported for HFrEF.

We evaluated two meta-analyses [11, 12] investigating the effect of GDMT on hospital admissions due to HFrEF, HFmrEF and HFpEF, based on their respective definitions in the ESC-guidelines [42]. In line with the most recent large RCTs [47, 48], HFmrEF and HFpEF were pooled and summarized as HFpEF. For HFrEF, a Hazard Ratio (HR) of 0.32 (95% CI: 0.24–0.43) was reported [11]. For HFpEF, the HR was 0.74 (95% CI: 0.67–0.83) [11, 12].

To assess how improved guideline adherence by PCPs might impact the number of PAHs due to COPD, we employed a similar methodology: (1) based on a literature search, we identified the proportion of visits in which therapeutic prescriptions by PCPs were in compliance with GOLD guidelines (see search strategy above), (2) we estimated the proportion of patients who were hospitalized despite receiving GOLD-compliant treatment, assuming the remaining hospitalizations were attributable to the non-adherent group; (3) we projected the potential reduction in PAHs by modelling gradual GDMT-implementation among non-adherent patients.

With respect to step (1), Mangold et al. [33] reported that 65% of COPD patients receive GOLD-compliant prescriptions from Swiss PCPs. For step (2), Mannino et al. [13] reported that adherence to GOLD guidelines was associated with a significant reduction in all-cause hospitalizations, with an odds ratio of 0.69 (95% CI: 0.51–0.94) compared to non-adherence [13].

In our modelling approach, we treated the odds ratio as a hazard ratio, applying the “rare disease” assumption. The validity of this assumption was provided by Mannino et al. [13], who reported that less than 10% of their sample was hospitalized.

Additionally, several cohort studies, including the work by Mannino et al. [13], assessed the impact of adherence to GOLD guidelines on hospitalization only for COPD patients aged 40 years and older [13, 33, 49]. For the scenario calculation, we adopted this inclusion criterion by considering only this subgroup in our projection, which accounted for more than 99% of our sample. The calculation of the effectiveness of optimal implementation of GOLD guidelines for COPD followed a logic identical to that used in the CHF scenario.

Regarding gradual GDMT-implementation, we modelled for both CHF and COPD that starting from 2025, 30% of the non-adherent group would become adherent, followed by a linear increase in levels of GDMT-implementation in non-adherent patient groups between 2026 and 2032, reaching optimal adherence (100%) by 2032. In both scenario analyses, we applied the confidence intervals of the original studies to our projections.

We conducted all analyses with R version 4.3.1 for Windows (64-bit).

In the base year 2019, PAHs across all five chronic conditions totaled 40,206. The majority occurred due to CHF (17,948) and COPD (10,664), followed by diabetes (6,332), hypertension (3,654) and asthma (1,608). The majority of hospitalized patients were female for CHF (50.7%), asthma (69.7%) and hypertension (71.2%), while less patients were female than male for hospitalizations for COPD (48.6%) and diabetes (35.4%).

Most PAHs across all diseases occurred in individuals aged 60 and above. Patients with CHF possessed the highest burden of comorbidities with the Elixhauser comorbidity index reaching 0.8. Patients suffering from CHF had on average the longest hospital stay (10.3 days) compared to the other conditions under investigation. Lastly, the average effective cost weight was higher than the average relative cost weight for asthma, COPD, CHF and diabetes, indicating that cost weight increases due to long lengths of stay dominated over reductions due to short lengths of stay.

Past trend (2012–2019/2022) and forecast due to demographic change (2019–2032, base scenario and scenario extension 1)

The total number of PAHs increased from 30.1 thousand in 2012 to 40.2 thousand in 2019 (33.6% increase), dropped during the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, and started increasing again in 2021 and 2022 (Fig. 1, Panel A). Considering demographic change in the extrapolation of the base scenario, the number of PAHs is projected to increase to 54.9 thousand (an almost 36.5% increase compared to 2019). When considering demograhic change also on the supply side, we project the number of PAHs to increase to 60.7 thousand by 2032 (+ 10.6% compared to the base scenario), resulting in an overall 51% growth from 2019 to 2032.

Based on the analysis of all five individual chronic diseases, we project both an ageing population and shifts in the healthcare workforce to result in an increase in PAHs (see Fig. 1, Panels B and C). CHF and COPD comprised more than 70% of PAHs, accounting for 17.9 thousand and 10.7 thousand in 2019, respectively.

Discussion

Our first research question was concerned with quantifying the past trend of PAHs. We found that the total number of PAHs increased by 33.6% between 2012 and 2019, rising from 30.1 thousand to 40.2 thousand cases. CHF and COPD cases accounted for the largest share of PAHs, comprising over 70% of the total. In 2019, the capacity needs for PAHs equaled 1,260 hospital beds, accounting for 415 to 444 million Swiss francs. Overall, our results describing past PAH-trends demonstrated a strong increase in the burden of chronic diseases in the hospital sector.

When analyzing specific chronic diseases, we observed a rise in PAHs particularly for patients suffering from CHF, COPD, diabetes and hypertension. Notably, our findings on COPD aligned with a recent study reporting an increase in COPD-related PAHs between 1998 and 2018 in Switzerland [7]. In contrast, while both studies show consistent results on asthma-related PAHs for the period 2012–2017, we observed a divergence in the trend in 2018. This might be explained by differences in inclusion criteria, as we included both Swiss and non-Swiss residents in our analysis. Furthermore, we identified a slight growth in PAHs due to hypertension, consistent with a previous study assessing a 20-year trend (1998–2018) for this disease in Switzerland. Lastly, our findings regarding COPD, CHF, hypertension and diabetes are supported by findings from a study utilizing the 2017 OECD definition for avoidable hospital admissions [50].

We observed a sharp drop in PAHs and PAH-associated resources during the COVID-19 pandemic, which was especially strong for CHF and COPD in 2020 and 2021. This trend likely stems from systemic policies and changes in patient behaviour during the pandemic. For CHF, existing scientific evidence suggests this drop may occur due to patients’ reluctance to visit hospitals and a reduction in respiratory tract infections, which commonly trigger heart failure decompensation [51, 52]. A recent systematic review based on studies conducted across nine countries in Europe and Asia showed COPD-related admissions to halve during the COVID-19 pandemic via a reduction in respiratory viral infections triggering exacerbations [53]. Further studies revealed mortality rates among hospitalized COPD patients were higher during the pandemic [54, 55]. Following the OECD definition, we excluded patients who died during their inpatient stay, which resulted in a smaller number of PAHs in 2020 and 2021. A sharp drop in PAHs due to cases of hypertension in 2022 could be attributed to the Omicron variant of COVID-19. While the risk of hospitalization for patients with high blood pressure doubled in the presence of the variant, these cases most likely were recorded with Omicron as the principal diagnosis [56].

Our findings on inpatient resource utilization, measured in terms of hospital beds and healthcare expenditures, highlight the substantial strain PAHs place on hospital resources and healthcare budgets. This strain represents a considerable opportunity cost, as resources allocated to PAHs could be utilized for more complex or urgent cases. Reduction in bed occupancy by freeing beds from PAH cases may lead to improved health outcomes, including a reduction in mortality rates [57, 58].

Our second research question explored potential future trajectories of PAHs due to demographic change on the demand and supply side. Due to an ageing population, PAHs are projected to grow by approximately 36.5% from 2019 to 2032, requiring over 1,700 hospital beds and raising financial expenditures to 565–604 million Swiss francs. Additionally, demographic change in PCPs may contribute to a 10.6% increase in PAHs in 2032, compared to the base scenario. Cumulatively, the results indicate a potential 51% growth in PAHs between 2019 and 2032 due to demographic change. These findings underscore the effects of a declining healthcare labor force on hospitalizations and associated inpatient resource consumption. Existing evidence supports the negative association between hospitalizations and access to primary care services [4, 5]. However, while previous studies investigated the link between PAHs and the access to outpatient care [5, 14, 16, 59, 60], they have not measured the effect of demographic shift in PCPs on the evolution of PAHs quantitatively. Our findings emphasize the urgent need for policy interventions to prevent further strain on the inpatient hospital sector.

Furthermore, we modelled PAH evolution using 2022 as a base year. These predictions served as a sensitivity analysis to account for potential structural shifts in hospitalization patterns that have emerged during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. The projected number of PAHs and associated inpatient resources with the base year 2022 is 11.4% smaller than with the base year 2019, highlighting the impact of these structural shifts on the projected trend. Currently, it is still unclear if and how fast hospitalization will “recover” to a similar level as before the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. The most realistic scenario is likely to be in between the extrapolation based on 2019 and the one based on 2022, i.e., in 2032 the total number of PAHs might lie somewhere in between 49.3 and 54.9 thousand. Still, even if the actual numbers will be closer to the lower end of these projections, the projected increase in PAHs still is considerable, requiring effective countermeasures.

Regarding our third research question, where we explored the potential impact of countermeasures, we found that, by 2032, optimal GDMT-implementation may decrease the number of PAHs in CHF and COPD by 31% (95% CI: 22.23–37.60%) and 14% (95% CI: 2.17–26.02%) respectively. While explorative, these results showcase the potential of improved guideline adherence in reducing hospitalizations. Similar studies have demonstrated the importance of adhering to guidelines for managing CHF and COPD [61–63]. Ultimately, strengthening guideline adherence in the ambulatory sector is a key system-level, quality-based lever to alleviate pressure created by chronic diseases on the inpatient sector.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has five distinct strengths. Critically, the novelty-factor is significant, as we are one of the first to forecast future PAH-burden. We do so for all five PAH-qualifying chronic diseases, as per the OECD-definition, and we demonstrate the methodological potential to model various effects on PAH-burden in different scenarios. Second, ensuring a robust and representative analysis, we utilize a comprehensive dataset from the Swiss FSO with 100% coverage of the Swiss hospital landscape, including all hospitals and 59 million hospitalizations across all cantons of Switzerland. Third, historic analyses were conducted for an extended period from 2012 to 2022, allowing us to track trends and changes in the number of PAHs over eleven consecutive years. Fourth, we use the internationally established OECD definition for PAHs, enabling our results to be compared with other studies, thus increasing relevance, validity and applicability of our study. Fifth, our estimation on inpatient healthcare expenditures using Swiss DRG codes and cantonal reference tariffs provides an accurate and thorough understanding of the inpatient-related financial burden associated with PAHs in Switzerland.

Our study has several limitations. The identification of PAHs is based on OECD guidelines and a federal-level dataset summarizing inpatient stays. While this approach ensures consistency with international standards, we question the generalizability of the OECD definition for PAHs, as some patients in our dataset underwent complex medical procedures (e.g., lung transplantation). Though theoretically, even such complex procedures may have been avoided had ambulatory care been of higher quality (among others, via greater effectiveness and/or better accessibility) for an extended period before the procedure, such procedures are not be avoidable any more at the time of hospitalization. This is not reflected in the exclusion criteria defined by the OECD. Notwithstanding, “potentially avoidable” seems to indicate that the hospitalization is still avoidable at the point in time of hospitalization, e.g., because the same level of care could also be provided in an outpatient setting.

Regarding inpatient healthcare expenditures, according to the regulations of the Swiss DRG system, DRG cost weights must be multiplied with hospital-individual base rates. As we could not identify individual hospitals in our dataset, we had to rely on cantonal reference base rates, which in some cases were not available for all years and/or no distinction between non-university and university base rates was available. For a more accurate calculation, we would need to be able to identify single hospitals and collect hospital-individual base rates for the observation period.

In scenario extenstion 1, we employed linear regression as a modelling approach due to the observed linear trend in historical PAH data. This model demonstrated a high fit, with R squared ranging from 0.973 to 0.989 (see Table 7 in the Supplementary material). While this high fit indicates that the model captures historical trends well, it also suggests that out-of-sample performance may be less robust.

To evaluate out-of-sample performance, we tested the model using PAH values in 2019 and obtained mean absolute error (MAE) to evaluate model performance: 9.92 for Asthma, 73.15 for COPD, 110.83 for CHF, 14 for Hypertension and 32.97 for diabetes. These MAEs were consistently four to five times lower than mean squared error of the mean for each condition, demonstrating acceptable predictive accuracy given the available data. Given the limited size of our dataset, we refrained from testing alternative models to avoid overfitting and maintain the robustness of our analysis.

Regarding our scenario on countermeasures for PAHs, we explored optimal guideline implementation, assuming full adherence by all PCPs by 2032. In heart failure, current registry data indicates contraindications to GDMT to amount to less than 2% [35]. Nonetheless, while our assumption appears optimistic, we deliberately sought to showcase the maximum utility full GDMT-implementation might have for two reasons [34]. First, because it provides a maximum value for current overarching therapeutic potential and an index value for future PAH-research and healthcare resource planning. Second, to facilitate comparisons of care quality to other countries, since the PAH-metric is employed to compare the performance of outpatient care at various geographical levels [14–16]. Notwithstanding, making a reasonable assumption on a realistic adherence rate remains arbitrary to some degree, even when deduced from, for instance, international comparisons, as health systems differ considerably. Thus, our results can be interpreted as an exploration of maximum potentials and in the context of the state of research regarding clinical practice guideline adherence [64, 65].

Future research

Further studies might consider extending the set of predictors to forecast the evolution of PAHs in the future. Possible additions could be the number and nature of comorbidities, readmissions, socioeconomic factors (e.g., marital status, income level, and education level), and lifestyle (e.g., smoking behaviour). Moreover, integrated care, which is increasingly adopted in Switzerland, also merits further investigation, as it has proven its potential to reduce the number of hospitalizations among chronic patients [66]. Specifically, health risk assessment, advanced digital support, nurse-led in-person care and combinations of these approaches could be considered when projecting PAHs [67–69]. Since optimal GDMT-implementation in itself represents an outcome, methods to reach full implementation, such as disease management programmes, could undergo further investigation [70, 71]. Researchers might also investigate the preventive potential of interventions on the patient side, including optimal self-management (e.g., medication adherence [72], diet and physical activity), potentially supported by digital health interventions [73–76].

In addition, an in-depth analysis of PAH development per canton would be useful for policy makers, given that healthcare decisions in Switzerland are made at the cantonal level.

Conclusion

Chronic diseases impose a substantial health and financial burden on the Swiss health system and the Swiss hospital sector. We are the first to forecast the future burden for the OECD’s PAH-metric in Switzerland and our results show that burden will aggravate in the future. We project a considerable increase in PAHs for asthma, COPD, CHF, hypertension and, diabetes due to demographic change both on the patient and the primary care side. Still, our results indicate that improvements to the effectiveness of primary care hold considerable potential to manage hospitalizations of patients suffering from CHF and COPD. The findings in our study are critical for developing evidence-based strategies to reduce PAHs and enhance chronic disease management in the ambulatory sector. They are a call for enhanced research activity and action at the policy-level.
