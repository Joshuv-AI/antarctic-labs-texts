On strong sharp phase transition in the random connection model
Mikhail Chebunin, Günter Last

Abstract
We consider a random connection model (RCM) ξ \xi driven by a Poisson process η \eta . We derive exponential moment bounds for an arbitrary cluster, provided that the intensity t t of η \eta is below a certain critical intensity t T t_{T} . The associated subcritical regime is characterized by a finite mean cluster size, uniformly in space. Under an exponential decay assumption on the connection function, we also show that the cluster diameters are exponentially small as well. In the important stationary marked case and under a uniform moment bound on the connection function, we show that t T t_{T} coincides with t c t_{c} , the largest t t for which ξ \xi does not percolate. In this case, we also derive some percolation mean field bounds. These findings generalize some of the main results in [ 3 ] . Even in the classical unmarked case, our results are more general than what has been previously known. Our proofs are partially based on some stochastic monotonicity properties, which might be of interest in their own right.



Keywords:
Random connection model, Poisson process, percolation,
sharp phase transition, mean field lower bound, stochastic monotonicity.

2
Formal definition of the RCM

To define a RCM we follow
[
4
]
. Let

{\mathbf{N}}
(
{\mathbf{N}}_{<\infty}
) denote the space of all simple locally finite
(finite) counting measures on
{\mathbb{X}}
, equipped with the standard

\sigma
-field, see e.g.
[
25
]
. A measure
\nu\in{\mathbf{N}}

is identified with its support
\{x\in{\mathbb{X}}:\nu(\{x\})=1\}
and
describes the set of vertices of a (deterministic) graph. If

\nu(\{x\})=1
we write
x\in\nu
.
Any
\nu\in{\mathbf{N}}
can be written as a finite or
infinite sum
\nu=\delta_{x_{1}}+\delta_{x_{2}}+\cdots
of Dirac measures,
where the
x_{i}
are pairwise distinct and do not accumulate in bounded sets. The
space of (undirected) graphs with vertices from
{\mathbb{X}}
(and no loops)
is described by the set
{\mathbf{G}}
of all counting measures
\mu
on

{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}}
with the following properties. First, we assume that the
measure
V(\mu):=\mu(\cdot\times{\mathbf{N}})
is locally finite and simple,
that is, an element of
{\mathbf{N}}
. We write
x\in\mu
if
x\in V(\mu)
(that is

\mu(\{x\}\times{\mathbf{N}})=1
). In this case, there is a unique
\psi_{x}\in{\mathbf{N}}
such
that
(x,\psi_{x})\in\mu
. We assume that
x\notin\psi_{x}
. Finally, if

x\in V(\mu)
and
y\in\psi_{x}
then we assume that
(y,\psi_{y})\in\mu

and
x\in\psi_{y}
. Also,
{\mathbf{G}}
is equipped with the standard

\sigma
-field. There is an edge between
x,y\in V(\mu)
if
y\in\psi_{x}
(and hence
x\in\psi_{y}
). If
\psi_{x}=0
, then
x
is
isolated
.

We write
|\mu|:=\mu({\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}})
for the cardinality of
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
and similarly for
\nu\in{\mathbf{N}}
. Hence
|\mu|=|V(\mu)|
.
For
x,y\in V(\mu)
we write
x\sim y
(in
\mu
) if there is an edge between
x
and
y

and
x\leftrightarrow y
(in
\mu
) if there is a path in
\mu
leading from
x
to
y
.
For
A\subset{\mathbb{X}}
we write
x\sim A
(in
\mu
) if there exists
y\in A\cap V(\mu)

such that
x\sim y
.
Let
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{G}}
. Then
\mu
is a
subgraph
of
\mu^{\prime}
if

V(\mu)\leq V(\mu^{\prime})
(as measures) and for each
(x,\psi)\in\mu
and

(x,\psi^{\prime})\in\mu^{\prime}
we have
\psi\leq\psi^{\prime}
. Note that this is not the same
as
\mu\leq\mu^{\prime}
.

Let
\chi
be a simple point process on
{\mathbb{X}}
, which is a random element of

{\mathbf{N}}
. The reader should think of
a Poisson process possibly augmented by additional (deterministic) points.
By
[
25
, Proposition 6.2]
there exist random elements
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
of

{\mathbb{X}}
such that
\chi=\sum^{|\chi|}_{n=1}\delta_{X_{n}}
,
where
X_{m}\neq X_{n}
whenever
m\neq n
and
m,n\leq|\chi|
.
Let
(Z_{m,n})_{m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a double sequence of random elements
uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
such that
Z_{m,n}=Z_{n,m}
for all

m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and such that
Z_{m,n}
,
m<n
, are independent.
Then the RCM (based on
\chi
) is the point process

\displaystyle\xi:=\sum^{|\chi|}_{m=1}\delta_{(X_{m},\Psi_{m})},

where

\displaystyle\Psi_{m}:=\sum^{|\chi|}_{n=1}{\mathbf{1}}\{n\neq m,Z_{m,n}\leq\varphi(X_{m},X_{n})\}\delta_{X_{n}}.

While the definition of
\xi
depends on the ordering of the points of
\chi
, its distribution does not.

We now introduce some notation used throughout the paper.
For
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{G}}
, we often interpret
\mu+\mu^{\prime}
as the
measure in
{\mathbf{G}}
with the same support as
\mu+\mu^{\prime}
.
A similar convention applies to
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{N}}
.
Let
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
. For
B\in{\mathcal{X}}
we write
\mu(B):=\mu(B\times{\mathbf{N}})
.
More generally, given a measurable function
f\colon{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
we write

\int f(x)\,\mu(dx):=\int f(x)\,\mu(dx\times{\mathbf{N}})
.
In the same spirit, we write
g(\mu):=g(V(\mu))
, whenever

g
is a mapping on
{\mathbf{N}}
.
These (slightly abusing) conventions lighten the notation and should not cause any confusion.
For
B\in{\mathcal{X}}

we denote by
\mu[B]\in{\mathbf{G}}
the
restriction
of
\mu
to
B
, that is the graph
with vertex set
V(\mu)\cap B
which keeps only those edges
from
\mu
with both end points from
B
. In the same way, we use the notation
\mu[\nu]
for
\nu\in{\mathbf{N}}
.
Similarly, for a measure
\nu
on
{\mathbb{X}}
(for instance for
\nu\in{\mathbf{N}}
)
we denote by
\nu_{B}:=\nu(B\cap\cdot)
the restriction of
\nu
to a set
B\in{\mathcal{X}}
.
Assume now that
\mu
is a subgraph of
\mu^{\prime}
. For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
let

C^{\mu}_{n}(\mu^{\prime})\in{\mathbf{G}}
denote the restriction of
\mu^{\prime}
to those
v\in V(\mu^{\prime})
with

d_{\mu^{\prime}}(v,\mu)=n
, where
d_{\mu^{\prime}}
denotes the distance within the graph
\mu^{\prime}
.
Note that
C^{\mu}_{0}(\mu^{\prime})
is just the graph
\mu
. Slightly abusing our notation,
we write
C^{\mu}_{0}(\mu^{\prime})=\mu
and
V^{\mu}_{n}(\mu^{\prime})=V(C^{\mu}_{n}(\mu^{\prime}))
. For
v\notin V(\mu^{\prime})
we set
C^{v}(\mu^{\prime}):=0
,
interpreted as an empty graph (a graph without vertices).
The cluster
C^{\mu}(\mu^{\prime})
of
\mu
in
\mu^{\prime}
is the graph
\mu^{\prime}
restricted to

\displaystyle V^{\mu}(\mu^{\prime})=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}V^{\mu}_{n}(\mu^{\prime}),

while
C^{\mu}_{\leq{n}}(\mu^{\prime})
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, is the graph
\mu^{\prime}
restricted to

V^{\mu}_{0}(\mu^{\prime})+\cdots+V^{\mu}_{n}(\mu^{\prime})
. For later purposes, it will be convenient to define
C^{\mu}_{\leq{-1}}(\mu^{\prime})=C^{\mu}_{-1}(\mu^{\prime}):=0
as the zero measure. Throughout we write

V^{V(\mu)}_{n}(\mu^{\prime}):=V^{\mu}_{n}(\mu^{\prime})
and

V^{\mu}_{n}(\mu^{\prime},\cdot):=V^{\mu}_{n}(\mu^{\prime})(\cdot)
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, and
similarly for
V^{\mu}_{\leq n}
and
V^{\mu}
. We also often refer to
C^{v}(\mu^{\prime})
as the
cluster
of
v
in
\mu^{\prime}
for
v\in V(\mu^{\prime})
.

Given an RCM based on a Poisson process
\eta
on

{\mathbb{X}}
with diffuse intensity measure
\lambda
,
we use the following notation. For
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
we set

\displaystyle C^{v}:=C^{v}(\xi^{v}),\quad V^{v}:=V^{v}(\xi^{v}),\quad C^{v}_{n}:=C^{v}_{n}(\xi^{v}),\quad V^{v}_{n}:=V^{v}_{n}(\xi^{v}),\quad C^{v}_{\leq n}:=C^{v}_{\leq n}(\xi^{v}),\quad V^{v}_{\leq n}:=V^{v}_{\leq n}(\xi^{v}),

or
C^{v}_{\lambda}
,
V^{v}_{\lambda}
,
C^{v}_{n,\lambda}
,
V^{v}_{n,\lambda}
,
C^{v}_{\leq n,\lambda}
and
V^{v}_{\leq n,\lambda}
, if we need to emphasize the dependence on
\lambda
.
Moreover, we write
V^{v!}_{\leq n}:=V^{v}_{\leq n}-\delta_{v}
and similarly for
V^{v}
.

3
The Mecke equation

Let
\xi
be a RCM based on a Poisson process
\eta
on

{\mathbb{X}}
with diffuse intensity measure
\lambda
.
Our first crucial tool is a version of the Mecke equation (see

[
25
, Chapter 4]
) for
\xi
. Given
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

\boldsymbol{x}_{n}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{X}}^{n}
we denote

\delta_{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}}:=\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}}
and

\eta^{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}}:=\eta+\delta_{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}}
(removing possible multiplicities)
and let
\xi^{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}}
denote a RCM based on
\eta^{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}}
.
It is useful to construct
\xi^{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}}
in a specific way as follows.
We connect
x_{1}
with the points in
\eta
using independent connection decisions which
are independent of
\xi
.
We then proceed inductively, finally connecting
x_{n}
to

\eta+\delta_{\boldsymbol{x}_{n-1}}
.
For a measurable function

f\colon{\mathbb{X}}^{n}\times{\mathbf{G}}\to[0,\infty]
the Mecke equation for
\xi
states that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\xi)\,\eta^{(n)}(d\boldsymbol{x}_{n})=\mathbb{E}\int f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\xi^{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}})\,\lambda^{n}(d\boldsymbol{x}_{n}),

(3.1)

where integration with respect to the
factorial measure

\eta^{(n)}

of
\eta
means summation over all
n
-tuples of pairwise distinct points
from
\eta
.

For given
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\in{\mathbb{X}}^{n}

we denote
(v,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}):=(v,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{X}}^{n+1}
.
We sometimes use (
3.1
) in the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\xi^{v})\,\eta^{(n)}(d\boldsymbol{x}_{n})=\mathbb{E}\int f(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\xi^{v,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}})\,\lambda^{n}(d\boldsymbol{x}_{n}).

(3.2)

The proofs of (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) can be found in
[
4
]
.

4
Percolation and critical intensities

4.1
Notation and terminology in the general case

Let
t\geq 0
be an intensity parameter
and let
\xi
be a RCM based on a Poisson process
\eta
on
{\mathbb{X}}
with an intensity measure
t\lambda
, where
\lambda
is a locally finite and diffuse measure on
{\mathbb{X}}
.
The RCM
\xi

percolates
if it has an infinite cluster, a component with infinitely many vertices.
We also say that the RCM (or
t
) is
subcritical
if all clusters have only a finite number of points, that is,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}\big(|V^{v}|<\infty\big)=1,\quad\lambda\text{-a.e.\ $v\in{\mathbb{X}}$}.

In accordance with (
1.2
)
we define the
critical intensity

t_{c}
as the supremum of all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that above holds.
A standard coupling argument shows that
\xi
is subcritical for all
t<t_{c}
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
t\geq 0
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Mean generations and cluster sizes are denoted by

\displaystyle\begin{split}c^{v}_{n}(t):=\mathbb{E}_{t}|V^{v}_{n}|,\quad c^{v}_{\leq n}(t):=\mathbb{E}_{t}|V^{v}_{\leq n}|,\quad c^{v}(t):=\mathbb{E}_{t}|V^{v}|.\end{split}

It is clear that
c^{v}_{1}(t)=tD_{\varphi}(v)
and

\displaystyle c^{v}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}^{v}(t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}c^{v}_{\leq n}(t).

For a measurable function
L\colon{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\pm\infty\}

we define the
\infty
-norm by
\|L\|_{\infty}:=\esssup_{v\in{\mathbb{X}}}|L(v)|
,
where the essential supremum refers to
\lambda
.
We abbreviate

\displaystyle D^{*}_{\varphi}:=\|D_{\varphi}\|_{\infty},\quad c^{*}_{n}(t):=\|c_{n}(t)\|_{\infty},\quad c^{*}_{\leq n}(t):=\|c_{\leq n}(t)\|_{\infty},\quad c^{*}(t):=\|c(t)\|_{\infty}.

(4.1)

The
second critical intensity

t_{T}
is defined as the
supremum of all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that
c^{*}(t)<\infty
. It is clear that

t_{T}\leq t_{c}
.

4.2
The stationary marked RCM

In this subsection, we consider the important special case of a stationary RCM; see e.g.
[
3
,
8
,
4
]
.
Let
{\mathbb{M}}
be a complete separable metric space (the mark space)
equipped with a probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
(the mark distribution).
Set
{\mathbb{X}}:={\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{M}}
be equipped with the
product of the Borel
\sigma
-field
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and the
Borel
\sigma
-field on
{\mathbb{M}}
. We assume that

\lambda=\lambda_{d}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
, where
\lambda_{d}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. If
(x,p)\in{\mathbb{X}}
then we call
x
location of
(x,p)
and
p
the mark of
x
. Instead of
{\mathbf{N}}
we consider the
(smaller set)
{\mathbf{N}}({\mathbb{X}})
of all counting measures
\chi
on
{\mathbb{X}}
such
that
\chi(\cdot\times{\mathbb{M}})
is locally finite (w.r.t. the Euclidean

We can and will assume that the Poisson process
\eta
is a random element of
{\mathbf{N}}({\mathbb{X}})

The symmetric connection function

\varphi\colon({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{M}})^{2}\to[0,1]
is assumed to satisfy

\displaystyle\varphi((x,p),(y,q))=\varphi((0,p),(y-x,q)).

(4.2)

This allows us to write
\varphi(x,p,q):=\varphi((0,p),(x,q))
,
where
0
denotes the origin in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
The RCM
\xi
is
stationary
in the sense
that
T_{x}\xi\overset{d}{=}\xi
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, where
for
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
, the measure
T_{x}\mu
is (shift of
\mu
by
x
) defined by

T_{x}\mu:=\int{\mathbf{1}}\{(y-x,q,\nu)\in\cdot\}\,\mu(d(y,q,\nu))
.
It is also ergodic; see
[
4
]
.

Remark 4.1
.

The argument in
[
4
]

can be extended to yield that
T_{x}\xi^{(x,p)}\overset{d}{=}\xi^{(0,p)}
for
\lambda_{d}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
-a.e.
(x,p)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{M}}
.
Hence, if
f\colon{\mathbf{G}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is measurable and shift invariant, then

f(\xi^{(x,p)})\overset{d}{=}f(\xi^{(0,p)})
for

\lambda_{d}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
-a.e.
(x,p)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{M}}
. Therefore, the definitions (
1.2
)
and (
1.3
) of
t_{c}
and
t_{T}
can be simplified. For instance, we have

\displaystyle t_{c}=\sup\{t\geq 0:\mathbb{P}_{t}(|C^{(0,p)}|<\infty)=0\;\text{for $\mathbb{Q}$-a.e.\ $p$}\}.

(4.3)

The function
D_{\varphi}
(defined by (
1.1
)) takes the form

\displaystyle D_{\varphi}((x,p))=\iint\varphi(y,p,q)\,dy\,\mathbb{Q}(dq),\quad(x,p)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{M}},

while
D^{*}_{\varphi}
is given by

\displaystyle\esssup_{p\in{\mathbb{M}}}\iint\varphi(y,p,q)\,dy\,\mathbb{Q}(dq),

where the essential supremum now refers to
\mathbb{Q}
.
Similar comments apply to other characteristics introduced in Subsection
4.1
.
For instance, we have

c^{*}(t)=\esssup_{p\in{\mathbb{M}}}c^{(0,p)}(t)
. We often write

\displaystyle\bar{c}_{n}(t):=\int c^{(0,p)}_{n}(t)\,\mathbb{Q}(dp),\quad\bar{c}_{\leq n}(t):=\int c^{(0,p)}_{n}(t)\,\mathbb{Q}(dp),\quad\bar{c}(t):=\int c^{(0,p)}(t)\,\mathbb{Q}(dp).

(4.4)

It is convenient to introduce a random element
Q_{0}
of
{\mathbb{M}}
which is independent of
\xi
.
and has distribution
\mathbb{Q}
. Then we denote by
C^{Q_{0}}
the cluster of
(0,Q_{0})

in the RCM
\xi^{Q_{0}}
arising from
\xi
by adding independent connections between
(0,Q_{0})
and
the points from
\eta
. This
is the cluster of a
typical vertex
and we let
V^{Q_{0}}
denote its vertex set.
Then we can write
\bar{c}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{t}|V^{Q_{0}}|
and similar for other quantities.

Define

\displaystyle\theta^{p}(t):=\mathbb{P}_{t}\big(\big|C^{(0,p)}\big|=\infty\big),\quad t\geq 0,\ p\in{\mathbb{M}},

as the probability that the cluster of a vertex
(0,p)\in{\mathbb{X}}
has
infinite size. In the following, we use the
L^{r}(\mathbb{Q})
-norms to determine the size of functions. For each

r\in\left[1,+\infty\right]
, we define the critical intensities

\displaystyle t_{c}^{(r)}

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\|\theta(t)\|_{r}>0\right\},

(4.5)

\displaystyle t_{T}^{(r)}

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\|c(t)\|_{r}=\infty\right\}.

(4.6)

It is clear that
t_{c}^{(r)}
is
r
-independent, since it only matters if

\theta^{p}(t)=0
for
\mathbb{Q}
-a.e.
p\in{\mathbb{M}}
or not. Therefore, all
the critical intensities
t_{c}^{(r)}
coincide with the first critical
intensity
t_{c}
, which we defined for a general RCM. Note that

t_{T}^{(r)}\leq t_{c}
for all
r
. Moreover,
t^{(\infty)}_{T}
coincides
with the second critical intensity
t_{T}
, which we have also defined for
a general RCM. From Jensen’s inequality, it is clear that
t_{T}^{(r)}
is non-increasing in

r
. Therefore,

0\leq t_{T}=t_{T}^{(\infty)}\leq t_{T}^{(r_{2})}\leq t_{T}^{(r_{1})}\leq t_{T}^{(1)}\leq t_{c},\quad 1\leq r_{1}\leq r_{2}\leq\infty.

(4.7)

Let

\displaystyle\bar{\theta}(t):=\|\theta(t)\|_{1}=\int\theta^{p}(t)\,\mathbb{Q}(dp),\quad t\geq 0,

denote the percolation probability, that is, the probability that the cluster of a typical vertex is
infinite. Let
C_{\infty}
be the set of all
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
such that

\mu
has an infinite cluster.
It is easy to see that
\bar{\theta}(t)>0
iff
\mathbb{P}_{t}(\xi\in C_{\infty})=1
.
In fact, if
t<t_{c}
then
\mathbb{P}_{t}(\xi\in C_{\infty})=0
and if
t>t_{c}
then
\mathbb{P}_{t}(\xi\in C_{\infty})=1
.
Under a natural irreducibility assumption,
\xi
can have at most one infinite cluster;
see
[
4
]
.

Remark 4.2
.

In the unmarked case (
|{\mathbb{M}}|=1
) the
connection function
\varphi
is just a function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Under the minimal assumption
0<\int\varphi(x)\,dx<\infty

it was shown in
[
34
]
that
t_{c}\in(0,\infty)
.

The marked RCM is a very rich and flexible model of a spatial random graph.
We refer to
[
3
,
4
,
15
]
for many examples.
For further reference, we provide just two of them here.

Example 4.3
.

Assume that
{\mathbb{M}}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and

\varphi(x,p,q)={\mathbf{1}}\{\|x\|\leq p+q\}
, where
\|x\|
denotes the Euclidean norm of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
The RCM
\xi
is known as the spherical Boolean model or as
Gilbert graph
with
radius distribution
\mathbb{Q}
; see e.g.
[
25
, Chapter 16]
for more detail.
We have that

\displaystyle D_{\varphi}((0,r))=\kappa_{d}\int(r+s)^{d}\,\mathbb{Q}(ds),\quad r\geq 0,

(4.8)

where
\kappa_{d}
stands for the volume of the unit ball. Therefore
our basic assumption (
1.1
) is
equivalent with
\int r^{d}\,\mathbb{Q}(dr)<\infty
.
This is the minimal assumption for having a reasonable model.
Under the additional assumption
\mathbb{Q}\{0\}<1
it was proved in
[
12
,
16
]
that
t_{c}\in(0,\infty)
.
On the other hand, if
\mathbb{Q}
has unbounded support, then
D^{*}_{\varphi}=\infty
and

t_{T}=0
; see Lemma
6.1
.

Example 4.4
.

Assume that
{\mathbb{M}}=(0,1)

equipped with Lebesgue measure
\mathbb{Q}
. Assume that

\displaystyle\varphi((x,p),(y,q))=\rho(g(p,q)\|x-y\|^{d}),

for a profile function
\rho\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
and a kernel function
g\colon(0,1)\times(0,1)\to[0,\infty)
.
We assume that
m_{\rho}:=\int\rho(\|x\|^{d})\,dx
is positive and finite. This model was studied in
[
15
]

under the name
weight-dependent random connection model
, for decreasing profile function and increasing kernel function.
Then

\displaystyle\iint\varphi(x,p,q)\,dx\,dq=m_{\rho}\int g(p,q)^{-1}\,dq,

and (
1.1
) holds if
g(p,\cdot)^{-1}
is integrable for each
p\in(0,1)
.
This is the case in all examples studied in
[
15
]
, where it is also asserted that
t_{c}<\infty
.
Sufficient conditions for
t_{c}\in(0,\infty)
can also be found in
[
3
,
6
]
.

5
A spatial Markov property and stochastic ordering

We consider a general RCM
\xi
based on a Poisson process
\eta
on
{\mathbb{X}}
with diffuse intensity measure
\lambda
.
Given
\mu\in{\mathbf{N}}
, we denote the RCM based on
\eta+\mu
by
\xi^{\mu}
.
We first recall the
spatial Markov property
, as formulated in
[
4
]
.

Let
\nu
be a locally finite and diffuse
measure on
{\mathbb{X}}
. We often write
\Pi_{\nu}
for the distribution of a Poisson process with intensity measure
\nu
.
We set
\bar{\varphi}:=1-\varphi
and define for
x\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
\nu\in{\mathbf{N}}

\displaystyle\bar{\varphi}(\nu,x):=\prod_{y\in\nu}\bar{\varphi}(x,y),\quad\varphi(\nu,x):=1-\bar{\varphi}(\nu,x),\ \quad\varphi_{\lambda}(\nu):=\int\varphi(\nu,x)\,\lambda(dx).

(5.1)

We recall our general convention
\varphi(\mu,x):=\varphi(V(\mu),x)

and
\varphi_{\lambda}(\mu):=\varphi_{\lambda}(V(\mu))
for
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
.
Next we define two kernels from
{\mathbf{N}}
to
{\mathbb{X}}
and from
{\mathbf{N}}\times{\mathbf{N}}
to
{\mathbb{X}}
(using the same notation
K_{\nu}
for simplicity), by

\displaystyle K_{\nu}(\mu,dx):=\bar{\varphi}(\mu,x)\nu(dx),\quad K_{\nu}(\mu,\mu^{\prime},dx):=\bar{\varphi}(\mu,x)\varphi(\mu^{\prime},x)\nu(dx).

(5.2)

Proposition
5.1
will provide an interpretation of this kernel.
Denoting by
0
the zero measure, we note that

\displaystyle K_{\nu}(0,dx)=\nu(dx),\quad K_{\nu}(0,\mu^{\prime},dx)=\varphi(\mu^{\prime},x)\nu(dx),\quad K_{\nu}(\mu,0,dx)=0.

(5.3)

We write
K_{\nu}(\mu):=K_{\nu}(\mu,\cdot)
and
K_{\nu}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):=K_{\nu}(\mu,\mu^{\prime},\cdot)
.
Note that
K_{\lambda}(0,\mu,{\mathbb{X}})=\varphi_{\lambda}(\mu)
; see (
5.1
).

The following spatial Markov property of the random graph
\xi^{v}

was proved in
[
4
]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

The sequence
(V^{v}_{\leq{n-1}},V^{v}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is a Markov
process with transition kernel

\displaystyle(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\mapsto\int{\mathbf{1}}\{(\mu+\mu^{\prime},\psi)\in\cdot\}\,\Pi_{K_{\lambda}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}(d\psi).

In the following, we often abbreviate
K:=K_{\lambda}
.
Given
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
we define a probability kernel
H_{n}
from
{\mathbf{N}}\times{\mathbf{N}}
to
{\mathbf{N}}
by

\displaystyle H_{n}(\mu,\mu^{\prime},\cdot):=\idotsint{\mathbf{1}}\{\psi_{n}\in\cdot\}\,\Pi_{K(\mu+\mu^{\prime}+\psi_{1}+\cdots+\psi_{n-2},\psi_{n-1})}(d\psi_{n})\cdots\Pi_{K(\mu+\mu^{\prime},\psi_{1})}(d\psi_{2})\Pi_{K(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}(d\psi_{1}).

(5.4)

The Proposition
5.1
implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(V^{v}_{n}\in\cdot)=H_{n}(0,\delta_{v},\cdot),\quad v\in{\mathbb{X}}.

(5.5)

Corollary 5.2
.

For
\lambda
-a.e.
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
under condition (
1.1
) we have
\mathbb{P}(|V^{v}_{n}|<\infty)=1
.

The following useful property of the kernel
K_{\lambda}
can be easily proved by induction.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in{\mathbf{N}}
.
Then

\displaystyle K_{\lambda}(0,\mu_{0})+K_{\lambda}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})+\cdots+K_{\lambda}(\mu_{0}+\cdots+\mu_{n-1},\mu_{n})=K_{\lambda}(0,\mu_{0}+\cdots+\mu_{n}).

Using a standard coupling argument, it is easy to establish the following facts.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and assume that
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}
. Then

\displaystyle V^{v}_{\lambda_{1}}\leq_{st}V^{v}_{\lambda_{2}},

(5.6)

and for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle V^{v}_{\leq n,\lambda_{1}}\leq_{st}V^{v}_{\leq n,\lambda_{2}}.

(5.7)

Proof.

Construct
\xi^{v}_{\lambda_{2}}
and then
\xi^{v}_{\lambda_{1}}
by independent thinning.
∎

The Proposition
5.4
implies monotonicity in the measure
\lambda
for

V^{v}_{\leq n}
and
V^{v}
. We can state similar property for the first and second generations, but things are getting tricky for higher
generations. This is an open question.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
m\in\{1,2\}
and assume that
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}
. Then

\displaystyle V^{v}_{m,\lambda_{1}}\leq_{st}V^{v}_{m,\lambda_{2}}.

(5.8)

Proof.

Construct
\xi^{v}_{\lambda_{2}}
and then
\xi^{v}_{\lambda_{1}}
by
independent thinning. Then

V^{v}_{1,\lambda_{1}}\leq V^{v}_{1,\lambda_{2}}
a.s..
The Proposition
5.1
shows that, given

V^{v}_{1,\lambda_{2}}
, the conditional distribution of

V^{v}_{2,\lambda_{2}}
is that of a Poisson process with an intensity measure
K_{\lambda_{2}}(\delta_{v},V^{v}_{1,\lambda_{2}})
. The statement follows from the facts that a Poisson process stochastically increases in
the intensity measure and that
K_{\lambda}
is increasing in
\lambda
and the second coordinate, i.e.

K_{\lambda_{2}}(\delta_{v},V^{v}_{1,\lambda_{2}})\geq K_{\lambda_{1}}(\delta_{v},V^{v}_{1,\lambda_{1}})
.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Suppose that

\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in{\mathbf{N}}_{<\infty}
have disjoint support and set

\mu:=\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}
. Assume that
\xi_{\mu_{1}}
and
\xi_{\mu_{2}}
are independent RCMs with distributions
\xi^{\mu_{1}}
and
\xi^{\mu_{2}}
, respectively.
Then

\displaystyle V^{\mu}_{n}(\xi^{\mu})\leq_{st}V_{n}^{\mu_{1}}(\xi_{\mu_{1}})+V_{n}^{\mu_{2}}(\xi_{\mu_{2}}),\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},

(5.9)

\displaystyle V^{\mu}_{\leq n}(\xi^{\mu})\leq_{st}V_{\leq n}^{\mu_{1}}(\xi_{\mu_{1}})+V_{\leq n}^{\mu_{2}}(\xi_{\mu_{2}}),\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.

(5.10)

Proof.

Define
V_{1,1}(\xi^{\mu}):=\{x\in\eta:x\sim\mu_{1}\}
as the set
of points from
\eta
which are connected to
\mu_{1}
in
\xi^{\mu}
, and

V_{1,2}(\xi^{\mu}):=\{x\in\eta:x\sim\mu_{2},\ x\nsim\mu_{1}\}
.
Then
V_{1,1}(\xi^{\mu})
and
V_{1,2}(\xi^{\mu})
are independent Poisson processes, and

V_{1}^{\mu}(\xi^{\mu})=V_{1,1}(\xi^{\mu})+V_{1,2}(\xi^{\mu})
.
For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
we define

V_{k,1}(\xi^{\mu})
as the set of points from
V^{\mu}_{k}(\xi^{\mu})
which are
connected to
V_{k-1,1}(\xi^{\mu})
and
V_{k,2}(\xi^{\mu})

as the set of points from
V^{\mu}_{k}(\xi^{\mu})
which are connected to

V_{k-1,2}(\xi^{\mu})
but not to
V_{k-1,1}(\xi^{\mu})
.
Then
V_{k}^{\mu}(\xi^{\mu})=V_{k,1}(\xi^{\mu})+V_{k,2}(\xi^{\mu})
. Moreover,

V_{k,1}(\xi^{\mu})
and
V_{k,2}(\xi^{\mu})
are conditionally independent Poisson
processes given
C^{\mu}_{\leq k-1}(\xi^{\mu})
.
Let
f\colon{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be measurable and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}f(V^{\mu}_{n}(\xi^{\mu}))=\mathbb{E}\,\mathbb{E}\big[f\left(V^{\mu}_{n}(\xi^{\mu})\right)\mid C^{\mu}_{\leq n-1}(\xi^{\mu})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\iint f\left(\psi^{1}_{n}+\psi^{2}_{n}\right)\,\Pi_{K(V^{\mu}_{\leq n-2}(\xi^{\mu})+V_{n-1,1}(\xi^{\mu}),V_{n-1,2}(\xi^{\mu}))}(d\psi^{2}_{n})\,\Pi_{K(V^{\mu}_{\leq n-2}(\xi^{\mu}),V_{n-1,1}(\xi^{\mu}))}(d\psi^{1}_{n}).

Recursively, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}f(V^{\mu}_{n}(\xi^{\mu}))

\displaystyle=\idotsint f\big(\psi^{1}_{n}+\psi^{2}_{n}\big)\,\Pi_{K(\mu+\psi^{1}_{1}+\psi^{2}_{1}+\cdots+\psi^{1}_{n-2}+\psi^{2}_{n-2}+\psi^{1}_{n-1},\psi^{2}_{n-1})}(d\psi^{2}_{n})\,\Pi_{K(\mu+\psi^{1}_{1}+\psi^{2}_{1}+\cdots+\psi^{1}_{n-2}+\psi^{2}_{n-2},\psi^{1}_{n-1})}(d\psi^{1}_{n})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\cdots\Pi_{K(\mu+\psi^{1}_{1},\psi^{2}_{1})}(d\psi^{2}_{2})\,\Pi_{K(\mu,\psi^{1}_{1})}(d\psi^{2}_{1})\,\Pi_{K(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})}(d\psi^{2}_{1})\,\Pi_{K(0,\mu_{1})}(d\psi^{1}_{1}).

(5.11)

On the other hand, we obtain from (
5.5
) and the independence of
\xi_{\mu_{1}}
and
\xi_{\mu_{2}}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}f\left(V^{\mu_{1}}_{n}(\xi_{\mu_{1}})+V^{\mu_{2}}_{n}(\xi_{\mu_{2}})\right)=\iint f\big(\psi^{1}_{n}+\psi^{2}_{n}\big)\,H_{n}(0,\mu_{1},d\psi^{1}_{n})\,H_{n}(0,\mu_{2},d\psi^{2}_{n}).

(5.12)

Assume now that
f
increases. We compare (
5
) and
(
5.12
) taking into account two facts.
First, Poisson processes increase stochastically in the intensity measure. Second,

K
decreases in the first argument and increases in
the second. This implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}f(V^{\mu}_{n}(\xi^{\mu}))\leq\mathbb{E}f\left(V^{\mu_{1}}_{n}(\xi_{\mu_{1}})+V^{\mu_{2}}_{n}(\xi_{\mu_{2}})\right),

that is (
5.9
). The proof of (
5.10
) is the same, up to the
fact that the argument of the function
f
has to be suitably modified.
∎

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
\mu\in{\mathbf{N}}_{<\infty}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle V^{\mu}_{n}(\xi^{\mu})

\displaystyle\leq_{st}\int V_{n}^{x}(\xi_{x})\,\mu(dx),

\displaystyle V^{\mu}_{\leq n}(\xi^{\mu})

\displaystyle\leq_{st}\int V_{\leq n}^{x}(\xi_{x})\,\mu(dx).

where
\xi_{x}
,
x\in\mu
, are independent and
\mathbb{P}(\xi_{x}\in\cdot)=\mathbb{P}(\xi^{x}\in\cdot)
.

Proof.

Write
\mu=\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{m}}
and apply
Lemma
5.6
inductively with

\mu_{1}=\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{i}}
and

\mu_{2}=\delta_{x_{i+1}}
for
i=1,\ldots,m-1
.
∎

In the following, we consider two families
\{\xi_{x}^{(n)}\}_{x\in V^{v}_{n}}

and
\{\xi^{[n]}_{x}\}_{x\in V^{v}_{n}}
of random graphs with the following properties:

1.

Given
C_{\leq n}^{v}
,
\xi_{x}^{(n)}
and
\xi^{[n]}_{x}
are RCM’s driven
by
\eta_{n}+\delta_{x}
and
\eta+\delta_{x}
, where
\eta_{n}
and
\eta

are Poisson processes with intensity measures

K_{\lambda}(V^{v}_{\leq n-1})
and
\lambda
respectively.

2.

The members of the families are conditionally independent, given
C_{\leq n}^{v}
.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
n,m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle V_{n+m}^{v}\leq_{st}\int V^{x}_{m}(\xi_{x}^{(n)})\,V^{v}_{n}(dx).

Proof.

Let
f\colon{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be measurable and increasing.
By Proposition
5.1
the conditional distribution of
V_{n+m}^{v}

given
C_{\leq n}^{v}
is that of
V_{m}^{V^{v}_{n}}(\xi^{(V^{v}_{\leq n-1}),V^{v}_{n}})
, where
\xi^{(V^{v}_{\leq n-1}),V^{v}_{n}}

is a RCM based on
\eta_{n}+V_{n}^{v}
and
\eta_{n}

is a Poisson process with intensity measure
K_{\lambda}(V^{v}_{\leq n-1})
. Hence,
by Corollary
5.7

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}f(V^{v}_{n+m})=\mathbb{E}\,\mathbb{E}\big[f(V^{v}_{n+m})\mid C^{v}_{\leq n}\big]=\mathbb{E}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[f(V_{m}^{V^{v}_{n}}(\xi^{(V^{v}_{\leq n-1}),V^{v}_{n}}))\mid C^{v}_{\leq n}\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}f\left(\int V^{x}_{m}(\xi_{x}^{(n)})\,V^{v}_{n}(dx)\right).

The assertion follows.
∎

For
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
we define the
intensity measures

\Lambda^{v}_{n}

of
V^{v}_{n}
by
\Lambda^{v}_{n}(B):=\mathbb{E}V^{v}_{n}(B)
for
B\in{\mathcal{X}}
.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
m\in\{1,2\}
. Then

\displaystyle V_{n+m}^{v}\leq_{st}\int V^{x}_{m}(\xi^{[n]}_{x})\,V^{v}_{n}(dx).

Moreover, we get

\displaystyle\Lambda^{v}_{n+m}\leq\int\Lambda^{x}_{m}\,\Lambda^{v}_{n}(dx).

Proof.

Let
f\colon{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be measurable and increasing. By Propositions
5.8
and
5.5
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}f(V^{v}_{n+m})=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}(f(V^{v}_{n+m})\mid C^{v}_{\leq n})\leq\mathbb{E}f\left(\int V^{x}_{m}(\xi_{x}^{(n)})\,V^{v}_{n}(dx)\right)\leq\mathbb{E}f\left(\int V^{x}_{m}(\xi_{x}^{[n]})\,V^{v}_{n}(dx)\right).

Since
\Lambda^{v}_{0}=\delta_{v}
for the second statement, we can assume that
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. For each
B\in{\mathcal{X}}
we have

\displaystyle\Lambda^{v}_{n+m}(B)=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}(V^{v}_{n+m}(B)\mid C^{v}_{\leq n})\leq\mathbb{E}\int\Lambda^{x}_{m}(B)\,V^{v}_{n}(dx)=\int\Lambda^{x}_{m}(B)\,\Lambda^{v}_{n}(dx),

where we have used Campbell’s formula.
∎

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
n,m\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then

\displaystyle V^{v}_{\leq n+m}\leq_{st}V^{v}_{\leq n-1}+\int V^{x}_{\leq m}(\xi^{(n)}_{x})\,V^{v}_{n}(dx)\leq_{st}V^{v}_{\leq n-1}+\int V^{x}_{\leq m}(\xi^{[n]}_{x})\,V^{v}_{n}(dx).

Proof.

Let
f\colon{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be measurable and increasing.
By Proposition
5.1
the conditional distribution of
V_{\leq n+m}^{v}
given
C_{\leq n}^{v}
is
that of
V_{\leq n-1}^{v}+V_{\leq m}^{V^{v}_{n}}(\xi^{(V^{v}_{\leq n-1}),V^{v}_{n}})
, where
\xi^{(V^{v}_{\leq n-1}),V^{v}_{n}}
is a RCM
based on
\eta_{n}+V_{n}^{v}
and
\eta_{n}
is a Poisson process with
intensity measure
K_{\lambda}(V^{v}_{\leq n-1})
. Hence, we obtain from
Corollary
5.7
and Proposition
5.4

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}f(V^{v}_{\leq n+m})=\mathbb{E}\,\mathbb{E}\big[f(V^{v}_{\leq n+m})\mid C^{v}_{\leq n}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}f\left(V^{v}_{\leq n-1}+\int V^{x}_{\leq m}(\xi_{x}^{(n)})\,V^{v}_{n}(dx)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}f\left(V^{v}_{\leq n-1}+\int V^{x}_{\leq m}(\xi^{[n]}_{x})\,V^{v}_{n}(dx)\right).

∎

Corollary 5.11
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then

\displaystyle V^{v}\leq_{st}V^{v}_{\leq n-1}+\int V^{x}(\xi^{[n]}_{x})\,V^{v}_{n}(dx).

Proof.

The claim follows from Proposition

, since with probability one

Given
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and a measurable function
h\colon{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
,
we define two spatial (Galton-Watson) branching processes
(W^{v,h}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
and
(\widetilde{W}^{v,h}_{k})_{k\geq 0}

along with a sequence of families of random graphs
\{\xi^{k}_{x}:x\in W^{v,h}_{k}\}
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
recursively as follows. We set

\displaystyle W^{v,h}_{0}=\widetilde{W}^{v,h}_{0}=\delta_{v},\quad W^{v,h}_{1}=V^{v}_{h(v)},\quad\widetilde{W}^{v,h}_{1}=V^{v!}_{\leq h(v)},

and
\{\xi^{0}_{x}:x\in W^{v,h}_{0}\}:=\{\xi^{v}\}
.
Given
k\geq 1
,
Z_{k}:=\big(\big(W^{v,h}_{n},\widetilde{W}^{v,h}_{n}\big)\big)_{n\leq k}
and
\big(\{\xi^{n}_{x}:x\in W^{v,h}_{n}\}\big)_{n\leq k-1}

we let
\{\xi^{k}_{x}:x\in W^{v,h}_{k}\}
be a family of random graphs which are conditionally independent given
Z_{k}
and
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi^{k}_{x}\in\cdot\mid Z_{k})=\mathbb{P}(\xi^{x}\in\cdot),\quad x\in W^{v,h}_{k}.

Then we define

\displaystyle W^{v,h}_{k+1}:=\int V^{x}_{h(x)}(\xi^{k}_{x})\,W^{v,h}_{k}(dx),\quad\widetilde{W}^{v,h}_{k+1}:=\int V^{x!}_{\leq h(x)}(\xi^{k}_{x})\,W^{v,h}_{k}(dx.)

Note that

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}^{v,h}_{k+1}=\int V^{x!}_{\leq h(x)-1}(\xi^{k}_{x})\,W^{v,h}_{k}(dx)+W^{v,h}_{k+1}.

Hence, for every point
x\in W^{v,h}_{k}
from the
k
-th
generation we run an independent RCM driven by

\eta+\delta_{x}
, where
\eta
is a Poisson process with intensity
measure
\lambda
and place all points of its
h(x)
-th generation in
our spatial branching process
k+1
-th generation, i.e.
W^{v,h}_{k+1}
.
The process
\big(\widetilde{W}^{v,h}_{k}\big)_{k\geq 0}
has a similar interpretation.
We define

\displaystyle W^{v,h}_{\leq k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}W^{v,h}_{i},\quad W^{v,h}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}W^{v,h}_{i},\quad\widetilde{W}^{v,h}_{\leq k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\widetilde{W}^{v,h}_{i},\quad\widetilde{W}^{v,h}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{W}^{v,h}_{i}.

The special case
h\equiv n
for some fixed
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
is of particular importance. In that
case we use the upper index
n
instead of
h
.

Remark 5.12
.

For each fixed
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the point process
\widetilde{W}^{v,h}_{\leq k}
is locally
finite. If the extinction probability of
W^{v,h}
is equal to one, then this is also
true for
\widetilde{W}^{v,h}
. Otherwise, one cannot be sure of the locally
bounded property. Corollary
6.6

provides a simple sufficient condition
(namely
D^{*}_{\varphi}<\infty
and
c^{*}_{n}(t)<1
for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
) when
W^{v,n}
is finite with probability
one. In the stationary case, it suffices to assume that
c^{*}_{n}(t)\leq 1
,
since the total size of
W^{v,n}
coincides with the total size of a
Galton–Watson process with offspring distribution
|V^{0}_{n}|
.

We have the following useful property.

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
k,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then

\displaystyle W^{v,n}_{k}

\displaystyle\leq_{st}W^{v,1}_{kn},

(5.13)

\displaystyle W^{v,n}_{\leq k}

\displaystyle\leq_{st}\sum_{i=1}^{k}W^{v,1}_{in}.

(5.14)

In particular, if
k=1
, then

\displaystyle V^{v}_{n}

\displaystyle\leq_{st}W^{v,1}_{n}.

(5.15)

Proof.

Define a kernel
\tilde{K}
from
{\mathbf{N}}
to
{\mathbf{N}}
by

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(\mu,\cdot):=\int K_{\lambda}(0,\delta_{x},\cdot)\,\mu(dx).

(5.16)

By Bernoulli’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle K_{\lambda}(\mu,\mu^{\prime},\cdot)\leq\tilde{K}(\mu^{\prime},\cdot),\quad\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{N}}.

(5.17)

Taking an increasing and measurable
f\colon{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we therefore obtain from (
5.5
)
and the monotonicity properties of a Poisson process that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}f(V^{v}_{n})\leq\idotsint f(\psi_{n})\,\Pi_{\tilde{K}(\psi_{n-1})}(d\psi_{n})\cdots\Pi_{\tilde{K}(\psi_{1})}(d\psi_{2})\Pi_{\tilde{K}(\delta_{v})}(d\psi_{1})=\mathbb{E}f(W^{v,1}_{n}).

(5.18)

This is (
5.13
) for
k=1
, i.e. (
5.15
).

By the definition of
W^{v,n}_{2}
and (
5.5
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}f(W^{v,n}_{2})=\mathbb{E}\int f(\psi)H^{\prime}_{n}(V^{v}_{n},d\psi),

(5.19)

where for given
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\delta_{\boldsymbol{x}_{m}}\in{\mathbf{N}}_{<\infty}

\displaystyle H^{\prime}_{n}(\mu,\cdot):=\idotsint{\mathbf{1}}\{\psi^{1}+\cdots+\psi^{m}\in\cdot\}\,H_{n}(0,\delta_{x_{1}},d\psi^{1})\cdots\,H_{n}(0,\delta_{x_{m}},d\psi^{m}).

Using (
5.17
) and the monotonicity properties of a Poisson process
in the definition (
5.4
) of kernels
H_{n}
once again, we see that

\displaystyle\int f(\psi)H^{\prime}_{n}(\mu,d\psi)

\displaystyle\leq\idotsint f(\psi^{1}_{n}+\cdots+\psi^{m}_{n})\,\Pi_{\tilde{K}(\psi^{1}_{n-1})}(d\psi^{1}_{n})\cdots\Pi_{\tilde{K}(\psi^{m}_{n-1})}(d\psi^{m}_{n})\times\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\Pi_{\tilde{K}(\delta_{x_{1}})}(d\psi^{1}_{1})\cdots\Pi_{\tilde{K}(\delta_{x_{m}})}(d\psi^{m}_{1}).

By the definition (
5.16
) of the kernel
\tilde{K}
and the fundamental properties of a Poisson process, the above
m
innermost integrals equal

\displaystyle\int f(\psi_{n})\,\Pi_{\tilde{K}(\psi^{1}_{n-1}+\cdots+\psi^{m}_{n-1})}(d\psi_{n}).

Proceeding inductively, we obtain that

\displaystyle\int f(\psi)H^{\prime}_{n}(\mu,d\psi)\leq\idotsint f(\psi_{n})\,\Pi_{\tilde{K}(\psi_{n-1})}(d\psi_{n})\cdots\Pi_{\tilde{K}(\mu)}(d\psi_{1}).

The above right-hand side is an increasing function of
\mu
. Hence, we can apply
(
5.13
) for
k=1
to obtain from (
5.19
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}f(W^{v,n}_{2})\leq\idotsint f(\psi_{n})\,\Pi_{\tilde{K}(\psi_{n-1})}(d\psi_{n})\cdots\Pi_{\tilde{K}(\chi_{n})}(d\psi_{1})\Pi_{\tilde{K}(\chi_{n-1})}(d\chi_{n})\cdots\Pi_{\tilde{K}(\delta_{v})}(d\chi_{1})=\mathbb{E}f(W^{v,1}_{2n}).

Hence, we have (
5.13
) for
k=2
. The case of a general
k
can be treated analogously.
The second assertion (
5.14
) can be proved in the same way, modifying
the arguments of the function
f
in an appropriate way.
∎

Proposition 5.14
.

Let

and

\displaystyle|W^{v,1}_{n}|\leq_{st}W_{n},\quad|W^{v,1}_{\leq n}|\leq_{st}W_{\leq n}.

Note that the total size of the offspring distribution

The following results are crucial for later purposes.

Proposition 5.15
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
n,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then

\displaystyle V^{v}_{\leq kn}\leq_{st}\widetilde{W}^{v,n}_{\leq k}.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.10
, from the similar definitions of
\{\xi_{x}^{[n]}:x\in V^{v}_{n}\}
and
\{\xi_{x}^{1}:x\in V^{v}_{n}\}
we get

\displaystyle V^{v}_{\leq kn}\leq_{st}V^{v}_{\leq n-1}+\int V^{x_{1}}_{\leq(k-1)n}\big(\xi^{1}_{x_{1}}\big)\,V^{v}_{n}(dx_{1}).

(5.20)

Define a point process
V^{[2]}_{n}
on
{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbb{X}}
by

\displaystyle V^{[2]}_{n}:=\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\cdot\}\,V^{x_{1}}_{n}(\xi^{1}_{x_{1}},dx_{2})\,V^{v}_{n}(dx_{1}).

Let
\big\{\xi^{[2]}_{x_{1},x_{2}}:(x_{1},x_{2})\in V^{[2]}_{n}\big\}
be a family of random graphs which are conditionally independent given
V^{[2]}_{n}
and
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi^{[2]}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\in\cdot\mid V^{[2]}_{n})=\mathbb{P}(\xi^{x_{2}}\in\cdot),\quad(x_{1},x_{2})\in V^{[2]}_{n}.

This implies that for each
x_{1}\in V^{v}_{n}
the random graphs

\xi^{[2]}_{x_{1},x_{2}}
,
x_{2}\in V^{x_{1}}_{n}(\xi^{1}_{x_{1}})
, are conditionally
independent given
V^{x_{1}}_{n}(\xi^{1}_{x_{1}})
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi^{[2]}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\in\cdot\mid V^{x_{1}}_{n}(\xi^{1}_{x_{1}}))=\mathbb{P}(\xi^{x_{2}}\in\cdot),\quad x_{2}\in V^{x_{1}}_{n}(\xi^{1}_{x_{1}}).

Therefore, we can apply Proposition
5.10
in (
5.20
) to the conditional distribution
(w.r.t.
V^{[2]}_{n}
) to see that

\displaystyle V^{v}_{\leq kn}\leq_{st}

\displaystyle V^{v}_{\leq n-1}+\int V^{x_{1}}_{\leq n-1}(\xi^{1}_{x_{1}})\,V^{v}_{n}(dx_{1})+\iint V^{x_{2}}_{\leq(k-2)n}(\xi^{[2]}_{x_{1},x_{2}})\,V^{x_{1}}_{n}(\xi^{1}_{x_{1}},dx_{2})\,V^{v}_{n}(dx_{1}),

It is easy to see that the distribution
of the above right-hand side does not change when replacing
\xi^{[2]}_{x_{1},x_{2}}
by
\xi^{2}_{x_{2}}
. It follows that

\displaystyle V^{v}_{\leq kn}\leq_{st}

\displaystyle V^{v}_{\leq n-1}+\int V^{x_{1}}_{\leq n-1}(\xi^{1}_{x_{1}})\,V^{v}_{n}(dx_{1})+\iint V^{x_{2}}_{\leq(k-2)n}(\xi^{2}_{x_{2}})\,V^{x_{1}}_{n}(\xi^{1}_{x_{1}},dx_{2})\,V^{v}_{n}(dx_{1}).

\displaystyle V^{v}_{\leq kn}\leq_{st}V^{v}_{\leq n-1}

\displaystyle+\int V^{x_{1}}_{\leq n-1}(\xi^{1}_{x_{1}})\,V^{v}_{n}(dx_{1})+\cdots+\idotsint V^{x_{k-2}}_{\leq n-1}(\xi^{k-2}_{x_{k-2}})\,V^{x_{k-3}}_{n}(\xi^{k-3}_{x_{k-3}},dx_{k-2})\cdots V^{v}_{n}(dx_{1})

Here, the last term can be written as

\displaystyle\idotsint V^{x_{k-2}}_{n}(\xi^{k-2}_{x_{k-2}})

\displaystyle\,V^{x_{k-3}}_{n}(\xi^{k-3}_{x_{k-3}},dx_{k-2})\cdots V^{v}_{n}(dx_{1})

6
A criterion for subcriticality and exponential moments

We establish the setting of Section
5

with intensity measure
\lambda
replaced by
t\lambda
for some
t\geq 0
.
We use the so-called generations method to build lower bounds on
t_{T}
, see e.g.
[
29
,
38
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
.
As is common in percolation theory, we denote the underlying probability measure by
\mathbb{P}_{t}
,
to stress the dependence on the intensity parameter.

Lemma 6.1
.

We have
t_{T}\geq(D^{*}_{\varphi})^{-1}
. Moreover, we have
t_{T}>0
if and only if
D^{*}_{\varphi}<\infty
,

Proof.

If
D^{*}_{\varphi}=0
, then each Poisson point is isolated and
t_{T}=\infty
. If

D^{*}_{\varphi}=\infty
, then
c^{*}_{1}(t)=tD^{*}_{\varphi}=\infty
for any
t>0
and
t_{T}=0
.
Let
t\geq 0
and assume that
0<D^{*}_{\varphi}<\infty
.
The Proposition
5.13
implies that
c^{v}_{n}(t)\leq\mathbb{E}_{t}|W^{v,1}_{n}|
. By Proposition
5.14
we have

\mathbb{E}_{t}|W^{v,1}_{n}|\leq\mathbb{E}_{t}W_{n}=(c^{*}_{1}(t))^{n}
for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Therefore,

t_{T}\geq 1/D^{*}_{\varphi}>0
.
∎

In the remainder of the section, we shall assume that
D^{*}_{\varphi}<\infty
.

Remark 6.2
.

One can attempt to improve the lower bound of
t_{T}
by obtaining an estimate of the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}(|V_{n+l}^{v}|\mid C_{\leq n}^{v})\leq\gamma(t)|V_{n}^{v}|

for all
n\geq n_{0}
and some
l,n_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then as soon as

\gamma(t_{0})<1
there is no percolation and
t_{T}>t_{0}
. It is true that
in continuous models, obtaining such estimates for
l\geq 2
involves
appreciable technical difficulties.

By Proposition
5.9
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
we have

\displaystyle c^{v}_{n+2}(t)\leq c^{*}_{2}(t){c}^{v}_{n}(t).

(6.1)

This easily implies that if
c^{*}_{2}(t)<1
, then
t<t_{T}
. One of the main goals of this section is
to show that if
D^{*}_{\varphi}<\infty
and
c^{*}_{n}(t)<1
for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then
t<t_{T}
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
n,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and assume that
D^{*}_{\varphi}<\infty
.
Then for any
t,\delta>0

\displaystyle\esssup_{v\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{\delta|W^{v,n}_{\leq k}|}<\infty.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.13
we have
W_{\leq k}^{v,n}\leq_{st}W^{v,1}_{\leq kn}
. Then by Proposition
5.14
we obtain

\displaystyle\esssup_{v\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{\delta|W^{v,n}_{\leq k}|}\leq\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{\delta W_{\leq kn}}.

Since the offspring distribution of
W_{\leq kn}
is Poisson with parameter
c^{*}_{1}(t)<\infty
, it is well-known that
W_{\leq kn}

has exponential moments of each order; see e.g.
[
33
]
.
∎

Corollary 6.4
.

Assume that
D^{*}_{\varphi}<\infty
and let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then for any
t,\delta>0

\displaystyle\esssup_{v\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{\delta|V_{\leq n}^{v}|}<\infty.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.15
we have
V_{\leq n}^{v}\leq_{st}W^{v,1}_{\leq n}
. Then the result follows from Lemma
6.3
.
∎

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
h\colon{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
be a measurable bounded function and
t\geq 0
.
Assume that
D^{*}_{\varphi}<\infty
and
c^{*}_{h}(t):=\esssup\limits_{v\in{\mathbb{X}}}c^{v}_{h(v)}(t)<1
. Then

\displaystyle\esssup_{v\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mathbb{E}_{t}|\widetilde{W}^{v,h}|\leq\frac{c^{*}_{\leq h^{*}}(t)}{1-c^{*}_{h}(t)}<\infty,

where
h^{*}:=\|h\|_{\infty}
.

Proof.

By Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}|W^{v,h}|

\displaystyle=1+c_{h(v)}^{v}(t)+\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{t}\int|V^{x}_{h(x)}(\xi_{x}^{k})|\,W^{v,h}_{k}(dx)=1+c_{h(v)}^{v}(t)+\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{t}\int c^{x}_{h(x)}(t)\,W^{v,h}_{k}(dx)

\displaystyle\leq 1+c_{h(v)}^{v}(t)+c^{*}_{h}(t)(\mathbb{E}_{t}|W^{v,h}|-1).

Therefore,
\mathbb{E}_{t}|W^{v,h}|\leq(1+c_{h(v)}^{v}(t)-c^{*}_{h}(t))/(1-c^{*}_{h}(t))\leq(1-c^{*}_{h}(t))^{-1}
for any
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
.
Moreover, by Corollary
6.4
and Fubini’s theorem, we obtain that for any
v\in{\mathbb{X}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}|\widetilde{W}^{v,h}|

\displaystyle=c_{\leq h(v)}^{v}(t)+\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{t}\int|V^{x!}_{\leq h(x)}(\xi_{x}^{k})|\,W^{v,h}_{k}(dx)=c_{\leq h(v)}^{v}(t)+\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{t}\int(c_{\leq h(x)}^{x}(t)-1)\,W^{v,h}_{k}(dx)

\displaystyle\leq c_{\leq h(v)}^{v}(t)+(c^{*}_{\leq h^{*}}(t)-1)(\mathbb{E}_{t}|W^{v,h}|-1)<\frac{c^{*}_{\leq h^{*}}(t)}{1-c^{*}_{h}(t)}<\infty.

This proves the result.
∎

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

In the following, we generalize Corollary

Proposition 6.7

(A special criterion for subcriticality)
.

Let
v_{0}\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
t\geq 0
. If
D^{*}_{\varphi}<\infty
,
c^{v_{0}}(t)=c^{*}(t)
and

,
then
c^{*}(t)\leq c^{v_{0}}_{\leq n-1}(t)/(1-c^{v_{0}}_{n}(t))<\infty
and

.

Proof.

By Corollary
5.11
and Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle c^{*}(t)=c^{v_{0}}(t)\leq c^{v_{0}}_{\leq n-1}(t)+\mathbb{E}_{t}\int c^{x}(t)\,V^{v_{0}}_{n}(dx)\leq c^{v_{0}}_{\leq n-1}(t)+c^{v_{0}}_{n}(t)c^{v_{0}}(t).

Therefore,
c^{v_{0}}(t)\leq c^{v_{0}}_{\leq n-1}(t)/(1-c^{v_{0}}_{n}(t))<\infty
.

Theorem 6.8
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
6.5
there exists
\delta=\delta(t,h^{*})>0
such that

\displaystyle\esssup_{v\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{\delta|\widetilde{W}^{v,h}|}<\infty.

Proof.

To describe the total population sizes of
W^{v,h}
and
\widetilde{W}^{v,h}
, we define an exploration process.
Let’s consider the following spatial Markov process
(X_{k})_{k\geq 0}=(Y_{k},Z_{k})_{k\geq 0}

along with a sequence of families of random graphs
\{\xi^{k}_{x}:x\in Z_{k}\}
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, recursively as follows. We set

\displaystyle X_{0}=(\delta_{v},\delta_{v}),\quad X_{1}=(V^{v}_{\leq h(v)},V^{v}_{h(v)}),

and
\{\xi^{0}_{x}:x\in Z_{0}\}:=\{\xi^{v}\}
. Given
k\geq 1
,
(X_{n})_{n\leq k}
and
\big(\{\xi^{n}_{x}:x\in Z_{n}\}\big)_{n\leq k-1}

we let
\{\xi^{k}_{x}:x\in Z_{k}\}
be a family of random graphs which are conditionally independent given
Z_{k}
and
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi^{k}_{x}\in\cdot\mid Z_{k})=\mathbb{P}(\xi^{x}\in\cdot),\quad x\in Z_{k}.

Then we define

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}=

\displaystyle Y_{k}+\int{\mathbf{1}}\{d(v,x)=\min_{y\in Z_{k}}d(v,y)\}V^{x!}_{\leq h(x)}(\xi_{x}^{k})\,Z_{k}(dx),

\displaystyle Z_{k+1}=

\displaystyle Z_{k}+\int{\mathbf{1}}\{d(v,x)=\min_{y\in Z_{k}}d(v,y)\}(V^{x}_{h(x)}(\xi_{x}^{k})-\delta_{x})\,Z_{k}(dx).

Let
\tau:=\min(k\geq 1:Z_{k}=\emptyset)
. Note that
\tau
is a
stopping time w.r.t.
\{Z_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. Moreover,
\tau=|W^{v,h}|

and
Y_{\tau}=\widetilde{W}^{v,h}
in distribution. Then we can use the
well-known test function criteria to prove the existence of an
exponential moment for
\tau
(see
[
21
, Corollary 2,
p. 115]
). Let
\delta,\varepsilon>0
and

\tau_{k}:=\min(\tau,k)
for
k\geq 1
. Then for any
k\geq 1
with
probability one

\displaystyle e^{\delta\tau_{k}}\leq e^{\delta\tau_{k}+\varepsilon|Z_{\tau_{k}}|}=e^{\varepsilon|Z_{0}|}+\sum_{m=0}^{\tau_{k}-1}\left(e^{\delta(m+1)+\varepsilon|Z_{m+1}|}-e^{\delta m+\varepsilon|Z_{m}|}\right).

Note that by Corollary
6.4
we have as
\varepsilon\to 0

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}

\displaystyle\left(e^{\delta(m+1)+\varepsilon|Z_{m+1}|}-e^{\delta m+\varepsilon|Z_{m}|}\mid Z_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{\delta m+\varepsilon|Z_{m}|}\mathbb{E}_{t}\left(\exp\left(\delta+\varepsilon\int{\mathbf{1}}\{d(v,x)=\min_{y\in Z_{m}}d(v,y)\}(|V^{x}_{h(x)}(\xi_{x}^{m})|-1)\,Z_{m}(dx)\right)-1\mid Z_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\sim e^{\delta m+\varepsilon|Z_{m}|}\left(e^{\delta}\left(1+\varepsilon\int{\mathbf{1}}\{d(v,x)=\min_{y\in Z_{m}}d(v,y)\}(c^{x}_{h(x)}(t)-1)\,Z_{m}(dx)\right)-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\delta m+\varepsilon|Z_{m}|}\left(e^{\delta}\left(1+\varepsilon(c^{*}_{h}(t)-1)\right)-1\right).

Then there exist
\varepsilon,\delta>0
such that for any
m\geq 0

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\left(e^{\delta(m+1)+\varepsilon|Z_{m+1}|}-e^{\delta m+\varepsilon|Z_{m}|}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{t}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}\left(e^{\delta(m+1)+\varepsilon|Z_{m+1}|}-e^{\delta m+\varepsilon|Z_{m}|}\mid Z_{m}\right)\leq 0.

Therefore,
\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{\delta\tau_{k}}\leq\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{\varepsilon|Z_{0}|}=e^{\varepsilon}
for any

. Letting

\tau
uniformly in
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
.

Note that by Propositions
5.15
and
5.14
we have for any
k\geq 1

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{\delta|Y_{k}|}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t}\left(e^{\delta|Y_{k-1}|}\mathbb{E}_{t}\left(\exp\left(\delta\int{\mathbf{1}}\{d(v,x)=\min_{y\in Z_{k-1}}d(v,y)\}|V^{x!}_{\leq h(x)}(\xi_{x}^{k-1})|\,Z_{k-1}(dx)\right)\mid X_{k-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t}\left(e^{\delta|Y_{k-1}|}\int{\mathbf{1}}\{d(v,x)=\min_{y\in Z_{k-1}}d(v,y)\}\mathbb{E}_{t}(e^{\delta|V^{x!}_{\leq h(x)}(\xi_{x}^{k-1})|}\mid Z_{k-1})\,Z_{k-1}(dx)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{\delta|Y_{k-1}|}\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{\delta(W_{\leq h^{*}}-1)}\leq\left(\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{\delta(W_{\leq h^{*}}-1)}\right)^{k}.

By Theorem
6.5

|\widetilde{W}^{v,h}|
is a proper random variable. Then, by Fubini’s theorem, Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and the previous inequality, we also have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{\delta|\widetilde{W}^{v,h}|}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t}\left(e^{\delta|\widetilde{W}^{v,h}|}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{\mathbf{1}}\{\tau=k\}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{t}(e^{\delta|Y_{k}|}{\mathbf{1}}\{\tau=k\})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{2\delta|Y_{k}|}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tau=k)}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\big(\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{2\delta(W_{\leq h^{*}}-1)}\big)^{k}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tau=k)}.

Since

has exponential moments of all orders (see e.g.
[

]
)
and
\lim\limits_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{2\delta(W_{\leq h^{*}}-1)}=1
we get the required result from the uniform light tail property for
\tau
.
∎

7
Diameter distribution

We establish the setting of Section
5
with an intensity measure
t\lambda
for
t\geq 0
.
Denote by

\displaystyle d_{m}(A_{1},A_{2}):=\sup_{x\in A_{1},y\in A_{2}}d(x,y)

the maximum distance between the points of
A_{1},A_{2}\subset{\mathbb{X}}
. We use
the convention that
d_{m}(A,\emptyset):=0
for any

A\subset{\mathbb{X}}
. Define by
D(A):=d_{m}(A,A)
the diameter of a set

A\subset{\mathbb{X}}
. We will also use the same notations for
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
identifying
A
with
V(\mu)
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}\subset{\mathbb{X}}
,
A=A_{1}\cup A_{2}
, and
v\in A
. If
A_{1}\cap A_{2}\neq\emptyset
, then

\displaystyle D(A)\leq\min(D(A_{1})+D(A_{2}),2d_{m}(v,A)).

Proof.

Note that for any
x\in A_{1}\cap A_{2}
by the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle d_{m}(A_{1},A_{2})\leq d_{m}(x,A_{1})+d_{m}(x,A_{2})\leq d_{m}(A_{1}\cap A_{2},A_{1})+d_{m}(A_{1}\cap A_{2},A_{2})\leq D(A_{1})+D(A_{2}).

Therefore, the result follows from the fact that
D(A)=\max(D(A_{1}),D(A_{2}),d_{m}(A_{1},A_{2}))
, and a simple consequence of the triangle inequality, i.e.
D(A)\leq 2d_{m}(v,A)
.
∎

Denote for
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
the maximum length of the edges between generations
n-1
and
n
by

\displaystyle E^{v}_{n}:=\max_{x\in V^{v}_{n-1},y\in V^{v}_{n}:x\sim y}d(x,y).

We also define by
E^{v}_{\leq n}:=\max(E^{v}_{1},E^{v}_{2},\dots,E^{v}_{n})
and
E^{v}:=\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}E^{v}_{\leq n}
the maximal length among the edges between generations in
C^{v}_{\leq n}
and
C^{v}
respectively. Note that
E^{v}_{1}=d_{m}(v,V^{v}_{1})
. For
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
t,r\geq 0
we denote by

\displaystyle\phi^{v}_{t}(r):=1-\exp\left(-t\int_{B^{c}_{r}(v)}\varphi(v,x)\,\lambda(dx)\right),

where
B_{r}(v):=\{x\in{\mathbb{X}}:d(v,x)\leq r\}
is the closed ball of radius
r
centered at a point
v
in
{\mathbb{X}}
.
We also denote by
\phi^{*}_{t}(r):=\esssup_{v\in{\mathbb{X}}}\phi^{v}_{t}(r)
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
t,r\geq 0
. Then
\mathbb{P}_{t}(E^{v}_{1}>r)=\phi^{v}_{t}(r)\leq\phi^{*}_{t}(r)
.

Proof.

The claim follows from Proposition
5.1
since
V^{v}_{1}
is a Poisson process with intensity measure
K_{t\lambda}(0,\delta_{v})
.
∎

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
r\geq 0
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}(E^{v}_{\leq n}>r)\leq c^{v}_{\leq n-1}(t)\phi^{*}_{t}(r),\quad\mathbb{P}_{t}(E^{v}>r)\leq c^{v}(t)\phi^{*}_{t}(r).

Proof.

By subadditivity of a probability measure and Proposition
5.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}(E^{v}_{\leq n}>r)=\mathbb{P}_{t}\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}\{E^{v}_{k}>r\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}_{t}(E^{v}_{k}>r)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}_{t}\left(\bigcup_{x\in V^{v}_{k-1}}\{\max_{y\in V_{k}^{v}:x\sim y}d(x,y)>r\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{t}\int\mathbb{P}_{t}\left(\max_{y\in V_{k}^{v}:x\sim y}d(x,y)>r\mid C^{v}_{\leq k-1}\right)\,V^{v}_{k-1}(dx)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{t}\int 1-\exp\left(-t\int_{B^{c}_{r}(x)}\varphi(x,y)\bar{\varphi}(V^{v}_{\leq k-2},y)\,\lambda(dy)\right)\,V^{v}_{k-1}(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{t}\int\phi^{x}_{t}(r)\,V^{v}_{k-1}(dx)\leq c^{v}_{\leq n-1}(t)\phi^{*}_{t}(r).

The second inequality follows immediately from the first one, since
E^{v}_{\leq n}\uparrow E^{v}
a.s. as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Denote by
\tau^{v}:=\min(n\in{\mathbb{N}}:V^{v}_{n}=\emptyset)
the depth of
C^{v}
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then with probability one

\displaystyle d_{m}(v,V^{v}_{\leq n})\leq nE^{v}_{\leq n},\quad d_{m}(v,V^{v})\leq\tau^{v}E^{v}.

Proof.

Denote for
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
and
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mu
the minimal length of the paths between
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
within the graph
\mu
by
d^{\mu}(v_{1},v_{2})
. Note that for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
by the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle d_{m}(v,V^{v}_{k})=\max_{x\in V^{v}_{k}}d(v,x)\leq\max_{x\in V^{v}_{k}}d^{C^{v}_{\leq k}}(v,x)\leq E_{1}^{v}+\dots+E_{k}^{v}.

Therefore

\displaystyle d_{m}(v,V^{v}_{\leq n})=\max(d_{m}(v,V^{v}_{1}),d_{m}(v,V^{v}_{2}),\dots,d_{m}(v,V^{v}_{n}))\leq E_{1}^{v}+\dots+E_{n}^{v}\leq nE^{v}_{\leq n}.

The second inequality follows from the first one by monotone convergence.
∎

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
t,r\geq 0
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}(d_{m}(v,V^{v}_{\leq n})>r)\leq c^{v}_{\leq n-1}(t)\phi^{*}_{t}(r/n),\quad\mathbb{P}_{t}(d_{m}(v,V^{v})>r)\leq c^{v}_{\leq n-1}(t)\phi^{*}_{t}(r/n)+\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tau^{v}>n).

Proof.

By Lemma
7.4
and Proposition
7.3
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}(d_{m}(v,V^{v}_{\leq n})>r)\leq\mathbb{P}_{t}(nE^{v}_{\leq n}>r)\leq c^{v}_{\leq n-1}(t)\phi^{*}_{t}(r/n).

The second inequality follows from the first one and the following inclusion

\displaystyle\{d_{m}(v,V^{v})>r\}\subset\{d_{m}(v,V^{v}_{\leq n})>r\}\cup\{\tau^{v}>n\}.

∎

Remark 7.8
.

Suppose that
\phi^{*}_{t}(r)
and the tail distribution of depth
\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tau^{v}>r)
decay exponentially fast as

with the
exponents
\delta_{1}>0
and
\delta_{2}>0
respectively. Then using

has a heavy-tail (e.g., decay with

has a light-tailed distribution, then one can build a ”better” upper bound for the tail distribution of

\displaystyle Y_{k}+\int{\mathbf{1}}\{d(v,x)=\min_{y\in Z_{k}}d(v,y)\}V^{x!}_{\leq h(x)}(\xi_{x}^{k})\,Z_{k}(dx),

\displaystyle Z_{k+1}=

\displaystyle Z_{k}+\int{\mathbf{1}}\{d(v,x)=\min_{y\in Z_{k}}d(v,y)\}(V^{x}_{h(x)}(\xi_{x}^{k})-\delta_{x})\,Z_{k}(dx).

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t}\left(e^{\delta D\left(\widetilde{W}^{v,h}\right)}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{\mathbf{1}}\{\tau=k\}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{t}(e^{\delta D(Y_{k})}{\mathbf{1}}\{\tau=k\})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{2\delta D(Y_{k})}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tau=k)}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\big(\esssup_{v\in{\mathbb{X}}}\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{2\delta D(V^{v}_{\leq h^{*}})}\big)^{k}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tau=k)}.

Let

and define the measurable functions

and

\displaystyle d_{\varphi}^{(n)}(p,q):=

(8.1)

where

and
p_{n}:=q

\displaystyle d_{\varphi}^{(n)}(p,q):=\int\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}d_{\varphi}(p_{i},p_{i+1})\,\mathbb{Q}^{n-1}(d\boldsymbol{p}_{n-1}).

It follows from the Mecke equation (

. Analogously,
t^{n}d_{\varphi}^{[n]}(p,\cdot)

is the
\mathbb{Q}
-density of the expected number of
self-avoiding walks

(paths without loops) of length
n
starting in
(0,p)
and ending in a point with a mark
in a set from
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{M}})
.
From the symmetry property of
\varphi
we obtain
that
d_{\varphi}^{(n)}
and
d_{\varphi}^{[n]}
are symmetric. From stationarity, one can also notice that for given

n\geq 1
and
p,q\in{\mathbb{M}}
we have

\displaystyle d_{\varphi}^{[2n]}(p,q)\leq\int d_{\varphi}^{[n]}(p,r)d_{\varphi}^{[n]}(r,q)\,\mathbb{Q}(dr).

(8.2)

For a given measurable
L\colon{\mathbb{M}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\left\{\pm\infty\right\}
and
r_{1},r_{2}\in\left[1,\infty\right)
, we define the norms

\displaystyle\|L\|_{r_{1},r_{2}}:=

\displaystyle\left(\int\left(\int|L(p,q)|^{r_{1}}\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)\right)^{\frac{r_{2}}{r_{1}}}\,\mathbb{Q}(dq)\right)^{\frac{1}{r_{2}}},

(8.3)

\displaystyle\|L\|_{\infty,r_{2}}:=

\displaystyle\esssup_{p\in{\mathbb{M}}}\left(\int|L(p,q)|^{r_{2}}\,\mathbb{Q}(dq)\right)^{\frac{1}{r_{2}}},

(8.4)

\displaystyle\|L\|_{\infty,\infty}:=

\displaystyle\esssup_{p,q\in{\mathbb{M}}}|L(p,q)|.

(8.5)

Note that, by definition, we have
D^{*}_{\varphi}=\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}
.
Take
p\in{\mathbb{M}}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
t\geq 0
. By the multivariate Mecke equation, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}V^{(0,p)}_{n}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\cdot)=\int{\mathbf{1}}\{q\in\cdot\}v^{p,q}_{n}(t)\,\mathbb{Q}(dq),

(8.6)

where the density
v^{p,q}_{n}(t)
can be written as a linear combination of
integrals similar to those occurring in (
8.1
).
Since we can bound
V^{(0,p)}_{n}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times B)
by counting all
paths of length
n
without loops and ending in a measurable
B\subset{\mathbb{M}}
,
we have

\displaystyle v^{p,q}_{n}(t)\leq t^{n}d_{\varphi}^{[n]}(p,q)\leq t^{n}d_{\varphi}^{(n)}(p,q),\quad\mathbb{Q}\text{-a.e.\ $q\in{\mathbb{M}}$}

(8.7)

and therefore

\displaystyle v^{p}_{n}(t):=v^{(0,p)}_{n}(t)\leq t^{n}\int d_{\varphi}^{[n]}(p,q)\,\mathbb{Q}(dq)\leq t^{n}\int d_{\varphi}^{(n)}(p,q)\,\mathbb{Q}(dq).

(8.8)

We define

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}(t):=c^{*}_{\leq n-1}(t)+\|v_{n}(t)\|_{\infty,\infty},

(8.9)

where we recall that the first term is the
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
-norm of
p\mapsto c^{(0,p)}_{\leq n-1}(t)

and the second term is defined as the
\|\cdot\|_{\infty,\infty}
-norm of the function

(p,q)\mapsto v^{p,q}_{n}(t)
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
t\geq 0
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. If
\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}<\infty
and
\|d_{\varphi}^{[n]}\|_{\infty,\infty}<\infty
,
then
\Delta_{n}(t)<\infty
. Moreover, if
\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}<\infty
and
\|d_{\varphi}^{[n]}\|_{\infty,2}<\infty
, then
\Delta_{2n}(t)<\infty
.

Proof.

By the recursive structure of
d^{(n)}
we have
\|d_{\varphi}^{(n)}\|_{\infty,1}\leq\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}^{n}
.
Therefore, we obtain from (
8.8
) that

\displaystyle c^{*}_{\leq 2n-1}(t)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{2n-1}\|v_{k}(t)\|_{\infty}\leq 1+\sum_{k=1}^{2n-1}t^{k}\|d_{\varphi}^{(k)}\|_{\infty,1}\leq\frac{(t\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1})^{2n}-1}{t\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}-1},

where the final upper bound has to be interpreted as
2n
if
t\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}=1
.
On the other hand, we obtain from the inequalities (
8.7
), (
8.2
)
and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\|v_{2n}(t)\|_{\infty,\infty}\leq t^{2n}\|d_{\varphi}^{[2n]}\|_{\infty,\infty}\leq t^{2n}\esssup_{p,q\in{\mathbb{M}}}\int d_{\varphi}^{[n]}(p,r)d_{\varphi}^{[n]}(r,q)\,\mathbb{Q}(dr)\leq t^{2n}\|d_{\varphi}^{[n]}\|_{\infty,2}^{2}.

Hence

\displaystyle\Delta_{2n}(t)\leq\frac{(t\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1})^{2n}-1}{t\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}-1}+t^{2n}\|d_{\varphi}^{[2n]}\|_{\infty,\infty}\leq\frac{(t\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1})^{2n}-1}{t\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}-1}+t^{2n}\|d_{\varphi}^{[n]}\|_{\infty,2}^{2}.

(8.10)

∎

Finally, we are ready to state our main result on the strong sharpness of the phase transition, which is a significant generalization of the main result from
[
37
]
and some of the results from
[
3
]
. The main condition under which we can prove the strong sharpness is that there exists
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
satisfying

\displaystyle\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}+\|d_{\varphi}^{[n]}\|_{\infty,\infty}<\infty.

(8.11)

Theorem 8.2
.

Assume that
\|d_{\varphi}\|_{1,1}>0
. We have the following:

(i)

t_{T}\geq\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}^{-1}
.

(ii)

For
t<t_{T}
there exists
\delta_{1}:=\delta_{1}(t)

such that
\esssup\limits_{p\in{\mathbb{M}}}\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{\delta_{1}|V^{(0,p)}|}<\infty
.

(iii)

If

\displaystyle\esssup\limits_{p\in{\mathbb{M}}}\int_{\|x\|>u}\varphi(x,p,q)\,dx\,\mathbb{Q}(dq)

decays exponentially fast as
u\to\infty
, then for
t<t_{T}
there exists
\delta_{2}:=\delta_{2}(t)
such that

\displaystyle\esssup\limits_{p\in{\mathbb{M}}}\mathbb{E}_{t}e^{\delta_{2}D(V^{(0,p)})}<\infty.

(iv)

Suppose
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
satisfies (
8.11
). Then
t_{T}=t_{c}\in(0,\infty)
and

\bar{c}_{t_{c}}=\mathbb{E}_{t_{c}}\int|V^{(0,p)}|\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)=\infty
.

(v)

Suppose
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
satisfies (
8.11
). Then

\displaystyle\|c(t)\|_{r}\geq\frac{t_{c}}{\Delta_{n}(t)(t_{c}-t)},

for
t<t_{c}
and for all
r\in[1,\infty]
.

(vi)

Suppose that
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
satisfies (
8.11
) and let
\delta>0
.
Then

\displaystyle\|\theta(t)\|_{r}\geq\left(\frac{\bar{\theta}(t_{c})}{\delta}+\frac{{\mathbf{1}}\{\bar{\theta}(t_{c})=0\}}{2t\Delta_{n}(t)}\right)(t-t_{c}),

for all
t\in[t_{c},t_{c}+\delta]
and
r\in\left[1,\infty\right]
.

Proof.

Assertion (i) follows from Lemma

6.1
, (ii) follows from Theorem
6.10
,
(iii) follows from Theorem
7.11
. Other claims will be proven later on in this section. More precisely, (iv)
follows from Proposition

, Corollary

8.22
and the definition of

(v) follows from Proposition
8.15
and
(vi) follows from Theorem

.
∎

8.1
Sufficient condition for non-triviality of the phase transition

We start with the following simple observation.

Proposition 8.6
.

We have that
t^{(1)}_{T}\geq\|d_{\varphi}\|_{2,2}^{-1}
.

Proof.

The claim is trivial for
\|d_{\varphi}\|_{2,2}\in\{0,\infty\}
. Suppose that
\|d_{\varphi}\|_{2,2}\in(0,\infty)
.
Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. From the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality we have

\displaystyle\|d^{(n)}_{\varphi}\|_{1,2}^{2}=\int\left(\int d^{(n-1)}_{\varphi}(p,q_{1})d_{\varphi}(q_{1},q)\,\mathbb{Q}^{2}(d(p,q_{1}))\right)^{2}\,\mathbb{Q}(dq)\leq\|d_{\varphi}\|_{2,2}^{2}\|d^{(n-1)}_{\varphi}\|_{1,2}^{2}.

Therefore
\|d^{(n)}_{\varphi}\|_{1,2}\leq\|d_{\varphi}\|_{2,2}^{n}
.
On the other hand, we obtain from (
8.8
) and Jensen’s inequality that

\displaystyle\bar{c}_{n}(t)\leq t^{n}\|d^{(n)}_{\varphi}\|_{1,1}\leq t^{n}\|d^{(n)}_{\varphi}\|_{1,2},\quad t>0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\bar{c}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\bar{c}_{n}(t)\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}\|d_{\varphi}\|_{2,2}^{n},

which converges if
t<\|d_{\varphi}\|_{2,2}^{-1}
.
∎

Remark 8.7

Remark 8.11
.

\displaystyle\|d_{\varphi}\|_{1,1}\leq\max(\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1},\|d_{\varphi}\|_{2,2})\leq\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,2}\leq\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,\infty}.

Example 8.12
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and consider

4.4
with

g(p,q):=(p\vee q)^{\varepsilon}:=\max(p,q)^{\varepsilon}

p+q\geq p\vee q
.
It is easy to see that
\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}<\infty
if and only if

and take

\displaystyle|d_{\varphi}^{(n)}\|_{\infty,2}^{2}\leq m_{\rho}^{2n}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{1}[p_{1}(p_{1}\vee p_{2})\cdots(p_{n-1}\vee p_{n})]^{-\varepsilon}\,d\boldsymbol{p}_{n-1}\right)^{2}\,dp_{n}.

.

8.2
Susceptibility mean-field bound

The following Proposition is a refinement of
[
3
, Lemma 2.3]

and
[
8
, Proposition 2.1]
.

Proposition 8.14
.

Suppose that
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
satisfies (
8.11
).
Then
t_{T}=t^{(r)}_{T}
for all
r\in[1,\infty]
.

Proof.

We exclude the trivial case
\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}=0
, since
then
t_{T}=t^{(r)}_{T}=\infty
. Assume that
\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}>0
.
We only need to show that
t_{T}=t^{(\infty)}_{T}\geq t^{(1)}_{T}
.
By Corollary
5.11
we have

\displaystyle c^{*}(t)\leq c^{*}_{\leq n-1}(t)+\esssup_{p\in{\mathbb{M}}}\mathbb{E}_{t}\int\mathbb{E}_{t}\big[|V^{(x,q)}(\xi^{[n]})|\bigm|C^{(0,p)}_{\leq n}\big]\,V^{(0,p)}_{n}(d(x,q)).

By the definition of
\xi^{[n]}
and stationarity, the conditional expectation in the above integral
equals
c^{(0,q)}(t)
. Therefore, we obtain from the definition (
8.6
) of the density

v^{p,q}_{n}(t)
that

\displaystyle c^{*}(t)\leq c^{*}_{\leq n-1}(t)+\esssup_{p\in{\mathbb{M}}}\int c^{(0,q)}(t)v^{p,q}_{n}(t)\,\mathbb{Q}(dq).

It follows that
c^{*}(t)\leq c^{*}_{\leq n-1}(t)+\bar{c}(t)\|v_{n}(t)\|_{\infty,\infty}

and since
\bar{c}(t)\geq 1
we obtain

\displaystyle c^{*}(t)\leq\Delta_{n}(t)\bar{c}(t).

(8.13)

By assumption (
8.11
) and Lemma
8.1
we have
\Delta_{n}(t)<\infty
,
so that the asserted inequality
t_{T}\geq t^{(1)}_{T}
follows.
∎

Proposition 8.15

(Susceptibility mean-field bound)
.

Suppose
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
satisfies (
8.11
). Then

\displaystyle\|c(t)\|_{r}\geq\frac{t_{T}}{\Delta_{n}(t)(t_{T}-t)}

(8.14)

for
t<t_{T}
and for all
r\in[1,\infty]
.

Proof.

To prove this result, we need to recall some of the notation from
[
3
]
. Define

\displaystyle T_{t}(p,q)=\int\mathbb{P}_{t}\big((0,p)\leftrightarrow(x,q)\text{ in }\xi^{(0,p),(x,q)}\big)\,dx

and let the integral operator
\mathcal{T}_{t}
act as

(\mathcal{T}_{t}f)(p)=\int T_{t}(p,q)f(q)\,\mathbb{Q}(dq)
, for every
square-integrable function
f
on
{\mathbb{M}}
(with respect to the
probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
). Let

t_{o}=\inf\{t>0:\|\mathcal{T}_{t}\|_{op}=\infty\}
, where, as usual,
\|\cdot\|_{op}
refers to the operator norm for a linear operator on a Banach space.

By
[
3
, Lemma 2.3]
(which applies without
Assumption D from that paper) and Proposition

8.14
we have
t_{o}=t_{T}
. From
[
8
, Lemma
3.2]
, we know

\|\mathcal{T}_{t}\|_{op}\leq\|T_{t}\|_{\infty,1}
(this is proven by
Schur’s test). Since
c^{(0,p)}(t)=1+t\int T_{t}(p,q)\,\mathbb{Q}(dq)
we have
c^{*}(t)=1+t\|T_{t}\|_{\infty,1}
. By

[
3
, Theorem 2.5]
(which again does not require
Assumption D) we have

\|\mathcal{T}_{t}\|_{op}\geq(t_{0}-t)^{-1}
for
t\in(0,t_{0})
. Hence

\|T_{t}\|_{\infty,1}\geq(t_{T}-t)^{-1}
and

c^{*}(t)\geq t_{T}(t_{T}-t)^{-1}
for
t\in(0,t_{T})
. For
r=1
, the asserted result now follows from the inequality (
8.13
). The general case
r\geq 1
follows from Hölder’s inequality.
∎

8.3
Strong sharpness of the phase transition

Analogously to
[
3
]
, we introduce a continuous and
mark-dependent analogy of the magnetization originally introduced by
Aizenman and Barsky
[
1
]
. Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
be a
parameter with which we enrich the marked RCM by adding to each vertex
a uniform
(0,1)
(Lebesgue) label (independent of everything else),
and let
\mathbb{P}_{t,\gamma}
denote the resulting probability measure. A
vertex
x\in\eta
is called a
ghost
vertex if its label is at
most
\gamma
, and we write
x\in{\mathcal{G}}
. Similarly, we write

x\leftrightarrow{\mathcal{G}}
if
x
is connected to a ghost vertex. We
define magnetization as follows

\displaystyle M(t,\gamma,p):=\mathbb{P}_{t,\gamma}((0,p)\leftrightarrow{\mathcal{G}}\text{ in }\xi^{(0,p)}).

(8.15)

In accordance with our previous notation, we
use the
L^{r}(\mathbb{Q})
-norms for
r\in[1,\infty]
to define

\widebar{M}(t,\gamma):=\|M(t,\gamma,\cdot)\|_{1},\quad M^{*}(t,\gamma):=\|M(t,\gamma,\cdot)\|_{\infty}.

(8.16)

Recall the definition of
\xi^{Q_{0}}
and
C^{Q_{0}}
in Subsection
4.2
.
We assume that
Q_{0}
is also independent of the labels.
Note that

\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)=\mathbb{P}_{t,\gamma}(\text{$(0,Q_{0})\leftrightarrow{\mathcal{G}}$ in $\xi^{Q_{0}}$})
.
We will also need the following functions. For

t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, we define

\displaystyle\bar{c}_{f}(t):=\mathbb{E}_{t}|C^{Q_{0}}|{\mathbf{1}}\{|C^{Q_{0}}|<\infty\}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}n\mathbb{P}_{t}(|C^{Q_{0}}|=n),

(8.17)

and for
\gamma\in(0,1)
we also define the “ghost-free” mean size of the cluster of a typical vertex

\bar{c}(t,\gamma)=\mathbb{E}_{t,\gamma}|C^{Q_{0}}|{\mathbf{1}}\{C^{Q_{0}}\cap{\mathcal{G}}=\emptyset\}.

(8.18)

It is easy to relate the above function to the mean size of the finite cluster of a typical vertex and
the percolation probability
\bar{\theta}(t)=\mathbb{P}_{t,\gamma}\big(\big|C^{Q_{0}}\big|=\infty\big)
.

Lemma 8.16
.

Let
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)=\bar{\theta}(t),\quad\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\bar{c}(t,\gamma)=\bar{c}_{f}(t).

Proof.

The proof is direct and follows the same lines as in
[
3
]
.
For
\gamma\in(0,1)
we have

\displaystyle\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}_{t,\gamma}((0,Q_{0})\nleftrightarrow{\mathcal{G}}\text{ in }\xi^{Q_{0}})=1-\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathbb{P}_{t,\gamma}(C^{Q_{0}}\cap{\mathcal{G}}=\emptyset,|C^{Q_{0}}|=n)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}(1-\gamma)^{n}\mathbb{P}_{t}(|C^{Q_{0}}|=n)=1-\mathbb{E}_{t}(1-\gamma)^{|C^{Q_{0}}|}.

(8.19)

Letting
\gamma\to 0
, we obtain the first result from monotone convergence.
It is also easy to see that the function

\gamma\mapsto\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)
is analytic on
(0,1)
for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

For
\gamma>0
we obtain from
our independence assumption that
|C^{Q_{0}}|<\infty

a.s. on the event
\{C^{Q_{0}}\cap{\mathcal{G}}=\emptyset\}
.
We then have

\displaystyle\bar{c}(t,\gamma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}n\mathbb{P}_{t,\gamma}(|C^{Q_{0}}|=n,C^{Q_{0}}\cap{\mathcal{G}}=\emptyset)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}n(1-\gamma)^{n}\mathbb{P}_{t}(|C^{Q_{0}}|=n).

(8.20)

As
\gamma\to 0
, we obtain the second result from monotone convergence.
∎

We will also need the following lemma on differential inequalities for the magnetization.

Lemma 8.17

(Aizenman-Barsky differential inequalities on the magnetization)
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
t>0
and assume that
\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}<\infty
. Then we have

(i)

\frac{\partial\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}{\partial t}\leq\frac{1-\gamma}{t}M^{*}(t,\gamma)\frac{\partial\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}{\partial\gamma}
.

(ii)

\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)\leq\gamma\frac{\partial\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}{\partial\gamma}+\|M(t,\gamma)\|_{2}^{2}+tM^{*}(t,\gamma)\frac{\partial\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}{\partial t}
.

Proof.

The results follow from the Leibniz differentiation rule and
[
3
, Lemma 3.8]
.
∎

Lemma 8.18
.

Let
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
. Then

\displaystyle M^{*}(t,\gamma)\leq\Delta_{n}(t)\widebar{M}(t,\gamma).

(8.21)

Proof.

If
\Delta_{n}(t)=\infty
or
\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}=0
,
then inequality (
8.21
) is trivial. Therefore, we can assume that

\Delta_{n}(t)<\infty
and
\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}>0
.
Fix
p\in{\mathbb{M}}
. We have

\displaystyle M(t,\gamma,p)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{t,\gamma}((0,p)\leftrightarrow{\mathcal{G}}\text{ in }\xi^{(0,p)})=\mathbb{P}_{t,\gamma}\bigg(\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\{C_{k}^{(0,p)}\cap{\mathcal{G}}\neq\emptyset\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{t,\gamma}\left(C_{\leq n-1}^{(0,p)}\cap{\mathcal{G}}\neq\emptyset\right)+\mathbb{P}_{t,\gamma}\left(C_{\geq n}^{(0,p)}\cap{\mathcal{G}}\neq\emptyset\right)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{E}_{t}(1-\gamma)^{|C_{\leq n-1}^{(0,p)}|}+\mathbb{P}_{t,\gamma}\left(C_{\geq n}^{(0,p)}\cap{\mathcal{G}}\neq\emptyset\right)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma c_{\leq n-1}^{(0,p)}(t)+\mathbb{E}_{t,\gamma}\int{\mathbf{1}}\big\{(x,q)\leftrightarrow{\mathcal{G}}\text{ in }C_{\geq n}^{(0,p)}\big\}\,C^{(0,p)}_{n}(d(x,q)),

where we have used the Bernoulli inequality for the last line.
To treat the above second term
I_{2}
, say, we let
D^{(0,p)}_{\leq n}
denote the graph

C^{(0,p)}_{\leq n}
, where all points (vertices) are marked by their labels, except those of

C^{(0,p)}_{n}
. Then

\displaystyle I_{2}=\mathbb{E}_{t,\gamma}\int\mathbb{P}\big((x,q)\leftrightarrow{\mathcal{G}}\text{ in }C_{\geq n}^{(0,p)}\bigm|D^{(0,p)}_{\leq n}\big)\,C^{(0,p)}_{n}(d(x,q)).

By the spatial Markov property (in fact, we need the more refined
[
4
, Theorem 7.1]
)
and stochastic monotonicity (as in Corollary
5.11
) the conditional probability occurring above can be
bounded by
M(t,\gamma,p)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle I_{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{t}\int M(t,\gamma,q)\,C^{(0,p)}_{n}(d(x,q))=\int M(t,\gamma,q)v^{p,q}_{n}(t)\,\mathbb{Q}(dq)\leq\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)\|v_{n}(t)\|_{\infty,\infty},

where the equality comes directly from the definition of the densities
v^{p,q}_{n}(t)
.
Since
\gamma\leq\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)
we obtain the assertion.
∎

Lemma 8.19
.

Let
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
and assume that
\bar{c}_{f}(t)=\infty
. Then

\displaystyle\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)\geq\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\Delta_{n}(t)+(\Delta_{n}(t))^{2}}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\frac{1}{\Delta_{n}(t)}.

(8.22)

Proof.

We proceed similarly to the proof of
[
3
, Corollary 3.11]
.
If
\Delta_{n}(t)=\infty
, then the inequality (
8.22
) is trivial. Assume that

\Delta_{n}(t)<\infty
and
\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}>0
. Then we can use the preceding lemmas. Inserting the first inequality of
Lemma
8.17
, and using simple
observation
\|M(t,\gamma)\|_{2}^{2}\leq M^{*}(t,\gamma)\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)

we obtain

\displaystyle\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)\leq\gamma\frac{\partial\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}{\partial\gamma}+M^{*}(t,\gamma)\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)+(1-\gamma)(M^{*}(t,\gamma))^{2}\frac{\partial\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}{\partial\gamma}.

Then by Lemma
8.18
we get

\displaystyle\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)\leq\gamma\frac{\partial\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}{\partial\gamma}+\Delta_{n}(t)\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)^{2}+(1-\gamma)\Delta_{n}^{2}(t)\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)^{2}\frac{\partial\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}{\partial\gamma}.

(8.23)

In the remainder of the proof, we fix
t
and drop this argument from our notation.
By (
8.19
) the function
M
(given by
\gamma\mapsto M(\gamma):=\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)
)
is strictly increasing and therefore has a differentiable inverse
M^{-1}
.
Then we can rewrite (
8.23
) for all
x
in the range of
M
as

\displaystyle x\leq\frac{M^{-1}(x)}{(M^{-1})^{\prime}(x)}+\Delta_{n}x^{2}+(1-M^{-1}(x))\frac{\Delta_{n}^{2}x^{2}}{(M^{-1})^{\prime}(x)}.

(8.24)

We have
\frac{d}{dx}(x^{-1}M^{-1}(x))=x^{-1}(M^{-1})^{\prime}(x)-x^{-2}M^{-1}(x)
.
Multiplying (
8.24
) by
x^{-2}(M^{-1})^{\prime}(x)
we therefore obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\big(x^{-1}M^{-1}(x)\big)\leq\Delta_{n}(M^{-1})^{\prime}(x)+(1-M^{-1}(x))\Delta_{n}^{2}\leq\Delta_{n}(M^{-1})^{\prime}(x)+\Delta_{n}^{2}.

We wish to integrate this inequality on
[0,y]
for some
y
in the range of
M
.
To do so, we note that
M^{-1}(0)=0
and

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{M^{-1}(x)}{x}=(M^{-1})^{\prime}(0)=\frac{1}{M^{\prime}(0)}=\frac{1}{\bar{c}_{f}(t)}=0,

where the penultimate identity follows from (
8.19
) and the final one
from our assumption
c_{f}(t)=\infty
. Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle y^{-1}M^{-1}(y)\leq\Delta_{n}M^{-1}(y)+\Delta_{n}^{2}y,

that is
\gamma/\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)\leq\Delta_{n}(t)\gamma+\Delta_{n}(t)^{2}\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)

for each
\gamma>0
. Since
\gamma\leq\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)
, the first
inequality in (
8.22
) follows.
The second is then a consequence of
\Delta_{n}(t)\geq 1
, which is true by definition.
∎

Proposition 8.20
.

Let
t>t_{T}^{(1)}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. If
\bar{\theta}(t_{T}^{(1)})=0
, then

\displaystyle\bar{\theta}(t)\geq\frac{t-t_{T}^{(1)}}{2t\Delta_{n}(t)}.

(8.25)

Proof.

Note that if
\Delta_{n}(t)=\infty
, then the inequality
(
8.25
) is trivial. Suppose that

\Delta_{n}(t)<\infty
and
\|d_{\varphi}\|_{\infty,1}>0
. Then we have
by Proposition
8.14
that
t_{T}=t_{T}^{(1)}=t_{T}^{(\infty)}
. Let

t^{\prime}>t_{T}
and
t\in(t_{T},t^{\prime}]
. Multiplying the second inequality of
Lemma
8.17
by

\gamma^{-1}\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)^{-1}
and using simple observation

\|M(t,\gamma)\|_{2}^{2}\leq M^{*}(t,\gamma)\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma}\leq\frac{1}{\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}\frac{\partial\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}{\partial\gamma}+\frac{M^{*}(t,\gamma)}{\gamma}+\frac{tM^{*}(t,\gamma)}{\gamma\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}\frac{\partial\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}{\partial t}.

By Lemma
8.18
we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}\frac{\partial\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}{\partial\gamma}+\frac{\Delta_{n}(t)\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}{\gamma}+\frac{t\Delta_{n}(t)}{\gamma}\frac{\partial\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}\frac{\partial\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}{\partial\gamma}+\frac{\Delta_{n}(t^{\prime})}{\gamma}\left(\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)+t\frac{\partial\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)}{\partial t}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\log(\widebar{M}(t,\gamma))}{\partial\gamma}+\frac{\Delta_{n}(t^{\prime})}{\gamma}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(t\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)\right).

We now integrate the above inequality over

(t,\gamma)\in[t_{T},t^{\prime}]\times[\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}]
, where

0<\gamma_{1}\leq\gamma_{2}<1
. Since all the integrands are non-negative, we can use Fubini’s theorem to exchange the order of
the integrals, and we will also use the properties of the function

\widebar{M}(t,\gamma)
, i.e. non-negativity and increasing in
t
and

\gamma
. Therefore

\displaystyle(t^{\prime}-t_{T})\log(\gamma_{2}/\gamma_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t_{T}}^{t^{\prime}}\log(\widebar{M}(t,\gamma_{2})/\widebar{M}(t,\gamma_{1}))\,dt+\Delta_{n}(t^{\prime})\int_{\gamma_{1}}^{\gamma_{2}}\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(t^{\prime}\widebar{M}(t^{\prime},\gamma)-t_{T}\widebar{M}(t_{T},\gamma)\right)\,d\gamma

\displaystyle\leq(t^{\prime}-t_{T})\log(\widebar{M}(t^{\prime},\gamma_{2})/\widebar{M}(t_{T},\gamma_{1}))+\Delta_{n}(t^{\prime})t^{\prime}\widebar{M}(t^{\prime},\gamma_{2})\log(\gamma_{2}/\gamma_{1}).

Dividing by
\log(\gamma_{2}/\gamma_{1})
we get

\displaystyle t^{\prime}-t_{T}\leq(t^{\prime}-t_{T})\left(\frac{\log(\widebar{M}(t^{\prime},\gamma_{2}))}{\log(\gamma_{2})-\log(\gamma_{1})}-\frac{\log(\widebar{M}(t_{T},\gamma_{1}))}{\log(\gamma_{2})-\log(\gamma_{1})}\right)+\Delta_{n}(t^{\prime})t^{\prime}\widebar{M}(t^{\prime},\gamma_{2}).

(8.26)

Since
\bar{\theta}(t_{T})=0
, the cluster of a typical vertex is finite
almost surely. Therefore
\bar{c}_{f}(t_{T})=\mathbb{E}_{t_{T}}|C^{Q_{0}}|
.
Moreover, letting
t\uparrow t_{T}
in (
8.14
),
we obtain
\bar{c}(t_{T})=\infty
.
By Lemma
8.19
we have for
0<\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}
that

\displaystyle-\frac{\log(\widebar{M}(t_{T},\gamma_{1}))}{\log(\gamma_{2})-\log(\gamma_{1})}\leq\frac{\log(\sqrt{2}\Delta_{n}(t_{T}))-\frac{1}{2}\log(\gamma_{1})}{\log(\gamma_{2})-\log(\gamma_{1})}.

As
\gamma_{1}\to 0
, the above right-hand side tends to
\frac{1}{2}
.
Hence it follows from (
8.26
) that

\displaystyle\widebar{M}(t^{\prime},\gamma_{2})\geq\frac{t^{\prime}-t_{T}}{2t^{\prime}\Delta_{n}(t^{\prime})}.

Letting
\gamma_{2}\to 0
and using Lemma
8.16
concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem 8.21

(Percolation mean-field bound)
.

Let
\delta>0
. Suppose that
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
satisfies (
8.11
). Then

\displaystyle\|\theta(t)\|_{r}\geq\left(\frac{\bar{\theta}(t_{T})}{\delta}+\frac{{\mathbf{1}}\{\bar{\theta}(t_{T})=0\}}{2t\Delta_{n}(t)}\right)(t-t_{T}),

(8.27)

for all
t\in[t_{T},t_{T}+\delta]
and
r\in[1,\infty]
.

Proof.

By Hölder’s inequality, we get
\|\theta(t)\|_{r}\geq\|\theta(t)\|_{1}\equiv\bar{\theta}(t)
for all
r\in\left[1,\infty\right]
.
If
\bar{\theta}(t_{T})>0
, then the result follows from the monotonicity of
\bar{\theta}(t)
.
Otherwise, the result follows from Proposition
8.20
.
∎

Corollary 8.22

(Sharpness of phase transition)
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
8.14
, we have
t_{c}=t_{T}
.

Proof.

As already noticed in Section
4
we always have
t_{T}\leq t_{c}
. Let
t>t_{T}
. By
Theorem
8.21
there exists
A\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{M}})
with
\mathbb{Q}(A)>0
and

\theta^{p}(t)>0
for all
p\in A
. Therefore
t\geq t_{c}
and hence
t_{T}\geq t_{c}
.
∎

Remark 8.23
.

Let us consider here the hyperbolic counterpart of the stationary marked RCM; see
[
7
]
.
In this case
{\mathbb{X}}:=\mathbb{H}^{d}\times{\mathbb{M}}
, where
\mathbb{H}^{d}
is the
d
-dimensional hyperbolic space (with
d\geq 2
)
as in Example
6.11
and
{\mathbb{M}}
is as before. Assume that

\lambda=\mathcal{H}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
, where
\mathcal{H}^{d}
denotes the Haar measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

and
\mathbb{Q}
is a probability measure on
{\mathbb{M}}
, also as before.
Assume that the connection function is given by
\varphi((x,p),(y,q))=\tilde{\varphi}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}(x,y),p,q)
for
some measurable
\tilde{\varphi}\colon{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{M}}^{2}\to[0,1]
such that
\tilde{\varphi}(x,\cdot)
is symmetric
for all
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
. Fix a point
o\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
.
Since the space
\mathbb{H}^{d}
is homogeneous, we can argue as
in Remark
4.1
to see that

\displaystyle t_{c}=\sup\{t\geq 0:\mathbb{P}_{t}(|C^{(o,p)}|<\infty)=0\text{ for $\mathbb{Q}$-a.e.\ $p$}\},\quad t_{T}=\sup\big\{t\geq 0:\esssup_{p\in{\mathbb{M}}}\mathbb{E}_{t}|C^{(o,p)}|<\infty\big\}.

Define
d_{\tilde{\varphi}}(p,q)=\int\tilde{\varphi}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}(x,y),p,q)\,\mathcal{H}^{d}(dx)
for

(p,q)\in{\mathbb{M}}^{2}
. Most of the results of this section
remain true in this setting, provided our assumptions are suitably modified
(replacement of
d_{\varphi}
by
d_{\tilde{\varphi}}
). Indeed, the geometry
of the ambient space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
does not enter most of our arguments.
One exception is Proposition
8.9
. However, we believe that,
under the assumption
\|d_{\tilde{\varphi}}\|_{1,1}<\infty
, the proof of

[
9
, Proposition 1.1]

can be extended to the marked case, just as in the Euclidean marked case.
We would then obtain that our condition (
8.11
)
(with hyperbolic
d_{\tilde{\varphi}}
) implies the strong sharp phase transition at
t_{T}=t_{c}\in(0,\infty)
, just as in the marked stationary Euclidean
case.
Thanks to
[
9
, Proposition 1.1]

the condition from Proposition
8.8
is sufficient for
t_{c}<\infty
.
