Composition for Pufferfish Privacy
Jiamu Bai, Guanlin He, Xin Gu, Daniel Kifer, Kiwan Maeng

Abstract
. When creating public data products out of confidential datasets, inferential/posterior-based privacy definitions, such as Pufferfish, provide compelling privacy semantics for data with correlations. However, such privacy definitions are rarely used in practice because they do not always compose. For example, it is possible to design algorithms for these privacy definitions that have no leakage when run once but reveal the entire dataset when run more than once. We prove necessary and sufficient conditions that must be added to ensure linear composition for Pufferfish mechanisms, hence avoiding such privacy collapse. These extra conditions turn out to be differential privacy-style inequalities, indicating that achieving both the interpretable semantics of Pufferfish for correlated data and composition benefits requires adopting differentially private mechanisms to Pufferfish. We show that such translation is possible through a concept called the a ⁡ ( b ) {{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}} -influence curve, and many existing differentially private algorithms can be translated with our framework into a composable Pufferfish algorithm. We illustrate the benefit of our new framework by designing composable Pufferfish algorithms for Markov chains that significantly outperform prior work.

Introduction

Differential privacy (DP)
(
16
)
is a gold standard in privacy protection
and is deployed in many important real-world applications by organizations such as the
U.S. Census Bureau
(
2
;
34
)
, Uber
(
27
;
26
)
, Apple
(
46
)
, Meta
(
38
;
53
)
, Microsoft
(
10
)
, and Google
(
19
;
49
;
22
)
.
This success is not only due to differential privacy’s rigorous guarantees against data reconstruction attacks
(
16
;
14
)
but also due to its composition properties
(
35
;
28
)
. Composition
measures the total privacy loss associated with a collection of mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1},\dots,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{k}
in terms of their individual
privacy losses. For example, if each
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{i}
satisfies differential privacy with parameter
\epsilon_{i}
, the combined release of all of their outputs satisfies differential privacy with parameter
\epsilon=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\epsilon_{i}
(i.e., the privacy parameter composes linearly). This property is critical because it allows a larger mechanism to be created out of smaller, simpler mechanisms. Without composition, performing a privacy analysis of a complex disclosure avoidance system becomes intractable
(
24
)
.

The semantics of differential privacy use the hypothetical/counterfactual world framework
(
47
)
: for every hypothetical database
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}
and secret about a person (e.g., the contents of one person’s record), there is an associated counterfactual database
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime}
in which the secret has been completely scrubbed from the database.
This counterfactual database is considered a risk-free baseline for that person, i.e., any data release from the counterfactual database
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime}
poses no risk to that person. The differential privacy constraints on a mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
guarantee that for any pair
({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime})
of the hypothetical database
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}
and any of its counterfactual databases
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime}
, inference about a
person based on the output of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})
would be nearly the same as the inference based on
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime})
.

However, for datasets with correlations, such a guarantee may not be meaningful
(
29
;
30
)
. For example, consider a medical dataset
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}
in which one person’s medical status (e.g., whether the person has an infectious disease) can have a causal effect on others (e.g., they may infect close relatives, who may then go on to infect others). Removing one person’s record creates a counterfactual database
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime}
that is not necessarily risk-free for that person, as other records in the counterfactual database provide strong clues about the missing record.
For differential privacy to provide a meaningful privacy guarantee in such cases, the risk-free counterfactual database must be the one with all the records removed (one contagious individual can potentially affect all records).
However, ensuring that a data release from a hypothetical database is similar to a release from this risk-free (empty) database would entirely ruin utility.

Inferential/posterior-based
privacy definitions have emerged as an alternative privacy concept for such settings where data are correlated
(
30
;
21
;
50
;
9
)
.
With these privacy definitions,
the data curator specifies a set of distributions for the dataset, and it is ensured that a Bayesian adversary using those distributions would learn little about any target individual.
However, posterior-based definitions are difficult to deploy in practice because
they do not guarantee composition
. We show that it is possible to design a mechanism that has zero privacy leakage under posterior-based definitions when run once but has a good chance of revealing the entire database (i.e., no privacy) when run more than once (Section
4
).

To counter the lack of composition guarantees,
we study the following question, focusing on one of the popular posterior-based privacy definitions,
Pufferfish

(
30
)
:
what additional constraints need to be added to Pufferfish in order to guarantee linear composition?
We prove necessary and sufficient conditions that establish a formal link between Pufferfish and differential privacy. That is, in order to
ensure that the Pufferfish privacy parameter
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
composes linearly,
one should design mechanisms that satisfy differential privacy with some
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
and translate it to Pufferfish privacy.
We show that such translation is possible through a concept called the
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve, and existing differential privacy mechanisms can be translated into composable Pufferfish mechanisms with our framework.
Our result generalizes the previous framework of Song et al.
(
45
)
, which showed the Laplace mechanism for differential privacy can be used to achieve composable Pufferfish for Markov networks.
Our contributions are the following:

•

We provide examples of posterior-based privacy mechanisms that cause privacy collapse upon composition (Section
4
), i.e., have zero leakage when run once but provide no meaningful privacy when run multiple times.
This privacy collapse can happen with as few as two runs. While these mechanisms are pathological, they illustrate the fact that the constraints imposed on algorithms by posterior-based definitions are incomplete, and additional constraints are needed to avoid privacy collapse.

•

We prove
necessary conditions
for a Pufferfish mechanism to ensure linear composition and avoid privacy collapse.
Our result shows that a Pufferfish mechanism must meet a class of inequalities that we call the NfC constraints (Section
5
), which subsume the differential privacy inequalities.
We also show that only a subset of the NfC constraints are post-processing invariant, and those subsets still contain differential privacy-style inequalities but rule out many others.
The implication is that Pufferfish should be augmented with differential privacy-style inequalities in order for its privacy parameter
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
to compose.

•

We next study
sufficient conditions
for composition — how to construct composable Pufferfish privacy mechanisms — for general tabular datasets (Section
6
).
We show that if a mechanism satisfies per-entry
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
-differential privacy (with a carefully chosen
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
), it also satisfies
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
-Pufferfish privacy with a certain
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
, and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
will compose linearly.
We introduce a concept called an
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve
, which links the
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
parameter to the appropriate
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
parameter. This part is a generalization of the work of Song et al.
(
45
)
, whose technique was limited to the Laplace mechanism and adversarial priors that all shared the same Markov network structure.

•

We empirically compare our approach to prior work on composable Pufferfish mechanisms
(
45
)
to show that we can answer popularity queries over Markov chains with significantly better accuracy, as our framework allows adapting other differential privacy mechanisms that are better-suited to the query of interest, while prior work
(
45
)
is fixated to Laplace mechanism.

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}
according to the prior distribution
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}}
.

Protecting privacy requires a specification of what needs to be protected. A potential secret
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{1}
is true or whether
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{2}
is true. Similarly, specifying
({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}},\neg{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}})
as a pair means the goal is to prevent an attacker from learning whether or not
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}
is true.
Let
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
denote a set of all pairs of potential secrets that a data curator wishes to protect.

2.1.
Differential Privacy

A privacy definition is a set of restrictions that a mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
has to follow. One of the most foundational is
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
-differential privacy, also known as pure differential privacy.

Definition 0 (Pure Differential Privacy
(
16
)
).

Given a privacy parameter
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}\geq 0
, a mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
satisfies
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
-differential privacy if, for all pairs of datasets
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime}
that differ on the contents of one person’s record, and all
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}
in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{range}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}})
,

\Pr[{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}]\;\leq\;e^{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}}\Pr[{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}].

(Note: the probability is with respect to the randomness in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
only.)

One can think of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}
as a hypothetical dataset and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime}
as a counterfactual dataset in which the record of one person has been replaced. Even if the attacker knows whether a person’s record has been replaced or not, differential privacy guarantees that inference about a person based on the output
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})
would be similar to inference based on
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime})
. Hence, if the person’s influence is truly eliminated in counterfactual worlds, her privacy is protected.

Differential privacy has two important properties:
post-processing invariance
and
linear composition
. Post-processing invariance means that if
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
satisfies
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
-differential privacy, and
f
is any postprocessing function, the combined function
f\circ{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
(which first runs
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
on the data and then
f
on the output of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
) satisfies differential privacy with the same privacy parameter
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
.
Linear composition means that if
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1},\dots,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{k}
are a sequence of mechanisms, and each
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{i}
satisfies
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}_{i}
-differential privacy, the mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{*}
, which runs all of those mechanisms and returns their outputs, satisfies
\sum_{i=1}^{k}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}_{i}
-differential privacy.
In the rest of this paper, when we say a mechanism ensures composition, we mean it ensures linear composition or better (sub-linear).

A special variant of differential privacy that is particularly useful for this paper is per-entry differential privacy:

Definition 0 (Per-Entry DP).

Given a privacy parameter
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}\geq 0
, a mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
satisfies per-entry
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
-differential privacy if, for all pairs of tabular datasets
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime}
that differ on the contents of one attribute of one person’s record, and all
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}
in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{range}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}})
,

\Pr[{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}]\;\leq\;e^{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}}\Pr[{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}].

The difference between Definitions
1
and
2
is that in the latter, the counterfactual database is formed by only replacing part of a record (the value of one attribute) instead of the entire record.
Definition
2
can naturally extend to a group of
k
entries, in which case the privacy linearly degrades with
k
:

Definition 0 (Group DP).

A per-entry
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
-differential privacy mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
achieves
k{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
-differential privacy for a group of
k
entries. That is, if
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime}
differ by
k
entries, for all
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}
in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{range}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}})
,

\Pr[{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}]\;\leq\;e^{k{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}}\Pr[{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}].

Group DP can potentially handle correlations in the database by considering all the entries that may be correlated as a group of protected entries. However, group DP often leaves too little utility; for example, when all the entries in the database are possibly correlated (even loosely) — as in our previous example of a person with an infectious disease — group DP degrades the privacy linearly with the number of total entries in the database.
In our evaluation, we use group DP as one of the simplest baselines.

2.2.
Posterior-based Privacy Definitions

While differential privacy treats datasets as deterministic quantities and has constraints for different possible datasets, posterior-based privacy definitions (e.g.,
(
30
;
21
;
50
;
9
)
) model attackers as Bayesian reasoners who view the dataset as a random variable. Some of our results concern posterior-based privacy definitions in general, while others concern a specific definition called Pufferfish privacy
(
30
)
. In this section, we first define the minimal requirements for what a posterior-based privacy definition is.

Definition 0 (Posterior-based Privacy).

A posterior-based privacy definition has a privacy parameter
\epsilon\geq 0
, a set
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}
of prior distributions an attacker may use, and a collection of potential secrets
\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{1},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{2},\dots\}
.
The definition must use these items to define a set of restrictions that a mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
must satisfy.
Furthermore, if
\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{i}~|~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}})=\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{i}~|~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}),{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}})
for all
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{i}
and all
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}}\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}
, then
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
satisfies the definition with privacy parameter
\epsilon=0
. That is, if the posterior distribution of secrets is the same as the prior distribution, then the assessed privacy leakage is
0
.
Note that the probability is taken with respect to randomness in both
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}}
, and larger values of
\epsilon
represent weaker privacy protections than smaller values of
\epsilon
.

A special case is called Pufferfish privacy:

Definition 0 (Pufferfish Privacy
(
30
)
).

Given a set
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}
of priors, a set
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
of pairs of potential secrets, and a privacy parameter
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}\geq 0
,
a randomized mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
satisfies
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
-Pufferfish privacy if, for all pairs of potential secrets
({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{i},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{j})\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
and all priors
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}}\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}
such that
\Pr[{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{i}|{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}}]>0
and
\Pr[{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{j}|{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}}]>0
, and for all possible outputs
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}
,

\Pr[{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\textit{Data}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}\mid{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{i},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}}]\;\leq\;e^{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}}\Pr[{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\textit{Data}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}\mid{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{j},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}}].

This definition guarantees the following. Let
\frac{\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{i}~|~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}})}{\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{j}~|~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}})}
be the prior odds, specifying how likely the attacker thinks
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{i}
is true compared to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{j}
being true before seeing the data. After seeing the data, the attacker forms the posterior odds,
\frac{\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{i}~|~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}})}{\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{j}~|~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}})}
. Pufferfish privacy guarantees that the posterior odds are at most
e^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
(resp., at least
e^{-{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}}
) times the prior odds. In other words, the attacker’s belief changed by a factor of at most
e^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
.
If the ratio between the prior and the posterior odds is the same, it means there is no privacy leakage (
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}=0
).
Like differential privacy, Pufferfish satisfies post-processing invariance
(
30
)
. However, Pufferfish privacy does not have composition guarantees
(
30
)
, unlike differential privacy. In Section
4
, we show that this problem is very severe and can lead to privacy collapse.

3.
Related Work

Inevitability of DP for Composition.

Blanc et al.
(
5
)
recently studied composition in a general axiomatic setting. If a privacy definition satisfies their axioms (some of which are related to composition), then any mechanism for that definition can be replaced by an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP mechanism whose accuracy, on statistical tasks involving
independent
records, is nearly the same.
However, their work cannot be applied to
correlated data
, and their axioms are restrictive: pure
\epsilon
-differential privacy violates their composition axioms, and posterior-based privacy definitions almost always violate their symmetry axioms.
Thus, the work targets different privacy definitions and data assumptions from ours.

Generally, composition results outside of differential privacy are limited in scope. Bhaskar et al.
(
4
)
studied composition for mechanisms where the only randomness comes from the prior. Their composition result required refreshing large parts of the data (i.e., resampling from the prior) in between queries.
An approach by Farokhi
(
20
)
studies a prior-free noise-free privacy definition and proves composition without restrictions, but the definition cannot always prevent a mechanism from leaking secret information.

Although arbitrary Pufferfish mechanisms will not compose
(
30
)
, Song et al.
(
44
;
45
)
showed that some specific mechanisms compose with themselves. They considered the set
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}
of priors to be all Markov networks with the same structure and showed that a Laplace mechanism satisfying differential privacy with a privacy parameter
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
can satisfy Pufferfish privacy with some other parameter
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
, and the particular Pufferfish privacy parameter will compose.
Our result is a generalization of this prior result: we propose a framework which allows translating
any
set of (per-entry)
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
-differential privacy mechanisms into composable
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
-Pufferfish privacy mechanisms for
any
data prior, just by adjusting their privacy parameters using a concept called an
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve.

Inferential/Posterior-based Definitions.

This work focuses on Pufferfish privacy
(
30
)
, a popular posterior-based privacy definition that is a generalization of many other posterior-based definitions
(
56
;
33
;
50
;
21
)
.
Other notable approaches related to posterior-based privacy include: adapting DP to attackers with deterministic knowledge about the data
(
23
)
, changing how the dissimilarities between posteriors are quantified
(
37
;
42
)
, and adapting the counterfactual framework of DP to compare attacker inference about a hypothetical database to a scrubbed database
(
3
;
9
)
.

Pufferfish Privacy and Composition.

Lack of composition in Pufferfish has been sidestepped in several ways. Pierquin et al.
(
42
)
, Zhang et al.
(
55
)
, and Liang et al.
(

5.
Necessary Conditions for Composition and Post-processing Invariance

The previous section demonstrated that posterior-based privacy definitions, including Pufferfish, can
suffer privacy collapse.
In this
section, we study how to avoid this situation. Specifically, suppose we have a collection of mechanisms

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1},\dots,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{k}
where each
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{i}
satisfies Pufferfish with privacy parameter
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}_{i}
.
What conditions do these mechanisms need to satisfy in order to linearly compose with each other, so that
the combined release of all of their outputs satisfies Pufferfish with privacy parameter
\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}_{i}
?

The necessary conditions that we prove (Theorem
1
) identify a class of constraints that we call the NfC (
n
ecessary-
f
or-
c
omposition) constraints. For each dataset
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}
, one or more NfC constraints need to be added (in addition to the Pufferfish constraints); otherwise, linear composition will fail. As we will show, the differential privacy constraints,
\log\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}})\leq\epsilon+\log\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}})
, are special cases of NfC constraints, which have the general form of:
\log\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}})\leq\epsilon+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\beta_{i}\log\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}_{i})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}})
.

Then, we study another important property of privacy definitions called
post-processing invariance
. Post-processing invariance means that if
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{i}
satisfies
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}_{i}
-Pufferfish and
f_{i}
is a possibly randomized algorithm, then the algorithm
f_{i}\circ{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{i}
, which releases
f_{i}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{i}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}))
, still satisfies Pufferfish with privacy parameter
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}_{i}
. Now that extra NfC constraints must be added, one needs to ensure that those constraints are also post-processing invariant. That is, if
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
satisfies the extra constraints, then
f_{i}\circ{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{i}
should also satisfy the constraints. Or, put another way, if
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1},\dots,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{k}
compose linearly, then so should
f_{1}\circ{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1},\dots,f_{k}\circ{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{k}
.

Our second result (Theorem
2
) states that a non-redundant subset of NfC constraints is post-processing invariant if and only if every constraint in that subset is a differential-privacy-style constraint.
Together, these results imply that if one wants to design Pufferfish mechanisms that compose with each other, those mechanisms should also satisfy a form of differential privacy. In Section
6.2
, we show how to design such algorithms for tabular datasets (i.e., sufficient conditions for composition).

5.1.

We present necessary conditions for a set of Pufferfish mechanisms to compose, when defending a set of pairs of potential secrets
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
against priors in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}
. The conditions use the concept of a
convex combination vector

\beta

\ell
in the vector is represented as
\beta_{\ell}

One condition in the following theorem is that the set of possible databases is finite (but arbitrarily large). In practice, this is not a restrictive condition because attribute domains are typically bounded (e.g., a 500-character limit for URLs, 64 bits for numeric values) and only finitely many records can be collected in a finite time (e.g., no database will have more than
2^{64}
records). This simplifies the proof as it avoids dealing with corner cases that only arise with infinite-dimensional vectors.
Similarly, the necessary conditions consider a set of mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1},\dots,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{m}

The overall idea is that we convert the statement “linear composition holds” into a collection of linear programs. We take the duals of the linear programs, and Equation
1

Since the number of possible outputs is finite, we can write them as
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{1},\dots,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{k}

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{i}

P({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{i}~|~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}_{\ell})

P({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{j}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}_{\ell})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{i})
, where
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{j}
is the mechanism whose range contains
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{i}
.

The data curator selects
r

\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1},\dots,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{m}\}

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}_{i}

\alpha_{i}

r\epsilon

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}}\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}

5.2.
Enforcing Post-processing Invariance

Theorem
1
tells us that if we want composition properties inside Pufferfish, we need to add additional NfC constraints (inequalities with the form of Equation
1
). However, such constraints are not necessarily post-processing invariant. That is, after post-processing, a mechanism may fail to satisfy the constraints, and the postprocessed mechanisms may not compose. We next show that if a set of NfC constraints is “non-redundant” and post-processing invariant, then each one of those constraints must have the form of:
\log\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}_{i})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}})\leq\epsilon+\log\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}_{j})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}})
, i.e., each one of those constraints must be a differential privacy-style constraint.

To understand redundancy, consider the following two constraints with two different convex combination vectors
\beta^{(0)}
,
\beta^{(1)}
:

\displaystyle\log\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{*})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}})

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}_{\ell}:{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{2}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}_{\ell})\text{ is true}}\beta^{(0)}_{\ell}\log\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}_{\ell})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}),

\displaystyle\log\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{*})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}})

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}_{\ell}:{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{2}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}_{\ell})\text{ is true}}\beta^{(1)}_{\ell}\log\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}_{\ell})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}).

Then, for any
c\in(0,1)
, one can define
\beta^{(c)}=c\beta^{(1)}+(1-c)\beta^{(1)}
to get the following redundant constraint:

\displaystyle\log\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{*})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}})

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}_{\ell}:{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}_{2}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}_{\ell})\text{ is true}}\beta^{(c)}_{\ell}\log\Pr({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}_{\ell})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}).

In general, a constraint is redundant if it is implied by the other constraints that we have. By iteratively removing redundant constraints until no redundant constraints are left, we obtain a non-redundant set of constraints.

Theorem 2 (NfC and Post-processing Invariance).

Suppose there are finitely many datasets and
m
non-redundant NfC constraints, where constraint

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}_{i}

6.
Constructing General Pufferfish Mechanisms with Composition

Theorem
1
shows that a set of linearly composable Pufferfish mechanisms must satisfy a set of constraints (NfC constraints) that are generalizations of the differential privacy constraints.
To support post-processing invariance, Theorem
2
whittles down the set of constraints
to the type that are used by differential privacy.

These insights provide formal support for prior work
(
44
;
45
;
6
)
that designed linearly composable Pufferfish mechanisms by taking
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
-differential privacy mechanisms and showing that they also satisfy
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
-Pufferfish for some
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}\geq{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
. Specifically, Song et al.
(
45
;
44
)
proposed an extension of the Laplace Mechanism (called the MQM) to defend against attacker priors that all share the same Markov network structure. Meanwhile, Cao et al.
(
6
)
extend arbitrary DP mechanisms to Markov chains and defend against finitely many Markov chain priors while allowing the Pufferish privacy parameter to compose.

In this section, we generalize these ideas to show that in any Pufferfish setting for tabular data (including finite Markov chains), the same recipe holds. Any collection of per-entry
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
-differential privacy mechanisms can be translated into
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
-Pufferfish privacy, and the Pufferfish parameters linearly compose. Furthermore, the translation between
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
is guided by a new concept we call an
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve. The
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve only depends on the set of secrets
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
and the priors in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}
. Although the
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve can be computationally expensive to compute exactly, one only needs an upper bound to get privacy guarantees. The tighter the upper bound is, the better the resulting utility. This is analogous to sensitivity computation in differential privacy, which is easy to upper bound (providing privacy guarantees), but can be computationally difficult to estimate exactly.

Before going into the technical details, we first explain the intuition behind the
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve. Suppose there is an infectious disease dataset and a person
A
in this dataset. Person
A
has a set
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}}_{1}
containing
b_{1}
people who are highly likely to get infected if
A
gets sick (e.g.,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}}_{1}
is the set of close family members), a set
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}}_{2}
(with
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}}_{1}\subset{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}}_{2}
) containing
b_{2}
people who are moderately likely to get infected if
A
gets sick (e.g.,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}}_{2}
contains
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}}_{1}
and close friends), and a third set
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}}_{3}
containing
b_{3}
people who are only slightly likely to get infected from
A
(e.g.,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}}_{3}
contains
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}}_{2}
and acquaintances).

Now, suppose
A
can increase the chance of infections of people not in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}}_{1}
by at most a factor of
a_{1}
, can increase the chance of infection of people not in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}}_{2}
(resp.,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}}_{3}
) by at most a factor of
a_{2}<a_{1}
(resp.
a_{3}<a_{2}
), and so on. These tuples
(a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2}),(a_{3},b_{3})
form the
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve. That is, we can think of the
a
value as a function of
b
.
When considering
b
number of people most closely associated with
A
(or the number of cells in a tabular dataset most closely associated with a secret),

a(b)
upper bounds the effect of
A
on anyone outside of this close group of size
b
.

This
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve provides translations between
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
as follows. An algorithm satisfying
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
-differential privacy also satisfies Pufferfish with privacy parameter
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}=b{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}+a(b)
for any choice of
b
. This means that the best
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
parameter is
\min_{b}b{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}+a(b)
. Thus, if one has the
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve, or knows at least a few points on the curve, they can compute the upper bound on the Pufferfish privacy parameter.

In order to show how the
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve is a generalization of the work of Song et al.
(
45
)
, we briefly describe the Markov Quilt Mechanism and

concept in Section

. Then, we formally define

and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}

. We also give worked out examples of the
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve in Section
6.3
.

Theorem
3
can be written as
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}=b{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}+a
, which can also be interpreted as saying that the Pufferfish parameter uses differential privacy’s group privacy with a group size of
b
(accounting for the
b{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
part of the formula) plus a penalty
a
for cross-record correlation that extends beyond the group.
Our next theorem, about composition, shows that this extra penalty only needs to be paid once, so that the composition of the Pufferfish parameter is still linear but is better than just adding up the Pufferfish parameters:

Theorem 4 (Sufficient Condition for Composition).

Fix a family of priors
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}
and a set of secret pairs
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}}
.
Consider
k
mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{1},...,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{k}
applied sequentially to the same dataset, each satisfying
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}_{1},...,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}_{k}
-differential privacy, where
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}_{\ell}\leq\frac{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}_{\ell}-a_{\ell}}{b_{\ell}}
.
Here,
(a_{\ell},b_{\ell})
are arbitrary points on the
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve of
({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}})
with
a_{\ell}<{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}_{\ell}
.
That is, each
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{\ell}
achieves
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}_{\ell}
-Pufferfish according to Theorem
3
.
Then, the joint release of the outputs
satisfies
(\max_{\ell}a_{\ell}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}_{\ell}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}a_{\ell})
-Pufferfish privacy.
Note that the composition is sub-additive due to the (
\max_{\ell}a_{\ell}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}a_{\ell})
factor, which is always negative.

By assuming an
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}

When
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}

For example, a person with an infectious disease will have a quickly decaying influence outside a small group of close people (or “clique”)
(

Data generated by a Markovian structure (e.g., Markov chain, hidden Markov model (HMM), etc.) is another example.
Many real-world phenomena (e.g., patients’ reaction to a medicine

(

Several mechanisms from prior works with posterior-based privacy can be seen as a special case of our framework.

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\textit{Data}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}}

(

(

Other works
(

Example
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence Curves

We visualize

-influence curves for some synthetic priors where the curve can be analytically calculated to gain better insights.

Markov Chain.
First, we explain how we can analytically calculate the
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve for a simple Markov chain. The proofs can be found in the appendix.

Example 5.

We consider a binary and time-homogeneous chain with each node’s value being 0 or 1.
We consider the value of each node as a secret.
Its transition matrix is:

P=\begin{bmatrix}p&1-p\\
1-q&q\end{bmatrix},\qquad 0<p,q<1,

and the prior distribution of the Markov chain is its stationary distribution. We assume the chain has length
T
, and
T
is large enough so we only consider
b<<T
.

In this case, we can have closed-form solution for
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve.
Let us consider an arbitrary
i
-th node
X_{i}
’s value as a secret.
for a fixed
b
, we partition the nodes into
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}
, such that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}}}
contains
X_{i}
and
b-1
surrounding nodes.
Let
d_{L}
(resp.,
d_{R}
) denote the distance from
X_{i}
to the closest node of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}
on the left (resp., right), so that
d_{L}+d_{R}-1=b
.
By the Markov property, conditioning on all variables in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}
influences
X_{i}
only through these two closest low-influence nodes, and the leakage through
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}
reduces to the leakage through those two nodes.

Thus, finding an optimal
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve becomes a one-dimensional optimization problem of finding
(d_{L},d_{R})
that maximizes the leakage.
The maximum leakage occurs when these nodes both have the same value of 0 or 1.
When
q>p
, the chain tends to stay at 1 more strongly than at 0, so the leakage is maximum when these nodes have values 0.
When
q\leq p
, the worst case is instead achieved when both have values
1
.
Finally, from midpoint convexity, the influence is minimized when the two nearest low-influence nodes are placed as evenly as possible on both sides of
X_{i}
, giving

d_{L}^{\star}=\lfloor(b+1)/2\rfloor
and
d_{R}^{\star}=\lceil(b+1)/2\rceil
.
In summary, the optimal
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve can be calculated as:

Theorem 6.

Given the binary Markov chain in Example
5
,
let
\lambda=q+p-1
, the
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve is calculated by

a^{\star}(b)=\Bigg|\log\frac{\pi_{0}+\lambda^{d_{L}}(1-\pi_{0})}{\pi_{0}-\lambda^{d_{L}}\pi_{0}}\Bigg|+\Bigg|\log\frac{\pi_{0}+\lambda^{d_{R}}(1-\pi_{0})}{\pi_{0}-\lambda^{d_{R}}\pi_{0}}\Bigg|\text{ if $q>p$},

a^{\star}(b)=\Bigg|\log\frac{\pi_{1}+\lambda^{d_{L}}(1-\pi_{1})}{\pi_{1}-\lambda^{d_{L}}\pi_{1}}\Bigg|+\Bigg|\log\frac{\pi_{1}+\lambda^{d_{R}}(1-\pi_{1})}{\pi_{1}-\lambda^{d_{R}}\pi_{1}}\Bigg|\text{ if $q\leq p$},

where
d_{L}=\Big\lfloor\frac{b+1}{2}\Big\rfloor
and
d_{R}=\Big\lceil\frac{b+1}{2}\Big\rceil
.
The star (
⋆
) indicates that this curve is optimum (tight).

Multivariate Gaussian.

Next, we illustrate how we can derive the
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve when the prior distribution
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}}
is a multivariate Gaussian.
While a closed-form solution is not possible, we can still efficiently calculate the optimal
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence.

Example 7.

Consider a single-record continuous dataset represented by a vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Throughout this example, the prior distribution is a truncated multivariate Gaussian,

\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)\ \ \mathbf{x}\in[-\gamma,\gamma]^{n},

where
\gamma>0
bounds the support.
The covariance matrix
\Sigma
is:

\Sigma_{jk}\;=\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{(j-k)^{2}}{\ell}\right),\qquad j,k\in[n].

The secret is defined as
region events
on each coordinate.
Specifically, the secret is whether each coordinate
x_{i}
’s value is in a certain range:

s_{i}(r)\ =\ \{x_{i}\in[r,r+\delta],\qquad r\in[-\gamma,\gamma-\delta]\}.

A secret pair on
i
is given by two disjoint region events
\bigl(s_{i}(r),\,s_{i}(r^{\prime})\bigr)
, where

[r,r+\delta]\cap[r^{\prime},r^{\prime}+\delta]=\emptyset
.

In this example, among all the entries, the middle coordinate
x_{i^{\star}}
has the greatest information leakage, where
i^{\star}=\lceil n/2\rceil
. Therefore, we only need to consider secrets defined on
x_{i^{\star}}
,
s(r)\equiv\{x_{i^{\star}}\in[r,r+\delta]\}
. For a fixed
b
, the optimal low-influence index set
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}^{\star}
with size
n-b
is the set of indices with the farthest distance to
i^{\star}
. Under
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}^{\star}
, we calculate
a^{\star}(b)=\max_{s(r),s(r^{\prime}),x_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}}\log\frac{\Pr[x_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}|x_{i^{\star}}\in[r,r+\delta]]}{\Pr[x_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}|x_{i^{\star}}\in[r^{\prime},r^{\prime}+\delta]]}
, which is the optimal (tight)
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
. Although
\mathbf{x}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}}
is high-dimensional, we have:

\Pr(\mathbf{x}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}}\mid x_{i^{\star}})\propto\Pr(x_{i^{\star}}\mid\mathbf{x}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}})/\Pr(x_{i^{\star}}).

The conditional distribution of
\Pr(x_{i^{\star}}\mid\mathbf{x}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}})
is
a one-dimensional Gaussian distribution, denoted as
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i^{\star},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}},\,\sigma^{2}_{i^{\star},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}})
.
As
\sigma^{2}_{i^{\star},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}}
does not depend on
\mathbf{x}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}}
, for fixed
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}
, the maximization over
\mathbf{x}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}}
reduces to sweeping over
\mu_{i^{\star},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}}

6.4.
Example Pufferfish Mechanisms

Our framework (Theorems
3
and
4
) allows translating existing differential privacy mechanisms into a composable Pufferfish mechanism.
In this section, we introduce concrete mechanisms that we use in our evaluation.
As these are just a re-instantiation of known differential privacy mechanisms, we only briefly explain them.

6.4.1.
Pufferfish Laplace Mechanism

The Laplace (differential privacy) mechanism answers real-valued queries by adding Laplacian noise to the query output, and is used when querying numeric statistics such as counts, sums, means, histograms, etc.
For an
L
-Lipschitz query function
F
(in
\ell_{1}
sensitivity), the following mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
ensures
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
-differential privacy:

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})=F({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})+\mathrm{Lap}(\frac{L}{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}}).

Following Theorem
3
, this also ensures
(b{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}+a(b))
-Pufferfish privacy with composition, when the prior follows a specific
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve (maximum over all possible
b
values yields the best utility).
The full algorithm is in Algorithm
1
.
For special cases when the data was generated by the Markov chain, this becomes the MQM mechanism from Song et al.
(
44
)
.
In the evaluation, we will be presenting the MQM mechanism as the baseline, rather than our proposed method, although it is also a special case of our framework.

6.4.2.
Pufferfish Exponential Mechanism

The Laplace mechanism of differential privacy is not well-suited for queries where the output space is discrete, and the utility must be captured through a scoring function, such as report noisy max or noisy top-
k
selection.
In these cases, the exponential mechanism of differential privacy
(
18
)

is known to work better.
As our framework allows translating an arbitrary differential privacy mechanism, we can create an exponential Pufferfish mechanism, which is expected to work better for these types of queries.

Below, we briefly review the exponential mechanism of differential privacy.
Let
\mathcal{R}
be a discrete output space and
u
be a utility (score) function. The sensitivity is:

\Delta_{u}=\max_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime}}\;\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\big|u({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}},r)-u({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime},r)\big|,

where
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime}
are standard DP neighboring datasets.
The exponential mechanism achieves
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
-differential privacy by samples response
r\in\mathcal{R}
with probability

\Pr\big[{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})=r\big]\propto\exp\left(\frac{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}\,u({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}},r)}{2\,\Delta_{u}}\right).

Again, this achieves
(b{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}+a(b))
-Pufferfish privacy with composition, where the maximum should be selected over the entire
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve for the tightest translation.
The full algorithm is in Algorithm
2
.

7.
Experiments

7.1.
Experimental Setup

We evaluate the utility of the Pufferfish mechanism additionally designed by our recipe in Section
6.4
to show that the newly discovered mechanisms have advantages over manually-designed Pufferfish mechanisms from prior work
(
45
)
.

7.1.1.
Dataset

We evaluate the mechanisms with two representative datasets where strong correlations exist between datapoints.

Foursquare check-in dataset

(
52
;
51
)
contains users’ location data with a corresponding timestamp across 77 countries.
Each location is associated with a semantic label, such as “Bar”, “Hotel”, or “Restaurant”. We merged similar labels (e.g., “Cocktail Bar” with “Bar”) and only chose the 77 most common labels worldwide, mapping all other locations as “Other”.
Each datapoint of the resulting dataset is location information (one of the 77 labels or “Other”) of a particular user at a particular time.
The rules for merging labels are in Appendix
D.4
.
Clearly, locations of a user within a short time period are expected to be highly correlated.

Capture24 dataset

(
7
;
8
)
contains 151 participants’ activity data monitored across 24 hours.
Participants are grouped by age (18–29, 30–37, 38–52, 53+). The dataset annotated activities in multiple ways, and we use the
WillettsMET2018
annotation, where participants’ activities are labelled into 11 activity categories.
Again, activities of a participant within a nearby time period are expected to be highly correlated.

7.1.2.
Query of Interest

In both settings, we assume the data analyst is interested in
noisy Top-
K
queries.
In particular, for the Foursquare check-in dataset, the query we evaluate is:
“Within each country, what are the Top-3 most frequently visited locations?”
.
For the Capture24 dataset. the query we evaluate is:
“Within each age group, what are the Top-3 most frequent activities?”
.
These are deliberately chosen to evaluate the types of queries that prior work
(
45
)
did not consider. For the queries that prior works studied (e.g., querying the average value
(
45
)
), mechanisms from prior works can be used.

7.1.3.
Evaluated Mechanisms

We evaluated the following mechanisms to enhance privacy:

MQM
is a Pufferfish mechanism proposed by prior work
(
45
)
and described in Section
6.4
. It adopts the Laplace mechanism of differential privacy to achieve Pufferfish privacy with composition.
The original MQM is for answering a scalar query, so we use it to calculate the Top-3 popular categories (locations/activities) by first querying the count of the
m
categories and sorting the noisy answer.
The privacy budget is evenly divided across the
m
queries.

Ours-Exp
is what can be achieved when we interpret the popular exponential mechanism of differential privacy
(
16
)
as a composable Pufferfish mechanism using Theorem
3
.
We choose the exponential mechanism, which is known to work well with Top-
K
queries
(
17
;
13
)
, to highlight the power of our Theorem
3
— instead of having to manually design Pufferfish mechanisms (e.g., MQM
(
45
)
), we can adopt mechanisms from the well-studied DP world depending on the application of interest.
In particular, we selected Top-
K
items iteratively using the exponential mechanism with quality score
u({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}},r)
. We perform
K
draws without replacement; at round
k
, we allocate budget
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}/K
, treating the sampled item as the
k
-th largest. The final ordering is by the selection sequence.

Group DP
simply assumes all the datapoints are maximally correlated and uses group DP
(
17
)
instead of using Pufferfish privacy.
It can use any DP mechanisms (Laplace or exponential), but the utility will be too bad to be practical. This is a (bad) baseline we include for completeness.

7.1.4.
Prior Construction

Using MQM or our method (Ours-Exp) requires knowledge of the prior
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}}
.
Following prior works
(
45
)
, we assume the temporal data are generated by a first-order Markov chain, and use the data to fit a Markov model with a stationary transition matrix.
For the Foursquare check-in dataset, each user’s location is a state, and a location change is considered a state transition.
We truncate the dataset to only leave the check-in data for the first 500 days.
We built a
78\times 78
transition matrix using the first 400 out of 500 days of data, and used the remaining 100 days for testing.
For the Capture24 dataset, each participant’s activity is a state, and activity change is considered a state transition.
We hold out 8 participants from each age group for testing and use the rest to fit the
11\times 11
transition matrix.
Note that MQM requires the prior
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta}}
to be a Markov chain, while our general approach from Section
6.2
works with an arbitrary prior.

7.1.5.
Utility Metrics

We evaluate our Top-
K
query result through the following metrics:

Acc@k
measures the accuracy of predicting the
k
-th popular category correctly. The exact ranking (i.e.,
k
) must be predicted correctly (e.g., if the most popular location is predicted as second-most popular, it is regarded as incorrect).

Hit Rate@K
measures the precision of the correct Top-
K
categories appearing in the predicted Top-
K
. The order does not matter, as long as the correct category appears in the predicted Top-
K
.

NDCG@K
, or Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain, is a frequently used metric in ranking systems
(
25
)
.
NDCG accounts for the order of the answer and rewards putting higher-ranked categories near the top, even if the exact rank was incorrect.
Precisely, NDCG is calculated by:

\text{DCG@}K=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\text{rel}(\hat{\pi}_{k})}{\log_{2}(k+1)},\quad\text{NDCG@}K=\frac{\text{DCG@}K}{\text{IDCG@}K},

where
\text{rel}(i)
is the ground-truth score for item
i
(we use true counts), and IDCG is the DCG of the ideal ranking.
By normalizing to the best possible ranking for the same query (IDCG), scores lie in
[0,1]
, and a higher score means better prediction.

\ell_{1}
Count Error
is the sum of the absolute difference between the predicted Top-
K
’s counts and the true Top-
K
’s counts.
We additionally included this metric because it can give us a sense of how different the actual counts are when misprediction occurs. For example, if the most and second-most popular categories have similar counts, this metric does not penalize swapping their ranking in prediction; however, if their counts differ significantly, getting the order wrong would significantly increase the error.
Specifically, the error is calculated by
\text{L1}\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big|\tilde{c}_{k}-c_{k}\big|
,
where
\tilde{c}_{k}
is the count of the category that the noisy mechanism places at rank
k
, and
c_{k}
is the count of the category that truly belongs at rank
k
.

Majority
We additionally show another example of what the accuracy would be if we use the Top-1/2/3 labels from the train data as the prediction to evaluate the complexity of the question. The Majority only achieved 18.61% and 16.67% accuracy averaged across 3 positions.

A full overview of the dataset details, prior constructions, and implementation choices are provided in Appendix
D
.

8.
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper, we study how to add composition properties to the Pufferfish framework.
We analyzed privacy collapses under composition and provided necessary
and sufficient conditions for linear composition. We conclude that the constraints on mechanisms
required by the
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{puffer}}}}
-Pufferfish framework should be augmented with
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{DP}}}}
-differential privacy
constraints. We introduced the
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a(b)}}
-influence curve to translate between those two
privacy parameters.
Future work includes algorithm design under various classes of priors and extending the
necessary and sufficient conditions for privacy definitions like Renyi-Pufferfish.
