Accelerated Screening of Zeolites for Methanol-to-Propylene Conversion Using Machine Learning with Interpretable Selectivity Function
Authors: Yuwei Pan, Ling Zhang, Lei Sun, Dong Zhai, Wenliang Li, Zhen Li, Tie Yu, Weiqiao Deng, Li Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Methanol-to-propylene (MTP) is one of the important propylene production technologies that is commonly achieved by the use of zeolite catalysts. However, effectively regulating the selectivity toward propylene remains challenging due to the insufficient understanding of the principles behind selectivity control. In this study, we proposed an interpretable function to assess the propylene/ethylene (P/E) selectivity based on a thorough investigation of the MTP selectivity control mechanism and implemented a machine learning (ML) method for high-throughput screening of zeolite frameworks for MTP. Density functional theory and grand canonical Monte Carlo simulations have been used to build the ML model. The P/E selectivity predictions are supported by the reported and our own experiments. We chose Mobil Five (MFI) (rank 374) and Deca-dodecasil 3R (DDR) (rank 5869) for experimental validation. Experimental results showed that the Ga-MFI catalyst achieved a P/E ratio of 12.3 and a remarkably longer operational lifespan of 121.3 h in the MTP process, surpassing previously reported values. Additionally, the marked superiority of Ga-MFI over Ga-DDR, especially in terms of P/E ratios and lifespans, further underscores the effectiveness of our zeolite screening strategy.

Introduction

Propylene, one of the major raw materials for synthetic materials, is primarily used to produce polymers. − Meanwhile, propylene readily undergoes substitution reactions and is widely applied to synthesize other polymer monomers (such as acrolein and acrylonitrile) and fine chemicals. , Traditional propylene production routes consist of steam cracking of petroleum naphtha or catalytic cracking of gas oil, both of which originate from petroleum resources. − As the gap between supply and demand for propylene continuously increases, alternative resources and the development of corresponding routes become necessary to improve the propylene output and meet industrial needs. For the past decades, the methanol-to-propylene (MTP) reaction, employing acidic zeolites as catalysts, has transformed methanol into propylene efficiently and has achieved industrialization, which also presents a much higher P/E ratio than the petroleum naphtha steam cracking process (P/E < 1). − Considering that nonpetroleum sources like natural gas, coal, and biomass can be efficiently transformed into methanol, the MTP reaction presents a viable alternative for converting these resources into propylene, thereby decreasing dependency on petroleum.

Based on previous mechanistic studies in the literature, it was considered that the MTP reaction experiences three stages: initial stage, transitional stage, and steady state. , In the initial stage, propylene and the byproduct ethylene are primarily generated through a direct mechanism. During the transitional stage, the olefins contribute to the formation of initial hydrocarbon pool (HCP) species. In the subsequent steady state, propylene is predominantly produced through the HCP mechanism. This mechanism assumes that certain HCP species are trapped within the cages of zeolites and continuously react with methanol and ultimately produce propylene through elimination reactions. Therefore, for the generation of propylene from the MTP reaction, a crucial feature of zeolites is their adsorption properties for core HCP intermediates. The topology of zeolites becomes imperative to accommodate these HCP intermediates, which is vital for the continuous progression of the MTP reaction. Building on this idea, Ferri et al. − systematically explored how HCP intermediate stability correlates with cage architecture in governing product selectivity; they introduced an energetic descriptor, Eint (7/5), which highlights the roles of cage topology, framework composition, and flexibility in stabilizing fully methylated intermediates (7MB+) and thereby allowing reliable prediction and tuning of the P/E ratio. In parallel, the geometric “cage-defining ring” descriptor was proposed as an equally powerful predictor of olefin selectivity, underscoring once again the decisive influence of cage architecture on MTP performance. , If the cavity structure in zeolites is insufficient to accommodate the aforementioned HCP intermediates, then the corresponding reaction chain cannot proceed continuously. Therefore, we can enhance the productivity of the MTP reaction by choosing zeolites with the appropriate pore structures. However, given the diversity of zeolite structures, selecting the right pore structure for the MTP reaction is still a challenge.

Conventional material development heavily relies on the experimentally driven “trial-and-error” approach. This method is not only time-consuming but also costly in terms of manpower and resources, resulting in low efficiency. It struggles to meet the increasingly complex design demands of modern high-performance materials. Digital-intellectual materials design (DIMD), as an emerging paradigm, offers a powerful solution to this challenge. This paradigm emphasizes the seamless integration of Big Data (derived from high-throughput calculations or experimental databases), Artificial Intelligence (AI), particularly ML algorithms, and Human Expertise. Its core advantages lie in its ability to efficiently uncover structure–property relationships, accurately predict the properties of unknown materials, and significantly accelerate targeted material discovery, thereby substantially surpassing the limitations of traditional methods. In the field of zeolites, a vital class of porous materials, ML technology, as a key driver of DIMD, demonstrates considerable application potential. Currently, the integration of theoretical calculations with ML algorithms in material design offers a solution to this challenge. Li et al. leveraged machine-learning-assisted crystal engineering to predict the mechanical properties of zeolite frameworks, demonstrating the power of ML in identifying and designing materials with desired structural characteristics. Erlebach et al. employed neural network potentials for accurate large-scale simulations of siliceous zeolites. This methodology allowed for the prediction of structure and vibrational properties, alongside simulations of reactive phase transformations with a notable accuracy that closely aligns with experimental data. Additionally, the work by Barros et al. on optimizing zeolites as adsorbents for rare earth elements in continuous flow systems via ML techniques highlighted the practical applications of ML in improving industrial processes.

In this study, we introduce a selectivity function based on a Sigmoid–Boltzmann mapping that converts DFT-calculated binding energies into the thermal equilibrium coverages of HCP intermediates, thereby yielding the corresponding P/E ratio. We then integrate descriptors, such as adsorption heats from GCMC simulations, into a machine-learning pipeline to perform high-throughput screening of over 1.0 × 104 zeolite frameworks. Based on the interpretable function for the P/E ratio and machine learning predictions, the zeolites were classified into three categories. To validate the screening results, two representative zeolites, MFI (rank 386, in the top 5%) and DDR (rank 5869, in the top 50–5%), were chosen for experimental study. In the experiment, active acidic sites were created by doping pure silica MFI and DDR zeolites with Ga atoms, and the comparable microenvironment of Ga within these zeolites was precisely controlled. The MTP performance results indicated that the Ga-MFI catalyst not only achieved a higher P/E ratio of 12.3, surpassing the 2.5 P/E ratio of Ga-DDR, but also exhibited a significantly extended operational lifespan of 121.3 h, which was almost 48 times longer than that of Ga-DDR. The experimental results confirm that our strategy for zeolite topology screening is an efficient approach to select zeolites for the MTP reaction. Moreover, the P/E ratio and lifespan of Ga-DDR reported in this study are higher than previously reported values.

Results and Discussion

Interpretable Function for P/E Selectivity

HCP species were demonstrated to play an important role in olefin formation during the transition stage and steady stage of MTP over zeolites. , In the previous study, it was found that some HCP species act as active intermediates and produce olefins through side-chain and paring paths based on 1,2,3,5-tetramethylbenzene (4M1) and hexamethylbenzene (6M1), as shown in Figure A,B. In detail, the propylene is produced via paring (4M1 → 4L3, 6M1 → 6L3) and side-chain (4M1 → 4M11, 6M1 → 6M11) pathways, while ethylene predominantly forms through the side-chain routes (4M1 → 4M6, 6M1 → 6M6), and the reaction pathways dominate the selectivity of ethylene and propylene in products.

To obtain an interpretable function describing the selectivity between propylene and ethylene, a three-step methodology was adopted. First, equilibrium coverages of HCP intermediates were calculated by using the Boltzmann distribution. Second, the DFT-calculated adsorption energies were rescaled through Sigmoid mapping within the Boltzmann framework to account for adsorption-site saturation and maintain numerical stability while preserving thermodynamic consistency. Finally, intermediate-specific equilibrium coverages were aggregated to determine pathway contributions and ultimately yield the global P/E ratio.

Equilibrium Coverages via Boltzmann Distribution

Using the Boltzmann distribution for evaluating the equilibrium coverage of adsorbed intermediates is grounded in the framework established by Beenakker et al. In their work, the density of species in microporous channels was shown to be exponentially dependent on adsorption energies, yielding a characteristic density ratio: n1n2=exp(−(E1−E2)kT) 1 where n 1 and n 2 represent the equilibrium densities of species 1 and 2, and E 1, E 2 are their respective adsorption energies. Inspired by this model, we generalized the formulation to compute the equilibrium coverage of each intermediate: θi=exp(−Ei/kT)∑jexp(−Ei/kT) 2 where k is the Boltzmann constant and T = 673 K corresponds to the typical reaction temperature used in experimental MTP tests. This ensures consistency between the statistical thermodynamic model and the reaction operating conditions. E i and E j denote the adsorption energies of intermediates i and j, respectively, with the denominator summing over all intermediates j, each associated with its corresponding adsorption energy E j .

Sigmoid Mapping for Energy Regularization

To retain thermodynamic interpretability while avoiding the unphysical dominance of extremely strong adsorption states, the adsorption energies calculated by DFT were regularized through Sigmoid mapping. The Sigmoid mapping is physically motivated by the Langmuir adsorption isotherm, which describes the fractional site occupancy θ i as a function of adsorption energy: θi=KiP1+KiP,Ki=K0exp(−Ei/kT) 3 where K i is the equilibrium constant for species i, P is the adsorbate partial pressure, and K 0 is the pre-exponential factor. At constant P, this relation can be rearranged into a logistic (sigmoid) form: θ~i11+exp[(Ei−E0)/kT] 4 where E 0 corresponds to the characteristic adsorption energy at half the coverage. This equation illustrates that the Langmuir isotherm inherently exhibits a sigmoid dependence on adsorption energy, reflecting the gradual saturation of adsorption sites as the energy increases. Inspired by this physical analogy, each DFT-calculated adsorption energy E i was transformed into a dimensionless corrected energy using the same logistic mapping: s(Ei)11+exp[(Ei−E0)/kT] 5

Here, E 0 is the arithmetic mean adsorption energy representing the reference binding level. This mapping confines s(E) within (0, 1), suppresses unrealistically strong binders, and maintains physical consistency with the Boltzmann/Langmuir form.

The equilibrium coverages of the two initiating intermediates, 4M1 and 6M1, are calculated as follows: θi=s(Ei)∑js(Ej) 6

In each corresponding subnetwork, the P/E ratio is defined as follows: P/E4M=θ4L3+θ4M11θ4M6,P/E6M=θ6L3+θ4M11θ6M6 7 where 4L3 and 4M11 (or 6L3 and 6M11) are the key intermediates contributing to propylene formation, while 4M6 (or 6M6) primarily leads to ethylene formation through the side-chain pathway.

The overall P/E ratio is then calculated as a weighted average of the two contributions: P/E=w4M×P/E4M+w6M×P/E6M 8 where the weighting factors correspond to the relative equilibrium coverages of the two initiating intermediates: w4M=θ4M1θ4M1+θ6M1,w6M=θ6M1θ4M1+θ6M1 9

ML Model for Zeolite Screen

In the P/E ratio function, the adsorption energies of the HCP intermediates form the fundamental basis for calculating the P/E ratio. However, due to the large size of zeolite frameworks, DFT calculations of intermediate-zeolite binding energies are computationally prohibitive, while classical force-field methods lack sufficient accuracy. To address this, we propose combining DFT with GCMC simulations to train a machine learning model for predicting binding energies between eight key intermediates and zeolite frameworks.

Figure C summarizes the proposed zeolite framework screening approach. The workflow is segmented into four parts: (i) automated extraction of comprehensive zeolite structural data sets from established databases, coupled with systematic computation of topological and chemical descriptors for framework characterization; (ii) determination of adsorption energies for eight key catalytic intermediates via DFT, followed by training machine learning models to establish robust structure–energy relationships; (iii) prediction of P/E selectivity ratios from derived adsorption energy profiles; and (iv) validation of model accuracy using experimental data. This screening strategy fundamentally seeks frameworks that stabilize HCP intermediates.

A total of 20,262 zeolite structures were collected for the construction and validation of the machine learning model. Of these, 20,000 zeolite structures were sourced from the Atlas of Prospective Zeolite Structures, − a virtual database of hypothetical frameworks, while the remaining 262 came from the Database of Zeolite Structures.

From these initial collections, a total of 462 zeolite structures (262 experimental frameworks from the Database of Zeolite Structures plus 200 virtual structures randomly selected from the Atlas of Prospective Zeolite Structures) were chosen for DFT calculations of binding energies. Of the 462 structures used for DFT calculations, 164 converged successfully, with failures mainly attributed to steric repulsion or unstable initial placements. These converged structures provided accurate binding-energy data for subsequent supervised machine-learning training and evaluation. In parallel, GCMC simulations were performed on the entire initial set of 20,262 zeolite structures to compute adsorption heats as complementary thermodynamic descriptors. Among the 20,262 structures, adsorption heats were successfully obtained for 11,818 structures, serving as features for large-scale inference. The final supervised machine-learning data set, comprising the 164 successfully converged DFT-calculated structures, was subsequently split into a training set (80%) and a test set (20%). The 164 zeolite–intermediate configurations, including eight key intermediates, were adsorbed on representative zeolite frameworks, such as MFI, CHA, DDR, AEI, FER, LTA, RTH, and ITE. These systems represent a variety of pore sizes and framework structures, ensuring a broad coverage of relevant zeolite properties in the context of methanol-to-olefin catalysis. Although this data set captures a broad range of pore geometries and adsorption motifs, the limited number of samples inevitably constrains the statistical representation of rare topologies and extreme adsorption energies. Such sparsity may introduce generalization bias, particularly when extrapolating to hypothetical or less-studied frameworks. To mitigate this issue, we incorporated complementary GCMC-derived thermodynamic descriptors into the feature space, which provide ensemble-averaged adsorption information and reduce the reliance on the finite DFT subset. The remaining structures, which did not yield converged DFT results, together with structures not included in the initial 462-structure subset, constituted the unseen prediction set.

A comprehensive set of 32 structural descriptors (Table S1) was initially considered. These descriptors encompass three main categories, including unit-cell structural parameters, bond-length and bond-angle statistics, and pore-channel structural features. Among these descriptors, the pore-channel structural features were computed through the Zeo++ package, while the remaining structural parameters and statistical metrics were obtained using the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE).

To eliminate redundancy and isolate the most informative variables, a Pearson correlation screening was carried out on the initial 32 descriptors (Figure S1). Any pair of descriptors with |r| > 0.80 was considered highly correlated, and one member of each such pair was removed. This procedure reduced the descriptor set to 14 key descriptors, the full list of which is summarized in Table S1. Additionally, adsorption heats (M_h, M = 4M1, 4M6, 4M11, 4L4, 6M1, 6M6, 6M11, and 6L3) computed from GCMC simulations using VDW FF were included as descriptors, − providing complementary thermodynamic information to further enhance the predictive capability and physical interpretability of the developed machine-learning model.

To identify the optimal machine-learning strategy for predicting binding energies of HCP intermediates in zeolites, two widely used and robust ensemble learning algorithmsGradient Boosting Regressor (GBR) and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)were systematically evaluated. Given the distinct physicochemical properties of each HCP intermediate (4M1, 4M6, 4M11, 4L4, 6M1, 6M6, 6M11, and 6L3), separate models were trained and optimized individually. Binding energies computed using DFT served as targets, while a refined set of 14 structural descriptors, together with adsorption heats from GCMC simulations, served as the input features.

Performance Evaluation of ML Models

To assess model performance systematically, both GBR and XGBoost were benchmarked on all eight HCP intermediates. For each intermediate, an independent model was trained and evaluated by 5-fold shuffled cross-validation. The resulting root-mean-square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and coefficient of determination (R 2) distributions are summarized as violin plots in Figure S2. Overall, XGBoost exhibits marginally lower median RMSE/MAE and slightly higher median R 2. XGBoost consistently delivers narrower error bands, steadier convergence, and slightly better median RMSE/MAE/R 2 than GBR for most intermediates. Benefiting from built-in column subsampling, second-order gradient updates, and explicit L1/L2 regularization, XGBoost is also less prone to overfitting. Therefore, XGBoost was selected as the unified predictive model for subsequent binding-energy screening and SHapley additive expansion (SHAP)-based interpretability analysis. Figure systematically summarizes the XGBoost model’s test-set performance and SHAP-derived feature importance across all eight HCP intermediates, with detailed statistics listed in Table S2. On the test set, the coefficient of R 2 spans 0.809 (4m11) to 0.918 (4l3), with a median of 0.882, while the MAE ranges from 0.837 kcal/mol (4l3) to 1.433 kcal/mol (6m6). All MAE values lie within the chemical-accuracy threshold for adsorption energies, underscoring both the model’s high precision and its robust generalizability. Complementing these metrics, the SHAP bar plots in Figure B,D,F,H,J,L,N,P reveal a remarkably consistent pattern of feature contributions. In all cases, the GCMC-derived heat of adsorption is the dominant feature (mean |SHAP| ≈ 1.8–2.5, accounting for ∼40–45% of the total influence), followed by the PLD (mean |SHAP| ≈ 0.6–1.2, ∼20–25%). A third tier of featuresincluding framework density, accessible volume (AV), statistical moments (mean, skewness, standard deviation) of Si–O and Si–O–Si bond angles, and space-group numbercollectively explains the remaining 30–35% of the output variance. The relative importance within this third tier shows only minor shifts between intermediates.

The SHAP analysis demonstrates that the adsorption heat and PLD are the most important features. Adsorption heat contributes to the stability of intermediates through stronger binding interactions, while PLD limits the size and stability of intermediates through spatial confinement within the zeolite framework. A high adsorption heat indicates strong binding, which stabilizes intermediates and enhances their contribution to the formation of the desired products, directly influencing selectivity. The pore size physically restricts the stability of larger intermediates, preventing certain intermediates from forming and blocking specific reaction pathways and thereby modulating overall selectivity.

HCP Intermediates and P/E Selectivity

The trained machine-learning model was then used to predict the binding energies of all key HCP intermediates for the unseen zeolite frameworks, and the corresponding P/E ratios were calculated with the selectivity function derived above. To systematically evaluate the impact of each HCP intermediate on MTP selectivity, we analyzed the predicted binding energies of all key intermediates across 11,818 zeolites and calculated the corresponding P/E ratios using the selectivity function derived previously. Considering the notable size difference between the larger 6M-series intermediates (originating from hexamethylbenzene) and the smaller 4M-series intermediates (originating from 1,2,3,5-tetramethylbenzene), the pore apertures of these zeolites may selectively accommodate or restrict the larger 6 M species, profoundly influencing the selectivity. As summarized in Figure and the comprehensive correlation matrix (Figure S3), the larger 6M-series intermediates exhibit significantly higher correlations between their binding energies and ln­(P/E) (natural logarithm of the propylene-to-ethylene ratio) compared to their smaller 4M-series counterparts. Specifically, the Pearson correlation coefficients between ln­(P/E) and binding energies are 0.588 for 6M6, 0.206 for 6M11, and 0.157 for 6M1. In contrast, for the 4M-series intermediates, the absolute value of the Pearson correlation coefficient reaches 0.179 for 4L3, while for all other species (4M11, 4M6, and 4M1), these coefficients remain below 0.052, close to zero. This clearly indicates that the 4M-series intermediates generally have weak correlations with selectivity, substantially lower than those observed in the 6 M series. Further insights can be drawn from the pairwise scatter plots presented in Figure B–D, which specifically explore the relationships between the binding energies of the critical 6M6 intermediate and other 6 M intermediates (6M1, 6L3, and 6M11). Notably, these plots explicitly highlight the pivotal role of the 6M6 intermediate, as it directly governs the formation of ethylene along the 6 M reaction pathway. As clearly illustrated, variations in the 6M6 binding energy show a pronounced and systematic influence on ln­(P/E), whereas analogous relationships for other intermediates are considerably less evident. This observation reinforces the conclusion that the 6M6 intermediate serves as the central determinant of catalytic selectivity within the 6 M reaction family. Taken together, these results highlight a mechanistic insight: the structural compatibility between the zeolite pore and larger hydrocarbon intermediatesparticularly the 6M6 speciesplays a key contributing role in modulating MTP selectivity. This correlation underpins our screening strategy, which aims to identify zeolite frameworks that can effectively stabilize key HCP intermediates, especially the 6M6 species that control the propylene/ethylene selectivity. Such stabilization offers guidance for the rational design of zeolite catalysts with enhanced propylene selectivity.

Comparison with Reported Experiments

To further validate the experimental relevance of the model’s predicted P/E selectivity, we compared our results with previously reported literature data. First, we selected experimental data reported by Corma et al. as a benchmark. Under uniform reaction conditions (623 K, WHSV = 0.8 h–1, catalyst weight = 50 mg), their team performed systematic catalytic performance tests on various zeolite frameworks, including LTA, AEI, LEV, CHA, and RTH. As shown in Table , the predicted P/E ratios for AEI, LEV, RTH, and ITE closely align with the experimental rankings reported by Corma and co-workers, specifically following the sequence of ITE > RTH > AEI > LEV, thus demonstrating robust model generalization capability. However, the predicted P/E ratio for the CHA framework was notably higher than experimental values, likely due to the model not fully capturing variables, such as unique acid-site distributions, diffusion limitations, or crystal size effects specific to CHA.

Limitation of the Model

Nonetheless, noticeable deviations were observed for the CHA and ERI frameworks. The equilibrium selectivity model, based on DFT adsorption energies and GCMC-derived coverage statistics, effectively captures the thermodynamic stabilization of intermediates and provides a quantitative descriptor for framework-dependent selectivity trends. However, it does not explicitly account for diffusion kinetics or acid-site heterogeneity. Ferri et al. reported that CHA-type zeolites exhibit strong diffusion constraints, with propylene diffusing 20–40 times more slowly than ethylene. This pronounced kinetic limitation, together with framework-specific site heterogeneity, likely leads to the lower experimental P/E ratio observed for CHA compared to the equilibrium prediction, highlighting the kinetic origin of this deviation. For the MFI framework, the equilibrium-based model tends to overestimate the P/E ratio relative to the experiment. This discrepancy arises because the current DFT/GCMC framework captures thermodynamic stabilization under equilibrium assumptions, whereas the MFI topology allows rapid diffusion and fast turnover of HCP intermediates under the reaction conditions. The dynamic nature of product desorption and β-scission therefore shortens the lifetime of bulky intermediates, such as 6M6, yielding a steady-state P/E ratio lower than that predicted by equilibrium statistics. These results indicate that kinetic accessibility and diffusional escape probabilities critically influence the experimentally observed selectivity. Future refinements could incorporate nonequilibrium effects through an effectiveness-factor correction or by coupling the equilibrium framework with microkinetic or kinetic Monte Carlo simulations to explicitly model reaction–diffusion interplay within zeolite channels. Our current model assumes a uniform distribution of acid sites in pure silica catalysts. However, real catalysts may exhibit variations in the acid-site distribution. Additionally, the model is based on thermodynamic principles, using the Boltzmann distribution, and does not currently incorporate kinetic effects like diffusion. In future work, we will include more complex acid-site models and consider kinetic effects, such as diffusion, to improve the accuracy of the model.

Zeolite Synthesis and Ga Coordination Structure Characterization

To rigorously validate the predictive capability of the developed machine learning model and elucidate the structure–selectivity relationships underlying MTP catalysis, we systematically selected representative zeolite frameworks for subsequent experimental validation. Specifically, predicted zeolite candidates were initially categorized into three distinct performance groups according to their computed P/E ratios: category A (high performance, top 5%), category B (medium performance, 5–50%), and category C (low performance, bottom 50%). Among the broader computational screening data set of 10,818 hypothetical zeolites, the MFI (ranked 386) and DDR (ranked 5870) frameworks emerged as representative candidates for categories A and B, respectively. To facilitate the translation from computational predictions to experimental validation, further analysis was performed on a refined subset comprising 107 experimentally known zeolites listed in the IZA database. Within this subset, zeolite GME demonstrated the highest predicted P/E ratio of 573.96, followed by TER (186.47) and SOR (145.78). Zeolite MFI ranked fourth, exhibiting a P/E ratio of 124.9, whereas DDR ranked 45th with a P/E ratio of 2.22. However, despite their high theoretical potential, zeolites, such as GME, TER, and SOR, impose considerable synthetic challenges, limiting their viability for practical or industrial applications. For instance, the synthesis of GME typically requires prolonged hydrothermal crystallization times exceeding 30 days, thus rendering its large-scale preparation impractical. Consequently, considering both the feasibility of the synthesis and industrial relevance, zeolites MFI and DDR were prioritized as optimal candidates. This balanced selection integrates predicted catalytic performance, mature synthetic methodologies, and established industrial applicability, thereby bridging the gap between computational design and practical catalytic implementations.

To validate the impact of the framework structure on catalytic performance, especially the P/E ratios, in this study, we employed Ga atoms to dope pure silica DDR and MFI zeolites and introduce acidity onto zeolites, which was the active site for methanol conversion. Ga-DDR and Ga-MFI, with an isolated and uniform Ga environment, were constructed through the ligand-protected hydrothermal synthesis method for the first time, which meant that less than a single Ga site was embedded into the framework per unit cell of the synthesized zeolites theoretically. The pure-phase DDR and MFI zeolites were first confirmed by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns (Figure S4) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Figure A–D) images. For DDR-type zeolites, the typical DDR peaks were recorded at 2θ = 6.5, 7.7, 12.8, 15.4, and 17.1°, etc., corresponding to the characteristic lattice planes of (003), (101), (110), (202), (024), etc. In Figure B, rhombohedral crystals for zeolite DDR with a particle size of around 1 μm were observed. For MFI-type zeolites, spherical particles with a comparable size of 1.0 μm were recorded, which excluded the possible influence of particle sizes on catalytic performance. The N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms in Figure S5 exhibited a typical type I isotherm, characteristic of microporous materials. Ga-DDR obtained in a 6.5 day crystallization period showed a high BET surface area (ABET) and a micropore volume (V mic, determined by t-plot method) of 316 m2 g–1 and 0.116 cm3 g–1, respectively (Table S3). − The ABET and Vmic of Ga-MFI were 442 m2 g–1 and 0.154 cm3 g–1, respectively. The Ga/Si mole ratios of Ga-DDR and Ga-MFI zeolite samples were determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) to be 0.012, and 0.014, respectively.

To confirm the microenvironment of Ga species in Ga-DDR and Ga-MFI, infrared spectroscopy experiments (Figure E,F), NMR (Figure G–J), and XANES (Figure K,L) were performed to examine their coordination structure. Compared with pure silica DDR and MFI zeolites, the identical adsorption peaks of IR spectra for corresponding Ga-DDR and Ga-MFI demonstrated the incorporation of Ga into the framework of zeolites. The high-frequency bands at 1103 and 800 cm–1 correspond to silicon and oxygen motion, respectively. The FT-IR spectrum of Ga-DDR (Figure E) was similar to that of DD3R. Ga-DDR exhibits bands at ca. 463 and 795 cm–1, which can be assigned to T–O–T bending and T–O (where T is Si or Ga) tetrahedral vibration, respectively. The T–O–T bending bond is centered at 463 cm–1 in the spectrum of DD3R, while it shifts to 453 cm–1 in the spectrum of Ga-DDR zeolite. The small peaks between 500 and 650 cm–1 were attributed to double-ring external linkage of the zeolite structure. The hydrophobicity of the DDR samples was confirmed by the weak adsorption at ∼3000 cm–1 (from −OH vibrations), which showed only a small amount of water. The FT-IR spectra of Ga-MFI (Figure F) were identical to our previous report about the synthesis of GaS-1 zeolites.

In Figure G, five major peaks at −110.8, −112.4, −118.2, −120.1, and −122.2 pm were observed in the typical Q4 range for Ga-DDR. , These signals represent various [SiO4] sites with different atomic and displacement parameters. No peaks ascribed to Si–(OSi)3OH were observed within the typical Q3 range (−90 to −100 ppm), demonstrating defect-free silicate structure. The 71Ga NMR spectrum showed a single peak around 150 ppm (Figure I), indicating that the Ga atoms are only located in a tetrahedral coordination. The 29Si NMR spectrum of Ga-MFI (Figure H) presented peaks at −118, −114 ppm (framework Q4 Si­(OSi)­4 structure), −107 ppm (framework Q4 Si­(OSi)3(OGa) structure), and −103 ppm (Si­(OSi)3(OH) structure), and their quantified proportion is listed in Table S4. , Figure J presents the 71Ga NMR spectrum of Ga-MFI, and the individual peak at 159 ppm was ascribed to the tetrahedral framework Ga­(OSi)4 structure, also proving the identical Ga coordination structure on the Ga-MFI framework. ,

X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) analysis was also performed to examine the local structure and electronic state of the Ga atoms. X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra at the Ga K-edge (Figure K) showed that the absorption edge energies of Ga-DDR and Ga-MFI were comparable, which defined the first inflection point of the XANES spectrum and was related to the charge state of the probed Ga atoms. The charge of Ga atoms on Ga-DDR and Ga-MFI was +3 since the position of absorption edge energy appeared at ca. 10,374 and 10,372 eV, respectively, similar to the reported standard Ga3+ species. The corresponding Fourier transform magnitude of the k3-weighted extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra of Ga-DDR and Ga-MFI only possessed a noticeable peak at 1.4 Å (Figure L), typical for the first shell scattering of the O atoms, indicating the absence of Ga2O3 on the prepared zeolites. The FT EXAFS spectra of Ga-DDR and Ga-MFI were fitted by the Artemis software package (Figures S6B, S7B and Table S5), and the results revealed the generation of a single Ga species in the form of tetrahedral coordination with framework O atoms on DDR and MFI zeolites.

Since the acid sites are the active sites for methanol conversion on zeolites, the ammonia temperature-programmed desorption (NH3-TPD) was conducted to estimate the acidity of Ga-DDR and Ga-MFI. The NH3 desorption curves (Figure S8) of both samples showed two peaks, and the overall content of acid sites strongly corresponded to the amount of Brönsted acid sites calculated from the Ga/Si ratio of the catalyst (Tables S6 and S7).

Catalytic Performance for MTP

MTP performance was conducted to validate the impact of topology structure on product distribution. , Herein, the MTP reactions were performed at 400 °C with a w8 hly space velocity (WHSV) of 1.0 h–1, as shown in Table S8 and Figure . For the Ga-DDR catalyst, a very concentrated product distribution was seen, which was primarily composed of light olefins (the selectivity of C2–C4 exceeding 87%) without any aromatic or large aliphatic (C6 +) hydrocarbons (Figure A and Table ). The propylene selectivity achieved 51.9% when the time-on-stream (TOS) arrived at 10 min. For the Ga-MFI catalyst (Figure B), C4 + products increased obviously, and even <3% benzene, toluene, and xylene (BTX) were formed, which explained that small-pore Ga-DDR zeolite was prone toward light olefin production, in comparison with medium-pore zeolite Ga-MFI. According to the feature importance results derived from DFT calculations, GCMC simulations, and machine learning models, the Ga-DDR zeolite exhibited a narrower product distribution compared to MFI, attributable to its more restricted pore structure. This structural feature closely correlated with the PLDthe most influential structure descriptor identified in our analysis. Following our theoretical framework, the PLD significantly affected the mobility of smaller molecules within the Ga-DDR zeolite. Consequently, smaller olefins like ethylene, propylene, and trans-2-butylene could traverse the Ga-DDR pores, while larger olefins, such as cis-2-butylene and trans-2-pentene, were limited by the pore size (3.6 × 4.4 Å). The confinement of these larger olefin molecules within the zeolite may lead to repeated methylation and β-scission reactions, facilitating the olefin-based catalytic cycle. It was worth mentioning that, compared with catalytic performance over various zeolites in the literature (in Figure S9 and Table ), the synthesized Ga-MFI in this study unveiled the highest P/E ratio, which was related to synergistic cooperation between active intermediates and their specific pore sizes and acidity for propylene production as mentioned above, just validating our screen result. Finally, compared with the Ga-DDR catalyst, Ga-MFI exhibited an ultralong lifetime of 121.3 h (methanol conversion >95%) for its lower S/W ratios, which was almost 48 times longer than that of Ga-DDR. Additionally, for Ga-MFI catalysts, the optimal Ga content was approximately 1.2 wt %. Lower Ga content resulted in insufficient methanol conversion, while higher Ga content led to a noticeable decrease in the P/E ratio. More details about the impact of acidity, temperature, and space velocity on the P/E ratios and lifetime of Ga-MFI catalysts can be found in our previous report. TG-DSC curves in Figure S10 proved that only 5.21% weight loss for the Ga-MFI catalyst in 121 h was induced by coking, confirming the positive impact of weak acid sites on lifetime elongation compared with the Al-MFI catalyst. Besides, the Ga–DDR sample presented a 5.19% weight loss from coke burning during the 2.5 h temperature-ramping process, which demonstrated its obvious coking phenomenon and the significant impact of the framework topology on this behavior, as illustrated in Figure S11. Besides, the decrease in surface area from 442 m2 g–1 (fresh Ga-MFI) to 322 m2 g–1 (spent Ga-MFI) and 316 m2 g–1 (fresh Ga-DDR) to 208 m2 g–1 (spent Ga-DDR) also confirmed the presence of coking on used catalysts.

Conclusions

In summary, we proposed an interpretable function to evaluate the P/E ratio based on the adsorption energy of HCP intermediates and demonstrated an ML approach to screen the optimal zeolite frameworks from 20,191 zeolite structures for the MTP reaction. The GCMC-derived heat of adsorption emerges as the most influential feature, followed by PLD, underscoring the combined contributions of spatial confinement and chemical interactions in modulating the selectivity. The ML prediction of P/E selectivity is supported by reported and our own experiments. MFI and DDR catalysts were selected for experiment validation. Ga atoms were incorporated into pure silica zeolites to create active acidic sites. According to the experimental results, Ga-DDR exhibited a P/E ratio of 2.5, whereas Ga-MFI demonstrated a significantly higher ratio of 12.3 and maintained catalytic activity for 121.3 h, which is 48 times longer than that of Ga-DDR. These results validate the accuracy of our screen method. This comprehensive strategy for screening the optimal zeolite frameworks for the MTP reaction provides opportunities to explore unknown zeolites and refine synthesis techniques. Our findings aim to contribute to ongoing zeolite research for MTP reactions, offering insights into the discovery of efficient catalysts.
