Age- and cell-type-specific effects of histone variant H2BE in the brain
Authors: Sean Louzon, Emily R Feierman, Qi Qiu, Marissa J Maroni, Camille N Quaye, Roxanne Perez-Tremble, Hao Wu, Erica Korb
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYTranscription is regulated in part through histone proteins. Histones can be replaced with variant forms that are particularly critical in the brain and accumulate throughout the lifespan. Recent findings demonstrated that the histone variant H2BE regulates chromatin structure, neuronal transcription, and mouse behavior. However, the role of H2BE in other cell types and throughout the lifespan remains unknown. Here, we discovered that H2BE is enriched in astrocytes and accumulates with age in both astrocytes and neurons. Further, we found that H2BE promotes synaptic gene expression in young neurons and astrocytes and is critical in both cell types for proper synaptic function. In aging brains, loss of H2BE similarly affects synaptic genes in neurons but dampens age-related transcriptional changes in both astrocytes and neurons. Lastly, H2BE loss disrupts long-term memory but improves working memory in aging mice. Together, these data link histone variants with aging-related gene-expression changes in both astrocytes and neurons.

INTRODUCTION

Chromatin consists of a complex of DNA and histone proteins that organize the genome and play a vital role in controlling gene expression and cellular function. Chromatin is regulated through several mechanisms, including incorporation of histone variants into nucleosomes in place of canonical histones. Histone variants contain unique amino acid sequences and are encoded by separate genes. There are a limited number of well-characterized histone variants, but their importance in the brain is becoming increasingly appreciated.1–10 Prior research, however, has primarily focused on their roles in neurons, and the impact of histone variants on glial cells has remained largely unstudied. In terminally differentiated cells, such as neurons, histone variants accumulate throughout the lifespan,2,5 as variants are uniquely synthesized throughout the cell cycle.11,12 It is unknown, however, whether the accumulation of variants in the brain with age is unique to neurons.

While multiple H2A and H3 variants have been well studied, the only mammalian H2B variant that has been functionally characterized outside of the germline is H2BE. H2BE was discovered in the mouse olfactory system, where it regulates olfactory neuron function and lifespan.13 The expression of H2BE was thought to be limited to the olfactory system until recent discoveries uncovered expression throughout the brain.14 Further, in young adult mice, H2BE promotes chromatin accessibility at critical neuronal genes to promote transcription, neuronal function, and memory.14 While prior work focused on the role of H2BE in neurons, loss of H2BE also results in differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in astrocytes as well as neurons,14 suggesting it may also function in astrocytes. In addition, while H2BE accumulates in the brain through postnatal development,14 it is unknown whether it continues to accumulate throughout aging, what cell types express H2BE throughout the lifespan, and whether it has distinct functions in the aging brain.

Here, we sought to define the age- and cell-type-dependent role of H2BE in the brain. We applied imaging, single-nucleus RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), microelectrode array recordings, and animal behavior to characterize the regulation and function of H2BE. We demonstrate that H2BE is highly expressed in astrocytes and accumulates with age in both astrocytes and neurons. Using single-nucleus RNA-seq, we find that loss of H2BE impairs synaptic gene expression in neurons and astrocytes, and, using microelectrode array recordings, we demonstrate that H2BE is required in both these cell types for proper synaptic firing. Notably, loss of H2BE in young brains causes transcriptional changes that mirror aging-induced changes. In contrast, loss of H2BE in aged brains has mixed transcriptional effects and dampens some aging-induced changes. Finally, we discovered that, in aged mice, H2BE loss impairs long-term memory, similar to results in young mice, but improves working memory. Taken together, these data demonstrate histone-variant function in astrocytes and define the relationship between H2BE and age-related changes in gene expression and cognition.

RESULTS

H2BE is enriched in astrocytes and localizes at active promoters

While the importance of histone variants in the brain is well established,1–10 most research thus far has focused on their role in neurons. Recent work demonstrated that the variant H2BE is critical in long-term memory and defined its role in neuronal gene expression and function.14 However, single-nucleus RNA-seq also revealed that H2BE knockout (KO) causes a striking disruption to gene expression in astrocytes.14 We therefore sought to determine whether H2BE is expressed within astrocytes in addition to neurons. We first consulted publicly available datasets to compare H2BE levels in different cell types in the brain and found that H2BE transcript (H2bc21) is enriched in astrocytes compared to both neurons and other glial cell types15 (Figure 1A). This enrichment in astrocytes is also atypical compared to most H2B genes15 (Figure S1A). To compare H2BE protein expression in different cell types, we generated coronal sections from adult wild-type (WT) mice. We then co-stained for the astrocyte maker Sox9,16 the neuronal marker NeuN, and H2BE using an antibody that we previously demonstrated is highly specific for H2BE, including in immunohistochemistry assays.14 We found strong colocalization of H2BE with both NeuN and Sox9 in the cortex (Figure 1B), demonstrating H2BE protein expression in both neurons and astrocytes. Furthermore, H2BE levels are significantly elevated in astrocytes compared to neurons and NeuN−,Sox9− nuclei (Figures 1C and S1B). Defining H2BE+ nuclei as those with H2BE intensity at least 2× greater than background staining, we found that nearly 100% of astrocytes are H2BE+ (Figure 1D). Additionally, we saw similar enrichment of H2BE in Aldh1l1+ astrocytes (Figure S1C) and across multiple brain regions (Figure S1D). This unexpected finding suggests that H2BE may play a critical role in astrocyte function in addition to established roles in neurons.

To further investigate H2BE expression in astrocytes, we generated primary cortical astrocyte cultures from postnatal day 1 pups and primary cortical neuronal cultures from embryonic day 16.5. We again detected robust H2BE expression in cultured astrocytes (Figure 1E) as well as GFAP expression, suggesting a reactive astrocyte state that is typical of most astrocyte cultures. We next performed cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag) with sequencing18 to define H2BE localization in the genome. Similar to previous findings in neurons,14 we found that H2BE is highly enriched in core promoter regions throughout the genome (Figures 1F–1H) with modest enrichment at enhancer regions (Figure S1E). By directly comparing the genes with H2BE peaks in astrocytes with those previously identified in neurons,14 we found that greater than 50% of the top genes are shared between the cell types (Figure S1F). Specifically in astrocytes, these genes are involved in diverse cellular processes including the cell cycle, DNA metabolism, and stress response (Figure 1I). Also, similarly to neurons, H2BE enrichment is positively correlated with chromatin accessibility in astrocytes (Figure 1J), assessed through transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (ATAC-seq),19 and with high levels of gene expression in astrocytes (Figures 1K and 1L; S1G). These data demonstrate that, within astrocytes, H2BE is enriched at core promoter regions of highly expressed and accessible genes. Notably, these findings also provide evidence of core histone-variant expression in astrocytes outside of disease contexts.

H2BE accumulates with age in the brain

Recent work demonstrated that H2BE expression increases in the cortex within the first 16 weeks of life,14 but this was not explored throughout adulthood or with aging. Using publicly available bulk RNA-seq data of astrocytes isolated from mouse cortices at various ages,20 we discovered that H2BE transcript expression accumulates with age (Figure 2A). The accumulation of H2BE is unique, as it is the only H2B gene (out of the 18 that are detected) that is induced with age (Figure 2A). Surprisingly, neither H2AZ nor H3.3 transcript expression increased in aged astrocytes (Figure 2B). While previous work demonstrated that they also accumulate in aged brain tissue,2,5 this may be restricted to neurons. RNA-seq of neurons isolated from mouse cortices at developmental time points suggest that H2BE transcript expression also accumulates in neurons (Figure S2A), fitting with prior analysis of protein accumulation up through young adulthood.14 To test whether human H2BE transcript similarly increases with age, we consulted publicly available transcriptomic data of the human brain throughout the lifespan.21,22 Human H2BE transcript levels follow a similar trajectory to H2AZ and H3.3 (Figures S2B–S2D), both of which increase throughout the lifespan at the protein level.2,5 While none appear to show increased transcript levels with age in these datasets, all are expressed throughout the lifespan, which provides the potential for protein accumulation within chromatin.

Because prior analysis of H2BE protein levels was limited to the first 16 weeks of life,14 we assessed H2BE protein abundance in the mouse cortex throughout the lifespan through mass spectrometry. We discovered that, while H2BE protein makes up only a small portion of all H2B proteins in the cortex, it significantly accumulates with age (Figure 2C). To assess what genomic loci accumulate H2BE during aging, we performed CUT&Tag with sequencing on 3-, 12-, and 18-month-old mouse cortices. As expected, H2BE was strongly enriched at promoter regions and overall increased with age (Figures 2D–2F and S2E).

Based on transcriptomic data, we hypothesized that increased H2BE protein expression with age is at least partially due to its accumulation in astrocytes. To test this, we performed immunohistochemistry of brain sections of 3- and 12-month-old mice and co-stained with H2BE, Sox9, and NeuN (Figure 2G). While we found that the portion of cells expressing H2BE in the cortex is not impacted by age (Figure 2H), H2BE levels are significantly increased with age in astrocytes, neurons, and NeuN−,Sox9− cells (Figure 2I). To then assess whether there are any morphological differences between H2BE+ and H2BE− cells, we measured nuclei size and shape. We found no significant differences in neurons and only minor effects in astrocytes, with astrocyte comparisons limited by the near-complete absence of H2BE− cells (Figures S2F and S2G). NeuN−,Sox9− cells that are H2BE+ have larger, more irregularly shaped nuclei (Figure S2H), which may be due to two or more different cell types disproportionately represented in H2BE− negative and H2BE+ cells (such as microglia, oligodendrocytes, or other types). Together, these data confirm that H2BE is highly enriched in astrocytes and demonstrate that it accumulates with age in astrocytes as well as neurons.

We next sought to determine the mechanisms that lead to increased H2BE expression in astrocytes. Based on previous work in olfactory epithelial neurons, H2BE KO alters the expression of cell-cycle and senescence-related genes and affects cell survival.13 We therefore began by examining whether H2BE expression in primary astrocytes is dynamic in response to cell death or senescence-inducing stimuli. Etoposide-induced apoptosis,23 oxidative stress-induced senescence,24 radiation-induced senescence,25 and serum deprivation each had no effects or only minor effects on H2BE levels (Figures S3A–S3D). Astrocyte stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) decreased H2BE expression (Figure S3E), matching the activity-induced decrease in H2BE observed in olfactory epithelial neurons.13 In contrast, passaging-induced replicative senescence significantly induced H2BE levels in astrocytes at extended times following passaging (Figures S3F–S3J). Notably, this induction is unique to H2BE, as canonical histone H2B and histone variants H2AZ and H3.3 do not similarly accumulate (Figures S3I and S3J). To further confirm this, we performed mass spectrometry of cultured astrocytes and found that H2BE is robustly increased following passaging (Figure S3K).

We next examined what factors regulate H2BE levels. We performed motif analysis of the H2BE promoter and discovered multiple binding motifs for the E2F-1 transcription factor (Figure S3L), a member of a family of transcription factors involved in regulating cell-cycle progression and DNA replication. Using publicly available data,15 we discovered that, similar to H2BE, E2F1 transcript levels are enriched in astrocytes (Figure S3M). To determine whether E2F-1 binds the H2BE promoter, we performed E2F-1 CUT&Tag with sequencing in cultured astrocytes. We discovered an overall increase with passaging throughout the genome (Figure S3N) and specifically at the promoter of H2BE (Figures S3O and S3P). Finally, to assess the impact on H2BE levels, we used an E2F inhibitor and found that it blocked the passaging-induced increase in H2BE (Figure S3Q). Taken together, these findings suggest that E2F-1 is a regulator of H2BE expression in astrocytes.

H2BE promotes synaptic gene expression in young and old neurons and astrocytes

Given the cell-type- and age-dependent regulation of H2BE, we next sought to investigate the impact of H2BE on gene expression in the aging brain for both astrocytes and neurons. We performed droplet-based single-nucleus RNA-seq (Drop-seq26) on cortical tissue from young (3-month-old, three biological replicates/genotype) and aged (18-month-old, two biological replicates/genotype) WT and H2BE whole-body KO male mice (Figure S4A). H2BE-KO mice were generated as previously described13 (the endogenous H2BE CDS was replaced with a membrane-targeted mCherry reporter sequence in C57BL/6 mice). We identified 24 unique cell clusters that include all expected cell types (Figure 3A). Cell identity was determined as previously described14 using established cortical marker genes (Figure S4B). We did not detect any significant changes in cluster identity across genotypes, but we did detect modest age-related changes, mainly in clusters containing small numbers of nuclei and those linked to age-related changes27 (Figures S4C–S4H).

DEGs between WT and KO were found in most neuronal and non-neuronal clusters (Figure 3B). In both young and old nuclei, loss of H2BE resulted in greater downregulation of genes in most clusters (Figure 3B), matching previous findings.14 Intriguingly, neuronal clusters had more DEGs in old nuclei compared to young, suggesting that the effects of H2BE KO accumulate in the brain with age. However, astrocytes were the exception to this trend, with more DEGs in young nuclei. While H2BE accumulates with age in astrocytes, it is already highly abundant in young astrocytes, potentially leading to maximal effects detected early that are later occluded by age-related changes in WT astrocytes.

To better understand the complex interaction of aging and H2BE loss on gene expression, we next focused on H2BE-dependent gene expression in specific clusters of neurons and astrocytes. In a representative excitatory neuron cluster (Ex_L6_1), we found that nearly all of the genes that are downregulated by loss of H2BE in young nuclei are similarly downregulated in old nuclei, with young DEGs representing a subset of those identified in old (Figure 3C). We expect that upregulated DEGs are not direct targets of H2BE given its role in promoting chromatin accessibility14 and are more likely to be secondary or compensatory responses. However, we still observed an overlap in upregulated genes, indicative of similar secondary responses to H2BE loss with age (Figure 3C). As expected based on our previous work,14 downregulated genes are enriched for functions related to synapse organization and synaptic signaling (Figures 3D, 3E, and S4I). These findings are similarly reflected in a representative inhibitory neuron cluster (Inh_Erbb4), with overlapping young and old DEGs and downregulation of synapse-related genes (Figures 3F–3H and S4J). These data suggest that H2BE causes similar gene-expression changes in neurons regardless of age, with greater changes observed in aging brains, fitting with data demonstrating greater neuronal H2BE expression with age (Figures 2G–2I).

We next compared gene-expression changes in astrocytes in young and old mice. H2BE loss results in considerably fewer overlapping DEGs in young and aging astrocytes (Figure 3I). Surprisingly, despite this, loss of H2BE in young and old astrocytes similarly leads to downregulation of genes that support synapse function (Figures 3J, 3K, and S4K), suggesting similar functional changes occur in response to H2BE loss. These include genes that encode proteins known to localize at synapses and whose expression levels in astrocytes are linked to neurological disorders, such as Magi2.28,29 These data demonstrate that, despite differences in overall DEG numbers, H2BE consistently promotes expression of genes related to synaptic function. These findings, along with the high expression of H2BE in astrocytes (Figures 1B–1D), suggest that astrocytes may contribute to the functional effects on synaptic function in H2BE-KO neurons.14

H2BE is necessary in both neurons and astrocytes for proper synaptic function

To test whether the effects of H2BE KO in both neurons and astrocytes lead to deficits in synaptic function, we used microelectrode-array recordings to determine the cell-type-specific effect of H2BE loss on neuronal firing (Figure 4A). We generated neuron-astrocyte cocultures containing WT neurons and WT astrocytes, KO neurons and KO astrocytes, WT neurons and KO astrocytes, or KO neurons and WT astrocytes. Intriguingly, loss of H2BE in neurons, or astrocytes, or both lead to impaired firing rate, burst frequency, and network burst frequency of neurons within the cocultures (Figures 4B–4E). In contrast, loss of H2BE in neurons but not astrocytes resulted in increased variability in network burst duration (Figure 4F). These effects held even when subsetting data to only compare replicates in which all electrodes were active (Figure S5), suggesting that differences were not due to variable electrode coverage. These findings demonstrate that H2BE is required in both neurons and astrocytes for proper synaptic firing and suggest that transcriptomic disruptions caused by H2BE loss result in functional changes in neuronal activity.

Loss of H2BE dampens aging-related transcriptional changes

Given the functional impact of H2BE on synapses and the overlapping effects of H2BE on synaptic gene expression in young and old brains, we further probed single-nucleus Drop-seq data to define age-related transcriptional changes. Examination of aging-induced DEGs (Figure 5A) demonstrated that significantly more genes are upregulated with age in neuronal clusters, consistent with prior studies.30–32 Oligodendrocytes and astrocytes have slightly more downregulated genes with age, but few overall DEGs in young-vs.-old comparisons. In addition, for almost all clusters, WT nuclei showed greater changes with aging than KO, indicating that loss of H2BE dampens age-related changes in gene expression. To test this, we assessed H2BE CUT&Tag signal at the promoters of aging-induced DEGs (Figure 5B). We observed that H2BE accumulates with age at the promoters of genes that are transcriptionally upregulated with age in excitatory and inhibitory neurons and in astrocytes. Further, this increase was specific to upregulated genes and was not detected in downregulated or unchanged genes. These findings support a model in which H2BE promotes age-induced transcriptional changes in the brain.

We next focused on age-dependent gene expression in specific clusters of neurons and astrocytes. In Ex_L6_1 neurons, the majority of aging-related DEGs in KO nuclei are shared with WT nuclei (Figure 5C), suggesting that similar genes are impacted despite the difference in number of DEGs. In both genotypes, we detected an enrichment of genes related to synaptic function and cellular metabolism that are upregulated with age (Figures 5D, 5E, and S6A), with similar effects observed in inhibitory neurons (Figures 5F–5H and S6B). In astrocytes, conversely, we found some overlap between the aging-related DEGs in WT and KO, but the most striking effect is the near-complete loss of DEGs in KO (Figure 5I). We found a significant downregulation of synapse-related genes with age in WT astrocytes (Figures 5J and 5K) but no significantly altered Gene Ontology (GO) terms in KO astrocytes, likely due to the low gene number. Taken together, these data indicate that loss of H2BE dampens age-induced transcriptional changes in both neurons and astrocytes.

Loss of H2BE in young brains mirrors age-related effects on gene expression, while loss of H2BE in old brains has mixed effects

Given the loss of age-related DEGs in KO nuclei, we next sought to directly compare H2BE-induced and aging-induced changes in gene expression. In Ex_L6_1 neurons, we found that most of the genes that are downregulated with aging are similarly downregulated with KO in young nuclei (Figure 6A). These data indicate that loss of H2BE in young excitatory neurons induces a transcriptional response that overlaps with transcriptional changes induced by aging in a WT context (Figure 6B). We observe similar overlaps when we compare genes that are upregulated with aging and upregulated with KO in young nuclei (Figures S6C and S6D). However, there is strikingly no overlap between genes that are induced in opposite directions with aging and KO in young nuclei (up in aged and down in KO or down in aged and up in KO) (Figure S6E).

We next examined whether genes that are differentially expressed with aging are further differentially expressed in aged KO nuclei compared to old WT nuclei. In Ex_L6_1 neurons, there are overlaps of genes that are differentially expressed with age and differentially expressed with KO in old nuclei, both in the same and opposite directions (Figures 6C, 6D, S6F, and S6G). This indicates that, in contrast to young neurons, there are aging-related transcriptional changes that are enhanced by KO in old neurons and aging-related transcriptional changes that are reversed by KO in old neurons. We note several genes in which there is a near-complete reversal of age-induced effects on gene expression (Figure 6D).

In Inh_Erbb4 neurons, we observe similar trends, with young KO nuclei transcriptionally mirroring old WT nuclei (Figures 6E, 6F, and S6H–S6J) and old KO neurons showing divergent overlaps with aging-induced changes (Figures 6G, 6H, S6K, and S6L). In astrocytes, we commonly observed even more drastic changes in young KO astrocytes that similarly resemble aging WT expression levels (Figures 6I, 6J, and S6M–S6O). Similar to neurons, there are also overlaps between aging-related gene-expression changes and KO-induced gene-expression changes in old astrocytes (Figures 6K, 6L, S6P, and S6Q). To further validate this finding, we isolated astrocytes from WT and KO mice paired with Aldh1l1-Cre and a Cre-dependent GFP-Sun1-Myc transgene to allow for isolation of astrocytes via isolation of nuclei tagged in specific cell types (INTACT).34,35 We then performed qPCR on isolated astrocyte nuclei and observed similar trends (Figure S6R).

To further explore the complex gene-expression changes across cell type, genotype, and age, we assessed cell-cell communication using CellChat.33 This analysis utilizes a database of known ligand-receptor complexes to infer intercellular signaling networks. First, comparing young WT and KO nuclei (Figure 6M), we detected a robust loss of signaling from astrocytes to other clusters, matching our previous findings of the significant effects of H2BE loss in young astrocytes (Figure 3B). Comparing old WT and KO nuclei (Figure 6N), we detected a significant loss of signaling from neurons, fitting with the greater impact of H2BE loss in aged neurons (Figure 3B). However, we also detected enhanced signaling in old astrocytes with loss of H2BE. When comparing signaling changes between young and old nuclei, we found substantial aging effects in WT nuclei and dampened effects in KO nuclei (Figures 6O and 6P), mirroring what we observed in overall DEG numbers (Figure 5A). Finally, we explored the specific signaling pathways that were disrupted in astrocytes and found that neurexin (NRXN) signaling was significantly impaired with both age and loss of H2BE (Figure S6S), fitting with a role for H2BE in astrocytes in promoting the expression of genes that support neuronal synaptic function. In summary, loss of H2BE disrupts transcription of genes underlying intercellular signaling pathways, with the most pronounced effects detected in astrocytes.

H2BE differentially impacts working and long-term memory in aged mice

Given the complex interactions between H2BE KO in old nuclei and aging-related transcriptional changes, we next examined the behavioral impacts of H2BE KO in 18-month-old mice to define the functional effects of these changes on cognition in an aging brain. Similar to our previous work in young mice,14 loss of H2BE had no effect on anxiety-like behaviors or overall activity levels in an open-field test (Figures 7A, 7B, and S7A). We then sought to examine the role of H2BE in working memory, which prior studies indicate is disrupted with aging in rodent models.36–39 In a T-maze test of working memory, WT and KO mice had similar activity levels and similar latency to start exploring (Figures 7C, S7B, and S7C). Remarkably, KO mice had a significantly higher rate of spontaneous alternation (Figure 7D), indicating improved working memory. This was a particularly notable finding given young KO mice showed no impact of H2BE on working memory and no behavioral outcome in which performance was improved by loss of H2BE.14 Further, this effect was consistent regardless of whether mice with low arm entry rates were included in the data or not (Figure S7D) and was observed across two separate cohorts (Figure S7E).

To next assess long-term memory, we performed a novel-object recognition (NOR) test. While there was no significant effect of KO on discrimination index (Figures 7E and S7F), we note that both WT and KO mice had lower discrimination indices than we previously observed in young mice14 and had overall low object-interaction times (Figures S7G–S7J). This suggests that NOR performance was impaired regardless of genotype and that, given low interaction time overall, the aged mice may not have interacted sufficiently with objects to allow for recognition at delayed times. While we therefore did not observe deficits in performance, it is also notable that, unlike T-maze testing of working memory, H2BE KO did not improve overall exploratory behavior or long-term memory in this assay.

We next performed additional tests of long-term memory using contextual and cued fear conditioning consisting of foot shocks paired with a tone and specific context. In contextual-recall trials, mice are returned to the testing chamber and freezing is measured as a fear response to test for memory of the conditioned experience. In cued-recall trials, mice are placed in an altered context and re-exposed to the tone without the accompanying foot shock. H2BE KOs had no differences in freezing behavior before the initial tone-shock pairing (prestim) or during the first presentation of the tone (tone 1), indicating no baseline differences in freezing behavior between genotypes. However, KO mice had decreased freezing behavior during the 24-h and 14-day contextual recall and during the 48-h cued recall (Figures 7F and 7G), indicating a deficit in long-term memory. These data broadly fit with effects observed in young mice,14 with KO mice showing trends or significant deficits in both contextual and cued fear conditioning.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that loss of H2BE in old mice has both beneficial and detrimental effects on mouse cognition. This finding mirrors the complex effects observed on gene expression in the aged brain, where loss of H2BE impairs neuronal signaling and connectivity pathways, enhances astrocyte signaling pathways, and reverses specific age-related signatures.

DISCUSSION

Here, we show that H2BE is enriched in astrocytes and localizes at active promoters, similar to its localization in neurons. H2BE levels also increase with age in both cell types. Further, we show that H2BE promotes synaptic gene expression in neurons and astrocytes and is required in both for proper synaptic function. Loss of H2BE also prevents age-related transcriptional changes. Notably, loss of H2BE in young brains transcriptionally mimics the effects of aging, while loss of H2BE in old nuclei has divergent effects on gene expression. Lastly, we show that loss of H2BE in old mice impairs long-term memory similar to its role in young mice, but it markedly improves working memory. In summary, we provide evidence of an H2B variant that regulates cognition and gene expression in neurons and astrocytes in an age-dependent manner.

Particularly notable is H2BE accumulation in the brain with age in non-neuronal cells. While canonical histones are translated and deposited into chromatin during S phase of the cell cycle, variants are synthesized and deposited throughout the cell cycle. As a result, variants such as H2AZ,5 H3.3,2 and H2BE accumulate throughout the lifespan of terminally differentiated neurons. However, it was unknown whether variants similarly accumulate in cells that maintain replicative capacity with aging, such as astrocytes. Here, we demonstrate that H2BE levels increase with age in astrocytes as well as neurons. The accumulation in astrocytes could be similarly caused by a decrease in proliferative capacity of aged astrocytes. However, this is likely not the only contributing factor, as we found H2AZ and H3.3 transcripts do not similarly increase with age in astrocytes. This suggests that H2BE has unique regulatory features in astrocytes that contribute to its accumulation with age. We found that the transcription factor E2F-1 is enriched in astrocytes and regulates H2BE expression in primary cultured astrocytes. However, additional in vivo work is needed to determine whether E2F-1 regulates H2BE expression throughout the lifespan in the brain.

H2BE expression appears to plateau between 1 and 2 years of age in mice (Figure 2C). Given that H2BE promotes increased gene expression (Figure 5B), it is possible that there are compensatory or feedback mechanisms that constrain ongoing H2BE accumulation to prevent deleterious enhanced expression of target genes. It is also possible that H2BE expression reaches a maximum in astrocytes by 1 year of age, given a limited number of active promoters within the genome able to incorporate H2BE. Notably, other recent work from our group suggests that activity can regulate H2BE in neurons,40 which may also influence the rate at which H2BE accumulates in neurons. Furthermore, additional signals may be involved that regulate H2BE translation and/or transcript stability, which affects protein dynamics.

We find many similarities in H2BE function between neurons and astrocytes. H2BE enrichment is correlated with chromatin accessibility and gene expression in both.14 This fits with an innate ability of H2BE to promote open chromatin, which we previously demonstrated in a cell-free context and thus expect to be consistent across cell types.14 Somewhat unexpected is our finding that H2BE impacts gene expression of related functional groups in neurons and astrocytes. In both young and old neurons and astrocytes, loss of H2BE downregulates genes involved in synapse organization and signaling. Additionally, in primary neuron-astrocyte cocultures, H2BE is required in both cell types for proper synaptic function. This suggests that loss of H2BE in astrocytes likely contributes to the deficits previously reported in synaptic strength in young KO mice.14

Within the complexities of the age- and cell-type-dependent gene-expression changes induced by H2BE loss, we find notable correlates to the observed behavioral changes in H2BE-KO animals. In young mice, loss of H2BE impairs long-term memory14 and causes gene-expression changes in both neurons and astrocytes that mimic aging-induced effects on transcription. This suggests that both neurons and astrocytes may contribute to the cognitive deficits in young KO mice. Loss of H2BE in old mice improves working memory, correlating with gene-expression changes in old neurons and astrocytes that counter age-related transcriptional changes. This suggests that both cell types may govern H2BE-induced changes in cognition in old mice.

In summary, this work reveals how the histone variant H2BE contributes to the complex transcriptional and cognitive changes that occur in the brain with age. Further, this research provides an in-depth characterization of histone-variant regulation and function in astrocytes and provides insights into age-related changes linked to chromatin regulation.

Limitations of the study

Although our data demonstrate that H2BE has dramatic effects on gene expression in both neurons and astrocytes, many questions remain. While we observed marked correlations between gene-expression changes in specific cell types and behavioral outcomes, we cannot determine specific cell-type contributions to the observed cognitive impairments and improvements with the whole-body H2BE-KO mouse. Conditional-KO mouse models will be required to definitively demonstrate whether observed behavioral changes are due to loss of H2BE in specific cell types. Similarly, conditional-KO mice would allow for additional behavioral testing and electrophysiology measurements that would further characterize the cell-type contributions to synaptic function. Finally, gene-expression analysis of conditional models has the potential to elucidate which of the observed gene-expression changes are direct and which are compensatory as a result of changes in other cell types.

In addition, current work cannot distinguish between the short-term and long-term effects of H2BE loss. The behavioral changes in H2BE-KO mice that we detect here could be directly due to the lack of H2BE in a given cell type at the age of measurement. Alternatively, they could be due to the cumulative effect of H2BE loss, causing long-term disruptions to neuronal circuits that will ultimately also affect behavior. This is particularly true for aging time points measured here, in which mice have lacked H2BE and thus have had long-term chromatin and transcriptional disruptions throughout their lifetimes. Pairing conditional animal models with inducible Cre systems could help to address these questions, although inducible loss of H2BE may be difficult to achieve, given that histones may be highly stable when incorporated into chromatin.

Finally, a major open question remains surrounding the mechanisms that govern H2BE localization. In line with our previous work in neurons,14 we find that H2BE is highly enriched at promoter regions in astrocytes. This suggests that the mechanisms contributing to H2BE genomic localization are not cell-type specific. Increased histone turnover at open chromatin regions like promoters could passively lead to H2BE accumulation, or there may be specific chaperone proteins that deposit/evict H2BE that are shared between cell types, similar to other variants.41,42 These findings would provide critical mechanistic insights into the age- and cell-type-dependent effects of H2BE in the brain.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Primary astrocyte culture

Cortices were dissected from P1 C57BL/6J pups and cultured in supplemented medium (DMEM/F12 [Gibco 11320–033], 10% FBS [Sigma F2442], 1% Pen/Strep [Gibco 15140–122]). They were plated in TC-treated 12- or 6-well plates coated with 0.05 mg/mL Poly-D-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich A-003-E). Un-passaged, untreated astrocytes were collected at 7 DIV. For passaging, cells were collected at 7 DIV using Trypsin Solution (Gibco 12605–010), pelleted at 170xg for 10 min, and split at a 1:4 ratio. Passaged astrocytes were then cultured up to 12 additional days.

Etoposide-induced apoptosis

Astrocyte cultures underwent etoposide-induced apoptosis following a protocol that was adapted from Ciacci-Zanella et al.23 In brief, cells were treated with 10μM etoposide (Sigma E1383) and collected 8 h or 24 h after treatment.

Oxidative stress-induced senescence

Astrocyte cultures underwent oxidative stress-induced senescence following a protocol that was adapted from Crowe et al.24 In brief, cells were treated with 200μM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for 2 h. Cells were then washed with PBS and returned to growth media in the incubator for 2–12 days.

Radiation-induced senescence

Astrocyte cultures underwent radiation-induced senescence following a protocol that was adapted from Hooten and Evans.25 In brief, media was removed from astrocyte cultures and replaced with PBS. Cells were then exposed to a single dose of 10 Gray of ionizing radiation using a gamma irradiator. The PBS on the astrocytes was then replaced with media and returned to the incubator for 2–12 days.

Serum deprivation

Astrocyte media was replaced with media lacking FBS at 6 DIV for cultures undergoing serum deprivation. Cells were then cultured for an additional 14 days before collection. Control cells for this experiment were cultured for the same total number of days with FBS-containing media.

LPS stimulation

1ug/mL of LPS (Sigma L8274) was added to astrocytes and cells were collected 1 to 72 h post stimulation. Stimulation occurred between DIV 6–9 and all cells were collected at DIV 9. LPS treatment was terminated at this time point as the cells are typically confluent and require passaging between 7 and 9 DIV.

E2F inhibitor treatment

9 days following passaging, astrocytes were treated with 40uM E2F inhibitor (MCE HLM006474) or DMSO as a control. Cells were collected 3 days later (12 days post passaging).

Primary neuronal culture

Neuronal cultures were obtained as previously described.14 In brief, cortices were dissected from E16.5 C57BL/6J embryos and cultured in supplemented neurobasal medium (Neurobasal [Gibco 21103–049], 2% B27 [Gibco 17504044], 1% GlutaMAX [Gibco 35050–061], 1% Pen-Strep [Gibco 15140–122]). They were plated in TC-treated 12- or 6-well plates coated with 0.05 mg/mL Poly-D-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich A-003-E). Neurons were treated with 0.5 μM AraC at 3–4 DIV and collected at 12 DIV.

Primary neuron-astrocyte coculture

Cocultures were obtained following a protocol that was adapted from Kulkarni et al.55 In brief, neurons and astrocytes were individually cultured as described above, with astrocytes plated 2 days prior to neurons. When astrocytes were 7 DIV, they were collected using Trypsin Solution (Gibco 12605–010), pelleted at 170xg for 10 min, and resuspended in coculture media (Neurobasal [Gibco 21103–049], 2% B-27 [Gibco 17504044], 1% G-5 [Gibco 17503012], 0.25% GlutaMAX [Gibco 35050–061], 1% Pen-Strep [Gibco 15140–122]). Neurons (5 DIV) underwent a media change to coculture media and astrocytes were then added at a neuron-to-astrocyte ratio of 3-to-1 (8×104 neurons and 2.7×104 astrocytes per well of a 48-well microelectrode array plate). After 2 days of coculture, 0.5uM AraC was added to promote astrocyte maturation and prevent cell division. Cocultures were analyzed using a microelectrode array 9 days after the addition of astrocytes (14 DIV total for neurons).

Mice

The H2BE KO mouse (The Jackson Laboratory strain #023819) was generated as previously described.13 In brief, the endogenous H2BE CDS was replaced with a membrane-targeted mCherry reporter sequence in C57BL/6 mice. In addition, Sun1-sfGFP mice (JAX Strain #030952) were crossed to NEX-Cre mice43 to allow for isolation of neuronal nuclei and to Aldh1l1-creERT2 mice (Jax Strain #031008) to allow for detection and isolation of astrocyte nuclei from cortical tissue. All experiments were conducted in accordance with and approval of the IACUC. All mice were housed in a 12-h light-dark cycle and fed a standard diet. Both male and female mice were included for all experiments except for CUT&Tag from cortical tissue and single-nucleus Drop-seq, which were performed on male mice only.

METHOD DETAILS

Immunohistochemistry

Brain sectioning

Mice were anesthetized with isofluorane and transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were collected and stored in 4% PFA overnight at 4C. They were subsequently rinsed with PBS and then placed in 30% sucrose and allowed to settle to the bottom at 4C. Brains were then cryopreserved in O.C.T. Coronal sections (for cortex and hippocampus images) and sagittal sections (for striatum and cerebellum images) were obtained at 10μm using a cryostat and mounted on Superfrost Plus slides.

Staining

Sections were washed once with PBS for 5 min, then blocked for 1 h at room temperature in blocking buffer (3% BSA supplemented with 0.2% Triton X-100). Primary antibody in blocking buffer (anti-H2BE, 1:500, Sigma ABE1384; anti-NeuN Alexa Fluor 488 conjugated, 1:200, Sigma MAB377X; anti-NeuN Alexa Fluor 555 conjugated, 1:200, Sigma MAB377A5; anti-Sox9 Alexa Fluor 555 conjugated, 1:200, AB5535-AF555; anti-GFP, 1:500, Novus Biologicals 100–1770) was then added to the sections and incubated overnight at 4C. Sections were then washed 3 times with PBS (5 min each) followed by a 1 h incubation in secondary antibody (Goat anti-Rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 647 conjugated, 1:500, A21244; Donkey anti-Goat IgG Alexa Fluor 488 conjugated, 1:500, A-11055) in blocking buffer at room temperature. Finally, sections were washed 2 times with PBS (5 min each), stained with 1ug/uL DAPI (Thermo Scientific 62247) in PBS for 10 min, washed 2 additional times with PBS (5 min each), and mounted with a coverslip using Prolong Gold mounting medium.

Imaging and data analysis

Slides were imaged on an upright Leica DM 6000, TCS SP8 laser scanning confocal microscope. The objective used was a ×63 HC PL APO CS2 oil objective with an NA of 1.40. Laser and imaging parameters were kept consistent within batches with each batch containing all conditions (sex and age) to allow for comparisons across conditions. Images in Figures 1 and 2 were collected from prefrontal cortex, focused primary motor areas, and primary somatosensory areas. Additional images from the dentate gyrus, CA1, striatum, and cerebellum are in Figure S1. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software and H2BE intensity was measured from summed Z-stacks using mean gray value. Cells were defined as positive for each marker if the mean gray value was greater than 2X background staining. Astrocytes were identified as Sox9+,NeuN− (or GFP+,NeuN−) and neurons as NeuN+,Sox9− (or NeuN+,GFP−).

Senescence β-galactosidase staining

Staining was performed following the kit’s protocol (Cell Signaling #9860). In brief, cultured astrocytes were washed once with PBS and fixed with Fixative Solution. They then underwent two additional PBS washes before being stained with β-Galactosidase Staining Solution overnight. The following morning, cells were washed with PBS and imaged on a brightfield microscope.

EdU cell proliferation imaging

EdU incorporation and imaging was performed following the kit’s protocol (Invitrogen #10338). In brief, Edu solution was added to the media of cultured astrocytes and incubated for 24 h. Following incubation, cells were fixed with 3.7% formaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100. The cells were then incubated with Click-iT reaction cocktail followed by Hoechst 33342 nuclear staining and finally imaging on a fluorescent microscope.

Protein extraction

Cultured cells were washed once with PBS and then scraped and collected in RIPA buffer (10% sucrose, 1% SDS, 5mM HEPES pH 7.9, 10mM sodium butyrate supplemented by protease inhibitor [Roche 04693124001], phosphatase inhibitor [Roche 04906837001], 1mM DTT, 1mM PMSF). Protein lysates were then mixed with 5X Loading Buffer (5% SDS, 0.3M Tris pH 6.8, 1.1mM Bromophenol blue, 37.5% glycerol), boiled for 10 min, and cooled on ice.

Western blotting

Protein lysates were separated by 4–20% Tris-glycine SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred to a 0.45-μm PVDF membrane (Sigma-Aldrich IPVH00010) for immunoblotting. Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature in 5% milk in 0.1% TBST and probed with primary antibody overnight at 4C (anti-H2BE, 1:2000, Sigma ABE1384; anti-H2B, 1:10000, ab1790; anti-NeuN, 1:500, MAB377; anti-GFAP, 1:2000, ab10062; anti-H2AZ, 1:1000, ab4174; anti-H3.3, 1:1000, 09–838; anti-Gapdh, 1:3000, ab8245). Membranes were then incubated with secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature (anti-Rabbit IgG HRP, 1:5000, ab6721; anti-Mouse IgG HRP, 1:5000, 58802).

CUT&Tag-sequencing

Library preparation and sequencing

Input samples were either 400,000 primary cortical astrocytes per biological replicate or 400,000 cortical nuclei. See “isolation of nuclei tagged in specific cell-types (INTACT)” section for details on nuclei isolation from cortex tissue. CUT&Tag was performed as previously described by Feierman et al.14 In brief, cells/nuclei were bound to Concanavalin A-coated beads (Bangs Laboratories BP531) and incubated with H2BE antibody (Millipore ABE1384), Rabbit IgG antibody (Sino Biological CR1), or E2F1 antibody (Invitrogen 32–1400) overnight at 4°C on a rocking shaker. Samples were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with secondary antibody (guinea pig anti-rabbit IgG (antibodies-online ABIN101961) or rabbit anti-mouse IgG (antibodies-online ABIN101785)). Tagmentation was then performed using pA-Tn5 adapter complex (Diagenode C01070001-T30) and Tagmentation Buffer (10mM MgCl2 in Dig-300 buffer(20mM HEPES pH 7.5, 300mM NaCl, 0.5mM spermidine, 0.01% digitonin)). Protein was then digested with Proteinase K and DNA was isolated by phenol-chloroform extraction. A universal i5 primer and uniquely barcoded i7 primer were ligated and libraries were amplified by PCR with NEBNext High-Fidelity 2X PCR Master Mix (NEB M0541). Library clean-up was performed with AMPure XP beads (Beckman A63880). Library size distribution was confirmed by capillary electrophoresis using an Agilent 4200 TapeStation with high sensitivity D1000 reagents (5067–5585), and libraries were quantified by qPCR using a KAPA Library Quantification Kit (Roche 07960140001). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq550 instrument (42-bp read length, paired end).

Data processing and analysis

Reads were mapped to Mus musculus genome build mm10 with Bowtie 2 (v2.4.5).44 H2BE biological replicates were merged using SAMtools (v1.15).5 Metaplots were generated using ngs.plot (v2.63)47 against the mouse genome. Peaks were called using MACS3 (v3.0.0b1)17 and annotated using HOMER (v4.10).48 We used a Peak Score cutoff of 25 for downstream analysis. IGV tools (2.12.3)50 was used to create genome browser views. The R package GenomicDistributions (v1.6.0)49 was utilized to assess the genomic distribution of peaks. DeepTools (v3.5.1)46 was used to generate raw counts at TSS coordinates ±500bp from mm10.

Gene ontology

For gene ontology analysis for CUT&Tag sequencing, peak coordinates were assigned gene names using HOMER. PANTHER (v18.0)51,56 was used to perform an overrepresentation test against the biological process complete ontology using default parameters. The Mus musculus genome was used as a background gene list. See “sNucDrop-seq” section for gene ontology analysis details for DEGs. For conciseness and visualization, terms were excluded that contained less than 5 or greater than 600 total genes.

ATAC-sequencing

Library preparation and sequencing

Input samples were 100,000 primary cortical astrocytes per biological replicate. Astrocytes were collected by scraping in cold lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-Cl (pH 7.5), 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.1% (vol/vol) NP-40, 0.1% (vol/vol) Tween 20 and 0.01% (vol/vol) digitonin) and washed in wash buffer (10 mM Tris-Cl (pH 7.5), 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2 and 0.1% (vol/vol) Tween 20). Transposition was performed with Tagment DNA TDE1 (Illumina, 15027865). Reactions were cleaned with AMPure XP beads (Beckman, A63880), and libraries were generated with NEBNext High-Fidelity 2X PCR Master Mix (NEB, M0541). Library size distribution was confirmed by capillary electrophoresis using an Agilent 4200 TapeStation with high sensitivity D1000 reagents (5067–5585). Libraries were also quantified by qPCR using KAPA Library Quantification Kit (Roche 07960140001). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq550 instrument (42-bp read length, paired end).

Data processing and analysis

Reads were mapped to Mus musculus genome build mm10 with Bowtie 2 (v2.4.5).44 The 3 biological replicates were merged (SAMtools v1.1545) and deepTools (v3.5.1)46 was used to generate raw counts at TSS coordinates ±500bp from mm10.

RNA-sequencing

Library preparation and sequencing

Input samples were 500,000 primary cortical astrocytes from 3 WT biological replicates. RNA was isolated using Zymo Quick-RNA Miniprep Plus Kit (R1057). RNA integrity was confirmed using an Agilent 4200 TapeStation with high sensitivity RNA reagents (5067–5579). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA kit (Illumina 20020595). Prior to sequencing, library size distribution was confirmed by capillary electrophoresis using an Agilent 4200 TapeStation with high sensitivity D1000 reagents (5067–5585), and libraries were quantified by qPCR using a KAPA Library Quantification Kit (Roche 07960140001). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq550 instrument (75-bp read length, single end).

Data processing and analysis

Reads were mapped to Mus musculus genome build mm10 with Star (v2.7.9a).53 The R package DESeq2 (v1.38.3)52 was used to generate normalized read counts. Genes with “no expression” were defined as those with less than 3 mean normalized reads. Remaining genes were then binned into 3 equally sized groups by mean normalized read counts to define “low expression”, “mid expression”, and “high expression”.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was collected utilizing Quick-RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo #R1055). cDNA was then generated using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems #4374967). RT-qPCR was then performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems #A46109). Primer sequences are provided in supplemental material.

Isolation of nuclei tagged in specific cell-types (INTACT)

For each biological replicate, one cortical hemisphere was dissected and flash frozen and stored at −80°C until INTACT was conducted. The INTACT procedure used was modified from Carpenter et al.34 and Mo et al.35 Tissue was homogenized using a loose pestle (10–15 strokes) in 1.2 mL of homogenization buffer supplemented with 1mM DTT, 0.15mM spermine, 0.5mM spermidine, and EDTA-free protease inhibitor (Roche). A 5% IGEPAL-630 solution was added (107ul), and the homogenate was further homogenized with a tight pestle (10–15 strokes). The sample was then mixed with 1.3 mL of 50% iodixanol density medium (Sigma D1556) and added to a polypropylene thin wall tube (Beckman and Coulter, 331372). The sample was then underlaid with a gradient of 30% and 40% iodixanol, and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 18 minutes (no brake) in a swinging bucket centrifuge at 4°C. During the spin, 80μL of Protein G Dynabeads (Life Technologies 10003D) were washed twice in wash buffer (0.4% IGEPAL-630 in homogenization buffer). Nuclei were collected at the 30%–40% interface and pre-cleared by incubating with 20μL of washed Protein G Dynabeads for 15 minutes. After removing the beads with a magnet, the mixture was split into two tubes (750μL each) and incubated with the GFP solution (5μL of 0.2 mg/mL rabbit monoclonal anti-GFP antibody [Life Technologies G10362] in 200μL of wash buffer) for 30 minutes. 30μL of washed Dynabeads were added to each of the two tubes, and the mixture was incubated for an additional 20 minutes. To increase yield, the bead-nuclei mixture was placed on a magnet for 1 minute, completely resuspended by inversion, and placed back on the magnet. This was repeated seven times. Bead-bound nuclei were then washed 3 times with 800uL of wash buffer. All steps were performed on ice or in the cold room, and all incubations were carried out using an end-to-end rotator. For purifying RNA following INTACT, RNasin Plus Ribonuclease Inhibitor (Promega N2611) was added to the antibody and bead buffers. RNA was then isolated using Zymo Quick-RNA Miniprep Plus Kit (R1057).

Quantitative mass spectrometry

Quantitative mass spectrometry was performed as previously described in Feierman et al.14 In brief, samples were solubilized and digested in-solution. Parallel Reaction Monitoring (PRM) assay57,58 was developed using targeted peptides unique to H2BE (EIQTSVR) and H2B (EIQTAVR). Samples were analyzed on a Q-Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermofisher Scientific San Jose, CA) coupled with an Ultimate 3000 nano UPLC system and an EasySpray source. The mass spectrometer was set to repetitively scan m/z from 300 to 1400 (R = 240,000) followed by data-dependent MS/MS scans on the twenty most abundant ions. Skyline 21.2.0.568 with its default settings was used to process the PRM raw data. For each sample, light/heavy peak area ratio was calculated and multiplied by the known heavy peptide amount per injection to estimate the light peptide abundance value.

Single-nucleus drop-sequencing (sNucDrop-seq)

Sample preparation and sequencing

Samples were prepared and sequenced as previously described by Feierman et al.14 In brief, snap-frozen cortical tissue was homogenized and nuclei were isolated using a sucrose-gradient. Single-nucleus suspensions were then co-encapsulated with barcoded beads (ChemGenes) using an Aquapel-coated PDMS microfluidic device (mFluidix) connected to syringe pumps (KD Scientific).59 Droplet breakage with Perfluoro-1-octanol (Sigma-Aldrich), reverse transcription, and exonuclease I treatment were performed. cDNA was subsequently amplified with 2x KAPA FAST qPCR readymix and tagmented using the Nextera XT DNA sample preparation kit (Illumina, FC-131–1096). The tagmented cDNA was then further amplified using the Illumina Nextera XT i7 primers along with a P5-TSO hybrid primer and quality control analysis was performed by Qubit 3.0 (Invitrogen) and a Bioanalyzer (Agilent). Finally, libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 550 instrument using the 75-cycle High Output v2 Kit (Illumina).

Preprocessing of sNucDrop-seq data

Data was processed as previously described by Feierman et al.14 In brief, each mRNA read was tagged with the cell barcode and UMI, trimmed of adaptors and poly-A sequences, and aligned (STAR v2.5.2a) to the mm10 mouse reference genome (Gencode release vM13). Digital expression matrices were generated by assembling a list of UMIs in each gene within each cell. The total number of unique UMI sequences was reported as the number of transcripts of that gene for a given nucleus.

Nuclei clustering and marker gene identification

Raw digital expression matrices were combined and loaded into the R package Seurat.54 For normalization, UMI counts for all nuclei were scaled by library size, multiplied by 10,000, and transformed to log scale. Nuclei with a high percentage of UMIs mapped to mitochondrial genes (0.2) were discarded. Additionally, nuclei with ≤500 UMI, ≥5000 UMI, or ≤250 genes were discarded. Only genes found to be expressing in at least 10 nuclei were retained. The Seurat object was then normalized and transformed using the Seurat functions NormalizeData and SCTransform. The Seurat functions SelectIntegrationFeatures (nfeatures = 3000), PrepSCTIntegration, FindIntegrationAnchors, and IntegrateData were used to integrate the datasets based on the top 3000 most variable features. Prior to clustering, we performed principal component analysis using the RunPCA function and selected 40 principal components for UMAP non-linear dimensional reduction. Based on UMAP, 24 clusters were identified using the Seurat functions Find Neighbors (dim = 40) and FindClusters (resolution = 0.5). To identify marker genes for each cluster, differential expression analysis was performed using the Seurat function FindAllMarkers. Differentially expressed genes that were expressed in at least 25% of the nuclei within the cluster and with a fold change more than 0.25 (log scale) were considered marker genes. Cell identity was determined using well-established marker genes for major cortical cell types. Marker gene analysis led to the identification of 17 cortical neuron clusters (10 excitatory, 7 inhibitory), 1 subcortical neuron cluster, 5 non-neuronal clusters, and 1 unidentified cluster (Miscellaneous). Neuronal clusters were annotated according to the cortical layer they occupy, or if unidentifiable by cortical layer, according to the gene most differentially expressed in that cluster relative to all other clusters.

Differential gene expression analysis

Differential gene expression analysis between groups was performed using the Seurat function FindMarkers (min.pct = .00001, logfc.threshold = 0) with a Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test. Genes with an adjusted pp-value <.05 were considered differentially expressed.

Intercellular signaling

Cell-cell communication was assessed utilizing the R package CellChat.33

Gene ontology

PANTHER (v18.0)51,56 was used to perform an overrepresentation test against the biological process complete ontology using default parameters. All genes detected by FindMarkers were used as a background gene list. For conciseness and visualization, terms were excluded that contained less than 5 or greater than 600 total genes.

Microelectrode array

CytoView MEA 48-well plates (Axion BioSystems M768-tMEA-48W) were coated with 50 μg/mL poly-D-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich A-003-E) in borate buffer pH 8.4, incubated overnight at 37°C, washed 3X with water, and air dried overnight. Wells were then coated with 20 μg/mL laminin (Roche 11243217001) in ice-cold OptiMEM (Gibco 51985091) and incubated for 2 h at 37°C. 8×104 neurons were then seeded into each well and allowed to incubate for 2 h before supplemented neurobasal media (see “primary neuronal culture” section for recipe) was added. Cocultures were then created and maintained as outlined in the “primary neuron-astrocyte coculture” section.

Recordings were performed using an Axion Maestro Pro multiwell microelectrode array with 5% CO2 at 37°C. Recordings were taken 9 days after the addition of astrocytes (14 DIV total for the neurons). Cells acclimated to the apparatus for 30 min before recording 5 min of neuronal activity. AxIS software (Axion Biosystems) was used for the extraction of spikes and bursts. Burst activity was defined as a minimum of 5 spikes with a maximum inter-spike interval of 100ms.

Behavioral assays

Behavioral cohorts

Male and female WT and KO mice were tested in the behavioral tests described below. Mice were 18 months old at the time of behavioral testing. One cohort of mice was used for open field testing. Two cohorts of mice were used for NOR, T-maze, and fear conditioning testing. During testing, the experimenter was blinded to the genotype of the mice. All tests were performed as previously described in young H2BE WT and KO mice in Feierman, 2024.14

NOR/open field

Novel object recognition and open field testing were performed as previously described by Korb et al.60,61 In brief, mice were placed in an empty arena for 10 min for a habituation period that also served as an open field assessment. 24 h later, mice were returned to the arena while two identical objects (stacked Lego structures) were placed in the box and mice were given 10 min to explore. 24 h later, mice were returned to the arena with one object they had previously seen and one new object in place of the original object and were allowed to explore for 10 min. Trials were recorded using EthoVision84 software. Time spent interacting with each object was manually analyzed. Discrimination index was calculated as (time with novel object — time with familiar object)/(time with novel object + time with familiar object).

T-maze

T-maze testing was performed as previously described by Feierman et al.14 In brief, mice were placed at the end of the center arm of a T-shaped maze enclosed with plexiglass. Mice freely explored the maze for 7 min. Arm entries were defined as the full body (not necessarily the tail) of the mouse entering an arm. A successful triad was defined as consecutive entries into each of the 3 arms. The rate of spontaneous alternation was calculated as correct triads/(total entries - 2).

Contextual and cued fear conditioning

Fear conditioning testing was performed as previously described by Feierman et al.14 On training day, mice were placed in individual chambers for 2 min followed by a loud tone lasting 30 s that co-terminated with a 2 s, 1.25-mA foot shock. One minute later mice received another tone-shock pairing and were then left undisturbed for 1 additional minute in the chamber before being returned to their home cage. Freezing, defined as no movement except for respiration, was determined before and after the tone-shock pairings and scored by MedAssociates VideoFreeze software. To test for context-dependent learning, mice were placed back into the same testing chambers 24 h later for a total of 5 min with no tone or shock and the total time spent freezing was measured. An additional 24 h later, we tested for cued fear memory by placing the mice into a novel chamber consisting of altered flooring, wall-panel inserts, and vanilla scent. After 2 min in the chamber, the cue tone was played for a total of 3 min, and the total time spent freezing during the cue tone was measured. Long-term contextual and cued fear memory were again tested with the same protocol at 14 days (contextual) or 15 days (cued) post-training.
