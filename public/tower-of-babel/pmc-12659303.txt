Systemic inflammation index mediated the associations between dietary intake for gut microbiota and muscle mass: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yuyang Shi, Huiqiao Zhou, Qihui Fu, Xiaoyan Wang, Xuebiao Wu, Wenjian Liu, Da Gan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe associations between a diet that associated with maintaining a healthy gut microbiota and muscle mass, as well as the role of inflammation in these associations, remain unclear. This study aimed to examine the associations between the dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM) and muscle mass, and to explore whether the systemic inflammation index (SII) mediates these associations.MethodsA total of 6,908 participants, aged 20 to 59 years, from the 2010-2018 cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, were included in this cross-sectional study. The DI-GM was calculated based on the dietary intake of 13 foods or nutrients, with a higher score indicating a diet associated with a healthier gut microbiota. Muscle mass was assessed using the skeletal muscle mass index (SMI), which was calculated by dividing the appendicular lean mass by the square of height. The SII was calculated by multiplying the platelet count and neutrophil count, and then dividing by the lymphocyte count. Weighted multivariable linear regression models were used to examine the associations between DI-GM and SMI. Mediation analysis was performed to investigate the role of SII in mediating the association between DI-GM and SMI.ResultsEach 1-point increase in DI-GM was associated with a 0.03 (95% CI: 0.01 to 0.04) increase in SMI. The stratified analyses revealed that, with the exception of alcohol consumption, age, gender, race, marital status, education level, income level, smoking status, physical activity, BMI categories, and the history of hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, or cancer did not modify the association between DI-GM and SMI (P values for all interaction terms >0.05). The positive association between DI-GM and SMI were more pronounced among non-drinkers when comparing the highest quintile to the lowest quintile (β: 0.29; 95% CI: 0.14 to 0.43). SII partially mediated the associations between DI-GM and SMI, with a mediation proportion of 11.6%.ConclusionDI-GM was positively associated SMI, with SII partially mediating this association. These findings suggested that a diet associated with maintaining a healthy gut microbiota may help prevent muscle mass loss by reducing systemic inflammation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-025-01035-7.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a progressive skeletal muscle disorder characterized by the accelerated loss of both muscle mass and function [1]. It increases the risk of adverse outcomes, including falls, fractures, and mortality [2]. The prevalence of sarcopenia ranges from 8% to 36% in individuals under 60 years, and from 10% to 27% in those aged 60 and older [3]. With the global aging population, the prevalence of sarcopenia is expected to rise [4]. Given that low muscle mass is a defining characteristic of sarcopenia, identifying strategies to preserve muscle mass is crucial for preventing sarcopenia and its associated complications.

In addition to well-established factors, such as physical activity, that help prevent low muscle mass, the gut microbiota also plays a crucial role in its onset [5–7]. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, characterized by reduced gut microbiota diversity and a diminished abundance of beneficial bacteria, was associated with an elevated risk of low muscle mass [6, 7]. These findings suggest that interventions aimed at improving gut microbiota may be beneficial in preventing loss of muscle mass. Diet is a key determinant of gut microbiota composition and function [8]. The role of diet-microbiota interactions in the development of various diseases, including cardiovascular disease (CVD), colon cancer, and metabolic syndrome has been well-documented [9]. However, the potential role of a diet associated with maintaining a healthy gut microbiota in preventing the loss of muscle mass remains unclear.

The dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM) developed by Kase et al. serves as a comprehensive tool for elucidating the role of diet-microbiota interaction in maintaining muscle mass [10]. DI-GM was constructed based on a systematic review of 106 studies examining the effect of specific food group/nutrient intake on gut microbiota diversity, composition and SCFA production, reflecting the quality of the diet in promoting a healthy gut microbiota [10]. However, few studies have explored the associations between DI-GM and muscle mass.

Furthermore, the underlying mechanism through which DI-GM influences the preservation of muscle mass remains unclear. Studies have demonstrated that specific foods or nutrients could exert anti-inflammatory effects by modulating the gut microbiota, suggesting a potential link between DI-GM and inflammation [11, 12]. Increased level of inflammation was a key risk factor for low muscle mass, contributing to muscle mass loss by accelerating muscle catabolism, impairing protein synthesis, and hindering muscle regeneration [13]. Therefore, we hypothesized that DI-GM may help prevent the loss of muscle mass by mitigating systemic inflammation.

Hence, this study aimed to examine the associations between DI-GM and muscle mass using nationally representative data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Furthermore, given that systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) was a reliable indicator of body’s local immune response and systemic inflammation status [14], we explored the mediated effect of SII on the association between DI-GM and muscle mass.

Methods

Study participants

Participants in this study were selected from four 2-year cycles of the NHANES (2011-2018) [15]. NHANES is a continuous cross-sectional survey designed to evaluate the health and nutritional status of the non-institutionalized U.S. population. To investigate the association between body composition and other health conditions and risk factors, the NHANES Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) examinations collected nationally representative data on body composition for participants aged 8 to 59 [16]. Therefore, a total of 31,473 participants under the age of 60 were included in the study. Participants who were younger than 20 years old (n = 16,539), pregnant (n = 247), or with missing data on dietary intake (n = 3,619), measurement of muscle mass (n = 2,196), and covariates (n = 1,660) were excluded. Finally, participants with extreme body mass index (BMI) (>60 kg/m2 or < 15 kg/m2; n = 9) or dietary energy intake (< 800 kcal/day or >4000 kcal/day for men and < 500 kcal/day or >3500 kcal/day for women; n = 295) were also excluded, resulting a final analytic sample of 6,908 participants (Fig. 1).Fig. 1The flowchart of participants selection

Assessment of DI-GM

Dietary intake information was obtained through two 24-hour dietary recalls. Participants’ food and beverage intake from the day prior to the interview was documented using the validated 5-step Automated Multiple-Pass Method [17]. The DI-GM comprised 14 components (10 beneficial and 4 unfavorable foods or nutrients). The beneficial components included avocados, broccoli, chickpeas, coffee, cranberries, fermented dairy, fiber, green tea, soybeans, and whole grains [10]. In this study, green tea was excluded from the DI-GM calculation because consumption data was unavailable in NHANES 2010-2011. A score of 1 was assigned to each beneficial component if its consumption was at or above the sex-specific median; otherwise, a score of 0 was given [10]. The beneficial to gut microbiota score (BGMS) was the sum of all beneficial components, ranging from 0 to 9 points. The unfavorable components were consisted of processed meat, red meat, refined grains, and a high-fat diet (≥ 40% energy from fat). In contrast to the beneficial components, a score of 1 was assigned to each unfavorable component if its intake were below the sex-specific median, or if the energy derived from fat was below 40%, with the unfavorable to gut microbiota score (UGMS) ranging from 0 to 4 [10]. The DI-GM was calculated as the sum of BGMS and UGMS, with scores ranging from 0 to 13. Based on the DI-GM, participants were categorized into quintiles: Q1 (0–3), Q2 (4), Q3 (5), Q4 (6), Q5 (7–11).

Assessment of muscle mass

The DXA was used to measure lean mass in the arms and legs. Appendicular lean mass was determined by summing the lean mass in both the arms and legs. The skeletal muscle mass index (SMI) was calculated as ASM (in kilograms) divided by the square of height (in meters) [2].

Definition of systemic immune-inflammation index

Blood specimens were processed with the Beckman Coulter MAXM instrument to measure complete blood count parameters, including platelet, neutrophil, and lymphocyte counts. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) was calculated by multiplying the platelet count and neutrophil count and then dividing by the lymphocyte count [14].

Assessment of covariates

Covariate information included demographic variables (age, gender, and race), socioeconomic factors (marital status, education level, and income level), lifestyle behaviors (smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity), and self-reported medical history (hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cancer), all of which were collected through a questionnaire survey. Income level was assessed using the poverty income ratio (PIR), which was calculated by dividing total household income by the poverty guidelines threshold set by the Department of Health and Human Services [18]. The metabolic equivalents of task hours per week (MET-hour/week) expended through physical activity were assessed using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire [19]. Participants were classified as either physically active or inactive based on the median value of MET-hour/week consumed. BMI was calculated by dividing weight (in kilograms) by the square of height (in meters).

Statistical analyses

In accordance with the analytical guidelines, the participants’ characteristics across quintiles of DI-GM were presented, accounting for the complex sampling design and sampling weights of NHANES. Continuous variables were presented as weighted means (with standard errors), and a weighted one-way analysis of variance was conducted to compare differences across DI-GM quintiles. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (with weighted percentages), and a weighted Chi-square test was performed to compare differences across DI-GM quintiles.

Weighted linear regression models were used to examine the associations between DI-GM and SMI, with coefficient (β) and 95% confidence interval (CI) estimated. Several covariates were adjusted for in the multivariable model, including age (continuous), gender (men or women), BMI, race (Mexican American, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanics Black, Other Hispanic, or Other race), marital status (not married, married/living with partners, or widowed/separated/divorced), education level (below high school, high school, or college and above), PIR categories (≤ 1.3, 1.3- 3.5, or >3.5), smoking status (never smoker, former smoker, current smoker), alcohol consumption (no, yes), physical activity (≤ 28, >28 MET-hours/week), dietary energy intake, and percentage of energy from protein. Based on the multivariable model, a history of hypertension, diabetes, CVD, and cancer was further adjusted in the fully adjusted model to account for the influence of common comorbidities. Restricted cubic spline analyses with three knots were performed to investigate the potential nonlinear relationship between DI-GM and SMI.

Stratified analyses were performed to determine whether associations between DI-GM and SMI varied across participants with different demographic variables, socioeconomic factors, lifestyle behaviors, and medical history.

For the secondary analyses, causal mediation analyses were conducted to explore the mediating effect of SII on the associations between DI-GM and SMI, using the R package “Mediation” (Version 4.5.0) [20]. The direct, indirect, and total effects of the casual mediation analyses were estimated through a nonparametric bootstrapping method with 10000 resamples.

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, a series of sensitivity analyses were performed. First, participants were categorized into quartile based on the value of DI-GM to explore whether the associations between DI-GM and SMI were influenced by the grouping method. Second, the SMI was calculated by dividing ASM by BMI to determine whether the calculating method of SMI influenced the associations between DI-GM and muscle mass [21]. Third, we included data on green tea intake in the DI-GM calculation and reran the analyses using data from participants in NHANES 2013-2018. Fourth, participants with a history of hypertension, diabetes, CVD, or cancer were excluded to minimize the potential confounding from common comorbidities on the observed associations. Fifth, we employed weighted linear regression models to investigate the associations between DI-GM and SII.

The Stata/SE 15.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) and R (version 4.1.1) were used for statistical analyses. The statistical significance level was set at 0.05 (two-sided).

The sensitivity analyses revealed that categorizing participants into quartiles did not materially change the results. Participants in the highest quartile of DI-GM had a higher level of SMI than those in the lowest quartile (Supplemental Table 1). The positive associations between DI-GM and SMI remained unchanged when SMI was calculated by dividing ASM by BMI (Supplemental Table 2). Including data on green tea intake in the calculation of DI-GM yielded similar results. Estimates of the associations between DI-GM quintiles and SMI were similar when restricting the analyses to participants without hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and cancer. In estimating the associations between DI-GM and SII, each one-point increase in DI-GM was associated with a decrease of 0.08 units (95% CI: −0.14 to −0.02) in SII (Supplemental Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, higher DI-GM was associated with an increase of SMI. Except for alcohol consumption, these associations were not modified by age, gender, race, marital status, education level, PIR categories, smoking status, physical activity, BMI categories, or a history of hypertension, diabetes, CVD, or cancer. The positive association between DI-GM and SMI was more pronounced among non-drinkers. Mediation analysis indicated that SII partially mediated the positive associations between DI-GM and SMI.

The health benefits of DI-GM have been highlighted in previous studies, including reduced risk of stroke, depression, metabolic dysfunction-associated liver disease, and accelerated biological aging [22–25]. Our study found a positive association between DI-GM and muscle mass. These findings can be attributed to the characteristics of DI-GM, including improved gut microbiota diversity, elevated total SCFA levels, and modulation of specific gut bacteria—each of which was strongly associated with muscle mass [10, 26–32]. A study of 88 individuals aged over 65 years demonstrated that reduced richness and evenness in gut microbiota diversity (indicated by observed ASVs, the Shannon index, and the Chao 1 index) were associated with an increased risk of low muscle mass [26]. Additionally, evidence from both human epidemiological studies and animal studies supported the benefit of SCFAs in maintaining muscle mass. Participants with low muscle mass exhibited decreased capacity for gut microbial synthesis of the SCFAs butyrate [27]. Feeding 16-month-old female C57BL/6 mice a diet containing sodium butyrate for 10 months resulted in less hindlimb muscle mass loss compared to those fed the control diet during aging [28]. SCFAs help maintain muscle health by reducing intramuscular fat infiltration, enhancing muscle mitochondrial biogenesis, and exerting anti-inflammatory effects, among other mechanisms [29, 30]. Furthermore, the modulation of specific gut bacteria induced by DI-GM, such as an increase in Roseburia and a decrease in Clostridium perfringens, was also associated with muscle mass [10, 31, 32]. A study involving 87 elderly Chinese individuals reported that the abundance of the genus Roseburia was lower in participants with low muscle mass [31], and a study of 127 patients who underwent hepatectomy, pancreatoduodenectomy, or esophagectomy found that the fecal count of Clostridium perfringens was inversely associated with the standardized total psoas area [32]. Our findings suggested that a diet associated with maintaining a healthy gut microbiota may be recommended for preventing muscle mass loss.

In addition, the positive association between DI-GM and SMI was modified by alcohol consumption, with stronger associations observed among non-drinkers. This phenomenon may be explained by the adverse effect of alcohol on gut microbiota [33, 34]. Alcohol was associated with reduced gut microbiota alpha-diversity, lower butyric acid levels, and a higher abundance of pro-inflammatory microbiota [33, 34]. Therefore, alcohol may reduce the beneficial effects of DI-GM on gut microbiota, thereby attenuating its positive impact on muscle mass.

Our findings revealed that SII partially mediates the positive association between DI-GM and SMI, indicating that systemic inflammation may be the underlying mechanism driving these associations. Dietary foods or nutrients that prompt a healthy gut microbiota were helpful in alleviating systemic inflammation [11, 12]. For example, a 17-week randomized, prospective study found that the high-fermented food diet intervention increased the gut microbiota diversity and decreased inflammatory signals and activity, including IL-6, IL-10, and IL-12b [35]. Our studies also showed that DI-GM was inversely associated with SII, which was a comprehensive indicator of systemic inflammation [14].

Systemic inflammation is a well-established risk factor for muscle health [13]. The elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines, such as Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α) and Interleukin-6 (IL-6), accelerate muscle protein degradation by activating the NF-κB signaling pathways [36, 37]. Additionally, TNF-α and IL-6 may impair muscle protein synthesis by inhibiting the activity of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [38]. Human epidemiological studies have also shown a significant association between markers of inflammation and muscle health [39, 40]. For instance, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio was inversely associated with muscle area, and elevated levels of interleukin-6 and C-reactive protein were associated with reduced mitochondrial oxidative capacity in skeletal muscle [39, 40]. Compared to other inflammatory markers, such as the pan-immune-inflammation value and the derived neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, SII was a more robust predictor of muscle mass and strength, and was less influenced by confounders [41]. A systematic review and meta-analysis also reported higher levels of SII in adults with low muscle mass compared to those without [42]. Therefore, our study examined the mediating effect of SII on the associations between DI-GM and SMI, showing that SII accounted for 11.6% of these associations. Further studies are needed to elucidate the role of systemic inflammation in the associations between DI-GM and muscle mass.

Height squared-adjusted ALM and BMI-adjusted ALM are two widely used indicators of muscle mass [2, 21]. The prevalence of low muscle mass and its associations with adverse health outcomes varied depending on the method used to calculate the muscle mass indicator [43, 44]. For instance, a study conducted on 526 individuals aged 20 years and older found that low muscle mass, defined using BMI-adjusted ALM, was positively associated with HOMA-IR and the risk of metabolic syndrome, whereas low muscle mass defined using height squared-adjusted ALM was not [43, 44]. Therefore, we analyzed the associations between DI-GM and muscle mass using different method to calculate SMI and observed similar associations, thus supporting the robustness of our findings.

To the best of our knowledge, our study was the first to reveal that SII partially mediated the associations between DI-GM and muscle mass in middle-aged adults. However, several limitations of the study should be noted. First, as an observational study, it could not establish a causal relationship between DI-GM and muscle mass. Second, dietary intake data obtained through a questionnaire survey introduced measurement bias. Third, DI-GM was developed based on findings from previous studies, without including foods or nutrients that have not been investigated in relation to gut microbiota [10]. Although the DI-GM incorporates 14 foods or nutrients, it may not capture the effects of all dietary components on gut microbiota. As new evidence emerge, DI-GM will need to be revised to incorporate these developments. Fourth, because data on muscle mass were available only for participants aged 20 to 59 years, the generalizability of these findings is limited to middle-aged adults. The associations between DI-GM and muscle mass may vary in older populations; therefore, further studies involving participants aged 60 and older is needed to determine whether these findings apply to the elderly. Fifth, while this study explored the associations between DI-GM and muscle mass, it did not assess muscle strength or physical performance, which are also important components of sarcopenia. Future research should investigate these associations. Sixth, because measurements of gut microbiota were not conducted, this study could not identify which specific gut microbiota or aspects of microbiota diversity contributed to the findings. Seventh, as all participants in this study were from the United States, the generalizability of these findings to other regions or populations is limited. Future studies conducted in other areas or among different populations are necessary to validate these results. Finally, despite the adjustment for multiple covariates in the models, unmeasured or residual confounders could not be fully accounted for.

Conclusion

In conclusion, DI-GM was positively associated with muscle mass, with SII partially mediating this association. These findings suggested that a diet associated with maintaining a healthy gut microbiota may help prevent muscle mass loss by reducing systemic inflammation. Studies involving diverse populations are crucial to validate the findings, establish causality, and elucidate the underlying mechanisms of these associations.
