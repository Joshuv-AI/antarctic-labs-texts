Deterministic Suffix-reading Automata
R Keerthan ORCID:0009-0003-8278-0951, B Srivathsan ORCID:0000-0003-2666-0691, R Venkatesh ORCID:0009-0007-7747-4457, Sagar Verma ORCID:0009-0000-9987-4151

Abstract
. We introduce deterministic suffix-reading automata (DSA), a new automaton model over finite words. Transitions in a DSA are labeled with words. From a state, a DSA triggers an outgoing transition on seeing a word ending with the transition’s label. Therefore, rather than moving along an input word letter by letter, a DSA can jump along blocks of letters, with each block ending in a suitable suffix. This feature allows DSAs to recognize regular languages more concisely, compared to DFAs. In this work, we focus on questions around finding a “minimal” DSA for a regular language. In this context, the number of states is not a faithful measure of the size of a DSA, since the transition-labels contain strings of arbitrary length. Hence, we consider total-size (number of states + number of edges + total length of transition-labels) as the size measure of DSAs. We start by formally defining the model and providing a DSA-to-DFA conversion that allows to compare the expressiveness and succinctness of DSA with related automata models. Our main technical contribution is a method to derive DSAs from a given DFA: a DFA-to-DSA conversion. A surprising observation is that the smallest DSA derived from the canonical DFA of a regular language L L need not be a minimal DSA for L L . This observation leads to a fundamental bottleneck in deriving a minimal DSA for a regular language. In fact, we prove that given a DFA and a number k ≥ 0 k\geq 0 , the problem of deciding if there exists an equivalent DSA of total-size ≤ k \leq k is NP-complete. On the other hand, we impose a restriction on the DSA model and show that our derivation procedure can be adapted to produce a minimal automaton within this class of restricted DSAs, starting from the canonical DFA.

1.
Introduction

Deterministic Finite Automata (DFA) are fundamental to many areas in
Computer Science. Apart from being the cornerstone in the study of
regular languages, automata have been applied in several contexts:
such as text processing
[
22
]
,
model-checking
[
4
]
, software
verification
[
2
,
1
,
11
]
, and formal specification
languages
[
14
]
. The popularity of automata has also led to the development
of several automata handling libraries
[
21
]
.

A central challenge in the application of automata, in almost all of these
contexts, is the large size of the DFA involved.
Although automata are an intuitive way to describe computations using states and transitions, their descriptions are at a low level, resulting in automata of large size for non-trivial applications. Our objective in this paper is to investigate an automaton model with a more concise representation of the computation.
Non-determinism is one way that is well-known to give exponential succinctness. However, a
deterministic model is useful in formal specifications and automata
implementations. The literature offers several ways to tackle the problem
of large DFA size, while staying within the realm of determinism. In this
work, we propose a new solution to this problem.

The size of a DFA could be large due to various reasons. One of them is simply the size of the alphabet. For
instance, consider the alphabet of all ASCII characters. Having a
transition for each letter from each state blows up the size of the
automata.
Symbolic automata

[
23
,
6
]
have been proposed to handle large
alphabets. Letters on the edges are replaced by formulas, which club
together several transitions between a pair of states into one
symbolic transition. If the domain
is the set of natural numbers, a transition

q\xrightarrow{\operatorname{odd(x)}}q^{\prime}
with a predicate

\operatorname{odd(x)}
is a placeholder for all odd
numbers
[
7
]
. Symbolic automata have been implemented in many
tools and have been widely applied (see
[
7
]
for a list
of tools and applications).

In text processing, automata are used as an index for a large set
U

of strings. Given a text, the goal is to find if it contains one of
the strings from the set. A naïve DFA that recognizes the set
U
is
typically large.
Suffix automata
(different from our model of
suffix-reading
automata) are DFA that accept the set of all
suffixes of words present in
U
. These automata are used as more
succinct indices to represent
U
and also make the pattern matching
problem more efficient
[
22
]
.

Another dimension for reducing the DFA size is to consider
transitions over a block of letters.
Generalized automata
(GA) are
extensions of non-deterministic finite automata (NFAs) that can
contain strings, instead of letters, on transitions. A word
w
is
accepted if it can be broken down as
w_{1}w_{2}\dots w_{k}
such that
each segment is read by a transition. This model was defined by
Eilenberg
[
8
]
, and later
Hashiguchi
[
15
]
proved that for every
regular language
L
there is a minimal GA in which the edge labels
have length at most a polynomial function in
m
, where
m
is the size of the syntactic monoid of
L
.

Giammarresi
et al.

[
10
]

consider
Deterministic Generalized Automata
(DGA) and propose an
algorithm to generate a minimal DGA (in terms of the number of states)
in which the edges have labels of length at most the size of the minimal
DFA. The algorithm uses a method to suppress states of a DFA and create longer labels. The key observation is that minimal DGAs can be derived from
the canonical DFA by suppressing states.
The paper also throws open
a more natural question of minimality in the total-size of the
automaton, which includes the number of states, edges and the sum of
label lengths.

A natural extension from strings on transitions to more complex objects is to consider regular
expressions. The resulting automaton model is called
Expression automata

in
[
13
]
. Expression Automata were already considered
in
[
3
]
to convert automata to
regular expressions. Every DFA can be converted to a two state
expression automaton with a regular expression connecting them.
A model of
Deterministic Expression automata
(DEA) was proposed
in
[
13
]
. To get determinism, every pair of
outgoing transitions from a state is required to have disjoint regular languages, and
moreover, each expression in a transition needs to be prefix-free: for
each
w
in the language, no proper prefix of
w
is present in the
language. This restriction makes deterministic expression automata
less expressive than DFAs: it is shown that DEA languages are
prefix-free regular languages. An algorithm to convert a DFA to a DEA,
by repeated state elimination, is proposed
in
[
13
]
. The resulting DEA is minimal in the
number of states. The issue with generic Expression automata is the high expressivity of
the transition condition, that makes states almost irrelevant. On the
other hand, DEA have restrictions on the syntax that make the model less
expressive than DFAs. We consider an intermediate model.

Our model.

In this work, we introduce
Deterministic
Suffix-reading Automata (DSAs)
. We continue to work with strings
on transitions, as in DGA. However, the meaning of transitions is
different. A transition

stays in a state until one of them appears as the

Suffix-reading automata have the ability to wait at a state, reading
long words until a matching pattern is seen. This results
in an arguably more readable specification for languages which are
“pattern-intensive”. This representation is orthogonal to the
approaches considered so far. Symbolic automata club together
transitions between a pair of states, whereas DSA can do this clubbing
across several states and transitions. DGA have this facility of
clubbing across states, but they cannot ignore intermediate letters,
which results in extra states and transitions.

Overview of results.

We formally present deterministic suffix-reading automata and its
semantics, quantify its size in comparison to an equivalent DFA, and
study an algorithm to construct a DSA starting from a DFA. This is in
the same spirit as in DGAs, where smaller DGAs are obtained by suppressing
states. For automata models with strings on transitions, the number of
states is not a faithful measure of the size of a DSA. As described
in
[
10
]
for DGAs, we consider the total-size of
a DSA which includes the number of states, edges, and the sum of label
lengths. The key contributions of this paper are:

Introduction to the DSA model

We formally present deterministic suffix-reading automata (Section
3
), and compare its size to DFAs and DGAs (Section
4
).
We prove that DSAs
accept regular languages, and nothing more.
Every complete DFA can be seen as a DSA. For the converse, we prove
that for every DSA of size
k
, there is a DFA with size at most

2k\cdot(1+2|\Sigma|)
, where
\Sigma
is the alphabet
(Lemma
4
,
Theorem
4
).
This answers the question of how small DSAs can be in comparison to
DFAs for a certain language : if
n
is the size of the minimal DFA
for a language
L
, minimal DSAs for
L
cannot be smaller than

\frac{n}{2\cdot(1+2|\Sigma|)}
.
When the alphabet is large, one could expect smaller sized DSAs. We
describe a family of languages
L_{n}
, with alphabet size
n
, for
which the size of the minimal DFA is quadratic in
n
, whereas size of
the corresponding DSA is a linear function of
n
(Lemma
4
).

Complexity of minimization

We focus on the minimization problem for DSAs in this paper. Minimization for DFAs can be done efficiently in polynomial-time, thanks to the Myhill-Nerode characterization. For DGAs,
[
10
]
provides a polynomial-time method to find DGAs with smallest number of states, starting from the canonical DFA. For DSAs we prove the following problem to be
\mathsf{NP}
-complete in Section
8
: given a DFA and a number
k
, does there exist a language equivalent DSA with total size at most
k
? This result exhibits an inherent difficulty in minimizing DSAs.

A method to derive small DSAs from a given DFA

Similar in spirit to the work on DGAs, we would like to be able to get small DSAs starting from a given DFA. Our main technical constribution is a procedure to derive language equivalent DSAs from a given complete DFA. Of course, the given complete DFA itself can be considered as a DSA. However, our goal is to generate DSAs with as small a total size as possible.

•

The DFA-to-DSA
derivation method is presented in Section
5
. In a nutshell,
the derivation procedure selects subsets of DFA-states, and adds
transitions labeled with (some of) the acyclic paths between
them. The technical challenge lies in identifying sufficient conditions on
the selected subset of states, so that the derivation procedure
preserves the language (Theorem
5.1
).

•

We remark that minimal DSAs need not be unique, and make a
surprising observation: the smallest DSA that we derive from the
canonical DFA of a language
L
need not be a minimal DSA. We find this
surprising because (1) firstly, our derivation procedure is
surjective: every DSA (satisfying some natural assumptions) can be
derived from some corresponding DFA (Proposition
6
), and in particular, a minimal
DSA can be derived from some DFA; (2) the observation suggests that
one may need to start with a bigger DFA in order to derive a minimal
DSA – so, starting with a bigger DFA may result in a smaller DSA
(Section
6
).

•

Inspired by the above observation, we present a restriction to the definition of DSA, called
strong DSA
(Section
7
). We show that minimal strong DSAs can in fact be derived from the canonical DFA, through our derivation procedure. This provides more insights into our derivation procedure and in some sense explains better the capabilities of our procedure.

This introductory work on DSAs opens several threads for future research. We discuss a few of them in the Conclusion (Section
9
). A shorter version of this work appeared in the conference proceedings
[
19
]
. In the current version, Section
7
is completely new and does not appear in the conference proceedings.

Related work.

The closest to our work
is
[
10
]
which introduces DGAs, and
gives a procedure to derive DGAs from DFAs. The focus however is on
getting DGAs with as few states as possible. The observations presented in
Section

of our work, also apply for
state-minimality: the same example shows that in order to get a DSA with fewer
states, one may have to start with a bigger DFA. This is in sharp
contrast to the DGA setting, where the derivation procedure of

]
yields a minimal DGA (in the

deriving DGAs with minimal total-size was left open
in
[

, and continues to remain so, to
the best of our knowledge.
Expression automata
[
13
]
allow regular
expressions as transition labels. This model was already considered
in
[
3
]
to convert automata to
regular expressions. Every DFA can be converted to a two state
expression automaton with a regular expression connecting them. A
model of deterministic Expression automata (DEA) was proposed
in
[
13
]
with restrictions that limit the
expressive power. An algorithm to convert a DFA to a DEA, by repeated
state elimination, is proposed in
[

. The
resulting DEA is minimal in the number of states.
Minimization of NFAs was studied
in
[

]
and shown to be

.

Here are some works that are related to the spirit of finding more readable specifications.
[
9
]
has used the model of deterministic GA to
develop a learning algorithm for some simple forms of regular

applications in learning DTD specifications for XML code. In this

specify regular
languages. They also claim that the translation algorithms from DFAs

Another work that talks about readability of specifications
is
[

. It considers automata based

Suffix-tracking sets – obtaining DSA from DFA

In this section, we are interested in the following task: given a DFA, extract a language equivalent DSA with as small a total size as possible. For instance, given the DFA on the right of Figure

For DGAs, a

smaller automata (in total

More importantly, when can we suppress states? DGAs cannot “ignore”
parts of the word. This in particular leads to the requirement that a
state with a self-loop cannot be suppressed. DSAs have a more
sophisticated transition semantics. Therefore, the procedure to
suppress states is not as simple. This is the subject of this
section. We deviate from the DGA setting in two ways: we will select a subset of good states from which we can
construct a DSA (essentially, this means the rest of the states are
suppressed); secondly, our starting point will be complete DFA, on
which we make the choice of states (in DGAs, one could start
with any DGA and suppress states). Our procedure can be broken down
into two steps: (1) Start from a complete DFA, select a subset of states and
build an induced DSA by connecting states using
acyclic paths between them; (2) Remove some redundant
transitions.

Section

This section is inspired by a question that was left open in the earlier version of this work

: when does the smallest DSA derived from the canonical DFA correspond to a minimal DSA? This provides new insights about the derivation procedure. Moreover, as we will see, many DSAs discussed in this paper are already strong. We also provide a real-life-inspired example of a strong DSA. residual language
of the state. Similarly, for a DSA
\mathcal{A}
and a state
q
of
\mathcal{A}
, we can define the residual language
L^{\mathcal{A}}(q)
.
We say that two states
p,q
of a DFA/DSA are
equivalent
if their residual languages are equal. In the setting of DFAs, we know that in the canonical (minimal-state) automaton, no two states are equivalent. The next lemma establishes this property even in the DSA setting.

Lemma
\thethm
.

No two states of a minimal DSA can be equivalent.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a minimal DSA. Suppose

L^{\mathcal{A}}(p)=L^{\mathcal{A}}(q),p\neq q
. We will
now construct a DSA
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
with smaller size
than
\mathcal{A}
. This will be a contradiction. To get
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, we will
re-orient all transitions going to
q
to now point towards
p
:
remove each
transition
(r,\alpha,q)
and add the transition
(r,\alpha,p)
; then
remove
q
and other unreachable states after this
transformation. Since
L^{\mathcal{A}}(p)=L^{\mathcal{A}}(q)
, this construction
preserves the language. For all the states
r
that remain in
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
we
still have the same
\operatorname{Out}(r)
as in
\mathcal{A}
. Finally we need to argue that
|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|<|\mathcal{A}|
. This is easy to see since
q
has been removed from
\mathcal{A}
,
and there are no new additions to
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
.
∎

Now we come to properties specific to sDSAs. The key idea is to consider the tracking DFA (Definition
4
and Figure
5
) and investigate equivalence between states in this tracking DFA.

Lemma
\thethm
.

For any minimal sDSA
\mathcal{A}
, its tracking DFA
M_{\mathcal{A}}
cannot have a ‘DSA state’ equivalent to any other state, i.e. if
(p,\alpha),(q,\beta)
are states of
M_{\mathcal{A}}
and also equivalent, then we have both
\alpha\neq\varepsilon
and
\beta\neq\varepsilon
.

Proof.

From Lemma
7.2
, we know that no two states of any minimal DSA can be equivalent. So it is not possible to have any distinct
(p,\varepsilon),(q,\varepsilon)\in M_{\mathcal{A}}
to be equivalent. Suppose
L^{M_{\mathcal{A}}}(p,\varepsilon)=L^{M_{\mathcal{A}}}(q,\beta)
for some
\beta\neq\varepsilon
(and
q
could be
p
as well). We will construct a smaller sDSA
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
.

Consider state
q
of
\mathcal{A}
. Due to the presence of state
(q,\beta)
in
M_{\mathcal{A}}
, there exists some outgoing label of the form
\beta\alpha
from
q
in
\mathcal{A}
: that is,
\beta\alpha\in\operatorname{Out}(q)
for some
\alpha
with
|\alpha|\geq 1
. Let the corresponding transition be
q\xrightarrow{\beta\alpha}r
. To get
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
: remove this transition
q\xrightarrow{\beta\alpha}r
and add the transition
q\xrightarrow{\beta}p
. Clearly
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is smaller than
\mathcal{A}
, since total size is reduced by
|\alpha|
. Language accepted is the same.
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is also an sDSA since the only change is replacing
\beta\alpha
with
\beta
in
\operatorname{Out}(q)
: any string in
\overline{\operatorname{Out}}(q)
that is a factor of
\beta
would also be a factor of
\beta\alpha
, and any prefix of
\beta
is also a prefix of
\beta\alpha
. This contradicts the assumption that
\mathcal{A}
was a minimal sDSA, thus proving the lemma.
∎

We remark that the above proof does not work for general DSAs. Consider the transition
q\xrightarrow{\beta\alpha}r
that was removed, and modified to
q\xrightarrow{\beta}p
. There could be another transition
q\xrightarrow{\beta\alpha^{\prime}}r^{\prime}
in the DSA — this violates the strong DSA property since
\beta\alpha
and
\beta\alpha^{\prime}
are outgoing labels, and the prefix
\beta
of the former appears as a suffix of the latter. On seeing the word
\beta\alpha^{\prime}
from
q
, the original DSA moves to
r^{\prime}
. However, in the new DSA, as soon as
\alpha
is seen the state changes to
p
. This can modify the language. Such a situation does not happen in an sDSA due to the restriction on the outgoing labels. We now prove the main result of this section.

Theorem
\thethm
.

Every minimal sDSA for a language
L
can be derived from the canonical DFA for
L
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(Q^{\mathcal{A}},\Sigma,\Delta^{\mathcal{A}},F^{\mathcal{A}})
be an arbitrary minimal sDSA.
Any minimal sDSA is well-formed and has no redundant transitions. Hence
\mathcal{A}
can be derived from its tracking DFA
M_{\mathcal{A}}
, thanks to Proposition
6
. If the tracking DFA
M_{\mathcal{A}}
is canonical, we are done. Otherwise, there are distinct states of
M_{\mathcal{A}}
that are equivalent. From Lemma
7.2
, we know that a state
(q,\varepsilon)
of
M_{\mathcal{A}}
cannot be equivalent to any other state. Therefore, two equivalent states are of the form
(p,\alpha)
and
(q,\beta)
, with
\alpha
and
\beta
both non-empty. We will merge such equivalent states together to build the minimal automaton and make use of this specific structure to show that
\mathcal{A}
can be derived from the minimized DFA. For the proof, we will show the following steps.

(1)

Suppose a state
(q,\alpha)
is equivalent to
(q,\beta)
(with the same
q
), then
\alpha\not\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{pr}}\beta
(i.e. the two states cannot be ‘tracking’ the same
\mathcal{A}
-transition out of
q
).

(2)

Build a DFA
M^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}}
by quotienting equivalent states of
M_{\mathcal{A}}
, with
[q]
representing the equivalence class of
q
. The resulting automaton
M^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}}
is the canonical DFA. We use (1) to show that every simple path from a state
(q,\varepsilon)
to
(q,\beta)
is preserved in the quotiented automaton
M^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}}
, from
[(q,\varepsilon)]
to
[(q,\beta)]
.

(3)

From (2), we show that the set of states
S^{\prime}:=\{[(q,\varepsilon)]\mid q\in Q^{\mathcal{A}}\}
forms a suffix-tracking set. The DSA derived using
S^{\prime}
turns out to be
\mathcal{A}
, proving that
\mathcal{A}
can be derived from the canonical DFA.

Step 1.

If two states
(q,\alpha),(q,\beta)\in M_{\mathcal{A}}
are equivalent, then
\alpha\not\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{pr}}\beta
(i.e. the two states are not ‘tracking’ the same
\mathcal{A}
-transition). In other words, two states in the tracking DFA that lie on the same path corresponding to a given DSA transition, cannot be equivalent. Suppose
\alpha\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{pr}}\beta
. We know that
\beta\in\overline{\operatorname{Out}}(q)
, so there is a string
\gamma
such that
\beta\gamma\in\operatorname{Out}(q)
i.e. reading
\gamma
from
(q,\beta)
leads to a ‘DSA state’, say
(q^{\prime},\varepsilon)
. Let
s
be the state reached on reading
\gamma
from
(q,\alpha)
. Since
(q,\alpha)
and
(q,\beta)
are equivalent, the state
s
will be equivalent to
(q^{\prime},\varepsilon)
. From Lemma
7.2
, this means
s=(q^{\prime},\varepsilon)
. This gives transition labels
\alpha\gamma
(induced by the simple path just discussed) and
\beta\gamma
from

in
\mathcal{A}
, with
\alpha\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{pr}}\beta
, contradicting the fact that
\mathcal{A}
was an sDSA. Hence we must have
\alpha\not\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{pr}}\beta
.

Step 2.

For two states
s,s^{\prime}
of
M_{\mathcal{A}}
, define
s\equiv_{M_{\mathcal{A}}}s^{\prime}
if
L^{M_{\mathcal{A}}}(s)=L^{M_{\mathcal{A}}}(s^{\prime})
. We denote the equivalence class of

by

. From Lemma
7.2
,
[(q,\varepsilon)]=\{(q,\varepsilon)\}
. Consider a quotient DFA

based on this equivalence. States are the equivalence classes of
\equiv_{M_{\mathcal{A}}},\{[s]\mid s\in M_{\mathcal{A}}\}
.
The initial state is
[(q^{\mathcal{A}}_{in},\varepsilon)]
and final states are
\{[(q,\varepsilon)]\mid q\in F^{\mathcal{A}}\}
. Transitions are given by
\{[s]\xrightarrow{a}[s^{\prime}]\mid s\xrightarrow{a}s^{\prime}\in M_{\mathcal{A}}\}
. This is deterministic because whenever we have
[s_{1}]=[s^{\prime}_{1}]
and
s_{1}\xrightarrow{a}s_{2},s^{\prime}_{1}\xrightarrow{a}s^{\prime}_{2}\in M_{\mathcal{A}}
, we also have
[s_{2}]=[s^{\prime}_{2}]
.
Note that
M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
is minimal, by definition.

Consider a transition

of the sDSA
\mathcal{A}
, with
\alpha=a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{n}
. This transitions gives states
(q,\varepsilon)
,
(q,a_{1})

(q,a_{1}a_{2})

(q,a_{1}\dots a_{n-1})

. equivalent. Hence

is a simple path from

to

that does not visit any state of the form

in between.

Step 3.

Let
S^{\prime}:=\bigcup_{q\in\mathcal{A}}\{[(q,\varepsilon)]\}
. We will show that

is suffix-tracking. Consider a simple path

from

[(q,\beta)]

(q,\varepsilon)
to

in

).
In the tracking DFA

, every transition moves to a state tracking the longest possible suffix:
(q,\beta)\xrightarrow{a}(q,\beta^{\prime})
in

means
\beta^{\prime}
is the longest word in
\overline{\operatorname{Out}}(q)
s.t
\beta^{\prime}\sigma a
. From (2),
\beta^{\prime}
is a simple path from
[(q,\varepsilon)]
to
[(q,\beta^{\prime})]
, in

. Moreover, we have
[(q,\beta)]\xrightarrow{a}[(q,\beta^{\prime})]
by definition.

We claim

\beta^{\prime}

[(q,\varepsilon)]
to
[(q,\beta^{\prime})]

. This is a contradiction. Hence

is the longest simple-path suffix of

in

, making

The set
S^{\prime}
is also well-formed since
\mathcal{A}
is well-formed. Suppose it was not. That means there exist states

, and words

\alpha\beta^{\prime}

are paths corresponding to

-transitions, this means

is not an sDSA, which is a contradiction. Hence we have

to be suffix-tracking. In the induced DSA

, every simple path from
[(q,\varepsilon)]
to
[(q^{\prime},\varepsilon)]
will be present. Hence every transition

in
\mathcal{A}
is present in

. Any transition out of

Thus

can be derived from

∎

8.
Complexity of minimization

The goal of this section is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem
\thethm

Given a DFA

and positive integer

, deciding whether there

\leq k

If

Suppressing a state.

9.
Conclusion

We have introduced the model of deterministic suffix-reading automata,
compared its size with DFAs and DGAs, proposed a method to derive DSAs
from DFAs, and presented the complexity of minimization.
The work on DGAs
[
10
]
inspired us to look for methods to derive DSAs from
DFAs, and investigate whether they lead to minimal DSAs for a
language. This led to our technique of suffix-tracking sets, which
derives DSAs from DFAs. The technique imposes some natural conditions
on subsets of states, for them to be tracking patterns at each
state.
However, surprisingly, the smallest DSA that we can derive from the
canonical DFA need not correspond to the minimal DSA of a
language. We have shown that when restricting the syntax, our derivation method is able to generate a minimal DSA in the restricted class, starting from the canonical DFA.

In this introductory work on DSAs, our goal has been to present the model, its motivations and establish ingredients for a deeper study of the model, both from a practical and a theoretical perspective. Recently, we have enhanced the DSA syntax to include a parallel composition operator
\parallel
