Evaluating the appropriateness of prescribing antibiotics among persons with HIV (PWH) in an Urban HIV outpatient clinic in Uganda
Authors: Brian Otaalo, Grace Banturaki, Joseph Musaazi, Derek Ngabirano, Herman Mwanja, Reuben Kiggundu, Eva Agnes Laker Odongpiny, Mackline Hope, Francis Kakooza, Andrew Kambugu, Noela C Owarwo, Barbara Castelnuovo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSub-Saharan Africa (SSA) faces a high burden of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Inappropriate antibiotic use is the major driver of AMR. Populations at high risk for infections, such as persons living with HIV (PWH), are more likely to experience AMR. However, data on the appropriateness of antibiotic prescribing in outpatient settings for PWH are limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective review of antibiotic prescriptions from 1st January 2016 to 31st December 2022 in an HIV tertiary care outpatient clinic in Uganda. We extracted data from patients’ records and assessed appropriateness using a customized tool. Data were abstracted across three points of care: the general clinic, the urgent care unit, and the TB clinic for presumptive TB patients. Prescriptions were considered appropriate if they had a documented indication, the right drug, dose, frequency, and duration, when evaluated against national guidelines and the national formulary in some instances. Appropriateness was measured in proportions, and differences in patient characteristics were analyzed. Factors associated with inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions were assessed using a modified Poisson regression model.ResultsWe reviewed the medical records of 1367 participants, with a median age of 49 years (IQR: 42–57), and the majority, 944 (69.1%), were female. In total, the participants contributed 1823 antibiotic prescriptions, of which 1605 (88%) were inappropriate. Of the 1605 inappropriate prescriptions, 842 (52.5%) were due to absence of an indication, 539 (33.5%) to nonadherence to the guidelines, and 224 (14%) didn’t have a suspected or confirmed infection. The urgent care unit had the highest proportion of inappropriate antibiotic use (82.4%), p = 0.02. Fluoroquinolones 14/124 (11.3%), macrolides 22/189 (11.6%), and cephalosporins 56/479 (11.7%) were the least appropriately prescribed classes of antibiotics. The male participants had a 17% lower prevalence of inappropriate prescriptions compared to females [adjusted PR 0.83 (95% CI: 0.75–0.92)].ConclusionWe found a high level of inappropriate antibiotic use among PWH in outpatient care. Background

There is a high burden of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [1] in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), which if uncontrolled could potentially contribute to over 1.4 million deaths by 2050 [2]. In Uganda, widespread resistance to commonly used antibiotics has already been reported [3]. Antimicrobial resistance is primarily driven by antibiotic misuse and overuse [4]. Inappropriate antibiotic use could result from multiple factors such as nonadherence to treatment guidelines [5], clinician perceptions of patient expectations, and prescription pressure [6], which are usually exacerbated by limited diagnostic testing capabilities [7] and antibiotic shortages [8] in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) is therefore a significant strategy to enhance the effective use of antimicrobials [4].

Persons with human immunodeficiency virus (PWH) are at a higher risk of AMR [9] due to multiple factors. They are more vulnerable to opportunistic and bacterial infections [10]. In addition, due to regular scheduled visits and frequent clinician encounters, PWH have a higher likelihood of getting antibiotic prescriptions and are at a higher risk of getting hospital-acquired infections [9]. Some studies from SSA have shown higher antibiotic usage in PWH as compared to people without HIV [11, 12].

The World Health Organization (WHO) has prioritized research about antibiotic use and appropriateness to include key populations such as PWH [13]. However, there are scarce data on the appropriateness of antibiotic prescriptions among PWH. A study done in Eswatini among 100 PWH showed about 20% of the antibiotic prescriptions were inappropriate due to an incorrect diagnosis, while 10% were inappropriate due to either an incorrect dose or duration" [14].

We conducted a study to assess the appropriateness of antibiotic prescriptions in a large urban outpatient HIV care and treatment clinic in Kampala, Uganda.

Methods

We retrospectively reviewed medical records of antibiotic prescriptions among PWH who attended the HIV clinic between 1st January 2016 and 31st December 2022. Participants were from the Infectious Diseases Institute (IDI), an urban tertiary outpatient HIV Clinic in Kampala, Uganda, with over 8000 registered patients.

In addition to Antiretroviral therapy (ART), the clinic’s pharmacy dispenses a range of medications, including antibiotics for the treatment of opportunistic and other infections, and drugs for non-communicable diseases, all provided at no cost to patients. The study's protocol has been published [15].

Study population and sampling

We included antibiotic prescriptions of PWH aged 18 years and older who received at least one antibiotic during the studied period. We included PWH who were attended to at three different points of care: 1) the General Clinic, where patients with minimal or no symptoms are reviewed 2) the TB clinic where Patients screened for tuberculosis (TB) are reviewed, and 3) Urgent Care Unit (UCU) where very sick patients or those who need priority care reviewed.

We used systematic random sampling to select every 10th patient from approximately 8000 individuals receiving care from the general clinic during the study period. Sampling was stratified by the WHO HIV stage (Stage I-II, and Stage III-IV), and duration on ART (< 2 years, 2–10 years, and ≥ 10 years), with proportion-to-size allocation across strata. Similarly, every 10th patient from approximately 2500 individuals screened for TB in the TB clinic was selected. All eligible patients reviewed in the UCU were included. For participants with multiple visits involving antibiotic prescriptions during the study period, one prescription date was randomly selected. We excluded prescriptions with missing clinical notes on the selected prescription dates, prescriptions of intravenous antibiotics, and prescriptions for diagnoses not in the Uganda Clinical Guidelines (UCG) [16] except for those with “Respiratory Tract Infection,” or “Upper Respiratory Tract Infections” for which we additionally used the Practical Guidelines for Dispensing at Higher Level Health Centres 2015 (PGDHC). This was because these infections are not broadly categorized in the UCG 2016. Antibiotics administered intramuscularly for conditions such as sexually transmitted infections were included. Cotrimoxazole prescriptions given for prophylaxis were also excluded from this review.

Procedures

Data source

We utilized patient medical data, which are routinely recorded electronically and in paper-based charts for every clinic visit at IDI. The clinic has a custom-made electronic medical records (EMR) system called the Integrated Clinic Enterprise Application (ICEA), which captures high-quality data routinely [17]. These data include: patients’ demographic information, point of care through which a patient received treatment, WHO HIV clinical stage, ART regimen, duration of ART, laboratory investigations, and prescription details. The pharmacy module in ICEA captures detailed prescription information. For each prescribed medication, it records the drug name, dosage, frequency of administration, duration of treatment, and the identity of the prescriber. However, details about patient history, physical examination, other investigations, diagnoses, follow-up clinical plans, admission, and referral letters are recorded on every clinic visit in their medical charts only and not in ICEA.

Data collection

Two study doctors abstracted participant data using both the ICEA database and charts as complementary data sources. This was done using a structured customized abstraction tool [18], which was digitized into a web-based electronic system—REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture). To reduce errors, the data extraction tool in REDCap was designed with internal quality control measures such as skip and validation logic, where dependent fields were hidden until certain responses were selected. Both study doctors separately entered each participant’s records into REDCap and independently judged the appropriateness of each antibiotic prescription. Any discrepancies between the two were resolved by reaching a consensus after review and discussion with a third reviewer (a physician).

Definition of appropriateness

An antibiotic prescription was considered appropriate only if it met all the following criteria: a written diagnosis/indication, the right class, dose, frequency, duration, and considered drug-drug interactions. If any of these criteria were not met, the prescription was deemed inappropriate. For each diagnosis, we assessed if the antibiotic prescription was consistent with the UCG [16] recommendation for class, dose, frequency, and duration, except for respiratory tract infections, where the PGDHC [19] was additionally used as stated before. We used the University of Liverpool’s drug interaction checker to assess for drug-drug interactions [20]. Patients who were already prescribed an antibiotic for the same illness were assessed for appropriateness based on the recommended second line. The antibiotic class instead of the specific drug was used to determine appropriateness, provided it was in the same pharmacological class as the guideline-recommended antibiotic and had recognized activity against the suspected bacterial infection. This approach reflects real-world prescribing, where the exact drug may not always be available. Where antibiotics were prescribed in combination, we assessed the appropriateness of the individual drugs as well as the combination.

Data analysis

The data were summarized using descriptive statistics for the three sub-groups (routine care patients, presumptive TB patients, and patients who needed urgent care). We summarized patient characteristics at the point at which the antibiotic was prescribed, except for CD4 cell count, which was at enrollment into the clinic. The appropriateness of antibiotic prescriptions was estimated as an overall proportion of correct prescriptions out of the total antibiotic prescriptions, and thereafter stratified by point of care. Comparison by the different participants’ characteristics was performed using the Pearson Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and using the Kruskal–Wallis. We explored factors associated with inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions using a modified Poisson regression model, adjusting for sex, age, baseline CD4 cell count, viral load, cadre of prescriber, year of prescription, and point of care.

Results

We reviewed the medical records of 1367 participants; the median age was 49 years (IQR: 42–57), and the majority, 944/1367 (69.1%), were female. Only half of the participants, 685/1367 (50.1%), had a written indication for their antibiotic prescription. Overall inappropriateness of antibiotic prescriptions

From the 1367 patients sampled, we identified a total of 1823 antibiotic prescriptions. Overall, 1605/1823 (88.0%) of the antibiotic prescriptions were inappropriate. Of these, 842 (52.5%) were inappropriate due to the absence of an indication, 539 (33.5%) to nonadherence to the guidelines, and 224 (14%) were prescriptions without a confirmed or suspected infection. Only 425 of the 981 (43.3%) prescriptions with an indication had the right class of antibiotics, and about half of these, 217/425 (51.1%) also had appropriate doses, frequency, and duration. Fluoroquinolones 14/124 (11.3%), macrolides 22/189 (11.6%), and cephalosporins 56/479 (11.7%) were the least appropriately prescribed classes of antibiotics, Table 2. Most of the antibiotics, 1085 (59.5%) were from the Access category of the WHO AWaRe classification [21], 681 (37.4) from the Watch category, and 57 (3.1) were unclassified (Supplementary Table 1).

Inappropriateness of antibiotics prescriptions in only those with a documented indication

Of the 981 prescriptions with an indication, 763 (77.8%) were inappropriate. The main reasons for inappropriate prescriptions were wrong class 330 (43.3%), and having neither confirmed nor suspected bacterial infection, 224 (29.4%).

The urgent care unit had the highest proportion of inappropriate prescriptions (82.4%) compared to the General clinic (77.5%) and presumptive TB patients (67.4%), p = 0.020. In addition, it also had the largest percentage of prescriptions with an inappropriate class, 149/204 (73.0%), p = 0.001. Presumptive TB patients had the highest proportion of prescriptions without a suspected or confirmed bacterial infection, 50/91 (54.9%), p < 0.001. The appropriateness of antibiotic prescriptions and the reasons for inappropriateness by point of care are summarized in Fig. 1. Of the 232 antibiotic prescriptions given as a combination of two or more antibiotics, 187 (80.6%) weren’t justified. The most common reason for inappropriate antibiotic combinations was the use of more than one antibiotic when they were not needed, accounting for 151 (80.7%) cases, while 36 (19.3%) involved incorrect drug choices within combinations that were clinically indicated.

In the multivariate analysis, male patients had a 17% lower prevalence of inappropriate prescriptions compared to females [adjusted PR 0.83 (95% CI: 0.75–0.92)]. Patients being screened for TB had a 20% lower prevalence of inappropriate prescriptions compared to those reviewed in the general clinic [adjusted PR 0.80 (95% CI: 0.69–0.94)] (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

About 9 in 10 antibiotic prescriptions were inappropriately prescribed, and half of the prescriptions lacked a corresponding written indication. We also found that the majority of the inappropriately used drugs were from the Watch category, most antibiotics given in combination were not justified, and that patients who needed urgent or priority care had the highest percentages of inappropriate antibiotic use.

The high inappropriate antibiotic use observed in our study among only PWH is higher than that reported in similar studies conducted among heterogeneous populations (both with and without HIV) [8, 11, 22]. Although this was largely due to the many prescriptions without indications, it could also be due to limitations in diagnostics, patient expectations [23], the use of other guidelines as references, and a lack of training on the national guidelines. Our findings may also be a signal of unique prescription behaviors among PWH, such as higher empirical antibiotic prescriptions [12]. We observed a modest reduction in inappropriate prescriptions from 88 to 78% when only prescriptions with documented indications were considered, suggesting that the lack of documentation played a limited role in driving inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions. This also highlights the challenges inadequate documentation poses to clinical audits and the implementation of antimicrobial stewardship programs, and therefore the need to improve documentation of clinical notes and diagnoses.

Although compared to other points of care, the urgent care unit had the highest proportion of prescriptions with documented indications, it also had the greatest proportion of inappropriate antibiotic use. This finding likely reflects the complexity of antimicrobial stewardship in emergency settings as reported in other studies, given clinical decisions have to be made quickly and antibiotics are usually given empirically [24]. Additionally, IDI as a tertiary-level facility, often manages antibiotic-experienced patients with advanced HIV disease whose complex care needs may not be exhaustively addressed by national guidelines, and clinical judgement is often used to weigh between patient survival and antimicrobial resistance.

Among the inappropriately prescribed antibiotic combinations, the majority (81%) were due to the use of combinations where a single antibiotic would have been sufficient, likely reflecting efforts to broaden antibiotic coverage due to diagnostic uncertainty, as seen in other studies [25, 26], or the documentation of only one indication when two or more were likely present. The remaining 19% had justified indications for combination therapy (mostly for syndromic management), but involved drug substitutions that were not recommended by national guidelines, possibly influenced by drug availability. This inappropriate use of combinations not only contributes to antimicrobial resistance but also increases the risks for adverse effects and raises healthcare costs.

Our analysis also revealed that 59.5% of the prescribed antibiotics were from the Access class of the AWaRe classification, which approximates to the WHO recommendation of 60% [21]. However, the most inappropriately prescribed classes were fluoroquinolones, macrolides, and cephalosporins, which all except cephalexin belong to the Watch category, which requires stricter stewardship and oversight. This finding underscores the risk that inappropriate prescriptions pose to antimicrobial resistance, given that Watch category antibiotics generally have a higher potential for resistance [27].

Our study has several strengths: The use of a comprehensive tool to assess prescription appropriateness and a large sample size of PWH improved the precision and reliability of our findings, and were major strengths of our study. In addition, the inclusion of multiple points of care gave a comprehensive overview of outpatient antibiotic prescribing practices. The use of two independent reviewers with a third to resolve discrepancies also enhanced the reliability of our classifications and minimized the potential for reviewer bias. Lastly, the study was conducted at a tertiary facility with diverse cadres of health workers and more comprehensive patient records, which together enhanced the quality and reliability of the data collected.

However, our study had some limitations. First, prescriptions lacking documented indications were deemed inappropriate by default, potentially overestimating the magnitude of inappropriate prescribing. This is supported by the observed reduction in the proportion of inappropriate prescriptions when a sub-analysis of only those with indications was done. Second, because the study was retrospective, it was not possible to assess the clinical context of each prescription, for example previous prescriptions from a different clinic, due to inadequate documentation. Third, the exclusion of intravenous medications and cases with missing notes may have introduced selection bias.

Conclusions

We found a high proportion of inappropriately prescribed antibiotics among PWH, driven by both poor documentation and nonadherence to the national treatment guidelines. This calls for targeted stewardship interventions to promote rational prescribing and more antimicrobial stewardship research among PWH. In our clinic, we are piloting an intervention which comprises of continuous training, a prescription checklist, and involvement of pharmacists in reviewing the prescriptions.
