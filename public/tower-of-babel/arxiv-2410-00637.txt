High-order numerical integration on self-affine sets
Patrick Joly, Maryna Kachanovska, Zoïs Moitier

Abstract
We construct an interpolatory high-order cubature rule to compute integrals of smooth functions over self-affine sets with respect to an invariant measure. The main difficulty is the computation of the cubature weights, which we characterize algebraically, by exploiting a self-similarity property of the integral. We propose an h h -version and a p p -version of the cubature, present an error analysis and conduct numerical experiments.

We will denote by

Definition 2.1
.

An
Iterated Function System
(IFS) on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a finite family of contractive maps
\mathscr{S}=\{S_{\ell}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\>{:}\>\mathopen{}\ell=1,2,\ldots,L\}

Namely, for
\ell\in\mathbb{L}\coloneqq\{1,2,\ldots,L\}

0\leq\rho_{\ell}<1

S_{\ell}
satisfy the estimation
\lvert S_{\ell}(x)-S_{\ell}(y)\rvert_{2}\leq\rho_{\ell}\,\lvert x-y\rvert_{2}

The fixed point of a contractive mapping

Definition 2.2

Let

denote the set of all nonempty compact sets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

For an IFS
\mathscr{S}=\{S_{\ell}\>{:}\>\mathopen{}\ell\in\mathbb{L}\}

\OpSet\colon\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{K}\quad\text{such that}\quad\OpSet(E)\coloneqq S_{1}\lparen E\rparen\cup\cdots\cup S_{L}\lparen E\rparen.

The above definition is justified by the following result from
[
Fal14
, Thm. 9.1]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

For an IFS
\mathscr{S}=\{S_{\ell}\>{:}\>\mathopen{}\ell\in\mathbb{L}\}

, which is a fixed point of

\Gamma=\OpSet\lparen\Gamma\rparen
. This set is called an

.

An important class of IFS are the

S_{\ell}
are affine:

S_{\ell}\colon x\longmapsto A_{\ell}x+b_{\ell}\quad\text{and}\quad c_{\ell}=\left\lparen I-A_{\ell}\right\rparen^{-1}b_{\ell}.

(2.1)

Then the contraction

A classic subclass of affine IFS are the

where the maps
S_{\ell}
are contractive similarities, meaning that the maps
S_{\ell}

\lvert S_{\ell}(x)-S_{\ell}(y)\rvert_{2}=\rho_{\ell}\,\lvert x-y\rvert_{2}

which is equivalent to saying that

A_{\ell}=\rho_{\ell}\,T_{\ell},\qquad\text{where}\ T_{\ell}\ \text{is an orthogonal matrix}.

(2.2)

In what follows, we will refer to an attractor of an IFS in

.
For the particular case when the IFS is affine (resp. similar), the corresponding attractor will be referred to as a

From the above definition, we have immediately the following result, which will be of importance later.

Lemma 2.4

The pre-image of
\Gamma
by the maps

,

Since

S_{\ell}^{-1}\left\lparen\Gamma\right\rparen=S_{\ell}^{-1}\left\lparen\OpSet(\Gamma)\right\rparen=S_{\ell}^{-1}\left\lparen S_{1}(\Gamma)\right\rparen\cup\cdots\cup S_{\ell}^{-1}\left\lparen S_{L-1}(\Gamma)\right\rparen\supset S_{\ell}^{-1}\left\lparen S_{\ell}(\Gamma)\right\rparen.

The desired result follows from

.

2.1.2
Invariant measure

The exposition on invariant measure of an IFS is taken from
[
Hut81
]
.
Given a compact subset
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, equipped with the usual Euclidean topology, one can define an
outer measure
on
K
as a map
\nu\colon 2^{K}\to[0,\>+\infty]
such that
\nu(\varnothing)=0
(the
null empty set
condition) and, for any
A,B_{0},B_{1},\ldots\subset K
,
A\subset\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}B_{i}
implies
\nu\lparen A\rparen\leq\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\nu\lparen B_{i}\rparen
(the
countably subadditive
condition).
We say that a set
A\subset K
is
\nu
-
measurable
,
if
\nu(E)=\nu(E\cap A)+\nu(E\setminus A)
for all
E\subset K
(the Carathéodory’s criterion).
The family of measurable sets forms a
\sigma
-algebra.
When restricted to
\nu
-measurable sets, the outer measure
\nu
produces a measure.
In the sequel, for a reason that will appear in
Theorem

2.8
, we shall restrict ourselves to
Borel regular
measures: an outer measure
\nu
is
Borel regular
if, and only if, all Borel sets are measurable, and, for all
A\subset K
there exists a Borel set
B
,
s.t.

A\subset B
and
\nu(A)=\nu(B)
.
The support of the measure
\nu
is defined by
\Supp\nu\coloneqq K\setminus\bigcup\{V\ \text{open}\>|\>\mathopen{}\nu(V)=0\}
.
Let us further introduce

\mathcal{M}_{K}^{1}\coloneqq\left\{\nu\>\middle|\>\mathopen{}\nu\text{ is Borel regular and}\ \nu(K)=1\right\}.

Given
g\colon K\to K
, measurable, the push-forward measure
\nu\circ g^{-1}\in\mathcal{M}_{K}^{1}
is defined by

\displaystyle\forall A\subset K,\quad\nu\circ g^{-1}\lparen A\rparen\coloneqq\nu\lparen g^{-1}(A)\rparen,\text{ with }g^{-1}(A)\coloneqq\{x\in K\>|\>\mathopen{}g(x)\in A\}.

(2.4)

Let us now construct an invariant measure associated to an affine IFS
\mathscr{S}
given by the maps
\{S_{\ell}\>{:}\>\mathopen{}\ell\in\mathbb{L}\}
.
First, let
K
satisfy the assumption of
Lemma

2.6
(
i.e.

\OpSet\lparen K\rparen\subset K
).

Given
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{L-1})\in\lparen 0,\>1\rparen^{L}
such that
\lvert\bm{\mu}\rvert_{1}\coloneqq\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}}\mu_{\ell}=1
, let us define the map
\OpMsr\colon\mathcal{M}_{K}^{1}\to\mathcal{M}_{K}^{1}
via the following identity
1
1

1

The interested reader can verify that
\OpMsr
maps
\mathcal{M}_{K}^{1}

\lvert\bm{\mu}\rvert_{1}=1
.
:

\OpMsr\colon\nu\longmapsto\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}}\,\mu_{\ell}\ \nu\circ S_{\ell}^{-1}.

(2.5)

A measure satisfying
\nu=\OpMsr\nu
is called

with respect to
\lparen\mathscr{S},\bm{\mu}\rparen
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
\mathscr{S}=\{S_{\ell}\>{:}\>\mathopen{}\ell\in\mathbb{L}\}
and
\bm{\mu}

be as in

Then there exists a unique invariant measure

Furthermore, the support of the measure

Proof.

This proof is due to

We equip

Remark 2.9

(Hausdorff measure and application to the wave scattering by fractal screens)

Given an IFS, one can construct infinitely many invariant measures, parametrized by

Nonetheless, in some cases, this measure coincides with the (normalized)

Assume that the IFS

Moreover, let the IFS be a similar IFS,

In this case one can show that the Hausdorff dimension

A non-exhaustive list of self-similar sets satisfying the OSC includes Sierpinski gasket, Koch snowflake and Cantor sets.
Let us remark that one of favorable properties of the Hausdorff measure compared

This can be used for efficient computation of singular integrals arising in the Hausdorff BEM,

2.1.3
Integrals with respect to invariant measures and a Ruelle operator

Let us fix the sets

As discussed in the introduction, the goal of this work is to approximate numerically

This will be done by using a certain self-similarity property of the integral, which is introduced in the present section.
The proof of this property is in turn based on the following change-of-variables formula, see

\int_{\Gamma}f(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lparen\mu\circ g^{-1}\rparen=\int_{g^{-1}(\Gamma)}f\circ g(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,

(2.6)

In the above,

is measurable, and the measurable function

is such that

; the push-forward measure

Motivation

By analogy with the Hutchinson operator
\OpSet

2.2

, one can introduce a so-called Ruelle operator

, by:

\OpFct\colon f\in\mathscr{C}\lparen\mathbb{R}^{n}\rparen\longmapsto\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}}\mu_{\ell}\;f\circ S_{\ell}\in\mathscr{C}\lparen\mathbb{R}^{n}\rparen.

(2.7)

The reader will note that, with respect to the operator

.

Given an IFS

with

2.7
.

Proof.

Given

, we compute, by definition of
\OpMsr
in (

) and

,

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\Gamma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\left\lparen\OpMsr\mu\right\rparen=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}}\mu_{\ell}\int_{\Gamma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\left\lparen\mu\circ S_{\ell}^{-1}\right\rparen.

(2.8)

From the change of variable formula

where the latter identity follows from

Ruelle operator on spaces of polynomials for affine IFS

We shall be particularly interested in the action of
\OpFct
on polynomials.
From now on, we will limit the discussion to
affine
IFS.
We denote by
\mathbb{P}
the space of polynomials of
n
variables and, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, by
\mathbb{P}_{k}
the subspace of polynomials of total degree less or equal to
k
.
If case of ambiguity, we add the number of variables
n
as a superscript to
\mathbb{P}^{n}
and
\mathbb{P}_{k}^{n}
.
The space of polynomials can be decomposed into a direct sum of homogeneous polynomials of degree
k
:
for any multi-index
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we define the monomial
x^{\bm{\alpha}}\coloneqq x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\in\mathbb{P}
of total degree

and

\mathbb{H}_{k}\coloneqq\vect\big\{x^{\bm{\alpha}}\>\big|\>\mathopen{}\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert_{1}=k\big\},\mbox{ so that }\mathbb{P}_{k}=\bigoplus_{k^{\prime}\leq k}\,\mathbb{H}_{k^{\prime}}\mbox{ and }\mathbb{P}=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\,\mathbb{H}_{k}

(2.9)

Seen as a subspace of
\mathscr{C}\lparen\mathbb{R}^{n}\rparen
,
\mathbb{P}
is kept invariant by
\OpFct
and the action of

does not increase the total degree, in other words, for all

, we have
\OpFct p\in\mathbb{P}_{k}
.
This means that

has an upper triangular structure with respect to the decomposition (

) of

: there exists a

\OpFct_{k,k^{\prime}}\in\Hom\lparen{\mathbb{H}_{k}},\mathbb{H}_{k^{\prime}}\rparen
, with

\OpFct p=\sum_{k^{\prime}\leq k}\OpFct_{k,k^{\prime}}p,\quad\text{for all }p\in\mathbb{H}_{k}.

(2.10)

In

3

restricted to the space of polynomials
\mathbb{P}
, which we denote by
\OpFct_{\mathbb{P}}
.
Let us denote by

the set of eigenvalues of

\displaystyle\sigma\lparen\OpFct_{\mathbb{P}}\rparen=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sigma\big\lparen\OpFct_{k,k}\big\rparen.

(2.11)

For

\OpFct_{0,0}=\Id,\quad(\text{the identity operator in $\mathbb{P}_{0}$}),

(2.12)

Lemma 2.12

For any

Proof.

With

.
From this observation, we infer the following identity, valid for all
p\in\mathbb{H}_{k}
:

\OpFct_{k,k}p(x)=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}}\mu_{\ell}\,p\left\lparen A_{\ell}x\right\rparen.

Let
B=\overline{B}(0,1)
be the unit closed ball of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
p\in\mathbb{H}_{k}
be an eigenvector of
\OpFct_{k,k}
associated to
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.
Of course
\lVert p\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(B)}\neq 0
and there exists
x_{*}\in B
such that
\lvert p\lparen x_{*}\rparen\rvert=\lVert p\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(B)}
.
However,
\OpFct_{k,k}p=\lambda\,p
implies
\OpFct_{k,k}p\lparen x_{*}\rparen=\lambda\,p\lparen x_{*}\rparen
, thus

\left\lvert\lambda\,p\left\lparen x_{*}\right\rparen\right\rvert\leq\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}}\mu_{\ell}\,\left\lvert p\left\lparen A_{\ell}x_{*}\right\rparen\right\rvert

and since each matrix
A_{\ell}
is
\rho_{\ell}
-contractive,
A_{\ell}x_{*}\in\rho_{\ell}B
, thus, by homogeneity,

\left\lvert p\left\lparen A_{\ell}x_{*}\right\rparen\right\rvert\leq\max_{x\in\rho_{\ell}B}\left\lvert p(x)\right\rvert=\max_{y\in B}\left\lvert p\left\lparen\rho_{\ell}y\right\rparen\right\rvert=\rho_{\ell}^{k}\,\max_{y\in B}\left\lvert p\left\lparen y\right\rparen\right\rvert=\rho_{\ell}^{k}\,\left\lvert p\left\lparen x_{*}\right\rparen\right\rvert.

We then conclude the proof since, as
|\bm{\mu}|_{1}=1
,

\displaystyle\lvert\lambda\rvert\leq\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}}\mu_{\ell}\rho_{\ell}^{k}\leq{{\rho}}_{\max}^{k}\left\lparen\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}}\mu_{\ell}\right\rparen={{\rho}}_{\max}^{k}
.

∎

Remark 2.13
.

In dimension
n=1
, since all the spaces
\mathbb{H}_{k}
are one-dimensional, the operators
\OpFct_{k,k}
are reduced to the multiplication operators.
The eigenvalues
\sigma\lparen\OpFct_{\mathbb{P}}\rparen
of the operator
\OpFct_{\mathbb{P}}
are known explicitly; they are real and positive.
Indeed, from the proof of
Lemma

2.12
, one easily sees that

\sigma\lparen\OpFct_{\mathbb{P}}\rparen=\Big\{\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}}\mu_{\ell}\,\rho_{\ell}^{k},k\in\mathbb{N}\Big\}.

The above reasoning can be extended to a higher dimensional case, when all matrices
A_{\ell}
are diagonal,
A_{\ell}=\diag\left\lparen a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,n}\right\rparen
with
\left\lvert a_{\ell,i}\right\rvert\leq\rho_{\ell}<1
.
In this case, it is readily seen that
\lparen S_{\ell}\lparen x\rparen\rparen^{\bm{\alpha}}=a_{\ell}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,x^{\bm{\alpha}}+R_{\bm{\alpha}}
, where
a_{\ell}^{\bm{\alpha}}=a_{\ell,1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots a_{\ell,n}^{\alpha_{n}}

and
R_{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{P}_{k-1}
. Thus the operator
\OpFct_{k,k}
is diagonal in the basis
\{x^{\bm{\alpha}},\text{ s.t. }\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert_{1}=k\}
of
\mathbb{H}_{k}
and
we have

\sigma\left\lparen\OpFct_{\mathbb{P}}\right\rparen=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\Big\{\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}}\mu_{\ell}\,a_{\ell}^{\bm{\alpha}},\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert_{1}=k\Big\}.

2.2
Interpolatory cubature on self-affine sets

2.2.1
Definition and associated operators

Let us assume that
\Gamma\subset K
, where
K
is defined as in
Lemma

2.6
.
As discussed in the introduction, the goal of this article is to construct a cubature for an approximation of the integral of the function
f\in\mathscr{C}(K)
over
\Gamma
with the invariant measure
\mu
.
One of the commonly used types of cubature formulae provides such an approximation of the form

\int_{\Gamma}f(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\simeq\Quad[f]\coloneqq\sum_{1\leq i\leq M}w_{i}\,f\left\lparen x_{i}\right\rparen,

(2.13)

where
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\{x_{i}\>{:}\>\mathopen{}1\leq i\leq M\}\subset K
is the set of
cubature points
and the vector
\bm{w}=\lparen w_{1},\ldots,w_{M}\rparen\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
is the vector of
cubature weights
.

Remark 2.14
.

As the cubature formula depends of on both
\mathcal{X}
and
\bm{w}
, a better notation would be
\Quad_{\mathcal{X},\bm{w}}
.
However, we have abandoned this option in order to avoid heavy notation, the role of
\mathcal{X}
and
\bm{w}
being implicit.

Let us remark that common cubature formulae (
e.g.
designed for
\Gamma
being an interval and
\mu
being the Lebesgue’s measure) rely on a set of points
\mathcal{X}\subset\Gamma
.
For self-affine sets, the condition
\mathcal{X}\subset\Gamma
can be difficult to ensure, and therefore, we do not provide any constraints on the location of the points
\mathcal{X}
, but the ones described below.
For constructing
\Quad
, we shall follow the classic paradigm for constructing a cubature formula (interpolatory cubature), where one requires that “
\simeq
” becomes “
=
” in
Eq.

2.13
, when
f
belongs to a finite dimensional space
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{P}
of polynomials.
The idea behind being that any continuous function can be approximated uniformly by a sequence of polynomials.
In the rest of the article, we assume that
\mathbb{P}_{0}\subset\mathcal{P}
.

Remark 2.15

(Choice of the space
\mathcal{P}
)
.

The usual practical choice for the space is
\mathcal{P}=\mathbb{P}_{k}
or
\mathcal{P}=\mathbb{Q}_{k}
, both choices being particularly well-suited for the error analysis.
Recall that the space
\mathbb{Q}_{k}
is defined as
\Span\{x^{\bm{\alpha}}\>|\>\mathopen{}\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert_{\infty}\leq k\}
with
\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\alpha_{i}
.
However, all theoretical arguments extend to a general space
\mathcal{P}
.

Given
\mathcal{P}
, the usual procedure is first to consider a set
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\{x_{i}\>{:}\>\mathopen{}1\leq i\leq M\}
, where
M=\dim\mathcal{P}
, of cubature points which is
\mathcal{P}
-unisolvent.

Definition 2.16
.

The evaluation operator associates to any continuous functions the set of its values on
\mathcal{X}
:

\Eval\colon\mathscr{C}\left\lparen\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\rparen\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M},\ f\longmapsto\bm{f}\coloneqq\left\lparen f\left\lparen x_{1}\right\rparen,\ldots,f\left\lparen x_{M}\right\rparen\right\rparen.

Then, by definition the set of points
\mathcal{X}
is
\mathcal{P}
-unisolvent if, and only if, the map
\Eval
is injective from
\mathcal{P}
into
\mathbb{R}^{M}
.

If
\mathcal{X}
is
\mathcal{P}
-unisolvent,
\Eval_{\mathcal{P}}\coloneqq\Eval\arrowvert_{\mathcal{P}}
is thus bijective from
\mathcal{P}
to
\mathbb{R}^{M}
.
The
\mathcal{P}
-unisolvency in
\cal X
is a necessary and sufficient condition for constructing the set of Lagrange polynomials
\Lpoly_{i}
in
\mathcal{P}
, associated to the set
\mathcal{X}
, defined by
\Lpoly_{i}\left\lparen x_{j}\right\rparen=\delta_{i,j}
, which is equivalent to
\Lpoly_{i}=\Eval^{-1}_{\mathcal{P}}\left\lparen\bm{e}_{i}\right\rparen
,
where
\{\bm{e}_{i}\}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{M}
.
The set
\lparen\Lpoly_{i}\rparen_{i=1}^{M}
is a basis of
\mathcal{P}
and

\forall p\in\mathcal{P},\qquad p(x)=\sum_{1\leq i\leq M}p\left\lparen x_{i}\right\rparen\;\Lpoly_{i}(x).

(2.14)

Remark 2.17
.

Even though this is not explicit in the notation, the Lagrange polynomials
\Lpoly_{j}
do depend on
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{X}
.

Remark 2.18
.

The inverse of the operator
\Eval_{\mathcal{P}}
is nothing but the
interpolation
operator that associates to a vector
\bm{a}=\lparen a_{1},\ldots,a_{M}\rparen
the unique polynomial of
\mathcal{P}
that takes the value
a_{i}
at point
x_{i}
, namely

\Interp_{\mathcal{P}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathcal{P}\quad\text{ such that }\quad\Interp_{\mathcal{P}}\lparen\bm{a}\rparen=\sum_{1\leq i\leq M}a_{i}\,\Lpoly_{i}.

(2.15)

We shall use later the adjoint operator
\mathcal{I}^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}:\,(\mathcal{P};\|.\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Gamma;\mu)})\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
(where
\mathbb{R}^{M}
is equipped with the euclidean inner product). The reader will easily verify that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}f=\bm{f},\quad f_{i}=\int_{\Gamma}f\,\Lpoly_{i}\,d\mu,\quad i=1,\ldots,M.

(2.16)

Note that the cubature formula
Eq.

2.13
can be rewritten in algebraic form as

\forall f\in\mathscr{C}(K),\quad\Quad[f]=\bm{w}\cdot\Eval(f)

(2.17)

where
x\cdot y
the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{M}
.
Given
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{X}
a
\mathcal{P}
-unisolvent set, we would like to find
\bm{w}
in such a way that we integrate exactly all the functions in
\mathcal{P}
.
The (trivial) answer is provided by the following lemma.

Lemma 2.19
.

Given
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{P}
and a
\mathcal{P}
-unisolvent set
\mathcal{X}
, there exists a unique choice of cubature weights
\{w_{i}\>{:}\>\mathopen{}1\leq i\leq M\}
such that
\int_{\Gamma}p(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\Quad[p]
, for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
.
These cubature weights are given by

w_{i}=\int_{\Gamma}\Lpoly_{i}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,\qquad\forall 1\leq i\leq M.

(2.18)

Lemma

2.19
is essentially of theoretical interest since exploiting it would require to compute the integrals in
Eq.

2.18
.
This justifies an alternative approach to define cubature weights that we develop in the next sections.

Before proceeding into the definition of the cubature weights, we will need to introduce the notion of
\mathscr{S}
-invariance of a polynomial space.
We shall see, in
Section

3
, that cubature weights based on such polynomial spaces can be computed in a purely algebraic manner.
On the other hand, as we shall see in
Section

4
, for polynomial spaces not satisfying this condition, the natural extension of the algebraic method will provide a set of practically useful cubature weights.

2.2.2
Definition of
\mathscr{S}
-invariant spaces

Definition 2.20
.

A finite-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{P}
of
\mathbb{P}
is said to be
\mathscr{S}
-invariant if and only if
\mathbb{P}_{0}\subset\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}
is stable by the operator
\OpFct
.

Remark 2.21
.

The reader will easily verify that for any
\mathscr{S}
and any
k
, the space
\mathbb{P}_{k}
is
\mathscr{S}
-invariant.
If, for all
\ell
, the matrix
A_{\ell}
has in each row and column only one non-zero element, the space
\mathbb{Q}_{k}
is
\mathscr{S}
-invariant.
However, in general, the space
\mathbb{Q}_{k}
is not
\mathscr{S}
-invariant, as shown in the next example.

Example 2.22
.

Let
n=2
,
L=2
, and the matrices
A_{k}
in
Eq.

2.1
are defined as follows:
A_{0}=\rho\Id
and
A_{1}=\rho\,T
, where

T=\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Tx=\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\left\lparen x_{1}+x_{2},x_{1}-x_{2}\right\rparen^{\intercal}.

We observe that
p(x)=x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k}\in\mathbb{Q}_{k}
but
p\lparen Tx\rparen=\frac{1}{2}\lparen x_{1}+x_{2}\rparen^{k}\lparen x_{1}-x_{2}\rparen^{k}\notin\mathbb{Q}_{k}
.
The space
\OpFct\lparen\mathbb{Q}_{k}\rparen
is included into
\mathbb{P}_{nk}
.
This inclusion can be strict.
Indeed, in the above case, for
k=1
, we have that, the polynomials
p_{i,j}(x)=x_{1}^{i}x_{2}^{j}
,
i,j\in\{0,1\}
, satisfy
p_{i,j}\circ T\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
if
ij=0
, and
p_{1,1}\circ T=\frac{1}{2}(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})
.
The above computation implies that
\OpFct\lparen\mathbb{Q}_{k}\rparen=\vect\{1,x_{1},x_{2},x_{1}x_{2},x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}\}
, which is a strict subspace of
\mathbb{P}_{2}
.

3
Cubature based on
\mathscr{S}
-invariant polynomial spaces

The key assumption of this section, unless stated otherwise, is that thehe space
\mathcal{P}
is
\mathscr{S}
-invariant..

3.1
Algebraic definition of the cubature weights

Recall that our goal is to construct a cubature rule
Eq.

2.13
, which would be exact for
p\in\mathcal{P}
.
As shown in
Lemma

2.19
, this condition is ensured by the unique choice
w_{i}=\int_{\Gamma}\Lpoly_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
, for
1\leq i\leq M
.
On the other hand, by
Proposition

2.11
, we can relate the integrals of the Lagrange polynomials to the integrals of their images by the Ruelle operator:

w_{i}=\int_{\Gamma}\Lpoly_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\Gamma}\OpFct[\Lpoly_{i}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.

(3.1)

As
\mathcal{P}
is
\mathscr{S}
-invariant,
\OpFct[\Lpoly_{i}]\in\mathcal{P}
, and is thus integrated exactly by the cubature rule
\Quad
.
In other words, the cubature weights satisfy the following identity:

w_{i}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq M}w_{j}\OpFct[\Lpoly_{i}]\left\lparen x_{j}\right\rparen=\sum_{1\leq j\leq M}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}}\mu_{\ell}\,\Lpoly_{i}\circ S_{\ell}\left\lparen x_{j}\right\rparen\,w_{j}.

In order to write the above in the algebraic form, let us define the
M\times M
matrix

\bm{S}_{i,j}\coloneqq\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}}\mu_{\ell}\,\Lpoly_{j}\circ S_{\ell}\left\lparen x_{i}\right\rparen,\qquad\text{for}\ 1\leq i,j\leq M.

(3.2)

With this definition, the above said is summarized in the following lemma, which provides
an algebraic property
of the cubature weights.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
\mathcal{P}
is
\mathscr{S}
-invariant and that
\mathcal{X}
is
\mathcal{P}
-unisolvent, then the vector
\bm{w}
given by
Eq.

2.18
is an eigenvector of
\bm{S}^{\intercal}
, associated to the eigenvalue
1
:

\bm{S}^{\intercal}\bm{w}=\bm{w}.

(3.3)

Obviously,
Eq.

3.3
not sufficient to characterize
\bm{w}\in\Ker\lparen I-\bm{S}^{\intercal}\rparen
.
We must complete it by requiring that the cubature formula is exact for the constant function equal to
1
, namely, using the algebraic version
Eq.

2.17
of
\Quad
,

\bm{w}\cdot\bm{1}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq M}w_{i}=1,\qquad\text{where}\ \bm{1}\coloneqq\Eval(x\mapsto 1).

(3.4)

Thus, the problem to be solved, to compute the vector
\bm{w}
of cubature weights writes

\text{Find}\ \bm{w}\in\Ker\lparen I-\bm{S}^{\intercal}\rparen\ \text{satisfying \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:normal}}.

(3.5)

By
Lemma

3.1
, we know that this problem admits at least one solution
\bm{w}
given by
Eq.

2.18
.
However, a priori it is unclear that this solution would be unique.
The answer to this question is directly linked to the spectral structure of
\bm{S}^{\intercal}
and more precisely to the multiplicity of the eigenvalue
1
.
Because the spectra of the matrices
\bm{S}^{\intercal}
and
\bm{S}
(together with multiplicities) coincide, we will work with the matrix
\bm{S}
, which appears to have a convenient structure for the analysis.

3.2
Properties of the matrix
\bm{S}^{\intercal}
and well-posedness of
Eq.

3.5

The matrix
Eq.

3.2
is strongly linked to the Ruelle operator
\OpFct
, which we will use to characterize its spectrum.
The first part of the following lemma will be used immediately, while the second part will be of use later.

Lemma 3.2
.

For all
p\in\mathcal{P},
where the space
\mathcal{P}
is not necessarily
\mathscr{S}
-invariant, it holds that
\Eval\left\lparen\OpFct p\right\rparen=\bm{S}\left\lparen\Eval p\right\rparen
.
We also have
\Eval_{\mathcal{P}}\Interp_{\mathcal{P}}\Eval\left\lparen\OpFct p\right\rparen=\bm{S}\left\lparen\Eval p\right\rparen
.

Proof.

From the definition of
\OpFct
, see
Eq.

2.7
and the definition of
\bm{S}
, see
Eq.

3.2
, the columns of
\bm{S}
are given by
\bm{S}_{\cdot,j}=\mathcal{E}\lparen\OpFct\Lpoly_{j}\rparen
, for
1\leq j\leq M
.
Next, with the Lagrange decomposition
Eq.

2.14
of
p
and by linearity of
\mathcal{E}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\OpFct p)=\sum_{1\leq j\leq M}p\left\lparen x_{j}\right\rparen\,\mathcal{E}\left\lparen\OpFct\Lpoly_{j}\right\rparen=\sum_{1\leq j\leq M}\bm{S}_{\cdot,j}\,p\left\lparen x_{j}\right\rparen.

The first statement is obtained using the definition of
\mathcal{E}
.
The second statement follows by recalling that
\Eval_{\mathcal{P}}\Interp_{\mathcal{P}}=\operatorname{Id}_{\mathbb{R}^{M}}
and applying
\Eval_{\mathcal{P}}\Interp_{\mathcal{P}}
to the first identity.
∎

The
\mathscr{S}
-invariance of
\mathcal{P}
implies in particular that
\OpFct_{\mathcal{P}}=\OpFct\arrowvert_{\mathcal{P}}\in\Hom\lparen\mathcal{P}\rparen
.
Using the fact that
{\Eval}_{\mathcal{P}}
is invertible (recall that its inverse is the interpolation operator
\Interp_{\mathcal{P}}
, see
Remark

2.18
),
Lemma

3.2
implies that

\OpFct_{\mathcal{P}}=\Eval_{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}\bm{S}\Eval_{\mathcal{P}},

(3.6)

in other words that the spectra of
\OpFct_{\mathcal{P}}
and
\bm{S}
coincide.

Lemma 3.3
.

The eigenvalue
1
is a simple eigenvalue of
\bm{S}
(or, equivalently,
{\OpFct}_{\mathcal{P}}
according to
Eq.

3.6
) and all other eigenvalues are strictly less than
1
in modulus.

Moreover, the corresponding eigenspace is given by
\operatorname{span}\{\bm{1}\}
.

Proof.

Given the
\mathscr{S}
-invariant space
\mathcal{P}
, let the smallest
k
be such that
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{P}_{k}
.
Let
W
be a complement of
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{P}_{k}
.
Let
\OpFct_{k}\in\Hom\lparen\mathbb{P}_{k}\rparen
be the restriction of
\OpFct
to
\mathbb{P}_{k}
, the
\mathscr{S}
-invariance property means that, with respect to the decomposition
\mathbb{P}_{k}=\mathcal{P}\oplus W
, the operator
\OpFct_{k}
has a triangular block structure

\OpFct_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\OpFct_{\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{P}}&\OpFct_{W\to\mathcal{P}}\\
0&\OpFct_{W\to W}\end{pmatrix}.

As a consequence, we have the inclusion
\sigma\left\lparen\OpFct_{\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{P}}\right\rparen\subset\sigma\left\lparen\OpFct_{k}\right\rparen
. On the other hand, remark that
\sigma\left\lparen\OpFct_{k}\right\rparen=\bigcup_{0\leq m\leq k}\sigma\left\lparen\OpFct_{m,m}\right\rparen
, cf. (
2.10
). The announced result about the eigenvalues is easily deduced from
Lemma

2.12
and (
2.12
).

It remains to show that the corresponding eigenspace is given by
\operatorname{span}\{\bm{1}\}
. This is easily seen from (
3.6
): for
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, it holds that
\bm{S}\bm{v}=\bm{v}
if and only if
\OpFct_{\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}\bm{v}=\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}\bm{v}
. On the other hand,
\OpFct_{\mathcal{P}}1=1
, and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{P}}1=\bm{1}
, and thus
\bm{v}
satisfying
\bm{S}\bm{v}=\bm{v}
necessarily belongs to
\operatorname{span}\{\bm{1}\}
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The proof of
Lemma

3.3
provides a more precise characterization of the spectrum

As a consequence of
Lemma

3.3
, we have the following result.

Theorem 3.5
.

The problem
Eq.

3.5
is well-posed and characterizes the vector
\bm{w}
of cubature weights.

The proof of this result relies partially on a standard linear algebra argument, which we repeat for the convenience of the reader and which will be used later in the paper.

Lemma 3.6
.

Assume that
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
, and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be a simple eigenvalue of
\bm{A}
(i.e. its algebraic, and thus, geometric multiplicity equals to
1
). Then the corresponding left and right eigenvectors
\bm{v}_{l}
and
\bm{v}_{r}
are not orthogonal. I.e. if
\bm{v}_{l},\,\bm{v}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
,
\bm{v}_{l}\neq 0
,
\bm{v}_{r}\neq 0
, are s.t.
\bm{v}_{l}^{\top}\bm{A}=\lambda\bm{v}_{l}^{\top}
and
\bm{A}\bm{v}_{r}=\lambda\bm{v}_{r}
, then, necessarily,
\bm{v}_{l}\cdot\bm{v}_{r}\neq 0
.

Proof.

We reason by contradiction. Assume that
\bm{v}_{l}\cdot\bm{v}_{r}=0
. Next, recall the decomposition of the space
\mathbb{R}^{M}=\operatorname{Ker}(\lambda-\bm{A})\oplus_{\perp}\operatorname{Im}(\lambda-\bm{A}^{\top})
. Since
\bm{v}_{r}\in\operatorname{Ker}(\lambda-\bm{A})
, and
\bm{v}_{l}\cdot\bm{v}_{r}=0
, then, necessarily,
\bm{v}_{l}\in\operatorname{Im}(\lambda-\bm{A}^{\top})
. Therefore, there exists
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, s.t.
(\lambda-\bm{A}^{\top})\bm{y}=\bm{v}_{l}
. Remark that
\bm{y}\notin\operatorname{span}\{\bm{v}_{l}\}
, and is thus a generalized eigenvector. This contradicts the fact that
\lambda
is a simple eigenvalue of
\bm{A}^{\top}
(equivalently, of
\bm{A}
).
∎

Proof of
Theorem

3.5
.

Because the eigenvalues of
\bm{S}
and
\bm{S}^{\top}
coincide (together with multiplicities), by
Lemma

3.3
we conclude that there exists
\bm{v}\neq 0
, s.t.
\bm{S}^{\top}\bm{v}=\bm{v}
, and the corresponding eigenvalue is simple. Using
Lemma

3.3
, we also have that
\bm{S}\bm{1}=\bm{1}
. Applying
Lemma

3.6
with
\bm{A}=\bm{S}
,
\lambda=1
shows that, necessarily,
\bm{v}\cdot\bm{1}\neq 0
.

It is easy to verify that
\widetilde{\bm{w}}=(\bm{v}\cdot\bm{1})^{-1}\bm{v}
is a unique solution to the problem (
3.5
), which is thus well-posed. Since the exact cubature weights satisfy (
3.5
), necessarily,
\widetilde{\bm{w}}
coincides with the vector
\bm{w}
.
∎

In what follows, we will need a rewriting of
Theorem

3.5
, which will serve us in a sequel (see
Theorem

4.3
).

Corollary 3.7
.

Consider the following problem:

\displaystyle\text{Find $\omega\in\mathcal{P}$, s.t. }\int_{\Gamma}\omega\,p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\Gamma}\omega\OpFct p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\Gamma}\omega\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=1.

(3.7)

This problem is well-posed, and the unique solution is given by
\omega=1
.

Proof.

The result follows from the equivalence of the problem (
3.7
) and the problem (
3.5
), in the following sense:

(A)

if
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
solves (
3.5
), then
\omega=\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}\right)^{-1}\bm{w}
satisfies (
3.7
);

(B)

if
\omega\in\mathcal{P}
satisfies (
3.7
), then
\bm{w}=\mathcal{I}^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}\omega
solves (
3.7
),

where we recall that
\cal{I}^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}
is given by (
2.16
). To prove (A) and (B), we remark that

\displaystyle\text{for all }f,\,p\in\mathcal{P},\qquad\int_{\Gamma}f\,p\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\sum\limits_{1\leq i\leq M}p\left\lparen x_{i}\right\rparen\int_{\Gamma}f\,\Lpoly_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}f\cdot\,\mathcal{E}p.

As a consequence, using the
\mathscr{S}
-invariance of the space
\mathcal{P}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\omega p\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\Gamma}\omega\OpFct p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\iff\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\omega\cdot\,\mathcal{E}p=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\omega\cdot\,\mathcal{E}\OpFct p.

By
Lemma

3.2
, we rewrite the above as

\int_{\Gamma}\omega p\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\Gamma}\omega\OpFct p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\iff\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\omega\cdot\,\mathcal{E}p=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\omega\cdot\,\bm{S}(\mathcal{E}p).

(3.8)

Since
\mathcal{E}
is surjective from
{\mathcal{P}}
to
\mathbb{R}^{M}
, (
3.8
) leads to the following equivalence:

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\omega p\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\Gamma}\omega\OpFct p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}\iff\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\omega\cdot\,\bm{p}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\,\omega\cdot\,\bm{S}\bm{p},\quad\forall\;\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}.

(3.9)

In a similar manner,

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\omega\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=1\iff\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\,\omega\cdot\,\mathcal{E}1=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\,\omega\cdot\,\bm{1}=1.

(3.10)

This proves (A) and (B).
Finally, we recognize in the right-hand side of equivalences (
3.9
), (
3.10
) the problem (
3.5
); the desired conclusion follows from
Theorem

3.5
.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

Remarkably, given two different sets of cubature points
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
each of them being
\mathcal{P}
-unisolvent, the spectrum of the corresponding matrices
\bm{S}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\bm{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}
(which differ a priori) is the same.
Indeed, these matrices are representations of the same operator
\OpFct_{\mathcal{P}}
,
cf.

Eq.

3.6
.

4
Cubature based on non-
\mathscr{S}
-invariant polynomial spaces

One motivation for looking at the case of non-
\mathscr{S}
-invariant polynomial spaces stems from a practical difficulty of choosing cubature points that would be unisolvent in
\mathbb{P}_{k}
, and possess Lebesgue’s constants of mild growth, with respect to the number of points (this property is favorable for convergence of quadratures formulae, as we will see in
Section

5.2
).
Therefore, instead, we will look at the tensor product spaces
\mathbb{Q}_{k}
, which has an additional favorable property: for well-chosen unisolvent sets of points, the associated Lagrange polynomials are easy to compute using barycentric formulae,
cf.

[
Tre13
]
.
It appears that the spaces
\mathbb{Q}_{k}
in general are not
\mathscr{S}
-invariant, as discussed in
Section

2.2.2
. Therefore, a priori, the cubature weights on the space
\mathbb{Q}_{k}
no longer satisfy the algebraic property
Eq.

3.5
.
This is illustrated numerically in
Section

6
.

4.1
An alternative definition of the cubature weights

Let
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{P}
with
\dim\mathcal{P}<+\infty
and
\mathbb{P}_{0}\subset\mathcal{P}
.
According to
Lemma

2.19
, the cubature weights
\bm{w}
given by
Eq.

2.18
provide a cubature formula
\Quad[f]
which is exact in
\mathcal{P}
.
In the previous section, under the assumption that
\mathcal{P}
is
\mathscr{S}
-invariant, we have shown that the corresponding weights can be characterized as a unique solution to the purely algebraic problem
Eq.

3.5
.
However, this is no longer true if the spaces
\mathcal{P}
are not
\mathscr{S}
-invariant.

Our idea is to abandon the constraint imposed by the cubature formula of being exact in
\mathcal{P}
, but instead use
Eq.

3.5
as a characterization of the cubature weights, in the same way as the property (
3.5
) characterized
exact
cubature weights for
\mathscr{S}
-invariant spaces, see
Theorem

3.5
.
More precisely, instead of the exact weights
\bm{w}
, defined in
Lemma

2.19
, we will look for weights
{\widetilde{\bm{w}}}
as a solution to the following problem:

\text{Find}\ \widetilde{\bm{w}}\in\Ker\lparen I-\bm{S}^{\intercal}\rparen\ \text{satisfying}\ \widetilde{\bm{w}}\cdot\bm{1}=1.

(4.1)

Recall that the constraint
\widetilde{\bm{w}}\cdot\bm{1}=1
in
Eq.

4.1
ensures that
\mathbb{P}_{0}
is integrated exactly. This new cubature rule, defined by

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\approx\widetilde{\Quad}[f]\coloneqq\sum_{1\leq i\leq M}\widetilde{w}_{i}f\left\lparen x_{i}\right\rparen,

is consistent with the invariance property
Proposition

2.11
of the integral, in sense of

Lemma 4.1
.

If
\displaystyle\widetilde{\Quad}[f]=\!\!\sum_{1\leq i\leq M}\widetilde{w}_{i}f\lparen x_{i}\rparen
, the following two properties are equivalent:

(i)\,\mbox{ for all $p\in\mathcal{P}$, $\widetilde{\Quad}[p]=\widetilde{\Quad}[\OpFct p]$;}\qquad(ii)\,\widetilde{\bm{w}}\in\Ker\lparen I-\bm{S}^{\intercal}\rparen.

We leave the proof of this result to the reader.
Following the above discussion, we single out two important questions about the “new” cubature weights
Eq.

4.1
:

•

the well-posedness of the problem
Eq.

4.1
;

•

the error committed by replacing the cubature rule
\Quad
with its perturbed version
\widetilde{\Quad}
when integrating polynomials
p\in\mathcal{P}
.

4.2
On the well-posedness of the problem
Eq.

4.1

Unfortunately, we were able to show only a partial well-posedness result.

Theorem 4.2
.

The value
\lambda=1
is an eigenvalue of the matrix
\bm{S}^{\intercal}
.
If this eigenvalue is simple, then
\widetilde{\bm{w}}\cdot\bm{1}\neq 0
.

Proof.

The first statement follows by remarking that
\bm{S}\bm{1}=\bm{1}
(the result follows by the same reasoning as in the proof of the second statement of
Lemma

3.3
). To prove the second statement, let us assume that
\widetilde{\bm{w}}\neq 0
is s.t.
\bm{S}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{w}}=\widetilde{\bm{w}}
. Applying
Lemma

3.6
with
\bm{A}=\bm{S}
and
\lambda=1
implies that
\widetilde{\bm{w}}\cdot\bm{1}\neq 0
.
∎

According to the proof of the above result, the condition that
\lambda=1
is a
simple
eigenvalue ensures that (
4.1
) is well-posed, in particular, the constraint
\widetilde{\bm{w}}\cdot\bm{1}=1
is satisfied. One can verify that since
\bm{S}
is real,
\widetilde{\bm{w}}
satisfying (
4.1
) is necessarily real, too.

4.3
Subspaces of
\mathcal{P}
integrated exactly by the inexact cubature

Since the error of the cubature is closely related to the largest polynomial space integrated exactly by the cubature, it is natural to ask a question of characterization of spaces of polynomials in
\mathcal{P}
that are integrated exactly,
i.e.

\mathcal{P}_{\Quad}:=\Big\{p\in\mathcal{P}\;\Big|\;\int_{\Gamma}p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\widetilde{\Quad}[p]\Big\}.

(4.2)

Since the integral and
\widetilde{\Quad}
are linear forms, this space has co-dimension 1 (thus dimension
\Dim\mathcal{P}-1
) in
\mathcal{P}
independently of the vector
\widetilde{\bm{w}}
(!).
A more precise characterization of the space
\mathcal{P}_{\Quad}
is given in the theorem below.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that
\bm{S}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\bm{w}}=\widetilde{\bm{w}}
and
\widetilde{\bm{w}}\cdot\bm{1}=1
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{S}}
be the largest
\mathscr{S}
-invariant subspace of
\mathcal{P}
.
Then the inexact cubature formula
\widetilde{\Quad}
integrates polynomials in the space
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{S}}
exactly, in other words,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{S}}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\Quad}
.

Proof.

First, let us argue that we can rewrite the algebraic problem
Eq.

4.1
in the variational form resembling the statement of
Corollary

3.7
.
Recall
Eq.

4.1
satisfied by
\widetilde{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, rewritten in an equivalent form, see
Lemma

3.2
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{w}}^{\intercal}=\widetilde{\bm{w}}^{\intercal}\bm{S}\iff\widetilde{\bm{w}}\cdot\Eval{p}=\widetilde{\bm{w}}\cdot\Eval_{\mathcal{P}}\Interp_{\mathcal{P}}\Eval\OpFct p,\qquad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}.

(4.3)

Remark that in the above identity, it is the operator
\Interp_{\mathcal{P}}\Eval\OpFct
that appears, rather than
\OpFct
.
Moreover, because of non-
\mathscr{S}
-invariance of
\mathcal{P}
,
\Interp_{\mathcal{P}}\Eval\OpFct\neq\OpFct
on
\mathcal{P}
.
Denoting
\widetilde{\omega}\coloneqq\lparen\Interp_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\rparen^{-1}\widetilde{\bm{w}}
(
cf.
the proof of the
Corollary

3.7
), we obtain the following counterpart of the identity in the right of
Eq.

4.3
:

\int_{\Gamma}\widetilde{\omega}\,p\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\Gamma}\widetilde{\omega}\Interp_{\mathcal{P}}\Eval\OpFct p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}.

(4.4)

Next, let us decompose the space
\mathcal{P}
into two subspaces orthogonal with respect to
L^{2}(\Gamma)
-inner-product:
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{S}}\oplus_{\perp}\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{S}}^{\perp}
.
In particular, any
v\in\mathcal{P}
can be written as orthogonal sum
v=v_{\mathscr{S}}+v_{\perp}
, where
v_{\mathscr{S}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{S}}
and
\int_{\Gamma}v_{\perp}\,v_{\mathscr{S}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=0
. In a similar manner we rewrite
\widetilde{\omega}=\widetilde{\omega}_{\mathscr{S}}+\widetilde{\omega}_{\perp}
.
Applying this decomposition to
Eq.

4.4
, and restricting the space of test functions to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{S}}
yields the new identity for
\widetilde{\omega}_{\mathscr{S}}
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\widetilde{\omega}_{\mathscr{S}}p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\Gamma}\widetilde{\omega}_{\mathscr{S}}\OpFct p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,\qquad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{S}},

(4.5)

where we used the fact that
\Interp_{\mathcal{P}}\Eval\OpFct p=\OpFct p
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{S}}
: since
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{S}}
is
\mathscr{S}
-invariant,
\Eval\OpFct p\in{\cal P}
when
p\in{\cal P}_{\mathscr{S}}
.
Moreover, from
\widetilde{\bm{w}}\cdot\bm{1}=1
, and
\mathbb{P}_{0}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{S}}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\widetilde{\omega}_{\mathscr{S}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=1.

(4.6)

In (
4.5
), (
4.6
), we recognize the problem of
Corollary

3.7
, where
\mathcal{P}
is replaced by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{S}}
.
Therefore,
\widetilde{\omega}_{\mathscr{S}}=1
. since for all
p\in\cal{P}_{\mathscr{S}}
, it holds that

\widetilde{\Quad}[p]=\widetilde{\bm{w}}\cdot\Eval p=\int_{\Gamma}\widetilde{\omega}p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\Gamma}\widetilde{\omega}_{\mathscr{S}}p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\Gamma}p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,

where the second equality above follows from the definition
\widetilde{\omega}=(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{P}}^{*})^{-1}\widetilde{\bm{w}}
, and the third is a consequence of the orthogonality, we conclude that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{S}}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\Quad}
.
∎

The above result may seem somewhat surprising: indeed, even if the matrix
\bm{S}^{\intercal}
admits several eigenfunctions satisfying the assumptions of
Theorem

4.3
, the cubature rule based on any such eigenfunction is exact in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{S}}
.

Remark 4.4
.

The above result enables us to use the inexact cubature weights to compute the weights exact in the space
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{S}}
.
For example, to integrate polynomials in the space
\mathbb{Q}_{k}
exactly, one could have computed the inexact weights in
\mathbb{Q}_{nk}
.
Such weights would have integrated the polynomials in the space
\mathbb{P}_{nk}
(which is
\mathscr{S}
-invariant) exactly, in particular the Lagrange polynomials for
\mathbb{Q}_{k}
, which define the exact cubature weights.
However, this can be potentially computationally expensive.

5
Error estimates

In this section we will quantify the approximation error of
\int_{\Gamma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
using the cubature rule introduced in the previous sections.
Such estimates will be obtained from quite standard arguments for cubature’s error estimates and cubature on regular sets.
We suggest two versions of approximation of
\int_{\Gamma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
:

•

The
h
-version, where, very roughly speaking, we decompose
\Gamma
into smaller subsets
\mu\lparen\Gamma_{j}\rparen\leq h
, and approximate
\int_{\Gamma_{j}}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
by a cubature of a fixed degree
M
.
Convergence will be assured by taking
h\to 0
.

•

The
p
-version, where we approximate directly
\int_{\Gamma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
by the cubature of the degree
M
.
Convergence is then assured by taking
M\to+\infty
.

In both cases, the error analysis relies on the following (classic) estimate, which we recall here. Let
{\cal P}_{\cal Q}
be defined as in (
4.2
). Then

\displaystyle\left\lvert e[f]\right\rvert\leq\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\Quad}}\left\lVert f-p\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(K)}\,\big[1+\left\lvert\bm{w}\right\rvert_{1}\big],\quad\mbox{where}\quad e[f]\coloneqq\int_{\Gamma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu-\Quad[f]

(5.1)

which follows from the fact that, for any
p\in\mathcal{P}_{\Quad}
,
e[f]=\int_{\Gamma}(f-p)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu-\Quad[f-p].

5.1
Convergence by refining the mesh

5.1.1
Nested meshes on the IFS

To define a mesh on
\Gamma
, it is natural to decompose it into sub-fractals using the IFS contractive maps. Let us fix the mesh size
h>0
. The first, simplest decomposition, can be obtained from the following observation.
Since
\Gamma=\OpSet\lparen\Gamma\rparen
is a fixed point of the Hutchinson operator
\OpSet
, see
Theorem

2.3
, it is also a fixed point of the
p
-times iterated operator
\OpSet^{p}
.
This gives the following decomposition of
\Gamma
, using
\bm{m}=\lparen m_{1},\ldots,m_{p}\rparen\in\mathbb{L}^{p}
:

\Gamma=\bigcup_{\bm{m}\in\mathbb{L}^{p}}\Gamma_{\bm{m}}\qquad\text{where}\ \Gamma_{\bm{m}}=S_{m_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ S_{m_{p}}\lparen\Gamma\rparen.

(5.2)

The maximal refinement level
p
is chosen so that
\operatorname{diam}\Gamma_{\bm{m}}<h
, for all
\bm{m}\in\mathbb{L}^{p}
.
Remark that in the above, a priori
\mu(\Gamma_{\bm{m}}\cap\Gamma_{\bm{m^{\prime}}})\neq 0
for
\bm{m}\neq\bm{m}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}^{p}
.
The use of the mesh (
5.2
) for the integral evaluation can be computationally inefficient, since the diameters of the patches
\Gamma_{\bm{m}}
in the above decomposition can vary significantly (in other words, some regions of
\Gamma
are refined too finely). Therefore, instead we will use an approach suggested in
[
GHM23
]
. Let us introduce some useful notation (with
\bm{m}\in\mathbb{L}^{p}
)

\left|\begin{array}[]{lll}S_{\bm{m}}:=S_{m_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ S_{m_{p}},\quad\rho_{\bm{m}}:=\rho_{m_{1}}\cdots\rho_{m_{p}},\quad\mu_{\bm{m}}:=\mu_{m_{1}}\cdots\mu_{m_{p}}.\\[10.0pt]
{\bf L}^{p}=\mathbb{L}^{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathbb{L}^{p},\quad\mbox{for any }p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\cup\{\infty\}.\end{array}\right.

(5.3)

For
p=0
, by convention, we define
\mathbb{L}^{0}=\{\emptyset\}
,
\Gamma_{\emptyset}=\Gamma
,
S_{\emptyset}=\Id
,
\rho_{\emptyset}=1
, and
\mu_{\emptyset}=1
.
Given
h>0
, let us define the set of multi-indices
\mathcal{L}_{h}\subset{\bf L}^{\infty}
by the following:

\displaystyle\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}\iff\rho_{\bm{m}}\Diam\Gamma\leq h,\quad\text{ and }\rho_{\ell}^{-1}\rho_{\bm{m}}\Diam\Gamma>h,\text{ for some }\ell\in\mathbb{L},

(5.4)

and the corresponding decomposition of
\Gamma
via

\displaystyle\Gamma=\bigcup\limits_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}}\Gamma_{\bm{m}}.

(5.5)

We will see below that these definitions and decompositions make sense. Let us remark that in (
5.4
), we used
\rho_{\bm{m}}\Diam\Gamma
instead of
\Diam\Gamma_{\bm{m}}
, because for self-affine sets
\Diam\Gamma_{\bm{m}}
is not easily computable. On the other hand,
\Diam\Gamma_{\bm{m}}\leq\rho_{\bm{m}}\Diam\Gamma
. The first condition in (
5.4
) thus ensures that
\operatorname{diam}\Gamma_{\bm{m}}<h
for all patches
\Gamma_{\bm{m}}
, while the second condition is a requirement of minimality of the set
{\cal L}_{h}
, in the sense that the patches
\Gamma_{\bm{m}}
cannot become too small.

The set
\mathcal{L}_{h}
can be obtained by the following algorithm that constructs a sequence
\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k}
, with
\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k}\subset{\bf L}^{k}
, which is stationary after a finite number of steps. Then,
\mathcal{L}_{h}
is defined as the stationary point of this algorithm.
The algorithm proceeds as follows.
Starting from
\mathcal{L}_{h}^{0}=\{\emptyset\}
, we deduce
\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k+1}
from
\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k}
by looking at the (possibly empty) set
\mathcal{N}_{h}^{k}=\{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k}\>|\>\mathopen{}\rho_{\bm{m}}\Diam\Gamma>h\}
and setting

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k+1}=\left\lparen\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{h}^{k}\right\rparen\cup\left\{\left\lparen m_{1},\ldots,m_{k},\ell\right\rparen\>{:}\>\mathopen{}\left\lparen m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\right\rparen\in\mathcal{N}_{h}^{k}\ \text{and}\ \ell\in\mathbb{L}\right\}.

(5.6)

The reader will easily verify that 1) the stationary point

\mathcal{L}_{h}
is characterized by (
5.4
); 2) for all
k\geq 0
,
\Gamma=\bigcup_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k}}\Gamma_{\bm{m}}
(by induction on
k
), which implies (
5.5
).

Remark 5.1
.

Setting
\displaystyle\rho_{\max}=\max_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}}\rho_{\ell}
, the algorithm stops after
k_{\max}
iteration with

k_{\max}\leq k_{*}\coloneqq\inf\,\big\{k\in\mathbb{N}\;\big|\;\rho_{\max}^{k}\Diam\Gamma\leq h\big\}\quad\text{\emph{i.e.}}\quad\mbox{$k_{*}=\left\lceil\frac{\log\lparen h/\Diam\Gamma\rparen}{\log\rho_{\max}}\right\rceil$}.

Remark 5.2
.

For similar IFS, it holds that
\rho_{\min}h<\Diam\Gamma_{\bm{m}}\leq h
, for all
\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}
.

Just like in (
5.2
),
\Gamma_{\bm{m}}
and
\Gamma_{\bm{m}^{\prime}}
in (
5.5
) may have non-trivial intersections. However, this decomposition defines a ’partition’ of
\Gamma
in the sense of the following result.

Lemma 5.3
.

For
h>0
and
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}\lparen\Gamma;\mu\rparen
, we have, for
k\geq 0
,

(i)\quad\int_{\Gamma}f\lparen x\rparen\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k}}\mu_{\bm{m}}\int_{\Gamma}f\circ S_{\bm{m}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,\quad(ii)\quad\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k}}\mu_{\bm{m}}=1

(5.7)

Proof.

We proceed by induction.
The result is true for
k=0
(trivial); for
k=1
, we remark that
\mathcal{L}_{h}^{1}=\mathbb{L}
. Then (
5.7
) is direct from
Proposition

2.11
. Next, assuming
Eq.

5.7
, by definition (
5.6
) of
\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k+1}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k+1}}\mu_{\bm{m}}\int_{\Gamma}f\circ S_{\bm{m}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{h}^{k}}\mu_{\bm{m}}\int_{\Gamma}f\circ S_{\bm{m}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle+\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{N}_{h}^{k}}\mu_{\bm{m}}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}}\mu_{\ell}\int_{\Gamma}f\circ S_{\bm{m}}\circ S_{\ell}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.

By
Proposition

2.11
,
\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{L}}\mu_{\ell}\int_{\Gamma}f\circ S_{\bm{m}}\circ S_{\ell}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\Gamma}f\circ S_{\bm{m}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
, thus

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k+1}}\mu_{\bm{m}}\int_{\Gamma}f\circ S_{\bm{m}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{h}^{k}}\mu_{\bm{m}}\int_{\Gamma}f\circ S_{\bm{m}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu+\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{N}_{h}^{k}}\mu_{\bm{m}}\int_{\Gamma}f\circ S_{\bm{m}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}^{k}}\mu_{\bm{m}}\int_{\Gamma}f\circ S_{\bm{m}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,

which proves (
5.7
)(i). The equality (
5.7
)(ii) follows from taking
f=1
.
∎

5.1.2
Mesh refinement cubature

Let
K\supset\Gamma
be a compact of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as in
Lemma

2.6
, and
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}\subset K
be a set of points that are
\mathcal{P}
-unisolvent on
K
.
Let us assume that the weights
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
are such that the cubature rule
\Quad
is exact on
\mathbb{P}_{k}\subset\mathcal{P}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Following
Lemma

5.3
, we define a cubature rule
\Quad_{h}
:

\displaystyle\Quad_{h}[f]:=\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}}\mu_{\bm{m}}\Quad\left[f\circ S_{\bm{m}}\right].

(5.8)

We define the set

K_{h}=\bigcup_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}}S_{\bm{m}}\lparen K\rparen\mbox{ which satisfies }\Gamma\subset K_{h}\subsetneq K\quad(\mbox{by }\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{lem:existence-large-compact}).

(5.9)

It can can be shown that
K_{h}
converges to
\Gamma
as
h\to 0
, for the Hausdorff distance.
By construction, the
h
-cubature formula (
5.8
) uses the values of the function
f
in the set
K_{h}
, rather than the whole compact
K
(the interest of this is discussed in
Remark

5.5
). Accordingly, we have the following result.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
K
be a convex set and satisfy
Lemma

2.6
.
Let
H>0
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let a cubature
\Quad
be exact on
\mathbb{P}_{k}
.
For all
f\in\mathscr{C}^{k+1}\lparen K_{H}\rparen
and
h\leq H
, we have

\big|\Quad_{h}\left[f\right]-\int_{\Gamma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\big|\leq C\,h^{k+1}\big\|\operatorname{D}^{k+1}f\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}\left\lparen K_{H}\right\rparen},

where

\displaystyle\lVert\operatorname{D}^{k+1}f\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}\lparen K_{H}\rparen}=\max_{\lvert\bm{\beta}\rvert_{1}=k+1}\lVert\partial^{\bm{\beta}}f\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}\lparen K_{H}\rparen}

and the constant
C
is defined by

C=\mbox{ $\left\lparen\frac{\Diam K}{\Diam\Gamma}\right\rparen^{k+1}\left\lparen\left\lvert\bm{w}\right\rvert_{1}+1\right\rparen\sum_{\lvert\bm{\beta}\rvert=k+1}\frac{1}{\bm{\beta}!}.$}

Remark 5.5
.

As seen from the statement of the above result, the convergence of the cubature depends on the regularity of the integrand in the set
K_{H}
. This allows to use efficiently such an
h
-quadrature to integrate functions that have singularities inside a convex set
K
, but regular in a small vicinity of
\Gamma
,
cf.

Fig.

1
.

Proof of
Theorem

5.4
.

Let
f\in\mathscr{C}^{k+1}\lparen K_{H}\rparen
. Using
Lemma

5.3
and
Eq.

5.8
,

\left\lvert\Quad_{h}[f]-\int_{\Gamma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right\rvert=\left\lvert\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}}\mu_{\bm{m}}\left[\Quad\left[f\circ S_{\bm{m}}\right]-\int_{\Gamma}f\circ S_{\bm{m}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right]\right\rvert\leq\max_{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}}e_{\bm{m}}

(5.10)

where

e_{\bm{m}}=\left\lvert\Quad\left[f\circ S_{\bm{m}}\right]-\int_{\Gamma}f\circ S_{\bm{m}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right\rvert,

and we used (
5.7
)(ii).
To estimate each of the terms
e_{\bm{m}}
, we use (
5.1
), where we recall that the polynomials
\mathbb{P}_{k}
are integrated exactly:

\displaystyle|e_{\bm{m}}|\leq\inf_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{k}}\left\lVert f\circ S_{\bm{m}}-p\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(K)}\left[1+\left\lvert\bm{w}\right\rvert_{1}\right].

(5.11)

To estimate the right-hand side, we use the multivariate Taylor series expansion of
f
in the point
z_{\bm{m}}={S}_{\bm{m}}z
, with arbitrary
z\in K
. It holds that
f=t_{\bm{m}}+r_{\bm{m}}
, where

\displaystyle t_{\bm{m}}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert_{1}\leq k}\frac{\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}f\left\lparen z_{\bm{m}}\right\rparen}{\bm{\alpha}!}\left\lparen y-z_{\bm{m}}\right\rparen^{\bm{\alpha}},\qquad r_{\bm{m}}(y)=\sum_{\lvert\bm{\beta}\rvert_{1}=k+1}h_{\bm{\beta}}\lparen y\rparen\left\lparen y-z_{\bm{m}}\right\rparen^{\bm{\beta}},

(5.12)

\displaystyle h_{\bm{\beta}}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{k+1}{\bm{\beta}!}\int_{0}^{1}\lparen 1-t\rparen^{k}D^{\bm{\beta}}f(z_{\bm{m}}+t(y-z_{\bm{m}}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(5.13)

Remark that
t_{\bm{m}}\in\mathbb{P}_{k}
, and thus
t_{\bm{m}}\circ S_{\bm{m}}\in\mathbb{P}_{k}
; then (
5.11
) implies that

\displaystyle|e_{\bm{m}}|\leq\left\lVert r_{\bm{m}}\circ S_{\bm{m}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(K)}\left[1+\left\lvert\bm{w}\right\rvert_{1}\right].

(5.14)

From (
5.12
) it follows that

\lVert r_{\bm{m}}\circ S_{\bm{m}}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(K)}\leq\sum_{\lvert\bm{\beta}\rvert_{1}=k+1}\left\lVert h_{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(S_{\bm{m}}(K))}\left\lvert S_{\bm{m}}y-S_{\bm{m}}z\right\rvert^{k+1}_{2},

(5.15)

where we used the bound
\lvert\lparen y-z_{\bm{m}}\rparen^{\bm{\beta}}\rvert\leq\lvert y-z_{\bm{m}}\rvert_{2}^{\lvert\bm{\beta}\rvert_{1}}
.
Using (
5.13
), convexity of
K
and the fact that
z_{\bm{m}}=S_{\bm{m}}z\in K_{h}
, we conclude that

\left\lVert h_{\bm{\beta}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}\left\lparen S_{\bm{m}}\lparen K\rparen\right\rparen}\leq\frac{1}{\bm{\beta}!}\left\lVert\partial^{\bm{\beta}}f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}\left\lparen K_{h}\right\rparen}.

(5.16)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\left\lvert S_{\bm{m}}y-S_{\bm{m}}z\right\rvert^{k+1}_{2}\leq\rho_{\bm{m}}^{k+1}\lvert y-z\rvert^{k+1}\leq\left\lparen\frac{h\Diam K}{\Diam\Gamma}\right\rparen^{k+1},

(5.17)

where we use the fact that
\bm{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{h}
, and thus
\rho^{\bm{m}}\Diam\Gamma\leq h
.
Combining
Eqs.

5.16
and
5.17
into
Eq.

5.15
, inserting the resulting bound into
Eq.

5.14
and using (
5.10
) yields the desired result.
∎

5.2
Convergence by increasing the quadrature order

Alternatively, we can evaluate the cubature by computing
\Quad[f]
directly, without splitting
\Gamma
into sub-sets. In this case the convergence is achieved by increasing the number of cubature points
M
(we will denote the corresponding cubature by
\Quad^{M}[f]
).
The goal of this section is to quantify the speed of convergence with respect to
M
. Recall the estimate
Eq.

5.1
, where we emphasize the dependence of all quantities on
M
, by using appropriate indices:

\displaystyle\left\lvert e^{M}[f]\right\rvert\leq\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\Quad}^{M}}\left\lVert f-p\right\rVert_{\infty}\left[1+\left\lvert\bm{w}^{M}\right\rvert_{1}\right].

(5.18)

Unlike in the previous section, both factors in the above estimate will play an important role, since, in general,
\lvert\bm{w}^{M}\rvert_{1}
can be unbounded as
M\to+\infty
.
We will start our discussion with an estimate on
\lvert\bm{w}^{M}\rvert_{1}
. Next, we present convergence estimates for
\mathscr{C}^{k}
-functions in
Theorem

5.10
and finally estimates for analytic functions in
Theorem

5.11
.

Many of the estimates will be made more explicit for the tensor-product cubature rule satisfying the following assumption.

Assumption 5.6
.

Assume that
K=\Pi_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}
, where
\Pi_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}=[a_{1},b_{1}]\times\cdots\times[a_{\ell},b_{\ell}]
.
Let additionally
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{1}^{N}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{X}_{\ell}^{N}
, where
\mathcal{X}_{i}^{N}
are
(N+1)
Chebyshev (first or second kind) quadrature nodes on
[a_{i},\,b_{i}]

[
Tre13
, Chapter 2 and Exercise 2.4]
.
The corresponding unisolvent polynomial space is the tensor product space
{\cal P}=\mathbb{Q}_{N}
with
M=(N+1)^{n}
.

We denote by
\bm{w}_{C}^{N,n}
and
\Quad_{C}^{N,n}
(the index
C
stands for “Chebyshev”) the respective cubature weights and cubature rule, computed with the method of this paper.

Remark 5.7
.

Remark that the cubature points depend on the choice of the compact
K
, i.e.
K=\Pi_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}
.

We choose the Chebyshev points in particular because, for this case, we are able to obtain the most optimal error estimates (this is related to the behavior of the bounds on
\lvert\bm{w}^{M}\rvert_{1}
, which we connect in
Section

5.2.1
to the Lebesgue constants of the points
\mathcal{X}_{i}^{N}
, and that behave asymptotically optimally for Chebyshev points).

5.2.1
An estimate on
\lvert\bm{w}^{M}\rvert_{1}
for
\mathscr{S}
-invariant spaces

In this case, the weights provided with our algebraic approach satisfy
w_{i}^{M}=\int_{\Gamma}\Lpoly_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
(cf. Section
3
) and
Theorem

3.5
.
These weights may not be positive (see the experiments in
Section

6.2
), and therefore, even though they sum to 1,
|\bm{w}^{M}|_{1}
can still be unbounded as
M\to+\infty
.
Nonetheless, this quantity grows very mildly, provided a suitable choice of the cubature points.
This is summarized in the following result, whose proof is left to the reader.

Lemma 5.8
.

The exact weights
w_{j}^{M}=\int_{\Gamma}\Lpoly_{j}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
satisfy the following bound:

\displaystyle\lvert\bm{w}^{M}\rvert_{1}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq M}\lvert w_{j}^{M}\rvert\leq\Lambda_{\mathcal{X}}^{1}(\Gamma)\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\Gamma}\sum_{1\leq i\leq M}\left\lvert\Lpoly_{i}(x)\right\rvert,

where
\Lambda_{\mathcal{X}}^{1}(\Gamma)
is the Lebesgue’s constant for
\mathcal{X}
.

In particular, for cubature based on
5.6
, we have the following bound.

Proposition 5.9
.

For the cubature as in
5.6
, there exists
C(n)>0
, s.t.

\big|\bm{w}_{C}^{N,n}\big|_{1}\leq C(n)\log^{n}(N+1),\quad\text{ for all }N\geq 1.

Proof.

We use the bound of
Lemma

5.8
.
In our setting Lagrange polynomials are tensor products of univariate Lagrange polynomials, thus

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathcal{X}}^{\Gamma}\leq\Lambda_{\mathcal{X}}^{K}\leq\prod_{1\leq i\leq n}\Lambda_{\mathcal{X}_{i}}^{\left[a_{i},\>b_{i}\right]},

where
\Lambda_{\mathcal{X}_{i}}^{[a_{i},\>b_{i}]}
is a Lebesgue constant for interpolation in
\mathcal{X}_{i}
on
[a_{i},\>b_{i}]
,
cf.

[
Tre13
, Ch. 15]
.
It remains to apply to the above the exact asymptotic of Lebesgue’s constant of the Chebyshev points,
cf.

[
Bru97
]
and references therein.
∎

5.2.2
Error analysis for
\mathscr{C}^{k}
-functions

The goal of this section is to provide error estimates for the quadrature rule
Q^{M}[f]
, provided that
f\in\mathscr{C}^{k}({K})
. Recall the error bound (
5.18
). To obtain convergence estimates, it remains to quantify

\displaystyle\left\lvert e^{M}_{\mathcal{P}}[f]\right\rvert\coloneqq\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{M}}\lVert f-p\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(K)},

(5.19)

where
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{M}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}
is defined in (
4.2
).
The following estimate is fairly well-known.

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
K
be a convex compact set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
If
f\in\mathscr{C}^{k}({K})
, and if
\mathbb{P}_{N}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{M}
, for some
N\geq 1
, then there exists a constant
C(K,n,k)
independent of

and

, such that

\left\lvert e^{M}_{\mathcal{P}}[f]\right\rvert\leq\frac{C(K,n,k)}{N^{k}}\sum_{\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert_{1}=k}\left\lVert\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(K)}.

(5.20)

Proof.

Please see the multivariate version of Jackson’s theorem as stated in
[
Sch69
, Thm. 4.10]
(see p. 168 of
[
Sch69
]
for the justification that
\operatorname{int}K
is a regular set, as required by
[
Sch69
, Thm. 4.10]
),
cf.
as well
[
BBL02
, Thm. 2]
for a similar result.
In the first reference, the result is formulated for
\mathbb{Q}_{N}
instead of
\mathbb{P}_{N}
; nonetheless, it is still valid for
\mathbb{P}_{N}
(with a different constant compared to
[

), since
\mathbb{Q}_{\lfloor N/n\rfloor}\subset\mathbb{P}_{N}
.
∎

Combining the estimates of
Theorem

5.10
and
Lemma

5.8
yields the following result.

Theorem 5.11
.

Let the cubature rule be like in
5.6
. Assume that

.
Then, there exists
C(f,K,n,k)>0
, s.t. for all

,

\Big|\,Q_{C}^{N,n}[f]-\int_{\Gamma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\Big|\leq C(f,K,n,k)\left\lparen 1+\big|\bm{w}_{C}^{N,n}\big|_{1}\right\rparen N^{-k}.

(5.21)

In the case when
\mathcal{P}^{N,n}_{C}
are
\mathscr{S}
-invariant for all

, the above estimate yields

\Big|\,Q_{C}^{N,n}[f]-\int_{\Gamma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\Big|\leq c\cdot C(f,K,n,k)N^{-k}\log^{n}(N+1),\quad\text{ with some }c>0.

Proof.

Both results follow from the expression

5.18
combined with

5.10
.
The estimate in the

5.9
.
∎

Compared to the error estimates of the tensor-product cubatures on the domains

5.2.3
Error analysis for analytic functions

In this section, we fully restrict our attention to cubatures satisfying
5.6
.
We will also work with analytic functions.
This is of interest when evaluating regular integrals in boundary element methods,
e.g.

\int_{\Gamma_{\bm{m}}}\int_{\Gamma_{\bm{n}}}G(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{y}
, where
G
is the fundamental solution of the Helmholtz equation.
The ideas and definitions that we present here are now standard, and we follow the exposition in
[
SS11
, Sec. 5.3.2.2]
, which we simplify and adapt to our setting.
We will work with componentwise analytic functions, as defined below.

Definition 5.12
.

The function
f\colon\Pi_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}\to\mathbb{C}
is componentwise analytic, if there exists
\zeta>1
, such that, for each
i
, and for all
\bm{y}_{i}\coloneqq(y_{1},\ldots,y_{i-1},y_{i+1},\ldots,y_{n})
with
y_{\ell}\in[a_{\ell},b_{\ell}]
, the function

f_{\bm{y}_{i}}(t)\coloneqq f\left\lparen y_{1},\ldots,y_{i-1},t,y_{i+1},\ldots,y_{n}\right\rparen\colon[a_{i},\>b_{i}]\to\mathbb{C},

admits an analytic extension
F_{\bm{y}_{i}}(z)
in the Bernstein ellipse

\mathscr{E}_{a_{i},b_{i}}^{\zeta}=\left\{\frac{a_{i}+b_{i}}{2}+\frac{a_{i}-b_{i}}{2}\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}\>{:}\>\mathopen{}z\in B_{\zeta}(0)\right\},

and is additionally continuous in the closure of this ellipse.

Next, to estimate
Eq.

5.1
, in particular, the expression (
5.19
), we will choose a particular polynomial from
\mathcal{P}^{M}_{\Quad}
.
To construct it, let us define a Chebyshev weighted
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-projection operator of degree
m
in the direction
i
, which is helpful because we work with tensorized cubature rules.
In particular, let
\{T_{k}(t)\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
,
T_{k}(t)=\cos(k\arccos t)
,
t\in[-1,\>1]
, be the set of Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind, see
[
Tre13
, Ch. 3 and Thm. 3.1]
for the relevant discussion.
For each given
\bm{y}_{i}
as in
Definition

5.12
, we set

\left|\begin{array}[]{lll}\Pi_{i}^{(m)}&\displaystyle\colon\lparen\mathscr{C}(K),\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\infty}\rparen\to\lparen\mathscr{C}(K),\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\infty}\rparen,\quad\Pi_{i}^{(m)}f\left\lparen\bm{y}\right\rparen\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{m}a_{\bm{y}_{i},k}T_{k}(y_{i}),\\[10.0pt]
a_{\bm{y}_{i},k}&\displaystyle\coloneqq c_{k}\int_{-1}^{1}T_{k}(t)f_{\bm{y}_{i}}\left\lparen\tfrac{b_{i}-a_{i}}{2}t+\tfrac{a_{i}+b_{i}}{2}\right\rparen(1-t^{2})^{-1/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,\end{array}\right.

where
c_{0}=2/\pi
and
c_{k}=1/\pi
for
k>0
.
Let us introduce the product operator

\displaystyle\bm{\Pi}^{(m)}f\coloneqq\Pi_{1}^{(m)}\Pi_{2}^{(m)}\cdots\Pi_{n}^{(m)}f\in\mathbb{Q}_{m}.

(5.23)

We shall use this operator to prove the following approximation result.

Proposition 5.13
.

Assume that
f
is as in
Definition

5.12
.
Assume that
\mathbb{Q}_{N}\subset\mathcal{P}^{M}_{\Quad}
,
N\geq 1
.
Then there exists
C_{f}>0
, such that

\displaystyle e_{\mathcal{P}}^{M}=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\Quad}^{M}}\left\lVert f-p\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(K)}\leq C_{f}\,\log^{n-1}(N+1)\,\zeta^{-N}/(\zeta-1).

Proof.

We start by bounding
e_{\mathcal{P}}^{M}\leq e_{N}:=\big\|f-\bm{\Pi}^{(N)}f\big\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}
.
Since
f-\bm{\Pi}^{(N)}f=f-\Pi^{(N)}_{1}f+\Pi^{(N)}_{1}f-\Pi_{1}^{(N)}\Pi_{2}^{(N)}\cdots\Pi_{n}^{(N)}f
, by the triangle inequality,

e_{N}\leq\big\|f-\Pi^{(N)}_{1}f\big\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}+\big\|\Pi_{1}^{(N)}\big\|\big\|f-\Pi_{2}^{(N)}\cdots\Pi_{n}^{(N)}f\big\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}.

Repeating this procedure
n-2
times, with different operators
\Pi_{k}^{(N)}
, yields the bound:

e_{N}\leq\big\|f-\Pi_{1}^{(N)}f\big\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\Big(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\Big\|\Pi_{\ell}^{(N)}\big\|\Big)\,\big\|f-\Pi_{k+1}^{(N)}f\big\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}.

One concludes with the help of the following bounds

\big\|\Pi_{i}^{(N)}\big\|\leq C\log(N+1)\;\mbox{ and }\;\big\|f-\Pi_{k+1}^{(N)}f\big\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\leq C_{f}\,\zeta^{-N}/(\zeta-1),

easily deduced from existing 1D bounds in
[
Pow67
,
Mas80
]

and
[
Tre13
, Thm. 8.2]
respectively.
∎

Combining
Proposition

5.13
and
Lemma

5.8
allows to quantify the cubature error.

Theorem 5.14
.

Assume that
f
is like in
Definition

5.12
with
\zeta>1
.
In the situation of
5.6
, there exists a constant
C_{f}>0
, such that the following holds true.
If the spaces
\mathbb{Q}_{N}
,
N\geq 1
, are
\mathscr{S}
-invariant, then,

\Big|\Quad_{C}^{N,n}[f]-\int_{\Gamma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\Big|\leq\frac{C_{f}\;\zeta^{-N}}{\zeta-1}\log^{2n-1}(N+1).

(5.24)

Otherwise,

\Big|\Quad_{C}^{N,n}[f]-\int_{\Gamma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\Big|\leq\frac{C_{f}\zeta^{-\lfloor N/n\rfloor}}{\zeta-1}\Big(1+\big|\bm{w}^{N,n}_{C}\big|_{1}\Big)

(5.25)

Proof.

Assume that
\mathbb{Q}_{N}
,
N\geq 1
, are
\mathscr{S}
-invariant.
Then (
5.24
) ifollows from
Eq.

5.18
,
Proposition

5.9
and
Proposition

5.13
. If
\mathbb{Q}_{N}
is not
\mathscr{S}
-invariant, by
Theorem

4.3
, the respective cubature rule integrates the polynomials in
\mathbb{P}_{N}
exactly.
Because
\mathbb{Q}_{\lfloor N/n\rfloor}\subset\mathbb{P}_{N}
, we can again combine
Eq.

5.18
and
Proposition

5.13
to get (
5.25
).
∎

Remark 5.15
.

In the
\mathscr{S}
-invariant case, compared to the multivariate integration on product domains based on the tensor-product Gauss cubature (
cf.

[
SS11
, Thm. 5.3.13]
), our cubature error is slightly worse, by a factor
\log^{2n-1}(N+1)
.

Moreover, according to the estimate (
5.25
), the non-
\mathscr{S}
-invariant case might suffer from a deteriorated convergence rate. We think that this estimate is non-optimal, due to the technique of proof based on
\mathbb{Q}_{N}
spaces. We believe that one could recover, under stronger assumptions on

than the ones in

5.12
, the same convergence rate as for the
\mathscr{S}
-invariant case by adapting the approach of
[

]
to

.

6
Numerical experiments

In this section we present several numerical experiments, illustrating statements of different results in our paper, as well as performance of the new cubature rule.

6.1
Algorithmic realization and implementation

We implemented the cubature rule following
5.6
, as a
Julia
code, see
[
Moi24
]
. In all experiments we used Chebyshev points of the first kind.

Finding
K

Finding a bounding box for an IFS is a subject of research
[
CC03
,
Mar09
]
.
The bounding rectangle
K=\Pi_{i=1}^{n}[a_{i},\>b_{i}]
is either explicitly known (for “classic” fractals like Cantor dust), or can be computed as the smallest hypercube containing the fractal.
To approximate the latter, we used a minimization procedure applied to
Eq.

2.3
, with
\lvert\,\cdot\,\rvert_{2}
replaced by
\lvert\,\cdot\,\rvert_{\infty}
-norm.
2
2

2

We used the
Julia
package
Optim.jl

[
MR18
]
for this.
This is justified by
Lemma

2.6
; remark that a ball in
\lvert\,\cdot\,\rvert_{\infty}
-norm is exactly a hypercube.

Computation of cubature weights

To compute the cubature weights based on the algebraic characterization
Eq.

3.5
, we need to evaluate the entries of the matrix
\bm{S}
(Lagrange polynomials), and solve the corresponding eigenvalue problem.
The Lagrange polynomials were evaluated using the barycentric interpolation formula from
[
Tre13
, Ch. 5]
.
Recall solving the eigenvalue problem in the
\mathscr{S}
-invariant case amounts to finding the eigenvector of
\bm{S}^{\intercal}
corresponding to the largest (in modulus) eigenvalue
\lambda=1
,
cf.

Lemma

3.3
. Thus we used the power iteration method, which converges geometrically fast.
We used the same method in the non-
\mathscr{S}
-invariant case. Despite the absence of theory, in all our numerical experiments,
\lambda=1
appeared to be the largest in modulus and a simple eigenvalue.

Reference solution and errors
