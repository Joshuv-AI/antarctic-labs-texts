Aging, biofilms, and diabetic foot ulcers: disrupting chronic infections with super-oxidized solutions and addressing age-related vulnerabilities
Authors: Nor Afifah Supardy, Rejith R S Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic Foot Ulcers (DFUs) are a serious complication of diabetes, often leading to chronic infections, prolonged hospitalization, and, in severe cases, amputations. A major challenge in DFU treatment is the presence of polymicrobial biofilms, complex bacterial communities encased in a protective extracellular matrix, which enhances their survival, evade immune responses, and contributes to antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Conventional treatments, such as systemic antibiotics, antiseptics, and mechanical debridement, often fail to fully eliminate biofilms, highlighting the need for more effective therapeutic strategies. Considering the existing ineffective anti-biofilm treatments, super-oxidized solution (SOS) has emerged as a promising non-antibiotic alternative for DFU infections. As a reactive oxygen species (ROS)-based therapy, SOS effectively disrupts biofilm integrity, enhances bacterial eradication, and promotes wound healing by reducing inflammation and supporting tissue regeneration. Additionally, its mechanism of action could significantly minimize the risk of AMR, making it a valuable adjunct to conventional therapies. The purpose of this review is to explore the mechanisms of biofilm formation in DFUs, evaluate the limitations of current treatment approaches, and highlight the therapeutic potential of SOS. Integrating SOS into DFU treatment protocols could improve infection control, reduce chronic wound burden, and enhance patient outcomes. By advancing non-antibiotic solutions like SOS, DFU management can shift toward more effective and sustainable wound care strategies, ultimately reducing the need for surgical interventions and improving the quality of life for diabetic patients.Keywords: Diabetic foot ulcers, Biofilms, Antimicrobial resistance, Super-oxidized solution, Wound healing

Background

Diabetic Foot Ulcer (DFU) is a serious and debilitating complication of uncontrolled diabetes, typically presenting as an ulcer on the bottom of the foot [1, 2]. Roughly 15% of people with diabetes will develop DFUs, and among those, 14% to 24% will face amputation due to bone infections or other complications related to the ulcer [3, 4]. The diabetic foot is biologically compromised, a result of multiple contributing factors [5, 6]. The major underlying causes are peripheral neuropathy and ischemia from peripheral arterial disease (PAD) [7, 8]. In the presence of these factors, even moderate ischemia can cause ulcers and impair healing [9, 10]. Recent studies have highlighted that the incidence of DFUs is increasing globally, with biofilms playing a key role in delaying healing. This section is enhanced with updated statistics from open-access reviews to emphasize the growing burden [11–14].

Neurological factors in diabetic foot pathology

Peripheral neuropathy is one of the major factors contributing to diabetic ulceration [15, 16]. Due to the loss of nociceptive sensation, patients are unable to perceive local foot trauma [17, 18]. As a result, the foot is at high risk for repeated injuries and ulceration, which, if left untreated, could ultimately lead to amputation [19, 20]. The underlying mechanisms of DFUs involve a triad of critical factors: neuropathy, vascular insufficiency, and secondary infections arising from minor foot trauma [3, 21]. DFUs are often initially present as disruptions of the epidermis and partial dermis [22, 23]. Common early lesions include calluses, blisters, and areas of localized warmth, redness, and shallow skin breakdown, all of which can easily progress to full-thickness ulcers if not promptly addressed [24, 25].

Disease progression and functional impairments

The progression of DFUs typically unfolds in three stages [26, 27]. The first stage involves callus formation, closely linked to diabetic neuropathy [15, 28]. Motor neuropathy can cause foot deformities, such as claw toes or Charcot foot, while sensory neuropathy leads to the loss of protective sensation, exposing the foot to chronic, unnoticed mechanical stresses [10, 29]. Autonomic neuropathy further contributes by causing dry, fissured skin, weakening the skin barrier, and increasing susceptibility to infection [5, 16]. In the early or less severe stages of diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), sensory impairments are predominant [30, 31]. However, as DPN progresses, it can lead to major dysfunction of the neuromuscular system [32, 33]. This may manifest muscle atrophy and weakness, delayed muscle contraction, and loss of strength and endurance [34, 35]. Combined with sensory deficits, these motor impairments contribute to decreased functional capacity, impaired mobility, altered gait, and an increased risk of falls [30, 36]. Diabetic neuropathy, particularly complex polyneuropathy, significantly increases the risk of foot ulceration, infection, and ultimately, amputation [22, 37]. Ulcerations in neuropathic feet typically develop on the plantar surface, an area exposed to elevated mechanical pressures [4, 7]. Persistent hyperglycemia exacerbates these problems by impairing circulation through endothelial damage of blood vessels, even in previously healthy tissues [38, 39]. As circulation declines, nerve function deteriorates, sweat production diminishes, and the skin becomes dry and prone to cracking [40, 41]. Neuronal ischemia results in numbness, increasing the likelihood of unnoticed injuries [1, 42].

Types and mechanisms of diabetic neuropathy

Distal symmetric polyneuropathy (DSPN) is the most common diabetic complication characterized by symmetrical sensory loss, typically starting in the feet [43, 44]. It often develops gradually and may remain asymptomatic for a long time [45, 46]. Reflexes, particularly ankle and Achilles, are usually reduced or absent [47, 48]. Motor weakness is uncommon, and unsteadiness may occur due to impaired proprioception [49, 50]. Approximately 75% of diabetic neuropathies are classified as DSPN, the most prevalent form [9, 51]. DSPN can be further categorized into three subtypes: predominantly small-fiber neuropathy, primarily large-fiber neuropathy, and mixed small- and large-fiber neuropathy [52, 53]. Small-fiber neuropathy impacts the smaller fibers responsible for pain and temperature sensations, leading to symptoms like burning or tingling in the feet [54, 55]. Primarily, large-fiber neuropathy affects the larger fibers responsible for vibration sense, proprioception, and motor function, causing numbness, muscle weakness, and balance issues [56, 57]. Mixed small- and large-fiber neuropathy involves small and large fibers, resulting in sensory loss, pain, and motor dysfunction [58, 59]. DSPN is considered the most important risk factor for foot ulceration and serves as a prerequisite for the development of Charcot neuroarthropathy (CN) [60, 61]. Both foot ulceration and CN are late-stage complications of DSPN and are major contributors to increased amputation risk, healthcare costs, and mortality among patients with diabetes [62, 63]. The insensitivity of the foot to pain, pressure, vibration, and temperature results from injuries to the nerves responsible for transmitting sensory information [64, 65]. This condition is referred to as sensory neuropathy [66, 67]. Both patients and doctors often overlook serious ulcerations due to the absence or reduction of pain sensation [68, 69]. As a result of this diminished sensitivity, pre-ulcerative lesions and minor wounds can go unnoticed and are frequently re-injured, leading to ulcer development and delayed detection [5, 70]. Muscle denervation has a direct impact on foot function [30, 71]. The small muscles of the foot, such as the lumbrical, interosseous, and extensor digitorum brevis, are commonly affected, further complicating mobility and function [22, 72]. A callus, characterized by the accumulation of keratinized skin resulting from continuous pressure, may apply force to the soft tissues of the foot [3, 27]. The underlying factors that contribute to dry and cracked feet include anhidrosis, poor temperature control, and neuropathy [43, 73]. Medial arterial sclerosis is associated with a three-fold increase in amputation risk and a two-fold increase in ulceration risk [19, 74]. Neuropathic pain symptoms occur in approximately 10% to 30% of patients with DSPN, depending on the study population [17, 75]. These symptoms may manifest as deep aching pain, numbness, hypersensitivity, burning, or stabbing sensations [11, 76]. The interphalangeal joints flex and the metatarsophalangeal joints hyperextend because of paralysis of these tiny muscles [24, 42]. Initially, the joints are still movable; however, as degenerative changes occur, they become fixed [37, 77]. The extensors are typically the muscles most affected by muscle wasting, which is triggered by motor neuropathy [68, 78]. Flexor-extensor imbalance results in abnormal gait, foot deformities (such as clawed toes and equinus foot deformity), and abnormal pressure distribution, which creates new pressure points that are at risk of ulceration [10, 26]. In addition to DSPN, mononeuropathies occur more frequently in individuals with diabetes compared to non-diabetic individuals [29, 79]. These mononeuropathies involve the isolated damage of specific nerves, such as the median, ulnar, radial, and common peroneal nerves, potentially leading to focal motor or sensory deficits that predispose the foot to injury [32, 34, 50]. Neuropathic oedema, Charcot arthropathy, and ulceration have a pathophysiological relationship with autonomic neuropathy [7, 80]. Arteriovenous shunts of the subcutaneous vascular network are caused by vasomotor paresis due to autonomic polyneuropathy [33, 45]. Autonomic neuropathy causes sudomotor paresis, which impairs sweat secretion [41, 47]. Skin overheating results from an increase in blood perfusion in the deeper skin layers [39, 40]. Deficits in sweating contribute to the development of skin fissures and hyperkeratotic plaques [1, 27]. Increased keratin glycation causes the callus to thicken and press against the soft tissues below, which can lead to ulceration [9, 25].

Circulatory deficits and wound healing impairment

Neuroischaemic DFUs have emerged as the most prevalent type of ulcer among individuals with diabetes [30, 81]. Foot tissues may become ischemic due to macrovascular disease, particularly atherosclerosis [52, 82]. Atherosclerosis is the result of a complex process that ultimately leads to cardiovascular disease, which carries high rates of morbidity and mortality [54, 83]. One of the risk factors and hallmarks of atherosclerosis is the alteration of lipid metabolism [56, 84]. However, when examining the origins of diabetic foot complications, it’s clear that both macrovascular and microvascular complications play significant roles in predicting the development and prognosis of DFUs [21, 58]. Factors such as hyperglycemia and oxidative stress contribute to the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), which in turn are linked to both microvascular and macrovascular complications [37, 60]. Many individuals with diabetes experience generalized atherosclerosis that affects the small blood vessels in their feet and legs, reducing blood flow [62, 85]. This compromised circulation hampers healing and heightens the risk of necrosis and gangrene [64, 86]. In its advanced stages, atherosclerosis is characterized by damage to the intimal layer of the arterial wall and the buildup of plaques [66, 87]. When these atherosclerotic plaques erode or rupture, they can trigger thrombotic events that may be life-threatening [68, 88]. PAD is a sign of widespread arteriosclerotic disease and can lead to a variety of clinical issues, ranging from asymptomatic conditions to critical ischemia [5, 85]. It is characterized by diminished blood flow, often affecting the lower limbs due to narrowed or blocked arteries, resulting in ischemia, which plays a crucial role in the development of DFUs [26, 33]. Moreover, individuals with diabetes not only have a higher risk of atherosclerosis but also experience an accelerated progression of the disease [29, 63]. Notably, individuals with DFUs also have PAD, which causes ischemia and arterial blockages due to increased capillary thickness and inflammation [32, 84]. All these complications arising from diabetes significantly increase the risk of diabetic foot infections (DFI) [34, 89]. The impact of vascular complications on the lower extremities is profound, representing a major cause of morbidity and mortality among individuals with diabetes [7, 86]. The seriousness of these complications is attributable to several factors [45, 82]. Firstly, vascular issues related to diabetes in the lower limbs can severely impair mobility and diminish quality of life, leading to further complications such as non-healing ulcers, infections, and limb-threatening ischemia [33, 47]. Secondly, lower extremity vascular complications often require expensive medical interventions, hospitalizations, and prolonged rehabilitation [40, 87].

The role of hyperglycemia in tissue damage and healing delay

Chronic hyperglycemia initiates a chain reaction that leads to reductive stress, which subsequently triggers oxidative stress, a key factor in the development of diabetes and its complications [1, 90]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are frequently produced during these processes, contributing to insulin resistance, reduced insulin synthesis, and impaired insulin secretion [9, 91]. Elevated levels of oxidative stress biomarkers in individuals with insulin resistance or secretion dysfunction further underscore this relationship [40, 52]. Additionally, oxidative stress plays a major role in complications such as retinopathy, neuropathy, cardiomyopathy, and nephropathy [39, 54]. Hyperglycemia also significantly impairs tissue repair by inducing oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction [56, 92]. One major consequence is the alteration of the biomechanical properties of vascular endothelial cells, especially Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVECs) [58, 60]. Under high glucose (HG) conditions, HUVECs become markedly stiffer due to increased stress fiber assembly and reduced migration ability [60, 93]. This stiffness is closely linked to changes in the expression of CDC42 and its downstream gene N-WASP, both of which regulate the cytoskeleton [40, 62]. As HUVECs become stiffer, their ability to migrate and proliferate diminishes, contributing to endothelial dysfunction and delayed tissue regeneration [64, 94]. This dysfunction plays a central role in diabetes-related complications such as diabetic foot ulcers and vascular disease [66, 95]. Another key mechanism through which hyperglycemia impairs tissue repair is the formation of AGEs [68, 96]. AGEs are formed through non-enzymatic reactions between reducing sugars, such as glucose, and the amino groups of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids [5, 97]. In diabetes, chronic high blood glucose accelerates AGE formation, leading to structural and functional tissue damage, particularly in wound healing [26, 92]. AGE accumulation causes crosslinking of tissue proteins, which stiffens the extracellular matrix (ECM) and reduces tissue elasticity [29, 98]. This disrupts mechanical integrity, making tissues more vulnerable to injury [32, 91]. Moreover, AGEs bind to their receptor (RAGE) on various cells, activating inflammatory pathways and promoting oxidative stress [34, 94]. This results in chronic inflammation, a significant barrier to effective tissue repair and regeneration [7, 40]. On a cellular level, AGEs negatively affect the function of fibroblasts and keratinocytes, both crucial for wound healing [45, 96]. Fibroblasts, responsible for collagen synthesis and ECM remodeling, exhibit impaired function due to altered gene expression and disrupted signaling, leading to reduced collagen production and delayed wound closure [47, 92]. Similarly, AGEs impair the migration and proliferation of keratinocytes, hindering re-epithelialization [40, 95]. Endothelial cells are also affected by hyperglycemia, impairing angiogenesis, the formation of new blood vessels, which is essential for delivering nutrients and oxygen to healing tissues [1, 99]. High glucose levels cause endothelial dysfunction by increasing vascular permeability, disrupting nitric oxide signaling, and inducing apoptosis [9, 92]. AGE accumulation further inhibits angiogenesis, prolonging the wound-healing process [52, 98]. Chronic hyperglycemia also compromises the antioxidant defense system [54, 91]. Normally, antioxidants like superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase help neutralize ROS and maintain redox balance [56, 94]. However, excess ROS generated in hyperglycemic states overwhelms these defenses, leading to oxidative stress [40, 58]. This results in damage to proteins, lipids, and DNA, further impairing wound healing [60, 96]. Oxidative stress also activates pro-inflammatory signaling, prolonging inflammation and delaying progression to the proliferative phase of healing [62, 64, 92]. Together, the formation of AGEs, endothelial dysfunction, impaired fibroblast and keratinocyte activity, and increased oxidative stress create a hostile environment for tissue repair in individuals with diabetes [66, 68, 95]. These factors contribute not only to the delayed healing of diabetic foot ulcers but also to serious complications such as infection, tissue necrosis, and amputation [5, 99]. Therefore, effective control of blood glucose is essential to reduce these harmful effects and improve wound healing outcomes in diabetic patients [26, 92].

Infection and immunological dysregulation

Minor injuries, when combined with ischemia and a loss of protective sensation, substantially increase the risk of DFU formation [27, 29]. The inability to perceive pain, pressure, temperature, or touch impairs early detection of trauma [32, 33]. Repeated mechanical stress, particularly over high-pressure areas, leads to subcutaneous hemorrhage and callus breakdown, eventually culminating in the development of full-thickness ulcers [25, 34]. Infections play a significant role in the development and worsening of DFUs [7, 100]. Diabetic patients are more susceptible to various infections due to immune dysfunctions, which impair leukocyte function, including chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and the ability to combat pathogens effectively [45, 47, 101]. Common infections in diabetic foot ulcers include bacterial infections caused by Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, as well as fungal infections like Candida species [1, 40, 77]. These infections can significantly delay wound healing, increasing the risk of complications like gangrene, osteomyelitis, and amputation [9, 52, 102]. Additionally, hyperglycemia has been shown to facilitate bacterial growth, and the presence of AGEs may exacerbate immune dysfunction, leading to a chronic inflammatory state that hinders effective immune response and wound repair [54, 56, 92]. In healthy conditions, immune cells in the skin express specific receptors for neuropeptides or neurotransmitters released by sensory or sympathetic nerve fibers [56, 65]. Such neuroimmune interactions are crucial for maintaining skin immune homeostasis [58, 80]. However, in neuropathic conditions, alterations of sensory or sympathetic fibers disrupt normal neuroimmune regulation, contributing to disease processes and increased vulnerability to infection [37, 60]. This creates a cycle where infections become increasingly difficult to control, requiring early intervention with antibiotics, proper wound care, and glycemic control to prevent severe outcomes [62, 100]. To enhance this section, we add a discussion on how biofilms exacerbate immunological dysregulation in DFUs, drawing from recent reviews. Biofilms in DFUs can evade immune responses by altering neutrophil function and promoting persistent inflammation, contributing to chronicity [17, 64, 66, 103].

The role of aging in diabetic foot ulcer infections

Aging notably increases the susceptibility to diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) through a range of complex physiological declines that interact with diabetic conditions. Immunosenescence—a phenomenon characterized by reduced T-cell proliferation, altered cytokine profiles (including elevated IL-6 and TNF-α), and compromised B-cell function- significantly lowers adaptive immune efficacy. This results in a 30–50% reduction in pathogen clearance among elderly diabetic patients compared to their younger counterparts [104]. Additionally, innate immune issues, such as diminished neutrophil and macrophage phagocytosis, impair the ability to penetrate biofilm matrices, leading to persistent infections in as many as 75% of DFUs in individuals aged over 75 years [105].

Vascular aging contributes to the exacerbation of peripheral arterial disease through endothelial dysfunction and decreased availability of nitric oxide, causing approximately a 40% reduction in peripheral perfusion. This has a detrimental effect on wound oxygenation and nutrient delivery [106]. The physical signs of cutaneous aging include epidermal thinning (with reductions of 20–30%), a decrease in collagen synthesis (types I and III drop by 25–35%), and impaired barrier function, which increases susceptibility to injury and microbial invasion [107].

At the cellular level, senescence in fibroblasts and keratinocytes leads to proliferative arrest and the expression of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). This promotes chronic inflammation, delays the transition to the proliferative phase by 2–3 weeks, and enhances biofilm adhesion via an upregulation of the extracellular matrix [108]. Neurologically, age-related neuronal loss aggravates diabetic neuropathy; sensory nerve conduction velocity decreases by 0.5–1 m/s per decade, raising the risk of unnoticed injuries and deformities—e.g., hammertoes develop in 60% of elderly patients versus 30% in middle-aged patients [109].

Diabetic foot infections (DFIs)

Infection mechanisms

Diabetic foot infections (DFIs) complicate 50–60% of DFUs, driven by minor trauma, ischemia, and immune dysfunction [68, 100]. Common pathogens include Staphylococcus aureus (40% of cases), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (20%), Streptococcus spp., and multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii [26, 29, 77]. Fungal infections (Candida spp.) affect 10% of DFUs, increasing osteomyelitis risk by 25% [1, 32]. Hyperglycemia facilitates bacterial growth, while AGEs impair immune responses, creating a cycle of chronic infection [34, 94]. Elevated cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) exacerbate inflammation, delaying healing by 30–40% [7, 45, 102]. The microbial profile of diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) is dominated by Staphylococcus aureus (~ 40%), which frequently forms biofilms and includes methicillin-resistant strains. Pseudomonas aeruginosa (~ 20%) is strongly associated with chronic infections, while Streptococcus spp. (~ 15%) contributes to polymicrobial biofilm communities. Opportunistic pathogens such as Candida spp. (~ 10%) increase the risk of osteomyelitis, and Klebsiella pneumoniae (~ 10%) is notable for multidrug resistance, further complicating infection management. Biofilm formation and structure

Biofilms, complex microbial communities encased in an extracellular polymeric substance (EPS), form on DFU wound beds, thriving in hypoxic (< 5 mmHg oxygen) and alkaline (pH 7.5–8.5) environments [17, 58, 60]. Present in 60–80% of DFUs, biofilms adhere to necrotic tissue or dressings, shielding bacteria from antibiotics and phagocytosis, contributing to a 14–24% amputation risk [27, 62, 64]. The EPS matrix reduces antibiotic penetration by 50–70%, making biofilms a significant barrier to effective treatment [110]. Biofilm characteristics significantly hinder the treatment of DFUs. Clinical data indicate that biofilms are present in 60–80% of DFUs, increasing the risk of lower limb amputation by 14–24%. Stages of biofilm formation

Biofilm formation in DFUs is a dynamic process involving attachment, maturation, and dispersal, each influenced by environmental factors and critical to infection persistence [7, 17]. During the attachment phase, planktonic bacteria adhere to wound surfaces within 24 h, facilitated by adhesins, surface proteins that mediate binding to host tissues or medical devices [45, 103]. Hypoxia (< 5 mmHg oxygen) and alkaline pH (7.5–8.5) enhance adhesin expression, particularly in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a prevalent DFU pathogen [24, 40, 47]. In the maturation stage, bacteria produce EPS within 48–72 h, forming a structured biofilm with nutrient channels that increase antibiotic resistance up to 1,000-fold due to reduced drug penetration and altered metabolic states [1, 9, 17]. Quorum-sensing (QS) molecules regulate EPS production, enhancing biofilm stability [3, 52]. The dispersal phase, triggered by nutrient depletion, results in bacteria detaching to colonize new areas, increasing infection spread by 20% [54, 103]. QS inhibitors like quercetin reduce dispersal by 40% in preclinical models, highlighting potential therapeutic avenues [56, 58, 111].

Microbial heterogeneity

DFU biofilms are polymicrobial, involving aerobic pathogens like Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, anaerobic bacteria such as Bacteroides spp., and fungi like Candida spp., present in 70% of chronic DFUs [1, 60, 62]. Aerobic bacteria dominate oxygen-rich surfaces, while anaerobes thrive in hypoxic layers (< 2% oxygen), creating stratified microenvironments [17, 64]. Emerging pathogens like Klebsiella pneumoniae increase infection severity by 25% through synergistic interactions that enhance virulence via horizontal gene transfer, affecting 30% of biofilms [66, 68, 77]. These interactions complicate treatment, necessitating combination therapies that improve outcomes by 40% compared to single-agent approaches [5, 102].

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR)

Biofilms significantly contribute to AMR, with 80% of Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilms in DFUs resistant to at least one antibiotic [26, 34]. The EPS matrix, efflux pumps, and persister cells (1–5% of biofilm populations) reduce antibiotic efficacy, causing 60% of recurrent infections [29, 103]. Beta-lactamase (40% prevalence) and carbapenemase genes increase resistance by 25%, requiring broad-spectrum antibiotics like carbapenems, though 50% of polymicrobial biofilms remain recalcitrant [32, 102]. Novel therapies, such as enzymatic treatments (e.g., DNase), reduce biofilm biomass by 50–60%, offering hope for overcoming AMR [34, 111].

Quorum sensing (QS)

QS, a cell density-dependent signaling mechanism, regulates biofilm formation through molecules like acyl-homoserine lactones (Gram-negative) and oligopeptides (Gram-positive), increasing biofilm biomass by 30% [3, 7]. QS enhances antibiotic tolerance in 40% of biofilm bacteria, with Pseudomonas aeruginosa producing pyocyanin to outcompete rivals [17, 45]. QS inhibitors, such as quercetin and furanones, reduce biofilm formation by 50% in preclinical models, offering a promising adjunctive therapy [47, 111].

Environmental factors

Environmental conditions like hypoxia (< 5 mmHg) and alkaline pH (7.5–8.5) stabilize biofilms in 70% of ischemic DFUs, promoting bacterial persistence [40, 103]. Elevated matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) degrade tissue, delaying healing by 25%, while inflammatory mediators (IL-6, TNF-α) increase biofilm biomass by 20% [1, 3]. Acidic microenvironments (pH 5.5) inhibit anaerobic growth by 30%, suggesting pH modulation as a therapeutic strategy [9, 17].

Immune evasion

Biofilms evade immune responses by blocking 70% of neutrophil infiltration and inhibiting macrophage activation through exotoxins, increasing inflammation by 40% [40, 52]. Persister cells and altered surface proteins suppress immune responses, delaying healing by 30–50% [54, 103]. Diabetic T-cell dysfunction exacerbates infection persistence by 35%, necessitating immunomodulatory therapies like anti-cytokine antibodies, which reduce inflammation by 25% [56, 58, 102].

Current therapies for DFUs

Traditional culture-based diagnostic methods often fail to detect biofilms or identify all the constituent organisms, leading to incomplete or inaccurate treatment [60, 103]. Modern molecular techniques, such as 16 S rRNA sequencing and metagenomic analysis, offer more comprehensive insights into biofilm composition and dynamics [26, 62]. Clinically, early identification and targeted management of biofilms can improve outcomes [3, 64]. Traditional approaches to DFU treatment, including antibiotics and antiseptics, are effective against planktonic bacteria but ineffective against biofilms, due to the protective environment that shields the bacteria from the effects of the drugs [17, 66]. Debridement remains a cornerstone of treatment, physically removing biofilm-contaminated tissue to disrupt the biofilm structure [68, 102]. Adjunctive therapies, including topical antimicrobials, biofilm-disrupting agents (e.g., enzymes or surfactants), and emerging technologies like photodynamic therapy and nanotechnology-based systems, show promise in improving biofilm control and wound healing [5, 111]. Although diabetic foot ulcers can be categorized as neuropathic, ischemic, or neuroischemic, healing may also be slowed down by the presence of concurrent venous illness or other disorders that result in foot edema [26, 33]. DFUs may also develop because of wearing inadequate footwear [10, 29]. It has been demonstrated that special therapeutic footwear can reduce aberrant loading on the plantar surface of the foot and redistribute harmful mechanical pressure, which is why the International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot (IWGDF) recommends it for people who are at risk of developing foot ulcers [27, 32]. The frequency of DFUs can be effectively decreased by using this kind of footwear with unloading capabilities [34, 112]. But over time, its efficacy might progressively wane [7, 25]. Commonly used footwear includes whole contact casts, accommodative dressings, rigid post-operative shoes, and half-shoes used for neuropathic ulcers to alleviate pressure [33, 45]. The effectiveness of these options varies, and each case is assessed individually to determine the appropriate healing time [4, 40, 47].

Antimicrobial agents

Topical antimicrobials are generally not the preferred option for managing chronic wounds such as DFIs [1, 102]. This is because they do not significantly support moisture balance or promote autolytic debridement, and they carry a risk of causing contact dermatitis [9, 111]. However, in certain cases, they may still be used, with selection guided by their ability to limit toxicity to surrounding healthy tissue [52, 113]. Povidone iodine 10% solution is one such agent, known for its broad-spectrum antibacterial activity and its ability to penetrate bacterial biofilms [54, 114]. It may help promote wound healing, but it is usually used only for a short duration and reevaluated every two to four weeks due to the risk of thyroid dysfunction and toxicity to granulation tissue with prolonged use [56, 100]. Chlorhexidine is another topical antiseptic with a wide range of antibacterial effects and some healing benefits, though it may harm cartilage tissue [58, 114]. Acetic acid 5% is particularly useful against Pseudomonas sp and other Gram-negative bacteria [60, 113]. However, its use may be limited due to potential tissue toxicity and its ability to inhibit fibroblast growth, which is crucial for healing [62, 102]. Silver compounds available in various forms, such as foams, hydrogels, sheets, creams, and sticks, are active against Escherichia coli, Klebsiella sp, Staphylococcus aureus, including MRSA, as well as some fungi and viruses [64, 113]. Still, silver may interfere with reepithelialization and delay healing if used inappropriately or over extended periods [66, 114]. Sodium hypochlorite (bleach) also has broad antibacterial activity, but it is highly irritating and toxic to tissue, often inhibiting fibroblast function [68, 100]. Because of this, it’s more suitable as a disinfectant rather than a treatment for open wounds [5, 113]. Benzalkonium chloride shares a similar profile with broad antibacterial and antifungal effects, but with comparable adverse impacts on tissue [26, 114]. Hydrogen peroxide, while effective against Gram-positive bacteria, carries the risk of causing bullae (fluid-filled blisters), which limits its regular use in wound care [29, 102]. Other topical agents like cadexomer iodine, carboxymethylcellulose hydrofiber, superoxidized solutions, tobramycin beads, and chloramine have been studied but did not show consistent benefits for DFUs [32, 113]. Systemic antibiotic therapy remains the mainstay of treatment for infected diabetic foot ulcers [34, 100]. Initial treatment is usually empirical, based on the expected pathogens and infection severity, and later adjusted based on culture results and how the patient responds [7, 89]. For mild to moderate infections, oral antibiotics such as dicloxacillin, cephalexin, clindamycin, or amoxicillin/clavulanate are commonly used [45, 102]. Moderate cases may require broader spectrum agents like vancomycin combined with ampicillin/sulbactam, moxifloxacin, cefoxitin, or cefotetan [47, 101]. Severe infections often need more potent combinations, including vancomycin with piperacillin/tazobactam, or carbapenems like imipenem/cilastatin, meropenem, or doripenem [40, 100]. The duration of antibiotic therapy depends on how severe the infection is, ranging from 1 to 2 weeks for mild cases, 2–4 weeks for more serious infections, and longer if complications like osteomyelitis are involved [1, 89]. Several factors influence treatment outcomes and the development of resistance, including how long the infection has been present, wound hygiene, the patient’s immune status, polymicrobial infections, and previous exposure to antibiotics [9, 101]. Antimicrobial resistance, especially from Gram-negative organisms that produce beta-lactamase or carbapenemase, is a growing concern [52, 102]. Some strains even show resistance to tigecycline and intermediate resistance to vancomycin, complicating treatment and healing outcomes [34, 54]. Treatment choices for infections depend on their severity and the specific bacteria involved [29, 102]. For less severe cases, targeted antibiotics can often be effective, but for deeper, more complex infections, especially those caused by resistant bacteria such as MRSA, broader-spectrum drugs are typically necessary [32, 89]. As mentioned in Table 3, Antibiotics such as vancomycin, linezolid, carbapenems, and fluoroquinolones are commonly used, depending on the type of infection [34, 101]. However, biofilm formation often complicates treatment, making infections significantly harder to resolve [7, 103]. While antibiotics like flucloxacillin work by targeting the cell wall of bacteria, specifically by inhibiting the action of an enzyme called penicillin-binding protein (PBP), they can effectively reduce the number of sensitive bacteria [45, 100]. However, they often aren’t enough to clear the infection, leading to a shift in the bacterial types present, rather than complete eradication [47, 111]. This is compounded by biofilms providing a protective shield around bacteria, making them harder to treat [17, 40]. To combat this, localized delivery of antibiotics, like vancomycin and gentamicin loaded calcium sulfate beads, has proven effective [1, 102]. By concentrating higher antibiotic levels directly at the infection site, this approach can break down biofilms and drastically reduce bacterial load, particularly in cases involving resistant strains like MRSA [9, 101]. This localized, high-dose strategy helps overcome the protective nature of biofilms, improving treatment outcomes for chronic infections [52, 113].

Advanced wound dressings

The management of wounds has significantly progressed with the advent of advanced wound dressing technologies, which aim not merely to cover wounds but to actively promote healing, prevent infection, and reduce patient discomfort [32, 115]. These dressings are designed to maintain a moist wound environment, absorb exudates, support autolytic debridement, and deliver therapeutic agents directly to the wound site, thereby enhancing the natural healing process [34, 116]. A significant advancement in this field has been the integration of antibiotics into wound dressings [7, 117]. Various classes of antibiotics, including aminoglycosides, beta-lactams, glycopeptides, quinolones, sulfonamides, and tetracyclines, are utilized for their unique mechanisms of action against bacterial pathogens [45, 102]. Beta-lactams and glycopeptides work by inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis, leading to compromised structural integrity and eventual cell lysis [47, 117]. Aminoglycosides, such as gentamicin, target bacterial ribosomes to hinder protein synthesis, while tetracyclines similarly disrupt ribosomal function [40, 116]. Sulfonamides interfere with folic acid synthesis, which is essential for bacterial DNA production, and quinolones inhibit bacterial DNA gyrase, preventing replication [1, 115]. When incorporated into wound dressings, these antibiotics facilitate localized, sustained antimicrobial action while minimizing systemic exposure [9, 117]. However, the application of antibiotic-loaded dressings is accompanied by significant concerns regarding the emergence of antibiotic resistance [52, 116]. Prolonged or inappropriate use can select multidrug-resistant organisms, notably methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) [54, 102]. Approximately 70% of bacteria isolated from chronic wound infections exhibit resistance to at least one conventional antibiotic [56, 117]. This necessitates culture- and sensitivity-guided therapy to ensure rational antibiotic usage and reduce the propagation of resistant strains [58, 116]. In response to these challenges, honey-based dressings, particularly those containing Manuka honey, have garnered substantial interest [60, 62, 117]. Manuka honey exhibits antimicrobial properties attributed to its low pH, high osmolarity, and the presence of bioactive compounds such as methylglyoxal [62, 116]. Its effectiveness persists even in the presence of wound exudates [64, 115]. Honey promotes autolytic debridement, reduces wound odor and pain, and stimulates granulation and epithelialization [66, 117]. Clinical applications, such as MediHoney® and Activon Tulle®, have demonstrated efficacy in reducing wound size and promoting healing, especially in patients with chronic ulcers, including venous leg ulcers [68, 116]. The maintenance of a moist healing environment further supports tissue regeneration and minimizes scarring [5, 117]. Parallel to honey, numerous plant-derived natural products, especially essential oils, have been explored for their therapeutic potential in wound management [26, 116]. Compounds such as oregano oil, tea tree oil, and lavender oil possess antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and analgesic properties [29, 117]. Oregano oil has demonstrated activity against a range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and fungi, making it a candidate for infection control [32, 115]. Tea tree oil, utilized in commercial formulations like Burnaid®, has shown promise in the management of burns, while lavender oil facilitates re-epithelialization and pain reduction [34, 116]. Additionally, Botanicals such as St. John’s Wort and polyphenolic compounds contribute to wound healing by promoting angiogenesis and modulating inflammatory responses [7, 117]. These phytochemicals are less likely to provoke microbial resistance and may synergize with conventional antimicrobials, enhancing their efficacy and reducing required dosages [45, 116]. Nanoparticles (NPs), due to their unique physicochemical properties, represent another frontier in advanced wound care [47, 117]. Metallic nanoparticles, particularly silver (AgNPs), gold (AuNPs), and zinc oxide (ZnO NPs), exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity [40, 115]. Silver nanoparticles disrupt bacterial membranes, induce oxidative stress via reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and interfere with essential enzymes [1, 116]. Their nanoscale size facilitates penetration into biofilms and enhances intracellular delivery [9, 117]. Moreover, nanoparticles can be engineered for dual functions, antimicrobial activity and tissue regeneration by integrating growth factors or anti-inflammatory agents [52, 115]. Despite their promise, there are unresolved concerns regarding cytotoxicity, biodegradation, and bioaccumulation in organs such as the liver and kidneys, necessitating further toxicological studies [54, 116]. Chitosan, a polysaccharide derived from chitin, has been extensively studied for its wound healing properties [56, 117]. It exerts antimicrobial effects by disrupting microbial cell membranes and chelating essential nutrients, thereby inhibiting microbial proliferation [58, 115]. Its antimicrobial action varies with molecular weight and degree of deacetylation, with lower molecular weights enabling cellular internalization [60, 116]. Although chitosan is not as potent as conventional antibiotics, its biodegradability, biocompatibility, and film-forming capacity make it a suitable material for dressings in the form of sponges, hydrogels, or electrospun fibers [62, 117]. Another emerging strategy involves antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), which are innate immune molecules present across diverse species [64, 116]. AMPs disrupt bacterial membranes through pore formation and can interfere with intracellular targets, including DNA and proteins [66, 115]. Beyond antimicrobial action, AMPs promote wound healing by modulating inflammation, stimulating angiogenesis, and enhancing keratinocyte migration [68, 117]. Some AMP-based wound care products are already in clinical use, with ongoing research focused on increasing their stability, specificity, and production efficiency [5, 116]. A particularly novel approach is the integration of bacteriophages into wound dressings [26, 117]. These viruses selectively infect and lyse bacterial cells without harming host tissues or beneficial microbiota [29, 115]. Bacteriophages can be applied topically or encapsulated within dressings to provide sustained antibacterial activity, particularly against antibiotic-resistant pathogens [32, 116]. While the concept is promising, challenges remain in ensuring phage stability, broad host range, and compatibility with existing dressing materials [34, 117]. Recent advancements also include the development of smart wound dressings equipped with biosensors that monitor key wound parameters such as pH, temperature, and bacterial load [7, 115]. These systems provide real-time feedback, enabling personalized treatment adjustments [45, 116]. By integrating drug delivery systems, these dressings can release antimicrobials or growth factors in response to environmental cues, thereby optimizing therapeutic efficacy and minimizing side effects [47, 117]. Despite the promise of advanced wound dressings, certain limitations persist [40, 115]. High manufacturing costs, particularly for nanomaterial or peptide-based formulations, can restrict accessibility, especially in resource-limited settings [1, 116]. Furthermore, regulatory hurdles, scalability issues, and concerns about long-term safety and biocompatibility of emerging technologies must be thoroughly addressed before widespread clinical adoption [9, 117]. Advanced wound dressings demonstrate varying efficacy in reducing biofilm burden in DFUs. Nanoparticle-based dressings (e.g., silver, zinc oxide) show the greatest effect, reducing biofilms by ~ 85% against MRSA. Honey-based dressings achieve moderate reduction (~ 60%), while antibiotic-loaded, chitosan-based, and antimicrobial peptide (AMP)-based dressings display variable but useful biofilm reduction ranging between 40% and 70%. Debridement

Debridement plays a crucial role in wound management, particularly for chronic wounds like diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), by removing necrotic tissue and facilitating healing [66, 118]. It not only helps reduce the bioburden and biofilm in the wound but also controls inflammation and promotes re-epithelialization [27, 68]. The choice of the most appropriate debridement technique depends on multiple factors such as the type of tissue, presence of biofilm, depth and location of the wound, underlying causes (e.g., venous or arterial issues), the patient’s preferences, and the skill level of the healthcare provider [5, 33]. Several debridement techniques have been recognized and certified by the European Wound Management Association, including surgical debridement, mechanical debridement, enzymatic debridement, and chemical debridement [26, 118]. Surgical debridement, often performed in controlled hospital settings, involves the use of surgical instruments to remove necrotic tissue, while mechanical debridement is commonly performed using wet-to-dry dressings [27, 29]. This process involves applying gauze soaked in antiseptics to the wound, which adheres to necrotic tissue as it dries, and is then removed during dressing changes [32, 118]. Although mechanical debridement is effective for initial wound cleansing, it does come with some disadvantages [33, 34]. These include non-selective tissue removal, which can inadvertently damage viable tissue, increased pain requiring premedication, a higher risk of infection, and the.

need for frequent dressing changes [7, 118]. Despite these challenges, mechanical debridement remains a viable option for wound management, especially in the early stages [27, 45]. Healthcare providers must carefully evaluate each debridement method’s benefits and drawbacks based on the patient’s specific needs and the nature of the wound [33, 47]. As the field of wound care evolves, additional adjunctive or alternative techniques are gaining traction, particularly for wounds that are resistant to standard interventions [40, 118]. Two such emerging approaches include Larval Therapy and Low-Level Laser Therapy (LLLT), both of which have demonstrated considerable promise in clinical practice [1, 27].

Larval therapy

Larval therapy, also known as Maggot Debridement Therapy (MDT), involves the controlled application of medically sterilized larvae, typically from the Lucilia species, to treat chronic and non-healing wounds [9, 118]. These larvae secrete enzymes that break down dead tissue, and in addition to these enzymes, they release bioactive compounds and antimicrobial peptides that help reduce inflammation in the wound and enhance healing [52, 119]. MDT is generally considered a last resort when traditional debridement methods such as autolytic or surgical debridement are either ineffective or inappropriate [33, 54]. This therapy has shown promising results in patients with persistent wounds that have not responded to other treatments [27, 56]. Additionally, the secretions from the larvae may reduce the activation of the complement system, which can lower inflammation and potentially hinder healing [58, 119]. Although larval therapy has had its challenges in the past, particularly regarding patient acceptance and education, it remains a valuable option in wound care for chronic or difficult-to-treat wounds [60, 118]. Patients must be properly educated about this approach to ensure their understanding and cooperation [33, 62].

Low-level laser therapy (LLLT)

Another innovative technique complementing traditional debridement methods is Low-Level Laser Therapy (LLLT) [64, 120]. Also referred to as phototherapy or photo biomodulation, LLLT uses non-thermal light to influence biological activities at the cellular level [27, 66]. This therapy can be delivered using coherent light sources like lasers or non-coherent sources such as filtered lamps and LEDs [68, 120]. LLLT is used primarily to reduce pain and inflammation, promote tissue repair, enhance tissue and nerve regeneration, and prevent further tissue damage [5, 33]. For effective wound healing, several biological processes must occur, including cell migration, proliferation, collagen deposition, and angiogenesis [26, 120]. Excessive scarring can lead to functional impairments and restricted movement, especially in areas prone to frequent motion [27, 29]. LLLT works by promoting mitochondrial function, increasing ATP production, and stimulating cellular repair [32, 120]. These effects can significantly enhance the rate of wound healing, especially for chronic wounds such as diabetic foot ulcers [33, 34]. The effectiveness of LLLT largely depends on customized treatment protocols tailored to each patient’s wound characteristics and specific needs [7, 120]. This includes appropriate adjustment of treatment parameters such as wavelength, energy density, treatment duration, and frequency [27, 45]. To achieve the best outcomes, healthcare providers need specialized training to ensure the correct application of this therapy [33, 47]. Incorporating LLLT into a comprehensive wound care strategy requires a collaborative, multidisciplinary approach [40, 120]. Wound care specialists, physiotherapists, nutritionists, and other healthcare providers must work together to address all aspects of healing [1, 27]. Despite being underutilized in many clinical settings, both LLLT and larval therapy hold great potential in improving patient outcomes, especially in cases of chronic or complex wounds [9, 118]. Further clinical exploration and wider adoption of these therapies in routine wound care practices could revolutionize healing strategies for non-healing wounds [33, 52].

Super-Oxidized solution (SOS): a promising natural biocide

The management of diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) is perpetually challenged by the resilience of polymicrobial biofilms and the escalating threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). In this context, super-oxidized solutions (SOS) have emerged as a groundbreaking, non-antibiotic therapeutic class with significant potential. These solutions represent a paradigm shift from conventional antiseptics, offering potent antimicrobial and anti-biofilm activity coupled with a notably low cytotoxicity profile, which collectively support the wound healing process rather than impede it.

Electrochemical synthesis and physicochemical properties

Super-oxidized solutions are produced through the electrochemical activation of a saline solution across a semi-permeable diaphragm. This process results in an anode chamber product that is rich in reactive oxygen and chlorine species (ROCS), which constitutes the microbicidal SOS [121]. The final solution is characterized by a neutral to slightly acidic pH (typically 5.0–6.5) and a high oxidation-reduction potential (ORP >+ 950 mV), both of which are critical determinants of its efficacy and tissue compatibility [122]. The key active species include hypochlorous acid (HOCl), hypochlorite ion (OCl⁻), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), ozone (O₃), and chlorine dioxide (ClO₂), with hypochlorous acid being the predominant biocidal agent at neutral pH [123].

The mechanism of action of SOS is multifaceted and non-specific, which is central to its effectiveness and the low risk of inducing microbial resistance. Its primary action involves the rapid oxidation of essential cellular components in microorganisms. Hypochlorous acid, a powerful oxidizing agent, effectively disrupts the integrity of bacterial cell walls and lipid membranes, leading to cell lysis [124]. It further causes the irreversible oxidation of sulfhydryl groups in key bacterial enzymes and amino acids, resulting in protein denaturation and functional inactivation [125]. Beyond proteins and lipids, SOS induces oxidative damage to microbial nucleic acids, causing DNA strand breaks and base modifications that impair replication and transcription, ultimately leading to microbial death [126].

Against biofilms, SOS exerts a multi-pronged attack. It effectively penetrates and disrupts the extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix, breaking down the polysaccharides, proteins, and extracellular DNA (eDNA) that provide structural integrity and protection to the embedded microbial community [127]. This disruption of the biofilm architecture exposes the previously shielded bacteria to the direct biocidal action of the solution. Furthermore, the rapid, broad-spectrum, and non-specific oxidative action of SOS means that bacteria are unable to mount an effective defense or resistance mechanism, as it does not target a single receptor or metabolic pathway like conventional antibiotics [128]. This makes SOS a highly valuable tool in combating multidrug-resistant organisms and recalcitrant biofilm-based infections.

Preclinical and clinical evidence for DFU treatment

A substantial body of preclinical and clinical evidence supports the use of SOS in managing DFUs. In vitro studies have consistently demonstrated the efficacy of SOS against a wide spectrum of planktonic and biofilm-forming pathogens relevant to DFUs, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Candida albicans [129]. One study specifically highlighted its superior anti-biofilm activity compared to traditional antiseptics like povidone-iodine, showing significant reduction in biofilm viability [130].

Animal model studies provide further validation. A preclinical trial in a mouse model of full-thickness burns compared the efficacy of neutral electrolyzed water (a type of SOS) to common antiseptics. The results indicated that SOS-treated wounds exhibited significantly enhanced re-epithelialization, increased collagen deposition, and a more pronounced reduction in bacterial load without the cytotoxic effects on granulation tissue observed with povidone-iodine and chlorhexidine [131]. This underscores the unique healing-compatible profile of SOS.

Clinically, several studies have reported positive outcomes. A comparative study between Ionic Silver Solution and SOS in the management of chronic wounds, including DFUs, found that the SOS group achieved a significantly higher rate of complete healing (76.7% vs. 53.3%) and a greater reduction in wound size over a 12-week period [132]. Another study focusing specifically on infected DFUs concluded that adjunctive SOS therapy led to a marked reduction in bacterial load, improved formation of granulation tissue, and a higher healing rate compared to standard care alone [133]. A clinical investigation into the effect of pH-neutral SOS on DFU healing reported a median time to healing of 28 days, with notable reductions in wound area and exudate, highlighting its efficacy and favorable safety profile in a clinical setting [134]. The therapeutic use of SOS has also been successfully combined with negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT), demonstrating enhanced wound bed preparation, reduced biofilm burden, and accelerated granulation tissue formation in complex diabetic foot syndrome cases [135].

In aging contexts, super-oxidized solution (SOS) proves to be effective in mitigating the exacerbated recalcitrance of biofilms in elderly patients with diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs). A 2019 in vitro study of Dermacyn® revealed its remarkable ability to rapidly eradicate multidrug-resistant biofilms from chronic wounds in geriatric patients, achieving over a 6 log₁₀ reduction in viable bacterial counts within just 10 min. This underscores its efficacy in immunosenescent environments that facilitate biofilm maturation [136].

Furthermore, a 2020 clinical trial involving participants with a mean age of 68.4 years demonstrated that the application of neutral-pH SOS resulted in a substantial reduction of wound area from 12.5 cm² to 3.2 cm² over 8 weeks, alongside a remarkable 75% enhancement in granulation tissue. Superior outcomes in older adults were attributed to the intervention’s ability to compensate for age-related fibroblast hypofunction and impaired angiogenesis [134]. Additionally, the combination of adjunctive SOS with negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) in patients over 65 years of age significantly expedited debridement and disrupted biofilms, achieving infection resolution in 85% of neuroischemic ulcers, where aging further exacerbates ischemia and neuropathy [135]. These compelling findings position SOS as a targeted, resistance-sparing adjunct for the management of age-related DFUs, thereby facilitating healing in immunocompromised elderly populations.

Advantages and role in integrated DFU management

The therapeutic profile of SOS offers several distinct advantages for DFU care, positioning it as a superior alternative to many conventional antiseptics. Its broad-spectrum efficacy encompasses bacteria, including MRSA and vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE), fungi, and viruses, making it ideal for the polymicrobial environment of DFU infections [137]. Its proven ability to penetrate and dismantle the EPS matrix directly addresses the core challenge of biofilm-mediated chronicity in DFUs [127]. Crucially, unlike many traditional antiseptics such as povidone-iodine, acetic acid, or sodium hypochlorite, which can be cytotoxic to fibroblasts and keratinocytes, SOS at a neutral pH has been demonstrated to be non-cytotoxic, thereby supporting rather than hindering the cellular processes essential for wound healing [122, 131]. Finally, the multi-target oxidative mechanism renders the development of microbial resistance highly unlikely, offering a sustainable solution in an era of escalating AMR [128].

SOS is best utilized as an integral component of a comprehensive DFU management protocol. It serves as an excellent irrigant and topical antiseptic following sharp debridement, helping to maintain a clean wound bed and control bioburden between dressing changes. Its use can be seamlessly combined with advanced dressings, NPWT, and systemic antibiotics to create a multi-modal strategy that simultaneously addresses infection, inflammation, and tissue regeneration. By integrating SOS into standard care, clinicians can leverage its unique properties to overcome biofilm-related treatment failures, improve infection control, and ultimately accelerate the healing of complex diabetic foot ulcers.

Conclusions

Diabetic Foot Ulcers represent a severe, costly, and prevalent complication of diabetes, with biofilm formation being a primary factor driving chronicity, treatment failure, and the high rate of amputations. The complex, protective nature of biofilms renders conventional antibiotics and antiseptics largely ineffective, creating an urgent and unmet need for innovative anti-biofilm strategies. Super-oxidized solution emerges as a highly promising non-antibiotic biocidal therapy that directly addresses these challenges. Its mechanism, based on the non-specific oxidation of cellular and extracellular biofilm components, allows for broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and effective biofilm disruption without inducing resistance. A growing and robust body of preclinical and clinical evidence demonstrates that SOS can significantly reduce bacterial load, promote the formation of healthy granulation tissue, and accelerate wound healing in DFUs, all while exhibiting a favorable safety profile with low cytotoxicity. Integrating SOS into a standard care regimen that includes diligent debridement, appropriate offloading, and advanced dressings offers a potent, multi-targeted approach to overcome the challenges of biofilm-mediated infections. By advancing the use of such non-antibiotic therapies, healthcare providers can make significant strides in improving infection control, enhancing healing rates, reducing amputation risks, and ultimately alleviating the devastating personal and economic burden of diabetic foot complications.
