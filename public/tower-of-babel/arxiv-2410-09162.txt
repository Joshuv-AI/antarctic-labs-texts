Extending the cosmic distance ladder two orders of magnitude with strongly lensed Cepheids, carbon AGB, and RGB stars.
J. M. Diego, S. P. Willner, J.M. Palencia

Abstract
Extreme magnification of stars near the caustics of clusters allow to detect individual luminous stars at high redshift. We show how deep exposures of caustic crossing galaxies at z ≲ 1 z\lesssim 1 with IR telescopes such as JWST, can detect non-explosive distance calibrators, such as Cepheids, carbon stars in the asymptotic giant branch or stars in the tip of the red giant branch. A large number of such detections, combined with careful modelling of the magnification affecting these stars (including microlensing), opens the door to using them as alternative standard candles to SNe in a redshift range that would extend the cosmic distance ladder two orders of magnitude for these distance calibrators. As a bonus, strongly lensed stars detected in deep exposures provide also a robust method of mapping small dark matter substructures since they cluster around the critical curves of small scale dark matter halos.

Introduction

One of the most interesting puzzles in Cosmology is the apparent tension in the expansion rate of the universe, or Hubble constant
H_{o}
, that emerges when this rate is measured through different observations. Fluctuations in the cosmic microwave background combined with the spatial correlation between galaxies when the universe was half its age prefer a relative slow rate of expansion of
H_{o}\approx 68
km  s
-1
Mpc
-1

(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
)

-1
Mpc

(
Riess et al. 2019
)

\approx 4\sigma

Multiple efforts are being made to make more precise measurements of
H_{o}
using alternative methods, often involving standard candles (SC), of which SNe are just one example. Other SC include Cepheids, Asymptotic Giant Branch (AGB) carbon stars, and the Tip of the Red Giant Branch (TRGB) which are used to estimate distances to nearby galaxies and calibrate the distance ladder
(
Riess et al. 2019
;
Freedman et al. 2024
)
. In this work we refer to these SC as non explosive SC or NESC, in order to differentiate them form SNe.
The range of distances over which the NESC can be applied is limited by their luminosity, which is orders of magnitude below that of SNe.

However, in specific circumstances, some of these NESC can be observed at cosmological distances comparable to those where most SNe are found. Thanks to extreme magnification near the critical curves (CC) of galaxy clusters, luminous stars can be detected even at high redshift. The first high-z star discovered this way was Icarus at
z=1.49

(
Kelly et al. 2018
)
, which was quickly followed by many more examples
(
Chen et al. 2019

Kaurov et al. 2019

Diego et al. 2022

extending the range of redshifts of strongly lensed stars up to

Welch et al. 2022

One reason is because this requires light curves with multiple epochs (for pulsating stars such as Cepheids), or a large number of stars from the same galaxy that allows to identify feature in color magnitude diagrams (CMD). A second reason is the depth of the observations since most of the NESC are expected to be very faint and difficult to detect even for telescopes such as JWST and for the large magnification factors present near the CC of galaxy clusters.

However, given these limitations, recent observations by JWST are starting to show large numbers of luminous stars in individual galaxies at

3
Probability of Magnification

The magnification experienced by the luminous stars in the Dragon is a combination of the galaxy cluster (or macromodel) magnification and microlenses in the ICM, whose area of influence (in terms of distortions in the magnification pattern) can intersect the line of sight of some of the stars. We can distinguish two regimes regarding the microlensing effect. In the optically thin regime a given line of sight to a bright star in the Dragon is unlikely to be affected by a microlens. On the contrary, in the optically thick regime, microlensing effects by stars in the ICM is likely.
The transition between regimes takes place when the effective surface mass density of microlenses is close to the critical surface mass density,
\Sigma_{\rm eff}=\mu_{\rm m}\Sigma_{*}\approx\Sigma_{\rm crit}
, where
\mu_{\rm m}
is the macromodel magnification and
\Sigma_{*}
is the microlens surface mass density.
At the position of the Dragon,
\Sigma_{*}\approx 30\,{\rm M}_{\odot}\,{\rm pc}^{-2}
, and for the redshifts of A370 and the Dragon

\Sigma_{\rm crit}=3640\,{\rm M}_{\odot}\,{\rm pc}^{-2}

(
Diego et al. 2024b
)
.
Hence, the the optically thick regime corresponds to values of
\mu_{\rm m}\gtrsim 120
. The macromodel magnification varies along the Dragon arc from values of a few tens at separations of the CC of a few arcseconds to very large values,
\mu_{\rm m}>>100
, at a fraction of an arcsecond from the CC. The exact location of the CC is unknown and different models predict different locations but in general most models predict that the magnification scales with the separation,
d
, to the CC as
\mu_{\rm m}=A/d
, where
A
is a parameter that varies along the CC position but takes values
30\gtrsim A\lesssim 100
when
d
is expressed in arcseconds.

Depending on the value of
\mu_{\rm m}
, or similarly, depending on the separation,
d
, to the cluster CC, the probability distribution function (PDF) of magnification and the role of microlenses varies. At large separations
d>2"
, in the optically thin regime, the macromodel magnification is
\mu_{\rm m}<100
and the PDF of the magnification has a relatively narrow peak near
\mu_{\rm m}
and a long tail scaling as
\mu^{-3}
at magnifications much larger than
\mu_{\rm m}
. Very close to the CC, when
\mu_{\rm m}>>100
, in the optically thick regime, the microlenses play a very important role in shaping the PDF magnification considerably. In this regime, microlenses introduce constant small scale perturbations in the deflection filed increasing its curvature. Since the magnification is inversely proportional to the curvature of the deflection filed, the most likely value for the magnification drops below
\mu_{\rm m}
and the PDF of the magnification becomes a lognormal. In between these two extremes the PDF of magnification transitions between the two shapes of the PDF as it is shown in Figure
2
, where we present the PDF of magnification at different separations from the CC (or similarly, for different values of
\mu_{\rm m}
) when microlenses with
\Sigma_{*}=30\,{\rm M}_{\odot}\,{\rm pc}^{-2}
are present. The curves are derived with the code M_SMiLe
Palencia et al. 2024a
;
Palencia et al. 2024b
which computes the PDF of magnification for any combination of redshifts,
\mu_{\rm m}
and
\Sigma_{*}
. One important property of the PDF of magnification is that when
\Sigma_{\rm eff}>>\Sigma_{\rm crit}
, the PDF becomes a universal lognormal similar to the last curve (yellow) in Figure
2
.This is an interesting property that we can exploit since it guarantees that at sufficiently close distances to the CC all events are affected by the same PDF of magnification, allowing robust forward modelling through the known PDF of magnification of the background luminosity function of stars. These models can later be confronted with the observations to extract the features characteristic of the NESC.

The curves shown in Figure
2
are obtained from inverse ray tracing of large simulations but with a finite pixel size. This pixel size is comparable to the size of a large star with radius
\approx 300
times the solar radius. Smaller stars can attain larger magnifications when they are crossing a microcaustic that are not being reproduced by the curves shown in the figure, so for these smaller stars it is possible to attain magnifications beyond
\approx 10000
. This very high magnification factors can be typically maintained for just a few hours to a few days, depending on the star radius and its relative velocity with respect to the microcaustic.

6
Discussion

The differences in the LF for different cosmological models are subtle, but we have seen how the sharp TRGB feature survives after being magnified although the measurement of the position of the lensed TRB break requires very deep exposures with JWST reaching 30.6 mag in F200W. The JAGB feature, despite corresponding to brighter stars, is broader and harder to recognize in the LF. However, a careful selection of carbon AGB stars based on their color or other spectral features may allow also to use them as NESC at this redshift. Cepheids on the other hand can be brighter and be detected at more modest magnification factors. In addition, their intrinsic and periodic nature may enable to identify them more easily, provided sufficient observations are made to construct their light curves. A challenge will be the contaminating effect of microlenses that will alter the Cepheid light curves. In most cases, these perturbations will be small since the time scale of variability of Cepheids (days to weeks) is much shorter than the typical scale of microlensing (years). In the very rare occasion where a Cepheid is close to crossing a microcaustic, where the change of magnification can take place in a short scale, this can also be identified thanks to the known dependency of magnification with time in microlensing,
\mu\propto 1/\sqrt{t-t_{o}}
, where
t
is time and
t_{o}
is the crossing time.
Perhaps the most serious challenge for Cepheids as NESC in strongly lensed galaxies is the unknown magnification from the macromodel, which is degenerate with the distance modulus that one is trying to measure. This degeneracy can be broken if multiple Cepheids are observed at relatively short distances from each other, in which case the unknown magnification can be constrained from the canonical
\mu_{\rm m}=A/d
law. Alternatively, Cepheids can be used to constrain the magnification of the macromodel at their positions, which in turn can be used to improve the precision of the lens model.

On the other hand, the brightest stars (orange dots) are distributed more uniformly across the arc. These bright stars require less magnification to be detected, and in some cases can appear as transients even without microlensing. This is the case of Cepheids, which can change their flux by a measurable amount within days or weeks. Detecting Cepheids in the Dragon offers a new avenue to constrain
H_{o}
in a redshift range never tested before with this type of standard candle. A challenge would be the unknown magnification affecting the Cepheids but this can be addressed statistically if sufficient Cepheids are observed. Also, Cepheids can be used to constrain the magnification (within some percent level), which in turn can be used in an analysis where both the lens model (its magnification) and the distance module to the Cepheids can be constrained iteratively. The specific details and limitations of such iterative study go well beyond the scope of this paper but will be addressed in future work. However, this possibility begs the question, how far can we see Cepheid stars with JWST? To address this question in a simplified way we consider the Spock, a galaxy at redshift
z=1.0054
that is highly magnified by the galaxy cluster MACS0416. At this redshift, the luminosity distance is
\approx 50\%
times larger than at
z=0.725
so the increase in distance module makes the same object appear
\approx 0.9
magnitudes fainter at
z=1.0054
than at
z=0.725

We can obtain a more quantitative sense by following a procedure similar to the one for the Dragon. In this case we focus only on the brightest Cepheids observed in the central region of the LMC. In particular we adopt the sample of
Riess et al. 2019
, containing 70 luminous Cepheids near the center of the LMC. Only a portion of the Spock galaxy is strongly magnified, so the smaller portion of the LMC with the sample of Cepheids considered here may be representative of that smaller portion of the Spock being magnified. From the I and H bands in
Riess et al. 2019

The sample of 70 Cepheid stars is magnified 10 times to mimic observations taken at 10 different times, with sufficient separation in time between them so the magnifications do not correlate in time (usually years apart). The resulting apparent magnitudes (after magnification) are shown in Figure

From this figure, highly magnified long-period Cepheids in Spock would be accessible with regular medium-depth observations (

The maximum magnification in this figure does not exceed a few thousand, but larger magnification factors are still possible, so in principle Cepheids could be observed in the Spock, at least during a microlensing event, with even shorter exposures. These events are often short-lived, with large magnification factors lasting a few weeks, but in some scenarios, large magnification factors can be maintained during several years. For example if a dark matter substructure is near the lensed stars producing long-term high magnifications. This type of rare scenario is suspected to be responsible for the long-lasting high-magnification of stars such as Godzilla

Long-term monitoring of these arcs will allow to confirm some of these transients as Cepheids, since they should reappear at regular times. Microlensing distortions to the magnification will certainly contaminate the light curves, but these are either subtle, with long-term variations extending years, or sudden if the Cepheid star crosses a microcaustic, with magnification which can be well modelled by the canonical law

7
Conclusions

We have simulated how the brightest stars in the LMC would appear if they are placed in the Dragon galaxy at redshift
z=0.725
and amplified by the galaxy cluster A370. The large magnification factors near the cluster CC, together with microlensing from stars in the ICM, amplify the flux of the stars making some of them detectable. In particular, we find than 1122 stars should be detected with JWST in observations reaching 30.6 mag in F200W. These stars would be detected as transients (microlensing events) in difference images taken
\approx 1
year apart.

A change in the slope of the observed luminosity function of stars is expected to be detected at
\approx 30.4
mag for
h=0.74
, and at
\approx 30.6
for
h=0.68
. This feature is due to the large number of RGB stars that start to be detected at this magnitudes.
These magnitudes are within reach of JWST in very deep exposures. Galaxies at lower redshift would show the break in the power law of the luminosity function at brighter magnitudes. For instance, a caustic crossing galaxy at
z=0.6
would show this break at a position 0.5 magnitudes brighter, requiring
\approx 2.5
less JWST exposure time. Cepheids should be also easily detectable at these magnitudes. Regular observations of the Dragon (or similar strongly lensed arcs) will allow t construct light curves of the Cepheids, and derive their period and luminosities. The degeneracy between macromodel magnification and distance modulus can be broken by combining several Cepheids from the same galaxy opening the door to using them as standard candles at high redshift.

We discuss how the large number of stars detected in deep exposures allows to trace with great accuracy the exact location of the cluster CC. More importantly, the faintest stars, such as the RGBs, will concentrate also around the position of invisible DM substructures which create their own small CC around them, allowing to map the DM distribution down to small scales.

Finally, we discuss the prospects of detecting Cepheid stars up to
z=1
and show how this is in principle possible, with galaxies such as Warhol and Spock already providing candidates to Cepheid stars at this redshift.
