Determinants of cervical cancer screening among women of childbearing age in four sub-Saharan African countries: insights from large population surveys
Authors: Enyew Getaneh Mekonen, Deresse Abebe Gebrehana, Tadesse Tarik Tamir
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer is the most common cause of cancer-related mortality among women in Africa. Cervical cancer screening in women is associated with decreased incidence and mortality of cervical cancer. There is a dearth of recent data regarding the prevalence and associated factors of cervical cancer screening in sub-Saharan Africa. Therefore, this study is intended to determine the prevalence and associated factors impacting cervical cancer screening among women in four sub-Saharan African countries.MethodsData from the recent demographic and health surveys of four countries in sub-Saharan Africa conducted between 2022 and 2023. Multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression was used to determine the factors associated with the outcome variable. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 were declared statistically significant.ResultsThe prevalence of cervical cancer screening among women of childbearing age in four sub-Saharan African countries was 8.90% (95% CI: 8.67%, 9.13%). Background

Cervical cancer is a significant public health risk for middle-aged women, especially in nations with less developed infrastructure [1]. The second most prevalent malignant tumor in women worldwide is cervical cancer, which poses a major threat to their health [2]. It is among the main reasons why women die from cancer [3]. Cervical cancer is diagnosed in about 500,000 women worldwide each year, and over 300,000 women die from the disease. 90% of cervical cancer cases happen in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) that don’t have access to human papilloma virus (HPV) vaccination programs or structured screening [4]. A persistent infection with sexually transmitted oncogenic strains of HPV is the most common cause of cervical cancer [5]. Ninety to hundred% of cervical cancer incidences in women, particularly in those under 35 years, are caused by oncogenic strains of HPV [6]. The World Health Organization (WHO) predicts that by 2030, cervical cancer will claim the lives of about 443,000 women annually worldwide [7].

Despite being one of the few diseases that may be avoided with straightforward diagnostics, cervical cancer strikes women in their prime, resulting in unnecessary misery and death for a significant portion of the global population [8]. The most common cause of cancer-related mortality among women in eastern, western, middle, and southern Africa was cervical cancer [1]. The absence of pathology services, inadequate healthcare infrastructure, inadequate diagnosis and treatment facilities, and a large exodus of highly qualified healthcare personnel are all factors impeding the treatment of cervical cancer in Africa [8]. Over 75,000 new cases and nearly 50,000 deaths from cervical cancer occur each year in various sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries, making it the most frequent malignancy among women [9, 10]. In underdeveloped nations, particularly those in the SSA, it has historically been given less priority [11, 12].

The World Health Organization recommends screening as a crucial cancer control and preventive method for all women aged 30 years and older, with screening starting even younger for certain high-risk groups, such as women living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), since cervical cancer is a preventable cancer [13]. Sustaining progress in preventing cervical cancer necessitates a persistently watchful strategy that involves providing all women with affordable, high-quality screening services as well as the recommended HPV vaccination [14]. Cervical cancer screening in women is associated with decreased incidence and mortality of cervical cancer [15]. Over the past few decades, there have been notable declines in the incidence and death of cervical cancer in the United States, which have been linked to the widespread implementation of population-based screening [16]. It is well known that screening a woman for HPV and cervical dysplasia can radically decrease her risk of dying from cervical cancer [17].

Previous studies conducted elsewhere showed that the prevalence of cervical cancer screening was 18.3% in Nepal [18], 54.7% in Japan [19], 19.0% in five SSA countries [20], 24.3% in South Africa [21], 16.81% in Kenya [22], 4% in Cameroon [23], 20.6% and 4.8% in Uganda [24, 25], and 2.9% and 13.46% in Ethiopia [26, 27]. Literature also showed that factors like women’s age, women’s educational status, health insurance coverage, socioeconomic status, residence, history of abortion, using contraceptives, media exposure, visiting health facility within the past 12 months, HIV status, community level education, community poverty level, ever undergone HIV screening, marital status, history of sexually transmitted diseases, having multiple sexual partners, knowing methods of cervical cancer prevention, employment status, HPV vaccination, knowledge about cervical cancer screening, knowing someone who had ever been screened, getting advice from health care providers, and women’s attitude were determinants of cervical cancer screening [18–27].

The incidence and death rates of the vast majority of nations showed trends toward stability or decline, particularly in those with successful programs for cervical cancer screening and HPV vaccination [28]. It’s time to create initiatives in Africa for cancer patients’ palliation, treatment, early identification, and prevention [8]. However, there is a dearth of recent data regarding the prevalence and associated factors of cervical cancer screening in the four SSA. Therefore, this study is intended to determine the prevalence of cervical cancer screening and identify factors associated with it using the most recent demographic and healthy survey (DHS) datasets (2022–23) from four SSA countries to give insight for health policy makers and program managers who work on non-communicable diseases to design interventions for the prevention and early detection of cervical cancer.

Methods and materials

Sampling, data sources, and study design

Utilizing the most recent DHS data (between 2022 and 2023) from four SSA nations, a cross-sectional study was conducted. Health and demographic surveys from four SSA nations: Ghana (2022), Kenya (2022), Mozambique (2022–23), and Tanzania (2022) were employed. In order to determine the pooled prevalence of cervical cancer screening and the parameters that are linked with it in the SSA nations, the data were appended. Each country’s survey has different datasets, such as those for males, females, children, births, and households. The women’s individual record (IR) file was employed in the current study. The DHS is a national survey that is primarily conducted in LMICs every five years. By using uniform methods for sampling, questionnaires, data collection, cleaning, coding, and analysis, it enables cross-country comparison [29]. The current study included a weighted sample of 60,352 women who were of reproductive age (Table 1). The DHS uses a two-stage, stratified sampling method [30]. The first step is creating a sample frame, which is a list of enumeration areas (EAs) or primary sampling units (PSUs) that encompass the entire nation. This list is typically created using the most recent national census that is available. The systematic sampling of the homes included in each cluster, or EA, is the second step. You may find more details on survey sample techniques in the DHS guidelines [31].

Study variables

Dependent variable

The outcome variable of the study was cervical cancer screening (yes or no) established by asking eligible women if they had undergone cervical cancer screening prior to the survey. This information was gathered by asking questions such as “Have you ever had a cervical examination?” and “Have you ever been screened for cervical cancer?” Questions for cervical cancer screening are available in more detail [32].

Independent variables

The current study examined variables at the individual and community levels by consulting earlier research [20, 22]. The following variables are included under the individual level: respondent age (15–24 years, 25–34 years, 35–49 years), educational status (no education, primary, secondary/higher), marital status (married, single), employment status (no, yes), wealth status (lowest, middle, and highest), media exposure (no, yes), breastfeeding status (no, yes), use of contraceptives (no, yes), recent sexual activity (never had sex, active in the last 4 weeks, not active in the last 4 weeks), distance to the health facility (big problem, not a big problem), and visit a healthcare facility in the last 12 months (no, yes). The following variables were included under the community level: urban or rural residency; low or high community literacy; low or high community media exposure; and low or high community poverty level (Fig. 1).

Description of independent variables

Wealth index quintiles are reduced to tertiles by combining poorest and poorer into one category, “lowest,” middle wealth level into the second category, “middle,” and richer and richest into the third category, “highest [33, 34].”

The term “media exposure” refers to the combination of a respondent’s reading of newspapers and magazines, radio listening, and television watching. It is coded as “yes” if the mother was exposed to at least one of these media and “no” if not [33, 34].

Community-level media exposure is the percentage of women who have been exposed to at least one media, such as a newspaper, radio, or television. It is classified as low (communities where less than 50% of women are exposed) or high (communities where more than 50% of women are exposed) based on the national median value [33, 34].

Community literacy is the percentage of women who have completed at least primary school, as determined by survey respondents’ educational attainment. After that, it was divided into two categories based on the national median value: low (communities where less than 50% of women have completed elementary school) and high (communities where more than 50% of women have completed primary education) [33, 34].

Community poverty level: recoded as low and high community poverty level correspondingly. Aggregated variable from household wealth status (percentage of women from poor and rich wealth status) [33, 34].

Data management and analysis

STATA/SE version 14.0 statistical software was used to clean, recode, and analyze data that was taken from the most recent DHS data sets. To control for non-responses and sampling mistakes, sample weight was used. After categorizing continuous variables, categorical variables underwent additional reclassification. The results were presented in frequencies and percentages using descriptive analysis. The DHS data variables were organized in clusters; 60,352 women are nested within households, which were nested within 1692 clusters. The assumptions of independent observations and equal variance across clusters were violated in order to apply the traditional logistic regression model. This suggests that accounting for between-cluster effects requires the use of a complex model. Multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression was therefore employed to identify the variables associated with cervical cancer screening. The null model (outcome variable only), model I (only individual-level variables), model II (only community-level variables), and model III (both individual and community-level variables) are the four models that multilevel mixed effect logistic regression uses. The null model, which is devoid of independent variables, was employed to examine the variation in cervical cancer screening rates throughout the cluster. The association of individual-level variables with the outcome variable (Model I) and the association of community-level variables with the outcome variable (Model II) were assessed. In the final model (Model III), the association of both individual and community-level variables was fitted simultaneously with the outcome variable (cervical cancer screening).

Through the use of the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) and proportional change in variance (PCV), the magnitude of the clustering effect and the extent to which community-level factors explain the unexplained variance of the null model were assessed. The model that suited the data the best was the one with the lowest deviation (Model III). Ultimately, variables associated with cervical cancer screening that had a p-value of less than 0.05 and an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) were deemed statistically significant. A variance inflation factor (VIF) was performed to rule out multi-collinearity of the covariates. The tolerance values were within acceptable bounds (1-10), demonstrating the lack of significant collinearity among independent variables.

Random-effect results

The methods of estimating random effects or measures of variation of the outcome variable were the PCV, ICC, and median odds ratio (MOR). The ICC and PCV were used to measure the variation between clusters. Using clusters as a random variable, the ICC indicates that ICC = VC/(VC + 3.29) ×100% represents the variation in cervical cancer screening between clusters. When two clusters are randomly selected, using clusters as a random variable, the MOR is the median value of the odds ratio between the areas of the highest and lowest risk for cervical cancer screening; MOR = 𝑒 0.95√VC. Moreover, the PCV, which is calculated as PCV = (Vnull-VC)/Vnull×100%, shows how variations in the prevalence of cervical cancer screening are explained by covariates. Here, Vnull represents the variance of the null model, and VC stands for cluster-level variance. The likelihood of cervical cancer screening was compared to individual and community-level independent variables using the fixed effects method.

Results

Childbearing age women’s characteristics at the individual and communal levels

In all, 60,352 women who were fertile were enrolled in this study. The average age of the participants was 29.08 ± 0.04 years, with 38.92% falling between the 15 and 24-year age range. More than half (59.33%) of women were married, and 46.80% of them completed secondary or higher education. Of the women, 57.89% had jobs, and nearly three-fourths (74.04%) had media exposure. Over two-thirds (66.90%) of women reported never using a kind of birth control, and 43.63% of them were the wealthiest. More than three-fourths (77.82%) of women didn’t breastfeed, and 50.24% of them were sexually active in the last four weeks. More than half (55.43%) of women visited healthcare facilities in the last 12 months, and 71.22% of them perceived that distance to the health facility was not a big problem. Concerning place of residence, 58.55% of women reside in rural areas. More than half (55.27%), 50.48%, and 51.39% of women were from communities with high literacy levels, high media exposure, and low poverty levels, respectively (Table 2).

Pooled prevalence of cervical cancer screening among women of childbearing age

The current study revealed that the prevalence of cervical cancer screening among women of childbearing age in four sub-Saharan African countries was 8.90% (95% CI: 8.67%, 9.13%) (Fig. 2). The prevalence of cervical cancer screening varied by country, with the highest prevalence in Kenya (13.91%) and the lowest in Ghana (4.60%) (Fig. 3).

In the final fitted model of multivariable multilevel logistic regression analysis (model III), respondent’s age, educational status, employment status, media exposure, wealth status, breastfeeding status, contraceptive use, recent sexual activity, visited healthcare facility in the last 12 months, residence, community literacy, and community media exposure were significantly associated with cervical cancer screening among childbearing age women.

The odds of cervical cancer screening were 2.85 and 5.30 times higher among women aged 25–34 years and 35–49 years compared with those aged 15–24 years, respectively [AOR = 2.85; 95% CI (2.57, 3.16)] and [AOR = 5.30; 95% CI (4.77, 5.88)]. Women who completed primary and secondary/higher education were 2.17 and 2.50 times more likely to practice cervical cancer screening than those who had no formal education [AOR = 2.17; 95% CI (1.91, 2.46)] and [AOR = 2.50; 95% CI (2.19, 2.84)]. Employed women were 1.24 times more likely to practice cervical cancer screening compared with their counterparts [AOR = 1.24; 95% CI (1.15, 1.33)]. Women exposed to media were 1.31 times more likely to practice cervical cancer screening than those who had no media exposure [AOR = 1.31; 95% CI (1.19, 1.45)]. The odds of cervical cancer screening were 1.38 and 2.23 times higher among women with middle and highest wealth status compared with those with lowest wealth status, respectively [AOR = 1.38; 95% CI (1.24, 1.54)] and [AOR = 2.23; 95% CI (2.02, 2.48)].

Breastfeeding women were 25% less likely to practice cervical cancer screening than non-breastfeeding women [AOR = 0.75; 95% CI (0.69, 0.82)]. Using contraceptive methods increases the odds of cervical cancer screening by 1.36 times [AOR = 1.36; 95% CI (1.28, 1.45)]. Women who visited a healthcare facility in the last 12 months were 1.58 times more likely to practice cervical cancer screening than their counterparts [AOR = 1.58; 95% CI (1.48, 1.69)]. Discussion

Teenage females and males can be vaccinated against oncogenic strains of HPV through widespread and very effective national programs that aim to reduce morbidity and death from cervical cancer and primary prevent cervical pre-cancers. By focusing on the populations in the nations surveyed that are most at risk and adhering to WHO Guidelines, this study aids in concentrating screening efforts. The main objective of cervical cancer screening is to lower the incidence, mortality, and treatment-related morbidity of cervical cancer by identifying curable abnormalities and pre-cancers that are likely to progress to invasive cancer [35]. The present study was conducted to determine the prevalence of cervical cancer screening and identify its associated factors among childbearing-age women in four SSA countries using the most recent DHS datasets. Accordingly, the prevalence of cervical cancer screening was 8.90% (95% CI: 8.67%, 9.13%). This finding was lower than studies conducted in Ethiopia (13.46%) [27], Uganda (20.6%) [24], Kenya (16.81%) [22], South Africa (24.3%) [21], five SSA countries (19.0%) [20], Nepal (18.3%) [18], and Japan (54.7%) [19]. On the other hand, it was higher than studies conducted in Cameroon (4%) [23], Uganda (4.8%) [25], and Ethiopia (2.9%) [27]. The possible justification for this discrepancy could be due to differences in media exposure, educational attainment, and socioeconomic status. The observed disparity may also be explained by cultural differences, since some cultural norms place limitations on women’s access to healthcare facilities. Furthermore, compared to developed nations, SSA countries may have a lower rate of cervical cancer screening due to financial constraints. Even though many SSA nations offer free cervical screening services, concerns about hidden costs and out-of-pocket expenses may make it difficult for people to take advantage of these services.

The current study also identified individual and community-level variables associated with cervical cancer screening. The odds of cervical cancer screening were higher among women aged 25–34 years and 35–49 years compared with those aged 15–24 years. This finding was in agreement with studies conducted in Ethiopia [26, 27], five SSA countries [20], Cameroon [23], and Japan [19]. One possible explanation for this could be that older women seek medical attention and screening services because they believe they are in danger of invasive cervical cancer. Additionally, women in this age range have a higher possibility of giving birth, having more gynecological exams, and learning more about sexual and reproductive health, including cervical cancer, which encourages screening services.

Educational impediments

Women who completed primary and secondary/higher education were more likely to practice cervical cancer screening than those who had no formal education. Likewise, women from communities with a high level of literacy were more likely to practice cervical cancer screening than their counterparts. This finding was consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia [26, 27], Cameroon [23], Kenya [22], South Africa [21], and Nepal [18]. Health literacy may be hampered by lower educational attainment because of a restricted capacity to read and completely understand the provided information [36]. Greater adoption of preventative measures is correlated with higher educational attainment [37]. Women who were exposed to media were more likely to practice cervical cancer screening than those who had no media exposure. Similarly, women from communities with a high level of media exposure were more likely to practice cervical cancer screening than their counterparts. This finding was in agreement with a study conducted in Kenya [22]. Women who are exposed to the media are more likely to learn about the financial and health benefits of cervical cancer screening, which could be one explanation. Exposure to the media can also help women become more aware of and knowledgeable about worldwide trends and viewpoints, empowering them to discriminate between material that is accurate and taboo in their culture. A favorable attitude toward the use of healthcare services, particularly the identification of cervical cancer, can be fostered by this greater knowledge and discernment. Women who visited a healthcare facility in the last 12 months were more likely to practice cervical cancer screening than their counterparts. This finding was in agreement with a study conducted in Kenya [22]. This could be because healthcare professionals may educate women about cervical cancer prevention strategies and promote screening. Additionally, screening might coincide with other medical disorders’ pre- or post-treatment phases.

Economic impediments

Employed women were more likely to practice cervical cancer screening compared with their counterparts. This finding was in line with a study conducted in Japan [19]. This might be due to the fact that employed women might have greater access to health screenings and information about health issues from their employers, which could contribute to the higher screening rate. The odds of cervical cancer screening were higher among women with middle and highest wealth status compared with those with the lowest wealth status. This finding was consistent with studies conducted in South Africa [21], Cameroon [23], Uganda [24], and Japan [19]. In a nation where access to health insurance is restricted, the high frequency of cervical cancer screening among women with a high income index may suggest that these women may afford screening services.

Behavioral impediments

Breastfeeding women were less likely to practice cervical cancer screening than non-breastfeeding women. This might be due to the fact that the experience of breastfeeding and the length of breastfeeding are linked to a lower incidence of cervical cancer [38]. It provides support for the promotion of breastfeeding and calls for more investigation into factors that can be changed to lower the risk of cervical cancer. Using contraceptive methods increases the odds of cervical cancer screening. This finding was consistent with a study conducted in Kenya [22]. One explanation would be that those who use contraception might be more likely to regularly interact with medical providers. Healthcare providers may have taken advantage of this chance to tell their patients about screening and treatment options during these exchanges. Women who were sexually active or not active in the last 4 weeks were more likely to practice cervical cancer screening compared with those who never had sex. This finding was in line with a study conducted in Ethiopia [26]. One explanation could be that a woman’s chances of contracting HIV and other sexually transmitted infections increase with being sexually active, as HPV is one of the most common risk factors for cervical cancer. They would therefore have a greater probability of contracting a sexually transmitted infection and experiencing its symptoms, which would raise the number of trips to medical facilities. Additionally, they might receive greater health counseling regarding HPV, invasive cervical cancer, pre-cervical cancer, and screening to encourage the use of screening services.

Geographic impediments

The odds of cervical cancer screening were higher among women who reside in urban areas than those from rural areas. This finding was consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia [27], South Africa [21] and Cameroon [23]. This might be due to the fact that, compared to women who live in rural areas, those who live in urban areas typically have easy access to health care. Women who reside in urban areas are also more likely to have access to media and healthcare facilities, which increases their chances of obtaining screening services, including cervical cancer screening.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The following are the strengths of this study: First, a large sample size drawn from weighted national representative data from four SSA countries is used in the study. Second, in order to account for the hierarchical nature of the DHS data, the study used a multilevel approach to produce a reliable standard error and estimate. Thirdly, because this study used national survey data, it can provide policymakers with valuable insights on how to develop and execute effective cervical cancer screening intervention strategies. The study also has some limitations. First, because the DHS survey relied on women’s self-reports, recall and social desirability bias may be present. It was also impossible to determine the temporal association between cervical cancer screening and independent variables because the survey was cross-sectional in nature. Finally, because the data were secondary, we were unable to include factors like women’s knowledge and attitudes toward cervical cancer and screening for the disease.

Conclusion

The pooled prevalence of cervical cancer screening among childbearing-age women in four SSA countries was found to be low. Being older, educated, non-breastfeeding, employed, wealthier, sexually active, and from communities with a high level of literacy and media exposure, using contraceptives, having media exposure, visiting healthcare facilities in the last 12 months, and residing in urban areas were associated with higher odds of cervical cancer screening. More support for women’s empowerment, regular visits to healthcare facilities, or focusing on undefined screening strategies for women of few financial resources living in often remote rural villages are recommended to improve cervical cancer screening practices among women of childbearing age. Since women who are or have been sexually active are at risk for infection from oncogenic strains of HPV, countries should sharpen their focus on screening for oncogenic HPV viruses.
