Predictively Oriented Posteriors
Yann McLatchie ⋆, Badr-Eddine Chérief-Abdellatif ⋆, David T. Frazier ⋆, Jeremias Knoblauch ⋆

Abstract
We advocate for a new statistical principle that combines the most desirable aspects of both parameter inference and density estimation. This leads us to the predictively oriented ( PrO ) posterior, which expresses uncertainty as a consequence of predictive ability. Doing so leads to inferences which predictively dominate both classical and generalised Bayes posterior predictive distributions: up to logarithmic factors, PrO posteriors converge to the predictively optimal model average at rate n − 1 / 2 n^{-1/2} . Whereas classical and generalised Bayes posteriors only achieve this rate if the model can recover the data-generating process, PrO posteriors adapt to the level of model misspecification. This means that they concentrate around the true model at rate n 1 / 2 n^{1/2} in the same way as Bayes and Gibbs posteriors if the model can recover the data-generating distribution, but do not concentrate in the presence of non-trivial forms of model misspecification. Instead, they stabilise towards a predictively optimal posterior whose degree of irreducible uncertainty admits an interpretation as the degree of model misspecification—a sharp contrast to how Bayesian uncertainty and its existing extensions behave. Lastly, we show that PrO posteriors can be sampled from by evolving particles based on mean field Langevin dynamics, and verify the practical significance of our theoretical developments on a number of numerical examples.

Often motivated through its favourable decision-theoretic properties, the primary goal of Bayesian statistical inference is to quantify uncertainty about a parameter of interest given a set of prior beliefs
(
Robert
et al. 2007
, e.g.)
.
All other inferential tasks are derivatives of this primary directive.
In the current era of black-box prediction models and the wide-spread adoption of machine learning techniques across science and society, this principle leads to a fundamental tension with Bayesian statistical methods: in many modern problems, prediction is the primary goal of interest, with parameter inference being only a secondary concern.
Because much of Bayesian methodology is
centered on parameter inference, its predictive performance quickly falters outside of the narrow conditions that make Bayesian belief updates optimal
(
Zellner 1988
;
Aitchison 1975
, see)
.
In other words, the practice of Bayesian statistics is overwhelmingly focused on parameter inference, not prediction.

One of the clearest ways in which this manifests is
posterior collapse
: as data accumulate, the Bayes posterior generally concentrates around a single parameter value
(see e.g
van der Vaart 1998
, Section 10.2)
.
This is sensible for parameter inference when

uncertainty in a parameter of interest arises due to missing observations.
If a complete population is observed, then the parameter of interest can be assumed to be known precisely.

(
Fong
et al. 2023
)

In the context of prediction however, posterior collapse is generally not desirable: it implies that as the sample size increases, marginalising over the Bayes posterior ceases to function as a genuine combination of individual predictive distributions.
Instead of averaging over a model class, such predictive distributions essentially predict from a single model.
Crucially, this behaviour persists even for generalised Bayesian methods that replace a likelihood-based approach with a loss-based belief update
(
Bissiri
et al. 2016
;
Jiang and
Tanner 2008
;
Zhang 2006
;
Knoblauch
et al. 2022
;
Alquier 2024
;
Syring and
Martin 2019
;
Martin and
Syring 2022
)

As a result, the posterior predictives obtained from both classical and generalised Bayes approaches quickly resemble those obtained from a point estimator as sample size grows
(
McLatchie et al. 2025
)
.

To better understand the impact of this phenomenon on prediction, it is useful to distinguish between two regimes:
when a statistical model is well-specified so that the data-generating process is recoverable by a singular parameter value, posterior concentration is the desired outcome:
as more data accumulates, uncertainty contracts around the true parameter, and the posterior predictive distribution is well-calibrated.
In practice however, statistical analysis is almost always subject to some degree of model misspecification.
In this regime, no single parameter value is capable of
describing the data generating process,
so that the posterior predictives based on standard or generalised Bayesian posteriors become poorly calibrated
(
Syring and
Martin 2019
)
.
Importantly, this issue is not caused by obtaining predictive distributions through the marginalisation of a posterior, but by marginalising over a posterior which collapses to a point mass.
This in turn provides strong motivation for methods whose parameter uncertainty can be guaranteed
not
to collapse to a point mass when this

is appropriate and desirable, since […] some uncertainty always remains about the true (idealised) distribution, no matter how much data are observed.

(
Miller and
Dunson 2019
)

In this paper, we synthesise the above discussion into a new statistical principle for inference that results in a
predictively oriented
(
PrO
) posterior.
The resulting approach is distinct from existing inference frameworks in two main ways:
first, it treats prediction as its main goal without requiring a black box approach, while naturally inducing uncertainty quantification for a parameter of interest as a secondary inferential outcome.
Second, it is
adaptive
:
the
PrO
posterior collapses to a point mass only in the appropriate setting where the model is well-specified.
When the model is misspecified however,
PrO
posteriors acknowledge that no single parameter is sufficient for capturing the true data-generating process, and yield an irreducible level of uncertainty, even with increasing sample size.

Conceptually,
PrO
posteriors represent a useful intermediary between existing statistical paradigms and compromise between the two tenants of
data modelling
and
algorithmic modelling
sub-cultures in statistics that were famously identified by
Breiman 2001
.
In practical terms, this means that
PrO
posteriors reconcile parameter inference and predictive inference in situations where the choice between either is difficult.
Intriguingly, they do so through a posterior that expresses epistemological uncertainty in a way that is meaningfully distinct from standard and generalised Bayesian approaches.

We strengthen this intuitive appeal by demonstrating that
PrO
posteriors possess strong theoretical guarantees and an algorithmically viable approach for computation.
In particular, we formally establish that
PrO
posteriors predictively dominate classical and generalised Bayesian posteriors.
Further, we characterise the type of parameter uncertainty they confer, and show that
PrO
posteriors are adaptive to model misspecification.
Lastly, we introduce a sampling algorithm which can be used to sample from any such posterior, and illustrate its performance across a range of examples.

In the remainder, we motivate the need for
PrO
posteriors in more detail (Section
1
), develop new mathematical tools for our theoretical analysis (Section
2
), and then present our main findings (Section
3
).
A full discussion of all results and the assumptions we use is deferred to the end of the paper (Section
7
).
We then introduce computationally feasible algorithms for
PrO
posteriors (Section
4
) and by illustrating their theoretical performance on a number of numerical examples (Section
5
) before concluding with a detailed discussion of our findings (Section
6
).

1
Motivation

Throughout, we consider data
{x}_{1:n}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
for
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
and
i=1,\dots,n
.
We assume that each
x_{i}
was generated independently from an unknown distribution
P_{0}
, and denote expectations of a function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to
P_{0}
by
\mathbb{E}[f(X)]
or by
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{0}}[f(X)]
if we wish to emphasise the sampling distribution.
To learn about
P_{0}
from
x_{1:n}
, we follow the tenants of classical Bayesian analysis, and posit a model class
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}:=\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
and a prior
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
over elements of
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
, where
\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
denotes the space of probability measures on
\Theta
.

Instead of attempting to learn the best possible
parameter
, as in classical (or even generalised) Bayesian inference, we focus on learning the best possible
predictive
distribution.
To rigorously formulate this optimality, we use scoring rules.

Definition 1

(Scoring Rule)
.

Let
S:\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be an extended real-valued function. Then
S
is a proper scoring rule, relative to a class
\mathcal{P}
, if:

(i)

the expected score
\mathcal{S}(P,P^{\prime}):=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P^{\prime}}\left\{S(P,X)\right\}
, is finite for some
P,P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
;

(ii)

\mathcal{S}(P^{\prime},P^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{S}(P,P^{\prime})
for all
P,P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
, so that
\mathcal{S}
induces the statistical divergence

\mathcal{D}_{S}(P,P^{\prime}):=\mathcal{S}(P,P^{\prime})-\mathcal{S}(P^{\prime},P^{\prime})\geq 0
for which
\mathcal{D}_{S}(P,P^{\prime})=0\Longleftrightarrow P=P^{\prime}
.

Scoring rules are foundational statistical objects concerned with measuring accuracy of predictive distributions (see, e.g.,
Gneiting and
Raftery 2007
). Given a target distribution
P_{0}
, a candidate predictive distribution
P
is judged to be more accurate than an alternative candidate
P^{\prime}
by an expected score
\mathcal{S}
if and only if
\mathcal{S}(P,P_{0})\leq\mathcal{S}(P^{\prime},P_{0})
.
The logarithmic scoring rule, given by

S(P,x)=-\log P(x)
, is the most common example and is related to the Kullback-Leibler divergence via
\mathrm{KL}(P^{\prime}\|P)=\mathcal{D}_{S}(P,P^{\prime})
.
Other popular choices include
the spherical score, cumulative ranked probability score (CRPS), the energy score, and squared maximum mean discrepancy (MMD), which for a kernel function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and the Dirac measure at
x
denoted by
\updelta_{x}

is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(P,\updelta_{x})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P,X^{\prime}\sim P}[k(X,X^{\prime})]-2\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[k(X,x)].

Throughout the paper, we restrict our analysis to any score that satisfies the following minimal convexity assumption, which is satisfied for all scoring rules mentioned above.

Assumption 1
.

The scoring rule
S(\cdot,x)
is convex in its first argument for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and its corresponding divergence
\mathcal{D}_{S}
is jointly convex in both arguments.

1.1
Key Ideas

When
P_{\theta}
has density
p_{\theta}
, the Bayes posterior
\Pi_{n}
and its associated posterior predictive distribution are

\displaystyle\Pi_{n}(A):=\frac{\int_{A}p_{\theta}(x_{1:n})\mathsf{d}\Pi(\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}p_{\theta}(x_{1:n})\mathsf{d}\Pi(\theta)},\quad\quad P_{\Pi_{n}}:=\int_{\Theta}P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}\Pi_{n}(\theta).

In this set-up, the predictive distribution
P_{\Pi_{n}}
is derived as a consequence of the Bayes posterior
\Pi_{n}
—which itself is primarily geared towards quantifying the uncertainty about which parameter
\theta
best describes the observed data
x_{1:n}
.
In this paper, we reverse that order: the posterior predictive
P_{Q}:=\int_{\Theta}P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)
is the object of interest, and we obtain a predictively oriented (
PrO
) posterior as the measure
Q_{n}
which quantifies uncertainty about
\theta
in a manner that is optimal for prediction.

The key motivation for this novel methodology is that while
P_{\Pi_{n}}
is predictively optimal if
P_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}

(
Aitchison 1975
, see)
, this optimality breaks down once
P_{0}\notin\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
.
Indeed, the Bayes posterior predictive can yield extremely poor predictive performance under even seemingly innocuous model misspecification (see, e.g.,
Grünwald and
van Ommen 2017
).
Though this is well-understood, robust Bayesian methods usually still treat prediction as a second-order concern, and are more concerned with parameter uncertainty.
One family emblematic of this kind of research are
Gibbs posteriors
.

(
Jiang and
Tanner 2008
;
Bissiri
et al. 2016
;
Zhang 2006
;
Alquier 2024
;
Syring and
Martin 2019
;
Martin and
Syring 2022
;
Knoblauch
et al. 2018
;
Schmon
et al. 2020
;
Chérief-Abdellatif and Alquier 2020
;
Matsubara et al. 2022
;
Matsubara et al. 2023
;
Altamirano
et al. 2023a
;
Altamirano
et al. 2023b
;
McLatchie et al. 2025
;
Duran-Martin
et al. 2024
;
Laplante et al. 2025
;
Frazier et al. 2025
, e.g.)
.
Many of these posteriors are based on an scoring rule evaluated on the sample, and given by, for
A\subseteq\Theta
,

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{\dagger}(A)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{A}\exp\left\{-\frac{\lambda_{n}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S(P_{\theta},x_{i})\right\}\mathsf{d}\Pi(\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left\{-\frac{\lambda_{n}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S(P_{\theta},x_{i})\right\}\mathsf{d}\Pi(\theta)},\quad\quad P_{Q_{n}^{\dagger}}:=\int_{\Theta}P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q_{n}^{\dagger}(\theta),

where the tuning parameter
\lambda_{n}
is often called the
learning rate
.
Using Gibbs posteriors for parameter inference is often called
generalised Bayesian inference
because they recover the Bayes posterior for the special case where
S
is the logarithmic scoring rule and
\lambda_{n}=n
.
To contrast them with
PrO
posteriors, it will prove instructive to rewrite Gibbs posteriors as the outcome of the following variational optimisation problem:

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\left\{\frac{\lambda_{n}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int S(P_{\theta},x_{i})\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)+\mathrm{KL}(Q\|\Pi)\right\}.

(1)

The above representation is well-established
(
Knoblauch
et al. 2022
, see e.g., Theorem 1 of)
,
and makes clear that
all
Gibbs posteriors—including the Bayes posterior—seek to minimise the averaged scoring rule, rather than the score of the averaged model.
This in turn means that the inferences they produce are targeted towards finding the
parameter
, rather than the
predictive
, which best describes the data.
Crucially, these two goals do not overlap outside of well-specified models, where
P_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}

(
Aitchison 1975
, see)
.
The result is that in empirical applications, Bayes posteriors and Gibbs posteriors alike often deliver poorly performing posterior predictives.

What then should we do if we wish to predict from a parametric model, but also suspect that
P_{0}\notin\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
? Now, we argue that the quantity of interest should be the
predictive

P_{Q}:=\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)
,
and should thus be scored
directly
. Doing so gives rise to the
PrO
posterior given by

{Q}_{n}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\left\{\frac{\lambda_{n}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{S}\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),x_{i}\right)+\mathrm{KL}(Q\|\Pi)\right\},

where we write
\widehat{S}\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),x_{i}\right)
instead of
{S}\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),x_{i}\right)
to highlight that throughout our theoretical and methodological development,
PrO
posteriors can be computed based on either exact or approximate predictive scoring rules.

1.2
Initial Comparison: Gibbs Posteriors vs PrO Posteriors

As we show later,
PrO
posteriors have several important properties, but the most fundamentally useful property is that they are
adaptive
: in contrast to Bayes and Gibbs posteriors,
PrO
posteriors adapt to model misspecification.
We illustrate what this means by comparing the predictive distributions associated to
Q_{n}
and
Q_{n}^{\dagger}
under
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}=\{P_{\theta}:\,\mathsf{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2}=1)\}
for a range of data distributions.
This model class is particularly educational here as the predictive is obtained from the posterior via smoothing with a Gaussian kernel with unit scaling.
For predictive scores based on squared MMD, Figure
1

plots the results for
the well-specified setting where

P_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
and three misspecified settings where
P_{0}\notin\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
.
While the
PrO
posterior behaves similarly to the MMD-based Gibbs posterior if the model is well-specified, it
adapts
to misspecification, and continues to produce reasonable predictive distributions across each setting.
In contrast, Gibbs posteriors are blind to the mismatch between model and data.

As our example uses Gaussian likelihoods, one may misinterpret the phenomenon displayed as a cherry-picked pathology.
In parametric models, however, Bayes posteriors and Gibbs posteriors are usually asymptotically Gaussian, and will concentrate around a single element of
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
.
As we formally show later on, this property is not shared by
PrO
posteriors. On the contrary, depending on the severity of misspecification, they can be multi-modal and need not concentrate around a single element of
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
.
Instead, they stabilise towards the predictively optimal average of elements in
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
for
P_{0}
. As a result—and unlike Bayes posteriors and Gibbs posteriors—
PrO
posteriors produce predictively meaningful parameter uncertainty even under severe forms of misspecification.

1.3
PrO Posteriors: A Third Modelling Culture?

In his prescient contribution,
Breiman 2001
identified two divergent sub-cultures within statistical science.
On the one hand, he spoke of a
data modelling culture
interested in hand-tailoring interpretable models for a given research problem and thus strongly associated to classical statistical concerns.
On the other hand, he identified an increasing interest in machine learning and the associated
algorithmic modelling culture
focused on predictive accuracy and black box density estimation techniques.
Does their reliance on a parametric base model
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
make
PrO
posteriors part of the data modelling culture?
Or does their focus on predictive performance make them into an offshoot of machine learning and the associated algorithmic modelling culture?

We believe that
PrO
posteriors constitute a meaningful and novel attempt to incorporate the most useful aspects of both modelling cultures: they rely on interpretable parameters born out of the data modelling culture, but gear inference towards predictive accuracy.
When both interpretability and predictive performance are relevant, practitioners are often forced into a fraught choice:
should they prefer an interpretable but predictively suboptimal model;
or should they resort to a black box model that will improve predictive performance at the cost of interpretability and meaningful guarantees?

PrO
posteriors do not force us into this problematic binary choice:
they rely on interpretable parameters born out of the data modelling culture, but gear inference towards predictive accuracy, and as a result perform well on both tasks.
In Figure
2
, we illustrate this by studying body measurements for different penguin species in the Palmer Archipelago
(
Horst
et al. 2020
)
.
The
PrO
posterior not only identifies the data set’s fundamental structure by correctly discovering the three different species in it, but also produces a predictive distribution of excellent quality.

. We plot both the
kernel density estimate (KDE)
and the
PrO
posterior predictive

PrO
posterior
overlaid with the average measurements for each of the three penguin species in the data set (right).
For inference with the

The results show that the
PrO

For further details, see Appendix
D.2
.

As we shall see in the remainder, this behaviour is not an accident:

PrO
posteriors provide an impactful third way of learning from data that combines predictive accuracy with interpretable uncertainty estimates. This behaviour is desirable for a wide array of scientific problems, including in digital twins, simulation models, causal modelling with observational data in public health and medicine, and forecasting applications in energy markets and finance.
In operationalising a compromise between parameter inference and predictive accuracy,
PrO
posteriors also yield an altogether new kind of uncertainty quantification different from that of classical approaches.
The next section elaborates on this, and explains why we can think of
PrO
posteriors as providing a novel type of
epistemological uncertainty
beyond what is currently attainable in classical Bayesian inference.

1.4
The Nature of PrO Posterior Uncertainty

To start, it is worth contrasting
PrO
posterior uncertainty expressed with a set of ideas and methods known under the umbrella of
predictive Bayes

(
Berti
et al. 2021
;
Fortini and
Petrone 2020
;
Fortini and
Petrone 2023
;
Fong
et al. 2023
;
Fortini and
Petrone 2025
, see e.g.)
.
Despite similar sounding names, the roles played by prediction in the construction of
PrO
posteriors and predictive Bayes approaches are fundamentally different:
the
PrO
posterior is geared towards
learning
the predictive from
seen data
, predictive Bayes approaches instead
posits
a sequence of predictive distributions to model the
unseen data
.
Put differently, while
PrO
posteriors make the predictive distribution the object of
posterior inference
, predictive Bayes methods specify a predictive distribution instead of a prior and likelihood.

In fact, specifying priors and likelihoods is just a convenient way of specifying a sequence of predictive distributions on unseen data:
formalising this in a recent read paper at the Royal Statistical Society,
Fong
et al. 2023
used Doob’s Theorem to argue that the ultimate source of Bayesian uncertainty is unseen data.
Taking this to its logical extreme, if we observe infinitely many data points, no uncertainty about the data remains and we can be perfectly confident in the conclusions we draw from data.
This is in fact true more generally, as similar reasoning can be applied to various generalisations of Bayesian uncertainty, including the uncertainty expressed by Gibbs posteriors.
One of the key implications is that both Bayesian and generalised Bayesian inference schemes result in a form of epistemological uncertainty that vanishes as the number of observations increase.

But should epistemological uncertainty indeed vanish as more data are observed? Though it may be tempting to answer this question in the affirmative, doing so would overlook an important practical limitation: for interpretability and computational reasons, statistical methods express epistemological uncertainty relative to a parameter
\theta\in\Theta
.
Crucially, this implies that
the only
setting in which larger sample sizes should lead to a decrease in epistemological uncertainty is the rare situation where a
singular
value of
\theta\in\Theta
can summarise all properties of
P_{0}
that we are interested in.
In other words, the kind of epistemological uncertainty conferred by (generalised) Bayesian methods must always be viewed relative
only
to the model class, not with respect to the choice of model class itself, rendering inference and particularly uncertainty quantification unreliable whenever
P_{0}\notin\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
.

The epistemological uncertainty produced by
PrO
posteriors is more expansive: it delivers both parameter uncertainty
and
uncertainty about the inherent ability of
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
to adequately explain the observed data.
Indeed, when the model is misspecified,
PrO
posteriors generally
do not collapse
to a single element of
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
as more data are observed.
When
P_{0}\notin\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
, they instead stabilise towards an irreducible form of uncertainty that originates in the fact that
no single value
of
\theta\in\Theta
is sufficient to summarise all properties of
P_{0}
we take an interest in.
This is epistemological uncertainty in its truest form: because the model is misspecified, we
should
be uncertain about its ability to represent the observed data, even if we had observed the entire population
P_{0}
.

But what if the model is well-specified, so that
P_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
?
In this case, the epistemological uncertainty produced by Bayes and Gibbs posteriors is sufficient: there exists some
\theta\in\Theta
that allows us to completely describe the observed data—and as more data accumulates, we should become increasingly certain about the ability of this value to correctly explain the data, and hence
P_{0}
.
As it turns out,
PrO
posteriors square the circle: in the special case where the model is well-specified, they
do
behave like Bayes and Gibbs posteriors, and concentrate towards the parameter value that recovers
P_{0}
.

2
Background and Theoretical Groundwork

To set the stage for our theoretical developments, we introduce three key ideas that guide our methodological and theoretical innovations.
In Section
2.1
, we study representations and approximations of scoring rules whose structure leads to a tractable objective, from which we derive a novel PAC-Bayes type bound in Section
2.2
, which serves as the foundation of our investigation into the predictive performance of
PrO
posteriors.

2.1
Exact and Approximate Representations of Predictive Scores

To simplify language and to highlight that
PrO
posteriors apply scoring rules to the predictive distribution
P_{Q}=\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)
, the remainder of this paper will use the shorthand
predictive scoring rule
to refer to a scoring rule which is applied to the predictive distribution
P_{Q}
.
One of the challenges of working with predictive scoring rules is that they do not admit simple representations.
However, Assumption
1
and Jensen’s inequality imply that

\displaystyle S\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),x\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int S\left(P_{\theta},x\right)\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)-\Delta(Q,x),

(2)

for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and some non-negative remainder term
\Delta:\mathcal{P}(\Theta)\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
This representation has important practical ramifications:
depending on the form of
\Delta(Q,x)
, a predictive score is either intractable, or can be tractably approximated. In the remainder, we call a predictive scoring rule
tractable
when it can be written as the expectation over a product measure for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
; so that for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and a loss
L:\Theta^{k}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, one obtains

S\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),x\right)=\int L(\theta_{1:k},x)\mathsf{d}Q^{k}(\theta_{1:k}),\quad Q^{k}(\theta_{1:k})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}Q(\theta_{i}).

(3)

If one can sample from
Q
, this allows approximation to arbitrary precision via Monte Carlo methods. For example, kernel scores, such as MMD and CRPS, are tractable with
k=2
(see Lemma
3
in Appendix
B
).

While tractable predictive scoring rules facilitate computation,
several predictive scores of interest are
intractable
—most notably the logarithmic predictive scoring rule (see Lemma
4
in Appendix
B
).
From the decomposition in (
2
), its associated Jensen’s gap depends on an intermediate value between
p_{\theta}(x)
and
\int p_{\theta}(x)\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)
denoted
g_{x}(p_{\theta},Q)
, and is

\displaystyle\Delta_{\log}(Q,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{2g_{x}(p_{\theta_{1}},Q)^{2}}\left\{p_{\theta_{1}}(x)-\int p_{\theta_{2}}(x)\mathsf{d}Q(\theta_{2})\right\}^{2}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta_{1}),

which is clearly intractable due to the dependence of
g_{x}(p_{\theta_{1}},Q)
on
\int p_{\theta}(x)\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)
.
To approximate
\Delta_{\log}
in a tractable manner,
Masegosa 2020
assumed that
\sup_{\theta\in\Theta,x\in\mathcal{X}}p_{\theta}(x)\leq u
for some
u>0
, so that
\Delta_{\log}(Q,x)\geq\int\frac{1}{2\sup_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}p_{\theta^{\prime}}(x)}\left\{p_{\theta_{1}}(x)-p_{\theta_{2}}(x)\right\}^{2}\mathsf{d}Q^{2}(\theta_{1:2})
, which leads to a tractable
approximation
,
\widehat{S}_{\operatorname{DI}}
, that upper-bounds the predictive logarithmic score via

\displaystyle S\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),x\right)\leq\widehat{S}_{\operatorname{DI}}\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),x\right)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int L_{\operatorname{DI}}(\theta_{1:2},x)\mathsf{d}Q^{2}(\theta_{1:2}),

\displaystyle L_{\operatorname{DI}}(\theta_{1:2},x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle-\log p_{\theta_{1}}(x)-\frac{\left\{p_{\theta_{1}}(x)-p_{\theta_{2}}(x)\right\}^{2}}{2\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}p_{\theta}(x)}.

Since the term
\Delta_{\log}
can be viewed as a variance or diversity term,
Masegosa 2020
referred to this approximation strategy as
diversity inducing
.
We will pay homage to this work by referring to all tractable predictive scoring rules constructed from the representation in (
2
)—whether exact or approximate—as
diversity inducing (DI)
.

While the approximations suggested by
Masegosa 2020
in the log score setting allowed for the derivation of a second-order PAC-Bayes bound, they are not practically appealing:

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X},\theta\in\Theta}p_{\theta}(x)\leq u
means that the bound only applies to models whose density or mass function is bounded, which rules out even simple models such as Gaussians.
To address this deficiency,
Morningstar
et al. 2022
proposed a second type of tractable approximation based on
multi sample (MS)
bounds in variational inference.
While originally proposed exclusively for the logarithmic predictive scoring rule, the approach clearly generalises to any convex score.
In particular, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we can define the tractable upper bound approximation
\widehat{S}_{\operatorname{MS}}
as

\displaystyle S\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),x\right)\leq\widehat{S}_{\operatorname{MS}}^{k}\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),x\right)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int L_{\operatorname{MS}}^{k}(\theta_{1:k},x)\mathsf{d}Q^{k}(\theta_{1:k}),

\displaystyle L_{\operatorname{MS}}^{k}(\theta_{1:k},x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle S\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}P_{\theta_{j}},x\right).

For larger choices of
k
, this bound tightens, and
the approximation error vanishes as
k\to\infty
.
For the special case of the logarithmic score,
Morningstar
et al. 2022
attempted to translate this into a PAC-Bayesian generalisation bound for a
PrO
posterior.
However, the resulting bound was vacuous, and failed to discern whether the resulting posteriors conferred any advantages over Bayes posteriors.
We rectify this omission, and deliver such a result: under mild regularity conditions and for arbitrary scoring rules,
PrO
posteriors based on
\widehat{S}^{k}_{\operatorname{MS}}\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),x\right)
strictly improve predictive performance relative to Bayes posteriors and Gibbs posteriors.

To end this section, we will summarise and generalise our observations on previous approaches through a mild assumption that restricts how the exact or approximate predictive scores giving rise to
PrO
posteriors will be computed.
Importantly, this assumption will ensure that we
will not have to
differentiate explicitly between the case of exact or approximate predictive scores.
Instead, we only assume that the (exact or approximate) predictive score can be computed as an average of form

\displaystyle\widehat{S}\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),x\right)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int L(\theta_{1:k},x)\mathsf{d}Q^{k}(\theta_{1:k}),

where
L
is subject to further requirements that generalise both the DI-based approach of
Masegosa 2020
and the MS-based approach of
Morningstar
et al. 2022
.

Assumption 2
.

\widehat{S}\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),x\right)=\int L(\theta_{1:k},x)\mathsf{d}Q^{k}(\theta_{1:k})
is the approximate or exact predictive scoring rule, where
L:\Theta^{k}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies conditions
\operatorname{(DI)}
or
\operatorname{(MS)}
below.

\operatorname{(DI)}

L(\theta_{1:k},x)=S(P_{\theta_{1}},x)-\delta(\theta_{1:k},x)
, for some
\delta:\Theta^{k}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\Delta(Q,x)\geq\int\delta(\theta_{1:k},x)\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)
, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\theta_{1:k}\in\Theta^{k}
.
Further, for any
\varphi\in\Theta
, if
\theta_{1:k}=(\varphi,\varphi,\dots\varphi)
, then
\delta(\theta_{1:k},x)=0
, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

\operatorname{(MS)}

L(\theta_{1:k},x)=S(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}P_{\theta_{j}},x)
.

2.2
A Key Lemma for PrO Posteriors

Assumption
2
allows us to formally define the
PrO
posterior irrespective of the sub-case considered, which can now be conveniently represented in the form:

Q_{n}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\left\{\lambda_{n}\int L_{n}(\theta_{1:k})\mathsf{d}Q^{k}(\theta_{1:k})+\mathrm{KL}(Q\|\Pi)\right\},

(4)

where
\lambda_{n}>0
is a positive scalar,

\displaystyle L_{n}(\theta_{1:k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(\theta_{1:k},x_{i})

denotes the in-sample loss corresponding to the (exact or approximate) score. This representation is the basis for a key Lemma that we build on throughout the remainder, and which is inspired by arguments prevalent for the study of Gibbs posteriors.

Crucially, the theoretical analysis of
Q_{n}
will deviate sharply from standard approaches due to the appearance of product measures, the generally non-standard loss
L_{n}(\theta_{1:k})
, and the focus on the predictive
P_{Q}:=\int P_{\theta}\;\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)
. Despite these differences, we impose Hoeffding-type moment conditions on the function
L_{n}(\theta_{1:k})
that are very similar to those arising in the study of Gibbs posteriors.

Assumption 3
.

Define the expectation
\theta_{1:k}\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\theta_{1:k}):=\mathbb{E}[L(\theta_{1:k},X)]
. For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and some
C_{k}>0
,

\mathop{\text{\large$\int$}}\nolimits\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda\{\mathcal{L}(\theta_{1:k})-L_{n}(\theta_{1:k})\}}\right]\mathsf{d}\Pi^{k}(\theta_{1:k})\leq e^{\lambda^{2}C_{k}^{2}/n}.

Usually, the above condition is unproblematic: it automatically holds for scoring rules based on the squared MMD with uniformly bounded kernels.
For the log score, the situation is more delicate. The log score
must always
be approximated, so Assumption
3
must be checked for a given approximation strategy.
While this is a well-known drawback of imposing Hoeffding-type assumptions in the context of logarithmic scores
(
Germain et al. 2016
, see e.g.)
, it is generally hard to avoid them for this kind of analysis.
Indeed, Assumption
3
was also crucial for the developments of both
Masegosa 2020
and
Morningstar
et al. 2022
.

With our three main assumptions in place, we can now state our first generic guarantee for
PrO
posteriors.
Recalling that
P_{Q}=\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)
, and
\mathcal{S}(P,P_{0})=\mathbb{E}[S(P,X)]
with
\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{Q},P_{0})=\mathcal{S}(P_{Q},P_{0})-\mathcal{S}(P_{0},P_{0})
, we
obtain a PAC-Bayes type inequality.

Lemma 1
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
3
, the following two statements hold:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{S}(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0})\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\left\{\int L_{n}(\theta_{1:k})\mathsf{d}Q^{k}(\theta_{1:k})+\frac{\mathrm{KL}({Q^{k}\|\Pi^{k}})}{k\lambda_{n}}\right\}+\frac{\lambda_{n}{k}C_{k}^{2}}{n};

if the scoring rule is exact so that
\widehat{S}=S
, i.e., (
3
) holds, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0})\right]\leq\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\left\{\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q},P_{0}\right)+\frac{\mathrm{KL}({Q^{k}\|\Pi^{k}})}{k\lambda_{n}}\right\}+\frac{\lambda_{n}{k}C_{k}^{2}}{n}.

Lemma
1
delivers a meaningful preliminary bound on the predictive performance of
PrO
posteriors. Further, this result applies to
PrO
posterior based on
any predictive score
—whether approximate or exact—so long as it satisfies Assumptions
1
-
3
.
In the specific case of logarithmic score, it also recovers an expectation version of the bounds in both
Masegosa 2020
and
Morningstar
et al. 2022
.
The implications are immediate. Lemma
1
provides the first known theoretical guarantee for the MMD-based
PrO
posterior proposed in
Shen
et al. 2025
, and lends theoretical rigour to justify variational approaches seeking to approximate
Q_{n}

(
Sheth and
Khardon 2020
;
Wei
et al. 2021
;
Jankowiak
et al. 2020b
;
Jankowiak
et al. 2020a
;
Lai and
Yao 2024
, e.g.)
.

3
Properties of PrO Posteriors

While Lemma
1
superficially resembles a PAC-Bayes bound used in the study of Gibbs posteriors, this in no way implies that
PrO
posteriors behave similarly to Gibbs posteriors.
In fact, we will show that unlike Gibbs posteriors,
PrO
posteriors
adapt
to the level of discord between an assumed model and the observed data.

3.1
Model Misspecification Settings

Before investigating performance and adaptivity of
PrO
posteriors in more detail, we posit a taxonomy of model misspecification regimes under which we will compare the behaviour of the Gibbs and
PrO
posteriors.

Definition 2
.

We say that
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
is
well-specified
if
P_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
, and
misspecified
if
P_{0}\notin\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
.
Further, we call a misspecified
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}

trivially misspecified
with respect to a scoring rule
\mathcal{S}
if there exists
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
so that

\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\mathcal{S}(P_{Q},P_{0})\geq\mathcal{S}(P_{\theta^{\prime}},P_{0})
, and
non-trivially misspecified
if
\mathcal{S}(P_{Q},P_{0})<\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{S}(P_{\theta},P_{0})
for some
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
.
Further, we say that a misspecified
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
allows
convex recovery
if there exists
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
such that
P_{Q}=P_{0}
.

\{P_{Q}:Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)\}
obtained via convex combinations of
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
contains
P_{0}
and is therefore well-specified.

3.2
Key Results

Equipped with the different forms of misspecification, we now compare the impact of different misspecification regimes on
PrO
posteriors
Q_{n}
, and compare them to Gibbs posteriors
Q_{n}^{\dagger}
.
Whenever
Q_{n}
and
Q_{n}^{\dagger}
are compared directly, we assume that both are constructed from the same scoring rule.
While Gibbs posteriors are always assumed to be constructed based on exact scoring rules,
PrO
posteriors may be based on approximations which adhere to

(
DI
)

or

(
MS
)

of Assumption
2
.

We first present results applicable to
exact and tractable
predictive scores, and then obtain similar results for the setting of
approximate
predictive scores.
Doing so reveals important differences in the approximation quality of approaches based on

(
DI
)

versus

(
MS
)

, and allows us to obtain corollaries for
PrO
posteriors based on squared MMD as well as the logarithmic scoring rule.
A full discussion of all underlying regularity conditions and a careful dissemination of the associated technical challenges is deferred to Section
7
.

3.2.1
Tractable Predictive Scoring Rules

Before turning to the case of approximate predictive scores, it is informative to investigate the best case scenario:
when the exact predictive scoring rule is tractable and (
3
) holds with equality, we obtain the strongest results.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that the predictive scoring rule is tractable so that (
3
) holds with equality.
If Assumptions
1
–
3
and additional regularity conditions hold, then the following are satisfied for
n
sufficiently large.

\operatorname{(WS)}

If the model is well-specified,
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{Q_{n}^{\dagger}},P_{0})]\leq\dfrac{\log(n)}{n^{1/2}}
;

\operatorname{(NT)}

If the model is non-trivially misspecified,
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)\right]<\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{Q_{n}^{\dagger}},P_{0})]
.

With further assumptions,
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)\right]\leq\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}(P_{Q},P_{0})+\dfrac{\log(n)}{n^{1/2}}
;

\operatorname{(CR)}

If the model allows convex recovery, then
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)\right]\leq\dfrac{\log(n)}{n^{1/2}}
.

Since
k
is fixed throughout all inequalities above, the constants associated to
k
play no role in the presented bounds since
n
can always be chosen large enough to render them insubstantial.
As in Lemma
1
, all results in Theorem
1
evaluate the
PrO
posterior predictive
P_{Q_{n}}
and its relationship to the Gibbs posterior predictive
P_{Q_{n}^{\dagger}}
in terms of the divergence
\mathcal{D}_{S}
induced by the score chosen to construct these posteriors.

(
WS
)

concerns the well-specified setting.
Here, Gibbs posteriors typically concentrate towards the correct model parameter at the usual
\log(n)/n^{1/2}
rate.
As the result shows, the difference in predictive performance between
PrO
posteriors and Gibbs posteriors in this setting is small, and of order
\log(n)/n^{1/2}
.
In fact, in Section
7
, we show that in the case of a well-specified model, the
PrO
posterior behaves similarly to a Gibbs posterior, and converges to a point mass at the data-generating parameter.
For the case of non-trivial misspecification studied in

(
NT
)

, the situation changes: now,
PrO
posteriors deliver predictive performance that strictly outperforms those of Bayes and Gibbs posteriors.
In particular, this performance gap will be of order
\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{\theta},P_{0})-\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{Q},P_{0})
, which grows larger as the benefits of averaging over
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
become more pronounced.
To confirm this intuition, the result in

(
CR
)

considers the special case of convex recovery, where
\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{Q},P_{0})=0
.
For this case, the rate at which the
PrO
posterior recovers the true data-generating process is
\log(n)/n^{1/2}
.
In contrast, the Gibbs posterior predictive
P_{Q_{n}^{\dagger}}

never
recovers the true data generating process in this setting, and instead converges to a point mass at
\theta^{\star}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{\theta},P_{0})
.

The above result assumes that the exact predictive scoring rule is tractable, so that
\widehat{S}=S
and (
3
) holds.
Lemma
3
in Appendix
B
shows that this is satisfied for a number of scoring rules, for which Theorem
1
applies immediately.

Lemma 2
.

For all kernel scores such as the squared MMD, the energy score, and the CRPS, the predictive scoring rule is tractable so that (
3
) holds exactly.

Interestingly, this result constitutes the only guarantees for the approach recently introduced in
Shen
et al. 2025
, and shows that what was motivated as little more than a heuristic does in fact deliver strong and rigorous performance guarantees.

3.2.2
Approximate Scores

While the special case of exact predictive scores is very useful, certain scoring rules do not take the form in (
3
), and need to be approximated.
To analyse what happens in this setting, one requires a refined version of Theorem
1
which introduces the difference between the exact and approximate score as
\operatorname{Gap}(k)
.
While the below result denotes this difference generically, its magnitude and behaviour depend on the exact approximation chosen.

Theorem 2
.

If Assumptions
1
–
3
and additional regularity conditions hold, then for the same
C_{k}
as in Lemma
1
, the following are satisfied for
n
sufficiently large:
for
n
sufficiently large but finite, it holds for the same
C_{k}
as in Lemma
1
that

\operatorname{(WS)}

If the model is well-specified,
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{Q_{n}^{\dagger}},P_{0})]\leq C_{k}\log(n)\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}
;

\operatorname{(NT)}

If the model is non-trivially misspecified,
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)\right]<\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{Q_{n}^{\dagger}},P_{0})]
.

With further assumptions,
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)\right]\leq\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}(P_{Q},P_{0})+\operatorname{Gap}(k)+C_{k}\log(n)\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}
;

\operatorname{(CR)}

If the model allows convex recovery, then
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)\right]\leq\operatorname{Gap}(k)+C_{k}\log(n)\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}
.

Intriguingly, the above shows that so long as predictive scoring rules are approximated in line with Assumption
2
, our key conclusions regarding the predictive performance of
PrO
posteriors are the same as in Theorem
1
: the
PrO
posterior performs comparably well to the Gibbs posterior in the well-specified setting, and strictly dominates it in the non-trivially misspecified setting.
In fact, the
\operatorname{Gap}(k)
term is only relevnt in situations where we bound the expected predictive performance of the
PrO
posterior directly, and without comparison to the Gibbs posterior.
To tackle these bounds, we need to delineate along the axes of

(
DI
)

and

(
MS
)

defined in Assumption
2
.
The case of DI-based approximations to predictive scores is straightforward: since
k
is fixed and cannot be varied, the gap term reverts to a fixed constant.
In other words, relative to exact predictive scores,
PrO
posteriors based on inexact DI-based approximations may lead to an irreducible error of size
\operatorname{Gap}(k)
.
In contrast, for MS-based
PrO
posteriors,
\operatorname{Gap}(k)\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.
To ensure that the bounds become non-vacuous however, the increase in
k
needs to be carefully balanced against
n
to prevent
C_{k}\log(n)\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}
from diverging.
The rate at which
k
can be allowed to increase will vary depending on the score chosen.
For example, for the special case of the logarithmic scores, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 1
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
1
,
if we approximate the logarithmic predictive scoring rule as in

(
MS
)

of Assumption
2
and choose
k\propto\log(n)/n^{1/3}
, then there exists a constant
C>0
so that
\operatorname{Gap}(k)+C_{k}\log(n)\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\leq C\cdot\log(n)/n^{1/3}
.

As expected, the result reveals that relative to computing
PrO
posteriors based on exact predictive scoring rules, MS-based approximations incur an additional cost and slow the rate of convergence to
\log(n)/n^{1/3}
.
Despite the fact that the convergence slows, this is far from a negative finding.
Indeed, to the best of our knowledge, Corollary
1
is the first known guarantee of its kind for
PrO
posterior type objects based on logarithmic scoring rules, and constitutes a strict improvement over previous bounds derived in
Masegosa 2020
and
Morningstar
et al. 2022
.

3.2.3
Posterior Concentration

The results in

(
NT
)

and

(
CR
)

of both Theorems
1
and
2
show that the
PrO
posterior
predictive
concentrates towards to an element close to
P_{0}
in terms of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}
.
For the special case of well-specified models, this suggests that the
PrO
posterior should also behave similarly to Gibbs posteriors in the parameter space
\Theta
, and concentrate towards a point mass at
\theta^{\star}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}(P_{\theta},P_{0})
.
Below, we formally prove this.

Corollary 2
.

If Assumptions
1
–
3
and additional regularity conditions hold,
if the model is well-specified, and if the predictive scoring rule satisfies

(
MS
)

of Assumption
2
or is exact,
then
Q_{n}
concentrates to
\theta^{\star}
at rate
(\log(n)\sqrt{k/n})^{1/\alpha}
for some
\alpha>1
.

The fact that the above result has to exclude
PrO
posteriors using DI-based approximate predictive scores is a direct consequence of the difficulty of controlling the rate at which its corresponding gap term
\operatorname{Gap}(k)
vanishes as
n
increases.
However, the result still applies in most settings of interest, including for the exact predictive scores associated to the logarithmic score or the squared MMD.
For both cases, one can show that the result holds with
\alpha=2
, which implies that the
PrO
posteriors based on these scores contract at the same rate.
While the exact predictive logarithmic score is not computationally tractable, Corollary
2
also extends its MS-based approximation.
This case is somewhat more involved however, and discussion is deferred to Sections
7.1.4
and
7.2
.

4
Computation of PrO Posteriors

The tools for the computation of
PrO
posteriors are limited.
In fact, most previous work targeting related objects did not even consider the possibility of exact computation, and instead focused on approximating
Q_{n}
with a parameterised variational family
(
Jankowiak
et al. 2020a
;
Jankowiak
et al. 2020b
;
Masegosa 2020
;
Morningstar
et al. 2022
;
Lai and
Yao 2024
, see e.g.)
.
This approach has several shortcomings: on a practical level, most variational approaches have to be fine-tuned to a given scoring rule, and depend on biased gradient estimates that suffer from high variance.
More importantly however, as a
PrO
posterior is only defined through an optimisation problem and does not have an analytic form, it is almost impossible to ascertain if a given variational family is appropriate for capturing its most important characteristics.
The only exception to this is the work of
Shen
et al. 2025
, which built on the ideas in
Wild et al. 2023
and
Del Moral 2013
to derive an asymptotically exact sampler for the special case of
PrO
posteriors that used the squared MMD as the scoring rule.
In the remainder, we will explain how this idea generalises to any
PrO
posterior whose scoring rule satisfies Assumption
2
so that
\widehat{S}\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),x\right)=\int L(\theta_{1:k},x)\mathsf{d}Q^{k}(\theta_{1:k})
.

4.1
A Sampling Method for PrO Posteriors

Assuming for simplicity that
\Pi
,
Q
and
Q^{k}
have corresponding Lebesgue densities
\pi
,
q
and
q^{k}
, we may rewrite the objective minimised in (
4
) as

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}(q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\int L_{n}(\theta_{1:k})q^{k}(\theta_{1:k})\mathsf{d}\theta_{1:k}-\int\log\pi(\theta)q(\theta)\mathsf{d}\theta+\int\log q(\theta)q(\theta)\mathsf{d}\theta,

(5)

which we can think of as a polynomial function in
q

with a strictly convex regulariser in the form of the entropy of
q
.
For functionals like this, the Wasserstein gradient flow (WGF) can be used to construct a gradient descent scheme on the space of measures with finite moments.
This in turn will allow us to compute it
PrO
posteriors with finite second moment by optimising the above functional.
Implementing this optimisation procedure yields an interacting particle system derived from a McKean-Vlasov process.
The resulting algorithm has been extensively discussed and analysed under the name of
mean field Langevin dynamics
in the monograph of
Del Moral 2013
and was previously suggested in a similar context by
Wild et al. 2023
and
Shen
et al. 2025
.

To construct the particle system driving this algorithm, we first require the Wasserstein gradient of
\mathscr{L}(q)
.
Denoting
v^{(\mapsto j)}[\vartheta]=(v_{1},\dots v_{j-1},\vartheta,v_{j},\dots v_{k-1})
as the vector obtained by inserting
\vartheta\in\Theta
at location
j
of the vector
v
with length
k-1
,
we define
\widetilde{L}_{n}(\vartheta,\theta_{1:(k-1)},j)=L_{n}(\theta_{1:(k-1)}^{(\mapsto j)}[\vartheta])
and obtain the first variation of
\mathscr{L}
with respect to
q
at
\vartheta
as

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathscr{L}}{\delta q}[\vartheta]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\int\sum_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{L}_{n}(\vartheta,\theta_{1:(k-1)},j)\;q^{k-1}(\theta_{1:(k-1)})\mathsf{d}\theta_{1:(k-1)}-\log\pi(\vartheta)+\log q(\vartheta),

from which one obtains the Wasserstein gradient after differentiating with respect to
\vartheta
.
To simplify notation, we introduce the symmetrised loss function
L_{n}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\vartheta,\theta_{1:(k-1)})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{L}_{n}(\vartheta,\theta_{1:(k-1)},j)
, denote its gradient with respect to
\vartheta
by
\nabla_{1}L_{n}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\vartheta,\theta_{1:(k-1)})
, and the gradients of
\log\pi(\vartheta)
and
\log q(\vartheta)
by
\nabla\log\pi(\vartheta)
and
\nabla\log q(\vartheta)
.
With this, one obtains the Wasserstein gradient of
\mathscr{L}
with respect to
q
evaluated at
\vartheta
as

\displaystyle\nabla_{\operatorname{W}}\mathscr{L}(q)[\vartheta]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}k\int\nabla_{1}L_{n}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\vartheta,\theta_{1:(k-1)})\;q^{k}(\theta_{1:(k-1)})\mathsf{d}\theta_{1:(k-1)}-\nabla\log\pi(\vartheta)+\nabla\log q(\vartheta).

The WGF
\{q_{t}\}_{t>0}
defined by this gradient is simply the continuous-time process that evolves in the direction of
\nabla_{\operatorname{W}}\mathscr{L}(q)
:
the direction of steepest descent relative to the convex functional
\mathscr{L}(q)
.
As
\mathscr{L}(q)
is convex, and the minimiser of
\mathscr{L}(q)
is precisely the
PrO
posterior, this means that we should think of the WGF as a continuously-valued version of gradient descent, and intuitively expect
q_{t}
to converge to the
PrO
posterior as
t\to\infty
.

While we could in principle use the WGF to construct an optimisation scheme directly on the space of measures with finite second moments via a partial differential equation (PDE), this would generally lead to an intractable algorithm, as PDE solvers scale poorly with
\operatorname{dim}(\Theta)
and
k
.
With this in mind, we will instead resort to its alternative mean-field representation, which gives rise to the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}\vartheta_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\lambda_{n}k\hskip-2.84544pt\int\hskip-2.84544pt\nabla_{1}L_{n}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\vartheta_{t},\theta_{1:(k-1)})q_{t}^{k-1}(\theta_{1:(k-1)})\mathsf{d}\theta_{1:(k-1)}-\nabla\log\pi(\vartheta_{t})\right\}\mathsf{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathsf{d}B_{t};\;\;\vartheta_{t}\sim q_{t},

where
B_{t}
denotes a Brownian motion.
This term appears because the WGF of entropy corresponds
exactly
to the heat equation
(
Jordan
et al. 1998
)
, which in turn governs the evolution of Brownian motion.
Unfortunately, the above SDE still depends on an intractable integral with respect to
q_{t}^{k-1}
, which makes exact forward simulation impossible.
To remedy this, we use the approximation
q_{t}(\theta)\approx\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\updelta_{\vartheta_{t}^{(j)}}
, where
\updelta_{x}
denotes the Dirac measure at
x
, and
\vartheta_{t}^{(j)}
for
j=1,2,\dots p
are a set of
p
interacting particles.
To state the necessary evolution equations, we take
\sigma(j,p,k)
as the set of all
{p-1\choose p-k}
possible permutations of the set
\{1,\dots j-1,j+1,\dots p\}
with size
k-1
, and write the corresponding set of particles for any set of indices
\mathcal{I}\in\sigma(j,p,k)
with
\mathcal{I}=\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots i_{k-1}\}
as
\vartheta_{t}^{[\mathcal{I}]}=(\vartheta_{t}^{(i_{1})},\vartheta_{t}^{(i_{2})},\dots\vartheta_{t}^{(i_{k-1})})
.
Based on this, we evolve the particles as

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}\vartheta_{t}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{\lambda_{n}k}{{p-1\choose p-k}}\sum_{\mathcal{I}\in\sigma(j,p,k)}\nabla_{1}L_{n}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\vartheta_{t}^{(j)},\vartheta_{t}^{[\mathcal{I}]})-\nabla\log\pi(\vartheta_{t}^{(j)})\right\}\mathsf{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathsf{d}B_{t}^{(j)},

where
\{B_{t}^{(j)}:j=1,2,\dots p\}
denotes a set of
p
independent Brownian motions.
To obtain a high-fidelity approximation of the
PrO
posterior from this particle system in practice, we first have to discretise it into time steps
t_{1},t_{2},\dots
, and then evolve it for sufficiently many particles and a sufficiently long time.
Afterwards, we average over all time steps past an initial burn-in period of length
\tau
and approximate the
PrO
posterior as

\displaystyle Q_{n}(\theta)\approx\frac{1}{|\{t_{i}:t_{i}>\tau\}|}\sum_{t_{i}>\tau}\widehat{Q}[t_{i}](\theta)\quad\text{ for }\quad\widehat{Q}[t_{i}](\theta)=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\updelta_{\vartheta_{t_{i}}^{(j)}}(\theta).

Here, the notation highlights that this type of approximation should be thought of as an average of
|\{t_{i}:t_{i}>\tau\}|
atomic measures
\widehat{Q}[t]
, each of which will recover
Q_{n}
exactly in the limit as both
p\to\infty
and
t\to\infty
.

Examples: MMD and logarithmic score

Our above development recovers the algorithm of
Shen
et al. 2025
for the special case of the squared MMD.
For this case,
k=2
, and the objective takes a particularly simple form.
In particular, we have that
\widetilde{L}_{n}(\vartheta,\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{L}_{\operatorname{MMD}}(\vartheta,\theta,x_{i})
, where
\mathsf{L}_{\operatorname{MMD}}(\vartheta,\theta,x)=\langle\mu(P_{\vartheta})-\mu(\updelta_{x}),\mu(P_{\theta})-\mu(\updelta_{x})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
is a symmetric function so that
\mathsf{L}_{\operatorname{MMD}}(\vartheta,\theta,x)=\mathsf{L}_{\operatorname{MMD}}(\theta,\vartheta,x)
.
This symmetry simplifies the evolution equation and leads to

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}\vartheta_{t}^{(j)}=\left\{\frac{2\lambda_{n}}{p-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
\ell\neq j\end{subarray}}^{p}\nabla_{1}\widetilde{L}_{n}(\vartheta_{t}^{(j)},\vartheta_{t}^{(\ell)})-\nabla\log\pi(\vartheta_{t}^{(j)})\right\}\mathsf{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathsf{d}B_{t}^{(j)}.

Similarly, choosing MS-based approximations to the log score of order

, we find that

(\vartheta,\theta_{1:(k-1)})

with size
k-1

\mathsf{d}\vartheta_{t}^{(j)}=-\left\{\frac{\lambda_{n}k}{{p-1\choose k-1}}\sum_{\mathcal{I}\in\xi(j,p,k)}\nabla_{1}L_{n}^{\operatorname{sym}}(\vartheta_{t}^{(j)},\vartheta_{t}^{[\mathcal{I}]})+\nabla\log\pi(\vartheta_{t}^{(j)})\right\}\mathsf{d}t\\

In contrast, the single particle evolved according to MALA bounces around a singular mode.

While the interacting particle approach has the distinct advantage of being able to sample from any
PrO
posterior, it introduces several hyper-parameters that may require careful tuning in practice.
The most important of these are the step sizes for the discretisation of the underlying SDE, the number of particles
p
, as well as the exact choice of
\lambda_{n}
and
k
.
Here, the choice of step size and the number of particles
p
constitute a
direct
trade-off between approximation quality and computational efficiency: choosing the step size too small or the number of particles too large will in principle increase accuracy, but do so at the cost of exploding computational cost and substantively slowed convergence.
While little is known about choosing these hyper-parameters in our setting, recent work by
Chazal et al. 2025
may finally provide a principled way for this.
In contrast, we have clear ideas how
k
and
\lambda_{n}
should be set from the theory developed in Section
3
.
While
k
is generally fixed for DI-based approximations, we can freely choose it for MS-based approximations:
by way of example, for the logarithmic score, one can show that
k\asymp\log(n)/n^{1/3}
is the predictively optimal rate (see Corollay
1
).
Likewise, the learning rate
\lambda_{n}
should be set to maximise the predictive performance.
For the exact scoring rules as covered in Theorem
1
as well as approximate DI-based approaches, this is achieved by setting
\lambda_{n}\asymp n^{1/2}/\log(n)
.
For MS-based approximations, the optimal choice depends on the optimal rate of growth for
k
.
In the special case of the logarithmic score covered in Corollary
1
, the optimal rate is the same as that for
k
, and given by
\lambda_{n}\asymp n^{1/3}/\log(n)
.

6
Discussion

In this paper, we have advocated for a new statistical paradigm that combines the best aspects of both parameter inference and density estimation.
This led to
PrO
posteriors, which can operate at a similar predictive accuracy as density estimation techniques while simultaneously delivering practically meaningful uncertainty quantification for interpretable model parameters.
We argued that
PrO
posteriors
bridge the two main statistical traditions identified by
Breiman 2001
, and asserted that they lead to a version of epistemological uncertainty that is more expansive, adapts to model misspecification, and can avoid posterior collapse whenever doing so is benefits prediction.
Further, we proved that
PrO
posteriors predictively dominate both Bayes and Gibbs posteriors.
After introducing sampling algorithms for
PrO
posteriors, we illustrated our findings on several numerical examples.

PrO
posteriors are a rigorous exploration of themes that have been present in several research communities, and build directly on the pioneering work of
Masegosa 2020
,
Morningstar
et al. 2022
, and
Shen
et al. 2025
.
In a more applied setting, related ideas have also previously been studied for variational methods
(
Futami
et al. 2021
;
Futami et al. 2022
;
Wei
et al. 2021
;
Lai and
Yao 2024
)
, in the context of Gaussian processes
(
Jankowiak
et al. 2020a
;
Jankowiak
et al. 2020b
)
, and for mixture models
(
Wang and
Liu 2019
)
.
Within statistical learning, an important area of research with significant parallels to
PrO
posteriors are PAC-Bayesian bounds which are concerned with introducing diversity into an ensemble.
The most important example is weighted majority voting, whereby
a set of classifiers are assigned weights, and the final prediction is the label supported by the largest total weight across the ensemble
(
Lacasse et al. 2006
;
Laviolette
et al. 2011
;
Germain et al. 2015
;
Laviolette et al. 2017
;
Lorenzen
et al. 2019
;
Masegosa
et al. 2020
;
Viallard et al. 2021
;
Wu
et al. 2021
;
Wu and
Seldin 2022
;
Vignault
et al. 2023
;
Theisen et al. 2024
)
.
Beyond majority voting, related PAC-Bayesian theories were also discussed in
Audibert 2004
,
Ortega
et al. 2022
,
Hauptvogel and
Igel 2024
and
Pitas
et al. 2025
.

Throughout, robustness to model misspecification was an important motivation in our development of
PrO
posteriors.
Robustness is also one of the key drivers behind the recent emergence of generalised Bayesian methods, and particularly Gibbs posteriors: for example,
Altamirano
et al. 2023a
;
Altamirano
et al. 2023b
;
Matsubara et al. 2022
;
Matsubara et al. 2023
;
Laplante et al. 2025
;
Duran-Martin
et al. 2024
all propose Gibbs posteriors which produce posteriors that are Bayes-like, but robust to outliers.
However, these methods guard against misspecification only if our main interest remains parameter inference and our misspecification can be described through the famous Huber contamination model
(
Huber 1964
)
.
If the misspecification falls far outside of this class of model perturbations, Gibbs posteriors constructed with this setting in mind generally perform poorly.
Though the underlying meta-model is more intricate than the Huber contamination model, a similar conclusion holds for the
coarsened Bayes
posteriors of
Miller and
Dunson 2019
.
In contrast,
PrO
posteriors do not explicitly rely on a meta-model: while the results in Theorems
1
and
2
demonstrate strong performance guarantees whenever the data-generating process is a mixture over elements in
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
, they also show that
PrO
posteriors continue to dominate the predictive outputs of Gibbs and Bayes posteriors well beyond this setting.

We presented our findings as a post-Bayesian idea: grounded in the type of epistemological uncertainty produced by Bayesian methods, but not reliant on Bayes’ rule.
It is interesting to note that one could also interpret our methodology as a nonparametric frequentist estimation technique: for the case of the logarithmic scoring rule, one could even interpret the
PrO
posterior objective as an KL-regularised version of a nonparametric maximum likelihood estimation
(
Laird 1978
;
Lindsay 1995
, e.g.)
.
Extending this logic,
PrO
posteriors for general scoring rules could be thought of as frequentist KL-regularised nonparametric minimum distance estimators for the model class
\{P_{Q}:Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)\}
parameterised by a measure
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
.
However, as our parameter of interest is actually
\theta\in\Theta
, and since
PrO
posteriors directly incorporate our prior beliefs,
\Pi
, we argue that they should foremost be viewed as belief distributions over the parameters of interest, and are therefore more closely related to Bayesian and generalised Bayesian methods.

Despite a thorough exploration of
PrO
posteriors in the current paper, much remains to be done.
Perhaps the most pressing issue is computation: to improve the sampling scheme we proposed, more insight is needed on hyper-parameter tuning and convergence diagnostics.
We are optimistic that the recent paper of
Chazal et al. 2025
may remedy this: on top of providing a new suite of diagnostics and convergence guarantees, it also proposes new algorithms applicable to
PrO
posteriors.
Beyond computation, there are several promising methodological avenues:
for instance, we should be able to derive statistical tests that use
PrO
posteriors to diagnose model misspecification.
Unlike previous tests, they would not operate on posterior summaries, but directly on the difference in uncertainty between
PrO
posteriors and Gibbs posteriors.
Further, the current theory is limited to observations which are independently drawn from a common data-generating mechanism.
As there is considerable interest in forecasting and prediction for time series, graphical models, and spatial settings, it would be of great practical interest to extend our findings to dependent data.
While this would be a technically challenging undertaking, our experimental evidence suggests that
PrO
posteriors should be expected to perform well in these settings.
Other questions that are currently unaddressed include the choice of scoring rule: in finite samples, are there settings where one scoring rule should be preferred over another? What are the differences between
PrO
posteriors and their posterior predictives as we vary the underlying scoring rules?
Likewise, we have not discussed prior choice in the current paper: though it seems intuitive to think of the prior as being conceptually similar to the generalised Bayesian case, we have not attempted to formalise this analogy.
Doing so would have the potential of shedding more light on the role of prior regularisation in
PrO
posteriors, and could raise several key questions.
For example, can we quantify how exactly the prior impacts the
PrO
posterior?
Similarly, is it possible to adapt the arguments given by
Catoni 2007
to
PrO
posteriors, and to derive priors which are in some sense predictively optimal?

In conclusion then,
PrO
posteriors open up many new possibilities, and allow us to reason about parameter uncertainty and prediction-centric statistical methods in a new light.
They mark a clear departure from existing paradigms focused on either parameter inference or density estimation, and introduce a novel form of epistemological uncertainty unaccounted for by classical statistical methods.
While the introduction of
PrO
posteriors results in numerous challenges that must be resolved before they are ready for wide-spread adoption, we believe that the scale of these challenges is matched by their transformative potential for modern statistical science.

7
Detailed Theoretical Developments

Herein, we fully develop and explain the results summarised in Theorems
1
and
2
, and discuss some additional results left out from the main paper.
From a technical standpoint, the technology and arguments used to treat the case of exact and DI-based score approximations are compatible, while the
case of MS-based approximations requires slightly different arguments and assumptions.
To reflect this, our structure diverges slightly from that of Section
3.2
: we treat both DI-based approximations and exact scores for which (
3
) holds with
\widehat{S}=S
in Section
7.1
; and then treat MS-based approximations in Section
7.2
.
Lastly, it is important to note that throughout all results,
\lambda_{n}
is optimized with respect to the upper bound in Lemma
1
, and thus taken to be of order
\lambda_{n}\propto\sqrt{n/kC_{k}^{2}}
.

7.1
Details: DI-based Approximations and Exact Predictive Scores

Throughout this section, we will deal with losses taking the form given in Assumption
2
part

(
DI
)

, which notably includes the case of exact scores for which (
3
) holds so that
\widehat{S}=S
.

7.1.1
Generic Misspecification

Under mild regularity conditions, Bayes posteriors and Gibbs posteriors based on expected scoring rules
\mathcal{S}
concentrate towards
\theta^{\star}:=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{S}(P_{\theta},P_{0})
, which parametrises the model closest to
P_{0}
in terms of
\mathcal{S}
.
In fact, results like this only require existence of
\theta^{\star}
and mild conditions on the probability mass that
\Pi
has around
\theta^{\star}
.
The next assumption provides such conditions, and is sufficient for Gibbs posterior concentration to occur.
In the remainder, we will use these assumptions to compare the behaviour of Gibbs posteriors with that of
PrO
posteriors as
n
increases.

Assumption 4
.

There exists
\theta^{\star}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{S}(P_{\theta},P_{0})
, and constants
c_{S}>0
and
d_{S}>0
and some
r_{0}>0
such that, for any
r\leq r_{0}
, and
\mathcal{B}_{r}:=\left\{\theta\in\Theta:\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{\theta},P_{0})-\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{\theta^{\star}},P_{0})\leq r\right\}
,
\Pi\left(\mathcal{B}_{r}\right)\geq({r}/{c_{S}})^{d_{S}}
.

The above is slightly stronger than the classical prior mass condition of
Ghosal
et al. 2000
, insofar as the condition is essentially geared toward parametric models:
while it may not be satisfied outside of Euclidean spaces, it is extremely mild and unrestrictive when
\Theta
is a Euclidean space.
In fact, the above assumption is considered standard in the PAC-Bayes literature, where interest traditionally is more focused on parametric models (see, e.g., Section 4.4 of
Alquier 2024
).
Using Assumption
4
, we obtain the first result comparing predictive performance between
PrO
posteriors and Gibbs posteriors in the case where we allow for generic model misspecification.

Theorem 3
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2

(
DI
)

,
3
,
4
,

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)-\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{Q_{n}^{\dagger}},P_{0})]\leq C\frac{\log(n)}{n^{1/2}}.

In words,
Theorem
3
says that the
PrO
posteriors predictive performance is never worse than the Gibbs posterior based on the same scoring rule. While this may be reassuring, it does not show that the
PrO
posterior confers a clear advantage.
The reason for this is that Assumption
4
does not rule out the case where the predictively optimal average
Q^{\star}\in\operatornamewithlimits{arginf}_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\mathcal{S}(P_{Q},P_{0})
is a point mass at
\theta^{\star}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}(P_{\theta},P_{0})
, the score-minimising value.
In this case—which is unlikely to occur in sufficiently complex data sets—Gibbs and
PrO
posteriors alike would allocate most of their probability mass around
\theta^{\star}
as
n\to\infty
, and not produce meaningfully different predictive distributions.

7.1.2
Non-Trivial Misspecification

Conversely, by excluding trivial forms of misspecification, we can significantly strengthen our results. Following the idea of
bad misspecification
presented by
Grünwald and
van Ommen 2017
, the notion of non-trivial misspecification in Definition
2
ensures that averaging over
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
is predictively advantageous.

While non-trivial misspecification is sufficient to ensure that
PrO
posteriors strictly dominate Bayes and Gibbs posterior in terms of predictive accuracy when the score can be computed exactly, a further condition is required to ensure this behaviour for approximate DI-based predictive scores.
In particular, we require the gap between approximate and exact predictive score to be of smaller magnitude than the size of the model misspecification.
To formalise this, we define the approximation gap for the score as

\displaystyle{\mathsf{AppGap}}(Q,k):=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{0}}\left[\widehat{S}\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),X\right)-S\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),X\right)\right].

While
{\mathsf{AppGap}}(Q,k)=0
for any
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
when the score is exact, it will generally be strictly positive when the score is approximated.
Our next assumption now uses
{\mathsf{AppGap}}(Q,k)
to demand that the size of the approximation error be dominated by the magnitude of the underlying non-trivial model misspecification.

Assumption 5
.

For
Q^{\star}\in\arg\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q},P_{0}\right)
, we have

\mathsf{AppGap}(Q^{\star},k)<\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{\theta^{\star}},P_{0}\right)-\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q^{\star}},P_{0}\right).

Assumption
5
reflects a trade-off between the approximation error on the lefthand side with the magnitude of non-trivial misspecification on the right-hand side.
When the magnitude of misspecification is small, the Gibbs posterior is already highly performant, so that the
PrO
posterior can only achieve predictive superiority if the approximation error is small—which will in turn require a highly accurate approximation to the predictive score.
Conversely, when the magnitude of misspecification is large, the
PrO
posterior can dominate Gibbs even with relatively poor approximation.
When no approximation is used, no restriction is required and strict dominance is guaranteed
directly
by assuming non-trivial misspecification as in Definition
2
.
Thus, assuming non-trivial misspecification is sufficient to achieve predictive domination for any predictive score that can be computed exactly, which notably includes all kernel scores (see Lemma
3
in Appendix
B
).

The next assumption we impose is a mild technical continuity-type condition.

Assumption 6
.

For any
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
, and any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\widetilde{Q}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
, possibly depending on
\varepsilon
, so that
\textnormal{KL}(\widetilde{Q}\|\Pi)<\infty
and

\left|\int L(\theta_{1:k})\mathsf{d}Q^{k}(\theta_{1:k})-\int L(\theta_{1:k})\mathsf{d}\widetilde{Q}^{k}(\theta_{1:k})\right|\leq\varepsilon.

This assumption’s primary purpose is to ensure that whenever the
PrO
posterior would converge to a discrete mixture over
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
as
n\to\infty
, it is still possible to find arbitrarily good approximations to this discrete measure that are absolutely continuous with respect to
\Pi
.
In practice, this is unrestrictive so long as the scoring rules and models used are continuous, and just means that we should favour priors that are sufficiently diffuse to have (arbitrarily small) positive mass in neighbourhoods of positive support for
Q^{\star}
.

With this, we can now prove a strengthened version of Theorem
3
for the case of non-trivially misspecified models, which finally shows that there is a strict performance gap between the predictive distributions of
PrO
posteriors on the one hand, and Bayes or Gibbs posteriors on the other.

Theorem 4
.

Under Assumptions

1
,
2

(
DI
)

,
3
-
6
and for
n
large enough but finite, it holds that
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)\right]<\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{Q_{n}^{\dagger}},P_{0})]
. In addition, if
Q^{\star}
is such that
\mathrm{KL}(Q^{\star}\|\Pi)<\infty
, then
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)-\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{Q^{\star}},P_{0})\right]\leq\mathsf{AppGap}(Q^{\star},k)+C\frac{\log(n)}{n^{1/2}}.

The above result constitutes part

(
NT
)

in Theorem
1
as well as

(
NT
)

in Theorem
2
for the sub-case of approximate scores that satisfy Assumption
2
part

(
DI
)

.
The result shows that under suitable regularity conditions,
PrO
posteriors constructed from exact or DI-based scoring rules strictly dominate both Bayes posteriors and Gibbs posteriors in terms of their predictive performance.
Crucially, this is not an asymptotic result:
it holds in finite samples.
While the result shows the superior predictive performance of
PrO
posteriors, it leaves several key questions unanswered:
what is the qualitative behaviour of
PrO
posteriors?
Can we characterise the shape they take, at least asymptotically?
And how practically significant is the performance gap?
Unfortunately, it is not possible to establish these properties in full generality:
without further assumptions on the nature of the misspecification, we could be arbitrarily close to being in the trivially misspecified regime.

7.1.3
Misspecification with Convex Recovery

From Definition
2
, we recall that convex recovery implies the existence of some
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
for which
P_{Q}
can recover the data-generating process
P_{0}
. Interestingly, when using exact scores, we can show that the
PrO
posterior recovers the truth so long as
P_{0}
is convexly recoverable as either a purely discrete or continuous mixture.

Assumption 7
.

One of the following is satisfied:

(i)

There exists
Q^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
, with
\mathrm{KL}(Q^{\star}\|\Pi)<\infty
, such that
P_{0}=\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q^{\star}(\theta)
;

(ii)

For some
J\in\mathbb{N}
,

P_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\omega_{j}P_{\theta_{j}^{\star}}
, where
(\omega_{j})_{j=1}^{J}\in\Delta^{J-1}
are non-negative weights summing to unity, and

\theta_{j}^{\star}\in\Theta
. For
\mathcal{B}_{r,j}=\{\theta:\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{\theta},P_{\theta_{j}^{\star}})\leq r_{j}\}
, there are constants
c_{S},d_{S}>0
so that, for any
r\leq r_{0}
,
\Pi(\mathcal{B}_{r,j})\geq(r/c_{S})^{d_{S}}
for
j=1,2,\dots J
.

While Assumption
7
(i) is similar to the condition maintained in Theorem
4
which relied on the condition that
\mathrm{KL}(Q^{\star}\|\Pi)<\infty
, Assumption
7
(ii) is the discrete analogue, and requires that the prior should place enough mass near each component of the discrete mixture representing the true data generating process
P_{0}
.

Theorem 5
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2

(
DI
)

,
3
and
5
-
7
,
and for
n
sufficiently large but finite,

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)\right]<\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{Q_{n}^{\dagger}},P_{0})]
. Further, if
S=\widehat{S}
so that equation (
3
) holds with equality, then
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)\right]\leq C\frac{\log(n)}{n^{1/2}}
.

The above result constitutes part

(
CR
)

in Theorem
1
as well as

(
CR
)

in Theorem
2
for the sub-case of approximate scores that satisfy Assumption
2
part

(
DI
)

.
When using an exact score, Theorem
5
shows that we recover the true data generating process
P_{0}
at the standard parametric rate of
n^{-1/2}
, up to logarithmic factors.
In the more general setting where we have to approximate the score, all we can say is that the DI-based approximation still produces better predictive distributions than Bayes or Gibbs posteriors—so long as the magnitude of misspecification is at least as large as the error we make in the approximation of the predictive score.

7.1.4
Correct Model Specification

Theorems
4
and
5
prove that the
PrO
posterior has superior predictive properties to Gibbs and Bayes posteriors under model misspecification, but it is unclear what penalty we pay when using the
PrO
posterior in the context of a well-specified model.
The next result shows that there is little to worry about—even for approximate predictive scores.

Corollary 3
.

If Assumptions
1
,
2

(
DI
)

,
3
and
7
(ii) hold for
J=1
and
\theta^{\star}_{1}=\theta^{\star}
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{Q^{\dagger}_{n}},P_{0})]\leq C\frac{\log(n)}{n^{1/2}}.

The above result constitutes part

(
WS
)

in Theorem
1
as well as part

(
DI
)

in Theorem
2
for the sub-case of approximate scores that satisfy Assumption
2
.
This result shows that there is little evidence for a loss in predictive statistical efficiency when using
PrO
posteriors in the well-specified regime: the worst case difference relative to Gibbs posteriors based on the same scoring rule is bounded by a vanishing sequence of order
\log(n)/n^{1/2}
.

Since Gibbs posteriors concentrate onto the true parameter
\theta^{*}
in well-specified models, Corollary
3
raises the question of whether or not
PrO
posteriors behave the same.
By imposing additional assumptions, we can demonstrate that this is indeed the case.
For
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
, let
\mathsf{d}_{1}(P,Q)
denote a metric on the space of probability measures.
Further, let
\mathsf{d}_{2}(P,Q)
be a symmetric distance on
\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
.
Here,
\mathsf{d}_{2}(P,Q)
need not be a metric, but is assumed to satisfy a weak triangle inequality.

Assumption 8
.

(i)

The map
\theta\mapsto P_{\theta}
is continuous and identifiable: there exist
\theta^{\star}\in\Theta
such that
\updelta_{\theta^{\star}}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{Q},P_{0})
;

(ii)

there exists constant
C
, and
\varepsilon>0
such that, for any
Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
, if
\mathsf{d}_{2}(P_{Q},P_{Q^{\prime}})\leq\varepsilon
, then

\mathsf{d}_{1}(Q,Q^{\prime})\leq C\mathsf{d}_{2}(P_{Q},P_{Q^{\prime}})
; and

(iii)

for some
\alpha\geq 1
,
\mathsf{d}_{2}(P_{Q},P_{Q^{\prime}})^{\alpha}\leq\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{Q},P_{Q^{\prime}})
.

Assumption
8
provides conditions that allow us to translate concentration in the
PrO
posterior predictive
P_{Q_{n}}
to concentration in the
PrO
posterior
Q_{n}
defined on the parameter space.
While part
(i)

is standard, part
(ii)
gives a form of identifiability condition of the posterior.
In particular, it ensures that as the two predictives
P_{Q},P_{Q^{\prime}}
become closer, then their underlying posteriors
Q,Q^{\prime}
must do, too.
Without such an assumption, concentration in the predictive space,
P_{Q}
, would not necessarily translate into concentration over
Q
.
Lastly, part
(iii)
is a type of reverse Hölder continuity, that depends on the choice of score, distance, and the model class
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
.
Its purpose is to relate the score-induced discrepancy measure to a more regular measure of distance on the space of measures, which is needed to transfer convergence.
While it is difficult to establish these conditions in general, they can be shown to hold for the squared MMD and the logarithmic scoring rule for several model classes of interest, including Gaussians.

Corollary 4
.

If (
3
) holds so that
S=\widehat{S}
, and if Assumptions
1
-
3
, and
7
(ii), for
J=1
and
\theta^{\star}_{1}=\theta^{\star}
,
and
8
hold, then it also holds that

\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{d}_{1}(Q_{n},\updelta_{\theta^{\star}})]\leq C(\log n/n^{1/2})^{1/\alpha}.

Under various choices of
\alpha
,
\mathrm{d}_{1}
, and
\mathrm{d}_{2}
, Corollary
4
applies to both the squared MMD (with
\alpha=2
and
\mathrm{d}_{2}(P,Q)=\mathrm{MMD}(P,Q)
) and to the intractable exact logarithmic scoring rule (with
\alpha=2
and
\mathrm{d}_{2}(P,Q)=\mathrm{TV}(P,Q)
), which satisfy Assumption
8
part
(iii)
.
For the special case of regular parametric models, the local equivalence between
\mathrm{d}_{2}(P_{\theta},P_{\theta^{\star}})
and the Euclidean distance
\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}
around the true value
\theta^{\star}
allows us to refine our conclusions.
In particular, in this setting, for any posterior
Q
concentrating around
\theta^{\star}
, the distance
\mathrm{d}_{2}(P_{Q},P_{\theta^{\star}})
behaves like
\sqrt{\int\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)}
.
Thus, if we now chose a metric
\mathrm{d}_{1}
on posterior distributions that satisfied Assumption
8
part
(ii)
and locally
also
behaves like
\mathrm{d}_{1}(Q,\delta_{\theta^{\star}})^{2}\asymp\int\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)
, we would recover posterior concentration rates directly in the Euclidean distance.
Put differently, up to a local approximation, the notion of concentration used in Corollary
4
via such a metric
\mathrm{d}_{1}
is equivalent to the classical definition of posterior concentration with respect to the Euclidean distance for regular parametric models.
In this setting, and if
\alpha=2
, our results would now translate to a concentration rate of
n^{-1/4}
in the Euclidean distance, which the reader will notice is slower than the standard
n^{-1/2}
rate that is usually achievable in parametric models.
Critically, this behaviour is
not
a feature of the
PrO
posterior in particular, but of the temperature scaling employed within PAC-Bayes-type bounds.
Indeed, the
n^{-1/4}
rate we achieve in the Euclidean space is a direct consequence of minimising our PAC-Bayes-type generalisation bounds for the
PrO
posterior by choosing the rate
\lambda_{n}\propto n^{1/2}
.
Note that this result is entirely consistent with the application of PAC-Bayes bounds to Gibbs posteriors: here too, one often obtains
\lambda_{n}\propto n^{1/2}
by minimising generalisation bounds, which would then
also
result in a
n^{1/4}
concentration rate on the Euclidean space by the same arguments as before
(
Alquier
et al. 2016
, see e.g.)
.

7.2
Details: MS-based Approximations

DI-based predictive scores are harder to construct in general, but can recover the exact predictive score in certain cases.
In contrast, MS-based predictive scoring rules are very easy to construct, but are
always
approximate.
This means that the role of the approximation gap is a key part of their theoretical analysis.
In particular, we will have to leverage that one should expect that as
k\rightarrow\infty
, it also holds that
\mathsf{AppGap}(Q^{\star},k)\rightarrow 0
.
As this gap crucially depends on the type of score chosen, this then means that MS-based
PrO
posteriors have to be analysed
case-by-case
.
In what follows, we illustrate this by focusing on the most interesting MS-based approximation: that for the logarithmic score. This is the scoring rule for which
Morningstar
et al. 2022
originally proposed MS-type approximations, is arguably the most commonly used scoring rule, and is the basis for Bayes posteriors.
To control its approximation gap, we impose the following assumption.

Assumption 9
.

There exists

Q^{\star}\in\operatornamewithlimits{arginf}_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\mathrm{KL}(P_{0}\|P_{Q})
where
\mathrm{KL}(Q^{\star}\|\Pi)<\infty
, and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\{\int\hskip-4.26773pt\left(\frac{p_{\theta}(X)}{\int p_{\theta^{\prime}}(X)\mathsf{d}Q^{\star}(\theta^{\prime})}\right)^{4}\hskip-5.69046pt\mathsf{d}Q^{\star}(\theta)\right\}^{1/2}\hskip-2.84544pt\left\{\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\int\hskip-2.84544pt\log\left(\frac{p_{\theta}(X)}{\int p_{\theta^{\prime}}(X)\mathsf{d}Q^{\star}(\theta^{\prime})}\right)^{4}\hskip-5.69046pt\mathsf{d}Q^{\star}(\theta)\right)^{1/4}\hskip-8.5359pt+1\right\}\right]<\infty.

This technical condition
ensures that the normalized weights
{p_{\theta}(x)}/\int p_{\theta}(x)\mathsf{d}Q^{\star}(\theta)
admit finite moments, is similar to standard requirement in the variational inference literature, and is inspired by Assumption (A4) of
Cherief-Abdellatif et al. 2025
. Under Assumption
9
, we can obtain a more delicate level of control for MS-style approximations beyond that which was obtained in Lemma
1
.

Theorem 6
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2

(
MS
)

,
3
and
9
, and for
S(P,x)=\log P(x)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\textnormal{KL}\left(P_{0}\,\|P_{Q_{n}}\right)\right]\leq\mathrm{KL}\left(P_{0}\|P_{Q^{\star}}\right)+\frac{\textnormal{KL}(Q^{\star}\|\Pi)}{\lambda_{n}}+\frac{\lambda_{n}kC_{k}^{2}}{n}+\frac{M_{2}}{k},

where
M_{2}
is a positive constant independent of
n
and
k
.

Theorem
6
works by controlling the approximation gap
\mathsf{AppGap}(Q^{\star},k)
at the predictively optimal
Q^{\star}
.
It demonstrates that doing this results in a
PrO
posterior with three key features:

1.

they are covered by a non-asymptotic guarantee:
the distance between the
PrO
posterior predictive and
P_{0}
can be controlled in finite samples;

2.

the difference between the
PrO
posterior predictive and the true data-generating process vanishes as
n\to\infty
whenever both
k
and
\lambda_{n}
grow sufficiently slowly with
n
;

3.

they are the first of their kind and improve the previous attempts of
Morningstar
et al. 2022
whose bounds diverged in
k
due to the presence of a term independent of
n
but increasing in
k
.

Interestingly, Theorem
6
shows that in general, the classical
n^{-1/2}
rate of of convergence is not recoverable by tuning
\lambda_{n}
and
k
: if one were to choose
k\propto n^{1/2}
in an attempt to make the third term of the bound proportional to
n^{-1/2}
, one would force the second term to diverge at a rate much slower than
n^{-1/2}
.
While this remains true even in the unlikely event where
C_{k}
is constant, the problem is worsened by the fact that
C_{k}
will generally diverge as
k\rightarrow\infty
.
While it would be possible to formally derive a rate slower than
n^{-1/2}
without any further assumptions on
C_{k}
, the exact speed of this rate would depend on the speed at which
C_{k}
diverges in
k
.
As
C_{k}
is not an interpretable quantity in general, we impose a further assumption that is well-understood in the literature on PAC-Bayes, and which controls the growth of
C_{k}
to be at most logarithmic in
k
.

Assumption 10
.

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, any
\theta\in\Theta
and some
C>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\Big\{\log p_{\theta}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\log p_{\theta}(X)]\Big\}\right)\right]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}C^{2}}\,.

The above assumption is a weaker version of Assumption
3
: instead of imposing a Hoeffding-type condition on
\log p_{\theta_{1:k}}(X)
for any
k
, it imposes this type of condition only for the special case of
k=1
.
This condition is standard in PAC-Bayes analyses, and the precise condition used in Theorem 2 of
Morningstar
et al. 2022
.
Importantly, there is a subtle difference in the nature of the Hoeffding condition between Assumption
3
and Assumption
10
: for the former, sub-Gaussianity is assumed to hold
jointly
over the data space and the parameter space, while the latter assumes that it holds marginally over the data space, but pointwise
everywhere
on
\Theta
.
Though the joint version of this condition was previously used in Corollaries 4 and 5 of
Germain et al. 2016
, the proof provided therein is incorrect, and actually requires the pointwise everywhere sub-Gaussianity in Assumption
10
that is also used by
Morningstar
et al. 2022
to work.

Under this assumption,
C_{k}^{2}
now grows at most logarithmically with
k
.
This allows for a stronger bound, which we state below, and which implies that up to logarithmic terms, the choices
k\propto n^{1/3}
and
\lambda_{n}\propto n^{1/3}
yield a convergence rate of
n^{-1/3}
.

Theorem 7
.

If Assumption
10
is satisfied, then Assumption
3
holds with
C_{k}^{2}\propto C^{2}\log k
.
Consequently, under Assumptions
1
,
2

(
MS
)

, and
9
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\textnormal{KL}\left(P_{0}\,\|P_{Q_{n}}\right)\right]\leq\mathrm{KL}\left(P_{0}\|P_{Q^{\star}}\right)+\frac{\textnormal{KL}(Q^{\star}\|\Pi)}{\lambda_{n}}+\frac{M_{1}C^{2}\lambda_{n}k\log(k)}{n}+\frac{M_{2}}{k},

where
M_{1},M_{2}
are positive constants independent of
n
and
k
. Setting
\lambda_{n}\propto(n/k\log k)^{1/2}
and
k\propto(n/\log n)^{1/3}
, we have for some
M>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\textnormal{KL}\left(P_{0}\,\big\|P_{Q_{n}}\right)\right]\leq\mathrm{KL}\left(P_{0}\big\|P_{Q^{\star}}\right)+M\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{1/3}.

The inequalities in Theorem
7
are entirely new, and demonstrate that, under mild conditions, the gap between the predictions of a
PrO
posterior based on an MS-approximate predictive score and those of the predictively optimal oracle
Q^{\star}
remains small in finite samples.
More importantly, this gap vanishes provided the approximation quality of the score (indexed by
k
) improves sufficiently slowly with
n
.
This bound represents a substantial improvement over the prior result of
Morningstar
et al. 2022
:
whereas their bound
diverged
at rate
\log k
for their optimal
\lambda_{n}
as
k
increases—rendering it completely vacuous for large
k
—the above bound
converges
at rate
(\log(n)/n)^{1/3}
if both
\lambda_{n}
and
k
are chosen to grow at rate
(n/\log(n))^{1/3}
.

Critically, Theorem
7
made no assumption on the precise nature of misspecification. This enables the following result, which trivially follows by combining Theorem
7
with various misspecification regimes, and is thus presented without proof.
Importantly, the below corresponds exactly to Corollary
1
in the main text.

Corollary 5

Under the Assumptions of Theorem

,

\operatorname{(WS)}

\mathbb{E}\left[\textnormal{KL}\left(P_{0}\,\big\|P_{Q_{n}}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}[\textnormal{KL}(P_{0}\,\big\|P_{Q_{n}^{\dagger}})]\leq C(\log n/n)^{1/3}

This section contains several useful lemmas that are used throughout the remainder of the text.
First, we demonstrate our claim that

To prove this, take

, let
\kappa(x,x^{\prime})
denote a positive-definite kernel function with feature map
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}

\kappa(x,x^{\prime})=\langle\phi(x),\phi(x^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\theta\in\Theta

\mu_{\theta}\in\mathcal{H}
by

\mu_{\theta}:=\int\phi(z)\,\mathsf{d}P_{\theta}(z)
and the inner product

\langle\mu_{\theta},\mu_{\theta^{\prime}}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\int\!\!\int\kappa(z,z^{\prime})\,\mathsf{d}P_{\theta}(z)\mathsf{d}P_{\theta^{\prime}}(z^{\prime}).
Following
Gneiting
et al. 2007
, kernel scoring rules can be expressed as

S(P,x)=\mathbb{E}_{X,X^{\prime}\sim P}[\kappa(X,X^{\prime})]-2\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[\kappa(X,x)]+\kappa(x,x).

Lemma 3
.

For any kernel scoring rule, and for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
: (i) The score
P_{\theta}\mapsto S(P_{\theta},x)
is convex; (ii)
S(P_{Q},x)=\int L(\theta_{1:2},x)\mathsf{d}Q^{2}(\theta_{1:2})
with

L(\theta_{1:2},x)=S(P_{\theta_{1}},x)-\frac{1}{2}\|\mu_{\theta_{1}}-\mu_{\theta_{2}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}.

Proof.

To see that (i) is satisfied, use the feature map representation to write the score as

S(P_{\theta},x)=\|\mu_{\theta}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-2\langle\mu_{\theta},\phi(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+\|\phi(x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

The map
P_{\theta}\mapsto\mu_{\theta}
is linear, so that
P_{\theta}\mapsto\|\mu_{\theta}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}
is quadratic. Thus,
P_{\theta}\mapsto S(P_{\theta},x)
is the composition of a linear map and a convex function, and is convex. To deduce the second result, write

S\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),x\right)=\int S(P_{\theta},x)\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)+\underbrace{S\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta),x\right)-\int S(P_{\theta},x)\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)}_{=-\Delta(Q,x)\text{ in \eqref{eq:predictive-score-decomposition}}}.

Expand the first integral, and use the mean embedding definition for
\mu_{\theta}
, to write

\displaystyle S\left(P_{Q},x\right)=\int\int\int\int\kappa(z,z^{\prime})\,\mathsf{d}P_{\theta}(z)\mathsf{d}P_{\theta^{\prime}}(z^{\prime})\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)\mathsf{d}Q(\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q}(\mu_{\theta}),\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{\prime}\sim Q}(\mu_{\theta^{\prime}})\rangle

\displaystyle=\|\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q}(\mu_{\theta})\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}.

Similarly, write

\displaystyle\int S\left(P_{\theta},x\right)\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int\int\int\kappa(z,z^{\prime})\,\mathsf{d}P_{\theta}(z)\mathsf{d}P_{\theta}(z^{\prime})\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q}[\|\mu_{\theta}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}].

Hence, for
\Delta(Q,x)
as in (
2
),

\displaystyle-\Delta(Q,x)=\|\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q}[\mu_{\theta}]\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}-\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q}[\|\mu_{\theta}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q}\left[\|\mu_{\theta}-\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{\prime}\sim Q}[\mu_{\theta^{\prime}}]\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int\|\mu_{\theta}-\mu_{\theta^{\prime}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\mathsf{d}Q(\theta)\mathsf{d}Q(\theta^{\prime}).

∎

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q_{n}(\theta),P_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int\mathcal{S}(P_{\theta},P_{0})\mathsf{d}Q_{n}(\theta)-\int\delta(\theta_{1:k})\mathsf{d}Q^{k}(\theta_{1:k})\equiv\int L(\theta_{1:k})\mathsf{d}Q_{n}(\theta_{1:k}),

where
\delta(\theta_{1:k})=\mathbb{E}[\delta(\theta_{1:k},X)]
; alternatively, by Assumption
1
, under the MS-class of approximations (Assumption
2
(MS)), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\left(\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q_{n}(\theta),P_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}P_{\theta_{j}},P_{0}\right)\mathsf{d}Q_{n}^{k}(\theta_{1:k})\equiv\int L(\theta_{1:k})\mathsf{d}Q_{n}(\theta_{1:k}).

Finally, and recalling
P_{Q_{n}}=\int P_{\theta}\mathsf{d}Q_{n}(\theta)
, this gives us

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\left\{\mathop{\text{\large$\int$}}\nolimits L_{n}(\theta_{1:k})\mathsf{d}Q^{k}(\theta_{1:k})+\frac{\text{KL}(Q^{k}\|\Pi^{k})}{k\lambda_{n}}\right\}+\frac{\lambda_{n}kC_{k}^{2}}{n}

(8)

as desired.

To obtain the second result, notice that when no approximation is used, we have for any
Q
and any
x
that

S\left(P_{Q},x\right)=\widehat{S}\left(P_{Q},x\right)\equiv\int L(\theta_{1:k},x)\mathsf{d}Q^{k}(\theta_{1:k}),

which implies that for any
Q
, we have

S_{n}(P_{Q})\equiv\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S\left(P_{Q},x_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int L(\theta_{1:k},x_{i})\mathsf{d}Q^{k}(\theta_{1:k})\equiv\int L_{n}(\theta_{1:k})\mathsf{d}Q^{k}(\theta_{1:k}).

Using the above, we can rewrite equation (
8
) as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathcal{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\left\{\mathop{\text{\large$\int$}}\nolimits L_{n}(\theta_{1:k})\mathsf{d}Q^{k}(\theta_{1:k})+\frac{\text{KL}(Q^{k}\|\Pi^{k})}{k\lambda_{n}}\right\}\right]+\frac{\lambda_{n}kC_{k}^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\left\{S_{n}(P_{Q})+\frac{\text{KL}(Q^{k}\|\Pi^{k})}{k\lambda_{n}}\right\}\right]+\frac{\lambda_{n}kC_{k}^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\left\{\mathcal{S}(P_{Q},P_{0})+\frac{\text{KL}(Q^{k}\|\Pi^{k})}{k\lambda_{n}}\right\}+\frac{\lambda_{n}kC_{k}^{2}}{n}.

Recalling that
\mathcal{S}(P_{0},P_{0})=\mathbb{E}S_{n}(P_{0})
, we can subtract
\mathcal{S}(P_{0},P_{0})
from both sides and write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{\mathcal{D}_{S}(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0})\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathcal{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)-\mathcal{S}(P_{0},P_{0})\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\left\{\mathcal{S}(P_{Q},P_{0})-\mathcal{S}(P_{Q},P_{0})+\frac{\text{KL}(Q^{k}\|\Pi^{k})}{k\lambda_{n}}\right\}+\frac{\lambda_{n}kC_{k}^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}\left\{\mathcal{D}(P_{Q},P_{0})+\frac{\text{KL}(Q^{k}\|\Pi^{k})}{k\lambda_{n}}\right\}+\frac{\lambda_{n}kC_{k}^{2}}{n}.

∎

where the first bound comes from Theorem
5
, and the second from Lemma
5
. Applying the above bounds into (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{d}_{2}(P_{Q_{n}},P_{Q_{n}^{\dagger}})]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\{\log(n)/n^{1/2}\}^{1/\alpha}.

(11)

From equation (
11
), and Assumption
8
(i) - identifiability and continuity of
\theta\mapsto P_{\theta}
- conclude that
P_{Q_{n}}\rightarrow P_{0}
and
P_{Q_{n}^{\dagger}}\rightarrow P_{0}
. Hence, by Assumption

large enough so that, for
n\geq N_{1}

Since

For

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{d}_{1}(Q_{n},\updelta_{\theta^{\star}})]\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{d}_{1}(Q_{n},Q_{n}^{\dagger})]+\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{d}_{1}(Q_{n}^{\dagger},\updelta_{\theta^{\star}})]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{d}_{2}(P_{Q_{n}},P_{Q_{n}^{\dagger}})]+C\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{d}_{2}(P_{Q_{n}^{\dagger}},P_{0})]

Proof of Theorem

.

The proof exploits the following upper bound from Lemma

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{D}_{S}\left(P_{Q_{n}},P_{0}\right)\right]

Q^{\star}(\theta_{1:k})=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{K}Q^{\star}(\theta)

\displaystyle\overset{(6)}{=}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{0}}\left[\sqrt{\frac{1}{k}\cdot\mathbb{V}_{\theta\sim Q^{\star}}\left[\frac{p_{\theta}(x)}{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q^{\star}}\left[p_{\theta}(x)\right]}\right]}\cdot\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1:k}\sim Q^{\star}(\theta_{1:k})}\left[\log\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{p_{\theta_{j}}(x)}{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q^{\star}}\left[p_{\theta}(x)\right]}\right)^{2}\right]}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(7)}{\leq}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{0}}\bigg[\sqrt{\frac{1}{k}\cdot\mathbb{V}_{\theta\sim Q^{\star}}\left[\frac{p_{\theta}(x)}{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q^{\star}}\left[p_{\theta}(x)\right]}\right]}\times

\displaystyle\cdot\frac{M^{\prime}}{\sqrt{k}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q^{\star}}\left[\left(\frac{p_{\theta}(x)}{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q^{\star}}\left[p_{\theta}(x)\right]}\right)^{4}\right]^{1/4}\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q^{\star}}\left[\log\left(\frac{p_{\theta}(x)}{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q^{\star}}\left[p_{\theta}(x)\right]}\right)^{4}\right]^{1/4}+1\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle\overset{(8)}{\leq}\frac{M^{\prime}}{k}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{0}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q^{\star}}\left[\left(\frac{p_{\theta}(x)}{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q^{\star}}\left[p_{\theta}(x)\right]}\right)^{4}\right]^{1/2}\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q^{\star}}\left[\log\left(\frac{p_{\theta}(x)}{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q^{\star}}\left[p_{\theta}(x)\right]}\right)^{4}\right]^{1/4}+1\right)\right].

(0)

So we have

Proof of Theorem
7

.

The only change from the proof of Theorem
6
here consists in establishing that Assumption
3
holds with
C_{k}^{2}\propto C^{2}\log k
under Assumption
10
. The rest of the proof directly follows from Equation (
12
).

We now show Assumption
3
holds with
C_{k}^{2}\propto C^{2}\log k
under Assumption
10
. Our proof relies on three key observations:

1.

Everywhere vs. Joint sub-Gaussianity.

As discussed in Section
7.2
, the
everywhere
counterpart of Assumption
3

\forall(\theta_{1:k})\in\Theta^{k},\quad\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda(\mathcal{L}(\theta_{1:k})-L_{n}(\theta_{1:k}))}\right]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}C_{k}^{2}/n},

is stronger than the
joint
version we use in the paper, so we directly derive the everywhere version in this proof.

2.

Sub-Gaussianity and Concentration.

Assumption
3
is essentially a sub-Gaussianity condition. The factor
1/n
in the bound arises because we consider empirical averages:

L_{n}(\theta_{1:k})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(\theta_{1:k},x_{i}).

Therefore, it suffices to show the sub-Gaussian bound for a single variable:

\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[e^{\lambda(\mathcal{L}(\theta_{1:k})-L(\theta_{1:k},X))}\right]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}C_{k}^{2}}.

3.

Alternative Definition of Sub-Gaussianity.

In the proof, we use the following characterization of sub-Gaussianity:

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{(Z-\mathbb{E}[Z])^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{2}}\right)\right]\leq 2,

where the minimal
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{2}
(the
sub-Gaussian norm
) is proportional to the minimal
C_{k}^{2}
used in Assumption
3
(the
sub-Gaussian parameter
), see e.g.
Leskelä and
Zhukov 2025
.

We can now state the result.

Theorem 8
.

Assume that Assumption
10
is satisfied, that is, for any single
\theta\in\Theta
, the log-likelihood is sub-Gaussian: for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\Big\{\log p_{\theta}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\log p_{\theta}(X)]\Big\}\right)\right]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}C^{2}}.

Then, for any
(\theta_{1:k})\in\Theta^{k}
, there exists a constant
\gamma>0
independent of
(\theta_{1:k})
such that, for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\Bigg\{\log\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{\theta_{j}}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{X}\Big[\log\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{\theta_{j}}(X)\Big]\Bigg\}\right)\right]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}\gamma C^{2}\log k}.

In other words,
C_{k}^{2}\propto C^{2}\log k
for the log-score.

Proof.

We look for a constant
\mathcal{C}_{k}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X}\exp\Bigg(\frac{\Big(\log\big(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{\theta_{j}}(X)\big)-\mathbb{E}_{X}\big[\log\big(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{\theta_{j}}(X)\big)\big]\Big)^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{2}}\Bigg)\leq 2.

(13)

Once (
13
) is established, we immediately obtain that there exists
C_{k}^{2}=\gamma\mathcal{C}_{k}^{2}
such that

\mathbb{E}_{X}\Big[\exp\big(\lambda\big\{\log(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{\theta_{j}}(X))-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\log(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{\theta_{j}}(X))]\big\}\big)\Big]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}\gamma C_{k}^{2}}

for some
\gamma>0
, by the equivalence between sub-Gaussian norms and parameters.

Step 1: Rewrite using the log-sum-exp function.

For any
(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{k})\in\Theta^{k}
, almost surely:

\log\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{\theta_{j}}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{X}\big[\log\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{\theta_{j}}(X)\big]=\text{LSE}(\{\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)\}_{1\leq j\leq k})-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\text{LSE}(\{\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)\}_{1\leq j\leq k})],

where
\text{LSE}(z_{1:k})=\log\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{z_{j}}
.

Step 2: Apply Jensen and Lipschitz properties.

For any
\mathcal{C}_{k}>0
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X}\exp\Bigg(\frac{\big(\text{LSE}(\{\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)\})-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\text{LSE}(\{\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)\})]\big)^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{2}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\exp\Bigg(\frac{\big(\text{LSE}(\{\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)\})-\mathbb{E}_{Y}[\text{LSE}(\{\log p_{\theta_{j}}(Y)\})]\big)^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{2}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\exp\Bigg(\frac{\big(\text{LSE}(\{\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)\})-\text{LSE}(\{\log p_{\theta_{j}}(Y)\})\big)^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{2}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\exp\Bigg(\frac{\big\|\{\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)\}-\{\log p_{\theta_{j}}(Y)\}\big\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{2}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\exp\Bigg(\frac{\sup_{1\leq j\leq k}\big((\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)])-(\log p_{\theta_{j}}(Y)-\mathbb{E}_{Y}[\log p_{\theta_{j}}(Y)])\big)^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{2}}\Bigg).

Step 3: Apply the inequality
(a-b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
and Jensen.

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\exp\left(\frac{\sup_{1\leq j\leq k}\left\{2\left(\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)]\right)^{2}+2\left(\log p_{\theta_{j}}(Y)-\mathbb{E}_{Y}[\log p_{\theta_{j}}(Y)]\right)^{2}\right\}}{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X,Y}\exp\left(\frac{2\sup_{1\leq j\leq k}\left(\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)]\right)^{2}+2\sup_{1\leq j\leq k}\left(\log p_{\theta_{j}}(Y)-\mathbb{E}_{Y}[\log p_{\theta_{j}}(Y)]\right)^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\exp\left(\frac{2\sup_{1\leq j\leq k}\left(\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)]\right)^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{2}}\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\exp\left(\frac{2\sup_{1\leq j\leq k}\left(\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)]\right)^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{2}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\exp\left(\frac{4\sup_{1\leq j\leq k}\left(\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)]\right)^{2}}{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\sup_{1\leq j\leq k}\left(\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)]\right)^{2}}{(\mathcal{C}_{k}/2)^{2}}\right)\right]\,.

Step 4: Bound the supremum via standard sub-Gaussian inequalities.

By definition, the last term is bounded by
2
if
(\mathcal{C}_{k}/2)^{2}
is taken as the squared sub-Gaussian norm of

\sup_{j}(\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)])
, which is itself upper bounded (up to a universal factor) by the maximum of the squared sub-Gaussian norms of the individual
\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)
multiplied by
\log k
(see, e.g., Lemma 2.2.2 in
Van Der Vaart and
Wellner 1996
applied to
\psi(x)=e^{x^{2}}-1
).

Step 5: Conclude.

Since each
\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\log p_{\theta_{j}}(X)]
has sub-Gaussian parameter
C^{2}
, it also has squared sub-Gaussian norm
C^{2}
up to a factor, and we finally have

\mathcal{C}_{k}^{2}\leq\gamma^{\prime}\,C^{2}\log k,

for some universal constant
\gamma^{\prime}>0
. This establishes (
13
) and thus proves the theorem.
∎

sec:synthetic-regression-supp
Denote

\ell(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\,X,y)\approx\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\kappa(Y_{i},Y^{\prime}_{j})-\frac{1}{m}\kappa(Y_{i},y)-\frac{1}{m}\kappa(Y_{i}^{\prime},y),

and

Garreau
et al. 2018

D.6
Synthetic linear regression

In Section
5.2
we set
\xi=2,\,\sigma^{2}=1,
and
\eta=0.5
to generate
n=500
training data.
The log point-wise predictive density (lppd) is estimated from
S
samples from the posterior
\{\theta^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^{S}
at a point
\{X_{i},y_{i}\}
through

\operatorname{lppd}_{i}=\log\left\{\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}P_{\theta^{(s)}}(y_{i}\mid X_{i})\right\}.

In the right-most panel of Figure
6
we plot the difference
\Delta_{i}=\operatorname{lppd}_{i}^{(\operatorname{PrO})}-\operatorname{lppd}_{i}^{(\operatorname{Gibbs})}
.
And since we want to maximise the log score, if
\Delta_{i}>0
we say that the
PrO
posterior induces better predictive performance.
The expected log predictive density (elpd) on a test set,
\{X_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{test}}}
is computed as

\operatorname{elpd}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{test}}}\operatorname{lppd}_{i}.

The
PrO
posterior attains an elpd of
-1834.68
on
n_{\mathsf{test}}=1,000
test data, while the Gibbs posterior performs much worse with an elpd of
-3593.67
on the same data.

The MMD was estimated using
m=2^{5}
Monte Carlo samples, and the Wasserstein gradient flow was run with
\mathsf{d}t=5\times 10^{-3}
using
p=2^{4}
particles and
\lambda_{n}=n^{1/2}
for
4,000
iterations, while MALA was run with
\mathsf{d}t=10^{-4}
also for
4,000
iterations after
96,000
warmup iterations to ensure convergence.
