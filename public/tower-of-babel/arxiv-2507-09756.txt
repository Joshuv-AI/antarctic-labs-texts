Evidence for Intrinsic Galaxy Alignments in Ellipticity Autocorrelations out to 100 ​ h − 1 ​ Mpc 100\,h^{-1}\,{\rm Mpc} from SDSS Galaxies with DESI Imaging
Teppei Okumura

Abstract
Measuring the autocorrelation of galaxy shapes, the so-called intrinsic-intrinsic (II) correlation is important for both cosmology and understanding the formation of massive elliptical galaxies. However, such measurements are significantly more challenging than the cross-correlation with galaxy density (GI correlation) due to the much lower signal-to-noise ratio. In this paper, we report the first observational evidence for large-scale intrinsic alignments measured from the ellipticity autocorrelations, extending out to 100 ​ h − 1 ​ Mpc 100\,h^{-1}\,{\rm Mpc} . From SDSS and SDSS-III BOSS, we analyze over the redshift range 0.16 ≤ z ≤ 0.70 0.16\leq z\leq 0.70 luminous red galaxies (LRG), LOWZ and CMASS galaxy samples, the latter two of which are cross-matched with high-quality DESI imaging data. By expanding one of the two II correlation functions, II ⁡ ( − ) \mathrm{II}(-) , in terms of the associated Legendre polynomials, we effectively isolate the line-of-sight projection effects and enhance the signal. The resulting correlation for all three samples exhibits a clear power-law form. We also show that combining the two II correlations, II ⁡ ( + ) \mathrm{II}(+) and II ⁡ ( − ) \mathrm{II}(-) , increases the detection significance by ∼ 10 % \sim 10\% , though they appear equivalent mathematically under the linear alignment model. Importantly, this measurement opens a new observational window for probing signals uniquely encoded in shape autocorrelations, such as tensor perturbations from the gravitational waves. This analysis establishes a practical framework for probing such effects.



I
Introduction

Intrinsic alignments (IA) of galaxy shapes are a key phenomenon linking large-scale structure with galaxy formation and offering a complementary probe of cosmology.
Originally proposed as a contaminant to weak gravitational lensing measurements, the autocorrelation of galaxy shapes, known as the intrinsic–intrinsic shape (II) correlation, was first studied

(
Heavens et al. 2000
;
Croft & Metzler 2000
;
Lee & Pen 2000
;
Catelan et al. 2001
;
Jing 2002
;
Crittenden et al. 2002
)
.

Hirata & Seljak 2004
later emphasized that the cross-correlation between galaxy positions and intrinsic shape (GI correlation) can be an even more serious systematic.
Because GI correlations are easier to measure observationally, especially using bright galaxies or clusters, they have become the dominant focus in large-scale IA studies, both theoretically and observationally
(
Mandelbaum et al. 2006
;
Hirata et al. 2007
;
Okumura & Jing 2009
;
Joachimi et al. 2011
;
Smargon et al. 2012
;
Li et al. 2013
;
Singh et al. 2015
;
van Uitert & Joachimi 2017
;
Tonegawa & Okumura 2022
;
Desai & Ryden 2022
;
Xu et al. 2023a
;
Hervas Peters et al. 2024
;
Shi et al. 2024
;
Lamman et al. 2024
)
.
In contrast, II correlations, which directly probe alignments between galaxy shapes, are significantly noisier and more difficult to detect at large scales. Consequently, only a few studies have succeeded in measuring them with high significance.
Okumura et al. 2009
reported the clearest detection to date, finding an ellipticity autocorrelation signal out to
\sim 30\,h^{-1}\,{\rm Mpc}
using luminous red galaxies (LRGs) from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS).

Recently, the potential of IA measurements as cosmological probes has attracted growing attention. In particular, the use of ellipticity correlations in three dimensions has been shown to enable constraints on redshift-space distortions (RSD) and baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO), offering both dynamical and geometric information
(
Chisari & Dvorkin 2013
;
Okumura et al. 2019
;
Taruya & Okumura 2020
;
Okumura & Taruya 2022
;
Chuang et al. 2022
;
Lee et al. 2023
;
Saga et al. 2023
;
Shim et al. 2025
)
. However, observational constraints at large scales continue to rely almost exclusively on GI correlations
(
Okumura & Taruya 2023
;
Kurita & Takada 2023
;
Xu et al. 2023b
)
, due to the difficulty of extracting clean II signals from data.

Despite the challenges, measuring shape autocorrelations remains essential because they carry unique information inaccessible to GI cross-correlations. Particularly, shape autocorrelations play a critical role in probing new physics, such as the stochastic gravitational wave (GW) background, through their imprint on galaxy orientations

(
Schmidt et al. 2014
;
Biagetti & Orlando 2020
;
Akitsu et al. 2023a
;
Philcox et al. 2024
;
Saga et al. 2024
;
Okumura & Sasaki 2024
)
.
Recent work showed that IA signals projected along the line of sight are more naturally expanded using the associated Legendre basis rather than the standard Legendre one, due to the geometric anisotropy of projected shapes
(
Kurita & Takada 2022
;
Okumura et al. 2024
;
Singh et al. 2024
;
Inoue et al. 2025
)
. This formalism improves the separation of physical signals from noise and enhances interpretability at large scales.

In this paper, we measure the II correlation functions using various galaxy samples from the SDSS and SDSS-III Baryon Oscillation Spectroscopic Survey (BOSS), covering the redshift range
0.16\leq z\leq 0.70
. To maximize the signal-to-noise ratio (S/N), we expand the II correlations in the associated Legendre basis. Following
Xu et al. 2023b
, we further improve the shape measurements by cross-matching the BOSS spectroscopic galaxy samples with Dark Energy Spectrograph Instrument (DESI) imaging data. This allows us to report the first observational evidence for intrinsic galaxy alignments in ellipticity autocorrelations out to
100\,h^{-1}\,{\rm Mpc}
, a scale comparable to that of the BAO. Our results demonstrate that galaxy shape autocorrelations can be robustly measured at cosmological scales, opening a new window for using IA as a cosmological probe. Throughout this paper, we adopt a flat
\Lambda
CDM model determined by
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
.

0.16\leq z\leq 0.70
using spectroscopic samples from SDSS-II

Eisenstein et al. 2001

, 355781, and 737435, respectively.

. Since our primary interest is in the statistical accuracy of the shape correlations, rather than the amplitude of IA, this simplification does not affect our conclusions.

III
Measurement of Correlation Functions

We measure two types of intrinsic ellipticity autocorrelations,
\mathrm{II}(+)
and
\mathrm{II}(-)
correlations
denoted by
\xi_{+}
and
\xi_{-}
, respectively.
These are
defined as density-weighted two-point statistics of galaxy ellipticities:

\xi_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{r})=\left\langle\left[1+\delta_{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\right]\left[1+\delta_{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\right]W_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\right\rangle,

(5)

where
W_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2})=\gamma_{+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\gamma_{+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\pm\gamma_{\times}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\gamma_{\times}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})
.
We estimate
\xi_{\pm}
using the pair-based estimator
(
Mandelbaum et al. 2006
)
,

\displaystyle\xi_{\pm}({\boldsymbol{r}})=\frac{S_{+}S_{+}\pm S_{\times}S_{\times}}{RR}\ ,

(6)

where
RR
is the normalized count of random-random pairs, and
S_{+}S_{+}=\sum_{i\neq j|{\boldsymbol{r}}}{\gamma_{+}(j|i)\gamma_{+}(i|j)}
and similarly for
S_{\times}S_{\times}
.

Since the IA correlations are anisotropic functions, we bin galaxy pairs in both radial separation
r=|\boldsymbol{r}|
and the cosine of the angle to the line of sight,
\mu_{\boldsymbol{r}}
.
We then compute the multipole moments of the correlation functions using the Legendre expansion:

\xi_{\pm,\ell}(r)=\frac{2\ell+1}{2}\sum_{i}\Delta\mu_{\boldsymbol{r}}~\xi_{\pm}(r,\mu_{\boldsymbol{r},i}){\cal L}_{\ell}(\mu_{\boldsymbol{r},i})~,

(7)

where
{\cal L}_{\ell}
is the
\ell
th-order Legendre polynomial and
\Delta\mu_{\boldsymbol{r}}=0.1
is the angular bin size.
Due to the geometric factor arising from the projection of the shape field along the line of sight (see Eq. (
14
) below), the
\mathrm{II}(-)
correlation is more naturally expanded in terms of the associated Legendre polynomials,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\xi}_{-,L}(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\Delta\mu_{\boldsymbol{r}}\,\xi_{-}(r,\mu_{\boldsymbol{r},i})\,\Theta_{L}^{m=4}(\mu_{\boldsymbol{r},i})~,

(8)

where
\Theta_{L}^{m}(\mu)
is the normalized associated Legendre function related to the unnormalized one by

\Theta_{L}^{m}(\mu)=\sqrt{\frac{2L+1}{2}\frac{(L-m)!}{(L+m)!}}\mathcal{L}_{L}^{m}(\mu_{\boldsymbol{k}})
and
L\geq m=4
.
We use a tilde to distinguish these coefficients from those expanded in the standard Legendre basis.
The summation in Eqs. (
7
) and (
8
) is performed over
-1\leq\mu_{\boldsymbol{r}}\leq 1
.

While not an unbiased estimator, the jackknife method provides reliable error bars when the dominant noise is shot noise

.

In this analysis, we focus only on the two lowest multipoles: the monopole (
\ell=0
) and quadrupole (
\ell=2
), as the hexadecapole (
\ell=4
) is too noisy.
Even for the lower multipoles, it is non-trivial to argue up to which scale the correlation is detected.
The monopoles of
\mathrm{II}(+)
appear non-zero for all three samples up to several tens of
\,h^{-1}\,{\rm Mpc}
, while all the quadrupoles remain statistically consistent with zero.
For the
\mathrm{II}(-)
correlation, the monopoles are better measured than those of
\mathrm{II}(+)
.
The detection for LRG up to
\sim 40\,h^{-1}\,{\rm Mpc}
corresponds to the earlier finding of
Okumura et al. 2009
.
Notably, the LOWZ and CMASS results are significantly improved compared to those in
Okumura & Taruya 2023
, due to the enhanced imaging depth and quality provided by the DESI data.
The quadrupole of
\mathrm{II}(-)
for CMASS appears non-zero over a relatively large range of scales.
However, because the signal is distributed over multiple multipoles and the overall measurements remain noisy, these results do not allow a definitive detection based solely on the standard Legendre expansion.

In the upper row of Fig.
2
, we present the
\mathrm{II}(-)
correlations expanded in the associated Legendre basis. The lower row shows the same quantities, but with the vertical axis rescaled by
r^{2}
, allowing direct comparison with the lower set of Figure
1
.
While both figures represent the same underlying correlations, they are shown in different functional bases.
In contrast to the standard Legendre expansion, where the signal is split across several multipoles, the linear-order intrinsic alignment signal is concentrated entirely in the
L=4
mode when expanded in the associated Legendre basis.

The resulting measurements exhibit a clear, smooth power-law trend extending to
100\,h^{-1}\,{\rm Mpc}
for all three samples.
This includes the LRG sample, which closely matches that used in
Okumura et al. 2009
, except for a modest extension from SDSS DR6 to DR7. The much clearer detection achieved here is therefore entirely due to the improved basis choice.
Furthermore, the combination of this optimal basis and the deep DESI imaging enables similarly robust detections of
\mathrm{II}(-)
autocorrelations for the LOWZ and CMASS samples.
These constitute the first unambiguous measurements of intrinsic shape autocorrelations extending to
100\,h^{-1}\,{\rm Mpc}
, across multiple redshift bins.

To model the IA statistics, we consider the nonlinear alignment (NLA) model, which assumes a linear relation between the intrinsic ellipticity and underlying nonlinear tidal field,
\left(\frac{\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}}{\nabla^{2}}\right)\delta_{m}(\boldsymbol{x})

(
Catelan et al. 2001
;
Hirata & Seljak 2004
;
Bridle & King 2007
)
, In Fourier
space, the ellipticity projected along the line of sight (
z
-axis) is
given by

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\gamma_{+}\\
\gamma_{\times}\end{array}\right)(\boldsymbol{k})=b_{K}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}(k_{x}^{2}-k_{y}^{2})/k^{2}\\
2k_{x}k_{y}/k^{2}\end{array}\right)\delta_{m}(\boldsymbol{k}),

where

\delta_{m}
is the nonlinear matter density field and

b_{K}
is the redshift-dependent coefficient of the IA, which we refer to as the shape bias.
Furthermore, the redshift-space shape field is multiplied by the damping function due to the Finger-of-God (FoG) effect.
We then define E-/B-modes,
\gamma_{(E,B)}
, which are the
rotation-invariant decomposition of the ellipticity field

(
Crittenden et al. 2002
)
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{E}(\boldsymbol{k})+i\gamma_{B}(\boldsymbol{k})=e^{-2i\phi_{k}}\left[\gamma_{+}(\boldsymbol{k})+i\gamma_{\times}(\boldsymbol{k})\right],

(13)

where
\phi_{k}
is the azimuthal angle of the wavevector projected on the celestial sphere.
Adopting the NLA model, the autopower spectrum of the
E
-mode in redshift space is given by
(
Okumura et al. 2024
)
,

\displaystyle P_{EE}(\boldsymbol{k})

\displaystyle=b_{K}^{2}(1-\mu_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2})^{2}P_{\delta\delta}(k)D_{\rm FoG}^{2}(k\mu_{\boldsymbol{k}}\sigma_{v})~,

(14)

where
k=|\boldsymbol{k}|
,
\mu_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is the direction cosine between the
line of sight and the wavevector
\boldsymbol{k}
, and

P_{\delta\delta}
is the nonlinear matter power spectrum computed by the revised
Halofit

(
Takahashi et al. 2012
)
.

Since the FoG effect comes into IA power spectra in the same way as the conventional galaxy power spectrum, we adopt a simple Gaussian function to characterize the FoG damping,
D_{\rm FoG}(k\mu_{\boldsymbol{k}}\sigma_{v})=\exp{\left(-k^{2}\mu_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}\sigma_{v}^{2}/2\right)}
, with
\sigma_{v}
the nonlinear velocity dispersion parameter.
Since
P_{\delta\delta}
is proportional to the square of the normalization
parameter of the density fluctuation,
\sigma_{8}^{2}(z)
, free parameters for this model are
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(b_{K}\sigma_{8},\sigma_{v})
.

To take into account the line-of-sight anisotropy in Eq. (
14
), we use the multipole expansion in terms of the standard or associated Legendre polynomials
(
Kurita & Takada 2022
;
Okumura et al. 2024
)
,

\displaystyle P_{EE}(\boldsymbol{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}P_{EE,\ell}(k){\cal L}_{\ell}(\mu_{\boldsymbol{k}})

(15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{L\geq 4}\widetilde{P}_{EE,L}(k)\Theta_{L}^{m=4}(\mu_{\boldsymbol{k}})~.

(16)

The
\mathrm{II}(+)
and
\mathrm{II}(-)
correlation functions are expanded in terms of the standard and associated Legendre bases, respectively, and given by the inverse Hankel transform,

\displaystyle\xi_{+,\ell}(r)=\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{-1}\left[P_{EE,\ell}(k)\right](r)~,

(17)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\xi}_{-,L}(r)=\mathcal{H}_{L}^{-1}\left[\widetilde{P}_{EE,L}(k)\right](r)~,

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{-1}\left[g(k)\right](r)\equiv i^{\ell}\int\frac{k^{2}dk}{2\pi^{2}}j_{\ell}(kr)g(k)~.

(19)

Converting the
\mathrm{II}(-)
correlation function from the associated to the standard Legendre bases can be performed via the linear transform,

\displaystyle\xi_{-,\ell}(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{L}A_{\ell L}\,\widetilde{\xi}_{-,L}(r)~,

(20)

where the coefficients
A_{\ell L}
are constants and provided in Appendix
A
.
In the linear theory limit,
\sigma_{v}\to 0
and thus
D_{\rm FoG}=1
. Hence,
Equation (
14
) converges to the linear theory formulas derived
in
Okumura & Taruya 2020

(
Okumura et al. 2024
, see also Appendix C of)
.

Comparison with Models

We perform the likelihood analysis to quantitatively evaluate the detection significance of II autocorrelations for the three SDSS galaxy samples.
We compute the
\chi^{2}
statistics for each measured statistic of
\xi_{+,\ell},\xi_{-,\ell},\widetilde{\xi}_{-,L}
.
We use the
\ell=0,2
multipoles for
\xi_{+,\ell}
and
\xi_{-,\ell}
and the
L=4
multipole for
\widetilde{\xi}_{-,L}
, all of which were shown in Figures
1
and
2
.
The full covariance matrix is used to include the correlations between different bins.
We have two fitting parameters,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(b_{K}\sigma_{8},\sigma_{v})
.
The analysis is performed over the scales adopted,

6\leq r\leq 100~[\,h^{-1}\,{\rm Mpc}]
.
Namely, we have 6 data points for each of the multipoles.
See Table
1
for the degree of freedom for each analysis.
The data points used for the analysis are enclosed by the
vertical lines in Figure
2
.

Figure
3
shows the parameter constraints obtained from the LOWZ sample.
The results for the LRG and CMASS samples are not shown here because the trend of the constraints and their interpretation are very similar to the LOWZ result.
The green, orange, and blue contours are results with
\xi_{+,\ell},\xi_{-,\ell}
, and
\widetilde{\xi}_{-,L}
, respectively.
We obtain similar constraints on
b_{K}
from
\xi_{-,\ell}
and
\widetilde{\xi}_{-,L}
.
It is expected because they are the same statistics but expanded by different bases
(
Inoue et al. 2025
, see also)
.
This demonstrates that the linear-level signal of the
\mathrm{II}(-)
correlation is well compressed into the single
L=4
multipole if it is expanded by the associated Legendre basis.
Thus, this allows us to emphasize the first clear evidence of the ellipticity autocorrelation up to
\sim 100\,h^{-1}\,{\rm Mpc}
via the appropriate choice of the basis and using the high-quality imaging data.

The
\mathrm{II}(+)
correlation is not sensitive to the FoG effect, and the best-fit
b_{K}
value is lower than that from the
\mathrm{II}(-)
correlation. Our model includes nonlinear RSD but still adopts the linear shape bias model, and overpredicts the amplitude of the
\mathrm{II}(+)
correlation
(
Okumura et al. 2024
)
. The nonlinear shape bias beyond the NLA model has been extensively studied

(
Vlah et al. 2020
;
Akitsu et al. 2021
;
Bakx et al. 2023
;
Matsubara 2024
;
Chen & Kokron 2024
;
Akitsu et al. 2023b
)
. Accounting for such higher-order shape bias effects will enable us to predict the
\mathrm{II}(+)
correlation and to use the measured IA correlation functions down to
smaller scales, which will further enhance the science return from IA of galaxies.

Finally, let us investigate the full signals of the ellipticity correlations, namely, jointly analyze the
\mathrm{II}(+)
and
\mathrm{II}(-)
correlation functions.
Since the latter has a flexibility of choice for the basis, we consider the two data vectors,
(\xi_{+,\ell},\xi_{+,\ell^{\prime}})
and
(\xi_{+,\ell},\widetilde{\xi}_{-,4})
with
\ell,\ell^{\prime}=\{0,2\}
and construct the corresponding full covariance matrix.
The constraints are shown in Table
1
.
As seen in Eqs. (
15
)–(
18
), all the ellipticity autocorrelation functions are derived from
P_{EE}
. Nevertheless, surprisingly, combining them leads to
\sim 10\%
improvement for the detectability compared to the
\mathrm{II}(-)
-only constraints. Even in this case, the choice of the associated Legendre basis for the
\mathrm{II}(-)
correlation provides almost the equivalent of the case of the standard Legendre basis that requires the measurement of the multipoles at least
\ell=0
and 2.

V
conclusions

We have presented the first observational evidence for IA of galaxies in ellipticity autocorrelation functions extending to
100\,h^{-1}\,{\rm Mpc}
, using the LRG, LOWZ and CMASS galaxy samples from SDSS and SDSS-III BOSS over the redshift range
0.16\leq z\leq 0.70
.
While such autocorrelations are significantly more difficult to measure than cross-correlations with galaxy density fields, they contain unique physical information, including potential imprints of gravitational waves, which are inaccessible to GI correlations.

To mitigate the projection effects of galaxy images along the line of sight, we expand the

\mathrm{II}(-)
correlation in terms of associated Legendre polynomials, which isolate the linear-order information into a single multipole.
This basis choice, combined with high-quality DESI imaging data, yields a clear power-law correlation extending to scales comparable to the BAO features.
The results represent the first unambiguous evidence of large-scale shape autocorrelations across multiple redshift bins.
Although the
\mathrm{II}(+)
correlation is noisier, combining it with
\mathrm{II}(-)
leads to a
\sim 10\%
improvement in detection significance. This enhancement is notable, given that the two are mathematically equivalent under the linear alignment model.

Our analysis establishes a robust observational foundation for cosmological applications of ellipticity autocorrelations. These include future efforts to constrain the stochastic gravitational wave background as well as the expansion history of the universe. In particular, since BAO signatures are also encoded in IA statistics
(
Chisari & Dvorkin 2013
;
Okumura et al. 2019
;
Xu et al. 2023b
)
, combining IA measurements with galaxy clustering promises improved geometric constraints. The joint constraints on growth and expansion rates with galaxy IA will be presented in our upcoming paper
(
Okumura 2025
)
.

When we were finalizing the work based on the SDSS spectroscopy data and limited to the redshift of
0.16\leq z\leq 0.70
