Broad targeting of HPV E proteins by intratumoral and systemic B cell responses in HPV+ head and neck cancer
Authors: Ilham Bahhar, Jacob P Conarty, Songzhu Zhao, Mengda Yu, Bhavna Kumar, James W Rocco, Andreas Wieland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTB cells can account for a substantial proportion of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and have been associated with improved outcomes in several malignancies. Yet, their antigen specificity, a key factor for ascribing a distinct function, is mostly unknown. Here, we demonstrate that in tumors of patients with human papillomavirus-positive head and neck cancer (HPV+ HNC) a substantial proportion of intratumoral antibody-secreting cells (ASCs) are specific for HPV E proteins. Using IgG ELISpots on TILs, we show that HPV E protein-specific ASCs are detectable in virtually all patients and can account for up to almost a third of all intratumoral IgG+ ASCs. ASC responses against the viral proteins E1 and E2 consistently outnumbered those against the classic oncoproteins E6 and E7. HPV-specific intratumoral ASCs correlated strongly between primary tumor and metastatic lymph nodes as well as with plasma IgG titers, indicating that intratumoral tumor-specific ASCs, at least partially, contribute to systemic anti-tumor responses. To further resolve the makeup of tumor-specific antibody responses, we developed a novel Multiplex Antigen Flow-based ImmunoAssay (MAFIA) enabling simultaneous and standardized quantification of HPV E-specific antibodies across all major Ig isotypes and IgG subclasses. Applying MAFIA to plasma samples of patients with HPV+ HNC revealed that HPV-specific plasma antibodies mainly consisted of IgG1, IgG3, and IgA, with the highest median responses directed against E1 and E2. Overall, our study delineates the antigenic hierarchy and makeup of HPV-specific humoral responses in HPV+ HNC, both intratumorally and systemically, thus providing a framework for future biomarker as well as next-generation therapeutics development.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus-positive head and neck cancer (HPV+ HNC), particularly oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma, is a virally driven malignancy that currently accounts for 15,000–18,000 new cases per year in the United States and is projected to rise to 30,000 annual cases by 2030. 1 , 2 The viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 drive malignant transformation through targeted degradation of p53 and inactivation of the retinoblastoma protein (pRb), respectively, and their continued expression renders HPV-driven tumors immunologically distinct from HPV-negative tumors arising from oncogenic mutations in host genes. Importantly, while genomic integration of HPV typically restricts viral gene expression to E6 and E7, episomal copies of the viral genome are present in the vast majority of HPV+ HNCs, permitting the additional expression of the episomal state-dependent early proteins E1, E2, E4, and E5. 3 , 4 The expression of additional viral proteins beyond the classical oncoproteins E6 and E7 thus substantially broadens the tumor-associated antigenic repertoire available for immune recognition. 5 Because of their constitutive expression by tumor cells, foreign nature, and absence from normal uninfected tissue, HPV E proteins thus represent compelling targets for both immune surveillance and therapeutic intervention. Yet, which HPV proteins are predominantly targeted by the immune system, especially within the tumor microenvironment (TME), has not been addressed in detail. This knowledge gap is of particular clinical relevance given that current immunotherapeutic strategies solely targeting E6 and E7, including therapeutic vaccines and T-cell receptor gene therapies, have demonstrated limited clinical efficacy, 6 , 7 underscoring the need to comprehensively define the antigenic breadth and composition of HPV-specific immune responses in the TME to ultimately inform the rational design of next-generation interventions.

Accumulating evidence indicates that B cells are active components of the TME, with B cell infiltration and tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) associated with improved survival and response to immune checkpoint blockade across multiple solid tumor types. 8-10 HPV+ HNC tumors harbor a distinct immune landscape compared to HPV-negative tumors 11 and are specifically enriched for transcriptional signatures of germinal center B cells and lymphoid structures, both of which correlate with favorable clinical outcomes. 12 Of note, recent work by our group demonstrates that the HPV+ HNC TME contains activated B cells, germinal center B cells as well as antibody-secreting cells (ASCs) reactive against E2, E6, and E7, establishing that local B cells can recognize viral tumor antigens and actively contribute to intratumoral humoral immunity. 13 Importantly, our data also showed that, while active B cell responses such as HPV-specific IgG+ ASCs are readily detectable in the TME, these responses are highly localized to the TME, with only memory B cells and antibodies being detectable in circulation. 13 Yet, a comprehensive analysis assessing the antigenic breadth of HPV-specific B cell responses, both intratumorally as well as systemically, against all HPV E proteins is still lacking.

Circulating antibodies against HPV E proteins have been investigated as biomarkers for HPV+ HNC diagnosis, risk stratification, and post-treatment surveillance. 14-24 HPV16 E6 seropositivity can precede clinical diagnosis by several years—in some cases even decades—and is strongly associated with a strikingly elevated risk of developing oropharyngeal cancer. 21 , 23 , 25 More recent studies also suggest that combining serology against additional E proteins, including E1, E2, and E4, with E6 and E7 measurements may further improve early detection and risk stratification. 22 , 26 However, existing serologic approaches typically only measure total Ig or IgG titers against a limited number of antigens and do not resolve the Ig isotype and IgG subclass composition of HPV E protein-specific humoral responses, an important determinant of downstream Fc effector functionality of these antibody responses. 27 , 28 Whether Ig isotypes and IgG subclasses are differentially elicited against individual HPV E proteins or amongst patients thus still represents a significant gap in our understanding of HPV-specific humoral immunity.

To address these gaps, we employed two complementary approaches to delineate HPV-specific humoral immunity at both local and systemic levels in patients with HPV+ HNC. We first used IgG ELISpot assays to enumerate HPV-specific IgG+ ASCs in paired primary tumor and metastatic lymph node (metLN) specimens from patients with HPV+ HNC, enabling direct quantification of local antigen-specific B cell responses across two distinct tumor sites and correlation of local responses with systemic antibody levels. We then developed and validated a novel Multiplex Antigen Flow-based ImmunoAssay (MAFIA) for the simultaneous and standardized quantification of plasma antibodies against HPV16 E1, E2, E4, E6, E7, as well as influenza H1 hemagglutinin (HA) across six immunoglobulin isotypes and subclasses, namely IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, IgG4, IgA, and IgM. Together, this integrated approach provides unprecedented insights into the breadth, isotype/subclass composition, and anatomical context of HPV-specific humoral immune responses in HPV+ HNC.

Material and methods

Sample collection

Patients with HPV+ HNC undergoing treatment at The Ohio State Comprehensive Cancer Center were recruited in accordance with a protocol approved by The Ohio State University Cancer Institutional Review Board (2017C0050). All patients provided written informed consent prior to sample collection. HPV status was confirmed by both circulating tumor DNA (NavDx) as well as in situ hybridization. Patients were divided into two cohorts based on tumor tissue availability (Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Figure S1). Cohort 1, used for all intratumoral analysis and ELISAs, as well as a selection used for MAFIA validation, was confirmed to be HPV16+, except for two patients who were only confirmed as positive for high-risk HPV. All patients in cohort 2, the extended MAFIA cohort, were HPV16+, with the exception of one patient who was only noted to be positive for high-risk HPV. The vast majority of patients in both cohorts were treatment-naive, except for two patients in cohort 1 and six in cohort 2. Primary tumor and metLN samples obtained from transoral robotic surgery and/or radical neck dissection were collected and stored in Leibovitz’s L-15 medium and immediately processed as previously described. 13 Briefly, tissue samples were cut into small pieces and digested with an enzymatic cocktail containing 125 µg/ml collagenase I, II, and IV, 50 µg/ml elastase, and 50 µg/ml DNAse. TILs were isolated using a 44%/67% Percoll gradient. Peripheral blood samples were collected in BD Vacutainer EDTA tubes and immediately processed to obtain peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and plasma. StemCell Technologies SepMate tubes were used to isolate PBMCs, with remaining red blood cells lysed by ACK lysis buffer and washed three times with PBS + 2% FBS. TILs were utilized freshly for ASC ELISpots. Plasma was stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Antigen generation

Recombinant HPV16 antigens N-terminally fused with maltose-binding protein (MBP) were expressed as previously described. 13 Briefly, MBP-HPV fusion proteins with a C-terminal His-Tag and an N-terminal Twin-Strep-Tag were expressed utilizing NEB Express Escherichia Coli cells and purified with Cube Biotech Ni-Indigo resin. Proteins were stored in PBS with 50% glycerol and 2 mM DTT at −80 °C. Recombinant C-terminally His-tagged H1 hemagglutinin (HA) was produced using the Expi293 expression system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and purified from cell culture supernatants with Cube Biotech Ni-Indigo resin. The seasonal influenza vaccine GSK FLUARIX of the 2022/2023 season was used as antigen for ELISpots and ELISAs.

ELISpot

ELISpots were performed as previously described. 13 Briefly, 1.5 µg of recombinant MBP fusion protein or MBP alone, 1 µg of anti-human IgG, or 0.5 µg of FLUARIX was coated on Millipore MSINP40W plates. Plates were coated for one hour at 37 °C and then blocked for one hour at 37 °C with RPMI supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 2 mM GlutaMAX, 55 µM β-mercaptoethanol, and 10% fetal bovine serum (R10). TILs were plated with threefold serial dilutions overnight starting with 210,000 TILs per well. The next day, lymphocytes were washed off with PBS, then the plates were washed with PBS-T and incubated for two hours with 1/1000 biotinylated anti-human IgG diluted in PBS-T + 1% FBS, followed by a one-hour incubation with 1/1000 HRP-conjugated avidin diluted in PBS-T + 1% FBS. Plates were then washed with PBS-T and PBS, followed by development with 3-amino-9-ethylcarbazole (AEC) substrate. After drying, plates were imaged and spots were manually assessed.

ELISA

ELISAs were performed as previously described. 13 Briefly, 50 ng of recombinant MBP-HPV16 fusion protein or a 1/80 dilution of the FLUARIX vaccine was coated on Thermo Scientific Nunc Maxisorp plates overnight in PBS. Plates were then washed with PBS-T and blocked for two hours at room temperature with PBS-T + 10% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA). Plates were then washed with PBS-T and then incubated with threefold serial dilutions of patient plasma, starting at 1/100 in PBS-T + 1% BSA for two hours. Plates were then washed with PBS-T and incubated with a 1:10,000 dilution of either HRP-conjugated anti-IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, IgG4, IgA, or IgM (Supplementary Table S2) in PBS-T + 1% BSA. Plates were then developed utilizing Sigmafast OPD tablets, and the reaction was stopped by addition of 1 M HCl. Absorbance was measured at 492 nm, and the absorbance of the MBP-coated wells was subtracted from the wells containing HPV proteins to account for background. Ig ELISA titers are represented as the interpolated plasma dilution required to obtain an A492nm of 0.15 for HPV E proteins and 0.2 for influenza.

Preparation of HPV E protein-coupled beads

Recombinant HPV16 E proteins and control antigens were coupled to spectrally distinct carboxylated LEGENDplex beads (Biolegend; Supplementary Table S2) through StrepTactin-XT/TwinStrep-tag interaction, using an EDC/sulfo-NHS-based bead functionalization approach adapted from Brown et al. 29 with modifications (Figure 1A). Individual bead populations were assigned to HPV16 E1, E2, E4, E6, E7, influenza H1 hemagglutinin (Flu HA), or maltose-binding protein (MBP), with each recombinant protein loaded onto one bead population. H1 HA was included as a positive control antigen, whereas MBP was included as a negative control antigen. Briefly, all incubation steps involving beads were performed at 1000 rpm on a shaker unless otherwise noted. A total of 2 × 106 beads from each population were activated in 0.1 M sodium phosphate coupling buffer, pH 6.0, with sulfo-NHS (N-hydroxysulfosuccinimide) and EDC (1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide) for 20 min at room temperature (RT). Activated beads were washed and incubated with StrepTactin-XT for 2 hours at RT. To block remaining reactive sites, beads were incubated in PBS containing 1% BSA for 1 hour, washed, resuspended in storage buffer consisting of PBS, 0.01% Tween-20, 0.05% sodium azide, and 0.1% BSA, and stored protected from light at 4 °C. Before use, recombinant proteins were loaded onto the corresponding StrepTactin-XT-functionalized bead populations for 30 minutes at RT. Antigen-loaded beads were washed twice with storage buffer and resuspended in sample dilution buffer (PBS, 1% BSA) before use in MAFIA.

Multiplex antigen flow-based immunoassay (MAFIA) for plasma Ig isotype and IgG subclass quantification

An overview of MAFIA is illustrated in Figure 1A. Antigen-loaded beads corresponding to HPV16 E1, E2, E4, E6, E7, influenza H1 HA (Flu HA), and MBP were pooled in sample dilution buffer. Human plasma samples were diluted 1:10 in sample dilution buffer (PBS, 1% BSA) and incubated with the pooled bead mixture for 1 hour at room temperature (RT) with shaking at 1000 rpm. After incubation, beads were washed to remove unbound plasma antibodies, and bound antigen-specific antibodies were detected using fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies against human IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, IgG4, IgA, or IgM, as listed in Supplementary Table S2. Antibody staining was performed for 1 hour at RT protected from light. The anti-human IgA detection antibody was conjugated in-house to Alexa Fluor 532 using the Mix-n-Stain CF Dye Antibody Labeling Kit (Biotium). Beads were then washed, resuspended in dilution buffer (PBS, 1% BSA), and analyzed by flow cytometry using a five-laser Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer. Bead events were identified by forward and side scatter, and individual antigen-loaded bead populations were distinguished based on their intrinsic fluorescence barcode. For each bead population, antigen-specific antibody binding was quantified as median fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the corresponding secondary antibody detection channel. To account for nonspecific binding to the MBP fusion tag shared by all recombinant antigens, background MFI measured against MBP-loaded beads was subtracted from the MFI of each HPV16 E protein bead population before downstream analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.6.1 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Correlation analysis of intratumoral ASCs in matched tumors and metLNs was performed using Spearman’s rank-order correlation. Comparisons across multiple antigen groups were performed using the Friedman test with Dunn’s multiple-comparisons correction. The comparison between the number of intratumoral ASCs as well as antibody titers between the groups of E1, E2, and E4 versus E6 and E7 were determined via Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test. Correlation analysis of intratumoral ASCs and plasma endpoint titers was performed using Spearman's rank-order.

Standard curves for each antibody isotype and subclass were generated by fitting a four-parameter logistic (4PL) model to MFI values plotted against the log-transformed concentration of HPV E2-specific hmAb variants spanning IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, IgG4, IgA, and IgM. Goodness-of-fit was assessed by the coefficient of determination (R2). The limit of detection (LOD) for each isotype and subclass was defined as the concentration corresponding to the mean MFI of the blank plus three standard deviations, interpolated from the fitted standard curve. Antigen-specific antibody concentrations below the LOD were assigned a value equal to half the LOD for visualization purposes. Plasma antibody concentrations are presented as individual data points on a log10 scale with the median indicated. Correlation between multiplex-derived antibody concentrations and ELISA endpoint titers was assessed using Pearson's correlation analysis. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Broad recognition of HPV E proteins by intratumoral B cells

We obtained surgically resected primary tumors and metLNs from 29 treatment-naive patients with HPV+ HNC and two previously treated patients with recurrent HPV+ HNC, nearly all of which were confirmed to be positive for HPV16 (Supplementary Table S1), the most prevalent strain in HPV+ HNC, 4 , 30 , 31 via circulating tumor DNA (NavDx) and in situ hybridization staining. We initially quantified total IgG-secreting ASCs from tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) isolated from primary tumors using IgG ELISpots, mostly due to dominance of IgG+ ASCs in the TME. 13 Across all samples, IgG-secreting cells accounted for 0.02% to 4.8% of TILs, with an average of about 0.5% (Supplementary Figure S2A), demonstrating a robust presence of IgG+ ASCs in the vast majority of tumors.

We next wanted to determine the antigenic breadth of the intratumoral ASC response and especially address the question of which HPV E proteins elicit vigorous intratumoral ASC responses. We thus quantified IgG+ ASC responses against the HPV E proteins E1, E2, E4, E6, and E7, as well as against influenza virus, a highly prevalent antigen reactivity in the human population and thus an exquisite surrogate for tumor antigen-unrelated bystander activity 13 (Figure 2A). Of note, we did not assess ASC responses directed against E5, which, due to its tremendous hydrophobicity and membrane-buried state, is challenging to recombinantly produce and only elicits minor humoral responses. 24 , 32 While the magnitude of HPV-specific IgG+ ASC responses in primary tumors and metLNs showed marked heterogeneity between patients and individual antigens, ranging from undetectable to 19% of all IgG-secreting cells, most patients exhibited responses to most assessed HPV E proteins (Figure 2B), with HPV reactivities accounting for up to 29% of intratumoral IgG+ ASCs. In fact, only one patient in our cohort of 30 patients lacked detectable intratumoral HPV-specific IgG+ ASC responses altogether. Most patients exhibited ASC responses to multiple HPV proteins, with E1 being targeted in 58% (14/24), E2 in 75% (18/24), E4 in 50% (12/24), E6 in 67% (16/24), and E7 in 54% (13/24) of primary tumors. Notably, while E1 and E2 responses tended to be the two most dominant responses, E4 was the weakest HPV antigen with low to undetectable ASC responses in the vast majority of patients. Influenza-reactive bystander ASCs were detectable in about 50% of tumors but substantially lower in terms of magnitude compared to tumor/HPV-specific responses. Importantly, the antigen response hierarchy observed in primary tumors was mirrored in metLN as evidenced by a strong correlation between HPV-specific ASC responses in primary tumor and metLN (Figure 2C). Of note, the intratumoral ASC response against the HPV proteins E1, E2, and E4, which are currently not considered in immunotherapeutic strategies, significantly outnumbered the response to the classic HPV oncoproteins E6 and E7, with the cumulative response against E1, E2, and E4 being on average about two-fold higher (Figure 2D). As E4 responses were minimal, similar observations were also made when comparing the cumulative response against E1 and E2 versus E6 and E7 (Supplementary Figure S3A-C).

Correlation of intratumoral and systemic HPV-specific B-cell responses

We next examined HPV-specific antibody titers in the plasma of the 31 patients with HPV+ HNC in cohort 1. IgG antibodies against most HPV E proteins were readily detectable in the vast majority of patients (Figure 3A). Comparable to our intratumoral ASC data (Figure 2B), substantial responses were detected against E1, E2, E6, and E7, with E4-specific IgG antibodies only being detectable in about 50% of samples and generally representing the weakest reactivity. Despite vigorous HPV-specific IgG responses, patients with HPV+ HNC exhibited substantially lower or even undetectable IgA and IgM responses specific for HPV E proteins (Figure 3B and C). In contrast, while influenza-specific responses were detectable in all patients and dominated by the IgG isotype, patients also consistently exhibited detectable influenza-specific titers of IgA and IgM. Of note, circulating HPV-specific IgG titers strongly correlated with the number of HPV-specific intratumoral IgG+ ASCs (Figure 3D), a correlation that was absent for bystander IgG+ ASCs reactive to influenza and their respective plasma IgG titers (Supplementary Figure S2B). When analyzing the cumulative plasma antibody response directed against the classic oncoproteins E6 and E7 and directly comparing it against the other E proteins, we observed that, across all three isotypes, antibody titers against E1, E2, and E4 were significantly higher than against E6 and E7 (Figure 3E−G).

Development of an HPV-specific Multiplex Antigen Flow-based ImmunoAssay (MAFIA)

ELISAs have been a staple in biomedical research, providing an easy yet highly specific method to determine antigen-specific antibody titers. A major limitation of traditional ELISAs, however, is their lack of multiplexing capability, requiring the detection of one analyte with one detection reagent at a time. In addition, serial dilutions –often up to a dozen- are required to derive widely used ELISA endpoint titers. We thus set out to develop a Multiplex Antigen Flow-based ImmunoAssay (MAFIA) to enable the high-throughput analysis of HPV-specific antibody responses simultaneously against five HPV16 E proteins (E1, E2, E4, E6, E7), an MBP negative control and the hemagglutinin (HA) of influenza H1 across immunoglobulin isotypes and subclasses (IgM, IgA, IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, IgG4). In addition, we employ Ig isotype and IgG subclass variants of an HPV16 E2-specific human monoclonal antibody (hmAb) 13 as bona fide antigen-specific standards to achieve standardized quantification of antigen-specific antibody responses across immunoglobulin isotypes and IgG subclasses. Our MAFIA platform loads individual recombinant Strep-tagged proteins such as HPV E proteins and influenza HA to spectrally distinct fluorescent beads we functionalized in-house with Strep-Tactin, enabling resolution and standardized quantification of antigen-specific IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, IgG4, IgA, and IgM responses in a single reaction (Figure 1A). Individual antigen-loaded bead populations were discriminated by their intrinsic fluorescence barcode (Figure 1B), and antigen-specific antibody binding was quantified as median fluorescence intensity (MFI) in the corresponding detection channel (Figure 1C), followed by determining the standardized antibody concentration using our HPV16 E2-specific hmAb standards.

MAFIA demonstrates high sensitivity and broad dynamic range across isotypes and subclasses

To assess sensitivity and quantitative performance of MAFIA, we generated standard curves for each immunoglobulin isotype and subclass using serial dilutions of HPV16 E2-specific hmAb variants spanning IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, IgG4, IgA, and IgM. Each antibody standard was incubated with E2-coupled beads at defined concentrations and detected using fluorophore-conjugated anti-human secondary antibodies (Supplementary Table S2). The MFI was plotted against the antibody concentration and fitted using a four-parameter logistic model (Figure 4A). All six standard curves demonstrated excellent goodness-of-fit, with R2 values of 0.9951 for IgG1, 0.9970 for IgG2, 0.9919 for IgG3, 0.9967 for IgG4, 0.9952 for IgA, and 0.9961 for IgM. The limits of detection (LOD), defined as the mean blank MFI plus three standard deviations and interpolated from the fitted standard curve, ranged from 151 pg/mL for IgG3 to 579 pg/mL for IgG1, with LODs of 263 pg/mL for IgG2, 242 pg/mL for IgG4, 285 pg/mL for IgA, and 302 pg/mL for IgM. Overall, these results demonstrate that MAFIA achieves sub-nanogram sensitivity across all measured isotypes and subclasses.

MAFIA-derived antibody concentrations correlate strongly with ELISA endpoint titers

To validate MAFIA-derived antibody measurements against an orthogonal method, we analyzed antibodies in the plasma of 24 treatment-naive patients with HPV+ HNC and compared antigen-specific antibody concentrations with “traditional” ELISA endpoint titers for HPV16 early proteins E1, E2, E4, E6, and E7 and across IgA, IgM, IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, and IgG4. For the dominant isotypes IgG1, IgG3, IgA, and IgM, MAFIA-derived antibody concentrations correlated strongly with ELISA endpoint titers across all five antigens, with Pearson R2 values of 0.9085 (IgG1; n = 40 antigen-sample pairs above both assay thresholds), 0.9097 (IgG3), 0.9902 (IgA), and 0.8313 (IgM), all P < 0.0001 (Figure 4B). The strongest concordance was observed for IgA (R2 = 0.9902), while IgM showed a wider scatter at low concentrations (R2 = 0.8313), likely reflecting the greater heterogeneity of IgM responses near the assay detection limits. HPV-specific IgG2 and IgG4 responses were, as previously reported by us, 13 undetectable in virtually all plasma samples, limiting their utility for formal correlation analyses (Supplementary Figure S4A, S4B). Together, these findings demonstrate that our MAFIA platform allows for quantitative measurements that are concordant with conventional ELISAs across HPV16 E antigens as well as Ig isotypes and IgG subclasses.

Plasma HPV-specific antibody responses are dominated by IgG1 and IgA

We next applied MAFIA to define the Ig isotype and IgG subclass composition of plasma HPV-specific antibody responses in an extended cohort of 50 patients with HPV+ HNC. IgG1 and IgA were the predominant antibody types detected across HPV16 E1, E2, E4, E6, and E7 (Figure 5A−F, Supplementary Figure S5 and S6). The highest median IgG1 concentrations were observed against E1 and E2, with the vast majority of patients exhibiting detectable antibody levels that were on par or even higher than influenza H1 HA-specific titers. Of note, in contrast to the other HPV16 E proteins, E4-specific IgG1 antibodies were mostly undetectable (Figure 5A). IgG2 antibodies were detectable in most patients but predominantly targeted E2 (Figure 5B). IgG3 responses were detectable in most patients, particularly against E1, with lower responses to E2 and E7 and generally undetectable for E4 and E6 (Figure 5C). IgG4 responses were generally sparse across all five antigens, consistent with our ELISA validation dataset (Figure 5D; Supplementary Figure S4) and our previous work. 13 IgA antibody levels followed a similar pattern as IgG1, with the highest median concentrations detected against E1 and E2, and notably robust individual responses against E4 and E6 in a subset of patients (Figure 5E). IgM responses were detectable against E1 and E2 in a subset of patients but were largely undetectable for E4, E6, and E7 (Figure 5F). Collectively, these data reveal antigen- and isotype/subclass-dependent heterogeneity in HPV-specific humoral responses in patients with HPV+ HNC, with IgG1 and IgA representing the dominant antibody classes in the majority of patients and the strongest median responses directed against E1 and E2.

Discussion

In this study, we provide a comprehensive characterization of the breadth, anatomical context, and isotype/subclass composition of HPV-specific humoral immunity in patients with HPV+ HNC. Using IgG ELISpots on paired primary tumor and metLN specimens, we demonstrate that intratumoral ASC responses against HPV16 E proteins are nearly universal in this patient population and are predominantly directed against the HPV proteins E1 and E2 rather than the classical oncoproteins E6 and E7. This intratumoral antigen hierarchy is faithfully mirrored systemically, where HPV-specific plasma antibody titers, profiled across six immunoglobulin isotypes and subclasses using our newly developed MAFIA platform, are similarly dominated by E1- and E2-specific IgG1 and IgA. Taken together, our findings reframe the antigenic landscape of HPV-specific humoral immunity in HPV+ HNC, with potential implications for both biomarker development and next-generation therapeutic vaccine design.

One of the most striking findings of our study is the consistent dominance of E1- and E2-specific responses at both intratumoral and systemic levels. Several non-mutually exclusive mechanisms likely contribute to this distinct reactivity pattern. First, in the majority of HPV+ HNC tumors, the viral genome is maintained in episomal form, either exclusively or alongside integrated copies, permitting expression of the full HPV early gene repertoire, including E1 and E2. 3 , 4 In addition, E1 and E2 are the largest HPV early proteins, thus offering a substantially larger number of epitopes for antibody recognition and in turn likely triggering more vigorous B cell responses. Of note, while E6 and E7, compared to E1 and E2, are substantially higherat the RNA level, 3 , 4 it is currently not known whether this also translates into increased protein levels. It is thus possible that differential translation and stability rates result in higher protein levels of E1 and E2 and thus increased antigen abundance to trigger humoral responses. Future studies combining the humoral analyses described above with detailed transcriptional and proteomic profiling of HPV+ HNC tumors will provide crucial insights into the mechanisms underlying the observed immunodominance of E1 and E2.

Our ELISpot data reveal that HPV E protein-specific IgG+ ASCs are not only present in the primary tumor but are equally detectable in metLNs, with a strong correlation between the two compartments. This finding extends our prior work demonstrating the presence of HPV-reactive B cells in the HPV+ HNC TME 13 and indicates that active tumor-specific humoral immunity is a consistent feature of this disease at both the primary and metastatic levels. Of note, intratumoral HPV-specific IgG+ ASCs correlated strongly with matched plasma IgG titers, whereas bystander influenza-reactive IgG+ ASCs, which were consistently low or undetectable, did not. This dissociation thus not only implies a functional contribution of local tumor-specific ASCs to circulating antibody levels but also underlines that the presence of tumor-specific ASCs is driven predominantly by cognate antigen and not a result of inflammation-induced recruitment and retention of random antigen reactivities in the TME. 13 Our findings align with a growing body of evidence that intratumoral B cells and tertiary lymphoid structures are not merely passive bystanders but active contributors to local and systemic immunity in solid tumors. 8-10 , 33 Together, our data support a model in which the tumor itself serves as a site of active, ongoing B cell activity that contributes to seeding systemic HPV-specific humoral immunity, a concept with important implications for understanding how intratumoral immune responses are coupled to systemic anti-tumor immunity.

A central technical contribution of this work is the development and validation of MAFIA, a multiplex bead-based flow cytometry platform enabling simultaneous and absolute quantification of antigen-specific antibodies against five HPV16 E proteins, an MBP negative control as well as an influenza control across six immunoglobulin isotypes and subclasses in a single reaction. Importantly, MAFIA can be easily expanded and adopted from the current seven antigen reactivities to a total of 13 reactivities by including additional fluorescently coded bead populations, with additional expansion capabilities through inclusion of distinct fluorescent beads from other manufacturers. The technical framework of MAFIA is conceptually related to the FlowBEAT platform recently described by Babcock and colleagues, 34 which similarly leverages bead-based multiplexing and flow cytometry to simultaneously resolve antibody isotypes against multiple antigens in the context of SARS-CoV-2 infection yet lacked absolute antibody quantification. Prior serological studies of HPV-specific humoral immunity in HNC have largely relied on either single-analyte ELISAs 15-17 , 19 , 24 or multiplexed antigen platforms. 26 , 35 While these platforms have been transformative for establishing HPV seropositivity as a diagnostic and potentially prognostic biomarker in HNC, 14-23 they are subject to distinct limitations which might also have contributed to discordant conclusions regarding the prognostic value of HPV E-specific antibodies in HPV+ HNC. Importantly, previous studies have not performed detailed Ig isotype and IgG subclass profiling of HPV-specific antibodies but rather relied on measuring IgG 15 , 16 , 20 or even total Ig (IgG + IgA + IgM). 14 , 23 However, given the heterogeneity of Ig isotype and subclass composition of HPV-specific antibodies and the divergent Fc effector activities of different isotypes and IgG subclasses, 28 , 36-38 by providing in-depth Ig profiling information, MAFIA represents an important advancement to assist with the assessment of the prognostic value of HPV-specific antibodies in future studies. A major advantage of MAFIA over previous approaches to measure HPV-specific antibody responses is the use of defined antigen-reactive hmAb standards, affording standardized quantitation of HPV-specific antibodies across isotypes and IgG subclasses. Importantly, by anchoring measurements to standardized antibody concentrations rather than arbitrary optical density or MFI readouts, MAFIA enables direct cross-study and cross-laboratory comparisons, a key step toward harmonizing ongoing HPV E serology efforts.

Application of MAFIA to plasma from 50 patients with HPV+ HNC in our study revealed that HPV-specific antibody responses are dominated by IgG1 and IgA, with IgG3 detectable in a subset of patients, particularly against E1. The predominance of IgG1 is consistent with our prior study 13 and is reflective of a canonical T helper cell type 1-driven isotype switching expected in the context of a viral disease. 39 , 40 The robust IgA response against HPV E proteins, particularly E1 and E2, is a novel finding that has not been systematically reported previously, largely because existing platforms have not specifically profiled IgA alongside IgG. Of note, IgA is the predominant mucosal Ig and is increasingly recognized as an important effector in anti-tumor immunity through various mechanisms. 38 , 41-43 The robust presence of HPV-specific IgA in the plasma of patients with HPV+ HNC patients may reflect active mucosal immune responses at the oropharyngeal site of HPV infection and tumor development and raises the important question of whether local IgA production potentially contributes to anti-tumor immunity.

In summary, our findings and tools developed herein carry potential implications for both biomarker development and therapeutic vaccine design. With respect to biomarker applications, the strong correlation between intratumoral HPV-specific ASCs and plasma antibody titers suggests that circulating HPV-specific antibodies, particularly those against E1 and E2, may serve as faithful surrogates for the intratumoral immune state. Given that HPV16 E6 seropositivity can precede clinical diagnosis by years or even decades 21 , 23 , 25 and that early detection is critical for optimal outcomes in HNC, it should be assessed in future studies whether the addition of E1 and E2 can further improve the sensitivity of serological screenings. 22 With respect to therapeutic vaccine development, our data support further investigation into the inclusion of E1 and E2 antigens into therapeutic vaccines to boost the breadth and magnitude of endogenous anti-tumor immune responses, with hopes to ultimately improve the limited clinical efficacy of currently tested vaccines solely targeting E6 and E7. 6 , 7
