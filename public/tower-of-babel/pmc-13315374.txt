Incidence and Predictors of Mortality Among Adults on Antiretroviral Therapy in Ethiopia: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Muluken Amare Wudu, Fantawork Samuel Mojo, Etsegenet Arega Asmamaw, Selamyhun Tadesse Yosef, Aragaw Tesfaye Gelmo, Yimer Seid Ali, Tarikua Afework Birhanu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the United Nations’ plan to end Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) as a public health concern by 2030, it remains a significant issue in low-income countries, including Ethiopia. Currently, there are no pooled estimates of mortality rate among adults on antiretroviral therapy in Ethiopia. This meta-analysis was conducted to fill this information gap, focusing on the incidence and predictors of mortality among adults receiving antiretroviral therapy in Ethiopia.MethodsThis systematic review followed the PRISMA guidelines, and relevant studies were obtained from the PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, EMBASE, and Google Scholar databases. Data analysis for pooled estimates of incidence and predictors of mortality was conducted via STATA 17 software with the DerSimonian and Laird random-effects model. Heterogeneity was evaluated via Cochrane’s Q-test and the I² statistic, and publication bias was assessed through funnel plots, Egger’s test, and Trim-and-fill analysis.ResultsOf the 121,621 studies initially identified, only 31 met the rigorous inclusion criteria for the final meta-analysis, encompassing 24,504 participants in total. The pooled mortality rate among adults on antiretroviral therapy was 4 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 3–5, I² = 95.27%), based on 74,042.8 person-years of observation. Furthermore, advanced WHO clinical stage (pooled Adjusted Hazard Ratio(AHR) 1.68, 95% CI: 1.58–1.78), bedridden functional status (AHR 1.81, 95% CI: 1.67–1.95), anemia (AHR 1.89, 95% CI: 1.77–2.03), CD4 count below 50/mm³ (AHR 1.53, 95% CI: 1.42–1.65), fair or poor ART adherence (AHR 1.60, 95% CI: 1.45–1.75), and Tuberculosis co-infection (AHR 1.42, 95% CI: 1.32–1.53) were identified as predictors of mortality.ConclusionThe mortality rate observed in the current meta-analysis was clinically meaningful, indicating the need for strengthened efforts to reduce preventable deaths and achieve the 2030 targets. Moreover, advanced WHO clinical stage, bedridden functional status, anemia, CD4 count below 50/mm³, fair or poor ART adherence, and tuberculosis co-infection were identified as predictors of HIV-related mortality. This suggests that early HIV testing, timely initiation of treatment, and robust monitoring and evaluation of treatment should be prioritized to address the identified predictors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44197-026-00568-1.Keywords: Incidence, Mortality, Predictors, Adults, Antiretroviral Therapy, Ethiopia, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus), a persistent global health concern, has claimed nearly 33 million lives to date and took one life every minute in 2022 [1]. In 2022, 76% of the 39 million people worldwide living with HIV were undergoing antiretroviral therapy [2]. Although HIV treatment prevented approximately 21 million AIDS (acquired immunodeficiency syndrome)-related fatalities from 1996 to 2022, a substantial number of AIDS-related deaths (630,000) still occurred in 2022 [1, 2]. Despite the expansion of antiretroviral therapy (ART), reaching 82% coverage in sub-Saharan Africa and Asia, these areas still bear a high burden of AIDS-related deaths [3]. Moreover, in Southern and Eastern Africa, 83% of the 20.8 million people living with HIV were on treatment, 260,000 AIDS-related deaths were recorded in 2022, and mortality decreased by 58% compared to 2010 [1, 4].

In 2023, in Ethiopia, it is estimated that a total of 605,238 people were living with HIV. Of these, 578,187 were adults. Among the adult People Living with HIV (PLHIV), 529,836 knew their status, 501,062 (95%) were currently on treatment, and 8,924 adults died in the same year [5]. In 2023, the Amhara region in Ethiopia had the highest number of adults living with HIV, totaling 165,207, while the Oromia region recorded the highest number of adult AIDS-related deaths, with 2,430 cases [5]. Evidence showed that the incidence of HIV-related mortality in Ethiopia ranges from 1.52 to 14 per 100 person-years [6, 7].

While HIV/AIDS-related deaths remain a significant health challenge, their impact extends beyond healthcare, affecting various aspects of society, including demographics, economics, security, governance, gender dynamics, economic development, and the stability of the public, agricultural, and private sectors [8–11].

Several studies have revealed that sociodemographic factors (such as age, marital status, and sex) [12–14], Body Mass Index (BMI) [15–17], hemoglobin (Hgb) level [18–22], disclosure status [23–28], functional status [29–32], CD4 count [32–34], viral load [35–37], World Health Organization (WHO) clinical stage [16, 19, 23, 26, 38], isoniazid preventive therapy [13, 16, 18, 23, 32], adherence level [18, 21, 25, 31], comorbidities [17, 23, 26, 27, 39], and opportunistic infections [27, 29, 35, 40] were predictors of HIV-related mortality.

Despite Ethiopia’s commitment to and implementation of the UNAIDS 95-95-95 program, efforts to expand service availability, and capacity-building through provider training, the incidence of HIV-related mortality remains significant and continues to pose a public health concern [1, 5].

Although primary studies have reported the HIV-related mortality rate [12, 17, 23, 28, 34, 39], their findings remained heterogeneous and regionally inconsistent, and no systematic review and meta-analysis had specifically examined HIV-related mortality rate and its predictors in Ethiopia. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aim to address this inconsistency and provide evidence-based recommendations to significantly reduce HIV-related mortality rate for key stakeholders. Additionally, this analysis can assist healthcare providers and health managers in identifying strategies to improve the quality of ART care and develop more effective interventions to reduce or delay HIV-related mortality.

Materials and Methods

Protocol Registrations

To prevent redundancy, an extensive review of registered protocols and systematic reviews was conducted. The protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis was registered in the PROSPERO database (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/, ID= CRD42024599737) to ensure transparency and accountability. To ensure the credibility of the methodology and other sections, this review adhered to the PRISMA guidelines (supplementary file 1).

Inclusion Exclusion Criteria

Studies were included if they met the following eligibility criteria. The review considered cohort studies (prospective or retrospective follow-up) conducted among adults living with HIV who were receiving ART in health care facilities in Ethiopia. Studies were required to report the ART-related mortality rate and/or its predictors. Moreover, the eligibility criteria were developed using the PICO framework as follows: Population – adults receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART) in Ethiopia; Intervention/Exposure – predictors of mortality; Comparator – not applicable; Outcome – incidence of mortality. Both published and unpublished studies (gray literature), including studies retrieved from electronic databases, institutional repositories, and accessible theses/dissertations, were considered. The review included studies published between 2010 and 2025, and no language restrictions were applied during the selection process. Furthermore, studies were included only if they were assessed as having low to moderate risk of bias according to the methodological quality appraisal criteria of the Joanna Briggs Institute.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: studies employing cross-sectional, case–control, qualitative, or mixed-method designs without a cohort component; studies conducted among populations other than adults receiving ART; studies that did not report the incidence of ART-related mortality or its predictors; and studies assessed as having a high risk of bias according to the critical appraisal criteria of the Joanna Briggs Institute. In addition, duplicate publications, conference abstracts without full texts, commentaries, editorials, review articles, and national reports that did not meet the required study design or reporting criteria were excluded from the review.

Information Source

Relevant literature was obtained from PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL, EMBASE, and Google Scholar databases. Additionally, the search included dissertations and government reports, considering all relevant publications up to January 10, 2025.

Search Strategy

Our review encompassed studies involving human participants conducted prior to the designated search date. We employed a search methodology designed for optimal sensitivity, incorporating a blend of keywords, MeSH terms, and Boolean operators (‘OR’ and ‘AND’).The search process included the following steps: (((((((((((Adult) OR (people older than 15 years old)) AND (Antiretroviral Therapy)) OR (ART)) AND (Ethiopia)) AND (Incidence))) AND (Predictors)) OR (Risk factors)) OR (determinants)) AND (Mortality)) OR (Death). Furthermore, in addition to the main database search, an extensive review of Google Scholar was conducted using the designated search terms: ‘Incidence and Predictors of Mortality Among Adults on Antiretroviral Therapy in Ethiopia.’ To verify that no significant studies were missed, a cross-referencing strategy was employed to identify additional relevant research (supplementary file 2).

Study Selection Process

Only survival studies that focus on the incidence and predictors of mortality among adults on ART in Ethiopia were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. Titles and abstracts were screened by three authors (MA, TA, ST), and full-text articles were assessed for eligibility based on the inclusion criteria. Duplicate records were removed using EndNote 20, and any discrepancies were resolved through consensus among the remaining authors.

Data Extraction

Data extraction was independently performed by three authors (MA, TA, and ST) using a structured Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Additionally, the authors of non-open-access articles were contacted via email. Articles for which the full text could not be obtained were excluded. Furthermore, data extraction targets included study region, sample size, number of events, study setting (single or multi-center), level of health facilities, study design, statistical model, incidence of mortality, person-time observation, author’s surname, publication year, and predictors of mortality with 95% confidence intervals. The disagreements were resolved through discussion involving the other authors.

Outcomes

One of the primary outcomes of this meta-analysis was the mortality rate among adults on ART in Ethiopia. The pooled mortality rate was calculated by dividing the number of death events by person-time per year, along with the standard errors (calculated by dividing the square root of the number of events by person-time per year). Moreover, predictors of mortality were computed using Hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence intervals were extracted from eligible studies. We transformed HRs to the natural logarithmic scale using log (HR) or using the following formula: gen loghr = log(hr). The standard error of log (HR) was calculated from the reported 95% confidence intervals using the formula: gen se_loghr = (log(ci_upper) - log(ci_lower))/(2 * 1.96). Hazard ratios derived from Cox proportional hazards, Weibull, and cause-specific hazard regression models were considered comparable because they estimate relative hazard effects. All hazard ratios were transformed to their natural logarithm before pooling.

Study Risk of Bias Assessment

The risk of bias was evaluated by three authors (MA, TA, and EB) using the 8-item Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist. Studies were assigned a risk of bias rating of low (≥ 7), medium (5–6), or high (≤ 4) based on the JBI tool. To ensure the quality of the meta-analysis, only studies with a low or medium risk of bias were included. Any disagreements regarding the risk of bias assessment were resolved through collaborative discussion among the research team (supplementary file 3).

Data Synthesis and Analysis

The outcome analysis was conducted via STATA version 17, applying the DerSimonian‒Laird random- effects model. Additionally, heterogeneity among the included studies was evaluated via the Cochrane Q‒test and I² statistic. Moreover, sub-group analysis was done. Publication bias was detected through the use of a funnel plot and Egger’s test. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the influence of each individual study on the pooled HIV-related mortality rate. The pooled estimate was recalculated sequentially after excluding one study at a time to evaluate the robustness of the overall findings.

Results

Study Selection

This systematic review and meta-analysis began with a total of 121,621 identified studies. Through a rigorous evaluation process, 6100 were excluded before the screening stage, 115,440 were excluded after reviewing titles and abstracts, 5 were inaccessible for review, and 45 were excluded due to incorrect population (pediatrics), design, statistical analysis, or lack of full data. Finally, only 31 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Quality Assessment

Based on the JBI criteria, out of the 31 included studies, 18 were assessed as low risk and the remaining 13 were assessed as moderate risk (Supplementary File 3).

Characteristics of the Included Studies

This meta-analysis encompassed 31 studies published since 2010, comprising a total of 24,504 participants. Among these, 2,425 individuals died, with a total of 74,042.8 person-years of observation. Data were gathered from multiple areas across Ethiopia, encompassing the Southern region, Tigray, Amhara, Oromia, Addis Ababa, Afar, and Somali. In terms of healthcare setting, 18 studies were conducted in comprehensive specialized hospitals, 7 in general hospitals, 4 in teaching hospitals, and the remaining 2 in primary-level hospitals. Moreover, 20 studies were conducted in single-institution settings, while the remaining 11 were multi-center studies. In terms of follow-up, 30 studies were retrospective, whereas one study was prospective. Furthermore, in terms of statistical analysis models, twenty-eight studies used the Cox proportional hazards model, two used the Weibull proportional hazards model, and the remaining study used the cause-specific proportional hazards model (Table 1).

Incidence of HIV-Rrelated Mortality

Of the total 24,504 participants, 9.9% (2,425) died during the follow-up period. Moreover, the pooled mortality rate among adults on ART was 4 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 3–5, I² = 95.27%, p < 0.001), based on 74,042.8 person-years of observation. The results indicated substantial heterogeneity among the studies (Fig. 2).

Assessment of Publication Bias

To assess publication bias, both a funnel plot and Egger’s test were used. The funnel plot indicated that the majority of points were on the upper right side. This suggests that publication bias may exist, as smaller studies with significant or larger effect sizes (on the right side) are more likely to be published, while smaller studies with non-significant or smaller effect sizes (which would typically appear on the left side) may be missing. Moreover, the concentration of points on the right side suggests that the overall effect size might be overestimated. Smaller studies with larger effect sizes can disproportionately influence the pooled estimate, leading to an inflated perception of the true effect. On the other hand, one point was observed on the lower right side. This outlier may represent a study with a large effect size but high standard error, which could indicate variability in results or potential publication bias. Overall, the asymmetry suggests potential publication bias, as smaller studies with non-significant results might be underrepresented (Fig. 3). Consider conducting additional tests, such as Egger’s test, for further confirmation. The Egger’s test yielded a p-value of 0.000, indicating significant evidence of publication bias. The beta coefficient of 7.088 suggested that smaller studies tended to report larger effect sizes. Additionally, the confidence interval (5.42 to 8.75) did not include zero, further supporting the presence of bias (Table 4). Overall, these findings indicate that the study results may be affected by publication bias, with smaller studies showing significant findings being more likely to be published. The trim-and-fill technique was employed to address this issue. The trim-and-fill analysis showed no evidence of missing studies, as no studies were imputed. The adjusted pooled effect size remained unchanged (0.040; 95% CI: 0.034–0.046), indicating that publication bias had minimal or no effect on the pooled estimate (Fig. 4).

Sensitivity Analysis

The sensitivity analysis showed that the pooled estimate for HIV-related mortality incidence remained stable, unaffected by any single study. The confidence intervals were narrow and aligned with the pooled estimate, and the consistently significant p-values (p = 0.000) further supported the robustness of the findings (Fig. 5).

Predictors of HIV-Related Mortality

For predictors of mortality rate, adjusted hazard ratios (AHRs) were extracted and pooled whenever available, as these estimates account for potential confounding. When multiple models were reported, effect estimates from the fully adjusted model were used. Across the included studies, adjustment commonly included key demographic, clinical, and treatment-related confounders such as age, sex, baseline CD4 cell count, WHO clinical stage, functional status, ART adherence, regimen type, and presence of co-morbidity, although the specific covariates varied by study. Unadjusted estimates were not pooled to avoid bias due to uncontrolled confounding.

The meta-analysis of hazard ratio revealed that advanced WHO clinical stage, bedridden functional status, anemia, CD4 count below 50/mm³, fair or poor ART adherence, and TB co-infection were predictors of HIV-related mortality.

Twenty-two studies indicated that adult patients on ART in an advanced WHO clinical stage (III & IV) had a 1.68-fold higher HIV-related mortality rate than did those in WHO clinical stages I and II (AHR 1.68, 95% CI: 1.58–1.78, I² = 0.0%) (Table 5). Fifteen studies revealed that adult patients on ART who were in a bedridden functional status had a 1.81-fold higher HIV-related mortality rate than did those in an ambulatory status (AHR 1.81, 95% CI: 1.67–1.95, I² = 0.0%) (Table 5). Moreover, 14 studies showed that adult patients on ART who were anemic had a 1.89-fold higher HIV-related mortality rate than those who were non-anemic (AHR 1.89, 95% CI: 1.77–2.03, I² = 0.0%) (Table 5). Likewise, 12 studies revealed that adult patients on ART with a CD4 count below 50/mm³ had a 1.53-fold higher HIV-related mortality rate than those with a CD4 count above 50/mm³ (AHR 1.53, 95% CI: 1.42–1.65, I² = 0.0%) (Table 5). Similarly, 10 studies indicated that adult patients on ART with fair or poor ART adherence had a 1.60-fold higher HIV-related mortality rate than those with good adherence (AHR 1.60, 95% CI: 1.45–1.75, I² = 0.0%) (Table 5). Furthermore, seven studies revealed that adult patients on ART with TB co-infection had a 1.42-fold higher HIV-related mortality rate than those without TB co-infection (AHR 1.42, 95% CI: 1.32–1.53, I² = 0.0%) (Table 5).

Discussions

Despite the substantial growth in ART service coverage, the incidence of AIDS-related mortality remains a major public health concern in Ethiopia. As a result, this study aimed to estimate the pooled incidence of mortality and identified advanced WHO clinical stage, bedridden functional status, anemia, CD4 count below 50/mm³, fair or poor ART adherence, and TB co-infection as predictors of mortality.

Accordingly, the pooled AIDS-related mortality rate among adults on ART in Ethiopia was 4 per 100 person-years (PY). This figure is similar to results from Tanzania, at 3.8 per 100 person-years (PY) [41], and findings from studies in four countries—Malawi, Kenya, Uganda, and Cambodia—at 2.28 per 100 person-years (PY) [42]. However, the mortality rate in this meta-analysis was lower than the rates reported in other studies: 7.66 per 100 PY in India, 7.44 per 100 PY in Kenya, 7.6 per 100 PY in Nigeria, and 5.9 per 100 PY in South Africa [43–46]. On the other hand, the AIDS-related mortality rate in this meta-analysis was much higher than in Europe and North America (0.81 per 100 PY) [47]. The discrepancy might be due to variations in sociodemographic factors, sample size, health service availability, the total duration of the study period, and political commitment. The findings suggest that improving access to ART, strengthening health services, and addressing socioeconomic barriers are critical to reducing AIDS-related mortality in Ethiopia. Additionally, the results emphasize the need for tailored interventions that consider local contexts to achieve global HIV targets.

Adults on ART in advanced WHO clinical stages (III and IV) had a higher mortality rate than did those in early WHO clinical stages (I and II) in this meta-analysis. This finding is similar with results from Tanzania, studies in four countries—Malawi, Kenya, Uganda, and Cambodia—as well as from Kenya, Nigeria, India, and Europe & north America [41–45, 47]. The possible explanations might include disease progression: advanced stages indicate more severe disease and greater immunosuppression, leading to increased vulnerability to opportunistic infections and complications [48]. Moreover, delayed diagnosis and treatment can result in delayed initiation of ART, leading to poorer health outcomes, including death [48, 49]. Similarly, higher viral load: more advanced stages are often associated with higher viral loads, which can accelerate disease progression and increase the risk of mortality [36, 37, 48]. Furthermore, comorbidities: patients in advanced stages may have more comorbid conditions, which contribute to higher mortality [38–40, 49]. This implies the need to educate communities about the importance of early testing and adherence to ART to reduce stigma and encourage timely treatment. In addition, care providers need to emphasize the importance of early HIV diagnosis and the initiation of ART to prevent progression to advanced stages. Moreover, healthcare infrastructure should be improved to support early diagnosis and continuous care, reducing progression to advanced stages. Multidisciplinary care, including specialists in infectious diseases, nutrition, and mental health, should be provided to offer comprehensive care. Furthermore, regular monitoring should be implemented for patients in advanced stages to manage opportunistic infections and complications effectively.

Similarly, Adults on ART with bedridden functional status had greater mortality rate than did those with ambulatory status in this meta-analysis. This finding is comparable with results from India, Tanzania, Kenya and Nigeria [41, 43–45]. The possible explanation might be that bedridden status often indicates severe disease progression or complications, leading to a higher risk of mortality [26, 27, 48, 49]. Moreover, bedridden individuals may experience poor nutrition, which can further weaken the immune system and complicate recovery [18, 28, 32]. In addition, they may face barriers to accessing healthcare. Bedridden patients often have multiple health issues (increased morbidities), which can exacerbate their overall condition and lead to higher mortality [1, 29, 34]. Furthermore, lack of mobility can result in complications such as pressure sores, thrombosis, and muscle wasting, which further impact health outcomes [6, 7, 48, 49]. This implies the need to prioritize intensive monitoring and care for bedridden patients to manage complications and improve outcomes. Moreover, a multidisciplinary care approach, including physiotherapists, nutritionists, and social workers, is essential to address the complex needs of bedridden patients. Furthermore, community support programs should be developed to assist with mobility and rehabilitation, reduce stigma, and provide social support to improve quality of life.

Adults on ART with anemia had greater mortality rate than did those without anemia in this meta-analysis. This finding is consistent with results from Europe and North America, as well as from studies in low- and middle-income countries and Eastern Africa [47, 50, 51]. A possible explanation might be that complications from ART medications can cause or worsen anemia, adding to the health burden, complicating treatment, and increasing the risk of mortality [51, 52]. In addition, anemia can exacerbate immune suppression, making individuals more susceptible to infections and complications, resulting in an increased risk of mortality [51, 53]. Furthermore, anemia often results from nutritional deficiencies or indicates underlying health conditions that contribute to an increased risk of mortality [51, 53, 54]. This suggests that routine anemia screening requires improvement, along with providing nutritional interventions and supplements to address potential deficiencies contributing to anemia. Additionally, ART regimens that may contribute to anemia should be monitored and adjusted to ensure a balance between effective HIV management and anemia treatment. Furthermore, health education campaigns should involve healthcare providers and communities to raise awareness about the link between anemia and increased mortality in people on ART.

Accordingly, adults on ART with a CD4 count of less than 50 cells/mm³ had a higher mortality rate compared to those with a CD4 count above 50 cells/mm³ in this meta-analysis. A possible explanation is that a CD4 count below 50/mm³ indicates severe immunosuppression, which may result from co-morbid conditions, higher viral load, and delayed ART initiation [55, 56]. This, in turn, leads to faster disease progression, increased vulnerability to opportunistic infections, and a higher mortality risk [57–59]. This implies the importance of encouraging early initiation of ART and prioritizing regular CD4 count monitoring to identify severe immunosuppression early and adjust treatment accordingly. Moreover, communities should be educated about the importance of early testing and adherence to ART, and healthcare systems should be strengthened to support early HIV testing and continuous care to prevent progression to severe immunosuppression.

This meta-analysis revealed that adult patients on ART with fair or poor adherence had a higher mortality rate compared to those with good adherence. This finding is consistent with results from studies conducted in India, low- and middle-income countries, and Eastern Africa [43, 50, 51]. Numerous factors may contribute to the incidence of mortality in ART programs. These include insufficient understanding of ART, complex treatment regimens with side effects, treatment fatigue, mental health issues, lack of social support, perceived stigma, economic barriers, and disruptions in care [48, 56, 57, 60, 61]. Consequently, these factors likely contribute to loss to follow-up and an increased risk of mortality [49]. This suggests that comprehensive community-based initiatives should focus on improving health literacy, promoting support networks among peers, combating social stigma, and tackling financial obstacles. Furthermore, healthcare professionals and managers need to enhance their monitoring and assessment methods, create tailored treatment plans, combine various care approaches, streamline medication regimens, and educate both patients and medical staff about the significance of treatment adherence and its potential outcomes.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

This meta-analysis includes a sufficient quantity of primary studies, providing a thorough representation of the data and enhancing the credibility of its findings. While this study provides valuable evidence, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, substantial heterogeneity was observed across the included studies, which remained even after subgroup analysis, sensitivity analysis, and meta-regression. Although a random-effects model was applied, unexplained heterogeneity may still have affected the precision of the pooled estimates. Second, important study-level and measurement-level variations were present. These include inconsistent adherence measurement tools, differences in anemia definitions and hemoglobin thresholds, and variable follow-up durations across studies. In addition, potential misclassification of outcomes may have occurred due to differences in measurement and reporting methods, which could have influenced the accuracy and comparability of pooled predictor estimates. Third, publication bias was detected using Egger’s test, suggesting possible small-study effects. While this may have influenced the magnitude of the pooled estimates, the overall direction of the findings is likely robust, although overestimation cannot be fully excluded. Fourth, the included studies employed different survival analysis models, including Cox proportional hazards, Weibull, and cause-specific hazard models. Although all effect sizes were standardized as hazard ratios and log-transformed for pooling, differences in model assumptions may have introduced additional methodological variability. Furthermore, the predominance of retrospective studies may have contributed to selection bias and incomplete data, which could not be fully controlled. Finally, the lack of uniform reporting of outcomes such as mean or median time to death limited more detailed time-to-event synthesis beyond hazard ratios.

Conclusions

The HIV-related mortality rate observed in the current meta-analysis was clinically meaningful and indicates a persistent burden that may hinder progress toward the United Nations goal of ending AIDS as a public health threat by 2030, highlighting the need for strengthened interventions. In addition, advanced WHO clinical stage, bedridden functional status, anemia, CD4 count below 50/mm³, poor or fair ART adherence, and TB co-infection were identified as significant predictors of AIDS-related mortality.

Clinical and Public Health Implications

This suggests that intensive community and facility-based awareness efforts need to be improved regarding early HIV testing and treatment, the implementation of routine HIV management monitoring and evaluation, and the encouragement of a multidisciplinary care approach to address mortality due to advanced WHO clinical stage, bedridden functional status, anemia, CD4 count below 50/mm³, poor or fair ART adherence, and TB co-infection.
