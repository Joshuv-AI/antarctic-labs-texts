Effect evaluation of fluid movement and rock properties for artificial seismic activity on hydrocarbons
Authors: Anoop Kanjirakat, Laith Abughaush, Arnel Carvero, Harris Rabbani, Mahmood Amani, Albertus Retnanto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the effectiveness of seismic stimulation in enhancing oil production from reservoirs, the impact of high-frequency vibrations on oil droplets confined within pore throats has not been adequately investigated. This laboratory-scale experimental study examines the impact of low and high-frequency sinusoidal vibrations in dislodging oil droplets trapped in a specific pore-throat region. Sinusoidal vibrations ranging from 10 to 80 Hz and with maximum displacement amplitudes of 1.2–0.06 mm are applied. By comparing the images captured prior to and after the application of seismic vibrations, the amount of oil recovered is determined. Commercial gasoline and diesel are used as trapped oil samples, and water is used as the injection fluid to bring out the effect of fluid viscosities in seismic dislodgment. While applying vibrations to trapped oil in the pore throat, three different recovery modes are observed: continuous, snap-off, and emulsified. The continuous oil recovery mode is observed in cases with minimal flow resistance through the pore throat, whereas a snap-off or intermittent recovery mode is observed when the flow resistance is high, and the vibration frequencies fall within the midrange of 40–60 Hz. An emulsified oil recovery mode is observed at high vibration frequencies. The amount of recovered oil increases as geometrical restrictions and trapped oil viscosity decrease. Seismic mobilization is observed to be directly proportional to the mobilization velocity and inversely proportional to the entrapped oil viscosity and geometrical restriction factors.

Introduction

The oil and gas industry is continuously developing alternative methods for improving hydrocarbon recovery from oil reservoirs. One such method used in industry is enhanced oil recovery (EOR), wherein chemicals, gases, and heat are injected into reservoirs to improve oil extraction and production. EOR techniques have several limitations and are inapplicable to all reservoir conditions. In addition, a maximum recovery of 60 % is only possible from various EOR methods [1]. The combination of two or more EOR techniques, known as improved oil recovery (IOR), is currently envisaged by the oil and gas industry to enhance effective oil recovery from a reservoir.

IOR techniques apply to various rock types, including sandstones, carbonates (limestones and dolomites), coals, and shales [2]. IOR techniques are utilized to extract additional oil from reservoirs that have already undergone primary and secondary recovery methods. It is possible to use several techniques to improve oil recovery, such as water flooding, gas injection, chemical injection, and thermal recovery [3]. However, it is essential to note that the effectiveness of these techniques can vary depending on the reservoir characteristics and the type of rock. Therefore, interpreters face a challenge in selecting the suitable method to improve subsurface geology interpretation and oil production in fields because there is no unique solution that can be applied to every field [4,5].

Several occurrences of increased oil production have been reported following earthquakes and similar seismic activities in oil fields [6]. This has triggered scientific attention and research on seismic stimulation as a prospective technique for IOR [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Seismic stimulation is emerging as a promising IOR technique because of its high effectiveness and low possibility of formation damage [11]. A schematic representation of such an IOR application is shown in Fig. 1. Here, a water-flooded reservoir is simultaneously stimulated by seismic vibrations. The inset shows oil droplets trapped within the porous matrix of the rock. The externally applied vibrations help mobilize the entrapped oil droplets within the rock pores.

IOR using seismic vibrations.

For seismic stimulation, low-frequency excitations are preferred in oil fields because high-frequency elastic waves get attenuated in porous media owing to their short wavelengths [12]. Although several studies have been conducted to clarify the mechanism by which seismic vibrational waves release entrapped globules, no conclusive theoretical model has been developed [13]. Limited laboratory experiments on this topic have been conducted. Kuznetsov et al. [14] proposed that seismic vibrations decrease water–oil interfacial tension, which in turn, facilitates the coalescence and mobilization of trapped oil ganglia. Nikolaevskiy [15] observed that low-frequency vibrations induce flows at the microscopic level, enabling the movement of trapped fluids. An earlier study [16] demonstrated that the application of acoustic vibrations with a 200 Hz frequency in water-driven kerosene reservoirs resulted in a 12 % increase in output. An experimental study [17] showed that subjecting a rock core sample to stress can alter wettability and cause the redistribution of two non-mixing phases. The effect of low-frequency vibration on the mobility of organic ganglia through a network of tiny pores was demonstrated by Li et al. [18]. An increase in the vibration acceleration amplitude was found to improve the mobilization of oil ganglia for a specific vibration frequency. In addition, decreasing the vibration frequency was more advantageous for a given acceleration amplitude. Dusseault et al. [19] examined the impact of pressure pulses and microscopic vibration mechanisms on IOR using flow visualization. They observed that when pressure waves move through the porous material, it makes the pores expand and speeds up the fluid flow. In another experimental study [20] using kerosene-saturated quartz-sand packs, a significant increase in the recovery was observed when subjected to acoustic waves. An acceleration of 0.8g (where g is the acceleration due to gravity) was generated during the experiment. A previous study [21] also observed an IOR of approximately 19 % when applying acoustic waves (within a frequency range of 9–40 Hz) in experiments conducted with a fine quartz-sand test section during water flooding processes.

Seismic stimulation involves the utilization of elastic waves having low frequency and high energy to mobilize oil from underground reservoirs. Methods include elastic-wave vibration, acoustic stimulation, ultrasound wave stimulation, pressure pulsing, and vibro-seismic stimulation [13]. The vibroseis method is a common technique employed for seismic stimulation on land. This method relies on the use of a shaker to generate vibrations via a piston-reaction mass device that is powered by a truck or buggy. The resultant wave field emanating from the source station propagates in a three-dimensional manner in all directions. The impact of high-frequency seismic waves on porous media is complex and depends on various factors, such as the medium's properties and wave characteristics [22].

The process of freeing oil droplets that are trapped in porous media is a complex phenomenon that involves the dynamics of multiphase flow. The mechanisms responsible for the mobilization process are crucial to understand, especially the impact of different vibration amplitudes and frequencies on dislodging the oil from the pores. Despite the importance of this area of study, there is a lack of controlled experimental observations at the laboratory scale, particularly when it comes to higher vibration frequencies. Therefore, further research is required to gain a more in-depth comprehension of the mobilization mechanisms and contribute to the development of effective approaches for the recovery of oil from porous media. Such research is crucial for the oil industry, as it can lead to the optimization of oil extraction processes and ultimately increase oil production. To address this issue, initial visualization and quantification studies were conducted by our group [23]. In the study, a diesel globule was trapped between two pore throats at a given injection pressure using water and was subjected to vibrations. A methodology of correlating oil recovery through analysis of images of the test section was developed. The analysis focused on the effects of vibrations on the entrapped oil globule, utilizing images captured prior to and following the application of seismic stimulations to the test section. It was observed that the effective change obtained through the image analysis represented a form of the oil recovery factor. The study was restricted to a single geometry and a single type of trapped oil.

In the current investigation, we are extending the scope of the preceding study. To facilitate future numerical and theoretical validation studies, a single pore throat restriction is considered in the present study. Two types of oils, commercial diesel and gasoline, each with distinct hydrodynamic properties, are used. Additionally, the experimental analysis considers two different pore-throat constrictions. The vibration amplitude and frequency values are varied to investigate the effects on the mobilization of entrapped oil. Sinusoidal vibrations with maximum displacement amplitudes of 1.2–0.06 mm and vibration frequencies of 10–80 Hz are applied.

Methodology

Experimental setup

The experimental facility utilized in our previous study [23] is employed here, and the diagram of the experimental setup is depicted in Fig. 2(a). A vibrating test section consists of a 3D-printed substrate having a single pore throat, as shown in Fig. 2(b). The test section is subjected to vibration that simulates seismic excitation using a benchtop Bruel and Kjaer vibration exciter (Type 4809). The exciter is powered by a Bruel & Kjaer 2718 power amplifier and a Bruel & Kjaer 3160 signal generator. The exciter's input is provided as an applied voltage, along with a vibration mode and frequency. The applied voltages ranged from 100 to 400 mV, and the frequencies ranged from 10 to 80 Hz. The increase in applied voltage corresponded to a rise in vibration amplitude, and the vibration mode is assigned a sinusoidal waveform. The exciter transfers vibration pulses to the test section via a connecting rod attached to a light-emitting diode (LED) pad that moves on the rollers. Horizontal vibrations are introduced to the test section, aligning with the direction of the pressure gradient. A steady flow is ensured through the section by employing a gravity-fed system from a reservoir to maintain a uniform pressure head. Water is used as the injection fluid. Constant inlet pressure of the injection is maintained using a two-reservoir system. During the experiments, the primary reservoir tank supplied a continuous amount of fluid to the adjustable reservoir. A built-in overflow outlet port in the height-adjustable reservoir ensures a steady water level. By altering the adjustable reservoir's vertical position, it was possible to control the inlet pressures at location 1, as illustrated in Fig. 2(a), within the test section. The fluid from the test section was discharged at atmospheric pressure from location 2. A predetermined quantity of oil to be entrapped in the micropore was administered into the test section through location 3 (T-junction), utilizing a SPETEC Symax 10–0102 syringe pump. A single-shot-dosing mode of the syringe pump was used for this purpose. The oil globule was entrapped in the test section through the manipulation of inlet pressure at location 1. The OMAX 3.2 MP USB digital camera, attached to a trinocular boom stand stereo microscope, captured images of the fluid movement during the seismic vibration [23].

(a) Schematic of experimental setup; (b) Top view of test section showing constriction.

The flow restriction at the pore throat is described based on the maximum (Wmax) and minimum (Wmin) dimensions of the pore-throat width values. The pore-throat geometric restriction factor, W∗, is defined as the ratio of the maximum width to the minimum width of the pore. Two constriction geometries are considered in this study: W∗ values of 10 and 5. Water is used as the injection fluid, and the oil entrapped in the throat region is either diesel or gasoline. Crude oil was not considered entrapment oil due to its sticking nature, which limited the visual observations in the test section. The specific details of these three cases are listed in Table 1. Constant injection pressure is exerted at the pore-throat region for all cases analyzed in this study.

The viscosities of the fluids were measured under ambient conditions using an OFITE-Model 800 viscometer. During the experiment, the height-adjustable reservoir level was maintained under a threshold level to entrap the fluid within the constriction. For all the cases investigated in this study, the height of the reservoir was constant such that the inlet injection pressure was 77 Pa. No flow occurred in the test section when fluid was trapped in the throat. After maintaining these stagnant flow conditions, seismic vibrations were applied. The test section was subjected to sinusoidal vibrations characterized by varying amplitudes and frequencies for a duration of 5 s. During experimentation, the effect of gasoline solubility on results is neglected due to the short interaction time between hydrocarbons and water. The entrapped fluid moved in the test section, depending on the vibration conditions. The experiment involved extracting two images from the visual recording: one captured prior to the application of sinusoidal vibrations and another 90 s thereafter. These images were analyzed in detail to estimate the amount of oil dislodged by external vibrations.

Test section

A top view of the test section with details of the constriction (throat) is shown in Fig. 2(b). Because the effect of fluid dislodgment caused by seismic vibrations required imaging of the fluid within the test section, a clear test section needs to be fabricated. An ANYCUBIC Photon Mono X three-dimensional printer was used for stereolithographic printing, together with an ANYCUBIC wash-and-cure station. The three-dimensional-printed pore models were cleaned and polished to obtain smooth scratch-free surfaces. The test section consisted of two inlet ports and an exit collection point. The injection fluid (water) was introduced into the test section via the primary inlet port. A syringe pump was then injected into the fluid for entrapment through the T-junction. The fluid exited directly into the outlet portion under atmospheric conditions to minimize the effects of back pressure. A sinusoidal design was used for the throat profile, following the model, y = 0.675sin(0.25πx), where x and y values are in millimeters. Wmax and Wmin (Fig. 2(b)) are the maximum and minimum widths of the constriction of the designed channel, respectively. Two constriction geometries are considered in this study. Wmin is kept constant at a value of 0.3 mm. Two Wmax values (3 and 1.5 mm) are used for the throat, resulting in Wmax/Wmin ratios of 10 and 5, respectively. The inlet ducts from the water and diesel inlet ports have a constant width of 1 mm. The vertical dimension of all channels, inclusive of the constriction within the pore model oriented perpendicular to Fig. 2 (referred to as the z-direction), remains constant at 1 mm.

Vibration characteristics

The voltage and frequency supplied to the shaker were altered to adjust the intensity of the vibrations in the test. Consequently, to accurately assess the displacements created by the shaker, initial experiments to characterize the vibrations were conducted. This involved attaching an accelerometer (Bruel & Kjaer Model 4534) to the vibration table. The signals from the accelerometer were recorded using a data-acquisition program provided by Bruel and Kjaer. The collected transient acceleration data were integrated twice in the frequency domain and converted to the time domain to derive displacement data using an in-house MATLAB code. For generating the sinusoidal waveform, the shaker utilized two input values: voltage, measured in millivolts (mV), and frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). In this study, the input voltage values varied from 100 to 400 mV, and the frequencies varied from 10 to 80 Hz.

The bubble charts in Fig. 3 present the displacement amplitudes that have been calculated from the accelerometer data, corresponding to the input values supplied to the shaker. The bubble size represents the amplitude of the physical displacement (Am [mm]), and the color of the bubbles corresponds to the maximum acceleration value recorded for each case. Even with the same applied voltage to the shaker, an exponential decrease in the displacement amplitude was observed with an increase in frequency, particularly at higher input voltages. For a specific frequency, the displacement amplitude increased with increasing input voltage. In addition, increasing the vibration frequency decreased the displacement amplitude for a particular voltage input. The acceleration induced by the vibration was higher at larger input voltages and frequencies, whereas it was lower when the voltage and frequency values were lower.

Displacement amplitude (Am) and maximum acceleration values for different voltage and frequency input conditions.

In this study, displacement amplitudes lower than 1.5 mm are only considered as they equate to earthquakes with a magnitude of three on the Richter scale. Such earthquakes are rarely experienced and are not significant environmental hazards. Hence, in the study, the shaker's displacement amplitude was kept below 1.2 mm for all examined frequencies.

Data analysis

To determine the quantity of entrapped fluid dislodged from the constriction, images acquired prior to and subsequent to the application of seismic vibrations are analyzed. A control zone near the pore throat constriction is designated for this purpose. Visible changes occurring in this zone are quantified [23]. For this purpose, the images initially cropped to the control region were binarized. A blue dye added to the water helped in binarizing the images, where the region filled with water had a pixel value of one, and the region filled with the entrapped fluid had a pixel value of zero. The total counts of white pixels in the binary images obtained before (Nbf) and after (Naf) applying vibrations were noted. The difference in the above counts would correspond to the amount of water added to the control zone, which in turn was proportional to the amount of trapped oil dislodged from the control zone. A reference parameter (Nref) is required to normalize the above difference. This reference condition would correspond to the possible scenario where the entrapped oil is fully dislodged by the injection fluid. Results and observations

After evaluating the vibration characteristics of the shaker, the test section with entrapped oil globules is subjected to sinusoidal vibration for a preset duration. The factors that can influence trapped oil recovery from the pore throat during seismic stimulations are the (i) vibration amplitude, (ii) vibration frequency, (iii) direction of applied vibration, (iv) transport properties of the trapped oil, (v) transport properties of the injection fluid, and (vi) geometric properties of the pore throat [13]. These factors are considered in our experimental study. The vibration amplitude (Am) or the peak-to-peak displacement and frequency were determined based on the applied voltage (V [mV]) and frequency (f [Hz]) applied to the seismic exciter or shaker (Fig. 3). The fluid transport properties were determined based on the type of fluid used. The measured viscosity values of the commercial diesel and gasoline used in the experiments were 3.7 ± 0.25 and 0.6 ± 0.25 cP, respectively. The measured viscosity value of the injection fluid (water) was 1 ± 0.25 cP. Hence, the viscosity ratios (μ∗, defined as the ratio of the viscosity of oil to the viscosity of water, μ∗=μoilμwater) are 3.7 and 0.6 using diesel and gasoline as the trapping fluids, respectively. The geometric flow restriction at the pore throat is governed by the geometry factor, W∗, which is maintained at 10 and 5. In all the cases, the injection pressure at the inlet of the test section was kept constant at 77 ± 10 Pa. Prior investigation for the duration of vibrations applied to the test section showed that 5 s of application was optimal to indicate distinguishable changes in the plots for the conditions selected in the study.

The entrapped oil subjected to seismic vibrations displayed various fluid motion patterns in the constriction zone, which are explained in the next section. The observations on the effect of vibration amplitude, frequency, geometric constriction, and entrapped oil viscosity ratio with the injection fluid are quantified for three cases (Table 1) and are discussed in detail in the next sections.

Recovery modes

Three distinct oil recovery modes (or fluid flow patterns) are observed during the experiments. These are schematically presented in Fig. 4(a)–4(c). When sinusoidal vibrations parallel to the flow direction are applied with a specific amplitude and frequency, they create forward and backwards flows near the pore-throat region due to inertial forces. The forward motion causes the trapped oil globules to overcome the resisting surface tension forces and pass through the pores. During the backward vibration cycle, reverse flow occurs in the throat region. Depending on the amplitude, frequency, and fluid transport characteristics, an oil globule could pass through the throat region in three possible modes, as shown in Fig. 4. Fig. 4(a) shows the conditions under which continuous oil recovery was observed while applying seismic vibrations. The vibration amplitude and frequency were generated such that a constant stream of oil flow was produced, even during the negative phase of the vibration. A continuous oil recovery mode was observed when the flow resistance through the pore throat was minimal. An increased vibration amplitude also imparted continuous recovery. Fig. 4(b) shows a scenario in which oil is recovered via snap-off. The vibration amplitude and frequency created a flow condition that resulted in oil globule breakage from its steady recovery. Such snap-off behavior (or intermittent mode) was observed when the flow resistance was high and the vibration frequencies were in the midranges (40–60 Hz). The third recovery mode was emulsified oil recovery. This was generally observed at high vibration frequencies. At high vibration frequencies, the forward and backward motions of the fluid flowing through the pore throat occur at a rapid rate, such that the oil or water breaks up or is emulsified. A schematic representation of this phenomenon is shown in Fig. 4(c). These three modes were experimentally observed in this study, and the details of their occurrence are described in the next section.

Oil recovery modes observed during seismic stimulations: (a) continuous oil recovery; (b) snap-off oil recovery; (c) emulsified oil recovery. The green color represents oil, and the blue color represents water.

A comparison of Fig. 6, Fig. 7 shows that the amount of oil recovered is higher when the geometrical restriction is lower (W∗ = 5). At increased W∗ values, the amount of recovered oil decreased. In addition, the emulsified recovery mode was observed when a pore throat with a high W∗ value was used. Sinusoidal vibrations transported some water to the left side of the throat region, where oil was initially entrapped (Fig. 7). This is potentially due to water's lower viscosity compared to diesel, which allows it to easily pass through the pore throats. Higher-frequency vibrations and increased flow resistance resulted in the emulsification of the tapped oil, as shown in Fig. 7.

The effect of the viscosity values of the trapped oil can be compared in Fig. 7, Fig. 8. Water was observed to be emulsified in Fig. 8, whereas oil was observed to be emulsified in Fig. 7. Moreover, the amount of recovered oil was greater when using less viscous oil (gasoline, or when μ∗ = 0.6) than when using more viscous oil (diesel, or when μ∗ = 3.7).

Quantification of oil recovery for the three cases

In this section, the three cases analyzed in this study are compared. While Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 8 show the transient and qualitative representation of the dislodgement process, Fig. 9, Fig. 10, Fig. 11 represent the quantitative measurement of the percentage change (averaged values) of oil dislodgment in the pore throat resulting from the applied vibrations. The bubble size in the chart represents the magnitude of the average value of the percentage effective change obtained, and the bubble color represents the recovery mode. The average value is obtained after conducting a minimum of three experimental trials. The maximum experimental uncertainty observed for the percentage effective change is ±2.5 % [23]. Fig. 9, Fig. 10, Fig. 11 show the effective percentage changes (averaged values from three repeated trials) for Case1, Case2, and Case3, respectively. The x- and y-axes in these figures represent the voltage and frequency of the vibration exciter, respectively. As shown in Fig. 3, the corresponding vibration displacement movements were imparted to the test section and pore throat. Fig. 9 shows the bubble chart representing the percentage effective change generated for Case1, with W∗ = 5 and μ∗ = 3.7. In this case, the geometric flow restriction is low, and more viscous diesel is used as the trapping oil. No oil recovery was observed at a low input voltage (V = 100 mV), even at an increased vibration frequency. At an input voltage of V = 200 mV, oil recovery increased with increasing vibration frequency up to 40 Hz. However, beyond 40 Hz, an increase in the vibration frequency did not cause a proportional increase in oil recovery. At input voltages of 300 and 400 mV, higher magnitudes of effective changes (or oil recovery) were observed for all frequencies. A continuous oil recovery mode was observed at low frequencies. In contrast, emulsified recovery was observed when the vibration input voltage and frequency were high (V = 400 mV and f = 80 Hz). In all other cases, snap-off recovery modes were observed.

Fig. 10 shows the percentage effective change in the control zone observed for Case2, where W∗ = 10 and μ∗ = 3.7. The geometric flow restriction factor is high (compared to Case1), and diesel is used as the trapping oil. The percentage effective change caused by the seismic vibrations in Fig. 10 is smaller than those in Fig. 9. A decreased amount of recovered oil with increased geometrical pore-throat restriction was observed. Unlike in Case1, no significant quantities of recovered oil were observed at lower vibration frequencies in Case2, even at higher input voltages. At a given vibration frequency (for example, 60 Hz), an increase in the applied voltage resulted in increased oil recovery. However, at a given input voltage (for example, 300 mV), an increase in the vibration frequency resulted in a nonmonotonic variation in oil recovery. Above a particular vibration frequency value (∼60 Hz), the percentage change caused by seismic stimulation decreased with increasing frequency. This trend may be attributed to the inherent characteristics of the vibration exciter, in which the vibration displacement amplitude decreases at higher frequencies. In Case2, no significant continuous recovery modes were observed. Snap-ff or intermittent mode of recovery was observed in mid-frequency and voltage ranges. A higher geometric pore-throat restriction resulted in a more emulsified recovery mode compared to a lower geometric pore-throat restriction.

Fig. 11 shows the percentage effective changes generated for Case 3, with W∗ = 10 and μ∗ = 0.6. Gasoline was used as the trapping fluid for the results presented in Fig. 11. Comparisons between Fig. 10, Fig. 11 illustrate how the viscosity of entrapped oil affects seismic recovery for a specific pore throat constriction. Unlike in Case2, considerable oil recovery occurred at 10 Hz in Case3. However, the percentage effective change in Case3 was significantly lower than that in Case1. In other words, at lower vibration frequencies, reducing flow restriction has a greater impact on improving oil recovery than reducing the viscosity of trapped oil. For example, a comparison of Fig. 9, Fig. 11 (at V = 400 mV and f = 10 Hz) showed that the magnitude of the percentage effective change was at least five times higher in Case1 than in Case3. For the conditions presented in Fig. 11, snap-off (or intermittent) recovery modes were not observed, and continuous modes were observed at low vibration frequencies. However, highly emulsified recovery modes were observed in most cases, particularly at higher frequencies.

Relation between parameters and limitations of the study

It may be noted that in all the cases studied here, recovery was not observed while applying 100 mV input voltage at 10Hz. The maximum displacement vibration amplitude generated by the above condition was 0.25 mm. However, no mobilization of the trapped oil globule was observed. In contrast, mobilization was observed while applying 200 mV and 60 Hz, which generated a smaller vibration displacement of 0.18 mm. Hence, it may be inferred that mobilization would depend not solely on the vibration displacement but also on the vibration frequency.

In light of our findings, it has been observed that the mobilization effect is directly proportional to the product of vibration amplitude, denoted as Am (length), and frequency, denoted as f (1/time). This product can be defined as the mobilization velocity (length/time). In addition, the magnitude of seismic mobilization is observed more when the pore throat geometrical restriction (W∗) is small and when the entrapped oil has lower viscosity (μ) values. Hence, the seismic mobilization factor can be modeled, as expressed by Eq. (2).

The higher the mobilization factor, the higher the amount of oil recovery caused by seismic vibrations. In addition, based on visual observations during the experiment, it was found that continuous oil recovery mode outperformed snap-off and emulsified modes in terms of producing oil. This finding suggests that continuous mode may be a more effective approach for oil recovery. However, further studies may be required to validate these observations.

In this study, the ideal case of oil entrapment was experimentally investigated. In a practical scenario, the oil globule is trapped in a complex porous domain like that shown in Fig. 12. The oil is trapped within multiple throat regions with varying pore-throat geometry factors (W∗). Applying seismic vibrations would result in the mobilization or breakup of the globule, which may not be predictable from the current analysis. However, the localized mobility in each pore-throat region (W1∗, W2∗e tc.) will be governed by the mobilization factor, as expressed by Eq. (2). The combined effect would result in the global mobilization or breakup of the oil droplet. The mobilization factor derived above is based on the limited experimental observations in this present study. More controlled experimental studies are required to derive a comprehensive mobilization factor.

Trapped oil in porous structure.

Our experiments also did not include the effect of gases. The degassing mechanism caused by elastic waves could enhance the oil's relative permeability, which would improve oil recovery. In addition, during the degassing process, gas movement to the pore walls could change rock wettability and impact oil and water relative permeability [24,25]. Another limitation of this work is the fact that the effect of the wetting nature of the fluids is not appropriately compared. It is worth noting that gasoline and diesel may not accurately represent the wetting behavior of crude oil when it interacts with rock surfaces. Although these fuels can indicate changes in viscosity, they might not be a reliable indicator in situations where trapped oil is impacted by surface wetting (oil-wet). Hence, it is crucial to bear in mind that the experimental outcomes are more relevant to water-wet conditions, where crude oil is subjected to pore-throat capillary pressures. It is not recommended to extend these findings to oil-wet situations where trapped oil is influenced by surface wetting. This study could not also obtain quantifiable data on the impact of varied pore pressures on seismic vibrations due to the sturdy nature of the 3D-printed test section walls.

The images captured in this study were obtained at lower frame rates. In order to better understand the fluid velocities induced by vibrations within the pore region and how they correlate with oil recovery, we plan to conduct imaging studies at higher frame rates in the future. The application of vibration perpendicular to the injection direction is also an area of interest and will be studied in the future.

Conclusions

This study examined the effectiveness of sinusoidal vibrations in removing trapped oil globules in a single pore-throat region. Vibrations with displacement amplitudes between 1.2 and 0.06 mm and frequencies from 10 to 80 Hz were applied parallel to the flow direction. Oil recovery was measured by comparing images before and after applying the vibrations. The following significant conclusions are drawn from the experimental study.

i)Three oil recovery modes are observed: continuous, snap-off (or intermittent), and emulsified. High amplitude and low-frequency vibrations cause continuous recovery, while snap-off and emulsified recovery occur at higher frequencies.

ii)A continuous recovery displayed more efficient oil recovery when compared to an emulsified recovery mode.

A continuous recovery displayed more efficient oil recovery when compared to an emulsified recovery mode.

iii)The oil recovery increased when the geometrical restriction at the pore throat decreased at a specific viscosity of the oil trapped at the throat.

iv)Lower viscosity values of the trapped oil also improved recovery rates for a given pore-throat size.

v)Seismic mobilization is observed to be proportional to the mobilization velocity, which is the product of the vibration amplitude and frequency.

In conclusion, it may be posited that seismic stimulation is an efficacious means of augmenting oil recovery. The technique operates by engendering pressure waves and vibrations that facilitate the mobilization of trapped oil and, in turn, render it more readily extractable. This approach enables the retrieval of additional oil that would otherwise be irremediable and is characterized by salutary effects that improve oil recovery from depleted reservoirs.
