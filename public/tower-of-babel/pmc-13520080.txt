Effects of Thymus vulgaris, Euphorbia hirta, and Allium sativum as phytogenic feed additives on nutrient digestibility, gut microbiota, and performance of Kabir chickens in cameroon
Authors: Felix Ngwese Ebwekoh, Emmanuel Takor Ojong, Joseline Sob Motsa’a, Jean Paul Toukala, Kenneth JN Ndamukong, Christian Keambou Tiambo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First evaluation of T. vulgaris, E. hirta, and A. sativum as phytogenic feed additives in Cameroon Kabir chickens under local tropical conditions.•E. hirta at 0.75% significantly improved carbohydrate digestibility, while multiple plant treatments enhanced ash and cellulose digestibility.•All three phytogenic additives markedly reduced total caecal bacterial counts compared to both negative control and oxytetracycline positive control (P < 0.000), with 1.5% E. hirta showing the strongest antimicrobial effect.•Supplementation with the three plants generally outperformed oxytetracycline in modulating gut microbiota, including reductions in Escherichia coli, Salmonella, Shigella, and Proteus spp.•Demonstrates locally available Cameroonian medicinal plants as promising, sustainable natural alternatives to antibiotic growth promoters for indigenous dual-purpose chickens.Keywords: Phytogenic feed additives, Kabir chicken, Digestibility, Gut microbiota, Thymus vulgaris, Euphorbia hirta, Allium sativum, Antibiotic alternative

Introduction

Phytogenic feed additives (PFAs) are plant-derived compounds incorporated into animal diets to enhance productivity by improving digestibility, nutrient absorption, and gut microbiota balance (Athanasiadou et al., 2007). While conventional feed additives such as antibiotics, enzymes, and antioxidants improve feed intake and growth rate (Abouelfetouh & Moussa, 2012), the widespread use of antibiotic growth promoters (AGPs) has raised major concerns regarding antimicrobial resistance, drug residues in animal products, and environmental contamination (Aminullah et al., 2025). This has spurred global interest in PFAs as safer natural alternatives with antimicrobial, antioxidant, digestive, and immune-modulating properties (Hassan et al., 2024; Oni & Oke, 2025; Wang et al., 2024).

PFAs exert their beneficial effects through multiple mechanisms, including stimulation of digestive enzyme secretion, enhancement of nutrient digestibility, modulation of intestinal morphology (increased villus height and reduced crypt depth), and favourable shifts in gut microbiota by promoting beneficial bacteria while suppressing pathogens (Guo et al., 2004a, 2004b; Hashemi et al., 2009; Hosseinzadeh et al., 2015).

Among promising PFAs, Thymus vulgaris (thyme), Allium sativum (garlic), and Euphorbia hirta have shown notable potential. Milewski et al. (2026) reported that dietary thyme (1%), alone or combined with garlic, improved body weight gain, feed conversion ratio, organic matter digestibility, and carcass quality while reducing E. coli populations. Saied et al. (2025) and Bandey et al. (2025) similarly demonstrated enhanced growth performance, digestive enzyme activity, antioxidant status, and gut health with thyme supplementation. Garlic, rich in allicin and sulfur compounds, exhibits strong antimicrobial and digestion stimulating effects (Oni & Oke, 2025). Euphorbia hirta, though less studied, has demonstrated improved feed conversion, reduced Salmonella colonisation, and better intestinal morphology in broilers due to its flavonoids, tannins, and polyphenols (Hashemi et al., 2014).

Research on indigenous dual-purpose breeds such as the Cameroon Kabir chicken remains limited, despite their adaptation to tropical smallholder systems. Previous studies on Kabir chickens have shown positive responses to some phytogenics (Ngantu et al., 2016; Ojong et al., 2020 and Nghonjuyi et al., 2024), but data on the combined use of T. vulgaris, E. hirta, and A. sativum particularly their effects on nutrient digestibility and gut microbiota under local conditions are scarce. This study therefore aimed to evaluate the effects of graded levels of these three locally available plant powders on growth performance, nutrient digestibility, gut microbiota, and survival of Cameroon Kabir chicken roosters as potential antibiotic alternatives.

Materials and methods

Study site

This research was carried out at the Buea’s Agri-Science Action and Development Poultry Research, South-West Region of Cameroon (521 m above sea level at latitude 4° 09′ 22.89″ N and longitude 9° 18′ 12.89″ E) and at the Life Sciences Laboratory of the Faculty of Science, University of Buea and the Laboratory of Animal Physiology and Health, FASA, University of Dschang.

Preparation of plants and animals

Thyme and garlic were purchased from the local markets, while euphorbia was collected in Buea and surroundings. The plants were prepared following the methods proposed by Ngantu et al. (2016). Eight-week-old Kabir chicks were provided by the Association of Farmers and Breeders of Kevin Foto Dschang (AEKDS), Cameroon. They were raised in an intensive system using battery cages. On arrival, their average weight was 1159.00 g They were given anti-stress and water and then acclimatised for two weeks while feeding them the basal diets (Table 1). After the acclimatisation period their average weight was 1282.11 g.

Diets and experimental design

All the roosters were weighed and randomly allocated to eight treatment groups (Table 2), in two replicates each (10 birds per replicate). Each bird served as an experimental unit. The birds were given fresh, cool and clean water each morning and had access to feed ad libitum. After the two weeks of acclimatisation, the birds were fed with the experimental diets for 16 weeks. The chickens were maintained under a natural lighting cycle throughout the experimental period. They were weighed weekly, and feed intake was evaluated weekly. The grower feed was administered to the roosters for 12 weeks, then the reproduction feed for 4 weeks.

Evaluation of digestibility or bioavailable nutrients

Fifty grams of fresh faecal samples were aseptically collected from chicken in the various treatments into separate sterile labelled containers and taken to the laboratory, where they were dried at 60 °C till constant weight in a hot air oven. Subsequently, dried faeces were milled using a homemade tri-hammer mill and kept in plastic bags for analysis to determine the dry matter content, carbohydrate, ash, cellulose, crude protein, fat and metabolisable energy according to the official method of analysis described by the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC 1990).

Assessing the effects of feed additives on the gut microbiota of the chickens in the various treatment

At the end of the experiment, 5 birds per replicate were collected and slaughtered for easy collection of gut content to estimate the total microbes in the various treatments. Fresh samples were collected from the caecum into labelled tubes in ice blocks and transferred to the laboratory immediately. The standard dehydrated Tryptone Glucose Extract Agar (beef extract-3 g, tryptone-5 g, dextrose/glucose-1 g and agar-15 g) was the general-purpose medium used to culture bacteria. After incubation and subculturing, various techniques were used to identify the bacteria, as shown in Table 3. The characteristics used to describe the morphology of the bacterial colonies were size, shape, colour (pigmentation), texture, height (elevation), and edge (margin). Physical characteristics such as cell wall, shape and linkages of the bacteria were also observed. Standard laboratory procedures such as subculturing, Gram reaction, haemolysis, growth on blood agar, facultatively anaerobic/microphilic reactions, catalase test, H₂O₂ production, yellow pigment production, motility, Simon citrate test, urease test, H₂S gas production, indole test, and acid from lactose reaction were used to further narrow down the identification. A Gram stain was done in order to observe the colony appearance and the individual features and help in the identification of gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria. Also, the number of mortalities was recorded for each treatment group.

Statistical analyses

All the data collected for weight gain, feed intake, feed conversion ratio, digestibility and gut microbiota were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) at P < 0.05. Where differences were significant, Duncan multiple range tests were used to separate means. The statistical power for growth performance parameters was calculated as 0.59. All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences software (SPSS, IBM version 21.0).

Results

Effects of the additives on growth performances of Kabir chickens

The effect of the various feed additives on the growth performances of Kabir chickens is presented in Table 4. Although the effects of the various feed additives on the growth performances were not statistically significant, chickens that were fed with feed that contained 1.0% Allium sativum (garlic), 1.5% Euphorbia hirta and 0.75% Euphorbia hirta recorded the highest feed intake, while that parameter was lower for the two control groups, 1.0% thyme and 0.5% garlic. Chickens supplemented with 1.5% Euphorbia hirta recorded the highest daily weight gain (22.2 g), while the best feed conversion ratio was obtained in chickens receiving 1.0% Allium sativum (Table 5).

Effects of Thymus vulgaris, Euphorbia hirta and Allium sativum on digestibility

The effects of Thymus vulgaris, Euphorbia hirta and Allium sativum on carbohydrate, ash and cellulose digestibility were significantly different (P < 0.011, P < 0.006, P < 0.002, respectively) (Table 6). Euphorbia hirta at 0.75% produced the highest effect on carbohydrate digestibility (86.6% DM), while 0.5% Allium sativum had the least effect (77.9% DM). The ash digestibility values for 0.75% Euphorbia hirta, 1.5% Euphorbia hirta, 0.5% thyme, 1.0% thyme and 0.5% garlic were lower than that for the negative control. While 1.5% Euphorbia, 0.5% Thyme and 1.0% Garlic recorded values for cellulose digestibility which were higher than that for the negative control, 0.75% Euphorbia, 1.0% Thyme and 0.5% Garlic recorded values that were lower than the negative control. The results of this study also revealed that the effects of Thymus vulgaris, Euphorbia hirta and Allium sativum on protein digestibility, fat digestibility and dry matter digestibility were not significantly different.

Effects of thymus vulgaris, euphorbia hirta and allium sativum on gut microbiota and survival of the chickens

The species of bacteria that were identified as gut microbiota included Streptococcus species, Lactobacillus species, E. coli species, Shigella species, Salmonella species and Proteus species. All the three plants (Thymus vulgaris, Euphorbia hirta and Allium sativum) had significant effects on the gut microbiota of the chickens in the various treatment groups (Table 7). The effects of 0.75% Euphorbia hirta, 1.5% Euphorbia hirta, 0.5% thyme, 1.0% thyme, 0.5% garlic and 1.0% garlic were all higher than those of the negative control group. The study also revealed that 0.75% Euphorbia hirta, 1.5% Euphorbia hirta, 0.5% thyme, 1.0% thyme, and 1.0% garlic equally had effects that were higher than that of the positive control group. 1.5% Euphorbia hirta powder produced the greatest effect (potency), while oxytetracycline at a dosage of 0.5 g / 1 L of H₂O produced the least effect on gut microbiota.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that dietary supplementation with Thymus vulgaris, Euphorbia hirta, and Allium sativum at the tested inclusion levels had no significant effects on feed intake, body weight gain, or feed conversion ratio (FCR) in Cameroon Kabir chicken roosters. These findings align with several earlier reports indicating limited or no growth-promoting effects of garlic and thyme under specific conditions. For instance, Sarica et al. (2005) observed comparable weight gain between chickens fed garlic and thyme extract supplemented diets and control birds. Similarly, Bolukbasi et al. (2006) and Ojong et al. (2020) reported no significant improvements in weight gain or FCR following garlic or thyme supplementation in broilers and Cameroon Kabir chicken roosters respectively.

These outcomes can be mechanistically justified by the age of the experimental birds. The roosters in this study were already 8 weeks old at the onset of the trial, a stage at which the rapid early growth phase has largely subsided. This pattern was empirically supported by Amad et al. (2011), who noted superior feed efficiency during the initial weeks of phytogenic feed additive (PFA) supplementation, with diminishing returns as birds aged, ultimately resulting in non-significant effects on body weight, weight gain, and feed intake across treatments.

Environmental factors likely compounded these effects. The trial period (February to May) coincided with lower light intensity, which is known to reduce locomotor activity and feed seeking behavior in poultry (Ocak et al., 2008). Collectively, these results underscore that the efficacy of phytogenic additives is modulated by multiple interacting factors, including animal age, diet composition, hygiene status, environmental conditions, and product quality (Jang et al., 2007; Shams et al., 2011).

In contrast to the lack of growth performance benefits, supplementation with T. vulgaris, E. hirta, and A. sativum exerted significant effects on the digestibility of carbohydrates, ash, and cellulose, while showing no significant influence on dry matter, crude protein, crude fat, or metabolisable energy. This selective improvement in nutrient digestibility is consistent with dose dependent responses reported in the literature. Amad et al. (2011) demonstrated linear increases in the apparent ileal digestibility of crude ash, crude protein, crude fat, calcium, and phosphorus with escalating PFA concentrations (150 −1500 mg/kg) across all production phases. Hernández et al. (2004) similarly found enhanced apparent ileal digestibility of dry matter and starch in starter diets, and improved faecal digestibility of dry matter and crude protein in finisher diets following PFA inclusion. The positive effects observed here on carbohydrate, ash, and cellulose digestibility align with these patterns and support the broader evidence that phytogenics can enhance nutrient utilisation (Amad et al., 2011; Milewski et al., 2026).

Several plausible mechanisms explain these digestibility improvements. Phytogenic compounds may stimulate digestive enzyme secretion from the pancreas and intestinal mucosa, prolong enzyme activity, or increase bile flow, thereby facilitating better breakdown and absorption of nutrients in the small intestine (Amad et al., 2011; Bandey et al., 2025; Saied et al., 2025). Additionally, morphological changes in the gut, such as increased villus height and reduced mucosal thickness can expand the absorptive surface area and enhance enzymatic activity without inflammation (Ganapathi et al., 2015; Murugesan et al., 2014). Other contributing factors, including modulation of gut microbiota, goblet cell activity, mucus production, and immune function, likely play supportive roles (Hafeez et al., 2016).

Notably, certain inclusion levels (e.g., 1.5% E. hirta, 0.5–1.0% T. vulgaris, and 0.5–1.0% A. sativum) resulted in lower carbohydrate digestibility compared to the negative control. This suggests potential inhibitory effects at specific doses or stages, possibly through interference with carbohydrate digesting enzymes or altered substrate availability. The dose dependent nature of garlic’s influence implies that higher inclusions may be more suitable for starter/grower phases, while lower doses could be preferable during finishing. The absence of effects on metabolisable energy is consistent with Petrolli et al. (2012), who reported no significant changes following herbal extract supplementation.

Supplementation with the three plants also significantly reduced gut microbial loads relative to controls (P < 0.001), outperforming oxytetracycline in this regard. This aligns with documented antimicrobial properties: E. hirta extracts inhibit E. coli, S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, and Bacillus subtilis (Ogbulie et al., 2007); thyme reduces ileal aerobic bacteria (Shams et al., 2011); and garlic exhibits broad-spectrum activity against gram positive and gram-negative pathogens, including Salmonella, E. coli, and others (Daka, 2011; Gedif & Hahn, 2002; Kumar et al., 2010). The superior reduction compared to oxytetracycline is attributable to the latter’s high systemic absorption in the proximal gastrointestinal tract, leaving minimal luminal concentrations for direct antimicrobial action, compounded by pre-existing resistance among commensal bacteria (Ian & Marilyn, 2001 and Fairchild et al., 2005). These plants thus show promise as natural alternatives to antibiotics, with effects that are dose dependent and most pronounced at higher inclusions (e.g., 1.5% E. hirta).

Finally, no significant differences in survival rates were observed among treatments (P = 0.762), consistent with Yalçın et al. (2006), who found no mortality impact from garlic powder in laying hens. Although oxytetracycline numerically supported the highest survival, further studies are warranted to clarify interactions under varying health challenges.

While T. vulgaris, E. hirta, and A. sativum did not enhance growth performance under the conditions of this study, they improved specific nutrient digestibilities and exerted strong antimicrobial effects on gut microbiota. These benefits appear mediated by enzymatic stimulation, gut morphology modulation, and direct antimicrobial activity, highlighting their potential as targeted phytogenic feed additives in Kabir chicken production, particularly as antibiotic alternatives. Optimal inclusion levels and timing should be refined based on bird age and production phase to maximize advantages while minimizing any inhibitory effects.

Limitations of the study

The study had a limited number of replicates (n = 2 per treatment), resulting in a statistical power of 0.59 for growth performance parameters. This low power increases the risk of Type II error (false negatives), meaning that potential effects on weight gain and feed conversion ratio may have gone undetected. Although sufficient for detecting changes in microbiota and digestibility, future studies with larger sample sizes and more replicates are recommended to confirm growth-related findings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that dietary supplementation of Thymus vulgaris, Euphorbia hirta, and Allium sativum powders at the tested inclusion levels had no significant effects on feed intake, body weight gain, feed conversion ratio, or survival rate of Cameroon Kabir chicken roosters. However, the study had limited statistical power (0.59) to detect differences in growth parameters, highlighting the need for future research with larger sample sizes. However, the phytogenics significantly improved the digestibility of carbohydrates, ash, and cellulose while exerting potent antimicrobial effects that substantially reduced gut microbial loads compared to both the negative control and oxytetracycline-treated groups. These benefits are likely mediated through enhanced digestive enzyme activity, favorable modulation of intestinal morphology, and direct antibacterial properties of the bioactive compounds present in the plants. The findings highlight the potential of these locally available herbs as natural alternatives to synthetic antibiotics in poultry production, particularly for improving nutrient utilisation and gut health. Nevertheless, their growth promoting efficacy appears limited in older birds under suboptimal environmental conditions, underscoring the need for further research to optimize inclusion levels, extract standardization, and application timing across different production phases for maximum economic and performance benefits in Kabir chicken farming.
