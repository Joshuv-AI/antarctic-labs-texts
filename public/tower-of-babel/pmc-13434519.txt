Upadacitinib in Asian inflammatory bowel disease practice: encouraging real-world efficacy
Authors: Eun Soo Kim
Published: 2026

The therapeutic landscape of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) has evolved rapidly over the past decade, shifting from broad immunosuppression toward increasingly targeted approaches [1]. Among these, Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors have attracted considerable attention because of their rapid onset of action, oral administration, and efficacy in biologic-refractory disease. Upadacitinib, a selective JAK1 inhibitor, has emerged as one of the most promising advanced therapies for both ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD), supported by robust phase 3 trial data [2]. Yet, despite expanding global use, real-world evidence from Asian populations has remained limited.

In this issue of Intestinal Research, Kim et al. [3] report one of the earliest Korean real-world experiences evaluating upadacitinib in patients with moderate-to-severe IBD. The investigators demonstrated high rates of symptom-based clinical remission at 6 months in both CD (82.4%) and UC (81.8%), despite the overwhelming majority of patients having prior biologic exposure and multiple biologic failures. These findings provide important early insight into the practical utility of upadacitinib in routine Asian IBD care.

Several aspects of this study deserve attention from clinicians. First, the cohort represents a particularly difficult-to-treat population. Nearly all patients were biologic-experienced, and more than three-quarters had failed at least 2 advanced therapies. Such patients are frequently encountered in tertiary referral centers, where therapeutic sequencing has become increasingly complex. In this context, the observed response rates are clinically meaningful and reinforce the emerging perception that upadacitinib may retain efficacy even after multiple biologic failures. Second, the study emphasizes symptom-based patient-reported outcomes (PROs) rather than relying exclusively on conventional indices such as the Crohn’s Disease Activity Index or Mayo score. This approach reflects an important shift in modern IBD practice. While regulatory trials prioritize standardized composite endpoints, clinicians are often more concerned with rapid symptom relief, restoration of daily functioning, and steroid avoidance. From a practical standpoint, improvement in diarrhea frequency, abdominal pain, stool urgency, and rectal bleeding may better capture what truly matters to patients in routine care.

Nevertheless, several issues warrant careful interpretation before these findings are generalized too broadly. Most importantly, the remarkably high remission rates should be interpreted cautiously. Real-world observational studies frequently report outcomes that differ substantially from randomized controlled trials because of heterogeneity in endpoint definitions, follow-up completeness, and treatment selection bias. In the present study, remission was assessed primarily using symptom-based criteria, and follow-up data at 6 months were unavailable for a proportion of the original cohort. Moreover, objective inflammatory assessments—including standardized endoscopic evaluation and comprehensive biomarker monitoring—were limited. Although symptom improvement is clinically relevant, discordance between symptomatic and endoscopic remission is well recognized in IBD, particularly in CD. Indeed, the biomarker data in this study highlight this important gap. Only a subset of patients who achieved PRO-based remission also demonstrated fecal calprotectin normalization. This discrepancy raises a clinically relevant question: are we observing true disease modification or predominantly symptomatic control? As therapeutic targets in IBD increasingly move toward transmural healing and endoscopic remission, reliance on symptom-based outcomes alone may overestimate long-term disease control.

The safety findings are similarly reassuring but should not yet be considered definitive. No serious adverse events, thromboembolic complications, or treatment discontinuations were reported. However, the relatively short follow-up duration and limited sample size substantially constrain interpretation of uncommon but clinically important events. This issue is particularly relevant in the broader context of JAK inhibitor safety concerns following the ORAL Surveillance study of tofacitinib. Although upadacitinib’s greater JAK1 selectivity may theoretically reduce off-target toxicity, whether this translates into a superior long-term safety profile in IBD remains incompletely established. Clinicians should therefore resist the temptation to interpret the favorable short-term safety signal as proof of class-wide reassurance. Careful patient selection remains essential, particularly in older patients and those with cardiovascular risk factors, prior thromboembolic disease, malignancy history, or herpes zoster susceptibility. Vaccination strategies and laboratory monitoring should remain integral components of JAK inhibitor use in daily practice.

An additional clinically important observation is that 6 patients with CD ultimately required surgery during treatment. Rather than representing therapeutic failure alone, these cases underscore a central limitation of advanced medical therapy in structurally complicated CD. Once fibrostenotic or penetrating disease becomes established, pharmacologic suppression of inflammation may not reverse irreversible bowel damage. This finding reinforces a broader principle increasingly recognized in IBD management: timing matters. Highly effective therapies may have their greatest impact when introduced before cumulative bowel injury develops. From a therapeutic positioning perspective, studies such as this contribute to the ongoing debate regarding where upadacitinib should be placed within treatment algorithms. Traditionally, JAK inhibitors have often been reserved for later-line use after biologic failure because of safety concerns. However, the rapid efficacy, oral administration, and robust effectiveness observed across multiple refractory cohorts increasingly challenge this conservative sequencing paradigm [4]. Whether upadacitinib should remain primarily a rescue therapy or move earlier in selected high-risk patients is likely to become a major discussion point in future guideline development [5]. Importantly, Asian-specific data are particularly valuable in this context. Ethnic differences in IBD phenotype, infectious risk, pharmacogenomics, and healthcare systems may significantly influence both effectiveness and safety outcomes [6]. Therefore, regional real-world evidence should not merely be viewed as confirmatory but rather as necessary for contextualizing global trial data within local clinical practice.

Ultimately, the study by Kim et al.3 provides encouraging early evidence supporting the real-world utility of upadacitinib in Korean patients with IBD. At the same time, it also highlights the persistent gap between symptomatic improvement and objective disease control, as well as the need for longer-term safety surveillance. For clinicians, the key message is not simply that upadacitinib “works,” but rather that its optimal use will require careful patient selection, timely intervention, and continued vigilance regarding long-term outcomes. As the therapeutic armamentarium for IBD continues to expand, the challenge is no longer merely achieving clinical response, but determining which therapy should be used, in which patient, and at what stage of disease progression. Real-world studies such as this represent an important step toward answering those increasingly complex questions.
