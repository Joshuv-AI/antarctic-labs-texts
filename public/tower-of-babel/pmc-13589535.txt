Nonoperative Management of a T1 Spinous Process Apophyseal Stress Fracture in an Adolescent Baseball Pitcher: A Case Report
Authors: Yuya Fukuda, Kinshi Kato, Kenichi Otoshi, Takahiro Kaga, Yoshihiro Matsumoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOveruse-related stress injuries of the thoracic spine are uncommon among adolescent athletes, and apophyseal stress fractures in this region are particularly rare.Case PresentationWe report a rare case of an apophyseal stress fracture of the first thoracic (T1) spinous process in a 13-year-old elite baseball pitcher. The patient presented with focal upper thoracic pain without any history of trauma. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed an open apophyseal physis with cortical irregularity and bone marrow edema at the T1 spinous process, consistent with an apophyseal stress fracture. The patient was managed nonoperatively with upper body rest, followed by a gradual return-to-throwing program. However, at the 7-month follow-up, CT showed persistent apophyseal fragmentation without osseous union, despite the patient being clinically asymptomatic.ConclusionThis case highlights the value of MRI for early detection of thoracic apophyseal stress fractures in adolescent athletes. Importantly, it demonstrates that clinical recovery and return-to-sport can occur even in the absence of radiologic union.Keywords: apophyseal injury, thoracic spine, athlete, conservative treatment

Introduction

Avulsion-type fractures of the lower cervical and upper thoracic spinous processes, commonly referred to as clay shoveler’s fractures, are reported predominantly in adults and are typically caused by acute trauma or sudden traction forces.1,2 In adolescents, the presence of an open apophyseal growth plate at the tip of the spinous process creates a specific structural vulnerability. As a result, repetitive submaximal loading may lead to stress-related apophyseal injury rather than an acute avulsion.3–5

This case report describes a 13-year-old elite pitcher and highlights both the diagnostic considerations and the nonoperative management approach for this rare injury.

Case Presentation

A 13-year-old right-handed elite male baseball pitcher presented with localized upper thoracic pain that worsened with pitching during the competitive season. He first noted discomfort during a practice session while flexing his neck, and the pain intensified after he pitched four innings the following day. He reported no history of acute trauma, collisions, or weight training.

At presentation, the patient’s height and weight were 178 cm and 65 kg, respectively. Physical examination revealed focal tenderness over the spinous process of the first thoracic vertebra (T1) (Figure 1). Cervical flexion and left rotation reproduced the pain, whereas extension and right rotation did not. Neurological examination findings were normal. His medical history was notable for a prior right first rib stress fracture and lumbar spondylolysis.

Physical examination revealed focal tenderness over the T1 spinous process.

Clinical assessment demonstrated limited internal rotation of the lead hip, reduced trunk lateral flexion at maximal shoulder external rotation, and excessive ipsilateral cervical motion during the follow-through phase. Plain cervical radiographs were unremarkable (Figure 2). Computed tomography (CT) demonstrated an open apophyseal physis with cortical irregularity at the tip of the T1 spinous process (Figure 3). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed hyperintensity in the apophyseal region on short tau inversion recovery sequences (Figure 4), consistent with bone marrow edema.

Cervical radiographs (anteroposterior and lateral views) were unremarkable.

CT (sagittal and axial images) demonstrated an open apophyseal physis with cortical irregularity at the tip of the T1 spinous process (arrow).

MRI (sagittal and axial images) showed hyperintensity in short tau inversion recovery at the apophyseal region (arrow).

Based on the clinical history and imaging findings, the injury was considered stress related rather than resulting from acute trauma. Nonoperative management was instituted, consisting of a 5-week period of upper body rest followed by a rehabilitation program targeting trunk control, hip mobility, and correction of pitching mechanics. The patient subsequently commenced a gradual throwing program. Follow-up MRI at 7 weeks (Figure 5) demonstrated persistent bone marrow edema; however, the patient reported substantial symptomatic improvement. Return to full competitive pitching was achieved approximately 12 weeks after symptom onset. Follow-up CT at 7 months (Figure 6) demonstrated persistent apophyseal fragmentation without osseous union, although the patient remained asymptomatic.

MRI (sagittal and axial images) showed persistent bone marrow edema of T1 (arrow) after 7 weeks.

Follow-up CT (sagittal and axial images) obtained 4 months after the patient returned to competitive pitching, showing a persistent apophyseal fragment without osseous union (arrow).

Discussion

In skeletally immature individuals, the spinous process apophysis represents a structural vulnerability to repetitive traction forces during overhead throwing.3,5 Eccentric contractions of the trapezius, rhomboid, and spinal extensor muscles can impose cumulative stress on the T1 spinous process.6 Limited hip internal rotation and reduced trunk mobility may have increased compensatory cervical loading during pitching, with excessive cervical motion required to maintain a high release point. The patient’s history of prior overuse-related skeletal injuries further suggests cumulative mechanical stress.6,7

Despite persistent apophyseal fragmentation on follow-up CT, the patient achieved symptomatic resolution and returned to high-level pitching. Similar discrepancies between radiologic findings and clinical presentation have been reported in apophyseal conditions such as Osgood–Schlatter disease, in which symptoms may resolve despite persistent fragmentation. Symptom resolution in such cases may result from subsiding inflammation and fibrous stabilization.8 This principle likely applies to the present injury.

In symptomatic nonunion, pain has been attributed to fragment micromotion, repetitive muscular traction, and sustained local inflammation. Conversely, early unloading may reduce fragment mobility and promote fibrous stabilization.4,8 MRI facilitates early detection of apophyseal bone marrow edema. Delayed diagnosis may lead to persistent symptoms and progression to symptomatic nonunion, occasionally requiring fragment excision.4 Clinical improvement often precedes radiological resolution and guides return-to-sport.

In the present patient, early rest, mechanical load reduction, and correction of throwing mechanics likely prevented chronic inflammation and facilitated fibrous stabilization. Symptomatic improvement was prioritized over imaging normalization, although long-term monitoring remains warranted.

This case report highlights several important considerations regarding cervicothoracic apophyseal stress injuries in adolescent overhead athletes. Repetitive mechanical loading of a structurally immature apophysis can result in stress-related injuries even in the absence of acute trauma. Clinical recovery may occur despite persistent imaging abnormalities. MRI and CT facilitate early diagnosis, and early reduction of local mechanical stress may help prevent chronic symptoms.

Limitations

This case report has some limitations. Because it describes a single patient, the findings may not be generalizable, and further studies are needed to determine optimal management strategies and return-to-sport criteria. In addition, detailed information regarding pitch volume and training workload was unavailable, limiting evaluation of their contribution to injury development.

Conclusion

This case report describes a rare apophyseal stress fracture of the T1 spinous process in an adolescent baseball pitcher. Conservative management led to full clinical recovery and return to competitive pitching, despite persistent apophyseal fragmentation. Early recognition and reduction of local mechanical load are critical for achieving favorable clinical outcomes.
