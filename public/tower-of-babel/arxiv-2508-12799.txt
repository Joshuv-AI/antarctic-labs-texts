Ensured Energy: A simulation game to elicit preferences around Swiss energy transition pathways
Toby Simpson, Saara Jones, Gracia Brückmann, Walid El-Ajou, Erwan Moreira, Borja Martinez Oltra, Rolf Krause, Michael Multerer, Isabelle Stadelmann

Abstract
The 2015 Paris Agreement on global warming specifies national objectives for the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions. In support of Switzerland’s energy and climate strategy for 2050, researchers investigate scenarios for the transition of energy systems towards a higher share of renewables, assessing their social, environmental and economic impact. Their results guide stakeholders and policy makers in designing resilient and sustainable systems. Political scientists use surveys to quantify public acceptance of energy policy, but the complexity and long time horizon of the subject creates difficulties, both for researchers in posing contextually relevant questions, and for respondents in assimilating enough information to give meaningful answers. A population survey was therefore augmented with an online serious game in which players experience an accurate simulation of current and future energy provision and manage transition towards a sustainable future. This interactive environment allows better informed and engaged decisions, and provides richer information on public opinion. In this paper we motivate and describe the design of the game and report initial findings on player characteristics and engagement. We show that a serious game can successfully attract participants from diverse societal groups and highlight the challenge of balancing complexity and entertainment.

II
Gameplay Design

II-A

Gameplay Objectives

We adhere to the Gameplay/Purpose/Scope (GPS) framework,
which states that serious games have three different purposes: to communicate, to educate, and to collect data
[
36
]
.
Ensured Energy
informs players about the potentials and
limitations of the Swiss energy system and collects their priorities
regarding energy transition through their in-game decisions. The latter is the
most relevant aspect of the game, effectively performing a Multiple-Criteria
Decision Analysis (MCDA)
[
37
]
study with lay subjects. Although the game is not designed as an educational tool in the narrow sense, it contains informative elements and feedback relating to the Swiss energy landscape.

When it comes to data collection, agency and the ability to choose between
different options are crucial in understanding preferences. The fictive point of view of a national energy manager is therefore chosen, allowing players to plan energy transition, manage different resources, and trade-off
outcomes. The gameplay of simulation/management games fits this frame

[
36
]
, although some of the main characteristics of the genre had to be
abandoned in order to give a more realistic context to the game. The traditional
Tabula Rasa or blank slate of simulation games
[
38
]
did not
make sense in a game that replicates the current energy system of Switzerland. The game therefore is initialised with a Swiss map that contains multiple power plants representing the current reality. Players allocate capacity for power generation to nuclear, hydroelectric, solar, wind, biomass, biogas and waste.
Instead of unlimited unused space, as, e.g., in
SimCity

[
39
]
,
construction sites are limited by the geography of Switzerland. Even though the
Swiss map is used as the background of the game interface, the geographical
location of these sites has no impact on the constructed power plants, both as a
simplification and to avoid regional bias. Another necessary change was in resource
management. While there has been a shift in the goals and game loops of
simulation games recently, the most common purpose is still growth (population,
economic, etc.). While there is growth in the model that underlies the game, it leads to increasing energy demand and is portrayed more as a problem to solve that cannot be fully controlled by the player. In
addition, the player has no way to earn money but instead must manage a given
budget.
Several game elements help shape a more realistic political landscape.
Switzerland has decided to opt out of nuclear power
[
40
]
, so there is no possibility
to build new nuclear reactors in the game. Moreover, differing construction times for power plants are accounted for by type, with an appropriate delay between the player’s decision to build and the moment at which a plant contributes to energy supply
[
41
]
.
Similarly, resistance to large solar parks requires the player to implement policies that allow an increased number of upgrades to solar plants in-game.

II-B

Gameplay Overview

During the game, the player controls a simulation of energy transition strategy of
Switzerland over the next 30 years. The game is structured into 10 turns and the player must provide sufficient energy for both summer and winter at each turn in order to move on to the next.
The player has different options to reach the required energy demand:

•

Select a free building area and build new power plants.

•

Increase or decrease the capacity of existing power plants.

•

Import energy from abroad.

•

Implement political policies that reduce demand or help build
power plants more quickly.

•

Hold political campaigns to increase the chances of policies being
accepted.

Each power plant has characteristics such as financial cost per energy output,
construction time and impacts on different factors such as land use, emissions
and the seasonal availability of electricity. Policies have a probability of
acceptance, which can increase through information campaigns or be lowered
according to the player’s choices for energy supply. Throughout the game, the
player must react to various random shocks, see Table
III
, that
impact energy supply and demand, testing the resilience of their strategy.

In the introductory text at the start of the game, the player is assigned one of two objective frames which were linked to the survey: one instructs them to focus solely on maintaining energy supply, while the other emphasizes the need to effectively manage the transition to renewables. Regardless of this context, all players are free to set own goals, as long as there is sufficient energy supplied in each round. Each player is scored according to a set of metrics which are calculated and displayed at the end of the game. Scores are ranked and stored on a leader board that can be ordered by each of the metrics. The game is available in four languages: German, French, Italian and English.

III
Model and Calculation

III-A

Energy Balance

Fig. 3:
Excerpt from
[
43
]
showing a schematic representation of
the energy balance for 2022.

To correctly record decisions, realistic questions must be posed. The combined
models of the SWEET SURE project are a fully-coupled economic, environmental
and social simulation, with detail including international markets, power grid loading, transport infrastructure and demographics, among many
others. The complexity of the existing models presents two challenges to
the design of the game: Firstly, they are too technical to be accessible to
non-specialists. Secondly, the calculation times required are too
long to allow for dynamic gaming interaction. We therefore use a
reduced representation of the energy system that remains accurate with respect
to the quantitative nature of the full model, while providing simplified and
timely interaction for the player.

Underlying the game is an economic and environmental model that takes its
structure from the annual energy balance for Switzerland
[
43
]
. The energy
balance report is part of the official statistics for most countries

[
44
]
and accounts for the flow of energy through the economy. A
schematic view is shown in Figure
3
and a brief description
follows:

1.

Primary energy enters the economy either in a physical form such as fuels
(oil, gas, nuclear) which may be imported, exported, produced domestically,
moved to and from stocks, or drawn directly from the environment (such as
hydroelectric, wind or solar energy).

2.

Transformation converts primary energy in its raw state into other
forms, most importantly electricity. Installations such as nuclear or gas
fuelled power plants, hydroelectric facilities, solar panels or wind turbines
perform these functions. Conversion processes are not completely efficient,
and thus total energy is reduced during transformation.

3.

Delivery of energy in its various forms to final users must then take
place, either via power distribution networks, pipelines, or by transport
of physical fuel. Delivery incurs losses of energy which must also be
accounted for.

4.

Final consumption of energy then takes place, either in its raw or
transformed state by the five main economic sectors: households,
industry, services, transport and agriculture. Excess energy may be stored
or exported, and (most significantly for electricity) any shortfall must
be imported directly.

Thus, all of the energy entering, leaving and transformed by the system is
accounted for. Changing these quantities over time while maintaining the balance
of energy allows the model to describe different scenarios for energy
transition.

III-B

Scenario Construction

The model constructs a complete energy balance for each year from 2022 to 2050,
coinciding with the time period of the game, starting from the energy balance in its current state with forecasts
based on historic data, as described in the next section.

Players can change the allocation of resources to energy conversion processes,
adding capacity by building power plants or reducing it by closing them down.
The energy balance is maintained by the resulting changes to demand for primary
energy and supply of final energy. Similarly, they may change demand for final
energy by enacting policies that subsidise renewable power generation or reduce
consumption. Changes to supply and demand may also arise due to shocks to the
system as described in the previous section.

The calculation of a synthetic energy balance therefore takes place as follows:

1.

Given forecasts for final consumption, the total delivery requirement
for each type of energy is calculated.

2.

Losses associated with storage and delivery of each energy type are
added to give the amounts required after transformation.

3.

The quantities of each energy type made available by the various
transformation processes are deducted from the amounts required.

4.

Any remaining unfulfilled requirement is satisfied by imports
and excesses of supply removed via exports.

The approach reflects and extends the work of

[
45
,
46
]
, providing a complete description of the
Swiss energy balance over time.

III-C

Calibration & Forecasting

In order to reconstruct the energy balance at a future point in time, we require
estimates for levels of production, consumption, energy transformation capacities,
and the efficiencies and losses associated with each process. The model is calibrated to historic energy balance data

[
43
,
47
]
which includes time series for traditional sources since 1980
and renewables since 1990.

Linear regression is performed over 8 years of time series data which provides
a time-averaged value at the start of the simulation and a linear trend for
forecasting. The regression removes some of the effects of the COVID19 pandemic
which caused large distortions to recent data points, mostly due to reduced
industrial activity, travel and tourism. Forecasts are made by extrapolation and
are clamped at zero to avoid negative values for supply and demand. The linear
model was chosen to minimise the number of parameters and to allow consistent
data fitting for all values. Rapid non linear growth in new technologies is
especially hard to forecast, and introducing more parameters adds to this problem.
Capacities for energy transformation are held constant at their current level as
change to future capacities is the key decision for the players of the game.

Historic data are annualised but in reality there are daily, weekly and
annual fluctuations in supply, demand and processing. There is greater demand
for heating in winter and cooling in summer. Solar and run-of-river have more
availability in summer, while wind is greater in winter. Waste incineration and
pump storage are constantly available, while nuclear generation is run down in
summer. Seasonal calibration uses hourly energy market data
[
48
]
over a
four year history. For annual seasonality the data was aggregated by quarter
and the ratio of supply or demand between summer quarters and the whole year
was calculated as a scalar value. Demand for electricity also fluctuates
throughout the day and at weekends
[
44
]
, although this is beyond the
scope of the model.

III-E

Model Implementation

The model is implemented on a web server using the LAMP architecture.
Interaction is via HTML, XMLHTTP or JSON interfaces. Details of the API can be
provided on request. Actions made by players are stored in an SQL database along
with cookies that allow cross-referencing of game instances with survey data.

IV
Integration of Survey and Game

IV-A

Survey Overview

In order to use the game for research purposes, it was
embedded in a larger survey study. The sample for the project was drawn from the
official Swiss population register. The Swiss Federal Statistical Office
provided the contact addresses of 6,000 randomly selected Swiss residents
between the ages of 18 and 75. We have used a stratified sample across the 26
Swiss cantons to account for differences in population size and to ensure
representation from all cantons. Respondents were recruited through a postal
invitation containing a link to an online survey and a personal identification
token. The self-administered survey was prepared in German, French, Italian, and
English using
Qualtrics
software, with essential infrastructure and support provided by the Decision Sciences Laboratory
2
2

2

https://www.descil.ethz.ch/
at ETH Zurich.
All respondents entered the survey and filled out a first survey block assessing pre-treatment variables (e.g., environmental attitudes, perceived efficacy of individuals and the society to deal with the energy transition). After this first survey block respondents were assigned either to treatment or control group. For the assignment, a blocked randomization approach was based on respondents age, gender, language and pre-treatment individual and collective efficacy levels. Blocking ensures that respondents are precisely randomized on the included variables, while maintaining average randomization on all other variables

[
55
]
. The treatment group was then invited to play
the game before continuing with the second part of the survey. The control group directly continued with the second part of the survey. Only after finishing the survey was the control group invited to play the game.

Using a personal identification token, we combined survey responses with information on decisions taken by respondents during the game. Specifically, the game recorded parameter
changes, including adjustments in energy production for different technologies
and changes in various indicators at each round. In addition, the game stored
information about shocks and policies, including their timing, options provided to players, and the responses they chose.

Respondents’ survey answers, including socio-demographic characteristics and attitudes are thus linked to their in-game decisions. We can therefore explore and compare how different groups interact with and respond to
the game environment.

IV-B

Participants and Group Composition

In this subsection, we examine which respondents participated in the game and explore key group differences in socio-demographic variables, and pre-existing attitudes. We thus investigate the effectiveness of the game as an information treatment, but do not intend to draw conclusions about game engagement in general.

Fig. 5:
Rounds played in the game, by random treatment assignment.

A total of 2025 respondents were either assigned to the treatment or the control group. Figure
5
shows the number of rounds played in the game by experimental group. From the figure it is apparent, that while both groups show a similar number of people entering the game, many more respondents in the control group left the game within the first round, conversely more respondents in the treatment group played until the final round.

1758 respondents also completed the second part of the survey, 1007 of whom were in the control group and 751 in the treatment group. For these respondents, survey information about socio-demographic and attitudinal information is available to investigate group composition. To show how engagement with the game is related to respondents’ socio-demographic and attitudinal characteristics, we define “engagement” as playing a minimum of 7 (out of 10) rounds. This number can be regarded as arbitrarily chosen but does not substantially affect the following results.
In fact, Figure

did not enter the game at all, entered but did not even complete
the first round (1) or, in contrast, finished the game (10).

To explore potential associations between game engagement patterns and socio-demographic variables, we consider age, gender, language, and educational attainment levels (see Figure

). Looking at the age distribution of respondents by engagement, the median age for the group of engaged respondents has a lower median age (40 years old) compared to the non-engaged respondents (51 years old). This reflects similar patterns observed in the Canadian context by Yiannakoulias et al.

Regarding gender, we see that respondents identifying as male or non-binary have slightly higher proportions of engagement with the game compared to female respondents. In contrast, there are no distinct patterns based on the language of the respondents. A slightly higher percentage of individuals from tertiary and secondary 1 education levels engaged with the game compared to those from secondary 2.

Fig. 7:
Game engagement by political attitudes.

Figure

depicts game engagement by varying political attitudes. In general, the findings reveal that the game engaged people with varying attitudes to a similar degree. However, it can be observed that participation was slightly higher among respondents with a politically left-leaning political orientation and among respondents with a higher climate concern, as well as among respondents who stated at the beginning of the survey that they consider energy politics a priority issue.

Integrating a serious game into a large-scale population survey proved to be a high-risk high-gain strategy. Previous research suggested that the immersive nature of a serious game could facilitate individual preference formation in the complex energy field, especially for those without strong prior knowledge or motivation to engage with the topic in a more traditional way.
However, from a survey design perspective, it was unclear to what extent a broader population sample would be willing to engage with a serious game within a survey context. In general, this study demonstrated that the approach worked rather well, as only a relatively low number of respondents were deterred from the
survey because of the game treatment. At the same time, an important share of respondents, despite being assigned to the treatment, did not really engage with the game. Moreover, game engagement showed to be systematically dependent on socio-demographic and attitudinal factors. We conclude that the serious game was likely not able to reach and engage those individuals who would profit most from it. Much rather, the capacity to form own preferences on energy transition
pathways and game engagement is likely driven by similar factors.

V
Conclusions

V-A

Review

In this paper, we motivated and described the development of a serious game around the Swiss energy transition and its integration into a large-scale population survey to elicit individual preferences. As the article documents, the game was successfully designed, developed and released as an interdisciplinary collaboration between political science, computational science, and professional game and graphic designs. This interdisciplinarity enabled us to create a game that is as realistic as possible in terms of the pathways for the Swiss energy system towards net zero.

Based on the successful integration of the game into a population survey three important conclusions can be drawn. First, the game "works", i.e, it is in itself engaging and self-explanatory. User comments which were submitted alongside both the the survey and game suggest that the game was evaluated positively by many (interested) players. Second, our analyses of socio-demographic and attitudinal characteristics of the players documents that the game was able to engage clearly beyond the stereotypical group of young males. While men were slightly more likely to engage with the game, the difference is not large. The same is true for education and age. Importantly, also, even though the energy transition is strongly politicized in Switzerland, players with different ideological views engaged with the game in a similar way. Third, and finally, however, one caveat is that individuals less climate concern and those less interested in politics seem difficult to reach.

V-B

Insights for Future Design

Here we return to the original question, can a game be entertaining and informative and still return valid data for scientific analysis? The answer is yes, but our experience shows that the key element in design is to find the balance between the complexity of the information which is presented and the simplicity required for the game to be accessible and enjoyable for the players. While the interdisciplinary work was an important element for the game to become relevant, playable and feasible at the same time, the different disciplinary perspectives also are a challenge in terms of varying interests and expertise.

Moreover, from the initial analysis of the results and from user feedback, it is clear that those interested and invested in the subject matter were happy to engage with the game. What is harder is to engage those who are not naturally drawn to the subject matter or intimidated by its complexity. Given the nature of this journal we would offer this conclusion as advice to designers of future serious games, they should be careful to understand the trade-offs between realism, complexity, accessibility and engagement when considering their objectives, as this will guide the success or failure of their work.

Another conclusion that is difficult to quantify but nonetheless valuable, is the fact that the game was developed for a desktop-only browser environment. It became apparent during the survey that potential players were attempting to access the game using smartphone browsers, but were not able to continue due to the incompatibility of their devices. This represents a loss of engagement and data from a potentially valuable cohort to the study, most notably young people, who could, in turn, be hardest to engage through formal channels. Again we would advise future serious game designers to fully support smartphone users. The size and functionality of a touchscreen user interface thus places constraints on gameplay design which should be carefully considered.
