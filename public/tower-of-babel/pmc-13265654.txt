A Longitudinal Study of Antimicrobial Resistance in Oral Bacterial Isolates From Periodontitis Patients in Taiwan
Authors: Jung-Chang Kung, Chern-Hsiung Lai, Ping-Ho Chen, Ting-Hsun Lan, Chi-Jen Shih, Da-You Yan, Li-Ting Kao, Wei-Te Yang, Bao Shiuan Tseng, Tsung-Ying Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and AimsThe oral cavity harbours a complex microbial ecosystem that plays a critical role in periodontal health and disease. Despite growing concerns over antimicrobial resistance (AMR), long-term surveillance of antibiotic susceptibility in oral bacteria remains limited. This study aimed to assess the temporal trends in AMR among oral bacteria isolated from periodontitis patients in Taiwan over a 14-year period, with emphasis on genus-specific, Gram-specific, and sex-related patterns.MethodsA total of 980 bacterial isolates were obtained from 466 periodontitis patients between 2008 and 2021. E-strip testing was performed for six antibiotics: amoxicillin, ciprofloxacin, clindamycin, metronidazole, minocycline, and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid. Susceptibility data were analysed by year and bacterial genus.ResultsPolymicrobial infections were predominant (98.1%), and resistance rates increased significantly over time, particularly for metronidazole (from 37.8% to 90.9%) and ciprofloxacin (from 33.1% to 51.5%). Streptococcus, Peptostreptococcus, and Capnocytophaga were more prevalent in females, while Neisseria, Fusobacterium, and Pseudomonas were more common in males. High resistance rates were observed in Neisseria, Escherichia, and Staphylococcus, with over 80% of some isolates resistant to multiple agents.ConclusionOur findings reveal a progressive rise in AMR among oral bacteria and highlight the need for localized antibiotic stewardship in dentistry. This is the first long-term oral AMR surveillance study in Taiwan and offers a foundation for updating empirical treatment guidelines and informing public health policy.Clinical RelevanceThis study provides long-term, real-world AMR data for oral bacteria in Taiwan. The findings support more informed antibiotic selection in periodontal therapy and highlight the need to re-evaluate empirical use of metronidazole and fluoroquinolones in routine dental practice.Key words: Oral microbiota, Antimicrobial resistance, Periodontitis, Antibiotic susceptibility, Antibiotic stewardship, Dentistry

Introduction

The human oral cavity harbours one of the most diverse and dynamic microbial ecosystems in the body, with over 700 bacterial species forming complex multispecies biofilms on dental and mucosal surfaces.1 These communities exist in a delicate balance, where commensal bacteria help defend against pathogens. However, when dysbiosis occurs – due to factors such as poor oral hygiene, systemic disease, or antibiotic exposure – pathogenic bacteria proliferate and contribute to chronic conditions such as periodontitis.2,3 Periodontal disease, characterized by inflammation and tissue destruction of the supporting structures of the teeth, affects an estimated 11% of the global population and represents a major cause of tooth loss.4,5

Antimicrobial therapy is often integrated into periodontal treatment, particularly in cases that fail to respond to mechanical debridement alone. Agents such as amoxicillin, metronidazole, clindamycin, and ciprofloxacin are commonly employed to target anaerobic and facultative oral pathogens.6 However, the accelerating global crisis of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses significant challenges to this paradigm. The World Health Organization ranks AMR among the top 10 threats to human health, attributing over 1 million deaths annually to resistant infections, though oral bacterial reservoirs are not always accounted for in national surveillance efforts.7,8 Yet emerging studies indicate that antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) are pervasive across the oral microbiome, with sequenced biofilms harbouring as many as 159 unique ARGs spanning 22 antibiotic classes.9

Despite this, the role of the oral cavity as an AMR reservoir remains understudied. Horizontal gene transfer among oral bacteria, which facilitated by mobile genetic elements, enables the spread of resistance mechanisms not only within commensal flora but also to opportunistic or systemic pathogens.10, 11, 12 Recent metagenomic surveys have revealed that the oral resistome reflects local antibiotic usage, with tetracycline and macrolide resistance genes among the most abundant.13 These findings underscore the importance of examining oral AMR kinetics, especially as global antibiotic stewardship efforts ramp up.

Previous studies on oral antibiotic resistance have largely been limited, focusing on single clinical isolates or short-term surveillance.14, 15, 16 While Porphyromonas gingivalis susceptibility has been evaluated in East Asia with 3 to 5 years of sampling, long-term and multiantibiotic surveillance across multiple bacterial genera remains largely unexplored.14, 15, 16 In this scenario, empiric use of antibiotics like metronidazole or ciprofloxacin may be based on outdated local epidemiology, resulting in reduced therapeutic efficacy and unintended positive selection for resistant strains.

In addition, many existing studies overlook subdominant but clinically relevant oral taxa – such as Capnocytophaga, Campylobacter, Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus, Escherichia, and Prevotella – which have been increasingly identified in periodontal infections and systemic disease.17, 18, 19 These organisms may harbour unique resistance patterns or act as reservoirs of multidrug resistance. Moreover, extraoral pathogens such as Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa are now being recovered from periodontal lesions and peri-implantitis, suggesting transmission between body sites and potential environmental exposure. Therefore, surveillance limited to traditional anaerobic species may vastly underestimate both the complexity and the public health implications of oral AMR.

To this end, we conducted a comprehensive, retrospective surveillance study over 14 years (2008-2021) in Taiwan, with 980 oral isolates from 466 periodontitis patients. The antimicrobial susceptibility of six commonly used antibiotics were examined using E-strip testing across multiple genera. We sought to enhance the rational use of antibiotics in dentistry via constructing the evidence base for oral AMR dynamics over time.

Materials and methods

Study design and inclusion criteria

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kaohsiung Medical University (approval number: KMUHIRB-E(II)-20240235). Clinical specimens were collected from patients diagnosed with periodontitis and analysed at the Anaerobic and Oral Microbiology Research Laboratory of Kaohsiung Medical University. Patients’ electronic records from 2008 to 2021 were reviewed. Inclusion criteria for the samples were as follows: (1) samples were collected from periodontitis-affected teeth with probing pocket depth ≥5 mm; (2) patients had not received systemic antibiotics prior to sampling; (3) patients had not undergone local antibiotic therapy prior to sampling.

Data collection

Patients’ electronic records were retrieved from the laboratory’s database. Information regarding patient age distribution, bacterial species composition, taxonomic identification, and antibiotic resistance profiles was extracted and analysed. All samples included in the analysis were obtained prior to any periodontal treatment intervention, ensuring that no antimicrobial or mechanical disruption occurred before specimen acquisition. Clinical records documented probing depths, prior antibiotic exposure, and the location of the affected teeth.

Sampling protocol

Supragingival plaque was first gently removed using a periodontal scaler, taking care to avoid trauma to the surrounding soft tissues and prevent bleeding. In cases where bleeding occurred, sample collection was delayed until bleeding ceased. The targeted area was irrigated with sterile saline and isolated using cotton rolls placed on the buccal and lingual sides to minimize salivary contamination. The tooth surface was air-dried before a sterile #25 paper point was inserted into the periodontal pocket to the previously measured depth. After remaining in place for 10 seconds, the paper point was immediately transferred into an anaerobic transport vial. Each vial was labelled with the patient’s identifying information, collection date, clinician’s name, and a sample ID. A completed microbiological examination request form accompanied each sample, which was delivered to the laboratory within 24 hours.

Bacterial identification

Samples were cultured under anaerobic conditions for 5 to 7 days. The two most predominant bacterial colonies were selected for further identification, and in cases of unclear distinction, a third colony was included. Colonies were suspended in solution and analysed using the Biolog GEN III Microbial Identification System. A 96-well microplate containing 71 carbon source utilization assays and 23 chemical sensitivity tests was utilized for bacterial identification. The bacterial metabolic activity results in the reduction of a tetrazolium-based redox dye, causing a visible colour change from colourless to purple. The phenotypic fingerprint thus obtained was matched against the Biolog reference database to determine the species of each isolate.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antibiotic susceptibility was assessed using the E-test method. Six antibiotics were evaluated in this study: amoxicillin, ciprofloxacin, clindamycin, minocycline, metronidazole, and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid. For each bacterial isolate, E-test strips containing a predefined gradient of antibiotic concentrations were applied to agar plates inoculated with the target organism. After incubation, the minimum inhibitory concentration was determined based on the intersection point between the bacterial growth inhibition zone and the scale on the E-test strip.

Results

Patient demographics and sample overview

In this 14-year retrospective study, we enrolled 466 patients diagnosed with periodontitis for oral bacterial isolation from periodontal pockets. Among them, 175 were male (37.6%), and 291 were female (62.4%) (Table 1). The age profile was similar across sexes; most patients were between 41 and 60 years old, accounting for 55.4% of males and 54.3% of females. Individuals aged 21 to 40 were the next largest group (25.1% in males vs 30.2% in females), with smaller proportions falling below 20 or above 60 years of age. Polymicrobial infections were nearly universal, observed in 457 out of 466 cases (98.1%) (Table 1), with similar distributions in males (98.3%) and females (97.9%). Only nine patients had monomicrobial infections. 980 bacterial isolates were recovered, with females contributing more isolates (n = 607) than males (n = 373), reflecting the enrolment distribution. Gram-negative bacteria made up 55.1% of isolates overall, and appeared more frequently in males (57.1%) than in females (53.9%).

At the genus level, Streptococcus spp. (19.7%) and Peptostreptococcus spp. (18.1%) were most frequently isolated among Gram-positive species (Table 1). On the other hand, Neisseria spp. (9.9%) and Fusobacterium spp. (9.2%) dominated the Gram-negative group. Some sex-based differences were noted that Streptococcus spp. was more commonly found in females (21.1%, 128/607) than in males (17.4%, 65/373). In contrast, a higher proportion of Peptostreptococcus spp. was isolated in males (18.8%, 70/373) than in females (17.6%, 107/607). Bacteria such as Neisseria spp., Fusobacterium spp., Pseudomonas spp., and Prevotella spp. were more prevalent among males, with proportions of 10.5% (39/373), 10.2 (38/373), 5.9 (22/373), and 6.2% (23/373), respectively. Capnocytophaga spp., Campylobacter spp., Staphylococcus spp., and Escherichia spp. were found in higher ratios in females, with percentages of 8.7% (53/607), 7.6% (46/607), 6.9 (42/607), and 6.1 (37/607), respectively. Enterococcus spp. and Vibrio spp. were only detected in male patients, whereas Burkholderia spp. and Acinetobacter spp. appeared exclusively in females. These patterns may point to subtle, yet clinically relevant, sex-related differences in oral microbiota composition.

Overall and gram-stratified AMR profiles

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed on all 980 isolates using E-strip for six antibiotics. The overall and gram-stratified resistant profiles were listed in Table 2. For amoxicillin, 30.7% (301/980) of isolates were resistant overall. 17.0% (75/440) of Gram-positive and 41.9% (226/540) of Gram-negative isolates were resistant. 40.8% (400/980) of isolates were resistant to ciprofloxacin, with 37.0% (163/440) as Gram-positive and 43.9% (237/540) as Gram-negative. Resistance to clindamycin was observed in 44.3% (434/980) overall, where 34.1% (150/440) were Gram-positive, and 52.6% (284/540) were Gram-negative bacteria. Minocycline resistance was detected in 46.0% (451/980), comprising 40.0% (176/440) of Gram-positive and 50.9% (275/540) of Gram-negative isolates. For metronidazole, the overall resistance rate was 62.1% (609/980), nearly equally distributed between Gram-positive (63.6%, 280/440) and Gram-negative (60.9%, 329/540) groups. Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid showed the lowest resistance rate among the six antibiotics at 21.6% (212/980), with 15.9% (70/440) of Gram-positive and 26.3% (142/540) of Gram-negative isolates being resistant. These data indicate that Gram-negative oral pathogens exhibited higher resistance burdens, especially to amoxicillin and clindamycin. In contrast, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid maintained relatively better activity, particularly against Gram-positive organisms.

Longitudinal analysis of overall AMR trends

To assess changes over time, we analysed nonsusceptibility rates for each antibiotic from 2008 to 2021 (Figure 1). The slope represented the average annual change in nonsusceptibility percentage. A positive slope indicates an increasing resistance trend; a negative slope indicates decreasing resistance. That near-zero suggests no obvious change. Metronidazole had a dramatic rise, with the nonsusceptibility increasing from 37.8% (56/148) in 2008 to 90.9% (30/33) in 2021 (slope = +4.70) (Table S1). Ciprofloxacin rose from 33.1% (49/148) to 51.5% (17/33) (slope = +1.62) over the 14 years we observed. Clindamycin also showed an increase from 21.6% (32/148) to 51.5% (17/33) (slope = +1.24). Amoxicillin increased modestly from 31.1% (46/148) in 2008 to 39.4% (13/33) in 2021 (slope = +1.13). Minocycline showed mostly no change, with nonsusceptible rates at 44.6% (66/148) in 2008 and 51.5% (17/33) in 2021 (slope = −0.08). Notably, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid showed a decline in trend, from 36.5% (54/148) in 2008 to 18.2% (6/33) in 2021 (slope = −1.55), reflecting improved susceptibility or reduced selective pressure. In recent years, especially post-2016, ciprofloxacin, clindamycin, and metronidazole resistance rose noticeably. These findings underscore the dynamic resistance landscape, highlighting the importance of resistance surveillance and the need to reevaluate empirical treatment strategies in dental settings.

Gram-negative AMR trends over time

For Gram-negative isolates in this study (Figure 2A), resistant patterns over time revealed marked upward trends for five antibiotics. The slope represents the rate of change in nonsusceptibility between 2008 and 2021 (Table S2), with positive values indicating increasing resistance. Ciprofloxacin nonsusceptibility rose from 29.7% (19/64) in 2008 to 78.9% (15/19) in 2021 (slope = +3.96), representing the most prominent increase among Gram-negative pathogens. Similarly, clindamycin nonsusceptibility increased from 26.6% (17/64) to 78.9% (15/19) over the same period (slope = +3.05). Different from that of the overall trend, minocycline resistance of Gram-negative rose from 37.5% (24/64) to 79.0% (15/19), with a slope of +2.00. Metronidazole showed a comparable increase, from 42.2% (27/64) in 2008 to 84.2% (16/19) in 2021 (slope = +2.33). Amoxicillin resistance also trended upward, increasing from 39.1% (25/64) to 63.2% (12/19) (slope = +2.11). In contrast, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid was the only antibiotic to show a downward trend, with nonsusceptibility decreasing from 43.8% (28/64) to 26.4% (5/19), corresponding to a slope of −1.77. To sum up, resistance to ciprofloxacin and clindamycin increased sharply in the latter half of the study period, and even agents traditionally used against anaerobes, such as metronidazole, showed diminished effectiveness. These data suggest substantial shifts in Gram-negative oral microbiota resistance profiles over the past decade.

Gram-positive AMR trends over time

Variable nonsusceptibility trends were found for Gram-positive isolates over the 14 years we observed (Figure 2B). As in the previous sections, the slope reflects the change in resistance rate between 2008 and 2021 (Table S3). Metronidazole showed the most striking rise in resistance, increasing from 34.5% (29/84) in 2008 to 100% (14/14) in 2021, with a slope of +7.13. In contrast to those in Gram-negative isolates, four antibiotics exhibited decreasing resistance rates over the same period. Minocycline resistance declined from 50.0% (42/84) to 14.3% (2/14) (slope = −2.94), suggesting a notable recovery in susceptibility. Ciprofloxacin nonsusceptibility fell from 35.7% (30/84) to 14.3% (2/14) (slope = −1.45), and clindamycin from 17.9% (15/84) to 14.3% (2/14) (slope = −1.27). Similarly, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid resistance decreased from 31.0% (26/84) to 7.1% (1/14) (slope = −1.73). Amoxicillin resistance remained relatively stable, decreasing slightly from 25.0% (21/84) to 7.1% (1/14) (slope = −0.48), with minor fluctuations across intermediate years. Collectively, these data suggest that Gram-positive oral bacteria may have experienced a reduction in antibiotic resistance, with the major exception being metronidazole, which reached complete nonsusceptibility in recent years. This divergence emphasizes the importance of stratified resistance surveillance and tailored antibiotic stewardship strategies.

Antimicrobial resistance profiles of eight most commonly isolated genera.

Among Gram-positive genera, Streptococcus spp. (N = 193) displayed high susceptibility to amoxicillin (95.3%) and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (89.6%) (Table 3). However, resistance to ciprofloxacin and minocycline was observed in 38.3% and 46.1% of isolates, respectively. Notably, 36.8% were resistant to clindamycin, and 63.7% to metronidazole, indicating that traditional anaerobic coverage may be insufficient for a substantial subset of Streptococcus isolates. Peptostreptococcus spp. (N = 177) showed a similarly high susceptibility to amoxicillin (88.7%) and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (87.0%), but had slightly lower susceptibility to clindamycin (76.8%) and ciprofloxacin (72.9%). Resistance to metronidazole was also substantial, with 61.6% of isolates classified as resistant, reinforcing the need to reassess its empirical use against Gram-positive anaerobes. In contrast, Staphylococcus spp. (N = 62) presented a markedly different resistance profile. Only 30.6% of isolates were susceptible to amoxicillin, and over half demonstrated resistance to all other tested agents: 56.5% to ciprofloxacin, clindamycin, and metronidazole; 54.8% to minocycline; and 41.9% to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid. These findings align with Staphylococcus’ known capacity for multidrug resistance and may reflect the selective pressure exerted by frequent systemic antibiotic exposure or biofilm-associated resistance phenotypes within periodontal niches.

Among Gram-negative bacteria, Neisseria spp. (N = 97) showed notable resistance across nearly all agents (Table 3). While 49.5% of isolates remained susceptible to amoxicillin, equal numbers were resistant. Ciprofloxacin and minocycline resistance rates exceeded 60%, and clindamycin resistance reached 80.4%. Resistance to metronidazole was highest at 82.5%, suggesting poor coverage by conventional anaerobic therapies. These results confirm the role of Neisseria as a key resistance reservoir and highlight their potential for horizontal resistance gene dissemination within oral microbiota. Fusobacterium spp. (N = 90), traditionally considered susceptible to metronidazole, showed a surprising 24.4% resistance rate, though 75.6% of isolates remained sensitive. Susceptibility rates were highest for amoxicillin (84.4%), ciprofloxacin (77.8%), clindamycin (78.9%), and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (83.3%), suggesting that empirical regimens containing these agents may still retain efficacy. However, the presence of multidrug-resistant isolates (eg, those resistant to all tested agents) cannot be ruled out and warrants close surveillance. Capnocytophaga spp. (N = 81) demonstrated moderate susceptibility patterns: 77.8% were sensitive to amoxicillin, 53.1% to ciprofloxacin, and 60.5% to clindamycin. Resistance to metronidazole was present in 56.8% of isolates, and only 46.9% were susceptible to minocycline. Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid appeared most effective, with 80.2% sensitivity. The overall profile suggests heterogeneity in resistance mechanisms among this genus and variable reliability of first-line antibiotics.

Campylobacter spp. (N = 68) showed variable susceptibility: 75% to amoxicillin, 61.8% to clindamycin, and 82.4% to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (Table 3). However, ciprofloxacin and minocycline resistance rates exceeded 50%, and metronidazole resistance reached 52.9%, suggesting limitations of these agents in empirical therapy. These findings are consistent with regional reports of fluoroquinolone resistance in Campylobacter and may relate to widespread use of these agents in both human and veterinary medicine.

The resistance profile of Escherichia spp. (N = 59) was strikingly high (Table 3). Only 16.9% of isolates were susceptible to amoxicillin, and >70% were resistant to ciprofloxacin (69.5%), clindamycin (94.9%), minocycline (74.6%), and metronidazole (98.3%). Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid susceptibility was relatively higher (54.2%), but still insufficient to suggest reliable empirical coverage. This pattern likely reflects extended-spectrum beta-lactamase activity or other multidrug resistance mechanisms and underscores the importance of targeted therapy when E. coli is isolated from oral samples.

Overall, this genus-level analysis underscores significant variability in resistance profiles across oral bacterial species. While some genera, such as Streptococcus and Peptostreptococcus, retain relatively high susceptibility to beta-lactams and macrolides, others, like Neisseria and Escherichia, demonstrate high levels of multidrug resistance. Empirical therapy, especially for severe or refractory infections, should be guided by local susceptibility trends and adjusted based on species identification wherever feasible. The high resistance rates to metronidazole across most genera also cast doubt on its continued utility as a first-line treatment for anaerobic dental infections, warranting reconsideration in clinical practice guidelines.

Discussion

The prevalence of AMR in oral bacteria should be interpreted within the broader context of global AMR trends across human microbiota. Within this context, our 14-year retrospective surveillance of AMR patterns in oral bacteria isolated from patients with periodontitis provides longitudinal data on microbial trends in the Taiwanese population. The study incorporates a relatively large sample size (466 patients, 980 isolates), spans an extended time period, includes multiple genera of oral pathogens, and considers sex-associated microbial patterns. These findings offer clinically relevant observations with potential public health implications, while contributing to the limited body of region-specific data in the literature.

The data confirm that polymicrobial infections dominated periodontal pockets, with 98.1% of cases involving multiple bacterial species. This observation aligns with current understanding of periodontal disease as a polymicrobial infection involving anaerobic and facultative anaerobic species interacting in biofilms.20 Importantly, our analysis revealed a number of sex-specific microbial trends: Streptococcus, Peptostreptococcus, and Capnocytophaga were more commonly isolated from female patients, while Neisseria, Fusobacterium, and Pseudomonas were more prevalent in males. These patterns are consistent with previous findings, which reported that sex hormones and host immune responses can influence the composition of the oral microbiome.21, 22, 23 For instance, higher estrogen levels in females have been associated with increased colonization of Gram-positive bacteria such as Streptococcus spp.,24, 25, 26 whereas testosterone has been linked to enhanced colonization by Gram-negative anaerobes. Understanding these differences may support the development of sex-specific risk profiles and guide empiric antibiotic selection.

In a previous study, Almeida et al27 collected saliva and subgingival plaque samples from 110 participants (22.8% healthy, 55.4% with gingivitis, and 21.8% with periodontitis) and analysed bacterial diversity as well as the distribution of AMR genes. More than 70% of the samples harboured at least one ARG, with erm genes being the most prevalent (58.2%), followed by blaTEM, mecA, and aac(6′). These findings suggest that oral microbial communities constitute a widespread reservoir of AMR, and notably, that resistance genes are present in the oral cavities of individuals regardless of their oral health status. Our findings suggest an increasing trend in nonsusceptibility across most antibiotic classes. Notably, resistance to metronidazole increased from 37.8% to 90.9%, and ciprofloxacin resistance from 33.1% to 51.5% over the surveillance period. These findings mirror global reports of escalating AMR among anaerobes and facultative anaerobes in dental infections.14,28 The detection of multidrug-resistant Streptococcus species warrants close monitoring, especially in light of the growing global prevalence of resistance genes such as ermB and tetM.29,30 Peptostreptococcus spp., although generally susceptible to clindamycin and metronidazole, demonstrated 23.2% and 61.6% resistance rates, respectively. This represents a significant increase compared to earlier literature,31,32 suggesting that resistance among this genus is emerging locally. Similarly, Neisseria spp., traditionally considered nonpathogenic, exhibited resistance rates of 67.0% to ciprofloxacin and 82.5% to metronidazole. As Neisseria species are known reservoirs of AMR genes, their increasing resistance is concerning due to their potential role in horizontal gene transfer.33,34 Contrary to expectations of universal susceptibility, 24.4% of Fusobacterium spp. isolates were resistant to metronidazole. This correlates with recent studies indicating the acquisition of nim genes and other metronidazole resistance mechanisms among anaerobes.35,36 These findings suggest a shifting resistance landscape in oral anaerobes and call into question the continued empirical use of metronidazole and clindamycin in periodontal therapy.

Among facultative Gram-negative bacilli, Escherichia spp. and Pseudomonas spp. demonstrated high levels of resistance to clindamycin and metronidazole exceeded 90% in both groups, with amoxicillin susceptibility rates of only 16.9% and 28.3%, respectively. These patterns may attribute to the extended-spectrum beta-lactamase activity and efflux mechanisms common in nosocomial strains.37,38 Although their role in the oral cavity is debated, their high resistance potential warrants attention, particularly in refractory or opportunistic infections. Lastly, Prevotella spp. (N = 50), an established periodontal pathogen, showed substantial resistance to clindamycin (56%) and metronidazole (62%), aligning with recent observations that Prevotella increasingly harbours nim and ermF genes linked to nitroimidazole and macrolide resistance, respectively.39 These data collectively support the emerging view that formerly low-risk oral microbes are evolving towards clinically relevant resistance profiles.

Additional genera of clinical importance were also assessed in this study, many of which have received comparatively less attention in prior oral AMR surveillance literature. Capnocytophaga spp. (N = 81), a facultative anaerobe implicated in periodontitis and systemic infections in immunocompromised hosts, displayed moderate susceptibility to amoxicillin (77.8%) and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (80.2%), but over half of isolates were resistant to ciprofloxacin, minocycline, and metronidazole. These findings reflect recent reports of beta-lactamase–producing Capnocytophaga strains in dental and medical settings.40,41 Campylobacter spp. (N = 68), though more commonly associated with gastrointestinal disease, are also isolated from subgingival plaque; over 50% resistance to ciprofloxacin and metronidazole in our data mirrors patterns seen in global enteric isolates and raises concern for cross-domain resistance selection.42,43 Our surveillance period was significantly longer than that of most comparable studies, which were typically limited to 3 to 5 years,14, 15, 16 yet few studies have integrated resistance analysis across such a wide array of genera and antibiotics. Our integrative and long-term study design allowed to detect antibiotic resistance trends over time among different genera of oral pathogens. In this study, we observed that Gram-negative isolates generally exhibited greater resistance than Gram-positives, especially to ciprofloxacin, clindamycin, and amoxicillin. These findings are consistent with known differences in membrane permeability and efflux mechanisms between Gram classes.38,44

This study has two major limitations, including the lack of genotypic analysis of resistance genes and the absence of a healthy control group to account for baseline resistance gene distribution. In addition, due to the retrospective nature of the study and the lack of systematic cryopreservation, the isolates could not be reanalysed for additional antimicrobial susceptibility testing and genotyping. Future studies incorporating molecular resistance profiling (eg, erm, tet, nim, gyr) and longitudinal tracking of resistance dynamics using next-generation sequencing are warranted. Such studies should include both healthy and diseased cohorts to better delineate the role of periodontal status in shaping the oral resistome. Despite these limitations, this study provides long-term surveillance data on oral bacterial resistance trends in Taiwan. The high rates of resistance to metronidazole and ciprofloxacin, the emergence of multidrug-resistant anaerobes, and the observed sex-associated microbial patterns highlight potential challenges in periodontal therapy. These findings may inform clinical decision-making and public health strategies, underscoring the importance of localized, evidence-based approaches to antibiotic use in dentistry.
