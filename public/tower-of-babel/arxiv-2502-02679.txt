Networks with Finite VC Dimension: Pro and Contra
Věra Kůrková, Marcello Sanguineti

Abstract
Neural networks are expected both to learn from data and to approximate task-relevant functions with sufficient accuracy. While finite Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) dimension provides a classical guarantee of learnability through polynomial growth of the hypothesis class and uniform convergence of empirical errors, its implications for approximation accuracy have not been systematically examined. We develop a unifying probabilistic framework in which the relevance of functions to a given class of tasks is encoded by a probabilistic prior. Rather than studying worst-case bounds, we investigate how the distribution of approximation errors depends on the growth of sets of network input-output functions. Our results reveal a fundamental asymmetry: polynomial growth ensures uniform convergence of empirical errors, and hence learnability, but can cause approximation errors to concentrate around their mean that may be large. This mean is largest under a uniform prior, demonstrating the practical usefulness of uniform approximation theorems. It is shown that good approximation accuracy depends critically on the task distribution being aligned with the network’s input-output function class. We further discuss the influence of network depth and the number of units on both the learnability and approximation accuracy of ReLU networks.

1
Introduction

For the practical performance of neural networks, it is desirable not only that they
learn from samples of data
, but also that they
compute with sufficient accuracy
the tasks relevant to a given application domain. The classical guarantee of learnability
Vapnik and Chervonenkis (1971)
is the finiteness of the Vapnik-Cervonenkis (VC) dimension of a hypothesis class. Finite VC dimension limits the growth function of the class (e.g., the set of input-output functions realizable by a network), causing its growth to change from exponential to polynomial and thereby yielding uniform convergence of empirical errors. The approximation capabilities of neural networks, in contrast, have traditionally been studied through rates of decrease of approximation errors with growth of network complexity, characterized by network width and/or depth. Typically, approximation accuracy is quantified in terms of worst-case errors over classes of functions defined by norm constraints that impose various forms of smoothness, such as Sobolev
Yarotsky and Zhevnerchuk (2020)
and variational norms
Barron (1993)
;
Kůrková (2012)
.

In this paper, we introduce a unified framework for studying learnability and approximation accuracy. As a
prior defining the classes of functions to be approximated
, we consider the relevance of functions, represented by probabilities that reflect their likelihood of occurrence within a given task. Rather than focusing on worst-case approximation errors, we study
distributions of approximation errors
and investigate how these distributions depend on network complexity, measured by the
growth function of the sets of input-output functions
realizable by the network.

Because contemporary neural networks are routinely applied to large datasets, we study learnability and function approximation in a high-dimensional setting. We show that the polynomial growth of the sets of input-output functions that guarantees learnability also makes these sets “geometrically thin” within the space of binary classifiers, which can, in turn, limit the approximation capacity of the network.

To formalize our approach, we represent classifiers as vectors in spaces whose dimension is equal to the size of the dataset, which is typically very large, and exploit properties of high-dimensional geometry. In particular, we use the concentration-of-measure phenomenon
Milman and Schechtman (1986)
;
Ledoux (2001)
;
Dubhashi and Panconesi (2009)
, according to which sufficiently smooth functions of many random variables concentrate sharply around their mean values. We show that both the approximation-error and empirical-error functionals satisfy an appropriate smoothness condition, which can be interpreted as a coordinate-wise Lipschitz property. This allows us to apply McDiarmid’s bounded-differences concentration inequality
McDiarmid (1989)
. Combining the resulting concentration bounds for the two error functionals with the polynomial growth of the sets of network input-output functions, we establish their almost-deterministic behavior in high dimensions.

For the concentration of approximation errors, it is sufficient to assume independence of the random variables induced by a function drawn from the probabilistic prior. For learnability, however, a stronger i.i.d. assumption is required: convergence of the empirical error to the theoretical error relies on all random variables having the same mean value.

Our results highlight a fundamental difference between the roles played by finite VC dimension in statistical learning theory and in neural-network approximation theory. For empirical errors, finite VC dimension ensures uniform convergence and hence supports learnability. For approximation errors, by contrast, it leads to concentration around potentially large values unless the network contains input-output functions that are strongly correlated with the underlying probability distribution. Thus, learnability and approximation accuracy impose distinct requirements, even though both are governed by the same growth properties of the underlying hypothesis class.

The resulting estimates of approximation errors show that the polynomial growth of the sets of input-output functions associated with networks of finite VC dimension is insufficient to compensate for the exponentially decreasing volume of their neighborhoods in high-dimensional spaces. In particular, in the absence of prior knowledge, when a uniform distribution over functions is a natural assumption, approximation errors concentrate around relatively large values. This reveals practical limitations on the expressive power of neural networks. Although the universal approximation property guarantees that sufficiently large networks can approximate arbitrary functions, achieving good accuracy for almost all functions requires a high VC dimension and, consequently, high model complexity. Conversely, our results show that when the data distribution is highly non-uniform, relatively small neural networks may achieve good approximation accuracy.

Our proofs rely on geometric properties of high-dimensional spaces, often referred to as the “blessing of dimensionality”
Donoho (2000)
;
Gorban and Tyukin (2018)
;
Gorban et al. (2019)
;
Kůrková (2019)
. These include the exponential growth of quasi-orthogonal dimension
Kainen and Kůrková (1993)
, concentration of volume and surface measure in high-dimensional spheres
Ball (1997)
, and dimensionality reduction via random projections
Johnson and Lindenstrauss (1984)
. Related applications of these phenomena in neurocomputing address highly variable functions
Kůrková and Sanguineti (2016)
, robustness, and the intrinsic dimensionality of data
Sutton et al. (2023)
;
Bastounis et al. (2023)
. Applying these properties to both the empirical error and approximation error functional implies concentration of empirical errors around the theoretical error and hence almost deterministic behavior of approximation errors.

The dependence of learnability and the distribution of approximation errors on network architecture is indirect, mediated by the effect of architecture on the network’s growth function. We investigate this dependence for the currently popular class of deep ReLU networks, using recent estimates of their VC dimension
Bartlett et al. (2019)
;
Bartlett et al. (2021)
. Some preliminary results were presented in the conference proceedings
Kůrková (2023)
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the basic concepts and notation. Section
3
develops a probabilistic framework for function approximation and establishes smoothness conditions that guarantee concentration of both approximation and empirical errors. Section
4
presents the main results on accuracy and consistency. Section
5
combines probabilistic bounds with estimates of growth functions for deep ReLU networks and derives implications for their suitability in tasks governed by probability distributions. Finally, Section
6
summarizes and discusses the main findings.

3
Probabilistic framework for learnability and function approximation

We study approximation and learning of binary-valued functions on finite sets of data
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
that are constrained by a probabilistic prior that models the likelihood of a function occurring in a given type of application. We assume there is a
probability distribution

{{\rm P}}
on
{\mathcal{B}}(X)
, according to which functions to be computed are chosen.
We consider the case when
{\rm P}
can be expressed as the product

{\rm P}(f):=\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\rm P}_{i}(f(x_{i}))=\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\rm P}(f(x_{i})|x_{i}),

which implies that the random variables
f(x_{1}),\dots,f(x_{m})
are
independent
. Unless specified otherwise, we do not assume that they are identically distributed.

Further, we assume that in addition to the probability distribution
{\rm P}
on
{\mathcal{B}}(X)
, there is a probability
{\rm P}_{X}
on
X
. The
training samples
of
n
pairs of input-output data (where
n
is typically much smaller than
m
)
are drawn from the probability
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
defined in terms of both
{\rm P}
and
{\rm P}_{X}
as

{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(S):=\prod_{j=1}^{n}{\rm P}_{X}(x_{i_{j}}){\rm P}(y_{i_{j}}|x_{i_{j}}).

For any hypothesis set
{\mathcal{H}}(X)
and a
loss function

\ell:X\times\{-1,1\}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+},

we denote by
{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell,h,n}:(X\times\{-1,1\})^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
the
empirical error functional

defined for a sample
S=((x_{i_{1}},y_{i_{1}}),\dots,(x_{i_{n}},y_{i_{n}}))
as

{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell,h,n}(S):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\ell\bigl(y_{i_{j}},h(x_{i_{j}})\bigr).

We denote by
\eta_{h}:{\mathcal{B}}(X)\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
the
square of the
l_{2}
-distance of
h^{\circ}
from normalized functions from
{\mathcal{B}}(X)

\eta_{h}(f):=\|f^{\circ}-h^{\circ}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\|f-h\|_{2}^{2}.

We study
\eta_{h}
as a function of random variables
f(x_{1}),\dots,f(x_{m})
drawn from
\{-1,1\}
according to
{{\rm P}(f)}
and the functional
{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell,n,h}
as a function of random variables
(x_{i_{1}},y_{i_{1}}),\dots,(x_{i_{n}},y_{i_{n}})
drawn from
(X\times\{-1,1\})
according to
{{\mathcal{P}}}_{n}(S)
.

We employ a concentration inequality that holds for functions of independent random variables satisfying a smoothness assumption, which can be viewed as a coordinate-wise Lipschitz property.
A function

\phi:A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies the
bounded differences (BD) condition
with the parameter vector

c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{m})
if for all
i=1,\dots,m
and all vectors
a,a^{\prime}\in A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{m}
that differ only in the
i
-th coordinate,

|\phi(a)-\phi(a^{\prime})|\leq c_{i}.

(1)

Note that with sufficiently large parameters, the BD condition holds for every bounded function. The following theorem by McDiarmid
(
Dubhashi and Panconesi, 2009
, p.70)
,
McDiarmid (1989)
shows that when the coefficients are small enough, then the BD condition implies concentration of function values around their mean value.

Theorem 1

(McDiarmid Bound)
.

Let
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
\phi:\prod_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies the bounded differences condition with the vector of parameters
c:=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})
,
{\mathcal{P}}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\mathcal{P}}_{i}
a probability on
\prod_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}
, and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}
be independent random variables with values in ranges
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
, drawn according to
{\mathcal{P}}_{i}
, resp. Then for every
\lambda>0
,

{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl[\,\Bigl|\phi(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})-{\rm E}(\phi)\Bigr|>\lambda\Bigr]\leq e^{-2\lambda^{2}/\|c\|_{2}^{2}}\,.

(2)

When
\|c\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{s}{,m}
for
c
constant, and
\lambda
is fixed, then the probability that values of
\phi
are close to
{\rm E}(\phi)
converges with
m
increasing quickly to
1
. Thus, the behavior of
\phi
is almost deterministic.

To apply the McDiarmid bound to the functionals
\eta_{h}
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell,h,n}
, we first explore parameters for which they satisfy the BD condition.

Proposition 2
.

Let
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
h\in{\mathcal{B}}(X)
,
\ell:\{-1,1\}\times\{-1,1\}\to[0,b]
be a loss function.

(i)
\eta_{h}:{\mathcal{B}}(X)\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
satisfies the BD condition with the parameter vector
c
with all
c_{i}=\frac{4}{m}
;

(ii)
{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell,h,n}:(X\times\{-1,1\})^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
satisfies the BD condition with the parameter vector
c
with all
c_{i}=\frac{2b}{n}
.

Proof.
Without loss of generality, we assume that in both cases the coordinate to be changed is the first one.

(i) Let
f(x_{1})=y_{1}
and
f^{\prime}(x_{1})={y_{1}}^{\prime}
and
f(x_{i})=f^{\prime}(x_{i})=y_{i}
,
i=2,\dots,m
. Then we have

\frac{1}{m}\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(f(x_{i})-h(x_{i})\bigr)^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(f^{\prime}(x_{i})-h(x_{i})\bigr)^{2}\Bigr|=\frac{1}{m}\Bigl|\bigl(y_{1}-h(x_{1})\bigr)^{2}-\bigl({y_{1}}^{\prime}-h(x_{1})\bigr)^{2}\Bigr|\leq\frac{4}{m}
.

(ii)

\frac{1}{n}\Bigl|\,\ell(y_{1},h(x_{1}))+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\ell(y_{i},h(x_{i}))-\ell(y^{\prime}_{1},h(x^{\prime}_{1}))-\sum_{i=2}^{n}\ell(y_{i},h(x_{i}))\,\Bigr|=

\frac{1}{n}\Bigl|\ell(y_{1},h(x_{1}))-\ell(y^{\prime}_{1},h(x^{\prime}_{1}))\Bigr|\leq\frac{2b}{n}.

\Box

Proposition
2
shows that for every
h\in{\mathcal{B}}(X)
, the functional
\eta_{h}
satisfies the BD condition with
\|c\|^{2}_{2}=\frac{16}{m}
and the functional
{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell,h,n}
with
\|c\|^{2}_{2}=\frac{4b^{2}}{n}
.
So for a sufficiently large
m
and
n
, resp., these two functionals behave almost deterministically.

4
Accuracy of approximation and learnability

On infinite domains (
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
or its open subsets), I/O functions of neural networks of many types determine network parameters
uniquely up to permutations and sign flips
(see, e.g.,
Sussman (1992)
;
Albertini and Sontag (1993)
;
Kůrková and Kainen (1994)
;
Kůrková and Kainen (1996)
).
On finite domains, neural network function representations are typically highly redundant. In particular, in the case of families of binary-valued functions, infinitely many parameterizations generate only finitely many functions. Vapnik and Chervonenkis
Vapnik and Chervonenkis (1971)
discovered the crucial role of these sizes in learnability from data samples.
For a family of binary-valued functions
{\mathcal{H}}

on any
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the
growth function

\Pi_{{\mathcal{H}}}(m):{\mathbb{N}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{N}}_{+}
is defined as

\Pi_{{\mathcal{H}}}(m):=\max_{V\subset U,{\rm card}V=m}{\rm card}{\mathcal{H}}_{|\,V}.

The next theorem proves that the growth function
\Pi_{{\mathcal{H}}}
also influences
the concentration of errors in the approximation of functions by
{\mathcal{H}}
. For each
h\in{\mathcal{H}}(X)
, let

\mu_{h}={\rm E}(\eta_{h})

denote the
mean value
of
\eta_{h}
with respect to
{\rm P}
and

\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}=\min_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}(X)}\mu_{h}.

Theorem 3
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}\subset{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
{\rm P}
be a product probability distribution on
{\mathcal{B}}(X)
and
{\mathcal{H}}(X)\subset{\mathcal{B}}(X)
. Then for every
\lambda>0

(i)\quad{\rm P}\Bigl[\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}-\lambda\leq\|f^{\circ}-{\mathcal{H}}(X)^{\circ}\|^{2}_{2}\Bigr]>1-\Pi_{{\mathcal{H}}}(m)\,e^{-\frac{m\lambda^{2}}{8}};

(ii)\quad{\rm P}\Bigl[\|f^{\circ}-{\mathcal{H}}(X)^{\circ}\|^{2}_{2}\leq\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}+\lambda\Bigr]>1-e^{-\frac{m\lambda^{2}}{8}}.

Proof.
Combining the McDiarmid bound (Theorem
2
) with Proposition
2
we obtain for every
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
,

{\rm P}\Bigl[\mu_{h}-\lambda\leq\|f^{\circ}-h^{\circ}\|^{2}_{2}\leq\mu_{h}+\lambda\Bigr]>1-e^{-\frac{m\lambda^{2}}{8}}.

(3)

Hence

{\rm P}\Bigl[(\forall h\in{\mathcal{H}}(X))\,\Bigl(\mu_{h}-\lambda\leq\|f^{\circ}-h^{\circ}\|^{2}_{2}\Bigr)\Bigr]>1-{\rm card}{\mathcal{H}}(X)\,e^{-\frac{m\lambda^{2}}{8}}\geq 1-\Pi_{{\mathcal{H}}}(m)\,e^{-\frac{m\lambda^{2}}{8}}.

As
\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}\leq\mu_{h}
for all
h\in{\mathcal{H}}(X)
, (i) holds.
As
{\mathcal{H}}(X)
is finite, for every
f\in{\mathcal{B}}(X)
there exists
h_{f}\in{\mathcal{H}}(X)
for which
\|f-{\mathcal{H}}(X)\|_{2}=\|f-h_{f}\|
.
So (ii) follows.

\Box

To obtain conditions on the concentration of empirical errors applying McDiarmid’s bound, we have to strengthen assumptions. We assume that the probabilities
{\rm P}_{1},\dots,{\rm P}_{m}
on

are not only independent but also identically distributed;
otherwise the mean values would be sample-specific
. We set
{\rm P}_{0}={\rm P}_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,m
, and denote by

{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}(h)={\rm E}(\ell(y,h(x))

the mean value of the functional
\ell(y,h(x))
with respect to the probability

{{\mathcal{P}}}={\rm P}_{X}(x){\rm P}_{0}(y|x).

Theorem 4
.

Let
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
{\rm P}
be a probability distribution on
{\mathcal{B}}(X)
such that
{\rm P}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\rm P}_{i}
where
{\rm P}_{i}
are i.i.d.,

{\mathcal{H}}\subset{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and
\ell:\{-1,1\}\times\{-1,1\}\to[0,b]
be a loss function. Then for every
h\in{\mathcal{H}}(X)
, every positive integer
n\leq m
, every random sample
S=(x_{i_{1}},y_{i_{1}}),\dots,(x_{i_{n}},y_{i_{n}})
, and every
\lambda>0

{{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}\bigl[\,\bigl(\forall h\in{\mathcal{H}}(X)\bigr)\left|\,{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell,h,n,}(S)-{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}(h)\,\bigr|\leq\lambda\,\right]\,>1-\Pi_{{\mathcal{H}}}(n)e^{-\frac{n\lambda^{2}}{2b^{2}}}.

Proof.
Let
U_{n}\subset X
with
{\rm card}\,U_{n}=n
and

S=(\,(x_{i_{1}},y_{i_{1}}),\dots,(x_{i_{n}},y_{i_{n}})\,)
be a sample such that
\{x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{n}}\}=U_{n}
. As the random variables
(x_{i_{j}},y_{i_{j}})
are i.i.d., for every
h\in{\mathcal{B}}(U_{n})
,
we have

{\rm E}\bigl({\mathcal{E}}_{\ell,h,n}(h)\bigr)={\rm E}\bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\ell(y_{j_{i}},h(x_{j_{i}}))\,\bigr)={\rm E}\bigl(\ell(y,h(x)\bigr)={\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}(h),

where the mean value is considered with respect to
{\mathcal{P}}={\rm P}_{X}(x){\rm P}_{0}(y|x)
.
By Theorem
2
,

{\mathcal{P}}\bigl[\,\left|\,{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell,h,n}(S)-{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}(h)\,\bigr|>\lambda\,\right]\,\leq e^{-\frac{n\lambda^{2}}{2b^{2}}}.

As
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(S)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}{\rm P}_{X}(x_{i_{j}})\Bigl({\rm P}_{0}(y_{i_{j}}|x_{i_{j}})\Bigr)^{n}
, we have

{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\bigl[\,(\forall h\in{\mathcal{H}})\left|\,{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell,h,n}(S)-{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}(h)\,\bigr|>\lambda\,\right]\,\leq\Pi_{{\mathcal{H}}}(n)e^{-\frac{n\lambda^{2}}{2b^{2}}}.

\Box

For
\lambda=n^{-1/4}
, Theorems
3
and
4
imply

{\rm P}\Bigl[\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}-n^{-1/4}\leq\|f^{\circ}-{\mathcal{H}}(X)^{\circ}\|^{2}_{2}\Bigr]>1-\Pi_{{\mathcal{H}}}(n)\,e^{-\frac{n^{1/2}}{8}}

(4)

So

{\rm P}\Bigl[\bigl\|f^{\circ}-{\mathcal{H}}(X)^{\circ}\bigr\|^{2}_{2}\leq\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}+n^{-1/4}\Bigr]>1-e^{-\frac{n^{1/2}}{8}}.

(5)

{{\mathcal{P}}}_{n}\bigl[\bigl(\forall h\in{\mathcal{H}}(X)\bigr)\,\left|\,{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell,h,n}(S)-{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}(h)\,\bigr|\leq n^{-1/4}\,\right]\,>1-\Pi_{{\mathcal{H}}}(n)e^{-\frac{n^{1/2}}{2b^{2}}}.

(6)

\Box

Theorem
3
shows that the size of the set
{\mathcal{H}}(X)
strongly influences the concentration of approximation errors around their mean value. When the growth function
\Pi_{{\mathcal{H}}}(m)
does not outweigh the term
e^{-\frac{m\lambda^{2}}{8}}
, the approximation errors with respect to
{\mathcal{H}}
behave almost deterministically. Similarly, Theorem
4
shows that when the growth function
\Pi_{{\mathcal{H}}}(n)
does not outweigh

e^{-\frac{n\lambda^{2}}{2b^{4}}}
, then for all functions
h\in{\mathcal{H}}(X)
and for almost all samples of size
n
, the empirical errors
{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell,h,n}
are close to the theoretical error
{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}(h)
.

The convergence of empirical errors implied by Theorem
4
is a well-known result
Vapnik and Chervonenkis (1971)
in statistical learning theory concerning the consistency of learning from data samples.
Our contribution is
pairing this theorem in a unifying framework with Theorem
3
. Both theorems are based on the same concentration property, but applied to different functionals. The i.i.d. condition in Theorem
4
is needed to guarantee that the mean value is not sample-specific.

In the absence of prior knowledge, we have to assume that all functions in
{\mathcal{B}}(X)
are equally likely to occur in a task, i.e., that
{\rm P}
is uniform. By symmetry, this implies
{\rm E}(\eta_{h})=2
for all
h\in{\mathcal{B}}(X)
, and hence
\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}=2
for any class
{\mathcal{H}}(X)
. Consequently, for any network class with growth function
\Pi_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}
increasing with
{\rm card}X=m
slowler than
e^{\frac{m\lambda^{2}}{8}}
, approximation errors of uniformly chosen functions concentrate around the mean value
\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}=2
. This finding shows a practical limitation of the universal approximation property: unless the size of the function class exceeds

e^{\frac{m{\lambda}^{2}}{8}}
, the majority of functions cannot be approximated with errors smaller than
2-\lambda
(see Figure
2

\lambda=m^{-1/4}

Neural networks with finite VC dimension

The theorems in the previous section imply that both the concentration of approximation errors and the convergence of empirical errors hold for networks whose sets of I/O functions grow polynomially with the size
m
of their domain..

A fundamental result by Vapnik and Chervonenkis
Vapnik and Chervonenkis (1971)
establishes that the growth function of any set
{\mathcal{H}}
is either equal to
2^{m}
for all m, or there exists a finite threshold, called the
VC dimension
, beyond which the growth function is bounded by a polynomial in
m
.

We denote

d_{V}({\mathcal{H}})=\max_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}\{\Pi_{{\mathcal{H}}}(m)=2^{m}\},

if such maximum exists, otherwise
d_{V}({\mathcal{H}})=\infty
.
Vapnik and Chervonenkis
(
Vapnik and Chervonenkis, 1971
, Theorem 1)
proved that for
m>d_{V}

\Pi_{{\mathcal{H}}}(m)\leq m^{d_{V}+1}.

(7)

A slightly better result was obtained independently by Shelah
Shelah (1972)
and Sauer
Sauer (1972)
.

As any class with finite VC dimension has a growth function bounded by a polynomial, Theorem
3
implies that approximation errors of networks whose I/O function classes have finite VC dimension concentrate around their mean values. Moreover, the smaller
\Pi_{{\mathcal{H}}}(m)
, the higher the probability that the approximation accuracy for a randomly drawn function is close to
\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}
.

While consistency in learning from data is a desirable property of a class of neural networks, the concentration of approximation errors near
\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}
may impair approximation capability. If
\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}
is not sufficiently small, then almost all functions drawn according to
{\rm P}
cannot be approximated to a desired accuracy by networks with sets of I/O functions of finite VC dimension. In this case, their squared normalized
l_{2}
errors are at least
\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}-\lambda
.
On the other hand, if
\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}
is sufficiently small, then by the upper bound in Theorem
3
(ii),
almost all functions can be approximated with error at most
\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}+\lambda
using a single function

h^{*}
(i.e., any function for which
\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}=\mu_{h^{*}}
). Thus, network classes with finite VC dimension are not capable of approximating most functions in
{\mathcal{B}}(X)
with high accuracy. Interpreting functions as vectors in
\{-1,1\}^{m}
, this reflects the fact that, in high dimensions, most functions are nearly orthogonal to any hypothesis class
{\mathcal{H}}(X)
whose growth function is only polynomial in
m
.

The problem of estimating the number of binary-valued functions realizable on
m
-point subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
has been studied since the 19th century. Schläfli
Schläfli (1901)
;
Schläfli (1950)
derived an upper bound on the number of linearly separable dichotomies of
m

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. In the language of neural networks, this corresponds to the maximal number of functions that can be implemented by a single perceptron with a Heaviside activation function on such subsets. Later, Cover
Cover (1965)
established function-counting theorems that estimate the number of dichotomies induced by certain nonlinear decision surfaces, including hyperspheres and hypercones.

Bartlett et al.
Bartlett et al. (1998)

W

\Pi_{ReLU(L,W)}(m)\leq\bigl(4eLm\bigr)^{LW}.

Thus, the growth function of deep ReLU networks depends polynomially on the sample size
m
, with degree equal to the product
LW

X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

and
\lambda>0

3

\quad{\rm P}\Bigl[\mu_{ReLU(L,W)(X)}-\lambda\leq\|f^{\circ}-{\mathcal{H}}(X)^{\circ}\|^{2}_{2}\Bigr]>1-\bigl(4eLm\bigr)^{LW}\,e^{-\frac{m\lambda^{2}}{8}}\,

and Theorem

In many practical settings, it is reasonable to assume that both
L
and
W
are much smaller than the cardinality
m
of the domain
X
(since networks with as many layers or parameters as data points would be inefficient). Consequently, for sufficiently large
m
, the approximation errors of random functions by commonly used deep ReLU networks become highly concentrated and therefore behave almost deterministically.
Almost all errors are close to
\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}=\mu_{h^{*}}
. So the network either approximates well almost all relevant functions or almost all functions are far from its set of I/O functions.

Increasing network depth and width may improve approximation accuracy by reducing

\mu_{{\mathcal{H}}(X)}
. However, by the bound (
9

n
to ensure that the exponential term
e^{-\frac{n\lambda^{2}}{2b^{2}}}
dominates the polynomial factor
\bigl(4eLn\bigr)^{LW}
.

Currently, some highly complex deep networks are exploited in applications. Such networks have very large VC dimensions, and so the concentration phenomenon may no longer occur. In such cases, the uniform convergence of empirical errors is not guaranteed. In
Bartlett et al. (2021)
, it was conjectured that such deep models can be decomposed into a high-complexity component that allows for a very good fit to the data, and a low-complexity component for which classical uniform convergence holds. This conjecture appears to be consistent with our results, which guarantee for the low-complexity component classical uniform convergence of empirical errors, together with concentration of approximation errors around a single value, which might be improved in the high-complexity component.

6
Discussion

Our results reveal a fundamental issue: finite VC dimension of a network’s
input-output (I/O) function class guarantees learnability through uniform
convergence of empirical errors, yet it simultaneously may impair approximation
accuracy. The polynomial growth of the set of input-output functions, which is the source of
learnability, also makes the class geometrically sparse in the
high-dimensional space of binary classifiers. Consequently, approximation
errors concentrate around the minimum mean distance
\mu_{\mathcal{H}(X)}
,
becoming almost deterministic as the dataset size
m
increases. When

\mu_{\mathcal{H}(X)}
is large, almost all functions drawn from the task
distribution cannot be accurately approximated, regardless of how the network
is trained.

The behavior is sharply distribution-dependent. Under a uniform prior, nearly
all functions incur large approximation errors, since no single I/O function
lies close to most of the classifier space. Under a non-uniform distribution
concentrated near the function class, however,
\mu_{\mathcal{H}(X)}
can be
small, and a single function
h^{*}
suffices to approximate almost every
task-relevant function accurately (see Figure
3
). This shows that the task
distribution is as decisive as network architecture. By contrast, convergence of empirical errors
is distribution-independent, though it additionally requires i.i.d. data,
whereas our concentration bounds for approximation errors require only
independence.

For deep ReLU networks with
L
layers and
W
parameters, the growth
function satisfies
\Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(m)\leq(4eLm)^{LW}
. Larger depth and
width can reduce
\mu_{\mathcal{H}(X)}
for some distributions, but do
not escape the fundamental issue: approximation errors remain concentrated,
and good performance ultimately depends on aligning the function class with
the task distribution rather than on model size.

Our findings show that learnability and approximation accuracy, although being controlled by the same growth properties of the underlying hypothesis class, impose different requirements. For empirical errors, finite VC dimension ensures uniform convergence and hence supports learnability. For approximation errors, by contrast, it leads to concentration around potentially large values unless the network contains input-output functions that are strongly correlated with the underlying probability distribution.

Our results provide some insight into the recent discussion from
(
Bartlett et al., 2021
, p. 90)
, concerning near-optimal fits of very large deep networks to noisy training data together with excellent generalization performance. In
Bartlett et al. (2021)
, it was conjectured that such models can be decomposed into low-complexity ones, for which uniform convergence of empirical risk works, and high-complexity ones that enable good fit to data. Our analysis provides support to the conjecture by demonstrating the fundamental trade-off between learnability and accuracy. A combination of low- and high-complexity models may be a way to achieve both objectives.

Note that the large VC-dimension of deep ReLU networks is an anomaly with respect to classical machine-learning approaches: for a fixed number of parameters it increases with the number of layers exponentially, and so it becomes very large for commonly-used deep networks, which have hundreds of layers. In addition, an arbitrarily small perturbation of the ReLU nonlinearity may make the VC-dimension of the corresponding class change from a small constant to infinity
(
Bartlett et al., 2021
, Example 2.6)
, and the same phenomenon holds for a slightly perturbed sigmoid nonlinearity
(
Anthony and Bartlett, 1999
, Lemma 7.2)
.

The framework of this paper covers binary-valued functions on finite domains with the normalized
\ell_{2}
metric. Extensions to real-valued outputs and other loss functions – relevant, e.g.,
to neural approximation of differential and integral equations (see
Patawari et al. (2025)
;
Patawari and Das (2026)
and the references therein) –
and to correlated data
Kůrková and Sanguineti (2021)
are directions for future work.
