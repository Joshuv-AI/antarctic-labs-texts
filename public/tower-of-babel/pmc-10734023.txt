Class-III Hydrate Reservoir Depressurization Production Enhancement by Volume Fracturing and Cyclic N2 Stimulation Combination
Authors: Zhizeng Xia, Xuewu Wang, Weiwei Ren
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe depressurization effect is limited to class III hydrate reservoir recovery. To improve the depressurization effect, a new method of volume-fracturing and cyclic N2 stimulation combination (VFCS) was proposed. The production performance of this method was investigated by using numerical models based on reservoir parameters of the SH7 hydrate site in the South China Sea. The results show that (1) VFCS can greatly enhance the production performance with the average CH4 production rate being approximately 2.85 times higher than that of pure depressurization. This method combines the effects of volume fracturing and cyclic N2 stimulation by improving the seepage environment and further reducing the CH4 partial pressure in the gas phase. (2) High reservoir permeability, medium hydrate saturation, large volume-fracturing scale, low bottom-hole pressure, and high N2 injection amount can increase CH4 production by VFCS. (3) Although VFCS has the largest CH4 production volume and the highest hydrate dissociation degree among the studied production strategies, the reservoir temperature drop is significant by VFCS and future studies can be focused on the external heat supply to the reservoir to further improve the production.

Introduction

Natural gas hydrates (mainly methane hydrates, MHs) are crystalline inclusion compounds formed by hydrocarbon molecules and water molecules under low-temperature and high-pressure conditions.1 The MHs are recognized as promising energy resources with great potential because of their wide distribution and substantial reserves. Thus, it is of great significance to effectively recover methane from MHs. According to the geological structure and reservoir conditions, there are three main research types2 (class I–class III) of MH accumulations (referred to as hydrate reservoirs), as shown in Figure 1. The recovery methods of hydrate reservoirs mainly include the depressurization method, thermal stimulation method, chemical injection method, and CO2 replacement method.3

Sketch of the hydrate reservoir types.

From an economic and technical perspective, the depressurization method is recognized as the simplest and most effective method for the large-scale production of hydrate reservoirs and is also the most studied method, especially in recent years. From an experimental aspect, for example, Almenningen et al.4 studied the hydrate dissociation behavior by depressurization using hydrate-filled sandstone cores and a 2D porous micromodel. They found that the hydrate in contact with free gas dissociated earlier than the hydrate surrounded by water and the hydrate started to dissociate at slightly higher pressures than theoretical dissociation values. Yamada et al.5 investigated the sensible heat effect on gas production behavior using MH cores at different initial temperatures and found that increasing the original temperature of the hydrate reservoir was important to enhance the gas production rate during depressurization. Sun et al.6 studied the dissociation behavior of gas-excess and water-excess MHs using a 1D simulator and found that improving the reservoir permeability and suppressing water seepage were quite important during hydrate depressurization production. Liu et al.7 developed an integrated experimental system for gas hydrate drilling and exploitation, and the validity of the experimental system was verified through an experiment on CO2 hydrate production by depressurization. Sun et al.8 experimentally studied three modes of depressurization and water–gas flow on hydrate dissociation and found that a high water–gas flow rate ratio and fast depressurization rate were beneficial for hydrate dissociation.

In addition to experimental studies, many numerical studies are also carried out on hydrate depressurization production. For example, Feng et al.9 studied the gas production from a layered hydrate reservoir with three hydrate-bearing layers (HBLs) by depressurization based on the reservoir parameter of the Eastern Nankai Trough and found that the gas production performance could be enhanced with different well configurations and complex production strategies. Wang et al.10 studied the mass-transfer-limitation effect on MH dissociation inside the pore channels during depressurization with a mathematical model. They found that the mass-transfer-limitation effect could lower cumulative gas production, increase the residual hydrate amount, and inhibit the heat change. Zhou et al.11 studied the mechanical responses of the before-, during-, and after-hydrate dissociation stages during depressurization production and observed volumetric yielding and shear yielding during the production, indicating potential geo-mechanical risks. Liu et al.12 studied continuous and intermittent electrical heating methods to improve gas production by depressurization. They found that electrical heating was beneficial for rapid gas production from hydrate and intermittent heating showed higher electrical energy utilization efficiency than continuous heating. Gu et al.13 studied a method of deep depressurization combined with underburden sealing and found that underburden sealing could enhance the gas production of deep depressurization, and a relatively higher sealing ratio could achieve better effects. Cao et al.14 numerically studied the depressurization potential and geometric parameter effect for the Class I hydrate reservoir by multilateral wells and found that good production performance could be achieved but possible interference between well branches was unfavorable for gas recovery.

At present, the hydrate recovery method used in field production trials worldwide is mainly the depressurization method, as shown in Table 1. For Class I and Class II hydrate reservoirs, the effective pressure reduction of the HBL can be achieved by extracting fluid from the bottom moveable-fluid layer (gas or water layers). However, there exists only a single HBL in the Class III hydrate reservoir, and the effectiveness of simple depressurization is quite limited.

Hydrate Production Trials around the World

Hydraulic volume fracturing can be used in the reconstruction of low-permeability gas or oil reservoirs. By creating a complex fracture network in the formation, the fluid seepage conditions can be significantly improved, and thus the production will increase. This technology has been widely used in the exploitation of tight oil/gas resources. For hydrate reservoirs, the reservoir permeability is generally low because of the existence of hydrates. Experimental studies have been carried out to evaluate the possibility of hydraulic fracturing in hydrate reservoirs. Konno et al.22 investigated the fracturing behaviors in gas-hydrate-bearing sediments through a triaxial pressure cell and observed laminar fractures similar to those in consolidated rock. Too et al.23 examined the susceptibility of fracture creation in synthetic hydrate-bearing sand with high hydrate saturation and observed penny-shaped cracks formed in the sample inside the volume reactor. Liu et al.24 proposed an evaluation model of hydraulic fracturing in HBLs based on representative samples and found that fractures could be created in HBL when the fracability index was higher than 0.72. Wang et al.25 investigated the fracturing behavior of MH-bearing sediments using a triaxial hydraulic fracturing apparatus and found the fracturing initiation pressure was positive with the hydrate saturation and the average gas production rate was 3 times higher after fracturing. As mentioned above, hydraulic fracturing has great potential to improve the depressurization effect of hydrate reservoirs through artificially created fractures in HBLs. Some authors have evaluated hydrate production after reservoir fracturing. Wang et al.26 and Han et al.27 investigated the multifractured well method in developing the gas hydrate reservoir, and they found it had great potential for improving the gas production. Ma et al.28 established a hydraulic fracturing numerical model based on the clayey silt hydrate reservoir property and analyzed the fracture morphology and stress interference during the multicluster fracturing process. They found that fractures tended to propagate to the middle or upper parts of the reservoir without fracture interference, and fracture distribution patterns were different between simultaneous fracturing and sequential fracturing processes. Mao et al.29 investigated the hydrate production by multifractures combined with a multilateral well and found that it could enhance the stimulated reservoir volume and increase the gas production by more than 20 times compared with depressurization alone. Xu et al.30 investigated the influence of complex fractures on gas hydrate production through an embedded discrete fracture model. They found that the main fracture connection to the wellbore and secondary steering of the fractures had a great impact on the production.

Gas stimulation is also an important method to improve the production performance of hydrate reservoirs. CO2/N2 mixed gas injection is currently the most studied injection system. Koh et al.31 injected a CO2/N2 gas mixture into the hydrate core of a 1D 8 m-long high-pressure reactor and concluded that adequate contact time was necessary for the replacement reaction. Li et al.32 investigated the gas production from fracture-filled CH4 hydrate by using pure CO2 and CO2/N2 mixed gas and found that the existence of N2 could promote the dissociation of CH4 hydrate. Tupsakhare and Castaldi33 investigated methane recovery from gas hydrates with a CO2/N2 gas mixture combined with thermal stimulation and found the highest methane recovery using this method. Yasue et al.34 carried out experiments on continuous N2/CO2 gas mixture injection into hydrate-bearing cores with different hydrate saturations and concluded that a higher N2 concentration could enhance CH4 production. In addition, our previous study35 showed that pure N2 injection was more effective than CO2/N2 mixed gas from the perspective of CH4 production.

At present, research on volume fracturing and gas stimulation for hydrate reservoir production is mostly laboratory-scale experiments, and field-scale simulation studies are quite limited. Considering the unsatisfactory effectiveness of Class III hydrate reservoir production by pure depressurization (PD), this paper proposed a new method of volume-fracturing and cyclic N2 stimulation combination (VFCS) to enhance the production performance and comparatively investigated the production characteristics of cyclic N2 stimulation (CS), volume-fractured depressurization (VF), and VFCS. Sensitivity analyses of VFCS were carried out on absolute reservoir permeability, original hydrate saturation, bottom-hole pressure (BHP), volume-fracturing scale, and N2 injection amount. This study can provide a reference for improving the PD production performance of Class III hydrate reservoirs.

Fundamentals

In the simulation study, the production system is composed of three phases: the CH4 hydrate phase (solid phase), water phase (mobile phase), and gas phase (mobile phase). The flows of the water phase and gas phase follow Darcy’s law. The components include CH4·NH2O, CH4, H2O, and N2. N2 hydrate is not considered because it is difficult for N2 to form hydrates under the temperature and pressure conditions of a typical hydrate reservoir (Figure 2). Model Parameters

Reservoir Parameters

A 3D Cartesian grid system was adopted to carry out this research, and a rectangular Class III hydrate reservoir model was established based on the HBL parameters of the SH7 site in the Shenhu area of the South China Sea.49,50 The model consists of an overburden layer, an HBL, and an underburden layer, and their thicknesses were 30, 44, and 30 m, respectively. The size of the model was 150 × 150 × 84 m, and the corresponding gridding number was 51 × 51 × 34. The meshing scheme was determined after conducting sensitivity analysis on the condition of PD production with a BHP of 4 MPa, as shown in Figure 4. When the planar gridding number is higher than 51 × 51, the CH4 production remains almost unchanged, so this value is selected to save computation time.

CH4 production under different meshing schemes.

The Class III hydrate reservoir model was in a closed boundary condition. The absolute reservoir permeability was 75 mD with a reservoir porosity of 0.41. The original saturations of hydrate and water were 0.44 and 0.56, respectively. The original pressure and temperature at the bottom of the HBL were 13.83 MPa and 14.15 °C, with a geothermal gradient of 4.3 °C/100 m and a pressure gradient of 0.98 MPa/100 m, respectively. The irreducible water saturation and gas saturation were 0.29 and 0.09, respectively. When the hydrate saturation was 0.44, the reservoir permeability was 0.6453 mD and the reservoir porosity was 0.23, according to eqs 4 and 5, respectively.

Production Strategies

To investigate the production performance of the new method of volume fracturing and the CS combination, a total of four simulation models were established with production strategies shown in Figure 7, and the total production times were all set to 860 days each.

Schematics of the production strategies.

Strategy 1: PD

The production well was produced at a constant BHP of 4 MPa.

Strategy 2: VF

The reservoir was volume fractured and the production well was open for depressurization production at a constant BHP of 4 MPa. The half-length of the main fracture was set to 30 m. The absolute permeability of the fracture was assumed to be 1 D.

Strategy 3: CS

A total of four production cycles were carried out, and each cycle was 215 days in length. During each cycle, N2 was injected for 30 days, and after a 5-day well shut-in, the well was open for 180 days of production at a constant BHP of 4 MPa.

Strategy 4: VFCS

Strategy 4 combines VF (Strategy 2) and CS (Strategy 3). The CS was carried out after the reservoir was fractured, and operation parameters were the same as Strategy 3.

Results and Discussion

Production Characteristic

CH4 Production of VF

From the base model, the CH4 production rates and cumulative CH4 volumes of VF and PD are shown in Figure 8. The average CH4 production rate of VF is approximately 2.1 times higher than that of PD. At the end of the simulation, the CH4 volumes of VF and PD are 5.39 × 106 and 2.45 × 106 m3, respectively.

CH4 production curves of VF.

For VF production, the seepage environment in the fractured area is significantly improved. Figure 9a,b shows the reservoir permeability distribution of PD and VF. The reservoir permeability in the near-wellbore zone is close to the absolute reservoir permeability of 75 mD for PD, while for VF, the reservoir permeability in the fractured area is close to 1000 mD, and the high-permeability area is significantly larger than that of PD.

Reservoir permeability distribution at the end of the simulation (16th layer).

The existence of fractures greatly enhances the efficiency of pressure propagation and fluid flow. Therefore, hydrate dissociation and CH4 production are accelerated, and the production performance of VF is better than that of PD because of the reservoir permeability improvement.

The average CH4 production rate of CS is approximately 1.6 times higher than that of PD. At the end of the simulation, the CH4 volumes of CS and PD are 3.81 × 106 and 2.45 × 106 m3, respectively. Hydrate dissociation is mainly induced by CH4 partial pressure reduction in the gas phase, and its dissociation rate depends on the driving force, i.e., the difference between the CH4 partial pressure and the corresponding equilibrium pressure of the CH4 hydrate. Unlike PD, N2 injection can further increase the driving force by decreasing the CH4 partial pressure due to the presence of N2, thus promoting the hydrate dissociation rate. In the initial condition, there is no gas in the reservoir. The CH4 hydrate remained undissociated because the pressure was above the equilibrium pressure. It starts to dissociate when the CH4 partial pressure is reduced below the equilibrium pressure after the N2 injection. During the N2 injection and well-shut periods, the mole fraction change of CH4 and N2 in the reservoir can reflect the dissociation process. From Figure 12, during the well-shut period, the CH4 mole fraction increases and the N2 mole fraction decreases, indicating the CH4 hydrate dissociation caused by N2.

Mole fractions of CH4 and N2 in the gas phase in HBL in the 1st cycle (vertical view).

This effect is especially significant during the late production stage (the third or fourth cycle, for example), when the reservoir pressure drops close to the BHP and the dissociation driving force is limited for PD. Figure 13a,b shows the CH4 partial pressure distribution in the gas phase for hydrate dissociation under the conditions of PD and CS. The CH4 partial pressure is close to zero in the near-well area in CS, and therefore, hydrate dissociation is greatly enhanced. The production performance of CS is better than that of PD because of its better CH4-pressure reduction ability.

CH4 partial pressure distribution in HBL at the end of N2 injection in the 3rd cycle (vertical view).

CH4 Production of VFCS

From the analysis above, VFCS could combine the effects of reservoir permeability improvement (Figure 9c) and further CH4 partial pressure reduction (Figure 13c) and should perform better than any other methods. The pressure propagation and fluid flow are the easiest in VFCS and the coeffect of fractures and N2 stimulation enables the most effective depressurization for hydrate dissociation.

Figure 14 shows the CH4 production rates and cumulative CH4 production volumes of PD and VFCS. The average CH4 production rate of VFCS is approximately 2.85 times higher than that of PD. At the end of the simulation, the CH4 volumes of CS and PD are 6.98 × 106 and 2.45 × 106 m3, respectively.

CH4 production curves of VFCS.

The cumulative CH4 volumes and hydrate dissociation ratios of the four production strategies studied above are shown in Figure 15, where the hydrate dissociation ratio is defined as the ratio of the dissociated hydrate mass to the initial hydrate mass. The hydrate dissociation ratio reflects the degree of hydrate dissociation degree. For different production strategies, CH4 production is positively correlated with the hydrate dissociation degree. This is because for the Class III hydrate reservoir, hydrate dissociation is the only source of CH4 production. The higher the hydrate dissociation degree is, the greater the CH4 production volume. Among these production strategies, VFCS has the best production performance and the highest dissociation degree of 32.3%.

Cumulative CH4 production and hydrate dissociation ratio.

Hydrate Saturation Distribution

At the end of the simulation, hydrate saturation distributions of the HBL achieved by PD, VF, CS, and VFCS are shown in Figure 16. For these production strategies, the top and bottom of the HBL have low hydrate saturation and high hydrate dissociation degree. This is mainly because these areas are in direct contact with under- and overburden layers, where a large amount of latent heat exists, which is beneficial for hydrate dissociation.

Hydrate saturation distribution in HBL at the end of production (vertical view).

The hydrate saturation in the near-wellbore zone is low, and the undissociated hydrate mainly exists in the far-well zone. The hydrate saturation reduction degree is the highest for VFCS with the average hydrate saturation of 0.364, 0.321, 0.345, and 0.301 for PD, VF, CS, and VFCS, respectively.

The dissociation of hydrate requires a sufficient heat supply.53 At the end of the simulation, the temperature distribution of the reservoir is shown in Figure 17. The temperature of the HBL dropped significantly, and the temperature of the top and bottom layers near the HBL also decreased, where the latent heat provided part of the heat for hydrate dissociation. The low-temperature area of VFCS is the largest because of the greatest amount of hydrate dissociation, i.e., the greatest heat consumption. External heat may be supplied to further promote the production performance of VFCS.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis has been carried out to study the production performances of VFCS at different absolute reservoir permeabilities, original hydrate saturations, BHPs, volume-fracturing scales, and N2 injection amounts.

Influence of Absolute Reservoir Permeability

Reservoir permeability is one of the most important reservoir physical parameters. As shown in Figure 18, the degree of hydrate dissociation and CH4 production are significantly improved with increasing reservoir permeability. This is mainly because fluid flow is difficult when the reservoir permeability is low, making it difficult for pressure propagation. High absolute reservoir permeability is favorable for pressure propagation and hydrate dissociation and this enables high CH4 production.

CH4 production volumes and hydrate dissociation ratios at different reservoir permeabilities.

Influence of Original Hydrate Saturation

From Figure 19, the hydrate dissociation degree increases significantly with a decrease in original hydrate saturation. Too high (0.6, for example) or too low (0.2, for example) original hydrate saturation is unfavorable for CH4 production. Hydrate saturation influences the reservoir permeability (eq 4). When the original hydrate saturation is too high, the reservoir seepage condition becomes poor, limiting the hydrate dissociation, and when the original hydrate saturation is too low, the gas reserves are low, which affects the long-term gas production potential. Medium hydrate saturation can achieve relatively good production performance (0.3–0.5, for example).

CH4 production volumes and hydrate dissociation ratios at different original hydrate saturations.

Influence of Volume-Fracturing Scale

The half-length of the main fracture reflects the scale of volume-fracturing. As shown in Figure 20, the hydrate dissociation degree and CH4 production increase as the volume-fracturing scale increases. This is mainly because the large scale of volume-fracturing represents large high-permeability regions, which is beneficial for hydrate dissociation acceleration and production performance improvement. However, the increase in production slows when the volume-fracturing scale becomes too large.

CH4 production volumes and hydrate dissociation ratios at different volume-fracturing scales.

Influence of BHP

As shown in Figure 21, the hydrate dissociation degree and CH4 production are significantly improved with decreasing BHP. This is mainly because the lower the BHP is, the greater the production pressure difference for hydrate dissociation and therefore, the greater the CH4 production.

CH4 production volumes and hydrate dissociation ratios at different BHPs.

Influence of N2 Injection Amount

The simulation results of VFCS at different N2 injection amounts of each cycle are shown in Figure 22. The hydrate dissociation degree and CH4 production are improved with increasing N2 injection amounts. This is because the more the N2 injected, the greater the depressurization effect in enhancing hydrate dissociation, making more CH4 produced.

CH4 production volumes and hydrate dissociation ratios at different N2 injection amounts for each cycle.

Summary and Conclusions

This paper proposed a new method, i.e., VFCS, to enhance the PD production of the Class III hydrate reservoir. Based on the hydrate reservoir parameters in the Shenhu area of the South China Sea, a Class III hydrate reservoir model was established to evaluate the production performance of this method. Under the research conditions of this paper, the main conclusions are as follows concerning the Class III hydrate reservoir:

(1)Both VF and CS can enhance the production performance by PD. For VF, the reservoir permeability increases greatly after volume fractured which improves the seepage environment in the fractured area. CS induces hydrate dissociation by reducing the CH4 partial pressure in the gas phase through N2 injection.

CS induces hydrate dissociation by reducing the CH4 partial pressure in the gas phase through N2 injection.

(2)VFCS can combine the effect of VF and CS, and it has the best production performance (the largest CH4 production volume and the highest hydrate dissociation degree) among the studied production strategies.

VFCS can combine the effect of VF and CS, and it has the best production performance (the largest CH4 production volume and the highest hydrate dissociation degree) among the studied production strategies.

(3)Good production performance of VFCS can be achieved under the conditions of high reservoir permeability and medium original hydrate saturation. Large volume-fracturing scale, low BHP, and high N2 injection amount can increase the CH4 production.

Good production performance of VFCS can be achieved under the conditions of high reservoir permeability and medium original hydrate saturation. Large volume-fracturing scale, low BHP, and high N2 injection amount can increase the CH4 production.

(4)Although the depressurization production performance of the Class III hydrate reservoir can be significantly enhanced by VFCS, heat is greatly consumed, accompanied by large amounts of hydrate dissociated. Therefore, external heat can be supplied to further enhance the hydrate development effect, such as warm water from geotherm extraction or electric or microwave heating. Besides, the external heat supply could avoid the potential risk of pore blocking by ice or secondary hydrate formation due to rapid temperature drop.
