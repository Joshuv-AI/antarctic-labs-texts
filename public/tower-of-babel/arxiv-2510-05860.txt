Automated Boilerplate: Prevalence and Quality of Contract Generators in the Context of Swiss Privacy Policies Conference: TBD; ;
Luka Nenadic, David Rodriguez

Abstract




Abstract

It has become increasingly challenging for firms to comply with a plethora of novel digital regulations. This is especially true for smaller businesses that often lack both the resources and know-how to draft complex legal documents. Instead of seeking costly legal advice from attorneys, firms may turn to cheaper alternative legal service providers such as automated contract generators. While these services have a long-standing presence, there is little empirical evidence on their prevalence and output quality.

We address this gap in the context of a 2023 Swiss privacy law revision. To enable a systematic evaluation, we create and annotate a multilingual benchmark dataset that captures key compliance obligations under Swiss and EU privacy law. Using this dataset, we validate a novel GPT-5–based method for large-scale compliance assessment of privacy policies, allowing us to measure the impact of the revision. We observe compliance increases indicating an effect of the revision. Generators, explicitly referenced by 18% of local websites, are associated with substantially higher levels of compliance, with increases of up to 15 percentage points compared to privacy policies without generator use. These findings contribute to three debates: the potential of LLMs for cross-lingual legal analysis, the Brussels Effect of EU regulations, and, crucially, the role of automated tools in improving compliance and contractual quality.

1.
Introduction

In recent years, there has been a noticeable surge in digital regulations in the European Union (
EU
) and many countries. While these rules usually aim to protect consumers or enhance competition and innovation, they can simultaneously create administrative burdens. Drafting disclosures and modifying contracts can be particularly challenging for smaller businesses that often lack the legal resources and expertise. In the wake of the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (
GDPR
)
(
noa 2016
)
, many small businesses eager to comply with the regulation admitted not to abide by key requirements
(
GDPR.EU 2019
)
.

As a result, some firms unable or unwilling to obtain costly legal advice may simply have hoped not to get caught in the crossfire of regulators. Others could have traditionally resorted to copying (and maybe slightly altering) legal documents from other firms. With the advancement of large language models (
LLMs
), it is conceivable that an increasing number of disclosures and contracts nowadays are drafted by generative AI. Neither approach is ideal: copied disclosures and contracts are often unsuitable in different contexts and LLMs are prone to hallucinations in the legal domain
(
Magesh et al. 2025
)
.

Generators can offer a solution to this conundrum. We define generators as tools that guide users through a questionnaire or similar process that ultimately results in a tailored and (almost) ready-made contract.
Current
generators do not draft novel text, but rather draw from a pre-defined list of clauses with variations, which are often vetted by lawyers. Even though generators predate the proliferation of generative AI, our definition is technology-neutral and thus does not exclude its future inclusion. To understand the concept of a generator, it can be beneficial to think of a catering service: you tick the boxes for the types, quantities, and restrictions of meals that you desire and ultimately obtain a tailor-made food delivery for your event. The contract, akin to the catering, is customized to the specific user input. For example, a firm that processes data outside the EU will have a clause on international data transfers in its privacy policy, while a firm that processes data locally will not.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Automated Contract Drafting

While generators exhibit a significant degree of automation, developments in the contract drafting process are, of course, nothing new. As Ayres & Klass
(
Ayres and Klass 2025
)
rightly note, innovations in the technology of drafting have often spurred important changes to contract law. One example is the proliferation of cheap printing and mass markets leading to the rise of standard-form contracts in the twentieth century
(
Ayres and Klass 2025
)
. Moreover, lawyers have widely been using templates to streamline repetitive contract drafting tasks. Analyzing M&A deals, Badawi et al.
(
Badawi et al. 2023
)
found several shared templates among the agreements and identified important clauses that are more likely to be prioritized under time pressure. Bartlett
(
Bartlett 2023
)
observed that venture capital contracts based on an industry-standard template as well as the ones drafted by specific law firms form discernible, albeit not perfectly uniform clusters
(
Bartlett 2023
, p. 31)
. Finally, Nyarko
(
Nyarko 2021
)
demonstrated how some contractual gaps are likely caused by lawyers heavily relying on templates.

More recently, scholars have started investigating the potential of LLMs for drafting contracts. Lam et al.
(
Lam et al. 2023
)
showed that LLMs can simplify the contract drafting procedure for lawyers, while Thaldar
(
Thaldar 2025
)
reports that ChatGPT-4 can draft a data transfer agreement with all standard clauses in an iterative procedure. Moreover, Williams
(
Williams 2024
)
suggests that GPT-4 can draft various types of simple contracts that are both functional and enforceable.

On the topic of generators, Betts & Jeap
(
Betts and Jaep 2017
)
provide a historical overview of their use by both lawyers and their clients. They also describe how machine learning could provide a future path to full automation if some hurdles can be overcome. In a similar vein, Contreras
(
Contreras 2025
)
suggests that generative AI can address the shortcomings in scope and range of traditional generators. Finally, Barton & Rhode
(
Barton and Rhode 2019
)
discuss the different lawsuits and regulatory restrictions that consumer-oriented legal services such as generators have been facing in the US.

Empirical data related to generators is scarce. In a report from the American Bar Association in 2014
(
American Bar Association 2014
)
, more than a third of lawyers stated that they regularly use contract drafting software. More recently, SportDraft
(
SpotDraft 2025
)
revealed that only 12% of legal teams in the US achieved end-to-end contract automation. There is however, a noticeable lack of evidence on the prevalence and quality of the actual contracts created by generators.

Finally, the use of generators also relates to the literature on the diffusion of legal innovation. At the macro level, Engel & Weinshall
(
Engel and Weinshall 2022
)
differentiate legislators, courts, or parties and their legal representatives as diffusers of legal innovation. Through this lens, (partially) automating the contract drafting process with generators may constitute diffusion of the last type. Imagine that a popular generator creates a new
“Disclaimer of Generative AI”
clause. As more and more parties implement such clause into their contracts, the clause may become pervasive over time. Davis
(
Davis 2013
)
also theorizes that generators can produce contractual innovation.

2.2.
The Brussels Effect

To define the phenomenon of laws affecting actors outside the source jurisdiction, Bradford
(
Bradford 2012
;
Bradford 2020
)
coined the term
“Brussels Effect,”
which denotes the EU’s unilateral power to regulate global markets. In a broader and geographically agnostic sense, a Brussels Effect is a specific form of an international regulatory spillover. Davis & Marotta-Wurgler
(
Davis and Marotta-Wurgler 2024
)
define the latter as
“an instance in which actors consciously comply with a law adopted by a particular nation-state outside of its territory in circumstances when the law is not strictly applicable”

(
Davis and Marotta-Wurgler 2024
, p. 3)
. According to Bradford
(
Bradford 2020
)
, a Brussels Effect occurs if the source jurisdiction has a large market size coupled with a substantial regulatory capacity. The enacted rules must further be the strictest standards worldwide and apply to inelastic targets, such as general consumers. Finally, the key criterion for a Brussels Effect is the non-divisibility of goods, i.e., the benefits of a single international standard must outweigh the benefits of differential compliance from the perspective of firms. Furthermore, Bradford distinguishes between the
de facto
and
de jure
Brussels Effect. While the former explains how firms globally comply with EU rules, the latter refers to foreign governments adopting similar rules to the EU
(
Bradford 2023
)
. The
de jure
Brussels Effect can also be perceived as a specific form of legal transplant
(
Birnhack and Mundlak 2025
)
.

Assessing the extent of the Brussels Effect is an empirical question
(
Bradford 2023
, p. 331)
. Besides other domains such as environmental regulations
(
Vogel 2000
)
and competition law
(
Bradford et al. 2019
;
Picht et al. 2025
)
, scholars have identified privacy law as a key area to observe the extent of the Brussels Effect
(
Bradford 2020
;
Davis and Marotta-Wurgler 2024
)
. Many of the over 100 countries with a privacy law have been influenced by EU provisions
(
European Data Protection Supervisor [n. d.]
)
. Overall, empirical studies on the GDPR’s Brussels Effect have yielded mixed results. On the one hand, some researchers have documented a relatively pronounced Brussels Effect. Peukert et al.
(
Peukert et al. 2022
)
found that even websites not legally bound by the GDPR interacted significantly less with third-party web technology providers as a result of the GDPR. Davis and Marotta-Wurgler
(
Davis and Marotta-Wurgler 2024
)
report that more than half of the US-facing policies they inspected were at least partially influenced by the GDPR. Finally, Linden et al.
(
Linden et al. 2020
)
observed that, while its influence is most pronounced in the EU, the regulation led to improved privacy rights even outside of its territorial scope.

On the other hand, Frankenreiter
(
Frankenreiter 2022
)
found that online services offered worse data protection terms to US consumers than their EU counterparts. Dabrowski et al. further report that
“EU consumers encounter significantly less unconditional usage of persistent cookies when surfing the web than US visitors”

(
Dabrowski et al. 2019
, p. 268)
. While these studies examine different potential manifestations of the Brussels Effect and are thus not necessarily conflicting, their inconclusive findings call for further empirical research on the Brussels Effect.

2.3.
Computational Analyses of Policies

Early work on automated policy analysis relied on symbolic methods: handcrafted lexicons, grammars, and ontologies enabled precise yet brittle representations of data practices
(
Alamo et al. 2022
;
d’Aquin et al. 2018
;
Andow et al. 2019
;
Andow et al. 2020
)
. These approaches surfaced inconsistencies (e.g., conflicting disclosures) and distinctions between first- and third-party recipients, but required labor-intensive engineering. Hybrid pipelines such as Privee
(
Zimmeck and Bellovin 2014
)
combined rules with statistical classifiers, while supervised ML models (e.g., SVMs, logistic regression, or ensembles) proved effective for detecting collection, sharing, or user choices at scale
(
Zimmeck et al. 2019
;
Costante et al. 2012
;
Liu et al. 2014
;
Verderame et al. 2020
)
. Unsupervised methods like topic models also helped cluster or align statements
(
Massey et al. 2013
)
. Over time, neural systems such as Polisis
(
Harkous et al. 2018
)
and BERT-based models
(
Ravichander et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2016
)
outperformed traditional classifiers, but still relied heavily on annotated corpora and faced limitations in cross-lingual and regulatory generalization.

Large Language Models (
LLMs
) have been replacing feature engineering with natural-language instructions. Rodriguez et al.
(
Rodriguez et al. 2024b
)
showed that GPT-4 Turbo reached F
1
scores above 93% on benchmark corpora with minimal task-specific tuning, matching or surpassing prior systems such as MAPS
(
Zimmeck et al. 2019
)
and Polisis
(
Harkous et al. 2018
)
. Tang et al.
(
Tang et al. 2023
)
demonstrated strong zero-shot performance, while prompt design was shown to substantially affect outcomes
(
Goknil et al. 2024
)
. More recently, Cory et al.
(
Cory et al. 2025
)
used a two-stage LLM pipeline for word-level annotation of GDPR transparency duties, and Xie et al.
(
Xie et al. 2025
)
evaluated policies against clauses from the GDPR and US state laws, achieving F
1
scores of 0.94. These studies illustrate how LLMs extend prior work towards more fine-grained and legally grounded compliance assessments at scale.

2.4.
Datasets for Policy Analysis

Progress in automated policy analysis has been closely tied to annotated datasets. For early symbolic and statistical approaches, OPP-115
(
Wilson et al. 2016
)
and APP-350
(
Zimmeck et al. 2019
)
provided labeled statements on collection and sharing practices. MAPP
(
Arora et al. 2022
)
added a multilingual dimension (English and German), while IT-100
(
Guamán et al. 2023
)
focused on GDPR disclosures of international transfers. These corpora were essential both for training supervised models and for benchmarking. While LLMs do not require task-specific training, they still rely on such resources for evaluation
(
Guha et al. 2023
)
.

Later datasets broadened both task design and scale. PrivacyQA
(
Ravichander et al. 2019
)
and PolicyQA
(
Ahmad et al. 2020
)
framed policy understanding as question answering, linking text to curated evidence. Longitudinal corpora such as the Princeton–Leuven dataset
(
Amos et al. 2021
)
, Linden et al.
(
Linden et al. 2019
)
, and Wagner
(
Wagner 2023
)
captured policy evolution after the GDPR, while APPCorp
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
annotated document structure and Marotta-Wurgler
(
Marotta-Wurgler and Stein 2025
)
introduced long-text multi-class labels. More recent resources are aligned with specific legal frameworks and jurisdictions. GDPR-NER
(
Darji et al. 2024
)
labeled European policies using the Data Privacy Vocabulary
(
Pandit et al. 2024
)
. Others expand linguistic and geographic scope, including an Arabic dataset of 1,000 policies
(
Mashaabi et al. 2023
)
and the 100-Platforms corpus
(
Pałka et al. 2023
)
. Overall, the field seems to be moving towards regulatory-specific and multilingual corpora.

3.
Legal Background

The GDPR follows the dual regulatory goal of providing safeguards for the processing of personal data and allowing the free movement of personal data in the EU (Art. 1). Its territorial scope encompasses not only controllers established in the EU, but also any entity processing the data of data subjects in the EU by means of offering them goods or services or monitoring their behavior (Art. 3 GDPR). The GDPR also lays down some key data processing principles (Art. 5-11), as well as the rights of the data subjects (Art. 12-23). Many countries have introduced or revised their privacy laws on the basis of the GDPR (
de jure
Brussels Effect). Switzerland’s revision of its Federal Data Protection Act (
FADP
)
(
Swiss Confederation 2020
)
also strongly followed the GDPR, with the Swiss government noting that firms having implemented the GDPR would have only minimal changes to make
(
Swiss Federal Department of Economic Affairs, Education and Research 2024
)
. Nonetheless, the Swiss law also diverges from the GDPR in several aspects. A notable example is that the FADP fines private persons, not companies (Art. 60 et seq.). The FADP also imposes less information duties upon the controller than the GDPR (see Section
5.2
).

4.
Dataset

The dataset for this project was created using the scraper by Bernhard et al.
(
Bernhard et al. 2025
)
. The scraper automatically collects the policies and terms of services of 800 000 websites on a monthly basis and supports 37 languages. Most notably, the scraper reaches recall scores ranging between 0.78 and 0.91 for policies in English, German, Italian, and French, meaning that it rarely misses existing website policies (see Appendix
A.1
for the detailed metrics).

For the purposes of this paper, we scraped a stratified sample of 90 000 websites each in a) Switzerland and b) EFTA countries (all EU countries, Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Norway), as well as Great Britain. These were not the most popular websites but rather a representative sample of websites with various levels of popularity in August 2023. By sampling websites from different popularity buckets, we can analyze a comprehensive range of various websites. Due to overlaps, the procedure resulted in the initial selection of around 150 000 websites. Each website was scraped once one week before the entry into force of the revised FADP (August 23-28, 2023) and once one month thereafter (October 4-10, 2023). This relatively short time frame ensures that all observed changes are likely to have occurred due to the revision.

5.
Method

5.1.
Motivation

The analysis of policies poses a persistent challenge, as these documents are lengthy, vague, and full of legal jargon, which complicates automated processing
(
Alamo et al. 2022
;
Rodriguez et al. 2024a
)
. In Switzerland, this difficulty is compounded by the multilingual environment. As a result, any empirical compliance assessment must be able to accommodate this linguistic diversity without introducing systematic biases.

Traditional computational approaches (see Section
2.3
) are poorly suited to this task. Symbolic methods and lexicon-based classifiers have been developed almost exclusively for English. Supervised learning approaches depend on annotated corpora that exist primarily in English (e.g., OPP-115) and cannot be directly applied to other languages without extensive re-annotation. Even deep learning models such as Polisis
(
Harkous et al. 2018
)
or MAPS
(
Zimmeck et al. 2019
)
rely on English-only training data and fixed taxonomies, reinforcing an anglophone bias
(
Mhaidli et al. 2023
)
that limits their adequacy in the Swiss context.

LLMs provide a more flexible alternative. A single model can be applied across different languages without retraining, avoiding the need for language-specific resources or feature engineering. At the same time, LLMs can capture compliance-relevant obligations—such as duties to inform users—in a consistent manner across the languages. Importantly, their performance must still be assessed against independent human-coded data to ensure the accuracy, completeness, and legal relevance of model outputs, as even LLMs are not immune to decreased performance in specific languages.

5.2.
Codebook and Annotations

In line with the “text-as-data” approach in computational social sciences
(
Grimmer et al. 2022
)
, we constructed a multilingual benchmark based on a systematically developed codebook. The codebook translates statutory obligations from the GDPR and the revised FADP into discrete annotation questions. These questions are used both to construct the human-annotated ground truth and to guide the automated evaluation, as the codebook is supplied to the LLM in the prompt.

Since translating complex legal requirements into discrete annotations is no simple task, we first had to identify obligations that a) are reflected in privacy policies and b) can be assessed objectively. The former condition led us to the information duties under Art. 13 GDPR, which we distilled to the following obligations that are reasonably objective to annotate: the controller’s identity (including contact details) and the purposes of processing (Art. 13(1) GDPR), as well as the rights of data subjects (see Table
1
).

5.3.
Method Design

Our automated method applies OpenAI models to classify policies according to the dimensions defined in the codebook. Throughout this section, we refer to each of the nine dimensions by its abbreviated code as defined in Table
1
. For each policy, we issue a single inference request to the model and require it to return a JSON object conforming to a predefined schema. We rely on OpenAI’s structured output functionality
(
OpenAI 2024a
)
, which enforces the presence and type of all nine fields and prevents the generation of invalid formats or hallucinated attributes. The call is structured with a fixed
system message
specifying the global task and rules, followed by two
user messages
: 1) the codebook questions with their instructions, and 2) the raw policy text. We release the final prompts and the schema
verbatim
with our artifacts to foster transparency and reproducibility.

The user prompt mirrors the codebook by including each question along with the same decision rules, examples, and edge-case clarifications used by the annotators. To further reduce ambiguity, we incorporate few-shot examples demonstrating positive and negative cases for each item. Prior work has shown that such examples significantly improve the reliability of LLM-output in this domain
(
Rodriguez et al. 2024b
)
, and their inclusion follows standard practice in human codebook development, where annotated examples guide consistent application of coding rules
(
Zlabinger et al. 2020
;
V7 Labs 2022
;
Tseng et al. 2020
)
. This alignment also allows direct comparison between manual and automated annotations.

The model processes policies in German, Italian, French, and English without translation. To reduce the risk of the model relying on superficial entity cues (e.g., company name) and to mitigate privacy risks, all identifying information is anonymized with placeholders using Presidio
(
Microsoft 2025
)
prior to inference. A key feature of our approach is that the model evaluates all compliance dimensions in a single prompt. Prior work has typically addressed one practice at a time (e.g., data retention periods), whereas our design extracts the full set of obligations in one pass. This substantially reduces costs and increases efficiency as opposed to individual API requests. No fine-tuning or task-specific training is applied, as model behavior is entirely determined by the fixed prompts and schema.

or
.swiss
TLD and b) are
not
in any EU-country popularity bucket should have been impacted the most by the revised FADP. As these are likely not websites catering to or being accessed by EU users, they should not have been impacted by the GDPR according to theory. These websites represent the
CH
group. For websites with a Swiss TLD that
are
in any EU-country popularity bucket, we might expect a dimmed effect of the revised FADP, as websites that had already complied with the GDPR (
de facto
Brussels Effect) would only have to make minimal changes to their policies (see Section
3
). Finally, the
EU
group consists of websites with a German, Austrian, French, or Italian TLD that are
not
in any Swiss popularity bucket. These websites represent a quasi control group, as it is unlikely they are subject to Swiss privacy law. In the further analysis, the
EU
group will provide a useful baseline and sanity check for assessing the effects of the revised FADP. We have excluded websites with generic TLDs (e.g.,
.com
), as it remains unclear which countries these websites primarily target.

The group-wise summary statistics for August and October are depicted in Table

. We can observe that there are more than 5 000 websites in each group and that German is the predominant language in all three groups. We can also observe that the prevalence of privacy policies increases by more than 5 p.p. in the
CH
and
CH & EU
groups, with a minimal increase of 1.6 p.p. in the
EU
group; a foretaste of the findings to come. Table
4
shows the group-wise differences in median word counts. As expected, the
EU
policies remain similarly long, while both the
CH
and the
CH & EU
policies increase in median length by around 300 words. The increase is proportionally larger for the
CH
group.

“DSGVO”

CH

6.2.
Compliance and Brussels Effect

Before delving into further findings, we must provide some clarifications regarding our use of the term
“compliance.”
First, while both Swiss and EU privacy law require
contr
and
purp
to be disclosed, Swiss privacy law does
not
mandate the dat subject rights
rect
,
forg
,
port
, and
comp
to be mentioned, in contrast to the GDPR. If Swiss-facing websites do indicate these rights, this constitutes a
de facto
Brussels Effect. Second,
hum
is a
conditional
information obligation, i.e., nothing needs to be disclosed whenever the controller does not perform any automated decisions. Finally, we do not check for actual website website behavior, e.g., whether the website uses personal data for the stated purposes.

To assess whether observed changes in compliance reflect more than sampling noise, we compared proportions of compliant policies between August and October for each obligation within all groups (
EU
,
CH
,
CH & EU
) using the
two-proportion
z
-test
, the standard large-sample method for contrasting independent binomial rates
(
Agresti 2013
;
Fleiss et al. 2003
)
. Because multiple obligations are tested in parallel, we applied the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (
FDR
) correction at
\alpha=0.05
to avoid inflation of false positives while retaining power
(
Benjamini and Hochberg 1995
)
. To qualify the magnitude of differences, we computed Cohen’s
h
, the conventional effect size index for differences in proportions
(
Cohen 1988
;
Lakens 2013
)
. Finally, we derived the minimum detectable effect (
MDE
) at 80% power to ensure that statistically significant results are also practically detectable given our sample sizes
(
Chow et al. 2017
)
. This combination identifies statistically and practically meaningful differences.

We report all results in Table
5
. The
EU
group serves as a control and shows no meaningful change between August and October: all results remain insignificant after FDR correction (
q\geq 0.75
) and effect sizes are minimal (
\max|h|\approx 0.021
). By contrast, the
CH
and
CH & EU
groups exhibit significant and MDE-exceeding increases. For the
CH
group, we observe significant increases for all obligations except for
purp
, which is likely due to the very high baseline in August. For the
CH & EU
group, the increases are also insignificant for the
forg
and
hum
categories. The p.p. increases are generally lower for higher levels of baseline compliance. While all
|h|
values remain “below small” on Cohen’s scale (max
\approx 0.135
), the MDE analysis shows that these changes are statistically reliable and practically detectable. The effect sizes must be interpreted in light of the specific context: we are likely underestimating the total effect of the revision, as some websites modified their policies before August or after October 2023 to implement the revision.

7.
Contract Generators

7.1.
Characteristics

While the various business models and technical implementations of generators make it hard to nail down a single definition, the core principle of a generator is that
users are guided through a questionnaire or similar process that ultimately results in a tailored and (almost) ready-made contract
. For example, the most popular generator at the time, SwissAnwalt, made users tick the relevant boxes for their website (e.g., use of a contact form; see Appendix
A.4
). Other generators achieve a higher level of automation, essentially skipping the error-prone, manual entry of information by the user. PrivacyBee
(
PrivacyBee [n. d.]
)
advertises a policy generator on
“autopilot,”
claiming to automatically recognize all privacy-relevant services used on a given website and to generate as well as regularly update the according policy.

We have identified the generators in our sample through a semi-automated, iterative procedure (see Appendix
A.5
). None of the generators in our sample state using generative AI to draft the policies. While this may change in the future and our definition does not preclude it, we assume that policy texts are standardized enough for generators to entirely rely on pre-defined clauses that are combined together depending on the user input. On the lower spectrum of automation, we ought to delimit generators from mere templates that users must manually complete or modify, as generators are based on the notion that the
user is not intended to directly interact with the actual text of the contract
.

One hypothesis for this finding could be that using the most popular generator at that time, SwissAnwalt, gained traction through network effects. Swiss-facing websites might have heard from competitors using the generator and decided to follow along. It is also striking that EU generators are used by Swiss websites as well, but

As a next step, we wanted to elucidate to what extent generators output standardized policies. For this purpose, we visualized all German October policies on a two-dimensional space using t-SNE

to preserve the semantic structure of the policies. The embeddings were based on each policy’s first 8 192 tokens, due to the model’s context window limitation. While some policies exceed this window, this approach constitutes a major improvement over the mere first 1 000 characters used by Badawi et al.
(

, p. 16)

8.
Discussion

Our analysis indicates that the revised FADP noticeably affected Swiss-facing websites. The
CH
group showed lower levels of baseline compliance in August and experienced higher p.p. increases in October. This suggests that some Swiss websites operating in the EU had already (at least partially) complied with the GDPR, an indication for a Brussels Effect. For jurisdictions pondering to implement EU rules, this highlights the importance of investigating the potential impact for local businesses. Moreover, the revision of the FADP is associated with increased mentions of data subject rights that already existed before the revision, and for which the FADP still does not mandate disclosure.

We also find that generators are not only particularly prevalent for smaller German websites, but that their use is connected to higher levels of compliance in the context of this study. Policymakers should take note, as generators do not necessarily need to be for-profit: for instance, the UK’s Information Commissioner’s Office provides a free policy generator
(
Information Commissioner’s Office (2024) ICO
)
. We welcome this effort, as our findings suggest that this can help small businesses comply with regulations. While creating and updating a generator entails certain expenses, we believe that these are offset by decreased enforcement costs in the longer term. We also do not think that generators are confined to the domain of privacy. Consumer protection agencies could, for example, create generators for terms of service of e-commerce platforms. This way, they may nudge businesses into avoiding intransparent or one-sided terms. If policymakers decide against providing public generators, the Swiss context still offers valuable insights from a relatively liberal legal market, as opposed to the regulatory constraints faced by alternative legal services in the US
(
Barton and Rhode 2019
)
.

This is not to say that generators are flawless. First, while they have the potential to diffuse beneficial legal innovations very fast, they can equally entrench nefarious terms. Think of a poorly drafted clause that a court renders unenforceable: the issue could simultaneously impact thousands of contracts. This problem is exacerbated by the fact that small businesses using generators are unlikely to question their output. Moreover, generators may have little incentives to continuously upgrade their tools, leading to a suboptimal level of legal innovation.

9.
Limitations and Future Work

Our study has limitations typical of empirical work in this area. First, our grouping procedure (see Section
6.1
) inevitably oversimplifies the territorial scope of the GDPR and the FADP, although we believe it approximates the websites subject to each privacy law regime reasonably well. Second, our dataset focuses on a restricted set of variables. Additional dimensions such as website categories would enable to explore heterogeneity across industries or to use them as controls in regression analyses to identify causal effects. Finally, while manual annotations inevitably involve a degree of subjectivity, we minimized this through an iterative refinement of the codebook until annotators reached at least acceptable inter-annotator agreement. By contrast, the LLM-based policy analysis does not entail subjectivity, but it does raise concerns about reproducibility, since model outputs may vary slightly across runs.

Despite these limitations, our paper sheds light on the role of generators in diffusing contractual terms and paves the way for future research along the following dimensions:

(1)

Are generators equally widespread in other jurisdictions and for different contracts such as terms of service? Such research would allow the generalization of our findings.

(2)

What are the legal implications of the use of generators? Addressing these questions requires carefully evaluating restrictions on the unauthorized practice of law and the potential liability of generators and their users.

(3)

Can our multilingual, LLM-based information extraction method be used in different contexts? For example, one could use it to efficiently evaluate how different types of contracts vary from jurisdiction to jurisdiction.

10.
Conclusion

We showed that the revision of Swiss privacy law had a noticeable effect on Swiss-facing websites and that generators can play a crucial role in improving compliance, particularly among smaller websites. Together, these results provide new empirical insights into the interplay between legal innovation, regulatory diffusion, and automated contract drafting. Our contributions highlight both the potential and the risks of automated boilerplate generation. While our study focused on privacy, the dynamics we document likely extend to other contracts. We hope that our findings will inform future research and policy debates on how automation can improve compliance and contractual quality.

A.2.
Iterative Codebook Development

We developed and refined our codebook in an iterative procedure consisting of three phases. In the first phase, three annotators independently applied the full set of ten questions to 15 English policies, yielding promising reliability metrics (Krippendorff’s
\alpha=0.74
–
1.0
). Disagreements led us to refine Q3 (controller information), Q8 (data portability), and Q10 (automated decision-making). A second phase with 20 policies produced
\alpha
-values between 0.65 and 0.93. Following standard guidelines
(
Krippendorff 2004
)
, values above 0.80 indicate strong reliability, while values between 0.67 and 0.80 are generally considered acceptable.

All retained dimensions reached at least the acceptable threshold, with most exceeding 0.80 (see Table
1
). The “data protection officer” question was dropped due to inconsistent terminology across policies. In the final phase, the updated codebook was applied to 120 policies (30 each in German, English, Italian, and French). At this stage, each policy was independently annotated by a single coder proficient in the respective language, a common practice once high agreement has been reached
(
Wong et al. 2021
;
Artstein and Poesio 2008
)
. For consistency, additional global rules were enforced for both human annotators and the model: if
ispol
is false, all other booleans must also be false and the date fields set to “NA”. Dates are normalized to the format DD/MM/YYYY, with the most recent date mentioned in the policy selected, or “NA” if none is present.

To ensure consistency across phases, all policies from the three annotation phases were drawn from the customized dataset introduced in Section
4
. Random sampling was performed from the August 2023 snapshot, independently for each phase, which formally allowed overlaps, though in practice only one repetition occurred. These subsets provided a basis for iterative refinement of the codebook and for establishing a multilingual ground truth to validate the method.

A.5.
Generator Identification Method

The first step to detect the prevalence of generators was to identify all major providers. For this purpose, in our LLM-based analysis (see Section
5
), we introduced an exploratory question
org
capturing whether a policy states that it was generated with the help of a tool or template. This broad definition was used to capture all potential generators (independent of naming), with the possibility to discard false positives in a subsequent step. Unlike the nine dimensions defined in Table
1
,
org
is a string value and was not part of the manual annotations due to its exploratory nature. In a second step, we manually inspected the model output to distill the actual generators from mere templates and other false positives. In an iterative procedure, we were ultimately able to define a reliable, conservative dictionary of RegEx patterns for each generator. This two-step procedure was necessary, since the model cannot recognize
ex ante
whether the stated source of a policy is an actual generator or a mere template.
