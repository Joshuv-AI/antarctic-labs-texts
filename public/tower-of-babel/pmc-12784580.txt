Asylum seekers and refugees’ access to oral health care services in Switzerland: a qualitative study
Authors: Lujain Alchalabi, Nicole Probst-Hensch, Nicola Ursula Zitzmann, Sonja Merten
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAsylum seekers and refugees in Switzerland face major barriers to oral health care services, often limited to urgent treatments like tooth extractions. Access depends largely on legal status, canton of residence and duration in the country, whereby it is largely unclear for those affected as to which treatments are actually covered.MethodsSixteen interviews and three in-clinic observations were conducted with Arabic-speaking asylum seekers and refugees in Basel-Stadt and Basel-Landschaft, two German-speaking Swiss cantons.ResultsIt was documented that financial constraints, permit restrictions, communication challenges, and limited autonomy often led participants to delay or avoid dental care, worsening their oral health. While frameworks like Levesque’s model help explain access challenges, they do not fully capture how systemic barriers shape asylum seekers and refugees’ abilities to recognize needs, seek care, and participate in their own health decisions. If these deeper structural issues are not addressed, there is a risk that efforts to improve care will fall short.ConclusionThe current findings point to the need for systemic reforms to improve coverage, communication, and preventive care, while promoting equitable, tooth-preserving treatment options for asylum seekers and refugees.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02711-z.Keywords: Oral health, Asylum seekers, Refugees, Dental care, Healthcare access, Barriers

Background

Oral health (OH) is an element of physical and mental well-being. Impaired dentition is associated with a lower quality of life, low-quantity diet, and an increased risk of non-communicable diseases. In addition, OH conditions affect socializing and interacting with others [1, 2]. Oral health care (OHC) encompasses a range of preventive, diagnostic, and therapeutic services, including health promotion and rehabilitation, and is recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) as an essential part of universal health coverage [3]. The achievements from preventive measures in improving OH have been documented over decades [4–6].

Given the strong relationship between lower socioeconomic status and OH status [2, 7], noticeable inequalities occurred between low- and high-income European populations regarding unmet OH needs [8]. Among the Swiss population, socially disadvantaged groups tend to have fewer dental visits and worse oral hygiene, which leads to significantly worse OH [9]. Poor OH can negatively affect employment and productivity, leading to lower socioeconomic status and fewer resources for healthcare, including dental care, thus reinforcing a cycle of disadvantage [2].

Since immigrants have reported worse health compared to Swiss citizens [10], their OH status is assumed to be worse as well [5], especially for asylum seekers and refugees (ASR). ASR have a high prevalence of oral diseases, unmet OH needs and inadequate oral hygiene [11]. Cross-sectional studies from Europe highlight substantial OH disparities between ASR and host populations. In Norway, ASR had four times more decayed teeth and six times fewer fillings than nationals [12]. A German study reported a mean Decayed Teeth Score of 3.8 among ASR versus 0.5 in the German population [13]. Among school-aged ASR children in Sweden, the prevalence of untreated caries reached 48% [14]. However, there is a notable lack of longitudinal data directly comparing OH outcomes between displaced and host populations.

The compromised life circumstances prior, during and post-migration as well as limited access to the host healthcare system are common factors of poor OH among ASR [15]. Hence, language barriers, financial constraints and broader challenges of resettlement and integration further limit access to OHC services. Inadequate OHC and OH literacy reflect access to information (health promotion) in the country of origin, a gap that could be improved by the host country’s OHC services if they were utilized appropriately [16].

In Switzerland, OHC services are mainly paid out of pocket or partially covered by dental insurance, which must be purchased separately, as the basic health insurance does not include OHC coverage. This adds a financial burden on individuals with limited incomes and might deter them from seeking essential OHC services, leading to unmet OH needs and potential long-term consequences [17–19]. Recipients of social welfare assistance or supplementary benefits with incomes below the poverty line receive financial support for standardized dental treatment, which must comply with the guidelines of the Swiss Association of Cantonal Chief Dental Officers. These guidelines also apply to ASR, at least 83% of whom receive welfare assistance payments [20]. According to these guidelines, which serve as a recommendation to the social welfare authorities, primary care with non-postponable, mainly temporary measures in emergency cases and for pain relief are provided (e.g., extraction, wire clasp dentures, no endodontics in molar teeth, no fixed dental prostheses). Secondary measures with simple and appropriate treatments are applied only to ensure the chewing function by maintaining at least 10 antagonistic occluding pairs.

Generally, in ASR with permit N (asylum seeker permit), NEE (asylum application rejected), or S (return-oriented protection status), cantons mainly apply the guidelines in a very limited scope that ensure pain relief. ASR with permit F (provisional admission after rejection of asylum application, but deportation back to their country of origin is currently impossible) are financed for primary, non-deferrable measures and emergency dental treatments that ensure pain relief and ability to eat. Dentists’ treatment proposals must be in accordance with simple, economic and efficient treatment criteria. Refugees with permit B (recognized refugee permit) who are dependent on social welfare assistance can apply for public social welfare to sponsor their dental treatment costs, calculated on a case-by-case basis [21–25].

Given that the perceived need to utilize OHC services could be shaped by social and cultural norms [26, 27], and the fact that OHC service coverage is not the same for all ASR in Switzerland [21–24], there is a need to explore ASR perceptions and opinions on OHC services using a patient-centeredapproach. Understanding the specific barriers and facilitators of ASR access to OHC services is key to identifying efficient pathways to improve it, which can positively influence overall OH [3, 28, 29]. The impact of improved access to OHC services and preventive measures on OH is emphasized even more by the role of OH in shaping functional, physical, psychological, and social well-being, collectively reflected in oral health-related quality of life [30, 31]. Based on the experiences of the Arabic-speaking ASR population in Switzerland, the overarching goal of this study is to identify the key elements influencing access to OHC services. In this study, experiences are understood as the subjective ways in which ASR perceive and interpret events and interactions.

Methods

To achieve a comprehensive understanding of the barriers and facilitators related to OHC, the current analysis will draw on the comprehensive model of healthcare access of Levesque et al. as a sensitizing concept [32]. This widely applied theoretical framework has been used recently in research related to dental services [33] and offers a structured lens for examining both health system- and individual-level (demand side) dimensions of access to care, fostering a patient-centered perspective [27, 34]. The framework highlights five health-system-side dimensions of access: approachability, acceptability, availability, affordability, and appropriateness, which are then paired with the corresponding patient abilities needed to access care effectively [34]. While the framework offers a robust foundation, its emphasis on individual capabilities does not fully account for structural and institutional constraints, such as systemic racism, anti-immigrant sentiment, financial dependency on welfare, or legal and administrative barriers to care. In the context of Basel region in Switzerland, this study will critically extend the Levesque framework to better capture the lived experiences of Arabic-speaking ASR navigating the OHC services system.

This qualitative study is part of a broader mixed-methods research project focusing on access to OHC services among Arabic-speaking ASR in Switzerland [35]. For this study, qualitative data from adult ASR residing in the Basel region (Canton Basel-Stadt and Basel-Landschaft) were analyzed, with a focus on their OH-related experiences and perceptions. The current study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Canton of Northwestern Switzerland (Date: 18.07.2023, EKNZ, No. 2023 − 00871). It was carried out in line with the revised Declaration of Helsinki [36], with respect to participants’ privacies and a written informed consent obtained from all participants beforehand.

Participants’ recruitment

Eligible participants were adult Arabic-speaking asylum seekers and refugees living within the geographical regions of Basel-Landschaft and Basel-Stadt. To recruit eligible participants, the first author contacted Arabic schools and dental clinics where she presented the study and distributed study flyers to invite interested persons to contact her by phone or email. The first contact with the interested person entailed an explanation of the study’s purpose and participation procedures, a quick screening to ensure eligibility, and scheduling a date, time and place for the interview. Snowballing was then used for additional recruitment, which ended once no new information or themes emerged from the interviews, indicating the data saturation had been reached. The recruitment, piloting, and data collection were conducted between August 2023 and December 2023.

Data collection

Interviews

In-depth interviews, each lasting around one hour, were conducted in the Arabic language by the first author, who came to Switzerland as a qualified dentist and a refugee, thus sharing some of the experiences in the asylum process. The interviews took place in various settings, including coffee shops, gardens, or the participants’ homes, based on their preferences. In some cases, the participant preferred to have a friend or a relative close-by during the interview.

All participants provided informed, written consent, including permission to audio record the interviews. The interviews were recorded using Open Data Kit software on a tablet, a suite of open-source tools that helps organizations and researchers collect, manage, and use data [37]. The first author transcribed the interviews, and the completed transcriptions were uploaded to MAX-QDA 22 (VERBI Software, Berlin, Germany) for analysis.

A semi-structured interview guide was developed for the qualitative study (see Table A1 in Additional file 1). The interview guide represented the final set of questions following the piloting phase (2 pilot interviews). If the interviewer noticed that a particular aspect had not been mentioned by the participant, she used visual prompts, specifically the icons representing experiences related to accessing OH services, to guide further probing and asked targeted questions to elicit relevant information on the topic (see Table A1 in Additional File 1). After each interview, a picture of the chosen icons was captured and automatically attached to the participant’s recording in the Open Data Kit database. Throughout the study, pseudonyms were used for the participants and the individuals mentioned in their interviews.

As compensation, the participants received a shopping voucher from a Swiss local supermarket and a manual toothbrush. All the participants’ questions about OH that they were eager to ask were answered by the first author after completing the interview.

In-clinic observations

To have a holistic view on the interactions and behaviors during the dental visits, in-clinic observations were conducted. Participants were informed that we were interested in accompanying them to any upcoming dental appointments, a proposition that all participants welcomed and approved. After the first author instructed them to notify her once they scheduled an appointment, three participants contacted her about their upcoming dental visits and requested her assistance with interpretation during their appointments. The first author conducted three in-clinic observations with adult participants, each lasting no more than one hour. During the in-clinic observations, the first author was not able to take notes due to the many tasks required to assist the participants during their dental visit. To minimize observer effect, the first author chose not to disclose her professional background as a dentist during the observations, as this could have influenced the behavior of the dental service provider or the communication. The first author created a memory protocol immediately after each observation [38]. The first author audio-recorded the entire observation directly after leaving the participant. Each observation was transcribed on the same day and connected with the interview that had previously taken place with the participant.

Analytical approach

To achieve a better understanding of the retrieved data, a combination of a thematic and narrative analysis was conducted. Combining these two approaches adds a comprehensive view and understanding of the current context and the migration history [39].

Thematic analysis

Thematic analysis is a method used to understand behaviors, experiences, and thoughts through qualitative data. It allows the researcher to identify and develop themes that offer new perspectives, interpretations, and connections within the data [40]. In the current sub-study, inductive thematic analysis helped to uncover common barriers, perceptions, and experiences related to accessing OHC in Switzerland, as well as the conceptual links between these themes. This analytic method not only provided a clear structure but also enabled us to enhance the theoretical framework on access to healthcare [41, 42], allowing us to identify latent themes that were not immediately obvious and to understand the deeper connections among them.

Narrative approach

Narrative research methods in healthcare focus on understanding how individuals construct and communicate their personal stories, offering insights into how their stories unfolded over time and the meaning they attach to their experiences [43]. In the current study, where participants shared not only their OH-related experiences but also painful memories related to their asylum journey, the narrative analysis was particularly valuable. It allowed exploring the deeper context behind their stories, highlighting the emotional and psychological dimensions of their struggles. Examining how participants narrated their experiences improved the understanding of how their past and present challenges intersected with their OH and accessing the services, providing a richer, more holistic view of the data.

Step-by-step combination between thematic and narrative approaches

Initially, the first author conducted a first read-through of the interview transcripts to familiarize herself with the data. This was followed by a second, more detailed reading where the first author applied line-by-line coding (descriptive codes), which served to identify key concepts and patterns within the data using an inductive approach. The first author used analytic memos to document and share the reasoning behind decisions made during coding, categorizing, or theme development, providing transparency in the analysis process. The memos enabled establishing connections between themes or between codes (inductive approach).

After this thematic analysis, we revisited the data to perform a narrative analysis for each interview. For the narrative approach, we added emotion codes, process codes and sub-codes when needed. The narrative approach allowed us to delve deeper into individual stories, examining the sequence of events and the personal meaning participants attached to their experiences. This enabled us to consider the context and emotional nuances in how participants told their stories and how they felt about their experiences.

By combining both thematic and narrative analyses, we achieved a more nuanced understanding of participants’ access to OHC and their perceptions of OH. The narrative analysis offered insights into the unique details, emotions and trajectories within each story, enhancing our understanding of the lived experiences of the participants in their specific contexts. In parallel, the thematic analysis enabled the identification of patterns across stories, grouping similar topics together and recognizing trends that were shared by certain groups of participants. This dual approach provided both depth and structure in understanding the data.

Finally, we juxtaposed our analysis to the Levesque’s access to care model [34]. The framework supported the final presentation of the themes and facilitated exploring their interrelationships, and was then applied as an interpretive lens to assess how the findings mapped onto existing dimensions of access, where gaps remained, and where our results extended beyond the model (corresponding to an abductive approach).

After finalizing the analysis, participants were not asked to provide feedback on the findings due to limited resources, particularly the need to translate and simplify the findings and then re-establish contact and organize follow-up discussions.

Reflective journal/diary

A reflective diary was maintained throughout the research process to document the first author’s (interviewer/observer/data analyst) emotional and cognitive responses, ensuring reflexivity. This diary served as a tool to track the first author’s evolving perceptions and reactions, allowing her to connect these reflections to her memos during analysis. By systematically documenting her thoughts, she could recognize and minimize her influence on the analytical process, enhancing the study’s objectivity.

The primary interviewer, a Syrian refugee and trained female dentist with a master’s degree in epidemiology and public health, brought a unique and valuable perspective to the study. Her clinical background likely enhanced data collection by enabling a deeper understanding of oral health issues and fostering a strong rapport with participants who shared similar experiences of dental care challenges. This professional expertise allowed her to probe into more complex oral health-related topics.

However, being a dentist may have also introduced certain biases. There was a potential overemphasis on the clinical aspects, which could have led to less focus on the emotional, psychological, and systemic barriers to accessing care. Additionally, the author’s position as an authority figure in the eyes of the participants might have shaped their responses, possibly limiting the expression of broader personal experiences. Some participants may have feared judgment or sought to present themselves to the author positively, potentially limiting the disclosure of their OH concerns.

Results

The study involved seventeen participants (eleven females and six males), aged between 20 and 66 (see Table A2 in Additional File 1). A total of sixteen interviews (including 2 pilot interviews and one joint interview with a couple) and three in-clinic observations were conducted. Fifteen participants were from Syria, with one participant originating from Sudan and another from Palestine. Although we probed for a broad range of experiences related to OHC services (from routine check-ups to clinical procedures), participants predominantly focused on access to dental treatments (DT), with little to no mention of other aspects such as oral health promotion, prevention, or education.

Our data analysis revealed the following themes: emerging notions of legal personhood, experiences of welfare pluralism and bureaucracy, restricted self-determination, transcultural communication barriers, and the emerging theme of social personhood. The identified themes interconnected with the participants’ life trajectories, especially the history related to migration. To help the readers understand how the themes were embedded in real life and intertwined with each other, two spotlight cases followed by detailed themes were summarized.

Spotlight cases

Case 1: Salem

Salem, a man in his sixties from Syria, was living in Basel-Stadt, Switzerland, with his wife Sahar, holding Permit-F. The couple dreamed of establishing a catering business but struggled to cover the expenses as they were reliant on social welfare. Occasionally, they sold home-cooked food at local markets, striving to sustain a sense of self-reliance. Their daughter, Sara, initially brought her father’s story to light during our interview with her. Sara expressed how her father’s dental health has significantly deteriorated since their arrival in Switzerland.

Coming here affected his teeth, I do not know, but they removed all his teeth and they did not repair anything. They gave him a denture. Until now, it affects him a lot. (Sara 4 − 01, Salem’s daughter, 26 years old, mother of one child, Basel-Stadt, Permit-F)

When we met Salem and his wife, at the very beginning they emphasized their need for privacy and anonymity, fearing any risk of harm or identity disclosure. Salem’s story begins with his dental struggles that started in Syria, just before he embarked on his migration journey.

I went to a dentist and Sahar came with me. She said ‘this dentist is not clean, his instruments and equipment are not clean. Do not go to him.’ I said no, I came here and I will continue. He drilled in my teeth and so on. You can say that he damaged the teeth. We said okay, no problem. After a while 2–3 months my teeth, two three of them, started to move and be loose. (Salem)

Upon arriving in Switzerland, Salem sought OHC services, but his condition kept worsening. Each visit resulted in the extraction of more teeth. When he asked about the cause, he was told it was a bacterial infection and the only solution that was offered was extraction.

For the past 4–5 years, Salem was eating with an ill-fitting denture that was provided for him by the OHC services. Salem’s discomfort with his denture caused a persistent gag reflex, leading him to consume sugary mints frequently, increasing the risk of diabetes that runs in his family. Salem felt embarrassed in all the social settings because he was unable to chew harder food and his denture fractured often, necessitating repairs and leaving him without it for days. Sahar echoed Salem’s frustration from finding a solution to cover the costs of replacing the denture:

They do not give, they do not give. His mental health deteriorated. This denture is making him eat those sugars. How many time we tried and went to the dentists but they do not listen. (Sahar)

Salem’s permit (Permit-F) restricted him from traveling outside Switzerland to get more affordable dental treatments. Salem’s income from social welfare does not allow him to make savings. He could secure some financial assets from his family members to travel for treatment, which was not possible due to the travel restriction by his permit type (F-permit).

Salem expressed deep disappointment and a sense of loss as he told his story, particularly explaining how the system’s restrictions took from him the freedom to address his dental needs, leaving him without control over his own health.

Case 2: Layal and her mother Rola

Layal (originally coming from Syria) spent several years as a teenager in Turkey before arriving in Switzerland. During Layal’s early childhood, she endured an accident causing broken anterior teeth, necessitating unpleasant frequent visits to the dentist. She faced mockery from her peers due to this dental issue. Despite undergoing fillings after fleeing the war to Turkey, Layal encountered persistent issues, leading to mounting frustration from the host dental care system.

And another tooth broke there and I fixed it …I could not have more frequent visits, the distances in Turkey are long and with the kimlik of Syrians is very hard …kimlik is the ID, the temporary ID. So when you want to book an appointment it is a problem on its own and once you get there the treatment from the doctors is another level of racism. I hated the hospital literally. (Layal)

Layal’s perception of OHC in Turkey was profoundly shaped by her personal experiences and observations while accompanying her father to hospitals. She was hurt by the discrimination and inadequate treatment faced by herself and her parents.

My dad injured his leg in Syria and had multiple operations. I used to accompany him to the hospitals and observed the reactions. It was enough for me to realize that, in such situations, I do not want to seek medical care for myself. (Layal)

After arriving in Switzerland, Layal’s perception of OHC was negatively affected by her experience in Turkey and yet influenced by what she heard from the people around her:

Here (in Switzerland), I went to the dentist alone for the first time. I was afraid because I was new here, and my German was not very good. I wondered what they would do. What I heard made me even more scared. I heard that if they clean your teeth, they will actually get worse than before. […] More than one woman visited us as a guest, and I was like, ‘What?! It’s just cleaning—it should improve the teeth!’ But they kept saying that it hurts and that after the cleaning, the gums will always be inflamed. So when I went there, my expectations were very negative. (Layal)

As for the legal status, Layal and her parents faced rejection for their asylum case, followed by significant pressure to leave voluntarily. While struggling with language barriers, Layal became her parent’s primary assistant (a) in navigating healthcare, while also (b) balancing familial obligations such as visiting her sick grandmother and (c) contacting social welfare, cantonal authorities and legal representatives appealing against their rejected asylum case.

Despite the life pressures and the challenging history of dental care, Layal demonstrated good knowledge of personal oral hygiene, which positively impacted her experience with Swiss dental care, characterized by effective communication with her initial dentist.

Her (the dentist) way of talking and explaining is very good and simple and showed me what I should do and what has changed. (Layal)

However, Layal’s mother, Rola, did not share the same positive perception of Swiss dental care. While experiencing difficulties obtaining treatment for a displaced dental bridge, Layal’s mother underwent tooth extraction due to unbearable pain and irreparable damage. Initially, the mother declined a dentist’s recommendation to extract the teeth beneath the displaced dental bridge, opting to wait until she could afford to have the bridge re-cemented at her own expense. However, due to severe pain and irreparable damage, she reluctantly underwent tooth extraction shortly afterward.

Stress, exacerbated by the challenges endured since the onset of the Syrian war, led Rola (Layal’s mother) to smoke heavily, which she acknowledged as the reason for her deteriorating oral health, besides the lack of calcium during pregnancy.

Rola shared with us her decision to refrain from extracting several damaged teeth. She was aware that these teeth could have been saved, but the necessary dental procedures to preserve them were neither covered by social welfare nor affordable for her. Instead, she opted to wait, hoping to gather the required funds for treatment. Unfortunately, over time, Rola’s teeth deteriorated further to the extent that they could no longer be reconstructed by any dental procedure. Eventually, Rola had no choice but to undergo extraction. Rola admitted that without the insistence of her daughter Layal, she would not have visited the dentist until she experienced severe pain. Her attitude while telling us her story indirectly revealed a blend of distrust, frustration, and dissatisfaction with her dental experiences in Switzerland.

A short while ago, I went to fix a dental bridge. I had originally got this bridge fixed in Turkey before coming here. In the first camp I was placed in, I was eating something like Turkish thick bread. Even though I had already fixed my bridge before coming, while chewing, it got stuck and came loose. I gave up trying to explain that I just needed them to cement it back in place. When they finally agreed to take me, the hospital immediately wanted to extract my tooth. I refused again and again, deciding to wait until I could afford to fix it properly. When the time was finally right, my teeth started hurting. My daughter, A, booked my appointment, she was the one who opened the door for me to get my teeth fixed. I went there, but it seemed like they were tired of trying to fix it and decided they had to remove my teeth. They performed an operation and extracted them. Now, I have no teeth here. I cannot eat, and I cannot… (Rola, Layal’s Mother, 47 years old, Basel-Stadt, Permit-N)

While managing her parents’ appointments and responding to familial needs, Layal experiences anxiety regarding dental appointments due to the absence of an interpreter, requiring her presence for translation. Her concern is particularly for her mother Rola, as Rola is coping with her mother’s cancer, her father’s situation in elderly care, and the care of her two siblings with mental disabilities.

I complained on behalf of my mom because we needed an interpreter, but they said there were no interpreters available at the dental hospital. This is a big problem for me. I used to go with my mom and dad to the hospitals whenever I didn’t have school and had free time. But dental appointments are mostly scheduled during school hours, so I have to send interpreters with them instead. (Layal)

Emerging theme: legal personhood

The two spotlight cases illustrate participants’ perceptions of the importance of legal status for access to DT. Salem’s story describes how his Permit-F (temporarily admitted person) affected his perceived OH from a state where he had “perfect teeth” to a state where he had lost all his teeth; then he was struggling with his denture, while feeling unheard and unacknowledged by the authorities and dental professionals. Layal and her mother’s story illustrate how her perception of OHC services evolved through various phases and locations of her migration journey. Layal held the responsibility of her family’s health and well-being, knowing that their asylum application was rejected and their rights for health care were reduced to the minimum.

According to the collected data, ASR in Switzerland are subject to the regulations of their respective cantons of residence and the local municipal authorities. These regulations may vary across different regions and even across different municipalities in the same region. Simultaneously, the entitlement to receive cost coverage for DT could vary depending on the location and the type of permit held by the ASR. The findings shed light on the diversity of DT coverage, which can be influenced by both the location of residence and the specific legal permits held by ASR. At the same time, ASR cannot choose their location of residence, neither canton nor commune.

Participants holding permits permitting travel outside Switzerland (B-permit) have expressed intentions to seek affordable DT services in other European countries; meanwhile, those with permits prohibiting travel outside Switzerland (F-permit, N-permit) are deprived of this option (see Table A3 Additional file 1). Participants perceived the disparity in rights between various permits as unfair, particularly when their dental treatment needs were not covered by welfare services and could be obtained at a significantly more affordable price in another country. For example, Salem (spotlight case 1) said the following describing how his permit restricted him from obtaining alternative solutions to fix his teeth:

From the perspective of the residency permit, my teeth are damaged, and I cannot travel to another country to get them fixed. My brother said, ‘Get permission to travel, and I will send you to Turkey or Hungary—wherever you go, I will send you money to fix your teeth.’ But here, they do not grant me this permission. (Salem)

Amin, a 30-year-old, shared his experience of traveling to Romania to seek dental treatment in order to avoid the unaffordable costs that he has been offered in Switzerland:

This was my first travel and it was for seeking a dentist. Honestly, they are very cheap. I am talking about treating all the caries and dental crowns, it would cost 2000 euro. In Switzerland, the dental implant would cost 4500 Swiss francs. I do have 3–4 teeth to be treated, and that would mount up to 20–25 thousand Swiss francs at least. I do not have this amount of money! (Amin, 30 years old male, B-permit, Basel-Stadt)

Amin received a professional qualification in Switzerland, then later had a full-time job, a car and good knowledge of the German language, which enabled him to obtain offers abroad.

Welfare pluralism and bureaucracy

Access to the formal welfare system is related to legal personhood (Theme 1). Swiss welfare services play a crucial role in facilitating access to DT for ASR at every stage of the immigration and migration process. The vast majority of study participants relied on social welfare for their income and were unaware of how dental treatments were covered in Switzerland until they gained knowledge about the Swiss OHC system through direct engagement with it. It is important to note that standard Swiss health insurance does not include coverage for adult OHC services. However, for financially dependent individuals receiving social welfare assistance, the system generally provides coverage for selected DT options.

The necessity of authorizing each dental visit and each DT and being unable to afford treatments other than extraction were seen by the participants as having less control of one’s own health (see also under Theme 3: restricted self-determination). The welfare system involves various stakeholders, which may differ across cantons. This diversity often limits the autonomy of ASR, as they are subjected to administrative protocols that strictly govern their access to DT.

The participants highlighted disparities in basic welfare service incomes between the two cantons, Basel-Stadt and Basel-Landschaft. They noted that in Basel-Stadt, not only is the monthly income higher, but residents also have access to many aid services from organizations that could significantly reduce their expenses. Additionally, participants living in canton Basel-Stadt enjoyed the benefit of reminder invitations for annual check-ups and professional dental cleaning covered by social welfare services, a privilege not extended to the participants living in canton Basel-Landschaft.

Overall, the participants exhibited a lack of understanding regarding the intersection of access to DT with welfare services. For instance, residents of Basel-Landschaft were uncertain whether they were permitted to seek DT at the university dental clinic, located in Basel-Stadt. Additionally, they expressed a desire for a deeper understanding of how the system operates and its flexibility regarding access to DT.

Hikamt, a 60-year-old woman who recently moved from Basel-Landschaft to Basel-Stadt after changing her Permit-F to Permit-B, described the dental challenges faced by herself and fellow refugees without sufficient funds.

The problem that we are suffering from, me and other refugees without enough money to repair their teeth, the tooth might need 400–500 franks or even 600 to treat it. The dentist says we cannot repair it for you as the commune does not pay more than 150. One time the commune wanted to help more, they paid me once 250. It depends on the case, form one person to another. For some they might refuse the treatment and for others they might accept it. We do not know on what basis they do that. (Hikamt (9 − 01), a 60-year-old female, recently moved from Basel-Landschaft to Basel-Stadt due to marriage, which also led to a change from Permit-F to Permit-B.)

In fact, narratives from two participants having the same legal status (permit), living in the same canton but in different communes varied in terms of their experiences in accessing DT (restorative and annual preventive measures) without them knowing the reason.

Restricted self-determination

When ASR perceive the need for DT (often prompted by pain or discomfort), they must notify their personally assigned social welfare worker. Subsequently, social welfare workers either issue a written authorization for ASR to present when booking an appointment or directly arrange the appointment themselves. Notably, our participants had no agency to help in selecting dental clinics, which may be a regulation imposed by social welfare. Approval from social welfare is mandatory for each appointment, failure of which leads to payment denial, placing the financial burden on the ASR.

Participants perceived the lack of autonomy in booking appointments, particularly in urgent situations such as severe dental pain, as a restrictive barrier. This limitation led to delayed access to DT and worsened OH outcomes. The participants expressed their inability to make independent decisions about their OH problems until they reached an emergency state. As a result, their OH deteriorated due to the postponed treatment. Moreover, some participants mentioned that restricting autonomy in accessing DT raises ethical concerns, as it undermines the fundamental principles of dignity and fairness in care delivery.

Nabila, a 42-year-old woman living in Basel-Landschaft with a Permit-F, recounts her struggle with severe tooth pain and the challenges she faced as she was not allowed to go to the dentist without permission from her social welfare worker.

When I first came here, almost a year ago, I had severe tooth pain. I informed social welfare, and they told me to go to a specific dental clinic, which I did. The dentist examined my tooth but did not remove it. I went back home, but since it was the weekend—Saturday and Sunday—the pain became unbelievably intense. On Monday, I went straight back to the same clinic, where they finally removed the tooth. The procedure cost 160 or 165 Swiss francs. However, the social welfare authority refused to cover the bill. They said, ‘We only pay if you inform us about the visit in advance. Since you did not, we are not responsible.’ But I had already informed them on a specific day, and yet the dentist did not remove the tooth. As a result, I suffered from unbearable pain over the weekend. (Nabila (7 − 01), 42 years old female, Basel-Landschaft, Permit-F)

The current study’s in-clinic observations indicated that social welfare covers the costs of fillings (only for teeth involved in occlusion) and extractions. Participants noted that root canal treatment expenses were not covered, with extraction being the alternative covered option. Due to their limited income, ASR often cannot afford the expenses of uncovered dental treatments. Many participants shared experiences of being in pain, unable to afford root canal treatment (removing infected tissue inside a tooth, cleaning and sealing it, relieving pain and saving the tooth), and consequently having to opt for extraction, the only covered affordable treatment option for their discomfort. Salem and his daughter Sara talked about that in the following:

I removed all my teeth. There is no chance to fix them here. The government does not cover it, it is expensive. (Salem)

They know how much it will cost me to rescue the tooth by treating the nerve (root canal treatment). I guess the social welfare try to avoid pricy treatments, so they accept removing the tooth and that is it. (Sara 4 − 01, Salem’s daughter, 26 years old, mother of one child, Basel-Stadt, Permit-F)

The participants perceived the option of extraction alone as unjust for several reasons. Mostly, newcomers to the country face a period of language acquisition and integration, often relying on welfare services’ income during this transitional phase. With limited resources, saving money for emergencies is not feasible, leaving borrowing money as the only recourse to cover dental expenses and avoid extraction.

Transcultural communication barriers

There were many discussions reflecting on communication challenges and opportunities during the interviews. The language barrier was mentioned by all our participants in different ways. Mostly, language knowledge was addressed as one of the major issues that affected communication with dentists; thus, interpretation services are needed.

In general, interpretation is not covered by the social welfare for dental visits. The interpretation service was offered for some participants in their first appointment following their arrival to Switzerland, organized by social welfare. It seemed that it was expected from the participants to start understanding the German language within months, but this was not the case for all of them. Despite having “so-perceived” enough language skills after a couple of years in Basel, we observed (during in-clinic observations) that the comprehension and facilitation of conversations in the dental clinic was not efficient. Misunderstandings in the dental clinic can have a range from simple to very complicated consequences, according to the participants’ narratives. One such consequence is the inability to distinguish between extraction as the only covered treatment option (due to structural financial constraints) and extraction as the only medically necessary option in a given case. One respondent (Sara) has told us her mother’s story, having had a tooth extracted:

My mother went once to a dentist and they did not understand each other, then the dentist extracted my mother’s tooth by mistake. The dentist did not understand what my mother wants, so she removed the tooth. And afterwards my mother cried a lot after realizing that her tooth is gone. (Sara 4 − 01, Salem’s daughter, 26 years old, mother for one child, Basel-Stadt, Permit-F)

Communication barriers were found to be multidimensional, given the cultural and social circumstances surrounding ASR. The participants wanted to be heard and to understand the diagnosis and the treatment options, even if the treatment options were very limited. The collected data indicated that more effort is required in communicating the diagnosis, treatment options and related problems.

There are times when we ask questions and the dentists answer very quickly and end the conversation. They do not deliver what they want to say. (Sara 4 − 01, Salem’s daughter, 26 years old, mother of one child, Basel-Stadt, Permit-F)

As an example from the in-clinic observation, when a dental problem caused by inadequate oral hygiene was diagnosed, the diagnosis was communicated without a thorough explanation of the possible causes. Perhaps dentists think that the ASR were aware of the ideal personal oral hygiene measures, but they were not. In fact, the majority of our respondents reported inadequate personal oral hygiene behavior, especially a notable lack of knowledge about interdental cleaning.

I noticed that all the caries points the dentist marked on his monitor were located between the teeth. However, the dentist did not mention this to the patient. So, I pointed out that either the patient is unaware that she needs to clean between her teeth, or there is an underlying issue in those areas that she is unaware of. In response, the dentist informed the patient that the caries were between her teeth, indicating inadequate cleaning in those areas. (First author’s in-clinic observation with Rima)

Layal (Spotlight case 2) shared a positive experience with Swiss dental care. Acknowledging the patient’s pain, careful listening and caring are expected from the dentist; otherwise, a gap starts to form between them during the treatment process, or even before it starts.

I like the way the dentists treat us, as some of them explain a bit more for the patient: what is going to happen, what do you want, to pay attention to their work. (Layal)

Emerging theme: social personhood

According to the current study, social and cultural dynamics appeared to influence access to DT, and the restricted access to DT could in turn influence socialization and other aspects of quality of life.

Our participants’ cultural fabric is woven with robust social bonds, where one can anticipate support from children, spouses, and parents. There is a sense of obligation to care for sick family members to the best of one’s ability. Friends and distant relatives also offer their assistance. The aid described by our participants ranges from arranging and coordinating dental appointments, providing interpretation during dental visits (related to Theme 3: Transcultural Communication Barriers), offering financial support, to giving recommendations and advice. However, the strong values placed by the participants on family support has led to misunderstandings and conflicts in dental clinics.

The participants described the high social value placed on having good-looking teeth and the emotional stress it can create. When appropriate DT was not accessible (for example, being offered only extractions instead of restorative treatments), visible tooth loss became a source of distress. This impacted the participants’ self-esteem, confidence, and social participation. Fear of judgment or ridicule, especially for participants wearing dentures or with broken or missing teeth, led to feelings of shame and pressure to hide their smile. Consequently, many participants withdrew socially and experienced emotional strain, sometimes leading to feeling depressed. The participants considered their teeth essential not only for eating and speaking but also for dignity, identity, and maintaining social connections.

Having problems with teeth affects mental health and can cause depression. If the person does not have the financial ability to repair his teeth or beautify them, he would feel ashamed about it and would not be able to reach out to friends. (Reda, 62 years old male, F-permit, Basel-Landschaft)

When extraction was the only option offered, some participants delayed treatment, hoping to afford alternatives such as root canal therapy, operative or prosthodontic treatments. Their conditions worsened over time, making extraction unavoidable. Others feared that any dental visit would lead to tooth loss (extraction), so they endured pain to avoid treatment altogether. This led to frustration, stress, and mistrust in the OHC system, causing many to delay/avoid care until the pain became intolerable.

If I have a tooth removed every time it hurts, I will end up with no teeth. (Nabila (7 − 01), 42 years old female, Basel-Land, Permit-F)

Negative experiences of the participants’ social network were frequently emphasized and appeared to shape their opinions about Swiss OHC services, often contributing to mistrust or dissatisfaction.

Discussion

The present study examined access to OHC services (particularly DT) among adult Arabic-speaking ASR in the Basel region of Switzerland and revealed a multifaceted interplay of structural, cultural, and individual factors that significantly influenced care-seeking behaviors and outcomes. These constraints erode the autonomy of ASR, forcing them to navigate OHC services within limited treatment options and bureaucratic hurdles. Emerging themes included legal personhood linked to the permit, welfare pluralism across cantons and communes, and the bureaucracy of navigating this system, leading to restricted self-determination. In addition, transcultural communication barriers and negative experiences related to OH limited the use of OHC services (including DT). Although an inductive approach was used, many of the themes that emerged are conceptually interrelated. These interconnections will be illustrated in the sections below.

ASR are especially vulnerable to poor OH due to factors related to their migration and socioeconomic conditions before and after migration [1, 16]. In Switzerland, DT for ASR focuses on pain relief and ensuring the ability to eat [23]. However, the specific treatments covered under social dentistry were not clearly outlined in the official federal documents [21] and could vary at the cantonal and communal levels and even vary based on the permit held by the ASR (Theme 1). By law, ASR must have basic health insurance to ensure access to medical care and emergency DT. However, ASR do not choose the canton to which they are assigned, and the cantonal authority chooses the health insurance for them and limits the choices of the health providers [44].

In the context of DT of ASR, social welfare workers and other professionals have a certain degree of discretion in the decision-making process in addition to the federal and cantonal legislations. Healthcare providers, social welfare workers, and administrators act as ‘street-level bureaucrats’ [45], with considerable power in determining access to DT in the absence of robust and uniform state regulations. Their decisions are often influenced by factors such as the permit status of the ASR (Theme 1), welfare approvals (Theme 2), and local cantonal regulations [21], which are balanced against each other. Examples of street-level bureaucrats from our study’s data include (a) dentists who decide what treatments to offer based on their assessment of the patient’s welfare status and expected compliance [33], (b) social welfare workers (enabled by the cantonal and communal regulations) who assess a person’s formal eligibility for care, and (c) clinic administrators who interpret and enforce bureaucratic rules regarding patient access [33]. This discretion can lead to inconsistent care, with ASR facing limitations in accessing non-emergency services (Theme 2).

Participants in the current study felt discouraged from going to dentists after facing challenges in accessing DT services. ASR who receive social welfare income must obtain authorization from social workers before accessing DT. Authorization determines whether appointments are approved, leaving ASR with little to no active role in choosing their dental provider and sometimes facing denial of cost coverage altogether (Themes 2 and 3). This finding partially aligns with the work of Due et al., who mentioned the need for transparent referral rules for subsidized OHC [46]. It also aligns with Kidane et al. and Riggs et al., who concluded that access to OHC services by ASR is highly bureaucratic in Germany and Australia [33, 47].

The strongest reaction observed was in response to the limited coverage by the welfare system, which only provides extraction as a treatment option for relieving toothache that could not be treated by fillings and/or root-canal treatments, which are more expensive than extraction. Financial constraints often cause reduced OHC services (including DT) seeking [46, 48]. In Switzerland, social welfare coverage is typically limited to extractions, essential fillings under certain conditions, or removable dentures to restore chewing function. We found that financial barriers and restrictive welfare policies limit the range of treatment options available and affordable to ASR (Themes 2 and 3), similar to the conclusion of the scoping review by Zinah et al. [16]. Moreover, this limited coverage reduces the ability of ASR to make autonomous decisions about their OH, as they are often left with no choice but to accept the available treatment options to relieve their pain, limiting their perceived control over their OH-related decisions. When treatments, such as extractions, are offered primarily due to financial limitations rather than medical necessity, feelings of low autonomy and reduced self-determination were found (Themes 2 and 3). This allocation of Swiss DT resources does not go along with the latest declaration of WHO, stating concerns on the unmet OH needs among vulnerable populations and that OH diseases must be treated promptly due to their effect on individuals’ public health [49]. It has been established that providing dental care to welfare recipients can significantly enhance employment prospects and support self-sufficiency, given that untreated oral disease may hinder workforce participation to a degree comparable with physical or mental health issues [50].

An additional side of the financial and bureaucratic constraints is the scarce availability of interpretation services (Theme 4), which leaves ASR reliant on family or peer help for communication with the dentist (Theme 5). Communication barriers compounded the structural issues mentioned above, with limited access to professional interpreter hindering informed decision-making and engagement. At dental clinics, we observed that ASR face significant communication challenges with receptionists, nurses, and dentists due to language barriers (Theme 4). In agreement with Wainman et al. and Kidane et al., we found that the lack of professional interpreters further complicates the situation, preventing ASR from fully understanding their care options or the procedures being (or will be) undertaken [33, 51]. This lack of communication impacts the ability of ASR to make informed decisions and diminishes their health literacy, which in turn affects their confidence and competence in managing their OH (Theme 4) [44]. Although the “right to be understood” was recommended in the context of access to healthcare, there is no specific Swiss legislation obligating health facilities to organize or finance interpretation services when needed [44]. In fact, we found that family and friends often helped out by interpreting during dental visits and managing appointments, facilitating access of ASR to DT (Theme 5), which complements earlier research about the role of family and peers in providing support while seeking care [46, 51].

Family and peer support can enhance the patient’s ability to navigate the healthcare system by increasing their self-efficacy, encouraging them to seek care, and offering emotional reassurance [34, 46]. However, we have noticed that the negative experiences of social networks may limit overall trust and engagement with OH service providers, not only during dental treatments (e.g., attending to professional hygiene and adherence with the advice). This dynamic can influence the acceptability of services, as experiences made within social networks can either facilitate or hinder a person’s willingness to seek and continue care (Theme 5). In fact, Wainman et al. mentioned that ASR community members shared information and supported each other in accessing OHC services [51]. Our findings agree with the conclusion of Wainman et al., who added a broader view on community support as a potential barrier if negative experiences were shared.

For individuals who cannot afford a medically feasible tooth restoration or replacement, extraction becomes the only option, which means permanent tooth loss. Recognized refugees (with legal permits or status) may seek DT in other countries to avoid tooth extraction, as mentioned by Nurelhuda et al. in a Canadian study [48], but many ASR lack the necessary permits to travel (Theme 1). Even when nearby cross-border DT is possible, it should never be a primary solution due to treatment quality uncertainties, lack of follow-up care, and logistical challenges.

ASR who undergo extractions might unwillingly develop hesitancy toward future care seeking, not only concerning DT but also OHC services, including OH education, OH promotion, and preventive measures, reflecting a cycle of avoidance and worsening OH (see Fig. 1). Our finding resonates with the conclusion of Due et al. on the effect of past experiences with OHC services on the ‘Future’s help-seeking’ among middle eastern ASR [46]. Previous research in other regions also confirmed a resulting erosion of both their willingness to engage with healthcare services and their ability to navigate healthcare systems [34, 46, 52].

Conversely, those who delay care in hopes of gathering financial assets or travel permissions face heightened risks of severe dental issues (Theme 5). This points to the relevance of systemic barriers, such as the inability to pay for the treatment or lack of permission or financial assets to travel, exacerbating health inequities, limiting access to care. Patients might delay care without recognizing the urgency of their OH needs due to restricted access to DT, leading to poorer OH outcomes. In agreement with previous research, such experiences illustrate the broader consequence of restricted access, as poor OH significantly diminishes the overall quality of life by impacting oral health-related quality of life such as dietary choices, socialization, and physical and psychological well-being [30, 53].

The present study showed that ASR delayed care to avoid tooth extraction. This reluctance to undergo extraction extended beyond concerns about loss of tooth function and reflected a deeper fear of social stigma due to visible tooth loss. This could be explained by the oral health-related stigma of Daughty et al., who concluded that compromised dental appearance could be perceived as an indicator of self-neglect and/or lower social status [54]. This fear of negative judgment could explain the avoidance of seeking OHC and avoiding extraction even when significant dental pain is experienced. Stigma (feelings of exclusion and being unfairly judged) was found to be attributed to barriers in systems that do not support peoples’ needs [55] and cause health inequality [56]. According to our data, avoiding stigma caused by extraction was often an attempt to preserve not only oral function but also dignity, identity, and social acceptability (Theme 5).

In conclusion, extraction was not perceived by many participants as a choice but as a necessity driven by structural constraints (particularly in emergency situations where other medically feasible, tooth-preserving DT were not covered by the welfare system). This lack of agency reduces their trust in Swiss OH services, making them more likely to disengage from care and even disregard OH promotion advice.

Levesque’s access to care model

While the Levesque framework [34] played an important role in our study (Fig. 1) by providing a comprehensive look at access to DT, the themes that emerged in our interviews were more directly related to structural factors that shaped Individual abilities [34]. According to our findings, four structural dimensions of Levesque were compromised: approachability, availability, affordability, and appropriateness. Correspondingly, all individual abilities were also hindered (ability to perceive, seek, reach, pay, and engage).

Levesque’s model lacked the consideration of compromised self-determination, as ASR could struggle to manage their OH within the constraints of the system. Additionally, Levesque’s model did not address the relationship between restricted access and the first step of future access. Therefore, our analysis proposes the following possible vicious cycle (Fig. 1): limited access DT leads to extraction being the only treatment offered, and the negative experience of extraction then discourages ASR from seeking timely care in the future, leading to further deterioration of OH.

In conclusion, the structural barriers were so pronounced that they left no room to support or enhance these individual abilities, thereby perpetuating a cycle of limited access and disempowerment. Barriers such as permit restrictions, financial dependence, and limited decision-making power do not just slow down access but also take away the ability of ASR to recognize when they need care, seek care, and actively engage in their own OHC decisions. These real and lived challenges are not fully captured by the model alone and overlooking them could risk missing the experience of ASR with OHC.

Limitations

The analyses in this study were specific to a particular geographic location and socio-cultural background, limiting the generalizability of the findings to other contexts. Additionally, participants may have withheld some information due to the potential impact of past or ongoing trauma. Given that the first author conducted the interviews and was known by them to be a dentist, participants might have provided responses they believed were socially desirable or aligned with her professional role. Furthermore, it is possible that individuals with unique experiences regarding access to DT were more motivated to participate, which could have resulted in a sampling bias, potentially excluding insights from those with either unremarkable experiences or extreme cases of dental trauma. Additionally, the use of a snowball sampling technique may have introduced selection bias, as participants were recruited through referrals. This method can limit the diversity of the sample by involving individuals who are connected or share similar characteristics, thereby potentially restricting the range of perspectives and experiences captured in the study.

Conclusion

This study highlights the multifaceted barriers faced by ASR in accessing DT in Switzerland, including bureaucratic complexities, limited autonomy, and communication barriers. While these findings highlight the need for clearer social welfare guidelines on coverage and improved communication, they also underscore the importance of addressing structural limitations in DT coverage. Participants’ narratives suggest that empowering ASR to make informed decisions and access preventive, tooth-preserving treatments may improve their engagement with OHC. Expanding access to affordable options, where available, could be explored further to avoid unnecessary extractions. Future research should examine how health system policies and coverage schemes influence equitable access to OHC among marginalized populations.
