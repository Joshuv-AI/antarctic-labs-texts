Prognostic value of distance–saturation product in COPD: Korean nationwide cohort study
Authors: Tai Joon An, Chin Kook Rhee, Tae-Hyung Kim, Deog Kyeom Kim, Yong-Il Hwang, Seung Won Ra, Shinhee Park, Yu-il Kim, Kwang Ha Yoo, Seong Yong Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe distance–saturation product (DSP), calculated by multiplying 6-min walk distance (6MWD) by lowest oxygen saturation, is a composite physiological marker of exercise capacity and gas exchange impairment. Although DSP has shown prognostic potential in Western COPD cohorts, its utility and optimal threshold in Asian populations remain unestablished. This study evaluated whether DSP <290 m·% predicts exacerbations and mortality in COPD, compared with the BODE index.MethodsWe analysed 1995 patients with valid 6MWD data from the Korean COPD Subgroup Study, a nationwide prospective cohort. DSP was dichotomised at 290 m·% based on restricted cubic spline analyses. Primary outcomes were 3-year acute exacerbations (AEs) and all-cause mortality. Associations were evaluated using multivariable logistic, negative binomial and Cox regression models, with the BODE index as comparator.ResultsPatients with DSP <290 m·% had more severe symptoms, worse airflow limitation and more frequent prior-year AEs. Low DSP was independently associated with more frequent severe (adjusted incidence rate ratios (aIRRs) 2.27–2.54) and moderate (aIRRs 1.42–1.52) exacerbations, with significantly increased moderate AE risk observed only in year 1 (aOR 1.60; 95% CI 1.05–2.45). Patients with low DSP also had higher 3-year mortality (9.2% versus 2.6%, p<0.001), with a significantly increased risk of death (adjusted hazard ratio 2.74; 95% CI 1.68–4.45). DSP-based models demonstrated superior discrimination for 3-year mortality (area under the curve (AUC) 0.673) compared to the BODE index (AUC 0.636).ConclusionsDSP is a simple, scalable prognostic marker in COPD patients. A threshold of 290 m·% identifies patients at higher risk of exacerbations and mortality.

Introduction

COPD is a chronic inflammatory airway disease characterised by persistent airflow limitation [1]. It is a major public health burden, ranked as the third leading cause of death globally [2, 3]. COPD significantly impairs patients’ quality of life, particularly among older adults and even among the economically active population, by limiting physical activity and daily functioning due to dyspnoea and exercise intolerance [4, 5]. Therefore, a valuable tool for risk stratification remains a critical unmet need in COPD, particularly reflecting individual dyspnoea severity, exercise performance and quality of life.

Assessment of exercise performance is a key component in evaluating COPD severity and predicting clinical outcomes such as exacerbations and mortality [6]. The 6-min walk test (6MWT) is widely used in clinical and research settings to assess functional capacity, with the distance walked (6MWD) serving as a core parameter [7, 8]. The BODE index, which incorporates 6MWD, body mass index (BMI), airflow obstruction and dyspnoea scale, is a well-validated multidimensional tool for mortality prediction in COPD [9].

Nevertheless, one of the most important limitations of 6MWD is that it does not fully capture other physiological impairments such as exertional desaturation, which has been independently associated with adverse outcomes in COPD [10]. To address this limitation, the distance–saturation product (DSP)—a novel composite marker combining the 6MWD with the lowest oxygen saturation during the 6MWT—was first introduced in 2006, demonstrating its potential to better stratify mortality risk compared to distance or desaturation alone in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [11].

Subsequently, the ECLIPSE study extended the application of DSP to a large, multinational COPD cohort composed entirely of Western populations [7]. Although DSP is a simple, integrative, useful composite index and severe smaller studies have reported lower DSP values are associated with worse outcomes including increased mortality and exacerbation risk, universal cut-off value is not yet widely established and needs further validation in large COPD cohorts before widespread clinical application [7, 12].

To address this gap, we evaluated the prognostic value and clinical utility of DSP in a large Korean COPD cohort—the largest single-country study to date using DSP, and the first to investigate its relevance in a racially homogeneous Asian population.

Methods

Study population and data source

This study utilised 3-year longitudinal data from Korean COPD Subgroup Study (KOCOSS), a nationwide, prospective observational cohort initiated in 2012 and conducted across 60 referral centres in South Korea, with data collection at 6-month intervals (ClinicalTrials.gov ID CT02800499) [13–18]. Participants were eligible if they were aged ≥40 years, had a post-bronchodilator (BD) forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity ratio <0.7, and reported respiratory symptoms. Exclusion criteria included: inability to perform spirometry, a history of myocardial infarction or cerebrovascular event within 3 months, current pregnancy, rheumatoid diseases, active malignancy or use of systemic corticosteroids (prednisolone-equivalent dose 10 mg·day−1 or more) within the past month. Additionally, individuals lacking valid data on 6MWT distance or oxygen saturation were excluded from the analysis. Among the 3459 participants, 1995 who had both 6MWT distance and oxygen saturation measurements were included in the analysis (figure 1).

Schematic flow of study. Among a total of 3459 participants in the Korean COPD Subgroup Study cohort, 1995 patients with complete data on the 6-min walk test (6MWT) and the lowest oxygen saturation during the test were included in the analysis. Participants were divided into two groups based on the distance–saturation product (DSP) threshold of 290 m·%: 448 patients had a DSP <290 m·%, and 1547 had a DSP ≥290 m·%. SpO2: peripheral oxygen saturation.

Definition of DSP and summary of study variables

The 6MWT was performed according to the American Thoracic Society guideline [19]. The DSP was calculated by multiplying the 6MWD (in metres) by the lowest oxygen saturation (%) during the 6MWT and dividing the result by 100 [20]. The distribution of DSP values across the cohort is shown in supplementary figure S1. To determine an optimal threshold for outcome stratification, DSP was first analysed as a continuous variable, and its nonlinear relationship with clinical outcomes was assessed using restricted cubic spline modelling (figure 2). We subsequently performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses to evaluate the discriminative performance of candidate cut-off points—290, 310, 320 and 340 m·%—based on prior literature and exploratory analysis (supplementary table S1). Among these, the 290 m·% threshold consistently demonstrated the robust association with clinical outcomes, including exacerbations and mortality. Therefore, we classified DSP into binary categories: Low DSP (<290 m·%) versus High DSP (≥290 m·%).

Nonparametric analysis of distance–saturation product (DSP) and clinical outcomes. DSP was calculated as (6MWD×lowest SpO2)/100. Its nonlinear association with clinical outcomes was evaluated using restricted cubic spline analysis. 6MWD: 6-min walking distance; SpO2: peripheral oxygen saturation; AEs: acute exacerbations.

Study variables included: 1) demographic and clinical characteristics—age, sex, smoking status, BMI categories (<18.5, 18.5–22.9, 23.0–24.9, and ≥25 kg·m−2) and history of heart disease; and 2) COPD-related indicators—blood eosinophil count (BEC >300 cells·μL−1), inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) use, Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) grade, history of prior-year acute exacerbations (AEs), COPD Assessment Test (CAT) score and St. George's Respiratory Questionnaire for COPD (SGRQ-C).

Study outcomes

The primary outcomes were the association between DSP group and 1) AEs and 2) all-cause mortality over a 3-year period. AEs were defined as an acute worsening of respiratory symptoms—such as increased cough, sputum production or dyspnoea—compared with the patient's baseline condition, requiring additional treatment [21, 22]. Moderate AEs, defined as events managed with systemic corticosteroids or antibiotics in an outpatient setting, and severe AEs, requiring emergency department visits or hospitalisation, were classified according to the GOLD guidelines [23]. Secondary outcomes included longitudinal changes in post-BD FEV1, CAT scores and SGRQ-C scores [24–26].

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables were compared using t-test and chi-squared test, respectively. Longitudinal changes in FEV1 (% predicted), CAT and SGRQ-C scores over 3 years were analysed using linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts and slopes, assuming data were missing at random. Associations between DSP and AEs were assessed using multivariable logistic regression for AE risks, yielding adjusted odds ratios (aORs), and negative binomial regression for AE frequencies, yielding adjusted incidence rate ratios (aIRRs). All logistic and negative binomial models were adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, BMI category, history of heart disease, BEC (≥300 cells·μL−1), ICS use, prior-year exacerbations and GOLD stage. Survival analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests for group comparisons. Cox proportional hazards models were fitted using the following covariates (age, sex, smoking status, BMI category, BEC, GOLD stage) to avoid model overfitting. Discriminative performance of DSP and the BODE index was compared using ROC curves and DeLong's test. DSP quartiles were generated by dividing the distribution of DSP values into four equal-size groups (Q1–Q4). Quartile-based subgroup analyses were then conducted to evaluate graded associations. All statistical tests were two-sided with a significance threshold of p<0.05. Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.3; https://www.r-project.org).

Ethical approval

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study enrolment, and strict confidentiality of personal data was maintained throughout the study. The study protocol for KOCOSS was reviewed and approved by the institutional review boards of all participating centres.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Patients with lower DSP (<290 m·%) were significantly older (mean age 71.3 versus 67.9 years, p<0.001) and had a higher proportion of underweight individuals (BMI group <18.5 kg·m−2: 12.9% versus 7.4%) compared to those with DSP ≥290 m·% (table 1). They also exhibited more severe airflow limitation (GOLD stage 3–4: 39.7% versus 30.0%, p<0.001), greater symptom burden (CAT baseline: 16.6 versus 13.4, p<0.001; SGRQ-C baseline: 38.1 versus 28.7, p<0.001), and more frequent prior-year exacerbations (28.3% versus 19.9%, p=0.004). Furthermore, they experienced significantly more moderate and severe AEs over the 3-year follow-up period (all p<0.01), as well as a markedly higher all-cause mortality (9.2% versus 2.6%, p<0.001). In contrast, variables such as sex, underlying heart disease, BEC >300 cells·μL−1 and baseline ICS use were not significantly different between DSP groups (all p>0.05).

Trajectories of lung function and symptom scores by DSP group

Figure 3a illustrates the longitudinal trajectories of FEV1 (% predicted), CAT and SGRQ-C scores over 3 years, stratified by DSP group using the 290 m·% cut-off. There were no statistically significant differences in the annual changes of FEV1, CAT or SGRQ-C scores between the two groups (FEV1: 0.73 versus 0.45; CAT: −0.06 versus −0.16; SGRQ-C: −0.57 versus −0.39; all p>0.05), despite consistently worse absolute scores in the DSP <290 m·% group across all timepoints (all p<0.05). These findings reflect baseline separation rather than divergent trajectories and were reproduced in quartile-based analyses (figure 3b, supplementary table S2).

1-, 2-, and 3-year exacerbations according to DSP group

Unmatched logistic and negative binomial regression analysis for risk and frequency of AEs demonstrated increased OR and IRR in most cases for the DSP Low group (table 2). After adjustment, the DSP Low group demonstrated significantly higher risk and frequency of severe exacerbations in all years except year 1 (aORs 2.30–2.76; aIRRs 2.27–2.54; all p<0.01). For moderate exacerbations, the DSP Low group also showed a consistently higher frequency across all years (aIRRs, 1.42–1.52; all p<0.05), although the moderate AE risk was significantly increased only in year 1 (aOR 1.60; 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.05–2.45; p=0.030). In subgroup analysis, Cochran–Armitage trend analysis revealed significant dose–response relationships across DSP groups (>340 m·%, 290–340 m·% and <290 m·%) for both moderate and severe exacerbations over 1, 2 and 3 years, as well as 3-year mortality (figure 4, all p for trend <0.01).

3-year survival outcomes according to DSP group

Kaplan–Meier survival curves revealed significantly higher 3-year mortality in the DSP <290 m·% group compared to the DSP >290 m·% group (log-rank p<0.001) (figure 5a). In multivariable Cox regression models, the low DSP group remained independently associated with increased 3-year mortality (adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) 2.74; 95% CI 1.68–4.45; p<0.001). Quartile-based analysis showed consistent trends, with stepwise decrease in mortality risk across increasing DSP quartiles (figure 5b). Compared to the lowest quartile (Q1), the risk of 3-year mortality was significantly lower in Q2 (aHR 0.45; 95% CI 0.25–0.82), Q3 (aHR 0.32; 95% CI 0.15–0.67) and Q4 (aHR 0.31; 95% CI 0.14–0.68). The model demonstrated good discriminatory performance (C-index=0.741) and was statistically significant overall (likelihood ratio test p<0.001).

Prognostic utility of the DSP versus BODE index

In the prediction of 3-year mortality, both DSP-based models—using a binary threshold of 290 m·% (area under the curve (AUC)=0.646) and quartile classification (AUC=0.673)—showed higher performance than the traditional BODE index (AUC=0.636), although the differences were not statistically significant. In contrast, the BODE index consistently showed significantly higher performance for predicting moderate and severe exacerbations (AUC 0.663∼0.735), whereas DSP-based models yielded modest performance (0.529∼0.641) (table 3, supplementary table S3).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that the DSP, a composite metric derived from exercise performance and oxygen desaturation during the 6MWT, is significantly associated with key clinical outcomes in patients with COPD. Our findings suggest that patients with low DSP values are consistently at increased risk of exacerbations, higher symptom burden and long-term mortality, highlighting the prognostic importance of this easily obtainable physiological marker. These associations remained robust after adjustment for key clinical and physiological covariates. Our results provide robust evidence, within a large-scale, single-nation and single-ethnicity cohort, that DSP not only reflects exercise limitation but also integrates the burden of pulmonary gas exchange impairment and systemic disease status.

From a pathophysiological perspective, DSP reflects two core components of COPD-related impairment: exercise intolerance and gas exchange inefficiency [11]. The former may result from airflow limitation, dynamic hyperinflation, ventilatory inefficiency, cardiovascular dysfunction, sarcopenia or frailty [27, 28]. The latter often results from ventilation–perfusion mismatch, loss of alveolar–capillary interface or pulmonary vascular abnormalities [29]. By integrating walking distance and oxygen desaturation into a single measure, DSP mitigates the confounding between exercise performance and gas exchange limitations, offering a more comprehensive representation of physiological burden than either metric alone. Importantly, because DSP captures both distance and desaturation burden, a low DSP may also reflect reduced exercise adherence, positioning DSP as a meaningful target or monitoring metric in pulmonary rehabilitation. In our cohort, a cut-off value of 290 m·% consistently distinguished patients at higher risk, showing the strongest associations with severe outcomes such as 3-year mortality and hospitalisation, whereas its influence on medication changes was comparatively modest. This consistent and robust prognostic power suggests that DSP may serve as a practical surrogate marker of survival. These findings reinforce the clinical relevance of low DSP (<290 m·%) as a scalable risk stratification tool in real-world COPD management.

Our study offers several strengths and novel insights about DSP. First, our study not only supports but also extends previous findings from the ECLIPSE cohort, which reported 290 m·% and 310 m·% as respective thresholds for mortality and hospitalisation. In contrast, within our large-scale, single-nation and single-ethnicity Korean COPD cohort, a single threshold of 290 m·% consistently demonstrated the strongest prognostic values across outcomes including mortality and exacerbations. This divergence may reflect ethnic and clinical differences, such as lower BMI and lower cardiovascular comorbidity. Importantly, our results revalidate the prognostic value of the 290 m·% DSP thresholds across diverse clinical outcomes. Second, we compared DSP to the widely accepted BODE index and found that DSP quartile classification showed higher predictive power for 3-year mortality (AUC 0.673 versus 0.636), whereas BODE better predicted exacerbations. This finding highlights DSP's unique ability to reflect physiological reserve, integrating both exercise limitation and gas exchange inefficiency—dimensions that are not inadequately captured by the BODE index. Third, our trajectory analyses provided clinically intuitive insights. Patients with low DSP (<290 m·%) exhibited consistently worse FEV1, CAT and SGRQ-C scores across all timepoints, indicating a persistently greater symptom burden and lower functional capacity. Importantly, the annual rate of decline did not differ significantly, reinforcing that baseline DSP reflects an overall poorer long-term clinical trajectory, rather than faster deterioration. This highlights DSP's potential to identify individuals at risk of sustained poor quality of life, even before accelerated decline is evident. Fourth, our study benefits from high internal validity, leveraging a well-characterised, prospective multicentre cohort with standardised 6MWT protocols and long-term follow-up. We employed a range of statistical approaches—including logistic regression, negative binomial modelling and Cox proportional hazards analysis—to rigorously evaluate the prognostic value of DSP across diverse clinical outcomes. Furthermore, our use of quartile-based analysis enhanced interpretability by capturing a stepwise gradient of risk. These findings validate DSP as a continuous and scalable biomarker of physiological reserve, applicable beyond binary thresholds.

This study has several limitations. First, the cohort predominantly consisted of male patients, reflecting the high male prevalence of COPD in Korea due to low smoking rates among women. While this may limit generalisability to other populations, the homogeneity of a single-nation, single-ethnicity cohort enhances internal validity and reduces variability in interpreting DSP. Second, our analysis was based on baseline DSP measurements, without accounting for post-baseline changes or interventions such as pulmonary rehabilitation or initiation of long-term oxygen therapy. Future studies should investigate the responsiveness of DSP to treatment and its utility in tracking disease progression or therapeutic response over time.

Conclusion

The DSP is a simple, low-cost marker derived from the 6MWT that effectively integrates exercise performance and gas exchange. Despite its simplicity, DSP showed strong and consistent associations with mortality and long-term symptom burden. These findings support its use as a practical and scalable prognostic tool in routine COPD care. Future prospective studies should explore whether DSP-guided pharmacological and nonpharmacological interventions improves COPD outcomes.
