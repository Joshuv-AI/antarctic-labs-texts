Solvent‐Driven Conformational Landscapes of a Tetradentate Schiff Base Ligand and Its Metal Complexes: A Genetic Algorithm–Guided VCD Investigation
Authors: Amanda Nhi Tran, Mutasem Alshalalfeh, Colton D. Carlson, Andra Aniţa Radu, Vlad Ţeposu, Joseph Cheramy, Lili Zeng, Hui Zhang, Valentin Paul Nicu, Yunjie Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChiral salen ligands are a versatile platform for asymmetric catalysis, where understanding their conformational preferences is key to optimizing the stereo‐inductive performance of their metal complexes. Here, we examine the conformational landscapes of (R)/(S)‐N,N’‐bis(3,5‐di‐tert‐butylsalicylidene)‐1‐methyldiamine ligand and its Cu(II) and Ni(II) complexes in CDCl3 solution using vibrational circular dichroism (VCD) and infrared (IR) spectroscopy, supported by extensive conformational searches and DFT calculations. Contrary to its benign reputation, CDCl3 profoundly alters the ligand conformational landscape while leaving its metal complexes largely unaffected. This drastic yet selective solvent effect, missed by IR but captured by VCD, is interpreted using a genetic algorithm (GA) approach. Microsolvation modeling with Grimme's quantum cluster growth program reveals how weak solute‐solvent van der Waals (vdW) interactions reshape conformational landscapes in CDCl3. The challenges in predicting the outcome of the delicate balance between intra‐solute and solute‐solvent vdW interactions are discussed in the context of current solvation models.

Introduction

Salen ligands represent a major class of ligands widely employed in asymmetric catalysis. Their metal complexes are known for their ability to induce near‐perfect enantio‐ and diastereoselectivity across a wide range of catalytic transformations [1, 2]. Since the pioneering work of Jacobsen in the 1990s [3], chiral salen metal complexes have found widespread application across numerous key asymmetric transformations, as extensively reviewed over the years [1, 2, 4]. The steric and electronic properties of the salen ligand, along with the conformations adopted by both the free ligand and the metal complexes, are widely regarded as key determinants of the stereochemical outcomes [1]. However, detailed stereochemical characterization and conformational studies of these systems in solution remain limited [1]. This gap in mechanistic insight arises in part from the challenge of accessing detailed conformational and stereochemical information under solution conditions.

Significant advances in both experiment and theory in the past two decades have established vibrational optical activity (VOA) spectroscopy—including vibrational circular dichroism (VCD) and Raman optical activity (ROA) [5, 6]—as a powerful tool for investigating chiral phenomena in solution. The fingerprint region typically contains a large number of vibrational bands, and the corresponding VCD and ROA signatures are highly sensitive to subtle perturbations, leading to, for example, discovery of new chiral Raman spectroscopy [7, 8, 9] and of chirality transfer and amplification mechanisms in atomically‐precise metal clusters [10, 11]. One main application of VOA is in the determination of absolute configuration (AC) and conformational landscapes across a broad range of chiral molecular systems, including transition metal complexes [12, 13, 14].

In this work, we investigate the conformational landscapes of a pair of enantiomers of a flexible chiral salen‐type ligand, namely (R)‐ and (S)‐N,N’‐Bis(3,5‐di‐tert‐butylsalicylidene)‐1‐methyldiamine (abbreviated as PN thereafter) and their metal complexes with Cu(II) and Ni(II) (abbreviated as Cu(II)‐PN and Ni(II)‐PN, respectively) using a combination of infrared (IR) and VCD spectroscopies, aided by extensive conformational searches and theoretical calculations. ROA measurements were attempted, but high fluorescence prevented further investigation. The structural formulae of the (R)‐PN ligand and its metal complexes are shown in Scheme 1. The PN ligand closely resembles a previously studied classic Jacobsen ligand using VCD [15], except that the cyclohexyl bridge is now replaced with the methyl diamine backbone. This structural modification allows considerably more conformational flexibility, offering an opportunity to investigate the conformational landscapes of the PN ligand, as well as the Cu(II) and Ni(II) complexes, since it was previously reported that the rigidity of the cyclohexyl bridge imposes a specific metal helicity preference in the resulting metal complexes [15].

The molecular formula of the (R)‐PN ligand and its metal complexes, Ni(II)‐(R)‐PN and Cu(II)‐(R)‐PN. t Bu indicates the tert‐butyl group.

The standard VCD study protocol typically involves (1) systematically searching for low‐energy conformational candidates, (2) optimizing candidate geometries using density functional theory (DFT) to identify low‐energy minima, and (3) simulating IR and VCD spectra based on a Boltzmann‐weighted average of the conformer ensemble for comparison with experiment. As elaborated in the current investigation, this approach failed miserably for PN, highlighting the need for an alternative strategy. We therefore applied a recently developed genetic algorithm (GA)‐based refinement [16, 17, 18] that integrates both energetic and spectral information, enabling an experimentally guided determination of AC and conformer populations.

Another focus of this work is to understand why even a solvent generally regarded as benign, such as CDCl3, can alter the conformational landscape of PN so drastically. In solution, only species stabilized by strong non‐covalent interactions (NCIs) have typically been identified based on their distinctive VCD signatures, for example, carboxylic acid dimers [19], dihydrogen‐bonded amine–borane complexes [20], and small hydrated clusters [21, 22]. Their investigation led to the development of the simplified “clusters‐in‐a‐liquid” model of solvation [23]. A recent perspective extended this model by introducing more solvent molecules at binding sites in a microsolvation approach [24], while noting that CDCl3 is usually considered a noninteracting solvent. Although ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) approaches for VCD have been reported [25, 26], their applications remain limited. Current implementations are computationally demanding and do not reliably capture heavy‐atom conformational changes, particularly those governed by weak vdW interactions (vide infra). Generally, vdW interactions, including dispersion forces, though weak and nonspecific, are omnipresent. Their collective roles in shaping conformational preferences in solution and influencing chemical outcomes are increasingly recognized but remain poorly understood [27], making a deeper investigation both timely and urgent. Overall, the synergy among experimental, theoretical, and data‐driven methods underscores the power of an integrated approach in addressing persistent challenges in solvation modeling and conformational weighting in VCD analyses.

Following the Standard VCD Protocol

All experimental syntheses and measurement details are summarized in Point S1. For consistency, all calculations, experimental comparisons, and discussions herein are based on the (R)‐enantiomer of the PN ligand in its free form and the corresponding metal complexes unless otherwise specified.

Four initial PN conformers were generated by chemical intuition by varying the bridge N–C–C–N dihedral angle, as highlighted below in Figure 1, leading to four optimized structures. The results of the initial studies, including the optimized geometries, calculation details, and IR/VCD spectral analyses, are summarized in Point S2. Similar to the related (R,R)‐bis(pyrrol‐2‐ylmethyleneamine)‐cyclohexane (chxn1) and (R,R)‐N,N’‐Bis(3,5‐di‐tert‐butylsalicylidene)‐1,2‐cyclohexanediamine (chxn2) ligands [15, 28], only one heavy‐atom conformation was identified within a free energy window of 10 kJ mol−1. This global minimum (Figure S2.1) was predicted to account for nearly 100% of the Boltzmann population at room temperature based on relative free energies. However, while the simulated IR spectrum based on this predicted Boltzmann average agreed well with experiment, the corresponding VCD spectrum failed to reproduce most major experimental features (Figure S2.2). This initial failure caused some confusion and motivated extensive and systematic conformational searches for both the free ligand and its metal complexes (vide infra). Interestingly, the most stable conformer identified in the preliminary stage remains the global minimum after the exhaustive search, hinting at other causes for the discrepancy observed.

Systematic Conformational Searches of the PN Ligand

The PN ligand possesses considerable degrees of freedom arising from its seven rotatable single bonds associated with the heavy atoms, and the corresponding key dihedral angles associated with these rotations are shown in Figure 1. Of these, three dihedral angles (bridge, cc1, and cc2) determine the relative orientation of the ligand's main groups, while four dihedral angles (b11, b12, b21, and b22) define the orientation of the tert‐butyl groups. In addition to the heavy‐atom dihedral angles outlined above, the rotation of the phenolic O─H group about the C–O bond also constitutes a formal degree of freedom, which is subsequently explored in the conformational search. All theoretical details regarding conformational searches and final DFT calculations are presented in Point S3, while the main points are mentioned below.

To systematically sample the conformational space and comprehensively identify the low‐energy conformational candidates, we utilized CREST (Conformer–Rotamer Ensemble Sampling Tool), a powerful tool for efficient and automated exploration of molecular chemical space [29]. CREST has been successfully applied and benchmarked in rotational spectroscopic studies of a wide range of molecules and aggregates [30, 31], including those only differing subtly in OH orientations [32, 33] and, more recently, in several chiroptical studies of flexible molecules [22, 34, 35]. Briefly, four initial configurations of the PN ligand, differing in the N–C–C–N dihedral angle and corresponding heavy‐atom conformations (Figure S2.1), were used as input geometries for the CREST conformational searches at the GFN2‐xTB level. In parallel, a force field conformational search using MMFF94 [36] with standard sampling parameters as implemented in MacroModel [37] was also performed. In total, 10,719 CREST and 256 force field trial candidates were identified. Additional intermediate screening steps [38] were implemented to refine the combined ensemble and reduce redundancy in the conformational output.

Similarly, the conformational searches for the metal complexes were carried out with CREST. 129 Cu(II)‐(R)‐PN and 58 Ni(II)‐(R)‐PN candidates were identified at the GFN2‐xTB level. The substantially larger number of structural candidates obtained for Cu(II) compared to Ni(II), even with the same ligand backbone, likely arises from the open‐shell d9 electronic configuration of Cu(II), which introduces an unpaired electron and contributes to a more complex potential energy surface. Similar observations have been reported for chxn2 complexes, where open‐shell transition metal centres such as Co(II), Co(III), and Mn(III) yielded a greater number of candidates in the conformational search than the closed‐shell Ni(II) counterpart [15, 39].

Final geometry optimizations and harmonic frequency calculations for IR and VCD were performed at the B3LYP [40]‐D3 [41] (BJ) [42]/6‐311++G(d,p)/PCM(CDCl3) [43] level. Overall, 114, 8, and 13 true minima were identified for PN ligand, Cu(II)‐(R)‐PN, and Ni(II)‐R)‐PN complexes, respectively. The final set of optimizations was performed with the Gaussian 16 (G16, revision C.01) suite of programs [44] with tight convergence criteria and a superfine numerical integration grid. The Cartesian coordinates of all identified minima, labelled in the order of increasing electronic energies, are included as three ZIP files for the three systems studied in the Supporting Information.

NCIs were analyzed using Weitao Yang's Reduced Density Gradient (RDG) method, [45] also known as NCI analysis. RDG and sign(λ2)ρ cube files were generated from Gaussian 16‐formatted checkpoint files using MultiWFN [46]. Lastly, ChimeraX [47] was used to visualize all model molecules.

Families of Structural Conformers of the PN Ligand and NCI Analyses

Based on their structural features and the relative electronic (∆E) and Gibbs free energies (∆G) at 298 K, the PN conformers are classified into five structural families (I, II, III, IV, and V). Representative conformers from each structural family, i.e., the lowest‐energy member of each family, are illustrated in Figure 1. Structurally, the primary difference between these families lies in the relative orientation of the two N‐3,5‐di‐tert‐butylsalicylidene and N’‐3’,5’‐di‐tert‐butylsalicylidene groups, i.e., the two ligand arms. Additional conformational variations within a family arise primarily from the rotation of the tert‐butyl groups, resulting in staggered and eclipsed forms at the 3,5 and 3’,5’ positions, as well as the axial and equatorial orientations of the methyl group at the diamine bridge.

For a qualitative assessment of the NCIs that contribute to the relative family stability, the isosurfaces and RDG plots are illustrated in Figure 1. Interestingly, the rotation of the hydroxyl groups is significantly restricted due to the formation of two intramolecular O─H···N hydrogen bonds in all conformers. Within each family, structural and energetic differences are minor. From Family I to Family V, a consistent trend emerges: as the two ligand arms progressively move apart, the extent of favorable vdW interactions decreases. This decrease is evidenced by the reduction in green isosurfaces area observed in the NCI plots across the families, as well as the diminished density in regions near sign(λ2)ρ ≈ 0 in the corresponding RDG plots.

Family I represent the lowest‐energy group of conformers, making up roughly 70% of the total number of conformers within a 15 kJ mol−1 window and contributing approximately 97% to the Boltzmann population. In these conformers, the two ligand arms are positioned in close proximity. Family II (∼20% of the total number of conformers within the 15 kJ mol−1 window) contributes less than 2% to the Boltzmann population. These structures are characterized by a partial rotation of one N‐3,5‐di‐tert‐butylsalicylidene group, resulting in a nonparallel alignment and an increase in the interplanar angle between the two aromatic units. Family III (∼10% of the total number of conformers within the 15 kJ mol−1 window) begins populating at ∼14 kJ mol−1 and contributes roughly 1% to the Boltzmann population. In this family, the interplanar angle ranges from near‐perpendicular (90°) to nearly 180°, as one aromatic group undergoes progressive rotation. Family IV, located in the 20–30 kJ mol−1 relative energy range, includes conformers in which both N‐salicylidene groups adopt a nearly coplanar arrangement, maintaining an interplanar angle close to 180°. Family V, the highest in relative energy, starting at ∼24 kJ mol−1, comprises conformers where the two ligand arms are at maximal separation, resulting in greatly diminished dispersion interactions between the arms.

Simulated IR and VCD Spectra of Families and Experimental Conformational Abundances

In Figure 2, the simulated IR and VCD spectra of the five representative conformers from Families I–V are compared with the corresponding experimental spectra. The simulated IR spectra differ only slightly among the five families and largely resemble the experimental IR spectrum. In contrast, the representative VCD patterns of the five families exhibit striking differences, particularly in the 1300–1500 cm−1 region, reflecting their distinct geometrical differences. Most importantly, a straightforward visual inspection reveals that Family I, which is predicted to account for 97% of the population based on DFT free energy Boltzmann factors, does not exhibit much similarity to the experimental VCD. By contrast, the other families, although neglected on the basis of Boltzmann populations as predicted by calculations, demonstrate clear contributions to the experimental VCD, as evidenced by the prominent bisignate bands in the 1390–1360 cm−1 region in Family V, for example.

The similarity index introduced by Shen et al. [48] was used to evaluate the agreement between the Boltzmann‐averaged (BF) and experimental IR and VCD spectra (see Figure 2). All relevant analysis details, including the similarity function used and the precise selection criteria, are provided in Point S4, while key results are summarized below. In the GA‐VCD procedure (vide infra), this index is represented as “T” [18]. The similarity index T ranges from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates no similarity, and 1 represents complete overlap between the pair of spectra being considered. While the Boltzmann‐averaged IR spectrum shows excellent agreement between theory and experiment, reflected by a similarity index of 0.84, the VCD spectrum has a T value of only 0.05—indicating less than 5% agreement. For instance, the strong predicted VCD signals at 1650, 1450, 1420, and across the 1100–1300 cm−1 range in the BF VCD spectrum were not observed experimentally. These discrepancies observed in Figure 2 suggest that conformers beyond those in Family I likely make meaningful contributions to the true conformational landscape of PN in solution, contributions that are not fully captured by the Boltzmann average derived from DFT relative energies. The observed discrepancies—likely arising from solvent‐induced changes in conformational abundances—highlight the broader challenge of accurately modelling the conformational dynamics of highly flexible systems in VCD studies, whose spectral features are extremely sensitive to molecular conformation and thus provide richer and exquisitely more sensitive information about conformational distribution in solution than IR.

Given the large number of identified conformers and the high sensitivity of VCD to their populations, a manual weighting scheme is impractical. Instead, we apply a systematic, data‐driven refinement strategy that is the GA‐VCD procedure [16, 17]. Assuming that all the important conformers have been identified with the extensive systematic searches and the IR and VCD spectra are computed at a sufficiently high level of theory to yield reliable spectral features for all of them, the GA approach can be applied to optimize the conformational populations to achieve optimal agreement with experiment, using T as a fitness function. Starting from the DFT‐predicted Gibbs free energies of individual conformers, trial ensembles are generated by introducing random perturbations within a user‐defined energy uncertainty window, followed by Boltzmann weighting and spectral comparison. Through iterative selection, crossover, and mutation steps, the population evolves toward an ensemble that best reproduces the experimental spectra. The GA results with an energy uncertainty window of 0 kcal mol−1 (same as the DFT‐based Boltzmann‐averaged spectra) and 3 kcal mol−1 (∼12 kJ mol−1) are summarized in Figure 3.

The similarity index T values are also provided. See text for details. Frequency scaling factors of 0.9775 and 0.9720 were applied to the 900–1490 cm−1 and 1490–1900 cm−1 regions, respectively. Notably, while both the DFT and GA‐refined ensembles reproduced the experimental IR spectrum well (T = 0.84 and 0.82, respectively), the GA refinement markedly improved the VCD agreement, increasing the T from 0.05 to 0.72. This result indicates that the reweighted conformer ensemble more accurately reflects the experimental observations. The experimental vibrational band assignments—based on the calculated IR and VCD spectra are summarized in Table S4.1.

To assess the sensitivity of the protocol to the energy threshold used in GA, the GA‐VCD protocol was also performed with a 5 kcal mol−1 (∼21 kJ mol−1) energy uncertainty. No significant changes were observed in the dominant conformers identified by the GA or their associated populations between the two energy uncertainty levels. In other words, both uncertainty levels converge to the same refined conformer ensemble, underscoring the robustness of the GA‐VCD approach. The results of the second GA‐VCD analysis are provided in Figure S4.1.

The GA‐refined ensembles—guided by both energetic and spectral considerations—reveal that the experimental ensemble comprises a much broader and more diverse conformational distribution with meaningful contributions from all five families (see Table S4.2). Example main conformers are provided in Figure S4.4, along with their corresponding IR and VCD spectral features. Some of these conformers are largely neglected previously by the Boltzmann weighting scheme due to their higher relative energies. While conformers from Family I remain among the key contributors, their relative population drops significantly—from 97% (DFT) to ∼39% (GA). In contrast, contributions from Families II, III, IV, and V become much more pronounced, with total populations of approximately 16%, 16%, 6%, and 23%, respectively. The previously unexplained bisignate VCD features near 1250 cm−1 and in the 1392–1361 cm−1 region are now accounted for by contributions from Families II and III, and Family V, respectively.

As a further test, the GA‐VCD spectra of the opposite enantiomer were generated with energy uncertainties of 3 and 5 kcal mol−1, as shown in Figures S4.2 and S4.3, respectively. Notably, increasing the energy uncertainty from 3 to 5 kcal mol−1 only marginally improves the T value (from 0.72 to 0.76) but simultaneously increases the risk of misassignment, as reflected by the higher T value obtained for the opposite enantiomer. This test serves to verify whether a similarly high similarity index T can be achieved using the VCD spectrum of the opposite enantiomer, thereby assessing the confidence of the AC assignment (see Point S4 for details). These results suggest that an energy uncertainty of up to 5 kcal mol−1 can still yield a reliable AC assignment for the ligand; however, more stringent energy criteria are required for the metal complexes, as elaborated in the next section.

Helicity and Conformational Landscapes of the Metal Complexes of PN

For Ni(II)‐PN and Cu(II)‐PN, metal coordination not only significantly constrains the ligand backbone flexibility but also introduces additional stereoelectronic factors that give rise to the helicity of the complexes. Here, we examine the conformational landscapes of these complexes and their impact on the corresponding VCD spectral signatures, and evaluate the potential of metal coordination as a strategy for rapid AC assignment of flexible salen‐type ligands.

The geometries of the 8 and 13 conformers identified for the Cu(II)‐(R)‐PN and Ni(II)‐(R)‐PN complexes are shown in Figure S5.1 and S5.2, Point S5, respectively. Theoretical calculations indicate that all metal complexes of PN adopt distorted square‐planar geometries. The definitions of the distortion angle θ, where θ = 0° corresponds to an ideal square‐planar geometry and θ = 90° to a tetrahedral geometry, and helicity (Δ/Λ) are given in Figures S5.3, while information on helicity, axial/equatorial labels, θ, ∆G and Boltzmann factors for Cu(II)‐(R)‐PN and Ni(II)‐(R)‐PN conformers is summarized in Tables S5.1 and S5.2, respectively. For Cu(II)‐(R)‐PN, θ varies little around 11.0°, whereas in Ni(II)‐(R)‐PN, it fluctuates between 0.6° and 6.0°. As expected for a d8 metal, the Ni(II) conformers are closer to square‐planar geometry than those of Cu(II) (d9).

Structurally, the Cu(II) and Ni(II) metal complex conformers can be classified into two families: axial (ax), where the methyl group on the diamine bridge is oriented nearly perpendicular to the N–C–C–N plane, and equatorial (eq), where the methyl group lies approximately in‐plane. Within each family, the variation arises from the rotation of the tert‐butyl substituents, which produces negligible changes in the IR and VCD spectral signatures, similar to the ligand case. For both Cu(II)‐(R)‐PN and Ni(II)‐(R)‐PN, axial conformers adopt Δ helicity and are consistently more stable than the equatorial Λ counterparts by at least 6.2 and 5.3 kJ mol−1, respectively. Consequently, the axial Δ conformers constitute 94% and 88% of the total populations for Cu(II)‐(R)‐PN and Ni(II)‐(R)‐PN, respectively.

In the closely related Ni(II) and Cu(II) complexes of (R,R)‐chxn1 and ‐chxn2 ligands, intense bisignate +/− (high to low cm−1) VCD features were experimentally observed at the C═N and C═C stretching region near 1600 cm−1, opposite to the corresponding −/+ VCD features reported here for the Ni(II) and Cu(II) complexes of (R)‐PN ligands. These bisignate +/− (or −/+) VCD features are directly correlated to the Λ (or Δ) helicity. Additionally, only Λ conformers with the (R,R)‐chxn1 and ‐chxn2 ligands were found to be stable, while the Δ counterparts were predicted to have negligible populations in the equilibrium conformational landscapes [15, 28]. This exclusive helicity preference in chxn1 and chxn2 complexes was believed to arise from the conformational constraint imposed by the cyclohexane ring, which enforces a chair conformation and thereby fixes the chirality outcome of metal coordination. Replacing the rigid cyclohexyl bridge with a more flexible methyl‐substituted diamine backbone appears to relieve these geometric constraints, allowing both helicities to be accessible, highlighting the helicity‐tuning ability of the substituents at the linking bridge.

The simulated individual and Boltzmann‐averaged IR and VCD spectra of the two axial and equatorial families for Ni(II)‐(R)‐PN are compared with the experimental spectra in Figure 4, while the results for Cu(II)‐(R)‐PN are presented in Figure S5.4. Individual IR and VCD spectra of all conformers are summarized in Figures S5.5 and S5.6, while the experimental vibrational band assignments are in Tables S5.3 and S5.4 for the two complexes. The Boltzmann‐averaged IR and VCD spectra of both Cu(II) and Ni(II) complexes show good agreement with the experimental data, confirming that the solution populations are dominated by the axial Δ conformers, with minor contributions from the equatorial Λ conformers. Compared to the free ligand, the C═N and C═C stretching region near 1600 cm−1 exhibits a much more intense bisignate VCD feature in both metal complexes, in good agreement with the calculated spectra. Similar intense bisignate VCD features were reported for the closely related Cu(II) and Ni (II) chxn complexes [15, 28]. This likely reflects the structural rigidity introduced by metal coordination, greatly reducing the cancellation effect introduced by conformational averaging observed in the corresponding free ligands. In addition, the aromatic ring C═C stretching band is red‐shifted to ∼1530 from ∼1596 cm−1 in the ligand.

A striking observation is that the axial Δ and equatorial Λ conformers, though both derived from the same (R)‐PN, exhibit nearly mirror‐image VCD spectra, i.e., conformational enantiomorphism. This indicates that the VCD responses of the PN metal complexes primarily originate from the coordination helicity. In Figure S5.7, the experimental VCD spectra of Cu(II)‐(R)‐PN and Cu(II)‐(S)‐PN complexes are compared with the calculated Δ and Λ spectra of Cu(II)‐(R))‐PN where the sign inversion is prominently displayed at the C═N and C═C stretching bands, as well as the tert‐butyl and bridge C─H bending bands. In contrast, in the 1400–1270 cm−1 region, dominated by the aromatic C─H bending, the extent of sign inversion is less pronounced. This likely suggests that VCD features of these vibrations are more affected by ligand chirality than by the metal‐centered stereochemical environment.

In contrast to the ligand case, the DFT relative energies—and thus the theoretical Boltzmann populations—perform well for the two metal complexes.

Because of the pronounced conformational enantiomorphism observed in the PN metal complexes, the GA‐VCD analysis indicated that particular care is required when establishing AC assignment in such cases (see Point S5 for details). Specifically, unless the energy uncertainty used for the GA‐VCD analysis is smaller than the ΔG difference between the enantiomorphic pairs (Δ and Λ conformers), the same ensemble of conformers and corresponding spectra can yield comparable T values for opposite enantiomers, rendering the AC assignment highly sensitive to the relative energies predicted by DFT. In the case of Ni(II)‐(R)‐PN, the free energy difference between the most stable Δ and Λ conformers is approximately 5 kJ mol−1 (see Table S5.2). In this context, the GA‐VCD analysis provides an effective diagnostic by flagging the presence of conformational enantiomorphism [18].

Why does a generally benign solvent such as CDCl3 cause such drastic changes in ligand conformational populations, whereas it exercises little effect on the related transition metal complexes? We address this important issue in the next section in an effort to provide crucial insights into the solvent effects in benign solvents such as CDCl3.

Solute‐Solvent van der Waals Interactions

It is clear from the results presented above that the CDCl3 solvent plays a major role in the conformational preferences and that the implicit solvent models, such as PCM, could not predict the outcome correctly. Hybrid implicit‐explicit solvation models are computationally demanding and not currently available in standard computational workflows [27]. A simple, static “clusters‐in‐a‐liquid” [23] model was initially proposed, supported by the observation of VCD signatures arising from water itself [49, 50], indicating the presence of small long‐lived hydration clusters. In this model, small long‐lived solute‐solvent clusters, constructed following MD simulations and subsequent radial distribution functions (RDF) analyses or the ellipsoid method for cluster‐in‐solvent (EMCS) and essential dynamics (ED) approach [51] or added ad hoc, are placed inside a PCM of solvent. It has been utilized for polar solvents with strong hydrogen‐bonding tendency, such as water [52, 53], and DMSO‐d6 [54, 55, 56]. More recently, AIMD simulations have also been applied to VCD studies, demonstrating their power in reproducing chiral vibrational signatures, including those of the solvent molecules [25, 57, 58]. However, AIMD remains computationally demanding, as the weighting of the heavy‐atom conformations usually needs to be done manually because of the formidable cost [35].

In contrast, CDCl3 is often considered a benign solvent, whose effect can typically be captured using a PCM model, providing a reference point compared to strongly interacting solvents such as ACN‐d3 [59]. The observed drastic change in conformational distribution induced by CDCl3 is both striking and unexpected, yet it offers a unique opportunity to probe the fundamental nature of solvent‐driven conformational control, especially those associated with the weak vdW interactions [27]. Since it is challenging to accurately capture weak vdW solute–solvent interactions, especially dispersion forces, due to their weak and nondirectional nature, we applied Grimme's quantum cluster growth (QCG) approach—a systematic framework for constructing microsolvated clusters by iteratively placing solvent molecules around the solute, enabling quantum mechanics treatment of explicit solute–solvent interactions without the need for extensive MD simulations [60]. QCG has recently been used in VCD studies of monosaccharides in water [61] and of a series of small organic compounds in DMSO [62].

Representative optimized geometries of Families I–V were selected as the input solute structures, and a pre‐optimized CDCl3 solvent molecule was used for all docking steps. Additional details of the QCG results are provided in Point S6, together with DFT‐simulated IR and VCD spectra of selected explicitly solvated PN–CDCl3 clusters, as well as a discussion of the limitations of both static cluster and MD‐based approaches. Using an energy window of 50 kJ mol−1 and an RMSD threshold of 0.125 Å, a total of 585, 1529, 1777, 2501, and 1610 unique clusters were identified for n = 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively, where n represents the number of explicit solvent molecules included. The large number of possible solute‐solvent complexes underscores the current challenge of capturing the pronounced effects of weakly interacting solvents. Accordingly, we focus on elucidating how the relative conformational stability shifts as additional solvent molecules are introduced, rather than precisely predicting the conformational abundances. Visual inspection of the solvated ensembles reveals several recurring NCI motifs, including CH···π, CH···O, and CH···HC close contacts between the solvent molecule and the salicylidene moieties. The energies of the conformers listed are at the GFN2‐xTB level of theory, which has recently been shown to capture the role of vdW solute–solvent interactions in stabilizing enantio‐determining transition states [27].

Across all solvation levels with n = 1 to 4, several interesting observations emerge: (1) Family II conformers consistently emerge as the lowest‐energy structures, even though Family I conformers are predicted to be most stable at the GFN2‐xTB and final DFT levels for the isolated monomer. In Family II, the extra stabilization predominantly involves a CDCl3 molecule occupying the salen cavity and forming CH···π contacts with the aromatic rings. With n > 1, the additional CDCl3 molecules typically position around the periphery of the ligand, while one remains inside the cavity; (2) As the number of explicit CDCl3 molecules increases, the relative stabilities of Families II, III, IV, and V remain largely consistent, although the ordering of Families IV and V fluctuates slightly; (3) The stability of Family I decreases steadily relative to the rest of the conformer ensemble with increasing n; (4) At n = 4, a notable shift in the conformational energy landscape occurs where Family I conformers are approximately 27 kJ mol−1 above the global minimum, while Family II continues to be the lowest, following by III, IV, and V at ∼11, 14, and 15 kJ mol−1 higher, respectively. This drastic shift reflects the fact that Family I’s compact geometry limits solvent accessibility, preventing maximum effective solute–solvent interactions. One may extrapolate that in solution, the different degrees of solvent accessibility play a significant role in the relative stability of all five families of conformers.

Comparing the pronounced solvent effects observed in PN with the relatively minor solvent influences in its Ni(II) and Cu(II) complexes, the origin of the dramatic conformational preference shift in PN becomes clear. The relative stability of PN families is largely governed by the cumulative effects of many weak NCIs; as such, they can be replaced by the weak vdW solvent‐solute interactions. In contrast, in terms of the ligand conformations in the Ni(II) and Cu(II) complexes, solvent accessibility plays a negligible role in the relative stability, as the rigid tetradentate coordination effectively locks the ligand conformation across all identified conformers. On the other hand, the effects of different polar solvents on the relative abundances of Λ and Δ helical isomers of transition metal complexes have also been reported [63], further highlighting the complexity of solvent effects. To accurately predict the outcome of the delicate competition between the cumulative solvent‐solute dispersion contacts and the intrinsic intramolecular vdW interactions remains a formidable task currently. Under such conditions, the GA‐VCD approach provides a practical and experimentally guided alternative, enabling these dispersion‐stabilized conformers to be identified without the need for exhaustive explicit‐solvent modelling—provided that a comprehensive conformational search is carried out to capture all relevant candidates.

Conclusion

In this work, we examined the conformational landscape and NCIs of the PN ligand and its Ni(II) and Cu(II) complexes using IR and VCD spectroscopy combined with extensive conformational searches, DFT calculations, and the GA‐guided approach. For the PN ligand, pronounced solvent effects on the conformational landscape were revealed. Analyses with GA‐VCD and QCG microsolvation models demonstrate how a delicate balance between intermolecular solute‐solvent and intramolecular solute vdW interactions governs the observed outcome. Our results highlight that even weak solute–solvent vdW interactions can significantly reorder conformer stabilities when their cumulative effects rival the intramolecular vdW forces, shaping the isolated ligand. While no existing computational method can predict such cases reliably, the experimental VCD‐guided GA approach provided an effective strategy for interpreting VCD spectra under these conditions, integrating energetic and spectral information beyond the standard Boltzmann framework.

In contrast, tetradentate coordination with Ni(II) and Cu(II) minimizes vdW interactions between two ligand arms, avoiding competition between intramolecular and intermolecular solute‐solvent NCI interactions and thereby greatly simplifying the conformational analyses. The (R)‐PN metal complexes are dominated by axial conformers adopting Δ helicity, with Λ‐ equatorial conformers contributing minor populations. Remarkably, the Λ and Δ conformers derived from the same (R)‐PN exhibit nearly mirror‐image VCD spectra—a consequence of the rigid, nearly square‐planar coordination that amplifies subtle geometric differences into inverted chiroptical responses. In such rigid systems, although the need for a GA approach is reduced because of limited conformational diversity, the built‐in VCD analysis still provides an effective diagnostic for detecting conformational enantiomorphism.

Overall, these insights advance a systematic understanding of how ligand flexibility, solvent effects, and metal coordination interplay to shape stereochemical outcomes. More broadly, they demonstrate metal coordination as a practical means for AC assignment of flexible ligands and underscore the critical role of weak vdW solute‐solvent interactions and of integrating experimental, theoretical, and data‐driven approaches to address long‐standing challenges in chiroptical analysis.
