The application of nanoparticles green-formulated by Lycium barbarum leaf aqueous extract in the treatment of myocardial infarction
Authors: Yunzhong Liu, Fengying Gao, Yu Kong, Xuliang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Cardiovascular diseases, including myocardial infarction, remain a main cause of mortality and morbidity worldwide, and raising attention has been directed toward plant-derived bioactive compounds and green nanotechnology for developing safer remedial approaches. This research described a green synthesis of copper nanoparticles (Cu NPs) utilizing leaf extract from Lycium barbarum. Several spectroscopic techniques were used to characterize Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum, and their remedial efficacies against myocardial infarction was examined. The synthesized nanoparticles exhibited predominantly spherical morphology. Based on the XRD data, the five characteristic signals observed at 36.4°, 42.5°, and 61.7° correspond to the (111), (200), and (220) planes of cuprous oxide, respectively. The signals present in the EDX diagram, located at 0.22 keV for C Lα, 0.51 keV for O Lα, and 0.93 keV for Cu Lα, confirm the formation of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum.Methods:In the in vivo condition, mice were randomly assigned into five experimental groups: control group, untreated myocardial infarction group, Propranolol hydrochloride (5 mg/kg) + myocardial infarction, Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum (80 µg/kg) + myocardial infarction, and Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum (160 µg/kg) + myocardial infarction. Animals in the treatment groups received 0.5 mL of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum orally through gavage once daily for 14 days. Treatment was initiated before myocardial infarction induction and continued throughout the study period. The untreated myocardial infarction and control groups received 0.5 mL normal saline using the same administration schedule. A myocardial infarction was induced with isoproterenol in mice. Following treatment with Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum at different dosages, cardiac function was assessed using electrocardiogram (ECG), immunological, molecular, and biochemical investigations. The PPAR-γ/NF-κB and cytokines activation was evaluated using real-time PCR.Results:Compared with untreated myocardial infarction animals, Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum treatment significantly improved ST-segment abnormalities. Additionally, Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum improve ventricular wall integrity and reduce myocardial tissue damage. Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum also reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines in the myocardial infarction mice heart. The nanoparticles beneficial effects may be associated with the phosphorylated gene expression normalization. Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum significantly decreased cell death and the expression of inflammatory cytokines. According to available data, Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum has cardioprotective effects on isoproterenol-induced myocardial infarction, which may be related to PPAR-γ activation and NF-κB signaling inhibition.Conclusion:The present study offers a contemporary corrective approach for the myocardial infarction therapeutic management.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Copper nanoparticles, Lycium barbarum, Leaf extract, Myocardial infarction

Introduction

The morbidity and death rate of ischemic heart disease are still high globally, even if more and more novel medications and interventional devices are being created to treat myocardial infarction [1]. Since myocardial infarct size has been shown in clinical trials to be a significant predictor of patient prognosis, creating innovative methods to reduce infarct size is a very important and promising avenue [2]. Since both ischemia and reperfusion injuries contribute to infarct size, prompt reperfusion is the main treatment objective in clinical practice. A longer ischemic period results in a greater infarct size. However, reperfusion has the potential to cause extra harm, increasing the size of the infarct. Thus, finding ways to treat both ischemia and reperfusion injuries—especially the ischemic injury, which mostly contributes to the size of the infarct—could significantly lower the incidence of infarction [3]. Plant-synthesized nanoparticles have gained increasing attention as potential therapeutic agents for myocardial infarction because they combine the biological effects of plant-derived phytochemicals with the unique physicochemical properties of nanomaterials [4, 5]. Motivated by this, our goal is to create a therapeutic system generated from Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum and achieve cross-species application for the treatment of myocardial infarction.

Lycium barbarum L., commonly known as wolfberry or goji berry, is a medicinal plant widely used in traditional Chinese medicine owing to its significant pharmacological potentials. Its fruits are rich in bioactive compounds, including carotenoids (especially zeaxanthin), Lycium barbarum polysaccharides (LBPs), betaine, phenolic acids, flavonoids, amino acids, vitamins, and essential minerals [6, 7]. These phytochemicals contribute to its potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory effects. Numerous research has revealed that L. barbarum exhibits nephroprotective, cardioprotective, neuroprotective, hepatoprotective, and antidiabetic efficacies by reducing oxidative stress and regulating inflammatory pathways [6–8]. Furthermore, its extracts have indicated promising anticancer properties through the induction of apoptosis, inhibition of tumor cell proliferation, and modulation of signaling pathways such as MAPK, PI3K/Akt, and NF-κB. The high zeaxanthin content also supports retinal health and protects against age-related macular degeneration [7, 8]. Owing to its rich phytochemical composition and broad spectrum of therapeutic effects, L. barbarum is considered a valuable source for the development of nutraceuticals, functional foods, and novel therapeutic agents [6, 8].

One of the most important and rapidly evolving areas of material and engineering research is nanotechnology, which includes the formulation, characterization, and production of nanoparticles [9, 10]. Among the metallic nanoparticles, copper nanoparticles (CuNPs) have also been shown to exhibit potent antibacterial, antifungal, antioxidant, anticancer, and enzyme inhibitory qualities [9, 11]. Because of its rapid reaction, ease of acquiring various simplicity, and biomolecules of plant extract, researchers in a variety of multidisciplinary domains are increasingly focusing on the plant-based mediated nanoparticles synthesis [12–14]. However, among these numerous natural sources, medicinal plants and its constituent parts are seen to be the most promising choices for creating nanoparticles [15]. In order to ascertain the pharmacological effects of plant extracts, the scientific community has concentrated recently on their presence of associated bioactive chemicals [14, 15]. Additionally, plant extracts aid in the production of nanoparticles and aid in controlling their optimal size and shape. The herbal extract is consequently a rich medicinal phytochemicals source since they are potent reducing and stabilizing agents in the synthesis process [16, 17]. Plant-mediated copper nanoparticles indicate potential cardioprotective potentials through anti-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and endothelial-modulating mechanisms. The proposed idea is that plant extracts used during nanoparticle synthesis contribute bioactive compounds (alkaloids, flavonoids, polyphenols, terpenoids), while copper itself is an essential trace element involved in antioxidant enzymes and cellular metabolism and makes high cardioprotecitve effects [12]. Experimental research offers mitigation of oxidative cardiac injury and amelioration of biochemical markers; however, concerns regarding dose-dependent toxicity and the absence of human clinical evidence limit current therapeutic application [12, 13].

The present research has direct relevance to the food science and nutrition field because Lycium barbarum is a plant-derived source of bioactive compounds with potential nutraceutical applications. Food-derived medicinal plants and phytochemicals are increasingly being evaluated as functional ingredients because of their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and health-promoting effects. Natural compounds originating from plant sources have received notable attention in nutrition research for their possible use in functional foods and nutraceutical formulations designed to prevent chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disorders [6, 7]. In the recent study, Lycium barbarum leaf aqueous extract was utilized as a green stabilizing and reducing agent for copper nanoparticle synthesis, while simultaneously considering as a source of biologically active phytochemicals. Such plant-mediated nanoparticle systems may ameliorate the bioavailability, stability, and biological activity of naturally derived compounds, offering potential applications in food-derived bioactive delivery and nutraceutical development systems. Therefore, although remedial activity was evaluated in a myocardial infarction model, the outcomes may contribute to future research focused on functional foods and nutrition-based preventive strategies for cardiovascular health. The present study novelty extends beyond merely replacing the plant source used for nanoparticle synthesis. While previous research has reported green-synthesized nanoparticles and their remedial properties in myocardial infarction models, limited information is available regarding Lycium barbarum -mediated copper nanoparticles and their cardioprotective mechanisms. The recent study integrates several novel aspects: (i) the green synthesis of copper nanoparticles using Lycium barbarum leaf aqueous extract, which possesses unique phytochemical constituents that may affect biological activity and nanoparticle characteristics; (ii) the simultaneous assessment of cardioprotective effect through biochemical, immunological, molecular, and electrocardiographic analyses; and (iii) mechanistic evaluation of the PPAR-γ/NF-κB signaling pathway related to inflammatory cytokine regulation. The novelty therefore lies not only in the Lycium barbarum-mediated synthesis approach but also in the integrated evaluation of nanoparticle-mediated modulation of inflammatory signaling pathways and myocardial protection. In addition, the research has a possible connection between nanotechnology-assisted therapeutic strategies and plant-derived bioactive compounds that may have future applications in functional food and nutraceutical research.

Experimental

Materials

All chemicals used in this study were purchased from Merck and Sigma-Aldrich and were used as received without further purification.

Preparation of Lycium barbarum leaf extract

Fresh Lycium barbarum leaves were carefully chosen and then properly cleaned with double-distilled water. The leaves were pulverized using a mortar and pestle after being dehydrated for a whole night at 50 °C in a drying oven. Ten grams of powdered leaves were macerated in 150 milliliters of distilled water. After cooling to 25 °C and heating to 100 °C for two minutes, the mixture was filtered through Whatman 1 filter paper to extract the bioactive components. Until it was prepared for additional analysis, the extracted material was kept at 4 °C [15].

Green formulation of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum

Copper nitrate was furnished by Sigma-Aldrich. To prepare a precursor solution for producing Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum, dissolve 0.0169 g of Cu(NO₃)2 to 1 mM in 150 ml of distilled water. 5 ml of Lycium barbarum leaf extract were added dropwise to 50 ml of the 1 mM copper nitrate solution following an undisturbed incubation period of 20 °C. Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum started to form when the colorless aqueous Cu(NO₃)2 solution turned dark green. As the synthesis progressed, the solution’s hue changed to dark green, indicating that the Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum was mature [15].

Chemical characterization of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum

FT-IR analysis was performed to identify the Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum functional groups and to investigate the interactions between the capping biomolecules and the synthesized Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum. Air-dried Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum was analyzed using a Thermo Scientific FT-IR spectrophotometer within the spectral range of 400–4000 cm⁻¹ [8].

The crystal’s dimensions of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum were ascertained using the XRD technique with X’Pertpro. With copper potassium (α) radiation, analytical X-ray 107 diffraction ranges from 10° to 80° for the Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum [15, 17].

The size, morphology, and structural characteristics of the mediated Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum were examined by TEM. A nanoparticle powder thin layer was deposited onto carbon-coated copper TEM grids and treated with 1% osmium (VIII) oxide to raise sample stability. After standing for 2 h, the samples were characterized using a Hitachi H-600 TEM instrument [8, 15].

The synthesized Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum elemental composition was determined by EDX analysis coupled with SEM. During electron beam irradiation, characteristic X-rays emitted from the sample were recorded using an energy-dispersive spectrometer operating at 20 keV, enabling quantitative and qualitative elemental analysis of the Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum. Particle distribution and surface morphology of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum were further determined using FE-SEM. Samples were prepared by dispersing Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum in suspension (20 mg/L), depositing a drop onto a carbon-coated FE-SEM grid, and letting it to dry. Residual solution was removed using blotting paper, and the grid was exposed to a mercury lamp for 6 min to ensure complete drying. Before imaging, the Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum samples were sputter-coated with gold to minimize charging efficacies. FE-SEM observations were done using a MIRA3 TESCAN microscope at an accelerating voltage of 30 keV [8].

The surface charge of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum was measured by determining the zeta potential (ζ). The analysis was conducted using a ZetaCompact laser zeta meter (CAD, Les Essarts-le-Roi, France) based on the micro-electrophoresis technique. Measurements were performed at 25 °C and at a pH of 6.17 ± 0.1 for Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum.

Animals

In the present research, 100 male C57BL/6 mice weighing 39–41 g were obtained from the Animal Laboratory of Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital. The mice were housed under controlled environmental conditions at a temperature of 20–25 °C with a relative humidity of 50–60% and maintained under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. Mice were provided with standard laboratory chow and had free access to water throughout the experimental period. Before the study initiation, the mice were acclimatized to laboratory conditions for 7 days. Following acclimatization, mice were randomly assigned to the experimental groups to minimize selection bias. Randomization was done using the randomization function available in GraphPad Prism. Additionally, investigators involved in treatment administration, data collection, and outcome assessment were blinded to group allocation to decrease potential observer bias. All animal experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval No. V1.02025417; approval date: 2025.04.17) and were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

The sample size used in the recent research was determined according to previous experimental research employing isoproterenol-induced myocardial infarction animal models and similar nanoparticle intervention protocols [18, 19]. A total of 100 animals were included to ensure sufficient representation across all experimental groups and to let reliable evaluation of electrocardiographic, biochemical, immunological, and molecular parameters. Group allocation was conducted to provide adequate statistical power for detecting biologically meaningful differences between treatment and control groups.

Where applicable, the sample size was chosen to gain a statistical power of 80% at a significance level of 0.05 (α = 0.05) for detecting expected treatment efficacies while minimizing unnecessary animal use in accordance with ethical principles and the reduction component of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement).

Using the technique outlined in the before work, isoproterenol (85 mg/kg) was dissolved in sodium chloride 0.9% and injected subcutaneously at 24-hour intervals at days 13 and 14 of the study to induce myocardial infarction [18]. Following anesthesia with 0.1 ml/20 gm intraperitoneal at doses of 2.5 mg/mL Xylazine and 17.5 mg/mL Ketamine, samples from the left ventricle were taken and analyzed using H&E techniques. Identification of the whitish region revealed necrotic damage from the cardiac attack [18]. The animals were put under anesthesia following the isoproterenol final dose. An evaluation of ST-segment depression was conducted. The animals were put down on the 5th day so that the immunological and biochemical characteristics could be examined. The homogenized left ventricular heart tissues supernatant’s cytokine expression levels were assessed using the ELISA method. By using the ELISA test to analyze blood troponin concentrations, the degree of cardiac cellular damage is determined [18].

Total RNA was extracted from cardiac tissue samples using the Qiasol reagent based on the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity and concentration of the isolated RNA were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer by determining absorbance at 260 and 280 nm. RNA quality was evaluated using the A260/A280 ratio. For complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, a defined quantity of total RNA was used based on the manufacturer’s cDNA synthesis kit protocol. The reaction mixture containing RNA template and cDNA synthesis reagents was prepared according to the recommended reaction volume and incubated in a thermocycler under appropriate conditions for reverse transcription. The synthesized cDNA samples were stored at − 20 °C until more analysis. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was conducted to assess the expression levels of target genes, including IκB-α, NF-κB, PPAR-γ, and IKKα/β, using GAPDH as the internal reference gene. Amplification was done by an initial denaturation step, followed by repeated cycles of denaturation, primer annealing, and extension based on the manufacturer’s recommended thermal cycling conditions. Relative gene expression levels were computed using the comparative threshold cycle (2^−ΔΔCt) method, where Ct values of target genes were normalized against the housekeeping gene and compared among experimental groups [18].

Statistical analysis

All biological and nanoparticle characterization tests were repeated three times. Descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations) were calculated for all biological response variables. Before inferential analyses, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and appropriate tests for variance equality, respectively. Variables that satisfied these assumptions were subsequently analyzed using the General Linear Model (GLM) procedure in SPSS (2022). In the first experimental phase, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to evaluate treatment effects on measured biological parameters, followed by Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test for post hoc comparisons when significant differences were detected. In the second experimental phase, two-way ANOVA was used to assess the effects of experimental and treatment factors, including their interactions. For measurements collected repeatedly over time, repeated-measures ANOVA within the GLM framework was applied to account for within-subject correlations and temporal changes in response variables. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results and discussion

Analysis of chemical characterization of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum

The research purpose is to investigate how intraperitoneal (IP) injection of green-formed copper nanoparticles from Lycium barbarum affects heart damage caused by isoproterenol. Using different dosages of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum, the effects of isoproterenol on left ventricular dysfunction, oxidative stress, and molecular changes were examined and altered. The characterization of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum was followed by EDX, FT-IR, XRD, FE-SEM, TEM, and zeta potential.

The Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum surface functional groups, which are in reduction and stability charge, can be detected by FT-IR [19–21]. The hydroxyl groups of polyphenolic substances are responsible for this investigation’s significant absorption peak at 3425.99 cm− 1 (Fig. 1). The FT-IR reported the amide and C–H sp3 stretching peaks at 1615.96 cm− 1 and 2926.78 cm− 1, respectively. At 1384.39 cm− 1, the alkenes C = C stretching was noted. The Cu–O stretching band in CuNPs is shown by the absorption peak in the Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum spectra at 618.41 cm− 1 [22–24].

The results of the Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum EDX investigation are shown in Fig. 2. Signals of 0.51 keV for O Lα, 0.22 keV for C Lα, and 0.93 keV for Cu Lα are among the EDX diagram presence signals that validate the Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum establishment. Gu et al. have observed the same signals for the components of Cu NPs produced with the Calendula officinalis extract [26]. The green-formulated copper nanoparticles showed the same signals, according to Wang et al. (2024) [25].

Ultimately, XRD assay was used to verify the phase structure and crystallinity of the mediated CuNPs@Lycium barbarum (Fig. 3). Cu’s XRD results (JCPDS NO. 65–3288) closely match those of the corresponding material. Several typical signals located at 36.4°, 42.5°, and 61.7°, which linked to the (111), (200), and (220) cuprous oxide planes, respectively, similar to the previous study [20–23]. Using the Debye Scherrer equation, the crystal size of CuNPs@Lycium barbarum was found to be 19.71 nm.

FE-SEM and TEM are widely used analytical techniques for characterizing nanoparticles, as they provide high-resolution visualization of particle distribution, size, and morphology, particularly in plant-extract-mediated copper nanoparticle systems [21–24]. These techniques let direct observation of the nanoparticles physical structure, including their shape uniformity, aggregation degree, and size range at the nanoscale level [21, 22].

In the present research, FE-SEM and TEM analyses exhibited that CuNPs@Lycium barbarum possessed an average particle size of 18.52 nm, as revealed in Fig. 4. The nanoparticles indicated predominantly spherical and occasionally oval morphologies, revealing relatively uniform formation during the green formulation process. The reported nanoscale size offers efficient stabilization and reduction of copper ions by phytochemical constituents present in Lycium barbarum leaf aqueous extract. It should be noted that although copepr nanoparticles synthesized using Lycium barbarum extract may appear visually similar at the macroscopic level, detailed morphological and microscopic characterization reveals clear differences in particle size distribution and shape. These variations are typically affected by differences in phytochemical composition, which influence nucleation and growth processes during CuNPs@Lycium barbarum formation. The data obtained in this research are consistent with previous reports revealing that plant-mediated nanoparticles synthesized using Lycium barbarum leaf aqueous extract generally fall within the nanoscale range, often below 100 nm [24–26]. This approves the Lycium barbarum extract suitability as an effective green stabilizing and reducing agent for the stable copper nanoparticles synthesis.

(a) FE-SEM image of CuNPs@Lycium barbarum in the 200 nm scale bar. (b) TEM image of CuNPs@Lycium barbarum in the 10 nm scale bar

A discrepancy was seen between the XRD analysis crystallite size and the particle size measured by TEM. In the present research, the CuNPs@Lycium barbarum crystallite size calculated using the Debye–Scherrer equation was 19.71 nm, which is generally higher than the average particle size reported by TEM. This difference is usually reported in nanoparticle characterization research and can be associated with the fact that XRD estimates the crystallite (coherently diffracting domain) size, whereas TEM determines the individual nanoparticles actual physical dimensions [24, 26]. A single nanoparticle may consist of one or more crystallites, especially if it is polycrystalline in nature or contains internal grain boundaries. Additionally, lattice strain, instrumental broadening, and assumptions inherent in the Debye–Scherrer equation (such as neglecting strain effects and assuming ideal spherical crystallites) may contribute to differences between the two measurements [26]. Despite this variation, both TEM and XRD analyses consistently approve that CuNPs@Lycium barbarum are within the nanoscale range, supporting successful synthesis of crystalline copper nanoparticles using the Lycium barbarum-mediated approach.

Zeta potential analysis is commonly used to evaluate the surface charge and colloidal stability of nanoparticles. For copper nanoparticles, values more negative than approximately − 30 mV are generally considered indicative of good electrostatic stability, whereas less negative values may suggest a higher tendency toward aggregation [26]. Zeta potential reflects the degree of electrostatic repulsion between particles in suspension; in copper nanoparticle systems, a sufficiently negative surface charge helps prevent aggregation and maintains colloidal dispersion [26].

In the present study, the zeta potential of CuNPs@Lycium barbarum was − 28 mV (Fig. 5), indicating good colloidal stability and effective dispersion of nanoparticles in suspension at pH 7 and 25 °C. The negative value confirms the presence of a negatively charged surface on the CuNPs@Lycium barbarum, which contributes to their stability in the colloidal state.

Treatment of myocardial infarction by CuNPs@Lycium barbarum

Globally, acute myocardial infarction is the primary cause of illness and death. It is a severe ischemic heart disease. Animals that receive the synthetic β-adrenoceptor agonist isoproterenol experience myocardial infarction, which results in cellular damage and persistent infarct-like necrosis [27–29]. The myocardial necrosis first stage is characterized by changes in blood pressure, electrocardiogram (ECG), and left ventricular dysfunction, just like myocardial infarction. Isoproterenol-induced myocardial infarction in a rat model provides a reliable, non-invasive method to evaluate the effectiveness of a number of potential cardioprotective medications [30]. One of the most significant causal factors among some mechanisms put out to describe isoproterenol-induced cardiac damage is the auto-oxidation of catecholamines, which produces highly cytotoxic free radicals [31]. The cardioprotective effectiveness of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum on myocardial infarction has not yet been established, despite the plant’s strong antioxidant properties [6, 7].

The research revealed an environmentally friendly copper nanoparticles formulation that was synthesized using Lycium barbarum. Some spectroscopic ways were applied to assess the copper nanoparticles, and their remedial efficacies on myocardial infarction. Here, the preventive potential of nanoparticles produced from Lycium barbarum was investigated in connection with cardiac damage. This was accomplished by using isoproterenol to create a model of myocardial infarction. The levels of PPAR-γ/GAPDH, p-NF-κB p65/NF-κB p65, IKKα/β/IKKα/β, and p-IkBα/IkBα were decreased significantly (P ≤ 0.05) by Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum made using environmentally friendly techniques similar to Propranolol hydrochloride (see Fig. 6). Additionally, the number of CD68 + cells and the mRNA expression of IL1β, TNFα, and IL6 were notably lowered (P ≤ 0.05) by the copper nanoparticles produced utilizing green techniques with Lycium barbarum and Propranolol hydrochloride (Figs. 7–9). Substantial collagen buildup and substantial cardiac damage were hallmarks of the untreated mice’ myocardial infarction model. Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum reveals notable promise in the isoproterenol administration-caused myocardial damage treatment.

After two days of intraperitoneal administration at dosages of 80 and 160 µg/kg, the mice treated with isoproterenol showed clear and dose-dependent protection of heart edema and damage, demonstrating the Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum anti-edematogenic action. Following treatment with all dosages of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum, the heart-to-body weight ratio significantly (P ≤ 0.05) decreased, suggesting that the nanoparticles had a protective impact against cardiac hypertrophy and edema. These activities may be related to the anti-inflammatory and anti-edematous properties of linalyl and linalyl acetate, the main components of plant extracts [32]. Furthermore, the isoproterenol subcutaneous injection significantly decreased the LVdP/dtmax, relaxation, and arterial pressure indices while increasing the LVEDP. Important measures in the treatment of post-myocardial infarction heart failure include increasing the heart’s contractility and reducing the LVEDP. In the current study, several indications showed the beneficial effects of the nanoparticles, including a decrease in LVEDP and an improvement in arterial blood pressure. According to research by Patel et al. and Kinugawa et al., the generation of ROS following myocardial infarction drives membrane-bound PUFAs to start peroxiding, which promotes ventricular remodeling [33, 34]. The anti-inflammatory qualities of the copper nanoparticles demonstrated in this study may be due to the addition of plant linalool and linalyl acetate. Different concentrations of the nanoparticles demonstrated myocardial healing in mice administered isoproterenol, suggesting a potent protective effect of the nanoparticles against myocardial infarction that may be related to their antioxidant qualities. According to phytochemical investigations, the primary components of the plant extract include triterpenoids such coumarin and ursolic, flavonoids like linalyl acetate, 1,8-cineol, camphor, luteolin, and linalool, as well as a few additional mono and sesquiterpenes [6, 7]. These findings suggest that most of the cardioprotective properties of Lycium barbarum may be attributed to these components. Green-formulated nanoparticles made from plant extract significantly reduce the typical ST segment depression in heart failure animal model (Xu et al., 2024). Additionally, fatality rates decrease and ventricular wall infarction is markedly improved with nanoparticle therapy. Furthermore, following the application of nanoparticles, animals suffering from myocardial infarction demonstrated a reduction in the hearts inflammatory milieu. As a result, the rise in pro-inflammatory cytokines was successfully stopped. Based on our findings, the normalization of PPAR-γ and PPAR-γ/NF-κB/IκB-α/IKKα/β phosphorylation may be related to the positive effects of nanoparticles [35]. In an in vitro study, the nanoparticles administration revealed a notable decrease in the cell death inhibition. The cardioprotective efficacies of the nanoparticle therapy appear to be more beneficial for the myocardial infarction mice in the pre + post-isoproterenol group than for the post-isoproterenol group [35]. Nanoparticles exhibit cardioprotective effects in myocardial infarction produced by isoproterenol, per the Xu et al. (2024) research findings. This beneficial effect might be due to PPAR-γ activation and NF-κB signaling suppression [35].

The recent outcomes revealed that treatment with Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum was related to ameliorations in cardiac function, decreased myocardial injury markers, and reduced inflammatory responses in isoproterenol-induced myocardial infarction. Additionally, alterations in the expression patterns of PPAR-γ and NF-κB-related genes were reported following Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum treatment. These data offer a potential association between the cardioprotective efficacies of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum and inflammatory signaling pathways modulation. However, the recent mechanistic interpretation should be considered with caution because the molecular outcomes were primarily based on gene expression analysis. Alterations in mRNA expression may not necessarily correspond directly to changes in protein abundance or functional pathway activity. Therefore, although the findings offer a possible involvement of NF-κB signaling modulation and PPAR-γ activation in the observed cardioprotective efficacies, the recent research does not establish a definitive mechanistic relationship. More investigations using protein-level analyses, such as immunohistochemical staining, Western blotting, or pathway inhibition/activation research, are necessary to approve the functional role of these signaling pathways and to provide stronger mechanistic evidence for the biological efficacies of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum. Overall, the results reveal that Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum exert cardioprotective potentials against isoproterenol-induced myocardial infarction and that these efficacies may be related to modulation of inflammatory responses and PPAR-γ/NF-κB-related signaling pathways.

Limitations and future recommendations

Although the recent research revealed the cardioprotective efficacy of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum against isoproterenol-induced myocardial infarction, various limitations should be acknowledged. First, the research was performed using an animal model, which may not completely replicate the human myocardial infarction pathophysiology complexity. Therefore, caution should be exercised when translating these outcomes directly to clinical settings. Second, the sample size was relatively limited, and longer-term research was not conducted to assess the sustained remedial potential and possible chronic toxicity of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum. Third, although the research identified the PPAR-γ/NF-κB signaling pathway involvement, other molecular pathways and mechanisms contributing to the cardioprotective efficacies were not comprehensively evaluated. Additionally, the pharmacokinetic behavior, biodistribution, and long-term biosafety profile of the synthesized Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum were not determined.

Future research should focus on assessing the long-term therapeutic potential and safety of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum using larger experimental populations and extended observation periods. More experiments are also required to explore additional molecular mechanisms related to the observed cardioprotective effect. Also, research examining nanoparticle biodistribution, toxicity, metabolism, and pharmacokinetic characteristics are necessary before clinical translation. Finally, clinical assessments and human trials are warranted to validate the remedial applicability of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum for myocardial infarction management.

Conclusion

An environmentally friendly process for the bio-synthesis of copper nanocomposite encased by Lycium barbarum distillate as the bio-reducing factor was briefly discussed in the current work. This innovative treatment focuses specifically on the PPAR-γ/NF-κB signaling pathway in an effort to treat myocardial infarction in animals. To define the characteristics of the intended Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum, a few analytical techniques have been devised and used. The therapeutical properties of Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum may be linked to the normalization of PPAR-γ/NF-κB/ΙκB-α/ΙΚΚα/β and the PPAR-γ phosphorylation. The medicinal research indicates that Cu NPs@Lycium barbarum reduces effectively the pro-inflammatory cytokines levels. Additionally, they have been shown to improve ventricular wall infarction, lower death rates, and considerably lower the levels of myocardial damage indicators.
