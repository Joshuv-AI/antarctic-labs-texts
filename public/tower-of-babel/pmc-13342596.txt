Detection of papillomavirus DNA in uterine flushing samples from healthy mares reveals diverse genotypes with differing host ranges
Authors: Mariassunta Claudia D’Amato, Anna Cutarelli, Edoardo Vigetti, Tommaso di Palma, Serena Montagnaro, Fabio del Piero, Michele Napoletano, Giovanna Fusco, Sante Roperto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA molecular study to assess the presence of some papillomaviruses was performed on 105 uterine flushing samples of healthy mares using real time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and digital PCR (dPCR). Bovine, equine, and ovine papillomavirus DNA was detected in 27 uterine flushing samples through dPCR (~ 25.71%); qPCR was able to detect 18 positive samples (~ 17.14%). Differences between the two molecular protocols were significant as shown by McNemar’s test (p˂ 0.005). EcPV DNA was detected in 10 samples by dPCR (~ 9.52%) of uterine flushing samples. qPCR detected EcPV DNA in 7 samples (~ 6.66). dPCR revealed OaPV1 DNA in 13 samples (12.38%). qPCR revealed OaPV1 DNA in 9 samples (8.57%). BPV DNA was found by dPCR in 4 samples (~ 3.80%); qPCR revealed BPV DNA in two uterine flushing samples, that is ~ 1.90% of healthy uterine flushing samples. Overall, OaPV1 was the most detected papillomavirus from mare uterus. Similar molecular findings were found in the virobiota of mare vagina. EcPV9 was the most prevalent equine PV genotype. EcPV8, BPV13, ChPV1, ChPV2, OaPV2, OaPV3, and OaPV4 were not detected. Our findings are consistent with non-strict host specificity of BPVs and OaPVs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52384-z.Keywords: Bovine papillomavirus, Equine papillomavirus, Ovine papillomavirus, Real time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), Digital PCR (dPCR), Uterine flushingSubject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Diseases, Genetics, Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Papillomaviruses (PVs) are small, non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA viruses able to infect the skin and mucosae of a wide variety of animals in a species-specific manner1. PVs have co-evolved with their hosts for millions of years, leading to the hypothesis of “host-linked evolution”2.

Equine papillomaviruses (EcPVs) include ten genotypes assigned to three genera: EcPV1 to the Zetapapillomavirus genus; EcPV2, EcPV4, EcPV5, EcPV9, and EcPV10 to the Dyoiotapapillomavirus genus; EcPV3, EcPV6, and EcPV7 to the Dyorhopapillomavirus genus. EcPV8 remains unclassified3. EcPV1 has been associated with cutaneous papillomas in young horses4; EcPV3, EcPV4, EcPV5, and EcPV6 have been detected in aural plaques from numerous horses5–7; EcPV7 has been associated with aural plaques and penile squamous cell carcinomas8. EcPV8 is associated with generalized papillomatoses, viral plaques, and cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma9,10. EcPV9 has been associated with penile lesions11. EcPV10 has been identified in a subfertile thoroughbred mare12. Currently, the far best studied equine papillomavirus is EcPV2, which is known to play a causal role in the pathogenesis of equine genital epithelial tumors13, and in reproductive disorders appearing to be involved in equine abortions14.

The bovine papillomavirus (BPV) family comprises 46 genotypes classified into five genera3,15. Among these, the Deltapapillomavirus genus (including BPV1, BPV2, BPV13, and BPV14) is of particular relevance, as it includes highly pathogenic fibropapillomaviruses associated with both cutaneous and bladder carcinogenesis in cattle16–18. The Xipapillomavirus genus represents the largest and most heterogeneous group, comprising multiple genotypes, while the Epsilonpapillomavirus genus includes BPV5, BPV8, BPV25, and BPV44. The Dyokappapapillomavirus genus comprises BPV16, BPV18, and BPV22, and the Dyoxipapillomavirus genus includes BPV7. The remaining genotypes are currently unclassified3.

Ovine papillomaviruses (OaPVs) comprise four members. OaPV1, OaPV2, and OaPV4 are fibropapillomaviruses belonging to the genus Deltapapillomavirus, and OaPV3 that is an epitheliotropic Dyokappapapillomavirus3. OaPVs have been shown to be associated with skin tumors of sheep19,20.

BPVs and OaPVs are the only papillomaviruses known to be responsible for cross-species transmission and infection in large domestic animals.

Besides cattle, BPVs have been found in other animal species, including the skin and bladder tumors of water buffaloes (Bubalus bubalis)21,22, oral fibropapillomatosis of newborn lambs23, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of healthy sheep and goats24–26. BPVs have been associated with equine sarcoids, being BPV1 and BPV2 believed to be the predominant viruses responsible for BPV-associated diseases in horses27–29. BPVs have been found in the commercial semen of stallions30. OaPVs are responsible of cross-species infection in cattle resulting in bladder tumors17,31. OaPV DNA has been found in a sarcoid-like mass in the mouth of a pig32 as well as in equine sarcoids33,34 and in the vaginal virobiota of healthy mares35. OaPVs have been detected to be transcriptionally active in the equine semen36.

The Caprine papillomavirus (ChPV) family has two genotypes: Capra hircus papillomavirus 1 (ChPV1), that belongs to the genus Phipapillomavirus, and ChPV2 which is a unclassified Xipapillomavirus3. ChPV1 was isolated from teat papillomatosis cases in hair goats37, and ChPV2 was isolated from teat lesions of a Damascus goat38,39. Both ChPV1 and ChPV2 have been detected in the peripheral blood of healthy goats40.

Digital polymerase chain reaction (dPCR), an improved biotechnology of traditional PCR, is currently believed to be a method of choice for nucleic acid detection thus being very useful in the diagnosis of infectious diseases, including viral diseases41. dPCR has proven to be a very sensitive and accurate molecular diagnostic tool as it significantly increases detection of infectious agents, especially of low-abundance pathogens25,42–45. Traditional PCR-based detection of PV nucleic acids is a molecular assay regarded as a “gold standard” for the PV detection44,45. However, it has been shown that dPCR outperforms qPCR in detecting and quantifying animal PVs, including human ones. Therefore, it has been suggested that dPCR may serve as a reliable diagnostic method for studying PV biology35 as it allows the detection of very low viral DNA copy numbers, which may be particularly relevant in the context of subclinical or early-stage infections, where viral loads are expected to be minimal.

Here we investigate the microbial community of the healthy mare uterus in order to gain insights into its virobiota by an accurate identification of some unprecedented PV genotypes in uterine flushing samples using dPCR. The rationale for investigating PVs of large and small ruminants lies in the well-documented ability of certain papillomaviruses—particularly those belonging to the Deltapapillomavirus (δPV) genus—to cross species barriers and establish infection in heterologous hosts. Among papillomaviruses, these δPVs provide the strongest and most consistent evidence of interspecies transmission reported so far.

Results

We examined 105 uterine flushing samples from apparently healthy mares. Samples were considered positive only when PV DNA detection and quantification were confirmed by both digital PCR approaches. No significant differences in viral copy numbers were observed between the two methods.

Overall, PV DNA was detected in 27/105 samples (~ 25.7%; 95% CI 17.4–34.1%) by dPCR and in 18/105 samples (~ 17.1%; 95% CI 9.9–24.4%) using qPCR. The differences between the two molecular protocols were statistically significant using McNemar’s test (p˂ 0.005) (Fig. 1).

Detection of PV DNA in 27 (25.71%) and in 18 of the 105 uterine flushing samples (17.14%) through dPCR and qPCR. Differences were significant using McNemar’s test (p value ˂ 0.05).

All positive samples showed single infections, except for one case of co-infection. EcPV DNA was detected in 10/105 uterine flushing samples (9.5%; 95% CI 3.9–15.1) by dPCR and in 7/105 samples (6.7%; 95% CI 1.9–11.0) by qPCR. OaPV1 DNA was identified in 13/105 samples (12.4%; 95% CI 6.1–18.7%) by dPCR and in 9/105 samples (8.6%; 95% CI 3.2–13.9%) by qPCR. BPV DNA was detected in 4/105 samples (3.8%; 95% CI 0.2–7.5) by dPCR and in 2/105 (1.9%; 95% CI 0.0–4.5) by qPCR (Fig. 2).

Only one double infection composed of EcPV10/OaPV1 was identified. Among single infections, OaPV1 was the most prevalent type, detected in 13/105 uterine flushing samples using dPCR (12.4%) and in 9/105 (~ 8.6%) by qPCR. EcPV9 was the most frequently detected equine PV identified in 6/105 samples (~ 5.7%; 95% CI 1.3–10.2) by dPCR and in 3/105 samples (~ 2.9%; 95% CI 0.00–6.04) by qPCR. Two single infections by EcPV2 were consistently detected by both methods. BPV1 DNA was found in only one sample by dPCR, while qPCR failed to detect (Fig. 2). EcPV8, BPV13, ChPV1, ChPV2, OaPV2, OaPV3, and OaPV4 were not detected. Overall, most positive samples showed low viral copy numbers, suggesting a low-abundance viral presence consistent with limited viral burden in the uterine cavity. To control for potential contamination and false-positive results, a no-template control (NTC) was included in each analytical run. The absence of amplification in all NTCs confirmed the lack of detectable cross-contamination during sample processing. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study showing distinct EcPV, BPV, and OaPV genotypes in the uterine microbial community of healthy mares using dPCR, an emerging PV detection technology. Viral DNA copy numbers measured by dPCR were consistent with the expected inverse relationship with qPCR cycle threshold (Ct) values. In general, higher Ct values were associated with lower viral DNA copy numbers/µL. The analysis of the uterine microbiome remains challenging due to the very low microbial biomass. Digital PCR is a diagnostic procedure that is capable of detecting otherwise undetectable microbes, particularly viruses, at very low concentrations in the uterus of mares46–48. The higher analytical sensitivity of dPCR compared to qPCR is consistent with its partitioning-based design, which enables absolute quantification and improved detection of low-copy targets.

Overall, OaPV1 was the most detected papillomaviral agent of the endometrial virobiota. It is worth noting that OaPV1 was also the most detected genotype from the virobiota of vagina of healthy mares and commercial semen of stallions35,36. BPV1, BPV2, and BPV14 were the fibropapillomavirus genotypes found in the uterus of healthy mares in a few cases. The detection of OaPVs and BPVs in equine uterine flushing samples is consistent with previous reports describing the ability of fibropapillomaviruses to infect multiple host species17,29–31,35,36. Our findings document the presence of these viral DNAs within the uterus of clinically healthy mares, thereby extending our current knowledge to a previously underexplored anatomical site within the equine reproductive tract. We do not exclude that other OaPV and BPV genotypes may be present in the equine uterus, as our analysis was limited to a select panel of targets. Modern husbandry practices may increase sympatry among mammalian species. Therefore, direct and indirect exposure among sympatric hosts may facilitate host switching of OaPVs and BPVs. PV DNA has recently been detected in environmental matrices such as river water and hay, suggesting a potential role of feed and water in PV dissemination. In addition, insects have been hypothesized as potential vectors for PVs. The potential role of iatrogenic transmission via fomites should also be considered as a plausible route. However, our findings should be interpreted with caution, as detection frequency does not necessarily reflect biological relevance or active infection. EcPV9 was the most frequently detected equine genotype in this study, with a prevalence of approximately 5.7%. Previous traditional real time PCR-based studies reported a 5.3% prevalence of EcPV9 DNA from vaginal vestibulum brush samples from asymptomatic horses49, despite the microbial biomass of the vagina is believed to be 100 to 10,000 times higher than microbial amount of the uterus. These molecular findings are further evidence of the high sensitivity of dPCR in PV detection. EcPVs have previously been detected in asymptomatic horses, indicating that their presence is not necessarily associated with overt clinical disease. Therefore, the detection of EcPV9 and EcPV10 in clinically healthy mares may reflect their inclusion within the normal uterine virome or alternatively, subclinical or latent infections,. However, the biological significance of these findings remains unclear, as the absence of clinical signs does not exclude a potential role in early or subclinical stages of disease. Further investigations are required to clarify whether these viruses, in these anatomical sites, contribute to equine reproductive disorders. No ChPVs were detected, which may suggest that ChPVs spread in a more host-specific manner. Until now, the majority of studies on the microbial composition of the reproductive tract of mares have focused on bacteria and fungi, while little is known about its viral community48,50,51. This study showed that the viral community of the uterus of healthy mares usually shares some viruses with the viral community of vagina since fibropapillomaviruses have also been found to be part of the vaginal virobiota of clinically healthy mares35.

Studies in humans and large domestic animals indicate a relationship between uterine microbial composition and endometrial receptivity47. Indeed, it has been suggested that microbial communities of female genital tract are closely associated with gynecological diseases. However, the impact of uterine virobiota within the reproductive tract on disease and fertility in mares is still unknown and requires further investigation51. Our understanding of PVs infecting horses is limited, as little is known about their mechanisms of pathogenicity, routes of transmission, the existence of a carrier state, or the impact of PVs on host health. Therefore, the biological significance of PVs inhabiting the upper female reproductive tract of mares remains to be fully understood, which limits the ability to draw causal inferences. It has been suggested that commensal uterine microbiota plays a crucial role in modulating the immune properties of the equine uterus, which may be crucial for maintaining the health of mare and fetus46. The equine endometrium is populated by Th1 and Th2 lymphocytes, dendritic cells, regulatory T cell (Treg), along with cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-4, IL-13)52,53. PVs are known to impair immune signaling pathways mediated by retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I)-like receptors (RLRs)54, and cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING)55, which may lead to persistent PV infection. Increasing evidence suggests a potential link between persistent PV infection and both male and female infertility56. In particular, persistent PV infection of the genital tract is believed to increase the risk of pregnancy failure and abortion. Recently, persistent EcPV2 infection has been studied in Chinese horses. EcPV2 was detected in lungs of aborted fetuses of Yili horses, which could indicate that EcPV2 may be a new pathogen responsible for causing abortions14. Furthermore, both BPVs and OaPVs have been associated with congenital infections resulting in reproductive disorders57,58. Future studies should further explore the relationship between PV infection and reproductive disorders in horses, in order to clarify the possible role of PVs, if any, in mare reproductive failure. In particular, a deeper understanding of the uterine virobiota and its interactions with the immune system could significantly advance our knowledge of the pathophysiology of equine fertility since viruses are responsible for 5%–15% of infectious equine abortions59. It is important to recognize that the study of the viruses of male and female genital tract is an emerging field and that our understanding of how these viruses interact with the immune system is currently minimal.

Regarding colonization mechanisms of the upper genital tract (UGT) such as the uterus, UGT microbes may derive from microbes that ascend from the vagina, either directly or carried by semen. It is conceivable that semen harboring PVs plays a crucial role in PV epidemiology and genotype distribution in horses via natural mating and/or instrumental insemination. Our suggestions appear to be supported by the fact that infectious pathogens found in the semen of stallions are usually isolated from the genital tracts of mares after natural mating. Communication between the vagina and uterus is facilitated by peristaltic contractions, cervical fluid consistency, and immune response. UGT colonization could also occur via hematogenous spread. However, we do not exclude the possibility that certain gynecological procedures could transfer some viruses from the vagina to the uterus.

Methods

This study was conducted on 105 apparently healthy mares in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

The animal collection and handling and tissue sampling procedures were performed in accordance with the ethics guidelines of the Naples University Federico II and Italian Law. All animal studies were approved by the Institutional Ethical Animal Care and Use Committee of the Naples University Federico II (Approval number: PG/2025/0069658). Permission to collect material was obtained by oral informed consent from the animals’ owners who were previously informed and in agreement with the purpose and methods used.

Uterine sample collection

The 105 samples included in this study were obtained from 105 4-8-year-old mares that live in different geographical areas such as Campania, Basilicata, and Calabria regions in southern Italy. The samples consisted of the uterine flushing performed together with clinicians specialized in equine reproduction. All samples were obtained from mares during estrus since ultrasonographic evaluation showed follicle with a diameter > 35 mm and endometrial edema > 2 on a scale of 0/5. The mares were led individually into stocks, and their tails were wrapped in a plastic tail bag and tied to the side. The entire perianal area was thoroughly scrupped with warm water and betadine for three times and dried with a paper towel. For uterine lavage, a sterile flushing catheter was passed through the vagina and cervix. A volume of 1000 mL of lactated Ringer’s solution was infused into the uterus, and drained back to its recipient by gravity flow. The drained fluid was subdivided into three 50-mL sterile Falcon tubes. One tube was used for culture, and two were snap frozen and stored for molecular analysis.

All procedures were performed on conscious mares under routine clinical conditions. No general anesthesia was required; when necessary, animals were gently restrained in stocks to minimize stress and discomfort.

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from lavage fluid centrifuged pellets using DNAeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen, Wilmington, DE, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instruction. A 25 mL aliquot of each uterine flushing sample was centrifuged in a Falcon tube at 4000 rpm (2500 x g) for 30 min at 4 °C. DNA was extracted from 200 µL of the pelleted uterine flushing samples. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA was quantified using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific NanoDrop One Microvolume UV Spectrophotometer).

Digital polymerase chain reaction (dPCR)

Samples were processed at the Istituto Zooprofilattico Sperimentale del Mezzogiorno in Portici using droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) and Caserta using QuantStudio Absolute Q Digital PCR System (Life Technologies, Waltham, MA, USA). Each sample was evaluated by these different digital PCR systems in triplicate to ensure accuracy.

The reaction was performed in a final volume of 22 µL, consisting of 11 µL of ddPCR Supermix for Probes (2X; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA), 0.9 µM of primer, and 0.25 µM of probe, along with 7 µL of sample DNA corresponding to 100 ng.The primer and probe sequences for OaPVs, BPVs, EcPVs, and ChPVs (Supplemental Table S2), as well as the tool to generate droplets and a thermal profile, have been described previously31,33.Primers and probes were selected based on the availability of validated and genotype-specific molecular assays targeting conserved genomic regions of well-characterized PVs. This approach ensures high analytical sensitivity and specificity for the targeted genotypes24,25,30,31,42. The droplets were detected using a Bio-Rad QX200 Droplet Reader. Data were analyzed to determine OaPV, BPV, EcPV and ChPV genotypes and copy numbers using QuantaSoft software version 1.4 (Bio-Rad). A manual threshold line was used to discriminate between positive (blue) and negative (gray) droplets. Samples and controls were analyzed in duplicate to ensure accuracy. Samples were considered PV-positive if, in both analyses, at least three droplets containing PV amplicons were present, as suggested for PV infections in humans and veterinary medicine36,41. Samples with very few positive droplets were re-analyzed to ensure that these low-copy number samples were not due to cross-contamination.

Droplet dPCR (ddPCR)

For ddPCR (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA), a QX200 ddPCR system was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The reaction was performed in a final volume of 22 µL, consisting of 11 µL of ddPCR Supermix for Probes (2X; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA), 0.9 µM of primer, and 0.25 µM of probe, along with 7 µL of sample DNA corresponding to 100 ng.

The primer and probe sequences for OaPVs, BPVs, EcPVs, and ChPVs (Supplemental Table S2), as well as the tool to generate droplets and a thermal profile, have been described previously31,33.

Primers and probes were selected based on the availability of validated and genotype-specific molecular assays targeting conserved genomic regions of well-characterized PVs. This approach ensures high analytical sensitivity and specificity for the targeted genotypes24,25,30,31,42. The droplets were detected using a Bio-Rad QX200 Droplet Reader. Data were analyzed to determine OaPV, BPV, EcPV and ChPV genotypes and copy numbers using QuantaSoft software version 1.4 (Bio-Rad). A manual threshold line was used to discriminate between positive (blue) and negative (gray) droplets. Samples and controls were analyzed in duplicate to ensure accuracy. Samples were considered PV-positive if, in both analyses, at least three droplets containing PV amplicons were present, as suggested for PV infections in humans and veterinary medicine36,41. Samples with very few positive droplets were re-analyzed to ensure that these low-copy number samples were not due to cross-contamination.

B.QuantStudio™ Absolute Q™ Digital PCR SystemFor Absolute Q digital PCR (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), the reaction was prepared in a final volume of 10 µL, containing 7.5 µL (100 ng) of template DNA, 2 µL of Absolute Q DNA Digital PCR Master Mix (5X) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA USA), and 0.5 µL of Digital PCR assay (20X). As same primers and probes of ddPCR were used (Supplemental Table S2). Runs were carried out under the following thermal cycling conditions: 96 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 15 s. Positive and no-template controls (sterile water) were included. A fluorescence threshold of 4000 RFU was empirically established to distinguish positive from background partitions based on fluorescence amplitude distribution. Samples were considered positive when at least three partitions exhibited fluorescence values above the threshold and were clearly separated from the negative population. The amount of DNA and the thermal profile were carefully optimized to ensure reliable detection of low-abundance targets. Data was analyzed using the Applied Biosystems™ QuantStudio™ Absolute Q™ Digital PCR Software (version 6.2.1).

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

The qPCR assays were performed using the CFX96 Real-Time System of the C1000 Touch™ Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The thermal cycling conditions were: 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 10 min, and 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 58 °C for 60 s. The reaction mixture was prepared by adding 7 µL of template DNA (100 ng genomic DNA), 10 µL of 2x SsoAdvanced™ Universal Probes Supermix (BioRad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA), 1 µL of target probe (FAM)/primer mix in a total volume of 20 µL. DNA quality and concentration were assessed using a Nanodrop (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA). Each sample was amplified in triplicate. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using CFX Maestro™ (Bio-Rad). The same samples used as positive controls for dPCR were also tested using qPCR.

Positive controls

The positive controls of OaPV1 and OaPV 2 were artificially created plasmids (vector: pUCIDT-AMP), containing 270 and 603 base pairs (bp) of the sequence of E5 and the major capsid protein, respectively (IDT, Integrated DNA Technologies, IA, USA). The positive control of OaPV3 was a plasmid (vector: pUC19) that contained the complete genome of OaPV3, and the positive control tissue for OaPV4 was a cutaneous fibropapillomatosis sample, both from the Department of Veterinary Medicine of Sassari University (kind gifts from Prof. A. Alberti).

The BPV positive controls comprised: BPV-1 DNA from a zebra sarcoid (a kind gift provided by Dr. Altamura, University of Naples, Italy); BPV-2 clone DNA (a kind gift by Dr A. Venuti, IRCSS Regina Elena, National Cancer Institute, Rome, Italy); and BPV-13 and BPV-14 DNA from bovine bladder tumours from our laboratories18,60.

As far as EcPV is concerned, positive controls were artificially created plasmids (vector: pUCIDT-AMP), containing 62 base pairs (bp) of the sequence of the EcPV2 E5, 65 bp of the EcPV8 as well as EcPV9 L1 sequences, respectively, and 66 bp of the sequence of the EcPV10 L1 (IDT, Integrated DNA Technologies, IA, USA). Positive controls were artificially created by inserting 270 and 400 bp of the ChPV1 and ChPV2 E7 sequences, respectively, into a plasmid (vector: pUCIDT-AMP) (IDT, Integrated DNA Technologies, IA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 7.00 for Windows (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, California, USA). The difference in prevalence detected by ddPCR and qPCR was tested using McNemar’s test. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. Prevalence rates of PV genotypes were expressed as percentages with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI).
