Association of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic burden with recurrence after first ablation in patients with persistent atrial fibrillation
Authors: Peiyang Zheng, Zhuoran Jia, Huimin Wang, Xiaohe Wu, Ao Liang, Danni Li, Yinguang Fan, Xiaohu Li, Ren Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecurrence after first radiofrequency catheter ablation remains common in patients with non-valvular persistent atrial fibrillation. Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic burden may contribute to atrial remodeling and adverse ablation outcomes, but its relationship with post-ablation recurrence and downstream atrial substrate markers remains incompletely understood.MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled 730 patients with non-valvular persistent atrial fibrillation who underwent first-time radiofrequency catheter ablation. Cardiac, kidney, and metabolic domains were integrated into a weighted cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic burden score, and patients were stratified by tertiles. Sequential Cox regression models were constructed to evaluate the association between burden and recurrence after adjustment for clinical characteristics and downstream atrial substrate markers, including left atrial diameter, log(BNP), and fine f-wave. Model discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s C-index and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic analysis.ResultsDuring follow-up, 228 patients (31.2%) developed recurrence. Compared with the low-burden tertile, intermediate- and high-burden tertiles were associated with a significantly higher risk of recurrence in the primary adjusted model (intermediate vs. low: HR = 1.49, 95% CI 1.05–2.11; high vs. low: HR = 1.60, 95% CI 1.13–2.27). Further adjustment for left atrial diameter, log(BNP), and fine f-wave attenuated these associations, suggesting partial statistical overlap between upstream multisystem burden and downstream atrial substrate abnormalities. In continuous analyses, each 1-standard deviation increase in weighted burden remained associated with recurrence risk. Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is one of the most common sustained arrhythmias and is closely associated with increased risks of stroke, heart failure, and all-cause mortality [1, 2]. Radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) has become an important rhythm-control strategy for symptomatic AF; however, recurrence after ablation remains frequent in patients with non-valvular persistent atrial fibrillation (NV-PersAF), limiting long-term benefits [3, 4]. Achieving more precise preprocedural risk stratification and identifying modifiable multisystem risk factors are therefore key priorities in contemporary rhythm management.

Existing evidence suggests that AF recurrence is not driven solely by “focal triggers”; rather, a more fundamental determinant is the ongoing structural and electrophysiological remodeling of the atrial substrate [5–7]. Substrate markers such as enlarged left atrial diameter (LAD), elevated myocardial stress biomarkers (e.g., B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), and electrocardiographic fine f-wave are closely associated with recurrence risk and may represent downstream phenotypes linking multisystem cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) burden to post-ablation outcomes [8–10]. However, these measures largely represent “downstream phenotypes,” reflecting an established remodeling state. As such, they offer limited insight into upstream systemic drivers and do not fully address why certain patients are more prone to develop an adverse substrate and subsequently recur. In contrast, commonly used clinical risk scores (e.g., CHA₂DS₂-VASc) were primarily developed for thromboembolic risk assessment [11]. Although they correlate with overall cardiovascular risk, their ability to predict post-ablation recurrence is modest, and they do not adequately capture multisystem pathological processes such as metabolic derangements and renal dysfunction.

In recent years, the CKM syndrome framework has highlighted that the development and progression of cardiovascular disease are often jointly driven by the interplay among cardiac dysfunction, kidney impairment, and metabolic dysregulation [12, 13]. In AF, metabolic abnormalities may promote atrial fibrosis through lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammatory pathways; renal impairment may compromise atrial electrical stability via uremic toxin accumulation, neurohormonal activation, and chronic inflammation; and myocardial stress/congestive status may further increase atrial pressure load and structural remodeling [14–16]. Insulin resistance, a hallmark of metabolic dysfunction, promotes atrial structural and electrical remodeling and has been linked to adverse rhythm outcomes [17, 18]. We therefore selected the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index and the triglyceride-to-HDL-C (TG/HDL-C) ratio as surrogate measures of insulin resistance. These indices are readily available in routine clinical practice and have been validated as robust markers of metabolic risk, supporting their use for quantifying metabolic burden. Despite the strong mechanistic coherence of these pathways, integrated quantification of multisystem CKM burden remains limited in post-ablation recurrence risk stratification. Previous studies have focused on single markers or isolated organ-system abnormalities, lacking a parsimonious index system that simultaneously captures burdens across the three axes for clinical stratification [12, 19]. Moreover, the relationships between upstream CKM burden and downstream atrial substrate phenotypes (structural remodeling, stress phenotypes, and electrical remodeling), and the extent to which adjustment for these phenotypes attenuates observed risk associations, have not been systematically tested in ablation cohorts.

Accordingly, we operationalized the CKM framework into a quantitative three-axis scoring system (cardiac, kidney, and metabolic) and an integrated weighted CKM burden score in a first-time RFCA cohort of NV-PersAF. We aimed to (1) examine associations between CKM burden and post-ablation recurrence under sequential adjustment, (2) evaluate the extent to which downstream atrial substrate markers (LAD, log(BNP), and fine f-wave) statistically attenuate the CKM–recurrence association, and (3) dissect axis-specific associations and exploratory combined phenotypes. We hypothesized that higher multisystem CKM burden would be associated with higher recurrence risk, and that myocardial stress and renal dysfunction may jointly characterize a higher-risk phenotype.

Methods

Study design and participants

This single-center retrospective cohort study enrolled consecutive patients with non-valvular persistent AF (NV-PersAF) undergoing first-time radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA). Baseline pre-ablation data included demographics, comorbidities, laboratory tests, and echocardiographic/electrocardiographic parameters. Follow-up started on the ablation date and continued to the first recurrence event or last visit (Time). The endpoint was post-ablation AF recurrence. In total, 730 patients were included.

The protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (PJ 2024-13-76) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because this was a retrospective study using de-identified existing clinical data.

RFCA procedure

All procedures were performed by operators with ≥ 10 years of experience using a standardized strategy: circumferential pulmonary vein isolation (CPVI) plus left atrial substrate modification. Substrate modification comprised posterior wall box isolation (BOX; endpoint: posterior wall electrical isolation) and linear ablation (e.g., mitral isthmus and anterior wall lines), with additional posterior wall/septal/free-wall lines as guided by residual inducibility and/or electroanatomic mapping. Bidirectional block was required for all linear lesions. Ethanol infusion in the vein of Marshall was used when mitral isthmus block was difficult or perimitral tachyarrhythmia was present. Typical flutter was treated by cavotricuspid isthmus ablation with bidirectional block. The procedural endpoint was complete bilateral PV isolation with no sustained inducible atrial tachyarrhythmia after isoproterenol provocation.

Follow-up strategy and endpoints

Patients were followed via outpatient visits, telephone contacts, and ambulatory ECG monitoring. A 12-lead ECG and 24-h Holter were scheduled at 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months; additional ECG was performed within 48 h if symptoms occurred. Data were independently entered by two investigators and adjudicated by a third reviewer if needed.

AF recurrence was defined as any atrial tachyarrhythmia lasting ≥ 30 s occurring after a 3-month blanking period and documented by ECG or Holter. Persistent early recurrence of AF (ERAF) was defined as atrial tachyarrhythmia ≥ 30 s within 3 months that (i) persisted or recurred through the end of blanking, and/or (ii) required drug adjustment, cardioversion, or repeat ablation, and/or (iii) remained evident after blanking; this was used for phenotyping/sensitivity analyses only.

Definition of CKM burden framework

This study did not aim to diagnose or stage CKM per se; rather, it quantified baseline multisystem cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic burden under a prespecified three-axis framework to evaluate its association with post-ablation recurrence.

Construction of the weighted CKM burden score

The weighted CKM burden score was constructed using a prespecified three-axis framework. The cardiac axis was defined as a binary composite indicating the presence of either coronary heart disease or heart failure [C = I (CHD = 1 or HF = 1)]. The kidney axis was defined as the maximum of reverse-coded standardized eGFR and standardized BUN/CRE [K = max (− Z(eGFR), Z(BUN/CRE))]. The metabolic axis was defined as the maximum of standardized TyG, TG/HDL, and waist circumference [M = max(Z(TyG), Z(TG/HDL), Z(WC))] (Table S1). Continuous variables were standardized using sample-based Z scores. Because lower eGFR reflects worse renal function, eGFR was reverse-coded before within-axis synthesis so that higher aligned values consistently indicated greater adverse burden. The three axis scores were then simultaneously entered into a Cox proportional hazards model for recurrence to derive axis-level weighting coefficients (βC,βK,βM). The final weighted CKM burden score was calculated as (Table S2). The continuous weighted CKM burden score was further standardized for per-1 SD analyses and categorized into tertiles for stratified analyses (Table S3).

No missing values were present for score-construction variables in the analytic cohort. Therefore, no imputation was required for CHD, HF, eGFR, BUN/CRE, TyG, TG/HDL, or waist circumference, and all 730 patients contributed to the score-construction dataset. To assess the robustness of the within-axis max rule, we additionally performed sensitivity analyses using alternative aggregation strategies, including mean-based aggregation and PCA-derived continuous axis scores for the kidney and metabolic domains.

LAD, log(BNP), and fine f-wave were considered downstream atrial substrate markers and were not included in wCKM construction. Tertile-based HRs were reported in the main text; per-1 SD results were provided in the Supplement.

Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals (cox.zph). Multicollinearity was evaluated by variance inflation factors (VIF > 5 as a sensitivity threshold). Attenuation of the wCKM association after adding downstream substrates was quantified by comparing coefficients across models (descriptive attenuation, not formal mediation).

Formal causal mediation analysis was not performed in the present study for the following reasons. Firstly, all CKM exposures and atrial substrate markers were measured at a single baseline time point, precluding the establishment of temporal ordering. Secondly, no repeated-measures or longitudinal mediator data were available, what’s more, a valid causal mediation framework requires assumptions that are unlikely to hold in a retrospective observational design. Accordingly, the observed attenuation is interpreted as descriptive statistical attenuation, reflecting the overlap in prognostic information, rather than as evidence of a causal mediating pathway.

Associations between CKM axes and atrial substrates were examined using linear regression (continuous substrates) or logistic regression (fine f-wave), modeling axis scores per 1 SD with prespecified adjustment. Axis-specific Cox analyses mutually adjusted the three axis scores. Prespecified subgroup analyses used interaction terms in Cox models with likelihood ratio tests. For exploratory visualization of the combined BNP/BUN–CRE phenotype, BNP and BUN/CRE were dichotomized using clinically interpretable, non-data-driven thresholds. BNP was classified as high at ≥ 100 pg/mL, and BUN/CRE as high at > 20. These thresholds were selected a priori for descriptive subgroup visualization and were not optimized against recurrence outcomes.

To address potential optimism arising from deriving and evaluating the weighted CKM burden score within the same dataset, we performed bootstrap internal validation with 1000 resamples. Importantly, in each bootstrap sample, the entire score-construction procedure was repeated, including estimation of the axis-level Cox coefficients and derivation of the weighted CKM burden score. The model was then refit in the bootstrap sample, and its performance was evaluated both in the bootstrap sample and in the original dataset. Optimism was estimated as the mean difference in performance between these two settings, and optimism-corrected C-index values were calculated accordingly. Calibration slope and the corresponding shrinkage factor were also estimated.

To further reduce dependence on a single random partition and to provide a hold-out validation framework, the 70:30 derivation–testing split-sample validation was repeated 1000 times. In each repetition, axis-level coefficients were estimated exclusively in the derivation cohort and then applied unchanged to the hold-out testing cohort, in which the association between derivation-derived wCKM and recurrence and model discrimination were evaluated.

Model performance was evaluated by Harrell’s C-index and time-dependent ROC/AUC at prespecified times; clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis at 24 months.

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.1) using the survival package and related packages for data processing and figure generation.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

A total of 730 patients with NV-PersAF were included. During follow-up, 228 patients experienced recurrence (31.2%), whereas 502 remained recurrence-free. After stratification by CKM burden tertiles (Low/Intermediate/High), the corresponding sample sizes were 244, 243, and 243, respectively.

Among recurrence-free patients, increasing CKM burden was associated with a progressively lower proportion of males (78.0%→69.3%→61.5%, P = 0.004) and older age (P = 0.012). The prevalence of diabetes increased (P = 0.037), and the proportions of CHD and HF rose markedly (both P < 0.001). CHA₂DS₂-VASc scores also increased stepwise across tertiles (P < 0.001). In parallel, metabolic-related indices—including BMI, waist WC, TG/HDL-C, TyG, and UA—increased with higher CKM burden (multiple P < 0.05). Renal function, as reflected by eGFR, declined across tertiles (P < 0.001), while BUN/CRE increased (P < 0.001).

Among patients with recurrence, a similar overall pattern was observed. The high CKM burden group was older (P = 0.036) and had higher prevalences of CHD and HF (P = 0.008 and P = 0.001, respectively), along with higher CHA₂DS₂-VASc scores (P = 0.002). BMI and WC were also higher (P = 0.010 and P = 0.009, respectively). Renal function was worse, with lower eGFR and higher BUN/CRE (both P < 0.001). In addition, TG/HDL-C and TyG were higher (both P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Sensitivity analysis: CKM burden (per 1 SD) and PH assumption

After standardization, wCKM was entered into the Cox model per 1-SD increase. Each 1-SD increase in CKM burden was significantly associated with a higher risk of recurrence (HR = 1.25, 95% CI 1.10–1.43; P = 0.00085).

Tests of the proportional hazards (PH) assumption indicated no meaningful violation in the main model (illustrated using the model including CKM tertiles plus CHA₂DS₂-VASc): CKM tertile P = 0.539, CHA₂DS₂-VASc P = 0.454, and global P = 0.700 (Table S4).

Sensitivity analyses using alternative within-axis aggregation strategies

To assess whether the main findings depended on the max-based within-axis summarization rule, we repeated the analyses using alternative aggregation strategies, including mean-based aggregation and PCA-derived axis scores. Across all three specifications, higher CKM burden remained directionally associated with a higher recurrence risk in the primary model adjusted for CHA₂DS₂-VASc. Compared with the low-burden tertile, the high-burden tertile was associated with increased recurrence risk for the max-based score (HR 1.63, 95% CI 1.15–2.31, P = 0.006), the mean-based score (HR 2.03, 95% CI 1.42–2.89, P < 0.001), and the PCA-based score (HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.14–2.31, P = 0.007) (Table S6).

After further adjustment for downstream substrate markers (LAD, log[BNP], and fine f-wave), effect estimates were attenuated across all aggregation methods, although the overall direction of association remained similar. In this fully adjusted setting, the high-burden tertile remained significantly associated with recurrence for the mean-based specification (HR 1.67, 95% CI 1.17–2.39, P = 0.005), whereas the corresponding estimates for the max-based and PCA-based specifications were of similar direction but did not reach statistical significance (Table S6B).

Discrimination metrics were broadly comparable across aggregation methods. In the primary model, the C-index was 0.618 for the max-based score, 0.631 for the mean-based score, and 0.624 for the PCA-based score. Bootstrap comparisons of ΔC-index showed no material discrimination difference between the max-based score and either the mean-based score (ΔC = -0.013, 95% CI -0.031 to 0.005) or the PCA-based score (ΔC = -0.006, 95% CI -0.032 to 0.019) (Table S7). Similar findings were observed in the combined models.

To address potential optimism arising from within-sample derivation of the weighted CKM burden score, we performed bootstrap internal validation with 1000 resamples, repeating the entire score-construction process in each bootstrap sample. The apparent C-index of the primary wCKM-based model was 0.620, with a mean optimism of 0.006, yielding an optimism-corrected C-index of 0.613. The apparent calibration slope was 1.000, and the optimism-corrected calibration slope was 0.967, corresponding to a uniform shrinkage factor of 0.967 (Table S8). As an additional robustness analysis, we performed repeated 70:30 derivation–hold-out testing validation with 1000 random splits. In each split, axis-level coefficients were estimated exclusively in the derivation cohort and then applied unchanged to the testing cohort. Across repeated testing cohorts, the association between derivation-derived wCKM and recurrence remained directionally consistent. The mean HRs were 1.49 for intermediate versus low burden, 1.64 for high versus low burden, and 1.20 per 1-SD increase in wCKM. The corresponding proportions of splits with HR > 1 were 87.9%, 94.9%, and 97.3%, respectively. The mean testing C-index was 0.625 for the tertile model and 0.620 for the per-SD model (Table S9). Collectively, these internal validation analyses suggested limited optimism and directional robustness of the wCKM framework, while external validation remains warranted.

Overall, sensitivity analyses confirmed that higher CKM burden was consistently associated with recurrence across max-, mean-, and PCA-based aggregation, with modest attenuation after adjusting for substrate markers. Discrimination and calibration were similar across methods, and bootstrap validation together with repeated split-sample validation suggested limited optimism and directional robustness of the wCKM-based model.

Multicollinearity assessment

Variance inflation factor (VIF) diagnostics were performed for Models B–D and the axis-specific models. All covariates had VIF values below the prespecified threshold (VIF ≤ 5), indicating no substantial multicollinearity or parameter instability (Fig. S1). Therefore, the attenuation of CKM-related effects observed in Models C/D is more likely to reflect explanatory contributions from downstream atrial substrate markers rather than being driven by collinearity.

Attenuation analysis

To quantify the attenuation pattern observed in the tertile-based sequential models on a comparable continuous scale, we performed a coefficient-based attenuation analysis using standardized wCKM (per 1 SD). Relative to Model B, the association between wCKM and recurrence attenuated stepwise after adding downstream atrial substrate markers. Specifically, in continuous analyses with wCKM entered per 1-SD increase, the wCKM–recurrence effect size (expressed as the log hazard ratio, log[HR]) was attenuated by 24.6% after adding LAD and log(BNP) (Model C), and by a further 39.1% after additionally including fine f-wave (Model D) (Fig. S2). These findings indicate that part of the prognostic information captured by wCKM overlaps statistically with downstream atrial substrate phenotypes—atrial structural remodeling, myocardial stress, and electrical remodeling.

Model discrimination: C-index and incremental value

Model discrimination analyses showed that Model B (main model) achieved a C-index of 0.618 (95% CI 0.580–0.656). After adding LAD and log(BNP), the C-index increased to 0.694 (0.659–0.728) in Model C, corresponding to an incremental gain versus Model B of ΔC = 0.077 (0.049–0.110). After further adding fine f-wave, Model D achieved a C-index of 0.729 (0.698–0.760), with ΔC = 0.111 (0.079–0.149) versus Model B and ΔC = 0.035 (0.016–0.057) versus Model C (Table S13).

Time-dependent ROC analyses demonstrated a clear stepwise improvement in discrimination with progressive model adjustment. At t = 6 months, the AUCs for Models B/C/D were 0.631/0.717/0.761, respectively; at t = 12 months, 0.636/0.721/0.761; and at t = 24 months, 0.624/0.718/0.761 (Fig. 4). Consistently, the AUC(t) curves showed that Model D maintained the highest AUC throughout follow-up, followed by Model C, with Model B performing the worst (Fig. S3). Collectively, these findings indicate that, on top of CKM burden plus CHA₂DS₂-VASc, adding LAD/log(BNP) and subsequently fine f-wave yields a stable and sustained improvement in discriminatory performance.

Decision curve analysis (DCA) evaluated clinical net benefit at t = 24 months. Across most threshold probabilities, Models B, C, and D achieved higher net benefit than the “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies, with Model D consistently providing the greatest net benefit over the key threshold range (approximately 0.05–0.45), followed by Model C; Model B also outperformed “treat-all/none” (Fig. S4). These findings suggest that stepwise incorporation of downstream atrial substrate markers not only improves statistical discrimination but also enhances potential clinical decision value.

To further clarify the incremental contribution of wCKM beyond established atrial substrate markers, we compared a clinical-only model, a CKM-only model, a substrate-only model, and a combined model (Table S14). Relative to the clinical-only model, the CKM-only model showed improved discrimination, with the C-index increasing from 0.601 to 0.618 (ΔC-index 0.020, 95% CI 0.003–0.044). As expected, the substrate-only model showed substantially stronger discrimination than the CKM-only model (C-index 0.726 vs. 0.618), consistent with the more proximal relationship of LAD, log(BNP), and fine f-wave to recurrence risk. The combined model achieved the highest point estimate for discrimination (C-index 0.729), but the incremental gain beyond the substrate-only model was modest (ΔC-index = 0.005, 95% CI 0.000 to 0.014). Reclassification analyses similarly suggested limited but non-zero incremental value after adding wCKM to the substrate-only model (IDI 0.033, 95% CI 0.001–0.097), whereas the continuous NRI was small and not clearly significant overall (Table S14).

Overall, discrimination improved stepwise: adding LAD/log(BNP) and fine f-wave to CKM + clinical variables increased the C-index from 0.618 to 0.729. Time-dependent AUCs and decision curves confirmed sustained predictive and clinical benefit. Substrate markers contributed most, while wCKM added minimal incremental discrimination beyond the substrate-only model.

Discussion

In this retrospective NV-PersAF first-ablation cohort, we developed and internally evaluated an integrated CKM burden framework comprising three axis-specific scores and a weighted overall score (wCKM). Higher CKM burden was associated with recurrence risk, but this association was progressively attenuated after adjustment for downstream atrial substrate markers (LAD, log(BNP), and fine f-wave), indicating partial statistical overlap between multisystem CKM burden and downstream atrial substrate phenotypes rather than proving a causal upstream–downstream pathway. Kidney and metabolic axes showed stronger statistical associations than the binary cardiac axis after mutual adjustment, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the coarser binary construction of the cardiac axis. Collectively, these findings support a clinically relevant framework integrating upstream multisystem CKM burden with downstream substrate phenotypes for post-ablation risk stratification.

Upstream CKM burden and recurrence: from single markers to multisystem integration

Prior studies have linked metabolic abnormalities (e.g., higher TG/HDL-C and insulin resistance), renal impairment (e.g., reduced eGFR and uremic toxin–related inflammation), and clinical cardiovascular phenotypes to AF onset and recurrence [9, 15, 20–22]. However, most evidence has focused on a single system or individual markers, limiting the ability to capture—preprocedurally and in a parsimonious manner—the true pathological landscape of “multisystem co-driving.” In this study, we decomposed CKM burden into three axes (cardiac, kidney, and metabolic) and further integrated them into a weighted continuous overall score, enabling information from different systems to be expressed on a comparable scale and thereby providing a holistic quantification of the multisystem risk background. Our findings show that higher CKM burden was associated with increased recurrence risk, with robust results when modeled continuously per SD. This pattern suggests that CKM burden is unlikely to be driven by any single abnormality alone; instead, it may reflect the cumulative effects of chronic multisystem stress and injury over time [12, 23]. It is worth noting that atrial myopathy constitutes a central element in the pathophysiological cascade of atrial fibrillation and is deeply associated with CKM factors [24, 25]. The association patterns observed in this study, linking the metabolic and kidney axis burdens with enlarged LAD and the presence of fine f-wave, can be understood from the perspective that systemic metabolic dysregulation, inflammation, and hemodynamic stress jointly drive atrial myopathy, encompassing structural, electrical, and autonomic remodeling.

Interpreting the attenuation phenomenon: downstream atrial substrate phenotypes as statistical explanatory markers of the CKM–recurrence association

Notably, the association between CKM burden and recurrence was substantially attenuated after adjustment for LAD and log(BNP), and this attenuation became more pronounced after further inclusion of fine f-wave. It must be emphasized that this attenuation pattern should not be interpreted as evidence of a formal causal mediation effect or as definitive proof of an upstream to downstream pathogenic pathway [26]. Because all CKM indicators and atrial substrate phenotypes were measured at a single baseline time point and the study did not employ a longitudinal mediation framework, temporal ordering cannot be established, and causal inference is not possible.

Rather, this attenuation indicates that part of the prognostic information carried by CKM burden overlaps statistically with the downstream phenotypes of atrial structural remodeling, myocardial stress, and electrical remodeling [10, 27, 28]. In this context, LAD, BNP, and fine f-wave may be viewed as more proximate substrate-related risk markers that absorb part of the recurrence signal associated with upstream multisystem burden [29]. Accordingly, these findings are best interpreted as supporting an integrated risk architecture in which upstream CKM burden and downstream atrial substrate abnormalities jointly contribute to recurrence risk stratification, rather than as proof of a formal upstream–downstream causal pathway.

Axis-specific associations within the CKM framework

When all three axes were entered simultaneously, the kidney and metabolic axes retained statistical associations with recurrence, whereas the binary cardiac axis was attenuated. This pattern may reflect two considerations. First, the cardiac axis in our study was constructed primarily from clinical CVD phenotypes, which may provide a relatively coarse representation of cardiac burden. Second, the prognostic information captured by the cardiac axis may overlap more strongly with downstream myocardial stress (BNP) and atrial structural remodeling (LAD), and thus becomes more attenuated after sequential adjustment for these substrate markers. Therefore, the apparent weaker performance of the cardiac axis should be interpreted primarily as a methodological limitation of the current axis construction rather than evidence of lower physiological importance.

Renal impairment and metabolic dysregulation may participate in the inflammatory/oxidative stress milieu, neurohormonal activation, and atrial fibrotic remodeling, thereby providing biologically plausible pathways linking these axes to recurrence risk [15, 30–32]. From an actionability standpoint, strengthening preprocedural identification and optimization of renal function and metabolic status may represent the most practically valuable entry point for applying the CKM framework in post-ablation recurrence management.

The mild inverse association between the metabolic axis and log(BNP) should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect case-mix characteristics, treatment patterns, or competing clinical phenotypes in this ablation cohort rather than a simple biologic antagonism.

Combined BNP/BUN–CRE phenotype: potential clinical implications

In exploratory analyses using non-data-driven clinical thresholds, concurrent elevation of BNP and BUN/CRE identified a higher-risk combined phenotype, although formal multiplicative interaction was not established. These findings suggest that, within the CKM framework, myocardial stress and kidney dysfunction may jointly characterize patients with higher recurrence risk [12, 33–35]. On the one hand, renal impairment–related volume overload and neurohormonal activation can aggravate congestion and atrial pressure load, accelerating atrial structural and electrical remodeling [15, 25, 33]. On the other hand, sustained myocardial stress and congestive status may further compromise renal perfusion and metabolic clearance, a pattern compatible with bidirectional cardiorenal interaction described in prior literature that undermines long-term rhythm-control durability [33, 36, 37].

For exploratory visualization, we constructed four combined BNP/BUN–CRE strata using clinically interpretable, non-data-driven thresholds. The Kaplan–Meier curves showed separation in recurrence-free survival, with the BNP High + BUN/CRE High group exhibiting the lowest recurrence-free survival. This subgroup display should be regarded as descriptive and hypothesis-generating, rather than as establishing stable clinical decision boundaries.

From a clinical perspective, the observed combined high-BNP/high-BUN–CRE phenotype may help illustrate a potentially higher-risk subgroup, but this descriptive stratification requires confirmation in external datasets before any threshold-based clinical application. Periprocedurally, greater emphasis could be placed on optimizing volume status and renal protection while comprehensively managing concomitant metabolic risk factors [2, 33, 38]. During follow-up, more intensive rhythm monitoring and early-warning strategies for recurrence may be warranted to improve the durability of post-ablation rhythm maintenance and long-term outcomes.

Clinical implications: from risk estimation to a management pathway

From a practical standpoint, the primary role of the wCKM burden score is not to replace more powerful proximal predictors such as LAD, BNP, or fine f-wave. Indeed, once these substrate markers are available, the incremental discrimination contributed by wCKM is minimal; therefore, when comprehensive echocardiography, biomarker, and ECG data have been obtained pre-procedurally, substrate-based models should be prioritized. Our findings suggest two key implications. First, CKM burden—particularly information from the kidney and metabolic axes—may serve as a complementary preprocedural stratification dimension, addressing the limited multisystem coverage of conventional scores. Second, substrate markers provide the major discriminatory gain, whereas wCKM may offer complementary upstream systemic context rather than substantial incremental discrimination once these markers are available, making it suitable for a decision-oriented risk stratification pathway. For example, CKM burden could be used as an initial screen to identify “high-burden” patients, followed by substrate markers to refine risk tiers and guide ablation strategy selection, intensity of periprocedural management, and follow-up frequency. The core value of wCKM lies in its potential as a low-cost, readily accessible pre-procedural screening tool, particularly in settings where detailed echocardiography or biomarker testing is not immediately available, helping to identify high-burden patients who may benefit from timely referral and comprehensive substrate evaluation.

For patients identified as having high CKM burden, a potential management pathway could include more intensive pre-ablation optimization, including weight management, glycemic control, nephrology co-management, and consideration of SGLT2 inhibitors when indicated, for those with very high burden and concomitant adverse substrate features, a procedural strategy that leans toward more adequate adjunctive substrate modification for which prospective confirmation is required, and more intensive post-ablation rhythm monitoring coupled with tighter comorbidity control. Interventional studies [39, 40] have already suggested that aggressive optimization of metabolic and weight status through lifestyle intervention may be associated with improvement in atrial substrate markers and better ablation outcomes. Although the cross-sectional design of the current study cannot verify the reversibility of CKM-associated substrate, this framework supports future prospective investigations exploring whether a reduction in CKM burden can translate into regression of downstream substrate and thus genuinely improve ablation prognosis.

Strengths

This study systematically introduced the CKM framework into recurrence risk stratification in an NV-PersAF ablation cohort. We developed an interpretable three-axis weighted continuous score and evaluated its relevance across multiple layers—from association to substrate-adjusted explanatory analyses and model performance—using sequential models, axis-specific analyses, sensitivity analyses with alternative aggregation strategies and a continuous cardiac-axis specification, bootstrap internal validation, repeated split-sample validation, and multidimensional performance evaluation (C-index, time-dependent ROC, and DCA). Importantly, LAD, BNP, and fine f-wave were explicitly treated as downstream substrate covariates rather than CKM components, reducing the risk of double counting. Together with multicollinearity diagnostics, this design strengthens the statistical basis for interpreting the observed attenuation as a descriptive statistical phenomenon rather than a modeling artifact.

Limitations

Several limitations merit consideration. First, this was a single-center retrospective study and may therefore be subject to selection bias, residual confounding, and limited generalizability. Although bootstrap internal validation and repeated 70:30 split-sample validation suggested limited optimism and directional robustness, external validation and potential recalibration in independent cohorts remain necessary before broader clinical application. Second, unlike the continuous measures used for the kidney and metabolic axes, the cardiac axis in this study was defined as a binary indicator based on clinical diagnoses (coronary heart disease or heart failure). While this approach is simple and clinically clear, its relative “coarseness” may explain why the cardiac axis lost independent significance when modeled alongside the other two axes. In sensitivity analyses, we reconstructed the cardiac axis using continuous LVEF and LVMI measures, and the cardiac axis became significantly associated with recurrence. This finding suggests that the weaker performance of the original cardiac axis was likely influenced, at least in part, by its binary construction. Nevertheless, we retained the CHD/HF-based cardiac axis as the primary definition because it reflects clinically recognized cardiovascular comorbidity burden within the CKM framework. Third, the Kaplan–Meier subgroup display for the combined BNP/BUN–CRE phenotype was intended for exploratory visualization only, and the resulting combined phenotype should be regarded as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than as a validated clinical decision boundary. Fourth, because all CKM indicators and atrial substrate markers were measured at baseline and no formal mediation framework was applied, the attenuation observed after adjustment should not be interpreted as evidence of causal mediation. Rather, it should be understood as descriptive statistical attenuation reflecting partial overlap in prognostic information. Fifth, we could not directly evaluate mechanistic intermediates such as inflammation, fibrosis, or neurohormonal activation, and further studies integrating imaging and biomarker data are needed to strengthen biological interpretation. Furthermore, the wCKM employs Z-score standardization and Cox regression-derived weights that are inherently dependent on the distributional characteristics of the current study population. Although internal bootstrap validation suggested only limited optimism, this scoring system should not be directly generalized without recalibration in an independent external validation cohort. Future studies intending to apply this framework should recalculate standardized Z values using population-specific means and SDs and recalibrate the inter-axis weighting coefficients in the target cohort. In addition, exploring a simplified version of CKM assessment that does not rely on internal weights—such as an unweighted count of abnormal metabolic, renal, and clinical variables—may offer better external applicability and clinical feasibility and warrants further investigation. Multicenter prospective studies are warranted to externally validate the wCKM framework and determine whether CKM-informed management strategies can improve rhythm-control durability after ablation.

Conclusion

In this first-time ablation cohort of NV-PersAF, the weighted continuous overall score derived from the CKM three-axis burden was significantly associated with recurrence risk. This association was attenuated after adjustment for downstream atrial substrate markers (LAD, log(BNP), and fine f-wave), suggesting partial statistical overlap between upstream multisystem CKM burden and adverse atrial structural and electrophysiological substrate phenotypes. Overall, CKM assessment may support upstream risk screening and individualized preprocedural counseling, particularly when detailed substrate markers are not yet available.
