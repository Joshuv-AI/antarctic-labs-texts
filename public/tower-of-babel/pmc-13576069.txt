Warming accelerates drought–flood oscillations and stratifies socioeconomic exposure
Authors: Wei Qi, Ruiting Huang, Yanpeng Cai, Qian Tan, Minglei Ren, Yanli Liu, Lisa Levin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnthropogenic warming intensifies the hydrological cycle, increasing the likelihood of drought and flood events. However, disaster assessments predominantly evaluate these hazards in isolation, obscuring the distinct physical characteristics and societal impacts of drought–flood alternations. Here, we integrate bias-corrected climate projections with hydrological and socioeconomic mapping to quantify the evolving dynamics of three drought–flood switching event types—drought–flood alternation events, drought-to-flood transitions, and flood-to-drought transitions—and their unequal burdens across warming levels from 1.5 to 4.5 °C in China, a region facing the world's most severe flood impacts. We show that warming systematically compresses transition intervals between drought and flood conditions, driving marked increases in the frequency and spatial extent of these event types. This physical acceleration amplifies socioeconomic exposure, revealing a stark distributional asymmetry. While wealthy regions experience the highest absolute exposure, lower-income populations face disproportionately high relative increases in human and economic exposure risks. Our results demonstrate that the temporal compression of hydrological extreme reversals creates a class of compound risk that reduces preparedness windows and stratifies vulnerability along socioeconomic lines. These findings underscore the urgency of incorporating rapid hydrological transitions into adaptation strategies to address the inequal escalation of climate risk, a critical dimension of vulnerability that has long been overlooked.Keywords: flood, drought, extreme events, climate change

Traditional climate risk assessments often evaluate floods and droughts in isolation, overlooking the danger of rapid transitions between them. This study reveals that global warming is accelerating these hydrological reversals, compressing the critical recovery window between extremes. We demonstrate that this temporal compression creates a compound risk that disproportionately impacts lower-income populations, who face the steepest relative increases in exposure. By linking physical hazard reversals with socioeconomic inequality, our work challenges current adaptation frameworks and provides a crucial evidence base for designing equitable climate resilience policies that account for the accelerating hazard reversals and uneven burdens of a warming world.

Introduction

Climate change is altering the frequency, intensity, timing, and spatial distribution of natural hazards (1, 2), with evidence of unequal impacts across socioeconomic and racial lines (3–8). Recent work identifies large increases in flood risk with poorer and marginalized groups disproportionately affected (9–13). Furthermore, vulnerability is concentrated among poorer or marginalized groups across heat and coastal hazards (14–17). Beyond aggregate patterns, recent diagnostics show that dry–wet extremes are more synchronized across space (18), while assessments document the concurrent intensification of heat, drought, and flood signals (19–23). Moreover, sectoral evidence shows that drought–flood alternation (DFA) depresses crop yield (24), weakens vegetation growth (25), and heightens the sensitivity of typical karst-region crops and disease to climate change (26–28).

Despite rapid progress, three important gaps remain. First, most inequality-oriented disaster studies still evaluate floods and droughts separately, thereby overlooking the transition process between opposing hydrological states (3, 29–35). Second, existing studies on DFA events and related dry–wet switches have mainly focused on changes in frequency, timing, or spatial distribution, but many of them identify wet conditions using precipitation or moisture anomalies rather than hydrologically defined flood events (20, 36–38). Third, few studies have linked DFA dynamics with poverty-stratified population and economic exposure under policy-relevant global warming levels (39). As a result, it remains unclear how warming reshapes the physical dynamics of DFA events and whether the resulting exposure burdens are distributed unevenly across socioeconomic groups. Consequently, they fail to situate these dynamics within policy-relevant global warming levels or explicitly link exposure to poverty-stratified populations and assets (40, 41).

Here, we quantify three drought–flood switching event types—DFA events, drought-to-flood (D2F) transitions, and flood-to-drought (F2D) transitions—considering onset and termination timing, transition duration, frequency, and areal coverage, at policy-relevant global warming levels (1.5–4.5 °C), while jointly mapping population and gross domestic product (GDP) exposure by poverty tier. This unified framework resolves three event categories—DFA, D2F, and F2D—simultaneously (Fig. S1) and quantifies how their associated population and GDP exposure and resulting inequalities evolve across the global warming spectrum. We utilized bias-adjusted climate projections from the Inter-Sectoral Impact Model Intercomparison Project Phase 3b (ISIMIP3b) and the extensively calibrated and validated global hydrological model WEB-DHM-SG (Water and Energy Budget-based Distributed Hydrological Model with improved Snow physics for Global simulation) (42, 43). This study focuses on China, which is among the countries most severely affected by floods worldwide. According to a statistical review, China ranks first globally in terms of both flood-induced economic losses and flood-affected population (44). Therefore, China provides a critical case for evaluating how DFAs reshape socioeconomic exposure under climate warming. By integrating hazard-switching dynamics into a distributional risk framework aligned with Paris Agreement warming levels, this study establishes a mechanistic and policy-relevant foundation for adapting to DFA risks under ongoing climate change. Moreover, by elucidating the inequities resulting from DFAs, our study directly supports progress toward the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities).

Results

Our analysis reveals that anthropogenic warming is fundamentally reorganizing the temporal structure of hydrological extremes to produce a class of compound transition hazards (Fig. 1). We find that the transition gap between droughts and floods is contracting systematically. The hydrological extreme oscillations expose a paradox in climate vulnerability: while social development concentrates population and capital in high-exposure zones, producing massive absolute exposures, it is the socioeconomically marginalized who bear the brunt of relative destabilization. Thus, we demonstrate that climate change, manifested here as the rate of hazard alternation, acts as a regressive force, deepening the divide between the resilient wealthy and the vulnerable poor. This study situates drought–flood switching as a distinctive driver of distributional climate risk, requiring a paradigm shift from managing isolated disasters to managing the volatility of hydrological switching itself.

Compressing transition gaps

Climate warming consistently shortens the drought–flood event gap while markedly increasing both the frequency and spatial extent of the three event types considered in this study—DFA, D2F, and F2D (Fig. 2). The three core indicators all show significant (P < 0.05) responses to warming (Fig. 2a–c). The mean transition gap declines from 30.38 days in the historical period to 24.17 days at 4.5 °C, with a regression slope of –0.30 days per 0.5 °C (P < 0.05) and a mean reduction of 1.44% per 0.5 °C additional warming (Fig. 2a). In contrast, event frequency increases sharply, with a slope of 3,597 events per 0.5 °C (P < 0.05) and a mean increase of 10.09% per 0.5 °C (Fig. 2b). Similarly, the affected area expands markedly, from 27.10 × 103 to 2.16 × 106 km2, corresponding to a slope of 1.34 × 105 km2 per 0.5 °C (P < 0.05) and a mean increase of 9.22% per 0.5 °C (Fig. 2c). Together, these results show a consistent pattern: as warming intensifies, the transition gap shortens, while event frequency and spatial extent increase significantly (P < 0.05).

Spatial patterns of event frequency show that the northeastern and southern China are expected to experience increasing DFA events, both from flood to drought and from drought to floods (Fig. 2d–f). Spatial patterns of transition gap show heterogeneous responses (Fig. 2g–i). The transition gaps display strong decreases in northeastern and southwestern regions, implying faster drought–flood switching and increasing instability of hydrological regimes.

The timing responses of the three event types diverge notably under warming (Fig. S2). DFA events show overall temporal stability, D2F events shift toward earlier start and end dates under warming, and F2D events remain largely unchanged. DFA timing remains broadly stable with only minor and nonsignificant variations (Fig. S2a and b). The start shows a negligible trend (slope = 0.05 days per 0.5 °C, nonsignificant), while the end advances slightly (slope = −0.22 days per 0.5 °C, nonsignificant), suggesting that DFA events are temporally robust across warming levels. D2F transitions occur increasingly earlier as warming intensifies (Fig. S2c and d). The start shifts modestly forward (slope = −0.18 days per 0.5 °C, nonsignificant), and the end advances more clearly (slope = −0.46 days per 0.5 °C, P < 0.05), revealing a significant (P < 0.05) temporal compression of D2F events under higher temperatures. F2D transitions display no significant response to warming (Fig. S2e and f). Both the start (slope = 0.04 days per 0.5 °C) and end (slope = −0.14 days per 0.5 °C) remain nearly constant, indicating minimal climatic sensitivity in F2D sequences.

The frequency of all three event types—DFA, D2F, and F2D—shows a consistent increase under warming (Figs. S3–S5). In general, all scenarios from 1.5 to 4.5 °C reveal a gradual expansion of high-frequency zones, particularly across southern China. These regionally heterogeneous yet generally consistent patterns indicate that warming accelerates the transition process, causing widespread shifts in timing and leading to quicker drought–flood transitions (Figs. S5 and 2g–i).

For Julian day start, most regions show a negative trend (purple shading), implying earlier initiation of DFA events with rising temperature (Fig. S6a–c). For Julian day end, the dominant pattern is a temporal advancement as well, although more pronounced in magnitude (Fig. S6d–f).

All three event types intensify with warming, with drought-initiated alternations showing the strongest sensitivity to temperature rise and flood-initiated ones responding more moderately (Figs. S7 and 2a–c). Each category shows a contraction in transition gap accompanied by a pronounced expansion in frequency and affected area, although the strength of response follows a clear gradient (D2F > DFA > F2D). For D2F, the warming response is most pronounced (Fig. S7a–c). Transition gap decreases by 2.19% (−0.43 days per 0.5 °C), while frequency and area increase substantially—1,805.71 events per 0.5 °C (9.75%) and 1.34 × 105 km2 (9.27%), respectively. The rise underscores that DFAs accelerate markedly with temperature rise, signifying greater hydrological instability. In contrast, F2D transitions exhibit a milder yet consistent warming response (Fig. S7d–f). The gap shortens slightly (−0.19 days per 0.5 °C; −0.95%), while frequency and area still rise—1,791.07 events per 0.5 °C (10.45%) and 133 × 109 m2 (10.06%), respectively. Although these increases remain significant (P < 0.05), their gentler slopes suggest that F2D events evolve more gradually than drought-initiated sequences.

Both onset and end timings exhibit distinct regional contrasts (Fig. S8–S13). For DFA events, the onset timing is generally concentrated between early summer and mid-autumn, showing earlier initiation in southern and later in the northern and northeastern regions (Fig. S8). With increasing warming, widespread advancement of onset dates is observed, particularly in the Yangtze River Basin and South, indicating earlier emergence of DFAs. Correspondingly, the end timing (Fig. S11) shows a similar spatial pattern but with broader spatial coherence, suggesting that while DFA events tend to begin earlier, their termination also advances under warming, leading to a modest overall contraction of gaps.

For F2D events, onset timing is relatively late compared with DFA events, typically peaking in late summer to early autumn across central and southeastern regions (Figs. S9 and S12). For D2F events, onset and end timings occur predominantly in late spring to early summer, showing earlier initiation and termination in southern and southwestern regions than in northern regions (Figs. S10 and S13).

Across warming scenarios, DFA, D2F, and F2D exhibit pronounced spatial heterogeneity and consistent intensification at both provincial and basin scales, with clear differences in their sensitivities: D2F responds most strongly to warming, DFA shows widespread but moderate amplification, and F2D displays the weakest response (Figs. S14–S16). For DFA, as warming increases from 1.5 to 4.5 °C, the frequency increases steadily, with the strongest amplification emerging in the middle–lower Yangtze, Sichuan, and parts of southwestern regions. The event gap shortens substantially, indicating faster switching, especially in northern, southwestern, and northwestern China, whereas the affected area expands clearly across major regions, including the Yangtze River Basin, southwestern river regions, and northern China.

Population and GDP exposures both increase significantly (P < 0.05) with warming (Fig. 3a and b). Population exposure rises from 247 million in the historical baseline to 401 million at 4.5 °C, with a slope of 20.89 million people per 0.5 °C (P < 0.05) and a mean increase of 8.65% per 0.5 °C warming. GDP exposure shows an even steeper escalation, increasing from US$9.06 trillion historically to US$27.4 trillion at 4.5 °C, with a slope of US$3.15 trillion per 0.5 °C (P < 0.05) and a mean increase of 20.83% per 0.5 °C. Spatial patterns are broadly similar across DFA, F2D, and D2F events. For GDP exposure (Fig. 3c–e), events produce hotspots concentrated in eastern and southeastern regions, where economic density is highest. For population exposure (Fig. 3f–h), the spatial distribution is similar to GDP exposure.

As warming intensifies, the overall exposure gradient becomes more heterogeneous, with economically dynamic regions and densely populated belts showing greater exposure (Figs. S17–S19). Areas around the Yangtze River Basin, southeastern provinces, and several central–southern basins evolve into major risk centers. At grid scale, the spatial distribution of GDP exposure shows a pronounced amplification under progressive warming scenarios in eastern and southeastern regions (Figs. S20–S22). Population exposure also exhibits a consistent intensification as GDP exposure under global warming (Figs. S23–S25).

Population and GDP exposure all increase with global warming (Figs. S26 and 3a and b). GDP exposure is more sensitive to warming than population exposure, and F2D events tend to produce the largest relative economic risk, whereas DFA events reflect the overall compound exposure pattern. Population exposure increases from 246 to 400 million people, nearly identical to the DFA pattern, with a steady rise until 4.5 °C (Fig. S26a and b). GDP exposure grows from US$9 trillion to US$27.3 trillion, maintaining a similar slope to DFA. This parallel trend suggests that D2F events contribute comparably to national exposure escalation. Slopes of exposure to F2D events are slightly lower but display the same warming-linked amplification (Fig. S26c and d). Population exposure rises from 231 million to 377 million people (63%), while GDP exposure increases from US$8.2 trillion to US$25 trillion (205%).

For DFA events, economic exposure was recorded at 23.68% for extreme poverty, 24.13% for lower-middle poverty, and 24.48% for upper-middle poverty, before dropping to 20.71% in the high-income group (Fig. 4a). Correspondingly, human exposure ranged from 13.00% in extreme poverty to 8.50% in high-income populations. For D2F events, economic exposure values rose from 23.69% in extreme poverty through 24.14% in lower-middle to a maximum of 24.51% in upper-middle poverty, followed by a decline to 20.75% in high-income (Fig. 4b). Human exposure decreased stepwise, observed at 13.02%, 12.60%, 11.92%, and 8.52% across the poverty gradient. Under F2D events, economic exposure peaked in the upper-middle poverty group at 24.70%, compared with 23.95% in extreme poverty, 24.24% in lower-middle, and 20.98% in high-income groups (Fig. 4c). Human exposure reached 13.36% in extreme poverty, then gradually declined through 12.70 and 12.04%, reaching the lowest value of 8.60% in the high-income group.

For GDP exposure, the extreme poverty group increases from US$67.91 billion at 1.5 °C to US$239.01 billion at 4.5 °C, with a slope of US$30.24 billion (P < 0.05) and a mean increase of 23.68% per 0.5 °C (Fig. 4d–g). The lower-middle poverty group rises from US$72.64 billion to US$259.54 billion, corresponding to a slope of US$32.10 billion (P < 0.05) and a mean of 24.13%. The upper-middle poverty group shows the steepest relative escalation, from US$142.51 billion to US$516.7 billion, with a slope of US$65.27 billion (P < 0.05) and a mean increase of 24.48%. In contrast, the high-income group reaches far higher absolute levels, increasing from US$7.91 trillion to US$24.01 trillion, with a slope of US$3.02 trillion (P < 0.05) but a lower mean relative increase of 20.71%.

For population exposure, the extreme poverty group grows from 2.03 million people to 4.17 million, with a slope of 0.30 million (P < 0.05) and a mean increase of 13.00% (Fig. 4h–k). The lower-middle poverty group rises from 2.03 million to 4.06 million, with a slope of 0.28 million (P < 0.05) and a mean of 12.58%. The upper-middle poverty group expands from 4.89 million to 9.41 million, with a slope of 0.62 million (P < 0.05) and a mean of 11.87%. The high-income group, although less sensitive in relative terms, accounts for the largest absolute exposure, from 221.61 million people at 1.5 °C to 358.98 million at 4.5 °C, with a slope of 19.68 million (P < 0.05) and a mean increase of 8.50%.

Across all scenarios, both GDP and population exposure to DFA, D2F, and F2D events increase markedly (Figs. S27, S28, and 4d–k). The high-income regions contribute the largest total exposure due to their high economic baseline, whereas lower-income populations exhibit faster relative growth in exposure, highlighting disproportionate vulnerability and the widening inequality in climate-related risks under future warming trajectories.

Discussion and implications

This study provides a warming-level-explicit assessment of three drought–flood switching event types—DFA, F2D, and D2F—revealing how areal coverage, frequency, and socioeconomic exposure evolve as global temperature rises. By diagnosing event durations and quantifying their timing, transition gap, and exposure across 1.5–4.5 °C warming levels, we identified a robust and coherent pattern. Warming systematically accelerates hydrological switching while dramatically expanding the spatial footprint and population and economic exposure associated with both drought- and flood-initiated alternations. These findings highlight a fundamental shift in the behavior of compound hydrological extremes under climate change: one that cannot be captured by considering droughts and floods in isolation (40, 45–48).

Across all warming scenarios, the transition gap of DFA, D2F, and F2D events shortens while event frequency and affected area rise sharply, indicating that the hydrological system oscillates more rapidly between drought and flood conditions under strengthened climate forcing (49, 50). The accelerated switching reflects the influence of warming-induced hydroclimatic instability (51–53). Under such conditions, both D2F and F2D shifts occur within shorter transition intervals because the underlying hydroclimatic states fluctuate more sharply in response to climate change. D2F exhibits the strongest sensitivity, with earlier onset and termination and the steepest rise in event frequency. By comparison, F2D alterations evolve more gradually, consistent with a slower adjustment of saturated surface systems under warming climate (49, 54).

The spatial patterns further demonstrate that climate change does not only intensify hazard levels but also reorganizes the geography of hydroclimatic instability (55, 56). The Yangtze Basin and North China Plain consistently emerge as national hotspots across all indicators, reflecting their systemic exposure to climate-driven instability (55, 57). The temporal structure also shifts noticeably earlier in many regions, especially for D2F events, signaling a warming-induced seasonal compression of hydroclimatic extremes (56, 58). This advancement challenges the assumptions embedded in traditional hydrological calendars and implies that, under continued climate change, alternating drought–flood phases may emerge earlier and switch over shorter intervals than in the historical climate system (58).

Compared with previous studies that typically examine droughts and floods separately or focus on simultaneous compound extremes, our work advances understanding by analyzing the transitions between drought and flood phases and by quantifying how warming alters the process. This transition-based framework reveals the dynamic instability of the hydrological system rather than treating extremes as disconnected events. Additionally, our warming-level design provides temperature–response relationships that are more interpretable than scenario-based projections, showing, for example, that socioeconomic exposure grows marked with warming (56, 59). Another important contribution is the integration of physical hazards with socioeconomic conditions (60). We show that GDP exposure increases more rapidly than population exposure (61, 62). Meanwhile, poorer groups experience the highest relative increases in economic and human exposures, despite the high-income contributing the greatest absolute exposure. This dual pattern, high absolute exposure in wealthy regions but high vulnerability in poor groups, reveals a nuanced form of climate inequality that has rarely been quantified in previous research on DFA events.

Although WEB-DHM-SG has been extensively calibrated and validated in China, uncertainties remain in the country-scale projections. These uncertainties may arise from climate forcing data, hydrological model parameterization, spatial regridding, and the representation of local hydrological processes at the national scale. In addition, drought and flood identification depends on threshold-based definitions, including the standardized runoff index (SRI) drought threshold and return-period-based flood-protection thresholds. The flood-protection dataset may also not fully capture recent infrastructure upgrades or local variations in levee conditions, reservoir operation, drainage capacity, and emergency management. Therefore, the absolute estimates of event frequency, affected area, and exposure should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, because the same model framework, historical baseline period, and threshold definitions were consistently applied across all warming levels, the relative changes and warming-dependent trends are more comparable across scenarios than the absolute magnitudes.

Although our flood-event definition improves upon studies that use precipitation or wet anomalies as flood proxies, it still has limitations. Flood events were identified as simulated discharge exceedances of FLOPROS-based flood defense thresholds, which provide a hydrologically grounded and spatially explicit criterion. However, FLOPROS represents generalized protection standards and may not fully capture local-scale variations in levee conditions, reservoir operation, drainage capacity, floodplain management, or emergency responses. Moreover, threshold exceedance does not necessarily imply realized inundation, damage, or displacement. Therefore, the flood events identified here should be interpreted as potential hydrological flood events or flood-hazard exceedances rather than directly observed disaster events. This limitation may influence the absolute frequency, affected-area, and exposure estimates, particularly because flood-protection standards may not fully reflect recent infrastructure upgrades. In addition, the use of 1985–2014 as the historical reference period may not capture the most recent changes in hydrological regimes or protection capacity. However, the consistent use of the same historical baseline and fixed threshold definitions across warming levels enables robust comparison of relative changes and warming-dependent trends in drought–flood switching risk.

Several limitations should be noted when interpreting the exposure indicators used in this study. GDP and population are widely used proxies for economic and demographic exposure, but they do not fully capture vulnerability, adaptive capacity, or realized losses. In particular, gridded GDP products are often spatially downscaled using auxiliary information such as population distribution, land use, or nighttime lights, which may introduce partial dependence between GDP and population exposure estimates. Therefore, similarities between GDP and population exposure hotspots should be interpreted cautiously and should not be taken as fully independent lines of evidence. In addition, GDP exposure represents the amount of economic activity or assets exposed to DFA hazards, rather than household income or poverty status. Regions with high industrial GDP may still contain substantial low-income populations. To avoid treating GDP as a direct proxy for poverty, we separately stratified exposure by poverty groups and focused on relative changes within each socioeconomic group. These limitations may affect the interpretation of absolute exposure magnitudes, but they do not alter the central finding that warming increases exposure to DFA, D2F, and F2D events and that lower-income groups experience disproportionately large relative increases.

Methodology

Climate datasets

Future climate forcing was obtained from the bias-corrected Inter-Sectoral Impact Model Intercomparison Project phase 3b (ISIMIP3b) (63) projections, which provide daily meteorological variables (precipitation, temperature, radiation, humidity, wind speed, and surface pressure) under multiple warming pathways. Global warming levels of 1.5 to 4.5 °C were identified by comparing simulated temperatures against the 1850–1900 climatology, using 30-year running windows to ensure stable estimates of temperature departures from the pre-industrial baseline. Historical precipitation was derived from the Asian Precipitation–Highly Resolved Observational Data Integration Towards Evaluation (APHRODITE) (64) dataset (0.25°), while temperature, radiation, and wind speed were sourced from the China Meteorological Forcing Dataset (0.1°) (65). The climate data were re-gridded to 0.1° to maintain compatibility.

The model

Hydrological conditions were simulated using a calibrated large-scale hydrological–hydraulic model: WEB-DHM-SG (42), a physically based distributed model that explicitly resolves interactions among surface runoff, subsurface flow, and groundwater storage. WEB-DHM-SG has been applied extensively in mountainous and monsoon-driven basins and is well validated for capturing both flood and drought hydrological regimes.

The model was developed by our team and has been extensively calibrated and validated in China in our previous studies (43, 66), showing good performance. Across 36 hydrological gauges, the model attains a mean Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency of 0.88 (66), and the relative bias in simulating flood-induced human displacement is 10.7% (43). Snow-related processes are also well captured. Evaluations in Northeast China and the Tibetan Plateau show that the model realistically represents snowmelt dynamics and seasonal snowpack evolution (67–69), with relative bias of snow cover fractions being 1.0% in Northeast China (67) and between −11.0 and 2.7% in Southwest River basins (69). Therefore, it was used in the current study. Daily hydrometeorological drivers from the ISIMIP3b dataset were used to force the model. These drivers enabled the simulation of surface runoff, river discharge, and multi-year hydrological variability under future climate scenarios.

Analysis approach

Building on the simulated hydrological states, drought and flood conditions were diagnosed. In this study, three drought–flood switching event types were considered: DFA events, D2F transitions, and F2D transitions. DFA denotes drought–flood alternation events without emphasizing a specific transition direction, whereas D2F and F2D denote directional transitions from drought to flood and from flood to drought, respectively. These event types were extracted using a rule-based identification method that detects consecutive shifts between drought and flood states (Fig. S1). For each event category, key metrics including onset day, end day, transition-gap duration, frequency, and spatial extent were computed, allowing for a detailed assessment of how events interact with socioeconomic exposure across space and time. Drought events were identified using the SRI method. The SRI was calculated at the daily scale using simulated discharge for each grid cell, with the historical period 1985–2014 used as the reference baseline for standardization. Specifically, a grid cell was classified as experiencing drought when SRI ≤ −0.5 (70). Flood events were identified as simulated discharge exceedances above flood-protection standards derived from the FLOPROS dataset (71). FLOPROS provides flood-protection standards in the form of return periods and integrates three layers of information: a design layer based on existing flood-protection infrastructure, a policy layer based on regulatory protection standards, and a model layer estimated using flood-risk modeling and socioeconomic information. For China, FLOPROS includes policy/legal protection-standard information at both national and regional scales; therefore, the China-related thresholds are not solely GDP-derived estimates. In this study, we used the spatially varying return-period standards from the official FLOPROS merged layer. These return periods were converted into grid-level discharge thresholds using the Pearson Type III distribution based on simulated historical discharge during 1985–2014, which is officially recommended in China for flood-frequency analysis. A flood event was identified when simulated discharge exceeded the corresponding return-period-based discharge threshold.

During the baseline period, we validated our simulated flood-induced human displacement risk against statistics from the Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre. The relative bias for these estimates was calculated at 10.7% (43). Therefore, the application of FLOPROS is robustly justified for this study.

For each warming level, the characteristics of DFA, D2F, and F2D events were assessed for all grid cells to determine changes in timing, frequency, and affected area. Exposure was calculated by overlaying transition-affected grids with population and GDP distributions. Socioeconomic exposure was further stratified by poverty group using the World Bank international and complementary poverty lines expressed in 2011 purchasing power parity (PPP) US dollars. Specifically, we used US$1.90 per person per day as the international extreme poverty line, US$3.20 per person per day as the lower-middle-income poverty line, and US$5.50 per person per day as the upper-middle-income poverty line. Based on these thresholds, four mutually exclusive groups were defined: extreme poverty (<US$1.90 day−1), lower-middle poverty (US$1.90–3.20 day−1), upper-middle poverty (US$3.20–5.50 day−1), and high-income (>US$5.50 day−1). All thresholds are reported in 2011 PPP terms.

Temporal trends were evaluated using the Mann–Kendall trend test, and magnitudes were quantified with Sen's slope (P < 0.05). Spatial analyses were carried out to highlight regional hotspots of hydrological switching, increasing hazards, and heightened exposure to DFA, D2F, and F2D events under progressive warming.

For point plots, marker positions represent the numerical values of the corresponding metrics. Marker sizes were kept uniform within each panel and do not encode additional information. Error bars represent SDs, as described in the corresponding figure captions. Dashed lines indicate linear regression trends.
