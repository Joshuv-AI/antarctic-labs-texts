Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) remains a leading cause of severe vision loss among older adults in developed countries1. To improve patient outcomes, it is critical to elucidate the complex pathogenesis of AMD and to refine clinical and therapeutic strategies. Historically, choroidal circulatory disturbances, the accumulation of retinal waste products, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation have been implicated2. Subsequently, the understanding and treatment of AMD have advanced dramatically following two major breakthroughs: the 2004 confirmation of the efficacy of intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) injections for neovascular AMD3 and the 2005 discovery of the complement factor H gene’s involvement4–6. Recent research has expanded this framework to include systemic complement activation7 and extracellular matrix mechanics alongside inflammatory pathways8. Despite these major advances, important gaps remain in our mechanistic understanding, in the objective assessment of visual function, in the prediction of disease progression, and in the refinement of therapeutic strategies. This special feature, on “Macular Degeneration”, brings together a diverse range of papers that explore these frontiers across imaging-based prediction, treatment optimization, mechanistic biology, and patient-centered outcomes to inform the next generation of AMD management.

Collection overview

Haas et al. and Moon et al.9,10 both utilized imaging for predictive purposes. Haas et al.9 employed multimodal imaging to evaluate the conversion rate of nonexudative macular neovascularization to an exudative stage. A total of 241 Caucasian patients with unilateral exudative neovascular AMD were screened for the presence of nonexudative neovascularization in the fellow eye. Of the eyes screened, 16.6% presented with nonexudative neovascularization at baseline, with nearly one-third progressing to exudation within an average of 12.6 months. The study identified shallow irregular retinal pigment epithelial elevation on structural optical coherence tomography as a statistically significant biomarker for predicting this conversion, emphasizing its role in identifying high-risk patients. Moon et al.10 investigated the feasibility of using a deep learning model to predict visual acuity directly from optical coherence tomography scans in patients with neovascular AMD who received anti-VEGF therapy. This retrospective study enrolled 240 patients (240 eyes), yielding 2,400 images for analysis. A pre-trained VGG16 convolutional neural network was adapted for five-class visual acuity classification. The model achieved an average binary accuracy of 79.6% in distinguishing between good and poor vision. Notably, for high-confidence predictions (representing 29.2% of the samples), the binary accuracy increased to 94.2%. These results highlight the potential of artificial intelligence to assist clinicians in objective functional assessment using only anatomical imaging data.

Expanding on these predictive insights, the remaining six papers in this Collection further explore therapeutic innovations and underlying disease mechanisms. Masaoka et al.11 evaluated treatment strategies. They compared the short-term efficacy of switching from aflibercept to faricimab during the maintenance phase of neovascular AMD. Their study found that faricimab provided a longer duration of significant fluid suppression compared with aflibercept, particularly in reducing intraretinal fluid in eyes without pachychoroid. These results suggest that faricimab may offer superior exudative control for specific neovascular AMD subtypes. Hernandez et al. and Forest et al.12,13 examined inflammatory mechanisms, focusing specifically on the involvement of C-reactive protein (CRP) isoforms and the alternative complement pathway. Hernandez et al.12 investigated the role of CRP isoforms in driving choroidal neovascularization. Their study demonstrated that pentameric CRP dissociates into the monomeric CRP isoform in proinflammatory settings in vitro and in vivo. This dissociation process exacerbates neovascularization and retinal edema, identifying monomeric CRP as a potent local inflammatory driver and a potential therapeutic target. Forest et al.13 investigated the link between systemic inflammation and choroidal thickness in advanced non-neovascular AMD. They identified a significant negative association between the plasma levels of complement factor Bb and choroidal thickness. This finding implies that systemic activation of the alternative complement pathway may play a direct role in the progression of advanced non-neovascular AMD. Wolfram et al.14 explored how the mechanical properties of the extracellular matrix modulate retinal pigment epithelial cells. Their research revealed that softer substrates trigger proangiogenic signals such as VEGF, whereas stiffer substrates promote antiangiogenic responses. These responses suggest that the mechanical environment of Bruch’s membrane is a key driver of the angiogenic switch in AMD. Terheyden et al.15,16 provided two complementary studies on patient-reported outcomes. The first demonstrated that vision impairment in low luminance, as measured by the Vision Impairment in Low Luminance (VILL) questionnaire, is associated with bilateral visual function deficits (particularly contrast sensitivity) across all AMD stages, specifically in intermediate AMD15. The second report compared the VILL to the generic NEI VFQ, demonstrating that the VILL has fewer ceiling effects16. These findings establish the VILL as a robust and highly sensitive patient-reported outcome measure, superior to generic instruments for evaluating treatment efficacy in intermediate AMD clinical trials where functional deficits are often subtle.

Concluding remarks

The studies in this Collection highlight a significant shift toward personalized AMD management by integrating advanced imaging, molecular insights, and mechanical disease drivers. By combining bench-side biology with refined clinical strategies and patient-centered outcomes, these works bridge the gap between structural stability and functional quality of life for older adults. We hope this Collection serves as both a definitive resource and a foundation for continued innovation in the next generation of AMD interventions.
