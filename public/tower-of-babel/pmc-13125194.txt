Establishment of prediction equations for available energy values of sorghum in broilers using machine learning and stepwise regression methods
Authors: Xunyu Guo, Xingbo Liu, Zichen Cheng, Ziqi Zhao, Zhibin Ban, Yuming Guo, Lei Yan, Na Sui, Wei Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to determine the available energy values of sorghum for Arbor Acres (AA) broilers and to develop and compare prediction equations using multivariate linear stepwise regression (MLSR) and machine learning-based linear regression (LR). It is important to clarify that the LR used in this study is fundamentally a linear regression model. The primary difference from MLSR lies in the variable selection process and optimization algorithm. Ten sorghum samples with varied chemical compositions were used. Test diets were formulated by replacing 40% of the energy-yielding components of a reference diet with individual sorghum. A total of 7929-day-old and 39623-day-old male AA broilers were used in two balance trials. Apparent metabolizable energy (AME), nitrogen-corrected AME (AMEn), and net energy (NE) were determined using the substitution method and respiratory calorimetry. Correlation analyses between energy values and chemical components were performed. Prediction equations for AME and NE were developed using both MLSR (in SPSS) and LR (in Python scikit-learn). The AME, AMEn, and NE values for sorghum were significantly higher in 26-28-day-old broilers compared to 12-14-day-old broilers (P < 0.05). Tannin (TN) content showed significant negative correlations with AME and AMEn. Accurate prediction equations for AME and NE were established for both age stages using key predictors such as acid detergent fiber (ADF), TN, phytic acid (PA), dry matter (DM), and crude protein (CP). Equations developed with both MLSR and LR methods demonstrated a good fit to the development data. Validation results indicated that the predictive accuracy and performance of the two methods were comparable. This study provides definitive available energy values of sorghum for broilers at different ages. The developed prediction equations, whether based on MLSR or LR, are effective tools for estimating the energy content of sorghum. The findings support the use of sorghum as a viable alternative energy source in broiler diets and provide a scientific basis for its precise application in feed formulation.Keywords: Sorghum, AA broiler, Effective energy value, Prediction equation

Introduction

Energy constitutes a significant portion of feed costs, accounting for at least 60% of the total cost of poultry diets (Barzegar et al., 2020). Accurate evaluation of the available energy in feed ingredients is essential for meeting animal nutritional requirements and reducing feed expenses. The concept of animal precision nutrition, though relatively recent, holds substantial strategic importance. In terms of energy evaluation systems, the conventional metabolizable energy system fails to account for heat increment (HI) (Swick et al., 2013), making it inadequate for accurately describing the energy allocation between maintenance and growth in practical poultry production. Consequently, the net energy (NE) system has gained increasing attention in recent years as a more precise evaluation method (Carre et al., 2014). NE, defined as apparent metabolizable energy (ME) minus HI (Noblet et al., 1994), represents the portion of energy actually available to the animal and is categorized into NE for maintenance and NE for production (Barzegar et al., 2019). Research has demonstrated a strong correlation between metabolizable energy and NE, and incorporating ME as a predictor in NE estimation models significantly improves model fit (Tay-Zar et al., 2024). The NE system more accurately reflects the utilizable energy content of feed, enabling the formulation of optimized diets that closely match poultry nutritional needs, thereby enhancing both production performance and farming profitability (Zou et al., 2021). Thus, establishing an accurate NE system and reliable prediction equations is of critical importance.

Sorghum, recognized as one of the world's most crucial cereal grains, is extensively cultivated globally due to its desirable agronomic traits such as drought tolerance and salt-alkali resistance (Masenya et al., 2021). It has been established as a relatively stable energy source for broilers (Selle et al., 2018). Despite the long-standing dominance of corn as the primary energy ingredient in poultry diets in China, numerous studies have demonstrated that partial substitution of corn with sorghum has no adverse effect on key production metrics in poultry, including production performance (Manyelo et al., 2019), intestinal morphology (Sitohang et al., 2024), and organ indices (Garcia et al., 2004). However, a significant gap remains in these studies, as they fail to provide precise available energy values for sorghum, thereby limiting the ability to offer accurate guidance for determining optimal inclusion levels of sorghum in broiler diets.

Therefore, this study was conducted with the objective to develop prediction equations for the available energy values of sorghum in Arbor Acres (AA) broilers, and to evaluate the efficacy of machine learning versus stepwise regression in modeling these equations, thereby providing scientific guidance for the application of sorghum in poultry diets.

Materials and methods

Animal welfare statement

The study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of China Agricultural University (Aw31015202-1-3) and was conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for Laboratory Animals established by the Chinese Ministry of Science and Technology (Beijing, China).

Sorghum and diets

Ten sorghum samples (sorghum 1 to sorghum 10) were selected for the experiment, with their chemical compositions presented in Table 1. Sorghum samples 2 and 6 were​ from the United States. Sorghum 7 originated from Argentina. Other Sorghums were collected from various regions in China, including Jilin (sorghum 1, 3, 8), Jiangsu (sorghum 4), Hebei (sorghum 5), Liaoning (sorghum 9), and Inner Mongolia (sorghum 10). The experimental diets included 1 reference diet and 10 test diets, with their formulations and nutrient compositions detailed in Table 2, Table 3, respectively. All diets were formulated to meet the nutritional requirements for broilers as specified in the Feeding Standard of Chickens (NY/T 33-2004, China). The test diets were prepared by replacing 40% of the energy-yielding components in the reference diet (air-dry basis) with individual sorghum samples, following the method described by Liu et al. (2023). Vitamin supplements, minerals (including calcium), and other non-energy components were adjusted to ensure equal inclusion rates in all diets.

Calorimetry chambers

Calorimetry chambers have been described in detail by Liu et al. (2017). Briefly, this system contains 12 respiratory thermometry chambers. The refrigeration, heating, and dehumidification equipment installed in the respiratory thermometry chambers can keep the temperature and humidity in the chambers within the appropriate range for poultry. The gas sensors and gas analyzers installed in each chamber can analyze and measure the O2 consumption and CO2 production in each chamber and calculate the respiratory quotient (RQ).

Broilers and management

All male AA broilers were purchased from a local commercial hatchery. Broiler management followed the protocol described by Ning et al. (2022). At 9 days of age, a total of 792 broilers were randomly assigned to 11 experimental treatments, each with 6 replicates of 12 broilers per replicate. Within each replicate, 8 broilers were placed in metabolic cages for determination of dietary metabolizable energy, and 4 broilers were placed in calorimetry chambers for measurement of heat production. At 23 days of age, a total of 396 broilers were randomly assigned to 11 experimental treatments, each with 6 replicates of 6 broilers per replicate. Within each replicate, 4 broilers were placed in metabolic cages for determination of dietary metabolizable energy, and 2 broilers were placed in calorimetry chambers for measurement of heat production. Throughout the experiment, the variation in average body weight among replicates did not exceed 3% of the overall mean body weight of all broilers. Broilers had ad libitum access to water and feed.

Experimental design

A completely randomized design was used to evaluate the 11 different diets in 11 respiratory chambers with six runs per diet. Each treatment period was 6 days, consisting of a 3-day adaptation period and a 3-day experimental period. Adaptation to corresponding diets and environments (chicken house or chamber) was conducted for 3 days for broilers at 9 and 23 days of age. During the next 3 days of the experimental period, feed intake was recorded and total feces were collected by replicates. Broilers in the respiratory chamber were used to determine heat production (HP). HP was calculated by measuring O2 consumption and CO2 production for three consecutive days. In addition, broilers were all weighed on days 12 and 15 and on days 26 and 29 in each chamber to calculate the average body weight during the formal experiment period.

Analyses of sorghum, diets, and excreta

The total excreta of each cage were dried at 65°C for 72 h to make air-dried samples. Sorghum, diets, and excreta samples were ground to pass through a sieve of 40 mesh size and stored at -4°C before chemical analysis. A bomb calorimeter (C 2000; IKA, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China) was used to determine the gross energy (GE) of sorghum, diets, and excreta samples. Sorghum, diets, and excreta samples were analyzed for dry matter (DM, method 934.01, AOAC 2019), crude protein (CP, method 990.03, AOAC 2019), ash (method 920.39, AOAC 2019), crude fiber (CF, method 978.10, AOAC 2019), and ether extract (EE, method 942.05, AOAC 2019). Ankom220 Fiber Analyzer (Ankom Technology, New York, NY) was used to determine neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) based on Van Soest et al. (1991). Tannin (TN) content in sorghum was determined according to the Chinese national standards (GB/T 15686-2008). Phytic acid content of sorghum was determined using a phytic acid content kit (ADS-W-QT017, Jiangsu Aidisheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd).

Calculations

The AME, AMEn, and NE of sorghum were calculated according to the substitution method. In the following formulas, a% is the proportion of the reference diet's energy-contributing ingredients remaining in the test diet (i.e., 60% in this case, since 40% was replaced), and b% is the substitution level of the test ingredient (40% in both trials).

The dietary energy values (AMEdiet, AMEndiet, NEdiet) were calculated based on the method described by Mera-Zúñiga et al. (2019).

Statistical analyses

Analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 software. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Tukey’s multiple-range test was used to determine significant differences between treatment means. Correlation analysis was conducted using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Experimental results are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with P < 0.05 indicating a significant difference.

Two methods were used to construct the energy prediction equation for sorghum: Method 1, A multivariable linear stepwise regression (MLSR) was used to construct the prediction equation using the SPSS program. Method 2: Linear regression (LR) was performed using the LinearRegression function (with fit_intercept=True) from the Python 3.11 scikit-learn library. The dataset was used to develop and evaluate the models. To assess model stability, 5-fold cross-validation was conducted on the full dataset. As a standard linear regression model was used, no hyperparameters required tuning; therefore, the cross-validation was solely employed to estimate the stability of the model's error. Model performance was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R²) and mean squared error (MSE). It should be noted that, due to the limited number of sorghum samples, model validation was performed on the same dataset used for model development, and thus the reported fit statistics primarily reflect the models' goodness-of-fit rather than their predictive performance on independent data.

Results

Chemical composition of sorghum

Table 1 summarizes the chemical composition of the ten sorghum samples, revealing a distinct pattern of variability across different nutritional components. Most parameters had coefficients of variation (CV) exceeding 10%. TN content exhibited the highest variability among all components, with a CV reaching 75.37%. In contrast, Gross Energy (GE) demonstrated consistency, showing the minimal variation with a CV of only 0.88%. DM and CP were also relatively uniform, with CVs of 1.14% and 9.16%, respectively. Other nutritional components, including EE, CF, NDF, ADF, Ash, and PA, all displayed high variability, with their CV values ranging from 12.84% to 34.97%.

Energy values and energy utilization of sorghum

As shown in Table 4, the AME, AMEn, and NE of sorghum in broilers of 12-14 days of age ranged from 10.27 to 15.50 MJ/kg, 9.94 to 15.16 MJ/kg, and 6.33 to 10.92 MJ/kg, respectively. Sorghum 4 consistently yielded the highest values for AME (15.50 MJ/kg), AMEn (15.16 MJ/kg), and the ratio of AME to GE (82.78%), which were significantly higher than all other sorghum varieties except sorghum 1 and 2 (P < 0.05). In contrast, sorghum 10 showed the lowest values for AME (10.27 MJ/kg) and AMEn (9.94 MJ/kg), both significantly lower than all other varieties (P < 0.05), while sorghum 8 resulted in the lowest NE value (6.33 MJ/kg), which was significantly lower than sorghums 1 to 7 and sorghum 9 (P < 0.05). For energy utilization, the AME/GE, AMEn/GE, NE/AME, and NE/AMEn of sorghum for 12-14 days old broilers ranged from 56.03 to 82.78%, 54.26 to 80.97%, 57.63 to 73.52%, and 58.90 to 75.02%, respectively. Sorghum 3 and 5 produced the highest NE/AME ratios (73.36% and 73.52%, respectively), which significantly higher than sorghum 7 and 8 (P < 0.05). Whereas sorghum 8 showed the lowest efficiency for this parameter (57.63%), significantly lower than all other varieties except sorghum 7 (P < 0.05). Furthermore, significant differences were observed in energy values and energy utilization among the different sorghum varieties (P < 0.05).

Validation of prediction equation

We constructed prediction equations using the same predictive factors with both MLSR and LR methods, and compared the predicted values obtained from both methods with the observed values to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the equations (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). The results showed that, for both the 12-14 day and 26-28 day age stages in chickens, the R² values for both methods were similar, indicating comparable predictive accuracy. Furthermore, the slopes of the equations were also similar, and the fit between the predicted and observed values was good. These findings suggest that both MLSR and LR methods demonstrated similar performance in this study and were both capable of accurately predicting the AME and NE values of sorghum for chickens at different ages.

Discussion

Analysis of conventional nutritional components in sorghum raw materials

In this experiment, substantial variations were observed in the chemical composition of the 10 different sorghum varieties. The CV for EE, CF, NDF, ADF, Ash, TN, and PA all exceeded 10%, indicating that the sorghum samples selected for determining available energy values possess a certain degree of representativeness. The chemical composition of the 10 sorghum varieties used in this study was comparable to that of sorghum samples listed in Barua et al. (2021) and Selle et al. (2012). Based on TN content categories (0%-0.5%, 0.5%-1.0%, and ≥1.0%) (Price et al., 1978), the varieties were classified into four low-TN sorghums (Sorghum 1-4), two medium- TN sorghums (Sorghum 5, 6), and four high-TN sorghums (Sorghum 7-10). Significant differences existed in the conventional nutrient levels among the different types of sorghum, which is primarily attributed to factors such as variety, geographical origin, agronomic practices, and growing season (Mateos et al., 2018).

Analysis of available energy and energy conversion efficiency in sorghum

TN, a class of polyphenolic compounds present in sorghum, are anti-nutritional factors toxic to broilers (Scott et al., 1955). Previous studies have consistently demonstrated a significant negative correlation between dietary AME, AMEn, TME, and TN content (King et al., 1997). In the present study, classification based on TN content revealed mean AME values of 15.20 MJ/kg for low-tannin sorghums, 13.46 MJ/kg for medium-tannin sorghums, and 11.71 MJ/kg for high-tannin sorghums. This pattern of decreasing AME with increasing TN content aligns with the findings of prior research. TN can cause direct damage to the intestinal mucosa, leading to villus shortening and even necrosis, which further reduces the energy available from the diet. Interestingly, Sorghum 6 had the highest TN content (1.59%) but its AME (11.88 MJ/kg) was not the lowest among the 10 varieties. Conversely, Sorghum 9, with the lowest TN content (0.07%), had an AME of 15.05 MJ/kg, which was not the highest. Therefore, predicting AME based solely on TN content may lead to deviation. A similar pattern was observed for NE utilization: Sorghum 8 had the lowest NE/AME ratio but was not the highest in tannin content. This suggests that factors beyond tannin concentration influence the efficiency of NE utilization. One possible explanation is the variation in fiber content, as higher dietary fiber increases HI and reduces NE availability (Carré et al., 2014; Noblet et al., 1994). Additionally, other anti-nutritional factors may also affect energy partitioning by limiting protein and starch digestibility (Selle et al., 2010). Therefore, the energy utilization efficiency of sorghum varieties is likely determined by multiple chemical and structural characteristics, rather than by TN content alone.

The determined AME values of sorghum for broilers aged 26-28 days ranged from 12.96 MJ/kg to 16.77 MJ/kg, including the AME values reported by Kriegshauser (14.06 MJ/kg and 14.69 MJ/kg) for Cobb roosters aged 15-21 days (Kriegshauser et al., 2006). The wider range observed in this study can be attributed to the selection of 10 distinct sorghum varieties with substantial chemical variation, which further substantiates the reference value of the available energy data obtained. Numerous studies have reported that dietary AME values are higher in older broilers compared to younger ones (Scott et al., 1998 and Zelenka et al., 1968). Consistent with this, the energy values and energy utilization were significantly higher in older broilers (26-28 days) than in younger broilers (12-14 days). This might be due to two reasons. Firstly, as age increases, intestinal surface area and digestive enzyme secretion enhance nutrient absorption capacity, directly improving the digestibility of grains like sorghum (Uni et al., 1999). This would further enhance the utilization rate of sorghum energy by broilers. Secondly, as broilers age, the maturation of their digestive organs and development of a more robust intestinal barrier lead to significantly enhanced digestive capacity and resilience to anti-nutritional factors (Rodrigues et al., 2002). Consequently, the inclusion of sorghum as a substitute for other ingredients may be more beneficial in feeds for older broilers.

Currently, data on the NE of sorghum ingredients for poultry are limited. The NE values determined in this study for broilers aged 26-28 days (8.67 MJ/kg to 13.47 MJ/kg) were comparable to the sorghum NE results reported by Wu et al. (2019). Furthermore, these values were also close to the NE values of corn (11.41 MJ/kg to 12.90 MJ/kg) measured by Swick et al. (2013) in older broilers, indicating the potential for high-quality sorghum to replace corn.

Analysis of correlations between chemical composition and available energy in sorghum, and prediction equations

High positive correlations were observed between sorghum's energy parameters (GE, AME, AMEn, AME/GE, AMEn/GE, NE) and its CP content, coupled with the negative correlations with EE. This phenomenon may be explained by the intercorrelations among sorghum components. A possible explanation is that sorghum with higher CP content and relatively lower moisture and EE contents likely contains lower levels of TN and PA (Tasie et al., 2020). Therefore, the lower levels of these anti-nutritional factors likely contribute to the higher available energy values and conversion efficiency, which is consistent with the principle that nutrient composition modulates the metabolic utilization of feedstuffs (Carré et al., 2014).

In younger broilers, due to incomplete intestinal development, crude fiber (CF) often exerts an inert effect in the digestive tract (Oscar et al., 2021 and Cao et al., 2003), negatively impacting the available energy value of feed ingredients. Consequently, significant negative correlations were observed between sorghum's AME, AMEn, AME/GE, AMEn/GE, and NE with its CF content. This aligns with the findings of Tay et al. (2024). The efficacy of exogenous enzyme supplementation in mitigating such negative effects further underscores the role of digestive capacity (Olukosi et al., 2019). In older broilers aged 26-28 days, intestinal development is more mature, and their ability to counteract anti-nutritional factors is enhanced (Noy et al., 2001). Accordingly, the correlation analysis for this age group showed no significant relationship between the available energy values or energy conversion efficiency of sorghum and its CF content. A consistent pattern was observed regarding the relationship between available energy values (and their conversion efficiencies) and TN content. Although AME, AMEn, AME/GE, and AMEn/GE still maintained a significant negative correlation with TN content as broilers aged, the magnitude of the correlation coefficients markedly decreased (0.87 vs. 0.33).

Currently, robust prediction equations for both ME and NE of sorghum specific to broilers remain scarce in the literature. TN content has been consistently identified as a primary negative predictor in such models (Tay-Zar et al., 2024). Furthermore, we found that incorporating PA as an indicator also contributes to improved prediction accuracy. While MLSR serves as a fundamental and widely applied technique for energy prediction in animal nutrition (Ning et al., 2022; Tay-Zar et al., 2024), we employed LR for algorithmic refinement. The MLSR method, by iteratively selecting variables and optimizing coefficients, aims to yield a more parsimonious and robust coefficient matrix, thereby potentially minimizing prediction variance (Ryo et al., 2020). In this study, equations optimized via LR demonstrated increased R² values compared to those developed using MLSR, indicating that the LR approach enhances the goodness-of-fit of prediction equations. This difference is likely attributable to the distinct optimization objectives of the two methods, as LR minimizes training set errors whereas MLSR uses stepwise predictor selection, rather than an inherent superiority of the algorithm. Consequently, the LR-based equations are more suitable for estimating the available energy values of sorghum raw materials. This improvement was particularly evident for NE prediction in younger broilers. Notably, for the 12-14-day-old broilers, the R² values for the MLSR NE equations (0.57 and 0.49) are considerably lower than for the ME equations. This reflects the greater complexity of predicting NE in younger broilers. This may be attributed to the underdeveloped digestive system of young broilers, leading to higher variability in HI, which is the critical difference between ME and NE (Noblet et al., 1994). Consequently, NE, as a more integrative measure of energy utilization, may be more susceptible to individual variations in digestion and metabolism, making it more challenging to predict. In contrast, the LR method significantly improved the fit for NE prediction (R²=0.75), suggesting that the machine learning-based linear regression approach offers a potential advantage when dealing with more complex energy prediction scenarios. Interestingly, when the measured values obtained in this experiment were substituted into the prediction equations to generate predicted values, and a linear relationship was established between these predicted and measured values, the predicted AME and NE values for the ten sorghum varieties derived from our equations were found to be close to the measured values. Moreover, comparing the validation results of equations constructed by LR and MLSR revealed similar K-values and R², suggesting that both methods produce equations with comparably goodness-of-fit.

Conclusions

The AME, nitrogen-corrected AMEn, and NE values of sorghum for 12-14-day-old AA broilers ranged from 10.27 to 15.50 MJ/kg, 9.94 to 15.16 MJ/kg, and 6.33 to 10.92 MJ/kg, respectively. For 26-28-day-old AA broilers, the AME, AMEn, and NE values of sorghum ranged from 12.96 to 16.77 MJ/kg, 12.88 to 16.62 MJ/kg, and 8.67 to 13.47 MJ/kg, respectively. The prediction equations developed using both MLSR and LR methods demonstrated satisfactory goodness-of-fit and provided reasonably accurate predictions of available energy values in sorghum.
