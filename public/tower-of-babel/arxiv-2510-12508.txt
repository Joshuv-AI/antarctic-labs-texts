Abstract
We study ex-ante Pareto efficiency of feasible outcomes in games with incomplete information. Our main observation is that excessive randomization leads to inefficiency. With a nonatomic prior, generically, efficient outcomes are pure almost surely. In finite state spaces, the restriction is weaker: generically, the total number of actions used across states must be strictly less than the sum of the number of players and states. We apply these results to three environments. In an allocation problem without transfers, the welfare-maximizing incentive-compatible outcome is inefficient. In cheap talk, generically, an outcome is efficient only if it is pure; with state-independent sender payoffs, it is efficient if and only if the sender’s most preferred action is induced with certainty. In Bayesian persuasion with one safe action and several risky actions, outcomes are generically inefficient across broad sets of priors and receiver preferences.



Keywords:
Pareto efficiency, incomplete information, randomization, Bayesian persuasion, cheap talk, mechanism design without transfers.

JEL classification:
C72, D61, D82, D83.

Introduction

A central question in economics is how individual incentives interact with social welfare. A natural benchmark for studying this relationship is Pareto efficiency—outcomes in which no individual can be made strictly better off without making another worse off. However, in many real-world settings, individuals make decisions under incomplete information, making it unclear when efficiency can be achieved. They face uncertainty about the underlying state of the world and about what others know. Information could, in principle, improve welfare, yet individual incentives often stand in the way of efficiency.

The question is especially relevant to information design and mechanism design. In information design, uncertainty concerns an underlying state of the world, and the designer chooses how information about that state is revealed. In mechanism design, uncertainty concerns agents’ privately known types, and the designer chooses rules that map their reports into allocations. In both cases, the resulting outcome maps the underlying uncertainty—states or type profiles—into distributions over actions or allocations. Understanding when such outcomes are Pareto efficient is therefore key to understanding the scope and limits of design under incomplete information. We evaluate outcomes by players’ ex-ante expected payoffs and assess Pareto efficiency relative to the set of all feasible outcomes.

We start with finite state spaces. We provide a simple necessary condition for Pareto efficiency that depends only on the number of actions used across states. Generically, an outcome is efficient only if the total number of actions used across all states is strictly less than the sum of the number of players and the number of states (
Theorem

1
). Notably, this condition does not depend on the prior, the specific actions used, or the probabilities with which they are used. Thus, excessive randomization over actions leads to inefficiency. With two players, an outcome can be pure; quasi-pure, meaning deterministic in all but one state where two actions are used; or mixed, meaning neither pure nor quasi-pure. Generically, only pure or quasi-pure outcomes can be efficient.

We then extend the analysis to infinite state spaces with a nonatomic prior. Generically, an outcome is efficient only if it is pure almost surely (
Theorem

2
). Thus, while the finite state result limits the amount of randomization compatible with efficiency, the infinite state result rules out randomization on any set of states with positive probability.

The key insight behind our main results is geometric and links ex-ante and ex-post efficiency. Ex-post efficiency in a given state requires the state-contingent outcome to maximize some weighted sum of the players’ payoffs in that state. An outcome can be ex-post efficient in every state even if the supporting weights differ across states. Ex-ante efficiency is more demanding: there must exist a
common
vector of welfare weights that supports the state-contingent outcome across states (
Propositions

1
and
2
). Randomization across actions creates indifferences that this common vector must satisfy. Generically, once the outcome uses too many actions across states, such a common vector does not exist.

We first apply the infinite state result to the allocation problem without transfers studied by
Miralles (2012)
. Two indivisible objects need to be allocated among agents with privately known valuations. An outcome assigns to each valuation profile a lottery over allocations. The planner chooses a Bayesian incentive-compatible mechanism to maximize utilitarian welfare. When all agents prefer the same object, the optimal allocation rule randomizes over allocations to elicit the intensity of their preferences. However, this randomization cannot be supported by a common vector of welfare weights across such valuation profiles, implying that the induced outcome is Pareto inefficient (
Proposition

3
). Thus, the randomization that helps satisfy incentive constraints comes at the cost of Pareto efficiency.

We next consider canonical communication models: cheap talk and Bayesian persuasion. In cheap talk, the sender cannot commit to a signaling policy. Any stochastic equilibrium outcome requires at least one player to be indifferent: if the sender mixes across messages, she must be indifferent between the induced receiver responses; if the receiver mixes across actions, he must be indifferent between those actions under the induced belief. For such an outcome to be efficient, the other player must be indifferent as well. Such simultaneous indifferences are non-generic, so only pure cheap talk outcomes can generically be efficient (
Proposition

4
). When the sender’s payoff is state-independent, we obtain a sharper characterization under mild assumptions: a cheap talk outcome is efficient if and only if the sender’s most preferred action is induced with certainty (
Proposition

5
). Consequently, any cheap talk equilibrium in which communication affects the receiver’s action is inefficient.

In Bayesian persuasion, we study a natural environment with one safe action and several risky actions. The sender prefers risky actions, whereas the receiver takes a risky action only when sufficiently confident about the corresponding state. To increase the probability of risky actions while satisfying obedience, the sender often randomizes over action recommendations. For a full-dimensional set of priors and a broad class of receiver preferences, the Bayesian persuasion outcome uses too many actions across states to satisfy the necessary condition in
Theorem

1
, and is therefore generically inefficient (
Propositions

6
and
7
).

Our focus is different: rather than asking whether equilibria are efficient, whether efficient outcomes can be implemented, or how they can be reached, we characterize which feasible outcomes can lie on the ex-ante Pareto frontier. Model and Notion of Efficiency

We first present the model for a finite state space. The infinite state extension is given in Section
4
. There are
k\geq 2
players and a finite set
A
of feasible actions. For example,
A
may be the Cartesian product
A=\prod_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
, where
A_{i}
is player
i
’s action set. Alternatively,
A
may represent the set of feasible allocations. The state of the world is drawn from a finite set
\Omega
according to a common prior
p\in\mathrm{int}\,\Delta(\Omega)
. Player
i
’s payoff is
u_{i}:\Omega\times A\to\mathbb{R}
. Let

u(\omega,a):=\bigl(u_{1}(\omega,a),\ldots,u_{k}(\omega,a)\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

denote the vector of players’ payoffs.

An
outcome
is a mapping
\mu\colon\Omega\to\Delta(A)
, where
\mu(a\mid\omega)
is the probability that action
a
is played in state
\omega
. When
A=\prod_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
, one can think of an outcome as generated by a mediator who observes the realized state and, possibly at random, recommends an action to each player, which the players follow. These recommendations need not be incentive compatible; our efficiency benchmark is the set of all feasible outcomes.
2
2

2

Players may also have private information about the payoff-relevant state. Such information can affect incentive-compatibility constraints, but not the feasible payoff set.

The payoff vector induced by outcome
\mu
given prior
p
is

u(\mu):=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}p(\omega)\sum_{a\in A}\mu(a\mid\omega)u(\omega,a).

(1)

The set of feasible payoff vectors given prior
p
is

F_{p}:=\{\bar{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\bar{u}=u(\mu)\text{ for some outcome }\mu\colon\Omega\to\Delta(A)\}.

(2)

It is a convex polytope, and its extreme points are induced by pure outcomes.

Definition 1
.

Given a compact convex set of feasible payoffs
F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
, a vector
\bar{u}\in F
is
efficient
if there is no
\bar{v}\in F
such that
\bar{v}\geq\bar{u}
and
\bar{v}_{i}>\bar{u}_{i}
for at least one player
i
.

In the finite model, every efficient feasible payoff vector maximizes a strictly positive weighted sum of the players’ payoffs.
3
3

3

This equivalence need not hold for arbitrary feasible payoff sets; see

for a related “near” weighted-sum characterization. In our finite model, it holds because
F_{p}
is a convex polytope.

Since each outcome induces a feasible payoff vector, we define efficiency of an outcome through the efficiency of its induced payoff vector.

Definition 2
.

An outcome
\mu\colon\Omega\to\Delta(A)
is
efficient
if
u(\mu)
is efficient with respect to
F_{p}
.

Given an outcome
\mu
, we refer to
\mu(\omega)\in\Delta(A)
and
u(\mu\mid\omega):=\sum_{a\in A}\mu(a\mid\omega)u(\omega,a)
as the state-contingent outcome and the state-contingent payoff vector. Let

F_{\omega}:=\mathrm{co}\{u(\omega,a):a\in A\}

(3)

denote the feasible payoff vectors in state
\omega
. The feasible payoff set given a prior can be decomposed as the weighted Minkowski sum of the state-contingent feasible sets,

F_{p}=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}p(\omega)F_{\omega}.

(4)

Our notion of efficiency is ex ante: outcomes are evaluated before the state is realized.
4
4

4

There is no unique notion of efficiency under incomplete information.
Holmström and Myerson (1983)
distinguish several notions based on timing and feasibility. Our notions correspond to their ex-ante and ex-post classical efficiency.
For comparison, we also define ex-post efficiency, which evaluates outcomes after the state is realized.

Definition 3
.

An outcome
\mu\colon\Omega\to\Delta(A)
is
ex-post efficient
in state
\omega
if
u(\mu\mid\omega)
is efficient with respect to
F_{\omega}
.

Ex-ante efficiency implies ex-post efficiency in every state, but the converse need not hold. The following proposition gives the geometric relation between the two notions.

Proposition 1
.

An outcome
\mu\colon\Omega\to\Delta(A)
is efficient if and only if there exists a strictly positive vector

n\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{k}
such that, for every state
\omega\in\Omega
,

u(\mu\mid\omega)\in\arg\max_{x\in F_{\omega}}n^{\top}x.

Intuitively, ex-ante efficiency requires a single strictly positive weight vector to support the outcome in every state. If an outcome fails to maximize the corresponding weighted sum in some state, then some alternative action gives a strictly higher weighted payoff in that state. Replacing the outcome in that state with this action would therefore raise the overall weighted payoff, contradicting efficiency. In particular, efficiency depends only on the state-contingent support of the outcome, not on the probabilities assigned to actions within the support. By contrast, ex-post efficiency in state
\omega
only requires that the state-contingent outcome be supported by some strictly positive weight vector
n_{\omega}
. Even if the outcome is ex-post efficient in every state, the supporting weights may vary across states, so the outcome need not be ex-ante efficient.

Necessary condition for efficiency

Our main result provides a necessary condition for efficiency based on the number of actions taken across states. Given an outcome
\mu
and a state
\omega
, let
|\mu(\omega)|
denote the size of the support of
\mu(\omega)
, that is, the number of actions taken with positive probability in that state. Throughout this section, we say a property holds generically if it holds outside a subset of the payoff space
\mathbb{R}^{k|\Omega||A|}
with Lebesgue measure zero.

Generically, efficiency requires that the total number of actions taken across all states be strictly less than the sum of the number of players and states; excessive randomization therefore leads to inefficiency. Efficiency requires a common strictly positive weight vector that supports the outcome in every state. Generically, when too many actions are taken across states, no such vector exists.

Theorem 1
.

Generically, an outcome
\mu\colon\Omega\to\Delta(A)
is efficient only if

\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}|\mu(\omega)|<k+|\Omega|.

(5)

Proof.

By
Proposition

1
, if
\mu
is efficient, there exists a common
strictly positive weight vector
n\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{++}
that supports the
outcome in every state. Fix a state
\omega
and let
a_{1},\dots,a_{|\mu(\omega)|}
denote the pure actions used by
\mu
in that state. Since each of these actions must maximize the same weighted payoff,

n^{\top}\bigl(u(\omega,a_{i})-u(\omega,a_{1})\bigr)=0\quad\text{for }i=2,\dots,|\mu(\omega)|.

Collect these indifference conditions across states in a matrix

D_{\mu}
. It has

r=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}(|\mu(\omega)|-1)

rows, and the indifference conditions across all states can be written as
D_{\mu}n=0
.

Fix the supports of the outcome across states. If
r\geq k
, then, except on a measure-zero set of payoff profiles, at least
k
of the associated payoff-difference vectors are linearly independent. Hence,
D_{\mu}
has rank
k
, so
D_{\mu}n=0
implies
n=0
. Since
A
and
\Omega
are finite, there are only finitely many possible supports across states; taking the union of such sets preserves measure zero.

Thus, if

\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}|\mu(\omega)|\geq k+|\Omega|,

then
r\geq k
. Generically, no strictly positive common supporting vector exists, so
\mu
is inefficient.
∎

Notably, the bound depends only on the number of actions taken across states, not on the interior prior, the specific actions taken, or the probabilities with which they are taken. It also implies the state-wise bound
|\mu(\omega)|\leq k
for all
\omega\in\Omega
. Generically, the same bound is necessary even for ex-post efficiency in a given state.

For two-player games, outcomes can be pure (a deterministic action in every state), quasi-pure (a deterministic action in all but one state, where exactly two actions are taken), or mixed (any other case). Generically, only pure or quasi-pure outcomes can be efficient.

For two-state games with
k
players, the restriction becomes particularly strong under independent randomization. Suppose
k_{i}
players independently randomize over at least two actions in state
\omega_{i}
for
i=0,1
. Then
|\mu(\omega_{i})|\geq 2^{k_{i}}
, so generically efficiency requires

2^{k_{0}}+2^{k_{1}}<k+2.

Hence, as the number of players grows, only a vanishing fraction of players can independently randomize. The same conclusion holds more generally for any fixed number of states.

The necessary condition in
Theorem

1
is far from sufficient. For instance, in
Example

1
, the outcome is quasi-pure and satisfies the bound in cases (a) and (c), yet is inefficient in both cases. Even if the outcome is pure and ex-post efficient in every state, the overall outcome can still be inefficient. Efficiency is guaranteed, for example, when the outcome induces one player’s unique most preferred action in every state. In
Example

1
, this occurs when the sender fully reveals the state and the receiver takes his optimal action in each state, or when the sender’s preferred action is chosen in every state, as in case (b).

A complete characterization of efficiency is provided in
Proposition

8
in the Appendix. Although we take an ex-ante view, efficiency can be assessed by examining the payoff changes generated by deviations in each state. An outcome is efficient if and only if no nonnegative combination of these statewise deviations leads to a Pareto improvement. This condition depends only on the state-contingent support of the outcome, not on the interior prior or the randomization probabilities.

Efficiency in Infinite State Spaces

In this section, we extend the analysis to infinite state spaces with a nonatomic prior. The main insight remains the same: excessive randomization leads to inefficiency. In fact, the conclusion becomes sharper: generically, every efficient outcome is pure almost surely.

Let
\Omega
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
p
denote the nonatomic
common prior, a Borel probability measure on
\Omega
. The set of actions
A
remains finite. Let
u
denote the payoff profile, where
u(\omega,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is the vector of players’ payoffs from action
a
in state
\omega
. For each
a\in A
, assume
\omega\mapsto u(\omega,a)
is measurable and bounded. An outcome is a measurable mapping
\mu\colon\Omega\to\Delta(A)
, and it induces the payoff vector

u(\mu):=\int_{\Omega}\sum_{a\in A}\mu(a\mid\omega)u(\omega,a)\,dp(\omega).

(6)

With infinite states, the feasible payoff set given prior
p
can be written as the Aumann integral of the state-contingent feasible sets
5
5

5

For a measurable correspondence

\omega\mapsto F_{\omega}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
, its Aumann integral is

\int_{\Omega}F_{\omega}\,dp(\omega):=\left\{\int_{\Omega}f(\omega)\,dp(\omega):f\text{ is a measurable integrable selection of }F_{\omega}\right\},

where
f
is a selection if
f(\omega)\in F_{\omega}
for
p
-almost every

\omega
. In our setting, integrability follows from the boundedness of payoffs.

F_{p}=\int_{\Omega}F_{\omega}\,dp(\omega).

(7)

The set
F_{p}
is compact and convex.

As in the finite-state case, efficiency requires a common supporting weight vector in almost every state, although here the vector need only be nonzero and nonnegative.

Proposition 2
.

If an outcome
\mu\colon\Omega\to\Delta(A)
is efficient, then there exists a nonzero nonnegative vector
n\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
such that, for
p
-almost every state
\omega
,

u(\mu\mid\omega)\in\arg\max_{x\in F_{\omega}}n^{\top}x.

Since the feasible payoff set is obtained by integrating the state-contingent feasible sets
F_{\omega}
, a weight vector supporting an ex-ante efficient payoff must support the outcome in almost every state. Otherwise, there would be a set of states with positive probability in which the outcome fails to maximize the weighted payoff. Changing the outcome on that set would increase the overall weighted payoff, contradicting the fact that the ex-ante payoff is supported by this weight vector.

Let
\mathcal{U}
denote the space of bounded measurable payoff profiles
u\colon\Omega\times A\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
, endowed with the supremum norm. Because
\mathcal{U}
is infinite dimensional, there is no natural analogue of
Lebesgue measure on the entire space. We therefore use prevalence as our measure-theoretic notion of genericity; see
Hunt et al. (1992)
and
Anderson and Zame (2001)
.
6
6

6

Formal definitions of shyness, prevalence, and finite prevalence are provided in
Section

A.4
.
Throughout this section, a property holds generically if it holds on a
prevalent subset of
\mathcal{U}
.

We now state our main result for infinite state spaces.

Theorem 2
.

Generically, an outcome
\mu\colon\Omega\to\Delta(A)
is efficient only if it is pure
p
-almost surely.

Proof.

The key argument is that efficiency requires a common vector of welfare weights to support the outcome in almost every state. Thus, if an efficient outcome randomizes between two distinct actions on a set of states with positive probability, these pure actions must have the same weighted payoff on that set. We show that, given a nonatomic prior, payoff profiles exhibiting such weighted ties on a set of states with positive probability form a finitely shy set.

Formally, let

\mathcal{B}:=\left\{u\in\mathcal{U}\colon\text{some efficient outcome is not pure $p$-almost surely}\right\}

denote the set of payoff profiles that admit at least one efficient outcome which randomizes on a set of states of positive probability.

Fix
u\in\mathcal{B}
, and let
\mu
be an efficient outcome that is not pure
p
-almost surely. By

Proposition

2
, there exists

n\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
such that, for
p
-almost every state
\omega
, every action in the support of
\mu(\cdot\mid\omega)
maximizes
n^{\top}u(\omega,\cdot)
over
A
.

Since
\mu
is not pure
p
-almost surely and
A
is finite, there exist distinct actions
a,b\in A
that both belong to the support of
\mu(\cdot\mid\omega)
on a set with positive probability. For almost every state in this set, both actions maximize the same weighted payoff. Hence,

p\Bigl(\bigl\{\omega\in\Omega:n^{\top}u(\omega,a)=n^{\top}u(\omega,b)\bigr\}\Bigr)>0.

Let
E_{a,b}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
denote the set of payoff profiles for which the above weighted tie condition holds for some

n\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k}\setminus\{0\}
. Thus, every
u\in\mathcal{B}
belongs to
E_{a,b}
for some distinct
a,b\in A
, and hence

\mathcal{B}\subseteq\bigcup_{a\neq b}E_{a,b}.

By
Proposition

9
in the Appendix, each
E_{a,b}
is finitely shy. Since
A
is finite,
\mathcal{B}
is also finitely shy. Hence, its complement is finitely prevalent, and therefore prevalent. Thus, generically, every efficient outcome is pure
p
-almost surely.
∎

Unlike the finite state result, which limits the extent of randomization,
Theorem

2
rules out randomization on any set of states with positive probability.

The result is relevant for mechanism design without transfers. The state space
\Omega
may represent values, costs, qualities, or a profile of privately observed types, while
A
is the finite set of feasible allocations—for example, which agent receives an object. Thus, with a nonatomic prior, stochastic allocation rules are generically incompatible with Pareto efficiency, even when randomization is useful for incentive compatibility
(
Kováč and Mylovanov, 2009
;
Miralles, 2012
)
.
7
7

7

The genericity statement is with respect to the unrestricted space of payoff profiles. Many applications impose additional structure on payoffs; for example, in mechanism design an agent’s payoff may depend only on her own type and the allocation. In such cases, one may study genericity relative to the restricted class or apply Proposition
2
directly, as in Section
5.1
.

5

Applications

We apply our results to three canonical environments: Bayesian allocation without transfers, cheap talk, and Bayesian persuasion. The first application
uses the infinite state result, while the latter two use the finite state analysis.

5.1

Bayesian allocation without transfers

We first apply the infinite state result to an allocation problem without monetary transfers, studied by
Miralles (2012)
. Two ex-ante identical indivisible objects are allocated among
k
agents. Agent
i
privately observes her valuation vector
\omega_{i}=(\omega_{i1},\omega_{i2})\in[0,1]^{2}
, drawn independently from a common distribution
F
with a symmetric density. The state is the valuation profile of all agents
\omega=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{k})
. A deterministic allocation is
a=(a_{1},a_{2})
, where
a_{j}
indicates which agent receives object
j
, allowing an object to remain unassigned. Let
A
denote the finite set of such allocations. Valuations are additive across objects, so agent
i
’s payoff is

u_{i}(\omega,a)=\omega_{i1}\mathbf{1}\{a_{1}=i\}+\omega_{i2}\mathbf{1}\{a_{2}=i\}.

Agents report their valuations to the planner, and a mechanism specifies a lottery over allocations for every profile of reports. Bayesian incentive compatibility requires that truthful reporting maximize each agent’s interim expected utility, given that the other agents report truthfully. The planner chooses a Bayesian incentive-compatible mechanism to maximize the sum of the agents’ ex-ante expected payoffs. Under truthful reporting, we identify the allocation rule with the induced outcome

\mu\colon\Omega\to\Delta(A)
.

Miralles (2012)
characterizes the optimal allocation rule when
F
is regular and satisfies a monotonicity condition.
8
8

8

Roughly, regularity ensures that assigning an object to an agent who prefers it generates more welfare than assigning it to an agent who moderately prefers the other object. The monotonicity condition ensures that, when all agents prefer the same object, the other object is assigned to the agent with the most moderate preferences. See
Miralles (2012)
for formal definitions.
We maintain these assumptions throughout this subsection. The optimal allocation rule is implemented through a combination of lotteries and auctions.

For illustration, consider the case with two agents. If they prefer different objects, each receives her preferred object. If they prefer the same object, each is initially given probability
1/2
of receiving it, while the other object is auctioned. The winner pays her bid in probability units of the preferred object, which are transferred to the loser. Hence, when both agents prefer object
1
and agent
1
wins the auction for object
2
, the mechanism randomizes between giving both objects to agent
1
and giving object
2
to agent
1
and object
1
to agent
2
. Notably, each allocation in this randomization is ex-post efficient. With more than two agents, the auction is supplemented by an insurance scheme that relaxes the constraint imposed by agents’ initial probability endowments and ensures feasibility.

Randomization plays a central role in the optimal allocation rule. Allocation probabilities serve as a substitute for monetary transfers and allow the planner to exploit the intensity with which agents prefer one object to the other.
Theorem

2
suggests that such randomization is generically incompatible with efficiency. In this environment, we can go further and show that the optimal allocation rule is inefficient.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose
F
is regular and satisfies the monotonicity condition. Then, for every
k\geq 2
, the outcome induced by the optimal allocation rule is inefficient.

To see why, consider the case with two agents and recall
Proposition

2
. If the allocation rule were efficient, there would exist a common vector

n=(n_{1},n_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
that supports the outcome at almost every valuation profile. In particular, it must support the outcome at almost every valuation profile where the allocation rule randomizes. The weighted welfare from giving both objects to agent
1
is
n_{1}(\omega_{11}+\omega_{12})
, whereas that from giving object
2
to agent
1
and object
1
to agent
2
is

n_{1}\omega_{12}+n_{2}\omega_{21}
. Since both allocations are in the support of the allocation rule on a set of positive probability, efficiency would require

n_{1}\omega_{11}=n_{2}\omega_{21}

for almost every valuation profile in this set. But since
F
has a density, for any fixed
n
this equality holds with probability zero. Thus, no common vector can support this allocation rule.

Although the mechanism maximizes utilitarian welfare subject to incentive compatibility, the induced outcome is Pareto inefficient. Thus, the randomization used to elicit private information forces the welfare-maximizing allocation rule away from the underlying Pareto frontier.

5.2

Cheap talk

We consider the cheap talk model
(
Crawford and Sobel, 1982
;
Green and Stokey, 2007
)
, in which an informed sender communicates with an uninformed receiver without commitment.

The state
\omega\in\Omega
is drawn according to the common prior
p
and observed by the sender, who sends a message
m\in M
. After observing the message, the receiver chooses an action
a\in A
. The sender’s and receiver’s payoffs are
u_{S}(\omega,a)
and
u_{R}(\omega,a)
, respectively. The sender’s strategy
\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Delta(M)
and the receiver’s strategy
\tau\colon M\to\Delta(A)
induce the outcome

\mu(a\mid\omega)=\sum_{m\in M}\sigma(m\mid\omega)\tau(a\mid m).

We refer to any outcome induced by a Perfect Bayesian equilibrium as a
cheap talk outcome
. Cheap talk generally admits multiple equilibria, including a
babbling equilibrium
, which always exists: no information is conveyed, and the receiver plays a best response to the prior.
9
9

9

Unlike in Bayesian persuasion, we cannot restrict attention to direct signaling policies, since some cheap talk equilibria require the receiver to randomize between actions after a given message.

For cheap talk,
Theorem

1
can be strengthened: generically, any stochastic outcome, including a quasi-pure one, is inefficient.

Proposition 4
.

Generically, a cheap talk outcome is efficient only if it is pure.

In any stochastic cheap talk outcome, either the sender mixes across messages in some state or the receiver mixes after an on-path message. In the first case, the sender must be indifferent between the induced lotteries over actions in that state; in the second, the receiver must be indifferent between the pure actions played at the induced posterior. Efficiency requires the other player to be indifferent as well. However, these simultaneous indifferences are non-generic. Thus, only pure cheap talk outcomes can be efficient.

Recently,
Kamenica and Lin (2025)
show that, generically, if the sender must randomize in all of his preferred cheap talk equilibria, then he values commitment. Together with our result, this shows that a pure outcome is necessary for efficiency and, among the sender’s preferred equilibria, for commitment to have no value.

For any receiver belief
q\in\Delta(\Omega)
, let

A^{*}(q):=\arg\max_{a\in A}\mathbb{E}_{q}[u_{R}(\omega,a)]

denote the receiver’s set of best responses, and define the sender’s value function by

V(q):=\max_{a\in A^{*}(q)}\mathbb{E}_{q}[u_{S}(\omega,a)].

Thus,

is the sender’s expected payoff when the receiver selects the sender’s preferred action among his best responses.

Proposition 5

Suppose the action set is non-redundant and the sender’s payoffs are distinct across actions. If the sender’s payoff is state-independent, a cheap talk outcome is efficient if and only if the sender’s most preferred action

5.3

Bayesian persuasion

We now turn to Bayesian persuasion
(
Kamenica and Gentzkow, 2011
)
, in which the sender commits to a signaling policy before the state is realized.

The sender chooses a message space
M
and a signaling policy
\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Delta(M)
. After the state
\omega\in\Omega
is drawn according to the prior
p
, a message is sent according to
\sigma
, and the receiver chooses an action
a\in A
. As is standard, ties are broken in favor of the sender. Without loss, we restrict attention to direct signaling policies
\mu\colon\Omega\to\Delta(A)
, where messages are action recommendations. Given prior
p
, let
\mu_{p}^{*}
denote the Bayesian persuasion outcome.
10
10

10

Generically, the Bayesian persuasion outcome is unique.
The
Bayesian persuasion (BP) outcome

\mu_{p}^{*}\colon\Omega\to\Delta(A)
solves

\max_{\mu\colon\Omega\to\Delta(A)}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}p(\omega)\sum_{a\in A}\mu(a\mid\omega)u_{S}(\omega,a)

(8)

subject to

\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}p(\omega)\mu(a\mid\omega)\bigl(u_{R}(\omega,a)-u_{R}(\omega,b)\bigr)\geq 0\qquad\forall a,b\in A.

(9)

Equation

9
is the receiver’s obedience condition: upon receiving recommendation
a
, the receiver prefers following it to choosing any other action
b
.

Kamenica and Gentzkow (2011)
characterize the BP outcome using the concavification approach
(
Aumann and Maschler, 1995
)
. Let
\mathrm{Cav}\;V\colon\Delta(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
denote the concave envelope of the value function
V
. The sender’s expected payoff in the equilibrium outcome is given by the evaluation of the concave envelope at the prior:
\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{*}_{p}}[u_{S}(\omega,a)]=\mathrm{Cav}\;V(p)
.

p\in[0.2,0.4]
and inefficient for

.

Safe and risky actions

We next study an environment with one safe action and several risky actions. The receiver takes risky action
a_{i}
only when sufficiently confident that state
\omega_{i}
has occurred; otherwise, he chooses the safe action
a_{0}

The action set is
A=\{a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}

a_{0}
is safe and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
are risky. The state space is
\Omega=\{\omega_{0},\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}\}
, with
a_{i}
optimal for the receiver in state
\omega_{i}
. The sender’s payoff is state independent: he gets
u_{S}(a_{i})>0
from every risky action and
u_{S}(a_{0})=0
from the safe action. We assume that the prior
p\in\operatorname{int}\Delta(\Omega)
lies in the region where
a_{0}
is optimal for the receiver. To describe the receiver’s preferences more generally, we specify the set of beliefs under which each action is optimal.

Recall that
A^{*}(q)
denotes the set of receiver’s best responses given belief
q
. Let
C_{i}\subseteq\Delta(\Omega)
denote the set of beliefs at which action
a_{i}
is a receiver best response:

C_{i}:=\{q\in\Delta(\Omega)\colon a_{i}\in A^{*}(q)\}.

The collection

covers the belief space
\Delta(\Omega)

1.

For each

\omega_{i}\in C_{i}

C_{i}
contains an open neighborhood of
\omega_{i}
.

2.

For any distinct

C_{i}\cap C_{j}=\emptyset
.

Condition 1 ensures that action

is optimal for beliefs sufficiently close to

a_{i}

a_{i}
and the safe action
a_{0}

\omega_{i}
to
\omega_{j}

o_{ij}\in C_{i}
, for
j\neq i
.

6

Conclusion

This paper studied Pareto efficiency in games with incomplete information. Our main observation is that excessive randomization over actions leads to inefficiency. Efficiency requires that all state-contingent outcomes be supported by a common vector of welfare weights. When several actions are used across states, this requirement imposes multiple indifference conditions that generically cannot be satisfied simultaneously.

In finite state spaces, we derived a simple necessary condition for efficiency: generically, the total number of actions played across states must be strictly less than the sum of the number of players and the number of states. With a nonatomic prior, generically, every efficient outcome is pure almost surely. Across the three applications, the same tension emerges. Randomization can help satisfy incentive and equilibrium requirements, yet the resulting indifferences are difficult to reconcile with Pareto efficiency.

Suppose that
\mu

F_{p}

u(\mu)

n\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{++}

n^{\top}u(\mu)=\max_{z\in F_{p}}n^{\top}z

We claim that the same vector
n

u(\mu\mid\omega)

\omega^{*}

y\in F_{\omega^{*}}

n^{\top}y>n^{\top}u(\mu\mid\omega^{*})

u(\mu\mid\omega^{*})

z=p(\omega^{*})y+\sum_{\omega\neq\omega^{*}}p(\omega)u(\mu\mid\omega).

Since
p(\omega^{*})>0
, we have
n^{\top}z>n^{\top}u(\mu)
, contradicting the maximality of
u(\mu)
over
F_{p}

u(\mu\mid\omega)\in\argmax_{x\in F_{\omega}}n^{\top}x

\omega

n\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{++}

\omega

z\in F_{p}

F_{p}=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}p(\omega)F_{\omega}

By state-wise optimality,
n^{\top}x_{\omega}\leq n^{\top}u(\mu\mid\omega)

\omega

u(\mu)

n^{\top}x

In the following proposition, we provide a necessary and sufficient condition for an outcome to be efficient. To check for efficiency, one must look at the possible payoff changes from deviating to any action in each state. Define

\omega

\mu(\omega)\in\Delta(A)

\mathcal{D}_{\mu}=\{\tilde{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\tilde{d}=d_{\mu}(\omega,a)\text{ for some }\omega\in\Omega,a\in A\}

denote the set of all deviations given outcome
