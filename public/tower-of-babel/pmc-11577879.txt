Common mental disorders and associated factors among adults after COVID-19 pandemic in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Temesgen Gebeyehu Wondmeneh, Zelalem Solomon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobal mental health has been threatened by the COVID-19 pandemic, both directly through long-lasting neuropsychiatric disorders that occur during primary infection in affected individuals and indirectly through stressful and disruptive societal changes. Thus, this study determined the pooled prevalence of common mental disorders and associated factors among adults after COVID-19 pandemic in Ethiopia.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted using primary published and unpublished studies that were retrieved from various databases. Studies conducted on adults, published in English, and conducted in Ethiopia were included in this review. A standardized data extraction format developed from Excel was used to collect the data. A random effect meta-analysis model was used to estimate the pooled effect size of all included studies at a 95% confidence interval. The heterogeneity was evaluated by Cochran Q test and the I-squared. Funnel plot and egger tests were used to determine publication bias.ResultsA total of 20 studies were eligible for this systematic review. The pooled prevalence of common mental disorders among adults was 40.44% (95%CI: 31.86–49.02%). Female gender (AOR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.33–2.44), unemployed (AOR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.12–2.98), poor social support (AOR = 3.12, 95% CI: 2.08–4.17), substance use (AOR = 2.5, 95% CI: 1.58–3.41), history of mental illness (AOR = 1.73, 95% CI: 1.01–2.44), family history of mental illness (AOR = 2.47, 95% CI: 1.54–3.41), and chronic medical illness (AOR = 1.6, 95%CI:1.02–2.17) were risk factors for common mental disorders.ConclusionIn this study, more than one-third of adults were affected by common mental disorders after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. This indicated that, in order to reduce the prevalence of common mental disorders, enhancing the provision of mental health services should be improved after the COVID-19 pandemic. Screening for common mental disorders should be given, especially to females, unemployed people, substance users, chronic medically ill people, those with a history of mental illnesses, and those with a family history of mental illness. Strengthening social support during the COVID-19 pandemic is also important.PROSPERO protocol registrationThe registration ID for this systematic review is CRD42024496826.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-024-06290-1.Keywords: Common, Mental disorders, Adults, After COVID-19, Pandemic, Ethiopia

Introduction

A mental disorder is a clinically significant disruption in a person’s cognition, emotional regulation, or behavior [1, 2]. Common mental disorders are a set of distress states characterized by anxiety, depression, and unexplained somatic symptoms commonly found in primary care settings [3, 4]. Mental disorders were the leading cause of global health-related burden, with anxiety and depression disorders accounting for the majority of this burden [5]. Mental health disorders affect nearly 13% of people worldwide of any age [6]. One in five people worldwide was found to have a common mental disorder [7]. Global mental health has been put at risk by the COVID-19 pandemic, which has an effect both directly through neuropsychiatric symptoms following the SARS-CoV-2 infection and indirectly by disruptive societal changes [8]. The prevalence of common mental health disorders is predicted to increase during the post-pandemic period as a consequence of the long-term effects of the pandemic, restrictive measures such as social separation and quarantine, and socio-economic effects [9, 10]. The most common psychiatric symptoms of long-term COVID-19 syndrome were fatigue, cognitive disturbances/impairments, depression, and anxiety symptoms [11, 12]. According to the WHO report for 2022, one in every eight people in the world lives with a mental disorder [1]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the pooled global prevalence of depression and anxiety was 29.98% and 33.59%, respectively [13], while in another study carried out during the COVID-19 pandemic, the estimated global prevalence of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress symptoms was 28.0%, 26.9%, and 24.1%, respectively [14]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the prevalence of common mental disorders was 16.1% in Hong Kong [15], while in Brazil, it was 39.7% in medical students [16]. In Kenya, 32.1% of healthcare workers were affected by depression, 36% by generalized anxiety, and 24.2% by insomnia during COVID-19 [17].

In Ethiopia, the pooled prevalence of common mental disorders was 62.29% in prisoners [18], 28.83% in HIV-infected adults [19], and 37.73% in khat chewers [20]. The prevalence of common mental illnesses was 21.58% in the general population and 36.43% in patients with comorbid problems [21]. In district-level studies of Ethiopia, the prevalence of common mental disorders among adults was 39.7% in the Silte Zone [22], 32.4% in Dilla town [23] and 27.2% in the Illu Ababore Zone before the COVID-19 pandemic [24].

Individuals with a history of mental disorder, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, and traits experienced greater trouble recovering after the COVID-19 pandemic and lockdown release [25]. This could increase the risk of negative outcomes [26]. Risk factors for common mental disorders were living in a rural area [24], having a history of chronic medical illness, and having a family history of psychiatric illness [27]. Females, divorced or widowed, daily laborers and chronic medical illnesses are independent predictors of common mental disorders [28–30]. Poor social support, substance use, diabetes mellitus [31], single marital status, and unemployment [19] were significant risk factors for common mental disorders.

Although there is preliminary data on the prevalence of common mental disorders post-COVID-19 pandemic, there is a lack of understanding about the pooled prevalence of common mental disorders and associated risk factors among adults in Ethiopia. The primary studies investigated after the COVID-19 pandemic in Ethiopia had great variety and inconsistency related to the prevalence of common mental disorders and associated risk factors among adults in Ethiopia. Therefore, the aim of this systematic review was to determine the pooled prevalence of common mental disorders and associated risk factors among adults in Ethiopia. This up-to-date information on COVID-19’s effects on mental health is essential for informing the actions of the health system. Policymakers and other concerned bodies used the findings to design appropriate interventional programs.

Research questions.

What is the pooled prevalence of common mental disorders among adults in Ethiopia after the COVID-19 pandemic?

What is the pooled effect size of associated factors with common mental disorders among adults in Ethiopia after the COVID-19 pandemic?

Methods

Protocol and registration

The current systematic review and meta-analysis protocol was registered in the international prospective register of systemic review (PROSPERO), available online (ID = CRD42024496826). The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-2020) checklists [32] appropriate guideline for systematic reviews and meta-analyses reports was used (S1 File).

Search strategies

The searches were conducted in the PubMed, CINAHL, Google Scholar, and African journals online databases. An additional search for relevant articles was conducted by scanning the reference lists of the included articles. The search was restricted to studies published in the English language, and the publication date was restricted from January 1, 2020, to October 10, 2024, where a database could allow this filter. The MeSH terms and keywords “adult,” “mental disorders,” “Ethiopia,” and the phrase “common mental disorders” were also used in the search. The MeSH terms or keywords included “adult,” “mental disorders,” and “Ethiopia,” which were combined by the boolean operator “AND.” Synonyms for each of the key search terms were combined using the “OR” Boolean operator. The search terms were built up by considering population, outcome, and context (POCo). The initial search dates were from January 15–22/2024 and then updated from October 8–10/2024 (S2 File).

Eligible criteria

The inclusion criteria, according to population outcome and context (POCo), were as follows:

Study population: adults whose ages were 18 years or greater.

The outcome: common mental disorder.

Study setting (context): studies conducted in Ethiopia.

Study type: All descriptive and observational study designs (i.e., cross-sectional, case-control, and cohort) reported the prevalence of common mental disorders that can be compatible with the POCo framework.

Publication types: published and unpublished free full text.

Language: only studies published in English were reviewed.

Study dates: this meta-analysis comprised studies conducted between 2020/01/01 and 2024/10/10.

Exclusion criteria

Studies that did not provide accurate information about the magnitude of common mental disorders. Articles without full text or extraction difficulties were excluded despite contacting the corresponding author repeatedly. The reasons for excluding these articles were that the lack of full text made it impossible to evaluate the articles’ quality. Editorials, commentary, reviews, and qualitative studies were not considered.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experimental studies were not used because such types of designs are very limited in this study topic to be consistent with the POCo framework.

Study selection

The first studies were found by applying the search strings and filters in the databases, as well as other relevant sources. Articles identified in different search databases were exported to Endnote X8.1 software, and duplicated articles were removed. We reviewed abstracts and titles to make sure they related to the objective of the systematic review. Articles considered essential based on a review of their abstracts and titles were selected for thorough evaluation by reading the full text. After the full text evaluation, articles that were found to be eligible were included in this systematic review. TGW and ZS independently screened the full texts for eligibility, and the disagreements were resolved by scientific consensus.

Data extraction

Two authors (TGW and ZS) independently extracted all the required data from the included primary papers using a standardized data extraction format developed from Microsoft Excel. The final data extraction format included the first author’s name, the publication year, the data collection period, the study region, study type, study subjects, assessment tools used to screen for common mental disorders, sample size, the number of cases with common mental disorders, the prevalence of common mental disorders, and the response rate. For factors associated with common mental disorders, a two-by-two table data extraction format was prepared. Disagreements that arose throughout the data extraction process were settled by scientific consensus through discussion. The details of the extracted data were attached (S3 File).

Operational definition

Common mental disorders are a set of distress states that include anxiety, depression, and unexplained somatic symptoms [4].

Outcome measurement

In this study, the prevalence of common mental disorders is determined as a percentage of cases with common mental disorders. Two parameters were required to estimate the prevalence of common mental disorders: the number of cases with common mental disorders and the total number of study participants (sample size). The prevalence was calculated by dividing the number of cases with common mental disorders by the total number of study participants (sample size) multiplied by 100.

Quality assessment

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist was used for the sake of scientific strength [32]. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, which has been adapted for cross-sectional study quality appraisal, was used to assess the quality of the primary studies that were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis [33]. The tool has ten-star ratings in three domain categories. The first domain has five components used to assess the methodological quality of each study: sample representativeness (1 star), sampling technique (1 star), response rate (1 star), and exposure ascertainment (2 stars). The second domain examines the comparability of primary studies with confounding control (data or results adjusted for relevant predictors or risk factors) (2 stars). The third domain measures the quality of original articles in terms of outcome measures (2 stars) and statistical analysis (1 star). Two authors (TGW and ZS) evaluated the quality of each primary paper independently. Subjectivities between the two authors were settled through scientific consensus. Studies with quality scores of less than 50%, 50–69%, and greater than or equal to 70% were classified as low quality, medium quality, or high quality, respectively. Studies fulfilling at least 50% of the quality assessment criteria were included in this review.

Statistical analysis

The retrieved data from the Microsoft Excel spreadsheet was imported into STATA version 15 for statistical analysis. Tables, forest plots, and texts were used to describe and present the findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis. In this study, a random effects model meta-analysis was used to determine the pooled prevalence of common mental disorders with a 95% confidence interval because of the significant heterogeneity (I2 = 99.06%, p < 0.001). The heterogeneity was evaluated by the Chi-square test (Cochran Q test) with a significance of p < 0.01, and its level of magnitude was determined by the I-squared (I2) [34], where substantial heterogeneity was assumed if the I2 value was ≥ 50%. To find the source of heterogeneity, subgroup analysis was conducted by study region, data collection period, study setting, study subjects, assessment tools, and sample size. Sensitivity analysis was used to examine the impact of a single study on the overall prevalence [35]. Egger’s correlation tests and funnel plots were also used to investigate publication bias in primary research [36, 37]. To find the association between dependent and independent variables, the pooled effect size was estimated using the AOR and 95% confidence interval for each factor.

Results

Search results

The first literature search provided a total of 388 articles using databases of PubMed (n=87), CINHAL (n = 14), Google Scholar (n = 276), and African journals online (n = 9). Using the included papers’ reference lists, five other articles were also found. All retrieved articles were imported to Endnote X8.1 software, and 153 duplicated articles were eliminated. After duplicates were removed, 238 articles were deemed eligible for review of the title and abstract. Upon the removal of 203 irrelevant articles based on their title and abstract, 35 articles were retained for further full-text evaluation. After the full texts were read, 15 articles were deleted due to failure to report the magnitude of the outcome, review articles, studies conducted before the COVID-19 pandemic, and differences in the study population. In the end, it was found that 20 articles met the requirements for inclusion in this systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Shows the PRISMA flow chart for the selection of studies for systematic review

Included studies characteristics

A total of 20 eligible studies were included for systematic review and meta-analysis. With the exception of four community-based cross-sectional studies [38–41], the majority of the included studies were institutional cross-sectional studies. There were 11,041 study participants from the regions of Amhara, Oromia, Addis Ababa, Harari, and Southern Nation Nationality People (SNNP); 4089 of these participants had common mental disorders. There were seven studies conducted in the Amhara region [39, 42–47], four in the Harari region [40, 48–50], three in the Oromia regions [38, 41, 51], and four in Addis Ababa administrative city [52–55]. The Southern Nation Nationality People (SNNP) had two studies [56, 57]. Five studies were conducted on pregnant women [38, 39, 41, 42, 53]. Four studies were conducted on chronic patients, such as glaucoma patients [46], cancer patients [52], kidney patients [47], and those with diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and asthma [56]. Two studies were conducted on the primary caregivers of mentally ill adults [48, 50]. There were three studies conducted among prisoners [44, 45, 54]. A single study was carried out on COVID-19 suspected cases [49], middle-east returned migrants of Ethiopians [55], healthcare workers [51], lactating mothers [57], and adult holy water users in Orthodox Tewahido Christian Churches [43]. A comparable number of studies were conducted in the 2020, 2021, and 2022 study periods. Nineteen out of twenty studies used the SRQ-20 instrument to measure common mental disorders, with the exception of one that used the BPRS instrument [43]. The largest sample size was 1168 [40], while the smallest sample size was 215 [48]. The maximum prevalence of common mental disorders was 74.6% [45], whereas the minimum prevalence was 13.1% [39]. The minimum response rate was 87.5% (Table 1).

Quality of included studies

The quality of all included studies was assessed by two authors (TGW and ZS) independently with the New Castle Ottawa appraisal check list for cross-sectional studies. Any disagreement was resolved by scientific consensus. Out of a total of 20 included studies, three scored 90%, and the remaining 17 studies scored a perfect 100%. Thus, the quality of all the included studies was high (S4 file).

Pooled prevalence of common mental disorder among adults in Ethiopia

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, a total of 11,041 study participants, of whom 4089 had common mental disorders, emanated from twenty eligible primary studies to determine the pooled prevalence of common mental disorders. The pooled prevalence of common mental disorders among adults was 40.44% (95%CI: 31.86–49.02), with high significant heterogeneity (I2 = 99.09, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Forest plot for the pooled prevalence of common mental disorders

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis based on study region, study setting, study subjects, study period, and assessment tools used to measure common mental disorders was conducted since the pooled prevalence of common mental disorders was influenced by heterogeneity. In this subgroup analysis, the highest pooled prevalence of common mental disorder was in Addis Ababa at 54% (95%CI: 30–79%), followed by Amhara (43%, 95%CI: 24–61%) and Southern Nation Nationality People (41% (95%CI: 38–44%). The prevalence of common mental disorders in the Oromia and Harari regions was 33% (95%CI: 24–42%) and 28% (95%CI: 17–40%), respectively. High heterogeneity was observed in the Amhara (I2 = 99.45%, p < 0.001), Addis Ababa (I2 = 99.24%, p < 0.001), and Harari (I2 = 97.2%, p < 0.001) regions; however, in the Oromia region and Southern Nation Nationality People (SNNP), there is no heterogeneity. The prevalence of common mental disorders was 44% (95%CI: 33–54%) in institutional-based studies (I2 = 99%, p < 0.001), while it was 28% (95%CI: 15–40%) in the community-based studies (I2 = 98.9%, p < 0.001). The highest prevalence of common mental disorders was observed in study subjects with returned migrants of Ethiopian origin from the Middle East, prisoners, holy water users of orthodox Christian fellowship, and chronic patients at 73% (95%CI: 68–77), 64% (95%CI: 52–76), 59% (95%CI: 54–64), and 47% (95%CI: 30 − 63), respectively. The prevalence of common mental disorders was 40% (95%CI: 36–44), 23% (95%CI: 19–27), and 14% (95%CI: 10–17) in primary caregivers of mental ill adults, healthcare workers, and quarantine COVID-19 suspected cases, respectively. In a community study among adults, the prevalence of common mental disorders was 21% (95% CI: 19–24). Among lactating and pregnant women, the prevalence of common mental disorders was 33% (95% CI: 29–38) and 26% respectively. The prevalence of common mental disorders was 44% (95%CI: 24–65), 49% (95%CI: 37–61), and 28% (95%CI: 19–38) in 2020, 2021, and 2022, respectively. In every study period, there was a significant amount of heterogeneity. The prevalence of common mental disorders using the SRQ-20 measurement tools was 39% (95%CI: 31–48) with a high level of heterogeneity (I2 = 99.1%, p < 0.001), whereas the prevalence using the BPRS measurement tools was 59% (95%CI: 54–64) with no heterogeneity (Table 2).

Publication bias

The funnel plot and the Eggers test were used to determine the presence of publication bias in the included studies. The funnel plot demonstrated the asymmetric distribution and the existence of publication bias in the included studies. Additionally, the Eggers test confirmed the existence of publication bias (bias = 25.1, 95%CI: 11.4–38.8, p = 0.001). Thus, the trim-and-fill analysis was carried out. The meta-trim-and-fill determined the number of missing studies to be 8, imputed the omitted studies, and then combined the observed and imputed studies to give 28 studies. The results of the random meta-trim-and-fill analysis showed that the pooled prevalence of common mental disorders was 40.4% (95% CI: 31.9–49) for observed studies and 25.5% (95% CI: 15.9–35.2) for both observed and imputed studies. The imputed studies reduced the pooled common mental disorders nearly by 15% with a slightly wider confidence interval. The funnel plot is more symmetrical due to the imputed studies (Fig. 3).

Funnel graph for the prevalence of common mental disorders in adults

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was conducted to investigate the heterogeneity of those studies by systematically removing one study in order to determine whether each study’s findings affected the overall prevalence of common mental disorders. As can be seen from the results, every value is within the estimated 95% CI, suggesting that the prevalence of this systematic review was not significantly affected by the omission of one study (S5 file).

Associated risk factors for common mental disorders

Risk factors for common mental disorders were females, unemployed, poor social support, substance use, a history of mental disorders, a family history of mental disorders, and chronic medical illness.

The association between gender and common mental disorders

Seven studies [40, 46–49, 51, 52] were used to determine the association between being female and having common mental disorders. All of the included studies’ findings revealed that females had more common mental disorders compared with males. The pooled effect size of the meta-analysis showed that females had 1.88 times higher common mental disorders compared with males (AOR = 1.88; 95% CI: 1.33–2.44) with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 40.3%, p = 0.123) (Fig. 4).

The association between being females’ gender and common mental disorders

The association between residence and common mental disorders

The association between living in a rural area and common mental disorders was investigated using data from five primary studies [39, 43, 44, 46, 49]. Four studies [43, 44, 46, 49] did not show a significant association, while one study [39] showed a significant association. The findings of the current study revealed that rural residence did not have a significant difference in common mental disorders compared to urban residence (AOR = 1.32, 95% CI: 0.35–2.28). There is high heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 84.2%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5).

The association between rural residence and common mental disorders

The association between marital status and common mental disorders

To determine the association between marital status and common mental disorders, four studies [40, 43, 47, 48] were identified. Of these three studies, only one [40] revealed that being single or divorced had a significant association with common mental disorders, and the remaining two did not show this [43, 47, 48]. In this meta-analysis, being single (AOR = 1.36, 95% CI: 0.83–1.89) and divorced (AOR = 1.8, 95% CI: 0.84–2.76) did not have a significant risk association with common mental disorders compared to being married. Moderate heterogeneity was observed among the studies (Fig. 6).

The association between marital status and common mental disorders

The association between occupational status and common mental disorders

Five studies [40, 43, 44, 52, 55] were identified to determine the association between occupational status and common mental disorders. All included studies categorized occupational status as employed and unemployed. Only one study [44] showed a non-significant association between unemployment and common mental disorders, while the others remaining showed a significant association. The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that unemployed peoples were two times more likely to develop common mental disorders compared to employed peoples (AOR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.12–2.98), with high insignificant heterogeneity (I2 = 55%, p = 0.064) (Fig. 7).

The association between unemployed and common mental disorders

The association between social support and common mental disorders

To assess the association between social support and common mental disorders, eight studies were used [43, 44, 47, 50–52, 56, 57]. Using the Oslo-Social Support Scale, social support was rated as strong, moderate, or poor in all included studies. All studies that examined the relationship between poor social support and common mental disorders were significant. Two studies [43, 56] from those examining the relationship between moderate social support and common mental disorders showed significant relationships; the remaining five studies [44, 48, 50–52] did not. According to the meta-analysis’s findings, adults who had poor social support had a three times higher risk of common mental disorders than adults who had strong social support (AOR = 3.12, 95% CI: 2.08–4.17), with no evidence of heterogeneity (I2 = 0, p = 0.707). However, adults with moderate social supports did not reveal a significant association with common mental disorders (AOR = 1.67, 95% CI: 0.87–2.47), with high significant heterogeneity (I2 = 56.4%, p = 0.033) (Fig. 8).

The association between social support and common mental disorders

The association between substance use and common mental disorders

Five studies [38, 48, 49, 51, 52] were used to determine the association between substance use and common mental disorders. All of the studies reported that substance use was a risk factor for common mental disorders. The results of the current study showed that those substance users were 2.5 times more likely to develop common mental disorders than those without substance users (AOR = 2.5, 95% CI: 1.58–3.41). There is low heterogeneity among the included studies (I2 = 15.6%, p = 0.315) (Fig. 9).

The association between substance use and common mental disorders

The association between history of mental disorders and common mental disorders

To investigate the relationship between a history of mental illness and common mental disorders, five studies [38, 39, 43, 44, 54] were used. Three studies reported a significant association [43, 44, 54], whereas the remaining two studies reported no significant association [38, 39]. The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis found that those adults with a history of mental illness were more likely to develop common mental disorders than those without a history of mental illness (AOR = 1.73, 95% CI: 1.01–2.44). Heterogeneity among included studies was absent (I2 = 0, p = 0.472) (Fig. 10).

The association between history of mental illness and common mental disorders

The relation of family history of mental disorders with common mental disorders

The relationship between family history of mental illness and common mental disorders was determined by six studies [39, 40, 47, 48, 50, 56]; of these, five of the studies [39, 40, 47, 48, 50] reported that family history of mental illness is a significant risk factor for common mental illness, while the other one did not report it as a significant risk factor [56]. The meta-analysis’s findings revealed that those adults with a family history of mental illness had nearly 2.5 times higher rates of developing common mental disorders compared to those who did not have a family history of mental illness (AOR = 2.47, 95% CI: 1.54–3.41). There is no heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 0, p = 0.658) (Fig. 11).

The relationship between family history of mental illness and common mental disorders

The association of chronic medical illness with common mental disorders

A total of ten studies [38, 39, 43, 44, 47, 49, 53–56] were used to determine the association between chronic medical illness and common mental disorders. Six studies [47, 48, 53–56] showed significant associations, whereas the remaining four studies [38, 39, 43, 44] did not. This systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that adults with chronic medical illness were more likely to develop common mental disorders than those who did not have chronic medical illness (AOR = 1.6, 95% CI: 1.02–2.17). There was moderate heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 48.6%, p = 0.042) (Fig. 12).

The association of chronic medical illness with common mental disorders

Discussion

This is the first systematic review and meta-analysis study in Ethiopia among adults following the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. This study was conducted to estimate the pooled prevalence and associated factors of common mental disorders among adults after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in Ethiopia.

In the current systematic review and meta-analysis, the pooled common mental disorder among adults after the COVID-19 pandemic was 40.44%. This magnitude is higher than the previous global studies reports [6, 7] and the WHO report of 2022 [1]. In the current study, the impact of COVID-19 may have increased the magnitude of common mental disorders [8–10] in comparison to a prior study [7] that was carried out before the COVID-19 pandemic. The other contributing factor to the increased prevalence of common mental disorders in the current review compared to the WHO report [1] is the possibility of underreporting in the WHO report. The discrepancy can also result from a prior study’s different study objective [6], which may have underreported the magnitude of common mental disorders. The results of this systematic review and meta-analysis are also higher than those of earlier research undertaken during the COVID-19 pandemic at the global level [13, 14] and a study carried out in Kenya [17], in which only the magnitude of anxiety and depression was reported separately. Differences in study subjects and variations in outcome measurements could be the cause of this. Furthermore, studies conducted in Hong Kong [15] that reported common mental disorders of 16.1% had a lower magnitude of common mental disorders compared to the current systematic review. However, the current prevalence of common mental disorders is nearly comparable to that of a study conducted in Brazil [16]. The explanation for this could also be due to the fact that study subjects in Hong Kong are of different ages. The other main reason for the difference in magnitude of common mental disorders may be caused by using different tools to measure the outcome and using SRQ-20 measurement tools with different cut-off points for measuring outcome. When comparing the present magnitude of common mental disorders with the previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses conducted in Ethiopia, it was lower than a study conducted among prisoners [18] but higher than other previous studies [19–21]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the higher prevalence of mental disorders among prisoners, which could be caused by family separation of prisoners that aggravates psychological distress. Another possible explanation for the higher prevalence of common mental disorders in the current meta-analysis compared to the previous studies [19–21] could be related to the long-term psychological effects of COVID-19 [11, 12] on the current study participants and disruption in social change by the COVID-19 pandemic [8]. The magnitude of common mental disorders for the current systematic review and meta-analysis was comparable at a district-level study of the Silte Zone of Ethiopia [22]. The comparability of the magnitude of common mental disorders in a study of the Silte Zone with the current study could be due to the fact that Silte Zone study participants’ lifestyle factors, such as stressful life events, and psychosocial factors, such as poor social supports, It was, however, higher than that of other district-level studies in the Dilla [23] and Illu Ababure [24] Zones of Ethiopia. This could be the result of the long-term effects of the COVID-19 pandemic [11, 12], restriction measures such as social separation or quarantine, and the socioeconomic effects of the pandemic [9, 10]. Using different cut-off points for SRQ-20 tools to measure the outcome could be another possible variation.

In the subgroup analysis, the highest common mental disorder is observed in Addis Ababa administrative city (54%), followed by Amhara (43%) and Southern Nation Nationality People (41%). The prevalence of common mental disorders was highest in studies conducted in institutions. This evidence provides support for the findings of previous studies showing that common mental disorders were commonly found in primary care settings [3, 4]. The highest prevalence of common mental disorders was in returned migrants of Ethiopian origin from the Middle East (73%). The high magnitude of common mental disorders among returned migrants of Ethiopian origin from the Middle East may be caused by stigmatization and discrimination by employees and long-term family separation. Therefore, for those returned migrants, strict mental health provisions should be initiated. The current magnitude of common mental disorders among prisoners (64%) is slightly comparable with the previous study conducted in Ethiopia among prisoners [18], where the prevalence was 62.3%. The explanation for the comparability may be that the two studies were carried out in the same study areas and over nearly the same time period. Holy Water users of orthodox Christian fellowship had a high magnitude of common mental disorders (59%). This high magnitude of common mental disorders among holy water users can indicate that mentally ill people may have a preference for religious sites due to their belief that the supernatural is the source of mental illnesses. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, common mental disorders were present in 47% of chronic patients. This magnitude is greater compared to the prior studies among HIV patients [19] and comorbid patients [21] in Ethiopia, as well as a district-level study among medical and surgical ward patients in Dilla Hospital [23]. The increased magnitude among the current study participants may be due to the psychological effects of both comorbidity and the COVID-19 pandemic on these people. In the subgroup analysis, a significant proportion of primary caregivers of mentally ill adults had common mental disorders (40%). This suggests that the provision of mental health services should be made available to both mental illness patients and their primary caregivers. In this study, the magnitude of common mental disorders among healthcare workers was 23%. This magnitude is lower than a study in Brazil [16] that was 39.7% and another study in Kenya [17] that found that 32.1%, 36%, and 24% of healthcare workers were affected by depression, anxiety, and insomnia, respectively. This may be due to the early spread of the virus and its severity in Brazil and Kenya. The prevalence of common mental disorders among COVID-19 suspected quarantine cases was 14%. The pooled prevalence of common mental disorders was 26% and 33% among pregnant and lactating women, respectively, which is lower than a previous study conducted among pregnant women that was 35.8% in Ethiopia before the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic [27]. Women and COVID-19 suspected quarantine cases may have a lower prevalence of common mental disorders because the Ethiopian government established a rapid response team that offered psychological first aid, reducing psychological distress and common mental disorder symptoms. The prevalence of common mental disorders was 44% in 2020, 49% in 2021, and 28% in 2022. This indicates that the magnitude of common mental disorders increased from the early pandemic to 2021 and then decreased in 2022. The reduction of common mental disorders after 2021 could be due to COVID-19 vaccination production, which may alleviate psychological stress. The prevalence of common mental disorders was higher when utilizing measurement tools like BPRS, indicating it has high sensitivity to detect the outcome; however, there is only a single study that used the BPRS tool to measure the outcome, which may not have low reliability. The low prevalence of common mental disorders in the SRQ-20 tool in comparison to the BPRS tool could be caused by the use of different cut-off points in the SRQ-20 tool among included studies to measure the outcome.

Females were more affected by common mental disorders than males. This evidence is in line with the previous studies [28–30]. Women reported more psychological distress than men. This can be associated with family-work conflict [58]. Unemployed adults were more likely to have common mental disorders; this finding supports the earlier study findings [19]. The COVID-19 pandemic may be the root cause of the unemployment rate since it imposed limitations on socioeconomic measures [9, 10], which may have an indirect negative impact on mental health [8]. Adults with poor social support were more likely to develop common mental disorders than those with strong social support. This evidence is in line with a previous study [31]. This could be because those with strong social support are better able to handle stressful events, which lowers depression [59]. Substance users were at higher risk of developing common mental disorders than non-substance users. This finding supports previous study findings [31]. Multiple and specific substances may progressively exacerbate psychological distress and disorders [60]. Adults who had a history of mental illness or a family history of mental illness were more likely to develop common mental disorders. This evidence is in line with the previous studies [25, 27]. The neuropsychiatric symptoms following the COVID-19 infection and the pandemic’s disruption of societal change [8] may exacerbate the public’s mental illnesses in people who are already vulnerable to mental illness. Adults with chronic medical illness were more likely to develop common mental disorders than those without chronic mental disorders. This finding is consistence with the previous study [28–30].

The limitation of this study is that it did not ascertain temporal relationships due to the cross-sectional nature of all included studies. All regions were not represented by this study. There is high heterogeneity among the included studies. The main causes of this high heterogeneity among included studies can be the use of different cut-off points for SRQ-20 tools to measure the outcome and the inclusion of different characteristics of study subjects such as pregnant women, prisoners, chronic patients, primary caregivers, holy water users, migrants, healthcare workers, COVID-19 suspected cases, and general people. A systematic review and meta-analysis with adequate study power should be conducted separately on these study subjects in the future in order to reduce heterogeneity among them. There are a few or a single study without heterogeneity in some subgroup analyses, indicating that inadequate sample size in this subgroup led to an inaccurate estimation of the outcomes. Another issue in this systematic review and meta-analysis is the existence of publication bias, indicating that small studies affect the overall pooled prevalence of common mental disorders. However, this review is essential to examine the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on mental health disorders and to focus more on the availability and use of mental health services throughout the pandemic. The review’s other strength is that more focus was placed on subgroup analysis as a result of considering the inclusion of different study subject characteristics. For example, study subjects with different characteristics from the subgroup analysis were compared separately in the discussion section.

Conclusion

More than one-third of adults in Ethiopia were affected by common mental disorders during the COVID-19 pandemic. The prevalence of common mental disorders was highest among returned migrants of Ethiopian origin from the Middle East, followed by prisoners, holy water users of Orthodox Christian fellowship, and chronic patients, respectively. A significant percentage of primary caregivers of adult mentally ill patients also had common mental disorders. The magnitude of common mental disorders increased from the early pandemic to 2021 and then decreased in 2022. Mental health services should be given to those vulnerable groups, such as females, employers, those with chronic medical illnesses, poor social supports, substance users, and those with a history of mental illness or a family history of mental illness.
