Generative Modelling via Quantile Regression
Johannes Schmidt-Hieber, Petr Zamolodtchikov

Abstract
We link conditional generative modelling to quantile regression. We propose a suitable loss function and derive minimax convergence rates for the associated risk under smoothness assumptions imposed on the conditional distribution. To establish the lower bound, we show that nonparametric regression can be seen as a sub-problem of the considered generative modelling framework. Finally, we discuss extensions of our work to generate data from multivariate distributions.

Introduction

Generative modelling emerged as its own subfield of artificial intelligence with a series of successes
[
14
,
22
,
9
]
including the recent introduction of diffusion models
[
24
]
and ChatGPT
[
29
]
. These techniques enable computers to produce realistic fake pictures or human-like textual interactions. The potency and novelty of the resulting tools come with an equal amount of complexity, as is suggested by a certain lack of consensus—see, e.g.
[
25
]
, where the authors question the well-foundedness of Wasserstein GANs
[
2
]
. This pinpoints a need for a more theoretical understanding of generative modelling in all its aspects. Informally, generative modelling corresponds to the following task. Given samples from a distribution
\operatorname{P},
the aim is to produce a surrogate distribution
\widehat{\operatorname{P}}
such that samples from
\widehat{\operatorname{P}}
are indistinguishable from the original ones.

Inverse transform sampling provides a simple yet effective tool for sampling from a univariate distribution
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{Y}}
whenever the associated cumulative distribution function (cdf)
\operatorname{F}_{\mathrm{Y}}
admits a left-inverse. If
U
is uniform on the unit interval, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{F}_{\mathrm{Y}}^{-1}(U)\sim\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{Y}},

with
\operatorname{F}_{\mathrm{Y}}^{-1}
the quantile function. Given a covariate
X,
approximating samples from the distribution of
Y|X
will be called
conditional generative modelling
and can be achieved through the conditional quantile function. Estimating the conditional quantile function is commonly referred to as quantile regression. Formally, the aim is to reconstruct

\displaystyle\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}^{-1}(\tau|x)\colon(\tau,x)\mapsto\inf\big\{t\in\mathbb{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|x)\geq\tau\big\}

(1)

from an i.i.d. sample
S_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{(X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},Y_{n})\}
with the same distribution as
(X,Y).
Here,
\tau
is called the quantile level and
\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}
is the cdf of
Y|X,

\displaystyle\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathbb{P}\big\{Y\leq t|X=x\big\}.

While regression focuses on the conditional mean given the covariates, quantile regression, introduced by
[
16
]
in 1978, also captures the effects of the covariates on the entire distribution of the response variable. This results in a finer understanding of the regressors’ impact on the outcome and makes quantile regression an attractive tool in various fields of research such as economics
[
18
]
, clinical research
[
6
]
, or environmental science
[
4
]
.

From a theoretical standpoint, inferential aspects of quantile regression are well understood. Asymptotic normality is obtained in various settings
[
15
,
1
,
8
]
, and, more recently, analyses have been carried out under modern settings such as trend filtering
[
17
]
, high-dimensional sparse models
[
3
]
and ReLU networks regression
[
20
]
. Much of the research has focused on estimating the quantile function at a finite set of fixed levels, which, although sufficient for most applications, is unsuitable for generative purposes—obtaining good quality samples from the conditional distribution
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}
requires reconstruction of the whole function
\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}^{-1}.
Few research has given insights into this aspect.
[
23
]
suggests using deep learning to fit the entire conditional quantile function. They show empirically that such an approach is promising. However, more theory is required for a proper understanding of their results.
[
19
]
and
[
3
]
obtained uniform rates in
(\tau,x)
for quantile regression. In particular,
[
19
]
consider the setting of isotonic regression with smoothness assumptions. They achieve rates comparable to nonparametric regression uniformly over
\tau
in an increasing sequence of compact subsets of
(0,1).
The choice of these subsets mitigates the inconvenient property that the quantile function is typically divergent at zero and one, and a finite sample is insufficient to make a good guess around the boundary values
0
and
1.
Other notable results are obtained in
[
30
]
for approximately linear models and a fixed range of quantile levels.

We study the generative properties of quantile regression estimates. In Section
2
, we introduce a loss function
\mathcal{L}
that averages the error over all quantile levels, circumventing the above mentioned thresholding issue. We then introduce the estimator and our working assumptions in the second part of Section
2
. Upper bounds are provided for a smoothed conditional cdf estimator for quantile regression over a natural extension of Hölder classes in Section
3
. We then show that nonparametric regression under smoothness assumptions can be seen as a sub-problem of quantile regression under the
\mathcal{L}
loss, implying the minimax optimality of the derived rates. The theoretical contributions are closely related to the very recent work
[
21
]
. For a more in-depth discussion see Section
3
. A short discussion of possible extensions is finally provided in Section
4
. All the proofs are deferred to the appendix.

2
Problem setup

We assume that
X\sim\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
is supported on
[0,1]^{d}
and
Y\sim\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{Y}},
where
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{Y}}
is atomless and univariate. Conditional generative modelling aims to transport a simple distribution, say the uniform distribution
\mathcal{U},
towards
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}.
More precisely, the goal is to approximate a measurable function
T
such that the pushforward of
\mathcal{U}
through
T
is
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}.
That is,
T(U|X)\sim\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}},
for
U\sim\mathcal{U}.
Such a function is called a transport map. As mentioned in Section
1
, the conditional quantile function (
1
) is a suitable transport map. This links conditional generative modelling to quantile regression.

2.1
Model and loss

If we know
\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}^{-1},
we can generate
Y
by sampling
U
uniformly on
[0,1]
and interpreting
\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}^{-1}
as a conditional generative model, that is,

\displaystyle Y=\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}^{-1}(U|X).

(2)

For unknown
\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}^{-1},
we first construct an estimator
\smash{\widehat{\operatorname{F}}}^{-1}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}
from the sample
S_{n}=\{(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})\}
and obtain instead

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}=\widehat{Y}(S_{n})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\smash{\widehat{\operatorname{F}}}^{-1}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(U|X).

To measure the fidelity of the generated variable, we introduce the loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\widehat{Y},Y)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\operatorname{E}\big[|\widehat{Y}-Y|\big|S_{n}\big],

(3)

and the associated risk is

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}\big[|\widehat{Y}-Y\big|\big].

Plugging the expressions of
\widehat{Y}
and
Y
into (
3
) shows that
\mathcal{L}
is the
L^{1}(\mathcal{U}\otimes\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})
distance between the conditional quantile functions, which corresponds to an averaged Wasserstein-
1
distance between the conditional distributions. Finally, it is known that, in the univariate setting, the Wasserstein-
1
distance can be expressed as the
L^{1}
distance between the cdfs (see, e.g.,
[
26
, Corollary 2.2]
) so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\widehat{Y},Y)=\operatorname{E}_{\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|X)-\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\widehat{\mathrm{Y}}}|\mathrm{X}}(t|X)\big|\,dt\bigg].

Hence, quantile regression in
\mathcal{L}
-loss is equivalent to the estimation of the conditional cdf. The loss
\mathcal{L}
is finite as long as both variables admit a first absolute moment and no thresholding is required on the range of estimated quantile levels. This makes
\mathcal{L}
a more convenient choice if compared to the sup-norm loss which has issues for quantiles close to
0
and
1
.

2.2
Local polynomial estimators

The proposed estimator in this article is based on local polynomial estimators, which we briefly summarize. Let
\ell
be an integer and recall that
m
is the cardinality of
\{\bm{\alpha}\in\{0,\dots,\ell\}^{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}|\bm{\alpha}|\leq\ell\}.
Let
V(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(x^{\bm{\alpha}}/\bm{\alpha}!)_{0\leq|\bm{\alpha}|\leq\ell},

where the coordinates are ordered lexicographically on
\{0,\dots,\ell\}^{d}.
The local polynomial estimator of order
\ell
with kernel
K(u)=\mathds{1}(u\in\operatorname{B}(0,1))
is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{n}(x)\in\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg[Y_{i}-\theta^{T}V\bigg(\frac{X_{i}-x}{h}\bigg)\bigg]^{2}\mathds{1}(X_{i}\in\operatorname{B}(x,h)).

(4)

In the case
\ell=0,
the above reduces to the Nadaraya-Watson estimator. The generalisation to
\ell>1
allows to exploit higher order smoothness of the unknown signal. More precisely, if the data generating process satisfies
Y_{i}=f(X_{i})
with
f
sufficiently smooth, then, intuitively, solutions to the minimisation problem (
4
) should be close to the Taylor expansion of
f
at
x.
The choice of the kernel
K(u)=\mathds{1}(u\in\operatorname{B}(0,1))
simplifies the analysis. Following the same steps as in
[
28
, Section 1.6]
, we see that whenever the matrix

\displaystyle D_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{nh^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}V\bigg(\frac{X_{i}-x}{h}\bigg)V^{T}\bigg(\frac{X_{i}-x}{h}\bigg)\mathds{1}(X_{i}\in\operatorname{B}(x,h))

is invertible, the solution
\widehat{\theta}_{n}(x)
is unique. Its first coordinate
\widehat{\theta}_{n,1}(x)
provides a linear estimator of the conditional expectation of
Y
given
X=x
and admits the following expression.

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{n,1}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}W_{n,i}(x),

(5)

where the scalars
W_{n,i}(x)
are called
\operatorname{LP}(\ell)
weights and are expressed as

\displaystyle W_{n,i}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{nh^{d}}V^{T}(0)D_{n}(x)^{-1}V\bigg(\frac{X_{i}-x}{h}\bigg)\mathds{1}\big(X_{i}\in\operatorname{B}(x,h)\big).

(6)

The linearity of
\widehat{\theta}_{n,1}
in the
\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
suggests to consider
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}(Y_{i}\leq t)W_{n,i}(x)
as an estimator of
\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t,x).
However, since we consider a global loss function, the estimator must be well-defined on the whole support of
X,
while
D_{n}(x)
will likely fail to be invertible (almost) everywhere. To deal with this we introduce a thresholding step in the construction of the estimator. To motivate the approach, observe that by the law of large numbers,
D_{n}(x)
is close to its expectation. Assuming that
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
admits a density
p
and applying a change of variables leads to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\operatorname{E}[D_{n}(x)]&=\frac{1}{h^{d}}\int_{[x-h,x+h]^{d}}V^{T}\bigg(\frac{t-x}{h}\bigg)V\bigg(\frac{t-x}{h}\bigg)\,d\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}(t)\\
&=\int_{[-1,1]^{d}}V^{T}(t)V(t)p(x+th)\,dt,\end{split}

(7)

If the density
p
is bounded away from zero, then, using the fact that
[x-h,x+h]^{d}\cap[0,1]^{d}
has Lebesgue measure at least
h/2^{d},
we can relate the smallest eigenvalue of
\operatorname{E}[D_{n}(x)]
to the smallest eigenvalue of

\displaystyle D\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}V(v)V^{T}(v)\,dv.

(8)

The smallest eigenvalue of
D
is strictly positive since for
z\neq 0,

v\mapsto\|z^{T}V(v)\|_{2}^{2}
is a non-zero polynomial and thus
z^{T}Dz=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\|z^{T}V(v)\|_{2}^{2}\,dv>0.
Concentration arguments then show that the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(D_{n}(x))
of
D_{n}(x)
is bounded away from zero on a large subset of
[0,1]^{d}.
The threshold
\lambda_{1}^{thr}>0
allows us to control the size of this subset. This motivates the use of the estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{\operatorname{F}}_{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}(t|x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\begin{cases}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}(Y_{i}\leq t)W_{n,i}(x)&\text{if }\lambda_{1}(D_{n}(x))\geq\lambda_{1}^{thr},\text{ and}\\
\mathds{1}(t\geq 0)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(9)

Estimating the conditional cdf requires additional regularity assumptions, which we introduce in the following subsection.

2.3
Smoothness and tail assumptions

We consider a form of smoothness classes described in the following definition.

Definition 1

(Smoothness class)
.

Let
K>0
and
\beta=\ell+\gamma
where
\ell
is an integer and
\gamma\in(0,1].
We define the class
\mathcal{D}(K,\beta)
of conditional cumulative distribution functions
\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}
such that

(i)

for any
t,
the partial derivatives
\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|x)
exist for all
\bm{\alpha}
with
|\bm{\alpha}|\leq\ell
(recalling that derivatives are with respect to
x\mapsto\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|x)
), and

(ii)

for any
|\bm{\alpha}|=\ell
and any
x,y\in[0,1]^{d},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|x)-\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|y)\big|\,dt\leq K\|x-y\|^{\gamma}.

The smoothness property
(ii)
of Definition
1
adds an integral with respect to
t
if compared to the more standard condition
|\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|x)-\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|y)|\leq K\|x-y\|^{\gamma}
encountered in e.g.
[
19
]
or
[
20
]
. Taking the square inside the integral in
(ii)
leads to the assumption made in
[
21
]
. Their analysis is mentioned in more detail in Section
3
.
In addition to the smoothness of the cdf, we impose two conditions on the distribution of
(X,Y).

Assumption 2
.

(i)

There exists
C,\delta>0
such that the distribution of the pair
(X,Y)
satisfies
\operatorname{E}[|Y|^{2+\delta}|X=x]\leq C
for all
x\in[0,1]^{d},
and

(ii)

the marginal distribution
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
admits a density
p
that satisfies
\underline{p}\leq p(x)\leq\overline{p}
for all
x\in[0,1]^{d},
where
0<\underline{p}\leq\overline{p}<\infty.

We denote by

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(K,\beta,C,\delta,\underline{p},\overline{p})

the class of pairs
(X,Y)
that satisfy Assumption
2
with constant
C,\delta,\underline{p},\overline{p},
and whose conditional cdf belongs to
\mathcal{D}(K,\beta).

3
Rates of convergence

In the first subsection, we discuss the upper bounds on the minimax risk induced by the
\mathcal{L}
-loss over the aforementioned smoothness classes. The second subsection shows that our problem is reducible to nonparametric regression in
L^{1}
-norm, leading to a matching lower bound for the minimax risk.

3.1
Upper bound

We are now ready to state our first result, where the estimator
\widehat{\operatorname{F}}_{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}
is used to derive an upper bound for the risk associated with
\mathcal{L}
over the class
\mathcal{P}.

Theorem 3
.

Let
K,C,\delta>0,\ 0<\underline{p}\leq\overline{p}<\infty
and
\beta=\ell+\gamma
with integer
\ell
and
\gamma\in(0,1].
Then, there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\operatorname{F}}_{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}\ \sup_{(X,Y)\in\mathcal{P}(K,\beta,C,\delta,\underline{p},\overline{p})}\operatorname{E}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|X)-\widehat{\operatorname{F}}_{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}(t|X)\big|\,dt\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}n^{-\beta/(2\beta+d)},

where the infimum is taken over all estimators and
C_{0}
depends only on
K,\beta,C,\delta,

\underline{p},\overline{p},D
and
d.
In particular, this upper bound holds for the estimator (
9
) with the choice
\lambda_{1}^{thr}=\underline{p}\lambda_{1}(D)/2,
and
h=n^{-1/(2\beta+d)}.

In the proof of Theorem
3
, condition
(i)
of Assumption
2
is sufficient to control the variance of
\widehat{\operatorname{F}}_{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}.
Smoothness gives control over the bias term. Assumption
2

(ii)
is used throughout the proof and could potentially be weakened. This assumption is nonetheless standard in nonparametric regression. Theorem
3
shows that our setting leads to generative rates of the same order as in nonparametric regression. We show in the next section that those rates are actually minimax optimal.

The analysis in this article shares similarities with several other works. Up to the thresholding, the estimator (
9
) seems to have first appeared in
[
11
]
in the case
\ell\in\{0,1\}
and for general kernel function. Slight variations of the latter have been used for quantile regression, where the estimator usually undergoes a specific inversion scheme to approximate the quantile function. This avoids the commonly known crossing quantile curves phenomenon, where a quantile regressor might not be monotone in
\tau.
See, e.g.,
[
31
]
for
d=\ell=1
or
[
8
]
for
d=1
and
\ell\in\{0,1\}
as well as the references therein. In particular, the results of
[
31
,
8
]
concern asymptotic normality and pointwise mean squared error at fixed level
\tau.
This is incomparable with the global convergence rates obtained in our analysis. Additionally, the crossing quantile curves phenomenon is an issue for inference but does not impact generative tasks as long as the generated variable mimics the original variable well enough. More recently,
[
7
]
uses (
9
) with
\ell=0
to estimate fixed quantiles of heavy-tailed conditional distributions. Departing from the quantile regression setting, global convergence rates for thresholded local polynomial estimators seem to have been achieved for the first time in
[
5
]
for interpolating regressors and the
L^{2}
risk. The very recent and closely related work
[
21
]
analyses (
9
) for
\ell=0
in the context of distributional regression. They consider the risk
\operatorname{E}\big[\int(\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|X)-\widehat{\operatorname{F}}_{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}(t|X))^{2}\,dt\big]
and derive minimax rates with respect to the class
\mathcal{D}(K,\beta)
with
\beta\in(0,1],
and as mentioned below Definition
1
, the smoothness assumption
(ii)
in Definition
1
replaced by

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|x)-\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|y)\big)^{2}\,dt\leq K\|x-y\|^{2\beta}.

We stress that extending their analysis to the case
\beta>1
can be obtained from the arguments used in our upper and lower bounds.

3.2
Lower bound

Let
f_{0}\colon x\mapsto\operatorname{E}[Y|X=x]
denote the true conditional mean. From a generative procedure
\widehat{Y}
one can obtain the estimator
\widehat{f}\colon x\mapsto\operatorname{E}[\widehat{Y}|X=x,S_{n}],
where, as before,
S_{n}
denotes the sample. Applying Jensen’s inequality to the definition of
\mathcal{L}
leads to the reduction

\displaystyle\begin{split}\operatorname{E}[\mathcal{L}(\widehat{Y},Y)]&=\operatorname{E}\big[|\widehat{Y}-Y|\big]\\
&\geq\operatorname{E}\big[\big|\operatorname{E}[\widehat{Y}|X,S_{n}]-\operatorname{E}[Y|X]\big|\big]\\
&=\operatorname{E}\big[\|f_{0}-\widehat{f}\|_{L^{1}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}\big].\end{split}

(10)

Hence, the generative problem in
\mathcal{L}
loss is at least as hard as a regression problem in
L^{1}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})
loss. The exact setting of the corresponding regression problem is, however, unknown as the definition of the class
\mathcal{D}(L,\beta)
does not yield immediate information on the smoothness of
f_{0}
and the behaviour of the residuals
Y-f_{0}(X).
We now relate the two problems and obtain a lower bound that matches the upper bound obtained in Theorem
3
.

The upper bound is achieved over all distributions of
(X,Y)
satisfying Assumption
2
and Definition
1
. For the lower bound, it is sufficient to consider a submodel. Consider nonparametric regression with Gaussian noise
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
that is independent of
X
and assume there exists a function
f_{0}
such that the data generating process satisfies

\displaystyle Y=f_{0}(X)+\varepsilon.

(11)

Then,
f_{0}(x)=\operatorname{E}[Y|X=x]
and thanks to (
10
), we obtain a lower bound for the generative risk if we can derive a lower bound for estimating the underlying regression function
f_{0}.

While the upper bound is taken over the class
\mathcal{D}(K,\beta)
of conditional cumulative distribution functions
\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}
introduced in Definition
1
,
we will now show that for the lower bounds it is enough to assume that
f_{0}
lies in a Hölder class

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\Bigg\{f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\sum_{0\leq|\bm{\alpha}|\leq\ell-1}\big\|\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}f\|_{\infty}+\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\bm{\alpha}|=\ell\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{\big|\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}f(x)-\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}f(y)\big|}{\|x-y\|^{\gamma}}\leq L\Bigg\}.

Compared to the generative setting of Section
2
, regression only requires estimating the conditional mean of
Y.
Model (
11
) corresponds to a particular case of Model (
2
), where the distribution of
Y|X
is known up to its mean. Indeed, in the regression model, the conditional cdf of
Y
is given by

\displaystyle\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|x)=\Phi\bigg(\frac{t-f_{0}(x)}{\sigma}\bigg),

where
\Phi
denotes cdf of
\mathcal{N}(0,1).
Therefore, estimating
f_{0}
can be achieved by considering Model (
2
) over the class

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(L,\beta,\sigma)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\bigg\{\operatorname{F}\colon(x,t)\mapsto\Phi\bigg(\frac{t-f(x)}{\sigma}\bigg)\ \text{ for }f\in\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)\bigg\}.

At this point, taking the supremum over the class
\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)
and the infimum over all estimators in (
10
) shows that

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\operatorname{F}}_{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}\sup_{\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(L,\beta,\sigma)}\operatorname{E}\big[\mathcal{L}(\widehat{Y},Y)\big]

4
Conclusion

We have shown how the problem of conditional generative modelling is reduced to quantile regression for univariate

and have derived the corresponding minimax-optimal rates of convergence under smoothness assumptions. A natural future direction is to consider the case where

is multivariate. In general dimension and under mild conditions, the Knothe-Rosenblatt map generalizes the quantile transform. Nevertheless, it is hard to predict whether the Knothe-Rosenblatt map leads to minimax optimal rates and which assumptions on the generative procedure need to be imposed.

and
\gamma\in(0,1].
By assumption

\displaystyle\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|x)=\Phi\bigg(\frac{t-f(x)}{\sigma}\bigg).

Set
g(x,t)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(t-f(x))/\sigma.
Notice that
p_{\sigma}(t,f(x))=\sigma^{-1}p(g(x,t)),
and for all multi-indexes
\bm{\beta}\in\{0,\dots,\ell\}^{d},
with
|\bm{\beta}|\geq 1,

\partial^{\bm{\beta}}g(x,t)=-\sigma^{-1}\partial^{\bm{\beta}}f(x).
Additionally, any partition
\pi
contains at least one element. Applying Proposition
10
and applying the Hermite polynomials defined in (
25
) shows that

\displaystyle\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|x)

\displaystyle=\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}(\Phi\circ g)(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi}\Phi^{(|\pi|)}(g(x,t))\prod_{B\in\pi}\partial^{B_{*}}g_{\bm{\alpha}}(x_{\bm{\alpha}},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi}\Phi^{(|\pi|)}\bigg(\frac{t-f(x)}{\sigma}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{-1}{\sigma}\bigg)^{|\pi|}\prod_{B\in\pi}\partial^{B_{*}}f_{\bm{\alpha}}(x_{\bm{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=p(g(x,t))\sum_{\pi\in\Pi}\frac{(-1)^{2|\pi|-1}}{\sigma^{|\pi|}}H_{|\pi|-1}\bigg(\frac{t-f(x)}{\sigma}\bigg)\prod_{B\in\pi}\partial^{B_{*}}f_{\bm{\alpha}}(x_{\bm{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=-p_{\sigma}(t,f(x))\underbrace{\sum_{\pi\in\Pi}H_{|\pi|-1}\bigg(\frac{t-f(x)}{\sigma}\bigg)\frac{1}{\sigma^{|\pi|-1}}\prod_{B\in\pi}\partial^{B_{*}}f_{\bm{\alpha}}(x_{\bm{\alpha}})}_{=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}Q(t,x)}.

Using
|a_{1}b_{1}-a_{2}b_{2}|\leq|a_{1}-a_{2}||b_{2}|+|a_{1}||b_{1}-b_{2}|,
we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\big|\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|x)-\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|y)\big|&=\big|p_{\sigma}(t,f(x))Q(t,x)-p_{\sigma}(t,f(y))Q(t,y)\big|\\
&\leq\underbrace{p_{\sigma}(t,f(x))\big|Q(t,x)-Q(t,y)\big|}_{(a)}\\
&\quad+\underbrace{\big|p_{\sigma}(t,f(x))-p_{\sigma}(t,f(y))\big||Q(t,y)|}_{(b)}.\end{split}

(26)

We now bound the terms
(a)
and
(b)
separately.

Bound for
(a).
Since
H_{|\pi|-1}
is a Hermite polynomial, it follows that
t\mapsto Q(t,x)
is a polynomial for any
x.
Denote by
\{q_{k}(x)\}_{k=0}^{\ell}
its coefficients so that

\displaystyle Q(t,x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}q_{k}(x)t^{k}.

Each
q_{k}(x)
is a polynomial in the partial derivatives up to order
\ell
of
f
at
x.
Applying Lemma
11
to each of the
q_{k}(x)
ensures the existence of a constant
M
independent of
x,t
and
f
such that for all
0\leq k\leq\ell,\ |q_{k}(x)-q_{k}(y)|\leq ML\|x-y\|^{\gamma}.
This, together with the triangle inequality and
P(|t|)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=M\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}|t|^{k},
shows that

\displaystyle(a)=p_{\sigma}(t,f(x))\big|Q(t,x)-Q(t,y)\big|\leq p_{\sigma}(t,f(x))L\|x-y\|^{\gamma}P(|t|)

(27)

and concludes the bound for
(a).

Bound for
(b).
We can write

\displaystyle\big|p_{\sigma}(t,f(x))-p_{\sigma}(t,f(y))\big|

\displaystyle=p_{\sigma}(t,0)\frac{|p_{\sigma}(t,f(x))-p_{\sigma}(t,f(y))|}{p_{\sigma}(t,0)}

\displaystyle=p_{\sigma}(t,0)\bigg|\exp\bigg(\frac{2tf(x)-f(x)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad-\exp\bigg(\frac{2tf(y)-f(y)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\bigg)\bigg|.

The function
\mu\mapsto\exp\big((2t\mu-\mu^{2})/(2\sigma^{2})\big)
is differentiable and its derivative is bounded by
(|t|+\mu)/\sigma^{2}.
Its restriction to
[-L,L]
is therefore Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant at most
(|t|+L)/\sigma^{2}.
Consequently

\displaystyle\big|p_{\sigma}(t,f(x))-p_{\sigma}(t,f(y))\big|

\displaystyle\leq p_{\sigma}(t,0)|f(x)-f(y)|\frac{|t|+L}{\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq p_{\sigma}(t,0)L\|x-y\|^{\gamma}\frac{|t|+L}{\sigma^{2}}.

Recall that
Q(t,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}q_{k}(y)t^{k}
and that the coefficients
q_{k}(y)
are polynomials in the derivatives of
f
up to order
\ell
at
y.
Since
f\in\mathcal{H}(L,\beta),
it holds that
|q_{k}(y)|\leq a_{k},
where
a_{k}
is a linear combination of powers of
L
and does not depend on
y.
With
R(|t|)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=((|t|+L)/\sigma^{2})\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}a_{k}|t|^{k},

\displaystyle(b)=\big|p_{\sigma}(t,f(x))-p_{\sigma}(t,f(y))\big||Q_{y}(t)|\leq p_{\sigma}(t,0)L\|x-y\|^{\gamma}R(|t|),

(28)

which concludes the bound for
(b).
Plugging (
27
) and (
28
) into (
26
) results in

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|x)-\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|y)\big|\,dt

\displaystyle\leq L\|x-y\|^{\gamma}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}}R(|t|)p_{\sigma}(t,0)\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathbb{R}}P(|t|)p_{\sigma}(t,f(x))\,dt\bigg].

Both integrals on the right-hand side are linear combinations of absolute moments of
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
and
\mathcal{N}(f(x),\sigma^{2})
with positive coefficients. Absolute moments of
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
of all orders exist and are increasing functions of
|\mu|.
Hence we can bound
\int_{\mathbb{R}}P(|t|)p_{\sigma}(t,f(x))\,dt\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}P(|t|)p_{\sigma}(t,L)\,dt.
The latter is independent of
x
and
y.
Finally, since
x,y
are arbitrary, and by eventually taking the max over
|\bm{\alpha}|=\ell,
we conclude that there exists a constant
K>0
such that

\displaystyle\max_{|\bm{\alpha}|=\ell}\ \sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in[0,1]^{d}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\|x-y\|^{-\gamma}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|x)-\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\operatorname{F}_{\smash{\mathrm{Y}|\mathrm{X}}}(t|y)\big|\,dt\leq LK.

∎

Proof of Proposition
4
.

The claim follows directly by applying Proposition
12
with
L
small enough.

For a self-contained presentation of the material, we include a lower bound for nonparametric regression under smoothness constraints with respect to the
L^{1}
loss. While this is a known result, we could not find exactly this version in the literature.

Proposition 13
.

Work under the assumptions of Model (
11
). If
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
admits a density
p
supported on
[0,1]^{d}
such that
0<\underline{p}\leq p\leq\overline{p}<\infty
for some
\underline{p}
and
\overline{p},
then there exists
c=c(L,\beta,\sigma^{2},\underline{p},\overline{p},d)
such that

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{f}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)}\operatorname{E}\big[\|\widehat{f}-f\|_{L^{1}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}\big]

\displaystyle\geq cn^{-\beta/(2\beta+d)},

where the infimum is taken over all estimators.

Proof.

We wish to lower bound the quantity

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{f}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)}\operatorname{E}\big[\|\widehat{f}-f\|_{L^{1}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}\big],

(29)

where the infimum is taken over all estimators. To do so, we will apply Assouad’s lemma
[
28
, Lemma 2.12]
. Prior to that, we proceed by reducing our problem to the estimation of a vector in Hamming distance. For
p\in[1,\infty),
denote by
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}}
the
L^{p}
norm with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
[-1,1]^{d}.
Consider a kernel
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(i)

\Phi
is supported on
[-1,1]^{d},

(ii)

\Phi\in\mathcal{H}(1,\beta),

(iii)

0<\|\Phi\|_{L^{1}}=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}C_{\Phi}<\infty,

(iv)

\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\leq 1.

To see that such kernel function exists, consider the univariate function
\psi\colon x\mapsto\exp(-1/(1-x^{2}))\mathds{1}(|x|<1).
This function is infinitely smooth on the real line, and all its derivatives vanish outside of
(-1,1).
Now, consider the
d
-variate function
g(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\psi(x_{i}).
For any
\beta=\ell+\gamma,
the quantity
K_{\beta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max\{\|\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}g\|_{\infty}=\|\prod_{i=1}^{d}\psi^{(\alpha_{i})}\|_{\infty}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}|\bm{\alpha}|\leq\ell\}
is well-defined because the all the derivatives of
\psi
are well-defined and bounded in infinite norm. Let
\Phi(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=g(x)/(2\sqrt{d}K_{\beta}).
This function trivially fulfils
(i)
and
(iv),
its derivatives are all at most
1
-Lipschitz up to order
\ell.
Finally, for all
x,y\in[-1,1]^{d}
and all
|\bm{\alpha}|=\ell,
it holds that

\displaystyle\big|\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}g(x)-\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}g(y)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d}}\|x-y\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d}}\|x-y\|\mathds{1}(\|x-y\|\leq 1)+\mathds{1}(\|x-y\|>1)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d}}\|x-y\|^{\gamma}\mathds{1}(\|x-y\|\leq 1)+\|x-y\|^{\gamma}\mathds{1}(\|x-y\|>1)

\displaystyle\leq\|x-y\|^{\gamma}.

This shows that
\Phi
is an example of such kernel function. Let
z_{1},\dots,z_{m}
be the centres of the elements of a packing of
[0,1]^{d}
by hypercubes of side-length
2h
where
0<h<2^{-1/d}
will be specified later. There exists a
c_{P}=c_{P}(d)>0
such that

\displaystyle c_{P}^{-1}h^{-d}\leq m\leq c_{P}h^{-d}.

(30)

Consider
\Omega\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{0,1\}^{m}
and define
M=2^{m}
alternatives as follows: for all
w\in\Omega,
set

\displaystyle f_{w}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\phi_{i},

where

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=Lh^{\beta}\Phi\bigg(\frac{x-z_{i}}{h}\bigg),\qquad 1\leq i\leq m.

Because the
z_{i}
are
2h
-separated, and the
\phi_{i}
are supported on
\operatorname{B}(z_{i},h),
the
\phi_{i}
have disjoint support. Since moreover
\phi_{i}\in\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)
for all
1\leq i\leq m,
it holds that
f_{w}\in\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)
for all
w\in\Omega.
We can now lower bound (
29
):

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{f}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)}\operatorname{E}\big[\|\widehat{f}-f\|_{L^{1}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}\big]

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\widehat{f}}\max_{w\in\Omega}\operatorname{E}\big[\|\widehat{f}-f_{w}\|_{L^{1}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}\big].

(31)

Further, because the supports of the
\phi_{i}
are mutually disjoint, for any estimator
\widehat{f}
and any
w\in\Omega,

\displaystyle\big\|\widehat{f}-f_{w}\big\|_{L^{1}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\underbrace{\int_{\operatorname{B}_{i}}\big|\widehat{f}(x)-w_{i}\phi_{i}(x)\big|\,d\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}(x)}_{=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}T_{i}(w_{i},\widehat{f})},

where
\operatorname{B}_{i}
denotes the ball
\operatorname{B}(z_{i},h).
This leads, just as in Example 2.2 in
[
28
]
, to define
\widehat{w}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\argmin_{t\in\{0,1\}}T_{i}(t,\widehat{f}).
Let
\widehat{w}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\widehat{w}_{1},\dots,\widehat{w}_{M}).
Using the triangle inequality and the definition of
T_{i}
shows that

\displaystyle\big\|f_{\widehat{w}}-f_{w}\big\|_{L^{1}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\int_{\operatorname{B}_{i}}\big|(\widehat{w}_{i}-w_{i})\phi_{i}(x)\big|\,d\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bigg[\int_{\operatorname{B}_{i}}\big|\widehat{f}(x)-w_{i}\phi_{i}(x)\big|\,d\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}(x)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\operatorname{B}_{i}}\big|\widehat{f}(x)-\widehat{w}_{i}\phi_{i}(x)\big|\,d\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}(x)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Big[T_{i}(w_{i},\widehat{f})+T_{i}(\widehat{w}_{i},\widehat{f})\Big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{M}T_{i}(w_{i},\widehat{f})=2\big\|\widehat{f}-f_{w}\big\|_{L^{1}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}.

This last inequality and the mutually disjoint supports of the
\phi_{i}
’s enable us to reduce (
31
) further to

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{f}}\max_{w\in\Omega}\operatorname{E}\big[\|\widehat{f}-f_{w}\|_{L^{1}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}\big]\geq\frac{1}{2}\inf_{\widehat{w}}\max_{w\in\Omega}\|f_{\widehat{w}}-f_{w}\|_{L^{1}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}.

(32)

Next, we observe that

\displaystyle\|f_{\widehat{w}}-f_{w}\|_{L^{1}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}

\displaystyle=\int\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\widehat{w}_{i}-w_{i}|\phi_{i}(x)\,d\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\widehat{w}_{i}-w_{i}|\int\phi_{i}(x)\,d\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{Ham}(\widehat{w},w)\underline{p}\int\phi_{1}(x)\,dx=\operatorname{Ham}(\widehat{w},w)\underline{p}Lh^{\beta+d}\|\Phi\|_{L^{1}},

where
\underline{p}
denotes the assumed lower bound on the density of
\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}}
on
[0,1]^{d}
and
\operatorname{Ham}(\widehat{w},w)
is the Hamming distance between
\widehat{w}
and
w
defined as
\operatorname{Ham}(\widehat{w},w)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathds{1}(\widehat{w}_{i}\neq w_{i}).
Applying this last inequality to the right-hand side of (
32
) shows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\inf_{\widehat{w}}\max_{w\in\Omega}\|f_{\widehat{w}}-f_{w}\|_{L^{1}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}\geq\frac{Lh^{\beta+d}\underline{p}}{2}\inf_{\widehat{w}}\max_{w\in\Omega}\operatorname{E}\big[\operatorname{Ham}(\widehat{w},w)\big]\|\Phi\|_{L^{1}},

We are now ready to apply Assouad’s lemma in the Kullback-Leibler version
[
28
, equation (2.28)]
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{w}}\max_{w\in\Omega}\operatorname{E}\big[\operatorname{Ham}(\widehat{w},w)\big]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{m}{2}\Big(e^{-\alpha}\vee(1-\sqrt{\alpha/2})\Big),

where
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max_{w_{1},w_{2}\in\Omega\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\operatorname{Ham}(w_{1},w_{2})=1}\operatorname{KL}(\operatorname{P}_{w_{1}},\operatorname{P}_{w_{2}})
and
\operatorname{P}_{w}
is the distribution of an i.i.d. sample
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
under model (
11
) when the underlying regression function is
f_{w}.
Using
\operatorname{KL}(\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},\sigma^{2}),\mathcal{N}(\mu_{2},\sigma^{2}))=(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}),

\operatorname{KL}(\operatorname{P}^{\otimes n},\operatorname{Q}^{\otimes n})=n\operatorname{KL}(\operatorname{P},\operatorname{Q}),
and
\|\Phi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\|\Phi\|_{L^{1}}\leq\|\Phi\|_{L^{1}}
lead to

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{1},w_{2}\in\Omega\\
\operatorname{Ham}(w_{1},w_{2})=1\end{subarray}}\frac{n\|f_{w_{1}}-f_{w_{2}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}}{2\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{2\sigma^{2}}\max_{1\leq i,j\leq m}\big\{\|\phi_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}+\|\phi_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}^{2}\big\}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\big\|\phi_{i}\big\|_{L^{2}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}^{2}\leq\frac{nL^{2}h^{2\beta+d}\overline{p}}{\sigma^{2}}\|\Phi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{nL^{2}h^{2\beta+d}\overline{p}}{\sigma^{2}}\|\Phi\|_{L^{1}}.

Pick
h=(\lambda L^{2}\|\Phi\|_{L^{1}}\overline{p}n/\sigma^{2})^{-1/(2\beta+d)}
with
\lambda>0.
Then
\alpha\leq\lambda.
Recall that
c_{P}
is a constant defined in (
30
). Combining all of the previous results leads to

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{f}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)}\operatorname{E}\big[\|\widehat{f}-f\|_{L^{1}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}\big]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{mLh^{\beta+d}\underline{p}\|\Phi\|_{L^{1}}}{4}\Big(e^{-\alpha}\vee(1-\sqrt{\alpha/2})\Big)

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\geq}\frac{Lh^{\beta}\|\Phi\|_{L^{1}}\underline{p}}{4c_{P}}e^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{L\|\Phi\|_{L^{1}}\underline{p}}{4c_{P}}\bigg(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\lambda nL^{2}\|\Phi\|_{L^{1}}^{2}\overline{p}}\bigg)^{\beta/(2\beta+d)}e^{-\lambda}.

Step
(i)
uses
m\geq c_{P}^{-1}h^{-d}
from (
30
). Choosing
\lambda\geq 2^{(2\beta+d)/(\beta d)}\sigma^{2}/(L^{2}\|\Phi\|_{L^{1}}\overline{p})
guarantees that
h\leq 2^{-1/d}.
Finally, for all
n\geq 1,
it holds that there exists
c=c(L,\beta,\sigma^{2},\underline{p},\overline{p},d)>0
such that

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{f}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}(L,\beta)}\operatorname{E}\big[\|\widehat{f}-f\|_{L^{1}(\operatorname{P}_{\mathrm{X}})}\big]

\displaystyle\geq cn^{-\beta/(2\beta+d)}.

∎
