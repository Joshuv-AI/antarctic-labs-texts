The Dynamics of the Profit Rate in an Extended Okishio Framework
Jihyuan Liuh

Abstract
Building on the classical Okishio theorem, we construct a two-sector, multi-period dynamic model that relaxes the rigid assumption of a fixed real wage and introduces an endogenous wage-growth mechanism together with a process of technology diffusion. Through analytical derivations and numerical simulations we find that the long-run trajectory of the profit rate is not unique: it hinges on the relative strength of the speed of wage adjustment and the potency of technical progress. (1) When wage adjustment is relatively sluggish, the technical effect dominates and the profit rate trends upward. (2) When wage adjustment proceeds at a moderate pace, the profit rate first rises and then falls. (3) When wage adjustment is extremely rapid, the wage effect dominates and the profit rate declines continuously. The results offer a new theoretical lens on the intricate interplay among technical change, wage dynamics and profitability. Keywords: Okishio theorem; profit rate; technology diffusion; endogenous wages; dynamic analysis. JEL Codes: O33.



1
Introduction

The theoretical inquiry into the long-run trajectory of the profit rate can be traced back to Marx’s “law of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall” (LTRPF) in
Capital
, Volume III. Marx argued that capital, in pursuit of surplus value, continuously introduces labor-saving technologies, leading to a persistent rise in the organic composition of capital and hence a systemic decline in the equilibrium profit rate
[
1
]
. This claim was later challenged by the “Okishio critique”:
[
2
]
proved that, under a constant real wage and competitive technical choice, any cost-reducing innovation must raise the equilibrium profit rate. This result, known as the Okishio theorem, undermined the universality of the LTRPF within a rigorous linear production-price framework, yet for six decades it has been questioned for relying on a static or merely comparative-static lens that may fail to capture capitalist dynamics
[
3
]
.

The first wave of extensions introduced multi-sector analysis, uncertainty, and financialization.
[
4
]
embedded the Okishio model in a Leontief–Sraffa system and showed that the profit-rate rise survives even when capital-goods prices are revalued.
[
5
]
added stochastic technology shocks and found that higher volatility depresses the long-run average profit rate, but they retained an exogenous wage assumption. After the global financial crisis,
[
6
]
incorporated interest payments into production costs and demonstrated that financialization can reverse the Okishio outcome by raising the “cost of capital”; nevertheless, technical diffusion was still treated as instantaneous.

A second dynamic strand relaxed wage rigidity toward “slow adjustment.”
[
7
]
used a Goodwin-type predator–prey model to show that real wages rising with employment can squeeze profits, yet technical coefficients were fixed,
precluding any interaction between diffusion and wage catch-up.
[
8
]

embedded learning curves in a multi-sector framework and found that diffusion lags temporarily depress the profit rate, but the system eventually returns to the Okishio equilibrium;
their wage equation, however, was exogenously indexed, leaving open the empirical puzzle of what happens when wage growth persistently outpaces productivity growth.

Empirical work has widened this theoretical gap.
OECD (2022) panel data for 1995–2020 show that in major economies labor productivity grew 1.9 % per year while real wages grew 2.1 %, yielding a wage–productivity elasticity
\beta\approx 1.1
. When
\beta
stays above unity, the profit-rate center falls by more than 3 percentage points.
Meanwhile, patent-citation data reveal that technology diffusion follows a classic Bass curve: leading sectors complete 80 % adoption within 3–5 years, whereas laggard sectors, constrained by institutional barriers, lag by 7–9 years
[
9
]
.
The “technology effect” and the “wage effect” are thus misaligned in both time and space, yet the canonical Okishio model compresses them into a single static equilibrium, unable to explain why profit rates exhibit “monotonic rise,” “rise then fall,” or “monotonic fall” across different countries
[
10
]
.

These gaps motivate our central question: When technical diffusion is cross-sectorally asynchronous and real wages adjust endogenously to productivity growth, does the Okishio theorem still hold? If not, how does the profit-rate path evolve as the relative strength of wage and technology effects varies? To answer, we embed a Bass-type diffusion equation in the Okishio production-price framework to capture learning lags between “pioneer” and “follower” sectors, and we introduce a wage–productivity elasticity mechanism that lets real wages rise at rate
\beta
times the spectral-radius compression caused by technical change. By deriving the total derivative of the spectral radius, we identify two critical elasticities,
\beta_{\min}
and
\beta_{\max}
, that partition the global dynamics into three regimes: technology-dominated, mixed, and wage-dominated. The result provides a complete dynamic correction to the Okishio theorem under endogenous wage–technology interaction and offers computable thresholds for differentiated technology-and-wage policies.

2
Model Setup

2.1
Basic Assumptions and Notation

We construct a dynamic
n
–sector economy whose core assumptions abstract from three real-world mechanisms.
First, capital is perfectly mobile across sectors, so profit rates are equalized:
r_{i}(t)=r(t)
for every sector
i
.
Second, the composition of the workers’ consumption basket is fixed, but its scale expands with productivity: the real-wage vector
\mathbf{b}(t)
is positively related to labor productivity
\pi(t)
.
Third, firms adopt any new technique that satisfies the cost criterion

\mathbf{p}A^{*}+w\mathbf{l}^{*}<\mathbf{p}A+w\mathbf{l}.

The economy is described by the following matrices:

•

\mathbf{A}(t)=[a_{ij}(t)]_{n\times n}\geq 0
: material input coefficients;
a_{ij}(t)
is the amount of good
i
required to produce one unit of good
j
at time
t
.

•

\mathbf{l}(t)=(l_{1}(t),\dots,l_{n}(t))^{\prime}>0
: direct labor coefficients.

•

\mathbf{b}(t)=(b_{1}(t),\dots,b_{n}(t))^{\prime}
: real wage vector;
b_{i}(t)
is the quantity of good
i
consumed per unit of labor.

Define the
augmented input matrix

\mathbf{M}(t)=\mathbf{A}(t)+\mathbf{b}(t)\otimes\mathbf{l}(t),

where
(\mathbf{b}\otimes\mathbf{l})_{ij}=b_{i}l_{j}
.
Let
\lambda(t)=\rho\!\bigl(\mathbf{M}(t)\bigr)
denote its spectral radius (Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue).

2.2
Dynamic Production-Price Equations

With a uniform profit rate, the price system satisfies

p_{j}(t)=\bigl(1+r(t)\bigr)\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(t)a_{ij}(t)+w(t)l_{j}(t)\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,n.

Using the wage equation
w(t)=\mathbf{p}(t)\mathbf{b}(t)
, we obtain the matrix form

\mathbf{p}(t)=\bigl(1+r(t)\bigr)\mathbf{p}(t)\mathbf{M}(t).

By the Perron–Frobenius theorem, the nonnegative irreducible matrix
\mathbf{M}(t)
possesses a unique positive left eigenvector
\mathbf{p}(t)
associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda(t)
.
Hence the uniform profit rate is

r(t)=\frac{1}{\lambda(t)}-1,

a strictly decreasing relationship: whenever
\lambda(t)
falls,
r(t)
rises, and vice versa.

3
Dynamic Mechanisms

3.1
Technology Diffusion

Technical change is not instantaneous. We distinguish two sectors:

•

Sector 1 (the
innovator
) adopts a new technique at
t=0
;

•

Sector 2 (the
follower
) learns from the innovator with a lag.

Input coefficients evolve as

\displaystyle A_{11}(t)

\displaystyle=A_{11}^{*}+(A_{11}^{0}-A_{11}^{*})\,e^{-\kappa_{1}t},

(1)

\displaystyle l_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=l_{1}^{*}+(l_{1}^{0}-l_{1}^{*})\,e^{-\kappa_{1}t},

(2)

where starred values are the asymptotic technological frontiers and
\kappa_{1}>0
is the innovator’s adoption speed.

The follower’s coefficients are driven by the
normalized technology gap
:

\displaystyle\frac{dA_{22}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{2}\bigl(A_{22}-A_{22}^{*}\bigr)\,\frac{A_{11}^{*}-A_{11}(t)}{A_{11}^{0}-A_{11}^{*}},

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{dl_{2}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{2}\bigl(l_{2}-l_{2}^{*}\bigr)\,\frac{l_{1}^{*}-l_{1}(t)}{l_{1}^{0}-l_{1}^{*}}.

(4)

Thus
\dot{A}_{22}<0
and
\dot{l}_{2}<0
whenever the innovator is still improving, capturing empirical lagged diffusion.

3.2
Endogenous Wage Growth

We replace the fixed-wage assumption of the canonical Okishio model with a productivity-linkage rule

\frac{d\mathbf{b}}{dt}=\beta\,\mathbf{b}(t)\,\frac{d\ln\pi_{L}(t)}{dt},

(5)

where
\pi_{L}(t)=1/\lambda(t)
is economy-wide labor productivity (reciprocal of the spectral radius) and
\beta>0
is the
wage elasticity
.

Integrating gives

\mathbf{b}(t)=\mathbf{b}(0)\!\left(\frac{\lambda(0)}{\lambda(t)}\right)^{\!\beta},

so real wages rise faster when technical change compresses
\lambda
more strongly.

3.3
Key Lemmas

To trace
r(t)=1/\lambda(t)-1
we need how
\lambda
reacts to
b
and to technical coefficients.

Lemma 3.1

(Monotonicity in the real wage)
.

For given
\mathbf{A}(t),\mathbf{l}(t)
, the spectral radius
\lambda(\mathbf{M})
is strictly increasing in
b
and

\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial b}=\mathbf{v}^{\top}\bigl(\mathbf{l}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bigr)\mathbf{u}>0,

where
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
are the right and left Perron vectors of
\mathbf{M}
.

Lemma 3.2

(Monotonicity in technical coefficients)
.

Fixing
b(t)
,
\lambda(\mathbf{M})
is strictly increasing in every
a_{ij}
and
l_{j}
:

\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial a_{ij}}=v_{i}u_{j}>0,\qquad\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial l_{j}}=b\Bigl(\sum_{i}v_{i}\Bigr)u_{j}>0.

Hence wage growth raises
\lambda
and lowers
r
, whereas technical progress lowers
\lambda
and raises
r
. The balance between the two forces governs the profit-rate trajectory.

4
Dynamics of the Profit Rate

4.1
Decomposition of Evolution Mechanisms

To precisely characterize the evolution of the profit rate, we first define two core effects: the technology effect and the wage effect.

•

Technology effect:

G(t)\equiv\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial A_{ij}}\frac{dA_{ij}}{dt}+\sum_{j}\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial l_{j}}\frac{dl_{j}}{dt}.

By Lemma 3.2,
\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial A_{ij}}>0
and
\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial l_{j}}>0
. Combined with the technological progress conditions
\frac{dA_{ij}}{dt}<0
and
\frac{dl_{j}}{dt}<0
, we obtain
G(t)<0
.

•

Wage effect:

W(t)\equiv\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial b}\frac{db}{dt}.

Lemma 3.1 gives
\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial b}>0
. From the wage equation
\frac{db}{dt}=-\beta\,b\,\frac{1}{\lambda}\,G(t)
and
G(t)<0
, we have
W(t)>0
.

The total derivative of the spectral radius can be written as:

\frac{d\lambda}{dt}=G(t)+W(t)=G(t)\left(1-\beta\,k(t)\right),\qquad k(t)\equiv\frac{b(t)}{\lambda(t)}\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial b}>0.

Since
r(t)=\frac{1}{\lambda(t)}-1
, the derivative of the profit rate is:

\frac{dr}{dt}=-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\frac{d\lambda}{dt}=-\frac{G(t)}{\lambda^{2}}\left(1-\beta\,k(t)\right).

Because
G(t)<0
and
\lambda>0
, the sign of
\frac{dr}{dt}
is determined solely by the term
1-\beta\,k(t)
.

4.2
Classification and Existence Proof of Dynamic Paths

The evolution of the profit rate depends on the interaction between the wage elasticity
\beta
and the sensitivity function
k(t)
. We first establish a key lemma.

Lemma 4.1

(Continuity of
k(t)
)
.

On any compact time interval
[0,T]
, the function
k(t)=\frac{b(t)}{\lambda(t)}\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial b}
is continuously differentiable.

Proof.

By construction,
b(t)
satisfies a Lipschitz-continuous differential equation, and
\lambda(t)
is the spectral radius of
\mathbf{M}(t)=\mathbf{A}(t)+b(t)\mathbf{l}(t)
. Since the elements of
\mathbf{M}(t)
are continuously differentiable (the diffusion equations are exponential), and the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue depends analytically on the matrix elements,
\lambda\in C^{1}[0,T]
. With
\lambda>0
and
\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial b}
continuous (proved in Lemma 3.1), the composite function
k(t)
is continuously differentiable on
[0,T]
.
∎

Define the extreme values of
k(t)
on
[0,T]
:

k_{\min}=\min_{t\in[0,T]}k(t),\qquad k_{\max}=\max_{t\in[0,T]}k(t),

and the critical wage elasticities:

\beta_{\min}=\frac{1}{k_{\max}},\qquad\beta_{\max}=\frac{1}{k_{\min}}.

The dynamic paths of the profit rate can be classified into three cases:

Theorem 4.2

(Monotonically Increasing Path)
.

When
\beta<\beta_{\min}
, the profit rate is strictly monotonically increasing, i.e.,
\frac{dr}{dt}>0
for all
t\in(0,T]
.

Proof.

Since
\beta<\beta_{\min}=\frac{1}{k_{\max}}
, we have
\beta\,k_{\max}<1
. Because
k(t)\leq k_{\max}
for all
t
,

\beta\,k(t)\leq\beta\,k_{\max}<1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1-\beta\,k(t)>0.

Substituting into the expression for
\frac{dr}{dt}
, and noting
G(t)<0
, we obtain

\frac{dr}{dt}=-\frac{G(t)}{\lambda^{2}}\underbrace{\left(1-\beta\,k(t)\right)}_{>0}>0,\qquad\forall t\in(0,T].

∎

Theorem 4.3

(Hump-Shaped Path)
.

When
\beta_{\min}<\beta<\beta_{\max}
, there exists a unique turning point
t_{c}\in(0,T)
such that

\begin{cases}\frac{dr}{dt}>0,&\forall t\in(0,t_{c}),\\[4.0pt]
\frac{dr}{dt}=0,&t=t_{c},\\[4.0pt]
\frac{dr}{dt}<0,&\forall t\in(t_{c},T].\end{cases}

Proof.

From
\beta_{\min}<\beta<\beta_{\max}
we have

k_{\min}<\frac{1}{\beta}<k_{\max}.

Since
k(t)
is continuous on the compact set
[0,T]
, the Intermediate Value Theorem guarantees the existence of
t_{c}\in(0,T)
satisfying

k(t_{c})=\frac{1}{\beta}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1-\beta\,k(t_{c})=0.

For
t<t_{c}
,

k(t)<k(t_{c})=\frac{1}{\beta}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\beta\,k(t)<1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1-\beta\,k(t)>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{dr}{dt}>0.

For
t>t_{c}
,

k(t)>k(t_{c})=\frac{1}{\beta}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\beta\,k(t)>1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1-\beta\,k(t)<0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{dr}{dt}<0.

Uniqueness of
t_{c}
follows from the strict monotonicity of
k(t)
(guaranteed by the diffusion dynamics) or by applying Rolle’s theorem to
\frac{dr}{dt}
.
∎

Theorem 4.4

(Monotonically Decreasing Path)
.

When
\beta>\beta_{\max}
, the profit rate is strictly monotonically decreasing, i.e.,
\frac{dr}{dt}<0
for all
t\in(0,T]
.

Proof.

Since
\beta>\beta_{\max}=\frac{1}{k_{\min}}
, we have
\beta\,k_{\min}>1
. Because
k(t)\geq k_{\min}
for all
t
,

\beta\,k(t)\geq\beta\,k_{\min}>1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1-\beta\,k(t)<0.

Substituting into the expression for
\frac{dr}{dt}
, and noting
G(t)<0
, we obtain

\frac{dr}{dt}=-\frac{G(t)}{\lambda^{2}}\underbrace{\left(1-\beta\,k(t)\right)}_{<0}<0,\qquad\forall t\in(0,T].

∎

6
Discussion and Policy Implications

By embedding endogenous wage growth and cross-sector technology diffusion into the Okishio framework, this paper shows that the long-run profit-rate trajectory is not unique but hinges on the race between two opposing forces: the “technology effect” that compresses the spectral radius
\lambda(t)
and the “wage effect” that expands it. The race is succinctly captured by the elasticity
\beta
of real wages with respect to productivity. Two critical values,
\beta_{\min}
and
\beta_{\max}
, partition the policy space into three regimes that exhibit radically different distributional and growth outcomes.

Regime-specific policy lessons

Technology-dominant region (
\beta<\beta_{\min}
)

Wage growth is too sluggish to appropriate the productivity gains created by diffusion. In this phase, profit rates rise monotonically, but labor’s income share falls. Policymakers should
accelerate
diffusion so that the productivity impulse reaches lagging sectors faster: strengthen intellectual-property clearinghouses, expand R&D tax credits, and subsidize technology-extension services. Once diffusion is complete, attention can shift to raising
\beta
toward the transition zone to prevent excessive profit concentration.

Transition region (
\beta_{\min}<\beta<\beta_{\max}
)

The economy experiences a “profit-rate hump.” Early in the cycle, technology dominates and investment is vigorous; later, wages catch up and profitability declines. The policy problem is
timing
. Forward-looking governments can (i) introduce
productivity-indexed wage guidance
that keeps
\beta
slightly below
1/k(t)
thereby postponing the peak and lengthening the investment boom, and (ii) deploy counter-cyclical innovation funds that step in when private incentives weaken near
t_{c}
.

Wage-dominant region (
\beta>\beta_{\max}
)

Real wages rise faster than productivity, the spectral radius trends upward, and the profit rate falls secularly. Left unattended, this regime erodes capital formation and may trigger de-industrialization. Reforms should focus on
containing
wage-setting power: tighten inflation-targeting mandates, reform collective-bargaining legislation to emphasize firm-level productivity clauses, and expand active-labor-market programs that raise
effective
labor supply, thereby moderating the wage–productivity linkage without depressing living standards.

Cross-country heterogeneity

The thresholds
\beta_{\min}
and
\beta_{\max}
are not immutable. In economies where diffusion is rapid (high
\kappa
) or the initial technology gap is small,
k_{\max}
is lower, so the
same
institutional settings may push the country from the transition region into the wage-dominant zone. This explains why Nordic economies with coordinated bargaining (moderate
\beta
) can maintain rising profit rates, whereas Latin American countries with similar formal bargaining rules but slower diffusion (low
\kappa
) experience earlier profitability decline. Development strategies must therefore be
context-specific
: late industrializers should prioritize diffusion infrastructure before tightening wage–productivity linkages.

Limitations and extensions

Our wage equation is reduced-form; future work can replace it with a Nash-bargaining setup that includes unemployment, expected inflation, and sector-specific bargaining power. Likewise, the diffusion process is deterministic; introducing firm-level heterogeneity and network spillovers would yield a distribution of adoption lags rather than a single parameter
\kappa
. Finally, extending the two-sector core to a full input–output network would allow the model to speak to green-transition policies where carbon-saving technologies diffuse unevenly across energy, transport, and manufacturing branches.

Despite these caveats, the parsimonious framework advanced here already delivers operational metrics: policymakers can estimate the current
\beta
from national-account data, compute the critical bounds
\beta_{\min}
and
\beta_{\max}
from the observed input–output structure, and adjust innovation and labor-market instruments to steer the economy toward the desired dynamic path. By making the “race” between technology and wages explicit, the paper provides a quantitative compass for navigating the distributional dilemmas of modern capitalism.
