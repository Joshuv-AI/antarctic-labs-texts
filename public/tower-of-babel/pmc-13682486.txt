A case report on the pathogenesis of BAP1 inactivating melanocytomas (BIMTs)
Authors: Alexander Michael Diaz, Lawrence Adam Schiffman, Evan Miller, Stephen M. Schleicher
Published: 2026

Introduction

BRCA1-associated protein 1 (BAP1) inactivating melanocytic tumors (BIMTs) are a distinct class of melanocytic neoplasms that can be both clinically and histopathologically difficult to diagnose. They typically present as well-circumscribed, flesh-colored, dome-shaped papules with no atypical features, often mistaken for a common intradermal nevus.1 Histologically, they are composed of large epithelioid melanocytes with eosinophilic cytoplasm and nuclear atypia, features that overlap with melanoma.2 We report an incidental BIMT identified during a routine total body skin examination and discuss its histopathologic features, the role of BAP1 immunohistochemistry in diagnosis, and the implications for germline testing and surveillance in BAP1 tumor predisposition syndrome.

Case presentation

We present a case involving a 50-year-old woman who visited the dermatologist’s office for a full body skin examination. The patient denied previous history of precancerous lesions, skin cancers, or moles of recent onset. She had no family history of skin cancer. The patient had a Type III skin type. Aside from several benign skin findings, a telangiectatic, well-circumscribed, flesh-colored, dome-shaped papule on the skin overlying the patient’s upper right trapezius was noted (Fig 1). On dermoscopy, the lesion demonstrated telangiectasias without a definitive benign pattern. Since these findings were indeterminate, the dermatologist repeatedly returned to the lesion before deciding to biopsy. Histopathologic findings

Histology revealed an atypical melanocytic neoplasm primarily within the dermal layer devoid of a significant intraepidermal component (Fig 2). The dermis was comprised of sheets of epithelioid, non-pigmented cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and pronounced nuclear pleomorphism (Fig 3). Cellular morphology varied with notable differences observed in both nuclear size and cytoplasmic proportions. Despite the degree of cytologic atypia, mitotic figures were absent. A lymphocytic infiltrate was present. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positive staining for SOX10 (Fig 4) and S100, while P63, PRAME, and CD68 were negative. Select cells exhibited positivity for P16 and lacked BAP1 nuclear staining. The final diagnosis was a BAP1-inactivated melanocytoma with concern for potential malignant transformation given the degree of nuclear atypia and pleomorphism observed. Discussion

This case highlights the value of a comprehensive skin examination. A flesh-colored papule lacking clinical signs of malignancy was biopsied and diagnosed as a BIMT, with histopathology raising concern that prompted further evaluation. BIMTs are worth recognizing because they can be mistaken for either benign nevi or melanoma, and they can signal an underlying tumor predisposition syndrome.

BAP1 (BRCA1-associated protein 1) is a tumor suppressor gene located on chromosome 3p21 that helps maintain genomic stability.2 It encodes a deubiquitinating enzyme (DUB) that regulates chromatin remodeling, DNA damage response, and cell cycle progression, through its interaction with p16 (CDKN2A).2,3 Germline loss of function gives rise to BAP1 tumor predisposition syndrome (BAP1-TPDS), an autosomal dominant condition that increases the risk of uveal melanoma, malignant mesothelioma, renal cell carcinoma, and certain cutaneous epithelioid-melanocytic neoplasms.1,2,4 BAP1 loss is rare in conventional cutaneous melanoma but defines a specific class of melanocytic tumors known as BAP1-inactivated melanocytic tumors (BIMTs).1

The penetrance of BAP1-TPDS is high.4 In an analysis of 181 families with BAP1 germline variants, approximately 85% of null variant carriers developed at least one BAP1-associated tumor during their lifetime, with risk approaching 100% with increasing age.4 Site-specific lifetime risks are approximately 36% for uveal melanoma, 24% for mesothelioma, 23% for cutaneous melanoma, and 6% for renal cell carcinoma.5

The selectivity of BAP1 loss for certain tumor types remains incompletely understood. Although BAP1 is present in every cell type, experimental data suggest that its loss is tolerated in mesothelial cells and melanocytes but induces cell death in others, which may explain why tumorigenesis occurs most often in these tissues.6 Environmental factors such as asbestos (mesothelioma) and ultraviolet radiation (cutaneous melanoma) also contribute in BAP1 carriers.6

BIMTs arise from biallelic inactivation of the BAP1 gene followed by a driver mutation activating the MAP kinase pathway, most commonly BRAF V600E.1 This molecular profile distinguishes BIMTs from conventional cutaneous melanoma and uveal melanomas. Conventional melanomas are typically driven by BRAF or NRAS mutations without BAP1 loss, whereas uveal melanomas are driven by GNAQ or GNA11 mutations.7 BIMTs are characterized as biphenotypic melanocytic proliferations, displaying features that overlap with both benign melanocytic nevi and melanomas, making them difficult to diagnose.2 Histologically BIMTs may exhibit spitzoid morphology with large epithelioid melanocytes and varying degrees of cytologic atypia.2 For lesions with atypical histopathologic features, the term “melanocytoma” has been applied. Despite this atypia, mitotic figures are typically low or absent, a feature that helps distinguish BIMTs from melanoma.2,8

BAP1 immunohistochemistry is not routinely indicated for all melanocytic workups. It is most useful in dermal epithelioid-melanocytic lesions where BIMT is on the differential, with loss of nuclear BAP1 expression supporting the diagnosis.3 Pairing BAP1 with PRAME further improves the diagnosis, as PRAME staining is typically negative in BIMTs,8 but stains positively in conventional melanomas.9 In this case, BAP1 loss with negative PRAME supported the diagnosis of BIMT over melanoma.

BIMTs can progress to melanoma in both BAP1-TPDS carriers and sporadic cases.10 Germline BAP1 testing is recommended for patients with 2 or more cutaneous BIMTs, a personal or family history of BAP1-associated tumors, or uveal melanoma diagnosed before age 30.5 Besides family planning, identification of a germline BAP1 variant guides surveillance for early cancer detection. Current recommendations include annual skin examinations starting at age 18, annual ophthalmic examinations starting at age 16, and annual renal MRI screening starting at age 30.5 Routine mesothelioma surveillance is not currently recommended.5 Counseling on avoidance of known carcinogenic exposures, such as asbestos, is also recommended.

Given the diagnosis of a BAP1-inactivated melanocytoma, the patient was referred for genetic counseling. Although she does not meet established criteria for germline BAP1 testing (a solitary BIMT without a personal or family history of BAP1-associated tumors), counseling allows for individualized discussion of testing and surveillance. While BIMTs generally follow a benign course, malignant transformation remains a concern, warranting close clinical follow-up.
