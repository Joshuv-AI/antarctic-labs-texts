Why Self-Training Helps and Hurts: Denoising vs. Signal Forgetting
Mingqi Wu, Archer Y. Yang, Qiang Sun

Abstract
Iterative self-training (self-distillation) repeatedly refits a model on pseudo-labels generated by its own predictions. We study this procedure in overparameterized linear regression: an initial estimator is trained on noisy labels, and each subsequent iterate is trained on fresh covariates with noiseless pseudo-labels from the previous model. In the high-dimensional regime, we derive deterministic-equivalent recursions for the prediction risk and effective noise across iterations, and prove that the empirical quantities concentrate sharply around these limits. The recursion separates two competing forces: a systematic component that grows with iteration due to progressive signal forgetting , and a stochastic component that decays due to denoising via repeated data-dependent projections. Their interaction yields a U U -shaped test-risk curve and an optimal early-stopping time. In spiked covariance models, iteration further acts as an iteration-dependent spectral filter that preserves strong eigendirections while suppressing weaker ones, inducing an implicit form of soft feature selection distinct from ridge regression. Finally, we propose an iterated generalized cross-validation criterion and prove its uniform consistency for estimating the risk along the self-training trajectory, enabling fully data-driven selection of the stopping time and regularization. Experiments on synthetic covariances validate the theory and illustrate the predicted denoising–forgetting trade-off.

Introduction

Iterative self-training, repeatedly refining a model using its own predictions, is a powerful yet paradoxical paradigm in modern machine learning. On the one hand, it has a long record of success in semi-supervised learning, where it leverages large unlabeled datasets to achieve state-of-the-art performance
(
26
;
35
;
33
;
28
)
. On the other hand, the same recursion can fail catastrophically. Recent work identifies a major failure mode,
model collapse
, in which training on synthetic, self-generated outputs progressively degrades performance and can lead the model to forget the original data distribution
(
27
;
9
)
. Mitigating collapse, for example by mixing real data into each iteration, is an active research direction
(
12
;
13
)
. These observations expose a fundamental tension that motivates our central question:

When does iteration improve generalization,

and when does it hurt?

A useful intuition is to view each self-training step as a lossy teacher–student transfer. The student is trained only on the teacher’s predictions, so each iteration necessarily discards information: some discarded components correspond to noise, but others contain signal. In high dimensions, this transfer resembles repeatedly compressing (or projecting) the current estimate through a fresh, data-dependent subspace. Unstable, noise-like components are removed quickly, whereas strong signal directions that are consistently supported by the data geometry persist for several iterations. When the signal is strong, early iterations remove more noise than signal and can improve generalization; once most noise has been removed, further iterations begin to erode signal as well, increasing systematic error. This trade-off yields the characteristic
U
-shaped risk curve and motivates early stopping.

These dynamics are closely connected to several core concepts in learning theory. Iterative self-training can be viewed as a form of knowledge distillation
(
18
)
, where the estimator at step
t
serves as the teacher for step
t{+}1
. It also instantiates weak-to-strong generalization
(
3
)
, in which a stronger model is trained using labels produced by a weaker supervisor. Despite strong empirical performance, a precise statistical characterization of the iterative mechanism remains open.

In this paper, we answer this question in an analytically tractable setting, overparameterized linear regression, using tools from high-dimensional asymptotic theory
(
15
;
14
;
7
;
32
)
. We focus on the proportional asymptotic regime where
p>n
. Figure
1
previews the central trade-off: early iterations denoise by attenuating stochastic error inherited from the initial noisy fit, but subsequent updates rely only on pseudo-labels and therefore gradually discard signal (
signal forgetting
), which accumulates as systematic error. The resulting competition between denoising and signal forgetting produces a characteristic
U
-shaped test-error curve as a function of the number of iterations and implies an optimal stopping time.

Our main contributions are fourfold. (i) We derive a rigorous decomposition of the generalization error for iterative self-training in overparameterized linear regression, showing that the error splits into two parts: a systematic error that increases with iteration (
signal forgetting
) and a stochastic error that decreases (
denoising
). (ii) We prove that the competition between these two parts yields a characteristic
U
-shaped risk curve and implies an optimal number of iterations, explaining why excessive self-training is harmful without proposing a specific tuning procedure. (iii) We identify the mechanism driving these dynamics as an implicit regularization effect distinct from ridge regression: self-training preserves strong signal directions aligned with the feature covariance while suppressing stochastic components that lie in large, randomly oriented null spaces of the design matrices. This perspective explains both the denoising behavior and the student–teacher performance gap. (iv) We propose an iterated generalized cross-validation (GCV) procedure to estimate the optimal stopping time.

2
Preliminaries

This section introduces the setup for our analysis. We first define the high-dimensional linear model and the prediction risk. We then describe the
ridge
and
ridgeless
estimators, which underpin the iterative self-training procedure studied in this paper. Finally, we state our assumptions on the data-generating process.

2.1
The linear model and prediction risk

Consider a high-dimensional linear model with training data

\mathcal{D}_{0}:=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. The observations are i.i.d. samples generated by:

\displaystyle y_{i}=x_{i}^{\top}\beta+\varepsilon_{i},~~i=1,\ldots,n,

(1)

\displaystyle\text{where }(x_{i},\varepsilon_{i})\sim P_{x}\times P_{\varepsilon}.

Here
P_{x}
is a distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
P_{\varepsilon}
is a distribution on
\mathbb{R}
with mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. In matrix notation, we write

Y_{0}=X_{0}\beta+E_{0},

with
Y_{0}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
X_{0}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, and
E_{0}=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

To evaluate an estimator
\widehat{\beta}
, we use the (out-of-sample) prediction risk, defined as the expected squared error on an independent test point
x_{\textrm{new}}\sim P_{x}
:

\!\!\!\!\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\beta};\beta)\!=\!\mathbbm{E}\!\left[\left(x_{\textrm{new}}^{\top}\widehat{\beta}-x_{\textrm{new}}^{\top}\beta\right)^{2}\right]\!=\!\left\|\Sigma^{1/2}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)\right\|_{2}^{2},

(2)

where
\Sigma=\mathbbm{E}[x_{\textrm{new}}x_{\textrm{new}}^{\top}]

is the population feature covariance. The expectation is over
x_{\textrm{new}}
conditional on the training data.

2.2
The iterative self-training algorithm

This section introduces the iterative self-training procedure and the associated estimators. We begin with the ridge and ridgeless estimators
(
15
)
.

Standard ridge and ridgeless estimators.

We focus on two standard estimators. The ridge estimator is the solution to

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}_{0}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{b\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\{\frac{1}{2n}\|Y_{0}-X_{0}b\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|b\|_{2}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=(X_{0}^{\top}X_{0}+n\lambda I)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}Y_{0},

where
\lambda>0
and
I\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is the identity matrix.

In the limit
\lambda\to 0^{+}
, ridge converges to the ridgeless (minimum-norm) least-squares estimator
(
15
)
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}_{0}

\displaystyle:=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{b\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\Big\{\|b\|_{2}:b\text{ minimizes }\|Y_{0}-X_{0}b\|_{2}^{2}\Big\}

\displaystyle=(X_{0}^{\top}X_{0})^{+}X_{0}^{\top}Y_{0}=\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}(X_{0}^{\top}X_{0}+n\lambda I)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}Y_{0},

where
(\cdot)^{+}
denotes the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse.

Iterative self-training.

We now introduce the main object of study: an iterative procedure that refines an initial linear estimator. Starting from
\widehat{\beta}_{0}
fit on noisy data, each iteration draws a fresh feature matrix
X_{t}
, produces pseudo-labels
Y_{t}=X_{t}\widehat{\beta}_{t-1}
, and fits a new estimator on the resulting synthetic (noiseless) dataset. The procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Iterative self-training.

Input:

\mathcal{D}_{0}:=\{X_{0},Y_{0}\}
, regularization parameters
\{\lambda_{t}\}_{t=0}^{K}
, iterations
K
.

Initialization:

\widehat{\beta}_{0}\leftarrow(X_{0}^{\top}X_{0}+n\lambda_{0}I)^{+}X_{0}^{\top}Y_{0}
.

for

t=1,2,\dots,K

do

Sample
X_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}\times p}
independently.

Set
Y_{t}\leftarrow X_{t}\widehat{\beta}_{t-1}
.

Update
\widehat{\beta}_{t}\leftarrow(X_{t}^{\top}X_{t}+n_{t}\lambda_{t}I)^{+}X_{t}^{\top}Y_{t}
.

end

for

Output:

\{\widehat{\beta}_{0},\widehat{\beta}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\beta}_{K}\}
.

Self-training as self-distillation.

Algorithm
1
can be viewed as self-distillation:
\widehat{\beta}_{t-1}
acts as a teacher that generates pseudo-labels for fresh features, and
\widehat{\beta}_{t}
is the student trained on these labels. Because
Y_{t}
contains no additive noise, each step can attenuate the stochastic error inherited from the initial fit, but it may also discard signal through repeated teacher–student transfer. Our analysis tracks the evolution of the prediction risk of
\{\widehat{\beta}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{K}
across iterations.

2.3
Assumptions

Our analysis relies on the following assumptions on the data-generating process.

Assumption 2.1

(Independent feature matrices)
.

The feature matrices
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
used across iterations are mutually independent.

Assumption 2.2

(Gaussian covariates)
.

For each iteration
t\geq 0
, the feature matrix
X_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}\times p}
has i.i.d. rows
\{x_{t,i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{t}}
with

x_{t,i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{t}),

where
\Sigma_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is positive definite.

Assumption 2.3

(Uniformly well-conditioned covariances)
.

The training covariances
\{\Sigma_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and the test covariance
\Sigma
are uniformly well-conditioned: there exist constants
0<c_{\lambda}\leq C_{\lambda}<\infty
such that for every

S\in\{\Sigma_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\cup\{\Sigma\}
,

c_{\lambda}\leq\lambda_{\min}(S)\leq\lambda_{\max}(S)\leq C_{\lambda}.

Assumption 2.4

(Initial Gaussian noise)
.

Noise is present only at
t=0
. Specifically,
E_{0}=(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n})^{\top}
satisfies

E_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{n}),

for some
\sigma^{2}>0
, and is independent of
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.

Assumption
2.1
isolates the teacher–student transfer effect: each iteration trains a new student on pseudo-labels using an independent feature matrix. This independence makes the dynamics analytically tractable and clarifies how information is discarded across iterations. Assumption
2.2
(Gaussian covariates) is primarily imposed for simplicity and can often be relaxed to bounded-moment conditions
(
15
;
7
)
. Assumption
2.3
is a standard regularity condition that precludes degeneracy and ensures that the deterministic equivalents used in our high-dimensional analysis are well behaved. Finally, Assumption
2.4
(noise only at
t=0
) cleanly separates two forces: early iterations attenuate the stochastic error inherited from the initial noisy fit (denoising), while repeated self-training gradually erodes the signal component (signal forgetting).

3
Warm-up: Risk Dynamics in the Spiked Covariance Model

To build intuition for the general theory in Section
4
, we first analyze the iterative self-training dynamics under the
spiked covariance model
. This setting yields an interpretable description of how iteration denoises the estimate while gradually forgetting signal.

Analysis framework.

Unless otherwise stated, all results in this section share the following settings:

•

Ridgeless limit:
We study the minimum-norm interpolator by taking
\lambda_{t}\to 0^{+}
for all
t\geq 0
.

•

Asymptotic overparameterization:
We consider the high-dimensional limit
p,n\to\infty
with

\rho:=p/n>1
fixed, and define the effective regularization
\tau:=\rho-1>0
.

We specialize the general framework from Section
2
to the following concrete model.

Assumption 3.1

(Single-spiked covariance model)
.

The data-generating process satisfies:

•

Spiked covariance:
For all
t\geq 0
, the feature covariance is

\Sigma=(s-1)u_{1}u_{1}^{\top}+I_{p}
, where
s>1
is the spike strength and
u_{1}
is the leading eigenvector.

•

Aligned signal:
The signal is aligned with the spike:
\beta=ru_{1}
, where
r^{2}=\|\beta\|_{2}^{2}
is the signal power.

\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}

(Stochastic error reduction)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem

, the stochastic error vanishes asymptotically:
\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}\to 0

.

The decomposition in Theorem

thus highlights the fundamental trade-off:
each iteration reduces stochastic error
\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}

\mathcal{B}_{t}^{*}

U

In the signal-free limit

, systematic error
\mathcal{B}_{t}^{*}

\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau(1+\tau)^{t}}

\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}

\mathcal{R}_{t}^{*}
in

, as seen in Figure

-shape risk
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{*}
therefore emerges strictly from
anisotropy
(
s>1
), which induces a rate mismatch: noise error
\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}
decays rapidly at rate
(1+\tau)^{-t}
, while the signal-forgetting term
\mathcal{B}_{t}^{*}
grows much more slowly because contraction factor

is very close to one. As shown in Figure
3

this disparity creates a temporary window where denoising dominates before signal forgetting takes over.

3.2
Implicit feature selection

We now study the regime in which the signal is easily detectable, i.e., the spike strength
s
is large. In this regime, the iterates behave like a
direction-adaptive spectral filter
: they preserve the dominant signal direction while repeatedly suppressing isotropic components induced by noise and resampling. The following corollary characterizes the risk as the spike strength diverges.

Corollary 3.4

(Asymptotics for strong spikes)
.

As
s\to\infty
, the risk components satisfy

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle\to 0,\quad\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}\to\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau(1+\tau)^{t}}.

In this limit, the systematic error vanishes entirely. The algorithm achieves ideal separation: it perfectly preserves the signal component while attenuating the stochastic error at an exponential rate.

, this factor is close to
1
, so the algorithm barely shrinks the signal, consistent with
\mathcal{B}_{t}^{*}
being small. More precisely, a first-order expansion yields

1-\left(\frac{s}{s+\tau}\right)^{t+1}=1-\left(1-\frac{\tau}{s+\tau}\right)^{t+1}\approx\frac{(t+1)\tau}{s},

and hence
\mathcal{B}_{t}^{*}\approx r^{2}(t+1)^{2}\tau^{2}/s\to 0
as
s\to\infty
. This quantifies the crucial insight:
strong spikes prevent signal forgetting.

Mechanism: repeated random projections.
To see why the stochastic component is reduced more aggressively, rewrite the ridgeless update as

\widehat{\beta}_{t}=(X_{t}^{\top}X_{t})^{+}X_{t}^{\top}Y_{t}=(X_{t}^{\top}X_{t})^{+}X_{t}^{\top}X_{t}\widehat{\beta}_{t-1}=:P_{t}\widehat{\beta}_{t-1}.

The operator
P_{t}
is the orthogonal projection onto the row space of
X_{t}
(equivalently, the span of the top
n_{t}
right singular vectors of
X_{t}
). In the underparameterized regime
p\leq n_{t}
,
X_{t}^{\top}X_{t}
is invertible and
P_{t}=I
, so the iterates remain unchanged; hence
nontrivial dynamics arise only when
p>n_{t}
.
This projection acts differently on signal and noise components of the estimator:

1.

Signal preservation.

For large
s
, the leading singular direction of
X_{t}
aligns with
u_{1}
. Consequently,
P_{t}u_{1}\approx u_{1}
. The signal component lies largely in the projected subspace and is retained across iterations.

2.

Noise suppression.

Noise components are approximately isotropic.
At each step,
P_{t}
annihilates the noise component falling into its
(p-n_{t})
-dimensional null space. Because the feature matrices
\{X_{t}\}
are independent, their null spaces are incoherent; repeated projection progressively shears away noise in all directions.

Together, these effects yield an
implicit feature-selection
behavior: the algorithm preserves persistent, high-variance directions while progressively eliminating transient, randomly oriented components. This explains the algorithm’s effectiveness in the strong-signal regime and parallels the mechanism of weak-to-strong generalization
(
3
)
:
a student trained on labels produced by a teacher can denoise while preserving the teacher’s reliable signal directions.

Connection to ridge and the performance crossover.

Ridge regression imposes
global
shrinkage controlled by a single
\lambda
, shrinking all directions as a function of their empirical eigenvalues. In contrast, iterative self-training implements an
iteration-dependent, direction-adaptive
filter: in the strong-spike regime it leaves the dominant signal direction nearly unshrunk while repeatedly contracting the isotropic subspace at an effective rate close to
(1+\tau)^{-1}
per iteration. This mechanistic difference explains the crossover in Figure
3(b)
: as
s
grows, self-training can denoise without incurring the signal shrinkage that globally regularized methods may still pay.

3.3
Generalization to multi-spiked covariance

The single-spike model reveals the fundamental mechanism of signal preservation versus forgetting. However, real-world data typically possesses a richer spectral structure with multiple latent features of varying strengths. To capture this, we extend our analysis to the
multi-spike covariance model
. This setting demonstrates that the iterative self-training procedure acts as a
spectral filter
, selectively preserving strong signal components while suppressing weaker ones.

Assumption 3.5

(Multi-spiked covariance model)
.

Assume that, for all
t\geq 0
, the feature covariance and signal take the form

\Sigma_{t}=\Sigma=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(s_{j}-1)u_{j}u_{j}^{\top}+I_{p},\text{ with }s_{1}\geq\cdots\geq s_{k}>1,

and the true signal lies in the spiked subspace:
\beta=\sum_{j=1}^{k}r_{j}u_{j}
.

The next theorem shows that the risk evolution superposes across spike directions.

Theorem 3.6

(Risk decomposition for the multi-spike model)
.

Assume Assumptions
2.1
–
2.4
and Assumption
3.5
. In the asymptotic limit, the deterministic prediction risk satisfies

\mathcal{R}_{t}^{*}=\mathcal{B}_{t}^{*}+\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}r_{j}^{2}s_{j}\left(1-\left(\frac{s_{j}}{s_{j}+\tau}\right)^{t+1}\right)^{2},

(6)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{V}_{t-1}^{*}}{1+\tau}+\tau\sum_{j=1}^{k}r_{j}^{2}\frac{s_{j}^{2t+1}}{(s_{j}+\tau)^{2(t+1)}},

(7)

with
t\geq 1
and

\mathcal{V}_{0}^{*}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\tau\,r_{j}^{2}s_{j}}{(s_{j}+\tau)^{2}}
.

Iterative learning as spectral filtering.

Theorem
3.6
shows that different eigendirections are filtered at different rates. The survival of the signal component along direction
u_{j}
is governed by the contraction factor
\kappa_{j}:=\frac{s_{j}}{s_{j}+\tau}
.
For strong features (
s_{j}\gg\tau
), we have
\kappa_{j}\approx 1
, and thus the signal is largely preserved across iterations. For weak features (
s_{j}\approx 1
), we have
\kappa_{j}\ll 1
, and thus the signal is rapidly forgotten, resembling the isotropic noise.
Thus,
iterative self-training performs soft feature selection: it retains directions that are strong relative to the stochastic noise while filtering out weaker signals together with stochastic noise.

4
Main Results

This section presents our main theoretical results. We focus on the overparameterized regime in which the feature dimension exceeds the sample size at every iteration, i.e.,
p>n_{t}
. As discussed in Section
3.2
, this is precisely the regime where iterative self-training is non-trivial: each update applies a non-identity projection and induces rich signal–noise dynamics. We derive a precise, non-asymptotic characterization of the estimators
\{\widehat{\beta}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq K}
generated by our iterative self-training scheme.

4.1
Deterministic equivalents

The high-dimensional behavior of the ridge estimator at iteration
t
is characterized by two scalar
effective parameters
: an effective regularization level
\tau_{t}
and an effective noise variance
\gamma_{t}^{2}
. Let
\rho_{n,t}:=p/n_{t}
denote the aspect ratio at iteration
t
. For each
t\geq 0
,
(\tau_{t},\gamma_{t}^{2})
is defined as the unique positive solution to the following fixed-point system.

Definition 4.1

(Effective parameters)
.

For each
t=0,1,\ldots,K
, the pair
(\tau_{t},\gamma_{t}^{2})
is the unique positive solution to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho_{n,t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\operatorname{tr}\!\Big((\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\Big)+\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\tau_{t}},

(8)

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\sigma^{2}+\tau_{0}^{2}\big\|(\Sigma_{0}+\tau_{0}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{0}^{1/2}\beta\big\|_{2}^{2}}{\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\tau_{0}}+\frac{\tau_{0}}{p}\operatorname{tr}\!\Big((\Sigma_{0}+\tau_{0}I)^{-2}\Sigma_{0}\Big)}&\text{when }t=0,\\[11.99998pt]
\frac{\tau_{t}^{2}\big\|(\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}\widehat{\beta}_{t-1}\big\|_{2}^{2}}{\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\tau_{t}}+\frac{\tau_{t}}{p}\operatorname{tr}\!\Big((\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-2}\Sigma_{t}\Big)}&\text{when }t\geq 1.\end{cases}

(9)

Existence and uniqueness of a positive solution pair for (
8
)–(
9
) follow from
14
.

Deterministic equivalents.

Define the shrinkage operator

Q_{t}:=(\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{t},

and the cumulative propagator
Q_{a:b}:=Q_{a}Q_{a-1}\cdots Q_{b}
for
b\leq a
, with the convention
Q_{a:a+1}:=I
. We define the deterministic prediction risk
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{*}
and the deterministic effective noise variance
D_{t}^{2}
below, and later show that they uniformly approximate the empirical risks and effective noise variances over
t
; hence, they serve as deterministic equivalents.

Define the
deterministic prediction risk
as:

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{t}^{*}&:=\beta^{\top}(Q_{t:0}-I)^{\top}\Sigma(Q_{t:0}-I)\beta\\
&\qquad+\textstyle\sum\limits_{h=0}^{t}\frac{D_{h}^{2}}{p}\operatorname{tr}\!\Big(Q_{t:h+1}^{\top}\Sigma Q_{t:h+1}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times Q_{h}(\Sigma_{h}+\tau_{h}I)^{-1}\Big).\end{split}

(10)

Define the
deterministic effective noise variance
for
t\geq 1
recursively as:

\displaystyle L_{t}D_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tau_{t}^{2}\beta^{\top}Q_{t:0}^{\top}(\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-1}Q_{t-1:0}\beta

\displaystyle\quad+\tau_{t}^{2}\sum\limits_{h=0}^{t-1}\frac{D_{h}^{2}}{p}\operatorname{tr}\Big(Q_{t:h+1}^{\top}(\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times Q_{t-1:h}(\Sigma_{h}+\tau_{h}I)^{-1}\Big)

with base case
D_{0}^{2}:=\gamma_{0}^{2}
, where

L_{t}:=\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\tau_{t}}+\frac{\tau_{t}}{p}\operatorname{tr}\!\Big((\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-2}\Sigma_{t}\Big).

Spectral shrinkage and error propagation.

The matrix
Q_{t}
acts as a spectral shrinkage operator. If
\Sigma_{t}=U\Lambda_{t}U^{\top}
with eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{t,j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
, then
Q_{t}
shares the same eigenvectors and applies the scalar filter

\phi_{t}(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\tau_{t}}\in(0,1).

Thus, high-variance directions are largely preserved (
\phi_{t}(\lambda)\approx 1
), whereas low-variance directions are attenuated (
\phi_{t}(\lambda)\ll 1
). The cumulative propagator
Q_{t:0}=Q_{t:0}=Q_{t}Q_{t-1}\cdots Q_{0}
therefore implements an iteration-dependent, data-adaptive low-pass filtering in the eigenbasis of the covariances.

With this viewpoint, (
10
) admits the decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\beta^{\top}(Q_{t:0}-I)^{\top}\Sigma(Q_{t:0}-I)\beta}_{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\text{systematic error / signal forgetting}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\textstyle\sum\limits_{h=0}^{t}\frac{D_{h}^{2}}{p}\operatorname{tr}\!\Big(Q_{t:h+1}^{\top}\Sigma Q_{t:h+1}\,Q_{h}(\Sigma_{h}+\tau_{h}I)^{-1}\Big)}_{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\text{stochastic error}}.

The first term increases as the signal is repeatedly transformed by
Q_{t:0}
, while the second term quantifies how injected noise is geometrically filtered through the propagators
Q_{t:h+1}
.
This signal-forgetting–denoising tension underlies the empirically observed
U
-shaped generalization curve and motivates early stopping.

Concentration around deterministic equivalents.

We now show that the prediction risk concentrates around its deterministic equivalent
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{*}
in (
10
), and that the effective noise variance
\gamma_{t}^{2}
concentrates around
D_{t}^{2}
. This justifies using these deterministic quantities as accurate proxies for their empirical counterparts.

Theorem 4.2

(Concentration around deterministic equivalents)
.

Let Assumptions
2.1
–
2.4
hold. Suppose there exists
M>0
such that for all
0\leq t\leq K
:
(i)

,
(ii)
\lambda_{t}\leq M
and
\sigma^{2}\leq M

(iii)

and
\|\Sigma_{t}^{-1}\|_{2}\leq M

Fix any
R
such that
\|\beta\|_{2}\leq R
and

C=C(M)
such that for any

How to use the theorem in practice.

Theorem
4.2
implies that for large
(n,p)

\mathcal{R}_{t}^{*}
as a reliable proxy for the empirical risk curve and select an early-stopping time by minimizing
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{*}
over

4.2
Iterated GCV for risk estimation

A central practical challenge in iterative self-training is to select the stopping time and regularization level without access to the true signal or an independent validation set.

Let
A_{t}=P_{t}\cdots P_{1}
denote the cumulative projection operator after
t
iterations, where
P_{t}
is the (data-dependent) projection at iteration
t
. We propose an
iterated GCV (iGCV)
estimator for the prediction risk. Define the leverage-dependent multiplier

\!\!\!\!\!M_{t}(\lambda):=\frac{\operatorname{tr}\!\Big(X_{0}A_{t}\big(X_{0}^{\top}X_{0}/n_{0}+\lambda I\big)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}\Big)/n_{0}}{1-\operatorname{tr}\!\Big(X_{0}\big(X_{0}^{\top}X_{0}/n_{0}+\lambda I\big)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}\Big)/n_{0}}.

(11)

The iGCV estimator
\widehat{\mathrm{GCV}}^{(t)}(\lambda)
is the average of corrected residuals,

\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}\left[y_{i}-x_{i}^{\top}A_{t}\widehat{\beta}_{0}+\bigl(y_{i}-x_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\beta}_{0}\bigr)M_{t}(\lambda)\right]^{2},

(12)

where
(x_{i},y_{i})
are the initial noisy training samples in
\mathcal{D}_{0}
and
\widehat{\beta}_{0}
is the initial ridge estimator.

In the ridgeless limit (
\lambda\to 0^{+}
) with
p>n_{0}
, the correction term in (
12
) becomes ill-conditioned. Following
15
;
24
, we define the
\lambda=0
profile by continuity, replacing the inverse with the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse so that the iGCV curve remains well defined even in the interpolating regime.

Theorem 4.3

(Uniform consistency of iterated GCV)
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
–
2.4
hold. For any fixed
t\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, the iGCV estimator (
12
) satisfies

\sup_{\lambda\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\widehat{\mathrm{GCV}}^{(t)}(\lambda)-\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\beta}_{t})-\sigma^{2}\right|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0,

as
n_{0},p\to\infty
with
p/n_{0}\to\rho>1
, where
\mathcal{I}
is any compact subinterval of
[0,+\infty]
.

6
Conclusions and Discussion

We analyzed iterative self-training in overparameterized linear regression through high-dimensional asymptotics, formalizing the tension between early-stage
denoising
and progressive
signal forgetting
. This trade-off manifests as a
U
-shaped risk curve, driven by an underlying
spectral filtering
mechanism that performs
soft feature selection
. Our deterministic recursions yield precise finite-sample predictions, while our iterated GCV enables optimal early stopping by balancing denoising gains against accumulating systematic drift without requiring external validation.

To bridge this framework with practice, extensions to reused data and filtered pseudo-labels are necessary to address finite-sample constraints and to formalize heuristics that modulate the denoising–forgetting balance. Investigating nonlinear models is also critical to determine whether evolving representations stabilize signal or exacerbate drift. Finally, while simulations show self-training can surpass ridge, a theorem-level comparison—specifying the asymptotic tuning rule for ridge as a function of
s
is left for future work. Whether analogous deterministic-equivalent descriptions persist in these richer settings remains a fundamental open problem.

Appendix B
The Isotropic Covariance Case

This section presents the risk dynamics in the istropic covariance case.

Corollary B.1

(Special cases: no signal and isotropic features)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.2
, the following special cases hold:

1.

No signal (
r^{2}=0
):
\mathcal{B}_{t}^{*}=0
and

\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau(1+\tau)^{t}},

corresponding to pure denoising.

2.

Isotropic features (
s=1
): The risk simplifies to

\mathcal{R}_{t}^{*}=r^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{(1+\tau)^{t+1}}\right)^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau(1+\tau)^{t}}.

In the isotropic setting (
s=1
),
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{*}
is monotone in
t
, unlike the spiked covariance case. Indeed,

\mathcal{R}_{t+1}^{*}-\mathcal{R}_{t}^{*}=\frac{1}{(1+\tau)^{t+2}}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{\tau}-\sigma^{2}(1+\tau)\right),

whose sign is independent of
t
. Iteration helps if and only if the initial error is noise-dominated, i.e.,

r^{2}/\sigma^{2}<\tau(1+\tau)
, confirming that a genuine
U
-shape requires anisotropy (
s>1
).

Proof.

We specialize Theorem 3.2 to the two cases provided in the corollary.

Case 1: No signal (
r^{2}=0
).

Substituting
r^{2}=0
into (
4
) and (
5
), the systematic error
\mathcal{B}^{*}_{t}
vanishes for all
t
. The initial condition and recursive step for the stochastic error simplify to:

\mathcal{V}^{*}_{0}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau},\quad\mathcal{V}^{*}_{t}=\frac{\mathcal{V}^{*}_{t-1}}{1+\tau}.

This is a pure geometric progression. Solving the recurrence yields
V^{*}_{t}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau(1+\tau)^{t}}
, which represents pure denoising.

Case 2: Isotropic features (
s=1
).

When
s=1
, the contraction factor for the signal becomes
1/(1+\tau)
, which is identical to the contraction factor of the noise. The systematic error term (
4
) becomes:

\mathcal{B}^{*}_{t}=r^{2}\left(1-\left[\frac{1}{1+\tau}\right]^{t+1}\right)^{2}.

The stochastic error recurrence (
5
) simplifies to:

\mathcal{V}^{*}_{t}=\frac{\mathcal{V}^{*}_{t-1}}{1+\tau}+\frac{\tau r^{2}}{(1+\tau)^{2(t+1)}},\quad\text{with}\quad\mathcal{V}^{*}_{0}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau}+\frac{\tau r^{2}}{(1+\tau)^{2}}.

In this case, the risk
\mathcal{R}^{*}_{t}=\mathcal{B}^{*}_{t}+\mathcal{V}^{*}_{t}
is monotonic in
t
. The lack of a ”rate mismatch” between signal and noise contraction prevents the emergence of the U-shaped risk curve. Iteration only helps if the initial variance reduction outweighs the bias increase, which depends solely on the initial signal-to-noise ratio.

: Risk Recursion in the Spiked Model

Proof.

The proof proceeds by specializing the general deterministic equivalents for risk and effective noise variance from Theorem
4.2
to the single-spiked covariance model (Assumption
3.1
). We analyze the behavior of the key matrices in this model under the high-dimensional limit (
n,p\to\infty
with
p/n\to\rho>1
).

Step 1: Asymptotic Analysis of the Effective Regularization,
\tau

In the spiked model, the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{t}=\Sigma=(s-1)e_{1}e_{1}^{\top}+I_{p}
is constant for all
t
. Consequently, the effective regularization
\tau_{t}
will also be a constant, denoted by
\tau
. Its value is determined by the fixed-point equation from Definition
4.1
(assuming
\lambda_{t}\to 0^{+}
for ridgeless regression):

\frac{n}{p}=\frac{1}{p}\operatorname{tr}((\Sigma+\tau I)^{-1}\Sigma).

The matrix inside the trace,
Q:=(\Sigma+\tau I)^{-1}\Sigma
is diagonal in the eigenbasis of
\Sigma
. Specifically:

(i)

The eigenvector
e_{1}
has an eigenvalue of
\frac{s}{s+\tau}
.

(ii)

The
p-1
eigenvectors orthogonal to
e_{1}
have an eigenvalue of
\frac{1}{1+\tau}
.

The trace is the sum of these eigenvalues:

\operatorname{tr}(Q)=\frac{1}{p}\operatorname{tr}((\Sigma+\tau I)^{-1}\Sigma)=\frac{1}{p}\left(\frac{s}{s+\tau}+(p-1)\frac{1}{1+\tau}\right).

In the asymptotic limit where
p\to\infty
, the term involving the spike vanishes, yielding:

\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{tr}(Q)=\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p}\left(\frac{s}{s+\tau}+(p-1)\frac{1}{1+\tau}\right)=\frac{1}{1+\tau}.

Substituting this back into the fixed-point equation gives
\frac{1}{\rho}=\frac{1}{1+\tau}
gives the constant effective regularization:

\tau=\rho-1.

Step 2: Derivation of the systematic error,
\mathcal{B}_{t}^{*}

The systematic error term
\mathcal{B}_{t}^{*}
corresponds to the signal-forgetting component in the general risk formula from Theorem
4.2
:

\mathcal{B}_{t}^{*}=\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\beta^{\top}((Q_{t:0})^{\top}-I)\Sigma(Q_{t:0}-I)\beta=\|\Sigma^{1/2}(Q_{t:0}-I)\beta\|_{2}^{2}.

In the spiked model, the shrinkage operator
Q
is constant over iterations, so the cumulative propagator
Q_{t:0}=Q^{t+1}
. We substitute the aligned signal
\beta=re_{1}
. Since
e_{1}
is an eigenvector of
Q
with eigenvalue
\frac{s}{s+\tau}
, we have:

\Sigma^{1/2}(Q_{t:0}-I)\beta=\Sigma^{1/2}(Q^{t+1}-I)re_{1}=r\sqrt{s}\left(1-\left(\frac{s}{s+\tau}\right)^{t+1}\right)e_{1}.

Taking the squared Euclidean norm yields the desired expression for the systematic bias:

\mathcal{B}_{t}^{*}=r^{2}s\left(1-\left(\frac{s}{s+\tau}\right)^{t+1}\right)^{2}.

Step 3: Derivation of the Stochastic Error Recursion,
\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}

The stochastic error term
\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}
is defined as the remainder of the total risk after subtracting the systematic error. It is the asymptotic limit of the propagated noise terms in the general risk formula.

Initial Condition (
\mathcal{V}_{0}^{*}
):

From Theorem
4.2
, the initial stochastic error
\mathcal{V}_{0}^{*}
is determined by the initial deterministic effective noise
D_{0}
. In the asymptotic limit, let’s denote this as
D_{t}^{*}=\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}D_{t}
The formula for
D_{0}
is
L_{0}D_{0}^{2}=\sigma^{2}+\tau\|(\Sigma+\tau I)^{-1}\Sigma^{1/2}\beta\|_{2}^{2}
. We analyze these components in the limit
p\to\infty
:

•

The squared norm term evaluates to:

\|(\Sigma+\tau I)^{-1}\Sigma^{1/2}re_{1}\|_{2}^{2}=r^{2}s\left\|\frac{1}{s+\tau}e_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{r^{2}s}{(s+\tau)^{2}}.

•

The term
L_{0}
:

\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}L_{0}=\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\tau}{p}\operatorname{tr}((\Sigma+\tau I)^{-2}\Sigma)=\frac{\tau}{(1+\tau)^{2}}

Substituting these limits, we obtain:

\frac{\tau}{(1+\tau)^{2}}D_{0}^{*2}=\sigma^{2}+\frac{\tau r^{2}s}{(s+\tau)^{2}}.

Finally, for
\mathcal{V}_{0}^{*}
, we have:

\mathcal{V}_{0}^{*}=\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{D_{0}^{2}}{p}\operatorname{tr}((\Sigma+\tau I)^{-2}\Sigma)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau}+\frac{r^{2}s}{(s+\tau)^{2}}

This matches the formula for
\mathcal{V}_{0}^{*}
in the theorem statement.

Step 3.1: Asymptotic Recursion for Deterministic Noise
D_{t}^{*}
.

We evaluate the general recursion for
D_{t}^{2}
from Theorem 4.3 in the high-dimensional limit:

L_{t}D_{t}^{2}=\tau_{t}^{2}\beta^{\top}Q_{t-1:0}^{\top}Q_{t}(\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-1}Q_{t-1:0}\beta+\tau_{t}^{2}\sum\limits_{h=0}^{t-1}\frac{D_{h}^{2}}{p}\operatorname{tr}\left(Q_{t-1:h+1}^{\top}Q_{t}(\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-1}Q_{t-1:h+1}Q_{h}(\Sigma_{h}+\tau_{h}I)^{-1}\right).

For constant spiked matrices, the recursion simplifies to:

L^{*}D_{t}^{*2}=\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{2}\beta^{\top}(Q^{t})^{\top}Q(\Sigma+\tau I)^{-1}Q^{t}\beta+\tau^{2}\sum_{h=0}^{t-1}D_{h}^{*2}\lim_{p\to\infty}\frac{1}{p}\operatorname{tr}\left((Q^{t-h-1})^{\top}Q(\Sigma+\tau I)^{-1}Q^{t-h-1}Q(\Sigma+\tau I)^{-1}\right).

We evaluate each component’s limit:

•

The bias term is exactly:
r^{2}\left(\frac{s}{s+\tau}\right)^{2t}\frac{s}{(s+\tau)^{2}}
.

•

The trace term is dominated by the
p-1
isotropic eigenvectors:
\frac{1}{(1+\tau)^{2(k-h+1)}}
.

Substituting
L^{*}=\frac{\tau}{(1+\tau)^{2}}
, we obtain:

\frac{\tau}{(1+\tau)^{2}}D_{t}^{*2}=\tau^{2}r^{2}\frac{s^{2t+1}}{(s+\tau)^{2t+2}}+\tau^{2}\sum_{h=0}^{t-1}\frac{D_{h}^{*2}}{(1+\tau)^{2(t-h+1)}}.

(13)

Step 3.2: Relating
\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}
to
D_{t}^{*}
.

The stochastic error
\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}
is the asymptotic limit of the sum-over-noise term. For the spiked model:

\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}=\lim_{p\to\infty}\sum_{h=0}^{t}\frac{D_{h}^{2}}{p}\operatorname{tr}\left((Q^{t-h})^{\top}\Sigma Q^{t-h}Q(\Sigma+\tau I)^{-1}\right)=\sum_{h=0}^{t}\frac{D_{h}^{*2}}{(1+\tau)^{2(t-h+1)}}.

(14)

Separating the
t
-th term from the sum gives:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{D_{t}^{*2}}{(1+\tau)^{2}}+\sum_{h=0}^{t-1}\frac{D_{h}^{*2}}{(1+\tau)^{2(t-h+1)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{D_{t}^{*2}}{(1+\tau)^{2}}+\frac{1}{(1+\tau)^{2}}\sum_{h=0}^{t-1}\frac{D_{h}^{*2}}{(1+\tau)^{2(t-h)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{D_{t}^{*2}}{(1+\tau)^{2}}+\frac{\mathcal{V}_{t-1}^{*}}{(1+\tau)^{2}}.

(15)

This identity shows how the total stochastic error evolves by adding the newly generated noise at step
t
to the propagated noise from the previous step.

Step 3.3: Final Derivation.

Our goal is to express
\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}
solely in terms of
\mathcal{V}_{t-1}^{*}
. We can achieve this by substituting the recursion for
D_{t}^{*}
(
13
) into our expression for
\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}
(
15
).
From (
13
), we have:

\frac{D_{t}^{*2}}{(1+\tau)^{2}}=\tau r^{2}\frac{s^{2t+1}}{(s+\tau)^{2t+2}}+\tau\sum_{h=0}^{t-1}\frac{D_{h}^{*2}}{(1+\tau)^{2(t-h+1)}}.

From (
14
), we have:

\frac{D_{t}^{*2}}{(1+\tau)^{2}}=\tau r^{2}\frac{s^{2t+1}}{(s+\tau)^{2t+2}}+\frac{\tau\mathcal{V}_{t-1}^{*}}{(1+\tau)^{2}}.

Now, substitute this into the relation for
\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}
from (
15
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle=\left(\tau r^{2}\frac{s^{2t+1}}{(s+\tau)^{2t+2}}+\frac{\tau\mathcal{V}_{t-1}^{*}}{(1+\tau)^{2}}\right)+\frac{\mathcal{V}_{t-1}^{*}}{(1+\tau)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\tau r^{2}\frac{s^{2t+1}}{(s+\tau)^{2t+2}}+\frac{\mathcal{V}_{t-1}^{*}}{1+\tau}.

This completes the proof.
∎

C.2
Proof of Corollary
3.3

Proof.

From the recursion derived in Theorem
3.2
, the stochastic error
\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}
follows a linear inhomogeneous recurrence:

\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}=\frac{1}{1+\tau}\mathcal{V}_{t-1}^{*}+C_{t},\quad\text{where}\quad C_{t}=\tau r^{2}\frac{s^{2t+1}}{(s+\tau)^{2t+2}}.

(16)

Since the aspect ratio
\rho>1
, the effective regularization
\tau=\rho-1
is strictly positive. This implies that the contraction coefficient
1/(1+\tau)
lies in the interval
(0,1)
. To prove that
V^{*}_{t}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, we analyze the driving term
C_{t}
:

C_{t}=\frac{\tau r^{2}}{s}\left(\frac{s}{s+\tau}\right)^{2t+2}.

Since
s>1
and
\tau>0
, the ratio
\kappa^{2}=(\frac{s}{s+\tau})^{2}
is strictly less than
1
. Thus,
C_{t}
is a geometric sequence that decays exponentially to zero as
t\to\infty
.

Unrolling the recurrence (
16
) from
t
down to
0
, we obtain:

V^{*}_{t}=\left(\frac{1}{1+\tau}\right)^{t}V^{*}_{0}+\sum_{h=1}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{1+\tau}\right)^{t-h}C_{h}.

The first term
\left(\frac{1}{1+\tau}\right)^{t}V^{*}_{0}
vanishes as
t\to\infty
. The second term is a convolution of two sequences that both decay exponentially to zero. By the properties of convergent sequences (specifically, the Cauchy product or a direct application of the squeeze theorem), we conclude:

\lim_{t\to\infty}V^{*}_{t}=0.

This demonstrates that iterative self-training consistently attenuates the stochastic error inherited from the initial fit and the random projections.
∎

C.3
Proof of Corollary
3.4

Proof.

We analyze the limits of the risk components as the spike strength
s\to\infty
for a fixed iteration
t
.

Part 1: Limit of the systematic error (
\mathcal{B}_{t}^{*}
)

The expression for the systematic error is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle=r^{2}s\left(1-\left(\frac{s}{s+\tau}\right)^{t+1}\right)^{2}.

Using a first-order Taylor expansion for large
s
:

\left(\frac{s}{s+\tau}\right)^{t+1}=\left(1+\frac{\tau}{s}\right)^{-(t+1)}=1-\frac{(t+1)\tau}{s}+O(s^{-2}).

Substituting this into the bias formula:

B^{*}_{t}=r^{2}s\left(\frac{(t+1)\tau}{s}+O(s^{-2})\right)^{2}=\frac{r^{2}(t+1)^{2}\tau^{2}}{s}+O(s^{-2}).

Taking the limit
s\to\infty
, we find
B^{*}_{t}\to 0
. Physically, this means that for a sufficiently strong spike, the iterative projection barely erodes the signal aligned with that direction.

Part 1: Limit of the systematic error (
\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}
)

We proceed by induction on
t
.

Base Case (
t=0
):
From the initial condition in Theorem
3.2
:

\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{V}_{0}^{*}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau}+\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\tau s}{(s+\tau)^{2}}r^{2}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau}.

This matches the target formula
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau(1+\tau)^{t}}
for
t=0
.

Inductive Step:
Assume the result holds for some
t-1
, i.e.,
\mathcal{V}_{t-1}^{*}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau(1+\tau)^{t-1}}
. For iteration
t
, the recurrence gives:

\lim_{s\to\infty}V^{*}_{t}=\lim_{s\to\infty}\left(\frac{V^{*}_{t-1}}{1+\tau}+\tau r^{2}\frac{s^{2t+1}}{(s+\tau)^{2(t+1)}}\right).

Analyze the second term:

\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{s^{2t+1}}{(s+\tau)^{2t+2}}=\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{1}{s}\left(\frac{1}{1+\tau/s}\right)^{2t+2}=0\cdot 1=0.

Thus, the limit satisfies:

\lim_{s\to\infty}V^{*}_{t}=\frac{1}{1+\tau}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau(1+\tau)^{t-1}}\right)+0=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau(1+\tau)^{t}}.

This completes the induction. The result shows that in the strong-spike regime, the systematic error vanishes while the noise is filtered at a rate of
(1+\tau)^{-1}
per iteration.
∎

D.1
The Distribution of Ridge(less) Regression Estimator

This section characterizes the distribution of the Ridge and ridgeless estimator,
\widehat{\beta}_{\lambda}
within the linear model defined in (
1
). We introduce a connection to a corresponding Ridge estimator in a simpler Gaussian sequence model. For a given covariance matrix
\Sigma

, coefficient vector
\beta
, and noise level
\gamma>0
, this sequence model is defined as:

y^{\text{seq}}_{(\Sigma,\beta)}(\gamma)=\Sigma^{1/2}\beta+\frac{\gamma g}{\sqrt{p}},\quad g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).

(17)

It is crucial to note that this effective noise level,
\gamma
, is distinct from the noise variance,
\sigma^{2}
, in the original linear model. Instead,
\gamma
is endogenously determined as part of the solution to a system of fixed-point equations that will be introduced in the subsequent theorem.

The Ridge estimator, denoted as
\widehat{\beta}^{\text{seq}}_{(\Sigma,\beta)}(\gamma;\tau)
, with a regularization parameter
\tau\geq 0
in the Gaussian sequence model (
17
), is the solution to the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}^{\text{seq}}_{(\Sigma,\beta)}(\gamma;\tau)

\displaystyle:=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{b\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\{\frac{1}{2n}\|\Sigma^{1/2}b-y^{\text{seq}}_{(\Sigma,\beta)}(\gamma)\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\tau}{2}\|b\|_{2}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=(\Sigma+\tau I)^{-1}\Sigma^{1/2}\left(\Sigma^{1/2}\beta+\frac{\gamma g}{\sqrt{p}}\right).

(18)

The following theorem, which extends Theorem 2.3 in
(
14
)
, characterizes the distribution of
\widehat{\beta}_{\lambda}
in terms of
\widehat{\beta}^{\text{seq}}_{(\Sigma,\beta)}
. Our extension specifically addresses the case where the noise variance,
\sigma^{2}=0
, can be zero.

Theorem D.1

(Distributional characterization
(
14
;
19
)
)
.

Assume Assumptions
2.2
-
2.4
hold. Further, assume that for some constant
M>0
, we have
, and that
1+1/M<\rho_{n}\leq M
and
\sigma^{2}
,
\|\Sigma\|_{2}
,
\|\Sigma^{-1}\|_{2}\leq M
. Then, for any
L
-Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
L<L(M)
, there exists a constant
C=C(M)
such that
for any
\epsilon\in(0,1/2]
, the following holds:

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\lambda\in[0,M]}\left|f(\widehat{\beta}_{\lambda})-\mathbbm{E}_{g}f(\widehat{\beta}^{\text{seq}}_{(\Sigma,\beta)}(\gamma;\tau))\right|>\epsilon\right)\leq Cpe^{-p\epsilon^{4}/C},

(19)

where
R<M
, and
(\gamma,\tau)
is the unique positive solution of the following wing fixed point equations:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \rho_{n}&=\frac{1}{p}\operatorname{tr}\left((\Sigma+\tau I)^{-1}\Sigma\right)+\frac{\lambda}{\tau},\\
\gamma^{2}&=\frac{\sigma^{2}+\tau^{2}\|(\Sigma+\tau I)^{-1}\Sigma^{1/2}\beta\|_{2}^{2}}{\frac{\lambda}{\tau}+\frac{\tau}{p}\operatorname{tr}\left((\Sigma+\tau I)^{-2}\Sigma\right)}.\end{aligned}\right.

(20)

Proof.

The primary distinction between this result and Theorem 2.3 in
(
14
)
is our accommodation for a zero noise level,
\sigma^{2}=0
. In the original proof, a non-zero noise level is crucial for establishing a lower bound on the effective noise, specifically
\gamma^{2}>\frac{1}{C}
for some constant
C>0
. To extend the characterization to the noiseless setting, we have removed the requirement for the bound to hold uniformly over
\beta
.

We now consider the case where
\sigma^{2}=0
. The expression for the effective noise becomes:

\gamma^{2}=\frac{\tau^{2}\|(\Sigma+\tau I)^{-1}\Sigma^{1/2}\beta\|_{2}^{2}}{\frac{\lambda}{\tau}+\frac{\tau}{p}\operatorname{tr}\left((\Sigma+\tau I)^{-2}\Sigma\right)}\geq\frac{\tau\|(\Sigma+\tau I)^{-1}\Sigma^{1/2}\beta\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\Sigma^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}/(\|\Sigma^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}+\tau)^{2}}.

From this expression, it is evident that
\gamma=0
if and only if
\beta=0
(since we have assumed
\|\Sigma^{-1}\|_{\text{op}}\leq M
).

Let us examine the estimators in this specific scenario where
\sigma^{2}=0
and
\beta=0
. The ridge estimator is given by:

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=(X^{\top}X+n\lambda I)^{-1}{X}^{\top}Y

\displaystyle=(X^{\top}X+n\lambda I)^{-1}{X}^{\top}X\beta+(X^{\top}X+n\lambda I)^{-1}{X}^{\top}\varepsilon

Since
\beta=0
and
\sigma^{2}=0
implies
\varepsilon=0
, we have
\widehat{\beta}_{\lambda}=0
.

Similarly, from equation (
18
), the ridge estimator in the sequence model is:

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}^{\text{seq}}_{(\Sigma,0)}(0;\tau)

\displaystyle=(\Sigma+\tau I)^{-1}\Sigma^{1/2}\left(\Sigma^{1/2}\cdot 0+\frac{0\cdot g}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=0.

In the case where
\sigma^{2}=0
and
\beta=0
, both estimators are zero. Therefore, the statement of the theorem holds, as the difference between the functions of the estimators is zero. For the case where
\beta\neq 0
, the effective noise
\gamma
remains positive, and the arguments from the proof of Theorem 2.3 in
(
14
)
continue to apply. This completes the extension of the theorem to the noiseless setting.
∎

Proof of Theorem

As a preliminary step for the proof of Theorem

, we must first establish uniform high-probability bounds on the norms of the estimators generated by our iterative scheme. The following lemma provides these crucial bounds, extending the result of Proposition 11 in

Lemma D.2

(Uniform High-Probability Bounds on Estimator Norms)
.

Suppose the assumptions of Theorem
4.2
hold. Specifically, assume that for some constant
M>1
,
1/M\leq\rho_{n},\sigma^{2}\leq M

\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}(M,c)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{c})\leq Ce^{-p/C}

A

\|A\widehat{\beta}_{t}-\beta\|_{2}\leq\|\beta\|_{2}+c.

Proof.

The proof proceeds by induction on the iteration index
t
.

Base Case
(t=0)
:

The initial estimator is

\widehat{\beta}_{0}=(X_{0}^{T}X_{0}+n\lambda_{0}I)^{+}X_{0}^{T}Y_{0}=(X_{0}^{T}X_{0}+n_{0}\lambda_{0}I)^{+}X_{0}^{T}(X_{0}\beta+E)

\widehat{\beta}_{0}=P_{0}\beta+(X_{0}^{T}X_{0}+n_{0}\lambda_{0}I)^{+}X_{0}^{T}E.

Proposition 11 in
(
19
)
establishes that on the high-probability event

, the stochastic term is bounded:

\|(X_{0}^{T}X_{0})^{+}X_{0}^{T}E\|_{2}\leq c.

(21)

1.

Bound on
\|\widehat{\beta}_{0}\|_{2}
:
Using the triangle inequality and the fact that

\|\widehat{\beta}_{0}\|_{2}=\|P_{0}\beta\|_{2}+\|(X_{0}^{T}X_{0}+n_{0}\lambda_{0}I)^{+}X_{0}^{T}E\|_{2}\leq\|P_{0}\|_{2}\|\beta\|_{2}+c\leq\|\beta\|_{2}+c.

2.

\displaystyle\|A\widehat{\beta}_{0}-\beta\|_{2}^{2}=\|A(P_{0}\beta-\beta+(X_{0}^{T}X_{0}+n_{0}\lambda_{0}I)^{+}X_{0}^{T}E)\|_{2}

Thus, the base case holds on the event
\mathcal{E}
.

Inductive Step:

Assume that for some

, the bounds hold for
\widehat{\beta}_{t-1}
. We need to show they also hold for
\widehat{\beta}_{t}
. For

\widehat{\beta}_{t}=(X_{t}^{T}X_{t}+n_{t}\lambda_{t}I)^{+}X_{t}^{T}Y_{t}=(X_{t}^{T}X_{t}+n_{t}\lambda_{t}I)^{+}X_{t}^{T}X_{t}\widehat{\beta}_{t-1}.

This matrix is positive semi-definite, and

.

1.

Bound on

2.

By the inductive hypothesis, since

This completes the inductive step. The claims hold for all

.

The following lemma establishes the Lipschitz continuity of two key functions that appear in the proof of Theorem

Lemma D.3

(Lipschitz Properties)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem

from Lemma

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{1}(\widehat{\beta}_{t})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|2A^{\top}\Sigma(A\widehat{\beta}_{t}-\beta)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|A\|_{2}\|\Sigma\|_{2}\|A\widehat{\beta}_{t}-\beta\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2M(\|\beta\|_{2}+c),

where last inequality follow from Lemma

.

1. Analysis of
f_{2,k}(b)
:

The gradient with respect to
b
is

\nabla f_{2,k}(b)=2A(\Sigma_{k}+\tau_{k}I)^{-2}\Sigma_{k}A\widehat{\beta}_{t}.

Now, we bound its norm when evaluated at
b=\widehat{\beta}_{t}
:

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{2}(\widehat{\beta}_{t})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|2A(\Sigma_{k}+\tau_{k}I)^{-2}\Sigma_{k}A\widehat{\beta}_{t}\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\|A\|_{2}^{2}\|(\Sigma_{k}+\tau_{k}I)^{-2}\Sigma_{k}\|_{2}\|\widehat{\beta}_{t}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2(\|\beta\|_{2}+c)(\lambda_{k,0}\vee\lambda_{k,p}^{-1})

\displaystyle\leq 2M(\|\beta\|_{2}+c),

where
\lambda_{k,0}
and
\lambda_{k,p}
are the largest and smallest eigenvalue of
\Sigma_{k}
, respectively. This establishes the bound
L
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

The proof proceeds by induction on the iteration index
t
. The core of the argument is the repeated application of the distributional characterization from Theorem
D.1
. This allows us to replace the random estimator
\widehat{\beta}_{t}

with its simpler Gaussian sequence model equivalent,
\widehat{\beta}_{t}^{\text{seq}}
, when computing the expectation of any Lipschitz function. By fully ”unrolling” the recursive definition of
\widehat{\beta}_{t}^{\text{seq}}
, we can express it as a function of the initial signal
\beta
and the history of independent Gaussian noise vectors. This allows for a direct calculation of the deterministic equivalents.

We begin by defining the sequence-model estimators using the propagator notation
Q_{t}=(\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{t}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}_{0}^{\text{seq}}

\displaystyle=Q_{0}\beta+\frac{\gamma_{0}}{\sqrt{p}}(\Sigma_{0}+\tau_{0}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{0}^{1/2}g_{0}

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}_{t}^{\text{seq}}

\displaystyle=Q_{t}\widehat{\beta}_{t-1}+\frac{\gamma_{t}}{\sqrt{p}}(\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}g_{t},

where
\{g_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq K}
are independent standard Gaussian vectors.

Part 1: Deterministic Equivalent of the Prediction Risk

The prediction risk
\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\beta}_{t})=\|\Sigma^{1/2}(\widehat{\beta}_{t}-\beta)\|_{2}^{2}
is a Lipschitz function of
\widehat{\beta}_{t}
(Lemma
D.3
). Its deterministic equivalent
\mathcal{R}_{t}
is therefore
\mathbbm{E}_{g_{t}}[\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\beta}_{t}^{\text{seq}})]
.

1. Base Case (
t=0
):

The deterministic risk
\mathcal{R}_{0}
is the expectation of the risk of the sequence-model estimator:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{E}_{g_{0}}\left[\|\Sigma^{1/2}(\widehat{\beta}_{0}^{\text{seq}}-\beta)\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{E}_{g_{0}}\left[\left\|\Sigma^{1/2}\left((Q_{0}-I)\beta+\frac{\gamma_{0}}{\sqrt{p}}(\Sigma_{0}+\tau_{0}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{0}^{1/2}g_{0}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].

We expand the squared norm. The cross-term vanishes because
\mathbbm{E}[g_{0}]=0
. We are left with the sum of the squared norms of the deterministic bias and the expected noise term:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{0}

\displaystyle=\|\Sigma^{1/2}(Q_{0}-I)\beta\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{p}\mathbbm{E}_{g_{0}}\left[\|\Sigma^{1/2}(\Sigma+\tau_{0}I)^{-1}\Sigma^{1/2}g_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\beta^{\top}(Q_{0}-I)^{\top}\Sigma(Q_{0}-I)\beta+\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{p}\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma(\Sigma_{0}+\tau_{0}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{0}(\Sigma_{0}+\tau_{0}I)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\beta^{\top}(Q_{0}-I)^{\top}\Sigma(Q_{0}-I)\beta+\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{p}\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma Q_{0}(\Sigma_{0}+\tau_{0}I)^{-1}\right).

This matches the formula for
\mathcal{R}_{t}
in the theorem statement for
t=0
.

2. Inductive Step:

This proof is more direct by unrolling the full recursion. By repeatedly substituting the definition, we express
\widehat{\beta}_{t}^{\text{seq}}
in terms of
\beta
and the full history of noise
\{g_{h}\}_{0\leq h\leq t}
:

\widehat{\beta}_{t}^{\text{seq}}=Q_{t:0}\beta+\sum_{h=0}^{t}\frac{\gamma_{h}^{2}}{\sqrt{p}}Q_{t:h+1}(\Sigma_{h}+\tau_{h}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{h}^{1/2}g_{h}

The deterministic risk
\mathcal{R}_{t}
is the expected risk of this fully-unrolled estimator, where the expectation is over the entire history of independent noise vectors:

\mathcal{R}_{t}=\mathbbm{E}_{\{g_{h}\}_{0\leq h\leq t}}\left[\left\|\Sigma^{1/2}\left((Q_{t:0}-I)\beta+\sum_{h=0}^{t}\frac{\gamma_{h}^{2}}{\sqrt{p}}Q_{t:h+1}(\Sigma_{h}+\tau_{h}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{h}^{1/2}g_{h}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].

Because the noise vectors
\{g_{h}\}_{0\leq h\leq t}
are independent and have zero mean, all cross-terms of the form
\mathbbm{E}[g_{h}^{\top}Ag_{j}
for
h\neq j
are zero. The expectation of the squared norm becomes the squared norm of the bias term plus the sum of the expected squared norms of the noise terms:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{t}

\displaystyle=\|\Sigma^{1/2}(Q_{t:0}-I)\beta\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{h=0}^{t}\frac{\gamma_{h}^{2}}{p}\mathbbm{E}_{g_{h}}\left[\|\Sigma^{1/2}Q_{t:h+1}(\Sigma_{h}+\tau_{h}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{h}^{1/2}g_{h}\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\beta^{\top}(Q_{k:0}-I)^{\top}\Sigma(Q_{k:0}-I)\beta+\sum_{h=0}^{t}\frac{\gamma_{h}^{2}}{p}\operatorname{tr}\left(Q_{k:h+1}^{\top}\Sigma Q_{k:h+1}Q_{h}(\Sigma_{h}+\tau_{h}I)^{-1}\right).

This confirms the expression for
\mathcal{R}_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
.

Part 2: Deterministic Recursion for Effective Noise

The same logic applies to the recursion for the deterministic effective noise,
D_{t}
. The numerator in the fixed-point equation for
1\leq t\leq K
is

N_{t}=\tau_{t}^{2}\|(\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}\widehat{\beta}_{t-1}\|_{2}^{2}.

This is a Lipschitz function of
\widehat{\beta}_{t-1}
. Its deterministic equivalent, which we use to define
D_{t}
, is found by replacing
\widehat{\beta}_{t-1}
with
\widehat{\beta}_{t-1}^{\text{seq}}
and taking the full expectation.

L_{t}D_{t}=\mathbbm{E}_{\{g_{h}\}_{0\leq h\leq t}}\left[\tau_{t}^{2}\|(\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}\widehat{\beta}_{t-1}^{\text{seq}}\|_{2}^{2}\right].

We use the unrolled expression for
\widehat{\beta}_{t-1}^{\text{seq}}
:

\widehat{\beta}_{t-1}^{\text{seq}}=Q_{t-1:0}\beta+\sum_{h=0}^{t-1}\frac{\gamma_{h}^{2}}{\sqrt{p}}Q_{t-1:h+1}(\Sigma_{h}+\tau_{h}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{h}^{1/2}g_{h}.

Substituting this into the expectation, and again using the independence of the
\{g_{h}\}_{0\leq h\leq t}
to eliminate cross-terms, we get:

\displaystyle L_{t}D_{t}=\tau_{t}^{2}\|(\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}Q_{t-1:0}\beta\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{h=0}^{t-1}\frac{\gamma_{h}^{2}}{p}\mathbbm{E}_{g_{h}}\left[\tau_{t}^{2}\|(\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}Q_{t-1:h+1}(\Sigma_{h}+\tau_{h}I)^{-1}\Sigma_{h}^{1/2}g_{h}\|_{2}^{2}\right].

Replacing the random
\gamma_{h}^{2}
with their deterministic equivalents
D_{h}
and simplifying the expressions within the norm and trace gives:

L_{t}D_{t}^{2}=\tau_{t}^{2}\beta^{\top}Q_{t-1:0}^{\top}Q_{t}(\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-1}Q_{t-1:0}\beta+\tau_{t}^{2}\sum\limits_{h=0}^{t-1}\frac{D_{h}^{2}}{p}\operatorname{tr}\left(Q_{t-1:h+1}^{\top}Q_{t}(\Sigma_{t}+\tau_{t}I)^{-1}Q_{t-1:h+1}Q_{h}(\Sigma_{h}+\tau_{h}I)^{-1}\right)

This completes the proof of the recursive formula for the deterministic effective noise. The concentration bounds follow directly from applying Theorem
D.1
at each step.
∎

Appendix E
Proof of Iterated GCV

This section provides the formal proof for the consistency of the Iterated Generalized Cross-Validation (iGCV) estimator. Our proof extends the uniform GCV analysis of
24
to the iterative self-training setting. The primary technical challenge arises from the fact that our estimator at iteration
t
is defined as
\widehat{\beta}_{t}(\lambda)=A_{t}\widehat{\beta}_{0}(\lambda)
, where
A_{t}=P_{t}P_{t-1}\cdots P_{1}
is a cumulative projection operator that introduces a directional dependency not present in standard ridge regression.

Notation and Setup.

Throughout this section, we denote the initial sample covariance matrix as
\widehat{\Sigma}:=\frac{1}{n}X_{0}^{\top}X_{0}
and the population covariance as
\Sigma
. We operate in the proportional asymptotic regime where
n,p\to\infty
such that
p/n\to\rho\in(1,\infty)
. We assume the cumulative projection matrix
A_{t}
is independent of the initial training data
(X_{0},Y_{0})
, which holds by Assumption 2.1. We further define the iterative residual operator
\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)
and the leverage multiplier
M_{t}(\lambda)
as specified in the main text.

Proof Strategy.

The proof is structured as follows:

1.

Bias-Variance Decomposition:
We first decompose both the prediction risk
\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\beta}_{t};\beta)
and the iGCV estimator
\widehat{\text{GCV}}^{(t)}(\lambda)
into their respective bias and variance components. This isolates the deterministic signal-dependent terms from the stochastic noise-driven terms.

2.

Asymptotic Equivalence:
We then demonstrate that the bias of the iGCV estimator asymptotically tracks the prediction bias, and the iGCV variance tracks the prediction variance (up to the irreducible noise level
\sigma^{2}
). This is achieved by showing that the leverage-dependent multiplier
M_{t}(\lambda)
effectively corrects the in-sample bias induced by overparameterization.

3.

Uniformity on
\lambda
:
Finally, we extend the point-wise convergence results to hold uniformly over
\lambda
within any compact interval. This ensures that the GCV profile can be reliably used for hyperparameter optimization and identifying the optimal stopping time
t
.

Lemma E.1

(Asymptotic Risk Decomposition)
.

For any fixed iteration
t\geq 0
and regularization
\lambda\geq 0
, the prediction risk
\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\beta}_{t}(\lambda);\beta)
of the iterative self-training estimator converges almost surely:

\lim_{n,p\to\infty}\left|\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\beta}_{t}(\lambda);\beta)-\left(\mathcal{B}_{t}(\lambda)+\mathcal{V}_{t}(\lambda)\right)\right|=0,,

(22)

where the bias and variance components are defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{t}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\beta^{\top}\left(A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}-I_{p}\right)^{\top}\Sigma\left(A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}-I_{p}\right)\beta,

(23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{t}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mathrm{tr}\left(\Sigma A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}A_{t}^{\top}\right).

(24)

Proof.

To derive the decomposition, we first expand the estimation error
\widehat{\beta}_{t}-\beta
. Recall that
\widehat{\beta}_{t}=A_{t}\widehat{\beta}_{0}
, where
\widehat{\beta}_{0}=(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\frac{1}{n}X_{0}^{\top}Y_{0}
is the initial ridge estimator. Substituting
Y_{0}=X_{0}\beta+E_{0}
, we can write:

\widehat{\beta}_{t}-\beta=\underbrace{\left(A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}-I_{p}\right)\beta}_{\Delta_{b}}+\underbrace{A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\frac{1}{n}X_{0}^{\top}E_{0}}_{\Delta_{v}},

where
\Delta_{b}
represents the bias error vector and
\Delta_{v}
represents the stochastic error vector. The prediction risk is then given by the quadratic form:

\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\beta}_{t};\beta)=(\Delta_{b}+\Delta_{v})^{\top}\Sigma(\Delta_{b}+\Delta_{v})=\Delta_{b}^{\top}\Sigma\Delta_{b}+\Delta_{v}^{\top}\Sigma\Delta_{v}+2\Delta_{b}^{\top}\Sigma\Delta_{v}.

(25)

We now analyze the three terms in the limit
n,p\to\infty
:

1.

Bias Term:
The first term
\Delta_{b}^{\top}\Sigma\Delta_{b}
corresponds exactly to
\mathcal{B}_{t}(\lambda)
.

2.

Variance Term:
For the second term, we take the expectation with respect to the noise
E_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{n})
. Using the cyclic property of the trace and
\widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}X_{0}^{\top}X_{0}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{E_{0}}[\Delta_{v}^{\top}\Sigma\Delta_{v}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathrm{tr}\left(\Sigma A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[E_{0}E_{0}^{\top}]X_{0}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}A_{t}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mathrm{tr}\left(\Sigma A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}A_{t}^{\top}\right).

This concentration follows from standard random matrix theory results for quadratic forms in the overparameterized regime.

3.

Cross Term:
The term
2\Delta_{b}^{\top}\Sigma\Delta_{v}
is linear in
E_{0}
. Since
\mathbb{E}[E_{0}]=0
and
E_{0}
is independent of the signal
\beta
and all feature matrices
\{X_{\tau}\}_{\tau=0}^{t}
, this term converges to zero almost surely as
n\to\infty
by the law of large numbers (see e.g., Lemma 5.1 in
(
24
)
).

Summing these components yields the desired decomposition
\mathcal{B}_{t}(\lambda)+\mathcal{V}_{t}(\lambda)
.
∎

Lemma E.2

(Asymptotic iGCV Decomposition)
.

Define the iterative bias operator
\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and the iterative variance operator
\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\left(I_{p}-A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\right)+M_{t}(\lambda)\left(I_{p}-(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\right),

(26)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\left(I_{n}-\frac{1}{n}X_{0}A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}\right)+M_{t}(\lambda)\left(I_{n}-\frac{1}{n}X_{0}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}\right).

(27)

The iterated GCV estimator
\widehat{\text{GCV}}^{(t)}(\lambda)
converges almost surely to:

\lim_{n,p\to\infty}\left|\widehat{\text{GCV}}^{(t)}(\lambda)-\left(\text{GCV}_{b}^{(t)}(\lambda)+\text{GCV}_{v}^{(t)}(\lambda)\right)\right|=0,

(28)

where the bias and variance components are expressed as:

\displaystyle\text{GCV}_{b}^{(t)}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\beta^{\top}\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}\,\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)\beta,

(29)

\displaystyle\text{GCV}_{v}^{(t)}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mathrm{tr}\left(\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}(\lambda)^{\top}\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}(\lambda)\right).

(30)

Proof.

The proof relies on decomposing the corrected residual vector into signal and noise contributions. The iGCV estimator is defined as the normalized squared norm of the corrected residuals:

\widehat{\text{GCV}}^{(t)}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{n}\left\|(Y_{0}-X_{0}\widehat{\beta}_{t})+M_{t}(\lambda)(Y_{0}-X_{0}\widehat{\beta}_{0})\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(31)

By substituting the data generating process
Y_{0}=X_{0}\beta+E_{0}
and the iterative estimators
\widehat{\beta}_{0}=(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\frac{1}{n}X_{0}^{\top}Y_{0}
and
\widehat{\beta}_{t}=A_{t}\widehat{\beta}_{0}
, we expand the residual vector:

\displaystyle\text{Res}_{\text{corr}}

\displaystyle=(X_{0}\beta+E_{0}-X_{0}A_{t}\widehat{\beta}_{0})+M_{t}(\lambda)(X_{0}\beta+E_{0}-X_{0}\widehat{\beta}_{0})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{X_{0}\left[(I_{p}-A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma})\beta+M_{t}(I_{p}-(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma})\beta\right]}_{\text{Signal Part: }\xi_{b}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\left[(I_{n}-\frac{1}{n}X_{0}A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top})E_{0}+M_{t}(I_{n}-\frac{1}{n}X_{0}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top})E_{0}\right]}_{\text{Noise Part: }\xi_{v}}.

Signal Contribution (
\xi_{b}
).

Setting the noise
E_{0}=0
, the signal residual at iteration
t
corrected by the
t=0
residual is:

\displaystyle\xi_{b}

\displaystyle=X_{0}\left(I_{p}-A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\right)\beta+M_{t}X_{0}\left(I_{p}-(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\right)\beta

\displaystyle=X_{0}\left[\left(I_{p}-A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\right)+M_{t}\left(I_{p}-(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\right)\right]\beta.

Using the definition of
\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)
in (
26
), we have
\xi_{b}=X_{0}\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)\beta
. Its contribution to the GCV profile is:

\frac{1}{n}\|\xi_{b}\|_{2}^{2}=\beta^{\top}\mathcal{O}_{t}^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}X_{0}^{\top}X_{0}\right)\mathcal{O}_{t}\beta=\beta^{\top}\mathcal{O}_{t}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}\mathcal{O}_{t}\beta,

which matches
\text{GCV}_{b}^{(t)}(\lambda)
.

Noise Contribution (
\xi_{v}
).

Setting the signal
\beta=0
, the noise-driven residuals are expressed in the observation space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The corrected noise residual is:

\displaystyle\xi_{v}

\displaystyle=\left(I_{n}-\frac{1}{n}X_{0}A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}\right)E_{0}+M_{t}(\lambda)\left(I_{n}-\frac{1}{n}X_{0}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}\right)E_{0}=\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}(\lambda)E_{0}.

Taking the expectation over the initial noise
E_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{n})
, we apply the concentration of quadratic forms for high-dimensional random vectors:

\frac{1}{n}\|\xi_{v}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}E_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}E_{0}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}).

This trace represents the noise variance component
\text{GCV}_{v}^{(t)}(\lambda)
. Note that
\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}
is not necessarily symmetric since
A_{t}
may not commute with
\widehat{\Sigma}
; thus the product
\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}^{\top}
is required for the general case.

Cross Term.

The cross-term
\frac{2}{n}\xi_{b}^{\top}\xi_{v}=\frac{2}{n}\beta^{\top}\mathcal{O}_{t}^{\top}X_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}E_{0}
is linear in
E_{0}
. Since
\mathbb{E}[E_{0}]=0
and
E_{0}
is independent of
(X_{0},A_{t})
, this term converges to zero almost surely as
n\to\infty
by the law of large numbers. Combining these components yields the desired decomposition.
∎

Lemma E.3

(Asymptotic Equivalence of iGCV Bias)
.

For any fixed iteration
t\geq 1
and
\lambda\geq 0
, the bias component of the iGCV estimator satisfies:

\left|\text{GCV}_{b}^{(t)}(\lambda)-\mathcal{B}_{t}(\lambda)\right|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

(32)

Proof.

Recall that the GCV bias component is defined as
\text{GCV}_{b}^{(t)}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{0,i}^{\top}\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)\beta)^{2}
. We analyze the convergence of the term
\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)^{\top}x_{0,i}
. By definition of the operator
\mathcal{O}_{t}
, we split it into two terms:

\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)^{\top}x_{0,i}=\underbrace{\left(I_{p}-\widehat{\Sigma}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}A_{t}\right)x_{0,i}}_{\text{Term I}}+\underbrace{M_{t}(\lambda)\left(I_{p}-\widehat{\Sigma}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\right)x_{0,i}}_{\text{Term II}}.

(33)

Expansion of Term I.

Substituting
\widehat{\Sigma}=\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+x_{0,i}x_{0,i}^{\top}/n
and applying the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula to
(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}
, we have:

Term I

\displaystyle=x_{0,i}-(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+x_{0,i}x_{0,i}^{\top}/n)(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+x_{0,i}x_{0,i}^{\top}/n+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}A_{t}x_{0,i}

\displaystyle=x_{0,i}-(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+x_{0,i}x_{0,i}^{\top}/n)\left[(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}-\frac{(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}x_{0,i}x_{0,i}^{\top}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}/n}{1+x_{0,i}^{\top}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}x_{0,i}/n}\right]A_{t}x_{0,i}.

Expanding the product of the terms inside the square bracket:

Term I

\displaystyle=x_{0,i}-\left(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}-\frac{\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}x_{0,i}x_{0,i}^{\top}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}/n}{1+x_{0,i}^{\top}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}x_{0,i}/n}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\frac{x_{0,i}x_{0,i}^{\top}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}}{n}-\frac{x_{0,i}x_{0,i}^{\top}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}x_{0,i}x_{0,i}^{\top}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}}{n^{2}(1+x_{0,i}^{\top}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}x_{0,i}/n)}\right)A_{t}x_{0,i}.

Combining the last two terms in the bracket using a common denominator, Term I simplifies to:

Term I

\displaystyle=x_{0,i}-\left(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}+\frac{(I_{p}-\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1})x_{0,i}x_{0,i}^{\top}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}/n}{1+x_{0,i}^{\top}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}x_{0,i}/n}\right)A_{t}x_{0,i}

\displaystyle=\left(I_{p}-\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}A_{t}\right)x_{0,i}-\frac{\left(I_{p}-\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\right)x_{0,i}}{1+x_{0,i}^{\top}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}x_{0,i}/n}\left(\frac{1}{n}x_{0,i}^{\top}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}A_{t}x_{0,i}\right).

Expansion of Term II.

Following a similar Woodbury expansion for Term II, the
x_{0,i}
term interacts with the sample covariance to yield:

\text{Term II}=M_{t}(\lambda)\frac{\left(I_{p}-\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\right)x_{0,i}}{1+x_{0,i}^{\top}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}x_{0,i}/n}.

Synthesis and Convergence.

Summing Term I and Term II, we collect the common factors:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)^{\top}x_{0,i}

\displaystyle=\left(I_{p}-\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}A_{t}\right)x_{0,i}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\left(I_{p}-\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\right)x_{0,i}}{1+x_{0,i}^{\top}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}x_{0,i}/n}\underbrace{\left(M_{t}(\lambda)-\frac{1}{n}x_{0,i}^{\top}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}A_{t}x_{0,i}\right)}_{\delta_{i}}.

By Lemma
F.4
and Lemma
F.1
, the fluctuation term
\delta_{i}
converges to zero almost surely as
n,p\to\infty
. Consequently, the entire correction term vanishes, and we are left with
\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)^{\top}x_{0,i}\xrightarrow{a.s.}(I_{p}-\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}A_{t})x_{0,i}
.

Final Convergence to Prediction Bias.

Since
\max_{i}|\delta_{i}|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0
as shown above, the second term in the expansion of
\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)^{\top}x_{0,i}
is negligible. Let
\Delta_{b,-i}:=\left(I_{p}-A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}\right)\beta
denote the ”leave-one-out” bias vector. We can then approximate the GCV bias component as:

\displaystyle\text{GCV}_{b}^{(t)}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{0,i}^{\top}\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)\beta)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta^{\top}\left(I_{p}-\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}A_{t}^{\top}\right)x_{0,i}x_{0,i}^{\top}\left(I_{p}-A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}\right)\beta+o_{a.s.}(1).

Conditioning on the feature matrices and the signal
\beta
, we apply the concentration of the sum of quadratic forms (Lemma
F.2
). For each term in the sum,
x_{0,i}x_{0,i}^{\top}
is independent of
\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}
and
A_{t}
. Therefore, the sum concentrates around its expectation over
x_{0,i}
:

\displaystyle\text{GCV}_{b}^{(t)}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{a.s.}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma\left(I_{p}-A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}\right)\beta\beta^{\top}\left(I_{p}-\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}A_{t}^{\top}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta^{\top}\left(I_{p}-\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}A_{t}^{\top}\right)\Sigma\left(I_{p}-A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}\right)\beta.

As
n\to\infty
, the leave-one-out sample covariance
\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}
is asymptotically equivalent to the full sample covariance
\widehat{\Sigma}
(since the rank-1 update
x_{0,i}x_{0,i}^{\top}/n
vanishes in the spectral norm). Thus, the average of the
n
identical terms simplifies to:

\text{GCV}_{b}^{(t)}(\lambda)\xrightarrow{a.s.}\beta^{\top}\left(I_{p}-\widehat{\Sigma}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}A_{t}^{\top}\right)\Sigma\left(I_{p}-A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\right)\beta.

Comparing this with the definition of the prediction bias
\mathcal{B}_{t}(\lambda)
in Lemma
E.1
, we conclude:

\left|\text{GCV}_{b}^{(t)}(\lambda)-\mathcal{B}_{t}(\lambda)\right|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0,

Extension to
\lambda=0
and Uniformity.

The pointwise convergence derived above for
\lambda>0
extends to the ridgeless case
\lambda=0
and holds uniformly over any compact interval
I\subset[0,\infty)
. To see this, we follow the argument of
24
. First, we note that for
p>n
, the sample covariance
\widehat{\Sigma}
has at most
n
non-zero eigenvalues. The ridgeless estimator
\widehat{\beta}_{0}(0)
is the limit of
\widehat{\beta}_{0}(\lambda)
as
\lambda\to 0^{+}
.

The core of the uniformity argument lies in the boundedness of the GCV numerator
u_{n}(\lambda)
and denominator
v_{n}(\lambda)
, along with their derivatives. Specifically, the operator
A_{t}
in our iterative scheme is a product of orthogonal projections, satisfying
\|A_{t}\|_{2}\leq 1
. Consequently, the presence of
A_{t}
does not affect the uniform boundedness or the Lipschitz continuity of the risk and GCV functional components with respect to
\lambda
. Following
24
, the uniform boundedness of the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
(Assumption
2.3
) and the strong law of large numbers for
\|y\|^{2}/n
ensure that the family of functions
\{\text{GCV}_{b}^{(t)}(\lambda)\}_{n}
is equicontinuous. By the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, the pointwise convergence implies uniform convergence over
\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{\max}]
, thereby justifying the extension to the ridgeless limit
\lambda=0

∎

Lemma E.4

(Asymptotic Equivalence of iGCV Variance)
.

For any fixed iteration
t\geq 1
and
\lambda\geq 0
, the variance component of the iGCV estimator satisfies:

\left|\text{GCV}_{v}^{(t)}(\lambda)-\mathcal{V}_{t}(\lambda)-\sigma^{2}\right|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

(34)

Proof.

Recall that
\text{GCV}_{v}^{(t)}(\lambda)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}(\lambda)^{\top}\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}(\lambda))
. Using the corrected residual operator
\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}
defined in (
27
) and its symmetry, we expand the trace as:

\displaystyle\text{GCV}_{v}^{(t)}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\operatorname{tr}\left(\left((1+M_{t})I_{n}-\frac{1}{n}X_{0}(A_{t}+M_{t}I)(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}(1+M_{t})\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{\Xi}_{t})-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\operatorname{tr}\left(X_{0}(A_{t}+M_{t}I)(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}\right).

(35)

Step 1: Analysis of the first term.

Using the definition of
\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}
and the linearity of the trace, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{\Xi}_{t})

\displaystyle=(1+M_{t})-\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{1}{n}X_{0}(A_{t}+M_{t}I)(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=(1+M_{t})-\operatorname{tr}\left((A_{t}+M_{t}I)(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\right)/n

\displaystyle=(1+M_{t})-\left[\operatorname{tr}(A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma})/n+M_{t}\operatorname{tr}((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma})/n\right].

Recall from (
11
) that
M_{t}=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma})/n}{1-\operatorname{tr}((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma})/n}
. Substituting this into the above expression, the terms cancel such that
\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{\Xi}_{t})=1
. Thus,
Term A
simplifies to
\sigma^{2}(1+M_{t})
.

Step 2: Trace manipulation and parameter space mapping.

We analyze the second term in (
E
), denoted as Term B. Using the cyclic property of the trace
\operatorname{tr}(ABCD)=\operatorname{tr}(DABC)
, we move
X_{0}^{\top}
to interact with
X_{0}
:

Term B

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\operatorname{tr}\left((A_{t}+M_{t}I)(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{\Xi}_{t}X_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\operatorname{tr}\left((A_{t}+M_{t}I)(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}\left((1+M_{t})X_{0}-\frac{1}{n}X_{0}(A_{t}+M_{t}I)(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}X_{0}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\operatorname{tr}\left((A_{t}+M_{t}I)(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}X_{0}\left((1+M_{t})I_{p}-(A_{t}+M_{t}I)(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\right)\right).

By the definition of the iterative residual operator
\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)
in (
26
), the term in the rightmost bracket is precisely
\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)
. Substituting
\widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}X_{0}^{\top}X_{0}
, we have:

\text{Term B}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\operatorname{tr}\left((A_{t}+M_{t}I)(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)\right).

(36)

Step 3: Decomposition into B1 and B2.

We split Term B into two components based on the operator
(A_{t}+M_{t}I)
:

B1

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\operatorname{tr}\left(A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)\right)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{0,i}^{\top}\mathcal{O}_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}A_{t}x_{0,i},

B2

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}M_{t}}{n}\operatorname{tr}\left((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\mathcal{O}_{t}(\lambda)\right)=\frac{\sigma^{2}M_{t}}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{0,i}^{\top}\mathcal{O}_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}x_{0,i}.

Step 4: Asymptotic limits of B1 and B2.

Applying the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury expansion for
x_{0,i}^{\top}\mathcal{O}_{t}
as derived in the proof of Lemma
E.3
, and leveraging the concentration results from Lemma
F.1
and Lemma
F.4
:

B1

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{a.s.}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\operatorname{tr}\left((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma A_{t}\right)-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\operatorname{tr}\left(A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\sigma^{2}\frac{\operatorname{tr}\left((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma A_{t}\right)\operatorname{tr}\left((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma\right)/n^{2}}{1+\operatorname{tr}\left((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma\right)/n}+\sigma^{2}\frac{\operatorname{tr}\left((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma A_{t}\right)\operatorname{tr}\left(A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\right)/n^{2}}{1+\operatorname{tr}\left((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma\right)/n}.

B2

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{a.s.}\sigma^{2}\frac{\operatorname{tr}\left((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma A_{t}\right)\operatorname{tr}\left((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma\right)/n^{2}}{1+\operatorname{tr}\left((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma\right)/n}-\sigma^{2}\frac{\operatorname{tr}\left((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma A_{t}\right)\operatorname{tr}\left(A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\right)/n^{2}}{1+\operatorname{tr}\left((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma\right)/n}.

Step 5: Final Summation.

Summing Term A, B1, and B2, the complex terms in B1 and B2 involving the multiplier
M_{t}
and the GCV denominators cancel each other exactly. The remaining terms are:

\text{GCV}_{v}^{(t)}(\lambda)\xrightarrow{a.s.}\sigma^{2}(1+M_{t})-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\operatorname{tr}\left((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma A_{t}\right)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\operatorname{tr}\left(A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma}\right).

This expression simplifies to
\mathcal{V}_{t}(\lambda)+\sigma^{2}
, matching the predicted stochastic risk.
∎

E.1
Proof of Theorem 4.5 (Uniform Consistency of iGCV)

In this section, we complete the proof of Theorem
4.3
, which establishes the uniform consistency of the iterated GCV estimator
\widehat{\text{GCV}}^{(t)}(\lambda)
as a proxy for the prediction risk
\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\beta}_{t})
plus the noise floor
\sigma^{2}
.

Proof.

The proof is structured into three main steps: pointwise convergence, uniform boundedness of derivatives, and the application of the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem.

Step 1: Pointwise Convergence.

For any fixed
\lambda\in\mathcal{I}
, we decompose both the iGCV estimator and the prediction risk into their respective bias and variance components using Lemma
E.2
and Lemma
E.1
. Specifically:

\displaystyle\widehat{\text{GCV}}^{(t)}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\text{GCV}_{b}^{(t)}(\lambda)+\text{GCV}_{v}^{(t)}(\lambda)+o_{a.s.}(1),

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\beta}_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{t}(\lambda)+\mathcal{V}_{t}(\lambda).

From Lemma
E.3
(Bias Equivalence), we have
|\text{GCV}_{b}^{(t)}(\lambda)-\mathcal{B}_{t}(\lambda)|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0
. From Lemma
E.4
(Variance Equivalence), we have
|\text{GCV}_{v}^{(t)}(\lambda)-(\mathcal{V}_{t}(\lambda)+\sigma^{2})|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0
. Summing these results, we obtain the pointwise convergence for any fixed
\lambda\in\mathcal{I}
:

\left|\widehat{\text{GCV}}^{(t)}(\lambda)-\left(\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\beta}_{t})+\sigma^{2}\right)\right|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

(37)

Step 2: Equicontinuity and Boundedness.

To elevate the pointwise convergence to uniform convergence over the compact interval
\mathcal{I}
, we show that the sequence of functions
f_{n}(\lambda)=\widehat{\text{GCV}}^{(t)}(\lambda)
is equicontinuous. Following the approach in
24
, it suffices to show that the derivative
|\frac{d}{d\lambda}\widehat{\text{GCV}}^{(t)}(\lambda)|
is uniformly bounded on
\mathcal{I}
.

The iGCV estimator is a ratio of quadratic forms
u_{n}(\lambda)/v_{n}(\lambda)
. As shown in the proofs of Lemma
E.3
and
E.4
, the presence of the iterative operator
A_{t}
modifies the spectral filtering. However, since
A_{t}
is a product of orthogonal projections, its operator norm is bounded by unity (
\|A_{t}\|_{2}\leq 1
). Under Assumption 2.3, the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
and
\widehat{\Sigma}
are bounded away from zero and infinity almost surely for sufficiently large
n
. Thus, the numerator
u_{n}(\lambda)
and denominator
v_{n}(\lambda)
, as well as their derivatives with respect to
\lambda
, remain uniformly bounded on any compact interval
\mathcal{I}\subset[0,\infty]
. Specifically:

\left|\frac{d}{d\lambda}\widehat{\text{GCV}}^{(t)}(\lambda)\right|\leq C\frac{\|Y_{0}\|^{2}}{n}\left(\frac{s_{\max}+\lambda}{s_{\min}+\lambda}\right)^{k},

for some constant
C
and power
k
. Since
\|Y_{0}\|^{2}/n
is almost surely bounded by the strong law of large numbers, the family
\{\widehat{\text{GCV}}^{(t)}(\lambda)\}_{n}
is Lipschitz continuous with a uniform Lipschitz constant, which implies equicontinuity.

Step 3: Uniform Convergence.

Since
\mathcal{I}
is a compact interval and (
37
) establishes pointwise convergence for a sequence of equicontinuous functions, the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem implies that the convergence is uniform over
\mathcal{I}
:

\sup_{\lambda\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\widehat{\text{GCV}}^{(t)}(\lambda)-\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\beta}_{t})-\sigma^{2}\right|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

This uniform consistency ensures that the optimal regularization
\lambda^{*}
and the optimal stopping time
t
identified by minimizing
\widehat{\text{GCV}}^{(t)}(\lambda)
are asymptotically equivalent to those that minimize the true prediction risk.
∎

Appendix F
Technical Lemmas

In this section, we collect several established results from high-dimensional probability and random matrix theory that are instrumental in our proofs.

Lemma F.1

(Concentration of maximum of quadratic forms, adapted from
(
15
;
1
)
)
.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
be random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
that satisfy Assumptions 2.2 and 2.3. Let
G_{1},\dots,G_{n}
be random matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
such that each
G_{i}
is independent of
x_{i}
(but may depend on all other
x_{j},j\neq i
) and has operator norm uniformly bounded in
n
. Then as
n,p\to\infty
:

\max_{i=1,\dots,n}\left|\frac{1}{n}x_{i}^{\top}G_{i}x_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}(G_{i}\Sigma)\right|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

(38)

Lemma F.2

(Concentration of sum of quadratic forms, adapted from
(
25
)
)
.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
be random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
that satisfy Assumptions 2.2 and 2.3. Let
H_{1},\dots,H_{n}
be random matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
such that each
H_{i}
is independent of
x_{i}
and has trace norm uniformly bounded in
n
. Then as
n,p\to\infty
:

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\top}H_{i}x_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{tr}(H_{i}\Sigma)\right|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

(39)

Lemma F.3

(Basic GCV denominator lemma, adapted from
(
24
)
)
.

Under the proportional asymptotic regime
n,p\to\infty,p/n\to\rho>1
, for any
\lambda>0
:

1-\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}\left(\widehat{\Sigma}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\right)\xrightarrow{a.s.}\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\right)},

(40)

where
\widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}X_{0}^{\top}X_{0}
and
\Sigma
is the population covariance.

Lemma F.4

(Asymptotic Convergence of the Leverage Multiplier)
.

The leverage-dependent multiplier
M_{t}(\lambda)
converges almost surely:

M_{t}(\lambda)-\operatorname{tr}\left(A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\Sigma\right)/n\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

Proof.

By definition (
11
), we express the multiplier as a ratio of traces:

M_{t}(\lambda)=\frac{\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}\big(X_{0}A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}\big)}{1-\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}\big(X_{0}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}X_{0}^{\top}\big)}=:\frac{\mathcal{N}_{n}}{\mathcal{D}_{n}}.

Analysis of the Numerator
\mathcal{N}_{n}
:

Expanding the trace as a sum of quadratic forms, we have
\mathcal{N}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{0,i}^{\top}A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}x_{0,i}
. To handle the dependence between
x_{0,i}
and
\widehat{\Sigma}
, we apply the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{0,i}^{\top}A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}x_{0,i}}{1+\frac{1}{n}x_{0,i}^{\top}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{-i}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}x_{0,i}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{a.s.}\frac{\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}(A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\Sigma)}{1+\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\Sigma)},

where the numerator and denominator of the summand converge almost surely by applying Lemma
F.1
and Lemma
F.2
respectively, leveraging the independence of
A_{t}
from
X_{0}
.

Analysis of the Denominator
\mathcal{D}_{n}
:

Using Lemma
F.3
, we have:

\mathcal{D}_{n}=1-\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\widehat{\Sigma})\xrightarrow{a.s.}\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\Sigma)}.

Combining Results:

Dividing the limits of
\mathcal{N}_{n}
by
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, the common factor
(1+\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}((\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\Sigma))^{-1}
in the denominators cancels out, yielding:

M_{t}(\lambda)\xrightarrow{a.s.}\operatorname{tr}\left(A_{t}(\widehat{\Sigma}+\lambda I_{p})^{-1}\Sigma\right)/n.

This concludes the proof.
∎
