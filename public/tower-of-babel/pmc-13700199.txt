Effectiveness of the “Level-Points” online training model and influencing factors in enhancing scoring accuracy among surgical examiners
Authors: Lina Zhang, Mengling Yan, Yiwen Qiu, Haolei Cai, Yunxian Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnsuring a standardized level of scoring capability among surgical examiners is crucial for the fairness and validity of competency-based assessments in standardized residency training. However, traditional in-person training models face significant challenges in scalability and accessibility. The aim of this study was to evaluate the “Level-Points” online training model for enhancing scoring accuracy among surgical examiners and identify its key influencing factors.MethodsThis study included data from the 2024 national surgical examiner training program. The training adopted a model integrating level-based progression and a point-reward system, designed in accordance with the national surgical resident competency assessment standards and covering five dimensions. Both before and after the training, the examiners were required to rate the five dimensions in the clinical scenario videos, with scoring deviation and improvement values as the outcomes.ResultsA total of 670 surgical examiners were included. The mean dimensional scoring deviation decreased 3.82 points from 19.34 (pre-assessment) to 15.52 (post-assessment) (P < 0.001, adjusted P < 0.001), with statistically significant improvements consistently observed across all five dimensions (All adjusted P < 0.001). Effect sizes ranged from − 0.41 to -1.15 (Cohen’s d), with the overall effect size being large (d = -1.09). For baseline scoring deviations, significant associations were found only with examiners’ specialty background after FDR correction. For training effectiveness, none of the variables showed statistically significant differences after FDR correction. In dimension-specific analyses, four associations remained statistically significant: plastic surgery in the patient encounter (β = -3.345, se = 1.065, P = 0.002, adjusted P = 0.012) and specialty-specific clinical reasoning (β = 3.915, se = 0.896, P < 0.001, adjusted P < 0.001) dimensions, cardiothoracic surgery in the patient encounter dimension (β = -1.996, se = 0.834, P = 0.017, adjusted P = 0.047), and neurosurgery in the communication skills dimension (β = 4.400, se = 0.933, P < 0.001, adjusted P < 0.001). Professional title and geographic region exhibited moderating effects in specific dimensions but did not survive FDR correction. No significant impact on improvement was observed for age or gender (all adjusted P > 0.05).ConclusionThe “Level-Points” online training model effectively enhanced scoring accuracy among surgical examiners, demonstrating significant scalability advantages. Specialty background emerged as a key factor influencing both baseline scoring deviation and dimension-specific improvements, highlighting the need for refined, discipline-sensitive calibration strategies within standardized assessment frameworks.

Introduction

In the competency-focused standardized residency training model for surgeons, the core objective is to cultivate qualified surgical physicians capable of independent medical practice. To this end, the training system is increasingly strengthening the assessment of trainees’ clinical practical competence in surgery [1–4].The adoption of standardized assessment tools is crucial for ensuring the fairness and validity of evaluations of surgical clinical practical skills [5]. In China, the National Health Commission actively promoted the establishment of assessment standards for surgical specialties and developed a standardized, comparable system of assessment tools for surgical clinical competence in 2023 [6]. However, standardized tools do not automatically yield uniform judgments [7]. The variation in examiner stringency is a widely recognized issue in assessments [8]. Such inter-rater variability significantly undermines the reliability and fairness of high-stakes examinations [9].

While large-scale examiner training represents a feasible approach to mitigate individual scoring variations [10], conventional in-person models face logistical constraints in time, cost, and scalability. This challenge transcends national boundaries, as evidenced by ongoing difficulties in ensuring consistent milestone evaluations even within the U.S. competency-based system [11, 12]. Various alternatives have been explored: video-based score comparison methods lack interactive engagement [13]; and remote calibration platforms, while promising, often adopt passive formats with limited feedback and are rarely evaluated across multiple specialties [14, 15]. These gaps highlight the need for a more engaging, scalable, and specialty-sensitive training model.

Our research team developed and implemented a “Level-Points” online training model for surgical examiners. This study aims to evaluate the model’s effectiveness in improving scoring accuracy in skill assessments, identify key factors influencing its efficacy, and ultimately propose evidence-based solutions within a Chinese context to address this global challenge.

Methods

Participants

This study analyzed data from the 2024 national surgical examiner training program. Participants met the following inclusion criteria: (1) holding the title of attending physician or higher; (2) serving as examiners for the clinical practice proficiency certification in surgical residency training; (3) completing all required courses within the designated timeframe.

Training design

Assessment standards and dimensions

The assessment criteria of this training system were established based on the “Clinical Practice Competency Assessment Standards for Completion of Standardized Training for Resident Physicians in China (2023 Edition) (Surgery and Surgical Subspecialties).” Five dimensions are defined: (1) patient encounter; (2) specialty-specific clinical reasoning; (3) general surgery clinical reasoning; (4) clinical procedural skills; (5) communication skills. Each dimension employs distinct, content-tailored clinical scenario videos as assessment materials. In the patient encounter dimension, the video shows the resident conducting history-taking and physical examination with a standardized patient, and requires the resident to make an initial clinical judgment based on the findings. In the clinical reasoning dimension, the video sequentially presents structured clinical data including patient history, physical examination findings, laboratory results, and imaging pictures in accordance with the pace of the resident’s questioning. The resident is then required to synthesize the information and present the case characteristics, diagnosis with supporting evidence, differential diagnoses, and management plan. In the clinical procedural skills dimension, the video captures the resident performing the required procedure on a model such as a mannequin, simulator, animal specimen, or simulated alternative. During the procedure, the resident is expected to verbalize each step and respond to related questions. In the communication skills dimension, clinical scenarios are designed around surgical cases, with standardized patients engaging the resident in conversations covering breaking bad news, informed consent, preoperative discussion, and discharge counseling.

Examiners evaluate residents’ video-recorded performance using the scoring rubrics specified in the assessment standards, assigning scores on a 100-point scale. The scoring rubrics were accompanied by a detailed deduction reference sheet, developed by the expert committee, which specified the number of points to be deducted for each type of error or omission across all rubric items. To establish the reference standard, five surgical experts, who participated in developing the assessment standards and represented all subspecialties included in this study, independently reviewed each video case and scored every rubric item with reference to both the scoring rubrics and the deduction sheet. The reference score for each item was calculated as the mean of the five experts’ ratings. Inter-rater reliability, assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on item-level ratings, ranged from 0.82 to 0.92 across the five competency dimensions (all > 0.75), indicating good consistency among the expert panel.

Video-based assessment materials

All videos were produced specifically for this project. A standardized multi-camera filming protocol was employed to ensure complete visualization of procedural details. With the exception of the specialty-specific clinical reasoning dimension, all assessment videos used in the other four dimensions were selected from general surgery cases in the national standardized examination bank, rather than being tailored to any specific surgical subspecialty. A total of 45 videos were recorded across the five competency dimensions, comprising 8 pre-assessment videos (one each for the patient encounter, general surgery clinical reasoning, clinical procedural skills, and communication skills dimensions, and four for the specialty-specific clinical reasoning dimension, covering neurosurgery, cardiothoracic surgery, urology, and plastic surgery cases respectively; examiners were required to select one case from the four specialty-specific videos to score), 8 post-assessment videos (with the same structure as the pre-assessment videos), and 29 training videos (no fewer than 4 per dimension). The pre- and post-assessment videos were designed as parallel versions, featuring similar clinical scenarios, comparable complexity, and equivalent difficulty, but performed by different resident physicians to prevent memorization effects. The equivalence of the two video sets in terms of scenario complexity, case mix, and assessment duration was verified by the five-member expert panel. Content validity was established through expert consensus: each video was independently reviewed to confirm that the clinical scenario was clearly presented, the rubric items were fully observable, and the performance level was appropriate for assessment purposes. All videos were standardized with respect to recording quality, duration, and presentation format to ensure consistent viewing conditions.

Training platform

The training was delivered through a custom-built online platform developed specifically for this program. The platform features a modular dashboard interface presenting the five competency dimensions as sequentially arranged independent modules. Each module contains video-based assessment materials and an integrated scoring interface that allows examiners to rate performance according to the national rubrics. The platform provides real-time feedback on scoring deviations (both numerical and visual trend lines) and tracks learners’ progress, accumulated points, and deviation trends through a personalized dashboard. The training program was conducted in three sequential phases, as detailed below.

Phase 1: Pre-assessment

After registration, the system automatically pushes the pre-assessment tasks to the examiner. The examiner must complete the scoring independently. The system calculates the scoring deviation in real time as the baseline competency assessment metric.

Phase 2: Level-based and point-reward training

The "Level-Points" training model integrates two complementary mechanisms. The level-based progression design adopts the concept of "game levels," dividing the five competency dimensions into sequentially unlocked independent modules. Each module is displayed as a "level" on the dashboard, with unlockable modules indicated by an unlocked icon and locked modules shown as a locked icon. Examiners must meet the clearance requirements of the current dimension to unlock and proceed to the next dimension. The point-reward system requires examiners to earn base points through accurate scoring performance (e.g., 2 points for a deviation within 15 points from the reference score; 1 point for a deviation within 25 points). Points are awarded instantly upon score submission and presented as numerical scores on the dashboard. Additional points can be acquired by engaging in extended learning activities, such as repeated practice with supplementary videos. A minimum of 2 cumulative points must be achieved in the current dimension to unlock the subsequent dimension, thereby realizing the synergistic linkage between the level-clearance and point-reward mechanisms. The system automatically unlocks the next module once the requirement is met; otherwise, it prompts the examiner to continue practicing.

Phase 3: Post-assessment

At the end of the two-week learning period, the system uniformly activates the post-assessment module. This part employs video case studies that are parallel to the pre-assessment but feature entirely new content. All examiners are required to complete the five dimensions' scoring tasks within the specified timeframe. The platform then automatically calculates the pre-to-post changes in scoring deviations as the final measure of training effectiveness.

Definition of outcome

Scoring deviation for each dimension is defined as the sum of absolute differences between the examiner’s score and the reference score across all items within that dimension. The pre-assessment overall deviation score is calculated as the average of the five dimension-specific deviations; the post-assessment overall deviation score is calculated analogously. The scoring deviation is an inverse indicator, where lower values indicate higher scoring accuracy of the examiners. The deviation improvement value is defined as the pre-assessment overall deviation score minus the post-assessment overall deviation score. It is a positive indicator, where a larger value signifies a more significant improvement.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD), while categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage (N, %). The effectiveness of the training, both overall and across the five dimensions, was assessed using paired-sample t-tests to compare pre- and post-training scores. Cohen’s d is calculated for an estimation of efect sizes for group comparisons. It can be interpreted according to Cohen’s (1988) criteria [16]. To explore factors associated with baseline assessment scores, a multivariable linear regression model was employed, with all variables listed in Table 3 (age, gender, professional title, specialty, and region) entered simultaneously. To identify influencing factors of training effectiveness (Table 4), a separate multivariable linear regression model was used, including all variables shown in Table 4. Results are presented as adjusted regression coefficients (β) with corresponding standard errors (se). Multiple linear regression models were constructed for each dimension to compare the determinants of training effectiveness across the five dimensions, and the results were visualized in forest plots. Given the multiple hypothesis tests in this study, the False Discovery Rate (FDR) was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method, and adjusted P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical tests were two-sided. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1).

Results

Characteristics of examiners

A total of 670 surgical examiners were ultimately included in this study. The mean age of examiners was 43.69 ± 5.99 years, with an age range of 31 to 62 years. 89.9% examiners were male. The proportion of professional title was 16.3% chief physicians, 53.4% associate chief physicians, 30.3% attending physicians. The specialty distribution of general surgery, urology, cardiothoracic surgery, neurosurgery and plastic surgery was 37.8%, 16.9%, 16.0%, 13.0% and 8.5%, respectively; while other specialties (including emergency medicine, dermatology, etc.) was only 7.9%. Geographical distribution showed that the majority of examiners were from Central and Western China (39.5%), followed by Eastern (21.8%) and Southern (21.9%); Northern and Northeast accounted for 13.9% and 2.8%, respectively. Training effectiveness: comparison the deviation score before and after training

As shown in Table 2, scoring deviation improved significantly after the training. The overall mean scoring deviation declined from 19.34 ± 3.60 to 15.52 ± 3.42 (P < 0.001, adjusted P < 0.001), with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = -1.09, 95% CI: -1.17 to -1.00). Statistically significant reductions were consistently observed across all five assessment dimensions (all P < 0.001, all adjusted P < 0.001), with effect sizes ranging from small-to-medium to large (Cohen’s d: -0.41 to -1.15). Among all dimensions, the patient encounter dimension demonstrated the most substantial improvement (6.83 points, Cohen’s d = -1.15, large effect). After training, the communication skills dimension showed the lowest scoring deviation (14.03 points).

Analysis of factors influencing performance in the five dimensions

The results indicated that improvement in the remaining four dimensions was influenced by specialty background, except for the general surgery clinical reasoning dimension, while professional title and regional factors also demonstrated moderating effects in specific dimensions. After FDR correction, only plastic surgery in the patient encounter dimension (β = -3.345, se = 1.065, P = 0.002, adjusted P = 0.012) (Fig. 1A), cardiothoracic surgery in the patient encounter dimension (β = -1.996, se = 0.834, P = 0.017, adjusted P = 0.047) (Fig. 1A), plastic surgery in the specialty-specific clinical reasoning dimension (β = 3.915, se = 0.896, P < 0.001, adjusted P < 0.001) (Fig. 1B), and neurosurgery in the communication skills dimension (β = 4.400, se = 0.933, P < 0.001, adjusted P < 0.001) (Fig. 1E) remained statistically significant. All other previously suggested associations, including associate chief physicians in the patient encounter dimension, Southern China in the specialty-specific clinical reasoning dimension, and cardiothoracic surgery in the clinical procedural skills dimension, did not survive FDR correction (all adjusted P > 0.05). Neither gender nor age showed significance in any dimension (all adjusted P > 0.05) (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the “Level-Points” online training model significantly improves the scoring accuracy of surgical examiners in a national-scale assessment context. The mean total scoring deviation decreased by 3.82 points (from 19.34 to 15.52, P < 0.001, adjusted P < 0.001). Improvement was consistently observed across all five assessment dimensions (all adjusted P < 0.001). The large overall effect size (Cohen’s d = -1.09) and the dimension-specific effect sizes ranging from small-to-medium to large further support the educational significance of these improvements. All interpretations below are grounded in the quantitative findings presented in the Results section and are discussed with due consideration of the study’s exploratory nature.

The effectiveness of our intervention can be explained through two complementary theoretical frameworks. First, Self-Determination Theory (SDT) posits that competence, autonomy, and relatedness drive engagement [17]. Our tiered unlocking mechanism and point-reward system address learners’ need for competence through scaffolded learning progression coupled with immediate feedback and tangible recognition, while the flexible two-week learning period upholds learner-centered principles by allowing participants to autonomously decide when and where to engage in the training [18]. Second, the model embodies the principles of mastery learning [19, 20], in which learners progress only after demonstrating proficiency at each checkpoint, ensuring baseline competency prior to advancement. Unlike passive video-review approaches commonly used in assessor calibration [13], our model actively reinforced accurate scoring behavior through behavioral contingencies, thereby promoting deeper cognitive processing of rubric criteria. This aligns with recent calls to integrate motivational design into health professions education technology [21].

Our “Level-Points” model differs from previous examiner training approaches in two key respects. First, whereas most video-based calibration methods rely on passive review of benchmarking scores [13], our model actively engages examiners through level-based progression and point rewards, drawing on gamification principles to promote sustained motivation and self-regulated learning. Second, unlike time- and resource-intensive in-person workshops [10], the online, self-paced format of our program allows simultaneous training of 670 geographically dispersed examiners, demonstrating its scalability for national-level implementation. These distinctive features position the “Level-Points” model as a scalable and engaging alternative to existing examiner training approaches.

Further analysis reveals that specialty background is a key factor influencing baseline scoring deviation. Prior to intervention, after FDR correction, neurosurgery (mean deviation = 20.11) and urology (19.06) exhibited significantly higher deviations than general surgery (18.77; adjusted P = 0.012 and adjusted P < 0.001, respectively), suggesting that specialty-specific cognitive schemas influence how raters interpret and apply generic rubrics [22]. This aligns with Yeates et al.’s [23] argument that different examiners focus on disparate dimensions when evaluating candidate performance, and is also highly consistent with Park et al.’s [24] call for establishing a rater qualification framework. Notably, specialty background also emerged as the primary factor influencing improvements in specific dimensions. After FDR correction, four associations remained statistically significant: plastic surgery in the patient encounter and specialty-specific clinical reasoning dimensions, cardiothoracic surgery in the patient encounter dimension, and neurosurgery in the communication skills dimension. The observation that plastic surgery examiners showed significantly less improvement in patient encounter scoring (β = -3.345, se = 1.065, P = 0.002, adjusted P = 0.012) but significantly greater improvement in specialty-specific clinical reasoning (β = 3.915, se = 0.896, P < 0.001, adjusted P < 0.001) suggests that the nature of training effects may vary qualitatively across dimensions, possibly reflecting the different cognitive demands and content familiarity associated with each dimension. The finding that cardiothoracic surgery examiners showed significantly less improvement in patient encounter scoring (β = -1.996, se = 0.834, P = 0.017, adjusted P = 0.047) may reflect the highly contextualized, emergency-oriented nature of cardiothoracic practice, where communication and patient interaction patterns may differ from the standardized encounter formats captured in the assessment videos [25]. Conversely, the significant improvement observed in neurosurgery examiners in the communication skills dimension (β = 4.400, se = 0.933, P < 0.001, adjusted P < 0.001) may reflect the protocol-driven, structured nature of neurosurgical practice, which may facilitate alignment with standardized rubrics [26]. It should be noted that, with the exception of the specialty-specific clinical reasoning dimension which included subspecialty-specific cases, all examiners in the other four dimensions were required to score the same set of videos using the same national rubrics, with a detailed deduction reference sheet published by the expert committee provided as a supplementary reference. This design aligns with the actual examination context, where examiners are expected to assess residents’ competence against standardized criteria independent of their specialty affiliation. Therefore, the observed specialty-specific differences in baseline scores and dimension-specific improvements are more likely to reflect variations in how examiners from different specialties interpret and apply the rubrics.

Although professional title and region were not significant predictors of overall training effectiveness after FDR correction, certain moderating effects were observed in specific dimensions, though none retained statistical significance after FDR adjustment. For instance, associate chief physicians showed a pattern of less improvement in patient encounter scoring than attending physicians (β = -1.565, se = 0.785, P = 0.047, adjusted P = 0.268), possibly reflecting entrenched “expert intuition” that may resist standardized frameworks [27], though this observation requires confirmation. Similarly, examiners from Southern China demonstrated a pattern of lower gains in specialty-specific clinical reasoning (β = -1.350, se = 0.686, P = 0.049, adjusted P = 0.224), potentially due to regional differences in medical education traditions or resource allocation [28], but this finding did not survive FDR correction and should be interpreted with caution. The absence of significant effects for professional title and region after FDR correction may indicate that these factors do not exert robust influences on training effectiveness, or that the present study was not sufficiently powered to detect such effects in subgroup analyses.

In terms of overall training effectiveness (improvement in total deviation score), although neurosurgery and cardiothoracic surgery showed significant associations based on raw P-values (P = 0.038 and P = 0.039, respectively), neither remained significant after FDR correction (adjusted P > 0.05 for both). This finding suggests that, at the overall level, the training model may be broadly effective across specialty backgrounds, with no single specialty demonstrating a reliably superior or inferior response after controlling for multiple comparisons. This is an encouraging finding for the scalability of the model, as it implies that the training may be generalizable across different surgical specialties.

These findings suggest several practical implications for building a national, scalable examiner calibration system. First, our data confirm that remote training can achieve standardized calibration among geographically dispersed examiner panels, addressing a critical evidence gap in the field of digital rater preparation [14, 15]. This advancement aligns with the World Federation for Medical Education’s (WFME) recommendation that examiner training should be mandatory and evidence-based [29]. Second, the “Level-Points” model requires only internet access, substantially lowering implementation barriers and enabling equitable dissemination of high-quality assessment resources to grassroots and remote settings, thereby promoting educational equity [30]. Third, baseline deviation scores can be used to identify “high-variability” examiners, providing a basis for targeted remediation and supporting a risk-stratified approach to assessor certification. Fourth, the observed heterogeneity in training outcomes across different groups suggests that examiner training can be innovated toward greater intelligence and precision. In the future, AI technologies could be deeply integrated to develop an intelligent training platform capable of real-time scoring analysis, personalized learning pathways, and predictive interventions, thereby enabling a paradigm shift from standardization to intelligence and from uniformity to personalization [31].

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged in this study. First, the absence of a control group precludes definitive causal inference, and the observed improvements may partly reflect test-retest effects. Nevertheless, the magnitude and domain-specific patterns of change suggest a genuine training effect. Second, the long-term retention of training effects and their transferability to real-world OSCE settings remain to be verified. Third, although this study used general surgical cases to mirror the actual examination context, the potential influence of content familiarity on scoring performance cannot be entirely ruled out; future studies could incorporate specialty-matched video cases to further disentangle calibration differences from content familiarity effects. Fourth, while the point-reward system was designed to enhance calibration, we acknowledge that it may inadvertently encourage conformity to reference scores rather than independent judgment; this potential influence on examiner autonomy and assessment behavior, though partially mitigated by the model’s design features, was not empirically examined in this study and warrants further investigation. Fifth, information regarding prior examiner experience, previous assessor training, and years of involvement in competency assessments was not collected; these factors may influence both baseline scoring accuracy and responsiveness to training and should be considered as potential confounders. Future studies should systematically collect and adjust for these variables to strengthen the robustness of the findings. Sixth, this study did not conduct a systematic accounting of the development, operation, and implementation costs of the training system, including direct costs such as platform construction, video production, expert review, and technical support, as well as indirect costs such as examiners’ time for participation; future research should integrate cost-benefit analyses to comprehensively evaluate the economic feasibility of this model, providing evidence for its dissemination in resource-limited settings.

Conclusion

In summary, the findings of this study suggest that the “Level-Points” online training model may effectively enhance scoring accuracy among surgical examiners and offer scalability advantages over traditional offline training. Furthermore, the results indicate that examiners’ specialty backgrounds may serve as an important factor influencing both baseline scoring deviation and post-training improvement. However, given the exploratory nature of this study and the absence of a control group, these observations should be interpreted with caution and warrant confirmation in future hypothesis-driven research.
