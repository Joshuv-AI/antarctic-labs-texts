Glyceamic variability is a pridictor of in-hopital and long-term mortality of patients with dilated cardiomyopathy: a 5-year retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yang Liu, Qiwei Shen, Hongyu Liu, Gregory Y. H. Lip, Kui Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a major cause of heart failure with high morbidity and mortality, yet reliable prognostic markers remain limited. Glycaemic variability (GV), reflecting fluctuations in glucose levels, has been linked to adverse cardiovascular outcomes, but its role in DCM remains uncertain.MethodsData were obtained from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston. We included patients with DCM as the primary diagnosis and ≥ 3 glucose measurements during hospitalization. GV was defined as the natural logarithm of the standard deviation-to-mean glucose ratio and analyzed continuously and categorically (GV ≥ 2.9 vs. <2.9). Outcomes included in-hospital, and 1-, 3-, and 5-year all-cause mortalities. Multivariable Cox regression models were adjusted for demographic, clinical, and laboratory covariates.ResultsA total of 580 patients with DCM were included (mean age 64.20 (14.80); 73% male; 55% White). Patients with GV ≥ 2.9 had higher rates of in-hospital mortality (5.6% vs. 0.7%), ICU admission (52% vs. 28%) and worse long-term outcomes, including 1-year (20% vs. 10%), 3-year (26% vs. 17%), and 5-year (29% vs. 19%) mortalities. After adjusting for demographic, clinical, and laboratory variables, Each unit increase in GV increased the risk of in-hospital [adjusted OR(aOR): 5.12, 95% CI: 1.07-31.2], ICU admission (aOR 2.59),1-year [adjusted HR (aHR) 1.84], 3-year (aHR 1.65), and 5-year (aHR 1.60) death.ConclusionGV is associated with both short- and long-term mortality in patients with DCM. The cohort median GV value of 2.9 might help stratify patients according to clinical risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06338-y.

Introduction

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a clinically and genetically heterogeneous myocardial disease characterized by left ventricular or biventricular dilation and impaired systolic function, often leading to heart failure [1]. DCM represents one of the leading causes of heart failure in younger adults and remains a major indication for heart transplantation [1]. Although natriuretic peptides are widely used as a biomarker for diagnosis and prognosis [1, 2], their ability to predict long-term outcomes in patients with DCM is limited. This highlights the need for novel in-hospital biomarkers with stronger prognostic value to guide risk stratification, support personalized treatment and improve outcomes.

While chronic hyperglycaemia is a well-established risk factor for cardiovascular complications, recent evidence suggests that acute glucose fluctuations may also play a critical role in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular events [3, 4]. Glycaemic variability (GV), defined as fluctuations in blood glucose levels over time referred to glucose homoeostasis, has emerged as an independent risk factor for diabetic complications [5, 6]. These pathophysiological changes are known to exacerbate reactive oxygen species production and vascular damage [7], which are highly relevant to cardiovascular disease.

Despite its recognized biological importance, the prognostic significance of GV in DCM has not been investigated, especially in individuals without diabetes. This study aimed to evaluate the association between GV and both short- and long-term mortalities in hospitalized patients with DCM, thereby offering a new avenue for risk stratification and personalized treatment approaches.

Methods

Data source

The Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV is a large de-identified dataset of patients admitted to the emergency department or an intensive care unit at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, MA. MIMIC-IV contains data for over 65,000 patients [8, 9]. An approved researcher, Yang Liu (CITI No. 12163712), extracted study data from MIMIC-IV (v 3.1) PhysioNet (https://physionet.org/content/mimiciv/3.1/). The code for data query and extraction is available from the MIMIC Code Repository (https://github.com/MIT-LCP/mimic-code).

Study population

The population of interest in this study was identified from the health record system in the MIMIC-IV database v3.1. Patients with DCM, identified by ICD-10 code I42.0 as the primary diagnosis, and with at least three glucose measurements during hospitalization, were included in the study. At baseline, data on age, sex, race, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, chronic pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD), diabetes, hyperlipidemia, atrial fibrillation, circulatory shock, mechanical ventilation, medications, and key laboratory parameters were collected during hospitalization. Deaths occurring during hospitalization and after discharge were recorded during follow-up. Patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy or missing information on any of the above variables were excluded from the analysis. To minimize the potential influence of extreme monitoring frequency on GV estimation, patients with more than 99th percentile of glucose measurement counts, were excluded. The ICD codes were shown in detail in Supplementary Table 1.

Glycaemic variability and outcomes

GV was calculated by taking the natural logarithm of the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean blood glucose level value [10]. GV was calculated using real-time blood glucose records collected during routine clinical care throughout hospitalization. Given the real-world nature of this retrospective study, the frequency and timing of glucose measurements were not standardized but were determined by physicians according to each patient’s clinical condition [3, 11]. The outcomes were short-term mortality (in-hospital mortality), ICU admission and long-term mortality (1-year, 3-year, and 5-year mortalities). ICU admission was defined as whether patients were admitted to the ICU during the hospitalization.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are represented by means ± standard deviations (SDs), and categorical variables were represented by frequencies and percentages (n (%)). The length of hospital stay was abnormally distributed and represented by the median [interquartile range (IQR)]. The participants were divided by the median GV (2.9). The cumulative mortality rates of patients with GVs < 2.9 and those with GVs ≥ 2.9 were compared using the chi-square test.

Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves were used for survival analysis, and differences were estimated with the log-rank test. For descriptive and secondary categorical analyses, participants were divided according to the cohort median GV (2.9). This value was used as an internal cohort-specific stratification point rather than a validated clinical threshold. To assess the association between GV and in-hospital mortality, multivariate logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To evaluate the association between GV (as continuous variable) and clinical outcomes, R estricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were used to explore the nonlinear relationships between GV and outcomes [12]. Reasonable knots were selected according to the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The knots were set as 3. Reference was set as median of GV (2.9).

In addition, a multivariate Cox proportional hazard was applied for survival analysis to compare the associations between adverse outcomes and GV of different levels. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were calculated for indicators by comparing patients with GV < 2.9 and those with GV ≥ 2.9, as shown in the table and forest plot. To evaluate the independent associations between GV and clinical outcomes, two multivariate Cox regression models were constructed. Model 1 was adjusted for basic demographic variables, including age, sex, race (White vs. non-White), and BMI. Model 2 was further adjusted for clinical comorbidities, including hypertension, chronic pulmonary disease, CKD, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, atrial fibrillation (AF), valvular heart disease and myocarditis; medication use during hospitalization, including β-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/ angiotensin receptor blockers/angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ACEI/ARB/ ARNI), calcium channel blockers (CCBs), amiodarone, statins, inotropes (dopamine, dobutamine, epinephrine, milrinone, or digoxin), SGLT2 inhibitors, metformin and insulin; and key laboratory parameters, including anion gap, platelet count, red blood cell (RBC), white blood cell (WBC), and glucose level. After adjustment, the results were presented as adjusted ORs (aORs) and adjusted HRs (aHRs). Considering the potential influence of acute illness severity, we further performed a sensitivity analysis adjusting for available acute severity markers, including sepsis, mechanical circulatory support (including IABP and ECMO) and mechanical ventilation.

Statistical analysis for this study was conducted using R 4.3.1 (R Core Team 2020, Vienna, Austria). The results with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Overall, 580 participants with dilated cardiomyopathy were included in the study (mean age 64.20 ± 14.80 years; 73% male; 55% White). Table 1 summarizes baseline characteristics stratified by GV using the median value 2.9 as the cutoff. Patients with GV ≥ 2.9 had higher proportions of CKD (40% vs. 31%, P = 0.025) and diabetes (51% vs. 21%, P < 0.001). The proportions of hypertension (76% vs. 69%, P = 0.064) and hyperlipidaemia (55% vs. 47%, P = 0.056) were numerically higher in patients with GV ≥ 2.9. Higher WBC counts (9.80 ± 4.79 vs. 8.18 ± 3.34, P < 0.001) and glucose levels (148.23 ± 67.11 vs. 113.38 ± 27.12, P < 0.001) were detected in patients with GV ≥ 2.9. In addition, patients with GV ≥ 2.9 had higher rates of sepsis (11% vs. 4.1%, P = 0.002), mechanical ventilation (22% vs. 7.8%, P < 0.001), and mechanical circulatory support (3.1% vs. 0.3%, P = 0.010).

Outcomes in-hospital and at follow-up

Patients with GV ≥ 2.9 had higher hospital mortality rates (5.6% vs. 0.7%), higher rates of ICU admission (52% vs. 28%), and longer hospital stays [8.87 IQR (4.80, 14.02) vs. 5.62 IQR (3.74, 8.59)], as shown in Supplementary Table 2. During the follow-up period, patients with higher GV also had higher 1-year (20% vs. 10%), 3-year (26% vs. 17%), and 5-year mortality rates (29% vs. 19%).

KM curves were used to estimate the survival probability in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy (Fig. 1). The results revealed that patients with GV ≥ 2.9 had a lower survival probability at the 1-year, 3-year and 5-yearfollow-up periods (All Log-rank P < 0.001) .

Glycaemic variability and in-hospital and follow-up mortality

As shown in the RCS curves (Fig. 2), higher GV was consistently associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes, including in-hospital mortality, ICU admission, and over 1-year, 3-year and 5-year of mortality. No significant non-linear association was observed (all P for non-linearity > 0.05), indicating an approximately linear relationship between GV and mortality risk.

Fig. 2Restricted cubic spline curve of glyceamic variability with in-hospital and long-term mortality

Restricted cubic spline curve of glyceamic variability with in-hospital and long-term mortality

Figures 3 and 4 show the associations between GV and patient outcomes. After adjustment for age, sex, race, BMI, comorbidities, treatment, and laboratory tests, increased GV was associated with a higher risk of ICU admission [adjusted OR (aOR) 2.59, 95% CI (1.74, 3.96), P < 0.001], 1-year death [adjusted HR (aHR) 1.84, 95% CI (1.28, 2.64), P < 0.001], 3-year death [aHR 1.65, 95% CI (1.21, 2.24), P = 0.002], and 5-year death [aHR 1.60, 95% CI (1.20, 2.15), P = 0.002]. The association between continuous GV and in-hospital death was borderline significant [aOR 5.12, 95% CI (1.07, 31.2), P = 0.052].

After adjustment for the above covariates, patients with GV ≥ 2.9 had a greater risk of in-hospital death [aOR 9.01, 95% CI (1.26, 117), P = 0.049], ICU admission [aOR 2.33, 95% CI (1.47, 3.70), P < 0.001], 1-year death [aHR 2.01, 95% CI (1.23, 3.31), P = 0.006], 3-year death [aHR 1.69, 95% CI (1.13, 2.54), P = 0.011], and 5-year death [aHR 1.71, 95% CI (1.17, 2.50), P = 0.006].

Subgroup analysis

To further assess whether the association between GV and mortality differed across clinically relevant subgroups, we performed subgroup analyses (Table 2). After adjustment for age, sex, race, BMI, comorbidities, treatment, and laboratory tests, GV remained significantly associated with 5-year mortality in patients with AF [aHR 2.29, 95% CI (1.54, 3.39), P < 0.001], whereas the association was not significant in patients without AF [aHR 0.99, 95% CI (0.60, 1.63), P = 0.62; P for interaction = 0.025]. No significant effect modification was observed for age, diabetes, renal disease, hypertension, or hyperlipidaemia (all P for interaction > 0.05).

To further address the potential confounding effect of acute illness severity, we performed sensitivity analyses with additional adjustment for sepsis, mechanical ventilation, and mechanical circulatory support, as shown in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4. After this adjustment, the association between continuous GV and in-hospital mortality was attenuated and no longer statistically significant [aOR 3.70, 95% CI (0.49, 28.6), P = 0.20], whereas GV remained significantly associated with ICU admission [aOR 2.29, 95% CI (1.48, 3.62), P < 0.001], 1-year death [aHR 1.82, 95% CI (1.26, 2.63), P = 0.001], 3-year death [aHR 1.64, 95% CI (1.20, 2.24), P = 0.002], and 5-year death [aHR 1.60, 95% CI (1.19, 2.15), P = 0.002]. Similar results were observed in categorical analyses, in which GV ≥ 2.9 showed a non-significant trend toward higher in-hospital mortality [aOR 9.11, 95% CI (0.89, 192), P = 0.095], but remained significantly associated with ICU admission and long-term mortality.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that higher GV was associated with increased risks of adverse clinical outcomes in patients with DCM. Each unit increase in GV was associated with a fourfold increase in in-hospital mortality and a 1.5- to 2-fold increase in long-term mortality. Notably, we used the median GV value (2.9) as the cutoff for group comparisons. Patients with GV levels ≥ 2.9 had significantly higher mortality risks than those with GV < 2.9 did, which manifested as increased in-hospital mortality and substantially elevated risks of 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year mortalities during longitudinal follow-up.

The present study further demonstrated that each unit increase in GV was significantly associated with elevated risks of both in-hospital and long-term mortalities among patients with DCM. To date, the relationship between GV and DCM has not been well characterized in published studies. Nevertheless, the impact of GV on other cardiovascular conditions has been extensively investigated. For example, a registry study including 2,617 patients with heart failure from Korea indicated that high GV (coefficient of variation > 21%) was associated with a risk of 1-year (HR 1.56, 95% CI 1.26–1.92) mortality [13]. One retrospective study among heart failure patients with preserved ejection fractions from Taiwan revealed that the highest CV tertile was associated with an increased risk of death (HR 1.65, 95% CI: 1.16–2.35) [14]. In addition, the mean amplitude of glycaemic excursions was also identified as an independent predictor of major adverse cardiac events, including acute myocardial infarction (HR 3.645, 95% CI 1.29–10.33) [15]. Similarly, a higher risk of all-cause mortality was identified in the ALLHAT cohort [16]. These results from the above studies are well aligned with our study.

Importantly, we found the patients with GV above the cohort median (2.9) had significantly higher risks of in-hospital and 1-, 3-, and 5-year mortality than those with GV < 2.9. Notably, although previous studies have proposed different GV cut-off values depending on the disease context, an established threshold for DCM remains unknown. For example, in patients with acute pancreatitis, a GV cut-off of 0.25 was proposed as optimal for predicting all-cause mortality [17]. In addition, a U-shaped association between GV and prognosis was observed in patients with haemorrhagic stroke [18] or in critically ill patients with nontraumatic subarachnoid haemorrhage [19]. These studies further support the prognostic relevance of GV in different clinical settings, whereas our study provides a novel potential GV threshold for risk stratification in patients with DCM.

Overall, our findings suggest that GV provides prognostic information in DCM beyond diabetes burden or acute metabolic instability. Previous studies have shown that oscillating glucose fluctuations can induce greater oxidative stress [20–22], endothelial dysfunction [23, 24], inflammatory activation [21] and apoptosis [4] than sustained hyperglycaemia. In heart failure, myocardial substrate utilization and energy metabolism are profoundly altered, with impaired glucose uptake, glucose oxidation, mitochondrial function, and ATP generation [25]. These metabolic abnormalities occur in a pathophysiological milieu characterized by neurohormonal activation, adverse ventricular remodelling, and progressive contractile dysfunction [26, 27]. Additionally, transcriptomic analyses revealed the downregulation of key glycolysis genes (PFKM, LDHA, and DLAT) in the myocardium of patients with DCM, which is linked to immune signalling and m6A methylation [28]. Collectively, these findings suggest that glucose metabolism may represent not only a marker of disease severity but also a clinically relevant metabolic phenotype worthy of further investigation in DCM.

The interaction between metabolic regulation and guideline-directed medical therapy may further support the clinical relevance of GV in DCM. For example, a recent longitudinal study reported that dapagliflozin (SGLT2 inhibitor) significantly reduced myocardial fibrosis, reflected by a greater decrease in extracellular volume fraction, and improved cardiac remodelling, with LVMI decreasing by 8.2 g/m² compared with 2.1 g/m² [29]. However, evidence from HFpEF populations should be interpreted cautiously because contemporary HFpEF criteria may include older multimorbid patients with modest natriuretic peptide elevation or age-related echocardiographic changes, rather than definite haemodynamic congestion [30, 31]. In addition, exercise intolerance and acute congestion in patients with preserved ejection fraction may be driven by non-HFpEF mechanisms, including severe valvular disease, rhythm disorders, pulmonary hypertension, renal dysfunction, or other structural cardiac abnormalities [30–32]. Moreover, mechanistic trials in non-diabetic HFrEF showed that empagliflozin improved left ventricular volumes, left ventricular mass, systolic function, functional capacity, and myocardial fibrosis/stiffness, suggesting that metabolic modulation may influence heart failure remodelling beyond glucose lowering alone [33, 34]. In addition, Guideline-directed therapy in acute or vulnerable settings requires careful monitoring because bradycardia, hyperkalaemia, renal dysfunction, hypotension, and hypoglycaemia may affect treatment tolerance [35]. Thus, GV may provide additional information on systemic metabolic vulnerability and treatment-related risk in hospitalized patients with DCM.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. We identified GV as a novel predictor of both short- and long-term mortalities in patients with DCM, regardless of diabetes status. We also used the cohort median GV value as an internal stratification point to explore its potential clinical relevance. Moreover, we demonstrated that the association between GV and mortality is independent not only in patients with diabetes but also in those without diabetes, highlighting the broader prognostic value of GV beyond glycaemic disorders. Importantly, by incorporating both in-hospital and long-term follow-up data, our analysis offers a comprehensive assessment of the prognostic impact of GV. These findings suggest that GV, which is often overlooked in clinical practice, might provide additional prognostic information for risk stratification in hospitalized patients with DCM. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and to evaluate therapeutic interventions to stabilize glucose fluctuations in the DCM population.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in the present study. As this was a single-centre, retrospective study, the findings might be subject to selection bias and may not be fully generalizable to other populations. Although several ventricular dilation and cardiomyopathy were excluded using ICD codes, some clinical variables were unavailable in database; therefore, residual diagnostic misclassification could not be completely avoided. Although we adjusted for multiple confounders, unmeasured variables could still influence the observed association between GV and mortality. For example, HbA1c data were missing in a large proportion of patients, and information on diabetes duration, detailed intensity of diabetes therapy, and echocardiographic parameters was not consistently available. GV was calculated from glucose values obtained during routine clinical care [3, 11], potential variation in sampling conditions and monitoring frequency may have introduced heterogeneity in GV estimation. The lack of continuous glucose monitoring data inevitably limits the precision of GV measurements.

Conclusion

This study identified GV as an independent and significant predictor of both in-hospital and long-term mortality in patients with DCM. A GV threshold of 2.9 effectively stratifies patients with DCM according to risk, offering a potential biomarker for the early identification of high-risk individuals. Importantly, the prognostic impact of increased GV was observed regardless of diabetes status.
