Molecular Response Assessment From Circulating Tumor DNA in Patients With Ovarian Cancer Treated With the WEE1 Inhibitor Azenosertib
Authors: Jinkil Jeong, Jianhui Ma, Mona Abed, Heekyung Chung, Doris Kim, Nandini Molden, Divya Rajendran, Changhwan Shim, Danielle D. Jandial, Fiona Simpkins, Funda Meric-Bernstam, Leslie M. Randall, Mark R. Lackner, Olivier Harismendy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEMolecular response (MR) based on circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is emerging as a promising early biomarker of treatment efficacy in solid tumors; however, its clinical utility in high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) remains to be established. This study evaluates the potential predictive value of ctDNA-based MR in with patients HGSOC treated with the WEE1 inhibitor azenosertib.METHODSPlasma cell-free DNA was collected at baseline and after the first and/or second cycle of treatment from 123 patients with recurrent HGSOC enrolled in clinical trials of azenosertib (N = 123). Using the set samples evaluable by high throughput DNA sequencing, MR was defined as a reduction of TP53 variant allelic fraction >50% at the earliest evaluable on-treatment time point. MR was compared with radiographic response per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors v1.1, time to progression (TTP), and CA-125 dynamics.RESULTSMolecular responders showed a higher objective response rate (P = .012, odds ratio = 0.26), greater tumor shrinkage (P = 5.1e-5, Kruskal-Wallis), and a significantly longer TTP than nonresponders (TTP median 5.49 months v 2.69 months, P = 3.8e-5, hazard ratios [HR] = 0.43). MR was able to identify patients with prolonged survival among those having stable disease at the first radiographic assessment (P = 3.8e-3, HR = 0.44), and their MR preceded the best overall response by several weeks. Accounting for matching collection time points, MR was evaluable in more patients than CA-125, 88% (102 of 116) versus 71% (85 of 116), respectively, leading to similar predictive value (HR = 0.41 v 0.45, TTP evaluation).CONCLUSIONOur findings support the validity and potential clinical utility of ctDNA-based MR as a minimally invasive, rapid, and reliable early surrogate end point in HGSOC.

INTRODUCTION

Considered a potential surrogate for clinical response, molecular response (MR) consists of changes in circulating biomarkers observed within the first 1-3 cycles of treatment. MR may provide an early indication of antitumor activity and whether patients with stable disease (SD) are benefiting from treatment. In the context of experimental therapeutics for solid tumors, MR within the first cycle of therapy may provide an early surrogate end point for efficacy of the investigational treatment compared with later survival end points. MR has typically been evaluated retrospectively in clinical trials1,2 and is useful for decision making around the dose and activity of experimental anticancer agents. Pending prospective validation, MR could potentially offer data-driven opportunities to conduct early phase studies, plan additional arms evaluating alternate doses or combinations, explore indications or patient selection strategies without waiting for a full readout of the study, or support rapid end point assessment for drug approvals. While MR is not currently acceptable as an approval end point across solid tumor studies, it remains a valuable exploratory end point in early phase studies. For example, MR data may inform early signal for potential clinical activity of an investigational drug when evaluating different dosing regimens and exposures or when comparing molecularly defined subsets of patients, ahead of traditional clinical response criteria such as progression-free or overall survival that may take longer to mature.

In high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125) has long been recognized as a serum biomarker of tumor burden and antitumor activity and decades of research have led to common guidelines on its use for monitoring.3-6 More recently, monitoring of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) features such as mutations, structural variants, methylation, and fragment size has emerged. These approaches have shown promise in multiple indications such as lung or colorectal cancers1,2 or monitoring of ovarian cancer progression.7 However, the investigation of MR in HGSOC has been limited to small, nonrandomized studies with restricted sample sizes, with heterogenous assays and methodologies—including digital PCR monitoring of tumor-informed TP53 variant allelic fraction (VAF)8,9 or gene panel sequencing for multiple variants or genetic features.10-14 Consequently, the use of ctDNA-based MR is still under study, and additional evidence and methodological assessments are needed to assess its performance and demonstrate its utility in HGSOC.

Here, we present the retrospective monitoring of ctDNA in a large cohort of 123 patients with HGSOC treated with the investigational oral WEE1 inhibitor azenosertib. Relying on the nearly ubiquitous presence of TP53 mutations in the plasma of patients with HGSOC, we used next-generation sequencing of plasma cell-free DNA to measure changes in TP53 VAF within the first two cycles of treatment, develop an MR classification, and correlate MR with established efficacy end points. We specifically highlight its utility in predicting the clinical outcome of patients with SD at the first radiographic assessment. Finally, we report and discuss the differences between monitoring MR from ctDNA and CA-125 changes in the same cohort.

METHODS

Human Investigations

ZN-c3-001 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04158336), ZN-c3-005 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05128825), and ZN-c3-006 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05198804) clinical trials were conducted following approval by the Institutional Review Boards (or equivalent) at the applicable sites and under an (US) Food and Drug Administration–approved investigational new drug application in the United States and ex-US equivalents, where applicable. All patients enrolled in the clinical trials provided written informed consent.

Study Design and Sample Collection

Among 220 patients with platinum-resistant recurrent HGSOC enrolled in open-label single-agent azenosertib studies—ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04158336 (n = 57), ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05128825 (n = 102), ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05198804 (n = 61)—and who were administered a pharmacologically active total daily dose of 300 mg once every day or higher of azenosertib on a 5-day-on, 2-day-off (5:2) intermittent dosing schedule, 139 were eligible and included in this post hoc study based on the criteria that plasma cell-free DNA was successfully profiled at baseline (ie, within 7 days before treatment Cycle 1 Day 1; C1D1) and at least one on-treatment time point before dosing at Cycle 2 Day 1 (C2D1) or Cycle 3 Day 1 (C3D1; Data Supplement, Fig A1). A treatment cycle consisted of 3 weeks of treatment, and radiographic tumor assessments were conducted every 6 weeks using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1. Time to progression (TTP) was defined as the interval from C1D1 to the date of disease progression or death because of disease progression. Patients with non–disease-related or unknown reason of death were censored at the date of death and included in TTP analyses. All clinical and efficacy data were extracted from an open database as of the data extract on 27 January 2025, before database lock.

Liquid Biopsy Data Generation

Plasma samples (4 mL) were used for cell-free DNA extraction and subsequent NGS analysis performed by Tempus using xF+ liquid biopsy assay. VAF of individual TP53 variants with known oncogenic function provided by Tempus (limit of detection [LOD] 0.25% with >99.9% sensitivity and >99.9% specificity) was used to determine MR. If a patient had multiple TP53 variants, the median VAF was used. The presence of at least one matching TP53 variant in the tumor and its low-to-moderate likelihood of originating from clonal hematopoiesis (Tempus CH classification) were confirmed in 91% (92 of 101) of patients with matching tissue DNA sequencing data, a proportion higher than that reported in previous multigene studies that controlled for collection timing.15

CA-125 data were collected from multiple centers of two studies (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05128825, NCT05198804). The upper limit of normal (ULN) was defined as a serum CA-125 concentration of 35 units/mL.3 Baseline CA-125 was measured within 14 days before the start of treatment.

Molecular Response Classification

The definition and classification of all MR stages and MR types are provided in the Data Supplement (Table S1). Molecular responders were identified as patients with a decrease of TP53 VAF > 50% between baseline and the earliest available on-treatment sample between C2D1 and C3D1. MR rate (MRR) was defined as the fraction of molecular responders in the MR evaluable population. Similarly, a 4-stage MR category was developed using the extent of the VAF change to stratify MR into molecular complete response (mCR), molecular partial response (mPR), molecular SD (mSD), and molecular progressive disease (mPD). Confirmed positive MR (cMR+) was defined as positive MR in both C2D1 and C3D1 time points, unconfirmed positive MR (uMR+) as positive MR in either of two time points, and confirmed negative MR (cMR-) as negative MR at both time points.

CA-125 Response

To compare the early predictive performance of CA-125 and ctDNA, postbaseline CA-125 data points for analysis were restricted to those measured within 8 days from corresponding ctDNA measurements for individual patients. Patient evaluability of CA-125 was determined by the GCIG guideline4 that a baseline CA-125 level should be greater than or equal to twice of ULN (ie, 70 units/mL). CA-125 response was defined as a decrease of CA-125 level >50% between baseline and ctDNA-matched postbaseline, following the GCIG guideline.

Statistics

The association between the biomarker and TTP was evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with group comparisons performed using the two-sided log-rank test and the Cox proportional hazards model to estimate hazard ratios (HRs). The relationship between biomarker levels and the best percentage change in tumor size was evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test. Positive predictive value (PPV) was defined as the proportion of molecular responders who achieved a clinical response. Negative predictive value (NPV) was defined as the proportion of molecular nonresponders who did not achieve a clinical response. Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2) and packages including survival, survminer, and epiR. P values <.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Longitudinal ctDNA was successfully profiled for 139 patients with HGSOC. Of those, 123 (88.5%) showed one or more pathogenic TP53 variants detectable at baseline and were considered evaluable for MR analysis. TP53 variant allelic frequency at baseline ranged from 0.26% to 73.3% (median 3.9%, Fig 1A). The earliest on-treatment time point was used to determine MR, which was at the beginning of the second cycle (C2D1, n = 112) or third cycle (C3D1, n = 11). At the time of data extraction, TTP was evaluable for all 123 patients (median follow-up time 8.6 months), and RECIST 1.1 Best Overall Response was evaluable for 122 patients including confirmed response (n = 25), SD (n = 71), or progressive disease (n = 26). Similar to RECIST criteria measuring changes in the size of a cancer lesion, and following recommendation made for the analysis of ctDNA,16 the changes in TP53 VAF were classified into four categories (Fig 1B). Twenty-three percent (28 of 123) of patients showed mCR with clearance of circulating TP53 mutation DNA fragments below LOD. Next, 34% of patients (42 of 123) showed mPR. While mCR and mPR compose all molecular responders the remaining 43% of molecular nonresponders (53 of 123) were subclassified into (mSD, n = 33) and (mPD, n = 20). Importantly, the molecular response stages were not associated with the TP53 variant allelic frequency at baseline and VAF lower than 1% was observed in 36%, 5%, 15%, and 45% of patients with mCR, mPR, mSD, and mPD, respectively (Data Supplement, Fig S2A), suggesting that MR is independent of the baseline TP53 VAF and that the assay sensitivity and LOD are unlikely to skew the measurement and classification of VAF change.

Molecular Response Is Associated With RECIST Response

To evaluate MR as a possible surrogate for efficacy, the MRR for patients with different best overall response (BOR) was measured (Fig 2A). Patients with complete response/partial response (CR/PR) showed the highest 80% MRR (20 of 25), patients with SD showed 56% MRR (40 of 71), and patients with progressive disease (PD) showed the lowest 35% MRR (9 of 26), resulting in a significant association between MR and BOR (P = .0094, chi-squared). Reciprocally, BOR was compared between molecular responders and nonresponders (Fig 2B). Molecular responders had 29% (20 of 70) objective response rate (ORR), whereas molecular nonresponders showed 9% (5 of 53) ORR (P = .012, Fisher's exact, two-sided; odds ratio = 0.26). On the other hand, molecular nonresponders had a higher rate of progressive disease compared with molecular responders (32% v 13%, P = .014, Fisher's exact). Consistently, we observed a significant correlation between MR and tumor size change (Fig 2C, P = 5.1e-5, Kruskal-Wallis), with mCR showing the largest shrinkage (-30.6%), whereas other groups had proportionally smaller or no reduction in lesion size (-18.2%, -7.3%, and 5.7% for mPR, mSD, and mPD, respectively). Similar to baseline TP53 VAF, the baseline size of the target lesion was not correlated with MR, suggesting that MR is independent of baseline disease burden (Data Supplement, Fig S2B). Taken together, the results showed a significant correlation between MR and RECIST response, implying that MR may provide reliable efficacy information.

Molecular Response Stratifies Time to Progression

Next, we asked whether MR correlates with TTP and determined that molecular responders had a significantly longer TTP than molecular nonresponders patients (TTP median 5.49 months v 2.69 months, P = 3.8e-5, HR = 0.43, Fig 3A and Table 1). Importantly, the same trend was observed in patients with SD at the first assessment (SD1A patients; TTP median 6.14 v 3.56, P = 3.8e-3, HR = 0.44, Fig 3B and Table 1), suggesting that MR measured within the first cycles of azenosertib treatment may help predict clinical benefit even when the first tumor assessment only shows SD. Furthermore, we determined that patients with mCR (ie, TP53 ctDNA clearance) or confirmed MR (cMR+, ie, MR at both C2D1 and C3D1 time points) had the longest TTP (6.47 and 6.14, respectively; Fig 3C, D and Table 1), suggesting that the depth and duration of the MR provide additional information. Interestingly, patients with mCR and cMR+ response type were not the same (P = 1, Fisher's exact; Data Supplement, Table S2), indicating possible independence between the depth and duration of MR.

Molecular Response Precedes RECIST Outcome

To examine the utility of MR as an early surrogate end point, we evaluated if and how early MR can predict a latent RECIST response in the SD1A cohort. Of 24 SD1A patients who were molecular responders, 5 (21%) eventually responded (Fig 4A). Their MR was observed a median of 14 weeks before the confirmation of response per RECIST. Reciprocally, of 28 SD1A patients who were molecular nonresponders, 26 (93%) eventually progressed (Fig 4B). For those, the lack of MR predicted disease progression about 8 weeks (median) in advance. The results require prospective confirmation but demonstrate the potential clinical utility of MR as an early predictor of clinical response, which can take several months to observe by radiographic imaging.

Comparison Between MR and CA-125

ctDNA-based MR can also be evaluated by comparison with CA-125, a conventional serum biomarker in gynecologic malignancies. However, the current guidelines assume frequent sampling and require consecutive, consistent change to confirm response. To compare responses from changes in circulating CA-125 and TP53 VAF, we restricted postbaseline CA-125 measurements at time points matching those of cell-free DNA collection for each patient (see the Methods section). There were 116 patients with both cell-free DNA and CA-125 analyzed at matching time points. Of those, MR and CA-125 response were evaluable in 88% (102 of 116) and 71% (85 of 116) of patients, respectively, including 73 patients with both biomarkers evaluable and used in the subsequent analysis. Response according to each biomarker were consistent (P = .015, Fisher's exact). Fifty-nine percent (43 of 73) of patients showed concordant responses, including 16 responders and 27 nonresponders. The depth of response, as measured by the percentage change from baseline, also showed a significant positive correlation (ρ = 0.53, P = 1.4e-6; Data Supplement, Fig S3).

Next, MR and CA-125 response were each compared for association with objective response (OR) and TTP. Both CA-125 response and MR each showed significant association with OR (P values of .0019 and .025, respectively, Fisher's exact; Data Supplement, Tables S3 and S4). Similarly, CA-125 response and MR also showed significant stratification of TTP, respectively (P value .014 and 6.3e-4, respectively, log-rank; Fig 5 and Table 1). Despite slight numerical differences with stronger association between MR and PFS and between CA-125 and OR, the two methods had similar performance in our study and MR and CA-125 response measured at the first treatment cycles identified significantly overlapped liquid biopsy responders and showed significant association with clinical outcomes.

DISCUSSION

This report suggests that ctDNA-based MR is significantly associated with clinical outcomes in a large cohort of patients with platinum-resistant HGSOC treated with azenosertib. MRs with early ctDNA decrease had a higher response rate, greater tumor shrinkage, and a longer TTP. This association was not only observed in the entire cohort but, notably, also in patients with SD at their first tumor assessment, suggesting a practical predictive value of MR. Stratification by depth or duration of MR further improved the association. Finally, using matching collection time points, we showed that monitoring ctDNA also had a similar performance to the more established CA-125 plasma concentration for predicting TTP.

A few previous studies have investigated ctDNA-based MR in HGSOC in various settings and using different methodologies.8-14 Since TP53 mutation is a hallmark of HGSOC with more than 90% prevalence, ctDNA measurement based on TP53 variants has been the most favorable method regardless of whether digital PCR or NGS was used. To date, it remains unclear whether ctDNA measurement relying on multiple mutations offers a superior performance for MR in HGSOC. In the absence of matched DNA from buffy coat or a well-trained machine learning classification of variants, multigene panel sequencing of cell-free DNA will identify inherited variants or clonal hematopoiesis mutations which will confound the ctDNA estimates.17 More sensitive methods relying on matched tumor samples or multimodal analysis developed for early diagnostics or minimal residual disease (MRD) may also offer better performance but are yet to be applied to MR analysis.7,18

Previous MR studies in HGSOC were often limited by the small size of the cohorts, which led to anecdotal observations of the relationship between MR and treatment response. Nonetheless, Parkinson et al8 retrospectively studied a relatively larger cohort of 40 patients with HGSOC for longitudinal changes of TP53 ctDNA level under standard-of-care treatments. They used digital PCR to measure VAF change of FFPE tissue-informed TP53 variants in 25 patients with relapsed HGSOC treated with platinum and nonplatinum chemotherapies and showed that reduction of TP53 VAF after the first treatment cycle was significantly associated with TTP. Our consistent finding of MR and TTP association further supports the predictive nature of MR for patient survival, regardless of the type of treatment or ctDNA measurement technique.

The wide range of TP53 ctDNA VAF changes observed in our cohort was categorized into hierarchical 2-stage and 4-stage MR, respectively (Fig 1), both correlated with clinical outcomes. Alternate classification of MR that mimics closer the RECIST response criteria has been proposed,16,19 with the common idea to classify ctDNA level change into complete reduction, partial reduction, increase, and stable. Importantly, the definition of mCR—or ctDNA clearance—is dependent on the assay LOD in a manner that may be more critical than the minimal size of the lesion detectable by a radiologist. The LOD of the xF+ assay used in our study has been reported at 0.25% VAF,20 but technological advances are increasing the sensitivity of the assays, notably to identify minimal residual disease.21 Standardization of the classification will help with the approval of MR as a universal surrogate end point and comparison across studies, but significant challenges will remain because of the differences in the nature of the molecular landscape—presence of strong oncogenic driver or wide range of mutational burden—the anatomic location of the tumors, their vascularization, and overall the lack of understanding of the precise source and mechanisms for ctDNA shedding in the blood stream. Additional studies and benchmarking, including larger consortium-driven benchmarking studies,22,23 will be needed, similar to those attempting to standardize measurement of homologous recombination deficiency or tumor mutational burden.24,25

The definition of MR in our study relied solely on the observed change in TP53 VAF—to mirror the method used for CA-125 or radiographic measurement of the size of the tumor lesions. Our analysis showed that both analytes were equally predictive of outcome, with TP53 VAF evaluable in slightly more patients. It is possible, however, that an approach combining both markers may lead to superior results as demonstrated for the early detection of ovarian cancer.18,26 Similarly, more refined methodologies are emerging elsewhere. A recent study developed a more elaborate ctDNA-based model predicting survival of patients with metastatic non–small cell lung cancer treated with an immune checkpoint inhibitor,27 leveraging multiple features from longitudinal cell-free DNA NGS assays and clinical covariates. In our study, features such as cell-free DNA yield, ctDNA VAF, fragmentomics, and CA-125 could improve the model, provided that they are collected from a sufficiently large cohort to prevent overfitting.

Radiographic tumor assessment has been widely used as an end point in cancer drug trials,28 but its value is being challenged by observations of poor correlation with overall survival.29 In contrast to radiographic imaging of a few selected lesions, MR measures a more systemic tumor burden in the circulation and can predict overall survival better than RECIST.27,30 Anecdotally, discrepancy between RECIST and MR was observed in our study with one patient with mCR. At the time point matching the plasma collection, this patient showed a reduction of the target lesion by 34% at the but was determined as PD due to the emergence of a new lesion that may not contribute significantly to the circulating tumor DNA (Fig 2A). Furthermore, MR is particularly valuable as a surrogate end point for patients who were neither responders nor progressors at the first radiologic assessment (Fig 3B), with MR preceding RECIST outcome by several weeks (Fig 4). Taken together, MR has a significant potential value as an early surrogate end point that could perform better than RECIST in earlier stages of treatment. Future prospective studies or large meta-analysis of exploratory end points in past studies would be needed to formally demonstrate the validity of MR as a surrogate end point, similar to how the MRD end point was developed and accepted for multiple myeloma studies.31 In the meantime, MR may be a helpful tool that physicians and investigators consider for clinical decision making—akin to the current use of CA-125 in practice.
