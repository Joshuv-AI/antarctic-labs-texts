A miniaturized wireless and passive antenna sensor with meandering structure for integrated multi-directional strain and temperature sensing
Authors: Liangjie Guo, Helei Dong, Siyu Liang, Xin Tian, He Fu, Tengteng Dong, Liangkun Guo, Dongyang Wu, Qiulin Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To meet the demand for real-time synchronous monitoring of multidirectional mechanical states and temperature in complex working environments such as new energy fields, high-speed rotating machinery, large-scale building structures, and special enclosed systems, where space is limited, wiring is restricted, and power supply is difficult, this paper proposes a wireless, passive, and miniaturized integrated sensor for multidirectional strain and temperature sensing. The sensor adopts a novel stepped trapezoidal three-dimensional layout to reduce mutual coupling effects and integrates three patch antennas for strain sensing at 0°, 45°/135° directions and temperature sensing, respectively. By introducing T-shaped slots on the patch surfaces through meandering technology to extend the equivalent current paths, the sensor achieves miniaturization, resulting in a reduction of radiation area by 53%, 29.3%, and 42.3% for the three patches compared with conventional designs of the same resonant frequency and material, and an overall size reduction of 59.7%. Additionally, a high-temperature resistant broadband coplanar waveguide antenna is designed to replace the traditional horn antenna as the interrogation antenna, enabling signal transmission in high-temperature environments. To comprehensively evaluate the sensor performance, three test systems were established: a room-temperature strain test system, a temperature test system, and a variable-temperature strain test system. The room-temperature strain test system measures strain in the 0°, 45°, and 135° directions within the range of 0–500 με. The temperature test system monitors temperature in the range of 15–800 °C. The variable-temperature strain test system characterizes strain in the same temperature zone for the three aforementioned directions within 0–500 με, based on which a temperature correction algorithm for strain signals is proposed to decouple strain signals under varying temperatures. Experiments show that the measured results agree well with simulations, and the sensor is capable of synchronous monitoring of multidirectional strain and temperature. The average strain sensitivity reaches up to 26.14 kHz/με, with measurement errors ≤5%, and the maximum temperature sensitivity is 341.67 kHz/°C. The fitting errors for both room-temperature strain and temperature tests are below 0.1%. In repeatability tests, the standard deviations for room-temperature strain and temperature monitoring are less than 0.1 MHz and 0.9 MHz, respectively. This sensor provides an effective integrated solution for multi-parameter monitoring under complex working conditions.

Introduction

In the operation process of modern industrial equipment and infrastructure, various complex loads (such as mechanical forces, vibrations, wind loads, and thermal loads) result in multi-directional stress distributions at key structural locations, making strain measurement in a single direction insufficient to fully reflect their true mechanical state and health condition1–3. As these components directly affect the safety and performance of the entire system, real-time synchronous monitoring of multi-directional strain is particularly critical. For instance, wind turbine blade roots and towers are susceptible to multiaxial fatigue damage under coupled aerodynamic, gravitational, and inertial loads. Multi-directional strain monitoring can accurately assess cumulative damage, thereby significantly enhancing operational reliability and service life4. The mechanical arms of industrial robots experience complex stress distributions during multi-degree-of-freedom movements and loading processes. Synchronous multi-directional strain measurement enables high-precision force feedback control and real-time condition diagnosis, improving human-robot collaboration safety5. The multi-directional micro-strains generated in the spindle of precision CNC machine tools under high-speed rotation and cutting forces directly affect machining accuracy. Embedding such sensors can provide real-time force-state information support for error compensation and intelligent control6. In the power battery packs of new energy vehicles, the thermal runaway process is often accompanied by intense temperature rise and structural deformation, with local temperatures reaching 500–800 °C and significant internal stress and strain generated. Simultaneous real-time monitoring of these two key parameters can precisely capture early abnormal temperature increases and mechanical deformation, thereby enabling earlier and more reliable warning of thermal runaway risks, effectively preventing battery fires and ensuring the safety of passengers and vehicles7.

The characteristics of these application scenarios include limited space, harsh environments, rotating or enclosed monitoring locations, and stringent requirements for monitoring accuracy. Traditional wired sensors present numerous inconveniences in these environments, such as susceptibility of cables to damage and difficulties in maintenance. In contrast, wireless and passive sensors stand out due to their advantages of requiring no external power supply, easy installation, and low maintenance costs8,9.

Currently, there are three common types of wireless and passive measurement methods: LC resonant sensing, surface acoustic wave sensing, and microwave scattering sensing. LC resonant sensors utilize near-field coupling technology, which not only enables real-time data reading in special environments but also allows for the integration of multi-parameter sensors. However, they suffer from shortcomings such as short transmission distance and low quality factor10–12. Surface acoustic wave sensing is sensitive to high-temperature and high-pressure environments, making it suitable for harsh conditions, but it also encounters issues like electromagnetic interference and complex packaging13,14.

As a typical implementation method of microwave scattering sensing, microstrip patch antenna sensors have garnered significant attention in recent years. This type of sensor detects measured physical quantities through changes in parameters such as the antenna’s resonant frequency, inheriting the advantages of microwave scattering technology, including strong anti-interference capability and the ability to achieve long-distance reading. Additionally, it possesses inherent strengths such as simple structure, low cost, convenient installation, and ease of conformality15,16.

Research on microstrip patch antenna-based strain sensors or temperature sensors has been conducted both domestically and internationally. In terms of strain measurement: In 2015, Xiaohua Yi et al.17 achieved wireless transmission of patch antenna sensor signals by embedding an RFID chip into the patch antenna. However, this sensor could only measure strain in a single direction and suffered from limitations such as large size and low sensitivity (approximately 760 Hz/µε), resulting in poor applicability in practical engineering applications. In 2016, Chunhee Cho et al.18 designed a wireless microstrip antenna sensor for strain and crack sensing based on frequency multiplication technology, with a strain sensitivity of −5.232 kHz/µε. However, the sensor’s excessively large area restricted its scope of application. In 2018, F Mbanya Tchafa et al.19 developed a wired microstrip antenna sensor capable of simultaneously measuring temperature and unidirectional strain. Nevertheless, its temperature measurement range was limited to 20–80 °C, the measurable strain direction was singular, and the wired connection method constrained its use in complex environments. In 2021, Zhiping Liu et al.20 designed and fabricated a wired multi-layer patch antenna sensor for strain and crack sensing, but its strain sensitivity was very low at only 0.2008 kHz/µε. In 2025, A.P. Praveen et al.21 utilized a wired microstrip antenna sensor with an auxetic structure to measure strain in only a single direction, achieving a strain sensitivity of 4.157 kHz/µε. In the field of temperature measurement: In 2022, Xianzhi Li et al.22 designed and manufactured a wired microstrip antenna sensor for measuring temperature and crack sensing. However, its temperature measurement range was only 20–70 °C, with a maximum temperature sensitivity of 0.193 MHz/°C, making it difficult to meet the demands of high-temperature monitoring in practical applications. In 2025, Xiangxiang Zhang et al.23 designed a dual-band microstrip antenna sensor with a low-profile complementary split-ring resonator (CSRR) structure, achieving a maximum sensitivity of 183 kHz/°C. However, its temperature measurement range was only 0–350 °C. Although wireless transmission was realized, it still failed to break through the measurement limitations in higher temperature ranges.

In summary, current microstrip antenna sensors, both domestically and internationally, still exhibit significant limitations. On one hand, most existing sensors are only capable of sensing a single parameter, such as temperature or unidirectional strain, making it difficult to monitor temperature and multi-dimensional mechanical states simultaneously. Additionally, they generally suffer from issues like low sensitivity and insufficient upper temperature limits, rendering them inadequate for real-time synchronous monitoring of strain and temperature in complex environments. On the other hand, such sensors mostly rely on wired connections and have relatively large volumes, facing challenges such as difficult wiring and limited deployment in confined or structurally compact scenarios. These shortcomings severely restrict the application of such sensors in complex working conditions, including new energy vehicles, rotating machinery, large-scale building structures, and special enclosed systems. Therefore, developing a multifunctional sensor that is miniaturized, wireless and passive, resistant to harsh environments, and capable of real-time monitoring of multi-directional strain along with high-temperature measurement holds significant research importance and promising engineering application prospects.

Miniaturization of sensors can be achieved through several methods, including high-permittivity dielectric substrate materials, antenna loading technology, active network technology, and patch meandering technology24. However, both antenna loading and the addition of active networks introduce drawbacks such as extra components and circuits, heat-intolerant devices, and complex fabrication processes. These issues can lead to sensor failure at high temperatures, increased sensor failure rates, and compromised structural integrity of the dielectric layer25,26. As shown in Eq. (6), the resonant frequency is inversely proportional to the dielectric constant of the substrate material. Thus, for a fixed resonant frequency, employing a substrate with a higher dielectric constant enables the reduction of antenna size. This method is often combined with other miniaturization techniques. Meandering technology achieves miniaturization by altering the current propagation path directly, effectively lengthening the equivalent path of the current. This approach offers advantages such as lower simulation costs, no need for additional circuits or components, and a straightforward fabrication process27.

Based on the aforementioned application background, this paper employs meander-line technology and selects high-permittivity alumina ceramic (with a dielectric constant of 9.8) as the substrate to achieve sensor miniaturization. Building upon this, a wireless passive multidirectional strain/temperature integrated sensor with four resonant points has been developed. Through a novel stepped configuration, the sensor integrates a first patch antenna for sensing 0° directional strain, a second patch antenna for sensing 45° and 135° directional strain, and a third patch antenna for temperature sensing into a single unit. This structure effectively reduces mutual coupling among these patch antennas. The operational frequency band of the sensor is designed within 2–3.5 GHz, which is not only significantly higher than the primary energy distribution bands of common industrial interference sources but also effectively avoids the crowded 2.4–2.5 GHz ISM band28. Consequently, it exhibits excellent anti-interference capability and transmission stability in complex industrial electromagnetic environments. The spacing between each resonant point is no less than 0.3 GHz, ensuring that multiple sensing units can operate independently without mutual interference, enabling synchronous real-time measurement of strain in three directions or temperature monitoring. To address high-temperature measurement requirements, this paper designs a high-temperature-resistant coplanar waveguide antenna as the interrogation antenna, replacing the traditional horn antenna. To evaluate the sensor’s performance, two separate test systems were constructed for room temperature and high temperature, respectively. The room temperature system utilizes a horn antenna to achieve synchronous measurement of multidirectional strain within the range of 0–500 με. The high-temperature system employs the self-developed high-temperature-resistant coplanar waveguide antenna as the interrogation antenna, replacing the traditional horn antenna to resolve high-temperature tolerance issues. Based on this antenna, a temperature testing platform and a variable-temperature strain testing platform were established. Temperature measurements within the range of 15–800 °C and variable-temperature strain tests within the same temperature zone for 0–500 με were conducted. By establishing a two-dimensional third-order polynomial surface fitting model for the strain signals, effective decoupling of strain signals in variable temperature environments was achieved.

Principles of wireless transmission

As shown in Fig. 1a, to achieve wireless transmission of sensor signals, an excitation signal containing the sensor’s resonant frequency (fr) range must be emitted. When the frequency of the excitation signal matches the sensor’s resonant frequency, the electromagnetic waves within the resonant cavity gradually attenuate due to multiple reflections, while signals of other frequencies return to the interrogation antenna in the form of echoes. Miniaturization principle using meandering technology

The surface current distribution on a microstrip antenna radiating element is determined by its geometric configuration. According to Formula 6, the resonant frequency of the antenna is inversely proportional to its electrical size; that is, an increase in electrical size leads to a decrease in resonant frequency. Meandering technology utilizes this principle by introducing slots at specific locations of the current distribution to create perturbations, which force the current to follow a longer, meandering path. This design effectively extends the equivalent propagation path of the current, thereby increasing the electrical size of the antenna relatively. To restore the resonant frequency to its original value, the physical size of the antenna must be reduced in accordance with the physical relationship expressed in Formula (5), thereby achieving antenna miniaturization35,36.

Miniaturization simulation of a coaxially-fed microstrip antenna

Firstly, HFSS software was employed to simulate the three microstrip antennas in their unslotted state, each fed by a coaxial line. The radiator thickness was designed to be 0.035 mm. Alumina ceramic, which features a high dielectric constant (9.8), high melting point, excellent thermal stability, and resistance to thermal shock at elevated temperatures, was selected as the substrate material to achieve preliminary miniaturization of the patch antennas. The substrate thickness was set to 1 mm. Both the radiating patch and the ground plane were made of platinum paste, which offers stable characteristics, high oxidation resistance, and high electrical conductivity under high-temperature conditions.

Subsequently, the meandering technology was applied to achieve further miniaturization. By introducing T-shaped slots at the edges of the radiating element, the equivalent propagation path of the current was extended, thereby reducing the resonant frequency. The antenna dimensions were then reduced to tune the resonant frequency back to its original value.

Using HFSS software, the slotted antennas were simulated and optimized (with coaxial feeding). Parameter sweeps were performed on the dimensions of the radiating element, substrate, feed position, and the number and size of the T-shaped slots until the original resonant frequency was restored. A schematic of the miniaturization simulation for Patch 1 is shown in Fig. 2a. Compared to the initial structure with a substrate size of 48 mm × 36 mm × 1 mm and a radiating element size of 25.3 mm × 18.5 mm (non-slotted), the miniaturized design achieved a 70.1% reduction in substrate area and over a 53% reduction in radiating element area. The miniaturization simulation for Patch 2 is shown in Fig. 2c. Compared to its initial structure with a substrate size of 45 mm × 32 mm × 1 mm and a radiating element size of 22.5 mm × 16.5 mm (non-slotted), the optimized design resulted in a 45.7% reduction in substrate area and a 29.3% reduction in radiating element area. The miniaturization simulation for Patch 3 is shown in Fig. 2e. Compared to the original structure with a substrate size of 40 mm × 30 mm × 1 mm and a radiating element size of 19.5 mm × 14.4 mm (non-slotted), the final design achieved a 61.7% reduction in substrate area and a 42.3% reduction in radiating element area. Simulation of strain principle

An equivalent modeling strategy is employed to directly simulate the material’s Poisson effect and the dynamic strain process. This strategy approximates the structural deformation behavior induced by strain at room temperature by manually adjusting the dimensions of the radiating patch in specific directions. The dimensional adjustment is strictly calculated based on the linear strain formula ΔL = ε × L₀. Taking sensor No. 2’s patch as an example: for the 45° direction, its original length L₀(45°) = 17.5 mm. To simulate the maximum strain = 500 με, the total elongation is calculated as ΔL = 8.75 μm. In the simulation, the dimensional scanning step for this direction is set to 1.75 μm, corresponding to a strain step of 100 με, thereby covering the complete strain range of 0–500 με. Similarly, for the 135° direction, its original length L₀(135°) = 15.0 mm, resulting in a total dimensional change of 7.5 μm within the same strain range, and the scanning step is set to 1.5 μm. All other structural parameters remain unchanged throughout the simulation, thereby effectively isolating the influence of a single strain direction on the sensor’s resonant frequency. Simulation results indicate a directional correlation between each resonant point and strain in the corresponding direction. As shown in Fig. 5, when strain is applied in a specific direction, only the resonant point associated with the dimension in that strain direction shifts toward lower frequencies as the strain increases. The other resonant points primarily exhibit amplitude fluctuations, with no significant change in resonant frequency observed. Furthermore, under various strain conditions, the shift of the resonant points satisfies the impedance matching requirement of a return loss below −10 dB. This demonstrates that the sensor can maintain directional independence in strain measurement even when strain is applied simultaneously in all three directions, thereby validating the effectiveness of the proposed strain sensing mechanism at room temperature.Fig. 50° direction strain simulation.a Simulation diagram. b Return loss diagram (overall). c second resonance point amplified. 45° direction strain simulation d Simulation diagram. e Return loss diagram (overall). f first resonance point amplified. 135° direction strain simulation g Simulation diagram. h 135° direction strain simulation (overall). i third resonance point amplified

Simulation of coplanar waveguide antennas and temperature sensing

An interrogation antenna is typically a broadband, high-gain antenna designed to cover all potential frequency variations of the sensor. In addition to waveguide antennas, horn antennas, and patch-type coplanar waveguide antennas, broadband antennas also include helical antennas and loop antennas37. However, as the testing environment of this sensor involves high temperatures, the interrogation antenna occasionally needs to be deployed within high-temperature zones for signal detection. Since waveguide and horn antennas cannot withstand harsh high-temperature conditions, this paper presents a high-temperature-resistant coplanar waveguide antenna designed to serve as the temperature interrogation antenna38. This antenna uses alumina ceramic as the high-temperature-resistant dielectric layer and platinum for both the conductive layer and the ground plane material.

The interrogation antenna was simulated separately using HFSS software (its physical dimensions are shown in Fig. 6a). The results demonstrate that the antenna exhibits a broad operating bandwidth, with a −10 dB return loss (S₁₁) bandwidth exceeding 1.5 GHz, covering the possible frequency variation range and meeting the requirements for an interrogation antenna (see Fig. 6c). The three-dimensional gain pattern (Fig. 6b) shows hemispherical radiation characteristics, with a maximum gain of 2.1 dB occurring at θ = 0° (i.e., the positive z-axis direction). The radiating patch and ground plane are formed from platinum paste (ESL, 5541-A, USA), which exhibits stable properties at high temperatures, oxidation resistance, and high electrical conductivity, through a sintering process. The wire mesh stencil was aligned and fixed with the alumina ceramic substrate. Figure 14c–e only provides the strain‑frequency calibration relationship of the sensor at several discrete temperature points (15 °C, 100 °C, …, 800 °C); however, in practical applications the sensor often operates in a continuously varying temperature field where its resonant frequency is simultaneously affected by the coupled influence of temperature and strain. Under such conditions, merely establishing a mapping relationship among frequency, strain, and temperature makes it impossible to uniquely determine the strain value from the measured signal. Therefore, temperature compensation is required to separate strain from temperature by constructing an analytical model that takes ambient temperature and resonant frequency as inputs and directly outputs strain, thereby realizing decoupling. Benefiting from the integrated independent temperature‑measurement unit in this sensor, its output provides a real‑time temperature reference for strain sensing in each direction, supplying the basis for temperature compensation. Specifically, the ambient temperature is first estimated from the resonant frequency of the temperature-sensitive unit, i.e., the fourth resonant point. Then, a two-dimensional third-order polynomial surface fitting model is used to establish the relationship between the strain in a given direction, the resonant frequency of the corresponding resonant point in that direction, and the ambient temperature, thereby achieving decoupling between strain and temperature. Let Z0 denote the actual strain applied to the sensor in a given direction. By substituting the corresponding temperature x and the resonant frequency y of the corresponding resonant point in that direction into the aforementioned decoupling formula, the theoretical strain value Z can be calculated. The measurement error of the sensor is computed as (Z0-Z)/Z0, with the resulting error distributions for each direction shown in Fig. 15d–f. The results indicate that under different strain directions, different strain levels, and different temperatures, the measurement errors are all below 5%. The maximum error of 4.884% occurs under the conditions of the 135° direction, 400 °C, and a resonant frequency of 2.8362 GHz. These results demonstrate that the established decoupling model, within the range of model measurement errors, meets the measurement accuracy requirements for general industrial applications.

Conclusion

This study proposes a novel miniaturized, wireless passive, multidirectional strain and temperature integrated sensor to meet the demand for real-time, multi-dimensional, and wireless monitoring of mechanical states and temperature in complex working conditions such as those encountered in new energy applications and high-speed rotating machinery. The sensor adopts a meander-line structure and a high-permittivity alumina ceramic substrate, achieving a size reduction of over 59%. Additionally, a high-temperature-resistant miniaturized broadband coplanar waveguide antenna is designed as the interrogation antenna to replace the traditional horn antenna, enabling wireless signal transmission in high-temperature environments. Through HFSS simulation optimization combined with multidirectional strain loading and temperature-rise simulations, the feasibility of the sensor for simultaneously monitoring multidirectional strain and temperature is verified. Wireless transmission performance tests show that the sensor signal is optimal at a distance of 4 cm from the interrogation antenna. To comprehensively evaluate the sensor performance, two separate test systems were established for room temperature and high temperature, respectively. The room-temperature strain test system employs a horn antenna to achieve accurate synchronous monitoring of strain in three directions within the range of 0–500 με. The high-temperature testing part consists of two independent subsystems: a high-temperature temperature test system is used to complete temperature calibration in the range of 15 °C to 800 °C; and a variable-temperature strain test system is used to achieve multidirectional characterization of strain from 0–500 με within the same temperature range. Based on the test data, a temperature compensation model for the strain signals is established, enabling effective decoupling of strain and temperature under varying temperature conditions. The experimental results indicate that the maximum temperature sensitivity of the sensor is 341.67 kHz/°C, and the average strain sensitivity under varying temperature environments reaches up to 26.14 kHz/με, with measurement errors not exceeding 5%. The fitting errors for both room-temperature strain and temperature measurements are less than 0.1%. Repeatability tests further confirm the stability of the sensor performance.
