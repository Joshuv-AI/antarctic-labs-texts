Information and Education Needs in Prostate Cancer Care in Western Guangxi, China: A Single-Center Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Jianing Cao, Changying He, Jing Ji, Ning Cao, Xiaohuan Peng, Lifang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMen receiving prostate cancer care often need clear information about disease status, tests, treatment effects, and follow-up. This study described information and education needs among men with prostate cancer in western Guangxi, China and examined routinely available factors that may help healthcare teams identify patients who need more support.Patients and MethodsIn this single-center cross-sectional study, 320 men with histologically confirmed prostate cancer were recruited from outpatient and inpatient urology units in western Guangxi from January 2024 to June 2025. Participants completed the Chinese 34-item Short-Form Supportive Care Needs Survey (SCNS-SF34), Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale, Medical Coping Modes Questionnaire, and Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-Prostate. The health system and information needs (HSIN) domain of the SCNS-SF34 was the primary outcome. Group comparisons, Spearman correlations, multiple linear regression with HC3 robust standard errors, and false-discovery-rate correction for exploratory testing families were used.ResultsThe mean HSIN score was 45.01 ± 21.25, and 252 participants (78.8%) reported moderate-to-high need on at least one HSIN item. The leading priorities were knowing whether cancer was under control or improving (33.1%), receiving test results promptly (32.2%), and receiving written care information (31.9%). In the main adjusted model, higher HSIN scores were associated with poorer Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (B = 5.162; 95% CI, 1.791–8.533; P = 0.003) and monthly household income of CNY 6000–9999 compared with CNY < 3000 (B = 7.222; 95% CI, 1.424–13.019; P = 0.015).ConclusionInformation needs were common and centered on disease-status explanation, timely test-result communication, and written materials. Brief HSIN screening may support urology, nursing, and follow-up teams in tailoring patient education and care coordination. These cross-sectional findings identify practical targets for future service-improvement studies.

Introduction

Prostate cancer care often requires patients and families to interpret disease status, prostate-specific antigen (PSA) monitoring, treatment goals, adverse effects, functional changes, and follow-up plans across several care transitions.1–7 In this setting, information is not only educational content; it is part of care delivery. Patients need explanations that are timely, understandable, and easy to revisit after the clinical encounter.

The 34-item Short-Form Supportive Care Needs Survey (SCNS-SF34) measures supportive care needs across physical and daily living, psychological, sexuality, patient care and support, and health system and information needs (HSIN) domains.8–10 The HSIN domain is especially relevant to multidisciplinary healthcare because it covers disease and treatment explanations, test-result feedback, written information, reassurance from staff, and access to someone who can discuss care concerns.

Western Guangxi is served by tertiary referral hospitals as well as county and rural care networks. Schooling, household income, travel burden, language environment, health literacy, and family caregiving capacity may shape how patients seek, understand, and use health information.11–13 Ethnicity was therefore treated as a sociocultural and healthcare-access variable, not as a biological category.

Evidence on supportive care needs among Chinese men with prostate cancer remains limited, and item-level information priorities are less clearly described.10,14 Identifying the most common information needs, and the routine clinical or functional markers associated with them, may help urologists, oncology nurses, and follow-up staff decide when a short explanation is enough and when more structured education is needed.

This study aimed to describe the level and item-level pattern of HSIN among men with prostate cancer in western Guangxi, China, and to examine demographic, clinical, functional, and psychosocial correlates that may inform patient education and care coordination. Because of the cross-sectional design, the study was intended to generate practice-relevant hypotheses rather than determine directionality.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Setting

We carried out a single-center, cross-sectional study in the outpatient and inpatient urology units of the Affiliated Hospital of Youjiang Medical University for Nationalities, a tertiary referral center in Baise, western Guangxi, China. This setting serves patients from urban, county, and rural areas with variable access to specialist urology and oncology services. Enrollment ran from January 1, 2024 through June 30, 2025, and participants were recruited by convenience sampling. Reporting follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement (Checklist S1).15

Participants and Recruitment

The inclusion criteria were: (1) histopathologically confirmed prostate cancer; (2) age ≥18 years; (3) ability to understand the study and complete the questionnaire survey; and (4) voluntary participation with written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were: (1) concomitant other primary malignancies; (2) severe mental disorder, cognitive impairment, or communication impairment affecting scale assessment; (3) clinical status judged by the treatment team as unsuitable for questionnaire completion; and (4) questionnaire completion rate <80%.

During the study period, 448 patients with histopathologically confirmed prostate cancer were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 116 were excluded before the questionnaire survey (concomitant other primary malignancy, n = 20; severe mental disorder, cognitive impairment, or communication impairment, n = 7; clinical status judged unsuitable for questionnaire completion, n = 11; declined to participate, n = 78). The remaining 332 eligible patients provided written informed consent and completed the questionnaires on site. After data quality control, 12 questionnaires were excluded (completion rate <80%, n = 9; inconsistent responses or implausible values not reconciled by source verification, n = 3). The final analytic sample comprised 320 patients (Figure 1). Sample Size

Empirical guidance for multiple linear regression holds that the required sample size depends jointly on the number of candidate variables, the anticipated effect size, and model stability. Green’s widely cited rule of thumb, N ≥ 50 + 8m, expresses this in terms of m, the number of candidate explanatory variables.16 With roughly 12 to 15 such variables anticipated, the rule implied a minimum of about 146 to 170 patients, and the achieved sample of 320 comfortably satisfied the requirement for the primary multiple linear regression.

Study Instruments

Sociodemographic and clinical data form. A researcher-designed form collected sociodemographic characteristics (age, ethnicity, marital status, educational level, occupation, residence, monthly household income, and medical payment method) and clinical variables (TNM stage, Gleason score, PSA level, treatment modality, current treatment phase, Charlson Comorbidity Index [CCI] score, number of comorbidities, time since diagnosis, and ECOG performance status [ECOG PS]). Ethnicity was self-reported and was collected because western Guangxi is a multiethnic region in which language, health literacy, and healthcare access may influence patients’ information-seeking behavior and communication experience; ethnicity was treated as a sociocultural rather than a biological variable. ECOG PS was evaluated according to the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status criteria.17

SCNS-SF34. We administered the validated Chinese-language SCNS-SF34.8,10,18 Its 34 items are distributed across five domains: physical and daily living (5 items), psychological (10 items), sexuality (3 items), patient care and support (5 items), and HSIN (11 items). Responses use a 5-point Likert format from 1 (no need/not applicable) to 5 (high need), and raw domain totals were converted to standardized 0–100 scores following the manual.8 The standardized HSIN score served as the primary outcome. At the item level, a rating ≥3 denoted low-or-higher need, ≥4 moderate-to-high need, and 5 high need. Internal consistency in our sample was excellent (Cronbach α = 0.956 for the full SCNS-SF34 and 0.877 for the HSIN subscale).

CD-RISC. Psychological resilience was measured with the validated 25-item Chinese Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC)19,20 (Cronbach α = 0.910). MCMQ. Disease-related coping was captured with the validated 20-item Chinese Medical Coping Modes Questionnaire (MCMQ), comprising confrontation (α = 0.835), avoidance (α = 0.750), and resignation (α = 0.786) subscales.21,22 FACT-P. Quality of life was assessed using the validated Chinese Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-Prostate (FACT-P);23,24 the FACT-P39 total score (α = 0.950) was the principal QoL measure, and the Trial Outcome Index (TOI) captured physical function and disease-specific outcomes.

Data Collection and Quality Control

Trained research staff briefed each patient, during clinic visits or while hospitalized, on the study’s aims, what completing the questionnaire involved, and privacy safeguards. After written informed consent, patients completed the instruments on site. When a patient could comprehend but had difficulty reading, staff read every item and response option aloud following a standardized script, avoiding interpretive prompts. Each returned questionnaire was inspected for completeness immediately, and two researchers independently cross-checked clinical data against the electronic medical record. Every retained questionnaire had a completion rate of at least 80% (observed range 85.0%–99.9%; mean 92.48%). Missing values were addressed by listwise deletion; because the overall missing rate was below 3%, no item-level imputation was carried out.

Conceptual Framework and Covariate Selection

Covariate selection was guided a priori by a conceptual framework rather than by univariate screening alone. The framework specified six assessment domains relevant to HSIN and patient education priorities: sociodemographic context, disease and treatment burden, functional status, psychosocial adaptation, prostate cancer-related quality of life, and contextual healthcare-access factors (Figure 2). Sociodemographic context included age, education, household income, residence, and ethnicity; ethnicity was treated as a sociocultural and contextual variable, not as a biological variable. Disease and treatment burden included TNM stage, PSA level, Gleason score, treatment modality, treatment phase, time since diagnosis, and comorbidity burden. ECOG performance status (ECOG PS) was prespecified as a functional-status marker and was included in the main adjusted model because it is routinely available in clinical care and may summarize functional burden relevant to information use. Overall TNM stage and individual T, N, and M categories were not entered simultaneously to avoid redundant representation of disease extent. Coping style, resilience, and FACT-P scores were treated as exploratory psychosocial correlates because temporal ordering cannot be determined in a cross-sectional survey. Statistical Analysis

Primary analyses were run in SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and additional exploratory interaction and adjusted-prediction analyses were run in Python 3.12. Continuous data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) and categorical data as counts and percentages. The distribution of standardized HSIN scores was checked with the Shapiro–Wilk test; because these scores departed from normality, groups were compared with the Mann–Whitney U-test (two groups) or the Kruskal–Wallis H-test (more than two groups), and pairwise associations were examined by Spearman rank correlation (rho).

The standardized HSIN score was analyzed as the primary continuous outcome. The main adjusted model included variables selected from the prespecified framework: age, education, household income, ethnicity, residence, TNM stage, Gleason score, log10(PSA + 1), treatment modality, treatment phase, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, time since diagnosis, and ECOG PS. A stage-focused sensitivity model omitted ECOG PS to show how the stage association appeared before accounting for functional status. Coping style, resilience, and quality-of-life measures were examined descriptively and in bivariate analyses as exploratory psychosocial correlates rather than as causal mediators. Additional exploratory analyses examined whether associations differed between Zhuang and Han participants by fitting ethnicity-stratified adjusted models and Han-by-predictor interaction terms for ECOG PS, household income category, and TNM stage. Because income was collected as four ordered categories rather than exact monetary values, restricted cubic spline modeling was not undertaken; instead, adjusted predicted HSIN means were estimated for each income category with bootstrap 95% CIs, and an ordinal quadratic income sensitivity model was fitted. HC3 heteroscedasticity-consistent robust standard errors were used, and unstandardized coefficients (B), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and P values were reported. The adjusted model was specified from the conceptual framework and was therefore treated as the primary model-estimation set rather than as a multiple-screening exercise. For exploratory testing families, Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate (FDR) q values were calculated for univariate group comparisons, Spearman correlations, ethnicity interaction tests, and stratified follow-up estimates; these q values are reported in the supplementary tables. A supplementary logistic regression using moderate-to-high HSIN as a binary outcome was retained only as supportive evidence because this outcome was common in the sample. Tests were two-sided with significance set at P < 0.05. Missing values were handled by listwise deletion; the overall missing rate was below 3%. All variance inflation factors (VIFs) were <10.

The mean age was 70.48 ± 8.39 years (median 71.00; IQR, 65.00–77.00), and most participants were recruited during inpatient care (n = 236, 73.8%). Han (n = 171, 53.4%) and Zhuang (n = 137, 42.8%) were the predominant ethnic groups, 75.3% had junior middle school education or below, and 46.3% lived in rural areas. Exploratory Correlates of HSIN

Exploratory bivariate analyses were strengthened by Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction (Tables S3 and S4). After correction, standardized HSIN scores still differed by ethnicity, TNM stage, T stage, N stage, M stage, treatment modality, and ECOG PS (all q ≤ 0.036). FDR-corrected Spearman analyses showed positive correlations of HSIN with PSA level, Gleason score, ECOG PS, and MCMQ resignation, and inverse correlations with MCMQ confrontation, FACT-P39 total score, and FACT-P Trial Outcome Index (TOI) (all q < 0.002). Age, time since diagnosis, comorbidity markers, CD-RISC total score, and MCMQ avoidance were not associated with HSIN after FDR correction. Because all variables were measured at one time point, these associations were interpreted as exploratory rather than directional.

Monthly household income of CNY 6000–9999 was also associated with higher HSIN compared with income below CNY 3000 (B = 7.222; 95% CI, 1.424–13.019; P = 0.015). In the stage-focused sensitivity model without ECOG PS (R2 = 0.184; adjusted R2 = 0.126), TNM stage IV was associated with higher HSIN (B = 14.744; 95% CI, 3.738–25.749; P = 0.009), and monthly household income of CNY 6000–9999 remained associated with higher HSIN (B = 6.773; 95% CI, 0.819–12.727; P = 0.026) (Table 4 and Table S6). The attenuation of the stage IV coefficient after ECOG PS was added may indicate that functional status summarizes part of the clinical burden relevant to information needs, but mediation cannot be inferred from these cross-sectional data.

For completeness, the supportive logistic regression using moderate-to-high HSIN as a binary outcome is provided in Table S7. Because 78.8% of participants met this threshold, the binary analysis had limited ability to separate degrees of information need and was not used as the primary basis for interpretation.

FDR-corrected exploratory interaction analyses supported a more nuanced reading of the multiethnic setting and the income signal (Tables S8–S10). Among Zhuang, Han, and other ethnicities, observed mean standardized HSIN scores were 42.40, 47.59, and 37.88, respectively. Among the three Han-by-predictor interaction tests in Zhuang and Han participants, only the ethnicity-by-income interaction remained below the FDR threshold (global P = 0.008; q = 0.024); interactions for ECOG PS and TNM stage did not (q = 0.446 and q = 0.684, respectively). Stratified adjusted estimates were therefore used to describe direction and magnitude rather than to define subgroup-specific rules. Income of CNY 6000–9999 and CNY ≥ 10,000 showed larger positive coefficients among Zhuang participants, whereas these coefficients were near zero among Han participants (Table S9). Adjusted predicted HSIN by income category was highest in the CNY 6000–9999 group and similar in the CNY ≥ 10,000 group, but the ordinal quadratic income term was not significant (P = 0.802).

Discussion

Principal Findings

In this single-center cross-sectional study, HSIN was examined as a healthcare delivery and patient education issue, complementing prior prostate cancer supportive-care literature that identifies information and communication needs as a recurrent component of unmet care needs.3,6 Among 320 men with prostate cancer in western Guangxi, nearly four in five participants reported moderate-to-high need on at least one HSIN item. Their leading priorities were practical: knowing whether the cancer was controlled or improving, receiving test results promptly, and obtaining written information about important aspects of care. In adjusted analyses, ECOG PS and a practical socioeconomic signal were associated with higher HSIN, whereas the stage IV association weakened after functional status was included. Exploratory stratified analyses suggested that the income signal was concentrated among Zhuang participants, while ECOG PS remained clearer among Han participants. These findings may inform need assessment and service design, but they do not constitute a clinical prediction model.

Patient Education Priorities in Routine Care

The leading HSIN items show that patients wanted information that helps them understand where they stand clinically and what they should do next. This pattern is consistent with qualitative evidence from prostate cancer survivors and broader cancer literature indicating that patients often value information that preserves a sense of control and clarifies prognosis, diagnosis, treatment options, and the next steps in care.25,26 Disease-control status and timely test-result feedback are central to uncertainty management during prostate cancer treatment and follow-up. Written information matters because patients and families often need to review explanations after discharge or outpatient visits. For older patients, patients with limited schooling, and those who travel between county-level and tertiary care settings, a short verbal explanation may not be enough.

These findings suggest several practical education strategies that could be adapted and tested in routine care: a written care summary, a structured explanation of PSA and imaging results, family-inclusive teaching when the patient agrees, and clear navigation information for follow-up appointments, reimbursement questions, and warning symptoms. Question prompt lists and patient-centered communication frameworks offer related, testable approaches for helping patients raise concerns, clarify priorities, and participate more actively in consultations.27,28 Because the study was observational, these strategies should be treated as service-improvement hypotheses rather than established recommendations.

Functional Status as a Practical Assessment Marker

ECOG PS was associated with higher HSIN in the main adjusted model. This has practical relevance because ECOG PS is already documented in many oncology and urology settings and is closely connected to a patient’s ability to attend visits, absorb information, manage treatment effects, and rely on family caregivers. A recent study of men with metastatic hormone-naive prostate cancer also reported that clinician-rated ECOG PS varied with FACT-P and other patient-reported health-related quality-of-life scores, supporting the clinical plausibility of this marker while not making it interchangeable with patient-reported experience.29 The attenuation of the stage IV coefficient after ECOG PS was added does not establish mediation; stage and function were measured at the same time. It does suggest that functional status may summarize part of the illness burden that makes information support more difficult and more necessary.

Socioeconomic Context and the Income Signal

The association between household income and HSIN should be interpreted cautiously. Income did not show a simple dose-response gradient, and exact income values were not available for spline modeling. Adjusted predicted HSIN was highest in the CNY 6000–9999 group and remained similar in the highest-income category. The FDR-corrected interaction analysis suggested that the income-HSIN association differed between Zhuang and Han participants. The stratified estimates pointed to larger income coefficients among Zhuang participants, although this pattern may reflect unmeasured differences in language-concordant communication, health literacy, travel burden, reimbursement concerns, or expectations for specialist care. These interpretations are consistent with cancer communication and health-literacy literature showing that understanding, recall, and participation can be constrained by literacy and communication fit, and with prostate cancer survivorship literature linking health literacy with self-management and patient-reported outcomes.30–32 Clinically, this pattern supports asking about practical access barriers and information preferences rather than relying on income or ethnicity alone.

Psychosocial Correlates

Lower confrontation coping, higher resignation coping, poorer FACT-P39 and TOI scores, and higher HSIN were correlated in bivariate analyses. These relationships may reflect shared illness burden, communication barriers, unmet supportive care needs, or emotional distress; the direction cannot be determined. This interpretation is consistent with earlier evidence that supportive care needs in prostate cancer often coexist with quality-of-life and psychosocial concerns, although our bivariate analyses cannot determine temporal ordering.3,6,14,23,24 The lack of a significant correlation between CD-RISC total score and HSIN also should not be read as evidence that resilience is irrelevant. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether information support changes coping, quality of life, or follow-up behavior, or whether these factors identify patients who need more support.

Implications for Multidisciplinary Healthcare

Patient education and follow-up communication in this setting involve more than one discipline. Urologists and oncologists may use the item priorities to structure disease-status and test-result explanations. Oncology nurses and patient educators may adapt those explanations into written summaries and teach-back checks. Follow-up coordinators or social workers may support appointment navigation, reimbursement questions, and links to community or county-level care. These actions are consistent with patient-centered cancer communication and question-prompt approaches, as well as health-literacy work emphasizing confirmation of understanding.27,28,30,31 In a multiethnic service region, the exploratory income-by-ethnicity signal also supports asking about language preference, family involvement, local care access, and preferred formats for written information rather than assuming that the same marker identifies information needs in every subgroup. Limitations

Several limitations warrant mention. First, the study was conducted at a single center using convenience sampling, and the inpatient predominance may limit generalizability to primary hospitals, community follow-up, or differently resourced settings. Second, the cross-sectional design cannot establish causal direction among disease stage, functional status, coping style, quality of life, and HSIN. Third, several variables came from self-reported scales and may be affected by recall or social desirability bias. Fourth, the models explained only part of the variance in HSIN (R2 = 0.209 for the main adjusted model and R2 = 0.184 for the sensitivity model), suggesting that unmeasured factors remain important. Health literacy, family caregiver capacity, social support, provider communication quality, digital access, and language-concordant communication were not directly measured. Fifth, the exploratory testing families were additionally evaluated with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, and the income-by-ethnicity interaction remained below q < 0.05; nevertheless, the ethnicity-stratified estimates should be interpreted as direction-and-magnitude summaries because the other-ethnicity group was too small for separate modeling and income was recorded in categories rather than exact monetary values. Sixth, the logistic analysis used a common binary outcome and was therefore retained only as supportive evidence.

Conclusion

In this single-center cross-sectional study, information and education needs were common among men receiving prostate cancer care in western Guangxi. The highest priorities concerned disease-control status, timely test-result feedback, and written care information. ECOG PS and practical socioeconomic or contextual information may help healthcare teams identify patients who may benefit from more detailed assessment and accessible education. Future multicenter longitudinal and intervention studies should test whether structured result feedback, written care summaries, and family-inclusive education improve understanding, follow-up behavior, and patient experience.
