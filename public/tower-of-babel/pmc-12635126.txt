Analysis of the effect of arterial embolization combined with external fixation in the treatment of unstable pelvic fracture with hemorrhagic shock
Authors: Jiadong Shao, Changyuan Xie, Weigen Yao, Gang Huang, Jisen Chu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To assess the clinical efficacy and safety of comparing arterial embolization (AE) combined with external fixation (EF) versus EF alone in the treatment of unstable pelvic fractures with hemorrhagic shock. Ninety patients with unstable pelvic fracture with hemorrhagic shock admitted to our hospital from March 2017 to February 2025 were collected and divided into AE + EF group (n = 45) and EF group (n = 45) according to treatment. Initial vital signs, ISS score, shock index SI, initial systolic blood pressure SBP, postoperative 24 h red blood cell transfusion volume, hemostasis success rate, ICU stay time, and in-hospital mortality rate were recorded and compared between the two groups. Baseline characteristics (age, ISS score, shock index, preoperative and intraoperative blood transfusions, and initial systolic blood pressure) were not statistically different between the two groups (P > 0.05). Compared with the EF group, the AE + EF group had a low 24-h postoperative red blood cell transfusion (2.0 [0–3.0] vs. 3.0 [2.0–3.5] U, P = 0.028), a high success rate of hemostasis (97.78% vs. 82.22%, P = 0.030), and a short ICU stay ( 4.0 [2.0–8.0] vs. 7.0 [5.0–9.0] days, P = 0.020), and low mortality (4.44% vs. 24.44%, P = 0.016). Compared with EF alone, AE combined with EF significantly improves hemostasis, reduces transfusion dependence, decreases ICU burden, and results in decreased mortality in patients with unstable pelvic fractures with hemorrhagic shock. It is recommended as the preferred treatment option for patients with hemodynamically unstable pelvic fractures with hemorrhagic shock.

Introduction

Unstable pelvic fracture with hemorrhagic shock is one of the most challenging emergencies in the field of traumatology and orthopedics, with an in-hospital mortality rate of 15–40%1–3. Such injuries often result from high-energy trauma, and the accompanying arterial rupture and venous plexus hemorrhage can rapidly trigger hemodynamic collapse4. In recent years, External Fixation (EF) has been widely used as a classical means of mechanical stabilization of the pelvic ring to control bleeding from pelvic fractures5, but its limitations for arterial hemorrhage are becoming apparent6. With the development of interventional radiology techniques, arterial embolization (AE) has become a complementary hemostatic strategy, but its synergistic effect with EF and its safety still need to be thoroughly explored.

Existing studies have shown that EF alone controls bleeding of venous origin by 70–85%7, but it has limited effect on persistent bleeding due to injury to the branches of the internal iliac arteries8. A study by Grilz et al.9 noted that untreated arterial hemorrhage can increase mortality by 3.2 times. In contrast, the AE technique, by selectively embolizing the bleeding artery, can theoretically achieve precise hemostasis, but there are potential risks such as delayed operation time and contrast nephropathy10. Of note, a systematic review by Cullinane et al.11 showed that combination therapy may shorten ICU stays, but the evidence for this is still limited to small sample cohort studies. The aim of this study is to clarify the effect of combined treatment in terms of transfusion requirements, ICU stay time and hemostatic success rate, as well as to systematically assess its safety by comparatively analyzing the clinical outcomes of AE + EF versus EF alone. The results of the study will provide a high-level evidence-based basis for optimizing the EF process in patients with unstable pelvic fracture with hemorrhagic shock.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study utilized a retrospective cohort study design and followed the STROBE statement12. Five hundred and sixty-two patients with pelvic fractures admitted to Yuyao People’s Hospital from March 2017 to February 2025 were included, among which 102 cases of unstable pelvic fractures with hemodynamic instability were eligible, and 90 patients with unstable pelvic fracture with hemorrhagic shock (Tile C type/Young-Burgess APC III/LC III) were included after the exclusion criteria. They were categorized into AE + EF group (n = 45) and EF group (n = 45) according to the treatment modality, as shown in Fig. 1.The exclusion criteria included 1) Age ≤ 18 years or ≥ 75 years (n = 5), 2) Death in emergency surgery (n = 3), 3) Thoracic and abdominal surgery during treatment (n = 2), 4) Time from injury to admission exceeding 6 h (n = 1), 5) End-stage renal disease (n = 1).Fig. 1Patient grouping flow chart. Intervention

Patients in the EF group underwent pelvic external fixation in accordance with the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma guidelines13, targeting a pelvic volume compression of ≥ 30%14. Patients in the AE + EF group initially underwent arterial embolization under digital subtraction angiography (DSA) guidance. The procedural sequence encompassed puncturing the femoral artery on the healthy side or the less severely injured side, followed by catheter insertion. A 5F pigtail catheter (Terumo Corporation, Japan) was positioned superior to the abdominal aortic bifurcation for angiography, thereby imaging the bilateral internal iliac arteries. In cases where angiography revealed positive findings (such as contrast extravasation, arteriovenous fistula, or pseudoaneurysm), a superselective microcatheter was advanced to the bleeding vessel, and gelatin sponge particles (350–560 μm) manufactured by Hangzhou Alicon Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. were employed to embolize the target site, with follow-up angiography confirming the resolution of bleeding signs; in instances of negative angiography, the corresponding branches of the internal iliac artery were directly embolized based on imaging localization of severe fracture regions. Subsequently, akin to the EF group, external fixation was performed, and the catheter sheath was removed after 24 h. The hemostatic approaches employed in this investigation were rigorously confined to external fixation (EF) alone or in conjunction with arterial embolization (AE). At our institution, the management of hemodynamically unstable pelvic fracture patients follows a multidisciplinary diagnostic and treatment protocol aligned with current guidelines. Arterial embolization (AE) is considered a first-line treatment option for patients in hemorrhagic shock with imaging evidence of active arterial bleeding. The decision to proceed directly with AE or to combine it with preperitoneal pelvic packing (PPP) and external fixation (EF) in the operating room is made collaboratively by trauma surgeons, interventional radiologists, and anesthesiologists, based on the patient’s physiological condition, response to resuscitation, and the availability of a hybrid operating room.

Data collection

Initial vital signs, ISS score15, and shock index SI16 were recorded in both groups. Observational indicators: 24 h postoperative red blood cell transfusion (performed according to Massive Transfusion Protocol)17, hemostatic success rate (defined as stable SBP > 90 mmHg for 1 h without secondary intervention)18, ICU stay (meeting the criteria for transfer out: FiO2 < 40% and lactate < 2 mmol/L19), Hospitalization mortality, Total operation time consumption/minutes. Additional recording of DSA findings, number of embolized vessels, and complications (classified by SIR) in the treatment group20.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0. Measurement data were expressed as median [IQR] using the Mann–Whitney U test; count data were expressed as rate (%) using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact probability method. P < 0.05 was considered a statistically significant difference.

Hemostatic effect

The amount of red blood cells transfused at 24 h postoperatively was significantly lower in the AE + EF group than in the EF group (2.0 [0–3.0] vs. 3.0 [2.0–3.5] U, P = 0.028), and the overall hemostatic success rate was higher (97.78% vs. 82.22%, P = 0.030). In the AE + EF group, 28 patients (62.22%) exhibited positive results on digital subtraction angiography (DSA), while 17 patients (37.78%) showed negative results.

Prognostic indicators

ICU stay (4.0 [2.0–8.0] vs. 7.0 [5.0–9.0] days, P = 0.020) and total hospitalization time (23.0 [18.0–27.0] vs. 29.0 [22.0–36.0] days, P = 0.015) were significantly shorter in the combination therapy group. For mortality, it was 4.44% (2/45) in the AE + EF group, which was significantly lower than 24.44% (11/45) in the EF group (P = 0.016). And there is a significant difference in the duration of the surgery [42.60 (30.10; 55.10) vs. 75.40(57.20; 93.60), P < 0.01].

Discussion

Unstable pelvic fracture with hemorrhagic shock is a major challenge in emergency trauma care, with a mortality rate that can be as high as 20%-30%, and the main causes of death include sustained blood loss, multi-organ failure, and infection21. Lethal hemorrhage in pelvic fractures arises primarily from lacerations of the fracture breaks to branches of the internal iliac arteries or to the pelvic venous plexus, as well as secondary injuries to the soft tissues by the fracture fragments22. EF alone reduces pelvic volume by mechanical compression (volume compression rate of approximately 15–25%)23, but its control of bleeding of arterial origin is limited, especially when the source of bleeding is located in the presacral or obturator arteries. In this study, the 24-h postoperative erythrocyte transfusion in the AE + EF group (2.0 U) was significantly lower than that in the EF group (3.0 U), and the overall hemostatic success rate was as high as 97.78%. The reasons considered may be targeted embolization to block the source of bleeding, gelatin sponge particles can directly block active bleeding through selective embolization of contrast extravasation vessels, rather than relying on passive compression of the pelvic volume; reduce secondary coagulation dysfunction, and large amounts of transfusion are prone to trigger dilutional coagulopathy24, but AE may indirectly improve coagulation by reducing the need for blood transfusions.

In this study, the AE + EF group had significantly shorter ICU stay (4 days vs. 7 days) and hospitalization (23 days vs. 29 days), and the mortality rate was reduced to 4.44%, and this advantage may stem from the fact that: systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) was reduced in the AE + EF group, and external fixation in combination with ERAS concepts25 (e.g., early disembarkation) inhibited inflammatory cascade response, while AE further reduces the inflammatory load through rapid hemostasis. The source of active bleeding (contrast extravasation or pseudoaneurysm forming vessels) was precisely blocked by AE26, which enabled the patient to rapidly reach hemodynamic stabilization (SBP > 90 mmHg for 1 h)27, which enabled the patient to rapidly reach hemodynamic stabilization (SBP > 90 mmHg for 1 h). Sequential treatment with AE combined with EF has significant minimally invasive advantages over traditional open surgery28. Gelatin sponge embolization only requires percutaneous puncture and cannulation for target vessel occlusion, avoiding extensive tissue stripping, while external fixation with Orthofix® stents placed through a limited incision maximizes the protection of the integrity of peripelvic soft tissues and neurovascular bundles, reducing the risk of medically induced injuries (e.g., supraglottic artery misligation or sciatic nerve injuries)29. AE directly reduces shock-related ischemia–reperfusion injury by blocking bleeding of arterial origin (approximately 15% of pelvic hemorrhage30), while EF reduces venous plexus bleeding pressure by restoring the negative pressure effect of the pelvic ring and prevents clot dislodgement triggered by fracture end micromotion. By effectively controlling arterial and venous bleeding, the combined AE + EF methodology may aid in suppressing the cascade activation of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), thereby mitigating mortality rates24. This study confirms for the first time, by strictly matching baseline data, that the combination of AE + EF reduces mortality in patients with unstable pelvic fracture shock to 4.44%, which is an 81.8% reduction compared to EF alone, a result close to that of the study by Oliver et al31 (mortality 8.1%). Similar to the retrospective analysis conducted by DuanMu et al., which encompassed 161 patients with severe pelvic fractures between January 2019 and November 2024, the shock index exhibited a significant reduction following arterial embolization (AE), particularly among those who underwent early AE intervention, and their mortality rates were correspondingly lower32.

For this study, we need to clarify the shortcomings that, as a single-center retrospective cohort analysis, the allocation of treatment regimens did not use a randomization process and relied mainly on individualized decision-making by clinicians (e.g., technology preference, equipment accessibility, and risk assessment in emergencies), which may introduce selection bias. Interventional embolization (e.g., gelatin sponge particle size selection, target vessel selection criteria) and external fixation implementation details (stent type, compression force adjustment) lacked standardization, and differences in operator experience and institutional hardware conditions (e.g., DSA equipment resolution) may have confounding effects on hemostatic efficacy and complication rates. The current results reflect the characteristics of the treatment system in specific medical centers (tertiary trauma centers), whose efficiency of multidisciplinary collaboration (e.g., interventional-orthopedic response time ≤ 1 h) may be significantly better than that in primary care hospitals, and thus the generalizability of the therapeutic advantages needs to be further verified. In this retrospective study, the omission of arterial embolization (AE) in the control group was not based on research protocol requirements but was a result of clinical decision-making at the time, influenced by factors such as the patient’s hemodynamic status, assessment of suspected bleeding sources, and the availability of hospital resources.

Conclusion

AE combined with EF significantly improves the success rate of hemostasis, reduces the need for blood transfusion and mortality in patients with unstable pelvic fracture with hemorrhagic shock, and is recommended as the first choice of treatment for patients with hemodynamically unstable pelvic fracture with hemorrhagic shock.
