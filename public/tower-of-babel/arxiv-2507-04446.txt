Tail-Aware Adversarial Attacks: A Distributional Approach to Efficient LLM Jailbreaking
Tim Beyer, Yan Scholten, Leo Schwinn, Stephan Günnemann

Abstract
To guarantee safe and robust deployment of large language models (LLMs) at scale, it is critical to accurately assess their adversarial robustness. Existing adversarial attacks typically target harmful responses in single-point, greedy generations, overlooking the inherently stochastic nature of LLMs. In this paper, we propose a novel framework for adversarial robustness evaluation that explicitly models the entire output distribution, including tail-risks, providing better estimates for model robustness at scale. By casting the attack process as a resource allocation problem between optimization and sampling, we determine compute-optimal tradeoffs and show that integrating sampling into existing attacks boosts ASR by up to 48% and improves efficiency by up to two orders of magnitude. Our framework also enables us to analyze how different attack algorithms affect output harm distributions. Surprisingly, we find that most optimization strategies have little effect on output harmfulness. Finally, we introduce a data-free proof-of-concept objective based on entropy-maximization to demonstrate how our tail-aware perspective enables new optimization targets. Overall, our findings highlight the importance of tail-aware attacks and evaluation protocols to accurately assess and strengthen LLM safety. 1 1 1 Project page: https://www.cs.cit.tum.de/daml/tail-aware-attacks/

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) exhibit impressive performance across a wide range of tasks, yet ensuring their safe and reliable deployment continues to pose significant challenges
(
Achiam et al. 2023
;
Schwinn et al. 2025
)
. A fundamental goal of LLM safety research is to minimize the risk that models exhibit harmful behaviors or can be maliciously exploited
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
;
Bai et al. 2022
)
. While the probability of such failures may be low in well-aligned models, even rare harmful outputs can have severe consequences when deployed at scale
(
Jones et al. 2025
)
.

Despite the importance of LLM safety, existing robustness evaluations largely rely on point estimates rather than principled risk assessment. After generating an attack (e.g., via gradient-based methods), models are typically evaluated using a single response (often greedily generated) to assess attack success
(
Zou et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Chao et al. 2023
)
. This perspective has two key limitations: First, it ignores the likelihood of harmful outputs under realistic sampling conditions. Second, it overlooks the attacker’s effort required to elevate the risk of harmful generations.

Figure 1:
Sampling-aware variants of existing algorithms improve efficiency by 2 OOM and increase attack success rate. Data shown for Llama 3.1 8B.

In this paper, we propose a fundamental shift in how adversarial attacks against LLMs are optimized and evaluated. Rather than relying on deterministic point estimates, we advocate for focusing on the risk of obtaining a harmful response
under sampling
.

This distributional perspective opens new avenues for enhancing adversarial attacks by combining optimization with sampling, increasing the probability of observing harmful responses while minimizing attack costs.
Rather than deferring evaluation until the optimization is fully completed, we can leverage the fact that high-risk samples can be elicited with reasonable probability much earlier in the optimization, enabling us to substantially improve the effectiveness of existing attacks (Figures
1
&
2
).

We further show that constraints on computational budgets introduce a resource allocation problem between balancing the optimization of adversarial inputs with sampling from the evolving output distribution. Highly vulnerable models may produce harmful outputs with minimal optimization, whereas more robust models require extensive optimization before sampling becomes worthwhile.

To enhance attacks in sampling environments, we further propose a novel model-agnostic and data-free optimization objective based on maximizing the entropy of the distribution of the first predicted token. We demonstrate that this objective matches the widely used manually designed affirmative response objective while yielding more natural responses.

In an exhaustive experimental evaluation, we empirically demonstrate that state-of-the-art attacks consistently overestimate LLM robustness. Notably, our proposed perspective shift allows attack strategies that consistently outperform existing attacks in efficiency by up to two orders of magnitude and increase attack success rates by up to 48 percentage points.

Our key contributions are:

•

We introduce a distributional perspective on adversarial attacks, focusing on maximizing the risk of harmful responses under sampling rather than relying on deterministic point estimates.

•

We demonstrate that our tail-aware approach leads to more efficient and effective adversarial attacks, achieving up to two orders of magnitude reduction in computational cost and a 35 percentage point increase in attack success rates compared to state-of-the-art methods.

•

We investigate the effect of different optimization strategies on the output harm distribution of the attacked model.

•

We propose a novel label-free and adaptive attack objective based on maximizing the entropy of the distribution of the first predicted token, which is specifically designed for tail-aware evaluation and leads to more natural responses.

Overall, our novel perspective on adversarial attacks provides a more accurate and efficient framework for evaluating LLM safety, enabling better risk assessment towards stronger mitigation strategies.

Related work

Existing LLM attacks.
The current literature on LLM attacks presents two distinct attack strategies. One set of methods focuses on finding a single prompt that reliably elicits a harmful response
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Zou et al. 2023
)
.

Zou et al. 2023
first show that a single adversarial prompt can consistently trigger affirmative responses to harmful queries. Their approach, Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG), optimizes adversarial suffix tokens to maximize the likelihood of a predefined affirmative target response to circumvent alignment.
Later work uses genetic algorithms to generate jailbreaks resembling natural language inputs, making them harder to detect by perplexity defenses
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
.
The other class of methods relies on sampling a large number of completions while achieving low per-sample success rates. For example, by sampling one completion at every attack iteration
(
Schwinn et al. 2024
;
Hughes et al. 2024
;
Andriushchenko et al. 2024
)
or generating multiple candidate jailbreaks for every sample
(
Liao & Sun 2024
)
.

Both strategies overlook the probabilistic nature of the generation process, where even a small chance of a harmful response can trigger long-tail risk through repeated sampling with the same attack.
While developing reliable and consistent jailbreak prompts is valuable for many practical use cases, accurately characterizing tail risks remains crucial for providing safety guarantees, especially in large-scale real-world deployments
(
Jones et al. 2025
)
.
Moreover, even approaches that use multiple attempts often rely on overly simplistic point estimates of robustness provided by greedy generations, which fail to capture true risk and lead to an overestimation of model robustness
(
Scholten et al. 2024
)
.
Motivated by these limitations, we propose to bridge this gap by integrating both optimization and sampling to evaluate the combined effect of both paradigms in a principled probabilistic framework.

Attacking LLMs via sampling.

Huang et al. 2023
evaluate using decoding hyperparameters to elicit harmful attacks from models but do not investigate adversarial attacks combined with sampling.
Another recently proposed black-box method, Best-of-N jailbreaks
(
Hughes et al. 2024
)
, applies simple perturbations to a harmful prompt to generate a large amount (10,000) of independent augmented prompts, and then evaluates them for harmfulness.

Scholten et al. 2024
challenges the greedy generation paradigm and systematically evaluates unlearning and toxicity over output distributions rather than using the greedy generation as a point-wise estimator, but does not consider models under attack.

Jones et al. 2025
sample multiple generations to forecast rare events (including harmful behavior) before widespread model deployment.
None of these methods explores improving adversarial attack optimization and evaluation via an optimal combination of sampling and optimization.

Objectives for LLM adversarial attacks.

Zou et al. 2023
introduced the affirmative response objective aimed at consistently generating harmful responses by inducing the victim model to generate a compliant prefix.

Zhu et al. 2024
used uncensored models to produce more natural, in-distribution affirmative targets, which are easier to optimize for.
Other work employs attacker LLMs to automatically generate jailbreaks for targeted models
(
Chao et al. 2023
)
.
A recent approach by
Geisler et al. 2025
uses feedback from LLM-judges via REINFORCE and combines this with affirmative targets, which results in a flexible optimization objective. Notably, all of these approaches use guidance in their optimization approaches, either through optimization labels or judge models. In this work, we propose a label-free objective designed for tail-aware attack strategies (see Section
5.1
).

3
Background and preliminaries

Large language models.
Given a vocabulary
V
, we model LLMs as parameterized functions
f_{\theta}:V^{*}\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}}(R)
, which take a sequence of tokens
q\in V^{*}
as input and return a probability distribution over responses
R\subseteq V^{*}
. LLMs can only be assessed sequentially,
f_{\theta}(y|q)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{\theta}(y_{i}|y_{<i},q)
, where
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
is a response of length
m
and
f_{\theta}(y_{i}|y_{<i},q)
is the conditional probability for the next token
y_{i}
. In practice, the supported response space is determined either by a fixed maximum response length
m
or by a stopping criterion, such as the end-of-text token.

Threat model.
In this paper, we focus on evasion settings where adversaries manipulate the input prompt
q
to generate harmful responses from the model. Attacks against LLMs may assume certain constraints on the adversarial inputs, and we generically model this attack space as
{\mathcal{C}}(q)\subseteq V^{*}
for a benign input
q
.
For example, suffix attacks assume that the adversary can only append an adversarial suffix to the prompt, i.e.,
{\mathcal{C}}(q)=\{q+x\mid x\in V^{*}\}
, where
+
denotes concatenation. Most attacks do not specify any constraints on the attack space, for which we have
{\mathcal{C}}(q)=V^{*}
.

Harmfulness.

We model harmfulness of responses using a bounded score function
h:V^{*}\rightarrow[0,1]
. Here, a score of 0 indicates that the response is not harmful, while a score of 1 indicates that it is harmful. In practice, we deploy a judge model (a specialized LLM) to determine the harmfulness of a response. In this paper, we are interested in the random variable
h(Y)
for responses
Y\sim f_{\theta}(q)
.

4
Attack objective for tail-aware adversarial attacks

In this paper, we argue that existing approaches focusing on greedy decoding fail to account for the inherent variability in model outputs and tail events. This is particularly problematic when the output distribution is multimodal or exhibits heavy tails, where harmful responses may occur infrequently but with high severity. In real-world deployments, LLMs are typically integrated into interactive systems at scale, where users receive responses sampled from the model’s distribution – making tail events especially critical. This setting necessitates a fundamental rethinking of adversarial attack design and evaluation. We advocate for a shift toward a tail-aware objective by modeling adversaries that maximize the expected worst-case harmful response under sampling:

Definition 1

(Tail-aware adversarial attacks)
.

Given a large language model
f_{\theta}
and a benign prompt
q\in V^{*}
, we introduce tail-aware adversarial attacks against LLMs as the maximization of the expected maximum harmfulness score of
n
independent samples from the model:

q_{\text{adv}}=\argmax_{\tilde{q}\in{\mathcal{C}}(q)}\;\mathbb{E}_{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\sim f_{\theta}(\tilde{q})}\left[\max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}h(Y_{i})\right]\\

(1)

where
{\mathcal{C}}(q)
is the (constrained) input space of possible adversarial prompts for benign prompt
q\in V^{*}
.

Intuitively, we propose to assess adversarial risk by considering adversaries that maximize the expected
worst-case
outcome across multiple samples, i.e. the maximum harmfulness score among several independent generations. This shift towards tail-aware evaluations better reflects how LLMs are used in practice and enables a more meaningful assessment of their robustness and safety.

4.1
Tail-aware adversarial attacks against LLMs

Solving the optimization problem in
Equation 1
is infeasible in practice due to combinatorial structure of the discrete input space and the complex, multimodal harmfulness distribution over model outputs.
In practice, we can only sample a finite number of responses from the model to approximate the expected maximum harmfulness score. We define this
empirical attack objective
of approximation quality
K
under
n
samples for input prompt
q
as follows:

{\mathcal{A}}_{K}(q,n)\triangleq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\;\max_{y_{1}^{(j)},\ldots,y_{n}^{(j)}\sim f_{\theta}(q)}h(y_{i})

(2)

where
y_{i}^{(j)}
are
n
independent samples from the model for the
j
-th refinement, and
K
is the number of refinements to approximate the expectation.
While
Equation 2
provides a practical way to evaluate the tail-aware adversarial attack objective, it is not directly amenable to optimization due to the non-differentiability of the sampling step. Instead, adversarial attacks can iteratively refine the input
q_{t}
by optimizing a proxy objective
{\mathcal{L}}_{K}
that approximates
{\mathcal{A}}_{K}
at each step
t
:

q_{t}=\locargmax_{\tilde{q}\in{\mathcal{C}}(q_{t-1})}\;{\mathcal{L}}_{K}(\tilde{q},n_{t})

(3)

where
q_{0}
is the benign input prompt
q
, and
\locargmax
denotes a local maximization procedure, such as gradient descent. The number of samples
n_{t}
used at step
t
can be fixed or adaptively chosen based on the optimization progress or computational budget.

4.2
Constrained tail-aware adversarial attacks against LLMs

In practice, we must account for the computational cost of sampling and optimization, which constrains the number of model queries and the extent of the search. In such settings, tail-aware adversarial attacks amount to a resource-allocation problem. Specifically, we assume that each optimization step incurs a cost
C_{\text{opt}}
and each sample incurs a cost
C_{\text{sample}}
. Given a computational budget
B
, we can then formulate the constrained attack objective as follows:

\displaystyle q_{t}=\locargmax_{\tilde{q}\in{\mathcal{C}}(q_{t-1})}\;{\mathcal{L}}_{K}(\tilde{q},n_{t})\quad\quad\forall t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}

Intuitively, adversaries seek a local optimum of

by optimizing a proxy objective (

) for

t
the adversary can sample
n_{t}
responses from the model. The optimization is subject to a computational budget
B
, which limits the total number of optimization steps and samples that can be drawn from the model.

5
Efficient tail-aware adversarial attacks against LLMs

The iterative optimization problem in
Equation 3
provides a foundation for tail-aware adversarial attacks and supports a wide range of algorithmic strategies, including those employed by existing methods. While our framework is compatible with prior optimization-based approaches, these methods typically use proxy objectives that are not tail-aware and perform little to no sampling during optimization, either relying on greedy decoding or a single sample per iteration
(
Chao et al. 2023
)
.

Efficient tail-aware attacks.
As the first to explore the direction of tail-aware attacks, we show that evaluating the empirical attack objective
{\mathcal{A}}_{K}
(
Equation 2
) at intermediate optimization steps of existing adversarial proxy objectives
{\mathcal{L}}
is already sufficient to achieve state-of-the-art, efficient adversarial attacks against LLMs under fixed optimization budgets. Specifically, this iterative approach of evaluating the empirical attack objective at intermediate steps results in adversarial attacks that are an order of magnitude more efficient while achieving higher attack success rates at the same time without conducting any changes to the adversarial attacks themselves.

The constrained optimization problem in
Equation 4
is a combinatorial problem, as it involves both the number of samples and the decision to continue or stop optimization. This makes exploring the full space computationally infeasible, especially under realistic resource constraints. To make the problem tractable, we restrict our analysis to a practical setting: a fixed number of optimization steps
t
, a fixed number of samples
n
after optimization, and approximation quality
K=1
.

5.1
Label-free loss for tail-aware adversarial attacks

Current optimization-based attacks focus maximizing the mean harm, either directly via reinforcement learning
(
Geisler et al. 2025
)
, or more commonly via proxy objectives
{\mathcal{L}}
, such as the affirmative response target.
In this objective, attackers attempt to elicit a specific predefined response prefix taken from a dataset
(
Zou et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2023
)
.
These responses typically follow a rigid structure of the form ”Sure, here’s [rephrased prompt]…” which are out-of-distribution for many modern models, introducing bias and making them hard to reach through prompt optimization
(
Zhu et al. 2024
;
Beyer et al. 2025
)
. Furthermore, due to its prevalence, model defenses will likely prioritize robustness against this particular objective
(
Geisler et al. 2025
)
.

Motivated by our tail-aware optimization perspective, we design a loss function focused specifically on maximizing the tail-aware objective introduced in
Equation 1
.
To this end, we propose a simple but powerful alternative to existing losses that is unbiased and does not rely on specific target sequences.
We introduce an
entropy-maximization objective
, where prompts are optimized to maximize the entropy of the victim model’s first-token predictions, thereby inducing a wide variety of responses:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{entropy}}(q)=-H\left(f_{\theta}(y_{1}\mid q,y_{1}\in S)\right)

where
f_{\theta}(y_{1}|q,y_{1}\in S)
is the next-token distribution over the vocabulary
V
conditioned on an allowed token-set
S
, and
H(p)
denotes the entropy of the distribution
p
.
Conditioning on allowed tokens ensures that sampling yields valid and diverse generations from a wide space of possible completions without yielding undesirable control tokens (e.g. end-of-text tokens). Unlike prior methods that primarily attempt to shift the mean or greedy mode of the harm distribution, our approach targets its spread, ultimately leading to a higher likelihood of tail-risk events.

6
Experiments

We conduct an extensive empirical study including over 5 billion generated tokens across models and attack strategies. Our experiments yield four key insights:

•

Tail-aware evaluation.
Scoring with our tail-aware objective exposes rare but severe failures, leading to significantly lower estimates for model robustness.

•

Compute-optimal sampling-augmented attacks.
We integrate sampling with existing optimization algorithms and determine pareto-optimal tradeoffs between optimization and sampling, boosting
ASR
by 47 p.p. and matching baseline
ASR
and
s_{harm}
at two OOM lower cost.

•

Harm-distribution evolution.
Tracking
h(Y)
throughout optimization provides insights to how attacks move probability mass from refusals toward harmful modes.

•

Entropy objective.
We show that tail-aware optimization opens new opportunities for optimization by presenting results for our data-free entropy-maximization objective.

Measuring attacker budget.
As proposed by
Boreiko et al. 2024
, we consider an adversary constrained by a fixed compute budget, measured in FLOPs to ensure hardware-agnostic evaluation.
Furthermore, FLOPs are likely a reasonable proxy for the cost of running hosted models and limit the impact of implementation details between attacks, which may be more or less optimized for wall-time, peak memory usage, or memory bandwidth.
3
3

3

Software for pure inference workloads is typically highly optimized, so treating inference FLOPs as equal to other operations likely overestimates their relative time and energy cost. That said, achieving commensurate wall-time speed-ups requires batching generations to avoid other latency bottlenecks.

In all experiments, we track FLOPs using commonly used approximations from
Kaplan et al. 2020
:
\text{FLOPs}_{\text{fwd}}\!=\!2\cdot N_{params}\!\cdot\!\left(N_{input}\!+\!N_{output}\right)
and
\text{FLOPs}_{\text{bwd}}\!=\!4\cdot N_{params}\!\cdot\!\left(N_{input}\!+\!N_{output}\right)
.
We carefully take into account KV caching and other optimizations to ensure fair and accurate comparisons across different attacks, models, and prompts.

Effect of optimization-based attacks on harm distributions.

We analyze how the harm distribution,
h(Y)
, evolves during an attack to gain insight into the optimization dynamics.
Empirically, we observe that harm distributions are often bi- or trimodal, roughly corresponding to three categories: refusals (
h(Y)\mathbin{<}0.1

0.1\mathbin{\leq}h(Y)\mathbin{\leq}0.5

h(Y)\mathbin{>}0.5

We also see high-severity outliers, which are not captured by standard evaluations, appear early during optimization.
Notably, our findings indicate that for most models, optimization-based attacks primarily suppress refusals, without significantly shifting the harmfulness of compliant responses (

This phenomenon is likely caused by the affirmative objective, which rewards compliance, but provides no signal towards genuine harm on already compliant responses.

This suggests that a hybrid approach, starting with entropy-maximization and transitioning to the affirmative objective could offer the best of both worlds.

Other findings.

Across all our experiments, we found no statistically significant difference between greedy responses and single probabilistic samples. The average success rates (ASR) were 16.43% for greedy responses and 14.07% for probabilistic samples.
A two-tailed z-test yields a p-value of 0.063, indicating that the observed difference is not statistically meaningful.

7
Limitations & Future Work

Our experiments focus on two-stage approaches that first optimize and then sample.
Exploring more sophisticated schedules that interleave optimization and sampling to inform the optimization with information from intermediate samples.
Such algorithms could, for example, adaptively estimate whether sampling or optimizing is likely to be more effective to elicit the targeted harm level and could adapt optimization effort to each prompt, reaching better trade-offs.
To draw samples, we used standard multinomial sampling. Exploring adversarial attacks in environments employing different strategies, such as min-p or nucleus sampling may offer additional insights.
Due to resource constraints, we focus on open-weights models under 10 billion parameters from the Gemma and Llama families.
While the observed trends are strong and highly robust, verifying that they also hold for other architectures and especially for larger models would be valuable.

8
Conclusion

We introduced a principled, tail-aware framework for evaluating and optimizing adversarial attacks on LLMs.
By explicitly accounting for tail-risk events, our approach provides a more realistic and robust assessment about the behavior of models deployed at scale.
We further demonstrated that sampling-based strategies can be integrated into existing adversarial attacks, yielding substantial improvements in both attack success rate and efficiency.
Notably, our approach is modular and can be stacked on top of any existing optimization-based attack without changing the underlying algorithm.

Through an analysis of the dynamics of the harm distribution throughout an attack, we observed that, for most models and attacks, optimization primarily reduces refusal rates but does not increase the average severity of compliant responses, with most optimization strategies having overall little effect on prompt effectiveness.
Finally, by adopting a distributional perspective, we enable new avenues for attack objectives. We showcase this by introducing a novel, data-free entropy-maximization objective which is particularly effective at high sample counts.

Broader Impact

This work aims to improve the robustness of large language models (LLMs) against adversarial attacks, which is crucial for ensuring their safe deployment in real-world applications. By enhancing the evaluation and optimization of adversarial attacks, we contribute to a better understanding of LLM vulnerabilities and the development of more resilient models. However, the potential misuse of adversarial techniques for malicious purposes remains a concern, and we emphasize the importance of responsible research practices and ethical considerations in this field.
