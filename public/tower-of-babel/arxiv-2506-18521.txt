Semiconvection unlocks dynamo action in stably-stratified regions of gaseous planets • XSHELLS: https://nschaeff.bitbucket.io/xshells/ • planetMagFields: https://ankitbarik.github.io/planetMagFields/
Paul Pružina, David Cébron, Nathanäel Schaeffer

Abstract
Stably-stratified fluid layers are ubiquitous in gaseous planets, stellar and liquid cores, and have previously been thought not to be capable of driving a dynamo. We demonstrate that semiconvection (convection driven by a destabilising thermal gradient in an overall stably stratified region) can actually lead to dynamo action in such regions. Motivated by recent works suggesting that significant regions of Jupiter and Saturn may be prone to semiconvection, we conduct direct numerical simulations in spherical shells in the planetary relevant limit of low magnetic Prandtl numbers. We retrieve key characteristics of planetary magnetic fields, including dipolarity, magnetic field strength, and the form of the energy spectrum. This short demonstration opens the possibility for further more detailed studies of semiconvection dynamos, that can also be relevant for stellar interiors.

I
Introduction

Gaseous planets such as Jupiter and Saturn are thought to be comprised of a surface layer of molecular hydrogen, above a deep core of metallic hydrogen, so-called for its high electrical conductivity. Planetary magnetic fields are likely maintained by the dynamo action of convection-driven flows in this metallic hydrogen core. The transition between these regions is gradual, but peak conductivity is reached by radius
0.8R_{J}
for Jupiter, and
0.5R_{S}
in Saturn.
(
C. A. Jones 2011
)

Previous dynamo models have focused on overturning convection
(
T. Gastine et al. 2012
;
C. Jones 2014
, e.g.)
, but convective layers in planets may be too deep deep inside the planetary interior to explain the features of the observed magnetic fields
(
F. Debras & G. Chabrier 2019
, e.g.)
. Recent structure models propose that, closer to the surface, there may exist large regions with a stably stratified density, but destabilising temperature gradients
(
J. Leconte & G. Chabrier 2012
;
F. Debras & G. Chabrier 2019
)
. These regions are unstable to semiconvection, a form of double diffusive convection. This is a phenomenon in which fluid motions are driven by differences in the gradients of two scalars that both contribute to the fluid density, that diffuse at different rates
(
M. Schwarzschild & R. Härm 1958
)
. In astrophysical applications, the fast-diffusing scalar is temperature
T
, and the slow-diffusing scalar is composition
C
of some heavy element (e.g. helium). However, semiconvection also occurs in terrestrial oceans, where salinity is the slow-diffusing component, and has been observed in laboratory experiments of salt-sugar solutions. The semiconvection regime is characterised by a destabilising thermal gradient, and stabilising compositional gradient; the opposite case is referred to as fingering convection.
An excellent introduction to the subject is provided by
T. Radko 2013
.

It is thought that the radial extent of dynamo action in a planet can be estimated based on the magnetic energy spectrum
(
F. Lowes 1974
)
. Using this approach for Jupiter, with the data from the JUNO mission, gives an estimate of
r_{\text{dyn}}\approx 0.8R_{J}
, where
R_{J}
is the radius of Jupiter
(
J. Connerney et al. 2022
)
. However, based on the flow strength required to generate a dynamo,
L. D. Duarte et al. 2018
suggest a much shallower estimate, with the dynamo layer extending to
0.9R_{J}
. For either estimate, it is likely that at least part of the dynamo region is semiconvective, with a stabilising composition gradient and destabilising temperature gradient
(
J. Leconte & G. Chabrier 2012
;
F. Debras & G. Chabrier 2019
)
.

Double-diffusive fluids display a rich range of behaviours, with very different turbulent transport properties to single-component convection
(
P. Garaud 2018
, e.g.)
One notable feature is the tendency to form density layers
(
P. Pružina 2025
, e.g.)
, where wide regions of relatively uniform density are separated by sharp interfaces. Much of the recent work on rotating semiconvection has focussed on this layered regime
(
R. Moll & P. Garaud 2016
;
J. Fuentes 2025
, e.g.)
, leaving the behaviour outside layered states poorly understood.

In fingering convection, several studies have shown the formation of strong zonal jets
(
R. Monville et al. 2019
;
C. Guervilly 2022
;
T. Tassin et al. 2024
, e.g.)
. Shear instabilities acting on such a zonal jet structure are known to be able to support a planetary dynamo
(
H. Spruit 2002
;
C. Guervilly et al. 2012
, e.g.)
; it seems reasonable that could also provide a mechanism for semiconvection to drive a dynamo. Despite this, there have been few previous attempts to demonstrate a dynamo maintained by semiconvection.
J. F. Mather & R. D. Simitev 2021
concluded that it was only possibly for unrealistically large values of the magnetic Prandtl number, which characterises the ratio of viscous to magnetic diffusion, and thus semiconvection dynamos were unlikely to be relevant to stellar and planetary magnetic fields. By exploring a wider range of parameter space, we demonstrate a self-sustaining semiconvection-driven dynamo, for parameter values relevant to Jupiter, with a realistic dipolar form, and ratio of magnetic to kinetic energies.

While our primary motivation is to explain the observed magnetic fields of planets in our solar system, dynamo action also supports stellar magnetic fields.
(
C. A. Jones et al. 2010
;
P. Charbonneau 2014
, e.g.)
. With semiconvective regions also present in main sequence stars
(
W. J. Merryfield 1995
;
H. Spruit 2013
;
F. Zaussinger & H. Spruit 2013
, e.g.)
, and likely to be present in exoplanets, the possibility of semiconvection to drive a dynamo has the potential to significantly improve our understanding of astrophysical magnetic fields.

In this letter, we present simulation results demonstrating a semiconvection dynamo in a rapidly rotating spherical shell, for low magnetic Prandtl numbers. In §
II
, we present the physical setup and governing equations for the problem. In §
III
we discuss the parameter ranges and expected structure of a Jupiter-like magnetic field; and in §
IV
we present simulation results and compare them with the predictions of §
III
. Finally, in §
V
we summarise our findings and discuss the potential for future studies.

II
Governing equations

Based on the work of
F. Debras & G. Chabrier 2019
, we consider the semiconvection region to be a spherical shell
R_{i}\leq r\leq R_{o}
, rotating at rate
\Omega
and sandwiched between two convective regions. We assume that the temperature and composition in these convective regions are fixed, with
T(R_{o})-T(R_{i})=-\Delta T
and
C(R_{o})-C(R_{i})=\Delta C
. We adopt spherical polar coordinates, with radial, polar and azimuthal basis vectors
\{\bm{e}_{r},\bm{e}_{\theta},\bm{e}_{\phi}\}
defined such
\theta=0
when the position vector
\bm{r}
is aligned with the rotation vector
\bm{\Omega}=\Omega\bm{e}_{z}
. A summary of this setup is shown in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Outline of the physical setup of the problem.

The evolution of the fluid velocity
\bm{u}=u_{r}\bm{e}_{r}+u_{\theta}\bm{e}_{\theta}+u_{\phi}\bm{e}_{\phi}
, reduced pressure
p
(that includes centrifugal effects), temperature
T
, composition
C
and magnetic field
\bm{b}
are governed by the Boussinesq equations in the presence of background temperature and composition fields
T_{0}(\bm{r})
and
C_{0}(\bm{r})
, coupled with the magnetic induction equation, expressed in dimensionless form as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{u}+\left(\frac{2}{{\rm{Ek}}}\bm{e}_{z}+\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{u}\right)\times\bm{u}=-\bm{\nabla}p

\displaystyle\>-(T+C)\bm{e}_{r}+(\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{b})\times\bm{b}+\nabla^{2}\bm{u},

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}T+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\left(T+T_{0}\right)=\frac{1}{\Pr}\nabla^{2}T,

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}C+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\left(C+C_{0}\right)=\frac{1}{{\rm{Sc}}}\nabla^{2}C,

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{b}=\bm{\nabla}\times\left(\bm{u}\times\bm{b}-\frac{1}{{\rm{Pm}}}\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{b}\right)

(4)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}=0,

(5)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{b}=0.

(6)

These equations are nondimensionalised based on the outer sphere radius
R_{o}
and viscous timescale
\tau_{\nu}=R_{o}^{2}/\nu
, where
\nu
is the kinematic viscosity. The dimensional fields can be recovered as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{dim}=\frac{\nu}{R_{o}}\bm{u},

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{dim}=\sqrt{\rho_{0}\mu_{0}}\frac{\nu}{R_{o}}\bm{b},

\displaystyle\quad T_{dim}=\frac{\nu^{2}}{g\alpha_{T}R_{o}^{3}}T,

\displaystyle C_{dim}=\frac{\nu^{2}}{g\alpha_{C}R_{o}^{3}}C,

(7)

where
\alpha_{T}
and
\alpha_{C}
are the coefficients of thermal and compositional expansion,
g
is gravitational acceleration
\rho_{0}
is a reference density and
\mu_{0}
the vacuum permeability.
The system (
1
)–(
6
) is controlled by four dimensionless parameters: the Ekman number

{\rm{Ek}}=\frac{\nu}{R_{o}^{2}\Omega}

(8)

and the Prandtl, Schmidt and magnetic Prandtl numbers

\Pr=\frac{\nu}{\kappa_{T}},\quad{\rm{Sc}}=\frac{\nu}{\kappa_{C}},\quad{\rm{Pm}}=\frac{\nu}{\eta},

(9)

where
\kappa_{T}
,
\kappa_{C}
and
\eta
are the thermal, compositional and magnetic diffusivities. The ratio of the compositional to thermal diffusivities is called the Lewis number
L={\rm{Sc}}/\Pr
. The shell thickness is nondimensionalised to
1-\Delta R\leq r\leq 1
, where
\Delta R=(R_{o}-R_{i})/R_{o}
. We assume a constant gravitational acceleration, pointing in the radial direction
\bm{e}_{r}
.

The background temperature and salinity fields are given by

T_{0}=\frac{{\rm{Ra}}_{T}}{\Pr}\delta(r),\quad C_{0}=\frac{{\rm{Ra}}_{C}}{{\rm{Sc}}}\delta(r),

(10)

where
\delta(r)=-\left(1-\Delta R\right)\left(1-r\right)/r\Delta R
is the spherically symmetric solution to Laplace’s equation in a spherical shell. The thermal and compositional Rayleigh numbers are defined as

{\rm{Ra}}_{T}=\frac{\alpha_{T}g\Delta TR_{o}^{3}}{\kappa_{T}\nu},\quad{\rm{Ra}}_{C}=-\frac{\alpha_{C}g\Delta CR_{o}^{3}}{\kappa_{C}\nu},

(11)

where
\alpha_{T}
and
\alpha_{C}
are the thermal and compositional expansion coefficients,
g
is the gravitational acceleration, and
\Delta T
and
\Delta C
are the dimensional thermal and compositional differences across the layer, as shown in figure
1
. The backgrounds fields can be used to define the Brunt-Väisälä frequency
N
, representing the frequency of oscillations of a displaced particle in the stratified fluid,

N^{2}=-\frac{{\rm{Ra}}_{T}}{\Pr}-\frac{{\rm{Ra}}_{C}}{{\rm{Sc}}}

(12)

In a fluid that is statically stable to top-heavy convection,
N^{2}>0
. The semiconvection region is defined by a stabilising compositional gradient and destabilising temperature gradient, i.e.

{\rm{Ra}}_{T}>0,\quad{\rm{Ra}}_{C}<0.

(13)

Within this regime, the semiconvection instability will develop if

1\leq R_{\rho}^{*}=\left|\frac{{\rm{Ra}}_{C}/Sc}{{\rm{Ra}}_{T}/Pr}\right|\leq\frac{\Pr+1}{\Pr+\Pr/{\rm{Sc}}},

(14)

where the quantity
R_{\rho}^{*}
is referred to as the (inverse) density ratio
(
J. S. Turner 1979
, e.g.)
.

To approximate the convective regions above and below the shell, we adopt fixed temperature, fixed composition and stress-free velocity boundary conditions as follows.

\begin{cases}T=0\\
C=0\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(u_{\theta}/r\right)=0\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(u_{\phi}/r\right)=0\\
\bm{e}_{r}\cdot\bm{u}=0\\
\end{cases}\text{ at }r=1-\Delta R,1.

(15)

We assume that inner convective region is electrically conducting, as suggested by
F. Debras & G. Chabrier 2019
, with the same electrical conductivity than the semiconvection layer, and adopt an insulating boundary condition on the outer sphere, to represent the low conductivity of molecular hydrogen. At the inner boundary,
\bm{b}
and
\partial\bm{b}/\partial r
are set to be continuous. Indeed, we consider the same conductivity and permeability in the fluid and in the inner domain. We have also tested a setup with an insulating core; the results are not significantly different, and it is computationally more expensive. The total angular momentum is set to zero.

We use the XSHELLS code
(
N. Schaeffer 2013
;
N. Schaeffer et al. 2017
)
to solve equations (
1
)–(
6
), in the presence of background fields (
10
), with boundary conditions (
15
).

III
Conditions for a Jupiter-like dynamo

While the results that follow are relevant to all gaseous planets (and rotating magnetic fluids in general), we are motivated in particular by Jupiter. Hence we aim to adopt parameter values, and judge our results, based on observations of Jupiter.
Astrophysical fluids are characterised by small Prandtl number
\Pr\ll 1
, large Lewis number
L\gg 1

(
P. Garaud 2018
)
, as well as small magnetic Prandtl number
{\rm{Pm}}\ll 1

(
P. H. Roberts & G. A. Glatzmaier 2000
)
. At leading order, Jupiter’s radial magnetic field is strongly dipolar, with the dipolar fraction of the magnetic energy
f_{dip}\approx 0.7

(
J. Connerney et al. 2022
)
.

The huge scales of gaseous planets are still inaccessible for direct numerical simulations. For Jupiter, we estimate the thermal Rayleigh number and Ekman number, as defined by equations (
11
) and (
8
) (details given in Appendix
A
)

{\rm{Ra}}_{T}=O(10^{36}),\quad{\rm{Ek}}=O(10^{-19}).

(16)

To locate the correct parameter space for our simulations, we follow
R. Monville et al. 2019
, who found that, for the fingering regime of double diffusive convection, the hydrodynamic behaviour could be characterised based on the stratification-rotation ratio

\frac{N}{\Omega}={\rm{Ek}}\sqrt{-{\rm{Ra}}_{T}/\Pr-{\rm{Ra}}_{C}/{\rm{Sc}}}

(17)

For the semiconvection layer of Jupiter,

0.01\lesssim N/\Omega\lesssim 1.

(18)

Details of these estimates are given in Appendix
A
.

From the kinetic energy, we define the Reynolds number (the ratio of inertial to viscous forces) as follows

{\rm{Re}}=\frac{\sqrt{2\langle E_{u}\rangle_{V}}R_{o}}{\nu},

(19)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{V}
is a space-average across the entire domain. We define the magnetic Reynolds number (the ratio of induction to magnetic diffusion) as :

{\rm{R}}_{m}={\rm{Pm}}{\rm{Re}},

(20)

For a dynamo to develop, induction must dominate diffusion, i.e.
{\rm{R}}_{m}\gg 1
. The exact value depends on the geometry and flow dynamics, but
{\rm{R}}_{m}\geq O(10^{3})
is generally sufficient. It follows from equation (
20
) that for a small-
{\rm{Pm}}
dynamo,
{\rm{R}}_{m}
must be large, requiring strong turbulent motions.

The flow structure of Jupiter takes the form of a series of zonal jets, alternating in direction east/west. Observations show a large number of these jets responsible for the striking striped pattern at the surface. At the surface, these are very strong, with characteristic velocities
U=O(10-100)~$\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$
, however beneath the surface layer the velocities reduce sharply to
O(0.01-1)~$\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$

(
K. Moore et al. 2019
)
.
U. R. Christensen & P. N. Wulff 2024
argue that this reduction in jet strength is due to interaction with the stably stratified semiconvection layer, where buoyancy and electromagnetic forces act as a brake on the flow.

The kinetic and magnetic energy densities can be calculated as

e_{U}=\frac{1}{2}\rho U^{2},\quad e_{B}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{B^{2}}{\mu_{0}},

(21)

where
\rho
is the fluid density,
\mu
is the magnetic permeability,
U
and
B
are the dimensional velocity and magnetic field strength. Taking values appropriate for the semiconvection layer
\rho=10^{3}~$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{m}^{-3}$
,
\mu_{0}=4\pi\times 10^{-7}~$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\,\mathrm{A}^{-2}$

(
M. French et al. 2012
)
,
U=O(0.01-1)~$\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$

(
K. Moore et al. 2019
)
and
B=4\times 10^{-4}~$\mathrm{T}$

(
J. Connerney et al. 2022
)
, we estimate the energy ratio as

10^{-4}\lesssim\frac{e_{B}}{e_{U}}\lesssim 1.

(22)

Hence, it is possible that the magnetic energy may be of a similar size to the kinetic energy, and as such the magnetic field may have significant effects on the flow structure via the Lorentz force.

IV
Simulation results

In this section, we first briefly discuss the general behaviour of magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) simulations, and the critical parameters for a dynamo to develop. We then describe in detail a single long simulation, chosen to demonstrate a self-sustaining dynamo in the planetary relevant regime of low
{\rm{Pm}}
. We consider the variation in the magnetic energy, the three-dimensional form of the fields, and the energy spectra, and show that these compare favourably to our expectations for Jupiter.

IV.1
Onset of Dynamo Action

To find the suitable parameter ranges for dynamo action, we have conducted a number of simulations for a range of parameter values. With
\Pr=0.3
,
{\rm{Sc}}=3
and
\Delta R=0.5
fixed, we vary
{\rm{Ek}}
,
{\rm{Ra}}_{C}
,
{\rm{Ra}}_{T}
and
{\rm{Pm}}
. We run hydrodynamic simulations until the semiconvection instability has saturated, then seed a random small-amplitude magnetic field. After an initial adjustment period, the simulation magnetic energy
E_{b}^{S}
undergoes a linear growth or decay phase
E_{b}^{S}\sim\exp(\sigma t)
. Figure

shows a summary of the behaviour of simulations in
{\rm{Ek}}
–

{\rm{R}}_{m}
above a critical value
{\rm{R}}_{m}^{C}({\rm{Ek}})
, which appears to be roughly independent of
{\rm{Pm}}
. For
10^{-6}\leq{\rm{Ek}}\leq 10^{-4}
,
250\lesssim{\rm{R}}_{m}^{C}\lesssim 850
. Figure

shows that
{\rm{R}}_{m}^{C}
increasing as
{\rm{Ek}}
increases, meaning that simulations with relatively small values of

are favoured to achieve a dynamo at low

.

V
Discussion & Conclusions

In this paper, we have demonstrated the possibility of semiconvection driving a planetary dynamo at low magnetic Prandtl number. Our simulation results show striking resemblance to observational data from Jupiter, with a strongly dipolar surface field for the majority of the time, and higher-
l
structures visible near the equator. The ratio of magnetic to kinetic energies lies within our predicted range, and an estimation based on this range obtains a very similar magnetic field strength to observational data. The existence of a semiconvection dynamo at low
{\rm{Pm}}
runs contrary to the work of
J. F. Mather & R. D. Simitev 2021
, who concluded that unrealistically high values of
{\rm{Pm}}
would be required, and thus semiconvection was unlikely to be important for planetary magnetic fields. The simulations of
J. F. Mather & R. D. Simitev 2021
were performed at a relatively slow rotation rate
Ek\approx 10^{-4}
and relatively modest temperature and composition gradients
{\rm{Ra}}_{T}=O(10^{5})
,
{\rm{Ra}}_{C}=O(10^{6})
. By contrast, we take
{\rm{Ek}}=2\times 10^{-6}
,
{\rm{Ra}}_{C}=2\times 10^{10}
and
{\rm{Ra}}_{T}=1.67\times 10^{9}
. These values, while still far from realistic astrophysical parameters, result in a semiconvection field with much higher
{\rm{Re}}
, and therefore a value of
{\rm{R}}_{m}
above the dynamo threshold can be achieved for much smaller
{\rm{Pm}}
.

Here, we consider parameter values that are fairly modest compared to true planetary values, but provide computational challenges. Simulations with these values are made possible by the tremendous efficiency of the XSHELLS solver
N. Schaeffer 2013
, and particularly it’s GPU implementation. Thus, any more extreme parameters would require incredible large numbers of GPU-hours. A key difference from a more realistic planetary setup is the aspect ratio;
F. Debras & G. Chabrier 2019
suggest a maximum thickness of
0.25R_{J}
for the semiconvection layer — much less than our
\Delta R=0.5
. However the reduction in layer depth proves to be rather costly computationally, with a significantly larger spectral resolution for similar parameters. At
{\rm{Pm}}=0.2
, we are also near the maximum value that could truly be considered ‘low-
{\rm{Pm}}
’. An extension to even lower values would be an useful development, as would an investigation of how the form of the magnetic field changes as
{\rm{Pm}}
is varied.

Of course, this is only a preliminary demonstration of a subject that is sure to provoke significant further research. The semiconvective instability in a rotating spherical geometry is still poorly understood, and a thorough study of both the onset of semiconvection and the form of the resultant flows is needed. A more detailed investigation of the dynamo, considering a wide range of parameter space, would allow general trends to be identified, and the results to be extrapolated to true planetary scales. We hope to report on both of these themes in the near future.

Appendix B
Magnetic field strength

To estimate the dimensional magnetic field strength, we assume that the ratio of the mean magnetic to kinetic energies in the simulation is the same as in Jupiter, i.e.

\frac{\bar{E}_{b}^{S}}{\bar{E}_{u}^{S}}=\frac{e_{B}}{e_{U}}.

(B1)

From fig.
3
, the mean dimensionless kinetic energy in the simulation is
\bar{E}_{u}^{S}\approx 6\times 10^{6}
, giving an energy density of
\bar{E}_{u}^{S}/V=1.64\times 10^{6}
, where
V
is the fluid volume. We estimate the local kinetic energy density in the semiconvection layer of Jupiter taking density
\rho_{0}=10^{3}~$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{m}^{-3}$
and velocity
U=1~$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}^{-1}$
(at the upper end of the range given by
K. Moore et al. 2019
), to give
e_{U}=500~$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{m}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-2}$
. The ratio of the planetary energy density to simulation energy is then

Q=\frac{e_{U}}{\bar{E}_{u}^{S}}=8.33\times 10^{-5}~$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{m}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-2}$

(B2)

Using this ratio in equation (
B1
), we obtain an estimate
\tilde{e}_{B}
for the local magnetic energy density:

\tilde{e}_{B}=Q\bar{E}_{b}^{S}=167~$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{m}^{-1}\mathrm{s}^{-2}$

(B3)

For the magnetic field, the simulation field is rescaled by the square root of the ratio
Q
multiplied by the vacuum permeability (cf. equation
21
), giving the dimensional estimate
\tilde{\bm{B}}
(in Tesla):

\tilde{\bm{B}}=\sqrt{\mu_{0}Q}\bm{b}=0.014\bm{b},

(B4)

which is plotted in fig.
6
.

It should be noted that the value of
Q
given by equation (
B2
) depends strongly on the choice of velocity scale
U
, which we have taken to be at the upper end of the range given by
K. Moore et al. 2019
, with
Q\propto U^{2}
. As such, the redimensionaled field
\tilde{\bm{B}}
plotted in fig.
6
(b) is also also on the upper end of the range, so our simulations certainly underestimate
\tilde{\bm{B}}
compared to the true planetary values in fig.
6
(c).
