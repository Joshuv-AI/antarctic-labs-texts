Testing parametric models for the angular measure for bivariate extremes
Stéphane Lhaut, Johan Segers

Abstract
The angular measure on the unit sphere characterizes the first-order dependence structure of the components of a random vector in extreme regions and is defined in terms of standardized margins. Its statistical recovery is an important step in learning problems involving observations far away from the center. In this paper, we test the goodness-of-fit of a given parametric model to the extremal dependence structure of a bivariate random sample. The proposed test statistic consists of a weighted L 1 L_{1} -Wasserstein distance between a nonparametric, rank-based estimator of the true angular measure obtained by maximizing a Euclidean likelihood on the one hand, and a parametric estimator of the angular measure on the other hand. The asymptotic distribution of the test statistic under the null hypothesis is derived and is used to obtain critical values for the proposed testing procedure via a parametric bootstrap. Consistency of the bootstrap algorithm is proved. A simulation study illustrates the finite-sample performance of the test for the logistic and Hüsler–Reiss models. We apply the method to test for the Hüsler–Reiss model in the context of river discharge data.

Introduction

In various domains, such as meteorology, finance or engineering, multivariate extreme events may induce important perturbations of the system of interest, so that the occurrence probabilities of such events are of main concern. Dangerous combinations of wave heights and still water levels could lead to the collapse of a dike at a certain point at the coast
[
12
]
.
The financial portfolio selection problem involves maximizing the return on investment subject to risk constraints; if those constraints are expressed in terms of quantities such as the Value-at-Risk or the Expected Shortfall, tail quantiles of the portfolio need to be estimated accurately
[
36
]
.
Floating ystems in an offshore environment are submitted to natural forces such as wind, waves and currents, and may be sensible to certain combinations of large values of these forces, the probabilities of which need to be assessed
[
32
]
.

Extreme value theory
[
13
,
35
]
provides a convenient and solid mathematical framework to answer those questions. In the multivariate case, the classical assumption is that the random vector of interest
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
, with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, belongs to the
maximal domain of attraction
of a multivariate extreme value distribution. This hypothesis comprises two parts:

1.

the marginal distributions of
X
belong to the maximal domains of attraction of some univariate extreme value distributions;

2.

after marginal transformation, through the probability integral transform or a variation thereof, the joint distribution of the transformed vector belongs to the maximal domain of attraction of a multivariate extreme value distribution with pre-specified margins.

Under the side assumption that the marginal cumulative distribution functions of
X
are continuous, which we make in this paper, point 2 above only involves the copula of
X
in view of Sklar’s theorem. Point 2 can be imposed on its own, that is, independently of the assumptions on the margins in point 1, and this is what we will do in this paper.

Angular measure.

The multivariate extreme value distribution to which the distribution in point 2 is attracted is fully determined by an
angular measure
, denoted here by
\Phi
, which is a measure on the intersection between the positive orthant
[0,\infty)^{d}
with the unit sphere with respect to a given norm. This measure, originally called spectral measure in
[
15
]
, describes the first-order dependence structure of joint extremes of
X
and is rooted in the theory of multivariate regular variation
[
35
, Chapter 5]
.

Inference on the angular measure plays a major role in various problems related to extremes. Without being exhaustive, we mention the following examples: estimating the probability of a failure set
[
12
,
16
]
, estimating extreme quantile regions
[
18
]
, anomaly detection
[
25
]
, approximating conditional densities of an element of a random vector given that the others are large
[
10
]
, and binary classification in extreme regions
[
29
]
.

For a given dimension
d
, the collection of all angular measures does not form a parametric family. Hence, a natural way to reduce the complexity of the inference problem is to
assume
a parametric form for
\Phi
. Many models have been proposed, sometimes expressed in terms of other objects, such as the stable tail dependence function: the original bivariate logistic model
[
26
]
, the asymmetric bivariate logistic model
[
38
]
, the tilted Dirichlet model
[
8
]
, the pairwise beta model
[
9
]
, the Hüsler–Reiss model
[
27
,
24
]
, among others.
For conclusions within the proposed models to be reliable, the parametric assumption has to be tested itself.

Testing parametric models for extremal dependence.

Goodness-of-fit tests for parametric models of the tail dependence structure have already been proposed in the literature.
A test based on a weighted
L_{2}
-distance between the tail empirical copula and the estimated copula under the postulated model with estimation of the parameter performed by censored likelihood maximization and critical values computed on the basis of parametric bootstrap is studied in
[
14
]
.
In a similar way, the limiting distribution of a test statistic consisting of a
L_{2}
-distance between the empirical stable tail dependence function and a parametric estimator with parameter estimation performed by the method of moments is derived in
[
20
]
; computation of critical values is not considered, however.
Another
L_{2}
-distance type of statistic based on the tail copula is considered in
[
2
]
with parameter under the null estimated via a minimum distance method and critical values computed on the basis of a multiplier bootstrap.
A different approach is proposed in
[
6
]
where authors develop asymptotically distribution-free tests based on various distances between a semi-parametric estimator of the tail copula and the estimated tail copula under the postulated model. Critical values are computed by simulating directly from limit law of the test statistic, which is distribution-free, that is, does not depend on unknown quantities. The computation of the test statistic itself is more involved.

In this paper, we propose a new test based on the angular measure directly. Our nonparametric estimator is a variation on the empirical angular measure, on which certain moment constraints are enforced by means of a Euclidean empirical likelihood in the spirit of
[
11
]
, an idea going back to
[
33
]
. The discrepancy between this nonparametric estimator and the one estimated within the parametric model is quantified by a weighted version of the
L_{1}
-
Wasserstein distance
coming from optimal transport
[
41
,
42
]
. The Wasserstein distance has a long history in hypothesis testing in the non-extreme setting, especially in the one-dimensional situation where it admits an explicit representation, see, e.g., the survey paper
[
34
]
for a review of its main statistical applications.
Working with bivariate extremes, the angular measure is defined on a one-dimensional space, so that we may use the explicit formula to compute our test statistic. We derive its limiting distribution under the null hypothesis and propose a consistent method to estimate the associated critical values.

We only consider the bivariate case, the extension to higher dimensions being far from trivial, because, besides consistency
[
19
]
and
[
30
]
, even the limit distribution of the empirical angular measure is not known in higher dimension. In the application in Section
5
, we apply the test to several pairs of variables at once, correcting the
p
-values in order to account for the multiple hypothesis problem.

2
Regular variation and the angular measure

2.1
Regular variation

Let
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
denote the marginal cumulative distribution functions of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, respectively, assumed to be continuous.
As in
[
19
,
22
]
, our working hypothesis is that the distribution of the standardized random vector

U=(U_{1},U_{2})=(1-F_{1}(X_{1}),1-F_{2}(X_{2})),

is
multivariate regularly varying
in the sense that there exists a Radon measure
\Lambda
on
\mathbb{E}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}[0,\infty]^{2}\setminus\{(\infty,\infty)\}
such that

t^{-1}\mathsf{P}\left[U/t\in\cdot\,\right]\xrightarrow{v}\Lambda(\,\cdot\,),\qquad t\rightarrow 0,

(1)

where
\xrightarrow{v}
denotes
vague convergence
of measures, see
[
35
, Section 3.4]
.
This is equivalent to assume that
U
is in the minimal domain of attraction of a min-stable distribution with standardized margins
[
35
, Proposition 5.17]
.
In terms of sets, vague convergence is equivalent to the following property: for any Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{E}
bounded away from the point
(\infty,\infty)
and with
\Lambda(\partial B)=0
, we have

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}t^{-1}\mathsf{P}\left[U\in tB\right]=\Lambda(B),

(2)

where
tB=\{(tx,ty):(x,y)\in B\}
.

Remark 1

(Link with the exponent measure)
.

Usually, multivariate regular variation is expressed through the standardized vector
(Z_{1},Z_{2})=(1/U_{1},1/U_{2})
with Pareto margins instead of uniform ones, and the vague convergence takes place on
\mathbb{E}^{-1}=[0,\infty]^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
instead. The limiting measure obtained through this standardization is often denoted by
\nu
and is referred to as the
exponent measure
. Here, we work with
\Lambda
for convenience, but it is clear that both measures are linked by the one-to-one relation

\nu=\iota_{\#}\Lambda=\Lambda\circ\iota^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Lambda=\iota^{-1}_{\#}\nu=\nu\circ\iota,

(3)

where
\iota:(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{E}\mapsto\iota(x_{1},x_{2})=(x_{1}^{-1},x_{2}^{-1})\in\mathbb{E}^{-1}
is the inverse map and where the subscript
\#
indicates the push-forward measure.

Below, we list some properties of the measure
\Lambda
which will be useful later:

•

\Lambda
has Lebesgue margins: for any
0\leq u<\infty
,

\Lambda([0,u]\times[0,\infty])=\Lambda([0,\infty]\times[0,u])=u.

(4)

•

\Lambda
is homogeneous: for any
c>0
and any Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{E}
,

\Lambda(cB)=c\Lambda(B).

(5)

2.2
Angular measure

The angular measure is a finite measure derived from the exponent measure and with support contained in the intersection of the unit sphere and the positive orthant
[0,\infty)^{2}
. Even though the angular measure can be defined with respect to any norm, we will only consider
L_{p}
-norms, the ones most used in practice, which are defined, for
p\in[1,\infty]
and
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, by

\|x\|_{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}(|x_{1}|^{p}+|x_{2}|^{p})^{1/p}&\text{if $1\leq p<\infty$,}\\
\max(x_{1},x_{2})&\text{if $p=\infty$.}\end{cases}

(6)

For
p\in[1,\infty]
, we will let
\Phi_{p}
denote the
angular measure
associated to the measure
\Lambda
in (
1
), defined on any Borel set
A\subset[0,\pi/2]
by

\Phi_{p}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\Lambda\left(\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in(0,\infty]^{2}:\|(x_{1}^{-1},x_{2}^{-1})\|_{p}\geq 1,\arctan(x_{2}/x_{1})\in A\}\right).

(7)

The inverses appearing in the radial component come from the fact that
\Phi_{p}
is usually defined in terms of
\nu
in (
3
) rather than
\Lambda
.
If, for
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
, we introduce the sets

C_{p,\theta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}([0,\infty]\times\{0\})\cup(\{\infty\}\times[0,1])&\text{if }\theta=0,\\
\left\{(x,y):0\leq x\leq\infty,0\leq y\leq\min\{x\tan\theta,y_{p}(x)\}\right\}&\text{if }0<\theta<\pi/2,\\
\left\{(x,y):0\leq x\leq\infty,0\leq y\leq y_{p}(x)\right\}&\text{if }\theta=\pi/2,\end{cases}

(8)

where

y_{p}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}\infty&\text{if }x\in[0,1),\\
\left(1+\frac{1}{x^{p}-1}\right)^{1/p}&\text{if }x\in[1,\infty]\text{ and }p\in[1,\infty),\\
1&\text{if }x\in[1,\infty]\text{ and }p=\infty,\end{cases}

then it follows that

\Phi_{p}(\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\Phi_{p}([0,\theta])=\Lambda(C_{p,\theta}).

For
x\geq 1
,
y_{p}(x)
is the smallest value of
y\geq 1
solving the equation
\|(x^{-1},y^{-1})\|_{p}=1
, while
x\tan\theta<y_{p}(x)
if and only if
x<x_{p}(\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\|(1,\cot\theta)\|_{p}
.

in case
p=1
and
0<\theta<\pi/2
.

The Lebesgue margins of
\Lambda

\Phi_{p}

(9)

It will be convenient to work with the

Q_{p}
defined for Borel sets

Q_{p}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\Phi_{p}(A)}{\Phi_{p}([0,\pi/2])},

\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{\sin\theta}{\|(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\|_{p}}\,\mathrm{d}Q_{p}(\theta)=m(Q_{p})=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{\cos\theta}{\|(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\|_{p}}\,\mathrm{d}Q_{p}(\theta),

(11)

m(Q_{p})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\Phi_{p}([0,\pi/2])^{-1}
.

2.3
Maximum empirical Euclidean likelihood estimator

Given is an independent random sample
\{(X_{i1},X_{i2}):i=1,\ldots,n\}
with the same distribution as
(X_{1},X_{2})
. We would like to obtain a nonparametric estimator of
\Phi_{p}
or, equivalently,
Q_{p}
. One way to proceed is to observe that, by (
2
) and (
7
), for any Borel set
A\subset[0,\pi/2]
whose topological boundary is a
\Phi_{p}
-null set, we have, writing
\overline{F}_{j}=1-F_{j}
,

\Phi_{p}(A)=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}t^{-1}\mathsf{P}\left[\left\|\left(\frac{1}{\overline{F}_{1}(X_{1})},\frac{1}{\overline{F}_{2}(X_{2})}\right)\right\|_{p}\geq t^{-1},\;\arctan\left\{\frac{\overline{F}_{2}(X_{2})}{\overline{F}_{1}(X_{1})}\right\}\in A\right].

Let
k=k(n)
be such that
k\rightarrow\infty
and
k/n\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Replacing
t
by
k/n
, the law of
(X_{1},X_{2})
by its empirical counterpart on the sample and estimating the marginal distribution functions by
\widehat{F}_{j}(x_{j})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{X_{ij}<x_{j}\}
with survival functions
\widehat{\overline{F}}_{j}=1-\widehat{F}_{j}
for
j=1,2
, leads to the
empirical angular measure
with distribution function

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi}_{p}(\theta)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{n}{k}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|\left(\frac{1}{\widehat{\overline{F}}_{1}(X_{i1})},\frac{1}{\widehat{\overline{F}}_{2}(X_{i2})}\right)\right\|_{p}\geq\frac{n}{k},\;\arctan\left\{\frac{\widehat{\overline{F}}_{2}(X_{i2})}{\widehat{\overline{F}}_{1}(X_{i1})}\right\}\leq\theta\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\Big\{(n+1-R_{i1})^{-p}+(n+1-R_{i2})^{-p}\geq k^{-p},\;\frac{n+1-R_{i2}}{n+1-R_{i1}}\leq\tan\theta\Big\},

(12)

for
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
; here,
R_{ij}
denotes the rank of
X_{ij}
in the marginal sample
X_{1j},\ldots,X_{nj}
.
This estimator has been studied from the asymptotic point of view in
[
19
,
22
]
and from a finite-sample perspective (in general dimension) in
[
7
]
.

The estimator
\widehat{\Phi}_{p}
is not guaranteed to satisfy the marginal constraints (
9
) and is therefore not a
true
angular measure itself. One solution is to enforce the constraints by maximizing an empirical Euclidean likelihood as done in
[
11
]
for the case
p=1
. In particular, they show that this procedure improves finite-sample performance of the estimator.
Here, we extend their approach to the general case
p\in[1,\infty]
to make use of the resulting estimator in our test.

Let

K\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|\left(\frac{1}{\widehat{\overline{F}}_{1}(X_{i1})},\frac{1}{\widehat{\overline{F}}_{2}(X_{i2})}\right)\right\|_{p}\geq\frac{n}{k}\right\}

be the (random) number of data points exceeding
n/k
on the Pareto scale and denote these points by
(X_{i_{j},1},X_{i_{j},2})
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
.
The collection of angles associated to those points is

\widehat{\theta}_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\arctan\left\{\frac{\widehat{\overline{F}}_{2}(X_{i_{j},2})}{\widehat{\overline{F}}_{1}(X_{i_{j},1})}\right\},\qquad j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}.

(13)

It is then easily seen from (
12
) that for any
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
,

\widehat{Q}_{p}(\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\widehat{\Phi}_{p}(\theta)}{\widehat{\Phi}_{p}(\pi/2)}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}K^{-1}\mathbb{I}\{\widehat{\theta}_{j}\leq\theta\}.

Following in the footsteps of
[
11
]
, we suggest to modify this estimator by introducing the
maximum (empirical) Euclidean likelihood estimator

\widetilde{Q}_{p}(\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\widehat{p}_{j}\mathbb{I}\left\{\widehat{\theta}_{j}\leq\theta\right\},\qquad\theta\in[0,\pi/2],

(14)

with
\widehat{\theta}_{j}
as in (
13
) and where
\widehat{p}=(\widehat{p}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{p}_{K})
is the solution the optimization problem

\min_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{K}(Kp_{j}-1)^{2}

(15)

under the constraints

\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\;=\;}l}\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{j}&1,\\[4.30554pt]
\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{j}f(\widehat{\theta}_{j})&0,\end{array}\right.

where we wrote

f(\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\sin\theta-\cos\theta}{\|(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\|_{p}},\qquad\theta\in[0,\pi/2].

(16)

The constraints ensure that the estimated measure has total mass equal to one and that (
11
) is satisfied. The objective function in (
15
) is a second-order approximation to a negative empirical log-likelihood known as an empirical Euclidean log-likelihood
[
33
]
, in view of the use of the Euclidean distance between the probability mass vector
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{K})
and the uniform distribution
(1/K,\ldots,1/K)
.
The above optimization problem can be solved by the method of Lagrange multipliers, with solution

\widehat{p}_{j}=\frac{1}{K}\left\{1-\frac{\overline{f_{\widehat{\theta}}}}{\sigma_{f_{\widehat{\theta}}}^{2}}\left(f(\widehat{\theta}_{j})-\overline{f_{\widehat{\theta}}}\right)\right\},\qquad j\in\{1,\ldots,K\},

where we used the notation

\overline{f_{\widehat{\theta}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}f(\widehat{\theta}_{j})\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{f_{\widehat{\theta}}}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left(f(\widehat{\theta}_{j})-\overline{f_{\widehat{\theta}}}\right)^{2}.

Note that the weights
\widehat{p}_{j}
may be negative, but that the set on which this happens has probability tending to zero, see
[
11
, page 5]
. This follows from the facts that

\overline{f_{\widehat{\theta}}}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{P}}\mu_{Q_{p}}(f)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{f_{\widehat{\theta}}}^{2}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{P}}\sigma_{Q_{p}}^{2}(f)>0

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, where we used the notation in (
25
), together with
\left|f(\widehat{\theta}_{j})-\overline{f_{\widehat{\theta}}}\,\right|\leq 2
.

2.4
Asymptotic expansion and distribution

The asymptotic distribution of the empirical process

\left\{\sqrt{k}\left(\widetilde{Q}_{p}(\theta)-Q_{p}(\theta)\right):\theta\in[0,\pi/2]\right\}

(17)

associated to the maximum Euclidean likelihood estimator derived in the previous section can be obtained on the basis of the one of the process
\sqrt{k}(\widehat{\Phi}_{p}-\Phi_{p})
associated to the empirical angular measure. The latter was analyzed in
[
19
]
for the case
p=\infty
and in
[
22
]
for general
p\in[1,\infty]
. Based on those results, an asymptotic expansion was also derived in
[
31
]
which will be needed to derive the limiting distribution of our test statistic as the sample size grows to infinity. First we recall the assumptions and the precise statement and then we derive an asymptotic expansion for the process (
17
).

Assumption 1
.

The measure
\Lambda
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure with a density
\lambda
that is continuous on
[0,\infty)^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
.
Furthermore,
\Lambda(\{\infty\}\times[0,1])=\Lambda([0,1]\times\{\infty\})=0
.

Assumption
1
implies that
\Phi_{p}
is concentrated on
(0,\pi/2)
, thereby excluding asymptotic independence. Indeed, a calculation shows that
\Phi_{p}(\{0\})=\Lambda(\{\infty\}\times[0,1])
and
\Phi_{p}(\{\pi/2\})=\Lambda([0,1]\times\{\infty\})
, both of which are zero by Assumption
1
.
It also implies that the map
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)\mapsto\Phi_{p}(\theta)
has a continuous derivative
\varphi_{p}
on
(0,\pi/2)
. In particular,
\lambda
and
\varphi_{p}
are related through the following expression: for every
x,y>0
,

\lambda(x,y)=\frac{xy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\|(x,y)\|_{p}^{-1}\varphi_{p}(\arctan(y/x)).

(18)

The proof of this identity can be found in
[
31
, Appendix A]
. Note that relation (
18
) implies

\lambda(ax,ay)=a^{-1}\lambda(x,y),\qquad a>0,\ (x,y)\in[0,\infty)^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}.

(19)

Evaluating expression (
18
) at
(x,y)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
for
\theta\in(0/\pi/2)
leads to the useful formula

\varphi_{p}(\theta)=\frac{\|(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\|_{p}}{\cos\theta\sin\theta}\,\lambda(\cos\theta,\sin\theta).

(20)

Assumption 2
.

If
c
denotes the density of
U=(1-F_{1}(X_{1}),1-F_{2}(X_{2}))
, the quantity

\mathcal{D}_{T}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\iint_{\mathcal{L}_{T}}|tc(tu_{1},tu_{2})-\lambda(u_{1},u_{2})|\,\,\mathrm{d}u_{1}\,\mathrm{d}u_{2},\quad 1\leq T<\infty,\,t>0,

where
\mathcal{L}_{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{(u_{1},u_{2})\in[0,T]^{2}:u_{1}\wedge u_{2}\leq 1\}
, satisfies
\mathcal{D}_{1/t}(t)=\Oh(t^{\alpha_{1}})
and for any
p\in[1,\infty]
,

\Phi_{p}(t)=\Oh(t^{\alpha_{2}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Phi_{p}\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)-\Phi_{p}\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-t\right)=\Oh(t^{\alpha_{3}})

for some
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}>0
as
t\rightarrow 0
, which can be taken independently of
p
. Furthermore,

k=\oh\left(n^{\tfrac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}\right),\qquad\text{where }\alpha\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\min\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\}.

Note in particular that under Assumption
2
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{k}\,\mathcal{D}_{n/k}(k/n)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{k}\,\Phi_{p}\left(\tfrac{k}{n}\right)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{k}\,\left\{\Phi_{p}\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)-\Phi_{p}\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac{k}{n}\right)\right\}=0.

Theorem 1
.

Let

be the law of

and let

denote the empirical measure of an independent sample

. We set

and set

. Define the tail marginal empirical processes

for

Under Assumptions

\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/2]}\left|\sqrt{k}\left(\widehat{\Phi}_{p}(\theta)-\Phi_{p}(\theta)\right)-E_{n,p}(\theta)\right|=\oh_{\mathsf{P}}(1),

as

\displaystyle E_{n,p}(\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sqrt{k}\left\{\tfrac{n}{k}P_{n}\left(\tfrac{k}{n}C_{p,\theta}\right)-\tfrac{n}{k}P\left(\tfrac{k}{n}C_{p,\theta}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{x_{p}(\theta)}\lambda(x,x\tan\theta)\left\{w_{1n}(x)\tan\theta-w_{2n}\left(x\tan\theta\right)\right\}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\begin{cases}\int_{x_{p}(\theta)}^{\infty}\lambda(x,y_{p}(x))\left\{y_{p}^{\prime}(x)w_{1n}(x)-w_{2n}\left(y_{p}(x)\right)\right\}\,\mathrm{d}x&\text{ if }p<\infty,\\
-w_{1n}(1)\int_{1}^{1\vee\tan\theta}\lambda(1,y)\,\mathrm{d}y-w_{2n}(1)\int_{1\vee\cot\theta}^{\infty}\lambda(x,1)\,\mathrm{d}x&\text{ if }p=\infty.\end{cases}

Consequently, for any

\left\{\sqrt{k}\left(\widehat{\Phi}_{p}(\theta)-\Phi_{p}(\theta)\right)\right\}_{\theta\in[0,\pi/2]}\rightsquigarrow\left\{\alpha_{p}(\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}W_{\Lambda}(C_{p,\theta})+Z_{p}(\theta)\right\}_{\theta\in[0,\pi/2]}

where the process

\displaystyle Z_{p}(\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{x_{p}(\theta)}\lambda(x,x\tan\theta)\left[W_{1}(x)\tan\theta-W_{2}(x\tan\theta)\right]\,\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\,+\begin{cases}\int_{x_{p}(\theta)}^{\infty}\lambda(x,y_{p}(x))\left[W_{1}(x)y_{p}^{\prime}(x)-W_{2}(y_{p}(x))\right]\,\,\mathrm{d}x&\text{if }p<\infty,\\

with

and with

and

the marginal processes.

Based on Theorem

\sup_{\theta\in[0,\pi/2]}\left|\sqrt{k}\left(\widehat{Q}_{p}(\theta)-Q_{p}(\theta)\right)-E_{n,p}^{Q}(\theta)\right|=\oh_{\mathsf{P}}(1),

(21)

where

E_{n,p}^{Q}(\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{E_{n,p}(\theta)\Phi_{p}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})-\Phi_{p}(\theta)E_{n,p}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})}{\Phi_{p}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})^{2}},\qquad\theta\in[0,\pi/2].

(22)

By the second part of Theorem

\left\{\sqrt{k}\left(\widehat{Q}_{p}(\theta)-Q_{p}(\theta)\right)\right\}_{\theta\in[0,\pi/2]}\rightsquigarrow\left\{\beta_{p}(\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\alpha_{p}(\theta)\Phi_{p}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})-\Phi_{p}(\theta)\alpha_{p}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})}{\Phi_{p}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})^{2}}\right\}_{\theta\in[0,\pi/2]}.

Let

(\Psi(F))(\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}F(\theta)-\frac{\mu_{F}(f)}{\sigma^{2}_{F}(f)}\int_{0}^{\theta}\left(f(\psi)-\mu_{F}(f)\right)\,\mathrm{d}F(\psi),

Corollary 1

The map

3
Goodness-of-fit tests for parametric models

3.1
Test statistic

Given a parametric model
\mathcal{M}=\{\Phi_{p,r}:r\in\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}\}
for the angular measure
\Phi_{p}
, our main goal is to test

H_{0}:\Phi_{p}\in\mathcal{M}\qquad\text{vs}\qquad H_{1}:\Phi_{p}\notin\mathcal{M}.

(26)

As a test statistic, we propose to use a weighted version of the
L_{1}
-Wasserstein distance
from optimal transport theory
[
41
]
between
\widetilde{Q}_{p}
in (
14
) and
Q_{p,\widehat{r}_{n}}
, where
\widehat{r}_{n}
is some estimator of the true parameter
r_{0}
under
H_{0}
, which can be seen as a nuisance parameter. More explicitly, our test statistic will be of the form

T_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sqrt{k}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left|\widetilde{Q}_{p}(\theta)-Q_{p,\widehat{r}_{n}}(\theta)\right|q(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

(27)

where
q:(0,\pi/2)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is some positive, integrable weight function. The special case
q\equiv 1
reduces to the classical
L_{1}
-Wasserstein distance. Under differentiability assumptions on the model
\mathcal{M}
and general conditions on the estimator
\widehat{r}_{n}
, we can derive the asymptotic distribution of
T_{n}
in Section
3.3
. Before we do so, we develop an asymptotic expansion of
\widehat{r}_{n}
in Section
3.2
.

3.2
Parameter estimator and empirical stable tail dependence function

The condition on the parameter estimator will be formulated in terms of the
stable tail dependence function

\ell:[0,\infty)^{2}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
obtained by taking the limit

\ell(x_{1},x_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}t^{-1}\mathsf{P}\left[U_{1}\leq tx_{1}\text{ or }U_{2}\leq tx_{2}\right],

(28)

which is known to exist due to (
2
).
This function describes the extremal dependence structure of a random vector and has a close link with the angular measure, see
[
3
, Section 8.6.2]
.
As for the angular measure, we may estimate
\ell
nonparametrically by replacing the distribution functions in the expression by their empirical counterparts and by replacing
t
by
k/n
. This leads to the rank-based estimator

\widehat{\ell}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\left\{R_{i1}>n+\tfrac{1}{2}-kx_{1}\text{ or }R_{i2}>n+\tfrac{1}{2}-kx_{2}\right\},

(29)

where
R_{ij}
denotes the rank of
X_{ij}
in the marginal sample
X_{1j},\ldots,X_{nj}
. The constant
1/2
is added in the indicators to improve finite-sample properties but will not play any role in our asymptotic considerations
[
21
, Equation (7.1)]
.

Let
x\boldsymbol{\cdot}y
denote the scalar product of
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and let
|x|
denote the Euclidean norm of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Assumption 3
.

Let
r_{0}\in\mathcal{R}
denote the parameter value such that
\Phi_{p}=\Phi_{p,r_{0}}
under
H_{0}
.

1.

The point
r_{0}
lies in the interior of
\mathcal{R}
and the map
\mathfrak{R}:r\in\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}\mapsto Q_{p,r}\in\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])
is Hadamard differentiable at
r_{0}
with derivative
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}_{r_{0}}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])
.
For every
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
, the map
r\in\mathcal{R}\mapsto\varrho_{p,r}(\theta)
is differentiable and the derivative satisfies

\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left|\nabla_{r}\varrho_{p,r_{0}}(\theta)\right|\,\mathrm{d}\theta<\infty.

Differentiation with respect to
r
and integration with respect to
\theta
can be exchanged while considering the map
(r,\theta)\in\mathcal{R}\times[0,\pi/2]\mapsto\varrho_{p,r}(\theta)
, so that

(\mathfrak{R}_{r}^{\prime}[h])(\theta)=\int_{0}^{\theta}\nabla_{r}\varrho_{p,r}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\boldsymbol{\cdot}h.

2.

The estimator
\widehat{r}_{n}
satisfies the following expansion
[
28
, Assumption 4.6]
: there exists a finite Borel measure
\sigma
on
[0,1]^{2}
and a function
g:[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
in
\left(L_{2}([0,1]^{2},\sigma)\right)^{m}
such that, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\sqrt{k}(\widehat{r}_{n}-r_{0})=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\sqrt{k}\left(\widehat{\ell}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})-\ell(x_{1},x_{2})\right)g(x_{1},x_{2})\,\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x_{1},x_{2})+\oh_{\mathsf{P}}(1).

The second part of Assumption
3
will permit to derive an asymptotic expansion of
\sqrt{k}\,(\widehat{r}_{n}-r_{0})
on the basis of an expansion of the process
\sqrt{k}\,(\widehat{\ell}_{n}-\ell)
and hence to derive the limit distribution of
T_{n}
under
H_{0}
using the first part of the assumption. It is satisfied by many common estimators, e.g., M-estimators (and hence moment estimators) or weighted least-square estimators, see Examples 4.8–10 in
[
28
]
.

Asymptotic theory for the process
\sqrt{k}\,(\widehat{\ell}_{n}-\ell)
is studied in
[
21
]
, in general dimension
d\geq 2
(the definitions are analogous to the bivariate case). For any
T>0
, the process is asymptotically Gaussian in the space
\ell^{\infty}([0,T]^{d})
, and this under general conditions which are met in our bivariate setting. Under the same assumptions as in
[
21
]
, we provide an asymptotic expansion of the process in general dimension too.

Assumption 4
.

Let
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
be a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with continuous distribution function
F
and marginal distribution functions
F_{1},\ldots,F_{d}
. Let
U=(1-F_{1}(X_{1}),\ldots,1-F_{d}(X_{d}))
be its standardization to uniform margins, whose law will be denoted by
P
, for which the stable tail dependence function is assumed to exist, that is, for
x\in[0,\infty)^{d}
, the limit

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}t^{-1}P\left[tA_{x}\right]=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}t^{-1}\mathsf{P}\left[U_{1}\leq tx_{1}\text{ or }\ldots\text{ or }U_{d}\leq tx_{d}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\ell(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})

exists, with
A_{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{u\in[0,\infty)^{d}:u_{1}\leq x_{1}\text{ or }\ldots\text{ or }u_{d}\leq x_{d}\}
. Moreover, for some
\alpha>0
,

1.

|t^{-1}\mathsf{P}\left[U_{1}\leq tx_{1}\text{ or }\ldots\text{ or }U_{d}\leq tx_{d}\right]-\ell(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})|=\Oh(t^{\alpha})
uniformly in
x\in[0,\infty)^{d}
such that
x_{1}+\cdots+x_{d}=1
as
t\rightarrow 0
;

2.

k=\oh(n^{2\alpha/(1+2\alpha)})
and
k\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
;

3.

for every
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, the first-order partial derivative of
\ell
with respect to
x_{j}
, denoted by
\dot{\ell}_{j}
, exists and is continuous on the set of points
x
such that
x_{j}>0
.

Theorem 2

(Asymptotic expansion of the empirical stable tail dependence function)
.

Under Assumption
4
, let
P_{n}
denote the empirical distribution of an independent random sample from
P
of size
n
.
Then for every
T>0
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\sup_{x\in[0,T]^{d}}\left|\sqrt{k}\left(\ell_{n}(x)-\ell(x)\right)-\left(v_{n}(x)-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\dot{\ell}_{j}(x)v_{nj}(x_{j})\right)\right|=\oh_{\mathsf{P}}(1),

where, for every
x\in[0,\infty)^{d}
and
j=1,\ldots,d
,

v_{n}(x)=\sqrt{k}\left\{\tfrac{n}{k}P_{n}\left(\tfrac{k}{n}A_{x}\right)-\tfrac{n}{k}P\left(\tfrac{k}{n}A_{x}\right)\right\},\quad v_{nj}(x_{j})=v_{n}(0,\ldots,0,x_{j},0,\ldots,0),

and where

is taken to be the right-hand partial derivative if

for some

(the latter always exists, see

Furthermore, if

The proof of Theorem

Theorem 3

If Assumptions

.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
and

, let

\mathcal{Q}_{\widehat{r}_{n}}(1-\alpha)\xrightarrow{\mathsf{P}}\mathcal{Q}_{r_{0}}(1-\alpha),\qquad\alpha\in(0,1),

Remark 2
.

Point 1 of Assumption

is formulated in terms of the map

Simulations and finite-sample performance

Theorems
3
and
4
provide a natural way to perform the goodness-of-fit test proposed in Eq. (

\alpha\in(0,1)

\mathcal{Q}_{r}(1-\alpha)
for any

by directly simulating the random variable

and, by interpolation, leads to critical values for any value of the parameter. Once those values have been obtained, they can be used for any dataset for which the model needs to be tested, provided that the estimator of the model parameter satisfies the same expansion as the one used to obtain the critical values. The procedure is illustrated for the logistic (Section
4.1
) and the Hüsler–Reiss (Section
4.2
) models. Details underlying the simulation of

are to be found in Appendix

. An

-package is under construction, and in the meantime, software will be available from the first author on GitHub

4.1
Logistic model

Model.

One of the most famous and oldest parametric families of bivariate extreme value dependence structures is the
logistic model
with the stable tail dependence function (
28
)

\ell_{r}(x,y)=\left(x^{1/r}+y^{1/r}\right)^{r},\qquad r\in\mathcal{R}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(0,1].

The model ranges from asymptotic full dependence (
r\rightarrow 0
) to asymptotic independence (
r=1
). Since
\ell_{r}(x,y)=x+y-\Lambda_{r}([0,x]\times[0,y])
, one easily obtains

\lambda_{r}(x,y)=\left(\frac{1}{r}-1\right)\frac{(xy)^{\frac{1}{r}-1}}{(x^{1/r}+y^{1/r})^{2-r}},\qquad x,y>0.

For
r\in(0,1)
, the angular measure
\Phi_{p,r}
on the
L_{p}
-sphere, for
1\leq p<\infty
, satisfies
\Phi_{p}(\{0,\pi/2\})=0
, while on the interior of the sphere
(0,\pi/2)
, it is absolutely continuous with a density which can be computed using relation (
20
) to be

Parameter estimation.

Given a random sample

of the model will be estimated by inverting the

[
3

is satisfied with

4.2
Hüsler–Reiss model

Model.

Also highly popular in extreme value analysis is the
Hüsler–Reiss
model
[
27
]
, with stable tail dependence function (
28
)

\ell_{r}(x,y)=x\,\varPhi\left(r+(2r)^{-1}\log(x/y)\right)+y\,\varPhi\left(r+(2r)^{-1}\log(y/x)\right),\qquad r\in(0,\infty),

where
\varPhi
is the standard normal distribution function, not to be confounded with the angular measure
\Phi
. Again, the model covers the full range from asymptotic full dependence (
r\rightarrow 0
) to asymptotic independence (
r\rightarrow\infty
). Straightforward computations give

\lambda_{r}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2ry}\phi\left(r+(2r)^{-1}\log(x/y)\right)\left(0.5-(4r^{2})^{-1}\log(x/y)\right)\\
+\frac{1}{2rx}\phi\left(r+(2r)^{-1}\log(y/x)\right)\left(0.5-(4r^{2})^{-1}\log(y/x)\right),\qquad x,y>0,

where
\phi
is the standard normal density. The
L_{p}
-sphere angular measure
\Phi_{p,r}
, for
1\leq p<\infty
, again satisfies
\Phi_{p}(\{0,\pi/2\})=0
, while on the interior of the sphere, its density can be computed via relation (
20
). In particular, Assumption
1
is satisfied for this model. Point 1 of Assumption
3
and Assumption
5
are also satisfied for
r_{0}=1
.

Parameter estimation.

Given a random sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, the unknown model parameter

which equals
\chi=2\varPhi(r)
in this case, yielding

\widehat{r}_{n}=\varPhi^{-1}\left(\frac{\widehat{\ell}_{n}(1,1)}{2}\right).

(33)

By the delta method, we deduce

\sqrt{k}\left(\widehat{r}_{n}-r\right)=\sqrt{k}\left(\widehat{\ell}_{n}(1,1)-\ell(1,1)\right)\cdot\frac{1}{2\phi(r)}+\oh_{\mathsf{P}}(1),

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, implying that point 2 of Assumption
3
is satisfied with

g\equiv\frac{1}{2\phi(r)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma=\delta_{(1,1)}.

6
Conclusion

We propose a new inference method based on the angular measure to evaluate whether the asymptotic dependence structure of a bivariate dataset aligns with a given parametric model. Unlike previous approaches, our method involves sampling directly from the asymptotic distribution of the test statistic under the estimated parameter value. Leveraging continuity of the asymptotic quantile function, this leads to a detailed proof of the method’s consistency. The finite-sample performance is also compelling when compared to other techniques, and the ease of computing the test statistic makes it a strong candidate for practical applications. Although the method is currently limited to bivariate data, many multivariate extreme value methods rely on multiple bivariate analyses, and we demonstrate how this can be done on a real dataset.

Extending the procedure to the general multivariate case is certainly of interest but requires a deeper understanding of the asymptotic behavior of the empirical angular measure, about which little is currently known. A promising approach would be to focus on discrete angular measures, for which more concrete results might be achievable. Using the Wasserstein distance as a test statistic to quantify the discrepancy between
\widetilde{Q}_{p}
and
Q_{p,\widehat{r}_{n}}
could be effective, leveraging results from
[
37
]
, where the Wasserstein distance is shown to be Hadamard differentiable with respect to its arguments for measures with discrete supports.

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
2

To facilitate the notation later in the proof, we introduce the following functions: for
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,

•

Q_{nj}(u_{j}):=U_{\lceil nu_{j}\rceil:n,j},\qquad u\in[0,1]^{d}
;

•

S_{nj}(x_{j}):=\frac{n}{k}Q_{nj}\left(\frac{k}{n}x_{j}\right),\quad S_{n}(x):=(S_{n1}(x_{1}),\ldots,S_{nd}(x_{d})),\quad x\in[0,\infty)^{d}
;

where
U_{1:n,j}\leq U_{2:n,j}\leq\ldots\leq U_{n:n,j}
are the order statistics of the marginal sample
U_{1j},\ldots,U_{nj}
and
\lceil a\rceil
is the smallest integer not smaller than
a\in\mathbb{R}
. We also write for
x\in[0,\infty)^{d}
,

\displaystyle V_{n}(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{n}{k}\mathsf{P}\left[U_{1}\leq\frac{k}{n}x_{1}\text{ or }\ldots\text{ or }U_{d}\leq\frac{k}{n}x_{d}\right];

\displaystyle T_{n}(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{n}{k}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\left\{U_{i1}\leq\frac{k}{n}x_{1}\text{ or }\ldots\text{ or }U_{id}\leq\frac{k}{n}x_{d}\right\};

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{n}{k}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\left\{U_{i1}\leq\frac{k}{n}S_{n1}(x_{1})\text{ or }\ldots\text{ or }U_{id}\leq\frac{k}{n}S_{nd}(x_{d})\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\left\{R_{i1}>n+1-kx_{1}\text{ or }\ldots\text{ or }R_{id}>n+1-kx_{d}\right\}.

With this notation, we note that
v_{n}(x)=\sqrt{k}\{T_{n}(x)-V_{n}(x)\}
and
\hat{L}_{n}(x)=T_{n}(S_{n}(x))
. As claimed in
[
21
, Equation (7.1)]
, it is easily seen that the asymptotic properties of
\ell_{n}
and
\hat{L}_{n}
are the same so that we will consider the process
\sqrt{k}(\hat{L}_{n}-\ell)
instead.

We consider the following decomposition: for
x\in[0,\infty)^{d}
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{k}(\hat{L}_{n}(x)-\ell(x))

\displaystyle=\sqrt{k}\left(T_{n}(S_{n}(x))-V_{n}(S_{n}(x))\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sqrt{k}\left(V_{n}(S_{n}(x))-\ell(S_{n}(x))\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sqrt{k}\left(\ell(S_{n}(x))-\ell(x)\right),

where we call the first term of the decomposition the
stochastic term
(and denote it by
D_{1}(x)
), the second term will be called the
bias term
(and will be denoted by
D_{2}(x)
) and the last term will be called the
random set term
(and will be denoted by
D_{3}(x)
).

Skorokhod construction.

As in
[
21
]
, we will work on a Skorokhod construction, without changing the notation to keep the proof readable.

For fixed
T>0
, Theorem 3.1 in
[
17
]
ensures that on a Skorokhod construction (depending on

), there exists a sequence of processes
(v_{n})
and a Wiener process
W_{l}(x)=W_{\Lambda}(A_{x})
, where

is the
d

1
, such that as

,

\sup_{x\in[0,2T]^{d}}\left|v_{n}(x)-W_{l}(x)\right|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

In particular, this implies the marginal convergences

\sup_{x_{j}\in[0,2T]}\left|v_{nj}(x_{j})-W_{j}(x_{j})\right|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0,\qquad j\in\{1,\ldots,d\},

(37)

where
W_{j}(x_{j})=W_{l}(0,\ldots,0,x_{j},0,\ldots,0)
. By Vervaat’s Lemma

, we also have the convergence of the marginal quantile processes:

\sup_{x_{j}\in[0,2T]}\left|\sqrt{k}\left(S_{nj}(x_{j})-x_{j}\right)+W_{j}(x_{j})\right|\xrightarrow{a.s.}0,\qquad j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}.

(38)

Asymptotic expansions of the bias term.

We show that
D_{2}
is asymptotically negligible for our purposes, that is,
\sup_{x\in[0,T]^{d}}|D_{2}(x)|=\oh_{\mathsf{P}}(1)
as

. It follows from (

) that
S_{nj}(x_{j})=x_{j}+\Oh_{\mathsf{P}}(1/\sqrt{k})
where the
\Oh_{\mathsf{P}}
term is uniform in

so that, with probability tending to one, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[0,T]^{d}}|D_{2}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in[0,2T]^{d}}\sqrt{k}\left|V_{n}(y)-\ell(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2T\sqrt{k}\sup_{w\in[0,2T]^{d},\|w\|_{1}=1}\left|V_{n}(w)-\ell(w)\right|.

By points 1 and 2 of Assumption
4
, the latter expression is of the order

\sqrt{k}\Oh\left((k/n)^{\alpha}\right)=\Oh\left(\left(\frac{k}{n^{2\alpha/(1+2\alpha)}}\right)^{1/2+\alpha}\right)=\oh(1)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Asymptotic expansions of the stochastic term.

Note that for any
x\in[0,T]^{d}
,
D_{1}(x)=v_{n}(S_{n}(x))
. Our aim is to show that

\sup_{x\in[0,T]^{d}}\left|v_{n}(S_{n}(x))-v_{n}(x)\right|=\oh_{\mathsf{P}}(1),

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. By the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[0,T]^{d}}\left|v_{n}(S_{n}(x))-v_{n}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in[0,T]^{d}}\left|v_{n}(S_{n}(x))-W_{l}(S_{n}(x))\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\sup_{x\in[0,T]^{d}}\left|W_{l}(S_{n}(x))-W_{l}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\sup_{x\in[0,T]^{d}}\left|W_{l}(x)-v_{n}(x)\right|.

Arguing as for the bias term, by (
38
), we see that the first term in this decomposition is bounded with probability tending to one by
\sup_{y\in[0,2T]^{d}}|v_{n}(y)-W_{l}(y)|
which is
\oh_{\mathsf{P}}(1)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, by (
36
). The second term of this decomposition is
\oh_{\mathsf{P}}(1)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
by uniform continuity of the limiting process
W_{l}
. Finally, the last term of this decomposition is immediately
\oh_{\mathsf{P}}(1)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
by (
36
).

Asymptotic expansions of the random set term.

It follows from point 3 in Assumption
4
and the mean value theorem that for
x\in[0,T]^{d}
,

D_{3}(x)=\sqrt{k}\left(\ell(S_{n}(x))-\ell(x)\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sqrt{k}\left(S_{nj}(x_{j})-x_{j}\right)\dot{\ell}_{j}(\xi_{n}),

for some
\xi_{n}
such that
\xi_{nj}\in(x_{j},S_{nj}(x_{j}))
for any
j=1,\ldots,d
.
Hence, by the triangle inequality, we are done proving our expansion if we show

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[0,T]^{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|\sqrt{k}\left(S_{nj}(x_{j})-x_{j}\right)\dot{\ell}_{j}(\xi_{n})+v_{nj}(x_{j})\dot{\ell}_{j}(x)\right|=\oh_{\mathsf{P}}(1).

Every term in this sum can be dealt with in the exact same way so that we only consider
j=1
.

Fix
0<\delta<T
. By the triangle inequality and (
38
),

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[0,T]^{d}}\left|\sqrt{k}\left(S_{n1}(x_{1})-x_{1}\right)\dot{\ell}_{1}(\xi_{n})+v_{n1}(x_{1})\dot{\ell}_{1}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sup_{y\in[0,2T]^{d}}\left|\dot{\ell}_{1}(y)\right|\cdot\sup_{x_{1}\in[0,T]}\left|\sqrt{k}\left(S_{n1}(x_{1})-x_{1}\right)+v_{n1}(x_{1})\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+\sup_{x_{1}\in[0,T]}\left|v_{n1}(x_{1})\right|\cdot\sup_{x\in[\delta,T]\times[0,T]^{d-1}}\left|\dot{\ell}_{1}(\xi_{n})-\dot{\ell}_{1}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+2\sup_{y\in[0,2T]^{d}}\left|\dot{\ell}_{1}(y)\right|\cdot\sup_{x_{1}\in[0,\delta]}\left|v_{n1}(x_{1})\right|.

In each term of the latter upper bound, it is easy to see that the first factor in the product is bounded (in probability) and that the second factor converges to zero (in probability).
For the first term, we simply use that
0\leq\dot{\ell}_{1}\leq 1
and we combine (
37
) and (
38
).
For the second term, we use (
37
) which shows that
v_{n1}
is uniformly bounded in probability on
[0,T]^{d}
by Prohorov’s theorem and the fact that
\dot{\ell}_{1}
is continuous on the compact set
[\delta,T]\times[0,T]^{d-1}
, hence, uniformly continuous on the same set.
For the third term, we again use that
0\leq\dot{\ell}_{1}\leq 1
and (
37
) which ensures that by picking
n
large enough,
\sup_{x_{1}\in[0,\delta]}\left|v_{n1}(x_{1})\right|
is arbitrary close to
\sup_{x_{1}\in[0,\delta]}\left|W_{1}(x_{1})\right|
in probability and, by picking
\delta
small enough, the latter supremum will be arbitrary close to zero.
This completes the proof of the first part of the theorem.

For the second part, note that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2}>0\\
x_{1}+x_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\left|t^{-1}\mathsf{P}\left[U_{1}\leq tx_{1},U_{2}\leq tx_{2}\right]-\ell(x_{1},x_{2})\right|\\
\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2}>0\\
x_{1}+x_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\iint_{A_{(x_{1},x_{2})}}\left|tc(tu_{1},tu_{2})-\lambda(u_{1},u_{2})\right|\,\mathrm{d}u_{1}\,\mathrm{d}u_{2},

where we recall
A_{(x_{1},x_{2})}=\{(u_{1},u_{2})\in[0,\infty)^{2}:u_{1}\leq x_{1}\text{ or }u_{2}\leq x_{2}\}
. One observes that the latter supremum can be bounded, independently of
(x_{1},x_{2})
by

\mathcal{D}_{1/t}(t)+\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{1}\wedge u_{2}\leq 1\\
u_{1}\vee u_{2}>1/t\end{subarray}}\left|tc(tu_{1},tu_{2})-\lambda(u_{1},u_{2})\right|\,\mathrm{d}u_{1}\,\mathrm{d}u_{2}.

Under Assumption
2
, the first term satisfies
\mathcal{D}_{1/t}(t)=\Oh(t^{\alpha})
as
t\rightarrow\infty
. For the second term, assuming
t<1
, it equals

\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{1}\wedge u_{2}\leq 1\\
u_{1}\vee u_{2}>1/t\end{subarray}}\lambda(u_{1},u_{2})\,\mathrm{d}u_{1}\,\mathrm{d}u_{2},

since the copula density
c\equiv 0
outside of the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
. Moving to polar coordinates, one shows that the latter integral is bounded by

\Phi_{p}(t)+\left\{\Phi_{p}\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)-\Phi_{p}\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-t\right)\right\}=O(t^{\alpha}),

where the equality also follows from Assumption
2
. This shows that condition 1 of Assumption
4
is verified in this setting. Condition 2 is immediate from Assumption
2
. Condition 3 follows from Assumption
1
since for
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in[0,\infty)^{2}
, using the fact that
\Lambda
has Lebesgue margins,

\displaystyle\ell(x)=\Lambda(A_{x})

\displaystyle=\Lambda\left([0,x_{1}]\times[0,\infty)\right)+\Lambda\left([0,\infty)\times[0,x_{2}]\right)-\Lambda\left([0,x_{1}]\times[0,x_{2}]\right)

\displaystyle=x_{1}+x_{2}-\Lambda\left([0,x_{1}]\times[0,x_{2}]\right),

which is continuously differentiable under Assumption
1
.

Proof of Theorem
3

We assume that we are working under
H_{0}
and that
r_{0}\in\mathcal{R}
denotes the true parameter underlying the data.

First observe that

T_{n}=F\left[\sqrt{k}(\widetilde{Q}_{p}-Q_{p,\widehat{r}_{n}})\right],

where
F:\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is defined for any
z\in\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])
by

F(z)=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}|z(\theta)|q(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

The map
F
is continuous since if
(z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])
is such that
z_{n}\rightarrow z\in\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])
,

|F(z_{n})-F(z)|\leq\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left||z_{n}(\theta)|-|z(\theta)|\right|q(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\leq\|z_{n}-z\|_{\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}q(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\rightarrow 0,

as
n\rightarrow\infty
since
q
is assumed to be integrable. Consequently, in view of the continuous mapping theorem, it suffices to determine the asymptotic distribution of the process

\left\{\sqrt{k}\left(\widetilde{Q}_{p}(\theta)-Q_{p,\widehat{r}_{n}}(\theta)\right):\theta\in[0,\pi/2]\right\},

in
\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])
to determine the one of our test statistic.

For any
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
, we have

\sqrt{k}\left(\widetilde{Q}_{p}(\theta)-Q_{p,\widehat{r}_{n}}(\theta)\right)=\sqrt{k}\left(\widetilde{Q}_{p}(\theta)-Q_{p,r_{0}}(\theta)\right)-\sqrt{k}\left(Q_{p,\widehat{r}_{n}}(\theta)-Q_{p,r_{0}}(\theta)\right).

In view of Corollary
1
, the first term in the right hand side satisfies

\sqrt{k}\left(\widetilde{Q}_{p}(\theta)-Q_{p,r_{0}}(\theta)\right)=E_{n,p}^{Q_{p,r_{0}}}(\theta)+\frac{\int_{0}^{\pi/2}E_{n,p}^{Q_{p,r_{0}}}(\psi)f^{\prime}(\psi)\,\mathrm{d}\psi}{\sigma_{Q_{p,r_{0}}}^{2}(f)}\int_{0}^{\theta}f(\psi)\,\mathrm{d}Q_{p,r_{0}}(\psi)+\oh_{\mathsf{P}^{*}}(1),

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, where the reminder is uniform in
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
. In view of Assumption
3
and Theorem
2
, the second term satisfies,

\displaystyle\sqrt{k}\left(Q_{p,\widehat{r}_{n}}(\theta)-Q_{p,r_{0}}(\theta)\right)=\sqrt{k}\left(\mathfrak{R}(\widehat{r}_{n})-\mathfrak{R}(r_{0})\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{0}^{\theta}\nabla_{r}\varrho_{p,r_{0}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\boldsymbol{\cdot}\sqrt{k}(\widehat{r}_{n}-r_{0})+\oh_{\mathsf{P}}(1)

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{0}^{\theta}\nabla_{r}\varrho_{p,r_{0}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\boldsymbol{\cdot}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\sqrt{k}\left(\widehat{\ell}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})-\ell_{r_{0}}(x_{1},x_{2})\right)g(x_{1},x_{2})\,\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x_{1},x_{2})+\oh_{\mathsf{P}}(1)

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{0}^{\theta}\nabla_{r}\varrho_{p,r_{0}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\boldsymbol{\cdot}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\left(v_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})-\sum_{j=1}^{2}\dot{\ell}_{r_{0},j}(x_{1},x_{2})v_{nj}(x_{j})\right)g(x_{1},x_{2})\,\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\oh_{\mathsf{P}}(1),

where the reminder is uniform in
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
.

Summarizing, we have shown that, up to asymptotically negligible terms, all the terms appearing in the decomposition of the process
\sqrt{k}(\widetilde{Q}_{p}-Q_{p,\widehat{r}_{n}})
are asymptotically equivalent to continuous functionals of the tail empirical process

W_{n,k}(C)=\sqrt{k}\left\{\frac{n}{k}P_{n}\left(\frac{k}{n}C\right)-\frac{n}{k}P\left(\frac{k}{n}C\right)\right\},

evaluated on sets belonging to a collection
\mathcal{C}
which is of finite VC dimension. Asymptotics of this process and weighted marginal versions of it are described in Proposition 1 and Proposition 2 of
[
31
]
, respectively.
Combining those results with a suitable application of the continuous mapping theorem
[
40
, Theorem 1.3.6]
permits to conclude the proof.

\displaystyle B(3t^{\tfrac{1}{1-2\eta}},t,d_{2})

\displaystyle\subseteq[2t^{\tfrac{1}{1-2\eta}},4t^{\tfrac{1}{1-2\eta}}]

\displaystyle\hskip 5.69054pt\vdots

hence showing that

In practice, if we are able to decompose

\displaystyle\|T_{n}(e)-T(e)\|_{G}

0\leq f^{\prime}\leq 2
.
It readily follows that if
\|z\|_{\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])}\leq 1
, then

\|T_{\gamma,r_{0}}(z)\|_{\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])}\leq 1+\frac{\pi}{\sigma_{Q_{p,r_{0}}}^{2}(f)}<\infty,

so
T_{\gamma,r_{0}}
is a bounded operator.
Now, let
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{R}
be a sequence such that
r_{n}\rightarrow r_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
For
\|z\|_{\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])}\leq 1
, we compute

\displaystyle\|T_{\gamma,r_{n}}(z)-T_{\gamma,r_{0}}(z)\|_{\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])}

\displaystyle\leq\pi\left\|\frac{\int_{0}^{\theta}f(\psi)\,\mathrm{d}Q_{p,r_{n}}(\psi)}{\sigma_{Q_{p,r_{n}}}^{2}(f)}-\frac{\int_{0}^{\theta}f(\psi)\,\mathrm{d}Q_{p,r_{0}}(\psi)}{\sigma_{Q_{p,r_{0}}}^{2}(f)}\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])}.

Fubini’s theorem leads to

\left\|\int_{0}^{\theta}f(\psi)\,\mathrm{d}Q_{p,r_{n}}(\psi)-\int_{0}^{\theta}f(\psi)\,\mathrm{d}Q_{p,r_{0}}(\psi)\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])}\leq(1+\pi)\|Q_{p,r_{n}}-Q_{p,r_{0}}\|_{\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])},

and the latter quantity converges to zero as
n\rightarrow 0
since
Q_{p,r_{n}}\rightarrow Q_{p,r_{0}}
in
\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])
. The same argument implies

\sigma_{Q_{p,r_{n}}}^{2}(f)\to\sigma_{Q_{p,r_{0}}}^{2}(f)>0,\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty,

and this suffices to verify (
46
) for the operator
T_{\gamma,r_{0}}
.

Process associated with the derivative.

The last operator to be considered is the linear operator
T_{\nabla,r_{0}}:\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{F})\rightarrow\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])
which is such that for
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
,

\left(T_{\nabla,r_{0}}\left(W_{\Lambda,r_{0}}\right)\right)(\theta)=\int_{0}^{\theta}\nabla_{r}\varrho_{p,r_{0}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\boldsymbol{\cdot}I_{g,\sigma,r_{0}},

(55)

where we recall that

I_{g,\sigma,r_{0}}=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\Big(W_{\Lambda_{r_{0}}}(A_{(x_{1},x_{2})})-\sum_{j=1}^{2}\dot{\ell}_{r,j}(x_{1},x_{2})W_{j}(x_{j})\Big)g(x_{1},x_{2})\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x_{1},x_{2}),

for some finite Borel measure
\sigma
on
[0,1]^{2}
and a function
g:[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
in
\left(L_{2}([0,1]^{2},\sigma)\right)^{m}
. The latter is naturally decomposed as
T_{\nabla,r_{0}}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}T_{\nabla,k,r_{0}}
where the operators
T_{\nabla,k,r_{0}}:\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{F})\rightarrow\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])
are defined by

\left(T_{\nabla,K,r_{0}}\left(W_{\Lambda,r_{0}}\right)\right)(\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left(I_{g,\sigma,r_{0}}\right)_{k}\int_{0}^{\theta}\partial_{r_{k}}\varrho_{p,r_{0}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}.

Fix
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
and let
z\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{F})
be such that
\|z\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{F})}\leq 1
. Using the fact that
\dot{\ell}_{r_{0},j}\leq 1
for any
j\in\{1,2\}
, we readily see that

\|T_{\nabla,K,r_{0}}(z)\|_{\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])}\leq 3\int_{[0,1]^{2}}g_{k}(x_{1},x_{2})\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x_{1},x_{2})\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left|\partial_{r_{k}}\varrho_{p,r_{0}}(x)\right|\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty,

so that
T_{\nabla,K,r_{0}}
is bounded since the partial derivative
\partial_{r_{k}}\varrho_{p,r_{0}}
is assumed to be integrable on
[0,\pi/2]
by point 1 of Assumption
3
. Let us now consider a sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{R}
which is such that
r_{n}\rightarrow r_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. We compute

\displaystyle\|T_{\nabla,k,r_{n}}(z)-T_{\nabla,k,r_{0}}(z)\|_{\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left|\partial_{r_{k}}\varrho_{p,r_{n}}(x)\right|\,\mathrm{d}x\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\left|\dot{\ell}_{r_{n},j}(x_{1},x_{2})-\dot{\ell}_{r_{0},j}(x_{1},x_{2})\right|g(x_{1},x_{2})\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle\qquad+3\int_{[0,1]^{2}}g_{k}(x_{1},x_{2})\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(x_{1},x_{2})\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left|\partial_{r_{k}}\varrho_{p,r_{n}}(x)-\partial_{r_{k}}\varrho_{p,r_{0}}(x)\right|\,\mathrm{d}x.

Using the fact that for any
r\in\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle\ell_{r}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=x_{1}+x_{2}-\Lambda_{r}([0,x_{1}]\times[0,x_{2}])

\displaystyle=x_{1}+x_{2}-\iint_{[0,x_{1}]\times[0,x_{2}]}\lambda_{r}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=x_{1}+x_{2}-\iint_{[0,x_{1}]\times[0,x_{2}]}\frac{xy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\|(x,y)\|_{p}^{-1}\varphi_{p,r}(\arctan(y/x))\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y,

where the last equality makes use of (
18
). A change of variable then leads to

\displaystyle\dot{\ell}_{r,1}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=1-\int_{0}^{\arctan(\tfrac{x_{2}}{x_{1}})}\frac{\tan\theta}{(1+(\tan\theta)^{p})^{1/p}}\varphi_{p,r}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\dot{\ell}_{r,2}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=1-\int_{\arctan(\tfrac{x_{2}}{x_{1}})}^{\pi/2}\frac{\tan(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\theta)}{(1+(\tan(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\theta)^{p})^{1/p}}\varphi_{p,r}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

Point 2 of Assumption
5
then leads to

\dot{\ell}_{r_{n},j}(x_{1},x_{2})\to\dot{\ell}_{r_{0},j}(x_{1},x_{2}),\qquad\forall(x_{1},x_{2})\in[0,1]^{2},\ j\in\{1,2\}

as
n\to\infty
. The dominated convergence theorem permits to conclude that the first term in the upper bound converges to zero.

Thanks to point 1 of Assumption
3

The second term also converges to zero by point 2 of Assumption
5
, showing that condition (
46
) is satisfied for
T_{\nabla,r_{0}}
.

Combining all the above arguments permit to conclude the proof of Lemma
2
.
∎

If
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{R}
is a sequence such that
r_{n}\rightarrow r_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
for some interior point
r_{0}\in\mathcal{R}
, Lemma
2
implies pointwise convergence of the cumulative distribution associated to
L_{r_{n}}
to the one of
L_{r_{0}}
which is continuous. Since it is also strictly increasing, we get the pointwise convergence
\mathcal{Q}_{r_{n}}(1-\alpha)\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}_{r_{0}}(1-\alpha)
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
. The continuous mapping then permits to conclude that

\mathcal{Q}_{\widehat{r}_{n}}(1-\alpha)\xrightarrow{\mathsf{P}}\mathcal{Q}_{r_{0}}(1-\alpha),\qquad\alpha\in(0,1),

for any consistent estimator
\widehat{r}_{n}
of
r_{0}
. The local uniformity follows from the continuity and monotonicity of the map
\alpha\in(0,1)\mapsto\mathcal{Q}_{r_{0}}(1-\alpha)
.

Appendix E
Sampling from the asymptotic distribution of the test statistic

In this section, we briefly describe how to generate samples from the asymptotic distribution of
T_{n}
in Theorem
3
, as it could be of independent interest. The underlying principle is not new and has been used, e.g., in
[
19
]
to draw confidence bounds for the empirical angular measure based on its asymptotic distribution. However, no detailed procedure could be found in the literature and our aim is to fill in this gap.

As in the proof of Lemma
2
in Appendix
D
, the key observation is that the random variable
L_{r_{0}}
appearing as the limit in distribution of
T_{n}
can be written as

L_{r_{0}}=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left|X_{r_{0}}(\theta)\right|\,q(\theta)\,\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

for some process
X_{r_{0}}\in\ell^{\infty}([0,\pi/2])
which is a linear transformation of the Gaussian process
W_{\Lambda_{r_{0}}}
introduced before Theorem
1
. From now on, we will omit the subscript
r_{0}
to alleviate the notation. The random variable
L
will be approximated by the Riemann sum

L\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|X(\theta_{i})|\,q(\theta_{i})

for a large equidistant grid
0<\theta_{1}<\ldots<\theta_{N}<\pi/2
. Typically, we choose
N=1\,000
. Hence, to obtain a large number
B
of simulated values for
L
, it suffices to generate
B
trajectories of the process
X
on the chosen grid. In Section
4
, we choose
B=2\,000
while the
p
-value computations in Section
5
are based on
B=4\,000
replications. We give an overview of the procedure below.

The first step is to obtain a realization of the underlying process
W_{\Lambda}
by considering a sufficiently large rectangular grid of points
\{(x_{i},y_{j}):i,j=1,\ldots,M\}
on
\mathbb{E}
of the form

x_{i}=(i-1)h\qquad\text{and}\qquad y_{j}=(j-1)h,

for a small step size
h>0
. Typically, we choose
h=0.05
and
M=1000
. These points are used to define the cells

C_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{E}:x\in[x_{i},x_{i+1})\text{ and }y\in[y_{j},y_{j+1})\right\},\qquad i,j\in\{1,\ldots,M-1\},

which are considered as an approximate disjoint covering of the space
\mathbb{E}
. The fact that the cells do not cover points
(x,y)\in\mathbb{E}
for which
x\vee y>(M-1)h
is not supposed to affect the results too much as the variance of
W_{\Lambda}
is negligible on this part of the space.

Associated with those cells, we generate a matrix
W=(W_{ij})_{i,j=1,\ldots,M-1}
of independent centered Gaussian random variables with
\Var(W_{ij})=\Lambda(C_{ij})
. The property
\EE[W_{\Lambda}(C)W_{\Lambda}(C^{\prime})]=0
for disjoint Borel sets
C,C^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{E}
justifies the approximation

\widetilde{W}_{\Lambda}(C)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}W_{ij}\,\mathbb{I}\{(x_{i},y_{j})\in C\}

(56)

for any Borel set
C\subset\mathbb{E}
to obtain a realization of the process
W_{\Lambda}
.

Of particular interest are sets of the form
C=C_{p,\theta}
for some
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
, for which formula (
56
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{\Lambda}(C_{p,\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}W_{ij}\,\mathbb{I}\{y_{j}\leq\min(y_{p}(x_{i}),x_{i}\tan\theta)\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\left[\tfrac{y_{p}(x_{i})}{h}+1\right]\wedge(M-1)}W_{ij}\,\mathbb{I}\left\{(j-1)\leq(i-1)\tan\theta\right\},

where
[x]
denotes the integer part of
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Similar approximations based on (
56
) can be found for the marginal processes
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
, leading to

\widetilde{W}_{1}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\left[\frac{x}{h}+1\right]\wedge(M-1)}W_{i\bullet}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{W}_{2}(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{\left[\frac{y}{h}+1\right]\wedge(M-1)}W_{\bullet j},

where
W_{i\bullet}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}W_{ij}
and
W_{\bullet j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}W_{ij}
for
(i,j)\in\{1,\ldots,M-1\}^{2}
. For
C=A_{(x,y)}
as introduced in Assumption
4
and appearing in the formula for
X(\theta)
, we get

\widetilde{W}_{\Lambda}\left(A_{(x,y)}\right)=\widetilde{W}_{1}(x)+\widetilde{W}_{2}(y)-\sum_{i=1}^{\left[\frac{x}{h}+1\right]\wedge(M-1)}\sum_{j=1}^{\left[\frac{y}{h}+1\right]\wedge(M-1)}W_{ij}.

As the random variable
X(\theta)
consists of a linear transformation of the process
W_{\Lambda}
evaluated on sets of the above forms, these formulas will lead to a convenient approximation for the random vector
\left(X(\theta_{1}),\ldots,X(\theta_{N})\right)
as scalar products between
W
and weights, possibly depending on
\theta
, which can be computed independently of the generation of
W
, before the evaluation of the scalar products, which can be done in an efficient way using parallel computing. More details can be found on the GitHub repository of the first author
