Strict germs on normal surface singularities
Matteo Ruggiero

Abstract
. We show that any holomorphic germ f : ( X , x 0 ) → ( Y , y 0 ) f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0}) of topological degree 1 1 between normal surface singularities can be written as f = π ∘ σ f={\pi}\circ{\sigma} , where π : Y ′ → ( Y , y 0 ) {\pi}\colon Y^{\prime}\to(Y,y_{0}) is a modification and σ : ( X , x 0 ) → ( Y ′ , y 1 ) {\sigma}\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y^{\prime},y_{1}) is a local isomorphism sending x 0 x_{0} to a point y 1 ∈ π − 1 ​ ( y 0 ) y_{1}\in{\pi}^{-1}(y_{0}) . A result by Fantini, Favre and myself guarantees that when f f is a selfmap, then ( X , x 0 ) (X,x_{0}) is a sandwiched singularity. We give here an alternative proof based on the construction of the associated Kato surfaces, and valuative dynamics.

Introduction

Given a geometric object
X
, one of the most natural tasks is to determine its symmetries.
When
(X,x_{0})
is the germ of a complex analytic space
X
at a point
x_{0}
, a natural object to study is the group of analytic automorphisms
\operatorname{Aut}(X,x_{0})
.
This space is always huge: a theorem of Müller
[
Mül87
, p.230–231]
states that any singularity
(X,x_{0})
carries countably many analytic vector fields tangent to
(X,x_{0})
and linearly independent.
By taking the flow of such vector fields, we deduce that the automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(X,x_{0})
is always infinite-dimensional.

However, the automorphisms built with Müller’s construction are often small perturbations of the identity (the vector fields have high order at the singularity
x_{0}
).
The existence of “special” automorphisms restricts greatly the geometry of the singularity
(X,x_{0})
.
For example, a singularity admits a
contracting
automorphism (i.e.,
f(X)\Subset X
and
\displaystyle\bigcap_{n\geq 0}f^{n}(X)=\{x_{0}\}
for a suitable representant of the germ
f
) if and only if the singularity is
quasihomogeneous
(see
[
OW71
,
CMS09
,
FR14
]
in dimension
2
and
[
OV24
,
Mor24
]
in higher dimensions).

Similar rigidity phenomena appear when one looks at the space
\operatorname{End}(X,x_{0})
of analytic endomorphisms.
For example, while any singularity admits lots of dynamically interesting endomorphisms (see
Remark
4.4
), having a finite-to-one selfmap (which is not an automorphism) greatly restricts the geometry of the singularity (it must be
log-canonical
, see
[
Wah90
,
Fav10
]
in dimension
2
, and
[
BdFF12
,
BH14
,
Zha17
]
in higher dimensions).

In these notes, we focus on the case of
generically injective
selfmaps
f\colon(X,x_{0})\tiny{\!\!\!\lx@xy@svg{\hbox{\raise 1.7014pt\hbox{\kern 4.14584pt\hbox{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\hbox{\vtop{\halign{\entry@#!@&&\entry@@#!@\cr\crcr}}}\ignorespaces{\hbox{\kern-4.14584pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 3.0pt\raise-1.7014pt\hbox{$\textstyle{\phantom{|}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}$}}}}}}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces{}{}{}{}{{}{{}{{}{{}{{}}{}{{}{{}}{}{{}}{}{{}{{}}{}{{}}}}}}}}{}\ignorespaces\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{{}{}{{}}{{}{}{}{}}{}}}}\ignorespaces{}\ignorespaces{}{}{}{}{{}{{}}{}{{}}{}{{}}{}{{}}{}{{}{{}{{}}{}{{}}{}{{}}}}}{\hbox{\kern 2.37866pt\raise 2.1464pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\lx@xy@tip{1}\lx@xy@tip{-1}}}}}}\ignorespaces\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{{}{}{}{{}}{{}{}{}{}\lx@xy@spline@}{}}}}\ignorespaces{}\ignorespaces\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{{}{}{{}}{{}{}{}{}}{}}}}\ignorespaces{}\ignorespaces}}}}\ignorespaces\hskip 11.38109pt}
, i.e., of topological degree
1
. The ones that are not automorphisms are called
strict germs
.

A special class of strict germs is given by
Kato germs
, i.e., germs of the form
f={\pi}\circ{\sigma}
, where
{\pi}\colon X^{\prime}\to(X,x_{0})
is a modification (a proper bimeromorphic map which is an isomorphism outside
x_{0}
), and
{\sigma}\colon(X,x_{0})\to(X^{\prime},x_{1})
is a local isomorphism, where
x_{1}\in{\pi}^{-1}(x_{0})
.

When
(X,x_{0})\cong(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
is regular, it is known by experts that any strict germ
f\colon(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
admits a Kato decomposition (see, e.g.,
[
Fav00
, Proposition 0.5]
); however, to my knowledge, an explicit proof of this fact seems to be missing in the literature.
In these notes, we prove this statement in the more general setting of strict germs between normal surface singularities.

Theorem A
.

Let
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
be a strict germ between two normal surface singularities.
Then there exists a modification
{\pi}\colon Y^{\prime}\to(Y,y_{0})
, a point
y_{1}\in{\pi}^{-1}(y_{0})
, and a local isomorphism
{\sigma}\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y^{\prime},y_{1})
so that
f={\pi}\circ{\sigma}
.

In particular, any strict selfmap
f\colon(X,x_{0})\tiny{\!\!\!\lx@xy@svg{\hbox{\raise 1.7014pt\hbox{\kern 4.14584pt\hbox{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\hbox{\vtop{\halign{\entry@#!@&&\entry@@#!@\cr\crcr}}}\ignorespaces{\hbox{\kern-4.14584pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 3.0pt\raise-1.7014pt\hbox{$\textstyle{\phantom{|}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}$}}}}}}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces{}{}{}{}{{}{{}{{}{{}{{}}{}{{}{{}}{}{{}}{}{{}{{}}{}{{}}}}}}}}{}\ignorespaces\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{{}{}{{}}{{}{}{}{}}{}}}}\ignorespaces{}\ignorespaces{}{}{}{}{{}{{}}{}{{}}{}{{}}{}{{}}{}{{}{{}{{}}{}{{}}{}{{}}}}}{\hbox{\kern 2.37866pt\raise 2.1464pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\lx@xy@tip{1}\lx@xy@tip{-1}}}}}}\ignorespaces\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{{}{}{}{{}}{{}{}{}{}\lx@xy@spline@}{}}}}\ignorespaces{}\ignorespaces\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{{}{}{{}}{{}{}{}{}}{}}}}\ignorespaces{}\ignorespaces}}}}\ignorespaces\hskip 11.38109pt}
of a normal surface singularity is a Kato germ.
Admitting a Kato germ can be seen as a
selfsimilarity
property for a singularity
(X,x_{0})
(other selfsimilarity concepts have been considered in the literature, see, e.g.,
[
dF19
]
).
In
[
FFR20
]
, the authors classify selfsimilar normal surface singularities, showing that they are exactly the
sandwiched
singularities, i.e., singularities that bimeromorphically dominate
(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
. These singularities play a central role in the proof of the resolution of singularities via Nash transformations (see, e.g.,
[
Spi90
]
).

By putting together
Theorem
A
and the classification of selfsimilar singularities, we get the following characterization of normal surface singularities admitting strict selfmaps.

Theorem B
.

Let
(X,x_{0})
be a normal surface singularity. Then
(X,x_{0})
admits a strict selfmap if and only if
(X,x_{0})
is a sandwiched singularity.

In these notes, we will present two proofs of
Theorem
A
.
The first, essentially due to Charles Favre,
is based on local flattening techniques of Hironaka, Lejeune-Jalabert and Teissier
[
Hir73
,
Hir75
,
HLJT73
]
.
The second
relies on the geometrical properties of the action
f_{\bullet}\colon{\mathcal{V}_{X}}\to{\mathcal{V}_{Y}}
induced on valuation spaces.

For what concerns
Theorem
B
, the proof given in
[
FFR20
]
works over fields of any characteristic, and relies on extending the plumbing techniques of
[
Spi90
]
when working over fields of positive characteristic, on analytic non-archimedean techniques on Berkovich spaces and non-archimedean links, and the combinatorics of self-similar dual graphs.
Here we present an alternative proof (see also
[
Rug25
, Section 3.4]
) that works over
\mathbb{C}
, which is more geometrical in nature: it relies on the geometry of Kato surfaces (suitable compactifications of the orbit spaces of
f
), as well as on the description portrayed in
[
GR21
]
of the dynamics of the action induced by selfmaps on valuation spaces.

The material is organized as follows. In
Section
1
and
Section
2
we recall a few basic facts about the valuation space
{\mathcal{V}_{X}}
associated to a normal surface singularity
(X,x_{0})
, and the action
f_{\bullet}\colon{\mathcal{V}_{X}}\to{\mathcal{V}_{Y}}
induced by a dominant germ
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
. These two sections can easily be skipped by experts.

In
Section
3
, we introduce the topological degree of a dominant germ
f
, and describe the dynamical properties of
f_{\bullet}
when
f
is a strict selfmap. In particular, we recall Gignac-Ruggiero’s result of existence and uniqueness of the eigenvaluation
\nu_{\star}
.

In
Section
4
, we give the two proofs of
Theorem
A
, and comment on some of the difficulties in higher dimensions.
Finally, in
Section
5
, we give a proof of
Theorem
B
assuming that
\nu_{\star}
is not divisorial. We prove this latter statement in
Section
6
.
We conclude with some explicit examples, and comments on the geometry of Kato surfaces depending on the type of their associated eigenvaluation.

1.
Basics on valuation spaces

1.1.
Valuation spaces

Let
(X,x_{0})
be a normal surface singularity. In this section we will recall a few facts about the associated space
\mathcal{V}_{X}
of (semi-)valuations.

We denote by
R_{X}
the completed local ring
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{X,x_{0}}
and by
\mathfrak{m}_{X}
its unique maximal ideal.

Definition 1.1
.

A
rank one semivaluation
on
R_{X}
is a function
\nu\colon R_{X}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
satisfying
\nu(\phi\psi)=\nu(\phi)+\nu(\psi)
and
\nu(\phi+\psi)\geq\min\{\nu(\phi),\nu(\psi)\}
for all
\phi,\psi\in R_{X}
.

Such a semivaluation is said to be
centered
if in addition
\nu(\phi)\geq 0
for all
\phi\in R_{X}
and
\nu(\phi)>0
if and only if
\phi\in\mathfrak{m}_{X}
. The collection of all rank one centered semivaluations on
R_{X}
will be denoted by
{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}}
.

Equivalently, one may define rank one centered semivaluations on
R_{X}
not as functions on
R_{X}
itself, but on the set of ideals of
R_{X}
.
The correspondence between the two points of view is given by setting
\nu(\mathfrak{a})=\min\{\nu(\phi)\ |\ \phi\in\mathfrak{a}\}
(the minimum exists by noetherianity of
R_{X}
), or conversely
\nu(\phi)=\nu(\phi R_{X})
.
In these terms, centered valuations are determined by the conditions
\nu(R_{X})=0
and
\nu(\mathfrak{m}_{X})>0
.

The set
{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}}
is endowed with the so called
weak topology
, which is the weakest topology for which the evaluations
\nu\mapsto\nu(\phi)
are continuous for any
\phi\in R_{X}
.

Notice that for any
\lambda>0
and
\nu\in{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}}
, we have that
\lambda\nu\in{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}}
as well. All these valuations
(\lambda\nu)_{\lambda\in(0,+\infty)}
are distinct, unless
\nu=\operatorname{triv}_{x_{0}}
is the trivial valuation, determined by
\operatorname{triv}_{x_{0}}(\mathfrak{m}_{X})=+\infty
.
These observations lead to the following definitions.

Definition 1.2
.

A semivaluation
\nu\in\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}
is
finite
if
\nu(\mathfrak{m}_{X})<+\infty
. We denote by
{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}^{*}}={\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}}\setminus\{\operatorname{triv}_{x_{0}}\}
the set of all finite semivaluations.

We also denote by

\mathcal{V}_{X}:=\mathbb{P}({\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}})={\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}^{*}}/\sim\text{.}

the set of finite valuations modulo the equivalence relation
\nu\sim\lambda\nu
for all
\lambda\in(0,+\infty)
.

The weak topology on
{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}}
induces one on
{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}^{*}}
by restriction, and on
{\mathcal{V}_{X}}
by quotient.

In the regular setting
(X,x_{0})=(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
, the space
{\mathcal{V}}={\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}}
, named the
valuative tree
due to its structure as a
\mathbb{R}
-tree, has been introduced and intensively studied by Favre and Jonsson (see
[
FJ04
,
FJ07
,
Jon12
]
).
We also refer to
[
Fav10
,
GR21
]
for the singular setting.

In most cases, it is often useful to fix a normalization for valuation spaces, i.e., a section of the natural projection
\operatorname{pr}:{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}^{*}}\to\mathcal{V}_{X}
.
The most common choice is to take the unique element
\nu
in an equivalence class satisfying
\nu(\mathfrak{m}_{X})=1
.
This allows to naturally identify the space
\mathcal{V}_{X}
with the space

\{\nu\in{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}^{*}}\ |\ \nu(\mathfrak{m}_{X})=1\}
.

Remark 1.3
.

Semivaluations can also be interpreted as their multiplicative counterpart, seminorms.
In fact, given a semivaluation
\nu\in{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}^{*}}
, we can associate the (non-archimedean) seminorm
{\left|{{\displaystyle\cdot}}\right|}_{\nu}:=e^{-\nu({\displaystyle\cdot})}
.
This gives a direct correspondence between valuation spaces and Berkovich spaces (see, e.g.,
[
Ber90
]
,
[
FJ04
, Section 4.5]
, or
[
DFR25
, Section 1]
).
The four types of seminorms in (
1
-dimensional) Berkovich spaces, points of type I, II, III, and IV, correspond respectively to curve semivaluations, divisorial valuations, irrational valuations and infinitely singular valuations, see the discussion below.

In this language, the set
\mathcal{V}_{X}
corresponds to the non-archimedean link
\operatorname{NL}(X,x_{0})
associated to the local ring
(R_{X},\mathfrak{m}_{X})
over
\mathbb{C}
considered with the trivial valuation, see
[
Fan14
,
Fan18
,
Thu07
,
FFR20
,
GR21
]
. We also refer to
[
dF19
]
for an interpretation of valuation spaces as largest Hausdorff quotient of local Riemann-Zariski spaces.

1.2.
Types of valuations

We recall here the geometric description of valuations of a normal surface singularity
(X,x_{0})
.

Modifications

First, recall that a
modification

\pi\colon X_{\pi}\to(X,x_{0})
, is a proper bimeromorphic map that is an isomorphism above
X\setminus\{x_{0}\}
.
Without further mention, we will always assume that
X_{\pi}
is normal.
Among modifications, there are:

•

resolutions
:
X_{\pi}
is regular;

•

good resolutions
: resolutions for which
\pi^{-1}(x_{0})
has simple normal crossings;

•

log-resolutions (of
\mathfrak{m}_{X}
)
: good resolutions for which the ideal sheaf
\mathfrak{m}_{X}\mathcal{O}_{X_{\pi}}
is locally principal.

Given a modification
\pi\colon X_{\pi}\to(X,x_{0})
, we denote by
\varGamma_{\pi}^{*}
the set of all
\pi
-exceptional primes, i.e., irreducible components of
\pi^{-1}(x_{0})
.
We also denote by

\mathcal{E}(\pi)_{\mathbb{K}}=\left\{\sum_{E\in\varGamma_{\pi}^{*}}\alpha_{E}E\ :\ \alpha_{E}\in\mathbb{K}\right\}

the free module of Weil
\mathbb{K}
-divisors supported on
\pi^{-1}(x_{0})
, where
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{R}
.

1.2.1.
Divisorial valuations

Given a modification
\pi\colon X_{\pi}\to(X,x_{0})
, the order of vanishing
\mathrm{ord}_{E}
along
E
defines a valuation over
\mathcal{O}_{X_{\pi},\eta}
, where
\eta
is the generic point of
E
.
Its push-forward
\mathrm{div}_{E}:=\pi_{*}\mathrm{ord}_{E}
, defined by
\phi\mapsto\mathrm{ord}_{E}(\phi\circ\pi)
, and its positive multiples
\lambda\mathrm{div}_{E}
, are called
divisorial valuations
; they correspond to points of type II in the Berkovich language.
The space of divisorial valuations is denoted by
{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}^{\mathrm{div}}}
, and it is a dense subset of
{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}}
. Similarly, the space of normalized divisorial valuations is denoted by
{\mathcal{V}_{X}^{\mathrm{div}}}
, and the normalized equivalent of
\mathrm{div}_{E}
is denoted by
\nu_{E}
.

1.2.2.
Quasimonomial valuations

More generally, assume that
\pi\colon X_{\pi}\to(X,x_{0})
is a good resolution; consider a closed point
p\in\pi^{-1}(x_{0})
, that we may assume to be
satellite
:
p\in E\cap F
for two exceptional primes
E,F\in\varGamma_{\pi}^{*}
.
Take local coordinates
(z,w)
at
p

adapted
to
\pi^{-1}(x_{0})
, i.e., such that
E=\{z=0\}
and
F=\{w=0\}
.
Given a pair
(r,s)
of non-negative real numbers (not both zero), we consider the monomial valuation
\mu_{r,s}
at
p
, defined as

\mu_{r,s}\Big(\sum_{i,j}a_{i,j}z^{i}w^{j}\Big):=\min\left\{ri+sj\ |\ a_{i,j}\neq 0\right\}\text{.}

Its push-forward
\nu_{r,s}:=\pi_{\star}\mu_{r,s}
is called a
quasimonomial valuation
, and the set of all such valuations is denoted by
{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}^{\mathrm{qm}}}
.

When
(r,s)
are positive and rationally dependent, that we may assume to be comprime integers modulo equivalence, then
\nu_{r,s}
is in fact divisorial, associated to the exceptional prime
E_{\frac{s}{r}}
, obtained as the weighted blow-up of
p
of weight
(r,s)
.
The extreme cases
(r,s)=(1,0)
and
(r,s)=(0,1)
also correspond to divisorial valuations, associated to the exceptional primes
E_{0}=E
and
E_{\infty}=F
respectively.
When
(r,s)
are rationally independent, the valuation
\nu_{r,s}
is called
irrational
, and corresponds to points of type III in the Berkovich language.

1.2.3.
Curve semivaluations

Consider a branch
C
at
x_{0}
, i.e., a germ of (possibly formal) irreducible curve
(C,x_{0})\subset(X,x_{0})
.
Then we get a semivaluation by setting

\mathrm{int}_{C}(\phi):=C\cdot(\phi=0)

where
\cdot
stands for the local intersection at
x_{0}
, i.e.,
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{R_{X}}{I_{C}+\langle\phi\rangle}
.

Any positive multiple of
\mathrm{int}_{C}
is called a
curve (semi-)valuation
; they correspond to type I points (or to the subclass of rigid points, depending on the precise setting in Berkovich theory, see e.g.
[
DFR25
, Section 1]
).
The prefix (semi-) alludes to the fact that
\mathrm{int}_{C}
takes the value
+\infty
not only on
0
, but on the whole ideal
I_{C}
.
The normalized equivalent of
\mathrm{int}_{C}
is denoted by
\nu_{C}
.

1.2.4.
Infinitely singular valuations

The remaining valuations are called
infinitely singular
. In terms of Berkovich geometry, they correspond to points of type IV (or to the non-rigid type I points).
There are several ways to characterize them. One is in terms of certain algebraic invariants (they are the ones with rank and rational rank equal to
1
, and transcendental degree equal to
0
); they are also characterized by having a non-finitely generated value group over
\mathbb{Z}
.
One can think of them as curve valuations associated to curves of “infinite multiplicity” (hence the name).

1.3.
Center of a valuation

One can also characterize valuations in terms of their associated sequence of infinitely near points, built as follows.
Recall that, given a semivaluation
\nu\in{\mathcal{V}_{X}}
(or more precisely, in the sense of Krull valuations, i.e., valuations with values on an arbitrary totally ordered abelian group), and a modification
\pi\colon X_{\pi}\to(X,x_{0})
, the center
\operatorname{cen}_{\pi}(\nu)
is the unique scheme-theoretic point
\xi\in X_{\pi_{0}}
such that
\mathcal{O}_{X_{\pi},\xi}
is dominated by the local ring
(R_{\nu},\mathfrak{m}_{\nu})
, where
R_{\nu}=\{\phi\in\operatorname{Frac}(R_{X})\ |\ \nu(\phi)\geq 0\}
, and
\mathfrak{m}_{\nu}=\{\phi\in\operatorname{Frac}(R_{X})\ |\ \nu(\phi)>0\}
is its unique maximal ideal.

Then, start with any resolution
\pi_{0}\colon X_{\pi_{0}}\to(X,x_{0})
, and denote by
p_{0}=\operatorname{cen}_{\pi_{0}}(\nu)
the center of
\nu
in
X_{\pi_{0}}
.
If
p_{0}
is a closed point, take the blow-up of
X_{\pi_{1}}\to(X_{\pi_{0}},p_{0})
, and set
p_{1}=\operatorname{cen}_{\pi_{1}}(\nu)
.
In this way, we construct a (possibly finite) sequence of models
\pi_{n}\colon X_{n}\to(X,x_{0})
and of infinitely-near points
(p_{n})_{n}
with
p_{n}\in\pi_{n}^{-1}(x_{0})
.

The type of a valuation
\nu\in{\mathcal{V}_{X}}
is determined by this sequence
(p_{n})_{n}
, as follows:

•

\nu
is divisorial iff the sequence
(p_{n})
is finite;

•

\nu
is irrational iff
p_{n}
is satellite for any
n\gg 0
;

•

\nu
is a curve semivaluation iff
p_{n}
is free for any
n\gg 0
;

•

\nu
is infinitely singular iff the sequence
(p_{n})
contains infinitely many free points and infinitely many satellite points.

Notice that replacing the initial resolution
\pi_{0}
with any other changes the sequence of infinitely near points, but preserves the properties listed above.

Remark 1.4
.

To be precise, there are two possible cases when
(p_{n})_{n}
is a sequence of eventually satellite points.

•

Suppose there exists an exceptional prime
E\in\varGamma_{\pi_{N}}^{*}
for some
N\gg 0
such that
p_{n}
belongs to the strict transform of
E
for any
n>N
.
In this case
\nu
is an
exceptional curve valuation
, which is a Krull valuation of rank
2
, not associated to a valuation
\nu\in{\mathcal{V}_{X}}
, but rather to a tangent vector at the divisorial valuation
\nu_{E}
.

•

If there is no such exceptional prime
E
as above, then
\nu
is an irrational valuation, of the form
(\pi_{N})_{*}\mu
, with
\mu
a monomial valuation, and
N
such that
p_{n}
is satellite for any
n>N
.

Remark 1.5
.

Centers give also a geometrical interpretation of the weak topology on
{\mathcal{V}_{X}}
.
Given a modification
\pi\colon X_{\pi}\to(X,x_{0})
, and a point
p\in\pi^{-1}(x_{0})
, we denote by
U_{\pi}(p)
the set of valuations whose center in
X_{\pi}
is
p
.
Then this set is weakly open, and the collection of
U_{\pi}(p)
, when
\pi
varies among all possible modifications and
p
among all closed points in
\pi^{-1}(x_{0})
, forms a prebase for the weak topology.

1.4.
Valuation spaces as projective limits of dual graphs

Given a good resolution
\pi\colon X_{\pi}\to(X,x_{0})
, we can consider the finite set
\mathcal{S}_{\pi}^{*}=\{\nu_{E}\ |\ E\in\varGamma_{\pi}^{*}\}
of normalized divisorial valuations associated to the exceptional primes of
\pi
.

Their convex hull
\mathcal{S}_{\pi}
in
{\mathcal{V}_{X}}
, called the
skeleton
with respect to
\pi
, is obtained from
\mathcal{S}_{\pi}^{*}
by adding all normalized valuations that are monomial in a satellite point of
X_{\pi}
.
If moreover
\pi
is a log-resolution of
\mathfrak{m}_{X}
, then, for any satellite point
p\in E\cap F
, the segment
[\nu_{E},\nu_{F}]_{p}
consists of the monomial valuations
\nu_{r,s}=\pi_{*}\mu_{r,s}
at
p
satisfying
rb_{E}+sb_{F}=1
, where
b_{E}=\mathrm{ord}_{E}(\pi^{*}\mathfrak{m}_{X})
and
b_{F}=\mathrm{ord}_{F}(\pi^{*}\mathfrak{m}_{X})
.

We deduce that
\mathcal{S}_{\pi}
has a natural structure of graph, isomorphic to the dual graph
\varGamma_{\pi}
of the resolution
\pi
.
Given
\pi^{\prime}\colon X_{\pi^{\prime}}\to(X,x_{0})
another log-resolution of
\mathfrak{m}_{X}
dominating
\pi
, we clearly have that
\mathcal{S}_{\pi}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\pi^{\prime}}
.
We also have a natural
retraction map

\rho_{\pi^{\prime},\pi}\colon\mathcal{S}_{\pi^{\prime}}\to\mathcal{S}_{\pi}
, defined as follows.
Set
p=\operatorname{cen}_{\pi}(\nu)
. If
p
is a free point, belonging to the exceptional prime
E
, then
\rho_{\pi^{\prime},\pi}(\nu)=\nu_{E}
.
If
p\in E\cap F
is a satellite point, then
\rho_{\pi^{\prime},\pi}(\nu)=\pi_{*}\mu_{r,s}
is the (opportunely renormalized) monomial valuation at
p
with weights
r=\nu(z)
and
s=\nu(z)
, where
(z,w)
are local coordinates at
p
adapted to
\pi^{-1}(x_{0})
.

Since any quasimonomial valuation can be found in a skeleton
\mathcal{S}_{\pi}
for a suitable good resolution
\pi
, we deduce that
{\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{qm}}}
can be described as the injective limit of all skeleta.
Similarly, the set of normalized valuations
{\mathcal{V}_{X}}
can be described as the projective limt of these skeleta (with respect to the inverse system of good resolutions and the retraction maps).
We refer to
[
Thu07
,
Jon12
]
for more details.

2.
Map induced on valuation spaces

Any dominant holomorphic germ
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
between two normal surface singularities induces a map
f_{*}\colon{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}}\to{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{Y}}
on centered semivaluations, and consequently a map
f_{\bullet}\colon{\mathcal{V}_{X}}\to{\mathcal{V}_{Y}}
at the level of normalized semivaluations.
In this section, we describe some basic properties of these maps, and refer to
[
FJ07
,
Fav10
,
GR21
]
for further details.

2.1.
Definitions and first properties

Let
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
be a holomorphic germ between two normal surface singularities.

This induces a local ring homomorphism
f^{*}\colon(R_{Y},\mathfrak{m}_{Y})\to(R_{X},\mathfrak{m}_{X})
by pullback:
f^{*}\psi:=\psi\circ f
.
By duality, we get a map
f_{*}\colon{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}}\to{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{Y}}
acting on centered valuations, defined by
f_{*}\nu:=\nu\circ f^{*}
. This map
f_{*}
is continuous with respect to the weak topologies on
{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}}
and
{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{Y}}
and commutes with scalar multiplication of semivaluations.

If
\nu\in{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}^{*}}
is a finite semivaluation then
f_{*}\nu
will again be finite, except in exactly one situation: if
\nu
is a curve semivaluation associated to a curve germ
(C,x_{0})\subset(X,x_{0})
which is contracted by
f
, i.e.,
f(C)=y_{0}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{C}_{f}
the (finite) set of curve semivaluations
\nu_{C}
such that
f(C)=y_{0}
. The case when
\mathcal{C}_{f}=\emptyset
is rather special: in this case
f
is called
finite
.

From the discussion above, it follows that
f_{*}
induces a (continuous) map
f_{\bullet}\colon{\mathcal{V}_{X}}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{f}\to{\mathcal{V}_{Y}}
, which in fact extends by continuity to a map
f_{\bullet}\colon{\mathcal{V}_{X}}\to{\mathcal{V}_{Y}}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
be any dominant holomorphic germ between normal surface singularities.

•

If
\nu\in{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}^{*}}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{f}
is not a contracted curve semivaluation, then
f_{\bullet}\nu
is of the same type (curve, divisorial, irrational, infinitely singular) as
\nu
.

•

If
\nu
is a contracted curve semivaluation, then
f_{\bullet}\nu
is divisorial.

2.2.
Geometric interpretation of the action

For our purposes, we need more geometrical insight about the action of
f_{*}\colon{\mathcal{V}_{X}}\to{\mathcal{V}_{Y}}
on divisorial valuations and curve semivaluations.

We start with divisorial valuations.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
FJ07
, Prop. 2.5]
)
.

Let
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
be a dominant germ between two normal surface singularities, and let
\nu_{E}\in{\mathcal{V}_{X}}
be a divisorial valuation.
Denote by
\nu_{E^{\prime}}:=f_{\bullet}\nu_{E}
its image, which is also divisorial by
Proposition
2.1
.
Let
\pi\colon X_{\pi}\to(X,x_{0})
and
\varpi\colon Y_{\varpi}\to(Y,y_{0})
be two good resolutions such that
E\in\varGamma_{\pi}^{*}
and
E^{\prime}\in\varGamma_{\varpi}^{*}
.

Then the lift
\hat{f}\colon X_{\pi}\dashrightarrow Y_{\varpi}
satisfies
\hat{f}|_{E}(E)=E^{\prime}
, and

f_{*}\mathrm{div}_{E}=k_{E}\mathrm{div}E^{\prime}\text{,}

where
k_{E}
is the ramification index of
\hat{f}|_{E}
, i.e., the coefficient of
E
in the divisor
\hat{f}^{*}E^{\prime}
.

If
C
is a branch at
x_{0}
, then its image
f(C)
is either a branch at
y_{0}
, or is contracted to
y_{0}
by
f
.
The next two propositions describe what happens in each case.

Proposition 2.3

(
[
FJ07
, Prop. 2.6]
)
.

Let
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
be a dominant germ between normal surface singularities, and let
C
be a branch at
(X,x_{0})
that is not contracted by
f
.
Then
f(C)=:C^{\prime}
is a branch at
(Y,y_{0})
, and

f_{*}\mathrm{int}_{C}=e_{C}\mathrm{int}_{C^{\prime}}\text{,}

where
e_{C}
is the local topological degree of
f|_{C}\colon(C,x_{0})\to(C^{\prime},y_{0})
.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
FJ07
, Prop. 2.7]
)
.

Let
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
be a dominant germ between normal surface singularities, and let
C
be a branch at
(X,x_{0})
such that
f(C)=y_{0}
.
By
Proposition
2.1
, its image
f_{\bullet}\nu_{C}=\nu_{G}
is divisorial.
Then, for any good resolution
\varpi\colon Y_{\varpi}\to(Y,y_{0})
such that
G\in\varGamma_{\varpi}^{*}
, the lift
\hat{f}\colon(X,x_{0})\to Y_{\pi}
is such that
\hat{f}|_{C}\colon(C,x_{0})\to(G,q)
for a suitable point
q\in G
.

Remark 2.5
.

In terms of Krull valuations, i.e., if we look at the action induced on the Riemann-Zariski spaces, we have that the image of a contracted curve valuation
\nu_{C}
is an exceptional curve valuation (see
Remark
1.4
).

2.3.
Detecting indeterminacy points

The map
f_{\bullet}\colon{\mathcal{V}_{X}}\to{\mathcal{V}_{Y}}
also allows to detect the action and indeterminacy points of the lift
\hat{f}\colon X_{\pi}\dashrightarrow Y_{\varpi}
of a dominant germ
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
for any modifications
\pi\colon X_{\pi}\to(X,x_{0})
and
\varpi\colon Y_{\varpi}\to(Y,y_{0})
.

Recall that for any closed point
p\in\pi^{-1}(x_{0})
, then
U_{\pi}(p)\subset{\mathcal{V}_{X}}
stands for the weak open subset of semivaluations whose center in
X_{\pi}
is
p
.
If
\hat{f}
is holomorphic at a closed point
p\in\pi^{-1}(x_{0})
, and if
q=\hat{f}(p)
, then
f_{\bullet}(U_{\pi}(p))\subseteq U_{\varpi}(q)
; this is simply a matter of unraveling definitions. As a consequence of the valuative criterion of properness (we use here the normality of the spaces
X_{\pi}
and
Y_{\varpi}
), the converse is also true (see, e.g.,
[
FJ07
, Prop. 3.2]
or
[
GR21
, Proposition 4.12]
).

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
be a holomorphic germ between normal surface singularities.
Let
\pi:X_{\pi}\to(X,x_{0})
and
\varpi:Y_{\varpi}\to(Y,y_{0})
be two modifications (with
X_{\pi}
and
Y_{\varpi}
normal).
Then the map
\hat{f}=\varpi^{-1}\circ f\circ\pi
is holomorphic at a point
p\in\pi^{-1}(x_{0})
if and only if there exists a point
q\in\varpi^{-1}(y_{0})
so that
f_{\bullet}U_{\pi}(p)\subseteq U_{\varpi}(q)
.
In this case,
\hat{f}(p)=q
.

2.4.
Log-discrepancy

The log-discrepancy
{A}_{X}(\nu)
of a valuation
\nu\in{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}^{*}}
is a quantity that measures the positivity properties of the canonical bundle
K_{X}
along
\nu
, and it is a fundamental tool in birational geometry to measure how bad a singularity is (see, e.g.,
[
KM98
,
Kol13
]
). It is defined as follows.

Fix a nontrivial holomorphic
2
-form
\omega
on
(X,x_{0})
; the vanishing of
\omega
defines a Weil divisor on
X
that we denote by
\operatorname{Div}(\omega)
.
Given a resolution
\pi\colon X_{\pi}\to(X,x_{0})
, the
relative canonical divisor
of
\pi
is the divisor
K_{\pi}\in\mathcal{E}(\pi)_{\mathbb{Q}}
defined by the equality
\operatorname{Div}(\pi^{*}\omega)=\pi^{*}\operatorname{Div}(\omega)+K_{\pi}
, where
\pi^{*}\operatorname{Div}(\omega)
refers to the Mumford pull-back of
\operatorname{Div}(\omega)
(see, e.g.,
[
Mat02
, p. 195]
). The relative canonical divisor
K_{\pi}
does not depend on the choice of
\omega
.

The
log-discrepancy
of an exceptional prime
E\in\varGamma_{\pi}^{*}
is given by
{A}_{X}(E):=1+\mathrm{ord}_{E}K_{\pi}
.

Remark 2.7
.

Besides a direct computation of log-discrepancies, one can compute them via adjunction formula, as follows.

Fix a good resolution
\pi\colon X_{\pi}\to(X,x_{0})
, and label its exceptional primes
E_{1},\ldots,E_{n}
.
We set
{A}_{i}:={A}(E_{i})
, and denote by
{A}=({A}_{i})_{i}
the vector of log-discrepancies of the exceptional primes in
\varGamma_{\pi}^{*}
.
The adjunction formula for
X_{\pi}
gives

(1)

2g(E)-2=K_{E}=(K_{\pi}+E)\cdot E\text{.}

for every
E\in\varGamma_{\pi}^{*}
,
where here
g(E)
denotes the genus of
E
, and
K_{E}
denotes the canonical bundle of
E
.
By applying
Equation (
1
)
to
E_{j}
for all
j=1,\ldots,n
, we get

2g(E_{j})-2=\sum_{i}{A}_{i}E_{i}\cdot E_{j}-\sum_{i\neq j}E_{i}\cdot E_{j}=(M{A})_{j}-s(E_{j})\text{,}

where
s(E_{j})
is the degree of
E_{j}
in the graph
\varGamma_{\pi}
, and
M
is the intersection matrix on
\mathcal{E}(\pi)
.
Hence, we can compute the vector of log-discrepancies by the formula

(2)

{A}=M^{-1}(2g-2+s)\text{,}

where
2g-2+s
denotes the vector of entries
2g(E_{j})-2+s(E_{j})
.

If
\pi^{\prime}
is another good resolution dominating
\pi
, i.e., the map
\eta=\pi^{-1}\circ\pi^{\prime}\colon X_{\pi^{\prime}}\to X_{\pi}
is regular, then we have
\eta_{*}K_{\pi^{\prime}}=K_{\pi}
.
We deduce that the log-discrepancy
{A}_{X}(E)
does not depend on the choice of the model
\pi
realizing
E
: it is a function of the associated valuation
\mathrm{ord}_{E}
.
Log-discrepancy is then defined by homogeneity to all divisorial valuations
\lambda\mathrm{ord}_{E}
, by setting
{A}(\lambda\mathrm{ord}_{E}):=\lambda{A}(\mathrm{ord}_{E})
for any
\lambda>0
, any good resolution
\pi
and any exceptional prime
E\in\varGamma_{\pi}^{*}
.

A very convenient property of log-discrepancies is that they behave well on monomial valuations, in the following sense.
Assume we are in the situation of
Subsection
1.2.2
, then for any rationally-dependent weights
(r,s)
, we have that
\nu_{r,s}
is divisorial, and a direct computation yields

(3)

{A}(\nu_{r,s})=r{A}(E_{0})+s{A}(E_{1})\text{.}

One can then use
Equation (
3
)
to extend the log-discrepancy to a continuous functional on the space
{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}^{\mathrm{qm}}}
of quasimonomial valuations, and extend it further to a lower semi-continuous functional
{A}_{X}\colon{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}^{*}}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
defined on finite semivaluations (see
[
Fav10
, Proposition 1.6]
).

2.5.
Jacobian formula

The Jacobian formula allows to compare the log-discrepancies of a valuation and its image by a dominant map.
A proof of this result can be found in
[
GR21
, Section 4.5]
, see also
[
Fav10
, Proposition 1.9]
for the finite case.

Proposition 2.8

(The Jacobian Formula)
.

Let
(X,x_{0})
and
(Y,y_{0})
be two normal surface singularities, and let
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
be a dominant holomorphic map.
Then for any
\nu\in{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}^{*}}
, we have

(4)

{A}_{Y}(f_{*}\nu)={A}_{X}(\nu)+\nu(R_{f})\text{,}

where
R_{f}
, called the
Jacobian divisor
of
f
, is the Weil divisor on
X
defined by

(5)

R_{f}=\operatorname{Div}(f^{*}\omega)-f^{*}\operatorname{Div}(\omega)\text{,}

with
\omega
any holomorphic
2
-form on
(Y,y_{0})
.

When
(X,x_{0})\cong(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
, then one can take
\omega=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\wedge\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y
, where
(x,y)
are local coordinates at
0
. Then
R_{f}
is the divisor associated to the jacobian determinant of
f
. In particular,
R_{f}
is effective and non-trivial in this case, and
\nu(R_{f})>0
for any
\nu\in{\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{X}^{*}}
.

Similarly, for finite germs
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
, the divisor
R_{f}
is effective, even though it might be trivial (see
[
Fav10
, Theorem B]
).
In fact, at any point
x\in X\setminus\{x_{0}\}
, the germ
f\colon(X,x)\to(Y,f(x))
is between two regular surfaces, and again
R_{f}
is locally described at
x
as the jacobian determinant of
f
.

One can also show that
R_{f}
is effective whenever
(Y,y_{0})
is canonical (i.e., the discrepancies
{a}(E):={A}(E)-1
of all exceptional primes are
\geq 0
), see
[
GR21
, Proposition 4.33]
.

In general however,
R_{f}
is not effective, and the difference

{A}_{Y}(f_{*}\nu)-{A}_{X}(\nu)

might be
\leq 0
.
It is rather simple to find examples of this phenomenon, for example by taking
f
to be the resolution of a non log-canonical singularity (see
[
GR21
, Example 4.29]
).
One can also construct examples of selfmaps
f\colon(X,x_{0})\tiny{\!\!\!\lx@xy@svg{\hbox{\raise 1.7014pt\hbox{\kern 4.14584pt\hbox{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\hbox{\vtop{\halign{\entry@#!@&&\entry@@#!@\cr\crcr}}}\ignorespaces{\hbox{\kern-4.14584pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 3.0pt\raise-1.7014pt\hbox{$\textstyle{\phantom{|}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}$}}}}}}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces{}{}{}{}{{}{{}{{}{{}{{}}{}{{}{{}}{}{{}}{}{{}{{}}{}{{}}}}}}}}{}\ignorespaces\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{{}{}{{}}{{}{}{}{}}{}}}}\ignorespaces{}\ignorespaces{}{}{}{}{{}{{}}{}{{}}{}{{}}{}{{}}{}{{}{{}{{}}{}{{}}{}{{}}}}}{\hbox{\kern 2.37866pt\raise 2.1464pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\lx@xy@tip{1}\lx@xy@tip{-1}}}}}}\ignorespaces\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{{}{}{}{{}}{{}{}{}{}\lx@xy@spline@}{}}}}\ignorespaces{}\ignorespaces\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{{}{}{{}}{{}{}{}{}}{}}}}\ignorespaces{}\ignorespaces}}}}\ignorespaces\hskip 11.38109pt}

).

3.
Topological degree and strict germs

3.1.
Generalities on topological degree

Let
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
be a dominant holomorphic germ between two (normal surface) singularities.
When
f
is
finite
,
the topological degree of
f
is defined as the number of preimages (counted with multiplicity) of a generic point. It can be also computed as the dimension of
\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{\small{$R_{X}$}}\kern-1.79993pt\big/\kern-1.99997pt\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small{$f^{*}\mathfrak{m}_{Y}$}}
(see
[
AGZV85
, Section 5.4 Proposition 2]
for a proof in the regular setting).

A third interpretation in the regular setting is given by the degree of the map induced by
f
on the links
\mathbb{S}^{3}
(see
[
AGZV85
, Section 5.2 Theorem 1]
).

In the
generically finite
case (i.e., the germ
f
is dominant, but not necessarily finite), we consider the following definition of topological degree.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
be a dominant holomorphic germ between two (normal surface) singularities.
We define the
topological degree
of
f
as:

\operatorname{topdeg}f=\inf_{U}\sup_{y}\#f|_{U}^{-1}(y)

where
U
varies among open neighborhoods of
x_{0}
in
U
, and
y
varies among points in
Y
which have finite preimage by
f|_{U}
.

Notice that the topological degree does not depend on the representant of
f
chosen; it is a positive integer (we will see that the topological degree is always finite).

Example 3.2
.

Given a matrix
A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix},

with
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we consider the map
f_{A}\colon(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
given by
f(z,w)=(z^{a}w^{b},z^{c}w^{d})
.
If
\det A\neq 0
, then
f
is a dominant germ, which contracts the coordinate axes
\{zw=0\}
to the origin
0
.
Notice also that the points in
\{zw=0\}\setminus\{0\}
do not belong to the image of
f
, while for any point outside the coordinate axes, we have exactly
{\left|{\det A}\right|}
preimages. We deduce that
\operatorname{topdeg}f={\left|{\det A}\right|}
.
These maps belong to class
6
of
[
Fav00
]
.

Example 3.3
.

Fix any
a\geq 2
and
c\geq 1
.
For any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we consider the maps
f_{k}\colon(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
, given by
f_{k}(z,w)=(z^{a},z^{c}w+z^{k})
, which belong to the special case of class
4
of
[
Fav00
]
.
The map
f_{k}
is in normal form when
k\leq c
or
k=\frac{ac}{a-1}
, while for the remaining values of
k
, the germ
f_{k}
is conjugated to
f_{\infty}\colon(z,w)\mapsto(z^{a},z^{c}w)
.
By
[
Fav00
, Proposition 1.5]
, we have

\operatorname{topdeg}f_{k}=\begin{cases}\gcd(a,k)&\text{if }k\leq c\text{,}\\
a&\text{if }k>c\text{.}\end{cases}

The interpretation of the topological degree in terms of the dimension of
\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{\small{$\mathcal{O}_{X}$}}\kern-1.79993pt\big/\kern-1.99997pt\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small{$f^{*}\mathfrak{m}_{Y}$}}
does not work in the generically finite setting, since this dimension is infinite in this case.
However, a related concept is the degree
\delta(f)
of the field extension of
\operatorname{Frac}(R_{X})
over
f^{*}\operatorname{Frac}(R_{Y})
.

Example 3.4
.

For the previous examples, a direct computation gives

\delta(f_{A})={\left|{\det A}\right|}=\operatorname{topdeg}f_{A}\text{,}\hskip 20.00003pt\delta(f_{k})=p\geq\operatorname{topdeg}f_{A}\text{.}

Another way to compute the topological degree comes from the action
f_{\bullet}\colon{\mathcal{V}_{X}}\to{\mathcal{V}_{Y}}
on valuation spaces.
The following result is a consequence of
[
GR21
, Theorem 4.18]
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
be a dominant holomorphic germ between two normal surface singularities.
Then

\operatorname{topdeg}{f}=\max_{\nu\in{\mathcal{V}_{Y}}}\#f_{\bullet}^{-1}(\nu)\text{.}

In fact, the number of preimages by
f_{\bullet}
(counted with multiplicity) of a valuation
\nu\in{\mathcal{V}_{Y}}
is exactly
\operatorname{topdeg}f
in the finite case, while it can be strictly less for some valuations
\nu
in the generically finite case (see
[
GR21
, Remark 4.19]
).

Proposition
3.5
gives a sharp bound on a number of preimages of a valuation with respect to
[
FJ07
, Proposition 2.4]
, where the authors show that the cardinality of
f_{\bullet}^{-1}(\nu)
is bounded by
\delta(f)
.
In particular, we deduce that
\operatorname{topdeg}f\leq\delta(f)
for any
f
, and we have seen that the inequality can be strict.

The discrepancy between
\delta(f)
and
\operatorname{topdeg}f
is due to the fact that the latter measures the number of preimages that remain close to
x_{0}
, while the former counts also the preimages that escape small neighborhoods of
x_{0}
.

3.2.
Strict germs

Definition 3.6
.

A holomorphic germ
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
between normal surface singularities (or more generally between germs complex analytic spaces of the same pure dimension) is called
strict
if it has topological degree
1
and it is not an isomorphism.

Remark 3.7
.

Sometimes in the literature the family of strict germs contains also local isomorphism.

As a direct consequence of
Proposition
3.5
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
be a strict germ between normal surface singularities. The map
f_{\bullet}\colon{\mathcal{V}_{X}}\to{\mathcal{V}_{Y}}
is injective.

The next result describes the critical set of a strict germ, showing that it consists of contracted curves.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
f\colon(X,x_{0})\to(Y,y_{0})
be a strict germ between two normal surface singularities.
Let
C=\mathcal{C}(f)
be the critical set of
f
. Then
f(C)=y_{0}
.

Proof.

Arguing by contradiction, suppose there is
x\in C

Remark 3.11
.

We will spend some words about the proof of

This distance is defined in terms of intersection of b-divisors associated to valuations, or equivalently as

\rho_{X}(\nu,\nu^{\prime}):=\log\beta_{X}(\nu|\nu^{\prime})\beta_{X}(\nu^{\prime}|\nu)\text{, \hskip 20.00003ptwhere }\beta_{X}(\nu|\nu^{\prime}):=\sup_{\mathfrak{a}}\frac{\nu(\mathfrak{a})}{\nu^{\prime}(\mathfrak{a})}

The angular distance plays the role of the Poincaré distance for hyperbolic Riemann surfaces, in the sense that for any dominant germ

Via valuation theory

The second proof of

Proof of

(via valuation theory).

Let

By

4.3.
Remarks

Let

We have showed that if

There are several ways one could try to generalize this result.

First, notice that local isomorphisms are exactly the germs of topological degree

Then one could wonder if it is true that any map of topological degree

is surjective.

6.4.
Remarks on Kato surfaces and eigenvaluations

Let

and the local isomorphism

Notice that, given the Kato germ

Kato surfaces are compact, have negative Kodaira dimension and first Betti number

(in particular, they are non-Kähler), which places them in class VII of Kodaira’s classification.
Their minimal model has positive second Betti number

]

Regular

[

]

In the singular setting,
[
Kat79
]
classifies surfaces admitting a “global strongly pseudoconvex hypersurfaces”, that in our case appear as an embedding of the link of the singularity

inside

(see
[
FFR20

We have seen (see

Regular Kato germs form a special class of contracting

Remark 6.2
.

In higher dimensions, this is not true anymore: there are examples of regular Kato germs with infinitely many irreducible components in

.
