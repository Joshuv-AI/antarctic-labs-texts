Timing of food intake impacts cardiovascular and neuroendocrine physiology and risk of disease
Authors: Mary N Latimer, Megan K Rhoads, David M Pollock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite many advances in the last 75 years in the fight against cardiovascular disease mortality, it remains the leading cause of death worldwide. Despite improved therapeutic approaches, cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of death worldwide. Mounting evidence suggests that restricting food intake to a limited period in the day or extending periods of fasting may be a lifestyle intervention that reduces progression of cardiovascular disease. Various protocols for time-restricted feeding paradigms suggest significant benefits throughout the cardiovascular system. Despite some limitations to their use, the general mechanism is thought to revolve around circadian alignment of cellular and organ function with behavioural and environmental patterns. Here, we review recent evidence in a rapidly expanding field that strives to understand how timing of food intake regulates circadian physiology of cardiovascular systems focusing on heart, vascular, and neuroendocrine physiology. We review both pre-clinical and clinical reports showing how timed feeding may alter organ function in both health and disease.Keywords: Circadian rhythms, Time-restricted feeding, Intermittent fasting, Heart failure, Autonomic nervous system

Introduction

Molecular clocks in health and disease

The invention of the practical lightbulb by Thomas Edison in 1879 allowed a reliable, efficient, and cost-effective way to simulate the sun at night. Prior to this, work had to be performed during the day or by muted candlelight at night. This abrupt change in the availability of bright light caused a massive shift in our sleep and eating patterns over a relatively short period of time. The 24 h culture that has emerged from these innovations has fostered an environment of global connectivity that calls for earlier mornings, later nights, and less sleep than ever before. While this has moved us forward as a culture, it has also caused significant, well-documented, health impacts.

Shift work, technology use, global connectivity, and late-night entertainment encourage both disrupted sleep and late-night eating patterns. This accumulation of mis-timed meals and lost sleep leads to circadian disruption where the master clock in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) does not align with the peripheral clocks in the heart or vasculature (Figure 1). Multiple health problems have been tied to this disruption with certain sects of workers at greatest risk. These jobs include multiple public safety professions such as police officers, firefighters, and hospital workers, which routinely require work outside the traditional ‘9 to 5’ schedule.4 Current research has highlighted the detrimental effects of night shift work on cardiovascular disease (CVD), particularly its association with increased risk of all-cause mortality,5 coronary heart disease,6 and ischaemic stroke.7 However, we have known about the detrimental effects of shift work since the early 1970s. Multiple papers from this time period highlighted the increased CVD risk in shift workers8–10 with one of the first reviews published in 1983.11 This begs the question, why we have yet to establish a mechanism linking CVD and shift work with implementation of interventions aimed at reducing this risk.

To gain answers to this decade’s old conundrum, we posit that you must consider the fundamental physiologic underlying mechanism, the circadian clock. Our cardiovascular system depends on well-orchestrated rhythms in both physiologic and metabolic demand to maintain optimal function.12Disruptions, such as eating at the wrong time of the day, impair this system leading to an increased risk for CVD.4,7,11 The epidemiological evidence indicates that night shift workers, whose circadian clocks are misaligned with the natural day-night cycle, exhibit higher rates of CVD and other comorbid conditions, including hypertension and diabetes, which further exacerbate CVD risk.13,14

Multiple mechanisms have been recognized as contributors to CVD, and so it is becoming increasingly clear that this disease is the result of dramatic metabolic, inflammatory, and structural changes. The question remains, however, whether there is a common mechanism that contributes to all these perturbations dramatically increasing the risk of CVD. One such novel mechanism is the circadian clock. Circadian clocks are cell-autonomous molecular mechanisms, which confer the selective advantage of anticipation, allowing cells to prepare for predicted stimuli/stressors prior to their onset.15 Functional circadian clocks are present in all cardiovascular cell types investigated to date such as cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells. Transcriptional modulators are at the core of the circadian clock and include brain/muscle aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator-like protein 1 (BMAL1; Figure 2) among many others.16 The general molecular regulation of this feedback system has been extensively reviewed elsewhere, and so the reader is referred to these more specific reviews.12,16 In brief, circadian clocks drive time-of-day-dependent alterations in cellular functions by modulating expression of numerous genes. Importantly, time-of-day-dependent changes have been reported for numerous biological processes that are critical for cardiovascular function.17 The molecular clock controls thousands of genes that are expressed rhythmically with many being critically important for controlling cell metabolism including glucose, amino acid, and fatty acid metabolism as well as systems that control rhythmic mitochondrial function.16,18

Caloric restriction

Caloric restriction is broadly defined as a dietary regimen which restricts daily food intake without causing malnutrition. Caloric restriction has a long history of improving lifespan in short-lived species from 50% to upwards of 300%.19 This improvement may be translatable to humans as suggested by studies using caloric restriction in young adults. The landmark Comprehensive Assessment of Long-term Effects of Reducing Intake of Energy trial assigned adults to 25% caloric restriction or maintenance of normal dietary intake.20 Overall, the trial had substandard adherence with participants achieving caloric restriction of roughly 11.9% but did result in weight loss associated with reduced oxidative stress, decreased blood pressure, and improvements in working memory assessments.21,22

Despite the beneficial effects, caloric restriction comes with a host of adverse side effects which limit its clinical usefulness to populations of mainly young or middle-aged healthy adults. Risks to older populations include the probability of osteoporosis,23 diminished capacity for aerobic exercise,21 and potential loss of both lean and fat mass (reviewed in Locher et al.24) Given the limitations of both effectiveness and adherence, alternatives for caloric restriction have been of greater interest in the last 20 years. These are regimens which mimic the effectiveness of caloric restriction without the need for reducing total caloric intake. This is a broad concept but encompasses any regimen that includes periods of voluntary abstinence from food. Patterson et al.25 in 2016 termed any such regimen as intermittent fasting (IF) and reported beneficial outcomes associated with each regimen in terms of weight loss, cardiometabolic parameters, and adherence.

de Cabo and Mattson26 in 2019 reviewed IF regimens with a narrower focus on cellular mechanisms of restoration and their impact on ageing as well as disease susceptibility. They concluded that one of the driving forces at work in caloric restriction regimens may be that the animals or humans are choosing to consume their calorie allotment in a narrow window. Once the calories are consumed, the participant must then fast for the remainder of the day; in rodent studies, this is sometimes as long as 20 h.26 Chaix et al.27 showed benefits in metabolic disease end points arising from a variety of obesogenic diets that were proportional to the fasting duration. Similar results were reported in male mice subjected to daily 20 h of fasting with improvements in health and survival regardless of diet composition or calories eaten.28 However, these studies incorporate a prolonged (>12 h) fasting period, which is known to have its own beneficial effects on metabolic and cardiovascular parameters.29

Time-restricted feeding

Separate from these studies of caloric restriction and fasting, several researchers were investigating the circadian clock’s role in regulating whole-body metabolism. For example, Turek et al.30 first showed that loss of the central circadian gene, Clock, led to obesity and metabolic syndrome in mice. However, many argued that a whole-body loss of a central circadian gene would also affect the central circadian pacemaker in the SCN, thus disturbing feeding and/or activity rhythms. However, conditional circadian pancreatic31 and liver32,33 specific knockouts both displayed similar degrees of metabolic dysfunction leading to the conclusion that the circadian clock was at least partially responsible for regulating metabolic activity. In tandem, Kohsaka et al.34 found that high-fat diet (HFD) was sufficient to change the period of activity and alter the expression/cycling of clock-controlled genes involved in fuel utilization. Human studies had also previously shown that eating at night, termed nocturnal eating syndrome, was positively associated with body mass index and binge eating35 With the knowledge that the clock could be disrupted by HFD, and that loss of core clock genes led to changes in fuel utilization many hypothesized that the timing of food intake may play a role in broad metabolic dysfunction and/or weight gain.

Several papers, in quick succession, supporting the hypothesis that limiting the consumption of HFD to the active (or awake) period of the mouse was sufficient to protect them from metabolic dysfunction.36–38 The methods in these early papers were somewhat varied with Bray et al.36 finding that high-fat meals at the beginning of the mouse’s active period were more beneficial than high-fat meals at the end of the active period (e.g. right before bed). Both Hatori et al.38 and Arble et al.37 broadened this time period of high-fat feeding to the entire 12 h active phase with similar results. Importantly, all three of these publications reported no change in caloric intake when compared with ad libitum fed control groups. This led to the adoption of the term, time-restricted feeding (TRF), which was meant to encompass any regimen which restricted the timing of food availability but did not aim to change total calories eaten.

The effects of diet-induced obesity on heart structure and function also appear to be attenuated by TRF in mice. Mia et al.39 reported that myocardial collagen deposition due to 17 weeks of HFD in C57BL6 mice is reduced during an additional 2 weeks of food availability only during the active period. This was also associated with an improvement in metabolic flexibility as determined by stronger diurnal patterns in whole-body respiration.

Other terminology has since been used to describe specific eating times or food availability including time-restricted eating (TRE) and Ramadan type fasting (RTF). There are considerable variations in the timing of food availability that have been used in these studies, but the terms are often used interchangeably. We highlight the differential responses by type of fasting protocol (e.g. TRE vs. TRF vs. RTF), time of food availability in TRF/TRE (e.g. 9 vs. 12 vs. 15 h), food availability only during the inactive phase (iTRF) and/or only available in the beginning (early TRF) or end (late TRF) of the active phase. Studies are mixed in their application of these protocols leading to challenges in the interpretation of results obtained. Animal studies generally report no change in food intake when using TRF/TRE or RTF regimens as the animals will have food access for 9–12 h allowing them time to consume ample food to match the caloric intake of ad-lib fed controls. However, human studies are more variable and unless tightly controlled can lead to caloric restriction. This makes untangling the effects of TRF/TRE/RTF from caloric restriction more difficult in humans. To us, this wealth of vocabulary and interpretation of results also underscores the importance of accurate reporting of both timing of feeding, timing of fasting, and type of diet used in any study which modifies food intake patterns (Figure 3).

Many studies have since profiled the effects of ‘TRF’ but under these host of different names and with strikingly different methodologies. As an example, Farooq and et al.40 published a study of rats exposed to RTF and its effects on intestinal and liver adaptation. In this study, rats had food access restricted to the lights off (active period) or lights on (inactive period) and found that regardless of active or inactive period fasting, all rats had a lowering of serum glucose and cholesterol following 30 days of RTF in agreement with studies on humans following RTF.40 The adaptations seen were not isolated to whole-body metabolic changes as studies found that rat kidneys adapt to RTF through enhancement of phosphate metabolism to maintain the homeostatic balance needed for sustained metabolic activity in the rat kidney cortex.41 However, these results are difficult to interpret in the context of other studies as the rats were fasted of food and water, which differs significantly from most TRF/TRE/RTF strategies that offer water ad libitum.

Population considerations

The beneficial effects of TRF/TRE/RTF strategies must be considered in the framework of the question being asked, in animal studies, and the population being considered in human studies. While these eating patterns have more broad palatability than traditional caloric restriction, they come with limitations in terms of implementation and adherence. Certain populations will not benefit from TRF/TRE/RTF due to medical conditions, lifestyle factors, and/or willingness. For example, Type 1 diabetics have traditionally been considered an ‘at risk’ population in terms of implementation of TRF/TRE. However, more recent evidence has suggested there is clinical potential for implementing some type of fasting in this population (reviewed in Varady et al.42). Mechanistic considerations

The clock’s role in mediating the effects of TRF is consistently described as aligning the bodies’ central and peripheral clocks by eating at a time of day when the peripheral clocks are primed by the central clock to utilize the energy efficiently. In other words, diurnal species (e.g. humans) are wired to be awake and active during the daylight hours, and so eating to align with this activity will ensure efficient utilization of nutrients. However, this is mediated by a host of other autonomic and neurohumoral regulators. For example, cortisol is elevated in the early morning, and this rhythm is not disrupted during short-term (5 day) 8 h (feeding; 16 h fasting) TRF.81 However, voluntary TRF, through the skipping of breakfast, was found to decrease morning cortisol and increase midday cortisol levels in women.82 Cortisol is also sensitive to stress, and its interaction with TRF was studied in professional firefighters. It was found that after an 8 week 10 h (feeding; 14 h fasting) TRF protocol, the cortisol spike in response to a stressful situation was significantly attenuated in those in the TRF group.48

In addition to neurohumoral regulation, there are also rhythms in endothelial and vascular function with both vascular smooth muscle cells and endothelial cells having peripheral molecular clocks that operate in coordination responding to paracrine signals such as nitric oxide, which itself is regulated by and regulates the clock.83,84 However, we know these rhythms can operate independently and are partially dependent on the milieu to which they are subjected. Metabolism is also subjected to circadian variation, and multiple reviews have profiled the effects of time-of-day on myocardial metabolism85 and mitochondrial function.86

Cardiovascular circadian physiology

Functional rhythms of the heart

Cardiac functional parameters are subject to significant variation depending on time of day. These include measures of both electrophysiology and contractility, which vary depending on the time of day at which they are measured. For example, the ventricular effective refractory period is shortest upon waking and longest during sleep,87 while the time between consecutive R waves on an electrocardiogram (RR interval; a measure of ventricular rate) is highest at night.88 These parameters are both related to heart rate (HR) with RR being used to calculate HR variability (HRV), which can become dysregulated in disease states.89 These changes had been attributed to changes in both sympathetic and autonomic tone. However, when individuals are on a forced desynchrony protocol, that is, environmental timing cues are set at a 20 h day, the 24 h rhythms in cardiovascular functional rhythms persist consistent with an intrinsic control mechanism.90 These rhythms in cardiovascular function are also present in rodent and dog models allowing for investigations into intrinsic control of these rhythms.91,92 For a comprehensive review, see Zhang et al.93 To support these changes in function, the heart relies on multiple metabolic substrates to maintain homeostasis.

The myocardium has a marked diurnal variation in its use of metabolic substrates. In the case of glucose utilization this is likely secondary to the behavioural sleep/wake and fasting/feeding cycles, which increase demand due to increased workload and fluctuations in hormones (e.g. insulin). These changes stimulate glucose oxidative and non-oxidative metabolism, which is well established to be higher in the active period vs. the sleep period.94–97 However, the astute reader will know that the (healthy) heart relies on fatty acid β-oxidation for ∼70% of the myocardial adenosine triphosphate (ATP) required to maintain function. Similarly to glucose, the heart displays marked diurnal variation in lipid metabolism with triglyceride synthesis being highest at the end of the active phase (i.e. right before sleep)98 and fatty acid β-oxidation peaking at the end of the sleep phase.99 These rhythms allow the heart to adapt to changes in both metabolic substrate availability and contractile demand over the course of the day (Figure 4).

With this knowledge groups began to research if the heart could maintain this metabolic flexibility in pathophysiologic conditions. For example, contractile performance, carbohydrate oxidation, and oxygen consumption are normally greatest in the middle of the active period in rats while pressure overload hypertrophy abolishes these rhythms.94 Loss of rhythms in fuel utilization and signalling are also apparent in branched chain amino acids, which when given at the end of the mouse active period induce greater hypertrophy following transverse aortic constriction.100 This loss of normal variation in substrate availability and utilization in pathophysiologic conditions suggests a timed dietary intervention could be beneficial to realign substrate availability with demand. Discussed in the next sections will be the role of TRF in treatment of heart failure (HF), cardiac arrhythmias, and CVD.

Heart failure

HF encompasses a wide variety of aetiologies that is often chronic and will progress due to structural or functional changes in the heart muscle.101 These changes are accompanied by derangements in cardiac metabolism due to a deficit of ATP production in the failing heart, which loses metabolic flexibility to change between readily available fuel sources.102,103 Complete changes in substrate availability and utilization during HF are nicely reviewed in Lopaschuk et al.104 This loss of metabolic flexibility is also accompanied by a loss of rhythmicity in the production and use of fuels in the failing heart.94

In Drosophila, TRF was found to improve cardiac functional parameters related to both high-fat and age-related cardiac decline.105 This study also demonstrated that TRF could not only prevent decline in cardiac function but a short period, later in life, was also able to improve cardiac performance.105 Interestingly, these changes were not associated with significant changes in cardiac gene expression between the TRF and ad libitum groups. However, TRF was associated with improved synchrony, amplitude, and alignment of peak phase in multiple coordinated transcripts. Finally, this change was not present in circadian-disrupted models, highlighting the importance of the molecular circadian clock in controlling the response of these flies to diurnally imposed feeding paradigms. Given these improvements seen during TRF, others sought to understand the underlying mechanisms. Ulgherait et al.106 developed an intermittent TRF (inTRF) protocol in Drosophila that significantly improved both health and lifespan with the novel finding that regulation of autophagy was mediating many of the beneficial effects of inTRF. This led to the exploration of autophagic pathways in models of IF after cardiac injury. In mice, preconditioning with IF was able to protect against ischaemia–reperfusion-related damage to the myocardium resulting in smaller infarct sizes and preservation of cardiac function.107 This was largely mediated by an induction of autophagy in the cardiomyocytes and was not present in animals with a Lamp2, a key autophagic component, mutation.107 In rats, IF similarly reduced infarct size following myocardial infarction (MI) (permanent coronary artery ligation) with attenuation of both left ventricular (LV) remodelling and improved LV function.108

While IF and TRF likely involve many similar pathways, studies focused on TRF have also identified other important pathways and mechanisms by which TRF confers protection. In mice, TRF was able to protect against HF development by upregulating circulating amino acids, increasing cardiac amino acid utilization, and causing activation of the cardiac urea cycle.109 The authors also found that cardiac amino acid utilization and urea cycle activity were down-regulated in humans with reduced cardiac function, which suggests that TRF in humans may be a promising strategy for the treatment of HF.109 In rats, TRF was able to ameliorate cardiac dysfunction (similar to HF) induced by hind-limb unloading; a model of simulated microgravity.110 Hind-limb unloading also lowered both pyruvate dehydrogenase activity and glucose utilization in the heart, which were both conserved by TRF.110 This conservation of glucose utilization and pyruvate dehydrogenase activity led to an increase in contractile function in cardiomyocytes and was mediated by enhanced fibroblast growth factor-21 signalling.110

Human studies looking at the effects of IF/TRF in HF patients have more mixed conclusions as this patient population has not been studied in as much as depth as those with the broader classification of CVD. In one study of acute HF during the month of Ramadan in seven Middle Eastern countries, the authors found that patients had an improved volume status, defined as less prevalent volume overload symptoms (e.g. peripheral oedema, ascites, and pleural effusion).111 The authors also reported improvements in atrial arrhythmias as well as total cholesterol levels during this month. However, the study did not directly assess patient adherence to RTF, so the results need further validation. A second study enrolled only patients who self-reported adherence to RTF and who had pre-existing HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF).112 The primary endpoint was assessment of stability to determine if RTF was safe to undertake in this patient population. Authors concluded that RTF along with medication adherence was safe in patients with HFrEF and found that 92% of the patients either stayed stable or improved their New York Heart Association functional classification status.112

In contrast to studies demonstrating benefits of IF/TRF protocols, observational data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey revealed that patients with HF in Western countries had benefits in cardiovascular mortality rates when their eating window was extended to >11 h.113 Further analysis showed that an eating duration <8 h was associated with higher cardiovascular mortality, but not with all-cause and cancer mortality. These findings clearly demonstrate the limitations of data based on recall and the need for more carefully controlled studies that focus on more specific patient populations.

Cardiac arrhythmias

Sudden cardiac death in patients with HF and diabetes is often associated with fatal arrhythmias. Due to the heart’s intrinsic daily oscillations in several cardiovascular electrical parameters such as HR,114 HRV,115 and Q-T interval duration,116 it is not surprising that sudden cardiac death also follows a circadian pattern with increased incidence in the early morning.117–121 This increase in arrhythmogenic events is partially due to variations in autonomic function, which largely controls HR through its two branches, the sympathetic and parasympathetic. For example, the HR slows at night leading to a lengthening of the HR corrected QT (QTc) interval, which is a broad measure of time to ventricular repolarization.122 This diurnal variation in ventricular repolarization may partially explain the diurnal variation seen in some ventricular arrhythmias.122 There is also a well-known circadian variation in most ion channels important for establishing the ventricular refractory period, nicely summarized in Bernardi et al.123 (Figure 5).

Arrhythmia incidence shows a rapid rise in the morning and sustained incidence throughout the day. Intrinsic oscillations in several cardiac electrical parameters may partially account for this trend. Figure adapted from Mallavarapu et al.2.

The central transcription factor Bmal1 is known to contribute to the rhythmicity in expression of the primary voltage-gated sodium channel in cardiomyocytes, Nav1.5 (Scn5a).92 Mice with inducible cardiomyocyte-specific loss of Bmal1 (iCSBmal1) were able to slow HR and increase arrhythmias, primarily through decreases in expression of Nav1.5.92 In addition, mice with iCSBmal1 had changes in circadian potassium channel expression, specifically the alpha unit of the Kv11.1 channel (Kcnh2).124 Kv11.1 is important in the repolarization phase of the cardiac cycle by rapidly opening this channel allowing potassium to flow out of the cardiomyocyte. Loss of this rhythmicity in iCSBmal1 mice was able to prolong the Q-T interval primarily during the resting phase.124 Loss of these protective mechanisms in iCSBmal1 mice suggests that environmental factors which alter the cardiomyocyte circadian clock may impact the susceptibility to arrhythmias. The normal circadian variation in electrical function is also often disturbed in disease states due to changes in circulating factors including hormones and various metabolites. This is due to the circadian control of metabolism, which temporally partitions biochemical processes in the most energy-efficient fashion.125 However, this temporal control is often disrupted and can lead to changes in the availability of substrate or misalignment of the central and peripheral clocks.125 TRF has been shown to attenuate these changes and holds promise as a therapeutic intervention in cardiac arrhythmias.

Research on TRF and IF in pre-clinical models as interventions for arrhythmias is scarce, but the few studies available show contrasting results. In Drosophila, TRF was shown to slow age-related changes in cardiac function parameters in flies on both normal and higher fat diets.105 This included significant decreases in the arrhythmia index in flies on TRF at 5 weeks. Perhaps more exciting was the decreased arrhythmia index in flies which were switched to TRF later in life after an initial period of ad libitum feeding. This was later attributed to an induction of oscillations in genes related to triglyceride metabolism.126 Specifically, diacylglycerol O-acetyltransferase 2 (Dgat2) was diurnally expressed during TRF with a peak during the feeding period.126 Overexpression of Dgat2 was also able to preserve cardiac performance in aged flies under constant light (circadian disruption) conditions.126 A study in obese mice (8 weeks of HFD) found that there was an increased risk for atrial fibrillation and atrial remodelling when the diet was offered ad libitum.127 When subjected to IF [defined in this study as alternate-day fasting (ADF) for 5 weeks], these mice had significant reductions in atrial fibrillation inducibility and a reversal of atrial remodelling including hypertrophy and fibrosis.127 This effect appears mediated by an IF-induced upregulation of sirtuin 3 (SIRT-3) as pharmacological blockade or genetic deletion attenuated the phenotype.127 In male Sprague-Dawley rats on a regular chow diet (NIH-07), IF decreased sympathetic tone while increasing parasympathetic activity.128 Interestingly, even when animals were returned to a continuous ad libitum diet, autonomic tone remained changed for an additional 4 weeks.128 The study authors conclude that this IF regimen may be beneficial by decreasing sympathetic activity potentially preventing or delaying age-associated cardiac arrhythmia susceptibility. In rats, a short-term fast, defined as 3 days of water-only fasting, was able to protect against ischaemia/reperfusion associated arrhythmias.129 This protection was defined as reducing the total number of premature ventricular complexes and limiting the duration of ventricular tachycardia during the first 3 min of reperfusion.129 Interestingly, another study of ischaemia/reperfusion in rats found that restricting food to the daytime hours did not protect from post ischaemic arrhythmias.130 Importantly, these authors referred to their protocol as IF, but did not distinguish in the title or abstract that this was actually iTRF highlighting the importance of the need for clarity in reporting timing of food intake.

Food restriction to the inactive period, referred to as iTRF, is commonly used in the literature to disrupt the alignment of the central pacemaker in the SCN with the peripheral tissues, including the heart. Often this regimen is used as a tool to uncouple central cues (i.e. light) with peripheral cues (i.e. food intake) to determine the impact on circadian-controlled cardiac electrophysiological processes. The available research is focused on understanding if misalignment is sufficient to induce changes in parameters such as HR and QTc. For example, mice with long QT syndrome 3 (LQT3) knock in and heterozygous for a three amino acid deletion in Scn5a (Delta KPQ; Scn5a+/deltaKPQ)have extended QT intervals and ventricular arrhythmias at baseline.131 These animals have demonstrated a lower HR that is exaggerated by iTRF.131 Translationally, this raised the possibility that in patients with LQT3, uncoupling of the central and peripheral clocks during circadian disruption may benefit from some type of chrono-food-therapy.

Myocardial infarction

Early pre-clinical studies provided evidence that timing of food intake can impact cardiovascular risk including those associated with MIs. In diabetic mice (db/db), active phase time restricted feeding (aTRF) has been shown to reduce plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) expression in the liver, a known risk factor underlying MI.132 However, a more recent study in normal mice revealed that neither iTRF nor aTRF had any effect on the circadian rhythm of PAI-1 in plasma.133 A more recent and more compelling study came from Mistry et al.134 using a coronary ligation model in mice. They showed that aTRF can attenuate the severity of cardiac injury, and furthermore, that this is dependent upon an intact gut microbiome. Mice treated with antibiotics had a significant impact on diversity of the microbiome and resulted in an attenuated effect of aTRF on cardiac injury in the infarction model. In humans, a recent systematic review of patients undergoing alternate-day fasting, modified alternate-day fasting, periodic fasting and TRE was unable to provide any clear conclusion whether these dietary patterns produced any reduction in all-cause mortality or the incidence of MI.135 The authors expressed the opinion that quality of data to address this question was very low and that more rigorous studies are needed.

Vascular functional rhythms

Rhythms in vascular tone are present in humans with greater responses to α-sympathetic stimulation in the early morning thought to contribute to increased blood pressure in the early morning hours.136 This rhythm is coordinated by the molecular circadian clock as mice with genetic disruptions to various clock genes have vascular impairments. For example, mice lacking Per2 have endothelial dysfunction associated with a decreased relaxation response to acetylcholine, which is exacerbated during the inactive to active phase transition.137 In addition, Bmal1 knockout and Clock mutant mice have aberrant vascular remodelling and injury coupled with endothelial dysfunction.138 Bmal1 is thought to be an important mediator of thrombogenesis due to the interaction of endothelial cells with platelets as genetic deletion of Bmal1 attenuates rhythms in thrombogenesis in the femoral artery of mice.139 The alignment of these clock rhythms in the various endothelial and smooth muscle cell types and their interactions with multiple circulating factors have yet to be clearly defined but may play an important role in the development and progression of atherosclerotic disease during circadian disruption.

Early studies in the whole-body Bmal1 knockout (KO) mouse found that naive aortas had endothelial dysfunction characterized by impaired endothelial-dependent vasorelaxation to acetylcholine with no change in smooth muscle cell response to nitric oxide.138 Interestingly, this effect was also observed in Clock mutant mice but only under constant dark conditions.138 Following these studies, Hussain and colleagues140 investigated global Bmal1 KO in the apolipoprotein E (ApoE) KO and the LDL receptor (LDLR) KO strains of mice, which are both genetically more susceptible to atherosclerotic disease. They found that ApoE KO/Bmal1 KO mice had increased numbers of lesions with those lesions being enriched in necrotic cores, collagen content, and macrophage infiltration.140 They also observed that LDLR KO/Bmal1 KO mice had similar increases in lesion number with both models having enhanced atherosclerotic progression when fed a Western style diet.140 This led others to investigate mechanisms related to loss of Bmal1 in specific cell types in these models. Tian and colleagues141 showed in 2017 that loss of Bmal1 in the monocytes and macrophages led to increased atherosclerotic disease progression. This was characterized by an increase in the total number of pro-inflammatory macrophages in the lesions.141 Soehnlein and colleagues142 expanded on this by showing that recruitment of the myeloid cells to the atherosclerotic lesions was also rhythmic, secondary to the time-of-day-dependent deposition of the chemokine CCL2 (Figure 6).

At the same time as these landmark studies in genetic KO models were happening, other groups were using environmental circadian disruption to study the effects of clock disruption in these atherosclerosis-prone models. Environmental circadian disruption in ApoE KO mice, accomplished by 12 weeks of continuous light exposure, found that constant light enhanced atherosclerotic progression in a sexually dimorphic manner with male mice having increased atherosclerosis, which was not present in female mice.143 A related model of atherosclerosis known as the ApoE*3-Leiden.CETP mouse with a similar hyperlipidaemic profile was exposed to circadian disruption in the form of weekly light–dark cycle reversal (LD-DL) for a period of 15 weeks.144 These mice showed an increase in both total lesion area and number of lesions characterized as severe, compared with mice who were subject to a 6 h phase advance (i.e. shift work) protocol.144 The lesions in the LD-DL group also had increased macrophage content with circulating increases in markers of inflammation, oxidative stress, and recruitment of leukocytes. These compelling results in two separate models of circadian disruption led to further investigations of whether TRF and/or IF could prevent or delay progression of atherosclerotic vascular disease and endothelial dysfunction as discussed below.

In mice maintained on a chronic HFD, Pati et al.3 recently demonstrated that 2 weeks of restricting food to the active (dark) period significantly reduced aortic stiffness (pulse wave velocity) and arterial thickness. They further demonstrated a significant reduction in vascular collagen deposition despite animals consuming the same amount of HFD as when given food ad libitum. Although the mechanisms are not clear, these investigators did observe that endothelial-dependent relaxation was attenuated in aorta from obese mice, but only during their active period. Relaxation was restored by TRF suggesting that endothelial function may play a role in the fibrotic response to diet-induced obesity. The time-of-day specificity of endothelial dysfunction in obese mice is consistent with a role for the molecular clock in mediating metabolic dysfunction in obesity.145

Atherosclerosis

Early studies in LDLR KO mice found that fasting every other day on a normal laboratory diet for a period of 3 months aggravated the development of atherosclerosis with increased spontaneous lesion area and increased levels of both plasma C-reactive protein and tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α.146 However, follow-up studies found that 3 days of ad libitum HFD with one day fasting in the LDLR KO mouse was able to substantially reduce total lesions and lesion severity.147 This discrepancy in results may be due to the observation that animals in the latter study were on a HFD and that the 1 day fast significantly decreased total food intake over the course of both 7 and 14 weeks.147 Soehnlein and colleagues148 found that, in agreement with early studies, TRF defined as 12 h access to HFD only during the active period, aggravated the development of atherosclerosis in LDLR as well as ApoE KO mice. They hypothesized this was due to an increase in leukocyte recruitment to the large vessels secondary to an entrainment response to TRF.148 It will be important to show whether this effect is observed in other models of atherosclerosis.

Studies in ApoE KO mice found that TRF, defined as 15 h of food access during the animal’s active period (ZT12 to ZT3), of a high-fat/high-cholesterol diet (HFCD) was unable to reduce the number of atherosclerotic plaques in the aorta.149 This work was supported by a separate study of IF using every other day fasting that reported ApoE KO mice were not protected from spontaneous lesion development on a HFCD but were protected on a normal laboratory chow diet.150 Interestingly, this paper also found sex-differences in the response to IF on the normal chow diet in ApoE KO mice with males having significant reductions in body weight after IF and females having no significant difference compared with ad libitum fed animals.150 These studies were further confirmed in more recent work showing that ADF (another term used for fasting every other day) of a Western diet (high fat/high cholesterol) for a period of 8 or 16 weeks aggravated atherosclerosis lesion formation.151 This was likely due to changes in cholesterol metabolism in the liver since activating transcription factor-3 was significantly suppressed by ADF.151

These previous studies stand in contrast to the most recent publication in this area. Panda and colleagues152 found that TRF, defined as 9 h of food availability during the animal’s active phase, of an isocaloric but pro-atherogenic diet, was able to significantly reduce body weight and attenuate atherosclerotic plaque development in LDLR KO but not ApoE KO mice. This protection is due in part to an increase in β-oxidation in the liver during the fasting period and a faecal loss of cholesterol and bile acids.152

Cardiometabolic disease risk

Disruption of daily patterns of sleep, food intake, and light exposure is known to increase risk on a range of disorders including cardiometabolic disease. Animal model studies where timing of light or food availability can lead to loss of blood pressure rhythmicity, cognitive function, inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation. These mechanisms have been reviewed extensively elsewhere, and so we refer the reader to other more focused articles.4,153–157 However, it is important to recognize a few important randomized controlled trials examining the impact of TRE in specific human populations. In 108 adults diagnosed with metabolic syndrome, investigators randomly assigned participants to a standard of care group that received only dietary counselling or a TRE group where restricted food intake to an 8–10 h period each day for 14 weeks.158 TRE produced a significant reduction in HbA1c compared with the control group. In overweight/obese women, Haganes and colleagues determined the effects of 7 week TRE, high-intensity interval training, or the combination of both on glycaemic control.159 They observed a lowering of HbA1c only in the group undergoing the combination of TRE and exercise although the number of subjects per arm was limited.

Endothelial dysfunction

Early studies in a Wistar albino rat model found that IF, defined as every other day fasting, for a period of 2 months, was able to improve endothelium-dependent vasorelaxation to acetylcholine.160 A study conducted in Sprague-Dawley rats induced obesity using 6 weeks of HFD with standard diet controls, and they then placed these obese rats on a TRF regimen defined as 8 h of food access from 9 am to 5 pm.161 They found that after 6 weeks of this inactive period TRF, rats lost a significant amount of body weight and had a significant improvement in endothelium-dependent relaxation.161 In agreement with these studies in rats a study in a spontaneous diabetic mouse model (Leprdb) found that ADF was able to improve endothelial function.162 They found that ADF improved endothelium-dependent vasorelaxation in the mesenteric arteries of the mouse after 12 weeks; interestingly, this was independent of a change in body weight in the Leprdb mice despite a 40% reduction in food intake.162 A paper in aged mice found similar results that 12 month-old mice subjected to TRF for an additional 12 months had an improvement in endothelium-dependent relaxation compared with the ad libitum fed aged controls.163 Finally, a recent paper in mice found that 2 weeks of TRF, defined as 12 h of food access during the animals active period, after 18 weeks of HFD was able to improve endothelial function but only when measured during the inactive period (Figure 7).3 It appears that both TRF and ADF do have the potential to improve endothelial function despite the timing and duration of these interventions being varied across studies.

Circadian physiology in the neuroendocrine system

Rhythms in autonomic physiology

In terms of autonomic regulation, the SCN receives environmental light information from the retina and generates the core circadian rhythm, coordinating both central and peripheral clocks through intricate signalling pathways involving neurons, hormones, and metabolism to synchronize physiological processes to the environment (Figure 8). This includes the activity of both arms of the autonomic nervous system, the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous system, which are critical to the regulation of the cardiovascular system.

Typical rhythms in human physiology showing approximate time of day for peak and trough values over a 24 h day. Adapted from Pati et al.3

Caloric restriction and ADF both modulate 24 h measures of heart rate variability (HRV) in rats, suggesting that food intake may decrease sympathetic activity and increase parasympathetic tone.128 Work from the laboratory of Dr Ming Gong at the University of Kentucky has shown that light period restricted feeding in C57BL/6J mice results in time-of-day-dependent changes in blood pressure (BP) that are not observed when pressures are averaged every 24 h.164 This BP change is associated with changes in autonomic nervous system activity, assessed by spectral analysis of HR and cardiac baroreflex sensitivity, and urinary norepinephrine/epinephrine excretion, suggesting that the autonomic nervous system may be a critical responder to changes in timing of food intake in mice. A recent follow-up report from the group explored if TRF could entrain BP and autonomic rhythms in an inducible Bmal1-knockout mouse model.165 During ad libitum feeding, animals without Bmal1 displayed no significant time-of-day differences in baroreflex sensitivity or spectral power parameters. TRF imposed significant rhythms in baroreflex sensitivity and spectral power parameters when animals were placed in constant darkness and in a 12:12 h LD cycle, suggesting that rhythmic feeding cues can entrain rhythms in autonomic function, even in the absence of Bmal1.

Pati et al.3 recently reported that 2 weeks of TRF after establishment of HFD-induced obesity is able to reduce markers of cardiovascular risk by improving diurnal BP and HR rhythms in mice. Spectral analysis of time-domain parameters revealed that root mean of the standard deviation of the N-N interval, a measure of vagal mediation of heart rate, was lowered during HFD and improved during the day with TRF of HFD.

Clinical studies have reported that fasting can shift the autonomic balance towards parasympathetic dominance as assessed by increased HRV. Hammoud et al.166 recently reported that HRV was increased in the afternoon in participants observing Ramadan fasting compared with a normal eating day after Ramadan ended. Conversely, a small study focused on hypertensive patients reported decreased HRV parameters during Ramadan fasting; however, it is possible that these results are broadly reflective of an increased sympathetic tone in hypertensives.167 Demirci et al.168 found that a month-long TRF protocol (15–16 h fasting) in 58 controlled hypertensive patients lowered blood pressure and increased high-frequency power and root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) compared with unrestricted feeding.

Functional rhythms in the HPA axis

The hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis dynamically regulates the production of glucocorticoids (GCs) under the control of neuronal populations in the brain or in response to real or perceived stress. This process is initiated by the release of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) from CRH neurons in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus, followed by the secretion of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) from the anterior pituitary gland, and finally the production of GCs (cortisol in humans, corticosterone in rodents) from the adrenal glands. Once produced, GCs can bind to the high-affinity mineralocorticoid receptors (MRs) or the low-affinity glucocorticoid receptors (GRs). This hierarchical system results in differential activation of the two primary GRs—the MR is activated during low circulating levels of GC, such as during basal levels induced by circadian control, whereas the GR is bound primarily during periods of high circulating GC, such as stress.169 Of course, the downstream effects of GCs are vast and influence major systems controlling immune function, energy balance, and the stress response. Interestingly, the relationship between the circadian system and the biological response to stress is bi-directional, with circadian systems mediating the stress response and the stress response disturbing circadian rhythms.170

Activation of the HPA axis has been shown to follow both circadian and ultradian oscillations, excellently reviewed here.171 Plasma ACTH and GC levels typically peak during the beginning of the active phase and decline by the beginning of the inactive phase, allowing the organism to have energy available during the appropriate time. This circadian rhythm is controlled in part by the activity of the SCN, which directly innervates the paraventricular nucleus, thus mediating the secretion of CRH. Temporal activation of the sympathetic nervous system, which innervates the adrenal gland, also contributes to the circadian rhythmicity of plasma GCs as activation of the autonomic nervous system induces GC production. It is worth noting that evidence suggests that GCs may be able to influence tissue clock gene expression, as rhythmic exogenous corticosterone induced rhythmic clock gene expression in the cerebellum of SCN-lesioned male rats.172

The HPA axis displays a basal level of activation with hourly pulsatile profiles in CRH, ACTH, and GC secretion, with circadian influence on the amplitude of these pulses.173 Simply, these hourly pulses both maintain the circadian rhythm of GCs and allow the rapid response of the system to stress, real or perceived. This ultradian (<24 h) GC rhythm has been found to be conserved in multiple species and is thought to be mediated by a complex interplay of positive feedforward and negative feedback signals from the pituitary and adrenal glands.174 Interestingly, emerging pre-clinical evidence has suggested that stress responsivity changes based on the temporal alignment of stressors with the ultradian rhythm of GC—that a larger downstream response occurs during the rising section of the ultradian pulse compared with the falling section of the pulse.174

Temporal changes in cortisol have been associated with increased risk of CVD in multiple patient groups. In public school fourth and fifth grade children in Maine, lower morning cortisol was associated with poor cardiovascular health parameters, such as poor lipid profiles and increased blood pressure.175 In HF patients, serum cortisol was elevated with less circadian variation throughout the day compared with age- and sex-matched controls.176 Gan et al.177 found that a flatter diurnal cortisol slope, a transformation of patient-matched serum cortisol collected at 8:00 am, 4:00 pm, and midnight, was associated with increased CVD risk in patients with hypertension with the association stronger in men than in women. Together, these clinical studies suggest that diurnal variations in cortisol, driven by the HPA axis, are important for cardiovascular health and prognosis. It is important to note that the contribution of stress-induced increases in GCs is nearly impossible to separate from pathophysiological effects in patient studies.

Loss of Bmal1 in the mouse adrenal zona glomerulosa altered BP rhythms and temporal feeding behaviours when animals were placed in metabolic cages, a mild stressor. Costello et al.178 found that lack of the adrenal circadian clock in male mice resulted in elevated serum corticosterone and a decrease in diurnal variation during mild stress induced by housing in metabolic cages. A follow-up paper by the group found that loss of the adrenal clock revealed critical differences in sex- and time-of-day-dependent handling of sodium and aldosterone homeostasis.179 These pre-clinical studies support that the endogenous adrenal molecular clock is likely involved in the regulation of serum corticosterone and that serum GCs can feedback on crucial cardiovascular and hypothalamic regions of the brain to alter behaviour and end organ function. Interestingly, this feedback loop may be different in males and females and warrants further examination.

Neuroendocrine disorders

Garcia-Luna et al.180 reported that limiting access to a highly palatable diet for 8 h during the active phase reduced body weight, increased beige adipocytes, and attenuated stress induced decreases in metabolic markers in rats. TRF also altered the gene expression of thyrotropin-releasing hormone, critical for adipose tissue energy storage, and neuropeptide Y, an orexigenic peptide, in the hypothalamus of stressed rats, suggesting that restricting the timing of food intake to the active phase allowed these feeding and metabolic systems to realign and suppress the stress-induced hyperphagic response. Serum corticosterone was not significantly different in IF-fed rats; however, this was perhaps due to the inability of a single collection timepoint to capture the variability in corticosterone in these complex experimental conditions. It is also worth noting that animals on this TRF protocol lost body weight, so it is difficult to determine if these effects are due to the timing of food intake or due to weight loss.

Wang et al.181 reported that daytime-restricted feeding of rats altered the temporal gene expression of molecular clock components and neuropeptides crucial to energy homeostasis in the arcuate nucleus and perifornical area of the hypothalamus. These feeding-induced changes are in contrast to the SCN wherein studies have shown that feeding does not alter clock gene expression, perhaps due to an inherent insensitivity of the SCN molecular clock to respond to food entrainment cues.182 Interestingly, these data suggest that different regions of the brain respond to zeitgebers differently, i.e. when the arcuate nucleus and perifornical area receive contrasting signals from the periphery and the SCN, resulting in changes in temporal gene expression.

Conclusions and future perspectives

The majority of evidence points to a significant health benefit of food timing as a possible lifestyle intervention in improving outcomes and overall health. The majority of controlled clinical trials shows that TRF protocols have beneficial effects and key cardiovascular end points related to metabolism and cardiovascular function. That said, not all approaches have been positive, and so future studies are needed to assess its safety and effectiveness in specific populations, such as shift workers, who are at higher risk for CVD.

Understanding the mechanisms behind the benefits of food restriction protocols is a key area for future research. While much of the work has focused on cellular and molecular systems impacted in a variety of models, it is important to tie these mechanisms to clinically relevant physiological end points. While this review has focused largely on disease-specific mechanisms, we can summarize what we know from a mechanistic level that cuts across issues related to myocardium, endothelial biology, and autonomic inputs (Figure 9). In general, our bodies’ rhythms are synchronized by key environmental inputs such as light, meal timing, and sleep/wake patterns. The idea behind TRF is that it maintains an alignment of key functions including metabolism and respiration to maintain optimal work/rest rhythms for the bodies’ organ systems. When these inputs are disrupted, functional rhythms in a wide range of systems become misaligned that contribute to mis-timed patterns of neuroendocrine and metabolic factors.

Key questions remain such as whether TRF works by aligning peripheral molecular clocks, improving metabolic efficiency, or strengthening circadian rhythms in neuroendocrine and organ function. Is it the regularity of meal timing or the prolonged fasting period that leads to positive outcomes? Another important factor is the degree to which diet quality relates to TRF. Addressing these questions will need to be accompanied by improved research practices including the consideration of time of day and timing of food availability in study design and interpretation.
