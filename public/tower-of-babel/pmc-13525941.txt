Suspected shrinking lung syndrome: a rare pulmonary manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus—a case-based review of an underrecognized condition
Authors: Maciej Maj, Maria Oknińska, Joanna Robaczyńska, Martyna Dziewit, Marzena Olesińska, Maria Maślińska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractShrinking lung syndrome (SLS) is a rare pulmonary manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). It is characterized by unexplained dyspnea, restrictive ventilatory defect, and diaphragmatic elevation without interstitial lung disease (ILD). A 32-year-old man with SLE presented with progressive dyspnea initially attributed to pleural and infectious processes. As systemic features evolved, SLE was confirmed. Pulmonary function tests (PFTs) showed severe restriction (forced vital capacity—FVC—28%, total lung capacity—TLC—37%), with elevated hemidiaphragm and no alternative pulmonary pathology, supporting the diagnosis of SLS. Treatment with highdose glucocorticoids, rituximab, and mycophenolate mofetil resulted in marked clinical improvement, substantial recovery of lung function (FVC 65%), and successful glucocorticoid withdrawal. A structured literature review (January 2021–March 2026) identified 19 studies reporting 37 SLS cases. Dyspnea was the predominant manifestation, frequently accompanied by pleuritic chest pain and dry cough. Restrictive ventilatory defects were consistently reported, with significantly reduced FVC, TLC, and transfer factor of the lung for carbon monoxide values. Imaging most commonly demonstrated elevated hemidiaphragm(s) and reduced lung volumes without ILD. Treatment predominantly involved glucocorticoids, often combined with steroid-sparing agents. Rituximab was frequently used as a steroid-sparing agent in severe/refractory cases and was generally associated with clinical and functional improvement. Based on the presented case and cases identified in literature, SLS remains a diagnostic challenge and should be considered in patients with SLE presenting with unexplained dyspnea and restrictive physiology. Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a systemic autoimmune disease with a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, often mimicking other conditions and posing a diagnostic challenge [1]. Pleural and pulmonary involvement is common in SLE, affecting approximately 50% of patients during the disease course, although reported prevalence ranges from 20% to 90% depending on the study population [2].

A rare pulmonary manifestation of SLE is shrinking lung syndrome (SLS), estimated to occur in approximately 0.5–1.53% of SLE patients. Importantly, SLS has also been described, although less frequently, in other autoimmune disorders, including systemic sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, or Sjögren’s disease [3]. Clinically, SLS is characterized by progressive dyspnoea, pleuritic chest pain, diaphragmatic dome elevation on imaging (without interstitial parenchymal abnormalities—interstitial lung disease, ILD), and a restrictive ventilatory impairment on pulmonary function tests (PFTs). Pathophysiology remains incompletely understood and is likely multifactorial, with proposed mechanisms including diaphragmatic myopathy, inflammatory myositis with reduced muscle thickness, chronic pleural inflammation and adhesions, surfactant alterations, and phrenic neuropathy [3, 4].

Management strategies are not standardized but most often include systemic glucocorticoids (GCs) as first-line treatment and adjunctive immunosuppressant therapy (e.g. cyclophosphamide—CYC, azathioprine—AZA, mycophenolate mofetil—MMF) in refractory cases. More recently, steroid-sparing strategies involving biological treatment, particularly B-cell-depleting agents, have shown favorable outcomes in selected patients [5].

Because of its rarity, nonspecific clinical presentation, diagnosis by exclusion, and lack of standardized treatment guidelines, SLS remains a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge [6]. Therefore, each newly reported case contributes to a better understanding of its pathogenesis, clinical course, and management.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old man was admitted to the Department of Connective Tissue Diseases at the National Institute of Geriatrics, Rheumatology and Rehabilitation in October 2024 due to a suspected diagnosis of SLE.

The patient’s initial symptoms dated back to May 2022, when he was hospitalized at the Pulmonary and Tuberculosis Department of the Mazovian Center for the Treatment of Pulmonary Diseases and Tuberculosis. At that time, he presented with left-sided pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea, and fever, accompanied by rapidly increasing left pleural effusion, pneumonia, and an abscess within the left pleural cavity. Video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) revealed a small amount of cloudy, purulent fluid in the left pleural cavity. However, microbiological analysis of pleural fluid was negative for bacteria and acid-fast bacilli, and only coronavirus genetic material was detected in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. He remained under outpatient follow-up in good general condition, with no recurrence of symptoms until June 2024.

In June 2024, the patient developed an inflammatory oligoarthritis involving the left ankle and right knee, accompanied by myalgia and progressively worsening exertional dyspnea over several preceding months. Laboratory investigations revealed markedly elevated inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein (CRP) 85 mg/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 50 mm/h), borderline proteinuria, erythrocyturia and a high-titer antinuclear antibodies (ANA) of 1:1280 (homogeneous pattern). ENA (Extractable Nuclear Antigen) panel and anti-dsDNA antibodies could not be performed at that time due to limited access to specialized diagnostic services in the patient’s local area.

Reactive arthritis was initially suspected and treated empirically with a seven-day course of amoxicillin/clavulanic acid combined with systemic glucocorticoid therapy (prednisone 0.5 mg/kg/day), resulting in complete resolution of clinical symptoms within 24 h.

In September 2024, in an external specialist center, persistent proteinuria in a 24-hour urine collection (0.51 g/24 h) prompt initiation of hydroxychloroquine (HCQ). Further immunological assessment revealed the presence of anti-nucleosome, anti-SSB, anti-histone and anti-Scl-70 antibodies, leading to a diagnosis of SLE. The anti-Scl-70 antibodies were considered clinically insignificant, as the patient exhibited no features suggestive of systemic sclerosis, including skin sclerosis, Raynaud’s phenomenon, or capillaroscopic abnormalities characteristic of the scleroderma spectrum. The patient was referred to our center for further management.

During the first hospitalization in October 2024, the patient reported migratory myalgia without muscle weakness, progressively worsening exertional dyspnea since June 2024, impaired concentration, unintentional weight loss of 10 kg over a four-month period, and long-standing cutaneous lesions on the lower extremities, present for approximately 10 years, with seasonal exacerbations during summer months. Laboratory tests confirmed ANA 1:1280 with homogeneous pattern, strongly positive anti-nucleosome, anti-SSB and positive anti-dsDNA antibodies. Lupus anticoagulant was also detected, whereas other antiphospholipid antibodies were negative. Components C3 and C4 of complement levels were within the normal range. Creatine kinase activity was decreased. Complete blood count demonstrated mild leukocytosis (11.58 × 10³/µL) with neutrophilia (8.89 × 10³/µL, 76.8%). Hemoglobin concentration, platelet count, and renal function were within the reference ranges, with a serum creatinine level of 0.96 mg/dL and an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) above 60 mL/min/1.73 m². Histopathological examination of the skin lesions did not reveal changes characteristic of SLE. The patient fulfilled the 2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria for SLE [7], with a total score of 23 points: ANA entry criterion, history of arthritis (6 points), pleural effusion (5 points), presence of lupus anticoagulant (2 points), proteinuria (4 points), and anti-dsDNA antibodies (6 points).

Disease activity was assessed as high with a SELENA-SLEDAI score of 12 points (hematuria, sterile pyuria, pleuritic chest pain with pleural effusion, increased anti-dsDNA binding), SLEDAI-2k score of 12 points, Physician Global Assessment (PGA) − 2.5.

Chest radiography showed elevation of the right hemidiaphragm with inflammatory and atelectatic changes in the right middle lobe, and band-like inflammatory or post-inflammatory changes present at the base of the right lung. A CT scan demonstrated a small right pleural effusion (up to 10 mm). Right-sided empyema was initially considered in the differential diagnosis but was subsequently excluded. Pulmonary embolism was excluded. No changes in the lung parenchyma were reported. Echocardiography did not reveal pericardial effusion. PFTs demonstrated severe restriction (forced vital capacity (FVC) − 28%, total lung capacity (TLC) − 37%), although transfer factor for carbon monoxide (TLCO) was not assessed at the patient’s request.

Given progressive dyspnea, severe restriction on PFT, elevated right hemidiaphragm, no significant parenchymal abnormalities apart from mild pleural effusion (Fig. 2), SLS associated with SLE was suspected. Although the patient had a history of presumed pleural empyema, pleural abscess, and VATS in 2022, these were considered less likely to explain the current restrictive pattern. The diagnosis of pleural empyema itself appears questionable because microbiological cultures were sterile. Moreover, the previous pleural disease affected the left hemithorax, whereas the elevated hemidiaphragm was observed on the right, making a direct causal relationship less likely.

Additional assessment of diaphragmatic function, including diaphragmatic ultrasonography, fluoroscopy (sniff test), and maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressure (MIP/MEP) measurements, could have provided further supportive evidence but was not performed.

The patient received intravenous methylprednisolone pulses (MP) at total dose 5 g followed by prednisone orally (40 mg/day) and HCQ (400 mg/day). Due to suspected infection, ceftriaxone 1 g/day was administered for 12 days. Further pulmonological evaluation, including bronchoscopy, ruled out tuberculosis and major infections, allowing continuation of immunosuppressive therapy.

As a steroid-sparing therapy, rituximab (RTX) was introduced in March 2025 (two infusions of 1000 mg administered 14 days apart). Low-dose MMF was introduced in September 2025 at a dose of 1 g/day orally and subsequently escalated to 2 g/day, after being initially deferred due to individualized precautions applied in our clinical practice based on the available reproductive safety information at that time. Glucocorticosteroids were progressively tapered and completely discontinued in November 2025.

The patient showed clinical improvement following treatment. Pulmonary function tests showed improved parameters: FVC 56%, TLC 57%, TLCO 60% in March 2025, and FVC 65%, TLC 58%, TLCO 62% in August 2025. A marked improvement in overall well-being was both clinically observed and subjectively reported by the patient. By August 2025 the disease activity was assessed as low, with a SELENA-SLEDAI score of 0.

The patient described in this text has given permission for his case details and imaging results to be published.

Search strategy

This article was prepared in accordance with the Case-Based Review Standards (CABARET). A structured literature search was performed in March 2026. Electronic databases—PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus—were screened by two reviewers (MM and AR) independently, using the search query: (“shrinking lung syndrome” OR SLS) AND (“systemic lupus erythematosus” OR SLE OR “B-cell-depleting therapy”). Studies published between January 2021 and March 2026 were considered.

The search strategy was designed to identify as many relevant studies as possible while ensuring alignment to the research question. After duplicate removal, titles (n = 158), abstracts (n = 95), and subsequently full texts (n = 47) were screened for eligibility.

Studies were taken into account if they reported clinical features, pathophysiology, or management of SLS in adults with SLE, including case reports, case series, and observational studies, and were published in English and available in full text. Review articles, including systematic reviews, conference abstracts, non-English publications, and studies lacking sufficient clinical detail were excluded. Discussion

The case presented in this study illustrates a challenging presentation of SLE complicated by probable SLS, with a delayed recognition of both—the underlying disease and its rare pulmonary manifestations.

The patient initially presented with pleural involvement and infectious-like features, which contributed to uncertainty. SLE was finally confirmed after the development of systemic features including oligoarthritis, characteristic serological abnormalities, and proteinuria.

SLS was not initially considered as the primary cause of the patient’s severe dyspnea and restrictive pattern observed on PFTs. The exclusion of more common causes, including infection, pulmonary embolism, or ILD, was performed, consistent with the diagnosis of exclusion approach described in literature—Table 1 [6]. The combination of progressive dyspnea, a severe restrictive pattern on PFTs, elevated hemidiaphragm, and absence of significant parenchymal abnormalities supported the diagnosis of SLS. Initial attribution of symptoms to infection and reactive arthritis contributed to delayed suspicion of SLS. Due to its low prevalence, SLS is often misdiagnosed—a 2016 systematic review reported that 48% of cases experienced a delay of more than one year before an accurate diagnosis was made [24].

Across reported cases (Table 1), most common clinical features of SLS were: dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, and dry cough. Physical examination is often unremarkable, with normal auscultation in majority of patients and only mild abnormalities in a minority of cases [5, 15, 16, 20, 21]. Restrictive ventilatory defects on PFT—typically reduced FVC (among collected in Table 1 cases: mean FVC = 50.1 ± 12.9%) and TLC (among collected in Table 1 cases: mean TLC value = 59.6 ± 9.4%), with mildly/moderately reduced TLCO (among collected in Table 1 cases: mean TLCO = 52.8 ± 18.9%) were typically described.

Imaging evaluation most commonly included chest X-ray and CT scan, particularly HRCT (high-resolution computed tomography). Less frequently, diaphragmatic ultrasound or fluoroscopy were used to assess diaphragmatic function (Table 1).

Across studies in Table 1, the most consistent finding of SLS was elevation of one or both hemidiaphragms, often accompanied by reduced lung volumes. Atelectasis, when present, was typically basal or subsegmental. Pleural involvement, including mild effusions or pleural thickening, was described in a subset of patients but was not a dominant feature and was unlikely to account for the patients’ symptoms. HRCT, was performed in order to exclude ILD which would rule out the diagnosis of SLS. CT angiography, were occasionally used to eliminate the diagnosis of pulmonary embolism.

Notably, the probable diagnosis of SLS in the presented case represents an uncommon occurrence in a male patient, consistent with the known female predominance of both SLE and SLS; 33/37 of cases revied in Table 1 occurred in women [25, 26]. In addition, the degree of restrictive impairment at presentation (FVC 28%, TLC 37%) was more severe than typically reported in literature (Table 1).

Imaging findings were broadly consistent with SLS, although the presence of mild pleural effusion and atypical atelectatic changes highlights the potential for overlapping or non-classical features. Importantly, these abnormalities were insufficient to account for the severity of functional impairment, reinforcing the diagnosis.

Therapeutic strategies for SLS in reported cases predominantly rely on systemic GCs, often initiated as high-dose intravenous pulses. In one case, IVIG were primarily used [10].

However, relapse and incomplete response are common, often requiring additional immunosuppressive therapy. Conventional agents—including MMF, AZA, CYC, methotrexate (MTX), HCQ—are widely used. More recently, B-cell-targeted biologics, particularly RTX and belimumab (BEL), have been added to GC therapy as steroid-sparing strategies for long-term disease control. RTX appears to be especially effective in refractory or recurrent SLS, enabling GCs tapering and long-term disease control in patients in whom GCs alone [17] or with MMF, AZA, and HCQ [22, 23], or even BEL therapy [14], did not show satisfactory results. One case also reported successful use of tofacitinib as a steroid-sparing option for recurrent SLS [22]. Preliminary data further suggest a potential benefit of inspiratory muscle training, although its efficacy, particularly in severe restrictive disease, requires further evaluation [27].

Outcomes in SLS are generally favorable, although recovery is often incomplete. While substantial improvement in pulmonary function is common, persistent restrictive defects are frequently observed, particularly in patients with more severe baseline impairment. This observation supports the hypothesis that irreversible diaphragmatic dysfunction may develop in advanced disease. Conversely, near-complete recovery has been reported in some patients, suggesting that early recognition and timely treatment may improve outcomes.

In the present case, combined treatment with high-dose GCs, RTX, and MMF resulted in marked clinical and functional improvement, with remission of systemic disease activity and substantial recovery of lung function. Nevertheless, residual restriction persisted, consistent with patterns observed in literature. Although paternal exposure to MMF is currently considered compatible with reproductive planning, its initiation was initially deferred in this patient because of concerns regarding the limited reproductive safety data and the contraception recommendations included in the product information at that time. Given the severe restrictive impairment, RTX was selected as the initial steroid-sparing therapy.

The favorable outcome is consistent with previous reports describing improvement after intensive glucocorticoid-based immunosuppression, including MMF- and RTX-containing regimens [8, 17, 23], supporting the use of RTX as part of a steroid-sparing strategy in selected SLS patients. However, as GCs, RTX, and MMF were administered sequentially/in combination, the contribution of any individual treatment to the observed improvement cannot be determined.

Limitations

This case-based review has certain limitations. Most importantly, SLS was considered the most probable diagnosis, but could not be confirmed definitively because objective assessment of diaphragmatic function, including MIP/MEP measurements, diaphragmatic ultrasonography, and sniff test or fluoroscopic evaluation, was not performed. Previous left-sided empyema, VATS, pleural adhesions, and atelectatic changes could have contributed to the restrictive ventilatory impairment and cannot be completely excluded as alternative explanations to SLS. In addition, before hospitalization in October 2024, the patient was managed at external specialist centers, limiting access to detailed information on the earlier diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making.

Furthermore, the available evidence on SLS is limited mainly to case reports and small case series, resulting in publication bias and substantial heterogeneity. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and further high-quality studies are needed to better characterize the clinical features, management, and outcomes of SLS.

Conclusion

SLS is an underrecognized pulmonary manifestation of SLE that typically presents with unexplained dyspnea, a restrictive ventilatory defect on PFT, elevated hemidiaphragm, and minimal radiological abnormalities. Diagnosis requires a high index of suspicion and exclusion of more common causes, including infection, pulmonary embolism, and ILD, as delayed recognition may result in more severe functional impairment. Early immunosuppressive treatment is essential. Although glucocorticoids remain the cornerstone of therapy, steroid-sparing agents are often required. RTX may be an effective option for maintaining remission and facilitating glucocorticoid tapering, particularly in patients with severe restrictive ventilatory impairment. This case adds to the growing evidence that prompt diagnosis and targeted immunosuppressive therapy may improve outcomes and reduce long-term respiratory disability in SLS.
