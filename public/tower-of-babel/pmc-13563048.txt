Blood Pressure Mediates the Association Between Carotid‐Femoral Pulse Wave Velocity and Basal Ganglia Enlarged Perivascular Spaces: A Community‐Based Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Shang Li, Yuan Liu, Peipei Yang, Shuohua Chen, Ying Hui, Xinyu Zhao, Xian‐Quan Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlthough carotid‐femoral pulse wave velocity (cf‐PWV) is the non‐invasive gold standard for assessing aortic stiffness, evidence linking cf‐PWV to basal ganglia enlarged perivascular spaces (BG‐EPVS) in the general population is limited. We aimed to evaluate this association and quantify the mediating role of blood pressure (BP) in community‐based adults. This cross‐sectional study included 485 participants from the Kailuan cohort. Cf‐PWV was measured by ultrasound, and BG‐EPVS were graded on 3.0‐Tesla MRI. Logistic regression, restricted cubic spline, and mediation analyses were applied to quantify the indirect effects of systolic BP (SBP), diastolic BP (DBP), and pulse pressure (PP). BG‐EPVS were identified in 59.4% of participants. After adjustment for cardiovascular risk factors other than BP, cf‐PWV ≥ 10 m/s was independently associated with BG‐EPVS (OR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.27–3.36), with a linear dose‐response relationship (p for non‐linearity = 0.668). The association was stronger among participants aged ≤ 70 years and among males. SBP and PP mediated 43.0% and 43.6% of the total cf‐PWV effect, respectively, whereas DBP did not function as a significant mediator. Higher cf‐PWV was independently and dose‐dependently associated with BG‐EPVS, with pulsatile BP components mediating approximately 43% of this association.

Introduction

Cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD) is a chronic progressive disorder that refers to a group of conditions affecting the small blood vessels in the brain [1]. Among its typical identified cSVD subtypes including white matter hyperintensitie, lacunae of presumed vascular origi, cerebral microbleed, and enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS) [2], EPVS are drawing more attention nowadays. EPVS affect more than half of community‐dwelling adults, with burden increasing progressively with age and cumulative vascular risk [3]. We focus on the basal ganglia EPVS (BG‐EPVS), because BG‐EPVS plays a crucial role in facilitating executive function and information processing, Greater BG‐EPVS burden has been independently associated with cognitive impairment, incident dementia, and cerebrovascular events [4, 5].

Arterial stiffness, quantified by pulse wave velocity (PWV), is a key modifiable risk factor for cSVD. Though several evidence links arterial stiffness specifically to BG‐EPVS, there were two main limitations of the previous studies. Firstly, these studies were largely restricted to specific populations, including hypertensive patients [6], older adults [7], or individuals with established neurological disease [8, 9]. Secondly, Carotid‐femoral pulse wave velocity (cf‐PWV) is widely recognized as the non‐invasive gold standard for assessing arterial stiffness, primarily reflecting aortic stiffness [10]. However, due to the requirement for skilled operators, exposure of the inguinal region, and time‐consuming procedures. Only one study restricted to hypertensive patients without stroke or dementia showed that cf‐PWV is associated with BG‐EPVS [6]. No community‐based study has evaluated association between cf‐PWV and BG‐EPVS and its underlying mechanisms.

Blood pressure (BP) has been a well‐known modifiable risk factor, which is independently associated with BG‐EPVS burden [4, 11]. Additionally, arterial stiffness is also mechanistically linked to elevated BP through impaired Windkessel function and premature wave reflection [12]. Therefore, BP constitutes a biologically plausible mediator in the cf‐PWV–BG‐EPVS pathway. Importantly, BP is a readily modifiable target. Effective BP control may therefore attenuate the downstream path from arterial stiffness to BG‐EPVS, offering a clinically actionable strategy to reduce cerebral small vessel disease burden in community settings. Therefore, based on the Kailuan study, an ongoing community‐based cohort study, the present study aimed to (1) evaluate the independent association between cf‐PWV and BG‐EPVS; (2) quantify the mediating roles of BP in the cf‐PWV–BG‐EPVS pathway using mediation analysis.

Method

Study Population

This is a cross‐sectional multi‐modality medical imaging study based on an ongoing longitudinal prospective cohort study, the Kailuan study, named META‐KLS as detailed elsewhere [13, 14, 15]. Briefly, from 2006 to 2007, 101 510 participants in the Kailuan community were enrolled, and follow‐up surveys were conducted biennially. At the 2020–2022 survey, 506 participants complete multi‐modality medical imaging, including brain MR imaging scan, cf‐PWV assessment, and so on. We excluded 21 participants due to the following: (1) having a stroke at present examination (N = 3); (2) having cancer at present examination (N = 1); and (3) missing BP assessment (N = 17). Finally, 485 participants were included in the analysis.

Ethic Statement

The Medical Ethics Committee of Kailuan General Hospital has approved both the Kailuan Study and META‐KLS (IRB Number: 2008 No.1 and 2021002, respectively). These two cohort studies were registered online (ChiCTR2000029767 on chictr.org.cn and NCT05453877 on Clinicaltrials.gov, respectively). All the procedures were performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants included in the study.

Cf‐PWV and BP Assessment

Cf‐PWV was measured using the M80 ultrasound system (VINNO Technology, Suzhou, China) equipped with the integrated Automatic Measurement of Arterial Stiffness (AMAS) module and a linear array transducer (X4‐12L, 6.5–18.0 MHz) [16]. The AMAS module is a Doppler ultrasound‐based semi‐automatic methodology that has been previously validated against the conventional manual Doppler reference method, demonstrating excellent agreement (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.915, 95% CI: 0.876–0.942) and high reproducibility across observers [17]. Participants rested supine for 10 min before measurement. After ECG stabilization (≥ 30 s), the right common carotid artery was scanned longitudinally 2 cm proximal to the bifurcation, and Doppler flow velocity waveforms were acquired over 15–20 consecutive cardiac cycles with simultaneous ECG gating. An identical protocol was subsequently applied to the right common femoral artery. The Doppler sampling sites at both arteries were marked on the body surface, and the direct straight‐line distance between the two markers was measured three times with an inelastic tape measure and averaged. In accordance with the 2012 European expert consensus, the transit distance was calculated as 80% of this direct surface distance [18]. The AMAS module automatically tracked spectral envelopes from both arteries, identified the foot‐to‐foot transit time between the ECG R‐wave peak and the waveform onset points using proprietary edge‐detection algorithms, and computed cf‐PWV as the transit distance divided by the transit time. All measurements were performed by a single trained operator and verified by a second; discrepancies were adjudicated by a senior physician.

The BP, including SBP and DBP, was measured in the left upper arm using a calibrated mercury sphygmomanometer while the participant sat. At least 2 BP measurements were taken after 5 min of rest. BP was then measured again if the difference between the two measurements was more than 5 mm Hg. The final BP value was recorded as the average value of the BP measurements, and pulse pressure (PP) was calculated as SBP minus DBP.

MR Imaging and Definition of BG‐EPVS

The brain MR imaging was collected using a 3.0 Tesla scanner and an eight‐channel phased‐array coil (GE 750 W; General Electric Medical Systems, Milwaukee, WI, USA). The sequences used in the MRI examination included the following: axial T2‐weighted imaging, three‐dimensional (3D) brain volume (BRAVO) for high‐resolution T1‐weighted imaging, 3D fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery imaging, diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI), and susceptibility‐weighted angiography (SWAN). According to the Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging 2 (STRIVE‐2), EPVS was defined as there were moderate to severe (grade 2–4) EPVS in the basal ganglia [2]. All images were independently evaluated by two experienced neuroradiologists. The consistency of vision has been proved in our previous study [19].

Potential Confounders

We conducted questionnaire surveys to collect the following information, including age group (18–69 years old, or ≥ 70 years old), sex, education level (junior high school or below, senior high school, or college and above), smoking status (never smoking, past smoking, or current smoking), drinking status (never drinking, past drinking, or everyday drinking), Trained field workers measured height and weight, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Laboratory tests, including fasting blood glucose, triglycerides (TG), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐c), and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐c) were assessed in the central lab. Diabetes was defined as fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, taking oral hypoglycaemic agents or insulin, or having a self‐reported physician diagnosis. Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mm Hg or DBP ≥ 90 mm Hg, currently receiving antihypertensive treatment, or having a self‐reported physician diagnosis.

Statistical Methods

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or median (25th, 75th percentile) for skewed data. Group comparisons were performed using analysis of variance or the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as number (percentage, %), and comparisons were examined using the chi‐square test. In addition, participants were categorized into three groups according to standard clinical criteria for hypertension, based on self‐reported disease history and antihypertensive medication use from the questionnaire, in combination with BP measured at the examination: normotension (no self‐reported history of hypertension and measured BP <140/90 mm Hg), controlled hypertension (self‐reported current antihypertensive medication use with measured BP <140/90 mm Hg), and uncontrolled hypertension (self‐reported history of hypertension with or without antihypertensive medication use, and measured BP ≥ 140/90 mm Hg). Participants who did not complete the relevant questionnaire items were excluded from this analysis. Group differences in the prevalence of BG‐EPVS were examined using the chi‐square test, with the significance level for pairwise comparisons adjusted to 0.05/3 = 0.017 by the Bonferroni method.

Unconditional logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between cf‐PWV and EPVS. Firstly, based on clinical relevance, we estimated the effect of increased arterial stiffness (cf‐PWV ≥ 10 m/s) on EPVS. Three multivariate models were constructed to control for confounding: (1) multivariate model 1, adjusted for age and sex; (2) multivariate model 2, based on model 1 and additionally adjusted for drinking status, smoking status, BMI, FBG, TG, HDL‐c, and LDL‐c; and (3) multivariate model 3, based on model 2 and additionally adjusted for SBP and DBP.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were used to examine potential non‐linear associations of cf‐PWV, SBP, DBP, and PP with BG‐EPVS. Three knots were placed at the fifth, 50th, and 95th percentiles, with reference values set at 10 m/s for cf‐PWV, 140 mm Hg for SBP, 90 mm Hg for DBP, and 50 mm Hg (median) for PP. The cf‐PWV model was adjusted for the covariates in multivariable model 3. The BP component models were adjusted for covariates in multivariable model 2; SBP and DBP were not mutually adjusted to avoid collinearity.

To explore the underlying biological mechanisms, the correlations of SBP, DBP, and PP with cf‐PWV were first assessed using Spearman rank correlation analysis, given the non‐normal distribution of cf‐PWV (Shapiro‐Wilk test, p < 0.001). Then mediation analysis was performed using a causal inference framework. The total effect of cf‐PWV (exposure) on EPVS (outcome) was decomposed into two components: (1) the indirect effect, representing the effect mediated through SBP, DBP, or PP; and (2) the direct effect, representing the effect independent of these mediators. A non‐parametric bootstrapping method with 10,000 resamples was applied to estimate the total, direct, and indirect effects and their corresponding 95% CIs. Covariates included in the exposure‐mediator and exposure‐outcome models were identical to those in the fully adjusted logistic regression model (Model 2).

Analyses were implemented with SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc. Cary, NC) or R version 3.5.2 (https://www.r‐project.org/). The R package “Mediation” was used for mediation analysis. The p‐values < 0.05 were statistically significant.

Compared with participants without BG‐EPVS, those with BG‐EPVS were older, higher proportions of male, current smokers, and drinkers, had higher BMI, SBP, DBP, FBG, and TG level. But HDL‐c and LDL‐c did not differ significantly between groups (Table 1).

Association Between cf‐PWV and BG‐EPVS

A total of 159 (32.8%) participants had cf‐PWV ≥ 10 m/s and 77.4% of these people have BG‐EPVS, which was significantly higher than that in the participants with cf‐PWV < 10 m/s (50.6%). Unadjusted logistic regression also showed that cf‐PWV ≥ 10 m/s was significantly associated with high risk of BG‐EPVS (OR = 3.33, 95%CI: 2.17–5.13; p < 0.001). The association remained significant after adjustment for age and sex (OR = 2.37, 95% CI: 1.50–3.73; p = 0.0002) and after further adjustment for smoking status, drinking status, BMI, FBG, TG, HDL‐c and LDL‐c (OR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.27–3.36; p = 0.004). But, after additional adjustment for SBP and DBP, the association was attenuated toward the null and no longer reached statistical significance (OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 0.85–2.43; p = 0.180), suggesting a potential mediating role of BP in this association (Table 2).

Subgroup and Interaction Analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine the association between cf‐PWV ≥ 10 m/s and BG‐EPVS across strata defined by sex, age group, and hypertension status. No statistically significant interactions were detected for sex (p for interaction = 0.497), age group (p for interaction = 0.318), or hypertension status (p for interaction = 0.065). (Table 2)

In the sex‐stratified analysis, the crude association was significant in both males (OR = 2.40, 95% CI: 1.27–4.54; p = 0.007) and females (OR = 3.26, 95% CI: 1.77–6.02; p < 0.001). After multivariate adjustment (Model 2), the association remained significant in males (OR = 2.35, 95% CI: 1.16–4.78; p = 0.018) but became non‐significant in females (OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 0.83–3.38; p = 0.148). After further adjustment for SBP and DBP (Model 3), the association was no longer statistically significant in either group (males: OR = 2.06, 95% CI: 0.96–4.40, p = 0.064; females: OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.42–2.03, p = 0.845). (Table 2)

In the age‐stratified analysis, among participants aged ≤ 70 years, the crude association was significant (OR = 3.04, 95% CI: 1.92–4.83; p < 0.001) and remained significant in Model 2 (OR = 2.12, 95% CI: 1.26–3.55; p = 0.004), but became non‐significant after additional adjustment for SBP and DBP (Model 3: OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 0.82–2.51; p = 0.203). Among participants aged > 70 years, no significant crude or age‐ and sex‐adjusted association was observed (Crude: OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 0.26–6.45, p = 0.746; Model 1: OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 0.26–6.65, p = 0.752). Estimates from Models 2 and 3 in this subgroup were highly unstable due to the small sample size (n = 59) and are therefore not reported. (Table 2)

In the hypertension‐stratified analysis, among participants without hypertension, a significant crude association was observed (OR = 3.72, 95% CI: 1.74–7.93; p < 0.001), which remained significant in Model 1 (OR = 2.65, 95% CI: 1.20–5.88; p = 0.016) but was attenuated to non‐significance in Models 2 and 3 (Model 3: OR = 1.95, 95% CI: 0.81–4.69; p = 0.135). Among participants with hypertension, no significant association was observed across all models. (Table 2)

Mediation Analyses of cf‐PWV, BP and BG‐EPVS

Cf‐PWV was positively correlated with SBP (Spearman ρ = 0.499), DBP (ρ = 0.299), and PP (ρ = 0.425) (all p < 0.001; Figure 3). Mediation analyses showed that SBP mediated 43.0% of the total effect (indirect effect β = 0.065, 95% CI: 0.029 to 0.101; p = 0.002), and the direct effect of cf‐PWV after accounting for SBP was no longer statistically significant (β = 0.083, 95% CI: −0.021 to 0.184; p = 0.102). Similarly, PP mediated 43.6% of the total effect (indirect effect β = 0.063, 95% CI: 0.029 to 0.102; p < 0.001), with the direct effect also becoming non‐significant (β = 0.082, 95% CI: −0.014 to 0.185; p = 0.110). In contrast, although DBP showed a significant total effect (β = 0.150, 95% CI: 0.046 to 0.245; p = 0.006) and a significant direct effect (β = 0.141, 95% CI: 0.039 to 0.242; p = 0.008), its indirect effect was not statistically significant (β = 0.009, 95% CI: −0.010 to 0.028; p = 0.328), indicating that DBP did not function as a significant mediator. Discussion

In this community‐based cross‐sectional study, we identified that cf‐PWV was independently associated with BG‐EPVS, especially in the participants aged ≤ 70 years. Additionally, we quantified that approximately 43% of the association was mediated by SBP or PP, indicating Effective BP control can effectively reduce the risk of cSVD.

Our finding of a significant, dose‐response association between cf‐PWV and BG‐EPVS is consistent with the ISSYS study, which reported that each 1‐SD increase in cf‐PWV was independently associated with extensive BG‐EPVS (OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.16–1.67) in hypertensive individuals without stroke or dementia [6]. Other prior studies linking arterial stiffness to BG‐EPVS have predominantly employed brachial‐ankle PWV (baPWV), which reflects stiffness across both central and peripheral arterial segments and is therefore less specific to aortic stiffness than cf‐PWV [20]. Moreover, these studies were largely confined to specific populations, including hypertensive patients [6], older adults [7], or individuals with established neurological disease [8, 9], limiting the generalizability of their findings. Furthermore, our RCS analysis extends this observation by characterizing a monotonically linear dose‐response relationship across the full range of cf‐PWV values in a general adult population (p for linearity = 0.004; p for nonlinearity = 0.668), suggesting that the cerebrovascular risk attributable to aortic stiffness accumulates continuously rather than emerging only beyond a discrete clinical threshold.

Given that arterial stiffness and cSVD are known to differ substantially by age and sex, we performed stratified analyses to examine whether the cf‐PWV and BG‐EPVS association varied across these demographic strata. In the sex‐stratified analysis, the association between cf‐PWV ≥ 10 m/s and BG‐EPVS remained significant in males (Model 2: OR = 2.35, 95% CI: 1.16–4.78) but was attenuated to non‐significance in females (OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 0.83–3.38), although the formal interaction test was not statistically significant (p for interaction = 0.497). This sex‐differential pattern may reflect two underlying mechanisms. First, estrogen exposure during the premenopausal and early postmenopausal periods exerts vasoprotective effects through enhanced endothelial nitric oxide bioavailability, attenuation of oxidative stress, and inhibition of arterial wall remodeling, which may partially buffer the deleterious haemodynamic consequences of arterial stiffening on the cerebral microvasculature in women [21]. Second, sex‐specific clustering of vascular risk factors, such as the higher prevalence of smoking and alcohol consumption observed among men, may further amplify cf‐PWV‐related cerebral microvascular injury in males.

In the age‐stratified analysis, the cf‐PWV and BG‐EPVS association was significant among participants aged ≤ 70 years (Model 2: OR = 2.12, 95% CI: 1.26–3.55) but not among those aged > 70 years, which contrasts with several prior studies conducted in older populations. In a Japanese cohort of older adults (mean age 73 years) with subjective memory complaints or mild cognitive impairment, higher ba‐PWV was independently associated with BG‐EPVS severity [8]. Similarly, in a U.S. cohort of cognitively heterogeneous older adults (≥ 60 years), aortic PWV measured by cardiac MRI was associated with increased BG‐EPVS volume and number [5]. Several factors may explain this apparent discrepancy. First, the >70‐year subgroup in our study was relatively small (n = 59), substantially limiting statistical power to detect a true association and producing unstable effect estimates. Second, in advanced age the high background prevalence of arterial stiffening and cumulative cerebrovascular injury may produce a ceiling effect, whereby older individuals have already sustained substantial BG‐EPVS burden regardless of current cf‐PWV values, attenuating the marginal contribution of arterial stiffness measurable in cross‐section [22]. Third, the prior studies reporting positive associations in older populations were conducted in cohorts with established or evolving neurocognitive impairment, whereas our >70‐year stratum comprised community‐dwelling individuals without overt neurological disease. In populations with manifest cerebral pathology, arterial stiffening may interact synergistically with concurrent neurodegenerative processes to accelerate BG‐EPVS formation, thereby amplifying tshe detectable cf‐PWV and BG‐EPVS association.

BP is a well‐established risk factor for both arterial stiffness and BG‐EPVS. In our multivariate analysis, the effect estimate for cf‐PWV was substantially attenuated and rendered non‐significant after additional adjustment for SBP and DBP (Model 3: OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 0.85–2.43). The interaction between cf‐PWV and hypertension status did not reach statistical significance, although the p value was of borderline magnitude (p for interaction = 0.065). Moreover, the hypertension‐stratified analysis revealed a stronger crude association between cf‐PWV ≥ 10 m/s and BG‐EPVS among normotensive participants (OR = 3.72, 95% CI: 1.74–7.93) than among hypertensive participants (OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 0.82–2.73), suggesting that BP may not function as an effect modifier but rather as an intermediate variable on the causal pathway from cf‐PWV to BG‐EPVS.

The mediation analysis demonstrated that SBP mediated 43.0% of the total cf‐PWV effect on BG‐EPVS, and PP mediated a comparable 43.6%. In contrast, DBP did not function as a significant mediator (indirect effect p = 0.328). As aortic stiffness increases, the Windkessel cushioning capacity of the central arteries is progressively lost, resulting in elevated SBP, widened PP, and exaggerated forward pressure wave transmission to the cerebral microcirculation [12, 22]. SBP and PP both capture the pulsatile component of arterial load, which is the principal determinant of mechanical stress on the deep perforating arteries supplying the basal ganglia [24]. Chronic exposure to this pulsatile burden promotes arteriolar wall remodeling, disrupts perivascular glymphatic drainage, and ultimately drives EPVS enlargement [24]. In contrast, the negligible mediation by DBP is consistent with its physiological determinants, as DBP is principally governed by peripheral vascular resistance rather than by large‐artery compliance, and therefore lies largely outside the cf‐PWV–BG‐EPVS causal pathway.

This study has several strengths. First, it is, to our knowledge, the first community‐based study to confirm the deleterious effect of cf‐PWV on BG‐EPVS in a general adult population, with this risk effect being particularly pronounced among participants aged ≤ 70 years. Through causal mediation analysis, we further established BP control as a critical intermediate step in this pathway, indicating that effective BP management, particularly of its pulsatile components, may substantially reduce the risk of BG‐EPVS. Second, the pulsatile components of BP (SBP and PP) each mediated approximately 43% of the cf‐PWV effect on BG‐EPVS, whereas the steady‐state component (DBP) did not function as a significant mediator, providing a quantitative basis for pulsatile BP‐targeted interventions in cerebral small vessel disease prevention. Third, the study features a community‐based sample, standardized cf‐PWV measurement, and 3.0‐Tesla MRI assessment of BG‐EPVS graded independently by two experienced neuroradiologists according to STRIVE‐2 criteria, ensuring the reliability and reproducibility of our conclusions. Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference; although mediation analysis is grounded in a causal framework, the temporal sequence of arterial stiffening, BP elevation, and BG‐EPVS development cannot be definitively established without longitudinal follow‐up. Future prospective studies tracking the progression of these parameters over time are needed to confirm the directionality of the proposed pathway. Second, cf‐PWV was measured using the AMAS Doppler ultrasound‐based semi‐automatic system rather than applanation tonometry‐based reference devices. Nevertheless, as both Doppler ultrasound and tonometry capture the same underlying haemodynamic phenomenon, the rank‐order and dose‐response relationships between cf‐PWV and cardiovascular outcomes are likely to be preserved across measurement modalities.

Conclusion

In this cross‐sectional analysis of community‐based Chinese adults, higher cf‐PWV was independently and dose‐dependently associated with greater BG‐EPVS burden, and the pulsatile components of BP (SBP and PP)—but not DBP—mediated approximately 43% of the association. The findings position arterial stiffness and pulsatile blood‐pressure regulation as potentially modifiable upstream targets for reducing BG‐EPVS burden, and warrant confirmation in longitudinal cohort studies and interventional trials.
