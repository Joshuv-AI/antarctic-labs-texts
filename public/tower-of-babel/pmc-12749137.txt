EEG classification for neurological disorders using frequency band deciles
Authors: Jonah Fernandez, Bianca Innocenti, Beatriz López
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectroencephalography (EEG) is a widely used non-invasive technique for monitoring brain activity, offering valuable insights into neurological disorders. Feature extraction methods based on signal processing approaches have been shown to be effective, but they tend to overlook the statistical properties of EEG signals. This study proposes a decile-based feature extraction method for EEG signal analysis, aimed at improving classification performance while maintaining simplicity and interpretability. The method was evaluated across multiple tasks, including the classification of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and seizure detection, using three machine learning models: Random Forest (RF), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and LightGBM. Experimental results demonstrate that the decile-based approach, particularly when paired with RF and KNN, achieves competitive classification accuracy. Furthermore, the proposed method showed robustness to reduced channel counts, suggesting its potential relevance for low-cost, wearable EEG systems. While model performance varied across datasets, particularly for LightGBM, the results indicate that decile-based features provide a useful and interpretable representation for diverse EEG classification tasks. Further studies in larger and more heterogeneous EEG populations are needed to assess generalizability and establish the potential clinical applicability of early diagnosis and real-time monitoring of neurological conditions, especially in resource-constrained or ambulatory settings.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Electroencephalography (EEG) plays a crucial role in diagnosing neurological disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and epilepsy. Its non-invasive nature and high temporal resolution make it an attractive alternative to imaging techniques like MRI and ECoG1. However, despite its advantages, EEG analysis remains challenging due to high inter-subject variability, noise sensitivity, and complex spatiotemporal dynamics2.

EEG has been analyzed with traditional processing methods, such as spectral power analysis and time-frequency representations, which provide valuable insights but often overlook finer statistical properties of EEG signals, limiting their ability to distinguish neurological conditions with subtle yet significant variations in brain activity. Fourier-based power analyses may disregard temporal information, while event-related potential (ERP) analyses often fail to capture non-phase-locked signals3.

Neurological disorders exhibit distinct signal characteristics, highlighting the need for precise feature extraction. For instance, AD is associated with increased low-frequency activity and reduced high-frequency power4, whereas PD is characterized by increased relative power across all frequency bands, especially in the right temporal region, alongside abnormal beta oscillations and altered cortical synchronization5. Similarly, epilepsy involves specific spectral dynamics and entropy patterns during absence seizures, requiring advanced quantitative EEG techniques for accurate detection6.

On the other hand, while EEG offers significant advantages, its practical application is often limited by the requirement for extensive, high-density electrode setups. These setups can be cumbersome and uncomfortable, hindering long-term monitoring and limiting real-world application. The growing interest in wearable EEG devices, which offer increased portability and user comfort, presents an opportunity to bridge this gap7. However, the reduced channel count and inherent noise in wearable systems pose additional challenges to accurate analysis8, making robust feature extraction methods even more critical.

Recent studies have highlighted the promise of statistical distribution-based features for EEG analysis. For instance, quantile- and percentile-derived descriptors have been shown to capture subtle, non-linear shifts in EEG dynamics that are often missed by mean-based or purely spectral features9,10. Such approaches provide robustness against noise and inter-subject variability, which is particularly valuable in clinical and wearable EEG applications11,12. Moreover, unlike deep learning methods that require large datasets and extensive hyperparameter tuning, quantile-based features offer a lightweight and interpretable alternative that can generalize across different recording setups13. For example, Dong et al. proposed a normative model-based framework using quantile rank features for large-scale EEG analysis, demonstrating improved prediction accuracy, stability, and interpretability, highlighting the potential of quantile-based representations in EEG research14.

Building on this evidence, we propose a frequency band decile-based feature extraction method, which captures detailed statistical distributions of EEG voltage fluctuations across standard frequency bands.

While traditional spectral, time-frequency, and deep learning approaches perform well in many EEG tasks, they can be computationally intensive, sensitive to inter-subject variability, and less robust in low-channel or noisy settings. Decile-based feature extraction is particularly advantageous in wearable EEG applications, low-density recordings, or heterogeneous datasets, where a lightweight, interpretable, and robust representation is needed.

Related work

Despite decades of progress in EEG signal processing, the use of quantile-based techniques—specifically, dividing EEG signals into quantiles or quartiles to extract distributional features—remains surprisingly underexplored in the literature. Quantiles are statistical markers that divide a distribution into equal-sized, ordered subgroups. Among these, quartiles split the data into four parts (each representing 25% of the data), while deciles divide it into ten parts (each capturing 10%). Deciles, therefore, provide a more granular representation of the data distribution compared to quartiles, allowing finer detection of subtle shifts in EEG amplitude dynamics. While traditional approaches emphasize time-domain features, spectral analysis, and transformations such as Fourier or wavelet methods, relatively few studies have considered non-parametric statistical descriptors like quantiles for feature extraction.

Recent studies have applied deep learning to EEG for neurological disorder classification. For AD and mild cognitive impairment, CascadeNet combined with DWT-based feature extraction has been used for accurate classification on public datasets15. For PD, EEG microstate dynamics analyzed with deep learning have successfully differentiated motor subtypes with high AUC16. While these approaches are highly effective, they are computationally intensive and less interpretable, whereas decile-based features offer a lightweight, interpretable alternative suitable for real-time or resource-constrained settings.

The approach presented in this study introduces a lightweight, interpretable, and potentially real-time-compatible method for EEG characterization based on quantile partitioning of the signal. It is important to distinguish this from quantile graph (QG) methods, which, although also grounded in quantile concepts, take a fundamentally different route. QGs map transitions between quantile-defined states into graph structures, enabling the use of complex network metrics to study EEG dynamics17. The QG method has shown promise in differentiating between physiological and pathological brain states. For example, Campanharo et al.9 applied QGs to EEG signals, revealing that network metrics derived from QGs could effectively detect differences in brain dynamics associated with various neurological conditions. Similarly, Pineda et al.10 employed QGs for the diagnosis of AD, demonstrating the method’s capability to capture alterations in EEG patterns characteristic of the disease. While inspired by similar distributional thinking, the method proposed here retains a direct focus on the raw signal’s quantile structure rather than transforming it into a topological representation. This distinction is crucial, as it supports the development of interpretable and computationally efficient EEG features tailored for real-time or resource-constrained environments.

Building on the idea of statistical signal representation, quantitative EEG (qEEG) has gained traction for its ability to extract measurable features that correlate with brain function. Traditional approaches have primarily utilized linear methods, such as spectral power analysis, to evaluate frequency components of EEG data. However, these methods often fall short in capturing the complex, non-linear dynamics inherent in brain activity. qEEG has been particularly valuable in clinical applications, where studies have shown that automatically extracted qEEG features can outperform clinical examinations in stratifying neonatal seizure risk during therapeutic hypothermia18, underscoring its potential as a practical bedside tool. In this context, quantile-based approaches offer a complementary perspective by describing the distribution of EEG power across different frequency bands without relying on assumptions of normality. Other frequently used statistical feature extraction techniques include mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis, entropy, and higher-order moments11. These statistical metrics offer a compact and interpretable way to summarize signal properties, especially when dealing with long-term recordings or large datasets. In parallel, time-domain analysis, frequency decomposition, and non-linear dynamic methods have traditionally been used to extract meaningful patterns from EEG signals. Frequency-based methods such as the Fourier Transform (FT), Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), and Wavelet Transform (WT) have been widely adopted for their ability to capture oscillatory brain activity within distinct frequency bands19. Among these, the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) has been extensively employed in EEG classification tasks due to its multi-resolution capability and sensitivity to the non-stationary components of EEG signals20. However, DWT methods can be computationally demanding and may struggle with generalization across different datasets or hardware configurations, making them less ideal for resource-constrained or real-time environments.

As EEG applications increasingly move beyond clinical settings, wearable technology has introduced both new possibilities and new challenges by offering increased portability and user comfort. These devices facilitate long-term monitoring and real-world applications, albeit with challenges such as reduced channel count and increased susceptibility to noise. Research efforts have been directed toward optimizing feature extraction methods tailored for wearable systems. For instance, a study by Ferrari et al.12 proposed a one-class autoencoder approach to identify optimal electrode setups for wearable EEG event monitoring, aiming to balance minimal electrode usage with effective detection capabilities. Additionally, Ingolfsson et al.21 implemented seizure detection algorithms on a parallel ultra-low-power embedded platform, demonstrating the feasibility of long-term non-invasive monitoring for epilepsy using wearable EEG devices. Richer et al.22 also explored mental state recognition via wearable EEG, demonstrating the potential of consumer-grade EEG headsets in differentiating cognitive states induced by various stimuli.

Collectively, these studies underscore the ongoing advancements in EEG feature extraction techniques and the development of wearable EEG technologies, all contributing to improved diagnosis and monitoring of neurological disorders.

Methods

This study proposes a methodology for EEG-based classification of neurological disorders, including AD, FTD, and PD, as well as seizure detection, using frequency band deciles.

Machine learning models, including K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forest (RF), and LightGBM, were applied to classify the data. The methodology was evaluated on various experiments, including neurological disorder classification, seizure detection, and robustness to reduced channel count, ensuring its applicability to both high-density and wearable EEG systems.

Datasets description

In this study, four different EEG datasets were utilized to explore the classification of neurological disorders and seizure detection.

The first dataset, AD OpenNeuro23, comprises EEG recordings from 88 subjects in a resting state with closed eyes. Participants are categorized into three groups based on Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores: Alzheimer’s disease (AD, 36 subjects), frontotemporal dementia (FTD, 23 subjects), and cognitively normal (CN, 29 subjects). The EEG data was collected using 19 scalp electrodes (Fp1, Fp2, F7, F3, Fz, F4, F8, T3, C3, Cz, C4, T4, T5, P3, Pz, P4, T6, O1, and O2), sampled at 500 Hz, and underwent preprocessing that included a bandpass filter (0.5–45 Hz), referencing, and artifact removal.

The second dataset, PD Gait24, was collected from 39 subjects, including 26 patients with PD and 13 CN controls. EEG signals were recorded during cognitive and pedaling motor tasks using a 64-channel cap at a 500 Hz sampling rate. The data were referenced to an average, with artifact correction performed through Independent Component Analysis (ICA).

The third dataset, PD UNM/UI25, consists of EEG recordings from 82 subjects (41 PD patients and 41 CN controls), collected across two institutions: the University of New Mexico (UNM) and the University of Iowa (UI). EEG recordings were obtained under both eyes-open and eyes-closed conditions for the UNM cohort, while the Iowa cohort was recorded only with eyes open. The data was sampled at 500 Hz using a 64-channel Brain Vision system, and the analysis focused on the 62 channels common to both datasets after preprocessing.

For epilepsy detection, a subset of the TUH EEG Corpus26, specifically the TUH EEG Seizure Corpus (TUSZ), was used. This subset contains EEG recordings labeled with seizure events, offering annotated seizure and non-seizure segments, which are employed for classification tasks related to seizure detection.

Data preparation

For the seizure detection pipeline, additional data preparation steps were necessary to extract relevant segments from the TUH EEG Seizure Corpus. The corpus follows a hierarchical folder structure, where recordings are organized by patient ID, session, and individual EEG trials (see Fig. 2). Specifically, the data is stored in directories labeled with a unique patient identifier, followed by session-level subfolders, and finally individual trial files in .edf format.

Data preparation workflow for seizure data. Raw EEG recordings and simplified annotation files (background vs. seizure) are organized by patient, session, and trial. Relevant segments containing seizure and non-seizure activity are extracted, and a balanced subset of 33 patients is selected with uniform channel distribution for classification.

Each recording contains raw EEG signals along with corresponding annotation files, which indicate the start and end times of seizure events. There are two types of annotation files: the detailed annotation files provide event-based labels using all available seizure types, while the simplified annotation files offer binary labels, distinguishing only between background and seizure activity. For our analysis, we used the simplified annotation files, which reduce the complexity of the classification task by focusing solely on background (non-seizure) and seizure labels.

To minimize the risk of unbalancing, a subset of 33 patients was selected, focusing on cases with well-annotated seizure episodes. Only EEG trials containing seizure events were retained, and these trials were selected to ensure uniform channel distribution across the standard 10-20 electrode system. Preprocessing steps, including filtering, ICA-based artifact removal, and re-referencing, were uniformly applied to maintain consistency with the neurodegenerative disease EEG datasets. The final dataset structure facilitated precise alignment between seizure and non-seizure events, ensuring the extracted features accurately reflected seizure-related patterns.

On average, the final dataset contained approximately 149 (±103) non-seizure segments and 50 (±83) seizure segments per patient, reflecting a realistic representation of interictal and ictal activity without the need for oversampling or synthetic augmentation. The full per-patient segment counts are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Preprocessing

To ensure consistency, the entire preprocessing pipeline was uniformly applied to all datasets. Standardizing these steps was crucial, as the datasets had previously been processed with different filtering and referencing methods.

First, a bandpass filter ranging from 1 to 45 Hz was applied to isolate relevant EEG signal frequencies, as described in27. In our implementation, this was achieved using a zero-phase, finite impulse response (FIR) filter with a Hamming window design, with filter length and transition bandwidth determined adaptively. This filtering process enhances the detection of relevant neural activity while effectively reducing noise and unwanted artifacts outside this range. Following filtering, artifact removal was performed using ICA. The procedure was automated, with independent components corresponding to common artifacts (e.g., ocular, muscular, and line noise) identified using spatial topographies and frequency signatures, and then removed algorithmically28. This approach reduces subjectivity because the removal is performed algorithmically rather than relying on manual inspection, ensuring reproducibility across datasets.

Additionally, all EEG signals were re-referenced to an average reference to reduce bias associated with the choice of a single reference electrode. This adjustment provides a more balanced representation of neural activity across the scalp, enhancing comparability across different recording sessions and experimental conditions29.

Feature extraction

To characterize EEG signals for both neurodegenerative disease classification and seizure detection, we employ a decile-based decomposition approach that captures fine-grained variations in the preprocessed voltage time series (see Fig. 3).

Schematic representation of the feature extraction process. Preprocessed EEG signals were divided into task-specific analysis windows (10-second non-overlapping windows for seizure detection and full recordings for AD/PD tasks). Within each window, every channel was filtered into the five frequency bands. For every channel–band pair, nine deciles (D10–D90) of the amplitude distribution were computed, forming decile vectors. These vectors were concatenated across all channels and bands to create the final feature representation used for classification.

Each multichannel EEG recording is divided into analysis windows, with the window duration tailored to the target task. For epilepsy detection, we segment recordings into non-overlapping 10-second windows, capturing local temporal dynamics relevant to seizure events. The 10-second window size has been determined experimentally (see Supplementary Table S2). For AD and PD classification, we treat the entire EEG recording as a single window, focusing on overall distributional characteristics of brain activity. The different windowing in seizure and AD, FTD and PD reflects task-specific requirements: AD/FTD/PD classification relies on holistic, long-term patterns, whereas seizure detection requires modeling transient events. Computational complexity

The proposed feature–extraction pipeline consists of (i) bandpass filtering using Butterworth IIR filters and (ii) computing deciles for each channel-band pair. The zero-phase filtering step operates in linear time with respect to the number of samples M in each window, i.e., O(M) per channel and frequency band, because the filter order is constant. Computing the deciles requires partial sorting of the filtered signal, which has worst-case complexity . Therefore, the overall per-window complexity of the proposed method is dominated by the quantile extraction step and scales as for N channels and B frequency bands. Since this complexity is defined per window, it can be controlled by appropriately choosing the window length.

Given typical wearable-EEG settings (e.g., –8 channels, bands, samples for a 10-second window at 256 Hz), the full feature extraction for one window completes in under a few milliseconds on a standard CPU. This is supported by our empirical runtime measurements reported in Supplementary Table S3, confirming that the method is lightweight and suitable for real-time or wearable implementations.

Classification

The extracted frequency band decile features were used as input for machine learning models to classify EEG signals.

Three supervised learning models were evaluated: KNN, RF, and LightGBM. KNN is a non-parametric classifier that assigns class labels based on the majority vote of the nearest neighbors in feature space. RF, an ensemble learning method, constructs multiple decision trees to enhance robustness and reduce overfitting. LightGBM, a gradient-boosting framework optimized for high-dimensional data, is known for its efficiency and strong classification performance for EEG32,33.

To ensure a fair comparison across models and feature types, random search hyperparameter tuning was applied consistently for KNN, RF, and LightGBM. Search ranges were based on commonly used values (e.g., varying k for KNN, number of trees and maximum depth for RF, and number of estimators for LightGBM).

Experimental setup

Four distinct experiments were conducted in this study. First, an experiment was performed to classify neurodegenerative disorders by distinguishing EEG patterns in affected individuals from those in healthy controls. Second, a seizure detection experiment was carried out using time-segmented EEG data to identify seizure and non-seizure events. Third, the proposed decile-based feature extraction method was compared against a state-of-the-art DWT-based approach to benchmark its performance. Fourth, an additional experiment was designed to assess the method’s robustness under reduced EEG channel configurations, simulating constraints common in wearable EEG systems.

Experiment 1: neurodegenerative disorder classification

The aim of this experiment was to test the feasibility of the proposed method for identifying neurodegenerative diseases in comparison to healthy individuals. Thus, three classification tasks were defined. The AD OpenNeuro dataset was used for two separate binary classification tasks: one for AD and another for FTD, using the same control group (CN) for both tasks (AD versus CN and FTD versus CN). On the other hand, the PD Gait and PD UNM/UI datasets were each used for the classification of PD versus CN. No segmentation was applied to the EEG data; instead, the entire registered recordings were used for classification. This experiment aimed to distinguish pathological brain activity from healthy brain activity using full-length EEG trials.

Experiment 2: seizure detection

This experiment aimed to assess the feasibility of the proposed method for seizure detection using segmented EEG data. EEG recordings from the TUH EEG Seizure Corpus were divided into non-overlapping 10-second windows. Each window was labeled using the binary annotations previously extracted from the simplified annotation files during the data preparation step. As explained in the Data Preparation section, patients without seizure events were excluded, as they could not contribute to the supervised classification task. The seizure dataset is naturally imbalanced, with far more non-seizure than seizure segments, reflecting real-world prevalence. We explored strategies such as undersampling the majority class and synthetic oversampling using SMOTE34. However, both approaches led to worse performance. Undersampling discarded valuable non-seizure information, while SMOTE sometimes generated unrealistic synthetic EEG samples due to the complex, non-linear dynamics of the signals. Therefore, we retained the original segment distribution. These windows were then used to train machine learning models for the task of classifying seizure and non-seizure events. This segmentation of the data allowed for detailed analysis of seizure dynamics over short intervals, focusing on identifying seizure patterns within each 10-second window.

Experiment 3: comparison with DWT-based method

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed frequency band decile-based feature extraction approach, a comparative experiment was performed using a well-established state-of-the-art method based on the DWT35. This experiment served as a baseline to contrast the results obtained in both Experiment 1 and Experiment 2. In this setup, the DWT was applied to each EEG signal using the Daubechies wavelet (db4), which is commonly used in EEG analysis due to its ability to capture localized time-frequency information36,37. Signals were decomposed into multiple wavelet levels corresponding to standard EEG frequency bands. From each decomposition level, statistical features were extracted, including mean, standard deviation, energy, and entropy, resulting in a comprehensive time-frequency representation of the EEG data. For the neurodegenerative disorder datasets, full-length recordings were used without segmentation, replicating the conditions of Experiment 1. For the seizure detection dataset, EEG signals were segmented into 10-second non-overlapping windows, as described in Experiment 2. Identical preprocessing steps, channel configurations, and classification pipelines were applied to ensure fair comparison between the DWT-based and decile-based approaches. Additionally, to provide statistical rigor, we performed paired t-tests across the folds comparing the decile-based and DWT-based features for each task and classifier.

Experiment 4: robustness to reduced channel count

To assess the robustness of the proposed frequency band decile-based feature extraction method in low-channel EEG setups, an additional experiment was conducted by reducing the channel count across all datasets. The channel reduction process was carried out in two steps:

Initial standardization to 19 channels First, all datasets were reduced to the same 19-channel configuration used for the AD and FTD dataset. This step ensures consistency across datasets and simulates a moderate-density EEG setup. The 19 selected channels, reflecting regions typically implicated in AD and FTD pathology, span the frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes.

To assess the performance of each model, we evaluated classification accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. For tasks where each subject contributed only a single recording (e.g., PD datasets), standard 5-fold cross-validation was applied across subjects, with reported metrics representing the average over all folds. For tasks involving multiple windows per subject, such as seizure detection with the TUH corpus, we employed 5-fold cross-validation with subject-wise grouping to prevent information leakage. In this approach, all windows from a given subject were assigned to the same fold, ensuring that no data from the same patient appeared in both training and validation sets. Performance metrics were also averaged across the five folds to provide unbiased estimates of model generalization.

Seizure detection performance using DWT (Table 3) was also noticeably weaker compared to the decile-based method (see Table 2). These results demonstrate that the proposed frequency-band decile-based method is at least competitive with, and often superior to, DWT-based approaches. Paired t-tests across folds indicate that the performance gains of the decile-based method are statistically significant for most tasks and classifiers. For tasks where p-values were higher (e.g., FTD or PD Gait with LightGBM), the differences were not statistically significant, suggesting comparable performance between the two feature extraction methods in those specific cases. Furthermore, the proposed method has the advantage of being computationally simpler and more interpretable, making it a promising alternative for deployment in clinical or wearable EEG systems.

KNN dropped from 0.83 to 0.67 in accuracy, while RF and LightGBM maintained their previous performance levels.

For seizure detection, the models showed stable or slightly improved performance compared to the results in Table 2. KNN accuracy remained unchanged at 0.83, while RF improved slightly from 0.81 to 0.87, and LightGBM from 0.77 to 0.81. These results suggest that seizure-relevant information is relatively well preserved—and possibly even enhanced—after this standardized reduction to 19 channels.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the effectiveness of a decile-based feature extraction method for EEG-based classification of neurological disorders. The decile-based method generally achieved competitive performance. When the methods exhibited notably lower accuracy, such as LightGBM on the PD UNM/UI dataset (Acc: 0.33), similarly low performance was observed for the same dataset using state-of-the-art features (such as DWT), indicating that these outcomes likely reflect inherent challenges of the dataset—such as small sample size, class imbalance, and heterogeneous recording conditions. These observations highlight that model effectiveness can depend on both dataset characteristics and classifier choice, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation and further validation on diverse EEG cohorts. Future research could explore how the decile-based approach interacts with different machine learning techniques, as some models may be more sensitive to the extracted features.

Compared to the best-performing DWT-based results, considered one of the state-of-the-art approaches, the decile approach yielded notable gains in accuracy: an increase of 16% for AD classification, 15% and 33% for the two PD classification tasks, and 15% for seizure detection. These improvements demonstrate the robustness and adaptability of the method across different conditions.

When compared to other state-of-the-art methods applied to the same datasets, our approach achieved competitive results while maintaining simplicity and interpretability. For AD and FTD classification, our accuracies of 0.85 and 0.82 are directly comparable to the multi-feature fusion framework (0.84 and 0.81, respectively)43, demonstrating that decile-based features can match more complex handcrafted feature sets. For seizure detection, our method achieved 0.87 accuracy, lower than the 0.96 reported by a CNN-based approach44. However, the decile-based features maintained performance under substantial channel reduction, suggesting a trade-off between peak accuracy achieved by deep full-channel models and the robustness and hardware-feasibility of our lightweight representation. The largest gap was observed in PD classification, where deep CNNs reported accuracies above 0.9545, whereas our method reached 0.88 (PD Gait) and 0.83 (PD UNM/UI). Importantly, these deep learning studies rely on computationally intensive architectures trained on full-channel recordings, while our method explicitly investigates robustness to channel reduction and is designed to remain suitable for wearable EEG systems. This highlights a complementary contribution: decile-based features offer a lightweight, interpretable, and hardware-feasible alternative, with performance that remains competitive across multiple disorders, whereas most existing studies focus on a single application domain.

In reduced-channel scenarios, the method maintained performance using 19 channels in the PD Gait task and even showed a 4% increase in seizure detection accuracy. With as few as 6–8 channels, AD and FTD maintained accuracies of 85% and 82% respectively, while PD classification retained 88% and 83% accuracy for the two PD tasks. Seizure detection also improved by 4% compared to the full-channel setup. The results suggest strong potential for deploying this method in wearable EEG systems, where hardware constraints often limit spatial resolution.

The clinical potential of this approach is significant, particularly for the diagnosis and monitoring of neurological disorders such as AD, FTD, and PD. The ability to classify these conditions with relatively fewer EEG channels could lead to more accessible diagnostic tools, even in remote or resource-limited settings. The strong performance of models such as RF and KNN in distinguishing between these disorders underscores the potential of using portable EEG devices for early diagnosis and ongoing monitoring46. Additionally, the effectiveness of the method in seizure detection provides new possibilities for continuous, real-time monitoring of patients with epilepsy. This could lead to quicker intervention during seizures, potentially reducing the risk of injury and improving patients’ quality of life. These findings underscore the potential for using this method in clinical settings, where accessibility, cost-effectiveness, and patient convenience are paramount47.

Despite the promising results, several limitations must be considered. A key aspect to explore is the generalizability of the method across diverse populations and settings48. While the datasets used in this study were relatively controlled, EEG recordings in practical scenarios often exhibit greater variability. Factors such as age, disease progression, comorbidities, and recording environments may influence performance.

In addition, some datasets, such as the FTD cohort with only 23 cases, had relatively small sample sizes, which may limit the robustness of the reported findings. Variability in acquisition setups, including differences in electrode counts and institutional protocols, may also affect comparability across datasets. Our reliance on publicly available datasets provides transparency and reproducibility but may not fully capture the complexity of clinical recording conditions. Finally, results may be sensitive to preprocessing choices, such as the use of ICA and filtering, which could introduce biases or affect reproducibility across pipelines.

Future research should focus on expanding the scope of the decile-based feature extraction method to include more diverse datasets. For instance, exploring additional disease categories or seizure types would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the method’s capabilities and limitations. Incorporating data from patients across different stages of disease progression could also offer insights into how well the method can track changes over time, which is particularly valuable for longitudinal studies in clinical practice.

Conclusion

EEG holds significant promise for non-invasive detection and monitoring of neurological disorders, but effective feature extraction remains a key challenge, especially in settings with limited hardware resources or noisy data. Traditional approaches like wavelet transforms can be computationally intensive or less robust to signal variability. Motivated by the need for a simple yet effective representation of EEG statistical content, this study introduced a decile-based feature extraction method aimed at providing a simple and interpretable statistical representation of EEG signals.

The experimental results show that the proposed approach, particularly when combined with RF and KNN classifiers, achieves competitive performance across various classification tasks, including neurodegenerative disease detection and seizure identification. Importantly, the method exhibited strong robustness under reduced EEG channel conditions, with some models even improving in performance. These findings suggest that decile-based features may be a promising direction for low-density or wearable EEG systems, which are particularly relevant for clinical applications in resource-constrained environments. However, further validation in larger and more heterogeneous datasets will be important to establish the method’s generalizability and to assess its potential clinical applicability.
