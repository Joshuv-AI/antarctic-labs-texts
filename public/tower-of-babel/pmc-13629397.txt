Experimental and Numerical Simulation Study of the Main Controlling Factors of Borehole Stability with Different Coal Physical Properties
Authors: Xianzhong Li, Hanyu Lou, Zhenhua Li, Shuai Heng, Xiaodong Zhang, Sheng Zhang, Qingchao Li, Jiawen Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To resolve the low efficiency of gas drainage in soft coal seams caused by borehole breakout during coalbed gas drainage, it is essential to understand the mechanisms of borehole deformation and the main controlling factors of borehole stability with different coal physical properties. This paper investigates the geometric differences of borehole breakout in different preloaded coal samples under various factors using the Particle Flow Code (PFC). First, the tensile and compressive strengths were measured through Brazilian splitting and uniaxial compression tests. By integrating multiple parameters such as stress–strain curves and macroscopic fracture morphology, precise calibration of DEM macro-mesoscopic parameters was achieved. Subsequently, a series of batch simulations were conducted to investigate the critical conditions and failure modes of coal borehole instability under the influence of different factors. The results show that: (1) Through the trial-and-error calibration method using both Brazilian splitting and uniaxial compression tests, the error in key macro-mesoscopic mechanical parameters can be controlled within 3.13%, revealing the existence of stability-failure-collapse states during the borehole instability process. (2) By integrating key mechanical parameters and taking the microscopic parameter of coal physical cohesion (Cohesion, Coh) in PFC as the main controlling factor affecting borehole stability, it is found that hard coal (Coh = 23.5 MPa and 17 MPa) remains stable under stress difference (Stress Difference Ratio, N) ≤ 3.0 and borehole diameter (Diameter, D) ≥ 120 mm. Medium-soft coal (Coh = 10.5 MPa) exhibits a critical stress difference N = 2.5, a critical failure borehole diameter D = 90 mm, and a collapse borehole diameter D = 100 mm. Soft coal (Coh = 4 MPa) has an extremely low critical value, making it prone to collapse. Based on these findings, the critical conditions for stability-failure-collapse states are established. (3) Beyond the critical stress difference or borehole diameter, the V-shaped breach deepens and widens, with its normalized depth and fracture span angle increasing nonlinearly as the critical value for failure increases. The critical conditions for gas drainage borehole instability are universal and can provide theoretical support for the stability of drainage boreholes.

Introduction

The occurrence conditions of coal resources in China are complex, with over 95% of coal seams in high-gas mines being low-permeability seams, posing severe challenges to safe and efficient coal mining. Focusing on typical mining areas in China, the coal seams have undergone multiple tectonic movements, resulting in extensively developed fractures. Consequently, the coal structure has been damaged, exhibiting occurrence characteristics characterized by soft coal, low permeability, and high gas content. Technologies such as hydraulic flushing for permeability enhancement and gas predrainage via boreholes have become crucial for ensuring mine safety production. The effectiveness of these technologies heavily depends on the stability of the drainage boreholes themselves. , Under a complex underground geological environment and mining influences, boreholes are highly susceptible to instability and failure, including deformation, collapse, and even blockage. This leads to gas drainage efficiency far below expectations, resulting in resource waste and potentially creating safety hazards due to substandard drainage performance. , Therefore, an in-depth investigation into the stability evolution laws of gas drainage boreholes in coal seams holds significant theoretical value and engineering practical significance for improving gas control levels and ensuring safe coal mine production.

In recent years, extensive research on the borehole stability has been conducted globally. It is now understood that borehole stability is governed by the coupled control of multiple factors, including in situ stress, borehole size, coal physical properties, temperature, and drilling methods. , Among these, in situ stress, borehole size, and coal physical properties are widely recognized as the three dominant controlling factors. , In situ stress acts as the primary driver of borehole instability. Field observations and theoretical analyses have confirmed that when the ratio of maximum to minimum principal stress exceeds a critical value, the borehole will fail due to high stress concentration. , This phenomenon is particularly common in deep, high-stress mining areas and serves as the main controlling factor for shear slip and fracture propagation around the borehole. Borehole size, as a key engineering-controllable parameter, exhibits a significant scaling effect. Research indicates that increasing the borehole diameter not only directly expands the stress disturbance range after drilling but also alters the gradient distribution of the stress field around the borehole, consequently influencing the extent of the damage zone and the final fracture morphology. , Particularly in soft coal seams, larger-diameter boreholes are more prone to inducing interconnected macroscopic fractures, leading to rapid failure of the effective drainage channel, a phenomenon validated by field tests. Coal physical properties constitute the fundamental controlling conditions. Parameters such as the uniaxial compressive strength, elastic modulus, and cohesion determine the damage evolution around the borehole and significantly impact its stability. Cohesion, in essence, represents the inherent property of coal to resist failure under external forces. Coal with higher cohesion typically undergoes localized brittle spalling, while soft coal with lower cohesion tends toward progressive, layered spalling characterized by large-scale shear slip. This drastically increases the difficulty of borehole formation in soft coal. − Furthermore, research on borehole failure morphology has progressed from early macroscopic descriptions toward a focus on its mesoscopic evolutionary processes. The formation mechanism of V-shaped breakouts and the influence of in situ stress, borehole size, and coal physical properties on this phenomenon remain key issues requiring further investigation.

As research has progressed, numerical simulations have emerged as a key approach to revealing the mechanisms of borehole stability. The discrete element method (DEM) and its embedded flat-joint model (FJM), owing to their capability to visualize discontinuous fracture processes, have been widely applied in borehole stability studies. Some scholars have successfully simulated the V-shaped spalling process of boreholes using DEM, , replicating the macroscopic failure behavior of boreholes. Furthermore, they developed a particle removal algorithm based on the strain energy release criterion, significantly improving the consistency between numerical simulations and experimental results. Subsequently, the research focus gradually shifted from macroscopic phenomena to dominant controlling factors affecting borehole stability. Investigations into the controlling effects of in situ stress and borehole geometry confirmed that higher in situ stress anisotropy significantly alters the distribution pattern of shear failure around the borehole. While introducing stability criteria based on yield zone extent and maximum displacement, studies also revealed the controlling mechanism of the borehole size effect, demonstrating that smaller borehole sizes can significantly amplify the spalling initiation stress. With the influence of in situ stress and borehole diameter becoming clearer, research attention turned toward the structural characteristics of the coal mass itself. For weakly cemented strata, , simulations indicated that borehole excavation triggers in situ stress redistribution and plastic zone development; when the cohesive bond strength between particles is insufficient, particle migration into the borehole leads to hole collapse. The regulatory mechanism of interparticle cohesion on failure modes was revealed, showing that increased cohesion expands the yield surface and alters the critical mean stress between different failure modes. These findings propelled researchers toward systematically investigating the physical properties of coal mass, analyzing instability patterns under various combinations of in situ stress and borehole diameter. They clarified the influence of coal elastic modulus on failure morphology and preliminarily proposed critical failure values under the influence of multiple factors.

In summary, this study employs a combined approach of laboratory experiments and PFC numerical simulation to systematically investigate the failure characteristics and critical conditions of borehole instability under the coupled influence of multiple controlling factors, including coal physical properties, in situ stress difference, and borehole size. Based on the macroscopic mechanical parameters obtained from uniaxial compression and Brazilian splitting tests, a prestressed borehole Flat-Joint Model (FJM) was constructed, enabling a macro-mesoscopic description of the critical state of borehole instability. By systematically adjusting key parameters such as coal physical properties (Coh = 4–23.5 MPa), stress difference ratio (N = 1.0–3.0), and borehole diameter (D = 80–120 mm), the failure mechanisms of boreholes under the influence of multiple controlling factors were revealed, and the critical conditions for borehole failure were established. The research findings can provide a theoretical basis for the safe construction and stability control of boreholes in deep coal seams.

Basic Physical Parameters of Coal

Sample and Test Equipment

The coal samples used in this test were collected from the No. 3 coal seam of Chengzhuang Coal Mine, Jincheng Coal Mining Group. The coal in this seam exhibits high density and high hardness, with well-developed and interconnected joints and fissures, presenting an overall uniform blocky structure. The apparent relative density of the coal samples ranges from 1.43 to 1.46 g/cm3. Following the specifications of GB/T 50266-2021, “Standard for Test Methods of Engineering Rock Masses,” standard Brazilian disc specimens (diameter: 50 ± 0.5 mm, height: 25 ± 0.5 mm) and standard uniaxial compression specimens (diameter: 50 ± 0.5 mm, height: 100 ± 0.5 mm) were prepared using a rock drilling machine.

The loading tests were conducted using the GCTS RTX-3000 rock mechanics testing system, which is capable of high-temperature and high-pressure conditions and has a maximum axial load capacity of 1500 kN. Axial displacement was monitored using displacement sensors, while lateral displacement was measured with a circumferential extensometer. To obtain stable stress–strain curves, the tests employed axial displacement control at a constant loading rate of 0.005 mm/s. The testing procedure is illustrated in Figure .

Calibration of Macro-Micro Parameters

The flat-joint model (FJM) , implemented in the Particle Flow Code (PFC) offers distinct advantages for simulating materials like coal, which exhibit pronounced brittle characteristics and complex fracture behavior. It can accurately reproduce macroscopic properties such as the uniaxial compressive strength-to-tensile strength ratio (UCS/TS) and Poisson’s ratio.

As a typical heterogeneous brittle material, the failure process of coal is often characterized by the initiation, propagation, and coalescence of microcracks driven by the combined action of tensile and shear stresses. The FJM introduces a flat-joint contact (FJC) with a finite area between particles in Figure , enabling a more realistic simulation of continuum mechanics behavior within the coal mass. These contact interfaces can simultaneously bear normal and shear stresses, and the tensile or shear failure of the bond is determined according to the Coulomb criterion. Consequently, this approach naturally captures the tensile-shear composite failure modes commonly observed in coal.

Schematic diagram of the FJM and mechanical behavior (reprinted from Itasca Consulting Group, Inc. (2014)).

The mesoscopic parameters that need to be defined in the FJM model can be summarized into three categories: geometric parameters, including the particle size distribution range (D min –D max ), the installation gap (gs), and the number of radial elements (Nr); mechanical property parameters, covering the stiffness ratio (k n /k s ), effective modulus (E c ), friction coefficient (μ), and internal friction angle (φ); and strength parameters, primarily the bond tensile strength (σ c ) and bond cohesion (C). The principles for selecting key parameters are as follows: The model resolution Davg (specimen diameter D/D max ) must satisfy the critical condition of being greater than 20 to ensure the spatial uniformity of macroscopic parameters. Without considering gradation, where R max /R min = 1.66, an optimized setting for the installation gap gs (typically taken as gs = 0.2 D min ) can significantly improve the numerical simulation accuracy of the contact network coordination number. In the calibration of mechanical parameters, the effective modulus E c can be matched to the macroscopic elastic modulus E. The stiffness ratio k n /k s (within the recommended range of 1 to 3) can characterize the typical Poisson’s ratio range (0.1 to 0.3) of brittle rocks. For strength parameter selection, the bond cohesion C is adjusted in uniaxial compression numerical tests to match the macroscopic uniaxial compressive strength (UCS). In Brazilian splitting numerical tests, the bond tensile strength σ c is adjusted to match the macroscopic tensile strength (TS). , The internal friction angle φ and the number of radial elements Nr are assigned empirical values (e.g., φ taken as 0.5 or 0.4 based on experience) and recommended values, respectively, as they have a relatively minor influence on prepeak behavior.

Calibration Principle, Process, and Results of Macro and Micro Parameters

Regarding the calibration method for the FJM model, numerous scholars have conducted relevant research. This study adopts a trial-and-error method based on dual tests (Brazilian splitting and uniaxial compression) for calibration. To avoid the influence of randomness in particle arrangement, three random seeds are used for simulation and to assess strength fluctuations. If the fluctuations are significant, the aforementioned calibration steps are repeated for fine-tuning, ensuring that the model’s key characteristics, such as the slope in the elastic stage and the tensile/compressive strengths, are consistent with those of the actual coal. The specific requirements are: first, to ensure that the simulated stress–strain curves match the experimental curves; and second, to ensure that the fracture morphology of the simulated model matches that of the experimental specimens.

The specific calibration steps are illustrated in Figure : ① Set the internal friction angle φ, number of radial elements Nr, and friction coefficient μ to 0, 4, and 0.5, respectively. Initially select a stiffness ratio k n /k s = 1.5 and a bond cohesion C = 1e20. Vary the effective modulus E c to match the elastic modulus E. ② Set the bond tensile strength σ t equal to the bond cohesion C. Vary σ t to match the tensile strength TS. ③ Set σ t equal to the target TS. Vary the bond cohesion C to match the uniaxial compressive strength UCS. ④ Adjust the stiffness ratio k n /k s according to the Poisson’s ratio. ⑤ Check whether the resulting tensile and compressive strengths simultaneously match the macroscopic mechanical parameters. If not, repeat calibration steps ② and ③ until the data align.

Trial-and-error method is used to calibrate the microscopic parameters of coal.

In PFC, there are microparameters related to rock properties and joint properties that control the mechanical behavior of the model and ultimately influence the simulation results. However, these parameters cannot be directly obtained from physical experiments and must be determined through a parameter calibration process. The most effective calibration process requires a trial-and-error approach, where the macroscopic mechanical response of the PFC model is repeatedly compared with physical experiment results until a satisfactory agreement is achieved. This process is time-consuming and data-intensive. In this study, PFC was used to perform multiple trial-and-error calibrations for Brazilian splitting and uniaxial compression tests, continuously optimizing parameters such as elastic modulus, Poisson’s ratio, tensile strength, and uniaxial compressive strength to reflect the actual experimental results. Figure presents a comparison of the calibrated curves between the coal experiments and simulations, while Figure shows a comparison of the coal fracture morphology.

Mechanical Parameters of Coal

To achieve a strength-based classification of coal (soft vs hard), this study introduces the strength coefficient (f-value), widely used in coal mine engineering, as the classification standard for coal strength. − This coefficient has a clear conversion relationship with the uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) of coal (UCS ≈ 10f). Previous research has shown that the cohesion (Coh) in the FJM model significantly influences the UCS of uniaxial compression specimens. Building on this, the initially calibrated coal sample (Coh = 23.5 MPa, corresponding UCS = 29.35 MPa) was evaluated first, yielding an f-value of approximately 2.93. According to industry specifications (f ≥ 1.5 defines hard coal), this sample falls within the typical hard coal category. To determine the lower strength parameter limit for soft coal, based on the specification’s definition of soft coal as f < 0.5, the upper UCS limit for soft coal was calculated to be 5 MPa. Accordingly, through a systematic trial-and-error inversion calibration, it was determined that when Coh = 4 MPa, the uniaxial compressive strength exhibited by the numerical model is 4.8 MPa, corresponding to an f-value of 0.48, which conforms to the strength characteristics of soft coal.

Drilling Stability Simulation Scheme

Numerous previous studies have demonstrated that the discrete element numerical simulation method can more accurately simulate the phenomenon of the borehole breakout. Depending on the test design, these experiments can be categorized into predrilled tests and prestressed tests. The predrilled loading method significantly reduces the strength of the sample before stress application, which negatively interferes with the investigation of borehole failure behavior. In contrast, the prestressed loading method better represents actual in situ conditions. Therefore, this study adopts the prestressed loading method. The construction of the numerical model is illustrated in Figure . The length and width of the model are set to 600 mm, with the borehole located at the geometric center of the model.

Prestressed drilling FJM model.

Based on the aforementioned calibration results from the Brazilian splitting and uniaxial compression tests, Coh values of 4 and 23.5 MPa were selected as the strength characterization parameters for soft coal and hard coal, respectively. While maintaining a constant tensile-to-compressive strength ratio (Coh/Ten), Coh was set to 4, 10.5, 17, and 23.5 MPa to systematically investigate the influence of a continuous variation in coal strength from soft to hard on borehole stability. The stress difference ratio N was set to 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, and 3.0. This parameter range primarily references the discrete element study on coal seam borehole failure by Huang, which clearly identified the stress difference ratio as a key factor controlling the geometry of V-shaped borehole breakouts. The borehole diameter D was set to 80, 90, 100, 110, and 120 mm, a range that covers the common borehole diameters used in coal mine gas drainage engineering. Based on the in situ stress measurement data from the Chengzhuang Coal Mine and typical deep coal seam conditions in China, the minimum horizontal principal stress (σh) is set to a constant value of 10 MPa in this study to represent a realistic engineering context. The maximum horizontal principal stress (σH) is then varied according to the stress difference ratio N = σH/σh (ranging from 1.0 to 3.0). This setup allows for a systematic investigation of the impact of stress anisotropy on borehole stability under a representative absolute stress level. Analysis of Numerical Simulation Results

The Evolution Process of Progressive Instability Failure of Borehole

To investigate the process and critical conditions of borehole instability, a borehole model with parameters Coh = 10.5 MPa, N = 3.0, and D = 100 mm was selected for simulation to observe the progression from stability to failure. The model state was recorded every 10 timesteps. The cumulative number of cracks at different timesteps is shown in Figure . The borehole instability morphologies corresponding to timesteps 60, 90, 240, 980, 2010, and 4100 are presented in Figure . In these figures, gray particles represent the coal matrix, yellow particles represent detached coal fragments, and black and red line segments indicate tensile microcracks and shear microcracks, respectively. The proportion of shear cracks and the volume change at each stage were also recorded, as shown in Figure . To better quantify and compare borehole deformation and to establish a clear definition for borehole instability failure, the normalized depth (L/R) and fracture span angle (θ) are introduced here. Both values depend on the final failure geometry of the borehole. , Normalized depth represents the ratio of the distance L from the top of the borehole breakout to the borehole center, divided by the borehole radius R. The fracture span angle (θ) denotes the maximum angle of fractures contacting the borehole wall and center during the failure process (Figure and ).

Progressive failure procedure of borehole breakout.

Variation of microcracks as a function of timestep.

Proportion of shear cracks and volume change.

The results indicate that the number of tensile cracks increased rapidly after borehole formation. By timestep 60, the total crack count reached 518, and shear cracks began to appear. At this stage, no significant change in the macroscopic borehole morphology was observed, and it remained stable. By timestep 90, the total crack count increased to 1552. Shear cracks appeared on the right side of the borehole, while those on the left side propagated into the coal mass. Local damage manifested as coal particle spalling, but the overall borehole integrity remained intact, indicating good stability. By timestep 240, the total crack count reached 3001. Shear cracks concentrated on both sides of the borehole. An incipient V-shaped breakout formed on the left, while coal spalling occurred on the right. The normalized depth increased to 1.07, and the fracture span angle was 91.5°. The borehole morphology began to change, and stability started to decrease. By timestep 980, the total crack count reached 5594. The V-shaped breakout on the left deepened, forming a distinct shear zone, while an incipient V-shaped breakout appeared on the right. The normalized depth increased to 1.12, and the fracture span angle was 93.4°, consistent with findings in the literature. , By timestep 2010, the total crack count reached 6521. The crack density growth on the left slowed, but coal blocks began to spall and slide, forming a typical V-shaped breakout. Crack development accelerated on the right, also forming a V-shaped breakout. The normalized depth increased stepwise to 1.37, and the fracture span angle reached 111.7°, indicating intensified borehole failure. By timestep 4100, crack growth ceased at a total of 8878. Failure on the left side stopped, while cracks on the right continued to develop. The normalized depth increased to 1.42, and the fracture span angle reached 115.9°. The V-shaped breakout deepened and widened, with the degree of failure exceeding that on the left. Multiple closely spaced shear zones formed on both sides of the borehole, macroscopically reaching a state of borehole collapse.

The simulation results demonstrate that borehole failure initiates with the rapid propagation of microcracks induced by stress redistribution. By timestep 240, with the growth rate of shear cracks and the rate of volume change declining, the incipient V-shaped breakout emerged, indicating that the onset of failure is an extremely rapid process. Subsequently, shear crack development entered a gradual growth phase, corresponding to the progressive accumulation of damage and the gradual increase in normalized depth and fracture span angle. By timestep 2010, the accumulated shear cracks formed a distinct shear zone. The normalized depth and fracture span angle exhibited a substantial increase, from approximately 1.1 to around 1.4 and from 90° to approximately 110°, respectively. Macroscopically, the accumulated damage reached the threshold for instability, forming a clearly discernible V-shaped breakout. With further timesteps, the shear fractures interacted, generating multiple shear zones. However, the increases in normalized depth and fracture span angle gradually stabilized around 1.4 and 110°, respectively. The V-shaped breakout became clearly visible, macroscopically achieving a state of borehole collapse.

Based on the analysis, the borehole failure morphology under the influence of multiple factors can be categorized into three stages: First, the stable stage, where failure initiation occurs but shear fractures have not yet accumulated to a significant scale. At this stage, the normalized depth is approximately 1.1, and the fracture span angle is around 90°. Macroscopically, local damage in the form of coal particle spalling is observed, but the overall borehole integrity remains intact, indicating good stability. Second, the unstable stage, where shear cracks accumulate to a certain extent, forming a distinct shear zone. Here, the normalized depth increases substantially from approximately 1.1 to 1.4, and the fracture span angle increases from around 90° to approximately 110°. Macroscopically, failure propagates deeper and laterally, forming a clear V-shaped breakout. Third, the collapse stage, characterized by large-scale development of shear fractures, resulting in multiple shear zones. In this stage, the normalized depth stabilizes around 1.4, and the fracture span angle stabilizes around 110°, exhibiting only minor increases. Macroscopically, damage accumulates around the V-shaped breakout, leading to large-scale borehole deformation.

The Influence of Coal Physical Properties on Borehole Stability

To systematically investigate the controlling mechanism of coal physical properties on borehole stability, this section conducts comparative simulations on four coal types with different strengths, corresponding to Coh = 23.5, 17, 10.5, and 4 MPa, under fixed conditions of stress difference ratio N = 2.0 and borehole diameter D = 100 mm (Figure ). By analyzing the final borehole failure morphology shown in Figure , the microcrack development curves in Figure , and the borehole wall deformation characteristics in Figure , the patterns of stability evolution are revealed.

Borehole failure pattern.

The simulation results indicate that for Coh = 23.5 MPa (Figure a), only 1,440 microcracks were generated around the borehole, predominantly tensile cracks, with only a few shear cracks appearing on the left side of the borehole wall. Microcracks propagated rapidly in the initial stage of borehole formation and stabilized after 1,350 timesteps (Figure ). Localized minor damage in the form of coal particle spalling occurred on the left side of the borehole wall, but no macroscopic shear zone formed, and the borehole remained largely stable overall (Figure ). As Coh decreased to 17 MPa (Figure b), the number of cracks around the borehole increased to 1,978, and the proportion of shear cracks rose from 2.57% to 6.33%. Shear cracks developed significantly on the left side of the borehole wall and extended into the coal mass. Microcrack propagation ceased after 1,450 timesteps. The damaged area on the left side of the borehole wall extended inward, forming an incipient V-shaped breakout, while localized minor damage appeared on the right side. Shear cracks showed a tendency to aggregate but did not form a connected shear zone; the borehole remained in a stable state. For Coh = 10.5 MPa (Figure c), the number of microcracks around the borehole increased to 2,651, and the proportion of shear cracks rose significantly to 15.80%, forming large-scale shear crack zones on both sides of the borehole wall. Microcrack propagation continued until 1,480 timesteps. Distinct V-shaped breakouts formed on both sides of the borehole wall, with shear cracks coalescing into zones. At this stage, the normalized depth was 1.09, and the fracture span angle was 93.8°, indicating that the borehole had undergone unstable failure. For Coh = 4 MPa (Figure d), the number of microcracks around the borehole increased rapidly to 8,406, and the proportion of shear cracks reached 57.19%. The failure mechanism transitioned from tensile-dominated to shear-dominated. Microcrack propagation continued until 4,680 timesteps. The V-shaped breakouts extended deep into the coal mass, with significantly increased width and depth. Multiple connected shear zones formed on both sides of the borehole wall. At this stage, the normalized depth reached 1.47, and the fracture span angle was 132.5°, indicating severe instability and failure of the borehole, exhibiting typical characteristics of borehole collapse.

The simulation results demonstrate that the coal physical properties exert a significant controlling influence on borehole stability. As Coh decreased from 23.5 to 4 MPa, the borehole failure mode transitioned. The total number of microcracks increased substantially from 1,440 to 8,406, while the proportion of shear cracks rose from 2.57% to 57.19%, indicating a shift in the failure mechanism from tensile-dominated to shear-dominated. The duration of crack development extended from 1,350 to 4,680 timesteps, reflecting a more prolonged energy release process. Regarding the evolution of the borehole wall morphology, the failure mode gradually progressed from localized minor damage to bilaterally developed V-shaped breakouts, accompanied by the formation of connected shear zones. Notably, when Coh decreased from 23.5 to 10.5 MPa, borehole stability exhibited a gradually declining trend, with a relatively slow increase in crack count and limited change in the duration of crack propagation. As mentioned earlier, coal seam boreholes exhibit three distinct states under different working conditions: stability, failure, and collapse. The formation of through-going shear zones, with the normalized depth reaching approximately 1.1 and the fracture span angle reaching approximately 90°, marks the onset of borehole failure. Subsequently, as failure progresses, multiple shear zones develop around the borehole, manifesting macroscopically as V-shaped breakouts. When the normalized depth reaches approximately 1.4 and the fracture span angle reaches approximately 110°, it signifies borehole collapse. Discussion

In Section , the three main controlling factors affecting borehole stabilitycoal physical properties, in situ stress, and borehole sizewere analyzed, and the failure states of boreholes under 100 simulated conditions were presented. The simulation results indicate that the influence of these three main controlling factors is not linear but rather exhibits critical conditions. Furthermore, the differences in coal strength determine the sensitivity of boreholes to variations in in situ stress and borehole diameter. Therefore, this section aims to systematically investigate the critical failure values dominated by coal physical properties, providing guidance for gas drainage borehole practices.

The overall evolution process was gradual, with a periodic jump in the number of fractures occurring only when the stress difference ratio N ≥ 2.5. This is because coal particles with high cohesion possess strong bond strength; in situ stress needs to accumulate to a relatively high level to exceed the bond threshold, triggering concentrated propagation of shear cracks. However, through-going failure did not form at this stage. As the coal softened to Coh = 17 MPa, although the failure duration and total number of fractures at this stage increased, they did not exceed the threshold for borehole failure. The borehole transitioned from a failure state to a collapse state, with stress difference ratio N = 2.5 becoming the critical collapse threshold for coal of this strength. In the soft coal stage (Coh = 4 MPa), under all stress difference conditions, the failure duration and number of fractures remained at high levels without any obvious abrupt change point. This indicates that the bond strength between soft coal particles is extremely weak; even minor in situ stress disturbances (N = 1.0) can quickly induce the propagation and coalescence of shear cracks, directly leading to a collapse state.

Statistical analysis of the normalized depth (L/R) and fracture span angle (θ) further quantifies the influence of coal physical properties on borehole failure under the effect of in situ stress. For soft coal (Coh = 4 MPa), the normalized depth steadily increased from 1.35 at N = 1.0 to 1.43 at N = 3.0, and the fracture span angle increased from 114.2° to 116.2°. The growth was gradual with no obvious inflection point. This phenomenon indicates that once failure occurs in a soft coal borehole, it directly enters a stable collapse state; an increase in the stress difference ratio can only slightly exacerbate the degree of failure but cannot alter the failure mode. For medium-soft coal (Coh = 10.5 MPa), the normalized depth (L/R) and fracture span angle (θ) exhibited segmented growth characteristics. When N ≤ 2.0, the normalized depth remained between 1.12 and 1.16, and the fracture span angle between 90.3° and 96.9°, corresponding to the failure stage. When N ≥ 2.5, the normalized depth rapidly increased to 1.32, and the fracture span angle jumped to 112.3°, approaching the damage level characteristic of the collapse state. This validates the critical collapse threshold of N = 2.5. This is because the increase in Coh endows the coal with a certain shear resistance capacity; the stress difference ratio needs to exceed a threshold to cause shear zone coalescence, leading to an escalation in the degree of failure. For hard coal (Coh = 17–23.5 MPa), the boreholes remained stable without obvious failure, indicating that high-Coh coal can effectively inhibit the propagation of failure induced by in situ stress concentration, consistently maintaining a stable state.

The mechanism underlying the variation of these critical values can be summarized as follows: coal physical properties fundamentally determine the resistance to shear slip between particles. The higher the Coh, the greater the stress difference required to overcome the bond resistance and form through-going shear zones. When Coh ≥ 17 MPa, the bond strength of the coal is sufficient to withstand in situ stress disturbances corresponding to N = 1.0–3.0. When Coh = 10.5 MPa, the bond strength and the stress difference ratio form a critical equilibrium state, with N = 2.5 becoming the threshold for this balance. When Coh ≤ 4 MPa, the bond strength is insufficient to resist even minimal in situ stress disturbances, resulting in a collapse state across all conditions. This pattern of critical values provides a quantitative basis for borehole design and in situ stress management in coal seams of different strengths. For soft coal strata with Coh ≤ 4 MPa, the stress difference ratio should be controlled below N < 1.0. This can be achieved through measures such as advanced pressure relief and sectional drilling to reduce in situ stress anisotropy, while simultaneously employing casing support to prevent borehole collapse. For medium-soft coal strata with Coh = 10.5 MPa, the stress difference ratio should be strictly controlled within N ≤ 2.0. If geological conditions necessitate N ≥ 2.5, high-strength support materials or reduced borehole diameters should be adopted to inhibit the coalescence of shear zones. For hard coal strata with Coh ≥ 17 MPa, under in situ stress conditions with N ≤ 3.0, large-diameter boreholes can be utilized to enhance drainage efficiency.

2It is revealed that coal physical properties are the core intrinsic factor controlling borehole stability. As Coh decreases from 23.5 MPa (hard coal) to 4 MPa (soft coal), the borehole failure mode transitions from tensile-dominated local microdamage to shear-dominated large-scale collapse. The total number of microcracks increases nearly 5-fold, the proportion of shear cracks rises from less than 3% to over 57%, and the failure duration is significantly prolonged. The study clarifies the existence of a critical transition interval for coal strength (Coh ≈ 10.5 MPa), below which the sensitivity of boreholes to variations in in situ stress and borehole diameter is significantly enhanced.
