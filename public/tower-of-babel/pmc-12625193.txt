The relationship of university students’ attitudes towards future with substance abuse proclivity
Authors: Duygu ÖZER, Özlem ŞAHİN ALTUN, Mustafa AKYÜZ
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aimed to examine the effect of university students’ attitudes toward the future on their substance abuse proclivity.MethodThis cross-sectional study was conducted in January 2025 with 579 students from the Faculty of Health Sciences and the Vocational School of Health Services at a university in Eastern Anatolia, Türkiye. Data was collected using the Attitudes Toward the Future Scale (ATFS), and the Substance Abuse Proclivity Scale (SAPS).ResultsOf the students, 50.9% were associate degree students. The mean total ATFS score was 50.87(10.37), with the highest sub-dimension score in the positive attitude subscale 18.76(4.88). The mean total SAPS score was 15.76(3.93), and 47.7% of students showed proclivity toward substance use. Undergraduate students had a significantly higher proclivity for substance abuse than associate degree students (p < 0.05). A very weak but significant negative correlation was found between the positive attitude sub-dimension and substance abuse proclivity (r = -0.102; p < 0.05), while a very weak but significant positive correlation was observed between the anxious attitude sub-dimension and substance abuse proclivity (r = 0.126; p < 0.05). The positive attitude sub-dimension predicted 0.6% of the variance in SAPS scores, while the anxious attitude sub-dimension predicted 8.1%.ConclusionStudents with positive attitudes toward the future had lower substance abuse proclivity, while those with anxious attitudes had higher proclivity. Interventions focusing on anxiety management and substance use prevention should be implemented, particularly for students with anxious future outlooks. Additionally, incorporating mandatory addiction education into university curricula may contribute to prevention efforts.Keywords: Attitude towards future, University students, Substance abuse proclivity

Introduction

The 21 st century has brought about profound changes in human life, encompassing social, cultural, economic, and political domains [1]. Economic instability, rising unemployment rates, and shifting lifestyles have significantly influenced individuals’ attitudes and perceptions regarding the future [1, 2]. The concept of attitude toward the future refers to individuals’ expectations, hopes, motivations, and life goals [3], and also includes the capacity to anticipate future events and engage in planning [4]. Individuals who can envision and plan for the future are more likely to regulate their development and adjust their behaviors in alignment with their aspirations [4, 5]. Accordingly, one’s attitude toward the future plays a significant role in facilitating behavioral change [2].

Emerging adulthood, particularly during university years, represents a critical developmental stage marked by identity formation, uncertainty, and transition. Students frequently encounter life challenges such as leaving home, adapting to new environments, developing professional goals, and facing job market uncertainties [6, 7], which collectively shape their attitudes toward the future [7]. A positive outlook is associated with optimism, hope, and persistence in achieving personal goals [8, 9]. The studies have linked positive future expectations to enhanced psychological well-being [10], higher perceived success, greater autonomy, and increased employability [7, 11, 12]. In contrast, negative expectations are linked with pessimism, anxiety, and greater risk for mental health problems such as depression and anxiety disorders [8, 9]. Because attitudes toward the future affect both individual mental health and broader societal dynamics, understanding these attitudes among university students, the future workforce and citizens, holds critical importance.

One of the most pressing challenges faced by youth today is substance abuse, which has reached alarming rates worldwide and in Türkiye [13–15]. Substance use among young people not only affects individuals but also poses risks to families and society. It is associated with negative outcomes, including physical, psychological, social, and legal problems [14]. Early identification of at-risk groups and the implementation of effective prevention strategies are essential. Various risk factors have been documented in the literature, including peer influence, family dysfunction, childhood trauma, existing mental health problems, low coping skills, and low self-efficacy [16–18]. Attitudes toward the future may also act as protective or risk factors for substance use proclivity. While limited, existing studies suggest that hopelessness is positively associated with substance use proclivity [14], whereas stronger future expectations may enhance self-efficacy against addiction [19]. However, international research on this relationship remains scarce.

Given the potential implications for individual and societal well-being, examining the link between future orientation and substance use among university students is essential. Findings from this study may contribute to the early detection of individuals at risk and inform preventive strategies. Moreover, this study provides an original contribution by analyzing the association between future-oriented attitudes and substance abuse proclivity in Türkiye, offering culturally relevant insights to support the development of targeted interventions.

Aim

This study aims to examine the relationship between university students’ attitudes towards the future, and their substance use proclivities.

Method

Study design

A descriptive and cross-sectional study was conducted and reported using the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Population and sample of the study

The population of the study consisted of 2841 university students enrolled in the Vocational School of Health Services (n = 2040) and Faculty of Health Sciences (n = 801) of a state university in Türkiye. The study sample was calculated as a minimum of 339 students with a 95% confidence interval and 5% margin of error according to the minimum sample size formula (n = N*Z²pq/d²(N − 1) + Z²pq). Using a convenience sampling method, 579 students voluntarily participated in the study between January 1–30, 2025.

Data collection tools

An online questionnaire was used to reach many students. Data forms were prepared using Google Forms. A survey link was shared with a social media group for students, and the data were collected via this online questionnaire. All students were informed about the study before they started to complete the forms, and those who volunteered to participate in the study were asked to complete the scales. In addition, the students were informed that they could leave the study whenever they wanted and that their data would be kept confidential. Because the scales were completed online and it was compulsory to answer each question, there was no missing data. In the online forms, settings were arranged so that each student answered the questions only once.

Data measurement

The Information Form, Attitude Towards Future Scale, and Substance Abuse Proclivity Scale were used to collect the study data.

Information form

The form, which was created by the researchers in line with the literature [14, 19], consists of a total of 17 questions including questions such as age, gender, academic achievement, family economic status, and voluntary choice of the department.

Attitude towards future scale (ATFS)

It was developed by Güler [20] to measure university students’ attitudes towards their personal future. After the initial 40-item form of the scale, factor analysis studies were repeated, and a 15-item short form was created. According to the 15-item short form, three factors explaining 59% of the total variance were obtained according to the principal component analysis with promax rotation. The scale is a 5-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. There are three sub-dimensions (positive, anxious and planned attitude towards the future) each consisting of five items. Each sub-dimension ranges from 5 to 25 points; the higher the scores calculated for the sub-dimension, the higher the attitude towards the future measured by that sub-dimension. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were found to be 0.80, 0.81 and 0.79 for the Positive, Anxious and Planned subscales, respectively, and 0.84 for the whole scale [20]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of the subscales were found to be 0.84, 0.87 and 0.83, and the total Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.87 (Table 2).

Substance abuse proclivity scale (SAPS)

The scale developed by MacAndrew [21] determines whether individuals are prone to substance use. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Ceyhun et al. [22]. The scale consists of 36 items and is scored as “True = 1” and “False = 0”. A score of 16 and above on the scale means that the person has a proclivity to substance abuse, and the reverse scored items were determined as 9, 12, 13, 19, 24, 25, 26, 29, 35. In the validity and reliability study conducted by Ceyhun et al. [22], the test-retest reliability of the scale was analyzed by Pearson Product Moment Correlation technique and found to be 0.63. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was found to be 0.75 (Table 2).

Ethics

This study was conducted in compliance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. Before the study was conducted, the ethics committee approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of X university (numbered 2024-SBB-0971 and dated December 11, 2024) and the institutional permission was obtained from Vocational School of Health Services and Faculty of Health Sciences, where the study was to be conducted. Before accessing the survey, all participants were required to read an informed consent statement online and provide electronic confirmation by selecting the ‘I agree’ option, which was considered as written informed consent in the online environment.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). The normality of the data was evaluated through both the Shapiro-Wilk test, whereas Levene’s test was conducted to assess the homogeneity of variances. Parametric tests (e.g., independent-samples t-test, one-way ANOVA) were used when both assumptions of normality and homogeneity were met. When these assumptions were violated, non-parametric alternatives (e.g., Mann-Whitney U test, Kruskal-Wallis test) were applied. This approach ensured the appropriateness of statistical methods for the distributional characteristics of the data. Descriptive data were presented using numbers, percentages, means, and standard deviations. Group comparisons were conducted using Independent t-tests, ANOVA, Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis, and Chi-square tests as appropriate. Pearson correlation analysis and linear regression analysis were used to examine the relationships between the scale scores. Effect sizes were calculated for all statistical tests to assess the practical significance of the findings, considering the relatively large sample size. Cohen’s d was used for independent-samples t-tests, eta squared (η²) for ANOVA, r for Mann-Whitney U, and epsilon squared (ε²) for Kruskal-Wallis. Effect sizes were interpreted according to established thresholds: Cohen’s d (0.2 = small, 0.5 = medium, 0.8 = large), eta squared (η²) (0.01 = small, 0.06 = medium, 0.14 = large), and r (0.1 = small, 0.3 = medium, 0.5 = large) [23]. For all tests, p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Due to the number of statistical tests conducted, we acknowledge the potential inflation of Type I error. However, no correction (e.g., Bonferroni) was applied in order to avoid overly conservative results that may increase Type II error risk. This decision was made in line with previous similar studies and the exploratory nature of this research.

When sociodemographic characteristics were compared with scale scores, statistically significant differences emerged in the ATFS sub-dimension scores with respect to gender, family income level, academic grade point average, willingness to choose the department, and concerns about post-graduation employment (p < 0.05). Female students scored significantly higher across all ATFS sub-dimensions than male students (d = 0.446). Students whose family income matched their expenditures reported higher scores in the positive and planned attitude sub-dimensions compared to those whose income was below expenditure level (η²= 0.013). Students with a grade point average of 3.0 or above scored higher in the planned attitude sub-dimension than those with a grade point average between 0 and 1.5 (ε²=0.000). Additionally, students who had chosen their department willingly had higher scores in the positive attitude sub-dimension and lower scores in the anxious attitude sub-dimension than those who had chosen their department unwillingly (d = 0.026). Students with anxiety about post-graduation employment had significantly higher scores in the anxious attitude sub-dimension than those without such concerns (η²= 0.028) (Table 1).

Regarding SAPS scores, significant differences were observed based on gender, part-time employment status, smoking status, educational level (associate vs. undergraduate), academic performance, and voluntary choice of department (p < 0.05). Male students (d=−0.450), part-time workers (r = 0.134), and smokers (d=−0.630) had significantly higher SAPS scores. Undergraduate students scored higher than associate degree students, and students with a grade point average between 0 and 1.5 had higher SAPS scores than those with a grade point average above 2.01 (ε²=0.000). Furthermore, students who chose their department reluctantly had higher SAPS scores than those who did so willingly (d = 0.227) (Table 1).

Substance abuse proclivity was identified in 47.7% of the sample. Students with a proclivity for substance abuse scored significantly lower in the ATFS positive attitude sub-dimension and higher in the anxious attitude sub-dimension compared to those without such proclivity (p < 0.05) (Table 3). In the correlation analysis between the mean scores of the scale, it was found that there was a very weak significant negative correlation (r=−0.102; p < 0.05) between the SAPS total mean score and the ATFS positive attitude, while there was a very weak significant positive correlation (r = 0.126. p < 0.05) with the ATFS anxious attitude sub-dimension (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, university students were generally found to have a moderately positive attitude toward the future. However, approximately half of the participants demonstrated proclivity for substance abuse. While substance abuse proclivities were lower among students with positive future attitudes, they were higher among those with anxious attitudes. Factors such as being male, working part-time, having a low-income level, being an undergraduate student, having a low academic grade point average, studying in a department unwillingly, and experiencing anxiety about future employment were identified as risk factors influencing both future attitudes and substance use proclivities. The effect sizes associated with these findings ranged from small to moderate and were interpreted cautiously in light of existing literature.

Future-oriented attitude refers to individuals’ cognitive frameworks that encompass their interests, perspectives, and concerns about the future [10]. In the present study, university students were found to have a moderately positive attitude toward the future. Although the literature includes only a limited number of studies on this subject, Toker and Bahadır Kalıpçı [24] found that university students demonstrated a moderately positive attitude toward the future, whereas Topçu [25] reported that Turkish adults exhibited a highly positive future-oriented attitude. Previous research suggests that positive attitudes toward the future not only enhance young individuals’ happiness and psychological well-being but also contribute to their active and productive participation in society [10, 24]. Moreover, future-oriented attitudes have been emphasized as a critical factor for building a successful life and for individuals to maintain a meaningful existence within society [26, 27]. These findings underline the importance of supporting young people in developing constructive and hopeful perspectives toward their future, especially during transitional periods such as university education.

This study also revealed that several sociodemographic factors influence students’ attitudes toward the future. Female students displayed more positive and planned, but also more anxious, attitudes compared to male students. The effect size was moderate, suggesting a measurable but not substantial difference between genders in their attitudes toward the future. Considering that the participants were students in health-related departments, the higher representation of women in the healthcare workforce and their stronger employment prospects may explain their more optimistic and structured future attitudes [28]. On the other hand, given that women are more likely to experience anxiety and depression [29], their higher levels of anxious future attitudes are also understandable. Although the effect size was approaching a moderate level, it still indicates that gender-related differences in future orientation are meaningful and worthy of attention in the psychosocial context.

Family income level, willingness in choosing a department, and anxiety about post-graduation employment also affected future attitudes. Although the difference was statistically significant, the effect size was negligible, indicating limited practical relevance. Students from low-income families had lower scores in the positive and planned sub-dimensions, consistent with findings that financial hardship can negatively influence mental health and future outlook [30, 31]. Similarly, employment-related concerns may foster fear and hopelessness about the future [7, 32], whereas perceived employability has been shown to contribute to more positive orientations [33]. In this context, labor market fluctuations and economic uncertainty may further intensify young people’s concerns about the future. In the current study, students with higher grade point averages were found to exhibit more planned attitudes toward the future. However, although the results were statistically significant, the effect size was negligible, suggesting limited practical significance. Additionally, students who had willingly chosen their academic departments reported more positive future attitudes, while those who selected their departments unwillingly displayed greater anxiety. The prospect of working in a desired profession may be associated with increased optimism, whereas being confined to an undesired career path may elevate stress and reduce hope. Although the effect sizes for these variables were small, they still point to a potential role of academic and socioeconomic factors in shaping students’ future outlook. Even small effects, when persistent, may influence motivation, self-efficacy, and long-term decision-making processes during critical transitions such as university education.

A striking result of this study is that 47.7% of participants were identified as having a proclivity for substance use. Although actual smoking, alcohol, and drug use rates were relatively low, this finding suggests that a significant portion of students are at risk. It is important to note that this result reflects a psychological tendency or predisposition toward substance use, as measured by the SAPS, rather than actual engagement in substance use behaviors. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as an indicator of risk tendency rather than confirmed actions and may be important for prevention efforts. Previous studies have reported varying levels of proclivity for substance use among university students. For example, Bayindir Cevik et al. [34] found a prevalence rate of 63.6%, while Sancak and Aktan [35] reported high proclivities for substance use in 44.3% of their sample. Similarly, Köşger and Altınöz [36] found that 34.1% of medical students exhibited high levels of proclivity for substance use. National-level studies consistently indicate that substance use tendencies among young individuals are alarmingly high. Although no international studies on proclivities for substance use have been found, the ESPAD [37] study, conducted in 35 countries, reported a decrease in smoking and drinking among youth but noted rising trends in cannabis use and behavioral addictions. These findings suggest that substance-related risks among youth may become more severe. Prior research has also shown that alcohol and tobacco consumption often begins in university years [38–40] and may increase susceptibility to substance use, underlining the need for sustained prevention programs targeting students.

The study also explored the factors associated with increased substance abuse proclivity. Male students exhibited higher scores on substance use proclivity than female students, with a moderate effect size indicating a meaningful but not substantial gender difference in substance-related risk tendencies. This pattern aligns with previous findings in the literature [37, 41–44]. This may be related to social norms that are more tolerant of male substance use and more stigmatizing toward female use [45], as well as higher sensation-seeking tendencies reported among men [16]. Beyond gender-specific expectations, broader cultural and societal norms in Türkiye also play a role in shaping young people’s attitudes toward both their future and substance use. Traditional family structures, community expectations, and strong cultural emphasis on academic and professional success may increase pressure on students. While social tolerance of substance use among males may contribute to higher proclivity, female students often face stricter sanctions [40]. Moreover, the anxiety stemming from employment uncertainties within a competitive socio-cultural environment may further influence substance use proclivities. These dynamics highlight the importance of considering culturally sensitive approaches in prevention strategies.

Part-time employment was also associated with higher substance abuse proclivity. Previous studies have suggested that working students, particularly those entering the labor market due to financial hardship or educational challenges, may turn to substances as a coping mechanism [39]. In this study, undergraduate students also showed higher proclivity than associate degree students. Although some studies report contrary results [41], it is known that high-stress roles in health professions, especially those involving direct patient care, can increase susceptibility to substance use [46]. In addition, in this study, students with lower academic achievement and those who did not willingly choose their department had higher proclivity scores. These individuals may experience greater dissatisfaction and stress, which could lead to substance use as a maladaptive coping strategy. The effect size was negligible, indicating limited practical relevance. Although the effect sizes of sociodemographic variables are modest in this study, they indicate a cumulative risk profile for substance use among students.

Crucially, the study demonstrated that students with positive attitudes toward the future had lower substance abuse proclivity, whereas those with anxious attitudes had higher proclivity. Regression analysis indicated that anxious attitudes were stronger predictors of substance abuse tendency. Although statistically significant, the predictive power was relatively weak, suggesting that other psychological, environmental, and social factors may exert stronger influences. Thus, attitudes toward the future should be considered as only one of many contributing variables. This may partly be due to the moderate internal consistency level of the Substance Abuse Proclivity Scale (α = 0.75), which could have slightly attenuated the observed associations. A review of the literature reveals a limited number of studies supporting this relationship. For instance, Sen [19] found that higher future expectations increased high school students’ self-efficacy against substance use. Similarly, Colak and Unal [14] reported a positive relationship between hopelessness and substance use tendencies. Other research has shown that hope and optimism reduce substance use [47, 48], whereas anxiety symptoms are positively associated with substance use [13, 49]. Given that anxious attitudes toward the future are more strongly associated with substance abuse proclivity, initiatives that promote students’ mental well-being, academic satisfaction, and career preparedness may help foster a more positive future outlook. Moreover, as this is a cross-sectional study, causal conclusions cannot be drawn. Nonetheless, the incorporation of future-oriented psychological interventions, such as hope-enhancement programs, career counseling, and stress management training, could be explored in future research as potential means of supporting students at risk.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. As a cross-sectional design, it cannot establish causality between future attitudes and substance abuse proclivity. Data were collected through self-report, which may lead to bias, particularly on sensitive topics such as substance use. Furthermore, since the sample consisted only of students from health-related departments, the findings may not be generalizable to students from other disciplines such as law, engineering, or arts. In addition, self-selection bias may have affected the results, as participation was voluntary and limited to students who chose to complete the survey. Moreover, because the survey was conducted online via Google Forms, students without internet access or sufficient digital literacy may have been excluded, potentially limiting the representativeness of the sample. Future studies with more diverse and representative student populations are recommended to enhance external validity.

Conclusion

While the students participating in this study were generally found to have positive attitudes toward the future, approximately half exhibited a proclivity toward substance use. It was observed that students with positive attitudes toward the future had lower substance abuse proclivity, whereas those with anxious attitudes had higher proclivity levels. Factors such as being male, working part-time, having a low-income level, being an undergraduate student, having a low academic grade point average, studying in a department unwillingly, and experiencing anxiety about future employment were identified as risk factors influencing both future attitudes and substance use proclivities. Although the effect sizes of sociodemographic variables in this study were modest, they indicate a cumulative risk profile for substance use and future outlook among students.

Substance abuse proclivities among university students represent a critical issue that warrants close examination. Given the significant personal, familial, and societal consequences of addiction, preventive interventions should be initiated before problematic use begins. Since university years are a high-risk period for substance initiation, early and targeted preventive efforts are essential for this population. Compulsory courses on addiction awareness, regular seminars, and educational programs should be incorporated into university curricula. In addition, psychological support services that aim to reduce students’ stress and anxiety should be strengthened, and students at risk should be referred to guidance and counseling centers or offered individual therapy when needed. Furthermore, helping students create post-graduation career plans, organizing seminars on employment opportunities, and providing academic support can enhance their sense of purpose and hope for the future. When university students, who are in a pivotal stage of their personal and professional development, can envision and plan their futures clearly, they are more likely to avoid serious problems such as substance abuse and contribute positively to societal growth and development.
