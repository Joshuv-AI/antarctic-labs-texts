Epidemiology, Mechanisms, and Treatments of Musculoskeletal Injuries in Beach Volleyball
Authors: Rishi Trikha, Luis Ornelas, Christian L Blough, Mia McCarthy, Orr Limpisvasti, Michael B Banffy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Beach volleyball is a globally popular sport that places unique biomechanical and tactical demands on athletes. Although there is some literature on the epidemiology of indoor volleyball injuries, the injury patterns associated with beach volleyball remain comparatively understudied.Purpose/Hypothesis:The purpose of this study was to characterize the epidemiology, mechanisms, and treatment of musculoskeletal injuries sustained by beach volleyball athletes. The authors hypothesized that knee and shoulder injuries would represent the most common injury locations and that the mechanisms of injury would differ from those reported in indoor volleyball.Study Design:Case series; Level of evidence, 4.Methods:A retrospective cross-sectional case series was performed utilizing an institutional electronic medical record and internal search engine from January 1, 2000, to December 31, 2025. Patient characteristics, anatomic injury location, mechanism of injury and treatment characteristics were analyzed. Injury distributions were compared between male and female athletes. Continuous variables are summarized using means and standard deviations. Categorical variables are summarized using counts and percentages.Results:A total of 175 injuries sustained in 160 patients were included in this study. Of these injuries, 102 (58.3%) occurred in male cases and 73 (41.7%) in female cases (P = .63). The most common injury locations were the knee (74; 42.3%), wrist/hand (29; 16.6%), and shoulder (25; 14.3%). The distribution of injuries by anatomic location did not differ significantly between male and female athletes in any anatomic location. The most common mechanism among all injuries was a noncontact twisting knee event while pursuing the ball (26.5%), followed by overuse injuries (16.2%), ball contact while blocking or spiking (16.2%), and landing from a jump (14.5%). Among injuries with available treatment data, 59 of 168 (35.1%) required surgical intervention, the most common of which were anterior cruciate ligament reconstructions (n = 21) and meniscal surgeries (n = 18).Conclusion:Our study demonstrated that knee injuries were the most common injuries sustained by beach volleyball players. Acute injuries frequently occurred while pursuing the ball, highlighting the unique movement and court coverage demands of beach volleyball. These findings contribute to the limited epidemiological literature on beach volleyball injuries and have the potential to guide future injury prevention strategies.Keywords: beach athlete, injury epidemiology, injury mechanisms, injury treatment, gender

Beach volleyball is a globally popular sport that has continued to grow at all competitive levels since its inclusion in the Olympics in 1996. 6 The sport is played with 2 athletes per team and requires rapid lateral and vertical movements on an uneven surface.8,23 There are different situational tactical styles as well as biomechanical differences between beach and indoor volleyball.23,24 Consequently, the musculoskeletal injury patterns sustained during beach volleyball may also be unique and potentially differ from those observed in indoor volleyball.13,14

Existing studies on beach volleyball injuries remain limited by relatively small sample sizes and low external applicability given the focus on predominantly female, high-level athletes.2,11,25 A study of professional beach volleyball players reported that acute time-loss injuries occur at lower rates than those in many other sports; however, overuse injuries of the back, knee, and shoulder represent a substantial cause of impaired performance. 2 Similarly, collegiate data demonstrated that beach volleyball players sustained relatively more shoulder overuse injuries and abdominal muscle strains.11,25 Potential explanations include the increased number of overhead ball contacts required in a 2-player format, greater reliance on digging and diving, and biomechanical differences between beach and indoor volleyball. 25 Compared with indoor volleyball, beach volleyball players demonstrate reduced jump height, reduced joint transmission and energy return from sand, and reduced horizontal velocity during the approach for jumps and lateral movements. 24 Consistent with these differences, a 2021 study of National Collegiate Athletic Association Division I female athletes found that indoor volleyball players sustained nearly 3 times more injuries than beach volleyball players, although beach volleyball injuries were associated with longer recovery times, potentially reflecting the high physical demands of a 2-person team in an uneven, energy-absorbing surface. 11

While literature analyzing injury patterns in indoor volleyball injuries exists, particularly at the collegiate level, there is a documented need for literature on beach volleyball injury patterns given the relative paucity. 25 As a result, injury patterns, mechanisms of injury, and treatment characteristics among beach volleyball players at all levels remain incompletely characterized. Given the continued growth in participation across multiple levels of play, a more comprehensive understanding of injury characteristics becomes increasingly important for the everyday athlete and clinicians alike.

The purpose of this study was to characterize the epidemiology, mechanisms, and treatments of musculoskeletal injuries affecting female and male beach volleyball players across all levels of competition. We hypothesized that shoulder and knee injuries would be among the most prevalent injuries. We further hypothesized that injury mechanisms would differ from the reported mechanisms of indoor volleyball injuries.

Methods

A retrospective cross-sectional case series was performed at a single tertiary care, academic, multidisciplinary medical center after ethics approval from the respective institutional review board (STUDY00004527). The institution is a tertiary care academic referral center that provides orthopaedic and sports medicine care to recreational, collegiate, and professional athletes from a large metropolitan region. The authors’ institutional electronic medical record was queried using an internal search engine to identify individuals who sustained an injury related to beach volleyball from January 1, 2000, to December 31, 2025. All eligible consecutive beach volleyball–related musculoskeletal injuries identified during the study period were included, and no sampling or case selection beyond the predefined eligibility criteria was performed. The initial search was performed on January 7, 2026, and included the following terms: (“sand volleyball” OR “beach volleyball” OR “outdoor volleyball”) AND (“injury” OR “injuries”). This search yielded 217 patients.

To reduce potential sources of bias, the standardized electronic medical records of all patients were subsequently reviewed by 2 independent reviewers (R.T. and L.O.) to confirm eligibility. Injuries were included if they represented a musculoskeletal injury sustained during participation in beach volleyball that resulted in a medical evaluation by a physician or physician assistant. Nonmusculoskeletal injuries, injuries sustained outside of beach volleyball activity, and patients who had opted out of research participation were excluded. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved by a third reviewer (M.M.). A total of 57 cases were excluded due to injuries not occurring during beach volleyball participation, a nonmusculoskeletal injury sustained, or no actual injury sustained. The final series consisted of 175 injuries in 160 patients. Multiple injuries sustained at different time points by a single patient were recorded as separate injury events. All diagnoses were established by either a physician or physician assistant clinically from either an orthopaedic service line or a primary care service line, and, when indicated, imaging and/or operative findings were abstracted directly from the medical record. Age is reported as mean ± standard deviation.

Injuries were grouped by anatomic location, sex, mechanism of injury, treatment, and whether operative intervention was performed. Injuries were categorized into the following anatomic locations: shoulder, elbow, wrist/hand, chest, back, hip/groin, thigh, knee, lower leg, and foot/ankle. The most common injuries by anatomic location were further stratified by specific diagnosis. An injury that resulted in multiple diagnoses was recorded as separate diagnoses. Comparisons between sexes were performed.

When available, the following variables were also recorded: whether the injury was acute or chronic, mechanism of injury, hand dominance, treatment, whether surgical intervention was performed, and whether the injury caused time away from sport. Missing data were handled by complete-case analysis for the relevant variable.

Descriptive statistics are used to characterize injury patterns. Continuous variables are summarized using means and standard deviations. Categorical variables are summarized using counts, percentages, and corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Confidence intervals for proportions were calculated using the Wilson method and are presented for the primary descriptive injury distributions, mechanisms, and treatment characteristics. Chi-square tests were used to compare injury distributions by anatomic location between males and females as well as to assess differences in specific injury diagnoses within each anatomic location. When counts were <5, Fisher exact tests were used. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Version 29 (IBM). Statistical significance was defined as a P value <.05.

Results

A total of 175 injuries sustained in 160 patients were included in this study. Of these injuries, 102 (58.3%) occurred in male cases and 73 (41.7%) occurred in female cases. The mean age of athletes sustaining an injury was 40.5 ± 14.5 years. The most common injury locations were the knee (n = 74; 42.3%; 95% CI, 35.2%-49.7%), wrist/hand (n = 29; 16.6%; 95% CI, 11.8%-22.8%), and shoulder (n = 25; 14.3%; 95% CI, 9.9%-20.2%). The distribution of injuries by anatomic location did not differ significantly between male and female athletes in any anatomic location (Table 1).

The 2 most common knee diagnoses were meniscal tears (n = 22; 27.5% of knee injuries) and anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tears (n = 21; 26.3%). Neither diagnosis demonstrated a significant sex difference (P = .32 for meniscal tears; P = .29 for ACL tears). The mean age of a player sustaining a meniscal tear was 45.6 ± 15.3 years. Of the 22 meniscal tears, 4 (18.2%) were discovered as a chronic exacerbation of a preexisting condition rather than a single acute injury event. The mean age of a player sustaining an ACL tear was 38.6 ± 10.9 years. Of the 19 ACL tears with available mechanism data, 16 (84.2%) occurred due to a noncontact twisting knee injury while attempting to pursue the ball. Two were from a contact injury with another player while attempting to go for a ball, and 1 occurred while landing after a jump. All patients with ACL tears underwent ACL reconstruction. Fourteen ligament or capsular strains were seen including 5 medial collateral ligament sprains, 4 ACL sprains, 2 medial patellofemoral ligament sprains, 2 capsular sprains, and 1 lateral collateral ligament sprain. Six muscle or tendon strains were seen including 3 quadriceps strains, 2 hamstring strains, and 1 patellar tendon strain. Four injuries in 3 patients represented exacerbations of patellar tendinopathy, with 1 patient sustaining this injury at 2 separate time points. No significant gender differences were observed when evaluating all knee injuries collectively (P = .94) (Table 2).

Among injuries with available treatment data, 59 of 168 (35.1%) were treated surgically. The most common anatomic location for surgery was the knee (n = 43; 72.9%; 95% CI, 60.4%-82.6%), followed by the shoulder (n = 7; 11.9%; 95% CI, 5.9%-22.5%). The most common surgical procedure was an ACL reconstruction (n = 21), followed by 18 meniscal surgeries (10 partial meniscectomies and 8 meniscal repairs). Four surgeries for the shoulder were rotator cuff repairs, 2 surgeries were labral repairs for instability, and one patient underwent a subacromial decompression and distal clavicle resection. Regarding hand/wrist injuries that needed surgery, 1 proximal phalanx fracture required closed reduction and percutaneous pinning. Another proximal phalanx fracture presented in a delayed fashion and required open reduction internal fixation due to malunion. One extensor tendon rupture and 1 ulnar collateral ligament tear required surgery. Four surgeries were performed on the hip, all to address femoroacetabular impingement. Of the 109 nonsurgical injuries, 34 (31.2%) were managed with rest, ice, and elevation followed by physical therapy without bracing. Bracing or supportive wrapping was used in 24 of 109 (22.0%) nonsurgical injuries, 23 of which were accompanied by a prescription of physical therapy. Anti-inflammatory medications were prescribed for 49 (45.0%) nonsurgical injuries. Of available data on whether time away from sport was required, 127 of 160 (79.4%) injuries required some time away from sport. Among nonoperatively managed injuries that missed time, the mean time lost from sport was 31.4 ± 13.8 days, with a median of 36 days (range, 7-60 days). Among surgically managed injuries that missed time, the mean time lost from sport was 240.1 ± 57.0 days, with a median of 274 days (range, 212-365 days).

Discussion

The major findings of our work showed that the most common injuries sustained by beach volleyball players at a single institution over a 25-year period were knee, wrist/hand, and shoulder injuries, which together accounted for nearly 75% of all injuries. Knee injuries were most commonly ACL and meniscal pathologies, while wrist and hand injuries were most commonly fractures and sprains related to ball contact. The distribution of injuries did not significantly differ between genders across all anatomic locations, and the majority of injuries were managed nonoperatively.

Compared with indoor volleyball injury epidemiology, beach volleyball may demonstrate a distinct injury profile. Studies on indoor volleyball have reported ankle sprains as one of the most common injuries, frequently occurring during landing at the net.1,25 Conversely, ankle injuries were not as common in the current study, possibly owing to the shock-absorbing properties of sand as well as reduced player congestion at the net. Beach volleyball athletes are also burdened with covering a larger area per player. A standard beach volleyball court measures 128 m2 in surface area compared to an indoor volleyball court measuring 162 m2. Given that there are only 2 players on a typical beach volleyball team and 6 on a typical indoor team, the coverage area per player is much larger for beach volleyball. 7 Consistent with this increased coverage demand, the current study demonstrated that many injuries occurred while pursuing the ball, potentially reflecting the frequency and/or distance of defensive pursuits for balls. 2 It should also be noted that important demographic differences likely exist between indoor and beach volleyball players. The mean age of the current beach volleyball cohort is 40.5 years, whereas much of the existing indoor volleyball literature largely focuses on collegiate and youth players.1,11,15,19,25

There were 21 ACL tears in our patient series, which accounted for 26.3% of all knee injuries. ACL tears in indoor volleyball have predominantly been described as noncontact from landing after a block or spike action.5,19,22 The current study, however, demonstrated that an overwhelming majority of ACL tears occurred while pursuing the ball laterally rather than during vertical landing movements. One possible explanation is the relatively lower loads placed on the knee while landing on a soft surface. 17 Additionally, previous biomechanical studies have demonstrated that landing on sand typically occurs in less of a valgus moment than landing on firm ground, which may partially explain why ACL injury mechanisms in beach volleyball appear to differ from those described in indoor volleyball. 18 These findings suggest that preventative techniques may be beneficial in beach volleyball, especially given the effectiveness of neuromuscular training programs on noncontact mechanisms of ACL tears.10,21 Even if a formal program is not used, the everyday athlete should be aware of this ACL tear risk and can use a short warm-up before play, akin to the Fédération Internationale de Football Association 11+ soccer warm-up program.3,9

The second most common injury mechanism was ball contact while blocking or spiking. The majority of documented fractures occurred in the phalanges. It is suggested that improper finger positioning as a result of high ball velocity during blocking or spiking may contribute to fracture or sprain risk, a claim that is supported by the current study's findings. 15 While prophylactic finger taping and splinting are commonly used techniques to limit hyperextension moments, there is limited evidence demonstrating that this intervention truly reduces injury risk.9,16 As such, future studies should evaluate technique modifications and/or taping strategies as preventative measures to mitigate these injuries.

Shoulder injuries represented the third most commonly injured anatomic location in the current study, most commonly rotator cuff or subacromial pathology, with a substantial proportion representing chronic overuse injuries. This finding is consistent with recent volleyball literature underscoring overhead demands, particularly during serving and spiking, which generate high angular velocities and stress about the glenohumeral joint.4,20 The repetitive exposure to these high loads could lead to cumulative microtrauma, ultimately predisposing athletes to rotator cuff tears over time. 12 Furthermore, scapular dyskinesia, a history of shoulder injury, strength imbalance, and being a starter have been shown to be predisposing factors to injury. 4 The current study may support the substantial burden of shoulder pathology in beach volleyball athletes and underscores the importance of preventive strategies. The current findings are also consistent with indoor volleyball literature demonstrating that shoulder injuries are frequently related to chronic overuse injuries rather than a single traumatic event.16,20

This study has several limitations in addition to the inherent limitations of a retrospective single-institution study. As this was a retrospective cross-sectional case series, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive estimates of injury characteristics rather than measures of incidence, risk, or causal association. Although the current study found no sex differences between any injuries, the number of injuries reported is relatively limited, which restricts statistical power. The observational, tertiary care nature of this study introduces potential selection and reporting bias in that many patients are referrals. Furthermore, there is sampling bias against uncomplicated cases managed by trainers, therapists, and others whose treatment plans would not have shown up in our medical record. Because these data are purely observational, they do not yield any exposure information, meaning that frequency of injuries per exposure hours cannot be analyzed. Data were obtained from electronic medical records, meaning that treatment regimens may not have been as detailed as what had been discussed during a clinical visit. Additionally, as a retrospective cross-sectional case series, standardized longitudinal outcome measures and follow-up assessments were not consistently available. Missing mechanistic data were also present during chart review. This study did not include a comparison series of indoor volleyball players, limiting the ability to directly compare injury patterns between beach and indoor volleyball. As such, although comparisons to existing indoor volleyball literature provide context, they are hypothesis generating rather than definitive or mechanistic. Despite these limitations, the current study represents one of the largest and most widely applicable series evaluating musculoskeletal injuries in beach volleyball athletes to date.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that knee injuries were the most common injuries sustained by beach volleyball players. Acute injuries frequently occurred while pursuing the ball, highlighting the unique movement and court coverage demands of beach volleyball. These findings contribute to the limited epidemiological literature on beach volleyball injuries and have the potential to guide future injury prevention strategies.
