Background

Thalamoperforating artery (TPA) pseudoaneurysm are exceedingly rare and present significant management challenges [1].The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying pseudoaneurysm formation remain poorly understood. These lesions are characterized by the absence of a normal vascular wall structure, rendering them susceptible to rapid formation, enlargement, and potential rupture. Importantly, TPA pseudoaneurysms may not be visible on initial angiographic studies, necessitating repeated angiographic evaluations for accurate diagnosis. The therapeutic approach is complex, often involving craniotomy or endovascular interventions, both of which are technically demanding and associated with suboptimal outcomes, including the risk of permanent neurological deficits [2]. To date, electrocoagulation as a treatment modality for TPA pseudoaneurysms has not been documented in the literature. In this report, we describe a rare case of an acute TPA pseudoaneurysm scuuessfully managed with embolization through electrocoagulation endovascular therapy.

Case presentation

A 49-year-old male patient was admitted to our hospital presenting with headache and vomiting. His medical history included hypertension and prior heart surgery, but he had no history of medication use or familial genetic disorders. The physical examination was notable for neck stiffness as the sole significant neurological finding. Computed tomography (CT) and initial computed tomography angiography (CTA) identified a subarachnoid hemorrhage and an aneurysm of the left internal carotid artery (ICA), measuring approximately 3.0 mm × 3.5 mm × 3.2 mm (Fig. 1A and B). Subsequent digital subtraction angiography (DSA) confirmed the presence of the left ICA aneurysm and a variant arch, with no additional aneurysms detected (Fig. 1C and D, and 1E). Given the potential of the left ICA aneurysm to cause subarachnoid hemorrhage, surgical intervention was deemed necessary. We proceeded with an endovascular embolization procedure utilizing a stent and three coils. Post-procedural DSA demonstrated the successful obliteration of the aneurysm while maintaining patency of the left ICA and its critical branches (Fig. 1F). On the day following the procedure, the patient received an initial dose of aspirin (300 mg) and clopidogrel (300 mg), followed by a daily regimen of aspirin (100 mg) and clopidogrel (100 mg). The patient’s symptoms showed significant improvement and interval decrease in subarachnoid hemorrhage was observed (Fig. 1G). within the first few days post-treatment. However, three days post-surgery, the patient’s headache intensified compared to preoperative levels, and the patient’s blood pressure escalated to 187/85 mmHg. Emergency CT and vascular imaging identified a new subarachnoid hemorrhage and a TPA pseudoaneurysm, measuring 2.0 mm × 2.5 mm × 2.2 mm(Fig. 2A and B), which was not evident in the initial vascular scans. Immediate endovascular embolization was undertaken. DSA confirmed the presence of the TPA pseudoaneurysm; however, the associated vessel measured approximately 0.4 mm in diameter (Fig. 2C). During the interventional procedure, attempts to advance the Marathon microcatheter (ev3, USA) into the aneurysm cavity were unsuccessful. The microcatheter was gradually maneuvered to the proximal to the P1 segment of the posterior cerebral artery near the aneurysm’s parent vessel, and the Traxcess 14 guidewire (Microvention, USA) was positioned adjacent to the aneurysm. Subsequently, the guidewire was utilized as the stent wire and integrated with the Solitaire stent separation system (ev3, USA). Initially, electrocoagulation was conducted at 1.0 V and 0.05 mA for a duration two minutes; however, the aneurysm persisted. Subsequently, the procedure was extended for an additional three minutes, yet no change in the aneurysm was observed. The parameters were then adjusted to 1.0 V and 0.1 mA for two minutes, resulting in a reduction in the size of the aneurysm. Electrocoagulation was further continued fo approximately one more minute until the aneurysm was completely resolved (Fig. 2D).

The patient demonstrated a favorable recovery following this procedure, as evidenced by imaging evaluations, neurological examinations and scale assessments during follow-up. Follow-up assessments included CT and angiography (Fig. 2E-H) as well as telephone consultations. Over the course of one year of follow-up, the patient maintained a good recovery, exgibited no neurological deficits, and showed no recurrence of the aneurysm.

Discussion and conclusions

Aneurysms of the thalamoperforating artery (TPA) are exceedingly rare and pose significant challenges for detection through vascular imaging techniques. Presently, TPA aneurysms are recognized as complex vascular anomalies, with several case reports documenting their occurrence [2–5]. However, due to the paucity of data from large-scale studies, the precise incidence rate remains undetermined. A pseudoaneurysm is a specific type of aneurysm characterized by damage to the entire vessel wall. The absence of a normal vascular wall structure in pseudoaneurysms predisposes them to rapid formation, enlargement, and potential rupture. The mechanisms underlying pseudoaneurysm formation are not fully understood; however, four primary theories have been proposed regarding its pathogenesis: the vascular injury theory, the long-term radiation-induced nerve cell death theory, the autoimmune response theory, and the free radical damage theory [6]. Additionally, factors such as acute intractable hypertension, atherosclerosis, and certain drug use are considered to increase susceptibility to pseudoaneurysm development [7]– [8]. The patient presented with hypertension, atherosclerosis, and had been administered antiplatelet aggregation and vasospasm-resisting medications. It is hypothesized that the patient developed a pseudoaneurysm due to several factors. Notably, the patient’s vascular wall was chronically compromised by early-stage atherosclerosis. In this instance, the patient had no recent history of trauma, and the initial surgical procedure did not approach the area of the TPA aneurysm, thereby excluding traumatic and iatrogenic causes. Furthermore, the TPA aneurysm was not identified during the initial surgery but emerged on the third day following the embolization of the left ICA aneurysm, indicating a rapid onset. Aneurysms typically occur in individuals aged 30–60 or older. Additional contributing factors included the acute deterioration of the vascular wall structure following a sudden increase in blood pressure, as well as the administration of antiplatelet aggregation and vasospasm-resisting drugs, and the presence of aortic arch variation malformation.The confluence of these factors resulted in the development of an acute pseudoaneurysm, aligning with the vascular injury hypothesis. However, further experimental validation is essential to comprehensively elucidate the underlying mechanisms. TPA pseudoaneurysms present a distinct management challenge due to the fragile nature of the pseudoaneurysm wall, which poses a significant risk of rupture and necessitates early detection. It is important to note that TPA pseudoaneurysms may not be identified during initial angiographic assessments, thereby necessitating repeated angiographic evaluations.

TPA pseudoaneurysms are among the most challenging vascular lesions to manage, as surgical and endovascular interventions in the affected arteries can be complex and may lead to both immediate and delayed complications. The literature on the treatment of TPA pseudoaneurysms is sparse and predominantly consists of isolated case reports [9–12]. Certain patients receive conservative pharmacological treatment due to the presence of perforating vessels that supply blood to neural structures. Nonetheless, recurrent hemorrhaging may occur during the acute or subacute phases, potentially leading to irreversible neurological impairment [6, 13–15]. To mitigate the risk of recurrent bleeding, surgical intervention on the ruptured vessel is imperative. Surgical options include craniotomy and endovascular therapy, with the latter being favored for its ability to access deep-seated lesions with minimal invasiveness [16–19]. Hamel et al. reported an instance where microcatheter navigation into the aneurysm was unsuccessful, necessitating surgical clipping instead of endovascular therapy, which subsequently resulted in tension pneumocephalus and midbrain infarction [20]. Peschillo et al. documented that three patients underwent endovascular treatment with flow-diverter stents, one of whom unfortunately succumbed to the procedure [21]. In conclusion, these surgical interventions present considerable challenges, and the outcomes are frequently suboptimal. In this instance, the TPA pseudoaneurysm measured 2.5 mm, while its associated vessel was approximately 0.4 mm in diameter. Consequently, multiple attempts to navigate the microcatheter into the aneurysm were unsuccessful, rendering the standard surgical approach challenging. Yuhua Jiang et al. successfully addressed a PICA aneurysm using electrocoagulation, as microcatheter access was not feasible [22]. We employed a similar electrocoagulation technique; however, this method presents potential limitations. One concern is that the heat generated by electrocoagulation may extend beyond the aneurysm wall, potentially affecting adjacent tissues and causing damage to nearby peripheral nerves. Additionally, there is a risk that thrombus formation or heat-induced adhesions may lead to vascular injury during the removal of the aneurysm catheter. Furthermore, the long-term efficacy of this approach remains uncertain. To mitigate these issues, we attempted to advance the microcatheter in proximity to the aneurysm’s parent vessel, positioned microwires near the aneurysm, and utilized the guide wire as a stent wire, integrating it with the Solitaire stent separation system. Electrocoagulation was conducted utilizing microwires to deliver heat and facilitate the electrocoagulation of the aneurysm. Various durations of heat application and electrocoagulation were employed to optimize treatment efficacy. Notably, post-procedure imaging revealed the absence of the aneurysm. The patient experienced no complications following this intervention and was monitored using CT and angiography. The patient has demonstrated a favorable recovery, with no recurrence of the aneurysm.

Electrocoagulation presents a viable treatment option in scenarios where a microcatheter cannot be navigated to the aneurysm. Nonetheless, several critical considerations regarding this endovascular technique must be addressed, including the regulation of heat magnitude and timing during electrocoagulation, the timing of catheter withdrawal, and the overall durability of the procedure. To date, the application of electrocoagulation for the treatment of TPA pseudoaneurysm has not been documented in the literature, and our findings are based on a single case study of this specific technique. Crucially, the long-term durability of this approach requires ongoing monitoring through follow-up assessments.

In summary, the detection of TPA pseudoaneurysms is challenging, necessitating repeated angiographic examinations. Pseudoaneurysms within the posterior circulation present distinct management challenges due to the fragile nature of their walls, which significantly increases the risk of rupture and necessitates intervention. The treatment options for ruptured pseudoaneurysms in small-caliber vessels are limited due to these unique challenges. Electrocoagulation treatment for TPA pseudoaneurysms has not been previously documented in the literature. Nonetheless, electrocoagulation may serve as a viable alternative when a microcatheter cannot be navigated into the aneurysm, although potential limitations must be carefully considered.
