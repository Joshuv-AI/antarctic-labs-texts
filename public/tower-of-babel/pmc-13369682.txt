Research on induction tempering heat treatment process of cutter ring of gradient hardness
Authors: Chu Shijun, Jiang Lei, Chai Huizhen, Zeng Jun, Xiong Guotao, Xie Houzhe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnder the complex geological conditions of high quartz content, high compressive strength and composite strata, the full-section hard rock tunnel boring machine (TBM) cutter ring is prone to fracture and wear when subjected to a large impact load, which seriously reduces the tunneling efficiency of the TBM and increases the construction cost. In order to solve the above problems, this paper aims to improve the hardness of the cutter ring while taking into account the high toughness of the cutter ring as the goal, based on the DFEORM to establish a gradient hardness cutter ring induction tempering heat treatment finite element simulation model, to study the tempering process parameters of the cutter ring hardness and toughness of the influence of the law, and through the induction tempering experiments on its verification, and ultimately get the edge part of the high hardness and high tenacity of the high performance of the gradient hardness of the cutter ring heart part. The results show that increasing the heating time, current density and current frequency can effectively expand the softening area of the cutter ring to improve the toughness of the cutter ring heart, and increasing the air gap can effectively reduce the softening degree of the cutter ring to improve the hardness of the cutter ring. The optimal combination of the induction tempering parameters is as follows: current density between 5.31 A/mm2 and 5.94 A/mm2, current frequency between 5 kHz and 10 kHz, induction tempering time between 6 min and 8 min, and air gap between 4 mm and 8 mm.Keywords: Disc cutter ring, Induction tempering, Finite element, Gradient hardnessSubject terms: Mechanical engineering, Metals and alloys

Introduction

TBM (Tunnel Boring Machine) is a large-scale integrated construction equipment, integrating tunnel tunneling, mucking, and support functions1–3. Disc cutters, which penetrate into the rock mass and roll to cut and break rocks as the cutter head rotates, are key and vulnerable components in the rock-breaking process of TBM4,5. The cutter ring, located at the outermost side of the disc cutter and the first to contact the rock mass6, is shown in Fig. 1. With the development and application of TBM technology, increasingly complex engineering environments pose higher challenges to the wear resistance and impact resistance of cutter rings. Traditional integral furnace heating methods for cutter rings inevitably sacrifice toughness while improving hardness, failing to enhance impact resistance. To reduce the core hardness of the cutter ring while maintaining high edge hardness and improving overall toughness, induction tempering heat treatment is adopted7,8. The gradient hardness cutter ring prepared by this method can effectively reduce cutter ring consumption, minimize cutter replacement time due to rapid tool failure, and avoid damage to other components caused by abnormal cutter failure, thereby enhancing the overall toughness of the ring and improving its resistance to fracture failure. Therefore, studying and optimizing the induction tempering heat treatment process for gradient hardness cutter rings is of great significance for improving tunneling efficiency and reducing construction costs and risks.

Physical and internal structure of the cutter.

Current research by domestic and foreign scholars mainly focuses on adjusting annealing, quenching, and tempering process parameters to obtain optimal microstructure and grain size, thereby improving the overall mechanical properties of cutter rings. Vishal Jagota et al.9 studied the effects of austenitizing temperature, tempering temperature, and tempering time on the wear behavior of H13 steel and proposed an RSM model to predict wear under different heat treatment parameters. Feng et al.10 established a heat treatment database for H13 steel with Ti addition using Deform-3D, simulating the evolution of temperature, phase transformation, and stress fields during water-cooled quenching, and emphasized the need for cooling processes with uniform stress distribution. Tarang Shinde11 found that cryogenic treatment helps prevent premature failure of H13 steel forging dies under mechanical and thermal stresses, reducing carbide particle size and wear coefficient by 51% compared to traditional treatments. Deirmina et al.12 compared direct tempering and quenching before tempering for laser-deposited the H13 steel, revealing better high-temperature tempering softening resistance in directly tempered specimens. Lopez et al.13 analyzed the effects of cryogenic treatment before pre-tempering on the microstructure and mechanical properties of H13 tool steel, finding that cryogenic treatment promotes martensitic transformation, refinement of martensite laths, and formation of fine carbides. Meshkabad et al.14 demonstrated that cyclic heat treatment improves mechanical properties, with the optimal scheme reducing grain size by 41% and significantly enhancing toughness.For preparing gradient hardness, methods such as plasma spark sintering, laser cladding, carburizing/nitriding/boronizing/aluminizing, electromagnetic induction heating, explosion hardening, in-situ solid-state diffusion, and ultrasonic shot peening have been adopted. Guo et al.15 achieved a 2.5 mm deep gradient hardness layer in titanium plates via explosion hardening. Lu et al.16 prepared a 102 μm gradient hardness layer on pure titanium by optimizing boronizing and aluminizing conditions. Li et al.17 used plasma spark sintering to fabricate 18 mm continuous symmetric gradient silicon nitride ceramics with hard edges and tough cores. Ni et al.18 developed 85 μm nanocomposites with gradient functions via two-step sintering under 30 MPa, studying the effects of fracture morphology and element distribution on mechanical properties. Bai et al.19,20 proposed in-situ solid-state diffusion to prepare 5 mm thick TiC-Fe gradient coatings with high hardness and interfacial bonding strength. Li Hongbo et al.21 strengthened and repaired H13 model steel with 3.5 mm thick laser-clad gradient layers. Shi Rengui et al.22 improved surface hardness to over 58 HRC and hardness gradient via carburizing, quenching, and induction hardening on gear shafts, with a theoretical hardening depth of 11 mm.

Recent research primarily relies on laboratory experiments for gradient hardness cutter rings, with limited numerical simulation studies on heat treatment processes. This paper therefore simulates the induction tempering process of gradient hardness cutter rings using finite element analysis, investigates the influence of tempering parameters on hardness, and verifies results via experiments using vacuum tempering furnaces. The findings provide a theoretical basis for selecting heat treatment parameters to ensure cutter rings have both high hardness and toughness.

In the tempering treatment of the cutter ring material, defining the transformation model of the microstructure is rather complex. As carbides precipitate, changes in their quantity and morphology lead to the precipitation of different phases, such as tempered martensite, tempered troostite, and tempered sorbite. Boundary conditions

To reduce simulation time and numerical calculation errors, this study uses the boundary element method in the induction module of DFEORM software to establish a finite element model for the induction tempering heat treatment of gradient hardness cutter rings. According to the three types of thermal boundary conditions, the outer surface of the workpiece is set as both the induction heating surface and heat exchange surface; for the induction coil, the current input and output surfaces are designated as induction heating surfaces, while the entire coil is defined as the heat exchange surface. The convective heat transfer coefficient of the workpiece surface is set, with an air convection coefficient of 20 W/ m °C. Since the workpiece may deform during heat treatment, DEFORM software requires defining non-deforming positions. In this simulation, the contact area between the cutter ring and the rotating equipment is selected as the region with zero deformation velocity, with its axial deformation velocity set to zero. The heat field in the simulation is controllable, allowing for a smooth transition of the microstructure of the cutting ring, thereby reducing the concentration of residual stress in the cutting ring.

Analysis of influencing factors of cutter ring tempering process

Effect of heating time on cutter ring hardness

With a current frequency of 10 kHz, current density of 5.94 A/mm2, and air gap of 4 mm, hardness distributions at heating times of 6 min, 8 min, and 10 min. The hardness distributions of the cutter ring under different heating times are obtained, as shown in Fig. 6.

Hardness distribution of cutter ring with different heating time.

As shown in Fig. 6, due to uneven heating during induction tempering of the cutter ring, the hardness softening region is mainly confined to the lower right corner when the heating time is 6 min. As the heating time extends to 8 min, the softening region expands to both left and right ends of the cutter ring core. When further increased to 10 min, the softening region significantly expands to cover most of the core area. The hardness exhibits a gradual increase from the core to the edge, which is consistent with the temperature distribution characteristics during the tempering process.

Figure 7 presents the radial hardness curve of the cutter ring. At a heating time of 6 min, the softening distance reaches 11.1 mm, and the minimum hardness decreases to 52.5 HRC. As the heating time extends to 8 min, the softening distance significantly expands to 20.9 mm, with the minimum hardness further decreasing to 38.3 HRC. At 10 min, the softening distance reaches a maximum of 29.1 mm, and the minimum hardness drops to 37.0 HRC. When the heating time increases from 6 to 8 min (a 33.3% increase), the softening distance increases by 88.3%; when increasing from 8 to 10 min (a 25% increase), the softening distance increases by 39.2%. This indicates a non-linear growth trend in the effect of heating time on the softening distance. At 6 and 8 min, hardness rises rapidly with increasing distance from the core, stabilizing gradually as it approaches the edge. At 10 min, hardness increases slowly with distance initially, then accelerates, and finally slows down near the edge.

Cutter ring hardness at different heating times.

Effect of current density on cutter ring hardness

The current frequency is defined as 10 kHz, the heating time is 8 min, and the air gap is 4 mm. The current densities are set to 4.69 A/mm², 5.31 A/mm², 5.94 A/mm², and 6.56 A/mm² respectively. The hardness distributions of the cutter ring under different current densities are obtained, as shown in Fig. 8.

Hardness distribution of cutter ring under different current densities.

As can be seen from Fig. 8, when the current densities are 4.69 A/mm² and 5.31 A/mm², the hardness of the cutter ring remains unchanged at 58 HRC. When the current density is 5.94 A/mm², the hardness at the right-hand side of the cutter ring core softens significantly, while the left-hand side softens slightly. When the current density is 6.56 A/mm², the hardness in most of the core area decreases significantly, and the hardness gradually increases from the core to the edge. The current density has a significant impact on the hardness distribution of the cutter ring. As the current density increases, the softened area of the cutter ring expands significantly.

Figure 9 shows the hardness variation curve in the radial direction of the cutter ring. It can be seen from the figure that the current density has a significant influence on the hardness distribution of the cutter ring. When the current density is increased to 6.56 A/mm², the softening distance further expands to 29.1 mm, and the minimum hardness is 37.0 HRC. As the current density increases from 5.94 A/mm² to 6.56 A/mm² (a 10.5% increase), the softening distance of the cutter ring hardness increases by 110.9%. When the current density is 5.94 A/mm², the hardness increases rapidly with the increase of the distance and gradually stabilizes when approaching the edge. When the current density is 6.56 A/mm², the hardness increases slowly with the increase of the distance at first, then the increasing speed accelerates, and finally slows down when approaching the edge. The current frequencies are set at 5 kHz, 10 kHz, and 15 kHz respectively. The hardness distributions of the cutter ring under different current frequencies are obtained, as shown in Fig. 10.

Hardness distribution of cutter ring under different current frequencies.

It can be seen from Fig. 10 that when the current frequency is 5 kHz, there is no significant change in the hardness of the cutter ring. When the current frequency is increased to 15 kHz, the hardness-softened region expands to the entire core area. Effect of air gap on cutter ring hardness

The current density is defined as 6.56 A/mm², the heating time is 8 min, and the current frequency is 10 kHz. The air gaps are set to 4 mm, 6 mm, and 8 mm respectively. The hardness distributions of the cutter ring under different air gaps are obtained, as shown in Fig. 12.

Hardness distribution of cutter ring at different air gaps.

It can be seen from Fig. 12 that as the air gap increases, the hardness-softened area of the cutter ring decreases significantly towards the lower-right corner. When the air gap is 4 mm, the hardness-softened area of the cutter ring is widely distributed in the core, showing obvious softening. However, when the air gap is increased to 8 mm, the hardness-softened area is significantly reduced, only appearing in the lower-right corner of the cutter ring, and the degree of softening is also correspondingly reduced.

Figure 13 shows the hardness variation curve of the cutter ring in the radial direction. As can be seen from the figure, when the air gap is 4 mm, the softening distance reaches a maximum of 29.1 mm, and the minimum hardness decreases to 37.0 HRC. When the air gap is 6 mm, the softened area shrinks to 14.0 mm, and the minimum hardness of the cutter ring is 42.1 HRC. When the air gap is 8 mm, the softened area continues to shrink to 11.1 mm, and the minimum hardness of the cutter ring increases to 50.8 HRC. When the air gap increases from 4 mm to 6 mm (a 50% increase in the air gap), the hardness-softened distance of the cutter ring decreases by 107.9%. When the air gap increases from 6 mm to 8 mm (a 33.3% increase), the hardness-softened distance decreases by 26%. The influence of the air gap on the hardness-softened distance shows a decreasing trend that is fast at first and then slow, which is consistent with the principle of induction heating and the temperature-field distribution of the cutter ring. As the air gap increases, the magnetic-field strength weakens, and the energy obtained by the cutter ring decreases. Therefore, the temperature-rising speed slows down, and the hardness-softened area decreases.

Analysis of optimization of induction tempering process parameters for tool rings

Research shows that when cracks propagate from brittle materials to ductile materials, there will be a noticeable crack arrest behavior as the material’s fracture toughness increases26. Therefore, through communication with the manufacturer and in conjunction with engineering practices, the following design guidelines for the gradient hardness of the cutter ring are proposed.

To ensure the wear resistance of the milling cutter, the hardness of the cutter ring edge within a 30 mm radius should be maintained in the high hardness range of 58-61HRC.

Based on the fact that crack propagation will exhibit significant crack-stopping behavior with the increase in material fracture toughness, the design of the knife ring hardness starts from 30 mm away from the blade edge and rapidly decreases in a gradient distribution along the radial direction to the center of the knife ring. In the area approximately 20 mm from the center of the cutter ring, the rate of hardness decrease should significantly slow down, or even remain relatively stable, to ensure the overall impact toughness of the cutter ring center is guaranteed, thereby enhancing its ability to resist fracture failure.

To prevent the ring from fracturing under complex working conditions and to achieve a long service life for the ring, the hardness of the ring’s core must be greater than 40HRC, and its toughness should be greater than 10 J/cm²27. This will effectively reduce or even avoid the risk of ring fracture failure due to excessive impact or thrust during the tunneling process.

Conclusions

This study aims to address the contradiction between the hardness and toughness of disc cutter rings and achieve the preparation of wear-resistant and tough gradient hardness cutter rings. Through numerical simulation and experimental verification, the influence of induction tempering process parameters on the microstructure and hardness of cutter rings was revealed, the induction tempering process was optimized, and the optimal parameter combination for induction tempering of cutter rings was obtained. The following conclusions can be drawn to guide the optimization of induction tempering heat treatment processes for gradient hardness cutter rings:

As the heating time increases, the hardness exhibits a gradual increase from the core to the edge of the cutter ring. With increasing current density, the softened area of the cutter ring expands significantly. As the current frequency increases, the hardness in the softened area of the cutter ring also gradually increases from the core to the edge. With an increase in the air gap, the softened hardness region transitions from covering the entire core to appearing only in the edge area, with a gradual reduction in the degree of softening.

As the heating time increases, the hardness exhibits a gradual increase from the core to the edge of the cutter ring. With increasing current density, the softened area of the cutter ring expands significantly. As the current frequency increases, the hardness in the softened area of the cutter ring also gradually increases from the core to the edge. With an increase in the air gap, the softened hardness region transitions from covering the entire core to appearing only in the edge area, with a gradual reduction in the degree of softening.

Based on finite element simulation and experimental verification, the optimized induction tempering parameter combination is determined as follows: current density between 5.31 A/mm² and 5.94 A/mm², current frequency between 5 kHz and 10 kHz, induction tempering time between 6 min and 8 min, and air gap between 4 mm and 8 mm.

It is worth noting that all the above conclusions are based on the assumption of “identical positions and uniform wear of the cutter rings”. In practical tunneling operations, cutters located at different positions on the cutterhead experience significantly different loading environments, including variations in normal force, rolling contact stress, and tunneling parameters. These differences may lead to position-dependent wear degradation and failure mechanisms28,29. Consequently, the proposed induction tempering strategy should be considered as a general design framework rather than a universal solution applicable to all cutter positions.

Future work will focus on investigating position-dependent degradation mechanisms and optimizing gradient property distribution for different cutter locations to further improve the reliability and adaptability of induction-tempered cutter rings under complex geological and operational conditions.
