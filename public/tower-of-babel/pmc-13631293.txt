Engineering moiré potentials in twisted bilayer MoS2 for selective single-molecule sensing
Authors: Mingchuang Zhao, Haoqi He, Zhijie Wang, Yichao Bai, Liang Chang, Linxuan Sun, Cong Wang, Jianxiang Gao, Wenbo Li, Xudong Liu, Tianyi Zhang, Jiahui Zhang, Na Zhang, Lin Gan, Bilu Liu, Jia Li, Mauricio Terrones, Yu Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwisted bilayer MoS2 forms a quantum-engineered sensing platform, where the moiré superlattice creates a configurable surface potential for molecular control. Here we present a periodically modulated landscape in bilayer MoS2 by twist-angle tuning, and use it for molecular trapping and selective sensing. The native 54 meV moiré potential confines aromatic molecules (e.g., rhodamine 6G, R6G; methylene blue, MB) at AA-stacking sites. Introducing extended vacancies deepens confinement to 238 meV, enhancing adsorption. Twist angle enables band alignment with molecules, driving charge-transfer resonances. This integrated design, moiré potentials, defect-enhanced trapping and band-aligned charge transfer, synergistically amplifies Raman signals, achieving single-molecule sensitivity down to 10−20 M. Importantly, the platform exhibits molecule-specific selectivity: R6G is optimally detected at 10° and 50° bilayer MoS2, while MB responds at 15° and 45°, demonstrating twist-angle encoding of molecular recognition. Our twisted MoS2 with extended vacancies establishes a moiré system as a versatile platform for selective molecular sensing.Subject terms: Sensors and biosensors, Biosensors

Introduction

When two layers of a two-dimensional (2D) material are stacked with a twist, they form a moiré superlattice, a nanoscale periodic structure that modulates the electronic landscape1–3. The distinct spatial configurations of different stacking regions impose varying local chemical potentials on charge carriers1,4–6, ultimately producing a periodic potential in real space that alters the electronic structure7–9. Crucially, lateral variations in this surface potential generate in-plane electric fields, which can act as nanoscale electrostatic traps for atoms or single molecules10. When the molecular size matches the period of the moiré superlattice11, these polarized electric fields can effectively confine the molecule, maximizing charge transfer between the substrate and the molecules. This establishes the foundation of moiré chemistry: the deterministic control of interlayer twist angles to modulate adsorption energetics, confinement potentials, and charge-transfer resonance, thereby enabling selective molecular adsorption and detection12,13. Despite this promise, the role of moiré superlattices in achieving selective molecular sensing, particularly for surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS), remains largely unexplored14. Elucidating how moiré chemistry governs molecule-surface interactions, for example with aromatic molecules like rhodamine 6G (R6G) and methylene blue (MB), is therefore critical. It offers transformative insights for quantum-enhanced sensing and nanophotonics.

Herein, we present an ultrasensitive and selective SERS substrate that leverages moiré chemistry through the strategic manipulation of twist angles in bilayer MoS2 (T-MoS2). By further engineering plasma-induced extended vacancies (TD-MoS2), we create active sites that dramatically enhance molecular adsorption and chemical reactivity at ultralow concentrations (< 10−15 M). These extended vacancies generate localized states near the Fermi level, while the twist-tuned moiré potentials synergistically optimize charge-transfer conditions. When combined with resonant Raman scattering (aligning the Fermi level to the molecule’s Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital- Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital, HOMO-LUMO) enables quantum-enhanced single-molecule sensing. We demonstrate selective detection of R6G at 10°- and 50°-TD-MoS2, and MB at 15°- and 45°-TD-MoS2, achieving a quantum-limited detection of 1 × 10−20 M. This work establishes moiré chemistry, integrating twist-engineered surface potentials with vacancy-mediated adsorption, as a transformative paradigm for quantum-confined molecular sensing.

Results

Plasma-induced extended vacancies in T-MoS2

As shown in Fig. 1a (The crystal structure was drawn using VESTA software15), monolayer MoS2 was synthesized via liquid -phase-precursor chemical vapor deposition (CVD), and the moiré structure of T-MoS2 was assembled via deterministic transfer (details are provided in Methods and Supplementary Figs. 1-5). A primary challenge for SERS applications using monolayer MoS2 is the absence of dangling bonds on the surface, which hinders molecular adsorption. To address this, we utilize twisted bilayers to generate periodic moiré surface potential. Additionally, plasma treatment was employed to create point and extended vacancies that only provide dangling bonds, thereby further enhancing surface reactivity (Supplementary Figs. 6-7). The structural modulation of TD-MoS2 was precisely controlled by varying the plasma treatment time. An optimal duration of 3 seconds at 35 W was identified, which introduced extended vacancies (Mo-centered extended vacancies, size about 0.5 nm) in the top MoS2 layer without damaging the bottom layer, as confirmed by high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images (Supplementary Fig. 8). Statistical analysis showed a maximum extended vacancies density of 1.07 × 1013/cm2 at this condition. At longer treatment times (e.g., 5 seconds), the extended vacancies coalesced, leading to larger nanopores and a reduced overall density, which ultimately penetrates the MoS2 layered structure. Low-temperature photoluminescence (PL) spectra show more pronounced bound excitons in plasma-treated MoS2, indicating their association with the introduced extended vacancies (Supplementary Fig. 9)16.

In addition to introducing extended vacancies in the top layer, the periodic moiré superlattice, acting as a reactive periodic potential, is clearly observable at 55°-twist-extended vacancies MoS2 (55°-TD-MoS2) as depicted in Fig. 1b. This moiré superlattice is composed primarily of AA and AB stacking regions, along with intermediate configurations. Notably, extended vacancies are predominantly formed in the AA regions, whereas few are observed in the AB stacking regions. Supplementary Fig. 10 indicates that the formation probability of extended vacancies in the AA region is about 3 times higher than that in AB regions. This selectivity arises because different stacking configurations in moiré superlattices (AA or AB stacking) exhibit different interlayer distances, which alter the defect formation energy17. By carrying out density functional theory (DFT) calculations, it is confirmed that the defect formation energy at AA regions is about 41 meV/atom lower than that at AB regions (Supplementary Fig. 11). Beyond modulating defect formation, the moiré superlattice itself has a tunable period that depends on the twist angle. As seen in Fig. 1c, the moiré superlattice size of 55°-TD-MoS2 is about 3.6 nm. The structure of TD-MoS2 was further characterized by selective-area electron diffraction (SAED). The SAED pattern in Fig. 1d indicates two sets of hexagonal diffraction spots with relative rotational offset, corresponding to a twist angle of 54.8° between the MoS2 bilayers, confirming the twist- angle deviation is within 1°.

Moreover, Fig. 1d also reveals the characteristic contrast of extended vacancies while preserving the underlying MoS2 structure, suggesting that the plasma treatment selectively disrupts the top MoS2 layer while leaving the bottom layer intact. Similar moiré and extended-vacancies features are observed for 50°-TD-MoS2 (Fig. 1e, f, twist angle 50.3°), which has a smaller moiré period of ~1.9 nm. Conversely, for 0°-TD-MoS2 (Fig. 1g, h), the moiré feature is absent due to lattice alignment, though the extended vacancies remain visible.

Figure 1i summarizes that the moiré period varies symmetrically around 30° due to the threefold rotational symmetry of MoS2. Consequently, twist angles θ and 60°-θ (e.g., 10° and 50°, or 15° and 45°) yield similar superlattice sizes, consistent with STEM data and previously reported similarities in interlayer coupling and optical spectra17,18.

Statistical analysis of extended vacancies diameters shows no significant correlation with the twist angle (Fig. 1j), but a pronounced dependence on plasma treatment duration (Supplementary Fig. 12). This indicates that under constant treatment conditions (35 W, 3 seconds), the plasma-induced extended-vacancies in the top MoS2 layer are dominated by a characteristic size of ~0.5 nm. We then investigated the extended-vacancy distribution across different twist angles under the same processing conditions. The lowest extended-vacancy concentration was found at 0°, whereas 50°-TD-MoS2 contained the highest concentration (Supplementary Fig. 13). This trend correlates with the areal proportion of AA-stacking regions, which is largest at 50° and facilitates vacancy aggregation, in agreement with our DFT calculations and AA-region vacancy statistics (Supplementary Figs. 10,11). Excellent reproducibility was confirmed across different sample batches, with consistent extended-vacancy densities (Batch 1: 1.12 × 1013/cm2; Batch 2: 1.21 × 1013/cm2; Batch 3: 1.09 × 1013/cm2; Supplementary Fig. 14). Our DFT calculations further reveal that introducing extended vacancies significantly alters the density of states of the top MoS2 layer, indicating effective modulation of its electronic structure to enhance charge transfer with adsorbed molecules (Supplementary Fig. 15).

Figure 1k shows the resonance Raman spectra of 50°-TD-MoS2 (with extended vacancies) and 50°-T-MoS2 (without extended vacancies). The 50°-T-MoS2 spectra show prominent Raman peaks at 227 cm−1 and 387 cm−1 corresponding to the LA band and E2g mode19. For 50°-TD-MoS2, the LA/E2g intensity ratio increases after plasma treatment, which is attributed to the higher extended-vacancies content in 50°-TD-MoS220,21.

In summary, these results demonstrate that moiré superlattices of different periods can be formed by adjusting the interlayer twist angle, while a fixed plasma treatment can introduce a similar type of extended vacancy across surfaces with different twists. Our DFT calculations indicate that these vacancies cause n-type doping in MoS2, which is expected to improve the adsorption of aromatic molecules such as R6G and MB.

Selective single-molecule SERS detection based on TD-MoS2

Typically, the Raman scattering cross-section of molecules is weak, but the signal can be enhanced by several orders of magnitude when the laser energy approaches the molecular HOMO-LUMO gap (resonance Raman scattering, RRS). This RRS condition enables ultrasensitive detection of low-concentration species22. Leveraging RRS, Fig. 2a identifies the optimal excitation wavelengths: 532 nm for R6G (2.33 eV, close to its ~2.30 eV HOMO-LUMO gap) and 633 nm for MB (~ 1.96 eV, close to its ~1.71 eV gap). To demonstrate the selective SERS detection of the moiré pattern, we performed Raman spectroscopy on T-MoS2 prior to plasma treatment. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 16, with R6G as the target molecule, stronger Raman signals were detected on 10°-T-MoS2 and 50°-T-MoS2; with MB as the target molecule, stronger Raman signals were detected on 15°-T- MoS2 and 45°-T- MoS2. This indicates that when the molecular size matches the moiré pattern, T-MoS2 could be used for selective detection. Subsequently, to enhance the sensitivity of the T-MoS2, extended vacancies were introduced into T-MoS2 for SERS measurement. Figure 2b summarizes the normalized SERS intensity at 1650 cm−1 for R6G on TD-MoS2 across different concentrations and twist angles23. At relatively high concentrations (10−7 M, 10−8 M), the signal is notably low for commensurate twist angles (0° and 60°). In contrast, the intensity increases significantly at incommensurate twist angles, particularly for 10° and 50° (Fig. 2b), indicating a strong twist-angle dependence of the R6G/TD-MoS2 interaction and highlighting the tunability of these systems. In addition, control experiments showed a 4-fold signal enhancement on 10°-T-MoS2 compared to 0°-T-MoS2. Furthermore, introducing vacancies (10°-TD-MoS2) provided an additional ~6-fold gain, resulting in a total enhancement factor of 25 (Supplementary Fig. 17).

To determine the limit of detection (LOD), R6G solutions from 10−6 M to 10−21 M were tested on 10°- and 50°-TD-MoS2. As shown in Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 18a, the LOD for R6G reaches 10−20 M. At this concentration, R6G signals were not detected in every measurement but with a finite probability (0.74%; Supplementary Fig. 19). Control experiments sequentially testing pristine 50°-TD-MoS2, ethanol (solvent) on 50°-TD-MoS2, and 10−20 M R6G on 50°-TD-MoS2 confirmed that the signals originated from the target analyte, not random contamination or solvent (Supplementary Fig. 20). No signal was detected at 10−21 M (the signal-to-noise ratios of Raman spectra were all <3), even after 8 hours of adsorption and eight SERS measurements (Supplementary Fig. 21), confirming an LOD of 10−20 M (single-molecule level). Supplementary Table 1 compares the LOD for R6G across various 2D materials, demonstrating that TD-MoS2 exhibits the lowest LOD, and showing sensitivity that is at least two orders of magnitude greater than that of other 2D materials24–27. The enhancement factor (EF) of 10°-TD-MoS2 was calculated to be 1.2 × 107 (bulk R6G as reference, Supplementary Fig. 22).

Single-molecule level detection was verified using the bi-analyte technique, which is an effective strategy to confirm single -molecule events in SERS detection (Supplementary Fig. 23)28. For the Raman mapping, R6G and erythrosine were selected, given that both analytes are resonant with the 532 nm excitation, ensuring comparable signal enhancement under identical conditions. At high concentrations (10−9 M Erythrosine/10−11 M R6G), signals from both analytes were observed. At lower concentrations (10−12 M/10−14 M), the map showed mixed events and single-molecule events. At ultralow concentrations (10−17 M/10−19 M), only isolated single-molecule signals appeared (Supplementary Fig. 23a, b). The corresponding spectra (Supplementary Fig. 23c) are statistically most likely from single molecules, confirming single-molecule detection capability on 50°-TD-MoS2.

A similar twist-angle dependence was observed for MB molecules (Fig. 2d). The SERS intensity at 1620 cm−1 of MB was weak at commensurate angles but strong at incommensurate angles29. The maximum enhancement for MB occurred at 15°- and 45°-TD-MoS2, also enabling single-molecule detection at 10−20 M (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 18b). Quantitative analysis of the SERS intensity versus concentration reveals two distinct linear regimes for both R6G (Supplementary Fig. 18c) and MB (Supplementary Fig. 18d): a steeper slope at higher concentrations (10−6 to 10−12 M) and a much shallower one below ~10−12 M extending to 10−20 M. This transition is characteristic of systems approaching the single-molecule limit, where the number of molecules per active site becomes minimal and the signal is dominated by discrete adsorption events30,31. The onset of single-molecule dominance below ~10−11 M was also corroborated by the bi-analyte statistical analysis (Supplementary Fig. 23).

To understand the selectivity, we correlated the moiré period with molecular dimensions. DFT-calculated sizes are ~1.8 nm for R6G and ~1.4 nm for MB (Supplementary Fig. 24)32, closely matching the moiré periods of 10°/50°-TD-MoS2 (~1.9 nm) and 15°/45°-TD-MoS2 (~1.4 nm), respectively (Fig. 1i). DFT-optimized configurations show that both molecules preferentially adsorb within the AA-stacking region of the moiré superlattice, with their benzene rings parallel to the surface (Supplementary Fig. 24). This suggests that tuning the twist angle matches the moiré potential well to specific molecular dimensions, thereby enhancing the van der Waals (vdW) interaction.

To verify twist-angle selectivity, we used mixed solutions of 10−8 M methyl violet (MV) and 10−9 M R6G. MV was chosen because both MV and MB have the same molecule size, but MV is resonant at 532 nm (similar to R6G) while MB is resonant at 633 nm. This ensures similar cross-sections, thereby guaranteeing that the signal changes are mainly due to the moiré superlattice. As shown in Fig. 2f, at 0°-TD-MoS2, the intensities of MV and R6G were nearly identical. However, when the twist angle is at 10°, the intensity of the R6G characteristic peak at 1650 cm−1 is apparently higher than that of the MV peak at 1620 cm−1. Furthermore, when the twist angle is turned to 15°, the intensity of the MV becomes higher than that of R6G. The Raman intensity variation of MV and R6G at different angles (0°, 10°, 15°) confirms that the moiré superlattice plays a role in selecting the molecules with comparable size, giving rise to detection selectivity. In addition, p-phenylenediamine (PPD, size about 0.5 nm) was selected for SERS measurement. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 25, the Raman intensity of the PPD does not exhibit periodic variations with changes in the twist angle. This is because the molecular size of PPD does not match that of the moiré superlattice. This result also provides indirect evidence that the moiré superlattice can selectively detect molecules of similar size. The 50°-TD-MoS2 substrate also shows excellent stability, with less than 20% signal drop after one month in air (Supplementary Fig. 26). In summary, the TD-MoS2 platform combines exceptional selectivity, stability, and ultrahigh sensitivity for diverse applications.

Raman imaging was employed to statistically assess the reliability of single-molecule detection by evaluating performance over a wider region and increasing the number of data points. Figure 3a–c show SERS maps (225 points each) of the characteristic peaks (1650 cm−1 for R6G, 1620 cm−1 for MB) at concentrations from 10−18 M to 10−20 M. The detection probability for both molecules depend strongly on the twist angle. Figure 3d, f present the statistical averages of detection probabilities across three distinct batches, with error bars displayed. At 10−19 M, R6G detection is highest on 10°- and 50°-TD-MoS2 (~1.63% for 10°, 1.78% for 50°), 2-4 times higher than at other angles. MB detection peaks at 15°- and 45°-TD-MoS2 (~1.85%), ~3 times higher than at other angles. At 10−20 M, finite probabilities remain (~ 0.74% for R6G on 50°-TD-MoS2; ~0.6% for MB on 15°-TD-MoS2), consistent with the spectral data in Fig. 2b, d. This confirms that twist-angle–defined moiré structures enhance molecular adsorption and SERS detection.

As expected, the detection probability decreases with concentration (Fig. 3e, g). For R6G on 50°-TD-MoS2, the concentration dependence follows a straight line on a log-log scale (R2 = 0.95; Fig. 3e), consistent with classical Gibbs thermodynamics at low concentrations33. A similar trend is observed for MB on 45°-TD-MoS2 (Fig. 3g). Measurement uncertainty at these ultralow concentrations is well controlled by Poisson square root law (error = 1/N, N is the total number of spectra with R6G signals in 225 Raman spectra per measurement)31,34. The simultaneous agreement with both classical Gibbs thermodynamics and Poisson statistics strongly validates the statistical reliability and rigor of our single-molecule detection measurements at ultralow concentrations.

SERS enhancement mechanism

The exceptional SERS performance of TD-MoS2, as illustrated in Fig. 4a, arises from a synergistic combination of three key effects which are attributed to chemical enhancement mechanisms: enhanced molecular adsorption via extended vacancies; moiré-potential-induced selectivity and optimized charge transfer; and resonant Raman scattering, RRS. Their interplay enables quantum-limited single-molecule detection.

Plasma-induced extended vacancies create dangling bonds that “anchor” probe molecules, significantly enhancing surface adsorption and enrichment from solution. To investigate this, we correlated the spatial distribution of vacancies with SERS signals. In MoS2, the intensity ratio of the LA and A1g Raman modes serves as a quantitative measure of vacancy density35. SERS mapping (monitoring the 1650 cm−1 R6G peak) and LA/A1g ratio mapping on 10°-TD-MoS2 show strong spatial correlation, confirming that extended vacancies act as primary adsorption sites for R6G (Fig. 4b, c). This enhanced adsorption was further quantified by UV-vis spectroscopy. The transmittance of R6G adsorbed on vacancy-engineered MoS2 (D-MoS2) at 567 nm was 2.2 times lower than on pristine MoS2, indicating a correspondingly stronger absorption and thus stronger molecule-substrate interaction (Supplementary Fig. 27).

The moiré superlattice creates a periodic surface potential that governs both molecular selectivity and charge-transfer efficiency. Differential phase-contrast scanning transmission electron microscopy (DPC-STEM) was employed to visualize and analyze the electric field distribution on the surface of two-dimensional materials36,37. The DPC-STEM image of 10°-TD-MoS2 in Fig. 4d reveals a periodic in-plane electric field in vacuum, with stronger polarization in AA-stacking regions than in AB regions (HAADF-STEM image in Supplementary Fig. 28). This field polarization, arising from the moiré-modulated charge distribution, directly influenced adsorption energetics38. Additionally, it was demonstrated that there are local variations in electric field strength around the extended vacancy by the Finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) (Supplementary Fig. 29). DFT calculations of R6G adsorption along a path crossing AA and AB regions (Fig. 4d) show a periodic variation in adsorption energy (Ead) synchronized with the moiré lattice (Fig. 4e). The AA regions provide a 54 meV deeper adsorption well than AB regions, preferentially confining molecules. The moiré period of 10°-TD-MoS2 (~1.9 nm) is slightly larger than the size of an R6G molecule ( ~ 1.8 nm), enabling effective confinement within an AA-centered superlattice cell. Importantly, introducing an extended vacancies within the AA region deepens this adsorption well by an additional 184 meV, resulting in a total confinement depth of 238 meV relative to the AB region (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 30). This “deep well” effect, combining moiré potential and vacancy engineering, robustly traps molecules. This size-matching principle extends to other molecules: MB molecule, ~1.4 nm, is optimally confined by the ~1.4 nm moiré lattice of 15°/45°-TD-MoS2 (Supplementary Fig. 31), explaining the peak detection probabilities at these angles (Fig. 3d, f).

The moiré potential also modifies the electronic structure39.DFT calculations show that the HOMO level of R6G lies ~52 meV closer to the Fermi level (EF) on 10°-TD-MoS2 than on 0°-TD-MoS2 (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 32). In addition, we examined the twist-angle-dependent evolution of the conduction- and valence-band edges. The calculated CBM exhibits only minor variations with twist angle, whereas the VBM is modulated more substantially. Specifically, the energy between the TD-MoS2 VBM and the R6G HOMO decreases from 218 meV at 0° to 150 meV at 10°, corresponding to a reduction of 68 meV. This reduced energy provides a more favorable interfacial band alignment, thereby promoting electronic coupling and photoinduced charge transfer (PICT) between TD-MoS2 and R6G40,41.Differential charge analysis confirms an increase in interfacial charge transfer from 0.373 e (0°-TD-MoS2) to 0.393 e (10°-TD-MoS2). A similar enhancement is observed for MB (Supplementary Figs. 33, 34). This is attributed to moiré-induced band tuning. The valence band maximum (VBM) of 10°-TD-MoS2 (−5.554 eV) is lower than that of 0°-TD-MoS2 (−5.486 eV) or 15°-TD-MoS2 (−5.533 eV) (Fig. 4g, Supplementary Fig. 35). Consequently, the energy gap between the MoS2 VBM and the R6G LUMO shifts to 2.154 eV for 10°-TD-MoS2, which is closer to the 532 nm laser energy (2.33 eV) than the gaps for other angles (Fig. 4h). According to Herzberg-Teller vibronic coupling theory, this alignment resonantly enhances the PICT transition (μPICT) from the MoS2 VBM to the R6G LUMO, significantly amplifying the molecular polarizability and Raman signal42. The coupling term (hVI) further facilitates intensity borrowing, promoting charge transfer. The precise, atomic-scale tuning of both adsorption potentials and electronic bands represents the core of the quantum-enhanced mechanism. The enhancement can be quantified via the Kramers-Heisenberg-Dirac relation:

The molecular polarizability component RIVK is related to the laser frequency ω and the charge-transfer transition frequency ωVK43,44. Therefore, by engineering the moiré band structure to bring ωVK close to ω, we can maximize RIVK. Given that the SERS intensity (ISERS) is proportional to the square of RIVK, this directly translates into a strong chemical enhancement effect.

Subsequently, we verified that in the MB system. As is shown in Fig. 4g and Supplementary Figs. 35, 36, after the moiré modulation, the defect level of 15°-TD-MoS2 (−4.543 eV) up-shifts when compared to the defect level of 10°-TD-MoS2 (−4.589 eV) and 0°-TD-MoS2 (−4.579 eV). Consequently, the energy difference between defect level MoS2 and HOMO of MB changes from 1.671 eV (0°-TD-MoS2) to 1.681 eV (10°-TD-MoS2) then shifts to 1.717 eV (15°-TD-MoS2). This means defect level of 15°-TD-MoS2 (1.717 eV) is closer to 633 nm laser excitation energy (1.96 eV). This result strongly matches our experimental findings and provides compelling validation of the molecular selectivity in the twist angle system.

The final amplification component is achieved by matching the laser excitation energy with the molecular HOMO-LUMO gap. As shown in Fig. 4i, the signal intensity of R6G under resonant excitation is more than 20 times that under non-resonant excitation, as 532 nm is resonant with its electronic transition45. Furthermore, the signal intensity of PPD is highest when excited at 532 nm. However, under excitation conditions at 633 nm and 785 nm, no Raman signal was detected even after extending the integration time (Supplementary Fig. 37). This indicates that the PICT occurs only when excited at 532 nm, the enhancement effect resulting from the substrate (TD-MoS2) was very slight.

In summary, the quantum-limited sensitivity of the TD-MoS2 platform is achieved through the synergistic action of vacancy-enhanced adsorption, moiré-tuned selectivity/charge transfer, and laser-wavelength-matched RRS, enabling selective single-molecule detection of R6G (at 10°/50°) and MB (at 15°/45°).

Discussion

In this manuscript, we have established a quantum-enhanced molecular sensing platform by synergistically integrating moiré chemistry, vacancy engineering, and RRS to achieve selective single-molecule detection. Moiré chemistry provides the foundational control: by tuning the interlayer twist angle, we tailor the period of the moiré superlattice to match specific molecular dimensions. This match creates a periodic potential that selectively modulates adsorption energetics, forming an intrinsic 54 meV energy well to confine probe molecules at the AA-stacking regions. Concurrently, the moiré potential optimizes the band alignment, promoting efficient charge transfer. Vacancy engineering dramatically amplifies this confinement. By plasma-inducing extended vacancies preferentially at the AA regions, we deepen the adsorption well to 238 meV. This nearly fivefold enhancement robustly locks molecules at the designed sites and strengthens molecule-substrate interactions, serving as the cornerstone for ultra-high sensitivity. RRS provides the critical final amplification. By exciting molecules with laser wavelengths resonant with their HOMO-LUMO gaps, we achieve maximum scattering efficiency. This intrinsic resonance synergizes with the moiré-tuned charge-transfer pathways, creating a doubly resonant condition that pushes the sensitivity to the single-molecule level, with a detectable limit of 10−20 M and a finite detection probability (~ 0.74%) even at this extreme dilution. The confluence of these three engineered effects, moiré-directed selectivity, vacancy-amplified adsorption, and resonance-driven signal enhancement, endows the plasma-induced TD-MoS2 platform with quantum-limited sensitivity, exceptional selectivity, and remarkable stability. The twist angle, as the central tuning parameter of moiré chemistry, directly governs the sensing performance, enabling a versatile and programmable substrate. This work demonstrates that precise atomic-scale control over 2D heterostructures can unlock unprecedented sensing capabilities. The TD-MoS2 platform thus emerges as a transformative tool for applications demanding ultimate sensitivity, from early disease diagnostics and environmental monitoring to neuromorphic computing and quantum information processing.

Methods

Synthesis of monolayer-MoS2

Monolayer MoS2 flakes were grown via atmospheric-pressure CVD. Two precursor solutions were prepared: solution 1 contained ammonium heptamolybdate tetrahydrate (AHM, (NH4)6Mo7O24, 99.999%, 0.01 g) dissolved in deionized (DI) water (10 mL); solution 2 contained sodium cholate hydrate (C24H39NaO5·xH2O, 0.2 g) in DI water (10 mL). For synthesis, freshly mixed Solution 1 (20 μL) and Solution 2 (160 μL) were mixed and spin-coated onto a clean SiO2 (300 nm)/Si substrate (6000 rpm, 1 min). The coated substrate was then placed in the center of a quartz reaction tube, with sulfur powder (2 g) positioned upstream in an alumina boat. The furnace was heated to 820 °C in 30 min and held for 20 min, while sulfur was vaporized at 190 °C to sulfurize the precursor film. Prior to heating, the tube was purged with Ar (500 sccm, 10 min). Throughout the process, Ar carrier gas was maintained at 80 sccm.

Synthesis of T-MoS2

Twisted bilayer MoS2 (T-MoS2) was assembled via a deterministic dry-transfer method. Briefly, a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamp was moistened with atomized water and brought into contact with a monolayer MoS2/SiO2 substrate, lifting the flake via capillary adhesion. The PDMS stamp carrying the monolayer was then aligned and laminated onto a second monolayer MoS2 substrate using a precision transfer stage. After the transfer, the stack was placed on a hotplate at 60 °C for 30 min to evaporate residual water, after which the PDMS was gently peeled off, leaving a twisted bilayer on the target substrate. To improve interlayer coupling and remove contaminants, the transferred heterostructure was annealed in a tube furnace at 200 °C for 2 h under Ar flow, yielding clean, angle-controlled T-MoS2.

Air Plasma treatment

The as-prepared T-MoS2 samples were treated in a plasma cleaner (CPC-F, CIF) with air as the process gas. The chamber pressure was set to 30 Pa, the RF power to 35 W, and the exposure time varied from 0 to 5 s. After treatment, samples were immediately immersed in probe-molecule solutions for subsequent SERS measurements.

Sample characterization

Optical and fluorescence images were acquired using an Olympus BX53M microscope. Raman and photoluminescence spectra were collected with a WITec alpha300R confocal Raman system. HAADF-STEM was performed on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Spectra 300 aberration-corrected TEM operated at 80 kV. AFM images were obtained in tapping mode on a Bruker Dimension Icon system. UV-vis transmission spectra were recorded on a Cary 5000 spectrophotometer at a scan rate of 120 nm/min.

SERS measurements

R6G, MB, and MV were dissolved in ethanol to prepare stock solutions (1 × 10−5 M), which were serially diluted to concentrations ranging from 10−5 to 10−21 M. For adsorption, a Si substrate (≈ 0.5 × 0.5 cm2) with the TD-MoS2 sample was immersed in 1 mL of the target solution, sealed, and incubated for 1 h (or 10 min for concentrations ≥10−11 M; see Supplementary Fig. 38). The substrate was then dried under a N2 stream. To avoid cross-contamination, samples were tested in order from the lowest to the highest concentration, with an intermediate ethanol rinse and N2 drying between each step. All procedures were conducted under dark conditions to prevent photobleaching. Raman spectra were acquired on a WITec alpha300R system with a 100× objective (NA = 0.9). The 520 cm−1 phonon peak of the Si substrate was used for intensity calibration. R6G was excited with a 532 nm laser, and MB with a 633 nm laser; the laser power was maintained at 3 mW to avoid heating. The integration time is 1 s.

SERS mapping measurements

For spatial mapping, TD-MoS2 samples were immersed in 10−20 M R6G solution for 1 h, then measured under 532 nm excitation (3 mW). Each map consisted of 225 spectra (integration time 1 s per point). Three sets of parallel experiments were carried out. For each set of parallel experiments, in order to avoid experimental error, samples from different batches were immersed in solutions from different batches. After measurement, the sample was rinsed with ethanol, dried, and sequentially re-immersed in solutions of higher concentration (up to 10−5 M) following the same rinse-and-dry protocol. MB mapping was performed analogously using 633 nm excitation. The step size was 0.8 μm; thus, each spectral point may correspond to signals from multiple molecules within the laser spot. All the Raman spectra were collected by WITec alpha 300 R system with the ×100 objective (NA = 0.9). The 520 cm−1 phonon mode from the silicon substrate was used for calibration.

Bi-analyte SERS measurements

We used the bi-analyte SERS technique to demonstrate the single molecule sensitivity of TD-MoS2. In brief, two types of analytes were mixed and immersed in TD-MoS2. We choose R6G and Erythrosine as dual analytes, both molecules being resonant molecules at the excitation laser wavelength (532 nm). And four mixed solutions with different concentrations were prepared as mixed solution 1 (10−9 M Erythrosine and 10−11 M R6G), mixed solution 2 (10−10 M Erythrosine and10−12 M R6G), and mixed solution 3 (10−12 M Erythrosine and 10−14 M R6G), mixed solution 4 (10−17 M Erythrosine and 10−19 M R6G). SERS mapping was performed, and a total of 225 Raman spectra were acquired. The laser power is 3 mW. The integration time is 1 s.

DFT calculation

All calculations based on DFT were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)46. The projector augmented wave (PAW) potentials47 and generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional were used for the electron-ion interaction and exchange-correlation energy48, respectively. The cutoff energy of the plane wave basis was set to 400 eV. The total energy and force convergence criteria were set to 10⁻5 eV and 0.01 eV Å⁻1, respectively. The vacuum layer of at least 15 Å was chosen to eliminate the interactions between the periodic images. The DFT-D3 method considered the van der Waals interaction between R6G molecules and sample substrates49. The adsorption energy for R6G adsorbed on sample substrates was calculated using Eads = ER6G/sample – Esample – ER6G, where ER6G/sample, Esample, and ER6G are the energies of R6G adsorbed on sample substrates, sample substrates, and R6G molecules, respectively. For moiré structure with defects, we used 542 atoms, which is a large supercell for DFT calculations. To obtain the accurate band alignment, we first calculated the overall band structures of TD-MoS2 with interlayer coupling instead of two separate monolayers. Subsequently, we obtained the projected band structures of the top and bottom MoS2 layers from the overall band structure due to the different layers. Similar band alignment can be observed in twisted bilayer WSe2 and twisted MoSe2/WSe2 heterostructures8,9.

SERS data analysis

All Raman spectra were normalized to the 520 cm−1 phonon mode from the silicon and baseline-corrected using a shape-fitting algorithm in WITec Project software (Supplementary Fig. 39). To quantify signal trends, the integrated area under the characteristic bands (1620-1670 cm−1 for R6G; 1600-1640 cm−1 for MB) was calculated for each spectrum. Detection probabilities were determined by comparing the signal in the analyte window to the noise level, estimated from the standard deviation of the intensity in a silent region (1975-2025 cm−1). A spectrum was considered a positive detection when the signal-to-noise ratio exceeded 3 (confidence > 99.7%). The detection probability at each twist angle was then computed as the fraction of positive spectra within the 225-point map.
