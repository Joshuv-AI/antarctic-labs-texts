A Conserved Lignocellulolytic Secretome with Quantitative Modulation in Leucoagaricus gongylophorus, the Mutualistic Fungus of Leaf-Cutting Ants
Authors: Gabriela Cejas-Añón, Freddy Castillo-Alfonso, Ulises Carrasco-Navarro, Jorge Valenzuela-González, Roberto Olivares-Hernández, Gabriel Vigueras-Ramírez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeucoagaricus gongylophorus is essential to the mutualistic relationship between leaf-cutting ants and their fungal gardens. Known for its ability to degrade complex plant polysaccharides, this fungus produces a range of CAZymes and FOLymes enzymes that have significant potential for biotechnological applications. We investigated the growth kinetics, enzymatic activities and quantitative secretome modulation of L. gongylophorus grown in submerged cultures containing glucose, carboxymethylcellulose (CMC), xylan or pectin as sole carbon sources. In the comparative analysis, glucose was the preferred carbon source for biomass accumulation reaching 8.6 g L−1 at day 35, whereas cultures grown on pectin showed limited growth. In the case of enzyme activity, pectin cultures had the highest values in endo-β-1,4-glucanase, pectinase and laccase activities, reaching up to 50.7 U L−1, 206.6 U L−1 and 97.3 U L−1, respectively. In addition, a quantitative proteomic analysis was performed across the different growth conditions and identified 290 proteins, of which 201 were assigned to the secretome. Presence–absence analysis revealed 93 proteins shared across substrates, including 43 consistently detected at both time points, while pectin cultures exhibiting a more diverse set of exclusive secreted proteins. A key difference between conditions was in protein abundance rather in terms of protein profiles, cellulolytic enzymes were preferentially enriched in CMC cultures, pectinolytic enzymes in pectin, and oxidative proteins in xylan-grown cultures. Based on our results, L. gongylophorus adapts to different polysaccharides primarily through quantitative regulation of a shared secretome, reflecting a finely tuned enzymatic strategy that is consistent with its mutualistic lifestyle.

Introduction

The success of sustainable and economically feasible second-generation biofuels production is based mainly on the optimal conversion of lignocellulosic biomass into fermentable sugars [1–3], requiring enzyme cocktails capable of acting on complex composition of lignocellulosic biomass [4–6]. Under this context, white-rot basidiomycete fungi are attractive due to their well-known enzymatic repertoire for degrading cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, pectin, and polysaccharides [7–9]. This capability relies on the secretion of a wide variety of carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) and fungal oxidative lignin enzymes (FOLymes). Among these organisms, Leucoagaricus gongylophorus has been selected to study its capability to produce enzymes. This fungus establishes a mutualistic association with leaf-cutting ants of the genera Atta and Acromyrmex [10,11]. In this system, ants deposit fresh plant material into fungus gardens that serve as substrate for L. gongylophorus. The fungus degrades plant polymers through the secretion of extracellular enzymes to support its own growth while simultaneously providing nutrients for the ants [12]. Recent analyses of fungal genomes and proteomes have identified enzymes with the capability of degrade cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin and lignin-related compounds [13–17].

This addresses the need to study the potential of enzyme production by L. gongylophorus which remain limited when compared with extensively characterized industrial fungi such as Aspergillus spp., Trichoderma spp., Pleurotus spp., or Neurospora spp [18–20]. More recent studies have focused on enzyme activity under specific conditions, such as solid-state cultures or fungus garden material [21,22]. However, comprehensive analyses of the secretome under defined and controlled growth conditions remain scarce.

In the present study, a comparative analysis was conducted of the secretome of L. gongylophorus grown in submerged cultures supplemented with four different carbon sources: glucose, carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), xylan, and pectin. These substrates are selected as these substrates correspond to major structural components of plant cell walls with different chemical complexity and biological accessibility. The comparisons are based on parameters such as biomass production, total extracellular protein secretion and the activities of key lignocellulolytic enzymes at two distinct growth stages. The results contribute to unraveling the dynamic regulation of the secretome and provide a basis for future proteomic and functional studies of this fungus with ecological and biotechnological relevance.

Materials and methods

Fungal strain

Leucoagaricus gongylophorus strain LEU18496, whose internal transcribed spacer (ITS) sequence (KJ419350.1) and whole-genome assembly (ASM2245721v3) are available in GenBank [11,23], was propagated on malt extract agar (MEA-LP) containing 20 g·L−1 malt extract, 5 g·L−1 bacteriological peptone, 2 g·L−1 yeast extract, and 20 g·L−1 agar, with pH adjusted to 5.0. Cultures were incubated at 27 °C ± 1 °C. After propagation, the fungal strain was lyophilized on filter paper strips using a FreeZone 1 freeze dryer (Labconco, Kansas City, MO, USA). These strips were used for long-term storage at 4 °C ± 1 °C and as inoculum for subsequent experimental procedures.

Submerged culture conditions

Submerged cultures were performed using the mineral medium described by Ezekiel et al. [24], with the following modified composition (g·L−1): (NH4)2SO4·H2O, 1.5; urea, 0.3; KH2PO4, 2; CaCl2·2H2O, 0.36; MgSO4·7H2O, 0.3; CaSO4·2H2O, 0.25. Trace elements were added at 4 mL·L−1 from a stock solution containing (g/L): FeSO4·7H2O, 0.015; MnSO4, 0.012; ZnSO4·7H2O, 0.013; CuSO4·5H2O, 0.0023; and CoCl2·6H2O, 0.0015.

The culture medium was adjusted to pH 5.0 and sterilized by autoclaving for 15 min. Cultivations were carried out in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 50 mL of mineral medium supplemented with 10 g·L−1 of one of the following carbon sources: glucose (CAS 50-99-7), carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC; CAS 9000-11-7), xylan (CAS 9014-63-5), or pectin (CAS 9000-69-5). Cultures were incubated at 27 °C ± 1 °C and agitated at 120 rpm in an orbital shaker (New Brunswick™ I26 incubator shaker, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany; 2.5 cm orbit diameter) for 42 days.

Sample collection and biomass quantification

For each experimental condition (substrate × cultivation time), two biological replicates were established, resulting in a total of 20 samples distributed across ten experimental conditions. Samples were collected on days 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, and 42 of flask cultivation. Samples were centrifuged at 12,410 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C using an 5810 R centrifuge (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) to separate the supernatant from the fungal biomass. The supernatant was used for enzymatic activity measurements and for protein extraction corresponding to the secretome.

The recovered biomass was washed with Milli-Q water and lyophilized for 8 h using a using a FreeZone 1 freeze dryer (Labconco, Kansas City, MO, USA). Dry biomass was quantified by determining the dry weight. In all cases, treatments were performed with technical triplicates for enzymatic assays and biomass measurements to ensure robustness of the experimental data.

Enzyme activity assays

Endo-β-1,4-endoglucanase, endo-β-1,4-xylanase, and pectinase activities were determined using enzymatic assays. Substrate and enzyme extract controls were prepared to confirm the absence of reducing sugars prior to the reaction. The reaction mixture, as described by [25], consisted of 500 µL acetate buffer (0.1 M, pH 4.5), 500 µL enzyme extract, and 500 µL of 2% (w/v) CMC, xylan, or pectin. Tubes were incubated at 40 °C for 50 min. Released reducing sugars were quantified by measuring absorbance at 540 nm using the DNS method [26] with a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Evolution 300, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madison, WI, USA).

Laccase activity was determined by oxidation of 2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), monitoring the increase in absorbance at 420 nm every 20 s for 20 min, using an extinction coefficient of 36,000 M−1·cm−1. The reaction mixture consisted of 100 µL of 5 mM ABTS, 2 mL of 0.1 M sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.5), and 600 µL of enzyme extract. The reaction was incubated for 10 min at 25 °C.

All enzyme assays were performed in triplicate. One unit of enzyme activity (U) was defined as the amount of enzyme required to release 1 µmol of reducing sugars per minute per liter of reaction mixture (U·L−1). In the case of laccase, one unit was defined as the amount of enzyme required to oxidize 1 µmol of ABTS per minute under the assay conditions. Specific enzyme activity was expressed as U per milligram of protein (U·mgprotein−1).

Statistical analysis and Gompertz model fitting for biomass production

The experiments were conducted in triplicate. Prior to statistical analysis, data were evaluated for normality and homogeneity of variances using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Bartlett tests, respectively. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to compare among more than two groups. Tukey’s post hoc test was applied when significant differences were detected, considering a significance level of p < 0.05. The maximum biomass production rate (Vmax) was estimated using the integrated Gompertz model, defined as Vmax = 0.368 α·k, where α corresponds to the maximum biomass concentration (g·L−1) and k represents the biomass production rate constant (day−1). Model parameters were obtained using OriginPro 8 SR0 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

Extraction of the secretome for proteomic analysis

Proteins in the culture supernatant were precipitated overnight at −20 °C using a solution of 20% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid in acetone and 0.14% (w/v) dithiothreitol, following the protocol described by Carrasco-Navarro et al. [27]. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was washed twice with acetone (first 100% (v/v), then 80% (v/v)). The final precipitate was solubilized in 300 µL of 5 mM Tris-HCl, 6 M urea, pH 8.0. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford protein assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

One-dimensional SDS-PAGE was performed using 12% polyacrylamide gels (Mini-PROTEAN TGX, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Samples were prepared by mixing equal volumes of Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) containing β-mercaptoethanol and heating at 95 °C for 5 min prior to loading. Electrophoresis was carried out at 150 V (PowerPac Universal power supply, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The BLUltra™ prestained protein ladder (Cat. No. PM001-0500) was used as a molecular weight marker. Protein bands were visualized by Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 staining (Cat. No. 6104-58-1).

Protein identification by mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)

LC–MS/MS analysis of the eight secretome conditions (four carbon sources, two cultivation times: days 21 and 35) was performed at the Institut de Recherches Cliniques de Montréal (IRCM), Canada. Prior to analysis, protein digests were re-solubilized in 1% acetonitrile/1% formic acid and loaded onto a self-pack C18 column installed on an Easy-nLC 1200 system (Proxeon Biosystems, Roskilde, Denmark). Mobile phases were 0.2% formic acid (buffer A) and 90% acetonitrile/0.2% formic acid (buffer B). Peptides were eluted using a two-slope gradient at a flow rate of 250 nL·min−1. The LC system was coupled to an Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid™ mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) via a Nanospray Flex ion source. Peptide ions were fragmented in the HCD collision cell and analyzed in the linear ion trap using a target value of 1e4 and normalized collision energy of 29 V for MS2 scans. Ions selected for fragmentation were dynamically excluded for 30 s after two MS2 events.

Secretome mass spectrometry data analysis

Raw files were processed using MaxQuant v2.6.8.0 (Andromeda search engine), using a curated Leucoagaricus gongylophorus (LEU18496) FASTA database generated from the genome assembly (ASM2245721v3) [23]. Trypsin/P specificity was used with up to two missed cleavages; carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, and oxidation of methionine and protein N-terminal acetylation were set as variable modifications. Precursor mass tolerance followed standard Orbitrap settings in MaxQuant: 20 ppm for the first search and 4.5 ppm for the main search after internal recalibration; fragment (FTMS) mass tolerance was set to 20 ppm. An Andromeda score cutoff of 40 was applied for modified peptides, and a 1% false discovery rate (FDR) was enforced at the PSM, peptide, and protein levels. Match-between-runs was enabled (alignment time window 20 min; match time window 0.4 min), and Label Free Quantification (LFQ) quantification required MS/MS evidence for comparisons. Mass recalibration was effective, with mean absolute deviations between 0.51 and 0.95 ppm across runs, consistent with a 4.5 ppm main search. The mass spectrometry proteomics data and search results have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD073048 [28].

Data processing

After processing in Andromeda, “proteinGroups.txt” files were curated and reproducibly analyzed in R v4.4.3. First, entries annotated by MaxQuant as contaminants and reverse hits were removed, and LFQ intensities were log2-transformed.

A strict presence criterion per condition was applied: a protein was considered present in a condition only if both biological replicates for that condition showed LFQ > 0. If at least one replicate had LFQ = 0 (or missing), the condition was classified as absent for that protein. Proteins not present in any condition (all conditions absent) were excluded from the quantitative dataset. This filtering was complemented with a minimum sequence coverage threshold of ≥ 5% to remove low-confidence identifications and to favor more robust quantification.

Normalization relied on the MaxQuant LFQ algorithm, and no additional mean or quantile normalization was applied. The resulting quantitative dataset served as the basis for statistical and functional analyses (family and pathway annotation) and for figure generation.

Results and discussion

Growth profile on different carbon source

Figure 1 illustrates the growth profiles of L. gongylophorus cultivated on four different carbon sources (Glucose, CMC, Xylan, and Pectin, each at 10 g·L−1) over a 42-day period, with sampling performed at regular intervals (days 0, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, and 42). When glucose served as the sole carbon source, L. gongylophorus displayed sustained growth throughout the experimental period, reaching a biomass of approximately 8.6 g·L−1 by day 35 without signs of decline. Model fitting using the Gompertz equation showed that glucose supported the highest maximum biomass production rate (Vmax = 0.274 g·L−1·day−1), consistent with the rapid assimilation of this monosaccharide. Prior to this study, the growth behavior of L. gongylophorus under glucose as the sole carbon source had not been clearly characterized. This is particularly relevant given previous reports indicating that some strains can produce polysaccharide-degrading enzymes even in the presence of glucose, suggesting that this sugar does not necessarily act as a strong catabolite repressor in this species [14].

Growth profile of Leucoagaricus gongylophorus in submerged cultures using different carbon source, carboxymethyl cellulose (⬤), xylan (◇) or pectin (☐), and glucose control (▲). Data were fitted to the Gompertz model (–-). Bars represent standard deviation.

In contrast, xylan-containing cultures biomass reached 6.7 g·L−1 by day 28, with a Vmax of 0.230 g·L−1·day−1, which placed xylan as the second most effective substrate in terms of biomass production. Cultures grown on CMC attained a maximum biomass of 5.93 g·L−1 by day 35, with a Vmax of 0.156 g·L−1·day−1, and growth remained relatively stable without a pronounced decline during the evaluated timeframe. Finally, growth on pectin was markedly lower, with a maximum biomass of 2.4 g·L−1 and a Vmax of 0.069 g·L−1·day−1, followed by a decline beginning at day 28.

Based on Figure 1, days 21 and 35 were selected as the actively growing phase and the stationary growth phase, respectively, and to identify enzyme activity patterns across cultivation. Most previous studies addressing the growth of L. gongylophorus have relied on measures taken at a single cultivation time, providing limited information on growth kinetics or temporal changes in biomass production. In contrast, Maya-Yescas et al. [22] monitored CO2 evolution as an indirect proxy for growth and reported Gompertz-fitted profiles that indicate active exponential growth around day 21 in submerged cultures. However, because CO2 production was followed only up to day 30, the potential onset of the stationary growth phase was not captured, highlighting the value of longer time-course analyses based on direct biomass measurements, as performed in the present study.

Figure 2 shows the total protein concentration detected in the secretome of L. gongylophorus cultivated on the different carbon sources at days 21 and 35. In this assay, and in contrast to the biomass accumulation trends observed in Figure 1, glucose acted as a negative control, exhibiting the lowest protein concentration at day 21 (189.89 µg·mL−1). At the same sampling point, the highest level of protein secretion was observed in pectin-grown cultures (536.62 µg·mL−1), followed by CMC (384.41 µg·mL−1) and xylan (385.02 µg·mL−1), with no significant differences between the latter two substrates. This pattern is consistent with the role of glucose as an easily metabolizable carbon source and a potential mediator of catabolite repression, whereas the complex heteropolysaccharide structure of pectin appears to induce increased protein release into the culture medium, despite not supporting high biomass accumulation.

Total protein secreted in the culture medium of Leucoagaricus gongylophorus grown on different carbon sources (carboxymethyl cellulose, xylan or pectin) and glucose as control at 21 and 35 days of cultivation. Bars represent mean protein concentration values, and white dots correspond to biological replicates.

The secretion profiles patterns at day 35 differ from the patterns at day 21 among the tested substrates. In pectin-grown cultures, total extracellular protein levels declined by approximately 33.8%, reaching 355.46 µg·mL−1, whereas xylan-grown cultures showed a reduction of 19.2% (311.16 µg·mL−1). In contrast, cultures supplemented with glucose exhibited an increase in protein secretion over time, reaching 670 µg·mL−1 and exceeding all other conditions. Protein levels in CMC-grown cultures remained relatively stable at both sampling points.

By performing SDS-PAGE analysis (see Supplementary Figure S1) it is possible to observe the differences in protein profiles. The analysis reveals protein bands spanning a molecular weight range of approximately 6.5 to 270 kDa. When the fungus grown on pectin the SDS-PAGE analysis displayed a more intense and heterogeneous banding pattern compared to those grown on the other substrates, indicating a higher abundance and diversity of secreted proteins. By comparison, when glucose is the substrate there are fewer detectable bands, consistent with a reduced diversity of proteins released under this condition. Total protein estimated by the Bradford assay may not be equally represented in SDS-PAGE, as band intensity depends on molecular weight distribution and staining efficiency and does not necessarily reflect total protein concentration.

Figure 3 illustrates the enzymatic activities of endo-β-1,4-glucanase, endo-β-1,4-xylanase, pectinase, and laccase based on different substrates at the two sampling time points. Endo-β-1,4-glucanase, was observed to have activity in all evaluated substrates and cultivation times, except for the glucose cultures on day 21 of cultivation. The enzyme activity reached its maximum value of 50.71 U/L at day 35 in cultures with pectin, contrasting with a value of 44.64 U/L in carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) during active growth at day 21. For the remaining substrates, an increase in enzyme activity was observed on day 35, when the fungus reached its stationary growth phase (as shown in Figure 1), including glucose, with a value of 20.75 U/L.

Enzymatic activity profile of endo-β-1,4-glucanase, endo-β-1,4-xylanase, pectinase, and laccase produced by Leucoagaricus gongylophorus using glucose, carboxymethyl cellulose, pectin and xylan, as the sole carbon source in each culture. Light gray bars represent values at day 21, and dark gray bars represent values at day 35.

The endo-β-1,4-xylanase activity was observed in all evaluated substrates and cultivation times, reaching a maximum value of 52.74 U/L in glucose at day 21 of cultivation, followed by pectin and CMC, with no statistically significant differences between them. When L. gongylophorus entered its stationary growth phase, in contrast to the behavior observed for endo-β-1,4-glucanase, a considerable decrease in enzyme activity was observed for each substrate, apart from the xylan cultures, where relatively higher values were obtained compared to those observed during the exponential growth phase for this carbon source. Regarding pectinase and laccase activities, maximum enzyme activity values were obtained when pectin was used as the sole carbon source at both cultivation times. It was observed that in glucose, very low pectinase activity levels were detected at day 21 of cultivation, with no activity detected at day 35. In the case of laccase activity, the behavior in this substrate was similar to that observed for endo-β-1,4-glucanase activity, with no enzymatic activity detected at day 21.

The pronounced pectinolytic activity observed in this study is consistent with the ecological role of L. gongylophorus. Schiøtt et al. [29] demonstrated that pectinases secreted by this fungus not only participate in the initial decomposition of plant material but also remain active during their passage through the digestive tract of leaf-cutting ants, contributing to the coordinated degradation of biomass within fungal gardens.

LC–MS/MS-based proteomic analysis of the secretome

LC–MS/MS analysis enabled the identification of 642 proteins at a 1% false discovery rate (FDR) across the entire search space. After rigorous filtering, the final quantitative dataset comprised 290 proteins, which were used for all subsequent downstream analyses. From this curated dataset, a total of 201 proteins were assigned to the L. gongylophorus secretome, of which 181 (62.4%) were classified as extracellular, 20 (6.9%) as transmembrane, and 89 (30.7%) as intracellular. In addition, the dataset includes secretome profiles derived from fungal cultures grown on a mixed carbon source consisting of pectin, xylan and CMC (P/X/CMC). Because this condition is not directly comparable to the single-substrate cultures examined in the present study and does not align with the primary objective of this work, these results are not discussed further, although they are considered relevant for future studies. Supplementary Table S1 (Excel file) contains the complete list of identified proteins, their LFQ intensities, subcellular localization, and functional annotation. A Pearson correlation analysis based on log2-transformed LFQ intensities (see Supplementary Figure S2) showed high similarity between biological replicates and, despite the use of only two biological replicates per condition, supports the robustness of the dataset through conservative filtering and statistical approaches.

The presence–absence analysis of proteins within the extracellular fraction (Figure 4) revealed a shared set of 93 proteins across all substrates in all carbon sources when both time points were considered. Within this group, 43 proteins were consistently detected across all substrates at both cultivation times (days 21 and 35), representing a temporally stable subset of the secretome that is maintained across the conditions analyzed, and it is also accompanied by a very limited number of substrate-specific proteins (ranging from two to five per condition). This low qualitative variability indicates that L. gongylophorus maintains a stable and functionally robust secretory repertoire in response to external stimuli independent of certain conditions. In this context, changes in carbon source appear to primarily modulate the relative abundances of a conserved set of proteins rather than inducing the secretion of entirely new enzymatic repertoires.

UpSet plot and Venn diagrams showing the presence–absence patterns of extracellular and transmembrane proteins in the Leucoagaricus gongylophorus secretome grown on different carbon sources (glucose, carboxymethyl cellulose, xylan, pectin) at two cultivation time points 21 and 35 days.

The substrate-specific proteins identified for each substrate are: glucose, PEBP (g2439.t1) and chitin-binding protein type 3 (g1502.t1); CMC, GMC oxidoreductase (g2731.t1) and stress-response protein (g5822.t1); xylan, GH10 xylanase (g4019.t1) and S8 protease (g5012.t1) which did not exhibit a coherent functional pattern or a substantial contribution to the total secretome. In contrast, pectin-grown cultures showed a higher number of exclusive proteins, including galacturonidase (g2772.t1), polysaccharide lyase (g2849.t1), GH92 (g3559.t1), glucosidase (g4731.t1) and mannosidase (g5450.t1), together with oxidative enzymes such as laccase (g1417.t1) and pyranose dehydrogenase (g5538.t1). The larger number and functional diversity of these proteins suggest that growth on pectin is associated with a more diverse extracellular protein profile compared to the other carbon sources. A complete list of the core extracellular proteins shared among all conditions, as well as proteins exclusive to each substrate and condition, is provided in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Table S2, S3, S4, S5).

The constant enzymatic repertoire observed in this study may reflect patterns described in the model proposed by Aylward et al. [30], according to which the plasticity of the mutualism between L. gongylophorus and leaf-cutting ants relies on a highly specialized functional cooperation: worker ants select, fragment, and condition the plant material, while the fungus maintains a preadapted and efficient secretome optimized for the degradation of partially processed biomass.

Complementarily, Khadempour et al. [31] showed that L. gongylophorus exhibits a flexible but non-expansive enzymatic response: when exposed to recalcitrant substrates (leaves or flowers), the fungus increases the production of endoglucanases and β-glucosidases, whereas in complex substrate mixtures prioritizes the most readily available carbon sources, maintaining the overall secretome composition while adjusting its global activity.

Temporal differential analysis of the secretome

To quantitatively assess the observed differences, the limma package was applied to log2-transformed LFQ intensities, comparing day 35 relative to day 21 for each substrate (Figure 5). Proteins were considered differentially abundant (DAPs) when meeting an FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1.

Volcano plots of the extracellular and transmembrane proteins in the secretome of Leucoagaricus gongylophorus grown on different carbon sources (glucose, carboxymethyl cellulose, xylan, pectin). Each dot represents a protein quantified at both time points with two biological replicates per condition. The x-axis corresponds to the log2 fold change between day 35 and day 21, whereas the y-axis indicates the –log10 adjusted p-value. Those proteins above the significance thresholds (|log2FC| ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05) were classified as differentially abundant. Positive log2FC values indicate higher protein abundance at day 35, while negative values indicate higher abundance at day 21.

The number of differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) was limited but highly consistent across substrates. In total, 63 proteins met the significance criteria (p value < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1) in at least one substrate. Among them, 11 proteins were recurrently detected across all four substrates, defining a conserved temporal core of the secretome. This shared set included acetylxylan esterase CE1 (g1000.t1), the cell wall protein Kre9/Knh1 (g1597.t1), PNGase (g1468.t1), RlpA-like DPBB proteins (g1456.t1 and g1457.t1), hydrolases GH127 (g1602.t1), GH79 (g1686.t1), GH27 (g1107.t1), and GH43 (g2294.t1), as well as a hydrophobin (g1128.t1). The persistence of this protein set across all substrates indicates that temporal regulation in L. gongylophorus primarily operates through quantitative modulation of a stable extracellular repertoire rather than through substrate-specific recruitment of new enzymes.

The volcano plots confirmed that L. gongylophorus modulates protein abundances in response to cultivation time and carbon source. This behavior allows us to define two clearly differentiated metabolic stages: an early expansive hydrolytic phase (day 21), dominated by carbohydrate-degrading enzymes, and a late oxidative–structural phase (day 35), characterized by an increased representation of oxidoreductases and proteins related to extracellular structure and maintenance.

In glucose-grown cultures, most differentially abundant proteins were enriched at day 21, with a clear predominance of carbohydrate-active enzymes. This group included β-galactosidase (GH27, g1107.t1), β-glucuronidase (g1686.t1), α-L-arabinofuranosidase (g1602.t1), GH43 hydrolase (g2294.t1), as well as a hydrophobin (g1128.t1). By day 35, the abundance of these enzymes decreased markedly, a pattern that is characteristic of carbon catabolite repression in basidiomycetes, where glucose availability limits the production of carbohydrate hydrolases and shifts metabolism toward energy conservation [32]. Despite this global repression, a limited set of proteins increased in abundance at day 35, including acetylxylan esterase CE1 (g1000.t1), the cell wall protein Kre9/Knh1 (g1597.t1), and a heme-thiolate peroxidase (g3394.t1). This pattern suggests that L. gongylophorus maintains a minimal oxidative and structural protein set required to preserve extracellular integrity, even under repressive conditions.

The response observed in CMC-grown cultures appeared to represent a transition between active polysaccharide degradation and a later phase dominated by enzymes involved in cell wall reorganization and extracellular matrix remodeling. During the exponential growth phase (day 21), endo-β-1,4-glucanases (GH5), cellobiohydrolases (GH7), and β-glucosidases were predominantly detected, whereas at day 35 there was an increase in CE1 (g1000.t1), RlpA-like DPBB (g1456.t1), PNGase (g1468.t1), the cell wall protein Kre9/Knh1 (g1597.t1), and a S53 peptidase (g2225.t1). In contrast, cultures grown on xylan as the sole carbon source displayed an opposite regulatory pattern, characterized by a sustained or increased abundance of hemicellulolytic enzymes at the later stage. Xylanase (g4019.t1), β-xylosidase (g2294.t1) and α-L-arabinofuranosidase (g1602.t1), together with CE1 (g1000.t1) and Kre9/Knh1 (g1597.t1), were maintained or enriched at day 35, along with a multicopper oxidase. A similar temporal progression resembles patterns were reported for Pleurotus eryngii, in which early growth stages are dominated by glycoside hydrolases and polysaccharide lyases, followed by a later accumulation of oxidoreductases involved in cellulose and lignin depolymerization [33].

Under pectin-grown conditions, protein abundance patterns showed a more marked temporal reorganization than those observed for CMC or xylan. Early pectinolytic enzymes belonging to the CE8, GH28, and GH35 families decreased over time, whereas by day 35 there was a marked increase in the RlpA-like DPBB protein (g1456.t1), the S53 peptidase (g2225.t1), GH43 (g2294.t1), and, most notably, a chloroperoxidase (g3394.t1). This pattern is consistent with previous observations by Presley et al. [34] who showed that white-rot fungi typically exhibit early pectinolytic pretreatment followed by a subsequent increase in oxidative enzymes, a behavior that closely matches the response observed in L. gongylophorus.

To further explore the substrate-dependent differences detected by the ANOVA, heatmaps were generated for the top 40 significantly different extracellular and transmembrane proteins at the two-cultivation sampling time (Figure 6). These proteins represent the most strongly modulated components across CMC, xylan and pectin cultures and allow visualization of coordinated quantitative shifts rather than qualitative presence–absence effects. At day 21, the heatmap reveals a clear substrate-driven profile of the secretome. Cultures grown on CMC are characterized by elevated abundance of canonical cellulolytic enzymes, including endo- and exo-cellulases from the GH7 family (g1755.t1, g2688.t1) and the GH6 cellobiohydrolase (g681.t1), together with β-glucosidases from GH3 and GH5 families. This profile is consistent with the classical cellulolytic strategy described for white-rot and saprotrophic fungi, in which GH6 and GH7 enzymes constitute the core machinery for cellulose depolymerization [35,36].

In contrast, pectin-grown cultures display a well-defined pectinolytic signature dominated by enzymes acting on homogalacturonan and rhamnogalacturonan domains. These include pectinesterase CE8 (g4982.t1), rhamnogalacturonan acetylesterases CE12 (g4491.t1), rhamnosidase GH78 (g851.t1), and pectate lyases PL1/PL4 (g2683.t1, g2834.t1), which reach their highest abundances under this condition. The coordinated enrichment of multiple pectin-active enzyme families mirrors responses reported for Aspergillus, Penicillium and Myceliophthora species growing on pectin-rich substrates, where early and concerted induction of PLs, CE8, CE12 and GH78 supports efficient pectin depolymerization [37–39].

Xylan-grown cultures exhibit a distinct profile enriched in specific glucosidases and accessory or oxidative proteins. Notably, the exo-1,4-α-glucosidase GH15 (g2724.t1) and the cell wall-associated protein Cerato-platanin (g4732.t1) show their highest abundances under this condition. Cerato-platanins have been implicated in cell wall loosening and substrate interaction rather than direct hydrolysis, suggesting a facilitating role during hemicellulose exploitation [40]. In parallel, several oxidative enzymes, including multicopper laccases and AA3/AA5 oxidoreductases, contribute to the xylan-associated clustering, pointing to an early reinforcement of oxidative processes in response to hemicellulose-rich substrates.

At day 35, the heatmap reveals a partially reorganized yet coherent substrate-dependent pattern. Many proteins that were differentially abundant at day 21 remain significant.

Cellulolytic enzymes from GH7 and GH6 continue to be preferentially enriched in CMC cultures, while pectin-associated enzymes such as CE8, GH105 (g1955.t1), PL1/PL4 and CE12 acetylesterases (g4491.t1, g2124.t1) maintain their highest abundances in pectin cultures.

Previous studies have shown that the utilization of pectin as a carbon source is strongly influenced by the metabolic pathways required for the assimilation of its degradation products. In particular, assimilation of pectin-derived carbon relies primarily on galacturonic acid metabolism, a multistep pathway involving D-galacturonate reductase (GaaA), L-galactonate dehydratase (GaaB), 2-keto-3-deoxy-L-galactonate aldolase (GaaC) and L-glyceraldehyde reductase (GaaD), which together convert galacturonic acid into pyruvate and glycerol for entry into central metabolism [41,42]. In L. gongylophorus, earlier studies reported that pectin can be rapidly degraded but more slowly assimilated [13], a pattern that is consistent with the secretome profiles observed here. The reduced growth observed in pectin cultures may therefore be related to the intrinsic structural complexity of this polysaccharide, which has been described as the most structurally and functionally complex component of plant cell walls [43] and to the metabolic demand associated with its processing. Insufficient expression or incomplete regulation of these metabolic components could lead to a functional uncoupling between extracellular pectin degradation and intracellular assimilation, potentially resulting in the accumulation of metabolic intermediates and cellular stress [44]. Together, these factors provide a plausible explanation for the decline in fungal growth observed after day 28, even under conditions where pectinase activity remained detectable.

The heatmaps (Figure 6) suggest that L. gongylophorus may maintain an overlapping set of extracellular proteins across the different polysaccharide substrates, while redistributing protein abundances in a substrate- and time-dependent manner. This quantitative reallocation of a shared extracellular protein repertoire, rather than extensive qualitative remodeling, mirrors patterns reported for other lignocellulolytic fungi [45–47] and may reflect an adaptive strategy shaped by the chemically heterogeneous plant biomass processed within the leaf-cutter ant symbiosis.

Conclusions

It is worth noting that most previous studies on L. gongylophorus have evaluated growth and enzymatic activity at a single cultivation time, generally at the end of the culture period. In this work, we examined two distinct growth phases, allowing us to relate biomass production, enzyme activity, and secretome composition over time under submerged conditions. Thus, the combined growth profile and enzyme activity data across two defined growth stages are reported here for the first time for this fungus.

The results indicates that biomass concentration and protein secretion are not necessarily coupled and their differences relies on the type of substrate, particularly, pectin promoted functionally diverse secretory response despite limited biomass accumulation, pointing to a strong induction of extracellular enzymes under this condition. This suggests that carbon assimilation efficiency, rather than hydrolytic capacity alone, constrains growth on pectin. In contrast, glucose favored sustained biomass production while comparatively repressing extracellular enzyme secretion.

Despite these substrate dependent differences, a portion of the composition of the secretome remained consistent. The main changes occurred in the relative abundance of shared enzymes, with hydrolases predominating at earlier stages and oxidative enzymes together with cell wall–associated proteins becoming more represented later in cultivation. Together, these findings indicate that L. gongylophorus adapts to chemically diverse plant polysaccharides through dynamic quantitative regulation of a stable extracellular repertoire. Notably, the strong secretory response observed in pectin-grown cultures highlights this substrate as a promising inducer of enzyme production and a relevant target for further biotechnological exploration.

From an ecological perspective, these results suggest that the ability of L. gongylophorus to maintain a consistent secretome while modulating enzyme abundance in response to different carbon sources may play a key role in its symbiotic relationship with leaf-cutting ants. In the fungus garden, where the substrate is chemically heterogeneous and continuously renewed, this flexible yet stable enzymatic system would enable efficient degradation of diverse plant materials. Such adaptability likely contributes to the stability and efficiency of the mutualistic interaction, supporting nutrient acquisition for both the fungus and its ant hosts.
