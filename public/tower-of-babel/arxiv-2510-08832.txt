Distinguishing between Black Holes and Neutron Stars within a Population of Weak Tidal Measurements
Michael Müller, Reed Essick

Abstract
We study the ability of tidal signatures within the inspiral of compact binaries observed through gravitational waves (GWs) to distinguish between neutron stars (NSs) and black holes (BHs). After quantifying how hard this measurement is on a single-event basis, we investigate the ability of a large catalog of GW detections to constrain the fraction of NS in the population as a function of mass: f NS ​ ( m ) f_{\text{NS}}(m) . Using simulated catalogs with realistic measurement uncertainty, we find that > O ⁡ ( 200 ) >O(200) events will be needed before we can precisely measure f NS f_{\text{NS}} , and catalogs of > O ⁡ ( 100 ) >O(100) events will be needed before we can even rule out the possibility that all low-mass objects are BHs with GW data alone (i.e., without electromagnetic counterparts). Therefore, this is unlikely to occur with advanced detectors, even at design sensitivity. Nevertheless, it could be feasible with next-generation facilities like Cosmic Explorer and Einstein Telescope.



I
Introduction

Tidal signatures are difficult to detect in gravitational waves (GWs) from individual compact binary coalescences (CBCs).
This poses a significant challenge if one is to understand the true nature of a specific event in terms of its matter content; that is, whether the binary contained a neutron star (NS) or only contained black holes (BHs).
Typically, the leading-order tidal signatures, often taken to be a smoking-gun signature of matter,

become increasingly difficult to measure when the component masses are large because larger masses lead to more compact stars and correspondingly smaller tidal responses.
This makes direct measurements of the maximum mass of non-spinning NSs (
M_{\mathrm{TOV}}
) through tidal effects during GW inspirals impractical for individual events, since the difference between the tidal response of NSs at
M_{\mathrm{TOV}}
and the BH limit of zero can be more than an order of magnitude smaller than the measurement uncertainty
Chen et al. 2020
.

However, this does not mean that we cannot extract information from a catalog of noisy tidal observations.
We use hierarchical Bayesian inference to combine many noisy observations of tidal signatures within a catalog of CBCs observed through GWs.
In our analysis, we make the simplifying assumptions that the overall mass distribution (comprising both NSs and BHs) and the equation of state (EoS) are known.
We then measure the fraction of the compact object population that is made up of NSs as a function of mass and determine how well this can be measured at different mass scales.
In reality, both the EoS and the shape of the overall population will not be perfectly known, and therefore our estimates are likely optimistic as they do not account for this additional uncertainty.

The leading-order tidal signature within CBC waveforms enters the phase at 5th post-Newtonian (PN) order as a linear combination of the tidal deformabilities of the binary’s components weighted by their masses

\tilde{\Lambda}=\left(\frac{16}{13}\right)\frac{(m_{1}+12m_{2})m_{1}^{4}\Lambda_{1}+(m_{2}+12m_{1})m_{2}^{4}\Lambda_{2}}{\left(m_{1}+m_{2}\right)^{5}}

(1)

where
m_{1,2}
are the masses of the binary’s components and
\Lambda_{1,2}
are their dimensionless tidal deformabilities, defined as

\displaystyle\Lambda=\frac{2}{3}k_{2}\left(\frac{R}{m}\right)^{5}

(2)

where
k_{2}
is the gravitational Love number (which depends on the EoS),
R
is the NS radius, and
m
is the mass.
While the precise functional form of
\Lambda(m)
can vary, the general trend is that a larger mass leads to higher compactness and a significantly decreased tidal response.
See Fig.
1
for an example.

We expect
\Lambda=0
for non-spinning BHs (see Refs.
Fang & Lovelace 2005
;
Damour & Nagar 2009
;
Binnington & Poisson 2009

Spinning BHs could either have
\Lambda=0

Gralla 2018
;
Charalambous et al. 2021

However, NSs should have
\Lambda\gtrsim O(10)

.
Because the maximum possible
\Lambda

\Lambda

\Lambda=0
for simplicity.

In Sec.

This also implies that the likelihood of observing many events with clearly identified components is low.
Sec.
IV

III
Prospects for tidal measurements with individual events

We estimate the measurement uncertainty on
\tilde{\Lambda}
for an individual event with the Fisher information matrix
Vallisneri 2008
.
In general, this only provides a lower-bound on the measurement uncertainty, but nevertheless allows us to investigate trends with, e.g., component masses and spins.

The Fisher information matrix is defined as an integral over all possible data (
d
) given some true signal parameterized by
\mathbf{\theta}^{\mu}
,

\Gamma_{\mu\nu}=-\int dd\ p(d|\mathbf{\theta}_{\mathrm{true}})\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}\ln p(d|\mathbf{\theta})

(3)

where
\partial_{\mu}=\partial/\partial\mathbf{\theta}^{\mu}
.
In the high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) limit, a systematic expansion of the likelihood produces a multivariate normal distribution for
\mathbf{\theta}^{\mu}
at leading order with precision matrix
\Gamma_{\mu\nu}
(see, e.g.,
Vallisneri 2008
).
We therefore approximate the likelihood as a Gaussian centered on the maximum-likelihood estimate (
\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}}^{\mu}
)

\ln p(d|\mathbf{\theta})\sim-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{\theta}-\mathbf{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}})^{\mu}\Gamma_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{\theta}-\mathbf{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}})^{\nu}\ .

(4)

and approximate the posterior distribution by assuming a Gaussian prior also centered on
\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}}^{\mu}
with covariance
P_{\mu\nu}
so that the posterior distribution has covariance

C_{\mu\nu}=(\Gamma+P^{-1})^{-1}_{\mu\nu}\ .

(5)

In the limit
P\rightarrow\infty
, we obtain the result assuming a flat prior on
\theta^{\mu}
.
Otherwise, the prior can impose limits that roughly approximate physical boundaries.

If the data is a sum of a signal
h(\mathbf{\theta}^{\mu})
and zero-mean, stationary, Gaussian noise described by a one-sided Power Spectral Density (PSD)
S\left(f\right)
, then the Fisher Information Matrix elements simplify to

\Gamma_{\mu\nu}=4\int df\frac{\mathcal{R}\{\partial_{\mu}\tilde{h}^{\ast}\partial_{\nu}\tilde{h}\}}{S}

(6)

where
(\cdot)^{\ast}
denotes complex conjugation,
\mathcal{R}\{\cdot\}
denotes the real part of a complex number, and
\tilde{h}\left(f\right)=A\left(f\right)e^{i\psi\left(f\right)}
(with
A,\psi\in\mathcal{R}
) is the Fourier transform of the signal
h
.
If we further assume that
\theta^{\mu}
only affects the signal’s Fourier phase, so that

\partial_{\mu}\tilde{h}=i\tilde{h}\partial_{\mu}\psi

(7)

then we obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=4\int df\left(\frac{A^{2}}{S}\right)\partial_{\mu}\psi\ \partial_{\nu}\psi

\displaystyle=\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}^{2}\left(\frac{\int df(A^{2}/S)\partial_{\mu}\psi\partial_{\nu}\psi}{\int dfA^{2}/S}\right)

(8)

after remembering that the optimal SNR (
\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}
) is defined as

\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}^{2}=4\int df\frac{|\tilde{h}|^{2}}{S}=4\int df\frac{A^{2}}{S}\ .

(9)

Note that
\Gamma\propto\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}^{2}
, and therefore louder signals (higher
\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}
) will naturally produce more precise measurements.

We adopt a PN waveform model based on
Poisson & Will 1995
and include tidal corrections to the phase
\psi\left(f\right)
taken from Eq. (12) in
Flanagan &
Hinderer 2008
.
The full expressions depend on a reference time and phase, the chirp mass (
\mathcal{M}=(m_{1}m_{2})^{3/5}/(m_{1}+m_{2})^{1/5}
), the symmetric mass ratio (
\eta=m_{1}m_{2}/(m_{1}+m_{2})^{2}
), two spin parameters (
\beta
and
\sigma
), and
\tilde{\Lambda}
.
We adopt a diagonal prior covariance matrix with finite marginal variances only for the mass ratio and spin parameters (
\sigma_{\eta}=0.25
,
\sigma_{\beta}=8.5
, and
\sigma_{\sigma}=5.0
)
Vallisneri 2008
;
Poisson & Will 1995
.
We also adopt the
Planck18
cosmology to relate detector- and source-frame parameters, although this difference is negligible for low-mass systems with current detectors
Essick & Holz 2024
;
Essick et al. 2025
.
Finally, we use the power-law approximation to the maximum likelihood EoS from
Essick et al. 2021b
;
Essick et al. 2021c
shown in Fig.
1
.

Equipped with the Fisher information estimate of the marginal uncertainty in
\tilde{\Lambda}

Fig.

at the approximate detection threshold (
\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}=10
; see also
Essick 2023

In both cases, only low-mass binaries have a reasonable chance of producing precise constraints on
\tilde{\Lambda}
for realistic
\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}

For high-mass NS and for NS-BH binaries, the ratio of
\tilde{\Lambda}/\sigma_{\tilde{\Lambda}}
can be extremely small unless
\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}

10^{3}

\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}>100

For high-mass NS binaries, this is because both components’ tidal deformabilities become small near
M_{\mathrm{TOV}}
.
For NS-BH binaries, this is because
\tilde{\Lambda}
effectively scales as
\tilde{\Lambda}\sim\eta^{4}\Lambda_{2}
, and small
\eta

\Lambda_{2}
for small-mass secondaries.

These estimates provide further quantitative evidence that it may be nearly impossible to distinguish between a NS and a BH near
M_{\mathrm{TOV}}
from adiabatic tidal measurements within CBC inspirals.
They also show that it will be easier to precisely measure
\tilde{\Lambda}
for low-mass binaries, and we may even be guaranteed to make a relatively precise measurement (
\tilde{\Lambda}/\sigma_{\tilde{\Lambda}}\gtrsim 10
) for detected binaries with
m_{1,2}\leq M_{\odot}

Crescimbeni et al. 2024
;
Golomb et al. 2024
.
Both effects suggest that it may be easier to measure the fraction of NSs in a population at lower masses than at higher masses, which we now confirm with a full hierarchical inference.

IV
Hierarchical Analysis

We now study whether imprecise measurements of the adiabatic tides can nevertheless be used to identify sub-populations.
We use catalogs of simulated CBCs and conduct hierarchical inference with a suitable population model.

Given a model for the compact object population, described by hyperparameters
\lambda
which in turn describe the distribution over single-event parameters
\mathbf{\theta}
, we model the astrophysical population as an inhomogeneous Poisson process with differential rate-density

\frac{d\mathcal{N}}{d\mathbf{\theta}}=\mathcal{R}p\left(\mathbf{\theta}|\lambda\right)\ ,

(10)

where
\mathcal{R}
is an overall rate and
p\left(\mathbf{\theta}|\lambda\right)
is the model for the population.
The joint probability density of detecting
N_{\text{det}}
events with data and parameters
\left\{d_{i},\mathbf{\theta}_{i}\right\}
is then

p(\left\{d_{i},\mathbf{\theta}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{det}}},\lambda,\mathcal{R}|N_{\text{det}})=p\left(\lambda,\mathcal{R}\right)\mathcal{R}^{N_{\text{det}}}e^{-\mathcal{R}P(\mathbb{D}|\lambda)}\prod_{i}^{N_{\text{det}}}p(d_{i}|\mathbf{\theta}_{i})p(\mathbf{\theta}_{i}|\lambda)\ ,

(11)

where
\mathbb{D}
denotes detection, and only detected events appear in the product in Eq.
11
.
The selection effects are accounted for in the population-dependent selection function

\displaystyle P(\mathbb{D}|\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int d\mathbf{\theta}P\left(\mathbb{D}|\mathbf{\theta}\right)p\left(\mathbf{\theta}|\lambda\right)\ .

(12)

where
P(\mathbb{D}|\theta)
is the time-averaged probability that an individual event described by
\theta
would be detected.
See, e.g.,
Essick & Fishbach 2023
and
Essick et al. 2025
for more discussion.

Conditioning on the observed data and marginalizing over both the rate (with
p(\mathcal{R})\propto 1/\mathcal{R}
) and the single-event parameters (
\left\{\theta_{i}\right\}
) yields a posterior for just population level parameters

\displaystyle p\left(\lambda|\left\{d_{i}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\propto p\left(\lambda\right)\prod_{i}^{N_{\text{det}}}\frac{\int d\theta p(d_{i}|\theta)p(\theta|\lambda)}{P(\mathbb{D}|\lambda)}

(13)

For the inference to proceed, we have to specify a population model (Eq.
10
).
We focus on the mass, spin, and tidal parameters.
Our central assumptions are that the overall compact object distribution is well-known and that it is a mixture of
BHs and NSs:

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{N}}{dmds}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}p\left(m,s|\lambda\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}\sum_{\mathcal{O}}p\left(m,s|\mathcal{O},\lambda\right)P\left(\mathcal{O}|\lambda\right)\ ,

(14)

where
\mathcal{O}
denotes the object type (either BH or NS).
To fully specify the population, we relate the object-dependent distributions
p\left(m,s|\mathcal{O},\lambda\right)
to the assumed total distribution,
p\left(m,s|\lambda\right)
(or, equivalently,
d\mathcal{N}^{\mathcal{O}}/dmds
to
d\mathcal{N}/dmds
).
A particularly useful quantity to parameterize the mixture is the ratio of the object-dependent rate-density to the total rate-density,

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{O}}\left(m,s|\lambda\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d\mathcal{N}^{\mathcal{O}}/dmds}{d\mathcal{N}/dmds}=p\left(\mathcal{O}|m,s,\lambda\right)\ ,

(15)

which corresponds to the fraction of objects at a certain mass (and spin and tidal deformability) that belong to a specific sub-population, which we call the (object) mass fraction.
We study
f_{\text{NS}}\left(m,s|\lambda\right)
and obtain
f_{\text{BH}}\left(m,s|\lambda\right)=1-f_{\text{NS}}\left(m,s|\lambda\right)
.

While we take the shape of
p\left(m,s|\mathcal{O}\right)
as given, we infer the shape of
f_{\text{NS}}\left(m,s\right)
from the data.
To understand the behavior at a basic level, we further assume
f_{\text{NS}}
only depends on
m

To generate this “mock PE,” we follow the same procedure as in

We make use of a likelihood model that assumes independent (truncated) Gaussian likelihoods for the post-Newtonian detector frame parameters of the GW waveform,

Mock posterior and likelihood distributions are obtained by first scattering the true detector-frame PN parameters of the event

\mathbf{\theta}^{(\mathrm{ML})}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{8}\mathcal{N}\left(\vartheta_{i}|\vartheta_{i}^{(\mathrm{true})},\sigma_{i}\right)

7

7

\vartheta_{i}

\mu

V
Discussion and Outlook

We investigate the ability of adiabatic tidal signatures from the inspiral of CBCs detected through GWs to determine whether a NS is present within the system.
In agreement with the literature, we find that this is generally a difficult measurement to make for typical SNRs and binary masses.
Interestingly, though, we find that we are almost guaranteed to make a confident detection of tidal signatures for sub-solar mass binaries even at low SNRs near the detection threshold (see also Refs.
Crescimbeni et al. 2024
;
Golomb et al. 2024
).

We then address the question of whether we can learn about the fraction of NSs at a particular mass scale from an entire catalog of CBC detections.
By simulating large catalogs of CBCs and combining them within a hierarchical framework, we find that it will generally be difficult to constrain
f_{\text{NS}}
precisely even with catalogs of
O(200)
events.
As expected, the best constraints are obtained at low masses, and the high-mass fraction (near
M_{\mathrm{TOV}}
) is only very weakly informed even by the largest catalogs considered.

More optimistically, we find that astrophysically interesting conclusions may still be drawn from more modest catalogs.
In particular, we can expect to confidently rule out populations of only BHs at NS masses with fewer than
O(100)
events.

These large catalog sizes are likely driven by a few effects.
First, tidal measurements are only likely to be informative for low masses, but GW selection effects favor heavier systems
Essick et al. 2025
.
As such, high-mass BNS and NS-BH detections (which are more likely) will increase the catalog size but provide very little information about
f_{\text{NS}}
.
We typically find that 74.3% of our detected catalogs have
m_{2}\leq m_{1}\leq 1.7\,M_{\odot}
(
\tilde{\Lambda}/\sigma_{\tilde{\Lambda}}\geq 0.32
at
\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}=10
) and only 15.9% have
m_{2}\leq m_{1}\leq 1.3\,M_{\odot}
(
\tilde{\Lambda}/\sigma_{\tilde{\Lambda}}\geq 1.49
at
\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}=10
).

Given the relatively low rate of observed BNS with low masses, it is therefore unlikely that we will reach sufficient catalog sizes to determine
f_{\text{NS}}
precisely, or even to answer the simpler question of whether NSs and BHs exist at the same masses, based on GW observations with advanced detectors.
However, next-generation ground-based GW interferometers like Cosmic Explorer and Einstein Telescope are expected to extend the detection horizon for such systems by a factor of
\gtrsim 10

Essick & Holz 2024
.
This could increase the detection rate by as much as a factor of
\gtrsim 10^{3}
.
As such, catalogs of
>O(10^{3})
BNS are not implausible for next-generation detectors, leaving open the possibility of measuring
f_{\text{NS}}
directly with GWs.
