Human iPSC‐Derived Brain Organoids: A Disease‐Oriented Evaluation of Modeling Fidelity
Authors: Bishwa R Pokharel, Abigail Dickerson, Sanjana G Shenoy, Mira S Ramasamy, Niska Majumdar, Paul P Cook, Shaw M Akula
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTwo‐dimensional (2D) cell culture systems and conventional animal models have been crucial in the study of central nervous system (CNS) pathology. However, they are limited in their abilities to elucidate human‐specific neurobiology and disease etiology. Human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)‐derived brain organoids have emerged as a complementary platform that offers a three‐dimensional (3D) structure and human‐specific environment. They exhibit cellular heterogeneity, apical–basal polarity, and the ability to perform tissue‐specific functions such as absorption, secretion, and electrophysiological activity. This review critically evaluates the use of iPSC‐derived brain organoids as a disease‐focused approach to study neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration, infection, and aging. Organoids provide a model for early pathogenic events, cellular interactions, and human‐specific responses, providing insights into disease hallmarks such as tau and amyloid pathology, the vulnerability of dopaminergic neurons, and host–pathogen interactions. However, they are limited by developmental immaturity, inadequate systemic integration, and heterogeneity across models, which hinders their ability to represent late‐stage disease progression and human level physiology. Therefore, their primary use lies in modeling human‐specific early disease mechanisms, while integration with animal models and in vitro systems is crucial for progressing translational neuroscience.Keywords: brain, CNS, iPSC, organoids

Introduction

Understanding central nervous system (CNS) pathology requires experimental models that accurately recapitulate human‐specific cellular architecture, molecular signaling, and disease progression (Nikolakopoulou et al. 2020). Historically, mechanistic studies have relied upon two primary modalities: two‐dimensional (2D) cell culture systems and animal models. Even though both have contributed critical insights into neurobiology, their translational limitations are now widely acknowledged (Neziri et al. 2024; Solana‐Manrique et al. 2025). Animal models help to study systemic physiology, including endocrine, immunological systems, and behavior, yet interspecies differences in inflammatory responses and gene regulation significantly limit their translational ability (Loewa et al. 2023). On the other hand, 2D culture systems provide controlled mechanistic studies but lack multicellular structure, extracellular matrix (ECM), and spatial gradients needed for modeling tissue‐level human brain activity (Roth et al. 2021).

Recent progress in stem cell biology has generated human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)‐derived brain organoids, as an intermediary platform between simplified in vitro systems and complex in vivo models (Lee et al. 2017). These three‐dimensional (3D), self‐organizing structures replicate crucial aspects of early brain development, including progenitor diversity, ventricular zone‐like architecture, and neuronal differentiation (Sidhaye and Knoblich 2021). Technological innovations have widened organoid systems from initial cerebral models to region‐specific organoids (e.g., cortical, midbrain, and hippocampal) and assembloids that integrate multiple brain regions and models that have vascular or immune components, hence improving their physiological relevance (Bagley et al. 2017). Regardless of the progresses, iPSC‐ derived brain organoids still remain as an inadequate model of the human brain. Majority of organoids still represent fetal or early postnatal developmental stages, restricting their ability for modeling late‐onset neurodegenerative disorders (Urrestizala‐Arenaza et al. 2024). Moreover, they are without systemic interactions, like circulating endocrine signaling, immunological responses, and complete vascular perfusion, all of which are essential components affecting CNS pathologies within in vivo systems. The variability across iPSC lines, culture conditions, and differentiation protocols further worsens the challenges of standardization and reproducibility (Kim and Chang 2023). These limitations emphasize that iPSC‐ derived brain organoids should not be seen as substitutes for conventional animal models but consider them as complimentary systems. Animal models consistently surpass organoids in areas such as extensive neuronal circuitry, whole‐body pharmacokinetics, and behavioral phenotyping, whereas iPSC‐derived brain organoids provide distinct advantages in replicating human‐specific genetic variations, developmental pathways, and cellular interactions (Eichmuller and Knoblich 2022; Smirnova and Hartung 2024). The integration of these platforms is crucial for enhancing translational relevance in brain research.

To address these issues systematically, we used disease and application‐centered approach, focusing on major categories of CNS pathophysiology, like neurodegeneration, neuroinflammation, and infection, and aging‐related processes. For each category, we assessed four essential dimensions: (1) available experimental models (animal, 2D, organoid‐based), (2) disease‐relevant phenotypes captured by each model, (3) unique insights offered by organoid systems, and (4) limitations and challenges of each model. This approach highlights the specific contexts in which organoids provide significant mechanistic or translational advantages while recognizing the vital roles of conventional models.

Experimental Models in CNS Research: Strengths, Limitations, and Translational Gaps

Experimental modeling of CNS diseases has depended on conventional animal models and 2D culture in vitro systems, both of which have unique advantages but also have significant limitations that hinder their translational relevance. A comparative evaluation of these systems is vital for placing iPSC‐derived brain organoids within the wider experimental context.

Animal Models: Strengths and Translational Limitations

Animal models, especially rodents (mice) and nonhuman primates, have been important in neuroscience for studying disease mechanisms, therapeutic interventions, and brain circuits (Dovonou et al. 2023). Their primary strength is in their ability to capture whole‐body physiology, like systemic immune responses, vascular function, and behavior. These features are crucial for modeling complex CNS pathologies that include multi‐organ interactions, like neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration (Sanfeliu et al. 2023; Balestri et al. 2024).

Genetically modified mice have facilitated the mechanistic analysis of disease‐related genes and signaling pathways, especially in conditions like Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Parkinson's disease (PD) (Zhong et al. 2024; Lau et al. 2025). Despite this progress, translational failure remains a significant obstacle. Prior studies have demonstrated that inflammatory responses in mice often show a weak association with human disease pathophysiology (Seok et al. 2013). This limitation is further evident in drug development outcomes since a significant percentage of drugs demonstrating success in animal models does not translate in human clinical trials (Marshall et al. 2023; Ruscu et al. 2024). These discrepancies stem from species‐specific variations in neurodevelopment, gene expression, and the failure of animal models to fully replicate human‐specific pathologies, such as tau‐related pathways in AD. Moreover, neuropsychiatric disorders and complex neurodevelopmental diseases are challenging to recapitulate in conventional animal models due to the lack of human‐specific cognitive and cortical features (Monteggia et al. 2018). Despite these limitations, animal models are essential for investigating systemic pharmacokinetics and toxicity, long‐range neural circuitry, and the progression of chronic diseases over the lifetime, all of which need complete organismal physiology.

Two‐Dimensional In Vitro Systems: Reductionist but Scalable

The 2D cell culture systems, including primary neuronal cultures and immortalized cell lines, are widely used for mechanistic studies and pharmacological screening due to their scalability, simplicity, and experimental control (Peng, Luo, et al. 2021; Cardoso et al. 2023). These approaches provide high‐throughput analysis of gene expression, signaling cascades, and cellular responses, making them especially valuable for understanding molecular mechanisms and screening potential therapeutics. However, 2D cultures lack essential features of brain structure, such as complex cell–cell interactions, 3D architecture, and ECM context, all of which greatly affect cellular processes and response to stimuli (Edmondson et al. 2014). Accordingly, results obtained from 2D systems often do not translate efficiently to in vivo systems, in particular for processes depending on neuroimmune interactions, network‐level activity, and tissue‐level structure (Abou‐Hamdan et al. 2015; Duval et al. 2017; Walczak et al. 2021).

Positioning Brain Organoids as an Intermediate Model

Human iPSC‐derived brain organoids address significant limitations of 2D systems and animal models by providing a human‐specific multicellularity as well as 3D platforms (Bose et al. 2021). These platforms can replicate features of early neurodevelopment, like progenitor zone structure, regional patterning, and cellular diversity (Yang et al. 2022; Scuderi et al. 2025). In contrast to conventional animal models, organoids preserve human developmental pathways and genetic framework, helping the study of species‐specific disease etiology and progression. At the same time, they provide greater structural and functional complexity compared to 2D cultures, such as structured progenitor areas, neuronal differentiation, synaptogenesis, and the onset of fundamental neuronal network activity. However, iPSC‐derived brain organoids cannot recapitulate whole organismal physiology and so cannot replace conventional animal models in areas like vascular perfusion, systemic immune responses, or behavior. Their importance lies in offering a controlled platform for investigating human‐specific cellular interactions and the initial phases of disease progression. Modern iPSC‐Derived Brain Organoid Systems: From Cerebral Organoids to Integrated CNS Models

Human organoids can be derived from two primary sources: adult stem cells (ASCs) and pluripotent stem cells (PSCs). ASC‐derived organoids are usually tissue‐specific and arise from stem cells located in the epithelial lining of organs such as the intestine, liver, or lung. These organoids often maintain regional identity and are less prone to forming nontarget cell types. They are ideal for disease modeling in patients where biopsies can be obtained (Sato et al. 2011). PSCs, including human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) and iPSCs, offer broader differentiation potential. These cells can give rise to virtually any organoid type, including complex systems. For example, human iPSCs are valuable for generating patient‐specific organoids, useful in personalized disease modeling and drug screening (Kim et al. 2020). While ASC‐derived organoids are more stable and easier to culture long‐term, PSC‐derived organoids offer versatility in developmental modeling and can capture early organogenesis. Region‐Specific Organoids Versus Unguided Cerebral Organoids

Initial iPSC‐derived brain organoid models, sometimes called “unguided” cerebral organoids, depended on spontaneous patterning mechanisms that generated mixtures of brain regions (Jain et al. 2025). Though these approaches are effective in displaying broad developmental pathways, they exhibit significant batch variability and limited regional identity (Aiello et al. 2025). On the contrary, region‐specific organoids are generated by directed differentiation using controlled signaling pathways, such as sonic hedgehog (SHH), Wnt signaling, and fibroblast growth factor (FGF) (Susaimanickam et al. 2022; Mulder et al. 2023). These approaches allow the reproducibility of specific brain areas, including midbrain, cortical, hippocampal, and hypothalamic regions. Midbrain‐specific organoids can replicate dopaminergic neuronal development and are widely used for modeling PD‐related pathologies (Smits and Schwamborn 2020), whereas hypothalamic organoids display region‐specific neuroendocrine differentiation (Wu et al. 2024). Even though region‐specific organoids show enhanced reproducibility and physiological relevance, they are still developmentally immature and often fail to integrate with other brain areas, hence limiting their ability to represent higher order neuronal circuit pathologies (Huang et al. 2025).

Assembloids and Multiregion Circuit Modeling

To overcome the limitations of isolated regional systems, assembloids have been developed by integrating different region‐specific organoids to enable interregional interactions. These systems provide a platform for studying processes such as initial circuit development and neuronal migration. Some foundational studies have combined dorsal and ventral forebrain organoids to replicate interneuron migration and cortical circuit formation (Sloan et al. 2018; Miura et al. 2022; Onesto et al. 2024), and subsequent research has advanced this technique to investigate long‐range connectivity and synchronized network activity (Mancinelli et al. 2025). In spite of assembloids being a prominent development over region‐specific models, they do not entirely recapitulate the accuracy or magnitude of whole organismal neural networks, as they lack sensory input, extensive long‐range connectivity, and systemic regulatory signals (Kanton and Pasca 2022; Maisumu et al. 2025).

Incorporation of Microglia and Neuroimmune Components

Another significant breakthrough in organoid technology is the integration of microglia and neuroimmune components like astrocytes (Sabate‐Soler et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2023). Earlier brain organoids were without microglia, the native immune cells of the CNS, therefore limiting their use for studying neuroinflammatory processes. Current approaches have overcome this issue by co‐differentiating microglia‐like cells within organoids or by exogenous introduction (Mrza et al. 2024). Organoids with microglia enable the study of neuroinflammatory signaling, synaptic pruning, and host–pathogen interactions, with research showing that these cells may assume functional morphologies and react to inflammatory stimuli (Fagerlund et al. 2021; Cakir et al. 2022). However, these microglial populations demonstrate insufficient interaction with systemic immune components, including circulating macrophages and lymphocytes, which are crucial for accurately modeling neuroimmune responses (Buonfiglioli et al. 2025; Schepanski et al. 2025).

Vascularization and Blood–Brain Barrier Integration

The lack of functional vasculature has been a major limitation of organoid systems, as lack of diffusion result in hypoxia and formation of necrotic cores (Fumado Navarro et al. 2025; Pantula et al. 2025). To address this issue, several strategies have been developed, like co‐culture with endothelial cells, bioengineering techniques, and transplantation into host species. Vascularized brain organoids have shown improved maturation and metabolic support, as well as the growth of vessel‐like networks (Sun et al. 2022). Simultaneously, choroid plexus models and blood–brain barrier (BBB)‐like organoid systems have helped the investigation of barrier characteristics and CNS entry mechanisms (Shaker et al. 2024). Nevertheless, these platforms have not yet achieved completely perfusable or dynamically controlled vasculature, preventing their capacity to effectively mimic hemodynamics or systemic drug delivery accurately (Kistemaker et al. 2025).

Organoid‐on‐Chip and Perfusion‐Based Systems

Relevance in regard to organismal physiology has been achieved via integration of organoids with microfluidic platforms, known as organoid‐on‐chip systems. These approaches offer regulated perfusion, improved oxygen and nutrient circulation, and more efficient waste elimination, consequently increasing organoid maturation and viability (Papamichail et al. 2025). Microfluidic systems have demonstrated a decrease in necrotic core formation and increased reproducibility in experimental outcomes (Abdulla et al. 2023). Despite these advances, such systems remain technically complex and lack standardized protocols, hence limiting their scalability and accessibility (Carvalho et al. 2024).

Transplantation‐Based Maturation

Besides earlier in vitro organoid systems, recent studies have developed techniques that increase more physiological relevance via environmental integration and in vivo maturation. One such method is transplantation‐based maturation, where organoids are implanted into the brains of immunodeficient rodents (e.g., mice). Post‐transplantation, these organoids display enhanced differentiation, axonal elongation, synapse development, and functional incorporation into host neural circuits, including vascularization facilitated by host‐derived blood vessels (Shen and Kokaia 2025). These studies indicate that transplantation into an in vivo model provides critical developmental signals, such as trophic support, perfusion, and activity‐dependent signaling, which are not entirely recapitulated in vitro. However, this approach adds species‐specific effects and complicates interpretation due to hybrid human‐animal interactions, raising important conceptual and ethical considerations (Chen et al. 2019).

Disease‐Oriented Applications of iPSC‐Derived Brain Organoids in CNS Pathophysiology

A systematic assessment of iPSC‐derived brain organoids needs analysis within particular pathological conditions rather than generalized CNS diseases. Neurodegenerative disorders, neuroinflammatory and infectious conditions, and age‐related processes include different although partly overlapping domains (Azam et al. 2021; Shafqat et al. 2023). Notably, viral and inflammatory processes may have a role in neurodegeneration but are not recognized as neurodegenerative diseases themselves (Lotz et al. 2021). The following sections assess how various experimental systems capture disease‐relevant features and identify where iPSC‐derived brain organoids provide meaningful contributions.

AD

AD has been extensively modeled using transgenic rodents, notably mice, expressing mutations in the presenilin genes and amyloid precursor protein (APP) (Sanchez‐Varo et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2025). These models effectively recapitulate amyloid plaque formation and certain aspects of synaptic dysfunction; however, they often do not capture critical features of disease, such as progressive neuronal degeneration, pronounced tau pathology, and complex cognitive decline (Chen et al. 2023). The 2D neural cultures have helped comprehensive study of amyloid processing and tau phosphorylation; however, they lack multicellularity and structural context necessary for modeling disease progression (Cenini et al. 2021; Sahlgren Bendtsen and Hall 2023).

On the other hand, iPSC‐derived brain organoids have shown the ability to replicate key AD features, like tau hyperphosphorylation, amyloid‐β buildup, and synaptic deficits within a human‐specific 3D context (Choi et al. 2016; Cenini et al. 2021). These models provide the foundation for investigating disease mechanisms within human‐specific genetic contexts and allow interaction between glial cells and neurons, which are essential for AD pathology. A significant limitation is that organoids do not experience natural aging, which is one of the prominent risk factors for AD (Sainz et al. 2024). So, they are more appropriate for replicating initial pathogenic events rather than late‐stage neurodegeneration. Moreover, the lack of systemic immune responses and vascular contributions further restricts their translational abilities (Boylin et al. 2024; Urrestizala‐Arenaza et al. 2024).

PD

Research on PD has mostly used toxin‐induced platforms and animal models to investigate the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons and motor dysfunction (Dovonou et al. 2023). Although these models can replicate some features of motor dysfunction and neuronal degeneration, they often do not capture the gradual and multifactorial nature of human PD, like α‐synuclein aggregation and nonmotor symptoms (e.g., depression, apathy, and bradyphrenia). The 2D cultures of dopaminergic neurons have improved the understanding of mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress; however, they lack circuit‐level interactions and spatial organization (Gilmozzi et al. 2021). Midbrain‐specific organoids derived from human iPSCs have evolved as a viable platform for replicating PD‐related phenotypes. These organoids developed dopaminergic neurons in a controlled environment and have features such as neuromelanin synthesis and vulnerability to degeneration (Bagley et al. 2017; Monzel et al. 2017). Significantly, they allow the study of cellular susceptibilities to neurodegeneration and human‐specific genetic alterations that are challenging to replicate in animal models. Nonetheless, PD organoids remain developmentally immature and fail to replicate age‐related neurodegenerative processes. They also do not integrate with peripheral systems like gut–brain axis, peripheral immune system, and microbiome which are known as significant contributors to PD pathogenesis (Urrestizala‐Arenaza et al. 2024).

Viral Infections and Neuroinflammation

Neuroinflammatory and infectious diseases constitute a unique category of CNS disorders whereby both host immune responses and direct pathogen effects play a significant role in pathogenesis. Animal models are crucial for investigating BBB disruption, systemic immune activation, and viral spread; however, species‐specific variations in immune responses and viral tropism may restrict their relevance to human disease (Padmanabhan and Gotz 2023; Lin et al. 2024). On the other hand, 2D systems enable controlled investigation of infection at the cellular level, but they do not accurately replicate multicellular interactions and tissue architecture (Cacciamali et al. 2022).

Human iPSC‐derived brain organoids have provided key insights into human‐specific pathogen interactions within brain tissue. For example, organoid models have demonstrated that the Zika virus selectively infects brain progenitor cells, resulting in disrupted neurogenesis and microcephaly (Qian et al. 2017). Research using organoids has shown that SARS‐CoV‐2 can infect choroid plexus epithelial cells and compromise barrier integrity (Ostermann and Schaal 2023; Shaker et al. 2024). These results underscore the unique ability of human iPSC‐derived brain organoids to replicate pathogenic effects within a human‐relevant context. Even so, their limits are substantial, like the absence of systemic immune elements and the inability to replicate chronic immune responses or the dynamics involving whole organism infection.

Bacterial Infections and Neuroinflammatory Injury

Bacterial infections of the CNS, such as sepsis‐associated encephalopathy and meningitis, include complex interactions among immune responses, vascular impairment, and bacteria. Animal models are especially relevant in this context as they elucidate systemic inflammation, BBB disruption, and leukocyte infiltration (Peng, Chu, et al. 2021; Millan Solano et al. 2023). On the other hand, 2D culture systems enable the study of specific molecular pathways, like lipopolysaccharide‐induced inflammatory signaling; however, they lack physiological relevance to human (Honda and Inagawa 2023). Human iPSC‐derived brain organoids provide a complementary approach for investigating the effect of inflammatory mediators on human brain cells in a structured tissue environment. For example, organoids exposure to inflammatory cytokines or bacterial toxins may replicate aspects of neuroinflammation and neuronal dysfunction (Stoberl et al. 2023). But these models are incapable of replicating the whole range of immunological responses, including interactions with systemic inflammatory cascades and peripheral immune cells, which are essential to bacterial CNS pathology (Depla et al. 2022). Therefore, human iPSC‐derived brain organoids are most appropriate for investigating cell‐intrinsic and tissue‐level responses, rather than organism‐level disease mechanisms.

Aging and Age‐Associated CNS Decline

Aging is the predominant risk factor for numerous CNS disorders, especially neurodegenerative diseases (Culig et al. 2022). Full bodied organisms like animal models have been pivotal in identifying key hallmarks of aging, such as genomic instability, mitochondrial failure, decreased proteostasis, and persistent low‐grade inflammation (Xu et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025). These models allow the study of long‐term physiological alterations over the lifetime, which are essential for understanding disease onset and progression.

The 2D culture systems have significantly advanced aging research by providing a controlled environment to investigate cell‐autonomous mechanisms. These models facilitate the study of critical biological processes, like oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and proteostasis imbalance (Kim et al. 2025; Pai et al. 2025). The 2D culture allows precise manipulation of environmental and genetic variables, so 2D systems remain a standard platform for screening cellular stress responses (Pitrez et al. 2024). On the other hand, 2D systems have inherent limitations in modeling the complexities of brain aging. Neurons cultured in monolayers often show altered phenotypes and truncated synaptic connectivity, failing to recapitulate the advanced spatial architecture and neural networks (Montala‐Flaquer et al. 2022). Furthermore, these systems typically lack cellular diversity, specifically oligodendrocytes and microglia, which limits the comprehensive study of neuroimmune signaling and glial‐mediated aging (Pai et al. 2025). Moreover, 2D environments do not account for ECM interactions or the biomechanical cues that are essential for maintaining neuronal homeostasis (Holle et al. 2018). The absence of established metabolic gradients and vascular components also prevents the accurate replication of neurovascular dysfunction and hypoxia associated with aging (Caffrey et al. 2021). Moreover, prolonged culture may introduce stress‐induced artifacts that differ from natural aging, potentially limiting their use in progressive CNS decline (Bang et al. 2021).

In contrast, iPSC‐derived brain organoids intrinsically represent early developmental stages and do not fundamentally show aging‐related phenotypes. This mostly results from cellular reprogramming, which restores age‐related epigenetic markers, mitochondrial status, and telomere length, so successfully keeping cells in a fetal‐like state. Moreover, organoids show lack of essential characteristics of aging, like DNA damage, aging‐associated secretory phenotype, epigenetic drift, and proteostatic decline. Modern techniques, like extended culture, protein expression, induction of oxidative or DNA damage, and metabolic stress, have been investigated to induce aging‐like phenotypes within iPSC‐derived brain organoids. Although these approaches may partly replicate elements such as cellular senescence, high ROS, and altered transcriptional profiles, they are insufficient and do not entirely replicate organismal aging (Hu et al. 2018; Huang et al. 2025). This limitation poses a considerable challenge in using iPSC‐derived brain organoids as independent models for late‐onset neurodegenerative diseases.

Therefore, no single experimental model comprehensively captures the intricacies of CNS pathophysiology across different disease categories. Animal models are needed for investigating systemic physiology, chronic disease progression, and behavior, whereas 2D culture systems provide scalable platforms for mechanistic examination. On the other hand, iPSC‐derived brain organoids occupy an intermediate position, providing distinct advantages in replicating human‐specific cellular interactions and early disease pathways within a 3D framework. Albeit, their limitations in systemic integration, maturation, and reproducibility need their use within a complementary, multimodel framework. A critical and balanced combination of various techniques is necessary for developing translational neuroscience. Limitations, Challenges, and Realistic Positioning of iPSC‐Derived Brain Organoids

Despite rapid technological growth, iPSC‐derived brain organoids are still an inadequate representation of the human CNS, and their abilities should be acknowledged within clearly defined biological and experimental conditions (Andrews and Kriegstein 2022; Eichmuller and Knoblich 2022). A critical assessment of these systems is crucial to prevent the overestimation of their translational potential and to accurately align them with existing experimental models.

One of the most prominent limitations of these organoids is their developmental immaturity. Transcriptomic and epigenetic investigations consistently show that organoids mimic prenatal or early postnatal periods of human brain development even during prolonged culture periods (Amiri et al. 2018). This constraint considerably limits their use in replicating late‐onset neurodegenerative disorders, such as AD and PD, which depend upon cumulative, age‐associated mechanisms like mitochondrial failure, protein aggregation, and chronic inflammation. While experimental induction and prolonged culture with cellular stress have been investigated to replicate some aspects of aging, these approaches do not entirely capture the intricate, organism‐wide biology of aging.

A second significant problem is the absence of systemic integration, which separates organoids from conventional animal models. Disease progression and brain function are significantly affected by interactions with peripheral systems, such as endocrine, immunological, and vascular systems (Jeon et al. 2021; Zang et al. 2022). Regardless of advancements like vascularized organoids and BBB‐like models with improved physiological relevance, these systems have not yet achieved entirely perfusable or dynamically regulated vascular networks (Fumado Navarro et al. 2025). Thus, iPSC‐derived brain organoids are incapable of recapitulating systemic immune responses, pharmacokinetics, or whole‐body exposure models, which remain the domain of animal models (Xu et al. 2018; Singh et al. 2025).

Reproducibility and variability are additional significant limitations. The generation of organoids is significantly influenced by diversity in differentiation protocol, iPSC lines, and culture conditions, causing variation across batches and laboratories (Gehling et al. 2022; Glass et al. 2024). These variations can affect regional identity, cellular composition, and functional outcomes, confounding cross‐study comparisons and data interpretation. Recent approaches focus on protocol standardization and benchmarking to resolve these limitations; yet consistent reproducibility across labs remains challenging. Until robust quality control frameworks are extensively implemented, variability will continue to be a limiting factor for both basic research and translational applications.

Another significant limitation is the inadequate network organization and cytoarchitecture of organoids. Despite their ability to generate several neuronal cell types and demonstrate spontaneous electrical activity, these systems are lacking in long‐range connection, precise laminar structure, and sensory input features of an intact brain (Zhao and Haddad 2024). Therefore, their ability to model higher order functions like behavior, cognition, and intricate circuit dynamics is inherently limited. Even in assembloid systems designed to replicate interregional connections, their scale and quality significantly lag behind that of the human brain (Lombardozzi et al. 2025; Yokoi et al. 2025).

Approaches to limit variability in iPSC‐derived brain organoid research have centered on establishing standardized protocols and benchmarking frameworks to improve reproducibility and interstudy comparison. Foundational research on cerebral organoid generation highlighted the intrinsic variability of unguided differentiation methods and the need for better controlled and repeatable systems (Wang et al. 2025). Recent studies have integrated single‐cell transcriptomics to systematically evaluate brain organoid‐derived cell populations against developing human brain tissue, enabling objective analysis of cellular composition, regional identity, and developmental fidelity (Kanton et al. 2019; Velasco et al. 2019). These reference‐mapping techniques offer a standardized framework for comparing organoids against in vivo developmental trajectories and have become essential for assessing model fidelity and validity.

The use of electrophysiological profiling, single‐cell RNA sequencing, and imaging‐based analysis has helped a more uniform evaluation of organoid function and structure across various studies (Borten et al. 2018). However, achieving total uniformity is difficult owing to intrinsic heterogeneity across iPSC lines and the vulnerability of organoid formation to minor variations in experimental conditions (Yang et al. 2021; Cho et al. 2024). Consequently, benchmarking should be seen not as a method for completely removing variability but as a framework for measuring and analyzing it within specified experimental conditions. Ongoing efforts in consensus on cross‐laboratory validation, protocols, and transparent reporting standards will be crucial for improving the reproducibility and translational applicability of iPSC‐derived brain organoid models.

From a translational viewpoint, iPSC‐derived brain organoids are often categorized as highly predictive models for drug discovery, though such claims need meticulous consideration. Although organoids provide a human‐specific platform for assessing cellular responses to therapeutics, they fail to incorporate essential aspects influencing drug efficacy and safety, such as systemic metabolism, distribution, and long‐term toxicity. Therefore, organoids should be viewed as complementary tools for early‐stage screening and mechanistic studies, rather than standalone platforms for predicting clinical outcomes. Future directions in the area seek to overcome these limitations by integration of modern technology and refined protocols. Approaches like microfluidic perfusion, organoid‐on‐chip systems, and co‐culture with immunological and vascular elements are being implemented to improve physiological relevance (Song et al. 2026). Progresses in single‐cell multi‐omics, computational modeling, and spatial transcriptomics are improving the analytical rigor and standardization of iPSC‐derived brain organoid systems. These advances are expected to improve reproducibility and provide more accurate characterization of disease‐relevant features.

The ethical considerations surrounding iPSC‐derived brain organoids have generated extra scrutiny as these models continuously evolve in structural and functional complexity. Current ethical analyses consistently undermine the main concerns around research oversight, donor consent for human biomaterials, animal transplantation, and questions about consciousness or moral status, rather than treating consciousness as an established feature of present‐day organoids (Hyun et al. 2020). Interest in this matter heightened after research indicating that cortical organoids may exhibit spontaneous rhythmic network activity similar to some features of early human brain development. However, such results do not indicate awareness, and the authors characterized them as models of nascent network growth rather than proof of sentient or advanced cognition (Trujillo et al. 2019). While studies have reported spontaneous electrical activity resembling early developmental brain patterns, current evidence does not support the presence of consciousness or higher order cognitive function in organoid systems. This is mostly because consciousness demands large‐scale, integrated neural networks, sensory inputs, and diverse cell types, all of which are absent in existing iPSC‐derived brain organoid models (Hyun et al. 2020). Current organoid systems fail to replicate the conditions at the size and organizational complexity linked to conscious processing like in intact brains (Dehaene and Changeux 2011). Therefore, while ethical vigilance is necessary, concerns about organoid consciousness remain largely speculative and should not be overstated.

Hence, iPSCs‐derived brain organoids present themselves as strong but inherently limited experimental models. Their major advantage is in recapitulating human‐specific cellular and developmental processes within a regulated 3D environment, especially for host–pathogen interactions and early disease mechanisms. However, their limitations in systemic integration, maturation, reproducibility, and functional complexity need careful interpretation. Instead of replacing conventional in vitro systems or animal models, iPSC‐derived brain organoids are best situated within an integrated, multimodel framework, whereby each system provides complementary insights into CNS biology and pathology. Integration of Brain Organoids in Translational Neuroscience

Despite their obvious limitations, iPSC‐derived brain organoids are promising when combined with existing experimental models. In translational neuroscience, no single model can comprehensively capture the intricacies of human CNS disease, and successful research design increasingly depends on the integration of complementary platforms (Nikolakopoulou et al. 2020; Dedoni et al. 2023). In this context, iPSC‐derived brain organoids are especially adept at studying human‐specific early disease processes, cellular mechanisms, and genetic diversity. Organoids may function as an intermediary validation system between high‐throughput in vitro screening and in vivo animal research. For instance, prospective therapeutic targets or signaling pathways found in 2D systems may be further assessed in organoids to determine their relevance within a 3D human tissue context. Likewise, results from animal models may be verified in organoids to determine if the observed pathways are preserved in human brain cells. These two directional integrations enhance the translational relevance and robustness of experimental results.

Moreover, progress in multi‐omics technologies, like spatial profiling and single‐cell transcriptomics, is increasing the analytical abilities of iPSC‐derived brain organoid systems. These approaches offer high‐resolution characterization of cellular states and interactions, generating better understanding of disease mechanisms (Corsini and Knoblich 2022; Smirnov et al. 2023). When integrated with bioengineering innovations like co‐culture systems and microfluidic models, organoids can be used in increasingly complex experimental contexts.

Regardless of this potential, it is important that organoids be used within explicitly defined experimental contexts and analyzed in combination with data from clinical investigations and animal models. Their limitations in systemic integration, maturation, and long‐term modeling are significant, and over‐reliance on organoids without further validation may result in incomplete or inaccurate findings. Therefore, the optimal use of iPSC‐derived brain organoids in translational neuroscience is in meaningfully integrating them into a multimodel system, functioning as a human‐specific platform for mechanistic study and hypothesis validation. This integrated strategy is expected to offer the most reliable path for translating experimental results into meaningful understanding in comprehending and addressing CNS diseases.

Conclusion

Understanding CNS pathology needs experimental models that balance biological complexity with experimental control. Conventional animal models and 2D culture methods have generated critical insights into neurological diseases, although they are limited in their translational relevance. On the other hand, human iPSC‐derived brain organoids have emerged as a complementary platform that ties reductionist in vitro systems with whole‐organism models. In several disease domains, such as neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration, and aging, iPSC‐derived organoids provide unique advantages in replicating human‐specific cellular architecture, genetic composition, and early disease pathways. Their ability to recapitulate 3D multicellular interactions and tissue architecture enables the understanding of processes that are not accessible in 2D systems or limited by species‐specific variations in conventional animal models. However, iPSC‐derived brain organoids are limited by absence of systemic integration, developmental immaturity, and diversity across protocols, which hinder their ability to represent late‐stage diseases and organism‐level physiology.

Future progress will rely on the integration of iPSC‐derived brain organoids into a multimodel system instead of replacing current systems. Progress in multi‐omics and bioengineering may increase their applicability, but their limitations must be rigorously evaluated. Eventually, iPSC‐derived brain organoids will have the most significant impact as components of a coordinated experimental approach to enhance translational neuroscience.
