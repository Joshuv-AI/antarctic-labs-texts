Provable Failure of Language Models in Learning Majority Boolean Logic via Gradient Descent
Bo Chen, Zhenmei Shi, Zhao Song, Jiahao Zhang

Abstract
Recent advancements in Transformer-based architectures have led to impressive breakthroughs in natural language processing tasks, with models such as GPT-4, Claude, and Gemini demonstrating human-level reasoning abilities. However, despite their high performance, concerns remain about the inherent limitations of these models, especially when it comes to learning basic logical functions. While complexity-theoretic analyses indicate that Transformers can represent simple logic functions (e.g., 𝖠𝖭𝖣 \mathsf{AND} , 𝖮𝖱 \mathsf{OR} , and majority gates) by its nature of belonging to the 𝖳𝖢 0 \mathsf{TC}^{0} class, these results assume ideal parameter settings and do not account for the constraints imposed by gradient descent-based training methods. In this work, we investigate whether Transformers can truly learn simple majority functions when trained using gradient-based methods. We focus on a simplified variant of the Transformer architecture and consider both n = poly ⁡ ( d ) n=\mathrm{poly}(d) and n = exp ⁡ ( Ω ⁡ ( d ) ) n=\exp(\Omega(d)) number of training samples, where each sample is a d d -size binary string paired with the output of a basic majority function. Our analysis demonstrates that even after poly ⁡ ( d ) \mathrm{poly}(d) gradient queries, the generalization error of the Transformer model still remains substantially large, growing exponentially with d d . This work highlights fundamental optimization challenges in training Transformers for the simplest logical reasoning tasks and provides new insights into their theoretical limitations.

Complexity and Neural Networks.

Circuit complexity, a fundamental field within theories of computing, investigates specific capabilities and limitations of computational models built from logical gates. By categorizing Boolean circuits into distinct complexity classes, researchers can analyze machine learning models and uncover the inherent limits to their computational efficiency. An essential finding with significant implications for machine learning is the hierarchy among circuit complexity classes, specifically the inclusion relationship
\mathsf{AC}^{0}\subset\mathsf{TC}^{0}\subseteq\mathsf{NC}^{1}

Recently, Transformers and two main Transformer variants, Average-Head Attention Transformers (AHATs) and SoftMax-Attention Transformers (SMATs), have attracted significant attention. Existing studies illustrate that AHAT models can be efficiently approximated by non-uniform circuits with threshold operations and constrained to constant depth (thus within the

3.4
The Majority Problem

In this section, we introduce some definitions about the majority problem.

Definition 3.9

(The Majority Function)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
S\subseteq[d]
be the support of a
d

be a
d

\displaystyle\mathsf{MAJ}(x,S):=\begin{cases}+1,&\sum_{j\in S}x_{j}\geq 0;\\
-1,&\sum_{j\in S}x_{j}<0.\end{cases}

Definition 3.10

(The
k
-Majority Problem)
.

Let
d\geq k\geq 2
be positive integers. Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the set of all
k
-element subsets of
[d]
. Let there be a fixed but unknown support
S\in\mathcal{S}
, which is sampled uniformly at random from the set
\mathcal{S}
. The
k
-majority problem aims to find the underlying
p
by querying a finite number of
d
-bit inputs:

\displaystyle x:=(x_{j})_{j=1}^{d}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{\pm 1\}^{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

and their corresponding labels
y:=\mathsf{MAJ}(x,S)\in\{\pm 1\}
.

Remark 3.11

(Difference between Parity and Majority)
.

Let
x_{1}:=(1,1,\underbrace{-1,\ldots,-1}_{d-2})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
x_{2}:=(-1,-1,\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{d-2})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathsf{PAR}(x_{1},S)\neq\mathsf{PAR}(x_{2},S)
, while
\mathsf{MAJ}(x_{1},S)\neq\mathsf{MAJ}(x_{2},S)
. This indicates the inherent difference between the parity problem and the majority problem.

, in which the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\max}(G_{\mathcal{S}})
is upper bounded.

The following statement is true:

\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\langle f,h_{S}\rangle_{n}^{2}\leq\lambda_{\max}(G_{\mathcal{S}})\|f\|_{n}^{2}.

Then we give the important bound on gradient variance for majority learning.

Lemma 4.5

(Bound on Gradient Variance for Majority Learning, Formal version of Lemma
C.4
in Section
C.2
)
.

Let

be the gradient variance as defined in Definition
4.3
. Then, with probability at least
1-e^{-\Omega(d)}
over random sampling, the variance of the gradient of the empirical loss with respect to the target majority is bounded as:

\mathrm{Var}_{n}(\theta;\mathcal{S})\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\sup_{\theta,x}\|\nabla f_{\theta}(x)\|_{2}^{2}.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\mathrm{Var}_{n}(\theta;\mathcal{S})
be the gradient variance as defined in Definition
4.3
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, we have:

\displaystyle\Pr[\|\nabla L_{n,S_{1}}(\theta)-\E_{S_{2}\in\mathcal{S}}[\nabla L_{n,S_{2}}(\theta)]\|_{2}>\epsilon]\leq 2\epsilon^{-2}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\sup_{\theta,x}\|\nabla f_{\theta}(x)\|_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

The result directly follows from the Chebyshev’s inequality and Lemma

∎

4.3

In this section, we introduce some basic definitions of the gradient oracle.

Definition 4.7

(Mean Gradient)
.

We use

to denote the mean gradient for all
S\in\mathcal{S}
.

Definition 4.8

(
\epsilon
-Approximate Gradient Oracle in
[
46
]
)
.

We recall that
L_{n,S}(\theta)
is our empirical loss depending on a majority function
\mathsf{MAJ}(\cdot,S),S\in\mathcal{S}
and parameters

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}L_{n,S}(\theta):=\begin{cases}\overline{\nabla}(\theta),&\mathrm{if~}\|\nabla L_{n,S}(\theta)-\overline{\nabla}(\theta)\|_{2}\geq\epsilon;\\
\nabla L_{n,S}(\theta),&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Remark 4.9
.

In other words, if the true gradient

deviates from the mean gradient by more than

Intuitively, this construction ensures that the oracle only reveals the true gradient when it is already close to the overall average. Whenever

contains “too much” information about the specific choice of
S
, the oracle reverts to returning the mean gradient

, thus concealing the identity of
S
.

5
Discussion

In this section, we discuss the difference between our work and a previous work
[
27
]
. Both works share a similar high-level analysis framework, while the major difference lies in the type of binomial coefficients being bounded. Specifically,
[
27
]
needs to bound a series of binomial coefficients related to the parity function, while we analyze the the series of binomial coefficients related to the majority function.

To analyze the binomial coefficients, both works use the operator
\mathsf{Coeff}_{k}[f(t)]
, which denotes the
k
-th coefficients with respect to variable
t
in polynomial function
f(t)
, as shown in Definition
3.4
.

Previous Approach for Parity Binomial Coefficients
[
27
]
.

Let
A_{\mathrm{par}}:=\sum_{j=0}^{m/2}{m\choose 2j}{{d-m}\choose{k-2j}}
. The intuition behind
A_{\mathrm{par}}
is that it represents the cardinality of the set
\{S\in\mathcal{S}:\mathsf{PAR}(x,S)=1\}
, which denotes the number of support sets for which the parity function outputs
1
on a fixed
x
. Thus, let the number of
-1
s in
x
be
m
. To ensure that the parity function outputs
1
, we must select an even number of
-1
s from
x
to include in the support set. For each such choice, we can then select the remaining elements arbitrarily from the positions where
x_{i}=+1
. This directly yields the definition of
A_{\mathrm{par}}
, which sums over all even integers between
0
and
m
, computing the number of valid support sets for each such choice. Let
B=\frac{1}{2}{d\choose k}
. The goal is to show that (see Lemma 8 of
[
27
]
, page 17):

\displaystyle|\frac{A_{\mathrm{par}}}{B}-1|\leq e^{-\Omega(d)}.

Let
\Gamma(t)=(1+t)^{d-m}
. Then, they try to rewrite the equation as

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{par}}=\frac{1}{2}{d\choose k}+\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{Coeff}_{k}[\Gamma(t)\Delta_{\mathrm{par}}(t)].

The key question in their case is determining
\Delta_{\mathrm{par}}(t)
, and they show that
\Delta_{\mathrm{par}}(t)=(1-t)^{m}
. This reduces the original problem to proving the following bound:

\displaystyle|\frac{\mathsf{Coeff}_{k}[\Gamma(t)\Delta_{\mathrm{par}}(t)]}{{d\choose k}}|\leq e^{-\Omega(d)}.

Our Approach for Majority Binomial Coefficients.

Let
A_{\mathrm{maj}}:=\sum_{j=0}^{k/2}\binom{m}{j}\binom{d-m}{k-j}
. Our intuition for
A_{\mathrm{maj}}
differs from the parity case, where
A_{\mathrm{maj}}
represents the cardinality of the set
\{S\in\mathcal{S}:\mathsf{MAJ}(x,S)=1\}
, i.e., the number of support sets for which the majority function outputs
1
on a fixed
x
. We let the number of
-1
s in
x
be
m
, and we focus on the non-trivial case where
d-m\geq 0.5k
and
m\geq 0.5k
. This ensures that the output of
\mathsf{MAJ}(x,S)
depends meaningfully on the choice of support set
S
, rather than being trivially determined due to an overwhelming majority of either
-1
s or
+1
s in
x
. In this setting, we count the number of support sets
S
such that fewer
-1
s than
+1
s are covered. Specifically, for each
j\in\{0,1,\cdots,k/2\}
, we choose
j
elements from the
m
positions where
x_{i}=-1
, and
k-j
elements from the
d-m
positions where
x_{i}=+1
. This directly yields the definition of
A_{\mathrm{maj}}
.
Let
B=\frac{1}{2}{d\choose k}
. Our major goal is to prove that

\displaystyle|\frac{A_{\mathrm{maj}}}{B}-1|\leq e^{-\Omega(d)}.

The key idea is to rewrite
A_{\mathrm{maj}}
as follows (see Lemma
3.8
in Section
3.3
):

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{maj}}=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{2}\binom{d}{k}+\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{Coeff}_{k}[\Gamma(t)\Delta_{\mathrm{maj}}(t)].

In our case, the key question is determining
\Delta_{\mathrm{maj}}(t)
, and we show that

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{maj}}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{k/2}{m\choose j}t^{j}-\sum_{j=k/2+1}^{m}{m\choose j}t^{j}.

This reduces the original problem to proving the following bound:

\displaystyle|\frac{\mathsf{Coeff}_{k}[\Gamma(t)\Delta_{\mathrm{maj}}(t)]}{{d\choose k}}|\leq e^{-\Omega(d)}.

Since the form of
\Delta_{\mathrm{maj}}(t)
differs significantly from the form of
\Delta_{\mathrm{par}}(t)
, the bounding technique is also different. For details on how to obtain this new bound w.r.t.
\Delta_{\mathrm{maj}}(t)
, we refer the reader to Lemma
D.1
in Appendix
D
.

Other Parts of the Framework.

In this work, we study the majority problem (see Definition
3.10
), which differentiates from the original work
[
27
]
by substituting the parity function
\mathsf{PAR}(x,S):=\prod_{j\in S}x_{j}
with majority function in Definition
3.9
. We further show the distinction between these two problems in Remark
3.11
. Besides, this difference in the studied problem also leads to slight variations in the computation of the Gram matrix, which is useful for bounding the gradient variance. We refer readers to Fact
4.4
in Section
4.2
for a to check these differences.

6
Conclusion

Our paper provides a rigorous theoretical analysis of the majority function learning problem under polynomial and exponential-sample regimes, offering explicit hardness results. By integrating combinatorial and probabilistic techniques, we not only characterize tight gradient variance bounds but also illuminate the inherent difficulties in achieving high accuracy. Our findings lay a solid foundation for future research on extending majority learning to a wider range of models and scenarios.

Fact C.3

(Formal version of Fact
4.4
in Section
4.2
)
.

If the following conditions hold:

•

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of all the
k
-size support sets over
\{\pm 1\}^{d}
.

•

Let there be
n
fixed empirical samples
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
as defined in Definition
3.13
.

•

Let the empirical inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{n}
empirical norm
\|\cdot\|_{n}
as defined in Definition
3.13
.

•

Let
h_{S}:=\mathsf{MAJ}(x,S)
denote the majority function in Definition
3.9
for any
S\in\mathcal{S}
.

•

Let the gram matrix be
G_{\mathcal{S}}:=(\langle h_{S_{1}},h_{S_{2}}\rangle_{n})_{S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathcal{S}}
, in which the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\max}(G_{\mathcal{S}})
is upper bounded.

The following statement is true:

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\langle f,h_{S}\rangle_{n}^{2}\leq\lambda_{\max}(G_{\mathcal{S}})\|f\|_{n}^{2}.

Proof.

Since we already know the largest eigenvalue of
G_{\mathcal{S}}
is bounded, we can conclude that set
\{h_{S}:S\in\mathcal{S}\}
formulates a partial frame for the empirical norm.
More specifically, consdering an arbitrary
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we can decompose
f
into two orthogonal components, i.e.,

\displaystyle f=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{S}\cdot h_{S}+f_{0},\quad f_{0}\in(\mathrm{span}S)^{\bot},

(10)

for some coefficient vectors
\alpha=(\alpha_{S})_{S\in\mathcal{S}}
.

Thus, we have:

\displaystyle\|f\|_{n}^{2}\geq

\displaystyle~\|f-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{S_{1}}\alpha_{S_{2}}\langle h_{S_{1}},h_{S_{2}}\rangle_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\alpha^{\top}G_{\mathcal{S}}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\|G_{\mathcal{S}}^{1/2}\alpha\|_{2}^{2}

(11)

where the first step follows from the Pythagorean theorem in the empirical norm where
\|f\|_{n}^{2}=\|f-f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}+\|f_{0}\|_{n}^{2}
, the second step follows from Eq. (
10
), the third step is based on the definition of Gram matrix, and the last step follows the definition of vector
\ell_{2}
norm.

Therefore, we can obtain the desired result as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\langle f,h_{S}\rangle_{n}^{2}=

\displaystyle~\sum_{S_{1}\in\mathcal{S}}(\sum_{S_{2}\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{S_{2}}\langle h_{S_{2}},h_{S_{1}}\rangle_{n})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{S_{1}\in\mathcal{S}}(\langle\alpha,(G_{\cal S})_{S_{1},*}\rangle)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\|G_{\mathcal{S}}\alpha\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\|G_{\mathcal{S}}^{1/2}\|^{2}\|G_{\mathcal{S}}^{1/2}\alpha\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\lambda_{\max}(G_{\mathcal{S}})\|G_{\mathcal{S}}^{1/2}\alpha\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\lambda_{\max}(G_{\mathcal{S}})\|f\|_{n}^{2}.

where the first step can be derived by Eq. (
10
) and the linearity of the inner product, the second step follows from the definition of the Gram matrix, the third step follows from the definition of
\|\cdot\|_{2}
, the forth step follows basic matrix analysis, the fifth step follows from the definition of matrix spectral norm, and the last step is based on Eq. (
11

This finishes the proof.

Lemma C.4

(Bound on Gradient Variance for Majority Learning, Formal version of Lemma
4.5
in Section
4.2
)
.

Let
\mathrm{Var}_{n}(\theta;\mathcal{S})
be the gradient variance as defined in Definition
4.3
. Then, with probability at least
1-e^{-\Omega(d)}
over random sampling, the variance of the gradient of the empirical loss with respect to the target majority is bounded as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{n}(\theta;\mathcal{S})\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\sup_{\theta,x}\|\nabla f_{\theta}(x)\|_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

Part 1. Bounding Each Element.

By Definition
4.3
, we consider the variance of the empirical gradient
\nabla L_{n,S}
w.r.t. the target underlying support
S
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{n}(\theta;\mathcal{S})=\E_{S_{1}\in\mathcal{S}}[\|\nabla L_{n,S_{1}}(\theta)-\E_{S_{2}\in\mathcal{S}}[\nabla L_{n,S_{2}}(\theta)]\|_{2}^{2}].

(12)

For notation simplicity, we define function
h_{S}(\cdot):=\mathsf{MAJ}(x,S)
for a fixed support
S\in\mathcal{S}
. We proceed to evaluate the magnitude of
\mathrm{Var}_{n}(\theta;\mathcal{S})
. For
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
with
S_{1}\neq S_{2}
it holds that

\displaystyle\langle h_{S_{1}},h_{S_{2}}\rangle_{n}=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathsf{sgn}(\sum_{j_{1}\in S_{1}}x_{i,j_{1}})\cdot\mathsf{sgn}(\sum_{j_{2}\in S_{2}}x_{i,j_{2}})),

which directly follows from Definition
3.10
and the definition of empirical norm from Definition
3.13
.

Since
a_{i}:=\mathsf{sgn}(\sum_{j_{1}\in S_{1}}x_{i,j_{1}})\cdot\mathsf{sgn}(\sum_{j_{2}\in S_{2}}x_{i,j_{2}})
is i.i.d.
\mathrm{Unif}(\{\pm 1\})
for fixed
S_{1},S_{2}
, by applying a union bound over Hoeffding’s inequality from Lemma
A.2
, it follows for
\delta:=2\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}
that

\displaystyle\Pr[\sup_{S_{1}\neq S_{2}}|\langle h_{S_{1}},h_{S_{2}}\rangle_{n}|\geq\delta]\leq

\displaystyle~|\mathcal{S}|^{2}\exp(-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\binom{d}{k}^{2}e^{-2d}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~(\frac{2}{e})^{2d},

where the first step follows from Hoeffding’s inequality from Lemma
A.2
, the second step follows from Definition
3.10
where
|\mathcal{S}|=\binom{d}{k}
, and the third step follows from Fact
3.1
.

Part 2: Bounding the Largest Eigenvalue of the Gram Matrix.

Then with probability at least
1-e^{-\Omega(d)}
over random sampling, every off-diagonal component of the Gram matrix can be defined as:

\displaystyle G_{\mathcal{S}}:=(\langle h_{S_{1}},h_{S_{2}}\rangle_{n})_{S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathcal{S}},

(13)

which has magnitude at most
\delta
, while the diagonal entries are equal to
1
.

Thus, we have:

\displaystyle|\lambda_{\max}(G_{\mathcal{S}})|=

\displaystyle~\|G_{\mathcal{S}}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\|G_{\mathcal{S}}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~(\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{S}|}(G_{\mathcal{S}})_{i,j}^{2})^{-0.5}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~(|\mathcal{S}|^{2}\delta^{2})^{-0.5}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~|\mathcal{S}|\delta,

where basic algebra supports the first and the second steps, the third step is based on the definition of the Frobenius norm, and the fourth and final steps follow from basic algebra.

Thus, we have
\lambda_{\max}(G_{\mathcal{S}})\leq\delta|\mathcal{S}|
.

Part 3. Bounding the Variance.

Let

be the dimension of the model parameters. We can therefore bound

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{n}(\theta;\mathcal{S})=

\displaystyle~\inf_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\E_{S\in\mathcal{S}}[\|\nabla L_{n,S}(\theta)-\mu\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle~\E_{S\in\mathcal{S}}[\|\nabla L_{n,S}(\theta)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{\theta}(x_{i})\nabla f_{\theta}(x_{i})\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\E_{S\in\mathcal{S}}[\|\nabla_{\theta}(\frac{1}{2}\|h_{S}-f_{\theta}\|_{n}^{2})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{\theta}(x_{i})\nabla f_{\theta}(x_{i})\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\E_{S\in\mathcal{S}}[\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f_{\theta}(x_{i})-h_{S}(x_{i}))\nabla f_{\theta}(x_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{\theta}(x_{i})\nabla f_{\theta}(x_{i})\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\E_{S\in\mathcal{S}}[\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{S}(x_{i})\nabla f_{\theta}(x_{i})\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\E_{S\in\mathcal{S}}[\sum_{j=1}^{D}(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{S}(x_{i})\nabla f_{\theta_{j}}(x_{i}))^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\E_{S\in\mathcal{S}}[\sum_{j=1}^{D}\langle\nabla_{\theta_{j}}f_{\theta},h_{S}\rangle_{n}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{j=1}^{D}\langle\nabla_{\theta_{j}}f_{\theta},h_{S}\rangle_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\sum_{j=1}^{D}\frac{\lambda_{\max}(G_{\mathcal{S}})}{|\mathcal{S}|}\|\nabla_{\theta_{j}}f_{\theta}\|_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~2\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\sup_{\theta,x}\|\nabla f_{\theta}(x)\|_{2}^{2}.

where the first step follows from Eq. (
12
), the second step follows from selecting
\mu:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{\theta}(x_{i})\nabla f_{\theta}(x_{i})
, the third step follows from the definition of empirical loss in Definition
3.13
, the fourth step follows from taking the derivative w.r.t. the empirical loss, the fifth step and the sixth step follows from basic algebra, the seventh step follows from the definition of empirical norm in Definition
3.13
, the eighth step follows from the fact that all the support sets in
\mathcal{S}
is sampled uniformly in Definition
3.10

4.4

Thus, we complete the proof.

C.3

In this section, we introduce the proof of lemma about the property of the oracle.

Lemma C.5

(Guarantee of the Oracle, Formal version of Lemma
4.10
in Section
4.4
)
.

If the following conditions hold:

•

Let
Q\subseteq\mathcal{S}
denote the subset of the hypothesis space where the oracle defaults to the mean gradient.

•

Let
T
be the number of optimization steps.

•

Let
d
be the dimensionality of the input.

•

Let
n
be the sample size.

•

Let
f_{\theta}(x)
be the model’s prediction function.

Then the following statements are true:

•

During
T
optimization steps, the oracle reveals no information
about the true support
S\in\mathcal{S}
with probability at least:

\displaystyle\Pr[Q]\geq 1-\frac{2T}{\epsilon^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\sup_{\theta,x}\|\nabla f_{\theta}(x)\|_{2}^{2},

(14)

Proof.

We first analyze when the oracle reveals information about the true support
S
.

The oracle substitutes the true gradient
\nabla L_{n,S}(\theta)
with the expected gradient
\E_{S_{2}\in\mathcal{S}}[\nabla L_{n,S_{2}}(\theta)]
whenever the difference exceeds threshold
\epsilon
. Information about the true support
S
is revealed only when the oracle does not default to the mean gradient, which occurs when:

\displaystyle\|\nabla L_{n,S_{1}}(\theta)-\E_{S_{2}\in\mathcal{S}}[\nabla L_{n,S_{2}}(\theta)]\|\leq\epsilon.

We bound the probability of this occurrence for a single optimization step.

Let
X
be the event that
\|\nabla L_{n,S_{1}}(\theta)-\E_{S_{2}\in\mathcal{S}}[\nabla L_{n,S_{2}}(\theta)]\|\leq\epsilon
. By applying concentration inequalities and considering the variance of the gradient estimates, we can follow from Corollary
4.6
to show that:

\displaystyle\Pr[X]\leq 2\epsilon^{-2}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\sup_{\theta,x}\|\nabla f_{\theta}(x)\|_{2}^{2}.

(15)

Then we apply a union bound across all
T
optimization steps. Let
X_{t}
be the event that the oracle reveals information about
S
at step
t
. We denote the event such that the oracle reveals information in any step by
\Psi
. Then by the union bound, we have the following:

\displaystyle\Pr[\Psi]=

\displaystyle~\Pr[\bigcup_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Pr[X_{t}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{2T}{\epsilon^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\sup_{\theta,x}\|\nabla f_{\theta}(x)\|_{2}^{2},

where the first step comes from basic probability, the second step applies the union bound, and the last step follows from Eq. (
15
).

Therefore, since event
\Psi
is the complement of event
Q
, we can conclude by basic probability that:

\displaystyle\Pr[Q]=

\displaystyle~1-\Pr[\Psi]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~1-\frac{2T}{\epsilon^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\sup_{\theta,x}\|\nabla f_{\theta}(x)\|_{2}^{2}.

This completes the proof.

Theorem C.6

(Lower Bound on

Error, Formal version of Theorem

in Section

)
.

If the following conditions hold:

•

Let
Q\subseteq\mathcal{S}
be a noninformative set with an automorphism
\sigma:Q\to Q
having no fixed points.

•

Let function
f
be
f_{\theta({\cal A})}:\{\pm 1\}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Then the following statement is true:

\displaystyle\E_{S\in\mathcal{S}}[\sup_{x}|\mathsf{MAJ}(x,S)-f_{\theta({\cal A})}(x)|]\geq\Pr[S\in Q].

Proof.

Part 1: Restricting to the Noninformative Set.
Let
h_{S}(x):=\mathsf{MAJ}(x,S)
.
We can bound the
L_{\infty}
error by restricting to the noninformative set
Q
as follows:

\displaystyle\E_{S_{1}\in\mathcal{S}}[\sup_{x}|h_{S_{1}}(x)-f_{\theta({\cal A})}(x)|]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~\E_{S_{1}\in\mathcal{S}}[\mathbf{1}[S_{1}\in Q]\cdot\sup_{x}|h_{S_{1}}(x)-f(x)|]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{S_{1}\in Q}(\sup_{x}|h_{S_{1}}(x)-f(x)|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{S_{1}\in Q}\underbrace{(\sup_{x}|h_{S_{1}}(x)-f(x)|+\sup_{x}|\sigma\circ h_{S_{1}}(x)-f(x)|)}_{:=T(S_{1})},

(16)

where the first step follows from it is possible that the indicator function
\mathbf{1}[S_{1}\in Q]
will erase some non-negative terms to zero, the second step follows from the fact that all the support sets in
\mathcal{S}
are sampled from a uniform distribution as mentioned in Definition
3.10
, and the last step is based on the fact that
\sum_{S_{1}\in Q}\sup_{x}|h_{S_{1}}(x)-f(x)|=\sum_{S_{1}\in Q}\sup_{x}|\sigma\circ h_{S_{1}}(x)-f(x)|
.

Part 2: Bounding Each Term
T(S_{1})
.

Then for each
S\in Q
, let
x_{0}\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
be one specific binary vector such that
h_{S_{1}}(x_{0})\neq\sigma\circ h_{S_{1}}(x_{0})
. We can lower bound each term
T(S_{1})
in Eq. (
16
) by selecting a specific
x_{0}
from all the possible choices of
x
:

\displaystyle T(S_{1})=

\displaystyle~\sup_{x}|h_{S_{1}}(x)-f(x)|+\sup_{x}|\sigma\circ h_{S_{1}}(x)-f(x)|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~|h_{S_{1}}(x_{0})-f(x_{0})|+|\sigma\circ h_{S_{1}}(x_{0})-f(x_{0})|,

(17)

where the first step follows from the definition of
T(S_{1})
in Eq. (
16
), and the second step follows from basic algebra.

Since
h_{S_{1}}(x_{0})\neq\sigma\circ h_{S_{1}}(x_{0})
, without loss of generality, we can set
h_{S_{1}}(x_{0})=1
and
\sigma\circ h_{S_{1}}(x_{0})=-1
and obtain the following:

\displaystyle T(S_{1})\geq

\displaystyle~|1-f(x_{0})|+|-1-f(x_{0})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~|1-f(x_{0})|+|f(x_{0})+1|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~2,

(18)

where the first step follows from Eq. (
17
), the second step comes from basic algebra, and the last step is based on the triangle inequality.

Part 3: Obtaining the Final Lower Bound.
Therefore, we can combine the previous parts of the proof and get the desired bound as follows:

\displaystyle\E_{S_{1}\in\mathcal{S}}[\sup_{x}|h_{S_{1}}(x)-f_{\theta({\cal A})}(x)|]\geq

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{p\in Q}T(S_{1})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{S}|}\cdot 2|Q|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{|Q|}{|\mathcal{S}|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\Pr[S\in Q],

where the first step follows from Eq. (
16
), the second step follows from Eq. (
17
), the third and the last steps follow from basic algebra.
∎

In this section, we introduce the proofs of some important results.

Lemma C.7

(Formal version of Lemma
4.13
in Section
4.6
)
.

If
k=\Theta(d)
, it satisfies with probability at least
1-e^{-\Omega(d)}
over random sampling that

\displaystyle|\E_{S\in\mathcal{S}}[\mathsf{MAJ}(x,S)]|\leq e^{-\Omega(d)}.

Proof.

Part 1: High Probability Result for
m
Being Close to
\frac{d}{2}
.
We denote the number of negative ones in
x
by
m
. By the Chernoff bound from Lemma
A.1
for the binomial distribution,

\displaystyle\Pr[|m-\frac{d}{2}|\leq\frac{\delta d}{2}]\geq 1-2\exp(-\frac{\delta^{2}d}{6})

for a constant
\delta\in(0,1)
to be determined, so we assume the above event throughout the proof. Let
h_{S}(x):=\mathsf{MAJ}(x,S)
. Moreover denoting the complement support
S^{c}=[d]\setminus S
, it holds that
h_{S}(x)=x_{1}\cdots x_{d}\cdot h_{S^{c}}(x)
and
|\E_{S\in\mathcal{S}}[h_{S}(x)]|=|\E_{S\in\mathcal{S}}[h_{S^{c}}(x)]|
, so it suffices to consider the case where
2k\leq d
.

Part 2: Upper Bounding
\frac{|\mathcal{S}_{+}|}{|\mathcal{S}|}
.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{+}:=\{S\in\mathcal{S}:\mathsf{MAJ}(x,S)=1\}
and
m
be the number of negative ones in
x
. We consider three cases:

Case 1:
d-m<0.5k
.
In this case, the number of
+1
in the input
x
is sufficiently small, which ensures that
h_{S}(x)=-1
for all
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
and
S\in\mathcal{S}
. Thus, we have

Case 2:
m<0.5k
.
In this case, the number of
-1
in the input
x
is sufficiently small, which ensures that
h_{S}(x)=1
for all
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
.

Case 3:
d-m\geq 0.5k,m\geq 0.5k
.
In this case, not all
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
have the same result
h_{S}(x)
. First, by basic combinatorics, we can follow Definition
3.10
and obtain that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}|=\binom{d}{k}.

Besides, if we consider choosing less than
k/2
elements from
[m]
and choosing the remaining elements from
[d]\setminus[m]
, we can make sure that
h_{S}(x)=1
holds. Recall that we assume there are
m
numbers of
-1
in the input
x
and there are
d-m
numbers of
1
in the input
x
. Thus, we have:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}_{+}|=

\displaystyle~\sum_{j=0}^{k/2}\binom{m}{j}\binom{d-m}{k-j}.

We also have:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}|=

\displaystyle~\binom{d}{k}.

Therefore, we can conclude by Lemma
3.8
that

\displaystyle|\frac{|\mathcal{S}_{+}|}{|\mathcal{S}|}-1|\leq e^{-\Omega(d)}.

Part 3: The Final Bound.
From this, we conclude that

\displaystyle|\E_{S\in\mathcal{S}}[h_{S}(x)]|=|\frac{|\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{+}|-|\mathcal{S}_{+}|}{|\mathcal{S}|}|\leq e^{-\Omega(d)}

with probability
1-e^{-\Omega(d)}
.

∎

Theorem C.8

(Lower Bound on Mean Squared Error, Formal version of Theorem
4.14
in Section
4.6
)
.

If the following conditions hold:

•

We have a function
f_{\theta({\cal A})}:\{\pm 1\}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
learned by algorithm
{\cal A}
using
n
samples.

•

Let
\overline{f}_{\theta({\cal A})}:=\min\{\max\{f_{\theta({\cal A})}(x),-1\},1\}
denote the clipped version of function
f_{\theta({\cal A})}
.

•

Let
Q\subseteq\mathcal{S}
be a noninformative set.

•

We denote the number of iterations in optimization by
T
.

•

Let
\epsilon>0
denote an arbitrary gap between a single gradient and the expectation of gradient as defined in Corollary
4.6
.

Then the following statement is true:

\displaystyle\E_{S\in\mathcal{S},x}[(\mathsf{MAJ}(x,S)-f_{\theta({\cal A})}(x))^{2}]\geq 1-\frac{4T}{\epsilon^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\sup_{\theta,x}\|\nabla f_{\theta}(x)\|_{2}^{2}-e^{-\Omega(d)}.

Proof.

Part 1. Restricting to the Noninformative Set.
For notation simplicity, we let
h(x):=\mathsf{MAJ}(x,S)
, and denote functions
f_{\theta({\cal A})}
and
\overline{f}_{\theta({\cal A})}
by
f
and
\overline{f}
. We first restrict the mean squared error to the noninformative set
Q
:

\displaystyle~\E_{S\in\mathcal{S},x}[(h_{S}(x)-f(x))^{2}]

(19)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~\E_{S\in\mathcal{S},x}[\mathbf{1}[S\in Q](h_{S}(x)-f(x))^{2}]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~\E_{S\in\mathcal{S},x}[\mathbf{1}[S\in Q](h_{S}(x)-\overline{f}(x))^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\E_{S\in\mathcal{S},x}[\mathbf{1}[S\in Q]h_{S}(x)^{2}]-2\E_{S\in\mathcal{S},x}[\mathbf{1}[S\in Q]h_{S}(x)\overline{f}(x)]+\E_{S\in\mathcal{S},x}[\mathbf{1}[S\in Q]\overline{f}(x)^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\underbrace{\Pr[Q]}_{:=E_{1}}-2\underbrace{\E_{S\in\mathcal{S},x}[\mathbf{1}[S\in Q]h_{S}(x)\overline{f}(x)]}_{:=E_{2}}+\underbrace{\Pr[Q]\cdot\E_{x}[\overline{f}(x)^{2}]}_{:=E_{3}},

(20)

where the first step follows from erasing some non-negative terms to zero by the indicator function, the second step follows from Lemma
3.3
, the third step follows from basic algebra, and the last step follows from basic probability.

Part 2. Bounding the term
E_{2}
.
Specifically, we consider lower bounding the second expectation
E_{2}
in Eq. (
19
). We can first recall Lemma
4.13
to conclude that with probability
1-e^{-\Omega(d)}
over the sampling of
x
, we have

\displaystyle\E_{S\in\mathcal{S}}[h_{S}(x)]|\leq e^{-\Omega(d)}.

By basic probability, this directly implies:

\displaystyle|\E_{S\in\mathcal{S},x}[h_{S}(x)\overline{f}(x)]|\leq

\displaystyle~(1-e^{-\Omega(d)})|\E_{S\in\mathcal{S}}[h_{S}(x)]|\cdot|\E_{x}[\overline{f}(x)]|+e^{-\Omega(d)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~e^{-\Omega(d)}.

(21)

Therefore, the desired lower bound can be obtained as follows:

\displaystyle E_{2}=

\displaystyle~\E_{S\in\mathcal{S},x}[\mathbf{1}[S\in Q]h_{S}(x)\overline{f}(x)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\E_{S\in\mathcal{S},x}[h_{S}(x)\overline{f}(x)]-\E_{S\in\mathcal{S},x}[\mathbf{1}[\{p\notin Q\}]h_{S}(x)\overline{f}(x)]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~e^{-\Omega(d)}+(1-\Pr[Q])\E_{x}[|\overline{f}(x)|]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~e^{-\Omega(d)}+(1-\Pr[Q])\sqrt{\E_{x}[1^{2}]\cdot\E_{x}[\overline{f}(x)^{2}]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~e^{-\Omega(d)}+(1-\Pr[Q])\sqrt{\E_{x}[\overline{f}(x)^{2}]}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~e^{-\Omega(d)}+\frac{(1-\Pr[Q])^{2}}{2\Pr[Q]}+\frac{\Pr[Q]}{2}\E_{x}[\overline{f}(x)^{2}],

(22)

where the first step follows from the definition of
E_{2}
, the second step follows from basic probability, the third step follows from Eq, (
21
) and basic probability, the fourth step follows from the definition of expectation, the fifth step follows from basic algebra, and the last step follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Part 3. Bounding the Mean Squared Error.
Now we can recall Eq. (
19
) in the first part of this proof, and then bound the mean squared error as follows:

\displaystyle~\E_{S\in\mathcal{S},x}[(h_{S}(x)-f_{\theta({\cal A})}(x))^{2}]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~E_{1}-2E_{2}+E_{3}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~E_{1}-2(e^{-\Omega(d)}+\frac{(1-\Pr[Q])^{2}}{2\Pr[Q]}+\frac{\Pr[Q]}{2}\E_{x}[\overline{f}(x)^{2}])+E_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\Pr[Q]-2(e^{-\Omega(d)}+\frac{(1-\Pr[Q])^{2}}{2\Pr[Q]}+\frac{\Pr[Q]}{2}\E_{x}[\overline{f}(x)^{2}])+\Pr[Q]\cdot\E_{x}[\overline{f}(x)^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\Pr[Q]-\frac{(1-\Pr[Q])^{2}}{\Pr[Q]}-2e^{-\Omega(d)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~2-\frac{1}{\Pr[Q]}-2e^{-\Omega(d)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~1-2(1-\Pr[Q])-2e^{-\Omega(d)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~1-2(1-\Pr[Q])-e^{-\Omega(d)},

where the first step follows from Eq. (
19
), the second step follows from the substituting
E_{2}
with its upper bound in Eq (
22
), the third step follows from the definition of
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
in Eq. (
19
), the fourth and the fifth steps follow from basic algebra, the sixth step follows from the fact that
2-(1-p)^{-1}\geq 1-2t,\forall x\in[0,0.5]
, and the last step follows from absorbing the coefficient in
2e^{-\Omega(d)}
into
-\Omega(d)
.

Therefore, we can apply the lower bound of
\Pr[Q]
in Lemma
4.10
, and obtain the final result:

\displaystyle\E_{S\in\mathcal{S},x}[(h_{S}(x)-f_{\theta({\cal A})}(x))^{2}]\geq 1-\frac{4T}{\epsilon^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\sup_{\theta,x}\|\nabla f_{\theta}(x)\|_{2}^{2}-e^{-\Omega(d)}.

Thus we complete the proof.
∎

Appendix D
Bounding the Binomial Coefficient

This subsection aims to establish a technical lemma related to binomial coefficients.

Lemma D.1
.

If the following conditions hold:

•

Let
m,d,k
be non-negative integers.

•

We assume that
d\geq m\geq 0.5k
and
d-m\geq 0.5k
.

•

Let
\Gamma(t):=(1+t)^{d-m}
.

•

Let
\Delta(t):=\sum_{j=0}^{k/2}\binom{m}{j}t^{j}-\sum_{j=k/2+1}^{m}\binom{m}{j}t^{j}
.

The following statement is true:

\displaystyle|\frac{\mathsf{Coeff}_{k}[\Gamma(t)\Delta(t)]}{\binom{d}{k}}|<e^{-\Omega(d)}.

Proof.

Recall two definitions

\displaystyle\Gamma(t):=(1+t)^{d-m},\quad\Delta(t):=\sum_{j=0}^{k/2}\binom{m}{j}t^{j}-\sum_{j=k/2+1}^{m}\binom{m}{j}t^{j}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{Coeff}_{k}[\Gamma(t)\Delta(t)]=

\displaystyle~\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mathsf{Coeff}_{i}[\Gamma(t)]\cdot\mathsf{Coeff}_{k-i}[\Delta(t)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{d-m}{i}\cdot\delta_{k-i},

(23)

where the first step follows definition of
\mathsf{Coeff}
, and the last step from definition
\Gamma(t)
and
where
\delta_{j}=\mathsf{Coeff}_{j}[\Delta(t)]
, which is

•

\delta_{j}=\binom{m}{j}
for
j\leq k/2
.

•

\delta_{j}=-\binom{m}{j}
for
j>k/2
,

•

\delta_{j}=0
otherwise.

So we can further rewrite
\mathsf{Coeff}_{k}[\Gamma(t)\Delta(t)]
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Coeff}_{k}[\Gamma(t)\Delta(t)]=

\displaystyle~\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{d-m}{i}\cdot\delta_{k-i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{d-m}{k-j}\cdot\delta_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{j=0}^{k/2}\binom{d-m}{k-j}\binom{m}{j}-\sum_{j=k/2+1}^{m}\binom{d-m}{k-j}\binom{m}{j}.

(24)

where the first step follows from Eq. (
23
), the second step follows from renaming the summation index, the last step follows from definition of
\delta_{j}
.

Note that the result in Eq. (
24
) is close to a binomial convolution, but with alternating weights. If we replaced
\Delta(t)
by
(1+t)^{m}
, we would get:

\displaystyle(1+t)^{d}=\underbrace{(1+t)^{d-m}}_{\Gamma(t)}(1+t)^{m}.

So
\mathsf{Coeff}_{k}[(1+t)^{d}]=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{d-m}{k-j}\binom{m}{j}=\binom{d}{k}
. Therefore,
\Delta(t)
is a signed approximation to
(1+t)^{m}
, emphasizing low-degree terms and subtracting high-degree ones. Hence,
(1+t)^{d-m}\Delta(t)
is a truncated or ”tilted” version of
(1+t)^{d}
, designed to cancel much of the mass around the center.

Since
\Delta(t)
subtracts the tail of the binomial
(1+t)^{m}
, when we convolve with
(1+t)^{d-m}
, the resulting coefficient at position
k
will miss a significant amount of the mass from the ”usual”
\binom{d}{k}
, and due to cancellation between positive and negative parts, the residual will be very small.

More formally, define the random variable
X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(d,1/2)
, and consider the convolution:

\displaystyle\Pr[X=k]=\binom{d}{k}2^{-d}

and numerator is a signed combination over subsets. The signed sum in numerator can be shown to correspond to:

\displaystyle\Pr[X=k\mathrm{~and~}Y\leq k/2]-\Pr[X=k\mathrm{~and~}Y>k/2],

where
X=A+B
,
A\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(d-m,1/2)
,
B\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(m,1/2)
. The probabilities where
B
is large (i.e.,
>k/2
) cancel out the corresponding terms in the total
\Pr[X=k]
, leaving behind a small residue.

This residual is upper-bounded by the tail probability of
B\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(m,1/2)
deviating from its mean
m/2
by
\Omega(m)
, which is known to decay as
e^{-\Omega(m)}
. Since
m=\Theta(d)
, this gives:

\displaystyle|S|<\binom{d}{k}\cdot e^{-\Omega(d)},

and therefore we can get:

\displaystyle|\frac{\mathsf{Coeff}_{k}[\Gamma(t)\Delta(t)]}{\binom{d}{k}}|<e^{-\Omega(d)}.

Thus, we finally complete the proof.
∎
