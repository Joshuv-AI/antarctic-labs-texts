Sandwich regression for accurate and robust estimation in generalized linear multilevel and longitudinal models
Elliot H. Young, Rajen D. Shah

Abstract
Generalized linear models are a popular tool in applied statistics, with their maximum likelihood estimators enjoying asymptotic Gaussianity and efficiency. As all models are wrong, it is desirable to understand these estimators’ behaviours under model misspecification. We study semiparametric multilevel generalized linear models, where only the conditional mean of the response is taken to follow a specific parametric form. Pre-existing estimators from mixed effects models and generalized estimating equations require specificaiton of a conditional covariance, which when misspecified can result in inefficient estimates of fixed effects parameters. It is nevertheless often computationally attractive to consider a restricted, finite dimensional class of estimators, as these models naturally imply. We introduce sandwich regression, that selects the estimator of minimal variance within a parametric class of estimators over all distributions in the full semiparametric model. We demonstrate numerically on simulated and real data the attractive improvements our sandwich regression approach enjoys over classical mixed effects models and generalized estimating equations.

Introduction

All models are wrong, but some are useful. When a large quantity of high-quality data is available, it is natural to be minimal with regards to the model assumptions being imposed when performing statistical analyses, with assumption-lean estimators typically requiring data-driven estimation of nonparametric nuisance functions.
When dealing with smaller datasets, these nonparametric estimations become impractical, and instead often lead one to impose certain structural assumptions on the data generating process.
The generalized linear model (glm)
(
Nelder and Wedderburn,, 1972
)
has proved a popular structure to impose,
where the distribution of an outcome
Y\in\mathbb{R}
conditional on covariates
X\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times p}
lies in a finite dimensional parametric class. Together with a random design on the covariates, this defines a class of distributions
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{GLM}}
on the pair
(Y,X)
.
The maximum likelihood estimator corresponding to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{GLM}}
enjoys consistency, asymptotic Gaussianity and efficiency within this model.
This estimator remains efficient within a broader class of models
(
McCullagh,, 1983
)
provided the first two conditional moments implied by the glm hold i.e. the model

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{QGLM}}:=\Big\{P:g\big(\mathbb{E}_{P}[Y\,|\,X]\big)=X\beta,\;\mathrm{Var}_{P}(Y\,|\,X)=v(g^{-1}(X\beta))\Big\},

where
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a fixed effect parameter of interest,
g
is a strictly increasing link function, and
v
is the variance function of the glm.
This model is termed the quasi generalized linear model
(
Wedderburn,, 1974
)
, with corresponding estimator the quasi maximum likelihood (QML) estimator, and extends immediately to allow the variance function
v
to be an arbitrary function of the mean. The model
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{QGLM}}
therefore provides extra flexibility and robustness over
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{GLM}}
, but arguably is still restrictive in terms of the form of the conditional variance
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(Y\,|\,X)
being imposed.
This restriction can be alleviated
(
McCullagh and Nelder,, 1989
;
Nelder and Pregibon,, 1987
)
to allow the variance function to take an arbitrary function of the covariates

\mathcal{P}_{v}:=\Big\{P:g\big(\mathbb{E}_{P}[Y\,|\,X]\big)=X\beta,\;\mathrm{Var}_{P}(Y\,|\,X)=\nu_{\gamma}(X),\;\gamma\in\Gamma\Big\},

for some class of variance functions
\{\nu_{\gamma}:\gamma\in\Gamma\}
where
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}
for some
q\in\mathbb{N}
. This setting is sometimes termed pseudo maximum likelihood estimation
(
Gourieroux and Monfort,, 1993
;
Ziegler,, 2011
)
.

Given i.i.d. realisations
(Y_{i},X_{i})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
from a distribution (not necessarily in
\mathcal{P}_{v}
), and for each
\gamma\in\Gamma
defining a law in
\mathcal{P}_{v}
, a QML estimator
\tilde{\beta}(\gamma)
for
\beta
can be constructed, thus implying a class
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{QML}}
of estimators parametrised by the dispersion parameter
\gamma\in\Gamma
;

\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{QML}}=\bigg\{\tilde{\beta}(\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\;\sum_{i}\psi_{\text{QML}}(Y_{i},X_{i};\tilde{\beta}(\gamma))=0,\\
\psi_{\text{QML}}(Y_{i},X_{i},\beta)=\frac{(Y_{i}-g^{-1}(X_{i}\beta))}{g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(X_{i}\beta))\,\nu_{\gamma}(X_{i})}X_{i}^{T},\;\gamma\in\Gamma\bigg\},

When the ‘true distribution’ lies within the class
\mathcal{P}_{v}
, then provided the ‘true
\gamma
’ - that is the
\gamma\in\Gamma
that correctly specifies the variance function - can be estimated consistently, the resulting QML estimator for
\beta
is also efficient. In the semiparametric literature this property is termed
local efficiency

(
van der Laan and Robins,, 2003
;
Tsiatis,, 2006
;
Zhang et al.,, 2008
;
van der Laan and Rose,, 2018
)
.
In practice two prominent methods are commonly employed for dispersion estimation.
The first is extended quasi maximum likelihood estimation (EQML)
(
Nelder and Pregibon,, 1987
;
McCullagh and Nelder,, 1989
)
, that fits the pseudo-likelihood obtained if
Y\,|\,X
were normally distributed with variance
\nu_{\gamma}(X)
.
Note that in practice the terms EQML and QML are often used synonymously; for avoidance of doubt here we use EQML to refer to dispersion parameter estimation and QML to refer to specifically
\beta
estimation given a fixed dispersion
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
The second strategy is common in the literature for generalized estimating equations (GEE), which aims to estimate the dispersion parameter via a least squares regression of the squared Pearson residuals onto the hypothesised (scaled) variance model.
When the true distribution lies in
\mathcal{P}_{v}
both these strategies consistently estimate the optimal dispersion, resulting in locally efficient estimation.
However, local efficiency says nothing with regards to the variance of the estimator when the true distribution lies outside this model
\mathcal{P}_{v}
. We will study the broader model that only assumes the first conditional moment implied by the glm

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\mu}}:=\Big\{P:g\big(\mathbb{E}_{P}[Y\,|\,X]\big)=X\beta\Big\}.

Within
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\mu}}
every estimator in
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{QML}}
remains consistent and asymptotically Gaussian, albeit with differing variances. We will see that locally efficient estimators with respect to the class
\mathcal{P}_{v}
need not even be the estimator in
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{QML}}
of minimal variance when the true distribution lies within
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\mu}}\backslash\mathcal{P}_{v}
i.e. when the conditional variance model is misspecified, and in fact this suboptimality can be arbitrarily large (see Proposition
1
).
We begin by exploring a simple motivating example of the above in the clustered linear model (see Section
1.2.1
). First we introduce a few details specific to the linear model.

\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{QML}}
given by the quasi maximum likelihood

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\mu}}

1.1
Grouped data

The clustered quasi-Gaussian linear model is an example of a quasi generalized linear model for grouped data, asserting each cluster, consisting of a multivariate
Y
and matrix of covariates
X
, satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{P}[Y\,|\,X]=X\beta,\qquad\mathrm{Cov}_{P}(Y\,|\,X)=\Sigma_{\gamma}(X)\;(\gamma\in\Gamma),

(1)

for some variance model defined in terms of a dispersion parameter
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
Given i.i.d. data
(Y_{i},X_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(Y,X)
the class of QML estimators in the quasi-Gaussian linear model takes the form of the weighted least squares estimators

\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{QML}}=\bigg\{\tilde{\beta}(\gamma)=\Big(\sum_{i}X_{i}^{T}\Sigma_{\gamma}(X_{i})^{-1}X_{i}\Big)^{-1}\Big(\sum_{i}X_{i}^{T}\Sigma_{\gamma}(X_{i})^{-1}Y_{i}\Big):\,\gamma\in\Gamma\bigg\}.

(2)

A popular dispersion structure imposed for clustered data is the linear mixed effects model, which asserts

Y\,|\,X\sim N\big(X\beta\,,\;X^{(\text{sub})}\mathcal{V}X^{(\text{sub})T}+\sigma^{2}I\big),

for some subcollection
X^{\text{(sub)}}
of the full covariates
X
, positive definite matrix
\mathcal{V}
and
\sigma>0
, which motivates the class of weighted least squares estimators (
2
) with

\Sigma_{\gamma}(X)=X^{\text{(sub)}}\mathcal{V}X^{\text{(sub)}T}+\sigma^{2}I,\quad\gamma=(\mathcal{V},\sigma).

The quadratic conditional covariance introduced here is motivated through the imposition of fictitious ‘random effects’, whose aim is to motivate an estimator of reduced variance to the inefficient OLS estimator.
For longitudinal datasets the ARMA model is often used to imply a practical dispersion structure.
Given a working model
\Sigma_{\gamma}
, to estimate the dispersion parameter
\gamma
the EQML and GEE losses for the grouped linear model (
1
) correspond to

\displaystyle\gamma_{P,\mathrm{EQML}}

\displaystyle:=\underset{\gamma\in\Gamma}{\mathrm{argmin}}\,\mathbb{E}_{P}\Big[\log\Sigma_{\gamma}(X)+\varepsilon^{T}\Sigma_{\gamma}(X)^{-1}\varepsilon\Big]

\displaystyle\,=\underset{\gamma\in\Gamma}{\mathrm{argmin}}\,\mathbb{E}_{P}\Big[\log\Sigma_{\gamma}(X)+\mathrm{tr}\big\{\Sigma_{\gamma}(X)^{-1}\mathrm{Cov}_{P}(Y\,|\,X)\big\}\Big],

\displaystyle\gamma_{P,\mathrm{GEE}}

\displaystyle:=\underset{\gamma\in\Gamma}{\mathrm{argmin}}\,\mathbb{E}_{P}\Big[\big\|\Sigma_{\gamma}(X)-\varepsilon\varepsilon^{T}\|_{F}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\,=\underset{\gamma\in\Gamma}{\mathrm{argmin}}\,\mathbb{E}_{P}\Big[\big\|\Sigma_{\gamma}(X)-\mathrm{Cov}_{P}(Y\,|\,X)\|_{F}^{2}\Big],

(3)

at the population level, where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm. In the following example we explore the potential suboptimality of imposing the EQML loss to estimate the dispersion parameters in
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{QML}}
(
2
), with optimality given in terms of the variance of the
\beta
estimator. For similar examples also studying the GEE loss function see
Young and Shah, (2024)
.

Failure of (extended) quasi-maximum likelihood and generalized estimating equations

1.2.1
Longitudinal data example

We consider an example in the clustered linear model (
1
), where the conditional covariance is misspecified.
Suppose we have data consisting of i.i.d. groups each of size
50
and following the data generating mechanism

\displaystyle Y\,|\,X\sim N_{50}\big(X\beta,\,\Omega\big),\qquad X\sim N_{50}\big({\bf 0},\,0.9\,{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{T}+0.1\,I_{50}\big)

\displaystyle\Omega_{jk}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }j=k,\\
e^{-\frac{1}{2}|j-k|^{\frac{1}{4}}}+\frac{1}{4}\cos(|j-k|)e^{-\frac{1}{20}|j-k|}\;&\text{if }j\neq k.\end{cases}

where
\beta=1
is the estimand of interest. Note that by construction the model is homoscedastic, with correlation components bounded in
[0.24,0.74]
, and with
\Omega
of condition number approximately that of an
\text{AR}(1)
covariance matrix of the same group size and autoregressive parameter
0.9
.

2
) with weights
\Sigma_{\gamma}
taking the form of the covariance matrices of nested
\text{ARMA}(p,q)
processes with

(
Brockwell and Davis,, 1991
)
; and the quasi information (QIC) when solving GEEs
(
Pan,, 2001
;
Hardin and Hilbe,, 2003
)
. Both AIC and BIC are commonly used to compare mixed effects model structures
(
Pinheiro and Bates,, 2000
)
.

1.2.2
Heteroscedastic linear model

Whilst when the true law lies within the variance model
\mathcal{P}_{v}
the optimal dispersion parameter (that is the dispersion parameter that corresponds to the true conditional variance) is estimated consistently by a range of loss functions (including the EQML and GEE loss), when the true law lies within
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\mu}}\backslash\mathcal{P}_{v}
it is less clear precisely what the dispersion parameter being estimated by these objective functions converge to. As we see in the motivating example of Section
1.2.1
, when the quasi-maximum likelihood EQML loss is used to estimate dispersion, the resulting
\beta
estimator need not coincide with the
\beta
estimator of minimal variance amongst the class of estimators
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{QML}}
as in (
2
). In this section we explore this unfavourable behaviour in a theoretical framework.

In the remainder of this section we focus our attention on the ungrouped, univariate, heteroscedastic Gaussian linear model
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Het}}
, with the nested submodels

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Hom}}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{P:Y\,|\,X\sim N\big(X\beta,\,\sigma^{2}\big),\,\sigma>0\Big\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Var}}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{P:Y\,|\,X\sim N\big(X\beta,\,\nu_{\gamma}(X)\big),\,\gamma\in\Gamma\Big\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Het}}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{P:Y\,|\,X\sim N\big(X\beta,\,\nu_{\gamma}(X)\big),\,\mathbb{E}[\nu_{\gamma}(X)]<\infty,\,\nu_{\gamma}(X)>0\text{ a.s.}\Big\},

for a class of variance functions
\{\nu_{\gamma}:\gamma\in\Gamma\}
, and with random covariate design. In relation to the schematic of Figure
1
these models lie in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Hom}}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{GLM}}
,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Var}}\subset\mathcal{P}_{v}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Het}}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\mu}}
; we introduce these submodels to strengthen our theoretical results (Proposition
1
to follow). In this ungrouped setting the class of weighted least squares estimators
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{QML}}
takes the form (
2
) with
\Sigma_{\gamma}=\nu_{\gamma}
, with
\nu_{\gamma}
a scalar-valued variance function.
In particular, we show there exists distributions in the stricter Gaussian model
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Het}}

where optimising the EQML or GEE loss for dispersion estimation in the class of weighted least squares estimators (
2
) performs suboptimally in terms of the asymptotic variance of the
\beta
estimator

V_{P}(\gamma):={\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigg[\frac{X^{2}}{\nu_{\gamma}(X)}\bigg]}^{-2}{\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigg[\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{P}(Y\,|\,X)\,X^{2}}{\nu_{\gamma}(X)^{2}}\bigg]}.

(4)

We call this function the
sandwich loss
, which we later generalise to multilevel generalized linear models (see Section
3.1
), with this terminology motivated from the sandwich estimator of
Huber, (1967)
.

One might intuitively expect
if the ‘working variance’ model
\{\nu_{\gamma}:\,\gamma\in\Gamma\}
is misspecified but close to the true model then near-efficient estimation could follow; this is perhaps the general thinking behind a practitioner’s use of a specific working variance model.
However we show in the following proposition this is not necessarily the case.
Recall that the Kullback–Leibler divergence between distributions
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
both absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure is given by

\mathrm{KL}(P_{1}\,\|\,P_{2}):=\int\log\bigg(\frac{dP_{1}}{dP_{2}}\bigg)\,dP_{1}.

Proposition 1
.

Consider the semiparametric, ungrouped, univariate, linear model
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Het}}
. Also consider the asymptotic (population-level) variances (
4
) of the quasi-maximum likelihood (weighted least squares) estimators corresponding to the working variance model

\nu_{\gamma}\in\big\{x\mapsto\gamma_{1}\,\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x)+\gamma_{2}\,\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(x)\,:\,\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\big\},

(5)

for the EQML and GEE methods given by (
3
).
Then for any
\tau,\sigma>0
, arbitrarily small
\alpha>0
and arbitrarily large
\eta\geq 1
, there exists a distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Het}}
with

\displaystyle\inf_{\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Hom}}}\mathrm{KL}(\tilde{P}\,\|\,P)\leq\alpha\quad\;\mathrm{Var}_{P}(X)=\tau^{2},\quad\;\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mathrm{Var}_{P}(Y\,|\,X)]=\sigma^{2},

(6)

\displaystyle\frac{V_{P}(\gamma_{P,\mathrm{EQML}})\wedge V_{P}(\gamma_{P,\mathrm{GEE}})}{\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}V_{P}(\gamma)}\geq\eta.

(7)

It is perhaps worrying that taking Proposition
1
with
\eta
arbitrarily large that the EQML and GEE dispersion estimators can result in arbitrarily suboptimal
\beta
estimation within the class of estimators
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{QML}}
. Further, by taking
\alpha>0
arbitrarily small we see that this can occur for distributions arbitrarily close to the working variance model imposed, and even arbitrarily close to the homoscedastic model. It follows that the optimality of dispersion parameter estimation when the working (co)variance model is misspecified crucially hinges on the objective function used, with EQML and GEE based optimisations potentially performing rather poorly.
These issues however can be overcome by simply proposing (
4
) as the loss function for dispersion estimation, thereby targetting the variance of the
\beta
estimator directly.
This framework allows for a practitioner to achieve the best of both worlds; sufficiently restricting the class of estimators to be both computationally practical and avoid overfitting, whilst also maintaining optimal accuracy within the class of estimators proposed against an expansive class of statistical models.

1.3
Organisation of the remainder of the paper

From hereon we will focus on the setting of clustered data in the multilevel generalized linear model, due to variance gains tending to be more pronounced in practice in this setting. Our methodology however is equally applicable to unclustered generalized linear models. The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. In Section
2.1
we review some relevant literature on multivariate generalized linear models. We outline the semiparametric multilevel generalized linear model in Section
2
, and introduce the parametric class of fixed effects estimators from which our goal is to select amongst this class the estimator of minimal variance within the full semiparametric model.
In Section
3
we formally define the sandwich loss for this model, and introduce an empirical approximate Jackknife estimator for it in Section
3.1
.
An estimator for the standard errors of the resulting fixed effects estimators
is proposed in Section
3.2
, with applications to model selection discussed in Section
3.3
. Numerical experiments are explored in Section
4
, both on simulated data and two real-world datasets.

2
The semiparametric multilevel generalized linear model

Suppose we observe
I
independent data clusters, with
i
th cluster
(Y_{i},X_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}\times p}
of group size
n_{i}
satisfying the semiparametric multilevel generalized linear model

g\big(\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\,|\,X_{i}]\big)=X_{i}\beta,

(8)

for some population averaged estimand
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and known, strictly increasing link function
g
that we take with an abuse of notation to apply component-wise to a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
, and where the covariate design may be interpreted as either random or fixed. Note specifically we make no assumptions on the form of the conditional covariance
\mathrm{Cov}(Y_{i}\,|\,X_{i})
.
A semiparametric efficient estimator for
\beta
in this model solves the estimating equation

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{I}\psi_{\text{eff}}(Y_{i},X_{i};\beta)=0,\qquad\psi_{\text{eff}}(Y,X;\beta):=D(X;\beta)^{T}\,\mathrm{Cov}(Y\,|\,X)^{-1}\,\big(Y-g^{-1}(X\beta)\big),

\displaystyle\big(D(X;\beta)\big)_{jk}:=\Big(\frac{1}{g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(X\beta))}\circ X\Big)_{jk}=\frac{X_{jk}}{g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(X_{j\boldsymbol{\cdot}}\beta))}.

where
\psi_{\text{eff}}
is known as the (scaled) semiparametric efficient influence function; see for example
Tsiatis, (2006)
for a derivation. This estimator is that of minimal variance over all regular, asymptotically linear estimators for
\beta
over all distributions in (
8
).
To circumvent the need to estimate the non-parametric matrix-valued nuisance function
\mathrm{Cov}(Y\,|\,X)

we consider the class
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{QML}}
of quasi-maximum likelihood estimators given by the estimating equations

\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{QML}}:=\bigg\{\tilde{\beta}(\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\,\sum_{i=1}^{I}\psi(Y_{i},X_{i};\tilde{\beta}(\gamma),\gamma)=0,\;\psi(Y_{i},X_{i};\beta,\gamma)=D_{i}(\beta)W_{i}(\beta,\gamma)\big(Y_{i}-\mu_{i}(\beta)\big),\\
D_{i}(\beta):=\frac{\partial\mu_{i}(\beta)}{\partial\beta^{T}},\;\mu_{i}(\beta):=g^{-1}(X_{i}\beta),\;W_{i}(\beta,\gamma):=\Sigma_{i}(\gamma)^{-1}=A_{i}(\beta)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{P}_{i}(\gamma)^{-1}A_{i}(\beta)^{-\frac{1}{2}},\\
A_{i}(\beta):=\mathrm{diag}\big(v(\mu_{i}(\beta))\big),\;\gamma\in\Gamma\bigg\},

(9)

where
\mathrm{P}
is a pseudo-correlation matrix (that may also model variances in misspecified scenarios),
\Sigma
a full working variance model,
v
the generalized linear model’s variance function,
and
\Gamma
a Euclidean space spanning the possible dispersion parameters
\gamma\in\Gamma
. We explore a few examples of models that imply dispersion structures on
\mathrm{P}_{i}(\gamma)
and
\Sigma_{i}(\gamma)
. From hereon we denote dependence on
X_{i}
in quantities such as
\mathrm{P}_{i}(\gamma)
,
\Sigma_{i}(\gamma)
,
D_{i}(\beta)
,
\mu_{i}(\beta)
,
W_{i}(\beta,\gamma)
through the
i
subscript.

Example 1

(Classes of Mixed Effects Models)
.

Mixed effects models
(
Diggle et al.,, 2013
)
have proved popular in practice. Linear mixed effects models, as well as Tweedie generalized mixed effects models
(
Lee and Nelder,, 1996
;
Lee et al.,, 2006
)
with multiplicative random effects and a log-link function, have the popular robustness properties that the ‘subject specific’ and ‘population averaged’ quantities coincide. The Tweedie family of distributions allow for flexible modeling of positive continuous (e.g. zero-inflated) data as well as count data, with hierarchical and longitudinal extensions proposed by
Ma and Jørgensen, (2007)
and
Holst and Jørgensen, (2010)
respectively.

Example 2

(Multivariate Dispersion Families)
.

Jørgensen and Martınez, (2013)
;
Jørgensen, (2013)
construct a class of multivariate dispersion model for generalized linear models with multivariate responses. These provide a general and flexible class of models that include the elliptically contoured distributions.

Example 3

(ARMA, GARCH, and ARMA-GARCH models)
.

For longitudinal data ARMA
(
Box et al.,, 1994
)
and GARCH
(
Engle,, 1982
)
model structures can be used to model conditional correlation and variance respectively, with ARMA-GARCH models combining both. Together, these imply a parametric structure on
\mathrm{Cov}(Y_{i}\,|\,X_{i})

which is commonly estimated by (extended quasi) maximum likelihood. See
Box et al., (1994)
and
Brockwell and Davis, (1991)
for a review.

2.1
Some related literature in multilevel models

Given a dispersion model, such as those outlined in the previous subsection, two estimation strategies have dominated both theory and practice. Both follow the general paradigm of a weighted least squares regression of squared residuals to a given parametric covariance structure, namely solving the estimating equation

\sum_{i=1}^{I}\mathrm{tr}\big(\mathcal{W}_{i}(\hat{r}_{i}\hat{r}_{i}^{T}-\Sigma_{i}(\gamma))\big)=0,

(10)

for the dispersion parameter
\gamma
, where
\hat{r}_{i}=Y_{i}-g^{-1}(X_{i}\hat{\beta})
denotes the residuals of
I
clustered multivariate datapoints,
\Sigma_{i}(\gamma)
is some dispersion matrix parametrisation and
\mathcal{W}_{i}
is some weighting matrix that will vary depending on the method considered.

Nelder and Pregibon, (1987)
;
Hall and Severini, (1998)
propose the extended quasi maximum likelihood (EQML) estimating equation, that corresponds to maximising the likelihood were the residuals to follow a mean zero multivariate normal distribution with covariance
\Sigma_{i}(\gamma)
, subsequently adopted by
Ma and Jørgensen, (2007)
;
Holst and Jørgensen, (2010)
;
Jørgensen and Knudsen, (2004)
among others.
The second method is GEE1
(
Liang et al.,, 1992
)
(GEE), which determines the dispersion parameters by minimising a weighted least squares on the cross-products of the Pearson residuals
(
Zeger and Liang,, 1986
;
Hardin and Hilbe,, 2003
;
Ziegler,, 2011
)
. In theory, any residual could fall within this GEE framework, with Pearson residuals often employed in practice. In summary, these methods solve the estimating equation (
10
) with

\mathcal{W}_{i}=\begin{cases}\Sigma_{i}(\gamma)^{-1}\frac{\partial\Sigma_{i}(\gamma)}{\partial\gamma}\Sigma_{i}(\gamma)^{-1}&\text{for EQML \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{severini1}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}}\\
\mathrm{diag}\big(v(\mu_{i}(\hat{\beta}))\big)^{-1}\frac{\partial\Sigma_{i}(\gamma)}{\partial\gamma}\mathrm{diag}\big(v(\mu_{i}(\hat{\beta}))\big)^{-1}&\text{for GEE \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{zeger-liang}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}}\end{cases},

(11)

where
v
denotes the glm’s variance function and
\hat{\beta}
is a pilot estimator for
\beta
.
In finite samples both of these objectives have been adapted by adding a perturbation to the estimating equations such that the estimating equation (
10
) remains unbiased
(
McCullagh and Tibshirani,, 1990
;
Severini,, 2002
;
Jørgensen and Knudsen,, 2004
)
.

It is often noted that the homoscedasticity assumption, for which variance estimators of fixed effects estimators in generalized linear models often rely on, is often violated in practice. The sandwich estimator of the variance
(
Huber,, 1967
;
White,, 1980
;
Gourieroux et al.,, 1984
;
Royall,, 1986
;
Zeger and Liang,, 1986
)
is often employed to allow for asymptotically valid estimates of standard errors in heteroscedastic settings. Finite sample adaptations have been proposed in ordinary least squares regression, including so called ‘heteroscedasticity consistent’ estimators of the variance (HC1–5)
(
MacKinnon and White,, 1985
;
Cribari-Neto and da Silva,, 2011
)
, which in recent years has been extended to clustered data
(
MacKinnon et al., 2023a,
;
MacKinnon et al., 2023b,
)
.

3
Sandwich regression

3.1
The finite sample empirical sandwich loss

Consider the target of interest being a linear combination
c^{T}\beta
of the fixed effects, for some deterministic vector
c\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, with an attributed notion of optimality for an estimator
\hat{\beta}
being minimality of
\mathrm{Var}(c^{T}\hat{\beta})=c^{T}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta})c
in the class of QML estimators
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{QML}}
(
9
).
Recall sandwich regression estimates the optimal dispersion
\gamma
in
\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{QML}}
by minimising an estimate of the varaince of the scalar target of interest, in this case
\mathrm{Var}(c^{T}\tilde{\beta}(\gamma))
, which takes the form

\displaystyle V(\gamma)

\displaystyle:=\textrm{M}(\gamma)^{-1}\textrm{S}(\gamma)\textrm{M}(\gamma)^{-1},

(12)

\displaystyle\textrm{M}(\gamma)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\mathbb{E}\Big[D_{i}(\tilde{\beta}(\gamma))^{T}W_{i}(\tilde{\beta}(\gamma),\gamma)D_{i}(\tilde{\beta}(\gamma))\Big]

\displaystyle\textrm{S}(\gamma)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\mathbb{E}\Big[D_{i}(\tilde{\beta}(\gamma))^{T}W_{i}(\tilde{\beta}(\gamma),\gamma)\mathrm{Cov}(Y_{i}\,|\,X_{i})W_{i}(\tilde{\beta}(\gamma),\gamma)D_{i}(\tilde{\beta}(\gamma))\Big].

Young and Shah, (2024)
introduce an asymptotically unbiased estimator for this sandwich loss, which performs well in large samples but in finite samples is downwards biased. We therefore introduce the finite sample empirical sandwich loss, given by

L_{\mathrm{SL}}(\gamma):=c^{T}\tilde{V}(\gamma)c,\qquad\tilde{V}(\gamma):=\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\big(\tilde{\beta}_{(-i)}(\gamma)-\tilde{\beta}(\gamma)\big)\big(\tilde{\beta}_{(-i)}(\gamma)-\tilde{\beta}(\gamma)\big)^{T},

(13)

where
\tilde{\beta}_{(-i)}(\gamma)
solves the analogous estimating equation of (
9
) on the
i
th cluster leave-one-out sample.
This estimator approximates the jackknife estimator of
\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{\beta}(\gamma))
as in
Efron and Stein, (1981)
for a fixed
\gamma
.
In the linear (
g=\text{id}
), ungrouped (
n_{i}\equiv 1
), unweighted (
W_{i}
constant) setting this estimator reduces to the ‘hetersocedastic consistent’ (HC3) estimator of the OLS estimator’s variance; see for example
MacKinnon and White, (1985)
;
Cribari-Neto and da Silva, (2011)
for an overview and
MacKinnon et al., 2023a ()
for a number of relevant simulations. In recent years this HC3 estimator has been extended to the cluster regression setting
(
MacKinnon et al., 2023b,
)
for the OLS estimator.

The sandwich loss (
13
) can be calculated in
O(n)
operations for the linear model, and can be approximated for the generalized linear model at the same computational order, as outlined in Algorithm
1
. The computational approximation methods employed here are analogous to those used when performing leave-one-out cross-validation in glms
(
Hardin and Hilbe,, 2003
;
Beirami et al.,, 2017
)
by minimising a locally quadratic approximation about the full-sample solution
\tilde{\beta}(\gamma)
of the leave-one-out objective;

\tilde{\beta}_{(-i)}(\gamma)\approx\tilde{\beta}(\gamma)-\bigg(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{I}D_{i^{\prime}}^{T}W_{i^{\prime}}D_{i^{\prime}}-D_{i}^{T}W_{i}D_{i}\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(D_{i}^{T}W_{i}\big(Y_{i}-\mu_{i}(\tilde{\beta}(\gamma))\big)\bigg),

(14)

where for notational convenience we take
D_{i}:=D_{i}(\tilde{\beta}(\gamma))
and
W_{i}:=W_{i}(\tilde{\beta}(\gamma),\gamma)
.
Note that this approximation is exact in the linear model.

3.2
Estimating standard errors for fixed effects

Often confidence interval construction and test statistics in mixed effects models and GEEs rely on the asymptotic assumption that the dispersion parameter estimator
\hat{\gamma}
converges in probability to some deterministic vector
\gamma
at
\sqrt{n}
-rate. Whilst this is reasonable in an asymptotic regime, for small or even moderate samples not accounting for finite sample corrections can result in undercoverage of confidence intervals. These issues for mixed effects models and GEEs at finite samples also pervade sandwich regression.
We therefore introduce an estimator of the (co)variance of sandwich regression
\beta
estimators that takes into account the variation in dispersion estimation. This takes the form of a Jackknife estimator as in
Efron and Stein, (1981)
. Note ordinary heteroscedasticity-robust (HC3) estimators of the variance
(
MacKinnon and White,, 1985
;
MacKinnon et al., 2023a,
;
MacKinnon et al., 2023b,
;
Cribari-Neto and da Silva,, 2011
)
study a Jackknife estimator of the variance for the OLS estimator; we consider a generalisation that additionally allows for dispersion estimation, as well as extends to the generalized linear model.

The estimator for the variance of the sandwich regression
\hat{\beta}
estimator is given in Algorithm
2
. We highlight a few key components of this algorithm. First we introduce some notation. Denoting the sandwich loss (
13
) as
L_{\mathrm{SL}}
with the leave-(
i
)-out version
L_{\mathrm{SL}(-i)}
as (
13
) omitting the
i
th observation, each with minimisers
\hat{\gamma}
and
\hat{\gamma}_{(-i)}
respectively. Also recall the functions
\tilde{\beta},\tilde{\beta}_{(-i)}:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Then we define the terms

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}=\underset{\gamma\in\Gamma}{\mathrm{argmin}}\,L_{\mathrm{SL}}(\gamma),\qquad\tilde{\gamma}_{(-i)}=\underset{\gamma\in\Gamma}{\mathrm{argmin}}\,L_{\mathrm{SL}(-i)}(\gamma),

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{(-i)}=\tilde{\beta}_{(-i)}(\hat{\gamma}_{(-i)}),\qquad\check{\beta}_{(-i)}=\tilde{\beta}_{(-i)}(\hat{\gamma}),\qquad\hat{\beta}=\tilde{\beta}(\hat{\gamma}).

All these terms are used as intermediaries to calculate the Jackknife variance estimator
\hat{V}
, with
\hat{\gamma}_{(-i)}
,
\hat{\beta}_{(-i)}
and
\check{\beta}_{(-i)}
approximated. These are made computationally tractable by using Newton-type iterative steps of a locally quadratic approximation of their respective leave-one-out objectives about their full-sample minimisers.
Algorithm
2
performs one iterative step to approximate these quantities.
In substantially smaller samples, where both the above locally quadratic approximation may be unreasonable and the computational considerations at play are less of a burden, multiple iterative steps of the above optimisation can be performed, editing lines 5, 6 and/or 8 of Algorithm
2
accordingly.

3.3
Dispersion model selection

Constructing estimators in (
9
) requires a hypothesised (likely misspecified) working dispersion structure, parametrised by
\gamma\in\Gamma
. In practice the space of working variance functions must be kept small enough to avoid overfitting and remain computationally competitive, yet large enough for a sufficiently reduced variance. As seen in Section
1.2.1
model selection criteria such as AIC(c), BIC or QIC - that choose the model which minimises an estimator of a penalised KL divergence - can perform poorly under model misspecification of the dispersion structure.
The asymptotic variance estimator
\hat{V}
of Algorithm
2
could be used as a model selection criterion that is robust to variance model misspecification. To compare
M
working (co)variance models
\left\{\mathcal{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{M}_{M}\right\}
one could merely calculate
\hat{V}(\mathcal{M}_{1}),\ldots,\hat{V}(\mathcal{M}_{M})
for each model as in Algorithm
2
and select the optimal dispersion model
\hat{\mathcal{M}}:=\mathrm{argmin}_{\mathcal{M}\in\{\mathcal{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{M}_{M}\}}c^{T}\hat{V}(\mathcal{M})c
.
Whilst for a large number of models this could be relatively computationally complex, for nested models the computational burden could be alleviated by warm-starting the minimisations of of the augmented
\hat{\gamma}
by the minimising dispersion in the largest nested model.

Remark 2
.

If post selection inference is desired, the estimator
\hat{V}(\mathcal{M}_{\hat{m}})
no longer represents the variance of the corresponding
\beta
estimator (as the uncertainty in
\hat{m}
remains unaccounted for), as is standard in post selection inference. A simple (but computationally more involved) Jackknife adaptation of Algorithm
2
could be used in such settings for post selection inference.

4.1.1
The multilevel linear model

Consider the linear model

\displaystyle Y_{i}\,|\,X_{i}\sim N_{4}(X_{i}\beta,\,\Sigma(X_{i})),\qquad X_{i}\sim N_{4}({\bf 0},I_{4}),

\displaystyle\Sigma(X_{i})_{jk}:=\rho(j,k)\sigma(X_{ij})\sigma(X_{ik}),\quad\sigma(x):=1+\lambda\exp(-2x^{2}),\quad\rho(j,k):=\mathbbm{1}_{(j=k)}+0.5\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{(j\neq k)},

where
\beta=1

Note therefore that the case

\lambda=0

(heteroscedastic). The class of estimators in each case is taken as (
9

4.1.2
The multilevel binomial model

Consider the binomial model satisfying

\displaystyle Y_{ij}\,|\,X_{i}\sim\text{Bernoulli}\big(\text{expit}(X_{ij}\beta)\big),\qquad X_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}N_{20}({\bf 0}\,,\,0.5\cdot{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{T}+0.5\cdot I_{20}),

where
\beta=1

Well specified covariance structure:
We consider an equicorrelated setting where

\mathrm{Corr}(Y_{ij},Y_{ik})\approx 0.4,

for

generated through the copula

\displaystyle Z_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\text{ARMA}(2,2),\qquad U_{ij}:=\Phi(Z_{ij}),\qquad Y_{ij}:=\mathbbm{1}(\{U_{ij}\geq 1-\text{expit}(X_{ij}\beta)\}),

\rho=(1.00,\,-0.07,\,0.41,\,0.12,\,0.25,\,0.16,\,0.19,\,0.16,\,0.16,\,0.14,\,0.14,\,\\

We study two examples to demonstrate the benefits of sandwich regression on real world datasets. 5
Discussion

It is commonly said model misspecification should be recognised in estimation tasks
(
Box et al.,, 1994
;
Vansteelandt and Dukes,, 2022
)
. In settings where a practitioner only has access to a moderate quantity of data it may be unreasonable to make the minimal possible identifiability assumptions on the data generating distribution, and some form of model structure may be relied on. We have studied a broad class of (multilevel) generalized linear models where the first conditional moment of the response takes a parametric form, but no additional assumptions are made on the conditional covariance. We recover a notion of optimality within a user-chosen restricted class of estimators by minimising an empirical estimator of the sandwich loss, that is seen to work effectively on a number of simulated and real-world datasets.

The idea of relinquishing efficiency in light of computational or robustness considerations is certainly not new. Asking for a sense of global optimality given constraints on the estimator class has been studied for certain shape constrained classes:
Hampel, (1974)
considers minimal variance estimation given an upper bound on the gross error sensitivity; and
Feng et al., (2024)
consider convexity constraints on the loss function used in linear regression.
Broadly speaking, this work aims to add to a growing body of work that maintains notions of optimality globally within a broad class of models given constraints on the class of estimators considered.
Given the increasingly complex data structures studied in practice, and correspondingly the potential necessity for constrained estimation, we look forward to seeing future developments and insights more broadly in this field.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

We define a family of distributions
P_{\delta}
for
(Y,X)
in terms of the constant
\delta>0
, all of which satisfy the conditions (
6
); to obtain the lower bound (
7
) we will take
\delta
sufficiently large.

We first generate a class of marginal distributions on
X
parametrised by
\delta>0
. Define the constant

\lambda_{2}:=\frac{1}{2}\vee\bigg(1-\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}\bigg),

noting
\lambda_{2}\in\big[\frac{1}{2},1\big)
.
Also, given
\delta>0
define the functions

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\delta):=\frac{1-\lambda_{2}}{2\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{1}{1+x^{4}}dx},\qquad\nu^{2}(\delta):=\frac{\tau^{2}}{\lambda_{2}}-\frac{1-\lambda_{2}}{\lambda_{2}}\rho(\delta),\qquad\rho(\delta):=\frac{\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{x^{2}}{1+x^{4}}dx}{\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{1}{1+x^{4}}dx}.

It can be verified that
\rho(\delta)\in(0,1)
for all
\delta>0
(as
\rho
can be shown to be a strictly increasing function with
\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\rho(\delta)=0
and
\lim_{\delta\to\infty}\rho(\delta)=1
) and thus

\inf_{\delta>0}\nu^{2}(\delta)\geq\frac{\tau^{2}-1+\lambda_{2}}{\lambda_{2}}=1-\frac{2(1-\tau^{2})}{1\vee(2-\tau^{2})}>0,

noting
\tau>0
.
Now take
X
to have the density

p_{\delta}(x)=\frac{\lambda_{1}(\delta)}{1+x^{4}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|\leq\delta\}}+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\sqrt{2\pi\nu^{2}(\delta)}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\nu^{2}(\delta)}}.

(16)

Note in particular that
p_{\delta}
is positive on
\mathbb{R}
with

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{\delta}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}(\delta)\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}\frac{1}{1+x^{4}}dx+\lambda_{2}=1-\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{2}=1,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}xp_{\delta}(x)dx

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}p_{\delta}(x)dx

\displaystyle=2\lambda_{1}(\delta)\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{x^{2}}{1+x^{4}}dx+\lambda_{2}\nu^{2}(\delta)=(1-\lambda_{2})\lambda_{1}(\delta)+\tau^{2}-(1-\lambda_{2})\lambda_{1}(\delta)=\tau^{2}.

Thus for any
\delta>0
if
X
follows the distribution with density
p_{\delta}
then
\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}p_{\delta}(x)dx=\tau^{2}
.
Further,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{4}p_{\delta}(x)dx=2\lambda_{1}(\delta)\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{x^{4}}{1+x^{4}}dx=(1-\lambda_{2})B(\delta),

where

B(\delta):=\frac{\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{x^{4}}{1+x^{4}}dx}{\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{1}{1+x^{4}}dx}.

(17)

We now define a conditional law for
Y\,|\,X
. Take

Y\,|\,X\sim N\big(X\beta,\,\sigma^{2}(X)\big),\qquad\sigma^{2}(X):=c_{1}+c_{2}X^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(X),

(18)

for a pair of positive constants
(c_{1},c_{2})
satisfying
c_{1}+\frac{c_{2}\tau^{2}}{2}=\sigma^{2}
and
c_{2}\leq c_{1}
, to be determined shortly.
We denote by
P_{\delta}
the joint distribution on
(Y,X)
admitting the density
p(y\,|\,x)p_{\delta}(x)
where
p(y\,|\,x)
is the density given by (
18
) and
p_{\delta}(x)
is the density (
16
).
We also introduce the law
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Hom}}
to be the distribution with conditional distribution
Y\,|\,X\sim N(X\beta,c_{1})
, for which we denote the corresponding conditional density
p^{\prime}(y\,|\,x)
, and with marginal density for
X
given by
p_{\delta}(x)
. Then

\displaystyle\inf_{\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Hom}}}\mathrm{KL}(\tilde{P}\,\|\,P_{\delta})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{KL}(P^{\prime}\,\|\,P_{\delta})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}p^{\prime}(y\,|\,x)p_{\delta}(x)\log\bigg(\frac{p^{\prime}(y\,|\,x)p_{\delta}(x)}{p(y\,|\,x)p_{\delta}(x)}\bigg)\,dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{\delta}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}}p^{\prime}(y\,|\,x)\log\bigg(\frac{p^{\prime}(y\,|\,x)}{p(y\,|\,x)}\bigg)\,dydx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{\delta}(x)\Big\{\log\big(1+c_{1}^{-1}c_{2}x^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x)\big)+\frac{1}{1+c_{1}^{-1}c_{2}x^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x)}-1\Big\}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\delta}(x)\Big\{\log\big(1+c_{1}^{-1}c_{2}x^{2}\big)+\frac{1}{1+c_{1}^{-1}c_{2}x^{2}}-1\Big\}\,dx

\displaystyle=I\big(c_{1}^{-1}c_{2},\delta\big),

where

I(c,\delta):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\delta}(x)\Big\{\log\big(1+cx^{2}\big)+\frac{1}{1+cx^{2}}-1\Big\}\,dx,

and where we use the well known result

\mathrm{KL}\big(N(\mu,\sigma_{1}^{2})\,\|\,N(\mu,\sigma_{2}^{2})\big)=\frac{1}{2}\bigg\{\log\bigg(\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg)+\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}}{\sigma_{2}^{2}}-1\bigg\},

in addition to using Fubini’s Theorem, which can be applied as

\displaystyle\quad\;\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta,c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\\
c_{2}\leq c_{1}\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg|p^{\prime}(y\,|\,x)p_{\delta}(x)\log\bigg(\frac{p^{\prime}(y\,|\,x)}{p(y\,|\,x)}\bigg)\bigg|\,dydx

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta,c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\\
c_{2}\leq c_{1}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{\delta}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}}p^{\prime}(y\,|\,x)\bigg|\log\bigg(\frac{c_{1}+c_{2}x^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x)}{c_{1}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\bigg(\frac{1}{c_{1}+c_{2}x^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x)}-\frac{1}{c_{1}}\bigg)(y-x\beta)^{2}\bigg|\,dydx

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta,c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\\
c_{2}\leq c_{1}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{\delta}(x)\bigg|\log\big(1+c_{1}^{-1}c_{2}x^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x)\big)+\frac{1}{1+c_{1}^{-1}c_{2}x^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x)}-1\bigg|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta,c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\\
c_{2}\leq c_{1}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{\delta}(x)\bigg(\underbrace{\log\big(1+c_{1}^{-1}c_{2}x^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x)\big)}_{\leq\log(1+x^{2})\leq x^{2}}+\underbrace{\bigg|\frac{1}{1+c_{1}^{-1}c_{2}x^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x)}-1\bigg|}_{\leq 1}\bigg)dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tau^{2}+1}{2}<\infty.

The integral function
I(c,\delta)

is well-defined on
(0,1]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and can also easily be shown to be differentiable (its derivatives take explicit forms in terms of integrals that are also well-defined). Thus
I(c,\delta)
is continuous on
(0,1]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Further

\sup_{c\in(0,1]}\sup_{\delta>0}I(c,\delta)=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{c\in(0,1]}\sup_{\delta>0}\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\delta}(x)\Big|\log\big(1+cx^{2}\big)+\frac{1}{1+cx^{2}}-1\Big|\\
\leq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{c\in(0,1]}\sup_{\delta>0}\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\delta}(x)\Big\{\underbrace{\log\big(1+cx^{2}\big)}_{\leq\log(1+x^{2})\leq x^{2}}+\underbrace{\Big|\frac{1}{1+cx^{2}}-1\Big|}_{\leq 1}\Big\}\\
\leq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{c\in(0,1]}\sup_{\delta>0}\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}p_{\delta}(x)dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\delta}(x)dx\Big)\leq\frac{\tau^{2}+1}{4},

is bounded.
Also as
I(0,\delta)=0
for all
\delta>0
it follows that
i(c):=\sup_{\delta>0}I(c,\delta)
is continuous on
(0,1]
with
i(0)=0
. Therefore for any
\alpha>0
there exists a
\tilde{c}\in(0,1]
such that
i(\tilde{c})\leq\alpha
, and thus

\sup_{\delta>0}I(\tilde{c},\delta)\leq\alpha.

Now take

c_{1}:=\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{2+\tilde{c}\tau^{2}},\qquad c_{2}:=\frac{2\tilde{c}\sigma^{2}}{2+\tilde{c}\tau^{2}}.

(19)

It then follows that

\inf_{\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Hom}}}\mathrm{KL}(\tilde{P}\,\|\,P_{\delta})\leq I(c_{1}^{-1}c_{2},\delta)\leq\sup_{\delta>0}I(\tilde{c},\delta)\leq\alpha,

for all
\delta>0
.
Additionally, by construction of
(c_{1},c_{2})
in (
19

\mathbb{E}_{P_{\delta}}[\mathrm{Var}_{P_{\delta}}(Y\,|\,X)]=c_{1}+c_{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{P_{\delta}}[X^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(X)]=c_{1}+\frac{c_{2}\tau^{2}}{2}=\sigma^{2}.

Note similar arguments could be employed to construct a
(c_{1},c_{2})
pair such that additionally for all
\delta>0
we have
\inf_{\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Hom}}}\mathrm{KL}(P_{\delta}\,\|\,\tilde{P})\leq\alpha
.

To summarise, to this point we have constructed a class of laws
P_{\delta}
that satisfy

\inf_{\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Hom}}}\mathrm{KL}(\tilde{P}\,\|\,P_{\delta}),\quad\mathrm{Var}_{P_{\delta}}(X)=\tau^{2},\quad\mathbb{E}_{P_{\delta}}[\mathrm{Var}_{P_{\delta}}(Y\,|\,X)]=\sigma^{2},

for any
\delta>0
. The remainder of the proof will construct a specific law within this class such that (
7

\gamma
should be taken as with respect to
P_{\delta}
.

Now, it can be shown that the minimisers of each of the EQML and GEE losses (see e.g.
Young and Shah, (2024)
) over the class (
5

are

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathrm{EQML},1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{\mathrm{GEE},1}=\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{2}(X)\,|\,X\geq 0]=c_{1}+c_{2}\tau^{2},

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathrm{EQML},2}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{\mathrm{GEE},2}=\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{2}(X)\,|\,X<0]=c_{1},

and thus writing
\tilde{\gamma}:=\gamma_{\mathrm{EQML}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{GEE}}
it follows that

V(\gamma_{\mathrm{EQML}})=V(\gamma_{\mathrm{GEE}})=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{X^{2}}{\nu_{\tilde{\gamma}}(X)}\bigg]^{-2}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{X^{2}\sigma^{2}(X)}{\nu^{2}_{\tilde{\gamma}}(X)}\bigg].

Further, it follows immediately from the Cauchy–Schwarz Theorem that

\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}V(\gamma)=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{X^{2}}{\sigma^{2}(X)}\bigg]^{-1}.

Thus

\displaystyle\quad\frac{V(\gamma_{\mathrm{EQML}})\wedge V(\gamma_{\mathrm{GEE}})}{\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}V(\gamma)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{X^{2}}{\nu_{\tilde{\gamma}}(X)}\bigg]^{-2}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{X^{2}}{\sigma^{2}(X)}\bigg]\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{X^{2}\sigma^{2}(X)}{\nu^{2}_{\tilde{\gamma}}(X)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\Big(\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\big[X^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(X)\big]+\tilde{\gamma}_{2}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\big[X^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(X)\big]\Big)^{-2}

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\Big(\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{-2}\mathbb{E}\big[X^{2}\sigma^{2}(X)\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(X)\big]+\tilde{\gamma}_{2}^{-2}\mathbb{E}\big[X^{2}\sigma^{2}(X)\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(X)\big]\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\bigg(\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{X^{2}}{\sigma^{2}(X)}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(X)\bigg]+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{X^{2}}{\sigma^{2}(X)}\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(X)\bigg]\bigg)

\displaystyle=\Big((c_{1}+c_{2}\tau^{2})^{-1}\mathbb{E}\big[X^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(X)\big]+c_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\big[X^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(X)\big]\Big)^{-2}

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot{\Big((c_{1}+c_{2}\tau^{2})^{-2}\big(c_{1}\mathbb{E}\big[X^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(X)\big]+c_{2}\mathbb{E}\big[X^{4}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(X)\big]\big)+c_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\big[X^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(X)\big]\Big)}

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\bigg(\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{X^{2}}{c_{1}+c_{2}X^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(X)\bigg]+c_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\big[X^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(X)\big]\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(c_{1}+c_{2}\tau^{2})^{-2}c_{2}\mathbb{E}[X^{4}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(X)]\cdot c_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{E}[X^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(X)]}{\big(\frac{1}{c_{1}+c_{2}\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{c_{1}}\big)^{2}\big(\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}\big)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{1}c_{2}}{2\tau^{2}(c_{1}+2c_{2}\tau^{2})}\,\mathbb{E}[X^{4}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(X)]

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{1}c_{2}(1-\lambda_{2})}{2\tau^{2}(c_{1}+2c_{2}\tau^{2})}\,B(\delta),

making use of

\mathbb{E}[X^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(X)]=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(X)]=\int_{0}^{\delta}x^{2}p_{\delta}(x)dx=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]=\frac{\tau^{2}}{2},

as the density
p_{\delta}
is symmetric about zero. Recall that
\frac{2\tau^{2}(c_{1}+2c_{2}\tau^{2})}{c_{1}c_{2}(1-\lambda_{2})}>0
. Further, the function
B
in (

\delta

B(0)=0
and
B(\delta)\to+\infty
as
\delta\to+\infty
, and so for any
\eta>0
there exists some
\delta^{*}>0
such that
B(\delta^{*})\geq\frac{2\tau^{2}(c_{1}+2c_{2}\tau^{2})}{c_{1}c_{2}(1-\lambda_{2})}\,\eta
. Thus, the law
P_{\delta^{*}}
on
(Y,X)
satisfies

\frac{V(\gamma_{\mathrm{EQML}})\wedge V(\gamma_{\mathrm{GEE}})}{\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}V(\gamma)}\geq\eta,

alongside the additional conditions (
6

1
(that were shown to hold for all
P_{\delta}
with
\delta>0
and thus hold for

).
