Greenhouse gas balance of solar parks built on peatlands in Germany
Authors: Florian Schwill, Niclas Scott Bentsen, Carl Pump, Monika Hohlbein, Gerald Jurasinski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Drained peatlands are responsible for around 4% of anthropogenic GHG-emissions. To trigger the necessary large-scale rewetting, solar parks built on rewetted peatlands in Germany have been eligible for a feed-in tariff since 2023. Building a solar park on a rewetted peatland requires some technical alterations, particularly of the mounting structure. Building a regular solar park on a drained peatland might thus obstruct its rewetting, allowing for the peatlands’ GHG-emissions to be allocated to the solar park. This study finds that the GHG-emissions of solar parks built on drained peatlands are between 76 and 199 g CO2-eq kWh−1. The GHG-emissions from the drained peatlands contribute between 38 and 76% to these total emissions. The GHG-balances are between −95 and 27 g CO2-eq kWh−1. The positive GHG-balances highlight that ensuring that solar parks cannot be built on drained peatland anymore is crucial. Solar parks on rewetted peatlands require careful planning as water table regulations and implementation timelines might not be sufficiently strict in Germany.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27952-4.

Introduction

Up to one third of all soil carbon is stored in peatlands. These carbon stocks are a result of stable anaerobic conditions which have slowed down the decomposition of organic matter and thereby have allowed carbon to accumulate for millennia1. However, starting mainly in the 1700s and strongly accelerating from the 1950s, over 20% of global peatland areas have been drained, mainly to gain space for agriculture2. In Northern Europe, more than 80% of the peatland area has been drained2. Lowering the water table increases greenhouse gas (GHG)-emissions by introducing more oxygen into the soil3. This enables microorganisms to decompose peat more efficiently and, thus, quicker3. The lower the water table, the more carbon is released as carbon dioxide (CO2) and the less as methane (CH2)4. In addition, nitrous oxide (N2O) formation occurs in drained peatlands5. Its release depends less on the precise water table5. Instead, it is mainly formed in nutrient rich peatlands, in compressed soils and close to ditches, often in combination with fluctuating water tables5,6. Currently, drained peatlands emit around 2 Gigatons CO2-eq worldwide per year1. This equates to roughly 4% of global anthropogenic GHG-emissions1.

GHG-emissions from peatlands can be minimized through rewetting, ideally reconverting them into carbon sinks7. Most rewetting projects are currently funded by public institutions and foundations8. Especially paludiculture, the productive use of biomass from rewetted peatlands without further peat loss, can be a viable economic alternative but comes with high upfront conversion costs. Furthermore, the necessary supply chains and markets are still in the early stages of development9. To achieve the needed large-scale rewetting of peatlands, additional sources of funding are thus necessary1.

At the same time, GHG-emissions from the electricity sector need to be reduced through large-scale deployment of sustainable energy. Among all energy carriers, solar energy is exhibiting the highest year-on-year percentage growth in its installed capacity. This growth has been driven by steep cost reductions, supportive policies, and ongoing improvements in solar photovoltaic technologies. While solar energy globally generated 1.1% of all electricity in 2015, it produced 5.5% in 2023. This is a fivefold increase in its share of electricity production within 8 years. In the same time span, the share of electricity production from wind energy has doubled, accounting for 7.8% in 202310.

With around 50 g CO2-eq per kWh, the GHG-emission intensity of solar electricity is considerably higher than those of other renewable energy systems like wind farms (≈15 g CO2-eq kWh−1) and hydropower (≈ 5 g CO2-eq kWh−1)11–14. These values have been obtained with life cycle assessments (LCAs). Guidelines for LCA of solar parks, essentially a large number of ground-mounted solar panels, have been published by Frischknecht et al.15 The precise GHG-emission intensity of a specific solar park depends mainly on manufacturing location, type of solar panel, and solar irradiation at the installation site13. The largest contributor to the GHG-emissions is solar panel manufacturing (> 70%), followed by the mounting structure (≈20%)13. The remaining elements like inverter and cables are responsible for the final ≈10% of the GHG-emissions13.

With currently around one hectare (ha) per installed Megawatt capacity (MWp), solar parks require vast tracks of land16. As a result, diverse land use conflicts have been reported around the globe17. This includes (a) loss of livelihoods of indigenous communities in India due to the enclosure of common land, (b) loss of agricultural land for farmers in East-Germany as land rent with solar electricity production can be up to twenty times higher, (c) competition with numerous other development objectives such as wind turbines in Japan and d) many examples of conflict between forests and solar parks18–20.

A potential solution that addresses both issues—limited funding for rewetting peatlands, and land use conflicts around solar parks—may be building solar parks on peatland and using part of the financial gains from the sold electricity to rewet the peatland. So far, this idea has received little attention21,22. Partly this may be due to laws that prohibit substantial alterations of peatlands21. While these laws do not necessarily require peat protecting high water tables to be present, they usually prevent solar parks from being built21,23. Where solar parks are legally permitted on peatlands, potential additional costs associated with (a) rewetting the peatland, (b) using specialized machinery for maintenance with a high water table, and (c) modified solar park configurations may render the project economically unviable22,24.

A regular solar park, like it is built on mineral soils, would likely be unsuitable for rewetted peatlands due to negative impacts on the peatland and an increased risk of malfunctioning of the solar park. The mounting structure of a solar park built on rewetted peatland likely differs most. As wet organic soils provide less stability, also decreasing with the thickness of the peat layer, mounting structures might need to be anchored deeper in the soil (e.g., to the mineral layer)22. Alternatively, floating photovoltaics may be technically feasible but would require large, evenly structured sites22. They would also supress growth of helophytes under the panels, thus likely negatively impacting the peatland25. Ideally, higher mounting structures are also installed to allow for sufficient and even light distribution and to ensure that the water table does not reach the solar panels22. Galvanized iron as a material commonly used for mounting structures is environmentally undesirable as the constant exposure to water would cause the zinc to leach into the soil26. Possibly, other coating materials, e.g. epoxy resins would be needed to protect the mounting structure against water corrosion22. Adjusted anchoring mechanisms of the mounting structure that minimize surface sealing and leave no residues in the soil, also still need to be researched26. Lastly, solar panels should have glass-to-glass frames to maximize sunlight reaching the plant cover26. With few solar parks currently installed on rewetted peatlands, it remains unknown which of these changes are required and which are merely desirable22.

This may soon change as multiple research projects into solar parks built on peatland have been initiated, particularly in Germany. The growing interest is likely a result of a revision of the German renewable energy law EEG27. As of 2023, solar park operators can also receive a feed-in tariff for solar parks built on rewetted peatlands (EEG §37). While the EEG specifies the outline of the law, the concrete requirements are defined by the federal network agency (EEG §48).

Solar parks built on peatlands may receive a feed-in tariff (+ 0.0025 EUR kWh−1 in comparison to regular solar parks in 2024) if the peatland has been drained and used for agricultural purposes in the last 24 months (EEG §37, §38, §48). A first financial analysis concludes that this feed-in tariff allows for income generation comparable to agricultural production24. Rewetting of the peatland must have started once the solar park starts operating. Solar parks built on drained peatland without rewetting are excluded from receiving a feed-in tariff (EEG §37, §48). A soil is classified as peatland if it has a layer of at least 10 cm thickness within the upper 40cm with either > 7.5% organic carbon content or > 15% organic matter (EEG §3). The specific requirements are set by the federal network agency28. Accordingly, peatland is considered rewetted if the minimum water table in winter is above −10 cm with respect to the soil surface and above −30 cm in summer. Rewetting must be completed at the latest 5 years after the operation starts. The competent authority must be provided with confirmation that the water levels have been achieved. With a proper cause, an extension of five more years is possible.

A map of peatlands where solar parks eligible for a feed-in tariff can be built in Germany does not exist26. However, Wittnebel et al.29 report that there are 1.93 million ha of peatland area in Germany. More than 90% of the peatland area is drained and more than 70% of it is used for agriculture30. Based on this, the Fraunhofer Institute for Solar Energy Systems estimates that the technical potential of solar parks built on rewetted peatland in Germany is between 440 and 880 GWp. In theory all drained Peatlands alone could thus meet the total installed capacity of solar energy targeted by the German government of 400 GWp by 204027. However, much uncertainty still exists around this potential. Firstly, an on-site validation of the assumed reduced density of installed capacity of 0.3–0.6 MWp ha−1 is still missing. Secondly, part of the area might be protected and/or has not been used for agriculture in the past 24 months. Nordt et al.30 found that 335,000 ha of peatland area is drained and used intensively as cropland. Using this area yields a minimum technical potential of 100–200 GWp (with 0.3–0.6 MWp ha−1) for solar parks built on rewetted peatlands.

While research into solar parks built on peatland has started, only one study, a SWOT-analysis, has been published yet22. For wind farms built on peatlands, Smith et al.31 has shown that peatland GHG-emissions contribute more to the total GHG-emission intensity than building and decommissioning the wind farms themselves. Given the ongoing high GHG-emissions from drained peatlands, adapting the study of Smith et al.31 for solar parks appears like a worthwhile starting point for research into solar parks built on peatlands. Specifically, the following research question will be addressed: What is the GHG-emission intensity and GHG-balance of already built solar parks on drained peatlands in Germany? Based on the results, this study will derive implications for the feasibility of solar parks built on rewetted peatlands and suggest directions for future research.

Results

Identifying and characterizing solar parks built on Peatland

In total, 165 solar parks that are connected to 224 entries in the core energy data market register32 have been built on peatland in Germany until December 2023. The solar parks are primarily located in the peat-rich federal states Bavaria, followed by Schleswig–Holstein and Lower Saxony (Fig. 1). The total installed capacity is 643 MWp with an average of 3.9 MWp and a median of 1.2 MWp per solar park. At least five solar parks have been designed to withstand the restored high water tables required by the EEG27 and in three of these peatlands high water tables are also present.

Fig. 1Overview of locations of solar parks built on peatland in Germany.

Overview of locations of solar parks built on peatland in Germany.

41 of the solar parks are built on previous cropland, 25 on industrial land, 15 on conversion areas, and six on grassland. The remaining 78 peatland areas are located in the vicinity of a high- or a railway and a previous land use type was thus not available. Solar parks are primarily located on fens, which make up 62% (392 ha) of the total peatland area covered by solar parks, followed by peat succession soils at 32.5% (207 ha), and bogs at 5.5% (36 ha). The coverage of individual peatlands by solar parks ranges from 0.01 to 100% with a mean and median of 20% and 8%, respectively.

The maximum impacted area is ≈ 2.74 times larger than the minimum impacted area (Fig. 2), and 9% of the solar parks are located near the centre of a sizeable peatland, with the vast majority built closer to the edge.

GHG-emission intensity

The average GHG-emission intensity of solar parks built on drained peatland in Germany lies between 76 ± 40 (one standard deviation) g CO2-eq kWh−1 (minimum impacted area) and 199 ± 237 g CO2-eq kWh−1 (maximum impacted area) (Fig. 3). The peatland GHG-emission intensities contribute between 38% (minimum) and 76% (maximum), equaling 29 ± 19 g CO2-eq kWh−1 and 152 ± 230 g CO2-eq kWh−1, respectively. The difference arises primarily due to the area differences, as the GHG-emission rate of the minimally impacted area (30 ± 8 tons CO2-eq ha−1 year−1) is only slightly lower than that of the maximally impacted area (32 ± 9 tons CO2-eq ha−1 year−1). The GHG-emission intensity of the solar park ranges from 62% (minimum impacted area) to 24% (maximum impacted area). These percentages equate to a GHG-emission intensity of 47 ± 27 g CO2-eq kWh−1. The GHG-emission intensity of the solar park decreases over time due to (a) an increase in electricity production and (b) a decrease in GHG-emission intensity included in the input data. This reduction is more pronounced in the minimum impacted area due to the greater relative share of the solar park’s emissions in the total GHG-emission intensity (Fig. 3a).

On average, hourly average electricity displacement avoids 172 ± 87 g CO2-eq kWh−1. This value decreases over time as the share of renewable energy in the electricity grid increases of a land use change from cropland to solar parks avoids on average 3.5 ± 2 g CO2-eq kWh−1. The relative contributions to the avoided emissions by electricity displacement and by land use change are thus 98% and 2%, respectively. In total, this yields a GHG-balance of −95 ± 66 g CO2-eq kWh−1 (minimum impacted area) and 27 ± 230 g CO2-eq kWh−1 (maximum impacted area) (Fig. 4).

Avoided GHG-emissions methodology

The avoided GHG-emissions are on average 26 ± 9 g CO2-eq kWh−1 lower with a displacement of the average hourly electricity mix than with a displacement of the average yearly electricity mix. Assuming the solar park electricity production displaces marginal electricity production leads to avoided GHG-emissions that are on average 370 ± 26 g CO2-eq kWh−1 higher than with a displacement of the average hourly electricity mix (Fig. 5).

Rewetting of all peatlands

If the peatlands were rewetted and consequently their GHG-emissions were avoided, the average GHG-balance of a solar park on peatland would be between −394 ± 746 g CO2-eq kWh−1 (minimum impacted area) and − 707 ± 1463 g CO2-eq kWh−1 (maximum impacted area). From 2027 and onwards, the contribution to the avoided GHG-emissions by the minimally impacted area is expected to be larger than the contribution from electricity displacement for solar parks built on rewetted peatlands (Fig. 6).

Discussion

While Delafield et al.21 assumed that solar parks have not been built on peatlands in Scotland, this assumption can be refuted for Germany where 165 solar parks have already been built on peatland until December 2023. Before 2023, receiving a feed-in tariff for building solar parks on drained peatlands was not prohibited, as long as the designated peatland fell under one of the viable land use categories of the 2021 EEG §37. Most solar parks can only be built if a municipal council changes the development plan accordingly. Through this instrument, the municipal council can still stop a solar park or detail conditions under which the solar park is allowed to be built. Only two instances are known in which the municipal council detailed conditions to protect the peatland22. In fact, building solar parks on drained peatlands might have been favoured due to generally lower soils scores, a statutory indicator of the yield expectancy of a soil, of peatlands in comparison to other soil types. For a solar park built on agricultural land, the soil score generally must be below 30 to be eligible for feed-in tariffs (EEG §3, §38)27.

In the past, excluding solar parks from receiving a feed-in tariff would have sufficed to protect peatlands, as those solar parks were not profitable33. Nowadays, building solar parks that are not reliant on receiving a feed-in tariff is possible22. In this study, at least four solar parks are not receiving a feed-in tariff. Closing this legal gap is crucial for peatland protection22. Furthermore, it is still uncertain whether the revision of EEG §37and §4827 suffice to prevent solar parks from being built on drained peatland. For instance, while solar park developers have to legally declare that the desired area is not a nature protection area, they do not have to declare that the area is not a peatland (EEG §37). Thus, this task falls on to the local approval authority. A solar park operator (pers. communication) stated that the development plan did not mention that the proposed land was a peatland. Thus, it is conceivable that local approval authorities may continue to approve solar parks built on drained peatland.

Solar parks have been built on all three major peatland types in Germany—fens, bogs and peat succession soils. Slightly more solar parks have been built on fens and peat succession soils than expected given their proportion of total peatland area, while slightly fewer solar parks have been built on bogs29. One reason for the slightly higher percentage of peat succession soils covered by solar parks may be that most solar parks have been built at the edge of a peatland. Edges are more easily accessible and generally have a lower peat content and depth. Fewer stability issues with a lower peat content have therefore likely favoured building solar parks on peat succession soils34. An explanation for less solar parks on bogs could be that comparatively more of them are protected by nature protection areas. This would also explain why comparatively fewer solar parks have been built in Lower Saxony, the federal state with the most bogs29. However, cause and effect are unknown here. A central reason why most solar parks are built in Bavaria is that the state receives most solar irradiation in Germany.

This is the first study on the GHG-emission intensity and balance of solar parks built on drained peatland. Thus, only a comparison of the sub-results against literature is possible. Lauf et al.14 reported the average GHG-emission intensity of all solar energy systems installed in Germany as 57 g CO2-eq kWh−1. This is within one standard deviation of the result reported here (47 g CO2-eq kWh−1). Both results build on data from Hengstler et al.13, so a similarity is expected. The lower value can be explained by (a) this study focusing exclusively on solar parks, and (b) electricity production values in this study being slightly higher than common.

After excluding samples where the generic GHG-emission rate for grassland from Tiemeyer et al.4 was applied, the average annual GHG-emissions rate for both minimum and maximum impacted areas remain similar to the applied generic GHG-emission rate for grassland. A slightly higher standard deviation has been found for the maximum impacted area. This can be explained by diverse land uses—such as croplands, grasslands or forests—around the solar park with diverging GHG-emission rates that are included in the GHG-emission rate. That the average GHG emission intensity of the maximum impacted area is only slightly higher than of the minimum impacted area suggests that while some peatland area may be used for cropland, much of the peatland area adjacent to solar parks is likely used as grassland. The fact that 52% of drained peatland area in Germany is used as grassland and 19% as cropland supports this interpretation30.

We find that numerous solar parks built on drained peatland in Germany emit more GHGs than they avoid. This is a novel finding. However, the result is sensitive to the assumed method for electricity displacement. Under all assumptions (yearly, hourly or marginal displacement), the annually avoided GHG-emissions by electricity displacement are decreasing over time. This is a consequence of the overall decrease in GHG-emission intensity of the electricity mix in Germany35. For newly built solar park, a positive GHG-balance is therefore increasingly likely.

Similar to wind farms built on peatland, the peatland related GHG-emissions of solar parks contribute substantially to the overall GHG-emission intensity. However, in contrast to Nayak31, the peatlands in this study are not always the largest contributor to the total GHG-emission intensity. Part of the difference is explained by the fact that life cycle based GHG-emissions of solar parks can be up to three times higher than those of wind farms13. Methodological differences likely are also influential. For example, Nayak31 attributes peatland GHG-emissions after the decommissioning of a wind farm to the wind farm itself, whereas this study does not include GHG-emissions from peatlands after decommissioning. This decision was made because solar parks are often built without foundations and are simply rammed into the ground36. This may minimize the impact on the soil compared to wind farms, although more research is still required.

The coordinates of solar parks in the core energy market data register Core energy market data register are partly inaccurate37. While false positives were excluded by using orthophotos, false negatives could only be included if they have been built within hundred meters from a peatland. Compared to a peatland map based on the definition used in the EEG §3, Wittnebel et al.29 may underestimate peat succession soils with a thickness between 10 and 20 cm. Thus, more solar parks might have been built on peatland. While Wittnebel et al.29 published their peatland map in 2023, the data was collected earlier, 1930–2022 and predominantly in the last 30 years. Since then, more peat has degraded, possibly leading to changes in peatland area and type. While creating the peatland map, the use of a depreciation factor for peatland edges was considered but ultimately rejected because the dates of the underlying peatland measurements were often unknown (Tiemeyer, pers. communication). Updated data may lead to the exclusion of some solar parks included here.

The approach used here to delimit the hydrologic units sometimes produced unreasonably large maximum impacted areas. These explain most of the seven outliers in the GHG-emission intensity of solar parks (Fig. 4b). One explanation may be that those peatlands are drained with drainpipes instead of ditches, rendering the methodological approach used in this study unable to accurately delimit the hydrologic unit.

The GHG-emission intensities of solar parks reported here rely on Hengstler et al.13. This study therefore inherits their limitations. For example, this includes the assumption that the average electricity mix of a country is representative for electricity used to manufacture solar panels. The assessment would have benefited from using actual electricity production data. Simulations of electricity production from solar parks tend to overestimate the produced electricity, with pvlib python showing higher inaccuracies (+ 5%) compared to other simulation software like PVSyst (+ 3%)38,39. However, with pvlib python the input data can be changed automatically, thereby drastically decreasing the time needed to simulate the electricity production. The increased uncertainty introduced by using pvlib python here is assessed not to significantly influence the results and conclusions reported.

The absence of specific data on the GHG-emission intensity from peatlands covered by solar parks necessitates using a generic GHG-emission factor for grassland. The absence of specific data also affects the other two data sources—regional and national, site-specific GHG-emission data—as the influence of the solar park on the GHG-emissions from peatlands can only be indirectly included. Some of the regional, site-specific GHG-emission data relies on federal state-specific but outdated inputs. The central limitation of the national, site-specific GHG-emission data is an outdated water table database. All peatland GHG-emissions are assumed to be constant throughout the lifetime of the solar park, thereby disregarding, for example, diminishing GHG-emissions from possibly fully degraded peat succession soils.

The results have shown that if solar parks should be built on peatlands, those peatlands need to be rewetted to ensure climate benefits of the solar park. Thus, it is crucial to make sure that solar parks, with and without a feed-in tariff, cannot be built on drained peatlands22. For solar parks built on rewetted peatland, the avoided GHG-emissions from peatland rewetting are possibly higher than those from electricity displacement (Fig. 6). Thus, the main argument from a GHG-perspective for solar parks built on rewetted peatlands is not building the solar park itself but the prospective funding incentive for peatland rewetting. However, the GHG-balances of solar parks built on rewetted peatland can differ greatly: The EEG requires water tables of at least −30 cm in summer and −10 cm in winter. With these water tables, considerable GHG-emissions remain possible4,22. Furthermore, rewetting must only be completed 5 years after the solar park starts operating, with a possible extension of 5 years27. After the lifetime of the solar park, peatlands can be drained again27. This arrangement mainly aims to prevent solar park operators from being liable for peatland drainage post-decommissioning but can have the unintended consequence that some peatlands are actually redrained. Thus, attention to detail is key when planning a solar park built on rewetted peatland.

Without the solar parks, many of these peatlands may not be rewetted right away, as securing funding would be more difficult1,40. A first financial analysis concludes that mainly solar parks built on peatland, in contrast to e.g., paludiculture, allow for land rents comparable with common agricultural production24. However, besides funding availability, power relations also influence securing funding for peatland rewetting1. This is reflected by the decision of the German government to grant an additional compensation for solar parks built on rewetted peatlands (0.0025 EUR kWh−1 in 2024).

Policy implications may be (a) generally prohibiting building solar parks on drained peatlands, (b) a stricter rewetting criterion than detailed by the federal network agency, and (c) prohibiting re-draining of peatlands after the lifetime of the solar park. Seidel et al.22 also propose restricting the construction of solar parks to already degraded peatlands. This could be peat succession soils or heavily degraded soils after a deep drainage period. As peat succession soils can emit similar amounts of GHGs as fens and bogs, this proposal might not substantially alter the GHG-balances29. Instead, it ensures that unforeseen adverse environmental impacts of solar parks do not have a lasting effect on fens and bogs, showcasing that other environmental impacts of building a solar park on rewetted peatland also require consideration and research.

Besides the mentioned environmental issues, land conflicts may also arise, especially if peatland is used for agriculture and the farmer is not the landowner26. This leads to the more fundamental question of how many solar parks are needed for a fully renewable electricity supply. All scenarios used in this study to predict the GHG-emission intensity of the future electricity mix assumed economic growth and deduced an increased need of electricity production. Without economic growth fewer solar parks would be needed, likely leading to less environmental harm and less land use conflicts. Given that the global economic system is currently centred around economic growth, such a fundamental transformation may entail its own risks41. However, this study has provided further evidence that not considering such alternatives, e.g., by only devising future electricity scenarios that already include economic growth, poses tremendous risks as well.

Methodology

The approach to answering the research question had two steps: (1) Identifying and characterizing all solar parks built on peatland in Germany, and (2) based on this sample, calculating the GHG-emission intensity and balance of the solar parks. The second step again consisted of two sections: (2.1) calculating the GHG-emission intensity and balance of the solar parks and (2.2) calculating the GHG-emission intensity and balance of the peatlands.

Identifying and characterizing solar parks built on peatland

To identify solar parks built on peatland, the coordinates of all solar parks in Germany, obtained through the core energy market data register32, were overlapped with a buffered (100 m) peatland map of Germany29 in QGIS. In line with the EEG §327, peat succession soils in Wittnebel et al.29 were hereby also regarded as peatland. As Kotthoff et al.37 have reported data imprecision in the core energy market data register, orthophotos from Google Maps and, whenever outdated, Sentinel-2 satellite pictures were used to confirm the existence of solar parks and to draw polygons of the solar parks42. This took place in March and April 2024. The quality of the polygon areas was checked by calculating the density of installed capacity.

Table 1 provides an overview of the characteristics that were considered. Four characteristics were obtained through lookup tables of Wittnebel et al.29 and the core energy market data register32. The remaining characteristics required additional work.

To identify solar parks that have been built to withstand the restored high water table detailed by the federal network agency, the following checks were done:

(1) Scanning (grey) literature, (2) overlapping rewetting maps from federal states with the solar park map, (3) contacting peatland experts within the specific federal states, (4) using information on the water table included in a GHG-emission map of peatlands that was provided by the Thünen Institute43 and, (5) contacting the solar park operators to verify if the obtained solar parks with steps 1 to 4 have actually been built to withstand the restored high water tables.

For the peatland area impacted by a solar park a minimum and a maximum impacted area was calculated. The minimum impacted area was obtained by overlapping the solar park polygons with the peatland map29. However, if the solar park obstructs rewetting of an entire hydrologic unit, the area impacted by the solar park may be larger. To obtain this maximum impacted area, it was assumed that hydrologic units are separated by ditches, streets and settlements. For streets and settlements, this is due to the reduced hydraulic conductivity and for ditches it is because they are designed to drain water, disrupting the connectivity of water44. This assumption allowed for orthophotos from Google Maps to be used to delimit a maximum impacted area.

GHG-emission intensity of peatlands

In this study, GHG-emissions of peatlands were attributed to the regular solar parks built on them, assuming that these solar parks cannot function properly with the restored high water tables required by the federal network agency22,46. Thereby, regular solar parks obstruct the rewetting of peatlands, rendering them co-responsible for the GHG-emissions.

For the GHG-emission rate of peatlands, regional, site-specific data published by the federal states was used wherever available (Table 2). Avoided GHG-emissions from peatlands

If the peatland now covered by a solar park was previously used as cropland, some peatland GHG-emissions are also avoided by building a solar park on a drained peatland. This is because less peat is exposed to oxygen when there is no plowing5. Also, no fertilizer is needed when agricultural production stops5. This study crudely assumed that half of the difference between the generic GHG-emission rate of cropland and grassland, thus peatlands covered by a solar park, given in Tiemeyer et al.4 is explained by these management changes, whereas the other half is caused by different average water tables. The resulting assumption for a drained peatland, that a switch from cropland to a grassland covered by a solar park avoids 4.35 t CO2-eq ha−1 year−1, was verified against Hatano5.

The avoided GHG-emissions from peatlands previously used as cropland were calculated with Eq. 9. For solar parks built in the vicinity of a highway or railway track, the core energy data market register does not provide data on the previous land use. To those solar parks, a previous land use type, either cropland or non-cropland, was probabilistically assigned based on the prevalence of cropland among the remaining solar parks in this study. Sensitivity analysis

This study assumes the electricity produced by the solar park replaces the average hourly electricity mix. As this approach is still rather new, a sensitivity analysis assuming the displacement of (a) the average yearly electricity mix or (b) the marginal electricity source was conducted to contextualize the result (SI5 and Supplementary Table 7). Secondly, the rewetting of all peatlands was chosen as a scenario to provide insights into solar parks built on rewetted peatland (Table 3). To represent a best-case scenario, peatland GHG emissions were crudely assumed to be zero for this sensitivity-analysis.
