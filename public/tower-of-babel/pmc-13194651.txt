LGBTQ+ Health Care Training for OB/GYN Residents Using a Simulation-Based Learning Experience
Authors: Jonathan Seibert, Marisa Imbroane, Megan Stuhlman, Leslie Giles Nester, Melissa Woolworth, Erin Higgins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, and persons with other gender identities (LGBTQ+) report difficulties in accessing and receiving health care, extending into OB/GYN care, with patients frequently being seen in this setting for nuanced, sensitive concerns. Up to 90% of OB/GYN residents desire more education on caring for the LGBTQ+ community. We explored the impact of a simulation-based learning experience (SBLE) designed around effective communication and the health care needs of LGBTQ+ patients on OB/GYN residents’ confidence and knowledge when caring for this community.MethodsBetween 2024 and 2025, 12 third- and fourth-year OB/GYN residents participated in a 3-hour SBLE covering 4 LGBTQ+ topics delivered by trained standardized patients with lived experiences within the LGBTQ+ community. Participants’ confidence and knowledge were assessed pre-SBLE, immediately post-SBLE, and 4–6 months post-SBLE, utilizing a 7-item Likert scale self-report of confidence adapted from a previously published study and an internally validated 8-item multiple-choice knowledge assessment. Paired sample t tests and 1-way repeated measures ANOVAs were used to analyze the data.ResultsThere was a mean 20% improvement in the immediate confidence assessment (P < .001) and a mean 13% improvement in the immediate knowledge assessment (P = .01). Only confidence gains were maintained over the 4–6 month follow-up.DiscussionParticipation in SBLEs on LGBTQ+-related health care topics can improve OB/GYN residents’ confidence and knowledge in caring for this community. Continued education may be indicated to address knowledge decay over time.

Educational Objectives

By the end of this activity, learners will be able to: 1.Demonstrate respectful practices in obtaining an inclusive clinical history from LGBTQ+ standardized patients using patient-centered language and shared decision-making, as measured by standardized patient ratings utilizing the Communication Assessment Tool.2.Self-assess confidence in providing equitable and inclusive care for LGBTQ+ patients, as measured by change in mean confidence scores immediately postsession and 4–6 months after the training.3.Identify current best practices to counsel patients on common LGBTQ+ gynecologic and reproductive health care, as measured by an 8-item multiple-choice knowledge assessment immediately postsession.

Introduction

Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, and persons with other gender identities (LGBTQ+) have reported difficulties in accessing and receiving health care, likely contributing to the well-established health disparities experienced by these communities.1 When LGBTQ+ individuals can access health care, some have reported challenges in finding welcoming, responsible, and inclusive health care providers across specialty areas.2,3 With a growing percentage of patients identifying as LGBTQ+, the need for qualified providers also continues to grow.4 Obstetricians and gynecologists are uniquely situated to address a wide range of health care needs of LGBTQ+ patients, including health maintenance, sexual health, and family planning. One recent study indicated that 90% OB/GYN residents expressed a desire for “more” or “a lot more” education related to providing health care for the LGBTQ+ community.5 In a study of OB/GYN residency program directors, approximately half reported that their programs did not offer transgender health education. Of the programs not offering this education, lack of faculty expertise and curriculum materials were the most commonly cited barriers.6

Simulation is a widely accepted and efficacious tool in OB/GYN resident education, as it provides an opportunity for residents to practice in a safe teaching environment where they are able to learn from their mistakes.7–9 Although simulation in OB/GYN training has traditionally been used for clinical skills such as pelvic ultrasound, operative vaginal deliveries, or postpartum hemorrhage management, its utility goes beyond these situations.7 Literature on the use of simulation-based learning experience (SBLE) with standardized patients (SPs) is more limited for communication-related outcomes, but has garnered positive feedback from residents across various specialties.10–12

As medical learners advance in training, their education progresses through the 6 levels of Bloom's Taxonomy Pyramid, from “Remembering” to “Creating.”13 In the simulation setting, an undergraduate medical learner will largely be working on applying their knowledge (the pyramid's third level). However, a graduate medical education learner, like an OB/GYN resident, will focus on higher-level objectives, including the creation of treatment plans and complex medical counseling. Multiple published simulation curricula for LGBTQ+ health care focus on taking a comprehensive sexual history and do not require learners to provide medical expertise and counseling for patient concerns.14–18 Thus, these curricula are more applicable to medical student learning to apply their knowledge. There are several curricula focused on caring for transgender patients, ranging from learning how to ask patients for their pronouns (“Understanding”) to developing a plan for prescribing hormones and monitoring a patient's progress (“Creating”).19–21 Although these curricula extend to graduate medical education, the standardized patient experiences (SPEs) are not entirely within the scope of OB/GYN practice. Two published curricula that are the most applicable to an OB/GYN resident, and most reflective of our SBLE, are by King et al.22 and Croft et al.23 King et al.22 designed an SPE for OB/GYN residents on providing menstrual suppression for a nonbinary, pansexual patient. Positively, this study found increased resident comfort working with and knowledge of the health care needs of LGBTQ+ patients, but it only contained 1 SPE. Croft et al.23 designed an SPE focused on the challenges of end-of-life care in same-sex partnerships, a topic of value to OB/GYN residents. However, the curriculum is created for undergraduate medical learners to practice complex interviewing skills, but it does not provide the depth of education for the graduate medical learner who would need to counsel this patient. Therefore, a large gap in curricula for OB/GYN residents on providing care for LGBTQ+ patients remains.

Our novel SBLE, consisting of 4 SPEs, was designed to provide practice and training in engaging in patient-centered care and counseling for LGBTQ+ patients, specifically for OB/GYN residents. The goal of the SBLE is to equip OB/GYN residents with the communication skills, confidence, and knowledge needed to be an inclusive physician for LGBTQ+ patients. Additionally, this curriculum can serve as a resource for physician-educators who recognize the need to include this instruction in their training program but feel that they lack the content expertise to provide it.

Methods

Activity Design and Development

The SBLE curriculum was designed by Jonathan Seibert for OB/GYN residents in their third or fourth years and was completed at a single program in Charlotte, North Carolina. The 4 SPEs were adapted from real patient encounters in which OB/GYN resident physicians had previously reported feeling unprepared. The SPEs were then developed to be more generalizable and to represent a diverse range of the LGBTQ+ community. Next, the SPEs were piloted at 3 separate OB/GYN residency programs, with more than 30 learners participating at this stage. Through this iterative process, the SPEs were refined based on feedback from learners, community member SPs, subject-matter expert facilitators, and simulation staff.

SPs were recruited from the community, with a focus on those who had lived experience with the sexual orientation/gender identity of the patients they portrayed, either as a self-identified member of the LGBTQ+ community or a close ally of the community. The total number of SPs recruited was based on the number of learners. At least 1 SP per case was needed, with a 1:2 SP to learner ratio, which was found to be the fewest SPs required if each learner directly participated in each SPE. Prior to the SBLE, SPs underwent a 2-hour training session. The training focused on reviewing the objectives of the SBLE, each SP's specific case, and the tool and directions for completing communication assessment of the learners, and emphasized promoting participant and SP psychological safety. During the training, SPs rehearsed their SPE and received feedback on completing the resident assessment tool for open and inclusive communication (Appendix A).

Implementation

The SBLE was completed in an accredited simulation center, using 3 outpatient and 1 inpatient simulation rooms. The total duration of the SBLE was 3 hours (a sample schedule is found in Appendix A). This consisted of a 15-minute prebrief with a baseline self-assessment, four 15-minute SPEs, a 60-minute debrief, and completion of the immediate post-SBLE self-assessment. SPs were given 5 minutes to complete a structured assessment of the resident, focusing on communication after each SPE, as learners rotated to the next room. The SP could additionally provide written, open-ended feedback regarding the encounter on this assessment. The prebrief and debrief sessions took place in a small group discussion classroom.

The topics for the SPEs were (1) vaginitis and sexually transmitted infection evaluation for lesbian and bisexual women (Appendix B), (2) diagnosis and management of vaginal discharge in transgender women (Appendix C), (3) fertility considerations for lesbian and bisexual women and transgender patients (Appendix D), and (4) end-of-life counseling for same-sex partners without legal paperwork denoting their relationship (Appendix E). Each resident completed each SPE, with only 1 resident assigned to each room at a time. A brief overview of the presenting chief complaint was described on a door note prior to entry into the room (Appendix F). Learner documentation of the encounter was not part of the SBLE.

Following the completion of all SPEs, 2 OB/GYN generalist faculty facilitators guided the structured debriefing session with the participants (Appendix G). At the conclusion of the SBLE, the participants were given resources, also included in Appendix G, to expand their knowledge on the covered subjects and to reference in the future. These resources included 3 American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists bulletins and the guidelines for gender-affirming care documented by the University of California, San Francisco.24–27

The SBLE was run twice over a 2-year period, with each resident learner participating only once. This was done to allow for inclusion of learners who were not available during the first session. The SPs and faculty facilitators were the same for both sessions. The Wake Forest University Institutional Review Board approved this study (IRB No. 00104552).

Assessment and Evaluation

Three assessments of learners’ skills in providing inclusive and affirming care for LGBTQ+ patients were utilized during this SBLE: (1) a learner confidence assessment, (2) a learner knowledge assessment, and (3) an SP assessment of learners’ clinical interviewing/communication skills. The confidence and knowledge assessments were completed at the start of the SBLE session (pre-SBLE), immediately post-SBLE, and 4–6 months post-SBLE to evaluate the impact of the SBLE in these areas over time.

The confidence assessment was a 7-item, 5-point Likert scale adapted from Minturn et al.28 (Appendix H). The knowledge assessment consisted of 8 multiple-choice questions related to the content covered in the SPEs and debrief session (Appendix I). The questions were developed by Jonathan Seibert and internally validated by 3 national and local subject-matter experts during piloting of the SBLE.

The Clinical Assessment Tool (CAT), developed by Makoul et al.29 in 2007, was used by SPs to rate residents’ clinical interviewing/communication skills. The CAT was created as a brief communication checklist of 14 clinical interviewing and communication skills (using a 5-point Likert scale) and has been validated as a reliable instrument for patients, SPs, and physicians across specialties to assess physician clinical interviewing skills. The participants received their individual CAT results at the conclusion of the SBLE and were encouraged to review their results for each case and reflect on their patient communication feedback.

The assessments were anonymized, with the participants generating their own assessment identification number, which linked the multiple assessments (pre-SBLE/immediately post-SBLE/4-6 months post-SBLE) for each participant. Data on the participants’ previous educational experiences related to LGBTQ+ health care were collected at the beginning of the pre-SBLE assessment.

The learners also had the opportunity to complete an anonymous, general simulation survey at the conclusion of the session, providing feedback on the SBLE. This survey used a 5-point Likert scale from strongly agree to strongly disagree and also included open-ended feedback.

Results

In total, 12 OB/GYN residents participated in the SBLE. Pre-SBLE survey results indicated that previous resident training on providing care to members of the LGBTQ+ community came from lectures (75%), direct patient care (42%), prior readings (17%), and SPEs (8%). The extent of previous training on providing care to members of the LGBTQ+ community was found to be no previous training (17%), 1–2 hours (58%), 3–5 hours (17%), 6–10 hours (0%), and >10 hours (8%).

Comparison of Pre-SBLE and Immediate Post-SBLE Assessments

All 12 learners completed the pre-SBLE and immediate post-SBLE assessments. A paired sample t test was conducted to compare these assessments (Table 1). On the overall confidence assessment, the mean increased by 7.2 out of 35 possible points, from 17.3 (pre-SBLE) to 24.5 (immediately post-SBLE) (95% CI, 5.5–9.0; P < .001). Confidence was also assessed separately for both lesbian, gay, and bisexual and transgender patient populations. For the lesbian, gay, and bisexual population, there was a mean improvement of 4.1 out of 20 possible points from 10.1 to 14.2 (95% CI, 3.0–5.2; P < .001), and for the transgender population, there was a mean improvement of 4.2 out of 20 possible points from 9.5 to 13.7 (95% CI, 3.1–5.2; P < .001). On the knowledge assessment, the mean score increased 1 point from 6.6 pre-SBLE to 7.6 out of 8 possible points immediately post-SBLE (95% CI, 0.3–1.7; P = .01).

Comparison of Pre-SBLE, Immediately Post-SBLE, and 4–6 Months Post-SBLE Assessments

Eight of the initial 12 learners completed the 4–6 months post-SBLE assessment and were included in this analysis. Therefore, a 1-way repeated measures ANOVA was used to compare pre-SBLE, immediate post-SBLE, and 4–6 months post-SBLE assessments for this subset (Table 2). Results of the 1-way repeated measures ANOVAs indicated that the SBLE resulted in statistically significant differences in resident confidence across assessed time points (F(2, 14) = 28.40; P < .001). Results of post hoc testing indicated that both immediate post-SBLE and 4–6 months post-SBLE levels of confidence were significantly higher than pre-SBLE levels (+6.13 [95% CI, 4.22–8.05] and +5.00 [95% CI, 1.76–8.24], respectively). Although the 4–6 month confidence levels showed a slight decrease from immediate post-SBLE levels (–1.13 [95% CI, –3.93 to 1.68]), this decline was not statistically significant.

There was a statistically significant difference in the levels of resident knowledge when observed across the 3 time points (F(2, 14) = 5.69, P = .016; see Table 2). Results of post hoc testing indicated that only immediate post-SBLE levels of knowledge were significantly higher than pre-SBLE levels (+1.13 [95% CI, 0.29–2.22]). At the 4–6 months post-SBLE assessment, knowledge decreased from immediate post-SBLE levels (–0.63 [95% CI, –3.88 to 1.64]) and was no longer statistically significantly different from the pre-SBLE levels (–0.50 [95% CI, –1.52 to 0.52]).

CAT Results

SPs completed ratings of participant communication/clinical interviewing skills using the CAT. Table 3 presents the means, standard deviations, and observed ranges for each evaluated item. Across all CAT items, mean scores ranged from 3.56 (“The resident checked to be sure I understood everything”) to 4.04 (“The resident treated me with respect”), indicating generally positive ratings. Standard deviations varied between 0.75 and 1.07, suggesting moderate variability among responses. The overall average of all CAT ratings was 3.78 (SD = 0.75), with scores ranging from 2.29 to 5.00. There was also variation between the SPEs (see Table 3). The mean CAT score for SPE 2, covering vaginal discharge in a transgender woman, was the lowest at 3.2, whereas SPE 4, covering end-of-life counseling, was the highest at 4.4.

Learner Perspectives

Eleven learners completed the anonymous survey at the conclusion of the SBLE. All respondents reported strongly agreeing (73%) or agreeing (27%) that “simulation is an effective educational method to meet the objectives of this session” and that the “simulation experience was helpful in equipping me for clinical practice.” The learners listed “being more mindful in my history taking,” “how to discuss sex toys,” “caring for lesbian patients with BV [bacterial vaginosis],” and “working up vaginal discharge for transgender patients” as key takeaways that they plan to implement in clinical practice. Several learners recommended assigned prereadings as an area for improving the SBLE in the future.

Discussion

There is a documented need for increased education for OB/GYN residents on how to provide equitable care for LGBTQ+ patients, both from noted poor patient experiences in the health care setting and from provider demand to receive more training.1–3,5 Therefore, we developed an SBLE aimed to increase OB/GYN residents’ confidence and knowledge in providing care for LGBTQ+ patients and provide direct feedback from LGTBQ+ patients regarding their clinical communication. Our study demonstrated an increase in resident confidence in providing care to this patient population that was maintained through 4–6 months postintervention. Additionally, knowledge increased immediately after the intervention but returned to pre-SBLE levels after 4–6 months.

This SBLE could be adapted by other OB/GYN residency programs, including those with limited simulation resources. We believe that working with SPs with lived experience from the communities they represent adds authenticity to the experience and enhances SP feedback. We recognize that our SBLE structure, in which each resident participates in all 4 SPEs, is resource-intensive and could limit generalizability. However, our SPEs could easily be adapted to alternative SBLE methodologies, such as learner/observer, to overcome this. This curriculum, and the associated assessment tools, could also be tailored to other specialties, as providing health care to LGBTQ+ individuals is not limited to the field of OB/GYN.

Throughout the development and implementation of this curriculum, our faculty educators also gained valuable knowledge. An early iteration of this training utilized role-play among residents instead of SPs. A review of the literature and alignment with the Healthcare Simulations Standards for Best Practice prompted our shift to involve SPs with lived experience in the LGBTQ+ community.30 Additionally, upon reflection on feedback from participants, faculty educators will provide pre-SBLE readings as listed in the Resource Guide in Appendix G to allow learners to review relevant content ahead of the session.

Confidence in the stability of our findings is tempered by our small initial sample size paired with participant attrition (only 8 of the initial 12 participants completed the 4–6 months follow-up assessment). However, even with small numbers, we were able to demonstrate improvement in sustained confidence and immediate knowledge. Future efforts should aim to include larger samples and additional efforts to improve long-term follow-up. In addition, the high pretest scores on our 8-item multiple-choice knowledge assessment may have led to a ceiling effect and limited our ability to identify changes in knowledge gained during the SBLE and sustained at the 4–6 months follow-up. A refined knowledge assessment tool that better elucidates knowledge gaps could be developed for future implementations of this SBLE. Although confidence remains elevated at the 4–6 months time point, whereas knowledge returned to baseline, the questions asked in the confidence assessment do not reflect residents’ confidence in their “textbook” knowledge or ability to provide correct care, but instead more closely reflect their abilities to lead inclusive encounters with LGBTQ+ patients and seek out resources for these patients. To address the knowledge decay, we plan to include additional didactics on LGBTQ+ topics for intentional curricular repetition while maintaining this SBLE in the curriculum. Lastly, we attempted to include 4 SPEs that represented diverse life experiences of LGBTQ+ patients, but we recognize that they are not and cannot be all encompassing. Future efforts could include additional or rotating SPEs that would expand the breadth of unique patient experiences and health care topics covered during the SBLE.

Ultimately, we developed an SBLE curriculum for OB/GYN residents on providing care for LGBTQ+ patients. Completing 4 SPEs was effective in improving residents’ confidence in caring for this patient population, even 4–6 months after the intervention ended. Given our results, this SBLE curriculum could be a valuable resource for other OB/GYN residencies across the country and could also be tailored to other specialties.
