Genome-Wide Association study for root system architecture traits in field soybean [Glycine max (L.) Merr.]
Authors: Pallavi Rathore, Kuber Shivashakarappa, Niraj Ghimire, Korsi Dumenyo, Zeinab Yadegari, Ali Taheri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRoots play a crucial role in plant development, serving to absorb water and nutrients from the soil while also providing structural stability. However, the impacts of global warming can impede root growth by altering soil conditions that hinder overall plant growth. To address this challenge, there is a need to screen and identify plant genotypes with superior Root System Architecture traits (RSA), that can be used for future breeding efforts in enhancing their resilience to these environmental changes. In this project, 500 mid to late-maturity soybean accessions were grown on blue blotting papers hydroponically with six replicates and assessed seven RSA traits. Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) were carried out with root phenotypic data and SNP data from the SoySNP50K iSelect SNP BeadChip, using both the TASSEL 5.0 and FarmCPU techniques. A total of 26 significant SNP-trait correlations were discovered, with 11 SNPs on chromosome 13. After SNP selection, we identified 14 candidate genes within 100-kb regions flanking the SNPs, which are related to root architecture. Notably, Glyma.17G258700, which exhibited substantial differential expression in root tips and its Arabidopsis homolog, AT4G24190 (GRP94) is involved in the regulation of meristem size and organization. Other candidate genes includes Glyma.03G023000 and Glyma.13G273500 that are also play a key role in lateral root initiation and root meristem growth, respectively. These findings significantly contribute to the discovery of key genes associated with root system architecture, facilitating the breeding of resilient cultivars adaptable to changing climates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76515-6.Keywords: Root phenotyping, GWAS, SoybeanSubject terms: Plant sciences, Plant breeding, Plant genetics, Plant stress responses

Introduction

Soybean (Glycine max L. Merril) is a leguminous crop1 and a vital source of protein in human diet and animal feed. It is one of the most consumed and cultivated oilseed crops worldwide2. It is the only oilseed crop that is also a legume which means it can fix atmospheric nitrogen to usable nitrogen by forming nitrogen-fixing root nodules through a symbiotic relationship with Rhizobium in the soil3.

Climate change and global warming have a major impact on agriculture and plant productivity. Global warming can influence flowering, transpiration, photosynthesis, respiration, seed production, root growth, pollination, and plant reproduction4. It also alters root growth and architecture, impacting plant response to temperature and soil conditions. Therefore, there is a need to develop crop varieties with better yield and to establish food security in the upcoming years. One way to achieve this goal is to study plant roots and to breed plants with better RSA traits. Phenotyping root traits such as root length, diameter and branching habits is crucial for breeding resilient crops that can access water and nutrients in deeper soils and perform better under changing climate5. Understanding how elevated temperatures affect root systems aids in climate-adaptive agriculture, ensuring sustainable food production. Root phenotyping plays a key role in identifying plants better suited to changing environmental conditions6. Currently, three major approaches are being used for phenotyping root traits including manual measurements, 2-D imaging, and 3D imaging. Two-dimensional (2D) imaging can be carried out using a digital camera to study various root traits like root length and angle7. Whereas 3D imaging can be used to also obtain volumetric and spatial information8. This system provides 360-degree views of the plant roots with a high level of detail. 3D imaging of the roots can be done using X-ray computed tomography (CT) scanners9.

There is limited research on genetics associated with the Root Structure Architecture (RSA) in Soybean, and earlier research are mostly limited to early maturity soybean accessions. For example, Seck et al.10 used rhizoboxes and two-dimensional imaging to characterize twelve RSA-related variables in a panel of 137 early maturing soybean lines (Canadian soybean core collection). Using a mix of genotyping-by-sequencing and whole-genome sequencing, this panel was genotyped with 2.18 million genome-wide single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Through a genome-wide association (GWAS) investigation ten quantitative trait locus (QTL) were identified for root total length and primary root diameter11. However, all these studies are conducted on early maturity soybeans and there are very few pre-existing studies on late maturity soybeans. Studying the broad range of soybeans, especially late maturity groups for the genetics of the RSA traits can give us new insights in identifying genes that are regulating root traits in late maturity soybeans12. It can also help in determining if there is a difference in early vs. late maturity soybeans in terms of their root system architectural traits and genes that are regulating such traits.

In this study we have phenotype close to 500 soybean accessions belonging to maturity group III, IV, and V on blue blotting papers for seven RSA traits. We also conducted a Genome-wide Association Studies (GWAS) using root phenotypic data to identify potential SNPs associated with the RSA traits13.

Materials and methods

Plant material and root phenotyping

This study used soybean accessions from the USDA Soybean Germplasm Collection, which has a diverse range of germplasm, including landrace and modern cultivars (Glycine max), perennial Glycine species, and wild relatives (Glycine soja), (www.soybase.org).

For our study, we intentionally chose 500 accessions with late maturity. These accessions were selected from several geographic locations to guarantee a varied range of late-maturity varieties. The 500 accessions represent a wide range of regions and climates, providing an in-depth look into late-maturity soybean varieties suited to varied climatic situations. The USDA Germplasm Resources Information Network (GRIN) database (www.ars-grin.gov) provides detailed information on each accession, such as its name, number, and country of origin.

Root phenotyping was carried out in three stages: (1) Germination of seeds and growing plants on blue blotting papers in a hydroponics system, (2) capturing root images using inhouse imaging platform, and (3) analyzing the root images using Rootnav2.0.

There were 6 replicates for each genotype to guarantee measurement consistency and accuracy. “Williams 82” was used as a control in each batch to ensure uniformity and accuracy across batches. To reduce bias, experimental units were randomized, which means genotypes were randomly grown in the growth chamber and hydroponic system. This randomization assures that any reported data variances are attributable to genetics rather than external causes or systematic mistakes.

Seed sterilization and germination

Samples were prepared in multiple batches for ease of handling and minimizing errors. Ten undamaged seeds from each genotype were used for germination and surface sterilized in 100% bleach for two minutes in 50 mL falcon tubes. Subsequently, seeds were rinsed three times with water to eliminate residual bleach. Seeds were then pre-soaked in water for 2 h to promote germination. Brown germination papers were cut in half (19.5 cm × 9.2 cm), soaked in water, and autoclaved before use. Inside a biosafety cabinet, ten sterilized/pre-soaked seeds from each genotype were positioned 2 cm from the top edge on the brown germination papers, which were then rolled and secured with an elastic band. These rolls were transferred in a beaker containing 20 ml of water and covered with a transparent plastic sheet to sustain moisture for seed germination (Fig. 1). The assembly was placed in a growth chamber with 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness for one week. These papers were sprayed routinely during this period to ensure seeds receive enough water for proper germination.

Germination of soybean seeds in brown germination paper. Ten seeds from each accession was selected, sterilized, and presoaked before transferring into these germination papers.

Transferring and growing soybean plants on blue blotting papers

After one week, six uniform and healthy seedlings from each accession were transferred to autoclaved blue blotter paper (20 × 50 cm: Anchor Paper Company, St Paul, MN, USA) for further growth. One seedling weas used per each blotting paper. Each blue paper had a one-inch folded flap from the top portion with a hole in the middle of the sim that was used to hold the plant during its growth. Two blue papers carrying single seedlings were held back-to-back using binder clips and then were covered with black polythene sheets to avoid inhibitory effects of light on the root growth. The papers carrying soybean seedlings were then suspended on a metal platform over a black polypropylene tank (54.5 × 42.5 × 6.0 cm) that was filled with water and nutrient solutions. The papers were hung in such a way that their bottom portion was about 3–4 cm dipped inside the water. Seedlings were supplied with water and B&D nutrient solution (Broughton and Dilworth6) hydroponically (Fig. 2). This setup was kept in a growth chamber with a photoperiod of 16 h light and 8 h dark with 400 µmol m − 2 s− 1 flux density at 22 °C for 21 days. Every 2 days, the exposed portion of these blue papers was sprayed with soybean-specific fungicide (Pageant Intrinsic Fungicide) to avoid fungal contamination. Each paper was also labeled with a 2D barcode containing the USDA accession and replication numbers, which was later scanned during imaging. These barcodes were scanned by the software and decoded as text to save the filenames with no human intervention that helped in increasing efficiency and accuracy.

Design of in-house imaging platform and phenotypic data collection

Imaging of the roots was carried out using a Canon T5i camera and 135 mm lens. The camera was connected to a computer that had Smart Shooter program to capture the root images (Fig. 3). This program was also able to scan the barcodes on each blue paper and use it for storing the filenames for each image without human intervention. The images were acquired as standardized compressed image formats (JPG files), which were used for image analysis in the next step. The imaging platform also had two LED lights on opposite sides for proper illumination of the samples.

Imaging platform used for capturing soybean roots. System is equipped with a Canon T5i camera and Smart Shooter software that can read the barcodes on each sample and save the images with accession name automatically.

Statistical analysis

The data for root system architecture (RSA) traits from RootNav 2.0 were analysed using the arithmetic mean of all replicates for each attribute. This method was used to reduce the data and get a single estimate of the trait values. The arithmetic mean was chosen to reflect the central tendency of the trait assessments, decreasing variability and maintaining data consistency in subsequent analyses. To investigate the relationships among various phenotypic traits, we performed a Pearson correlation analysis. This analysis was conducted using a custom Python script in Google Colaboratory (September 1, 2023, https://colab.research.google.com/). The Pearson correlation coefficients, which range from − 1 to 1, were calculated to determine the strength and direction of linear relationships between the traits. A coefficient close to 1 indicates a strong positive correlation, while a value close to -1 indicates a strong negative correlation. Values near zero suggest weak or no linear relationships between the traits.

Normal distribution

To check for outliers in TASSEL, the phenotypic data was normalized. The replicates that were far from the rest of the data were considered as outliers and were removed. After eliminating the outliers, the data was checked to ensure that it was normally distributed, which is required for MLM and GLM models in TASSEL22. It is crucial to guarantee normal distribution before running GWAS to obtain better results because of TASSEL.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

PCA is a popular method to dimensionally reduce large data sets by transforming the large set of variables into smaller sets but with most of the information23. TASSEL 5 was used to conduct Principal Component Analysis. Then the scores from PC1, PC2, and PC3 (principal components) were used in GWAS (MLM model). Three PC components were used because they captured most of the variations and the rest components could be discarded without losing much information24.

SNP marker selection for GWAS

For the GWAS analysis, SNP markers for all the genotypes were retrieved from the Illumina Infinium SoySNP50K iSelect SNP Beadchip25. After preprocessing to remove SNPs with a minor allele frequency below 0.05 and monomorphic SNPs, 32,800 SNPs were selected for further analysis.

Kinship matrix (K)

The kinship coefficient measures the probability that an allele which is taken randomly from a population is identical by descent to another allele which is also chosen randomly from the population on the same locus as the first one26. TASSEL 5 was used to generate the kinship matrix (K) based on the “scaled Identity by State (IBS)” similarity matrix.

Linkage disequilibrium

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) was calculated to measure the correlation between the alleles at neighboring polymorphisms on the same chromosomes but not on the same locus in each population. It was calculated using the SNPs with MAF greater than 5% in the genotypic data. 50Kb window was used for LD decay during the analysis. First, the genotypes were clustered using TASSEL 5.0. We then used TASSEL 5.0 to calculate the pairwise LD (r8).

Genome-wide Association studies

In the course of our statistical analysis, the data generated in previous steps were utilized for Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) to identify genes associated with root traits10. Two common approaches, including (1) the Fixed and random model Circulating Probability Unification (FarmCPU) package in R (version 4.3.1), and (2) models in Tassel 5.0, were employed. This approach allowed for the comparison of our findings with previous reports that may have employed either of these software packages.

Within, we employed both the Mixed Linear Model (MLM) and the General Linear Model (GLM) using the TASSEL software to conduct association analyses. These models were selected to comprehensively account for various factors, including genotypic influences, population structure, and individual relatedness, thereby enhancing the robustness and validity of our findings.

The Mixed Linear Model (MLM) was specifically implemented to address both fixed and random effects, with a focus on capturing genotype and population structure. To evaluate the relatedness among individuals and control for confounding effects due to shared ancestry, we incorporated a kinship matrix (K) into the model. This matrix was generated using the “scaled Identity by State (IBS)” similarity matrix, which is a standard approach in genomic studies for accurately reflecting genetic relatedness. In addition to the kinship matrix, we used Principal Component Analysis (PCA) scores to further control for residual population structure. The first three principal components (PC1, PC2, and PC3) were included in the model to capture the primary sources of variance within the dataset, which was crucial for minimizing the potential for spurious associations due to population stratification. To ensure the assumptions of the MLM were met, we thoroughly examined the model residuals, confirming a good model fit. Furthermore, to control the possibility of false-positive findings due to multiple testing, we applied the Bonferroni correction to adjust the p-values. This conservative approach ensured that the associations identified were statistically robust and reliable.

In addition to the MLM, we also utilized the General Linear Model (GLM) within TASSEL as a supplementary approach. Unlike the MLM, the GLM did not incorporate a kinship matrix; instead, it focused on adjusting for population structure using PCA scores. The GLM was run with standard parameters, and similar to the MLM analysis, we examined the residuals to verify that the model assumptions were satisfied. To account for multiple comparisons in the GLM analysis, we again employed the Bonferroni correction. This adjustment was critical in maintaining the reliability of the significant associations identified in the analysis.

To further validate our findings, we conducted a comparative analysis using the Fixed and Random model Circulating Probability Unification (FarmCPU) package in R (version 4.3.1). The FarmCPU models were executed with default parameters, and as with the MLM and GLM analyses, we applied the Bonferroni correction to control for false associations.

Candidate genes and comparison with earlier reports

SoyBase (http://www.soybase.org) was used for identifying genes that are potentially associated with Root System Architecture (RSA) traits. The GlymaID from Gmax version 2.0 served as the reference system for searching candidate genes within 100Kb upstream and downstream to the significant SNPs identified from GWAS and determining the primary proteins encoded by these genes. To refine the selection of candidate genes, Gene Ontology (GO)-based molecular, biological functions, and cellular component descriptions relevant to root function were utilized27.

The genes identified through our study were subsequently cross-referenced with genes reported in earlier reports in soybean. This comparative analysis aimed to ascertain whether any common loci or genes were shared between our findings and those of earlier investigations. This approach enhances the contextual understanding of our results by establishing potential correlations or divergences with previously reported genetic associations in soybean root traits. After identifying the candidate genes, we assessed their expression levels using the ePlant database (https://bar.utoronto.ca/eplant_soybean/, accessed on November 18, 2023). A heatmap was subsequently generated to visualize the expression patterns of the selected genes, using custom python script in Google Colaboratory (November 22, 2023, https://colab.research.google.com/). To further understand the role of this gene in root architecture, homologs of the candidate gene were searched across other species. This analysis aimed to determine whether the homologs in these species similarly influence root architecture.

Results and discussion

Phenotyping

A well-structured research approach is imperative to address the challenges posed by declining agricultural yields. Roots are often regarded as the primary defense mechanism when plants encounter environmental stressors28. From a morpho-physiological perspective, roots are fundamental to the processes of water and nutrient uptake, transport, and assimilation29. The architecture and functionality of roots are pivotal to crop productivity, and consequently, research on root systems has gained increasing prominence in agricultural science as a means to enhance productivity more holistically. In contrast to other plant tissues, the genetic study of roots has been relatively underexplored. Nevertheless, there has been a growing recognition in recent years of the critical importance of root traits, with some positing that a deeper understanding and application of root trait data could herald a second Green Revolution in agricultur30. Phenotypic variation within a germplasm pool is indispensable for plant breeders to achieve progress through selection. In this study, seven root traits were assessed across 500 genotypes under hydroponic conditions.Due to low germination rate or poor plant growth, we only used 250 accessions that had at least 6 replicates for GWAS analysis. The RSML files generated by this software were converted to Excel format using RSMLx, a Windows-based Excel plug-in that is designed for examining and analyzing Root System Markup Language (RSML) file (http://rootsystemml.github.io/tools/excell_rsml)7.

The data acquired from root analysis included seven separate features for each soybean accession, with each variable assessed six times. These characteristics comprised the average lateral length, average primary length, convex hull area, tortuosity, lateral root count, total root length, and primary root count. The dataset was then checked for normal distribution to verify it matched the statistical assumptions (Fig. 5). The detected outliers were eliminated from the data to prepare for Multilevel Modelling (MLM) and Generalized Linear Modelling (GLM), which are used in Genome-Wide Association Studies to ensure accurate analysis. A list of top performing and low performing genotypes for each phenotype is provided in Table 2. To investigate the relationships among various phenotypic traits, we conducted a Pearson correlation analysis (Fig. 6). Our analysis reveals several noteworthy associations among the traits, particularly significant positive correlations. For instance, a positive correlation was observed between the convex hull area and total root length (r = 0.28, p < 0.05), suggesting that an increase in the convex hull area is generally associated with an increase in total root length. This indicates that larger root systems in spatial terms also tend to be longer overall. Additionally, the average lateral length shows a significant positive correlation with total length (r = 0.25, p < 0.05), implying that as lateral roots grow longer, the entire root system’s length increases proportionally.

The analysis also uncovered a significant positive correlation between primary root count and lateral root count (r = 0.21, p < 0.05). This suggests that plants with more primary roots are likely to develop more lateral roots as well, highlighting a potential relationship between these root traits that could contribute to overall root system density and complexity. Furthermore, a significant positive correlation was found between the convex hull area and primary root count (r = 0.20, p < 0.05), indicating that an increase in the number of primary roots is associated with a larger area occupied by the root system. The heatmap also includes correlations that are not statistically significant, as indicated by the absence of asterisks. As expected, the diagonal elements of the heatmap all display a correlation value of 1.00, reflecting the fact that each trait is perfectly correlated with itself. Interestingly, our phenotypic study found positive relationships between convex hull area and total root length, which are consistent with previous findings on root architecture in soybean landraces31.

Genome-wide associate studies

While several studies have focused on the agronomic traits of soybeans, only a limited number have applied the Genome-Wide Association Study method to explore the genetic basis of root traits in soybean. Conducting such research necessitates genetic variation within the germplasm resources being studied. Wild soybean demonstrates the greatest allelic diversity for various agronomic traits, followed by landraces, with cultivated soybean exhibiting the lowest level of diversity. In this study, a diverse collection of landraces was utilized for Genome-Wide Association Studies to identify genetic loci associated with root traits. The primary aim of this research was to determine the genetic basis underlying the phenotypic variation observed in the soybean root system. In Genome-Wide Association Studies, marker density plays a critical role in ensuring comprehensive genome coverage and capturing the full extent of haplotypic variation. A higher number of markers enables sufficient linkage disequilibrium, which is essential for identifying significant marker-trait associations. Previous studies exploring root traits using both QTL mapping and GWAS have often utilized smaller marker sets, ranging from 232 to 38,052 markers32–35. In this study, single Nucleotide Polymorphism markers for all genotypes were obtained using the Illumina Infinium SoySNP50K iSelect SNP Beadchip25. After preprocessing steps to exclude SNPs with a minor allele frequency below 0.05 and monomorphic SNPs, a total of 32,800 SNPs were retained for further analysis.

There were a total of 26 unique SNPs associated with seven different root traits were identified through both platforms TASSEL 5.0 (both GLM and MLM) and FarmCPU, allowed us to identify genomic regions connected to desirable traits. Recent GWAS studies on soybean root traits have revealed 10 chromosomal regions associated with root length and diameter, as well as four QTL regions related to lateral root number. MLM was chosen for GWAS because it considers population structure and kinship in the association analysis. It reduces Type I error due to relatedness and population structure, whereas GLM analysis accounts only for population structure. FarmCPU was used because it eliminates disorientation between the testing marker and both K and Q by allowing a fixed effect model and a random effect model to perform independently. TASSEL 5.0 identified 17 SNPs in total- 16 through GLM and 6 through MLM. Out of which there were 5 SNPs common between GLM and MLM models- ss715591121, ss715601599, ss715606127, ss715614574, ss715616949 for total root length, convex hull area, primary root count, tortuosity respectively. A total of 20 SNPs were identified through FarmCPU with 11 SNPs common with TASSEL (GLM or MLM) output. 3 SNPs were common between GLM, MLM, and FarmCPU- ss71559112, ss715614574, ss715616949 for total root length, primary root length, and tortuosity respectively. Maximum number (i.e. 9) of SNPs were identified on chromosome 13. For each specific trait multiple significant SNPs were identified: 8 for average lateral length, 16 for average primary length, 5 for primary root count, 6 for lateral root count, 1 for convex hull area, 7 for tortuosity, and 5 for total root length (Table 3). Additionally, the discovered 26 unique SNPs linked to seven root features in soybean, indicating substantial connections between parameters including average lateral length, total root length, and root count. These results are consistent with prior studies, that discovered substantial relationships between SNPs and root features such as lateral root number and primary root length10. In a recent Genome-Wide Association Study involving soybean landraces, several SNPs and MTP regions were identified as being associated with root traits39,40,31,35,41.

Overall, the multi-model GWAS technique used in this work, which combines MLM and GLM, is supported by comparable approaches in literature, which strengthens the validity of our findings40. Furthermore, the SNPs revealed in this study serve as a basis for future breeding programs targeted at enhancing root system efficiency under diverse climatic circumstances, which aligns with the aims mentioned in previous studies as well35.

Following the identification of unique SNPs associated with traits of interest using multi model GWAS approaches, we proceeded to identify putative candidate genes linked to these significant SNPs (Table 3). In total, we identified 285 genes within 100-kb regions flanking the SNPs. This initial set of genes was narrowed down to a shortlist of 14 based on their Gene Ontology (GO) annotations, which included molecular functions, biological processes, cellular component descriptions, and potential involvement in root development, as well as their expression profiles in root-specific organs and tissues (Table 4). In a similar study, Seck et al.10 identified two relevant candidate genes (Glyma.03g065700 and Glyma.07g096000) associated with soybean root development, particularly in root tips, using a comparable approach. Moreover, Glyma.17G258700, which exhibited significantly higher expression in root tips compared to other plant tissues (Supplementary Fig. 8), was analyzed for its differential expression across various tissues. This methodological framework has also been successfully applied in recent studies, such as elucidating candidate genes for tocopherol content in soybean seeds43.

The Arabidopsis homolog of Glyma.17G258700 is AT4G24190, which encodes an ortholog of GRP94, an ER-resident HSP90-like protein involved in regulating meristem size and organization. Mutants carrying recessive mutations in this gene display expanded shoot meristems, disorganized root meristems, and defective pollen tube elongation [http://arabidopsis.org, (November 2023)]. Further, we identified the Arabidopsis homolog of Glyma.03G023000 as AT2G45420, which encodes a LOB domain protein. LBD18/ASL20 is a transcription regulator that works in conjunction with LBD16 to promote lateral root initiation [http://arabidopsis.org, (November, 2023)]. Additionally, Glyma.13G273500’s homolog in Arabidopsis, AT3G15150, plays a key role in regulating root meristem growth [http://arabidopsis.org, (November 2023)]. Given the high expression levels of these genes and the role of their homologs in model plants in root architecture, these genes warrant further validation and expression analysis. Beyond these key genes, further research is essential to validate and confirm the identity and function of the remaining genes. Moreover, it is important to assess the expression profiles of all promising candidate genes to determine differences between genotypes/populations and to observe their expression patterns across different stages of root development.

Conclusion

Climate change has a negative impact on plant growth and productivity, and breeding plants that are adapted to such changing environment is of vital importance. Plants with better root structure can access water and nutrients from deeper soils and therefore can perform better under harsh conditions.

Our root phenotyping findings underscore the complex interrelationships among phenotypic traits. The significant positive correlations, such as those between average lateral length and total length, and between convex hull area and total length, suggest that these traits may be interconnected through shared biological processes or influenced by similar environmental factors. On the other hand, the non-significant correlations indicate that some traits may vary independently, reflecting the diversity of influences and interactions within the phenotype. These insights are crucial for understanding how different traits contribute to root system architecture and overall plant performance. This study makes major advances in understanding the genetic basis of root system design in soybeans, particularly in the context of climate change. The discovery of 26 SNPs related with critical root properties reveals how these genetic markers might improve root structure and plant resistance. The detection of SNPs using both GLM and MLM models in TASSEL, as well as the validation of 11 SNPs by both TASSEL and FarmCPU, demonstrates the robustness of these findings. The considerable overlap between the SNPs found in this study and those reported in previous studies indicates that these markers have the potential to improve soybean root characteristics. The 13 SNPs or genes that were shown to be common or near to prior findings might be used as first targets for functional research and breeding initiatives. These genetic markers are critical for creating soybean cultivars that can withstand harsh weather conditions.

Given that the phenotyping was done on blue blotting sheets, which mimic early seedling stages but differ from field circumstances, it is critical to confirm these findings in more natural settings. Future study should include field experiments to evaluate the performance of these SNPs and genes in real-world settings.

The discovered potential MTAs and genes represent intriguing opportunities for genetic engineering and breeding initiatives to improve soybean resilience and production. Using these markers, researchers and breeders could develop soybean lines that are more adapted to changing climates, ultimately contributing to global food security and agricultural sustainability.
