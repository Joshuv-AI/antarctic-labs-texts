Flatness of location-scale-shape models under the Wasserstein metric
Ayumu Fukushi, Yoshinori Nakanishi-Ohno, Takeru Matsuda

Abstract
In Wasserstein geometry, one-dimensional location–scale models are flat both intrinsically and extrinsically-that is, they are curvature-free as well as totally geodesic in the space of probability distributions. In this study, we introduce a class of one-dimensional statistical models, termed the location–scale–shape model, which generalizes several distributions used in extreme-value theory. This model has a shape parameter that specifies the tail heaviness. We investigate the Wasserstein geometry of the location-scale-shape model and show that it is intrinsically flat but extrinsically curved. Keywords: Wasserstein geometry, Wasserstein information matrix, location-scale-shape model, displacement interpolation



1
Introduction

In information geometry, a parametric family of probability distributions is called a statistical model. By viewing the parameters as coordinates, one regards the model as a manifold, equips it with various metrics and connections, and studies the resulting geometric properties; this viewpoint has many applications in statistics, machine learning, and related areas. In particular, the geometry induced by the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence has been extensively studied, and its close relationship to maximum likelihood estimation is well known
[
3
]
.

The Wasserstein distance is a central notion of distance between probability distributions in statistics and machine learning
[
7
,
19
,
20
]
. It arises from the field of optimal transport, and the associated geometry is known as the Wasserstein geometry
[
22
,
23
]
.
The associated metric is also known as Otto metric
[
18
]
.
In contrast to the KL divergence, the Wasserstein distance is a genuine metric (symmetric and satisfying the triangle inequality) and remains meaningful even when the supports of the distributions differ. Also, the Wasserstein distance reflects the metric structure of the underlying space through transportation cost.
Motivated by the fact that the Fisher information matrix arises as the second-order approximation to the KL divergence, Li and Zhao
[
14
]
proposed the Wasserstein information matrix. The intrinsic geometry induced by the Wasserstein information matrix and its statistical implications have been studied recently
[
2
,
5
,
6
,
13
,
12
,
14
]
.

Whereas the Wasserstein distance does not admit a closed form in general, it is obtained in closed form in location-scale models
[
10
]
.
This is the basis of recent studies on the Wasserstein geometry of location-scale models and its statistical application
[
1
,
2
,
15
]
.
Geometrically, location-scale models are intrinsically flat as Riemannian manifolds under the metric proposed by
[
14
]
.
Moreover, they are totally geodesic with respect to the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-Wasserstein distance (i.e., closed under displacement interpolation).

In this paper, we introduce the class of location-scale-shape models as an extension of the location-scale models and study its Wasserstein geometry. The shape parameter we introduce controls the tail heaviness, which is often the focus of statistical analysis. In particular, the generalized extreme value and generalized Pareto distributions—central to extreme value statistics, which concerns inference on rare events
[
9
]
—are not location-scale models but are the location-scale-shape models.
We show that, as in earlier work on location–scale models, location–scale–shape models are intrinsically flat, however they are not extrinsically flat.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we give a brief review of the framework by Li and Zhao
[
14
]
on the Wasserstein scores and the Wasserstein information matrix. In Section 3, we introduce the location-scale-shape model and derive its Wasserstein score functions and the Wasserstein information matrix. In Section 4, we discuss the Wasserstein–geometric properties of the location-scale-shape models. In particular, although the intrinsic curvature induced by the Wasserstein information matrix vanishes, the models is not totally geodesic with respect to the Wasserstein distance, i.e., it is not extrinsically flat when embedded in the Wasserstein space.

Preliminaries

2.1
Wasserstein information matrix

On a measure space
(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{B},\nu)
, a family of probability density functions
\mathcal{S}
with parameter space
\Theta
, an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, given by

\mathcal{S}=\{p(x;\theta)\mid\theta\in\Theta\},

is called a statistical model. In information geometry, a statistical model is regarded as a manifold with
\Theta
as a system of local coordinates, and one studies the relationship between geometric properties and the statistical properties of models and estimators.

Let
(\mathfrak{X},d)
be a complete separable metric space and let
p\geq 1
. Denote by
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathfrak{X})
the set of all Borel probability measures on
(\mathfrak{X},d)
with finite
p
-th moment:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathfrak{X})=\bigl\{\pi:\text{Borel probability measure on }(\mathfrak{X},d)\ \bigm|\ \int_{\mathfrak{X}}d(x,x_{0})^{p}\,\differential\mu(x)<\infty\,\bigr\}.

(1)

We define the
L^{p}
-Wasserstein distance
W_{p}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
on
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathfrak{X})
by

\displaystyle W_{p}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})^{p}:=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})}\int_{\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}}d(x,y)^{p}\differential\pi(x,y),

(2)

where

\displaystyle\Pi(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\Bigl\{\,\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X})\ \Bigm|\ \pi(A\times\mathfrak{X})=\mu_{1}(A),\ \pi(\mathfrak{X}\times B)=\mu_{2}(B)\ (A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{X}))\Bigr\}.

In what follows, we focus on the space
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, taking
\mathfrak{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
p=2
, and
d
to be the Euclidean distance on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Li and Zhao
[
14
]
introduced analogues of the Fisher score function
and Fisher information matrix
on a statistical model
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
under the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-Wasserstein distance.
The Wasserstein score functions
\Phi^{W}_{i}(x;\theta)
,
i=1,\dots,n
, are defined as the solutions to the Poisson-type partial differential equation

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\cdot\bigl(p(x;\theta)\,\nabla_{x}\Phi^{W}_{i}(x;\theta)\bigr)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}p(x;\theta),

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\Phi^{W}_{i}(X;\theta)]

\displaystyle=0,

(4)

where
\nabla_{x}
denotes the gradient with respect to
x
and
\nabla_{x}\cdot
denotes the divergence operator.
Note that equation (
3
) is the parametric analogue of the continuity equation which, in optimal transport theory, characterizes geodesics in the
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
In a related direction,
[
6
]
introduced Wasserstein score functions for the case where
\mathfrak{X}
is a discrete space equipped with a undirected graph structure.
The Wasserstein score functions are analogous to the Fisher score functions
\frac{\partial\log p(x;\theta)}{\partial\theta^{i}}
. We denote the Fisher score functions by
\Phi^{F}_{i}(x;\theta)
.

We define the Wasserstein information matrix
I^{W}(\theta)
by

\displaystyle I^{W}(\theta)_{ij}=\int\Phi^{W}_{i}(x;\theta)\,\frac{\partial p(x;\theta)}{\partial\theta^{j}}\,\differential x=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\!\left[\nabla_{x}\Phi^{W}_{i}(x;\theta)^{\top}\nabla_{x}\Phi^{W}_{j}(x;\theta)\right].

(5)

Same as the Fisher metric induced by the Fisher information matrix, the Wasserstein information matrix induces a Riemannian metric on
\mathcal{S}
. We call this the Wasserstein metric and denote it by
g_{W}
. This metric is also known as the Otto metric
[
18
]
.
The Wasserstein information matrix is analogous to the Fisher information matrix
I^{F}(\theta)_{ij}=\int\Phi^{F}_{i}(x;\theta)\frac{\partial p(x;\theta)}{\partial\theta^{j}}\differential x
.

The Wasserstein information matrix provides a second-order approximation of the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-Wasserstein distance:

\displaystyle W_{2}(p_{\theta},p_{\theta+\Delta\theta})=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta\theta^{\top}I^{W}(\theta)\,\Delta\theta+o(\|\Delta\theta\|^{2}).

This parallels the classical fact that the Fisher information matrix is the second-order approximation of the Kullback–Leibler divergence.
Li and Zhao
[
14
]
also introduced an analogue of the maximum likelihood estimator and a new covariance notion; they derived a Wasserstein-–Cramér-–Rao inequality showing that the inverse of
I^{W}(\theta)
gives a lower bound on the estimation variance. We omit the details here.

2.2
Location-scale model

Let
f
be a probability density function on
\mathbb{R}
, and let
\theta=(\mu,\sigma)
with
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma>0
. The two-para statistical model

\displaystyle p(x;\theta)=\frac{1}{\sigma}f\!\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)

is called the location-scale model, and we write
\mathcal{M}_{f}=\quantity{p(x;\theta)\mid\theta\in\bbr\times\bbr_{>0}}
.
Here,
\mu
is the location parameter and
\sigma
is the scale parameter.

For location-scale models, the Wasserstein distance (
2
) admits a closed form; moreover, they are totally geodesic in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
with respect to the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-Wasserstein distance (i.e., closed under displacement interpolation)
[
10
]
.

In the location-scale family, the Wasserstein score functions are

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mu}^{W}(x;\theta)

\displaystyle=x-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[X],

(6)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\sigma}^{W}(x;\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma}-\frac{V_{\theta}[X]+(\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[X]-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma},

(7)

and the Wasserstein information matrix
I^{W}(\theta)
is

\displaystyle I^{W}(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}1&{\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[X]-\mu\over\sigma}\\
{\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[X]-\mu\over\sigma}&{V_{\theta}[X]\over\sigma^{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

(8)

Note that if
f
has mean
0
and variance
1
, then (
8
) is the identity matrix. For a general
f
, the reparametrization
\omega=(\mu+\sigma m,\ \sigma s)
brings the model to mean
0
and variance
1
at
\omega=(0,1)
, where

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{(0,1)}[X],

(9)

\displaystyle s^{2}

\displaystyle:=V_{(0,1)}[X].

(10)

Since (
8
) can be made the identity matrix, the Riemannian curvature associated with the Wasserstein metric
g_{W}
vanishes over the model
\mathcal{M}_{f}
; it means
\mathcal{M}_{f}
is intrinsically flat as a Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{M}_{f},g_{W})
.

3
Location-scale-shape models

As an extension of the location-scale model, we introduce a location-scale-shape model that has one-dimensional location. scale, and shape parameter.

Definition 1
.

(location-scale-shape model)
Let
\Xi\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open set containing
0
, and let
f\in C^{4}(\mathbb{R})
be a probability density function on
\mathbb{R}
.
The three-parameter statistical model

\mathcal{S}_{f}=\{p(x;\theta)\mid\theta\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\Xi\}

is called the
location-scale-shape model
generated by
f
if the probability density function
p(x;\theta)
is written as

\displaystyle p(x;\theta)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(1+\xi\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{-1}f\left(\frac{1}{\xi}\log\left(1+\xi\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\right),&\text{if }\xi\neq 0,\\
\frac{1}{\sigma}f\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right),&\text{if }\xi=0.\end{cases}

As in the location-scale model, we call
\mu
the location parameter and
\sigma
the scale parameter. Also, we call
\xi
the shape parameter.

Since

\displaystyle\text{supp}\ p_{\theta}=\Bigl\{x\mid 1+\xi\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\geq 0,\ \frac{1}{\xi}\log\Bigl(1+\xi\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\Bigr)\in\text{supp}\ f\Bigr\},

the support of the distribution strongly depends on the parameters. Note that we require
f
to be of class
C^{4}(\mathbb{R})
for theoretical reasons. This assumption is minimal for the present framework and one has
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
in many important examples.

The shape parameter specifies the shape of the probability density function
f(x)
.
As that shape varies, the associated location and scale also vary accordingly. Note that this framework does not contain all types of "shape parameters" in statistics.
In Examples 1 and 2 below, we show figures in which only the shape parameter is varied.
Moreover, for each fixed
\xi\in\Xi
, define

\displaystyle f_{\xi}(x)=\begin{cases}(1+\xi x)^{-1}f\quantity({1\over\xi}\log\qty( 1 + \xi x)),&\text{if }\xi\neq 0,\\
f(x),&\text{if }\xi=0.\end{cases}

This determines a location-scale model
\mathcal{M}_{f_{\xi}}
. In other words, a location-scale-shape model is a statistical model that continuously deforms location-scale models. Geometrically, the family
\{\mathcal{M}_{f_{\xi}}\}_{\xi\in\Xi}
endows
\mathcal{S}_{f}
with a foliation
\mathcal{S}_{f}=\bigsqcup_{\xi}\mathcal{M}_{f_{\xi}}
.

Below, we present two examples of the location-scale-shape model. These models play a central role in extreme value theory. Other examples include the generalized normal and generalized logistic distributions introduced in
[
11
]
; these are used in hydrology (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
8
,
11
]
).

Example 1
.

(Generalized Extreme Value Distribution)

A probability distribution with density

p(x;\theta)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(1+\xi\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{-{1\over\xi}-1}\exp\left(-\left(1+\xi\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{-{1\over\xi}}\right),&\text{if }\xi\neq 0,\\
\frac{1}{\sigma}\exp\left(-\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\right)\exp\left(-\exp\left(-\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\right)\right),&\text{if }\xi=0\end{cases}

is called the generalized extreme value distribution
\mathrm{GEV}(\mu,\sigma,\xi)
.
This distribution is a location–scale–shape model generated by the standard Gumbel distribution,

\displaystyle f(z)=\exp(-\exp(-z)).

As will be shown later, the moment generating function
f
plays an important role in the location-scale-shape model; the moment generating function of the standard Gumbel distribution is given by

\displaystyle M_{f}(t)=\Gamma(1-t),\quad t<1,

where
\Gamma(x)
denotes the Gamma function.
The support of
\mathrm{GEV}(\mu,\sigma,\xi)
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x\in[\mu-{\sigma\over\xi},\infty),&\text{if }\xi>0\\
x\in[-\infty,\infty),&\text{if }\xi=0\\
x\in(-\infty,\mu-{\sigma\over\xi}],&\text{if }\xi<0.\end{cases}

Figure
1
shows how the density function changes as
\xi
varies for
\mathrm{GEV}(0,1,\xi)
.

Example 2
.

(Generalized Pareto Distribution)

A probability distribution with density

p(x;\theta)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(1+\xi\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{-{1\over\xi}-1},&\text{if }\xi\neq 0,\\
\frac{1}{\sigma}\exp\left(-\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\right),&\text{if }\xi=0.\end{cases}

is called the generalized Pareto distribution
\mathrm{GPD}(\mu,\sigma,\xi)

This distribution is a location–scale–shape model generated by the standard exponential distribution,

\displaystyle f(z)=\exp(-z).

The moment generating function of the standard exponential distribution is given by

\displaystyle M_{f}(t)=\frac{1}{1-t},\quad t<1.

The support of
\mathrm{GPD}(\mu,\sigma,\xi)
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x\in[\mu,\infty),&\text{if }\xi\geq 0,\\
x\in[\mu,\mu-{\sigma\over\xi}],&\text{if }\xi<0.\end{cases}

Figure
2
shows how the density function changes as
\xi
varies for
\mathrm{GPD}(0,1,\xi)
. Note that
\mathrm{GPD}(\mu,\sigma,\xi)
is uniform distribution when
\xi=-1
.

The following propositions demonstrate that the shape parameter
\xi
controls the tail heaviness of the distribution.
We denote by
F
the cumulative distribution function of
f
, and by
P_{\theta}(x)
the cumulative distribution function of
p(x;\theta)
.

Proposition 1
.

P_{\theta}(x)
is nonincreasing in
\xi
for fixed
x\in\mathbb{R},\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\sigma>0
.

Proof.

Note that
P_{\theta}(x)
is given by

\displaystyle P_{\theta}(x)=\begin{cases}F\quantity(\log\qty( 1 + \xi\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} )^{1\over\xi}),&\text{if }\xi\neq 0,\\
F\!\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right),&\text{if }\xi=0,\end{cases}

for
x\in\text{supp}\,p_{\theta}
. Let
s={x-\mu\over\sigma}
.
If
\xi
satisfies
1+\xi s>0
, we have

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\xi}P_{\theta}(x)

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\xi}F\quantity(\log(1+\xi s)^{1\over\xi})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\,F^{\prime}\quantity(\log(1+\xi s)^{1\over\xi})\quantity(\frac{\xi s}{1+\xi s}-\log(1+\xi s))<0,

where
F^{\prime}
denotes the derivative of
F
, and the final inequality holds because
cumulative distribution functions are nondecreasing and
\frac{y}{1+y}-\log(1+y)<0
for
y>-1
.

If
\xi
satisfies
1+\xi s<0
, it follows that
x\notin\text{supp}\,p_{\theta}
, and hence
\partialderivative{\xi}P_{\theta}(x)=0
.

∎

Proposition 2
.

Let
\bar{P}_{\theta}(x)=1-P_{\theta}(x)
. Then,
\xi_{1}>\xi_{2}>0
implies
\bar{P}_{\theta_{2}}(x)=O(\bar{P}_{\theta_{1}}(x))\ (x\to\infty)
where
\theta_{i}=(\mu_{i},\sigma_{i},\xi_{i})
for
i=1,2
.

Remark 1
.

Similarly,
\xi_{1}<\xi_{2}<0
implies
P_{\theta_{2}}(x)=O(P_{\theta_{1}}(x))\ (x\to-\infty)
. Therefore, a positive
\xi
corresponds to the right tail heavier, while a negative
\xi
corresponds to the left tail heavier.

Proof.

Let
\bar{F}(x):=1-F(x)
, which is a nonincreasing function. For
\xi_{1}>\xi_{2}>0
, we have

\displaystyle\log\quantity( 1 + \xi_1 \frac{x - \mu_1}{\sigma_1} )^{1\over\xi_{1}}-\log\quantity( 1 + \xi_2 \frac{x - \mu_2}{\sigma_2} )^{1\over\xi_{2}}=\Bigl(\frac{1}{\xi_{1}}-\frac{1}{\xi_{2}}\Bigr)\log x+O(1)\to-\infty\quad(x\to\infty).

This implies that for sufficiently large

, we have

\bar{P}_{\theta_{2}}(x)\leq\bar{P}_{\theta_{1}}(x)
.
As both
\bar{P}_{\theta_{1}}(x)
and
\bar{P}_{\theta_{2}}(x)
tend to
0
, this inequality implies

\bar{P}_{\theta_{2}}(x)=O\!\bigl(\bar{P}_{\theta_{1}}(x)\bigr)
as
x\to\infty
.
∎

The following lemma simplifies the computation of moments.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{f}=\{p(x;\theta)\mid\theta\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\Xi\}
,
Z\sim f(z)
and
X\sim p(x;\theta)
. Then for
\xi\neq 0
, the following holds:

\displaystyle X\overset{d}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma}{\xi}(\exp(\xi Z)-1)+\mu,

(11)

Here,
\overset{d}{=}
denotes that the random variables have the same distribution.

Proof.

Let

and set
W:=g(Z)
. We will show that
X\overset{d}{=}W
. Noting that the mapping

is one-to-one, we have

\displaystyle z=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\xi}\log\left(1+\xi\frac{w-\mu}{\sigma}\right),

\displaystyle\left|\frac{dz}{dw}\right|=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(1+\xi\frac{w-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{-1}.

Hence, the probability density function

of

is given by

\displaystyle q(w)=\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(1+\xi\frac{w-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{-1}f\left(\frac{1}{\xi}\log\left(1+\xi\frac{w-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\right).

This coincides with

. Therefore

.
∎

Then, from Lemma

We derive the Wasserstein score function in location-scale-shape models.

Theorem 1
.

In a location-scale-shape model
\mathcal{S}_{f}
, suppose

M_{f}(t)
exists on

. Then the Wasserstein score function for the parameter

is given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mu}^{W}(x;\theta)

\displaystyle=x-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[X],

\displaystyle\Phi_{\sigma}^{W}(x;\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma}-\frac{V_{\theta}[X]+(\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[X]-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma},

\displaystyle\Phi_{\xi}^{W}(x;\theta)

Remark 3

The Fisher score function

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{S}_{f}

Next, for

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\quantity(p(x;\theta){\del\Phi^W_\xi\over\del x})=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}p(x;\theta).

First, the derivative of
\Phi^{W}_{\xi}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Phi^{W}_{\xi}(x;\theta)=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial t}{\partial x}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi^{W}_{\xi}(t;\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma}{\xi^{2}}(t\log t-t+1).

Note that for

\displaystyle p(t;\theta)=\frac{1}{\sigma t}f\left(\log t^{1/\xi}\right).

) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\quantity(p(x;\theta){\del\Phi^W_\xi\over\del x})=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial t}{\partial x}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\Bigl(\log t-1+\frac{1}{t}\Bigr)f\left(\log t^{1/\xi}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma\xi t}\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{t}\right)f\left(\log t^{1/\xi}\right)+\frac{1}{\xi}\left(\log t-1+\frac{1}{t}\right)f^{\prime}\left(\log t^{1/\xi}\right)\right).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}p(x;\theta)=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial t}{\partial\xi}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p(t;\theta)+\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}p(t;\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t-1}{\xi}\cdot\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(-\frac{1}{t^{2}}f\left(\log t^{1/\xi}\right)+\frac{1}{\xi t^{2}}f^{\prime}\left(\log t^{1/\xi}\right)\right)-\frac{1}{\sigma\xi^{2}t}\log t\,f\left(\log t^{1/\xi}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sigma\xi t}\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{t}\right)f\left(\log t^{1/\xi}\right)+\frac{1}{\xi}\left(\log t-1+\frac{1}{t}\right)f^{\prime}\left(\log t^{1/\xi}\right)\right).

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}p(x;\theta){\partial\Phi^{W}_{\xi}\over\partial x}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}p(x;\theta).

Next, for
\xi\neq 0
we show that
\mathbb{E}[\Phi^{W}_{\xi}]=0
. Let
Z\sim f(z)
,
X\sim p(x;\theta)
, and
T=1+\xi\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}
, then by corollary
1
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\frac{T^{2}}{2}\log T-\frac{3T^{2}}{4}+T\Bigr]=\frac{\xi}{2}M_{f}^{\prime}(2\xi)-\frac{3}{4}M_{f}(2\xi)+M_{f}(\xi).

For
\xi=0
, it suffices to show that the limit as
\xi\to 0
coincides with (
18
). To avoid complications, let
a=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}
. Then, by the Taylor expansion of the logarithm,

\displaystyle\log(1+a\xi)=a\xi-\frac{a^{2}}{2}\xi^{2}+\frac{a^{3}}{3}\xi^{3}+O(\xi^{4}),\quad(\xi\to 0),

so that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\log t=\frac{1}{2}(1+a\xi)^{2}\log(1+a\xi)=\frac{a}{2}\xi+\frac{3}{4}a^{2}\xi^{2}+\frac{a^{3}}{6}\xi^{3}+O(\xi^{4}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\log t-\frac{3}{4}t^{2}+t=

\displaystyle\frac{a}{2}\xi+\frac{3}{4}a^{2}\xi^{2}+\frac{a^{3}}{6}\xi^{3}-\frac{3}{4}(1+a\xi)^{2}+(1+a\xi)+O(\xi^{4})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{6}a^{3}\xi^{3}+O(\xi^{4}).

On the other hand, since the moment generating function is four-times differentiable on
2\Xi
, by Taylor’s theorem we have

\displaystyle\frac{\xi}{2}M_{f}^{\prime}(2\xi)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}M_{f}^{\prime}(0)\xi+M_{f}^{\prime\prime}(0)\xi^{2}+M_{f}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)\xi^{3}+O(\xi^{4}),

\displaystyle M_{f}(\xi)=

\displaystyle M_{f}(0)+M_{f}^{\prime}(0)\xi+\frac{1}{2}M_{f}^{\prime\prime}(0)\xi^{2}+\frac{1}{6}M_{f}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)\xi^{3}+O(\xi^{4}),

\displaystyle\frac{3}{4}M_{f}(2\xi)=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{4}M_{f}(0)+\frac{3}{2}M_{f}^{\prime}(0)\xi+\frac{3}{2}M_{f}^{\prime\prime}(0)\xi^{2}+M_{f}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)\xi^{3}+O(\xi^{4}).

Combining these, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\xi}{2}M_{f}^{\prime}(2\xi)-\frac{3}{4}M_{f}(2\xi)+M_{f}(\xi)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}M_{f}(0)+\frac{1}{6}M_{f}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)\xi^{3}+O(\xi^{4})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{6}M_{f}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)\xi^{3}+O(\xi^{4}),

where we used the fact that
M_{f}(0)=1
by definition of the moment generating function.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Phi^{W}_{\xi}(x;\theta)=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\xi^{3}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\log t-\frac{3}{4}t^{2}+t-\frac{\xi}{2}M_{f}^{\prime}(2\xi)+\frac{3}{4}M_{f}(2\xi)-M_{f}(\xi)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\xi^{3}}\Bigl(\Bigl(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{6}a^{3}\xi^{3}\Bigr)-\Bigl(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{6}M_{f}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)\xi^{3}\Bigr)+O(\xi^{4})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{6}\Bigl(\Bigl(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\Bigr)^{3}-M_{f}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)\Bigr)+O(\xi).

Hence, when
\xi=0
,

\displaystyle\Phi^{W}_{\xi}(x;\theta)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{6}\left(\Bigl(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\Bigr)^{3}-M_{f}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)\right).

∎

The Wasserstein information matrix of the location–scale–shape model
\mathcal{S}_{f}

(20)

where we omit the lower-triangular entries since

4
Intrinsic and extrinsic Wasserstein geometry of location-scale-shape models

In this section, we study the Wasserstein geometry of location–scale–shape models.
First, we introduce new coordinates
\omega=(\alpha,\beta,\xi)
:

\displaystyle\omega=(\mu+\sigma m_{\xi},\ \sigma s_{\xi},\ \xi),

(23)

where

\displaystyle m_{\xi}:=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(0,1,\xi)}[X]={1\over\xi}\bigl(M_{f}(\xi)-1\bigr),

(24)

\displaystyle s_{\xi}^{2}:=

\displaystyle V_{(0,1,\xi)}[X]={1\over\xi^{2}}\bigl(M_{f}(2\xi)-M_{f}(\xi)^{2}\bigr).

(25)

The map
\theta\mapsto\omega
is a diffeomorphism from
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\Xi
onto itself, and its inverse transformation is given by

\displaystyle\omega=(\alpha,\beta,\xi)\mapsto\quantity(\alpha-{m_\xi\over s_\xi}\,\beta,\ {1\over s_\xi}\beta,\ \xi).

(26)

In the coordinates
\omega
, for each fixed
\xi
, the associated location-scale model has mean
\alpha
and variance
\beta^{2}
.

Proposition 3
.

The Wasserstein information matrix of the location–scale–shape model
\mathcal{S}_{f}
with respect to parametrization
\omega=(\alpha,\beta,\xi)
is given as

\displaystyle I^{W}(\omega)=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&\beta^{2}\psi(\xi)\end{pmatrix}

(27)

where
\psi(\xi)
is a function only depends on
\xi
.

Proof.

First, we show that the
I^{W}(\theta)
given by (
20
) can be rewritten using
m_{\xi}
and
s_{\xi}^{2}
as follows:

\displaystyle I^{W}(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}1&m_{\xi}&\sigma m^{\prime}_{\xi}\\
*&s_{\xi}^{2}+m_{\xi}^{2}&\sigma(s_{\xi}s^{\prime}_{\xi}+m_{\xi}m^{\prime}_{\xi})\\
*&*&I_{33}\end{pmatrix},

(28)

where
m^{\prime}_{\xi}:=\frac{d}{d\xi}m_{\xi}
,
s_{\xi}:=\sqrt{s_{\xi}^{2}}
, and
s^{\prime}_{\xi}:=\frac{d}{d\xi}s_{\xi}
.

Secondly, differentiate (
26
), we obtain the Jacobi matrix

\displaystyle{\partial\theta\over\partial\omega}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-{m_{\xi}\over s_{\xi}}&{m_{\xi}s^{\prime}_{\xi}-m^{\prime}_{\xi}s_{\xi}\over s_{\xi}^{2}}\beta\\
0&{1\over s_{\xi}}&-{s^{\prime}_{\xi}\over s^{2}_{\xi}}\beta\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

(29)

Hence combining (
28
) and (
29
), we have

\displaystyle I^{W}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\partial\theta\over\partial\omega}^{\top}I^{W}(\theta(\omega)){\partial\theta\over\partial\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
{-m_{\xi}\over s_{\xi}}&{1\over s_{\xi}}&0\\
{(m_{\xi}s^{\prime}_{\xi}-m^{\prime}_{\xi}s_{\xi})\beta\over s_{\xi}^{2}}&{-s^{\prime}_{\xi}\beta\over s^{2}_{\xi}}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&m_{\xi}&\sigma m^{\prime}_{\xi}\\
*&s_{\xi}^{2}+m_{\xi}^{2}&\sigma(s_{\xi}s^{\prime}_{\xi}+m_{\xi}m^{\prime}_{\xi})\\
*&*&I_{33}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&{-m_{\xi}\over s_{\xi}}&{(m_{\xi}s^{\prime}_{\xi}-m^{\prime}_{\xi}s_{\xi})\beta\over s_{\xi}^{2}}\\
0&{1\over s_{\xi}}&{-s^{\prime}_{\xi}\beta\over s^{2}_{\xi}}\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&I_{33}-{{s^{\prime}_{\xi}}^{2}+{m^{\prime}_{\xi}}^{2}\over s_{\xi}^{2}}\beta^{2}\end{pmatrix},

where the last equality holds because
\sigma={1\over s_{\xi}}\beta
. Recall that

\displaystyle I_{33}=

\displaystyle{\sigma^{2}\over\xi^{4}}(\xi^{2}M_{f}^{\prime\prime}(2\xi)-2\xi M_{f}^{\prime}(2\xi)+2\xi M_{f}^{\prime}(\xi)+M_{f}(2\xi)-2M_{f}(\xi)+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\beta^{2}\over\xi^{4}s_{\xi}^{2}}(\xi^{2}M_{f}^{\prime\prime}(2\xi)-2\xi M_{f}^{\prime}(2\xi)+2\xi M_{f}^{\prime}(\xi)+M_{f}(2\xi)-2M_{f}(\xi)+1),

we conclude

\displaystyle I^{W}(\omega)=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&\beta^{2}\psi(\xi)\end{pmatrix}

where

\displaystyle\psi(\xi)={1\over s_{\xi}^{2}}\quantity({\xi^2 M_f''(2\xi)-2\xi M_f'(2\xi)+2\xi M_f'(\xi)+M_f(2\xi)-2M_f(\xi)+1\over\xi^4}-({s'_\xi}^2+ {m'_\xi}^2)).

(30)

To restate Proposition
3
in geometric terms, we introduce the following definition.
Let
(M,g)
and
(N,h)
be Riemannian manifolds, and let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(M)
. Define a Riemannian metric
G
on
M\times N
by

\displaystyle G=\pi_{M}^{*}g+\bigl(\varphi\circ\pi_{M}\bigr)^{2}\,\pi_{N}^{*}h,

where
\pi_{M}
and
\pi_{N}
denote the natural projections from
M\times N
to
M
and
N
, respectively, and
∗
denotes the pullback. Then
(M\times N,G)
is called the warped product of
(M,g)
and
(N,h)
by
\varphi
. We often omit
G
and write
M\times_{\varphi}N
.
Warped products often appear in differential geometry related to general relativity; for details, see, for example,
[
17
]
.

With this terminology, Proposition
3
can be restated as follows.

Corollary 2
.

Define a function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
by
\varphi(\alpha,\beta)=\beta
. Then the Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{S}_{f},g_{W})
is isometric to the warped product

(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0})\times_{\varphi}\Xi
of
(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0},g_{\mathbb{R}^{2}})
and
(\Xi,g_{\mathbb{R}})
with respect to a function
\varphi
.

Finally, we establish the flatness of location-scale-shape model.

Theorem 3
.

The Riemannian curvature of the Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{S}_{f},g_{W})
vanishes.

Remark 4
.

In the proof below, we construct an affine coordinate system in which the metric becomes the identity matrix. If one is familiar with the computation of Riemannian curvature for warped products, one can readily verify directly that the curvature vanishes.

Proof.

To prove flatness, it is enough to have, locally, a coordinate system in which the metric is the identity matrix.
Let
\psi(\xi)
be as defined in (
30
).
Choose an open interval
I
with
I\subset\Xi
and
\int_{I}\sqrt{\psi(\xi)}\,\differential\xi<2\pi
.
Set
U=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times I
. Define a coordinate transformation from
(\alpha,\beta,\xi)\in U
to new local coordinates
(u,v,w)

by

\displaystyle(u,v,w):=\bigl(\alpha,\ \beta\cos\theta(\xi),\ \beta\sin\theta(\xi)\bigr),

where

\displaystyle\theta(\xi):=\int_{\inf I}^{\xi}\sqrt{\psi(x)}\,\differential x.

Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(u,v,w)}{\partial(\alpha,\beta,\xi)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\cos\theta(\xi)&-\,\beta\,\sqrt{\psi(\xi)}\sin\theta(\xi)\\
0&\sin\theta(\xi)&\;\;\beta\,\sqrt{\psi(\xi)}\cos\theta(\xi)\end{pmatrix},

and it immediately yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(u,v,w)}{\partial(\alpha,\beta,\xi)}^{\top}\frac{\partial(u,v,w)}{\partial(\alpha,\beta,\xi)}=I^{W}(\omega).

Consequently, we obtain

\displaystyle I^{W}(u,v,w)=\frac{\partial(\alpha,\beta,\xi)}{\partial(u,v,w)}^{\top}I^{W}(\omega)\frac{\partial(\alpha,\beta,\xi)}{\partial(u,v,w)}=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,1).

Noting that
\Xi
can be covered by such intervals
I

Next, we discuss the extrinsic geometry of
\mathcal{S}_{f}

\mathrm{L}^{2}

Let
F

f
.
For simplicity, assume that
F
has an inverse
F^{-1}

The cumulative distribution function of

\displaystyle P_{\theta}(x)=\begin{cases}F\quantity(\log\qty( 1 + \xi\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} )^{1\over\xi}),&\text{if }\xi\neq 0,\\
F({x-\mu\over\sigma}),&\text{if }\xi=0,\end{cases}

Since
P^{-1}_{\theta}(x)={\sigma\over\xi}(\exp(\xi F^{-1}(x))-1)+\mu
, OT map from
p_{\theta_{1}}
to
p_{\theta_{2}}
is given by

\displaystyle T_{\theta_{1}\to\theta_{2}}(x)=

\displaystyle P_{\theta_{2}}^{-1}(P_{\theta_{1}}(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\sigma_{2}\over\xi_{2}}(\exp(\xi_2 F^{-1}\qty(F\qty(\log\qty( 1 + \xi_1 \frac{x - \mu_1}{\sigma_1} )^{1\over\xi_1})))-1)+\mu_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\sigma_{2}\over\xi_{2}}\quantity(( 1 + \xi_1 \frac{x - \mu_1}{\sigma_1} )^{\xi_2\over\xi_1}- (1-\xi_2 \frac{ \mu_2}{\sigma_2})

Therefore, the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-Wasserstein Geodesics from

\displaystyle P_{t}(x)=

\displaystyle\quantity((1-t)x+tT_{\theta_1\to\theta_2}(x))_{\#}P_{\theta_{1}}\quad(t\in[0,1])

\displaystyle P_{\theta_{1}}(((1-t)\cdot+tT_{\theta_{1}\to\theta_{2}}(\cdot))^{-1}(x))

(31)

where

\displaystyle(1-t)x+tT_{\theta_{1}\to\theta_{2}}(x)=

\displaystyle 1+t{\sigma_{2}\over\xi_{2}}\quantity(( 1 + \xi_1 \frac{x - \mu_1}{\sigma_1} )^{\xi_2\over\xi_1}- (1+\xi_2 \frac{ x- \mu_2}{\sigma_2})).

Proposition 4
.

For
p_{\theta_{1}},p_{\theta_{2}}\in\mathcal{S}_{f}
,
\quantity{p_t}_{t\in(0,1)}
defined by (

If
\xi_{1}=\xi_{2}

\displaystyle(1-t)P^{-1}_{\theta_{1}}(x)+tP^{-1}_{\theta_{2}}(x).

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{t}}{\xi_{t}}\bigl(\exp(\xi_t F^{-1}(x))-1\bigr)+\mu_{t}

5
Discussion

In this paper, we introduced location-scale-shape models and studied their Wasserstein geometry. We showed that, as in the location-scale models, the Wasserstein score functions do not depend on the base density
f
, and that the entries of the Wasserstein information matrix have simple expressions in terms of the moment-generating function of
f
. Although the parameterization combines location and scale parameters with a shape parameter, the resulting Wasserstein metric is not a direct product but rather a warped product. Moreover, the associated Riemannian manifold is flat. In contrast to the location-scale case, the model is not totally geodesic with respect to the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance.

Our analysis focused on geometric aspects and did not address the statistical properties of these models. For location-scale models,
[
2
]
studied the
Z
-estimator defined via the Wasserstein score function (Wasserstein estimator). In addition,
[
16
]
showed that location-scale models form an
e
-geodesic in terms of the Wasserstein dual geometry proposed by
[
5
]
, and discussed the relation between these geodesics and Wasserstein–Cramer–Rao efficiency within the framework of
[
14
]
. This result is analogous to the Cramer–Rao efficiency of the maximum likelihood estimator in exponential families. In location-scale models, the Wasserstein estimator admits a closed-form expression, which facilitates various derivations; by contrast, no such closed form is available for location-scale-shape models. It is an interesting future work to study the statistical properties of location-scale-shape models and their connections with Wasserstein geometry.

We restricted our attention to univariate models in this study; extension to the multivariate setting is left for future work.
The Wasserstein geometry of the multivariate Gaussian model has been well elucidated
[
21
]
.
This result can be extended to location-scatter models, a multivariate extension of the location-scale model, and Wasserstein statistics of the location-scatter model has been studied recently
[
2
]
. For location-scale-shape models, however, even an appropriate multivariate extension is nontrivial. We may be able to propose a natural multivariate extension from the perspective of Wasserstein geometry.
