Frequency shift and viewing direction variations in gravitational lensing Preprint: APS/123-QED
Mikołaj Korzyński, Mateusz Kulejewski

Abstract
In a gravitational lensing system, the relative transverse velocities of the lens, source, and observer induce a frequency shift in the observed radiation. While this shift is typically negligible in most astrophysical contexts, strategies for its detection have been proposed for both electromagnetic and gravitational waves. This paper provides a rigorous theoretical treatment of the effect, deriving general expressions for the frequency shift within a lensing system embedded in a cosmological spacetime. Our formulation remains valid for arbitrary distances and velocities—including highly relativistic regimes—under any Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric. Expanding upon previous papers on moving lenses, we provide a detailed analysis of frequency effects induced by lenses moving at relativistic speeds. Furthermore, we extend standard lensing theory by deriving an exact formula for the variation in the source’s viewing direction. This result is of interest for strongly anisotropic emitters, such as compact binary systems emitting gravitational waves. Finally, we quantify the apparent misalignment between the lens and the source’s two images produced by time-delay effects in lens systems moving with high velocity.



I
Introduction

Gravitational lensing is caused by the deflection of a light ray by the gravitational field of a massive body. In a static lensing setup photon energy is conserved during the interaction with the gravitational field of the lens, which results in the radiation frequency remaining unchanged by the deflection. However, if the source, the observer or the lens are in relative motion, we may expect the lensing to induce a change in the wave frequency as well. Under the geometrical optics approximation, which is valid most of the time in astrophysical settings, the effect is identical for electromagnetic and gravitational radiation.

This frequency shift, or the spectroscopic effect of lensing, has recently attracted significant interest from the astrophysical community, particularly in the context of gravitational wave lensing. It turns out that analysing the interference effect of signals corresponding to two or more strongly lensed images offers a promising way to measure the transverse velocities of the source and the lens

Later, Wucknitz and Sperhake derived the deflection angle for a lens with arbitrary velocity

In most prior work, the frequency effect is computed using the first-order-in-velocity approximation (FOV) in the terminology of Wucknitz and Sperhake
44

22

In this paper, we aim to derive the most general formula for the frequency effect of gravitational lensing with arbitrary motion of the source, observer and lens. Our derivation is based on fairly general and physically motivated assumptions:

•

The entire lensing system is embedded in a Friedman-Lemaitre-Robinson-Walker metric describing the large-scale geometry of the Universe.

•

We assume the thin lens approximation and the small deviation angle approximation (first-order-in-deviation approximation, FOD). We make use of the standard definitions of the lensed and unlensed angular position of the source as well as the deflection angle.

•

We assume that the metric in the vicinity of the lens is static
in the lens rest frame
, and therefore, the frequency of the deflected radiation is strictly conserved
in the lens rest frame
.

Crucially, we do not assume the motions of the lens, source or observer to be slow with respect to the speed of light, nor do we make any restricting assumptions regarding the geometry of the lens or its lensing potential.

Another significant novel component of this work is the rigorous computation of the variation of the viewing direction of the source due to lensing. This variation is absent from the standard gravitational lensing formalism as described in the literature

However, it is crucial for the frequency variation effect. Moreover, it may also be important for highly anisotropic sources, such as binary systems emitting gravitational waves. For this type of GW sources, a change in the viewing direction corresponds directly to a variation in the phase and polarisation of the wave. The viewing direction formula we derive extends the standard lensing theory in an FLRW spacetime, predicting additional phase differences between multiple lensed images that may become observable by next-generation GW detectors. This general formula constitutes one of the key original results of this paper.

This paper significantly generalises previous findings regarding the frequency variations in gravitational lensing systems featuring moving lenses, sources and observers. In particular, our first aim is to extend the results of the paper by Wucknitz and Sperhake by lifting the FOV approximation from Section IV C of

Our work also extends the results of Kopeikin and Schäffer

The first one is the standard gravitational lensing theory, where the two-dimensional deflection angle is treated as an arbitrary function of the two-dimensional lensed position of the source. II
Geometric optics in FLRW spacetimes

II.1
Tetrads, geodesic deviation equation and bilocal geodesic operators

The propagation of light between the source and the lens and between the lens and the observer takes place in an FLRW metric. Since the size of the lens and the source are small with respect to the size of the Universe, we can approximate the behaviour of the light rays in this region by the first-order geodesic deviation equation (GDE). This way, we will be able to take advantage of its linearity and thus the linear dependence of the perturbed geodesic on the initial data.

The general solution of the GDE for null geodesics can be expressed easily in terms of the bilocal geodesic operators (BGO), also known in the literature as the transition matrix or Green’s function or Jacobi propagators. They connect the variation of a geodesic at a given point to its variation at all other points. We will now review shortly the basic results of the theory of the GDE and the BGOs we will use in this paper.

The GDE is an equation for a vector field
\displaystyle\xi^{\mu}
, defined along a fiducial null geodesic
\displaystyle\gamma_{0}
:

\nabla_{l}\nabla_{l}\xi^{\mu}-R^{\mu}{}_{\nu\alpha\beta}\,l^{\nu}\,l^{\alpha}\,\xi^{\beta}=0,

(II.1)

where
\displaystyle l^{\mu}
is the null tangent.
The perturbed geodesic defined by
\displaystyle\xi
is null if we assume additionally that
\displaystyle\nabla_{l}\xi^{\mu}\,l_{\mu}=0
. Moreover, for null geodesics emitted and received at the same moment, an even stronger condition holds
\displaystyle\xi\cdot l=0
, i.e.
\displaystyle\xi
is orthogonal to
\displaystyle l
at every point.

The GDE is easiest to solve if we express it in an appropriate tetrad. We consider tetrads for which the first vector
\displaystyle u^{\mu}
defines the observer, the next two are the transverse
\displaystyle e_{\bm{1}}^{\mu},e_{\bm{2}}^{\mu}

\displaystyle l^{\mu}
. The orthogonality relations read

\begin{split}u\cdot u&=-1,\\
u\cdot e_{\bm{A}}&=0,\\
l\cdot e_{\bm{A}}&=0,\\
e_{\bm{A}}\cdot e_{\bm{B}}&=\delta_{{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}},\end{split}

(II.2)

and
\displaystyle l\cdot l=0
by definition. We do not assume anything about the product
\displaystyle l\cdot u
except that it does not vanish. In this setup, any vector
\displaystyle\xi
orthogonal to
\displaystyle l
can be decomposed according to

\displaystyle\xi=\xi^{\bm{A}}\,e_{\bm{A}}+\xi^{l}\,l

\displaystyle(u^{\prime},f_{\bm{A}^{\prime}},l)
satisfying the orthogonality relations (
II.2

\displaystyle\xi^{\bm{A}^{\prime}}=R^{\bm{A}^{\prime}}{}_{\bm{B}^{\prime}}\,\xi^{\bm{B}^{\prime}}

in the source’s frame
:

\iota^{\bm{A}}=\frac{\Delta l^{\bm{A}}_{\mathcal{S}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{S}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{S}}}.

(II.23)

II.5
Lensing equation

We begin by re-deriving the main result of the standard gravitational lensing theory, the lensing equation connecting the lensed and unlensed angle, with arbitrary velocities of the observer, lens and source. Let
\displaystyle\xi^{\mu}(\lambda)
denote the solution of the geodesic deviation equation corresponding to the lensed ray. The ray undergoes a deflection at the lens plane, which in the thin lens approximation happens at a single point, leading to the discontinuity in the derivative
\displaystyle\nabla_{l}\xi^{\mu}
, but not in
\displaystyle\xi^{\mu}
.
The GDE is linear, so we can obtain the position of the deflected ray by simply adding the undeflected result
\displaystyle\xi_{u}
to a correction term
\displaystyle\xi_{c}
, i.e.

\xi(\lambda)=\xi_{u}(\lambda)+\xi_{c}(\lambda).

(II.24)

The undeflected solution is given in terms of the BGOs by

\xi_{u}(\lambda)=W_{XL}(\lambda,\lambda_{\mathcal{O}})\,\Delta l_{\mathcal{O}},

(II.25)

since at
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
the ray crosses the origin
\displaystyle\delta x_{\mathcal{O}}=0
.
The deflection vanishes between the observer and lens plane
\displaystyle\lambda<\lambda_{\mathcal{L}}
, while past the lens plane it does not vanish. It is easy to see that the correct expression is

\xi_{c}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}0&\text{for }\lambda<\lambda_{\mathcal{L}},\\
W_{XL}(\lambda,\lambda_{\mathcal{L}})\,\left(-\Delta l_{\text{defl}}\right)&\text{for }\lambda\geq\lambda_{\mathcal{L}}.\end{cases}

(II.26)

With this choice,
\displaystyle\xi
satisfies the geodesic deviation equation everywhere except the lens plane, it is unperturbed from the observed down to the lens, and it has the correct first derivative discontinuity at
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
.

We apply this formula to
\displaystyle\lambda=\lambda_{\mathcal{S}}
, obtaining

\delta x_{\mathcal{S}}=W_{XL}^{\mathcal{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{O}}{}\,\Delta l_{\mathcal{O}}+W_{XL}^{\mathcal{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{L}}\,\left(-\Delta l_{\textrm{defl}}\right),

(II.27)

where we have introduced the short-hand notation for the BGOs from the observation point
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
to the source
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

W_{XL}^{\mathcal{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{O}}:=W_{XL}(\lambda_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda_{\mathcal{O}}),

(II.28)

and the same for other pairs of points.
In a similar way, we can also derive the following relation for the derivative
\displaystyle\nabla_{l}\xi\big|_{\mathcal{S}}\equiv\Delta l_{\mathcal{S}}
at the emission point:

\Delta l_{\mathcal{S}}=W_{LL}^{\mathcal{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{O}}\,\Delta l_{\mathcal{O}}+W_{LL}^{\mathcal{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{L}}\,\left(-\Delta l_{\textrm{defl}}\right).

(II.29)

The lensing equation follows from the transverse components of (
II.27
). Recall that the transverse components of
\displaystyle W_{XL}
are related to the angular diameter distances according to (
II.11
):

\displaystyle\displaystyle W_{XL}^{\mathcal{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{O}}{}^{\bm{A}}{}_{\bm{B}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle(\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot U_{\mathcal{O}})^{-1}\,D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}\,\delta^{\bm{A}}{}_{\bm{B}},

(II.30)

\displaystyle\displaystyle W_{XL}^{\mathcal{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{L}}{}^{\bm{A}}{}_{\bm{B}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle(\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{L}}\cdot U_{\mathcal{L}})^{-1}\,D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}\,\delta^{\bm{A}}{}_{\bm{B}},

(II.31)

where
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}
,
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}
is the angular diameter distance
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
, respectively, given by (
II.14
). We also apply relations (
II.21
) and (
II.22
) to express
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\mathcal{O}}
and
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\text{defl}}
by the lensing angles
\displaystyle\theta
and
\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}
, obtaining

\delta x_{\mathcal{S}}^{\bm{A}}=\frac{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{O}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot U_{\mathcal{O}}}\,D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}\,\theta^{\bm{A}}-\frac{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{L}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{L}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{L}}\cdot U_{\mathcal{L}}}\,D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}\,\widehat{\alpha}^{\bm{A}},

(II.32)

This can be easily transformed into the proper lensing equation

\beta^{\bm{A}}=\theta^{\bm{A}}-\alpha^{\bm{A}},

(II.33)

with the unlensed angle
\displaystyle\beta^{\bm{A}}
defined in analogy to (
II.21
) as

\delta x_{\mathcal{S}}^{\bm{A}}={W_{XL}^{\mathcal{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{O}}}{}^{\bm{A}}{}_{\bm{B}}\,\left(\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{O}}\right)\,\beta^{\bm{B}},

(II.34)

or, equivalently,

\delta x_{\mathcal{S}}^{\bm{A}}=\frac{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{O}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot U_{\mathcal{O}}}\,D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}\,\beta^{\bm{A}},

(II.35)

and the rescaled deflection angle
\displaystyle\alpha^{\bm{A}}
defined by

\alpha^{\bm{A}}=\frac{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{L}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{L}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{L}}\cdot U_{\mathcal{L}}}\,\frac{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot U_{\mathcal{O}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{O}}}\,\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}\,\widehat{\alpha}^{\bm{A}}.

(II.36)

II.6
Frame-invariant approach to the frequency shift

We will now derive an important technical result: a simple and general formula for the variation of the redshift of an image due to the gravitational lensing, taking into account the effects of motions on the observer, lens and source, expressed in terms of the three angles

\displaystyle\theta^{\bm{A}}
,
\displaystyle\alpha^{\bm{A}}
and
\displaystyle\iota^{\bm{A}}
. The derivation is based on the assumption that in the vicinity of the lens, the metric is static to great accuracy, and thus we can assume that in the lens frame, the photon energy is conserved during the deflection. Note that we do not assume the Newtonian approximation to hold near the lens, unlike the standard derivations of the lensing theory. We only need the existence of a frame where the metric is static.

We have found it convenient in this context to make use of the logarithmic redshift
\displaystyle\zeta=\ln(1+z)
rather than the standard one (a similar approach was used in
33
). For a point source, it is given by the general formula

\zeta=\ln(l_{\mathcal{S}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{S}})-\ln(l_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{O}})

(II.37)

where
\displaystyle l_{\mathcal{S}}=\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{S}}+\Delta l_{\mathcal{S}}
,
\displaystyle\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{S}}
is the unperturbed tangent vector and
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\mathcal{S}}
is its variation due to the presence of the lens.
This can be rearranged to

\begin{split}\zeta=\ln\left(\frac{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{S}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{S}}}{\tilde{l}_{O}\cdot u_{\mathcal{O}}}\right)&+\ln\left(1+\frac{\Delta l_{\mathcal{S}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{S}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{S}}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{S}}\right)\\
&-\ln\left(1+\frac{\Delta l_{\mathcal{O}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{O}}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{O}}\right).\end{split}

(II.38)

The first term is the unlensed logarithmic redshift, which we will denote as
\displaystyle\zeta_{0}
. It is a constant for a given lens and source.
We then have

\begin{split}\zeta&=\zeta_{0}+\Delta\zeta,\\
\Delta\zeta&=\ln\left(1+\frac{\Delta l_{\mathcal{S}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{S}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{S}}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{S}}\right)\\
&-\ln\left(1+\frac{\Delta l_{\mathcal{O}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{O}}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{O}}\right).\end{split}

(II.39)

In this paper, we take the first order in deflection (FOD), borrowing the terminology from
44
, linearising this expression in
\displaystyle\Delta l
.
In the linear order, this amounts to

\Delta\zeta\equiv\frac{\Delta z}{1+z}=\frac{\Delta l_{\mathcal{S}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{S}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{S}}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{S}}-\frac{\Delta l_{\mathcal{O}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{O}}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{O}}.

(II.40)

For
\displaystyle\Delta\zeta\ll 1
the redshift variation is related to the frequency shift
\displaystyle\Delta f
of the wave via a simple formula
\displaystyle\frac{\Delta f}{f}=-\Delta\zeta
.

Both
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\mathcal{S}}
and
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\mathcal{O}}
are orthogonal to
\displaystyle\tilde{l}
in the FOD approximation. Thus, both can be decomposed into the transverse components and the line-of-sight component:

\Delta l_{\mathcal{O}}=\Delta l_{\mathcal{O}}^{\bm{A}}\,e_{\bm{A}}+\Delta l_{\mathcal{O}}^{l}\,\tilde{l},

(II.41)

and a similar decomposition for
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\mathcal{S}}

The transverse components are related to the angles
\displaystyle\theta
and
\displaystyle\iota
, respectively, see (
II.21
)-(
II.23
). In the next step, we want to evaluate the expression (
II.40
) in terms of the angles
\displaystyle\alpha
,
\displaystyle\theta
and
\displaystyle\iota
. For this purpose, we need to relate the line-of-sight components
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\cal S}^{l}
to
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\cal O}^{l}
to the lensing angles with the help of the energy conservation at the lens plane.

We will work in the parallel-transported adapted tetrad
\displaystyle\left(\widetilde{U_{\mathcal{L}}},e_{\bm{A}},\tilde{l}\right)
, where
\displaystyle U_{\mathcal{L}}\cdot e_{\bm{A}}=0
,
\displaystyle e_{\bm{A}}\cdot e_{\bm{B}}=\delta_{\bm{AB}}
,
\displaystyle\tilde{l}\cdot e_{\bm{A}}=0
. Here
\displaystyle\widetilde{U_{\mathcal{L}}}
denotes parallel transport of
\displaystyle U_{\mathcal{L}}
to all points along the fiducial null geodesic.

We note that at every point the tetrad
\displaystyle\left({U},e_{\bm{A}},\tilde{l}\right)
, obtained by replacing
\displaystyle\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{L}}
by the local cosmic frame
\displaystyle U^{\mu}
without affecting the rest, is also an adapted tetrad, i.e. we have
\displaystyle e_{\bm{A}}\cdot\widetilde{U_{\mathcal{L}}}=0
and other orthogonality relations (
II.2
) still hold. It follows that the decomposition of vectors orthogonal to
\displaystyle\tilde{l}
, i.e. such that
\displaystyle\xi^{\mu}\,\tilde{l}_{\mu}=0
, reads

\xi^{\mu}=\xi^{\bm{A}}\,e_{\bm{A}}^{\mu}+\xi^{l}\,\tilde{l}^{\mu},

(II.42)

with the components
\displaystyle\xi^{\bm{A}},\xi^{l}
are therefore identical in the parallel-transported tetrad
\displaystyle\big(\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{L}},e_{\bm{A}},\tilde{l}\,\big)
and the local adapted tetrad

\displaystyle\big({U},e_{\bm{A}},\tilde{l}\,\big)
. In other words, boost from
\displaystyle U
to
\displaystyle\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{L}}
, which is a boost along the line of sight, has no influence on the components of vectors orthogonal to
\displaystyle\tilde{l}
. We will make use of this observation when discussing
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\mathcal{O}}
and
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\mathcal{S}}
.

The energy conservation takes the form of
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\mathcal{L},\textrm{out}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{L}}=\Delta l_{\mathcal{L},\textrm{in}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{L}}
, or, equivalently

\Delta l_{\textrm{defl}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{L}}=0.

(II.43)

Obviously
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\textrm{defl}}\cdot\tilde{l}=0
, so it has the decomposition

\Delta l_{\textrm{defl}}=\widehat{\alpha}^{\bm{A}}\,(\tilde{l}\cdot u_{\cal L})\,e_{\bm{A}}+\Delta l_{\textrm{defl}}^{l}\,\tilde{l}.

(II.44)

From this formula and (
II.43
)
we get

\Delta l^{l}_{\textrm{defl}}=-\widehat{\alpha}^{\bm{A}}\,u^{\cal L}_{\bm{A}}.

(II.45)

We now move on to the
\displaystyle l
components of the vectors
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\mathcal{O}}
and
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\mathcal{S}}
. We take the component of (
II.29
) along
\displaystyle\tilde{l}
.

Since
\displaystyle{W_{LL}}^{l}{}_{l}=1
,
\displaystyle{W_{LL}}^{l}{}_{\bm{A}}=0
independently of the geometry
24
;
18
, it is equivalent to

\Delta l_{\cal S}^{l}=\Delta l_{\cal O}^{l}-\Delta l_{\textrm{defl}}^{l}.

(II.46)

Now, substituting (
II.45
) and (
II.46
) to (
II.40
), we get

\Delta\zeta=\frac{\Delta l_{\cal S}^{\bm{A}}}{\tilde{l}_{\cal S}\cdot u_{\cal S}}\,u^{\cal S}_{\bm{A}}+\widehat{\alpha}^{\bm{A}}\,u^{\cal L}_{\bm{A}}-\frac{\Delta l_{\cal O}^{\bm{A}}}{\tilde{l}_{\cal O}\cdot u_{\cal O}}\,u^{\cal O}_{\bm{A}}.

(II.47)

Finally, we make use of (
II.21
) and (
II.23
) to substitute
\displaystyle\theta^{\bm{A}}
and
\displaystyle\iota^{\bm{A}}
for the transverse components of
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\mathcal{O}}
and
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\mathcal{S}}
. In the end, we obtain a remarkably simple formula

\Delta\zeta=\iota^{\bm{A}}\,u^{\cal S}_{\bm{A}}+\widehat{\alpha}^{\bm{A}}\,u^{\cal L}_{\bm{A}}-\theta^{\bm{A}}\,u^{\cal O}_{\bm{A}}.

(II.48)

We have found it useful to express this formula in terms of “unphysical” angles, measured in the cosmological comoving frames at each of the three points rather than the appropriate rest frames, i.e.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iota_{U,\mathcal{S}}^{\bm{A}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\Delta l_{\cal S}^{\bm{A}}}{\tilde{l}_{\cal S}\cdot U_{\cal S}}=\frac{\tilde{l}_{\cal S}\cdot u_{\cal S}}{\tilde{l}_{\cal S}\cdot U_{\cal S}}\,\iota^{\bm{A}},

(II.49)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\theta_{U,\mathcal{O}}^{\bm{A}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\Delta l_{\cal O}^{\bm{A}}}{\tilde{l}_{\cal O}\cdot U_{\cal O}}=\frac{\tilde{l}_{\cal O}\cdot u_{\cal O}}{\tilde{l}_{\cal O}\cdot U_{\cal O}}\,\theta^{\bm{A}},

(II.50)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}^{\bm{A}}_{U,\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{l}_{\cal L}\cdot u_{\cal L}}{\tilde{l}_{\cal L}\cdot U_{\cal L}}\,\widehat{\alpha}^{\bm{A}}.

(II.51)

The angles are rescaled by Doppler prefactors with respect to those that have been measured at the observer, lens or source frame. The formula for
\displaystyle\Delta\zeta
takes the form of

\begin{split}\Delta\zeta=\iota_{U,\mathcal{S}}^{\bm{A}}\,u^{\cal S}_{\bm{A}}\,\frac{\tilde{l}_{\cal S}\cdot U_{\cal S}}{\tilde{l}_{\cal S}\cdot u_{\cal S}}&+\widehat{\alpha}^{\bm{A}}_{U,\mathcal{L}}\,u^{\cal L}_{\bm{A}}\,\frac{\tilde{l}_{\cal L}\cdot U_{\cal L}}{\tilde{l}_{\cal L}\cdot u_{\cal L}}\\
&-\theta^{\bm{A}}_{U,\mathcal{O}}\,u^{\cal O}_{\bm{A}}\,\frac{\tilde{l}_{\cal O}\cdot U_{\cal O}}{\tilde{l}_{\cal O}\cdot u_{\cal O}}.\end{split}

(II.52)

The advantage of this expression is that it can be easily recast into a manifestly frame-independent form, with the help of the transverse 4-velocity differences between the frames of the observer, lens and source and the appropriate cosmic frames. Namely, in the
\displaystyle\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{O}}
frame we have

\Delta\zeta=\iota_{U,\mathcal{S}}^{\bm{A}}\,\Delta u^{\mathcal{S}}_{\bm{A}}+\widehat{\alpha}_{U,\mathcal{L}}^{\bm{A}}\,\Delta u^{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{A}}-\theta_{U,\mathcal{O}}^{\bm{A}}\,\Delta u^{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{A}},

(II.53)

where each
\displaystyle\Delta u^{*}
is the transverse velocity difference defined by (
II.3
) between
\displaystyle u_{*}
and
\displaystyle U_{*}
, with
\displaystyle*
denoting
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
,
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
or
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
. This is due to the fact that in this frame
\displaystyle U_{\mathcal{S}}
and
\displaystyle U_{\mathcal{O}}
have vanishing transverse components.

Now, we note that the vectors
\displaystyle\Delta u_{*}
, just like
\displaystyle\Delta l_{O}
,
\displaystyle\Delta l_{\mathcal{S}}
and
\displaystyle\Delta_{\textrm{defl}}
are all orthogonal to
\displaystyle\tilde{l}
. It follows that each contraction of the transverse components in
(
II.53
) is simply equal to the full spacetime scalar product of
\displaystyle\Delta u_{*}
with appropriate
\displaystyle\Delta l_{*}
:

\Delta\zeta=\frac{\Delta l_{\mathcal{S}}\cdot\Delta u_{\mathcal{S}}}{\tilde{l}_{\cal S}\cdot U_{\cal S}}+\frac{\Delta l_{\textrm{defl}}^{\mathcal{L}}\cdot\Delta u_{\mathcal{L}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{L}}\cdot U_{\mathcal{L}}}-\frac{\Delta l_{\cal O}\cdot\Delta u_{\mathcal{O}}}{\tilde{l}_{\cal O}\cdot U_{\cal O}}.

(II.54)

This form of the formula for
\displaystyle\Delta\zeta
is manifestly covariant and can be applied in any tetrad or any coordinate-based frame.

III
Variation of the viewing direction

The formulas derived in the previous section express
\displaystyle\Delta\zeta
as a combination of terms involving all three angles
\displaystyle\theta^{\bm{A}}
,
\displaystyle\iota^{\bm{A}}
and
\displaystyle\alpha^{\bm{A}}
. However, these angles are not independent: in the lensing theory, each ray is described by the apparent position on the observer’s sky
\displaystyle\theta^{\bm{A}}
and the deflection angle
\displaystyle\alpha^{\bm{B}}
. Therefore, in this section, we will express the variation of the viewing direction angle
\displaystyle\iota^{\bm{A}}
via the pair
\displaystyle\theta^{\bm{A}}
and
\displaystyle\alpha^{\bm{A}}
in an FLRW Universe model. Later, we will trade
\displaystyle\alpha^{\bm{A}}
for the unlensed angle
\displaystyle\beta^{\bm{B}}
, since the pair
\displaystyle\left(\theta^{\bm{A}},\beta^{\bm{B}}\right)
appears more useful from the point of view of the lensing theory.

Just like in the previous section, our method is based on the geodesic deviation equation and the BGO formalism.
We combine the transverse components of (
II.29
) with the definitions of
\displaystyle\iota^{\bm{A}}
(
II.23
),

\displaystyle\theta^{\bm{A}}
(
II.21
) and
\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}^{\bm{A}}
(
II.22
), as well as the expression for
\displaystyle W_{LL}
(
II.18
), and get

\begin{split}\iota^{\bm{A}}&=\frac{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{O}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{S}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{S}}}\,\frac{a_{\mathcal{O}}}{a_{\mathcal{S}}}\,\Sigma(a_{\mathcal{S}},a_{\mathcal{O}})\,\theta^{\bm{A}}\\
&-\frac{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{L}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{L}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{S}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{S}}}\,\frac{a_{\mathcal{L}}}{a_{\mathcal{S}}}\,\Sigma(a_{\mathcal{S}},a_{\mathcal{L}})\,\widehat{\alpha}^{\bm{A}}.\end{split}

(III.1)

We then substitute
\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}^{\bm{A}}
by
\displaystyle\alpha^{\bm{A}}
using (
II.36
), making use of
\displaystyle\frac{a_{\mathcal{L}}}{a_{\mathcal{S}}}=\frac{\tilde{l}\cdot U_{\mathcal{S}}}{\tilde{l}\cdot U_{\mathcal{L}}}
to get

\frac{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{L}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{L}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{S}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{S}}}\,\frac{a_{\mathcal{L}}}{a_{\mathcal{S}}}\,\Sigma(a_{\mathcal{S}},a_{\mathcal{L}})\,\widehat{\alpha}^{\bm{A}}=\frac{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{S}}\cdot U_{\mathcal{S}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{S}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{S}}}\,\frac{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{O}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot U_{\mathcal{O}}}\,\Sigma(a_{\mathcal{S}},a_{\mathcal{L}})\,\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}\,\alpha^{\bm{A}}.

(III.2)

After we substitute this into (
III.1
), it is easy to see that the Doppler pre-factors in both terms on the right-hand side of (
III.1
) are identical. We obtain

\iota^{\bm{A}}=\nu\left(\Sigma(a_{\mathcal{S}},a_{\mathcal{O}})\,\theta^{\bm{A}}-\Sigma(a_{\mathcal{S}},a_{\mathcal{L}})\,\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}\,\alpha^{\bm{A}}\right),

(III.3)

with the Doppler prefactor
\displaystyle\nu

(III.4)

The prefactor
\displaystyle\nu
can be absorbed if we use rescaled angles, defined in appropriate cosmological frames, instead of the standard ones, according to (
II.49
)-(
II.51
).
With these defintions (
III.3
) can be rearranged to the following formula:

\iota^{\bm{A}}_{U,\mathcal{S}}=\Sigma(a_{\mathcal{S}},a_{\mathcal{O}})\,\theta^{\bm{A}}_{U,\mathcal{O}}-\Sigma(a_{\mathcal{S}},a_{\mathcal{L}})\,\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}\,\alpha_{U,\mathcal{O}}^{\bm{A}},

(III.5)

where we have introduced the rescaled angle
\displaystyle\alpha
in the cosmological frame at
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha_{U,\mathcal{O}}^{\bm{A}}=\frac{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{O}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot U_{\mathcal{O}}}\,\alpha^{\bm{A}}.

(III.6)

The formula (
III.5
) becomes even more useful if we express it in terms of the angles
\displaystyle\theta_{U,\mathcal{O}}
and the unlensed angle
\displaystyle\beta_{U,O}
, defining the position of the source. First, we define by analogy the unlensed angle in the cosmological frame
\displaystyle U_{\mathcal{O}}

\beta_{U,\mathcal{O}}^{\bm{A}}=\frac{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot u_{\mathcal{O}}}{\tilde{l}_{\mathcal{O}}\cdot U_{\mathcal{O}}}\,\beta^{\bm{A}}.

(III.7)

We note that in this setting, the lensing formula (
II.33
) is equivalent to

\beta_{U,\mathcal{O}}=\theta_{U,\mathcal{O}}-\alpha_{U,\mathcal{O}}.

(III.8)

After substituting this version of the lensing formula to (

) , we get

\begin{split}\iota^{\bm{A}}_{U,\mathcal{S}}&=\left(\Sigma(a_{\mathcal{S}},a_{\mathcal{O}})-\Sigma(a_{\mathcal{S}},a_{\mathcal{L}})\,\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}\right)\,\theta^{\bm{A}}_{U,\mathcal{O}}\\

It follows that

and

(III.12)

with
\displaystyle\nu
given by (
III.4
).

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\beta

(IV.1)

Substituting now the lensing equation (

(IV.2)

This formula, in turn, can be directly substituted into (

IV.1
Estimate of the redshift difference between two images

We will now estimate the frequency effect of strong lensing in the case of a point source undergoing multiple imaging. In contrast to
31

Its counterpart in the gravitational wave astronomy is the effect of frequency variation for two or more images. For persistent sources, this manifests as signal interference producing time-dependent modulation of the combined signal at the detector. For transient signals, such as binary inspirals, the effect may be observed as a subtle time domain rescaling (stretching or squeezing) of the successive signals arriving at the detector

;

Assume a point source at
\displaystyle\beta^{\bm{A}}

(IV.6)

IV.2
Point mass as a high velocity lens

As an example, we will now consider a point mass
\displaystyle M
with an arbitrary, potentially very high velocity as the lens.
This case has already been discussed in

\displaystyle v_{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle c=1

\displaystyle 0.7\,c

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathcal{L}}

The distance dependence of the frequency variation is fairly simple: the spectroscopic effect for a moving lens and a stationary source and observer is large if either the lens is close to the source or to the observer. V.1

A particular, but important case of an FLRW spacetime is the flat metric. It is appropriate as a physical model for the analysis of microlensing with the source and the lens in the immediate vicinity of the Galaxy. In this setting,

&+\beta^{\bm{A}}_{U}\,\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}\left(\Delta u_{\mathcal{S}\,\bm{A}}-\Delta u_{\mathcal{L}\,\bm{A}}\right).\end{split}

(V.2)

In the Minkowski spacetime, we have the freedom to pick the reference frame
\displaystyle U^{\mu}
. An obvious choice is to align it with the frame of the observer
\displaystyle u_{\mathcal{O}}
or the lens
\displaystyle u_{\mathcal{L}}
, which further simplifies both expressions.

In the observer frame (
\displaystyle U^{\mu}=u_{\mathcal{O}}^{\mu}
) we get

\iota_{\mathcal{O}}^{\bm{A}}=-\frac{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}\,\theta^{\bm{A}}+\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}\,\beta^{\bm{A}},

(V.3)

i. e. we may use the observer’s measured values of
\displaystyle\theta
and
\displaystyle\beta
, and
\displaystyle\iota_{\mathcal{O}}\equiv\iota_{U,\mathcal{S}}
is simply the viewing angle variation in the observer’s frame. We also get

\begin{split}\Delta\zeta&=\theta^{\bm{A}}\,\left(\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}\,\Delta u_{\mathcal{L}\,\bm{A}}-\frac{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}\,\Delta u_{\mathcal{S}\,\bm{A}}\right)\\
&+\beta^{\bm{A}}\,\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}\left(\Delta u_{\mathcal{S}\,\bm{A}}-\Delta u_{\mathcal{L}\,\bm{A}}\right),\end{split}

(V.4)

with
\displaystyle\Delta u_{\mathcal{L}}
and
\displaystyle\Delta u_{\mathcal{S}}
being the transverse velocity difference between the observer and the lens and the observer and the source ,respectively, see (
II.3
).

On the other hand, in the lens frame (
\displaystyle U^{\mu}=u_{\mathcal{L}}^{\mu}
), we have

\begin{split}\Delta\zeta&=-\theta^{\bm{A}}_{\mathcal{L}}\,\left(\Delta u_{\mathcal{O}\,\bm{A}}+\frac{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}\,\Delta u_{\mathcal{S}\,\bm{A}}\right)\\
&+\beta^{\bm{A}}_{\mathcal{L}}\,\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}\,\Delta u_{\mathcal{S}\,\bm{A}},\end{split}

(V.5)

with the transverse velocity differences, this time calculated with respect to the lens frame.

Let us now discuss the redshift difference between two images, just like in
IV.2
. In the observer’s frame we get

\Delta\zeta_{ij}=\Delta\theta_{ij}^{\bm{A}}\,\left(\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{S}}}u_{\mathcal{L}\,\bm{A}}-\frac{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{S}}}\,u_{\mathcal{S}\,\bm{A}}\right).

(V.6)

We can now go back to the model of a point lens with a known Einstein radius from Section
IV.2
, assuming
\displaystyle\Delta\theta_{12}^{\bm{A}}=2\theta_{E}\,n_{\bm{A}}
, where
\displaystyle n_{\bm{A}}
is the unit vector indicating the direction of the line connecting the two images. Applying the formula for the Einstein-Chwolson angle (
IV.15
) and assuming the FOV approximation we obtain (where we reintroduce the speed of light
\displaystyle c
)

\begin{split}c\,\Delta\zeta_{12}&=4\sqrt{\frac{GM}{c^{2}}\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}{{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}\,D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}}}}\,\times\\
&\times\left(\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}v_{\mathcal{L}\,\bm{A}}\,n^{\bm{A}}-\frac{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}\,v_{\mathcal{S}\,\bm{A}}\,n^{\bm{A}}\right),\end{split}

(V.7)

and the corresponding characteristic redshift variation as

\begin{split}c\,\Delta\zeta_{\text{char}}=\,&2\,v_{\text{eff}}\,\sqrt{\frac{4GM}{c^{2}}\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}}}\\
=\,&830\frac{\text{cm}}{\text{s}}\,\left(\frac{(D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}})/(D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}})}{1\text{ kpc}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{M}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{v_{\text{eff}}}{c}\\
=\,&0.28\frac{\text{cm}}{\text{s}}\,\left(\frac{(D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}})/(D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}})}{1\text{ kpc}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{M}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{v_{\text{eff}}}{100\text{ km/s}},\end{split}

(V.8)

where in the observer’s frame
\displaystyle v_{\text{eff}}^{\bm{A}}=\left(\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}u_{\mathcal{L}}^{\bm{A}}-\frac{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}u_{\mathcal{S}}^{\bm{A}}\right)
. The frequency variation is very small for velocities, masses and distances typical of microlensing events in the Galactic neighbourhood. Moreover, we can discuss three regimes when the formula (
V.8
) simplifies: lens very close the the source (
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}\ll D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}
,
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}\simeq D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}
), lens very close to the observer (
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}\ll D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}
,
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}\simeq D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}
) and the lens midway between the source and the observer (
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}\simeq D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}
,
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}\simeq 2D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}\simeq 2D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}
).

In the first case (
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}\ll D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}
), we get

\begin{split}c\,\Delta\zeta_{\textrm{char}}&=4\sqrt{\frac{GM}{c^{2}D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}}\,\left\|v_{\mathcal{L}}^{\textrm{tr}}-v_{\mathcal{S}}^{\textrm{tr}}\right\|\\
&=830\frac{\text{cm}}{\text{s}}\,\left(\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}{1\text{ kpc}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{M}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\left\|v_{\mathcal{L}}^{\textrm{tr}}-v_{\mathcal{S}}^{\textrm{tr}}\right\|}{c}.\end{split}

(V.9)

Thus, the relevant velocity is the relative velocity between the lens and the observer. In the second case (
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}\ll D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}
), the first term in the parentheses dominates over the second one, i.e.

\begin{split}c\,\Delta\zeta_{\textrm{char}}&=4\sqrt{\frac{GM}{c^{2}D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}}}\,v_{\mathcal{L}}^{\textrm{tr}}\\
&=830\frac{\text{cm}}{\text{s}}\,\left(\frac{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}}{1\text{ kpc}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{M}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{v_{\mathcal{L}}^{\textrm{tr}}}{c}.\end{split}

(V.10)

In the third case (
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}\simeq D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}
), we get

\begin{split}c\,\Delta\zeta_{\textrm{char}}&=4\sqrt{\frac{GM}{c^{2}D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}}\,\left\|2v_{\mathcal{L}}^{\textrm{tr}}-v_{\mathcal{S}}^{\textrm{tr}}\right\|\\
&=830\frac{\text{cm}}{\text{s}}\,\left(\frac{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{1\text{ kpc}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{M}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\left\|2v_{\mathcal{L}}^{\textrm{tr}}-v_{\mathcal{S}}^{\textrm{tr}}\right\|}{c}.\end{split}

(V.11)

We can see that in both cases of lens close either to the source, or to the observer, the distance prefactors cancel to leave the smallest distance in the final denominator: either
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}
or
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}
. This results in the amplification of the effect for such physical events as a microlensing by a black hole passing near the Solar System, or lensing by a companion in a binary system (for example, binary black hole system orbiting a massive black hole), situation also known as self-lensing
40
;
30
.

V.2
Breaking of axial symmetry for axisymmetric lens

We would like to point out one more effect happening for very fast lenses. Namely, due to significant time delay differences between the images, the perfect alignment between the two images and the lens is broken. In the stationary case with axisymmetric lensing potential (i.e. an axially or spherically symmetric lens, most notably microlensing by non-rotating stars and black holes), the images of a point source lie on a single line with both the source’s unlensed position and the lens. This is no longer the case when the source is in motion behind the lens. As the emission times of different images are, in general, different, the corresponding source positions are different as well. We will see that this results in the breaking of the perfect linear alignment as shown in Figure
7
. It is important to stress, however, that this setup distinguishes between the motion of the source and the observer in the lens’ frame: the motion of the observer incurs no additional time delay and thus does not contribute to breaking the axial symmetry of the images.

Figure 7:
Position of different objects of a lensing system with an axisymmetric lens in the observer’s sky. The yellow dot indicates the unlensed position of the source, the blue dot indicates the centre of the lens, while red and green dots indicate the positions of the two images. Left: without any motion, the resulting images are collinear with the centre of the lens. Right: When the source is moving behind the lens, the resulting images are no longer collinear with the centre of the lens. We define the angle
\displaystyle\Delta\phi
between the lines crossing the lens and each of the images.

For concreteness, let us consider a point mass lens and a point source in a flat spacetime, with the lensing equation (
IV.16
). We aim to calculate the time delay between two images
\displaystyle\theta_{1,2}
of the same source, with the lens being stationary with respect to the observer, while the source is moving with a non-relativistic velocity
\displaystyle v_{\mathcal{S},\textrm{tr}}^{\bm{B}}
. The positions of the images are the solutions to equation (
IV.16
) and read

\theta_{1,2}^{\bm{A}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\beta\pm\sqrt{\beta^{2}+4{\theta_{E}}^{2}}\right)\,n^{\bm{A}}.

(V.12)

However, in writing so we have assumed that the position of the source of both images was the same, which was not actually the case. It becomes explicit once we calculate the time of arrival of both rays. The total time delay consists of the sum of the geometrical time delay according to the path traversed by the ray and the Shapiro delay due to the passing through a potential well and reads
3

\Delta T=\frac{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\theta^{\bm{A}}-\beta^{\bm{A}}\right)^{2}-\Psi(\theta^{\bm{A}})\right),

(V.13)

where
\displaystyle\Psi
is the lensing potential of the lens.

Figure 8:
The lensing setup in a flat spacetime: the lens
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
and observer
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
at rest, with the light source
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
in transverse motion. The time runs in the upward direction, and one spatial dimension has been suppressed. On the source’s worldline, we have marked two different times of emission
\displaystyle t^{\mathcal{E}}_{1,2}
of light corresponding to the two images. Both of the light rays arrive at the observer at the same moment
\displaystyle t^{\mathcal{O}}
.

The difference in times of emission of both light rays can be summarised in the implicit equation for the time of observation. Using the notation of Figure (
8
):

\begin{split}t^{\mathcal{O}}&=t^{\mathcal{E}}_{1}+T(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t^{\mathcal{E}}_{1}))+\Delta T_{1}(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t^{\mathcal{E}}_{1}))\\
&=t^{\mathcal{E}}_{2}+T(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t^{\mathcal{E}}_{2}))+\Delta T_{2}(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t^{\mathcal{E}}_{2})),\end{split}

(V.14)

where
\displaystyle t^{\mathcal{O}}
is the time of observation,
\displaystyle t^{\mathcal{E}}_{1,2}
are the times of emission of light coming through branches
\displaystyle 1
and
\displaystyle 2
, respectively,
\displaystyle\beta^{\bm{A}}(t)
is the position of the source at time
\displaystyle t
,
\displaystyle T(\beta^{\bm{A}})
is the unlensed arrival time from the source at
\displaystyle\beta^{\bm{A}}
and
\displaystyle\Delta T_{1,2}(\beta^{\bm{A}})
is the time delay of the light coming through branches
\displaystyle 1
and
\displaystyle 2
, respectively, from the source at
\displaystyle\beta^{\bm{A}}
.

From this, we can calculate the difference in time of emissions, that is, the difference
\displaystyle\Delta t\equiv t^{\mathcal{E}}_{2}-t^{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
:

\begin{split}\Delta t\equiv t^{\mathcal{E}}_{2}-t^{\mathcal{E}}_{1}&=T(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}))+\Delta T_{1}(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}))-T(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{2}^{\mathcal{E}}))-\Delta T_{2}(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{2}^{\mathcal{E}}))\\
&=T(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}))+\Delta T_{1}(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}))-T(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}+\Delta t))-\Delta T_{2}(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}+\Delta t)),\end{split}

(V.15)

where the second-line expansion shows the presence of
\displaystyle\Delta t
-term in the RHS explicitly. We can solve this equation by expanding the
\displaystyle T
and
\displaystyle\Delta T
functions in Taylor series around
\displaystyle t^{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
:

\begin{split}T(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t^{\mathcal{E}}_{1}+\Delta t))&\simeq T\left(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t^{\mathcal{E}}_{1})+\frac{v_{\mathcal{S},\text{tr}}^{\bm{A}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}\Delta t\right)\\
&\simeq T(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t^{\mathcal{E}}_{1}))+\nabla_{\bm{B}}T(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t^{\mathcal{E}}_{1}))\frac{v_{\mathcal{S},\text{tr}}^{\bm{B}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}\Delta t,\end{split}

(V.16)

and accordingly for the
\displaystyle\Delta T_{2}
function. We have assumed here that the unlensed position
\displaystyle\beta^{\bm{A}}
varies according to
\displaystyle\beta^{\bm{A}}(t)=\beta_{0}^{\bm{A}}+\frac{v_{\mathcal{S},\textrm{tr}}^{\bm{A}}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}\,t
, with
\displaystyle\beta_{0}^{\bm{A}}
being a constant vector. The resulting difference in time of emissions reads

\begin{split}\Delta t&=\Delta T_{1}(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}))+\Big(\nabla_{\bm{B}}(\Delta T_{2})(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}))+\nabla_{\bm{B}}(T)(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}))\Big)\cdot\frac{v^{\bm{B}}_{\mathcal{S},\text{tr}}}{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}}\Delta t-\Delta T_{2}(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}})).\end{split}

(V.17)

Solving this equation for
\displaystyle\Delta t
yields

\begin{split}\Delta t&=\frac{\Delta T_{1}(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}))-\Delta T_{2}(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}))}{1-\left(\nabla_{\bm{B}}(\Delta T_{2})(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}))-\nabla_{\bm{B}}(T)(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}))\right)\cdot\frac{v^{\bm{B}}_{\mathcal{S},\text{tr}}}{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}}}\\
&\simeq\left(\Delta T_{1}(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}))-\Delta T_{2}(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}))\right)\left(1+\left(\nabla_{\bm{B}}(\Delta T_{2})(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}))+\nabla_{\bm{B}}(T)(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}))\right)\cdot\frac{v^{\bm{B}}_{\mathcal{S},\text{tr}}}{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}}\right).\end{split}

(V.18)

This complicated expression gives the difference in time of emissions between two branches, taking into account both the different time delays of different branches, as well as the change of the unlensed arrival time with the motion of the source. We could, however, notice that the main contribution to the expression comes from the difference between
\displaystyle\Delta T_{1}
and
\displaystyle\Delta T_{2}
functions - i.e. that light arrives via different branches of rays - as the second term in the parenthesis involves the transverse velocity
\displaystyle v_{\mathcal{S},\textrm{tr}}^{\bm{B}}
and is thus subdominant. We can thus approximate the difference in time of emissions by

\Delta t\simeq\Delta T_{1}(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}})-\Delta T_{2}(\beta^{\bm{A}}(t_{1}^{\mathcal{E}})).

(V.19)

The angle of asymmetry is related to the difference in time of emissions by the effective angular velocity of the two images revolving around the lens on the observer’s sky. It is straightforward that for a source moving with a constant velocity behind a lens with an impact parameter
\displaystyle b
, the angular velocity at the moment of closest approach to the lens is

\omega=\frac{v_{\mathcal{S},\text{tr}}}{b\,D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}.

(V.20)

Accordingly, we can calculate the angle of asymmetry in the image by

\Delta\phi_{\text{max}}=\frac{v_{\mathcal{S}\text{,tr}}}{b\,D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}\Delta t.

(V.21)

We can estimate the order of the time delay by setting
\displaystyle\beta=b
in Equations (
V.12
) and (
V.13
), calculating the time delay as if the source was positioned at the closest point to the lens.
For a full derivation, we refer to Appendix
C
.
It yields the maximal asymmetry angle as (where we again reintroduce the speed of light
\displaystyle c
and assume that the impact parameter is small
\displaystyle b/\theta_{E}\ll 1
and thus
\displaystyle\theta_{1}\approx\theta_{2}
)

\Delta\phi_{\text{max}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{v}{c}\frac{D_{\mathcal{O}\mathcal{L}}}{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{S}}}\sqrt{b^{2}+4\theta_{E}^{\,\,2}}.

(V.22)

We can see that this effect is strongly suppressed by the speed of light
\displaystyle c
and for typical astrophysical values
\displaystyle v\approx 100\text{ km/s}
,
\displaystyle b\approx\theta_{E}\approx\text{mas}
, the angle will be of the order of at most microarcseconds, far below the current observational resolution for single images.

VI
Summary and outlook

In this paper, we have presented a detailed derivation of the frequency effect of strong lensing in the most general situation, assuming a single lens in the thin lens approximation and any states of motion for the observer, lens and source. We have shown that the full spectroscopic effect can be expressed as a sum of two terms depending on different angles and discussed their dependence on the velocities of source.

In the electromagnetic domain, the frequency variation results in simultaneous splitting or broadening of spectral lines. In the gravitational wave domain, the result is the emergence of an interference pattern in the signal, both governed by the relative redshift difference
\displaystyle\Delta\zeta
.

Summarising the findings of Sections
IV
and
V
, we note that the general frequency effect of lensing scales like

\Delta\zeta\approx 4\sqrt{\frac{GM}{c^{2}\,D_{\textrm{eff}}}}\,\frac{v_{\textrm{eff}}}{c},

(VI.1)

with
\displaystyle M
being the mass of the lens,
\displaystyle v_{\textrm{eff}}
being the appropriately defined velocity scale, given by a combination of the velocities of the observer, lens and source, and
\displaystyle D_{\textrm{eff}}
being the relevant distance scale, and we have explicitly re-introduced the speed of light
\displaystyle c
. In practice,
\displaystyle D_{\textrm{eff}}
is the smaller of the two distances: the one between the lens and the source and between the lens and the observer.

It follows that the frequency effect of lensing is small in most astrophysical situations, since it is expressible as a product of two small numbers: the transverse velocity expressed in units of the speed of light and the square root of the ratio of the lens’s Schwarzschild radius to the distance scale. In fact, from (
IV.4
) it is obvious that the it can be large only in certain circumstances: if the distance between the images
\displaystyle\theta
is large, i. e. the lens is fairly close to the observer, if it is very massive, or if the distance between the lens and the source
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}}
is very small (small denominators). The latter situation is possible if the source is gravitationally bound to the lens in a binary system (self-lensing). As an example, consider a source orbiting a massive black hole on a fairly low orbit. The velocity can be fairly large in this case, i.e., close to
\displaystyle c
, and for a low orbit, the square root is likely to be large as well (
\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{S}}
of the order of up to a few Schwarzschild radii), and
\displaystyle\Delta\zeta
can reach values of 1. If the source happens to pass periodically behind the BH on an orbit of small orbital inclination, we would have a chance to measure a pronounced line doubling effect due to the lensing on the central mass. Recently, this scenario has been discussed in the context of gravitational waves emitted by compact coalescing binaries in
40
.

Appendix C
Derivation of the maximal asymmetry angle

We calculate the difference in time of emissions according to (
V.19
), with the time delay given by (
V.13
). The position of the source is given by

\beta^{\bm{A}}=b^{\bm{A}}+\frac{v_{\mathcal{S}}^{\bm{A}}\,t}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}},

(C.1)

and we choose
\displaystyle b^{\bm{A}}
such that it is orthogonal to
\displaystyle v^{\bm{A}}_{\mathcal{S}}
(i.e.
\displaystyle b
is the impact parameter). Then the difference in time of emissions evaluates to

\begin{split}\Delta t=\frac{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{S}}}\Big(&\frac{1}{2}\big(\theta_{1}-\beta\big)^{2}-\Psi(\theta_{1})\\
-&\frac{1}{2}\big(\theta_{2}-\beta\big)^{2}+\Psi(\theta_{2})\Big),\end{split}

(C.2)

where we suppress the vector indices as the lens is axisymmetric. This can be rearranged to

\begin{split}\Delta t=\frac{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{S}}}\Big(&\frac{1}{2}\big(\theta_{1}^{2}-\theta_{2}^{2}-2\beta(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})\big)\\
&-(\Psi(\theta_{1})-\Psi(\theta_{2})\Big).\end{split}

(C.3)

Putting in
\displaystyle\beta=\sqrt{b^{2}+({v_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}\,t^{2}})/{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}}
,
\displaystyle\Psi(\theta)=\theta_{E}^{\,\,2}\log(\theta/\theta_{E})
(point-mass lens) and
\displaystyle\theta
according to (
V.12
), we obtain

\begin{split}\Delta t=\frac{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{S}}}\Big[&\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{4}\big(\sqrt{b^{2}+\frac{v_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}\,t^{2}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}^{2}}}+\sqrt{b^{2}+\frac{v_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}\,t^{2}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}^{2}}+4\theta_{E}^{\,\,2}}\,\big)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\big(\sqrt{b^{2}+\frac{v_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}\,t^{2}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}^{2}}}-\sqrt{b^{2}+\frac{v_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}\,t^{2}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}^{2}}+4\theta_{E}^{\,\,2}}\,\big)^{2}\\
&-2\sqrt{b^{2}+\frac{v_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}\,t^{2}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}^{2}}}\sqrt{b^{2}+\frac{v_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}\,t^{2}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}^{2}}+4\theta_{E}^{\,\,2}}\Big)-\theta_{E}^{\,\,2}(\log\frac{\theta_{1}}{\theta_{E}}-\log\frac{\theta_{1}}{\theta_{E}}).\end{split}

(C.4)

This expression looks complicated, but the expansion of the difference of squares into the product of the sum and difference of the first and the second term cancels with the third term, yielding

\begin{split}\Delta t=\frac{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{S}}}\Big[&-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{b^{2}+\frac{v_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}\,t^{2}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}^{2}}}\sqrt{b^{2}+\frac{v_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}\,t^{2}}{D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}^{2}}+4\theta_{E}^{\,\,2}}\\
&-\theta_{E}^{\,\,2}\log{\frac{\theta_{1}}{\theta_{2}}}\Big].\end{split}

(C.5)

This expression gives the difference in time of emissions for any position of the source. The asymmetry angle we evaluate at
\displaystyle t=0
when the source is closest to the lens. The difference in time of emissions is then

\Delta t=\frac{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}}D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}}}{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{S}}}\Big(-\frac{b}{2}\sqrt{b^{2}+4\theta_{E}^{\,\,2}}-\log\frac{\theta_{1}}{\theta_{2}}\Big).

(C.6)

Multiplying it by the effective angular velocity
\displaystyle\omega=v_{\mathcal{S},\text{tr}}/(b\,D_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{O}})

\displaystyle b/\theta_{E}\ll 1
,
\displaystyle\theta_{1}\approx\theta_{2}
) and disregarding the overall sign (we are interested only in the magnitude of the angle), we obtain the final result:

\Delta\phi_{\text{max}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{v}{c}\frac{D_{\mathcal{O}\mathcal{L}}}{D_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{S}}}\sqrt{b^{2}+4\theta_{E}^{\,\,2}}.

(C.7)
