Hypertension care performance in Indonesia: evidence from three waves of nationally representative cross-sectional surveys
Authors: Farizal Rizky Muharram, Indah S Widyahening, Goodarz Danaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo examine national trends and determinants of hypertension diagnosis, treatment and control in Indonesia, and to identify factors influencing the performance of hypertension care across three waves of national health surveys.DesignRepeated cross-sectional analysis of three nationally representative health surveys (2013, 2018 and 2023).SettingHousehold-based, population-level surveys conducted across all provinces of Indonesia, representing primary healthcare settings.ParticipantsAdults aged ≥18 years included in the 2013, 2018 and 2023 Indonesian National Health Research surveys (Riset Kesehatan Dasar and Survei Kesehatan Indonesia). Participants with complete blood pressure measurements and information on diagnosis and treatment were included; those with missing data were excluded. The weighted sample sizes were representative of Indonesia’s adult population by sex, age group and urban–rural residence.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcomes were hypertension prevalence, diagnosis, treatment and control rates. Secondary analyses assessed sociodemographic, economic and health system factors associated with each stage of the hypertension care cascade using multivariate logistic regression. All estimates were adjusted for survey design and population weights.ResultsHypertension crude prevalence increased from 27.9% (95% CI 27.7% to 28.2%) in 2013 to 31.6% (95% CI 31.4% to 31.8%) in 2023. Diagnosis rates declined from 33.0% in 2013 to 24.1% in 2018, then slightly rose to 26.9% in 2023. Treatment rates doubled from 10.4% to 22.4% over the decade, corresponding to an estimated 10 million additional adults receiving antihypertensive therapy. However, control rates improved only modestly, from 2.3% to 4.2%, leaving over 95% of hypertensive adults with uncontrolled blood pressure. Women, urban residents and individuals in higher wealth quintiles had consistently better outcomes across all stages of care.ConclusionsIndonesia faces a growing hypertension burden, with most cases being undiagnosed. Although treatment coverage has doubled over the past decade, control rates have remained stagnant, and disparities between wealth groups persist. Strengthening long-term management, follow-up and equitable care is essential to improve outcomes.Keywords: Hypertension, Health Equity, Health Services Accessibility

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and hypertension is the most important modifiable CVD risk factor globally and in most low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs).1,3 The economic burden of CVD and hypertension is immense, straining healthcare systems and imposing significant costs on households, particularly in LMICs where healthcare resources are limited. Indonesia has one of the lowest rates of hypertension control globally. A recent global pooling analysis by NCDRisC in 2021 estimated that only 5.1% of females and 3.6% of males in Indonesia will achieve effective blood pressure management.4 5 Older studies using the Indonesia Family Life Survey (IFLS) have also highlighted the poor performance of the hypertension care cascade in Indonesia. IFLS 2007 data showed disparities in hypertension care, with male sex, lower education and lower healthcare utilisation associated with significantly higher odds of uncontrolled hypertension.6 7 Other previous studies analysing IFLS 2015 data revealed an increase in prevalence compared with 2007, with higher odds of hypertension among participants who were older, less educated and overweight.6 7

Recent developments, including the implementation of the National Health Insurance (NHI) scheme in 2014 and the COVID-19 pandemic, may have affected hypertension management in Indonesia. However, evidence on the potential effect of these developments on the hypertension care cascade is scarce. Furthermore, it is unclear which socioeconomic subgroups have been most affected by these developments.8 9 Such evidence is crucial for informing health policy and planning in Indonesia. To bridge this gap, we used data from three rounds of the national health survey and analysed trends in the prevalence of hypertension and its care cascade descriptively, both nationally and across age, sex and urban/rural subgroups. We aimed to examine national trends in hypertension prevalence, diagnosis, treatment and control in Indonesia from 2013 to 2023, and to identify sociodemographic and economic factors associated with performance along the hypertension care cascade, as well as how these trends differ across sociodemographic and economic groups.

Methods

We used data from three waves of Indonesia Health Survey (Riset Kesehatan Dasar or RISKESDAS 2013, 2018 and Survei Kesehatan Indonesia or SKI 2023) to determine the population living with hypertension and performance along the hypertension care cascade. RISKESDAS is a nationally representative health survey conducted by the Ministry of Health, Health Research and Development Agency (now Health Development Policy Agency), every 5 years. Participants were selected using a multistage random sampling method representative of the Indonesian population at the district level (details of survey sampling provided in online supplemental 1). We included all survey respondents over 18 years old with non-missing blood pressure measurements. Pregnant individuals were excluded. Missing data on wealth quintile, education and occupation data were categorised as ‘missing’.

Trained survey personnel measured participants’ systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP, DBP) twice (see details of blood pressure measurement methods in online supplemental 1). If there was a more than 10 mm Hg difference in SBP between the first and second measurements, the third measurement was performed. We used average blood pressure readings across the three measurements to define hypertension. If one measurement was missing, we used the average of the two complete blood pressure readings. If two measurements were missing, we used the remaining blood pressure reading as the basis for our calculation. The proportion of participants with at least two measurements was 95% in 2013, 96.5% in 2018 and 98% in 2023 (online supplemental table 1).

We defined hypertension according to WHO guidelines as having an SBP equal to or greater than 140 mm Hg or a DBP equal to or greater than 90 mm Hg or reporting ever being diagnosed with hypertension or taking antihypertensive medications.8 We further categorised hypertension into subtypes: isolated systolic hypertension (ISH), defined as SBP ≥140 mm Hg and DBP <90 mm Hg; isolated diastolic hypertension (IDH), defined as SBP <140 mm Hg and DBP ≥90 mm Hg; and combined systolic-diastolic hypertension, characterised by both SBP ≥140 mm Hg and DBP ≥90 mm Hg. For each cascade step, we determined the number of people with hypertension who were ‘diagnosed’, ‘treated’ and ‘controlled’ and divided each by the total population of people with hypertension to calculate the proportion of people with hypertension in every cascade step and every survey year. Being ‘controlled’ was defined as using antihypertensive medication and having an average SBP below 140 mm Hg and a DBP less than 90 mm Hg (online supplemental table 2).

The probability of being in each stage of the hypertension care cascade was analysed based on key covariates selected due to their established relevance to hypertension care. These included age (10-year categories), sex (male and female), domicile (urban and rural), wealth quintiles constructed within each survey year from household ownership (2013 and 2023) and household income (2018) (quintiles, first quintile as the poorest and fifth as the wealthiest) and health insurance enrolment status (‘have insurance’ and ‘no insurance’). Additional covariates, including education status (primary, secondary, tertiary, no formal education) and occupation (unemployed, student, formal sector, private sector, informal sector, others), were also analysed, and results were reported in the online supplemental material. Further details on the categorisation of covariates are provided in online supplemental table 3.

Statistical analysis

The prevalence of hypertension and performance along the hypertension care cascade were compared across the survey years. We used multivariate logistic regression to examine the associations between the care cascade (ie, diagnosed, treated and controlled) and the abovementioned variables. Predicted probabilities of reaching each step of the hypertension care cascade were generated after adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics.

All analyses accounted for the complex survey design, including the use of sampling weights as calculated by BPS (Badan Pusat Statistik, Central Statistics Agency), to ensure the results represent the Indonesian population at the time of each survey. We also incorporated the strata and clustering in all analyses. Age-standardised prevalence was calculated using the WHO standard population (2000–2025), facilitating comparison across years. Age standardisation with survey weights was done to account for the complex sampling design.9

Multivariate logistic regression was used to analyse the association of covariates with the hypertension care cascade (prevalence, diagnosis, treatment and control). In addition to the main effects, interaction terms between survey year and wealth quintile were included to examine how disparities in care cascade evolved across wealth groups. The statistical significance and goodness of fit of the interaction terms were evaluated using the Working (Rao-Scott) Likelihood Ratio Test to account for the complex survey design (online supplemental table 9). We also calculate relative adjusted odds compared with the poorest wealth quintile in each year to measure the gap in care between wealth and year (online supplemental 3). All statistical analyses were performed in R (V.4.2).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

The analytical sample included 1 895 354 participants across three survey years (table 1). Across all three survey years, there was a slight over-representation of female participants, with 52.1% in 2013, 53.6% in 2018 and 54.6% in 2023. The demographic profile of participants shows a slight increase in the average age over the years, with the mean age rising from 41.9 years in 2013 to 43.9 years in 2023. The proportion of participants residing in rural areas decreased from 53.8% in 2013 to 46.4% in 2023. The proportion of insured individuals increased from 56.8% in 2013 to 78.8% in 2023. Wealthier individuals tended to be overrepresented in the survey samples. After weighing in the 2023 survey, the highest proportion of the weighted sample was female (56.5%, 95% CI 56.1% to 56.9%), resided in urban areas (60.3%, 95% CI 59.9% to 60.6%) and were aged between 50 and 59 years (23.2%, 95% CI 22.9% to 23.5%) (online supplemental tables 4 and 5).

The prevalence of hypertension in Indonesia has fluctuated over the study years (online supplemental figure 1). In 2013, the crude prevalence of hypertension was 27.9% (95% CI 27.7% to 28.2%), which increased considerably to 34.2% (95% CI 34.0% to 34.4%) in 2018. This increase is consistent across all sexes, age groups and urban–rural divides. Then, it decreased slightly to 31.6% (95% CI 31.3% to 31.8%), although it remained higher than the 2013 levels. The decrease between 2018 and 2023 was more pronounced among males (31.0% in 2018 to 27.5% in 2023) than females (37.3% in 2018 to 35.6% in 2023) and the wealthiest quintiles (from 34.1% to 30.8%) than the poorest quintile (from 34.5% to 33.1%). Additionally, in 2023, urban areas exhibited a slightly higher prevalence of hypertension, at 32.5% (95% CI 32.2% to 32.8%), compared with rural areas, at 30.3% (95% CI 29.9% to 30.7%). The age-standardisation lowered the prevalence estimates for each year but did not change the observed trends between years.

In 2013, 33.0% of all hypertensives (95% CI 32.5% to 33.4%) were diagnosed. By 2018, the diagnosed rate had decreased to 24.1% (95% CI 23.7% to 24.4%) and subsequently improved slightly to 26.9% (95% CI 26.5% to 27.3%) in 2023 (figure 1, online supplemental table 6). Among those with undiagnosed hypertension in 2023, the majority had IDH, accounting for 40.0% (95% CI 39.6% to 40.4%), compared with ISH, which accounted for 24.3% (95% CI 23.9% to 24.6%) (online supplemental figure 2). Hypertension treatment showed improvement over time. In 2013, 10.4% (95% CI 10.1% to 10.6%) of the total population with hypertension received treatment. This proportion doubled to 20.9% (95% CI 20.6% to 21.2%) in 2018 and further increased to 22.4% (95% CI 22.0% to 22.7%) in 2023 (online supplemental table 7). Within 10 years, an additional 10 million individuals with hypertension were treated. Yet, 47 million out of 61 million people with hypertension were still left untreated in 2023. Hypertension control remained the most challenging aspect of the care cascade. In 2013, only 2.3% (95% CI 2.2% to 2.4%) of people with hypertension achieved control. This proportion increased to 3.7% (95% CI 3.6% to 3.8%) in 2018 and 4.2% (95% CI 4.1% to 4.4%) in 2023, corresponding to approximately 2.7 million people with hypertension being controlled and more than 95.8% of people with hypertension remaining uncontrolled (online supplemental table 8).

Across the three survey years (2013, 2018 and 2023), females consistently demonstrated better hypertension management than males (figure 2, online supplemental tables 6–8). In 2023, 31.1% (95% CI 30.6% to 31.6%) of females with hypertension were diagnosed, compared with 21.5% (95% CI 21.0% to 22.0%) of males. 26.1% (95% CI 25.6% to 26.5%) of females with hypertension received treatment, in contrast to 17.6% (95% CI 17.1% to 18.0%) of males. Additionally, 4.5% (95% CI 4.3% to 4.8%) of females with hypertension achieved control, compared with 3.8% (95% CI 3.6% to 4.1%) of males. This pattern was also observed in 2018 and 2013. There were also persistent differences in hypertension management between urban and rural populations. In 2023, urban areas showed better performance, with 29.7% (95% CI 29.1% to 30.2%) of hypertensive individuals diagnosed, compared with 22.7% (95% CI 22.1% to 23.3%) in rural areas (figure 2). Treatment rates were also higher in urban areas, with 24.8% (95% CI 24.4% to 25.3%) of individuals with hypertension receiving treatment, compared with 18.6% (95% CI 18.1% to 19.1%) in rural areas. Control rates followed a similar pattern, with 5.3% (95% CI 5.0% to 5.5%) of the hypertensive population achieving control in urban areas vs 2.7% (95% CI 2.5% to 2.9%) in rural areas. This urban–rural gap was consistent across the 3 years of the survey.

Between 2013 and 2023, the management of hypertension improved overall. However, disparities increased across wealth quintiles (figure 3). In 2013, the wealthiest quintile had a 3.9 percentage point higher rate of diagnosis compared with the poorest quintile. In 2023, this diagnosis gap widened to 9.3 percentage points. A similar pattern was observed in treatment. In 2013, the treatment gap among the wealthiest and the poorest was 7.5 percentage points. By 2018, treatment rates had almost doubled for all wealth quintiles, and the wealth gap had shrunken to 5.9 percentage points. By 2023, treatment rates among the poorest remained almost unchanged but continued to improve among the wealthiest, increasing the wealth gap between these two groups to 10.1 percentage points. Control rates followed a somewhat similar trend to treatment rates, with smaller wealth gaps, as all wealth quintiles had control rates of less than 10%. The wealth disparities did not change materially after adjustment for age, sex, insurance status and urban/rural residence (online supplemental tables 10 and 11). The adjusted OR for diagnosed hypertension comparing the wealthiest to the poorest quintile was 1.25 (95% CI 1.18 to 1.33) in 2013, 1.44 (95% CI 1.36 to 1.51) in 2018 and 1.66 (95% CI 1.57 to 1.75) in 2023. The adjusted OR for treated hypertension comparing the wealthiest to the poorest was 2.48 (95% CI 2.28 to 2.71) in 2013, 1.45 (95% CI 1.37 to 1.53) in 2018 and 1.87 (95% CI 1.76 to 1.98) in 2023.

Discussion

Our results indicate that while the prevalence of hypertension in Indonesia increased to 31.6% in 2023, diagnosis rates remained low at 26.5%. Although treatment rates have doubled from 11.2% to 22.8% within a decade, control rates remained low at 4.4%. Across the care cascade, females consistently demonstrated higher hypertension prevalence as well as better diagnosis, treatment and control rates than males. Urban populations also consistently outperformed rural populations. Subtype variance of hypertension, especially IDH, contributes to poor performance of diagnosis and treatment. There was a persistently widening wealth gap across the entire cascade, which only briefly narrowed with the receipt of treatment in 2018.

Indonesia, despite its status as an upper-middle-income country, shows a higher prevalence of hypertension among females than males; similar results have been well-documented in prior research.6 10 This pattern is commonly observed in lower-income countries. Our findings on hypertension management align with previous global analyses by NCD-RisC and studies using IFLS data from 2007 and 2015,4 11 which reported that nearly 70% of hypertensive individuals in Indonesia were undiagnosed, and 91% of patients had uncontrolled hypertension.6 7 12 Sex and age differences in 2007 persist. Meanwhile, a comparison between urban and rural areas shows a 50% relative difference in treatment and control rates, consistent with previous studies.6

The contributing factors to the increase in prevalence include population ageing, growth and shifts in lifestyle, such as dietary changes and increased sedentary behaviour.13 14 Interestingly, the prevalence of hypertension declined slightly from 2018 to 2023, with the higher wealth quintile (Q3–Q5) showing a more pronounced reduction between 2018 and 2023. Previous studies have yielded a mixed relationship between wealth and risky behaviours. While wealthier individuals tend to consume more fibre and have lower smoking rates, they also face higher risks of obesity and physical inactivity.15 16 Another possible explanation for the decline in hypertension prevalence is the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, which led to an estimated one million excess deaths in Indonesia.17 18 Given that hypertension is a major risk factor for severe COVID-19 outcomes, the pandemic likely contributed to higher mortality among the hypertension population.19

The largest drop in the hypertension care cascade was at the diagnosis stage. In 2013, 70.2% of hypertensive individuals were undiagnosed. By 2023, it increased to 73.6%. The growing proportion of undiagnosed cases highlights that the hypertensive population is expanding faster than the healthcare system’s ability to diagnose new cases, leaving nearly 45 million hypertensive individuals undiagnosed by 2023. The pattern of hypertension subtype in Indonesia is unusual; up to one-third of hypertension is isolated diastolic, while in other countries,20 21 this proportion is lower than 10%. Previous studies have documented that IDH is often underdiagnosed.22 In Indonesia’s case, IDH has contributed to 40% of undiagnosed cases (see online supplemental figure 2). Indonesia’s current approach to hypertension care relies heavily on opportunistic screening during patient visits. This approach partially explains higher diagnosis rates among women, who often access healthcare during maternal and child health services.23 24 Community-based health interventions historically aimed at maternal care have incidentally improved hypertension detection among women.25 26

The percentage of treated hypertension nearly tripled between 2013 and 2018, likely driven by the NHI) programme introduced in 2014 to achieve Universal Health Coverage (UHC).27 A reduction followed the increase in the percentage of treated hypertension between 2013 and 2018, in the gap in performance between the poorest and wealthiest. In 2013, approximately 57% of Indonesia’s population had insurance coverage, although it remained fragmented across regional and occupational-based schemes. After 2014, these fragmented systems were consolidated into a national single-payer system, which standardised payment mechanisms across primary, secondary and tertiary care. This system enables the entire population to access healthcare services for non-communicable disease (NCDs), including consultations, medications and hospital referrals.27,31 NHI programmes were positively associated with higher chronic and recurrent care utilisation and reduced financial barriers.32,35

Despite the improvement in treatment coverage, over 95% of the hypertensive population remains uncontrolled. The data from 2023, alongside comparisons with 2013 and 2018, suggest that while there have been slight improvements in the diagnosis and treatment of hypertension in Indonesia, the overall control of hypertension remains low.21,24 Better treatment coverage that added 10 million people to the treated population in the last 10 years did not improve controlled rates.6

District-level governments are now mandated to conduct hypertension screening and management, which is outlined in district-level minimum service standards, the National Medium-Term Development Plan for 2020–2024, and the Ministry of Health Strategic Plan for 2020–2024. However, the health system’s inability to achieve meaningful hypertension control outcomes suggests critical deficits in system competence. One possible reason for the stagnation in hypertension control is Indonesia’s low physician density with only 0.7 doctors per 1000 population in 2023.36 Physician scarcity represents a significant bottleneck, limiting access to care and the quality of care delivered.36 At this capacity, it is estimated that Indonesia’s healthcare system can realistically support just one annual patient visit per hypertensive individual—far below the required (at least 3, 6 and 12 times visit scenario) for effective chronic disease management.37 With the severity of insufficiency, the health system struggles to provide continuous counselling, monitoring and medication adjustment needed for effective hypertension management, often exchanging quality for quantity.38 One solution to limited physician availability is task shifting, which involves reallocating specific clinical responsibilities from physicians to trained non-physician healthcare workers, such as nurses or community health workers. Task-shifting has shown positive outcomes in various LMICs for infectious diseases and maternal-child health, and emerging evidence supports its potential for NCDs, including CVDs.39 40 Another crucial factor is provider competence. While Indonesia has consistently updated its national hypertension guidelines (the latest issued by the Indonesian Society of Hypertension in 2024),41 previous studies have shown that barriers to its implementation, including non-adherence to clinical guidelines, inadequate integration of guidelines into clinical practice and insufficient training on crucial aspects of hypertension management, such as drug titration and therapy optimisation.42 43

As of this writing, Indonesia is redesigning its primary care strategy to focus on integrated primary care, including NCD management, drawing on elements from the WHO HEARTS initiative.44 A national screening programme was launched in February 2025 to test all individuals aged 18 or older for NCD risk factors, including blood pressure, aiming to close the diagnosis gap.45 46 However, diagnosis is only the first step of the care cascade; it’s crucial to ensure the translation of diagnosis to effective treatment and blood pressure control. Indonesia has demonstrated commendable progress in improving the treatment rates over 5 years, yet failed to translate this progress into control rates, highlighting significant shortcomings in the quality of care. Improved access to healthcare does not necessarily equate to access to high-quality care.47

This study’s strengths lie in a large sample size with multiple years of surveys covering all provinces in Indonesia. This study allows for more robust subpopulation multivariate regression analyses and interaction estimates of sociodemographic factors influencing each stage of the hypertension care cascade between years. This study has several limitations. Although we used consistent sampling methods across survey years to minimise bias, repeated cross-sectional data limit our ability to track individual-level changes over time. Single-visit blood pressure measurement may bias prevalence and control estimates. However, averaging two to three readings per encounter, the absence of repeat-visit, home or ambulatory monitoring limits the exclusion of white-coat or transient hypertension. Additionally, Indonesia’s persistently low control rates are not well-explained due to a lack of research on barriers such as physician adherence to guidelines or patients’ compliance with treatment.25 48 Future studies should consider longitudinal designs to capture individual-level trends better and explore factors influencing regional disparities and the impact of UHC on hypertension management.

In conclusion, Indonesia faces a growing hypertension burden, yet the majority of the hypertensive population remains undiagnosed. Although treatment coverage has doubled over the last 10 years, controlled rates have remained stagnant, and disparities between wealth groups continue to widen. To manage the hypertension cascade, Indonesia should focus on designing long-term management strategies that support consistent follow-up, adherence to treatment and equitable care across socioeconomic groups.
