Quantifying multidisciplinary collaboration in Huntington’s disease before and after service redesign: a social network analysis
Authors: Yenni Lie, Susan Mathers, Ruth Hosken, Julie C Stout, Emily Mercieca, Yufei Xu, Henry Ma, Thanh G Phan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective multidisciplinary collaboration underpins integrated care for people living with Huntington’s disease (HD). Traditional evaluations focus on patient outcomes or self-reported collaboration, which may not capture the underlying structure of team interactions. Social network analysis (SNA) provides an objective method to map collaboration structures and quantify changes associated with service redesign. This study aimed to evaluate structural changes in multidisciplinary team collaboration following service redesign in a specialist HD clinic using encounter-derived SNA.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective before–after cohort study comparing pre-redesign (2014–2015, Before) and post-redesign (2018–2019, After) periods, including all clinic attendees. Provider participation was extracted from electronic health records and aggregated at the patient-day level. Co-occurrence networks were constructed using Pearson correlation coefficients of provider co-occurrence patterns across shared patient-days, generating weighted undirected networks of disciplinary collaboration. Network metrics, including community structure, modularity (Q, a measure of network division into communities), and local (degree, betweenness) and global (PageRank) centrality measures were used to characterise network structure. Secondary analyses examined patient-level outcomes, including survival, functional decline, non-elective hospital admissions, non-elective residential care placement, emergency services and specialist palliative care involvement, and advance care plan completion.ResultsA total of 685 patients contributed 9797 encounters (3926 Before; 5871 After). Co-occurrence patterns shifted from more concentrated dyadic co-participation toward more interconnected allied health involvement across patient-days. Network communities decreased from nine (Q = 0.603) to seven (Q = 0.262), reflecting reduced fragmentation and increased cross-disciplinary integration. Allied health providers showed the largest increases in degree centrality, while PageRank changes were heterogeneous across disciplines. The neurologist remained structurally central across both periods, while the clinical liaison showed reduced relative centrality. Overall, the network demonstrated increased multidisciplinary integration with sustained medical centrality. Short-term patient outcomes were largely unchanged.ConclusionsService redesign in a specialist HD clinic was associated with reduced network fragmentation and reorganisation of multidisciplinary collaboration, particularly among allied health providers, alongside minimal short-term changes in patient outcomes. Background

Huntington’s disease (HD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder affecting motor, cognitive, and psychiatric function, and associated with increasing disability, impaired quality of life, and substantial caregiver burden [1–3]. Its multisystem impact necessitates coordinated multidisciplinary care to address the complex and evolving needs of people living with HD and their caregivers [4]. Evidence from HD and other neurodegenerative disorders suggests that multidisciplinary care models can improve care coordination, selected patient-reported outcomes, and, in some cases, survival [5–7].

Research in healthcare team science indicates that multidisciplinary team effectiveness depends not only on the disciplines represented, but also on how team members interact and collaborate in practice [8, 9]. Collaborative professional networks can enhance communication, care coordination, and the quality and safety of healthcare delivery [10]. Despite this, the structural patterns of collaboration that underpin multidisciplinary team functioning are rarely quantified using objective, relational network approaches.

Evaluations of multidisciplinary teams often focus on patient-level outcomes, service utilisation, or survey-based measures of collaboration and team climate [11–13]. While informative, these approaches may not detect changes in the underlying relational structure of the team – including connectivity, centrality, and community organisation – that shape how care is organised and delivered [14]. Organisational redesign initiatives are commonly implemented to improve service delivery, coordination, and workforce integration [15–17], yet their impact on the structural patterns of collaboration within healthcare teams remains poorly understood.

In this study, a service redesign was undertaken within a specialist Huntington’s disease service with the aim of strengthening multidisciplinary integration. The redesign sought to transition from a predominantly neurologist-led model with episodic, referral-driven multidisciplinary input to a more systematically integrated interdisciplinary model. The intended goals were to improve coordination across disciplines, enhance integration of multidisciplinary care within routine clinical pathways, and enable more proactive, patient- and caregiver- responsive service delivery across the disease trajectory.

Social network analysis (SNA) provides an objective framework to map collaboration structures within teams. By representing team members as nodes and collaborative interactions as edges, SNA enables both visualisation and quantification of network properties such as centrality, community structure, and modularity, capturing provider roles, influence, and clustering within the team [14]. In healthcare, SNA has been used to examine communication patterns, knowledge exchange, professional influence, and team functioning [18–20]. However, few studies have applied encounter-derived SNA to quantify structural changes in multidisciplinary clinical teams using routinely collected service data, particularly in the context of service redesign.

This study aimed to evaluate structural changes in multidisciplinary team collaboration following service redesign in a specialist HD clinic using encounter-derived SNA. Secondary analyses of short-term patient-level outcomes were conducted to provide contextual insight into service performance.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective before-after cohort study at a publicly funded multidisciplinary Huntington’s disease (HD) clinic in Victoria, Australia.

A service redesign implemented during 2016–2017 changed care delivery from a predominantly neurologist-led model supported by a clinical liaison to an interdisciplinary service configuration. In the pre-redesign model, multidisciplinary input was accessed through referral-based, discipline-specific consultations initiated in response to clinical need. Following redesign, neurologists continued to provide specialist medical review and clinical leadership. Two service components were introduced. First, the clinical liaison role was formalised as a care coordination function across stages of disease progression, providing a consistent point of contact for coordinating internal and external service contacts. Second, structured interdisciplinary needs assessment appointments were introduced between routine medical reviews. These were conducted by the clinical liaison and an allied health clinician relevant to the clinical issue, and were scheduled in response to routine review findings, patient or caregiver reported concerns, or clinical liaison assessment. Findings from these assessments informed subsequent specialist review and were used by the treating neurologist to guide ongoing clinical management.

Two periods were examined: pre-redesign (Before, 2014–2015) and post-redesign (After, 2018–2019). Data from 2020 onwards were excluded to avoid confounding effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on service delivery, encounter modality, and healthcare utilisation. The study is reported in accordance with the SQUIRE 2.0 guidelines for reporting healthcare improvement studies [21]. Ethics approval was obtained from the Research Ethics and Ethics Committee at Calvary Health Care Bethlehem (CHCB 22,041,401). A waiver of individual consent was granted due to the retrospective use of routinely collected data.

Participants and data collection

All patients who attended the HD clinic during the study periods were included. Data were manually extracted from electronic health records (EHR). Baseline patient characteristics included age, gender, marital status, Cytosine-Adenine-Guanine (CAG) repeat length, and Unified Huntington’s Disease Rating Scale Total Functional Capacity (TFC) status [22].

For each clinical encounter, we extracted three domains of data [1]: provider participation [2], encounter characteristics, and [3] patient outcomes. Provider participation data recorded the presence or absence of each provider discipline at each encounter. Participation was coded as a binary variable (0 = absent, 1 = present) and used to construct co-occurrence ties for social network analysis. This approach was selected to capture collaboration embedded within routine clinical workflows, providing a consistent and objective proxy for structural integration of team members across study periods.

Encounter characteristics included mode of delivery (face-to-face, telephone, email, video, letter, and outreach visit) and reason for encounter. Encounters represented all documented clinical interactions in the electronic health record, regardless of communication modality. Encounter mode was recorded as a single primary category and summarised descriptively. Reasons for encounters were extracted from structured EHR fields and clinician-entered free-text documentation and mapped to six predefined categories representing the primary purpose(s) of each encounter: routine scheduled care, unscheduled needs-responsive care, multidisciplinary review or discussion, care coordination or external liaison, National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS)-related activities, and non-clinical activities. Each category was coded as a binary variable (0 = absent, 1 = present) at the encounter level, allowing multiple reasons to be assigned to a single encounter. For example, an encounter could be coded as both unscheduled needs-responsive care and multidisciplinary review or discussion if both were documented within the same encounter. Encounter reasons were summarised descriptively and were not included in inferential analyses.

Patient outcomes were assessed within the corresponding study period (Before: 2014–2015; After: 2018–2019). Outcomes included survival, change in Total Functional Capacity (TFC), non-elective hospital admissions, non-elective residential care placement, emergency services involvement, specialist palliative care involvement, and advance care plan completion. TFC is a validated measure of functional capacity ranging from 13 (fully functioning) to 0 (no functional capacity), with lower scores indicating more advanced disease severity. Change in TFC (Δ TFC) was analysed as a continuous outcome, while all other non-survival outcomes were analysed as binary variables (present vs absent during the study period).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise baseline characteristics of the Before and After cohorts. Continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Encounter-level data were summarised by mode and reason. Because multiple encounter reasons could be assigned to a single encounter, reason categories were not mutually exclusive and were therefore summarised descriptively. Chi-square tests were performed only for encounter mode to provide descriptive context. The analysis focused on absolute counts and percentage point changes rather than formal hypothesis testing. Structural changes in multidisciplinary collaboration were assessed using social network analysis (SNA) as the primary analysis, with secondary analyses examining patient-level outcomes to contextualise observed network changes.

Primary analysis: social network analysis

Provider participation data were used to construct undirected weighted networks, where nodes represented 15 predefined provider disciplines and edges reflected provider co-occurrence within patient-care days. Disciplines included medical specialties (neurology, psychiatry, palliative care), nursing, clinical liaison, allied health (e.g., occupational therapy, physiotherapy, speech pathology, dietetics, social work, psychology), and supportive care roles such as music therapy and pastoral care. While individual clinicians contributed within these disciplines, clinician-level identifiers were not consistently available due to workforce turnover and staffing changes over time; therefore, analyses were conducted at the discipline level to ensure comparability across study periods.

Encounter-level data were first aggregated at the patient-day level (unique patient identifier [UR] x date) such that provider participation reflected whether each discipline was present at least once on a given day. This approach ensured that multiple encounters occurring on the same day were not treated as independent observations and that discipline involvement reflected daily participation in patient care episodes.

Provider participation at each encounter was coded as a binary patient-day-by-discipline matrix (0 = absent, 1 = present). Pairwise co-occurrence between provider disciplines was then calculated using Pearson correlation coefficients (r) across patient-days, directly generating a one-mode weighted discipline network without bipartite projection. These correlations represented the degree to which provider disciplines tended to participate in the same patient-care days [14].

For the primary analysis, only positive correlations above a minimum threshold (r ≥ 0.1) were retained as edge weights. Correlations below this threshold and negative correlations were set to zero. This step was used to reduce the influence of weak associations arising from sparse co-occurrence patterns while retaining interpretable collaborative structure. Networks were constructed separately for the Before and After cohorts.

Community structure was evaluated using the Walktrap algorithm, which groups disciplines based on similarities in their co-participation patterns across patient-care days, identifying clusters that reflect closer patterns of collaboration within the broader multidisciplinary network. This approach was used to characterise the organisation of weighted collaboration patterns across disciplines, rather than relying solely on measures of network connectivity, which do not capture variation in interaction strength or clustering structure. Modularity (Q) was calculated to quantify network division into communities, with higher values indicating more distinct and isolated communities and lower values reflecting greater cross-disciplinary integration [14].

Provider influence within the network was assessed using centrality measures. Local centrality metrics, including degree and betweenness, capture aspects of a provider’s immediate network position: degree centrality reflects the number of direct connections a provider has, while betweenness centrality quantifies the extent to which a provider bridges different groups by measuring how often they lie on the shortest paths connecting other providers [23]. PageRank centrality, a global metric, quantifies structural prominence within the overall network, considering both the number of connections and the prominence of connected providers [24]. For betweenness calculations, edge weights were inverted to represent distance, such that stronger collaborations corresponded to shorter paths.

Changes in co-occurrence patterns, community structure, modularity, and centrality metrics were interpreted as indicators of redistribution of collaboration patterns following service redesign.

A sensitivity analysis using non-thresholded weighted networks was conducted to evaluate whether the observed findings were robust to the correlation thresholding procedure.

Secondary analysis: patient-level outcomes

Binary outcomes were analysed using logistic regression (odds ratio [OR], 95% confidence intervals [CI]), and survival was analysed using Cox proportional hazards models (hazard ratios [HR]). Changes in TFC (Δ TFC) were analysed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test due to non-normal distribution. Age-adjusted analyses were performed for selected outcomes where confounding by age was plausible. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, and all tests were two-sided.

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Social network analyses were performed using the igraph package, and correlation matrices were visualised with ggcorrplot. The datasets generated and/or analysed during this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 685 patients were included: 339 in the Before cohort (2014–2015) and 346 in the After cohort (2018–2019). The After cohort was younger than the Before cohort (median age 58 years, IQR 49–67, versus 63 years, IQR 54–73). Other baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were broadly comparable between cohorts (Table 1).

Encounter characteristics

Across the study periods, there were 3926 encounters in the Before cohort and 5871 encounters in the After cohort (Table 1). The distribution of encounter modes differed between cohorts (χ2 = 1031.4, df = 5, p < 0.001). Compared with the Before cohort, the After cohort demonstrated greater use of technology-enabled encounters, including email (15.2% vs 2.7%, +12.5% points), telephone (34.1% vs 21.8%, +12.3% points), and video (4.2% vs 1.3%, +2.9% points). These changes occurred alongside reductions in face-to-face (42.7% vs 62.7%, −20% points) and letter-based encounters (2.4% vs 10.7%, −8.3% points).

Reasons for encounters also differed between cohorts. Routine scheduled care decreased in the After cohort (42.1% vs 60.1%, −18% points), while unscheduled encounters addressing emerging or ongoing patient needs increased (16.7% vs 11.5%, +5.2% points). Multidisciplinary review and discussion increased from 8.9% to 13.3% (+4.4% points), and care coordination and external liaison activities increased from 23.1% to 37.2% (+14.1% points). National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS)-related activities, negligible in the Before period (0.1%), accounted for 8.6% of encounters in the After cohort (+8.5% points). Non-clinical activities were similar across periods (16.8% vs 17.1%). Notably, the proportion of care coordination and external liaison activities performed by the clinical liaison increased from 65% in the Before cohort to 91% in the After cohort.

Overall, encounters in the After cohort were more frequently technology-enabled and included a greater proportion of needs-responsive care, multidisciplinary input, and care coordination.

Primary analysis: provider network structure

Co-occurrence (patient-day) patterns

Analysis of provider co-occurrence patterns demonstrated changes in the structure of multidisciplinary collaboration following service redesign (Fig. 1). Prior to redesign, stronger co-occurrence patterns were concentrated within a small number of provider pairs, most notably between dietician and speech pathologist (r = 0.53), music therapist and pastoral care worker (r = 0.50), and clinical liaison and neurologist (r = 0.38). These patterns suggest more concentrated collaboration within discrete provider groupings.

Cell colours indicate the direction and strength of correlations (blue = positive, red = negative), and values within cells represent Pearson correlation coefficients (r). Providers are reordered using hierarchical clustering applied separately within each cohort to group providers with similar co-occurrence patterns

Following service redesign, stronger co-occurrence patterns shifted toward a more interconnected allied health group. The strongest post-redesign association was observed between occupational therapist and physiotherapist (r = 0.57), with additional strong associations between speech pathologist and occupational therapist (r = 0.45), speech pathologist and physiotherapist (r = 0.42), and dietician with both occupational therapist (r = 0.40) and physiotherapist (r = 0.34). In contrast, previously prominent associations, such as clinical liaison and neurologist, were no longer among the strongest observed relationships. Overall, these findings indicate a shift from concentrated dyadic collaboration toward broader multidisciplinary co-occurrence across allied health disciplines.

Community detection and global network structure

Walktrap community detection identified nine communities in the pre-redesign network (modularity Q = 0.603), indicating a highly segmented collaboration structure. Although nine communities were detected, several consisted of single providers or very small groups. The network was characterised by three broader groupings: a medical cluster (neurologist and clinical liaison), a dispersed set of allied health providers, and smaller peripheral clusters including music therapist and pastoral care worker (Fig. 2). The high modularity value reflects strong within-community connectivity and limited cross-community integration.

Following service redesign, the network reorganised into seven communities with markedly lower modularity (Q = 0.262), indicating reduced network division and greater cross-community connectivity. While small communities persisted, allied health providers formed a more cohesive cluster, including physiotherapist, occupational therapist, speech pathologist, dietician, and social worker (Fig. 2). Neurologist, clinical liaison, and research nurse formed a consolidated medical cluster. The reduction in the number of detected communities and modularity suggests reduced fragmentation and greater cross-disciplinary integration following service redesign.

Centrality measures

Analysis of provider centrality revealed shifts in the structure of multidisciplinary collaboration following service redesign (Table 2). Allied health providers demonstrated the largest increases in local connectivity. Occupational therapist and social worker showed the greatest gains in degree centrality (Δ +4 each), followed by dietician (Δ +3) and physiotherapist and speech pathologist (Δ +2), indicating increased shared participation across disciplines. Neurologist demonstrated a modest increase in degree (Δ +1) and betweenness (Δ +1), consistent with continued central involvement in multidisciplinary care.

From a global perspective, PageRank centrality showed heterogenous changes across disciplines, with increases observed for several allied health providers, including occupational therapist and social worker, while other disciplines demonstrated smaller or mixed changes. Clinical liaison showed a reduction in PageRank, suggesting relatively lower structural prominence in the co-occurrence network despite holding a key care coordination role. The neurologist remained among the most influential providers within the network across centrality measures.

Overall, these findings indicate a shift toward greater multidisciplinary integration, characterised by expanded allied health connectivity and sustained central involvement of core medical providers. Sensitivity analyses using non-thresholded weighted networks demonstrated broadly consistent patterns of reduced network fragmentation and increased multidisciplinary integration following service redesign, supporting the robustness of the primary findings (Supplementary Material).

Secondary analyses: patient-level outcomes

Short-term patient outcomes were largely similar between the Before and After cohorts (Table 3). Overall survival was similar after adjusting for baseline functional status (Total Functional Capacity, TFC) and age (Cox HR = 1.22, 95% CI 0.52–2.85, p = 0.65). Functional change over the study period, measured by Δ TFC, was minimal in both cohorts (median = 0, IQR = 1) and showed no statistically significant difference (p = 0.69, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Rates of non-elective hospital admission, emergency services involvement, and specialist palliative care involvement were comparable between cohorts. The After cohort experienced fewer unplanned residential care placement compared with the Before cohort (4.3% vs 9.4%, unadjusted OR = 0.43, 95% CI 0.22–0.81, p = 0.01). However, this association was attenuated after adjusting for age (adjusted OR = 0.56, 95% CI 0.29–1.09, p = 0.09), suggesting that age partially explained the difference. Advance care plan completion was slightly higher in the After cohort (35.8% vs 29.5%), reflecting a non-significant trend toward increased completion (OR = 1.35, 95% CI 0.97–1.85, p = 0.077). Overall, these findings suggest that short-term patient-level outcomes remained largely unchanged following service redesign.

Discussion

In this single-centre study of a specialist Huntington’s disease (HD) clinic, we used encounter-derived social network analysis (SNA) to evaluate changes in multidisciplinary collaboration associated with a service redesign aimed at strengthening multidisciplinary integration, improving coordination across disciplines, and embedding multidisciplinary input more systematically within routine care pathways. From a team science and care coordination perspective, these objectives reflect a shift from episodic, referral-driven collaboration toward more continuous and distributed interprofessional interaction embedded within routine workflows. The observed structural changes were consistent with these objectives, with increased co-occurrence-based connectivity, reduced network fragmentation, and greater cross-disciplinary clustering, particularly among allied health disciplines. Reductions in modularity and the emergence of more cohesive allied health groupings indicate reduced fragmentation in co-occurrence structures across disciplines. However, changes in centrality measures were more heterogeneous across disciplines and metrics, suggesting a redistribution of network position rather than a uniform shift in influence across the team. These findings demonstrate how encounter-derived SNA can operationalise intended organisational change and provide an implementation-sensitive framework for evaluating service redesign in complex care settings.

The post-redesign period also showed a substantial increase in total encounters and a shift toward greater use of technology-enabled modalities, alongside increases in care coordination, multidisciplinary interaction, and needs-responsive care. Although technology was not a primary target of the redesign, these modalities likely facilitated more flexible communications across disciplines and may have contributed to increased co-occurrence-based connectivity by enabling interaction beyond face-to-face encounters. However, their independent contribution to network change cannot be determined from this study. Future work could use modality-stratified network models to examine whether face-to-face and non-face-to-face interactions contribute differently to multidisciplinary integration.

The clinical liaison contributed extensively to care coordination and external liaison activities in the post-redesign period. However, its comparatively lower centrality within the co-occurrence network highlights an important distinction between functional coordination activities and structurally measured collaboration, as coordination work occurring outside shared patient-day encounters is not fully captured by co-occurrence-based SNA. This underscores the importance of interpreting structural network measures alongside activity-based indicators to ensure that key coordination functions are appropriately captured in service evaluations. In contrast, the neurologist remained consistently central across network measures in both periods, without marked change in relative prominence, consistent with prior studies showing persistent physician centrality in healthcare networks [25]. This suggests that increased multidisciplinary integration can occur alongside sustained medical leadership, reflecting a redistribution of collaboration activity across allied health disciplines rather than displacement of physician roles.

Short-term patient-level outcomes remained largely unchanged between cohorts. Non-elective residential care placement was lower post-redesign in unadjusted analyses but attenuated after age adjustment, suggesting partial confounding. The absence of measurable short-term outcome differences despite structural network changes aligns with implementation science perspectives that distinguish organisational and implementation outcomes from downstream patient outcomes [26]. From this perspective, changes in team structure and care processes may emerge before effects on patient outcomes become detectable, particularly in complex chronic conditions such as HD, where outcomes are shaped by disease heterogeneity and broader system factors [27]. Structural network measures may therefore serve as intermediate indicators of organisational change, capturing shifts in care configuration and co-occurrence-based integration that may precede longer-term clinical effects.

From a learning health system and implementation science perspective, these findings support the value of encounter-derived SNA as part of iterative service evaluation [28]. The observed structural changes provide preliminary evidence of shifts in multidisciplinary co-occurrence patterns following redesign, while also highlighting limitations in the extent to which co-occurrence networks capture interaction processes occurring outside documented encounters. This reflects a broader limitation of administrative or EHR-based network data, which do not capture the full spectrum of professional interaction and coordination [29]. Accordingly, network-derived metrics are most informative when interpreted alongside complementary data sources, particularly when assessing roles involving indirect coordination or cross-setting communication [26]. More broadly, SNA may be useful for generating hypotheses about how care is organised in complex multidisciplinary services and for identifying aspects of team interaction that warrant further investigation in subsequent evaluation cycles. In this way, it can contribute to ongoing refinement of service models by providing a scalable, data-driven approach to monitoring structural change in collaboration patterns over time.

Broader contextual influences may also have contributed to observed outcome patterns. The rollout of Australia’s National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS), which coincided with the post-redesign period, expanded access to individualised disability funding and home-based supports for eligible Australians under 65 years with permanent disability. For people living with HD, increased access to community supports and support coordination may have enabled longer maintenance of care at home and facilitated more proactive planning for future care needs. Consequently, the NDIS may have contributed to lower rates of unplanned residential care placement independently of the clinic redesign, highlighting the importance of considering concurrent policy and system-level changes when interpreting service-level outcomes [30, 31].

This study contributes to the health services and implementation science literature by applying an encounter-derived network approach to evaluate changes in multidisciplinary collaboration following service redesign. Healthcare SNA has historically relied on survey-based or self-reported relational data capturing perceived communication or collaboration patterns in cross-sectional designs, which are limited by recall bias and response rates [32, 33]. More recently, administrative, claims, and electronic health record (EHR)-derived data have been used to construct observed interaction networks, including patient-sharing and encounter-based structures [34]; however, these approaches remain underutilised in evaluations of multidisciplinary team functioning and service redesign in complex chronic care settings. In contrast, this study constructs collaboration networks directly from routinely collected encounter data, enabling measurement of co-occurrence patterns without reliance on surveys and providing a scalable framework for assessing structural change in multidisciplinary care using existing clinical data. Our findings demonstrate how network metrics can provide sensitive indicators of organisational change, complementing traditional implementation and outcome measures.

Strengths of this study include a relatively large cohort for a rare disease population within a single specialist centre (n = 685), supporting stable network construction and secondary outcome analyses. Use of routinely collected encounter data enabled objective mapping and quantification of provider co-occurrence without reliance on perceptual measures. The before-after design allowed assessment of structural changes in multidisciplinary team collaboration associated with the service redesign, and the focus on provider co-occurrence within shared patient-day encounters provided a consistent proxy for team collaboration over time.

Limitations include the retrospective single-centre design, which limits generalisability and causal inference. The post-redesign evaluation period was relatively short, as data from 2020 onwards were excluded to avoid confounding from the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have limited detection of longer-term outcome effects. Misclassification bias may be present in the classification of encounter reason due to manual extraction from clinical documentation. SNA captured structural co-occurrence relationships but not functional contributions occurring outside shared patient-day encounters, such as independent care coordination activities undertaken by the clinical liaison, nor the quality, intensity, or content of interactions. Because encounter-based networks are derived from documented provider participation, observed network structures may also be influenced by service configuration, workforce composition, and patterns of clinician availability. Unmeasured confounding and concurrent system-level changes may also have influenced observed outcomes.

Future research should examine longer-term follow-up to determine whether changes in provider network structure are associated with downstream clinical or service-level outcomes. Multi-centre application of similar SNA methods would clarify generalisability and contextual influences on network evolution. Integrating modality-specific networks, functional activity data, qualitative assessments, and patient-reported outcomes may further elucidate mechanisms linking team structure, care processes and patient outcomes.

Conclusions

Service redesign in a specialist HD clinic was associated with reduced network fragmentation and reorganisation of multidisciplinary collaboration, particularly among allied health providers, alongside minimal short-term differences in patient-level outcomes. Encounter-derived social network analysis provides an objective method to assess structural changes in team collaboration, complementing traditional outcome measures and supporting evaluation of multidisciplinary care processes in complex chronic healthcare settings.
