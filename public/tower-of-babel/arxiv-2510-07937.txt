A cross-diffusion system with independent drifts and fast diffusion
Charles Elbar, Filippo Santambrogio

Abstract
We study a one-dimensional cross-diffusion system for two interacting populations on the torus, with a fast-diffusion law with exponent 0 < α ≤ 1 0<\alpha\leq 1 and different external potentials. For arbitrary non-negative L 1 L^{1} initial data with bounded entropy and a mixing condition we prove the existence of global weak solutions. This extends the recent result of Mészáros, Parker from the linear diffusion ( α = 1 \alpha=1 ) to the fast-diffusion.

Introduction

Understanding how interacting species compete for space and resources is fundamental in many areas of biology, from cell migration and tumor growth to large-scale population dynamics. A common feature in these systems is that individuals adjust their movement to avoid overcrowded regions while also responding to heterogeneities in their environment.

In this paper we study a system that takes into account these dynamics for two interacting populations, whose densities are denoted by
\rho(t,x)
and
\mu(t,x)
. We consider their evolution on a one-dimensional torus
{\mathbb{T}}:=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
over a time interval
[0,T]
for some
T>0
(if the choice of the torus is motiviated by simplicity in order to avoid boundary issues, the choice of the one-dimensional setting is, on the other hand, crucial for our approach, as the reader will see). They are given by the following cross-diffusion system:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho-\partial_{x}\left(\rho\,\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(\rho+\mu)\right)-\partial_{x}\left(\rho\,\partial_{x}V\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu-\partial_{x}\left(\mu\,\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(\rho+\mu)\right)-\partial_{x}\left(\mu\,\partial_{x}W\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(1.2)

Here,
\rho
and
\mu
have initial densities
\rho(0,\cdot)=\rho_{0}
and
\mu(0,\cdot)=\mu_{0}
assumed to be non-negative. The functions
V,W\in C^{3}({\mathbb{T}})
are external potentials, and the nonlinear function
f^{\prime}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the pressure, assumed to be of fast-diffusion type. We choose to call it
f^{\prime}
since it is its antiderivative
f

which appears when expressing this system as a gradient flow. The main goal of this paper is to prove the existence of weak solutions to System (
1.1
)–(
1.2
).

Biological relevancy.
From a biological point of view, System (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) models several important mechanisms that we describe here. The term
\partial_{x}\bigl(\rho\,\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(\rho+\mu)\bigr)
(and similarly for
\mu
) is a
cross-diffusion
effect: the movement of each population is influenced by the gradient of a pressure that depends on the total density
\rho+\mu
as in
[
4
,
12
]
. This models the fact that the two populations compete for space and tend to avoid overcrowded regions. Such cross-diffusion mechanisms have been used to describe many biological effects, such as the territorial segregation in ecological systems or the movement of cells in tissues.

In addition to this pressure-driven movement, the populations feel external potentials,
V(x)
and
W(x)
, which model directed drift. They represent possible spatial heterogeneities in the environment, such as gradients of nutrients, chemotactic signals, or the presence of rivers, water columns etc.
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
11
,
6
,
7
,
20
]
. An important aspect of the model is that it allows these potentials to be different for each species (
V\neq W
), which allows the biological reality that different species or cells types may have different environmental preferences or may respond differently to chemotactic signals.

Here we choose a
fast diffusion
type nonlinearity for
f
, as our proof does not cover other regimes. More precisely,
f(s)
is assumed to behave like
s^{\alpha}
for some
0<\alpha\leq 1
, meaning that the diffusivity increases when the total density decreases. Indeed, we will see that the sum
S:=\rho+\mu
of the two densities will be driven by a diffusion PDE with a diffusive term
\Delta(\Phi(S))
, where
\Phi^{\prime}(s)=sf^{\prime\prime}(s)
; the function
\Phi
is homogeneous to
f
and it also behaves as
s^{\alpha}
, so that we face a fast-diffusion equation for
S
. This diffusivity is biologically relevant and it models the observation that, at low densities, populations tend to spread faster to occupy available space and to colonize low-density regions quickly. For example, in ecology, animal populations or plant seeds placed in an empty space often expand their range aggressively when the competition is low, to have access to resources before others arrive. In tissue dynamics, motile cells at the front and close to the wound move faster towards the wound
[
23
]
.

Comparison with the litterature.
Cross-diffusion systems have attracted a lot of interest, see for instance
[
18
,
13
,
21
,
9
,
10
,
15
,
17
,
14
]
because they allow one species’ movement to depend on another’s density gradient. A similar system than (
1.1
)–(
1.2
), with reaction terms instead of different potentials, was studied in
[
16
,
3
,

]
and then with optimal transport methods in
[
8
]
in the one-dimensional case. Indeed the system is the gradient flow of the functional

F[\rho,\mu]=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}f(\rho+\mu)+\rho V+\mu W

with respect to the Wasserstein metric. The very first existence result for weak solutions of our system appeared in
[
19
]
, under very restrictive assumptions the potentials and initial density, mainly that the density
with stronger drift in
x
direction sits on the right side on the
x
-axis, which corresponds to segregated solutions. This limits both generality and biological applications. A significant step came in
[
22
]
, where the authors tackled the one
-
dimensional case with
\Phi(S)=S
(
\alpha=1
). They proved existence for broad classes of initial data and interaction potentials. Unfortunately, their proof is rigid: any small perturbation of the logarithm breaks the key identities and causes the argument to fail.

In this paper, we improve on this result. We show that as long as we choose
\Phi(s)=s^{\alpha}
for
0<\alpha\leq 1,

one can obtain weak solutions under the same general assumptions on data and potentials. Our method does not depend on the exact form of
\Phi
and
f
, and we would be able to cover in fact more general diffusion laws but for simplicity of the exposition we will stick to the power case.

Notations and functional settings.
We denote by
L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
,
H^{s}({\mathbb{T}})=W^{s,2}({\mathbb{T}})
the usual Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces, and by
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}}
,
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}}
their corresponding norms. We often write
C
for a generic constant appearing in the different inequalities. Its value can change from one line to another, and its dependence to other constants can be specified by writing
C(a,...)

1.1
Main result

We state our main result here, that is the existence of weak solutions of System (
1.1
)–(
1.2
). We require
f
to satisfy a fast–diffusion type assumption.

Assumption 1.1
.

We assume that
\Phi(s)=s^{\alpha}
for some
0<\alpha\leq 1
, where
\Phi
is defined via
\Phi^{\prime}(s)=sf^{\prime\prime}(s)
.

Then we define the notion of weak solutions:

Definition 1.2

(Weak solution)
.

A pair
(\rho,\mu)
is a
weak solution
of
(
1.1
)–(
1.2
) on
(0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}}
if

1.

\rho,\mu\geq 0
and

\rho,\mu\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}))
;

2.

\sqrt{\rho}\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(\rho+\mu),\,\sqrt{\mu}\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(\rho+\mu)\in L^{2}((0,T);L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}))
;

3.

for every
\varphi,\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}})
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\left[-\rho\,\partial_{t}\varphi+\rho\,\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(\rho+\mu)\,\partial_{x}\varphi+\rho\,\partial_{x}V\,\partial_{x}\varphi\right]dx\,dt=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\rho_{0}(x)\varphi(0,x)\,dx,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\left[-\mu\,\partial_{t}\psi+\mu\,\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(\rho+\mu)\,\partial_{x}\psi+\mu\,\partial_{x}W\,\partial_{x}\psi\right]dx\,dt=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\mu_{0}(x)\psi(0,x)\,dx.

Our main result reads:

Theorem 1.3

(Global existence)
.

Suppose Assumption
1.1
holds and take
V,W\in C^{3}({\mathbb{T}})
,
\rho_{0},\mu_{0}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
two non-negative densities such that
\rho_{0}\log\rho_{0},\mu_{0}\log\mu_{0}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
and
\log(\rho_{0}/\mu_{0})\in BV({\mathbb{T}})
.
Then there exists at least one weak solution

(\rho,\mu)
of (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) on
[0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}

in the sense of Definition
1.2
.

Remark 1.4
.

•

The method we use to prove Theorem
1.3
covers also more general diffusions laws than Assumption
1.1
. In fact, we mainly use the integrability of some functionals involving
\Phi
. For instance both taking
\Phi(s)=s^{2}+s^{-1}
work, but we are not able to cover the case
\Phi(s)=s^{2}
.

•

In principle, the method could be adapted to most of nonlinearities, including the porous medium
\Phi(s)=t^{2}
, as long as one can prove lower and upper bounds on the total density
S=\rho+\mu
, up to considering also suitable initial data. However, we were not able to derive such estimates, as it does not seem that comparison principle holds.

Also, to simplify the exposition, we only prove Theorem
1.3
assuming we deal with smooth solutions and we obtain a priori estimates. All the computations can be justified in an approximated scheme, exactly as in
[
22
]
.

1.2
Sketch of proof

We outline here the core of the argument. Let us assume that we have an approximating scheme
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n}
,
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n}
, for instance adding an artificial viscotity. The difficulty in obtaining weak solution of the system is to pass to the limit in the nonlinear fluxes.

Consider (
1.1
). The critical product is

\rho_{n}\,\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(\rho_{n}+\mu_{n}),

because both factors are known a priori to converge only weakly. Indeed:

•

Since both equations preserve the mass, we easily get

L^{1}
-bounds on
S_{n}:=\rho_{n}+\mu_{n}
. Therefore

\rho_{n},\mu_{n}\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}))
, hence
\sqrt{\rho_{n}}
and
\sqrt{\mu_{n}}
converge
weakly (up to a subsequence) in
L^{2}
.

•

Looking at the dissipation of the energy
F
we easily obtain

\sqrt{\rho_{n}}\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(\rho_{n}+\mu_{n}),\sqrt{\mu_{n}}\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(\rho_{n}+\mu_{n})\in L^{2}\bigl((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}}\bigr)
, so this factor
also converges weakly in
L^{2}
.

To identify the limit of their product we therefore need to prove strong convergence
of
\rho_{n}
and
\mu_{n}
. In the fast-diffusion case, we are able to obtain an
H^{1}
bound on a quantity involving the sum
S
. This alone is not enough and we need a second gradient estimate involving another independent scalar of
(\rho_{n},\mu_{n})
.

A common approach in such cross-diffusion systems is to consider

r_{n}=g(\rho_{n}/\mu_{n})
. For
\Phi(s)=s
(or equivalently
f^{\prime}(s)=\log(s))
the authors of
[
22
]
introduce

r_{n}:=\log\Bigl(\frac{\rho_{n}}{\mu_{n}}\Bigr)+\bigl(V-W\bigr),

and obtain the transport equation

\partial_{t}r_{n}=(\partial_{x}r_{n})v_{n}+\text{l.o.t.},

where
v_{n}
is a velocity field (with no special regularity required) and l.o.t. are terms
with integrable spatial derivatives. It is well-known that in one space dimension transport equations preserve the BV norm. Here, taking
\partial_{x}
, then
multiplying by
\operatorname{sign}(\partial_{x}r_{n})
and integrating over
{\mathbb{T}}

gives the
\mathrm{BV}
-estimate

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\lvert\partial_{x}r_{n}(t,x)\rvert\,dx\leq C\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T].

Coupled with the gradient bound on
S_{n}
, this is enough to deduce strong
convergence of
\rho_{n}
and
\mu_{n}
. In our settings, we modify the idea and rather than seeking for a transport equation satisfied by a ratio variable we work directly
with space derivatives. Set

r_{n}=\log\left(\frac{\rho_{n}}{\mu_{n}}\right),\quad u_{n}=\partial_{x}r_{n}-(\partial_{x}V(x)-\partial_{x}W(x))\,y(S_{n}),

where

y
is choosen carefully in terms of
\Phi
. We obtain that
u_{n}
remains bounded in
L^{1}
. Controlling
\partial_{x}r_{n}
reduces to bounding

\|y(S_{n})\|_{L^{1}}
. In the fast diffusion case, we obtain
y(s)=Cs^{1-\alpha}
and hence
y(S_{n})
is integrable. Then we combine the
H^{1}
-bound on
S_{n}
, and the
\mathrm{BV}
-bound on
r_{n}
to obtain
\mathrm{BV}
bounds on
\rho_{n}
and
\mu_{n}
. Some difficulties arise because these
\mathrm{BV}
bounds are not integrable in time, which does not occur when
\alpha=1
. But adapating the proof of the Aubin–Lions lemma yields strong convergence of
\rho_{n}
and
\mu_{n}
in
L^{1}
towards some
\rho,\mu
.
With this we are able to show that the product
\rho_{n}\,\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(\rho_{n}+\mu_{n})
converges to the correct limit, and the pair
(\rho,\mu)
is a weak solution of (
1.1
)–(
1.2
). All the subsequent a priori estimates are independent of
n
and therefore we drop the index for clarity.

Estimates on
S

We consier in this section the behavior of the sum
S
of the two densities, defined as
S:=\rho+\mu
.
We begin by adding (
1.1
) and (
1.2
). We obtain

\partial_{t}S=\partial_{t}(\rho+\mu)=\partial_{x}\left(\rho\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(\rho+\mu)\right)+\partial_{x}\left(\mu\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(\rho+\mu)\right)+\partial_{x}\left(\rho\partial_{x}V\right)+\partial_{x}\left(\mu\partial_{x}W\right).

Since
\rho\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(S)+\mu\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(S)=\partial_{x}\Phi(S)
, we get

\partial_{t}S=\partial_{xx}\Phi(S)+\partial_{x}\left(\rho\partial_{x}V+\mu\partial_{x}W\right).

(2.1)

This expression yield estimates on
S
.

Proposition 2.1

(Spatial estimate on
S
)
.

We have the following estimates on
S
:

\displaystyle S\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})),\quad\partial_{x}S^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\in L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}}),\quad S^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\in L^{2}((0,T);L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}})),

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\log S\in L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}}),\quad\partial_{x}S^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\in L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}})\quad S^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\in L^{2}((0,T);L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}})),

\displaystyle S\in L^{2-\alpha}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}}),\quad\partial_{x}S^{1-\alpha}\in L^{1}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}}),\quad S^{1-\alpha}\in L^{1}((0,T);L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}})).

Proof.

We first recall that the nonnegativity of both
\rho
and
\mu
can be obtained from the transport equation structure and the nonnegativity of the initial conditions. The first estimate is obtained after integrating (
2.1
) in space and using periodic boundary conditions as well as the integrability of the initial conditions.
We recall that
\Phi(S)=S^{\alpha}
for
0<\alpha\leq 1
. We only do the proof of the second estimate for
\alpha<1
, the proof for
\alpha=1
being similar by considering the dissipation of
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}S\log S
.
Using (
2.1
),
\rho,\mu\leq S
,
|\partial_{x}V|,|\partial_{x}W|\leq C
and Young’s inequality we obtain for
0\leq\beta\leq 1

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}-S^{\beta}+\alpha\beta(1-\beta)\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}S^{-3+\alpha+\beta}(\partial_{x}S)^{2}\\
=\beta(\beta-1)\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\rho\partial_{x}VS^{\beta-2}\partial_{x}S+\beta(\beta-1)\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\mu\partial_{x}WS^{\beta-2}\partial_{x}S\\
\leq\frac{\alpha\beta(1-\beta)}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}S^{-3+\alpha+\beta}(\partial_{x}S)^{2}+C\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}S^{\beta+1-\alpha}.

Therefore we have

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}-S^{\beta}+\frac{2\alpha\beta(1-\beta)}{(\alpha+\beta-1)^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\left(\partial_{x}S^{\frac{\alpha+\beta-1}{2}}\right)^{2}\leq C\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}S^{\beta+1-\alpha}.

Note that in this expression, the term in the middle reads
\frac{\alpha\beta(1-\beta)}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left(\partial_{x}\log S\right)^{2}
when
\alpha+\beta=1
.

As
S\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}))
, to obtain a priori estimates from this dissipation we first choose
\beta=\alpha
and we obtain the second estimate after integrating in time and using also the integrability of the initial condition. The third estimate is a consequence of the first two estimates. Choosing then
\beta=1-\alpha
and using the seventh estimate that is proved independently below we obtain the fourth estimate (indeed, for the validity of the fourth estimate we would need
S\in L^{2-2\alpha}
and the seventh provides
S\in L^{2-\alpha}
; of course, when
\alpha>1/2
this argument is not necessary).
To obtain the fifth estimate we compute the sum of the dissipation of each entropies:

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\rho\log\rho+\mu\log\mu+\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}f^{\prime\prime}(S)|\partial_{x}S|^{2}=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\rho\partial_{xx}V+\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\mu\partial_{xx}W\leq C.

The last estimate of this inequality is a consequence of mass conservation and the assumptions on
V,W
. Since
f^{\prime\prime}(S)|\partial_{x}S|^{2}=C|\partial_{x}S^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|^{2}
we obtain the fifth estimate. We deduce the sixth estimate from the fifth.
From the eighth estimate we obtain the ninth, so it only remains to prove the seventh and eighth estimates. By using the previous estimates and Jensen’s inequality we obtain:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}S^{2-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}SS^{1-\alpha}\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\|S^{1-\alpha}\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\left|\partial_{x}S^{1-\alpha}\right|\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}S^{1-\frac{3\alpha}{2}}|\partial_{x}S^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}S^{2-3\alpha}}\sqrt{\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|\partial_{x}S^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|^{2}}\lesssim\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}S^{2-\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\frac{2-3\alpha}{2-\alpha}}

From these inequalities we obtain the last two estimates: the seventh because
\frac{1}{2}\frac{2-3\alpha}{2-\alpha}<1
and then the eighth one using the seventh estimate.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Eventhough such an estimate is not necessary in our analysis, we underline that it would be possible to obtain
S\in L^{1+\alpha}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}})
by computing the dissipation of the

The equations on
S
and
r

We recall that we use the notations

S(t,x):=\rho(t,x)+\mu(t,x),\qquad r(t,x):=\log\left(\rho(t,x)\right)-\log\left(\mu(t,x)\right).

We have

\rho=\frac{Se^{r}}{e^{r}+1},\qquad\mu=\frac{S}{e^{r}+1}.

(3.1)

Observe also that we have

\rho-\mu=Sh(r),

where we define

h(r):=\frac{e^{r}-1}{e^{r}+1}.

(3.2)

with
h^{\prime}(r)=\frac{2e^{r}}{(e^{r}+1)^{2}}
. We also introduce

g(r):=\log\left(\frac{e^{r}}{(e^{r}+1)^{2}}\right)

(3.3)

so that
g^{\prime}(r)=\frac{1-e^{r}}{1+e^{r}}
.

Next we rewrite the term
\rho\partial_{x}V+\mu\partial_{x}W
in a convenient form. Set

\mathbf{v}:=\frac{\partial_{x}V+\partial_{x}W}{2},\qquad\mathbf{w}:=\frac{\partial_{x}V-\partial_{x}W}{2}.

(3.4)

Then we obtain

\rho\partial_{x}V+\mu\partial_{x}W=\left(\rho+\mu\right)\mathbf{v}+\left(\rho-\mu\right)\mathbf{w}.

Hence, using (
3.1
) and (
3.2
),

\rho\partial_{x}V+\mu\partial_{x}W=S\mathbf{v}+Sh(r)\mathbf{w}.

(3.5)

Plugging (
3.5
) into (
2.1
) gives

\partial_{t}S=\partial_{xx}\Phi(S)+\partial_{x}\left(S\mathbf{v}+Sh(r)\mathbf{w}\right).

We now expand the last term carefully:

\partial_{x}\left(S\mathbf{v}+Sh(r)\mathbf{w}\right)=\partial_{x}S\mathbf{v}+S\partial_{x}\mathbf{v}+\partial_{x}Sh(r)\mathbf{w}+Sh^{\prime}(r)\partial_{x}r\mathbf{w}+Sh(r)\partial_{x}\mathbf{w}.

Hence

\partial_{t}S=\partial_{xx}\Phi(S)+\partial_{x}S\left[\mathbf{v}+h(r)\mathbf{w}\right]+S\left[\partial_{x}\mathbf{v}+h(r)\partial_{x}\mathbf{w}\right]+S\mathbf{w}h^{\prime}(r)\partial_{x}r.

(3.6)

Next we derive the evolution equation for
r
. We obtain

\partial_{t}r=\frac{1}{\rho}\left[\partial_{x}(\rho\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(S))+\partial_{x}(\rho\partial_{x}V)\right]-\frac{1}{\mu}\left[\partial_{x}(\mu\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(S))+\partial_{x}(\mu\partial_{x}W)\right].

Notice that the terms
\partial_{xx}f(S)
cancel out, thus:

\partial_{t}r=\partial_{x}r\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(S)+\partial_{x}\log\rho\,\partial_{x}V-\partial_{x}\log\mu\,\partial_{x}W+\partial_{xx}V-\partial_{xx}W.

We now handle the mixed term

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\log\rho\partial_{x}V-\partial_{x}\mu\partial_{x}W

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{x}\log\rho+\partial_{x}\log\mu\right)\mathbf{w}+\left(\partial_{x}\log\rho-\partial_{x}\log\mu\right)\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=2\partial_{x}\log S\mathbf{w}+\partial_{x}r\left(g^{\prime}(r)\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{v}\right).

Putting everything together, we obtain

\partial_{t}r=\partial_{x}r\left(\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(S)+g^{\prime}(r)\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{v}\right)+2\mathbf{w}\partial_{x}\log S+\partial_{xx}V-\partial_{xx}W.

(3.7)

4
BV estimates and compactness

In this section we prove
\mathrm{BV}
estimates on
r
and show that they imply
\mathrm{BV}
estimates on
\rho
and
\mu
. Combined with estimates on
\partial_{t}\rho
and
\partial_{t}\mu
and adapting the proof of the Aubin-Lions lemma, we are able to prove strong convergence of the approximating scheme towards a weak solution of (
1.1
)–(
1.2
). We start with
\mathrm{BV}
estimates on
r
.

4.1
BV estimates on
r

Proposition 4.1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.3
there exists
C
such that:

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|\partial_{x}r(t,\cdot)|\leq C.

Proof.

In order to derive a bound in
\mathrm{BV}
for
r
we differentiate the equation for
r
with respect to
x
and obtain

\partial_{t}\partial_{x}r=\partial_{x}\left(\partial_{x}r\left(\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(S)+g^{\prime}(r)\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{v}\right)\right)+2\partial_{xx}\log S\mathbf{w}+2\partial_{x}\mathbf{w}\partial_{x}\log S+\partial_{xxx}V-\partial_{xxx}W.

(4.1)

We now introduce a carefully chosen shift that cancels the highest-order terms. We fix a smooth function
\gamma=\gamma(x)
and another function
y=y(S)
depending only on
S
to be choosen later. Define

u(t,x):=\partial_{x}r(t,x)-\gamma\,y\left(S(t,x)\right).

(4.2)

We first compute

\partial_{t}u=\partial_{t}\partial_{x}r-\gamma\,y^{\prime}\left(S\right)\partial_{t}S.

Also recall (
4.1
) for
\partial_{t}\partial_{x}r
. Subtracting
\gamma\,y^{\prime}(S)\partial_{t}S
from (
4.1
) and using (
4.2
), we get

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}\left(u\left(\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(S)+g^{\prime}(r)\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{v}\right)\right)+\partial_{x}\left(\gamma y(S)\left(\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(S)+g^{\prime}(r)\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{v}\right)\right)

4.2
BV estimates on
\rho
and
\mu
and compactness

Some of the following bounds are not suitably integrable in time. However, we prove below that this does not prevent strong compactness of the approximating scheme.

Proposition 4.2

(Spatial BV estimates)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
1.3
, there exists an exponent
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
such that

\partial_{x}\rho,\quad\partial_{x}\mu\;\in\;L^{\varepsilon}((0,T);L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})).

Proof.

We only treat
\rho
, since the treatment of
\mu
is identical. Recall

\rho=\frac{Se^{r}}{1+e^{r}}.

Differentiating in
x
gives

\partial_{x}\rho=\frac{e^{r}}{1+e^{r}}\partial_{x}S+\frac{Se^{r}}{(1+e^{r})^{2}}\partial_{x}r.

Both factors
\frac{e^{r}}{1+e^{r}}
and
\frac{e^{r}}{(1+e^{r})^{2}}
are bounded uniformly.

Concerning the first term on the right hand side:

|\partial_{x}S|=C|\partial_{x}S^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|S^{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}

Therefore with Proposition
2.1
we deduce

\partial_{x}S\in L^{1}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}})

as a product of two factors each in
L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}})
.
With Proposition
2.1
that yields
S\in L^{1-\alpha}((0,T);L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}))
and
\partial_{x}r\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}))
from the previous section we also deduce that there exists
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
such that

S|\partial_{x}r|\in L^{\varepsilon}((0,T);L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})).

Indeed, from
\partial_{x}r\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}))
we just need
S\in L^{\varepsilon}((0,T);L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}))
, hence we can take
\varepsilon=\max\{1-\alpha,2\alpha-1\}
(we observe that we have
\varepsilon\geq 1/3
but this is not crucial).
We conclude
\partial_{x}\rho\in L^{\varepsilon}((0,T);L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}))
as claimed.
∎

We now prove compactness in time for
\rho
and
\mu
:

Proposition 4.3
.

There exists
s>0
such that

\partial_{t}\rho,\partial_{t}\mu\in L^{2}((0,T);H^{-s}({\mathbb{T}})).

Proof.

Once again we only prove the proposition for
\rho
. Proposition
2.1
shows that
\sqrt{S}\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(S)=C\partial_{x}S^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\in L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}})
and therefore
\sqrt{\rho}\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(S)\in L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}})
.
Since

\partial_{t}\rho=\partial_{x}(\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\rho}\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(S))+\partial_{x}(\rho\partial_{x}V)

with
\rho\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}))
we deduce that
\partial_{t}\rho\in L^{2}((0,T);Y)
where
Y
is the space of functions which are the derivatives of
L^{1}
functions. This space can be continuously embed in an
H^{-s}
space for some
s>0
.
∎

In order to conclude the main theorem it remains to prove the following proposition.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n}
be a sequence of nonnegative functions. Let
0<\varepsilon<1
. Assume there exists
C
independent of
n
such that

\displaystyle\|\rho_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}))}\leq C

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x}\rho_{n}\|_{L^{\varepsilon}((0,T);L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}))}\leq C

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}\rho_{n}\|_{L^{2}((0,T);H^{-s}({\mathbb{T}}))}\leq C.

Then up to a subsequence not relabed, there exists
\rho\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}))
with
\partial_{t}\rho\in L^{2}((0,T);H^{-s}({\mathbb{T}}))
such that
\rho_{n}\to\rho
strongly in
L^{1}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}})
and almost everywhere in
(0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}}.

Proof.

The existence of
\rho
and its regularity follows by weak star compactness with the Banach-Alaoglu theorem. Concerning strong compactness, we first prove equicontinuity in space. We let
M>0
,
f_{n}(t)=\|\partial_{x}\rho_{n}(t)\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})}
and the sets

A_{n}=\left\{t\in(0,T),\;f_{n}(t)\leq M\right\},\quad B_{n}=(0,T)\setminus A_{n}.

Thanks to our assumptions, there exists a constant
C
independent of
n
such that

|B_{n}|\leq\frac{C}{M^{\varepsilon}}.

Let
h>0
. We compute

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|\rho_{n}(t,x+h)-\rho_{n}(t,x)|

\displaystyle=\int_{A_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|\rho_{n}(t,x+h)-\rho_{n}(t,x)|+\int_{B_{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|\rho_{n}(t,x+h)-\rho_{n}(t,x)|

\displaystyle\leq h\int_{A_{n}}\|\partial_{x}\rho_{n}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})}+2|B_{n}|\|\rho_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}))}

\displaystyle\leq CMTh+\frac{C}{M^{\varepsilon}}.

Choosing
M^{1+\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{h}
we deduce that for all
n
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|\rho_{n}(t,x+h)-\rho_{n}(t,x)|\leq Ch^{\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}}.

Now we prove equicontinuity in time, and we consider a sequence of mollifiers in space
\varphi_{\delta}(x)=\frac{1}{\delta}\varphi\left(\frac{x}{\delta}\right)
for a smooth nonnegative
\varphi
. Let
k>0
and we compute

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-k}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|\rho_{n}(t+k,x)-\rho_{n}(t,x)|\leq\int_{0}^{T-k}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|\rho_{n}(t+k,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}-\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}|

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T-k}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|\rho_{n}(t+k,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}-\rho_{n}(t+k,x)|+\int_{0}^{T-k}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}-\rho_{n}(t,x)|

By the previous equicontinuity in space, we can prove (it is a corollary of the Riesz-Frechet-Kolmogorov theorem) that the last two integrals are bounded by some monotone
\omega_{1}(\delta)
independent of
n
with
\omega_{1}(\delta)\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
.

Concerning the first term on the right-hand side we use the uniform bound on
\partial_{t}\rho_{n}
which yields

\int_{0}^{T-k}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|\rho_{n}(t+k,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}-\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}|\leq Ck\sqrt{T}\|\varphi_{\delta}\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{T}})}\leq\frac{Ck\sqrt{T}}{\delta^{\frac{1}{2}+s}}.

Choosing
\delta^{\frac{1}{2}+s}=\sqrt{k}
we find that there exists a monotone
\omega_{2}(k)
such that
\omega_{2}(k)\to 0
as
k\to 0
such that

\int_{0}^{T-k}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|\rho_{n}(t+k,x)-\rho_{n}(t,x)|\leq\omega_{2}(k).

Combining both equicontinuity and applying the Riesz-Fréchet-Kolmogorov theorem then yields the result.
∎

With this proposition we are now able to pass to the limit and prove the main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n}
,
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n}

\mu_{n}
(after integration by parts against a test function). Let
\rho
and
\mu
be the limit of
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n}
and
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n}
respectively (easily obtained from the previous sections), and
S
their sum.
We write this term as

\rho_{n}\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(S_{n})=\frac{\rho_{n}}{\sqrt{S_{n}}}\sqrt{S_{n}}\partial_{x}f^{\prime}(S_{n})=C\frac{\rho_{n}}{\sqrt{S_{n}}}\partial_{x}S_{n}^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}.

We first show that
\frac{\rho_{n}}{\sqrt{S_{n}}}
converges strongly to
\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{S}}
in
L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}})
. From Proposition
4.4
we know that it converge a.e. to
\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{S}}
by continuity. Then

\left(\frac{\rho_{n}}{\sqrt{S_{n}}}\right)^{2}\leq\rho_{n},

where
\rho_{n}
converges strongly in
L^{1}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}})
from Proposition
4.4
and therefore is uniformly integrable. Thus by Vitali’s convergence theorem (or the generalized Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem),
\frac{\rho_{n}}{\sqrt{S_{n}}}
converges strongly in
L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}})
to
\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{S}}
. From Proposition
2.1
we know that
\partial_{x}S_{n}^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}
converges weakly in
L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}})
. To identify it’s limit it is enough to do it in the distributional sense, that is to identify the limit of

. By convergence a.e. of
S_{n}
and bounds on

in

(since its gradient is bounded in this space) we deduce that
S_{n}^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}
converges to
S^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}
strongly in
L^{1}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}})
by Vitali’s convergence theorem. This concludes the proof of the theorem.
