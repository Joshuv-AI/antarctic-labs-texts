Adrenal response at the onset of type 1 diabetes mellitus in children and its association with acute metabolic and kidney complications
Authors: Giusy Capasso, Stefano Guarino, Paola Tirelli, Anna Di Sessa, Gaia Pietropaolo, Federica Auricchio, Pier Luigi Palma, Dario Piatto, Angela Zanfardino, Dario Iafusco, Emanuele Miraglia del Giudice, Anna Grandone, Pierluigi Marzuillo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCortisol secretion reflects hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis activation during stress and illness. In children with new-onset type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and acute kidney injury (AKI) are common complications associated with metabolic stress. However, the relationship between cortisol dynamics, disease severity, and renal parameters at T1DM onset remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the association between cortisol levels and clinical, biochemical, and renal features in children with newly diagnosed T1DM.MethodsThis observational cross-sectional study included 153 children from the DiAKIdney cohort (2017–2019) with newly diagnosed T1DM. Serum cortisol levels were measured on the first morning after admission. Participants were stratified into three groups based on morning cortisol levels (< 10, 10–18, and > 18 µg/dL). Clinical data, biochemical markers, and renal parameters were compared across groups. Associations between cortisol levels and disease severity were analyzed using multivariate analyses.ResultsHigher cortisol levels were significantly associated with greater disease severity. Patients with cortisol > 18 µg/dL exhibited more severe clinical symptoms, greater severity of DKA, and increased tubular stress. Higher cortisol levels were also strongly associated with increased risk of DKA (odds ratio = 1.12;95% CI:1.063–1.169; p < 0.001). Conversely no significant association emerged between cortisol levels and AKI prevalence. Patients with cortisol < 10 µg/dL had milder clinical presentations without adverse outcomes.ConclusionsCortisol levels may serve as a practical marker of metabolic stress and risk stratification in children with new-onset T1DM, particularly in the context of DKA severity.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis plays a key role in regulating the stress response [1, 2]. Cortisol secretion increases during acute illness and is proportional to the severity of stress [2].

Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM) is a chronic, autoimmune disease, characterized by an insulin deficiency and resulting hyperglycemia [3]. In up to 30–50% of cases, T1DM onset may present with varying severity degrees of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) [3, 4], a critical event associated with a pronounced activation of the HPA axis and elevated cortisol levels [5–7]. Cortisol can be regarded as a double-edged sword: in DKA, it sustains the anti-stress response, by maintaining cardiovascular stability, preserving fluid balance, and regulating inflammatory pathways. At the same time, it acts as a potent anti-insulin hormone, promoting gluconeogenesis, lipolysis, and proteolysis, thereby worsening hyperglycemia and ketogenesis. This dual role may critically influence both the severity of metabolic derangement and the trajectory of recovery [5–8]. This transient hypercortisolemia has been shown to correlate inversely with blood pH and bicarbonate levels, confirming its role as a marker of metabolic stress [5, 7, 8].

Additionally, acute kidney injury (AKI) is a frequent complication of T1DM onset, occurring in up to 65% of children with DKA and 21% of those without it [9–11]. The impact of AKI on the HPA axis is poorly understood [12], although some cases of adrenal insufficiency presenting with AKI suggest a possible interaction [13].

However, in some acute conditions, it has been reported a critical illness–related corticosteroid insufficiency (CIRCI), a condition defined by inadequate corticosteroid activity relative to the severity of illness. CIRCI results from dysregulation of the HPA axis, altered cortisol metabolism, and tissue resistance to glucocorticoids [14]. It is characterized by low random cortisol levels (< 10 µg/dL) or an insufficient cortisol response to an adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) stimulation test (delta cortisol < 9 µg/dl) [14]. Dysregulated cortisol activity in CIRCI may also results in immune dysregulation, vascular instability, metabolic decompensation, and organ dysfunction [1], potentially contributing to renal injury. This condition has been described in several critical illnesses, including sepsis, shock, cardiac arrest, trauma, and severe pneumonia and has been associated with worse outcomes in critically ill patients [14, 15].

Based on this background, we hypothesized that serum cortisol levels could serve as a marker of disease severity at T1DM onset, with higher levels in patients presenting with more severe DKA and particularly in those with concurrent AKI. Moreover, although not classically described at T1DM onset, we hypothesized that CIRCI may occur in a subset of patients with impaired adrenal stress responses and could contribute to disease severity.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate adrenal response to the acute stress of T1DM onset in a pediatric population from the DiAKIdney cohort [11].

Methods

This observational cross-sectional study was conducted within the DiAKIdney cohort, which enrolled children and adolescents with newly diagnosed T1DM between December 2017 and August 2019, as previously described [11]. The study protocol was approved by Research Ethical Committee of Università degli Studi della Campania “Luigi Vanvitelli” (approval number 368), and written informed consent was obtained from all guardians and patients.

Participants were eligible if they were < 18 years of age at the time of T1DM diagnosis, tested positive for at least one diabetes-related autoantibody (glutamic acid decarboxylase, islet antigen two, insulin, or Zinc transporter eight antibodies), and were not receiving any medication other than intravenous 0.9% NaCl infusion at admission. The diagnosis of diabetes mellitus was established according to the American Diabetes Association criteria [16]. The classification as type 1 diabetes in the pediatric population (< 18 years) and the requirement of at least one diabetes-related autoantibody followed the ISPAD Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines [17]. All participants initiated insulin therapy at diagnosis and continued insulin treatment thereafter. Exclusion criteria included treatment with any chronic medication prior to T1DM onset and congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract. Data collected at T1DM onset included age, weight, height, body mass index (BMI) with standard deviation score (SDS), blood pressure, heart rate (HR), urinary output for the first 24 h, renal ultrasound and laboratory parameters. Weight and height percentiles were derived from the Italian Society for Pediatric Endocrinology (SIEDP) growth charts, while Body Mass Index (BMI) values were expressed as standard deviation score (SDS) using the SIEDP Growth Calculator [18, 19]. Blood pressure was measured after 15 min of rest, with three oscillometric measurements taken 5 min apart. If elevated values were observed, blood pressure was confirmed using three additional auscultatory measurements [20]. Blood pressure was expressed as SDS [21]. Laboratory parameters included blood glucose, creatinine (measured by the Jaffe method), ketones, serum electrolytes (sodium, chloride, phosphorus, calcium), pH, bicarbonates, glycate hemoglobin (Hb1Ac), and complete blood count [11]. Urinary analyses included dipstick testing, proteinuria, microalbuminuria, creatinine, sodium with fractional excretion of sodium, calcium, phosphate, beta-2-microglobulin as well as neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) [11]. Urine samples were collected within the first 24 h after admission, mostly by spontaneous micturition; catheterization was used only when clinically indicated.

All analyses, except for urinary NGAL, were prospectively performed at admission in the central laboratory of our hospital using standardized and validated methods to ensure consistency.

Serum cortisol and ACTH concentrations were measured using a chemiluminescent immunoassay (LIAISON®, DiaSorin S.p.A., Saluggia, Italy). The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were < 8%. Serum electrolytes, creatinine, glucose, HbA1c, and urinary proteins, microalbumin, creatinine, sodium, calcium, phosphate, and β2-microglobulin levels were determined using automated clinical chemistry analyzers (Architect c8000 System, Abbott Architect System, Abbott Laboratories, IL, USA). Blood pH and bicarbonate levels were measured using a Radiometer ABL90 FLEX analyzer, while ketones were measured with the Go-Keto Meter (Swiss Point of Care, Europe). Urine and blood samples were processed within 2 h from collection. Urine samples for NGAL measurement were collected and stored at − 80 °C until analysis at the end of enrollment, using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (NGAL ELISA kit 036; AntibodyShop, Grusbakken, Denmark).

Renal ultrasound was performed by the same experienced radiologist using high-resolution ultrasound system (Esaote MyLab Alpha, Esaote S.p.A., Genoa, Italy) within 24–48 h of admission (after initial stabilization in severe DKA), assessing kidney length, parenchymal echogenicity, corticomedullary differentiation, and pelvicalyceal dilatation [11]; Doppler was obtained when clinically indicated. For the purposes of this study, additional prospectively collected data on serum cortisol and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) levels at T1DM onset were included. On the first morning after admission, basal cortisol levels were measured in all patients between 8:00 and 9:00 a.m. to ensure consistency. At the time of cortisol assessment, none of the participants exhibited clinical signs suggestive of adrenal insufficiency, nor were they receiving medications known to interfere with HPA axis function. Children with major concurrent illnesses unrelated to diabetes onset (e.g., severe trauma, chronic systemic diseases, or ongoing steroid therapy) were excluded. Of the original 185 participants in DiAKIdney cohort, 32 were excluded due to missing cortisol data, resulting in a final study population of 153 participants.

Definitions

DKA was defined according to ISPAD clinical practice consensus guidelines as blood glucose level ≥ 200 mg/dL, venous pH ≤ 7.3 or serum bicarbonate ≤ 15 mEq/L, and elevation of serum ketones. DKA severity was classified as mild (pH < 7.3 or bicarbonate < 15 mmol/L), moderate (pH < 7.2 or bicarbonate < 10 mmol/L), severe (pH < 7.1 or bicarbonate < 5 mmol/L) [17]. AKI was defined based on Kidney Disease/Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria, using serum creatinine and/or urine output [22]. Prerenal-AKI (P-AKI) was defined meeting KDIGO creatinine and/or urine output criteria in combination with fractional excretion of sodium (FeNa) < 1% [23]. Renal tubular damage (RTD) was defined as the presence of at least one abnormal marker among the following: urinary beta-2-microglobulin >0.33 mg/L, NGAL values >95th percentile for age [24], tubular reabsorption of phosphate (TRP) < 85%, and FeNa >2%. TRP was calculated using the following formula: TRP (%) = 1 – [(Urinary phosphorus/Serum phosphorus) × (Serum creatinine/Urinary creatinine] [25]. Acute tubular damage (ATD) was defined as the presence of at least one of the following in the absence of AKI according to KDIGO criteria: urinary beta-2-microglobulin >0.33 mg/L [26] and/or NGAL values >95th percentile for age [24] and/or TRP < 85% [25, 27] and/ or FeNa >2% [22]. Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN) was defined by the presence of AKI according to KDIGO criteria [22] with either FENa >2% [23] or FENa between 1 and 2% combined with one or more markers of tubular injury: urinary beta-2-microglobulin >0.33 mg/L [26], NGAL values >95th percentile by age [24], TRP < 85% [25, 27]. FeNa was calculated as follows: FeNa (%) = (Urinary sodium/Urinary creatinine) / (Serum sodium/Serum creatinine) [23].

Patients’ classification

Patients were divided into three groups based on serum cortisol levels: Group 1 (< 10 µg/dL), Group 2 (10–18 µg/dL), and Group 3 (>18 µg/dL). These thresholds were pre-specified to describe the distribution of cortisol values across low, intermediate, and high levels. The cut-off of < 10 µg/dL was based on current guidelines for the diagnosis of CIRCI [14], while the threshold of >18 µg/dL reflects the conventional lower limit of an adequate adrenal response to ACTH stimulation testing [28].

Statistical analysis

A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Descriptive statistics were calculated separately for three groups based on cortisol levels (< 10 µg/dL, 10–18 µg/dL, > 18 µg/dL), with normally distributed continuous variables reported as mean ± standard deviation and non-normally distributed variables presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were expressed as percentages.

Comparisons between the three cortisol groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed continuous variables, followed by Bonferroni post-hoc tests, which are used to identify specific group differences. For non-normally distributed continuous variables, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied, with Dunn-Bonferroni post-hoc comparisons between groups. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test.

Additionally, the relationship between cortisol and ACTH levels was analyzed separately in patients with and without DKA and patients with and without AKI. Median cortisol and ACTH levels were compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney test.

To further investigate associations, correlation analyses were performed using Pearson’s test for parametric variables and Spearman’s test for non-parametric variables. Variables that remained significant after Bonferroni correction were included in multivariable regression models. Multiple linear regression was used to identify independent predictors of cortisol levels, with variables grouped into clinical, serum, and urinary factors. Log transformation was applied to non-parametric variables prior to their inclusion in univariate and multivariate analyses, in order to approximate normality. The variables included in the univariate analyses, in the multivariate analyses and then in the final model are specified in Table 6 and in the footnotes of this table.

A logistic regression model was used to explore associations between cortisol levels and the occurrence of DKA. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.

Results

General characteristics

A total of 153 children with newly diagnosed T1DM were enrolled in the study. Among them, DKA was found in 80 children (52.3%), AKI in 70 (45.8%), and RTD in 114 (74.5%). Regarding kidney involvement, 52 children (34%) presented with ATN, 18 (11.8%) with P-AKI, and 45 (29.4%) with ATD. More in detail, among the patients with DKA the prevalence of AKI, RTD, ATN, P-AKI and ATD were respectively 66.3%, 100%, 46.3%, 21.3%, and 31.3%, while among patients without DKA were respectively 23.3% (p < 0.001), 46.6% (p < 0.001), 20.5% (p = 0.001), 1.4% (p < 0.001), and 27.4% (p = 0.6). Out of the 153 enrolled children, 54 (35.3%) were Group 1 (cortisol levels < 10 µg/dL), 50 (32.7%) were in Group2 (cortisol levels between 10 and 18 µg/dL), and 49 (32%) were Group 3 (cortisol levels > 18 µg/dL). The median cortisol level in the cohort was 13.1 µg/dL (IQR = 13.7). The mean age across all groups was 8.98 years.

Group 2 had the highest proportion of males (62%) and T1DM family history (24%) (Table 1).

Group 3 showed significantly higher prevalence of DKA, greater weight loss, and increased frequency of Kussmaul breathing and coma (Table 1). These patients also had significantly higher HR and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) (Table 1).

Metabolically, Group 3 exhibited significantly lower levels of blood pH, serum bicarbonate, and phosphorus, along with significantly higher levels of serum ketones and chloride (Table 1).

With regard to kidney function, Group 3 presented significantly higher levels of urinary NGAL, β2-microglobulin, microalbuminuria, highest creatinine-to-basal creatinine ratio (HC/BC), FeNa, urinary protein to urinary creatinine ratio (UPr/UCr), and urinary calcium to urinary creatinine ratio (Table 1). TRP was significantly reduced. Proteinuria and increased renal echogenicity were more frequent in this group, although not statistical significance (Table 1).

The prevalence of ATN and RTD was significantly higher in Group 3 compared to Group 1 only (Table 1).

Regarding thyroid and hematological findings, Group 3 had significantly lower Free triiodothyronine (FT3) and free thyroxine (FT4) concentrations, and higher neutrophil counts. The prevalence of euthyroid sick syndrome was also higher, although not statistically significant (Table 1).

Patients in Group 3 showed higher HR, neutrophil count, FeNa, UPr/Cr, and microalbuminuria levels, as well as lower DBP-SDS and TRP levels. Additionally, they had a higher prevalence of HR > 2 SDS compared with patients in the other groups.

Factors correlating and independently influencing cortisol levels

Among clinical factors, cortisol positively correlated with weight loss and HR (Table 6). Among serum factors, cortisol negatively correlated with serum phosphorus, bicarbonate, pH, base excess, and estimated glomerular filtration rate, while positively correlated with hematocrit, neutrophils, ketones, and HC/BC ratio (Table 6). Among urinary factors, cortisol negatively correlated with TRP and positively with UPr/UCr, NGAL, FeNa (Table 6).

In multivariate analysis, significant independent associations were retained between cortisol and HR (clinical), pH (serum), and TRP (urinary) (Table 6). Adding all the clinical, serum and urinary factors significantly correlated with cortisol at multivariate analysis in a final multivariate model, only pH and TRP maintained a significant inverse association with cortisol levels (Table 6).

Association between cortisol levels and DKA

Logistic regression analysis adjusted for glycemia, age, gender, AKI, and RTD demonstrated a strong and statistically significant association between serum cortisol levels and the occurrence of DKA. Specifically, for each 1 µg/dL increase in cortisol, the odds of presenting with DKA increased by 1.15 times (OR = 1.15;95%CI:1.07–1.23; p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study explored the relationship between serum cortisol levels and clinical, biochemical, and renal parameters in children at the onset of T1DM. The literature on cortisol dynamics and its relation to kidney dysfunction at pediatric T1DM onset is still limited.

These findings suggest that cortisol levels at T1DM onset are strongly associated with the severity of acute metabolic decompensation, particularly in the context of DKA and systemic stress responses. Interestingly, no significant differences in the duration of polyuria or polydipsia were observed between groups, indicating that cortisol levels primarily reflect the intensity of acute metabolic stress rather than the duration of pre-existing symptoms.

Unlike prior studies that often focused only on pediatric patients with DKA or established diabetes [5–8], this design allowed a more comprehensive analysis across the entire T1DM onset spectrum. By stratifying patients by cortisol levels, the relationship between adrenal response and disease severity has been further characterized.

Notably, Group 3 (cortisol >18 µg/dL) presented with the most critical clinical picture, including more severe acidosis and a higher prevalence of acute complications, confirming cortisol role as a biomarker of acute stress and metabolic decompensation. The inverse correlation between cortisol and pH supports the view that cortisol secretion increases proportionally with the severity of acidosis, consistent with previous reports [5, 7, 8]. In the whole cohort (Table 1), higher cortisol was associated with more severe acidosis, lower bicarbonate, higher ketones, and greater prevalence of coma. In the DKA subgroup (Table 2), the same trend was observed, although without statistical significance, likely due to the smaller sample size and reduced variability.

Children in the highest cortisol group also exhibited an overall increase in biomarkers of tubular stress and impaired electrolyte handling, including markedly reduced TRP.

Interestingly, the HC/BC ratio has been identified as a significant predictor of acute tubular damage, with even mild elevations indicating early renal dysfunction in children with T1DM onset [11]. Moreover, hypophosphatemia, closely related to impaired TRP and strongly associated with DKA severity, appears to be a sensitive early indicator of tubular damage [11]. In this cohort, multivariate analyses confirmed TRP as an independent predictors of cortisol levels, further supporting the link between hypercortisolemia, impaired tubular phosphate handling, and acute metabolic stress. While these findings suggest that cortisol may contribute to kidney injury through tubular mechanisms, further research is needed to elucidate these pathways. In this context, a 2019 study reported that AKI significantly increases both plasma and urinary cortisol, potentially activating p21 gene expression in the kidney and implicating cortisol in stress-induced renal dysfunction [29].

It is well established that most children with new-onset T1DM, particularly those presenting with DKA, may develop AKI and/or RTD [30]. In this context, AKI is typically caused by dehydration and hypovolemia, and may progress to ATN in about one-third of cases; RTD is frequently observed in patients with DKA or AKI [9, 11]. Nevertheless, in this cohort, no significant differences were observed in the prevalence of AKI, RTD, ATN, P-AKI, or ATD across cortisol groups. Overall, these findings indicate that cortisol is associated with subclinical markers of tubular dysfunction rather than with overt kidney injury at T1DM onset. Importantly, this relationship appears to be largely mediated by DKA severity, as pH and TRP emerged as the strongest independent predictors of cortisol levels in multivariate models. Thus, hypercortisolemia in this context seems to reflect metabolic and acid–base derangements driving early tubular stress rather than a direct causal effect on renal injury. Furthermore, analysis of overlapping conditions showed that most ATN and RTD cases occurred in the context of DKA rather than in isolation, suggesting that renal involvement at T1DM onset is more severe in case of DKA. This is further supported by the finding of higher cortisol levels in patients with ATN (the most severe form of renal involvement) compared to those without ATN, and of lower cortisol levels in patients with P-AKI (a functional and thus mild form of AKI) compared to those without P-AKI, observed only in the DKA group (Table 4). Therefore, hypercortisolemia may mainly reflects DKA severity and its systemic consequences, while direct associations with isolated renal injury appear less pronounced.

Consistently, cortisol levels were significantly associated with DKA, but not with AKI, highlighting that adrenal response reflects metabolic instability more than renal dysfunction. Regression analysis further showed a progressive increase in DKA likelihood with rising cortisol concentrations, confirming cortisol as an independent risk factor for DKA. These results underscore its potential predictive value at T1DM onset, although further studies are required to validate its clinical utility. Notably, extremely high cortisol levels (>34 µg/dL) have been associated with an increased mortality risk in critically ill patients, emphasizing the potential prognostic relevance of marked hypercortisolemia [31, 32]. Moreover, a recent pediatric study demonstrated that basal serum cortisol levels at admission are significant predictors of DKA duration, with values >34 µg/dL associated with prolonged recovery and values < 18 µg/dL with faster resolution [8]. These findings reinforce the clinical utility of cortisol measurement, suggesting that beyond indicating DKA severity at onset, cortisol may also help anticipate disease course.

Beyond metabolic and renal implications, elevated cortisol levels were also associated with systemic stress responses, including altered vital signs, immune activation, and thyroid function changes. The observed increases in neutrophil counts align with the known effects of glucocorticoids on innate immunity, promoting neutrophil demargination and delaying apoptosis [33], which may reflect an acute inflammatory response mediated by HPA axis activation.

Similarly, the reduction in FT3 and FT4 observed in patients with high cortisol levels is consistent with a non-thyroidal illness syndrome, induced by acute stress. These findings align with previous data reporting a high prevalence of euthyroid sick syndrome in children with new-onset T1DM, especially those with DKA, AKI, and delayed recovery [34, 35]. The suppression of the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis by elevated cortisol levels likely contributes to this adaptive response, as cortisol is known to inhibit thyroid hormone production during acute illness [36].

Moreover, the associations between elevated cortisol, increased HR, and higher DBP further highlight the interaction between endocrine and cardiovascular responses in this setting. These findings are consistent with the known physiological effects of cortisol in activating the sympathetic nervous system and regulating vascular tone during acute stress [1]. Notably, HR remained independently associated with cortisol levels in multivariate analysis, underscoring its value as a dynamic clinical marker of stress. Elevated HR is also recognized as a clinical indicator of volume depletion and has been associated with severe AKI in pediatric diabetic populations [9, 11].

Of particular interest is the profile of patients in Group 1 (cortisol < 10 µg/dL), who exhibited milder clinical presentations and lower prevalence of complications. Although cortisol values in this group meet one of the diagnostic thresholds for CIRCI, no clinical signs of true adrenal insufficiency (e.g., hypotension unresponsive to fluid resuscitation or overt severity of illness) were observed [14]. In contrast, in critical care settings, CIRCI has been associated with poor outcomes, including increased inflammation, prolonged intensive care unit stay, and higher mortality [1, 14]. The present findings are consistent with previous pediatric studies that, although adopting a higher cortisol cut-off (< 18 µg/dL), similarly reported higher pH and bicarbonate levels and shorter DKA duration in children with lower cortisol values, without adverse clinical outcomes [7, 8].

The fact that not all patients in this cohort presented with critical clinical conditions may explain the differences observed compared to the literature on CIRCI.

Interestingly, during follow-up, none of the enrolled patients developed adrenal insufficiency, confirming that T1DM is rarely associated with Addison’s disease [37, 38] and underscoring that CIRCI is fundamentally different from Addison’s disease, with distinct pathophysiological mechanisms. These findings suggest that low cortisol levels at T1DM onset may represent a transient adaptive response to mild disease rather than true adrenal insufficiency. In this light, although our Group 1 patients exhibited low cortisol without adverse outcomes, careful attention to adrenal function in children with T1DM remains warranted, particularly during acute metabolic decompensation, when adequate stress responses could be critical.

A growing body of evidence suggests that diabetes is linked to a complex dysregulation pattern of HPA axis. Both human and animal studies have shown a pattern of chronic hyperactivation, particularly in patients with long-standing diabetes, and a reduced ability to mount an appropriate stress response [39–43]. In line with this, ACTH stimulation tests in pediatric T1DM populations have demonstrated blunted adrenal responsiveness even without adrenal autoantibodies, pointing toward a functional hypo-responsiveness of the adrenal cortex [44]. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether such patients have altered long-term HPA axis function.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study. Moreover, AKI was defined using serum creatinine, which may be affected by confounders in the context of DKA (e.g., acetoacetates, hyperglycemia, and HbA1c) [45–47]. Nevertheless, serum creatinine remains the standard marker of renal function in daily clinical practice, including in patients with DKA. Another limitation concerns systemic inflammation associated with DKA, which could increase urinary NGAL levels independently of true renal injury [48]. However, the inclusion of additional urinary biomarkers (i.e., urinary β2 microglobulin) likely improved the specificity of the assessment of tubular damage. In addition, cortisol levels may be influenced by procedural stress during venipuncture [49], which could have affected the absolute values observed. Adrenal autoantibodies and ACTH stimulation tests were not performed, limiting the ability to fully characterize adrenal reserve. Nevertheless, given the well-documented adaptive HPA axis alterations in T1DM [44], their absence is unlikely to alter the interpretation of these findings. Other adrenal hormones were not evaluated, further restricting the comprehensive assessment of adrenal function. A formal sample size calculation and power analysis were not performed, as this study included all consecutive patients from the DiAKIdney cohort during the study period. Nevertheless, the absence of a priori power calculation may limit the strength of inference from multivariable regression analyses, particularly for subgroup comparisons. Finally, given the cross-sectional design of this study, the observed associations between cortisol levels and renal or metabolic parameters cannot establish causality, and the direction of these relationships remains uncertain. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether elevated cortisol contributes to renal dysfunction or primarily reflects the severity of metabolic stress.

Conclusions

This observational cross-sectional study shows that elevated cortisol levels at T1DM onset are strongly associated with the presence and severity of DKA, confirming their role as a reliable biomarker of acute metabolic stress rather than of kidney complications.

Although less pronounced, associations with tubular markers suggest a potential link between cortisol dynamics and early tubular stress.

In clinical practice, low cortisol levels at T1DM onset should be interpreted cautiously and in context, as they were not associated with adverse outcomes in this cohort. Nonetheless, monitoring adrenal function in children with T1DM is advisable, both to assess stress response adequacy during acute decompensation and in light of their increased susceptibility to autoimmune comorbidities.

Future studies are needed to clarify the complex interplay between cortisol dynamics and renal outcomes, and to assess whether cortisol-guided risk stratification could be effectively complementing established measures such as pH and bicarbonate in predicting DKA severity. In particular, serum cortisol could potentially serve as an early marker to identify children at greater risk of severe DKA and metabolic decompensation, thereby guiding closer monitoring and more intensive management strategies at diagnosis. Therefore, prospective multicenter validation is required before its integration into routine care.
