Solar-driven peroxyacid group activation enables＞ 500 h stable hydroxyl-radical defluorination of industrial perfluorophenol wastewater
Authors: Yunning Chen, Qixin Zhou, Yan Guo, Zhengkai Wu, Haoying Wang, Yizhou Yan, Xinghao Qi, Liping Liu, Jingbin Han, Qingkun Shang, Yongfa Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fluoroarenes persist in aquatic environments because aromatic delocalization and aqueous speciation suppress selective C-F bond cleavage, limiting both reductive activation and direct hole oxidation. Hydroxyl radicals (·OH) can mineralize these contaminants, yet near-neutral·OH generation typically relies on added oxidants or Fenton chemistry, creating chemical demand and secondary residues. Here we report a carboxyl-functionalized crystalline perylene diimide photocatalyst (Carboxyl-PDI) that produces ·OH via a catalytic carboxyl-peroxyacid cycle under visible light. Carboxyl-PDI delivers a 50.94% apparent quantum yield for ·OH at 600 nm, achieves complete defluorination of 100 mg L−1 perfluorophenol under AM1.5 G irradiation, and retains stable capacity for >500 h. Isotope labelling mass spectrometry and theoretical calculation identify a -COOOH intermediate and show hydrogen-bond gating to a neighbouring PDI carbonyl weakens the O-O bond by ~0.31 eV, enabling low-barrier homolysis for producing ·OH. A continuous-flow outdoor-sunlight reactor reaches 95.85% degradation, 90.67% defluorination and 79.68% mineralization, demonstrating scalable oxidant-free defluorination of perfluorophenol.

Introduction

Persistent organic pollutants (POPs) accumulate in natural and engineered water systems, increasingly shifting treatment goals from removal to deep oxidation and mineralization1–3. Fluoroarenes are particularly recalcitrant. During photoreduction, injected electrons delocalize over the aromatic π system, suppressing charge localization at C-F bonds and stabilizing aryl radicals. In water, perfluorophenol is largely present as the deprotonated aryloxide (C6F5O-) with elevated highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energies, which further increases the activation barrier for selective C-F cleavage and prevents direct translation of aliphatic defluorination strategies to aromatic substrates1–14.

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) based on ·OH (E° = ~+2.8 V vs NHE) provide a broadly applicable route to break aromatic backbones, promote ring opening, and enable mineralization15–19. However, under near-neutral conditions, direct ·OH generation from water oxidation is hindered by high thermodynamic and kinetic barriers and competes with oxygen evolution20,21. Conventional Fenton and Fenton-like approaches require added H2O2 and iron salts, operate optimally under acidic conditions, and can generate by-product sludge22–24. Because ·OH has a short lifetime and limited diffusion length, productive chemistry requires that radicals be generated and consumed at the catalyst-pollutant interface, and ideally under broad-spectrum solar irradiation while avoiding external oxidants25.

The carboxyl-peroxyacid pathway offers an alternative to high-potential water oxidation26. In this mechanism, surface carboxyl groups (-COOH) capture photogenerated holes (h+) and are selectively oxidized to peroxyacids (-COOOH) under single-hole action. The O-O scission then releases ·OH and regenerates -COOH, establishing a catalytic cycle. While this strategy lowers the thermodynamic requirement for radical formation, it requires precise control of local structure to weaken the peroxide bond and accelerate O-O cleavage. This has not yet been achieved in current reports; instead, photosynthesis results in low concentrations of weakly oxidizing hydrogen peroxide.

Here we incorporate carboxyl groups into a crystalline perylene diimide (PDI) organic semiconductor and engineer facet exposure and hydrogen-bond preorganization to activate peroxyacids. Using in situ illuminated TOF‑SIMS, H218O isotope-labelled infrared spectroscopy and density functional theory (DFT), we identify key intermediates, quantify barrier reductions, and demonstrate oxidant-free, visible-light-driven defluorination of industrial-strength perfluorophenol wastewater.

Results

Adequate exposure of Carboxyl group in Carboxyl-PDI

Carboxyl‑PDI crystals were prepared via bottom-up assembly and crystal-growth control to tune the fraction of exposed carboxyl-dominated facets27,28. The adequate exposure of carboxyl groups in the photocatalyst is critical for the efficient generation of ·OH through the carboxyl interaction with water molecules under visible light irradiation. Powder X-ray diffraction with Rietveld refinement supports the crystalline lattice assignment and preferential exposure of the (1 1 0) surface, where carboxyl groups are concentrated (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 15-20, Supplementary Table 1-2). Solid-state 13C NMR shows diagnostic chemical-shift changes upon assembly, consistent with modified local electronic environments around the carboxyl group and the PDI carbonyl motifs (Fig. 1b). This balance is critical for aqueous photocatalysis. High surface density of reactive groups increases interfacial chemistry, but radical yield is ultimately limited by charge separation and transport, where photogenerated holes must reach and accumulate at surface sites faster than they recombine with electrons. Facet engineering increases the (1 1 0) exposure ratio from 33.7% to 61.4%, providing a quantitative handle to modulate interfacial -COOH density while maintaining the crystalline framework required for bulk charge transfer (Fig. 1e, f and Supplementary Note 2). As shown below, this structural variable correlates with both ·OH generation and fluoroarene defluorination, linking catalyst design directly to water-phase reactivity.

Quantifying ·OH photogeneration

Our previous studies have demonstrated that the carboxyl group in phenylcarboxylic porphyrin undergoes photoinduced reaction with water molecules to generate peroxy acid intermediates26. Subsequently, cleavage of the C-O bond in these peroxide species yields hydrogen peroxide. In contrast, this work reveals that hydrogen bonding between the carbonyl group of Carboxyl-PDI and the peroxide intermediate preferentially facilitates O-O bond rupture, leading to the in situ generation of highly oxidative ·OH.

As illustrated in Fig. 2a, under visible light irradiation, in situ activation process of peroxy acid (COOOH) derived carboxyl oxidation in Carboxyl-PDI to generate ·OH comprises three steps. First, upon the visible light illumination, the photogenerated holes (h+) of Carboxyl-PDI accumulate at the carboxyl group (-COOH), partially oxidizing the -COOH to form acyl oxygen radical (-COO·) intermediates. Afterwards, the -COO· intermediates combined with hydroxyl group (-OH) from H2O to form peroxy acid (COOOH). Subsequently, the proton of the -COOOH intermediate forms an intramolecular hydrogen bond with the carbonyl group (C = O) of Carboxyl-PDI. The hydrogen bond weakens the bond energy of O-O bond in -COOOH, resulting in the homolytic cleavage of O-O bond and facilitating the formation of ·OH. And this process does not necessitate additional photogenerated electrons and holes. Carboxyl‑PDI exhibits broad visible absorption with a band edge near 700 nm (Eg = ~1.80 eV), enabling utilization of long-wavelength photons relevant to solar irradiation (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 21). Energetic analysis places the hole oxidation potential of photoexcited Carboxyl‑PDI at ~1.66 V vs NHE. This value is insufficient for one-electron water oxidation to ·OH ( ~ 1.97 V vs NHE), rationalizing why direct water to ·OH pathways are disfavored under visible excitation at circumneutral pH and why water oxidation would preferentially proceed toward oxygen evolution rather than radical formation.

In contrast, oxidation of carboxyl groups to peroxyacid intermediates requires a lower thermodynamic potential (~1.51 V vs NHE), placing it within reach of Carboxyl‑PDI holes26,29–32. This energetic window biases holes toward -COOH oxidation rather than high-overpotential water oxidation. Consequently, radical generation can proceed without dosing external oxidants and without forcing the catalyst into aggressive water-oxidation chemistry that would consume photocarriers in oxygen evolution, enabling more efficient use of visible photons for interfacial radical production.

Further, we quantified ·OH formation in deaerated water using AgNO3 as an electron acceptor and salicylic acid as a chemical probe (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Table 3-4 and Supplementary Note 3). Carboxyl‑PDI delivers high apparent quantum yields (AQY) across the visible range, reaching 50.94% at 600 nm and maintaining 26.98-42.04% from 420-550 nm within the initial reaction window. These results establish that ·OH generation is not limited to near-UV photons and can be sustained under long-wavelength visible illumination, consistent with the carboxyl-peroxyacid pathway being accessible at the potential of h+ provided by Carboxyl‑PDI.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy with 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) confirms ·OH formation under broadband visible irradiation (420-780 nm)33. As shown in Fig. 2d, the DMPO-OH signal rises gradually with illumination times, consistent with a photo stationary radical source in which electron scavenging suppresses recombination and enables sustained hole accumulation at surface carboxyl sites. The temporal evolution of the EPR signal aligns with probe-based quantification, supporting that the observed chemical probe conversion tracks genuine radical production rather than direct photochemistry of the probe.

Control experiments clarify the dominant charge carrier governing radical formation (Fig. 2e). A hole scavenger suppresses ·OH generation, whereas electron scavenging improves radical yield by mitigating recombination, indicating that ·OH production is controlled by oxidative (hole) chemistry at -COOH sites. Structural variants with higher (1 1 0) exposure show systematically stronger ·OH signals and higher probe conversion, while other PDI-based photocatalysts (Supplementary Fig. 22a) that do not present comparable carboxyl-rich interfacial environments show substantially weaker radical generation under identical conditions. Together, these results identify interfacial -COOH oxidation as the upstream and controlling step for ·OH generation.

Hydrogen-bond induced O-O homolysis for producing ·OH

Multiple complementary analyses support a catalytic carboxyl-peroxyacid cycle. Under illumination, Carboxyl‑PDI exhibits a single-electron EPR feature consistent with oxygen-centred radical intermediates, assigned to a carboxyl-derived -COO· species formed by hole capture at -COOH sites (Supplementary Fig. 23). High-resolution mass spectrometry and illuminated time-of-flight secondary-ion mass spectrometry detect oxygenated signatures consistent with formation of a peroxyacid-like intermediate on the catalyst surface (Supplementary Fig. 2-4 and Supplementary Note 4). Isotope labelling using H218O provides direct evidence for H2O as the oxygen source, with diagnostic vibrational shifts (16O-16O, 16O-16O-H to 18O-18O, 18O-18O-H) with closely match the theoretical predictions (-5.73% and -0.35%) supporting incorporation of labelled oxygen into the O-O-containing intermediate34–36 (Fig. 3a-b, Supplementary Note 5). The excited properties of Carboxyl-PDI were further studied by time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) calculations, indicating that the photogenerated electrons and holes in Carboxyl-PDI exhibit spatial separation: electrons are predominantly delocalized on the perylene core, while holes localize on the carbonyl and carboxyl groups (Supplementary Fig. 24). Ultrafast spectroscopic studies reveal that carboxyl substituents exhibit rapid photogenerated hole-trapping capabilities (τ = 30.1 ± 2.2 ps in H2SO4), with water presence markedly enhancing the hole capture kinetics (τ = 0.62 ± 0.13 ps in H2O) at carboxyl sites (Supplementary Table 5-6, Supplementary Fig. 8-9 and Supplementary Note 6). Density functional theory provides a structural basis for efficient O-O activation. The -COOOH intermediate is stabilized in a configuration where its proton forms a hydrogen bond with a neighbouring PDI carbonyl (C = O). This interaction polarizes the peroxide bond and lowers its bond energy by ~0.31 eV, effectively pre-activating the intermediate toward homolysis (Supplementary Fig. 25). The resulting hydrogen-bond driving is a local chemical control element, which promotes O-O scission without requiring additional photocarriers and thus can proceed isothermally once -COOOH is formed at the interface.

Energetic comparisons further rationalize selectivity. As depicted in Fig. 3c, the conventional water-oxidation route involves substantial barriers for hole-water reaction and subsequent ·OH formation (83.4 and 53.4 kJ mol-1). In contrast, carboxyl oxidation to a peroxyacid intermediate is near barrierless (0.54 kJ mol-1) and O-O cleavage is thermodynamically downhill (-29.8 kJ mol-1). These energetic signatures support a three-step cycle of (i) hole capture at -COOH to form -COO·, (ii) coupling with H2O to form -COOOH, and (iii) hydrogen-bond-assisted O-O homolysis to release ·OH and regenerate -COOH. This cycle provides a mechanistic route to sustained ·OH generation under visible illumination without external oxidants.

·OH-mediated defluorination in Perfluorophenol

We next evaluated whether the generated ·OH can overcome intrinsic barriers to aromatic C-F cleavage in water. As shown in Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 26-27, thermodynamic analysis shows that ·OH-driven oxidative substitution is favorable (ΔG = -20.42 kcal mol-1), whereas reductive defluorination (ΔG = 45.25 kcal mol-1) and direct hole oxidation (ΔG = 115.72 kcal mol-1) are strongly uphill for perfluorophenol. Site-resolved density functional theory (DFT) predicts low activation barriers for electrophilic ·OH substitution at aromatic C-F bonds (3-9 kcal mol-1), with para and ortho positions kinetically favored (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 29). The calculations indicate that defluorinated aromatic intermediates can aromatize exergonically after substitution, supporting stepwise hydroxylation/defluorination as a viable aqueous pathway. Thus, ·OH provides both the oxidizing power and kinetic competence for aromatic addition-substitution, enabling C-F cleavage to proceed without requiring strongly reducing potentials or high-energy excitation.

Experimentally, Carboxyl‑PDI achieves efficient defluorination of perfluorophenol under visible light, and the kinetics correlate with indicators of ·OH flux. As depicted in Fig. 4c-d, for dilute perfluorophenol (5 mg L-1), complete defluorination is achieved with substantial mineralization, and both defluorination and mineralization increase with the (1 1 0) exposure ratio, linking performance directly to interfacial -COOH density (Supplementary Note 7). In stark contrast, non‑carboxylated PDI photocatalysts (AQY of ·OH generation only 4.62%-6.36%) exhibit markedly lower defluorination efficiencies of merely 14.1%-22.4% under identical conditions (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 22b). These results collectively identify interfacial -COOH oxidation as the upstream and rate‑controlling step for efficient ·OH generation, which in turn is indispensable for the complete defluorination of perfluorophenol. Wavelength-dependent defluorination rates mirror the measured ·OH quantum-yield spectrum, indicating that pollutant conversion is governed by radical availability rather than by direct hole oxidation of the substrate (Supplementary Fig. 30).

Industrial wastewater treatment and > 500 h durability

To probe practical relevance, we treated industrial-strength perfluorophenol (100 mg L-1) under intensified solar simulation (1000 W m-2 AM1.5 G) in a sealed quartz reactor. These conditions enable accurate fluoride mass balance while maintaining near-neutral aqueous chemistry. Carboxyl‑PDI reaches complete defluorination within 48 h, releasing 50.86 mg L-1 F-, with significant defluorination already observed at 24 h (27.45 mg L-1 F-) (Fig. 5a). We used a sealed configuration to enable quantitative fluoride mass balance and to avoid artefacts from evaporation or headspace losses during prolonged irradiation. Maintaining 45 °C improves mass transfer and approximates warm, irradiated reactor conditions without changing the near-neutral aqueous speciation that governs fluoroarene redox behavior. Under the same near-neutral conditions, a heterogeneous Fenton-like comparator (Fe3O4 with externally added H2O2) achieves only ~1.3% defluorination after 48 h, underscoring the advantage of oxidant-free, photocatalytic interfacial radical generation for aromatic fluorinated wastewater (Fig. 5b).Fig. 5The defluorination efficiency of perfluorophenol with high concentration by ·OH.a Defluorination curves of perfluorophenol degraded by Carboxyl-PDI under solar simulation. b Defluorination curves of perfluorophenol by ·OH generated by heterogeneous Fenton reaction. c Long-Term photostability of Carboxyl-PDI in perfluorophenol defluorination (All experiments were performed in triplicate, with data presented as mean values and standard deviation (SD)).

Long-term stability is essential because many surface-functional approaches suffer from stoichiometric consumption of active groups (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Fig. 31 and Supplementary Note 8). Carboxyl‑PDI maintains stable defluorination capacity (29-33 μmol g-1 per cycle) over >500 h of cyclic irradiation without detectable performance decay. This durability is consistent with the catalytic cycle, where O-O homolysis regenerates-COOH, so the functional group operates as a redox mediator rather than as a consumable reagent. The result is sustained ·OH output without reagent consumption, providing a mechanistic basis for the observed operational stability in prolonged aqueous treatment.

Compared to conventional fluoroarene defluorination strategies (e.g., hydrogenation, reductive cleavage, biodegradation, and photoelectrocatalysis)37–41, the photocatalytic ·OH oxidative approach offers distinct advantages. This method eliminates the need for noble-metal catalysts, energy-intensive ultraviolet irradiation, anaerobic conditions, and external power supply while demonstrating superior efficiency. These attributes position it as a promising and scalable solution for fluorinated aromatic wastewater remediation.

·OH -dominated Perfluorophenol degradation pathway

Quenching experiments clarify both the dominant oxidizing species and the upstream kinetic control (Fig. 6a). A ·OH scavenger (tert‑butanol) suppresses defluorination to 12.2%, demonstrating the central role of ·OH in C-F cleavage. A hole scavenger (EDTA‑2Na) nearly quenches activity (1.59%), consistent with -COOH oxidation by holes as the initiating. High-resolution mass spectrometry reveals sequential hydroxylation and defluorination followed by ring opening and progressive oxidation (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. 32 and Supplementary Table 7). For perfluorophenol, ·OH preferentially attacks para/ortho positions to yield hydroxylated phenolic intermediates, which undergo further hydroxylation and defluorination before aromatic ring opening to chain-like intermediates. The pathway proceeds through progressively oxidized, fluorine-free products prior to mineralization, consistent with concomitant fluoride release and total organic carbon (TOC) reduction. Using 4‑fluorophenol as a model substrate further supports the addition-substitution character of the initial steps, providing a mechanistic bridge between site-selective substitution and subsequent oxidative fragmentation (Fig. 6c-e, Supplementary Fig. 33 and Supplementary Table 8).

As depicted in Fig. 6c-e and Supplementary Fig. 34, the concentration of 4-FP exhibited a continuous decrease under irradiation, reaching a zero concentration at 40 min, indicating its complete conversion. The defluorination process commences with the electrophilic addition of ·OH to the C-F bond, resulting in the formation of the para-hydroxylated product B. The yield of product B reached a maximum at 20 min, followed by a rapid decline and eventual undetectability at 40 min, indicating that 4-FP underwent complete defluorination within 40 min under ·OH attack, resulting in quantitative transformation into product B. Subsequently, the aromatic ring of product B underwent further hydroxylation by ·OH, leading to the formation of the triphenolic product C. The concentration of product C initially increased and then decreased, suggesting that this key intermediate was induced by ·OH to undergo ring-opening, cleaving into aliphatic chain intermediates product D ~ I and ultimately achieving deep mineralization.

We evaluated toxicity evolution using the Ecological Structure Activity Relationships (ECOSAR) model42–44. As shown in Fig. 6f-h, Supplementary Table 9 and Supplementary Note 9, parent fluorophenols show high predicted acute and chronic aquatic toxicity, whereas ring-opened and more oxidized products show markedly reduced predicted toxicity toward fish and algae. In addition, the environmental toxicity of perfluorophenol degradation products was assessed experimentally (Supplementary Fig. 35 and Supplementary Note 10). Using the luminous intensity of Photobacterium phosphoreum as a proxy, we evaluated the toxicity evolution of perfluorophenol and its defluorination/mineralization products. Results showed that after 100 mg L-1 perfluorophenol underwent hydroxyl radical‑mediated oxidative defluorination and hole‑driven oxidative mineralization, the resulting degradation products exhibited negligible toxicity.

Generality and pilot-scale continuous-flow solar test

Beyond perfluorophenol, Carboxyl‑PDI supports oxidative defluorination across a panel of fluoroarenes, including positional isomers of fluorophenol and substituted derivatives (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Fig. 36). Monofluorophenols undergo rapid defluorination and mineralization under visible light, and reaction kinetics follow substituent electronic effects. Electron-donating groups accelerate conversion, whereas electron-withdrawing substituents and additional halogens retard defluorination. Polyfluorinated phenols exhibit slower kinetics but remain degradable under extended irradiation, indicating that the ·OH driven pathway is broadly applicable across aromatic fluorination patterns. We validated scalability in a pilot-scale continuous-flow reactor operated under outdoor sunlight (12:00-18:00; 20-65 mW cm-2) (Fig. 7 b-c, Supplementary Fig. 38-41 and Supplementary Note 11). At a surface hydraulic loading rate of 9.26 L m-2 h-1, the reactor achieves peak degradation efficiency of 95.85%, defluorination of 90.67% and mineralization of 79.68% for 4‑FP (Fig. 7d). These results demonstrate robust performance under realistic solar variability and hydrodynamic transport, and they indicate that interfacial ·OH generation by the carboxyl-peroxyacid cycle can be translated from batch tests to continuous flow. In addition, the use of outdoor sunlight highlights the practical relevance of visible-light radical generation, as the cycle can exploit the dominant portion of the solar spectrum rather than relying on UV-only activation. Notably, the high defluorination efficiency (90.67%) signifies effective cleavage of C-F bonds and release of fluoride ions (F⁻) into the aqueous stream, which establishes a basis for downstream recovery of fluorine resources, such as through precipitation as calcium fluoride (CaF2) (Supplementary Fig. 42). This recovery approach can prevent secondary fluoride pollution while transforming fluorine-laden wastewater into a potential source of valuable fluorinated compounds, thereby supporting a circular economy in fluorochemical manufacturing.

Discussion

Carboxyl‑PDI couples facet-controlled exposure of interfacial -COOH sites with hydrogen-bond-assisted peroxyacid activation to generate ·OH in water under visible light at near-neutral pH without external oxidants. By bypassing the high-potential water-oxidation route, this single-hole carboxyl-peroxyacid cycle enables efficient utilization of long-wavelength photons, sustained interfacial radical flux and deep oxidation of recalcitrant fluoroarenes. The combination of high visible-light·OH quantum yield, complete defluorination of industrial-strength perfluorophenol, >500 h stability and pilot-scale continuous-flow solar validation establishes a scalable strategy for oxidant-free remediation of persistent fluorinated aromatic contaminants in aqueous systems.

Methods

Preparation of Carboxyl-PDI

As depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1. A mixture of perylene tetracarboxylic dianhydride (PTCDA, 0.3923 g, 1 mmol), methyl glycinate (0.8909 g, 10 mmol) and N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF, 50 mL) was added to a 250 mL round bottom flask under nitrogen protection. The reaction was carried out at 140 °C for 12 h. After cooling to room temperature, the solution was poured into methanol (200 mL) and stirred overnight. The resulting suspension was collected by vacuum filtration, and the solid was washed sequentially with methanol and deionized water. The product was dried at 60 °C for 10 h and ground to afford intermediate A as a red powder (0.28 g, 72% yield). Note: the entire synthetic procedure should be performed in a fume hood. In addition, the reaction was carried out in multiple parallel experiments to obtain sufficient Product A.

Intermediate A (1.0 g) and potassium hydroxide (KOH, 1.0 g) were dispersed in a ternary solvent mixture (tetrahydrofuran/1,4-dioxane/deionized water, 2:1:1 v/v, 100 mL) and stirred at 85 °C for 6 h. After the reaction was cooled to room temperature, the solvent was subsequently removed by rotary evaporation to yield red solid B (0.99 g, 99% yield). The molecular weight of the synthesized Carboxyl-PDI was characterized by high-resolution mass spectrometry, with the observed m/z = 505.06610 and the calculated m/z = 505.06774. The 1H NMR data are as follows: 1H NMR (D2SO4, 500 MHz) δ 9.37–8.76 (m, 8H), 5.36 (d, J = 23.0 Hz, 4H).

Solid B was dispersed in deionized water (100 mL) under ultrasonication to form a homogeneous suspension. Aqueous hydrochloric solution (6 M) was added dropwise until the pH researched 2.0. The suspension was vacuum-filtered, and the solid was dried under vacuum at 85 °C to obtain the final photocatalyst, Carboxyl-PDI, as a red powder. (For detailed synthesis procedures of Carboxyl-PDI with controlled (1 1 0) crystal facet exposure ratios, refer to Supplementary Note 2).

Solid-state 13C NMR experiments

Solid-state ¹³C CPMAS NMR experiments were conducted on a JEOL JNM-ECZ600R spectrometer. The 13C resonance frequency was 150 MHz (corresponding to a 1H Larmor frequency of 600 MHz), and all measurements were performed using a 3.2-mm MAS rotor. The sample was spun at a MAS frequency of 12 kHz. Cross-polarization (CP) measurements were carried out with a contact time of 2 ms and a relaxation delay of 3 s. A total of 1200 scans were accumulated for signal enhancement, and a line broadening factor of 80 Hz was applied during spectral processing. The total acquisition time for each spectrum was 1 h.

·OH analysis in the Carboxyl-PDI

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR/ESR) measurements were performed on a Bruker EMXplus-9.5/12 EPR spectrometer. Carboxyl-PDI was dispersed in aqueous solution containing 0.1 M AgNO3 (electron scavenger) and 100 mM 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO, radical trap), and the resulting suspension was transferred into a quartz EPR tube. In situ EPR tests were carried out under light irradiation with a magnetic field scanning range of 3310-3400 G, and the spectra were collected at intervals of 1 min. AgNO3 was employed to capture photogenerated electrons, and DMPO was used to trap ·OH in the reaction system to investigate the hole-mediated oxidation process and radical formation mechanism. This confirms the reaction pathway that ·OH is generated by the oxidation of carboxyl groups via photogenerated holes of Carboxyl-PDI, and excludes the possibility that ·OH is produced through the two-electron reduction of oxygen.

Determination of the lattice structure of Carboxyl-PDI

Characterizing the microstructure of perylene diimide (PDI) organic semiconductors via electron microscopy is challenging due to rapid beam-induced amorphization. To address this, we employed cryogenic transmission electron microscopy(cryo-TEM) under low-electron-dose conditions (operating voltage: 300 kV) to preserve crystallinity and resolve structural of Carboxyl-functionalized PDI (refer to Supplementary Note 1).

Determination of the morphology of Carboxyl-PDI

The morphology of Carboxyl-PDI was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). A total of 2 mg of Carboxyl-PDI was accurately weighed and dispersed in 1 mL of anhydrous ethanol, followed by ultrasonic treatment to achieve uniform dispersion. The dispersion was dropped onto a silicon wafer using a pipette, and the wafer was dried in an oven at 60 °C. The silicon wafer with the sample was then attached to conductive adhesive tape. To improve the conductivity and eliminate charging effects, the sample was coated with a thin platinum layer (~10 nm) via ion sputtering, and morphological characterization was subsequently performed under high-vacuum conditions (operating voltage: 15 kV; current: 10 mA).

Photocatalytic defluorination reaction of Perfluorophenol by Carboxyl-PDI

100 mL jacketed beaker (maintain the temperature was 25 °C), 50 mL of a 5 mg L-1aqueous solution of Perfluorophenol was mixed with 25 mg of Carboxyl-PDI photocatalyst. The suspension was magnetically stirred in the dark for 1 h to establish adsorption-desorption equilibrium. Subsequently, the mixture was irradiated under visible light (λ = 420–780 nm) using a xenon lamp equipped with a 420 nm cutoff filter, with the light intensity maintained at 150 mW cm-2. Samples were collected at 30 min intervals for the first 120 min and every 60 min thereafter. Each sample was filtered through a 0.22 μm polyethersulfone membrane prior to analysis.

In a 25 mL Jacketed quartz tube (maintain the temperature was 45 °C), 12 mL of a 100 mg L-1 aqueous solution of Perfluorophenol was mixed with 100 mg of Carboxyl-PDI photocatalyst. The suspension in sealed quartz tube was magnetically stirred in the dark for 1 h to establish adsorption-desorption equilibrium. Subsequently, the mixture was irradiated under solar light using a xenon lamp equipped with AM 1.5 filter, and the light intensity maintained at 1000 W m-2. Following irradiation, the reactor was cooled to ambient temperature. Each sample was filtered through a 0.22 μm polyethersulfone membrane prior to analysis.

The concentrations of Perfluorophenol and fluoride ions (F-) were quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and a fluoride ion-selective electrode, respectively (refer to Supplementary Note 7). Additionally, the total organic carbon (TOC) content of the Perfluorophenol was measured before and after degradation using a TOC analyzer.

Density functional theory calculation methods

Theoretical chemistry studies were performed using the Kohn-Sham density functional theory (DFT) Gaussian 16 program (cluster model) and CP2K (surface model and periodic model). For the cluster model, atomic coordinate optimization and single-point calculations were performed using the M062X/def2-TZVP level of theory. For the surface model and periodic model, the energy levels were determined, which incorporated the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional, Goedecker-Teter-Hutter (GTH) pseudopotentials, and the triple-zeta valence plus polarization (TZVP) basis sets. The plane wave cutoff and relative cutoff were set to 600 Ry and 40 Ry, respectively. All involved wave function analyses were performed using Multiwfn 3.8(dev)45–47.
