Co-developing a vocational rehabilitation intervention for individuals with chronic low back pain across a regional spine centre and three municipalities in Denmark: a three-stage intervention development study guided by the Medical Research Council framework
Authors: Gitte Frydenlund, Ole Steen Mortensen, Søren O’Neill, Jens Søndergaard, Anders Hansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo co-develop the Stay-At-Work (SAW) intervention, a person-centred, interdisciplinary, cross-sectoral vocational rehabilitation (VR) programme for individuals on, or at risk of, sick leave due to chronic low back pain (CLBP) and to advance methodological understanding of how evidence, qualitative insights, stakeholder input and programme theory can be systematically integrated and operationalised into concrete intervention components.DesignA three-stage intervention development study, guided by the Medical Research Council framework for complex interventions, involving evidence review and stakeholder consultation (previously reported); multistakeholder co-design; and prototyping in clinical settings.SettingThe intervention was developed within a Danish public healthcare and welfare context, involving a secondary care spinal service, municipal employment services (n=3), municipal rehabilitation services (n=3) and general practice.ParticipantsStage 1: 17 individuals on long-term sick leave due to CLBP participated in interviews and focus groups. Stages 2 and 3: The co-design group included individuals living with CLBP (n=2); clinicians from a secondary care spine service (n=2) and municipal rehabilitation services (n=6); general practitioners (n=2); municipal employment service professionals (n=3) and local managers (n=3); and employer and employee representatives (n=2). Participants were purposively recruited based on their roles and experience in VR and return-to-work processes and contributed across different stages of intervention development.ResultsThe SAW intervention consists of eight structured activities, developed through multistakeholder co-design and underpinned by a programme theory with four core mechanisms: (1) strengthened interprofessional and cross-sectoral understanding and collaboration; (2) stronger alignment around functional capacity and work ability; (3) addressing psychological vulnerability through systematic screening and support; and (4) enhanced coordination of care and rehabilitation efforts across services.Stakeholder input from the co-design group informed the intervention’s content and procedures, thereby improving alignment with local contexts and strengthening cross-sector collaboration. Some proposals were excluded due to inconsistency with clinical guidelines.ConclusionThis study demonstrates how a structured, theory-informed co-development process can be used to integrate evidence, stakeholder input and programme theory into a coherent, cross-sectoral VR intervention. The approach enhanced transparency, supported alignment with real-world practice, and provides methodological insight to strengthen the reproducibility and transferability of complex interventions.Registration number: The Region of Southern Denmark’s Research Registry (journal no. 23/44927).Keywords: Chronic Pain, REHABILITATION MEDICINE, Health Services, PUBLIC HEALTH, Occupational Health Services

Background

Systematic and transparent development processes are widely recognised as essential for rigorous complex health interventions.1,4 However, empirical reporting of intervention development remains limited and inconsistent. This gap is especially pronounced within multiprofessional vocational rehabilitation (VR) interventions for individuals with chronic low back pain (CLBP), where operationalisation of collaborative processes across professional and sectoral boundaries is rarely made explicit.5 6

CLBP is a leading cause of long-term sick leave, reduced productivity and labour market exclusion.7 Consequently, healthcare and welfare systems face demands to deliver effective and sustainable return-to-work (RTW) support for people affected by CLBP.8

Qualitative studies have identified fragmented coordination among stakeholders as a significant barrier to RTW in chronic pain conditions, including LBP.9 10 These challenges often arise from conflicting professional paradigms, different organisational goals and legislative boundaries, hindering the development of tailored rehabilitation plans.10 11 RTW policies and service structures vary across countries, shaping how roles and responsibilities are distributed and experienced.12 In Denmark, people with CLBP must simultaneously navigate healthcare providers, employers, municipal employment services and sometimes private insurers, placing a coordination burden on them at a vulnerable time. Making such contextual arrangements explicit is essential to enable assessment of transferability and to support adaptation of cross-sectoral VR models across welfare systems.

While multiprofessional biopsychosocial rehabilitation can reduce pain and disability in people with CLBP,8 existing evidence offers limited guidance on how such programmes should be organised to support RTW.6 8 13 14 Clinical guidelines recommend involvement of multiple professionals,15,17 yet provide little direction on how collaboration should be structured across healthcare, employment and rehabilitation services. Interdisciplinary collaboration, characterised by shared goals, joint planning and coordinated communication, has been linked to more coherent rehabilitation pathways and efficient RTW processes.18 19 Thus, the central challenge lies not in whether multiple professionals are involved, but in how their collaboration is structured, justified and operationalised within VR pathways.

Despite these recommendations, the CLBP literature provides limited specification of how collaboration is operationalised during intervention development and delivery.6 Reports of VR interventions typically enumerate the professional groups involved, but seldom detail how decisions are made, responsibilities are delineated or coordination across sectors is enacted in practice.6 Furthermore, intervention development studies rarely articulate the hypothesised mechanisms of change or explicate how stakeholder-derived knowledge is systematically translated into discrete intervention components.4 5 20 Consequently, the developmental logic underpinning cross-sectoral VR interventions often remains implicit, thereby constraining transparency, reproducibility and transferability across welfare contexts.

Addressing these gaps requires both a shared conceptual framework to structure cross-sector communication and a systematic methodological approach to guide intervention development and make decision-making transparent. The International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) provides an internationally recognised biopsychosocial framework and offers a shared language for describing functioning across sectors.21 Embedding cross-sectoral VR interventions within an ICF framework is therefore important to support coordinated assessment, goal-setting and professional perspectives.

Complementing this conceptual alignment, the Medical Research Council (MRC) framework offers structured guidance for developing complex interventions in an iterative, theory-informed and context-attentive manner.4 It recommends early involvement of relevant stakeholders, which in this study included the target group, professionals delivering the intervention, decision-makers and contextual experts, to strengthen acceptability, feasibility and potential impact. By emphasising stakeholder involvement and explicit articulation of mechanisms of change, the framework supports transparency in how evidence and experiential knowledge are translated into intervention components.4

The Stay-At-Work (SAW) intervention was developed to address these challenges by providing a person-centred, interdisciplinary and cross-sectoral VR programme for people on, or at risk of, sick leave due to CLBP. The intervention is designed to strengthen coordination between healthcare and employment services with the aim of improving work functioning and supporting sustainable RTW.

This study aims to co-develop the SAW intervention while advancing methodological understanding of how evidence, qualitative insights, stakeholder input and programme theory can be systematically integrated and operationalised into concrete intervention components within a cross-sectoral VR development process, thereby strengthening reproducibility and transferability of complex VR interventions.

Methods

Setting

In Denmark, secondary care for spinal pain is provided within the publicly funded healthcare system through regional spine centres. In the Region of Southern Denmark, which comprises 22 municipalities, the spine centre brings together orthopaedic surgeons, rheumatologists, chiropractors, physiotherapists and nurses into interdisciplinary teams. Patients are referred from a general practitioner (GP), chiropractor or hospital department, typically after 3 months of an insufficient response to primary care management. The main focus is diagnostics and coordinated recommendations for conservative management.22 Regional spine centres can refer to municipal rehabilitation as part of the same publicly-funded system. Municipal rehabilitation is typically led by physiotherapists and focuses on exercise, education and functional restoration to support return to daily activities and work, aligned with the ICF framework’s.21

This study was initiated in collaboration between regional and university research units. Partnerships were established with rehabilitation centres and employment services in three municipalities (Middelfart, Kolding, Aabenraa), who contributed as members of a multistakeholder co-design group involved in shaping the content, structure and delivery of the intervention, and as active partners during feasibility and evaluation. Each department appointed a local manager as a management representative who participated in selected activities to strengthen feasibility and ownership. The author group functioned as a steering committee to provide role clarity and maintain decision-making structure in cases where consensus could not be achieved among the co-design group members, and approved the final intervention description.20

The co-design group comprised 20 members: 6 municipal physiotherapists, 3 social workers, 3 managers from municipal employment services, 2 representatives of GPs, 2 spine centre clinicians, 2 representatives of employers and employees and 2 individuals living with CLBP (patient partners).

Study design

The development process was guided by the MRC framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions,4 which provides overarching principles for iterative, theory-informed and context-sensitive development. To operationalise the development phase, we used the Identify, Network, Develop, Evaluate and eXecute (INDEX) principles,20 which provide practical guidance aligned with MRC recommendations for intervention development.4 20 Within this combined structure, stakeholder engagement, contextual adaptation, theory-based and evidence-based reasoning and ongoing refinement were prioritised to enhance acceptability, feasibility and potential impact.

Patient, public and stakeholder involvement

Patient and public involvement and broader stakeholder involvement were embedded at multiple stages of the research process. Individuals living with CLBP were involved during Stage 1 through qualitative interviews and focus groups to ensure that patient perspectives informed subsequent development priorities. Two patient partners also participated in the multistakeholder co-design group during Stage 2, informed by participatory research principles emphasising mutual respect, shared influence, attention to local contextual fit and the value of lived experience.3

To operationalise involvement across patient, public and professional stakeholder groups, we adopted an inclusive approach informed by the National Institute for Health Research’s practical guide23 and applied Smits et al’s Involvement Matrix to clarify expectations, roles and timing across project phases (preparation, execution, implementation).24 25 Role clarity and timely involvement were prioritised, as these support psychological safety and shared ownership, conditions essential for meaningful participation in cross-sectoral intervention development.1 4 The Involvement Matrix24 25 guided participation levels and decision-making structures across activities, including interviews and focus groups with individuals living with CLBP and expectation meetings and workshops with the multistakeholder co-design group. Involvement roles varied across stakeholder groups and stages of the development process, reflecting both participant preferences and organisational responsibilities (figure 1).

Stakeholder groups not listed in certain phases were not involved during those stages, and the ‘listener’ role from the original Involvement Matrix did not apply in this project, as all stakeholder groups participated in active roles.

Given the numerical predominance of professionals in the co-design group, specific facilitation strategies were applied to mitigate potential power imbalances and ensure balanced participation. This included structured turn-taking, mixed-group allocation and early integration of themes derived from the interviews with individuals living with CLBP (Stage 1) into workshop activities.

Frameworks guiding reporting and structuring the development process

To support transparency and provide a coherent structure for reporting, the article was organised according to Hawkins et al’s co-production and prototyping framework,1 which offers a three-stage narrative comprising evidence review and consultation, co-production and prototyping. This framework structured the reporting of the development process rather than informing methodological decisions.

In line with recent distinctions in the literature,2 the multistakeholder co-design group was responsible for developing the intervention, while the co-production group (clinicians delivering the feasibility study) was involved in later refinement.

Distinct reporting frameworks were applied at different levels of the study. The Guidance for Reporting Intervention Development (GUIDED) checklist5 provided a structured framework for reporting the overall intervention development process. The Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist26-guided detailed reporting of the final intervention components to support replication. The Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public (GRIPP2—short form)27 informed reporting of patient and public involvement. Completed checklists are provided in online supplemental files 1–3. An overview of the SAW intervention development process is illustrated in figure 2.

Stage 1: evidence review and stakeholder consultation

An evidence review and stakeholder interviews were undertaken to inform intervention priorities and ensure that Stage 2 (co-production) was grounded in real-world needs.

A scoping review was conducted to map multiprofessional VR interventions delivered within European healthcare settings for individuals with CLBP and designed to improve work functioning and reduce sick leave. Electronic databases (MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, AMED, OTseeker and Scopus) were systematically searched, complemented by grey literature and trial registry searches. Eligibility was defined using a Participants-Concept-Context framework, including working-age individuals on, or at risk of, sick leave due to CLBP, receiving multiprofessional VR initiated in primary or secondary healthcare. Titles, abstracts and full texts were independently double-screened by two reviewers. The full methodological details and results have been reported previously in a study by Frydenlund et al.6

To complement this evidence, a qualitative study explored how individuals with CLBP experienced and navigated RTW processes and identified suggestions for improving cross-sectoral coherence. 17 individuals (5 men and 12 women) with diverse occupational backgrounds on long-term sick leave (>30 days) due to CLBP (>12 weeks) participated in interviews and focus groups.28

Stage 2: co-production

Stage 2 focused on co-design activities aimed at developing the intervention content and identifying key uncertainties, drawing on stakeholders’ experiential knowledge and considering the contextual conditions that influence feasibility.

Co-designing

The intervention content was co-designed through an action research cycle of iterative reflection, idea generation, feedback and refinement across two 4-hour workshops and written correspondence.20 Workshop 1 centred on the four main themes from the qualitative study: (1) psychological vulnerability during sick leave; (2) the need for clearer diagnostic communication and professional consensus in RTW planning; (3) mistrust and fragmented cross-sector coordination; and (4) suggestions for more coherent rehabilitation, including shared documentation tools and coordinated stakeholder meetings. The themes and underlying mechanisms involved were discussed in interdisciplinary groups to generate activities targeting these mechanisms, followed by a plenary discussion. Between workshops, transcripts and proposals were circulated for feedback. Workshop 2 was structured using the World Café method29 and focused on refining and contextualising proposed components and identifying implementation considerations.

The facilitator synthesised input from group discussions and written feedback into preliminary intervention components, which were subsequently subjected to collective review and refinement. Consensus was pursued through facilitated discussion within the co-design group. In instances where consensus could not be achieved, the facilitator formulated a compromise proposal that balanced clinical, organisational and legislative considerations; this proposal was then collectively assessed for feasibility. Further refinement was achieved through iterative cycles of written and oral feedback until agreement was reached. The co-design process was led by the first author (GF), who has experience in workshop facilitation. The final intervention constituted the outcome of this co-design process and was formally approved by the steering committee, the co-design group and local management representatives. Developing programme theory

The initial programme theory was drafted by the first author (GF) based on findings from Stage 1 and the intervention components developed during the co-design workshops. Stakeholder input helped identify key contextual factors, intervention activities, impact factors and perceived mechanisms influencing RTW processes. These inputs were subsequently synthesised by GF into an explicit programme theory that describes hypothesised relationships among intervention components, mechanisms, contextual factors and outcomes. The programme theory was iteratively refined through discussions within the research team.

Stage 3: prototyping

An intervention manual was developed to guide feasibility assessment, with a description of each activity including rationale, procedures, materials, providers, setting and delivery. Profession-specific implementation guides were also created to ensure clarity and consistency across sites. These were tested in a prototyping phase, following the approach described by Hawkins et al,1 to evaluate delivery in real-world settings.

During the prototyping phase, the initial delivery of the intervention was tested with the first 10 participants in the feasibility study. At this stage, all participants had completed the initial VR assessment (Activity 3), while subsequent activities (Activities 4–8) were tested to varying extents as participants progressed through the intervention.

The prototyping phase served as a formative step to test the delivery of the intervention and identify practical implementation challenges, allowing for the refinement of procedures before formal evaluation.4 Feasibility was monitored through process logs and feedback from the co-production group, which documented adherence to planned activities and recruitment procedures. Feedback was collected through monthly meetings with spine centre clinicians and mid-point evaluation meetings in municipalities. When barriers affecting the feasibility or clarity were identified, refinements were made iteratively and incorporated into updated role-specific implementation guides, in line with recommendations for the process evaluation of complex interventions.30

Participants and recruitment

Participants for the prototyping phase were recruited from the spine centre at the Hospital of Southern Denmark. Eligibility criteria were: age 18–65 years, residence in Kolding, Middelfart or Aabenraa municipalities and CLBP resulting in, or indicating risk of, sick leave. Risk of sick leave required at least one primary indicator (either pain-related absence within the past 3 months or a Work Ability Score of 0–7 on the 0–10 scale, representing poor or moderate work ability and associated with increased risk of future long-term sick leave and disability pension in working populations31), and at least one secondary indicator (unmet needs for workplace accommodations or a self-reported likelihood of future absence if symptoms persisted).

Exclusion criteria were sick leave exceeding 1 year, severe spinal pathology (eg, cancer, fracture or infections), referral for spinal surgery, pregnancy, severe psychiatric disorder or insufficient Danish language proficiency. All participants provided written informed consent, including permission to share relevant health information with their municipality and GP.32

Results

Findings from the scoping review, qualitative interviews and stakeholder discussions identified three key challenges: fragmented cross-sector coordination, limited consensus around work ability assessment and unaddressed psychological vulnerability. The SAW intervention was therefore designed to address these barriers through structured cross-sector collaboration, systematic VR assessment and targeted psychosocial support.

The Stay-At-Work intervention

The intervention consists of eight structured activities. Activities 1–2 are preparatory activities for the co-production group, while Activities 3–8 are delivered to participants. To ensure consistent delivery, spine centre clinicians received structured training in the assessment, screening and communication components of the intervention delivered at the spine centre.

Activity 1: collaborative onboarding meetings

To align professionals across sectors before intervention launch, a 2.5-hour onboarding meeting was held at the municipal employment services in each municipality (Kolding, Middelfart and Aabenraa). Participants included physiotherapists, nurses and the project manager from the spine centre, as well as municipal managers, physiotherapists and social workers. Using the SAW intervention manual, profession-specific implementation guides and each department’s presentation of sector-specific tasks, roles and responsibilities were clarified and relevant legislation and professional boundaries were discussed.

Activity 2: cross-sector coordination meetings

Midway through the feasibility study, a 1.5-hour coordination meeting was held in each municipality to sustain dialogue between the spine centre and municipal professionals. Including the same participants and format as in Activity 1, the meetings focused on experience sharing, procedural clarification and minor adjustments to intervention delivery.

Activity 3: vocational rehabilitation assessment

Each participant attended an individual VR assessment at the spine centre within 14 days of the diagnostic assessment to develop a structured profile of the participant’s work-related limitations and rehabilitation needs. The assessment, which was conducted by a physiotherapist (60 min, with optional follow-up), included an in-depth, work-oriented anamnesis based on the ICF framework,21 identification of work-related limitations, exploration of the participant’s concerns about RTW, discussion of psychological well-being and goal setting and evaluating needs for subsequent cross-sectoral rehabilitation.

A systematic, person-centred approach was ensured using three validated instruments: the Work Rehabilitation Questionnaire (WORQ) to evaluate work-related limitations and guide goal setting33; the Patient-Specific Functional Scale to define rehabilitation goals34; and the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-42) to screen for psychological vulnerability and inform tailored psychosocial support.35 Standardised materials, including the WORQ/DASS-42 results and the leaflet ‘Understand your pain in the lower back’,36 were reviewed with and delivered to participants.

Activity 4: psychosocial screening and tailored support session

Participants identified as psychologically vulnerable, defined as scoring in the moderate to extremely severe range in any DASS-42 domain35 or based on clinical assessment in Activity 3, were offered a 45-minute nurse-led support session at the spine centre. The session was arranged on the same day as Activity 3, or no later than 14 days after. It includes an optional follow-up (in person or online) and focuses on coping strategies, pain management and assessing the need for GP follow-up.

Activity 5: shared vocational rehabilitation summary

After activities 3 and 4, the spine centre physiotherapist prepared a standardised shared VR summary and distributed it via secure electronic systems to the GP, the participant, the municipal employment service, and, if relevant, the municipal rehabilitation centre. The standardised summary template captured key clinical and vocational information, including diagnosis and pain explanation, rationale for imaging decisions, core functional and psychosocial findings (WORQ33 and DASS-4235), contextual factors, rehabilitation goals and rehabilitation recommendations, including Activity 6 to guide coordinated planning across sectors.

Activity 6: participant-centred coordination meeting

Within 5–8 weeks, the municipal caseworker arranged a participant-centred coordination meeting at the GP’s clinic or, where necessary, online. The 30–45 min meeting included the participant, GP, caseworker and, if relevant, the municipal therapist. Participation was mandatory for participants on long-term sick leave (more than 30 days) and optional for other participants. Using the shared VR summary as a common reference, the group reviewed rehabilitation goals, discussed work-related limitations and work functioning and addressed remaining barriers to ensure coordinated planning across sectors.

Activity 7: consistent patient education leaflet

For participants referred for municipal rehabilitation services, municipal physiotherapists introduced and discussed the spine centre leaflet ‘Understand your pain in the lower back’36 during the initial rehabilitation sessions. Using the same material as in Activity 3 ensured consistent messaging and promoted a shared understanding across sectors.

Activity 8: physiotherapy support during sick leave meetings

For participants on sick leave receiving municipal rehabilitation, the physiotherapist could, on request, join an online meeting with the participant’s employer (up to 30 min).

How evidence review and stakeholder involvement shaped the intervention

Findings from the scoping review6 informed structural decisions, with an emphasis on interdisciplinary rather than multidisciplinary collaboration. Insights from interviews and focus groups with individuals on long-term sick leave informed the focus on psychological vulnerability, diagnostic communication, and fragmented coordination.28 These needs were translated into specific design decisions during the workshops.

The co-design group contributed to the development of intervention components, participant-facing materials and recruitment strategies. They identified fragmented communication as a central barrier to coordinated RTW pathways and therefore prioritised collaborative onboarding and midway meetings (Activities 1–2), the development of a shared documentation tool (Activity 5), and coordinated stakeholder meetings (Activity 6).

During discussions, the proposal to introduce a dedicated care coordinator was considered but ultimately rejected, as the co-design group concluded that coordination should instead be achieved through structured interdisciplinary collaboration and joint goal-setting meetings across sectors. This decision aligned with findings from the scoping review emphasising interdisciplinary collaboration rather than additional coordinating roles.6

Similarly, the proposal for routine diagnostic imaging was rejected because it conflicted with current clinical guidelines.22 Instead, the co-design group agreed to incorporate the spine centre’s rationale for or against diagnostic imaging into the shared VR summary (Activity 5), ensuring that professionals across sectors communicated a consistent message about imaging decisions and addressing the diagnostic uncertainty identified in the qualitative interviews.28

Development of programme theory

The programme theory was iteratively developed across Stages 1–3 to clarify how the SAW intervention is expected to generate change through four core mechanisms (figure 4). The development process systematically linked identified needs to specific activities and, in turn, to hypothesised mechanisms of change, thereby shaping the anticipated outputs, outcomes and long-term impacts.

Logic model illustrating the programme theory for the SAW intervention. SAW, Stay-At-Work; VR, vocational rehabilitation.

Strengthening interprofessional and cross-sectoral understanding and collaboration Activities 1–2 and 5–7 were designed to strengthen cross-sector collaboration by establishing shared communication structures. Qualitative evidence identified fragmented coordination and inconsistent communication between healthcare and employment services as major barriers to coherent VR pathways.9,1128 Collaborative onboarding meetings (Activity 1) and midway coordination meetings (Activity 2) were therefore aimed at clarifying roles and responsibilities across sectors. The shared VR summary (Activity 5), the participant-centred coordination meeting (Activity 6) and consistent patient education materials (Activity 7) further supported a shared understanding of participants’ condition and rehabilitation plan, thereby promoting coordinated communication in RTW planning.

Achieving stronger alignment around functional capacity and workability Activities 3, 5 and 6 aimed to strengthen professional alignment regarding functional capacity and work ability. The qualitative study showed that unclear diagnostic communication and limited professional consensus created uncertainty in RTW planning,28 while the scoping review identified inconsistent approaches to assessing workability.6 The structured VR assessment (Activity 3), therefore, introduced systematic evaluation of work-related limitations using validated instruments. These findings were summarised in the shared VR summary (Activity 5) and discussed during the participant-centred coordination meeting (Activity 6), supporting shared decision-making across sectors.

Addressing psychological vulnerability through systematic screening and support Activities 3–5 address psychological vulnerability through systematic identification and support. Interviews highlighted psychological distress and uncertainty as barriers to RTW.28 The VR assessment (Activity 3), therefore, incorporated structured screening using the DASS-42 instrument.35 Participants identified as vulnerable were offered a nurse-led support session (Activity 4), focusing on coping strategies and pain management. Relevant findings were subsequently communicated across sectors through the shared VR summary (Activity 5), enabling coordinated follow-up where needed.

Enhancing coordination of care and rehabilitation efforts across sectors Activities 5–8 aimed to strengthen coordination across healthcare and employment services. Qualitative findings demonstrated that individuals often acted as intermediaries between sectors when communication and responsibilities were unclear.28 The shared VR summary (Activity 5), therefore, provided structured information accessible to all stakeholders, while the participant-centred coordination meeting (Activity 6) created a forum for jointly reviewing and agreeing on rehabilitation goals. Consistent patient education (Activity 7) and physiotherapist participation in employer meetings (Activity 8) further supported coordinated communication regarding work-related limitations and rehabilitation planning.

Together, these mechanisms are expected to improve coherence in rehabilitation pathways, strengthen alignment around work ability and support coordinated cross-sectoral decision-making. These changes are anticipated to lead to improved vocational functioning, reduced sickness absence and enhanced self-rated health, as illustrated in the programme theory (figure 4).

Prototyping

In the prototyping phase, recruitment procedures functioned as intended, while testing of intervention delivery identified minor practical challenges related to coordination, timing and role clarification across sectors. These included adjustments to timelines for the participant-centred coordination meeting, clarification of responsibilities for specific activities (eg, booking of Activity 4 and distribution of the shared VR summary) and increased flexibility in delivery formats, including the option to conduct the participant-centred coordination meeting (Activity 6) online when the participant’s GP was located outside the municipality. These refinements were incorporated into the final intervention manual.

Discussion

This study systematically developed the SAW intervention: a person-centred, interdisciplinary, cross-sectoral VR intervention for individuals on or at risk of, sick leave due to CLBP, aiming to support improvements in work functioning and sustainable RTW. The development followed an iterative, theory-informed and stakeholder-driven process, aligned with MRC guidance for complex interventions,4 operationalised through the INDEX principles20 and structured using Hawkins’ co-production model.1 By integrating evidence from the scoping review,6 qualitative insights28 and multistakeholder involvement, the intervention was designed to address well-documented barriers to coordinated VR, including unclear diagnostic communication, fragmented cross-sector collaboration and insufficient attention to psychological vulnerability.

Stakeholder contributions were embedded throughout the process. Patient partners in the co-design group contributed experiential perspectives on sick leave and RTW, while interview and focus group participants informed the identification of key needs and priorities. Employer and employee representatives highlighted organisational constraints, and clinicians and social workers focused on feasibility in everyday practice. Engagement levels varied across stages, reflecting stakeholder roles and the scope of decision-making. Process tools such as the Involvement Matrix24 supported clarity around expectations, and iterative workshops and written feedback promoted shared understanding. Through these mechanisms, stakeholder involvement contributed to refining materials, strengthening cross-sector alignment, and adjusting participant information.

Contextual and organisational factors shaped several design decisions during the development process. Limited GP availability required adaptations to the coordination model, including locating the participant-centred coordination meeting (Activity 6) in GP clinics. This adaptation illustrates how intervention components were adjusted to align with existing service structures and professional workflows. The involvement of local management representatives also proved essential for organisational feasibility, as managerial support facilitated cross-sector engagement and implementation planning. Such contextual influences highlight the importance of context-attentive development, as recommended in the MRC framework.4

Despite high overall engagement, several challenges influenced the refinement of the intervention. Some stakeholders had limited time to review materials, and the complexity of the intervention made feedback demanding. These constraints required active facilitation to maintain balanced participation across professional groups. The co-design process also highlighted tensions between stakeholder preferences and clinical or organisational constraints. For example, interview participants suggested routine diagnostic imaging to reduce uncertainty, but this proposal conflicted with clinical guidelines. Instead, the co-design group agreed to include the spine centre’s rationale for imaging decisions in the shared VR summary, enabling consistent explanations across sectors while maintaining adherence to evidence-based practice. This approach aligns with recommendations that patient education should address the limited utility of diagnostic imaging for non-specific CLBP.37 This example illustrates how stakeholder input informed the intervention while also requiring negotiation with existing clinical standards.

Several uncertainties remain. The feasibility and acceptability of the participant-centred coordination meeting (Activity 6) and physiotherapy support during sick leave meetings (Activity 8) require further examination, as these activities challenge traditional boundaries between healthcare and employment sectors. Introducing such cross-sector roles may affect clinicians’ willingness to engage in vocational coordination, participants’ acceptance of workplace-related discussions in clinical settings and employers’ responses. As these activities represent new forms of collaboration across sectors, their practical feasibility and acceptability remain uncertain and will therefore undergo feasibility testing, alongside preliminary outcome indicators. Recent research highlights mismatches in expectations between employers and employees regarding responsibility for managing musculoskeletal problems at work and employees’ concerns about disclosing symptoms due to fear of job loss.38 For Activity 6, uncertainties also relate to the revised 5–8 week timeframe and the extent to which this supports timely RTW. Evidence suggests that individuals with strong workplace ties may require less intensive coordination, whereas those with weaker ties may need more comprehensive support.39 40 These issues will be explored further in the upcoming feasibility study.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength was the structured use of tools such as the Involvement Matrix.24 Iterative workshops with written feedback enabled flexibility and integration of diverse perspectives, while active facilitation supported constructive dialogue. However, the process was resource-intensive and required substantial coordination across sectors. Limited time availability among some stakeholders and varying organisational priorities occasionally challenged continuity and feedback. Modifications to specific intervention components illustrate how stakeholder input informed the process; however, the causal impact of stakeholder involvement cannot be established with methodological certainty. Finally, several components of the intervention may be transferable across healthcare systems, including structured work-focused assessment, shared documentation, coordinated stakeholder meetings and the use of iterative co-design processes, because they address challenges widely reported in VR, such as fragmented cross-sector coordination and inconsistent communication between stakeholders.9,11 However, it is important to distinguish between context-dependent and more generic elements. The intervention was developed within the Danish healthcare and welfare system, characterised by tax-funded healthcare and municipally organised employment and rehabilitation services. As such, organisational features such as the division of responsibilities between sectors, the role of GPs and the legislative framework for sick leave are context-specific and may require adaptation in other settings.

Key lessons learnt

This study highlights both opportunities and challenges of embedding stakeholder involvement in intervention development. This study adds to the theoretical understanding of co-developing by illustrating how the researcher’s role shifts from knowledge provider to facilitator, responsible for creating safe spaces, balancing diverse perspectives and enabling stakeholders to generate feasible solutions. A key challenge was balancing inclusivity with feasibility: while all perspectives were welcomed, some proposals could not be implemented due to clinical guidelines or organisational constraints, making transparent communication essential for trust. Overall, co-developing is resource-intensive and context-dependent, but may yield interventions better aligned with real-world practice, supporting feasibility and acceptability in later testing.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a structured, theory-informed development process with sustained stakeholder involvement can be used to co-develop a cross-sectoral VR intervention grounded in evidence, programme theory and lived experience. By systematically integrating these sources into concrete intervention components, the study advances methodological understanding of how complex interventions can be operationalised in practice.

The resulting SAW intervention offers a coherent framework intended to address key vocational challenges associated with CLBP while remaining responsive to contextual constraints. The approach enhanced transparency and alignment with real-world practice and may strengthen the reproducibility and transferability of complex VR interventions. Its feasibility, acceptability and potential mechanisms of impact will be examined in a feasibility study.
