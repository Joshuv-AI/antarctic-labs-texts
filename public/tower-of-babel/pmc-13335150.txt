Medial meniscus RAMP lesions: a novel magnetic resonance arthrography-based classification
Authors: Luigi Tessitore, Chiara Macina, Attilio Prato, Marco Calvi, Eugenio Annibale Genovese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMeniscal ramp lesions (RAMP) are longitudinal tears at the meniscocapsular or meniscotibial attachments of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus, commonly associated with anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tears. Their preoperative detection on standard MRI remains limited, especially for “hidden” variants. Magnetic resonance arthrography (MRA) may theoretically enhance visualization of the posteromedial compartment through capsular distension and improved tissue contrast; however, its diagnostic superiority remains to be validated. This pictorial review introduces a conceptual MRA-based framework for describing ramp lesions (types A–E), derived from previously reported anatomical and imaging concepts in the literature and illustrated with representative institutional cases. The proposed framework emphasizes lesion topography, morphology, and contrast-medium behavior on MRA and is intended as an educational and hypothesis-generating model rather than as a validated diagnostic tool. Further studies with arthroscopic correlation, reproducibility assessment, and prospective validation are needed to determine the diagnostic and clinical value of this framework.Keywords: Ramp lesion, Meniscocapsular tear, Meniscotibial ligament, MR arthrography, Magnetic resonance imaging, Anterior cruciate ligament

Background

Ramp lesions are a specific type of meniscal injury that affects the posterior horn of the medial meniscus at its peripheral attachment to the posteromedial capsule [1]. Introduced for the first time by the German orthopedic surgeon M. Strobel in 1988, this term refers to vertical longitudinal tears of the red-red zone of the meniscus involving the meniscocapsular and/or meniscotibial ligaments [2].

Mechanism of formation

They are usually linked to injuries of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL). In this context, ramp lesions are caused by abnormal rotational or anterior translation forces of the tibia. These conditions are often exacerbated by instability due to ACL insufficiency [2, 3].

Diagnosis and relevance

Preoperative diagnosis of ramp lesions on conventional MRI can be challenging, and these lesions are often overlooked on routine examinations [1]. MRI may show indirect or direct findings such as posteromedial tibial edema, peripheral fluid signal, or capsular detachment [2]. However, posteromedial arthroscopic visualization remains the reference standard for diagnosis [1, 3].

Study design

This study was designed as a single-center pictorial review with a structured literature search, aimed at describing the appearance of medial meniscal ramp lesions on magnetic resonance arthrography (MRA) and proposing a conceptual MRA-based descriptive framework. The manuscript is intended as an educational and hypothesis-generating work rather than as a diagnostic accuracy study or a validated classification study.

All MRA images presented in this review were selected from the authors’ institutional archive and had originally been acquired for routine clinical indications unrelated to the present study. The archive includes approximately 450 knee MRA examinations collected over about 8 years of clinical practice. Representative cases were selected for illustrative and educational purposes on the basis of image quality, anatomical clarity, and their ability to depict one of the proposed lesion patterns. Arthroscopic confirmation was not sistematically available for the illustrated cases, as surgical follow-up could not be consistently obtained in this retrospective pictorial series.

A structured literature search was performed to identify articles relevant to the anatomy, biomechanics, imaging, diagnosis, and classification of ramp lesions. Because the aim of the manuscript was pictorial and conceptual, this search was conducted to support qualitative synthesis and framework development rather than to perform a formal systematic review or meta-analysis.

MRA technique

All illustrative examinations were performed on a 1.5-T Philips MRI system using a dedicated knee coil, according to the institutional musculoskeletal MR arthrography protocol. Arthrographic joint distension was obtained by ultrasound-guided intra-articular injection under sterile conditions using gadoterate meglumine (Dotarem) diluted to approximately 2 mmol/L, with a total injected volume of approximately 20–25 mL. MR arthrography of the knee was acquired using multiplanar fat-suppressed T1-weighted sequences in the sagittal, coronal, and axial planes, with a slice thickness of 3 mm, which served as the primary sequences for arthrographic assessment. In addition, a sagittal proton density (PD) sequence and a 3D T1-weighted sequence were included in the routine protocol. Image analysis focused on the posteromedial meniscocapsular and meniscotibial recesses, the peripheral red-red zone of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus, and the pattern of contrast-medium distribution. Representative cases were selected for illustrative and educational purposes on the basis of image quality, anatomical clarity, and correspondence with one of the proposed lesion patterns.

Findings from the literature and proposed MRA framework

The structured literature search did not identify studies specifically evaluating ramp lesions with MR arthrography. The available literature primarily addresses ramp lesions from arthroscopic, anatomical, biomechanical, or conventional MRI perspectives. Based on these sources, we reviewed the principal descriptive features of previously published classification systems and extracted concepts potentially applicable to MRA interpretation. On this basis, and using representative institutional MRA examinations for illustration, we developed a conceptual descriptive framework for ramp lesions on MRA.

Discussion

Meniscal ramp lesions (RAMP) are frequently found alongside anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries. These lesions are located at the junction between the posterior horn of the medial meniscus and the posteromedial capsule. Despite their biomechanical importance, radiological diagnosis remains challenging. Arthroscopic visualisation is difficult without targeted access to the posteromedial compartment [4, 5].

Numerous studies have investigated ramp lesions using conventional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and obtained variable results in terms of diagnostic accuracy [2, 5–7]. These studies have illustrated lesion anatomy, direct and indirect signs, and classifications [2], with sensitivities ranging from 48% to 75% and specificities generally above 80% [2, 5–7].

By contrast, dedicated literature on the appearance of ramp lesions at MR arthrography is essentially absent. This lack of evidence does not allow any conclusion regarding diagnostic superiority of MRA over conventional MRI. From an imaging standpoint, however, intra-articular contrast may theoretically improve depiction of the posteromedial recesses by distending the capsule and outlining subtle planes of separation between the meniscus, capsule, and tibial attachment.

Conventional MRI has shown variable sensitivity but generally high specificity for ramp lesions. Accordingly, routine MRI may be helpful when positive findings are present, but a negative examination does not reliably exclude all posteromedial peripheral lesions, especially subtle meniscocapsular, meniscotibial, or combined patterns [8, 9].

Several studies have emphasized that conventional MRI may miss subtle or peripheral ramp lesions and that arthroscopic inspection remains necessary when clinical suspicion persists despite inconclusive imaging [10–14].

The role of MRA has not yet been evaluated in the literature. According to some educational reviews, subtle lesions can be more easily detected using intra-articular contrast and flexion [3, 5]. However, there is no specific classification system in place for MRA appearances. A systematic review of classification systems by Allende et al. highlighted the inconsistency of MRI-based criteria, emphasizing the need for standardized approaches [15].

Studies on the epidemiology of the condition have shown that ramp is present in a significant proportion of knees with an ACL deficiency, with a prevalence of up to 44%, and that it frequently occurs alongside other injuries [13, 16, 17]. Failure to identify ramp can result in residual instability and an increased risk of failure of an ACL reconstruction [17].

The present work should therefore be interpreted as a conceptual pictorial proposal. Its purpose is not to demonstrate diagnostic performance, but to provide a structured descriptive language for MRA appearances of posteromedial peripheral meniscal lesions. The proposed framework organizes lesions according to the dominant anatomical site involved, lesion morphology, and contrast-medium behavior. From a practical perspective, this may help radiologists describe MRA findings more systematically when such examinations are performed in selected cases.

The possible role of MRA, if any, is more reasonably as a problem-solving technique in selected scenarios, such as equivocal conventional MRI findings, persistent clinical suspicion despite negative or indeterminate MRI, complex preoperative assessment, or revision settings. Whether MRA adds clinically meaningful information in such contexts remains unknown and should be evaluated prospectively.

The proposed framework is intended to provide a structured descriptive language for MRA appearances of ramp lesions rather than to define validated treatment-oriented categories. Some imaging patterns may suggest potentially stable or unstable configurations; however, these inferences remain descriptive and require arthroscopic and clinical validation.

Our RAMP classification in MRA has been designed to maximise the diagnostic value of contrast agents and facilitate the introduction of arthroscopic semeiotics with typical key signs:

The included studies address ramp lesions from various perspectives, including anatomical, biomechanical, arthroscopic, diagnostic and classification in conventional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [1–7, 13].

Limitations

This study has several limitations:

This study has several limitations. First, it is a pictorial and conceptual review rather than a prospective diagnostic investigation. Second, the proposed MRA-based framework has not been validated through arthroscopic correlation, diagnostic accuracy analysis, or interobserver/intraobserver reproducibility testing. Third, because no dedicated MRA literature on ramp lesions was identified, the framework was developed by extrapolating concepts from arthroscopic and conventional MRI studies and applying them to representative institutional MRA cases. Fourth, some distinctions within the framework, particularly between A1 and A2 and between C2 and D, remain qualitative and may be subjective. Finally, the illustrative cases were selected from a single-center archive for educational purposes and do not constitute a validation cohort.

Conclusions

Ramp lesions remain a diagnostically challenging and clinically relevant entity because of their posteromedial location, morphological heterogeneity, and the limited conspicuity of some lesion patterns on conventional MRI [1–3, 5]. In the absence of dedicated literature specifically addressing their appearance on MRA, the present pictorial review proposes a conceptual MRA-based descriptive framework for classifying ramp lesions according to lesion topography, morphology, and contrast-medium behavior. This framework is intended primarily as an educational and hypothesis-generating tool, aimed at facilitating more structured radiological description when MRA is performed in selected clinical scenarios. It should not be regarded as a validated diagnostic or treatment-guiding classification, and any potential advantage of MRA over conventional MRI should be considered theoretical at present. Further studies with arthroscopic correlation, reproducibility assessment, and prospective multicenter validation are needed to clarify the true diagnostic performance, clinical applicability, and potential added value of this proposed framework.
