Kőnig = Ramsey A compactness lemma for Ramsey categories Journal: Elsevier
Maximilian Hadek

Abstract
We prove a new characterization of the Ramsey property of categories in terms of a generalized form of Kőnig’s tree lemma. Afterwards, we discuss its applications to structural Ramsey theory. In particular, we provide a new proof of the existence and uniqueness of minimal Ramsey expansions and a new transfer theorem which uses Grothendieck opfibrations and unifies several known Ramsey transfers.



1
Introduction

Given some sets and some maps between them, is there a selection of elements, one from each set, which is compatible with all given maps?
The simplest case, when there are no maps, is precisely the axiom of choice: given any family of nonempty sets, one can pick a single element out of each of them. Slightly less simple, Kőnigs infinity lemma deals with the case when the sets and maps are arranged in sequence.

Theorem 1.1

(Kőnig, 1927
[
1
]
)
.

Consider finite nonempty sets
E_{1},E_{2},E_{3},\dots
and maps
E_{n+1}\xrightarrow{R_{n}}E_{n}
. We can find in every set
E_{n}
an element
a_{n}
such that
R_{n}(a_{n+1})=a_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
1
1

1

While this is almost the original wording of the lemma, some readers might be more familiar with a graph theoretic formulation in terms of trees. To see the similarity, draw all elements of all
E_{n}
as vertices and connect them by an edge whenever
R_{n}(a_{n+1})=a_{n}
.

More complicated shapes in which sets and maps can be arranged are categories
\mathcal{C}
, which are collections of points and arrows together with a formal composition of arrows. A diagram in the shape of
\mathcal{C}
assigns to every point a set and to every arrow a map, such that composition is respected, and
solutions
to a diagram are the selections of elements, one from each set, which are invariant under the given maps.
Our main theorem characterizes those categories, for which a generalized Kőnigs lemma holds, i.e. those shapes where every diagram of finite nonempty sets has a solution.

Theorem 1.2
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is an essentially small and locally finite category, then the following are equivalent:

1.

every diagram in the shape of
\mathcal{C}^{\mathop{op}}
which consists of finite nonempty sets has a solution

2.

\mathcal{C}
is confluent and has the Ramsey property.

The symbol
{}^{\mathop{op}}
means that the maps of the diagram point in the
opposite
direction as the arrows in
\mathcal{C}
.

After proving Theorem
1.2
in Section
3
, we discuss its applications to structural Ramsey theory. In particular, in Section
4
we generalize the notion of expansions to arbitrary categories and prove the following refinement of a theorem which initially appeared in the context of topological dynamics; see
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

If a locally finite and essentially small confluent category has a precompact confluent Ramsey expansion, then it has a precompact confluent Ramsey expansion with the expansion property. Moreover, this expansion is unique up to isomorphism of expansions.

Moreover, in Section
5
we prove a new Ramsey transfer theorem about Grothendieck opfibrations, providing a common generalization of three known Ramsey transfers, namely products
[
4
]
, discrete opfibrations
[
5
]
and blowups.

Theorem 1.4
.

If a Grothendieck opfibration is locally finite and essentially small, has confluent Ramsey fibers and a confluent Ramsey base, then it is confluent and Ramsey.

Preliminaries

2.1
Categories, Diagrams and Limits

Recall the elementary category theoretical notions required for Theorem
1.2
. See also any book on the subject, for example
[
6
]
or
[
7
]
.

Definition 2.5
.

A
category

\mathcal{C}
is a collection of objects and arrows between them, together with a formal composition operation of arrows. Objects are denoted as
C\in\mathcal{C}
and for an arrow
f
we specify its source and target object by writing
A\xrightarrow{f}B
. Given three objects
A,B,C
and two arrows
A\xrightarrow{f}B
and
B\xrightarrow{g}C
there is a composite arrow
A\xrightarrow{g\circ f}C
, so that the following axioms are fulfilled.

1.

(Identity) For every object
B\in\mathcal{C}
there is an arrow
B\xrightarrow{\id_{B}}B
which fulfills
f=\id_{B}\circ f
and
g=g\circ\id_{B}
for all arrows
A\xrightarrow{f}B
and
B\xrightarrow{g}C
.

2.

(Associativity) For any three arrows,
A\xrightarrow{f}B
,
B\xrightarrow{g}C
and
C\xrightarrow{h}D
we have
h\circ(g\circ f)=(h\circ g)\circ f
.

Throughout the article, all considered categories (except
\Set
and
\Cat
) are assumed to be small, which means that their objects and morphisms form a set and not a proper class. However, since the discussed properties are preserved by equivalence of categories, all theorems hold for
essentially small
categories, i.e.​ those which are equivalent to a small category. For example, the classes of relational structures considered in Section
4
are typically not small, but essentially small.

Definition 2.6
.

A set valued
diagram

\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{C}^{\mathop{op}}\to\Set
is a functor, it assigns to every object
C\in\mathcal{C}
a set
\mathcal{D}_{C}
and to each arrow
A\xrightarrow{f}B
in
\mathcal{C}
a map
\mathcal{D}_{B}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}_{f}}\mathcal{D}_{A}
in the opposite direction. Moreover, this assignment must respect the identity arrows and the composition of arrows, meaning that

\mathcal{D}_{\id_{C}}=\id_{\mathcal{D}_{C}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{D}_{f}\circ\mathcal{D}_{g}=\mathcal{D}_{g\circ f}

for any three objects
A,B,C
and arrows
A\xrightarrow{f}B
and
B\xrightarrow{g}C
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category, and let
\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{C}^{\mathop{op}}\to\Set
be a diagram.
A
solution
to
\mathcal{D}
is a tuple
(x_{C})_{C\in\mathcal{C}}
, where each
x_{C}
is an element of the set
\mathcal{D}_{C}
and
x_{A}=\mathcal{D}_{f}(x_{B})
holds for all arrows
A\xrightarrow{f}B
in
\mathcal{C}
.
The set of all solutions is called
limit
and is denoted as
\lim\mathcal{D}
.

\lim\mathcal{D}=\big\{(x_{C})_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\bigm|\forall(A\xrightarrow{f}B):x_{A}=\mathcal{D}_{f}(x_{B})\big\}\subseteq\prod_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{D}_{C}

2.2
Connectedness and Confluence

Definition 2.8
.

We say that two objects
A
and
B
in a category
\mathcal{C}
are
connected
, if there is a zigzag path of arrows between them.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{n-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}

A category is said to be connected if any two of its objects are connected.

Since there are no arrows between different connected components of a category, to find solutions to a diagram
\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{C}^{\mathop{op}}\to\Set
, it suffices to consider each component individually. Indeed, if
\mathcal{C}=\coprod_{i}\mathcal{C}_{i}
is a decomposition of
\mathcal{C}
into its connected components, then

\lim\mathcal{D}=\prod\lim_{i}\mathcal{D}|_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}

where
\mathcal{D}|_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}
is the restriction of
\mathcal{D}
to the
i
-th component of
\mathcal{C}
.

Example 2.9
.

Not every diagram has a solution.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{0\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\{0,1\}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{1\}}

Let
A\leftarrow D\to B
be the shape of this diagram, then sets
\mathcal{D}_{A}
and
\mathcal{D}_{B}
are independent, as are the maps leaving them. This can be circumvented by requiring the existence of an object
C
and two arrows
A\to C\leftarrow B
.

Definition 2.10
.

A category
\mathcal{C}
is called
confluent
if for any two connected objects
A
and
B
, there is a third object
C
and two arrows
A\to C\leftarrow B
.

A common assumption in Ramsey theory is that categories have the so-called
joint embedding property
, which says that for any two objects
A
and
B
there must be an object
C
and two arrows
A\to C\leftarrow B
. Hence, a category is confluent if all of its connected components have the joint embedding property. The following proposition generalizes Example
2.9
, showing that confluence is indeed necessary for Theorem
1.2
.

Proposition 2.11

(Kőnig
\Rightarrow
confluence)
.

If
\mathcal{C}
a category is not confluent, then there is a diagram of finite nonempty sets in the shape of
\mathcal{C}^{\mathop{op}}
, which does not have a solution.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{C}
is not confluent, then we can find two connected objects
A
and
B
such that there are no objects
C
with arrows
A\to C\leftarrow B
.
We define a diagram
\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{C}^{\mathop{op}}\to\Set
as follows.

C\mapsto\begin{cases}\{0\}&\text{if }A\xrightarrow{\exists}C\\
\{1\}&\text{if }B\xrightarrow{\exists}C\\
\{0,1\}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

The assumption guarantees that the first two cases are disjoint.
Given an arrow
C\xrightarrow{f}C^{\prime}
, we have
\mathcal{D}_{C^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{C}
, hence we can define
\mathcal{D}_{f}
to always be the inclusion map. Restricting
\mathcal{D}
to a path from
A
to
B
yields the following diagram.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}_{C_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}_{C_{n}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{0\}=\mathcal{D}_{A}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}_{C_{2}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}_{C_{n-1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}_{B}=\{1\}}

All maps are inclusions, so any solution would have to pick
0
out of
\mathcal{D}_{A}
,
\mathcal{D}_{C_{1}}
,
\mathcal{D}_{C_{2}}
and so on, eventually contradicting
\mathcal{D}_{B}=\{1\}
.
∎

2.3
The Ramsey Property

Example 2.12
.

The following diagram does not have a solution.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{*\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\{0,1\}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}

Definition 2.13
.

A category
2
2

2

Often, when defining the Ramsey property, one assumes that the category is
locally finite
, which means that there are only finitely many arrows between any two objects. This assumption is also necessary for Theorem
1.2
, however we are curious whether there is an appropriate adaptation of the Ramsey property for non locally finite categories, such that Theorem
1.2
holds.

\mathcal{C}
is said to be
Ramsey
if for any two objects
A
and
B
and any finite set
N
there exists an object
C
, such that for all maps
\hom(A,C)\xrightarrow{\chi}N
there exists an arrow
B\xrightarrow{g}C
such that the composition

\chi\circ g_{*}:\hom(A,B)\xrightarrow{g_{*}}\hom(A,C)\xrightarrow{\chi}N

is a constant map, where
g_{*}
is the composition map
f\mapsto g\circ f
. Any such object
C
is called a
Ramsey witness
of
A,B
and
N
.

Example 2.14
.

Any diagram
\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{C}^{\mathop{op}}\to\Set
induces a map
\chi
in the following way. Let
N:=\mathcal{D}_{A}
, pick an element
x\in\mathcal{D}_{C}
and define
\chi
as the following map.

\chi:\hom(A,C)\xrightarrow{}\mathcal{D}_{A},\quad(A\xrightarrow{h}C)\mapsto\mathcal{D}_{h}(x)

Let
B\xrightarrow{g}C
be the arrow as in the definition above, then
\mathcal{D}_{g}(x)\in\mathcal{D}_{B}
is sent to the same element in
\mathcal{D}_{A}
by all maps
\mathcal{D}_{f}
coming from arrows
A\xrightarrow{f}B
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}_{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}_{B}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}_{A}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}_{g}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}_{f^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}_{f}}

Indeed
\mathcal{D}_{f}(\mathcal{D}_{g}(x))=\mathcal{D}_{g\circ f}(x)=\chi(g\circ f)
which does not depend on
f
. In particular, there is a selection of elements from each
\mathcal{D}_{C}
, which respects all arrows from
A
to
B
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2

3.1
Ramsey implies Kőnig

Throughout the proof, we use the following notation. Given a set
M
of arrows in
\mathcal{C}
, we call a tuple
(x_{C})_{C\in\mathcal{C}}
an
M
-solution
of a diagram
\mathcal{D}
, if the tuple is compatible with all arrows in
M
. The set of
M
-solutions is denoted as
\lim_{M}\mathcal{D}
.

\lim\nolimits_{M}\mathcal{D}:=\big\{(x_{C})_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\bigm|\forall(A\xrightarrow{f}B)\in M:x_{A}=\mathcal{D}_{f}(x_{B})\big\}\subseteq\prod_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{D}_{C}

We start by iterating the technique from Example
2.14
to obtain
M
-solutions whenever
M
is a finite set of arrows.

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a connected confluent Ramsey category, let
M
be a finite set of arrows in
\mathcal{C}
, and let
\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{C}^{\mathop{op}}\to\Set
be a diagram. If all sets
\mathcal{D}_{C}
are finite and nonempty, then
\mathcal{D}
has an
M
-solution.

Proof.

Let
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
be a list of all objects which are the source or target of some arrow in
M
. Since
\mathcal{C}
is connected and confluent, there exists an object
C_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
together with arrows
B_{i}\xrightarrow{g_{i}}C_{0}
for every index
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Let
C_{1}
be a Ramsey witness of the pair
B_{1}
,
C_{0}
and the finite set
\mathcal{D}_{B_{1}}
.
Let
C_{2}
be a Ramsey witness of the pair
B_{2}
,
C_{1}
and the set
\mathcal{D}_{B_{2}}
and iterate until we defined
C_{n}
as a Ramsey witness of
B_{n},C_{n-1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{B_{n}}
. Then, pick any element
x_{n}\in\mathcal{D}_{C_{n}}
and consider the following map.

\chi_{n}:\hom(B_{n},C_{n})\to\mathcal{D}_{B_{n}},\quad f\mapsto\mathcal{D}_{f}(x_{n})

As
C_{n}
is a Ramsey witness of
B_{n}
,
C_{n-1}
and the set
\mathcal{D}_{B_{n}}
, there exists an arrow
C_{n-1}\xrightarrow{h_{n}}C_{n}
such that the map

\hom(B_{n},C_{n-1})\to\mathcal{D}_{B_{n}},\quad f\mapsto\chi_{n}(h_{n}\circ f)=\mathcal{D}_{f}(\mathcal{D}_{h_{n}}(x_{n}))

is constant.
Set
x_{n-1}:=\mathcal{D}_{h_{n}}(x_{n})
and iterate backwards, until we arrive at the following diagram.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B_{n}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\vdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\vdots}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h_{n}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g_{n}}

We claim that any tuple
(x_{C})_{C\in\mathcal{C}}
with
x_{B_{i}}=\mathcal{D}_{h_{n}\circ\dots\circ h_{1}\circ g_{i}}(x_{n})
is an
M
-solution. Indeed, given any arrow
B_{i}\xrightarrow{f}B_{j}
from
M
, the compositions

f_{0}:=h_{i-1}\circ\dots\circ h_{1}\circ g_{j}\circ f\quad\text{and}\quad f_{1}:=h_{i-1}\circ\dots\circ h_{1}\circ g_{i}

are arrows from
B_{i}
to
C_{i-1}
and therefore, by the definition of
h_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{f_{0}}(\mathcal{D}_{h_{i}}(x_{i}))

\displaystyle=\chi_{i}(h_{i}\circ f_{0})

\displaystyle=\chi_{i}(h_{i}\circ f_{1})=\mathcal{D}_{f_{1}}(\mathcal{D}_{h_{i}}(x_{i})).

Plugging in the definitions of
f_{0}
,
f_{1}
and
x_{i}
we get

\mathcal{D}_{f}\big(\mathcal{D}_{h_{n}\circ\dots\circ h_{1}\circ g_{j}}(x_{n})\big)=\mathcal{D}_{h_{n}\circ\dots\circ h_{1}\circ g_{i}}(x_{n})

which proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 3.16

(Compactness)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category and let
\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{C}^{\mathop{op}}\to\Set
be a diagram where all sets
\mathcal{D}_{C}
are finite. If
\mathcal{D}
has no solution, then there is a finite set
M
of arrows in
\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathcal{D}
does not even have an
M
-solution.

Proof.

Consider the sets
\mathcal{D}_{C}
as discrete (and, as they are finite, compact) topological spaces and take their product space

\prod_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{D}_{C}
.
By Tychonoff’s theorem, this space is compact and therefore every family of closed subspaces with empty intersection has a finite subfamily with empty intersection.
The limit of
\mathcal{D}
can be written as the following intersection of closed subspaces.

\lim\mathcal{D}=\bigcap_{A\xrightarrow{f}B}\{(x_{C})_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\mid x_{A}=\mathcal{D}_{f}(x_{B})\}

If this intersection is empty, then there is a finite set
M
of arrows, such that

\lim\nolimits_{M}\mathcal{D}=\bigcap_{f\in M}\{(x_{C})_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\mid x_{A}=\mathcal{D}_{f}(x_{B})\}

is also empty.
∎

Corollary 3.17

(Ramsey
\Rightarrow
Kőnig)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small confluent Ramsey category and let
\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{C}^{\mathop{op}}\to\Set
be a diagram. If all sets
\mathcal{D}_{C}
are finite and nonempty, then
\mathcal{D}
has a solution.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\coprod_{i}\mathcal{C}_{i}
be the decomposition of
\mathcal{C}
into its connected components, and let
\mathcal{D}|_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}
be the respective components of the diagram
\mathcal{D}
. As there are no arrows between different components, we can compute the limit of
\mathcal{D}
component-wise.

\lim\mathcal{D}=\prod_{i}\lim\mathcal{D}|_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}

Every component
\mathcal{C}_{i}
is connected, confluent and Ramsey, so by Proposition
3.15
there is an
M
-solution of
\mathcal{D}|_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}
for any finite set
M
of arrows in
\mathcal{C}_{i}
. Lemma
3.16
then implies that the limit of
\mathcal{D}|_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}
is nonempty.
∎

3.2
Kőnig implies Ramsey

The following proposition requires that the considered category
\mathcal{C}
be
locally finite
, which means that between any two objects there are only finitely many arrows.

Proposition 3.18

(Kőnig
\Rightarrow
Ramsey)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a locally finite category such that every diagram
\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{C}^{\mathop{op}}\to\Set
where all sets
\mathcal{D}_{C}
are finite and nonempty has is a solution. Then
\mathcal{C}
has the Ramsey property.

Proof.

Fix two objects
A
and
B
, a finite set
N
and consider the diagram
\mathcal{D}
, which assigns to every object
C
the set of maps
\chi
, which prevent
C
form being a Ramsey witness of
A
,
B
and
N
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{C}

\displaystyle:=\{\hom(A,C)\xrightarrow{\chi}N\mid\forall(B\xrightarrow{h}C):\chi\circ h_{*}\text{ is not constant}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{f}

\displaystyle:\mathcal{D}_{C}\to\mathcal{D}_{C^{\prime}},\quad\chi\mapsto\chi\circ f_{*}

for all objects
C,C^{\prime}
and arrows
C\xrightarrow{f}C
. To check that
\mathcal{D}_{f}
is well defined, take a map
\chi\in\mathcal{D}_{C_{2}}
, an arrow
B\xrightarrow{h}C_{1}
and observe that the map

\mathcal{D}_{f}(\chi)\circ h_{*}=\chi\circ f_{*}\circ h_{*}=\chi\circ(f\circ h)_{*}

is not constant, hence
\mathcal{D}_{f}(\chi)
is indeed an element of
\mathcal{D}_{C_{1}}
.
Observe that every set
\mathcal{D}_{C}
is finite because
\hom(A,C)
is finite and that
\mathcal{D}_{C}
is empty if and only if
C
is a Ramsey witness of
A
,
B
and
N
. Hence, the Ramsey property will follow once we show that
\mathcal{D}
does not have a solution.

Striving for a contradiction, assume that
(\chi_{C})_{C\in\mathcal{C}}
is a solution, which in particular means that
\mathcal{D}_{f}(\chi_{B})=\chi_{A}
for all arrows
A\xrightarrow{f}B
. The following computation shows that
\chi_{B}
must be a constant map.

\chi_{B}(f)=(\chi_{B}\circ f_{*})(\id_{A})=\mathcal{D}_{f}(\chi_{B})(\id_{A})=\chi_{A}(\id_{A})

Therefore
\chi_{B}\circ(\id_{B})_{*}
is constant and
\chi_{B}
cannot be an element of
\mathcal{D}_{B}
, giving the desired contradiction.
∎

4
Connections to Structural Ramsey Theory

4.1
Structures and Expansions

We connect Theorem
1.2
to established Ramsey theory using a notion of
abstract expansions
of a Ramsey class. First, recall some model theoretic notions commonly used in Ramsey theory, see for example a recent survey by Hubička and Konečný
[
8
]
.

Definition 4.19
.

A
relational language

\sigma
is a set of symbols
R
, each of which has an associated finite set
\ar R
, called its arity. A finite
\sigma
-
structure

\mathbb{C}
is a tuple
(C;R^{\mathbb{C}}\mid R\in\sigma)
where
C
is a finite set and each
R^{\mathbb{C}}
is a subset of
C^{\ar R}
.

Given two
\sigma
-structures
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{C}^{\prime}
we say that an injective map
C\xrightarrow{f}C^{\prime}
is an
embedding
from
\mathbb{C}
to
\mathbb{C}^{\prime}
if for any symbol
R\in\sigma
we have
\bar{c}\in R^{\mathbb{C}}
if and only if
f\circ\bar{c}\in R^{\mathbb{C}^{\prime}}
for all tuples
\ar R\xrightarrow{\bar{c}}C
.

When
\mathcal{C}
is a class of finite
\sigma
-structures, we consider it as a category using the embeddings as arrows. Any such category is locally finite and essentially small; hence, Theorem
1.2
applies.

Definition 4.20
.

Let
\sigma
and
\tau
be two disjoint relational languages, let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of
\sigma
-structures and let
\mathcal{E}
be a class of
\sigma\cup\tau
-structures. Given a structure
\mathbb{E}\in\mathcal{E}
we denote by
\mathbb{E}|_{\sigma}
the
\sigma
-structure, which is obtained from
\mathbb{E}
by forgetting all relations from
\tau
. We say that
\mathcal{E}
an
expansion
of
\mathcal{C}
if

\mathcal{C}=\{\mathbb{E}|_{\sigma}\mid\mathbb{E}\in\mathcal{E}\}.

An expansion called
precompact
if for every structure
\mathbb{C}\in\mathcal{C}
there are only finitely many structures
\mathbb{E}\in\mathcal{E}
with
\mathbb{E}|_{\sigma}=\mathbb{C}
.

To relate expansions to the diagrams in Theorem
1.2
, we generalize the notion of expansion from classes of relational structures to arbitrary categories.

Definition 4.21
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category. A functor
\mathcal{E}\xrightarrow{\pi}\mathcal{C}
is called
(abstract) expansion
of
\mathcal{C}
if

1.

(surjectivity) for every
C\in\mathcal{C}
there is
E\in\mathcal{E}
with
C=\pi(E)
and

2.

(discrete fibration) for every

4.2

If a class of relational structures has a precompact Ramsey expansion, then it has a unique minimal precompact Ramsey expansion. A similar statement first appeared in the context of topological dynamics in
[

and was later refined in
[
3

. We provide a further refinement and a generalization to arbitrary essentially small locally finite categories using abstract expansions and new auxiliary notions, namely homomorphisms and cores of expansions.

Definition 4.27
.

.

Let

, which is the sought-after homomorphism.

Lemma 4.29
.

Surjective homomorphisms of expansions are themselves expansions.

Proof.

One needs to verify that they are discrete fibrations. Using the notation form Definition

be an object and

an arrow in

5.2
Opfibrations

We prove a new Ramsey transfer theorem, namely that a Grothendieck opfibration is Ramsey whenever its base and its fibers are confluent and Ramsey. This provides a common generalization of three known transfers; products
[
4
]
, discrete opfibrations
[
5
]
(hence slice categories and adding constants
[
13
]
) and blowups. We use the latter as an illustration of the Grothendieck construction.

Example 5.41
.

Consider the following category
\mathcal{C}
of set partitions. Objects in
\mathcal{C}
are tuples
(N,(M_{n})_{n\in N})
, where
N
is a set of blocks and
M_{n}
is the
n
-th block. Arrows

(N,(M_{n})_{n\in N})\to(N^{\prime},(M_{n^{\prime}}^{\prime})_{n^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}})

in
\mathcal{C}
are tuples
(f,(g_{n})_{n\in N})
where
N\xrightarrow{f}N^{\prime}
is an injective map and every
g_{n}
is an injective map
M_{n}\to M^{\prime}_{f(n)}
.

\mathcal{C}
has a partition into subclasses, for every set
N
let
\mathcal{C}_{N}
consist of partitions with exactly
N
blocks. For each injective map
N\xrightarrow{f}N^{\prime}
, define a functor
\mathcal{C}_{N}\to\mathcal{C}_{N^{\prime}}
which adds empty blocks for all elements
n^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}
which are not in the image of
f
and leaves all other blocks unchanged. The class
\mathcal{C}
can be recovered from the fibers
\mathcal{C}_{N}
and the functors obtained from the
f
’s using the following construction.

Definition 5.42

(Grothendieck construction)
.

Consider a functor
\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{R}\to\Cat
form a category
\mathcal{R}
to the category of categories. We define a new category
\Elts\mathcal{S}
as follows. The objects are pairs
(R,S)
, where
R
is an object in
\mathcal{R}
and
S
is an object in
\mathcal{S}_{R}
. The arrows between pairs
(R,S)
and
(R^{\prime},S^{\prime})
are pairs
(f,\phi)
, where
R\xrightarrow{f}R^{\prime}
is an arrow in
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{S}_{f}(S)\xrightarrow{\phi}S^{\prime}
is an arrow in
\mathcal{S}_{R^{\prime}}
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ob}(\textstyle\Elts\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle=\{(R,S)\mid R\in\mathcal{R},S\in\mathcal{S}_{R}\}

\displaystyle\hom((R,S),(R^{\prime},S^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\{(f,\phi)\mid R\xrightarrow{f}R^{\prime},\mathcal{S}_{f}(S)\xrightarrow{\phi}S^{\prime}\}

Categories constructed this way are called
opfibrations
over
\mathcal{R}
, the categories
\mathcal{S}_{R}
are called its
fibers
, see, for example,
[
9
]
.

Example 5.43
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be the category of finite sets and injective maps, and let
\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{L}\to\Cat
send
N
to
\mathcal{I}^{N}
. For an injective map
N\xrightarrow{f}N^{\prime}
, let the functor
\mathcal{I}^{N}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{S}_{f}}\mathcal{I}^{N^{\prime}}
add empty blocks as in Example
5.41
. Then
\Elts\mathcal{S}
is the category of partitions described above.

Theorem 5.44
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a confluent Ramsey category and let
\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{R}\to\Cat
be a functor such that also
\mathcal{S}_{R}
is a confluent Ramsey category for all objects
R\in\mathcal{R}
. If all above categories are essentially small and locally finite, then
\Elts\mathcal{S}
is confluent and Ramsey.

Let us first sketch the proof. We need to show that diagrams in the shape of
\Elts\mathcal{S}
have a solution, a tuple compatible with all arrows. The partition of
\Elts\mathcal{S}
into its fibers
\mathcal{S}_{R}
distinguishes two classes of arrows: the ones inside the fibers

(R,S)\xrightarrow{(\id_{R},\phi)}(R,S^{\prime})

and the ones ’orthogonal’ to the fibers

(R,S)\xrightarrow{(f,\id)}(R^{\prime},\mathcal{S}_{f}(S)).

The first kind is no problem, every fiber is confluent Ramsey, hence there are partial solutions on every fiber. To deal with the second kind of arrows, note that any
R\xrightarrow{f}R^{\prime}
induces a map from partial solutions on
\mathcal{S}_{R^{\prime}}
to partial solutions on
\mathcal{S}_{R}
similar to Proposition
5.39
.

(x_{S^{\prime}})_{S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{R^{\prime}}}\mapsto\big(\mathcal{D}_{f,\id}(x_{\mathcal{S}_{f}(S)})\big)_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{R}}

This defines a diagram
\mathcal{R}^{\mathop{op}}\to\Set,R\mapsto\lim\mathcal{D}|_{\mathcal{S}_{R}}
whose solutions would correspond to solutions of
\mathcal{D}
. However, there might be infinitely many partial solutions to
\mathcal{S}_{R}
, hence the entire argument must be restricted to finite subcategories.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{D}:(\Elts\mathcal{S})^{\mathop{op}}\to\Set
be a diagram, and let
M
be a finite set of arrows in
\Elts\mathcal{S}
. We show that
\mathcal{D}
has an
M
-solution which will prove the claim by Lemma
3.16
and Theorem
1.2
.

Since
M
is finite, there is a finite list
(R_{i})
of objects in
\mathcal{R}
and finite lists
(S_{i}^{j})
of objects in
\mathcal{S}_{R_{i}}
such that every arrow in
M
is between pairs of the form
(R_{i},S_{i}^{j})
.

Let
\hat{\mathcal{S}}:\mathcal{R}\to\Cat
be the functor that sends
R\in\mathcal{R}
to the full subcategory
\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{R}\leq\mathcal{S}_{R}
, whose objects are of the form
\mathcal{S}_{f}(S_{i}^{j})\in\mathcal{S}_{R}
, where
R_{i}\xrightarrow{f}R
is any morphism from some
R_{i}
to
R
. Note that for each
R
there are only finitely many such objects, as there are finitely many
R_{i}
and hence only finitely many such arrows
f
.
Clearly
\Elts\hat{\mathcal{S}}
is a subcategory of
\Elts\mathcal{S}
which contains all morphisms in
M
, so if there is a partial solution to
\mathcal{D}
on
\Elts\hat{\mathcal{S}}
, then there is also an
M
-solution.

To find a partial solution to
\mathcal{D}
on
\Elts\hat{\mathcal{S}}
, define a diagram
\hat{\mathcal{D}}:\mathcal{R}^{\mathop{op}}\to\Set
that sends
R
to
\lim\mathcal{D}|_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{R}}
, the set of partial solutions to
\mathcal{D}
on
\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
.
This set is finite, since
\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
contains only finitely many objects and nonempty, because there are partial solutions to
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{S}_{R}
. In particular, there are partial solutions on
\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
.
For arrows
R\xrightarrow{f}R^{\prime}
, we define
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{f}:\lim\mathcal{D}|_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{R^{\prime}}}\to\lim\mathcal{D}|_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{R}}
as the following map.

(x_{S^{\prime}})_{S^{\prime}\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{R^{\prime}}}\mapsto\big(\mathcal{D}_{f,\id}(x_{\mathcal{S}_{f}(S)})\big)_{S\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}(R)}

Since
R
is also confluent and Ramsey,
\hat{\mathcal{D}}
will have a solution

\big((x_{R,S})_{S\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{R}}\big)_{R\in\mathcal{R}}

where for each
R\in\mathcal{R}
, the tuple
(x_{R,S})_{S\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{R}}
is a partial solution of
\mathcal{D}
on
\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
. To verify that
(x_{R,S})_{(R,S)\in\Elts\hat{\mathcal{S}}}
is a partial solution of
\mathcal{D}
, take an arrow
(R,S)\xrightarrow{(f,\phi)}(R^{\prime},S^{\prime})
in
\Elts\hat{\mathcal{S}}
and compute the following.

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{f,\phi}(x_{R^{\prime},S^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{f,\id}\big(\mathcal{D}_{\id,\phi}(x_{R^{\prime},S^{\prime}})\big)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{f,\id}(x_{R^{\prime},\mathcal{S}_{f}(S)})

\displaystyle=x_{R,S}

The second equality holds because
(X_{R^{\prime},S^{\prime}})_{S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{R^{\prime}}}
is a partial solution on the fiber
\mathcal{S}_{R^{\prime}}
and the third holds by definition of
\hat{\mathcal{D}}
.
∎

Corollary 5.45

(Theorem 2 in
[
4
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
are two essentially small locally finite confluent Ramsey categories, then their product
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D}
is also confluent and Ramsey.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{C}\to\Cat
is the constant functor
C\mapsto\mathcal{D}
, then
\Elts\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D}
.
∎

Corollary 5.46

(Theorem 5.1.c in
[
5
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is an essentially small locally finite confluent Ramsey category and
\mathcal{S}:A\to\Set
a functor, then
\Elts\mathcal{S}
is also confluent and Ramsey. In particular, if
A\in\mathcal{C}
is an object, then the slice category
A\backslash\mathcal{C}
is confluent Ramsey.

Proof.

Since discrete categories are confluent Ramsey, the first part follows directly from Theorem
5.44
. For the second part, take
\mathcal{S}:C\mapsto\hom(A,C)
, then
\Elts\mathcal{S}=A\backslash\mathcal{C}
is the slice category.
∎

Definition 5.47
.

Given two classes of relational structures
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
, their
blowup

\mathcal{C}\rtimes\mathcal{D}
is the following category. Objects of
\mathcal{C}\rtimes\mathcal{D}
are tuples
(\mathbb{C},(\mathbb{D}_{c})_{c\in C})
where
\mathbb{C}\in\mathcal{C}
and
\mathbb{D}_{c}\in\mathcal{D}
. Arrows between two objects
(\mathbb{C},(\mathbb{D}_{c})_{c\in C})
and
(\mathbb{C}^{\prime},({\mathbb{D}^{\prime}}_{c^{\prime}})_{c^{\prime}\in C^{\prime}})
are tuples
(f,(g_{c})_{c\in C})
where
f
is an embedding
\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{\prime}
and each
g_{c}
is an embedding
\mathbb{D}_{c}\to\mathbb{D}^{\prime}_{f(c)}
.

The following corollary is known, though we were unable to find a direct reference. It follows for example from Theorem 6.1 and Lemma 6.7 in
[
2
]
.

Corollary 5.48
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
be confluent Ramsey classes of relational structures. Then the blowup
\mathcal{C}\rtimes\mathcal{D}
is confluent and Ramsey.

Proof.

The blowup
\mathcal{C}\rtimes\mathcal{D}
can be constructed as
\Elts\mathcal{S}
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the following functor.

\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{C}\to\Cat,\quad\mathbb{C}\mapsto\mathcal{D}^{C}

Given an embedding
\mathbb{C}\xrightarrow{f}\mathbb{C}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{C}
let

\mathcal{S}_{f}:\begin{cases}\mathcal{D}^{C}\to\mathcal{D}^{C^{\prime}}\\

Here

Each

, hence so is

∎

5.3
Free superpositions

We include one more transfer theorem from Bodirsky
[
14
]
, namely that the free superposition of Ramsey classes is again Ramsey, assuming the strong amalgamation property. We consider the presented proof to be conceptually interesting, it uses the trivial transfer (Proposition
5.39
), but one of the two functors is obtained from Theorem
1.2
rather than constructed explicitly.

Definition 5.49
.

We say that a class of structures
\mathcal{C}
has the
strong amalgamation property
if the following holds. Given three structures
\mathbb{A}
,
\mathbb{B}
,
\mathbb{C}
in
\mathcal{C}
, two embeddings
\mathbb{B}\xleftarrow{f}\mathbb{A}\xrightarrow{g}\mathbb{C}
and two injective maps
B\xrightarrow{h}D\xleftarrow{k}C
such that
h\circ f=k\circ g
, and
\mathrm{im}(h)\cap\mathrm{im}(k)=\mathrm{im}(h\circ f)
, there is a structure
\mathbb{D}\in\mathcal{D}
with domain
D
, such that
h
and
k
are embeddings
\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{D}
respectively.

Definition 5.50
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
be two classes of finite relational structures in disjoint signatures
\sigma
and
\tau
, respectively. Let
\mathcal{C}\wedge\mathcal{D}
be the class of all
\sigma\cup\tau
-structures, whose
\sigma
-reduct is in
\mathcal{C}
and whose
\tau
-reduct is in
\mathcal{D}
. The class
\mathcal{C}\wedge\mathcal{D}
is called the
free superposition
of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
.

Proposition 5.51

(Theorem 1.5 in
[
14
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
be two Ramsey classes of finite relational structures, which are closed under taking induced substructures and contain only finitely many structures on any fixed domain. If both
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
have the strong amalgamation property, then the free superposition
\mathcal{C}\wedge\mathcal{D}
is also Ramsey.

Lemma 5.52
.

If a class
\mathcal{C}
of relational structures has the strong amalgamation property, then for a given structure
\mathbb{C}\in\mathcal{C}
and a surjective map
D\xrightarrow{\pi}C
, there is a structure
\mathbb{D}\in\mathcal{C}
with domain
D
, such that any right inverse
C\xrightarrow{s}D
of
\pi
(i.e. any map
s
with
\pi\circ s=\id_{C}
) is an embedding
\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{D}
.

Proof.

We use induction on the size of
D
. As a first step, observe that the statement is true if
D
has the same cardinality as
C
.

If
D
is larger than
C
, any surjective map
\pi:D\to C
factors through a set
D^{\prime}
that contains exactly one element less than
D
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}

By the induction hypothesis, there is a structure
\mathbb{D}^{\prime}
with domain
D^{\prime}
such that every right inverse of
q
is an embedding
\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{D}^{\prime}
.
Now, let
s
and
s^{\prime}
be the two right inverses of
p
and let
\mathbb{D}^{\prime\prime}
be the substructure of
\mathbb{D}^{\prime}
on the subset of
D^{\prime}
on which
s
and
s^{\prime}
agree. By the strong amalgamation property of
\mathcal{C}
, there is a structure
\mathbb{D}
with domain
D
, such that both
s
and
s^{\prime}
are embeddings
\mathbb{D}^{\prime}\to\mathbb{D}
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{D}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{D}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{D}^{\prime\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{D}^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s^{\prime}}

We claim that
\mathbb{D}
is the desired structure. In fact, any right inverse of
\pi
decomposes into a right inverse of

followed by either
s
or

, all of which are embeddings.
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.51
.

Note that there is the following inclusion functor.

\mathcal{C}\wedge\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D},\quad\mathbb{E}\mapsto(\mathbb{E}|_{\sigma},\mathbb{E}|_{\tau})

The aim is to find a functor
G:\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}\wedge\mathcal{D}
and a natural transformation
\Delta
as in the following diagram, then the claim will follow from Proposition
5.39
and Corollary
5.45
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}\wedge\mathcal{D}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}

To that end, for two given structures
\mathbb{C}\in\mathcal{C}
and
\mathbb{D}\in\mathcal{D}

with the following properties.

Every right inverse

of the first projection is an embedding

.

2.

Every right inverse

of the second projection is an embedding
\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{E}|_{\tau}

Such
\mathbb{E}
exist by Lemma

.
Now, define a diagram

to the set of all structures

that fulfill 1) and 2). Given a pair of embeddings

to send a structure

with domain

The sets

, and nonempty by Lemma

. Hence, it has a solution by Theorem

is a structure on domain

is right inverse to both projections, hence it is an embedding

be this embedding.
∎
