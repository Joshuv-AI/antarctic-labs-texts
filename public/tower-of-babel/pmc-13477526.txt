High-altitude polycythemia: State-of-the-art review
Authors: Xiaoming Chen, Ran Cheng, Shipeng Xu, Wuyang Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This review summarizes the research progress on high-altitude polycythemia (HAPC). At the fundamental theoretical level, it provides an in-depth analysis of the epidemiological characteristics influenced by factors such as altitude, genetics, and gender, as well as the pathological mechanisms triggered by chronic hypoxia, including alterations in gene expression, immune imbalance, and disorders of iron metabolism. In the section on clinical manifestations and diagnosis, the multisystem symptoms are elaborated in detail, diagnostic criteria based on hematological indicators combined with high-altitude residence history are clarified, and the key points for differentiating HAPC from primary and other secondary polycythemia are summarized. Regarding treatment strategies, the current application and efficacy of pharmaceutical interventions, non-pharmaceutical methods such as therapeutic erythrocytapheresis (TE), and comprehensive treatment regimens are outlined. Technological advancements focus on genetic research, biomarker development, and imaging technology innovation, providing support for precise diagnosis and treatment. A review of historical development and current status reveals the evolution of research, clarifies present trends in disease prevalence, and identifies challenges in diagnosis and treatment. In the outlook for the future, innovative preventive strategies are proposed, including health education, genetic screening, and the development of novel drug formulations. The research direction of multidisciplinary integration is emphasized, such as elucidating the gene-environment interaction mechanism and developing targeted drugs. Meanwhile, controversial issues including understanding of etiology, evaluation of treatment efficacy, and ethical and social implications, aiming to provide a systematic reference for HAPC research and clinical practice.

1. Epidemiological characteristics of HAPC

HAPC is a chronic adaptive disorder induced by hypoxic conditions at high altitudes, characterized by significant geographical variation and population susceptibility. Geographically, altitude serves as a core determinant influencing the prevalence of HAPC. In regions such as the Tibetan Plateau (≥4000 meters), chronic hypoxic exposure leads to dysregulation erythropoiesis, resulting in a significantly higher incidence of HAPC compared to low-altitude areas. 1 In terms of population susceptibility, genetic background, gender differences, and duration of residence are important factors contributing to the development of HAPC. Studies among Tibetan populations indicate that while some individuals exhibit adaptation to the high-altitude environments, a subset remains susceptible to HAPC. Research conducted in Yushu, Qinghai (altitude ≥3500 m), involving 63 Tibetan HAPC patients and 131 healthy Tibetan controls, demonstrated that multiple single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the EPAS1 gene are associated with susceptibility to HAPC in Tibetans, suggesting a genetic component in its pathogenesis. 2 Furthermore, the incidence appears higher in males than females, potentially due to sex hormone-mediated differences in erythropoiesis regulation. Additionally, long-term high-altitude residents may face an increased risk of HAPC with prolonged exposure.

The epidemiological trends of HAPC are influenced by multiple factors. With economic development and increased population mobility in high-altitude regions, more individuals are migrating to these areas for residence and work, potentially leading to a rising trend in HAPC cases. Moreover, lifestyle changes among high-altitude residents, such as dietary westernization and reduced physical activity, may further influence disease risk. Therefore, monitoring the prevalence trends of HAPC across different high-altitude populations is crucial for developing targeted prevention and treatment strategies.

2. Pathological mechanisms of HAPC

The pathological features of HAPC are based on a complex network of multi-level and multi-system interactions. This network encompasses several key dimensions, including hypoxia stress, gene expression regulation, immune system imbalance, and disordered iron metabolism. Chronic hypoxia, as the central etiology, triggers a compensatory erythropoietic response in the body. Comparative studies have revealed significant differences in various clinical parameters, such as red blood cell (RBC) count and hemoglobin (HGB) concentration, between HAPC patients and healthy controls. Transcriptome sequencing analysis have identified 550 significantly differentially expressed genes in HAPC patients, with upregulated genes primarily enriched in biological processes related to erythrocyte differentiation, development, and maintenance of cellular homeostasis. 3 Genome-wide analyses have identified EPAS1 and EGLN1 as key genes under selection for high-altitude adaptation in Tibetan populations. A SNP in the EPAS1 gene exhibits an allele frequency difference as high as 78% between Tibetan and Han Chinese populations.4,5 Furthermore, the allele frequencies of specific SNPs differ significantly between patients and healthy controls, suggesting an association between EPAS1 polymorphisms and susceptibility to HAPC.2,6,7 Recent studies indicate that EPAS1 and EGLN1 play central roles in hypoxia-induced polycythemia via the hypoxia-inducible factor 2-alpha (HIF-2α)/Erythropoietin (EPO) signaling pathway.8–10 The EPAS1 gene encodes HIF-2α. Under hypoxic conditions, the transcriptional upregulation of non-adaptive EPAS1 variants leads to increased HIF-2α production. 7 The EGLN1 gene encodes prolyl hydroxylase domain-containing protein 2 (PHD2), whose functional adaptation to hypoxia occurs at the post-translational level. 11 Under normoxic conditions, EGLN1 acts as an oxygen-dependent hydroxylase, promoting HIF-2α degradation. During hypoxia, however, EGLN1 hydroxylase activity is inhibited, leading to HIF-2α accumulation. The accumulated HIF-2α protein transactivates numerous downstream target genes—including EPO and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)—whose upregulation contributes to processes such as erythrocyte proliferation and angiogenesis, thereby promoting the development of HAPC. 10 The pathological mechanisms of HAPC are summarized in Figure 1.Figure 1.The pathological mechanisms of HAPC.

EPO is the primary regulator of erythroid proliferation in the bone marrow and plays a critical role in erythropoiesis. Circulating EPO binds to EPO receptors on erythroid progenitor cells in the bone marrow, leading to excessive erythrocyte proliferation.10,12 Animal models simulating hypobaric hypoxia provide key insights into bone marrow reprogramming under chronic hypoxia. This reprogramming is characterized by the selective expansion of megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitors at the expense of granulocyte-macrophage progenitors—a lineage bias associated with transcriptional reprogramming. 13 In a mouse model of HAPC, Liu et al. identified a unique erythroid-biased multipotent progenitor subpopulation that, upon re-exposure to high altitude, rapidly responds to hypoxic stimulation by maintaining a hypomethylated state of erythroid-specific genes. This suggests that hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells may possess a memory function, facilitating rapid adaptation. 14 Further mechanistic studies have revealed epigenetic alterations, notably hypermethylation of the VHL promoter in bone marrow cells, which stabilizes HIF-2α by preventing its ubiquitin-dependent degradation, thereby amplifying the hypoxic signaling cascade. 15 Additionally, in a HAPC mouse model, erythroid cells exhibited active metabolism and autophagy, along with high expression of CD47—a surface protein that protects RBC from phagocytic clearance. The same study demonstrated that CD47 blockade and/or interferon-alpha treatment effectively alleviated polycythemia in HAPC mice.14,16 Similarly, studies using a long-term hypobaric hypoxia mouse model reported a significant reduction in red pulp macrophages in the spleen, which may impair the spleen’s ability to clear erythrocytes, leading to splenomegaly and potentially sustaining excessive erythrocyte production in HAPC. 17

Angiogenesis is also a key component in the pathogenesis of HAPC. Beyond promoting erythrocyte proliferation, EPO possesses anti-apoptotic and pro-angiogenic properties and is involved in angiogenesis via the EPO/VEGF pathway. In a rat model of chronic mountain sickness, EPO was found not only to stimulate erythropoiesis but also to promote bone marrow microvessel formation by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby contributing to HAPC development. 18 VEGF is a mitogen for vascular endothelial cells and is involved in bone marrow microvessel generation in HAPC. Under high-altitude hypoxic conditions, decreased tissue oxygen tension upregulates HIF expression, which in turn binds to hypoxia-response elements and stimulates VEGF expression, mediating endothelial cell proliferation and microvessel formation. Studies have shown a positive correlation between the VEGF-A positivity rate in endothelial cells and microvessel density, highlighting the important role of VEGF-A in HAPC-associated angiogenesis. 19

Furthermore, alterations in immune cell populations influence HAPC susceptibility, patients often show reduced counts of certain immune cell types compared to controls, indicating modulated autoimmunity. Dysregulated iron metabolism also contributes to HAPC pathogenesis, enhanced iron storage and utilization have been observed in patients, potentially linked to disturbances in cytokines such as interleukin-10 and interleukin-22, which may affect ferritin expression and lead to iron metabolism imbalance. 20

3. Clinical manifestations and diagnosis of HAPC

The clinical presentation of HAPC is characterized by multisystem involvement, with its pathophysiology primarily attributed to hemodynamic disturbances and tissue hypoxia resulting from excessive erythrocyte proliferation. Neurological symptoms often constitute the initial clinical manifestation. Patients frequently experience headaches, dizziness, and cognitive impairment, consequences of significantly increased blood viscosity leading to cerebral hypoperfusion.21,22 Beyond neurological involvement, HAPC patients commonly exhibit respiratory symptoms, including dyspnea and shortness of breath. Some may also present with sleep disturbances, fatigue, localized cyanosis, burning sensations in the palms and soles, venous dilatation, musculoskeletal pain, and anorexia.23,24 Furthermore, HAPC can affect the cardiovascular system, leading to blood pressure abnormalities. Studies indicate a positive correlation between HAPC and hypertension, with a 26% increased risk of hypertension for every 10 g/L rise in HGB level. 25

The clinical diagnosis of HAPC requires an integration of multidimensional evidence. Key elements include a history of high-altitude exposure, abnormal hematological indices, and the exclusion of other etiologies for polycythemia. The prevailing diagnostic criteria suggest that an individual with a sustained residence at high altitude, presenting with a HGB level is ≥190 g/L in females or ≥210 g/L in males, accompanied by other hematological alterations indicative of polycythemia, can be preliminarily diagnosed with HAPC. 23 Diagnostic approaches encompass laboratory and ancillary investigations. Laboratory tests are fundamental, with a complete blood count serving as a cornerstone. In addition to elevated HGB and RBC counts, an increased hematocrit is commonly observed. A retrospective analysis of 155 HAPC patients confirmed that pre-treatment mean HGB levels and hematocrit were significantly above the normal range. 1 Furthermore, genetic testing is valuable for elucidating etiology and pathogenesis. Assessing polymorphisms in genes such as EPAS1 and EGLN1 helps evaluate genetic susceptibility. Research involving Tibetan HAPC patients, for instance, has demonstrated associations between disease susceptibility and polymorphisms at EPAS1 loci such as rs13419896 and rs12619696. 2 Ancillary tests like abdominal ultrasound are useful to exclude other conditions that may cause polycythemia, such as pathologies of the liver or spleen.

As a form of secondary polycythemia, diagnosing HAPC necessitates the exclusion of other potential causes of polycythemia. A critical first step is ruling out polycythemia vera (PV), a clonal myeloproliferative neoplasm with a distinct pathophysiology. PV patients typically harbor JAK2 gene mutations, exhibit histologic findings of age-adjusted hypercellular bone marrow with trilineage hyperplasia, have significantly low serum EPO levels, and face increased risks of thrombotic events as well as fibrotic or leukemic transformation. 26 Therefore, the absence of a JAK2 mutation in peripheral blood is crucial for differentiating HAPC from PV. Serum EPO measurement can also aid reliable differentiation, particularly in PV cases with JAK2 exon 12 mutations, where EPO levels are typically subnormal. 27 Furthermore, the clinical phenotype of secondary erythrocytosis differs from PV. patients with secondary erythrocytosis generally exhibit lower leukocyte and platelet counts, higher EPO levels, lower lactate dehydrogenase levels, and lack signs of splenomegaly. 28 Notably, a comparative diagnostic study conducted at high altitude demonstrated that these phenotypic distinctions remained entirely consistent between 110 cases of high-altitude-associated erythrocytosis and 22 cases of PV. 29 Therefore, assessment of these parameters can offer key insights for distinguishing PV from altitude-associated or secondary polycythemia. Secondary polycythemia has traditionally been considered a benign condition with a lower thrombotic risk than PV, which reportedly has a thrombosis incidence of 12-40%.30–33 However, a recent study revealed a comparable thrombotic risk between patients with secondary polycythemia and those with primary PV, suggesting that secondary polycythemia may not be benign from a thrombotic perspective. 28 In HAPC patients, thrombotic events primarily occur in the venous system, manifesting as deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, stroke, and splanchnic vein thrombosis.34–37 This elevated risk is attributed to the hypercoagulable state associated with HAPC. 38 Finally, other causes of secondary polycythemia must be excluded, including chronic lung diseases, congenital heart disease, obstructive sleep apnea hypopnea syndrome, renal artery stenosis or sclerosis, polycystic kidney disease, status post kidney transplantation, and other chronic conditions that exacerbate hypoxemia.

4. Treatment strategies of HAPC

Pharmacological treatment of HAPC primarily focuses on modulating erythropoiesis and improving multi-system function. Acetazolamide, a classic carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, can reduce serum EPO levels and hematocrit while improving the hypoxic ventilatory response. Multiple double-blind, randomized controlled trials over the past two decades have underscored its efficacy in treating HAPC, and it is now widely used in clinical management.39–41 Furthermore, a regimen of acetazolamide at 250 mg twice daily has been shown to significantly reduce proteinuria and HGB levels in patients with focal segmental glomerulosclerosis. The rebound of these parameters upon discontinuation further confirms treatment dependency. 42 Other medications, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, methylxanthines, adrenergic receptor blockers, dopamine receptor antagonists, and central or peripheral respiratory stimulants, have demonstrated effectiveness in other forms of polycythemia but lack robust clinical evidence regarding their safety and efficacy specifically for HAPC.43,44 With advancing research into molecular mechanisms, novel drug targets are continuously being identified. Agents such as lomustine and hydrazine compounds, which modulate aberrant erythropoiesis by targeting pathways related to neutrophil migration and glycoprotein metabolism, have emerged as potential therapeutic candidates. 45 Natural compounds like resveratrol can reverse hypoxia-induced proteomic changes and effectively correct abnormalities in HGB and platelet counts in rat models, offering a new direction for the development of natural therapeutics for HAPC. 46 These agents, through their diverse mechanisms of action, synergistically regulate erythropoiesis, providing a theoretical basis for personalized clinical pharmacotherapy.

Non-pharmacological interventions play an indispensable role in the treatment of HAPC, with therapeutic erythrocytapheresis (TE) and lifestyle modifications being central approaches. TE rapidly removes excess RBC, thereby significantly improving hemorheological parameters. Although randomized controlled trials on its safety and efficacy are currently lacking, a retrospective analysis of 155 HAPC patients showed that this procedure reduced RBC count by 1.5×1012/L, HGB by 52 g/L, and hematocrit by 14% (all P<0.001). 1 Another study involving 631 patients demonstrated that TE not only improved HGB levels but also enhanced renal function by increasing the glomerular filtration rate and reducing uric acid levels. The proportion of patients with negative urinary protein increased from 54.58% to 92.83%. 47 Chinese guidelines recommend initiating erythrocytapheresis when HGB exceeds 250 g/L in HAPC patients, and the indication may be extended to a HGB level ≥210 g/L if significant clinical symptoms or adverse factors such as coagulation dysfunction are present. 24 Compared to traditional phlebotomy, TE offers multiple advantages, with a one-year efficacy rate as high as 88.5%, longer remission duration, and lower recurrence rates. 48 Currently, the target hematocrit for erythrocytapheresis in HAPC remains undefined. Referencing recommendations from the Italian Society of Hematology and Transfusion for phlebotomy in polycythemia, a hematocrit below 45% is suggested. 49 While no randomized controlled trials have confirmed the necessity of antithrombotic therapy in HAPC, considering the discussed thrombotic risks, Chinese guidelines suggest the judicious use of enteric-coated aspirin (75-100 mg/day) for antiplatelet aggregation to prevent thromboembolism. 24 Additionally, lifestyle modifications can alleviate symptoms in some patients. For instance, appropriate physical exercise can enhance cardiopulmonary function and improve blood circulation. 50 Furthermore, dietary adjustments, such as controlled iron intake, may benefit HAPC patients with abnormal iron metabolism. 51

Comprehensive treatment regimens for HAPC integrate pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes. For HAPC patients, a combination of drug therapy and TE is commonly employed. Studies indicate that for HAPC patients complicated with focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, adding acetazolamide after reducing HGB via erythrocytapheresis can further decrease proteinuria and improve the clinical condition. 42 The comprehensive treatment process also requires holistic attention to the patient’s overall health. For patients with complications such as hypertension, active blood pressure control can reduce cardiovascular risk. 52 Simultaneously, for patients experiencing sleep disturbances, improving sleep quality is crucial, as adequate sleep aids physical recovery and regulate endocrine function, thereby indirectly influencing erythropoiesis. Moreover, regular patient follow-up, monitoring of hematological indicators, renal function, and other parameters, along with timely adjustment of the treatment plan based on individual conditions, is essential to ensure therapeutic efficacy and safety. Through comprehensive management, it is anticipated that the condition of HAPC patients can be better controlled, their quality of life improved, and the incidence of complications reduced.

5. Advances in the diagnosis and treatment of HAPC

The identification of biomarkers for HAPC holds significant importance for early diagnosis and disease monitoring. Metabolomic approaches applied to identify potential biomarkers and pathways associated with high-altitude pulmonary edema revealed dysregulated metabolic pathways involving pyrimidine metabolism, the citric acid cycle, sulfur metabolism, phenylalanine metabolism, and purine metabolism. Metabolites abnormally elevated within these dysregulated pathways show promise as potential biomarkers. 53 For instance, serum uric acid levels in high-altitude residents are often positively correlated with hematocrit, a phenomenon recently reconfirmed by large-scale epidemiological observations in high-altitude regions of China.54,55 Furthermore, several studies suggest that serum inflammatory cytokines may also serve as potential biomarkers for HAPC. In one study, participants were divided into plain control, plateau control, early HAPC, and confirmed HAPC groups. Serum inflammatory cytokines were measured using antibody microarray technology. The results indicated that levels of interleukin-2, interleukin-3, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 were significantly elevated in the early HAPC group. In the confirmed HAPC group, the levels of interleukin-1β, interleukin-2, interleukin-3, tumor necrosis factor-α, MCP-1, and interleukin-16 were all higher than those in the high-altitude control group. This suggests that these inflammatory cytokines may be involved in the pathogenesis of HAPC and the process of adaptation to the high-altitude environment, holding potential as biomarkers for early diagnosis and disease monitoring of HAPC. 56 Additionally, plasma proteomic analysis revealed that, compared to healthy control, seven proteins were upregulated and twelve were downregulated in HAPC patients. The differentially expressed proteins were primarily involved in complement and coagulation cascades, inflammation, and immune response. Among them, carnosinase dipeptidase 1 (CNDP1) was identified for the first time as a strong plasma biomarker for HAPC. 57 As an enzyme specifically degrading carnosine and anserine, CNDP1 is responsible for maintaining carnosine homeostasis in the body. CNDP1 may exert protective effects in metabolic diseases (including diabetic nephropathy), tumors, and cardiovascular diseases through mechanisms such as modulating constitutive androstane receptor levels, serving as an important diagnostic marker, biomarker, and potential therapeutic target for these conditions. 58 The mechanism of action of CNDP1 in HAPC remains unclear and requires further investigation using molecular biology tools such as cell and animal models to evaluate its role in HAPC pathogenesis. These potential biomarkers still require validation in larger sample sizes to confirm their potential.

Imaging techniques play a significant role in the diagnosis and differential diagnosis of HAPC. During the diagnostic evaluation of polycythemia, abdominal ultrasound can assess the morphology and function of organs such as the liver and spleen, aiding in the exclusion of other diseases that may cause polycythemia. For example, splenomegaly may be associated with certain hematological disorders. For patients suspected of having PV, combining abdominal ultrasound with other tests such as complete blood count can lead to a more accurate diagnosis. Retrospective studies performing both abdominal ultrasound and complete blood counts in patients evaluated for polycythemia found that, in addition to characteristic hematological changes, PV patients may also exhibit abnormal splenomegaly on ultrasound imaging. 29 Furthermore, for HAPC patients, chest imaging can be used to assess cardiopulmonary function and exclude secondary polycythemia caused by cardiopulmonary diseases. In clinical practice, it is observed that chest imaging in patients with cardiopulmonary diseases may reveal abnormalities such as pulmonary lesions or cardiomegaly. In contrast, chest imaging in HAPC patients typically shows no specific significant changes, which aids in differentiate HAPC from polycythemia secondary to cardiopulmonary diseases.

6. Controversies and challenges of HAPC

Although the etiology of HAPC is primarily linked to the hypoxic high-altitude environment, controversies persist regarding its precise pathogenesis. Firstly, the specific mechanisms by which genetic factors contribute to HAPC development have not been fully elucidated. Although studies have identified associations between susceptibility to HAPC and polymorphisms in multiple genes, such as EPAS1, CYP17A1, and CYP2E1, it remains unclear how these genetic variants precisely regulate erythropoiesis and interact with one another. 59 Secondly, significant debates exist concerning the interaction between environmental and genetic factors. In the same hypoxic high-altitude environment, some individuals develop the disease while others do not. Beyond genetic predisposition, how environmental factors such as diet and lifestyle interact with genetic factors to influence disease onset is not yet fully understood. Additionally, diagnosing the etiology proves challenging in specific cases, such as patients with comorbidities, it is difficult to determine whether elevated RBC counts are attributable to the high-altitude hypoxic environment, genetic factors, or other underlying diseases, posing a significant challenges for etiological diagnosis and treatment. The evaluation of HAPC treatment efficacy also faces major challenges. Currently, assessment of therapeutic effectiveness relies mainly on changes in hematological indicators, such as HGB levels and RBC counts. However, these indicators reflect only part of the patient’s physiological state and do not fully represent overall health status or quality of life. In clinical practice, some patients continue to experience symptoms like headache and dizziness despite improvements in hematological parameters, with no significant enhancement in their quality of life.

Evaluating the long-term efficacy of different treatment modalities presents considerable difficulties. For pharmacological treatments, the long-term safety and effectiveness of certain medications lack support from large-scale, long-term clinical studies, making it challenging to accurately assess their sustained therapeutic benefits. While non-pharmacological interventions like TE can rapidly reduce RBC levels, sufficient research data on their impact on key long-term outcomes—such as complication rates and survival—are still lacking. Furthermore, significant inter-individual variability exists in patient responses to treatment. How to develop personalized treatment plans based on individual patient characteristics and accurately evaluate their efficacy has become a pressing practical challenge.

7. Future perspectives of HAPC

HAPC remains a prevalent health issue in high-altitude regions, posing a significant threat to the quality of life and overall health of local residents. Regarding diagnosis, although relatively clear diagnostic criteria have been established, difficulties persist in identifying atypical cases, Further efforts are needed to further optimize diagnostic procedures and methodologies. In terms of treatment, TE continues to be a cornerstone intervention for rapidly reducing hematocrit. However, the recurrence of reactive polycythemia within weeks highlights the transient nature of its efficacy. Research suggests that adjunctive traditional Chinese medicine may partially mitigate this limitation, and the development of novel pharmaceutical agents to address the shortcomings of erythrocytapheresis represents a promising research direction. 43 In the realm of pharmacotherapy, methazolamide—a methylated, lipophilic analogue of acetazolamide—has demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction in hematocrit and blood viscosity in a rat model of high-altitude illness, with fewer side effects than acetazolamide.60,61 This indicates considerable potential for methazolamide in HAPC treatment. Pending further validation of its long-term efficacy and safety, it holds promise for future clinical application. A more crucial research direction involves targeted interventions that regulate erythropoietic pathways. For example, the HIF-2α/EPO signaling pathway plays a central role in hypoxia-induced polycythemia. Preclinical studies on HIF-2α inhibitors (e.g., PT2385) suggest their potential to suppress erythrocytosis. 62 However, as such drugs may lead to respiratory control deficits and hematopoietic imbalances, further in-depth investigation is required before clinical application. Another emerging therapeutic concept is the modulation of iron homeostasis through hepcidin mimetics. While no preclinical or clinical data are currently available for high-altitude polycythemia, the efficacy of rusfertide in controlling erythrocytosis and reducing phlebotomy requirements in polycythemia vera provides proof of concept that restricting iron availability may represent an alternative strategy to modulate excessive erythropoiesis. 63 Whether this approach could be applicable to hypoxia-driven erythrocytosis remains unknown and warrants further investigation. Innovative prevention strategies are essential for reducing the disease burden of HAPC. Establishing a tiered health education system to provide precise dietary guidance for individuals with iron metabolism abnormalities is crucial. Advancing the clinical translation of genetic testing to screen for risk genes and develop predictive models will facilitate early intervention for high-risk populations. Developing novel biomarker assays for early warning and disease monitoring is also vital. For instance, tandem mass tag based quantitative proteomics has identified CNDP1 as a strong plasma biomarker for HAPC (area under the ROC curve: 0.850, 95% CI: 0.753-0.920, p<0.0001), with a sensitivity of 86.5% and specificity of 90%, demonstrating both diagnostic and therapeutic potential. 57

Despite extensive research on the pathogenesis of HAPC, certain aspects remain unclear—such as the specific mechanisms underlying the interaction between particular genetic variants and environmental factors. This gap limits the development of more effective treatment strategies. Therefore, elucidating the molecular and cellular basis of HAPC should be a key focus of subsequent research efforts.

8. Conclusion

HAPC is a chronic adaptive disorder triggered by hypoxic conditions at high altitudes, exhibiting marked geographical variation and population-specific susceptibility. With economic growth and increased population mobility in high-altitude regions, the incidence of high-altitude polycythemia is rising. The limited efficacy of conventional treatments, coupled with an incomplete understanding of its underlying mechanisms, significantly hampers the development of effective therapies. In recent years, genetic and proteomic studies have offered valuable insights into the pathophysiology of this condition. Genomic technologies have enabled the identification of functional single-nucleotide polymorphisms, revealing that EPAS1 and EGLN1 genes play a central role in hypoxia-induced erythrocytosis via the HIF-2α/EPO signaling pathway. Preclinical studies on HIF-2α inhibitors have demonstrated their potential to suppress erythrocytosis. Meanwhile, proteomic research has identified multiple biomarkers strongly associated with high-altitude polycythemia. Further development of these biomarkers is expected to facilitate early detection and disease monitoring.
