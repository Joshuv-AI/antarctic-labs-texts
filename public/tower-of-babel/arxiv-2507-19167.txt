Studying propagating turbulent structures in the near wake of a sphere using Hilbert proper orthogonal decomposition
Shaun Davey, Callum Atkinson and Julio Soria

Abstract
Turbulent flows, despite their apparent randomness, exhibit coherent structures that underpin their dynamics. Proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) has been widely used to extract these structures from experimental data. While periodic features like vortex shedding can be identified using POD mode pairs when periodicity dominates the flow, detecting such structures in complex flows is more challenging. The Hilbert proper orthogonal decomposition (HPOD) addresses this by applying POD to the analytic signal of the turbulent fluctuations, yielding complex modes with a 90 ∘ 90^{\circ} phase shift between the real and imaginary components. These modes capture propagating structures effectively but introduce filtering artefacts from the Hilbert transform that is used to derive the analytic signal. The current work investigates the relationship between the modes of the POD and those of the HPOD on the velocity fluctuations in the wake of a sphere. By comparing their outputs, POD mode pairs that correspond to the same propagating structures revealed by HPOD are identified. Furthermore, this study explored whether computing the analytic signal of the POD modes can replicate the HPOD modes, offering a more computationally efficient method for determining the pairs of POD modes that represent propagating structures. The results show that the pairs of POD modes identified by the HPOD can be more efficiently determined using the Hilbert transform directly on the POD modes. This method enhances the interpretive power of POD, enabling more detailed analysis of turbulent dynamics without introducing the filtering from the Hilbert transform.

Introduction

While turbulence often appears random, many turbulent flows contain underlying structures, such as eddies and vortices, the scale and nature of which vary depending on the conditions of the flow.
Identifying these structures allows for a more detailed description of the flow, as well as the isolation of the dominant structures in the turbulence based on their contribution to the quantity or phenomenon of interest, such as
TKE
(
TKE
) or mixing.
Ranking these structures based on their significance to a given application or study allows the construction of parsimonious reduced-order models, which can be used to reduce the computational intensity of analyses or to reduce noise from experimental data.

The complex flow phenomena present in the flow over bluff bodies, even at low Reynolds numbers, develop as the Reynolds number increases, and are a subject of ongoing study.
In this respect, the flow over spheres serves as a canonical model for flows over axisymmetric bluff bodies, and is of particular interest for developing a broad understanding of these flow phenomena.
The phenomena observed in the wake of a sphere include transition from axisymmetry to planar symmetry to asymmetry, the formation and shedding of vortices, the development of shear layer instabilities, and flow separation.
As the flow over a sphere varies significantly with Reynolds number, experimental and numerical investigations have covered a wide range of Reynolds number regimes.

In the context of experimental studies of the flow over spheres, one of the main challenges is fixing the sphere in a wind or water tunnel test section with minimal disturbance of the flow, since more often than not, the mounting/support structure of the sphere can have a significant influence on the flow.
The supports chosen for an experiment should simultaneously provide rigid and stable positioning of the sphere, and minimise the disturbance to the flow, while also facilitating the placement of probes without obstructing optical measurement access.

2
Theoretical Considerations

2.1
Proper Orthogonal Decomposition

POD
decomposes statistically independent realisations of the random field
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
into orthonormal spatial modes
\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
with temporal coefficients
a_{k}(t)
, which are ranked by their energy
\lambda_{k}
, from largest to smallest, such that

\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}(t)\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(1)

and is analogous to
SVD
(
SVD
) and
PCA
(
PCA
).
In the application of
POD
to turbulence,
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
represents the turbulent fluctuations, and
\lambda_{k}
represents the contribution of the
k^{th}
mode to the total
TKE
of
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
.
A common use for
POD
is the definition of a reduced-order representation of
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
by reconstructing the original data using
\hat{K}<K
modes

\hat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\hat{K}}a_{k}(t)\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(2)

which represents a portion of the total kinetic energy equal to

TKE=100\times\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\hat{K}}\lambda_{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{k}}\ \%.

(3)

In order to perform
POD
, the two-point correlation of
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
in space

C(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\int_{T}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},t)\,dt,

(4)

is calculated, where
T
is the temporal domain.
The spatial modes and the corresponding energy are given by the eigendecomposition of
C(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

\int_{S}C(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\psi(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,d\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=\lambda\psi(\boldsymbol{x}),

(5)

where
S
is the spatial domain.
The temporal coefficients are the projection of
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
onto
\psi(\boldsymbol{x})

a(t)=\int_{S}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\psi(\boldsymbol{x})\,d\boldsymbol{x}.

(6)

Alternatively,
POD
can be performed using the method of snapshots, which first determines the temporal coefficients, and then the spatial modes.
This method is more computationally efficient when the number of spatial coordinates included in
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
is larger than the number of realisations of the field.
The two-point correlation in time

C(t,t^{\prime})=\int_{S}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t^{\prime})\,d\boldsymbol{x},

(7)

is used instead of that in space, given in equation (
4
).
The eigendecomposition of
C(t,t^{\prime})

\int_{T}C(t,t^{\prime})a(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}=\lambda a(t),

(8)

yields the eigenvalues and temporal coefficients.
The spatial modes are then determined by the projection of
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
onto
a(t)

\psi(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{T}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)a(t)\,dt.

(9)

The temporal coefficients of the
i^{th}
and
j^{th}

POD
modes can be expressed in terms of their phase angle
\phi_{i,j}
as

a_{i}(t)=\sqrt{2\lambda_{i}}\cos{\big(\phi_{i,j}(t)\big)},

(10)

and

a_{j}(t)=\sqrt{2\lambda_{j}}\sin{\big(\phi_{i,j}(t)\big)},

(11)

respectively.
Thus, the phase angle
\phi_{i,j}(t)
is given by

\phi_{i,j}(t)=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a_{j}(t)/\sqrt{2\lambda_{j}}}{a_{i}(t)/\sqrt{2\lambda_{i}}}\right).

(12)

The phase average of the data using the
i^{th}
and
j^{th}

POD
modes at the phase angle
\phi
is given by

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{x},\phi)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}(t)\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\,|\,\phi_{i,j}(t)=\phi

(13)

where
\phi_{i,j}(t)
is defined by equation (
12
).
While any two
POD
modes can be used for phase-averaging
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
, not all selections of modes result in physically meaningful phase averages.
In the case of strongly dominant periodic fluctuations, the temporal coefficients of the corresponding modes form a circle
[
26
]
.
Large periodic structures can also be identified by comparing the phase angle of the modes to an independent measurement of the phase of the flow, such as pressure signals
[
28
]
.
However, the less prominent the periodic structures, the more difficult it is to relate the
POD
modes which represent them.

2.2
Hilbert Proper Orthogonal Decomposition

HPOD
embeds the phase information in the modes by replacing
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
with its analytic signal
[
32
]

\boldsymbol{f}^{a}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)+i\mathcal{H}[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)],

(14)

where
\mathcal{H}
denotes the Hilbert transform of
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
in time

\mathcal{H}\big[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\big]=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},\tau)}{t-\tau}\,d\tau.

(15)

The Hilbert transform is equivalent to a
\mp 90^{\circ}
phase shift
[
36
]
of the fundamental frequencies of
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
in Fourier space

\mathcal{H}\big[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\big]=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\Big[-i\operatorname{sgn}(f)\mathcal{F}\big[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\big]\Big],

(16)

where
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
are the Fourier transform and inverse Fourier transform, respectively, and

\operatorname{sgn}(f)=\begin{cases}-1&if \it{f} $<$0\\
\phantom{-}0&if \it{f} = 0\\
\phantom{-}1&if \it{f} $>$0\\
\end{cases}

(17)

is the sign of the instantaneous frequency
f
of each term in the Fourier series given by
\mathcal{F}\big[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\big]
.

For time-resolved data, the Hilbert transform can be performed in time to determine the analytic signal
\boldsymbol{f}^{a}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
.

HPOD
is performed on time-resolved data by replacing
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
with its analytic signal in equations (
4
) to (
6
).
However, where the coherent structures propagate downstream, the Hilbert transform can be performed in the streamwise direction, as propagation in this direction serves as an analogy for a change in time, to apply this method to data that is not time-resolved
[
33
,
34
,
35
]

\mathcal{H}_{x}\big[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\big]=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\boldsymbol{f}\big((\xi,\ldots),t\big)}{x-\xi}\,d\xi.

(18)

In this case, the analytic signal of
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
is defined using
\mathcal{H}_{x}

\boldsymbol{f}^{a}_{x}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)+i\mathcal{H}_{x}\big[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\big],

(19)

and substituted for
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
in equations (
7
) to (
9
).
The resulting eigenvalues
\lambda^{a}_{k}
are the contribution of the
k^{th}
mode to the total
TKE
of the analytic signal, and are real-valued.
The spatial modes
\psi^{a}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
are complex, with the imaginary part of
\psi^{a}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
representing a phase shift of its real part by
\mp 90^{\circ}
.

The instantaneous amplitude and phase angle of the
k^{th}
mode are defined by the real
\Re
and imaginary
\Im
parts of
a^{a}_{k}(t)
as

|a^{a}_{k}(t)|=\sqrt{\Re{\big(a^{a}_{k}(t)\big)}^{2}+\Im{\big(a^{a}_{k}(t)\big)}^{2}},

(20)

and

\phi^{a}_{k}(t)=\tan^{-1}\left(\mp\frac{\Im\big(a^{a}_{k}(t)\big)}{\Re\big(a^{a}_{k}(t)\big)}\right),

(21)

respectively.
The instantaneous amplitude denotes the strength of the mode at each instant in time, and the instantaneous phase angle denotes the mode shape, which oscillates between the real and imaginary parts of
\psi_{k}^{a}(\boldsymbol{x})
at
\phi_{k}^{a}(t)
equal to
0^{\circ}
and
\mp 90^{\circ}
, respectively.

The analytic signal can be reconstructed using
\hat{K}
modes by

\hat{\boldsymbol{f}^{a}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\hat{K}}a^{a}_{k}(t)\psi^{a}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(22)

the real part of which is the reduced-order model of
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
including the modes which contribute the most
TKE
to the analytic signal.
This reconstruction favours the propagating components of
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
, but introduces artefacts due to the Hilbert transform.

2.3
Hilbert Transform of POD Modes

Although the
POD
modes do not contain temporal information, the streamwise Hilbert transform can be performed directly on each of the modes.
Thus, the analytic signal of the
POD
mode
\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
can be defined as

(\psi_{k})^{a}(\boldsymbol{x})=\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})+i\mathcal{H}_{x}\big[\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\big].

(23)

Substituting the analytic signal of the modes for the
POD
modes in equation (
1
) yields

\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}(t)\Big((\psi_{k})^{a}(\boldsymbol{x})\Big)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}(t)\Big(\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})+i\mathcal{H}_{x}\big[\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\big]\Big),

(24)

which can be expanded to

\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}(t)\Big((\psi_{k})^{a}(\boldsymbol{x})\Big)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}(t)\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})+a_{k}(t)i\mathcal{H}_{x}\big[\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\big].

(25)

As the Hilbert transform is a linear operator, this can be rewritten as

\begin{aligned} \sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}(t)\Big((\psi_{k})^{a}(\boldsymbol{x})\Big)&=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}(t)\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})+i\mathcal{H}_{x}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}(t)\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\Big]\\
&=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)+i\mathcal{H}_{x}[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)]\\
&=\boldsymbol{f}^{a}_{x}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\\
\end{aligned},

(26)

which is the analytic signal of
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
.

Therefore, applying the transform to each of the
POD
modes of
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
individually and using the resulting analytic signals in place of the original modes allows for the reconstruction of
\boldsymbol{f}^{a}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
.
However, the analytic signal of the
POD
modes are not equal to the
HPOD
modes due to the filtering of the Hilbert transform being introduced after the decomposition of
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
.
While this method is not equivalent to
HPOD
, where the propagation of structures is embedded in the modes, it is a computationally efficient alternative for matching pairs of
POD
modes which represent the same propagating structure.

3.2
Experimental Method

Measurements of the flow in the wake of the sphere were taken with a freestream velocity of
U_{\infty}=200
m/s, resulting in a Reynolds number of
Re_{D}=7780
.
Under these conditions, the background turbulence is 2%, and the mean convective time of the flow

t_{c}=\frac{D}{U_{\infty}},

(27)

is 0.2 seconds
[
39
]
.
The flow was seeded with 11 µm glass spheres, and the single-exposed particle image pairs were recorded using a PCO Panda 26 DS camera with a 5120
\times
5120 px array of 2.5 µm
\times
2.5 µm pixels.
A Zeiss lens with a fixed 50 mm focal length was used to capture a 190 mm
\times
190 mm field of view, resulting in a spatial resolution is 37 µm/px.
The field of view began just behind the trailing edge of the sphere, to avoid reflections off the surface, and extended to 5.2 diameters downstream of the sphere.
The field of view was aligned with the centreline of the sphere, and extended to 2.3 diameters either side of the centreline in the transverse direction.
The f-stop was set to 2.8, resulting in a 5.0 mm depth of field.
The diffraction limited minimum image diameter and expected particle image diameter for an 11 µm particle at these setting are 3.9 µm and 1.6 px, respectively.

The particle images were illuminated using 7 ns pulses from a pair of 400mJ Spectra Physics Nd:YAG lasers.
The beams of these lasers were aligned co-linearly before being shaped into a sheet aligned to the centre of the tunnel and the corresponding meridian of the sphere.
The thickness of the laser sheet throughout the field of view was 300 µm.
Pairs of single-exposed images where captured with a separation of
\Delta t=1
ms at an acquisition rate of 1 Hz (
\Delta T=1
s), using a BeagleBone Black pulse generator
[
40
]
to control the timing and synchronisation of the camera and laser pulses.

3.3
Data Processing

The mean background images of the first and second exposures were subtracted from the corresponding single-exposed particle image pairs.
The instantaneous streamwise and transverse velocities,

(

)

A final interrogation window of 64 px
\times

\times

The quality of the cross-correlation was increased through the application of an 8-point Hart filter.
A maximum displacement limit of 20 px and normalised local median filter
[

The validation criteria resulted in a rejection of 2.3% of vectors, which were replaced by an interpolation of a 2nd-order polynomial fit of their 13 nearest neighbours

A total of

4.1
POD and HPOD Modes

POD
was performed by arranging the turbulent fluctuations into the matrix

\boldsymbol{X}=\begin{bmatrix}u^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},t_{1})&\cdots&u^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}_{N_{S}},t_{1})&v^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},t_{1})&\cdots&v^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}_{N_{S}},t_{1})\\
\vdots&u^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},t_{m})&\vdots&\vdots&v^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},t_{m})&\vdots\\
u^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},t_{N_{T}})&\cdots&u^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}_{N_{S}},t_{N_{T}})&v^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},t_{N_{T}})&\cdots&v^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}_{N_{S}},t_{N_{T}})\\
\end{bmatrix}

(30)

where
N_{S}
is the number of velocity vectors per velocity field and
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}={(x,y)}_{n}
is the spatial coordinates of the
n^{th}
vector, which has fluctuating components denoted by
u^{\prime}
and
v^{\prime}
in the streamwise and transverse directions, respectively.
As
N_{S}>N_{T}
, the method of snapshots was used to perform the
POD
, with the two-point correlation of
\boldsymbol{X}
in time given by the matrix form of equation (
7
)

\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{T},

(31)

the eigendecomposition of which

\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\lambda\boldsymbol{\Phi},

(32)

yielded the eigenvectors
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
and eigenvalues
\lambda
.
The temporal coefficients were determined from
\boldsymbol{\Phi}

a_{k}(t_{m})=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{m,k},

(33)

and the spatial modes were given by the projection of
\boldsymbol{X}
onto
\boldsymbol{\Phi}

\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})=(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{T}\boldsymbol{X})_{k,n}.

(34)

The analytic signal of
\boldsymbol{X}

\boldsymbol{X}^{a}=\boldsymbol{X}+i\mathcal{H}_{x}[\boldsymbol{X}],

(35)

was determined using equations (
18
) and (
19
), and
HPOD
was performed by substituting
\boldsymbol{X}^{a}
for
\boldsymbol{X}
in equations (
31
) to (
34
).
The resulting eigenvalues, temporal coefficients and spatial modes are denoted by
\lambda^{a}_{k}
,
a_{k}^{a}(t)
and
\psi_{k}^{a}(\boldsymbol{x})
, respectively.

The contribution of the leading
HPOD
modes to the
TKE
of
\boldsymbol{X}^{a}
is significantly larger than the contribution of the leading
POD
modes to the
TKE
of
\boldsymbol{X}
.
The first, second and third modes of each decomposition contribute 7.70%, 4.53% and 3.70%, and 4.8%, 3.39% and 2.55%, respectively.
However, the difference in the contribution of the higher order modes to the
TKE
is less significant, as shown in figure
2
a.
The result is that the cumulative contribution to
TKE
of a given number of the
HPOD
modes is larger than that of the equivalent number of
POD
modes, as shown in figure
2
b and table
2
.
However, this is not a one-to-one comparison, as the total
TKE
of the analytic signal
\boldsymbol{X}^{a}
is larger than that of the data
\boldsymbol{X}
, at 571,871 m
2
/s
2
and 300,542 m
2
/s
2
, respectively.
This is due to the imaginary component of
\boldsymbol{X}^{a}
, which is not present in
\boldsymbol{X}
.

4.2
Pairing POD Modes using HPOD Modes

As
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
is given by the real part of
\boldsymbol{f}^{a}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
, it can be expressed in terms of the
HPOD
modes as

\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\Re\big(\boldsymbol{f}^{a}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\big)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Re\big(a_{k}^{a}(t)\psi^{a}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\big),

(36)

which can be expanded to

\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\Re\big(\boldsymbol{f}^{a}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\big)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Re\big(a_{k}^{a}(t)\big)\Re\big(\psi^{a}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)-\Im\big(a_{k}^{a}(t)\big)\Im\big(\psi_{k}^{a}(\boldsymbol{x})\big).

(37)

As the real and imaginary parts of
a_{k}^{a}(t)
can be expressed as
|a^{a}_{k}(t)|\cos(\phi^{a}_{k}(t))
and
-|a^{a}_{k}(t)|\sin(\phi^{a}_{k}(t))
, respectively, the shape of the
k^{th}

HPOD
mode at the phase angle
\phi_{k}^{a}(t)
is given by

\frac{\psi_{k}^{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\phi_{k}^{a}(t))}{|a_{k}^{a}(t)|}=\cos\big(\phi_{k}^{a}(t)\big)\Re\big(\psi_{k}^{a}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)+\sin\big(\phi_{k}^{a}(t)\big)\Im\big(\psi_{k}^{a}(\boldsymbol{x})\big).

(38)

The mode shape of the
k^{th}

HPOD
mode matches the
j^{th}

POD
mode when

\psi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\Delta\phi^{a}_{k,j})&\sin(\Delta\phi^{a}_{k,j})\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re\big(\psi^{a}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)\\
-\Im\big(\psi^{a}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)\\
\end{bmatrix},

(39)

where
\Delta\phi_{k,j}^{a}
is the phase offset of the
k^{th}

HPOD
mode which results in the best match of the
j^{th}

POD
mode.
The negative of the imaginary part is used to account for the negative introduced by the Hilbert transform for positive instantaneous frequencies, given in equation (
16
), which results in the phase of the
HPOD
modes being reversed relative to the motion of the structure.

The phase angles at which the
k^{th}

HPOD
mode matches each of the
POD
modes can be determined simultaneously by

\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\Delta\phi^{a}_{k,1})&\sin(\Delta\phi^{a}_{k,1})\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\cos(\Delta\phi^{a}_{k,j})&\sin(\Delta\phi^{a}_{k,j})\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\cos(\Delta\phi^{a}_{k,K})&\sin(\Delta\phi^{a}_{k,K})\\
\end{bmatrix}^{T}={\left(\begin{bmatrix}\Re\big(\psi^{a}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)\\
-\Im\big(\psi^{a}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re\big(\psi^{a}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)\\
-\Im\big(\psi^{a}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)\\
\end{bmatrix}^{T}\right)}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\Re\big(\psi^{a}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)\\
-\Im\big(\psi^{a}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{K}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\end{bmatrix}^{T},

(40)

and

\Delta\phi^{a}_{k,j}=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\sin(\Delta\phi^{a}_{k,j})}{\cos(\Delta\phi^{a}_{k,j})}\right).

(41)

The correlation coefficient of the
k^{th}

HPOD
mode at
\Delta\phi^{a}_{k,j}
and the
j^{th}

POD
mode is

\mathcal{R}_{k,j}=\frac{\operatorname{cov}\big(\psi_{k}^{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\Delta\phi^{a}_{k,j}),\psi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}\big(\psi_{k}^{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\Delta\phi^{a}_{k,j})\big)\operatorname{var}\big(\psi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x},)\big)}},

(42)

and the corresponding
R^{2}
value is

R^{2}_{k,j}=1-\frac{\sum\big(\psi_{k}^{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\Delta\phi^{a}_{k,j})-\psi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)^{2}}{\sum\big(\psi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)^{2}}.

(43)

4.3
Pairing POD Modes using the Hilbert Transform

While
HPOD
modes can be used to determine matched pairs of
POD
modes, this process requires performing both decompositions.
As the Hilbert transform can be applied in the streamwise direction, it can be applied directly to the
POD
modes despite their lack of temporal information.
The result is a shift of the fundamental wavenumbers of each
POD
mode by
\mp 90^{\circ}
, which is analogous to a propagation of the mode downstream.
Thus, the streamwise Hilbert transform of a
POD
mode is analogous to a
POD
mode which represents a
\mp 90^{\circ}
phase shift of the same streamwise periodic structure.

In order to determine matched pairs of
POD
modes using the Hilbert transform, the Hilbert transform of each
POD
mode
\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
in the streamwise direction
\mathcal{H}_{x}[\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})]
is used in place of the imaginary part of the
HPOD
modes to define the analytic signal of
\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})

{(\psi_{k})}^{a}(\boldsymbol{x})=\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})+i\mathcal{H}_{x}\big[\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\big].

(45)

The shape of the analytic signal of the
k^{th}

POD
mode at the phase angle
\phi_{k}
is given by

\big(\psi_{k}\big)^{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\phi_{k})=\cos(\phi_{k})\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})+\sin(\phi_{k})\mathcal{H}_{x}[\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})],

(46)

and the phase angle at which the
k^{th}

POD
mode best matches the
j^{th}

POD
mode is given by

\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\Delta\phi_{k,1})&\sin(\Delta\phi_{k,1})\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\cos(\Delta\phi_{k,j})&\sin(\Delta\phi_{k,j})\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\cos(\Delta\phi_{k,K})&\sin(\Delta\phi_{k,K})\\
\end{bmatrix}^{T}=\left(\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\mathcal{H}_{x}[\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})]\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\mathcal{H}_{x}[\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})]\\
\end{bmatrix}^{T}\right)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\mathcal{H}_{x}[\psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})]\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{K}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\end{bmatrix}^{T},

(47)

and

\Delta\phi_{k,j}=\tan^{-1}\frac{\sin(\Delta\phi_{k,j})}{\cos(\Delta\phi_{k,j})}.

(48)

The corresponding correlation coefficient is

\mathcal{R}_{k^{a},j}=\frac{\operatorname{cov}\big((\psi_{k})^{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\Delta\phi_{k,j}),\psi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}\big((\psi_{k})^{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\Delta\phi_{k,j})\big)\operatorname{var}\big(\psi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)}}.

(49)

The
R^{2}
value of the phase average of the
k^{th}
and
j^{th}

POD
modes compared to the phase average of the analytic signal of the
k^{th}

POD
mode is given by

R^{2}_{k^{a},\widetilde{(k,j)}}=1-\frac{\sum\big((\psi_{k})^{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\phi)-\widetilde{\psi}_{k,j}(\boldsymbol{x},\phi)\big)^{2}}{\sum\big(\widetilde{\psi}_{k,j}(\boldsymbol{x},\phi)\big)^{2}},

(50)

where
(\psi_{k})^{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\phi)
is the analytic signal of the
k^{th}

POD
mode and
\widetilde{\psi}_{k,j}(\boldsymbol{x},\phi)
is the phase average of the
k^{th}
and
j^{th}

POD
modes at phase angle
\phi
, which was determined using phase angles
\phi=[0,2\pi]
in increments of
0.01\pi
.

HPOD

The phase-averaging of the resulting modes thus results in more consistently shaped structures making it easier to identify and interpret the propagating structures.
However, the filtering out of non-propagating structures may result in a poorer representation of the flow structure where the flow is three-dimensional.
While applying the Hilbert transform directly to the
POD

HPOD

POD

POD
modes introduce non-physical artefacts into the analysis of the flow and this should be considered when interpreting results.

Concluding Remarks

The velocity in the wake of a sphere at
Re_{D}=7780
was measured using
2C-2D

MCCD
PIV
, and
POD
and
HPOD
were applied to the turbulent fluctuations.
The resulting
POD
and
HPOD
modes were compared to determine if the
HPOD
modes, which accentuate propagating structures, could be used to match
POD
modes which represented the same structure at different phase angles.
The Hilbert transform of the
POD
modes was then used as a substitute for the
HPOD
modes in order to determine paired
POD
modes without performing the computationally-intensive
HPOD
.
The
POD
mode pairs identified using these methods were then used to phase average the turbulent fluctuations to examine the propagating structures in the wake of the sphere.

The leading
HPOD
modes are shown to represent the same structures as the leading
POD
modes, with a greater emphasis on the propagating structures in the former.
While each
HPOD
mode consists of a real and imaginary part representing a phase-shift of 90
∘
in the mode, this comes at the cost of filtering from the Hilbert transform.
As a result, it can be difficult to differentiate between what is part of the flow structures and what is an artefact of this filtering, particularly in the case of two-dimensional measurements of a three-dimensional flow.
Performing the Hilbert transform on the
POD
modes to create an analytic signal of the
POD
modes is shown to identify the same pairs of
POD
modes identified using the
HPOD
modes.
This method allows the same structures identified using the
HPOD
modes to be identified, without introducing the filtering resulting from the
HPOD
.
The phase average of the turbulent fluctuations using the identified
POD
mode pairs represented flapping and pulsating motions in wake of the sphere.

As the velocity fields in the current work are not time-resolved, the Hilbert transform was performed in the streamwise direction, and thus the structures identified are those which propagate in that direction.
As a result, the temporal information of the structures is not given by either decomposition.
However, the paired
POD
modes gave insight into the dynamics of the structures in the turbulent fluctuations when the complexity of the flow made identifying related
POD
modes challenging.
