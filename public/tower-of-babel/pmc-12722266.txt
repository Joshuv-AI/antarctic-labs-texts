Scaling deep learning for material imaging with a pseudo 3D model for domain transfer
Authors: Kunning Tang, Ryan T. Armstrong, Peyman Mostaghimi, Yufu Niu, Quentin Meyer, Chuan Zhao, Donal P. Finegan, Melissa Popeil, Kamaljit Singh, Hannah Menke, Alexandros Patsoukis Dimou, Tom Bultreys, Arjen Mascini, Mark Knackstedt, Ying Da Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The recent introduction of deep learning methods for image processing has greatly advanced the characterization of materials using three-dimensional (3D) X-ray imaging techniques. However, deep learning models often have difficulty performing consistently across images owing to unavoidable variations in imaging conditions, which create inconsistencies even for the same material. As a result, networks must frequently be retrained for new datasets, limiting their applicability and generalization. Thus, it is critical to reduce the variations between images to enable a single model to process multiple datasets. Herein, we introduce P3T-Net, a pseudo-3D domain transfer network that transfers diverse 3D images into a uniform domain before processing using deep learning models. Remarkably, P3T-Net enables the reuse of previously trained networks for processing new images and considerably reduces the computational cost of transferring 3D images across domains. These unique capabilities were demonstrated in the following scenarios: (i) image enhancement of fast scans for geological rock and hydrogen fuel cells, (ii) enhancement of images to match the quality of multi-source imaging for lithium-ion batteries, (iii) accurate segmentation of images captured under different conditions, and (iv) tera-scale 3D transfer (1011 voxels) on a single GPU. Overall, the proposed approach addresses cross-domain inconsistencies across various materials and conditions, thereby enabling more robust and generalizable deep learning solutions for a wide range of material imaging tasks.

Introduction

3D imaging techniques such as micro/nano X-ray computed tomography (micro/nano-CT) have revealed the properties of complex and continually evolving materials, such as lithium-ion batteries, hydrogen fuel cells, electrolyzers, and materials that are also challenging to image owing to their heterogeneity, such as geological rocks and critical minerals1–6. The main challenges in characterizing these materials from 3D imaging techniques arise from two factors: (i) limitations in imaging quality, such as the trade-off between scan times and noise-to-signal ratio, and the trade-off between resolution and field of view (FOV)7, and (ii) difficulties in image processing, such as the complexity of multi-phase segmentation6. These challenges have been addressed by the rapid development of deep learning with the proliferation of data-driven and image-based tasks7,8. These tasks include image denoising, which shortens the acquisition time without sacrificing image quality9,10; unbiased multi-phase segmentation, which exceeds manual segmentation in accuracy and speed4,11; and super-resolution, which increases the pixels per feature size, thereby improving detail representation while maintaining an adequate FOV of the materials6,12,13.

Although these advancements have led to significant changes in the field, several key challenges remain. These include (i) achieving generalizability across different domains and under diverse conditions, (ii) the restricted learning capacity of 2D networks when applied to 3D images4,14–16, and (iii) addressing the memory limitations and reducing processing time associated with large 3D images, especially those with more than a billion (>109) voxels6,17–19. These challenges arise from the following fundamental issues: (i) domain mismatch between trained networks and inference inputs, which limits their generalizability20–22. This mismatch can arise from both intrinsic material characteristics, such as density, phase fraction, and heterogeneity, and extrinsic imaging factors, including the type of scanning device, scanning conditions, and physical state of the sample during imaging. (ii) The inability of 2D networks to process 3D images owing to information loss along the third axis23,24. Adjacent XY slices in 3D micro/nano-CT images are similar and linearly related in Z-space; however, 2D networks will not inherently understand this Z-space similarity and will output a nonlinear misalignment between these adjacent slices. This results in inaccurate quantification of material properties, such as pore connectivity and material structure. (iii) Incompatibility between memory-intensive 3D networks and handling of large-scale 3D data. Although the utilization of 3D networks can resolve third-axis information, the range of learnable features available in 3D training patches for the networks is restricted owing to the limitations of GPU memory capacity17,25.

To address these challenges, advanced deep learning solutions are needed for material characterization that can efficiently handle images from diverse samples scanned under different devices and conditions, as well as be capable of processing large-scale 3D data. The key to solving these tasks lies in adapting them to multiple domains, particularly in managing variations in information distribution, such as grayscale values, image semantics, and styles26,27. Image-to-image translation (I2I) and domain adaptation (DA) are essential techniques for aligning image distributions across multiple domains to address domain mismatches. I2I focuses on transforming images to match the styles or characteristics of different domains28,29, whereas DA adapts trained models to generalize effectively on new domains, ensuring consistent performance despite domain shifts30. Generative adversarial networks (GANs) are commonly used in I2I, including supervised approaches such as Pix2pix31; unsupervised methods such as CycleGAN32, CyCADA33, SCONE-GAN34, and NICE-GAN35; semi-supervised techniques such as Mixmatch36; and models that incorporate GAN with vision transformer architecture, such as ResViT37. For DA, approaches include reducing the discrepancy between domains38,39, employing contrastive learning40,41, diffusion-based methods42,43, and adversarial learning, which share similar objectives with I2I44,45. Both techniques have proven to be effective in aligning images under different distributions. Furthermore, considering that paired data where each image in the source domain is precisely aligned to the target domain is often difficult to obtain, especially for real materials, networks like CycleGAN and CyCADA are typically preferred for domain transfer. These networks can handle images without a one-to-one relationship or precise geometric alignment, known as unpaired data, and offer greater flexibility with fewer constraints on the training data.

When transferring unpaired 3D large-scale data across domains, both 2D and 3D networks have their respective strengths and limitations. From a computational perspective, 3D networks are highly memory-intensive and contain more trainable parameters, which makes them challenging to scale when processing large 3D material images46,47. The computational load increases with the size of the data and scales with the third power, which demands significant GPU memory. 2D networks are more scalable and memory efficient. However, 2D networks extract information only from 2D planes, missing spatial relationships along the third axis. This limitation can result in less accurate domain transfer when dealing with volumetric data. Although 3D networks can directly learn 3D structures from the data, preserving the continuity of spatial relationships along the third axis, training such networks to handle 3D nano-/micro-CT images, which typically range from 1000 to 20003 voxels in material imaging, is challenging owing to GPU memory constraints. Consequently, there is a trade-off between computational resources and model performance. Specifically, training a well-performing 3D domain transfer network often requires access to high-performance computing resources, such as H200 GPUs. In contrast, when using more commonly available GPUs that offer 11 to 48 GB of memory, with 24 GB being the most common12,48,49, 3D networks often face long training times and reduced performance owing to the need for smaller training patches or simplified architectures. In unpaired training, it is also important to ensure that the source and target patches share similar characteristics, such as material types and local physical features, to enable effective and robust learning. For 2D networks, this requirement is relatively easy to satisfy because patches can be sampled at or above the representative elementary volume (REV), ensuring adequate feature representation. However, in 3D, this becomes more challenging owing to the limited 3D patch size and the spatial variability of features across different slices within a volumetric patch. Because human experts cannot directly visualize the full 3D structure during sampling, ensuring characteristic similarity between the source and target domains becomes significantly more difficult.

We introduce the Pseudo-3D Domain Transfer Network (P3T-Net), an unpaired 3D domain transfer framework that uses two 2D networks to process the X-Y and Z planes, enabling effective 3D domain transfer at tera-large scales (>1010 voxels). By decoupling the 3D transfer task into two 2D perspectives, P3T-Net significantly reduces memory usage and the number of trainable parameters, thereby improving scalability and efficiency. This design supports the use of larger 2D patches, enhancing the network’s ability to learn global representative features, which is particularly important in unsupervised settings with unpaired data. We validated the network on 29 datasets that span diverse real-world materials and imaging conditions in geoscience, energy storage, and fuel cell applications. In addition to achieving strong pixel-wise and image-based accuracy, P3T-Net preserves key physical properties across domains. Finally, we compared its computational cost, training time, and scalability with those of conventional 3D domain transfer networks, demonstrating that P3T-Net can process images a thousand times larger using a single GPU.

Results

Overview of the pseudo-3D domain transfer network

P3T-Net is built on the principal concept of transforming a 3D space using three sequential 2D networks. This approach effectively addresses the third-axis misalignment of 2D networks on 3D data and enhances the scalability of 2D networks for 3D domains, thereby enabling the inference of large 3D domains on a single GPU. An overview of P3T-Net is shown in Fig. 1. P3T-Net consists of three main modules: a semantic indication module, a semantic-consistent domain transfer module, and a misalignment fixing module (Fig. 1a–c). During the training phase, each module was trained independently and sequentially. The process begins with training the semantic indication module, which utilizes 2D grayscale images from the target domain along with their corresponding segmentation ground truths. Next, the semantic-consistent domain transfer module was trained using 2D slices in the X-Y plane from both the source and target domains. In this stage, the previously trained semantic indication module was employed to extract semantic features and ensure consistency across domains. Subsequently, each 2D slice in the X-Y plane from the 3D source domain was processed through the trained domain transfer module and stacked to form the 3D transferred source domain. This transferred volume then serves as the input to the misalignment fixing module, which is trained to correct nonlinear misalignments along the third axis. During the inference phase, each 2D slice in the X-Y plane of the 3D source domain is passed through the semantic-consistent domain transfer module to produce the transferred output. These outputs are stacked to form a 3D volume, from which slices along the Y-Z plane are then passed to the misalignment fixing module to correct misalignment in that direction. Finally, the corrected slices were processed using the semantic indication module and assembled into the final segmented 3D domain. With the combination of these modules, the main benefits of P3T-Net are as follows: i) it is designed in a fully unpaired format using a CycleGAN architecture as the bottleneck (Fig. 1b), eliminating the need for paired image registration between training images from the source and target domains. ii) It enhances semantic correctness by minimizing the discrepancy between latent features of the transferred and target domains. These features, which are extracted using a trained semantic indication module (Fig. 1a), guide the optimization process. iii) Finally, all modules are built in 2D but can perform efficient domain transfer in 3D. This was achieved using an additional misalignment fixing module (Fig. 1c). By integrating this module, P3T-Net unlocks 2D scalability on 3D data, allowing it to learn third-axis information. The inference phase of P3T-Net is displayed in Fig. 1d. The detailed training schedule and loss functions can be found in the Methods section. To demonstrate the performance and effectiveness of P3T-Net, we applied it to four materials with various imaging conditions in the following sections.

Enhancing laboratory-based micro-CT

Laboratory-based micro-CT scanners capture internal material structures at the micrometer scale, but suffer from slow imaging times. Lower photon flux laboratory-based scanners, compared to fast scan sources (e.g., synchrotron), require long scans (1–10 h) to produce images with sufficient quality for further processing and physical quantification. For digital material analysis, it is crucial to scan numerous samples to ensure the accuracy of a study and to sufficiently represent the structure. With limited CT equipment, an effective way to increase the scan capacity is to reduce the scanning time while maintaining acceptable image quality.

To examine the effectiveness of P3T-Net in reducing the scanning time for laboratory-based scanners, sandstone rock suitable for geological storage of CO2 and H2 is used50. The sandstone was scanned for approximately 10 h and 7 min. Meanwhile, essential physical parameters for geo-rock research, such as phase connectivity (Euler number), phase surface area, pore size distribution, and two-phase relative permeability curve, have been validated51. The primary goal of conducting 3D imaging is to characterize these parameters. For a comparison of physical accuracy, we directly segmented the source domain using a traditional segmentation workflow (Fig. 2f)52–54, which included non-local means filtering (NLM), 3D watershed segmentation, and morphological opening and closing in the TM Avizo software. P3T-Net results, both before and after misalignment correction, were segmented using a trained semantic indication module (Fig. 2i, l). A visual comparison between the traditional segmentation result and the P3T-Net result is also shown in Fig. S1a. The percentage difference of the Euler number and surface area compared to the GT domain is reported in Fig. 3b, c. This shows that the semantic-consistent domain transfer module significantly improves the physical accuracy, reducing the difference in Euler numbers from the GT domain to the source domain by approximately 4, 25, and 215 times for pores, grains, and high-density phases, respectively. Similar to the surface area, the difference after transfer is reduced by 2-5 times compared to the source domain. When the misalignment fixing module is added, the difference from the GT domain is further minimized for both the Euler number and surface area, achieving an additional 2 to 18 times reduction compared to the result with misalignment. This indicates that the third-axis misalignment caused by the 2D network has a significant influence on the physical analysis and must be fixed. Furthermore, the pore-scale transport characteristics are also analyzed, showing close matches between the GT domain and P3T-Net after misalignment fixing in terms of pore size distributions and relative permeability curves simulated using the Lattice Boltzmann Method55 (Fig. 3d–f).

We also analyzed the stability of P3T-Net during training. As shown in Fig. S2a, b, by comparing the PSNR values of a 2D transferred image to the paired slice from the target domain, we observe that the training of the semantic-consistent domain transfer module stabilizes after 80 epochs. For the misalignment fixing module, the PSNR value of a 3D subblock plateaus after eight epochs. Figure S2c, d present visualizations of the 2D transferred image generated by the semantic-consistent domain transfer module and the Y-Z plane of the 3D subblock generated by the misalignment fixing module across various epochs. From this analysis, we can conclude that unlike typical GAN-based networks, where training is unstable and achieving equilibrium between the generator and discriminator is challenging, P3T-Net demonstrates stable training. This stability is attributed to its cycle-based architecture and the use of multiple pixel-to-pixel loss functions, such as cycle consistency loss and semantic consistency loss in the semantic-consistency domain transfer module, as well as pixel consistency loss in the misalignment fixing module, which effectively constrain the training process. Furthermore, since the semantic indication module and semantic consistency loss are closely linked to target domain segmentation, a segmentation-noise sensitivity analysis is conducted to evaluate P3T-Net’s performance under various types of noise in the target domain segmentation. Details of this analysis are provided in Section 1 and Fig. S3–S5 of the Supplementary Materials. We also added a detailed comparative study between P3T-Net and state-of-the-art 3D domain transfer networks, which can be found in Section 2 and Fig. S6 in the Supplementary Materials.

Scan time reduction and segmentation of 4D dynamic Images

The capabilities of P3T-Net have been extended to process low-quality, high-noise, 4D fast dynamic scans, such as images acquired at synchrotron sources. The transfer from low-quality synchrotron images to a long-scan high-quality domain is challenging because of the trade-off between scanning time (the capability of capturing the dynamic process) and image quality.

This case focuses on two-phase flow in porous rocks, which is crucial for underground water management, oil/gas recovery, and underground CO2/hydrogen storage56,57. A sandstone sample, imaged at the Diamond synchrotron (Diamond Light Source, Harwell Science and Innovation Campus, UK) at beamline I13-2, underwent drainage with decane-brine replacing water in the rock. Synchrotron-based images, acquired in 24 s (Fig. 4d–f), are noisy with minimal visual distinction between phases, making traditional segmentation challenging. Accompanying these highly noisy images is an unregistered long-scan image of another wet sandstone sample scanned for 7 h (Fig. 4a–c). Meanwhile, noisy synchrotron images are segmented using a traditional method involving binary watershed segmentation of the dry image, registration to the wet image, binary segmentation of water phases in the wet image, and morphological erosion/dilation to suit user-defined segmentation quality. This process takes approximately 30 h for 20 images (Fig. 4o). For a single experiment, over 200 images are commonly collected, requiring approximately two weeks of manual processing. The traditional segmentation results show under-segmentation of the water phase (Fig. 4q). In contrast to this inaccurate, multi-stage, manual segmentation workflow, P3T-Net achieves a more visually accurate result (Fig. 4r) in just 1.5 h for processing 20 images. A detailed visual comparison of the segmentation results obtained using the traditional method and P3T-Net is shown in Fig. S1b.

Unlike the first case, where the sandstone was scanned dry, we captured the dynamic distribution of the two fluids within the sample. Thus, an interesting physical parameter here is the fluid connectivity at different timesteps. The physical accuracy of phase connectivity, measured by the Euler number of the transferred segmented images, shows a negative value for both water and decane at a water saturation of 0.59 (Fig. 4s), indicating that both fluids are well-connected, which is physically reasonable at this water saturation58. This contrasts with the manual result, where decane is significantly disconnected owing to the segmentation error. We also plot the water saturation and Euler number of the decane for all 20 time-series transferred images in Fig. S10a, b. As anticipated, decane is sparsely connected at the beginning but becomes more connected as more water is removed58, whereas water is well-connected at the beginning but becomes disconnected after being replaced by decane. A visualization of the drainage process on a series of transferred images is shown in Fig. S10c.

The efficacy of P3T-Net is evident when considering the quality of the fast-time scan. Hardware limitations of X-ray systems have become prominent in capturing the dynamics of fast processes, with traditional image processing unable to produce analyzable images. In contrast, the efficient pseudo-3D architecture produces physically accurate segmentation results within a fraction of the time. A dynamic core flooding scan, which traditionally requires several weeks to process, can now be accomplished in a matter of hours. The results obtained at the synchrotron could thus be processed during the beam time. This represents a substantial shift in the planning and execution of experiments, given the limited time of synchrotron imaging59.

Segmentation of out-of-domain cathode nano-CT images

Successful domain transfer on images with diverse scan conditions is critical for scans performed with varying speeds and different instruments, requiring collective segmentation and analysis with minimal user bias. This is the key limitation of deep-learned image segmentation models, which are only valid on images acquired with the same imaging conditions, thus requiring both the training of a new model on a case-by-case basis and a human operator to systematically label images. P3T-Net, however, can individually learn the domain mapping of unpaired 3D images. Newly scanned images can be transferred to an existing domain, allowing a segmentation model trained on the existing domain to consistently deliver high-quality segmentation to new images.

Avoiding human bias is critical for battery characterization, as variations in user-selected segmentation parameters can induce large uncertainties. P3T-Net mitigates this efficiently by bringing out-of-domain images into the same domain for consistent segmentation. Figure 5 provides a visual comparison of four nano-CT images of lithium-ion battery cathodes, including two mono-model scans (absorption scan), one phase scan, and a dual-mode scan. Dual-mode scanning requires 60% more scan time than mono-mode scanning and a significant expense to install the second mode60. Transferring from mono-model to dual-mode scan quality is crucial for fast and efficient battery characterization. Large scale 3D domain transfer approaching tera-scale

P3T-Net addresses the computational intensity and low scalability of 3D deep learning methods64,65, which limits the deployability of most deep learning methods for large 3D datasets. To perform domain transfer on these images with 3D consistency instead of 2D consistency, which otherwise results in third-axis misalignment, P3T-Net applies two sequential 2D CNNs: first on the X-Y plane, where most domain difference occurs, and then a CNN on the Y-Z plane to resolve misalignment caused by nonlinear transformations in the X-Y plane. Once the X-Y and Y-Z plane images have been transferred and fixed, the X-Z plane is adjusted accordingly. This approach allows P3T-Net to scale in 2D during inference, enabling GPU resources to infer slice-by-slice rather than requiring the entire 3D domain. To highlight the substantial reduction in computational cost, we compared P3T-Net with the 3D version of CycleGAN, a widely utilized domain transfer network23,66. This comparison focused on five key aspects: training patch size, representative features, model size, GPU usage during inference, and inference time. Both networks were trained using the same target and source domains, as shown in Section “Scan Time Reduction and Segmentation of 4D Dynamic Images”. We trained the 3D CycleGAN on a 24 GB consumer-grade GPU. Our choice of consumer-grade GPU for benchmarking is intended to reflect a more commonly available scenario in academic research, providing a realistic baseline for the computational constraints faced by many researchers. As shown in Fig. 8a, a patch size of less than 1103 voxels with a batch size of one reaches the memory limit for 3D CycleGAN. Applying the quantization technique (Automatic Mixed Precision, AMP) to the 3D CycleGAN increases the maximum patch size to 1363 voxels. In contrast, for P3T-Net, which is entirely 2D-based, the patch size can easily reach 5122 pixels, using only 9.7 GB of GPU memory without AMP and 5.4 GB with AMP. The patch size is crucial for training, as illustrated in Fig. 8b. For this specific sample, a patch smaller than 1282 pixels contains limited representative features of the sample, and these features can be substantially different between patches from the source and target domains. For instance, at a size of 962 pixels, the patch from the source domain contains more pores, whereas the patch from the target domain primarily consists of fluid. Training 3D networks with these mismatched patches can lead to the generation of artifacts or hallucinations in the transferred images. Additionally, the model size and number of trainable parameters for 3D CycleGAN and P3T-Net were compared, as detailed in Tables S4 and S5. 3D CycleGAN has a model size that is 21 times larger than P3T-Net with a patch size of 962 pixels, and the number of trainable parameters is 1.6 times greater. The model sizes of 3D CycleGAN and P3T-Net were compared using different training patch sizes, as illustrated in Fig. S15. The model size of 3D CycleGAN increases sharply from 0.6 to 29 GB as the input patch size increases from 323 to 1283 voxels, whereas P3T-Net’s size increases steadily from 0.2 to 0.9 GB. This dramatic increase in the model size for 3D CycleGAN requires significantly more GPU memory and demonstrates its low scalability, which limits its applicability to large-scale 3D image processing.

A computational efficiency comparison during inference between P3T-Net and 3D CycleGAN showed that P3T-Net can transfer a domain with a size of over 4000 × 4 000 × 5000 voxels in a single inference. Fig. 8c shows the memory scalability of the network compared to 3D CycleGAN, with P3T-Net only reaching the GPU memory limit of 24 GB at a domain size of 40003+ voxels, whereas CycleGAN fails to reach 2563 voxels in a single inference. Although 3D CycleGAN transfers the source image to the target image, artifacts are evident, as highlighted in the yellow box, where pore spaces are converted into solid phases after the transfer. This observation aligns with our discussion of Fig. 8b, which attributes the issue to the limited patch size and representative features used for training the 3D model. This limitation leads to feature inconsistencies across patches, ultimately causing the generation of artifacts or hallucinations in the transferred images. The NIQE is calculated as a perceptual quantification of the transferred image. Discussion

This study introduces P3T-Net, a pseudo-3D domain transfer network that leverages efficient 2D architectures to accurately transfer large-scale 3D imaging data, overcoming the memory and scalability limitations of existing 3D approaches. P3T-Net was rigorously validated on 29 real-world 3D datasets across diverse materials and imaging conditions, demonstrating strong domain transfer and practical applicability. Its effectiveness is further highlighted through four engineering case studies from geoscience, energy, and materials domains, addressing critical challenges such as enhancing 4D imaging quality, reducing scan times, consistent segmentation, and improving characterization in battery and fuel cell systems. We also demonstrate the computational efficiency and tera-scale scalability of P3T-Net on a workstation-grade computer, a key factor determining the deployability of the network in processing large 3D images. The grayscale distribution shifts and image quality differences arising from variations in material density, phase fraction, imaging conditions, and scan timing were addressed using a CycleGAN-based architecture that transfers imaging styles while preserving semantic consistency through its semantic indication module. To handle phase heterogeneity, P3T-Net uses a pseudo-3D architecture that allows larger in-plane training patches, enabling better representation of both global features and local fluctuations across slices, which is critical for accurately transferring features in highly heterogeneous materials, such as fuel cells.

Although P3T-Net requires less human involvement during training, some conditions are necessary prior to training: (i) materials from the target and source domains should exhibit similar characteristics, such as material type (e.g., the material type for both domains should be sandstone, rather than sandstone as the target domain and carbonate as the source domain) and sample condition (e.g., dry or wet). A standard assumption of P3T-Net is that the source and target domains should contain shared cross-representative features. Maintaining these similarities is essential to ensure that the domain transfer is meaningful and that the transferred images accurately preserve the structural and compositional properties of the materials. (ii) Target and source domains should have similar pixels per average feature size (resolution) to ensure that they contain comparable FOV, and (iii) unpaired training patches sampled from target and source domains must contain similar features. Despite being designed for unpaired training, maintaining feature similarity is essential for stable network performance, especially for discriminators. For example, in fuel cell images, the model might fail if training patches for the target domain are exclusively from the gas diffusion layer, while those for the source domain are from the membrane and catalyst layer. Consistent sourcing is crucial to avoid training instability. This is also why staying in 2D for unpaired domain transfer is preferred because feature consistency is more difficult to achieve in 3D space owing to more complex 3D features and limited training patch sizes. With similar features presented in target and source training slices, P3T-Net supports domain transfer in arbitrary directions, meaning that any directional anisotropy (e.g., diagonal structures) can be captured and transferred.

In terms of future work, although P3T-Net has been demonstrated for single-source-to-single-target domain mapping, it is also capable of transferring multiple source domains to the same target domain (Fig. S16). However, it cannot handle multiple target domains. Addressing this requires incorporating target-class conditioning into the pipeline by linking the target-domain class to the source input to appropriately guide the model. Another direction is to explore the adaptability of P3T-Net to other imaging modalities, such as MRI, medical CT, and industrial X-rays, which will require understanding modality-specific imaging physics, grayscale semantics, and domain mismatches. The third direction is to assess the robustness of the model under large domain mismatches or unseen material types. While P3T-Net has demonstrated the ability to handle significant mismatches, it may struggle when there is no shared or cross-representative feature space between the source and target domains. In such ill-posed scenarios, the task shifts from domain transfer to generative synthesis, where no deterministic mapping can be guaranteed. Future research could explore strategies to better characterize or quantify the degree of domain mismatch prior to transfer, potentially incorporating feature variation estimation to identify cases in which meaningful transfer is not possible. Lastly, although P3T-Net effectively computes bulk physical properties and captures localized features, it remains limited in recovering rare features that appear infrequently in the target domain. This limitation can be addressed by incorporating user-defined annotations, such as through the semantic indication module, or by designing additional localized loss functions or attention mechanisms to guide the model toward these features. This would extend P3T-Net’s applicability to rare-event imaging tasks, such as defect detection and failure analysis.

The first case, where we used sandstone rock imaged with different speeds on a bench-top micro-CT, was used as the benchmark case for validating P3T-Net performance. During the imaging of the sandstone rock for the fast scan (source domain) and slow scan (target domain), the sample was consistently placed on the rotation stage, with no human intervention during either scan. Fast and slow scans were achieved by adjusting the number of projections, accumulations, and exposure time, which are parameters related to the X-ray source. Under these conditions, the fast and slow scans were naturally paired, capturing the sample in exactly the same state and coordinates. Thus, although P3T-Net was trained in an unpaired manner by cropping patches from different regions of the fast and slow scans, during inference, the transferred fast scan had a corresponding paired slow-scan ground truth for benchmarking. A schematic workflow for scanning the benchmark case is shown in Fig. S17. In this case, the domain transfer tends to denoise the fast scan to the same image quality as the slow scan. Because paired datasets are available, a paired denoising network, TomoGAN, was used for comparison with P3T-Net. TomoGAN is a modified version of the U-Net architecture67, with fewer downsampling and upsampling layers, zero-padding applied to all convolutional layers, and the use of three stacked adjacent input slices. A key strength of TomoGAN lies in its use of adjacent slices, which enhances the reconstruction quality by leveraging spatial continuity. Because adjacent slices often exhibit strong correlations-sharing similar shapes with slight variations in size-stacking them as multiple input channels in the first CNN layer effectively acts like applying a multivariate linear filter, helping to suppress noise.

P3T-Net architecture, loss functions, and training schedules

Semantic indication module

Semantic indication module (Fig. 1a) embeds a U-ResNet4,51 with residual connections for latent feature extraction and the subsequent image segmentation purposes. Training details and schedules

During the training stage, an unpaired 2D source (XS) and target images (XT) are input to CycleGAN (GS−T and GT−S), and the transferred source (XS−T) and target (XT−S) images can be obtained. However, with a GAN-based network, image-to-image transfer is stochastic. This poses challenges in digital material image processing because essential material information may be lost or inaccurately represented in the transferred images.

To maintain the consistency of the image information, we designed a semantic indication module and used it to extract semantic information during domain transfer training. The semantic indication module was designed as an n-phase segmentation network with the U-ResNet architecture (Fig. S18). It is trained using 2D slices from the target domain (XT) and their corresponding segmented ground truth in a paired manner. The detailed generation of the segmentation ground truth is discussed in the next section. The semantic indication module requires training each time for any new task to learn image semantics. After training the semantic indication module, XT, XT−S, XS, and XS−T are fed to the trained semantic indication module to obtain 1D latent features ZT, ZT−S, ZS, and ZS−T of size 1 × 10, respectively. Semantic consistency loss was then calculated for these latent features. Additionally, the XT−S−T that was transferred back to the target domain from XT−S is also input for the trained semantic indication module to obtain its segmentation result. This process was optimized by considering the cross-entropy loss between the segmentation result and the ground truth of XT. All semantic consistency losses were incorporated with the standard CycleGAN losses (cycle consistency loss, adversarial loss, and identity loss) to guarantee the transfer of both domain characteristics and material information.

After training the semantic-consistent domain transfer module, a misalignment fixing module is designed and trained to fix the third-axis misalignment of the output from the semantic-consistent domain transfer module (as shown in Fig. 1c). During training, an input 3D XS with shape [D, H, W] is treated as a 2D batch of consecutive images, with the 3D XS reshaped to 2D images (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{{S}^{{\prime} }}$$\end{document}XS′) with batch size D such that the shape of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{{S}^{{\prime} }}=[D,1,H,W]\equiv [N,C,H,W]$$\end{document}XS′=[D,1,H,W]≡[N,C,H,W], where N is the number of batch size, and C is the channel size. This allows \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{{S}^{{\prime} }}$$\end{document}XS′ to be passed directly into the trained 2D GS→T to obtain XS→T with a third-axis misalignment. XS→T is then transposed from [D, 1, H, W] to [H, 1, D, W] to obtain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{S\to T}^{{\prime} }$$\end{document}XS→T′ which is then passed to the misalignment fixing generator (GMF) for fixing the misalignment. GMF is optimised by calculating the adversarial loss between the GMF output with the original XT and the pixel loss between the XS→T and the transposed GMF output (transpose from [H, 1, D, W] back to [D, 1, H, W]). Detailed training steps of the misalignment fixing module can be found in Fig. S19.

During inference (Fig. 1d), each 2D slice in the X-Y plane from the 3D source domain is passed through GS−T in the semantic-consistent domain transfer module. After stacking all the 2D output images into a transferred 3D domain, each slice in the X-Z plane of the transferred 3D domain is input to GMF in the misalignment fixing module, resulting in a finalized 3D transferred domain with misalignment fixing. Additionally, if the final objective is to generate the segmentation result, the 3D transferred domain can be directly passed to the trained semantic indication module to generate the final 3D segmentation output.

In terms of the training schedule, the segmentation indication module is trained using the Adam optimizer. The training starts with an initial learning rate of 1e−5 and a batch size of 16. The training spans 50 epochs, during which the learning rate is halved whenever the loss plateaus for five epochs. Subsequently, together with the segmentation indication module pre-trained in the first stage, we trained the semantic-consistent domain transfer module with the unpaired images from the target and source domains, using the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 8. The training starts with an initial learning rate of 3e−5 and the same decay strategy is applied. Overall, this stage was trained for 150 epochs. Finally, we trained the misalignment fixing module using the images from the target domain and the inferred images from the second stage using the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 1. The training starts with an initial learning rate of 3e−5 and the same decay strategy is applied. This stage was trained for 30 epochs.

To train the semantic-consistent domain transfer module, 2D patches were sampled, whereas 3D patches were cropped to train the misalignment correction module. Specifically, we collected 1800 2D patches with a size of 2242 pixels and 900 3D patches with a size of 643 voxels for the laboratory-based micro-CT case (Section “Enhancing Laboratory-based micro-CT”), 2500 2D patches with a size of 2242 pixels and 600 3D patches with a size of 803 voxels for the synchrotron case (Section “Scan Time Reduction and Segmentation of 4D Dynamic Image”), 2100 2D patches with a size of 2242 pixels and 600 3D patches with a size of 803 voxels for the lithium-ion cathode case (Section “Segmentation of Out-of-Domain Cathode Nano-CT Images”), and 2160 2D patches with a size of 2242 pixels and 600 3D patches with a size of 843 voxels for the fuel cell case (Section “ Scanning Time Reduction and Multi-Label Segmentation of Out-of-Domain Fuel Cell micro-CT Images”). All these training patches were randomly cropped from the 3D target and source domains.

Note that for random cropping, it is generally advisable to use patch sizes that are close to or larger than the REV of the material. In imaging, the REV represents the smallest image size of a material that can be considered representative of its bulk properties. When the training patch size meets or exceeds the REV, each randomly cropped patch is more likely to capture statistically representative microstructural features. Consequently, patches from the source and target domains are more likely to contain similar features, which helps to maintain consistency in the learning process. To evaluate this in the selected patch sizes, we conducted REV analysis for all four cases to determine whether the patch sizes used satisfy the REV requirement. Specifically, the REV analysis was based on different structural properties: porosity of the sandstone rock in Case 1, volume fraction of decane in Case 2, porosity of the lithium-ion cathode in Case 3, and porosity of the gas diffusion layer in the fuel cell in Case 4. For each case, patches of varying sizes were randomly sampled from the segmented 3D target domains, and the corresponding property values were computed. Overall, 1,000 patches of each size were randomly sampled for this analysis. As shown in Fig. S20, the distribution of points indicates that as the 2D patch size increases, the calculated values converge toward the global value obtained from the 3D domain. The point distribution reaches a plateau (estimated REV) at a size of approximately 2002 pixels for cases 1, 2, and 3, and at 2242 pixels for case 4. The patch size selected for training the semantic-consistent domain transfer module was 2242 pixels, which meets or exceeds the estimated REV for all cases.

In addition to random cropping, several alternative sampling strategies can be considered. These include: (1) human-guided sampling, where patches from the source and target domains are selected to satisfy specific physical constraints. This ensures that the sampled regions are physically meaningful and representative, thereby improving the consistency of the training data across domains. (2) Similarity-based sampling, in which a pre-trained network is used to extract features from patches, and those with similar feature representations are paired across domains. It enhances latent feature-level alignment between patches from the target and source domains, which can reduce domain discrepancy; and (3) block-based sampling, where both the source and target 3D domains are divided into sub-blocks, and patches are sampled from blocks exhibiting similar characteristics, potentially with human guidance. It also enables structured and localized comparisons across the target and source domains to ensure that the patches contain similar features.

P3T-Net was developed and tested in PyTorch on a local workstation equipped with a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU (24 GB memory), AMD 3990 CPU, and 128 GB of RAM. The training times for the semantic-consistent domain transfer module and the misalignment fixing module in each case were as follows: 6.7 h for the laboratory-based micro-CT case, 8.9 h for the synchrotron case, 7.8 h for the lithium-ion cathode case, and 8.3 h for the fuel cell case.

Training image preparation for semantic indication module

The semantic indication module was trained with the target domain and its corresponding ground-truth segmentation. The preparation of the ground truth for the target domain is straightforward, given that these domains typically comprise high-quality, low-noise 3D images. Semantics are defined by the classes segmented from these images. Specifically, in case 1, the semantics of the sandstone rock target image include pores, grains, and high-density minerals. In case 2, the semantics of the dynamic synchrotron sandstone target image consist of water, decane, and solid. It is important to note that although the material is the same in Cases 1 and 2 (sandstone rock), the semantics can vary significantly based on human-defined classifications and the specific research focus. Additionally, for case 3, the semantics of the lithium-ion battery target image consist of the electrode and pore, and in case 4, the semantics of the fuel cell include the pore, fiber, binder, MPL, and catalyst layer.

The workflow for generating the target domain segmentation across the four cases is detailed in Table 2. For cases 1–3, the target domains were first preprocessed using the Non-Local Means (NLM) filter to remove noise. Subsequently, multiphase watershed segmentation is applied to cluster voxels into phases, which are defined as semantics. Watershed segmentation was performed using the Avizo software. Cases 1 and 2 were segmented into three phases and post-processed by opening and closing the segmented voxels to remove segmentation errors, especially at the phase boundaries. In the TWS process, we first annotated a subset of 2D slices to generate training labels, which were then used to train a machine-learning classifier. Data augmentation for segmentation network training

For the battery cathode material, the nano-CT images used were relatively similar, with the primary difference being the exposure time during scanning. For further comparison with the P3T-Net results, a data augmentation workflow was proposed for the segmentation network, as shown in Fig. S21. The two main differences among the four nano-CT images are the grayscale distribution and noise level. To incorporate these two differences in data augmentation, a histogram-matching technique is applied to the target domains using each source domain as the reference image. This histogram matching is performed in MATLAB using the ‘imhistmatch’ function with the ‘polynomial’ matching method. The target domain after histogram matching has the same grayscale distribution as the source domain. By doing this, three additional target domains are generated (aligned with the three source domains) as the data augmentation results.

In the next step, we trained a U-ResNet segmentation network (the same as the model used in the semantic indication module) using the original target domain image along with three target domain images obtained after histogram matching with three source domains. Before training, we considered that different domains have varying levels of noise, with Poisson noise being the most prevalent in nano-CT imaging70. Therefore, we randomly selected 50% of the training patches and added noise to them. A total of 3200 paired training patches, with 800 cropped from the original target domain and an additional 2400 patches generated from the three target domains after histogram matching, served as data augmentation. The trained U-ResNet was then applied to segment the source domains.

Apart from data augmentation, the histogram-matching technique can also be used as a domain transfer method. To test its performance, we applied the histogram-matching technique to all three source domains using the target domain as a reference, as shown in Fig. S22. Subsequently, three histogram-matched source domains were obtained and compared with our P3T-Net transferred results.

Pixel-wise and physical evaluating metrics

Image quality and perceptual index

PSNR, a typical pixel-wise criterion, has become the most prevalent method for assessing image fidelity. The PSNR between the domain-transferred image and its ground truth was calculated to evaluate the model’s performance in domain transfer from a pixel-wise perspective. The PSNR can be expressed as12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{PSNR}}}}(g,f)=10\,{\log }_{10}\left(\frac{25{5}^{2}}{\frac{1}{mn}\mathop{\sum }_{i=1}^{m}\mathop{\sum }_{j=1}^{n}{\left(g(i,j)-f(i,j)\right)}^{2}}\right)$$\end{document}PSNR(g,f)=10log1025521mn∑i=1m∑j=1ng(i,j)−f(i,j)2where g is the domain-transferred image, f is the ground truth image, m and n are the dimension sizes of a given 2D image. i and j are the pixel indices. SSIM is another classical method for measuring the similarity between two images71. Unlike PSNR, which focuses on pixel-wise information, SSIM evaluates the perceptual quality of images that includes luminance, contrast, and structure. The SSIM formula can be expressed as13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{SSIM}}}}(g,f)=\frac{\left(2{u}_{g}{u}_{f}+{c}_{1}\right)\,\left(2{\sigma }_{gf}+{c}_{2}\right)}{\left({u}_{g}^{2}+{u}_{f}^{2}+{c}_{1}\right)\,\left({\sigma }_{g}^{2}+{\sigma }_{f}^{2}+{c}_{2}\right)}$$\end{document}SSIM(g,f)=2uguf+c12σgf+c2ug2+uf2+c1σg2+σf2+c2where g and f are the domain-transferred image and the ground truth, respectively. ug and uf are the means of the domain-transferred image and ground truth, respectively. σg and σf are the standard deviations of the domain-transferred image and the ground truth, respectively. σgf is the covariance of the domain-transferred image and ground truth. c1 and c2 are the regularization constants for luminance and contrast, respectively: c1 = (0.01×L)2, c2 = (0.03×L)2, where L is the dynamic range of the pixel values. For the 8-bit images in all our cases, L = 255. The SSIM is computed locally for each pixel in an image by comparing it to other pixels within an 11 × 11 × 11 window. Then, all window SSIMs were averaged to obtain a global SSIM value. In addition to PSNR and SSIM, NIQE62 and PIQE72, as two non-reference image quality metrics, are introduced to estimate the image quality score of the source, target, and transferred domains. NIQE is designed to measure the naturalness of an image. It is assumed that natural images follow certain statistical distributions, and any deviation from these distributions indicates a reduction in image quality. PIQE focuses more on the human perception of the image. It aims to quantify the perceptual quality of an image by identifying and evaluating image distortions, artifacts, noise, and local variations. In most instances, PIQE performs comparably to NIQE. PIQE assesses blockwise distortion by measuring the local variance of the visibly distorted blocks to calculate the quality score. Generally, lower NIQE and PIQE scores indicate better image quality. Note that PIQE and NIQE are non-reference metrics pretrained on a database of known distortions and pristine images. However, for micro/nano-CT images, subtle textural details and features might be considered perceptible distortions, leading to higher PIQE and NIQE scores. Therefore, NIQE and PIQE are not suitable direct indicators of micro/nano-CT image quality. Instead, they were used to reflect the differences between domains in terms of grayscale values, noise levels, and image style.

Histogram distribution

The histogram distribution is an approximation method used to represent the distribution of voxel values by counting the number of data points in a bin. Histograms were applied to compare the voxel value distributions between the source, target, and transferred domains.

Volume fraction of phases

The volume fraction of each phase was calculated from the segmented image. It is defined as the ratio of the number of voxels clustered in each phase to the total number of voxels in the 3D image.

Pore size distribution

The pore size distribution was obtained by calculating the equivalent diameter of pores in the segmented image. First, each isolated individual pore is labeled from the 3D image using the Separating Object function in TM Avizo. Because the geometry of each pore is irregular, each pore is approximated as a sphere with the same volume. The equivalent diameter of each isolated pore was then calculated and counted to estimate the pore size distribution.

Surface area and phase connectivity

The surface area and connectivity of each phase as geometric properties are measured from Minkowski functionals73,74. We calculated the surface area of each phase in the images from the source domain, target domain, and transferred domain to evaluate the model’s performance. Effective diffusivity

The effective diffusivity of a porous domain can be defined based on Poisson’s equation; specifically, it is based on Fick’s law of diffusion75,76. The key consideration in the case of resolved porous structures is the geometry of the domain, that is, the configuration of the pore space and solid space. An elliptic diffusion equation was solved for the segmented void space of the micro/nano-CT images. Two-phase flow relative permeability

The Navier-Stokes continuum transport equations are estimated using the Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM). In this study, the Lattice Boltzmann for Porous Media (LBPM) software package55 is used, which models immiscible two-phase flow with interfacial tension and wettability interactions between the fluid and solid phases. No-slip boundary conditions were applied to the fluid-solid boundary, and periodic boundary conditions were applied at the prescribed inlet and outlet faces. Fluids are driven by a uniform body force applied to both phases to mimic co-injection experiments, and an accelerated steady-state simulation protocol is utilized based on the coupling of morphological operations with LBPM77.
