Methods for Reducing Ancilla-Overhead in Block Encodings
Francisca Vasconcelos, András Gilyén

Abstract
Block encodings are a fundamental primitive in quantum algorithms but often require large ancilla overhead. In this work, we introduce novel techniques for reducing this overhead in two distinct ways. In Part I, we devise a method for any block encoding that approximately uncomputes all but one of the ancillae (freeing up the ancillae for reuse in later parts of a quantum algorithm), thereby effectively providing a “space-time tradeoff”. More precisely, for any matrix A A , such that ‖ A ‖ ≤ 1 − δ \|A\|\leq 1-\delta , our protocol coherently computes an ε \varepsilon -approximate single-ancilla block-encoding of A A , constructed through O ⁡ ( 1 δ ​ log ⁡ ( 1 ε ) ) O(\frac{1}{\delta}\log(\frac{1}{\epsilon})) queries to any block encoding U U and U † U^{\dagger} . We establish a connection between this procedure and the problem of “phase correction” in modular quantum signal processing. In Part II, we evaluate the minimum number of ancillae required to perform coherent multiplication of block encodings. First, we prove that ⌈ log 2 ​ K ⌉ \lceil\log_{2}K\rceil ancillae is optimal for exact multiplication of block encodings. This lower-bound is saturated by a slight modification to the “compression gadget” of Low and Wiebe (2019). Furthermore, we study ε \varepsilon - approximate multiplication of block encodings in regimes where the block encodings have 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / K ) \mathcal{O}(1/K) -bounded deviation from the identity and propose the p p -Modular Addition Compression Gadget which is shown to achieve accuracy 𝒪 ⁡ ( K − 2 p ) \mathcal{O}(K^{-2^{p}}) using just p p additional ancillae. We discuss applications of the p p -MACG to Hamiltonian simulation and quantum differential equation solvers. Finally, we demonstrate how oblivious amplitude amplification can boost the success probability of an ε \varepsilon -approximate multiplcation of block encodings circuit to Ω ⁡ ( 1 − ε 2 ) \Omega(1-\varepsilon^{2}) .

Connections to Modular Quantum Signal Processing

We forsee the main application of this ancilla uncomputation procedure for algorithms such as the QSVT, enabling preated reuse of ancilla qubits in preparation of multiple block encodings. However, we conclude by describing a less immediate, yet interesting connection between our ancilla uncomputation procedure and the problem of “phase correction” in modular quantum signal processing.

Quantum signal processing (QSP)
[

]
is an important framework for implementing polynomial transformations of unitary operator eigenvalues, via sequences of controlled single-qubit rotations.
Formally, for the scalar

\displaystyle U(x)=\begin{pmatrix}x&\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\\

(5)

\displaystyle U_{\Phi}(x)=e^{i\phi_{0}\sigma_{z}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}O(x)e^{i\phi_{j}\sigma_{z}}=\begin{pmatrix}P(x)&-Q(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\\
Q^{*}(x)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}&-P^{*}(x)\end{pmatrix}.

(6)

\displaystyle\Phi[U_{0},\cdots,U_{t-1}]\equiv e^{i\phi_{0}\sigma_{z}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}U_{s_{j}}e^{i\phi_{j}Z},

2^{\lceil\log_{2}K\rceil}
(

).

For any sequence of

We describe how multiplication of block encodings with bounded distance from the identity arises naturally in applications such as Hamiltonian simulation and quantum differential equation solvers, motivating the practical relevance of the

3.1
An Ancilla Lower-Bound for Exact Multiplication of Block Encodings

The first main contribution of this work is an ancilla lower-bound for, what we refer to as,
exact
multiplication of block encodings (EMBE).

Definition 1

(EMBE)
.

A circuit achieves exact multiplication of block encodings (EMBE) for the sequence of block encodings
(U_{A_{i}})_{i=1}^{K}
if it implements a unitary
U_{\textsf{EMBE}}
satisfying

\displaystyle\bra{0^{m+a}}U_{\textsf{EMBE}}\ket{0^{m+a}}=A_{K}\cdots A_{2}A_{1}

(14)

In order to prove the lower-bound, we begin by formalizing the class of circuits implementing multiplication of block encodings. Suppose that we have a sequence of
K
block encodings
(U_{A_{i}})_{i=1}^{K}
such that, with
n
qubits in the computational register, and
a
ancillae for the block encoding. We will assume that the sequence of all “good” measurements is governed by projectors
(\Pi_{0^{a}})_{i=1}^{K}

that we want to post-select for. As we saw in
Section

1
, there are multiple possible circuits that coherently implement this multiplication of block encodings (especially consider a varying number of measurement ancillae
m
). We will, thus, refer to the class containing all such circuits as the Multiple Coherent Measurement (
MCM
) circuit class. We further prove that the following is a simple universal circuit parameterization for
MCM
circuits.

Definition 2

(
MCM
{}_{\vec{U}_{A},\vec{\Pi}}
Circuit Class)
.

Suppose we are given series of
K

(n+a)
-qubit block-encodings
\vec{U}_{A}=(U_{A_{i}})_{i=1}^{K}
. Without loss of generality, assume that all
U_{A_{i}}
have
\Pi_{0^{a}}
as the
a
-qubit projector onto “good” measurement outcome and
\Pi_{\perp}=I_{a}-\Pi_{0^{a}}
as the
a
-qubit projector onto “bad” measurement outcome. With this, we define
\textsf{MCM}_{\vec{U}_{A},K,m}
to be the class of all circuits of the form,

\displaystyle\textsf{MCM}_{\vec{U}_{A},K,m}(\vec{V},Q)=(Q\otimes U_{K})\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{K-1}\left(\left(V_{K-i}\otimes\Pi_{\perp}\otimes I_{n}\right)\left(I_{m+a}\otimes U_{K-i}\right)\right),

(15)

parameterized by the
m
-qubit unitaries
Q
and
\vec{V}=(V_{i})_{i=1}^{K-1}
. The circuit is depicted in
Figure

4
.

Figure 4:

\textsf{MCM}_{\vec{U}_{A},K,m}(\vec{V},Q)

Leveraging the structure of the
MCM
circuit class and a Hilbert-space dimension counting argument, we prove the following measurement ancilla lower-bound for multiplication of block encodings.

Theorem 3

(EMBE Ancilla Lower-Bound)
.

In order to coherently implement an exact multiplication of
K
block encodings, at least
m=\lceil\log_{2}K\rceil
measurement ancillae are required.

Since the previously described
[

]

3.2
Approximate Multiplication of Block Encodings

In an attempt to surpass the ancilla lower-bound for
exact
multiplication of block encodings (EMBE), we propose the notion of
approximate
multiplication of block encodings (AMBE).

Definition 3

(
\varepsilon
-AMBE)
.

A circuit achieves
\varepsilon
-approximate multiplication of block encodings (
\varepsilon
-AMBE) for the sequence of block encodings
(U_{A_{i}})_{i=1}^{K}
if it implements a unitary
U_{\textsf{AMBE}}
satisfying

\displaystyle\|A_{K}\cdots A_{2}A_{1}-\bra{0^{m+a}}U_{\textsf{AMBE}}\ket{0^{m+a}}\|\leq\varepsilon.

(16)

In other words, a circuit implementing
\varepsilon
-AMBE implements an
\varepsilon
-precise block encoding of the desired multiplication matrix
A_{K}\cdots A_{2}A_{1}
.

Since the logarithmic overhead of EMBE circuits is most problematic in the large-
K
limit, we focus on reducing the ancilla overhead in near-asymptotic regimes. In order for multiplication of block-encodings to have high enough success probability to be useful in near-asymptotic regimes, the block encodings themselves must have high success probability. Therefore, we specifically focus on regimes in which each block encoding has
\mathcal{O}(1/K)
-bounded deviation from the identity, i.e.

\displaystyle\|U_{A_{i}}-I\|\leq\eta_{\text{max}}=\mathcal{O}(1/K).

(17)

Although this constraint might seem strong, in
Section

3.3
we argue that it is well-motivated by practical quantum algorithms, such as Hamiltonian simulation and differential equation solvers.

In this regime, to surpass the EMBE ancilla lower-bound, we propose the
p
-Modular Addition Compression Gadget (
p
-MACG), as depicted in
Figure

3
. This is a generalization of the
[
LW19
]
compression gadget which, instead of acting on
m=\lceil\log_{2}K\rceil
measurement ancilla, parametrizes the number of measurement ancilla
m
by
p=\mathcal{O}(1)
and thus performs addition mod-
2^{p}
. Similar to the
[
LW19
]
compression gadget, the
p
-MACG always correctly identifies the good measurement sequence
\{0^{a}\}^{K}
. However, since
2^{p}<K
, there are certain bad measurement sequences with
2^{p}
total bad measurements that the
p
-MACG misclassifies as the good measurement sequence. This incurs an error in the block encoding. However, we prove the following key result, bounding the total error as a function of
p
.

Theorem 4

(
p
-MACG Error Bound)
.

Let
(U_{A_{i}})_{i=1}^{K}
be a sequence of
K
block encodings, with maximal deviation coefficent
\eta_{\max}=\frac{c}{K}
for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
For any
p\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
p=\mathcal{O}(1)
, the
p
-ACG implements an
\varepsilon
-precise block encoding of the desired multiplication sequence
A_{K}\cdots A_{2}A_{1}
, with inverse-polynomial precision in
K
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon=2e^{c}\cdot\left(\frac{e\cdot c^{2}}{K\cdot 2^{p}}\right)^{2^{p}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{K^{2^{p}}}\right).

(18)

Therefore, in the regime of block encodings with
\mathcal{O}(1/K)
-bounded operator norm distance from the identity, we prove that the
p
-MACG is an
\mathcal{O}(K^{-2^{p}})
-AMBE. This implies that even a
1
-MACG, using only a single measurement ancilla, can achieve an
\mathcal{O}(K^{-2})
-precise block encoding of
A_{K}\cdots A_{2}A_{1}
. Thus, the EMBE ancilla lower-bound can be surpassed in the approximate multiplication setting.

3.3
Practical Applications of the
p
-MACG

As previously discussed, the guarantees of the
p
-MACG only hold in regimes where all
K
block encodings have
\mathcal{O}(1/K)
-bounded distance from the identity (
Equation

17
). We will now motivate this constraint by demonstrating how it naturally emerges in the settings of Hamiltonian simulation and quantum differential equation solvers.

In Hamiltonian simulation, the goal is to implement the evolution operator of some Hamiltonian
H
at a specified time
t
, i.e.
U(t)=e^{-iHt}
. However, in practice, direct simulation of the full evolution is often intractable and approximations are necessary. A common approach is Trotterization, in which the Hamiltonian is decomposed into a sum of simpler terms, i.e.
H=\sum_{i}H_{i}
, to which the Trotter approximation
e^{\delta(H_{i}+H_{j})}\approx e^{\delta H_{i}}e^{\delta H_{j}}
can be applied. Thus, the evolution operator can be expressed as a product of short time-scale evolution terms
e^{-iH_{i}t/K}
, as

\displaystyle U(t)=e^{-iHt}=\left(e^{-i(\sum_{i}H_{i})t/K}\right)^{K}\approx\left(\prod_{i}e^{-iH_{i}t/K}\right)^{K}.

(19)

Expanding these small evolution terms via a Taylor series gives

\displaystyle e^{-iH_{i}t/K}=I-i\frac{t}{K}H_{i}-i\frac{t^{2}}{2K}H_{i}^{2}+\cdots.

(20)

Under the standard assumption that
\|H_{i}\|\leq 1
, for a fixed time
t
and sufficiently large
K
, the dominant deviation from the identity comes from the first term, i.e.

\displaystyle\|I-e^{-iH_{i}t/K}\|=\mathcal{O}\left(1/K\right).

(21)

All that remains is to ensure the block encoding of
e^{-iH_{i}t/K}
also has
\mathcal{O}\left(1/K\right)
-bounded distance from identity. Theoretically, this can be straightfowardly achieved using the block encoding

\displaystyle U_{e^{-iH_{i}t/K}}=\outerproduct{0}{0}\otimes e^{-iH_{i}t/K}+\outerproduct{1}{1}\otimes I,

(22)

which has the desired distance from the identity:

\displaystyle\|I-U_{e^{-iH_{i}t/K}}\|=\left\|\outerproduct{0}{0}\otimes(I-e^{-iH_{i}t/K})+\outerproduct{1}{1}\otimes(I-I)\right\|=\|I-e^{-iH_{i}t/K}\|=\mathcal{O}\left(1/K\right).

(23)

While such a block encoding might be practically challenging to implement, it demonstrates that block encodings satisfying the
p
-MACG criterion exist for Hamiltonian simulation in large-
K
regimes.

In the setting of quantum differential equation solvers, specifically for linear ordinary differential equations, the goal is to solve the initial value problem

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi(t)}=A(t)\ket{\psi(t)},\quad\ket{\psi(0)}=\ket{\psi_0}

(24)

for some
A(t)
, a matrix-valued function of the time variable
t
, to recover the state
\ket{\psi(T)}
at desired time
t=T
. This can be directly related to the problem of time-dependent Hamiltonian simulation by setting
A(t)=-iH(t)
for some Hamiltonian
H(t)
. For appropriately bounded
A
, the unique solution to the time-dependent differential equation is given by the Dyson evolution operator

\displaystyle\Xi=\mathcal{D}(t,t_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}A(s)ds\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-i)^{k}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}ds_{k}\int_{t_{0}}^{s_{k}}ds_{k-1}\cdots\int_{t_{0}}^{s_{2}}ds_{1}A(s_{k})\cdots A(s_{1})

(25)

where
\mathcal{T}
is the time-ordering operator. A common approximation approach is the time-marching strategy, as described in
[
FLT23
]
, in which the full time
T
is divided into shorter time intervals for which the system is solved and then the information is propagated. In the case that these intervals are choosen uniformly, i.e.
\Delta t=t/K
, the evolution operator is decomposed as

\displaystyle\Xi=\mathcal{D}(t,0)\approx\mathcal{D}(K\Delta t,(K-1)\Delta t)\cdots\mathcal{D}(2\Delta t,\Delta t)\cdot\mathcal{D}(\Delta t,0)=\Xi_{K}\Xi_{K-1}\cdots\Xi_{2}\Xi_{1},

(26)

where
\Xi_{j}
are themselves short time-scale Dyson evolution operators,

\displaystyle\Xi_{j}=\mathcal{D}(t_{j}+\Delta t,t_{j})=I-i\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j}+\Delta t}ds_{1}A(s_{1})-\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j}+\Delta t}ds_{2}\int_{t_{j}}^{s_{2}}ds_{1}A(s_{2})A(s_{1})+\cdots.

(27)

If
A(t)
is uniformly bounded, i.e.
\|A(t)\|\leq\lambda
for all time
t
, then the terms of
\Xi_{j}
are bounded as

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j}+\Delta t}ds_{1}A(s_{1})\right\|\leq\lambda\Delta t,\quad\left\|\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j}+\Delta t}ds_{2}\int_{t_{j}}^{s_{2}}ds_{1}A(s_{2})A(s_{1})\right\|\leq\frac{(\lambda\Delta t)^{2}}{2},\quad\cdots.

(28)

Thus, for sufficiently large
K
(sufficiently small
\Delta t=t/K
), the distance of
\Xi_{j}
from the identity is bounded, via triangle inequality, as

\displaystyle\|I-\Xi_{j}\|\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(\lambda\Delta t)^{k}}{k!}=e^{\lambda\Delta t}-1\approx\lambda\Delta t=\mathcal{O}(1/K).

(29)

Thus, as described in the previous example, we can strategically block encode
\Xi_{j}
such that
U_{\Xi_{j}}
also has
\mathcal{O}(1/K)
-bounded distance from the identity, thereby satisfying the
p
-MACG criterion.

3.4
Oblivious Amplitude Amplification for Approximate Multiplication

As discussed in the introduction, a key benefit of coherent multiplication of block encodings is that amplitude amplification can be used to boost the probability of the good measurement sequence. Recall that amplitude amplification (AA) is a Grover-style procedure, in which reflections are repeatedly performed around the desired “winner” state
\ket{\psi_\text{good}}=A_{K}\cdots A_{2}A_{1}\ket{\psi}
and the intial state
\ket{\psi_0}
. In oblivious amplitude amplification (OAA) the ancillary state
\ket{0^{m+a}}
signals the subspace of the desired block encoding, meaning reflections about the winner state are oblivious to the form of the state itself. For more detailed descriptions of AA and OAA, we refer the reader to
Section

B.4.1
and
Section

B.4.2
, respectively.

In
Section

B.4.3
, we describe how OAA can be implemented with queries to an EMBE circuit, so as to boost the probability of the good measurement sequence to
\Omega(1)
. In
Section

B.4.4
, we further prove the following lemma, demonstrating how OAA can similarly boost the probability of the good measurement sequence for an
\varepsilon
-AMBE circuit.

Lemma 5

(OAA for
\varepsilon
-AMBE)
.

Via repeated queries to an
\varepsilon
-approximate multiplication of block encodings circuit, oblivious amplitude amplification can be used to boost the fidelity between the circuit output and the desired state to
\Omega(1-\varepsilon^{2})
.

4
Discussion

This work provides efficient procedures for reducing ancilla overheads in block encodings. Namely, we prove guarantees for an approximate multiplication compression gadget in the regime where all
K
block encodings have
\mathcal{O}(1/K)
-bounded operator norm distance from identity and motivate this as a relevant assumption for practical applications such as Hamiltonian simulation and differential equation solvers. However, we leave it as future work to explore other regimes in which approximate compression gadgets may offer savings relative to the exact ancilla lower-bound, and to explore different applications where the presented techniques may offer substantial savings.

We note that the formulation and achieved savings of approximate multiplication of block encodings has parallels with classical sketching algorithms
[
Nel11
]
. In particular, since we only care about distinguishing the all-good measurement sequence from any bad measurement sequences, the ancilla workspace can be substantially compressed relative to a gadget tracking the exact form of the entire measurement sequence. It would be interesting to further explore the connections with classical sketching to assess whether advanced classical techniques could result in further ancilla-savings in the quantum setting, potentially for a broader set of block encoding tasks.

Appendix A
Part I: Approximate Single-Ancilla Block Encodings

In this section, we will show how to uncompute all but one of a block encoding’s ancillae. The uncomputed ancillae can then be reused for other tasks (beyond implementing the block encoding) in a quantum algorithm. This establishes a “space-time tradeoff” in which we can free up space (ancilla) via computation (time). Alternatively, this demonstrates that for any matrix
\mathcal{A}
, there exists an
\varepsilon
-precise block-encoding of
\mathcal{A}
requiring only one ancilla qubit.

In
Section

A.1
we describe this uncomputation procedure, in detail, for single-ancilla block encodings of Hermitian matrices
\mathcal{H}
. In
Section

A.2
we describe how this procedure can be generalized to single-ancilla block encodings of general matrices
\mathcal{A}
.

A.1
Single-Ancilla Block Encodings of Hermitian Matrices

Let
V_{\mathcal{H}}
be a
(1,a,0)
-block-encoding of

\mathcal{H}
, where
\norm{\herm}\leq 1-\delta

.

-block encoding of

In particular, we will prove the following main theorem.

Theorem 6
.

Let

be a
(1,a,0)
-block-encoding of Hermitian matrix

, where
\norm{\herm}\leq 1-\delta

B.2
Exact Compression Gadgets

We will now introduce the notions of

Definition 5

(Exact Multiplication of Block Encodings (EMBE))

As described in the introduction (

Definition 6

(Naive EMBE)
.

For a sequence of
K

(U_{A_{i}})_{i=1}^{K}

\textsf{MCM}_{\vec{U}_{A},K,m}(\vec{V},Q)

m=K-1

, and

\displaystyle V_{i}=X_{i}\otimes I_{[K-1]\backslash i}\quad\forall i\in[K-1].

(97)

However, as discussed in the introduction, this Naive EMBE is far from optimal in terms of the number of measurement ancillae

6

(Exact Compression Gadget (ECG))

For a sequence of

(U_{A_{i}})_{i=1}^{K}

B.2.1
The
[

In their work on quantum simulation via truncated Dyson series, Low and Wiebe

Definition 8

(
[

For a sequence of

(U_{A_{i}})_{i=1}^{K}

[

\textsf{MCM}_{\vec{U}_{A},K,m}(\vec{V},Q)

\displaystyle V_{i}=\textnormal{{ADD}${}_{m}$}\quad\forall i\in[K-1],

(98)

where
\textnormal{{ADD}${}_{m}$}\ket{c}=\ket{c+1 \bmod{2^m}}
is the addition operation mod-

To see gain intuition for why this DCG successfully implements a DMBE, note that the
m
-qubit measurment ancillae register can count bit strings up to
2^{m}=2^{\lceil\log_{2}(K+1)\rceil}\geq K+1
. The register is initialized to
\ket{0^m}
and, for each bad (
\perp
) measurement,
+1
is added, mapping to a basis state
\ket{x}\neq\ket{0}
. Furthermore, since at most, the
ADD

(K+1)

\ket{0}

\ket{0^m}

\ket{x}\neq\ket{0}
. Therefore, come measurement time, if we measure the
\ket{0^m}
state, the final state in the computation register is accepted. Otherwise, the final state is rejected.

B.2.2
An Ancilla Lower-Bound for Exact Compression Gadgets

We will now prove a tight lower bound on the number of measurement ancillae necessary to implement an ECG and, thus, EMBE within the
MCM
circuit class.

Theorem 12
.

(ECG Ancilla Lower-Bound)
Any
\textsf{MCM}_{\vec{U}_{A},K,m}(\vec{V},Q)
circuit that implements an exact multiplication of
K
block encodings requires at least
m=\lceil\log_{2}(K)\rceil
measurement ancillae.

Remark 1
.

This lower bound is tight, since the modified
[
LW19
]
ECG saturates this bound.

Proof of
Theorem

12
.

In order to implement a DMBE, we must ensure that we have enough degrees of freedom (measurement ancillae) to distinguish two cases: 1) all intermediate measurements are
0^{a}
and 2) any combination of intermediate measurements such that at least one is not
0^{a}
.

Note that we can directly determine whether or not the
K^{th}
block-encoding was successful via our ultimate measurement of the
a
-qubit block encoding ancilla register. Therefore, we only need to determine the success of the first
K-1
intermediate block encodings. If we think of each of these intermediate measurements as taking value
0^{a}
or value
\perp=(
not
0^{a})
, then the set of all possible intermediate measurement outcomes is
\mathcal{M}=\{0^{a},\perp\}^{K-1}
, such that
|\mathcal{M}|=2^{K-1}
. Our goal is to distinguish the
\{0^{a}\}^{K-1}
outcome from the
\mathcal{M}\backslash\{0^{a}\}^{K-1}
outcomes.

Leveraging the
MCM
circuit structure, described in
Lemma

11
, we can reason about the number of ancillary degrees of freedom needed to distinguish these measurement outcomes. Let
\mathcal{S}_{0}
denote the intermediate measurement indices corresponding to good
0^{a}
outcomes after the deferred measurement. We will define
\mathcal{S}_{\perp}=[K]\backslash\mathcal{S}_{0}
to be indices corresponding to bad
\perp
outcomes. Thus, before the deferred measurement, the measurement ancilla register of a
\textsf{MCM}_{\vec{U}_{A},\vec{\Pi}}(\vec{V},Q)
circuit will contain the state

\displaystyle\ket{\phi}=Q\left(\prod_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\perp}}V_{i}\right)\ket{0^m}.

(99)

Note that if all the measurements are good, i.e.
\mathcal{S}_{0}=[K-1]
and
\mathcal{S}_{\perp}=\emptyset
, the measurement ancilla register will simply contain the state
\ket{\phi_0}=Q\ket{0^m}
. At the end of the computation, to distinguish “good” from “bad” measurement sequences, we must perform a projective measurement onto the basis
\{\Pi_{\textnormal{good}},\Pi_{\textnormal{bad}}\}
, with projectors

\displaystyle\Pi_{\textnormal{good}}

\displaystyle:=Q\outerproduct{0^m}{0^m}Q^{\dagger}

(100)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\textnormal{bad}}

\displaystyle:=I-\Pi_{\textnormal{good}}.

(101)

With this projective measurement basis, if all the measurements are good, then

\displaystyle\bra{\phi_0}\Pi_{\textnormal{good}}\ket{\phi_0}=\bra{0^m}Q^{\dagger}Q\outerproduct{0^m}{0^m}Q^{\dagger}Q\ket{0^m}=1.

(102)

Therefore, in order to determinstically distinguish this state from all the bad measurement outcome sequences, there must exist sufficient ancilliary degrees of freedom to ensure that, for all
\mathcal{S}_{\perp}\subseteq[K]
such that
\mathcal{S}_{\perp}\neq\emptyset
,

\displaystyle\bra{\phi}\Pi_{\textnormal{bad}}\ket{\phi}

\displaystyle=1-\bra{0^m}\left(\prod_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\perp}}V_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\outerproduct{0^m}{0^m}\prod_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\perp}}V_{i}\ket{0^m}=1-\left|\bra{0^m}\prod_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\perp}}V_{i}\ket{0^m}\right|^{2}=1.

(103)

This implies that

\displaystyle\bra{0^m}\prod_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\perp}}V_{i}\ket{0^m}=0,\quad\forall\mathcal{S}_{\perp}\subseteq[K-1]~\text{ s.t. }\mathcal{S}_{\perp}\neq\emptyset,

(104)

meaning the set of states

\displaystyle\left\{V_{1}\ket{0^m},V_{2}\ket{0^m},V_{2}V_{1}\ket{0^m},V_{3}\ket{0^m},...,\prod_{i\in[K-1]}V_{i}\ket{0^m}\right\}

(105)

must all be perpendicular to the state
\ket{0^m}
.

In order to determine the minimum possible number of measurement ancillae
m
needed to ensure that all these constraints are satisfied and states are perpendicular, we will reason about the smallest possible Hilbert space in which all these states can be orthogonal. Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
be the Hilbert space containing all possible measurement ancilla states orthogonal to
\ket{0^m}
after measurement
i
. That is,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{0}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{span}\{\emptyset\},

(106)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{span}\{V_{1}\ket{0^m}\},

(107)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{span}\{V_{1}\ket{0^m},V_{2}\ket{0^m},V_{2}V_{1}\ket{0^m}\},

(108)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{3}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{span}\{V_{1}\ket{0^m},V_{2}\ket{0^m},V_{3}\ket{0^m},V_{2}V_{1}\ket{0^m},V_{3}V_{2}\ket{0^m},V_{3}V_{1}\ket{0^m},V_{3}V_{2}V_{1}\ket{0^m}\}

(109)

(110)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{span}\{V_{i+1}\ket{0^m},V_{i+1}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i},\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i}\}

(111)

We will now prove that,

\displaystyle\dim(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i+1})\geq\dim(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i})+1,\quad\forall i\in[K-1],

(112)

via proof by contradiction.

We begin by assuming that
\dim(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i+1})=\dim(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i})
. Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i+1}\supseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
, this implies that
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i+1}=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
. Thus, there exists a bijection between
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i+1}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
, governed by multiplication by the
V_{i+1}
unitary matrix and its inverse
V_{i+1}^{\dagger}
. From
Equation

111
, since
V_{i}\ket{0^m}
is an element of
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i+1}
, via the inverse mapping,

V_{i}^{\dagger}V_{i}\ket{0^m}=\ket{0^m}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
. This, however, is a contradiction, since
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
was defined to be the set of all possible measurement ancilla states
orthogonal
to
\ket{0^m}
after measurement
i
.

Following from our proof, after
K-1
measurements the dimension of Hilbert space orthogonal to
\ket{0^m}
is lower-bounded as
\dim(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{K-1})\geq K-1
. Note, however, that the full Hilbert space of the measurement ancillae,
\mathcal{H}_{K-1}
must also contain
\ket{0^m}
state, meaning

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{K-1}=\textnormal{span}\{\ket{0^m},\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{K-1}\},\quad\text{s.t. }\dim(\mathcal{H}_{K-1})\geq K.

(113)

Therefore, the number of measurement ancillae needed to produce an
\mathcal{H}_{K-1}
Hilbert space is

\displaystyle m\geq\lceil\log_{2}K\rceil,

(114)

obtaining our desired lower-bound.
∎

B.3
Approximate Compression Gadgets

In the standard block encodings literature, oftentimes creating exact block encodings can be too challenging or demanding. As such, there exists a notion of
approximation
for block encodings. In particular, the matrix
U_{B}
is considered an
\varepsilon
-precise block encoding of matrix
B
if

\displaystyle\|B-\bra{0^a}U_{B}\ket{0^a}\|\leq\varepsilon,

(115)

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the operator norm. Inspired by this measure of “closeness” to a desired block encoding, in this section, we will introduce the notions of
approximate
multiplication of block encodings and
approximate
compression gadgets. Leveraging these, we will show that in certain regimes of multiplication of block encodings, approximate compression gadgets can be constructed that achieve high-precision multiplication of block encodings while requiring only a constant number of measurement ancillae—surpassing the previously established EMBE lower-bound.

We begin by defining
approximate
multiplication of block encodings (AMBE) and
approximate
compression gadgets (ACGs) as follows.

Definition 9

(
\varepsilon
-Approximate Multiplication of Block Encodings (
\varepsilon
-AMBE))
.

We will refer to an
\textsf{MCM}_{\vec{U}_{A},K,m}(\vec{V},Q)
circuit as an
approximate multiplication of block encodings (
\varepsilon
-AMBE)
if

\displaystyle\|A_{[K]}-\bra{0^{m+a}}\textsf{MCM}_{\vec{U}_{A},K,m}(\vec{V},Q)\ket{0^{m+a}}\|\leq\varepsilon,

(116)

thereby implementing an
\varepsilon
-precise block encoding of the desired multiplication
A_{[K]}
.

Note that from this definition, it follows that for any input
\ket{\psi}
, the output state of an
\varepsilon
-approximate multiplication of block encodings circuit is
\varepsilon
-close to the desired
A_{[K]}\ket{\psi}
output state.

Claim 7
.

Let
U_{\widetilde{A}_{[K]}}
denote the unitary implemented by an
\varepsilon
-approximate multiplication of block encodings circuit, such that
\widetilde{A}_{[K]}=\bra{0^{m+a}}U_{\widetilde{A}_{[K]}}\ket{0^{m+a}}
. For an arbitrary input state
\ket{\psi}
, let
\ket*{\widetilde{\phi}}=\widetilde{A}_{[K]}\ket{\psi}
denote the post-selected
\varepsilon
-approximate circuit output and let
\ket{\phi}=A_{[K]}\ket{\psi}
denote the desired exact output. Then, the approximate and desired outputs have fidelity

\displaystyle|\bra{\phi}\ket*{\widetilde{\phi}}|^{2}\geq 1-\varepsilon^{2}.

(117)

Proof of
7
.

From the definition of
\varepsilon
-AMBE, we have that

\displaystyle\|A_{K}-\widetilde{A}_{[K]}\|\leq\varepsilon.

(118)

Since the input state to the circuit
\ket{\psi}
is normalized, i.e.
\|\ket{\psi}\|=1
, this implies that

\displaystyle\|\ket{\phi}-\ket*{\widetilde{\phi}}\|=\|A_{K}\ket{\psi}-\widetilde{A}_{[K]}\ket{\psi}\|\leq\|A_{K}-\widetilde{A}_{[K]}\|\cdot\|\ket{\psi}\|\leq\varepsilon.

(119)

By the properties of the operator norm,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\geq\|\ket{\phi}-\ket*{\widetilde{\phi}}\|^{2}=(\bra{\phi}-\bra*{\widetilde{\phi}})(\ket{\phi}-\ket*{\widetilde{\phi}})=2-2\Re{\bra{\phi}\ket*{\widetilde{\phi}}},

(120)

which implies that

\displaystyle|\bra{\phi}\ket*{\widetilde{\phi}}|\geq\Re{\bra{\phi}\ket*{\widetilde{\phi}}}\geq 1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}.

(121)

Via some arithmetic manipulation, we obtain the desired lower-bound:

\displaystyle|\bra{\phi}\ket*{\widetilde{\phi}}|^{2}\geq\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)^{2}=1-\varepsilon^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{4}\geq 1-\varepsilon^{2}.

(122)

This concludes the proof.
∎

In the last section, we established that

MBE can be implemented optimally with
m=\lceil\log_{2}K\rceil
measurement ancillae. Therefore, we will require that an approximate compression gadget use less than this number of measurement ancillae.

Definition 10

(
\varepsilon
-Approximate Compression Gadget (

For a sequence of
K

(U_{A_{i}})_{i=1}^{K}

\textsf{MCM}_{\vec{U}_{A},K,m}(\vec{V},Q)
circuit which implementing
\varepsilon

\varepsilon
-approximate compression gadget (

With these general definitions, we will now propose a specific compression gadget–the

-Modular Addition Compression Gadget (

B.3.1
The

Our proposal for an

qubits, which implies that the

(
p
-Modular Addition Compression Gadget (

\displaystyle V_{i}=\textsf{ADD}_{p},\quad\forall i\in[K-1],

All that remains is to show that the

\varepsilon

B.3.2
Surpassing the Ancilla Lower-Bound for Exact Compression Gadgets

We will now show that for a broad regime of multiplication of block encoding sequences, the

-MACG is an
\varepsilon
-ACG where
\varepsilon=\mathcal{O}(1/K^{2^{p}})
.

We begin by specifying the regime of multiplication of block encoding sequences that we will consider. Suppose we have a sequence of
K

(U_{A_{i}})_{i=0}^{K-1}
, such that

\displaystyle A_{i}=\bra{0^a}U_{A_{i}}\ket{0^a},

(124)

where
A_{i}
acts on
n
qubits and
U_{A_{i}}
acts on
n+a
qubits. Performing an asymptotic number of multiplications of block encodings only makes sense if each block encoding is fairly close to the identity, i.e., for all
i
,

\displaystyle\|U_{A_{i}}-I\|\leq\eta_{i}

(125)

for some small
\varepsilon_{i}<<1
. Since
\eta_{i}
captures the deviation of the
i^{\text{th}}
block encoding matrix from the identity, we will refer to
\eta_{i}
as the
i^{\text{th}}

\displaystyle U_{A_{i}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bra{0^a}U_{A_{i}}\ket{0^a}&\bra{0^a}U_{A_{i}}\ket{\perp}\\
\bra{\perp}U_{A_{i}}\ket{0^a}&\bra{\perp}U_{A_{i}}\ket{\perp}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{i}&B_{i}\\
C_{i}&D_{i}\end{bmatrix}.

(126)

This implies that

\displaystyle U_{A_{i}}=\outerproduct{0^a}{0^a}\otimes A_{i}+\outerproduct{0^a}{\perp}\otimes B_{i}+\outerproduct{\perp}{0^a}\otimes C_{i}+\outerproduct{\perp}{\perp}\otimes D_{i}

(127)

where
Equation

125
imposes that

\displaystyle 1-\eta_{i}\leq\|A_{i}\|\leq 1+\eta_{i},\quad\quad\|B_{i}\|\leq\eta_{i},\quad\|C_{i}\|\leq\eta_{i},\quad 1-\eta_{i}\leq\|D_{i}\|\leq 1+\eta_{i}.

(128)

Finally, we will denote the maximal deviation coefficient among all
K

\displaystyle\eta_{\text{max}}=\max_{i\in[K]}\eta_{i}.

(129)

With this new notation, we will now prove that in the regime of MBEs where
\eta=\mathcal{O}(1/K)
, (despite only requiring a constant number of measurement ancillae) a

-ACG can implement an
\varepsilon
-precise block-encoding of the desired multiplication with

.

Theorem 13
.

Let
(U_{A_{i}})_{i=1}^{K}
be a sequence of
K

\eta_{\max}=\frac{c}{K}
for some constant

.
For any
p\in\mathbb{Z}

\varepsilon

A_{[K]}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\|A_{[K]}-\bra{0^{p+a}}U_{pACG}\ket{0^{p+a}}\|\leq\varepsilon,

(130)

with inverse-polynomial precision in
K
:

\displaystyle\varepsilon=2e^{c}\cdot\left(\frac{e\cdot c^{2}}{K\cdot 2^{p}}\right)^{2^{p}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{K^{2^{p}}}\right).

(131)

Proof of
Theorem

13
.

For ease of notation, we will denote the block encoding error as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{BE}(U_{pACG})

\displaystyle=\|A_{[K]}-\bra{0^{m+a}}U_{pACG}\ket{0^{m+a}}\|.

(132)

Thus, the block encoding achieved by the
p
-ACG can be decomposed as:

\displaystyle\bra{0^{p+a}}

\displaystyle U_{pACG}\ket{0^{p+a}}

(133)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{K-1}}\bra{0^p}~\textsf{ADD}_{p}^{|x|}~\ket{0^p}\cdot\bra{0^a}U_{A_{K}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{K-1}\Pi_{0^{a}}^{1-x_{i}}\Pi_{\perp}^{x_{i}}U_{A_{i}}\right)\ket{0^a}

(134)

\displaystyle=\innerproduct{0^p}{0^p}\cdot\bra{0^a}U_{A_{K}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{K-1}\Pi_{0^{a}}U_{A_{i}}\right)\ket{0^a}

(135)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{K-1}\backslash 0^{K-1}}\bra{0^p}~\textsf{ADD}_{p}^{|x|}~\ket{0^p}\cdot\bra{0^a}U_{A_{K}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{K-1}\Pi_{0^{a}}^{1-x_{i}}\Pi_{\perp}^{x_{i}}U_{A_{i}}\right)\ket{0^a}

(136)

\displaystyle=A_{[K]}+\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{K-1}\backslash 0^{K-1}}\bra{0^p}~\textsf{ADD}_{p}^{|x|}~\ket{0^p}\cdot\bra{0^a}U_{A_{K}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{K-1}\Pi_{0^{a}}^{1-x_{i}}\Pi_{\perp}^{x_{i}}U_{A_{i}}\right)\ket{0^a}.

(137)

From hereon, we will denote the block encoded matrix corresponding to the bad measurement sequence governed by string
x
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{x}=\bra{0^a}U_{A_{K}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{K-1}\Pi_{0^{a}}^{1-x_{i}}\Pi_{\perp}^{x_{i}}U_{A_{i}}\right)\ket{0^a}.

(138)

Furthermore, note that

\displaystyle\bra{0^p}~\textsf{ADD}_{p}^{|x|}~\ket{0^p}=\delta\{|x|=0\bmod{2^{p}}\}.

(139)

Thus,
Equation

137
further simplifies to

\displaystyle\bra{0^{p+a}}U_{pACG}\ket{0^{p+a}}

\displaystyle=A_{[K]}+\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{K-1}\backslash 0^{K-1}}\mathcal{S}_{x}\cdot\delta\{|x|=0\bmod{2^{p}}\}

(140)

\displaystyle=A_{[K]}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\{0,1\}^{K-1}\backslash 0^{K-1}:\\
|x|=0\bmod{2^{p}}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{S}_{x},

(141)

which implies that the block encoding error can be upper-bounded as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{BE}(U_{pACG})

\displaystyle=\|A_{[K]}-\bra{0^{p+a}}U_{HR}\ket{0^{p+a}}\|=\left\|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\{0,1\}^{K-1}\backslash 0^{K-1}:\\
|x|=0\bmod{2^{p}}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{S}_{x}\right\|\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\{0,1\}^{K-1}\backslash 0^{K-1}:\\
|x|=0\bmod{2^{p}}\end{subarray}}\|\mathcal{S}_{x}\|.

(142)

We will now show that the
\|\mathcal{S}_{x}\|
terms in this expression have a simple upper-bound in terms of
\eta_{\max}
,
|x|
, and
K
.

Claim 8
.

\|\mathcal{S}_{x}\|\leq(\eta_{\max})^{2|x|}\cdot(1+\eta_{\max})^{K}

Proof of
8
.

For
x\in\{0,1\}^{K-1}\backslash 0^{K-1}
, the
\mathcal{S}_{x}
terms
encode all possible “bad” multiplication sequences. The length-
(K-1)
bit-string
x
encodes whether each measurement outcome was “good” (0) or “bad” (1). Therefore, in the length-
(K+1)
bit-string sequence
\widetilde{x}=(0,x,0)
, each time a 0 is followed by a 1, this represents a transition between the good and bad ancilla subspaces. This corresponds to the application of one of the off-diagonal block encoded matrices, i.e.
B_{i}
or
C_{i}
in
Equation

126
, which both have operator norm
\leq\eta_{i}
(
Equation

128
). Meanwhile, if a 0 is followed by a 0 or a 1 is followed by a 1, the measurement leaves the system in the good or bad subspace, respectively. This corresponds to the application of one of the off-diagonal block encoded matrices, i.e.
A_{i}
or
D_{i}
in
Equation

126
, which both have operator norm
\leq 1+\eta_{i}
(
Equation

128
).

Let
\Delta x
denote the length-
K
bit string that tracks these differences in
\widetilde{x}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\Delta x_{i}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }\widetilde{x}_{i+1}=\widetilde{x}_{i}\\
1,&\text{ if }\widetilde{x}_{i+1}\neq\widetilde{x}_{i}\end{cases}.

(143)

Therefore, leveraging the fact that
|\Delta x|\leq 2|x|
,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}_{x}\|

\displaystyle\leq(\eta_{\max})^{|\Delta x|}\cdot(1+\eta_{\max})^{K-|\Delta x|}\leq(\eta_{\max})^{2|x|}\cdot(1+\eta_{\max})^{K}.

(144)

This concludes the proof of
8
.
∎

Plugging this into the block encoding error upper-bound of
Equation

142
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{BE}(U_{pACG})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\{0,1\}^{K-1}\backslash 0^{K-1}:\\
|x|=0\bmod{2^{p}}\end{subarray}}(\eta_{\max})^{2|x|}\cdot(1+\eta_{\max})^{K}

(145)

\displaystyle=(1+\eta_{\max})^{K}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[K-1]:\\
r=0\bmod{2^{p}}\end{subarray}}\binom{K-1}{r}(\eta_{\max})^{2r}.

(146)

We will now derive an upper-bound for the summation in this expression.

Claim 9
.

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[K-1]:\\
r=0\bmod{P}\end{subarray}}\binom{K-1}{r}(\eta_{\max})^{2r}\leq 2\left(\frac{e\cdot\eta_{\max}^{2}\cdot K}{P}\right)^{P}

Proof of
9
.

We begin by re-expressing the sum as

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[K-1]:\\
r=0\bmod{P}\end{subarray}}\binom{K-1}{r}(\eta_{\max})^{2r}=\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor(K-1)/P\rfloor}\binom{K-1}{jP}(\eta_{\max})^{2jP}.

(147)

Since
\eta_{\max}<1
, we note that the largest contribution in this sum comes from the
j=1
term

\displaystyle T_{1}=\binom{K-1}{P}(\eta_{\max})^{2P}\leq\left(\frac{e(K-1)}{P}\right)^{P}(\eta_{\max})^{2P}=e^{P}\cdot\left(\eta_{\max}^{2}\cdot\frac{K-1}{P}\right)^{P}.

(148)

Furthermore, note that the ratio of the
(j+1)
st to the 1st term is:

\displaystyle R_{j+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{K-1}{(j+1)P}(\eta_{\max})^{2(j+1)P}}{\binom{K-1}{P}(\eta_{\max})^{2P}}=(\eta_{\max})^{2Pj}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{jP}\left(\frac{K-P-i}{P+i}\right)\leq\left(\eta_{\max}^{2}\cdot\frac{K-1}{P}\right)^{Pj}.

(149)

From hereon, we will denote

\displaystyle r=\left(\eta_{\max}^{2}\cdot\frac{K-1}{P}\right)^{P}.

(150)

With this notation, we have that

\displaystyle T_{1}\leq r\cdot e^{P},\quad R_{j+1}\leq r^{j}.

(151)

Furthermore, since we are in the regime where
\eta_{\max}=\mathcal{O}(1/K)
, note that
r=\mathcal{O}(1/K^{P})<1
.
With this, we can decompose
Equation

147
by factoring out the dominating
T_{1}
term and upper-bounding the resultant expression via a geometric series as

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor(K-1)/P\rfloor}\binom{K-1}{jP}(\eta_{\max})^{2jP}

\displaystyle=T_{1}\left[1+\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor(K-1)/P\rfloor-1}R_{j+1}\right]\leq T_{1}\left[1+\sum_{j\geq 1}R_{j+1}\right]

(152)

\displaystyle=T_{1}\left[1+\sum_{j\geq 1}r^{j}\right]=\frac{T_{1}}{1-r}\leq e^{P}\cdot\frac{r}{1-r}

(153)

Leveraging the fact that
r=\mathcal{O}(1/K^{P})\leq 1/2
, we achieve the desired bound,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor(K-1)/P\rfloor}\binom{K-1}{jP}(\eta_{\max})^{2jP}\leq e^{P}\cdot\frac{r}{1-r}\leq e^{P}\cdot\frac{r}{1-\frac{1}{2}}\leq 2e^{P}r\leq 2\left(\frac{e\cdot\eta_{\max}^{2}\cdot K}{P}\right)^{P}.

(154)

This concludes the proof of
9
.
∎

Plugging this upper-bound into
Equation

146
, and leveraging the fact that
(1+x)^{K}\leq e^{Kx}
for

, the block encoding error upper-bound simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{BE}(U_{pACG})\leq(1+\eta_{\max})^{K}\cdot 2\left(\frac{e\cdot\eta_{\max}^{2}\cdot K}{P}\right)^{P}\leq e^{K\cdot\eta_{\max}}\cdot 2\left(\frac{e\cdot\eta_{\max}^{2}\cdot K}{P}\right)^{P}.

(155)

Plugging in
\eta_{\max}=c/K
, we obtain the desired error bound,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{BE}(U_{pACG})\leq 2e^{c}\cdot\left(\frac{e\cdot c^{2}}{K\cdot P}\right)^{P}=2e^{c}\cdot\left(\frac{e\cdot c^{2}}{K\cdot 2^{p}}\right)^{2^{p}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{K^{2^{p}}}\right).

(156)

This concludes the proof of
Theorem

13
.
∎

B.4
Oblivious Amplitude Amplification for Multiplication of Block Encodings

As the number of measurements
K
in a multiplication of block encoding sequence increases, the probability of the all-good-measurement sequence
\{0^{a}\}^{K}
decreases. Thus, we would like a way to boost the probability of measuring this sequence.

To this end, we will briefly describe the standard quantum algorithms subroutines of amplitude amplification (AA) [
Section

B.4.1
] and oblivious amplitude amplification (OAA) [
Section

B.4.2
]. We will then show how OAA can be tailored to MBE, so as to boost the probability of measuring the all-good-measurement sequence. In particular, we will show that OAA can boost the probability to
\Omega(1)
for EMBE [
Section

B.4.3
] and
\Omega(1-\varepsilon^{2})
for
\varepsilon
-AMBE [
Section

B.4.4
].

B.4.1
Amplitude Amplification (AA)

Amplitude amplification (AA) was originally proposed by
[
BHMT00
]
and has become a standard sub-routine in quantum algorithms.
Suppose,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_0}=\alpha_{\text{good}}\ket{\psi_\text{good}}+\alpha_{\text{bad}}\ket{\psi_\text{bad}},

(159)

where
|\alpha_{\text{good}}|^{2}<<1
is the probability of being in the desired “good” state
\ket{\psi_\text{good}}
and
|\alpha_{\text{bad}}|^{2}=1-|\alpha_{\text{good}}|^{2}
is the probability of being in any orthogonal “bad” state
\ket{\psi_\text{bad}}
. AA enables us to map
\ket{\psi_0}
to a state with large overlap with
\ket{\psi_\text{good}}
or, in other words, AA amplifies the amplitude of the desired
\ket{\psi_\text{good}}
state.

In terms of implementation, AA requires access to two reflection oracles. The first is a reflection about the
\ket{\psi_\text{good}}
state,

\displaystyle R_{\text{good}}=I_{n}-2\outerproduct{\psi_\text{good}}{\psi_\text{good}},

(160)

and the second is a reflection with respect to the initial
\ket{\psi_0}
state,

\displaystyle R_{\psi_{0}}=2\outerproduct{\psi_0}{\psi_0}-I_{n}.

(161)

Following a similar procedure to Grover’s algorithm, these are used to implement the Grover iterate

\displaystyle G=R_{\psi_{0}}R_{\text{good}}.

(162)

Letting
k=\mathcal{O}\left(1/|\alpha_{\text{good}}|\right)
,
G^{k}\ket{\psi_0}
maps
\ket{\psi_0}
to a state with
\Omega(1)
overlap with
\ket{\psi_\text{good}}
.

B.4.2
Oblivious Amplitude Amplification (OAA)

The main downside of AA is that knowledge of the “good” state
\ket{\psi_\text{good}}
is required in order to implement the reflection oracle
R_{\text{good}}
. However, if
\ket{\psi_\text{good}}
is unknown or inefficient to implement, this requirement can be avoided with block encodings and so-called “signal” qubits. The form of AA we will now describe, which is “oblivious” to the precise form of the good state, is known as oblivious amplitude amplification (OAA) and was originally introduced by
[
LC19
]
.

In addition to
n
qubits encoding the good and bad states, the initial state will also include
m
signal ancilla qubits, distinguishing the good and bad subspaces. Specifically,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi^m_0}=U_{\psi^{m}_{0}}\ket{0^m}\ket{0^n}=\alpha_{\text{good}}\ket{0^m}\ket{\psi_\text{good}}+\alpha_{\text{bad}}\ket{\perp}\ket{\psi_\text{bad}}.

(163)

The signal qubits have value
\ket{0^m}
only if the main register is in the good state,
\ket{\psi_\text{good}}
. When the main register is in a bad state, the signal qubits are in a state orthogonal to
\ket{0^m}
, denoted
\ket{\perp}
. Thus, for
\Pi=\outerproduct{0^m}{0^m}
,

\displaystyle(\Pi\otimes I_{n})\ket{0^m}\ket{\psi_\text{good}}

\displaystyle=\ket{0^m}\ket{\psi_\text{good}}\quad\text{and}\quad(\Pi\otimes I_{n})\ket{\perp}\ket{\psi_\text{bad}}=0,

(164)

meaning that the good subspace can be easily identified by measuring
\ket{\psi^m_0}
’s signal ancillae and post-selecting the
0^{m}
outcome. In expectation,
1/|\alpha_{\text{good}}|^{2}
measurements of
\ket{\psi^m_0}
would be required to obtain
\ket{\psi_\text{good}}
. Once again, we can improve this quadratically by leveraging AA.

Since the good and bad states have signal ancillae, the AA reflection oracles can be simplified. Namely, reflection about the good state reduces to reflection only about the signal ancillae,

\displaystyle R_{\text{good}}=(I_{m}-2\outerproduct{0^m}{0^m})\otimes I_{n}.

(165)

Meanwhile, the reflection about the initial state
\ket{\psi^m_0}
is about both the main register and ancillae,

\displaystyle R_{\psi_{0}^{m}}=2\outerproduct{\psi^{m}_0}{\psi^{m}_0}-I_{m+n}.

(166)

Thus, the Grover iterate is defined as
G=R_{\psi_{0}^{m}}R_{\text{good}}
. For
k=\mathcal{O}\left(1/|\alpha_{\text{good}}|\right)
,
G^{k}\ket{\psi^m_0}
produces a state with
\Omega(1)
overlap with
\ket{\psi_\text{good}}
.

B.4.3
OAA for Exact Multiplication of Block Encodings

In an MBE circuit, each unitary
U_{A_{i}}
has probability
p_{i}
of the good
0^{a}
ancilla measurement outcome and, thus, probability
1-p_{i}
of a bad
\perp
outcome. Thus, the desired all-good-measurements sequence
\{0^{a}\}^{K}
has probability

\displaystyle p=\prod_{i=1}^{K}p_{i}

(167)

which can be quite small depending on the number of measurements
K
and individual measurement probabilities
p_{i}
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{O}\left(1/p\right)
repetitions of the MBE circuit would be required in expectation. However, via OAA, the success probability can be coherently amplified to a constant value with quadractic queries to an EMBE circuit.

In this case, the OAA signal ancillae are the
m
measurement ancillae and
a
block encoding ancillae of the EMBE circuit, which together indicate whether the multiplication sequence was successful or not. Specifically, in the successful case, the main
n
-qubit register is in the desired state
\ket{\psi_\text{good}}=A_{[K]}\ket{\psi}
and the
(m+a)
signal ancillae in the state
\ket{0^{m+a}}
. In the unsuccessful case, the
(m+a)
signal ancillae are in a state
\ket{\perp}
, orthogonal to
\ket{0^a}
, and the main
n
-qubit register is in a junk state
\ket{\psi_\text{bad}}
.

Formally, denoting the unitary implemented by the EMBE circuit as
U_{\textsf{EMBE}}
,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_\embe}=U_{\textsf{EMBE}}\ket{0^{m+a}}\ket{\psi_0}=\alpha_{\text{good}}\ket{0^{m+a}}\ket{\psi_\text{good}}+\alpha_{\text{bad}}\ket{\perp}\ket{\psi_\text{bad}},

(168)

where
|\alpha_{\text{good}}|^{2}=p
as defined in
Equation

167
.
This state is analogous to the initial state
\ket{\psi_0^m}
in OAA (
Equation

163
). Thus, to run OAA we construct the two reflection oracles:

\displaystyle R_{\text{good}}

\displaystyle=(I_{m+a}-2\outerproduct{0^{m+a}}{0^{m+a}})\otimes I_{n}

(169)

\displaystyle R_{\psi_{\textsf{EMBE}}}

\displaystyle=2\outerproduct{\psi_\embe}{\psi_\embe}-I_{m+a+n}=U_{\psi_{\textsf{EMBE}}}(2\outerproduct{0^{m+a},\psi_0}{0^{m+a},\psi_0}-I_{m+a+n})U_{\psi_{\textsf{EMBE}}}^{\dagger},

(170)

and the Grover iterate
G=R_{\psi_{\textsf{EMBE}}}R_{\text{good}}
. Letting
k=\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{p})
, then
G^{k}\ket{\psi_\embe}
produces a state with
\Omega(1)
overlap with
\ket{0^{m+a}}\ket{\psi_\text{good}}
, as desired.

B.4.4
OAA for Approximate Multiplication of Block Encodings

To conclude, we will demonstrate how OAA can be used to coherently boost the probability of the all-good-measurement sequence, even in the setting of
approximate
MBE. The OAA implementation for AMBE does not differ too much from that of EMBE, but the analysis is a bit more involved. In particular, we prove the following lemma.

Lemma 15

(OAA for
\varepsilon
-AMBE)
.

Via repeated queries to an
\varepsilon
-approximate multiplication of block encodings circuit, oblivious amplitude amplification can be used to boost the fidelity between the circuit output and the desired state to
\Omega(1-\varepsilon^{2})
.

Proof of
Lemma

15
.

Let
U_{\textsf{AMBE}}
denote the unitary implemented by an
\varepsilon
-AMBE circuit. The key difference from EMBE is that the
\varepsilon
-AMBE outputs a state of the form:

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_\ambe}=U_{\textsf{AMBE}}\ket{0^{m+a}}\ket{\psi_0}=\alpha_{\text{good}}\ket{0^{m+a}}\ket{\psi_\text{good}}+\alpha_{\text{wrong}}\ket{0^{m+a}}\ket{\psi_\text{wrong}}+\alpha_{\text{bad}}\ket{\perp}\ket{\psi_\text{bad}}.

(171)

As for EMBE,
\ket{\psi_\text{good}}=A_{[K]}\ket{\psi}
is the desired good state, with signal ancillae
\ket{0^{m+a}}
, and
\ket{\psi_\text{bad}}
is a junk state with signal ancillae
\ket{\perp}
(orthogonal to
\ket{0^{m+a}}
). However, for
\varepsilon
-AMBE there is an additional junk state
\ket{\psi_\text{wrong}}
, which has the same signal ancillae
\ket{0^{m+a}}
as the good state
\ket{\psi_\text{good}}
. For ease of notation, we will denote the
n
-qubit sub-state with signal ancilla
\ket{0^{m+a}}
as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_\text{sig}}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{\text{sig}}}\cdot\left(\alpha_{\text{good}}\ket{\psi_\text{good}}+\alpha_{\text{wrong}}\ket{\psi_\text{wrong}}\right),

(172)

where
\alpha_{\text{sig}}=\sqrt{|\alpha_{\text{good}}|^{2}+|\alpha_{\text{wrong}}|^{2}}
is a normalization factor. Note that
\ket{\psi_\text{sig}}
is the state obtained by post-selecting
\ket{\psi_\ambe}
for the ancillae outcome
0^{m+a}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_\text{sig}}=(\bra{0^{m+a}}\otimes I_{n})U_{\textsf{AMBE}}\ket{0^{m+a}}\ket{\psi_0}=(\bra{0^{m+a}}\otimes I_{n})\ket{\psi_\text{sig}}.

(173)

Thus,
Equation

171
simplifies to

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_\ambe}=\alpha_{\text{sig}}\ket{0^{m+a}}\ket{\psi_\text{sig}}+\alpha_{\text{bad}}\ket{\perp}\ket{\psi_\text{bad}}.

(174)

In this form, it is clear that OAA can be used to boost the amplitude of the
\ket{\psi_\text{sig}}
state. Specifically, OAA will be run with the Grover iterate
G=R_{\psi_{\textsf{AMBE}}}R_{\text{sig}}
, where:

\displaystyle R_{\text{sig}}

\displaystyle=(I_{m+a}-2\outerproduct{0^{m+a}}{0^{m+a}})\otimes I_{n}

(175)

\displaystyle R_{\psi_{\textsf{AMBE}}}

\displaystyle=2\outerproduct{\psi_\ambe}{\psi_\ambe}-I_{m+a+n}=U_{\psi_{\textsf{AMBE}}}(2\outerproduct{0^{m+a},\psi_0}{0^{m+a},\psi_0}-I_{m+a+n})U_{\psi_{\textsf{AMBE}}}^{\dagger}.

(176)

Letting
k=\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{|\alpha_{\text{sig}}|^{2}})
,
G^{k}\ket{\psi_\ambe}
produces a state with
\Omega(1)
overlap with
\ket{0^{m+a}}\ket{\psi_\text{sig}}
. Furthermore, by
7
,
\ket{\psi_\text{sig}}
has fidelity at least
1-\varepsilon^{2}
with
\ket{\psi_\text{good}}
. Thus, the output state of this OAA procedure has
\Omega(1-\varepsilon^{2})
overlap with the desired exact state
\ket{\psi_\text{good}}
.
∎
