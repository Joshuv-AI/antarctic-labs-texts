Dual mechanisms of Microbacterium algeriense C14 in alleviating cadmium toxicity during Zinnia elegans seed germination and early seedling growth
Authors: Hansheng Liu, Shengxu Wang, Jie Wang, Xingyu Ma, Mingtang Li, Chunli Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cadmium (Cd) contamination, a critical type of heavy metal pollution, exerts severe adverse effects on plant growth and poses substantial risks to food safety. Although plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) have demonstrated great potential in mitigating metal-induced stress in plants, a critical limitation remains: most PGPR strains only provide protective effects to plants, failing to reduce the bioavailability of toxic metals in the environment. We herein report the isolation and characterization of Microbacterium algeriense strain C14, which exhibits dual functionality: indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production to promote growth and Cd carboxylate precipitation to immobilize metals. Strain C14 demonstrated exceptional Cd tolerance (up to 120 mg·L−1) and maintained IAA production even under severe Cd stress (120 mg·L−1), with an IAA content of 5.87 ± 0.15 mg·L−1. Using Zinnia elegans as a model ornamental plant, we systematically evaluated strain C14’s protective effects during seed germination and early seedling development under gradient Cd concentrations (0-120 mg·L−1). Strain C14 inoculation significantly enhanced germination parameters, promoted seedling growth, elevated antioxidant enzyme activities, and reduced tissue Cd accumulation by 35.5–50.7% across Cd concentration gradients. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses confirmed that strain C14 secretes carboxyl-containing metabolites that coordinate with Cd2+ to form stable amorphous cadmium carboxylate precipitates, effectively reducing metal bioavailability. This study represents the first report of a Microbacterium species combining Cd biomineralization capability with growth-promoting traits during critical early plant developmental stages, providing new insights for developing integrated phytoremediation strategies for ornamental plants in contaminated urban environments.

Introduction

Soil heavy metal pollution has become a critical global environmental challenge, primarily driven by accelerated urbanization and rapid industrial development1,2. Among various heavy metal pollutants, cadmium (Cd) poses an especially severe threat due to its high toxicity, strong mobility, and resistance to degradation3–5. Globally, Cd-contaminated agricultural soils affect millions of hectares, with the situation being particularly severe in rapidly industrializing regions6,7. Seed germination and early seedling development are the most sensitive stages to environmental stress in the plant life cycle. Cd stress during these critical stages severely inhibits seed germination, damages cell membrane structures, and ultimately leads to impaired seedling growth or death8–10. Most current mitigation measures only focus on reducing Cd uptake by plants, but fail to fundamentally decrease the bioavailability of Cd in soil, making long-term remediation difficult to achieve11,12. Therefore, the development of innovative strategies that can both protect plants from Cd toxicity and effectively reduce Cd bioavailability in soil has become an urgent need.

Current remediation methods face significant limitations. Traditional physicochemical technologies are costly and damage soil structure13–15, while phytoremediation is restricted by the slow growth and limited biomass of hyperaccumulator plant16–19. Although plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) have shown potential in alleviating heavy metal stress in plants through mechanisms such as producing 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC) deaminase, secreting plant hormones, and chelating heavy metal20–22, their effects mainly focus on improving the stress resistance of plants themselves to reduce the direct toxicity of Cd to plants. Their contribution to reducing the bioavailability of Cd in soil is relatively limited, and they cannot fundamentally solve the core problem that existing technologies are difficult to achieve long-term remediation. In recent years, the microbially induced biomineralization technology has shown unique advantages23–25. Microbially induced biomineralization is a novel remediation technology that differs from traditional physical-chemical methods and plant hyperaccumulation. Compared to other Cd immobilization strategies, biomineralization achieves long-term Cd stabilization without secondary pollution, making it particularly suitable for sustainable remediation of contaminated soils. However, current research on this technology mainly focuses on mature plant stages or pure microbial systems, with limited attention to its role in seed germination and early seedling growth. This technology can convert soluble heavy metals in soil into stable and insoluble forms, providing a new direction for long-term remediation. However, there are still obvious gaps in current research: studies on the role of PGPR with Cd biomineralization capacity in seed germination and early seedling stages are relatively scarce, and there is a particular lack of systematic evaluation of strains with dual functions of growth promotion and precipitation. This greatly limits the application and advancement of this technology in practical remediation scenarios.

To address the aforementioned research gap regarding strains with dual functions of growth promotion and cadmium mineralization, the Microbacterium algeriense strain C14, isolated from cadmium-contaminated soil, exhibits core characteristics that perfectly match the required needs. It not only possesses excellent cadmium resistance (tolerating up to 60 mg·L−1 Cd2+, exceeding the typical Cd tolerance range of 20–40 mg·L−1 for most PGPR) but also significantly secretes indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and immobilizes cadmium through cadmium carboxylate biomineralization, fully meeting the dual functional requirements of “growth promotion + mineralization”. As a widely cultivated annual ornamental plant, Zinnia elegans (common zinnia) itself has a certain tolerance to cadmium-contaminated soil26–28. This makes it an ideal candidate plant to pair with the strain C14 strain for constructing an integrated remediation model of “microbial immobilization - plant uptake - landscape remediation,” providing a practically feasible new approach for efficient and long-term remediation of cadmium-contaminated soil.

Based on the combined remediation potential of the strain C14 strain and Zinnia elegans, this study further focused on their synergistic mechanism, systematically investigating the effects of M. algeriense C14 on the seed germination and seedling growth of Zinnia elegans under cadmium stress, as well as its dual regulatory pathways. A liquid culture system was employed in this study. Under gradient cadmium concentrations (0-120 mg·L−1), the effects of strain C14 inoculation on the germination parameters, seedling growth indices, physiological responses, cadmium uptake patterns, and cadmium speciation changes of Zinnia elegans were systematically evaluated. X-ray diffraction (XRD) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) techniques were used to clarify the specific pathways through which the strain C14 strain synergistically alleviates cadmium toxicity via IAA-induced growth promotion and cadmium biomineralization immobilization. This study is among the first to report the protective effect of M. algeriense with cadmium biomineralization capacity during the early growth stage of plants. It provides new insights into the interaction between PGPR and plants in response to heavy metal stress, and lays a scientific foundation for the development of plant-microbe combined remediation technology for urban ornamental plants.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain isolation and characterization

Rhizosphere soil samples were collected from the rhizosphere of healthy dominant Asteraceae plants at 6 representative sampling sites in Hongqiling Town (Jilin Province, China). For strain isolation, the collected soil samples were first shaken in sterile water for 2 h, and the upper suspension was then inoculated into Luria-Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with 400 mg·L−1 Ni2+ and 60 mg·L−1 Cd2+. The inoculated medium was incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 160 rpm for 3 days. After incubation, the culture was spread on LB solid medium for single colony isolation and purification. A total of 121 rhizosphere microbial strains were obtained, and all strains were preserved in the aforementioned LB medium (containing 400 mg·L−1 Ni2+ and 60 mg·L−1 Cd2+) to maintain selective pressure during preservation.

The plant growth-promoting traits of the isolated strains were evaluated using established standard methods, as follows: nitrogen-fixing capacity was determined via the Ashby solid medium method29; phosphorus-solubilizing capacity was assessed using the PKO medium method30; potassium-solubilizing capacity was detected through the potassium-solubilizing medium method31; IAA production was quantified by the Salkowski colorimetric method32; siderophore production was identified using the chrome azurol S (CAS) assay33; and ACC deaminase activity was measured via the 2-chlorobenzylhydrazine method34. For strain identification, the 16 S rRNA gene sequencing approach was adopted: genomic DNA was extracted via the CTAB method, the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using universal primers 27 F/1492R, PCR products were purified and sequenced by Allwegene Tech. Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), and sequences were aligned with the NCBI GenBank database via BLAST. Phylogenetic analysis was performed using MEGA 11.0 software with the neighbor-joining method (1000 bootstrap replicates).

Functional characterization of strain C14

Bacterial growth was monitored spectrophotometrically at an optical density of 600 nm (OD600) using a microplate reader (Thermo Multiskan FC, Waltham, MA, USA), the OD600 value of the control group (without Cd stress) was 0.8. For targeted validation of IAA production under Cd stress, IAA was quantified via the Salkowski colorimetric method with L-tryptophan as the sole precursor. The Cd2+ removal efficiency was determined by atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS; Shimadzu AA-7000, Kyoto, Japan), which featured a detection limit of 0.01 mg L− 1 and a recovery rate ranging from 95% to 105%. For precipitate characterization, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, Waltham, MA, USA) and X-ray diffraction (XRD; Rigaku MiniFlex 600, Tokyo, Japan) were employed. The XRD analysis was conducted with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) under standard operating conditions.

The fractionation experiment for metabolite-mediated Cd immobilization was conducted as follows: Strain C14 was cultured to the mid-logarithmic phase, and the fermentation broth was separated into three fractions (cell-free supernatant, washed cells, and whole broth) via centrifugation (8000 rpm, 10 min) and filtration (0.22 μm membrane). Each fraction was incubated with a 500 mg·L−1 Cd2+ solution at 30 °C with shaking (160 rpm) for 24 h. After incubation, the mixtures were centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 15 min), and the Cd2+ concentration in the supernatant was determined by AAS to calculate the Cd2+ removal efficiency of each fraction.

Plant materials and experimental design

Zinnia elegans Jacq. was used as the test plant (1000-seed weight: 9.5473 g). All experiments were conducted in the Laboratory of the College of Resources and Environment, Jilin Agricultural University (Changchun, China; latitude: 43°49’N, longitude: 125°24’E) during the period from December 2021 to June 2022.

Based on the Chinese national soil environmental quality standards (GB 36600 − 2018 and GB 15618 − 2018) as well as the results of preliminary experiments, six Cd2+ concentration treatments were set up, including 0 mg·L⁻¹ (control group, CK), 0.3 mg·L⁻¹ (C1), 3 mg·L⁻¹ (C2), 30 mg·L⁻¹ (C3), 60 mg·L⁻¹ (C4), and 120 mg·L⁻¹ (C5). For each Cd2+ concentration treatment, two subgroups were further designed: one inoculated with strain C14 (+ ) and the other without strain C14 (− ), resulting in a total of 12 experimental treatments. The Cd2+ stock solutions were prepared using analytical grade CdCl2·2.5H2O. strain C14 was cultured to the mid-logarithmic growth phase, and the bacterial suspension was adjusted to an OD600 of 0.8 for subsequent inoculation. For each treatment, 30 plump and uniform Zinnia elegans Jacq. seeds were selected for surface sterilization: first immersed in 75% ethanol for 1 min, then treated with 2% sodium hypochlorite for 10 min, followed by five rinses with sterile distilled water to remove residual disinfectants.

The sterilized seeds were evenly placed in 90 mm sterile plastic Petri dishes (PC Petri dishes) lined with double-layer sterile filter paper. Each Petri dish was supplemented with 10 mL of sterilized Cd2+ solution (with concentrations corresponding to the designed treatment gradients) and 0.5 mL of either strain C14 suspension (OD600 = 0.8) or sterile water (for the uninoculated control group).

All treatments were initially incubated in darkness for 24 h under controlled conditions (temperature: (20 ± 1) °C, relative humidity: 70 ± 5%). After the dark treatment, the Petri dishes were transferred to an artificial climate chamber simulating an optimal natural growth environment, with a 16 h photoperiod (photosynthetic photon flux density: 200 µmol·m−2·s−1), constant temperature of (20 ± 1) °C, and relative humidity maintained at 50–90%, for a total culture duration of 7 days. Each treatment was set with three biological replicates to ensure the reliability of experimental results.

At the end of the seedling culture period, the seedlings of each treatment were collected for morphological determination. Seedling samples were scanned using an EPSON Expression 11000XL scanner (Seiko Epson Corporation, Japan) with a resolution of 600 dpi. The scanned images were imported into WinRHIZO 2016 root analysis software (Regent Instruments Inc., Quebec, Canada) for quantitative analysis of growth indicators, including shoot length, root length, root surface area, root projected area, and root volume.

Physiological and biochemical analyses

The soluble protein content was determined via the Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 colorimetric method, with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard reference35. Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was measured using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method, with absorbance recorded at 532 nm and 600 nm (the latter used for background correction)36. Peroxidase (POD) activity was assayed by the guaiacol oxidation method, monitoring the absorbance change at 470 nm37. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined through the nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) photoreduction method, with absorbance measured at 560 nm38. All spectrophotometric measurements were performed on a microplate reader (Thermo Multiskan FC, Waltham, MA, USA) under standardized experimental conditions.

Cadmium accumulation analysis

Seedlings (with seed coats removed) were rinsed three times with deionized water, blotted to dryness with filter paper. 10 g of the samples (accurately weighed using an analytical balance) were transferred to polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) digestion tubes, and 5 mL of concentrated nitric acid (HNO3, 65%, v/v) was added. The mixtures were allowed to stand overnight at room temperature for pre-digestion. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of perchloric acid (HClO4, 70%, v/v) was added, and the tubes were heated at 180 °C for 45 min, followed by heating at 220 °C until white fumes ceased to evolve. After cooling to room temperature, 2 mL of 30% (v/v) hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was added, and the heating was resumed until the digestion solutions turned clear and colorless. The digested solutions were cooled thoroughly and then diluted to a final volume of 25 mL with ultrapure water. The Cd content in the diluted solutions was determined by atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS). Quality control was performed using a certified reference material (CRM; GBW10015, spinach), with a recovery rate of 98 ± 3%.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) based on three biological replicates. Before statistical analysis, the Shapiro-Wilk test was used to verify the normality of the data, and Levene’s test was applied to assess the homogeneity of variance. Statistical significance of differences between treatments was analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s new multiple range test for post-hoc comparison, using SPSS 20.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Phylogenetic analysis of the strains was conducted using MEGA 11.0 software, with the neighbor-joining method employed for tree construction. For the correlation analysis of indicators under cadmium, Spearman correlation analysis was performed using the psych package in R software (version 4.4.0). The results of the correlation analysis were visualized as heatmaps using the corrplot package, and the angular order of eigenvectors (AOE) sorting method was adopted to optimize the heatmap layout.

Results

Isolation and characterization of cadmium-mineralizing strain C14

From 121 rhizospheric microorganisms isolated from the rhizospheric soils of 18 Asteraceae plant species at 6 sampling sites in Hongqiling Town, seven high Cd-resistant strains (B6, C14, E7, G4, L3, O6, P7) were screened. Among these, only strain C14 simultaneously exhibited Cd biomineralization capacity and IAA-producing ability, which was significantly superior to other strains with a single function (Table 2). As shown in Fig. 1 is the functional verification diagram of the colony morphology, Cd precipitation product, and IAA assay of strain C14. Furthermore, 16 S rRNA gene sequence analysis revealed that strain C14 shared a sequence similarity of nearly 100% with Microbacterium algeriense (GenBank accession number: NR 180420), featuring a clear taxonomic status and species representativeness for subsequent studies. The phylogenetic tree of the Microbacterium algeriense C14 strain based on 16S rRNA gene sequence is shown in Fig. 2. Consequently, strain C14 was selected as the target strain for further investigations.

Cadmium resistance and IAA production under metal stress

As shown in Fig. 3, strain C14 exhibited exceptional Cd tolerance. it maintained robust growth when exposed to Cd2+ concentrations up to 120 mg·L⁻¹. Compared with the control group (without Cd2+ stress), the maximum OD600 of strain C14 was reduced by 15% at 60 mg·L⁻¹ Cd²⁺ and by 35% at 120 mg·L⁻¹ Cd2+, respectively. Significant growth inhibition was only observed when the Cd2+ concentration reached 180 mg·L⁻¹ (P < 0.01). Fig. 3Effects of different concentrations of Cd2+ on the growth of strain C14. To further quantify the survival status of strain C14 under short-term Cd stress, we determined the mortality rate of strain C14 at 6 h and 12 h after exposure to different Cd2+ concentrations (Fig. 4). At Cd2+ concentrations ≤ 60 mg·L⁻¹, the mortality rate of strain C14 remained below 5% at both 6 h and 12 h, indicating that the strain could adapt well to mild to moderate Cd stress in the short term. When the Cd2+ concentration increased to 120 mg·L-1, the mortality rate at 6 h was 12.3 ± 1.5%, and it increased to 18.7 ± 2.1% at 12 h—though this level of mortality still allowed the strain to maintain a viable population for growth and function. Only when the Cd2+ concentration reached 180 mg·L-1 did the mortality rate rise sharply: it reached 45.6 ± 3.2% at 6 h and further increased to 68.9 ± 4.5% at 12 h, which was consistent with the significant growth inhibition observed in the OD600 assay. This short-term mortality data further confirms the strong Cd tolerance of strain C14, especially its ability to survive and persist under high Cd concentrations (up to 120 mg·L-1 that are lethal to most PGPR strains.

Metabolite-mediated cadmium immobilization

As shown in Fig. 6, fractionation experiments demonstrated that cell-free supernatant removed 76.67 ± 0.5% of Cd2+ from solution (initial concentration: 500 mg·L−1), while washed cells removed only 20.7 ± 1.2%. Whole broth achieved 93.3 ± 1.5% removal, indicating that secreted metabolites (particularly carboxyl-containing organic acids) are the primary agents mediating cadmium immobilization rather than cell surface biosorption.

As shown in Fig. 7, XPS analysis of Cd-containing precipitates revealed distinct characteristic peaks: C1s exhibited three deconvoluted peaks (C-C/C-H at 284.80 eV, C-O at 286.0 eV, O-C = O carboxyl groups at 288.0 eV); O1s showed two peaks (C = O at 531.0 eV, C-O at 532.0 eV); N1s displayed a single symmetric peak (amino nitrogen at 399.8 eV); and Cd3d exhibited a typical doublet (Cd3d5/2 at 405.0 eV, Cd3d3/2 at 412.0 eV) with a spin-orbit splitting energy of 7.0 eV (a signature of Cd-oxygen coordination). XRD analysis showed two broad diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 20.738° and 30.262°, confirming the amorphous nature of the precipitates (ruling out crystalline Cd compounds such as CdCO3, Cd(OH)2, CdS). Combined XPS and XRD results confirmed that Cd2+ coordinates with carboxyl groups (-COOH) from bacterial metabolic products to form stable amorphous cadmium carboxylate precipitates (general formula: R-C2H3O2NCd, where R denotes the amino acid residue).

Effects of strain C14 inoculation on seed germination under cadmium stress

Cd stress inhibited the seed germination of Zinnia elegans in a dose-dependent manner. At 120 mg·L⁻¹ Cd2+ (treatment C5), the germination energy, germination rate, germination index (GI), and vigor index (VI) were reduced by 21.6%, 29.5%, 7.3%, and 62.91%, respectively, compared with the control group (P < 0.05).

As shown in Table 3, Inoculation with strain C14 significantly alleviated Cd-induced toxicity across all Cd concentration gradients. At 0.3 mg·L⁻¹ Cd2+ (treatment C1J), the inoculated seeds exhibited increases of 6.9%, 4.2%, and 49.2% in germination energy, germination rate, and VI, respectively, relative to the inoculated control group (CKJ) (P < 0.05). Even under severe Cd stress (120 mg·L⁻¹, treatment C5J), strain C14 inoculation still enhanced the germination energy, germination rate, GI, and VI by 20.0%, 20.9%, 39.5%, and 56.1%, respectively, compared with the uninoculated counterpart (treatment C5) (P < 0.01).

Effects of strain C14 inoculation on seedling growth

The results of the effects of Microbacterium algeriense C14 on the growth indexes of Zinnia elegans Jacq. are shown in Table 4. Cd stress reduced all seedling growth-related parameters in a dose-dependent manner. At the highest Cd concentration of 120 mg·L⁻¹ (treatment C5), the shoot length, root length, root surface area, root projected area, and root volume were significantly decreased by 52.5%, 71.9%, 53.5%, 64.8%, and 63.6%, respectively, compared with the non-Cd-stressed control group (P < 0.01).

Inoculation with strain C14 significantly enhanced seedling growth under Cd stress. At the lowest Cd concentration of 0.3 mg·L⁻¹ (treatment C1J), the shoot length, root length, root surface area, root projected area, and root volume of inoculated seedlings increased by 14.9%, 44.9%, 10.3%, 40.3%, and 50.0%, respectively, relative to the inoculated control group (CKJ, without Cd addition) (P < 0.05). Notably, all growth parameters in strain C14-inoculated groups remained significantly higher than those in corresponding uninoculated groups across all Cd concentration gradients, with the most pronounced growth-promoting effects observed at low to moderate Cd levels (0.3 – 20 mg·L⁻¹). Even under severe Cd stress (120 mg·L⁻¹, treatment C5J), strain C14 inoculation still remarkably increased the root length by 21.8%, shoot length by 7.1%, root surface area by 8.1%, root projected area by 8.5%, and root volume by 25.0%, compared with the uninoculated counterpart (treatment C5) (P < 0.01). These results indicate that strain strain C14 exerts a robust growth-promoting effect on seedlings under Cd stress, with a more prominent protective role against mild to moderate Cd toxicity.

Discussion

PGPR have emerged as promising biofertilizers for sustainable agriculture, particularly under heavy metal stress conditions39,40. In this study, 121 Cd-tolerant rhizobacterial strains were isolated from the rhizosphere of Zinnia elegans, highlighting the rhizosphere as a critical reservoir of stress-adapted microorganisms. Notably, Microbacterium algeriense C14 exhibited exceptional Cd tolerance (up to 120 mg·L− 1), which substantially exceeds that of most previously reported PGPR strains41–43, reflecting adaptive evolution to local selective pressures in Cd-contaminated soils.

PGPR alleviate heavy metal stress through multiple mechanisms, including phytohormone synthesis, ACC deaminase activity, and siderophore production44,45. Our results confirmed that strain C14 possesses IAA-producing capacity, which remained detectable even under severe Cd stress (120 mg·L− 1), supporting its potential as a biofertilizer. A biphasic IAA production response to Cd was observed: low Cd concentrations stimulated IAA synthesis, while high concentrations inhibited it. This pattern likely reflects hormetic effects and metabolic trade-offs between stress tolerance and secondary metabolite biosynthesis.

Notably, strain C14 mediates Cd immobilization via the secretion of carboxyl-containing metabolites, which coordinate with Cd2+ ions to form stable organometallic precipitates. Complementary XPS and XRD analyses confirmed these precipitates as amorphous cadmium carboxylates. Compared to crystalline Cd compounds, amorphous cadmium carboxylates exhibit potential acid-alkali resistance and reduced remobilization risk under fluctuating environmental conditions. This biomineralization mechanism represents a significant advancement over conventional PGPR strategies that primarily focus on plant protection without addressing metal bioavailability in the rhizosphere.

Seed germination is particularly sensitive to heavy metal toxicity. Our data revealed a biphasic response of Z. elegans seeds to Cd exposure: low concentrations (0.3 mg·L− 1) slightly stimulated germination, while higher concentrations progressively inhibited germination-related parameters. This observation is consistent with hormetic effects reported in other plant species46–49, which may reflect low-dose stress-induced activation of defense mechanisms that temporarily enhance metabolic activity. Importantly, strain C14 inoculation significantly mitigated Cd toxicity across all concentration gradients, improving germination potential, germination rate, germination index, vigor index, and early seedling growth. These findings align with previous reports in other plant-PGPR symbiotic systems50–52.

Physiological analyses demonstrated that strain C14 inoculation modulated antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, POD) and increased lipid peroxidation (as indicated by MDA content) under Cd stress53,54. The enhanced antioxidant capacity likely results from multiple synergistic mechanisms: (1) IAA-mediated upregulation of stress-responsive genes encoding antioxidant enzymes; (2) reduced Cd uptake due to extracellular biomineralization, thereby decreasing intracellular oxidative stress; and (3) potential secretion of additional protective metabolites by strain C14. Furthermore, increased soluble protein content in inoculated seedlings may represent a protective response, as soluble proteins can chelate free Cd2+ ions and function as molecular chaperones to maintain protein homeostasis under stress conditions55,56.

Importantly, Cd accumulation in Z. elegans seedling tissues was significantly reduced in the presence of strain C14, with reductions ranging from 35.5% to 50.7% across the Cd concentration gradient. This indicates that strain C14 limits plant metal uptake through multiple complementary pathways: (1) extracellular biomineralization, which reduces Cd2+ bioavailability in the growth medium; (2) potential biosorption of Cd2+ to bacterial cell surfaces; and (3) modulation of plant metal transport systems through IAA signaling pathways57–59. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis further confirmed that enhanced antioxidant capacity and reduced Cd accumulation were strongly associated with improved seedling establishment, validating the dual protective mechanisms of strain C14 under Cd stress.

While our study provides compelling evidence for the dual functionality of M. algeriense C14, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the liquid culture system used in this study, while ideal for mechanistic investigations and controlled experiments, does not fully replicate the complexity of natural soil environments, including soil structure, native microbial community interactions, and nutrient dynamics. Future research should validate these findings in soil-based pot experiments and field trials to assess the practical applicability of strain C14 in real-world remediation scenarios. Second, the current study focused on early developmental stages (7 days post-germination); longer-term studies are needed to evaluate strain C14’s effects on plant growth, Cd accumulation patterns, and biomineralization stability throughout the entire plant life cycle. Third, the molecular mechanisms underlying IAA-mediated stress tolerance and the specific carboxyl-containing organic acids responsible for Cd biomineralization require further elucidation through integrated transcriptomic, metabolomic, and proteomic approaches60.

Despite these limitations, our findings have important implications for developing integrated phytoremediation strategies. The combination of growth promotion and metal immobilization in a single bacterial strain represents a significant advancement over conventional approaches that address only one aspect of the remediation challenge61. For ornamental plants like Z. elegans, which serve both aesthetic and potential phytoremediation functions in urban green spaces, the application of multifunctional PGPR such as strain C14 could enable the establishment of “living remediation systems” that simultaneously beautify contaminated sites and reduce metal bioavailability62,63.

Future research directions should focus on the following key areas: (1) optimization of strain C14 inoculation protocols (dosage, timing, and formulation) for different soil types and Cd contamination levels; (2) investigation of strain C14’s interactions with native soil microbiomes and potential synergistic effects with other beneficial microorganisms; (3) assessment of the long-term stability and environmental fate of Cd carboxylate precipitates under varying pH, temperature, and moisture conditions; (4) exploration of strain C14’s effectiveness in mitigating toxicity of other heavy metals (e.g., Pb, Cu, Zn) and in multi-metal contaminated scenarios; and (5) development of cost-effective mass production and delivery systems for field-scale applications.
