Quantum Magic in Discrete-Time Quantum Walk
Vikash Mittal, Yi-Ping Huang

Abstract
Quantum magic, which accounts for the non-stabilizer content of a state, is essential for universal quantum computation beyond classically simulable resources. We investigate the generation and evolution of quantum magic in discrete-time quantum walks (DTQWs) using the Stabilizer Renyi Entropy as a measure of quantum magic. We investigate single- and two-walker quantum walks on a one-dimensional lattice, considering a wide range of initial coin states. Our results reveal that DTQWs can dynamically generate significant magic, with the amount and structure strongly dependent on the initial state of the coin. In the case of a single walker, the relationship between magic and entanglement is found to be nontrivial and complementary at long times. These findings position DTQWs as accessible and controllable platforms for producing quantum magic, offering a new perspective on their role in quantum information processing and reliable quantum computation.



I
Introduction

Quantum magic is a fundamental resource that enables quantum computational advantage
Gottesman 1997
;
Gottesman 1998
;
Aaronson and Gottesman 2004
;
Bravyi and Kitaev 2005
. Unlike coherence or entanglement, which are well-understood forms of quantum correlations, magic refers to the non-stabilizerness of quantum states—that is, their deviation from the set of stabilizer states. Stabilizer states and Clifford operations can be simulated efficiently on a classical computer; hence, states possessing magic provide a true quantum advantage. In addition to a quantum advantage, magic states are necessary for universal quantum computation
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
. Quantifying and understanding the generation of magic is therefore crucial in the study of quantum resources
Gottesman 1998
.

There exist several witnesses to quantify the non-stabilizerness in the system
Veitch et al. 2014
;
Tirrito et al. 2024
. However, in this work, we choose a recently introduced Stabilizer Renyi Entropy (SRE)
Leone et al. 2022
;
Leone and Bittel 2024
, which provides a scalable measure of how much a quantum state departs from the stabilizer subspace, or basically to quantify the non-stabilizerness or magic in the system. SRE is particularly useful in dynamical settings, where it can track the growth of magic under unitary evolution.

In this study, we explore quantum walks, driven by their versatility and general applicability, along with their successful implementation in various physical systems. Quantum walks, the quantum analog of classical random walks, have emerged as a versatile platform in quantum information science
Aharonov et al. 1993
;
Kempe 2003
;
Nayak and Vishwanath 2000
. They exhibit rich interference patterns, allow for coherent control, and have been widely studied in the context of quantum algorithms
Ambainis 2003
;
Childs and Goldstone 2004
;
Shenvi et al. 2003
, quantum computation
Childs 2009
;
Childs et al. 2013
;
Lovett et al. 2010
, and entanglement generation
Vieira et al. 2013
;
Singh et al. 2023
. In both discrete-time and continuous-time frameworks, quantum walks have provided insights into the spread of quantum correlations and have been proposed for tasks ranging from search problems to quantum simulation
Venegas-Andraca 2012
;
Kadian et al. 2021
.

Despite the extensive literature on entanglement and the computational applications of quantum walks, the role of quantum magic in these systems has yet to be explored, except for the work referenced in Ref.
Paşcu Moca et al. 2025
. In that reference, the authors provided a method for generating non-stabilizerness in the context of a multi-particle continuous-time quantum walk. However, the connection between the dynamical evolution of a quantum walker and the generation of quantum magic has not been addressed in the discrete-time quantum walk setup. In this study, we aim to bridge this gap by investigating the generation of quantum magic in discrete-time quantum walks and quantifying it using Stabilizer Renyi Entropy.

We study both single- and two-walker quantum walk systems. For the single-walker case, we numerically compute the time evolution of quantum magic for various initial coin states, and explore asymptotic behavior that captures its long-term behavior. The dynamics of entanglement and magic and their relation are topics of extensive present research. In the context of a simple setup of a discrete-time quantum walk in one dimension, we report a complementary dynamics between entanglement and magic. In the two-walker setting, we explore how quantum interference and correlations contribute to the creation of magic. We find that discrete-time quantum walks can serve not only as sources of entanglement but also as generators of quantum magic, thus extending their utility in quantum information processing.

II
Discrete-Time Quantum Walk

The discrete-time quantum walk (DTQW) models the unitary evolution of a quantum particle, a walker, on a one-dimensional lattice, governed by the internal states of the walker, which are usually taken to be two-dimensional in the case of a one-dimensional system and referred to as a coin. The total Hilbert space is given by
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{C}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{P}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{C}
is the coin space spanned by
\{\ket{\uparrow},\ket{\downarrow}\}
, and
\mathcal{H}_{P}
is the position space spanned by
\{\ket{x}\mid x\in\mathds{Z}\}
. The evolution proceeds through repeated applications of a unitary step operator composed of a coin flip followed by a conditional shift. In this work, we consider a Hadamard coin

H=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix},

(1)

which acts on
\mathcal{H}_{C}
, creating superpositions between the
\ket{\uparrow}
and
\ket{\downarrow}
states. The conditional shift operator
S
moves the walker to the left or right depending on the state of the coin:

S=\sum_{x\in\mathds{Z}}\left(\ket{\uparrow}\bra{\uparrow}\otimes\ket{x-1}\bra{x}+\ket{\downarrow}\bra{\downarrow}\otimes\ket{x+1}\bra{x}\right).

(2)

This operation entangles the coin and position degrees of freedom, introducing quantum interference into the walk dynamics. The unitary operator for one step of the walk is given by:

U=S\cdot(H\otimes\mathds{1}),

(3)

where
\mathds{1}
is the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}_{P}
. For a given initial state of the composite system,
\ket{\Psi(0)}
, the state after
t
time steps evolves as

\ket{\Psi(t)}=U^{t}\ket{\Psi(0)}.

(4)

While discrete-time quantum walks have been extensively studied for their entanglement properties, algorithmic applications, and transport behavior, their role in hosting or generating quantum magic has received comparatively little attention. Quantum magic refers to the non-stabilizer content of a quantum state—that is, the degree to which it departs from the set of stabilizer states. Since stabilizer states, along with Clifford operations, can be efficiently simulated on classical computers, the presence of magic is essential for achieving true quantum advantage in quantum computational processes. Quantifying magic is therefore central to understanding the usefulness of a quantum system for providing quantum advantage and enabling universal quantum computation.

II.1
Stabilizer Renyi Entropy (SRE)

The Stabilizer Renyi Entropy (SRE) is a quantifier of quantum magic, which measures the extent to which a quantum state deviates from the set of stabilizer states
Leone et al. 2022
;
Haug and Piroli 2023
. Stabilizer states form a subset of quantum states that can be described entirely by Clifford circuits and are efficiently simulable on classical computers. States with non-zero magic, as captured by the SRE, represent a fundamental resource as they are essential as a resource that cannot be simulated by a classical machine, and in achieving universal quantum computation.

Let
\rho
be a quantum state describing
n
-qubits. The Stabilizer Renyi Entropy of order
\alpha
is defined as

\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(\rho)=\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\left(\dfrac{\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\absolutevalue{\Tr(\rho P)}^{2\alpha}}{\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\absolutevalue{\Tr(\rho P)}^{2}}\right).

(5)

Here we make use of the Stabilizer Renyi Entropy of order
2
as a witness of magic, and it reads

\mathcal{M}(\rho)=-\log\left(\dfrac{\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\absolutevalue{\Tr(\rho P)}^{4}}{\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\absolutevalue{\Tr(\rho P)}^{2}}\right).

(6)

where
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is the
n
-qubit Pauli group string operators. For a pure state
\ket{\psi}
, it reduces to

\mathcal{M}(\rho)=-\dfrac{1}{2^{n}}\log\left(\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\absolutevalue{\expval{P}{\psi}}^{4}\right)

(7)

The SRE has several desirable properties: it is invariant under Clifford operations, additive under tensor products, and non-increasing under stabilizer-preserving operations
Leone et al. 2022
. These characteristics make it particularly well-suited for analyzing magic generation in dynamical systems such as quantum walks and circuits.

In Ref.
Wang and Li 2023
;
Cuffaro and Fuchs 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025
, the upper-bound of SRE of order
\alpha
, for a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space, was provided which reads

\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(\ket{\psi})\leq\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\dfrac{1+(d-1)(d+1)^{1-\alpha}}{d}.

(8)

So, for
\alpha=2
and for single- and two-qubit systems, this bound results in
\mathcal{M}_{2}(\ket{\psi})\leq\log(3/2)\approx 0.4055
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}(\ket{\psi})\leq\log(5/2)\approx 0.9163
respectively. We should note here that these bounds can be achieved (if possible) for pure quantum states in
d
-dimensional Hilbert space.

Building upon the framework of discrete-time quantum walks and the utilization of Stabilizer Renyi Entropy as a quantifier of quantum magic, we are now ready to focus on the dynamics of magic generation within a single-walker context. We aim to analyze how a quantum walker, initialized in a general coin superposition and subjected to unitary evolution on a one-dimensional lattice, accumulates magic over time.

We assume that the walker is initially localized at the origin of the lattice, with the coin represented by an arbitrary pure state, parametrized using the Bloch sphere. The initial state of the total system is thus read

\ket{\Psi(0)}=\left(\cos(\theta/2)\ket{\uparrow}+e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)\ket{\downarrow}\right)\otimes\ket{x = 0}

(9)

where
\theta\in[0,\pi]
and
\phi\in[0,2\pi]
.

We evolve the quantum walk with the time evolution operator
U
, and the total density matrix at time
t
is written as

\rho(t)=U^{t}\rho(0)(U^{\dagger})^{t}.

(10)

At each time step, we analyze the reduced coin state
(\rho_{C}(t))
obtained by tracing out the position degree of freedom, and compute the SRE given in Eq. (
6
) to quantify the amount of magic generated in the coin subsystem.

In Fig.

\ket{x = 0}

\ket{\uparrow}
and (b) a superposed state
\ket{\Psi(0)}_{C}\propto(\ket{\uparrow}+e^{i\pi/4}\ket{\downarrow})
. The difference between the two initial states is in the initial amount of quantum magic. The state
\ket{\uparrow}
initially has zero magic, while the other choice of initial state has finite magic initially. In both cases, the SRE shows a rapid initial increase followed by saturation, suggesting that quantum magic accumulates quickly and stabilizes as the walk proceeds. The amplitude of the oscillations is also sensitive to the initial state, and the higher final SRE in the superposition case highlights the crucial role of initial coin coherence in facilitating magic generation. This indicates that superposed coin states are more effective at populating non-stabilizer subspaces over time.

IV
DTQW with two walkers

A natural extension would be to probe the quantum magic with two walkers that provide more freedom as the number of parameters in the initial state of the two coins increases. To keep the analysis simple, we consider a straightforward extension of the discrete-time quantum walk of one walker to get a quantum walk with two walkers. The structure of the composite Hilbert space is
\mathcal{\bar{H}}=\mathcal{H}_{C_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{C_{2}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{P_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{P_{2}}
, and correspondingly, the time evolution operator for a unit time step reads

\bar{U}=(S_{2}\cdot H)(S_{1}\cdot H)

(11)

where
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are the shift operators for the first and second walker, respectively, and were introduced in the previous section. We use the Hadamard coin for both walkers. Note, we are skipping writing the identity explicitly to avoid the clutter.

In this case, we initialize the walkers in localized states in position, and the coins to be in a superposition, which reads

\ket{\Psi(0)}=\ket{\chi}\otimes\ket{x_1 = 0}\otimes\ket{x_2 = 0}

(12)

with

\ket{\chi}=\left(\cos(\theta/2)\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}+e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}\right)

(13)

where we again use the Bloch sphere-like parameterization for the state of the two coins with
\theta\in[0,\pi]
and
\phi\in[0,2\pi]
. We choose this parametrization for the state of the two coins because it allows us to explore two extremes, a product state (for
\theta=0
), and a maximally non-local entangled state (for
\theta=\pi/2
) for the case of pure states.

The Fig.
4
presents the time evolution of SRE for three different composite initial coin states:
\ket{\chi}=\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}
, a Bell state
\ket{\chi}=\left(\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}+\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}\right)/\sqrt{2}
, and the same Bell state after the application of a magic gate
T
to the first coin state. The magic gate for a single qubit is given by

T=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&e^{i\pi/4}\end{pmatrix}.

(14)

As expected, the application of the magic gate enhances the magic at time
t=0
, confirming its role in injecting non-stabilizer components into the state. To our surprise, here, an entangled and magic-enhanced state shows limited magic growth, while a simple product state produces significantly higher SRE, which is highly counterintuitive at this stage.

In Fig.
5
, we generalize the observations of a single walker in Fig.
2
to the two-walker case. We are plotting the SRE as a function of time and
\theta
for various fixed
\phi
values in the allowed range from Eq. (
12
). For all phases investigated, the SRE again shows a sensitivity towards
\theta
, although the patterns are more structured and peaked compared to the single-walker scenario. In this case, we do not see a region where magic experiences a total death as the walk evolves. Also, we observe a periodic behavior with
\theta
for all the values of the phase. The results indicate that magic generation in multi-walker systems is not as sensitive to the initial state of the system as in the case of a single walker. On the other hand, we see one similarity in this case, which was also present in the case of single-walker and at phase value
\phi=\pi/2
. The magic is zero after the first step of the quantum walk, irrespective of the initial amount of magic in the system.

We further compare the SRE for several initial coin states at two time points:
t=0
and
t=50
in Fig.
6
. Although all states begin with similar or zero SRE, their values diverge significantly over time. This divergence highlights the crucial role of dynamics in transforming initially classical or low-magic configurations into more complex states with a higher amount of magic. The results demonstrate that even states that are close to stabilizer form can evolve into highly magical states when subjected to quantum walk protocols. This showcases the potential of such systems in generating valuable computational resources.

Finally, we extend our analysis to include the mixed states for the choice of the initial state of the coins, for which we choose Werner states
Werner 1989
, which again allows us to probe the two extremes, maximally mixed and maximally entangled states. Werner states are a family of mixed states that is given by a convex sum of a maximally entangled state and a maximally mixed state. It reads

\rho(p)=p\outerproduct*{\Phi^+}{\Phi^+}+(1-p)\dfrac{\mathds{1}_{4}}{4},

(15)

where
\outerproduct*{\Phi^+}{\Phi^+}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{ \uparrow\uparrow}+\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}\right)
is a Bell state,
\mathds{1}/4
is the maximally mixed state on the two-qubit Hilbert space, and
p\in[0,1]
is a mixing parameter. For
p=1
, the state is pure and maximally entangled, while for
p=0
, it is fully mixed and contains no correlations, quantum or classical. In Fig.
7
, we again investigate the time evolution of the Stabilizer Renyi Entropy (SRE) for the reduced density matrix of the two coins states for different values of
p
and observe oscillating behavior as in the previous cases. Surprisingly, we observe that magic can be generated dynamically even when the initial state has no magic (
p=1
), whereas in other cases where the initial state has high magic (
p\approx 0.6
), the magic decays over time. This counterintuitive behavior highlights that the time evolution under the quantum walk is capable of redistributing and transforming quantum resources in a nontrivial way. It also suggests that initial magic is not always a reliable predictor of long-term behavior.

Note, for the sake of completeness, we also explored the initial states of the form,

\ket{\chi}=\left(\cos(\theta/2)\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}+e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}\right)

(16)

along with the initial state given in Eq. (
13
), and we observed the same dynamics, qualitatively.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we demonstrated one more application of discrete-time quantum walks, which serves as a platform for generating quantum magic from a wide range of initial coin states. Using Stabilizer Renyi Entropy as a quantitative measure, we analyzed the behavior of magic under time evolution for both single and two-walker systems. We showed that magic builds up rapidly and often saturates, with its long-term behavior strongly influenced by the initial configuration of the coin. We also found that the generation of magic can occur independently of entanglement growth, emphasizing its distinct role as a quantum resource. In two-walker settings, the interplay between interference and magic becomes richer, revealing parameter regimes with structured and persistent magic. These results highlight the potential of quantum walks not only for algorithmic and transport tasks but also as practical generators of quantum resources necessary for universal computation. In this work, we explored many, but still a handful of, initial states in a quantum walk with two walkers. One of the future directions will be to reflect on a broader class of two-qubit states and analyze the dynamical nature of magic in that setting. The other possible future direction is to understand the dynamics of entanglement and magic and to see how far quantum walk can take us to understand this fundamental question.
