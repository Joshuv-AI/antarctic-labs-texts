Alleviating effects of Gracilaria verrucosa supplement on non-specific immunity, antioxidant capacity and immune-related genes of pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) provoked with white spot syndrome virus
Authors: Gehad E Elshopakey, Abdelwahab A Abdelwarith, Elsayed M Younis, Simon J Davies, Samia Elbahnaswy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur work evaluated the possible underlying roles of dietary dried seaweed (Gracilaria verrucosa; GV) on the inherent immune response, antioxidant capacity, immune-related gene expression, and protection of whiteleg shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) contra white spot syndrome virus (WSSV). Three hundred and sixty healthy L. vannamei (15.26 g ± 1.29 g) were graded into four supplemental groups ( Triplicate/group) and fed with diets including 0 (control), 2, 4, and 8 g GV (kg diet) −1 for 21 days. Following the feeding period, each group of shrimp received an intramuscular WSSV injection (1.4 × 106 copies/ml). Hemolymph and gills samples were collected before and after the challenge with WSSV. Notably, the administration of dietary GV significantly enhanced the innate immune parameters of pacific white shrimp including total hemocyte count (THC), phagocytosis, phenoloxidase activity, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and lysozyme activity before and after challenge with WSSV. Additionally, dietary supplementation of 4, and 8 g of GV (kg diet)−1 remarkably elevated ACP, AKP, SOD, GPx, and catalase activities along with a decrease in the MDA level in gills of shrimp before and post-WSSV challenge. In response to the GV supplement, significant upregulation of expression of ALF1, CRU1, PEN4, and CTL with downregulation of TRAF6, STAT, TLR1, and NOS genes was recorded in the gills tissue before and post-challenge with WSSV, especially at a dose of 8.0 GV g kg − 1. Dietary inoculated shrimp with GV revealed notably higher survival percentages after being challenged with WSSV. Conclusively, these data indicate that Gracilaria verrucosa can be recommended as a valuable supplemented seaweed to stimulate the innate immunity and enhance the health of Litopenaeus vannamei against viral infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-024-04304-4.Keywords: Gracilaria verrucosa, Immune response, Antioxidant enzymes, Immune-related genes, Litopenaeus vannamei, WSSV

Introduction

The most promising commodity in the aquaculture sector with the greatest growth potential is the Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) which is indigenous to the Pacific Ocean [1]. Because of the exponential growth of this species’ aquaculture, mangrove ecosystems around Asia are being heavily exploited [2]. This shrimp is known for its quick development, high intensification, low protein requirements, strong tolerance to a broad range of water salinities, and other characteristics that fit highly intensive aquaculture [3]. However, issues with declining pond habitats have affected shrimp aquaculture, which has led to an increase in stress-induced disease occurrences, primarily with bacterial and viral etiologies [4].

In shrimp culture, viral infections are crucial since some of them cause significant crop and financial losses [5]. White spot disease (WSD) caused by the white spot syndrome virus (WSSV), is a condition that frequently attacks Pacific white shrimp [6]. WSSV, an enveloped double-stranded DNA virus, is recognized as a Whispovirus genus in the Nimaviridae family [7]. Since its initial appearance in Taiwan in the 1990s, the WSSV has quickly expanded to places used for shrimp aquaculture worldwide [8]. Because of its extreme virulence, which can cause 100% shrimp mortality within a few days of infection and result in significant financial deprivation for the business, WSSV is still one of the most critical diseases affecting shrimp mariculture [9, 10]. When shrimp are infected with WSSV, they become very weak, lose interest in food, and typically get it to the embankment’s edge before passing away [11]. Other features of WSSV include sluggishness, detached cuticle, frequently reddish discoloration, and white patches on the carapace, appendages, and the cuticle, measuring between 0.5 and 2.0 mm in diameter [12, 13]. The addition of an immunostimulant derived from ecologically acceptable plant materials is an attempt to stop the WSSV attack in penaeid shrimp production with no health hazards to the consumer or the environment [14, 15]. Since it initially appeared in the early 1990s and spread to all of the main shrimp-producing nations, WSD has grown to be the biggest danger to shrimp production. WSD is predicted to continue causing about US$1 billion in economic losses each year, with estimates of the disease’s overall economic harm ranging from US$8 to US$15 billion [16].

Shrimp, like other invertebrates, lack adaptive immunity and have only developed an innate immune system in which circulating hemocytes, such as granular cells (GCs), hyaline cells (HCs), and semi-granular cells (SGCs), are essential for protecting against pathogenic microorganisms [17]. Pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), which are foreign polysaccharides, are recognized and bound by host proteins known as pattern recognition proteins (PRPs) to initiate innate immunity. This leads to cellular and hematological reactions such as phagocytosis, nodulation, coagulation, and the prophenoloxidase (proPO) cascade, as well as the induction of antimicrobial peptide synthesis [18]. The phenoloxidase enzyme (PO) is activated by the proPO system after infection and/or dietary immunostimulant integration. This initiates the melanization process, which inactivates the foreign particles and shields the host from pathogen damage [19, 20]. Circulating hemocytes also remove foreign particles by phagocytosis [21]. After a foreign particle binds to a cell, the membrane-bound enzyme complex NADPH oxidase assembles to start the process. It reduces molecular oxygen to form superoxide anions, which react to produce other reactive oxygen species (ROS) like hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radical, and singlet oxygen [22]. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) scavenges superoxide anions to produce hydrogen peroxide, which glutathione peroxidase (GPx) or catalase subsequently reduces. The first byproduct of this process, referred to as respiratory bursts (RBs), is superoxide anions [23]. Moreover, the three primary signaling mechanisms that control shrimp innate immunity are the toll pathway, the immunological deficiency (IMD) pathway, and the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) pathway [24]. In order to carry out the immune responses against pathogen invasion, the signaling pathway’s activation leads to the production of several immune-related genes, including lysozyme, prophenoloxidase (proPO), and anti-microbial peptides (AMPs) [25]. In shrimp humoral immune responses, anti-microbial peptides such as crustins, penaeidins, and anti-lipopolysaccharide factor (ALF) are thought to be the most significant effector molecules with effective antimicrobial activity [26]. Another carbohydrate recognition protein is C-type lectin (CTL) which is mainly responsible for pathogen recognition as it can recognize and bind to a specific cell surface, viral carbohydrates, and glycoproteins for induction antiviral peptides [27].

Shrimp have thus far been exposed to a variety of immunostimulants, including marine yeast [28, 29], macroalgae [30, 31], , seaweed [32, 33], and microalgae [34, 35]. Regarding immunostimulants, seaweeds are a rich source of bioactive substances that are highly valued by the food and pharmaceutical industries, including polysaccharides and pigments [36, 37]. The use of dry, isolated components and/or extracts of seaweeds (red, brown, and green) as an aquafeed ingredient in shrimp farming is becoming more widely recognized [38]. Various characteristics include immune-modulatory, nutritional, growth-promoting, antibacterial, cytostatic, neuroprotective, antifungal, antioxidant, antiviral and hepatoprotective activities [39–43].

A common red seaweed, Gracilaria verrucosa, is a naturally occurring plant material that has the potential to be assessed as a secure and eco-friendly immunostimulant [15]. G. verrucosa has been extensively farmed as a common source of agar [44]. According to the chemical investigation, G. verrucosa is primarily composed of polysaccharides, as evidenced by the seaweed’s sugars (95.40%) and sulfate (3.4%) contents [45, 46]. According to Jasmanindara et al. [33] and Rudi et al. [32], potent-sulfated polysaccharides that were derived from G. verrucosa can functionally be used as immune stimulators for shrimp and boost immunity against infection. In tiger shrimp resistant to WSSV, the sulfate in G. verrucosa binds to galactose as galactan sulfate, which is a potential immune stimulant substance [31, 32]. Previous reports by Arulkumar et al. [47] and Mubarak et al. [48] demonstrated that G. verrucosa extract includes such as phenolic compounds, tannins, quinones, terpenoids, saponins, and flavonoids that could shield the cells and fight against a variety of diseases. Due to the presence of large amounts of polyphenols and sulfated polysaccharides, this seaweed has been shown to lessen the invasion of free radicals in tissues by reducing oxidative distress [49]. In addition to being an immunological stimulant, G. verrucosa may accelerate the shrimp’s pace of growth. White shrimp grew more quickly when fed a diet containing 2 g/kg G. verrucosa extract following previous studies by Jasminandar et al. [33] and Ihsan et al. [50].

Thus, this study explored the influence of short-term supplementing G. verrucosa on L. vannamei immunological parameters including the hemocyte count, ACP, AKP, PO and lysozyme activities as well as phagocytosis, ROS, antioxidant activity (SOD, CAT, and GPx), and immune-related genes (ALF1, CRU1, PEN4, CTL, TRAF6, STAT, TLR1, and NOS). The resistance of Litopenaeus vannamei fed on G. verrucosa-containing diets against WSSV infection was also evaluated.

Materials and methods

Virus propagation

The WSSV was originally isolated from infected cultured shrimp in Vietnam. Following the steps outlined by Motamedi Sedeh et al. [51], infected juvenile L. vannamei were used to isolate WSSV (IRWSSVBU1), which was then homogenized in TNE buffer (0.05 M Tris, 0.1 M NaCl, 0.001 M EDTA, pH 7.4) and centrifuged at 4000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. Filtration of the supernatant was done using a millipore (0.45 mm). To propagate the WSSV, 100 healthy Astacus leptodactylus crayfish were injected intramuscularly with 100 ml of the prepared supernatant. A portion of the crayfish appendage tissues was cut, collected, and homogenized to be tested using the Nested-PCR (IQ 2000 diagnostic kit, GeneReach Biotechnology Corp., Taiwan). Hemolymph was removed from infected shrimp and virus stocks were isolated using sucrose gradient centrifugation following the previously explained protocol [52]. The virus’ copy number was identified using qRT-PCR. Aliquots of virus samples were kept at − 80 °C.

Three or four shrimp were infected with WSSV stock suspension (–80 °C) to reactivate the virus before the challenge. One gram of the contaminated shrimp organ was taken, pounded into a fine powder, and then added to nine milliliters of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution. The solution was then centrifuged twice, for a total of thirty minutes at a speed of 3000 rpm and twenty minutes at a speed of 8000 rpm. Using a syringe and a filter holder with 0.45 μm filtering paper, the obtained supernatant was filtered. To infect Pacific white shrimp with WSSV, an injection was used. The third segment of the abdomen was injected intramuscularly with 0.1 ml of viral solution (1.4 × 106 copies/ml). The PBS alone was injected into the negative control shrimp [53].

Seaweed G. verrucosa and diet preparation

G. verrucosa was harvested from Hangzhou Bay located in the northern part of the East China Sea. G. verrucosa was cultivated using 63 cables, each measuring 250 m in length. Five to six floats supported the central section of the cable, creating floating ropes that covered a total area of 75,000 m^2. A 3.5-t fresh weight of G. verrucosa was moved from a seaweed culture factory and put in polyethylene bags measuring 10 m in length, 0.5 m in breadth, and 0.5 m in height. There were five to six polyethylene bags per cable when the bags were hung, for a total of 350 bags. A total of 350 bags, each with an average of 10 kg of G. verrucosa, were initially suspended from the cables in five to six bags per cable. G. verrucosa was harvested with a planting weight of 50 g strand. Adjacent wires had their bags intertwined. Bags were hung heavy things 1–2 m below the water’s surface to protect them from intense light.

To get rid of salt, bacteria, and other undesired things, the seaweed was rinsed with both freshwater and seawater. After being washed, the seaweed was allowed to air dry away from the sun. Seaweed was then roughly crushed and put through a fine sieve with a mesh size of 60. The dried biomass was pulverized and screened to a particle size of 200 μm or less. The control diet was obtained from Hengxing Group Co., Ltd. (Jiaxing, China), which has a 40% crude protein and 8% crude fat content. This feed was mainly manufactured from fermented soybean meal, fish meal, vitamins, etc., according to satisfy the requirements of Litopenaeus vannamei [54]. Dried seaweed was added to the control basal diet at doses of 2, 4, and 8 g kg− 1 and the carboxymethyl cellulose content was adjusted accordingly. There were four different dietary regimens in all, including a control diet without dried seaweed addition. Experimental design, sampling, and challenge test

Three hundred and sixty healthy shrimp with average body weight (15.26 g ± 1.29 g) were obtained from the El-Ekhlas shrimp hatchery in Damietta- Port Said Province, Egypt. The shrimp were housed in an indoor setup system running with natural seawater. Water quality parameters can be measured with the LaMotte® Aquaponics Test Kit (LaMotte Company, Chestertown, MD, USA), including salinity (‰) using a salinometer along with the pH value, dissolved oxygen (DO, mg L− 1) using an oximeter model YK-22DO, temperature (oC) using a water thermometer (Yellow Springs Comp., Yellow Springs, OH, USA), and many others were measured every day. Throughout the experimental period, shrimp water quality was maintained [salinity (30‰), DO (5.00-6.30 mg L− 1), pH (7.6 ± 0.3), and temperature (25 ± 2oC) were made. The shrimp were fed their respective diets (4% of their body weight) at 08:00 and 19:00 h. Following the welfare and ethical approval of laboratory animals, the shrimp was allowed to have adequate nutrition and proper water quality. The shrimp was also allowed to exhibit normal behavior patterns. The shrimp also was protected from pain, suffering, injury, and disease.

A composite sand filter was employed in the experiment to filter the seawater. Following their acclimation period, the shrimp were randomly divided into 120-liter fiber tanks (30 shrimp/tank). Each diet plan was assigned a triple tank (a total of 90 shrimp/ group). Both control and dried seaweed-containing diets, namely 0, 2, 4, and 8 g kg− 1 diet [2, 33], were given to the shrimp. After 21 days of the feeding period, 10 shrimp were collected from each tank. Hemolymph (100 µl) was collected from each shrimp into a syringe containing 900 µl of anticoagulant solution (Trisodium citrate 30 mM, sodium chloride 0.34 M, EDTA 10 mM, pH 7.5, osmolality 780 mOsm kg/1). Hemocytometers were used to measure cell density and trypan blue exclusion tests were used to assess cell viability. To estimate ROS generation, and phagocytic activity, harvested cells were adjusted to 5 × 105 cells/ml. The gills were minced and mixed with a shrimp PBS (pH 7.4) in a homogenizer yielding a homogenate that was then centrifuged forming a supernatant to be used in the estimation of ACP, AKP, SOD, GPx, catalase, and MDA. To be employed in the RNA extraction, gills (n = 5) were also obtained from shrimp in each group while maintaining sterility.

Following the use of RT-PCR to determine the virus’ copy number, the WSSV filtrate was used for the challenge test. The remaining shrimp from each group (60 shrimp/ group) received an intramuscular injection of 0.1 ml of WSSV (1.4 × 106 copies/ml). After injection, all shrimp groups were maintained in the same experimental tanks with the same conditions. WSSV infection-related symptoms were verified by looking for the distinctive white patches on the dead shrimp carapace. The polymerase chain reaction (RCR) test for DNA samples was randomly taken from the dead samples’ gills in each group and made also to confirm infection. Following the challenge with WSSV, the shrimp were fed diets with and without dried seaweed supplements. The shrimp were examined to check for signs of illness. The shrimp mortality in each tank was tracked for ten days, and the survival percentage was calculated by averaging the tanks in triplicate. The same samples as before the challenge were collected on the 5th day of the challenge.

Reaction conditions for the WSSV gene amplification

According to Saberi et al. [55] the oligonucleotide primers for WSSV F 5’ GTGACCAAGACCATCGAAAC 3’ and R 5’ TCAGTCATCTTGAAGTAGCC 3’ were as follows. That was intended to amplify (234 bp) all of the vp28 ORFs in the WSSV PCR: Using the GeneAmp Polymerase Chain Reaction (Creacon, Thermo cycler, Holand) system cycler, specific DNA was amplified. To obtain amplified genomic DNA, PCR was used. The cycling parameters for the second step reaction were as follows: a single cycle at 95 °C for two minutes, followed by 39 cycles at 95 °C, 65 °C, and 72 °C for 30 s each, and a final extension for two minutes at 72 °C [56].

Total hemocyte count

Using a hemocytometer (Boeco, Hamburg, Germany) and a 400x magnification light microscope, the total hemocyte count (THC) was determined.

A mixture of anticoagulant and hemolymph was treated for 20 min at room temperature with fixative (10% formalin in 0.45 M NaCl). After adding the same volume of rose bengal solution (1.2% rose bengal in 50% ethanol) to fixed hemocyte suspensions (20 µL), the mixture was incubated for an additional 20 min at 25 °C. After the smears were ready, they were fully air-dried and then counterstained for ten minutes using a hematoxylin solution. After counting at least 200 cells on each slide, the counts, and relative proportions of different hemocyte types were determined [(number of distinct hemocyte cell types / 200) x THC] [57].

Phagocytic activity

With a small modification, phagocytic activity was measured using Xian et al. [58] methodology. After gently mixing 500 µl of cell suspensions (5 × 105 cells/mL), the same volume of latex beads (~ 107 beads/mL; 1.094µ; Polysciences, USA) was added, and the mixture was incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The hemocyte bead mixture was fixed for 20 min at room temperature using the same volume of 10% formalin. Rose bengal and hematoxylin solutions were used to stain the hemocytes in the manner previously mentioned [57]. Phagocytosis (%) equals (phagocytic hemocytes / total hemocytes) x 100.

ROS production

Using a fluorescent probe called 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), intracellular ROS was measured using a modified technique as reported by Moss and Allam [59]. When reactive oxygen species (ROS) are present, DCFH-DA diffuses across the cell membrane and is enzymatically hydrolyzed by intracellular esterases to generate non-fluorescent 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescein (DCFH). This is subsequently quickly oxidized to form highly fluorescent 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescin (DCF). It is thought that the intensity of DCF fluorescence correlates with the quantity of ROS produced inside cells. At first, DCFH was thought to be a particular H2O2 indication. Later on, though, it was shown that nitric oxide (NO), peroxynitrite, hydroxyl (HO−), and peroxyl (ROO−) could also oxidize it (Xian et al., 2009). A final concentration of 0.1 mM of 2′, 7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, Sigma) was incubated for 30 min at room temperature with 100 µl of hemolymph (5 × 105 cells/mL). Zymosan suspensions were added at a ratio of 1 hemocyte: 50 particles to directly trigger the formation of ROS. Using Varioskan Flash Spectral Scanning Multimode Readers (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), the fluorescence reading was obtained at 502 nm excitation and 523 nm emission wavelengths following a 30-minute dark incubation period.

Lysozyme activity

The ability of the lysozyme to lyse Micrococcus lysodeikticus (Sigma Chemical Co., USA) was used to determine its activity, as per the previously published technique [60]. Briefly, the precipitate obtained from centrifuging the hemolymph-anticoagulant mixture was combined with 0.02% Micrococcus lysodeikticus diluted in shrimp saline. Using a microplate reader (Optica, Mikura Ltd, UK), the absorbance was measured at 1-minute intervals for 5 min at 540 nm after the reaction was administered at room temperature. Using lyophilized chicken egg-white lysozyme (Sigma, USA), a calibration curve was used to determine the lysozyme concentration.

Phenoloxidase activity

The production of dopachrome from L-dihydroxyphenylalanine (L-DOPA; Sigma, USA) was used to evaluate phenoloxidase (PO) activity, as previously reported by Hernández-López et al. [61]. Following homogenization of the suspensions (Hemolymph and cacodylate (CAC) buffer) using Pellet Pestles (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), the mixtures were centrifuged for 20 min at 15,000 rpm. Hemocyte lysate supernatant (HLS), the end product, was extracted and utilized as an enzyme source right away. Incubated at 25 °C, 200 µl of HLS were added to the same volume of trypsin (1 mg/mL CAC buffer). Following a 30-minute incubation period, 200 µl of L-DOPA (3 mg/mL CAC buffer) was introduced. Using Varioskan Flash Spectral Scanning Multimode Readers (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), the optical density was measured at 490 nm. According to Sung et al. [62], the phenoloxidase activity was determined as an increase in the optical density of 0.001/min/ mg protein. The Bradford [63] technique was used to determine the protein content of HLS.

Alkaline and acid phosphatase activities

Assays for alkaline (ALP) and acid phosphatase (ACP) activities were carried out using the guidelines provided by Vaseeharan et al. [64]. In summary, p-nitro phenyl phosphate (16 mM) was used as a standard substrate for the measurement of ALP and ACP. The ALP and ACP tests were conducted using glycine NaOH buffer and sodium acetate buffer, respectively. The hemolymph (50 µL) and the substrate (200 µL) were added to mixtures, which were then incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. At 410 nm, the amount of released p-nitrophenol in the resultant supernatants was measured and determined using the standard curve.

Oxidant/antioxidant status

Superoxide dismutase (SOD, Catalog NO.; EIASODC), Catalase (CAT, Catalog NO.; EIACATC), glutathione peroxidase (GPx, Catalog NO.; EEA010) and malondialdehyde (MDA, Catalog NO.; EEA015) were measured using colorimetric detection kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Karlsruhe, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

The enzyme’s capacity to stop the phenazine methosulfate-mediated reduction of nitroblue tetrazolium dye at a wavelength of 560 nm was used to evaluate SOD [65]. The CAT was computed using the enzyme reaction mixture, which included contained samples, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and potassium phosphate (pH 7.0). A UV–VIS spectrophotometer set at 240 nm was used to calculate the molar attenuation coefficient of H2O2 [66]. GPx activity was measured in tissue homogenate. In summary, 200 µL of homogenate supernatant was combined with 1 mL of pH 7.0 phosphate buffer, 10 mL glutathione (150 mM), 10 mL glutathione reductase (340 U/mL), 30 mL (25 mM EDTA), 30 mL NADPH (5 mM), 10 mL Triton X-100 (20%), and 50 µL H 33 2 NADP + O 2 (7.5mM). For three minutes, the oxidation of NADPH was monitored at 340 nm [67]. The thiobarbituric acid technique was used to determine the MDA level. Thiobarbituric acid’s reactive components were measured at 535 nm and then expressed in terms of MDA produced [68].

qRT-PCR analysis of immune-related genes

RNAiso Plus (TAKARA, Japan) was used to extract total RNA from various tissue samples in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA samples were examined for purity using an ND-1000 (Thermo Scientific, USA) NanoDrop spectrophotometer set to measure absorbance at 260 and 280 nm. RNA ratios (A260:A280) greater than 1.8 were selected for use in subsequent investigations. Subsequently, 1.0 µg of total RNA was used to create the cDNA using a ReverTra Ace qPCR RT kit (Toyobo, Japan) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The following technique was followed for doing the real-time PCR tests on cDNA samples using the real-time PCR System (CFX Connect, Bio-Rad, USA): A mixture of 7.5 µL SYBR® Green Master Mix, 3 µL gene-specific primers, and 3.5 µL nuclease-free water was combined with 1 µL of cDNA. Based on the sequences listed in Table 2, specific primers were created for ALF1, CRU1, PEN4, CTL, TRAF6, STAT, TLR1, NOS, and elongation factor-1α (an internal control gene) (1). The program used for amplification consisted of 40 cycles of 94 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 45 s, and 95 °C for three minutes. Following the procedures outlined by the threshold cycle (CT) was identified, and the comparative CT method (2−△△CT method) was utilized to assess the expression level of these genes in comparison to EF-1α [69].

Statistical analysis

After normality verification using Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with post-hoc Tukey’s multiple range tests, using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, California, USA) to examine the hematological and biochemical indices, as well as immunity response, antioxidant enzymes, and gene expression. The experimental data were expressed as means ± SE for each parameter. The probabilities of (p < 0.05) with different letters were considered significant in comparison with the pre-challenged control, whereas significant variation at *; P < 0.05, **; P < 0.01, and ***; P < 0.001 was compared with the post-challenged control.

Results

Clinical symptoms before and after WSSV challenge

The clinical symptoms of Litopenaeus vannamei shrimp after being challenged with the white spot syndrome virus are shown in Fig. 1. In most moribund shrimp in the GV0 group, white spots embedded within the exoskeleton were the most observed clinical sign. These spots ranged from small up to 3 mm in diameter, and they sometimes coalesced into larger plates (Fig. 1). In supplemented shrimp with Gracilaria verrucosa at GV2, GV4, and GV8 groups, these spots were found to disappear gradually on the carapace in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1).

The challenged shrimp, Litopenaeus vannamei fed three different doses of Gracilaria verrucosa (GV; 0, 2, 4, 8 g kg− 1 diet). GV0; white-colored spots are shown on the carapace of infected non-supplemented shrimp. Groups, GV2, GV4, GV8 showed gradual removal of white spots in a dose dependent trend

Total and Differential hemocyte count before and post-WSSV challenge

Before the viral challenge, HCs, GCs, SGCs, and THC in shrimp fed the diets containing G. verrucosa at 4.0 and 8.0 g kg− 1 were significantly higher than in shrimp fed the control diet (P ˂ 0.05) (Fig. 2A-D). After the challenge, GCs, SGCs, and THC in infected shrimp fed the control diet were significantly lower than in the control shrimp before infection (P ˂ 0.05). Whereas, a notable increase in HCs, GCs, SGCs, and THC was revealed in the infected shrimp fed the diets containing G. verrucosa at 4.0 and 8.0 g kg− 1 (P < 0.01; P < 0.001) in comparison with the infected control shrimp (Fig. 2A-D). Besides, SGCs and HCs in infected shrimp fed G. verrucosa at 2.0 g kg− 1 diet significantly increased (P ˂ 0.05) compared with the infected control shrimp.

Immune parameters before and post-WSSV challenge

The effects of microalgal G. verrucosa supplementation on the phagocytosis, lysozyme, respiratory burst (ROS), and phenoloxidase (PO) activities of Litopenaeus vannamei hemolymph pre- and post-WSSV infection are displayed in Fig. 3. Before the challenge, phagocytosis, lysozyme, ROS, and PO activities in shrimp fed the diets containing G. verrucosa at 2.0, 4.0, and 8.0 g kg− 1 were significantly higher than in shrimp fed the control diet (P ˂ 0.05) (Fig. 3A-D). The highest activities of above-mentioned immunological parameters were recorded in 8.0 g kg− 1 GV treated group. Following the challenge test, a marked decrease in the activities of lysozyme, ROS, and PO was observed in the infected control shrimp compared with the normal control one (P ˂ 0.05) (Fig. 3B-D). In comparison with WSSV-infected control shrimp, a significant enhancement in these previous indices was shown in the infected shrimp supplemented G. verrucosa diets at 4.0 and 8.0 g kg− 1 (P ˂ 0.05; P < 0.01; P < 0.001) (Fig. 3A-D). Similar enhancement was observed in phagocytosis and PO activity in the infected shrimp supplemented G. verrucosa diet at 2.0 g kg− 1 (P ˂ 0.05) (Fig. 3A, D).

Discussion

During recent decades, scientific research has focused on the impact of seaweeds as feed additives on the amelioration of the survival rates, healthy condition, immune response, and disease resistance of cultured fish and shrimp species [1, 70]. In the present study, we evaluated the positive effect of dietary seaweed Gracilaria verrucosa supplementation on the white spot syndrome resistance of the shrimp was positive as the results of the viral challenge test showed that the seaweed-supplemented diet can protect against viral WSSV infection. Indeed, the G. verrucosa-based dietary supplement markedly improved shrimp survival rates against the WSSV. This result conferred the interesting fact given the prospective antiviral protection by dietary inclusion of G. verrucosa in shrimp. A diet supplemented with extract from G. verrucosa at 4 g kg− 1 dose was shown to be also useful in the protection against WSSV infection in Pacific white shrimp [15]. In addition, shrimp white shrimp Litopenaeus vannamei receiving extracts of G. tenuistipitata at 2.0 g kg− 1 revealed potential protection with higher survival rates against Vibrio harveyi (V. harveyi) infection [71], as well as, against Vibrio alginolyticus or/ and WSSV pathogens [4]. The survival rate was significantly increased in whiteleg shrimp supplemented with Gracilaria corticata extracted diet and exposed to WSSV injection [10]. Effectively, bioactive compounds contained in Gracilaria species, such as polyunsaturated fatty acids (omega-3 fatty acids (ɷ-3)), sulfated polysaccharides (e.g., agar and carrageenan, sulphated galactans, and porphyrans), and lectins, have been largely associated with antiviral defenses via competing with WSSV entry to target tissues [4, 72, 73], giving their ability to boost the innate immunity of shrimp post-WSSV challenge as evidenced by gradual vanishing of white spots in supplemented shrimp exposed to WSSV. It was previously reported that shrimp could resist a wide range of infectious pathogen attacks because immunostimulants swiftly initiate phagocytosis and the generation of ROS [33]. The current study’s findings regarding the stimulation of non-specific immunity through the promotion of hemocyte count, ACP, AKP, PO, and lysozyme activities as well as phagocytosis, ROS, antioxidant activity, and induction of immune genes in shrimp may be partially responsible for the enhanced resistance of Litopenaeus vannamei against WSSV infection. Additionally, these findings support the theory that seaweed supplements improve host health by inducing immunological responses that protect against pathogenic microorganisms [2]. Consequently, adding G. verrucosa to shrimp diets could be a useful way to lessen the negative effects of WSSV on shrimp aquaculture.

Immune defenses of shrimp are represented by hemocytes which have a crucial role in both cellular and humoral immunity [74–76]. Besides, hemocyte count is a reliable indicator of penaeid shrimp health status [77]. In the present study, whiteleg shrimp juveniles fed G. verrucosa at 4.0 and 8.0 g kg− 1 diets before and after viral challenge, exhibited increased THCs, HCs, GCs, and SGCs. Incorporation of G. tenuistipitata extract containing diets at 2.0 g kg− 1 for 14 days showed marked augmentation in THCs together with HCs, GCs, and SGCs [71]. As well, the dietary administration of G. verrucosa extract at 2.0, 3.0, 4.0, and 5.0 g kg− 1 has increased the total hemocyte counts in normal supplemented and challenged Pacific white shrimp with white spot syndrome virus [15]. Total hemocyte counts have been significantly increased in the infected whiteleg shrimp with V. harveyi and supplemented with 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 g kg− 1 doses of G. verrucosa [32, 44]. Notably, the increment that occurred in the total and differential hemocytes indicated that crude sulfated polysaccharides (SPs) derived from G. verrucosa can stimulate innate hemocyte receptors in shrimp, hence increasing shrimp body humoral and cellular immune reactive defenses [2]. A similar increment is exhibited following the WSSV challenge via recognition of WSSV-associated molecular pattern particles (PAMP) by the hemocyte cell receptors [13, 33]. Indeed, the imperative immunostimulatory effect of incorporated G. verrucosa extract in diets of shrimp and prawns has been documented, subsequent to increasing the immune system and their survival against microbial pathogens attack [31, 33].

Phagocytosis is an indispensable mechanism of shrimp innate cellular immunity, which is initiated by recognizing the target particles via hyaline cells for phagosome formation, endocytosis, and engulfing of microbial pathogens [20, 78]. Following this mechanism, the oxygen-dependent respiratory burst produces superoxide anion and oxygen reactive species (ROS) formation to remove the invading pathogens within phagosome bodies [22, 79]. Whereas, humoral immunity involves the hemocyte’s secretion of prophenoloxidase system (proPO), lysozyme, and other antimicrobial peptides (crustins and lectins), which can trigger various immune responses [80]. During infection and/or dietary immunostimulant incorporation, the proPO system activates the phenoloxidase enzyme (PO), which triggers the melanization process to inactivate the foreign particles, and to protect the host against pathogens damage [19, 20]. Antimicrobial lysozyme is a substantial component of nonspecific immunity in penaeid shrimp that can defend against pathogenic microbes by catalyzing the hydrolysis of the bacterial cell walls [81, 82]. The current study displayed that G. verrucosa powder in feed significantly increased the phagocytosis, PO, ROS, and lysozyme activities of hemocytes in L. vannamei, pre-and post-WSSV challenge. Additional studies have shown that L. vannamei shrimp fed Gracilaria bailiniae powder (5.0 g kg− 1), increased the activity of phagocytosis, respiratory bursts, lysozyme, and PO [82]. Similarly, dietary G. verrucosa extract markedly increased the PO and phagocytic activities, and respiratory burst of L. vannamei before and after V. harveyi [2, 32, 44], as well as WSSV infection [15]. The higher plasma peroxidase and lysozyme activities were manifested in rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) fed with 5%Gracilaria vermiculophylla [72]. It is reported that the immunostimulant G. verrucosa extract has a significant effect on enhancing the immune system of L. vannamei challenged with WSSV via increasing phagocytosis and respiratory burst [13]. Interestingly, these results were consistent with the THCs and differential hemocyte counts in our study, showing that hemocyte numbers of shrimp are positively correlated with phagocytic activity and ROS intermediates via G. verrucosa supplementation, which could improve the number of hyaline cells involved in the phagocytotic activity of crustaceans [82, 83]. Subsequently, notable hemocyte ROS production in shrimp exhibited in feed supplemented with G. verrucosa indicated by higher phagocytic activity and oxygen-dependent killing mechanism of WSSV viral particles. We also speculated that the higher activity of PO and lysozyme is related to the interaction of sulfated galactans contained in red seaweed G. verrucosa with β-1, 3-glucan binding protein on the hemocytes membrane to stimulate their receptors to enhance the PO enzyme activation [31, 33]. Thus, phenoloxidase activity can identify the foreign particles that enter the shrimp body to initiate melanin production during infection. To our knowledge, it has been documented that the major polysaccharide (agar) contained in the cell walls of Gracilaria spp. is responsible for the modulation of the immunological response in fish species [70, 72].

From another perspective, phosphatase enzymes are reliable indicators for lysosomal enzymes that are released by hemocytes, taking part in the growth and nonspecific immune capacity in shrimp [84]. ALP is involved in the regulation of cellular metabolic functions, such as growth and nutrient transport as well as and it stores microbes in lysosomes [85]. ACP digests the invading organisms in aquatic crustaceans [86]. In our present study, there were significantly higher gills ACP and ALK activities in groups fed G. verrucose diets pre- and post-WSSV challenge. Similarly, an elevated trend in AKP and ACP activities was manifested in L. vannamei fed sulfate-based alginate polysaccharide extracted from red seaweeds [87]. Higher ACP activity was displayed in white leg shrimp supplemented with seaweed Laminaria japonica extract [88]. During cold stress, the incorporation of brown seaweed, Sargassum polycystum into shrimp diets markedly increased the levels of ACP and ALK [1]. We suggest that polysaccharides (agar) in G. verrucose may be responsible for the improved immune enzyme activity of L. vannamei during viral infection.

Antioxidant enzymes play remarkable roles in the shrimp’s immune defense by scavenging the superoxide anions and other ROS produced by hemocytes to minimize the oxidative stress as excess ROS production can oxidize the fatty acids that compose cell membranes, generating lipid peroxides like malondialdehyde (MDA) [89, 90]. Our study revealed that white shrimp juveniles fed G. verrucosa-containing diets after 21 days exhibited increased SOD, CAT, and GPx activity, altogether with decreased MDA levels post supplementation and challenge test. SOD enzymes stimulate the conversion of superoxide (O2−) free radicles into hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and oxygen to avoid host tissue damage from excess free radicals [91]. While GPx and CAT enzymes decompose the abundant H2O2 generated by phagocytes during the immune reactions into water (H2O) and O2 [90]. In addition, reduction of the lipid hydroperoxides can be conducted via GPx [92]. The dietary administration of G. tenuistipitata extract at 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 g kg− 1 for 16 weeks increased SOD activity in the hemolymph of white shrimp [71]. The muscle SOD activity was also increased in white leg shrimp supplemented with 5% G. verrucose pre- and post-white spot syndrome virus infection [13]. White shrimp, L. vannamei fed G. tenuistipitata extract at 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 g kg− 1 for 14 days showed an increase in SOD and GPx activities in the hemolymph [4]. It has been reported that juvenile white shrimp fed with Gracilaria bailiniae, significantly increased the enzyme activities of SOD, GPx, and CAT and diminished the content of MDA in the hepatopancrease of shrimps [82]. Nevertheless, increased the activity of hepatic catalase and glutathione reductase enzymes was assessed in the gilthead seabream (Sparus aurata) fed Gracilaria gracilis before and after challenge with Photobacterium damselae subsp. Piscicida [70]. Thus, the increment in the antioxidant targets along with reduced MDA content in G. verrucose supplemented groups prohibit the burst of cellular free radicles during defense against WSSV infections and safeguard shrimp cells from damage that is supported by the effective protective impact of G. verrucose supplementation in the normal cellular functions and scavenging ROS intermediates in normal and infected L. vannamei shrimp. Gracilaria sp. seaweeds can be utilized as a source of natural antioxidant compounds because of their active polyphenolic compounds (e.g., phenolic acids, flavonoids, phlorotannins, fucoxanthin, and bromophenols), terpenoids (α- and β-carotene, lutein, and zeaxanthin), and alkaloids [93], which they play essential functions in decreasing the lipid peroxidation along with enhancing the antioxidant capability against oxidative stress [38, 94]. Besides, dietary red seaweed Gracilaria vermiculophylla supplementation has been assessed as a vector for vitamins and halogenated minerals, which have antioxidation characteristics [95].

Shrimp humoral immunity involves the release of antimicrobial peptides from granular and semi-granular hemocytes, which can trigger various immune responses through intercellular communication. C-type lectins (CTLs) are Ca2+ dependent carbohydrate recognition proteins that are mainly responsible for pathogen recognition, and cell-cell interactions as they can recognize and bind to a specific cell surface and viral carbohydrates (saccharides) and glycoproteins for pathogens and hemocytes agglutination [20]. As well, CTLs are involved in the phagocytic activity, activation of proPO, melanization, and antiviral activation via binding to the envelope proteins of WSSV [96]. Crustins (CRU1), penaeidins (PEN4), and antilipolysaccharide factors (ALF1) are major antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) that restrain the bacterial growth inside the hemolymph of crustaceans [97]. Crustins are cationic proteins against Gram-positive bacteria; penaeidins are proline/arginine-rich peptides, that possess antimicrobial activities against both Gram-positive and -negative bacteria, as well as a potent chitin-binding activity of fungi [18]. ALFs are large proteins, exhibiting strong bactericidal activity and interfering with WSSV viral particle replication [98]. The results of the current research showed that dietary G. verrucose in three different doses effectively stimulated the expression of ALF1, CRU1, PEN4, and CTL genes in the gills of unchallenged shrimp. On the 7th day of WSSV infection, the expression of studied genes in all groups was notably downregulated. However, after the challenge with the WSSV, the expression levels of these genes in these previous groups were upregulated by G. verrucose supplementation in a dose-dependent manner. The gene expressions of lectin and penaeidin 3 were up-regulated in the hemocytes of shrimp that received carrageenan, a red seaweed polysaccharide at 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 g kg− 1 for 3 weeks [92]. As well, dietary inoculation of sulfated galactan (SG) from Gracilaria fishery (0.5, 1, and 2% w/w), significantly enhanced the expression of the antimicrobial peptides, like PEN-4 and CRU in L. vannamei shrimp [99]. So, dietary supplementation of G. verrucose due to its natural sulfated polysaccharides, such as carrageenan and agar, markedly improved the immune-related gene expressions, inducing favorable effects in the pathogen recognition of WSSV particles and viral agglutinations via lectins content of G. verrucose. Besides, previous studies have demonstrated that sulfated galactan from Gracilaria fishery binds to β-1,3-glucan binding proteins of shrimp hemocytes through lipopolysaccharide and β -1,3-glucan binding protein (LGBP) pathway [100], and hence various immune responses are activated with the release of antimicrobial peptides before and after exposure to WSSV.

Toll-like receptors (TLRs) is a crucial pattern recognition receptor (PRR), which senses and binds to specific pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) of microbes to trigger signaling pathways (e.g., TLR-TRAF6, IMD, and JAK-STAT) involved in immune responses of shrimp [101, 102]. Tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) is a pivotal signal transducer of TLR proteins mediated signaling pathway (TLR-TRAF6) [103], which is activated by PAMPs to activate nuclear factor-kappa B and transcript many pro-inflammatory genes as well as it activates the AMPs to combat viral infections [104, 105]. TRAF6 expression was markedly induced by WSSV and Vibrio alginolyticus challenge in various shrimp species [104, 105]. The same trend was observed in the expression of TLRs in shrimp after WSSV infection [101]. The signal transducer and activator of the transcription (STAT) gene has an important part of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway as it phosphorylated and dimerized during shrimp antiviral immunity against WSSV [106], as well as shrimp STAT is known to bind to the promoter region of the WSSV immediate early promotor gene ie1 to enhance its expression, subsequently, the expression of STAT was increased after WSSV infection [107]. Nitric oxide synthase (NOS) catalyzes the formation of the free radical Nitric oxide (NO), which is produced by hemocytes during phagocytosis during viral infection by directly damaging the DNA, protein, and lipid of pathogen membrane [108]. In the present study, the transcript levels of TRAF6, STAT, TLR1, and NOS in the gills of challenged control white shrimp were increased, however, they significantly downregulated in G. verrucose-supplemented challenged shrimp, except for NOS. Taken together, these data indicate that these genes participate in the regulation of innate immune responses of shrimp against WSSV infections by increasing their expression. At the same time, considering the WSSV host interaction, it could be proposed that sulfated galactan contained in G. verrucose binding with TLRs interrupted the viral utilization of the TLR-TRAF and JAK-STAT signaling pathways for viral replication in the host cell for preserving immune homeostasis.

However, THCs and differential hemocytes, as well as most of the immune parameters in control shrimps markedly declined within ten days of WSSV infection. Then, it was notably recovered and enhanced at various doses of G. verrucosa on day 10 of infection. The notable decrease in circulating hemocytes in viral-infected shrimp has been reported in many studies from hemocytes leaving the circulation and migrating to tissues; or it could be due to the restricted hematopoiesis to the hematopoietic tissue from WSSV invasion [10, 109]. Consequently, most cellular, and humoral immunity reactions may also be diminished as observed in control shrimp challenged with WSSV.

Because brown seaweeds contain polysaccharides including laminarin, alginate, and fucoidan., they can be utilized as an immunostimulant [31]. Through interactions with dectin-1 receptors on the surface of immune cells, seaweed polysaccharides can activate the non-specific immune system including phagocytic and respiratory burst activities [15]. Seaweed polysaccharides when added to food can help aquatic animals grow, strengthen their immune systems against viruses and bacteria, and improve their gut health and antioxidant capacity [110]. According to Ale et al. [111], fucoidan derived from brown seaweed, contains polysaccharide sulfate, which has immunomodulatory effects. The sulfate present in Gracilaria is coupled to galactose since sulfated galactans are an immunostimulator in tiger prawns and increase resistance to WSSV infection [31]. Other important pattern recognition proteins (PRPs), lipopolysaccharide and b-1,3-glucan-binding protein (LGBP) recognize lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and b-1,3-glucan (bG), sometimes referred to as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), and thereby initiate innate immunity in L. vannamei [112]. In addition to binding to LPS and bG, recombinant LGBP (rLvLGBP) also binds to fucoidan, laminarin, and alginate from seaweed. This resulted in the degranulation of shrimp hemocytes, and activation of the proPO system that subsequently leading to an increase in PO activity, indicating the stimulation of innate immunity [113].

Conclusion

According to the current study, shrimp-fed diets containing Gracilaria verrucosa (GV) extract demonstrated enhanced immunity as evidenced by increases in circulating hemocyte c, ACP, AKP, PO and lysozyme activities as well as phagocytosis, ROS, antioxidant activity (SOD, CAT, and GPx), and immune-related genes (ALF1, CRU1, PEN4, CTL, TRAF6, STAT, TLR1, and NOS). In addition, adding GV to shrimp meal greatly increases their resistance to white spot disease brought on by WSSV infections. The ideal dose of dietary GV treatment for pacific white shrimp is 8 g GV (kg diet)−1, which will increase their immunity and disease resistance.

Although many investigators have examined the protective effects of G. verrucosa extract on the immune response of pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei). Our study was the first to explore the influence of G. verrucosa-containing diets against WSSV infection in shrimp. Also, in this study, we examined new signaling pathways involved in shrimp antiviral immunity (TRAF6, JAK/STAT, TLR1, and NOS) that were not assessed in any previous study evaluating the protective effects of G. verrucosa against shrimp infection. Therefore, G. verrucosa and nucleotides could be included in the creation of aquafeeds to control disease infection. This pilot study recommends more research to investigate the ideal feeding period and ascertain the mechanism of action of this supplement on the antioxidant and immunological response.
