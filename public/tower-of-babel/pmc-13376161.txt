A multi-decadal assessment dataset of soil organic matter in the Sanjiang Plain black soil region (1984–2023)
Authors: Yu Zhang, Huanjun Liu, Chong Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate estimation of soil organic matter (SOM) content in cultivated land is essential for food security and climate change mitigation. Here we present SOM-SJP-40y, a 40-year (1984–2023) dataset of topsoil SOM dynamics for the Sanjiang Plain, a typical black soil region in China. The dataset integrates 364 field samples (0–20 cm) with decadal median composites of bare soil imagery derived from Landsat time series on the Google Earth Engine platform. SOM predictions were generated using a probabilistic hybrid modeling approach combined with K-means clustering, achieving a model performance of R² = 0.72, RMSE = 8.66 g kg⁻¹, and RPD = 1.75. Using SOM-SJP-40y, we observe spatially heterogeneous SOM dynamics, with localized SOM increases in some areas since the 2010s that may be associated with recent conservation policies. This dataset offers a valuable resource for soil quality assessment, farmland protection, and carbon management in black soil regions.

Background & Summary

Soil organic matter (SOM) is a key indicator of soil quality and agricultural productivity, and plays a vital role in maintaining global soil health and food security1–3. Under natural conditions, soil typically serves as a major sink for atmospheric carbon, with enhancing soil carbon sequestration considered one of the key pathways to achieving the 2050 carbon neutrality goal4–6. However, with the increasing intensity of human activities (such as fertilization and changes in land use), the SOM content in cultivated land has been declining, thereby threatening soil fertility, ecosystem stability, and climate regulation functions7–9. Therefore, understanding the spatiotemporal dynamics of SOM is of great significance for achieving sustainable agriculture and addressing climate change.

Over the past three decades, remote sensing has proven to be an effective approach for characterizing the spatial distribution of SOM and quantifying its spatial heterogeneity across multiple scales10,11. Most studies on SOM dynamics have relied primarily on satellite remote sensing imagery as the main data source12. Currently, commonly used satellites in digital soil mapping include the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS, 1999–present), Sentinel-2 (2017–present), and Gaofen-2 (2014–present)13,14. However, these sensors have only been available since the late 1990s, limiting their ability to reconstruct SOM spatial patterns prior to that period. In contrast, the Landsat series (from Landsat-1 to Landsat-9) provides a continuous Earth observation record spanning over five decades. In particular, Landsat-5 Thematic Mapper (TM, 1984–2011) and Landsat-8 Operational Land Imager (OLI, 2013–present) have enabled consistent global observations since 1984. This long-term data archive offers a unique opportunity for reconstructing spatially explicit, long-term SOM dynamics at regional to local scales, thereby providing strong support for land management and sustainable agriculture15,16. In addition, several global and regional SOC/SOM datasets have been widely used in soil monitoring studies. For example, SoilGrids provides global SOC predictions at a 250 m resolution based on machine learning approaches, while the Harmonized World Soil Database (HWSD) and the Global Soil Organic Carbon Map (GSOCmap) offer essential baseline information on global soil carbon distribution. However, most of these datasets are static products with relatively coarse spatial resolution, which limits their applicability for fine-scale agricultural management.

Recent studies have shown that utilizing soil spectral features during bare soil periods has become a mainstream approach for mapping SOM17. During these periods, spectral reflectance in the visible and near-infrared (VNIR, 450–1000 nm) regions exhibits strong correlations with SOM content18,19. Therefore, soil spectral analysis provides a reliable basis for predicting the spatial distribution of soil properties. However, in regions where bare soil imagery is unavailable, vegetation indices can serve as effective alternative proxies. These indices capture soil–vegetation interactions and thus provide indirect information on soil properties. In addition, integrating environmental covariates such as topography, climate, and land use can further improve the accuracy of SOM predictions20,21. For example, Li et al.17 successfully mapped the spatial distribution of SOC across China by incorporating multi-source environmental variables. Methodologically, traditional machine learning models struggle to effectively capture spatial heterogeneity, while hybrid models, though capable of improving local accuracy, suffer from spatial discontinuities. Probabilistic hybrid models, by incorporating smoothing mechanisms, are better able to characterize continuous spatial variations; however, their application in long-term SOM mapping remains limited16,22,23.

To address these gaps, this study reconstructs four decades (1984–2023) of SOM dynamics in the Sanjiang Plain using multi-temporal Landsat data and a probabilistic hybrid modeling framework. By integrating spectral information with environmental covariates, this approach improves prediction accuracy while capturing spatial heterogeneity. The objectives are to (1) produce long-term high-resolution SOM maps, (2) quantify their spatial–temporal dynamics, and (3) evaluate model performance. This work provides a robust framework for long-term SOM mapping in black soil regions.

Methods

Study area

Sanjiang Plain is located in the northeastern part of Heilongjiang Province, China, with geographic coordinates ranging from 43°49′55″ to 48°27′40″ N and 129°11′20″ to 135°05′26″ E. It is an important ecological functional area and one of the major grain production bases in China24. The total area of this region is about 108,900 km², accounting for 23.9% of the total land area of Heilongjiang Province. The cultivated land area is approximately 46,667 km², including about 23,707 km² of paddy fields25. Sanjiang Plain is alluviated by the confluence of three rivers, Heilongjiang, Songhua and Ussuri, forming a vast low level marshy landscape (Fig. 1)26. The region is located at the northern end of the second uplift zone of the New Huaxia tectonic system, an argillic zone, geologically belonging to the Tongjiang inland fault zone27. Sanjiang Plain belongs to temperate humid and semi-humid continental monsoon climate, the average annual temperature and annual precipitation are 1–4°C and 500–700 mm respectively, while 75–85% of the precipitation is concentrated in June-October28. According to the World Reference Base for Soil Resources (WRB), the main soil types in the region are Phaeozems (black soils), Cambisols (meadow soils) and Luvisols (dark brown soils)19. Among these, Phaeozems, which are equivalent to Mollisols in the USDA Soil Taxonomy, are characterized by a thick, organic matter-rich surface horizon (A horizon), high fertility, and strong carbon sequestration capacity29,30. These soils dominate the cultivated land of the Sanjiang Plain and form the primary focus of this study.

Collection and pre-processing of soil and remote sensing image data

A total of 364 surface soil samples (0–20 cm) were collected from agricultural fields in the Sanjiang Plain and used for model development. The 0–20 cm layer was selected as it represents the topsoil (plough layer), which largely corresponds to the pedological A horizon and contains the most active fraction of soil organic matter31,32. This standardized depth also ensures comparability across sampling sites and time periods. These samples consisted of 176 samples from the 2010s and 188 samples from the 2020 s (Table S1, Fig. 2). Based on these data, a predictive model was established to reconstruct SOM across four time periods (1990s, 2000s, 2010s, and 2020s) using corresponding remote sensing data. In addition, an independent dataset of 33 validation samples was used to evaluate model performance, including 8 samples from the 1990s and 25 samples from the 2020s. These validation samples were not included in the model development dataset. At each sampling site, five subsamples were collected within a sampling unit and mixed to form one composite sample. The geographic coordinates of each sampling location were recorded using a portable global positioning system (GPS). Soil samples were stored in cloth bags to prevent contamination during transport. In the laboratory, samples were ground, air-dried and sieved to ≤2 mm33. All Landsat TM/OLI cloud-free images covering the Sanjiang Plain from 1984 to 2023 were selected using Google Earth Engine (GEE) (https://code.earthengine.google.com/), and were processed with radiometric and atmospheric corrections on GEE, with specific parameters shown in Table 1. To ensure temporal consistency across periods, we constructed decadal median composites at 10-year intervals (1984–1993, 1994–2003, 2004–2013, and 2014–2023). Since the Sanjiang Plain is typically in bare soil condition from early April to mid-May, we used monthly median composites from April to May for each decade to minimize the influence of crop residue and snow cover. Given that SOM content exhibits relatively low interannual variability, these four periods are referred to as the 1990s, 2000s, 2010s, and 2020 s, respectively, in the ideal state.

TM

A key challenge in multi-sensor time-series analysis is the radiometric inconsistency between Landsat TM and OLI sensors. To address this, we applied the cross-calibration coefficients proposed by Meng et al.15, which use ETM+ as a bridging sensor to calibrate TM to the OLI level (Table S2). Notably, Landsat 7 ETM+ has experienced a Scan Line Corrector (SLC) failure since 2003, resulting in data gaps. To avoid the influence of such gaps, we used only ETM+ images acquired before the SLC failure (2000–2003) for the cross-calibration. For ETM+ images used in the decadal composites beyond 2003, we applied the standard gap-filling method recommended by the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS), which interpolates missing pixels using neighboring valid values. Additionally, pixels with insufficient valid observations during the composite period were excluded to further ensure data quality.

Covariate collection and pre-processing

In this study, a digital elevation model (DEM) with a spatial resolution of 30 m was obtained from the USGS website (https://gdex.cr.usgs.gov/gdex/), while slope gradient (SLP) and slope direction (ASP) of the study area were extracted using SAGA GIS software. The normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), enhanced vegetation index (EVI), and brightness index (BI) were calculated according to Meng et al.15 and Liu et al.17; in addition, we calculated the transformed vegetation index (TVI), soil-adjusted vegetation index (SAVI), land surface water index (LSWI), and moisture Stress Index (MSI), with detailed information in Table 2. In this study, a total of seven spectral indices and three topographic factors were taken for SOM content prediction.

K-means clustering

K-means clustering is a commonly used soil classification method, which can effectively improve the prediction performance in soil property prediction16,35. K-means algorithm determines the cluster centers and the different classes by minimizing an objective function of squared error, whose goal is to keep the cluster centers as far away from each other as possible and to associate the sample points with the cluster centers that are closest to them36–38. In this study, we utilized the fpc package in the R programming environment to determine the optimal number of clusters and evaluated them using profile coefficients. Subsequently, we constructed separate SOM prediction models for each clustered sample and combined the results of each clustering to obtain the final prediction. Modeling methods

To improve the prediction accuracy of SOM and account for spatial heterogeneity, both global and hybrid modeling approaches were developed and compared in this study. The overall workflow includes: (1) clustering of samples based on environmental variables, (2) construction of a global model using all samples, (3) development of hybrid models within each cluster, and (4) comparison of model performance to select the optimal model for SOM mapping (Fig. 3).

Research framework.

Global model (Random Forest)

The global model was developed using the Random Forest (RF) algorithm, a widely used ensemble learning method that constructs multiple regression trees and aggregates their predictions39. RF is known for its robustness and ability to reduce overfitting40. In this study, the RF model was implemented using the randomForest package in R. The number of trees (ntree) was set to 700, and the number of variables randomly sampled at each split (mtry) was set to one-third of the total number of predictor variables, which ensured stable model performance.

Probabilistic hybrid models

In addition to the global RF model, a probabilistic hybrid modeling approach was developed to better capture spatial heterogeneity. This approach assumes that the study area consists of multiple subpopulations, each with distinct relationships between environmental variables and SOM23,41. Through the probabilistic hybrid model, we use the pixel-level soil classification probabilities as weights to ensure that none of the pixels has a classification probability of more than 100%, thus improving the prediction performance of SOM42. Specifically, we multiply the probabilities of the clustering results by the respective SOM predictions and eventually add them together to obtain the final prediction of the SOM, a method described in detail by Wang et al.43.

Model evaluation and uncertainty analysis

The dataset was randomly divided into training and validation subsets using a 7:3 ratio, with 70% of the samples used for model training and 30% for independent validation. To ensure the representativeness of the validation dataset, samples were selected to cover the full range of SOM values, including low-, medium-, and high-SOM levels. Model performance was evaluated using multiple statistical metrics, including the coefficient of determination (R²) (Eq. 3), root mean square error (RMSE) (Eq. 4), and residual prediction deviation (RPD) (Eq. 5). Higher R² and RPD values and lower RMSE indicate better model performance44,45. In addition, a 10-fold cross-validation procedure was applied to assess model robustness and quantify prediction uncertainty. The dataset was randomly partitioned into ten subsets, and the model was trained and validated iteratively. The final prediction results were obtained by averaging the outputs from the ten folds, which were further used to generate the spatial distribution map of SOM content.

Data Records

This dataset provides a distribution map of soil organic matter in cultivated land in the Three Rivers Plain of Northeast China every 10 years from 1984 to 2023, with a nominal spatial resolution of 30 meters46. The dataset is stored in GeoTIFF format, with pixel values measured in grams per kilogram. The spatial range of the dataset is 43°49′55″ to 48°27′40″ north latitude and 129°11′20″ to 135°05′26″ east longitude. All data layers can be accessed through zenodo View and Download 10.5281/zenodo.17694315, and can be used for GIS software or remote sensing analysis platforms.

Technical Validation

The results showed significant correlations between vegetation indices, topographic factors, and SOM content (Fig. 4). In addition, the response ranges of environmental covariates varied considerably among different clusters, particularly for slope (SLP) and moisture stress index (MSI). Based on these findings, both global and hybrid models were developed for SOM prediction by integrating soil spectral characteristics and environmental covariates (Fig. 5). For the global model, the inclusion of environmental covariates significantly improved prediction accuracy, with R² increasing to 0.55, RMSE decreasing to 10.37 g kg−1, and RPD reaching 1.56. A similar trend was observed for the hybrid model, where the addition of environmental covariates increased R² by 0.18, reduced RMSE by 1.74 g kg−1, and improved RPD by 0.22. Notably, the hybrid model consistently outperformed the global model, achieving a maximum R² of 0.72, RMSE of 8.66 g kg−1, and RPD of 1.75, indicating its superior capability in capturing the complex relationships between SOM and environmental variables.

Validation results

Clustering results of sanjiang plain

In this study, we applied K-means clustering to partition the cultivated land of the Sanjiang Plain into relatively homogeneous zones prior to model development. The optimal number of clusters was determined to be 2, and the cultivated land was classified into two clusters (Cluster 1 and Cluster 2) based on the K-means algorithm (Table S3). From 1990s to 2020s, the distribution trends of cluster 1 and cluster 2 showed similarity, with cluster 1 accounting for about 61% of the four periods and the corresponding cluster 2 about 39% (Fig. 6). The two clusters exhibit distinct soil-landscape characteristics. Cluster 1 is mainly concentrated in the eastern part of Shuangyashan, Jiamusi, Qitaihe, and Jixi, characterized by lower elevation, flatter terrain, and higher SOM content, reflecting alluvial parent materials and favorable drainage conditions. Cluster 2 is primarily distributed in Hegang, Harbin, and Mudanjiang, with higher elevation, more variable topography, and lower SOM content, corresponding to areas with more intensive cultivation history.

In addition, according to the classification probability results, there is a significant difference between the classification probabilities of cluster 1 and cluster 2 (Fig. 7). For cluster 1, the classification probability of 2000s and 2010s is above 90%, while the classification probability of 1990s and 2020 s is between 75% and 90%; currently, the classification probability of cluster 2 area is less than 10%. SOM mapping results of Sanjiang Plain from 1984 to 2023

From 1984 to 2023, the Sanjiang Plain exhibited a general decline in SOM content, with regional averages decreasing from 46.05 g kg−1 in the 1990s to 44.61 g kg−1 in the 2020 s (Fig. 8). High-SOM areas (>50 g kg−1) were mainly concentrated in parts of Jiamusi and Shuangyashan, while low-SOM areas (<20 g kg−1) were found in northeastern Hegang, reflecting spatial heterogeneity driven by land use and environmental conditions. Overall, 52.95% of cultivated land experienced SOM loss over four decades, particularly during the 2000s–2010s, coinciding with intensified agriculture. Although localized recovery occurred after 2010—partly due to conservation practices—the overall trend highlights ongoing SOM degradation and the need for sustainable soil management. To further evaluate the reliability of the spatial patterns, we compared our SOM map with the SoilGrids dataset (Figure S1). The results show that both datasets exhibit generally consistent spatial distribution patterns, with higher values in the southeast Sanjiang Plain and lower values in the western regions. However, our dataset captures finer spatial variability at the regional scale. Differences in magnitude were observed, which may be attributed to differences in spatial resolution, input data, and modeling approaches, as well as the distinction between SOC stock (SoilGrids) and SOM content (this study). These results further support the reliability of the proposed SOM dataset.

Monitoring trends in SOM evolution of cultivated land using remote sensing

Previous studies on the trend of soil organic matter evolution in cultivated soils mainly used the sample point comparison method, soil carbon model simulation method and remote sensing monitoring method. For example, a comparative study of sample sites found that the average SOC storage in surface soil (0–20 cm) in China increased from 28.6 Mg ha−1 in 1980 to 32.9 Mg ha−1 in 201147. Furthermore, Xie et al.48 modeled the dynamic trend of SOM by using soil carbon cycle models such as RothC, Century, and DNDC, combined with historical climate data, land use changes, and management practices.

Remote sensing monitoring has become an effective tool for tracking SOM dynamics at the regional scale. In the black soil region, due to the vast area of cultivated land, long-term Landsat time series data since the 1990s, combined with field survey data, enable the development of empirical models to quantitatively reconstruct the spatial and temporal evolution of SOM. We also set up additional validation points to demonstrate that the model has good transferability (Figure S2). This method can objectively and efficiently track long-term SOM trends and provide a scientific basis for land use and management. However, it should be noted that the reconstruction of SOM in earlier periods (e.g., the 1990s and 2000s) relies on models calibrated using more recent soil samples, which may introduce uncertainty due to temporal extrapolation and potential mismatches between soil observations and remote sensing data.

A methodological consideration worth noting relates to the conversion of SOC to SOM. In this study, we applied the classical Van Bemmelen factor of 1.724, which assumes that SOM contains 58% carbon. This factor has been widely adopted in regional soil surveys of Northeast China, facilitating comparability with previous studies in the same area. However, as noted by49, the conversion factor can vary between 1.7 and 2.0 depending on soil type, depth, and organic matter composition. For black soils (Phaeozems) in particular, the appropriateness of this fixed factor may warrant further investigation, as variations in organic matter composition could influence the accuracy of SOM estimates50. Future studies incorporating site-specific conversion factors or direct SOM measurements would help refine the quantification of SOM dynamics in this region.

The temporal evolution of cultivated land SOM in the Sanjiang Plain showed a fluctuating downward trend during this research period. Cultivated land SOM content declined over time in most areas, especially in the central and northeastern parts of Jiamusi City; however, a few areas showed an increase in SOM content during a specific period (Figure S3). These findings are consistent with those of Meng et al.15, who found that SOC content decreased significantly in northeast China during the first phase (1984–2000), while SOC maintained a moderate decreasing trend during the second phase (2001–2021) (Figure S4). Nevertheless, the magnitude of long-term changes should be interpreted with caution, as uncertainties associated with model-based reconstruction and limited historical soil observations may influence the absolute estimates of SOM dynamics.

A potential source of uncertainty in this study is the assumption of a fixed bare soil window (April–May) across the 40-year study period. Agricultural practices in the Sanjiang Plain, such as planting schedules, residue management, and tillage systems, have evolved over time and may have altered the exact timing and duration of bare soil exposure. Such changes could introduce temporal inconsistencies in the representation of bare soil conditions. However, April–May generally corresponds to the early growing season in this region, prior to full crop emergence, and has been widely recognized as the period with minimal vegetation cover. Nevertheless, some residual uncertainty may remain. Future studies could improve this aspect by incorporating dynamic phenology-based time windows or long-term crop calendar datasets, which would allow for a more precise characterization of bare soil conditions under changing agricultural practices. Although the hybrid model showed improved performance compared to the global model, the reported accuracy should be interpreted with caution due to the potential influence of spatial autocorrelation, which may lead to optimistic estimates under conventional cross-validation. In addition, while uncertainty was partially reflected by variability across cross-validation folds, spatially explicit uncertainty information was not provided. These limitations may affect the robustness and interpretability of the results. Future studies should incorporate spatial cross-validation and generate uncertainty maps to further improve model reliability and the usability of the dataset.
